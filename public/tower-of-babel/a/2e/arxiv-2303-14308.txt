Polynomial Optimization Relaxations for Generalized Semi-Infinite Programs
Xiaomeng Hu and Jiawang Nie

Abstract
. This paper studies generalized semi-infinite programs (GSIPs) given by polynomials. We propose a hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations to solve them. They are based on Lagrange multiplier expressions and polynomial extensions. Moment-SOS relaxations are applied to solve the polynomial optimization. The convergence of this hierarchy is shown under certain conditions. In particular, the classical semi-infinite programs (SIPs) can be solved as a special case of GSIPs. We also study GSIPs that have convex infinity constraints and show that they can be solved exactly by a single polynomial optimization relaxation. The computational efficiency is demonstrated by extensive numerical results.

1.
Introduction

We consider the generalized semi-infinite program (GSIP):

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g(x,u)\geq 0\quad\forall u\in U(x),\\
&x\in X,\\
\end{array}\right.

where the constraining sets
X
and
U(x)
are given as

(1.2)

X=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{lc}c_{i}(x)=0\,(i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}),\\
c_{j}(x)\geq 0\,(j\in\mathcal{J}_{1})\end{array}\right\},

(1.3)

U(x)=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}h_{i}(x,u)=0\,(i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}),\\
h_{j}(x,u)\geq 0\,(j\in\mathcal{J}_{2})\end{array}\right\}.

The constraining function
g(x,u)
in (
1.1
)
is the
s
-dimensional vector

g(x,u)\,\coloneqq\,\big(g_{1}(x,u),\ldots,g_{s}(x,u)\big).

In this paper, we assume that all functions are given by polynomials, and

\mathcal{I}_{1},\,\mathcal{I}_{2},\,\mathcal{J}_{1}
and
\mathcal{J}_{2}

are finite label sets.
For each feasible
x
, the constraint
g(x,u)\geq 0

is required to hold for
every
point
u\in U(x)
.
In particular, for the special case
U(x)=\emptyset
(the empty set),
this constraint automatically holds for all
x\in X
.
Throughout the paper,
a\geq b
means that
the entries of
a-b
are all nonnegative.
Generally,
U(x)
is an infinite set,
which makes it challenging to solve the GSIP.
The feasible set of (
1.1
) is denoted as

(1.4)

\mathcal{F}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{x\in X:g(x,u)\geq 0\,\forall u\in U(x)\big\}.

The problem (
1.1
) is called a
semi-infinite program (SIP) if the set
U(x)\equiv U

is independent of
x
.

GSIPs have broad applications.
One is the centering design problem,
which aims to maximize the volume of a parameterized set
U(x)
,
such as balls/ellipsoids
[
6
]
,
boxes
[
2
]
and gemstones
[
53
]
,
inscribed in a given region
[
43
]
.
For instance, an ellipsoid can be expressed as

U(x)\,=\,\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,(u-a(x))^{T}P(x)^{-1}(u-a(x))\leq 1\big\},

where
a(x)
is a vector parameterizing the center and

P(x)
is a symmetric positive definite matrix
(i.e.,
P(x)\succ 0
for
x\in X
)
parameterizing the ellipsoid shape.
The maximum ellipsoid design problem is to
look for
x\in X
such that
U(x)
has maximum volume and

U(x)\subseteq Y
, for a given set
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
If
Y=\{u:g(u)\geq 0\}
for a vector function
g
,
this design problem can be formulated as the GSIP
(see
[
43
,
44
]
):

(1.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max&\det\,P(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g(u)\geq 0\,\,\forall\,u\in U(x),\\
&x\in X.\end{array}\right.

Other applications include
min-max optimization
[
41
,
54
]
,
portfolio optimization
[
26
]
,
disjunctive programming
[
23
]
,
the Stackelberg game
[
43
]
,
and dynamical network classification
[
52
]
.
More applications can be found in

[
14
,
29
,
45
]
.

There are various methods for solving SIPs
(i.e.,
U(x)\equiv U
is constant).
Some methods first approximate
U
by a finite subset and
then solve the resulting approximation optimization
problems with finitely many constraints.
They subsequently update the approximation
until a certain termination criterion is met
[
23
]
.
When SIPs are given by polynomials, a semidefinite relaxation
method is given in
[
50
]
.
Different methods can be obtained
when
U
is approximated by different schemes.
Typical tricks for the approximation are the exchange,
discretization and local reduction. We refer to
[
4
,
19
,
29
,
31
]

for classical surveys on SIPs.

It is harder to solve GSIPs than SIPs.
The methods for SIPs are difficult to be extended to solve GSIPs,
when the set
U(x)
depends on
x
.
To show the difficulty for doing this, suppose
\mathcal{F}_{0}

is an initial approximation for
the feasible set
\mathcal{F}
of (
1.1
)
and solve the relaxation problem

(1.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{F}_{0}.\end{array}\right.

Suppose
\hat{x}
is an optimizer for (
1.6
).
Typically, the point
\hat{x}
is not a feasible point in
\mathcal{F}

(otherwise,
\hat{x}
is a minimizer for the GSIP),
i.e., there exists a point
\hat{u}\in U(\hat{x})
such that

g(\hat{x},\hat{u})\not\geq 0.

Such
\hat{u}
can be obtained by solving the
optimization problem about
u
:

(1.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq s}g_{j}(\hat{x},u)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&u\in U(\hat{x}).\end{array}\right.

The classical exchange technique (see
[
19
]
)
is to solve the strengthened relaxation problem

(1.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g(x,\hat{u})\geq 0,\,x\in\mathcal{F}_{0}.\end{array}\right.

However, the new optimization (
1.8
)
may not contain any minimizer
x^{*}
of (
1.1
)
as a feasible point. This is because

g(x^{*},u)\geq 0
is only required to hold for all
u\in U(x^{*})
.
But we may not have
g(x^{*},\hat{u})\geq 0
,
since it is possible that
\hat{u}\not\in U(x^{*})
.
This phenomenon is shown in Example
3.1
.
The classical exchange trick may not solve GSIPs correctly.
Other types of computational methods for SIPs
experience similar difficulties for solving GSIPs.
We refer to
[
14
,
45
]

for the classical work.

Some methods transform GSIPs into other equivalent problems
(see
[
26
]
for related work).
When the objective
f
is convex,
g(x,u)

is linear in
x
and is quadratic in
u
,
a branch-and-bound method is proposed in
[
27
]
,
which transforms a GSIP into a finite number of convex programs.
Under certain assumptions, GSIPs can be transformed
to SIPs
[
45
]

or some bilevel programs
[
43
]
.
The GSIP can be converted to min-max optimization
[
49
]
.
An approach transforming GSIPs into
penalty problems with finitely many constraints
is given in
[
13
]
.
In computational practice, these equivalent problems
or reformulations are also hard to solve.
It is still a big challenge to solve GSIPs.

Contributions

To solve the GSIP (
1.1
) efficiently and reliably,
we propose a hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations
(P_{k})
.
They have the same objective
f(x)
but their feasible sets

\mathcal{F}_{k}
satisfy the nesting containment

\mathcal{F}_{0}\supsetneqq\mathcal{F}_{1}\supsetneqq\cdots\mathcal{F}_{k}\supsetneqq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal{F}.

The initial relaxation
\mathcal{F}_{0}
can be any set containing
\mathcal{F}
.
At the
k
th stage, we solve the relaxation problem

(P_{k}):\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}.\end{array}\right.

Suppose
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer of
(P_{k})
.
If
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
),
i.e.,
g(\hat{x}_{k},u)\geq 0
for all
u\in U(\hat{x}_{k})
,
then it is an optimizer for the GSIP.
If it is not feasible for (
1.1
),
we need to update
(P_{k})
to get
the next tighter relaxation
(P_{k+1})
.

When
\hat{x}_{k}
is not feasible for (
1.1
),
there must exist
\hat{u}_{k}\in U(\hat{x}_{k})
such that

g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{u}_{k})<0
for some
j
, i.e.,
the inequality
g(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{u}_{k})\geq 0
is violated.
When
U(x)
depends on
x
, we cannot simply select
the new relaxation set
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
as

\mathcal{F}_{k+1}\,=\,\big\{x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}:g(x,\hat{u}_{k})\geq 0\big\}.

This is because we may not have
\hat{u}_{k}\in U(x^{*})

for any minimizer
x^{*}
of (
1.1
).
The classical exchange trick does not work for GSIPs.
To fix this issue, we look for a polynomial vector function
q_{k}(x)
such that

q_{k}(\hat{x}_{k})\,=\,\hat{u}_{k},\quad q_{k}(x)\in U(x)\quad\forall\,x\in X.

Such
q_{k}
is called a polynomial extension at
\hat{u}_{k}
.
It is important to observe that if such
q_{k}
exists, then
every feasible point
x
of (
1.1
) must satisfy the inequality

g(x,q_{k}(x))\geq 0.

Therefore, if
x^{*}
is a minimizer of (
1.1
),
then we must have

g(x^{*},q_{k}(x^{*}))\geq 0.

Therefore, we can select the new polynomial optimization relaxation
(P_{k+1})
as

(1.9)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g(x,q_{k}(x))\geq 0,\,x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}.\end{array}\right.

Equivalently, the new feasible set
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
is updated as

\mathcal{F}_{k+1}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}:\,g(x,q_{k}(x))\geq 0\big\}.

Continuing to do the above, we can get a hierarchy of
polynomial optimization relaxations.
This hierarchy gives a method for solving the GSIP.
It produces a sequence of points
\hat{x}_{k}
,
which serve as approximate optimizers for (
1.1
).
It is expected that either
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer of
(
1.1
) for some
k

(this case is called the finite convergence),
or an accumulation point of
the sequence
\{\hat{x}_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=0}

is a minimizer of (
1.1
)
(this case is called the asymptotic convergence).
Under certain assumptions, we can prove that
the hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations
has either finite convergence or asymptotic convergence.

This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we review some preliminaries about polynomial optimization.
In Section
3
, we show how to use polynomial extensions
to construct the hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations.
Its convergence is also shown there.
In Section
4
, we show how to get polynomial extensions.
In Section
5
, we discuss how to solve GSIPs
that have convex infinite constraints.
In Section
6
, various numerical examples for both GSIPs and SIPs
are presented to show the efficiency of the proposed method.
Some conclusions and discussions are made in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

Notation

Let
\mathbb{N}
(resp.,
\mathbb{R}
) denote the set of nonnegative integers
(resp., real numbers). For
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\left\lceil t\right\rceil

denotes the smallest integer greater than or equal to
t
.
For a finite set
A
, we use
|A|
to denote its cardinality.
The symbol
\mathbb{R}[x]=\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]

denotes the ring of polynomials in

x\coloneqq(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
with real coefficients.
For a symmetric matrix
W
,
W\succeq 0
(resp.,
W\succ 0
)
means that
W
is positive semidefinite (resp., positive definite).
For a vector
u
,
\|u\|
denotes the standard Euclidean norm.
The notation
I_{n}
denotes
n
-dimensional identity matrix,
and
e
denotes the vector of all ones
(its length is determined by the operation on it).
For an integer
s>0
, denote the set
[s]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,s\}
.
For a power
\alpha\coloneqq(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,
we denote the monomial
x^{\alpha}\coloneqq x^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}\cdots x^{\alpha_{n}}_{n}
.
The notation

\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}\leq d\}

denotes the set of monomial powers with degrees at most
d
.
So,
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2d}}
denotes the space of
real vectors labeled by
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2d}
.
The notation
\nabla_{x}f
stands for the gradient of
f
in
x

and
\nabla_{u}g
stands for the gradient of
g
in
u
.

A polynomial tuple
h=\left(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\right)

in
\mathbb{R}[x]
generates the ideal

\text{Ideal}(h)\,\coloneqq\,h_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x],

the smallest ideal containing all
h_{i}
.
The
2k
-th degree truncation of
\text{Ideal}(h)
is

\text{Ideal}(h)_{2k}\coloneqq h_{1}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]_{2k-\operatorname{deg}\left(h_{1}\right)}+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot\mathbb{R}[x]_{2k-\operatorname{deg}\left(h_{m}\right)}.

A polynomial
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}[x]
is said to be a sum of squares (SOS) polynomial if
\sigma=\sigma_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{k}^{2}
for some

\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{R}[x]
.
The symbol
\Sigma[x]
denotes the set of all SOS polynomials in
x
.
We refer to
[
20
,
33
,
34
]
for related work
about SOS polynomials and matrices.
The quadratic module for a polynomial tuple
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{t})

is (
q_{0}\coloneqq 1
)

QM[q]\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\sigma_{i}q_{i}\,\middle|\,\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma[x]\right\}.

For a positive integer
k>0
,
the
2k
th degree truncation of
\text{QM}(q)
is

QM[q]_{2k}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{t}\sigma_{i}q_{i}\,\middle|\,\sigma_{i}\in\Sigma[x],\operatorname{deg}\left(\sigma_{i}q_{i}\right)\leq 2k\right\}.

The sum
\text{Ideal}(p)+

\text{QM}(q)
is said to be archimedean
if there exists
b\in\operatorname{Ideal}(p)+\operatorname{QM}(q)

such that the inequality
b(x)\geq 0
gives a compact set.

A vector labelled as

y\coloneqq(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2d}}

is called a truncated multi-sequences (tms) of degree
2d
.
For
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{2d}^{n}}
, define the operation

\langle p,y\rangle\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{2d}^{n}}p_{\alpha}y_{\alpha}\qquad\text{for}\quad p=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{2d}^{n}}p_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}.

For a given
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
with degree
\deg(p)\leq 2d
, let

t\coloneqq\lceil d-\deg(p)/2\rceil
.
Let
L^{(d)}_{p}[y]
denote the symmetric matrix such that

\langle p\phi^{2},y\rangle\quad=\quad\mathrm{vec}(\phi)^{T}\cdot L^{(d)}_{p}[y]\cdot\mathrm{vec}(\phi)\qquad\text{for all}\quad\phi\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{t}.

where
\mathrm{vec}(\phi)
denotes the coefficient vector of
\phi
,
ordered by the graded alphabetical ordering.
In particular, for the case
p=1
, we get the moment matrix

M_{d}[y]=L^{(d)}_{1}[y]
, which is equivalent to the expression

M_{d}[y]\coloneqq(y_{\alpha+\beta})_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}}.

For the polynomial
p
, let
\mathcal{V}_{p}^{(2d)}[y]
denote the vector such that

\langle p\phi,y\rangle\,=\,(\mathcal{V}_{p}^{(2d)}[y])^{T}\mathrm{vec}(\phi).

The
\mathcal{V}_{p}^{(2d)}[y]
is called the
localizing vector of
p
, generated by
y
.

2.1.
Moment-SOS relaxations

For convenience of description,
we consider the standard polynomial optimization problem

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}&f(x)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&p_{i}(x)=0\,(i=1,\ldots,m_{1})\\
&p_{i}(x)\geq 0\,(i=m_{1}+1,\ldots,m_{2}).\end{array}\right.

The Lasserre type Moment-SOS hierarchy
can be applied to solve (
2.1
).
The SOS relaxation is

(2.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max&\gamma\\
\mathit{s.t.}&f-\gamma\in\mathrm{Ideal}\left[p_{1},\ldots,p_{m_{1}}\right]_{2k}+\mathrm{QM}\left[p_{m_{1}+1},\ldots,p_{m_{2}}\right]_{2k}.\end{array}\right.

Its dual problem is the moment relaxation

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&\langle f,y\rangle\\
\mathit{s.t.}&\mathcal{V}_{p_{i}}^{(2k)}[y]=0\,(i=1,\ldots,m_{1}),\\
&L_{p_{i}}^{(k)}[y]\succeq 0\,(i=m_{1}+1,\ldots,m_{2}),\\
&M_{k}[y]\succeq 0,\\
&y_{0}=1,y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{2k}^{n}}.\end{array}\right.

Suppose
y^{*}
is an optimizer of
(
2.3
)
.
Denote the degree

d_{0}\coloneqq\max_{i\in[m_{2}]}\{\lceil\deg(f)/2\rceil,\lceil\deg(p_{i})/2\rceil\}.

If there exists a degree
t\in[d_{0},k]
such that

(2.4)

\text{rank}M_{t-d_{0}}[y^{*}]=\text{rank}M_{t}[y^{*}],

then we can extract
r\coloneqq\text{rank}M_{t}[y^{*}]
minimizers.
Generically, flat truncation is a sufficient and necessary condition for
extracting optimizers from Moment-SOS relaxations
[
35
]
.
We refer to
[
18
,
24
,
25
,
40
]

for general polynomial optimization theory and methods.

3.
Polynomial optimization relaxations

This section gives a hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations
for solving GSIPs.
It requires to use polynomial extensions.

3.1.
Polynomial extensions

To solve the GSIP (
1.1
) efficiently and reliably,
we relax its infinity constraints
into finitely many ones and then
subsequently update them to get better relaxation.
To achieve this, we need to construct a sequence of
polynomial optimization relaxations
(P_{k})
,
which have the same objective
f(x)
and have the feasible sets

\mathcal{F}_{k}
satisfying the nesting containment

(3.1)

\mathcal{F}_{0}\supsetneqq\mathcal{F}_{1}\supsetneqq\cdots\mathcal{F}_{k}\supsetneqq\cdots\supseteq\mathcal{F}.

The initial
\mathcal{F}_{0}
can be any set containing
\mathcal{F}

(e.g,
\mathcal{F}_{0}=X
).
For each
k=0,1,\ldots
, the GSIP (
1.1
) is relaxed to

(3.2)

(P_{k})\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{k}\coloneqq&\min&f(x)\\
&\mathit{s.t.}&x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}.\end{array}\right.

The minimal value for
(P_{k})
is denoted as
f_{k}
.
Clearly, the nesting relation (
3.1
) implies

(3.3)

f_{0}\leq f_{1}\leq\cdots f_{k}\leq\cdots\leq f^{*},

where
f^{*}
denotes the optimal value of (
1.1
).
Suppose
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer for (
3.2
).
To check if
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
) or not,
we need to solve the optimization problems (for
j=1,\ldots,s
),

(3.4)

(Q_{k,j})\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\hat{g}_{k,j}\coloneqq\min&g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k},u)\\
\mathit{s.t.}&u\in U(\hat{x}_{k}).\end{array}\right.

The minimal value of
(Q_{k,j})
is denoted as
\hat{g}_{k,j}
.
Note that
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
) if and only if

g(\hat{x}_{k},u)\geq 0
for all
u\in U(\hat{x}_{k})
. Therefore,

\hat{x}_{k}\,\text{is feasible for \eqref{primal-GSIP}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\hat{g}_{k,j}\geq 0,\,j=1,\ldots,s.

If some
\hat{g}_{k,j}<0
, then
\hat{x}_{k}

is not feasible for
(
1.1
)
.
Denote the label set

(3.5)

\mathcal{N}_{k}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{j\in[s]:\hat{g}_{k,j}<0\big\}.

Suppose
\hat{u}_{k,j}
is a minimizer of
(Q_{k,j})
.
Using the classical exchange trick, one may select the new set

\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
as

(3.6)

\mathcal{F}_{k+1}\,\coloneqq\,\{x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}:\,g(x,\hat{u}_{k,j})\geq 0,j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\}.

However, the above choice may result in that
x^{*}\not\in\mathcal{F}_{k+1}

for every optimizer
x^{*}
of (
1.1
).
The reason for this is that the constraining set

U(x)
depends on
x
. This can be shown in the following example.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the following GSIP from
[
32
]
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&-x_{1}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g\coloneqq u^{5}-3x^{2}_{2}\geq 0\quad\forall u\in U(x),\\
&x\in X,\end{array}\right.

where the constraining sets are

\begin{array}[]{ll}X=[0,1]^{2},\quad U(x)=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{1}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{ll}-u\geq 0,u+2\geq 0,\\
u^{5}+4x^{2}_{1}+x^{2}_{2}-1\geq 0\end{array}\right\}.\end{array}

The minimizers for
(P_{0})
and
(Q_{0})
are

\hat{x}_{0}\approx\begin{pmatrix}1.0000,0.4350\end{pmatrix}

and
\hat{u}_{0}\approx-1.2611
respectively.
The true minimizer for this GSIP is

x^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5000,0.0000\end{pmatrix}
.
However,
g(x^{*},\hat{u}_{0})\approx-3.1892<0
. If we let

\mathcal{F}_{1}=\{x\in\mathcal{F}_{0}:g(x,\hat{u}_{0})\geq 0\}
,
then
x^{*}\not\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{1}\not\supseteq\mathcal{F}
.
We cannot get the minimizer if
\mathcal{F}_{1}
is selected
as in (
3.6
).
More details for solving this GSIP is referred to
Example
6.3
.

To fix the above issue, we need to introduce the concept
of polynomial extension (also see
[
39
]
).

Definition 3.2
.

For a pair
(\hat{x},\hat{u})
with
\hat{x}\in X
and
\hat{u}\in U(\hat{x})
,
a polynomial vector function
q(x)

is called a polynomial extension of
\hat{u}
for
U(x)
if

(3.7)

q(\hat{x})=\hat{u},\qquad q(x)\in U(x)\quad\forall x\in X.

Recall that
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer of (
3.2
).
Consider the case that
\hat{g}_{k,j}<0
and

\hat{u}_{k,j}
is a minimizer of
Q_{k,j}
.
Assume
q_{k,j}(x)
is a polynomial extension of

\hat{u}_{k,j}
for
U(x)
, i.e.,

(3.8)

q_{k,j}(\hat{x}_{k})=\hat{u}_{k,j},\qquad q_{k,j}(x)\in U(x)\quad\forall x\in X.

Observe that
g(x,q_{k,j}(x))\geq 0
for every feasible point
x\in\mathcal{F}
.
Therefore, the new tighter relaxation
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}

can be selected as

(3.9)

\mathcal{F}_{k+1}\,\coloneqq\,\{x\in\mathcal{F}_{k}:\,g(x,q_{k,j}(x))\geq 0,\,j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}\}.

Continuing to do this, we can get a hierarchy of relaxations
\mathcal{F}_{k}

satisfying the nesting relation (
3.1
).

3.2.
The algorithm

Using polynomial extensions in the above,
we can get the following algorithm for solving GSIPs.

Algorithm 3.3
.

For the GSIP (
1.1
), do the following:

Step 0:

Let
k\coloneqq 0
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}\coloneqq X

(or any set
\mathcal{F}_{0}\supseteq\mathcal{F}
).

Step 1:

Solve the polynomial optimization relaxation
(P_{k})
as in (
3.2
).
If
(P_{k})
is infeasible, output that (
1.1
)
is infeasible and there are no minimizers.
If it is feasible and has minimizers, compute a minimizer
\hat{x}_{k}
.

Step 2:

Check whether or not
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
).
Solve the optimization problem
(Q_{k,j})
as in
(
3.4
), for each
j=1,\ldots,s
.
If
U(\hat{x}_{k})=\emptyset
,
then the constraint that
g(\hat{x}_{k},u)\geq 0

for all
u\in U(\hat{x}_{k})
is automatically satisfied.
For this case, let
\hat{g}_{k}\coloneqq+\infty
.
If
U(\hat{x}_{k})\neq\emptyset
,
compute its optimal value
\hat{g}_{k,j}

and a minimizer
\hat{u}_{k,j}
(if any) for
(Q_{k,j})
.
Then let

\hat{g}_{k}\coloneqq\min\limits_{j\in[s]}\,\hat{g}_{k,j}.

Step 3:

If
\hat{g}_{k}\geq 0
, output that
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer
for (
1.1
) and stop.
If
\hat{g}_{k}<0
, construct a polynomial extension

q_{k,j}(x)
satisfying (
3.8
)
for each
j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}
,
where
\mathcal{N}_{k}
is the label set as in (
3.5
).

Step 4:

Update the set
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
as in (
3.9
).
Let
k\coloneqq k+1
and go to Step 1.

We would like to remark that Algorithm
3.3

is in the framework of the
conceptual
exchange method
of Still’s work
[
46
, Algorithm 2]
.
General nonlinear functions are considered in
[
46
]
.
There are no efficient methods/packages for computing
global minimizers for general nonlinear optimization.
Moreover, computing continuous functions satisfying
feasible sets given by infinitely many constraints is also difficult.
The conceptual exchange method is
generally hard to be implemented in computational practice.
However, when GSIPs are given by polynomials,
the optimization problems
(P)_{k}
and
(Q_{k,j})

can be solved globally (by Moment-SOS relaxations, see section
2.1
),
and the polynomial extensions
can be computed for many frequently appearing feasible sets
(see Section
4
).
This is our major motivation for studying GSIPs of polynomials.

To check if
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
) or not,
the optimization problem
(Q_{k,j})
in (
3.4
)
need to be solved very accurately.
We can apply the tight relaxation method in

[
37
]
to solve them.
In Step 2, if
(Q_{k,j})
is unbounded below, i.e.,
\hat{g}_{k,j}=-\infty
,
we can just choose any point
\hat{u}_{k,j}

such that
g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{u}_{k,j})<0
.
In computational practice, we often select a priori accuracy parameter

\epsilon>0
(e.g,
\epsilon=10^{-6}
), to detect if

\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
) or not.
If
\hat{g}_{k}\geq-\epsilon
,
we can view
\hat{x}_{k}
as a feasible point,
i.e., it is an accurate minimizer for the GSIP.

The polynomial optimization relaxation
(P_{k})
can be solved
by the Lasserre type Moment-SOS relaxations
(see subsection
2.1
).
Under the archimedean condition,
it has the asymptotic convergence (see
[
24
]
).
Moreover, under some classical optimality conditions,
the finite convergence occurs (see
[
36
]
).
The convergence can be detected by the flat truncation condition
(see
[
35
]
).

3.3.
Convergence properties

We now study the convergence of Algorithm
3.3
.
First, if Algorithm
3.3
terminates at a certain loop,
the computed solution is a minimizer
for the GSIP(
1.1
).

Theorem 3.4

(Finite convergence)
.

In Algorithm
3.3
, if
\hat{g}_{k}\geq 0
at the
k
th loop,
then the minimizer
\hat{x}_{k}
for
(P_{k})

is also a minimizer for (
1.1
).

Proof.

The feasible sets
\mathcal{F}_{k}

satisfy the nesting relation (
3.1
)
and the optimal values
f_{k}
satisfy (
3.3
).
If
\hat{g}_{k}\geq 0
at the
k
th loop,
it means that
g(\hat{x}_{k},u)\geq 0
for all
u\in U(\hat{x}_{k})
,
i.e.,
\hat{x}_{k}
is feasible for (
1.1
).
So,
f_{k}=f(\hat{x}_{k})\geq f^{*}
, hence
f_{k}=f^{*}

and
\hat{x}_{k}
is a minimizer for (
1.1
).
∎∎

Second, we study the asymptotic convergence of Algorithm
3.3
.
Let
x^{*}
be an accumulation point of the sequence

\{\hat{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
.
We consider the value function

(3.10)

v(x)\,\coloneqq\,\min\limits_{j\in[s]}\min\limits_{u\in U(x)}g_{j}(x,u).

People often assume
v(x)
is continuous at
x^{*}
.
This can be ensured under certain conditions
(see
[
8
,
9
,
15
,
30
]
).
For convenience of notation, let
\hat{u}_{k}

denote the point
\hat{u}_{k,j}
such that

g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{u}_{k,j})=\hat{g}_{k}

and its polynomial extension
\hat{q}_{k,j}(x)
of
\hat{u}_{k,j}

is also denoted as
\hat{q}_{k}(x)
.

Theorem 3.5

(Asymptotic convergence)
.

Let
\{\hat{x}_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be the sequence
produced by Algorithm
3.3
. Suppose

x^{*}
is an accumulation point of this sequence.
Assume the following:

(i)

In the
k
th loop, the problems
(P_{k})
and
(Q_{k,j})

have minimizers
\hat{x}_{k}
and
\hat{u}_{k,j}
respectively;

(ii)

Suppose
(x^{*},u^{*})
is an accumulation point of

\{(\hat{x}_{k},\hat{u}_{k})\}^{\infty}_{k=0}
and
the value function
v(x)
as in (
3.10
) is continuous at
x^{*}
;

(iii)

The polynomial extensions
q_{k,j}(x)
are equicontinuous at
x^{*}.
1
1

1

This means that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0

such that
\|q_{k,j}(x)-q_{k,j}(x^{*})\|<\epsilon

for all
\|x-x^{*}\|<\delta
and for all
k,j
.

Then, we have
v(x^{*})=0
and
x^{*}

is a minimizer for the GSIP (
1.1
).

Proof.

Since
x^{*}
is an accumulation point of the sequence

\{\hat{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, there exists a subsequence
such that
\lim\limits_{l\rightarrow\infty}\hat{x}_{k_{l}}=x^{*}
.
Since
f
is a polynomial function,
the monotonicity relation (
3.3
) implies

f(x^{*})=\lim\limits_{l\rightarrow\infty}f(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})=\lim\limits_{k_{l}\rightarrow\infty}f_{k_{l}}\leq f^{*}.

If Algorithm
3.3
does not stop at the
k_{l}
th loop, we have

v(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})\,<\,0.

Letting
l\rightarrow\infty
, we get
v(x^{*})\leq 0
,
since
v(x)
is continuous at
x^{*}
.

Next, we pick an arbitrary
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
.
For every
k_{l}>k^{\prime}
, the feasibility of
the polynomial optimization relaxation
(P_{k_{l}})
implies that

g(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{q}_{k^{\prime}}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}}))\geq 0.

Letting
l\rightarrow\infty
, we then get

g(x^{*},\hat{q}_{k^{\prime}}(x^{*}))\geq 0.

Since
k^{\prime}
can be arbitrary, the above implies that

(3.11)

g(x^{*},\hat{q}_{k}(x^{*}))\geq 0,\quad\mbox{for all}\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Note that
\hat{u}_{k_{l}}
denotes the point
\hat{u}_{k_{l},j}
such that

g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l},j})=\hat{g}_{k_{l}}
, so

g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l}})\,=\,g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l},j}).

Since
\hat{u}_{k_{l},j}
is the minimizer of

g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},u)
over
u\in U(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})
,
we have

(3.12)

v(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})=\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l},j})=\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l}}).

Therefore, (
3.11
) and (
3.12
) imply that

\displaystyle v(x^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})+\big(v(x^{*})-v(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})\big)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Big(\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l}})-\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(x^{*},\hat{q}_{k_{l}}(x^{*}))\Big)+\big(v(x^{*})-v(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})\big).

By the polynomial extension and equicontinuity, we have

u^{*}=\lim\limits_{l\to\infty}\hat{u}_{k_{l}}=\lim\limits_{l\to\infty}\hat{q}_{k_{l}}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}})=\lim\limits_{l\to\infty}\hat{q}_{k_{l}}(x^{*}),

\lim\limits_{l\to\infty}\Big(\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(\hat{x}_{k_{l}},\hat{u}_{k_{l}})-\min\limits_{j\in[s]}g_{j}(x^{*},\hat{q}_{k_{l}}(x^{*})\Big)\,=\,0.

Since
v(x)
is continuous at
x^{*}
,
the above implies
v(x^{*})\geq 0
.

Combining the above, we have
v(x^{*})=0
,
so
g(x^{*},u)\geq 0
holds for all
u\in U(x^{*})
.
This implies that
x^{*}
is feasible for the GSIP (
1.1
),
i.e.,
x^{*}\in\mathcal{F}
. Hence,
f(x^{*})\geq f^{*}

and we must have
f(x^{*})=f^{*}
. In other words,
x^{*}

is a minimizer for the GSIP (
1.1
).
∎∎

When the sequence
\{\hat{x}_{k}\}^{\infty}_{k=0}
is bounded,
it always has an accumulation point.
This is the case if either
X
is compact or the objective

f
is coercive, i.e., the sublevel set
\{x\in X:f(x)\leq\upsilon\}

is bounded for each value
\upsilon
.
The equicontinuity condition in Algorithm
3.5
(iii)
can be ensured if the polynomial extension functions
q_{k,j}(x)

have uniformly bounded degrees and bounded coefficients.
In Section
4
, we will see that this is the case
for many common feasible sets.

4.
Polynomial Extensions

This section discusses how to compute polynomial extensions.
Suppose
\hat{x}
is an approximate optimizer obtained from
solving a polynomial optimization relaxation.
We consider the case that
\hat{x}
is not feasible
for the GSIP (
1.1
),
i.e., there exists
\hat{u}\in U(\hat{x})

such that
g_{j}(\hat{x},\hat{u})<0
for some
j\in[s]
.
For the special case of SIP,
the polynomial function
q(x)
can be selected to be
the constant
\hat{u}
. In the following,
we study how to compute a polynomial function

q(x)
satisfying (
3.7
) in Definition
3.2

for more general cases.

First, we look at some common feasible sets for which polynomial extensions
can be given explicitly. They are discussed in
[
39
]
.

Box type constraints

Suppose the infinity constraining set is

U(x)\,=\,\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,l(x)\leq u\leq w(x)\right\},

where
l(x)\,\coloneqq\,(l_{1}(x),\cdots,l_{p}(x))
and

w(x)\,\coloneqq\,(w_{1}(x),\cdots,w_{p}(x))

are given polynomial vectors.
Suppose
l(x)\leq w(x)
on
X
.
For the given pair
(\hat{x},\hat{u})
,
with
\hat{u}=(\hat{u}_{1},\ldots,\hat{u}_{p})
,
we can choose the polynomial extension

q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{p})
as

(4.1)

q_{j}(x)=\sigma_{j}l_{j}(x)+\left(1-\sigma_{j}\right)w_{j}(x),\quad j=1,\ldots,p,

where each scalar

\sigma_{j}\,\coloneqq\,(w_{j}(\hat{x})-\hat{u}_{j})/(w_{j}(\hat{x})-l_{j}(\hat{x}))\in[0,1].

For the case
w_{j}(\hat{x})-l_{j}(\hat{x})=0
,
we just let
\sigma_{j}=0
.
Then, for each
j=1,\ldots,p
,

q_{j}(\hat{x})=\sigma_{j}l_{j}(\hat{x})+\left(1-\sigma_{j}\right)w_{j}(\hat{x})=\hat{u}_{j}.

Clearly,
q(x)\in U(x)
for all
x\in X
.
Such
q(x)
satisfies (
3.7
).

Simplex type constraints
Suppose the infinite constraining set is

U(x)\,=\,\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,l(x)\leq u,\,e^{T}u\leq w(x)\right\},

where
l(x)\,\coloneqq\,(l_{1}(x),\cdots,l_{p}(x))

is a polynomial vector and

w(x)
is a scalar polynomial.
Here,
e
is the vector of all ones.
Suppose
w(x)-e^{T}l(x)\geq 0
on
X
.
For given
(\hat{x},\hat{u})
,
with
\hat{u}=(\hat{u}_{1},\ldots,\hat{u}_{p})
,
we can choose the polynomial extension

q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{p})
as

(4.2)

q_{j}(x)=\mu_{j}\left(w(x)-e^{T}l(x)\right)+l_{j}(x),\quad j=1,\ldots,p,

where each scalar

\mu_{j}=(\hat{u}_{j}-l_{j}(\hat{x}))/(w(\hat{x})-e^{T}l(\hat{x}))\geq 0.

For the case
w(\hat{x})-e^{T}l(\hat{x})=0
,
one can just let
\mu_{j}=0
.
Note that
\sum_{i=}^{p}\mu_{i}\leq 1
and

q_{j}(\hat{x})=\mu_{j}\cdot(w(\hat{x})-e^{T}l(\hat{x}))+l_{j}(\hat{x})=\hat{u}_{j}.

Since
e^{T}l(x)\leq w(x)
on
X
, we have
q(x)\geq l(x)
and

e^{T}q(x)=e^{T}l(x)(1-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{j})+(\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mu_{j})w(x)\leq w(x).

Therefore,
q(x)\in U(x)
for all
x\in X
. Such
q(x)
satisfies (
3.7
).

Ball type constraints
Suppose the infinite constraining set is

U(x)=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:l(x)\leq\|u-a(x)\|\leq w(x)\right\},

where
a(x)=(a_{1}(x),\ldots,a_{p}(x))
is a polynomial vector
and
l(x),w(x)
are given polynomials such that

0\leq l(x)\leq w(x)
on
X
.
We can choose the polynomial extension
q
such that

(4.3)

q(x)\coloneqq a(x)+\tilde{q}(x)v,\quad\tilde{q}(x)=\mu_{1}l(x)+\mu_{2}w(x)

where the scalars
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
and the vector
v

are selected such that

\begin{array}[]{c}\|\hat{u}-a(\hat{x})\|=\mu_{1}l(\hat{x})+\mu_{2}w(\hat{x}),\\
\mu_{1}\geq 0,\,\mu_{2}\geq 0,\,\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}=1,\\
v=(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))/\|\hat{u}-a(\hat{x})\|.\end{array}

For the case
\hat{u}-a(\hat{x})=0
,
we just let
v=e/\sqrt{p}
.
Since
\tilde{q}(\hat{x})=\|\hat{u}-a(\hat{x})\|

and
\|v\|=1
, one can check that

\hat{u}-q(\hat{x})=(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))-(q(\hat{x})-a(\hat{x}))=(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))-\tilde{q}(\hat{x})v=0,

\|q(x)-a(x)\|=\left\|\tilde{q}(x)v\right\|=\left|\tilde{q}(x)\right|\cdot\|v\|=\left|\tilde{q}(x)\right|.

Since
0\leq l(x)\leq w(x)
on
X
, we have

l(x)\leq\|q(x)-a(x)\|\leq w(x).

The above
q(x)
satisfies (
3.7
).

Beyond the above, we consider the case that
the infinity constraining set is given
by a parameterized ellipsoid

U(x)\,=\,\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\,(u-a(x))^{T}(D(x)^{T}D(x))^{-1}(u-a(x))\leq 1\big\}.

Here,
a(x)
is the center vector, and
D(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}

is a polynomial matrix that is nonsingular on
X
.
We can choose the polynomial extension as

(4.4)

q(x)=a(x)+D(x)^{T}D(\hat{x})^{-T}(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x})).

One can see that

q(\hat{x})=a(\hat{x})+D(\hat{x})^{T}D(\hat{x})^{-T}(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))=\hat{u}.

Moreover, we have
q(x)\in U(x)
for all
x
, since

\begin{array}[]{rl}&(q(x)-a(x))^{T}(D(x)^{T}D(x))^{-1}(q-a(x))\\
=&(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))^{T}D(\hat{x})^{-1}D(\hat{x})^{-T}(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))\\
=&(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))^{T}(D(\hat{x})^{T}D(\hat{x}))^{-1}(\hat{u}-a(\hat{x}))\\
\leq&1.\end{array}

The last inequality is due to that
\hat{u}\in U(\hat{x})
.
The
q(x)
as in (
4.4
)
is a satisfactory polynomial extension.

In the following, we propose a numerical method for
obtaining polynomial extensions.
Recall that the infinity constraining set
U(x)
is given by

h_{i}(x,u)\,=\,0\,(i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}),\quad h_{i}(x,u)\,\geq\,0\,(i\in\mathcal{J}_{2}).

When
h_{i}(x,u)
is nonlinear in
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{p})
,
how to obtain polynomial extensions
is generally highly challenging.
This question is mostly open, to the best of the authors’s knowledge.
Here, we consider a typical case that
all
h_{i}(x,u)
are linear in
u

(but they can be nonlinear in
x
), i.e.,

h_{i}(x,u)\,=\,h_{i,0}(x)+(h_{i,1}(x))^{T}u,

where each
h_{i,1}
is a
p
-dimensional polynomial vector.
Recall that the constraining set
X
is given as in (
1.2
).
As required in Definition
3.2

for given
(\hat{x},\hat{u})
,
the polynomial extension vector
q=(\phi_{1}(x),\ldots,\phi_{p}(x))

can be obtained by solving the following feasibility problem
(for a given degree
k
)

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(\phi_{1}(\hat{x}),\ldots,\phi_{p}(\hat{x}))=\hat{u},\\
h_{i}(x,q(x))\in\mathrm{Ideal}[c_{eq}]_{2k}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\in\mathcal{I}_{2},\\
h_{i}(x,q(x))\in\mathrm{Ideal}[c_{eq}]_{2k}+\mathrm{QM}[c_{in}]_{2k}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i\in\mathcal{J}_{2},\\
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p}\in\mathbb{R}[x]_{\ell}.\end{array}\right.

In the above,
c_{eq}=(c_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}

is the tuple of equality constraining polynomials for
X

and
c_{in}=(c_{j})_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{1}}

is the one for inequality constraints, and

\ell\,=\,\min\limits_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\cup\mathcal{J}_{2}}\,\{2k-\deg(h_{i,1})\}.

The problem (
4.5
)
is a linear convex conic system about coefficients of
q
.
The second constraint implies
h_{i}(x,q(x))=0

for all
x\in X
and
i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
,
and the third one implies
h_{i}(x,q(x))\geq 0

for all
x\in X
and
i\in\mathcal{J}_{2}
.
Therefore, if
q
satisfies (
4.5
),
then
q
is a polynomial extension of
\hat{u}
at
\hat{x}
.
We would like to remark that (
4.5
)
can be solved as a semidefinite program, for the given degree
k
.
Its size can be estimated as follows.
For each of
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p}
, there are

\binom{n+\ell}{\ell}
coefficients.
For each
i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\cup\mathcal{J}_{2}
, the membership there
poses
\binom{n+2k}{2k}
linear equations about coefficients of
q
.
Each polynomial in
\mathrm{QM}[c_{in}]_{2k}

can be represented some psd matrix variables whose lengths
are at most
\binom{n+k}{k}
.
We refer to
[
40
, Sec. 2.5]
for more details
about semidefinite programs arising from (
4.5
).
The feasibility system (
4.5
)
can be implemented in
YALMIP

[
28
]

and solved by
SeDuMi

[
47
]
.

Example 4.1
.

Consider
X,U(x)
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{x}=(1/2,1),\ \hat{u}=(1/3,2/3),\,c(x)=(x_{2}-x_{1},x_{1},2-x_{1}-x_{2})\geq 0,

\displaystyle h(x,u)=(x_{2}-x_{1}u_{1}-x_{2}u_{2},u_{1}+x_{1}u_{2}-x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}+3u_{1}-u_{2},u_{1}-x^{2}_{1}u_{2})\geq 0.

A polynomial extension
q=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})
satisfying (
4.5
) is

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=0.2705-0.4539x_{1}-0.0395x_{2}+0.2690x^{2}_{1}-0.4113x_{1}x_{2}+0.0564x^{2}_{2},

\displaystyle\phi_{2}=0.7102-0.3609x_{1}+0.1369x_{2}.

One can check that
q(\hat{x})=\hat{u}
and for every
x\in X
,

h(x,q(x))\,\geq\,(3.9\cdot 10^{-10},1.0\cdot 10^{-8},0.0151,0.0072)\geq 0.

The system (
4.5
) is implemented
in
YALMIP

[
28
]

and solved by
SeDuMi

[
47
]
.
A numerical certificate for the above
q

satisfying (
4.5
) is given in Appendix

.

5.
Convex Infinity Constraints

This section discusses GSIPs that have convex infinity constraints.
Such a GSIP can be solved by
a single polynomial optimization relaxation.

Recall that

.

5.3
) holds.

) with variables

,

u
in

,

Example 6.9
.

Consider the following GSIP example:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\min&-x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}+2x_{2}\\
\mathit{s.t.}&g(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle u_{1}+u_{2}+\frac{x^{2}_{2}}{x_{1}+u_{1}}+\frac{x^{2}_{1}}{x_{2}+u_{2}}-2\\
x_{1}u_{2}+\displaystyle\frac{x^{2}_{2}+u^{2}_{2}}{1+u_{1}}-x^{2}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\geq 0\quad\forall u\in U(x),\\
&x\in X,\\
\end{array}\right.

where the constraining sets are

\begin{array}[]{rcl}X&=&\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:4-x^{T}x\geq 0,\,x_{1}x_{2}\geq\frac{1}{2},\,x_{1}\geq 0\big\},\\
U(x)&=&\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:u_{1}\geq 0,\,u_{2}-x_{2}u_{1}\geq 0,\,2x_{1}-x_{2}u_{1}-u_{2}\geq 0\}.\end{array}

The constraining function
g_{1}(x,u),\,g_{2}(x,u)
are both convex in
u=(u_{1},\,u_{2})

over
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, and the set
U(x)
is convex in
u
.
For any
x\in X
, we have
x>0
, and
\nabla_{u}h\not=0
.
Consider point
\hat{u}=(\frac{x_{1}}{2x_{2}},\,x_{1})
, then

h(x,\hat{u})=(\frac{x_{1}}{2x_{2}},\,\frac{x_{1}}{2},\,\frac{x_{1}}{2})>0
,
so the KKT condition holds for
u\in U(x)
.
The matrix polynomial
T(x,u)
satisfying equation (
5.6
) is:

T(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}2x_{1}-2x_{2}u_{1}&2x_{1}x_{2}-2x_{2}u_{2}&2x_{2}&2x_{2}&2x_{2}\\
-u_{1}&2x_{1}-u_{2}&1&1&1\\
-u_{1}&-u_{2}&1&1&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

for the denominator vector
\phi(x,u)=2x_{1}.

Solving the optimization (
5.8
) with variables
x,z_{1},z_{2}
,
where
z_{i}
served as the parameter
u
in
g_{i}(x,u),\,i=1,2
,
we get the optimizers and optimal values:

\displaystyle x^{*}\approx(1.1348,0.4406),\,z^{*}_{1}\approx(9.8\cdot 10^{-9},0.6941),\,z^{*}_{2}\approx(1.9,2.1)\cdot 10^{-9},

\displaystyle f(x^{*})\approx 1.5160,\,\big(g_{1}(x^{*},z^{*}_{1}),g_{2}(x^{*},z^{*}_{2})\big)\approx(4.9,0.2)\cdot 10^{-7}.

It took around
129
seconds.
This GSIP can also be solved by Algorithm
3.3
.
It terminates within
3
iterations and returns the same results.
Algorithm
3.3
took around
6
seconds.
This is faster than solving (
5.8
),
because (
5.8
) has extra variables
z_{1},z_{2}
,
which made the resulting Moment-SOS relaxations more expensive to solve.

7.
Conclusions and Discussions

This paper proposes a hierarchy of polynomial optimization relaxations
to solve GSIPs. The optimization problems are not assumed to be convex.
When the constraining set
U(x)
depends on
x
,
we use polynomial extensions to construct polynomial optimization relaxations.
We have also demonstrated how to get polynomial extensions for various feasible sets.
Moment-SOS relaxations are applied to solve the polynomial optimization.
The method is given in Algorithm
3.3
.
Its efficiency is demonstrated by extensive examples.
In particular, the SIP can be solved as a special case of the GSIP.
An interesting fact is that the finite convergence
of Algorithm
3.3
is observed numerically
in all our computational examples.

There is much interesting future work to do.
For instance, how do we get polynomial extensions for general set
U(x)
?
How do we efficiently solve the polynomial relaxation
(P_{k})

when there are a lot of constraints?
What are conveniently checkable conditions for
Algorithm
3.3
to have finite convergence?
These questions are mostly open, to the best of the authors’s knowledge.
