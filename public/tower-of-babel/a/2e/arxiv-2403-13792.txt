2.
An overview of the proof

Our approach starts with the naively simple idea that fewer triangles are created if many of the edges added are in few triangles (i.e., have small codegree). However, it turns out to be surprisingly difficult to make this approach rigorous, and so we shall consider (and condition on) a slightly different event.

We now attempt to explain intuitively how and why our proof works. We also introduce notation that will be used throughout the article. Set
N:=\binom{n}{2}
. We shall use the notation
\pm K
to denote an error of up to
|K|
, so that
a=b\pm K
means
a\in[b-K,b+K]
.

Our objective is to prove a lower bound on the probability,

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,<\,(1-\delta)\mu_{n,m}\right)\,,

that
G_{m}
contains many fewer triangles than expected, where
n^{-1}\leqslant\delta\leqslant C^{-1}n^{-3/4}
. We achieve this by defining an event, which is not too rare, and on which the conditional expectation of the triangle count is much smaller. Given an event
E
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,|\,E\right]\,\leqslant\,(1-2\delta)\mu_{n,m}

It follows from Markov’s inequality that

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,\geqslant\,(1-\delta)\mu_{n,m}|E\right)\,\leqslant\,\frac{(1-2\delta)\mu_{n,m}}{(1-\delta)\mu_{n,m}}\,\leqslant\,1-n^{-1}\,.

Considering the complementary event, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,<\,(1-\delta)\mu_{n,m}|E\right)\geqslant n^{-1}
, from which it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(N_{\triangle}(G_{m})\,<\,(1-\delta)\mu_{n,m}\right)\geqslant n^{-1}\mathbb{P}\left(E\right)\,.

In summary, to prove Theorem
1.1
it suffices to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
. There exists a constant
C>0
such that the following holds. Let

be such that

and let

be such that

, then there exists an event

(ii)

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{m})|E\right]\,\leqslant\,(1-2\delta)\mu_{n,m}
.

What kind of event

We may clearly define

Here is a first attempt at defining such an event

However, when calculating the conditional expectation

we must consider not only the triangles with two edges in

and one “new” edge, but also the triangles with two or three “new” edges. The conditional expectations of these latter two turn out to be challenging to calculate.

In fact, the calculations become easier if the graph (in the role of the graph

Definition 2.2

If

3.1.
Sequences of iid random variables

The definition of distance from normal applies even more naturally to random variables. Let
F
be the distribution function of a random variable
X
. Then we may define

d_{t}(X,\Phi)\,:=\,\left|F(t)-\Phi(t)\right|

and

d(X,\Phi)\,:=\,\sup\{d_{t}(\textbf{x},\Phi)\,:\,t\in\mathbb{R}\}\,.\phantom{\big|}

In fact, this is known as the Kolmogorov distance between the distributions.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
k\geqslant 1
. Let
X
be a random variable with
d(X,\Phi)\leqslant\varepsilon
. Let
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})
be a sequence of iid copies of
X
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbf{X}\text{ is }3\varepsilon\text{-far from normal}\right)\,\leqslant\,2\varepsilon^{-1}\,\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}\right)\,.

Proof.

We first prove for a fixed
t\in\mathbb{R}
that

(3.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{t}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,>\,2\varepsilon\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}\right)\,.

We then deduce the lemma by a simple
\varepsilon
-net argument.

Fix
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
p(t):=\mathbb{P}\left(X\leqslant t\right)
. As
d(X,\Phi)\leqslant\varepsilon
, we have
|p(t)-\Phi(t)|\leqslant\varepsilon
.

Let
Y:=|\{i:X_{i}\leqslant t\}|
. Note that
Y\sim\mathbf{Bin}(k,p(t))
and that
\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=kp(t)
, so that
|\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]-k\Phi(t)|\leqslant\varepsilon k
. We now have that

\mathbb{P}\left(d_{t}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,>\,2\varepsilon\right)\,=\,\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-k\Phi(t)|>2\varepsilon k\right)\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]|>\varepsilon k\right)\,.

By applying the Chernoff bound for the lower tail to both
Y
and
k-Y\sim\mathbf{Bin}(k,1-p(t))
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]|>\varepsilon k\right)\,\leqslant\,2\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}\right)\,,

as claimed.

We now proceed to the
\varepsilon
-net argument. For each
j=1,\dots,\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor
, let
t_{j}=\Phi^{-1}(j\varepsilon)
. We also extend the definition to
t_{0}=-\infty
and
t_{\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1}=\infty
. In a slight abuse of notation we extend our definition of
d_{t}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)
to include
d_{-\infty}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)=d_{\infty}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)=0
for any real vector
\mathbf{X}
.

It now follows by an easy monotonicity argument that for
t\in(t_{j-1},t_{j}]
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i\in I}\big(X_{i}\leqslant t\big)\,-\,\Phi(t)\,

\displaystyle\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}_{i\in I}\big(X_{i}\leqslant t_{j}\big)\,-\,\Phi(t_{j-1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\,d_{t_{j}}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,+\,\varepsilon\,.

By an essentially identical argument

\Phi(t)\,-\,\mathbb{P}_{i\in I}\big(X_{i}\leqslant t\big)\,\leqslant\,d_{t_{j-1}}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,+\,\varepsilon\,.

It follows that

(3.3)

d(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,\leqslant\,\max_{j}d_{t_{j}}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,+\,\varepsilon\,.

In other words, if we accept losing an additive term
\varepsilon
, we only need to consider the distance
d_{t}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)
at the finitely many points
t=t_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor
.

We may now complete the proof, as, if
\mathbf{X}
is
3\varepsilon
-far from normal, then by (
3.3
), we have that
d_{t}(\mathbf{X},\Phi)\,>\,2\varepsilon
for some
t\in\{t_{j}:j=1,\dots,\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor\}
. By (
3.2
) and a union bound, this has probability at most

\varepsilon^{-1}\,\cdot\,2\exp\left(\frac{-\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}\right)\,,

as required.
∎

X_{i}

Let

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
u\in G
, let

. Then
S^{p}(u,v|I)

are conditionally independent and identically distributed, given

Proof.

Since each edge of

3.2.
Sampling

Given a vector
\textbf{x}:=(x_{i}:i\in I)
and a subset
J\subseteq I
, we write
\textbf{x}_{J}:=(x_{j}:j\in J)
for the vector restricted to this subset of the indices. The following lemma states that if a vector is far from normal then the sample of it is also likely to be far from normal

5

5

There is nothing special about the normal distribution, but we state the result for normal as this is the case we require. One could also prove stronger bounds of course.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, let
1\leqslant k\leqslant K
and suppose that
\varepsilon^{2}k\geqslant 2
. Let
\textbf{x}:=(x_{1},\dots x_{K})
be a vector which is
2\varepsilon
-far from normal, and let
I\subseteq[K]
be a random subset chosen uniformly from
k
-element subsets of
[K]
. Then

\mathbb{P}\big(\textbf{x}_{I}\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-far from normal}\big)\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{2}\,.

Proof.

Reordering, if necessary, we may assume that
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{K})
is non-decreasing. As
d(\textbf{x},\Phi)\geqslant 2\varepsilon
, it follows that for some
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
d_{t}(\textbf{x},\Phi)\geqslant 2\varepsilon
. Let
\ell
be maximal such that
x_{\ell}\leqslant t
. The condition
d_{t}(\textbf{x},\Phi)\geqslant 2\varepsilon
gives us that

(3.5)

\big|\ell-\Phi(t)K\big|\,>\,2\varepsilon K\,.

We now consider the vector
\textbf{x}_{I}
. Let
Y
be the random variable
|I\cap[\ell]|
. Note that
d_{t}(\textbf{x}_{I},\Phi)=|Y-\Phi(t)k|/k
, and so the vector
\textbf{x}_{I}
is certainly
\varepsilon
-far from normal in the case

(3.6)

|Y-\Phi(t)k|\,\geqslant\,\varepsilon k\,.

We note that
Y
is distributed as a hypergeometric random variable,
Y\sim\textrm{Hyp}(K,\ell,k)
, which has expected value
\mu=\ell k/K
, and so, by (
3.5
), we have
|\mu-\Phi(t)k|\geqslant 2\varepsilon k
. Now, the probability
\textbf{x}_{I}
is
not

\varepsilon
-far from normal is at most

\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-\Phi(t)k|\,<\,\varepsilon k\right)\,\leqslant\,\mathbb{P}\left(|Y-\mu|\,>\,\varepsilon k\right)\,.

For hypergeometric random variables, the variance satisfies
\sigma^{2}\leqslant\mu\leqslant\ell k/K
. And so, it follows from Chebychev’s inequality that this probability is at most

\frac{\ell k}{\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}K}\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}k}\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{2}\,.

∎

In particular, this may be applied to realisations of random vectors
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

Corollary 3.7

Let
\varepsilon>0

1\leqslant k\leqslant K

\varepsilon^{2}k\geqslant 2

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}

\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}\text{ is }2\varepsilon\text{-far from normal})\,\leqslant\,2\,\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X}_{I}\text{ is }\varepsilon\text{-far from normal})\,.

Let

For each

4.
Relating Codegrees and Synergies

As we explained in the overview, our plan is to consider an event which biases our selection towards edges with smaller synergy. The results of this section relate synergies to codegrees, which is vital to our approach. We also prove some properties of the graph
F_{-}
.

More concretely, our event will involve taking
(1+\alpha)/2
proportion of the remaining
m_{1}=m-m_{0}
edges from
F_{-}
rather than its complement
F_{+}:=K_{n}\setminus(G_{0}\cup F_{-})
. One contribution to the final number of triangles are those with two edges in
G_{0}
and one in
G_{1}
. Conditioned on this event this conditional expectation becomes

\sum_{uv\in F_{-}}d_{G_{0}}(u,v)\frac{(1+\alpha)m_{1}}{N-m_{0}}+\sum_{uv\in F_{+}}d_{G_{0}}(u,v)\frac{(1-\alpha)m_{1}}{N-m_{0}}\,.

This clearly motivates are interest in the expression

\sum_{uv\in F_{-}}d_{G_{0}}(u,v)\,-\,\sum_{uv\in F_{+}}d_{G_{0}}(u,v)\,.

We now state the required result on this quantity. Let us fix for the remainder of the article
\varepsilon:=n^{-1/5}

.

There exists

c_{(2,1)}=cp
, and using Lemma
5.2
, we now have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(2,1)}|E_{\alpha}\right]\,&\leqslant\,3p_{0}^{2}p_{1}\binom{n}{3}\,-\,c_{(2,1)}\,\eta\alpha n^{5/2}\,.\end{split}

6.3.
Proof of Lemma
6.3

We now proceed to the third type of triangles,
\triangle_{(1,2)}
. A triangle of type
(1,2)
consists of vertices
u,v,w
, such that
vw
is an edge of
G_{0}
and both
uv
and
uw
are edges of
G_{1}
. So that

\triangle_{(1,2)}\,=\,\sum_{u,v,w}1_{vw\in E(G_{0})}1_{uv,uw\in E(G_{1})}\,.

Let
e(u):=e(G_{0}[V\setminus(N(u)\cup\{u\})])
denote the number of edges of
G_{0}
amongst the non-neighbours of
u
. Each edge of
E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{0})
is in
G_{1}
with probability
r_{1}:=p_{1}/(1-p_{0})
, and each such pair of edges is in
G_{1}
with probability
r_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2})
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}[\triangle_{(1,2)}|G_{0}]\,=\,r_{1}^{2}\sum_{u}e(u)\,\pm\,O(n)\,.

If we condition only on the event
E_{0}
then, as
r_{1}(1-p_{0})=p_{1}
, and using Lemma
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(1,2)}|E_{0}\right]\,

\displaystyle=\,r_{1}^{2}\sum_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[e(u)|E_{0}\right]\,\pm\,O(n)

\displaystyle=\,r_{1}^{2}\sum_{u}\mathbb{E}\left[e(u)\right]\,\pm\,O(n)

\displaystyle=\,3p_{0}p_{1}^{2}\binom{n}{3}\,\pm\,O(n)\,.

What changes when we condition on
E_{\alpha}
? As
E_{\alpha}=E_{0}\cap E(\alpha)
, for a graph
G_{0}
satisfying the conditions of
E_{0}
, the conditioning affects the probabilities that the edges
uv
and
uw
are included in
G_{1}
. As they will now depend on whether
uv
and
uw
belong to
F_{-}
or
F_{+}
. If both belong to
F_{-}
then the probability
r_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2})
is multiplied by a factor of
(1+\alpha)^{2}
. If one is in
F_{-}
and the other in
F_{+}
then the factor is
(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha)
. Finally, if both are in
F_{+}
then the factor is
(1-\alpha)^{2}
.

Let us define
e_{-}(u):=e(G_{0}[N_{F_{-}}(u)])
to be the number of edges of
G_{0}
in the
F_{-}
-neighbourhood of
u
. Respectively
e_{\pm}(u):=e(G_{0}[N_{F_{-}}(u),N_{F_{+}}(u)])
and
e_{+}(u):=e(G_{0}[N_{F_{+}}(u)])
. Note that
e(u)=e_{-}(u)+e_{\pm}(u)+e_{+}(u)
. Using this notation we see that for a fixed graph
G_{0}
satisfying
E_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(1,2)}|E(\alpha),G_{0}\right]\,

\displaystyle=\,r_{1}^{2}\,\sum_{u}\,\Big((1+\alpha)^{2}e_{-}(u)\,+\,(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha)e_{\pm}(u)\,+\,(1-\alpha)^{2}e_{-}(u)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt=\,r_{1}^{2}\sum_{u}e(u)\,+\,2r_{1}^{2}\alpha\sum_{u}\big(e_{-}(u)-e_{+}(u)\big)\,+\,r_{1}^{2}\alpha^{2}\sum_{u}\big(e_{-}(u)+e_{+}(u)-e_{\pm}(u)\big)\,,

up to an
\pm O(n)
error on each line. Rather than conditioning on a fixed graph
G_{0}
we need to condition just that
G_{0}
is in
E_{0}
. Doing so corresponds to taking the conditional expected value. Let
\mu_{-}=\mathbb{E}\left[e_{-}(u)|E_{0}\right]
,
\mu_{+}=\mathbb{E}\left[e_{+}(u)|E_{0}\right]
and
\mu_{\pm}=\mathbb{E}\left[e_{\pm}(u)|E_{0}\right]
, noting of course that these values are the same for all vertices
u
. And so

(6.3)

\mathbb{E}[\triangle_{(1,2)}|E_{\alpha}]\,=\,p_{0}p_{1}^{2}\binom{n}{3}\,+\,2r_{1}^{2}\alpha\big(\mu_{-}\,-\,\mu_{+}\big)n\,+\,r_{1}^{2}\alpha^{2}\big(\mu_{-}\,+\,\mu_{+}\,-\,\mu_{\pm}\big)n\,\pm\,O(n)\,.

In what follows, in the
O(\cdot)
notation the implicit constant may only depend on the constant
\lambda
of Proposition
2.1
and is not allowed to depend on other quantities such as our choices of
\eta
or
C^{\prime}
. In this context, we can state that
r_{1}=O(\eta)
.

As
r_{1}=O(\eta)
and
\alpha\leqslant n^{-1/4}
(see (
6.1
)), we can see from (
6.3
) that it will suffice to prove that
\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}=O(n^{3/2})
and
\mu_{-}+\mu_{+}-\mu_{\pm}=O(n^{8/5})
. In fact, we can do much better than that: we prove that for
every

G_{0}
satisfying
E_{0}
we have

(6.4)

\sum_{u\in V(G_{0})}\big(e_{-}(u)\,-\,e_{+}(u)\big)\,=\,O(n^{5/2})\,,

and

(6.5)

e_{-}(u)\,+\,e_{+}(u)\,-\,e_{\pm}(u)\,=\,O(n^{8/5})\phantom{\Big|}

for all vertices
u\in V(G_{0})
. Doing so will complete the proof of Lemma
6.3
.

Let us first introduce some notation that encapsulates the idea of
conditional deviation
. Let
\bar{d}(u):=(n-d(u)-1)/2
, half the number of non-edges of
u
in
G_{0}
. Let
D_{+}(u):=d_{F_{+}}(u)-\bar{d}(u)
and
D_{-}(u):=d_{F_{-}}(u)-\bar{d}(u)
be the deviations of these degrees. Using the handshaking lemma twice, we see that

\sum_{u\in V(G_{0})}D_{+}(u)\,=\,2e(F_{+})\,-\,(N-m_{0})\,=\,0\,.

Similarly
\sum_{u}D_{-}(u)=0
.

Proof of (
6.4
)
Let
G_{0}
be a graph satisfying
E_{0}
, that is, satisfying (
P1
)- (
P6
). By (
5.2
) (which is a consequence of (
P2
)) that
|D_{+}(u)|,|D_{-}(u)|\leqslant 2\varepsilon n
for all
u\in V(G_{0})
. The other source of “error” in
e_{-}(u)
and
e_{+}(u)
comes from that deviation of the edge count of
G_{0}
in the respective neighbourhoods
N_{F-}(u)
and
N_{F+}(u)
. These deviations are bounded by
n^{3/2}
by (
P5
). For example, as
d_{F-}(u)=\bar{d}(u)+D_{-}(u)
, we have

\displaystyle e_{-}(u)\,=\,e(G_{0}[N_{F-}(u)])\,

\displaystyle=\,\binom{d_{F_{-}}(u)}{2}\,\pm\,n^{3/2}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\big(\bar{d}(u)+D_{-}(u)\big)^{2}}{2}\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})

(6.6)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\bar{d}(u)^{2}}{2}\,+\,\bar{d}(u)D_{-}(u)\,+\,\frac{D_{-}(u)^{2}}{2}\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})\,.

By a similar argument

(6.7)

e_{+}(u)\,=\,\frac{\bar{d}(u)^{2}}{2}\,+\,\bar{d}(u)D_{+}(u)\,+\,\frac{D_{+}(u)^{2}}{2}\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})\,.

As
D_{+}(u)=-D_{-}(u)
, we have that
D_{+}(u)^{2}=D_{-}(u)^{2}
, and so these terms cancel, and we see that

e_{-}(u)\,-\,e_{+}(u)\,=\,\bar{d}(u)\big(D_{-}(u)-D_{+}(u)\big)\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})\,=\,2\bar{d}(u)D_{-}(u)\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})\,.

We now sum this expression over
u\in V(G_{0})
. We shall write
\bar{d}(u)=(n-d_{G_{0}}(u)-1)/2=(1-p_{0})(n-1)/2-D_{G_{0}}(u)/2
. We obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(G_{0})}\big(e_{-}(u)\,-\,e_{+}(u)\big)\,

\displaystyle=\,2\sum_{u}\bar{d}(u)D_{-}(u)\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})

\displaystyle\,=\,(1-p_{0})n\sum_{u}D_{-}(u)\,-\,\sum_{u}D_{G_{0}}(u)D_{-}(u)\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})

\displaystyle=\,O(n^{5/2})\,.\phantom{\big|}

The final bound uses that
\sum_{u}D_{-}(u)=0
and the estimates
|D_{G_{0}}(u)|\leqslant n^{1/2}\log{n}
by (
P3
) and
|D_{-}(u)|\leqslant 2\varepsilon n
, which we stated at the start of the proof of (
6.4
).

Proof of (
6.5
)
We begin by considering
e_{\pm}(u)
. Using (
P5
), and the fact that
D_{+}(u)=-D_{-}(u)
, we have

\displaystyle e_{\pm}(u)\,

\displaystyle=\,e(G_{0}[N_{F-}(u),N_{F_{+}}(u)])\phantom{\big|}

\displaystyle=\,d_{F_{-}}(u)d_{F_{+}}(u)\,\pm\,n^{3/2}\phantom{\Big|}

\displaystyle=\,\big(\bar{d}(u)+D_{-}(u)\big)\big(\bar{d}(u)+D_{+}(u)\big)\,\pm\,n^{3/2}\phantom{\Big|}

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\,\bar{d}(u)^{2}\,-\,D_{-}(u)^{2}\,\pm\,n^{3/2}\,.\phantom{\big|}

From (
6.6
), (
6.7
) and (
6.8
), we see that

e_{-}(u)\,+\,e_{+}(u)\,-\,e_{\pm}(u)\,=\,2D_{-}(u)^{2}\,\pm\,O(n^{3/2})

We now again use that
|D_{-}(u)|\leqslant 2\varepsilon n
, and
\varepsilon=n^{-1/5}
. It follows immediately that
e_{-}(u)\,+\,e_{+}(u)\,-\,e_{\pm}(u)=O(n^{8/5})
, as required.

6.4.
Proof of Lemma
6.4

We now proceed to the final type of triangle,
\triangle_{(0,3)}
. A triangle of type
(0,3)
consists of a set of three vertices
u,v,w
, such that all three pairs
uv,uw,vw
appear as edges of
G_{1}
. So that

\triangle_{(0,3)}\,=\,\sum_{\{u,v,w\}}1_{uv,uw,vw\in E(G_{1})}\,.

Each edge of
E(K_{n})\setminus E(G_{0})
is in
G_{1}
with probability
r_{1}:=p_{1}/(1-p_{0})
, and each such trio of edges is in
G_{1}
with probability
r_{1}^{3}+O(n^{-2})
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}[\triangle_{(0,3)}|G_{0}]\,=\,r_{1}^{3}N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n)\,.

If we condition only on the event
E_{0}
then, as
r_{1}(1-p_{0})=p_{1}
, and using Lemma
5.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(0,3)}|E_{0}\right]\,

\displaystyle=\,r_{1}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})|E_{0}\right]\,\pm\,O(n)

\displaystyle=\,r_{1}^{3}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\right]\,\pm\,O(n)

\displaystyle=\,p_{1}^{3}\binom{n}{3}\,\pm\,O(n)\,.

What changes when we condition on
E_{\alpha}
? As
E_{\alpha}=E_{0}\cap E(\alpha)
, for a graph
G_{0}
satisfying the conditions of
E_{0}
, the conditioning affects the probabilities that the edges
uv,uw
and
vw
are included in
G_{1}
. As they will now depend on whether these pairs belong to
F_{-}
or
F_{+}
. If all three belong to
F_{-}
then the probability
r_{1}^{2}+O(n^{-2})
is multiplied by a factor of
(1+\alpha)^{3}
. More generally, the factor is
(1+\alpha)^{2}(1-\alpha)
if just two are in
F_{-}
, it is
(1+\alpha)(1-\alpha)^{2}
if just one is in
F_{-}
, and is
(1-\alpha)^{3}
if all three are in
F_{+}
. For
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, let

denote the number of triangles in
G_{0}^{c}
, which have precisely
i
edges in
F_{-}
and
3-i
edges in
F_{+}
.

By the above discussion, we see that

(6.9)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(0,3)}|E(\alpha),G_{0}\right]\,&=\,p_{1}^{3}\binom{n}{3}\,\pm\,O(n)\\
&+\alpha r_{1}^{3}\big(3T_{3}\,+\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,-\,3T_{0}\big)\\
&+\,\alpha^{2}r_{1}^{3}\big(3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}\big)\\
&+\,\alpha^{3}r_{1}^{3}\big(T_{3}-T_{2}+T_{1}-T_{0}\big)\,.\end{split}

We shall prove for every choice of
G_{0}
which satisfies
E_{0}
(that is, satisfies (
P1
)- (

)) we have

(6.10)

3T_{3}\,+\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,-\,3T_{0}\,=\,O(n^{5/2})

and

(6.11)

3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}\,=\,\frac{(1-p_{0})^{3}n^{3}}{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{13/5})\,.

As in the previous subsection the implicit constants in the
O(\cdot)
notation may only depend on the constant
\lambda
and not on
\eta
or
C^{\prime}
.

Let us now observe that Lemma
6.4
would follow. Recall that
r_{1}=O(\eta)
. If (
6.10
) holds then the
second line of (
6.9
) is of the form
O(\alpha\eta^{3}n^{5/2})
. Note also that the final line is trivially at most
O(\alpha^{3}\eta^{3}n^{3})
, which is also
O(\alpha\eta^{3}n^{5/2})
, as
\alpha\leqslant n^{-1/4}
by (
6.1
). It follows that, if
G_{0}
satisfies (
6.10
) and (

), then

\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(0,3)}|E(\alpha),G_{0}\right]\,=\,p_{1}^{3}\binom{n}{3}\,\pm\,O\left(\alpha^{3}\eta^{3}\left[\frac{(1-p_{0})^{3}n^{3}}{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\right]\right)\,\pm\,O(\alpha\eta^{3}n^{5/2})\,.

The conditional expectation given the event
E_{\alpha}
is obtained by averaging this over
G_{0}
satisfying
E_{0}
, as we commented above in (
5.1
). By Lemma
5.2
, and the fact that
\mathbb{E}\left[N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\right]=(1-p_{0})^{3}\binom{n}{3}=(1-p_{0})^{3}n^{3}/6\pm n^{2}
, the first error term is of the form

. We obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[\triangle_{(0,3)}|E_{\alpha}\right]\,=\,p_{1}^{3}\binom{n}{3}\,\pm\,O(\alpha\eta^{3}n^{5/2})\,,

as required. Thus, to prove Lemma
6.4
it remains only to prove that (
6.10
) and (

) hold for all graphs
G_{0}
in
E_{0}
(that is, satisfying (
P1
)- (

)).

Proof of (
6.10
)
We begin by noting that
3T_{3}\,+\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,-\,3T_{0}
is the same value as that obtained by summing over triangles of
G_{0}^{c}
the number of edges in
F_{-}
minus the number of edges in
F_{+}
. Let
K_{3}(G)
denote the set of triangles (a triangle being considered a set of three edges) in a graph
G
. We have

\begin{split}3T_{3}\,+\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,-\,3T_{0}\,&=\,\sum_{A\subset K_{3}(G_{0}^{c})}|A\cap F_{-}|-|A\cap F_{+}|\\
&=\,\sum_{uw\not\in G_{0}}d_{G_{0}^{c}}(u,w)\big(1_{uw\in F_{-}}\,-\,1_{uw\in F_{+}}\big)\\
&=\,\sum_{uw\in F_{-}}D_{G_{0}^{c}}(u,w)\,-\,\sum_{uw\in F_{+}}D_{G_{0}^{c}}(u,w)\\
&\leqslant\,\left((N-m_{0})\sum_{uw\in G_{0}^{c}}D_{G_{0}^{c}}(u,w)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,\\
&\leqslant C_{D}^{1/2}n^{5/2},\phantom{\big|}\end{split}

where we used that
|F_{-}|=|F_{+}|
for the third equality, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the first inequality and the fact that
G_{0}
satisfies (
P1
) for the final inequality.

Proof of (
6.11
)

We may relate
3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}
to the number of monochromatic triangles in a two colouring of the edges of
G_{0}^{c}
. Indeed, if edges of
F_{-}
and
F_{+}
are coloured red and blue respectively, and
N_{MC}
denotes the number of monochromatic triangles, then we have

(6.12)

3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}\,=\,4N_{MC}\,-\,N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,.

We now state a Goodman type result for monocromatic triangles in an arbitrary coloured graph. The proof is essentially identical to that of Goodman’s formula, but we include it for completeness.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
G
be a graph and suppose its edges are red/blue coloured. Let
N_{MC}
be the number of monocromatic triangles in this colouring and let
N_{R}(v)
and
N_{B}(v)
denote the red and blue neighbourhoods of
v
, respectively. Then

2N_{MC}\,=\,\sum_{v\in V(G)}e\big(G[N_{R}(v)]\big)+\sum_{v\in V(G)}e\big(G[N_{B}(v)]\big)\,-\,N_{\triangle}(G).

Proof.

Let’s count how much each type of triangle contributes to the RHS. A red triangle contributes
3
to the first sum,
0
to the second and
-1
to the third one, giving a total of
2
. Similarly, a blue triangle contributes
0
,
3
and
-1
, giving a total of
2
. Now, let
uvw
be a non-monocramatic triangle in
G
. Without loss of generality suppose that
uv
and
uw
are red and that
vw
is blue. Then, this triangle adds
1
(as
vw
is an edge of
G[N_{R}(u)]
) to the first sum, and
0
and
-1
to the other two sums. This gives a total contribution of
0
. Therefore, the only triangles contributing to the sum are monocromatic and each one is counted twice, as claimed.
∎

From the lemma and (
6.12
), we see that
3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}
is precisely

2\sum_{u}e\big(G_{0}^{c}[N_{F_{-}}(u)]\big)\,+\,2\sum_{u}e\big(G_{0}^{c}[N_{F+}(u)]\big)\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,.

We now use (
P5
) to control the number of edges in the neighbourhoods, and that
2\binom{d}{2}=d^{2}+O(n)
for any
d\leqslant n
. Doing so, and using the notation
q_{0}:=1-p_{0}
, we see that
3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}
is given by

q_{0}\sum_{u}d_{F_{-}}(u)^{2}\,+\,q_{0}\sum_{u}d_{F+}(u)^{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})

We may now write
d_{F_{-}}(u)=\bar{d}(u)+D_{-}(u)
, as in the previous subsection. Our expression for
3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}
becomes

q_{0}\sum_{u}\big(\bar{d}(u)+D_{-}(u)\big)^{2}\,+\,q_{0}\sum_{u}\big(\bar{d}(u)+D_{+}(u)\big)^{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})\,.

As
D_{+}(u)=-D_{-}(u)
, the linear term will cancel, leaving

\displaystyle q_{0}

\displaystyle\sum_{u}\big(\bar{d}(u)^{2}\,+\,D_{-}(u)^{2}\big)\,+\,q_{0}\sum_{u}\big(\bar{d}(u)^{2}\,+\,D_{+}(u)^{2}\big)\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})

\displaystyle=\,2q_{0}\sum_{u}\bar{d}(u)^{2}\,+\,2q_{0}\sum_{u}D_{-}(u)^{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})

We shall now write
\bar{d}(u)=(n-d_{G_{0}}(u)-1)/2=q_{0}(n-1)/2-D_{G_{0}}(u)/2
. As the linear term in
D_{G_{0}}(u)
cancels, our expression for
3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}
becomes

\frac{q_{0}^{3}n^{3}}{2}\,+\,\frac{q_{0}}{2}\sum_{u}D_{G_{0}}(u)^{2}\,+\,2q_{0}\sum_{u}D_{-}(u)^{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{5/2})\,.

Finally, using that
|D_{-}(u)|\leqslant 2\varepsilon n=O(n^{4/5})
due to (
P2
) (see (
5.2
)) and (
P3
) to control to control
|D_{G_{0}}(u)|
, we see that

3T_{3}\,-\,T_{2}\,-\,T_{1}\,+\,3T_{0}\,=\,\frac{q_{0}^{3}n^{3}}{2}\,-\,3N_{\triangle}(G_{0}^{c})\,\pm\,O(n^{13/5})\,,

which completes the proof of (
6.11
).

Appendix A
Standard Deviations

During Section
3
we defined the following parameter

\sigma_{p}^{2}\,:=\,\textup{Var}\big(S_{G_{p}}^{p}(u,w)\mid uw\not\in E(G_{p})\big).

We deduce its value in the following claim.

Claim A.1
.

\sigma_{p}^{2}=p^{2}(1-p)^{2}(n-2).

Proof.

First, recall that
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{G_{p}}^{p}(u,w)\mid uw\not\in E(G_{p})\right]=0
and hence
\sigma_{p}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[S_{G_{p}}^{p}(u,w)^{2}\mid uw\not\in E(G_{p})]
. As
S_{G_{p}}^{p}(u,w)
can be written as
\sum_{v\in V\setminus\{u,w\}}Y_{v}
where
Y_{v}
are iid random variables which take values

\begin{matrix}(1-p)^{2}\qquad&\text{with probability}\qquad&p^{2}\\
p(1-p)\qquad&&2p(1-p)\\
p^{2}\qquad&&(1-p)^{2}.\end{matrix}

Using independence,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[S_{G_{p}}^{p}(u,w)^{2}]\,=\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{v\in V\setminus\{u,w\}}Y_{v}\right)^{2}\right]\,&=\,\sum_{v\in V\setminus\{u,w\}}\mathbb{E}\big[Y_{v}^{2}]\\
&=\,p^{2}(1-p)^{2}(n-2),\phantom{big|}\end{split}

as claimed.
∎

Appendix B
Hypergeometrics

The following lemma gives a lower bound for the tail of the hypergeometric distribution in the case that exactly half of the population count as “successes”. We remark that now that we are in the appendix we use
p
for the density of a random set (and not
m/N
, as in the rest of the article).

Lemma B.1
.

Let
p,\alpha>0
and let
M\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
(1+\alpha)pM
is an integer. Let
A
be a uniformly random subset of
2pM
elements of
[2M]
. Then

\mathbb{P}\left(|A\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,\geqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-p}{1-p}\alpha^{2}M\,-\,\frac{p-2p^{2}+2p^{3}}{(1-p)^{2}}\alpha^{3}M\,\pm\,O(\log{M})\right)\,.

Remark
.

In particular, if
\alpha^{3}M\geqslant\log{M}
, then this bound is of the form
\exp(-p\alpha^{2}M/(1-p)\,+\,O(\alpha^{3}M))
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that the probability in question is simply

\mathbb{P}\left(|A\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,=\,\frac{\binom{M}{(1+\alpha)pM}\binom{M}{(1-\alpha)pM}}{\binom{2M}{2pM}}\,.

We now use Stirling’s approximation
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
, which gives, in this context, that

\binom{n}{an}\,=\,n^{O(1)}\frac{n^{n}}{(an)^{an}(n(1-a))^{n(1-a)}}\,=\,\frac{n^{O(1)}}{a^{an}(1-a)^{(1-a)n}}\,.\phantom{\Bigg|}

And so the probability is given by

\displaystyle\frac{M^{O(1)}p^{2pM}(1-p)^{2(1-p)M}}{((1+\alpha)p)^{(1+\alpha)pM}((1-\alpha)p)^{(1-\alpha)pM}(1-p-p\alpha)^{(1-p-p\alpha)M}(1-p+p\alpha)^{(1-p+p\alpha)M}}

We note that the
p
terms cancel. If we also divide top and bottom by
(1-p)^{2(1-p)M}
we obtain

M^{O(1)}(1+\alpha)^{-(1+\alpha)pM}(1-\alpha)^{-(1-\alpha)pM}\left(1-\frac{p\alpha}{(1-p)}\right)^{-(1-p-p\alpha)M}\left(1+\frac{p\alpha}{(1-p)}\right)^{-(1-p+p\alpha)M}

which, by the difference of two squares
(1-\alpha^{2})=(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)
, becomes

M^{O(1)}(1-\alpha^{2})^{-(1-\alpha)pM}(1+\alpha)^{-2\alpha pM}\left(1-\frac{p^{2}\alpha^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}}\right)^{-(1-p-p\alpha)M}\left(1+\frac{p\alpha}{(1-p)}\right)^{-2\alpha pM}\,.

As
1+\alpha\leqslant e^{\alpha}
and
1-\alpha\leqslant e^{-\alpha}
, it follows that the probability is at least

\exp\left(\alpha^{2}M\left[p-2p+\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)}-\frac{2p^{2}}{(1-p)}\right]\,-\,\alpha^{3}M\left[p+\frac{p^{3}}{(1-p)^{2}}\right]\,\pm\,\log{M}\right)

This simplifies to

\exp\left(-\alpha^{2}M\left[p+\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)}\right]\,-\,\alpha^{3}M\left[p+\frac{p^{3}}{(1-p)^{2}}\right]\,\pm\,\log{M}\right)

which is equal to the desired expression.
∎

We shall only require this much weaker result, which we state as a corollary.

Corollary B.2
.

Given
\lambda>0
, for all
p\leqslant 1-\lambda
the following holds. Let
X
be distributed as the hypergeometric random variable
\textrm{Hyper}(M,M/2,pM)
and let
M^{-1/2}\log{M}\ll\alpha\ll 1
. Then, for all sufficiently large
M
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(X=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,\geqslant\,\exp(-\lambda^{-1}\alpha^{2}M)\,.

Proof.

The random variable
X
is distributed as
|A\cap[M]|
, the random variable considered in Lemma
B.1
. It follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(X=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,\geqslant\,\exp\left(\frac{-p}{1-p}\alpha^{2}M\,-\,\frac{p-2p^{2}+2p^{3}}{(1-p)^{2}}\alpha^{3}M\,\pm\,O(\log{M})\right)\,.

By the conditions on
\alpha
both the second and third terms are
o(\alpha^{2}M)
. As the
p/(1-p)<\lambda^{-1}
, the claimed bound holds for all sufficiently large
M
.
∎

We remark that an alternative proof of Lemma
B.1
, that would be even more precise, could be obtained by comparison with the conditioned Binomial distribution. Let
A_{p}
be the binomial random subset of
[2M]
in which each element is included independently with probability
p
. One may observe that

\mathbb{P}\left(|A\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,=\,\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\,\mid\,|A_{p}|=2pM\right)\,.

And so this probability is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM,|A_{p}|=2pM\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}|=2pM\right)}

\displaystyle=\,\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}|=2pM\,\mid\,|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}|=2pM\right)}\phantom{Bigg|}

\displaystyle=\,M^{O(1)}\,\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1-\alpha)pM\right)\mathbb{P}\left(|A_{p}\cap[M]|=(1+\alpha)pM\right)\,.\phantom{Bigg|}

One may then understand this probability in terms of the known tail bounds on the binomial distribution, such as that stated as Theorem 1.13 in
[
14
]
, which was adapated from
[
3
]
.
