The notion of centers of homogeneous polynomials was introduced in the 1970s by Harrison in his seminal work

of the algebraic theory of higher degree forms. The centers of homogeneous polynomials were rediscovered independently by several other authors

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall Harrison’s theory of centers and its application to direct sum decompositions of homogeneous polynomials. Then we apply the center theory to prove our main results in Section 3.

2.
Harrison centers of homogeneous polynomials

Let
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbbm{k}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a homogeneous polynomial, also called form, of degree
d.
Harrison
[
5
]
defined the center of
f=f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
as

Z(f):=\{X\in\mathbbm{k}^{n\times n}\mid HX=X^{T}H\},

where
H=(\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}})_{1\leq i,\ j\leq n}
is the hessian matrix.

A homogeneous polynomial
f
is said to be nondegenerate, if no variable can be removed by an invertible linear change of variables. It is not hard to observe that
f
is nondegenerate if and only if the first-order differentials
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}
are linearly independent. To avoid triviality, usually it is enough to consider nondegenerate homogeneous polynomials. A homogeneous polynomial
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
is called a direct sum if, after an invertible linear change of variables
y=Px,
it can be written as a sum of
t\geq 2
forms in disjoint sets of variables as follows

f=f_{1}(y_{1},\dots,y_{a_{1}})+f_{2}(y_{a_{1}+1},\dots,y_{a_{2}})+\cdots+f_{t}(y_{a_{t-1}+1},\dots,y_{n}).

If this is not the case, then
f
is said to be indecomposable. On the other extreme, if the
f_{i}
’s are forms in only one variable, then
f
is said to be diagonalizable.

Corresponding to the homogeneous polynomial
f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}),
there is an associated symmetric
d
-linear space as follows. First write
f
in the symmetric way

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},\cdots,i_{d}\leq n}a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{d}}

where the
a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}
’s are symmetric, i.e., they remain unchanged under any permutation of their subscripts. Then take a vector space
V
over
\mathbbm{k}
of dimension
n
with a basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
and define a symmetric
d
-linear function
\Theta\colon V\times\cdots\times V\longrightarrow\mathbbm{k}
by
\Theta(e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{d}})=a_{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}.
The pair
(V,\Theta)
is called the associated symmetric
d
-linear space of
f
under the basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}.
One can recover the polynomial
f
from
(V,\Theta)
as

f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\Theta\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}e_{i},\ \dots,\ \sum_{1\leq i\leq n}x_{i}e_{i}\right).

Clearly, a change of basis for
V
results in a change of variables for
f.

The center can also be equivalently defined in terms of symmetric
d
-linear space
(V,\Theta)
as

Z(V,\Theta):=\{\phi\in\operatorname{End}(V)\mid\Theta(\phi(v_{1}),v_{2},\dots,v_{d})=\Theta(v_{1},\phi(v_{2}),\dots,v_{d}),\ \forall v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{d}\in V\}.

Nonzero subspaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
of
(V,\Theta)
of a symmetric
d
-linear space
(V,\Theta)
are said to be orthogonal, if
\Theta(v_{1},\dots,v_{d})=0
for all
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}\in V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{t}
unless all the
v_{i}
’s are in the same
V_{s}
for some
1\leq s\leq t.
If
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{t}
for
t\geq 2
nonzero orthogonal subspaces, then call
(V,\Theta)
decomposable. Otherwise, call
(V,\Theta)
indecomposable. Clearly, the orthogonal decompositions of
(V,\Theta)
are in bijection with the direct sum decompositions of its associated homogeneous polynomial
f.
Suppose
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{t}
is an orthogonal decomposition. Choose a basis
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{a_{1}}
for
V_{1},
a basis
\epsilon_{a_{1}+1},\dots,\epsilon_{a_{2}}
for
V_{2},
and so on and so forth. Assume
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})P
with
P\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbbm{k}).
Take the change of variables
x=Py,
then we have the direct sum decomposition for the polynomial
f=f_{1}(y_{1},\dots,y_{a_{1}})+f_{2}(y_{a_{1}+1},\dots,y_{a_{2}})+\cdots+f_{t}(y_{a_{t-1}+1},\dots,y_{n}).
In fact,
f_{i}
is the associated polynomials of
(V_{i},\Theta|_{V_{i}}).
More precisely,

(2.1)

f_{i}=\Theta\left(\sum_{a_{i-1}+1\leq i\leq a_{i}}y_{i}\epsilon_{i},\ \dots,\ \sum_{a_{i-1}+1\leq i\leq a_{i}}y_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right).

Conversely, given a direct sum decomposition
f=f_{1}(y_{1},\dots,y_{a_{1}})+f_{2}(y_{a_{1}+1},\dots,y_{a_{2}})+\cdots+f_{t}(y_{a_{t-1}+1},\dots,y_{n})
with change of variables
x=Py,
let
(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})P.
Then let
V_{1}
be the subspace spanned by
\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{a_{1}},

V_{2}
be the subspace spanned by
\epsilon_{a_{1}+1},\dots,\epsilon_{a_{2}},
etc. It is clear that
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{t}
is an orthogonal decomposition.

The following are some useful facts about centers and direct sum decompositions of homogeneous polynomials obtained in
[

]
, see also
[

.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
f\in\mathbbm{k}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}]
is nondegenerate and is of degree
d.
Then

(1)

The center
Z(f)
is a commutative subalgebra of the full matrix algebra
\mathbbm{k}^{n\times n}.

(2)

If
f=f_{1}+f_{2}+\cdots+f_{t}
is a direct sum decomposition, then
Z(f)\cong Z(f_{1})\times Z(f_{2})\times\cdots\times Z(f_{t}).

(3)

There is a one-to-one correspondence between direct sum decompositions of
f
and complete sets of orthogonal idempotents of
Z(f).

(4)

If
f=g_{1}+g_{2}+\cdots+g_{s}
and
f=h_{1}+h_{2}+\cdots+h_{t}
are direct sum decompositions, where the
g_{i}
and
h_{j}
are indecomposable, then
s=t
and after a reordering
g_{i}=h_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq s.

(5)

f
is diagonalizable if and only if
Z(f)\cong\mathbbm{k}\times\mathbbm{k}\times\cdots\times\mathbbm{k}\ (
n
\ copies).

Proof.

For the convenience of the reader, we include a sketchy proof for item (4) which is the key ingredient used to prove our main results. More details can be found in the aforementioned references. Let
(V,\Theta)
be the associated symmetric
d
-linear space of
f.
Suppose
f=g_{1}+g_{2}+\cdots+g_{s}=h_{1}+h_{2}+\cdots+h_{t}
as in the proposition. Then there are two associated complete sets of primitive orthogonal idempotents in
Z(V,\Theta)
corresponding to the given two direct sum decompositions of
f.
However, the center algebra
Z(V,\Theta)
is finite-dimensional and commutative, so it has only one unique complete set of primitive orthogonal idempotents
[
9
]
. Thus for
V,
there is a unique orthogonal decomposition
V=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}
where each
V_{i}
is indecomposable. As (
2.1
), each
g_{i}
or
h_{j}
is the associated polynomial of some
(V_{k},\Theta|_{V_{k}}).
It follows directly that
s=t=r
and after a reordering
g_{i}=h_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq s.

∎

3.
Nonexistence of some products of sums of powers

In this section, we apply Harrison’s center theory, in particular the uniqueness of the direct sum decomposition of indecomposable summands, to prove the nonexistence of some products of sums of powers.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
d>2.
There is an identity of product of sums of powers

(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d})(y_{1}^{d}+y_{2}^{d}+\cdots+y_{n}^{d})=z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n}^{d}

where each
z_{k}
is a linear form in
y
with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}(x)
if and only if
r=1.

Proof.

View both sides of the previous identity as polynomials in
y
with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}(x).
First note that if the
z_{k}
are linearly dependent as linear forms in
y,
then the polynomial
z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n}^{d}
is degenerate in
\mathbbm{k}(x)[y]
as it can be presented by fewer variables, namely a maximally linearly independent subset of the
z_{k}.
Now assume the
z_{k}
are linearly independent. Then
z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n}^{d}
is a direct sum, and obviously each
z_{k}^{d}
is indecomposable. Thus by item (4) of Proposition
2.1
, after a reordering we may assume
(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d})y_{j}^{d}=z_{j}^{d}
for all
1\leq j\leq n.
By assumption
z_{k}\in\mathbbm{k}(x)[y],
hence
z_{k}=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}y_{k}
where
p(x),q(x)\in\mathbbm{k}[x].
It follows that
x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d}=\frac{p(x)^{d}}{q(x)^{d}}.
Since
\mathbbm{k}[x]
is a unique factorization domain, this forces
q(x)|p(x).
Therefore we can assume in the beginning
q(x)=1.
Now we have
x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d}=p(x)^{d}.
This means
p(x)
is a
\mathbbm{k}
-linear form in
x.
Obviously, this happens if and only if
r=1.

∎

Remark 3.2
.

It follows easily by the previous theorem that there are no nontrivial identities like (
1.3
) even if the
z_{k}
are allowed to be chosen from
\mathbbm{k}(x)[y].
Therefore, we confirm a stronger version of Nathason’s conjecture.

By the same idea, Theorem
3.1
can be slightly generalized to the following

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
r>1,\ n>1
and
(n,d)\neq(2,3).
Then there is no identity of the following type

(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d})(y_{1}^{d}+y_{2}^{d}+\cdots+y_{n}^{d})=z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n+1}^{d}

where each
z_{k}
is a linear form in
y
with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}(x).

Proof.

Assume the contrary and we will seek for a contradiction. Just as in the proof of the previous theorem, we may assume without loss of generality that
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n}
are linearly independent and
z_{n+1}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}a_{k}z_{k}
with
a_{k}\in\mathbbm{k}(x).
If some
a_{k}
are zero, say
a_{l}=\cdots=a_{n}=0,
then it is clear that
z_{n}
is an indecomposable summand and so
z_{n}^{d}=(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d})y_{j}^{d}
for some
j.
For the same reason as in Theorem
3.1
, this is impossible. If
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}\neq 0,
then we compute the center of
g=z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n+1}^{d}
and come up with a contradiction. Indeed, let
H
be the hessian matrix of
g,
then
H_{ii}=d(d-1)(z_{i}^{d-2}+a_{i}^{2}z_{n+1}^{d-2})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
and
H_{ij}=d(d-1)a_{i}a_{j}z_{n+1}^{d-2}
for all
i\neq j
. Suppose
X=(x_{ij})\in Z(g).
According to the definition of centers, compare the
ij
-entry and
ji
-entry of
HX
for all
i\neq j,
then we have

z_{i}^{d-2}x_{ij}+z_{n+1}^{d-2}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}a_{i}a_{k}x_{kj}=z_{j}^{d-2}x_{ji}+z_{n+1}^{d-2}\sum_{1\leq k\leq n}a_{j}a_{k}x_{ki}.

It is not hard to observe that if
(n,d)\neq(2,3),
then
z_{i}^{d-2},z_{j}^{d-2},z_{n+1}^{d-2}
are linearly independent. It follows easily that
x_{ij}=0
and
x_{ii}=x_{jj}
for all
i\neq j,
that is,
Z(g)\cong\mathbbm{k}(x).
This is absurd, since the center of
(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d}+\cdots+x_{r}^{d})(y_{1}^{d}+y_{2}^{d}+\cdots+y_{n}^{d})
is isomorphic to
\mathbbm{k}(x)^{n}
by item (5) of Proposition
2.1
. The proof is completed.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If
(n,d)=(2,3),
then
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
are obviously linearly dependent. Thus the previous argument does not work in this situation. For the moment, we do not know if one can deal with this special case by the theory of centers.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose
d>3.
There holds an identity

(3.1)

(x_{1}^{d}+x_{2}^{d})(y_{1}^{d}+y_{2}^{d})=z_{1}^{d}+z_{2}^{d}+\cdots+z_{n}^{d}

where each
z_{k}
is a linear form in
y
with coefficients in
\mathbbm{k}(x)
if and only if
n>3.

Proof.

Consider the special case
r=n=2
of Theorem
3.3
. It follows that the identity (
3.1
) holds only if
n>3.
Conversely, if
n>3,
let
z_{1}=x_{1}y_{1},\ z_{2}=x_{1}y_{2},\ z_{3}=x_{2}y_{1},\ z_{4}=x_{2}y_{2}
and the other
z_{k}=0,
then obviously
3.1
holds.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

This corollary confirms a special case of Becker’s conjecture, namely the case of

and

with
