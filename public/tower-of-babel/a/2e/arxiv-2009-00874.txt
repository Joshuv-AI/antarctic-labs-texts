A simple algorithm for global sensitivity analysis with Shapley effects
Takashi Goda

Abstract
Global sensitivity analysis aims at measuring the relative importance of different variables or groups of variables for the variability of a quantity of interest. Among several sensitivity indices, so-called Shapley effects have recently gained popularity mainly because the Shapley effects for all the individual variables are summed up to the overall variance, which gives a better interpretability than the classical sensitivity indices called main effects and total effects. In this paper, assuming that all the input variables are independent, we introduce a quite simple Monte Carlo algorithm to estimate the Shapley effects for all the individual variables simultaneously, which drastically simplifies the existing algorithms proposed in the literature. We present a short Matlab implementation of our algorithm and show some numerical results. A possible extension to the case where the input variables are dependent is also discussed.



1
Introduction

Global sensitivity analysis provides an indispensable framework in measuring the relative importance of different variables or groups of variables for the variability of a quantity of interest
[
1
,
2
]
, and has long been considered one of the central problems in reliability engineering
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. In particular, since his pioneering work by Sobol’
[
6
,
7
]
, variance-based sensitivity analysis has been studied intensively and applied to a variety of subjects in science and engineering. Two classical but still major sensitivity indices are called
main effect
and
total effect
. The main effect, also called the first-order effect, measures the variance explained only by an input variable or a group of input variables, whereas the total effect is given by the overall variance minus the variance explained only by the complement variables. When looking at these effects for the individual variables, the sum of the main effects is always less than or equal to the overall variance, while the sum of the total effects is always larger than or equal to the overall variance. In this way the classical sensitivity indices have a difficulty in normalization, and may cause some trouble when judging whether one input variable is more important than another.

Recently the connection between variance-based global sensitivity analysis and Shapley value from game theory has been studied by Owen
[
8
]
. The resulting sensitivity index defined for individual variables is called
Shapley effect
. The Shapley effect takes its value between the main effect and the total effect, and importantly, the sum of the Shapley effects over all the individual variables is exactly equal to the overall variance. This normalization property gives us a better interpretability in determining whether one input variable is more important than another. Moreover, the Shapley effect has been proven to remove the conceptual problem inherit to both the main and total effects when the input variables are correlated
[
9
,
10
]
.

In this paper we mainly focus on the case where the input variables are independent and study Monte Carlo algorithms to estimate the Shapley effects efficiently. Building upon the previous works by Song et al.
[
9
]
and Broto et al.
[
11
]
, which themselves build upon the idea from
[
12
]
, in conjunction with the famous pick-freeze scheme
[
7
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, we introduce a quite simple Monte Carlo algorithm to estimate the Shapley effects for all the individual variables simultaneously at the cost of
(d+1)N
, where
d
denotes the number of variables and
N
denotes the sample size. Our algorithm offers the following advantages:

1.

Each Shapley effect is estimated unbiasedly.

2.

The variance of the estimator is also estimated unbiasedly and decays at the canonical
1/N
rate under some assumption.

3.

The above advantages lead to an approximate confidence interval for the Shapley effect without requiring independent Monte Carlo trials or bootstrap resampling.

4.

The sum of the estimates for all the Shapley effects is an unbiased estimator of the overall variance.

We also discuss how to extend our algorithm to the case where the input variables are dependent. Although we are particularly interested in Monte Carlo sampling-based approaches in this paper, note that there are numerous different approaches to carry out a global sensitivity analysis proposed in the literature, such as random balance designs
[
18
]
, polynomial chaos expansion
[
19
]
, fast Fourier transform
[
20
]
, low-rank tensor approximation
[
21
]
and random forests
[
22
]
. We refer the reader to
[
23
]
for a recent review on various estimation algorithms for classical sensitivity indices.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we give an overview of variance-based global sensitivity analysis and introduce several sensitivity indices. In Section
3
we present our simple Monte Carlo algorithm to estimate the Shapley effects for all the input variables simultaneously. We also show some good properties of our algorithm as explained above. Numerical experiments in Section
4
confirm the effectiveness of our algorithm for three test cases. We conclude the paper in Section
5
with discussing two possible extensions of our algorithm, one for an additional cost saving and the other for the case where the input variables are dependent. In
A
, we provide a short Matlab implementation of our algorithm, which we use for one test case.

2
Variance-based sensitivity analysis

2.1
ANOVA decomposition

Let
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{d}=:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector of input random variables. Each variable
x_{j}
follows a probability distribution with density
\rho_{j}
defined over the interval
\Omega_{j}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Throughout this paper except Section
5.2
, we assume that all the random variables are independent with each other. Moreover, for simplicity of notation, we write
[1:d]:=\{1,\ldots,d\}
. For a subset
u\subseteq[1:d]
, we denote the complement of
u
by
-u=[1:d]\setminus u
and denote the cardinality of
u
by
|u|
. For vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\Omega
, we write
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}=(x_{j})_{j\in u}
and
(\boldsymbol{x}_{u},\boldsymbol{y}_{-u})=\boldsymbol{z}
with
z_{j}=x_{j}
if
j\in u
and
z_{j}=y_{j}
otherwise. The Cartesian product
\prod_{j\in u}\Omega_{j}
is denoted by
\Omega_{u}
, and the product of density functions
\prod_{j\in u}\rho_{j}(x_{j})
is denoted by
\rho_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})
with an exception for
u=[1:d]
in which case we simply write
\rho(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Now let
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a function which outputs a quantity of our interest. If the variance of
f
with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
is finite,
f
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{u\subseteq[1:d]}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u}),

(1)

where each summand is recursively defined by

f_{\emptyset}=\int_{\Omega}f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=:\mu

and

f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})=\int_{\Omega_{-u}}f(\boldsymbol{x})\rho_{-u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{-u}-\sum_{v\subsetneq u}f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{v})

for any non-empty subset
u
.
The following lemma shows important properties of this decomposition.

Lemma 1
.

With the notation above, the following holds true.

1.

For any non-empty subset
u
and coordinate
j\in u
, we have

\int_{\Omega_{j}}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})\rho_{j}(x_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x_{j}=0.

2.

For any
u,v\subseteq[1:d]
, we have

\int_{\Omega}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{v})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sigma_{u}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega_{u}}\left(f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})\right)^{2}\rho_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}_{u}&\text{if $u=v$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We refer the reader to
[
24
, Appendix A.3]
for the proof of the special case where
\rho_{j}
is the uniform distribution over the unit interval
[0,1]
, which can be easily generalized to a proof of this lemma.
∎

It follows from the second assertion of Lemma
1
that the variance of
f
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-\mu\right)^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\sum_{\emptyset\neq u\subseteq[1:d]}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})\right)^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\>=\sum_{\emptyset\neq u,v\subseteq[1:d]}\int_{\Omega}f_{u}(\boldsymbol{x}_{u})f_{v}(\boldsymbol{x}_{v})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\sum_{\emptyset\neq u\subseteq[1:d]}\sigma_{u}^{2}.

This way, the
overall variance

\sigma^{2}
is decomposed into
2^{d}-1
terms with each term
\sigma_{u}^{2}
being the variance of a lower-dimensional function
f_{u}
. This is why we call the decomposition (
1
) the
analysis of variance (ANOVA) decomposition
of
f
.

2.2
Sensitivity indices

For a non-empty subset
u\subseteq[1:d]
, the main effect and the total effect for a group of input variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
are defined by

\underline{\tau}^{2}_{u}:=\sum_{\emptyset\neq v\subseteq u}\sigma_{v}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\emptyset\neq v\subseteq[1:d]\\
v\cap u\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\sigma_{v}^{2}=\sigma^{2}-\underline{\tau}^{2}_{-u},

respectively. We see from the definition that the main effect measures the variance explained by the variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
. On the other hand, the total effect takes into account all possible interactions with the variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{u}
, so that we always have
\underline{\tau}^{2}_{u}\leq\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}
. We see from the second equality that the total effect measures the difference between the overall variance and the variance explained by the complement variables
\boldsymbol{x}_{-u}
.

The following identities on these effects are well-known:

\underline{\tau}^{2}_{u}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}f(\boldsymbol{x})f(\boldsymbol{x}_{u},\boldsymbol{y}_{-u})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}-\mu^{2},

and

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u},\boldsymbol{x}_{-u})\right)^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y},

(2)

see, e.g.,
[
24
, Appendix A.6]
. These identities have been used for constructing so-called
pick-freeze
Monte Carlo estimators of the main and total effects, respectively. We refer the reader to
[
7
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
among many others.

Looking at these effects for an individual variable, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma^{2}_{\{j\}}\leq\sigma^{2},

and

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}=\sum_{\emptyset\neq u\subseteq[1:d]}|u|\sigma_{u}^{2}\geq\sigma^{2}.

Therefore, the
rescaled
versions of these effects,
\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}/\sigma^{2}
and
\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}/\sigma^{2}
, are not summed up to 1. This normalization issue can be circumvented by employing a new class of sensitivity indices called Shapley effects
[
8
]
. For an individual variable
x_{j}
, the Shapley effect is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\phi_{j}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{u\subseteq-\{j\}}\binom{d-1}{|u|}^{-1}\left(\underline{\tau}^{2}_{u+j}-\underline{\tau}^{2}_{u}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{u\subseteq-\{j\}}\binom{d-1}{|u|}^{-1}\left(\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u+j}-\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}\right),

(3)

where we write
\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\emptyset}=\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\emptyset}=0
. Here the second equality is proven in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
. As pointed out in
[
8
]
,

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}=\sigma^{2}

holds, so that the Shapley effect enables an easy interpretation when measuring the relative importance of individual variables. Moreover, it is known from
[
8
, Theorem 1]
that

\phi_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\emptyset\neq u\subseteq[1:d]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\frac{\sigma_{u}^{2}}{|u|},

which leads to an inequality
\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}\leq\phi_{j}\leq\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}
.

3
Monte Carlo estimator for Shapley effect

3.1
Algorithm

In this section, we introduce a simple unbiased Monte Carlo algorithm to estimate the Shapley effects
\phi_{j}
for all
1\leq j\leq d
. Although we shall use the second expression of
\phi_{j}
in (
3
), it is also possible to construct a similar Monte Carlo algorithm based on the first expression. For a start, let us consider a single value
\phi_{j}
. Let
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
be a uniformly distributed discrete random variable, and given
\ell
, let
\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)\subseteq-\{j\}
be a uniformly distributed random subset with fixed cardinality
\ell
. Then it follows from (
3
) and (
2
) that the Shapley effect for a variable
x_{j}
is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq-\{j\}\\
|u|=\ell\end{subarray}}\left(\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u+j}-\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\left[\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j}-\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\left[\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y},

wherein we have defined

\displaystyle g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j)})\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)},\boldsymbol{x}_{-\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-\frac{f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)},\boldsymbol{x}_{-\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)})+f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j)})}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)},\boldsymbol{x}_{-\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)+j)})\right).

This representation naturally leads to the following unbiased Monte Carlo estimator of
\phi_{j}
:

\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{\mathcal{U}^{(n)}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}),

where
\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}^{(N)}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(N)}
are i.i.d. samples generated from the density
\rho
,
\ell^{(1)},\ldots,\ell^{(N)}
are i.i.d. samples of
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
, and for each
n
,
\mathcal{U}^{(n)}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})
denotes a random sample of
\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})
conditional on
\ell^{(n)}
.

The key difference from the algorithms proposed in
[
9
]
and
[
11
]
is that we directly apply the pick-freeze scheme to estimate the difference
\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u+j}-\overline{\tau}^{2}_{u}
instead of estimating two terms independently. This idea not only gives a tight coupling in estimating the difference, but also exploits a sensible recycling idea from
[
9
]
well further. In fact, if each of
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
is estimated independently, we need
3dN
total function evaluations since computing each of
g_{\mathcal{U}^{(n)}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)})
requires evaluating the function
f
three times, whereas we can reduce this cost to
(d+1)N
through the recycling technique by estimating all
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}
simultaneously.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(d))
denote a random permutation of
[1:d]
. For a fixed
1\leq j\leq d
, generating
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)
randomly is equivalent to finding
\ell
such that
\pi(\ell+1)=j
and set
\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)=\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}
. Note that we set
\mathcal{U}_{j}(0)
to the empty set. Because of this equivalence, by writing
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}=\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}
with
\ell
satisfying
\pi(\ell+1)=j
, we can rewrite our estimator as

\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}),

for i.i.d. permutations
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
of
[1:d]
. The recycling technique from
[
9
]
shares the same
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
for computing all of
\widehat{\phi}_{1,N},\ldots,\widehat{\phi}_{d,N}
, which still ensures the unbiaseness of estimators. After some rearrangements, our algorithm can be summarized as follows.

Algorithm 1

(Monte Carlo estimation of Shapley effects for all input variables)
.

Let
d
be the number of input variables and
N
be the sample size. Initialize
\widehat{\phi}_{1,N}=\cdots=\widehat{\phi}_{d,N}=0
. For
1\leq n\leq N
, do the following:

1.

Generate
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}\in\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}
randomly.

2.

Let
\ell=1
and compute
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathcal{F}^{-}_{n}=f(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)})
.

3.

(a)

Compute
\mathcal{F}^{+}_{n}=f(\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}},\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}_{-\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}})
.

(b)

Update

\widehat{\phi}_{\pi(\ell),N}=\widehat{\phi}_{\pi(\ell),N}+\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}-\frac{\mathcal{F}^{-}_{n}+\mathcal{F}^{+}_{n}}{2}\right)\left(\mathcal{F}^{-}_{n}-\mathcal{F}^{+}_{n}\right).

(c)

Let
\ell=\ell+1
. If
\ell\leq d
, let
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{n}=\mathcal{F}^{+}_{n}
and go to Step (a).

It is obvious that we evaluate function values only
d+1
times for each
n
, leading to the total computational cost of
(d+1)N
.

Remark 1
.

In Algorithm
1
, instead of generating
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
randomly for all
n
, one can use Latin hypercube sampling or (randomized) quasi-Monte Carlo sampling. However, improving the convergence of the estimated Shapley effects seems quite hard. The reason is that we also need to generate a discrete object
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}
and the integrand
g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{j}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
changes depending on the samples
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
, so that the standard theory of quasi-Monte Carlo sampling which requires a smoothness of integrands does not apply. This problem does not occur for the main and total effects.

3.2
Some properties

Because of the linearity of expectation, the first property on the unbiasedness of our Monte Carlo estimator, mentioned in Section
1
, can be easily shown as follows:

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}^{(n)}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\rho}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]=\phi_{j}.

This leads to the fourth property

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\phi_{j}=\sigma^{2}.

We now show the second property of the estimator. The third property immediately follows from the central limit theorem. Let us make the second property more explicit.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that

M_{4}(f):=\int_{\Omega}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{4}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}<\infty.

Then the following holds true.

1.

The variance of
\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}
is finite and decays at the rate of
1/N
for all
j
.

2.

The variance of
\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}
is estimated unbiasedly by

\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(g_{\mathcal{U}^{(n)}_{j}(\ell^{(n)})}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)})-\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

Since the second assertion is well-known
[
24
, Chapter 2]
, we only give a proof for the first assertion. Because of the independence between different samples, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[\widehat{\phi}_{j,N}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{V}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]}{N},

where the variance on the right-hand side is taken with respect to
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\rho
,
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
and
\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)\subseteq-\{j\}
. Thus it suffices to prove that
\mathbb{V}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]
is finite under the assumption
M_{4}(f)<\infty
. In fact, applying Jensen’s inequality twice, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left[g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\ell}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}\left[\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\left(g_{\mathcal{U}_{j}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right)^{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq-\{j\}\\
|u|=\ell\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(u+j)})\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u},\boldsymbol{x}_{-u})\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq-\{j\}\\
|u|=\ell\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(u+j)})\right)^{4}+\frac{1}{2}\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u},\boldsymbol{x}_{-u})\right)^{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{d-1}\binom{d-1}{\ell}^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\subseteq-\{j\}\\
|u|=\ell\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\rho(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times\left(8\left(f(\boldsymbol{x})\right)^{4}+4\left(f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u+j},\boldsymbol{x}_{-(u+j)})\right)^{4}+4\left(f(\boldsymbol{y}_{u},\boldsymbol{x}_{-u})\right)^{4}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=16M_{4}(f)<\infty.

Thus we are done.
∎

4
Numerical experiments

We conduct some numerical experiments to confirm the effectiveness of our Shapley effect estimator and compare the result with the
shapleyPermRand
estimator proposed in
[
9
]
, which is implemented in the
sensitivity
package of the

software
[
25

. For the first test case, we consider Ishigami function which is a function of only three variables but is interesting in terms of interaction and non-linearity. For the second test case, we consider Sobol’

.

4.1
Ishigami function

Let us consider the case where
\rho
is the uniform distribution over the domain
\Omega=[-\pi,\pi]^{3}
. Ishigami function is defined by

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\left(1+bx_{3}^{4}\right)\sin x_{1}+a\left(\sin x_{2}\right)^{2},

with some coefficients
a,b>0
. For this function, we see that

\displaystyle\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{1\}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\pi^{4}b}{5}\right)^{2},\quad\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{2\}}=\frac{a^{2}}{8},\quad\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{3\}}=0,

\displaystyle\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{1\}}=\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{1\}}+\frac{8\pi^{8}b^{2}}{225},\quad\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{2\}}=\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{2\}},\quad\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{3\}}=\frac{8\pi^{8}b^{2}}{225},

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{1\}}+\frac{4\pi^{8}b^{2}}{225},\quad\phi_{2}=\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{2\}},\quad\phi_{3}=\frac{4\pi^{8}b^{2}}{225}.

This means that the second variable
x_{2}
does not have any interaction with the other variables and that the third variable
x_{3}
affects the variation of
f
only through an interaction with the first variable
x_{1}
.

In what follows, we choose
a=7
and
b=0.1
. The Shapley effects for all the input variables are estimated according to Algorithm
1
. As a comparison, they are also estimated by the

4.2
Sobol’
g
function

As a high-dimensional example, let us consider the case where
\rho
is the uniform distribution over the domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
for large
d
. With its simplest form, Sobol’
g
function is defined by

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\frac{|4x_{j}-2|+a_{j}}{1+a_{j}},

with non-negative weight parameters
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
. For this function, we can easily obtain

\sigma^{2}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left[1+\frac{1}{3(1+a_{j})^{2}}\right]-1\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{u}^{2}=\prod_{j\in u}\frac{1}{3(1+a_{j})^{2}},

for any non-empty
u\subseteq[1:d]
. Thus, for an individual variable
x_{j}
, the main and total effects are given by

\underline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}=\frac{1}{3(1+a_{j})^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{j\}}=\frac{1}{3(1+a_{j})^{2}}\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq j\end{subarray}}^{d}\left[1+\frac{1}{3(1+a_{\ell})^{2}}\right]

respectively. The Shapley effect is

\phi_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\emptyset\neq u\subseteq[1:d]\\
j\in u\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|u|}\prod_{\ell\in u}\frac{1}{3(1+a_{\ell})^{2}},

which seems hard to simplify further.

5
Possible extensions

Finally we discuss two possible extensions of our Monte Carlo estimator. The first extension allows for an additional cost reduction from
(d+1)N
to
dN+1
or
dN
, which might be useful when
d
is small but
N
is large. The second extension considers the case where the input variables are dependent.

5.1
Additional cost saving

In Algorithm
1
, we generate two independent sequences for
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
, and consider a pairing
(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)})
for
1\leq n\leq N
. Because of the linearity of expectation, however, the unbiasedness of our Monte Carlo estimator still holds even by generating only one random sequence for
\boldsymbol{x}
and then considering a pairing
(\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{x}^{(n+1)})
for
1\leq n\leq N
instead. This strategy is called the
winding star
sampling
[
27
]
and enables to reduce the necessary cost from
(d+1)N
to
dN+1
. The cost of
dN
is possible by setting
\boldsymbol{x}^{(N+1)}=\boldsymbol{x}^{(1)}
, although the algorithm then becomes not extensible in
N
.

The variance of this lower-cost estimator is finite and decays at the
1/N
rate under the same assumption given in Theorem
1
. However, because of the dependence between consecutive samples, an unbiased estimation of the variance of the estimator is not possible in the same way as the second item of Theorem
1
.

5.2
Dependent input case

For
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim\rho
and any subset
u\subseteq[1:d]
, it holds that
(\boldsymbol{x}_{u},\boldsymbol{y}_{-u})\sim\rho
as long as the input variables are independent. This property plays a fundamental role in establishing the simple concatenation approach for two independent samples
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)},\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
in Algorithm
1
. In fact, if the input variables are dependent, we generally have
(\boldsymbol{x}_{u},\boldsymbol{y}_{-u})\not\sim\rho
, which prevents us from extending Algorithm
1
to the dependent input case in a straightforward manner.

In order to adapt Algorithm
1
to the dependent input variables, we need the following modifications:

1.

In the first step, generate only
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}\in\Omega
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(n)}
randomly.

2.

In the third step, generate
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}}
conditional only on
\boldsymbol{x}^{(n)}_{-\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}}
and independently of the existing ones
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1)\}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell-1)\}}
for all
1\leq\ell\leq d
.

This way, we still have an unbiased Monte Carlo estimator for the Shapley effects with the cost of
(d+1)N
. However, the independence between
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell-1)\}}
and
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}}
may lead to a large variance in estimating the difference
\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell)\}}-\overline{\tau}^{2}_{\{\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(\ell-1)\}}
. Further detailed investigation is needed to address this issue.

Appendix A
Matlab implementation

n = 2^14; % sample size
d = 10; % dimension
x = rand(n,d); % Monte Carlo samples for x
y = rand(n,d); % Monte Carlo samples for y
[~, pm] = sort(rand(n,d),2); % random permutation matrix

func = @(x,a)prod((abs(4.*x-2)+a)./(1+a),2); % Sobol g function
a = (0:d-1);

z = x;
fz1 = func(z,a);
fx = fz1;

phi1 = zeros(1,d); % initialization
phi2 = zeros(1,d); % initialization
for j=1:d
ind = bsxfun(@eq,pm(:,j),1:d); % compare j-th column with 1:d
z(ind) = y(ind);
fz2 = func(z,a);
fmarg = ((fx-fz1/2-fz2/2).*(fz1-fz2))’;
phi1 = phi1 + fmarg*ind/n;
phi2 = phi2 + fmarg.^2*ind/n;
fz1 = fz2;
end

s_all = sum(phi1); % variance of function
phi2 = (phi2-phi1.^2)./(n-1); % variance of Shapley estimates

disp(phi1); % Shapley estimates
disp(phi1-1.96*sqrt(phi2)); % lower confidence limit
disp(phi1+1.96*sqrt(phi2)); % upper confidence limit
