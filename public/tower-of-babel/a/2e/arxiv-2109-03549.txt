Rotating Majorana Zero Modes in a disk geometry
Liu Yang, Alessandro Principi, Niels R. Walet

Abstract
We study the manipulation of Majorana zero modes in a thin disk made from a p p -wave superconductor in order to understand their use as a building block for topological quantum computers. We analyze the second-order topological corner modes that arise when an in-plane magnetic field is applied and calculate their dynamical evolution when rotating the magnetic field, with special emphasis on non-adiabatic effects. We characterize the phase transition between high-frequency and near-adiabatic evolution using Floquet analysis. We show that oscillations persist even in the adiabatic phase because of a frequency-independent coupling between zero modes and excited states, which we have quantified numerically and analytically. These results show that controlling the rotation frequency can be a simple method to avoid the non-adiabatic errors originated from this coupling and thus increase the robustness of topological quantum computation.



I
Introduction

Topological quantum computing is a promising approach to quantum information processing since it provides robustness against local perturbations, see for instance
Nayak et al. 2008
;
O’Brien et al. 2018
. One way to implement topological quantum computing is through manipulating Majorana zero modes. Using their non-Abelian statistics, one can use adiabatic exchange of Majorana zero modes to construct topological quantum gates. One of the attractive aspects of topology is the robustness against local perturbations with symmetry protection, and thus if one can produce Majorana zero modes, they may provide a very robust platform for quantum computation
Kitaev 2001
;
Ivanov 2001
;
Aasen et al. 2016
;
O’Brien et al. 2018
.

One particular set of systems of interest consists of time-reversal invariant topological superconductors (TSCs) and superfluids in two and three dimensions. These can host gapless counter-propagating Majorana edge modes
Qi et al. 2009
. One of the candidates for the realization of
p
-wave TSCs are doped Bi
2
Se
3
compounds
Zhang et al. 2009a
;
Fu and Berg 2010
;
Liu et al. 2010
;
Hsieh and Fu 2012
;
Venderbos et al. 2016
;
Phong et al. 2017
;
Chirolli et al. 2017
, and several experiments have indeed given evidence for
p
-wave pairing in Cu
x
Bi
2
Se
3

Hor et al. 2010
;
Sasaki et al. 2011
.

Recent studies show that when a perturbation that breaks the symmetry protecting the topology is applied, the resulting system can host lower-dimensional topologically protected gap-less states called hinge or corner modes
Schindler et al. 2018
;
Zhu 2018
;
Yan 2019
. Such Majorana corner modes have been considered in various 2D systems
Zhang et al. 2013
;
Zhu 2018
;
Yan et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2018
;
Phong et al. 2017
;
Wu et al. 2020
.

In our work consider a quasi-two-dimensional system consisting of a thin disk of doped Bi
2
Se
3
, described by a model developed by Phong
et al.

Phong et al. 2017
, which can be turned into a second-order TSC. In that approach, an in-plane magnetic field breaks the time-reversal symmetry and stabilizes Majorana zero modes on the edge of the system. Adiabatically rotating the orientation of the magnetic field results in the movement of Majorana zero modes along the edge of the disk. This fact can be used to define a protocol for braiding Majorana zero modes of multiple disks, clearly showing the advantage of working with a disk geometry
Park and Recher 2015
;
Park et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Zhang et al. 2020b
.

Realistic braiding for topological quantum computation cannot be perfectly adiabatic. Thus, it is necessary to understand the nonadiabatic corrections that affect the movement of the Majorana zero modes
Cheng et al. 2011
;
Scheurer and Shnirman 2013
;
Karzig et al. 2013
;
Amorim et al. 2015
;
Karzig et al. 2015
;
Conlon et al. 2019
. In this paper, we will analyze in detail the evolution of Majorana states in the model considered in Ref.
Phong et al. 2017
. As discussed in section
II
, we derive a new effective one-dimensional description of the edge states, which allows us to analyze the behavior of the Majorana zero modes in detail. Numerical results for a fixed orientation of the magnetic field are shown in Sec.
III
. We then turn our attention to the dynamical evolution of zero modes for a rotating field as a function of frequency in Sec.
IV
. We concentrate on a detailed analysis of the overlap between the final state and the initial Majorana state in a one-cycle rotation. Through Floquet analysis of the evolution operator
Shirley 1965
;
Barone et al. 1977
, we find that, at the transition point between the high-frequency sudden phase and the low-frequency near-adiabatic phase, the quasi-energy spectral gap closes.
It is shown that the approach to the adiabatic limit is not uniform, but we find pronounced oscillations of the zero mode probability amplitude, which can be described by a frequency-independent tunneling in a co-rotating frame. This oscillatory behavior is problematic, since it makes it harder to control quasi-particle poisoning. In Sec.
V
, we calculate the oscillation frequency using perturbation theory. We gain a better analytic understanding of the source of these problems. Furthermore, we identify field-rotation frequencies that are best for high-fidelity braiding of Majorana zero modes. Finally, we draw some conclusions in Sec.
VI
.

We can then express the Bogoliubov–de Gennes Hamiltonian in polar coordinates as

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{r})=\bar{\epsilon}_{F}\left(\frac{1}{k_{F}^{2}r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{1}{k_{F}^{2}r^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}-1\right)\tau_{z}\otimes s_{z}

\displaystyle+\frac{\bar{\epsilon}_{F}\sqrt{2\gamma}}{k_{F}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{0}&{-e^{\mathrm{i}\theta}(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta})}\\

act in Nambu space, i.e., on the particle- and hole components of the wave function, and

are the spin matrices. The parameters

is the difference between the Fermi energy and band gap of the original three-dimensional insulator (

), while the Fermi wavenumber

\Delta_{\text{exp}}^{2}/(2\bar{\epsilon}_{F}^{2})

The spectrum of 2D

In such systems, the topological phase of the bulk is characterized in terms of the first Chern number, while its sign determines the propagation direction (chirality) of edge states

;
Stone and Roy 2004
;
Read and Green 2000
;
Qi et al. 2009
;
Phong et al. 2017
;
Sato and Ando 2017
;

+1
, and the other subspace has Chern number
-1

j=\ell+1/2
(
\ell

L_{z}=-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\theta}-\tau_{z}/2

L_{z}

J_{z}=\left(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\theta}-\frac{\tau_{z}}{2}\right)\otimes s_{0}\,.

(3)

When

\Psi(r=R,\theta)=0

.

In terms of a dimensionless radius and Fermi momentum,
\rho=r/R
and
\lambda=k_{F}R
, we can approximate the wave function for

,

\displaystyle\Psi_{j}^{\pm}(\rho,\theta)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{f(\rho)}{\sqrt{4\pi}}e^{\mathrm{i}j\theta}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta/2}\\
\mp e^{-\mathrm{i}\theta/2}\end{array}\right)\otimes|\pm\rangle,

\displaystyle f(\rho)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}{\sqrt{\rho}}e^{\lambda\sin{\xi}\rho}\sin{[\lambda\cos{\xi}(1-\rho)]}.

(6)

where we define the two-component spinors
|+\rangle=(1,0)^{T}

|-\rangle=(0,1)^{T}
to correspond to the left-hand and the right-hand chiral state, respectively.
The function

is the radial component of the wave function and is independent of

, and is normalized as

\int_{0}^{1}f^{2}(\rho)\rho\mathrm{d}\rho=1\,.

(7)

Finally, the parameter

6

\xi=\arctan{[\sqrt{2\gamma}/(1-\gamma)]}/2\,.

(8)

The detailed derivation of the approximate wave function is given in Appendix
A
. We can make a rough estimate of the parameters in the wave function, and we see that the radial wavenumber is approximated as

and the radial decay length of the wave function from the boundary to the interior of the disk is
k_{F}\sin{\xi}\approx\Delta_{\text{exp}}/(2k_{F}\bar{\epsilon}_{F})
which should be much smaller than the disk’s radius

for this model to be valid. In fact, the angular momentum of the edge states must be restricted to the region
|j|<\lambda\sqrt{1-\gamma/2}
. For larger

, such states lie outside the superconducting gap and are embedded in the continuum, and are therefore delocalized

. We have checked the validity of this inequality in a tight-binding model, as discussed in Appendix
B
. Note that, as shown in Ref.
Michaeli and Fu 2012
;
Phong et al. 2017
, the scalar disorder does not mix in-gap states.

In the limit of large

, the energy of

\displaystyle E_{j}^{\pm}=\mp\omega_{0}j,

where

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\bar{\epsilon}_{F}\frac{\sqrt{2\gamma}}{\lambda}=\frac{\Delta_{\text{exp}}}{k_{F}R}.

That is to say, the energy of edge states is approximately proportional to their angular momentum quantum number

. In what follows, a chiral edge state with total-angular-momentum quantum number

is denoted as

\displaystyle X=\tau_{0}\otimes s_{z},

Finally, Majorana zero modes are generated by breaking time-reversal symmetry

;
Zhu 2018
. This can be achieved by applying an

The Zeeman Hamiltonian describing the coupling to an external magnetic field is

\displaystyle H_{Z}(\phi)=E_{Z}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&e^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}&0\end{array}\right)\otimes s_{x},

where

While the in-plane Zeeman field breaks the time-reversal-symmetry, the Hamiltonian still retains the particle-hole symmetry and invariance under the product of time-reversal and chirality:

\displaystyle\{\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{Z},\hat{C}\}

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

\displaystyle[\hat{H}+\hat{H}_{Z},\hat{T}\hat{X}]

\displaystyle=0.

(15)

Since the in-plane Zeeman field couples states of opposite spin, it couples an edge state with angular momentum
j
and chirality
\chi

states with the opposite chirality

. This coupling scheme leads to two uncoupled subspaces spanned by two sets of edge states which are related by angular-momentum inversion:

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{1}

\displaystyle=(\ket{j_{\max},-},\ket{j_{\max}-1,+},\ldots,\ket{-j_{\max}+1,-})\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\ket{-j_{\max},-},\ket{-j_{\max}+1,+},\ldots,\ket{j_{\max}-1,-}).

(16)

Here

must be constrained to

Since the edge states are concentrated at the boundary, a complete two-dimensional treatment is unnecessarily complex: we would like to construct a one-dimensional effective Hamiltonian that only involves said edge states. Note that there is only one term in Eq. (
II

which is

\displaystyle H_{\text{edge}}(\theta;\phi)

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\left[-p_{\theta}\sigma_{z}-2\epsilon\sin(\theta-\phi)\sigma_{y}\right].

In the above equation,

is the dimensionless magnitude of the Zeeman field, and this Zeeman term is a Dirac mass term

The reduced wave function can be written as

\displaystyle\psi(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{+}(\theta),\psi^{-}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}^{T},

is half-integer. The two components of this spinor correspond to the angular functions for each of the two chiral blocks
\chi^{\pm}

In this effective model, the three basic symmetry operators of the original 2D model, the chirality operator
\hat{X}
, charge conjugation
\hat{C}
and time reversal

are now represented by the operators

\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle U^{\alpha}(\phi)=\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}p_{\theta}\phi\right)\exp\left(-\mathrm{i}\phi[\alpha h(\theta)-p_{\theta}]\right)\,.

More specifically, we define the overlap between the final state and the initial zero mode

as

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{m}_{{1/2}}|\hat{U}^{\alpha}(2\pi)|\psi^{m}_{{1/2}}\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{m}_{{1}}|\hat{U}^{\alpha}(2\pi)|\psi^{m}_{{2}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\text{Im}\{\mathcal{A}_{0}(\alpha)\},

(39)

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{m}_{2}|\hat{U}^{\alpha}(2\pi)|\psi^{m}_{1}\rangle

and

,

. We show the resulting overlap

Because of the drastic change in behavior of the overlap we observe when moving from the high-frequency region to the low-frequency region, we see that
\alpha=1
, where

, is a potential critical point. To understand this potential phase transition of the overlap

, we now resort to Floquet analysis for the periodically driven topological superconductor and study the spectrum of the one-cycle evolution operator

;
Shirley 1965
;
Bauer et al. 2019

as

\displaystyle h^{\alpha}_{F}(\theta)=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\pi}\ln U^{\alpha}(2\pi)=\alpha h(\theta)-p_{\theta}+\frac{1}{2},

(41)

where the factor

represents the minus sign introduced by the left exponential in Eq. (

.

We calculate the Floquet spectrum through a numerical matrix diagonalization within
\mathbf{J}_{1}
. To study the behavior of the quasi-energies near
\alpha=1

\alpha

to

in Fig.

\alpha=1
and that a new mode appears at fixed quasi-energy
1/2
, and thus phase
\pi
, in the quasi-energy spectrum when
\alpha
exceeds

. From the Floquet perspective, the collapse of the quasi-energy spectrum gives the critical point for the dynamical phase transition related to the evolution of the zero modes. The pi-phase mode in the low-frequency region (large
\alpha
) provides the mechanism by which the zero mode obtains the adiabatic
\pi
phase shift for a
2\pi
rotation. The transition point at
\alpha=1
gives the upper bound of the frequency by which we can preserve the Majorana zero modes under rotation as
\omega_{0}/\hbar=\Delta_{\text{exp}}/(\hbar k_{F}R)
(
\omega_{0}
is defined in Eq. (
10
)).

By application of the angle translation operator
\exp{i\jth\phi}
to the wave function, which takes its argument
\theta
to
\theta+\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi(\theta+\phi(t),t)}{\partial t}=\omega\left[h_{F}^{\alpha}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\right]\psi(\theta+\phi(t),t)

\displaystyle=[-p_{\theta}\left(\omega I+\omega_{0}\sigma_{z}\right)-2\omega_{0}\epsilon\sin{\theta}\sigma_{y}]\psi(\theta+\phi(t),t).

(42)

This equation shows that the Hamiltonian in the co-moving frame is
\omega[h_{F}^{\alpha}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}]
. Thus, the Floquet pi-mode of
h_{F}
is the zero mode of this Hamiltonian. For small
\omega
, the solutions to the domain-wall model contain soliton states made up of two counter-propagating chiral states, with the soliton located at the points where the Dirac mass term changes sign. However, when
\omega
exceeds
\omega_{0}
, both left-hand and right-hand chiral states all propagate in the same direction, and the domain-wall bound state disappears. In fact, a similar dynamic model has been used to describe moving domain wall solitons in an
infinite line

;
Karzig et al. 2013
;
Conlon et al. 2019
. In Ref.
Karzig et al. 2013
, the authors studied the exact spectrum of the boosted model through a global Lorentz transformation in a the flat metric. Unfortunately, the same global transformation fails in our reduced 1D model with circular geometry. Thus, we resort to a Floquet analysis. Note that our analysis can be applied not only to intercalated Bi
2
Se
3
but also to generic Dirac-type models in a disc geometry.

V
Non-adiabatic corrections to tunnelling in the low-frequency region

In this section, we use second-order perturbation theory
Mostafazadeh 1997
;
Frasca 1998
to interpret the frequency-dependence of the oscillations of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
in the low-frequency (large
\alpha
) regime and show that they can be related to non-adiabatic tunneling in the co-rotating frame.

To approach this problem, we work in the
k
-representation as set out in Eqs. (
III
-
37
) and work in a co-moving reference frame, Eq. (
42
). As before, we simplify the algebra by transforming the evolution operator by the operator
Q
, Eq. (
III
) so that the result can be expressed in terms of harmonic oscillator creation and annihilation operators. Using time-dependent perturbation theory, we expand the transformed evolution operator to the second order in
1/\alpha
and find

\displaystyle Q^{\dagger}\exp{-\mathrm{i}\phi[\alpha h(k)-\jth(k)]}Q

\displaystyle\approx\widetilde{U}_{\phi}+\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{\phi}\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}\widetilde{U}_{\phi-\phi_{1}}\sqrt{\epsilon}(a+a^{\dagger})\widetilde{U}_{\phi_{1}}

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\phi}\mathrm{d}\phi_{1}\int_{0}^{\phi_{1}}\mathrm{d}\phi_{2}\widetilde{U}_{\phi-\phi_{1}}\epsilon(a+a^{\dagger})\widetilde{U}_{\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}}(a+a^{\dagger})\widetilde{U}_{\phi_{2}},

(43)

where

\displaystyle\quad\widetilde{U}_{\phi}=\exp{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\phi h_{\text{eff}}(k)}.

(44)

Operating with the above effective evolution operator on the initial zero mode
\psi_{0}
, we have:

\displaystyle\exp{-\mathrm{i}\phi[\alpha h(\theta)-\jth]}\psi_{0}

\displaystyle\approx\sum\limits_{n=-1}^{1}a_{n}(\phi)\psi_{n},

(45)

\displaystyle a_{0}(\phi)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sin{^2(\alpha\epsilon_1\phi/2)}}{2\alpha^{2}},

(46)

\displaystyle a_{1}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-e^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\epsilon_{1}\phi}}{2\sqrt{2}\alpha}=a_{-1}^{\ast}(\phi).

(47)

In the above equations,
\epsilon_{1}=\sqrt{4\epsilon}
is the energy gap between the zero mode and the lowest positive energy state. Since the transition probabilities obey
|a_{0}(\phi)|^{2}\approx 1-(|a_{1}(\phi)|^{2}+|a_{-1}(\phi)|^{2})
, the non-adiabatic tunneling in the low-frequency regime mostly happens between
\psi_{0}
, and
\psi_{\pm 1}
as defined in Eq. (
34
). Using the result Eq. (
45
), we can obtain an effective expression of the overlap
\mathcal{A}_{0}
for a full one-cycle rotation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}=-a_{0}(2\pi)=-1+\frac{\sin{^2(\alpha\epsilon_1\pi)}}{2\alpha^{2}}.

(48)

This equation accurately describes the behavior of the frequency-dependent oscillation in the overlap
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\alpha)
. The minus sign originates from the anti-periodic boundary conditions on the wave function. We compare the results of a matrix diagonalization and the effective theory in Fig.
4
. We can see that the result obtained with the two methods are very close. In the inset of Fig.
4
, we show the highest and lowest points of the curve
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. The deviation between exact-diagonalization and effective results in Fig.
4
is about
0.1\%
.

Figure 4:
A comparison between the exact diagonalization results and the effective results for

. The blue line is the effective result while the orange dashed-line is the matrix diagonalization result. The insert shows the local maximums in the main figure, which are high-fidelity points with low error.

From Eq. (
48
), we can see that the non-adiabatic error can be almost zero when the rotation period of the magnetic field is an integer multiple of the frequency-independent period
2\pi/(\sqrt{4\epsilon}\omega_{0})
.

This result can be understood as follows. In the co-rotating frame, the time-dependent evolution is governed by Eq. (
42
). The term
-p_{\theta}
in
h_{F}
can make the initial zero mode tunnel to other eigenstates of the equilibrium Hamiltonian. Also, the transition amplitude between the initial zero mode and the final state is equal to
1-\omega^{2}\sin{^2}\left(\sqrt{4\epsilon}\omega_{0}t/2\right)/(2\omega_{0}^{2})
, and vanishes when the
t
is an integer multiple of the tunneling period, i.e., in the perturbative and adiabatic regime (in the case with
\epsilon=5
, the region starts at about
\alpha>4
), at frequencies

\omega_{\nu}(\Delta\phi)=\frac{\sqrt{4\epsilon}\omega_{0}\Delta\phi}{2\pi\nu},

(49)

with
\nu
an integer,
the final state after one full rotation of the magnetic field has overlap
-1
with the initial state. For a
2\pi
rotation (
\Delta\phi=2\pi
), the integer
\nu
should be larger than
18
within the regime
\alpha>4
. Since
\nu
is large, the difference of the two nearest-neighbor high-fidelity frequencies can be approximated as
\omega_{\nu}(2\pi)-\omega_{\nu+1}(2\pi)\approx\sqrt{4\epsilon}\omega_{0}/\nu^{2}
.

VI
Parameter Estimation and Conclusions

In order to see how realistic our theoretical approach is, we need to estimate the parameters of the model:
From Refs.
Phong et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2009b
;
Hor et al. 2010
;
Sasaki et al. 2011
;
Wray et al. 2011
;
Sandilands et al. 2014
, we estimate
\Delta_{\exp}=0.6\text{meV}
,
k_{F}=0.1\,\text{\AA}^{-1}
. For a device size
R=10\mu\text{m}
, we determine the dimensionless parameters as
\lambda=10^{4}
,
\gamma=\Delta_{\exp}^{2}/(2\bar{\epsilon}_{F}^{2})=4.5\times 10^{-6}
, and
\omega_{c}=\omega_{0}/\hbar\approx 91.2\,\text{MHz}
. To obtain a zero mode, one needs
\epsilon>2
, which in turn implies that the in-plane magnetic field should be larger than
B_{0}=4\omega_{0}/\mu_{e}\approx 4\,\text{mT}
. For
\epsilon=5
as used in the main text, the corresponding magnetic field is about
10\,\text{mT}
. In this case, when the rotation frequency exceeds
\omega_{c}=91.2\,\text{MHz}
, Majorana pi-modes appear and the region where we can perform quasi-adiabatic 2
\pi
rotations is
\alpha>4
, corresponding to
\omega<\omega_{c}/4\approx 22.8\,\text{MHz}
. Since the difference between the two nearest-neighbor high-fidelity frequencies is about
407/\nu^{2}
MHz and
\nu
is larger than 18, we require the frequency width of the rotating magnetic field smaller than
0.315
MHz.

In conclusion, we have made progress on two issues: Firstly, we show through a rigorous process how to reduce a 2D Hamiltonian of a second-order
p
-wave superconductor with disc geometry to a 1D Hamiltonian for edge space and build an effective model to give the approximated analytical solution, which improves on the results in Ref.
Phong et al. 2017
, and also allows us to study the evolution in detail; Secondly, we show that the separation between diabatic and adiabatic motion of rotating Majorana zero-modes in the system exhibits an interesting dynamical phase transition at
\alpha=1
. Beyond this transition point, we have been able to give a detailed numerical and analytical analysis of the approach to adiabaticity. Thus the effective matrix model studied here, with its simple solution, could be of further interest.

We show that at a set of regularly spaced rotation frequencies, we have substantially higher fidelity; by choosing to work at one of these optimal frequencies, one can dramatically reduce quasi-particle poisoning and increase the robustness of the translation of the Majorana states, and thus the manipulation of quantum information in such a device. Our analysis ignores other sources of errors, such as those caused by disorders or fluctuations in the magnetic field, etc. These will be the subject of future work.

D.1
Evolution of the Bogoliubov-de-Gennes quasi-particle

The Heisenberg equation for the quasi-particle of our driven TSC in the Fock representation is

\displaystyle\frac{d\gamma_{\text{BdG}}^{\dagger}(t)}{dt}=-\mathrm{i}[\hat{H}_{\text{sc}}(\phi(t)),\gamma_{\text{BdG}}^{\dagger}(t)].

(144)

The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{sc}}(\phi)
is constructed by
\mathcal{H}(\phi)
in Eq. (
113
):

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{sc}}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{\epsilon}_{F}}{2}\mathbf{C}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}(\phi)\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{2}H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}(\phi)c_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\sigma_{1}}^{-\tau_{1}}c_{\mathbf{r}_{2}\sigma_{2}}^{\tau_{2}},

(145)

\displaystyle c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}&\tau=1\\
c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{{\dagger}}&\tau=-1\end{array}\right..

Using the PHS and the hermiticity,
H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}-\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}=H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\ast}=H_{\mathbf{r}_{2}\mathbf{r}_{1}\tau_{2}\tau_{1}}^{\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}}
, and the anti-commutation relation of electrons operators,
\{c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}^{\tau},c_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}^{\tau^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\delta_{-\tau,\tau^{\prime}}
, we obtain the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\langle\mathbf{r}_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{1}|\psi(t)\rangle}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\langle\mathbf{r}_{2},\tau_{2},\sigma_{2}|\psi(t)\rangle,

(148)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial|\psi(t)\rangle}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{r}_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{1}\rangle H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\langle\mathbf{r}_{2},\tau_{2},\sigma_{2}|\psi(t)\rangle.

(149)

If we define

\displaystyle H=|\mathbf{r}_{1},\tau_{1},\sigma_{1}\rangle H_{\mathbf{r}_{1}\mathbf{r}_{2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}}^{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\langle\mathbf{r}_{2},\tau_{2},\sigma_{2}|,

(150)

then we can have the time-dependent Schrodinger equation for the quasi-particle wave function, which is equivalent to the Heisenberg equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{d|\Psi(t)\rangle}{dt}

\displaystyle=H(\phi(t))|\Psi(t)\rangle,

(151)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega(t).

D.2
Derivation of the Evolution operator in both approaches

To derive an analytical expression for the evolution operator, we first rewrite the Zeeman term as

\displaystyle H_{Z}(\phi)

\displaystyle=E_{Z}\mathcal{G}(\phi)\tau_{x}\otimes s_{x}\mathcal{G}^{{\dagger}}(\phi),

(153)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\begin{smallmatrix}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{e^{\mathrm{i}\phi/2}}&{0}\\
{0}&{e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi/2}}\end{array}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\otimes s_{0}.

(154)

Remember that the Hamiltonian for the disk system in long-wave approximation can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)H(\mathbf{r},\phi)\mathcal{G}(\phi)=H(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},0),

(155)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathcal{R}_{\text{2D}}(\phi)\mathbf{r}=r(\cos{(\theta-\phi}),\sin{(\theta-\phi})).

(156)

Here
\mathcal{R}_{\text{2D}}(\phi)
is the operator that implements a two-dimensional clockwise rotation over an angle
\phi
.
We now apply the transformation
\mathcal{G}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)
(
154
) to the wave function to obtain the time-dependent equation in a co-moving framework,

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},t),t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=[H(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},0)-\mathrm{i}\mathcal{G}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)\frac{\partial\mathcal{G}(\phi)}{\partial t}]\Psi^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=[H(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},0)+\frac{\omega(t)}{2}\tau_{z}\otimes s_{0}]\Psi^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t),

(157)

and

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta-\phi,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{{\dagger}}(\phi)\Psi(r,\theta,t).

(158)

We see that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},t),t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta,t)}{\partial t}-\omega(t)\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta,t)}{\partial\theta},

(159)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta^{\prime},t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle[H(r,\theta^{\prime},0)+\frac{\omega(t)}{2}\tau_{z}\otimes s_{0}+\mathrm{i}\omega(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta^{\prime}}]\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta^{\prime},t),

(160)

which can be summarised as

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta,t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=[H(r,\theta,0)-\omega(t)J_{z}(\theta)]\Psi^{\prime}(r,\theta,t).

(161)

In this equation,
J_{z}(\theta)=(-\mathrm{i}\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\tau_{z})\otimes s_{0}
, see Eq.
3
. For a constant rotation frequency
\omega(t)=\omega
,
the operator
H(r,\theta,0)-\omega J_{z}(\theta)
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
161
) is independent on

, so we can directly integrate the equation and obtain the evolution operator

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=\exp{\{-\mathrm{i}t[H(r,\theta,0)-\omega J_z(\theta)]\}}\,.

(162)

Thus,
\Psi(r,\theta,t)
becomes

\displaystyle\Psi(r,\theta,t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}(\phi)\psi^{\prime}(r,\theta-\phi,t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\partial_{\theta}\phi}\mathcal{G}(\phi)U^{\prime}(\phi)\Psi(r,\theta,0).

(163)

Therefore, the one-period evolution operator is:

\displaystyle U_{2\pi}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}2\pi\left[-\alpha h(\mathbf{r})+J_{z}(\theta)-\frac{1}{2}\right]\right\},

(164)

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{r},\phi=0).

(165)

Using the decomposition in Eq. (
A

\displaystyle h_{F}=\alpha h(\mathbf{\theta})-p_{\theta}(\theta)+\frac{1}{2}.

We can also derive the one-dimensional evolution operator through the reduced equation:

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\psi(\theta,t)}{\partial t}

After an unitary transformation
\exp{(ip_\theta\phi)}

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\psi^{\prime}(\theta,\phi)}{\partial\phi}
