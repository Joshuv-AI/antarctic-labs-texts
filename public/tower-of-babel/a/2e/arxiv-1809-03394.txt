The Horn Problem for Real Symmetric and Quaternionic Self-Dual Matrices
Robert COQUEREAUX † and Jean-Bernard ZUBER ‡

Abstract
Horn’s problem, i.e., the study of the eigenvalues of the sum C = A + B C=A+B of two matrices, given the spectrum of A A and of B B , is re-examined, comparing the case of real symmetric, complex Hermitian and self-dual quaternionic 3 × 3 3\times 3 matrices. In particular, what can be said on the probability distribution function (PDF) of the eigenvalues of C C if A A and B B are independently and uniformly distributed on their orbit under the action of, respectively, the orthogonal, unitary and symplectic group? While the two latter cases (Hermitian and quaternionic) may be studied by use of explicit formulae for the relevant orbital integrals, the case of real symmetric matrices is much harder. It is also quite intriguing, since numerical experiments reveal the occurrence of singularities where the PDF of the eigenvalues diverges. Here we show that the computation of the PDF of the symmetric functions of the eigenvalues for traceless 3 × 3 3\times 3 matrices may be carried out in terms of algebraic functions – roots of quartic polynomials – and their integrals. The computation is carried out in detail in a particular case, and reproduces the expected singular patterns. The divergences are of logarithmic or inverse power type. We also relate this PDF to the (rescaled) structure constants of zonal polynomials and introduce a zonal analogue of the Weyl SU ⁡ ( n ) {\rm SU}(n) characters.

Introduction

Recall what Horn’s problem is: given two
n\times n
matrices
A
and
B
of given spectrum of eigenvalues, what can be said about the spectrum of their sum
C=A+B
? The problem has been addressed by many authors, see
[
16
]

[
26
]
and by Knutson and Tao
[
27
,
28
,
29

In a recent paper
[
34
]
, a more specific question was considered: given
\alpha=\{\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}\}
and
\beta=\{\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\beta_{n}\}
, take the Hermitian matrices
A
and
B
uniformly and independently distributed on the orbit of
\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)
and
\operatorname{diag}(\beta)
under the action of the
{\rm SU}(n)

\gamma

[
12
,
15

The same question may, however, be raised if instead of Hermitian matrices, one considers other classes of matrices and the action of an appropriate group. The case of skew-symmetric real matrices was considered in
[
34
]
, but more interesting is that of real symmetric matrices and the action of the orthogonal group
{\rm SO}(n)

Let us start with a numerical experiment with specific

, say
\gamma_{1}
and

orbits of real symmetric matrices

orbits of such matrices, regarded as Hermitian, under conjugation by the unitary group

(c)

orbits of such matrices, regarded as quaternionic self-dual (i.e.,

,

Following the nomenclature introduced by Dyson, we label these three cases by the index

The orbital integrals for
\boldsymbol{\underline{\beta}=1,2,4}

Let us consider the
orbital integrals

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{\underline{\beta}}(X,A)=\int_{G_{\underline{\beta}}}{\rm d}\Omega\exp\big[\operatorname{tr}X\Omega A\Omega^{\dagger}\big]

in the following three cases:

1)

\underline{\beta}=1
,
\Omega\in G_{1}:={\rm SO}(n)
,
X
,
A
real symmetric matrices;

2)

\underline{\beta}=2
,
\Omega\in G_{2}:={\rm U}(n)
,
X
,
A
complex Hermitian matrices;

3)

\underline{\beta}=4
,
\Omega\in G_{4}:={\rm USp}(n)
,
X
,
A
real quaternionic self-dual matrices.

In fact each of these integrals depends only on the eigenvalues
\alpha_{i}
, resp.
x_{i}
, of
A
, resp.
X
, and by a small abuse of notations, we write them
{\mathcal{I}}_{\underline{\beta}}(x,\alpha)
in the following.

For
\underline{\beta}=2
, this orbital integral is well known
[
19
,
22
]

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{2}(x,\alpha)=\prod_{p=1}^{n-1}p!\sum_{P\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{\Delta(x)\Delta(\alpha_{P})}{\rm e}^{\sum x_{j}\alpha_{Pj}}=\prod_{p=1}^{n-1}p!\frac{\det{\rm e}^{x_{i}\alpha_{j}}}{\Delta(x)\Delta(\alpha)}.

Here and below,
\Delta
with no subscript is the Vandermonde determinant
\Delta(x)=\prod\limits_{i<j}(x_{i}-x_{j})
.

For
\underline{\beta}=4
and for a given
n
, as shown by
[
3
,
20
]
, we have closed formulae

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{4}(x,\alpha)=\hat{\kappa}_{n}\sum_{P\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{\Delta(x)^{3}\Delta(\alpha_{P})^{3}}{\rm e}^{\sum x_{j}\alpha_{Pj}}f_{n}(\tau(\alpha,P)),

(2.1)

where
f_{n}
a polynomial in the variables

\displaystyle\tau(\alpha,P)=\{\tau_{ij}:=(x_{i}-x_{j})(\alpha_{Pi}-\alpha_{Pj})\,|\,1\leq i<j\leq n\},

(2.2)

and
\hat{\kappa}_{n}
is an
\alpha
- and
x
-independent constant. For example for
n=2
,
\hat{\kappa}_{2}=-12
and
f_{2}(\tau(\alpha,P))=1-\frac{1}{2}\tau_{12}
while for
n=3
,
\hat{\kappa}_{3}=-2\times 3!\times 6!
and

\displaystyle f_{3}(\tau(\alpha,P))=1-\frac{1}{3}(\tau_{12}+\tau_{13}+\tau_{23})+\frac{1}{6}(\tau_{12}\tau_{13}+\tau_{13}\tau_{23}+\tau_{23}\tau_{12})-\frac{1}{12}\tau_{12}\tau_{13}\tau_{23}.

These closed formulae allow an explicit computation of the PDF, see
[
34
]
and below, Section
3
.

In contrast, for
\underline{\beta}=1
, i.e., for real symmetric matrices under the action of the orthogonal group
{\rm SO}(n)
, the best that can be achieved is an expansion in zonal polynomials
[
3
,
14
,
20
,
24
,
32
]

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x,\alpha)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!\prod\limits_{q=0}^{m-1}(1+2q)}\sum_{\kappa\vdash m}c(\kappa)\frac{Z(\kappa)(x)Z(\kappa)(\alpha)}{Z(\kappa)(I)},

(2.3)

where the second sum runs over partitions
\kappa
of
m
with no more than
n
parts, and
c(\kappa)
is a constant which depends on the normalization of the
Z(\kappa)
’s, see Section
6
.
If the zonal polynomials in the above formula are written with the so-called James normalization, one can use the values of
Z(\kappa)(I)
and of the coefficient
c(\kappa)
tabulated by James up to
m=4
, for all
n
(the dimension of
I
) in the Appendix of
[
24
, p. 157]
; one can also use commands contained in the Mathematica package
[
6
]
. The infinite sum in the previous expansion, however, makes the computation of the PDF intractable (as far as we can see), and we will have to follow another route, see below Section
4
.

In Section
6
, however, we return to this formulation in terms of zonal polynomials and show a connection between the PDF and the distribution of (rescaled) “zonal multiplicities” i.e., appropriate structure constants of zonal polynomials.

3
Quaternionic case for
\boldsymbol{n=3}

We start with a warming up exercise: compute the PDF of the eigenvalues
\gamma
for a sum of two self-dual quaternionic matrices that are independently and uniformly distributed on their orbit under the action of the unitary symplectic group. The computation of the PDF of the
\gamma
’s follows the same lines as that in the Hermitian case. We will therefore be a bit sketchy in its derivation, referring the reader to
[

Up to an overall factor, this PDF is given by the integral of three orbital integrals of the type
{\mathcal{I}}_{4}
in (
2.1

\displaystyle{\tt p}(\gamma|\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\kappa_{n}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{N}}\Delta(\gamma)^{4}\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}{\rm d}x_{i}\Delta(x)^{4}{\mathcal{I}}_{4}(x,\mathrm{i}\alpha){\mathcal{I}}_{4}(x,\mathrm{i}\beta){\mathcal{I}}_{4}(x,-\mathrm{i}\gamma),

\kappa_{n}=\frac{(2\pi)^{(N-n)/2}}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}((2j)!/2)}

N
variables to the
n
eigenvalues. Thus

\displaystyle{\tt p}(\gamma|\alpha,\beta)=\frac{2\kappa_{n}^{2}\hat{\kappa}_{n}^{3}}{(2\pi)^{N}}{(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}}\pi^{n}\delta\bigg(\sum_{k}(\alpha_{k}+\beta_{k}-\gamma_{k})\bigg)\frac{\Delta(\gamma)}{\Delta(\alpha)^{3}\Delta(\beta)^{3}}{\mathcal{J}}_{n},

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}_{n}=\frac{n!}{{\mathrm{i}^{n(n-1)/2}}\pi^{n-1}}\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in S_{n}}\varepsilon_{P}\varepsilon_{P^{\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}^{n-1}u}{\Delta^{\prime 5}(u)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\mathcal{J}}_n =}{}\times\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}u_{j}A_{j}(P,P^{\prime},I)}f_{n}(\tau(\mathrm{i}\alpha,P))f_{n}(\tau(\mathrm{i}\beta,P^{\prime}))f_{n}(\tau(-\mathrm{i}\gamma,I))

(3.2)

with

\displaystyle A_{j}(P,P^{\prime},P^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{j}(\alpha_{P(k)}+\beta_{P^{\prime}(k)}-\gamma_{P^{\prime\prime}(k)}).

(3.3)

The power 5 of

The support of the symmetric functions

For

\displaystyle\det(z\mathbb{I}_{3}-C)=\det\big(z\mathbb{I}_{3}-\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)-{\mathcal{R}}\operatorname{diag}(\beta){\mathcal{R}}^{T}\big)=z^{3}+P({\mathcal{R}})z+Q({\mathcal{R}}).

For given

regarded as a random variable uniformly distributed in SO(3) (in the sense of the Haar measure),

\displaystyle\rho(p,q)=\mathbb{E}\big(\delta(P-p)\delta(Q-q)\big)=\int D{\mathcal{R}}\delta(P({\mathcal{R}})-p)\delta(Q({\mathcal{R}})-q).

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}={\mathcal{R}}_{z}(\phi){\mathcal{R}}_{y}(\theta){\mathcal{R}}_{z}(\psi)

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi\leq 2\pi,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\qquad 0\leq\psi\leq 2\pi

\displaystyle D{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\sin\theta{\rm d}\theta{\rm d}\phi{\rm d}\psi.

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_p(c) =}{}+2xy({\alpha_{1}}-{\alpha_{2}})({\beta_{1}}-{\beta_{2}})\sin\phi\sin\psi\color{red}{c}

\displaystyle\hphantom{P_p(c) =}{}-\big(\alpha_{1}+2{\alpha_{2}}+{\alpha_{1}}x^{2}-{\alpha_{2}}x^{2}\big)\big({\beta_{1}}+2{\beta_{2}}+{\beta_{1}}y^{2}-{\beta_{2}}y^{2}\big){\color{red}{c^{2}}},

(4.4)

\displaystyle Q_{q}(c)=(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2})(\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})+(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2})(2\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})x^{2}

\displaystyle\hphantom{Q_q(c) =}{}+2xy(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\beta_{1}+\beta_{2})\sin\phi\sin\psi\color{red}{c}

\displaystyle\hphantom{Q_q(c) =}{}+\big((\alpha_{1}+2\alpha_{2})(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})(\beta_{1}+2\beta_{2})-(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-\beta_{1})(\beta_{1}+2\beta_{2})x^{2}

(with
\lambda=\mu=(8,8)
).

We conclude that already with
s=8
, the classical limit provided by the “volume” approximates very well the distribution of multiplicities.

7
Conclusion

To summarize:

–

we have reproduced the main features of the Horn problem for symmetric matrices and understood the analytic origin of the singularities, at least for
n=3
, and in detail for the particular case of
A\sim B\sim J_{z}
;

–

we have confirmed, at least in that particular case, that the divergences of the PDF occur on the same locus of non-analyticities as in the Hermitian and quaternionic cases;

–

we have also confirmed numerically the connection between the “volume functions” and the (asymptotic) distribution of multiplicities in the product of zonal/Schur/quaternionic polynomials.

This leaves, however, open several issues and room for further progress:

–

a more synthetic and general discussion of the singularities in the symmetric case would clearly be desirable. Can one understand their origin from a geometric point of view and assert a priori their location and nature without detailed calculations?

–

what happens for higher

and/or for generic
\underline{\beta}
(or

)? The methods developed recently in

should be helpful in that respect.

The points we find most challenging are the following:

–

There is an enhancement of particular eigenvalues in the Horn spectrum of real symmetric matrices,
due to the divergences of the PDF. Is this enhancement observable in some physical process?

–

The discussion of Section

has pointed to an analogue of the volume function for real symmetric or quaternionic matrices: is there an underlying geometric interpretation to this “volume”? is there a geometric object generalizing the polytope

of the Hermitian case, whose volume is computed there? on a representation theoretic side, what is the origin of the enhancement of certain multiplicities?

We leave these questions to the sagacity of our readers …

Appendix A
Analysis of the singularities

In this subsection, we proceed to a detailed – and lengthy – case-by-case analysis of the divergent singularities of
\rho
.

A preliminary observation is that, due to the
u\leftrightarrow z
symmetry of our particular case, the
u<z
and
u>z
sectors will contribute equally:

\displaystyle\int\int{\rm d}u{\rm d}z\delta(R(u,z))(2+u+z)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\int\int_{u<z}{\rm d}u{\rm d}z\delta(R(u,z))(2+u+z)+\int\int_{u>z}{\rm d}u{\rm d}z\delta(R(u,z))(2+u+z)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=2\int\int_{u>z}{\rm d}u{\rm d}z\delta(R(u,z))(2+u+z).

This will be apparent in the following.
As explained in the previous subsection, we proceed heuristically, making appropriate Taylor expansions close to the singularities. We do it in detail in the first case (singularity along the dashed line), and are then more sketchy.

1.

Along the dashed line
p+1-q=0
, the integrand
\varphi
has two non-integrable singularities:

for

and

•

For

remains equal to zero, and we determine the common value of the two roots at that point by plugging a series expansion of the form

\displaystyle u_{1,2}(0)=u_{s}=\frac{p+3}{2}+\frac{5+p}{2(9+p)}\epsilon+\cdots.

•

\displaystyle u_{1,2}(z)=u_{s}+\beta_{1,2}\zeta+\gamma_{1,2}\zeta^{2}+O\big(\zeta^{3}\big),

(resp.
