Abstract
. The algebra of smooth translation-invariant valuations on convex bodies, introduced by S. Alesker in the early 2000s, was in part proved and in part conjectured to satisfy properties formally analogous to those of the cohomology ring of a compact Kähler manifold: Poincaré duality, the hard Lefschetz theorem, and the Hodge–Riemann relations. Our main result establishes the hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge–Riemann relations in full generality. As a consequence, we obtain McMullen’s quadratic inequalities, which are valid for strongly isomorphic polytopes and known to fail in general, for convex bodies with smooth and strictly positively curved boundary. Our proof is based on elliptic operator theory and on perturbation theory applied to unbounded operators on a natural Hilbert space completion of the space of smooth translation-invariant valuations.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Geometric inequalities

An extremely fruitful approach to the classical isoperimetric problem was discovered by Minkowski at the end of the nineteenth century. In its core lies the observation that the isoperimetric inequality can be formulated as an inequality between mixed volumes of convex bodies. This perspective has led to striking generalizations and a vast array of related results, yet it seems far from exhausted. In this respect, we prove here a collection of new inequalities for mixed volumes that in particular broadly generalizes the isoperimetric inequality.

The mixed volume is the polarization of the Lebesgue measure under the Minkowski addition on the space
\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
of convex compact sets in the
n
-dimensional euclidean space, i.e., the (unique) symmetric, Minkowski-multilinear function
V:\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})^{n}\to\RR
such that
V(A,\dots,A)=\vol(A)
. It is non-negative and satisfies for all
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
the fundamental Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality

\displaystyle V(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},\dots,A_{n})^{2}\geq V(A_{1},A_{1},A_{3},\dots,A_{n})V(A_{2},A_{2},A_{3},\dots,A_{n}).

The Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality is of central importance in convex geometry and, in fact, far beyond. It subsumes many important inequalities for convex bodies, such as the Minkowski inequalities and the Brunn–Minkowski inequality. The two original proofs by Alexandrov
[
2
,
3
]
and various subsequent alternative proofs
[
47
,
32
,
38
,
77
,
73
,
26
]
revealed its deep, unexpected connections to different areas, in particular to algebraic and Kähler geometry. Let us also mention the recent breakthrough due to Shenfeld and van Handel
[
71
,
70
]
in the open problem of characterizing the equality cases (see also
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
41
,
40
]
for conjectures and partial results). Last but not least, the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality is intimately related to combinatorics
[
72
,
58
]
; for a broader perspective on this connection see also §
1.2
below.

Alexandrov’s first proof of the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality was based on approximating convex bodies by strongly isomorphic simple polytopes. This approach was later extended in a seminal paper of McMullen
[
61
]
where the following much more general result was proved (see also
[

,

]
):

Theorem 1.1

(McMullen
[
61
]
)
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
,
N\in\NN
,
x_{i}\in\RR
, and let
A_{j}^{i},C_{l}\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
be strongly isomorphic simple polytopes. If

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},C_{0},\dots C_{n-2k},\Cdot[k-1])=0,

then

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{j}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},A_{1}^{j},\dots,A_{k}^{j},C_{1},\dots,C_{n-2k})\geq 0

with equality if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},\Cdot[n-k])=0
.

The two equations in the statement of the theorem are to be understood as between functions on
polytopes,
[k]
stands for plugging in a polytope
k
times. A mixed volume with more than
n
arguments is zero by convention.

The case
k=0
of Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the non-negativity of the mixed volume, which is a well-known, yet non-trivial fact. For
k=1
, the theorem yields the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality. This is less obvious but still easy to see, cf.
[
76
]
or
[
56
, Corollary 8.1]
. Since these two inequalities extend to
all
convex bodies by continuity, it is tempting to expect the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
to hold without any assumption on
A_{j}^{i},C_{l}\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
. However, a naïve approximation argument does not apply to
k\geq 2
and, in fact, a recent counterexample due to van Handel
[
76
]
shows that the statement is
wrong
for general convex bodies. A natural question therefore is whether McMullen’s quadratic inequalities possibly hold for subclasses of convex bodies other than strongly isomorphic simple polytopes.

Inspired by Alexandrov’s second proof of the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality, we answer this question affirmatively by proving an analogue of Theorem
1.1
for sufficiently smooth convex bodies. Namely, denoting the class of convex bodies with smooth and strictly positively curved boundary by
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
, we prove the following theorem.

{MainTheorem}

Let
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
,
N\in\NN
,
x_{i}\in\RR
, and
A_{j}^{i},C_{l}\in\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
. If

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},C_{0},\dots C_{n-2k},\Cdot[k-1])=0,

then

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}x_{i}x_{j}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},A_{1}^{j},\dots,A_{k}^{j},C_{1},\dots,C_{n-2k})\geq 0

with equality if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}V(A_{1}^{i},\dots,A_{k}^{i},\Cdot[n-k])=0
.

The conclusion of Theorem
1.1
in fact holds under the weaker assumption that the support functions of the bodies
A_{j}^{i}\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
are of class
C^{1,1}
, see Corollary
6.17
.

1.2.
Hodge theory for smooth valuations

Although the statement of Theorem
1.1
is purely convex geometric, there is a remarkable algebraic structure hidden in the background. In fact, the inequalities can be, and were originally formulated in
[
61
]
in terms of McMullen’s polytope algebra, more precisely as the Hodge–Riemann relations for the subalgebra
\Pi(P)
generated by polytopes strongly isomorphic to a given simple polytope
P
. For more details about the polytope algebra, the reader is referred to
[
60
,
18
,
27
]
. Let us only mention here that the product in
\Pi(P)
is induced by the Minkowski sum.

An analytic counterpart to McMullens’s combinatorial theory was discovered in the 2000s through the seminal work of Alesker
[
9
,
11
]
. Alesker’s work along with many subsequent developments, in particular the paper
[
20
]
by the first-named author and Fu, revealed that the Minkowski sum, when restricted to convex bodies of class
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
, gives rise to an algebra
\Val^{\infty}
of smooth valuations. The algebra
\Val^{\infty}
exhibits numerous properties analogous to
\Pi(P)
and even more structure, some of which is reviewed in §
1.3
below. It satisfies Poincaré duality which turned out to have striking implications in integral geometry
[
37
,
21
,
22
,
53
,
23
]
. More recently, the investigation of the connection between the algebra of valuations and geometric inequalities was initiated
[
56
,
54
,
55
,
14
]
.

In our paper, we introduce new techniques that allow us to pursue this study far beyond the previous partial results and complete the analogy between
\Pi(P)
and
\Val^{\infty}
. More precisely, we establish new properties of the algebra of smooth valuations that in particular imply Theorem
1.1
.

To formulate our result, recall that a valuation is a function
\phi:\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})\to\CC
satisfying

\phi(K\cup L)=\phi(K)+\phi(L)-\phi(K\cap L)

whenever
K,L,K\cup L\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
. The Banach space of translation-invariant, continuous valuations is denoted by
\Val
. Alesker has discovered a natural dense subspace
\Val^{\infty}\subset\Val
of smooth valuations. We postpone the precise definition to §
2.2
; let us only mention here that the function

\phi(K)=V(A_{1},\dots,A_{k},K[n-k])

is a smooth valuation for any fixed
A_{i}\in\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In fact, it follows from a deep theorem of Alesker
[
9
]
together with a recent observation made independently by Knoerr
[
49
]
and van Handel that such valuations span
\Val^{\infty}
. Equivalently, the same holds for the valuations
\vol(\Cdot+A)
,
A\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
. The space of smooth valuations decomposes as

\displaystyle\Val^{\infty}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\Val_{k}^{\infty},

where
\Val_{k}^{\infty}\subset\Val^{\infty}
is the subspace of
k
-homogeneous valuations. The convolution product defined by

\vol(\Cdot+A_{1})*\vol(\Cdot+A_{2})=\vol(\Cdot+A_{1}+A_{2})

is continuous in the natural Fréchet space topology on
\Val^{\infty}
and turns it into a commutative, associative, graded algebra with Poincaré duality.

For
C\in\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}
we define the map
L_{C}:\Val_{k}^{\infty}\to\Val_{k-1}^{\infty}
by

(L_{C}\phi)(K)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\phi(K+tC).

Equivalently,
L_{C}
is given by the convolution product with
nV(C,\Cdot[n-1])
. For any tuple
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
of such convex bodies we set
L_{\mathbf{C}}=L_{C_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ L_{C_{m}}
which equals, up to a positive constant, the convolution with
V(C_{1},\dots,C_{m},\Cdot[n-m])
. Our main result is:

{MainTheorem}

Let
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
. Fix a tuple
\mathbf{C}_{0}=(C_{0},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
of convex bodies from
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
and denote
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
.

{enuma}

Hard Lefschetz theorem.
The map
L_{\mathbf{C}}:\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}\to\Val_{k}^{\infty}
is an isomorphism of topological vector spaces.

Hodge–Riemann relations.
The sesquilinear form

q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}(\phi)=(-1)^{k}\b{\phi}*L_{\mathbf{C}}\phi

on
\ker L_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}\subset\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
is positive definite.

Theorem
1.2
confirms a conjecture of the second-named author
[
56
, Conjecture D]
and generalizes many important partial results, previously obtained in various works. In the special case when all the bodies
C_{i}
are euclidean balls, the hard Lefschetz theorem was proved in
[
10
,
17
]
and the Hodge–Riemann relations in
[
56
,
55
]
. For arbitrary bodies
C_{i}
, however, Theorem
1.2
was known only for
k\leq 1

[
54
]
. As observed by van Handel, the previous partial results implied in particular the full statement in dimensions
n\leq 4
.

It is a remarkable phenomenon that versions of the hard Lefschetz theorem and Hodge–Riemann relations hold across a broad spectrum of separate theories. Indeed, apart from Kähler and algebraic geometry, where statements of this type acquired their names from, and McMullen’s polytope algebra
\Pi(P)
mentioned above, they have been proved in various different contexts on the frontier between geometry and combinatorics
[
16
,
1
,
33
,
42
,
45
,
25
]
. All these recent developments had striking consequences, in particular to combinatorial inequalities, many of which had been open for decades (see also the ICM addresses of Huh
[
43
,
44
]

1.3.
Our methods

There is a fundamental difference between Theorem
1.2
and the analogous statement in any other context mentioned above. Namely, to the best of our knowledge,
\Val^{\infty}
is the only
infinite-dimensional
algebra where the hard Lefschetz theorem and Hodge–Riemann relations are known to hold. This fact makes the proof somewhat more delicate, in particular, one cannot use the general approach of Cattani
[
30
,
29
]
and deduce the general statement directly from the previously established case of euclidean balls. Moreover, in a finite-dimensional situation, the hard Lefschetz theorem and Hodge–Riemann relations are usually proven simultaneously, using an inductive argument. This general scheme again fails for
\Val^{\infty}
since in particular the injectivity and surjectivity of the Lefschetz map
L_{\mathbf{C}}
are not equivalent.

The infinite-dimensional setting of Theorem
1.2
suggests that one should try to reformulate the statement in a way that is susceptible to the methods of functional analysis, even if it thus becomes formally stronger than the original problem. In this respect, our key observation is that there exist two natural Hilbert space completions
\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
\mathscr{F}_{k}
of
\Val^{\infty}_{k}
. Their existence is less obvious in the language of mixed volumes, but naturally suggests itself when describing smooth valuations in terms of smooth differential forms, see §
4.2
.

We prove the following statement which directly implies the Hodge–Riemann relations (Theorem
1.2
(b)) and thus Theorem
1.1
.

{MainTheorem}

With the notation of Theorem
1.2
, the sesquilinear form
q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}
, densely defined on
\ker L_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}\cap\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}\subset\ker(L_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}\colon\mathscr{E}_{n-k}\to\mathscr{E}_{k-1})
, is closable. Its closure
q^{\mathscr{E}}_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}
is the quadratic form of a unique self-adjoint operator
Q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}^{\mathscr{E}}
on
\ker L_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}
. The operator
Q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}^{\mathscr{E}}
is strictly positive, has discrete spectrum, and its eigenvalues have finite multiplicities.

The basic idea of our proof, which goes back to Hilbert’s proof of the Brunn–Minkowski inequality and was later generalized by Alexandrov in his second proof of the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality, is to continuously deform a given tuple
\mathbf{C}_{0}
of smooth convex bodies into euclidean balls, arguing that positivity of the sesquilinear form
q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}
is preserved during this process.
The perturbation theory of unbounded operators on a Hilbert space, in particular the Kato–Rellich theorem, will provide us with the desired theoretical framework to bring this approach to fruition.

Our strategy is to first establish the hard Lefschetz theorem for

L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\mathscr{E}_{n-k}\to\mathscr{E}_{k}

and to use it to define a family of (unbounded) operators to which perturbation theory can be applied.
Unlike Hilbert and Alexandrov we cannot directly work with a natural elliptic differential operator associated to
q_{\mathbf{C}_{0}}
and yet elliptic operator theory plays an important role in our proof. Elliptic estimates are crucial in the proof of the surjectivity of
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\mathscr{E}_{n-k}\to\mathscr{E}_{k}
and elliptic regularity allows us to obtain the surjectivity of
L_{\mathbf{C}}
also for smooth valuations. An important ingredient, entering our argument at two steps, are the Hodge–Riemann relations in the context of complex linear algebra, a result due to Timorin
[
74
]
and the most elementary incarnation of the Hodge–Riemann relations. We first use it to prove the injectivity of
L_{\mathbf{C}}
and later deduce from it the ellipticity of a particular second order differential operator.

Let us finally remark that in recent years spectral estimates and the closely related
L^{2}
method have played an important role in a number of high profile results in convex geometry. Let us mention the proof of the (B) conjecture by Cordero-Erausquin, Fradelizi, and Maurey
[
31
]
, the spectral gap estimate of Kolesnikov and Milman
[
52
,
51
]
in their work on the log-Brunn–Minkowski inequality
[
24
]
, the proof of the variance conjecture for unconditional convex bodies by Klartag
[
48
]
, and the confirmation of the Gardner–Zvavitch conjecture
[
50
,
34
]
.

Organization of the article

2.1.
Translation-invariant continuous valuations

Throughout the article,
B^{n}\subset\RR^{n}
will denote the euclidean unit ball and
S^{n-1}=\partial B^{n}
the euclidean unit sphere.

A convex body is a non-empty convex compact subset of
\RR^{n}
. The set of convex bodies, denoted by
\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
, is a locally compact metric space when equipped with the Hausdorff metric.

Definition 2.1
.

A valuation is a function
\phi\colon\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})\to\CC
satisfying

\phi(K\cup L)=\phi(K)+\phi(L)-\phi(K\cap L)

for any
K,L\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
whenever the union
K\cup L
is convex.

A valuation is called translation-invariant if
\phi(K+x)=\phi(K)
holds for all
K\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
and
x\in\RR^{n}
.
The vector space
\Val
of translation-invariant, continuous valuations on
\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
admits a natural grading by the degree of homogeneity. Namely, denoting by
\Val_{k}\subset\Val
the subspace of valuations satisfying
\phi(tK)=t^{k}\phi(K)
for
t>0
and
K\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
, it is a fundamental result of McMullen
[
59
]
that

(1)

\Val=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\Val_{k}.

An important example of a
k
-homogeneous, translation-invariant continuous valuation is given by the mixed volume

\phi(K)=V(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n-k},K[k]),

where
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n-k}
are arbitrary fixed convex bodies. The spaces
\Val_{0}
and
\Val_{n}
are one-dimensional;
\Val_{0}
consists of the constant valuations,
\Val_{n}
is spanned by the Lebesgue measure
\vol
. The latter is a non-trivial fact due to Hadwiger
[
39
]
.
The spaces
\Val_{k}
for
0<k<n
are infinite-dimensional.
The topology on
\Val
is the topology of uniform convergence on compact subsets. As observed e.g. in
[
8
]
, it is a consequence of (
1
) that this topology is in fact induced by the Banach space norm

\|\phi\|=\sup_{K\subset B^{n}}|\phi(K)|.

2.2.
Smooth valuations

The theory of valuations on convex bodies, as an established line of research, goes back to the solution of Hilbert’s 3rd problem by Dehn. In the early 2000s, the work of Alesker completely reshaped this classical subject. At the center of this transformation is Alesker’s discovery of the rich algebraic structure of the natural dense subspace of smooth valuations
\Val^{\infty}\subset\Val
. Below we present only a very narrow, if not to say distorted, image of the modern theory of valuations, focusing only on what is strictly necessary for this paper. In particular, we discuss neither the Alesker product
[
11
]
, the Fourier transform
[
12
,
36
]
, nor other natural operations on valuations.

To define smooth valuations, we need to introduce more terminology. First, the group
\GL(n)
acts on the Banach space
\Val
continuously by

(g\cdot\phi)(K)=\phi(g^{-1}K).

Second, the normal cycle of a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
is, as a set,

\nc(K)=\{(x,u)\in S\RR^{n}\colon u\text{ outward unit normal at }x\in K\}.

Here
S\RR^{n}=\RR^{n}\times S^{n-1}
is the sphere bundle of
\RR^{n}
. In fact,
\nc(K)\subset S\RR^{n}
is an oriented compact Lipschitz submanifold of dimension
n-1
and thus defines a current through integration. For each
\omega\in\Omega^{n-1}(S\RR^{n})
,

(2)

\displaystyle\phi(K)=\int_{\nc(K)}\omega

is a continuous valuation
[
6
]
. All differential forms we consider throughout this article are complex-valued. The cartesian product structure of the sphere bundle induces a bigrading
\Omega^{k}(S\RR^{n})=\bigoplus_{i+j=k}\Omega^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})
. The subspaces of forms invariant under the translations
(x,u)\mapsto(x+y,u)
are denoted by
\Omega^{k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
and

\Omega^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
. If
\omega\in\Omega^{k,n-k-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
, then the valuation (
2
) is in
\Val_{k}
. Finally, let
\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})\subset\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
be the class of convex bodies with
C^{\infty}
-smooth boundary and strictly positive Gauss curvature.

H^{n-k}(S^{n-1})
vanishes and hence there is
\omega\in\Omega^{k,n-k-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
such that
\tau=d\omega
. The same holds for
k=1
since
\int_{S^{n-1}}\tau=0
by assumption. Since
\tau
is vertical, one has
d\omega=D\omega
.

Note that by convention the integral condition in (
6
) is automatically satisfied for
k>1
. Lemma
2.4
and the kernel theorem imply at once one more equivalent characterization of smooth translation-invariant valuations, cf.
[
5
, Section 8]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The map

\Val^{\infty}\to\{(\tau,z)\mid\tau\in\Omega^{n}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\text{ satisfies \eqref{eq:closed_vertical}},z\in\CC\}

given by

\phi=\lcur c,\omega\rcur\mapsto\left(D\omega+cdx_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{n},\phi(\{0\})\right)

is a well-defined isomorphism.

2.3.
The convolution of smooth valuations

The first-named author and Fu
[
20
]
have discovered a continuous bilinear map
\Val^{\infty}\times\Val^{\infty}\to\Val^{\infty}
called convolution
that is uniquely determined by the following property:

(7)

\vol(\Cdot+A_{1})*\vol(\Cdot+A_{2})=\vol(\Cdot+A_{1}+A_{2}),\quad A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n}).

The convolution gives
\Val^{\infty}
the structure of a commutative graded algebra with
\vol
as identity element. Explicitly,
\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}*\Val_{n-l}^{\infty}\subset\Val_{n-k-l}^{\infty}
. If
\mathbf{A}
is a
k
-tuple and
\mathbf{C}
an
l
-tuple of convex bodies from
\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
with
k+l\leq n
, then (
7
) yields

(8)

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{A},\Cdot[n-k])*V(\mathbf{C},\Cdot[n-l])=\frac{(n-k)!(n-l)!}{n!(n-k-l)!}V(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{C},\Cdot[n-k-l]).

In particular,
V(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k},\Cdot[n-k])
and
V(A_{1},\Cdot[n-1])*\cdots*V(A_{k},\Cdot[n-1])
differ only by a positive factor.

The following description of the convolution in terms of differential forms will be crucial for our purposes.

Let
*\colon\largewedge^{k}(\RR^{n})^{*}\to\largewedge^{n-k}(\RR^{n})^{*}
be the Hodge star isomorphism given by
\alpha\wedge*\beta=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\vol
. We identify
\Omega^{k,l}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}=\largewedge^{k}(\RR^{n})^{*}\otimes\Omega^{l}(S^{n-1})
and define the isomorphism
*_{1}:\Omega^{k,l}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\to\Omega^{n-k,l}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
by

*_{1}=(-1)^{\binom{n-k}{2}}\ *\otimes\,\mathrm{id}.

Note that
d\,*_{1}=(-1)^{n}*_{1}d
.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
k_{1}+k_{2}\leq n
and suppose
\omega_{i}\in\Omega^{n-k_{i},k_{i}-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
for
i=1,2
. Then
\lcur 0,\omega_{1}\rcur*\lcur 0,\omega_{2}\rcur=\lcur 0,\omega\rcur
with

\omega=*_{1}^{-1}(*_{1}\omega_{1}\wedge*_{1}D\omega_{2}).

Moreover,

*_{1}D\omega=*_{1}D\omega_{1}\wedge*_{1}D\omega_{2}.

2.4.
Poincaré duality

The convolution of valuations defines a continuous bilinear form
\Val^{\infty}\times\Val^{\infty}\to\CC
, called the Poincaré pairing, by

(9)

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle=(\phi*\psi)(\{0\}).

In terms of differential forms, if
\phi_{1}=\lcur 0,\omega_{1}\rcur\in\Val_{k}^{\infty}
and
\phi_{2}=\lcur 0,\omega_{2}\rcur\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
have complementary degrees with
0<k<n
, then

\omega_{1}\wedge D\omega_{2}\in\Omega^{n,n-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\simeq\Omega^{n-1}(S^{n-1})

and

(10)

\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\rangle=(-1)^{k}\int_{S^{n-1}}\omega_{1}\wedge D\omega_{2},

see
[
78
, Proposition 4.2]
. A key property of the product of valuations is the following

Theorem 2.8
.

The Poincaré pairing (
9
) is perfect, i.e., the induced map

(11)

\Val^{\infty}(\RR^{n})\to\Val^{\infty}(\RR^{n})^{*}

is injective with dense image.

Elements of
\Val^{-\infty}=(\Val^{\infty})^{*}
are called generalized translation-invariant valuations, see
[
15
]
. Observe that for
\phi\in\Val^{-\infty}
and
K\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
it makes sense to define

(12)

\phi(K)=\langle\phi,\vol(\Cdot+K)\rangle,

since the equality is valid in the classical sense if we consider
\phi\in\Val^{\infty}
as a generalized valuations via the embedding (
11
).

3.
Differential forms on the sphere bundle

The hard Lefschetz theorem and the Hodge–Riemann relations for the algebra
\Val^{\infty}
will ultimately be reduced to the Hodge–Riemann relations in complex linear algebra as proven by Timorin. In this section we develop the linear algebra of differential forms on the sphere bundle necessary for this reduction and connect it with smooth translation-invariant valuations, in particular with mixed volumes. As a first application we will be able to prove injectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem.

3.1.
Complex linear algebra

To begin with, we state Timorin’s result, namely, the analog of Theorem
1.2
in the context of complex linear algebra.

Let
W
be a real vector space of dimension
2n
with a complex structure
J\colon W\to W
.
We denote by
\largewedge_{\CC}^{k}W=\largewedge^{k}W^{*}\otimes_{\RR}\CC
the space of complex-valued
k
-forms on the real vector space
W
. Let

\largewedge_{\CC}^{1}W^{*}=W^{\prime}\oplus\b{W}^{\prime}
be the decomposition of the space of
\RR
-linear maps
W\to\CC
into the
\CC
-linear and
\CC
-antilinear maps.
Then, denoting
\largewedge^{(p,q)}W^{*}=\largewedge^{p}W^{\prime}\otimes\largewedge^{q}\b{W}^{\prime}
, one has

(13)

\largewedge^{k}_{\CC}W^{*}=\bigoplus_{p+q=k}\largewedge^{(p,q)}W^{*}.

Let us clarify that we violate the standard notation here since in our applications
W
will usually be a direct sum of two vector spaces and
\largewedge^{p,q}W^{*}
will refer to the bidegree decomposition in this other context.

A
(1,1)
-form
\omega\in\largewedge^{(1,1)}W^{*}
is called positive if it is real, i.e.,
\b{\omega}=\omega
, and satisfies
\omega(w,Jw)>0
for each non-zero
w\in W
.

Theorem 3.1

(Timorin
[
74
]
)
.

Let
p+q\leq n
and let
\omega_{0},\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n-p-q}
be positive
(1,1)
-forms. Set
\Omega=\omega_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge\omega_{n-p-q}\in\largewedge^{(n-p-q,n-p-q)}W^{*}
. Then the following holds:

{enuma}

Hard Lefschetz theorem.
The wedge product with
\Omega
is an isomorphism

\Omega:\largewedge^{(p,q)}W^{*}\to\largewedge^{(n-q,n-p)}W^{*}.

Hodge–Riemann relations.
Let

P^{(p,q)}=\{\omega\in\largewedge^{(p,q)}W^{*}:\omega_{0}\wedge\Omega\wedge\omega=0\}

be the subspace of primitive forms. Then the sesquilinear form

q(\alpha,\beta)=\mathbf{i}^{p-q}(-1)^{\binom{p+q}{2}}\alpha\wedge\b{\beta}\wedge\Omega

on
P^{(p,q)}
is positive definite.

Here and in what follows we use the canonical orientation of
W
to orient
\largewedge^{2n}W^{*}
and the notation
\mathbf{i}=\sqrt{-1}
.

3.2.
The linear algebra of
(p,p)
-forms

We are going to apply the linear Hodge–Riemann relations in the situation
W=V\oplus V
, where
V
is an
n
-dimensional real vector space and
J(v,w)=(-w,v)
. To this end we first need to verify that a certain class of forms is of type
(p,p)
.

Let
K^{p}(V)\subset\largewedge_{\CC}^{p}V^{*}\otimes\largewedge_{\CC}^{p}V^{*}
be the kernel of the map

\largewedge_{\CC}^{p}V^{*}\otimes\largewedge_{\CC}^{p}V^{*}\to\largewedge^{p-1}_{\CC}V^{*}\otimes\largewedge^{p+1}_{\CC}V^{*}

given by
\beta\otimes\gamma\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\iota_{e_{i}}\beta\otimes\alpha_{i}\wedge\gamma,

where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
is a basis of
V
and
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
is the dual basis. We need the following fact, proved for example in
[
19
, Lemma 5.7]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

K^{p}(V)
is irreducible under the natural action of
\GL(n,\CC)
.

At the same time, we can view
K^{p}(V)
as a subspace of
\largewedge_{\CC}^{2p}W^{*}
, where
W=V\oplus V
. In this respect, the following fact will be important.

Proposition 3.3
.

K^{p}(V)\subset\largewedge^{(p,p)}W^{*}.

Proof 3.4
.

The decomposition (
13
) is compatible with the natural action of
\GL(n,\CC)
, i.e., each summand
\largewedge^{(p,q)}W^{*}
is an invariant subspace.
Observe that
K^{p}(V)
and
\largewedge^{(p,p)}W^{*}
intersect non-trivially; indeed, if
(x_{i},y_{i})
are coordinates on
W=V\oplus V
and
dz_{i}=dx_{i}+\mathbf{i}dy_{i}
, then

dz_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dz_{p}\wedge d\b{z}_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge d\b{z}_{p}=c\ dx_{1}\wedge\cdots dx_{p}\wedge dy_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dy_{p},

for some
c\neq 0
, lies in the intersection. Since
K^{p}(V)
is irreducible, the claim follows.

3.3.
Smooth valuations and
(p,p)
-forms

Let us write
(x,u)
for the standard coordinates on
\RR^{n}\times\RR^{n}
.
To each convex body
A\in\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
we associate its support function

h_{A}(u)=\sup_{a\in A}\langle a,u\rangle,\quad u\in\RR^{n},

and the following smooth differential forms on
\RR^{n}\times(\RR^{n}\setminus\{0\})
:

(14)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{A}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pder[h_{A}]{u_{i}}dx_{i},\\
\omega_{A}&=h_{A}\iota_{T}\vol,\\
\Omega_{A}&=-d\alpha_{A},\end{split}

where
T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\pder{x_{i}}
denotes the Reeb vector field and
\vol
is the volume form on the first factor.
By restriction we obtain differential forms on the sphere bundle
S\RR^{n}=\RR^{n}\times S^{n-1}
that we will not distinguish notationally.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
. We have

nV(A,K[n-1])=\int_{\nc(K)}\omega_{A},\quad K\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n}),

and

*_{1}D\omega_{A}=\Omega_{A}
.

Proof 3.6
.

First, if
P\in\mathcal{K}(\RR^{n})
is a polytope, then

nV(P[n-1],A)=\sum_{F}h_{A}(\nu_{F})\vol_{n-1}(F)=\int_{\nc(P)}\omega_{A},

where the sum is over the facets of
P
and
\nu_{F}
denotes the unit facet normal. The general case of the first statement then follows by continuity.

To prove the second identity, we first show that
d(\omega_{A}+\alpha\wedge\xi)
with

\xi=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pder[h_{A}]{u_{i}}\iota_{\pder{x_{i}}}\iota_{T}\vol

is vertical. We have

d\omega_{A}=dh_{A}\wedge\iota_{T}\vol+h_{A}d\iota_{T}\vol,

and the second term is vertical. Since

d\alpha\wedge\xi=\sum_{k=1}^{n}du_{k}\wedge dx_{k}\wedge\xi=-dh_{A}\wedge i_{T}\vol

we obtain

D\omega_{A}=d(\omega_{A}+\alpha\wedge\xi)=d\omega_{A}+d\alpha\wedge\xi-\alpha\wedge d\xi=h_{A}d\iota_{T}\vol-\alpha\wedge d\xi.

Next, we get

\displaystyle\alpha\wedge d\xi

\displaystyle=-\alpha\wedge\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{k}}du_{k}\wedge\iota_{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}}\iota_{T}\vol+\alpha\wedge\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}}\iota_{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}}d\iota_{T}\vol.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pder[h_{A}]{u_{i}}u_{i}=h_{A}
by homogeneity of
h_{A}
, the second term is equal to
h_{A}d\iota_{T}\vol
.

Using
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{k}}u_{i}=0
in the third equality, the claimed identity now follows from

\displaystyle*_{1}D\omega_{A}

\displaystyle=*_{1}\left(\alpha\wedge\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{k}}du_{k}\wedge\iota_{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}}\iota_{T}\vol\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{k}}*_{1}\left(\alpha\wedge\iota_{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}}\iota_{T}\vol\wedge du_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{k}}dx_{i}\wedge du_{k}

\displaystyle=-d\alpha_{A}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{A}.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
. If
\phi=\lcur 0,\omega\rcur
for
\omega\in\Omega^{k,n-k-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
, then

\phi(A)=\frac{(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}}{k!}\int_{S^{n-1}}\ast_{1}\omega\wedge\Omega_{A}^{k}.

Proof 3.8
.

Denote
\psi_{A}=nV(A,\Cdot[n-1])
. By Lemma
3.5
and Theorem
2.7
, we have

\displaystyle k!\phi(A)=(L_{A}^{k}\phi)(\{0\})=\langle\phi,\psi_{A}^{k}\rangle=(-1)^{\binom{n}{2}}\int_{S^{n-1}}\ast_{1}\omega\wedge\Omega_{A}^{k}.

The sphere bundle of any riemannian manifold has a natural contact structure given by a distribution of contact planes
H_{x,u}\subset T_{x,u}SM
. In the special case
M=\RR^{n}
, each contact plane
H_{x,u}=u^{\perp}\oplus u^{\perp}
is a complex vector space of dimension
n-1
with the complex structure
J(X_{1},X_{2})=(-X_{2},X_{1})
.

We say that a differential form
\omega\in\Omega^{p+q}(S\RR^{n})
is a
(p,q)
-form if each

\omega|_{x,u}
belongs to
\largewedge^{p+q}H^{*}_{x,u}
and is a
(p,q)
-form in the sense of Subsection
3.1
. Note that the first condition is equivalent to
i_{T}\omega=0
. A
(1,1)
-form
\omega\in\Omega^{2}(S\RR^{n})
is called positive if each
\omega|_{x,u}\in\largewedge^{1,1}H^{*}_{x,u}
is positive.

Proposition 3.9
.

If
\omega\in\Omega^{n-k,k-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
, then
*_{1}D\omega
is a
(k,k)
-form. Moreover, the
(1,1)
-form
*_{1}D\omega_{A}
is positive for each
A\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
.

Proof 3.10
.

First, the identities
\alpha\wedge D\omega=0
and
d\alpha\wedge D\omega=0
imply
i_{T}(*_{1}D\omega)=0
. Hence, by Proposition
3.3
,
*_{1}D\omega\in K^{k}(u^{\perp})\subset\largewedge^{(k,k)}H_{x,u}^{*}
. Second, since
A
has a strictly positively curved boundary,

\Omega_{A}(X,JX)=\Hess h_{A}(u)(X_{1},X_{1})+\Hess h_{A}(u)(X_{2},X_{2})>0

for every non-zero
X=(X_{1},X_{2})\in H_{x,u}
.

3.4.
Injectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem

We are now in a position to deduce injectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem for smooth valuations from the linear Hodge–Riemann relations.

Proposition 3.11
.

For each tuple
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
of convex bodies from
\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
, the map
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}\to\Val^{\infty}_{k}
is injective.

Proof 3.12
.

For
k=0
, this follows trivially from (
8
) and
V(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})\neq 0
. Let us assume
k>0
from now on. By translation invariance, we may further assume that each
C_{i}
contains the origin in its interior.

Let
\phi=\lcur 0,\omega\rcur
be in the kernel of
L_{\mathbf{C}}
. Put
\tau=*_{1}D\omega\in\Omega^{k,k}(S\RR^{n})
. By (
8
), Theorem
2.7
, Lemma
3.5
, and Proposition
3.9
,

(15)

\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{n-2k}}\wedge\tau=0.

Thus each
\tau|_{x,u}
is a
(k,k)
-form that is primitive with respect to the positive
(1,1)
-forms
\Omega_{C_{1}}|_{x,u},\ldots,\Omega_{C_{n-2k}}|_{x,u}
.

The linear Hodge–Riemann relations (Theorem
3.1
), for the complex vector space
(H_{x,u},J)
of complex dimension
n-1
and
p=q=k
yield

(-1)^{k}\tau\wedge\b{\tau}\wedge\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{n-2k-1}}\geq 0\quad\text{at }(x,u)

with equality if and only if
\tau|_{x,u}=0
. Each convex body
A\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
with the origin in the interior satisfies

\alpha_{A}|_{x,u}(u,0)=h_{A}(u)>0
. We thus have

(16)

(-1)^{k}\alpha_{C_{n-2k}}\wedge\tau\wedge\b{\tau}\wedge\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{n-2k-1}}\geq 0\quad\text{at }(x,u)

with equality if and only if
\tau|_{x,u}=0
.

Since
\tau
is closed and
H^{k}(S^{n-1})=0
, we may write
\tau=d\kappa.
Using Stokes’ theorem and (
15
),

\displaystyle\int_{S^{n-1}}\alpha_{C_{2k-n}}\wedge\tau\wedge\b{\tau}\wedge\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{2k-n-1}}

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{S^{n-1}}\Omega_{C_{2k-n}}\wedge\tau\wedge\b{\kappa}\wedge\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{2k-n-1}}=0.

This implies that equality holds in (
16
) for every
(x,u)
and thus
\tau=0
, which implies that
\phi=0
.

4.
Surjectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem

Let
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
be a tuple of convex bodies from
\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}(\RR^{n})
. The goal of this section is to establish the surjectivity of
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}\to\Val^{\infty}_{k}
, thus proving the hard Lefschetz theorem. As we have seen in the previous section, after a careful description of the Lefschetz map in terms of differential forms on the sphere bundle, the injectivity is an immediate consequence of Timorin’s linear Hodge–Riemann relations and Stokes’ theorem. Proving surjectivity however will require tools from PDE theory, in particular the machinery of Sobolev spaces, weak solutions, and elliptic regularity.

We will introduce in this section two natural Hilbert space completions, denoted
\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
\mathscr{F}_{k}
, of the space of smooth valuations
\Val_{k}^{\infty}
. The existence of these two Hilbert space completions is a result of the two descriptions of smooth valuations via smooth differential forms: forms of degree
n-1
or closed vertical
n
-forms. In the present section, the Hilbert space
\mathscr{E}_{k}
will play a lesser role, but it will be of central importance for our proof of the Hodge–Riemann relations.

4.1.
Facts from elliptic operator theory

Let us recall from
[
57
]
several fundamental results about elliptic operators.

The Sobolev
s
norm of a Schwartz function
u\colon\RR^{n}\to\CC^{p}
is defined by

(17)

\|u\|_{s}=\int_{\RR^{n}}(1+|\xi|)^{2s}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

where
\hat{u}
denotes the Fourier transform of
u
. Note that
\|\Cdot\|_{0}
is the
L^{2}
norm.

Let
E\to X
be a complex vector bundle over a compact manifold
X
. Let
U_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
, be an open cover of
X
by coordinate neighbourhoods such that
E
is locally trivial over each
U_{i}
. Fix local coordinates, local trivializations, and a partition of unity
(\rho_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
subordinate to
(U_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. Let
\Gamma(E)
denote the space of smooth sections of
E
. Any
u\in\Gamma(E)
can be written as
u=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}
with
u_{i}=\rho_{i}u
. For
s\in\RR
, the Sobolev
s
norm on
\Gamma(E)
is defined by

\|u\|_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|u_{i}\|_{s},

where, using
E|_{U_{i}}\cong U_{i}\times\CC^{p}
, the norms on the right-hand side are given by (
17
). Different choices of the coordinate system or the local trivializations give equivalent Sobolev norms. The completion of
\Gamma(E)
with respect to this norm is denoted
H^{s}(E)
. It is clear from (
17
) that
H^{s}(E)
is a Hilbert space.

Let
E
and
F
be complex vector bundles of ranks
p
and
q
over
X
. A linear map
P\colon\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(F)
is called a differential operator of order
m
if each point of
X
possesses an open neighbourhood
U
with coordinates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and a local trivialization
E|_{U}\cong U\times\CC^{p}
such that
P
can be written as

P=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}A^{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial x^{\alpha}},

where
A^{\alpha}
are
q\times p
matrices of complex-valued functions on
U
and
A^{\alpha}\neq 0
for some
\alpha
with
|\alpha|=m
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
P\colon\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(F)
be a differential operator of order
m
over a compact manifold
X
. For any
s\in\RR
, the differential operator
P
extends to a bounded operator
P\colon H^{s}(E)\to H^{s-m}(F)
.

The principal symbol of a differential operator
P
is a section
\sigma(P)
of
\pi^{*}\Hom(E,F)
, the pullback bundle of
\Hom(E,F)
under the canonical projection
\pi\colon T^{*}X\to X
, such that for
\xi=\sum\xi_{k}dx_{k}
,

\sigma_{\xi}(P)=\mathbf{i}^{m}\sum_{|\alpha|=m}A^{\alpha}(x)\xi^{\alpha},

where
\mathbf{i}=\sqrt{-1}
.
If
P\colon\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(F)
and
Q\colon\Gamma(F)\to\Gamma(G)
are differential operators, then
\sigma_{\xi}(Q\circ P)=\sigma_{\xi}(Q)\sigma_{\xi}(P)
.
The differential operator
P
is called elliptic if for non-zero cotangent vectors
\xi\in T^{*}X
the symbol
\sigma_{\xi}(P)\colon E_{x}\to F_{x}
is invertible for all
x\in X
. In particular
p=q
in this case.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
P\colon\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(F)
be an elliptic operator of order
m
over a compact manifold
X
.
The following holds:

{enuma}

Elliptic regularity.
For any open set
U\subset X
and any
u\in H^{s}(E)
,

Pu|_{U}\in C^{\infty}\Longrightarrow u|_{U}\in C^{\infty}.

For each
s
, the kernel of
P\colon H^{s}(E)\to H^{s-m}(F)
consists of smooth sections and its dimension is finite and independent of
s
.

Elliptic estimate.
For each
s
, there is a constant
C
such that

\|u\|_{s}\leq C(\|u\|_{s-m}+\|Pu\|_{s-m})

for all
u\in H^{s}(E)
.

For each
s
, there is a constant
C
such that

\|u\|_{s}\leq C\|Pu\|_{s-m}

for all
u\in(\ker P)^{\perp}
, the orthogonal complement of the kernel of
P
in
H^{s}(E)
.

Proof 4.3
.

Items (a), (b), and (c) are contained in
[

. A short argument (see, e.g., the proof of Lemma 4.14 in

A hermitian metric on

and a smooth measure
\mu
on

define an

\|u\|_{L^{2}}=\left(\int_{X}|u|^{2}d\mu\right)^{1/2}

on
\Gamma(E)
. If

characterized by the property

\langle Pu,v\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle u,P^{*}v\rangle_{L^{2}}

for

and

.

Example 4.4
.

Let
X
be a compact riemannian manifold.
The Laplace–Beltrami operator

is defined by

, where we use the standard notation

4.2.
Hilbert space completions of the space of valuations

Recall that if
Y\subset X
is a closed subspace, then
X/Y
is naturally a normed space with respect to
\|\pi(x)\|=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x+y\|
,
x\in X
, where
\pi:X\to X/Y
is the canonical projection.
Let
i\colon X\to\wt X
be the completion of
X
.
By parallelogram law, if
X
is an inner product space, then so are
X/Y
and
\wt X
. The following lemma is well known.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X
be an inner product space and
Y\subset X
a closed subspace. Let
i\colon X\to\wt X
be the completion of
X
. Then
\wt X/\overline{i(Y)}\cong i(Y)^{\perp}\subset\wt X
is the completion of
X/Y
.

Let
E_{i,j}\to S^{n-1}
denote the complex vector bundle
\largewedge_{\CC}^{i}(\RR^{n})^{*}\boxtimes\largewedge_{\CC}^{j}T^{*}S^{n-1}
with the canonical hermitian metric. The space of smooth sections of
E_{i,j}
is just
\Omega^{i,j}(S\mathbb{R}^{n})^{tr}
.
Let us introduce the notation
\Omega_{H^{s}}^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}=H^{s}(E_{i,j})
and
\Omega_{L^{2}}^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}=L^{2}(E_{i,j})
.
Explicitly, the
L^{2}
inner product on
\Omega_{L^{2}}^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
is given by

(18)

\langle\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{S^{n-1}}\omega_{1}\wedge\overline{*\omega_{2}},\quad\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega^{i,j}(S\mathbb{R}^{n})^{tr},

where
*
is the Hodge star operator.

Define for
1\leq k\leq n-1

(19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}X_{k}&=\Omega^{k,n-k-1}(S\RR^{n})^{tr},\\
Y_{k}&=\{\omega\in X_{k}\colon D\omega=0\},\\
Z_{k}&=\{\tau\in\Omega^{k,n-k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\colon\text{properties }\eqref{eq:closed_vertical}\text{ hold}\}.\end{split}

We consider
X_{k}
and
Z_{k}
as subspaces of
\Omega^{k,n-k-1}_{L^{2}}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
and
\Omega^{k,n-k}_{L^{2}}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
with the induced
L^{2}
inner product.
To see that
Y_{k}
is closed, just observe that the extension
D\colon\Omega^{k,n-1-k}_{L^{2}}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\to\Omega^{k,n-k}_{H^{-2}}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
is continuous.

Definition 4.6
.

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
, we denote by
\mathscr{E}_{k}
the Hilbert space completion of
X_{k}/Y_{k}
and by
\mathscr{F}_{k}
the Hilbert space completion of
Z_{k}
, in both cases with respect to the induced
L^{2}
inner product. Moreover, we set
\mathscr{E}_{0}=\mathscr{F}_{0}=\Val_{0}
and
\mathscr{E}_{n}=\mathscr{F}_{n}=\Val_{n}
, with the norms defined in such a way that
\|1\|_{\mathscr{E}}=\|1\|_{\mathscr{F}}=1
and
\|\vol\|_{\mathscr{E}}=\|\vol\|_{\mathscr{F}}=1
.

Example 4.7
.

Let us describe these inner products in the special case
k=n-1
in more familiar terms. In this degree, every smooth valuation can be expressed as
\phi(K)=nV(A,K[n-1])-nV(C,K[n-1])
with some convex bodies
A,C\in\mathcal{K}_{+}^{\infty}
. Hence by Lemma
3.5

\|\phi\|_{\mathscr{E}}=\|\omega_{A}-\omega_{C}\|_{X_{n-1}/Y_{n-1}}=\inf_{x\in\RR^{n}}\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}|h_{A+x}-h_{C}|^{2}du\right)^{1/2}

since
D(\omega_{A}-\omega_{C})=0
if and only if
h_{A}-h_{C}
is linear. Similarly,

\|\phi\|_{\mathscr{F}}=\|D\omega_{A}-D\omega_{C}\|_{Z_{n-1}}=\left(\int_{S^{n-1}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\left|\frac{\partial^{2}h_{A}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{j}}-\frac{\partial^{2}h_{C}}{\partial u_{i}\partial u_{j}}\right|^{2}du\right)^{1/2}.

Our first goal in this section is to prove a version of the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem. We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\tau\in\Omega^{m}_{L^{2}}(S^{n-1})
,
1\leq m\leq n-1
, satisfy
d\tau=0
or, if
m=n-1
, let
\tau
be perpendicular to the riemannian volume form. Then there exists
\omega\in\Omega_{H^{1}}^{m-1}(S^{n-1})
such that

{enuma}

d\omega=\tau
and

\omega\in\operatorname{im}(\delta\colon\Omega_{H^{2}}^{m}(S^{n-1})\to\Omega_{H^{1}}^{m-1}(S^{n-1}))
.

Moreover,
\omega
is uniquely determined by these two properties and there exists a constant
C=C(k,n)
depending only
k
and
n
such that

\|\omega\|_{1}\leq C\|\tau\|_{0}.

Proof 4.9
.

To prove existence observe that we can find
\sigma\in\Omega_{H^{2}}^{m}(S^{n-1})
such that
\Delta\sigma=\tau
. Indeed, if
m<n-1
then
\Delta
is a bijection, and if
m=n-1
, the additional condition on
\tau
implies that
\tau
is orthogonal to the kernel of
\Delta
. By the definition of the Laplace–Beltrami operator it follows that
\tau=d\omega+\delta\gamma
, where
\omega=\delta\sigma
and
\gamma=d\sigma
. Since the exact and co-exact forms are orthogonal,

\|\delta\gamma\|_{L^{2}}=\langle\delta\gamma,\delta\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\tau,\delta\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle d\tau,\gamma\rangle_{L^{2}}=0.

Thus
\tau=d\omega
with
\omega\in\operatorname{im}(\delta\colon\Omega_{H^{2}}^{m}(S^{n-1})\to\Omega_{H^{1}}^{m-1}(S^{n-1}))
.

To prove uniqueness let
\omega=\delta\sigma
and suppose
d\omega=0
. Thus
\omega
is harmonic and hence vanishes or, if
m=1
, is a constant function. But constant functions are perpendicular to the image of
\delta
and hence
\omega=0
in all cases.

Finally observe that
\delta\tau=\delta d\omega=\Delta\omega
. If
m>1
, then

\|\omega\|_{1}\leq C\|\Delta\omega\|_{-1}\leq C\|\tau\|_{0}

by Theorem
4.2
(d). If
m=1
, then
\omega
is perpendicular to the kernel of
\Delta
, since
\omega
is co-exact, and hence the inequalities are valid also in this case.

Proposition 4.10
.

We have continuous inclusions

\Val_{k}^{\infty}\subset\mathscr{F}_{k}\subset\mathscr{E}_{k}\subset\Val_{k}^{-\infty}.

Moreover,
\mathscr{F}_{k}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{E}_{k}
is compact.

Proof 4.11
.

We may assume that
1\leq k\leq n-1
. The
(n-1)
- and
n
-forms that make up the spaces
\mathscr{E}_{k}
and
\mathscr{F}_{k}
can be paired in the usual way with differential forms defining smooth valuations. Thus
\mathscr{E}_{k},\mathscr{F}_{k}\subset\Val_{k}^{-\infty}
and (
10
) shows that
\Val^{\infty}_{k}\subset\mathscr{E}_{k},\mathscr{F}_{k}
compatible with the former inclusions.

Let
\tau\in Z_{k}
and write
\tau=\sum_{I}dx_{I}\wedge\tau_{I}
with
\tau_{I}\in\Omega^{n-k}_{L^{2}}(S^{n-1})
, where
I
runs over all multi-indices with
|I|=k
. Since
\tau\in Z_{k}
is an exact generalized form, each
\tau_{I}
satisfies the assumption of Lemma
4.8
. Thus we can find
\omega=\sum_{I}dx_{I}\wedge\omega_{I}\in\Omega_{H^{1}}^{k,n-1-k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
such that
d\omega=\tau
and
\|\omega\|_{1}\leq C\|\tau\|_{0}
. This defines
\mathscr{F}_{k}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{E}_{k}
. Since
\Omega_{H^{1}}^{k,n-1-k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}\hookrightarrow\Omega_{L^{2}}^{k,n-1-k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
is compact by the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem (see, e.g.,
[

Remark 4.12
.

The space
\Val^{-\infty}_{k}
is natural in the sense that it is independent of the choice of a euclidean inner product and an orientation on
\RR^{n}
. The same is true for the subspaces
\mathscr{E}_{k},\mathscr{F}_{k}\subset\Val_{k}^{-\infty}
.

The following theorem clarifies the connection between the two Hilbert space structures.

Theorem 4.13

(Poincaré duality)
.

The Poincaré pairing defined via convolution
\Val_{k}^{\infty}\times\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}\to\CC
, extends to a continuous perfect pairing
\mathscr{E}_{k}\times\mathscr{F}_{n-k}\to\CC
. In particular, the induced map
\Pd_{k}\colon\mathscr{E}_{k}\to(\mathscr{F}_{n-k})^{*}
is an isomorphism of Hilbert spaces and is called

.

Proof 4.14
.

The Hodge star operator
*\colon\Omega^{k,l}(S\mathbb{R}^{n})^{tr}\to\Omega^{n-k,n-1-l}(S\mathbb{R}^{n})^{tr}
is an isometry for the
L^{2}
norms and satisfies
*^{2}=\mathrm{id}
. It is easy to check that for
1\leq k\leq n-1
it induces an isometric isomorphism

*\colon Z_{n-k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\longrightarrow}}Y_{k}^{\perp}\cap X_{k}

that extends to the
L^{2}
completions. The statement of the theorem now follows easily from (
18
) and (
10
).

Lemma 4.15
.

If
\phi\in\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}
, then convolution with

extends to continuous maps

\mathscr{E}_{l}\to\mathscr{E}_{l-k}
and
\mathscr{F}_{l}\to\mathscr{F}_{l-k}
for all

.

.

The cases

and

are obvious and the case

follows from Theorem

. If

, we have by Theorem

and

corresponding to the convolution with

4.3.
Our elliptic operator

Let
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
be a tuple of convex bodies from
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}
, where
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
.
Recall that the elements of
\Omega^{i,j}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
can be identified with the sections of the bundle
E_{i,j}
over
S^{n-1}
. We define the differential operators

\displaystyle P_{0}

\displaystyle:=d\colon\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})\to\Gamma(E_{n-k,k+1}),

\displaystyle P_{1}

\displaystyle:=\alpha\colon\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})\to\Gamma(E_{n-k+1,k}),

\displaystyle P_{2}

\displaystyle:=d\alpha\colon\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})\to\Gamma(E_{n-k+1,k+1}),

\displaystyle P_{3}

\displaystyle:=*_{1}^{-1}(\Omega_{\mathbf{C}}\wedge*_{1}\Cdot)\colon\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})\to\Gamma(E_{k,n-k}),

where
\Omega_{\mathbf{C}}=\Omega_{C_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\Omega_{C_{n-2k}}
. Note that only
P_{0}
is a differential operator of positive order . The operators
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}
have order
0
, i.e., are bundle morphisms. Note that
P_{3}=\mathrm{id}
if
k=\frac{n}{2}
.

Definition 4.17
.

Let
P_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})\to\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})
be the differential operator of order two defined by

P_{\mathbf{C}}=P_{0}^{*}P_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{3}P_{i}^{*}\Delta P_{i}.

The adjoints are formed with respect to the canonical hermitian metric on
E_{n-k,k}
and the canonical riemannian metric on
S^{n-1}
.

Proposition 4.18
.

P_{\mathbf{C}}
is elliptic and injective for all
0<k<\frac{n}{2}
.

Proof 4.19
.

The symbols are given for any
\xi\in T^{*}S^{n-1}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}(P_{0})(\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{i}\xi\wedge\tau,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}(P_{1})(\tau)

\displaystyle=\alpha\wedge\tau,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}(P_{2})(\tau)

\displaystyle=d\alpha\wedge\tau,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\xi}(P_{3})(\tau)

\displaystyle=*_{1}^{-1}(\Omega_{\mathbf{C}}\wedge*_{1}\tau),

and

\sigma_{\xi}(P_{\mathbf{C}})=\sigma_{\xi}(P_{0})^{*}\sigma_{\xi}(P_{0})+|\xi|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sigma_{\xi}(P_{i})^{*}\sigma_{\xi}(P_{i}).

Thus for
\xi\neq 0
we have
\sigma_{\xi}(P_{\mathbf{C}})(\tau)=0
if and only if
\sigma_{\xi}(P_{i})(\tau)=0
for each
i
.
The second and third equation,
\alpha\wedge\tau=0
and
d\alpha\wedge\tau=0
, imply by Proposition
3.3
that

*_{1}\tau
is a
(k,k)
-form; the fourth,
*_{1}^{-1}(\Omega_{\mathbf{C}}\wedge*_{1}\tau)=0
, says that
*_{1}\tau
is primitive with respect to the positive
(1,1)
-forms
\Omega_{C_{1}},\ldots,\Omega_{C_{n-2k}}
. Hence Timorin’s theorem (Theorem
3.1
) yields

(20)

(-1)^{k}{*_{1}\tau}\wedge\b{*_1\tau}\wedge\Omega_{\mathbf{C}_{\setminus 1}}\geq 0,

where
\mathbf{C}_{\setminus 1}=(C_{2},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
,
with equality if and only if
*_{1}\tau=0
. But the first equation implies that
*_{1}\tau
and
\b{*_1 \tau}
are both divisible by
\xi
, which forces equality in (
20
). Thus
*_{1}\tau=0
, as claimed.

To prove that
P_{\mathbf{C}}
is injective, first note that for each
\tau\in\Gamma(E_{n-k,k})
,

\langle P_{\mathbf{C}}\tau,\tau\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle P_{0}\tau,P_{0}\tau\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\langle\Delta P_{i}\tau,P_{i}\tau\rangle_{L^{2}}\geq 0

is a sum of non-negative terms. Therefore, if
P_{\mathbf{C}}\tau=0
, then
P_{0}\tau=0
and
\Delta P_{i}\tau=0
for
i=1,2,3
.

Recall that
P_{1}\tau\in\Gamma(E_{n-k+1,k})
and
P_{3}\tau\in\Gamma(E_{k,n-k})
. Since
1\leq k<n-1
,
\Delta
is injective on
\Omega^{k}(S^{n-1})
. It follows that
P_{1}\tau=0
. We thus have
d\tau=0
and
\alpha\wedge\tau=0
. In particular, there is
\phi\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
such that
\tau=\tau_{\phi}
.

If
k>1
,
\Delta
is injective on
\Omega^{n-k}(S^{n-1})
, hence
P_{3}\tau=0
. If
k=1
, we write
P_{3}\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(u)dx_{i}\wedge\vol_{S^{n-1}}
with smooth functions
f_{i}\in C^{\infty}(S^{n-1})
, where
\vol_{S^{n-1}}\in\Omega^{n-1}(S^{n-1})
is the volume form. Then on the one hand,
\alpha\wedge P_{3}\tau=0
implies that
u_{j}f_{i}(u)=u_{i}f_{j}(u)
for all
1\leq i,j\leq n
, and on the other hand
\Delta P_{3}\tau=0
implies that
f_{i}
is constant for all
i
. This is only possible if all
f_{i}
vanish, i.e., if
P_{3}\tau=0
. In both cases we thus get
L_{\mathbf{C}}\phi=0
. By injectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem (Theorem
3.11
),
\phi=0
and hence
\tau=0
.

4.4.
Proof of surjectivity in the hard Lefschetz theorem

Our strategy will be to first prove the existence of a solution
\psi
in
\mathscr{E}_{n-k}
to the equation
L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi=\phi
, where
\phi\in\Val_{k}^{\infty}
and
0<k<\frac{n}{2}
. Using our elliptic operator
P_{\mathbf{C}}
we will then be able to conclude that
\psi
must be in fact smooth. The existence of a weak solution
\psi\in\mathscr{E}_{n-k}
rests on an
L^{2}
estimate which is also a consequence of the ellipticity of
P_{\mathbf{C}}
.

For
0<k<n
let
\tau\colon\mathscr{E}_{n-k}\to\Omega_{H^{-2}}^{n-k,k}(S\RR^{n})^{tr}
denote the continuous extension of
\lcur 0,\omega\rcur\mapsto D\omega
.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
\psi\in\mathscr{E}_{n-k}
with
0<k<\frac{n}{2}
. Then

P_{\mathbf{C}}\tau_{\psi}=P_{3}^{*}\Delta\tau_{L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi}.

Proof 4.21
.

Note that both sides are well defined forms in
H^{-4}(E_{n-k,k})
by Lemma
4.1
. Suppose first that
\psi\in\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}
. Then
\tau_{L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi}=P_{3}\tau_{\psi}
, while
P_{i}\tau_{\psi}=0
for
i=0,1,2
, which implies the displayed equation. The general case follows by approximation, using Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.15
.

Proposition 4.22
.

For
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
, there exists a constant
c>0
, depending on
k
and
\mathbf{C}
, such that

\|\psi\|_{\mathscr{F}}\leq c\|L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi\|_{\mathscr{F}}

for all
\psi\in\mathscr{F}_{n-k}
.

Proof 4.23
.

The statement is trivial for
k=0
(with
c=\frac{1}{n!V(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n})}
) and
k=\frac{n}{2}
(with
c=1
). Let
0<k<\frac{n}{2}
. Let
\psi\in\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}
. Since
P_{\mathbf{C}}
is injective, Theorem
4.2
(d) and Lemma
4.1
imply that

\|\tau_{\psi}\|_{s}\leq c\|P_{\mathbf{C}}\tau_{\psi}\|_{s-2}=c\|P_{3}^{*}\Delta\tau_{L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi}\|_{s-2}\leq c\|\Delta\tau_{L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi}\|_{s-2}\leq c\|\tau_{L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi}\|_{s},

where in each step
c
denotes a possibly different constant. Choosing
s=0
yields the desired inequality in the smooth case. The general case follows by approximation, using Lemma
4.15
.

We are now ready to complete the proof of the hard Lefschetz theorem.

Theorem 4.24
.

For any
\phi\in\Val_{k}^{\infty},0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
there is
\psi\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
such that

L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi=\phi
.

Proof 4.25
.

The statement follows from injectivity of
L_{\mathbf{C}}
(Proposition
3.11
) for
k=0
and is trivial for
k=\frac{n}{2}
. We may therefore assume that
0<k<\frac{n}{2}
. By Proposition
4.10
we may consider
\phi
as an element of
\mathscr{E}_{k}
. Define the subspace

H:=\{L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho\mid\rho\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}\}\subset\mathscr{F}_{k}

and the following linear functional on
H
:

\ell(L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho)=\langle\phi,\rho\rangle,

where on the right-hand side is the (extended) Poincaré pairing on
\mathscr{E}_{k}\times\mathscr{F}_{n-k}
.
Note that
\ell
is well defined by the injectivity part of the hard Lefschetz theorem, Proposition
3.11
. Using the
L^{2}
estimate of Proposition
4.22
, we obtain

|\ell(L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho)|\leq\|\phi\|_{\mathscr{E}}\|\rho\|_{\mathscr{F}}\leq c\|\phi\|_{\mathscr{E}}\|L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho\|_{\mathscr{F}},

hence
\ell
is a bounded linear functional.

By the Hahn–Banach theorem
\ell
extends to a continuous linear functional
\tilde{\ell}
on
\mathscr{F}_{k}
. By Poincaré duality (Theorem
4.13
), there is
\psi\in\mathscr{E}_{n-k}
such that
\tilde{\ell}=\langle\psi,\Cdot\rangle
and hence

\langle\phi,\rho\rangle=\ell(L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho)=\langle\psi,L_{\mathbf{C}}\rho\rangle=\langle L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi,\rho\rangle

for all
\rho\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
. Consequently, we have
L_{\mathbf{C}}\psi=\phi
and therefore
P_{\mathbf{C}}\tau_{\psi}=P_{3}^{*}\Delta\tau_{\phi}
by Lemma
4.20
. Since the right-hand side is smooth if
\phi
is smooth and
P_{\mathbf{C}}
is elliptic, Theorem
4.2
(a) implies that
\tau_{\psi}
is smooth and hence
\psi\in\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}
.

Proof 4.26

(Proof of Theorem
1.2
(a))
.

Proposition
3.11
and Theorem
4.24
imply that the map
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\Val_{n-k}^{\infty}\to\Val_{k}^{\infty}
is a bijection. Since
L_{\mathbf{C}}
is continuous, the open mapping theorem for Fréchet spaces implies that
L_{\mathbf{C}}
is an isomorphism of topological vector spaces.

4.5.
Continuous dependence on the reference bodies

Identifying a convex body
A
with its support function
h_{A}
, we can view
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
as a subset of
C^{2}(S^{n-1})
. The topology inherited from this inclusion is called the
C^{2}
topology on
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
. If
X
and
Y
are Banach spaces we denote by
\mathscr{L}(X,Y)
the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
X
to
Y
.

The reason why we work in the following proposition with the
C^{2}
topology on
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})
and not on
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{+}(\RR^{n})
, the class of convex bodies with support function in
C^{2}
and strictly positive radii of curvature, is that we don’t know whether the Lefschetz map
L_{\mathbf{C}}
is surjective for reference bodies belonging merely to
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{+}(\RR^{n})
.

Proposition 4.27
.

Let
0\leq k\leq\frac{n}{2}
. The isomorphism
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\Val^{\infty}_{n-k}\to\Val^{\infty}_{k}
extends to an isomorphism
L_{\mathbf{C}}\colon\mathscr{F}_{n-k}\to\mathscr{F}_{k}
of Hilbert spaces. Moreover, the maps

\mathcal{K}^{\infty}_{+}(\RR^{n})^{n-2k}\to\mathscr{L}(\mathscr{F}_{n-k},\mathscr{F}_{k})
,

(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})\mapsto L_{\mathbf{C}},

and

5.
Hodge–Riemann relations

In this section we use the properties of the Lefschetz isomorphism, in particular its continuous dependence on the reference bodies, to prove the Hodge–Riemann relations. As mentioned already in the introduction, the basic idea is to continuously deform a given tuple of reference bodies
\mathbf{C}=(C_{0},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
into euclidean balls
\mathbf{B}=(B^{n},\ldots,B^{n})
and to argue that positivity of the Hodge–Riemann form
q_{\mathbf{C}}
is preserved during this process.

The Hodge–Riemann form
q_{\mathbf{C}}
extends to a bounded quadratic form on
\mathscr{P}_{\mathbf{C}}\subset\mathscr{F}_{n-k}
, the closed subspace of primitive valuations with respect to
\mathbf{C}
. However,
0
will always belong to the spectrum of
q_{\mathbf{C}}
and therefore we cannot directly conclude that small perturbations of
q_{\mathbf{C}}
will remain positive. Luckily we can also work with the Hilbert space
\mathscr{E}_{n-k}
, in which
q_{\mathbf{C}}
is strictly positive. The price we have to pay for this, however, is that our quadratic form becomes unbounded, which adds another layer of technical difficulties.

In this and the following section, we will slightly deviate from the previous notation. Since the additional body
C_{0}
is important in the definition of the primitive subspaces and hence in the Hodge–Riemann form, we will denote by
\mathbf{C}=(C_{0},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
the complete tuple of reference bodies and write
\mathbf{C}_{\setminus 0}:=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{n-2k})
if we wish to exclude
C_{0}
.

5.1.
Facts from perturbation theory

For the convenience of the reader, we review here some of the notions appearing in the statement of the Kato–Rellich theorem and refer to the books
[
64
,
62
,
46
]
for more information.

An operator in a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
is a linear map
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\to\mathscr{H}
defined on a subspace
\operatorname{dom}(T)
of
\mathscr{H}
. If
\operatorname{dom}(T)
, the domain of
T
, is a dense subspace of
\mathscr{H}
, then
T
is said to be densely defined. Note that if
T
is densely defined and bounded, then
T
can be uniquely extended to all of
\mathscr{H}
.

An operator
T
is called closed if its graph is a closed subset of
\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}
. If the closure of the graph of
T
happens to be a graph of an operator
\b{T}
, then
T
is called closable. In this case
\b{T}
is the smallest closed extension of
T
.

The adjoint
T^{*}
of a densely defined operator
T
on
\mathscr{H}
is defined as follows. Let
\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
denote the set of all
x\in\mathscr{H}
for which there is an
y\in\mathscr{H}
with
\langle Tz,x\rangle=\langle z,y\rangle
for all
z\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
. For each such
x\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
one defines
T^{*}x=y
. The adjoint need not to be densely defined and even
\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})=0
is possible. A densely defined operator
T
is called self-adjoint if
\operatorname{dom}(T)=\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
and
T^{*}=T
;

T
is called symmetric if
\operatorname{dom}(T)\subset\operatorname{dom}(T^{*})
and
T^{*}|_{\operatorname{dom}(T)}=T
. Another way to express this is to say that

\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,Ty\rangle

for all
x,y\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
.

A symmetric operator
T
is said to be bounded below by
M\in\RR
, if
\langle Tx,x\rangle\geq M\langle x,x\rangle
holds for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(T)
.
T
is called (strictly) positive if
T
is bounded below by a (strictly) positive number.
Operators that are bounded below by some number are also called semibounded. If
T
is bounded below by
M
and closable, then
\b{T}
is also bounded below by
M
.

A densely defined operator
T
is called essentially self-adjoint if
\b{T}
is self-adjoint. Proving that an operator is self-adjoint can be difficult. Below we will make use of the following criterion.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
62
, Theorem X.26]
)
.

Let
T
be a strictly positive symmetric operator. Then the following are equivalent

{enuma}

T
is essentially self-adjoint.

T
has dense image.

T
has only one semibounded self-adjoint extension.

For a closed operator
T
one defines its spectrum as follows. A complex number
\lambda
is said to be in the resolvent set of
T
if
\lambda-T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\to\mathscr{H}
is a bijection. The resolvent set is denoted
\rho(T)
. The complement of the resolvent set is the spectrum of
T
and is denoted by
\sigma(T)
.

The following lemma is a simple consequence of the spectral theorem for unbounded operators (see
[
64
, Theorem VIII.4]
or
[
65
, Theorem 12.32]
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
T
be a self-adjoint operator on
\calH
. Then
T
is bounded below by
M
if and only if
\sigma(T)\subset[M,\infty)
.

A basic problem in perturbation theory is to show that
A+B
inherits the properties of
A
if
B
is not too large compared to
A
. This idea of a “small perturbation” is made precise in the following definition.
Let
A
and
B
be densely defined operators on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. The operator
B
is said to be
A
-bounded if
\operatorname{dom}(A)\subset\operatorname{dom}(B)
and if there exist
a,b\in\RR
such

(21)

\|Bx\|\leq a\|Ax\|+b\|x\|

for all
x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)
.

Theorem 5.3

(Kato–Rellich theorem,
[
62
, Theorem X.12]
)
.

Suppose that
A
is self-adjoint,
B
is symmetric, and
B
is
A
-bounded with
a<1
. Then
A+B
is self-adjoint on
\operatorname{dom}(A)
. Further, if
A
is bounded below by
M
, then
A+B
is bounded below by
M-\max\{b/(1-a),a|M|+b\}
, where
a
and
b
are given by (
21
). In particular, if
A
is strictly positive and
b=0
, then
A+B
is strictly positive.

We are going to apply the Kato–Rellich theorem to very special perturbations. The most transparent way to present our argument is to formulate a general statement.

Lemma 5.4

(Homotopy lemma)
.

Let
T\colon\operatorname{dom}(T)\to\mathscr{H}
be an essentially self-adjoint, strictly positive operator on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. Let
S_{t}\colon\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}
,
t\in[0,1]
, be a family of bounded operators with the following properties:

{enuma}

S_{0}=\id
;

t\mapsto S_{t}
is continuous in the operator norm;

each
S_{t}
is invertible;

the operators
T_{t}:=S_{t}\circ T
are symmetric.

Then
T_{t}
is for every
t\in[0,1]
an essentially self-adjoint, strictly positive operator satisfying
\operatorname{dom}(\b{T}_{t})=\operatorname{dom}(\b{T})
.

Proof 5.5
.

First of all observe that since each
S_{t}
is invertible also
t\mapsto S_{t}^{-1}
is continuous in the operator norm.
Let
C:=\max_{t\in[0,1]}\|S_{t}^{-1}\|<\infty
. By continuity of
t\mapsto S_{t}
on the compact interval
[0,1]
, we find
\epsilon>0
such that

\|S_{t_{1}}-S_{t_{2}}\|\leq\frac{1}{2C}

whenever
|t_{1}-t_{2}|<\epsilon
.

Since
\id\oplus S_{t}
is an invertible operator on
\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}
, the closures satisfy
\b{T}_{t}=S_{t}\circ\b{T}
and
\operatorname{dom}(\b{T}_{t})=\operatorname{dom}(\b{T})
. Since
\b{T}_{t_{1}}-\b{T}_{t_{2}}=(S_{t_{1}}-S_{t_{2}})\circ S_{t_{1}}^{-1}\circ\b{T}_{t_{1}}
, we obtain for
|t_{1}-t_{2}|<\epsilon
and
x\in\operatorname{dom}(\b{T})
that

\displaystyle\|(\b{T}_{t_{1}}-\b{T}_{t_{2}})x\|

\displaystyle\leq\|S_{t_{1}}-S_{t_{2}}\|\cdot\|S_{t_{1}}^{-1}\|\cdot\|\b{T}_{t_{1}}x\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\b{T}_{t_{1}}x\|.

Hence
\b{T}_{t_{2}}
is a small perturbation of
\b{T}_{t_{1}}
in the sense of the Kato–Rellich theorem, with
a=\frac{1}{2}
and
b=0
. Thus
\b{T}_{t_{2}}
is self-adjoint and strictly positive if
\b{T}_{t_{1}}
has these properties. Iterating this argument finitely many times we see that each
\b{T}_{t}
is self-adjoint and strictly positive since
\b{T}=\b{T}_{0}
has these properties.

Primitive valuations

Assume

5.3.
Proof of the Hodge–Riemann relations

Our strategy will be to fit the Hodge–Riemann relations for smooth valuations into a framework where we can apply the Kato–Rellich theorem in the form of the homotopy lemma (Lemma

Let
\mathbf{B}
denote the tuple of reference bodies

Consider on

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{B}}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\phi*L_{\mathbf{B}_{\setminus 0}}\bar{\psi},

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathbf{C}}^{*}q_{\mathbf{C}}(\phi,\psi)

\displaystyle=q_{\mathbf{C}}(\pi_{\mathbf{C}}\phi,\pi_{\mathbf{C}}\psi)=(-1)^{k}\pi_{\mathbf{C}}\phi*(L_{\mathbf{C}_{\setminus 0}}\circ\pi_{\mathbf{C}}\bar{\psi}),

This is clear, since

6.
Spectral properties

In the final section we will investigate the properties of the Hodge–Riemann sesquilinear form
q_{\mathbf{C}}
as a quadratic form on the Hilbert spaces
\mathscr{P}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathscr{E}}
and
\mathscr{P}_{\mathbf{C}}^{\mathscr{F}}
. We will complete the proof of the Hodge–Riemann relations by a direct computation in the case where all reference bodies are euclidean balls, which is the initial point in the homotopy argument in the proof of Proposition
5.17
. We also clarify our choice of the Hilbert space
\mathscr{E}
instead of
\mathscr{F}
in this last section by showing that the properties of the Hodge–Riemann sesquilinear form on
\mathscr{E}
and
\mathscr{F}
are very distinct. The former is closable and has a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities accumulating at
+\infty
, while the latter is bounded and has a spectrum of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities accumulating at
0
. Using the
\mathscr{E}
completion, we may relax the smoothness assumption in Theorem
1.1
.

6.1.
Further facts about self-adjoint operators

Our proofs will rely on certain properties of self-adjoint operators that, for convenience of the reader, we review in this section.

An operator
A
with the property that
(A-\mu)^{-1}
is compact for all
\mu\in\rho(A)
is said to have compact resolvent. We will need the following characterization of such operators.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
63
, Theorem XIII.64]
)
.

Let
A
be a semibounded self-adjoint operator on
\mathscr{H}
. Then the following are equivalent:

{enuma}

is compact for some
\mu\in\rho(A)
.

(A-\mu)^{-1}
is compact for all
\mu\in\rho(A)
.

\{x\in\operatorname{dom}(A)\colon\|x\|\leq 1\text{ and }\|Ax\|\leq b\}
is compact for all
b
.

There exists a complete orthonormal basis
(x_{n})_{n\in\NN}
in
\operatorname{dom}(A)
so that
Ax_{n}=\mu_{n}x_{n}
with
\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots
and
\mu_{n}\to\infty
.

The spectrum of

is discrete and consists of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities.

Next, we need some facts about quadratic forms and refer to

64
, Section VIII.6]
for details. By a quadratic form

on a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
we will understand a sesquilinear form

defined on a dense subspace
\operatorname{dom}(q)
of

. If there is a number

such that

holds for all

, then

is said to be semibounded. If
\mathscr{H}
is complex, this implies that

is symmetric, i.e.,

for all

. If
M\geq 0
, then

is said to be positive. If
|q(x,x)|\leq M\|x\|^{2}
for some

and all

, then

is called bounded.

Let
q
be a quadratic form bounded below by
M
.

is called closed if
\operatorname{dom}(q)
is complete under the norm

\|x\|_{+1}=\sqrt{q(x,x)+(1-M)\|x\|^{2}}.

We will need the following fact.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
62
, Problem 15, Chapter VIII]
)
.

A semibounded quadratic form
q
is closed if and only if whenever

x_{n}\in\operatorname{dom}(q),\quad x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathscr{H}}}{{\to}}x,\quad\text{and}\quad q(x_{n}-x_{m},x_{n}-x_{m})\underset{m,n\to\infty}{\to}0,

and
q(x_{n}-x,x_{n}-x)\to 0
. If this is the case, we have

.

The closure of a quadratic form
q
, if it exists, is the smallest closed quadratic form which extends it. If a closure of
q
exists, then
q
is called closable.

Theorem 6.3

(Friedrichs extension theorem
[
62
, Theorem X.23]
)

Let
