On root-ratio multipoint methods for finding multiple zeros of univariate functions
Miodrag S. Petković, Ljiljana D. Petković, [2mm] Faculty of Electronic Engineering, University of Niš[-1mm] A. Medvedeva 14, 18000 Niš, Serbia

Abstract
Several root-ratio multipoint methods for finding multiple zeros of univariate functions were recently presented. The characteristic of these methods is that they deal with m m -th root of ratio of two functions (hence the name root-ratio methods), where m m is the multiplicity of the sought zero, known in advance. Some of these methods were presented without any derivation and motivation, it could be said, out of the blue. In this paper we present an easy and entirely natural way for constructing root-ratio multipoint iterative methods starting from multipoint methods for finding simple zeros. In this way, a vast number of root-ratio multipoint methods for multiple zeros, existing as well new ones, can be constructed. For demonstration, we derive four root-ratio methods for multiple zeros. Besides, we study computational cost of the considered methods and give a comparative analysis that involves CPU time needed for the extraction of the m m -th root. This analysis shows that root-ratio methods are pretty inefficient from the computational point of view and, thus, not suitable in practice. A general discussion on a practical need for multipoint methods of very high order is also considered. AMS Mathematical Subject Classification (2010): 65H05. Keywords: Solving nonlinear equations; Multipoint methods; Multiple zeros; Root-ratio approach; Computational efficiency.

Introduction

In the last ten years a lot of papers were published in the topic of iterative methods of optimal order for finding multiple zeros of univariate functions. Among them, several methods were constructed using
root-parameter approach
dealing with parameters computed by extracting
m
-th root of some values. In this paper this class of methods will be called
root-ratio methods
. We concentrate on the two issuses: (i) computational cost of these methods and (ii) demonstration of an easy procedure for natural construction of root-ratio methods starting from methods for finding simple zeros.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we present three two-point root-ratio methods of optimal order four published in
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
. In Section 3 we expose three tree-point root-ratio methods of optimal order eight proposed in
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
. An easy and fully natural procedure for constructing root-ratio methods of optimal order, demonstrated by three examples, is presented in Section 4. The computational efficiency of root-ratio methods is given in Section 5 applying a comparative analysis that involves CPU time needed for the extraction of the
m
-th root. This analysis has shown that root-ratio methods are pretty expensive and not-competitive with multipoint methods which do not require the extraction of the root even if the latter ones have lower order of convergence. See Remark 8 at the end of the paper.

2
Two-point fourth order methods

Let
\alpha
be a zero of the known multiplicity
m\geq 1
of a differentiable function
f,
and
k=0,1,\ldots
be the iteration index. We will assume that the chosen initial approximation
x_{0}
is sufficiently close to the zero
\alpha
so that the considered iterative processes are convergent.
We start with two-point root-ratio methods for finding multiple zero of functions of one variable. Zhou et al. proposed in
[
2
]
the following family of iterative two-step methods for finding multiple zeros:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-mG(u_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad u_{k}=\Bigl(\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})}\Bigr)^{1/m}\end{array}\right.\quad(k=0,1,\ldots).

(1)

Under the conditions
G(0)=0,\ G^{\prime}(0)=1,\ G^{\prime\prime}(0)=4,\ G^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)<+\infty,
the order of convergence of the methods (
1
) is four. The iterative formula was derived correctly but in a complicated way.

Lee et al. constructed in
[
3
]
the following family of fourth order methods:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\cdot\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})+\lambda f(x_{k})},\\[13.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-mW(u_{k})\cdot\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{n})+2\lambda f(x_{k})},\quad u_{k}=\Bigl(\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})}\Bigr)^{1/m},\end{array}\right.

(2)

where

W(u)=\frac{u(1+(c+2)u+ru^{2})}{1+cu}

and
\lambda,\ c,\ r
are arbitrary parameters.

Remark 1.
The parameter
\lambda
in (
2
) does not improve convergence characteristics of the methods (
2
). Indeed, if
m\geq 2
, then both values
f^{\prime}(x_{k})
and
f(x_{k})
are very small in magnitude if the approximation
x_{k}
is sufficiently close to the zero
\alpha
of
f
and, consequently, the parameter
\lambda
does not play any important role so that one can take
\lambda=0
in (
2
) without loss of generality. The derivatives of
W
are given by

W^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1+2(c+2)u+(2c+c^{2}+3r)u^{2}+2cru^{3}}{(1+cu)^{2}},\quad W^{\prime\prime}(u)=\frac{4+2ru(3+3cu+c^{2}u^{2})}{(1+cu)^{3}}.

Setting
u=0
one obtains
W(0)=0,\ \ W^{\prime}(0)=1,\ \ W^{\prime\prime}(0)=4.
Therefore, the method (
2
) is essentially a special case of the more general method (
1
).

Using root-ratio approach in a slightly different way, Liu et al. derived in
[
4
]
the family of two-point iterative methods

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-mG(t_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad t_{k}=\Bigl(\dfrac{f^{\prime}(y_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}\end{array}\right.\quad(k=0,1,\ldots),

(3)

which converges with the order of convergence four under the conditions

G(0)=0,\quad G^{\prime}(0)=1,\quad G^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{4m}{m-1}.

The family (
3
) cannot be applied for finding simple zeros (
m=1
), which is an obvious disadvantage. This drawback limits its applications in composite algorithms where a zero-finding method is part of the algorithm in which the case
m=1
is possible; actually, this case often appears in practical problems (for example, solving engineering problems).

All three two-point methods (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) are
optimal
in the sense of Kung-Traub conjecture
[
8
]
:
The highest order of convergence of an
n
-point method requiring
n+1
function evaluations is
2^{n}
.

4
Can we construct them easier?

In this section we show that some of the above-presented methods can be derived using an easy way, well known in the literature for almost 150 years. The idea was known to German mathematician E. Schröder, see his paper
[
9
]
and the English translation
[
10
]
, and goes as follows in the case of Newton’s method

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\quad(k=0,1,\ldots)

applied to the function
f
having a zero
\alpha
of multiplicity
m\geq 1.

4.1
Schröder’s
f^{1/m}
-approach

Let
F(x)=f(x)^{1/m}.
Then
\alpha
is a simple zero of the function
F.
Applying Newton’s method to
F
, we obtain

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{F(x_{k})}{F^{\prime}(x_{k})}=x_{k}-\frac{f(x)^{1/m}}{\frac{1}{m}f^{\prime}(x)f(x)^{1/m-1}}

and hence

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-m\frac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\quad(k=0,1,\ldots),

which is well-known Schröder’s method for finding multiple zero of the known multiplicity
m.
This useful idea was applied in many papers during the last 70 years.

Schröder’s approach can be applied to multipoint methods for finding a simple zero in order to construct corresponding multipoint mehods for multiple zeros. As above, let
f
be a function having a zero of the known multiplicity
m\geq 1.
First, we set
F(x)=f(x)^{1/m}
(Schröder’s
f^{1/m}
-approach) and derive the following relations:

\displaystyle\frac{F(x)}{F^{\prime}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(x)^{1/m}}{\frac{1}{m}f^{\prime}(x)f(x)^{1/m-1}}=m\frac{f(x)}{f^{\prime}(x)},

\displaystyle\frac{F(y)}{F^{\prime}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(y)^{1/m}}{\frac{1}{m}f^{\prime}(x)f(x)^{1/m-1}}=m\frac{f(x)}{f^{\prime}(x)}\Bigl(\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}\Bigr)^{1/m}=mu\frac{f(x)}{f^{\prime}(x)},\quad u=\Bigl(\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}\Bigr)^{1/m},

\displaystyle\frac{F(z)}{F^{\prime}(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(z)^{1/m}\cdot\frac{f(y)^{1/m}}{f(y)^{1/m}}}{\frac{1}{m}f^{\prime}(x)f(x)^{1/m-1}}=m\frac{f(x)}{f^{\prime}(x)}\Bigl(\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}\Bigr)^{1/m}\Bigl(\frac{f(z)}{f(y)}\Bigr)^{1/m}=muv\frac{f(x)}{f^{\prime}(x)},\quad v=\Bigl(\frac{f(z)}{f(y)}\Bigr)^{1/m},

\displaystyle\frac{F(y)}{F(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(y)^{1/m}}{f(x)^{1/m}}=u,

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{F(z)}{F(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(z)^{1/m}}{f(y)^{1/m}}=v,

\displaystyle\frac{F(z)}{F(x)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f(z)^{1/m}}{f(x)^{1/m}}=w=uv.

4.2
Eighth-order family
(
4
)
derived by Schröder’s approach

For demonstration, we will apply the presented Schröder’s approach to the

Assume that

where

u_{k}=\frac{F(y_{k})}{F(x_{k})},\quad v_{k}=\frac{F(z_{k})}{F(y_{k})}.

Using the relations (
9
), the family (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]

(13)

The weight functions

) when

According to Theorem 2, it follows that the three-point family (
12
) is
optimal
.

The simplest forms of the weight functions
P
and
Q
are their truncated Taylor series

P(u)=1+2u+\beta\,u^{2}+(4-2\beta)\,u^{3},\quad Q(u,v)=1+2u+v+4uv+(\beta+1)u^{2},\quad\beta\ \mbox{\rm is arbitrary}.

Finding a list of particular weight functions
P
and
Q
is a routine work and it is left to the interested reader as exercise.

4.3
Eighth-order family
(
7
)
derived by Schröder’s approach

The presented Schröder’s
f^{1/m}
-approach can also be applied for the derivation of the method (
7
). Let us start from the following
three-point method for simple zeros

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
z_{k}=y_{k}-u_{k}H(u_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=z_{k}-u_{k}P(u_{k})G(v_{k})L(w_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\end{array}\right.\quad(k=0,1,\ldots),

(17)

where

u_{k}=\frac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})},\quad v_{k}=\frac{f(z_{k})}{f(y_{k})},\quad w_{k}=\frac{f(z_{k})}{f(x_{k})}\,.

This method has the order 8 under the conditions

\displaystyle H(0)=1,\ H^{\prime}(0)=2,\ P^{\prime}(0)=2P(0),\ P^{\prime\prime}(0)=P(0)(2+H^{\prime\prime}(0)),

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(0)=2L(0),\ P^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=P(0)(H^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)+6H^{\prime\prime}(0)-24),

(18)

\displaystyle G(0)=0,\ G^{\prime}(0)=\dfrac{1}{L(0)P(0)},\ G^{\prime\prime}(0)=\dfrac{2}{L(0)P(0)}.

The denotation of weight functions in (
17
) and (
18
) is adjusted to the denotation used in
[
7
]
.

Applying Schröder’s
f^{1/m}
-approach and (
9
) we obtain the family of three-point methods for finding multiple zeros

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
z_{k}=y_{k}-mu_{k}H(u_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=z_{k}-mu_{k}P(u_{k})G^{*}(v_{k})L(w_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}\end{array}\right.\quad(k=0,1,\ldots),

(19)

which is equivalent to (
7
) setting
mG^{*}(v)=G(v)
(compare (
7
) and (
19
)).

4.4
A modification of the family
(
7
)

Since

w=\Bigl(\frac{f(z)}{f(x)}\Bigr)^{1/m}=\Bigl(\frac{f(y)}{f(x)}\Bigr)^{1/m}\Bigl(\frac{f(z)}{f(y)}\Bigr)^{1/m}=u\cdot v,

the weight function
L(w)
can be omitted in (
7
). Then we can construct in an easy way the following family of three-point methods for finding multiple zeros involving two parametric functions

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
z_{k}=y_{k}-mu_{k}H(u_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=z_{k}-mu_{k}v_{k}(1+2u_{k}v_{k})P(u_{k})G(v_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})}.\end{array}\right.

(20)

Making suitable changes in the above program in
Mathematica
(see §  4.2), we prove that the method (
20
) is of order eight under the following conditions:

\displaystyle H(0)=1,\ \ H^{\prime}(0)=2,\ \ H^{\prime\prime}(0)=m+9,

\displaystyle P(0)=1,\ \ P^{\prime}(0)=2,\ \ P^{\prime\prime}(0)=m+11,\ \ P^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=30+6m+H^{\prime\prime\prime}(0),

\displaystyle G(0)=1,\ \ H^{\prime}(0)=1.

4.5
Fourth order methods for multiple zeros

Chun constructed in
[
13
]
the following two-point family of iterative methods for finding simple zeros

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{h(u_{k})f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad u_{k}=\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})},\end{array}\right.

(21)

where
h(u)
is the weight function. The method (
21
) is of fourth order under the condition
h(0)=1,\ h^{\prime}(0)=-1,\ |h^{\prime\prime}(0)|<+\infty.
To generate suitable two-point methods, sometimes it is necessary to develop the function
h
into Taylor’s or geometric series.

A slightly more direct approach without altering the weight function, based on Chun’s idea, was given in
[
14
]
in the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-p(u_{k})\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad u_{k}=\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})},\end{array}\right.

(22)

The family (
22
) possesses the optimal order four if
p(0)=1,\ p^{\prime}(0)=2
and
|p^{\prime\prime}(0)|<+\infty.

Proceeding in the same way as in the case of the family of three-point methods (
10
) and using (
9
),
we obtain from (
22
) the fourth order two-point family of iterative methods for finding multiple zeros

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-m\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=y_{k}-mu_{k}P(u_{k})\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad u_{k}=\Bigl(\dfrac{f(y_{k})}{f(x_{k})}\Bigr)^{1/m},\end{array}\right.

(23)

where
P(u)
is the weight function which satisfies

P(0)=1,\ \ P^{\prime}(0)=2,

(24)

that is, its truncated Taylor series is

P(u)=1+2u.

This result is expected since the iterative formula (
23
) coincides with the first two steps of the family (
12
). Explicit expression of
\hat{\varepsilon}=\hat{x}-\alpha
(regarding (
23
)) is given by (
15
) (for
\varepsilon_{z}
).

Several examples of functions that satisfy (
24
) are listed below.

\displaystyle P(u)=\frac{1+\beta u}{1+(\beta-2)u}\ \ \mbox{\rm(of King's type, see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{king}{}{}]})},\quad P(u)=\Bigl(1+\frac{2u}{r}\Bigr)^{r},

\displaystyle P(u)=\frac{1+\gamma u^{2}}{1-2u},\quad P(u)=\frac{1}{1-2u+au^{2}},\quad P(u)=\frac{u^{2}+(c-2)u-1}{cu-1}.

where
r\in\mbox{\boldmath{$Q$}}
and
\gamma,\ a,\ c\in\mbox{\boldmath{$R$}}
are arbitrary parameters.

Some words about the equivalence of the methods presented in Section 2 and 3, and the methods derived by Schröder’s approach in Section 4.

Equivalence 1.
In Section 2 it was shown that the method (

Is there a good alternative method which is efficient and convenient for applications? The answer is yes, and it is very likely known to
many authors who work in the area of iterative processes. Li et al. proposed in

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-\frac{2m}{m+2}\cdot\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]

(27)

Zhou et al.
[
18
]
later proposed the generalization of the method (
27
) in the form

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{k}=x_{k}-\frac{2m}{m+2}\cdot\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\\[9.0pt]
x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\phi(t_{k})\cdot\dfrac{f(x_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\quad t_{k}=\dfrac{f^{\prime}(y_{k})}{f^{\prime}(x_{k})},\end{array}\right.

(28)

which has the order four under the specific conditions for the weight function
\phi.
See, also,
[
19
]
. The notion of “convenient” is explained in Remark 8.

We conclude this paper with two remarks of general interest.

Remark 7.
The presence of arbitrary parameters in any zero-finding iterative formula makes sense only if these parameters improve characteristics of presented methods (such as acceleration of convergence, wider domain of convergence, more accurate approximations, etc.). Otherwise, from an algorithmic point of view, free parameters should be chosen so that an iterative formula is as simple as possible – numerical analysts and programmers will always choose the simpler formula in such a way that the best characteristics of the employed methods are maintained. Inserting numerous useless parameters does not make a method better or more general in the genuine sense. Unfortunately, many authors construct “novel” iterative formulas by adding parameters in an artificial way or by varying different weight functions. In essence, such methods are only modest modifications of existing methods and offer a little contribution to the topic.

Remark 8.

It should be emphasized that very high accuracy of solutions of nonlinear equations, provided by root-solvers of order eight or more, is not needed for solving a huge number of practical problems; fourth order methods (such as (
27
) and (
28
)) produce quite satisfactory results in practice. The question
“how many decimals of zero approximations do we really need in practice?”
is equivalent to the question
“how many decimals of
\pi
do we really need in practice”?”
A pretty convincing answer can be found in the issue of
NASA/JPL Edu
, March 16, 2016:

\bullet
For interplanetary navigation with spacecraft Voyager 1 (launched in 1977, distant from Earth about 22 billion km), Jet Propulsion Labaratory (California Institute of California, Pasadena, USA) and NASA use very accurate calculations involving
\pi
with most 15 decimal digits! Not more! The distance error is about 5 cm!

\bullet
The radius of the visible universe is about 46 billion light years. To express the circumference of a circle with this radius via the diameter a hydrogen atom (the simplest atom) we need at most 40 decimal digits of
\pi!
