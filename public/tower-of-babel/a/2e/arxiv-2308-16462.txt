It is known that shock electron heating is described by an ambipolar, 𝑩 \bm{B} -parallel electric potential jump, Δ ​ ϕ ∥ \Delta\phi_{\parallel} , that scales roughly linearly with the electron temperature jump. Our simulations have Δ ​ ϕ ∥ / ( 0.5 ​ m i ​ u sh 2 ) ∼ 0.1 \Delta\phi_{\parallel}/(0.5m_{\mathrm{i}}{u_{\mathrm{sh}}}^{2})\sim 0.1 – 0.2 0.2 in units of the pre-shock ions’ bulk kinetic energy, in agreement with prior measurements and simulations. I
Introduction

Shocks heat and compress their host medium.
In an ordinary fluid, collisions mediate heating; different particle species in
the fluid share the same temperature after passing through a shock.
In a low-density collisionless plasma, like the solar wind or
interstellar medium, shocks partition their energy between various plasma waves
and sub-populations of ions and electrons in a more complex way.
We wish to know how much energy goes to electrons, and how and where within the
shock the energy is gained, in order to
(1) provide sub-grid electron heating prescriptions that may be used in
two-fluid or hybrid fluid/kinetic simulations, and
(2) help interpret observations of heliospheric and astrophysical shocks.

In this study, we take Earth’s quasi-perpendicular, low-beta magnetospheric bow
shock as an exemplar system.
Typical parameters are
upstream plasma beta (thermal/magnetic pressure ratio)
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}\sim 0.1
–
10
,
upstream magnetic field angle from shock normal
\theta_{Bn}\sim 55
–
90^{\circ}
,
and fast magnetosonic Mach number
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ms}}\sim 1
–
10

(
Farris et al. 1993
, Figure 1)
.
At such shocks, electrons may reflect from the shock front or leak from
downstream to upstream, whereas thermal ions are generally confined to the
downstream plasma after crossing the shock.

Electron heating can be described using a well-established cross-shock
potential model
(
Goodrich & Scudder 1984
;
Scudder 1995
;
Hull et al. 1998
)
.
In a shock’s de Hoffmann-Teller (HT) frame, electron and ion bulk flows are
parallel to
\bm{B}
both upstream and downstream.
Entering the shock, a
\bm{B}
-parallel electric potential
\phi_{\parallel}(x)

varying along the shock-normal coordinate
x
boosts electrons’ parallel
velocities.
Small-scale scattering within the shock is expected to cool and smooth the
post-shock electron distribution as compared to the adiabatic Liouville-mapping
limit (i.e., test-particle evolution in static, macroscopic (ion-scale)

\bm{B}(x)
and
\phi_{\parallel}(x)
)

(
Scudder et al. 1986a
;
Schwartz 2014
)
.

The cross-shock potential model works and recovers trends in observed
post-shock temperature anisotropy
(
Scudder et al. 1986a
;
Hull et al. 1998
;
Lefebvre et al. 2007
)
.
But, the model is silent on the origin of small-scale scattering waves required
to create stable post-shock electron distributions
(
Schwartz 2014
)
, e.g.,
flat-tops in Earth’s magnetosheath
(
Feldman et al. 1983b
)
.
Scattering waves diffuse electron velocities and may alter electron pressure;
the electron pressure tensor divergence in Ohm’s law sets
\phi_{\parallel}
, which in
turn sets the free particle energy available to drive scattering waves

(
Veltri et al. 1990
;
Veltri & Zimbardo 1993a
;
Veltri & Zimbardo 1993b
)
.

Wave-particle interactions
also mediate electron heating, in a
complementary view.
Ion-scale whistler waves oblique to
\bm{B}
are a prime suspect.
Low-Mach shocks host large-amplitude whistler precursors with a variety of
propagation angles

(
Ramírez Vélez et al. 2012
;
Wilson et al. 2017
;
Davis et al. 2021
)
.
Whistlers may radiate from the shock ramp

(
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
;
Dimmock et al. 2019
)

or be generated by instabilities in the shock foot

(
Wu et al. 1984
;
Winske et al. 1985
;
Muschietti & Lembège 2017
)
.
These whistlers have low-frequency
\bm{B}
-parallel electric fields that may trap and
heat electrons, and strong electron trapping (parallel electric field energy
comparable to the electrons’ thermal energy) induces phase mixing and
short-wavelength electrostatic structures

(
McBride et al. 1972
;
Matsukiyo & Scholer 2003
;
Matsukiyo & Scholer 2006
;
Matsukiyo 2010
)
.

The connection between scattering waves, electron heating, and the cross-shock
potential (or, ion-scale fields and waves more generally)
is not yet fully understood.
Recent Magnetospheric Multiscale (MMS) mission studies have given new insights.
For example,
Chen et al. 2018
suggest that the cross-shock potential could
arise from the cumulative effect of oblique, non-linear whistler wave

\bm{E}
-fields;
Sun et al. 2022
suggest that strong, Debye-scale
double layers may help form the cross-shock potential.

Cohen et al. 2019
have measured the electrostatic potential jump across an
interplanetary shock, along with collocated, strong, high-frequency
electrostatic fields.

Hull et al. 2020
showed the onset of electron heating as a transition
from adiabatic to non-adiabatic electron distributions over multiple
wavelengths of a shock’s oblique whistler precursor.
Much recent work has been done on electron scattering by coherent low- and
high-frequency whistlers at shocks

(
Hull et al. 2012
;
Wilson et al. 2016
;
Oka et al. 2017
;
Oka et al. 2019
;
Page et al. 2021
;
Artemyev et al. 2022
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
,
including the construction and use of general frameworks such as stochastic
shock drift acceleration
(
Katou & Amano 2019
;
Amano et al. 2020
)
and magnetic pumping

(
Lichko & Egedal 2020
)
, with particular success in explaining non-thermal
distributions.

In this manuscript, we use 1D and 2D particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations of
collisionless ion-electron shocks to
measure the strength and structure of the cross-shock potential.
We adopt
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25
(
\beta_{\mathrm{e}}=\beta_{\mathrm{i}}=0.125
), reasonable albeit on the low end
for Earth’s bow shock
(
Farris et al. 1993
)
, which emphasizes the shock’s
whistler-mode structure and reduces computational cost.
Our study builds upon prior fully-kinetic studies of quasi-perpendicular
shocks by

Forslund et al. 1984
;
Lembège & Dawson 1987
;
Liewer et al. 1991
;
Savoini & Lembege 1994
;
Krauss-Varban et al. 1995
;
Savoini & Lembege 1995
;
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
;
Scholer & Burgess 2007
;
Riquelme & Spitkovsky 2011
;
Bohdan et al. 2022
;
Morris et al. 2022
,
and it complements recent simulations focused on perpendicular or
nearly-perpendicular (
\theta_{Bn}\geq 80^{\circ}
) shocks

(
Matsukiyo & Scholer 2003
;
Scholer et al. 2003
;
Scholer & Burgess 2006
;
Matsukiyo & Scholer 2006
;
Matsukiyo & Scholer 2012
;
Umeda et al. 2012
;
Yang et al. 2018
;
Tran & Sironi 2020
)
.

We seek to address a few general questions.

First
, how much do electrons heat (
\Delta T_{\mathrm{e}}
,
T_{\mathrm{e}}/T_{\mathrm{i}}
) across
quasi-perpendicular shocks?
Does heating occur at the ramp (steepest density rise), foot (region in which
shock-reflected ions execute a single Larmor gyration), or farther upstream
within a precursor?
How does the heating amount and location depend on shock parameters (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ms}}
,

\theta_{Bn}
) and simulation parameters (mass ratio, domain
dimensionality)?

Second
, what is the origin and nature of the cross-shock potential in
PIC simulations?
What structure does
\phi_{\parallel}(x)
possess along the shock-normal coordinate

x
, beyond approximations such as
\phi_{\parallel}(x)\propto B(x)

(
Hull et al. 2000
;
Hull & Scudder 2000
;
Artemyev et al. 2022
)
?
How wide is the potential jump in
x
?

Third
, because
\phi_{\parallel}
is not easy to directly measure in the
heliosphere, how faithfully do various observational proxies (e.g., the
HT-frame electric field) reproduce
\phi_{\parallel}
?

II
Shock Setup

II.1
Simulation Methods

We simulate 1D and 2D ion-electron shocks using the relativistic
particle-in-cell (PIC) code TRISTAN-MP
(
Buneman 1993
;
Spitkovsky 2005
)
.
Our setup follows
Sironi & Spitkovsky 2009
;
Guo et al. 2014
;
Guo et al. 2017
;
Tran & Sironi 2020
.
We use Gaussian CGS units throughout.

To form a shock, we inject upstream plasma with velocity
-u_{0}\hat{\bm{x}}
that
reflects off a conducting wall at
x=0
in a uniform Cartesian

(x,y,z)
grid.
Coordinate unit vectors are
\hat{\bm{x}},\hat{\bm{y}},\hat{\bm{z}}
.
The reflected plasma interacts with upstream plasma to drive a shock
traveling towards
+\hat{\bm{x}}
.
The domain’s left-side (
x=0
) boundary specularly reflects all particles in the
simulation frame (
v_{x}\to-v_{x}
).
The domain’s right-side
x
boundary expands ahead of the shock, continuously
adding new plasma to the upstream; the expansion speed is manually chosen to
out-run waves, thermal electrons leaking from downstream to upstream,
and mirror-reflected (shock-drift accelerated) particles that may all travel
ahead of the shock.
Particles and waves exiting the right-side
x
boundary are deleted.
The
y
and
z
boundaries are periodic, and we simulate 2D shocks in the

x
-
y
plane.
All three Cartesian components of particle velocities and electromagnetic
fields are tracked in both 1D and 2D shocks.

The injected upstream plasma comprises Maxwellian ions and electrons with equal
density
n_{0}
and temperature
T_{0}
, carrying a magnetic field
\bm{B}
with
magnitude
B_{0}
and angle
\theta_{Bn}
with respect to shock normal
(
\hat{\bm{n}}=\hat{\bm{x}}
).
The upstream
\bm{B}
lies in the
x
-
y
plane.
The ion and electron plasma frequencies

\omega_{\mathrm{p\{i,e\}}}=\sqrt{4\pi n_{0}e^{2}/m_{\mathrm{\{i,e\}}}}
,
inertial lengths
d_{\mathrm{\{i,e\}}}=c/\omega_{\mathrm{p\{i,e\}}}
,
and cyclotron frequencies

\Omega_{\mathrm{c\{i,e\}}}=eB_{0}/(m_{\mathrm{\{i,e\}}}c)

are defined with respect to upstream plasma properties.
Subscripts
\mathrm{i}
and
\mathrm{e}
denote ions and electrons respectively.
Subscripts
\perp
and
\parallel
are generally defined with respect to
local
\bm{B}
.

A shock is specified by several dimensionless parameters:

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
, (or
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ms}}
),
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}
,
\theta_{Bn}
,
\Gamma
, and
c_{\mathrm{s}}/c
.
The total plasma beta
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}=16\pi n_{0}k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}/{B_{0}}^{2}
.
The fast magnetosonic, sonic, and Alfvén Mach numbers are

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ms}}=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/\sqrt{{c_{\mathrm{s}}}^{2}+{v_{\mathrm{A}}}^{2}}
,

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/c_{\mathrm{s}}
, and

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/v_{\mathrm{A}}

respectively.

1

1

The Mach number
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ms}}
is defined with respect to the MHD fast speed
for propagation perpendicular to
\bm{B}
.

Here,
u_{\mathrm{sh}}
is upstream plasma speed in the shock’s rest frame.
The sound speed
c_{\mathrm{s}}=\sqrt{2\Gamma k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}/(m_{\mathrm{i}}+m_{\mathrm{e}})}

and the Alfvén speed
v_{\mathrm{A}}=B_{0}/\sqrt{4\pi n_{0}(m_{\mathrm{i}}+m_{\mathrm{e}})}
,
with
\Gamma
the one-fluid adiabatic index.
We scale all velocities with respect to
c
by choosing any of the ratios

c_{\mathrm{s}}/c\propto v_{\mathrm{A}}/c\propto(\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}})^{-1}\propto\sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}/(m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2})}
,
for fixed
\{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}},\beta_{\mathrm{p}},m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}\}
.

How do we set
u_{0}
to obtain a desired Mach number?
In the simulation frame, the downstream (post-shock) plasma has bulk
u_{x}=0
,
and the shock speed is
u_{\mathrm{sh}}/r
, where
r
is the post-shock compression
ratio. We relate
u_{0}
and
u_{\mathrm{sh}}
by an explicit boost:

u_{0}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{sh}}-u_{\mathrm{sh}}/r}{1-({u_{\mathrm{sh}}}^{2}/r)/c^{2}}

taking both
u_{0},u_{\mathrm{sh}}>0
.
To compute
r=r(\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}},\Gamma,\beta_{\mathrm{p}},\theta_{Bn})
from the MHD Rankine-Hugoniot
(R-H) conditions
(
Tidman & Krall 1971
)
, we must guess the effective one-fluid
adiabatic index
\Gamma
, which is not known
a priori
for our
kinetic plasma.
We adopt
\Gamma=5/3
to set
u_{0}
for all simulations in this manuscript.
We expect
\Gamma\approx 5/3
for stronger or more oblique shocks with good
coupling between ion velocities parallel and perpendicular to
\bm{B}
.
But
\Gamma\approx 2
, corresponding to a 2D non-relativistic gas, may be more
appropriate for weak, nearly perpendicular shocks with little ion
isotropization.
Our choice of
\Gamma=5/3
to initialize shocks that are best described by

\Gamma\approx 2
can cause our stated Mach numbers to be inaccurate by

\mathord{\sim}10\%
or less.

We define the shock position
x_{\mathrm{sh}}
to be located where the magnetic
fluctuation
B/B_{0}-1
declines to one-half its maximum value, measured
rightwards (increasing
x
) from the maximum value of
B/B_{0}
just after the
shock ramp.

In oblique MHD shocks (i.e.,
\theta_{Bn}\neq 90^{\circ}
) with
\bm{B}
in the

x
-
y
plane, upstream plasma with bulk velocity
u_{y}=0

acquire a transverse drift
u_{y}\neq 0
after transiting the shock.
Our left-side (
x=0
) boundary accommodates this drift by imposing

E_{z}=(u_{y}/c)B_{0}\cos\theta_{Bn}
in the 10 left-most
x
-cells of the Yee mesh.
Particles reflect 5
x
-cells rightwards of the imposed-field region.
The boundary
u_{y}
is computed from the MHD R-H jump conditions, and it is
typically
\mathord{\sim}10\%
of
u_{\mathrm{sh}}

(
Tidman & Krall 1971
, Figures 1.5–1.6)
.
The boundary mimics a conducting wall that slides in the
y
direction.

Our choice of particle reflection procedure at the left boundary can alter
post-shock electron distributions.
Specular reflection,
v_{x}\to-v_{x}
, mixes field-parallel and perpendicular
particle momenta in the downstream rest frame and thus may alter particle
distributions’ anisotropy.
Other choices are possible, e.g.,
Krauss-Varban et al. 1995

let particles escape the domain at both upstream and downstream domain
boundaries, with new particles continuously injected from Maxwellian
distributions.
In nature, the post-shock boundary condition may be set by global system
effects;
e.g.,
Mitchell et al. 2012
;
Mitchell & Schwartz 2013
;
Mitchell & Schwartz 2014
;
Schwartz et al. 2019
;
Horaites et al. 2021

construct collisionless magnetosheath models wherein electron distributions are
regulated by the two points at which magnetosheath
\bm{B}
field lines
penetrate the bow shock.
Because we do not consider global transport effects upon shock structure,
our simulations can only inform us about electron heating local to a shock,
under the assumption that post-shock electrons catching up to the shock (i.e.,
traveling from downstream to upstream) have a similar distribution as those
streaming away from the shock.

Shock Parameters

We fix
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=200
and
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25
;
we vary the magnetic obliquity
\theta_{Bn}=55
–

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=3
–
10
.
The transverse shock width is
5.4\;d_{\mathrm{i}}
for 2D runs.

to
keep post-shock electrons marginally non-relativistic; lower
\Theta_{\mathrm{e}}

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=3
–
4

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=5

\Theta_{\mathrm{e}}=0.007

4.5

V
Parallel Potential Praxis

In quasi-perpendicular shocks where electrons are well-magnetized, and the de
Hoffmann-Teller (HT) frame is accessible (i.e., the shock is subluminal), the
space physics community has an established description of electron heating.
Electron motion,
at 0th order,
is assumed to obey quasi-static, macroscopic fields

\bm{B}(x)
,
\bm{E}(x)
varying only along
x
with no time dependence

(
Feldman et al. 1983b
;
Goodrich & Scudder 1984
;
Scudder et al. 1986a
;
Hull et al. 1998
;
Hull et al. 2000
;
Hull et al. 2001
)
.
By “macroscopic”, we mean that
\bm{B}
and
\bm{E}
are assumed to vary on
ion length scales.
Individual electron trajectories within the HT frame may be modeled as
conserving two invariants, energy
\varepsilon
and magnetic moment
\mu

(
Hull et al. 1998
)
:

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}m_{e}\left[{v_{\perp}}^{2}(x)+{v_{\parallel}}^{2}(x)\right]-e\phi_{\parallel}(x)\;,

(2)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{e}{v_{\perp}}^{2}(x)}{2B(x)}\;,

(3)

where
\phi_{\parallel}
is an electrostatic potential that forms within the shock
transition due to the
\bm{B}
-parallel electric field component,
E_{\parallel}
.
The parallel potential reads:

\phi_{\parallel}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{E_{\parallel}(x)}{\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\;,

(4)

where
\hat{\bm{b}}
is the magnetic field unit vector, and
E_{\parallel}
is set by the
electron momentum equation (i.e., generalized Ohm’s law):

\bm{E}=-\frac{\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}\times\bm{B}}{c}-\frac{\nabla\cdot\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}-\frac{m_{\mathrm{e}}}{e}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\;.

Here,
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron bulk velocity,

\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron pressure tensor,
n_{\mathrm{e}}
is the electron
density, and

\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t=\partial/\partial t+\bm{V}_{e}\cdot\nabla
is the total derivative.
Collisions are neglected.
The inertial term
\mathrm{d}\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}/\mathrm{d}t
is small,
2
2

2

Take
eE_{\mathrm{amb}}\approx\Delta P_{\mathrm{e}}/(n_{\mathrm{e}}L)

and
eE_{\mathrm{inertial}}\approx m_{\mathrm{e}}(\Delta V_{\mathrm{e}})V_{\mathrm{e}}/L
; here

\Delta P_{\mathrm{e}}
,
\Delta V_{\mathrm{e}}
are the shock jumps in electron
pressure and bulk velocity, and
L
is the gradient length.
Suppose
\Delta P_{\mathrm{e}}\sim P_{\mathrm{e}}
and
\Delta V_{\mathrm{e}}\sim V_{\mathrm{e}}
.
Then
E_{\mathrm{amb}}/E_{\mathrm{inertial}}\sim\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}^{-2}(m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}})\sim 10
in our
simulations and is larger in nature.

so the ambipolar term
\nabla\cdot\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
sets the parallel electric
field in all frames:

E_{\parallel}\approx\hat{\bm{b}}\cdot\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}=\frac{-\hat{\bm{b}}\cdot(\nabla\cdot\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}})}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\,.

In the HT frame,
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}
is parallel to
\bm{B}
, up to
usually-small correction terms localized within the shock layer

(
Scudder 1987
)
.
So,
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}\approx\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
as well.
In the following discussion, we treat simulation-frame measurements of

E_{\parallel}
,
\phi_{\parallel}
, and
\bm{B}
as equal to their HT-frame
values, because all three quantities are frame invariant to order

\mathcal{O}(u/c)
in boost velocity
u
.

This quasi-static, macroscopic description is not complete, for shocks host
high-frequency and short-wavelength fields that will scatter electrons and
break the invariants
\mu
and
\varepsilon

(
Veltri et al. 1990
;
Hull et al. 1998
;
See et al. 2013
;
Schwartz 2014
)
.
Within the context of this macroscopic-field model, scattering is required to
obtain physical (stable) electron distributions, and scattering will alter

\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
and therefore
\phi_{\parallel}
as well.
Nevertheless, the macroscopic fields do successfully describe the electron
dynamics observed by spacecraft
(
Scudder et al. 1986a
;
Lefebvre et al. 2007
)
.

Let us now compare various ways to measure
\phi_{\parallel}
, which we summarize
in Figures
6
and
7
for one
example shock simulation.
See also
Schwartz et al. 2021
for a recent, similar discussion
of
\phi_{\parallel}
measurement methods applied to MMS data.

V.1
Liouville Mapping Measurement of the Parallel Potential

We can estimate
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
using Liouville mapping fits of the electron
distribution function, as is commonly done to interpret satellite data

(
Scudder et al. 1986a
;
Schwartz et al. 1988
;
Hull et al. 1998
;
Lefebvre et al. 2007
;
Johlander et al. 2023
)
.
If electrons evolve adiabatically across a shock,
their distribution at small pitch angle will inflate in energy
following Liouville’s theorem;
by comparing upstream and downstream electron distributions, the amount of
energy gain can be fitted to infer
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
.

We extract both upstream (unshocked) and downstream (shocked) electron
distribution function cuts along
p_{\parallel}
by selecting particles
with pitch angle
<15^{\circ}
, all in the HT frame.
Downstream distributions are sampled from
x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}=-18
to
-3d_{\mathrm{i}}
.
Upstream distributions are sampled from

x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}=65d_{\mathrm{i}}
to
105d_{\mathrm{i}}
for
\theta_{Bn}=55^{\circ}
shocks,

45d_{\mathrm{i}}
to
85d_{\mathrm{i}}
for
\theta_{Bn}=65^{\circ}
shocks, and

25d_{\mathrm{i}}
to
65d_{\mathrm{i}}
for
\theta_{Bn}=75^{\circ}
shocks.

Next, we map the sampled upstream particles to a notional
downstream state, specified by a magnetic compression ratio
B_{2}/B_{0}
and
potential jump
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
.
We fix
B_{2}/B_{0}
to its volume-averaged value in the region

x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}=-18
to
-3d_{\mathrm{i}}
, while
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
is a free parameter.
Let
u_{\perp}=\gamma\beta_{\perp}
and
u_{\parallel}=\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
be the
dimensionless four-velocity components of each particle.
The Liouville-mapped particle momenta
u_{\perp 2}
and
u_{\parallel 2}
,
written in terms of the upstream momenta
u_{\perp 0}
and
u_{\parallel 0}
,
are:

\displaystyle u_{\perp 2}

\displaystyle=u_{\perp 0}\sqrt{B_{2}/B_{0}}

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+u_{\parallel 0}^{2}+u_{\perp 0}^{2}}+e\Delta\phi_{\parallel}/(m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2})

(6)

\displaystyle u_{\parallel 2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\gamma_{2}^{2}-1-u_{\perp 2}^{2}}\,.

(7)

The mapping applies to both incoming (
u_{\parallel 0}<0
) and outgoing
(
u_{\parallel 0}>0
) electron populations, so the outgoing electrons are
mapped backwards in time.
We then select particles with pitch angle
<15^{\circ}
and bin in
p_{\parallel}
to
form a Liouville-mapped distribution (Figure
6
,
orange curves), which can be compared to the true downstream distribution
(Figure
6
, blue curve).

The Liouville-mapped distributions are normalized to both momentum- and
real-space volumes.
The momentum-space volume is a cone fixed by the pitch-angle selection.
The real-space volume changes across the shock due to

\bm{B}
-perpendicular compression and

\bm{B}
-parallel velocity change across the shock; the ratio of 3D real-space volume
elements is

\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{2}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}_{0}}=\frac{B_{0}}{B_{2}}\frac{\beta_{\parallel 2}}{\beta_{\parallel 0}}\,.

(8)

In the non-relativistic limit,

\frac{\beta_{\parallel 2}}{\beta_{\parallel 0}}=\left[1-\frac{2e\Delta\phi_{\parallel}}{m_{\mathrm{e}}c^{2}\beta_{\parallel 2}^{2}}+\left(\frac{\beta_{\perp 2}}{\beta_{\parallel 2}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{B_{0}}{B_{2}}\right)\right]^{-1/2}\,.

If two particles are separated along a
\bm{B}
field line as they cross
the potential jump
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
, their time-staggered accelerations
will tend to increase their real-space separation.
This
\Delta\phi_{\parallel}
-induced dilation is most important for small pitch angles
and small initial parallel velocities, i.e.,
\beta_{\parallel 0}\sim 0
.

In contrast to our particle-based procedure, some studies

(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Cohen et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
perform Liouville mapping by shifting the
distribution
f
in energy coordinates,

f(\varepsilon)\to f(\varepsilon+e\Delta\phi_{\parallel})
.
We find general agreement between the two procedures in one-off experiments
with our simulation data.

The Liouville-mapped distributions are fitted to the downstream
distributions using a least-squares fit that minimizes a cost function

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log\left(\frac{f_{\mathrm{downstream}}(p_{\parallel,i})}{f_{\mathrm{mapped}}(p_{\parallel,i})}\right)\right]^{2}\,,

(9)

where
N
is the number of momentum bins.
We separately fit the
p_{\parallel}<0
and
p_{\parallel}>0
parts of
f
within
multiple bands of
f
values, manually chosen to lie below the downstream
flat-top
f
value and above counting noise at high energies.

shows the Liouville mapping
procedure for the 2D,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4

\theta_{Bn}=65^{\circ}

The mean best-fit

(HT-frame
p_{\parallel}<0

The mean best-fit

p_{\parallel}>0

Fit values have a

V.2
Direct Measurement of the Parallel Potential

In PIC simulations, we can measure
\phi_{\parallel}
from global particle and
field information that is not always available in satellite measurements.
We consider only 1D,
y
-averaged measures of
\phi_{\parallel}
and
neglect the possibility of different potential jumps along different
\bm{B}

field lines in 2D.
For this section of the manuscript, angle brackets
\langle\cdots\rangle

denote transverse (
y
-axis) averaging.
Integrals have the same limits as in Equation (
4
).

First, we may measure
E_{\parallel}
directly from the PIC mesh
(Figure
7
(b)):

\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{grid}}=\int\frac{\langle E_{\parallel}\rangle}{\langle\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle}\;\mathrm{d}x\,.

(10)

Second, we may measure
E_{\parallel}
from the electron pressure tensor
divergence (Figure
7
(c)):

\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{amb}}=\int\left\langle\frac{-\hat{\bm{b}}\cdot\left(\nabla\cdot\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}\right)}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\right\rangle\frac{1}{\langle\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle}\mathrm{d}x\,.

(11)

We compute
\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
on a grid by depositing particles with a
5
cell
flat-top kernel (
5\times 5
in 2D), and we evaluate
\nabla
using a 2nd-order
centered finite difference.

Third, we can simplify Equation (
11
). Assume a gyrotropic pressure
tensor

\bm{P}_{e}=P_{e\parallel}\hat{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{b}}+P_{e\perp}(\bm{I}-\hat{\bm{b}}\hat{\bm{b}})
,
following
Goodrich & Scudder 1984
, to obtain
(Figure
7
(d)):

\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{gyr}}=\int\left\langle\frac{-1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{e\parallel}}{\mathrm{d}x}-\left(P_{e\parallel}-P_{e\perp}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln B}{\mathrm{d}x}\right]\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x\,.

(12)

All
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}y
and
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}z
terms are neglected.
Note that the spatial average is taken over the entire integrand, unlike
Equations (
10
) and (
11
).

We have chosen a particular order for the transverse-average operations,

\langle\hat{\bm{b}}\cdot\bm{E}\rangle/\langle\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle

for Equation (
10
) and the same with
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
replacing

\bm{E}
for Equation (
11
).
We could have instead averaged prior to all the vector projections, e.g.,
integrate

\langle\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle\cdot\langle\bm{E}\rangle/\langle\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle
in Equation (
10
) (“early”
averaging).
Or, we could have deferred averaging until the entire integrand is formed,
e.g., integrate

\langle(\hat{\bm{b}}\cdot\bm{E})/(\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}})\rangle

in Equation (
10
) (“late” averaging).
We compared the three averaging procedures across the shock parameter range.
The “late” averaging gives poor results; it may be too sensitive to
local regions where
\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}
approaches zero, whereas

\langle\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}\rangle
is constrained to be non-zero in the
other procedures due to
B_{x}
conservation across the shock.
The “early” averaging agrees well with our adopted procedure in some cases,
but not all.
There is not a clear reason to favor or disfavor our adopted procedure as
compared to “early” averaging, due to the inherent approximation of
describing a 2D shock with a 1D
y
-averaged profile;
disagreement between the two procedures is due solely to 2D effects.

V.3
Indirect Measurement of the Parallel Potential

The HT-frame cross-shock potential (Figure
7
(e)),

\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}(x)=\int\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}(x)\cdot\hat{\bm{x}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,,

(13)

will approximately equal the parallel potential
\phi_{\parallel}(x)
if

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
points towards
+\hat{\bm{x}}
in the HT frame

(
Goodrich & Scudder 1984
)
.
The HT potential has been used to interpret electron heating in satellite
measurements, and it has been shown to agree with other proxies for
electron heating (most notably, Liouville mapping), within the large
systematic uncertainties of measuring low-frequency
\bm{E}
fields in space

(
Cohen et al. 2019
;
Schwartz et al. 2021
)
.
We compute
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}
by an explicit boost of simulation-frame fields with
a constant global boost velocity;
this may be contrasted with adaptive methods that account for local velocity
and
\bm{B}
variations
(
Comişel et al. 2015
;
Marghitu et al. 2017
)
.

Why should
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
point towards
+\hat{\bm{x}}
?
Suppose that
\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
is isotropic (scalar) and that the shock
structure varies solely along the shock-normal coordinate
x
.
Then, the ambipolar field

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}=-\frac{1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathrm{d}x}\hat{\bm{x}}\,,

and the parallel potential

\phi_{\parallel}=-\int\frac{1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e}}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Since
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}\approx\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
,
then
\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}\approx\phi_{\parallel}
.

Next, let us consider a situation in which
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
may not point
towards
+\hat{\bm{x}}
.
Suppose that
\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
is gyrotropic and not isotropic.
Then,
\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
has off-diagonal terms proportional to

(P_{\mathrm{e}\parallel}-P_{\mathrm{e}\perp})
because
\hat{\bm{b}}
does not coincide with
a coordinate axis in the HT frame.
Let us still assume a shock varying solely along
x
; i.e., neglect
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}y
and
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}z
terms.
Then, the ambipolar field

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}=-\frac{1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xx}}{\mathrm{d}x}\hat{\bm{x}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xy}}{\mathrm{d}x}\hat{\bm{y}}+\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xz}}{\mathrm{d}x}\hat{\bm{z}}\right)\,,

and the parallel potential

\phi_{\parallel}=-\int\frac{1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xx}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{b_{y}}{b_{x}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xy}}{\mathrm{d}x}+\frac{b_{z}}{b_{x}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xz}}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

Here,
b_{x}
,
b_{y}
,
b_{z}
are components of
\hat{\bm{b}}
;
notice
b_{x}=\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot\hat{\bm{b}}
.
Since
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}\approx\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
,

\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}=-\int\frac{1}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{e},xx}}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\mathrm{d}x\,.

(14)

We see
\phi_{\parallel}\neq\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}
due to the non-
x
components of

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
in this situation.
3
3

3

Analogous to the disagreement between normal-incidence and HT frame
cross-shock potentials explained by
Goodrich & Scudder 1984
.

This electron anisotropy effect is just one of several higher-order corrections
to the quasi-static field model as discussed by
Scudder 1987

\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
terms in the
simulation coordinate system by computing

\int\left\langle\frac{-\hat{\bm{x}}\cdot(\nabla\cdot\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}})}{en_{\mathrm{e}}}\right\rangle\mathrm{d}x\,,

shown as a blue dotted line in Figures

8
.

If
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{HT}}=\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
and
the shock is 1D-like

\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}y

\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}z

\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}

If in addition
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
points towards
+\hat{\bm{x}}

then Equation (

\phi_{\mathrm{HT}}
and
\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{amb}}
.
We shall see that these assumptions do not fully hold in our simulations.

\theta_{Bn}
in our
simulations.
Although less relevant to electron heating,
\phi_{\mathrm{NIF}}

important to a kinetic shock’s internal structure
(
Burgess & Scholer 2015
)
.
The NIF cross-shock potential’s dependence on Mach number has also been studied
in prior hybrid
(

Quest 1986

(

Our results are shown in the right column of

to

\phi_{\mathrm{NIF}}=\frac{2(r-1)(1+\beta_{\mathrm{e}})}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}\,.

E_{x}
of generalized

scalar
P_{\mathrm{e}}\propto n_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}

The upstream
\beta_{\mathrm{e}}=\beta_{\mathrm{p}}/2
in our simulations.
To evaluate the compression ratio

in Equation (

use the oblique MHD shock jump conditions, similar to

Our calculation of

introduces a weak
\theta_{Bn}
dependence

;

)
.
Both 2D and 1D shocks show that

varies with
\theta_{Bn}
, with
more oblique
\theta_{Bn}\sim 55^{\circ}
yielding higher
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{NIF}}

than more perpendicular
\theta_{Bn}
.
The 1D shocks also show stronger variation in
\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{NIF}}
with

\theta_{Bn}
.
We observe that going from
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=3
to
4
, the potential increases for all

\theta_{Bn}
considered.
Towards higher
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
, the potential
appears to level off or decrease slightly; the

not apply for these stronger shocks.

VII
Case Studies of Electron Dynamics Within the Parallel Potential

VII.1
Electron Phase Space

What electron distributions form within and generate the parallel potential?
We focus upon two shock cases that are weak (
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}<\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
, sub-critical) or strong
(
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}>\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
, super-critical) and therefore have quite different
structure.
The weaker case is the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
,
\theta_{Bn}=65^{\circ}
shock previously shown in
Figures
1
and
7
.
The stronger case is the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
,
\theta_{Bn}=65^{\circ}
shock.
Within Section
VII
, we refer to each case by its sonic Mach
number
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
alone.

Figure
11
surveys electron phase space evolution along the
shock-normal coordinate for our 2D
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case, in the manner of

Scudder et al. 1986b
and
Chen et al. 2018
.
We show electron momenta
\gamma\beta
in units of
m_{\mathrm{e}}c
, where
\gamma
is
the relativistic Lorentz factor and
\beta
is three-velocity in units of
c
.
The momentum coordinates
(\hat{\bm{n}}_{\parallel},\hat{\bm{n}}_{\perp 1},\hat{\bm{n}}_{\perp 2})

are an orthogonal, right-handed triad defined by
\hat{\bm{n}}_{\parallel}=\hat{\bm{b}}
,

\hat{\bm{n}}_{\perp 1}\propto(\hat{\bm{b}}\times\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}})\times\hat{\bm{b}}
,
and
\hat{\bm{n}}_{\perp 2}\propto\hat{\bm{b}}\times\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}
;
here,
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{e}}
is local electron bulk three-velocity, and
\hat{\bm{b}}
is
computed locally at each particle position.
This momentum coordinate system follows
Chen et al. 2018
.
By construction,
\gamma\beta_{\perp 1}
includes both bulk motion and Larmor
gyration, and
\gamma\beta_{\perp 2}
includes only Larmor gyration.

Figure
11
(a) shows that ion density oscillates in sync with
magnetic field strength, as expected for the compressible, fast
(whistler-branch) mode.
In panel (b), we adopt
\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{amb}}
as our proxy for the parallel potential.
Panels (c)–(r) show how electron momentum distributions evolve through the
shock, with momenta measured in the simulation frame.
The distributions are thermal Maxwellians far upstream of the shock,
at
x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}=40
–
45d_{\mathrm{i}}
(panels (j),(r)).
Within the shock precursor, the distributions become asymmetric in

\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
; we see a beam-like component with
\gamma\beta_{\parallel}<0

on top of a broader, “flat-top” component; the asymmetric form is most
prominent in strong magnetic troughs
with
\delta B/B_{0}\mathord{\sim}0.1

(panels (m),(o)).
Distributions within magnetic peaks
are closer to a single component in form, but with a skew towards negative

\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
(panels (n),(p)).
Downstream of the shock, the beam-like component disappears and the
distribution shows a smooth, flat region in
\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
centered near
zero (panels (c),(k)).

In some 2D momentum distributions (panels (e),(g)), particles with

\gamma\beta_{\parallel}>0
show a hint of anisotropy, with some particles at

\gamma\beta_{\parallel}\sim 0.25
having large
\gamma\beta_{\perp 2}\gtrsim\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
; the right side of the 2D distributions is stretched along
the
\gamma\beta_{\perp 2}
axis.
We expect that these back-streaming electrons were magnetic mirror-reflected
within the shock ramp or precursor.
Back-streaming electrons are also visible in the far-upstream 1D distributions
(panels (p),(q),(r)) when plotted with a logarithmic scale.

The evolution of parallel distributions through the shock, showing a transient
beam and flat-top structure, is broadly consistent with satellite observations

(
Feldman et al. 1983b
;
Feldman et al. 1983a
)
and prior 1D and 2D PIC
simulations
(
Savoini & Lembege 1994
)
.
It can be noted that
Feldman et al. 1983a
showed that weak,
interplanetary shocks with
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim 1
–
4
exhibit less beam/flat-top
structure as compared to the stronger bow shock at Earth’s magnetosphere.
Although our
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case has
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}<\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
and is sub-critical, it shows parallel
electron behavior similar to
space measurements of
stronger shocks.
This is partly an artifact of the reduced mass ratio adopted for our
simulations; we return to this point in Section
VII.2
.

Electron distributions in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
case (Figure
12
)
show similar distortions as the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case:
namely, a one-sided beam with
\gamma\beta_{\parallel}<0
accelerating towards the
shock and eroding through the shock ramp to leave a broad, flattened post-shock
distribution in
\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
.
The population of
back-streaming
electrons is more prominent than in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case.

Figure
13
shows how the HT-frame
\gamma\beta_{\parallel}
electron
distributions correlate with the potential
\phi_{\parallel}
for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
case studies in 1D and 2D.
In Figure
13
(e–h), the blue and black solid curves show
\phi_{\parallel}
recast as a
parallel velocity
\pm\sqrt{2e\phi_{\parallel}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
, and black dashed lines show
the approximate HT-frame downstream bulk velocity,

u_{\mathrm{HT2}}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{sh}}}{r}\left(1+(r^{2}-(u_{\mathrm{sh}}/c)^{2})\tan^{2}\theta_{Bn}\right)^{1/2}\,.

(17)

In Figure
13
(i–l), trapped electrons are defined to have total
energy
\varepsilon<0
(Equation (
2
)), while backstreaming
electrons have
\varepsilon>0
and
v_{\parallel}>0
.
The electron fractions in Figure
13
(i–l) are computed within

0.1d_{\mathrm{i}}
bins along
x
using the 1D
y
-averaged
\phi_{\parallel}(x)=\phi_{\parallel,\mathrm{amb}}(x)
.
Some downstream electrons with
v_{\parallel}<0
are not
counted as trapped in Figure
13
, but most such electrons are

de facto
trapped because reflection at the left boundary or scattering
within the downstream rest frame generally will not give them
\varepsilon>0

and
v_{\parallel}>0
as needed to escape back upstream.

We may understand an electron’s parallel velocity evolution by casting
Equations (
2
) and (
3
) into dimensionless form:

\frac{v_{\parallel}^{2}-v_{\parallel 0}^{2}}{c_{se}^{2}}=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}(x)-\frac{v_{\perp 0}^{2}}{c_{se}^{2}}\left(\frac{B(x)}{B_{0}}-1\right)

(18)

with
v_{\parallel 0}
and
v_{\perp 0}
being the electron’s initial HT-frame
velocity components far upstream, where
\phi_{\parallel}(x)=0
and
B(x)=B_{0}
.
The upstream electron thermal speed
c_{se}=\sqrt{\Gamma k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{0}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
, and
the normalized
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}=e\phi_{\parallel}/(0.5m_{\mathrm{i}}u_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2})
is
typically
0.1
–
0.2
in our simulated shocks
(Figure
10
).
Equation (
18
) shows that electrons gain parallel energy from

\phi_{\parallel}
and lose energy from magnetic mirroring, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}
moderates the
relative importance of
\phi_{\parallel}
versus mirroring.

Let us trace how electrons evolve within the potential
\phi_{\parallel}
and the
corresponding velocity floor/barrier
\pm\sqrt{2e\phi_{\parallel}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
, neglecting
scattering.
Suppose an electron enters the shock with
v_{\parallel 0}<0
and

v_{\perp 0}=0
(no magnetic mirroring) in the HT frame; further suppose that

|v_{\parallel 0}|\ll\sqrt{2e\phi_{\parallel}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
.
That electron will follow the negative potential floor

v_{\parallel}(x)=-\sqrt{2e\phi_{\parallel}(x)/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
into the shock.
It reflects at the left boundary by reversing the sign of
v_{x}
in the
simulation frame, i.e. the downstream rest frame.
The post-shock
v_{\parallel}
distribution is thus centered on the HT bulk flow
velocity
v_{\parallel}=-u_{\mathrm{HT2}}
(Equation (
17
)), shown by dashed
horizontal lines in Figure
13
(e–h).
Post-shock electrons may escape upstream if they have

v_{\parallel}\gtrsim\sqrt{2e\phi_{\parallel}/m_{\mathrm{e}}}
(more precisely,
\varepsilon>0

for non-zero pitch angle).
The fraction of downstream electrons that may escape upstream is
\lesssim 10\%
of the total downstream population in all case-study shocks
(Figure
13
(i–l)); most shocked electrons are confined to

x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}<0
, consistent with the prediction of Figure
2
.

If an electron entering the shock instead has non-zero
v_{\perp}
and a
large-enough pitch angle, it may mirror reflect specularly within the HT frame
(i.e.,
v_{\parallel}\to-v_{\parallel}
).
If the macroscopic
B(x)
and
\phi_{\parallel}(x)
are time-stationary and there is
no scattering, the reflected electron will freely escape back upstream with

\varepsilon>0
and
v_{\parallel}>0
.
In practice, reflection combined with scattering may lead to local electron
trapping and energization within the precursor wave train and shock ramp

(
Katou & Amano 2019
)
.

In each panel of Figure
13
, the potential floor (solid blue and
black lines with
v_{\parallel}<0
) tracks the deformation of the upstream thermal
electron beam entering the shock from right to left.
In the post-shock region, the potential floor roughly corresponds to the
anti-parallel (
v_{\parallel}<0
) edge of the electron distribution.
The agreement of the potential floor and the electron distribution edge in
Figure
13

corresponds to good agreement at
p_{\parallel}<0
in the Liouville mapping
procedure of Figure
6
.

Let us now compare 1D versus 2D for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case in Figure
13
.
The overall structure of
\phi_{\parallel}
is similar in both 1D and 2D, with a
gradual rise in the shock precursor region and jump at the shock ramp to a
final post-shock value of
\Delta\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}\approx 0.1
.
The 1D shock shows sharper
d_{\mathrm{i}}
-scale potential spikes embedded within the
precursor wave train; in both 1D and 2D the spikes occur within magnetic
troughs.
Electrons clump at local magnetic maxima, especially in 1D, which we
interpret as due to magnetic mirroring.
In 1D,
\phi_{\parallel}
spikes also occur at magnetic maxima within
x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}=0
–
5d_{\mathrm{i}}
(Figure
13
, top left panel) just ahead of the shock
ramp.

The 1D and 2D cases differ in the post-shock region
x<x_{\mathrm{sh}}
for both
the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and
7
cases.
The 2D-shock distributions are diffuse and smooth in
v_{\parallel}
, whereas the
1D-shock distributions show localized (in
x
) beams or clumps
that may correspond to transient phase space holes.
The 1D case also shows larger precursor fluctuations in the trapped and
untrapped electron fractions (and, larger magnetic and
\phi_{\parallel}

fluctuations) than the 2D case.
It is possible that similarly strong fluctuations occur in 2D but are hidden by

y
-averaging; however, inspection of
B/B_{0}
images suggests that the 2D
precursor fluctuations are coherent and so of lower amplitude than in 1D.

In the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
case, the 1D jump in
\phi_{\parallel}
is
\mathord{\sim}2\times

larger than the equivalent 2D shock.
The 2D shock shows more
v_{\parallel}
diffusion in the shock foot as electrons
approach the ramp (
x-x_{\mathrm{sh}}\sim 1d_{\mathrm{i}}
).
We suggest that strong, non-adiabatic scattering embedded within the shock ramp
drives so-called “infilling” of the parallel distribution
(
Hull et al. 1998
)

and hence a net cooling, as compared to the 1D case.

VII.2
Effects of Electron Plasma-Cyclotron Frequency Ratio and Ion-Electron Mass Ratio

Let us now explore the effects of the electron plasma-to-cyclotron frequency
ratio
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
and the mass ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
upon the parallel potential, for
fixed Mach number and
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}
.
In this Subsection, we focus solely on the 1D
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case for two reasons.
First, the extended
\phi_{\parallel}
structure over many
d_{\mathrm{i}}
in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
shock
precursor has not been studied before in a quasi-perpendicular shock, to our
knowledge.
Second, computing cost
and numerical noise both
rise as either
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
or
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
are raised towards realistic values;
the use of 1D simulations helps us limit both cost and noise.
In this Subsection (Figures
14
,
15
)
all simulations use
8192
upstream particles per cell, four times larger
than our standard runs (Section
II.2
).

Figure
14
shows that
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
does not have a strong
effect on
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}
or the electron phase-space behavior,
which we interpret as follows.
Suppose that
\phi_{\parallel}
scales with some electrostatic fluctuation strength,

\delta E_{\parallel}
, and is integrated over an ion skin depth:

e\phi_{\parallel}\sim e\delta E_{\parallel}d_{\mathrm{i}}\,.

When normalized to ion kinetic energy,

\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}=\frac{2e\phi_{\parallel}}{m_{\mathrm{i}}u_{\mathrm{sh}}^{2}}\sim\frac{2}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}}\left(\frac{\delta E_{\parallel}}{u_{\mathrm{sh}}B_{0}/c}\right)\,.

Let us now suppose that
\delta E_{\parallel}
scales like a whistler wave’s
electromagnetic fluctuation
\delta E_{\perp}
, which will be motional for low
frequencies:
\delta E_{\perp}\sim\delta u\delta B/c
(where
\delta u
and

\delta B
are the wave’s velocity and magnetic fluctuations).
Then

\delta E_{\parallel}\sim\delta E_{\perp}\sim\left(\frac{\delta u}{v_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\delta B}{B_{0}}\right)\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}}\frac{u_{\mathrm{sh}}B_{0}}{c}\,.

The fluctuations
\delta u/v_{\mathrm{A}}
and
\delta B/B_{0}
should not depend on

u_{\mathrm{sh}}/c
for the non-relativistic solar wind
(
Verscharen et al. 2020
)
.
Then,

\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}\sim\frac{2}{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}}\left(\frac{\delta u}{v_{\mathrm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\delta B}{B_{0}}\right)\,.

In this scaling,
we find that
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}
shows no explicit dependence on
u_{\mathrm{sh}}/c

or
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
.
Our heuristic argument has caveats.
The whistler wave strength
\delta B/B_{0}>0.1
is outside the regime of linear
fluctuations, especially at the shock ramp (
\delta B/B_{0}\sim 1
).
And, the supposed proportionality
\delta E_{\parallel}\sim\delta E_{\perp}
is
suspect; the strength of
\delta E_{\parallel}
could be regulated by secondary
electrostatic modes that could introduce
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
or
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
dependence
into the scaling argument.

Figure
15
shows with increasing mass ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
:

\phi_{\parallel}
decreases, the electron
v_{\parallel}
phase space is less distorted, and
magnetic mirroring from
\mu
conservation becomes more important.
Both downstream and upstream distributions are more centered on
v_{\parallel}=0

with increasing
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
.
Is the changed electron response due solely to the decrease in
\phi_{\parallel}
?
Or, would the electron response change even if
\phi_{\parallel}(x)
were identical for
both
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=200
and
1836
?

Suppose that we have two shocks with the same
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}(x)
,
and
B(x)
profiles, and the shocks differ only in their mass ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
.
The right-hand side of Equation (
18
) is not directly affected
by
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
, since
(v_{\perp 0}/c_{se})^{2}\sim 1
in the HT frame, so the
relative importance of
\phi_{\parallel}
and magnetic mirroring is unchanged.
In other words, the Liouville mapping of a single trajectory starting at any

(v_{\parallel 0},v_{\perp 0})
does not change with the mass ratio.
Instead,
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
influences the electron dynamics via
v_{\parallel 0}
.
When the HT-frame bulk upstream velocity
u_{\mathrm{HT}}=u_{\mathrm{sh}}/\cos\theta_{Bn}

is much larger than the electron thermal speed
c_{se}
, then

\frac{v_{\parallel 0}}{c_{se}}\sim\frac{u_{HT}}{c_{se}}=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}}{\cos\theta_{Bn}}\sqrt{\frac{m_{\mathrm{e}}}{m_{\mathrm{i}}}}\,.

(19)

In the opposite limit
u_{\mathrm{HT}}\ll c_{se}
,
v_{\parallel 0}/c_{se}\sim 1
.
The mass ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
shifts the distribution along
v_{\parallel 0}/c_{se}
in
the HT frame.
Although
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
does not change electron trajectories and separatrices in
phase space, which are fully determined by
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}(x)
and
B(x)

(Equation (
18
)),
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
does change the initial sampling of
said trajectories, and hence the relative fraction of electrons that pass into
the shock downstream as opposed to being reflected by magnetic mirroring.

Figure
16
summarizes the just-preceding discussion by
showing the test-particle
electron Liouville mapping for two idealized shocks having
identical
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\tilde{\phi}_{\parallel}(x)
, and
B(x)
and differing only in mass
ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
; see, e.g.,
Yuan et al. 2008
for a more sophisticated example of
such test-particle mapping.
The Liouville map is performed following
Equations (
5
) to (
8
).
For a fixed starting point
(v_{\perp 0},v_{\parallel 0})
, the Liouville mapping
(phase space flow) from pre- to post-shock is identical and has no dependence
upon mass ratio, when specified in terms of upstream electron thermal velocity

c_{se}
.
But, the starting electron distribution is offset farther from
v_{\parallel 0}=0

in the
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=200
case; this leads to a stronger
v_{\parallel}<0

VII.3
Precursor Wave Properties

In this Subsection, we verify that the precursor waves in our 2D
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
shocks are upstream-propagating
whistler waves, oblique to both
\bm{B}
and
\hat{\bm{n}}
, with phase and group
speeds exceeding the shock speed.
It is already well established that weak, low-beta, quasi-perpendicular solar
wind shocks are formed of, regulated by, and radiate low frequency
(
\omega\ll\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
) whistler-branch modes; evidence is given by
theory
(
Bickerton et al. 1971
;
Tidman & Krall 1971
;
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
)
,
experiments
(
Robson & Sheffield 1969
)
,
observations
(
Fairfield & Feldman 1975
;
Greenstadt et al. 1975
;
Mellott & Greenstadt 1984
;
Oka et al. 2006
;
Hull et al. 2012
;
Wilson et al. 2012
;
Wilson 2016
;
Wilson et al. 2017
;
Oka et al. 2019
;
Hull et al. 2020
;
Lalti et al. 2022
)
,
and fully-kinetic simulations
(
Liewer et al. 1991
;
Savoini & Lembege 1994
;
Savoini & Lembege 2010
;
Riquelme & Spitkovsky 2011
)
.
The goal is to frame our case-study shocks within a broader context.

What is the origin of the precursor whistlers?
A full answer to this question is beyond the scope of our work, but we
shall give some comments.
In the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case, the
shock-normal whistlers are consistent with a phase-standing wave train expected
to form for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}<\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}

(
Kennel et al. 1985
;
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
)
.
For the whistlers oblique to
\hat{\bm{n}}
, we consider two plausible
mechanisms:
nonlinear wave steepening within the shock ramp
(
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
)
, or
beam resonance with reflected ions gyrating within the shock foot, which may be
called “modified two stream instability” (MTSI) or “kinetic cross-field
streaming instability”

(
Wu et al. 1984
;
Hellinger et al. 1996
;
Hellinger & Mangeney 1997
;
Muschietti & Lembège 2017
)
.
There also exists a less-studied wave-wave decay instability that may generate
daughter whistlers having
\bm{k}
mis-aligned with respect to their parent
whistlers
(
Galeev & Karpman 1963
;
Decker & Robson 1972
)
, noted by

Mellott & Greenstadt 1984
, but we do not consider it
for now.

Lower hybrid drift instabilities have been invoked to explain shock structure
and electron heating
(
Krall & Liewer 1971
;
Wu et al. 1984
;
Stasiewicz & Eliasson 2020
)
; but, drift
waves propagate orthogonal to both
\bm{B}
and
\nabla P_{\mathrm{e}}
(i.e.,

\hat{\bm{n}}
) and are thus suppressed in our 2D simulations.
We also do not consider “slow” MTSI caused by a relative drift between incoming
ions and electrons
(
Muschietti & Lembège 2017
)
.
Both lower hybrid drifts and “slow” MTSI are expected to drive waves nearly
perpendicular to
\bm{B}
that are mainly electrostatic and so would not
explain the oblique, electromagnetic power that we are now considering.

Both
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and
7
shocks have
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}
below the non-linear whistler Mach
number
\sqrt{2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
as defined by
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
, and
Figure
18
shows that whistlers in range of
\theta_{Bk}
can
outrun both shocks.
Of note, our
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
shock having
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}<\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}<\sqrt{2}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
is similar to the
Cluster shock crossing presented by
Dimmock et al. 2019
, wherein
d_{\mathrm{e}}
-scale
shock ramp fluctuation was presented as evidence for nonlinear steepening as
the origin of oblique whistlers.
More broadly speaking, it seems plausible that 2D shock ramp rippling or
filamentation, coupled with wave steepening in the ramp, may cause whistler
wave emission at various angles
(
Sundkvist et al. 2012
, cf.)
.

Can reflected ions resonate with the oblique precursor waves?
We assess this by comparing the reflected ions’
x
-
y
plane velocity

\beta_{xy}=v_{xy}/c
against the precursor whistler phase speeds
(Equation (
21
)), all measured in the simulation frame.
We separate reflected ions from “core” ions using an
x
-
z
momentum-space
threshold
\gamma\beta_{z}>0.02-0.5\gamma\beta_{x}
, which selects ions with
high
v_{x}
and
v_{z}
being accelerated by the upstream motional field near the
shock ramp.
The same momentum-space cut is applied for both
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
.
We compute the mean and standard deviation of
\beta_{xy}
within
0.5d_{\mathrm{i}}
bins
along
x
, noting that the reflected ion distributions are not Gaussian or
particularly symmetric in phase space.

Figures
20
and
21
show the ion
distributions and measurements of
\beta_{xy}
.
Reflected ions appear within
\pm 1d_{\mathrm{i}}
of
x_{\mathrm{sh}}
for both the

\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and
7
shocks, with a shorter shock-foot gyration (
\lesssim 0.5d_{\mathrm{i}}

along
x
) for the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
shock.
In the last simulation snapshot, the reflected ions have a number density
contrast
\sim 3
–
8\%
(
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
) and
\mathord{\sim}20\%
(
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
) with respect to
the total ion population.

The
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
shock’s reflected ion beam has mean
v_{xy}\mathord{\sim}0.02
–
0.026c

(standard deviation
0.01c
).
It appears difficult for the reflected ions to resonate with the

\theta_{Bk}=35^{\circ}
whistler mode at
\omega/k\sim 0.046c
, but the ions
could (in principle) lie in beam resonance with the shock-normal

\theta_{Bk}=65^{\circ}
whistler at
\omega/k\sim 0.024c
.

The
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
shock’s reflected ion beam has mean
v_{xy}\mathord{\sim}0.03
–
0.04c

(standard deviation
0.02c
).
The reflected ions might resonate with the lower-frequency
\theta_{Bk}=40^{\circ}

and
25^{\circ}
whistler modes having
\omega/k\sim 0.033
and
0.04c

respectively.
The reflected ions may be too slow to resonate with the higher-frequency

\theta_{Bk}\lesssim 15^{\circ}
whistlers, which have
\omega/k\gtrsim 0.05c
.

Our analysis is limited because we do not consider any of (1) time or spatial
(2D) variation in the reflected ion properties, (2) angle between reflected
ions’ velocity vector and the precursor wavevectors, (3) energy or power
balance, (4) more detailed kinetic stability analysis.
A proper treatment of (1) may loosen our constraints if time-intermittent or
localized, gyrophase-bunched ion reflection can attain a larger range of beam
speeds and higher density with respect to the incoming flow.
A proper accounting of (2–4) should help tighten our constraints by culling
observed wave modes that could not plausibly be driven by a resonant ion beam.
Now, (1–4) are not unusually difficult to assess, but we emphasize that the
purpose of Sections
VII.3
and
VII.4
is
to provide broader context for our primary focus of electron heating physics.
We can at least conclude that reflected ions’ beam resonance likely does not
explain all of the oblique wave precursor power observed in our simulations,
and perhaps nonlinear shock ramp processes are needed.

We warn that the propagation angle
\theta_{Bk}
of our whistlers is subject to
both a mode selection effect from the simulation domain’s periodic boundary in

y
, as well as angle-dependent Landau and transit-time damping that may differ
between our simulated
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=200
and the true
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=1836
.
We also warn that our angles
\theta_{Bk}
are by-eye estimates, rather than being
obtained by a formal fitting procedure; we deem this acceptable since neither
precise wave angle determination nor wave driving mechanism determination is
the primary purpose of this manuscript, and there is considerable systematic
uncertainty due to the frequency aliasing in our
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=7
shock analysis
anyways.

Our most robust conclusion is that forward-propagating precursor whistlers
appear at a variety of propagation angles between
\theta_{Bk}=0^{\circ}
to

\theta_{Bk}=\theta_{Bn}
in our case study shocks.
These whistlers are important in setting the ion-scale structure of the
electron’s parallel potential.
We expect such precursor whistlers to occur in a wide range of simulated
parameter space as fully-kinetic simulations catch up to decades of spacecraft
observations.

VIII
Conclusions

VIII.1
Summary

We have measured electrons’ ambipolar
\bm{B}
-parallel potential
\phi_{\parallel}
in a survey
of 2D quasi-perpendicular PIC shocks spanning
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=3
–
10
(
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}\sim 1
–
5
)
and
\theta_{Bn}=85
–
55^{\circ}
, with upstream total plasma beta
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}=0.25
.
Different particle- and field-based measurements of
\phi_{\parallel}
agree in the
overall
\phi_{\parallel}
jump at the
\mathord{\sim}10\%
level, and we find that
\phi_{\parallel}

is most robustly estimated in our simulations from the electron pressure tensor
divergence.
Off-diagonal terms of the electron pressure tensor
\bm{P}_{\mathrm{e}}
are not
negligible for our low-
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}
shock simulations; the integral of the ambipolar
electric field
\bm{E}_{\mathrm{amb}}
projected along shock normal can
underestimate
\phi_{\parallel}
by
\mathord{\sim}10
–
30\%
as compared to other measurement
methods.
Both the magnitude of
\phi_{\parallel}
, and the correlation between
\phi_{\parallel}
and

\Delta T_{\mathrm{e}}
, are similar to prior reports based on observational data

(
Schwartz et al. 1988
;
Hull et al. 1998
;
Hull et al. 2000
)
.
We also measure the normal incidence frame (NIF) potential
\phi_{\mathrm{NIF}}
and show
its variation with
\theta_{Bn}
and Mach number.

We show that a quasi-perpendicular shock with Alfvén Mach number
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}

below a critical whistler Mach number,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}<\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{w}}
, can host a
\phi_{\parallel}(x)

profile extending over many tens of
d_{\mathrm{i}}
within a whistler wave precursor.
The potential shows transient spikes within magnetic troughs, as well as a
secular increase towards the shock over many wave cycles.
We speculate that the potential spikes (bipolar electric fields) are due to
non-linear steepening of the large-amplitude whistler wave’s electrostatic
field
(
Vasko et al. 2018a
;
An et al. 2019
)
, but more work is needed to properly
explore and test this hypothesis.

In two case-study shocks (
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}=200
,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
and
7
,
\theta_{Bn}=65^{\circ}
)
without backstreaming ions, we find that shock precursor whistlers span various
propagation angles between
\theta_{Bk}=0^{\circ}
to
\theta_{Bk}=\theta_{Bn}
, including
a phase-standing wave train along shock-normal in the
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}=4
case

(
Tidman & Northrop 1968
;
Kennel et al. 1985
)
.
The whistler precursors also host small-scale electrostatic structures,
including electron holes, with wavevector
\bm{k}
parallel to
\bm{B}
.
We tentatively find that shock-reflected (gyrating, not backstreaming) ions may
lie in beam resonance with some, but not all, of the precursor wave power.
Other mechanisms, such as nonlinear steepening within the shock ramp

(
Omidi & Winske 1988
;
Omidi & Winske 1990
;
Krasnoselskikh et al. 2002
)
or wave-wave decay instability

(
Galeev & Karpman 1963
;
Decker & Robson 1972
)
, may help explain some precursor wave power that
cannot be attributed to beam ions.

VIII.2
Future Directions

Our description of electron energization has been macroscopic,
focusing upon electric fields at ion scales;
we have not quantified the microscopic scattering and dissipation that is
needed to provide true irreversible heating and to regulate the strength of

\phi_{\parallel}
via the electron pressure tensor.
Moreover, the case-study simulations of Section
VII
have
electron holes as the main electrostatic structure, whereas MMS observations
have revealed electron and ion holes, double layers, ion acoustic waves, and
electron cyclotron harmonics
(
Goodrich et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
.
Towards higher
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}
, small-scale parallel whistlers will likely become
important
(
Hull et al. 2012
;
Page et al. 2021
)
.
Much recent work has been done with MMS and some tailored simulations

(
Goodrich et al. 2018
;
Vasko et al. 2018b
;
Wang et al. 2021
;
Shen et al. 2021
;
Kamaletdinov et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2022
)
.
Fewer fully-kinetic shock simulations have captured the macroscopic,
ion-scale coupling to microscopic electrostatic scales
(
Wilson et al. 2021
)
.
In such shock simulations, we would like to know: what types of electrostatic
structures appear, and where in the shock do said structures appear, as a
function of shock parameters
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}
,
\theta_{Bn}
, and
\beta_{\mathrm{p}}
?
What is their contribution to
\bm{B}
-parallel scattering and pitch-angle
scattering of electrons?
As the latter two questions are answered, can we find any qualitative or
quantitative trends in shock parameter space to test against MMS data?

A few caveats must be noted for further study of these secondary electrostatic
structures.
First, our simulations are limited by spatial resolution and numerical noise.
The upstream electron Debye length is marginally resolved with one cell, and
not resolved when PIC current filtering is accounted for; small-scale
structures may be biased towards lower
k
.
Discrete PIC macroparticles create numerical scattering
(
Birdsall & Langdon 1991
)

whose contribution needs to be separated from that of more-physical
collisionless scattering.
High mass ratio
m_{\mathrm{i}}/m_{\mathrm{e}}
, high particle sampling, and high space and time
resolution may be needed to unveil physics at the electron Debye scale in
future simulations; Vlasov solvers may be another way to make progress

(
Juno et al. 2018
, e.g.)
.
Second, we have only used equal-temperature ion-electron Maxwellian
distributions to model upstream plasma in our shocks.
Solar wind shocks have multiple populations: core, halo, and strahl electrons,
pick-up ions, et cetera.
The electron halo and strahl, in particular, may alter Landau damping rates.
Third, artificially low
\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}/\Omega_{\mathrm{ce}}
can modify the types and Fourier spectra
of electrostatic waves, e.g., for the electron cyclotron drift instability
(ECDI)
(
Muschietti & Lembège 2013
;
TenBarge et al. 2021
)
, and hence also modify the
energy exchange between ions and electrons.
Lastly, the use of 2D versus 3D simulations will matter – just as the electron
trapping is weaker in 2D versus 1D, so we also expect that 3D simulations may
reduce electron trapping, admit a broader spectrum of precursor wave modes, and
permit electrons to scatter and gain energy in different and possibly new ways

(
Trotta & Burgess 2019
)
.
