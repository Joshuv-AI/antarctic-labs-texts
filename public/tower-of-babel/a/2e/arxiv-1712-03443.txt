Uniqueness of Transformations with Prescribed Jacobian Determinant and Curl-Vector
Zicong Zhou, Xi Chen, Xian Xin Cai, Guojun Liao

Abstract
Numerical examples demonstrated that a prescribed positive Jacobian determinant alone can not uniquely determine a diffeomorphism. It is conjectured that the uniqueness of a transformation can be assured by its Jacobian determinant and the curl-vector. In this work, we study the uniqueness problem analytically and propose an approach to the proof of the uniqueness of a transformation with prescribed Jacobian determinant and curl-vector.

The
Variational

Method
for Mesh Generation

Let
\mathrm{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=2
, or
3
be the domain and a scalar function on
f_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})>0
and a vector-valued
\boldsymbol{g}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})
on the domain
\mathrm{\Omega}
with

\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}f_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}=|\mathrm{\Omega}|\text{ and }\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=0

(2.1)

We look for a diffeomorphism

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}):\mathrm{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathrm{\Omega}

(2.2)

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{old}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})

(2.3)

that minimizes the cost functional — sum of squared difference:

ssd=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}[(J(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}))-f_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}+|\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}))-\boldsymbol{g}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})|^{2}]d\boldsymbol{x}

(2.4)

where
J(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\text{det}\nabla(\boldsymbol{\phi}(\boldsymbol{x}))
, subject to the following constraints with control functions
f(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x})

\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{div }\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})&=f(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad in\quad\mathrm{\Omega}\\
\text{curl }\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad in\quad\mathrm{\Omega}\\
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad\text{ on }\partial\mathrm{\Omega}\end{aligned}\right.

The above div-curl equations lead to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{\Delta}u_{1}&=f(\boldsymbol{x})_{x_{1}}-g_{3x_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})+g_{2x_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})=F_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathrm{\Delta}u_{2}&=f(\boldsymbol{x})_{x_{2}}-g_{3x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})-g_{1x_{3}}(\boldsymbol{x})=F_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\mathrm{\Delta}u_{3}&=f(\boldsymbol{x})_{x_{3}}-g_{2x_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x})+g_{1x_{2}}(\boldsymbol{x})=F_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})\\
\end{aligned}\right.

i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\qquad\text{ in }\mathrm{\Omega}\\
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\boldsymbol{0}\qquad\text{ on }\partial\mathrm{\Omega}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.5)

3
The Uniqueness Problem

Suppose two smooth transformations
\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\mathrm{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathrm{\Omega}
,
\mathrm{\Omega}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
have the same Jacobian determinant and curl-vector, namely,

J(\boldsymbol{\phi})=J(\boldsymbol{\psi})

(3.1)

\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\text{curl }(\boldsymbol{\psi})

(3.2)

\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{\psi}\text{, on }\partial\mathrm{\Omega}

(3.3)

where
J(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\text{det}\nabla(\boldsymbol{\phi})
. We would like to ask: can the above conditions guarantee that
\boldsymbol{\phi}\equiv\boldsymbol{\psi}\text{ on }\mathrm{\Omega}
?

\mathbf{First}
, we consider a simple case as follows. Let
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}:\mathrm{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathrm{\Omega}
be two smooth transformations by

\boldsymbol{\phi}=\boldsymbol{id}+\boldsymbol{u}

(3.4)

\boldsymbol{\psi}=\boldsymbol{id}

(3.5)

where
\boldsymbol{u}
is sufficiently small transformation in the Sobolev space
{H_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}
. One can suppose that
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{H_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{\Omega})}<\epsilon

(3.6)

Hence, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<\epsilon\\
\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<\epsilon\\
\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<\epsilon\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(3.7)

\mathbf{Second}
, from
\boldsymbol{\phi}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x_{1}+u_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),x_{2}+u_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),x_{3}+u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}))
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})

, we may derive

J(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\begin{vmatrix}1+u_{1x_{1}}&u_{2x_{1}}&u_{3x_{1}}\\
u_{1x_{2}}&1+u_{2x_{2}}&u_{3x_{2}}\\
u_{1x_{3}}&u_{2x_{3}}&1+u_{3x_{3}}\end{vmatrix}

=1+u_{1x_{1}}+u_{2x_{2}}+u_{3x_{3}}

+u_{1x_{1}}u_{2x_{2}}u_{3x_{3}}+u_{1x_{3}}u_{2x_{1}}u_{3x_{2}}+u_{1x_{2}}u_{2x_{3}}u_{3x_{1}}

-u_{1x_{1}}u_{2x_{3}}u_{3x_{2}}-u_{1x_{2}}u_{2x_{1}}u_{3x_{3}}-u_{1x_{3}}u_{2x_{2}}u_{3x_{1}}

+u_{1x_{1}}u_{2x_{2}}+u_{1x_{1}}u_{3x_{3}}+u_{2x_{2}}u_{3x_{3}}

-u_{1x_{2}}u_{2x_{1}}-u_{1x_{3}}u_{3x_{1}}-u_{2x_{3}}u_{3x_{2}}

=1+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})+J(\boldsymbol{u})

+u_{1x_{1}}u_{2x_{2}}+u_{1x_{1}}u_{3x_{3}}+u_{2x_{2}}u_{3x_{3}}

-u_{1x_{2}}u_{2x_{1}}-u_{1x_{3}}u_{3x_{1}}-u_{2x_{3}}u_{3x_{2}}

=1+\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})

where we denote

\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})=-[J(\boldsymbol{u})+Tail(\boldsymbol{u})]

and

Tail(\boldsymbol{u})=u_{1x_{1}}u_{2x_{2}}+u_{1x_{1}}u_{3x_{3}}+u_{2x_{2}}u_{3x_{3}}

-u_{1x_{2}}u_{2x_{1}}-u_{1x_{3}}u_{3x_{1}}-u_{2x_{3}}u_{3x_{2}}

(3.8)

According to (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) we have,

0=J(\boldsymbol{\phi})-J(\boldsymbol{\psi})=1+div(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})-1=div(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})

\boldsymbol{0}=\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{\psi})=\begin{pmatrix}u_{3x_{2}}-u_{2x_{3}}\\
u_{1x_{3}}-u_{3x_{1}}\\
u_{1x_{2}}-u_{2x_{1}}\end{pmatrix}-\boldsymbol{0}=\text{curl }(\boldsymbol{u})

i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})&=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})\\
\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})&=\boldsymbol{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(3.9)

It follows from (
3.9
) that
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies the
Poisson
equations:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{\Delta}u_{1}&=\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{1}}-[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{3}]_{x_{2}}+[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{2}]_{x_{3}}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{1}}\\
\mathrm{\Delta}u_{2}&=\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{2}}-[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{3}]_{x_{1}}-[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{1}]_{x_{3}}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{2}}\\
\mathrm{\Delta}u_{3}&=\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{3}}-[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{2}]_{x_{1}}+[\text{curl}(\boldsymbol{u})_{1}]_{x_{2}}=\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{3}}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

\Rightarrow\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}=(\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{1}},\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{2}},\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})_{x_{3}})={\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}

Note that the dominating terms of
{\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}
at (
3.8
) are products of only the first partial derivatives of
\boldsymbol{u}
, so the dominating terms of
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}
at (
3.10
) are the terms in products of the first and second partial derivatives of
\boldsymbol{u}
. This means, by (
3.7
), we get

\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}=(\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{u})}\right\|_{L^{2}}

(3.10)

\Rightarrow\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}<\epsilon\cdot\epsilon=\epsilon^{2}

(3.11)

Next, we will establish an inequality for
\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}
. Since
\boldsymbol{u}=0\text{ on }\partial\mathrm{\Omega}
, by
Green^{\prime}s
formula, we can derive,

\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}=|\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}|

(3.12)

Applying the
Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality and properties of integration to the RHS of (
3.12
) to get

\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}=|\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}|\leq\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\boldsymbol{u}||\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}|\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}

\Rightarrow\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}

(3.13)

Applying the

3.12
),
\exists

, such that

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=C\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}

(3.14)

Next, we combine (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) to have

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}

(3.15)

\Rightarrow\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}<C\epsilon^{2}

(3.16)

And, as for
\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}
, by (
3.11
), (
3.13
) and (
3.16
), we may bound
\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}
as

\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq(\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}

(3.17)

\Rightarrow\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}<(C\epsilon^{2}\cdot\epsilon^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}=C^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{2}

(3.18)

Now,
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<C\epsilon^{2}\\
\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<C^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{2}\\
\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<\epsilon^{2}\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(3.19)

\mathbf{Third}
, let’s treat the procedure of (
3.7
) to (
3.19
) as Step-0, and repeat it again with replacing (

3.19
). So, plug (

(3.20)

Then, by (

), we get

\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}<C\cdot C^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{3}=C^{(1+\frac{1}{2})}\epsilon^{3}

(3.21)

and by (
3.17
), we get

\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq(\left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}<(C^{(1+\frac{1}{2})}\epsilon^{3}\cdot C^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{3})^{\frac{1}{2}}=C\epsilon^{3}

(3.22)

which above can be combined from (
3.19
), (

) and (

) into

\left\{\begin{aligned} \left\|\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<C^{(1+\frac{1}{2})}\epsilon^{3}\\
\left\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<C\epsilon^{3}\\
\left\|\mathrm{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}\right\|_{L^{2}}&<C^{\frac{1}{2}}\epsilon^{3}\\

(3.23)

Conclusion

In this research note, we describe an approach to the uniqueness problem based on the simple case which the two smooth transformations are close to each other and one of them is the
\boldsymbol{identity}
map. The general uniqueness problem from (
3.1
) to (
3.3
) remains open. An interesting intermediate step is to show that, for any two sufficiently close
\boldsymbol{\phi}
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}
, a similar argument can be applied.
