The minimum linear locality of linear codes
Pan Tan, Cuiling Fan, Cunsheng Ding and Zhengchun Zhou

Abstract
Locally recoverable codes (LRCs) were proposed for the recovery of data in distributed and cloud storage systems about nine years ago. A lot of progress on the study of LRCs has been made by now. However, there is a lack of general theory on the minimum linear locality of linear codes. In addition, the minimum linear locality of many known families of linear codes is not studied in the literature. Motivated by these two facts, this paper develops some general theory about the minimum linear locality of linear codes, and investigates the minimum linear locality of a number of families of linear codes, such as q q -ary Hamming codes, q q -ary Simplex codes, generalized Reed-Muller codes, ovoid codes, maximum arc codes, the extended hyperoval codes, and near MDS codes. Many classes of both distance-optimal and dimension-optimal LRCs are presented in this paper. The minimum linear locality of many families of linear codes are settled with the general theory developed in this paper.

But the converse may not be true. If a linear code has locality, it must have the minimum locality. The same is true for linear locality. Regarding linear locality, we have the following questions.

Question 1

What linear codes have linear locality?

Question 2

If a linear code has linear locality, what is the minimum linear locality and how does one compute the minimum linear locality?

The first objective of this paper is to answer the two questions above. We will develop some general theory answering these

II
Preliminaries

To study the minimum linear locality of linear codes, we need to introduce some basics of linear codes and cyclic codes.
Since our methodology is combinatorial and group-theoretic, we have to introduce the automorphism groups of linear codes
and combinatorial

II-A

BCH and cyclic codes

An
[n,k,d]
code
{\mathcal{C}}
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is said to be
cyclic
if for each

(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathcal{C}}
we have
(c_{n-1},c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-2})\in{\mathcal{C}}
.
We identify a vector
(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{n}

with the polynomial
c(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}x^{i}\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)[x]/(x^{n}-1).

Then a code
{\mathcal{C}}
of length
n
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
corresponds to a subset
{\mathcal{C}}(x)
of the quotient ring

{\mathrm{GF}}(q)[x]/(x^{n}-1)
, where

{\mathcal{C}}(x):=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}c_{i}x^{i}:c=(c_{0},c_{1},\cdots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathcal{C}}\right\}.

It is easy to see that
{\mathcal{C}}
is cyclic if and only if the set
{\mathcal{C}}(x)
is an ideal of the ring
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)[x]/(x^{n}-1)
.

It is well-known that each ideal of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)[x]/(x^{n}-1)
is principal. Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\langle g(x)\rangle
be a
cyclic code, where
g(x)
is monic and has the smallest degree among all the
generators of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
g(x)
is unique and called the
generator polynomial,

and
h(x)=(x^{n}-1)/g(x)
is referred to as the
check polynomial
of
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Let
n
be a positive integer with
\gcd(n,q)=1
, and let
m={\mathrm{ord}}_{n}(q)
be the order of
q
modulo
n
.
Let
\alpha
be a generator of the multiplicative group
{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})^{*}
. Put
\beta=\alpha^{(q^{m}-1)/n}
.
Then
\beta
is a primitive
n
-th root of unity in
{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})
. The minimal polynomial
{\mathsf{M}}_{\beta^{s}}(x)

of
\beta^{s}
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is defined to be the monic polynomial of the smallest degree over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

with
\beta^{s}
as a root and is given by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{M}}_{\beta^{s}}(x)=\prod_{i\in C_{s}}(x-\beta^{i})\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)[x],

(3)

where
C_{s}=\{sq^{i}\bmod n:0\leq i\leq m-1\}
and is called the
q
-cyclotomic class containing
s
.

Let
\delta
be an integer with
2\leq\delta\leq n
and let
h
be an integer.
A
BCH code
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

with length
n
and
designed distance

\delta
, denoted by
{\mathcal{C}}_{(q,n,\delta,h)}
, is a cyclic code with
generator polynomial

\displaystyle g_{(q,n,\delta,h)}={\mathrm{lcm}}({\mathsf{M}}_{\beta^{h}}(x),{\mathsf{M}}_{\beta^{h+1}}(x),\cdots,{\mathsf{M}}_{\beta^{h+\delta-2}}(x)).

(4)

If
h=1
, the code
{\mathcal{C}}_{(q,n,\delta,h)}
with the generator polynomial in (
4
) is referred to as
a
narrow-sense
BCH code. If
n=q^{m}-1
, then
{\mathcal{C}}_{(q,n,\delta,h)}
is called a
primitive
BCH code.

BCH codes form a subfamily of cyclic codes with very attractive properties and applications. In many cases BCH codes are the
best linear codes. For instance, among all binary cyclic codes of odd lengths
n
with
n\leq 125
the best cyclic code is always a BCH code
except for two special cases
[
11
]
. Reed-Solomon codes are also BCH codes and have been widely used in data storage systems, communication devices and consumer electronics.

Let

be a linear code with length

. Below we introduce several basic operations on

for obtaining new codes.
Let

, which has length

.
Let

on

, which

. After puncturing

of

, and is denoted by

. It is known that

The

\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}=\left\{(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1},c_{n})\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{n+1}:(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1})\in{\mathcal{C}}\mbox{ with }\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}=0\right\}.

Let

be a generator matrix of
{\mathcal{C}}
. Suppose that

. Then the

, denoted

, of

is the linear code over

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}G\\

denotes the all-

and dimension

.

II-C

Automorphism groups of linear codes

The
permutation automorphism group
of
{\mathcal{C}}
,
denoted by
{\mathrm{PAut}}({\mathcal{C}})
, is the set of coordinate permutations that map a code
{\mathcal{C}}
to itself.
A square matrix having exactly one nonzero element of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
in each row and column is
called a
monomial matrix
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
. A monomial matrix

can be written in

DP
or the form
PD_{1}
, where
P
is a permutation matrix and
D
and
D_{1}
are diagonal matrices.
The

of
{\mathcal{C}}
refers to the
set of monomial matrices that map
{\mathcal{C}}
to itself. Obviously,
{\mathrm{PAut}}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{MAut}}({\mathcal{C}})
.
The

of
{\mathcal{C}}
, denoted by
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
, is the set
of maps of the form
M\gamma
that map
{\mathcal{C}}
to itself,
where
M
is a monomial matrix and
\gamma
is a field automorphism. If

,

{\mathrm{PAut}}({\mathcal{C}})
,
{\mathrm{MAut}}({\mathcal{C}})
and

are the same. If

is a prime,
{\mathrm{MAut}}({\mathcal{C}})
and

{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
are identical. In general, we have

{\mathrm{PAut}}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{MAut}}({\mathcal{C}})\subseteq{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}).

By the definitions above, each element in
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is of the form
DP\gamma
, where
D
is a diagonal matrix,

P
is a permutation matrix, and
\gamma
is an automorphism of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
.
The automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is said to be
t
-transitive
if for every pair of
t
-element ordered
sets of coordinates, there is an element
DP\gamma
of the automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
such that its
permutation part
P
sends the first set to the second set.
The automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is said to be
t
-homogeneous
if for every pair of
t
-element sets of coordinates, there is an element
DP\gamma
of the automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
such that its
permutation part
P
sends the first set to the second set. If the automorphism group
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is
t
-transitive,
then it must be
t
-homogeneous. But the converse may not be true. For simplicity, we say that
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is transitive
(respectively, homogeneous) if
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is
1
-transitive
(respectively,
1
-homogeneous).

The support designs of linear codes

Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a set of
n

{\mathcal{B}}
be a set of

-subsets of
{\mathcal{P}}
, where

.
Let
t
be an integer with
1\leq t\leq k
. The pair

is an
incidence structure, where the incidence relation is the set membership. The incidence structure

(n,k,\lambda)

. The elements of

If

This paper considers only simple

A

There are different ways to construct

Let

For each

III
General theory about the minimum linear locality of linear codes

The objective of this section is to develop some general theory about the linear locality of linear codes over finite fields.
In particular, we will answer Questions
1
and
2
raised in Section
I
. The zero
code
\{{\mathbf{0}}\}
and the code
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{n}
are not interesting in both theory and practice, and are called

trivial codes
. If the dual distance
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
of
{\mathcal{C}}
is
1
, then
{\mathcal{C}}
has a zero coordinate, which can be
punctured away without affecting the error-correcting capability. Hence, in theory and practice codes with
d({\mathcal{C}})\leq 1
or
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})\leq 1
are not interesting, and are called trivial codes. In this paper, we consider the minimum linear locality
only for nontrivial linear codes, i.e., linear codes with
d({\mathcal{C}})\geq 2
and
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})\geq 2
. Recall that
we use the elements
in
[n]
to index the coordinate positions in a linear code of length
n
.

III-A

Some general theory the linear locality of nontrivial linear codes

The following lemma directly follows from the definition of linear locality of linear codes, and is well known in the literature.
We will need it later.

Lemma 5
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code of length
n
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}
has linear locality
r
if and only if for each
i\in[n]
the dual code
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
has a codeword
{\mathbf{c}}
of Hamming weight at most
r+1
such that
i\in{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{c}})
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code of lenth
n
. Then there exists a positive integer
w
with
2\leq w\leq n
such that
A_{w}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})>0
and

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{w}\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S\supseteq[n].

(6)

Let
w
be the smallest integer such that
A_{w}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})>0
and (
6
) holds. Then

{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
w-1
.

Proof.

Suppose that there is an integer
i
in
[n]
such that

\displaystyle i\not\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S.

Then
c_{i}^{\perp}=0
for all codewords
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}=(c_{0}^{\perp},\cdots,c_{n-1}^{\perp})
in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
.
Consequently, the vector
{\mathbf{c}}=(0,\cdots,0,1,0,\cdots,0)
of length
n
, which has only one nonzero coordinate
1
in coordinate position
i
,
is a codeword in
{\mathcal{C}}
. This is contrary to the assumption that
d\geq 2
.

Let
w
be the smallest integer such that
A_{w}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})>0
and (
6
) holds.
Then every integer
i\in[n]
is contained in
{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{c}}^{\perp})
, where
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}

is some codeword with weight at most
w
in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. Then the code symbol
c_{i}
in
{\mathcal{C}}

can be recovered by a linear combination of the coordinates in the positions in
{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{c}}^{\perp})\setminus\{i\}
.
Then
{\mathcal{C}}
has linear locality
w-1
.

If the code symbol
c_{i}
in every codeword
{\mathbf{c}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
can be recovered linearly by

c_{i}=u_{1}c_{i_{1}}+\cdots+u_{h}c_{i_{h}}

where
u_{i}\neq 0
and
R_{i}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{h}\}
is the corresponding recovering set of the code symbol
c_{i}
.
Then
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
has a codeword with weight
h+1
. Hence,
w-1
is the minimum
linear locality.
∎

Theorem
6
means that every nontrivial linear code has a minimum linear locality,
and tells us how to calculate the minimum linear locality. In practice, it is also necessary and important
to find a recovering set
R_{i}
for each code symbol
c_{i}
. But we will not deal with this problem in
this paper.

III-B

Linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}
with minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1

It follows from Lemma
5
that the minimum linear locality of a nontrivial linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
is at least

d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
. Hence, nontrivial linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}
with minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
would be very
interesting in both theory and practice. In this subsection, we develop some general results for such special
codes. It will be seen later that there are indeed nontrivial linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}
with minimum linear locality
more than
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
.

Corollary 7
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code of length
n
and put
d^{\perp}=d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
.
Then
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d^{\perp}-1
if and only if

\displaystyle\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S=[n].

(7)

Proof.

The desired conclusion directly follows from Theorem
6
and Lemma
5
.
∎

The following result is well known in the literature. We show that it is a corollary of Theorem
6
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial cyclic code of length
n
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
.

Proof.

Put
d^{\perp}=d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
.
Let
i\in[n]
.
Let
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}
be a minimum weight codeword in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. By definition, the Hamming weight
{\mathtt{wt}}({\mathbf{c}}^{\perp})\geq 2
.
Consequently,
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}
has a nonzero coordinate. Since
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
is also cyclic,
we can assume
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}_{i}\neq 0
. We then deduce that

\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S\supseteq\{i\}.

The desired conclusion then follows from Theorem
6
.
∎

Corollary 9
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code of length
n
and put
d^{\perp}=d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
.
If
({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}),{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}))
is a
1
-
(n,d^{\perp},\lambda_{1}^{\perp})
design with
\lambda_{1}^{\perp}\geq 1
,
then
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d^{\perp}-1
.

Proof.

By the definition of
1
-designs, every
i\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
is covered in
\lambda_{1}^{\perp}
blocks in
the block set
{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{d^{\perp}}\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S=\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S=[n].

The desired conclusion then follows from Theorem
6
.
∎

It should be noted that there are many nontrivial linear codes with minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
, but

({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}),{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}))
is not a
1
-design. Hence, the converse of Corollary
9
is
not true. Corollary
9
will be one of the tools for studying the minimum linear locality of some
families of linear codes in this paper. Another tool is documented in the following corollary.

Corollary 10
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code. If
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
or
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
is transitive, then
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality

d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
and
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}})-1
.

Proof.

Put
d^{\perp}=d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
.
Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
and have length
n
. Suppose that
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
is transitive. Let
{\mathbf{c}}^{\perp}
be a minimum
weight codeword in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. Then
{\mathtt{wt}}({\mathbf{c}}^{\perp})\geq 2
. Let
i\in{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{c}}^{\perp})
. For each
j\in[n]\setminus\{i\}
, there is an automorphism
DP\gamma
in
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
such that the permutation part
P
sends
i
to
j
,
as
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
is transitive. This means there is another minimum weight codeword
({\mathbf{c}}^{\prime})^{\perp}
in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}

such that
j\in{\mathrm{suppt}}(({\mathbf{c}}^{\prime})^{\perp})
. Consequently,

\bigcup_{j=1}^{d^{\perp}}\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{j}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S=\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S=[n].

It then follows from Theorem
6
that
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
.

In general,
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
and
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
are different. However, it is straightforward to prove that

{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}})
is transitive if and only if
{\mathrm{Aut}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
is so. Then the remaining desired conclusion follows
from the first conclusion proved above.
∎

Note that combining Theorem
4
and Corollary
9
gives another proof of
Corollary
10
.
Sometimes we may need to use Corollary
9
, as the automorphism group of a code may be unknown.
Sometimes it is more convenient to use Corollary
10
. Sometimes both corollaries can be used to
study the linear locality of some linear codes. In many cases, both corollaries cannot be used to do so.
It looks impossible to find out all nontrivial linear codes with minimum linear locality

d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
. But Corollaries
9
and
10
can be employed to find many families
of such codes. Most of the families of linear codes documented in the monograph
[
12
]
are such codes,
as they support
t
-designs with
t\geq 2
or their automorphism groups are doubly homogeneous.
Other families of such linear codes are not documented in
[
12
]
, as the monograph
[
12
]

does not include linear codes supporting
1
-designs but not
2
-designs.

The following result would also be useful in some cases.

Theorem 11
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial linear code. If
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
is spanned by its minimum weight codewords, then

{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
have length
n
. Let
i\in[n]
. If

i\not\in\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{d^{\perp}}({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})}S,

then
(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)
would be a codeword in
{\mathcal{C}}
, where the nonzero coordinate
1
is in coordinate
position
i
, as all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
span
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. This is contrary to the fact that

{\mathcal{C}}
is nontrivial. The desired conclusion then follows from Corollary
7
.
∎

III-C

The minimum linear locality of extended cyclic code

While any nontrivial cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
, extended
cyclic codes may not have such property. Note that even if
{\mathcal{C}}
is nontrivial, the extended code

\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
may be trivial, as
d(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})
could be
1
.
The automorphism group of any cyclic code is transitive and each cyclic code supports
1
-designs.
But these may not be true for extended cyclic codes.
In this section, we consider
the linear locality of the extended cyclic codes and their duals.

Let
H
and
\overline{H}
denote the parity-check matrix of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
, respectively.
Then we have the following well known lemma
[
23
]
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be an
[n,\kappa,d]
code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
. Then
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
is an
[n+1,\kappa,\overline{d}]
linear code, where
\overline{d}=d
or
d+1
. In the binary case,
\overline{d}=d
if
d
is even, and

\overline{d}=d+1
otherwise.

In addition, the parity-check matrix
\overline{H}
of
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
can be deduced from that of
{\mathcal{C}}

by

\displaystyle\overline{H}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{1}}&1\\
H&{\mathbf{0}}\end{array}\right],

where
{\mathbf{1}}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
and
{\mathbf{0}}=(0,0,\ldots,0)^{T}
.

We now prove the following result, which will be needed later.

Theorem 13
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial cyclic code. If
d(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})=d({\mathcal{C}})+1
, then
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}
has minimum linear
locality
d({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
have length
n
. By definition,
d({\mathcal{C}})\geq 2
and
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})\geq 2
. Since
d(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})=d({\mathcal{C}})+1
,
we know that
d((\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp})\geq 2
. Therefore,
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
is nontrivial. Let
{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{c}}_{h}
be
all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}
, and let
\overline{{\mathbf{c}}_{i}}
be the extended codeword of
{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
in
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
.
Since
d({\mathcal{C}})\geq 2
and
{\mathcal{C}}
is cyclic, we have

\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{c}}_{i})=[n].

Since
d(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})=d({\mathcal{C}})+1
, the extended coordinate in each
\overline{{\mathbf{c}}_{i}}
is nonzero. As a result, we get

\bigcup_{i=1}^{h}{\mathrm{suppt}}(\overline{{\mathbf{c}}_{i}})=[n+1].

The desired conclusion then follows from Corollary
7
.
∎

Corollary 14
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a nontrivial binary cyclic code. If
d({\mathcal{C}})
is odd, then
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}
has minimum linear
locality
d({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.

The desired conclusion follows from Lemma
12
and Theorem
13
.
∎

Corollary
14
has determined the minimum linear locality of
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}

for all binary cyclic codes. Specifically, either
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}
is a trivial code or nontrivial binary linear code
with minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}})
for each nontrivial cyclic code
{\mathcal{C}}
.

IV
The minimum linear locality of some known families of linear codes

The objective of this section is to study the minimum linear locality of several families of linear codes which
are geometric codes and their punctured and shortened codes.
We wish to find out some families of optimal LLRCs.

IV-A

The minimum linear locality of the
q
-ary Hamming codes and Simplex codes

A parity check matrix
H_{(q,m)}
of the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is defined by choosing
for its columns a nonzero vector from each one-dimensional subspace of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{m}
. In terms of
finite geometry, the columns of
H_{(q,m)}
are the points of the projective geometry
{\mathrm{PG}}(m-1,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
.
Hence
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
has length
n=(q^{m}-1)/(q-1)
and dimension
n-m
.
It is well known that
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
has minimum weight 3 and
any
[(q^{m}-1)/(q-1),(q^{m}-1)/(q-1)-m,3]
code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is monomially equivalent
to the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}

[
23
]
. Note that the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
is permutation-equivalent
to a cyclic code when
\gcd(m,q-1)=1
. By Corollary
8
, its minimum linear locality is known in this case.
However, its minimum locality may not be known for the case that
\gcd(m,q-1)\neq 1
. The linear locality of the binary
Hamming and Simplex codes was settled in
[
22
]
. Note that binary Hamming and Simplex codes are
permutation-equivalent to cyclic codes. In this subsection, we investigate the minimum linear locality of the
q
-ary
Hamming and Simplex codes.

The weight distribution of
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
is given in the following lemma
[
26
]
.

Lemma 15
.

The weight distribution of
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
is given by

\displaystyle q^{m}A_{k}({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq\frac{q^{m-1}-1}{q-1}\\
0\leq j\leq q^{m-1}\\
i+j=k\end{subarray}}\left[{\frac{q^{m-1}-1}{q-1}\choose i}{q^{m-1}\choose j}\bigg((q-1)^{k}+(-1)^{j}(q-1)^{i}(q^{m}-1)\bigg)\right]

for
0\leq k\leq(q^{m}-1)/(q-1)
.

The duals of the Hamming codes
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
are called Simplex codes, denoted by
{\mathcal{S}}_{(q,m)}
, which have parameters

[(q^{m}-1)/(q-1),m,q^{m-1}]
. The nonzero codewords of the
[(q^{m}-1)/(q-1),m,q^{m-1}]
Simplex
codes all have weight
q^{m-1}
.

Theorem 16
.

The Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
is an
(n,n-m,3,q;q^{m-1}-1)
-LLRC and the Simplex code
{\mathcal{S}}_{(q,m)}
is an

(n,m,q^{m-1},q;2)
-LLRC. Furthermore, the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{(q,m)}
are
k
-optimal.

Proof.

The Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
has parameters
[n,n-m,3]
and its dual code is a one-weight code. Then by the Assmus-Mattson Theorem, the codewords of minimum weight in the Hamming code and Simplex code both hold a 2-design. So
({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}),{\mathcal{B}}_{3}({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}))
and
({\mathcal{P}}({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}^{\perp}),{\mathcal{B}}_{q^{m-1}}({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}^{\perp}))
are
1
-designs. Hence, the conclusions on the minimum linear locality of the two codes follow from Corollary
9
.

We now prove the dimension optimality of
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
. Putting
t=1
and the parameters of the
(n,n-m,3,q;q^{m-1}-1)
-LLRC into
the right-hand side of the CM bound in (
2
), we have

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\leq\min_{s\in\mathbb{{Z}}_{+}}\{rs+k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-(r+1)s,d)\}

\displaystyle\leq r+k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-(r+1),d)

\displaystyle=q^{m-1}-1+k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-q^{m-1},3)

\displaystyle\leq n-m,

where the last inequality holds due to the fact that
k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-q^{m-1},3)\leq n-q^{m-1}-m+1
, which follows from the sphere packing bound. Therefore, the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)}
is
k
-optimal.

Putting
t=1
and the parameters of
(n,m,q^{m-1},q;2)
-LLRC into the right-hand side of the CM bound in (
2
), we have

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\leq r+k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-(r+1),d)

\displaystyle=2+k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-3,q^{m-1})

\displaystyle\leq m,

where the last inequality holds due to the fact that
k_{opt}^{(q)}(n-3,q^{m-1})\leq m-2
, which follows from the Plotkin bound. Therefore, the Simplex code
{\mathcal{S}}_{(q,m)}
is
k
-optimal.
This completes the proof.
∎

According to the Singleton-like bound in (
1
), we can obtain the following family of
d
-optimal LLRCs.

Corollary 17
.

When
m=3
, the Hamming code
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,3)}
is a
(q^{2}+q+1,q^{2}+q-2,3,q;q^{2}-1)
-LLRC and is both
d
-optimal and
k
-optimal.

Proof.

The parameters and dimension optimality of
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,3)}
directly follow from Theorem
16
. Hence, we only need to prove the distance optimality. It is easy to verify that the parameters of
{\mathcal{H}}_{(q,3)}
satisfy the equality in (
1
). Hence, it is a
d
-optimal
(q^{2}+q+1,q^{2}+q-2,3,q;q^{2}-1)
-LLRC. This completes the proof.
∎

If a linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
supports 2-designs, then the punctured code
{\mathcal{C}}^{\{t_{1}\}}
or shortened code
{\mathcal{C}}_{\{t_{1}\}}
may
support 1-designs. Then we can settle the minimum linear locality of
{\mathcal{C}}^{\{t_{1}\}}
or
{\mathcal{C}}_{\{t_{1}\}}
.
The parameters of some punctured codes and shortened codes of the Hamming code are given in the following
lemma
[

]
.

.

Let
n=(q^{m}-1)/(q-1)\geq 4
, and let
t_{1}
be any coordinate position of codewords in

•

[n-1,n-m-1,3]

\displaystyle A_{k}(({\mathcal{H}}_{(q,m)})_{\{t_{1}\}})

IV-B

The minimum linear locality of the generalised Reed-Muller codes over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

The general affine group
{\mathrm{GA}}_{1}({\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m}))
is defined by

{\mathrm{GA}}_{1}({\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m}))=\{ax+b:a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})^{*},\ b\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})\},

which acts on
{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})
doubly transitively
[
12
, Section 1.7]
.

We can index the coordinates of a linear code of length
q^{m}
with the elements of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})
. When each permutation
in
{\mathrm{GA}}_{1}({\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m}))
is applied to a codeword, it is applied to the indices of the coordinates. A linear code
{\mathcal{C}}

of length
q^{m}
is said to be affine-invariant if
{\mathrm{GA}}_{1}({\mathrm{GF}}(q))
fixes
{\mathcal{C}}
. It follows from Theorem

4
that affine-invariant codes supports
2
-designs. By Corollary
10
,
all affine-invariant codes
{\mathcal{C}}
have minimum locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
. There are many infinite families of affine-invariant
codes
[
12
, Chapter 6]
. Our objective in this section is to study the minimum linear locality of the generalised Reed-Muller
codes and obtain a class of either
k
-optimal or almost
k
-optimal LLRCs.

For any integer
j=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}j_{i}q^{i}
, where
0\leq j_{i}\leq q-1
for all
0\leq i\leq m-1
and
m
is a positive integer, we define

\displaystyle{\mathtt{wt}}_{q}(j)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}j_{i},

(11)

where the sum is taken over the ring of integers, and is called the
q
-weight of
j
.
Let
\ell
be a positive integer with
1\leq\ell<(q-1)m
. The
\ell
-th order

punctured generalized Reed-Muller code

{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)^{*}
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is the cyclic code of length
n=q^{m}-1
with generator polynomial

\displaystyle g(x)=\prod_{\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{1 \leq j \leq n-1}{ \wt_q(j) < (q-1)m-\ell}}(x-\alpha^{j}),

(12)

where
\alpha
is a generator of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q^{m})^{*}
. Since
{\mathtt{wt}}_{q}(j)
is a constant function on
each
q
-cyclotomic coset modulo
n=q^{m}-1
,
g(x)
is a polynomial over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
.

The generalized Reed-Muller code
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)
is defined to be the extended code
of
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)^{*}
, and its parameters are given below
[
3
]
.

Theorem 21
.

Let
0\leq\ell<q(m-1)
. Then the generalized Reed-Muller code
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)
has length
n=q^{m}
, dimension

\displaystyle\kappa=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}\binom{i-jq+m-1}{i-jq},

(13)

and minimum weight

\displaystyle d=(q-\ell_{0})q^{m-\ell_{1}-1},

(14)

where
\ell=\ell_{1}(q-1)+\ell_{0}
and

0\leq\ell_{0}<q-1
.

The minimum linear locality of the generalized Reed-Muller code
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)
is given in the following theorem.

Theorem 22
.

Let
0\leq\ell<q(m-1)
and
m(q-1)-1-\ell=\ell^{\prime}_{1}(q-1)+\ell^{\prime}_{0}
with
0\leq\ell^{\prime}_{0}<q-1
.
The generalized Reed-Muller code
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}(\ell,m)
is a
[q^{m},\kappa,d,q;(q-\ell^{\prime}_{0})q^{m-\ell^{\prime}_{1}-1}-1]
LLRC, where
\kappa
and
d
were

The minimum linear locality of ovoid codes

In the projective space PG
(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
with
q>2
, an ovoid
{\mathcal{V}}
is a set of
q^{2}+1
points such that
no three of them are collinear (i.e., on the same line). In other words, an ovoid is a
(q^{2}+1)
-cap
(a cap with
q^{2}+1
points) in PG
(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
, and thus a maximal cap. Two ovoids are said to be
equivalent if there is a collineation (i.e., automorphism) of PG
(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
that sends one to the
other.

A classical ovoid
{\mathcal{V}}
can be defined as the set of all points given by

{\mathcal{V}}=\{(0,0,1,0)\}\cup\{(x,y,x^{2}+xy+ay^{2},1):x,y\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\}

where
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
is such that the polynomial
x^{2}+x+a
has no root in
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
. Such ovoid is
called an elliptic quadric, as the points come from a non-degenerate elliptic quadratic form.

For
q=2^{2e+1}
with
e\geq 1
, there is an ovoid which is not an elliptic quadric, and is called the
Tits ovoid. It is
defined by

{\mathcal{T}}=\{(0,0,1,0)\}\cup\{(x,y,x^{\sigma}+xy+y^{\sigma+2},1):x,y\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\},

where
\sigma=2^{e+1}
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be an ovoid in PG
(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
with
q>2
. Denote

{\mathcal{V}}=\{{\mathbf{v}}_{1},{\mathbf{v}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{q^{2}+1}\},

where each
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
is a column vector in
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{4}
. Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{V}}
be the linear code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with generator
matrix

G_{\mathcal{V}}=[{\mathbf{v}}_{1}{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\cdots{\mathbf{v}}_{q^{2}+1}].

It is known that
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{V}}
is a
[q^{2}+1,4,q^{2}-q]
code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with weight enumerator

1+(q^{2}-q)(q^{2}+1)z^{q^{2}-q}+(q-1)(q^{2}+1)z^{q^{2}}

and its dual
{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
is a
[q^{2}+1,q^{2}-3,4]
almost MDS code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

[
12
, Chapter 13]
. Conversely, the set of
column vectors of a generator matrix of any
[q^{2}+1,4,q^{2}-q]
code over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
. Hence, ovoids in PG
(3,{\mathrm{GF}}(q))
and
[q^{2}+1,4,q^{2}-q]

{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

{\mathrm{GF}}(q)

Ovoid codes are very interesting in combinatorics, as they support
3
-designs, which are documented below

[

, Chapter 13]

The Singleton defect of an

{\mathcal{C}}

is so. Then an
[n,k]

over

]

[

]

]

Let

[n,k,n-k]

{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
.

•

If

, then
{\mathcal{C}}

n-k
and

Theorem 47
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd and let
f(x)
be an oval polynomial over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with coefficients in
{\mathrm{GF}}(2)

{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
is a
[q+1,3,q-2]
NMDS code
over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with weight enumerator

\displaystyle A(z)=1+(q-1)(q-2)z^{q-2}+\frac{(q-1)(q^{2}-5q+12)}{2}z^{q-1}+

\displaystyle(q-1)(4q-5)z^{q}+\frac{(q-1)(q^{2}-3q+4)}{2}z^{q+1}.

This class of NMDS codes are very important to us, as they demonstrate that some nontrivial NMDS codes

indeed

d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
rather than

. We will need the following lemma later.

Lemma 48
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd and let
f(x)
be an oval polynomial over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with coefficients in
{\mathrm{GF}}(2)
.
Then
f(x)+x+1=0
does not have a solution
x\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
.

Proof.

By the definition of oval polynomials,
0
and
1
are not not solutions of the equation
f(x)+x+1=0
.
Suppose that
f(a)+a+1=0
for some
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\setminus\{0,1\}
. Since
f(x)
has coefficients
in
{\mathrm{GF}}(2)
, we have
f(a^{2})+a^{2}+1=0
and
f(a^{4})+a^{4}+1=0
. It then follows from Lemma
43

that the set
\{a,a^{2},a^{4}\}
has cardinality at most
2
.
If
a=a^{2}
, then
a\in\{0,1\}
, which contradicts to the assumption that
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\setminus\{0,1\}
.
If
a=a^{4}
, then
a=0
or
a^{3}=1
. If
a^{3}=1
, we have
a=1
as
\gcd(3,q-1)=1
.
Hence,
a=a^{4}
implies that
a\in\{0,1\}
,
which contradicts to the assumption that
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\setminus\{0,1\}
.
If
a^{2}=a^{4}
, then
a\in\{0,1\}
, which contradicts to the assumption that
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)\setminus\{0,1\}
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 49
.

Let
m\geq 3
be odd and let
f(x)
be an oval polynomial over
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
with coefficients in
{\mathrm{GF}}(2)
, and let
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
be
the code in Theorem
47
. Then
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}})-1
and is a

d
-optimal and
k
-optimal
(q+1,q-2,3,q;q-3)
-LLRC, and
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp})

and is an almost
d
-optimal and
k
-optimal
(q+1,3,q-2,q;3)
-LLRC.

Proof.

Since all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
were not characterized in
[
38
]
, we have to do this job here.
Let
{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
and
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}
denote the first, second and third rows in the generator matrix
G_{f}
above.

We first first consider all the codewords
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+b{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
, where
b\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{*}\setminus\{1\}
. By definition,

{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+b{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=(b,1+\alpha^{1}+bf(\alpha^{1}),\ldots,1+\alpha^{q-2}+bf(\alpha^{q-2}),0,1+b).

For any
i
with
1\leq i\leq q-2
, put
b=(1+\alpha^{i})/f(\alpha^{i})
. It then follows from Lemma
48

that
b\neq 1
. Clearly,
b\neq 0
, as
\alpha
is a generator of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
. It then follows from Lemma
43

that there is a unique
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,q-2\}\setminus\{i\}
such that
b=(1+\alpha^{j})/f(\alpha^{j})
. Consequently,

{\mathtt{wt}}({\mathbf{v}}_{3}+{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+b{\mathbf{v}}_{1})=q+1-3=q-2.

The support of this codeword is

{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{v}}_{3}+{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+b{\mathbf{v}}_{1})=[q+1]\setminus\{i,j,q-1\}.

The total number of such choices of
b
is
(q-2)/2
. Since
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}
is the all-one codeword, we have already characterized

(q-1)(q-2)/2
minimum weight codewords of this form in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
.

We then consider all the codewords
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+a{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
, where
a\in{\mathrm{GF}}(q)^{*}\setminus\{1\}
. By definition,

{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+a{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=(a,1+a\alpha^{1}+f(\alpha^{1}),\ldots,1+a\alpha^{q-2}+f(\alpha^{q-2}),1+a,0).

For any
i
with
1\leq i\leq q-2
, put
a=(1+f(\alpha^{i}))/\alpha^{i}
.
It then follows from Lemma
48

that
a\neq 1
. Clearly,
a\neq 0
, as
\alpha
is a generator of
{\mathrm{GF}}(q)
and
f(1)=1
. It then follows from Lemma

43

that there is a unique
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,q-2\}\setminus\{i\}
such that
b=(1+f(\alpha^{j}))/\alpha^{j}
. Consequently,

{\mathtt{wt}}({\mathbf{v}}_{3}+a{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+{\mathbf{v}}_{1})=q+1-3=q-2.

The support of this codeword is

{\mathrm{suppt}}({\mathbf{v}}_{3}+a{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+{\mathbf{v}}_{1})=[q+1]\setminus\{i,j,q\}.

The total number of such choices of
a
is
(q-2)/2
. Since
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}
is the all-one codeword, we have already characterized

(q-1)(q-2)/2
minimum weight codewords of this form in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
.

In the first case above, the coordinate in position
q-1
in the codewords
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+b{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
is zero and the coordinate
in position
q
in these codewords is nozero.
In the second case above, the coordinate in position
q-1
in the codewords
{\mathbf{v}}_{3}+a{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
is nonzero and the coordinate in position
q
in these codewords is zero. Therefore, the minimum weight codewords in the two forms do not
overlap. By Theorem
47
, we have characterized all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
. From
the discussions above, we have

\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{q-2}({\mathcal{C}}_{f})}S=[q+1].

It then follows from Corollary
7
that
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp}
has minimum locality
d({\mathcal{C}}_{f})-1
.

The discussions above showed that the coordinate in position
0
in all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
is nonzero.
It then follows from Lemma
34
that the coordinate in position
0
in all the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp}
is zero. This means that

0\not\in\bigcup_{S\in{\mathcal{B}}_{3}({\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp})}S.

Hence,
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
does not have minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp})-1
. It then follows from Theorem
31

that
{\mathcal{C}}_{f}
has minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}_{f}^{\perp})
. The remaining desired conclusions then follow from
Theorems
47
and
32
.
∎

VI
Summary and concluding remarks

The objectives of this paper are to develop some general theory for the minimum linear locality of linear codes and search for
d
-optimal
or
k
-optimal LLRCs in known families of linear codes. Below is a summary of the major general results on the minimum linear locality of nontrivial linear codes developed in this paper.

1.

We proved that every nontrivial linear code has a minimum linear locality and showed how to find it
(see Theorem
6
).

2.

We gave a necessary and sufficient condition for a nontrivial linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
to have the minimum linear
locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
(see Corollary
7
).

3.

We determined the minimum linear locality for nontrivial linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}
such that the minimum weight
codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
support a
1
-design
(see Corollary
9
). This general result has settled the minimum linear locality of many families of linear codes,
as many families of linear codes support
1
-designs.

4.

We determined the minimum linear locality for nontrivial linear codes
{\mathcal{C}}
whose automorphism group is transitive
(see Corollary
10
). This general result has settled the minimum linear locality of many families of linear codes,
including the affine-invariant linear codes.

5.

We determined the minimum linear locality of
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}
under the condition that
d(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})=d({\mathcal{C}})+1

for each nontrivial linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
(see Theorem
13
), and settled the minimum linear locality of
(\overline{{\mathcal{C}}})^{\perp}
for each nontrivial binary linear code
{\mathcal{C}}
(see Corollary
14
).

6.

We proved that the minimum linear locality of an NMDS code
{\mathcal{C}}
is either
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
or
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})
(see Theorem
31
), and further proved that
{\mathcal{C}}
is either a
d
-optimal and
k
-optimal or an almost
d
-optimal and

k
-optimal LLRC (see Theorem
32
).

These general results have settled the minimum linear locality of many families of nontrivial linear codes. Furthermore, the minimum linear locality of these codes was not studied in the literature. Hence,
we have reached our first objective.

After studying a number of families of known linear codes with the general theory developed, we have found many classes of optimal LLRCs. These optimal LLRCs were not reported in the literature. Table
I
lists fourteen classes of
k
-optimal LLRCs. Table
II

lists nineteen classes of LLRCs which are both
d
-optimal and
k
-optimal and have different parameters. In both tables,

•

n_{h}=(q^{m}-1)/(q-1)
,

•

\surd
means that the code is optimal with the Singleton-like or CM bound,

•

A
means that the code is almost optimal with respect to the Singleton-like bound, and

•

?
means that the optimality is open.

These classes of optimal LLRCs demonstrate that we have reached our second objective.

We remark that the locality of locally recoverable codes in the literature actually is the linear locality, but may not be the
minimum linear locality. This paper has treated the minimum linear locality of nontrivial linear codes.
Availability is another interesting parameter of LLRCs. All the LLRCs presented in this paper naturally have availability
1
,
and some of them may have availability
2
or more. It is extremely hard to
develop general theory for LLRCs with availability more than
1
, although such LLRCs are available in the literature. To study the maximum availability of an LLRC code
{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to the minimum linear locality
d({\mathcal{C}}^{\perp})-1
,
one has to characterize the minimum weight codewords in
{\mathcal{C}}^{\perp}
. This is a very hard problem in general. The reader
is cordially invited to investigate the maximum availability of these optimal LLRCs documented in this paper.
Finally, we point out that all the LLRCs presented in this paper are from known linear codes in the literature and our objective
is to study their minimum linear locality and optimality with respect to the Singleton-like and CM bounds.
