Adjoint-based calibration of nonlinear stochastic differential equations
Jan Bartsch, Robert Denk, Stefan Volkwein

Abstract
To study the nonlinear properties of complex natural phenomena, the evolution of the quantity of interest can be often represented by systems of coupled nonlinear stochastic differential equations (SDEs). These SDEs typically contain several parameters which have to be chosen carefully to match the experimental data and to validate the effectiveness of the model. In the present paper the calibration of these parameters is described by nonlinear SDE-constrained optimization problems. In the optimize-before-discretize setting a rigorous analysis is carried out to ensure the existence of optimal solutions and to derive necessary first-order optimality conditions. For the numerical solution a Monte-Carlo method is applied using parallelization strategies to compensate for the high computational time. In the numerical examples an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and a stochastic Prandtl-Tomlinson bath model are considered.



keywords

Optimization of SDEs, first-order optimality conditions, Monte Carlo methods,
stochastic gradient methods, Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model, stochastic Prandtl-Tomlinson equations.

Introduction

Natural processes inherit noise and uncertainties and therefore are often modeled using stochastic differential equations (SDEs). Such equations consist of a deterministic part and a stochastic part, where the latter one is usually modeled by Brownian motions; see, e.g.,
[
55
,
56
]
. The application of SDEs ranges from physics and biology to finance; cf.
[
13
,
28
,
51
]
, for instance. All these SDEs include parameters that need to be calibrated. For this calibration (also called parameter identification), usually real-world data is used and parameters are searched that lead to the best agreement of measured and predicted data. Therefore, parameter identification and optimal control of such processes are of interest to many communities and have been the subject of extensive research.

The main motivation for our work is the calibration of models that investigate the rheological properties of fluids using the microrheology technique. Specifically, the study of the behavior of fluids using a so-called tracer particle suspended in a fluid.
Key tools to study properties of complex fluids are nonlinear bath models, for example, the Stochastic Prandtl-Tomlinson (SPT) model
[
30
,
29
,
43
]
.
While linear models have been investigated in the Markovian framework
[
15
,
52
,
53
]
, less is known for the nonlinear case together with memory effects in which the Markovian framework is no longer applicable.
To study the properties of fluids, systems of coupled nonlinear SDEs representing the movement of particles are studied.
The SDEs in this case typically possess the structure of (generalized) nonlinear Langevin equations
[
33
,
35
,
58
]
.

In this paper, we express the calibration problems as SDE-constrained optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\min J({\bm{X}},u)=j({\bm{X}})+j_{T}({\bm{X}}(T))+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,{\|u\|}_{\mathscr{U}}^{2}\\
&\hskip 2.84526pt\text{subject to }{\bm{X}}\text{ satisfies an SDE on }[0,T]\text{ for parameter or control }u\in{{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\subset{\mathscr{U}}\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

with
\kappa\geq 0
, a parameter or control space
{\mathscr{U}}
and with properly chosen functionals
j
,
j_{T}
which will be specified in
sections

3
and
4
, respectively. In particular, the tracking type functional

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} J({\bm{X}},u)&=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{0}^{T}{\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(t)])-{c^{\mathsf{d}}}(t)\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(T)])-{c^{\mathsf{d}}_{T}}\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\kappa\,{\|u\|}_{\mathscr{U}}^{2}\bigg)\end{aligned}

(2)

is included. Notice that the objective defined in (
2
) differs from the one typically used in the stochastic optimal control setting (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
46
]
), but is present in literature for calibration problems; see, e.g.,
[
20
,
17
,
22
,
31
,
36
]
).

To solve (
1
) let us mention three different approaches:

•

One can lift the problem to the level of partial differential equations (PDEs).
This lift can be viewed as taking the limit in several senses; see, e.g.,
[
45
]
for a review in the deterministic setting.
One way is to define a probability density function that contains information on the probability of the state of the model being in a certain configuration at a certain timestep. The PDE that governs the evolution of the probability density is given by the Fokker-Planck equation
[
9
,
1
]
.
In this setting, one can apply tools from PDE-constrained optimization; see, e.g.,
[
27
,
54
]
.
If one wants to account for the structure of the space of probability measures,
we refer to
[
4
,
7
,
14
,
21
]
.
However, one encounters certain challenges with this lift to the level of PDEs.
One of them is the exponential growth of numerical complexity with the dimension of the model since we encounter a high-dimensional PDE.
This is also known as the
curse of dimensionality
.

•

In another approach one stays on the microscopic level and directly characterizes the optimal control or calibration using SDEs
[
5
,
6
,
37
]
. Here, no probability density functions come into play and hence no PDEs have to be solved. However, their huge weakness is their quite slow convergence
[
24
]
.

•

Further, hybrid methods are utilized, where the models are solved on the microscopic level, but – to calculate the gradient – the density functions are assembled. These methods suffer from the time-consuming bottleneck of assembling probability density functions in hybrid methods
[
2
,
3
]
.

In the present paper we study (
1
) as an infinite dimensional optimization problem; see, e.g.,
[
27
,
38
,
54
]
.
The first objective is to derive sufficient conditions for the existence of optimal solutions and to carefully set up the correct framework in terms of solution spaces and control or parameter spaces.
After we guarantee sufficient differentiability of the cost functional and the constraints, we derive first-order necessary optimality conditions by an adjoint calculus.
This allows us to characterize an optimal control or optimal parameters; see, e.g.,
[
27
]
.
Up to the authors’ knowledge, the rigorous derivation of the reduced gradient for the calibration problem using adjoint calculus is not present in the literature.
Nevertheless, there are some works heading in a similar direction, in particular,
[
34
]
and very recently
[
11
]
.

To solve (
1
) numerically, one possibility is to start with deriving first-order necessary optimality conditions for the optimal control problem on the continuous level. After this, the system is discretized and optimization schemes are applied. This approach is also known as
first-optimize-then-discretize
.
Alternatively, in the
first-discretize-then-optimize
approach (
1
) is discretized first and then the first-order optimality system is derived for the discretized optimization problem.
Utilizing the Lagrangian framework, we derive an optimality system consisting of the state equation, the adjoint equation and the optimality condition. Both approaches are compared. Moreover, convergence of the value of the discretized cost functional to the value of the continuous one is proved.
Notice that the exploiting of adjoint-based models and staying on the microscopic level by using Monte-Carlo computations have several advantages, in particular, they are easy to implement and inherit huge flexibility
[
24
]
.

The paper is structured as follows: In
section

2
we introduce the notation and recall some preliminaries when dealing with SDEs. In
section

3
, a general state equation is introduced that governs the optimization problem that is defined in
section

4
, where we prove the existence of solutions to the optimization problem. In
section

5
we introduce a discretization for (
1
) and derive the associated first-order necessary optimality system which is the basis for a gradient-based method utilized for our numerical tests. The Fréchet differentiability of the constraints and the objective as well as the first-order optimality system for (
1
) are proved in
section

6
. The work is completed in
section

7
, where we formulate our optimization strategy and validate it using two different examples. At first, we apply our method to the mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process where we try to find a time-dependent control function that drives the evolution of the SDE to follow a desired trajectory.
Then, we solve the calibration model governed by the SPT model with one and two particles.

\displaystyle\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}\texttt{a}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\leq\Big(\int_{0}^{T}{\|\texttt{a}(s)\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}s\Big)^{2}\leq T\int_{0}^{T}{\|\texttt{a}(s)\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

2)

for some constant

. The proof of (

Taking the expectation and noting that the expectation of the stochastic integral equals zero, we obtain

\displaystyle\ell_{{\bm{\Lambda}}}({\bm{Y}})={\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\bm{Y}}_{\circ}^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\circ}+\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\mathtt{a}(t)^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}(t)+{\langle\mathtt{b}(t),\tilde{\mathtt{b}}(t)\rangle}_{\mathrm{F}}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t\Big],

Remark 3

\Delta B_{li}G_{l}^{ji}G_{\ell}^{jk}
, and we get

{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Delta B_{li}G_{l}^{ji}G_{\ell}^{jk}\,\Delta B_{\ell k}\Big]={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Delta B_{li}G_{l}^{ji}G_{\ell}^{jk}\Big]\,{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Delta B_{\ell k}\Big]=0.

In the same way, the expectation vanishes for
\ell<l
and for
i\not=k
. With this and
{\mathbb{E}}[(\Delta B_{li})^{2}]=t_{i+1}-t_{i}=\Delta t
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big\|\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}G_{l}\,\Delta{\bm{B}}_{l}\Big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbb{E}}\big[(G_{l}^{ji})^{2}\big]{\mathbb{E}}\big[(\Delta B_{li})^{2}\big]=\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}(G_{l}^{ji})^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\|G_{l}\|}_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\big].

From the definition of
G_{l}
and the Lipschitz continuity of
b
(see
19
-
2)
) it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\Big\|\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}\big(b({\bm{X}}^{N}_{l},u,t_{l})-b({\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{N}_{l},\tilde{u},t_{l})\big)\,\Delta{\bm{B}}_{l}\Big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\bigg]\leq 2L_{U}^{2}\bigg(\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\|\bm{\Delta}_{l}^{N}\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\big]+{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}^{2}\bigg).

Inserting this into (
47
) yields

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\|\bm{\Delta}^{N}_{\nu+1}\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\big]\leq c_{1}\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\|\bm{\Delta}_{l}^{N}\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\big]+c_{1}\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}^{2}\quad\text{for }\nu=0,\dots,N-1

with
c_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}4(T+1)L_{U}^{2}
. Now an application of the discrete Gronwall inequality (see
[
12
, Theorem 3.2]
) gives the desired estimate

\displaystyle\max_{\nu=0,\dots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\|{\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{N}-{\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{N}\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\big]\leq C_{1}\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}^{2}

with a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(T,L_{U})
, which shows (
45
).
The proof of (
46
) follows the same lines, writing

\displaystyle a({\bm{X}}^{N}_{l},u,t_{l})=\Big(a({\bm{X}}^{N}_{l},u,t_{l})-a(0,u,t_{l})\Big)+a(0,u,t_{l})

(see the proof of
theorem

10
). We obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\|^{2}\big]\leq c_{2}\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{l}\|^{2}\big]+c_{2}+c_{2}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\Delta t\Big({\|a(0,u,t_{l})\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}+{\|b(0,u,t_{l})\|}_{{\mathrm{F}}}^{2}\Big),

with a constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(L_{U},T,{\bm{X}}_{\circ})
. By
19
-
2)
, the (deterministic) coefficient
a(0,u,\cdot)
is continuous and therefore uniformly continuous in the interval
[0,T]
. Therefore,
\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\|a(0,u,t_{l})\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\chi_{\mathscr{I}^{\nu}}(\cdot)
converges uniformly to
\|a(0,u,\cdot)\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
for
N\to\infty
. Integration with respect to
t\in[0,T]
shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\Delta t\,{\|a(0,u,t_{l})\|}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}^{2}\to\int_{0}^{T}{\|a(0,u,\cdot)\|}^{2}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq L_{U}^{2},

and, in particular, this sum is bounded by a constant for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Treating the coefficient
b
in the same way, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu+1}\|^{2}\big]\leq c_{2}\Delta t\sum_{l=0}^{\nu}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{l}\|^{2}\big]+c_{2}^{\prime}\quad\text{ for }\nu=0,\dots,N-1

for some constant
c_{2}^{\prime}=c_{2}^{\prime}(L_{U},T,{\bm{X}}_{\circ})
. Now the discrete Gronwall inequality yields (
46
).

We set
{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{c^{\mathsf{d}}}(t_{\nu})
for
\nu=0,\dots,N
and introduce the discrete cost functional

\displaystyle J^{N}({\bm{X}}^{N},u)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{N}\big]\big)-{c_{N}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\,{\|u\|}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}^{2}

for
{\bm{X}}^{N}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{N}
and
u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
as well as the
discrete reduced cost functional

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{N}(u)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}J^{N}({\mathcal{S}}^{N}(u),u)\quad\text{for }u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}.

The following is the main result on convergence of the semidiscrete approximation.

Theorem 21
.

Let
19
hold.

1)

The map
\hat{J}^{N}\colon{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant independent of
N
, i.e., there exists a constant
L_{d}>0
such that for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all

we have

\displaystyle\big|\hat{J}^{N}(u)-\hat{J}^{N}(\tilde{u})\big|\leq L_{d}\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}.

2)

For any
u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
, we have
\hat{J}^{N}(u)\to\hat{J}(u)
for
N\to\infty
.

3)

Let
\{u^{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
be a sequence with

for

, and let
\{N_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subset{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence with

for

. Then

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{N_{k}}(u^{k})\to\hat{J}(u)\quad\text{for }k\to\infty.

Proof.

1) For
\nu=0,\dots,N
and
{\bm{X}}^{N},{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{N}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\big\|{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\big]-{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{N}_{\nu}\big]\big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

\displaystyle=\big\|{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}-{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{N}_{\nu}\big]\big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\leq\big\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}-\tilde{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\big\|_{L^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T}\,\big\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}-\tilde{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d})},

which shows that the map
{\mathscr{X}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;{\bm{X}}^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}]
is Lipschitz continuous.
Since
19
-
2)
holds, we can apply
lemma

20
.
We conclude that
{\mathcal{S}}^{N}\colon{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\to{\mathscr{X}}^{N}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant independent of
N
and that
{\mathcal{S}}^{N}({{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}})
is bounded by virtue of (

Therefore, the same holds for the map

\displaystyle\phi_{\nu}\colon{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\quad u\mapsto{\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{N}\big]\quad\text{with }{\bm{X}}^{N}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathcal{S}}^{N}(u).

By
19
-
1)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad x\mapsto\left\|{\mathcal{C}}(x)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}

(48)

is continuously differentiable and therefore Lipschitz on the bounded range of
\phi_{\nu}
. As the composition of Lipschitz functions is again a Lipschitz function, we see that

\displaystyle{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\to{\mathbb{R}},\quad u\mapsto\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}\big[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}\quad\text{with }{\bm{X}}^{N}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathcal{S}}^{N}(u)

is Lipschitz continuous with a Lipschitz constant
L>0
independent of
N
.
As
u\mapsto\|u\|^{2}_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}
is continuously differentiable and therefore Lipschitz with some constant
L^{\prime}>0
on the compact set
{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
, we obtain for
u,\tilde{u}\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
and for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\big|\hat{J}^{N}(u)-\hat{J}^{N}(\tilde{u})\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}L\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}+\frac{1}{2}L\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}+\frac{\kappa}{2}L^{\prime}\|u-\tilde{u}\|_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}

\displaystyle\leq L_{d}\,{\|u-\tilde{u}\|}_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}

for
L_{d}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}((T+1)L+\kappa L^{\prime})/2
, where we used
\Delta t=T/N
, which gives part 1).

2) Let
u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
and set
{\bm{X}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathcal{S}}(u)
and
{\bm{X}}^{N}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}{\mathcal{S}}^{N}(u)
for
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By
theorem

10
, we have
{\bm{X}}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
which implies that
{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(\cdot)]\in C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. From this and the continuity of
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{c^{\mathsf{d}}}
, we see that the integrand in the cost functional
J({\bm{X}},u)
(see (
26
)), which is given as
\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(\cdot)])-{c^{\mathsf{d}}}(\cdot)\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}
, is a continuous function on
[0,T]
and can be approximated uniformly by the step function

\displaystyle t\mapsto\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\big\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(t_{\nu})])-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}\chi_{\mathscr{I}^{\nu}}(t).

Integrating this with respect to
t\in[0,T]
, we obtain

J^{N}\big(({\bm{X}}(t_{\nu}))_{\nu=0,\dots,N},u\big)\to J({\bm{X}},u)\quad\text{for }N\to\infty.

(49)

Now we use the fact that
{\bm{X}}^{N}
is computed with the Euler-Murayama scheme (

) which is known to be of convergence order

(see
[

). Therefore, we can apply the convergence result from

which tells us that

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu=0,\dots,N}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\big\|{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}-{\bm{X}}(t_{\nu})\big\|^{2}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Big]\to 0\quad\text{for }N\to\infty.

This implies
{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}]\to{\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(t_{\nu})]
for

and, by uniform continuity of the function (

) on bounded subsets,

\displaystyle\sup_{\nu=0,\dots,N}\bigg|\big\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}^{N}_{\nu}])-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}-\big\|{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{E}}[{\bm{X}}(t_{\nu})])-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\big\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}\bigg|\to 0\quad\text{for }N\to\infty.

Summing up over
\nu
and using
\Delta t=T/N
again, we get

\displaystyle\Big|J^{N}\big({\bm{X}}^{N},u\big)-J^{N}\big(({\bm{X}}(t_{\nu}))_{\nu=0,\dots,N},u\big)\Big|\to 0\quad\text{for }N\to\infty.

(50)

As
J^{N}({\bm{X}}^{N},u)=\hat{J}^{N}(u)
and
J({\bm{X}},u)=\hat{J}(u)
, the statement in 2) now follows from (
49
) and (
50
).

3) This is an immediate consequence of parts 1) and 2). In fact, for
\varepsilon>0
we first choose
k_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
, we have
\|u^{k}-u\|_{{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}}\leq\varepsilon/(2L_{d})
with
L_{d}
from 1).
Then
|\hat{J}^{N}(u^{k})-\hat{J}^{N}(u)|\leq\varepsilon/2
holds by 1) for all

.
Now we apply 2) and

to see that there exists
k_{1}\geq k_{0}

. This yields
|\hat{J}^{N_{k}}(u^{k})-\hat{J}(u)|\leq\varepsilon
for

, which shows 3).

5.2
The fully discretized problem

In this section, we explain how we solve (
42
) numerically.
In
section

5.1
, we already described the discretization with respect to time and formulated a semi-discrete scheme (
44
).
In order to obtain a fully discrete scheme, we use a Monte Carlo approximation.
More specifically, we consider
M\gg 1
realizations of the SDE and for every realization and every time instant
t_{\nu}\in[0,T]
, we precompute samples of the Brownian motion. We denote these samples by

\displaystyle\Delta{\bm{B}}^{\mu}=\big[\Delta{\bm{B}}_{1}^{\mu}\big|\ldots\big|\Delta{\bm{B}}_{N}^{\mu}\big]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times N}\quad\text{with }\Delta{\bm{B}}_{\nu}^{\mu}=\big(\Delta B^{\mu}_{\nu,j}\big)_{j=1}^{m}\text{ and }\Delta B^{\mu}_{\nu,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t)

for
\mu=1,\ldots,M
. In order to simplify the notation, we set
h=(M,N)
.

We can now introduce the discretization of the state equation (
14
) as follows: For any given control
u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}
find

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}^{h}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{h}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\big\{{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{\mu}_{\nu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,\big|\,\nu=0,\ldots,N\text{ and }\mu=1,\ldots,M\big\}

satisfying

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}&={\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu}+a({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})\Delta t+b(\bm{X}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})\Delta\bm{B}_{\nu}^{\mu},&&\hskip-5.69054pt\mu=1,\ldots,M,\nu=0,\ldots,N-1,\\
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\mu}&={\bm{X}}_{\circ},&&\hskip-5.69054pt\mu=1,\ldots,M.\end{aligned}

(51)

Notice that the solution in (
51
) depends on the pre-computed samples of the Brownian motion.
For the discrete
{\bm{X}}^{h}
, we define the discrete expectation value as the arithmetic mean, i.e., for
\nu\in\{0,\ldots,N\}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{M}\left[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{\nu}\right]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}{\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{\mu}.

(52)

The finite-dimensional (Hilbert) space
{\mathscr{X}}^{h}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{(N+1)M}
and is supplied by the weighted inner product

\displaystyle{\langle{\bm{X}}^{h},{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{X}}^{h}}=\frac{\Delta t}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N}\big({\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\big)^{\top}{\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{\mu}={\mathbb{E}}^{M}\bigg[\Delta t\sum_{\nu=0}^{N}\big({\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{h}\big)^{\top}{\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{h}\bigg]\quad\text{for }{\bm{X}}^{h},{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{h},

where we set
{\bm{X}}^{h}_{\nu}=\{{\bm{X}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{M}
and
{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}_{\nu}=\{{\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\}_{\mu=1}^{M}
for
\nu\in\{0,\ldots,N\}
.
Notice that (
51
) is an explicit difference scheme. Thus, the existence of the sequence
{\bm{X}}^{h}
satisfying (
51
) is clear. In that case the discrete non-linear solution operator

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{h}:{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\to{\mathscr{X}}^{h},\quad

\displaystyle{\bm{X}}^{h}={\mathcal{S}}^{h}(u)\text{ is the unique solution to \eqref{SDEDisc} for }u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}

given the
M
pre-computed Brownian increments

is well defined. We formulate a discrete version of (
42
) as follows:

\displaystyle\min\hat{J}^{h}(u)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}},

(53)

where the discrete reduced cost functional is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{J}^{h}(u)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}J^{h}({\bm{X}}^{h},u)\quad\text{for }u\in{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}_{\mathsf{ad}}}\text{ and }{\bm{X}}^{h}={\mathcal{S}}^{h}(u)

with

\displaystyle J^{h}({\bm{X}}^{h},u)\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Delta t\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{\nu}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{N}\big]\big)-{c_{N}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\kappa\,{\|u\|}_{{\mathscr{U}}^{\mathsf{c}}}^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta t}{2}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\bigg\|{\mathcal{C}}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}{\bm{X}}^{h}_{\nu}\Big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\bigg\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\bigg\|{\mathcal{C}}\Big(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}{\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{N}\Big)-{c_{N}^{\mathsf{d}}}\bigg\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\big|u_{i}|^{2}.

The next step is to introduce the associated
Lagrange functional for the discretized problem (
53
). It is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}({\bm{X}}^{h},u,{\bm{\Lambda}}^{h})\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle:$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle:$}}}{=}}\\
&\qquad\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\Delta t\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[\bm{X}^{h}_{\nu}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{N}\big]\big)-{c_{N}^{\mathsf{d}}}\right\|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}^{2}+\kappa\,{\|u\|}_{\mathscr{U}}^{2}\bigg)\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\big[{\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}-{\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu}-a({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})\,\Delta t-b({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})\,\Delta{\bm{B}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\big]^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}\\
&\qquad+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{0}-{\bm{X}}_{\circ}^{\mu}\big]^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mu}_{0}\end{aligned}

(54)

for
{\bm{X}}^{h}=\{{\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu}\}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{h}
, and where we have introduced the Lagrange multiplier

\displaystyle{\bm{\Lambda}}^{h}=\{{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,|\,\nu=0,\ldots,N\text{ and }\mu=1,\ldots,M\}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{h}.

In particular,
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{0}^{\mu}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the initial condition.
Note that

\displaystyle\underbrace{b({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})}_{\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times m}}\underbrace{\Delta{\bm{B}}_{\nu}^{\mu}}_{\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Delta B_{\nu,j}^{\mu}\underbrace{b_{j}({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})}_{\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\quad\text{for }\mu=1,\ldots,M,\;\nu=0,\ldots,N-1.

Next, we derive the adjoint equation by computing the partial derivative
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{X}}
of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
{\bm{X}}^{h}
.
Let us refer to
[
31
]
for a similar approach, but in our case, we consider data that is given over the whole time interval
[0,T]
.
Notice that in the discretize-before-optimize approach, the solution of the adjoint problem is only the variable
{\bm{\Lambda}}^{h}
.
This is a crucial difference to the optimize-before-discretize approach, where the solution of the adjoint problem is in fact a tuple of stochastic processes (cf.
section

6.3
and
[
57
]
).
For any direction
{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}=\{{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{\mu}_{\nu}\}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{h}
,
we obtain the following expression

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\bm{X}}({\bm{X}}^{h},u,{\bm{\Lambda}}^{h}){\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}&=\Delta t\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\Big[{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{\nu}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\Big]^{\top}{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[\bm{X}^{h}_{\nu}\big]\big){\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}_{\nu}\big]\\
&\quad+\Big[{\mathcal{C}}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{N}\big]\big)-{c_{\nu}^{\mathsf{d}}}\Big]^{\top}{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\big({\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{X}}^{h}_{N}\big]\big){\mathbb{E}}^{M}\big[{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{h}_{N}\big]\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\Big[{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}-{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{\mu}_{\nu}-\big(a_{x}({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu}){\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\big)\,\Delta t\Big]^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\sum_{\nu=0}^{N-1}\bigg[\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Delta B_{\nu,j}^{\mu}\big(b_{jx}({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu}){\bm{\tilde{X}}}_{\nu}^{\mu}\big)\bigg]^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mu}_{\nu+1}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\big[{\bm{\tilde{X}}}^{\mu}_{0}\big]^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\mu}_{0}\end{aligned}

(55)

with
b_{jx}({\bm{X}}^{\mu}_{\nu},u,t_{\nu})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

Numerical experiments

In this section, we explain our optimization strategy. Afterwards, we perform numerical experiments. In the first one, we find an optimal control such that the mean and variance of the governing process follow precisely the prescribed data. The governing process in this case is the well-known mean-reverting Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.
In the second one, we calibrate a model consisting of systems of SDEs by finding optimal parameters.
The specific model is the so-called Stochastic Prandtl-Tomlinson (SPT) model that is used to study microrheological processes of viscous fluids.

Let us mention that in both examples our cost functionals are not of the specific form introduced in

To model the complex fluid, the colloidal particle is investigated with a coupling to one or more bath particles that describe the background. The system of differential equations describing the position of the tracer and
K
bath particles is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X_{1}(t)&=\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\bigg(-\partial_{x}V_{\mathrm{ext}}(X_{1}(t),t)-\sum_{k=2}^{K+1}V_{\mathrm{int},k}^{\prime}\big(X_{1}(t)-X_{k}(t)\big)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{1}(t),\\
\mathrm{d}X_{k}(t)&=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\,V_{\mathrm{int},k}^{\prime}\big(X_{1}(t)-X_{k}(t)\big)+\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\,\mathrm{d}B_{k}(t)\quad\text{for }k=2,\ldots,K+1,\end{aligned}

(105)

for
t\in(0,T]
, where for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,T]

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{ext}}(x,t)=\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{ext}}}{2}\,\big(x-v_{0}t\big)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{\mathrm{int},k}(x)=V_{0,k}\cos\bigg(\frac{2\pi x}{d_{k}}\bigg)\text{ for }k=2,\ldots,K+1.

The system (
105
) is completed with the initial condition
X_{i}(0)=X^{eq}_{i,\circ}
,
i=1,\ldots,K+1
, where
X^{eq}_{i,\circ}\in L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}})
is the equilibrium distribution of the corresponding particle given the external potential
V_{\mathrm{ext}}(X,0)
. In system (
105
), the variable
X_{1}
denotes the position of the tracer particle and the
X_{k}
’s the position of the bath particle
p_{k}
,
k=2,\ldots,K+1
. The
\gamma_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,K+1
, are the friction coefficients of the corresponding particle and are assumed to be known.
The external potential
V_{\mathrm{ext}}
may be applied using optical traps (laser beams) of strength
\kappa_{\mathrm{ext}}>0
to realize harmonic potentials.
This potential has a trapping effect, which means that the particle cannot leave the potential easily.
This trap is then pulled with a constant velocity
v_{0}
through the surrounding medium.
The internal forces between the tracer and the bath particles are denoted by
V_{\mathrm{int},k}
for
k=2,\ldots,K+1
.
The force

\displaystyle\text{``$\xi_{k}(t)=\dot{B}_{k}(t)$''}\quad\text{for }t\in[0,T]\text{ and }k=1,\ldots,K+1

resembles a random force for which the following mean value relation and fluctuation-dissipation theorem must hold

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\xi_{i}(t)\big]=0,\quad{\mathbb{E}}\big[\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\big]=2k_{B}\mathrm{T}\gamma_{i}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\quad\text{for }i,j=1,\ldots,K+1,

(106)

where
k_{B}>0
is Boltzmann’s constant and
\mathrm{T}>0
the temperature of the bath.
The requirements (
106
) are fulfilled by Brownian motion, hence we can apply our setting above.

Remark 35
.

Notice that in
[
29
,
30
]
, the authors did not specify any initial condition within the SPT model (
105
).
The reason for this is that the system needs to be in equilibrium.
To realize this numerically, we need to integrate over the transient phase in order to generate sensible data that will be used to calculate the simulation data.
To generate
{\bm{X}}^{eq}_{\circ}
, any initial condition can be used and the model needs to evolve for a certain equilibration time
t_{eq}>0
.
Then, we use the state of the system after
t_{eq}
as
{\bm{X}}^{eq}_{\circ}
.
In order to account for a possible high equilibration time, one needs to resort to parallelization techniques such as multi-threading that we use in our simulations.

The goal is to identify the pairs

\displaystyle=u_{2k-3}\cos\big(2\pi u_{2k-2}x\big)
