Generalized coupled dipole method for thermal far-field radiation
Florian Herz and Svend-Age Biehs ∗

Abstract
We introduce a many body theory for thermal far-field emission of dipolar dielectric and metallic nanoparticles in the vicinity of a substrate within the framework of fluctuational electrodynamics. Our theoretical model includes the possibility to define the temperatures of each nanoparticle, the substrate temperature, and the temperature of the background thermal radiation, separately. To demonstrate the versatility of our method, we apply it in an exemplary way by discussing the thermal radiation of four particle assemblies of SiC and Ag nanoparticles above a planar SiC and Ag substrate. Furthermore, we use discrete dipole approximation to determine the thermal emission of a spherical nanoparticle in free space and close to a substrate. Finally, the calculation of the thermal far-field radiation of a sharp Si tip close to a SiC substrate using the discrete dipole approximation including the near-field scattering by the tip as well as the thermal emission of the tip and the contribution of the substrate which is partially blocked by the tip serves as another example.



I
Introduction

The theory of near- and far-field thermal radiation in many-body systems has greatly advanced in the last decade. This development arose from the generalization of Draine’s work on dipolar many-body interactions
Draine 1988
by allowing for fluctuational and induced dipolar moments
Ben-Abdallah et al. 2011
. With this approach one could study thermally fluctuating fields generated by interacting dipolar objects and, thus, describe near-field as well as far-field thermal emission of an arbitrary number of dipolar objects each thermalized at its own heat bath with given temperature. Further works generalized this model rendering it possible to place dipolar objects in an environment at a given temperature
Messina et al. 2013
and to include magnetic dipolar contributions
Dong et al. 2017
. Therewith, thermal infrared radiation emitted by small metallic objects can be described more accurately since they are known to have a massive effective magnetic response due to the induction of eddy currents
Tomchuk and Grigorchuk 2006
;
Dedkov and Kyasov 2007
;
Chapuis et al. 2008a
;
Chapuis et al. 2008b
. It should be mentioned that also more general models were introduced in order to provide a theoretical basis for many-body systems with objects of arbitrary size and shape
Krüger et al. 2012
;
Messina and Antezza 2011a
;
Messina and Antezza 2011b
;
Zhu et al. 2018
as well as numerical methods
Rodriguez et al. 2013
;
Polimeridis et al. 2015
;
Jin et al. 2016
.

Here, we will focus on the dipole model only, which is the theoretical workhorse of many recent works, because of its conceptional and
numerical simplicity. For example, using this model it could be shown that the heat transfer between two dipolar objects can be enhanced
by the presence of a substrate or more general third objects supporting surface waves like phonon polaritonic media
Sääskilahti et al. 2014

Dong et al. 2018

Messina et al. 2018
;
Asheichyk et al. 2017
;
Asheichyk and Krüger 2018

Messina et al. 2018
, Moiré bilayer graphene
Yang et al. 2021

Zhang et al. 2019
;
Ott et al. 2021a
. For non-reciprocal media even more interesting many-body effects were demonstrated like persistent heat currents and fluxes
Zhu and Fan 2016
;
Silveirinha 2017
;
Guo et al. 2019
;
Ott et al. 2019a
, giant magnetic resistances
Latella and Ben-Abdallah 2017
;
Moncada-Villa et al. 2015
, Hall and anomalous Hall effect
Ben-Abdallah 2016
;
Ott et al. 2020
, circular polarized emission and thermal angular momentum and spin
Silveirinha 2017

Ott et al. 2018

In this work, we provide a generalized many-body theory for the thermal far-field emission of
N
dipolar objects in a given environment. Based on the coupled dipole method of Ben-Abdallah et al.
Ben-Abdallah et al. 2011
, first Edalatpour and Francoeur
Edalatpour and Francoeur 2014
and then Ekeroth et al.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
provided a model for near-field heat exchange and, subsequently, also for thermal emission in the context of discrete dipole approximation (DDA) of a macroscopic object divided into many small subvolumes, so called voxels, which act as fluctuating dipoles emitting thermal radiation. Here, we want to extend that description by including magnetic dipole moments to be able to describe metallic nanoparticles. Furthermore, we include an environment consisting of thermal radiation at a given temperature in which
N
dipolar objects and a material part at another temperature, for instance a substrate, are embedded. Therefore, our model can be regarded as a
N
-body extension of the dipole model in Refs.
Joulain et al. 2014
;
Jarzembski and Park 2017
;
Herz et al. 2018
;
Herz and Biehs 2021
. Our model can be used to study thermal emission of any
N
-body assembly close to a substrate, for instance, but it can also be employed for a discrete dipole approximation of a single or several macroscopic objects close to a substrate or environment as depicted in Fig.
1
. To demonstrate the versatility of our model, we will study the thermal emission of different combinations of four nanoparticles close to a planar substrate, the thermal emission of a macroscopic spherical particle close to a substrate using DDA, and the thermal emission and scattering of near-field thermal radiation by a sharp tip, again, using DDA. This allows us also to check the validity of our results against those of Refs.
Dong et al. 2017
;
Ekeroth et al. 2017
;
Edalatpour and Francoeur 2016
. Finally, we will also discuss the impact of different choices of the dressed polarizability to some extent.

Our paper is organized as follows: In Sec II. we introduce the theoretical framework and derive the general
N
-body expressions. This includes also the introduction of different definitions of the dressed polarizability. In Sec. III we provide several numerical results including those for the thermal emission of a single nanoparticle, of a combination of four nanoparticles with different temperatures, a DDA calculation for a spherical particle, and a DDA calculation for a sharp tip. Finally, in Sec. IV we summarize our findings.

II
Theoretical framework

In this chapter we introduce the general theoretical expressions for the emitted power of a heated system that consists of
N
nanoparticles treated as point-like dipoles in presence of a substrate as sketched in Fig.
1
. This results in two possible applications to determine thermal emission. One is pursued by approximating macroscopic objects by voxels like in Fig.
1
(a). In that case, we refer to this procedure as DDA. The other one describes the thermal emission of several separated subwavelength emitters as depicted in Fig.
1
(b). Since this situation is different from DDA, we will refer to it by using the term coupled dipole method (CDM). In the CDM-cases considered in the following, these emitters will be spherical. In both cases the same formalism applies which we develop in the following. We start by determining the electric and magnetic fields generated by these dipoles in terms of the dressed polarizability for which we also discuss the different choices used in literature. Finally, we apply the fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) to derive an expression for the emitted power of the
N
-body system through a “detection plane”.

II.1
Fluctuational fields

Let us start by considering a configuration of
1\leq\alpha\leq N
nanoparticles arbitrarily distributed in front of a substrate occupying the half space at
z<0
. We ascribe different temperatures
T_{\alpha}
,
T_{s}
, and
T_{b}
to the nanoparticles, substrate, and background, respectively. We assume that the nanoparticles fulfill the dipole approximation. That means their radii
R_{\alpha}
obey
3R_{\alpha}<d_{\alpha}\ll\lambda_{\text{th}}
with particle-substrate distance
d_{\alpha}
and thermal wavelength
\lambda_{\text{th}}
. Each nanoparticle and the substrate possess non-magnetic, homogeneous, and isotropic material properties, therefore, we neglect their permeabilities but we allow for eddy currents inside of the nanoparticles so that we can treat metallic nanoparticles
Dong et al. 2017
;
Tomchuk and Grigorchuk 2006
;
Dedkov and Kyasov 2007
;
Chapuis et al. 2008a
;
Chapuis et al. 2008b
. To include the effect of self interaction, we want to start by composing the equations for the fields in the nanoparticle
\alpha
within the framework of macroscopic electrodynamics. For these fields we make the ansatz (
k=\text{E},\text{H}
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})+\mu_{0}\omega\sum_{l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\biggl[\mathrm{i}\int_{V_{\alpha}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{3}r\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r})\mathbf{J}_{l}(\mathbf{r})+\omega\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\mathbf{d}_{l}^{\beta}\biggr].

(1)

Here
\mathbf{F}_{\text{E}}=\mathbf{E}
is the electric field,
\mathbf{F}_{\text{H}}=\mathbf{H}
the magnetic field,
\mathbf{d}_{\text{E}}=\mathbf{p}
the electric dipole moment, and
\mathbf{d}_{\text{H}}=\mathbf{m}
the magnetic dipole moment.
\mathbf{J}_{\text{H}}
describes the above mentioned eddy currents. The index “env” indicates a background field consisting of the radiation of the background and substrate,
\mathds{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
denotes the corresponding Green’s function,
V_{\alpha}
the volume of nanoparticle
\alpha
,
\omega
the angular frequency,
\mathrm{i}
the imaginary unit, and
\mu_{0}
the vacuum’s permeability. Exploiting the long wavelength approximation (LWA) and, thus, assuming that the current density is constant inside of the minuscule nanoparticle volume, we extract the current density from the volume integral so that we can interpret it as volume average of the Green’s function. After replacing the current density by the corresponding dipole moment, we obtain (
k=\text{E},\text{H}
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})+\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\sum_{l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\biggl[\expectationvalue{\mathds{G}_{kl} (\mathbf{r}_\alpha)}\mathbf{d}_{l}^{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\mathbf{d}_{l}^{\beta}\biggr]

(2)

with the volume averages

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\mathds{G} (\mathbf{r})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V_{\alpha}}\int_{V_{\alpha}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\mathds{G}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(3)

Note that this integral contains a singularity at
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}
. However, a rigorous treatment is provided by
Lakhtakia 1992
;
Fikioris 1965
;
Yaghjian 1980
. To obtain a closed formula for the fields, we decompose the dipole moments into a fluctuating part and an induced one, so that

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}^{\alpha}_{k,\text{fl}}+\varepsilon_{0}V_{\alpha}\chi_{k}^{\alpha}\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha}).

(4)

Here,
\varepsilon_{0}
denotes the vacuum’s permittivity and
\chi_{\text{E/H}}
are the electric and magnetic susceptibilities which will be specified later. Inserting Eq. (
4
) into Eq. (
2
), we find for the fields inside of nanoparticle
\alpha
the closed expression

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})+\sum_{l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\biggl[\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\expectationvalue{\mathds{G}_{kl} (\mathbf{r}_\alpha)}\mathbf{d}_{l,\text{fl}}^{\alpha}+k_{0}^{2}V_{\alpha}\chi_{l}^{\alpha}\expectationvalue{\mathds{G}_{kl} (\mathbf{r}_\alpha)}\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\beta\neq\alpha}\Bigl(\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\mathbf{d}_{l,\text{fl}}^{\beta}+k_{0}^{2}V_{\beta}\chi_{l}^{\beta}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{in}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Bigr)\biggr]

(5)

with the vacuum wave number
k_{0}=\omega/c
. This system of
N
vector equations for
1\leq\alpha\leq N
can be rewritten in a more compact way by exploiting the block matrix notation. Therefore, let us introduce the block vectors

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{F}}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{F}_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{1}),...,\mathbf{F}_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{N}))^{t},

(6)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{d}}_{k}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{d}_{k}^{1},...,\mathbf{d}_{k}^{N})^{t}

(7)

and the block matrices

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\expectationvalue{\mathds{G}_{kl} (\mathbf{r}_\beta)}+(1-\delta_{\alpha\beta})\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r}_{\beta}),

(8)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{k}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}V_{\beta}\chi_{k}^{\beta}.

(9)

With these definitions we can express the corresponding fields by the two block matrix equations (
k=\text{E},\text{H}
)

\displaystyle\underline{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\underline{\mathbf{F}}_{k,\text{env}}+\sum_{l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\Bigl[\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{kl}\underline{\mathbf{d}}_{l,\text{fl}}+k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{kl}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{l}\underline{\mathbf{F}}_{l,\text{in}}\Bigr].

(10)

Eventually, one can interpret these two block matrix equations as one new block matrix equation introducing the block vectors

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{F}}}

\displaystyle=(\underline{\mathbf{E}},\underline{\mathbf{H}})^{t},

(11)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{d}}}

\displaystyle=(\underline{\mathbf{p}},\underline{\mathbf{m}})^{t}

(12)

and the new block matrices

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EE}}&\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EH}}\\
\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{HE}}&\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EE}}\end{pmatrix},

(13)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{X}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{E}}&0\\
0&\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{H}}\end{pmatrix}.

(14)

Inserting the latter into Eq. (
10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{F}}}_{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=\Bigl[\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{X}}}\Bigr]^{-1}\Bigl(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{F}}}_{\text{env}}+\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{d}}}_{\text{fl}}\Bigr).

(15)

This expression allows us now to derive the fields outside of the nanoparticles. By inserting Eq. (
15

4

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{d}}}

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{1}}}+k_{0}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}\Bigr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{d}}}_{\text{fl}}+\varepsilon_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rightarrow}}{{\mathbf{F}}}_{\text{env}},

(16)

we obtain the closed form of the dipole moments. Here, we introduced the dressed polarizability

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{X}}}\Bigl[\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{X}}}\Bigr]^{-1}.

(17)

A general discussion of this dressed polarizability will follow up in the next section. Here, we focus on the derivation of the fields outside of the nanoparticles which are given by the fields generated by the dipole moments and the environmental fields so that we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r})+\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\sum_{l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathbf{d}_{l}^{\alpha}.

(18)

By inserting Eq. (
16
), we finally obtain the result for the fields outside of the nanoparticles

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r})+\sum_{l,m\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\mathds{G}_{kl}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\left(\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\mathds{A}_{lm}^{\alpha\beta}\mathbf{d}_{m,\text{fl}}^{\beta}+k_{0}^{2}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{lm}^{\alpha\beta}\mathbf{F}_{m,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\right)

(19)

with

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{A}}}

\displaystyle=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{1}}}+k_{0}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\leftrightarrow}}{{\mathds{G}}}.

(20)

Note, that this expression is very general and describes the fields generated by the fluctuational dipoles inside of the nanoparticles and the field of the background and substrate within near- and far-field regime. We will use these general expressions to compute the z-component of the mean Poynting vector integrated over a plane above the nanoparticle assembly. This will give us the spectral power emitted by the nanoparticles in vicinity of the substrate taking into account the different temperatures of the nanoparticles, substrate, and background. Hence, this power determines the emitted heat of the whole system which would also be measurable with a detector.

II.2
dressed polarizability

Actually, Eq. (
17
) contains four different dressed polarizabilities which are given by

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{kk}

\displaystyle=\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{k}\left[\underline{\underline{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{kk}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{k}-k_{0}^{4}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{k\bar{k}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\bar{k}}\left[\underline{\underline{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\bar{k}\bar{k}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\bar{k}}\right]^{-1}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\bar{k}k}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{k}\right]^{-1},

(21)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH/HE}}

\displaystyle=\underline{\underline{\mathds{F}}}_{\text{E/H}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH/EE}}=\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE/HH}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{E}}}_{\text{H/E}},

(22)

with

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathds{F}}}_{\text{E/H}}

\displaystyle=k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{E/H}}\left[\underline{\underline{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EE/HH}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{E/H}}\right]^{-1}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EH/HE}},

(23)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\mathds{E}}}_{\text{E/H}}

\displaystyle=k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{HE/EH}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{E/H}}\left[\underline{\underline{\mathds{1}}}-k_{0}^{2}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EE/HH}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{X}}}_{\text{E/H}}\right]^{-1}.

(24)

Note that the bar notation indicates
\bar{\text{E}}=\text{H}
and
\bar{\text{H}}=\text{E}
, respectively. Additionally, the relations

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE/HH}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{0}^{2}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{F}}}_{\text{E/H}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{G}}}_{\text{EH/HE}}^{-1}+\underline{\underline{\mathds{F}}}_{\text{E/H}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH/EE}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{E}}}_{\text{E/H}}

(25)

hold. These relations are the general
N
-body formulas for the dressed polarizabilities.

To show later that we can retrieve the known single-particle expressions from the general ones, we restrict ourselves to the electric polarizability by setting
\chi_{\text{H}}=0
. Then the above expressions simplify to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{EE}}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp}[\mathbf{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{x}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{y}]+\alpha_{\text{EE},z}\mathbf{e}_{z}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{z},

(26)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}

\displaystyle=\frac{V\chi_{\text{E}}}{1-k_{0}^{2}V\chi_{\text{E}}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE}, \perp/z}}},

(27)

whereas the other polarizabilities vanish. Here, the volume average of the components perpendicular and parallel to the surface normal split up in the vacuum contribution
\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,vac},\perp/z}}
and the contribution of the surface reflected fields
\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,ref},\perp/z}}
.
To further evaluate this expression, we require the volume averages of the Green’s functions. These averages depend on the shape of the nanoparticles. For pure vacuum, i.e. without substrate, they are well-known and long-established
Lakhtakia 1992
;
Fikioris 1965
;
Yaghjian 1980
. Nonetheless, throughout the literature different forms are used which lead to different expressions for the dressed polarizability
Fikioris 1965
;
Albaladejo et al. 2010
;
Ekeroth et al. 2017
;
Edalatpour et al. 2019
;
Draine 1988
;
Goedecke and O’Brien 1988
;
Draine and Goodman 1993
;
Messina et al. 2013
;
Carminati et al. 2006
, especially for DDA. When considering spherical nanoparticles, for instance, one finds the expressions
Albaladejo et al. 2010
;
Messina et al. 2013
;
Carminati et al. 2006
;
Ekeroth et al. 2017
;
Lakhtakia 1992

\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,vac},\perp/z}}_{\rm d-cdm}\approx-\frac{1}{3Vk_{0}^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{0}}{6\pi}

(28)

which includes the so-called “radiation correction” term and is also called “Draine’s form” of coupled dipole moments (D-CDM)
Lakhtakia 1992
. On the other hand, the expression
Fikioris 1965
;
Edalatpour et al. 2019
;
Lakhtakia 1992

\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,vac},\perp/z}}_{\rm s-cdm}=\biggl(\frac{2}{3}\left[1-\mathrm{i}k_{0}R\right]e^{\mathrm{i}k_{0}R}-1\biggr)\frac{1}{Vk_{0}^{2}}

. Note that in the limit

\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,vac},\perp/z}}_{\rm s-cdm}\approx-1/(3k_{0}^{2}V)=\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,vac},\perp/z}}_{\rm w-cdm}.

(30)

That means in the small sphere limit the strong form of coupled dipole moments converges to the “weak form” of coupled dipole moments (W-CDM) and the only difference between the weak/strong form and Draine’s form in that limit is the radiation correction term
\mathrm{i}k_{0}/6\pi
which is typically small in the limit. As a consequence, when using the susceptibility

\chi_{\text{E}}=\varepsilon-1

(31)

the polarizability for a nanoparticle in vacuum including the radiation correction is
Albaladejo et al. 2010
;
Messina et al. 2013
;
Carminati et al. 2006
;
Ekeroth et al. 2017
;
Lakhtakia 1992

{\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}}_{\rm d-cdm}=\frac{\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}}{1-\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{3}}{6\pi}}

(32)

with the Clausius-Mosotti-like polarizability of a spherical nanoparticle also obtained from the weak form of coupled dipole moments

\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}=3V\frac{\varepsilon-1}{\varepsilon+2}.

(33)

On the other hand, the strong form of coupled dipole moments leads to the polarizability

{\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}}_{\rm s-cdm}=\frac{V\chi_{\text{E}}}{1-\chi_{\text{E}}\left(\frac{2}{3}\left[1-\mathrm{i}k_{0}R\right]e^{\mathrm{i}k_{0}R}-1\right)}

(34)

which coincides in the limit

with the weak form
\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}
like it is used in Ref.
Edalatpour et al. 2019
.

Note, that these expressions are only valid without substrate. With substrate also the average of the scattered part of the Green’s function needs to be added. In that case we have the three forms

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}}_{\rm d-cdm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}}{1-\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}\frac{\mathrm{i}k_{0}^{3}}{6\pi}-k_{0}^{2}\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,ref},\perp/z}}},

(35)

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}}_{\rm s-cdm}

\displaystyle=\frac{V\chi_{\text{E}}}{1-\chi_{\text{E}}\left(\frac{2}{3}\left[1-\mathrm{i}k_{0}R\right]e^{\mathrm{i}k_{0}R}-1\right)-k_{0}^{2}V\chi_{E}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,ref},\perp/z}}},

(36)

\displaystyle{\alpha_{\text{EE},\perp/z}}_{\rm w-cdm}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}}{1-k_{0}^{2}\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE,ref},\perp/z}}}.

(37)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\chi_{\text{E}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{9V_{\alpha}\mathrm{i}}{2x_{\alpha}^{3}}a_{1},

(38)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\chi_{\text{H}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{9V_{\alpha}\mathrm{i}}{2x_{\alpha}^{3}}\frac{\mu_{0}}{\epsilon_{0}}b_{1}

(39)

where
Bohren and Huffman 1983

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})[x_{\alpha}j_{1}(x_{\alpha})]^{\prime}-j_{1}(x_{\alpha})[y_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})]^{\prime}}{\varepsilon_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})[x_{\alpha}h_{1}^{(1)}(x_{\alpha})]^{\prime}-h_{1}^{(1)}(x_{\alpha})[y_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})]^{\prime}},

(40)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{j_{1}(y_{\alpha})[x_{\alpha}j_{1}(x_{\alpha})]^{\prime}-j_{1}(x_{\alpha})[y_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})]^{\prime}}{j_{1}(y_{\alpha})[x_{\alpha}h_{1}^{(1)}(x_{\alpha})]^{\prime}-h_{1}^{(1)}(x_{\alpha})[y_{\alpha}j_{1}(y_{\alpha})]^{\prime}}

(41)

are the dipolar Mie coefficients with
x_{\alpha}=k_{0}R_{\alpha}
,
y_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{\alpha}}x_{\alpha}
, and the spherical Bessel and Hankel functions of the first kind
Dong et al. 2017
. The primes denote the derivatives with respect to the function’s argument.
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
denotes the permittivity of nanoparticle
\alpha
. In the limit
x_{\alpha},y_{\alpha}\ll 1
the susceptibilities simplify to

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\chi_{\text{E}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=3V_{\alpha}\frac{\varepsilon_{\alpha}-1}{\varepsilon_{\alpha}+2},

(42)

\displaystyle V_{\alpha}\chi_{\text{H}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{0}}{\epsilon_{0}}\frac{V_{\alpha}}{10}x_{\alpha}^{2}(\varepsilon_{\alpha}-1).

(43)

Note that in this limit
V_{\alpha}\chi_{\text{E}}^{\alpha}
equals
\alpha_{\rm w-cdm}
so that the electrical part retrieves the Clausius-Mosotti form of the polarizability. This mixed approach of the weak form with the Mie coefficients is called the “weak Mie form” (W-Mie-CDM) in the following.

In the numerical evaluations of thermal radiation of one or several nanoparticles within the CDM we will use the expressions for spherical nanoparticles. On the other hand, for DDA simulations we will use cubic volume elements of a single unit size to replace the macroscopic object by a assembly of voxels. More elaborated DDA simulations with rectangular voxels or voxels of different sizes and shapes for better approximation of the shape of the macroscopic object can, of course, also be used
Smunev et al. 2015
;
Edalatpour et al. 2015
. An overview on the DDA method in general can be found in Ref.
Yurkin and Hoekstra 2007
, for instance. A more detailed discussion on finite size effects of some of the considered polarizabilities which are important for large radii can be found in Ref.
Moskalensky and Yurkin 2021
.

II.3
Total emitted heat radiation

Now, we determine the total heat flux through a plane parallel to the substrate’s surface above the nanoparticle assembly. This heat flux is equivalent to the power emitted into the far-field. As mentioned before, we need the Poynting vector which is computed by means of the FDT
Rytov et al. 1989
. To this end, we assume that the background fields as well as the electric and magnetic dipole moments are uncorrelated. We obtain

\displaystyle P_{z}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{E}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathbf{H}_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{ij}

\displaystyle=P_{\text{s}}+P_{\text{np}}+P_{\text{env}}

(44)

with the power solely emitted by the background fields

\displaystyle P_{\text{s}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{E}_{\text{env}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathbf{H}_{\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{ij},

(45)

the power emanated from the nanoparticles

\displaystyle P_{\text{np}}

\displaystyle=2\mu_{0}^{2}\omega^{4}\sum_{k,l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\Bigl[\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathds{A}_{k\text{E}}^{\alpha\beta}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{p}_{\text{fl}}^{\beta}\otimes\mathbf{p}_{\text{fl}}^{\delta}\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}\mathds{A}_{l\text{E}}^{\gamma\delta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}l}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathds{A}_{k\text{H}}^{\alpha\beta}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{m}_{\text{fl}}^{\beta}\otimes\mathbf{m}_{\text{fl}}^{\delta}\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}\mathds{A}_{l\text{H}}^{\gamma\delta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}l}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\gamma})\Bigr]_{ij},

(46)

and the power stemming from interactions between substrate and nanoparticles

\displaystyle P_{\text{env}}

\displaystyle=2k_{0}^{2}\sum_{k,l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\Bigl[\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{E}_{\text{env}}(\mathbf{r})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}k}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\otimes\mathbf{H}_{\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}

\displaystyle\quad+k_{0}^{2}\sum_{m,n\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{n,\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\delta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{mn}^{\gamma\delta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}m}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\gamma})\Bigr]_{ij}.

(47)

Additionally,
\epsilon_{ijq}
denotes the Levi-Civita symbol,
\,\,\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r
the infinitesimal surface element of the x-y plane. The
\langle\!\langle\cdot\rangle\!\rangle
notation indicates an ensemble average over thermally fluctuating currents and fields. For the first term
P_{s}
in Eq. (
44
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\text{s}}

\displaystyle=A\hbar\omega\bigl(n_{\text{s}}-n_{\text{b}}\bigr)\int_{0}^{k_{0}}\frac{\text{d}k_{\perp}}{2\pi}k_{\perp}(2-|r_{\text{E}}|^{2}-|r_{\text{H}}|^{2}).

(48)

It conveys no additional information about the scattering process but describes the power transferred between substrate and background due to the different temperatures of the substrate and background
Polder and Hove 1971
. Here,
A
denotes an area in the x-y plane,
\hbar
the reduced Planck constant, and
r_{\text{E,H}}
are the Fresnel amplitude reflection coefficients for parallel and perpendicular polarized light, respectively (see appendix
A
).
n_{j}
is the Bose-Einstein occupation probability

\displaystyle n_{j}

\displaystyle=\left[e^{\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_{B}T_{j}}}-1\right]^{-1}

(49)

with the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
. The plane area
A
diverges when considering the whole x-y plane, so that we will later set
A
to be the geometrical cross section of the considered tip or nanoparticle. However, in an experiment the choice of
A
would correspond to the collection area of a detector. Nowadays, many experiments usually operate in tapping mode with a lock-in technique
Wang et al. 2018
;
Komiyama 2019
;
Kajihara et al. 2011
;
Wilde et al. 2006
;
Babuty et al. 2013
;
Jones and Raschke 2012
;
O’Callahan et al. 2014
, i.e. the distance between substrate surface and nanoparticle assembly, e.g. the tip of an atomic force or scanning tunnelling microscope, oscillates during measurements. Then, because
P_{\text{s}}
is constant with respect to the distance between nanoparticles and substrate surface, this quantity will average out and does not contribute to the detected signal.

For the evaluation of
P_{\text{np}}
we need two intermediate steps. At first, we exploit Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
25
) to rewrite
\mathds{A}_{\text{EH/HE}}^{\alpha\beta}
in terms of
\mathds{A}_{\text{EE/HH}}^{\alpha\beta}
by

\displaystyle\mathds{A}_{\text{EH/HE}}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{N}\mathds{F}_{\text{E/H}}^{\alpha\gamma}\mathds{A}_{\text{HH/EE}}^{\gamma\beta}.

(50)

Besides, we have to evaluate the correlation function which is done by using the FDT related to
Herz and Biehs 2021
:

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{d}_{k,\text{fl}}\otimes\mathbf{d}_{k,\text{fl}}^{\dagger}\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\hbar}{\mu_{0}\omega^{2}}\left(n_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\mathds{A}_{kk}^{\alpha\gamma-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\gamma\delta}\mathds{A}_{kk}^{\delta\beta-1\dagger}\delta_{\beta\alpha}

(51)

with the generalized susceptibilities

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=k_{0}^{2}\frac{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kk}^{\alpha\beta}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kk}^{\beta\alpha\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}-k_{0}^{4}\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kk}^{\alpha\gamma}\biggl(\frac{\mathds{G}_{kk}^{\gamma\delta}-\mathds{G}_{kk}^{\delta\gamma\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}+\sum_{\epsilon}\frac{\mathds{E}_{\bar{k}}^{\gamma\epsilon}\mathds{G}_{\bar{k}k}^{\epsilon\delta}-\mathds{G}_{\bar{k}k}^{\epsilon\gamma\dagger}\mathds{E}_{\bar{k}}^{\delta\epsilon\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}\biggr)\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kk}^{\beta\delta\dagger}.

(52)

These general susceptibilities used in the FDT are known for single arbitrary objects
Krüger et al. 2012
;
Herz and Biehs 2019
and in particular for a single dipolar object in a given environment as well as for many of them
.Ott and Biehs 2020
;
Herz and Biehs 2021
;
Ott et al. 2021b
;
Moskalensky and Yurkin 2021
;
Biehs et al. 2021
where the details of the derivation for arbitrary objects can be found in
Krüger et al. 2012
;
Herz and Biehs 2019
and for dipoles in
Moskalensky and Yurkin 2021
.
A more detailed justification for this evaluation will follow at the end of this paragraph. With that we obtain for
P_{\text{np}}

\displaystyle P_{\text{np}}

\displaystyle=4\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\Biggl(\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg\{\left[\left(n(T_{\alpha},\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\left(n(T_{\gamma},\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathds{F}_{k}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\bar{k}}_{\gamma\delta}\mathds{F}_{k}^{\beta\delta\dagger}\right]\mathds{G}_{\text{H}k}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\gamma}\left[\left(n(T_{\gamma},\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathds{F}_{k}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\bar{k}}_{\gamma\beta}+\left(n(T_{\alpha},\omega)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\gamma}\mathds{F}_{\bar{k}}^{\beta\gamma\dagger}\right]\mathds{G}_{\text{H}\bar{k}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Bigg\}\Biggr)_{ij}.

(53)

If the radiative heat sources in the substrate and background are uncorrelated, we can safely decompose the correlation function concerning the fields of the whole environment containing substrate and background into separated correlation functions dealing with either the substrate or the background. Then, we can follow the procedure outlined in Ref.
Herz and Biehs 2021
providing

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{\text{eq}}+\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{\text{leq}}

(54)

for the correlation function of the whole environment with

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=2\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left(n_{\text{b}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\mathds{G}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}-\mathds{G}_{lk}^{\beta\alpha\dagger}}{2\mathrm{i}}

(55)

and

\displaystyle\Big\langle\!\Big\langle\mathbf{F}_{k,\text{env}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\otimes\mathbf{F}_{l,\text{env}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Big\rangle\!\Big\rangle^{\omega}_{\text{leq}}

\displaystyle=2\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}k_{0}^{2}\left(n_{\text{s}}-n_{\text{b}}\right)\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{V_{\text{s}}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{3}r\mathds{G}_{k\text{E}}^{\text{s}}(\mathbf{r}_{\alpha},\mathbf{r})\mathds{G}_{l\text{E}}^{\text{s}\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta},\mathbf{r}).

(56)

In the latter equation,
V_{\text{s}}
denotes the substrate’s volume,
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}
its permittivity, and
\mathds{G}^{\text{s}}
describes the Green’s function for sources inside of the substrate. If one refers to spatial arguments which do not correspond to particle positions, the superscripts in the Green’s function in Eq. (
55
) are replaced by the usual argument notation with coordinates of the observation point and the source point. The first correlation function stems from forcing both fields to fulfill the global equilibrium correlation function Ref.
Eckhardt 1984
when they are at the same temperature. The second one, however, results from evaluating the correlation functions in local equilibrium for both fields separately. Using only the first correlation function in Eq. (
55
) and the relations between the dressed polarizabilities (
k={\rm E,H}
)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{kk}^{t}

\displaystyle=\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{kk},

(57)

\displaystyle\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}^{t}

\displaystyle=-\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}},

(58)

we obtain for the global equilibrium contribution of
P_{\text{env}}=P_{\text{env,eq}}+P_{\text{env,leq}}

\displaystyle P_{\text{env,eq}}

\displaystyle=-4\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left(n_{\text{b}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Biggl(\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\left[\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\mathds{F}_{k}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\bar{k}}_{\gamma\delta}\mathds{F}_{k}^{\beta\delta\dagger}\right]\mathds{G}_{\text{H}k}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\gamma}\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\left[\mathds{F}_{k}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\bar{k}}_{\gamma\beta}+\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\gamma}\mathds{F}_{\bar{k}}^{\beta\gamma\dagger}\right]\mathds{G}_{\text{H}\bar{k}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\Biggr)_{ij}.

(59)

Comparing
P_{\text{np}}
and
P_{\text{env,eq}}
, they only differ in sign and temperature. Therefore, we can merge them to one contribution to the whole system describing the heat transfer between all nanoparticles and the background. This also describes detailed balance between background and nanoparticles and also serves as justification for the evaluation of the dipole moment correlation functions a posteriori because this part vanishes when
T_{p}=T_{b}
as it should be. Together with the matrix abbreviations

\displaystyle\mathds{M}_{k}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle:=\left(n_{\alpha}-n_{b}\right)\boldsymbol{\chi}^{k}_{\alpha\beta}+\sum_{\gamma,\delta}\left(n_{\gamma}-n_{b}\right)\mathds{F}_{k}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\bar{k}}_{\gamma\delta}\mathds{F}_{k}^{\beta\delta\dagger}

(60)

and

\displaystyle\mathds{N}_{\text{c}}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\gamma}\left[\left(n_{\alpha}-n_{b}\right)\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\text{E}}_{\alpha\gamma}\mathds{F}_{\text{H}}^{\beta\gamma\dagger}+\left(n_{\gamma}-n_{b}\right)\mathds{F}_{\text{E}}^{\alpha\gamma}\boldsymbol{\chi}^{\text{H}}_{\gamma\beta}\right]

(61)

we simplify the merging of
P_{\text{np}}
and
P_{\text{env,eq}}
to

\displaystyle P_{\text{dir}}

\displaystyle=P_{\text{np}}+P_{\text{env,eq}}

\displaystyle=4\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\epsilon_{ijz}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\biggl(\sum_{k\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathds{M}_{k}^{\alpha\beta}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}k}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathds{N}_{\text{c}}^{\alpha\beta}\mathds{G}_{\text{HH}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})+\mathds{G}_{\text{EH}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\mathds{N}_{\text{c}}^{\beta\alpha\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\beta})\biggr)_{ij}.

(62)

Analytically simplifying the integrals as far as possible, we end up with

\displaystyle P_{\text{dir}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\omega k_{0}\mathrm{Re}\mathrm{Tr}\biggl(\underline{\underline{\mathds{M}}}_{\text{E}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{E}}+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{M}}}_{\text{H}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{H}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{N}}}_{\text{c}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{c}}\biggr)

(63)

The components of the tensors
\mathds{I}
are integral expressions listed in appendix
B
.

The remaining “local equilibrium contribution” (LEQC) consists of two parts

\displaystyle P_{\text{env,leq}}

\displaystyle=P_{\text{abs}}+P_{\text{scat}}

(64)

with

\displaystyle P_{\text{abs}}

\displaystyle=4k_{0}^{4}\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left(n_{\text{s}}-n_{\text{b}}\right)\sum_{k,l\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\int_{V_{\text{s}}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\epsilon_{ijz}\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigl[\mathds{G}_{\text{EE}}^{\text{s}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathds{G}_{l\text{E}}^{\text{s}\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}k}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}\mathds{G}_{l\text{E}}^{\text{s}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathds{G}_{\text{HE}}^{\text{s}\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\Bigr]_{ij}.

(65)

and

\displaystyle P_{\text{scat}}

\displaystyle=4k_{0}^{6}\hbar\mu_{0}\omega^{2}\left(n_{\text{s}}-n_{\text{b}}\right)\sum_{k,l,m,n\in\{\text{E,H}\}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta}\mathrm{Re}\int\!\!\mathrm{d}^{2}r\int_{V_{\text{s}}}\!\!\mathrm{d}^{3}r^{\prime}\epsilon_{ijz}\mathrm{Im}(\varepsilon_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigl[\mathds{G}_{\text{E}k}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\alpha})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kl}^{\alpha\beta}\mathds{G}_{l\text{E}}^{\text{s}}(\mathbf{r}_{\beta},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\mathds{G}_{n\text{E}}^{\text{s}\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{\delta},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{mn}^{\gamma\delta\dagger}\mathds{G}_{\text{H}m}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{\gamma})\Bigr]_{ij}.

(66)

Analytically simplifying the integrals as far as possible, we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\text{abs}}

\displaystyle=\left(n_{\text{b}}-n_{\text{s}}\right)\hbar\omega k_{0}^{3}\mathrm{Im}\mathrm{Tr}\biggl(\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{R}}}_{\text{E}}+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{R}}}_{\text{H}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}\underline{\underline{\mathds{R}}}_{\text{c}}\biggr)

(67)

and

\displaystyle P_{\text{scat}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(n_{\text{s}}-n_{\text{b}}\right)\hbar\omega k_{0}^{6}\mathrm{Re}\mathrm{Tr}\biggl(\biggl[\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{E}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}^{\dagger}+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{H}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{c}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}^{\dagger}\biggr]\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{E}}+\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\biggl[\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{E}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{H}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quad+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{c}}^{\dagger}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}^{\dagger}\biggr]\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{H}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\biggl[\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}\left(\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{E}}+\underline{\underline{\mathds{K}}}_{\text{E}}\right)\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}+\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}\left(\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{H}}+\underline{\underline{\mathds{K}}}_{\text{H}}\right)\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EE}}\left(\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{c}}+\underline{\underline{\mathds{K}}}_{\text{c}}\right)\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HH}}^{\dagger}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{EH}}\left(\underline{\underline{\mathbf{\Gamma}}}_{\text{c}}^{\dagger}-\underline{\underline{\mathds{K}}}_{\text{c}}^{\dagger}\right)\underline{\underline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}_{\text{HE}}^{\dagger}\biggr]\underline{\underline{\mathds{I}}}_{\text{c}}\biggr).

(68)

The integrals
\mathds{R}
and
\mathbf{\Gamma}
are listed in appendix
B
.

The total emitted spectral power is

P_{\rm tot}=P_{\rm dir}+P_{\rm abs}+P_{\rm s}+P_{\rm scat}

(69)

where
P_{\rm dir}
gives the direct thermal emission of the nanoparticles and
P_{\text{abs}}
describes heat flux between substrate and background that is absorbed in the nanoparticles which is encoded by the imaginary part. This power can become negative due to the choice of temperatures in the Bose-Einstein factors which is opposite to the one in
P_{\text{scat}}
. The latter describes the power transferred to the background by being scattered at the nanoparticles. The diagonal entries of the
\Gamma
-integrals indicating identical particle indices coincide with the integrals used to describe the heat flux between a small sphere and a semi-infinite half space without multiple reflections
Herz et al. 2020
;
Krüger et al. 2012
. Note, that in principle
P_{\text{abs}}
and
P_{\text{s}}
need to be considered together, because
P_{\text{abs}}
corresponds to the power which is absorbed from
P_{\text{s}}
due to the presence of the nanoparticles when
T_{\text{s}}>T_{\text{b}}
. Hence, when choosing
A
equal to the cross section of all nanoparticles the sum of
P_{\text{abs}}
and
P_{\text{s}}
would be positive even though
P_{\text{abs}}
itself would be negative if the dipole approximation is fulfilled.

III
Numerical Results

III.1
A single nanoparticle

To check for plausibility, we want to retrieve the result in
Herz and Biehs 2021
for the single nanoparticle case
N=1
. Then, all
3N\times 3N
block matrices reduce to ordinary
3\times 3
matrices corresponding to the
11
-entry of the former block matrix. For example, now, the Green’s functions are fully described by the volume averages. This yields the dressed polarizabilities (
k=\text{E},\text{H}
)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{kk}

\displaystyle=\frac{V_{p}\chi_{k}[\mathbf{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{x}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{y}]}{1-k_{0}^{2}V_{p}\chi_{k}\expectationvalue{G_{kk,\perp}}+\frac{k_{0}^{4}V_{p}^{2}\chi_{\text{E}}\chi_{\text{H}}\expectationvalue{G_\text{HE}}^{2}}{1-k_{0}^{2}V_{p}\chi_{\bar{k}}\expectationvalue{G_{\bar{kk},\perp}}}}+\frac{V_{p}\chi_{k}\mathbf{e}_{z}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{z}}{1-k_{0}^{2}V_{p}\chi_{k}\expectationvalue{G_{kk,z}}},

(70)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{EH}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{HE}}=\frac{k_{0}^{2}V_{p}^{2}\chi_{\text{E}}\chi_{\text{H}}\expectationvalue{G_\text{HE}}[\mathbf{e}_{x}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{y}-\mathbf{e}_{y}\otimes\mathbf{e}_{x}]}{\left(1-k_{0}^{2}V_{p}\chi_{\text{E}}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{EE},\perp}}\right)\left(1-k_{0}^{2}V_{p}\chi_{\text{H}}\expectationvalue{G_{\text{HH},\perp}}\right)+k_{0}^{4}V_{p}^{2}\chi_{\text{E}}\chi_{\text{H}}\expectationvalue{G_\text{HE}}^{2}}

(71)

which coincide with the dressed polarizabilities in
Herz and Biehs 2021
. The matrices
\mathds{F}
,
\mathds{E}
, and
\mathds{N}
follow the structure of Eq. (
71
), meaning that the matrices only possess a xy and yx entry. The matrices
\boldsymbol{\chi}
and
\mathds{M}
, however, look like Eq. (
70
) having three entries with two identical ones accounting for one direction parallel and two directions perpendicular to the substrate’s surface normal. Then, we obtain the identical results as in Ref.
Herz and Biehs 2021

III.3
Discrete dipole approximation - spherical nanoparticle

Here, we want to apply our general theory to perform DDA of a macroscopic object radiating heat into the far-field. Within this approximation method the macroscopic object is replaced by a number of small polarizable subvolumes, so called voxels, which are treated with the formalism of the coupled dipole method. The idea behind this approach is that, by replacing any arbitrarily shaped macroscopic object by a sufficiently great number of subvolumes or voxels (in the ideal case an infinite number of infinitely small voxels), the electrodynamical response of the macroscopic object is described exactly. This allows for treating electrodynamic problems of, in principle, arbitrarily shaped objects
Yurkin and Hoekstra 2007
. In the context of fluctuational electrodynamics this DDA method has been introduced to describe the thermal heat exchange between two macroscopic objects
Edalatpour and Francoeur 2014
by applying the corresponding CDM formalism first introduced in Ref.
Ben-Abdallah et al. 2011
. An extension to treat also the heat transfer between anisotropic and non-reciprocal objects can be found in Ref.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
. Furthermore, in Ref.
Edalatpour and Francoeur 2016
the DDA method for treating radiative heat exchange between an arbitrarily shaped object and a planar sample was introduced and, finally, in Ref.
Edalatpour et al. 2019
the same group used the DDA method to determine the near-field of a planar sample scattered into the far-field by an arbitrarily shaped object. Thermal emission of an arbitrarily shaped object into free space, i.e. without a sample, was introduced in Ref.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
. When using DDA, typically all voxels of a single object are considered to have a single temperature
T_{\rm p}
. However, in principle also temperature profiles and multiple objects can be modelled.

Our here introduced method is a generalization of the two latter methods in Refs.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
;
Edalatpour et al. 2019
. In Ref.
Edalatpour et al. 2019
, the intensity of the scattered near-field by a single object treated within DDA is considered, only. Therefore, since no other temperatures but the temperature of the substrate
T_{\rm s}>0\,{\rm K}
are involved in that approach the results correspond to our expression
P_{\rm scat}
for the scattered power assuming that
T_{\rm b}=T_{\rm p}=0\,{\rm K}
. But of course, even for this choice of temperature the substrate itself will directly emit heat which will be partially absorbed by the scatterer which is described by the terms
P_{\rm s}
and
P_{\rm abs}
in our theory. On the other hand, the approach in Ref.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
only considers thermal emission of a single object with temperature
T_{\rm p}
into vacuum with temperature
T_{\rm b}
. By replacing the vacuum Green’s function in the expressions in Ref.
Ekeroth et al. 2017
by that of a planar sample, as done in Ref.
.Ott and Biehs 2020
, and integrating the mean Poynting vector over the corresponding detection plane, this method would give
P_{\rm dir}
for the case that
T_{s}=T_{\rm b}
. In our approach, we do not only have access to
P_{\rm dir}
for different temperatures of the substrate and the background but also to all other heat flux contributions which shows that our approach generalizes those previous works.

We find that our DDA formalism works for materials where the electrical part dominates, only. That means that it cannot be applied to macroscopic metallic objects where the absorption or thermal emission is dominated by the contribution of eddy currents. Note that this does not mean that DDA is not able to describe for example metals in the optical regime. In that case, the response would, again, be dominated by the electrical fields. Mathematically, we find that the lack of resonance of the effective magnetic polarizability in the infrared regime leads to a negligible coupling between the voxels for the magnetic dipolar moments. Therefore, the overall response of the object, being replaced by a huge number of voxels, is the sum of the response of the individual voxels and it cannot be used to describe the response of the macroscopic object. That means that the contribution of eddy currents to the thermal emission of macroscopic objects cannot be treated within a DDA approximation using magnetic dipole moments. We will substantiate this claim at the end of this section. Hence, we use in the following only the electrical part of our theory to carry out the DDA calculations. As a first illustration we consider the thermal emission of a single spherical particle within the DDA as shown in Fig.

Edalatpour et al. 2019

Edalatpour et al. 2019

Furthermore, we also find that the position of the red-shifted SPhP resonance is well described be the results of a small spherical scatterer. It seems that the main features are captured by a mixture of the scattering of the foremost part and the bulk part of the tip which can each be described by dipole mode. .

Edalatpour et al. 2019
because it also includes the direct thermal emission of the tip as well as the substrate’s contribution and the part blocked by the tip. Actually, this last part can be relatively large as already pointed out by Komiyama
Komiyama 2019
;
Weng et al. 2018
and also found in our numerical calculations so that it is important to minimize this contribution in measurements.

IV
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have derived the expressions for the emitted power of
N
nanoparticles above a substrate where each particle temperature as well as the temperatures of the substrate and background radiation can be set, separately. Our expressions contain the direct power emitted by the nanoparticles, the power emitted by the substrate, the substrate emission which is absorbed by the nanoparticles, and the power due to the near-field scattering. Our model generalizes previous models and is, therefore, very versatile. It can be used to study the thermal emission of dielectric and metallic particle assemblies close to a substrate within CDM and to model thermal emission of a single or several macroscopic objects in the vicinity of a substrate within DDA. To illustrate this versatility we discussed the thermal emission of a four-particle configuration close to a dielectric and metallic substrate, the thermal emission of a sphere close to a substrate using DDA, and the thermal emission of a sharp tip as used in scattering type near-field thermal profiles, again, within DDA in the case of dielectric nanoparticles. We found that the dipole approximation can only be safely used for metallic nanoparticles close to polar substrates if the distance of the particle centers to the surface is larger than five times the particle radius. Furthermore, our study of the sharp tip showed that, in contrast to previously obtained results
Edalatpour et al. 2019
, the dipole model as used in Ref.
Babuty et al. 2013
can give qualitatively correct spectra of a sharp scattering tip even though a DDA model for the tip shape is definitely preferable to a simple dipole. We point out that the tip spectrum seems to be a mixture of the scattering of its foremost part which can be modelled by a small spherical scatterer and its bulk part which can be modelled by a large spherical scatterer. Our results indicate that the magnetic dipole contribution can be used in CDM simulations but not DDA simulations. We are convinced that our generalized model will be very useful in further more detailed studies of thermal emission of many-particle systems including substrates and the modelling of scattering type experiments as in Ref’s.
Wang et al. 2018
;
Komiyama 2019
;
Kajihara et al. 2011
;
Wilde et al. 2006
;
Babuty et al. 2013
;
Jones and Raschke 2012
;
O’Callahan et al. 2014
.
