Combinatorial games on Galton-Watson trees involving several-generation-jump moves
Dhruv Bhasin, Moumanti Podder

Abstract
. We study the k k -jump normal and k k -jump misère games on rooted Galton-Watson trees, expressing the probabilities of various possible outcomes of these games as specific fixed points of functions that depend on k k and the offspring distribution. We discuss phase transition results pertaining to draw probabilities when the offspring distribution is Poisson ( λ ) (\lambda) . We compare the probabilities of various outcomes of the 2 2 -jump normal game with those of the 2 2 -jump misère game, and a similar comparison is drawn between the 2 2 -jump normal game and the 1 1 -jump normal game, under the Poisson regime. We describe the rate of decay of the probability that the first player loses the 2 2 -jump normal game as λ → ∞ \lambda\rightarrow\infty . We also discuss a sufficient condition for the average duration of the k k -jump normal game to be finite.



1.
Introduction

The simplest yet intriguing versions of the normal and misère games on rooted random trees were studied in
[
15
]
. Each game involves two players (henceforth addressed as P1 and P2) and a token, and requires visualizing a given rooted tree as a directed graph in which an edge between a parent vertex
u
and its child
v
is assumed to be directed from
u
to
v
. In each game, the players take turns to move the token along these directed edges. In a normal game, the first player to get stuck at a leaf vertex (i.e. unable to move the token any further) loses, whereas in a misère game, this same player wins. The two games share a fair amount of similarities in their analysis, but the objective of each player in a normal game is precisely the opposite of that in a misére game. While the authors of
[
15
]
provide an incredibly thorough analysis of these two games when played on rooted Galton-Watson (henceforth abbreviated as GW) trees, they also pose several open questions. Our paper delves deeper into the fascinating world of these two-player combinatorial games and asks:
what if we do not restrict the players to only single-generation moves?
In other words, instead of allowing each player, when it is her turn, to move the token from its current position
u
to a child of
u
, we now permit her to move the token from
u
to any descendant of
u
that is at a distance at most
k
away from
u
, where
k\geqslant 1
is a pre-assigned positive integer. We call the corresponding versions of the normal and misére games the
k
-jump normal
and
k
-jump misère
games respectively.

1.1.
Introduction to the games

We begin with a description of the rooted GW trees on which our games are played. A Galton-Watson branching process (henceforth denoted
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
), introduced in
[
43
]
and independently studied in
[
8

as a model to investigate the extinction of ancestral family names, begins with a root
\phi

of children where

follows the

\chi

of non-negative integers). If

gives birth to

children with
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}

\chi

[
5

[
3

[
4

We now come to a formal description of the games studied in this paper. Given any realization
T
of
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
, any vertex
u
in
T
, and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\Gamma_{i}(u)
denote the set of all descendants

of
u
(excluding

itself) such that the distance between

and

is at most
i

. The players P1 and P2 take turns to make

(with P1 moving in the first round), where a move constitutes relocating the token from its current position, which is some vertex

in

is fixed

k
-jump normal game. Hence, in this game, each of P1 and P2 strives to relocate the token, obeying the rules of the game, from its current position to a leaf vertex of
T
, thereby making her opponent lose in the next round. On the other hand, the player who is unable to make a move wins the
k
-jump misère game. Therefore, in this game, each of P1 and P2 strives to force her opponent to relocate the token to a leaf vertex of
T
, thereby ensuring that she herself wins the game in the next round.

It is important to note here that a realization
T
of the random tree
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
is first generated and then revealed
in its entirety

both
P1 and P2,
before
the game begins. These games are thus
complete information
games. We also assume that P1 and P2 are both intelligent agents who play
optimally
, i.e. when a game is destined to end in a decision, the player who wins tries to end the game in as few rounds as possible, while the player who loses tries to prolong the game as much as possible.

A second, and perhaps equally compelling, motivation arises from viewing these games as tools for understanding a generalized notion of
finite state tree automata
(henceforth abbreviated as FSTA). As such, the simplest FSTA is a state machine that comprises a
finite
set
\Sigma=\{1,2,\ldots,r\}

or

f_{1}
, which is a function

v
in a rooted tree has
n_{i}
children that are in state

for each
i\in\Sigma
, then the state of
v
is given by
f_{1}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r})
. Given a fixed

, a
generalized depth-

FSTA
(henceforth abbreviated as a
k
-GFSTA), with rule
f_{k}
, extends the above definition as follows: given a rooted tree
T
, a vertex
v
of
T
and an assignment
\sigma:\Gamma_{k}(v)\rightarrow\Sigma
of states to the vertices of
\Gamma_{k}(v)
(recall from above that
\Gamma_{k}(v)
is the set of all descendants of
v
, other than
v
itself, that are at distance at most
k
away from
v
), the state of
v
, as dictated by this
k
-GFSTA, is given by
f_{k}\big((\sigma(w):w\in\Gamma_{k}(v))\big)
. For instance, a rule

may be considered such that if there are
n_{i}
vertices in
\Gamma_{k}(v)
that are in state

for each

, then the state of
v
is given by
f_{k}(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r})
. Denoting by
\NW_{k}
the set of vertices
v
such that P1 wins the

-jump normal game that starts at
v
, by
\NL_{k}
the set of vertices
v
such that P1 loses the
k
-jump normal game that starts at
v
, and by

the set of vertices

such that the

-jump normal game that starts at

for these definitions), we obtain a

-GFSTA with the state space

defined by (

) and (

A fixed point of an FSTA is a probability distribution

of the FSTA. We call a fixed point

1.5.
A brief discussion of the literature on combinatorial games

Before we plunge into our exploration of the
k
-jump normal and
k
-jump misère games, the rich and variegated literature on combinatorial games that has developed over the past several decades deserves some delineation. This extremely broad class of games (see, for example,
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
for a general introduction) constitutes primarily two-player games with perfect information, no chance moves, and the possible outcomes being victory for one player (and loss for the other) and draw for both players. Aside from being utilized in studying mathematical problems that belong to complexity classes harder than NP, these games have intimate connections with disciplines such as mathematical logic, automata theory, complexity theory, graph and matroid theory, networks, error-correcting codes, online algorithms. Outside of mathematics, these games find applications in biology, psychology, economics, insurance, actuarial studies and political sciences.

[
17
]
studies
percolation games
on oriented Euclidean lattices. Each site of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, independent of all other sites, is marked a “trap” or a “target” or “open” with probabilities
p
,
q
and
1-p-q
respectively, and the two players take turns to move a token from its current position
(x,y)
to either
(x+1,y)
or
(x,y+1)
. If a player moves to a target, she wins immediately, and if she moves to a trap, she loses immediately. The game’s outcome can be interpreted in terms of the evolution of a one-dimensional discrete-time probablistic cellular automaton (PCA) – specifically, the game having no chance of ending in a draw is shown to be equivalent to the ergodicity of this PCA.
[
17
]
also establishes a connection between the

(i.e. where

) on directed graphs in higher dimensions and the hard-core model on related undirected graphs with reduced dimensions.
[
6

studies the trapping game on undirected graphs, where the players take turns to move the token from the vertex at which it is currently located to an adjacent vertex that has never been visited before, and the player unable to make a move loses (note the evident connection between this game and the normal game described above). The outcome of this game is shown to have close ties with maximum-cardinality matchings, and a draw in this game relates to the sensitivity of such matchings to boundary conditions.
[
42

studies a related, two-person zero-sum game called

, to analyze minimum-weight matchings in edge-weighted graphs. In a related game called

2

Bearing some resemblance to slither are the

(
[

(2.2)

\displaystyle u\in\NL\Leftrightarrow\Gamma_{1}(u)=\emptyset\text{ or }v\in\NW\text{ for every }v\in\Gamma_{k}(u)\Leftrightarrow\Gamma_{k}(u)\subset\NW.

Next, we establish a compactness result which shows that, if a player is able to win the
k
-jump normal game on a rooted tree which is
locally finite
(i.e. every vertex of the tree has finite degree), then she can guarantee to do so within a finite number of rounds which can be specified in advance. Mathematically, this result can be stated as follows:

Lemma 2.1
.

In any
k
-jump normal game, we have
\NW=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\NW^{(n)}
and
\NL=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\NL^{(n)}
.

This result is proven essentially the same way as Proposition 7 of
[
15
]
, but we include a proof nonetheless (see §
10.1
of the Appendix) for the sake of completeness of this work. We note here that the offspring distribution
\chi
that we consider for
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
is supported on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, hence the (random) number of children of any vertex of
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
is almost surely finite.

As a consequence of Lemma
2.1
, we have
\NL^{(n)}\uparrow\NL
and
\NW^{(n)}\uparrow\NW
as
n\uparrow\infty
, which in turn yields

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nl^{(n)}=\nl\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nw^{(n)}=\nw.

The two main parts of the proof of Theorem
1.1
are outlined as follows: in §
2.1
, we show that

is a fixed point of the function

, and in §

.

2.1.
Showing that

For a vertex

\displaystyle u\in\NW^{(n+1)}\Leftrightarrow\exists\ v\in\Gamma_{k}(u)\text{ with }v\in\NL^{(n)}.

Recall the functions

We have

2.2.
Showing that

We begin with an outline for the contents of §
2.2
. The primary intention of §
2.2
is to establish that

, are both increasing on

, for every

. Once established, this fact aids, as follows, in concluding that

is the smallest positive fixed point of

.

Let
0<\eta\leqslant c_{k}
be a fixed point of

. From (

\nl^{(2n)}=H_{k}^{(n)}(\nl^{(0)})=H_{k}^{(n)}(0)
, where

indicates the
n

with itself. Note that,

(i)

since

is a fixed point of
H_{k}
by Corollary
4.2
,

(ii)

since the proof of Lemma
2.2
yields
0<c_{k}<c_{k-1}
and
H_{k}(0)=\chi(0)
(which follows from the fact that
F_{i}(0)=1
for all

),

(iii)

and since
H_{k}
is increasing on
[0,c_{k-1}]
,

we have
0<\chi(0)\leqslant H_{k}^{(n)}(0)=\nl^{(2n)}\leqslant H_{k}^{(n)}(\eta)=\eta\leqslant H_{k}^{(n)}(c_{k})=c_{k}<c_{k-1}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Upon taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
and using (
2.3
), this yields
\nl=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nl^{(2n)}\leqslant\eta
, thus allowing us to conclude that
\nl
is, indeed, the smallest positive fixed point of

.

We prove that

is increasing on
[0,c_{k-1}]
by showing that the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{k}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)=G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),

\displaystyle\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)-G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big),

is non-negative for
x\in[0,c_{k-1}]
. We accomplish this by showing that, for each
x\in[0,c_{k-1}]
,

(i)

that
G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\geqslant G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)
,

(ii)

that
\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\geqslant\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(F_{1}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)
,

(iii)

and the product
G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)
is non-negative.

We note that
G^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i\chi(i)x^{i-1}\geqslant 0
for all

, so that
G^{\prime}\big(g_{k-1}\big(1,F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\geqslant 0
for each

. So, to show (

\frac{d}{dx}g_{k-1}\big(1,F_{2}(x),F_{3}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)
is non-negative. Furthermore,

for all

, showing that

is increasing on

. Consequently, to show (

.

), (

) and (

and all

,

(2.14)

\displaystyle 1\geqslant g_{j}\big(F_{i_{1}}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),F_{k-j+2}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\geqslant g_{j}\big(F_{i_{2}}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),F_{k-j+2}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\geqslant 0,

and for

,

and all

(2.15)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{j}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),F_{k-j+2}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\geqslant\max\big\{0,\frac{d}{dx}g_{j}\big(F_{i}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),F_{k-j+2}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big\}.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{j+1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)=G^{\prime}\big(g_{j}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{j}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),

(2.16)

\displaystyle\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)-G^{\prime}\big(g_{j}\big(F_{k-j}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{j}\big(F_{k-j}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\geqslant 0,

(ii)

.

Next, for

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{j+1}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)-\frac{d}{dx}g_{j+1}\big(F_{i}(x),F_{k-j}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\Big[G^{\prime}\big(g_{j}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)\big)\frac{d}{dx}g_{j}\big(F_{0}(x),F_{k-j+1}(x),\ldots,F_{k}(x)\big)

, we obtain

(5.5)

F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=\exp\left\{\lambda F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})-\lambda c_{k,\lambda}-\lambda\right\}=c_{k,\lambda}\Longleftrightarrow F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})-c_{k,\lambda}-1=\frac{\ln c_{k,\lambda}}{\lambda}.

If
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}c_{k,\lambda}=c
for some
c>0
, then the right side will go to
0
, while the left side remains bounded above by
-c
since
F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})<1
(due to (
5.3
)), yielding a contradiction. Therefore, we must have
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}c_{k,\lambda}=0
.

5.2.
Understanding the behaviour of
F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1

Before we can establish the claims made in (
5.1
), we need to understand the behaviour of
F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})
as a function of
\lambda
, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
. To this end, note that, given
any
infinite sequence
\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n}
of positive reals with
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, since
0<F_{k-1,\lambda_{n}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n}}\right)<1
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
due to (
5.3
), the Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem guarantees the existence of an infinite subsequence
\left\{\lambda_{n_{i}}\right\}_{i}
such that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}F_{k-1,\lambda_{n_{i}}}(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}})\text{ exists and is in }[0,1]\implies\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}}{\lambda_{n_{i}}}\text{ exists and is in }[-1,0],\text{ due to \eqref{claim_3}}.

Suppose we assume that

(5.6)

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}}{\lambda_{n_{i}}}=-c\text{ for some }0<c\leqslant 1.

In what follows, our aim is to show that (
5.6
) leads to a contradiction.

5.2.1.
Proving that (
5.6
) leads to a contradiction

Given any
0<\epsilon<c
, (
5.6
) implies that there exists
i_{\epsilon}
such that
c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}<e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}
for all
i\geqslant i_{\epsilon}
. We then show, using an inductive argument with respect to the index
j
, that

(5.7)

F_{j,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right)>\exp\left\{-\sum_{t=1}^{j}\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\}\text{ for all }i\geqslant i_{\epsilon},\text{ for }1\leqslant j\leqslant k.

For
i\geqslant i_{\epsilon}
, we have

F_{1,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right)=e^{-\lambda_{n_{i}}c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}}>\exp\left\{-\lambda_{n_{i}}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\},

so that (
5.7
) holds for
j=1
, and the base case for the induction is thus verified. Assuming that (
5.7
) holds for some
j<k
and using
e^{-x}-1>-x
for all
x>0
, we have, for all
i\geqslant i_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle F_{j+1,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lambda_{n_{i}}F_{j,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right)-\lambda_{n_{i}}c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}-\lambda_{n_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle>\exp\left\{\lambda_{n_{i}}\exp\left\{-\sum_{t=1}^{j}\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\}-\lambda_{n_{i}}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}-\lambda_{n_{i}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\left\{-\lambda_{n_{i}}\sum_{t=1}^{j}\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}-\lambda_{n_{i}}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\sum_{t=1}^{j+1}\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\},

thus proving (
5.7
) by induction. Setting
j=k
, since
c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}
is the fixed point of
F_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}
, we have, for
i\geqslant i_{\epsilon}
,

c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}=F_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\left(c_{k,\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right)>\exp\left\{-\sum_{t=1}^{k}\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\right\}.

We know from §
5.1
that the left side of the above inequality goes to
0
as
i\rightarrow\infty
, whereas the right side approaches
1
, since
\lambda_{n_{i}}^{t}e^{(-c+\epsilon)\lambda_{n_{i}}}\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda_{n_{i}}\rightarrow\infty
for every
1\leqslant t\leqslant k
(since
-c+\epsilon<0
). This yields the desired contradiction.

5.2.2.
Concluding about the limit of
F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})
, for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1

The contradiction obtained in §
5.2.1
tells us that our assumption in (
5.6
) is wrong, which in turn implies that

(5.8)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\ln c_{k,\lambda}}{\lambda}=0.

By (
5.5
) and (
5.8
), we conclude that

(5.9)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=1.

Recall that in the proof of (
2.14
), we have shown that
F_{i,\lambda}(x)\geqslant F_{i+1,\lambda}(x)
for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
and
x\in[0,c_{k-1,\lambda}]
. Using this fact and (
5.9
), we obtain

(5.10)

1>F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\geqslant F_{2,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\geqslant\cdots\geqslant F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\text{ for }\lambda>0\implies\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}F_{i,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=1\text{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1.

5.3.
The behaviour of
\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}
, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, as functions of
\lambda

We lay down the final steps of the proof of (
5.1
). Setting
i=1
in (
5.10
) and using (
5.2
), we have
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda c_{k,\lambda}=0
. We show, via induction on
i
, that

(5.11)

F_{i,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=\exp\{-\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))\}\text{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1.

Assuming that (
5.11
) holds for some
i\leqslant k-2
, we have

(5.12)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}=0

due to (
5.10
), which further ensures that
c_{k,\lambda}=O(\lambda^{-i})
for all
\lambda
large enough. Using this fact, (
5.2
) and a Taylor expansion, we have

\displaystyle F_{i+1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lambda\left[e^{-\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))}-c_{k,\lambda}-1\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lambda\left[-\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))+O\left(\left\{\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))\right\}^{2}\right)-c_{k,\lambda}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\lambda^{i+1}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))\left[1+O\left(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}(1+O(\lambda^{-1}))\right)+O(\lambda^{-i})\right]\right\}

(5.13)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\lambda^{i+1}c_{k,\lambda}\left[1+O(\lambda^{-1})+O(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda})+O(\lambda^{-i})\right]\right\}

for all
\lambda
sufficiently large. Note that each of the terms
O(\lambda^{-1})
,
O(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda})
(by (
5.12
)) and
O(\lambda^{-i})
is
o(1)
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, so that the dominant term in the exponent of (
5.3
) is
-\lambda^{i+1}c_{k,\lambda}
. This fact, along with (
5.10
), yields
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^{i+1}c_{k,\lambda}=0
. This in turn yields
\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}=o(\lambda^{-1})
, so that the dominant term out of
O(\lambda^{-1})
,
O(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda})
and
O(\lambda^{-i})
in (
5.3
) is
O(\lambda^{-1})
. This completes the proof of (
5.11
) by induction.

Combining (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) for
i=k-1
, we conclude that
\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda}=0
. Using this fact and setting
i=k-1
in (
5.3
), we have
c_{k,\lambda}=F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=\exp\{-\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}[1+O(\lambda^{-1})+O(\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda})]\}
, which leads to
\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
because of the final conclusion of §
5.1
. The two conclusions drawn in this paragraph bring us to the end of the proof of (
5.1
).

5.4.
Stating the lemmas and connecting the dots

As promised in the outline of our argument chalked out right before (
5.1
), we now state two important lemmas (whose proofs are deferred to §
10.2
of the Appendix).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\{r_{i}\}_{0\leqslant i\leqslant k}
be a sequence of functions defined and differentiable on an interval
I
, with
(r_{i}(x),r_{k-j+1}(x),r_{k-j+2}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))\in\mathcal{D}_{j}
(see (
1.8
)) for all
x\in I
and all
0\leqslant i<i+j\leqslant k
. Then

(5.14)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}g_{k,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))=\lambda^{k-1}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}f_{k,i,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))(r^{\prime}_{i}(x)-r^{\prime}_{k}(x)),

in which

(5.15)

\displaystyle f_{k,0,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))=\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}G_{\lambda}\left(g_{t,\lambda}\left(r_{0}(x),r_{k-t+1}(x),r_{k-t+2}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x)\right)\right)

and

(5.16)

\displaystyle f_{k,i,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))=\prod_{t=1}^{k-i}G_{\lambda}\left(g_{t,\lambda}(r_{i}(x),r_{k-t+1}(x),r_{k-t+2}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))\right)\alpha_{k,i,\lambda}\left(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x)\right)

for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1
, where
\alpha_{k,1,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))=-1
and
\left|\alpha_{k,i,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))\right|
is bounded above by a constant
a_{k,i}
that depends on
k
and
i
but not on
\lambda
nor on the functions
r_{0},\ldots,r_{k}
, for
2\leqslant i\leqslant k
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
i\geqslant 1
and
x\in(0,c_{i-1})
, we have

(5.17)

F^{\prime}_{i,\lambda}(x)=-\lambda\left[\sum_{t=1}^{i-1}\lambda^{i-t}\prod_{j=t}^{i-1}F_{j,\lambda}(x)+1\right]F_{i,\lambda}(x).

We set
r_{i}(x)
to be the function
F_{i,\lambda}(x)
for each
i
, and
x=c_{k,\lambda}
, in Lemma
5.1
. Recall, from the outline chalked out right above §
5.1
, that we aim to show that the leading term in (
5.14
), in this case, will be of the same order of magnitude as
\lambda^{2k-1}c_{k,\lambda}
, so that the leading term of
H^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})
is shown to be of the same order of magnitude as
\lambda^{2k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2}
. To this end, from (
4.1
), we have

G_{\lambda}(g_{k-i,\lambda}(F_{i,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{i+1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})))=G_{\lambda}(F_{k-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})-c_{k,\lambda})=F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=c_{k,\lambda},

so that from (
5.16
), using the fact that both
\left|\alpha_{k,i,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))\right|
and
G_{\lambda}(x)
are
O(1)
, we obtain

(5.18)

f_{k,i,\lambda}(r_{0}(x),r_{1}(x),\ldots,r_{k}(x))=O(c_{k,\lambda})\text{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1.

Next, from Lemma
5.2
and (
5.10
), and using (
5.18
) in the second step, we deduce that

\displaystyle F^{\prime}_{i,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=O\left(\lambda^{i}\prod_{j=1}^{i}F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\right)=O(\lambda^{i})\text{ as }\lambda\rightarrow\infty,\text{ for }1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1

(5.19)

\displaystyle\implies\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}f_{k,i,\lambda}\left(F_{0,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\right)F^{\prime}_{i,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}O\left(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda}\right).

Next, for any
0<\epsilon<1
, Lemma
5.2
, the fact that
c_{k,\lambda}
is the fixed point of
F_{k,\lambda}
, and (
5.10
) together yield

(5.20)

\displaystyle O(\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda})\geqslant-F^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=\lambda^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})+O\left(\sum_{t=2}^{k}\lambda^{k-t+1}\prod_{j=t}^{k}F_{j,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\right)\geqslant\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}(1-\epsilon)+O(\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda})

as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. From (
5.18
) and (
5.20
), we deduce that

(5.21)

\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}f_{k,i,\lambda}(F_{0,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}))(-F^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}))=O(\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2}).

Finally, (
5.15
), (
4.1
) and (
5.10
) together yield, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
,

(5.22)

f_{k,0,\lambda}\left(F_{0,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\right)=\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}G_{\lambda}\left(F_{t-1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})-c_{k,\lambda}\right)=\prod_{t=1}^{k-1}F_{t,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\rightarrow 1.

Hence, from (
5.20
) and (
5.22
), for any
0<\epsilon<1
, as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(5.23)

f_{k,0,\lambda}(F_{0,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}))(-F^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}))\geqslant(1-\epsilon)\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}+O(\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda}).

Substituting (
5.4
), (
5.21
) and (
5.23
) in (
5.14
), using the fact that
G_{\lambda}(x)=e^{\lambda(x-1)}
so that

, and using Corollary

, we have, as

,

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})=G^{\prime}_{\lambda}(g_{k,\lambda}(F_{0,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),F_{1,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda}),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})))\frac{d}{dx}g_{k,\lambda}(F_{0,\lambda}(x),F_{1,\lambda}(x),\ldots,F_{k,\lambda}(x))\big|_{x=c_{k,\lambda}}

\displaystyle\geqslant\lambda H_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\lambda^{k-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}O(\lambda^{i}c_{k,\lambda})+O(\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2})+(1-\epsilon)\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}+O(\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda})\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}\left[O(\lambda^{k-1}c_{k,\lambda})+O(\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2})+(1-\epsilon)\lambda^{k}c_{k,\lambda}\right]=(1-\epsilon)\lambda^{2k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2}+O(\lambda^{2k-1}c_{k,\lambda}^{2}),

so that the leading term of
H^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})
is indeed of the same order of magnitude as
\lambda^{2k}c_{k,\lambda}^{2}
, and
H^{\prime}_{k,\lambda}(c_{k,\lambda})\rightarrow\infty
due to the second assertion made in (
5.1
). This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

6.1.
Showing strict convexity of
H_{2,\lambda}
on
[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
for
\lambda\geqslant 2

We begin by stating the first of the three objectives we wish to achieve in §
6
. We fix
k=2
, and we let the offspring distribution
\chi
of
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
be Poisson
(\lambda)
. We show that the curve
y=H_{2,\lambda}(x)
is strictly convex for all
x\in[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
, whenever
\lambda\geqslant 2
– we accomplish this by proving that the second derivative
H^{\prime\prime}_{2,\lambda}(x)
is strictly positive for all
x\in[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
, for every
\lambda\geqslant 2
.

In order to keep the expression for
H^{\prime\prime}_{2,\lambda}(x)
as uncluttered as possible, we set

\alpha(x)=G_{\lambda}(1-F_{2,\lambda}(x))\text{ and }\beta(x)=G_{\lambda}(F_{1,\lambda}(x)-F_{2,\lambda}(x)),

so that, from (
1.9
), we obtain

H_{2,\lambda}(x)=G_{\lambda}(\alpha(x)-\beta(x)).

Note, at the very outset, that since
F_{1,\lambda}(x)\leqslant 1
(evident from (
5.2
)) and
G_{\lambda}
is increasing, hence
\alpha(x)\geqslant\beta(x)
for
x\in[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
. From Lemma
5.2
, which yields
F^{\prime}_{1,\lambda}(x)=-\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)
and
F^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(x)=-\lambda\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{2,\lambda}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(x)=\lambda^{2}\alpha(x)\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{2,\lambda}(x),

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(x)=\lambda^{2}\beta(x)\left\{-F_{1,\lambda}(x)+\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{2,\lambda}(x)\right\}.

Utilizing these expressions, we have

(6.1)

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dx}G_{\lambda}(\alpha(x)-\beta(x))=\lambda H_{2,\lambda}(x)(\alpha^{\prime}(x)-\beta^{\prime}(x)),

and substituting the expressions for
\alpha^{\prime}(x)
and
\beta^{\prime}(x)
in (
6.1
), then differentiating again,

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}_{2,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{4}H_{2,\lambda}(x)\big[\lambda^{2}\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}^{2}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)^{2}(F_{2,\lambda}(x))^{2}+\lambda^{2}(\beta(x))^{2}(F_{1,\lambda}(x))^{2}+

\displaystyle 2\lambda^{2}\beta(x)\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x)+\lambda\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)^{2}(F_{2,\lambda}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle-\lambda\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x)-\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1)^{2}F_{2,\lambda}(x)

(6.2)

\displaystyle+2\lambda\beta(x)\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x)-\beta(x)F_{1,\lambda}(x)(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1)\big]

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{4}H_{2,\lambda}(x)\big[A_{1}+A_{2}+2A_{3}+A_{4}-A_{5}-A_{6}+2A_{7}-A_{8}\big],

where

\displaystyle A_{1}=\lambda^{2}\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}^{2}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)^{2}(F_{2,\lambda}(x))^{2},

\displaystyle A_{2}=\lambda^{2}(\beta(x))^{2}(F_{1,\lambda}(x))^{2},

\displaystyle A_{3}=\lambda^{2}\beta(x)\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x),

\displaystyle A_{4}=\lambda\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)^{2}(F_{2,\lambda}(x))^{2},

\displaystyle A_{5}=\lambda\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x),

\displaystyle A_{6}=\{\alpha(x)-\beta(x)\}(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1)^{2}F_{2,\lambda}(x),

\displaystyle A_{7}=\lambda\beta(x)\left(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1\right)F_{1,\lambda}(x)F_{2,\lambda}(x),

\displaystyle A_{8}=\beta(x)F_{1,\lambda}(x)(\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)+1),

and each
A_{i}
is non-negative (in fact, strictly positive except for
A_{1},A_{3},A_{4},A_{5}
and
A_{6}
at
x=0
). Thus, our aim now is to establish that the sum within the square brackets in (
6.3
) is strictly positive for each
x\in[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
, for every
\lambda\geqslant 2
. This is where Lemmas
6.2
through
6.6
, all of whose proofs are deferred to §
10.3
of the Appendix, come in. The objective each of them accomplishes is the collection of various terms from (
6.3
) and showing that their sums are strictly positive for
\lambda\geqslant 2
.

The primary idea we employ here is as follows: we split the interval
[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
into three pairwise disjoint sub-intervals (in some cases, we may combine two consecutive sub-intervals), namely
[0,\delta_{\lambda}]
,
(\delta_{\lambda},\gamma_{\lambda}]
and
(\gamma_{\lambda},c_{2,\lambda}]
, where we define
\gamma_{\lambda}
and
\delta_{\lambda}
as follows:

(6.4)

F_{2,\lambda}(\gamma_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\text{ and }F_{2,\lambda}(\delta_{\lambda})=\frac{5}{4\lambda}.

Evidently, to be able to do the above, we require

(6.5)

0<\delta_{\lambda}<\gamma_{\lambda}<c_{2,\lambda}.

Several aspects of the above paragraph need justification right away, before we can proceed. By Lemma
2.2
, we know that
F_{2,\lambda}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
, and
F_{2,\lambda}(0)=1
. By definition of
c_{2,\lambda}
, we have
F_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})=c_{2,\lambda}
. Therefore, if we can show that

(6.6)

c_{2,\lambda}<\frac{1}{\lambda}<\frac{5}{4\lambda}<1\text{ for all }\lambda\geqslant 2,

of which the second and third inequalities are immediately seen to hold, we can conclude that
\gamma_{\lambda}
and
\delta_{\lambda}
, defined via (
6.4
), exist and are unique, and that (
6.5
) holds as well. This is where Lemma
6.1
proves useful:

Lemma 6.1
.

The function
\eta_{\lambda}=\lambda c_{2,\lambda}
is strictly increasing for
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{0})
and strictly decreasing for
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},\infty)
, where
\lambda_{0}\approx 2.43634
. The maximum value of

is
\approx 0.52839925
.

The proof of this lemma is deferred to §
10.3
. This lemma guarantees that for all
\lambda>0
, we have
\eta_{\lambda}<1\implies c_{2,\lambda}<\frac{1}{\lambda}
, thus proving that (
6.6
) indeed holds.

Let us come back to chalking out an outline of our argument for showing that the expression in (
6.3
) is strictly positive for all
\lambda\geqslant 2
: for each
\lambda\geqslant 2
, on each of the sub-intervals
[0,\delta_{\lambda}]
,
(\delta_{\lambda},\gamma_{\lambda}]

(\gamma_{\lambda},c_{2,\lambda}]

6.3.
Proving the third and final part of Theorem
1.5

The objective of §
6.3
is to prove that there exists a critical value
\lambda_{c}
of
\lambda
such that
\nl_{2,\lambda}=c_{2,\lambda}
(and hence, by Theorem

,

) for all

, and
\nl_{2,\lambda}<c_{2,\lambda}
(and hence,
\nd_{2,\lambda}>0
) for all

.

The argument can be outlined as follows. The convexity of
H_{2,\lambda}
on
[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
for
\lambda\geqslant 2
, as proved in §
6.1
, guarantees that
y=H_{2,\lambda}(x)
intersects

at most twice in

. If the intersection happens twice, it must happen at the points
x=\nl_{2,\lambda}
and
x=c_{2,\lambda}
, in which case
\nl_{2,\lambda}<c_{2,\lambda}
. If the intersection takes place only once, then this must be at the point
x=c_{2,\lambda}
, in which case
\nl_{2,\lambda}=c_{2,\lambda}
. Recall, from §
5
, the justification as to why
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})\leqslant 1
for every
\lambda>0
.

If we can show that
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})
is strictly less than
1
at
\lambda=2
and it is strictly greater than
1
for some value of
\lambda
strictly exceeding
2
, then using our conclusion from §
6.2
, we can deduce that there is precisely one
\lambda_{c}>2
such that
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})<1
for
\lambda<\lambda_{c}
and
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})>1
for
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
. In the former case,
c_{2,\lambda}
has to be the only point of intersection between
y=H_{2,\lambda}(x)
and
y=x
within the interval
[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
(this follows by noticing that the curve
y=H_{2,\lambda}(x)
travels from above
y=x
to below
y=x
at
x=\nl_{2,\lambda}
, and travels from below
y=x
to above
y=x
at
x=c_{2,\lambda}
when
\nl_{2,\lambda}<c_{2,\lambda}
, so that in such a scenario, we would have
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})>1
). In the latter case, there have to be two points of intersection between
y=H_{2,\lambda}(x)
and
y=x
within the interval
[0,c_{2,\lambda}]
.

At
\lambda=2
, we have
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})\approx 0.721129<1
, and at
\lambda=2.5
, we have
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda}(c_{2,\lambda})\approx 1.06445>1
. This accomplishes the task we set for ourselves in the previous paragraph. Solving
H^{\prime}_{2,\lambda_{c}}\left(c_{2,\lambda_{c}}\right)=1
numerically, we obtain
\lambda_{c}\approx 2.41
. This accomplishes what we set out to prove in §
6.3
, and concludes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

7.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

We first show that
\ml_{2,\lambda}\leqslant\nl_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large. In fact, we prove that the inequality
\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}<\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}

, via induction on

(recall the definitions of these probabilities from §
1.3

\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(1)}=0
and
\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(1)}=e^{-\lambda}
, thus verifying the base case for the inductive argument. Suppose
\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}<\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
2.12
) and (
1.9
) (which is applied to

and Poisson
(\lambda)
offspring distribution), we have

(7.1)

\displaystyle\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}=H_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})=G_{\lambda}\left(g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)\right).

We also note (to be used later) that as
\nl_{2,\lambda}
is a fixed point of
H_{2,\lambda}
(by Theorem
1.1
), we have

(7.2)

\nl_{2,\lambda}=G_{\lambda}\left(g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})\right)\right)=\exp\left\{\lambda g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})\right)-\lambda\right\}.

A relation analogous to (
2.12
) derived in §
3
, (
1.11
) (again, for
k=2
and Poisson
(\lambda)
offspring), the fact (mentioned in §
3
and argued the same way as in §
2.2
) that

is increasing on
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
, the fact (deduced from (
1.7
) and (

)) that
\gamma_{2}(1,F_{1,\lambda}(x),F_{2,\lambda}(x))=e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}(1,F_{1,\lambda}(x),F_{2,\lambda}(x))
, and the induction hypothesis together yield

(7.3)

\displaystyle\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}=J_{2,\lambda}(\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\leqslant J_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})=G_{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)+e^{-\lambda}\right)-e^{-\lambda}.

Therefore, to complete the inductive step of showing that
\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}<\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}

) is strictly less than that in (

) for all
\lambda
large enough.

Note that
G^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda G_{\lambda}(x)
for all

to be our offspring distribution. Since

is convex. Theorem 21.2 of

for all

, we obtain, from (

) and (

\displaystyle\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}-\ml_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}

\displaystyle\geqslant-\lambda G_{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)+e^{-\lambda}\right)

(7.4)

\displaystyle\left[g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}-1\right)+e^{-\lambda}\right]+e^{-\lambda}.

7.2.1.

Since

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)+e^{-\lambda}\right)\leqslant G_{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})\right)+e^{-\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}\lambda\left[g_{2,\lambda}\left(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})\right)-1\right]+\lambda e^{-\lambda}+\lambda e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}-\lambda\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\nl_{2,\lambda}\exp\left\{\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\lambda e^{-\lambda})^{i}}{i!}+\lambda e^{-\lambda}\right\},\quad\text{since }e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}>1\text{ and }\nl_{2,\lambda}<1\text{ implies }(\nl_{2,\lambda})^{e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}}\leqslant\nl_{2,\lambda};

\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, implying that for all
\lambda
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}<i+1\Longleftrightarrow\frac{(\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda})^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}<\frac{(\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda})^{i}}{i!}\text{ for all }i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\text{ and }\lim_{\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda})^{i}}{i!}=0,

so that by the well-known alternating series estimation theorem (see, for instance, §8.4 of
[
37
]
), we have

(7.7)

e^{-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}}-1\leqslant-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}.

Applying Theorem
1.6
one more time, we know that each of
\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}
,
\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}
and
\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}
approaches
0
in the limit as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently,
\left|-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}/2-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right|\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover,

(7.8)

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}=0,\text{ again by Theorem\penalty\ \ref{lem:decay_rate_nl_{2,lambda}},}

thus showing that
\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}=o(\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda})
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, we have
-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}/2-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}<0
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large. Applying the alternating series estimation theorem and arguing the same way as above, we deduce that

\displaystyle\exp\left\{-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}

(7.9)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right)^{2}.

It is evident from (
7.8
) that the leading term of
-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}/2-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}
is
-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}
, so that we can write
(-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}/2-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda})^{2}=O(\lambda^{4}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2})
. This observation, along with Theorem
1.6
, reveals that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
, for all
\lambda
large enough, we have

(7.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lambda^{4}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{\nl_{2,\lambda}}=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lambda^{4}\nl_{2,\lambda}=0\implies\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right)^{2}\leqslant\epsilon\nl_{2,\lambda}.

We have now gathered all the bounds / approximations we need in order to proceed to find a suitable bound for
\nd_{2,\lambda}
: given any
\epsilon>0
, applying (
7.7
), followed by (
7.9
) and finally (
7.10
), we have

\displaystyle\nd_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle=F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})-\nl_{2,\lambda}=\exp\{\lambda e^{-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\}-\nl_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right\}-\nl_{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right)^{2}-\nl_{2,\lambda}

(7.11)

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}-(1-\epsilon)\nl_{2,\lambda}.

This completes our deduction of the desired upper bound on
\nd_{2,\lambda}
for
\lambda
large enough.

We now come to the derivation of a suitable lower bound on
\md_{2,\lambda}
. We first obtain a rather simple bound, using the inequality
e^{-x}>1-x
for all
x>0
:

\displaystyle\md_{2,\lambda}=F_{2,\lambda}(\ml_{2,\lambda})-\ml_{2,\lambda}-e^{-\lambda}=\exp\left\{\lambda e^{-\lambda\ml_{2,\lambda}}-\lambda\ml_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\right\}-\ml_{2,\lambda}-e^{-\lambda}

(7.12)

\displaystyle\geqslant\exp\{-\lambda^{2}\ml_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\ml_{2,\lambda}\}-\ml_{2,\lambda}-e^{-\lambda}\geqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\ml_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\ml_{2,\lambda}-\ml_{2,\lambda}-e^{-\lambda}.

Note that, since we have already proved while concluding §
7.2.2
that
\ml_{2,\lambda}\leqslant\nl_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough, we can further bound the expression on the right side of (
7.2.3
) to obtain
\md_{2,\lambda}\geqslant 1-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)\nl_{2,\lambda}-e^{-\lambda}
. However, this simple bound is not quite enough to compare with the bound obtained in (
7.2.3
) and arrive at our desired conclusion. This is the difficulty we overcome in what follows.

We know that
\nl_{2,\lambda}
is a fixed point of
H_{2,\lambda}
by Theorem
1.1
, that
\ml_{2,\lambda}
is a fixed point of
J_{2,\lambda}
by Theorem
1.2
, and that
J_{2,\lambda}
is increasing on
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
(mentioned in §
3
and argued the same way as in §
2.2
). Using the expressions for
J_{2,\lambda}
and
H_{2,\lambda}
that we obtain by considering
k=2
and Poisson
(\lambda)
offspring in (
1.11
) and (
1.9
), we have, given any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)\ml_{2,\lambda}+e^{-\lambda}=(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)J_{2,\lambda}(\ml_{2,\lambda})+e^{-\lambda}\leqslant(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)J_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})+e^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)G_{\lambda}\left(e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}g_{2,\lambda}(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}))+e^{-\lambda}\right)-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)\exp\left\{e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}\lambda\left[g_{2,\lambda}(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}))-1\right]+\lambda e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}+\lambda e^{-\lambda}-\lambda\right\}-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}

\displaystyle<(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)\exp\left\{e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}\lambda\left[g_{2,\lambda}(1,F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}),F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}))-1\right]+\lambda e^{-\lambda}\right\}-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}\left(\text{as }e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}>1\right);

(7.13)

\displaystyle=(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)(\nl_{2,\lambda})^{e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}}e^{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}<(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)(1+\epsilon)\nl_{2,\lambda}-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}.

for all
\lambda
large enough. From (
7.2.3
) and (
7.2.3
), we obtain

(7.14)

\md_{2,\lambda}>1-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+1)(1+\epsilon)\nl_{2,\lambda}+(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda},

so that from (
7.2.3
) and (
7.14
), we have

(7.15)

\displaystyle\md_{2,\lambda}-\nd_{2,\lambda}>-(\lambda^{2}+\lambda+2)\epsilon\nl_{2,\lambda}+(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)e^{-\lambda},

and our objective is to show that the expression on the right side of (
7.15
) is non-negative for all
\lambda
sufficiently large. Given any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\lambda^{2}+\lambda+2<(1+\epsilon)(\lambda^{2}+\lambda)
for all
\lambda
large enough, and from Theorem
1.9
(which is yet to be proved), we have
\nl_{2,\lambda}\leqslant\nl_{1,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough. To establish (
7.15
), it thus suffices to prove that, given any
\epsilon>0
such that
\epsilon(1+\epsilon)<1
,

(7.16)

\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}>\nl_{1,\lambda}\text{ for all }\lambda\text{ sufficiently large}.

To establish (
7.16
), we need to deduce certain properties of
\nl_{1,\lambda}
, which we do by examining the function
H_{1,\lambda}
(evident from Theorem
1.1
). Setting
k=1
and
\chi
to be Poisson
(\lambda)
in (
1.9
), we obtain

(7.17)

H_{1,\lambda}(x)=e^{-\lambda e^{-\lambda x}}\implies\lambda^{3}(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}-1)e^{-\lambda x-\lambda e^{-\lambda x}}.

Note that, using (
5.2
), for all
\lambda>e
, we have

F_{1,\lambda}\left(\frac{\ln\lambda}{\lambda}\right)=\exp\left\{-\lambda\cdot\frac{\ln\lambda}{\lambda}\right\}=\frac{1}{\lambda}<\frac{\ln\lambda}{\lambda},

and as Lemma
2.2
states that
F_{1,\lambda}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1]
and its unique fixed point is
c_{1,\lambda}
, we conclude that

c_{1,\lambda}<\frac{\ln\lambda}{\lambda}\text{ for all }\lambda>e\implies\lambda e^{-\lambda x}>1\text{ for all }x\in[0,c_{1,\lambda}],\text{ for all }\lambda>e.

This finding, when applied to (
7.17
), reveals that
H_{1,\lambda}
is strictly convex on
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
for all
\lambda>e
. Consequently, the curve
y=H_{1,\lambda}(x)
intersects the line
y=x
at at most two points inside the interval
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
– if there are two intersections, then these happen at
\nl_{1,\lambda}
and
c_{1,\lambda}
, and if there is only one intersection, then this happens at
c_{1,\lambda}
(in which case
\nl_{1,\lambda}=c_{1,\lambda}
). This conclusion, along with Theorem
1.4
whose proof guarantees that
\nl_{1,\lambda}<c_{1,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough, reveals that the curve
y=H_{1,\lambda}(x)
stays
above
the line
y=x
when
x\in[0,\nl_{1,\lambda})
, and
beneath
it when
x\in(\nl_{1,\lambda},c_{1,\lambda})
, for
\lambda
sufficiently large.

We now note that
\lambda c_{1,\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, due to (
5.1
), so that
\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}<c_{1,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large. Therefore, to establish (
7.16
), it suffices to show that
y=H_{1,\lambda}(x)
lies beneath
y=x
at
x=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}
. Using
x>1-e^{-x}
for all
x>0
, we have

\displaystyle e^{\lambda}H_{1,\lambda}\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda}\exp\left\{-\lambda\exp\left\{-\lambda\cdot\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\lambda\left[1-\exp\left\{-\frac{\lambda e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\right\}\right]\right\}<\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\right\}.

Since the right side of the above inequality goes to
1
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, and we chose
\epsilon
above such that
\epsilon(1+\epsilon)<1
, the right side of the above inequality is strictly less than
\frac{1}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large, thus proving that
H_{1,\lambda}\left(\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}\right)<\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}
. In other words, we have proved that
y=H_{1,\lambda}(x)
lies beneath
y=x
at
x=\frac{e^{-\lambda}}{\epsilon(1+\epsilon)}
, as desired. This concludes §
7.2.3
, and with it, the proof of the fact that
\nd_{2,\lambda}<\md_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough.

7.2.4.
Proving that
\nl_{2,\lambda}<\nw_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough

Proving
\nl_{2,\lambda}<\nw_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large establishes the third and final claim made in the statement of Theorem
1.7
. By Theorem
1.1
, we have
\nw_{2,\lambda}=1-F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})
, so that it suffices to show that
F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})<1-\nl_{2,\lambda}
. From (
5.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})

\displaystyle=\exp\{\lambda e^{-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left\{-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right\},\text{ by \eqref{alternating_approx_1}};

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}+\frac{\lambda^{3}\nl_{2,\lambda}^{2}}{2}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\right)^{2},\text{ by \eqref{alternating_approx_2}};

\displaystyle\leqslant 1-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}+\epsilon\nl_{2,\lambda}\text{ by \eqref{claim_9};}

and this is less than
1-\nl_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
large enough since
\nl_{2,\lambda}=o\left(\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}\right)
. This concludes §
7.2.4
and the proof of the entire Theorem
1.7
.

8.
Proof of Theorem
1.9

Recall that the objectives of §
8
is to show that the inequalities
\nl_{2,\lambda}\leqslant\nl_{1,\lambda}
,
\nd_{2,\lambda}<\nd_{1,\lambda}
and
\nw_{1,\lambda}<\nw_{2,\lambda}
hold for all
\lambda
sufficiently large.

8.1.
Showing that
\nl_{2,\lambda}\leqslant\nl_{1,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large

We begin with the first inequality, and we establish this by first showing that
\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}\leqslant\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which is proved by induction on
n
, then taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
and using (
2.3
).

Since
\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(1)}=\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(1)}=e^{-\lambda}
, the base case for the inductive argument is verified. Suppose
\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}\leqslant\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(n)}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. From (
2.12
), we have
\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(n+2)}=H_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(n)})
and
\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}=H_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})
, so that our task now is to compare these two quantities. To this end, we observe, setting
k=2
and considering Poisson
(\lambda)
offspring in (
1.9
),

\displaystyle H_{2,\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle=G_{\lambda}\big(G_{\lambda}(1-F_{2,\lambda}(x))-G_{\lambda}(F_{1,\lambda}(x)-F_{2,\lambda}(x))\big)

\displaystyle=G_{\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda F_{2,\lambda}(x)}-e^{\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)-\lambda F_{2,\lambda}(x)-\lambda}\right)=G_{\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda F_{2,\lambda}(x)}\left[1-e^{\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(x)-\lambda}\right]\right).

Applying this, the induction hypothesis and the increasing nature of
H_{1,\lambda}
on
[0,1]
(as proved in §
2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nl_{1,\lambda}^{(n+2)}-\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n+2)}

\displaystyle\geqslant H_{1,\lambda}\left(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}\right)-H_{2,\lambda}\left(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}\right)

(8.1)

\displaystyle=G_{\lambda}\left(1-F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)-G_{\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})}\left[1-e^{\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})-\lambda}\right]\right).

We now construct a non-negative lower bound for the expression in (
8.1
).

Since
\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)}\leqslant\nl_{2,\lambda}
(evident from (
1.5
) and (
2.3
)) and since
F_{2,\lambda}
is strictly decreasing (by Lemma
2.2
), given any
0<\epsilon<1
, we have, using
e^{-x}-1>-x
for all
x>0
,

(8.2)

\displaystyle F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\geqslant F_{2,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda})=\exp\{\lambda e^{-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\}\geqslant\exp\{-\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}-\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}\}>1-\epsilon

for all
\lambda
sufficiently large, since both
\lambda^{2}\nl_{2,\lambda}
and
\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}
converges to
0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
due to Theorem
1.6
. Note, crucially, that how large
\lambda
needs to be for (
8.2
) to hold depends on
\epsilon
alone, and not on
n
. Using (
8.2
) and
1-e^{-x}<x
for all
x>0
, the expression on the right side of (
8.1
) is bounded below by

\displaystyle G_{\lambda}\left(1-F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)-G_{\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda(1-\epsilon)}\left[1-e^{\lambda F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})-\lambda}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant G_{\lambda}\left(1-F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\right)-G_{\lambda}\left(e^{-\lambda(1-\epsilon)}\lambda\{1-F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{2,\lambda}^{(n)})\}\right),

and this is strictly positive for all
\lambda
sufficiently large since
\lambda e^{-\lambda(1-\epsilon)}\rightarrow 0
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
(and hence eventually becomes strictly less than
1
) and
G_{\lambda}
is strictly increasing. This completes the inductive proof and brings us to the end of §
8.1
.

8.2.
Showing that
\nd_{2,\lambda}<\nd_{1,\lambda}
and
\nw_{1,\lambda}<\nw_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large

From Theorem
1.1
, we have the following lower bound on
\nd_{1,\lambda}
:

(8.3)

\displaystyle\nd_{1,\lambda}=F_{1,\lambda}(\nl_{1,\lambda})-\nl_{1,\lambda}=e^{-\lambda\nl_{1,\lambda}}-\nl_{1,\lambda}\geqslant 1-\lambda\nl_{1,\lambda}-\nl_{1,\lambda},

and comparing this with the lower bound on

obtained in (

), we see that the objective of §
8.2
will be accomplished if we can show that
\nl_{1,\lambda}<\lambda\nl_{2,\lambda}
for all
\lambda
sufficiently large.

Recall that we estabilshed in §

that
H_{1,\lambda}
is strictly convex on
[0,c_{1,\lambda}]
for
\lambda>e
, so that
y=H_{1,\lambda}(x)
lies
above

y=x
on

, and
beneath

y=x
on
(\nl_{1,\lambda},c_{1,\lambda})

sufficiently large. From (

), we have
\lambda c_{1,\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
, whereas Theorem

, so that

.

Using

, it follows immediately from the previous conclusion and the conclusion drawn in §

\nw_{1,\lambda}<\nw_{2,\lambda}
for all

sufficiently large.

9.
Proof of Theorem
1.10

Recall that we intend to show that when
\nl_{k}=c_{k}
and
\max\left\{H^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k}),\left|F^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k})\right|\right\}<1
, the expected duration of the
k
-jump normal game is finite. Here, unlike the previous few sections, we consider
any
offspring distribution
\chi
that satisfies the restrictions discussed in §
1.3
.

Suppose
\nl_{k}=c_{k}
,
H^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k})\leqslant\gamma
and
|F^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k})|\leqslant\gamma
for some
\gamma<1
. Given
0<\epsilon<1-\gamma
, from (
2.3
) and the continuity of
H^{\prime}_{k}
and
F^{\prime}_{k}
(due to the continuity of
G^{\prime}
), we know there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
H^{\prime}_{k}(x)\leqslant\gamma+\epsilon
and
|F^{\prime}_{k}(x)|\leqslant\gamma+\epsilon
for all
\nl_{k}^{(N)}\leqslant x\leqslant\nl_{k}=c_{k}
. Using (
2.12
) and (
1.5
), for all
n\geqslant N
, we have

(9.1)

\displaystyle\nl_{k}-\nl_{k}^{(n+2)}=H_{k}(\nl_{k})-H_{k}(\nl_{k}^{(n)})=H^{\prime}_{k}(\xi_{n})(\nl_{k}-\nl_{k}^{(n)})\leqslant(\epsilon+\gamma)(\nl_{k}-\nl_{k}^{(n)}),

where
\nl_{k}^{(n)}<\xi_{n}<\nl_{k}
(by the mean value theorem). Likewise, from (
2.13
) and (
1.5
), for all
n\geqslant N
, we have

(9.2)

\displaystyle\nw_{k}-\nw_{k}^{(n+2)}\leqslant\nw_{k}-\nw_{k}^{(n+1)}=F_{k}(\nl_{k}^{(n)})-F_{k}(\nl_{k})\leqslant(\epsilon+\gamma)(\nl_{k}-\nl_{k}^{(n)}).

Denoting the (random) duration of the game (starting at the root
\phi
of
\mathcal{T}_{\chi}
) by
T
, recalling the definition of
\ND_{k}^{(n)}
from §
1.3
, and letting
C=\sum_{n=1}^{N+1}(1-\nw_{k}^{(n)}-\nl_{k}^{(n)})
, we have

\displaystyle\E[T]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Prob[T\geqslant n]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Prob[\phi\in\ND_{k}^{(n)}]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nd_{k}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=C+\sum_{n=N+2}^{\infty}(1-\nw_{k}^{(n)}-\nl_{k}^{(n)})=C+\sum_{n=N+2}^{\infty}\{(\nw_{k}-\nw_{k}^{(n)})+(\nl_{k}-\nl_{k}^{(n)})\},

since
1-\nw_{k}-\nl_{k}=\nd_{k}=0
(by Theorem
1.3
, as
\nl_{k}=c_{k}
). It converges as (
9.1
) and (
9.2
) guarantee exponential decay of the summands as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

To establish the second part of Theorem
1.10
, we show that
|F^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k})|<1\implies H^{\prime}_{k}(c_{k})<1
when
k=2,3
(which then guarantees that the first part of Theorem
1.10
holds). Letting

a_{0}=G^{\prime}(1-c_{2})=|F^{\prime}_{1}(c_{2})|\text{ and }a_{1}=G^{\prime}(F_{1}(c_{2})-c_{2}),

we have (also making use of using Lemma
4.1
)

F^{\prime}_{2}(c_{2})=-a_{1}(a_{0}+1)\text{ and }H^{\prime}_{2}(c_{2})=a_{1}^{2}(a_{0}^{2}+2a_{0}-a_{1}a_{0}-a_{1})<\{a_{1}(a_{0}+1)\}^{2},

thus proving our claim for
k=2
.

The proof is similar for
k=3
, albeit requiring more involved computations, as follows. Letting

b_{0}=\left|F^{\prime}_{1}(c_{3})\right|=G^{\prime}(1-c_{3}),\ b_{1}=G^{\prime}(F_{1}(c_{3})-c_{3})\text{ and }b_{2}=G^{\prime}(F_{2}(c_{3})-c_{3}),

we have
F^{\prime}_{3}(c_{3})=-b_{2}(b_{1}b_{0}+b_{1}+1)
. This, along with Lemma
