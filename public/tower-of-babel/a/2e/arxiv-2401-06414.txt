A note on the atomicity of arithmeticity
Michael Hoefnagel, Pierre-Alain Jacqmin

Abstract
The main aim of this note is to show that, in the regular context, every matrix property in the sense of [ 13 ] either implies the Mal’tsev property, or is implied by the majority property. When the regular category ℂ \mathbb{C} is arithmetical, i.e., both Mal’tsev and a majority category, then we show that ℂ \mathbb{C} satisfies every non-trivial matrix property.



Keywords—
Arithmetical category, Mal’tsev category, majority category, matrix property, Mal’tsev condition.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification—

18E13, 08B05, 18E08, 08A05, 06-08.

Introduction

Consider an extended matrix of variables

\mathsf{M}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1m}&y_{1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{nm}&y_{n}\end{array}\right]

where the
x_{ij}
’s and the
y_{i}
’s are (not necessarily distinct) variables from
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. We refer to the last column (the column of
y_{i}
’s in
\mathsf{M}
) as the
right column
of
\mathsf{M}
, and every other column as a
left column
. A
row-wise interpretation
of
\mathsf{M}
of type
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
is a matrix of the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}f_{1}(x_{11})&\cdots&f_{1}(x_{1m})&f_{1}(y_{1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
f_{n}(x_{n1})&\cdots&f_{n}(x_{nm})&f_{n}(y_{n})\end{array}\right]

where the
f_{i}\colon\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}\rightarrow X_{i}
are functions. Given any relation
R\subseteq X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
, we say that it is strictly
\mathsf{M}
-closed if for every row-wise interpretation
N
of
\mathsf{M}
of type
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
if the left columns of
N
are elements of
R
then the right column of
N
is also an element of
R
. This set-theoretic property of relations can be internalised (via the Yoneda embedding) in any finitely complete category
\mathbb{C}
, so that
\mathbb{C}
is then said to have
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations if every internal relation in
\mathbb{C}
is strictly
\mathsf{M}
-closed. We will also refer to the property of
\mathsf{M}
-closedness of internal relations in
\mathbb{C}
as simply the
matrix property

\mathsf{M}
.

Let us formulate this property in a way which we will use for the remainder of this paper. Given two morphisms
f
and
g
in a category
\mathbb{C}
with the same codomain, we will write
f\sqsubset g
if
f
factors through
g
. This relation on morphisms in
\mathbb{C}
defines a
cover relation
in the sense of
[
15
,
16
]
. Then we can reformulate the matrix property
\mathsf{M}
of a finitely complete category
\mathbb{C}
in the following way: given any internal relation
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
in
\mathbb{C}
and any row-wise interpretation

N=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}c_{1}&\cdots&c_{m}&y\end{array}\right]

of the matrix
\mathsf{M}
of type
(\hom(S,X_{1}),\dots,\hom(S,X_{n}))
where the
c_{i}
’s are the left columns of
N
and the
y
is the right column of
N
(viewed as morphisms
S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
), then, if for every
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
we have
c_{i}\sqsubset r
, then we have
y\sqsubset r
.

The first and most well-known example of a matrix property is the property of a finitely complete category to be a Mal’tsev category
[
5
,
4
]
, since a category
\mathbb{C}
with finite limits is a Mal’tsev category if and only if every internal relation is
difunctional

[
19
]
, i.e., every internal relation is strictly
\mathsf{Mal}
-closed where

\mathsf{Mal}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\end{array}\right].

Similarly to what was done in
[
9
]
, given integers
n,k>0
and
m\geqslant 0
, we write
\mathsf{matr}(n,m,k)
for the set of all matrices with
n
rows,
m+1
columns and whose entries are in the set
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\}
. Then
\mathsf{matr}
is the union of all such
\mathsf{matr}(n,m,k)
for
n,k>0
and
m\geqslant 0
. Corresponding to a matrix
\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}
, we will write
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}
for the collection of finitely complete categories which satisfy the matrix property
\mathsf{M}
, and refer to these collections as
matrix classes
. The collection of all such matrix classes is then denoted by
\mathsf{Mclex}
, i.e.,

\mathsf{Mclex}=\{\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}\mid\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}\}

and it has a poset structure given by inclusion of matrix classes. We will also write
\mathsf{Mclex}[n,m,k]
for the sub-poset of
\mathsf{Mclex}
of matrix classes
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}
determined by a matrix
\mathsf{M}
in
\mathsf{matr}(n,m,k)
. Among the elements of the poset
\mathsf{Mclex}
are two
trivial
ones, i.e., the matrix class of preorders with a single isomorphism class and the matrix class of finitely complete preorders. These are respectively the bottom element and the unique atom of
\mathsf{Mclex}
. They are determined by the so-called
trivial matrices
(see
[
11
]
). The top element of
\mathsf{Mclex}
, i.e., the matrix class of all finitely complete categories is called the
anti-trivial
element and is determined by the so-called
anti-trivial matrices
. The
degenerate matrix classes
(respectively the
degenerate matrices
) are the ones which are either trivial or anti-trivial.

As another example of a collection of categories determined by a matrix property, consider the matrix property corresponding to the matrix
\mathsf{Maj}
where

\mathsf{Maj}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}\end{array}\right].

Then,
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Maj}\}
is the collection of all finitely complete majority categories
[
7
]
. For another example, consider the matrix
\mathsf{Ari}
where

\mathsf{Ari}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\end{array}\right].

Then
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}
is the collection of all finitely complete arithmetical categories as defined in
[
11
]
. Note that in the Barr-exact context
[
1
]
with coequalisers this matrix property determines arithmetical categories as introduced first in
[
18
]
and later generalised to wider contexts in
[
2
,
6
]
.

Some matrix properties imply others, which is to say that some matrix classes are contained (as sub-collections) in other matrix classes. For example, we have that
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Maj}\}
and also
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Mal}\}
. Thus, the general question: is there a procedure for determining when a given matrix class contains another? This question has been recently answered in the paper
[
11
]
, where an algorithm was given which determines inclusions of the form
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{N}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}
, i.e., which determines implications of matrix properties. Computer implementation of this algorithm allows us to determine, for relatively small
n,m,k
, the posets
\mathsf{Mclex}[n,m,k]
. For instance, Figure
1
(which describes the same poset as Figure 2 in
[
11
]
and Figure 1 in
[
12
]
) gives a visual depiction of the poset of non-degenerate elements of
\mathsf{Mclex}[3,7,2]
as obtained by the computer, where each integer entry
i
corresponds to the variable
x_{i}
and the shaded column is the right column in the representing matrix. Note that most of these matrix classes are not represented here by
3\times(7+1)
matrices but up to duplication of rows and left columns, they can be turned to such matrices. One of the main results of
[
12
]
shows that among the non-trivial matrix classes in
\mathsf{Mclex}
represented by a matrix with at most two variables (i.e., binary matrices) the matrix class
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}
is the least. In fact, Figure
1
already illustrates this fact, as the matrix class farthest to the left is
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}
. However, in
\mathsf{Mclex}
we have non-trivial matrix classes which are strictly contained in
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}
.

Figure 1:
The poset of non-degenerate elements of
\mathsf{Mclex}[3,7,2]
. The matrix furthest to the left represents the matrix class
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}
and the only matrix with two rows represents
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Mal}\}
. The matrix which is just above this latter matrix represents the matrix class
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Maj}\}
.

Let us now write
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{M}\}
for the collection of all regular categories
[
1
]
satisfying the matrix property
\mathsf{M}
, i.e.,
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{M}\}
is the intersection of
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}
and the collection of all regular categories. Such classes of regular categories we refer to as
regular
matrix classes. We may then consider the poset
\mathsf{Mcreg}
of all regular matrix classes ordered by inclusion, and ask the analagous question: is there an algorithm for determining whether or not
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{M}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{N}\}
? As it stands, this question is still open, and it is known that
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{M}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{N}\}
need not imply that
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}\subseteq\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{N}\}
— see Section 5 of
[
11
]
.

In this paper we will show that the regular matrix classes corresponding to
\mathsf{Mal}
,
\mathsf{Maj}
and
\mathsf{Ari}
play a special role in
\mathsf{Mcreg}
. For one thing, we will show that every regular matrix class in
\mathsf{Mcreg}
is either contained in
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{Mal}\}
or contains
\mathsf{mcreg}\{\mathsf{Maj}\}
. We will also show that among the non-trivial members of
\mathsf{Mcreg}
, the least is the regular matrix class corresponding to
\mathsf{Ari}
. While, as shown in
[
12
]
, both results extend to the finitely complete context for binary matrices, we know they do not extend to that context in full generality.

2
A strong majority property

Given a category
\mathbb{C}
with binary products, in what follows we will write
\pi_{i,j}
for the two-fold projection
(\pi_{i},\pi_{j})\colon X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}\rightarrow X_{i}\times X_{j}
determined by
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{j}
, where
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For a natural number
n\geqslant 3
, we define the following property on a finitely complete category
\mathbb{C}
.

(M_{n})

For any morphism
y\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
and any monomorphism
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
, if
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
for any
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, then
y\sqsubset r
.

Note that for such
y\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
,
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
and
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, if
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
, then
\pi_{j,i}y\sqsubset\pi_{j,i}r
and
\pi_{i,i}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,i}r
; so that in the above description of
(M_{n})
, it is equivalent to ask
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
only for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
i<j
. As we will see shortly, the property
(M_{n})
for any integer
n\geqslant 3
is equivalent to a matrix property. Define a matrix
\mathsf{M}_{n}
with
n
rows,
m=\binom{n}{2}
left columns
c_{i,j}
indexed by all pairs of integers
(i,j)
where
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
and whose right column is the column vector containing only the variable
x_{1}
. Order the left columns
c_{i,j}<c_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}
from left to right according to the lexicographic order
(i,j)<(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
. In each column
c_{i,j}
place a
x_{1}
at the
i^{\textrm{th}}
and
j^{\textrm{th}}
entry. Then in each row, insert the variables
x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
(where
k=\binom{n-1}{2}+1
) in increasing order (of index) at each position which does not contain a
x_{1}
. For example, in the case
n=3
, the matrix
M_{3}
is nothing but

\mathsf{M}_{3}=\mathsf{Maj}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}\end{array}\right]

as defined in the Introduction. In the case
n=4
, we have

\mathsf{M}_{4}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc|c}x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{4}&x_{1}\\
x_{1}&x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{4}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{1}&x_{3}&x_{1}&x_{4}&x_{1}&x_{1}\\
x_{2}&x_{3}&x_{1}&x_{4}&x_{1}&x_{1}&x_{1}\end{array}\right].

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
be an integer. A finitely complete category
\mathbb{C}
satisfies
(M_{n})
if and only if
\mathbb{C}
has
\mathsf{M}_{n}
-closed relations, i.e.,
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the matrix property corresponding to
\mathsf{M}_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{C}
satisfies
(M_{n})
and let
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
be any monomorphism and

M^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1m}&y_{1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{nm}&y_{n}\end{array}\right]

be a row-wise interpretation of the matrix
\mathsf{M}_{n}
of type
(\hom(S,X_{1}),\dots,\hom(S,X_{n}))
where
m=\binom{n}{2}
. Each column of the matrix
M^{\prime}
above determines a (unique) morphism
S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
, and we will write
c^{\prime}_{i,j}\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
for the morphism corresponding to the left column
c_{i,j}
of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
. We write
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
for the morphism determined by the right column of
M^{\prime}
. We suppose that each
c^{\prime}_{i,j}
factorises through
r
and we must show that
y
also does. As earlier, we write
\pi_{i,j}\colon X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}\rightarrow X_{i}\times X_{j}
for the two-fold projection determined by
\pi_{i}
and
\pi_{j}
. It may then be seen that
\pi_{i,j}c^{\prime}_{i,j}=(y_{i},y_{j})
, since
M^{\prime}
is a row-wise interpretation of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
. Therefore
\pi_{i,j}y=\pi_{i,j}c^{\prime}_{i,j}\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
with
i<j
so that
y\sqsubset r
by
(M_{n})
.

Conversely, suppose that
\mathbb{C}
has
\mathsf{M}_{n}
-closed relations and that we are given a monomorphism
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
and a morphism
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
such that
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
for each
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. Thus, there are factorisations
f^{\prime}_{i,j}\colon S\rightarrow R
such that
\pi_{i,j}rf^{\prime}_{i,j}=(y_{i},y_{j})
. Form the matrix
M^{\prime}
whose left columns are determined by the morphisms
c^{\prime}_{i,j}=rf^{\prime}_{i,j}
(again ordering them via the lexicographic order on
\mathbb{N}^{2}
) and whose right column is determined by the morphism
y
. This matrix
M^{\prime}
is then a row-wise interpretation of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
of type
(\hom(S,X_{1}),\dots,\hom(S,X_{n}))
. Since
\mathbb{C}
has
\mathsf{M}_{n}
-closed relations, we deduce that
y\sqsubset r
.
∎

The matrix
\mathsf{M}_{4}
is identical (up to replacement of variables) to the matrix in Remark 2.4 of
[
12
]
. For this reason, we know we do
not
have
\mathsf{M}_{3}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{4}
although we do have
\mathsf{M}_{4}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{3}
. We can actually generalise this.

Proposition 2.2
.

We have a sequence of strict implications

\cdots\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{n+1}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{n}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\cdots\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{5}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{4}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{3}

and all these matrices are non-degenerate.

Proof.

According to Theorem 2.5 in
[
11
]
, a matrix is anti-trivial if and only if its right column appears among its left columns. Clearly, this is not the case for these matrices
\mathsf{M}_{n}
. Moreover, we can immediately deduce from Theorem 2.3 in
[
11
]
that these matrices are not trivial neither and so not degenerate.

Let us now prove that for
n\geqslant 3
, we have
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{n}
. To fix notation, let
m=\binom{n}{2}
,
k=\binom{n-1}{2}+1
,
m^{\prime}=\binom{n+1}{2}
and
k^{\prime}=\binom{n}{2}+1
so that
\mathsf{M}_{n}\in\mathsf{Mclex}[n,m,k]
and
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}\in\mathsf{Mclex}[{n+1},m^{\prime},k^{\prime}]
. According to the algorithm from
[
11
]
, to prove
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{n}
it is enough to prove that the matrix
M^{\prime}
formed by the first
n
rows of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
admits a row-wise interpretation of type
(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\},\dots,\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\})
whose left columns can be found among the left columns of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
and whose right column is the right column of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
. This can be easily seen by interpreting in
M^{\prime}
each
x_{1}
by
x_{1}
and each other variable (which appears only once in each row) by the only variable such that the left column
c^{\prime}_{i,j}
of
M^{\prime}
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
is interpreted as the left column
c_{i,j}
of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
, and the left column
c^{\prime}_{i,n+1}
of
M^{\prime}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
is interpreted as the left column
c_{i,n}
if
i<n
or
c_{1,n}
if
i=n
of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
.

It remains to prove that we do not have
\mathsf{M}_{n}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
. Again according to the algorithm from
[
11
]
, it is enough to prove that for any matrix
M^{\prime}
with
n+1
rows and whose each row is a row of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
, and for any row-wise interpretation
M^{\prime\prime}
of
M^{\prime}
of type
(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k^{\prime}}\},\dots,\{x_{1},\dots,x_{k^{\prime}}\})
, if the left columns of
M^{\prime\prime}
can be found among the left columns of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
, then so can its right column. By contradiction, suppose
M^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime\prime}
are such matrices such that the left columns of
M^{\prime\prime}
, but not its right column, are among the left columns of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
. In each row of
\mathsf{M}_{n}
(and so of
M^{\prime}
), there are exactly
n-1
many
x_{1}
’s in its left part (i.e., not counting its rightmost entry). If, for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, these
x_{1}
’s in the
j^{\textrm{th}}
row of
M^{\prime}
are interpreted in
M^{\prime\prime}
as
x_{l}
with
1<l\leqslant k^{\prime}
, then the left columns of
M^{\prime}
containing
x_{1}
in the
j^{\textrm{th}}
row are all interpreted the same in
M^{\prime\prime}
since
x_{l}
appears exactly once in the
j^{\textrm{th}}
row of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
. In that case, since for each
j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
, there is a left column of
M^{\prime}
with
x_{1}
as
j^{\textrm{th}}
and
j^{\prime\textrm{ th}}
entries, the right column of
M^{\prime}
(constituted only of
x_{1}
’s) is interpreted in
M^{\prime\prime}
in the same way as its left columns containing
x_{1}
in the
j^{\textrm{th}}
row. This would imply that the right column of
M^{\prime\prime}
must be found among the left columns of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, each
x_{1}
in the left part of
M^{\prime}
is interpreted as
x_{1}
in
M^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, there are at least
(n+1)(n-1)
many
x_{1}
’s in the left part of
M^{\prime\prime}
. However, each left column of
M^{\prime\prime}
being a left column of
\mathsf{M}_{n+1}
, they contain exactly two
x_{1}
’s each. There are thus exactly
2m=2\binom{n}{2}=n(n-1)
many
x_{1}
’s in the left part of
M^{\prime\prime}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

We are now able to prove the following.

Proposition 2.5
.

Given integers
n\geqslant 3
,
m\geqslant 0
and
k>0
and a matrix
\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}(n,m,k)
, we have that either
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
or
\mathsf{M}_{n}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}
, and these two implications cannot occur simultaneously.

Proof.

By construction, every selection of two rows from
\mathsf{M}_{n}
forms an anti-trivial matrix and so
\mathsf{M}_{n}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
does not hold by Proposition
2.4
. This already proves that the two implications of the statement cannot occur simultaneously. Suppose now that
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
does not hold. Then every selection of two rows of
\mathsf{M}
forms an anti-trivial matrix by Proposition
2.4
. Let
\mathbb{C}
be any finitely complete category in
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}_{n}\}
and let us prove it is in
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{M}\}
. Let
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
be any relation in
\mathbb{C}
and let

M^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}c^{\prime}_{1}&\cdots&c^{\prime}_{m}&y\end{array}\right]

be any row-wise interpretation of
\mathsf{M}
of type
(\hom(S,X_{1}),\dots,\hom(S,X_{n}))
where the left columns
c^{\prime}_{1},\dots,c^{\prime}_{m}
of
M^{\prime}
(viewed as morphisms
S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
) satisfy
c^{\prime}_{l}\sqsubset r
for each
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. We must show that the right column
y
of
M^{\prime}
(also viewed as a morphism
S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
) satisfy
y\sqsubset r
. Since, by Proposition
2.1
,
\mathbb{C}
satisfies the property
(M_{n})
, we only have to show that
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. For such
i
and
j
, since the matrix obtained by selecting the
i^{\textrm{th}}
and the
j^{\textrm{th}}
row of
\mathsf{M}
is anti-trivial, we know that
\pi_{i,j}y=\pi_{i,j}c^{\prime}_{l}
for some
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. Therefore,
\pi_{i,j}y=\pi_{i,j}c^{\prime}_{l}\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
as desired.
∎

3
The regular context

Recall that a category is
regular

[
1
]
if it is finitely complete, has coequalisers of kernel pairs and regular epimorphisms are stable under pullbacks. In that case, each morphism factorises as a regular epimorphism followed by a monomorphism. Although, in the finitely complete context, the implications of Proposition
2.2
are strict, this is not the case any more in the regular context as attested by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have a sequence of equivalences

\cdots\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{n+1}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{n}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\cdots\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{5}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{4}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{3}

i.e., for any integer
n\geqslant 3
, in the regular context, the property
(M_{n})
is equivalent to the majority property
(M_{3})
.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
2.2
and since
\mathsf{M}_{3}=\mathsf{Maj}
, it is enough to prove that any regular majority category
\mathbb{C}
satisfies
(M_{n})
for each
n\geqslant 3
. Given such
\mathbb{C}
and
n
, let
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
be any monomorphism and let
y\colon S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
be any morphism in
\mathbb{C}
such that, for any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
. We must show that
y\sqsubset r
. For all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{e_{i,j}}
\textstyle{R_{i,j}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m_{i,j}}
\textstyle{X_{i}\times X_{j}}

be the (regular epimorphism, monomorphism)-factorisation of the composite

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r}
\textstyle{X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{i,j}}
\textstyle{X_{i}\times X_{j}.}

By the equivalence of (i) and (v) of Theorem 5.1 in
[
8
]
, and Proposition 4.1 in
[
8
]
, the diagram

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(e_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{2}}}
\scriptstyle{r}
\textstyle{\prod\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}R_{i,j}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\prod\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}m_{i,j}}
\textstyle{\prod\limits_{l=1}^{n}X_{l}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(\pi_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,n\}^{2}}}
\textstyle{\prod\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}X_{i}\times X_{j}}

is a pullback. Since, for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have supposed
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r
, we know that
\pi_{i,j}y\sqsubset m_{i,j}
; producing a morphism
S\rightarrow\prod_{i,j=1}^{n}R_{i,j}
so that, by the universal property of the above pullback, we get
y\sqsubset r
.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

For any matrix
\mathsf{M}
in
\mathsf{matr}
, we have that either
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{Mal}
or
\mathsf{Maj}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}
, and these two implications cannot occur simultaneously.

Proof.

If these two implications occur simultaneously, we would have
\mathsf{Maj}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{Mal}
, which, by Theorem 2.3 in
[
10
]
, is equivalent to
\mathsf{Maj}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
. But this last implication does not hold, for instance by Proposition
2.4
.

Let us now prove that
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{Mal}
or
\mathsf{Maj}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}
. If
\mathsf{M}
is trivial, then
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{Mal}
; and if
\mathsf{M}
is anti-trivial, we have
\mathsf{Maj}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}
. It remains to treat the case where
\mathsf{M}
is non-degenerate. Suppose that
\mathsf{M}
has
n
rows. If
n\leqslant 2
, by Proposition
2.3
,
\mathsf{M}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
and so in particular
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{Mal}
. Let us now suppose that
n\geqslant 3
. Then, we have that either
\mathsf{M}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
or
\mathsf{M}_{n}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}
by Proposition
2.5
. The result then follows since
\mathsf{Maj}\Leftrightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 3
by Theorem
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Let us make clear here that, although Corollary
3.2
takes place in the regular context, it holds for the matrix properties in the sense of
[
13
]
but
not
for the matrix properties in the sense of
[
15
]
. Indeed, an example of these latter matrix properties is the property of being a Goursat category
[
3
]
. An example of a (regular) Goursat category which is not Mal’tsev is given by the category of implication algebras
[
17
]
while an example of a majority category which is not Goursat is given by the category of lattices
[
3
,
7
]
.

The proof of the theorem below makes use of the fact that
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
and
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Maj}
. These implications already appear in Figure
1
. For a proof of them, we refer the reader to Section 5 of
[
11
]
. There, it is actually shown that
\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Ari}\}=\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Mal}\}\cap\mathsf{mclex}\{\mathsf{Maj}\}
, i.e., a finitely complete category is arithmetical if and only if it is both Mal’tsev and majority.

Theorem 3.4
.

For any non-trivial matrix
\mathsf{M}
in
\mathsf{matr}
, we have
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{reg}}\mathsf{M}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathsf{M}
is any non-trivial matrix with
n>0
rows. If
n\leqslant 2
, then
\mathsf{Mal}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}
by Proposition
2.3
and therefore
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}M
since
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{Mal}
. We can therefore suppose without loss of generality that
n\geqslant 3
. Let
\mathbb{C}
be a regular arithmetical category,
r\colon R\rightarrowtail X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
be any monomorphism in
\mathbb{C}
and the matrix

M^{\prime}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c}x_{11}&\cdots&x_{1m}&y_{1}\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
x_{n1}&\cdots&x_{nm}&y_{n}\end{array}\right]

be a row-wise interpretation of the matrix
\mathsf{M}
of type
(\hom(S,X_{1}),\dots,\hom(S,X_{n}))
. Viewing each of the left columns
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
of
M^{\prime}
and its right column
y
as morphisms
S\rightarrow X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}
, we suppose that
c_{l}\sqsubset r
for each
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
and we must show
y\sqsubset r
. For any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, consider the (regular epimorphism, monomorphism)-factorisation

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{e_{i,j}}
\textstyle{R_{i,j}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m_{i,j}}
\textstyle{X_{i}\times X_{j}}

of the composite

\textstyle{R\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{r}
\textstyle{X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{n}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{i,j}}

For any such
i,j
, by Proposition 1.7 in
[
14
]
, since
\mathsf{M}
is non-trivial, the matrix

formed from selecting the

is non-trivial. Hence,
\mathsf{Mal}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}[i\colon\!\!,j\colon\!\!]

2.3
and so
\mathsf{Ari}\Rightarrow_{\mathsf{lex}}\mathsf{M}[i\colon\!\!,j\colon\!\!]
. Since
\pi_{i,j}c_{l}\sqsubset m_{i,j}
for each

such that

\textstyle{Q_{i,j}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\pi_{i,j}y\alpha_{i,j}\sqsubset\pi_{i,j}r

produces a regular epimorphism

A regular Mal’tsev category
\mathbb{C}
has been shown to have distributive lattices of equivalence relations if and only if it is a majority category
[

]
. Various alternative characterisations of

regular Mal’tsev categories have been given in
[

, as well as equivalence distributive Goursat categories. By the theorem above, every regular equivalence distributive Mal’tsev category satisfies each non-trivial matrix property from
\mathsf{Mclex}
.

By definition, a matrix
\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}
is trivial if any finitely complete category with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder. In
[
9
]
, it is proved that we can equivalently consider only finitely complete

\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}
is trivial if and only if any finitely complete pointed category with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder. As a corollary of Theorem
3.4
, we prove that we can equivalently only consider varieties of universal algebras.

Corollary 3.5
.

For a matrix
\mathsf{M}\in\mathsf{matr}
, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

\mathsf{M}
is trivial, i.e., any finitely complete category with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder.

(ii)

Any pointed finitely complete category with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder.

(iii)

Any regular category with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder.

(iv)

Any variety with
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations is a preorder, i.e., the equation
x=y
holds in its theory.

(v)

The dual of the category of sets
\mathsf{Set}^{\mathsf{op}}
does not have
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations.

(vi)

The dual of the category of pointed sets
\mathsf{Set}_{\ast}^{\mathsf{op}}
does not have
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations.

(vii)

The category of Boolean algebras
\mathsf{Bool}
does not have
\mathsf{M}
-closed relations.

Proof.

The equivalence
(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(v)
appears in
[
11
]
while the equivalences
(i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii)
\Leftrightarrow
(vi)
appear in
[

]
. The implications
(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv)
are obvious and the implication
(iv)

\mathsf{Bool}
is a variety of universal algebras which is not a preorder. Finally, the implication

follows immediately from Theorem

since
\mathsf{Bool}
is a regular arithmetical category.
∎
