Perturbation theory for killed Markov processes and quasi-stationary distributions


Abstract
Motivated by recent developments of quasi-stationary Monte Carlo methods, we investigate the stability of quasi-stationary distributions of killed Markov processes under perturbations of the generator. We first consider a general bounded self-adjoint perturbation operator, and after that, study a particular unbounded perturbation corresponding to truncation of the killing rate. In both scenarios, we quantify the difference between eigenfunctions of the smallest eigenvalue of the perturbed and unperturbed generators in a Hilbert space norm. As a consequence, ℒ 1 \mathcal{L}^{1} norm estimates of the difference of the resulting quasi-stationary distributions in terms of the perturbation are provided.

1
Introduction

Perturbation theory concerns the investigation of
the stability of a given system under small changes. In the context of Markov chains, one typically studies the impact of perturbing the transition mechanism, namely the transition kernel or matrix, on the behavior of the corresponding chain. For example, one can study the resultant effect on the limiting distribution and approximation properties, see e.g.

[
17
,
35
,
30
,
31
,
36
]
and the references therein.

In this work, we extend the study of perturbation theory to the setting of
killed
Markov processes which possess
quasi-stationary distributions
. This is particularly motivated by recent developments in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), where killed diffusions have been used to construct scalable algorithms to perform Bayesian inference for large data sets, see
[
33
,
21
]
. Convergence properties of this approach, known as
quasi-stationary Monte Carlo
(QSMC), have been investigated in
[
43
]
.

Given that such methods have been applied to perform Bayesian inference, it is important to study their stability. We ask the following question:
Can the quasi-stationary distribution be adversely affected by small perturbations of the system, which could arise, for instance, due to data corruption, or missing data?

We start by modeling the perturbation as a bounded self-adjoint operator and quantify the difference in a general setting between the eigenfunctions of the smallest eigenvalues with respect to the perturbed and unperturbed generators, see Theorem
11
.
As a consequence, one can develop
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-bounds on the difference between the corresponding quasi-stationary distributions, which is illustrated in the QSMC framework for logistic regression, see Proposition
21
.

In addition, we also consider a specific unbounded perturbation, which corresponds to truncating a divergent killing rate.
In practical implementations of QSMC methods, such a truncation can significantly reduce the complexity of the implementation, since simulating arrivals from a Poisson process with a bounded intensity is straightforward using Poisson thinning, whereas unbounded intensities are much more intricate.
We give an explicit bound on the
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-norm, depending on the truncation level, of the difference of the eigenfunctions of the smallest eigenvalue of the generators with and without truncation, see Theorem
3.24
. We apply this result to an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck diffusion with killing and obtain an estimate of the difference between the perturbed quasi-stationary distribution and a Gaussian target distribution in terms of an
\mathcal{L}^{1}
-norm, see Propositions
3.28
,
3.31
. In this setting, we find that truncation is robust with respect to the dimension; in any dimension
d\geq 1
, the error decays exponentially as a function of the truncation level.

1.1
Related results and literature review

Quasi-stationary distributions have been, and continue to be, actively studied within the probability theory literature; see for instance the book of
[
8
]
or the review paper
[
27
]
. More recently, the construction of schemes with a desired quasi-stationary distribution has been investigated in the context of computational statistics, see
[
33
,
21
,
43
]
. Quasi-stationary distributions have also been utilized recently as a tool to analyse other algorithms; see for instance
[
23
,
2
]
for analyses of Monte Carlo algorithms in the context of molecular dynamics.

Perturbation theory in the context of Markov chains (without killing) is richly studied in the literature; see for instance the references
[
17
,
35
,
30
,
31
,
16
,
36
,

,
15
,
32
,
13
]
.
However, there are key differences between the classical Markov chain setting and our quasi-stationary setting. For a Markov chain with killing, we typically study the conditional distribution after
n
steps
conditional on survival
. This distribution often converges to a quasi-stationary distribution, whereas in the classical Markov chain setting there is no such conditioning. This makes the approximate discrete-time Markov chain with killing setting more demanding, and we present a result in the subsequent Section
1.2
.

However, following our motivation of QSMC methods
[
33
]
, the main focus of this work will be on continuous-time diffusion processes with killing and their quasi-stationary distributions.
This is a departure from the traditional Markov chain perturbation theory literature, as cited above, and requires new tools.
Thus we apply functional analytic tools, building on techniques developed for the study of perturbations of linear operators, a rich and mature field
[
34
,
3
,
18
]
. Several of our results will thus consist of translating these abstract results appropriately into the setting of killed diffusions.

1.2
Result for discrete-time Markov chains

We now give a result for discrete-time Markov chains with killing; in our discussion
[
37
]

this has already been reported without proof.
We repeat the specific setting and the statement:
Let
(S_{0},\mathcal{S}_{0})
be a measurable space and
\partial\not\in S_{0}
an additional element, which serves as “trap” of the killed Markov chain. Moreover,
Q\colon S_{0}\times\mathcal{S}_{0}\to[0,1]
is a transition kernel and
\kappa\colon S_{0}\to[0,1]
is used to describe a state-dependent killing probability. For a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of random variables
X_{n}\colon\Omega\to S_{0}\cup\{\partial\}
determined by the following transition mechanism, depending on
Q
and
\kappa
with
X_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{0}\in S_{0}
. Given
X_{n}=x
with
x\in S_{0}\cup\{\partial\}
, the distribution of
X_{n+1}
is specified by two steps:

1.

If
x=\partial
, then return
X_{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\partial
;

2.

Otherwise, draw a uniformly distributed sample
u\sim\textrm{Unif}[0,1]
. Independently, draw a sample
y\sim Q(x,\cdot)
. Then, return

X_{n+1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}\partial&\textrm{if }u\leq\kappa(x),\\
y&\textrm{if }u>\kappa(x).\end{cases}

Thus,
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a Markov chain with killing, determined by
Q
,
\kappa
and initialization
x_{0}
. Now suppose we only have access to
\kappa
through an approximation
\widetilde{\kappa}\colon S_{0}\to[0,1]
with
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}
small. Setting
\widetilde{X}_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x_{0}
, let
(\widetilde{X}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be another sequence of random variables on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
which is constructed identically to
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, except that

\widetilde{\kappa}
is used in step
2
above instead of
\kappa
.
For the convenience of the reader, we write
\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}
instead of
\mathbb{P}
to indicate the initialization at
x_{0}
.
Now, under suitable regularity conditions we can quantify the difference of the conditional distributions of interest, derived from the Markov chains with killing rates
\kappa
and
\widetilde{\kappa}
.

Proposition 1.1

Assume that there exist
\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\in(0,1)
and
c_{\ell},\widetilde{c}_{\ell},c_{u},\widetilde{c}_{u}\colon S_{0}\to(0,\infty)
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{n}c_{\ell}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})\leq\alpha^{n}c_{u}(x_{0}),

(1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\alpha}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{\ell}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\leq\widetilde{\alpha}^{n}\widetilde{c}_{u}(x_{0}).

(2)

Then

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in\cdot\mid X_{n}\in S_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in\cdot\mid\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\right\|_{\text{\rm tv}}\leq\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}K(x_{0})\min\{n,|\alpha-\widetilde{\alpha}|^{-1}\},

where

K(x_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2\sup_{z\in S_{0}}\int_{S_{0}}\frac{\widetilde{c}_{u}(y)c_{u}(x_{0})}{\min\{c_{\ell}(x_{0}),\widetilde{c}_{\ell}(x_{0})\}}\,Q(z,{\rm d}y),

and
\|\cdot\|_{\text{\rm tv}}
denotes the total variation distance.

Note that for irreducible chains on finite state spaces the conditions (
1
) and (
2
) are generally mild assumptions; these can be straightforwardly seen to hold for some
\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\in(0,1)
by the Perron–Frobenius theorem,
[
38
]
.

At first glance (for finite
K(x_{0})
), this would appear to be a particularly good estimate, however, in the regime when
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}

is small, it is probable that
|\alpha-\widetilde{\alpha}|
is also small. Regardless, given
n
and if
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}
is negligible compared to
n^{-1}
— say
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}\leq n^{-2}
— then the right hand-side of the stated estimate is of order
n^{-1}
.

The proof of Proposition
1.1
is provided in Appendix
A.1
. A key aspect in the reasoning is handling the different normalizing constants in the conditional probabilities.
It is not clear how this
approach could be extended to the continuous-time killed Markov process setting, on which the QSMC setting relies. Therefore we follow here a different route based on Hilbert space techniques and spectral properties.

1.3
Background to quasi-stationary Monte Carlo

We provide some background to the framework and theoretical properties used in the development of QSMC schemes, largely following
[
43
]
.

For fixed dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\nabla
be the gradient operator, i.e., the
d
-dimensional vector with components
\nabla_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\partial/\partial x_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,d
. Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the
d
-dimensional reversible diffusion, which is defined as the (weak) solution of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\dif X_{t}=\nabla A(X_{t})\dif t+\dif W_{t}.

(3)

Here
W=(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
and

A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is an infinitely differentiable function such that the SDE (
3
) has a unique non-explosive weak solution. To be more precise:
Let
C
be the space of all continuous functions mapping from the non-negative real numbers into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
\omega\in C
and each
t\geq 0
define
X_{t}\colon C\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
to be the coordinate map
X_{t}(\omega)=\omega(t)
and let
\mathcal{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma(\{X_{t}\colon t\geq 0\})
. For
X_{0}=x
, with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
be the probability measure on
(C,\mathcal{C})
, such that
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the weak solution of (
3
) under
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}
. For arbitrary initial distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, that is,
X_{0}\sim\mu
, we have
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}_{x}(\cdot)\mu(\dif x)
.
We then augment the probability space with an additional independent unit exponential random variable
\xi
, denoting the resulting probability space by
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{x})
(or
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}_{\mu})
respectively).
Fix now a measurable non-negative function
\kappa\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
, the
killing rate
, and define the killing time,

\tau_{\partial}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\int_{0}^{t}\kappa(X_{s})\dif s\geq\xi\right\}.

(4)

By convention, set
\inf\emptyset=\infty
.

Now let
\pi
be the probability measure of interest on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given through a positive, infinitely-differentiable and integrable Lebesgue density. By an abuse of notation we also denote this density by
\pi
. The idea is to choose the function
\kappa
in such a way that
\pi
is
the unique
quasi-stationary distribution of
X
killed at rate
\kappa
: for each
t\geq 0
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(X_{t}\in\cdot\mid\tau_{\partial}>t)=\pi.

(5)

In
[
43
]
it has been shown that (
5
) is satisfied, under mild regularity conditions, if

\kappa=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta\pi}{\pi}-\frac{2\nabla A\cdot\nabla\pi}{\pi}-2\Delta A\right)+K,

(6)

with
K>0
being chosen such that
\kappa\geq 0
, and with
\Delta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial^{2}x_{i}}
being the Laplacian operator. In this reference it is shown that
\pi
is the unique quasi-stationary distribution of
X
and that the convergence of the distribution of
X_{t}
given
\tau_{\partial}>t
to
\pi
can take place geometrically.

2
Bounded perturbations

In this section we provide a self-contained proof of a norm-estimate of the difference between the eigenfunctions of two (potentially unbounded) self-adjoint operators, where one is considered to be a bounded perturbation of the other.

In the context of quasi-stationary Monte Carlo, such bounds are reassuring for practitioners wishing to implement QSMC methods, since our bound will say that small self-adjoint perturbations of the generator will not substantially affect the resulting quasi-stationary distribution.
Such self-adjoint perturbations can arise in practical situations when the target density has been perturbed slightly, since the target density typically only enters the QSMC algorithm via the killing rate (which is a multiplication operator, hence self-adjoint). For example, in a logistic regression scenario, such a perturbation could correspond to
corrupted
or
missing
data; see Section
2.4
.

2.1
Assumptions and first consequences

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real separable Hilbert space with inner-product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and denote by
\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|f\|=1\}
the corresponding unit sphere. For a linear subspace
\mathbb{V}
, we write
\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
for its orthogonal complement and define the linear operator
P_{\mathbb{V}}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{V}
as the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathbb{V}
.

Suppose we have a densely-defined self-adjoint positive semi-definite linear operator
L\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
with domain
\mathcal{D}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{H}
; we will simply write
(L,\mathcal{D}(L))
. Recall that the positive semi-definiteness implies that
\langle Lf,f\rangle\geq 0
for all
f\in\mathcal{D}(L)
. Then the spectrum of
L
, denoted by
\sigma(L)
, is nonempty and non-negative:
\sigma(L)\subset[0,\infty)
;
c.f.

[
14
, Proposition 5.12]
.
We define

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\sigma(L),\\
\lambda_{1}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf\left\{\sigma(L)\backslash\{\lambda_{0}\}\right\},\end{split}

which are crucial quantities that appear in the following assumption.

{assumption}

For the operator
L
we have that
\nu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}>0
, that is,
L
possesses a spectral gap. Additionally, we assume that the eigenspace corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
is
1
-dimensional.

Note that
under the assumption
\nu>0
,
\lambda_{0}
is indeed an eigenvalue of
L
(see, e.g.
[
20
, Theorem 4.1]
), which we assume to have a 1-dimensional eigenspace. Therefore, we have
L\varphi=\lambda_{0}\varphi
, where
\varphi\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})
denotes an eigenfunction of
L
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
.
By
[
34
, Theorem XIII.1]
we in fact have
the following variational representations of
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
:

\begin{split}\lambda_{0}&=\inf_{f\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\langle f,Lf\rangle,\\
\lambda_{1}&=\sup_{\mathbb{V}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\inf_{f\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\cap\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\langle f,Lf\rangle,\end{split}

(7)

where
\mathcal{V}_{1}
denotes the set of
1
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
.
Note that
\lambda_{1}
is not necessarily an eigenvalue of
L
.

Fix a bounded self-adjoint linear operator
H\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
, which serves as a “small” perturbation. With this we set

\widehat{L}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=L+H.

Note that we have
\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L})=\mathcal{D}(L)
,
self-adjointness of
\widehat{L}
as well as

\langle\widehat{L}f,f\rangle\geq-\|H\|
for any
f\in\mathcal{D}(L)
, with
\|H\|
being the operator norm of
H
.
Define

\begin{split}\widehat{\lambda}_{0}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\inf_{f\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\langle f,\widehat{L}f\rangle,\\
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{\mathbb{V}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\inf_{f\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\cap\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\langle f,\widehat{L}f\rangle.\end{split}

(8)

The following lemma is a classical result; see e.g.
[
34
, Section XIII.15]
. For completeness we give a proof in Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 2.1

(Weyl’s lemma)

For
j=0,1
, we have

|\lambda_{j}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}|\leq\|H\|.

(9)

In the next assumption we formalize the notion that
H
is a “small” perturbation.

{assumption}

Suppose that the operator norm of
H
satisfies
\|H\|<\nu/2
, where
\nu=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}
is the spectral gap; see Assumption
2.1
.

Under the formulated assumptions we have two important consequences which follow from fairly standard functional analytic techniques; see Appendix
A.3
for a full proof.

Lemma 2.2

Under Assumption
2.1
and
2.1
we have that

\widehat{\lambda}_{0}<\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
, and that
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
is an eigenvalue of
\widehat{L}
. Furthermore, the perturbed operator
\widehat{L}
possesses a unique eigenfunction, up to a sign,
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})
w.r.t. eigenvalue
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
, that is,

\widehat{L}\widehat{\varphi}=\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\widehat{\varphi}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\varphi,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle\geq 0
.

Remark 2.3

A consequence of the previous results is that

\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=\langle\widehat{\varphi},\widehat{L}\widehat{\varphi}\rangle=\inf_{f\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L})}\langle f,\widehat{L}f\rangle,

(10)

and the supremum in the definition of
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
in equation (
8
) is attained by
\mathbb{V}=\mathrm{span}\{\widehat{\varphi}\}
.

2.2
Main estimate

The goal now is to estimate

\|\varphi-\widehat{\varphi}\|
.
Now we state and prove the main estimate.

Theorem 2.4

Under Assumption
2.1
and
2.1
we have

\|\varphi-\widehat{\varphi}\|\leq\frac{\|H\varphi\|}{\nu-2\|H\|}.

(11)

Proof 2.5

Recall that
\rho\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\widehat{\varphi},\varphi\rangle\geq 0
and with
\mathbb{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathrm{span}\{\varphi\}
define

h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{P_{\mathbb{G}^{\perp}}\widehat{\varphi}}{\|P_{\mathbb{G}^{\perp}}\widehat{\varphi}\|}=(1-\rho^{2})^{-1/2}(\widehat{\varphi}-\rho\,\varphi).

(If
\rho=1
, then
\widehat{\varphi}=\varphi
and the result trivially holds true.) Note that
\|h\|=1

and
h\perp\varphi
as well as
Lh\perp\varphi
by the self-adjointness of
L
.
Consequently, by using the representation of
h
,
L\varphi=\lambda_{0}\varphi
and
\widehat{L}=L+H
we have that

\displaystyle\rho\lambda_{0}\varphi+\rho H\varphi+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\widehat{L}h=\rho\widehat{L}\varphi+\widehat{L}(\widehat{\varphi}-\rho\varphi)=\widehat{L}\widehat{\varphi}=\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\widehat{\varphi}.

Taking the inner product with
h
, employing
\langle\widehat{\varphi},h\rangle=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}
,
\varphi\perp h
and rearranging yields

-\rho\langle H\varphi,h\rangle=\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\left(\langle h,\widehat{L}h\rangle-\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\right).

(12)

Moreover, by
\widehat{L}=L+H
,
h\perp\varphi
and Lemma
2.1
we have

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle h,\widehat{L}h\rangle-\widehat{\lambda}_{0}=\langle h,Lh\rangle+\langle h,Hh\rangle-\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}+\langle h,Hh\rangle-\widehat{\lambda}_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}-\|H\|-\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\geq\lambda_{1}-2\|H\|-\lambda_{0}=\nu-2\|H\|,

which is, due to Assumption
2.1
, strictly positive.
Squaring (
12
), rearranging, and taking a square root, with
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\langle H\varphi,h\rangle|
we obtain

\rho=\frac{B}{\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}}.

Then

1-\rho=\frac{\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}-B}{\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}}=\frac{A^{2}}{(\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}+B)\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}}\leq\frac{A^{2}}{2B^{2}},

from which we have that
\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\leq A/B
.
The fact that we can represent
\widehat{\varphi}
by
the orthogonal decomposition

\widehat{\varphi}=P_{\mathbb{G}}\widehat{\varphi}+P_{\mathbb{G}^{\perp}}\widehat{\varphi}=\rho\,\varphi+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}h

gives, with
\rho=\langle\widehat{\varphi},\varphi\rangle\geq 0
, that

\widehat{\varphi}-\varphi=(\rho-1)\varphi+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}h.

Consequently,

\|\widehat{\varphi}-\varphi\|=\sqrt{2(1-\rho)}\leq\frac{A}{B}\leq\frac{\|H\varphi\|}{\nu-2\|H\|},

which finishes the proof.

Remark 2.6

A similar, and to some extent complementary, bound to (
11
) can be obtained via the sin
\theta
theorem of
[
10
]
, which is stated in Section
3.1
.
Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.1
, we obtain

\|\varphi-\widehat{\varphi}\|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\|H\varphi\|}{\nu}.

(13)

For the convenience of the reader we provide in Appendix
A.4
arguments to derive (

).

2.3
Additional auxiliary estimates

We now give some additional estimates which are useful for our subsequent examples.
Let
\pi,\widetilde{\pi}
be probability density functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Fix a positive function
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
, and let
\Gamma(\dif x)=\gamma(x)\dif x
be the corresponding measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
we denote the space of
\Gamma
-square-integrable functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2}\,\Gamma(\dif x)\right)^{1/2}
. Now define the functions

\phi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\pi(x)}{\gamma(x)},\qquad\widetilde{\phi}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(x)}{\gamma(x)},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We assume that
\phi,\widetilde{\phi}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Gamma)
: we have that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\pi(x)^{2}}{\gamma(x)}\dif x<\infty,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\widetilde{\pi}(x)^{2}}{\gamma(x)}\dif x<\infty.

(14)

Under this condition we state the first auxiliary estimate.

Lemma 2.7

Assume that (
14
) holds, and furthermore that
\Gamma
is a finite measure, i.e.,
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1/2}<\infty
.
Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\pi(x)-\widetilde{\pi}(x)|\dif x\leq\Lambda\|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\|_{2}.

Proof 2.8

We straightforwardly calculate

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\pi(x)-\widetilde{\pi}(x)|\dif x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}|\dif\Gamma\\
&\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}|^{2}\dif\Gamma\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dif\Gamma\right)^{1/2}=\Lambda\|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\|_{2},\end{split}

where we used Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

For the formulation of the next statement we need normalized functions
\phi
and
\widetilde{\phi}
w.r.t. the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
-norm, that is, we define

\varphi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\phi(x)}{Z},\qquad\widetilde{\varphi}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\widetilde{\phi}(x)}{\widetilde{Z}},

with
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\phi\|_{2},\widetilde{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\widetilde{\phi}\|_{2}
.

Lemma 2.9

Suppose that
(
14
) holds and
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1/2}<\infty
. Additionally, let

\|\varphi-\widetilde{\varphi}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon,

(15)

with
0<\epsilon<\Lambda^{-1}Z^{-1}
be satisfied.
Then, we have

\left\|\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\,\right\|_{2}\leq Z\epsilon,

(16)

and

|Z-\widetilde{Z}|\leq C\epsilon,

(17)

with
C=Z^{2}\Lambda/(1-\Lambda Z\epsilon)
.

Proof 2.10

Note that (
15
) immediately implies (
16
).
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\right)\dif\Gamma\right|\leq\int\left|\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\,\right|\dif\Gamma

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\,\right|^{2}\dif\Gamma\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\dif\Gamma\right)^{1/2}\leq\Lambda Z\epsilon.

We can then conclude (
17
), for instance by considering the behaviour of the function
t\mapsto h(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z/t
. Namely, the former inequality implies

h(Z)-\Lambda Z\varepsilon\leq h(\widetilde{Z})\leq h(Z)+\Lambda Z\varepsilon.

Now, taking the inverse and performing suitable transformations yields the result.

Lemma 2.11

Assume that (
14
) and (
15
) hold as well as that
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1/2}<\infty
. Then, we have

\|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\|_{2}\leq\epsilon(Z+C),

where
C>0
is defined as in Lemma
2.9
.

Proof 2.12

We calculate

\begin{split}\|\phi-\widetilde{\phi}\|_{2}&\leq\left\|\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\,\right\|_{2}+\left\|\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}-\widetilde{\phi}\,\right\|_{2}=\left\|\phi-\frac{Z}{\widetilde{Z}}\,\widetilde{\phi}\,\right\|_{2}+|Z-\widetilde{Z}|\leq Z\epsilon+C\epsilon,\end{split}

where we have used the triangle inequality and Lemma
2.9
.

Putting together the estimates of the former lemmas we obtain the following.

Proposition 2.13

Assume that (
14
), (
15
) hold, and that
\Gamma
is a finite measure, with
\Lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Gamma(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{1/2}<\infty
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\pi(x)-\widetilde{\pi}(x)|\dif x\leq\Lambda(Z+C)\epsilon,

where
C>0
is defined in Lemma
2.9
.

2.4
Example: logistic regression

We demonstrate how Theorem
11
, in combination with Proposition
2.13
, can be applied to the setting of logistic regression, which is currently the most popular application of QSMC methods, see
[
33
,
21
]
. In that scenario we justify that QSMC methods are robust to small perturbations in the observations, such as could arise from corrupted or missing data.

We consider the following model: There are

n
observed binary response variables
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, and we have a design matrix of covariates
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{X}_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, and write
\mathbf{X}_{[i]}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the
i
th row,
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. We consider the response variables and design matrix to be fixed
a priori
.
For a parameter vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(interpreted as regression coefficients) the likelihood function for the response variables is given as

\ell(x\mid y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(x)^{y_{i}}\cdot(1-p_{i}(x))^{1-y_{i}},

with

p_{i}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\mathbf{X}_{[i]}^{\top}x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Following
[
33
,
21
]
, we place an improper uniform prior on the parameters
x
, and obtain the Lebesgue density
\pi
of the posterior distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which satisfies

\pi(x)\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(x)^{y_{i}}\cdot(1-p_{i}(x))^{1-y_{i}}.

(18)

We assume that the response and design matrix are such that (
18
) defines a
bona fide
probability density function (that is, the right-hand side of (
18
) is Lebesgue integrable).

For our stochastic process, we are using a general reversible diffusion of the form (
3
), namely

\dif X_{t}=\nabla A(X_{t})\dif t+\dif W_{t},

where the drift
A
is chosen independently of the data. We assume that the relevant technical conditions on
A
detailed in
[
43
]
hold, to ensure that
\pi
is a valid quasi-stationary distribution, for details see also Section
1.3
. As in
[
43
]
, we work on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
, where
\Gamma(\dif x)=\gamma(x)\dif x
with
\gamma(x)=\exp(2A(x))
.

Note that the observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
and covariates
\mathbf{X}
only influence the killed diffusion through the killing rate
\kappa
and so any perturbation of the data results in a perturbation of the killing rate.

From (
6
) and following the calculations in
[
21
, Appendix C]
, we can express the killing rate as

\begin{split}\kappa(x)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-p_{i}(x)\right)\mathbf{X}_{i,j}\right]^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}(x)\left(1-p_{i}(x)\right)\mathbf{X}^{2}_{i,j}\\
-2\Delta A-2\left\langle\nabla A,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-p_{i}(x)\right){\mathbf{X}_{[i]}}\right\rangle\bigg)-\Phi,\end{split}

(19)

where the constant
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen to ensure that
\kappa\geq 0
everywhere; see Condition (1) of
[
33
]
for further discussion.
Let
L^{\kappa}
be the generator of the diffusion
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
killed at rate
\kappa
, which possesses
\varphi=\pi/\gamma
as its minimal eigenfunction corresponding to the bottom of the spectrum.
The fact that the eigenspace is one-dimensional follows from the Perron–Frobenius theorem,
[
34
, Chapter XIII.12]
.
Let us write
\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}
for the bottom of the spectrum of
L^{\kappa}
.
Then in fact it follows that
\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}=-\Phi
; see
[
43
, Section 3.3]
; that is, the bottom of the spectrum of the resulting operator is in fact equal to
-\Phi
.

We first consider the common choice of
A\equiv 0
, as in
[
33
,
21
]
. In this case, the expression (
19
) simplifies, and the resulting killing rate
\kappa
is uniformly bounded from above.
Moreover, since we are assuming that
\pi
is integrable, it follows that

\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\kappa(x)>\lambda_{0}^{\kappa},

(20)

with
|\cdot|
denoting the Euclidean norm.

As already mentioned we imagine having corrupted or missing data, giving us access only to a perturbed killing rate
\widetilde{\kappa}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
.

Remark 2.14

For example, it may be the case our covariates are corrupted, so we only have access to
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{i,j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{X}_{i,j}+\epsilon_{i,j}
, where the
(\epsilon_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
models some corrupting noise (alternatively, the case
\epsilon_{i,j}=-\mathbf{X}_{i,j}
could be considered a case of missing data).
Assume that the corresponding additive constants are equal,
\Phi=\widetilde{\Phi}
(which can be done by setting both equal to the maximum value
\Phi\vee\widetilde{\Phi}
.)
If we write
\|\mathbf{A}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{1\leq i\leq n,\;1\leq j\leq d}|\mathbf{A}_{i,j}|
for a matrix
\mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{A}_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, a straightforward calculation shows that for a fixed constant
C>0
we have

\|\widetilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}\leq Cn^{2}d\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\infty}(\|\epsilon\|_{\infty}^{2}+\|\epsilon\|_{\infty}).

Thus provided the data corruption level
\|\epsilon\|_{\infty}
is sufficiently small,
\|\widetilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}
can be made arbitrarily small.

Now we ask whether the difference of the eigenfunctions that determine the quasi-stationary distributions of the killed Markov processes based on
\kappa
and
\widetilde{\kappa}
is small when
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}
is sufficiently small.
Thus, we consider a perturbation operator
H\colon\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)\to\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
given by

Hf\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\widetilde{\kappa}-\kappa)f,\quad f\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma).

Note that, since
H
is just a multiplication operator, it is self-adjoint, and the operator norm coincides with
\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}
. We obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.15

We work in the case
A\equiv 0
and assume that
\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\kappa(x)
exists. Then there exists a
\delta>0
and constant
C>0
such that for any (perturbed) killing rate
\widetilde{\kappa}
with
\|\kappa-\tilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}<\delta
, there is a quasi-stationary distribution of the killed Markov process killed at rate
\widetilde{\kappa}
. In particular, this quasi-stationary distribution has a
\Gamma
-density
\widehat{\varphi}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)

with
\|\widehat{\varphi}\|_{2}=1
, which satisfies

\|\varphi-\widehat{\varphi}\|_{2}\leq C\|\tilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}.

Proof 2.16

We start with arguing that the generator
L^{\kappa}
has a spectral gap
\nu>0
. This fact follows readily by (
20
) and the fact that the base of the essential spectrum must be strictly larger than
\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}
by
[
9
, Lemma 14.4.1]
(see also
[
19
, Lemma 3.3(v)]
,
[
43
, Remark 1, Section 1.4]
.
We choose
\delta
to be

\delta=\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\nu,\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\kappa(x)-\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}\right\}.

Let
\|H\|
be the
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
-operator norm. Then, we have

\|H\|=\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{2}\left(\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\kappa(x)-\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}\right).

The latter inequality
ensures that the resulting minimal eigenfunction
\widehat{\varphi}
is also in
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
, by
[
7
, Theorem 4.5]
. The fact that the quasi-stationary distribution corresponds to the bottom of the spectrum follows from the argument of
[
39
]
; see also
[
19
, Section 4.2]
. The rest follows by applying our abstract result, Theorem
11
.

Remark 2.17

The bound in Proposition
2.15
is in terms of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
of
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
and concerns the functions
\varphi,\widehat{\varphi}
which are normalized in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
, that is,
\|{\varphi}\|_{2}=\|\widehat{\varphi}\|_{2}=1
. This cannot be straightforwardly translated into a bound on the corresponding densities
\pi,\widehat{\pi}
, which are normalised in
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
, since in this case
\Gamma(\dif x)=\dif x
is not a finite measure.

In light of the above remark, it is of interest to ask whether or not it is possible to construct an alternative diffusion to the case
A\equiv 0
, which still possesses
\pi
as a valid quasi-stationary distribution, but such that
\Gamma
is a finite measure. This would enable us, by an application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, to conclude robustness in
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
and not only in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
. This can be indeed done, by choosing
A
to satisfy the following properties.

{assumption}

The function
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function, and there exists some
M>0,\alpha>0
such that
A(x)=-\alpha|x|
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|x|\geq M
, and
\pi/\exp(2A)
is uniformly bounded from above.

This is largely a technical assumption for the following proof, although in addition, the resulting diffusion process with drift
\nabla A
will also be relatively straightforward to simulate in the tails due to its simple form.
However, such an
A
exists since
\pi
is a log-concave density function.
With this choice of
A
, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.18

Assume that the function
A\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies Assumption
2.4
and that
\lim_{\|x\|\to\infty}\kappa(x)
exists. Then, there exists a
\delta>0
and
C>0
, such that for any perturbed killing rate
\widetilde{\kappa}
that satisfies
\|\widetilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}<\delta
, there is a quasi-stationary distribution
\widehat{\pi}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
of the killed Markov process based on
\widetilde{\kappa}
. In particular, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\pi(x)-\widehat{\pi}(x)|\dif x\leq C\|\tilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}.

(21)

Proof 2.19

Note that by Assumption
2.4
we have that the killing rate
\kappa
from (
19
), which appears in the unperturbed killed Markov process, is bounded from above. Moreover, (
20
)
is satisfied and therefore there exists similarly by
[
9
, Lemma 14.4.1]
a spectral gap
\nu>0
for the generator
L^{\kappa}
. As in the proof of Proposition
2.15
it follows that

\|\varphi-\widehat{\varphi}\|_{2}\leq\widetilde{C}\|\tilde{\kappa}-\kappa\|_{\infty}

for the corresponding
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
-normalized eigenfunctions for some
\widetilde{C}>0
.
The desired
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
bound (
21
) follows since
\Gamma
is assumed to be a finite measure. Therefore, we can apply Proposition
2.13
and obtain the assertion.

3
Unbounded perturbation: truncation

In this section we consider a particular unbounded perturbation corresponding to
truncating
the killing rate. This is of practical importance, since for all current implementations of QSMC methods, when using either particles
[
33
,
11
,
6
]
or stochastic approximation
[
21
,
25
,
45
,
1
]
, it is necessary to simulate the killing times (
4
). This simulation is an intricate task when
\kappa
is unbounded; see
[
33
]
. On the other hand, when
\kappa
is uniformly bounded from above, simulation of such killing times is straightforward using Poisson thinning; see
[
24
]
.

The simulation of
piecewise-deterministic Markov processes
in the context of Monte Carlo
[
4
,
5
]
also requires the simulation of first event times. In that context, stability investigations with respect to truncation of the event rate have also appeared, in
[
12
, Section 10]
.

3.1
Projection notation

Following
[
10
]
, we make use of projections onto invariant subspaces. Now we describe the appropriate setting and notation, which will be used throughout Section
3
.

For an unbounded self-adjoint operator
(L,\mathcal{D}(L))
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we suppose that we have a one-dimensional
L
-invariant subspace, given by its orthogonal projection
P=v_{0}\langle\cdot,v_{0}\rangle
, for some
v_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|v_{0}\|=1
. In fact
v_{0}
must be an eigenvector of
L
, with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, say.
Let

P^{\perp}

denote the orthogonal projection onto the complement subspace
(\mathrm{span}\{v_{0}\})^{\perp}
.

Following
[
10
]
, we can define isometric mappings
E_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{K}(E_{0})\to\mathcal{H}
and
E_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{K}(E_{1})\to\mathcal{H}
, with source Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{K}(E_{0}),\mathcal{K}(E_{1})
, which satisfy

E_{0}E_{0}^{*}=P,\quad E_{1}E_{1}^{*}=P^{\perp},

where
E_{0}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}(E_{0})
and
E_{1}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}(E_{1})
are the corresponding adjoint operators.

Since
P
projects onto the one-dimensional subspace
\mathrm{span}\{v_{0}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
we can
identify
\mathcal{K}(E_{0})\equiv\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the Euclidean inner product. This allows the explicit characterization

\begin{split}E_{0}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H},\quad\theta\mapsto\theta v_{0},\\
E_{0}^{*}&\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R},\quad v\mapsto\langle v,v_{0}\rangle.\end{split}

By the
L
-invariance of
\mathrm{span}\{v_{0}\}
, we can now decompose
L\colon\mathcal{D}(L)\to\mathcal{H}
as

Lv=\begin{pmatrix}E_{0}&E_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}L_{0}&0\\
0&L_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}E_{0}^{*}v\\
E_{1}^{*}v\end{pmatrix},

for
v\in\mathcal{D}(L)
, where
L_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the bounded linear operator given by
L_{0}\theta=\lambda_{0}\theta
, and
L_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{D}(L_{1})\to\mathcal{K}(E_{1})
is the unbounded operator given by
L_{1}=E_{1}^{*}LE_{1}
with
\mathcal{D}(L_{1})=E_{1}^{*}\mathcal{D}(L)
.

Given another self-adjoint operator
(\widehat{L},\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L}))
on the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, which we will later think of as the
perturbed operator
, we assume that we have a one-dimensional
\widehat{L}
-invariant subspace given by the orthogonal projection
Q=\widehat{v}_{0}\langle\cdot,\widehat{v}_{0}\rangle
, where
\|\widehat{v}_{0}\|=1
. So
\widehat{v}_{0}
is an eigenvector of
\widehat{L}
, with corresponding eigenvalue
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, say. Let us write
Q^{\perp}
for the projection onto the orthogonal complement of
\mathrm{span}\{\widehat{v}_{0}\}
.

As above we can similarly define corresponding isometric mappings
F_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathcal{H}
and
F_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{K}(F_{1})\to\mathcal{H}
with
F_{0}\theta=\theta\widehat{v}_{0}
and
F_{0}^{*}v=\langle v,\widehat{v}_{0}\rangle
, such that
F_{0}F_{0}^{*}=Q
and
F_{1}F_{1}^{*}=Q^{\perp}
.
Moreover, with these definitions,

\widehat{L}\colon\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L})\to\mathcal{H}
can be decomposed for

v\in\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L})
as

\widehat{L}v=\begin{pmatrix}F_{0}&F_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{L}_{0}&0\\
0&\widehat{L}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}F_{0}^{*}v\\
F_{1}^{*}v\end{pmatrix},

(22)

where
\widehat{L}_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
\widehat{L}_{0}\theta=\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\theta
and
\widehat{L}_{1}\colon\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L}_{1})\to\mathcal{K}(F_{1})
is the unbounded operator given by
\widehat{L}_{1}=F_{1}^{*}\widehat{L}F_{1}
with
\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L}_{1})=F_{1}^{*}\mathcal{D}(\widehat{L})
.

With these definitions we are able to formulate the remarkable sin
\theta
theorem of
[
10
]
. For convenience of the reader, we state it here in our present notation.

Theorem 3.1

(The sin
\theta
theorem,
[
10
]
)

Suppose there is some real interval
[a_{0},a_{1}]
and
\delta>0
, such that the spectrum of
L_{0}
is contained within
[a_{0},a_{1}]
, and the spectrum of
\widehat{L}_{1}

lies
entirely outside the interval
(a_{0}-\delta,a_{1}+\delta)
. Then for
\sin\Theta_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{1-\langle v_{0},\widehat{v}_{0}\rangle^{2}}
and operator
R\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widehat{L}E_{0}-E_{0}L_{0}
, we have
|\sin\Theta_{0}|\leq\|R\|/\delta
.

In fact, this is a slight modification of the
\sin\,\theta
theorem as stated in
[
10
]
, since our operators
L,\widehat{L}
are in general unbounded; but see the discussion immediately preceding
[
10
, Theorem 5.2]
. These subtleties will be addressed in the sequel.

3.2
Further notation and results

We consider the situation described in Section
1.3
and set
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\Gamma)
, which consists of all measurable real-valued functions
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

satisfying

\|f\|_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)^{2}\dif\Gamma(y)\right)^{1/2}<\infty,

where the measure
\Gamma
is given by
\dif\Gamma(y)=\gamma(y)\dif y
and
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp(2A)
with
\dif y
denoting the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words
\mathcal{H}
is
the Hilbert space of functions, equipped with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)g(y)\dif\Gamma(y),

where
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. As before by
\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})
we denote the unit sphere in
\mathcal{H}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

The infinitesimal generator of the diffusion
X
killed at rate
\kappa
, see (
3
), is given by

L^{\kappa}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta-\nabla A\cdot\nabla+\kappa,

(23)

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplacian operator. As in
[
42
, Section 3.3.3]
, the operator
L^{\kappa}
can be realised as a densely-defined self-adjoint positive semibounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
with domain

\mathcal{D}(L^{\kappa})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta f-\nabla A\cdot\nabla f+\kappa f\in\mathcal{H}\right\},

(24)

where the derivatives are understood in the weak sense. Note that
\mathcal{D}(L^{\kappa})
contains
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the set of smooth, compactly supported functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Therefore we have a self-adjoint unperturbed operator on
\mathcal{H}
given by (
23
)
with domain
\mathcal{D}(L^{\kappa})
.

For brevity we write
L^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta-\nabla A\cdot\nabla
for the (formal) unkilled differential operator.
We impose the following condition on the killing rate.

{assumption}

The killing rate is measurable, locally bounded and nonnegative with
\kappa(x)\to\infty
as
|x|\to\infty
.

In the case where
A\equiv 0
and
\kappa
is given by (
6
), i.e., in the setting of QSMC with Brownian motion, Assumption
3.2
corresponds to a scenario where
\pi
has tails decaying at a faster-than-exponential rate (such as Gaussian or lighter).
A standard consequence of the assumption is the following:

Lemma 3.2

Under Assumption
3.2
, there is a strictly positive eigenfunction
\varphi_{0}\in\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})
of
L^{\kappa}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}
satisfying

\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}=\inf\sigma(L^{\kappa})
, i.e., it is given by the bottom of the spectrum of
L^{\kappa}
. Moreover,
\varphi_{0}
corresponds to the unique quasi-stationary distribution of
X
.

Proof 3.3

3.3
Example: unbounded case

We now illustrate our former truncation result on a Gaussian example. In this setting, many relevant quantities are analytically available, so we can carefully isolate the effect of the truncation. We consider a similar example to
[
43
]
, namely an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) diffusion targeting a Gaussian quasi-stationary distribution. We will see that in any dimension

M

\exp(-M)
up to some (dimension-dependent) multiplicative constant. In the univariate setting
d=1
, we will be able to explicitly compute this constant. Thus truncating the killing rate in this example appears to be robust with respect to dimension, in that the error decays exponentially for any dimension.

Throughout, we write
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
for the univariate Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance

\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)

\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

denotes the identity matrix on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

We take the diffusion to be a multivariate OU process on

(30)

[

]

\pi
is the quasi-stationary distribution of the killed Markov
process based on

, see also Section

.
We are now interested to derive quantitative bounds on the effect of truncating this divergent killing rate at a level

]
, we can explicitly write down the form of the eigenfunctions in the tails. Define

K_{\beta}^{\pm}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\beta-1}\exp(-r^{2}\pm\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the solution of (
34
) when
|x|>\sqrt{M}
is

\varphi(x)=\varphi^{(\lambda)}(x)=\begin{cases}c_{1}K_{2(M-\lambda)}^{+}(x),\quad x<-\sqrt{M},\\
c_{1}K_{2(M-\lambda)}^{-}(x),\quad x>\sqrt{M},\end{cases}

(35)

since the eigenfunction must be square integrable. Note that here we have a common constant
c_{1}>0
by symmetry.
Now, following
[
29
]
, we substitute

q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi,\quad p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\varphi^{\prime},

and change to polar coordinates: for suitable functions
\rho\colon\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
and
\alpha\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

q=\rho\cos\alpha,\quad p=\rho\sin\alpha.

With this substitution, we can modify (
34
) to the following equations,

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}\cos\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}\rho\sin\alpha

\displaystyle=\rho\sin\alpha,

(36)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}\sin\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\rho\cos\alpha

\displaystyle=2(\kappa_{M}-\lambda)\rho\cos\alpha+x\rho\sin\alpha.

(37)

Note that in contrast to
[
29
, (17)]
we require the additional final term in (
37
).

Then by multiplying (
36
) by
-\sin\alpha
and adding it to
\cos\alpha
times (
37
), we obtain a first-order differential equation for
\alpha
, given by

\alpha^{\prime}=2(\kappa_{M}-\lambda)\cos^{2}\alpha-\sin^{2}\alpha+x\sin\alpha\cos\alpha.

(38)

Noting that

\frac{\dif}{\dif x}K_{\beta}^{\pm}(x)=\pm\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\beta}\exp(-r^{2}\pm\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r,

we can compute that in the tails,

\tan\alpha(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\beta^{*}}\exp(-r^{2}+\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r}{\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{{\beta^{*}}-1}\exp(-r^{2}+\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r},\quad x\leq-\sqrt{M},\\
\frac{-\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\beta^{*}}\exp(-r^{2}-\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r}{\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{{\beta^{*}}-1}\exp(-r^{2}-\sqrt{2}rx)\dif r},\quad x\geq\sqrt{M},\end{cases}

(39)

with
{\beta^{*}}=2(M-\lambda)
.

Since we know the evolution of
\alpha(x)
is given by (
38
), and we have boundary values given by (
39
), in order for
\lambda
to be a valid eigenvalue, we require the following compatibility condition, analogously to
[
29
]
: For some integer
n
,

\alpha(\sqrt{M})=\alpha(-\sqrt{M})+\int_{-\sqrt{M}}^{\sqrt{M}}\alpha^{\prime}(x)\dif x+n\pi,

where
\alpha^{\prime}
is defined by (
38
), and
\alpha(\pm\sqrt{M})
are defined by (
39
).

Thus, again following the approach of
[
29
]
, we define

s_{M}(\lambda)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha(\sqrt{M})-\alpha(-\sqrt{M})-\int_{-\sqrt{M}}^{\sqrt{M}}\alpha^{\prime}(x)\dif x,

(40)

where the right-hand side can be computed numerically to arbitrary accuracy using a numerical ODE solver. Thus we need to find values of
\lambda
such that

s_{M}(\lambda)=n\pi,

(41)

for integers
n
. The corresponding values of
\lambda
for which (
41
) hold are precisely the eigenvalues for the truncated operator with truncation level
M
.
We have plotted the function
\lambda\mapsto s_{M}(\lambda)
in the cases
M=2,10,40
in Figure
1
.

Here
M=2
with numerically calculated
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}^{M}\approx 0.4879
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}^{M}\approx 1.8501
. This indicates that we may apply Theorem
3.24
with
\delta=1.3
.

Here
M=10
with numerically calculated
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}^{M}\approx 0.4999
and
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}^{M}\approx 1.9984
. This indicates that we may apply Theorem
3.24
with
\delta=1.4
.

Here
M=40
numerically calculated
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}^{M}\approx 0.49999
and

. This indicates that we may apply Theorem

with

Thus we can utilize Theorem
3.24
to derive the following bounds in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\Gamma)
:

Lemma 3.26

For our Ornstein–Uhlenbeck diffusion (
30
) with quasi-stationary distribution
\pi=\mathcal{N}(0,1/2)
, for any
M\geq 2
, there exists a perturbed quasi-stationary distribution
\pi_{M}
, and writing
\varphi_{0},\widehat{\varphi}_{0,M}
for the corresponding normalized (in
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\Gamma)
) eigenfunctions, we have

\|\varphi_{0}-\widehat{\varphi}_{0,M}\|_{2}\leq c_{2}\exp(-M/2),

with
c_{2}=\frac{\sqrt{3}\cdot 35^{1/4}}{2\delta}
.

Note that we do not prove Assumption
3.2
, but rather, as detailed above, we use the numerical method of
[
29
]
to verify that indeed for this example
\delta>0
as required. For computation of its value, see Remark
3.30
.

Proof 3.27

From Theorem
3.24
, we only need to compute
\|H_{M}E_{0}\|=\|H_{M}\varphi_{0}\|_{2}
. With
C=\left(\frac{3}{2\pi}\right)^{1/4}
we obtain

\displaystyle\|H_{M}\varphi_{0}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=C^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\kappa(x)-M)_{+}^{2}\exp(-x^{2})\dif\Gamma(x)

\displaystyle=2C^{2}\int_{\sqrt{M}}^{\infty}(x^{2}-M)^{2}\exp(-3x^{2}/2)\dif x

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{8}\exp(-x^{2})\dif x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\sqrt{M}}^{\infty}\exp(-2x^{2})\dif x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}(105\sqrt{\pi}/16)^{1/2}\left(\sqrt{\pi/2}\exp(-2M)\right)^{1/2},

where we have used Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, the fact that for
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
the eighth moment is
105\sigma^{8}
, and standard Gaussian tail bounds; see e.g.
[
41
, Proposition 2.5]
. Tidying up the constants, the lemma is proven.

We now translate our previous
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R},\Gamma)
estimate into a bound on the corresponding (normalized) quasi-stationary distributions in
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R},\Gamma)
.

Proposition 3.28

In the same setting as Lemma
3.26
we obtain
for any
M>2\log\left(2(2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{1}\right)\approx 5.58
,
that there exists a perturbed quasi-stationary distribution
\pi_{M}
, and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\pi(x)-\pi_{M}(x)|\dif x\leq c_{3}\exp(-M/2),

(42)

for
c_{3}\approx 5.49

defined explicitly in (
44
).

Proof 3.29

We first relate
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\pi(x)-\pi_{M}(x)|\dif x
to our previous bound.
With
\gamma(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp(-x^{2}/2)
let us write
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi/\gamma
,
\widehat{\phi}_{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\pi_{M}/\gamma
for the corresponding normalised densities with respect to
\Gamma
. Thus we have
\varphi_{0}=\phi/Z
and
\widehat{\varphi}_{0,M}=\widehat{\phi}_{M}/Z_{M}
, where the normalizing constants are given by
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\phi\|_{2}=(\frac{2}{3\pi})^{1/4}
and
Z_{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\|\widehat{\phi}_{M}\|_{2}
.

Thus, with
(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dif\Gamma)^{1/2}=(2\pi)^{1/4}
, we have from Lemma
2.7
that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\pi(x)-\pi_{M}(x)|\dif x\leq(2\pi)^{1/4}\|\phi-\widehat{\phi}_{M}\|_{2}.

From Lemma
3.26
, we obtain

\left\|\phi-\frac{Z\widehat{\phi}_{M}}{Z_{M}}\right\|_{2}\leq Zc_{2}\exp(-M/2).

(43)

By
Lemma
2.9
this gives

|Z_{M}-Z|<(2\pi)^{1/4}Z^{2}c_{2}\frac{\exp(-M/2)}{1-(2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{2}\exp(-M/2)},

provided that
M>2\log((2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{2})
.
By using Lemma
2.11

we also have

\displaystyle\|\phi-\widehat{\phi}_{M}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-M/2)\left(Zc_{2}+\frac{(2\pi)^{1/4}Z^{2}c_{2}}{1-(2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{2}\exp(-M/2)}\right).

Furthermore, if
M>2\log\left(2(2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{2}\right)
, the final term of the former bracket expression is bounded from above by
2(2\pi)^{1/4}Z^{2}c_{2}
. Putting the pieces together, for all
M>2\log\left(2(2\pi)^{1/4}Zc_{2}\right)
, by applying Proposition
2.13
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\pi-\pi_{M}|\dif x\leq c_{3}\exp(-M/2),

with

c_{3}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Zc_{2}(2\pi)^{1/4}\left(1+{2Z(2\pi)^{1/4}}\right).

(44)

Proposition 3.31

In the multivariate setting

\delta

\lambda_{0}^{\kappa}

\widehat{\lambda}_{1}^{M}

A.1
Proof of Proposition
1.1

Let
G(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-\kappa(y)
and
\widetilde{G}(y)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-\widetilde{\kappa}(y)
for all
y\in S_{0}
. Furthermore let
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a Markov chain on
S_{0}
with transition kernel
Q
starting at
x_{0}
. Then, for arbitrary

A\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
with
y_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=x
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A)

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{0}}\dots\int_{S_{0}}\mathbf{1}_{A}(y_{n})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(y_{j})Q(y_{n-1},{\rm d}y_{n})\dots Q(y_{1},{\rm d}y_{2})Q(y_{0},{\rm d}y_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}(Y_{n})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})\right],

and by the same arguments
\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}(Y_{n})\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right]
. In addition to that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A\mid X_{n}\in S_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A\mid\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}\right|+\left|\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}+\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}.

By the same arguments, just by adding and subtracting
\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})}
instead of
\frac{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A)}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})}
before the first inequality of the previous estimation, one obtains

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A\mid X_{n}\in S_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A\mid\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\right|\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|}{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})},

such that

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A\mid X_{n}\in S_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A\mid\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\right|\leq\frac{2\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|}{\max\{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0}),\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})\}}.

(45)

By induction over
n
one can verify for any
z_{0},\dots,z_{n-1}\in S_{0}
that

\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(z_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(z_{j})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}G(z_{j})(G(z_{i})-\widetilde{G}(z_{i}))\prod_{m=i+1}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(z_{m})\right].

Using this we get

\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}G(Y_{j})-\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{j})\right|\leq\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}G(Y_{j})\prod_{m=i+1}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{m})\right|

(46)

and eventually

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}}\left|\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}G(Y_{j})\prod_{m=i+1}^{n-1}\widetilde{G}(Y_{m})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G(x_{0})\underbrace{\int_{S_{0}}\dots\int_{S_{0}}}_{i-1\text{\,int.}}\int_{S_{0}}\underbrace{\int_{S_{0}}\dots\int_{S_{0}}}_{n-i-1\text{\,int.}}\widetilde{G}(y_{n-1})Q(y_{n-2},{\rm d}y_{n-1})\,\cdots\,\widetilde{G}(y_{i+1})Q(y_{i},{\rm d}y_{i+1})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times Q(y_{i-1},{\rm d}y_{i})G(y_{i-1})Q(y_{i-2},{\rm d}y_{i-1})\cdot\ldots\cdot G(y_{1})Q({x_{0}},{\rm d}y_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G({x_{0}})\underbrace{\int_{S_{0}}\dots\int_{S_{0}}}_{i-1\text{\,int.}}\int_{S_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{y_{i}}(\widetilde{X}_{n-i-1}\in S_{0})Q(y_{i-1},{\rm d}y_{i})G(y_{i-1})Q(y_{i-2},{\rm d}y_{i-1})\cdot\ldots\cdot G(y_{1})Q({x_{0}},{\rm d}y_{1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in S_{0}}\int_{S_{0}}\mathbb{P}_{\widetilde{x}}(\widetilde{X}_{n-i-1}\in S_{0})Q(z,{\rm d}\widetilde{x})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{i}\in S_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in S_{0}}\int_{S_{0}}\widetilde{c}_{u}(\widetilde{x})Q(z,{\rm d}\widetilde{x})\,\widetilde{\alpha}^{n-i-1}\cdot c_{u}({x_{0}})\alpha^{i},

where the last inequality follows from (
1
) and (
2
). Additionally, this conditions lead to

\max\left\{\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0}),\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in S_{0})\right\}\geq\min\{c_{\ell}({x_{0}}),\widetilde{c}_{\ell}({x_{0}})\}\max\{\widetilde{\alpha},\alpha\}^{n}.

Hence by the previous two estimates as well as (
45
) and (
46
) we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(X_{n}\in A\mid X_{n}\in S_{0})-\mathbb{P}_{x_{0}}(\widetilde{X}_{n}\in A\mid\widetilde{X}_{n}\in S_{0})\right|\leq K({x_{0}})\|\kappa-\widetilde{\kappa}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{i}\widetilde{\alpha}^{n-i-1}}{\max\{\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\}^{n}}.

Estimating the sum independently for the two cases
\alpha<\widetilde{\alpha}

and
\alpha\geq\widetilde{\alpha}
leads to

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\alpha^{i}\widetilde{\alpha}^{n-i-1}}{\max\{\alpha,\widetilde{\alpha}\}^{n}}\leq\min\{n,|\alpha-\widetilde{\alpha}|^{-1}\}

and the statement is proven.

Proof of Lemma

We prove the inequality for

\begin{split}\widehat{\lambda}_{1}&=\sup_{\mathbb{V}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}}\inf_{f\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\cap\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)}\langle f,\widehat{L}f\rangle\\

Proof of Lemma
2.2

By Lemma
2.1
and Assumption
2.1
, we have that

\widehat{\lambda}_{0}<\lambda_{0}+\frac{\nu}{2},\qquad\lambda_{1}-\frac{\nu}{2}<\widehat{\lambda}_{1}.

Adding the inequalities and exploiting
\nu=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{0}
gives the first statement.

We now turn to the second statement.
Clearly we have
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}\in\sigma(\widehat{L})
, see
[
34
, Theorem XIII.1]
. This is an eigenvalue since
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
is an isolated point in
\sigma(\widehat{L})
.
The fact that this is furthermore a 1-dimensional eigenspace follows from the simplicity of
\lambda_{0}
: suppose not, so we have two orthogonal eigenfunctions of
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
,
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
each with norm 1. Then given any
1
-dimensional linear subspace
\mathbb{V}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have that
\varphi^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\alpha\varphi_{1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\varphi_{2}\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\cap\mathsf{S}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathcal{D}(L)
, and note that
\varphi^{*}
is also an eigenfunction of
\widehat{\lambda}_{0}
with norm 1. Then we have that

\langle\varphi^{*},L\varphi^{*}\rangle\leq\widehat{\lambda}_{0}+\|H\|\leq\lambda_{0}+2\|H\|<\lambda_{1},

which contradicts the definition of
\lambda_{1}
in (
7
).
Thus the function which generates the eigenspace and has norm

is unique, up to the sign. Therefore, changing the sign if necessary, we may assume that the function

from the eigenspace satisfies

\langle\varphi,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle\geq 0
