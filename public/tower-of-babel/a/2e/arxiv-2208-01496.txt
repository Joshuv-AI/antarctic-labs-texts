A survey of support theories for Lie superalgebras and finite supergroup schemes Thanks: The first author was supported in part by Simons Collaboration Grant for Mathematicians No. 4269055. Thanks: The second author was supported in part by Simons Collaboration Grant for Mathematicians No. 525043.
Christopher M. Drupieski, Jonathan R. Kujawa

Abstract
. We survey the current state of various support variety theories for Lie superalgebras and finite supergroup schemes. We pay particular attention to the theory in characteristic zero developed by Boe, Kujawa, and Nakano using relative Lie superalgebra cohomology, and to the theory developed in positive characteristic in our previous work.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 17B56. Secondary 20G10, 17B50.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Let
A
be a Hopf algebra or similar algebraic structure over an algebraically closed field
k
. Support varieties are tools that help relate the representation theory of
A
to the geometry encoded by the spectrum
\left|A\right|
of the cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)=\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(k,k)
. The enveloping algebra
U(L)
of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra
L
has finite global dimension
d=\dim(L)
, so the Lie algebra cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)=\opH^{\bullet}(U(L),k)
vanishes in all large degrees, and hence its spectrum does not provide an interesting geometry. On the other hand, in positive characteristic the cohomology of finite-dimensional
restricted
Lie algebras—or more generally, of finite group schemes—frequently gives rise to rich algebro-geometric structures. Support variety theories in these contexts have been developed and explored by Friedlander and Parshall
[
23
]
, Suslin, Friedlander, and Bendel
[
45
]
, and Friedlander and Pevtsova
[
25
]
, and many others.

The aim of this paper is to give an overview of what is currently known about cohomological support varieties for Lie
super
algebras and finite
super
group schemes. For a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra
L
, the enveloping superalgebra
U(L)
may or may not have finite global dimension, depending on whether or not
L
contains any odd elements such that
[x,x]=0
. In characteristic zero, even if a Lie superalgebra
L
has infinite global dimension, the ordinary Lie superalgebra cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
may still be finite, and hence may not give rise to any interesting ambient geometry; see for example the calculations in
[
27
, §2.6]
. But replacing the ordinary Lie superalgebra cohomology ring with the relative cohomology ring for the pair
(L,L_{\overline{0}})
, one gets a rich support variety theory in many cases. In positive characteristic, the Lie superalgebra cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
gives rise to a nontrivial geometry whenever the enveloping superalgebra
U(L)
is not of finite global dimension. In general, support varieties for finite-dimensional restricted Lie superalgebras (and more generally, for finite supergroup schemes) are not completely understood, with the fullest picture available only for
p
-nilpotent restricted Lie superalgebras (and more generally, for unipotent finite supergroup schemes). What is known at present parallels the classical, non-super theory in many ways, but with interesting twists arising from new super phenomena.

The paper is organized as follows: We begin in Section
2
by recalling some of the basic definitions and properties for support varieties and Lie superalgebra cohomology. Part
I
of the paper is devoted to what is known in characteristic zero. In Section
3
we summarize the support variety theory for Lie superalgebras developed in a series of papers by Boe, Kujawa, Nakano, and collaborators using relative Lie superalgebra cohomology. In Section
4
we give a brief account of support varieties for finite supergroup schemes in characteristic zero. Part
II
of the paper is devoted to what is known in (odd) positive characteristic. In Section
5
we describe the support variety theory one gets for a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra
L
from considering its ordinary Lie superalgebra cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
. Then, to aid the reader in comparing the super theory with its classical (non-super) counterpart, in Section
6
we summarize the classical results for restricted Lie algebras and infinitesimal group schemes (as developed by Friedlander and Parshall and by Suslin, Friedlander, and Bendel), before discussing in Section
7
our work to date related to restricted Lie superalgebras and infinitesimal supergroup schemes. As a complement to our own work on infinitesimal supergroups, we also recommend to the reader the recent survey article by Benson, Iyengar, Krause, and Pevtsova
[
4
]
.

1.2.
Conventions

Throughout,
k
will denote a ground field of characteristic
p\neq 2
. For simplicity, we will assume that
k
is algebraically closed, though it may be possible to formulate some results without this general assumption. Except when specified, all vector spaces will be
k

. Given a
k
-vector space

be its

Set

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

2.
Background on support varieties and Lie superalgebra cohomology

2.1.
Basic notions for support varieties

In this section we recall some of the basic definitions concerning (cohomological) support varieties for supermodules over a Hopf superalgebra. For further details, the reader can consult
[
15
, §2]
.

Let
k
be a (for simplicity, algebraically closed) field of characteristic
p\neq 2
, and let
A
be a Hopf superalgebra over
k
. The cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)=\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(k,k)
is naturally a graded superalgebra: the
\mathbb{Z}
-component of the grading is the cohomological grading, and the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-component is the ‘internal’ grading coming from the superalgebra structure on
A
. The cup product makes
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
into a graded-commutative superalgebra in the sense of (
1.2.1
). In particular, the subspaces
\opH^{\textup{ev}}(A,k)_{\overline{1}}
and
\opH^{\textup{odd}}(A,k)_{\overline{0}}
of
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
consist of nilpotent elements. Then modulo its nilradical,
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
is a commutative ring in the ordinary sense, with the superdegree of a homogeneous element equal simply to the reduction modulo
2
of its
\mathbb{Z}
-degree.

Given
A
-supermodules
M
and
N
, the cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
acts on
\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(M,N)
via the cup product. In all of the examples we will consider, the following properties will hold:

(FG1)

\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
is a finitely-generated
k
-algebra

(FG2)

If
M
and
N
are finite-dimensional, then
\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(M,N)
is a finite
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
-module.

Let
I_{A}(M)
be the annihilator ideal for the left cup product action of
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
on
\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(M,M)
:

I_{A}(M)=\ann_{\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)}\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(M,M).

Equivalently,
I_{A}(M)
is the kernel of the algebra map
\Phi_{M}:\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(k,k)\to\Ext_{A}^{\bullet}(M,M)
induced by the functor
-\otimes M
. Now the cohomological spectrum of
A
is

\left|A\right|=\MaxSpec\opH^{\bullet}(A,k),

the maximal ideal spectrum of
\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
, and the support variety of
M
, denoted
\left|A\right|_{M}
, is the Zariski closed subset of
\left|A\right|
defined by
I_{A}(M)
:

\left|A\right|_{M}=\MaxSpec\big(\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)/I_{A}(M)\big).

Support varieties for supermodules over a Hopf superalgebra satisfy a list of standard properties; see for example
[
15
, §2.3]
or
[
46
, §8.3]
. In particular, if
A
is a finite-dimensional (hence self-injective) Hopf superalgebra, then under the ‘finite-generation’ hypotheses
(FG1)
and
(FG2)
, one gets for a finite-dimensional
A
-supermodule
M
that the geometric dimension of the support variety
\left|A\right|_{M}
is equal to
\cx_{A}(M)
, the complexity of
M
as an
A
-supermodule, and the support variety
\left|A\right|_{M}
is zero (i.e., is equal to a single point) if and only if
M
is projective; see
[
15
, Proposition 2.3.13]
.

In some contexts we may instead consider support varieties defined via either the prime ideal spectrum
\Spec\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
or the projective spectrum
\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
. When confusion is unlikely, we may write
\left|A\right|=\Spec\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)
and
\left|A\right|_{M}=\Spec\big(\opH^{\bullet}(A,k)/I_{A}(M)\big)
.

In Part
I
we will consider support varieties that are defined via relative cohomology groups. Aside from the switch to relative cohomology, the basic definitions of the cohomological spectrum and of support varieties are the same. Support varieties defined via relative cohomology may not satisfy the full list of ‘standard’ properties as those defined via non-relative cohomology.

2.2.
Generalities of Lie superalgebra cohomology

Let
L
be a Lie superalgebra over the field
k
, and let
U(L)
be the universal enveloping superalgebra of

L
-supermodule is the same thing as a
U(L)
-supermodule. The field

-supermodule via right multiplication. Set

Y(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})=U(\mathfrak{s})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{t})}\boldsymbol{A}(\mathfrak{s}/\mathfrak{t}).

The Koszul differential on
Y(\mathfrak{s})
induces a differential on
Y(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})
, which together with the induced augmentation map
U(\mathfrak{s})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{t})}\boldsymbol{A}(\mathfrak{s}/\mathfrak{t})\to U(\mathfrak{s})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{t})}k\to k
makes
Y(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})
into a resolution of the field
k
. We call this complex the
relative Koszul resolution for the pair
(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})
.

The relative Lie superalgebra cohomology of the pair
(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})
with coefficients in an
\mathfrak{s}
-supermodule
M
, denoted
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t};M)
, is by definition the cohomology of the cochain complex
\Hom_{U(\mathfrak{s})}(Y(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}),M)
. If
k
is a field of characteristic zero (e.g., if
k=\mathbb{C}
), and if
\mathfrak{s}
is finitely-semisimple under the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{t}
, then
Y(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t})
is a
(U(\mathfrak{s}),U(\mathfrak{t}))
-projective resolution of the field
k
, and hence
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t};M)
is equal to the relative
\Ext
-group
\Ext_{(U(\mathfrak{s}),U(\mathfrak{t}))}^{\bullet}(k,M)
as defined for example by Hochschild
[
31
]
. For more details on relative homological algebra, see for example
[
37
, Chapter III]
.

Part I
Characteristic zero

In this part, let
k=\mathbb{C}
.

3.
Support varieties via relative Lie superalgebra cohomology

3.1.
Relative cohomology and support varieties

A finite-dimensional complex Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is called
classical
if there is a connected reductive algebraic group
G_{\overline{0}}
such that
\Lie(G_{\overline{0}})=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
and if there is an action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
that differentiates to the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
. In this section we will assume
\mathfrak{g}
is classical. Our running example will be
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Recall that

(3.1.1)

\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)=\left\{\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}A&B\\
\hline\cr C&D\end{array}\right)\right\},

where
A
is a
m\times m
matrix,
B
is a
m\times n
matrix,
C
is a
n\times m
matrix, and
D
is a
n\times n
matrix. The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading is defined so that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
consists of those matrices with
B=0
and
C=0
,
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is those matrices with
A=0
and
D=0
, and the Lie bracket is the supercommutator. Note that
G_{\overline{0}}=GL_{m}\times GL_{n}
acts on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
by matrix conjugation, and
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is classical.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})
be the category of finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodules that are completely reducible when restricted to
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. It is common that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
is a semisimple Lie algebra, in which case
\mathcal{F}
is the category of all finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodules. One can use the relative cohomology introduced in Section
2.2
to compute cohomology in
\mathcal{F}
. Namely, given supermodules
M
and
N
in
\mathcal{F}
,
[
7
, Theorem 2.5]
states that for all
d\geq 0
,

\Ext^{d}_{\mathcal{F}}(M,N)\cong\opH^{d}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};M^{*}\otimes N)

as superspaces. In the particular case when
M=N=k
, it is easy to see that the differentials in the relative Koszul complex are identically zero and hence the cohomology ring is a ring of invariants:

\Ext_{\mathcal{F}}^{\bullet}(k,k)\cong\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)\cong S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}_{\overline{1}})^{\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}}=S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}^{*}_{\overline{1}})^{G_{\overline{0}}}.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is classical,
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
is a finitely-generated algebra and
\Ext^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{F}}(M,M)
is a finitely-generated
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
-module. Thus conditions
(FG1)
and
(FG2)
are satisfied.

Using notions from invariant theory allows us to compute
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
in many cases. If there exists an element
x_{0}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
such that the orbit
G_{\overline{0}}\cdot x_{0}
is both closed and has maximal dimension among
G_{\overline{0}}
-orbits, then the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is said to be
stable
. Set

\Stab_{G_{\overline{0}}}(x_{0})=\left\{g\in G_{\overline{0}}:g\cdot x_{0}=x_{0}\right\}.

When the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is stable one can define the detecting subalgebra
\mathfrak{f}=\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}
via

\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{\Stab_{G_{\overline{0}}}(x_{0})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}}=[\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}].

For short, we say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
stable
if the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is stable. Set

N=\Norm_{G_{\overline{0}}}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}})=\left\{g\in G_{\overline{0}}:g\cdot\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}\subseteq\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}\right\}.

If there exists a vector
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
such that
\dim_{k}\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}:\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}.x\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}.v\right\}=\dim S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{G_{\overline{0}}}
, then the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is said to be
polar
. In this case, after fixing a choice of such a
v\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
one can define another detecting subalgebra
\mathfrak{e}=\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}}
via

\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}}=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}:\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}.x\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}.v\right\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}}=[\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}}].

Set

W=\Norm_{G_{\overline{0}}}(\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}})/\Stab_{G_{\overline{0}}}(\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}}).

It is known for polar actions that
W
will always be a finite pseduo-reflection group. For short, we say that
\mathfrak{g}
is
polar
if the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is polar. Note that both
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{e}
will again be classical Lie superalgebras and, in the case when the action is both polar and stable, one can choose
x_{0}=v
and then
\mathfrak{e}\subseteq\mathfrak{f}
, and we will always do so.

By a case-by-case check of the classification of simple classical Lie superalgebras
[
34
]
, one sees each is either stable or polar, and most are both
[
7
, Table 5]
. The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is both stable and polar. In this case, the detecting subalgebras can be chosen so that
\mathfrak{f}
is all matrices of the form given in (
3.1.1
) where
A
,
B
,
C
, and
D
are diagonal with the additional requirement that the
(i,i)
and
(m+i,m+i)
entries are equal for all
1\leq i\leq m
. The subalgebra
\mathfrak{e}\subseteq\mathfrak{f}
is those matrices with the further condition that
(i,m+i)
and
(m+i,i)
are equal for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Both
\mathfrak{f}
and
\mathfrak{e}
are significantly smaller than
\mathfrak{g}
. For example,
\mathfrak{e}
is nearly abelian (namely,
[\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}},\mathfrak{e}]=0
) and
\mathfrak{f}
is only a little more complicated. In many respects, a detecting subalgebra simultaneously plays the role of the Cartan subalgebra in Lie theory and the role of the elementary abelian subgroups of a finite group in support variety theory. The following theorem illustrates this philosophy. Note that while the cited result is for classical Lie superalgebras that are stable
and
polar, the proof handles each case separately and so remains valid for those that are stable
or
polar.

Theorem 3.1.1

(
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Assume
\mathfrak{g}
is stable, or polar, or both. Then the restriction maps induced by the inclusions
\mathfrak{f}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
and
\mathfrak{e}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle\res

\displaystyle:\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)\to\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}};k),\quad\text{ and }

\displaystyle\res

\displaystyle:\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)\to\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}};k)

define algebra isomorphisms

\displaystyle\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)

\displaystyle\cong\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}};k)^{N}=S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}^{*}_{\overline{1}})^{N},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)

\displaystyle\cong\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}};k)^{W}=S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{e}^{*}_{\overline{1}})^{W}.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is polar, then the previous theorem describes
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
as the invariant ring of a finite pseduoreflection group, and hence
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
is a polynomial ring. A case-by-case check verifies that this remains true for the non-polar simple classical Lie superalgebras; see
[
7
, Appendix]
.

Given a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{a}
and a finite-dimensional supermodule
M\in\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}})
, we write
\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}})}(M)
for the
relative support variety
defined via the action of the relative cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}};k)
on the relative cohomology group
\Ext_{\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}})}^{\bullet}(M,M)\cong\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}};M^{*}\otimes M)
, i.e.,

\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}})}(M)=\MaxSpec\big(\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}};k)/\ann_{\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}};k)}\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}_{\overline{0}};M^{*}\otimes M)\big).

Importantly for both computations and for theoretical results, the elementary structure of
\mathfrak{e}
allows one to prove that its support varieties have a rank variety description:

Theorem 3.1.2

(
[
7
, Theorem 6.4]
)
.

Given an
\mathfrak{e}
-supermodule
M
, set

(3.1.2)

\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{e}}^{\rank}(M)=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{1}}:M\text{ is not projective as an $\langle x\rangle$-supermodule}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}.

Then there is an isomorphism

\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{e}}^{\rank}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}})}(\mathbb{C}),

which restricts for each finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{e}
-supermodule
M
to an isomorphism

\mathcal{V}_{\mathfrak{e}}^{\rank}(M)\cong\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}})}(M)

Let
M
be a supermodule in
\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})
. If the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is stable and/or polar, then there are morphisms of varieties induced by restriction,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}})}(M)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}})}(M)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M).

It was conjectured in
[
7
]
that these maps induce isomorphisms of varieties

(3.1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}})}(M)/N

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M),

(3.1.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{e}_{\overline{0}})}(M)/W

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M).

The conjecture was proven for the Type I classical Lie superalgebras (i.e., the classical Lie superalgebras with a compatible
\mathbb{Z}
-grading concentrated in degrees
-1
,
0
, and
1
) in
[
38
]
, and for the remaining simple classical Lie superalgebras in
[
30
]
using BBW parabolic subalgebras.

One important consequence of this identification is that the rank variety description for
\mathfrak{e}
-support varieties can be transported to
\mathfrak{f}
- and
\mathfrak{g}
-support varieties.
In particular, this identification implies that support varieties for these Lie superalgebras satisfy the Tensor Product Property:

\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M\otimes N)=\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(M)\cap\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(N).

Support varieties for simple supermodules and other interesting supermodules have been computed in some cases; see
[
6
]
.

(see Section

), in

3.2.
Defect, atypicality, and complexity

Based on other settings, it is reasonable to hope that the dimensions of relative support varieties should encode interesting representation-theoretic invariants. In many classical settings, the dimension of a module’s support variety is equal to the module’s
complexity
; that is, the rate of growth of the module’s minimal projective resolution. As we next explain, for classical Lie superalgebras something different occurs.

By definition, a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{0}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
of a classical Lie superalgebra is a choice of a Cartan subalgebra for
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
, and the roots
\Phi
of
\mathfrak{g}
are the roots of the adjoint action of
\mathfrak{h}
on
\mathfrak{g}
. In particular, we write
\Phi_{\overline{0}}
and
\Phi_{\overline{1}}
for the roots that have a root vector in
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
, respectively. By picking a Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, there is a corresponding set of postive (resp., negative) roots
\Phi^{+}
(resp.,
\Phi^{-}
). For
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, set
\Phi^{\pm}_{r}=\Phi^{\pm}\cap\Phi_{r}
. Put

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{\overline{0}}}\alpha-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}_{\overline{1}}}\alpha.

A classical Lie superalgebra is called
basic
if it admits a nondegenerate, invariant, supersymmetric, even bilinear form
(-,-)
. For example, the supertrace defines such a bilinear form on
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
by the formula
(X,Y)=\strace(XY)
. By the usual identifications, a basic classical Lie superalgebra then has a nondegenerate bilinear form on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, which we also write as
(-,-)
. By definition, the
defect
of
\mathfrak{g}
, denoted
\defect(\mathfrak{g})
, is the cardinality of a maximal set of mutually orthogonal, isotropic elements of
\Phi_{\overline{1}}^{+}
with respect to this bilinear form on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. For example, the defect of
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
is the minimum of
m
and
n
. We will assume
\mathfrak{g}
is basic classical for the remainder of this section.

Recall that for a simple basic classical Lie superalgebra the cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)
is a polynomial ring in some number of variables. A case-by-case check verifies that the number of variables is precisely the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
:

\defect(\mathfrak{g})=\dim\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}};k)=\dim\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(k).

Thus the dimension of the ambient space for support varieties encodes the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
. This raises the question of whether root system combinatorics can also be used to compute the dimension of support varieties for interesting supermodules (e.g., the simple supermodules). As we next explain, the answer appears to be yes.

Given
\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, the
atypicality
of
\lambda
, denoted
\atyp(\lambda)
, is the cardinality of a maximal set of mutually orthogonal, isotropic elements of
\Phi_{\overline{1}}^{+}
that are orthogonal to
\lambda+\rho
. Clearly,

\atyp(\lambda)\leq\defect(\mathfrak{g}).

As for ordinary Lie algebras, the simple
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodules in
\mathcal{F}
can be labeled by their highest weight with respect to our choice of Cartan and Borel subalgebras. Let

\left\{L(\lambda):\lambda\in X^{+}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}\right\}

be a complete, irredundant set of simple
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodules in
\mathcal{F}
. It is well-known that the atypicality of the highest weight encodes important representation-theoretic information about the simple supermodule it labels. For example, Kac and Wakimoto
[
35
]
conjectured that the superdimension of a simple supermodule,

\sdim_{k}L(\lambda):=\dim_{k}L(\lambda)_{\overline{0}}-\dim_{k}L(\lambda)_{\overline{1}},

should be nonzero if and only if
\atyp(\lambda)=\defect(\mathfrak{g})
. In
[
28
]
, the second author along with Geer and Paturau-Mirand formulated a generalization of this conjecture for all degrees of atypicality by relating it to the nonvanishing of certain modified dimension functions. The Kac–Wakimoto conjecture and its generalization are now known to be true for
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
, i.e., for types
ABCD
in the Kac classification; see
[
42
,
36
]
.

It is easy to see from the rank variety description given by (
3.1.2
) and (
3.1.3
) that if
\sdim_{k}L(\lambda)\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(L(\lambda))=\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(k)
. That is, the nonvanishing of the superdimension is related to the support variety being as large as possible, and hence having dimension equal to the defect of
\mathfrak{g}
. In light of the generalized Kac–Wakimoto conjecture, one could imagine that atypicality is related to the dimension of the support variety more generally. The next result confirms this speculation.

Theorem 3.2.1

(
[
6
, Theorem 4.7]
,
[
36
, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
or
\mathfrak{osp}(m|2n)
, and let
L(\lambda)
be a simple
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule in
\mathcal{F}
. Then,

\dim\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(L(\lambda))=\atyp(\lambda).

This gives a cohomological/geometric interpretation of atypicality. Moreover, it naturally suggests to define the atypicality of an arbitrary supermodule in
\mathcal{F}
as the dimension of its support variety.

Question 3.2.2
.

Suppose
\mathfrak{g}
is a classical Lie superalgebra that is not basic, e.g., a Lie superalgebra of type
P
or type
Q
. Can one give a combinatorial method of computing the dimension of a simple
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule’s support variety?

The dimension of a relative support variety does not directly equal the complexity of a supermodule, but we can ask if they are nevertheless related. It turns out that there are two relevant notions of complexity. As mentioned earlier, the
complexity
of a supermodule
M\in\mathcal{F}
is the rate of growth of the dimensions of the terms in a minimal projective resolution
P_{\bullet}
in
\mathcal{F}
of
M
. That is, the complexity of
M
is the minimal integer
c
such that there is a constant
K>0
for which
\dim_{k}P_{d}\leq Kd^{c-1}
for all
d\geq 0
(declaring the complexity to be infinite if no such
c
exists). A second invariant is the
z
-complexity
of
M
, defined to be the rate of growth of the number of indecomposable summands of the minimal resolution. Importantly, unlike complexity,
z
-complexity is invariant under category equivalences. Let us write
c_{\mathcal{F}}(M)
and
z_{\mathcal{F}}(M)
for the complexity and
z
-complexity of
M
, respectively.

The
odd nullcone
of a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
is the set

\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{g}}(k)=\left\{x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}:[x,x]=0\right\}.

Given
M
in
\mathcal{F}
, one has the
associated variety
introduced by Duflo and Serganova
[
20
]
:

(3.2.1)

\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{g}}(M)=\left\{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L):M|_{\langle x\rangle}\text{ is not free}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}.

If
\mathfrak{g}
is
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
,
\mathfrak{osp}(2|2n)
,
\mathfrak{osp}(k|2)
,
D(2,1;\alpha)
,
G(3)
, or
F(4)
, then it is known that the complexity and
z
-complexity of a simple supermodule each have a geometric interpretation.

Theorem 3.2.3

(
[
8
,
21
,
22
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie superalgebra from the above list and let
L
be a simple supermodule in
\mathcal{F}
. Then

\displaystyle c_{\mathcal{F}}(L)

\displaystyle=\dim\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})}(L)+\dim\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{g}}(L),\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle z_{\mathcal{F}}(L)

\displaystyle=\dim\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}})}(L).

If
\mathfrak{g}
is either
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
or
\mathfrak{osp}(2|2n)
, then
\mathfrak{g}
is of Type I and there is a compatible
\mathbb{Z}
-grading
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Given a simple
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
-supermodule
S
, one makes it into a
\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-supermodule by having
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
act trivially, and one then defines the associated
Kac supermodule

K(S)=U(\mathfrak{g})\otimes_{U(\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1})}S.

In the cases
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{osp}(2|2n)
, the previous theorem also holds true if
L
is replaced with
K(S)
. Similarly, the type
P
Lie superalgebra has so-called
thin
Kac supermodules, and the above formulas hold for these as well
[
5
]
.

Unfortunately, the above results are obtained by determining explicit formulas for the complexity and
z
-complexity and, separately, the dimensions of the relevant varieties. The proof is then completed by nodding sagely and observing that the two numbers in each equality are indeed equal. This leads to the following important open question:

Question 3.2.4
.

Do the formulas for
c_{\mathcal{F}}(L)
and
z_{\mathcal{F}}(L)
given in Theorem
3.2.3
hold for arbitrary classical Lie superalgebras and arbitrary supermodules in
\mathcal{F}
?

Answering this question will no doubt require new insights. In particular, the Duflo–Serganova associated variety is not (yet) known to have a cohomological description in characteristic zero. The fact that it appears alongside the support variety in these complexity formulas remains unexplained. For Type I simple Lie superalgebras, a hint that it has a role to play can be found in the fact that the associated variety vanishes if and only if the supermodule in question is projective
[
29
]
.

It is worth remarking that an interesting theory of relative support varieties can also be developed for certain non-classical Lie superalgebras. If
\mathfrak{g}=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathfrak{g}_{k}
has a suitable
\mathbb{Z}
-grading, then one can instead consider relative cohomology for the pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{0})
. This setup applies to the simple Lie superalgebras of type
WSH
in the Kac classification. For example, in
[
1
]
the theory is developed for the Lie superalgebras
W(n)
and, once again, the support variety of a simple supermodule is shown to capture its atypicality.

3.3.
The Balmer Spectrum

Let
\mathcal{K}:=\Stab(\mathcal{F})
be the stable supermodule category for
\mathcal{F}
obtained by factoring out the homomorphisms that factor through a projective supermodule. Since the projective and injective supermodules in
\mathcal{F}
coincide,
\mathcal{K}
is a triangulated category. The tensor structure inherited from
\mathcal{F}
makes
\mathcal{K}
a symmetric tensor triangulated category. To such a category, Balmer
[
2
]
introduced the notion of the spectrum of
\mathcal{K}
, denoted
\Spec(\mathcal{K})
.

Briefly, a
(thick) tensor ideal
of
\mathcal{K}
is a full, replete, triangulated subcategory
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{K}
that satisfies the following conditions:

•

If
X
and
Y
are objects of
\mathcal{K}
and
X\oplus Y
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
, then
X
and
Y
are objects of
\mathcal{I}
;

•

If
X
is an object of
\mathcal{K}
and
Y
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
, then
X\otimes Y
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
.

A tensor ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
prime
if it further satisfies the condition that whenever
X\otimes Y
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
, then either
X
or
Y
is an object of
\mathcal{I}
.

The Balmer spectrum
\Spec(\mathcal{K})
is the collection of all proper prime tensor ideals in
\mathcal{K}
, considered as a topological space via the Zariski topology. The
support
of an object
X
in
\mathcal{K}
is defined by

\supp(X)=\left\{\mathcal{P}\in\Spec(\mathcal{K}):X\text{ is not an object of }\mathcal{P}\right\}.

The Balmer spectrum and support are known to have many of the desirable properties of a support variety theory. They satisfy a certain universal property for such support variety theories, and they can be used to classify the tensor ideals of
\mathcal{K}
. For classical Lie superalgebras we have seen four interrelated support theories: the support varieties of
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{f}
,
\mathfrak{e}
, and the associated variety of Duflo and Serganova. It is an obvious question to ask how these are related to Balmer’s ‘universal’ support theory, and to hopefully give a more concrete description of Balmer’s theory for
\Stab(\mathcal{F})
.

As we explain, the detecting subalgebra
\mathfrak{f}
plays a key role. First, recall that
N
is the normalizer of the action of
G_{\overline{0}}
on
\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}
. There is an action of
N
on
S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}})=\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}};k)
. Let
X=\Proj(S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}))
be the set of homogeneous (with respect to the cohomological
\mathbb{Z}
-grading) prime ideals of
S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}})
, and let
X_{N}=N\text{-}\Proj(S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}))\subseteq X
be the set of homogeneous (with respect to the
\mathbb{Z}
-grading)
N
-prime ideals of
S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}})
. By an
N
-prime ideal we mean an
N
-stable ideal of
S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}^{*})
that is prime among the set of all
N
-stable ideals; see for example
[
39
]
. There is a canonical map
\pi:X\to X_{N}
given by
P\mapsto\bigcap_{n\in N}n\cdot P
.

The key feature of
X_{N}
is that its closed sets are in bijection, via
\pi
, with the
N
-stable closed sets of
X
. In particular, given a
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule
M
in
\mathcal{F}
, the projectivization of the support variety
\mathcal{V}_{(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{0}})}(M)
is an
N
-stable closed set in
X
, and its image under
\pi
is a closed set in
X_{N}
. Recall that in a topological space a subset is called
specialization closed
if it is a union of (arbitrarily many) closed subsets. Then the main result of
[
9
]
is the following:

Theorem 3.3.1

(
[
9
, Theorems 5.2.1 and 5.2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
or
\mathfrak{osp}(2|2n)
. Then there is a homeomorphism

X_{N}\to\Stab(\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}))

that identifies the cohomological support variety (with respect to the detecting subalgebra
\mathfrak{f}
) with the Balmer support.

Moreover, the thick tensor ideals of
\Stab(\mathcal{F}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}))
are in bijection with the specialization closed subsets of
X_{N}
.

Question 3.3.2
.

Can one obtain a similar description of the Balmer spectrum for classical Lie superalgebras in other types?

If one has a support variety theory, then there are results that can be used to identify it with Balmer’s support theory and, in turn, to identify the Balmer spectrum; see
[
9
, Theorem 3.5.1]
or
[
11
, Theorem 1.5]
. Using these results requires that the support variety theory in question satisfy a list of properties, including the ‘realizability’ condition that whenever
W
is a closed subset of the ambient space, then there is an object
M
in
\mathcal{K}
for which the support theory’s value on
M
is
W
. In the context of the previous theorem, this says, roughly, that given a closed
N
-stable subset of
\Proj(S^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{f}_{\overline{1}}))
, we must find a finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule that has the given closed subset as its
\mathfrak{f}
-support variety. The standard realization arguments are not helpful here. Instead, the necessary closed sets are constructed ‘by hand’ using Kac supermodules. Since Kac supermodules only exist for Type I Lie superalgebras, new methods would be required for the other types. Thus, Question
3.1.4
.

4.
Support varieties for finite supergroup schemes in characteristic zero

4.1.
Finite supergroup schemes in characteristic zero

The data of a finite supergroup scheme is equivalent to the data of a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf superalgebra. Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, these have a particularly simple form:

Theorem 4.1.1

(
[
15
, Corollary 3.1.2]
)
.

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, and let
A
be a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf superalgebra over
k
. Then there exists a finite group
G
, a finite-dimensional odd superspace
V
, and a representation of
G
on
V
such that
A
is isomorphic as a Hopf superalgebra to the smash product algebra
\Lambda(V)\#kG
. Here
kG
denotes the group algebra of
G
over
k
, considered as a purely even superalgebra.

We denote the finite supergroup scheme corresponding to the algebra
\Lambda(V)\#kG
by
V\rtimes G
, and we write
V/G
for the set of
G
-orbits in
V
. Over a field of characteristic zero, the group algebra
kG
is semisimple. This leads to the following calculation of the cohomological spectrum of
V\rtimes G
:

Theorem 4.1.2

(
[
15
, Theorem 3.2.2]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
V
be a finite-dimensional purely odd
kG
-module. Then there exist isomorphisms of varieties

\left|V\rtimes G\right|\cong\MaxSpec(S(V^{*})^{G})\cong V/G.

Write
[v]
for the
G
-orbit of an element
v\in V
, and write
\langle v\rangle
for the
k
-subalgebra of
\Lambda(V)
generated by
v
. By convention,
\langle 0\rangle=k
. The category of
V\rtimes G
-supermodules is equivalent to the category of supermodules over the superalgebra
\Lambda(V)\#kG
. Now one gets the following rank variety description for the support varieties of finite-dimensional
V\rtimes G
-supermodules:

Theorem 4.1.3

(
[
15
, Theorem 3.2.3]
)
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
V
be a finite-dimensional purely odd
kG
-module. Let
M
be a finite-dimensional
V\rtimes G
-supermodule. Then

\left|V\rtimes G\right|_{M}\cong\left\{[v]\in V/G:M|_{\langle v\rangle}\text{ is not free}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}.

Part II
Positive characteristic

In Part
II
, let
k
be a field of characteristic
p>0
. Usually
p\neq 2
, although some results can be extended to the case
p=2
; see
[
19
]
. For convenience we will assume that
k
is algebraically closed, although some results may hold without this assumption. Given a
k
-vector space
V
and an integer
r\geq 1
, let
V^{(r)}=V\otimes_{\varphi_{r}}k
be the
r
-th Frobenius twist of
V
, i.e., the
k
-vector space obtained from
V
via base change along the (
r
-th) Frobenius endomorphism
\varphi_{r}:k\to k
,
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{p^{r}}
. If
W=V^{(r)}
, then we may write
V=W^{(-r)}
. More generally, if
X
is an affine
k
-scheme (resp. affine algebraic variety) with coordinate algebra
k[X]
, then we may write
X^{(r)}
for the scheme (resp. variety) with coordinate algebra
k[X^{(r)}]=k[X]^{(r)}
, and if
Y=X^{(r)}
, then we may write
X=Y^{(-r)}
.

5.
Support varieties via ordinary Lie superalgebra cohomology

5.1.
Lie superalgebra cohomology in positive characteristic

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over the field
k
. As discussed in Section
2.2
, the Lie superalgebra cohomology ring
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
can be computed as the cohomology of the superexterior algebra
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(L^{*})
with respect to the Koszul differential
\partial
. Since
\partial
makes
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(L^{*})
into a differential graded superalgebra, and since
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(L^{*})
is graded-commutative in the sense of (
1.2.1
), it follows for all
f\in L_{\overline{1}}^{*}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{1}(L^{*})
that
\partial(f^{p})=0
. Then there exists a map of graded superalgebras

(5.1.1)

\varphi:S(L_{\overline{1}}^{*}[p])^{(1)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)

induced by the
p
-power map on
S(L_{\overline{1}}^{*})
and the inclusion
S(L_{\overline{1}}^{*})\subset\boldsymbol{\Lambda}(L^{*})
. If
M
and
N
are finite-dimensional
L
-supermodules, then
\Ext_{L}^{\bullet}(M,N)
is a finite module over the image of
\varphi
. In particular,
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
is a finitely-generated
k
-algebra, and
\Ext_{L}^{\bullet}(M,N)
is a finite
\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)
-module.

Given an
L
-supermodule
M
, let
J_{L}(M)
be the kernel of the composite map

S(L_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}\xrightarrow{\varphi}\opH^{\bullet}(L,k)\xrightarrow{-\otimes M}\Ext_{L}^{\bullet}(M,M),

and set

\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)=\MaxSpec\big(S(L_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}/J_{L}(M)\big).

Then
\varphi
induces for each
L
-supermodule
M
a homeomorphism of varieties

(5.1.2)

\varphi^{*}:\left|U(L)\right|_{M}\simeq\mathcal{X}_{L}(M).

In the case
M=k
, this provides the following explicit description of the cohomological spectrum for
U(L)
in terms of the
odd nullcone
of
L
.

Theorem 5.1.1

(
[
15
, Theorem 4.2.4]
)
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over
k
. Then

\mathcal{X}_{L}(k)^{(-1)}=\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L):=\left\{x\in L_{\overline{1}}:[x,x]=0\right\}.

5.2.
Support varieties as rank varieties

One of the main results of
[
18
]
was the following ‘rank variety’ description for
\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)
when
M
is finite-dimensional:

Theorem 5.2.1

(
[
18
, Corollary 3.3.2]
)
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over
k
, and let
M
be a finite-dimensional
L
-supermodule. Then

(5.2.1)

\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)^{(-1)}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L):M|_{\langle x\rangle}\text{ is not free}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}.

Here
M|_{\langle x\rangle}
denotes the restriction of
M
to the
k
-subalgebra of
U(L)
generated by
x
; for
x=0
this subalgebra is just the field
k
(and hence
M|_{\langle x\rangle}
is free), while for
0\neq x\in\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L)
this subalgebra is of the form
k[x]/(x^{2})
. The rank variety on the right-hand side of (
5.2.1
) is equivalent in definition to the ‘associated variety’ of an
L
-supermodule originally defined—without reference to cohomology—by Duflo and Serganova for Lie superalgebras in characteristic zero
[
20
]
, and mentioned earlier in Section
3.2
.

Let us say a few words about the strategy used in
[
18
]
to prove (
5.2.1
). Let
\mathcal{X}_{L}^{\prime}(M)
be the closed subset of
\mathcal{X}_{L}(k)=\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L)
such that
\mathcal{X}_{L}^{\prime}(M)^{(-1)}
is the right-hand side of (
5.2.1
). First, using naturality it is relatively easy to show that
\mathcal{X}_{L}^{\prime}(M)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)
. Equality for arbitrary
L
and
M
can then be reduced to showing for all
m\geq 1
that equality holds for the general linear Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|m)
and its natural representation
M=k^{m|m}
. To prove equality for
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}(m|m)
, we consider the Clifford filtration on
\mathfrak{g}
, that is, the Lie superalgebra filtration
F^{0}\mathfrak{g}\subseteq F^{1}\mathfrak{g}\subseteq F^{2}\mathfrak{g}
defined by
F^{0}\mathfrak{g}=0
,
F^{1}\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
, and
F^{2}\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}
. The associated graded Lie superalgebra
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
is concentrated in
\mathbb{Z}
-degrees
1
and
2
, with
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
and
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}\cong\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
as superspaces. The Lie bracket on
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
identifies with the original Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
(and hence retains information about the odd nullcone of
\mathfrak{g}
), while
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}_{2}
is now central in
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
. Given a choice of generators for the
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule
M
, one can also define an associated graded
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
-supermodule
\widetilde{M}
. Now the Clifford filtration gives rise to a spectral sequence relating
\opH^{\bullet}(\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}},k)
and
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, and by studying this spectral sequence one can relate the support of
M
as a
\mathfrak{g}
-supermodule and the support of
\widetilde{M}
as a
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
-supermodule. Finally, the support of
\widetilde{M}
as a
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
-supermodule is calculable, and thus can be used to help put an upper bound on the size of
\mathcal{X}_{\mathfrak{g}}(M)
. The support of the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
-supermodule
\widetilde{M}
is easier to calculate partly because
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
has a simpler Lie superalgebra structure than
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
, and partly because the
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}}
-action on
\widetilde{M}
factors through the action of a
p
-nilpotent restricted Lie superalgebra (equivalently, through the action of an infinitesimal unipotent supergroup scheme), for which we have a clearer picture of the support theory; see Remark
7.6.3
.

An immediate consequence of the ‘rank variety’ description (
5.2.1
) and the representation theory of the Hopf algebra
k[x]/(x^{2})
is that support varieties satisfy the Tensor Product Property:

Proposition 5.2.2
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over a field
k
of characteristic
p\geq 3
, and let
M
and
N
be finite-dimensional
L
-supermodules. Then

\mathcal{X}_{L}(M\otimes N)=\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)\cap\mathcal{X}_{L}(N).

Consequently,
\left|U(L)\right|_{M\otimes N}=\left|U(L)\right|_{M}\cap\left|U(L)\right|_{N}
.

As mentioned in Section
2.1
, if
A
is a
finite-dimensional
(and hence self-injective) Hopf superalgebra whose cohomology satisfies the finiteness properties
(FG1)
and
(FG2)
, then for each finite-dimensional
A
-supermodule
M
one gets
\dim(\left|A\right|_{M})=\cx_{A}(M)
. In particular, one gets that
\left|A\right|_{M}=\left\{0\right\}
if and only if
M
is projective. The cohomology of
U(L)
does satisfy
(FG1)
and
(FG2)
, but
U(L)
is not finite-dimensional unless
L
is a purely odd (abelian) Lie superalgebra. While
U(L)
is not self-injective in general, it is Gorenstein (i.e., it has finite injective dimension as a module over itself), and a finitely-generated
U(L)
-supermodule
M
has finite projective dimension if and only if it has finite injective dimension; see
[
18
, §2.4]
. For a finite-dimensional
U(L)
-supermodule
M
, one can show that
\left|U(L)\right|_{M}=\left\{0\right\}
if and only if
M
is of finite projective dimension. Reformulated in terms of rank varieties, this yields the following statement:

Theorem 5.2.3

(
[
18
, Theorem 3.4.2, Corollary 3.4.3]
)
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic
p\geq 3
, and let
M
be a finite-dimensional
L
-supermodule. Then

\left\{x\in\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L):M|_{\langle x\rangle}\text{ is not free}\right\}=\emptyset\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\projdim_{U(L)}(M)<\infty.

In particular, if
\mathcal{N}_{\textup{odd}}(L)=\left\{0\right\}
, then
U(L)
has finite global dimension (and conversely).

For the converse of the last statement, one observes that
\gldim(U(L^{\prime}))\leq\gldim(U(L))
for any Lie sub-superalgebra
L^{\prime}
of
L
, as a consequence of the fact that
U(L)
is free over
U(L^{\prime})
. So if
U(L)
has finite global dimension, it cannot have any subalgebras of the form
k[x]/(x^{2})
for
x\in L_{\overline{1}}
. The last statement of Theorem
5.2.3
was previously known in characteristic zero by work of Bøgvad
[
10
]
; for details, see
[
40
, Theorem 17.1.2]
.

Problem 5.2.4
.

Let
L
be a finite-dimensional Lie superalgebra over
k
, and let
M
be a finite-dimensional
L
-supermodule. Provide a general representation-theoretic interpretation for the geometric dimension of the support variety
\left|U(L)\right|_{M}\simeq\mathcal{X}_{L}(M)
.

6.
Finite group schemes: recollections from the non-super theory

6.1.
Restricted Lie algebras

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a finite-dimensional restricted Lie algebra over
k
. The restricted enveloping algebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, denoted
V(\mathfrak{g})
, is a finite-dimensional cocommutative Hopf algebra over
k
. The dual Hopf algebra
V(\mathfrak{g})^{*}=\Hom_{k}(V(\mathfrak{g}),k)
is then a finite-dimensional commutative Hopf algebra over
k
with the property that
f^{p}=0
for each element
f
of the augmentation ideal
I_{\varepsilon}
of
V(\mathfrak{g})^{*}
. Thus
V(\mathfrak{g})^{*}
is the coordinate algebra
k[G]
of a height-
1
infinitesimal group scheme
G
, i.e., a finite
k
-group scheme that is equal to its own first Frobenius kernel
G_{(1)}
. The category of (left)
V(\mathfrak{g})
-modules is then equivalent to the category of rational (left)
G
-modules, and cohomology for
V(\mathfrak{g})
may be identified with (rational) cohomology for
G
. For more details, see
[
33
, I.8, I.9]
.

Let
M
be a
V(\mathfrak{g})
-module (equivalently, a rational
G
-module). The powers of the augmentation ideal
I_{\varepsilon}\subset k[G]
give rise to a filtration on the Hochschild complex
C^{\bullet}(G,M)
that computes the cohomology group
\opH^{\bullet}(G,M)=\opH^{\bullet}(V(\mathfrak{g}),M)
. The filtration gives rise in turn to a spectral sequence, which for
p>2
can be written in the form

(6.1.1)

E_{2}^{i,j}(M)=S^{i/2}(\mathfrak{g}^{*})^{(1)}\otimes\opH^{j}(\mathfrak{g},M)\Rightarrow\opH^{i+j}(V(\mathfrak{g}),M).

Here
\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},M)
is the (ordinary) Lie algebra cohomology group of
M
, and the superscript
i/2
means that
E_{2}^{i,j}(M)=0
for odd
i
. In the case
M=k
, the horizontal edge map of (
6.1.1
) defines a finite map of graded
k
-algebras

(6.1.2)

\Phi^{*}:S(\mathfrak{g}^{*}[2])^{(1)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(V(\mathfrak{g}),k).

Here
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*}[2])
means that we consider the symmetric algebra
S(\mathfrak{g}^{*})
as generated in cohomological degree
2
. Passing to maximal ideal spectra, one gets a finite morphism of varieties

\Phi:\left|V(\mathfrak{g})\right|:=\MaxSpec\big(\opH^{\bullet}(V(\mathfrak{g}),k)\big)\to\MaxSpec\big(S(\mathfrak{g}^{*}[2])^{(1)}\big)=\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}

Jantzen
[
32
]
showed, as a consequence of explicit calculations in the case
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, that the image of the morphism
\Phi
is the restricted nullcone of
\mathfrak{g}
:

(6.1.3)

\Phi(\left|V(\mathfrak{g})\right|)^{(-1)}=\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathfrak{g}):=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}:x^{[p]}=0\}.

Then for each finite-dimensional
V(\mathfrak{g})
-module
M
, Friedlander and Parshall
[
23
]
computed the image of the support variety
\left|V(\mathfrak{g})\right|_{M}
under the morphism
\Phi
:

(6.1.4)

\Phi(\left|V(\mathfrak{g})\right|_{M})^{(-1)}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathfrak{g}):M|_{\langle x\rangle}\text{ is not free}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}.

Here
M|_{\langle x\rangle}
denotes the restriction of
M
to the
k
-subalgebra of
V(\mathfrak{g})
generated by
x
. If
x=0
this subalgebra is just the field
k
, while for
x\neq 0
the subalgebra has the form
k[x]/(x^{p})
.

6.2.
Cohomology of infinitesimal group schemes

For this section, fix an integer
r\geq 1
.

Using the theory of strict polynomial functors, Friedlander and Suslin
[
26
]
established the existence of certain ‘universal extension classes’ for the rational cohomology of
GL_{n}
:

(6.2.1)

e_{i}^{(r-i)}\in\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{n},\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{(r)}),\quad 1\leq i\leq r.

Here
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
denotes the adjoint representation of
GL_{n}
, and the superscript
(r)
indicates both that the vector space structure of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
has been twisted by the
r
-th Frobenius morphism of the field, and also that the rational
GL_{n}
-module structure has been twisted via pre-composition with the
r
-th Frobenius morphism
F^{r}:GL_{n}\to(GL_{n})^{(r)}
of the scheme
GL_{n}
. The restriction of
e_{i}^{(r-i)}
to the
r
-th Frobenius kernel
GL_{n(r)}
of
GL_{n}
determines an element of

\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{n(r)},\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{(r)})\cong\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{n(r)},k)\otimes\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{(r)}\cong\Hom_{k}(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*(r)},\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{n(r)},k)),

i.e., it determines a linear map
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*(r)}\to\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{n(r)},k)
, which then extends multiplicatively to a homomorphism of graded
k
-algebras
S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*(r)}[2p^{i-1}])\to\opH^{\bullet}(GL_{n(r)},k)
. Taking the product of these homomorphisms, one gets a map of graded
k
-algebras

(6.2.2)

\textstyle\phi_{GL_{n(r)}}:\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*(r)}[2p^{i-1}])\to\opH^{\bullet}(GL_{n(r)},k).

Now let
G
be an infinitesimal
k
-group scheme of height
\leq r
. Thus
G
is a finite
k
-group scheme with the property that
f^{p^{r}}=0
for each element
f
of the augmentation ideal of
k[G]
. Equivalently,
G
is equal to its own
r
-th Frobenius kernel
G_{(r)}
. Set
\mathfrak{g}=\Lie(G)
, and fix for some
n
a choice of closed embedding
\iota:G\hookrightarrow GL_{n}
, i.e., an embedding of
G
as a closed subgroup scheme of
GL_{n}
. Then the image of
G
is contained in
GL_{n(r)}
, and
\iota
differentiates to an injective homomorphism
d\iota:\mathfrak{g}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
of restricted Lie algebras. Composing
\phi_{GL_{n(r)}}
with the restriction map in cohomology, one gets a homomorphism of graded
k
-algebras

\textstyle\phi_{G}:\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}S(\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{*(r)}[2p^{i-1}])\to\opH^{\bullet}(G,k).

One of the main results of
[
26
]
then states that
\phi_{G}
is a finite algebra map, and that
\opH^{\bullet}(G,M)
is finite over the image of
\phi_{G}
whenever
M
is a finite-dimensional rational
G
-module. In particular,
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
is a finitely-generated
k
-algebra, and
\opH^{\bullet}(G,M)
is a finite
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
-module. In the special case
r=1
, the map
\phi_{G}
reduces to the map (
6.1.2
) considered by Friedlander and Parshall.

6.3.
Support varieties for infinitesimal group schemes

Given an affine
k
-group scheme
G
with coordinate algebra
k[G]
, and given a commutative
k
-algebra
A
, let
G_{A}=G\otimes_{k}A
be the affine
A
-group scheme with coordinate algebra
A[G_{A}]:=k[G]\otimes_{k}A
. If
M
is a rational
G
-module, then
M_{A}:=M\otimes_{k}A
is a rational
G_{A}
-module.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{a}
be the additive group scheme, and
\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
its
r
-th Frobenius kernel. Write
\mathsf{CAlg}(k)
for the category of commutative (unital, associative)
k
-algebras. The functor

V_{r}(G):\mathsf{CAlg}(k)\to\mathsf{Set}

is defined by

(6.3.1)

V_{r}(G)(A)=\Hom_{\mathsf{Grp}/A}(\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}A,G\otimes_{k}A),

the set of
A
-group scheme homomorphisms
\nu:\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}A\to G\otimes_{k}A
. If
G
is an affine algebraic
k
-group scheme (i.e., if
k[G]
is a finitely-generated
k
-algebra), then by
[
44
, Theorem 1.5]
,
V_{r}(G)
admits the structure of an affine
k
-scheme of finite type, called the scheme of one-parameter subgroups of height
\leq r
in
G
. In the special case
r=1
,
V_{1}(G)
identifies with
\mathcal{N}_{1}(\mathfrak{g})
, the restricted nullcone of
\mathfrak{g}=\Lie(G)
. More generally, for
G=GL_{n(r)}
one gets a natural identification

(6.3.2)

V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})(A)=\left\{(\alpha_{0},\cdots,\alpha_{r-1})\in\mathfrak{gl}_{n}(A):\alpha_{i}^{p}=0=[\alpha_{j},\alpha_{\ell}]\text{ for all }0\leq i,j,\ell<r\right\}.

The ‘group algebra’ of a finite
k
-group scheme, denoted
kG
, is the finite-dimensional Hopf algebra that is dual to the coordinate algebra of
G
:
kG=k[G]^{*}
. In the notation of
[
33
, I.8]
,
kG=M(G)
, and if
G
is infinitesimal, then
kG=\Dist(G)
. For a finite
k
-group scheme, the category of rational (left)
G
-modules is equivalent to the category of (left)
kG
-modules
[
33
, I.8.6]
, and rational cohomology for
G
identifies with cohomology for the Hopf algebra
kG
.

For
G=\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
, one has
k[\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}]=k[T]/(T^{p^{r}})
, so

(6.3.3)

k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\cong k[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1}]/(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-1}^{p}).

Here
u_{i}
is the functional such that
u_{i}(f)
is equal to the coefficient of
T^{p^{i}}
, for each
f\in k[T]/(T^{p^{r}})
. Given an integer
0\leq j<p^{r}
, let
j=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}j_{\ell}p^{\ell}
be its base-
p
decomposition (so
0\leq j_{\ell}<p
), and set

\gamma_{j}=\frac{u_{0}^{j_{0}}\cdot u_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots u_{r-1}^{j_{r-1}}}{j_{0}!\cdot j_{1}!\cdots j_{r-1}!}\in k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}.

Then the
\gamma_{j}
form a
k
-basis for
k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
. The coproduct
\Delta
and antipode
S
on
k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
are given by

(6.3.4)

\Delta(\gamma_{\ell})=\sum_{i+j=\ell}\gamma_{i}\otimes\gamma_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad S(\gamma_{j})=(-1)^{j}\gamma_{j}.

Given a point
\mathfrak{s}\in V_{r}(G)
(i.e., a prime ideal
\mathfrak{s}\in\Spec k[V_{r}(G)]
), let
k(\mathfrak{s})
be the residue field of
V_{r}(G)
at
\mathfrak{s}
, and let
\phi_{\mathfrak{s}}\in\Hom_{\mathsf{CAlg}(k)}(k[V_{r}(G)],k(\mathfrak{s}))
be the canonical
k
-algebra homomorphism. Then
\phi_{\mathfrak{s}}
defines a
k(\mathfrak{s})
-point of
V_{r}(G)
, and hence determines a homomorphism of
k(\mathfrak{s})
-group schemes
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}:\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s})\to G\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s})
. Now given a rational
G
-module
M
, let
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}^{*}(M\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s}))
be the rational
\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s})
-module (equivalently, the
k(\mathfrak{s})\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
-module) obtained via pullback along
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}
. Then the subset
V_{r}(G)_{M}
of
V_{r}(G)
is defined by

(6.3.5)

\begin{split}V_{r}(G)_{M}=\{&\mathfrak{s}\in V_{r}(G):\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}^{*}(M\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s}))\text{ is not projective over the subalgebra}\\
&k(\mathfrak{s})[u_{r-1}]/(u_{r-1}^{p})\subset k(\mathfrak{s})[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1}]/(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-1}^{p})=k(\mathfrak{s})\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\}.\end{split}

By
[
45
, Proposition 6.1]
, if
G
is an infinitesimal
k
-group scheme and if
M
is a finite-dimensional rational
G
-module, then
V_{r}(G)_{M}
is a Zariski closed conical subset of
V_{r}(G)
. If
r=1
, then the set of
k
-points of
V_{r}(G)_{M}
identifies with the ‘rank variety’ defined by the right-hand side of (
6.1.4
).

The identity map on
k[V_{r}(G)]
defines a
k[V_{r}(G)]
-point of
V_{r}(G)
, and hence determines a universal homomorphism
u:\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}k[V_{r}(G)]\to G\otimes_{k}k[V_{r}(G)]
of group schemes over
k[V_{r}(G)]
. In
[
44
]
, Suslin, Friedlander, and Bendel (SFB) used this map to define, for each affine (algebraic)
k
-group scheme
G
, a natural
k
-algebra homomorphism

(6.3.6)

\psi_{r}:H(G,k)\to k[V_{r}(G)]

Here
H(G,k)=\opH^{\textup{ev}}(G,k)
if
p
is odd, and
H(G,k)=\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
if
p=2
. Using the algebra relations among the extension classes (
6.2.1
), they also showed that
\phi_{GL_{n(r)}}
factors through a map

(6.3.7)

\overline{\phi}:k[V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})]\to H(GL_{n(r)},k).

Let
\Psi
and
\Phi
denote the morphisms of affine schemes induced by (
6.3.6
) and (
6.3.7
), respectively. Through a detailed analysis of how the universal extension classes (
6.2.1
) restrict to the Frobenius kernels of
\mathbb{G}_{a}
, SFB determined that the composite morphism

\Theta:V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})\xrightarrow{\Psi}\Spec H(GL_{n(r)},k)\xrightarrow{\Phi}V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})

equals the
r
-th Frobenius twist morphism for the scheme
V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})=V_{r}(GL_{n})

[
44
, Theorem 5.2]
. In particular, the homomorphism
\overline{\phi}:k[V_{r}(GL_{n(r)})]\to H(GL_{n(r)},k)
has nilpotent kernel, and by naturality it follows that the map
\psi_{r}:H(G,k)\to k[V_{r}(G)]
is surjective onto
p^{r}
-th powers for any infinitesimal
k
-group scheme
G
of height
\leq r
.

Next, SFB proved the following detection theorem for infinitesimal group schemes:

Theorem 6.3.1

(
[
45
, Theorem 4.3]
)
.

Let
G
be an infinitesimal group scheme of height
\leq r
over
k
and let
\Lambda
be an associative unital rational
G
-algebra. Then the following conditions on a cohomology class
z\in\opH^{n}(G,\Lambda)
are equivalent:

(1)

z
is nilpotent.

(2)

For every field extension
K/k
and every
K
-group scheme homomorphism
\nu:\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}K\to G\otimes_{k}K
, the cohomology class
\nu^{*}(z_{K})\in\opH^{n}(\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}K,\Lambda_{K})
is nilpotent.

(3)

For every point
\mathfrak{s}\in V_{r}(G)
, the class
\nu_{\mathfrak{s}}^{*}(z_{k(\mathfrak{s})})\in\opH^{n}(\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}k(\mathfrak{s}),\Lambda_{k(\mathfrak{s})})
is nilpotent.

The detection theorem was proved first for unipotent infinitesimal group schemes. The general case was then handled with the help of a spectral sequence relating the cohomology of a Frobenius kernel
G_{(r)}
of a reductive group
G
to the cohomology of the Frobenius kernel
B_{(r)}
of a Borel subgroup
B
of
G
. Similar ideas were also used in the proof of the equality (
6.1.4
).

Using the detection theorem, SFB deduced (for
G
infinitesimal of height
\leq r
) that the kernel of the
k
-algebra map
\psi_{r}:H(G,k)\to k[V_{r}(G)]
is also nilpotent, and hence
\psi_{r}
induces a finite universal homeomorphism of schemes

\Psi:V_{r}(G)\simeq\left|G\right|:=\Spec H(G,k).

Furthermore, they deduced for each finite-dimensional rational
G
-module
M
that
\Psi
restricts to a homeomorphism

\Psi:V_{r}(G)_{M}\simeq\left|G\right|_{M}:=\Spec\big(H(G,k)/I_{G}(M)\big).

The rank variety description for
\left|G\right|_{M}
afforded by
V_{r}(G)_{M}
implies the Tensor Product Property for finite-dimensional rational
G
-modules
M
and
N
:

\left|G\right|_{M\otimes N}=\left|G\right|_{M}\cap\left|G\right|_{N}.

6.4.
Finite group schemes

Let
G
be a finite
k
-group scheme. A
\pi
-point
of
G
is a flat map of
K
-algebras
\alpha:K[t]/(t^{p})\to KG_{K}
, for some field extension
K
of
k
, which factors through the group algebra
KE
of an abelian unipotent subgroup scheme
E
of
G_{K}
. Two
\pi
-points
\alpha:K[t]/(t^{p})\to KG_{K}
and
\beta:L[t]/(t^{p})\to LG_{L}
are equivalent, denoted
\alpha\sim\beta
, if for any finite-dimensional
kG
-module
M
, the pullback
\alpha^{*}(M_{K})
is projective over
K[t]/(t^{p})
if and only if the pullback
\beta^{*}(M_{L})
is projective over
L[t]/(t^{p})
. Denote the equivalence class of a
\pi
-point
\alpha
by
[\alpha]
, and denote the set of equivalence classes of
\pi
-points in
G
by
\Pi(G)
.

Given a finite-dimensional rational
G
-module
M
, set

\Pi(G)_{M}=\left\{[\alpha:K[t]/(t^{p})\to KG_{K}]\in\Pi(G):\alpha^{*}(M_{K})\text{ is not projective}\right\}.

Then the class of subsets
\left\{\Pi(G)_{M}:\text{$M$ finite-dimensional $kG$-module}\right\}
is the class of closed subsets of a Noetherian topology on
\Pi(G)
.

Given a
\pi
-point
\alpha:K[t]/(t^{p})\to KG
, let
\opH^{\bullet}(\alpha)
be the composite morphism

\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)=\Ext_{G}^{\bullet}(k,k)\xrightarrow{K\otimes_{k}-}\Ext_{G_{K}}^{\bullet}(K,K)\xrightarrow{\alpha^{*}}\Ext_{K[t]/(t^{p})}^{\bullet}(K,K).

Then
\sqrt{\ker(\opH^{\bullet}(\alpha))}
is a homogeneous prime ideal in
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
, and hence defines a point
\mathfrak{p}(\alpha)
in the projective spectrum
\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
. Conversely, each point
\mathfrak{p}\in\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
can be realized as
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}})
for some
\pi
-point
\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}}:K[t]/(t^{p})\to KG_{K}
of
G
. In this way, one gets a homeomorphism of topological spaces

\Psi_{G}:\Pi(G)\simeq\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(G,k),\quad[\alpha]\mapsto\mathfrak{p}(\alpha),

which satisfies

\Psi_{G}(\Pi(G)_{M})=\Proj(\left|G\right|_{M})=\Proj\big(\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)/I_{G}(M)\big)

for each finite-dimensional
kG
-module
M

[
25
, Theorem 3.6]
.

For an arbitrary (i.e., not necessarily finite-dimensional)
kG
-module
M
, the
\pi
-support of
M
, denoted
\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M)
, is the subset of
\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
defined by

\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M)=\left\{\mathfrak{p}\in\Proj\opH^{\bullet}(G,k):\alpha_{\mathfrak{p}}^{*}(M_{K})\text{ is not projective}\right\}.

If
M
is finite-dimensional, then
\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M)=\Psi_{G}(\Pi(G)_{M})
. For arbitrary
kG
-modules, one can show (see
[
25
,
4
]
) that
\pi
-support detects projectivity,

\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M)=\emptyset\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\text{$M$ is projective},

and
\pi
-support satisfies the Tensor Product Property:

\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M\otimes N)=\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(M)\cap\pi\text{-}\supp_{G}(N).

Let
\iota:k[t]/(t^{p})\to k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}
be the
k
-algebra map defined by
\iota(t)=u_{r-1}
. If
\nu:\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\otimes_{k}K\to G\otimes_{k}K
is a homomorphism defined over a field extension
K/k
, then the induced
K
-algebra map
\nu\circ\iota:K[t]/(t^{p})\to K\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\to KG_{K}
is a
\pi
-point of
G
. In this way, one gets for each infinitesimal
k
-group scheme
G
of height
\leq r
a bijection
\Proj V_{r}(G)\simeq\Pi(G)
, which restricts for each finite-dimensional
kG
-module
M
to a bijection
\Proj V_{r}(G)_{M}\simeq\Pi(G)_{M}
; see
[
24
, Proposition 3.8]
.

7.
Infinitesimal supergroup schemes

In this section, let
r\geq 1
be a fixed integer.

7.1.
Cohomology of infinitesimal supergroups

The general linear Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
identifies with the space
\Hom_{k}(k^{m|n},k^{m|n})
of
k
-linear endomorphisms of the superspace
k^{m|n}
. Then
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{0}}=\mathfrak{gl}_{m}\oplus\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{g}_{+1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{+1}=\Hom_{k}(k^{0|n},k^{m|0})
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}=\Hom_{k}(k^{m|0},k^{0|n})
. The adjoint action of the general linear supergroup
GL_{m|n}
restricts to a rational action of
GL_{m}\times GL_{n}
on each of
\mathfrak{gl}_{m}
,
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{+1}
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
, and the
r
-th Frobenius morphism on
GL_{m|n}
defines a supergroup scheme homomorphism
F^{r}:GL_{m|n}\to(GL_{m}\times GL_{n})^{(r)}
. Then via this map, the Frobenius twists
\mathfrak{gl}_{m}^{(r)}
,
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{(r)}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{+1}^{(r)}
, and
\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{(r)}
each become rational
GL_{m|n}
-supermodules.

In
[
13
]
, the first author used the theory of strict polynomial superfunctors to establish the existence of certain universal extension classes for the general linear supergroup:

(7.1.1)

\left.\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{(r-i)}&\in\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{m|n},\mathfrak{gl}_{m}^{(r)})\\
(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{(r-i)})^{\Pi}&\in\opH^{2p^{i-1}}(GL_{m|n},\mathfrak{gl}_{n}^{(r)})\end{aligned}\right\}\text{ for $1\leq i\leq r$, and }\left\{\begin{aligned} \boldsymbol{c}_{r}&\in\opH^{p^{r}}(GL_{m|n},\mathfrak{g}_{+1}^{(r)}),\\
\boldsymbol{c}_{r}^{\Pi}&\in\opH^{p^{r}}(GL_{m|n},\mathfrak{g}_{-1}^{(r)}).\end{aligned}\right.

In a manner similar to (
6.2.2
), the restriction of these classes to the
r
-th Frobenius kernel of
GL_{m|n}
can be used to construct a homomorphism of graded superalgebras

(7.1.2)

\textstyle\phi_{GL_{m|n(r)}}:\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{0}}^{*}[2p^{i-1}])^{(r)}\right)\otimes S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{1}}^{*}[p^{r}])^{(r)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(GL_{m|n(r)},k).

Given a closed sub-supergroup scheme
G
of
GL_{m|n(r)}
, one can compose with the restriction map in cohomology
\opH^{\bullet}(GL_{m|n(r)},k)\to\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
to get a homomorphism of graded superalgebras

\textstyle\phi_{G}:\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{0}}^{*}[2p^{i-1}])^{(r)}\right)\otimes S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{1}}^{*}[p^{r}])^{(r)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(G,k).

One of the main consequences of the work in
[
13
]
was that, if
G
is an infinitesimal
k
-supergroup scheme of height
\leq r
, embedded into some
GL_{m|n(r)}
, then
\phi_{G}
is a finite map and
\opH^{\bullet}(G,M)
is finite over the image of
\phi_{G}
whenever
M
is a finite-dimensional rational
G
-supermodule. In particular,
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
is a finitely-generated
k
-superalgebra, and
\opH^{\bullet}(G,M)
is a finite
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
-module.

In the case
r=1
, the map
\phi_{G}
can be interpreted via the edge maps of a spectral sequence. Indeed, let
G
be a height-
1
infinitesimal
k
-supergroup scheme, and let
\mathfrak{g}
be its restricted Lie superalgebra. Arguing in exactly the same manner as for (
6.1.1
), one gets a spectral sequence

(7.1.3)

E_{2}^{i,j}=S^{i/2}(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{*})^{(1)}\otimes\opH^{j}(\mathfrak{g},k)\Rightarrow\opH^{i+j}(G,k).

Fixing a choice of embedding
G\hookrightarrow GL_{m|n(1)}
, one gets a map of restricted Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)
. Then up to a nonzero scalar, the restricted map
\phi_{G}:S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{0}}^{*})^{(1)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
is the composition of the quotient map
S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{0}}^{*})^{(1)}\twoheadrightarrow S(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{*})^{(1)}
and the horizontal edge map of (
7.1.3
). Similarly, the composition of
\phi_{G}:S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)
with the horizontal edge map
\opH^{\bullet}(G,k)\to\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},k)
of (
7.1.3
) is equal, up to a nonzero scalar, to the composition of the quotient map
S(\mathfrak{gl}(m|n)_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}\twoheadrightarrow S(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}
and the homomorphism
\varphi:S(\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{*})^{(1)}\to\opH^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g},k)
of (
5.1.1
).

Unlike the classical situation of (
6.1.1
), in general the
E_{2}
-page of (
7.1.3
) is not finite over the evident subalgebra of permanent cycles generated by the terms on the horizontal axis. This shows how, starting immediately with the base case of
r=1
, the problem of establishing the basic finite-generation property
(FG1)
is more subtle for infinitesimal supergroups than for ordinary infinitesimal group schemes.

7.2.
Multiparameter supergroups

Define
\mathbb{M}_{r}
to be the affine
k
-supergroup scheme whose coordinate algebra
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
is generated by the odd element
\tau
and the even elements
\theta
and
\sigma_{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\tau^{2}=0
,
\sigma_{0}=1
,
\theta^{p^{r-1}}=\sigma_{1}
, and
\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\binom{i+j}{i}\sigma_{i+j}
, i.e.,

k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]=k[\tau,\theta,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3},\ldots]/\big(\tau^{2},\theta^{p^{r-1}}-\sigma_{1},\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\tbinom{i+j}{i}\sigma_{i+j}:i,j\in\mathbb{N}\big).

Then the set of monomials
\left\{\theta^{i}\sigma_{j},\tau\theta^{i}\sigma_{j}:0\leq i<p^{r-1},j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a homogeneous basis for
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
. The coproduct
\Delta
and the antipode
S
on
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
are defined on generators by the formulas

\displaystyle\Delta(\tau)

\displaystyle=\tau\otimes 1+1\otimes\tau,

\displaystyle S(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\tau,

\displaystyle\Delta(\theta)

\displaystyle=\theta\otimes 1+1\otimes\theta,

\displaystyle S(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\theta,

\displaystyle\Delta(\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{u+v=i}\sigma_{u}\otimes\sigma_{v}+\sum_{u+v+p=i}\sigma_{u}\tau\otimes\sigma_{v}\tau,

\displaystyle S(\sigma_{i})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\sigma_{i}.

The
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading on
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
lifts to a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading such that
\deg(\tau)=p^{r}
,
\deg(\theta)=2
, and
\deg(\sigma_{i})=2ip^{r-1}
. This makes
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
into a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Hopf algebra of finite type.

Let
k\mathbb{M}_{r}=k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]^{*}
be the ‘group algebra’ of
\mathbb{M}_{r}
, i.e., the
k
-algebra that is dual to the coalgebra structure on
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
. For
0\leq i\leq r-1
, let
u_{i}\in k\mathbb{M}_{r}
be the (even) linear functional that is dual to the basis vector
\theta^{p^{i}}
(so in particular,
u_{r-1}
is dual to
\theta^{p^{r-1}}=\sigma_{1}
), and let
v\in k\mathbb{M}_{r}
be the (odd) linear functional that is dual to the basis vector
\tau
. Then

k\mathbb{M}_{r}=\frac{k[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-2},v][[u_{r-1}]]}{(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-2}^{p},u_{r-1}^{p}+v^{2})}.

The graded dual of the
\mathbb{Z}
-graded vector space
k[\mathbb{M}_{r}]
is the subalgebra

\mathbb{P}_{r}=\frac{k[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1},v]}{(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-2}^{p},u_{r-1}^{p}+v^{2})}.

The algebra
\mathbb{P}_{r}
can be thought of as the ‘polynomial part’ of

. Since

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Hopf algebra of finite type, its graded dual is also a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded Hopf algebra (and hence a Hopf superalgebra, by reducing the
\mathbb{Z}
-grading modulo
2
). The coproduct
\Delta
and antipode
S
for
\mathbb{P}_{r}
are defined on the generator

by
\Delta(v)=v\otimes 1+1\otimes v
and
S(v)=-v
, and are defined on
u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1}
by the same formulas as in (

). In particular,
\Delta(u_{r-1}^{p})=u_{r-1}^{p}\otimes 1+1\otimes u_{r-1}^{p}
.

Let
f=\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}T^{p^{i}}
be an inseparable
p
-polynomial (i.e., a
p
-polynomial without a linear term), and let
\eta\in k
. Since
u_{0}
and
u_{r-1}
are both primitive in
\mathbb{P}_{r}
, the sum
f(u_{r-1})+\eta\cdot u_{0}
is also primitive in
\mathbb{P}_{r}
. Assuming that
f\neq 0
if
r\geq 2
, and that either
f\neq 0
or
\eta\neq 0
if

, the quotient

k\mathbb{M}_{r,f,\eta}:=\mathbb{P}_{r}/(f(u_{r-1})+\eta\cdot u_{0})

is then a finite-dimensional Hopf superalgebra. Let
\mathbb{M}_{r,f,\eta}
be the finite
k
-supergroup scheme whose group algebra is
k\mathbb{M}_{r,f,\eta}
. For
s\geq 1
, set
\mathbb{M}_{r,s,\eta}=\mathbb{M}_{r,T^{p^{s}},\eta}
, and set
\mathbb{M}_{r,s}=\mathbb{M}_{r,s,0}
. Then

\displaystyle k\mathbb{M}_{r,s,\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{k[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1},v]}{(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-2}^{p},u_{r-1}^{p}+v^{2},u_{r-1}^{p^{s}}+\eta\cdot u_{0})},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle k\mathbb{M}_{r,s}

\displaystyle=\frac{k[u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1},v]}{(u_{0}^{p},\ldots,u_{r-2}^{p},u_{r-1}^{p}+v^{2},u_{r-1}^{p^{s}})}.

Observe also that
\mathbb{P}_{r-1}\cong\mathbb{P}_{r}/(u_{0})
,
k\mathbb{G}_{a(r)}\cong\mathbb{P}_{r}/(v)
, and

k\mathbb{G}_{a}^{-}=k[v]/(v^{2})\cong\mathbb{P}_{r}/(u_{0},\ldots,u_{r-1}).

Here
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{-}
is the odd additive supergroup scheme. Its coordinate superalgebra is

with
\overline{t}=\overline{1}
. As a functor

\mathbb{G}_{a}^{-}(A)=(A_{\overline{1}},+)

denotes the category of (super)commutative
k
-superalgebras.

We say that a finite

-supergroup scheme

7.4.
Commuting varieties

The general case

(

Let
