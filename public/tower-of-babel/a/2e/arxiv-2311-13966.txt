Testing Continuous Spontaneous Localization model with charged macro-molecules
Emil Lenler-Eriksen, Michael Drewsen, Matteo Carlesso

Abstract
In the last decade, a growing interest has been devoted to models of spontaneous collapse of the wavefunction, known also as collapse models. They coherently solve the well-known quantum measurement problem by suitably modifying the Schrödinger evolution. Quantum experiments are now finally within the reach of testing such models (and thus testing the limits of quantum theory). Here, we propose a method based on a two-ions confined in a linear Paul trap to possibly enhance the testing capabilities of such experiments. The combination of an atomic and a macromolecular ion provide a good match for the cooling of the motional degrees of freedom and a non-negligible insight in the collapse mechanism, respectively.



The validity of quantum theory at the microscopic scale has been verified to astonishing levels of precision. However, at the macroscopic scale, we perceive a fully classical world, where quantum superpositions are never observed altough quantum mechanics does not pose any limitation. Somewhere, between these two scales, the universality of quantum mechanics, and of its superposition principle, breaks down. Modifications to the quantum theory connecting these two limits, can also resolve the well-known quantum measurement problem. Among these, collapse models
Bassi and Ghirardi 2003
— such as the Continuous Spontaneous Localisation (CSL) model
Pearle 1989
;
Ghirardi et al. 1990
— introduce non-linear and stochastic (noisy) terms in the Schrödinger equation that lead to a spontaneous collapse of the wavefunction and coherently describe a quantum-to-classical transition when moving from micro to macro systems. In the CSL model, such modifications are parametrised by the free phenomenological parameters
\lambda
and
r_{\text{\tiny C}}
, which are the collapse rate for a single nucleon and the correlation length of the collapse noise,respectively. An built-in amplification mechanism scaling as a function of the mass ensures that the more massive a system is, the faster it will collapse. Testing the possible values of such parameters is equivalent to testing the limits of quantum mechanics. The current state-of-the-art in testing collapse models is achieved by recently developed non-interferometric experiments
Carlesso et al. 2022
. These tests are based on the measurement of side-effects appearing when the collapse noise is included in the dynamics. Examples are diffusion, photon emission and heating, and they can be measured with a plethora of different experimental setups
Carlesso et al. 2022
.
Among these, optomechanical ones placed among the tightest experimental upper bounds to date based on the quantification of heating and diffusive action of the collapse noise on the mechanical resonator
Vinante et al. 2016
;
Carlesso et al. 2016
;
Vinante et al. 2017
;
Helou et al. 2017
;
Vinante et al. 2020
;
Pontin et al. 2020
;
Zheng et al. 2020
;
Komori et al. 2020
.

In this work, we propose an experimental scheme to study the CSL action through the detection of the motional heating of a two-ion system in a linear Paul trap, where the system is initially cooled to its ground state via Doppler and sideband laser cooling (See Ref.
Eschner et al. 2003
for various ion cooling schemes). As it is clear from the calculations below, the sensitivity to CSL effects increases with the physical dimensions of the individual ions involved. Hence, in order to both ensure the initial ground state cooling and a high CSL sensitivity, we will consider a two-ion system consisting of a single atomic ion
and a charged macro-molecule with an extended mass distribution. The former will be used for ground state sideband laser cooling of the common motional modes of the two-ion system, and any following heating of a specific mode will be measured to quantify/bound potential CSL effects.

The model. –

The CSL model effects can be described through the use of its master equation for the state
\hat{\rho}
of the system

Carlesso et al. 2022
:

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\hat{\rho}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{0},\hat{\rho}]-\frac{\lambda r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{3}}{2\pi^{3/2}m_{0}^{2}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\bf k}\,e^{-k^{2}r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{2}}[\hat{\mu}_{\bf k},[\hat{\mu}_{-{\bf k}},\hat{\rho}]],

(1)

where
m_{0}
is a reference mass, taken equal to that of a nucleon, and
\hat{\mu}_{\bf k}
is the Fourier transform of the mass density operator. Here, we also defined the free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
of the system, which is derived as follows.

The system consists of two ions of masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
and charges
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, respectively, placed in the same three-dimensional harmonic trap. The harmonic trap has a cylindrical symmetry with respect to the
z
-axis. When the ion species are trapped independently, their motion along the
z
-axis corresponding to the frequencies
\omega_{i,z}
with
i=1,2
, while
x
and
y
are the radial ones, corresponding to the frequencies
\omega_{i,r}
with
i=1,2
.
The total potential then reads

\hat{V}=\sum_{i=1,2}\tfrac{1}{2}m_{i}\left[\omega_{i,z}^{2}\hat{z}_{i}+\omega_{i,r}^{2}(\hat{x}_{i}^{2}+\hat{y}_{i}^{2})\right]+\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{|\hat{\bf r}_{1}-\hat{\bf r}_{2}|},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{0}
is the dielectric constant of vacuum.
The Coulomb interaction between the two ions couples their motional dynamics. For sufficiently strong radial confinement, the ions will oscillate around the classical equilibrium positions
{\bf r}_{\text{eq},i}=(0,0,z_{\text{eq},i})
, where
z^{3}_{\text{eq},1}=\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{1}\omega_{1}^{2}}({1+\tfrac{1}{Q}})^{-1}
,
z_{\text{eq},2}=-z_{\text{eq},1}/Q
and
Q=q_{2}/q_{1}
,
with a motion that
can be described in terms of six modes, where the two ions have in-phase or out-of-phase motions. By Taylor-expanding the Coulomb interaction to the second order in the displacements, one obtains the following effective frequencies for the in-phase (
+
) and out-of-phase (
-
) motions along the axial and radial directions:

\displaystyle({\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\pm}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K_{11}+K_{22}\mp\sqrt{(K_{11}-K_{22})^{2}+4K_{12}^{2}}}{2},

(3)

\displaystyle({\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\pm}})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{K_{33}+K_{44}\pm\sqrt{(K_{33}-K_{44})^{2}+4K_{34}^{2}}}{2},

where the coefficients
K_{ij}
depend on the original axial and radial frequencies
\omega_{i,z}
and
\omega_{i,r})
as well as the mass and charge ratios
M={m_{2}}/{m_{1}}
and
Q={q_{2}}/{q_{1}}
[cf. Appendix]. We note that, due to the cylindrical symmetry of the trap, the radial frequencies have degeneracy two.
The in-phase and out-of-phase modes can then be described in terms of the creation and annihilation operators
(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\pm},\hat{a}_{\pm})
,
(\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{\pm},\hat{b}_{\pm})
, and
(\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{\pm},\hat{c}_{\pm})
where
a
is for the axial modes and
(b,c)
denote the
(x,y)
radial modes respectively. In this paper we consider a cylindrically symmetrical trap, and as such the
x
and
y
directions can be treated identically. Thus, we can reduce the problem to a two-dimensional one. Hence, the free Hamiltonian of the two trapped ions can be expressed as

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\nu\in\{+,-\}}\left[\hbar\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}(\hat{a}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\nu}+\tfrac{1}{2})+\hbar\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}(\hat{b}_{\nu}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{\nu}+\tfrac{1}{2})\right].

(4)

One can express the displacement operators of the two ions with respect to the equilibrium positions in terms of
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
and their hermitian conjugates. In particular, the displacement operator of the
i
-th ion along the
z
direction with respect to
z_{\text{eq},i}
is

\hat{z}_{i}=\sum_{\nu\in\{+,-\}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m_{i}\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}}\alpha_{\nu,i}(\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{\nu}+\hat{a}_{\nu}),

(5)

while the motion along
x
(here the motion along
y
is neglected) can be obtained from Eq. (
5
) by applying the substitutions

\hat{a}_{\nu}\to\hat{b}_{\nu}
,
\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}\to\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}
and
\alpha_{\nu,i}\to\beta_{\nu,i}
. The coefficients
\alpha_{\nu,i}
and
\beta_{\nu,i}
are defined as, respectively, the first (
i=1
) and second (
i=2
) component of the normalized motional eigenvectors

\alpha_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm}\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad\beta_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tilde{\beta}_{\pm}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\beta}_{\pm}\\
1\end{pmatrix},

(6)

with
\tilde{\alpha}_{\pm}=[{(\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\pm})^{2}-K_{22}}]/{K_{12}}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{\pm}=[{(\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\pm})^{2}-K_{44}}]/{K_{34}}
, and where
\alpha_{\pm}
are the axial eigenmode vectors while
\beta_{\pm}
are the radial eigenmodes [cf. Appendix].

Finally, one computes the CSL-induced effects by computing the collapse action on the single mode Hamiltonian
\hat{h}_{\kappa}
, where
\kappa\in\{1,4\}
labels the modes. Specifically, from Eq. (
1
), one computes the CSL-induced heating through

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\hat{h}_{\kappa}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}=-\frac{\lambda r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{3}}{2\pi^{3/2}m_{0}^{2}}\int\operatorname{d}\!{\bf k}\,e^{-k^{2}r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{2}}[\hat{\mu}_{\bf k},[\hat{\mu}_{-{\bf k}},\hat{h}_{\kappa}]].

(7)

Assuming that we can consider both ion species as point-like masses, one can substitute
\hat{\mu}_{\bf k}=\sum_{i}m_{i}e^{-i{\bf k}({\bf r}_{\text{eq,i}}+\hat{\bf r}_{i})}
and
\hat{h}_{\kappa}=\hbar\omega_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\hat{u}_{\kappa}+\tfrac{1}{2})
, where
\hat{u}_{\kappa}
can be either
\hat{a}_{\kappa}
or
\hat{b}_{\kappa}
. Owning that

[{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i},\hat{u}_{\kappa}]=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m_{i}}}\sum_{\nu}\left(\frac{k_{x}\beta_{\nu,i}\delta_{\hat{b}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}}}+\frac{k_{z}\alpha_{\nu,i}\delta_{\hat{a}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}}\right),

(8)

is a c-number, and that
[{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i},\hat{u}_{\kappa}]=-[{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i},\hat{u}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}]
, we can express

[e^{-i{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i}},\hat{u}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}\hat{u}_{\kappa}]=i[{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i},\hat{u}_{\kappa}^{\dagger}](\hat{u}_{\kappa}-\hat{u}_{\kappa}^{\dagger})e^{-i{\bf k}\hat{\bf r}_{i}}.

(9)

Substituting such an expression in Eq. (
7
) and integrating over
{\bf k}
, we find that the CSL energy gain
P_{\kappa}=\braket{\frac{\operatorname{d}\!\hat{h}_{\kappa}}{\operatorname{d}\!t}}
of the mode
\kappa
is given by

P_{\kappa}=\frac{\lambda\hbar^{2}\omega_{\kappa}}{4m_{0}^{2}r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{2}}\sum_{ij}\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}e^{-(z_{\text{eq},i}-z_{\text{eq},j})^{2}/4r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{2}}\left[\sum_{\nu}\frac{\beta_{\nu,i}\beta_{\nu,j}^{*}}{\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}}\delta_{\hat{b}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}+\left(1-\tfrac{(z_{\text{eq},i}-z_{\text{eq},j})^{2}}{2r_{\text{\tiny C}}^{2}}\right)\sum_{\nu}\frac{\alpha_{\nu,i}\alpha_{\nu,j}^{*}}{\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}\delta_{\hat{a}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}\right],

(10)

and it can be used to bound the CSL parameters when compared to experimental data. In the Appendix, we go beyond such an approximation, where we consider the second ion as having an internal structure and a non-trivial non-deformable geometry.
The differences between the bounds computed with Eq. (
10
) and that computed beyond the point-like approximation become visible only for values of
r_{\text{\tiny C}}
smaller or comparable to the dimension of the macromolecule.

Competing effects. –

Several types of noises will be contributing to the overall heating mechanism. Among these, the strongest heating will be due to electrical noises in the Paul trap, which will couple to the charges of the ions
Brownnutt et al. 2015
. They can be accounted for via standard perturbation theory where the free Hamiltonian is
\hat{H}_{0}
and the perturbation is described by

\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(t)=-\sum_{i}q_{i}{\bf E}_{t}\hat{\bf r}_{i},

(11)

where the sum runs over the ions, and
{\bf E}_{t}
is a assumed spatially uniform stochastic electric field being characterised by zero mean
\mathbb{E}[{\bf E}_{s}]=0
and whose correlations are assumed to be

\mathbb{E}[E^{(k)}_{s}E^{(k^{\prime})}_{s^{\prime}}]=\frac{\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{d}\!\omega\,S^{(k)}_{\text{\tiny E}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega(s-s^{\prime})},

(12)

where
S^{(k)}_{\text{\tiny E}}(\omega)
is the corresponding power spectral density along the direction
k
. The consequent energy gain
P_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(\kappa)}
of the mode
\kappa
reads

P_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(\kappa)}=\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}\hbar\omega_{\kappa}\mathbb{E}\left[|c_{\text{\tiny FI}}|^{2}\right],

(13)

where
c_{\text{\tiny FI}}
is the transition amplitude between the initial state (ground state of the
\kappa
mode
\ket{0_{\kappa}}
) and final state (first excited state
\ket{1_{\kappa}}
), which reads

c_{\text{\tiny FI}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\,\braket{1_{\kappa}|e^{\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{0}s}\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(s)e^{-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{0}s}|0_{\kappa}}.

(14)

Following the detailed calculations reported in Appendix, and assuming that the electrical noises are spatially homogeneous, we find

\displaystyle P_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(\kappa)}=\frac{\omega_{\kappa}}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}}

\displaystyle S_{\text{\tiny E}}(\omega_{\kappa})\sum_{\nu}\left(\frac{\delta_{\hat{a}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}+\frac{\delta_{\hat{b}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}}\right),

(15)

which critically depends on the charges of the ions and their mass. By comparing the above expression with Eq. (
10
), one notice that
i)
Eq. (
10
) is independent from the electrical charge of the ions, while Eq. (
15
) goes as
\sim q_{i}q_{j}
;
ii)
the mass dependence is strongly different: Eq. (
10
)
\sim\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}
while Eq. (
15
)
\sim 1/\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}
. From heating rate measurements with different combinations of one- and two-ion systems, these different dependencies on the ions’ charges and masses make it possible to quantitatively differentiate motional heating due to electrical field fluctuations from other heating sources, including those expected from collapse models.
Then, being able to perform a precise characterisation of electrical effects, one can subtract their heating and provide more stringent bounds on the collapse mechanism.

Experimental setup. –

For the experimental realisation, a linear radio frequency (RF) trap
Drewsen and Brøner 2000
of rather large dimensions (distance between electrodes
\sim 5
mm, like that in Ref.
Poulsen et al. 2012
) will be applied. The trap will operated at cryogenic temperatures (
T\sim 4
K) to avoid strong influence of limiting heating mechanisms such as background gas collisions, thermally induced fluctuating electrical patch potentials on the electrodes, and scattering of blackbody radiation photons
Hansen et al. 2014
. In order to achieve highly stable trapping of both ion species in the RF trap (as well as have non-negligible cooling rates of at least one of the normal modes), we consider systems consisting of ions with similar mass-to-charge ratios. Consequently, we need a heavy atomic ion for ground state sideband laser cooling, and a massive potentially multiply charged macro-molecule. Heavy atomic ion species such as Yb
+
or Hg
+
seem best suited based on their high masses, but the UV light needed for the laser cooling process of these ions may potentially be absorbed by the molecular ion and lead to unwanted internal heating or even fragmentation. With that in mind, the Ba
+
ion seems a more favorable choice. Any massive complex molecular ion which can be multiply charged and brought into the ion trap could work. Here, we consider oligomers of porphyrin rings with each having two elementary charges, since a variety of such oligomers have been synthesized
Haver and Anderson 2019
and brought to the considered charge state using an electrospray ion source
Yamashita and Fenn 1984
suitable for bringing such molecular ions into an ion trap.

Once the atomic ion is trapped together with the macro-molecule, the following sequence will be applied.
First, the two-ion system will be cooled to a temperature of
\sim 1
mK by direct Doppler laser cooling of the atomic ion and indirect cooling of the molecular ion through the Coulomb interaction between the two ion species
Højbjerre et al. 2008
. At this point the ions perform common-mode oscillations around their equilibrium positions indicated in Fig.
1
a. Next, ground state cooling of one of the common-modes is implemented through sideband laser cooling via addressing a narrow transition of the atomic ion on the so-called red sideband, i.e., by exposing the ion to laser light with a frequency equal to the transition frequency minus the common-mode frequency. When the ground state has been reached (Fig.
1
b, lower left panel), absorption on the red sideband ceases since any energy conserving excitation should lead to a reduction of a single common-mode quanta. This means, the atomic ion can no longer be brought into the long-lived excited state of the narrow transition, which can be confirmed experimentally with near
100\%
efficiency through the application of a sequence of laser excitation pulses (See dark blue points in Fig.
1
c and Appendix)
Poulsen et al. 2012
. After in this way having checked the two-ion system is initialized in a common-mode ground state, the two-ion system is left unperturbed by laser light to heat by CSL mechanisms or other sources. After a time
\tau
, a check of whether the ion system is still in the ground state is performed. If an excitation of the common-mode has happened (Fig.
1
b, upper right panel), laser excitation can now take place and can clearly be detected experimentally as indicated by the contrast between the dark blue data points and the light blue data points in Fig.
1
c. Through repeated experiments with varying values of
\tau
, one can eventually establish an experimental heating rate of the particular common-mode
Poulsen et al. 2012
, which through comparisons with Eq. (
10
) can set an upper bound to CSL mechanisms, since any other unknown heating mechanisms would just lead to a faster and not slower heating of the ion system.

By conducting experiments with various molecular ion species, for which CSL predicts
different heating rates, one can distinguish the CSL heating from that of other sources.
Better bounds of the CSL heating rates can likewise be found by just changing the charge state of a specific molecule, since heating due to CSL is independent on the charge state [cf. Eq. (
10
)], while this is not the case for electrical field fluctuations [cf. Eq. (
15
)]. With reference to Ref.
Brownnutt et al. 2015
, we estimate that the corresponding ion heating rates from noise sources other than CSL can ultimtely be as low as
10^{-5}\sim 10^{-6}
s
-1
.

Our proposal suggests the employment of a linear Paul trap with two ions for testing models of spontaneous collapse of the wavefunction. The two ions are chosen so that one allows for a large interaction with the collapse noise, while the other is employed for the initial cooling and the final measurement stages. The proposed method can be easily scaled to larger macromolecules, which will provide stronger insights to the collapse mechanism. Indeed, the limits of the proposal are only those related to the stability of the Paul trap, and the coherence time of the laser necessary for performing high efficiency readout of the common motion.

Derivation of the axial and radial motion of two ions in a linear Paul trap

A.1
The Linear Paul Trap

In this work, we focus on a linear Paul trap such as the one depicted in Fig.
5
. The trap consists of four rods, each of which is split in three electrodes as depicted on the figure. We define our coordinate system such that the
z
-axis runs along the center line of the linear Paul trap, while the
(x,y)
-axes are radial. We define
z_{0}
as half the length of the center electrodes and
r_{0}
as half the distance between diagonally opposed rods. A graphical depiction of
z_{0}
and
r_{0}
can be seen on Fig.
5
. When a DC voltage
V_{\text{end}}
is applied to all electrodes located at the end of the rods (commonly referred to as endcap electrodes), the electrical potential in the center region of the trap will be

\phi_{\text{DC}}(z)=\frac{\kappa V_{\text{end}}}{z_{0}^{2}}z^{2},

(17)

where
\kappa
is a unitless constant determined by the specific geometry of the trap. Thus, a positively charged ion of mass
m
and charge
q
, when placed at the center of the trap, undergoes to the following harmonic potential:

V_{\text{DC}}(z)=\frac{q\kappa V_{\text{end}}}{z_{0}^{2}}z^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{z}^{2}z^{2},

A.3
Multivariable Taylor expansion of the potential

To derive the normal modes along the axial and radial directions, we explicitly include the coordinates along the
x
and
y

x
and
y

y

x

The potential energy of the system is then described by

V(z_{1},y_{1},z_{2},y_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(m_{1}\omega_{1,z}^{2}z_{1}^{2}+m_{2}\omega_{2,z}^{2}z_{2}^{2}+m_{1}\omega_{1,r}^{2}y_{1}^{2}+m_{2}\omega_{2,r}^{2}y_{2}^{2})+\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}+(y_{1}-y_{2})^{2}}}.

(34)

We now assume that the ions show only small oscillations around their equilibrium positions
{\bf r}_{\text{eq},i}=(0,0,z_{\text{eq},i})
and that these can be described in terms
of time-dependent mass-weighted displacements
\zeta_{j}(t)/\sqrt{m_{j}}

(35)

Now, we Taylor-expand the potential to the second order around the equilibrium positions, so that one has

V\approx V_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{4}K_{ij}\zeta_{i}\zeta_{j},

(36)

where
V_{0}
is a negligible constant energy offset and
K_{ij}=\left(\left.\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial r_{i}\partial r_{j}}\right|_{\text{eq}}\right)/\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}
with
\{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4}\}=\{z_{1},z_{2},y_{1},y_{2}\}
are the coefficients of the matrix

K=\begin{pmatrix}K_{11}&K_{12}&0&0\\
K_{21}&K_{22}&0&0\\
0&0&K_{33}&K_{34}\\
0&0&K_{43}&K_{44}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(37)

The explicit form of the terms of

are

\displaystyle K_{11}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1,z}^{2}+\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{1}}\frac{2}{(z_{\text{eq},1}-z_{\text{eq},2})^{3}}=\omega_{1,z}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2}{1+1/Q}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle K_{12}

\displaystyle=K_{21}=-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}\frac{2}{(z_{\text{eq},1}-z_{\text{eq},2})^{3}}=-\frac{2\omega_{1}^{2}}{\sqrt{M}(1+1/Q)},

\displaystyle K_{22}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2,z}^{2}+\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{2}}\frac{2}{(z_{\text{eq},1}-z_{\text{eq},2})^{3}}=\omega_{1,z}^{2}\frac{Q}{M}\left(1+\frac{2}{1+Q}\right),

\displaystyle K_{33}

\displaystyle K_{34}

\displaystyle=K_{43}=\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}\sqrt{m_{1}m_{2}}}\frac{1}{(z_{\text{eq},1}-z_{\text{eq},2})^{3}}=-\frac{1}{2}K_{12},

\displaystyle K_{44}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2,r}^{2}-\frac{q_{1}q_{2}}{4\pi\epsilon_{0}m_{2}}\frac{1}{(z_{\text{eq},1}-z_{\text{eq},2})^{3}}=\omega_{2,r}^{2}-\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{M(1+1/Q)},

\displaystyle(\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\mp})^{2}=\frac{K_{33}+K_{44}\mp\sqrt{(K_{33}-K_{44})^{2}+4K_{34}^{2}}}{2}.

Appendix B
Light Matter Interaction

To assess any motional heating of this system, we propose to perform an excitation on the so-called first red sideband (i.e. using a laser with a frequency equal to the transition frequency minus the frequency of the addressed common motional mode
\nu
) with respect to the narrow transition. This can be also applied for sideband laser cooling, and thus used to initialize the two-ion system in the ground state of the specific mode. With the laser being on resonance of this sideband transition, the sideband excitation probability of the
138
Ba
+

(49)

where
\Omega_{0}
is the carrier Rabi frequency when the two-ion system is in the motional ground state,
\eta_{\nu,1}=kw_{\nu,1}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m_{1}\omega_{\nu}}},
with

is the Lamb Dicke parameter
Morigi and Walther 2001b
;
Wübbena et al. 2012
describing the coupling strength of the laser light to the motional mode
\nu
, and

is the wave vector of the laser light. For Eq. (
51
) to be valid it requires
\eta_{\nu,1}\ll 1
.
By choosing a readout pulse length
\tau_{\text{read}}
such that

\eta\Omega_{0}\tau_{\text{read}}=\pi\to\tau_{\text{read}}=\frac{\pi}{\eta\Omega_{0}},

(50)

we assure maximal electronic excitation of the
138
Ba
+

A laser detuned to the first red sideband will, however, be able to off-resonantly excite the
138
Ba
+
ion on the carrier transition when in the motional ground state. With the laser tuned to the first red sideband, it is at the same time always detuned by

The heating action of an electrical noise can be accounted by adding to

\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(t)=-\sum_{i}q_{i}{\bf E}_{t}\hat{\bf r}_{i},

\mathbb{E}[{\bf E}_{s}]=0

(56)

where

is the corresponding power spectral density along the direction
k

P_{\text{\tiny E}}
can be then computed using perturbation theory and it reads

P_{\text{\tiny E}}=\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}\sum_{\text{\tiny I}}\sum_{\text{\tiny F}}\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny I}}\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny F}}(\epsilon_{\text{\tiny F}}-\epsilon_{\text{\tiny I}})\mathbb{E}\left[|c_{\text{\tiny FI}}|^{2}\right],

(57)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny I}}
/
\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny F}}
are, respectively, the probability of the initial/final state being
\ket{\text{I}}
/
\ket{\text{F}}
, whose corresponding energies are
\epsilon_{\text{\tiny F}}
and
\epsilon_{\text{\tiny I}}
. Key to the calculation is the transition amplitude
c_{\text{\tiny FI}}
between the initial and final states, which reads

c_{\text{\tiny FI}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\,\braket{\text{F}|e^{\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{0}s}\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(s)e^{-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{0}s}|\text{I}}.

(58)

In particular, here we are interested in the transition between the ground state
\ket{0_{\kappa}}
(with
\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny I}}=1
) and the first excited state
\ket{1_{\kappa}}
(with
\mathcal{N}_{\text{\tiny F}}=1
) of the
\kappa
-th mode of the two ions. Since these states are eigenstates of
\hat{H}_{0}
, the above expression becomes

c_{\text{\tiny FI}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\,e^{\tfrac{i}{\hbar}(\epsilon_{\text{\tiny F}}-\epsilon_{\text{\tiny I}})s}\braket{\text{F}|\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(s)|\text{I}}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\,e^{{i}{}\omega_{\kappa}s}\braket{{1_{\kappa}}|\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(s)|{0_{\kappa}}}.

(59)

By substituting the expressions of
\hat{\bf r}_{i}
in terms of the normal modes’ ladder operators
\hat{u}_{\kappa}
and
\hat{u}_{\kappa}
[cf. Eq. (
5
)], one finds that

\braket{{1_{\kappa}}|\hat{H}_{\text{\tiny E}}(s)|{0_{\kappa}}}=\sum_{i}q_{i}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m_{i}}}\left[E_{s}^{(z)}\sum_{\nu}\frac{\delta_{\hat{a}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}}+(E_{s}^{(x)}+E_{s}^{(y)})\sum_{\nu}\frac{\delta_{\hat{b}_{\nu},\hat{u}_{\kappa}}}{\sqrt{\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}}}\right].

(60)

Employing such a result, we see that
\mathbb{E}[|c_{\text{\tiny FI}}|^{2}]
contains terms of the form

\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s^{\prime}\,e^{i\omega_{\kappa}(s-s^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}[E^{(k)}_{s}E^{(k^{\prime})}_{s^{\prime}}]=2\pi\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{d}\!\omega\,S_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(k)}(\omega)\left(\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)\right)^{2},

(61)

where we defined
\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)
such that

\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\,e^{i(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)s}=2\pi e^{i(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)t/2}\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega),

(62)

for which the following relations hold

\left(\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)\right)^{2}\sim\frac{t}{2\pi}\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega),\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\delta^{(t)}(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega)=\delta(\omega_{\kappa}-\omega).

(63)

Then, in the long-time limit, Eq. (
61
) reduces to

\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s\int_{0}^{t}\operatorname{d}\!s^{\prime}\,e^{i\omega_{\kappa}(s-s^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}[E^{(k)}_{s}E^{(k^{\prime})}_{s^{\prime}}]\sim\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\ t\ S_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(k)}(\omega_{\kappa}).

(64)

It follows that the energy gain due to electrical noises on the
\kappa
mode is given by

P_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(\kappa)}=\frac{\omega_{\kappa}}{2}\sum_{ij}\frac{q_{i}q_{j}}{\sqrt{m_{i}m_{j}}}\left[\sum_{\nu}\frac{S_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(z)}(\omega_{\kappa})}{\omega_{\text{Ax}}^{\nu}}\delta_{\hat{a}_{\nu},\hat{c}_{\kappa}}+\sum_{\nu}\frac{(S_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(x)}(\omega_{\kappa})+S_{\text{\tiny E}}^{(y)}(\omega_{\kappa}))}{\omega_{\text{Rad}}^{\nu}}\delta_{\hat{b}_{\nu},\hat{c}_{\kappa}}\right],

(65)

which corresponds to Eq. (
15
), once the
y
direction is neglected.
