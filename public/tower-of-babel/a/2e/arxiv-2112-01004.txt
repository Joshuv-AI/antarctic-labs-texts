Asymptotic stability of small bound state of nonlinear quantum walks
Masaya Maeda

Abstract
In this paper, we study the long time behavior of nonlinear quantum walks when the initial data is small in l 2 l^{2} . In particular, we study the case where the linear part of the quantum walk evolution operator has exactly two eigenvalues and show that the solution decomposed into nonlinear bound states bifurcating from the eigenvalues and scattering waves.

Introduction

Discrete time quantum walks, which we refer quantum walks (QWs) in this paper, are space-time discrete dynamics which can be considered as a quantum counter part of classical random walks
[
1
]
.
Quantum mechanics is linear, so at first glance it seems difficult to consider nonlinear QWs.
However, it is possible to realize QWs with classical waves, such as light using optical devices
[
7
,
30
]
, and by this reason it becomes possible to implement nonlinearity in QWs.
In fact, models of nonlinear QWs are proposed using nonlinear optical elements
[
45
]
and feed-forward mechanisms
[
52
]
.
In addition to experimental realizations, nonlinear QWs can be used to simulate nonlinear Dirac equations
[
34
,
39
]
and computing long time dynamics of wave packet spreading in nonlinear distorted media
[
64
]
.

As Dirac equations
[
19
]
(which are the continuous limit of QWs
[
2
,
16
,
39
]
) and discrete Schrödinger equations
[
12
,
18
,
31
,
46
]
(also called continuous time QWs), QWs are dispersive
[
38
,
36
,
59
]
in the sense that the
l^{\infty}
norm of the solution decreases while
l^{2}
norm of the solution conserves.
Given a nonlinear dispersive equation, it is natural to ask the existence and stability of bound state (also called soliton) solutions.
Recall that for KdV equation, which is one of the most famous nonlinear dispersive equation, solitons were found even before KdV equation itself
[
50
]
.
The importance of solitons can also be seen by soliton resolution conjecture
[
58
]
, which claims generic solutions decouple into scattering waves and solitons, as in RAGE theorem for the linear case
[
20
,
51
]
.
Indeed, many papers studying nonlinear QWs numerically observe solitonic behavior of the solution and focus on the study of its dynamics
[
8
,
9
,
17
,
34
,
37
,
45
,
63
]
.
For the stability analysis of bound states, related to the study of topological phases
[
3
,
4
,
10
,
11
,
28
,
29
,
40
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
, Gerasimenko, Tarasinski, and Beenakker
[
22
]
, followed by Mochizuki, Kawakami and Obuse
[
44
]
studied the linear stability of bound states bifurcating from linear bound states.

Motivated by the above results, the main results of this paper is the following:

•

Construct nonlinear bound states bifurcating from linear bound states (Proposition
1.6
).

•

Prove the nonlinear (dynamical) stability of nonlinear bound states which we have constructed (Theorem
1.13
).

Actually, we will prove stronger result in Theorem
1.13
.
That is, we will show the soliton resolution for small solutions (i.e. we show all small solutions decouple into a nonlinear bound state and scattering waves).
Such asymptotic stability results have been investigated for nonlinear bound states for nonlinear Schrödinger equations
[
24
,
27
,
42
,
56
]
, nonlinear Dirac equations
[
5
,
6
,
47
]
and discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equations
[
15
,
26
,
35
,
43
]
.
However, for discrete dynamical systems having dispersive properties, including QWs, we are not aware of such results.

1.2
Set up and main results

We define the shift operator
S\in\mathcal{L}(l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}))
by

\displaystyle Su(x):=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\uparrow}(x-1)\\
u_{\downarrow}(x+1)\end{pmatrix},\ \text{where}\ u(x)=\begin{pmatrix}u_{\uparrow}(x)\\
u_{\downarrow}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

Let
C:{\mathbb{Z}}\to U(2)
, where
U(2)
is the set of
2\times 2
unitary matrices.
A coin operator
\hat{C}\in\mathcal{L}(l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}))
associated to such map is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{C}u(x):=C(x)u(x).

By abuse of notation we identify
C
and
\hat{C}
and simply write
\hat{C}=C
.
Recall that general elements of
U(2)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle e^{{\rm i}\theta}\begin{pmatrix}\beta&\overline{\alpha}\\
-\alpha&\overline{\beta}\end{pmatrix},\ \theta\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}\text{ and }\ |\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1.

We assume:

Assumption 1.1
.

There exists
C_{\infty}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{\infty}&\overline{\alpha_{\infty}}\\
-\alpha_{\infty}&\overline{\beta_{\infty}}\end{pmatrix}\in U(2)
satisfying
0<|\alpha_{\infty}|<1

s.t.

\|C(\cdot)-C_{\infty}\|_{l^{1,1}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2}))}<\infty.

Remark 1.2
.

There is no loss of generality assuming
\theta_{\infty}:=\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\theta(x)=0
.

The linear time evolution operator
U\in\mathcal{L}(l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}))
of quantum walk is given by

\displaystyle U=SC.

Similarly, we set
U_{\infty}:=SC_{\infty}
.
We note that all
S,C,C_{\infty},U,U_{\infty}
are unitary operators.

We next introduce the nonlinear coin operator.
Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
be self-adjoint and
g\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{R}})
with
g(0)=0
.
Then, we set
N=N_{\gamma,g}:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
by

\displaystyle N(u):=e^{{\rm i}g(\left\langle u,\gamma u\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})\gamma}u.

(1.1)

As the coin operator,
N
will be used to express the (nonlinear) operator on
l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
The nonlinear coin operator given in the above form covers many nonlinear QWs appeared in the literature such as
[
34
,
45
]
.

Remark 1.3
.

The nonlinearity considered in
[
45
]
actually have the form

\displaystyle N(u)=e^{{\rm i}g_{1}(\left\langle u,\gamma_{1}u\right\rangle)\gamma_{1}}e^{{\rm i}g_{2}(\left\langle u,\gamma_{2}u\right\rangle)\gamma_{2}}u,\ \gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

with
g_{1}(s)=g_{2}(s)=s
,
and so it can be considered as having two nonlinear coins.
One can handle such nonlinearity by trivial modification.

The specific choice of the nonlinearity (
1.1
) is also natural in the sense that when we consider the continuous limit of nonlinear QWs, the limit becomes nonlinear Dirac equation having Hamiltonian, see e.g.
[
39
]
.
The above specific form is used in Lemma

to obtain the ”orthogonality” of the linearized operator (see, Lemma

An important example which do not fall in the above framework is the model considered in
[

,
44
]
, with the nonlinear coin given by

\displaystyle N(u)=e^{{\rm i}g(\left\langle u,\sigma_{3}u\right\rangle)\sigma_{2}}u,

where
\sigma_{j}

Study of such wider class of nonlinearity may be a good direction for the future research.

Remark 1.4
.

The assumption
g(0)=0
is not essential.
For general

, we can write
CN(u)=Ce^{g(0)\gamma}Ce^{{\rm i}\left(g(\left\langle u,\gamma u\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}^{2})-g(0)\right)\gamma}u
.
Thus, replacing
C
by

and

g(0)=0
.

The time evolution of nonlinear QW is given by the following recursion relation:

\displaystyle u(t+1)=UN(u(t)).

(1.2)

In other words, if
u\in l^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}},l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}))
satisfies (
1.2

is a solution of nonlinear QW (
1.2
).
Notice that we have
\|u(t)\|_{l^{2}}=\|u(0)\|_{l^{2}}
for all

.

Remark 1.5
.

In the context of quantum mechanics,
\|u\|_{l^{2}}^{2}
is the total probability so it is natural to consider the case
\|u\|_{l^{2}}=1
and such restriction makes no difference in the linear case.
However, in this paper we mainly consider the case
\|u\|_{l^{2}}\ll 1
, which is a natural starting point when we consider nonlinear problems.
We note that considering the nonlinear QW with the coin

\displaystyle N(u)=e^{{\rm i}g(\chi\left\langle u,\gamma u\right\rangle)\gamma}u,

and
\chi\ll 1
with
\|u\|_{l^{2}}=1
is equivalent to considering the same coin with
\chi=1
and
\|u\|_{l^{2}}\ll 1
.
Such tuning parameter

is widely used in the study of nonlinear QWs and our result can be considered as a study of dynamics in the weak nonlinear regime.
We will study the nonlinear QWs in strong nonlinear regime (
\|u\|_{l^{2}}
and
\chi
are not small) in the forthcoming paper.

Notice that for given

Before going to the explanation of the main theorem, we would like to introduce a symmetry of QWs.
Set the ”zig-zag” transform
Z\in\mathcal{L}(l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}))
by

\displaystyle Zu(x)=(-1)^{x}u(x).

It is easy to show

\displaystyle UZ=-ZU.

(1.6)

This symmetry is called Chiral symmetry in
[
53
,
28
]
, sublattice symmetry in
[
28
]
and stroboscopic sublattice factorization in
[

]
.
By (
1.6
), we conclude:

Proposition 1.10
.

\sigma(U)=-\sigma(U)
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(U)=-\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(U)
.
Moreover, if
\phi
is the eigenfunction of
U
associated to
e^{{\rm i}\lambda}\in\sigma_{\mathrm{d}}(U)
,
Z\phi
is the eigenfunction of
U
associated to
-e^{{\rm i}\lambda}
.

Since
Z^{2}=1
, and
Z
is self-adjoint,

\displaystyle P_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1\pm Z\right),

are orthogonal projections satisfying
P_{+}+P_{-}=1
,
P_{+}P_{-}=0
.
Further, we have

\displaystyle UP_{\pm}=P_{\mp}U\text{ and }\ N(P_{\pm}\cdot)=P_{\pm}N(\cdot).

Thus, letting
u(t;u_{0})
be the solution of nonlinear QW (
1.2
) with
u(0)=u_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle u(t;u_{0})=u(t;P_{+}u_{0})+u(t;P_{-}u_{0}),

with
u(t;P_{\pm}u_{0})=P_{\pm}u(t;P_{\pm}u_{0})
if

u(t;P_{\pm}u_{0})=P_{\mp}u(t;P_{\pm}u_{0})
if

is odd.
This implies that nonlinear QW (
1.2
) consists of two non-interacting dynamics.
In the following, we will only consider solutions with
u(0)=P_{+}u(0)
.
Further, if we understand the dynamics of
u(t)
with
t\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}
, then we can compute
u(t+1)
easily by (
1.2
).
Therefore, we will only consider
t\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Having this in mind, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(u):=UN(UN(u)),

and by retaking the time, we consider the following evolution equation

\displaystyle u(t+1)=\mathcal{U}(u(t)),\ u(0)=u_{0}\in l_{+}^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}):=P_{+}l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2}),

(1.7)

instead of (
1.2
).
Notice that
\Phi_{+}[z]:=P_{+}\Phi[z]
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\Phi_{+}[z])=e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z]}\Phi_{+}[z],

(1.8)

where
\Lambda_{+}[z]:=2\Lambda[z]
so
u(t)=e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z]t}\Phi_{+}[z]
is a solution of (
1.7
) for
z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(0,\delta_{0})
.

For the main result.
We need additional assumptions for the linear evolution operator
U
and the nonlinear term
N
.
First, for the nonlinear term, we assume:

Assumption 1.11
.

g^{\prime}(0)=g^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
.

Thus, the typical nonlinearity we have in mind is
g(s)=s^{3}
.

For the spectrum of

, we assume:

Assumption 1.12
.

U
is generic and have exactly two discrete spectrum.

An explicit example we have in mind is given in
[
32
]
where the coin is perturbed only at the origin.
We set
\pm e^{{\rm i}\lambda}
be the two discrete spectrum of
U
with eigenvectors
\phi
and
Z\phi
.
Then,
e^{2{\rm i}\lambda}
will be the unique discrete spectrum of
U^{2}
restricted on
l_{+}^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
We normalize the corresponding eigenvector
\phi_{+}=P_{+}\phi
so that
\|\phi_{+}\|_{l^{2}}=1
.
We set

\displaystyle P_{c}=1-(\cdot,\phi_{+})\phi_{+}.

We are now in the position to state our main theorem.

Theorem 1.13
.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
s.t. if
u_{0}\in B_{l^{2}_{+}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})}(0,\epsilon_{0})
, then there exist
\eta_{+}\in l^{2}_{+}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})
,
\rho\geq 0
and
z\in l^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}\cap[0,\infty),\mathbb{C})
s.t.

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\|u(t)-\Phi_{+}[z(t)]-U_{\infty}^{2t}\eta_{+}\|_{l^{2}}=0,

(1.9)

where
u(t)
is the solution of nonlinear QW (
1.7
) and moreover, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}|z(t)|=\rho\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}},\ \|\eta_{+}\|_{l^{2}}\lesssim\|P_{c}u_{0}\|_{l^{2}}.

(1.10)

The equality (

) states that all small solutions asymptotically become a sum of nonlinear bound state
\Phi_{+}[z]
with
|z|=\rho
and linear scattering wave
U_{\infty}^{2t}\eta_{+}
as expected from soliton resolution conjecture.
Moreover, from (
1.10

converges modulo phase, and the norm of the scattering wave is comparable to the continuous spectrum component
P_{c}u_{0}
of the initial data.

By some additional argument, one can show the ”orbital stability” of small nonlinear bound states of nonlinear QWs.
Here, the precise definition of orbital stability is given by the following:

Definition 1.14
.

Let
\Phi\in l^{2}
and
\Lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and suppose
e^{{\rm i}\Lambda t}\Phi
be a solution of nonlinear QWs (

).
We say
e^{{\rm i}\Lambda t}\Phi
(or simply just

, there exists

\sup_{t>0}\inf_{\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}}\|u(t)-e^{{\rm i}\theta}\Phi\|_{l^{2}}<\epsilon

Corollary 1.15
.

There exists
\epsilon_{0}
s.t. if
|z|<\epsilon_{0}
, then
e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z]}\Phi_{+}[z]

We now explain the technical hypothesis Assumption
1.11
and
1.12
.
First, if we set
g(s)=s^{p}
(putting aside the fact that
g
is not smooth when
p>0
is not an integer), the problem becomes more hard when we let
p
smaller.
This can be understood from the the trivial inequality
\epsilon^{p_{1}}<\epsilon^{p_{2}}
for
p_{1}>p_{2}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the embedding
l^{2}\hookrightarrow l^{\infty}
and the fact that we are considering small in
l^{2}
solutions.
Our assumption corresponds to
p\geq 3
in the above nonlinearity.
This restriction is needed to close the estimates using Strichartz estimate (Proposition
4.1
), which is a consequence of the dispersive estimate
\|U^{t}P_{c}u_{0}\|_{l^{\infty}}\lesssim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-1/3}\|u_{0}\|_{l^{\infty}}
(Proposition
4.1
).
Same constraint is given in the study of asymptotic stability for discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equations
[
15
,
26
]
, which have the same dispersive estimate
\|e^{{\rm i}tH_{d}}P_{c}u_{0}\|_{l^{\infty}}\lesssim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-1/3}\|u_{0}\|_{l^{1}}
, where
H_{d}=-\Delta_{d}+V
is the discrete Schrödinger operator.
For the nonlinear Schrödinger equations such restriction is relaxed to
p\geq 2

[
42
]
, due to the fact that in the continuous case, we have better decay
\|e^{{\rm i}tH}P_{c}u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}}
, where
H=-\Delta+V
is the Schrödinger operator, see
[
23
]
.

Since the cases
p=1,2
appear more naturally in physics, there are several attempts to lower
p
.
However, it seems that with current technology, the only way to lower
p
below 3 is to strengthen the assumption (such as taking
u_{0}\in l^{1,1}
) or weakening the result (such as showing the convergence
u(t)-\Phi[z]\to 0
in a compact domain).
For example, one of the former type result is
[
43
]
proving asymptotic stability for
p>2.75
for discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equations by adopting refined decay estimates of Mielke and Patz
[
41
]
.
On the other hand, one of the latter results is
[
14
]
proving the convergence in the exponentially weighted space for all
p>0
for nonlinear Schrödinger equation with delta potential using the virial argument developed in the important paper
[
33
]
.
For QWs, we are not aware of any viral type inequalities, thus the next step may be to prove asymptotic stability for
p=2
with additional assumption on the initial data.
The case
p=1
seems to be completely out of reach.
From the decay estimate, one expects that the scattering wave needs to be modified already for
p\leq 4/3
and so
p=1<4/3
is not even in the threshold of long range scattering.
Thus, such result will be very interesting even if we are considering the case with no bound states.

The assumption that
U
is generic (i.e.
U
has no edge resonance) is needed for local decay type estimate.
In this paper, following
[
15
]
we have used Kato smoothness for the

-in-time estimate.
Here, Kato smoothness for unitary operator is prepared and since it may have independent interest (for application to smooth scattering theory for QWs, say), we have given the equivalent conditions in the appendix of this paper, see Theorem
B.1
.

The assumption that
U
have precisely
2
eigenvalues is equivalent to
U^{2}
having precisely
1
eigenvalue on
l_{+}^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
When, there is no eigenvalue, one can show the scattering by the argument of
[
36
]
using the estimates given in
[
38
]
.
On the other hand, if
U^{2}
have more than two eigenvalues on
l_{+}^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, we expect that the quasi-periodic (in time) solutions which exist in the linear case will disappear due to the nonlinear Fermi Golden Rule
[
54
,
57
]
.
For results in this direction, see
[
13
]
and reference therein.

We next explain the strategy of the proof of the main results.
The proof of the existence of small nonlinear bound states is simple and we just use standard contraction mapping argument.
However, the explicit exponential decay rate for the eigenfunctions given in Proposition
2.1
seems not to have appeared in the literature.
Thus, even though the proof is standard, we have put the proof of Proposition
2.1
in the appendix of this paper since there may be some independent interest.

The proof of Theorem
1.13
consists by the following three steps:

1.

Introduce a modulation coordinate
(z,\eta)\mapsto u=\Phi_{+}[z]+\xi
,

2.

prepare linear estimates,

3.

and bound
\|\xi\|_{l^{p}([0,N]\cap{\mathbb{Z}},l^{q,\sigma})}
for some specific
p,q,\sigma
by induction in
N
,

as established in
[
56
]
(of course the induction in step 3 is replaced by continuity argument).

In this paper, step.2 is based on the dispersive estimate and the integral representation of the resolvent given in
[
38
]
.
This allows us to prepare all necessary estimates by standard duality argument and Christ-Kiselev lemma.
As noted above, for the Kato smoothness
\|U^{t}P_{c}u_{0}\|_{l^{2}l^{2,-\sigma}}\lesssim\|u_{0}\|_{l^{2}}
, we have provided equivalent condition in Theorem
B.1
and sufficient condition in corollary
B.3
in the appendix of this paper.
After the preparation of step 1 and 2, step 3 is more or less standard, we note that the idea using Kato smoothness type estimates comes from
[
15
]
.

We would like to emphasis that the novelty of the proof of Theorem
1.13
is in step 1.
Before explaining our case, we review the modulation argument in the continuous case following
[
24
]
.
First, let us consider the linear Schrödinger equation
{\rm i}\partial_{t}u=Hu
where the Schrödinger operator having exactly one negative eigenvalue (
-\lambda
).
In this case, it is obvious that the solution having the eigenfunction
\phi
as an initial data do not decrease because it will evolve as
e^{{\rm i}\lambda t}\phi
.
So, it is natural to decompose
u=z\phi+\xi
, where
z=(u,\phi)
and
\xi=u-(u,\phi)\phi=:P_{c}u
, where we have normalized
\phi
.
Since the continuous component
\xi
satisfies
{\rm i}\partial_{t}\xi=HP_{c}\xi
, we can use the decay estimates of
HP_{c}
to show the decay (or scattering) of
\xi
.
Next, we consider the nonlinear Schrödinger equation
{\rm i}\partial_{t}u=Hu+g(|u|^{2})u
having nonlinear bound states
e^{{\rm i}\lambda[z]t}\phi[z]
for small
z
(we have
\phi[z]=z\phi+o(z)
).
As the linear case, we would like to write
u=\phi[z]+\xi
.
The ”continuous part”
\xi
should be determined by some orthogonality condition expressed by a subspace
\mathcal{H}_{c}\subset L^{2}
, which should be similar to the linear case
P_{c}L^{2}
.
The linearized equation for
\xi
is

\partial_{t}\xi=L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]\xi,\quad\text{where}\quad L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]=-{\rm i}\left(H+g(|\phi(z)|^{2})+2g^{\prime}(|\phi(z)|^{2})\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(\phi(z)\overline{\cdot})\phi(z)\right).

Since,
L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]
is only
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear due to the complex conjugate
\bar{\cdot}
and depends on
z
, the ”continuous subspace”
\mathcal{H}_{c}=\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
should also depend on
z
and it is only required to be
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear.
The specific choice of the orthogonality condition given in
[
24
]
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]:=\{u\in L^{2}\ |\ \forall w\in\mathbb{C},\ \left\langle u,{\rm i}D\phi[z]w\right\rangle=0\},

(1.11)

where
D\phi[z]w=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon=0}\phi[z+{\rm i}w]
(notice that
D\phi[z]
is only
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear).
The continuous space”
\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
can also viewed as the symplectic orthogonal space of the soliton manifold with the symplectic form
\Omega=\left\langle\cdot,{\rm i}\cdot\right\rangle
or the condition to eliminate the first order term of
\xi
in the expansion of the Hamiltonian (or energy)
E(\phi[z]+\xi)
, see e.g.
[
35
]
.
In any case, the point of this choice is that
\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
is near
P_{c}L^{2}
in the sense that
\mathcal{H}_{c}[0]=P_{c}L^{2}
and moreover it is compatible with the linear evolution of
\xi
.
That is, if
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
, we have
\partial_{t}\xi=L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
.

We now come back to the nonlinear QWs.
The difficulty for QWs is that QWs seems not to be Hamilton equations (actually, the author do not even know what ”Hamilton equation” means for time discretized system like QWs).
The effect of the lack of Hamiltonian/Energy immediately appears in the study of orbital stability of bound states.
For Schrödinger and discrete Schrödinger equations the orbital stability of bound states can be proved by the fact that bound states are trapped by the energy under the mass constraint
[
49
,
21
]
.
However, for QWs we cannot use such argument because we are not aware of conservation quantity corresponding to the energy, and in this paper we have proved orbital stability as a consequence of asymptotic stability which give a sieve constraint in the nonlinearity as we have discussed above.
Now, going back to the problem how to choose the ”continuous space”, first linearizing the dynamics of nonlinear QWs, we have

\xi(t+1)=L[z(t)]\xi(t)
, where
L[z]=D\mathcal{U}(\Phi_{+}[z])
.
As the nonlinear Schrödinger equations, we want to have if
\xi(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z(t)]
, then
\xi(t+1)=L[z(t)]\xi(t)\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z(t+1)]
.
The point is that unlike the continuous time case, we cannot fix
t
and discuss the orthogonality condition at
z(t)
but we need two points
z(t)
and
z(t+1)
, where
z(t)
also evolve in nonlinear manner by the (yet unspecified) orthogonality condition applied to nonlinear QWs.
The first idea to overcome this difficulty is to replace
z(t+1)
by
e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z(t)]}z(t)
and treat the remainder
Z(t)=e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z(t)]}-z(t+1)
as an error.
Indeed, in the end we will be able to show
Z\in l^{1}
(see Corollary
5.6
).
Once we have replaced
z(t+1)
by
e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z(t)]}z(t)
, what we want becomes
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
implies
L[z]\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[e^{{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z]}z]
.
Although it is not clear the choice (
1.11
) works, this space have the property
\mathcal{H}_{c}[e^{{\rm i}\theta}z]=e^{{\rm i}\theta}\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
, so the problem becomes to show
\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]\Rightarrow e^{-{\rm i}\Lambda_{+}[z]}L[z]\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
.
This property, which is the second difficulty, can be proved by using the ”orthogonality” of
L[z]
with respect to the symplectic form
\left\langle\cdot,{\rm i}\cdot\right\rangle
.
That is, we prove
\left\langle L[z]\xi_{1},{\rm i}\xi_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{1},{\rm i}L[z]^{-1}\xi_{2}\right\rangle
.
This can be thought as an analog relation to the continuous case, where
L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]
is symmetric with respect to the symplectic form, that is
\left\langle L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]\xi_{1},{\rm i}\xi_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle\xi_{1},{\rm i}L_{\mathrm{cont}}[z]\xi_{2}\right\rangle
.
For this property we will rely on the special structure (
1.1
) of the nonlinearity.
It will be interesting to specify the class of gauge invariant nonlinearity which have the above property.
Therefore, although the choice of the continuous space did not have a good reasoning, our choice works and we can complete the modulation argument.

As discussed in the introduction, we set the continuous space
\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{c}[z]:=\{u\in l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},\mathbb{C}^{2})\ |\ \forall w\in\mathbb{C},\ \left\langle u,{\rm i}D\Phi_{+}[z]w\right\rangle=0\}.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists
\epsilon>0
s.t. for

Set

\displaystyle F(z,u):=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle u-\Phi_{+}[z],{\rm i}D\Phi_{+}[z]1\right\rangle\\
\left\langle u-\Phi_{+}[z],{\rm i}D\Phi_{+}[z]{\rm i}\right\rangle\end{pmatrix}.

By the definition of

, the problem is reduced to find
z

for given small

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial F(z,u)}{\partial(z_{R},z_{I})}\right|_{(z,u)=(0,0)}=\begin{pmatrix}\left\langle-D\Phi_{+}[0]1,{\rm i}D\Phi_{+}[0]1\right\rangle\ \left\langle-D\Phi_{+}[0]{\rm i},{\rm i}D\Phi_{+}[0]1\right\rangle\\

Thus, we have the conclusion.

\displaystyle\|e^{-\epsilon|t|}AU^{t}\varphi\|_{l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\|AK(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)^{2}\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},H)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\|AK(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{L}(H))}^{2}\|K(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},H)}^{2}

Now, by (
B.6
), we have

\displaystyle\|K(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},H)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\varphi,\left(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon)\right)\varphi\right)\,d\lambda=2\pi\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\|AK(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{L}(H))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|K(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)A^{*}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{L}(H))}^{2}=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H}=1}\|K(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)A^{*}\varphi\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H}=1}\left|\left(\varphi,A\left(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon)\right)A^{*}\varphi\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{T}}\|A(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon))A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}.

Here, we used the fact that
A(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon))A^{*}
is self-adjoint, which follows from (
B.1
).
Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\|e^{-\epsilon|t|}AU^{t}\varphi\|_{l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},H)}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sup_{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\mu\neq 0}\|A(R(\mu)-R(\overline{\mu}))A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}.

This implies

\displaystyle\|AU^{\cdot}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H,l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},H))}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\sup_{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\mu\neq 0}\|A(R(\mu)-R(\overline{\mu}))A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}.

Next, let
\mu
be the spectral measure for
U
w.r.t.
A^{*}\varphi
.
Then, for any interval
[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}=[0,2\pi)
, we have

\displaystyle\mu([a,b))=\left(A^{*}\varphi,1_{[a,b)}(U)A^{*}\varphi\right)\leq\left(\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}}{|b-a|}\right)\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}|b-a|.

Thus,
\mu
is absolute continuous.
Further, there exists
g
s.t.
d\mu=gd\zeta
with

\displaystyle\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leq\left(\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}}{|b-a|}\right)\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}=\left(\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}^{2}}{|b-a|}\right)\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}.

(B.13)

Thus, by Lemma
B.4
(if there exists an eigenfunction
\varphi
of
U
s.t.
\varphi\not\in\mathrm{Ker}A
, then r.h.s. of (
B.13
) is infinite so without loss of generality we can assume
A(1_{(a,b)}(U)+1_{[a,b]}(U))=A1_{[a,b)}(U)
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\varphi,A(R(\mu)-R(\bar{\mu}))A^{*}\varphi\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{{\rm i}(\zeta-\lambda)}e^{\epsilon}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{{\rm i}(\zeta-\lambda)}e^{-\epsilon}-1}\right)g(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{{\rm i}(\zeta-\lambda)}e^{\epsilon}-1}-\frac{1}{e^{{\rm i}(\zeta-\lambda)}e^{-\epsilon}-1}\right)\,d\zeta\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}^{2}}{|b-a|}\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}

(B.14)

\displaystyle=\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}}{|b-a|}\|\varphi\|_{H}^{2}.

Notice that the integrand in (
B.14
) is positive from (
B.2
) with
U=e^{{\rm i}\zeta}
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\sup_{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\mu\neq 0}\|A(R(\mu)-R(\overline{\mu}))A^{*}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}\leq\sup_{[a,b)\subset\mathbb{T}}\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}^{2}}{|b-a|}.

Finally, from Stone’s formula (
B.5
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|A1_{[a,b)}(U)\|_{\mathcal{L}(H)}^{2}}{|b-a|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|b-a|}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H},\|\psi\|_{H}=1}\left(A^{*}\psi,1_{[a,b)}(U)\varphi\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{|b-a|}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H},\|\psi\|_{H}=1}\left(\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\int_{a}^{b}\left(A^{*}\psi,(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon)\varphi)\right)\,d\lambda\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H}=1}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\|A\left(R(\lambda+{\rm i}\epsilon)-R(\lambda-{\rm i}\epsilon)\right)\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T},H)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|\varphi\|_{H}=1}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\|e^{-\epsilon|\cdot|}AU^{\cdot}\varphi\|_{l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},H)}^{2}=\|AU^{\cdot}\varphi\|_{\mathcal{L}(H,l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}},H))}^{2}.

Therefore, we have the conclusion.
∎

Combining Lemmas
B.6
and
B.7
, we have Theorem
B.1
.
