Uncertainty Quantification in Case of Imperfect Models:
A Review

Abstract




December 11, 2020

Abstract

Uncertainty quantification of complex technical systems
is often based on a computer model of the system.
As all models such a computer model is always wrong in the sense that it does not describe the reality
perfectly. The purpose of this article is to give
a review of techniques which use observed values
of the technical systems in order to take into account
the inadequacy of a computer model in uncertainty
quantification. The techniques reviewed in this article
are illustrated and compared by applying them to
applications in mechanical engineering.

Key words and phrases:

Estimated input distributions, imperfect models, improved surrogate models, uncertainty quantification.

1
Introduction

Uncertainty quantification is a major research field of statistical methods with applications in engineering sciences.
Methods of uncertainty quantification are often used to analyse experiments with technical systems.
These experiments can be described by
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
-valued (random) variables
(X,Y)
, where
X
describes input parameters of the experiments and
Y
describes the outcome of the experiment.
E.g. if one considers drop tests of a spring damper as described in Subsection
2.1
the experimental outcome depends on the drop height, i.e. in this case the input dimension
d
is equal to
1
and the measured outcome is the maximal relative compression.

Usually to conduct real experiments is expensive and time consuming. To circumvent this problem computer models that simulate the experiment with the technical system are playing crucial role.

Santner et al., 2018
and
Fang et al., 2006
provide an overview of methods for the design and analysis of experiments conducted with these computer models.

Computer models are often computationally expensive and thus it is not possible to conduct a large quantity of computer experiments. To circumvent this problem one can use so-called surrogate models.
There exists different methods to estimate a surrogate model, e.g.
Bucher and Bourgund, 1990
,
Kim and Na, 1997
.
Das and Zheng, 2000
used quadratic response surfaces.
Hurtado, 2004
,
Deheeger and Lemaire, 2010
,
Bourinet et al., 2011
investigated surrogate models in context of support vector machines,
Papadrakakis and Lagaros, 2002
concentrated on neural networks,
Kaymaz, 2005
and
Bichon et al., 2008
used kriging.
Usually these surrogate models do not take an inherent error of the computer model into account and thus are themself imperfect in case of an imperfect computer model.

There is a number of different objectives in methods of uncertainty quantification. The main objectives are computer model calibration, computer model validation and quantifying the results of an experiment.

Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
and
Han et al., 2009
proposed a Bayesian method for computer model calibration and model validation based on Gaussian processes,
Higdon et al., 2013
proposed a similar approach as
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
, but used the ensemble Kalman filter.
Plumlee, 2017
also introduced a Bayesian computer model calibration method similar to the approach of
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
, but their prior distribution on the bias (model discrepancy) is modeled orthogonal to the gradient of the computer model.

Bayarri et al., 2007
proposed a validation method in case of time dependent systems, where they model the discrepancy between the computer experiments and the outcome of the technical system by a Gaussian process.

Wang et al., 2009
proposed a Bayesian method for model validation.

Gu and Wang, 2018
proposed the so-called scaled Gaussian Process and use it for model calibration. They claim, that the method bridges the gap between the
L_{2}
calibration and the Gaussian process calibration.

Damblin et al., 2018
proposed a method for computer model calibration based on perfect and imperfect computer models.

Tuo and Wu, 2015
proposed the
L_{2}
calibration model, to identify unknown computer model parameter. They also pointed out that the Bayesian approach of
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
might fail in case of an imperfect computer model, since there exists no values of the parameters which fit the technical system perfectly.

Plumlee, 2019
proposed a frequentist method for computer model calibration by constructing confidence intervals for the model parameter.

Based on perfect computer models,
Devroye et al., 2013
and
Bott et al., 2015
derived consistency and rate of convergence results for density estimators based on surrogate models,
Felber et al., 2015a
and
Felber et al., 2015b
proposed a method for the adaptive choice of smoothing parameters for density estimators based on surrogate models and
Kohler and Krzyżak, 2019
proposed a method to estimate quantiles based on surrogate models.

The articles mentioned above either assume that the underlying computer model is perfect, or only deal with computer model calibration. Normally, computer models are imperfect, i.e. they do not predict the outcome of real experiments perfectly, e.g. because of a relaxation of underlying physical dependencies (typically neglecting the friction or by considering it to be constant) to reduce complexity or because of missing knowledge about the technical system.
In this article we give an overview of existing methods based on imperfect computer models with the following goals.
The first aim is to construct an improved surrogate model and based on that quantify the outcome of the experiment
Y
either by density or quantile estimation. The second is to quantify the model error and thus enable the comparison of different computer models. The last aim is to quantify the influence of the computer model error on the quantification of
Y
.

1.1
Notation

Throughout this paper we use the following notation:

\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}
are the sets of positive integers, real numbers, and nonnegative real numbers, respectively.
For
z\in\mathbb{R}
we denote the smallest integer greater than or equal to
z
by
\lceil z\rceil
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote the
i
-th component of
x
by
x^{(i)}
.
If
X
is a random variable, then
{\mathbf{P}}_{X}
is the corresponding distribution, i.e., the measure associated with the random variable.
Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a real-valued function defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We write

x=\arg\min_{z\in D}f(z)
if
\min_{z\in{\mathcal{D}}}f(z)
exists and if
x
satisfies

x\in D\quad\mbox{and}\quad f(x)=\min_{z\in{\mathcal{D}}}f(z).

If
A
and
B
are sets, with
A\subseteq B
. then
I_{A}\colon B\to\{0,1\}
is the indicator function corresponding to
A
, i.e. the function which takes on the value 1 on A and is 0 elsewhere.

2.1
Modular active spring damper system

The modular active spring damper system (German acronym MAFDS) shown in Figure
1
is a suspension system that was designed with similar specifications and requirements as an air plane landing gear, although it is not a landing gear substitute.
It was developed in the collaborative research centre SFB 805 at the Technische Universität Darmstadt in order to investigate uncertainty in a load-bearing structural system when predicting the dynamic response.

Figure 1:
A photo of the MAFDS and its experimental test setup.

Its main components are an upper truss structure, a lower truss structure, guidance links that enable relative translation of the truss structures in vertical direction and a spring-damper. A detailed system description can be found in
Feldmann and Platz, 2019
and
Mallapur and Platz, 2019

Dynamic drop tests can be carried out similar to landing gear testing: the MAFDS is lifted up and dropped by a variable drop height
h

In a total of
100

0.05~\mathrm{m}
and a standard deviation of
0.0057~\mathrm{m}

As system output we regard the maximal relative compression
z_{\mathrm{r,max}}

Modelling of the system at hand yielded two different computer models to compute the maximum relative compression
z_{\mathrm{r,max}}

With both computer models we conduct
500
simulations each and furthermore generate
16.000.000
additional input values that will be subsequently used in this paper.

3
Data models

In an application, usually one of the following two different data models are available:

(i)

Full information model
:
In this case, a computer simulation
m\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the experiment with the technical system is available and can be evaluated at arbitrary values, an experimental data set

(X_{1},Y_{1}),\ldots,(X_{n},Y_{n})

(1)

of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
is available and the underlying distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{X}
of
X
is known thus one can generate an additional data set

X_{n+1},\ldots,X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}+N_{1,n}}

(2)

of additional
L_{n}+N_{1,n}+N_{2,n}
input values.

(ii)

Experimental setup model
: In this case, a computer simulation
m\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of the experiment with the technical system and the experimental data set (
1
) of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
are available. Here the underlying distribution
{\mathbf{P}}_{X}
of
X
is unknown.

In the following section we will describe a method to generate an additional sample of input values.
Thus, to simplify the presentation we will assume that the full information model is available for the description of the methods.
Note that for the MAFDS, cf. Subsection
2.1
, data model (i) is available and for the piezo-beam, cf. Subsection
2.2
, data model (ii) is available.

3.1
Generating additional input values

To apply methods of uncertainty quantification, often a large sample of input data is necessary. In some settings (especially if the input data is random as well as the outputs) only a small data set of input values is available. In this case it is necessary to generate more input data. But this is sometimes nearly as expensive and time consuming as producing more output data, e.g. in the application from Subsection
2.2
one has to produce a new disk spring and measure the four stiffness parameters and the height parameter. Thus generating more experimental input data is often not possible.

The randomness in input data is often induced by production margins or measurement errors and thus making a realistic assumption on the underlying class of distributions is often possible. A variety of methods for data generation in case of known distributions is given in
Devroye, 1986
. In the following, the in
Kersting and Kohler, 2019
proposed method to generate multivariate normally distributed random variables is described:

Assume
X
is a multivariate normally distributed random variable with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore assume that an independent and identically distributed sample

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

(3)

of
X
is available.

Then estimate the parameters of the distribution of
X
by a maximum likelihood estimate defined by

\hat{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

(4)

and

\hat{\Sigma}=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(X_{k}^{(i)}-\hat{\mu}^{(i)})(X_{k}^{(j)}-\hat{\mu}^{(j)})\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq d}.

(5)

The estimated values are then treated as if they were the real values.
Then one can generate a sample of size
N_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
which is independent and multivariate normally distributed with mean
\hat{\mu}
and covariance
\hat{\Sigma}
by firstly generating an independent sample
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{N_{n}}
of
d
-dimensional vectors, where for each vector the components are independent and standard normally distributed, and set for every
i=1,\ldots,N_{n}

\bar{X}_{i}=\hat{O}\hat{\Lambda}^{1/2}Z_{i}+\hat{\mu},

(6)

where
\hat{O}
and
\hat{\Lambda}
are defined by the eigendecomposition

\hat{\Sigma}=\hat{O}\hat{\Lambda}\hat{O}^{T}

of
\hat{\Sigma}
. Here
\hat{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{d})
is a diagonal matrix consisting of eigenvalues of
\hat{\Sigma}
and
\hat{O}
is a orthogonal matrix whose columns are eigenvectors of
\hat{\Sigma}
.

4
Uncertainty quantification based on improved surrogate models

As discussed above, computer models are usually imperfect. In this section, a method to estimate an improved surrogate model and it’s application in uncertainty quantification is described.

4.1
Estimating an improved surrogate model

Computer simulations of experiments with a technical system are often complex and computationally expensive to evaluate.
But for a proper analysis of the underlying technical system it is necessary to generate a large sample of these computer experiments.
In addition, the inherent error in the computer simulation, cf. Section
1
, will bias the results.
To circumvent these problems, a solution is to compute an improved surrogate model and use it instead of the computer simulation.

The first step is to generate a sample of input values evaluated with the computer simulation.

Kohler and Krzyżak, 2017
and
Götz et al., 2018
used a sample of the input quantity
X
,
Wong et al., 2017
suggested to use data generated in a specific range by Latin Hypercube sampling (see
McKay et al., 1979
), if the input data is not random, and
Kersting and Kohler, 2019
used a sample uniformly distributed on a centered cube to construct an estimator of a computer model.

The second step is to estimate a surrogate model of the computer simulation. There exists a vast variety of methods, some already mentioned above. One possibility is to use (penalized) least squares estimates, defined by

\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{f\in{\cal F}}\frac{1}{L_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L_{n}}|f(X_{i})-m(X_{i})|^{2}+pen_{n}^{2}(f),

(7)

where
{\cal F}
is a set of functions,
(X_{1},m(X_{1})),\ldots,(X_{L_{n}},m(X_{L_{n}}))
is a set of input values evaluated with the computer model of size
L_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
pen_{n}^{2}(\cdot)
is a penalty term which usually penalizes the roughness of the function and which is positive for each
f\in{\cal F}
, i.e.
pen_{n}^{2}(f)\geq 0
.
If the input dimension is smaller or equal to
3
than for example smoothing spline estimates can be applied as shown in
Kohler and Krzyżak, 2017
. For bigger input dimensions neural network estimates can be used as in
Götz et al., 2018
and
Kersting and Kohler, 2019
.

As discussed, usually every computer model has an inherent error.
To improve the surrogate model an estimator of the residuals can be constructed by first calculating the residuals of the surrogate model on the experimental data

\epsilon_{i}=Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{i})\quad(i=1,\ldots,n)

(8)

and by applying then a (penalized) least squares estimate to this sample, i.e. by computing

\hat{m}_{n}^{\epsilon}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{f\in\bar{{\cal F}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(X_{i})-\epsilon_{i}|^{2}+pen_{n}^{2}(f),

(9)

where
\bar{{\cal F}}
is a set of functions and
pen_{n}^{2}(\cdot)
is a penalty term for which
pen_{n}^{2}(f)\geq 0
holds, for every
f\in\bar{{\cal F}}
.

In the last step, the improved surrogate model is defined by

\hat{m}_{n}(x)=\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(x)+\hat{m}_{n}^{\epsilon}(x)\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}).

(10)

In case that only a small sample of experimental data is available, i.e.
n
is small, the residual estimator usually does not yield sufficient results. Then one can use a weighted (penalized) least squares estimate instead, defined by

\hat{m}_{n}^{\epsilon}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{f\in\bar{{\cal F}}}\left(\frac{w^{(n)}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(X_{i})-\epsilon_{i}|^{2}+\frac{(1-w^{(n)})}{N_{1,n}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{1,n}}|f(X_{i})-0|^{2}+pen_{n}^{2}(f)\right),

(11)

where
w^{(n)}\in[0,1]
is a weighting term, which should be chosen data dependent and
X_{n+L_{n}+1},\ldots,X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}}
is a set of additional input values of size
N_{1,n}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that in (
7
) and (
9
) different sets of functions are used. In an application this is usually due to different chosen function class parameters.

4.2
Density estimation based on an improved surrogate model

One approach to quantifying the uncertainty in the outcome of an experiment with a technical system is to estimate the underlying density of
Y
.
If the density
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is known, we can calculate the probability
{\mathbf{P}}\{Y\in B\}
for any given set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
by

\int_{B}g(x)\;dx.

(12)

In the following section we will describe a method to construct an estimator
\hat{g}
of the density
g
of
Y
.
A simple approach is to apply the kernel density estimate of
Rosenblatt, 1956
and
Parzen, 1962
defined by

g(y)=\frac{1}{n\cdot h_{n}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left(\frac{y-Y_{i}}{h_{n}}\right).

(13)

Usually in an application the sample size of experimental data will be too small to achieve satisfying results.
Instead one can use an improved surrogate model as described in Subsection
4.1
.

Kohler and Krzyżak, 2017
,
Götz et al., 2018
and
Kersting and Kohler, 2019
proposed similar methods to construct a density estimate based on an improved surrogate model, where the main difference is that the first two assumed that the full information model is available, where the last assumed that the experimental setup model is given and that
Kohler and Krzyżak, 2017
used smoothing spline estimates, where
Götz et al., 2018
and
Kersting and Kohler, 2019
used least squares neural network estimates to construct the improved surrogate model.
Summarizing, the method works as follows:

Estimate an improved surrogate model as discussed in Subsection
4.1
.
Next apply a kernel density estimator to an independent and identically distributed sample of
(X,\hat{m}_{n}(X))
of size
N_{2,n}\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. to

\hat{g}_{N_{2,n}}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{2,n}\cdot h_{N_{2,n}}}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2,n}}K\left(\frac{y-\hat{m}_{n}(X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}+i})}{h_{N_{2,n}}}\right),

(14)

where
h_{N_{2,n}}>0
so-called bandwidth and
K\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the kernel function are parameters of the estimate.
To choose the parameter
h_{N_{2,n}}
, a data dependent approach can be used as shown in
Felber et al., 2015a
or
Felber et al., 2015b
.

4.3
Quantile estimation based on an improved surrogate model

Another approach to quantify the uncertainty in the outcome of an experiment with a technical system is to estimate its
\alpha
-quantile, defined by

q_{Y,\alpha}=\inf\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:G(y)\geq\alpha\right\},

(15)

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
G
is the cumulative distribution function of
Y
given by

G(y)={\mathbf{P}}\left\{Y\leq y\right\}.

(16)

A simple and straight-forward approach is to use a Monte Carlo estimator.
Enss et al., 2016
and
Kohler and Krzyżak, 2018
proposed to use an estimator based on a surrogate model, where they assumed that the computer simulation fits the reality perfectly. As discussed before, a computer simulation is almost always imperfect and thus a simple modification is to use an improved surrogate model.
The estimator is then constructed as follows:

Estimate an improved surrogate model as discussed in Subsection
4.1
.
Next apply a Monte Carlo quantile estimator to an independent and identically distributed sample of
(X,\hat{m}_{n}(X))
of size
N_{2,n}\in\mathbb{N}
, defined by

\hat{q}_{\hat{m}_{n}(X),N_{2,n},\alpha}=\inf\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\hat{G}_{\hat{m}_{n}(X),N_{2,n}}(y)\geq\alpha\right\},

(17)

where

\hat{G}_{\hat{m}_{n}(X),N_{2,n}}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{2,n}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2,n}}I_{(-\infty,m_{n}(X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}+i})]}(y).

(18)

Note that this is equivalent to choosing the
\lceil N_{2,n}\cdot\alpha\rceil
biggest value of
\hat{m}_{n}(X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}+i})
,
\ldots
,
\hat{m}_{n}(X_{n+L_{n}+N_{1,n}+N_{2,n}})
.

5
Comparison of computer models

In this section methods which can be used to compare different computer simulations are described.

5.1
Comparison of computer models via cumulative distribution functions

Roy and Oberkampf, 2011
introduced a method to detect model form uncertainty. The term model form is in their context the result of all assumptions, conceptualizations, abstractions, approximations, and mathematical formulations on which the model relies. Consequently an imperfect model is from their perspective the result of wrong assumptions in the model form. To detect uncertainty in the model form, they proposed the so-called Area Validation Metric (AVM). The metric compares the cumulative density function of numerical simulated data and of experimental measurements. It uses a sample of experimental and computer simulated data

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad m(X_{n+1}),\ldots,m(X_{n+L_{n}})

(19)

of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
L_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, where as described in Section
3
,
m\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to
is a computer simulation which is designed to emulate an experiment with a technical system and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the corresponding input for the experiment and
Y\in\mathbb{R}
is the output.
Based on (
19
) the cumulative density functions are estimated by the empirical distribution function defined by

\hat{F}_{Y}(t)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{(-\infty,Y_{i}]}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{F}_{m(X)}(t)=\frac{1}{L_{n}}\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+L_{n}}I_{(-\infty,m(X_{i})]}(t).

(20)

Finally the AVM is calculated by

\int|\hat{F}_{Y}(t)-\hat{F}_{m(X)}(t)|dt.

(21)

5.2
A Bayesian approach for quantifying the computer model error

Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
proposed a Bayesian method for the calibration of computer models in case that the
underlying mathematical model is imperfect. They assume that their data is given by

Y=m(\theta,x)+\delta(x)+\epsilon

(22)

where
m(\cdot,\cdot)
describes the computer model depending on some parameter
\theta
and some vector
x
describing uncertain factors of the reality, where
\delta
is the model error and
\epsilon
is a normally distributed error with expectation zero which contains for instance measurement errors. They propose Bayesian techniques for the choice of the optimal parameter value
\theta^{*}
(so called calibration), which also model the model error
\delta
and are therefore able to quantify the model error of the
computer model. In the sequel we assume that our system is already calibrated in the sense that our computer model is based on some physical model for which we have adjusted the parameters already to reality based on separate experiments with our technical system. In this case the model of
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
simplifies to

Y=m(x)+\delta(x)+\epsilon.

(23)

Here
m(\cdot)
is the (already calibrated) computer model,
\delta
is the discrepancy term of this model, and
\epsilon
is a random normally distributed error with expectation zero and variance
\lambda
.
In addition
x
is (a possible random) value from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
describing input parameters of the experiment with the technical
system (e.g., drop height in experiments with the MAFDS, cf. Subsection
2.1
).

From model (
23
) it is assumed that
n
observations are observed, i.e., the observed data is

(X_{1},m(X_{1}),Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},m(X_{n}),Y_{n})

(24)

where

Y_{i}=m(X_{i})+\delta(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n).

(25)

Here
\epsilon,\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}

are independent and identically distributed.

Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
uses a Gaussian process with mean function
\mu(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and covariance function
c(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
to model the discrepancy term
\delta(\cdot)
.
This means that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

the vector

(\delta(X_{1}),\delta(X_{2}),\dots,\delta(X_{k}))^{T}

is multivariate normally distributed with mean vector
(\mu(X_{1}),\dots,\mu(X_{k}))^{T}

and covariance matrix
(c(X_{i},X_{j}))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}
.
The random error is chosen normally distributed with mean zero
and variance
\lambda
. So as soon as

\lambda
, the mean function
\mu(\cdot)
and the covariance function
c(\cdot,\cdot)

are chosen, the model (
23
) is completely specified
for given
x
.

In order to specify
\mu(\cdot)
and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
,
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
propose to use a hierarchical Bayesian model. Here the choice of
\mu(\cdot)
and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
could be based on prior knowledge about the technical system, but since this is difficult to incorporate in this model they suggest to use the following simple choice:
First prior densities for the parameters of the hierarchical Bayesian model are chosen from a parametric model. Therefore prior knowledge (of the technical system) can be used to choose these densities, or if such knowledge is not available, normal distributions or a choice of the form

p(t)=c\cdot\frac{1}{t},

(26)

with parameter
c\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and where
p(t)
must be set to zero for large
t
, is recommended.

In a second step they choose
\mu(\cdot)
as a constant function

\mu(x)=\beta\quad(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d})

(27)

and use for
c(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
the definition
c(z_{1},z_{2})=\sigma^{2}R(z_{1}.z_{2})
, where

R(z_{1},z_{2})=\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\omega_{j}\cdot(z_{1}^{(j)}-z_{2}^{(j)})^{2}\right)\quad(z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}).

(28)

As prior distributions they recommend normal distributions for
\lambda
and
\beta
, with mean and variance parameters
\mu_{\lambda},\sigma^{2}_{\lambda},\mu_{\beta}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\beta}
, and for all other parameters they recommend to use
(
26
), with parameters
c_{\sigma^{2}},c_{\omega_{1}},\ldots,c_{\omega_{d}}
.
Thus the hierarchical Bayesian model uses parameters

{\cal P}^{(1)}=\{\lambda,\beta,\sigma,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}\},

(29)

where the vector of parameters is a realization of a random variable

(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d}),

(30)

with parametric densities

p_{\Lambda}(\cdot),\ldots,p_{\Omega_{d}}(\cdot),

(31)

with distribution parameters

{\cal P}^{(2)}=\{\mu_{\Lambda},\sigma^{2}_{\Lambda},\mu_{B},\sigma^{2}_{B},c_{\Sigma^{2}},c_{\Omega_{1}},\ldots,c_{\Omega_{d}}\}.

(32)

Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
recommended to choose fixed values for the parameters in
{\cal P}^{(2)}
whenever possible, e.g. from information about the physical system, to reduce complexity.

The next step is to compute the posterior density of
(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})
given the data (
24
). From the
above definitions we get that the conditional density
of
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
given
(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})=(\lambda,\beta,\sigma,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})
is given by

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptf_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})|(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})=(\lambda,\beta,\sigma,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\cdot det(\Theta)}}\cdot\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\cdot(y_{1}-m(X_{1})-\beta,\dots,y_{n}-m(X_{n})-\beta)\cdot\Theta^{-1}\cdot

\displaystyle\hskip 156.49014pt(y_{1}-m(X_{1})-\beta,\dots,y_{n}-m(X_{n})-\beta)^{T}\Big)

(33)

where

\Theta=\left(c(X_{k},X_{l})+\lambda\cdot 1_{\{k=l\}}\right)_{1\leq k,l\leq n},

from which we can conclude that we have

\displaystyle\hskip-17.07164ptf_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})=

(34)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt\int\!\!\ldots\!\!\int f_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})|(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})=(\lambda,\beta,\sigma,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\cdot p_{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdots p_{\Omega_{d}}(\omega_{d})\,d\lambda\dots d\omega_{d}

and

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptf_{(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})|(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})}(\lambda,\dots,\omega_{d})=

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{f_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})|(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})=(\lambda,\beta,\sigma,\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\cdot p_{\Lambda}(\lambda)\cdot\dots\cdot p_{\Omega_{d}}(\omega_{d})}{f_{(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})}(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})},

(35)

where
p_{\Lambda}(\cdot),\ldots,p_{\Omega_{d}}(\cdot)
, are the above specified prior densities with the corresponding parameters
{\cal P}^{(2)}
.
Note that the value of (
5.2
) also depends on the values of the distribution parameters
{\cal P}^{(1)}
.

Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
propose next to estimate the parameters
{\cal P}^{(1)}\cup{\cal P}^{(2)}
by a maximum likelihood estimate and threat the estimated values as if they were the real values, i.e. they proposed to compute

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal P}}^{(1)}\cup\hat{{\cal P}}^{(2)}=\arg\max_{{\cal P}^{(1)}\cup{\cal P}^{(2)}}f_{(\Lambda,B,\Sigma,\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{d})|(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})}(\lambda,\dots,\omega_{d}),

(36)

where
\hat{{\cal P}}^{(1)}\cup\hat{{\cal P}}^{(2)}
are the resulting estimates of all parameters included in
{\cal P}^{(1)}\cup{\cal P}^{(2)}
.
To compute this estimate one can use a numerical (approximative) maximation of the posterior density (where a maximum with respect
to
2d+8
variables has to be computed).
To reduce the complexity in solving (
36
) one can also complete the square for
B
in (
5.2
)
to find

\hat{\beta}=\frac{(1,\ldots,1)\cdot\Theta^{-1}\cdot(y_{1}-m(X_{1}),\ldots,y_{n}-m(X_{n}))^{T}}{(1,\ldots,1)\cdot\Theta^{-1}\cdot(1,\ldots,1)^{T}}.

Then the above maximum has only be computed with respect to
2d+5
variables, since if
\hat{\beta}
is known, we do not need to estimate it’s distribution parameters
\mu_{B},\sigma_{B}^{2}
.
Alternatively one can simplify the computation by choosing for some of the above variables plausible estimates, e.g.,
B
can be estimated by

\hat{\beta}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-m(X_{i})).

(37)

As soon as we have these estimate we know the distribution
of the error

(Y_{i}-m(X_{i}))_{i=1,\dots,n}=(\delta(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n},

which is a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector

(\hat{\beta},\dots,\hat{\beta})^{T}
and covariance matrix

\left(\hat{\sigma}^{2}\cdot\exp\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\hat{\omega}_{j}\cdot(X_{k}^{(j)}-X_{l}^{(j)})^{2}\right)+\hat{\lambda}\cdot 1_{\{k=l\}}\right)_{1\leq k,l\leq n}.

From this result we can, e.g., estimate quantiles of the absolute
model error

(|\delta(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i}|)_{i=1,\dots,n}

occurring in the data (
24
) via Monte Carlo in order to quantify the model error
of our simulation model.

5.3
Quantifying the computer model error via bootstrap

Wong et al., 2017
proposed the following method for quantifying the the model error using a bootstrap approach:

First estimate a surrogate model
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}
by (
7
) as described in Subsection
4.1
. Next to quantify the model error,
B
bootstrap samples are generated. Therefore compute the residuals as in Subsection
4.1
by (
8
) and generate the bootstrap samples
(X_{1,b},\epsilon_{1,b})
,
\dots
,
(X_{n,b},\epsilon_{n,b})

(b=1\ldots,B)
by sampling randomly and
with replacement

n
values of
(X_{1},\epsilon_{1})
,
\ldots
,
(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})
.

Next we split the bootstrap samples in two parts, a learning and an evaluation sample. Therefore we choose a
n_{l}\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Then we construct
B
estimators of the residuals
\hat{m}_{n_{l},b}^{\epsilon}

(b=1\ldots,B)
by (
9
), where we replace
(X_{1},\epsilon_{1})
,
\ldots
,
(X_{n},\epsilon_{n})
by
(X_{1,b},\epsilon_{1,b})
,
\dots
,
(X_{n_{l},b},\epsilon_{n_{l},b})
.
Since we only use a part of the experimental data to construct the residual estimators this can lead to unsatisfying results, if the sample size is too small as discussed in Subsection
4.1
. In this case we recommend to use the weighted version of the estimator instead, as defined in (
11
).

For each bootstrap sample we estimate the
\alpha
-quantile
q_{|\epsilon|,\alpha}
of the absolute model error on the remaining input values evaluated with the bootstrap residual estimator. The
\alpha
-quantile estimator is then defined by

\hat{q}_{|\hat{m}_{n_{l},b}^{\epsilon}(X)|,n-n_{l},\alpha}=\min\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\frac{1}{n-n_{l}}\sum_{i=n_{l}+1}^{n}I_{\{|\hat{m}_{n_{l},b}^{\epsilon}(X_{i})|\leq y\}}\geq\alpha\right\}.

(38)

With this estimator one can compare two computer models by comparing the median of their bootstrap
\alpha
-quantiles.

5.5
Comparison

All methods proved useful in the estimation of the computer model error, although they differ in quality. The AVM of
Roy and Oberkampf, 2011
is quite simple.
It measures the accuracy of computer models by estimates of their cumulative distribution functions and compares these estimates to estimates of the cumulative distribution function based on experimental data. The difference is measured by the area between these estimates. But the result is hard to interpret.

Using the method of
Kennedy and O’Hagan, 2001
to quantify the model error, it is necessary to compute a maximum likelihood estimate. This step is quite unstable since one has to invert matrices, which leads in our examples to an overestimation of the computer model error. In fact in case of the MAFDS the maximal absolute model error of computer model 1 calculated on the

and
0.0052
for computer model 2, which is
70
and

times smaller than the estimated
0.95
-quantile of the model error, cf. Table

.

In the applications the method of

proved the most accurate. In fact, if we use the

6
Quantifying the influence of the computer model error

In the following section, two different methods which analyse the influence of the computer model error on density and quantile estimates are described. The first method aims to estimate a confidence interval for quantiles. The second method aims on estimating a confidence band of the density. With the aid of these methods, it is also possible to compare the influence of the error of computer models.

6.1
Confidence intervals for quantiles

Kohler et al., 2018
proposed a method to construct confidence intervals on quantiles related to experiments with a technical system. The aim of the method is to construct an estimator of the confidence interval of the
\alpha
-quantile
q_{Y,\alpha}
defined by

q_{Y,\alpha}=\min\{y\in\mathbb{R}\colon G_{Y}(y)\geq\alpha\},

(39)

where

G_{Y}\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,1],\quad G_{Y}(y)={\mathbf{P}}\{Y\leq y\},

(40)

i.e. for a given
\delta\in(0,1)
they want to construct a (random) interval
\hat{C}_{n}
such that

{\mathbf{P}}\{q_{Y,\alpha}\in\hat{C}_{n}\}\geq 1-\delta

(41)

holds.

To do this, in a first step a surrogate model
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}
of
m
as in Subsection
4.1
is defined by (
7
).
Then to construct a confidence interval a quantile estimator is constructed. Therefore define an estimator for the cumulative density function of
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X)
by

\hat{G}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n}}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{n}}I_{(-\infty,\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{n+L_{n}+i})]}(y)

(42)

and define the corresponding plug in estimate by

\hat{q}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},\alpha}=\min\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\hat{G}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n}}(y)\geq\alpha\}

(43)

for
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Next choose some suitable
\varDelta\delta\in(0,\delta)
, set

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{i})|,

\displaystyle N_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{n}\cdot\left(\alpha-\sqrt{-\frac{\log(\varDelta\delta/2)}{2N_{n}}}\right),

\displaystyle N_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{n}\cdot\left(\alpha+\sqrt{-\frac{\log(\varDelta\delta/2)}{2N_{n}}}\right),

and choose
\epsilon_{n}\in(0,1)
such that
(1-\epsilon_{n})^{n}<(\delta-\varDelta\delta)
holds and

\epsilon_{n}+\gamma_{n}=\epsilon_{n}+\sqrt{-\frac{\log(\delta-\varDelta\delta-(1-\epsilon_{n})^{n})}{2N_{n}}}

(44)

is minimal.

Finally the
\delta
confidence interval for
q_{Y,\alpha}
is defined by

\hat{C}_{n}=\left[\hat{q}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},\frac{N_{1}(n)}{N_{n}}-\epsilon_{n}-\gamma_{n}}-\hat{\beta}_{n},\hat{q}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},\frac{N_{2}(n)}{N_{n}}+\epsilon_{n}+\gamma_{n}}+\hat{\beta}_{n}\right].

(45)

6.2
Confidence bands for densities

Kohler and Krzyżak, 2019
proposed a method to construct confidence bands for the density of
Y
. The aim of their method is to construct for a finite sample size
n
lower and upper bounds
\hat{g}_{n}^{(lower)}
and
\hat{g}_{n}^{(upper)}
of
g
satisfying

\int_{I}\hat{g}_{n}^{(lower)}(x)\,dx\leq\int_{I}g(x)\,dx\leq\int_{I}\hat{g}_{n}^{(upper)}\,dx

(46)

for all intervals
I
with length
|I|\geq\kappa_{n}>0
, with probability
1-\delta
, where
\delta,\kappa_{n}>0
are given values.
To define the estimate the following notation is used:
For
\beta>0
define

I^{\beta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:[x-\beta,x+\beta]\cap I\neq\emptyset\}

and

I_{\beta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:[x-\beta,x+\beta]\subseteq I\}.

The empirical distribution
\hat{\mu}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}(X),N_{n}}}
of
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X)
is defined by

\hat{\mu}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n}}(A)=\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sum_{i=n+L_{n}+1}^{n+L_{n}+N_{n}}I_{A}(\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{i}))\quad(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}).

In a first step a surrogate model
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}
of
m
is defined as in Subsection
4.1
by (
7
).
Then construct a kernel density estimate based on a sample of
\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X)
, i.e. the estimator is defined by

\hat{f}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},h_{N_{n}}}(y)=\frac{1}{N_{n}\cdot h_{N_{n}}}\cdot\sum_{i=n+L_{n}+1}^{n+L_{n}+N_{n}}K\left(\frac{y-\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{i})}{h_{N_{n}}}\right),

for some bandwidth
h_{N_{n}}>0
and some kernel
K\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Set
\hat{\beta}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|Y_{i}-\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X_{i})|
and choose
\epsilon_{n}\in(0,1)
such that
(1-\epsilon_{n})^{n}+2/N_{n}^{2}<\delta
holds for a given
\delta\in(0,1)
and that

\gamma_{n}+\epsilon_{n}=\sqrt{\frac{-\ln(\delta-2/N_{n}^{2}-(1-\epsilon)^{n})}{2\cdot N_{n}}}+\epsilon_{n}

(47)

is minimal.
Finally for some given
\kappa_{n}>0
the upper and lower bounds on the density
g
are defined by

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{n}^{(upper)}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},h_{N_{n}}}(y)+\frac{\epsilon_{n}+\gamma_{n}+\frac{2\cdot\sqrt{\log N_{n}}}{\sqrt{N_{n}}}}{\kappa_{n}}

(48)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\cdot\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\text{ interval, }y\in J,\\
|J|>\kappa_{n}\end{subarray}}\Bigg(\hat{\mu}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n}}(J^{\hat{\beta}})-\int_{J}\hat{f}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},h_{N_{n}}}(t)dt\Bigg)

and

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{n}^{(lower)}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\Bigg\{\hat{f}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},h_{N_{n}}}(y)-\frac{\epsilon_{n}+\gamma_{n}+\frac{2\cdot\sqrt{\log N_{n}}}{\sqrt{N_{n}}}}{\kappa_{n}}

(49)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\kappa_{n}}\cdot\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\text{ interval, }y\in J,\\
|J|>\kappa_{n}\end{subarray}}\Bigg(\int_{J}\hat{f}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n},h_{N_{n}}}(t)dt-\hat{\mu}_{\hat{m}_{L_{n}}(X),N_{n}}(J_{\hat{\beta}})\Bigg),0\Bigg\}.

We choose
\kappa_{n}

h_{N_{n}}\in\{0.005,0.05,0.1,0.2,0.3\}
.
As kernel function

6.4
Comparison

The first method can be used to estimate confidence intervals for quantiles, the second to estimate confidence bands for densities.
By estimating a confidence band for a density one gains more information about the technical system. The problem is, that no real confidence band in a sup norm sense is estimated, i.e. as stated by (
46
), the estimated confidence band only contains the real density on intervals which are longer than
\kappa_{n}
with probability
1-\delta
. Thus it is quite hard to interpret the result.

On the other side, the estimated confidence intervals of quantiles do not yield a lot information about the experimental outcome, but it is simpler to analyse the influence of the model error and compare different computer models as illustrated above.
