Explicit Computations for the Classical and Quantum Integrability of the 3-Dimensional Rational Calogero-Moser System
Yana Staneva

Abstract
The integrability of the classical and quantum rational Calogero-Moser systems is verified explicitly via the Lax pair method for the case n = 3 n=3 . We provide an extensive survey of reflection groups and root systems. The Olshanetsky-Perelomov operators are constructed for a general root system via Dunkl operators, associated to root systems. The integrability of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser system is discussed via the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operators, which provide a set of commuting integrals of motion. The classical analogues of both the Dunkl and the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operators are also presented.



Keywords:
integrable systems, finite-dimensional Hamiltonian systems, Calogero-Moser system, classical integrability, quantum integrability, Lax pair, Dunkl operator

AMS Subject Classification:
37J35, 70H06

Introduction

Conservation of energy is one of the fundamental scientific concepts, being well known by both the academic community and the general public. Natural questions to ask are: what other properties of a given dynamical system remain constant over time and how are they related to each other? The answers to these questions are subject to the study of integrable systems, which currently lies at the crossroad of many ares of mathematics and within the scope of interest of experts within various research fields, including, but not limited to Mathematical Physics, Dynamical Systems, Representation Theory, Symplectic Geometry.

Various integrable systems were discovered in the
19^{\text{th}}
century, the Euler, Lagrange and Kovalevskaya tops, and the geodesic flow on an ellipsoid, to name a few
[
35
]
. The subject, however, remained somewhat dormant until the numerical results on the solitary waves phenomenon obtained by Zabusky & Kruskal in 1965
[
37
]
. Many equations have been studied since, the Korteweg-de Vries being the most prominent example, which yielded phenomenal results about the conserved quantities of the system at hand
[
37
]
. A considerable number of new integrable systems was discovered in the
70
s, among which are the Toda systems
[
33
]
and the main subject of this project – the Calogero-Moser systems. These are both
n
-particle systems whose energy is given by a Hamiltonian of the form
H=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}^{2}+U(q)
, where in the Calogero-Moser case the potential is given by
U(q)=\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq n}V(q_{j}-q_{k})
, describing the pairwise interaction between the
n
particles
[
4
]
.

Calogero-Moser systems have been studied in both the classical and the quantum settings, and are generally classified as rational, trigonometric (hyperbolic) or elliptic, based on the form of the potential
U(q)

[
28
]
. There exist relativistic and non-relativistic versions as well
[
30
]
. The main purpose of this project is to provide a somewhat complete and explicit overview of the rational Calogero-Moser system in both the classical and quantum sense. We investigate the integrability of the model via two separate techniques – the Lax pair formalism and by constructing the integrals of motion via Dunkl operators. Further, we utilize a simple
Mathematica
code in order to verify the explicit computations symbolically.

This paper is organized as follows. We begin by introducing the Hamiltonian formalism in the setting of classical mechanics in Section
1
. Then we provide a working definition of integrability and introduce the Lax method for extracting integrals of motion. Then we present the classical rational Calogero-Moser system and illustrate its integrability by explicitly verifying the
n=3
case via the
Mathematica
code listed in Appendix
A.1
.

Section
2
begins with a quick survey of quantum mechanics. Then we provide an analogous to the classical case definition of integrability and the Lax method. Hence we define the Hamiltonian of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser and explicitly show it is integrable in the
n=3
case via the
Mathematica
code listed in Appendix
A.2
.

In Section
3
we study reflection groups and root systems. We provide a detailed treatment of Coxeter graphs and Dynkin diagrams for root systems of different types.

Section
4
is a quick survey of polynomial invariant theory, in turn needed to prove the main results in Section
5
, which outlines the construction of the integrals of motion of the quantum Calogero-Moser system via the Dunkl and Olshanetsky-Perelomov operators. Finally, we present the classical analogue of Dunkl operators in Section
6
, which is then used in order to construct the classical Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator and thus, show the integrability of the Calogero-Moser system in the classical sense.

A working
Wolfram Mathematica
code for the computations with the Lax pairs is provided in Appendices
A
.

1
Classical Mechanics. Classical Rational Calogero-Moser.

1.1
The Classical Frame and Poisson Brackets.

The
configuration
(or
state
) of an
n
-dimensional classical mechanical system is described via a set of
2n
canonical variables (
coordinates
)
\{q,p\}
, where
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
and
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
are called
positions
and
momenta
, respectively, or by a point
P=(q,p)\in\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
is the
2n
-dimensional manifold describing the range of variation of the coordinates, called the phase space. Assume
\Gamma
is compact, so that the system is confined on a bounded region of space and the energy of the system is bounded as well. The physical quantities (
observables
), which describe the system are smooth functions
f(q,p)\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Every state
P
determines the values of the observables on that state and conversely, any state
P\in\Gamma
is uniquely determined by the values of all the observables on that state. Thus, there exists a duality relation between states and observables
[
32
]
.

The change of an observable
f
in time is measured by the time evolution of the canonical variables

q\to q_{t}=q(t),\quad p\to p_{t}=p(t),\quad f_{t}(q,p)=f(q_{t},p_{t}).

The time evolution of the variables is then given by Hamilton’s equations of motion,

\dot{q_{j}}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\dot{p_{j}}=-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}},

(1.1)

where
H=H(q,p)
is the Hamiltonian
[
32
]
.

The observables generate an abelian algebra
\mathcal{A}
of continuous functions on the compact phase space with a unit given by the constant function
1
and with multiplication given by the point-wise product of functions. Further,
\mathcal{A}
has identity
f=1
due to the natural
involution
(or
*
-operation
) given by the ordinary complex conjugation, which makes
\mathcal{A}
a
*
-algebra. The time evolution of every observable
f_{t}(q,p)\in\mathcal{A}
can be alternatively described by the differential equation

\dfrac{df_{t}(q,p)}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{dq_{j}}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{dp_{j}}{dt}\right).

(1.2)

Substituting Hamilton’s equations of motion (
1.1
) in Equations (
1.2
), we obtain

\dfrac{df_{t}(q,p)}{dt}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\left(-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{j}}\right)\right).

Then we define the following expression

\{f,g\}=\displaystyle{\sum_{j=1}^{n}}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right),

(1.3)

which allows us to concisely rewrite the time evolution of any observable
f
as

\dfrac{df}{dt}=\{H,f\}.

(1.4)

The bilinear map
\{\cdot,\cdot\}:C^{\infty}(\Gamma)\times C^{\infty}(\Gamma)\to C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
given by Equation (
1.3
) is called the
Poisson bracket
and it turns the algebra of classical observables
\mathcal{A}
into a Lie algebra with a Lie bracket given by the Poisson bracket, due to the following properties, shown and proved in Proposition
1
below.

Proposition 1
.

The Poisson brackets satisfy the following properties, for
f,g,k\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
,

1.

Linearity:

\{\lambda f+\mu g,k\}=\lambda\{f,k\}+\mu\{g,k\},\quad\forall\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

2.

Anticommutativity:

\{f,g\}+\{g,f\}=0.

3.

Leibniz rule:

\{f,gk\}=\{f,g\}k+g\{f,k\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{fg,k\}=\{f,k\}g+f\{g,k\}.

4.

Jacobi identity:

\{f,\{g,k\}\}+\{g,\{k,f\}\}+\{k,\{f,g\}\}=0.

Proof.

1.

Linearity. Using Equation (
1.3
) to write out the expression explicitly, we get

\displaystyle\{\lambda f+\mu g,k\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial\left(\lambda f+\mu g\right)}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial\left(\lambda f+\mu g\right)}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\lambda\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}+\mu\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\sum_{j}\left(\lambda\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}+\mu\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\right)\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}},

where we use linearity of the ordinary partial derivatives. Next we bring the constant coefficients in front of the summations to get

\displaystyle\{\lambda f+\mu g,k\}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\right)+\mu\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\right)-\lambda\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)-\mu\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right).

Grouping the summations with the same coefficients we obtain

\displaystyle\{\lambda f+\mu g,k\}

\displaystyle=\lambda\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)+\mu\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\{f,k\}+\mu\{g,k\},

using the definition of the Poisson bracket as per Equation (
1.3
) and we are done.

2.

Antisymmetry. Again, writing out the expression using Equation (
1.3
) we get

\displaystyle\{f,g\}+\{g,f\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)+\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cancel{\sum_{j}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}}-\bcancel{\sum_{j}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}}+\bcancel{\sum_{j}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}}-\cancel{\sum_{j}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}}

\displaystyle=0,

since all terms with opposite signs cancel out as illustrated above.

3.

Leibniz rule. We only prove the first of the two versions of the Leibniz rule. The second one follows the same logic. By Equation (
1.3
), we have

\displaystyle\{f,gk\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial\left(gk\right)}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial\left(gk\right)}{\partial p_{j}}\right)=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}k+g\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}k+g\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right),

where we have applied the product rule. Expanding the brackets, we get

\displaystyle\{f,gk\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\right)k+\sum_{j}g\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\right)-\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)k-\sum_{j}g\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right).

Now we group terms based on the non-differentiated functions
k
and
g
to obtain

\displaystyle\{f,gk\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)k+\sum_{j}g\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\{f,g\}k+g\{f,k\},

as required.

4.

Jacobi identity. In order to show that the Poisson brackets satisfy the Jacobi identity, we consider each term separately. Using the definition of the Poisson bracket as in Equation (
1.3
), we obtain the following expression for the first term, namely

\displaystyle\{f,\{g,k\}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{f,\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right\}.

Now after one more application of Equation (
1.3
) we get

\displaystyle\{f,\{g,k\}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial q_{j}\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}}_{a}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}}_{b}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}}_{c}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{d}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}}_{e}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{f}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}}_{g}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial p_{j}^{2}}}_{h}\right),

where the letter under each term is a label for the cancelation to be carried out later. Similarly for the second term using Equation (
1.3
) twice and underbracing the cancelation terms by letters yields

\displaystyle\{g,\{k,f\}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{g,\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial q_{j}\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}}_{f}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}}_{i}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}}_{b}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{j}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}}_{h}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{k}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}}_{d}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p_{j}^{2}}}_{l}\right).

In the same way we use Equation (
1.3
) twice and indicate via letters the pairing cancelations to get

\displaystyle\{k,\{f,g\}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{k,\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)-\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}-\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial q_{j}\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}}_{k}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}}_{c}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial q_{j}^{2}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}}_{i}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{g}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q_{j}}}_{l}-\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}}_{a}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial p_{j}\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{j}}}_{j}+\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial k}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q_{j}}\dfrac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial p_{j}^{2}}}_{e}\right).

All letters from
a
to
l
appear twice among the three expressions above, thus, all terms cancel and prove that the Jacobi identity holds as required.

∎

1.2
Integrals of Motion and Classical Integrability.

An observable
I\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
is called an
integral of motion
if it is constant in time. Using the Poisson bracket notation,
I
is an integral of motion if
\dot{I}=\{H,I\}=0
. If this holds, it is said that the observables
H
and
I
are
in involution
(or
Poisson commute
).

Definition 1
.

If an
n
-dimensional dynamical system has
n
functionally independent smooth functions
I_{1}=H,\dots,I_{n}
such that they are in pairwise involution, i.e.
\{I_{i},I_{j}\}=0
for
i\neq j
, then the system is said to be
integrable
.

Liouville showed that given an
n
-dimensional mechanical system with
n
independent integrals of motion in involution, the equations of motion can be integrated by normal means of integration
[
1
]
,
[
23
]
. One way of identifying the integrals of motion is via the Lax Pair method, which we discuss next.

1.3
Classical Integrability via the Lax Formalism.

The Lax Pair method is a powerful tool, which was first formulated by Peter Lax in his remarkable paper
[
22
]
. Given an integrable system, we wish to find a pair of matrices
L
and
M
, such that the Lax equation

\dot{L}=[L,M]

(1.5)

is equivalent to Hamilton’s equations of motion (
1.1
). The following proposition summarizes the key property of the Lax matrix
L
, which hints at the usefulness in determining the integrals of motion.

Proposition 2
.

The Lax matrix
L=L(t)
satisfies

L(t)=U^{-1}(t)L(0)U(t),

(1.6)

where
U
is such that
\dot{U}=UM
and
U(0)=\mathbb{I}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. In particular, the eigenvalues of
L
are independent of time.

Proof.

By definition of the inverse,
UU^{-1}=\mathbb{I}
. Differentiating and rearranging we get an expression for the derivative of
U^{-1}
, namely

\dfrac{dU^{-1}}{dt}=-U^{-1}\dfrac{dU}{dt}U^{-1}.

(1.7)

Now differentiating Equation (
1.6
) and substituting with Equation (
1.7
), we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{dL}{dt}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{dU^{-1}}{dt}L(0)U+U^{-1}L(0)\dfrac{dU}{dt}=-U^{-1}\dfrac{dU}{dt}U^{-1}L(0)U+U^{-1}L(0)\dfrac{dU}{dt}.

Using the condition that
\dfrac{dU}{dt}=UM
gives

\displaystyle\dfrac{dL}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\underbrace{U^{-1}U}_{\mathbb{I}}MU^{-1}L(0)U+U^{-1}L(0)UM=-ML+LM=[L,M],

hence, the Lax matrix
L
as in Equation (
1.6
) indeed provides the solution of the Lax equation (
1.5
) as required. Note that
L(t)
and
L(0)
are conjugate and therefore, have the same eigenvalues. Since
L(0)
is constant and thus, has constant eigenvalues, it follows that the eigenvalues of
L(t)
are also independent of time.
∎

Using the isospectral property of the Lax matrix
L
we can identify the integrals of motion associated with the mechanical system.

Theorem 1
.

The integrals of motion of system (
1.1
) with Lax pair (
1.5
) are given by the power traces of the Lax matrix
L
, that is

I_{k}=\dfrac{1}{k}\tr(L^{k}).

(1.8)

Remark
.

The classical integrals of motion are obtained by the characteristic polynomial of the matrix
L
. Hence, they are uniquely determined by a homogeneous polynomial expression with a fixed highest term in the momenta
p_{j}
. Therefore, they are unique.

The involutive property, however, is handled in a slightly more complicated manner. One way of showing that the integrals of motion given by Equation (
1.8
) Poisson commute with each other is utilizing a special object, called the
r
-matrix
, which satisfies the
Yang-Baxter equation (YBE)

[
2
]
. A thorough treatment and detailed discussion of the Lie theoretic perspective of the classical
r
-matrix is given in
[
31
]
. The general idea of the proof is that the existence of the
r
-matrix is equivalent to the eigenvalues of the matrix
L
being in involution, which in turn guarantees the involution of the integrals of motion. We refer the reader to consult
[
9
]
for more details on the matter.

On a separate note, verifying explicitly the number of integrals of motion is usually straightforward. Hence, we can now present the system of interest.

1.4
Classical Rational Calogero-Moser System.

Definition 2
.

The Hamiltonian of the classical rational Calogero-Moser System
[
26
]
is

\displaystyle{H}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{p}_{j}^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}\dfrac{k^{2}}{(q_{j}-q_{i})^{2}}.

(1.9)

It describes the motion of a system of
n
particles on the line with parameter of interaction
k\in\mathbb{R}
, as illustrated below in Figure
1
.

q_{1}
q_{2}
…
q_{i}
…
q_{n-1}
q_{n}
\longleftrightarrow
\frac{k^{2}}{(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}}
\longleftrightarrow
\frac{k^{2}}{(q_{n-1}-q_{n})^{2}}

Figure 1:
The configuration of the classical rational Calogero-Moser system.

Whenever we denote a generalized coordinate with a double subscript, i.e.
q_{ij}
we shall mean the difference between the
i
-th and
j
-th coordinate, that is,
(q_{i}-q_{j})
. Similarly,
q_{ij}^{(k)}
denotes
(q_{i}-q_{j})^{-k}
. Using Hamilton’s equations of motion (
1.1
) we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\dot{q_{i}}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\implies\dot{q_{i}}=p_{i}\implies\ddot{q_{i}}=\dot{p_{i}},\\
&\dot{p_{i}}=-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}\implies\dot{p_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}2k^{2}q_{ij}^{(3)},\end{aligned}

(1.10)

which are the equations of motion governing the dynamics of the rational Calogero-Moser system. Furthermore, it is an integrable system and we shall verify this statement via the Lax method.

1.5
Integrability of the Classical Calogero-Moser via Lax Formalism.

Theorem 2
.

A Lax pair for the system (
1.9
) is given by

\displaystyle L_{rs}=p_{r}\delta_{rs}+(1-\delta_{rs})\dfrac{ik}{q_{rs}},\qquad M_{rs}=-\dfrac{ik(1-\delta_{rs})}{q_{rs}^{2}}+\delta_{rs}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t=1\\
t\neq r\end{subarray}}^{n}\dfrac{ik}{q_{rt}^{2}},

(1.11)

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta,
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
r,s=1,\dots,n

[
25
]
.

Proof.

We explicitly compute the case
n=3
. Using the proposed formula, the Lax matrices
L
and
M
are given by

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&ikq_{12}^{(1)}&ikq_{13}^{(1)}\\

2
Quantum Mechanics. Quantum Rational Calogero-Moser.

Canonical quantization
is a procedure initially introduced in Paul Dirac’s PhD thesis
[
24
]
and further developed in
[
10
]
. The aim of the technique is to quantize a classical theory, while preserving formal structures and properties, such as symmetries and integrability. The Poisson brackets formulation of the classical theory
1.4
are generally only partially preserved in the canonical quantization
[
16
]
. However, this is not the case in the Calogero-Moser system as we shall now see.

The quantization of the classical mechanical system (
1.1
) is obtained by replacing the canonical momentum
p_{j}
in the Hamiltonian
H
with the
quantum momentum operator
\widehat{p}_{j}
by

p_{j}\mapsto-i\hbar\dfrac{\partial}{\partial q_{j}}\equiv\widehat{p_{j}},\quad j=1,\dots,n,

(2.1)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\hbar
is Planck’s constant (set
\hbar=1
). In this way we obtain a new quantum Hamiltonian, which we shall denote by
\widehat{H}
. Hence, the state of a quantum mechanical system is described via the set
\{\widehat{q},\widehat{p}\}
, where
\widehat{q}=(\widehat{q}_{1},\dots,\widehat{q}_{n})
and
\widehat{p}=(\widehat{p}_{1},\dots,\widehat{p}_{n})
are the quantum coordinates and momenta operators, respectively. The quantum variables satisfy the following relations,

\displaystyle[\widehat{q}_{j},\widehat{p}_{k}]=i\delta_{jk},\qquad[\widehat{q}_{j},\widehat{q}_{k}]=[\hat{p}_{j},\hat{p}_{k}]=0,\qquad\text{ where }j,k=1,\dots,n,

(2.2)

which are known as the
Heisenberg commutation relations
. The vector space over
\mathbb{C}
generated by
\widehat{q}
and
\widehat{p}
with Lie brackets given by Equation (
2.2
) is the
Heisenberg Lie algebra

[
32
]
. The time evolution equations for
\widehat{q}
and
\widehat{p}
are given by the
Heisenberg equations of motion

\dot{\widehat{q}_{j}}=i[\widehat{H},\widehat{q}_{j}]\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\dot{\widehat{p}}_{j}=i[\widehat{H},\widehat{p}_{j}],

(2.3)

in analogy with Hamilton’s Equations (
1.1
).

The quantum analogues of the classical observables are operators. An operator
F
satisfies Heisenberg’s equation

\dot{F}=i[\widehat{H},F],

in analogy with Equations (
1.4
)
[
32
]
.

2.1
Quantum Integrals of Motion and Quantum Integrability.

Given an operator
J
, such that
[\widehat{H},J]=0
,
J
is called a
quantum integral of motion
.

Definition 3
.

If an
n
-dimensional quantum dynamical system has
n
algebraically independent operators
J_{1}=\widehat{H},\dots,J_{n}
such that they are pairwisely commutative, i.e.
[J_{i},J_{j}]=0
for
i\neq j
, then the system is said to be
quantum integrable

[
30
]
.

It is of great importance to note that upon quantization, we obtain operators which
do not
necessarily commute. Thus, we need to carefully consider the order of multiplication, when doing computations in the quantum setting. The following identity turns out to be quite useful and clearly illustrates the lack of commutativity.

Lemma 1
.

The commutator of the quantum momentum operator
\hat{p}_{i}
and any function
f(\widehat{q}_{i})
is the total derivative of the function with respect to the
\widehat{q}_{i}
up to a scale, i.e.

\displaystyle[\widehat{p}_{i},f(\widehat{q}_{i})]=-if^{\prime}(\widehat{q}_{i}).

(2.4)

Proof.

Writing out the definition of the quantum momentum operator explicitly and substituting into the commutator relation, we get

\displaystyle[\widehat{p}_{i},f(\widehat{q}_{i})]

\displaystyle=-i\partial_{\widehat{q}_{i}}f(\widehat{q}_{i})-f(\widehat{q}_{i})\left(-i\partial_{\widehat{q}_{i}}\right)=\cancel{if(\widehat{q}_{i})\partial_{\widehat{q}_{i}}}-\cancel{if(\widehat{q}_{i})\partial_{\widehat{q}_{i}}}-if^{\prime}(\widehat{q}_{i})=-if^{\prime}(\widehat{q}_{i}),

where prime denotes derivative with respect to
\widehat{q}_{i}
.
∎

Thus, when conjugating by a first order partial differential operator, we think of this operation as computing the ordinary derivative of the function at hand. Next we present the quantum analogue of the Lax method, which is used in a similar way to extract the integrals of motion.

2.2
Integrability via the Lax Formalism.

The quantum analogue of the Lax pair method consists of finding a pair of operator-valued matrices
\widehat{L}
and
\widehat{M}
such that the quantum Lax equation

\dot{\widehat{L}}=i\left[\widehat{L},\widehat{M}\right],

(2.5)

is equivalent to the Heisenberg equation of motion

\dot{\widehat{L}}=i\left[\tilde{H},\widehat{L}\right],

(2.6)

where
\tilde{H}=\widehat{H}\mathbb{I}
,
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix
[
20
]
.
Then under some extra assumptions, which we shall discuss later on, the set of integrals of motion is given by summing the elements of the powers of
\widehat{L}
, i.e.

J_{m}=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j,k\neq 1}^{n}\left(\widehat{L}^{m}\right)_{jk}.

(2.7)

In contrast with the classical setting, where the Poisson bracket of any two integrals of motion is well defined, in the quantum setting this is not always the case when we compute the commutator of two operators
[
16
]
. The proof we provide here is outlined in great detail in
[
28
]
.

Proposition 3
.

The quantum integrals of motion given by (
2.7
) commute with the Hamiltonian of the system, i.e.
[\widehat{J}_{k},\widehat{H}]=0
, for all
k=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

The classical integrals
I_{k}
, which can be defined as the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial
\det[L-\lambda\mathbb{I}]
, where
L
is the Lax matrix and
\lambda
the corresponding eigenvalue as in Equation (
1.5
), correspond in the quantum case to the operators
{J}_{k}
, which can in turn be considered as the determinant
\det[\widehat{L}-\widehat{\lambda}\mathbb{I}]
for the corresponding quantum analogues as illustrated in great detail in
[
5
]
. However, the vanishing of the Poisson bracket of any two classical integrals of motion
\{I_{j},I_{k}\}
does not necessarily guarantee the vanishing of the commutator of two quantum integrals
[{J}_{j},{J}_{k}]
. It is sufficient to show that the
n
-th quantum integral of motion
J_{n}
commutes with
\widehat{H}
, since we have the following recursive relation

\displaystyle i{J}_{k-1}=\dfrac{1}{n-k+1}\left[\left(\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{q}_{l}\right),{J}_{k}\right].

(2.8)

Using the Jacobi identity for the operators
\displaystyle{\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{q}_{l}}
,
\widehat{H}
and
{J}_{k}
we have

\displaystyle\left[\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{q}_{l},\widehat{H}\right],{J}_{k}\right]=\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{q}_{l},\left[\widehat{H},{J}_{k}\right]\right]-\left[\widehat{H},\left[\sum_{l=1}^{n}\widehat{q}_{l},{J}_{k}\right]\right],

(2.9)

where the last term clearly indicates that if
{J}_{k}
is an integral of motion, then so is
{J}_{k-1}
. As shown in
[
5
]
, the
n
-th operator
{J}_{n}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle{J}_{n}=A_{12}\left(\widehat{p}_{1}\widehat{p}_{2}-f_{12}^{2}\right)+B_{1}\widehat{p}_{1}+B_{2}\widehat{p}_{2}+B_{12}f_{12}+C_{12},

where
f_{12}=f(\widehat{q}_{1},\widehat{q}_{2})
accounts for the potential of the Hamiltonian, and the coefficients
A_{12}
,
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
and
C_{12}

Lemma 2

Let
g\in W
, let
\sigma_{\alpha}
be such that
\langle\alpha,q\rangle=0
, and let
\lambda\in V
. Then

\displaystyle g\sigma_{\alpha}g^{-1}=\sigma_{g\alpha}\lambda.

Proof.

Writing out the action of
\sigma_{\alpha}
on the vector
\lambda
explicitly we get

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}\lambda=\lambda-\dfrac{2\langle\lambda,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha.

Then for
g\in W
, we have

\displaystyle g\sigma_{\alpha}g^{-1}\lambda

\displaystyle=g\left(g^{-1}(\lambda)-\dfrac{2\langle g^{-1}(\lambda),\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda-\dfrac{2\langle\lambda,g(\alpha)\rangle}{\langle g(\alpha),g(\alpha)\rangle}g(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{g\alpha}\lambda.

∎

4
Polynomial Invariant Theory.

In this section we discuss the structure of the subalgebra of polynomial functions, which remains invariant under the action of a subgroup of the general linear group. We then present a key result, known as
Chevalley’s theorem
, which we need to prove the integrability of the rational quantum Calogero-Moser system.

Let
V
be an
n
-dimensional vector space over a field
K
of characteristic
0
. Let
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be a basis for
V
. For
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in K
, if we write
\xi=x_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\xi_{n}
for
\xi\in V
, then
x_{j}(\xi)\in K
are the linear coordinate functions relative to the given basis of
V
. Let
A(V)
denote the commutative algebra of all functions on
V
with values in
K
.

Definition 22
.

The
algebra of polynomial functions on
V
, denoted by
P(V)
, is the smallest subalgebra of
A(V)
containing the linear coordinate functions
\xi\mapsto x_{j}(\xi)
.

Remark
.

Note that
P(V)
is independent of the choice of basis of
V
and any choice of basis in
V
yields an isomorphism
P(V)\cong K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
.

Now a basis of
P(V)
is given by the monomials
x_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{d_{n}}
, where
d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Consider the subspace
P_{d}(V)\subset P(V)
given by
P_{d}(V)=\{p\in P(V)\lvert p(t\xi)=t^{d}p(\xi),\forall t\in K,\xi\in V
. The elements of this subspace are called
homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

The general linear group
GL(V)
acts on the algebra of polynomial functions
P(V)
naturally, i.e. for
g\in GL(V)
,
p\in P(V)
and
\xi\in V
, we have

(g\cdot p)(\xi)=p(g^{-1}\xi).

Also, for
g,h\in GL(V)
and
p\in P(V)
we have
g\cdot(h\cdot p)=(gh)\cdot p
. Even further,
g\cdot P_{d}(V)\subset P_{d}(V)
for any
g\in GL(V)
. Next we formalize the idea of invariance under the action of an arbitrary subgroup
G
of
GL(V)
.

Definition 23
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
GL(V)
and let
p\in P(V)
. A polynomial
p
is said to be
G
-invariant
if
g\cdot p=p
,
\forall g\in G
. We denote the set of all
G
-invariant polynomial functions in
P(V)
by
P(V)^{G}
.

We illustrate all concept introduced so far via the following example.

Example 13
.

Let
p\in K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be given by
p=(c_{d_{1}}x_{1}^{d_{1}}+\dots+c_{d_{n}}x_{n}^{d_{n}})
, where
c_{d_{j}}
are constants, and let
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Then
\sigma
acts on
p
by permuting the indices of the elements in
p
, that is

\displaystyle\sigma\cdot p=c_{d_{1}}x_{\sigma(1)}^{d_{1}}+\dots+c_{d_{n}}x_{\sigma(n)}^{d_{n}}=c_{j_{\sigma(1)}}x_{1}^{j_{1}}+\dots+c_{j_{\sigma(n)}}x_{n}^{j_{n}}.

Polynomials which remain invariant under the action of the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
are called
symmetric polynomials
. The elementary symmetric polynomials are given by

\displaystyle p_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}\leq n}x_{j_{1}}\cdots x_{j_{n}},\quad k=1,\dots,n.

By a classical result in algebra, each symmetric polynomial is uniquely determined in the elementary symmetric polynomials, thus,
K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]^{\mathcal{S}_{n}}\cong K[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]
.

Next we state an important theorem due to Hilbert. An extensive proof can be found in
[
18
]
, Section 3, Theorem 3.2.

Theorem 4
.

Let
G
be a finite subgroup of
GL(V)
and let
P^{G}(V)
be the invariant subalgebra of
P(V)
. Then
P^{G}(V)
is finitely generated.

The next result is known as Chevalley’s theorem and is the main tool we shall employ to prove the integrability of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser system. The proof of the statement is omitted, but extensive ones can be found in both
[
18
]
see Section 3.2, Theorem 3.8 and
[
19
]
see Chapter 3, Section 3.5.

Theorem 5
.

Let
W
be a finite reflection group,
W\subset O(V)
. Then
P^{W}(V)\cong\mathbb{C}[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]
, where
p_{j}
are homogeneous polynomial functions of degree
d_{j}
, algebraically independent and invariant under the action of
W
.

Chevalley’s theorem suggests that the idea discussed in Example
13
can be generalized to the context of arbitrary Coxeter groups. We are now ready to introduce the main tool, used to obtain the integrals of motion of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser system, namely the
Dunkl operator
.

5

Dunkl operators are originally conceived in the rational differential setting by Charles Dunkl in the 1980s
[

. A whole framework is subsequently developed in the prominent works
[

,

,

]
. Here we restrict ourselves to the simplest case, the rational Dunkl operator.

5.1
The Dunkl Operator, Commutativity.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the standard inner product in the
n
-dimensional Euclidean vector space
V
with standard basis
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
. Let
R
be a normalized Coxeter root system with roots
\alpha\in R
. Denote by
\sigma_{\alpha}
the orthogonal reflection corresponding to the roots
\alpha\in R
and by
H^{\alpha}=\langle\alpha,x\rangle=0
with
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
the hyperplane orthogonal to
\alpha
as in Definition
5
. Next, define
V_{reg}=V-\cup_{\alpha}H^{\alpha}
. For
W
a finite reflection group, let
c:R\to\mathbb{C}
be a
W
-invariant function, known as a
multiplicity function
. For
\alpha\in R
we denote
c(\alpha)=c_{\alpha}
. Let
\alpha^{\vee}\in V
be the coroot of
\alpha
. Let
R_{+}
be the subset of
R
containing all positive roots with respect to a given partial ordering. Let
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
and let
\partial_{a}
be the directional derivative along the vector
a\in V
. Let
\mathbb{C}[V]
denote the algebra of
\mathbb{C}
-valued polynomial functions on
V
, let
\mathbb{C}W
be the group algebra, identified with the vector space of formal linear combination of elements of
W
, and finally, let
D(V)
be the algebra of differential operators on
V
.

Definition 24
.

The
rational Dunkl operator
is defined by

\displaystyle D_{a}=\partial_{a}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}).

The Dunkl operator is an element of the semi-direct product of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}W
and the algebra of differential operators on
V_{reg}
, i.e.
D_{a}\in\mathbb{C}W\ltimes D(V_{reg})
.

The following property of the Dunkl operators is of great importance for our further results.

Proposition 6
.

Let
w\in W
. Then
wD_{a}=D_{wa}w
.

Proof.

Consider the Dunkl operator as in Definition
24
. Let
w\in W
. Then using the obvious relation
w\partial_{a}=\partial_{w(a)}w
and the identity
w\sigma_{\alpha}=\sigma_{w\alpha}w
from Proposition
4
we have

\displaystyle wD_{a}

\displaystyle=w\partial_{a}-w\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\partial_{w(a)}w-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(w-\sigma_{w\alpha}w)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{w(a)}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{w\alpha})\right)w

\displaystyle=D_{w(a)}w.

∎

We can now discuss the main property of the Dunkl operators, namely, their commutativity.

Theorem 6
.

The Dunkl operators commute for any
a,b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, that is,
[D_{a},D_{b}]=0
.

Proof.

There are several ways to approach the proof of this statement. A rigorous one was used initially by Dunkl
[
11
]
. However, the calculations are quite tedious. Here we shall employ a clever trick, used by Etingof
[
12
]
. Here is a sketch of the proof. Instead of showing the commutativity of the operators explicitly, we consider the commutator of the Dunkl operator
D_{a}
with the function
\langle x,\xi\rangle:\mathbb{C}[x]\to\mathbb{C}[x]
. Then we consider the commutator of another Dunkl operator
D_{b}
,
b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
a\neq b
, with
[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle]
. Employing the Jacobi identity, we split the commutator in two symmetric parts, which in turn cancel. This leads to the conclusion that the Dunkl operators indeed commute.

Let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Consider first the following commutator.

\displaystyle[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle]=\left[\partial_{a}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}),\langle x,\xi\rangle\right].

Writing out the resulting expression explicitly, we get

\displaystyle\left[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]=[\partial_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle]-\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle},\langle\cdot,\xi\rangle\right]+\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right].

We only need to worry about the first and last commutators in the above expression, since the second one yields

\displaystyle-\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]=-\cancel{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\langle x,\xi\rangle}+\cancel{\langle x,\xi\rangle\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}}=0.

The first commutator yields

\displaystyle[\partial_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle]=\left[\sum_{i}a_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},\sum_{j}x_{j}\xi_{j}\right]=\sum_{i}a_{i}(\partial_{x_{i}}x_{i}-x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}})\xi_{i},

since
\partial_{x_{i}}x_{j}-x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}=0
for
i\neq j
. Using the guiding principle behind our Key Lemma
1
, which states that
\partial_{x_{i}}x_{i}-x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}=\dfrac{d}{dx_{i}}x_{i}=1
, we get

\displaystyle[\partial_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle]=\sum_{i}a_{i}\xi_{i}=\langle a,\xi\rangle.

Now, consider the third commutator. We have

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}\langle x,\xi\rangle-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}\langle x,\xi\rangle-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right).

Since
\sigma_{\alpha}
is an orthogonal reflection, we know that for any
u,v\in V
, we have
\langle\sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}u,v\rangle=\langle u,\sigma_{\alpha}v\rangle
. Hence, we get

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}\langle x,\xi\rangle-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\langle\sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\langle x,\sigma_{\alpha}\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right).

Now using the fact that
\sigma_{\alpha}\xi=\xi-2\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha=\xi-\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\alpha^{\vee}
, we have

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\langle x,(\xi-\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\alpha^{\vee})\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}-\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\cancel{\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}}-\langle x,\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}-\cancel{\langle x,\xi\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\langle x,\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}.

Combining the two results, we obtain

\displaystyle\left[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\langle a,\xi\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\langle x,\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\alpha^{\vee}\rangle\sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\cancel{\langle x,\alpha\rangle}}{\cancel{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}.

(5.1)

Next consider

\displaystyle\left[[D_{a},D_{b}],\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]=[[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{b}]-[[D_{b},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{a}],

(5.2)

where we have used the Jacobi identity to split the commutator in two. Using the result in Equation (
5.1
) the first commutator of Equation (
5.2
) is

\displaystyle[[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{b}]

\displaystyle=\left[\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},D_{b}\right]

\displaystyle=\cancelto{0}{\left[\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle,D_{b}\right]}+\left[-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha},D_{b}\right].

We only need to worry about the action of the orthogonal reflection
\sigma_{\alpha}
on the Dunkl operator
D_{b}
, given by
\sigma_{\alpha}D_{b}=D_{\sigma_{\alpha}(b)}\sigma_{\alpha}
, thus, yielding

\displaystyle[[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{b}]

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}D_{b}-D_{b}\sigma_{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\left(D_{b}\sigma_{\alpha}-\sigma_{\alpha}D_{b}\right).

Consider for a moment the expression in the brackets. We get

\displaystyle D_{b}\sigma_{\alpha}-\sigma_{\alpha}D_{b}=D_{b}\sigma_{\alpha}-D_{\sigma_{\alpha}(b)}\sigma_{\alpha}=(D_{b-\sigma_{\alpha}(b)})\sigma_{\alpha}.

Using Definition
5
, we get

\displaystyle D_{b}\sigma_{\alpha}-\sigma_{\alpha}D_{b}=\left(D_{b-b+2\frac{\langle\alpha,b\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\alpha}\right)\sigma_{\alpha}=D_{\langle\alpha,b\rangle\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}=\langle\alpha,b\rangle D_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}.

Hence, going back to the commutator we get

\displaystyle[[D_{a},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{b}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\langle\alpha,b\rangle D_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}.

(5.3)

Now consider the second commutator of Equation (
5.2
). We have

\displaystyle[[D_{b},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{a}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\beta}\langle\beta,b\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}\left(D_{a}\sigma_{\beta}-\sigma_{\beta}D_{a}\right).

Again using Definition
5
, we get

\displaystyle D_{a}\sigma_{\beta}-\sigma_{\beta}D_{a}=\left(D_{a-a+2\frac{\langle\beta,a\rangle}{\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}\beta}\right)\sigma_{\beta}=D_{\langle\beta,a\rangle\beta^{\vee}}\sigma_{\beta}=\langle\beta,a\rangle D_{\beta^{\vee}}\sigma_{\beta}.

Hence, the expression we obtain for the commutator is

\displaystyle[[D_{b},\langle x,\xi\rangle],D_{a}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\beta}\langle\beta,b\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}\langle\beta,a\rangle D_{\beta^{\vee}}\sigma_{\beta}.

(5.4)

Combining the expressions in (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), Equation (
5.2
) becomes

\displaystyle\left[[D_{a},D_{b}],\langle x,\xi\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}\langle\alpha,b\rangle D_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}-\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\beta}\langle\beta,b\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}\langle\beta,a\rangle D_{\beta^{\vee}}\sigma_{\beta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}(\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,b\rangle-\langle\alpha,b\rangle\langle\alpha,a\rangle)D_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}=0,

which indeed shows that the Dunkl operators commute for arbitrary
a,b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
∎

Before we proceed with the next big result, we need the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

Let
B(\cdot,\cdot)
be a bilinear form on
V
such that for any
a,b\in V
,
B(\sigma_{\alpha}a,\sigma_{\alpha}b)=B(a,b)
, where
\alpha\in R\cap\spn\langle a,b\rangle
. Let
w\in W
be a plane rotation, i.e.
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w
and
w\neq id
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\beta})}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}=0.

Proof.

Writing out the expression explicitly we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\beta})}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}=E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3},

where

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle},

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\left(-\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}\dfrac{1}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}-\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{1}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\left(-\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{1}{\langle\sigma_{\alpha}\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}-\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{1}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\beta}\right),\qquad\text{ and }

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\left(\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}\dfrac{1}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\left(\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{1}{\langle\sigma_{\alpha}\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}\right).

Let
S
be the normalized root system of the largest dihedral group
W(S)
and let
w
be an element of
S
. If
w=\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}
, then for any
\gamma\in S
we have
\sigma_{\gamma}w\sigma_{\gamma}=w^{-1}
. Hence,
\sigma_{\gamma}\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\gamma}\sigma_{\beta}=\sigma_{\beta}\sigma_{\alpha}
. For any
\gamma\in S
, we apply
\sigma_{\gamma}
to
E_{1}
to get

\displaystyle\sigma_{\gamma}E_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\sigma_{\gamma}\alpha,\sigma_{\gamma}\beta)\dfrac{1}{\langle\sigma_{\gamma}\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\sigma_{\gamma}\beta,\cdot\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\dfrac{1}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}=E_{1},

due to the invariance of the bilinear form
B\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the independence of the sum on the root system
R_{+}
. Now let
p_{S}=\prod_{\alpha\in S_{+}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle
, where
S_{+}=R_{+}\cap S
. Then for
\sigma_{\alpha}\in W(S)
, we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\alpha}(p_{S}E_{1})=\sigma_{\alpha}(p_{S})\sigma_{\alpha}(E_{1})=\sigma_{\alpha}(p_{S})E_{1}=-p_{S}E_{1},

for all
\alpha\in R_{+}\cap S
, thus, the polynomial
p_{S}E_{1}
is anti-invariant. But we have
p_{S}E_{1}
is homogeneous and
\deg(p_{S}E_{1})=\deg(p_{S})-\deg(E_{1})=\deg(p_{S})-2
, thus,
p_{S}E_{1}=0
.

Now consider the expression
E{2}
. Since
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w=\sigma_{\sigma_{\alpha}\beta}\sigma_{\alpha}
, and
B\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=B\langle\sigma_{\alpha}\beta,\sigma_{\alpha}\alpha\rangle=-B\langle\sigma_{\alpha}\beta,\alpha\rangle
, we see that
E_{2}
is as well identically zero.

Finally, consider
E_{3}
. We have

\displaystyle E_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\left(\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{1}{\langle\sigma_{\alpha}\beta,\cdot\rangle}\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle=-\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=w\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}B(\alpha,\beta)\dfrac{1}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\right)\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}=-E_{1}w=0.

∎

Our next goal is to use the commutativity property of the Dunkl operators to somehow recover the Hamiltonian of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser (
2.12
). The first thing to observe is that the Dunkl operator contains only first order differentials, while the momenta terms in the Hamiltonian (
2.12
) are of order two. This hints that we need to square the Dunkl operators.

5.2
The Olshanetsky-Perelomov Operator.

The ingenious idea is established by Olshanetsky and Perelomov in
[
28
]
. Here we follow the procedure as outlined in
[
12
]
. First we define a new operator, which is a polynomial function in terms of the squares the Dunkl operator. Then we dismiss the terms, which let the expression leave the subalgebra of
W
-invariant functions. In other words, we
restrict
the operator to the subspace of
W
-invariant functions. Hence, we see that this operator defines a quantum integrable system, which is closely related to our Calogero-Moser Hamiltonian. We begin by presenting the so called
Olshanetsky-Perelomov
operator.

Definition 25
.

The
Olshanetsky-Perelomov
operator associated with the
W
-invariant function
c_{\alpha}:R_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is the second order differential operator given by

\displaystyle L=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}},

(5.5)

where
\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{e_{j}}^{2}
is the Laplacian.

Note that the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator is
W
-invariant, which is clear due to the invariance of the Laplacian and the lack of terms involving reflections. Our aim is to obtain a relationship between the Dunkl and the Olshanetsky-Perelomov and in order to do so, we introduce the following restriction mechanism.

Definition 26
.

Define the
restriction operation

\res
of the operator
D_{a}
to the subspace of
W
-invariant differentials by

\displaystyle\res(D_{a}):(\mathbb{C}W\ltimes D(V_{reg}))^{W}\to(D(V_{reg}))^{W}.

Having introduced the restriction operation, we can now present the operator, consisting of the squared Dunkl operators, which we then restrict to the space of
W
-invariants. The result is due to Heckman
[
17
]
, but we follow the approach as in Etingof
[
12
]
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an orthonormal basis for
V
. Then we have

\displaystyle\res\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{e_{j}}^{2}\right)=\bar{L},

where

\displaystyle\bar{L}=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}.

(5.6)

Proof.

We begin by computing the product
D_{\xi}D_{\xi}
for
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and leave the summation until the end. Hence, we get

\displaystyle D_{\xi}D_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{\xi}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\right)\left(\partial_{\xi}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\partial_{\xi}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\partial_{\xi}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\left(\partial_{\xi}\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}+\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta}).

Now using that
\sigma_{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}=\partial_{\sigma_{\alpha}(\xi)}\sigma_{\alpha}
, we get

\displaystyle D_{\xi}D_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\partial_{\xi}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})+(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\partial_{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\partial_{\xi}-\partial_{\xi}\sigma_{\alpha}+\partial_{\xi}-\sigma_{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\partial_{\xi}-\partial_{\xi}\sigma_{\alpha}+\partial_{\xi}-\partial_{\sigma_{\alpha}(\xi)}\sigma_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(\partial_{\xi}-\partial_{\xi}\sigma_{\alpha}+\partial_{\xi}-\partial_{\xi}\sigma_{\alpha}+\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta}),

where we have simply applied the orthogonal reflection to the partial derivative
\partial_{\xi}
. Next we group the terms as follows

\displaystyle D_{\xi}D_{\xi}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\xi}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(2\partial_{\xi}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\right)-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle^{2}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\langle\beta,\xi\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta}).

Summing over
j=1,\dots,n
for all possible
e_{j}
we get

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}D_{e_{j}}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{e_{j}}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\alpha,e_{i}\rangle^{2}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle\langle\beta,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{e_{j}}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}+\cancelto{0}{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})(1-\sigma_{\beta})}

since
\displaystyle{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}\langle\alpha,e_{i}\rangle^{2}=\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle
and the last term is identically zero by Proposition
7
.
∎

5.3
Quantum Integrability.

We now use Chevalley’s Theorem
5
to conjecture that the restriction of the operator in Equation (
5.6
) defines a quantum integrable system.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose by the Chevalley theorem that
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}\cong\mathbb{C}[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]
with
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
being homogeneous polynomial functions of degrees
d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
. Then the set of operators
\bar{L}_{i}=\{\res(p_{i}(D_{e_{1}},\dots D_{e_{n}}))\}
forms a quantum integrable system.

Proof.

Let
p_{i}\in\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}
, for
i=1,\dots,n
. To see why the operators indeed commute consider the commutator

\displaystyle[\bar{L}_{i},\bar{L}_{j}]=\left[\res\left(p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right),\res\left(p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)\right].

Then we have

\displaystyle\res\left(p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)\res\left(p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\res\left(p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\res\left(p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\res\left(p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right)\res\left(p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)\right),

where
p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)=p_{j}\left(D_{\xi}\right)p_{i}\left(D_{\xi}\right)
holds due to the commutativity of the Dunkl operators.
∎

Finally, we are ready to establish the connection between Dunkl operators and the quantum rational Calogero-Moser Hamiltonian. In order to do so, we consider a gauge transformation of the operator
\bar{L}
, which yields the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator
6.3
. The procedure is discussed in the proposition below, following the method outlined in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\delta_{c}=\displaystyle{\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{c_{\alpha}}
and let
\bar{L}
and
L
be as in Equations (
6.3
) and (
5.6
) respectively. Then

\displaystyle\delta_{c}^{-1}\circ\bar{L}\circ\delta_{c}=L.

Proof.

We want to show that
\delta_{c}^{-1}\circ\bar{L}\circ\delta_{c}=L
. This is equivalent to

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ L\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}=\bar{L}=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha^{\vee}}=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha},

thus, our aim is to show that

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ L\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}.

Let us now perform the composition on
L
. We have

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ L\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}=\delta_{c}\circ\Delta\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}-\delta_{c}\circ\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}.

(5.7)

We treat the two terms separately. Consider first the composition of the Laplacian
\Delta
. We get

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ\Delta\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}=\delta_{c}\left(\Delta\delta_{c}^{-1}+2\sum_{j}\partial_{e_{j}}\delta_{c}^{-1}\partial_{e_{j}}+\delta_{c}^{-1}\Delta\right).

(5.8)

The first term in Equation (
5.8
) yields

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\left(\Delta\delta_{c}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{c}\left(\sum_{j}\partial_{e_{j}}^{2}\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{c_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{c}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}-c_{\alpha}(-c_{\alpha}-1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{-c_{\alpha}-2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{c_{\alpha}}\right),

where in the last term we have accounted for the different roots of
R_{+}
. Upon multiplying by
\delta_{c}
we get

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\left(\Delta\delta_{c}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\cancel{\delta_{c}}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\cancel{\delta_{c}^{-1}}+\cancel{\delta_{c}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\in R_{+}\\
\alpha\neq\beta\end{subarray}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}\cancel{\delta_{c}^{-1}}.

Consider the summation over the different roots
\alpha\neq\beta
. It is clearly
W
-invariant. Then the
W
-anti-invariant expression
\delta_{c}\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta\in R_{+}}
is of degree
\deg(\delta_{c}\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta\in R_{+}})=\deg(\delta_{c})-2
and hence, identically zero. Now consider the second term of Equation (
5.8
). We have

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\left(2\sum_{j}\partial_{e_{j}}\delta_{c}^{-1}\partial_{e_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{c}\left(\sum_{j}2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{-c_{\alpha}-1}\partial_{e_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cancel{\delta_{c}}\sum_{j}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\cancel{\delta_{c}^{-1}}\partial_{e_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{e_{j}}=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}.

The last term of Equation (
5.8
) is simply
\delta_{c}\delta_{c}^{-1}\Delta=\Delta
. Combining the three results, we get

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ\Delta\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}=\Delta+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}.

The only thing left is to consider the last term of Equation (
5.7
). Since there are no operators involved, the composition by
\delta_{c}
and
\delta_{c}^{-1}
is simply equivalent to the multiplication on the left and on the right by the respective terms. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{c}\circ L\circ\delta_{c}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Delta+\cancel{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\dfrac{\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}}+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}-\cancel{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}(c_{\alpha}+1)\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\Delta-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}=\bar{L},

where we have recovered the operator
\bar{L}
as required.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
7
, we have the following statement.

Corollary 3
.

The Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator
L
defines a quantum integrable system with
L_{i}=\delta_{c}^{-1}\circ\bar{L}_{i}\circ\delta_{c}
.

We are now ready to present the final result of this section, namely, the integrability of the quantum Calogero-Moser system.

Theorem 9
.

Let
R=A_{n-1}
. Then
L=\widehat{H}
, where
\widehat{H}
is the Hamiltonian of the quantum rational Calogero-Moser system (
2.12
), and the system is in turn integrable.

Proof.

The corresponding group
W
of the root system of type
A_{n-1}
is the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Thus,
c_{\alpha}=k
. The integrability is clear from the commutativity of the Dunkl operators.
∎

We shall not discuss the uniqueness of the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operators, but details can be found in
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
. In a similar manner, the Liouville integrability of the classical rational Calogero-Moser is obtained via the classical Dunkl operators, which we introduce in the next section.

6
Classical Integrability via Dunkl Operators.

6.1
Classical Dunkl Operators, Involution.

The classical analogue of the Dunkl operator
D_{a}
is derived by mapping the partial operator
\partial_{\widehat{q}_{j}}
to the classical momentum
p_{j}
, that is,
\partial_{\widehat{q}_{j}}\mapsto p_{j}
, under suitable conditions, see
[
21
]
. Hence, we have the following definition.

Definition 27
.

For
a\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the
classical rational Dunkl operator
is defined by

\displaystyle D_{a}^{cl}=p_{a}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}).

(6.1)

The classical Dunkl operator is an element of the semi-direct product of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}W
and the algebra of holomorphic functions on the cotangent space of
V_{reg}
, i.e.
D^{cl}_{a}\in\mathbb{C}W\ltimes\mathcal{O}(T^{*}(V_{reg}))
.

Remark
.

In order to be more precise, we go back to the quantum setting for a moment. Let
\hbar
denote Planck’s constant. Then the classical Dunkl operators are obtained by renormalizing the Dunkl operators
D_{a}
as

D_{a}(c,\hbar)=\hbar D_{a}(c/\hbar)

and then reducing modulo
\hbar
. See Etingof
[
12
]
for more details.

The classical Dunkl operators are also equivariant under
W
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
w\in W
. Then
wD^{cl}_{a}=D^{cl}_{wa}w
.

Proof.

Applying
w
on the left, we have

\displaystyle wD_{a}^{cl}

\displaystyle=wp_{a}-w\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})=p_{w(a)}w-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,w^{-1}\cdot\rangle}(w-\sigma_{w\alpha}w)

\displaystyle=p_{w(a)}w-\left(\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,w^{-1}\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{w\alpha})\right)w=\left(p_{w(a)}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,w^{-1}\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{w\alpha})\right)w

\displaystyle=D^{cl}_{w(a)}w.

∎

Similarly to the quantum operators, the classical ones are in involution. Here we demonstrate this property explicitly, but the reader is invited to consult
[
21
]
for a systematized approach on considering classical theories as the
\hbar\to 0
limit of quantum mechanics. Note that the involutiveness is in the sense of Equation (
1.3
) with respect to the canonical coordinates on the phase space
(x_{i},p_{i})
.

Theorem 10
.

The classical Dunkl operators Poisson commute for any
a,b\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, that is,
\{D^{cl}_{a},D^{cl}_{b}\}=0
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle D_{a}^{cl}=p_{a}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\text{ and }D_{b}^{cl}=p_{b}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,b\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}).

The Poisson bracket of the two Dunkl operators is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=a,b}\left(\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial p_{i}}\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial p_{i}}\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\cancelto{1}{\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial p_{a}}}\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{a}}-\cancelto{0}{\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial p_{a}}\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{a}}}+\cancelto{0}{\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial p_{b}}\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{b}}}-\cancelto{1}{\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial p_{b}}}\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{b}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{a}}-\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{b}}

(6.2)

as
D_{a}^{cl}
and
D_{b}^{cl}
are independent of
p_{b}
and
p_{a}
, respectively, and
\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial p_{a}}=\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial p_{b}}=1
. So, we get

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{a}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{a}}\left(p_{b}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,b\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\right)=\cancelto{0}{\dfrac{\partial p_{b}}{\partial x_{a}}}-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{a}}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,b\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,b\rangle\langle\alpha,a\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}),

and similarly,

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{b}}=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,a\rangle\langle\alpha,b\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}),

and substituting in Equation (
6.2
), we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial D_{b}^{cl}}{\partial x_{a}}-\dfrac{\partial D_{a}^{cl}}{\partial x_{b}}=0.

∎

Once again, our next step is to utilize the Poisson commutativity of the Classical Dunkl operators (
6.1
) in order to recover the Hamiltonian of the classical rational Calogero-Moser (
1.9
). The classical Dunkl operator (
6.1
) contains only first order momentum terms, in contrast with the squared momentum terms in the Hamiltonian (
1.9
).

6.2
The Classical Olshanetsky-Perelomov Operator.

Recall that in the quantum case, we recovered the Calogero-Moser Hamiltonian (
2.12
) via the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator (
5.6
). Similarly, we define the
classical version of the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator
as in
[
12
]
, which will then enable us to obtain the Hamiltonian
1.9
.

Definition 28
.

The
classical Olshanetsky-Perelomov
operator associated with the
W
-invariant function
c_{\alpha}:R_{+}\to\mathbb{C}
is defined as

\displaystyle L^{cl}=\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}.

(6.3)

As before, we define the restriction operation, necessary for the further computations.

Definition 29
.

Given a classical Dunkl operator
D_{a}^{cl}
, define the
restriction operation

\rescl
by the rule

\displaystyle\rescl(D_{a}^{cl}):(\mathbb{C}W\ltimes\mathcal{O}(T^{*}(V_{reg})))^{W}

\displaystyle\to(\mathcal{O}(T^{*}(V_{reg})))^{W},

\displaystyle\rescl\left(\sum f_{g}\cdot g\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum f_{g},

for
g\in W
,
f\in\mathcal{O}(T^{*}V_{reg})
.

Now that we are equipped with the necessary tools, we introduce the operator, containing the square of the classical Dunkl operator, which we shall then restrict to the space of
W
-invariants. We follow the steps outlined by Etingof in
[
12
]
.

Proposition 10
.

Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
be an orthonormal basis for
V
. Then we have

\displaystyle\rescl\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(D_{e_{j}}^{cl})^{2}\right)=\bar{L}^{cl},

where

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{cl}=\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha^{\vee}}.

(6.4)

Proof.

We begin by computing the square of the classical Dunkl operator, namely

\displaystyle(D_{e_{j}}^{cl})^{2}=p_{j}^{2}-p_{j}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})p_{j}

\displaystyle+\cancelto{0}{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\alpha})}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\dfrac{(1-\sigma_{\beta})}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}},

where again we cancel the last term due to Proposition
7
. Next, we have

\displaystyle(D_{e_{j}}^{cl})^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-p_{j}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})p_{j}

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(p_{j}(1-\sigma_{\alpha})+(1-\sigma_{\alpha})p_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(p_{j}-p_{j}\sigma_{\alpha}+p_{j}-\sigma_{\alpha}p_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(p_{j}-p_{j}\sigma_{\alpha}+p_{j}-p_{j}\sigma_{\alpha}+\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle p_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}\right),

where we have applied the orthogonal reflection to the
p_{j}
. Next we group the terms as follows

\displaystyle(D_{e_{j}}^{cl})^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}(2p_{j}(1-\sigma_{\alpha}))-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle^{2}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha^{\vee}}\sigma_{\alpha}

Now applying the restriction operation gives

\displaystyle\rescl\left((D_{e_{j}}^{cl})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\langle\alpha,e_{j}\rangle^{2}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha^{\vee}}

and upon summing over all values of
p_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,n
, we recover
\bar{L}^{cl}
as required.
∎

6.3
Classical Integrability.

The natural question to ask is how do we obtain the Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator from the restriction of the squared classical Dunkl operators. The procedure is somewhat identical to the one in the quantum setting. We follow the approach outlined in Etingof
[
12
]
.

Proposition 11
.

Define the automorphism
\theta_{c}:\mathbb{C}W\ltimes\mathcal{O}(T^{*}(V_{reg}))\to\mathbb{C}W\ltimes\mathcal{O}(T^{*}(V_{reg}))
by

\displaystyle\theta_{c}(x)=x,\quad\theta_{c}(\sigma_{\alpha})=\sigma_{\alpha},\quad\text{ and }\quad\theta_{c}(p_{a})=p_{a}+\partial_{a}\log\delta_{c},

where
\delta_{c}=\displaystyle{\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}}\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{c_{\alpha}}
for
x,a\in V
,
p\in\mathcal{O}(T^{*}V_{reg})
. Then the classical Olshanetsky-Perelomov
L^{cl}
is obtained via

\displaystyle L^{cl}=\theta_{c}\left(\bar{L}^{cl}\right).

Proof.

First we rewrite
\bar{L}^{cl}
as follows

\displaystyle\bar{L}^{cl}=\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha}

Next we apply
\theta_{c}
to
\bar{L}^{cl}
which yields

\displaystyle\theta_{c}\left(\bar{L}^{cl}\right)

\displaystyle=\theta_{c}\left(\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}\right)-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\theta_{c}(p_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(\left(p_{j}+\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}\right)\left(p_{j}+\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}\right)\right)-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\left(p_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}\log\delta_{c}\right).

Expanding the brackets we get

\displaystyle\theta_{c}\left(\bar{L}^{cl}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left(p_{j}^{2}+p_{j}\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}+\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}p_{j}+\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}\right)-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\partial_{\alpha}\log\delta_{c}.

(6.5)

Recall that the logarithm of a product is equal to the sum of the logarithms. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\log\delta_{c}=\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}.

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\theta_{c}\left(\bar{L}^{cl}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}+\cancel{\sum_{j}p_{j}\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}}+\cancel{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sum_{j}p_{j}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\beta}\sum_{j}\beta_{j}}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}-\cancel{\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}p_{\alpha}}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{2c_{\alpha}}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle}\sum_{\beta\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\beta}\langle\beta,\beta\rangle}{\langle\beta,\cdot\rangle}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{j}^{2}-\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\dfrac{c_{\alpha}^{2}\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle}{\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle^{2}}=L^{cl}.

∎

Analogously to the quantum setting, we use Chevalley’s theorem in order to extract a classical integrable sense from the classical Olshanetsky-Perelomov operator.

Theorem 11
.

Suppose by the Chevalley theorem that
\mathbb{C}[V]^{W}\cong\mathbb{C}[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]
with
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
being homogeneous polynomial functions of degrees
d_{1}\leq\dots\leq d_{n}
. Then the set of operators
L^{cl}_{i}=\rescl(\theta(p_{i}(D^{cl}_{e_{1}},\dots,D^{cl}_{e_{n}})))
forms a classical integrable system.

Proof.

The proof follows from Chevalley’s Theorem
5
and the Poisson commutativity of the classical Dunkl operators as in Theorem
10
.
∎

Finally, we can confirm the classical integrability of the rational Calogero-Moser system. We summarize this statement below.

Theorem 12
.

Let
R=A_{n-1}
. Then
L^{cl}=H
, where
H
is the Hamiltonian of the classical rational Calogero-Moser system (
1.9
), and the system is integrable.

Proof.

Once again, the corresponding group
W
of the root system of type
A_{n-1}
is the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Thus,
c_{\alpha}=k
. The integrability is clear from the Poisson commutativity of the classical Dunkl operators.
∎

Conclusion
