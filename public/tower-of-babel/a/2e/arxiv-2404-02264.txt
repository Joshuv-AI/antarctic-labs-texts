The Identity Problem in virtually solvable matrix groups over algebraic numbers
Corentin Bodart, Ruiwen Dong

Abstract
The Tits alternative states that a finitely generated matrix group either contains a nonabelian free subgroup F 2 F_{2} , or it is virtually solvable. This paper considers two decision problems in virtually solvable matrix groups: the Identity Problem (does a given finitely generated subsemigroup contain the identity matrix?), and the Group Problem (is a given finitely generated subsemigroup a group?). We show that both problems are decidable in virtually solvable matrix groups over the field of algebraic numbers ℚ ¯ \overline{\mathbb{Q}} . Our proof also extends the decidability result for nilpotent groups by Bodart, Ciobanu, Metcalfe and Shaffrir, and the decidability result for metabelian groups by Dong (STOC’24). Since the Identity Problem and the Group Problem are known to be undecidable in matrix groups containing F 2 × F 2 F_{2}\times F_{2} , our result significantly reduces the decidability gap for both decision problems.



Keywords:
matrix semigroups, virtually solvable groups, rational subsemigroups, computational group theory, Identity Problem

For both problems, we work in some fixed matrix group

(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4})
, where
f_{1}=1,f_{2}=X_{1}+X_{1}^{2},f_{3}=X_{1}^{3},f_{4}=X_{1}^{2}
.

Conversely, given any tuple of polynomials
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
, one can construct a
\mathcal{G}
-graph
\Gamma
such that
\boldsymbol{f}
is exactly its tuple of position polynomials.
Indeed, for each monomial
c\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{z}
appearing in
f_{\ell}
, we can draw
c\geq 1
edges of label
\ell
starting at vertex
(b_{\Omega(\ell)},z)
.

In the wake of Proposition
4.8
, we will characterize the following four properties of an
\mathcal{A}
-graph
\Gamma
using its position polynomials: (i) whether
\Gamma
is full-image, (ii) whether
\Gamma
is symmetric, (iii) whether
\Gamma
represents
0\in\mathcal{Y}
, (iv) whether
\Gamma
is face-accessible.
Properties (i)-(iii) are easy to characterize:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be an
\mathcal{A}
-graph with position polynomials
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
.

(i)

\Gamma
is full-image if and only if

f_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]\setminus\{0\},\text{ for }\ell=1,\ldots,t
.

(ii)

\Gamma
is symmetric if and only if for each
i=1,\ldots,s
, we have

\sum_{\ell\colon\Omega(\ell)=i}f_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell\colon\Delta(\ell)=i}f_{\ell}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{a_{\ell}}.

(iii)

\Gamma
represents
0
if and only if

\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}f_{\ell}\cdot y_{\ell}=0
.

To characterize the face-accessibility of
\Gamma
, we will use the
weighted degree
of a “contracted” version of position polynomials.
Let
\cdot
denote the dot product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Given a polynomial
f=\sum_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}c_{a}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{a}\in\mathbb{R}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{*}
and a vector
v\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, the
weighted degree
of
f
at direction
v
is defined as

\deg_{v}(f)\coloneqq\max\{v\cdot a\mid a\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},c_{a}\neq 0\}
.
Define additionally
\deg_{v}(0)=-\infty
.
We now introduce the notion of
partial contractions
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}
:

Definition 4.10

(Partial contraction)
.

A
partial contraction
of
\mathcal{A}
is a tuple
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
, where

(i)

S
is a non-empty subset of
\{1,\ldots,s\}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{T}
is a subset of
\{1,\ldots,t\}
, such that the transitions
\{\delta_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}
form a spanning tree of the state set
\{q_{i}\mid i\in S\}
in the underlying
undirected
graph. In particular,
|\mathcal{T}|=|S|-1
.

(iii)

\rho
is an element of
S
; the state
q_{\rho}
will be seen as the “root” of the undirected spanning tree.

Then in the automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
(recall its definition before Lemma
4.1
), for every index
i\in S
, there exists a unique path consisting of transitions in
\{\delta_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}\cup\{\delta_{\ell}^{-}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}
, that connects from
q_{i}
to
q_{\rho}
. See the automaton in Figure
9
for an illustration.

S=\{2,3,4\}
,
\mathcal{T}=\{4,5\}
,
\rho=3
. The contracted transitions (
\delta_{4}
and
\delta_{5}
) are marked with thick arrows, and
\Omega^{-1}(S)=\{2,3,4,5,6\}
.

Non-accessible face
F
becomes three petals after contraction.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
be the position polynomials of
\Gamma
.
For a set
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,s\}
, denote
\Omega^{-1}(S)\coloneqq\{\ell\mid\Omega(\ell)\in S\}
.
Given a partial contraction
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
and a tuple of position polynomials
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
, define the tuple of
contracted position polynomials

\boldsymbol{f}^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}\coloneqq\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\right)_{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)}\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{|\Omega^{-1}(S)|},

where the polynomial
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}],\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S),
is defined as follows: there is a unique path
P_{\Omega(\ell)}
in
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
, consisting of transitions in
\{\delta_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}\cup\{\delta_{\ell}^{-}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}
, that connects from
q_{\Omega(\ell)}
to
q_{\rho}
. Write its evaluation
\operatorname{ev}(P_{\Omega(\ell)})
as
(y_{P_{\Omega(\ell)}},z_{P_{\Omega(\ell)}})
.
We define
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\coloneqq f_{\ell}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{z_{P_{\Omega(\ell)}}}
.

Similarly, if
\Delta(\ell)\in S
, we denote by
P_{\Delta(\ell)}
the path in
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
from
q_{\Delta(\ell)}
to
q_{\rho}
, consisting of transitions in
\{\delta_{\ell}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}\cup\{\delta_{\ell}^{-}\mid\ell\in\mathcal{T}\}
.
The path
P_{\Delta(\ell)}
evaluates to an element
(y_{P_{\Delta(\ell)}},z_{P_{\Delta(\ell)}})
for some
z_{P_{\Delta(\ell)}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
Define the
contracted edge vectors
in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
:

\displaystyle a^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\coloneqq\begin{cases}a_{\ell}+z_{P_{\Delta(\ell)}}-z_{P_{\Omega(\ell)}},&\quad\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)\cap\Delta^{-1}(S),\\
0^{n}&\quad\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)\setminus\Delta^{-1}(S).\end{cases}

Example 4.11

(label=exa:cont)
.

Let us compute the contracted position polynomials for the example in Figure
9
.
As shown in the figure, let
f_{1}=1
,
f_{2}=X_{1}
,
f_{3}=X_{1}^{3}
,
f_{4}=X_{1}^{2}
,
f_{5}=X_{1}^{3}
and
f_{6}=X_{1}^{4}
.
The partial contraction we use is
S=\{2,3,4\}
,
\mathcal{T}=\{4,5\}
,
\rho=3
, so
\Omega^{-1}(S)=\{2,3,4,5,6\}
.
The path
P_{4}
from
q_{4}
to
q_{3}
evaluates to
(y_{4},-1)
, so
z_{P_{4}}=-1
.
We have
\Omega(2)=\Omega(3)=4
, therefore
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{3}=f_{3}\cdot X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}^{2}
and
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{4}=f_{4}\cdot X_{1}^{-1}=X_{1}
.
Similarly, the path
P_{2}
from
q_{2}
to
q_{3}
evaluates to
(y_{5},2)^{-1}=(-X_{1}^{-2}y_{5},-2)
, so

.
We have
\Omega(6)=2
, therefore
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{6}=f_{6}\cdot X_{1}^{-2}=X_{1}^{2}
.
Finally, we have
\Omega(2)=\Omega(5)=3
, therefore
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{2}=f_{2}=X_{1}
and
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{5}=f_{5}=X_{1}^{3}
.
The contracted position polynomials are therefore
(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{2},f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{3},f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{4},f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{5},f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{6})=(X_{1},X_{1}^{2},X_{1},X_{1}^{3},X_{1}^{2})
.

.

The contracted position polynomials can be seen as “position polynomials” of a graph
\Gamma^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
over
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, which is obtained from
\Gamma
by contracting the lattices
\Lambda_{i},i\in S
into a single lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and discarding the other lattices
\Lambda_{i},i\notin S
.
See Figure
9
for an illustration.
During the contraction, each lattice
\Lambda_{i}=\{b_{i}\}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n},i\in S,
is translated by the vector
(-b_{i},z_{P_{i}})
, where
z_{P_{i}}
is the sum of the
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-coordinates along the unique path (in the subgraph of
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
defined by
\mathcal{T}
) from
q_{i}
to
q_{\rho}
.
In particular, if two lattices
\Lambda_{i},\Lambda_{j},i,j\in S,
are connected by some edge
e
with label
\ell\in\mathcal{T}
, then the edge
e
will become a loop after the contraction (e.g. the green and blue edges in Figure
9
).
Similarly, the contracted edge vector
a^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}
corresponds to the edge vector of label
\ell
after the contraction.
The edges in the contracted graph
\Gamma^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
have labels in
\Omega^{-1}(S)
.
Edges going to a discarded lattice
\Lambda_{i},i\notin S
(e.g. the blown edge) can be seen as “dangling”: these are edges with labels in
\Omega^{-1}(S)\cap\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}
, where
\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,t\}\setminus\Delta^{-1}(S)
.
Note that when
S
is a singleton
\{\rho\}
, the set
\mathcal{T}
is empty, and the tuple
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\{\rho\},\emptyset,\rho)}
is simply
(f_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(\rho)}
.

Given a partial contraction
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
and a vector
v\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}
, define

M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})\coloneqq\left\{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)\;\middle|\;\deg_{v}\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\right)=\max_{\ell^{\prime}\in\Omega^{-1}(S)}\left\{\deg_{v}\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)\right\}\right\}.

This is the set of labels
\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)
such that
\deg_{v}\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\right)
is maximal.
In the contracted graph
\Gamma^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
, the source coordinate of edges with labels in
M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})
have the largest inner product with
v
.
Define additionally

O_{v}(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\coloneqq\left\{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)\;\middle|\;a^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\not\perp v\right\}.

This is the set of labels whose contracted edge vectors
a^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}
are not orthogonal to
v
.

Since we are working in dimension

Using the contracted position polynomials and the contracted edge vectors, we can characterize face-accessibility of
\Gamma
.
The following lemma is a formal way of expressing “
\Gamma
is face-accessible if and only if all its partial contractions
\Gamma^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
are face-accessible” (in particular, faces containing sources of “dangling” edges are considered accessible).

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\Gamma
be a symmetric
\mathcal{A}
-graph with position polynomials
\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
.
Then
\Gamma
is face-accessible if and only if for every partial contraction
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
, we have

\left(O_{v}(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\cup\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}\right)\cap M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})\neq\emptyset\quad\text{ for every }v\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}.

(3)

Sketch of proof.

See Appendix
A
for a full proof.
We show the contrapositive: the convex hull
C
of
V(\Gamma)
has a non-accessible face if and only if
\exists(S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\exists v\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*},
such that

\left(O_{v}(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\cup\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}\right)\cap M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})=\emptyset.

If
F
is a non-accessible face of
C
, then consider all the edges whose source is in
F
(their target will also be in
F
).
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
be the set of labels of these edges, and let
\widetilde{S}
be the index set of origin and destination states of all transitions with label in
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
.
Consider the subautomaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}
whose states are
q_{i},i\in\widetilde{S}
, and whose transitions are
\delta_{t},t\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
(we do not specify the initial or accepting state of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
).
Let
S\subseteq\widetilde{S}
be such that
\{q_{i}\mid i\in S\}
is a weakly connected component of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, let
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
be such that
\{\delta_{t}\mid t\in\mathcal{T}\}
is an undirected spanning tree of this weakly connected component.
Let
\rho
be any element of
S
.
We contract
\Gamma
according to
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
(see Figure
9
for an illustration). Since
F
is non-accessible, it will still be non-accessible after the contraction.
After contraction,
F
will be the extremal face at some direction
v\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}
(e.g. in Figure
9
,
v
would be
(-1)
, a vector pointing to the left).
This means that among the position polynomials
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell},\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S),
of
\Gamma^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
, the index of those with the highest
\deg_{v}(\cdot)
correspond to the labels of contracted edges that are orthogonal to
v
(e.g. the three loops in the leftmost position in the contracted graph of Figure
9
).
This is because
F
is orthogonal to
v
after contraction.
In terms of description by
\deg_{v}(\cdot)
, the indices of these polynomials are exactly
M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})\coloneqq\left\{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)\;\middle|\;\deg_{v}\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\right)=\max_{\ell^{\prime}\in\Omega^{-1}(S)}\left\{\deg_{v}\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell^{\prime}}\right)\right\}\right\}
.
Whereas the labels
\ell
whose corresponding contracted edges that are
not
orthogonal to
v
are either in
O_{v}(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
(if the contracted edge is not dangling), or in
\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}
(if the contracted edge is dangling).
Therefore
\left(O_{v}(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\cup\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}\right)\cap M_{v}((S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\boldsymbol{f})=\emptyset
.

The other implication direction can be proved similarly.
∎

The definition of
\boldsymbol{f}^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
can be naturally extended to the case where
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
instead of
\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
: write
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}^{+}-\boldsymbol{f}^{-}
where
\boldsymbol{f}^{+},\boldsymbol{f}^{-}\in\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
, and define
\boldsymbol{f}^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}\coloneqq\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{+}\right)^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}-\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{-}\right)^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}
.

Define

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{t})\in\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}\;\middle|\;\sum_{\ell\colon\Omega(\ell)=i}f_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell\colon\Delta(\ell)=i}f_{\ell}\cdot\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{a_{\ell}},\;i=1,\ldots,s;\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}f_{\ell}\cdot y_{\ell}=0\right\}.

(4)

This is the
\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]
-module consisting of all
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{t}
satisfying the conditions in Lemma
4.9
(ii) and (iii).
A finite set of generators for
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
can be effectively computed
[
Sch80
]
, for example using Gröbner basis
[
Eis13
]
.

We then want to include the condition in Lemma
4.13
. For this, we will replace the information of
\boldsymbol{f}
by all its contracted position polynomials.
Denote by
\mathcal{PC}
the set of all partial contractions of
\mathcal{A}
, denote
K\coloneqq\sum_{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\in\mathcal{PC}}|\Omega^{-1}(S)|
, and define the
\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]
-module

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\coloneqq\left\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\coloneqq\left(f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}\right)_{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\in\mathcal{PC},\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(S)}\in\mathbb{Z}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{K}\;\middle|\;\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}\right\}.

(5)

Note that each entry
f^{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}_{\ell}
in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
is obtained by multiplying
f_{\ell}
by a fixed monomial, independant of
\boldsymbol{f}
.
Therefore, if
\{\boldsymbol{f}_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a generating set of
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}
, then
\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a generating set of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
Note that we can recover
\boldsymbol{f}
as a sub-tuple of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
: for
\rho\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
, the sub-tuple
\boldsymbol{f}^{(\{\rho\},\emptyset,\rho)}
of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
is simply
(f_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Omega^{-1}(\rho)}
, and therefore
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
contains as a sub-tuple
(f_{\ell})_{\ell\in\bigcup_{\rho=1}^{s}\Omega^{-1}(\rho)}=(f_{\ell})_{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,t\}}
.

Using Lemma
4.13
, we now characterize the face-accessibility of
\Gamma
by its contracted position polynomials
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
.
For simplicity, rename the indices of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
by writing
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}=(\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{K})
, where
\widetilde{f}_{i}=f^{(S_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},\rho_{i})}_{\ell_{i}},i=1,\ldots,K
.
Similarly, define
\widetilde{a}_{i}=a^{(S_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},\rho_{i})}_{\ell_{i}},i=1,\ldots,K
.
For a partial contraction
(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)
, define the sets

\displaystyle I_{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}\coloneqq\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\;\middle|\;(S_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},\rho_{i})=(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)\right\},

\displaystyle J_{(S,\mathcal{T},\rho)}\coloneqq\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\;\middle|\;(S_{i},\mathcal{T}_{i},\rho_{i})=(S,\mathcal{T},\rho),\ell_{i}\in\Delta^{-1}(S)^{\complement}\right\}.

For a set
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,K\}
, define

M_{v}\left(I,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\right)\coloneqq\left\{i\in I\;\middle|\;\deg_{v}\left(\widetilde{f}_{i}\right)=\max_{i^{\prime}\in I}\left\{\deg_{v}\left(\widetilde{f}_{i^{\prime}}\right)\right\}\right\},

and define

O_{v}\coloneqq\left\{i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\;\middle|\;\widetilde{a}_{i}\not\perp v\right\}.

One can see that
\boldsymbol{f}\in\left(\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{*}\right)^{t}
if and only if
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\left(\mathbb{N}[\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muX\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{\pm}]^{*}\right)^{K}
, because
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{f}}
contains
\boldsymbol{f}
as a sub-tuple. Summarizing the definition of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
as well as Lemma
4.9
and
4.13
, we obtain:

Proposition 4.14
.

There exists a full-image symmetric face-accessible
\mathcal{A}
-graph
\Gamma
, if and only if there exists

5
Nilpotent groups of finite Prüfer rank

In this section we prove Theorem
3.3
:

See
3.3

The idea is to extend a weaker version of the theorem due to Shafrir, and independently, due to Bodart, Ciobanu and Metcalfe:

Theorem 5.1

(
[
Sha24
, Corollary 1]
, see also
[
BCM24
, Proposition 19]
)
.

Let
N
be a finitely generated nilpotent group and
M
be a subsemigroup of
N
. If
M[N,N]=N
, then
M=N
. More generally, if
M[N,N]
is a finite-index subgroup of
N
, then
M
is a finite-index subgroup of
N
.

This result should also be compared to the following theorem:

Theorem 5.2

(Folklore, see
[
Khu93
, Theorem 2.2.3]
)
.

Let
G
be a nilpotent group and
H
a subgroup. Suppose that
H[G,G]=G[G,G]
, then
H=G
.

We will extend Theorem
5.1
from finitely generated nilpotent groups to infinitely generated subgroups of
\mathsf{UT}(d,\mathbb{K})
. It should be noted that Theorem
5.1
does not extend to general nilpotent groups contrary to Theorem
5.2
.
Recall the classic notions of
isolators
and
Prüfer rank
.

Definition 5.3
.

The
isolator
of a subset
X\subseteq G
is the subset

I(X)\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid\exists m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},\;g^{m}\in X\}.

Lemma 5.4

(
[
Khu93
, Theorem 2.5.8]
)
.

If
G
is nilpotent and
H
a subgroup, then
I(H)
is a subgroup.

Definition 5.5

(Prüfer rank)
.

The
Prüfer rank
of a group
G
, denoted
\rk(G)
, is the maximum number of generators needed to generate a finitely generated subgroup
H\leq G
.

For instance, every finitely generated subgroup of
(\mathbb{Q},+)
is cyclic, therefore
\rk(\mathbb{Q})=1
.

Lemma 5.6

(Folklore, see
[
LR04
, p.85, 3.]
)
.

Let
G
be a group

(i)

If
H\leq G
, then
\rk(H)\leq\rk(G)
.

(ii)

If
N\trianglelefteq G
, then
\rk(G)\leq\rk(N)+\rk(G/N)
.

Theorem 5.7

(
[
KK96
, Theorem 2.5]
)
.

If
G
is nilpotent and has finite Prüfer rank, then there exists a finite set
B\subset G
such that
I(\left\langle B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{grp}})=G
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.3
.
We recall
[x,y]
denotes the element
xyx^{-1}y^{-1}
, called the
commutator
of
x
and
y
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

We prove the result under a slightly weaker condition:
[N,N]
has finite Prüfer rank. Let’s first observe this condition is indeed satisfied if
N\leq\mathsf{UT}(d,\mathbb{K})
. Indeed,

\rk[N,N]\leq\rk(N)\leq\rk(\mathsf{UT}(d,\mathbb{K}))\leq\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{K})\cdot{d\choose 2}<\infty

using Lemma
5.6
(i) twice, then part (ii) iteratively on the lower central series of
\mathsf{UT}(d,\mathbb{K})
.

From now on, we only suppose that
\rk[N,N]<\infty
. Using Theorem
5.7
, there exists a finite set
B\subset[N,N]
such that
I(\left\langle B\right\rangle_{\mathrm{grp}})=[N,N]
. Each element of
[N,N]
(hence each element of
B
) can be written as a product of commutators, so we can in turn find a finite set
A\subset N
such that

I\big(\!\left\langle\left\{[a,a^{\prime}]\;\big|\;a,a^{\prime}\in A\right\}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{grp}}\big)\supseteq[N,N]

As
M[N,N]=N
, we find a finite set
X\subset M
such that, for each
a\in A
there exists
x,y\in X
such that
x=a
and
y=a^{-1}\pmod{[N,N]}
. Fix
g\in M
. We take
h\in M
such that
h=g^{-1}\pmod{[N,N]}
. Let
\tilde{N}=\left\langle g,h,A,X\right\rangle_{\mathrm{grp}}
and
\tilde{M}=M\cap\tilde{N}
. By construction, we have

\tilde{M}\cdot I([\tilde{N},\tilde{N}])\supseteq\tilde{M}\cdot[N,N]\supseteq\left\langle g,h,X\right\rangle\cdot[N,N]=\tilde{N}\cdot[N,N]\supseteq\tilde{N}

hence
\tilde{M}\cdot I_{\tilde{N}}([\tilde{N},\tilde{N}])=\tilde{N}
(where
I_{\tilde{N}}(X)\coloneqq\{g\in\tilde{N}:\exists m>0,\;x^{m}\in X\}=I(X)\cap\tilde{N}
).

As
\tilde{N}
is finitely generated, this implies that
\tilde{M}[\tilde{N},\tilde{N}]
is a finite-index subgroup of
\tilde{N}
. Theorem
5.1
gives that
\tilde{M}
is a subgroup of
\tilde{N}
. In particular

admits an inverse in
\tilde{M}
, hence in

. We conclude that

is a subgroup, hence

using Theorem

.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Theorem
3.3
doesn’t hold for more general nilpotent groups.
For example, it does not hold when
N
is a subgroup of
\mathsf{UT}(3,\mathbb{Q}(X))
, where
\mathbb{Q}(X)
denotes the field of rational functions over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Indeed, let
N
be the group generated by the following elements:

A_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1&X^{i}&X^{2i+1}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,\,B_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-X^{i}&X^{2i+1}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,\,C_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&X^{2i+1}\\
0&1&X^{i}\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,\,D_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&X^{2i+1}\\
0&1&-X^{i}\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

for
i=0,1,2,\ldots
, that is,
N=\left\langle{\left\{A_{i},B_{i},C_{i},D_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{grp}}
.
Then

N=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&g&f\\
0&1&h\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\;f,g,h\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\right\},\quad[N,N]=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0&f\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\,\middle|\;f\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\right\}.

The latter equality is justified by the fact that
[N,N]
contains the elements

A_{i}C_{0}A_{i}^{-1}C_{0}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&X^{i}\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},\;i=0,1,2,\ldots.

Let
M
be the semigroup
\left\langle{\left\{A_{i},B_{i},C_{i},D_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}\right\rangle
, then
M[N,N]=N
.
However, we have
M\neq N
because
I\notin M
.
Indeed, let

P=\begin{pmatrix}1&g&f\\
0&1&h\\
0&0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}

any non-empty product of
A_{i},B_{i},C_{i},D_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}
, where
m\geq 0
is the largest index used.
Then
\deg(g)\leq m,\deg(h)\leq m
. Furthermore,
\deg(f)=2m+1
, and the coefficient of the term
X^{2m+1}
in
f
is positive.
So
f\neq 0
, and
P\neq I
.

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this paper we proved decidability of the Identity Problem and the Group Problem in virtually solvable subgroups of
\mathsf{GL}(d,\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
.
An immediate open question is whether our decidability result still holds when the field
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
is replaced by other effectively computable fields such as
\mathbb{Q}(X)
or
\mathbb{F}_{p}(X)
(where
\mathbb{F}_{p}
denotes the finite field of cardinality
p
).
A number of interesting solvable groups are not embeddable in
\mathsf{GL}(d,\overline{\mathbb{Q}})
, such as the
wreath product

\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
(which is embeddable in
\mathsf{GL}(2,\mathbb{Q}(X))
) and the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}\coloneqq\mathbb{F}_{2}[X^{\pm}]\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
(which is embeddable in
\mathsf{GL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{2}(X))
).
Many such groups still have decidable Identity Problem and Group Problem (direct consequence of Theorem
3.7
, also
[
Don24
]
), and the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
even has decidable Semigroup Membership
[
LSZ15
]
.
Therefore, one might expect the Identity Problem and the Group Problem to be decidable for virtually solvable matrix groups over some well-behaved fields other than
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
.
On the other hand, a celebrated result of Kharlampovich
[
Kha81
]
shows that general solvable groups are highly intractable: there exists a 3-step solvable group (a group
G
such that
G^{(3)}
is trivial), where the
Word Problem
is undecidable. Moreover, there exists center-by-metabelian groups with decidable Word Problem and undecidable Torsion Problem (hence undecidable Identity Problem)
[
ALM14
, Proposition 3.2]
. Therefore, one might expect the Identity Problem and the Group Problem to be undecidable for solvable matrix groups over more complicated fields.

As demonstrated in Remark
5.8
, one of our key theorems (Theorem
3.3
) no longer holds for the group
\mathsf{UT}(3,\mathbb{Q}(X))
.
Therefore, our proof of decidability for the Group Problem does not apply to solvable subgroups of
\mathsf{GL}(3,\mathbb{Q}(X))
.

\mathcal{L}
are invariant under scaling by a positive real, meaning
x\in Q\iff r\cdot x\in Q
for all cells
Q\in\mathcal{L}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Let
\pi\colon\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}\times\mathbb{R}^{K}\rightarrow\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*},(v,\boldsymbol{\alpha})\mapsto v
be the canonical projection.
For each
Q\in\mathcal{L}
, define the two-element partition
\{\pi(Q),\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}\setminus\pi(Q)\}
of
\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}
, and define

\mathcal{P}\coloneqq\bigvee_{Q\in\mathcal{L}}\{\pi(Q),\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}\setminus\pi(Q)\}.

By this definition, take any
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
Q\in\mathcal{L}
with
\pi^{-1}(P)\cap Q\neq\emptyset
; then for
v,v^{\prime}\in P
, there exists
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
with
(v,\boldsymbol{\alpha})\in Q
if and only if there exists
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
with
(v^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime})\in Q
.
See
[
Don24
, Figure 28]
for an illustration.

It is important to note that the partitions
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}},\mathcal{L}_{O},\mathcal{L}_{I_{\xi}},\xi\in\Xi,
are all defined using equalities and inequalities with
rational
coefficients.
Also, each inequality is strict, so every cell
Q\in\mathcal{L}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}
is relatively open (a polyhedron is called relative open if it is open in the smallest linear space containing it).
In other words, each cell is defined by a combination of equalities and
strict
inequalities.
We also point out that, like the cells of
\mathcal{L}
, the cell of
\mathcal{P}
are invariant under scaling by a positive real, meaning
x\in P\iff r\cdot x\in P
for all cells
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.
By the definition of
\mathcal{L}
, we immediately obtain the following.

(Generalization of

Fix a change of coordinates

Proving Theorem
4.15
: induction and a double procedure.

We now give the full proof of Theorem
4.15
.
As in
[
Don24
]
, the overall strategy is to use induction on
n
, while deciding the Conditions
(LocR)
and
(LocInf)
from the local-global principle (Theorem
C.2
).

See
4.15

Proof.

We use induction on
n
.
As remarked earlier, the base case
n=0
degenerates into linear programming (given an
\mathbb{R}
-submodule
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, decide whether
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{K}
contains an element).
Suppose we have a decision procedure for all
n_{0}<n
, we now construct a procedure for
n
.

By Theorem
C.2
it suffices to decide whether the two conditions
(LocR)
and
(LocInf)
are both satisfied.
First we check if
(LocR)
is true using Proposition
C.8
.
If
(LocR)
is false then we return False and conclude there is no
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathcal{M}\cap\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
satisfying (
7
).
If
(LocR)
is true then we proceed.

We now run the two following procedures
in parallel
:

1.

Procedure A:
We recursively enumerate all elements of the
\mathbb{Z}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{n}^{\pm}]
-module:

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}h_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{g}_{j}\;\middle|\;h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}[X_{1}^{\pm},\ldots,X_{n}^{\pm}]\right\}.

For each element
\boldsymbol{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
, check if
\boldsymbol{f}
is in
\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
and if it satisfies Property (
7
).
This can be done in the following way:
since the entries of
\boldsymbol{f}
contain finitely many monomials, it suffices to check Property (
7
) for a finite number of
v
.
Indeed, since each of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{K}
has only finitely many monomials, there exists a partition
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{f}}
of
\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)^{*}
such that for each cell
L\in\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{f}}
and for each
\xi\in\Xi
, the set
M_{v}(I_{\xi},\boldsymbol{f})
is the same for all
v\in L
.
Furthermore, for each cell
L\in\mathcal{L}_{O}
, the set
O_{v}
is the same for all
v\in L
.
Therefore, it suffices to check Property (
7
) for one vector
v
in each cell of the partition
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{f}}\vee\mathcal{L}_{O}
.
This can be done in finite time for any given
\boldsymbol{f}
.
If some element
\boldsymbol{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}
is in
\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
and satisfies Property (
7
), we stop the procedure and return True.

2.

Procedure B:
We recursively enumerate all
A\in\mathsf{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
For each
A
and
d
, compute
Op(A,d)
using Lemma
C.18
and the induction hypothesis on
n
.
Using Lemma
C.20
, we check if the statement
(LocInfCell)
(a) from Proposition
C.19
is false.
If for some
A,d
, the statement
(LocInfCell)
(a) is false, then we stop the procedure and return False.

We claim that one of the two above procedures must stop.

Indeed, if
\mathcal{M}
contains an element of
\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
satisfying Property (
7
), then there exists an element
\boldsymbol{f}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
that satisfies Property (
7
) (see Lemma
4.16
).
In this case, Procedure A terminates by finding an element
\boldsymbol{f}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
that satisfies Property (
7
).

If
\mathcal{M}
does not contain an element of
\left(\mathbb{A}^{+}\right)^{K}
satisfying Property (
7
), then by Theorem
C.2
, Condition
(LocInf)
must be false (since we have already checked
(LocR)
to be true).
By the chain of Propositions
C.10
,
C.16
and
C.19
, the statement
(LocInfCell)
(a) must be false for some
A\in\mathsf{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
.
In this case, Procedure B terminates by finding
A\in\mathsf{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and
d\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
where the statement
(LocInfCell)
(a) is false.

Therefore, by running Procedure A and Procedure B in parallel, we obtain an algorithm that always terminates for
n
.
∎
