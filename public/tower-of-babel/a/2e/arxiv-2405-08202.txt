The mean field stubborn voter model
Lisa Hartung Institut für Mathematik, Johannes Gutenberg-Universität Mainz lhartung@uni-mainz.de, Christian Mönch Independent researcher cmoench25@gmail.com

Abstract
We analyse the effect of agent-dependent heavy-tailed waiting times in the voter model on the complete graph with N N vertices. We derive a novel scaling limit and show the existence of a limiting infinite voter model on the slowest updating sites. We further derive the consensus probabilities in the limit model explicitly. In the mean-field setting, the limit is determined by the extreme-value landscape of the waiting times and depends only on the tail index. To obtain these results, we study the coalescing system of random walks that is dual to the limit voter model and prove, among other auxiliary results, that it comes down from infinity.

Introduction & main results

We investigate a variant of the voter model with agent-dependent update rates, introduced in the (socio-) physics literature by Masuda, Gibert and Redner under the name
heterogeneous voter model

[
12
]
. As voters with small update rates update slowly, we prefer to refer to this model as the
stubborn voter model
. In the mean-field setting, voters sit on the complete graph with vertices
[N]=\{1,\dots,N\}.
A voter at site
x\in[N]
is initially assigned a type (or opinion)
\smash{\eta_{0}^{(N)}(x)\in\{0,1\}}
and a clock whose inter-ring times are i.i.d.
\smash{\operatorname{Exp}(1/w_{x}^{(N)})}
, where the mean waiting times
\smash{w_{1}^{(N)}\geq w_{2}^{(N)}\geq\dots\geq w_{N}^{(N)}>0}
are the order statistics of
N
i.i.d. copies of a positive heavy-tailed random variable
w_{1}
. We assume that
w_{1}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}(w_{1}>t)=t^{-\alpha}L(t),\qquad\alpha\in(0,1),

(1.1)

where
L
is slowly varying at infinity. Whenever any clock rings, the corresponding voter copies the opinion of a uniformly chosen neighbour, which gives rise to a Markov process
\smash{(\eta_{t}^{(N)}(x))_{t\geq 0}}
with right continuous trajectories in
\{0,1\}^{[N]}
.

We show that a novel scaling limit emerges as the dynamics are dominated by the voters with the highest waiting times. Moreover, the limit depends only on the parameter
\alpha
.

The homogeneous voter model on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has been extensively studied in the 1970s and 1980s, see
[
1
,
7
,
15
,
2
]
for some important early results and Liggett’s monograph
[
11
]
for an overview of the classical theory in the general context of stochastic interacting particle systems. With the advent of Network Science in the last 20 years, interest in models for consensus dynamics has been renewed, particularly for agents that are networked in an inhomogeneous fashion as opposed to the classical translation invariant setting on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. As already mentioned, the finite-population voter model with agent-dependent update rates was introduced by Masuda, Gibert and Redner under the name
heterogeneous voter model

[
12
]
; related works have studied the influence of ‘stubborn’ or ‘zealous’ agents on opinion dynamics, see for instance
[
13
,
20
,
18
,
19
]
. Mathematically rigorous results for zealous/stubborn voters were obtained in
[
8
]
and
[
14
]
. In the latter models, stubbornness is usually modelled by the presence of a few selected opinions that
never
change, whereas we assume the presence of an inherent heavy-tailed stubbornness distribution that leads to some voters changing their opinion more slowly than others. The resulting model is closer in spirit to trap models and other dynamics in heavy-tailed random environments, except that here the heavy-tailed landscape interacts with an opinion system through the voter-model duality.

We now provide an overview of our main results. We call the sites with the largest weights
slow sites
: a large weight
w_{x}
implies a small clock rate
1/w_{x}
, so these sites take, on average, the longest to update their opinion.
Assume that the common law of
w_{1}
satisfies (
1.1
).
Let
\big(w_{i}^{(N)}\big)_{i=1}^{N}
denote the order statistics of the weights, with
w_{1}^{(N)}
being the largest weight. Let
(a_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be any deterministic sequence such that

N\mathbb{P}(w_{1}>a_{N})\longrightarrow 1\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty.

(1.2)

In the exact Pareto case, one may take
a_{N}=N^{1/\alpha}
. The slow sites then have weights of order
a_{N}
. More precisely, let
(\chi_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d.
\operatorname{Exp}(1)
-distributed and set

\xi_{i}=\Big(\sum_{j\in[i]}\chi_{j}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}},\quad i\in\mathbb{N},

then it is well known that

a_{N}^{-1}\left(\big(w_{i}^{(N)}\big)_{i=1}^{N},\sum_{i\in[N]}w_{i}^{(N)}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\left((\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty},\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\xi_{i}\right)\quad\text{ as }N\to\infty,

(1.3)

see for instance
[
16
, Chapter 5]
and
[
10
, Section 2.3]
; for the exact Pareto formulation above see also
[
17
, Theorem 2.33]
. Here and throughout
\overset{d}{\to}
denotes convergence in distribution. This convergence of the extreme landscape is the basic mechanism behind our results and is the reason why the limiting process should be viewed as a domain-of-attraction object for heavy-tailed mean-field voter models. Accordingly, the first main result is stated at the level of ranked-environment convergence: once (
1.3
) holds, the later arguments no longer use the exact microscopic law.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
w^{(N)}
and
\xi
be as in (
1.3
) and let
(\eta_{0}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
denote a sequence of initial conditions satisfying

\eta_{0}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\eta_{0}\text{ as }N\to\infty,

for some random or deterministic
\eta_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then the corresponding Markov processes
(\eta_{t}^{(N)})_{t\geq 0}
satisfy

\eta^{(N)}_{a_{N}(\cdot)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\eta_{(\cdot)}\text{ as }N\to\infty,

where the limit process
\eta
has initial distribution
\eta_{0}
and transition rates governed by
\xi
, i.e. conditionally on
\xi
,
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a voter model with update rates
\xi_{x},x\in\mathbb{N}.

Remark 1.2

(Remark (Universality, domain of attraction on the complete graph).)
.

Let
w_{1}
satisfy (
1.1
). Then, after temporal rescaling by
a_{N}
, the limiting infinite voter model depends only on the tail index
\alpha
and is independent of the choice of
L
.

Note that the existence of the limit process is highly non-trivial, since on the complete graph with vertex set
\mathbb{N}
, there is no such thing as a ‘uniformly chosen neighbour’ of a vertex. In fact, the assumption of heavy-tailed waiting times is essential for the limit process to exist. Throughout, we work under a fixed environment (quenched), i.e. under the distributions

\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{\xi}=\mathcal{L}(\eta|\xi)\qquad\text{or}\qquad\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}^{w}=\mathcal{L}(\eta^{(N)}|w).

We next state the consensus probabilities in the limit model.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\eta_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be any initial distribution and let

\tau=\inf_{t>0}\{\eta_{t}\equiv 0\text{ or }\eta_{t}\equiv 1\}

denote the consensus time. Then,

\mathbb{E}[\tau]<\infty\text{ for almost every realisation of }\xi.

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{\tau}\equiv 1)=\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\eta_{0}(x)\xi_{x}}{\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\xi_{x}}.

(1.4)

For completeness, we also state the corresponding formula for the consensus probability in the finite systems.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\eta_{0}^{(N)}
be any initial distribution and let

\tau^{(N)}=\inf_{t>0}\{\eta_{t}^{(N)}\equiv 0\text{ or }\eta_{t}^{(N)}\equiv 1\}

denote the consensus time.
Then,

\sup_{N}a_{N}^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\tau^{(N)}]<\infty\text{ for almost every realisation of }w.

Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(\eta_{\tau}^{(N)}\equiv 1)=\frac{\sum_{x\in[N]}\eta^{(N)}_{0}(x)w_{x}^{(N)}}{\sum_{x\in[N]}w^{(N)}_{x}}.

(1.5)

Note that the consensus time for
\eta^{(N)}
is trivially finite, because the system has only finitely many states and two of them are absorbing. However, due to the divergence of timescales, the expected consensus times for
\eta^{(N)}
are not uniformly bounded without rescaling. Below, we prove Theorem
1.3
directly, but an alternative way to derive the asymptotic consensus probabilities is to combine (
1.5
) with Theorem
1.1
and (
1.3
).

Remark 1.5

(Remark (Extension beyond the complete graph))
.

The complete graph is the only setting in which the geometry is fully homogenised and the slow-site landscape is the only source of inhomogeneity. We conjecture that an analogous domain-of-attraction statement should remain valid for broader mean-field graph sequences, at least when the dual random walk mixes on a time scale negligible compared to the extreme waiting-time scale and when its harmonic measure on the set of deepest traps is asymptotically close to uniform.

For asymptotically regular dense graphs, one may therefore expect the same limiting voter model, possibly after a deterministic time change. For more general graph classes, however, the effective motion between slow sites should depend on the graph geometry through a non-trivial trace kernel, so that the correct limit is likely a graph-dependent variant of our infinite voter model rather than a universal one.

Establishing such an extension would require new control of trace processes and meeting probabilities and would lie beyond the scope of the present paper.

Remark 1.6

(Remark (countably many opinions).)
.

It will be clear from the proofs that Theorems
1.1
–
1.4
remain valid mutatis mutandis, if opinions are drawn from a countable alphabet instead of
\{0,1\}
. In particular, we may initialise the infinite system in such a way that each voter in
\mathbb{N}
carries a distinct opinion. The expected consensus time in this case is still finite and the probability that the whole system ends up with the initial opinion of vertex
x\in\mathbb{N}
is
\xi_{x}/\sum_{z\in\mathbb{N}}\xi_{z}
.

Structure of the paper

In Section 2, we introduce the system of coalescing random walks that forms the dual of the voter model. Sections 3–5 are devoted to the dual process. In Section 3, we construct the Markov process
X
that describes the motion of a single walker among slow sites indexed by
\mathbb{N}
and derive some of its properties that are of independent interest. In Section 4, we prove an abstract ranked-environment convergence theorem for the walk
Y^{(N)}
on
[N]
and extend it to corresponding coalescing systems of finitely many walks. Combined with the extreme-value convergence (
1.3
), this yields the domain-of-attraction statement on the complete graph. In Section 5, we prove Theorem
5.1
, which shows that the limit system of coalescing walks comes down from infinity and therefore allows us to treat infinitely many lineages in the voter model simultaneously. Finally, in Section 6, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
.

2
The dual system: coalescing random walks with heavy tailed rates

The following duality relation for the voter model is well known, see e.g.
[
11
, Chapter V]
: Given
w^{(N)}
, consider the continuous time random walk
Y^{(N)}
on
[N]
with transition rates

q^{(N)}(x,y)=\frac{1}{Nw^{(N)}_{x}},\quad x,y\in[N].

Provided with some initial condition
\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\in\{0,1\}^{[N]}
, which we interpret as the positions of
\sum_{x\in[N]}\gamma^{(N)}_{0}(x)
particles, we let each of the particles move independently according to the walk
Y^{(N)}
and coalesce particles which are located in the same site. This leads to a Markovian system
\big(\gamma_{t}^{(N)}\big)_{t\geq 0}
of coalescing random walks that is dual to the voter model in the sense that for all
t\geq 0,A\subset[N]
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\eta^{(N)}_{t}(x)\equiv 1\text{ on }A\big|\eta^{(N)}_{0}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\eta^{(N)}_{0}(x)\equiv 1\text{ on }\supp\gamma^{(N)}_{t}\big|\supp\gamma^{(N)}_{0}=A\big),

(2.1)

where
\supp\gamma_{t}=\{x:\gamma_{t}(x)=1\},t\geq 0.
Note that the dual dynamics can be reconstructed from the primal dynamics and vice versa. We make use of this fact in Section 6 when proving Theorem
1.1
through duality.

3
Limiting walk on the slow sites

Construction and properties of the limit process

The convergence in (
1.3
) indicates that a natural choice of metric on the space of environments is the
\ell^{1}
-distance.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\xi^{(n)},\xi\in\ell_{1},n\geq 1,
be non-negative then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\xi^{(n)}_{x}=\xi_{x}
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\xi^{(n)}_{x}=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\xi_{x}

if and only if
\xi^{(n)}\to\xi
in

Proof.

The second assertion obviously implies the first one. For the converse implication, fix

and write
Z_{\geq N}(\xi)
for
\sum_{x\geq N}\xi_{x}
, then

|\xi-\xi^{(n)}|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\sum_{x<N}|\xi_{x}^{(n)}-\xi_{x}|+Z_{\geq N}(\xi)+Z_{\geq N}(\xi^{(n)}).

Fix
\epsilon>0
, then
N
so large that
Z_{\geq N}(\xi)\leq\epsilon
. Fix

so large that
|Z_{\geq N}(\xi)-Z_{\geq N}(\xi^{(n)})|\leq\epsilon
whenever

. We obtain for

|\xi-\xi^{(n)}|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\sum_{x<N}|\xi_{x}^{(n)}-\xi_{x}|+3\epsilon,

and taking the limit

yields
|\xi-\xi^{(n)}|_{\ell^{1}}<3\epsilon
due to coordinate-wise convergence, which concludes the argument since
\epsilon
is chosen arbitrarily.
∎

(3.1)

Here,

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

We consider the excursions of
X^{(M)}
from
[N]
. Set
T_{j}=T_{j}^{[N]}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Let, for fixed
t>0
,

\ell(t)=\max\{n\geq 0:T_{n}\leq\phi_{M,N}(t)\}.

Note that for any
t\geq 0
,

\phi_{M,N}(t)-t=T_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}E_{j}\geq 0,

with
E_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}
i.i.d. copies of
E^{[N]}
. In particular, for any fixed
\epsilon>0

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(\phi_{M,N}(t)-t>2\epsilon)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(T_{0}\geq\epsilon)+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}E_{j}>\epsilon\Big).

(3.8)

For the second term on the right hand side of (
3.8
), we first note that it does not depend on the initial condition (
3.4
). Moreover,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}E_{j}>\epsilon\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{L}E_{j}>\epsilon\Big)+\mathbb{P}(\ell(t)>L),\quad L\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.9)

We denote the incursion lengths inside
[N]
by
I_{1},I_{2},\dots
. Then, by Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathbb{P}(\ell(t)>L)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{L}I_{j}<t\Big)\leq\textup{e}^{\lambda t}\mathbb{E}\Big[\textup{e}^{-\lambda I_{1}}\Big]^{L}\leq\textup{e}^{-q_{N}(\lambda)L+\lambda t},\quad L\in\mathbb{N},\lambda>0,

where we used that by (
3.4
),

\mathbb{E}\Big[\textup{e}^{-\lambda I_{1}}\Big]=\frac{(M-N)(1-q_{N}(\lambda))}{M-N(1-q_{N}(\lambda))}\leq 1-{q_{N}(\lambda)},\quad\lambda>0,

(3.10)

with
q_{N}(\lambda)\equiv 1-N^{-1}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{1}{1+\lambda\xi_{x}}
.
Fixing
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain for any choice of
L=L(N,M)
satisfying
L>K_{0}N

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{M>N}\mathbb{P}(\ell(t)>L)\leq\textup{e}^{-K_{0}Z(\lambda)+\lambda t},\quad\lambda>0,

(3.11)

since
Nq_{N}(\lambda)=\sum_{y\in[N]}\frac{\lambda\xi_{y}}{1+\lambda\xi_{y}}\to Z(\lambda)
as
N\to\infty.
On the other hand, for
L=2K_{0}N
, we bound the second term in (
3.9
) from above by

\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{L}E_{j}>\epsilon\Big)\leq\frac{2K_{0}N\mathbb{E}E_{1}}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{CK_{0}}{\epsilon}N^{1-1/\alpha}

(3.12)

for some
C
depending only on
\xi
, by Markov’s inequality and (
3.6
). We may thus conclude from (
3.9
) and (
3.11
) that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{M>N}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell(t)}E_{j}>\epsilon\Big)=0,

(3.13)

since
K_{0}
can be made arbitrarily large. It remains to show that
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(T_{0}>\epsilon)
vanishes. If
X_{0}\in[N]
, then
T_{0}=0
. Otherwise, the first jump of
X
, say at time
\sigma_{0}
, either sends
X
to
[N]
in which case
\sigma_{0}=T_{0}
or to a uniformly distributed point in
[M]\setminus[N]
in which case the remaining time to enter
[N]
has precisely the distribution of
E_{1}
. We conclude

\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(T_{0}>\epsilon)\leq\sum_{x\in[M]\setminus[N]}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\sigma_{0}>\epsilon/2)\nu(x)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}>\epsilon/2)

and thus

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{M>N}\mathbb{P}_{\nu}(T_{0}>\epsilon)\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{M>N}\big(\mathbb{P}(\tau_{N+1}>\epsilon/2)+\mathbb{P}(E_{1}>\epsilon/2)\big)=0,

(3.14)

where we have used that
\sigma_{0}
is dominated by an
\operatorname{Exp}(1/\xi_{N+1})
random variable
\tau_{N+1}
uniformly in the starting point
x\in[M]\setminus[N]
and that
\sup_{M>N}\mathbb{P}(E_{1}>\epsilon/2)\in O(\epsilon^{-1}N^{-1/\alpha})
by (
3.6
) and Markov’s inequality. Combining (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) according to (
3.8
) concludes the proof for fixed
\epsilon>0
. The assertion for decaying
\epsilon_{N}
follows from the observation that (
3.11
) holds independently of the choice of
\epsilon
and that in the remaining estimates (
3.12
) and (
3.14
) the term on the right hand side of (
3.12
) dominates.
∎

We next describe the limiting process associated with the sequence
(X^{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
P^{(N)}=(P^{(N)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the semigroup of
X^{(N)}
given
\xi.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
(\nu^{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
be distributions with
\supp\nu^{M}\subset[M]
for each
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
\nu^{M}([N])\to 0
for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a family of probability laws
(Q_{t})_{t>0}
on
\mathbb{N}
, independent of
(\nu^{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
, such that

Q_{t}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\nu^{M}P^{(M)}_{t},\;t>0,\quad\mathbf{P}\text{-almost surely.}

Before we give the proof, we formulate an auxiliary result that is also useful later on.

Lemma 3.8
.

For any
M>N>2
and
t>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(X^{(M)}_{t}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus[N]

\displaystyle|X_{0}^{(M)}\in[M]\setminus[N])

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M-N}{M-N}\left(\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}+\frac{N}{s_{N}}\left(\frac{t}{\xi([N])}+C\right)\right),

where
C
is a universal constant and

s_{N}=s_{N}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1/\xi_{N+1}\wedge N/2\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[N])\right).

Proof.

Let
I=I^{[N]}
and
E=E^{[N]}
be the generic incursion and excursion lengths of
X^{(M)}
from
[N]
. For any
M>N
, we set

Z_{t}^{(M)}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{(M)}\in[M]\setminus[N]\}},\quad t\geq 0.

Conditionally on
X_{0}^{(M)}\in[M]\setminus[N]
, the starting point
X_{0}^{(M)}
is uniformly distributed on
[M]\setminus[N]
and thus the conditional distribution of
(Z_{t}^{(M)})_{t\geq 0}
is that of a simple two state renewal process, with transition function
p^{M}(r)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(Z_{r}^{(M)}=1|X_{0}^{(M)}\in[M]\setminus[N])
given by

p^{M}(r)=\bar{F}_{E}(r)+\int_{0}^{r}\bar{F}_{E}(r-s)\,\textup{d}\ell^{M}(s),r\geq 0.

(3.15)

Here,
\bar{F}_{E}(r)=\mathbb{P}(E>r)
and
\ell^{M}
is the renewal measure associated with the generic cycle length
E+I
. Let us first provide bounds on
\bar{F}_{E}
. Let
G
denote the number of stays inside
[M]\setminus[N]
that
X^{(M)}
accumulates during a generic excursion
E
, and let
H_{1},H_{2},\dots
denote independent generic
[M]\setminus[N]
-holding times. We have, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E>t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{G}H_{j}>t\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{N}{M}(1-N/M)^{j-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}H_{i}>t\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{N}{M}(1-N/M)^{j-1}\textup{e}^{-st}(\mathbb{E}\textup{e}^{sH_{1}})^{j}.

Using (
3.7
) and taking
s
equal to

s_{N}=s_{N}(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1/\xi_{N+1}\wedge N/2\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[N])\right),

which is positive
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely for all
N>2
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E>t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{M-N}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x=N+1}^{M}\frac{1}{1-s_{N}\xi_{x}}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N}{M-N}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{M-N+2s_{N}\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[N])}{M}\right)^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}\frac{N}{M-N}\frac{2M-N}{N}=\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}\frac{2M-N}{M-N}.

The second inequality uses a bound similar to (
3.2
). The third inequality is also true by the choice
s_{N}
. Inserting this estimate into (
3.15
) yields

p^{M}(t)\leq\frac{2M-N}{M-N}\left(\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}+\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}(t-s)}\,\textup{d}\ell^{M}(s)\right).

(3.16)

Note that
\ell^{M}
is dominated by the renewal measure
r^{N}
associated with the typical holding time
H=H^{[N]}
inside
[N]
, because each cycle contains at least one stay inside
[N]
. We obtain

\int_{0}^{t}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}(t-s)}\,\textup{d}\ell^{M}(s)\leq\frac{r^{N}(t)}{s_{N}}\int_{0}^{t}s_{N}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}(t-s)}\frac{1}{r^{N}(t)}\,\textup{d}r^{N}(s)\leq\frac{r^{N}(t)}{s_{N}},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that the integral can be viewed as the distribution function at
t
of the sum of two independent random variables. We conclude that

p^{M}(t)\leq\frac{2M-N}{M-N}\textup{e}^{-s_{N}t}+\frac{2M-N}{M-N}\frac{r^{N}(t)}{s_{N}},

(3.17)

and the renewal function
r^{N}(t)
satisfies the bound

r^{N}(t)\leq\frac{t}{\mathbb{E}H}+C\frac{\mathbb{E}H^{2}}{(\mathbb{E}H)^{2}}=\frac{tN}{\xi([N])}+C\frac{2N\sum_{x=1}^{N}\xi_{x}^{2}}{\xi([N])^{2}},

where
C
is some universal constant, see e.g.
[
3
]
. Combining this bound with (
3.17
) concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.7
.

We set

R_{t}^{M}(y)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{x\in[M]}\delta_{x}P^{(M)}_{t}(y)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(X^{(M)}_{t}=y),\quad y\in[M],\,M\in\mathbb{N},

and divide the proof into three parts: first we establish tightness of
(R_{t}^{(M)})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
, then we show the existence of a unique accumulation point, and finally we argue that the uniform distributions in the definition of
R^{(M)}_{t}
may be replaced by any sequence
(\nu^{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
that puts vanishing mass on the slow sites.

Part (i) – tightness:
Let
\epsilon>0
be given and fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let now
M_{0}>N/\epsilon
. We obtain

R_{t}^{M}(\mathbb{N}\setminus[N])\leq\epsilon+\frac{M-N}{M}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(X^{(M)}_{t}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus[N]|X_{0}^{(M)}\in[M]\setminus[N]),\quad M>M_{0},t>0.

(3.18)

Thus, Lemma
3.8
implies that for some constant
C
depending only on
\xi
, and all sufficiently large
M
,

R_{t}^{M}(\mathbb{N}\setminus[N])\leq\epsilon+CtN^{1-1/\alpha},

(3.19)

since
1/s_{N}=O(N^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}})

\mathbb{P}
-alomst surely. It follows that
(R_{t}^{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
is indeed tight.

Part (ii) – uniqueness:
We use a renewal theoretic argument to obtain the uniqueness of the accumulation point. Fix
y\in\mathbb{N}
and let

W_{s}^{(M)}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{(M)}_{s}=y\}},\quad s\geq 0.

Since, for each
y\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(X^{(M)}_{s}=y)=\mathbb{P}(W_{s}^{(M)}=1)
, it suffices to show that the sequence
(W^{(M)})_{M\geq 1}
of renewal processes converges in distribution, since this identifies the limits of
(R_{t}^{(M)}(y))_{M\in\mathbb{N}},y\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, on the event
\{X_{0}^{(M)}\neq y\}
, each
W^{(M)}
is entirely characterised by the sums

\sum_{j=1}^{n}E_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{j\text{ odd}}+I_{j}\mathbbm{1}_{j\text{ even}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
E_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}
are i.i.d. copies of
E(M)=I^{[M]\setminus\{y\}}
and
I_{j},j\in\mathbb{N}
are i.i.d. copies of
I(M):=I^{\{y\}}
, respectively. Since
\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}(X_{0}^{(M)}\neq y)=1
, it is thus easy to see that convergence in distribution of
E(M),I(M)
as
M\to\infty
implies the convergence of
(W^{(M)})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
. By (
3.3
),
I(M)
is the independent sum of
G\sim\operatorname{Geo}(1-1/M)
copies of
\operatorname{Exp}(1/\xi_{y})
, thus its convergence to
\operatorname{Exp}(1/\xi_{y})
is immediate. To obtain the limit of
E(M)
, we use the corresponding characteristic function

\psi_{M}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{e}^{i\lambda E(M)}\right]=\frac{\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{x\in[M]\setminus\{y\}}\frac{1}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}}{M-\sum_{x\in[M]\setminus\{y\}}\frac{1}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

which is obtained in the same manner as (
3.4
). The pointwise limit of
(\psi_{M})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
is simply

\psi(\lambda)=\frac{1}{1-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{y\}}\frac{i\lambda\xi_{x}}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R},

(3.20)

which is a well-defined point on the complex unit circle since
|1-i\lambda\xi_{x}|\geq 1
and thus
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{y\}}\left|\frac{i\lambda\xi_{x}}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}\right|\leq\lambda\xi(\mathbb{N})<\infty
, for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.
Lévy’s continuity theorem now yields that
\psi
is the characteristic function of a probability distribution if it is continuous in
0
, which follows from

\left|\frac{1}{1-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{y\}}\frac{i\lambda\xi_{x}}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}}-1\right|\leq\frac{\left|\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{y\}}\frac{i\lambda\xi_{x}}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}\right|}{\left|1-\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{y\}}\frac{i\lambda\xi_{x}}{1-i\lambda\xi_{x}}\right|}\leq\lambda\xi(\mathbb{N})|\psi(\lambda)|=\lambda\xi(\mathbb{N}).

We conclude, that
E((M))_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(I(M))_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
both have well-defined distributional limits, whence
(W^{(M)})_{M\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution to a well-defined limiting renewal process.

Part (iii) – independence of initial distribution:
Let
\tau
denote the first time that
{X}^{(M)}
jumps, then for any
y\in[M]

\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{M}}({X}^{(M)}_{\tau+s}=y)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{u}}({X}^{(M)}_{s}=y)=R_{s}^{M}(y),\quad s>0,

and, using the Markov property,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{M}}({X}^{(M)}_{t}=y,t>\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{M}}({X}^{(M)}_{\tau+(t-\tau)}=y,t>\tau)

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in[M]}\nu^{M}(x)\int_{0}^{t}R_{t-s}^{M}(y)\frac{\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{x}}}{\xi_{x}}\,\textup{d}s.

(3.22)

By the assumption that
\nu^{M}([N])
vanishes for any fixed
N
, we obtain that

\mathbb{P}(\tau>t)\leq\nu^{M}([N])+\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{N}}}{\xi_{N}},

which can be made arbitrarily small by choosing
N
large and then increasing
M
. Hence
\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{M}}(\tau<t)=1
. On the other hand, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in[M]\setminus[N]}\nu^{M}(x)\int_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle R_{t-s}^{M}(y)\frac{\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{x}}}{\xi_{x}}\,\textup{d}s\leq\sum_{x\in[M]}\nu^{M}(x)\int_{0}^{t}R_{t-s}^{M}(y)\frac{\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{x}}}{\xi_{x}}\,\textup{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in[M]\setminus[N]}\nu^{M}(x)\int_{0}^{t}R_{t-s}^{M}(y)\frac{\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{x}}}{\xi_{x}}\,\textup{d}s+\nu^{M}([N]),

and the weak convergence of
\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)
to
\delta_{0}
as
\lambda\to\infty
implies that both the first and the last term can be brought arbitrarily close to
R_{t}^{M}(y)
by choosing first
N
and then
M
large. It thus follows from (
3.21
) that

\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\nu^{M}}(X_{t}=y)=\lim_{M\to\infty}R_{t}^{M}(y)=Q_{t}(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{N},t>0.

∎

(Q_{t})_{t>0}
describe the limiting distribution of the processes

(3.26)

We conclude that

|q(\xi)-q(\xi^{\prime})|_{\ell^{1}}\leq\sum_{x=1}^{N}|q(\xi,\{x\})-q(\xi^{\prime},\{x\})|+C^{\prime}(\xi,\delta)tN^{1-1/\alpha},\quad\xi^{\prime}\in U(\delta,\xi),

(3.27)

from which continuity of
q(\cdot)
follows.

To address continuity for
P_{t}
in
\xi
, we write
P_{t}=P_{t}(\xi),P^{\prime}_{t}=P_{t}(\xi^{\prime})
and bound

\displaystyle|P_{t}f-P^{\prime}_{t}f|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x}|f(x)\mathbb{P}(\tau_{x}>t)-f(x)\mathbb{P}(\tau^{\prime}_{x}>t)|

(3.28)

\displaystyle+\sup_{x}\left|\int_{0}^{t}Q^{\prime}_{t-s}f\frac{1}{\xi^{\prime}_{x}}\textup{e}^{-s/\xi^{\prime}_{x}}-Q_{t-s}f\frac{1}{\xi_{x}}\textup{e}^{-s/\xi_{x}}\,\textup{d}s\right|.

Since
|f(x)|\leq 1
, the first term is at most

\sup_{x}|\textup{e}^{-t/\xi_{x}}-\textup{e}^{-t/\xi^{\prime}_{x}}|

which vanishes as
\xi^{\prime}\to\xi
in
\ell^{1}
. Similarly, the second term is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\int_{0}^{t}|(Q^{\prime}_{t-s}-Q_{t-s})|\frac{1}{\xi^{\prime}_{x}}\textup{e}^{-s/\xi^{\prime}_{x}}\,\textup{d}s+\sup_{x}|F_{\tau^{\prime}(x)}(t)-F_{\tau(x)}(t)|,

(3.29)

where
F_{\tau_{x}}
denotes the distribution function of
\tau_{x}
. It is straightforward to see, using that
C(\xi^{\prime},\delta)
does not depend on
t
in (
3.27
), that both terms vanish for any
t>0
as
\xi^{\prime}\to\xi
in
\ell^{1}
.
∎

The process
X
enters
\mathbb{N}
according to the entrance law
(Q_{t})_{t\geq 0}
at some site
x
and the random walk dynamics correspond to waiting an
\operatorname{Exp}(\xi_{x}^{-1})
-distributed time at site

, then jumping to infinity and instantaneously re-entering
\mathbb{N}
again via
(Q_{t})_{t>0}
. We next determine the invariant distribution of
X
.

Lemma 3.11
.

The invariant distribution of
X
is given by
\mu(x)=\frac{1}{\xi(\mathbb{N})}\xi_{x},x\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

X^{(N)}
is clearly ergodic, hence has a unique invariant distribution
\mu_{N}
. It is straightforward to deduce from the occupation time formula

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{(N)}_{s}=x\}}\,\textup{d}s}{t}=\mu_{N}(x),\;\mathbb{P}\text{-almost surely for }x\in\mathbb{N},

that
\mu_{N}(x)=\xi_{x}/\xi([N])
,
x\in[N]
, is the invariant distribution of
X^{N}
. According to Theorem
3.9
(c) the semigroups
P^{(N)}
converge to
P
and we clearly have
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu_{N}=\mu
, hence
[
5
, Theorem 9.10]
or a direct calculation yield that
\mu
is invariant for
X
.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
have finite support. Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle P_{t}^{(N)}f,g\rangle_{\mu_{N}}=\langle P_{t}f,g\rangle_{\mu},

and

\langle P_{t}f,g\rangle_{\mu}=\langle f,P_{t}g\rangle_{\mu}.

Proof.

The convergence result is a straightforward consequence of the convergence of
\mu_{N}
to
\mu
and of
P^{(N)}
to
P
. Self-adjointess of
P
w.r.t.
\mu
is inherited from
P^{(N)}
, since
X^{(N)}
is reversible.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
p_{t}^{(N)}(x,y)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}^{(N)}=y)
. Then, for
x\neq y
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\,\textup{d}}{\,\textup{d}t}p_{t}^{(N)}(x,y)|_{t=0}=0,

whereas

\lim_{N\to\infty}p_{t}^{(N)}(x,y)>0,\quad\text{for all }t>0.

Proof.

The first statement is simply a reformulation of the fact that the transition rates
r^{(N)}(x,y)
vanish as
N\to\infty
. To prove the second statement we rewrite

p_{t}(x,y)=\delta_{x}P_{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\}}=\mathbb{E}(Q_{t-\tau_{x}}(y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{x}<t\}}).

The claim follows once we show
\supp Q_{s}=\mathbb{N}
for any
s>0
. Note that for every
x\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
there exists some
K>x
such that
Q_{t}([K])>0
, hence there is a positive probability that
X
has visited
[K]
before time
t
. Upon entering
[K]
at time
T_{0}<t
, the position
X_{T_{0}}\in[K]
is uniformly distributed by Theorem
3.9
(d), and thus there is a positive probability that
X_{s}=x
for all
s\in[T_{0},t]
, hence
Q_{t}(x)>0
.
∎

Lemma 3.14
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle\left\|\,\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\mathbb{P}_{y}(X_{t}\in\cdot)\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}}\leq e^{-\xi_{1}^{-1}t},

(3.30)

and in particular

\displaystyle\left\|\,\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)-\mu\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}}\leq e^{-\xi_{1}^{-1}t}.

(3.31)

Proof.

We construct a Markovian coupling of two versions of the limit random walk
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
. If
X_{t}<Y_{t}
, then the exit rate
\xi_{Y_{t}}^{-1}
from the current state of
Y_{t}
is larger than the corresponding rate for
X_{t}
. Hence, we can couple the two exponentials governing the exit so that when
X_{t}
jumps,
Y_{t}
jumps as well. In the case of a joint exit, we can couple the random walks to move identically, as both movements are prescribed by
(Q_{t})_{t>0}
. The additional jumps of
Y_{t}
may cause
Y_{t}
to jump to the left of
X_{t}
, in which case the roles of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
reverse so that it is always the random walk with the lower index, which determines the coupling speed. Since
\xi_{1}
is the largest weight, the exit time for a random walk started in the slowest site dominates this coupling argument, which proves that the coupling time in this coupling is at most an exponential with parameter
\xi_{1}^{-1}
.∎

Note that the coupling used in the proof of Lemma
3.14
is not optimal, as it ignores the possibility that
Y_{t}
jumps on top of
X_{t}
for an earlier coupling. We proceed by stating two direct implications of the lemma.

Corollary 3.15
.

We have
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely that

a)

\left\|\,Q_{t}-\mu\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}}\leq e^{-\xi_{1}^{-1}t};

b)

(P_{t})
has a spectral gap of at least
\xi_{1}^{-1}
.

To construct a system of infinitely many coalescing versions of
X
in Section 4, it is technically convenient to extend the trajectory space.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\bar{\mathbb{N}}
denote the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{N}
with respect to the discrete topology. Then
X
can be extended to a Feller process on
\bar{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

We do not distinguish notationally between
X
and its extension. We use the probabilistic characterisation of the Feller property given in
[
9
, Lemma 17.3]
. This amounts to establishing the two conditions

\mathbb{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in\cdot)\to\mathbb{P}_{y}(X_{t}\in\cdot)\text{ for any }t\geq 0\text{ as }x\to y\text{ in }\bar{\mathbb{N}},

(3.32)

and

X_{t}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}X_{0}\text{ as }t\to 0.

(3.33)

If
y\in\mathbb{N}
, then (
3.32
) holds trivially. If
y=\infty
, then as
x\to y
the time of the first jump converges to
0
in probability and thus
P_{t}\delta_{x}
converges to
Q_{t}
as required for (
3.32
). Similarly, item (
3.33
) is trivial if the process is initiated in
\mathbb{N}
. If the process is initiated via the entrance law, i.e. it starts at
\infty
, then
X_{t}
must leave any finite subset of
\mathbb{N}
as

. But this is equivalent to
X_{t}\to\infty
in
\bar{\mathbb{N}}
, which yields (
3.33
).
∎

Multiple walks.

We now consider systems of finitely many independent limit random walks. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{1}\otimes\nu_{2}}
denote the distribution of two independent copies
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of the limiting walk
X
, started in distributions
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
, respectively. If the starting positions are fixed
\nu_{1}=\delta_{x},\nu_{2}=\delta_{y}
, then we write
\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}
instead. We say that
X
and
Y

meet
at time
t
, if
X_{t}=Y_{t}
.

Corollary 3.17
.

The probability that two independent random walks with fixed starting points meet at time
t
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(X_{t}=Y_{t})\geq\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}(1-\alpha)^{2}\sum_{z\leq h_{\alpha}(t)}\mu(z)^{2},

(3.34)

where
h_{\alpha}(t)=\sup\{x\in\mathbb{N}:\mu(x)\geq\alpha^{-1}e^{\xi_{1}^{-1}t}\}
.

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(X_{t}=Y_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}p_{t}(x,z)p_{t}(y,z)

(3.35)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\leq h_{\alpha}(t)}\big(\mu(z)+(p_{t}(x,z)-\mu(z))\big)\big(\mu(z)+(p_{t}(y,z)-\mu(z))\big)

(3.36)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{z\leq h_{\alpha}(t)}(\mu(z)-\left\|\,p_{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}})(\mu(z)-\left\|\,p_{t}(y,\cdot)-\mu\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}})

(3.37)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{z\leq h_{\alpha}(t)}(\mu(z)-e^{-\xi_{1}^{-1}t})(\mu(z)-e^{-\xi_{1}^{-1}t}),

(3.38)

where we used Corollary
3.15
in the last inequality and the fact that
\mu(z)>\left\|\,p_{t}(x,\cdot)-\mu\,\right\|_{\textup{TV}}>\sup_{w\in\mathbb{N}}|p_{t}(x,w)-\mu(w)|
, for
z\in h_{\alpha}(t)
in the second inequality.
Note, that by definition of
h_{\alpha}(t)
, the last expression is at least
(1-\alpha)^{2}\sum_{z\leq h_{\alpha}(t)}\mu(z)^{2}
. The claim now follows by taking the supremum in
\alpha
.
∎

It follows that for
t
of order
\xi_{1}^{-1}
there is a realistic chance that two independent random walks have met. This time-scale is also the best possible if we make no assumptions on the starting points, since on time scales much smaller than
\xi_{1}^{-1}
, two random walks starting in
1
and
2
have not jumped with high probability, hence they cannot have met. We next show that if we start both walks in the stationary distribution, they typically do not meet in high index sites.

Lemma 3.18
.

There is some constant
C<\infty
(independent of
\xi
) such that for any
A\subset\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
,
\mathbf{P}
-almost surely

\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}(X\text{ and }Y\text{ meet inside }A\text{ before time }T)\leq C\Big(T\,\frac{\mu(A)}{\xi(\mathbb{N})}+\sum_{z\in A}\mu(z)^{2}\Big).

Proof.

Let

\tau_{z}=\inf\{t:X_{t}=Y_{t}=z\},z\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
X
and
Y
individually leave
z
at rate
\xi_{z}^{-1}
, the joint leaving rate is
2\xi_{z}^{-1}
. Let
\sigma_{z}
be the waiting time for the next jump of either walker after
\tau_{z}
. Note that
\sigma_{z}
is independent of
\tau_{z}
by the Markov property. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T,\sigma_{z}\geq\epsilon\xi_{z}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T,\sigma_{z}<\epsilon\xi_{z}\right)

(3.39)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\int_{0}^{T+\epsilon\xi_{z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{t}=z\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{t}=z\}}\,\textup{d}t\geq\epsilon\xi_{z}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T\right)\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{z}<\epsilon\xi_{z}\right).

(3.40)

Note that the increase in the time interval from
[0,T]
to
[0,T+\epsilon\xi_{z}]
accommodates for the event that
\tau_{z}\in(T-\epsilon\xi_{z},T]
. Since
\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{z}<\epsilon\xi_{z}\right)=1-e^{-2\epsilon}
, we obtain by regrouping terms that

\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T\right)\leq e^{2\epsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\int_{0}^{T+\epsilon\xi_{z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{t}=z\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{t}=z\}}\,\textup{d}t\geq\epsilon\xi_{z}\right).

By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\int_{0}^{T+\epsilon\xi_{z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{t}=z\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{t}=z\}}\,\textup{d}t\geq\epsilon\xi_{z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\int_{0}^{T+\epsilon\xi_{z}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{t}=z\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y_{t}=z\}}\,\textup{d}t}{\epsilon\xi_{z}}

(3.41)

\displaystyle=\frac{(T+\epsilon\xi_{z})\mu(z)^{2}}{\epsilon\xi_{z}}=\Big(\xi_{z}+\frac{T}{\epsilon}\Big)\frac{\xi_{z}}{\xi(\mathbb{N})^{2}}.

(3.42)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}\left(\tau_{z}\leq T\right)\leq e^{2\epsilon}\Big(\xi_{z}+\frac{T}{\epsilon}\Big)\frac{\xi_{z}}{\xi(\mathbb{N})^{2}}

(3.43)

Choosing
\epsilon
of order one and applying a union bound over all
z\in A
gives the desired result.
∎

For arbitrary starting positions, we obtain the following corollary by using the simple fact that
\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(E)\leq\frac{1}{\mu(x)\mu(y)}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu\otimes\mu}}(E)
for any event
E
.

Corollary 3.19
.

Let
x<y
and
A\subset\mathbb{N}
, then

\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}(X\text{ and }Y\text{ meet in }A\text{ before time }T)\leq\frac{C}{\xi_{x}\xi_{y}}\big(T\,\xi(A)+{\sum_{z\in A}\xi_{z}^{2}}\big).

From ranked environments to the limit coalescing walk

The limiting walk
X
of Section 3 was constructed from the environment
\xi
. We now isolate the abstract convergence statement that connects the finite models to this limit. Given positive weights
w_{1}^{(N)}\geq\dots\geq w_{N}^{(N)}
and a scale
a_{N}
, define the rescaled ranked environment

\widehat{w}^{(N)}=\big(a_{N}^{-1}w_{1}^{(N)},\dots,a_{N}^{-1}w_{N}^{(N)},0,0,\dots\big)\in\ell_{1}.

By the
\ell_{1}
-criterion established at the beginning of the previous section, convergence
\widehat{w}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\to}\xi
in
\ell_{1}
is equivalent to pointwise convergence of the ranked weights together with convergence of the total mass. In the heavy-tailed setting introduced in Section 1, this is precisely the extreme-value input (
1.3
). The results of the present section use only this ranked-environment convergence and no further properties of the microscopic law.

Single-lineage convergence

Proposition 4.1

(Abstract ranked-environment convergence)
.

Let
w^{(N)}_{1}\geq w^{(N)}_{2}\geq\dots\geq w^{(N)}_{N}
be positive weights and let
\widehat{w}^{(N)}
be as above. Assume that

\widehat{w}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\xi\qquad\text{in }\ell_{1},

where
\xi
is the limiting environment used to construct
X
in Section 3. Let
Y^{(N)}
denote the random walk in the environment
(w^{(N)}_{i})_{i\in[N]}
started in position
y_{0}\leq M
with uniform jump distribution. Then

Y^{(N)}_{a_{N}(\cdot)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}X_{(\cdot)},\quad\mbox{as }N\to\infty.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\big(Y^{(N)}_{a_{N}t}\big)_{t\in[0,T]}
can be perfectly coupled to
X^{(N)}
(which coincides with the trace of
X
on
[N]
by Theorem
3.9
(d)) up to some error probability that vanishes as
N\to\infty
. Note that given the initial positions
Y_{0}^{(N)},\;X_{0}
the processes can be reconstructed from the environment of exponential clocks. Hence, it suffices to show that the Poisson field of clock processes corresponding to
w^{(N)}
at time scale
a_{N}
converges weakly to the Poisson field of clock processes parametrised by
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{N}
. The assumed convergence
\widehat{w}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\to}\xi
in
\ell_{1}
implies pointwise convergence of the clock intensities together with convergence of their total mass, and therefore convergence of the corresponding characteristic functionals. The claim now follows from the fact that a sequence of Poisson measures converges weakly to a limiting Poisson measure if and only if the corresponding characteristic functionals converge, see e.g.
[
4
, Proposition 11.1.VIII]
.
∎

In particular, once the ranked environments converge, the motion of a single lineage is universal on the time scale
a_{N}
. For the complete-graph models of interest here, the domain-of-attraction statement from the introduction is obtained by combining Proposition
4.1
with (
1.3
).

Finite coalescing systems

To pass from the motion of one lineage to the voter model via duality, we next extend the convergence to finitely many coalescing lineages. The only new issue is to control coalescences that take place far out among the fast sites. Corollary
3.19
provides precisely the required estimate.
Let us now formally define the coalescence operator
\operatorname{coal}(\cdot)
for an arbitrary collection
(X^{1},X^{2},\cdots)
of copies of
X
with distinct initial conditions. We assume
X^{1}_{0}=1,X^{2}_{0}=2,\dots
. This induces a natural order on the paths:
X^{i}<X^{j}
if and only if
X^{i}_{0}<X^{j}_{0}
. Note that this convention imposes no additional restriction since an arbitrary collection of paths can always be ordered according to starting positions and viewed as a projection of the full configuration. For any
i
denote by

\sigma_{i}=\inf\{t:X_{t}^{i}=X_{t}^{j}\text{ for any }j<i\}

the
coalescence time
of the
i
-th walker. Note that each
\sigma_{i}
is measurable with respect to the
\sigma
-field
\sigma(X^{j},j\leq i)
which is generated by only finitely many walkers and that
\sigma_{i}>0
almost surely for each
i
. In particular the coalescence time of any walker is well-defined and there are only countably many coalescence times. Define now

\operatorname{coal}\big((X^{1},X^{2},\dots)\big)_{t}=\Big(\sum_{j:\sigma_{j}>t}\delta_{X^{j}_{t}}(x)\Big)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}},\quad t\geq 0.

(4.1)

The existence of this infinite system of coalescing walks is deduced from standard results in the next proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

For each finite
M\subset\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathbb{Q}_{M}
denote the distribution of

\operatorname{coal}\big((X^{j},j\in M)\big).

Then

\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{L}\big(\operatorname{coal}\big((X^{1},X^{2},\dots)\big)\big)

is the projective limit of
\{\mathbb{Q}_{M},M\subset\mathbb{N}\text{ finite}\}.

Proof.

It is clear that if the projective limit exists, it must coincide with
\mathbb{Q}
and this would also establish the well-definedness of (
4.1
). To this end, we invoke a version of the Kolmogorov extension theorem, namely
[
9
, Theorem 8.23]
. Recall that by Lemma
3.16
we may view the particle trajectories as paths in
\bar{\mathbb{N}}
, which is Borel isomorphic to
\{1/n,n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{\infty\}
and thus a Borel space. Hence, we only need to show the consistency of the family
\{\mathbb{Q}_{M},M\subset\mathbb{N}\text{ finite}\}
. The latter is an immediate consequence of the definition of the coalescence times
\sigma_{j},j\in M
, if for each particle, one keeps track of the indices of the particles that have already merged with it
1
1

1

Formally, this requires to extend the probability spaces involved. However, we only use the additional structure in this proof and we found that the corresponding notation only obscures the argument
: Arguing inductively, it suffices to show that
\mathbb{Q}_{M^{\prime}}
coincides with the projection of
\mathbb{Q}_{M}
onto the particles with indices in
M^{\prime}
for
|M^{\prime}|=|M|-1
. Denote the index of the additional (
spare
) particle in
M
by
s
. If no other particle coalesces with
s
before
\sigma_{s}
, then there is nothing to show. Assume this is not the case, then there exist coalescence times
\sigma_{j_{1}}<\sigma_{j_{2}}<\dots<\sigma_{j_{k}}<\sigma_{s}
at which particles with indices in
M^{\prime}
have merged with the spare particle. Since these
virtual
coalescences do not occur in the smaller system, we need to define the trajectories of the non-spare particles involved after the virtual coalescence events. By coupling the
M^{\prime}
to the
M
system, we may declare the trajectory of the particle
j_{1}
involved in the first virtual coalescence to be that of the spare particle until the next coalescence event involving
j_{1}
. By construction, the latter will occur at time
\sigma_{j_{2}}
. Depending on whether
j_{1}<j_{2}
or
j_{1}>j_{2}
, one of the particles survives and we set it once again to follow the trajectory of the spare particle. We continue this procedure and thus inductively define a path for the
M^{\prime}
-coalescing random walk. It follows from the Markov property of the underlying walks that this path has indeed the distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{M^{\prime}}
.
∎

Note that the law
\mathbb{Q}
just constructed formally corresponds to a coalescing system with trajectories in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\times\mathbb{N}
, since by convention we do not coalesce particles that meet at
\infty
. That this is justified follows from Corollary
3.19
: the probability that particles meet ‘near’ the boundary point
\infty
vanishes in the finite system, which is also crucially used in the proof of Proposition
4.3
below. However, we may also consider the process
\gamma
derived from propagating an initial condition
\gamma_{0}
according to (projections of)
\mathbb{Q}
as a process on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, just as in Sections 1 and 2.

Proposition 4.3

(Finite-lineage convergence)
.

Let
\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\to}\gamma_{0}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
as
N\to\infty
, where
\supp\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\subset[K]
for some
K
, uniformly in
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the corresponding coalescing random walk systems

\big(\gamma_{a_{N}t}^{(N)}\big)_{t\geq 0}

admit a distributional limit
\gamma=(\gamma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
as
N\to\infty
, in which individual particle dynamics are given by i.i.d. copies of
X
and particles coalesce whenever they share the same location.

Proof.

We define the following approximations to the coalescence operator:

•

\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\cdot)
denotes the coalescence operator allowing coalescences only at sites in
[M]
;

•

\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon}(\cdot)
denotes the coalescence operator allowing coalescences only at sites in
[M]
and at times in
\epsilon\mathbb{N}
.

Set

\mathbf{Y}^{(N)}=(Y^{(1,N)},\dots,Y^{(k,N)}),\qquad\mathbf{X}=(X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(k)}).

By Proposition
4.1
, we have that for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
,
\epsilon>0
,
\vec{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{d})\in[0,T]^{d}
and
\vec{x}\in\bigtimes_{j=1}^{d}\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon a_{N}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}=\vec{x}\right)

(4.2)

\displaystyle\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{P}\Big(

\displaystyle\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon}(\mathbf{X})_{\vec{t}}=\vec{x}\Big).

We next remove the temporal discretisation and the spatial truncation on both sides of (
4.2
).

First consider the finite system. Under
\operatorname{coal}_{M}
there are at most
k-1
coalescence events. If one of them is not detected by
\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon a_{N}}
, then after the corresponding coalescence event at some site in
[M]
the merged particle must jump within the following
\epsilon a_{N}
time units. Hence

\mathbb{P}\Big(\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon a_{N}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\Big)\leq(k-1)\Big(1-\textup{e}^{-\epsilon a_{N}/w_{M}^{(N)}}\Big).

(4.3)

By (
1.3
), we have
a_{N}/w_{M}^{(N)}\to\xi_{M}^{-1}
for each fixed
M
, so that for fixed
M

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon a_{N}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\Big)\leq(k-1)\Big(1-\textup{e}^{-\epsilon/\xi_{M}}\Big),

(4.4)

which vanishes as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.

Next, the error caused by suppressing coalescences outside
[M]
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\binom{k}{2}\sup_{x,y\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}\Big(Y^{1,N}\text{ and }Y^{2,N}\text{ meet before time }Ta_{N}\text{ in }[N]\setminus[M]\Big).

(4.5)

By the same argument that led to Corollary
3.19
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}\Big(Y^{1,N}\text{ and }Y^{2,N}\text{ meet before time }Ta_{N}\text{ in }[N]\setminus[M]\Big)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{Ta_{N}\sum_{z=M+1}^{N}w^{(N)}_{z}}{w^{(N)}_{x}w^{(N)}_{y}}\right),

uniformly in
x,y\in[K]
. Moreover, for each fixed
x\in[K]
we have

a_{N}^{-1}w_{x}^{(N)}\to\xi_{x}
, and the tail masses satisfy

a_{N}^{-1}\sum_{z=M+1}^{N}w^{(N)}_{z}\to\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[M])
.
It follows that for every fixed
M

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}(\mathbf{Y}^{(N)})_{a_{N}\vec{t}}\big)\leq C(T,K,\xi)\,\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[M]),

(4.6)

and the right hand side vanishes as
M\to\infty
.

The same two estimates hold for the limiting system. Indeed, since after a coalescence event in
[M]
the merged limit particle leaves its current site at rate at most
\xi_{M}^{-1}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big(\operatorname{coal}_{M,\epsilon}(\mathbf{X})_{\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{X})_{\vec{t}}\Big)\leq(k-1)\Big(1-\textup{e}^{-\epsilon/\xi_{M}}\Big),

(4.7)

which tends to
0
as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
. Likewise,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\operatorname{coal}_{M}(\mathbf{X})_{\vec{t}}\neq\operatorname{coal}(\mathbf{X})_{\vec{t}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\binom{k}{2}\sup_{x,y\in[K]}\mathbb{P}_{(x,y)}\Big(X^{1}\text{ and }X^{2}\text{ meet before time }T\text{ in }\mathbb{N}\setminus[M]\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(T,K,\xi)\Big(\xi(\mathbb{N}\setminus[M])+\sum_{z>M}\xi_{z}^{2}\Big),

(4.8)

by Corollary
3.19
, and the right hand side vanishes as
M\to\infty
.

Combining (
4.2
), (
4.4
), (
4.6
), (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we may first let
N\to\infty
, then
\epsilon\downarrow 0
and finally
M\to\infty
. This shows that the finite-dimensional distributions of
\big(\gamma_{a_{N}t}^{(N)}\big)_{t\geq 0}
converge to those of the coalescing system generated by i.i.d. copies of
X
.
∎

5
Coalescence time and coming down from infinity

In this section, we establish that
\gamma
‘comes down from infinity’.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\gamma_{0}\equiv 1
. For almost every environment
\xi
, we have that

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{t}(x)<\infty\quad\text{ for any }t>0.

Furthermore,
\gamma
can be extended to a Feller process on
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\times 2^{\mathbb{N}}
that enters
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
continuously.

Theorem
5.1
is proved at the end of this section. The main ingredient for the proof is the following auxiliary statement in which we control the expected coalescence time
\mathbb{E}\tau_{M+1\to M}
. Here,
\tau_{k\rightarrow l}
denotes the first time at which the process initiated with
k
particles enters a state with
l
particles for
l<k
. In fact, it suffices to consider the process
\gamma
restricted to sites in
[N].

Proposition 5.2
.

There exists a constant
C(\xi)
so that, for any
M
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}\big|\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\big]\leq C(\xi)M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}},

(5.1)

uniformly in
N
and
\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\in\{0,1\}^{[N]}
.

The proof of Proposition
5.2

The proof is a direct consequence of Lemmas
5.3
–
5.5
in this paragraph. Since a coalescence can only happen when a particle jumps we need to control the total number of jumps
J^{(N)}_{t}
in a system of
M+1
coalescing random walks on
[N]
, which we do by means of a lower bound on
J_{t}^{(N)}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume there is a lower bound
J^{\prime}_{t}\leq J^{(N)}_{t}
valid until
\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}
, i.e.
J^{\prime}_{t}\leq J^{(N)}_{t}+\infty\mathbbm{1}_{t>\tau{(N)}_{M+1\to M}}
uniformly in all starting configurations
\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\in\{0,1\}^{[N]}
. Assume further that there are constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(\xi)
,
c_{2}=c_{2}(\xi)
satisfying

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{P}\Big(J^{\prime}_{t}<c_{1}NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}t\Big)\,\textup{d}t\leq c_{2}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.2)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}\big|\gamma_{0}^{(N)}\big]\leq(c_{1}^{-1}+c_{2})M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.3)

Proof.

Each jump in the system has probability
\frac{M}{N}
to land on top of another particle, leading to a coalescence. Since these jumps are independent, the number of jumps needed is a geometric random variable
Z
with success parameter
\frac{M}{N}
, and
\{\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}>t\}=\{Z>J^{(N)}_{t}\}
. Note that
Z
and
J^{(N)}_{t}
are not independent. By assumption there is a coupling
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{x}}
with
J_{t}^{\prime}\leq J_{t}^{(N)}
on the event
\{\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}>t\}
, where we set
\mathbf{x}=\gamma_{0}^{(N)}
for conciseness. Then, writing
\tau=\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}
, for any
a\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau>t\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau>t,Z\geq a\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau>t,Z<a\right)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(Z\geq a\right)+\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau>t,J^{(N)}_{t}<a\right)

(5.5)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(Z\geq a\right)+\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau>t,J^{\prime}_{t}<a\right)

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(Z\geq a\right)+\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(J^{\prime}_{t}<a\right).

(5.7)

With
a=\lfloor c_{1}NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}t\rfloor
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(Z\geq a\right)=\left(1-\frac{M}{N}\right)^{a-1}\leq e^{-c_{1}M^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}t}

(5.8)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(Z\geq a\right)\;dt\leq c_{1}^{-1}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.9)

By assumption,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(J^{\prime}_{t}<a\right)\;dt\leq c_{2}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}

(5.10)

and since
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}(\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}=t)=0

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}>t\right)\;dt\leq(c_{1}^{-1}+c_{2})M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.\qed

(5.11)

We now need to construct a good lower bound
J_{t}^{\prime}
on
J_{t}^{(N)}
. Let
X_{t}^{\prime}
be a random walk on
\{M+1,...,N\}
started in
M+1
. The mean waiting times in site
x
are given by
\xi_{x}
(so identical to
X_{t}^{(N)}
), but it then jumps to
y
with probability
\frac{1}{N}
for
y>M+1
and
\frac{M+1}{N}
for
y=M+1
. Let
J_{t}^{\prime}
denote the number of jumps this random walk performs until time
t
.

Lemma 5.4
.

The process
J^{\prime}_{t}
is a lower bound on
J^{(N)}_{t}
until
\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}
, that is,
J^{\prime}_{t}\prec J^{(N)}_{t}+\infty\mathbbm{1}_{t>\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}}
uniformly in all starting configurations
\gamma_{0}^{(N)}
, where ‘
\prec
’ indicates stochastic domination.

Proof.

Consider the system of coalescing random walks where we mark one particle, with
X_{t}^{\prime\prime}
the position of the marked particle. Then, clearly, the number of jumps performed by
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}
is a lower bound on the total number of jumps (we do not care what happens with the marked particle during a coalescence event, since the lower bound needs only to be valid until the first coalescence). The mechanism for marking is as follows: Initially, the particle with the largest position is marked, which is automatically at a position larger than
M
. Then, when the marked particle jumps to a site in
[M]
, we change the mark to the particle on the largest position, which is larger than
M
(again, assuming coalescence hasn’t happened yet). Note that by construction, until the first coalescence,
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}\geq M+1
.

We now couple
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}
with
X^{\prime}_{t}
. If
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}=X^{\prime}_{t}
, both particles move identically until
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}
jump into
[M]
. On this event, the marked particle changes to the largest particle, so
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}\geq M+1
, with the position random depending on the position of the other particles. On the other hand,
X^{\prime}_{t}
jumps to
M+1
. If
X^{\prime}_{t}=M+1
and
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}>M+1
the jump rate of
X_{t}^{\prime\prime}
is
\xi_{X^{\prime\prime}_{t}}^{-1}
and hence dominates the jump rate
\xi_{M+1}^{-1}
of
X_{t}^{\prime}
. Therefore we can couple the jump times so that if
X_{t}^{\prime}
jumps,
X^{\prime\prime}_{t}
jumps as well and both jump to the same target (unless the target is in
[M]
, in which case
X_{t}^{\prime}
jumps to
M+1
(and effectively does not move, but we do count this jump), and
X_{t}^{\prime\prime}
jumps to some site
\geq M+1
.

From this construction we obtain that whenever
X_{t}^{\prime}
jumps, so does
X_{t}^{\prime\prime}
, and hence
J_{t}^{\prime}\leq J_{t}^{(N)}
until the first coalescence time.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

J^{\prime}_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(J^{\prime}_{t}<\frac{NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{4(1+C(\xi))}t\Big)\;dt\leq\frac{4}{C(\xi)}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.12)

Proof.

Observe by Chebyshevs inequality

\mathbb{P}(J^{\prime}_{t}<a)=\mathbb{P}(\sum_{i=1}^{a}\sigma_{i}>t)\leq e^{-\beta t}\mathbb{E}e^{\beta\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}},

(5.13)

where
\sigma_{i}
is the waiting time for the
i
-th jump. These are
\operatorname{Exp}(\xi_{X^{\prime}_{i}}^{-1})
-distributed and independent given
X^{\prime}_{i}
,
i=1,2,...
, where
X^{\prime}_{i}
is the position of
X^{\prime}_{t}
before the
i
-th jump. We have
X^{\prime}_{1}=M+1
and the remaining positions are i.i.d. with
\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{i}=x)=\frac{1}{N}
,
x>M+1
and
\mathbb{P}(X^{\prime}_{i}=M+1)=\frac{M+1}{N}
.
Therefore, for
\beta\leq\frac{1}{2}\xi_{M+1}^{-1}
(and hence
\beta\xi_{x}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
x\geq M+1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\beta\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\mathbb{E}e^{\beta\sigma_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\mathbb{E}\frac{\xi_{X^{\prime}_{i}}^{-1}}{\xi_{X^{\prime}_{i}}^{-1}-\beta}=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{1-\beta\xi_{X^{\prime}_{i}}}

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1-\beta\xi_{x\lor(M+1)}}

(5.15)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{a}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}(1+2\beta\xi_{x\lor(M+1)})=\prod_{i=1}^{a}\big(1+\frac{2\beta}{N}\sum_{x=1}^{N}\xi_{x\lor(M+1)}\big)

(5.16)

By (
3
) and (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\sum_{x=1}^{N}\xi_{x\lor(M+1)}\leq M^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}+C(\xi)M^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}=(1+C(\xi))M^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.17)

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{\beta\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{a}(1+2\beta(1+C(\xi))M^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}N^{-1})\leq e^{2\beta(1+C(\xi))aM^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}N^{-1}}.

(5.18)

Assuming
t\geq 2(1+C(\xi))aM^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}N^{-1}
, we pick the maximal possible value for
\beta
, namely
\beta=\frac{1}{2}\xi_{M+1}^{-1}
. Plugging (
5.18
) into (
5.13
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(J^{\prime}_{t}<a)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{2}\xi_{M+1}^{-1}(t-2(1+C(\xi))aM^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}N^{-1})}.

(5.19)

Choosing
a=\frac{NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{4(1+C(\xi))}t
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(J^{\prime}_{t}<\frac{NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{4(1+C(\xi))}t\Big)\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}\xi_{M+1}^{-1}t}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}C(\xi)M^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}t}.

(5.20)

for all
t\geq 0
and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(J^{\prime}_{t}<\frac{NM^{\frac{1}{\alpha}-1}}{4(1+C(\xi))}t\Big)\;\,\textup{d}t\leq\frac{4}{C(\xi)}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}.

(5.21)

∎

The proof of Theorem
5.1

We conclude the section with the final derivation of the ‘coming down from infinity’-property.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

By Proposition
5.2
and Fatou’s lemma,

\mathbb{E}\tau_{\infty\to 1}=\sum_{M=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\tau_{M+1\to M}\leq\sum_{M=2}^{\infty}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\tau^{(N)}_{M+1\to M}\leq C(\xi)\sum_{M=2}^{\infty}M^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}<\infty.

(5.22)

In particular,
\tau_{\infty\to 1}
is a.s. finite. Let
\mathcal{N}(t)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{N}}\gamma_{t}(x)
the process counting the number of particles at time
t
. By (
5.22
) infinity is a continuous entrance point. By Lemma 1.2. together with Remark 2.3. of
[
6
]
, the finiteness

\sup_{m}\mathbb{E}[\tau_{m\to 1}]

implies that
(\mathcal{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
can be extended to a Feller process over
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
such that under the extended law,
\mathcal{N}
starts from
\infty
-many particles and instantaneously enters a configuration with only finitely many particles. The analogous statement for the whole process
\gamma
now follows from the Feller property of the particle count process together with Proposition
4.2
and Lemma
3.16
as well as the fact that we may encode the particles that reside at
\infty
at any time as an element of
2^{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

6
Proofs of the main results

In this section, we finally prove Theorem
1.1
, Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In Proposition
4.1
, it is shown that the processes
Y^{(N)}
under temporal rescaling by
a_{N}
converge in distribution to a limit random walk
X
on
\mathbb{N}
which is governed by rates
\xi
as in (
1.3
). Proposition
4.3
extends this to systems
(\gamma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of finitely many coalescing random walks. Hence, for any
t\geq 0
and any
A\subset\mathbb{N}
finite we may define the corresponding probabilities for the limiting voter model through

\mathbb{P}\big(\eta_{t}(x)\equiv 1\text{ on }A\big|\eta_{0}\big):=\mathbb{P}\big(\eta_{0}(x)\equiv 1\text{ on }\supp\gamma_{t}\big|\supp\gamma_{0}=A\big),

(6.1)

where
\supp\gamma_{t}=\{x:\gamma_{t}(x)=1\},t\geq 0.
The extension of the definition to arbitrary
A\subset\mathbb{N}
can now be achieved by observing that the dual limiting system is well defined for an arbitrary initial configuration of particles dispersed over
\mathbb{N}
by Proposition
4.2
.
∎

Proofs of Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.

We only derive Theorem
1.3
explicitly; the statements for the finite system can be obtained in the same fashion but without relying on properties of the limiting random walk
X
. By duality, the expectation of the consensus time
\tau
is clearly bounded above by the expectation of the time
\tau_{\infty\rightarrow 1}
that is needed in the dual system to coalesce the initial configuration with a particle at every site into one single particle. By Theorem
5.1
, this time has a finite mean, which proves the first part of Theorem
1.3
. To prove formula (
1.4
), note that at any fixed time
t_{0}>0
, we may pick a site
x
with probability proportional to
\xi_{x}
and obtain the type
\eta_{t_{0}}(x)=\eta_{0}(y)
by tracing the path of the corresponding particle in the dual for time
t_{0}
to some target site
y
. The distribution of this path is independent of whether consensus has occurred and agrees with the distribution of a path of the limiting walk
X
initiated from its invariant distribution by Lemma
3.11
. In particular,
y
has the invariant distribution
(\xi_{x}/\sum_{z}\xi_{z})_{x\in\mathbb{N}}
of
X
if
t_{0}\geq\tau
, which leads immediately to (
1.4
).
∎
