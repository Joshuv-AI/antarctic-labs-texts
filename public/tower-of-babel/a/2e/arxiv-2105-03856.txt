3
The Symbolic Poisson Resultant

Our proof of the
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
-symmetry of
D^{+}
involves two technical
tools: differentiation in a quotient ring and
the symbolic version of the Poisson resultant:

(I)

Let
R
be a ring and
c
an indeterminate.
We treat the polynomial
R[c]

as a differential ring with a differential
operator
\partial_{c}:R[c]\to R[c]
where
\partial_{c}(c)=1

and
\partial_{c}(a)=0
(
a\in R
). We will need to extend

\partial_{c}
to the quotient ring
R[c]/I
where

I\subseteq R[c]
is an ideal.
Let the equivalence class of
a\in R[c]
modulo
I

be denoted as
[a]\in R[c]/I
. Let us define
\partial_{c}([a])
to be

[\partial_{c}(a)]
. It is easy to check that
\partial_{c}([a])

is well-defined iff
I
is closed under
\partial_{c}
.

(II)

The resultant
{\tt res}(A,B)
of polynomials
A,B\in K[x]

has an algebraic-geometric dual nature in the following sense:
as an algebraic object,
{\tt res}(A,B)
is defined as an expression
in terms of the coefficients of
A
and
B
.
Hence
{\tt res}(A,B)\in K
.
As a geometric object, it is defined as an expression

\overline{{\tt res}}(A,B)
in terms of

\alpha_{i}
’s and
\beta_{j}
’s which are the roots of

A
and
B
in
\overline{K}
(the algebraic closure of
K
).
Hence
\overline{{\tt res}}(A,B)\in\overline{K}
.
We may call
\overline{{\tt res}}(A,B)
the
Poisson resultant
.
Naturally, the algebraic and geometric concepts coincide:

\overline{{\tt res}}(A,B)={\tt res}(A,B)
. In reference to this equality,
we may call

\overline{{\tt res}}(A,B)
the
Poisson formula
for
{\tt res}(A,B)
.
See
[
10
, Chap. VI.4]
for this classical approach.
In this paper, in order to give a rigorous proof
of our
D^{+}
-theorem (Theorem
3
below), we
introduce a symbolic version of the Poisson formula in which
the
\alpha_{i}
’s and
\beta_{j}
’s are just symbols.

We now state and prove the symbolic Poisson formula.
Assume
n,m\geq 1
.
Let
A=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}x^{m-i}
,
and
B=\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}x^{n-j}

where

{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m})
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}=(b_{0},\ldots,b_{n})
are

indeterminates.

We define

(eliminating

)
as the determinant of the Sylvester matrix

[
10
, Chap. III.3]
.
The non-zero entries of

contain the coefficients
of
A,B
. Therefore
{\tt res}(A,B)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}]
.

Next we introduce two sets of indeterminates,

\displaystyle G

5
Application to the Complexity of Root Clustering

As described in the introduction, the bit-complexity
of the root clustering
algorithm in Becker et al.
[
1
]

in the “benchmark case” has an additive term of the form

n\log\big(|D^{+}(p)|^{-1}\big)

where
n=\deg(p)
.
This quantity is bounded by
Corollary
4

in terms of
n,{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p)
and
a_{0}
,
the leading coefficient of
p
.
Since
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(p)
is not easily deduced from the input
p
,
our propose to bound the quantity

F_{n,m}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\max_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}^{\mu_{i}}

where
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
range over all partitions of
n

with exactly
m
parts.
We consider a closely related maximization problem

\displaystyle\Phi_{n,m}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega_{n,m}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})

(34)

where

\displaystyle{\varphi}(x)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}x\log x,

\displaystyle\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\Phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\varphi}(x_{i}),

(35)

\displaystyle\Omega_{n,m}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathrel{\,:=\,}}\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}:n-m+1\geq x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x_{m}\geq 1\right\}.

(36)

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\Omega_{n,m}
denote any maximum point of
\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})
,
i.e.,
\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})=\Phi_{n,m}
.
Since

\Phi_{n,m}
is a maximization over the real set
\Omega_{n,m}

while
F_{n,m}
is a maximization over integer points

(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})
of
\Omega_{n,m}
, we have the inequality

\log(F_{n,m})\leq\Phi_{n,m}.

(37)

Here and following, assume
\log
is the
natural logarithm
\log=\ln
.
In fact, we do have equality:

Theorem 4
.

The value
\Phi_{n,m}
is attained at the
unique point
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}=(n-m+1,1,\ldots,1)\in\Omega_{n,m}
:

\Phi_{n,m}=\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})=(n-m+1)\log(n-m+1).

Hence
\log(F_{n,m})=\Phi_{n,m}
.

Proof.
We first prove that the function
\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})

achieves its maximum on the boundary of
\Omega_{n,m}
.
Let
\partial_{i}\Phi=1+\log x_{i}
be
the partial derivative with respect to
x_{i}

(
i=1,\ldots,n
). The critical point
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
of

\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})
is where
\partial_{i}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
.
So
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=(e^{-1},\ldots,e^{-1})
where
e^{-1}=0.3679
.
The second partial derivatives
are given by
\partial_{ij}\Phi=\delta_{ij}\frac{1}{x_{i}}

where
\delta_{ij}
is Kronecker’s delta.
So the Hessian matrix is
H({\boldsymbol{x}})=(\partial_{ij}\Phi)_{i,j=1}^{n}

is the diagonal matrix
\diag(x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,x_{n}^{-1})
.
Clearly
H({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
is a positive definite matrix.
This proves that

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
is a minimum point. Since there are no other critical
points, we conclude that
\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})
attains its maximum value
in
\Omega_{n,m}
at some boundary point.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}=(x^{*}_{1},\ldots,x^{*}_{m})\in\Omega_{n,m}
be any maximum point.
If
x^{*}_{m}>1
then
n-m+1>x^{*}_{1}\geq x^{*}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq x^{*}_{m}>1
,
and so
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}
lies in the interior
of
\Omega_{n,m}
, contradiction. Therefore
x^{*}_{m}=1
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}=(x^{*}_{1},\ldots,x^{*}_{m-1})
,
and observe that
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{n-1,m-1}
.
Moreover,
\Phi_{n,m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\varphi}(x^{*}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}{\varphi}(x^{*}_{j})
.
Consider the injective map

\iota_{m}:\Omega_{n-1,m-1}\to\Omega_{n,m}

where
\iota_{m}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m-1},1)
.

\displaystyle\Phi_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\max_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\iota_{m}(\Omega_{n-1,m-1})}\Phi({\boldsymbol{x}})

(since
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}\in\iota_{m}(\Omega_{n-1,m-1})
)

\displaystyle=\max_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\Omega_{n-1,m-1}}\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}})

\displaystyle=\Phi_{n-1,m-1}.

By induction on
k=1,\ldots,m-1
,
we conclude that
\Phi_{n,m}=\Phi_{n-k,m-k}
.
When
k=m-1
, we see that
\Phi_{n-m+1,1}={\varphi}(n-m+1)

as claimed. The maximum point
x^{*}
in
\Omega_{n-m+1,1}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}

is unique:
x^{*}=n-m+1
. By taking the inverses
of the
\iota_{k}
’s, we conclude that the maximum point
in
\Omega_{n,m}
is
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{*}=(n-m+1,1,\ldots,1)
.

Q.E.D.

Corollary
5
.

Let
p
be an integer polynomial of degree
n
with
an
L
-bit leading coefficient
a_{0}
. Then

\log(|D^{+}(p)|^{-1})\leq 2n(\log n+L\log(2))=\widetilde{O}(nL)

Proof.

\displaystyle\log(|D^{+}(p)|^{-1})

\displaystyle\leq\log\big((n-m)!\big(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}^{\mu_{i}}\big)a_{0}^{n+m-2}\big)

(from (
33
))

\displaystyle\leq\log((n-m)!)+{\varphi}(n-m+1)+(n+m-2)\log(2^{L})

(from Theorem
4
)

\displaystyle\leq(n\log n)+(n\log n)+(2n)L\log(2).

Q.E.D.

6
Final Remarks

The Main Result of this paper proves the conjecture that the
root function
D^{+}_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
-symmetric,
by giving a determinantal formula for its gist.
This implies an exact formula for the
D-plus discriminant
D^{+}(p)
of a polynomial
p(x)

in terms of the coefficients of
p(x)
.
This leads to an exact lower bound on
|D^{+}(p)|

when
p
is an integer polynomial.
Our Main Result is established using an ideal-theoretic
proof. For this, we need to re-cast the classic Poisson
formula as the “symbolic Poisson formula”,
which has independent interest.
Finally, we apply our result to give a very sharp
bound on the term

n\log\big(|D^{+}(p)|^{-1}\big)

arising in the bit complexity of the root clustering algorithm of
Becker et al.
[
1
]
.
