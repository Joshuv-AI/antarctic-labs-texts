A Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm for Generalized DC Programming with Application to Imaging Data Thanks: Submitted to the editors XX 202X.
Hongjin He, Zhiyuan Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we consider a class of nonconvex (not necessarily differentiable) optimization problems called generalized DC (Difference-of-Convex functions) programming, which is minimizing the sum of two separable DC parts and one two-block-variable coupling function. To circumvent the nonconvexity and nonseparability of the problem under consideration, we accordingly introduce a Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm (UBAMA) by maximally exploiting the favorable DC structure of the objective. Specifically, we first follow the spirit of alternating minimization to update each block variable in a sequential order, which can efficiently tackle the nonseparablitity caused by the coupling function. Then, we employ the Fenchel-Young inequality to approximate the second DC components (i.e., concave parts) so that each subproblem reduces to a convex optimization problem, thereby alleviating the computational burden of the nonconvex DC parts. Moreover, each subproblem absorbs a Bregman proximal regularization term, which is usually beneficial for inducing closed-form solutions of subproblems for many cases via choosing appropriate Bregman kernel functions. It is remarkable that our algorithm not only provides an algorithmic framework to understand the iterative schemes of some novel existing algorithms, but also enjoys implementable schemes with easier subproblems than some state-of-the-art first-order algorithms developed for generic nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problems. Theoretically, we prove that the sequence generated by our algorithm globally converges to a critical point under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KŁ) condition. Besides, we estimate the local convergence rates of our algorithm when we further know the prior information of the KŁ exponent. A series of numerical experiments on imaging data demonstrate the reliability and efficiency of the proposed algorithmic framework.



keywords

DC programming, nonconvex optimization, Bregman distance, alternating minimization algorithm, first-order methods, Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property, image processing.

Introduction

With the rapid developments of artificial intelligence, nonconvex (differentiable/nondifferentiable) optimization problems frequently appear and play important roles in the areas of data sciences, imaging processing, computer visions, and machine learning, e.g., see some surveys
[
25
,
29
,
31
,
34
,
69
,
78
]
and the most recent monograph
[
33
]
on nonconvex topics. As the backbone of nonconvex programming and global optimization, in recent years, we have witnessed the fruitful developments of DC (Difference-of-Convex functions) programming and DCA (DC Algorithms) from theory to their applications. Here, we refer the reader to a recent survey
[
50
]
and reference therein for thirty years of developments along this direction. As stated by Le Thi and Pham Dinh in
[
50
]
: “
Despite the bright successes obtained by researchers and practitioners in the use of DC programming and DCA for modeling and solving nonconvex and global optimization problems, their works have not exploited the full power and creative freedom offered by these tools: their proposed DCAs, although more efficient than existing methods, but can be further improved to better handle large-scale problems
”, we are motivated to consider a class of structured DC programming and to design a structure-exploiting algorithm to solve the underlying problem.
Specifically, in this paper, we are interested in a class of generalized DC programming, which reads as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\;\Phi(x,y):=\underbrace{f_{1}(x)-g_{1}(x)}_{\theta_{1}(x)}+\underbrace{f_{2}(y)-g_{2}(y)}_{\theta_{2}(y)}+\underbrace{h^{+}(x,y)-h^{-}(x,y)}_{h(x,y)}\;\right\},

(1)

where
f_{i}(\cdot)
and
g_{i}(\cdot)
are convex (not necessarily smooth) functions for
i=1,2
, and
h(x,y)
is a continuously differentiable but coupling function, which is also assumed to be decomposable into two parts
h^{+}(x,y)
and
h^{-}(x,y)
similar yet slightly different to the case studied in
[
51
,
65
]
. Unlike the standard DC programming studied in the literature where the objective (
1
) possesses only one block variable (e.g., see
[
8
,
40
,
41
,
66
,
55
,
56
,
57
,
59
,
60
,
85
,
87
]
, to name just a few), our model (
1
) under consideration can be regarded as the complementary or generalized case to the typical DC programming. Here, it is worth pointing out that we only need to assume both components
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
being proper lower semicontinuous functions for convergence analysis, which makes our approach applicable to a wider range of nonconvex optimization models. Besides, we restrict the later discussion to the case of (
1
) with vector variables, all of our results are also available to the case with matrix variables (see Section
5
).

When applying the traditional DCA-type algorithms (e.g.,
[
2
,
30
,
35
,
47
,
80
]
) tailored for standard DC programming, the main computational challenge is that the appearance of coupling part
h(x,y)
makes these algorithms difficult to be implemented in many cases. Accordingly, a natural and efficient way to handle block coupling optimization problems is the so-named alternating minimization (a.k.a. block coordinate descent or Gauss-Seidel) algorithm (e.g., see
[
11
,
15
,
81
]
), which iteratively and alternately keeps one of block variables fixed and optimizes over the other. More concretely, for given initial point
(x^{0},y^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the iterative scheme of the alternating minimization algorithm reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\Phi(x,y^{k}),\\
y^{k+1}&\in\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\Phi(x^{k+1},y).\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

Unfortunately, it has been documented in
[
68
]
that (
2
) may cycle indefinitely without converging if the minimum in each step cannot be uniquely attained. Accordingly, in
[
12
,
14
,
42
,
58
,
77
]
, the authors studied some convergence behaviors of (
2
) under various assumptions, such as pseudoconvexity, quasiconvexity, convexity and continuity, on the objective
\Phi(x,y)
. In particular, some papers (e.g.,
[
6
,
43
]
) are contributed to removing the strict convexity assumption by imposing proximal regularization terms into the subproblems of (
2
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\left\{\Phi(x,y^{k})+\frac{c_{k}}{2}\left\|x-x^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\Phi(x^{k+1},y)+\frac{d_{k}}{2}\left\|y-y^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

(3)

where
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
are positive numbers. Clearly, both subproblems of (
3
) are strongly convex as long as the objective
\Phi(\cdot,\cdot)
is convex of one argument while the other is fixed, which can easily guarantee each subproblem to have a unique solution.
Furthermore, some novel works
[
4
,
5
]
are devoted to the convergence of (
3
) for handling generic nonconvex cases under the well-known Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KŁ) property (see
[
48
,
54
]
and also
[
16
]
).
However, the iterative scheme (
3
) probably suffers from difficult subproblems in many cases, for example when applying to nonnegative matrix/tensor factorization problems (see
[
1
,
19
,
83
]
), even though the first two separable functions
\theta_{1}(x)
and
\theta_{2}(y)
are void, and hence is potentially slow with a comparatively high computational complexity in practice. Therefore, to alleviate the computational cost caused by the coupling function, Bolte et al.
[
19
]
judiciously introduced a Proximal Alternating Linearized Minimization (PALM) algorithm, which considers an approximation of (
3
) via the standard proximal linearization of each subproblem, thereby maximally making its subproblems enough easy with closed-form solutions in some cases. Specifically, the PALM algorithm updates
x
and
y
alternately via

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\;\left\{\theta_{1}(x)+\left\langle\nabla_{x}h(x^{k},y^{k}),x-x^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{c_{k}}{2}\left\|x-x^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\theta_{2}(y)+\left\langle\nabla_{y}h(x^{k+1},y^{k}),y-y^{k}\right\rangle+\frac{d_{k}}{2}\left\|y-y^{k}\right\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

where
\nabla_{x}h(\cdot,y)
and
\nabla_{y}h(x,\cdot)
represent the block-partial gradients of
h(x,y)
, respectively. Moreover, some convergence results of (
4
) were established under the KŁ property and the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla h(x,y)
. Alternatively, when both
\theta_{1}(\cdot)
and
\theta_{2}(\cdot)
are assumed to be differentiable, the authors
[
49
,
62
]
considered locally quadratic approximations of
\theta_{1}(\cdot)
and
\theta_{2}(\cdot)
, and meanwhile exploited potentially explicit forms of the proximal operators with respect to the partial functions
h(\cdot,y^{k})
and
h(x^{k+1},\cdot)
, respectively. In the recent literature, a series of novel variants of (
3
) and (
4
) are developed for generic nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problems, e.g., see
[
1
,
20
,
38
,
45
,
46
,
63
,
67
,
83
,
84
]
, to mention just a few.

Actually, it is not difficult to observe that the efficiency of (
2
), (
3
), and (
4
) heavily depends on the complexity of their subproblems. With the above literature review, we can see that, when applying the aforementioned alternating minimization algorithms and their variants to our model (
1
), these algorithms ignore the DC structure of the objective function, thereby still possibly suffering from difficult subproblems. For instance, when the nonconvex function
\theta_{1}(\cdot)
(or
\theta_{2}(\cdot)
) is specified as the nonsmooth capped norms (see
[
73
]
or our experiments in Section
5
), the algorithms (
2
), (
3
), and (
4
) must solve a DC optimization subproblem via an inner loop, which is often an expensive procedure for solving large-scale problems. Therefore, how to design an algorithm that is able to make full use of the favorable DC structure of model (
1
) is the main motivation of this paper.

In this paper, we aim to introduce a Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm (UBAMA) to deal with the generalized DC programming (
1
). Our approach exploits the block-variable structure to update variables in a sequential order so that it can easily circumvent the nonseparability appeared in the objective function.
Then, instead of solving DC optimization subproblems directly, we consider approximations to the second DC components and the coupling part by employing the Fenchel-Young inequality and Bregman proximal regularization, respectively. Unlike many traditional DC algorithms for one-block DC programming, one remarkable advantage of our approach is that it does not require the second DC parts (i.e.,
g_{1}(\cdot)
and
g_{2}(\cdot)
) being differentiable. Another remarkable advantage is that the embedded Bregman proximal regularization terms make our approach easily implementable and flexible for many cases.
In other words, our approach provides an algorithmic framework to understand the iterative schemes of some state-of-the-art first-order algorithms proposed in
[
4
,
19
,
62
,
65
,
74
,
75
]
(see remarks in Section
3
) for nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization, so that these algorithms can be concatenated together. Besides, our approach allows us to derive some customized algorithms for some specific applications in the sense that the resulting algorithms often enjoy easy subproblems with closed-form solutions (see examples Section
5
).
Theoretically, we prove that the sequence generated by our algorithm is globally convergent to a critical point of (
1
) under the KŁ condition. Besides, we give the estimation of the local convergence rates of our algorithm under extra prior information of KŁ exponent. Finally, we modify some imaging optimization models to fall into the form of (
1
) for the purpose of verifying the reliability of our proposed algorithm. A series of numerical experiments on imaging data further demonstrate that our approach is promising and reliable for solving generalized DC programming (
1
).

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce some preliminaries and recall some basic properties for nonconvex optimization. In Section
3
, we present our algorithm and give some remarks to state that some classical iterative schemes for nonconvex optimization problems fall into the framework of our approach. In Section
4
, we establish some convergence properties of the proposed algorithm. In Section
5
, some numerical experiments on imaging data are conducted to support the idea of this paper. Finally, we close this paper with drawing some concluding remarks in Section
6
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we summarize some notions, definitions, and basic properties of KŁ inequality from (non) convex and nonsmooth analysis that will be used throughout this paper (e.g., see
[
70
,
71
]
).

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
n
-dimensional Euclidean space equipped with the standard inner product of vectors defined by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. For a given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq p<\infty
, we denote
\left\|x\right\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
as the
\ell_{p}
norm of
x
, where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th component of vector
x
. In particular, we denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm (i.e., Euclidean norm) of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\left\|x\right\|\equiv\sqrt{\langle x,\;x\rangle}
. Moreover, letting
M
be a positive definite matrix (denoted by
M\succ 0
), we define the
M
-norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\left\|x\right\|_{M}=\sqrt{\langle x,Mx\rangle}
. Given a matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote the nuclear norm of matrix
X
by
\left\|X\right\|_{*}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\min\{m,n\}}\sigma_{i}(X)
, where
\sigma_{i}(X)
is the
i
-th largest singular value of
X
. Besides, we denote
\|X\|_{F}
as the standard Frobenius norm of matrix
X
, and
\|X\|_{1}
is an extension of the
\ell_{1}
norm of vectors for matrices, which represents the sum of absolute values of all entries.

Below, we first recall some basic definitions for the coming analysis.

Definition 1
.

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
be an extended-real-valued function, and denote the domain and level set of
f(\cdot)
by

\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;f(x)<\infty\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{\bf Lev}_{f}(\alpha)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;f(x)\leq\alpha\},

respectively. Then, we say that the function
f(\cdot)
is

(i)

proper if
f(x)>-\infty
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}\neq\emptyset
;

(ii)

lower semicontinuous if and only if the level set
\mathrm{\bf Lev}_{f}(\alpha)
is closed for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iii)

lower level-bounded if its level set
\mathrm{\bf Lev}_{f}(\alpha)
is bounded (possibly empty) for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iv)

convex if
f\left(tx+(1-t)y\right)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
and
t\in[0,1]
;

(v)

\varrho
-strongly convex for a given
\varrho>0
if
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
is convex and the following inequality holds for any
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
and
t\in[0,1]
:

f(tx+(1-t)y)\leq tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)-\frac{\varrho}{2}t(1-t)\|x-y\|^{2}.

Definition 2
.

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper and lower semicontinuous function.

(i)

For each
x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
, the Fréchet subdifferential
\widehat{\partial}f(x)
of
f(\cdot)
at
x
is defined by

\widehat{\partial}f(x):=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\neq x\\
y\to x\end{subarray}}\frac{f(y)-f(x)-\langle\xi,y-x\rangle}{\|y-x\|}\geq 0\right\}.

In particular, when
x\notin\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
, we set
\widehat{\partial}f(x)=\emptyset
.

(ii)

The limiting subdifferential
\partial f(x)
of
f(\cdot)
at
x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
is defined by

\partial f(x):=\left\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\exists\left(x^{k},f(x^{k})\right)\to\left(x,f(x)\right),\xi^{k}\in\widehat{\partial}f(x^{k})\\
\text{such that }\;\xi^{k}\to\xi\;\text{ as }\;k\to+\infty\end{array}\right\}.

It follows from Definition
2
that
\widehat{\partial}f(x)\subset\partial f(x)
for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the first set
\widehat{\partial}f(x)
is convex and closed while the second one
\partial f(x)
is closed (e.g., see
[
71
, Theorem 8.6]
).
Let
(x^{k},\xi^{k})\in\mathrm{\bf graph}\,{(\partial f)}:=\left\{(x,\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\xi\in\partial f(x)\right\}
be a sequence. If
(x^{k},\xi^{k})
converges to
(x,\xi)
and
f(x^{k})
converges to
f(x)
, then
(x,\xi)\in\mathrm{\bf graph}\,{(\partial f)}
.
When
f(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable, the limiting subdifferential reduces to the gradient of
f(\cdot)
, denoted by
\nabla f
(e.g., see
[
71
, Exercise 8.8(b)]
). Moreover, when
f(\cdot)
is convex, the limiting subdifferential reduces to the classical subdifferential in convex analysis (see
[
71
, Proposition 8.12]
).
A necessary but not sufficient condition for
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be a local minimizer of
f(\cdot)
is that
x^{*}
is a (limiting-) critical point, i.e.,
0\in\partial f(x^{*}).
Throughout this paper, the set of critical points of
f(\cdot)
is denoted by
\mathrm{\bf crit}\,{(f)}:=\left\{x\;|\;0\in\partial f(x)\right\}
.

As shown in the literature (e.g., see
[
11
, Chapter 5]
), the following first-order characterizations of strong convexity are frequently used for analysis.

Lemma 3
.

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper closed and convex function. Then, for a given
\varrho>0
, the following three claims are equivalent:

(i)

f(\cdot)
is
\varrho
-strongly convex.

(ii)

f(\cdot)-\frac{\varrho}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex.

(iii)

f(y)\geq f(x)+\langle\xi,y-x\rangle+\frac{\varrho}{2}\|y-x\|^{2}
for any
x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}
,
y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}
, and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
.

(iv)

\langle\xi-\eta,x-y\rangle\geq\varrho\|x-y\|^{2}
for any
x,y\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
,
\eta\in\partial f(y)
.

Before stating the KŁ property, we first recall the definition of the following class of desingularizing functions.

Definition 4
.

For
\zeta\in(0,+\infty]
, we define a class of desingularizing functions (denoted by
\varUpsilon_{\zeta}
) as the set of all continuous concave functions
\phi(\cdot):[0,\zeta)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
satisfying the following three properties:

(i)

\phi(0)=0
;

(ii)

\phi(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable on
(0,\zeta)
;

(iii)

for all
s\in(0,\zeta)
,
\phi^{\prime}(s)>0
;

For any subset
\mathbb{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the distance from
x
to
\mathbb{S}
, denoted by
\mathrm{\bf dist}(x,\mathbb{S})
, is defined as

\mathrm{\bf dist}(x,\mathbb{S})=\inf\,\left\{\|y-x\|\;|\;y\in\mathbb{S}\right\}.

With the above preparations, we now state the KŁ property (e.g., see
[
4
, Definition 3.1]
).

Definition 5
(KŁ property, KŁ exponent, and KŁ function).

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper and lower semicontinuous function.

(i)

We say that the function
f(\cdot)
has the KŁ property at

\bar{x}\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\right\}

if there exist
\zeta\in(0,+\infty]
, a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\bar{x}
, a continuous concave function
\phi(\cdot)\in\varUpsilon_{\zeta}
, and for all
x\in\mathcal{N}\cap\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;f(\bar{x})<f(y)<f(\bar{x})+\zeta\right\}
, the following KŁ inequality holds, i.e.,

\phi^{\prime}\left(f(x)-f(\bar{x})\right)\mathrm{\bf dist}\left(0,\partial f(x)\right)\geq 1.

(5)

(ii)

If
f(\cdot)
satisfies the above KŁ property at each point of
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}
and
\phi(\cdot)
in (
5
) can be chosen as
\phi(s)=as^{1-\vartheta}
for some
\vartheta\in[0,1)
and
a>0
, then we say that
f(\cdot)
satisfies the KŁ property at
\bar{x}
with exponent
\vartheta
.

(iii)

If
f(\cdot)
satisfies the KŁ property at all points in
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}
, we say that
f(\cdot)
is a KŁ function. Moreover, if
f(\cdot)
satisfies the KŁ property with exponent
\vartheta\in[0,1)
at all points in
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}
, we say that
f(\cdot)
is a KŁ function with exponent
\vartheta
.

To further analyze the convergence of the proposed algorithm to a critical point of (
1
), we recall the uniformized KŁ property introduced in
[
19
, Lemma 6]
, which is a more general KŁ property.

Lemma 6
(uniformized KŁ property).

Let
\Gamma
be a compact set and let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper and lower semicontinuous function. Assume that
f(\cdot)
is constant on
\Gamma
and satisfies the KŁ property at each point of
\Gamma
. Then, there exist
\varepsilon>0
,
\zeta>0
and a continuous concave function
\phi\in\varUpsilon_{\zeta}
such that for each
\widehat{x}\in\Gamma
the following KŁ inequality

\phi^{\prime}\left(f(x)-f(\widehat{x})\right)\mathrm{\bf dist}\left(0,\partial f(x)\right)\geq 1,

holds for any

x\in\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\mathrm{\bf dist}(y,\Gamma)<\varepsilon\right\}\cap\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;f(\widehat{x})<f(y)<f(\widehat{x})+\zeta\right\}.

Interestingly, it is well documented in
[
16
,
17
,
19
]
that a broad class of real-world optimization problems satisfy the KŁ property, since most of them have semi-algebraic objective functions or semi-algebraic constraint sets. Therefore, by the semi-algebraic family
[
19
, Example 2]
, we can see that all problems in Section
5
satisfy the KŁ property.

Below, we recall the famous descent lemma in optimization (e.g., see
[
11
, Lemma 5.7]
).

Lemma 7
(descent lemma).

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be an
L
-smooth function (
L\geq 0
)
over a given convex set
\mathbb{S}
, i.e.,
f(\cdot)
is differentiable over
\mathbb{S}
and satisfies

\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\leq L\|x-y\|,\quad\forall x,y\in\mathbb{S}.

Then, for any
x,y\in\mathbb{S}
, we have

f(y)\leq f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}.

Definition 8
.

Let
f(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
be an extended-real-valued function. The conjugate function of
f(\cdot)
is defined by

f^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\langle y,x\rangle-f(x)\right\}.

It is well-know from
[
11
, Chapter 4]
that if
f(\cdot)
is proper, lower semicontinuous and convex, then
f^{*}(\cdot)
is also proper, lower semicontinuous and convex. Furthermore, it is easy to see from Definition
8
that

f(x)+f^{*}(y)\geq\langle x,y\rangle,

(6)

and in particular, the equality holds if and only if
y\in\partial f(x)
. Additionally, for any
x
and
y
, one has
y\in\partial f(x)
if and only if
x\in\partial f^{*}(y)
(e.g., see
[
11
, Theorem 4.20]
). In the literature, the inequality (
6
) is usually called Fenchel-Young inequality, which will be useful for our algorithmic design.

Definition 9
(proximal mapping).

Given a function
\theta(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
and a scalar
t>0
, the proximal mapping of
t\theta(\cdot)
is the operator given by

\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{t\theta}({\bm{a}})=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{t\theta(x)+\frac{1}{2}\|x-{\bm{a}}\|^{2}\right\}\quad\text{for any }{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7)

When
\theta(\cdot)
is a proper lower semicontinuous and convex function, the
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{t\theta}(\cdot)
is well-defined, i.e., the
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{t\theta}({\bm{a}})
is a singleton for any
{\bm{a}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In particular, when

•

\theta(x)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}}(x)
, where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}}(x)
is the indicator function associated to the nonempty closed convex set
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is defined

\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{ if }x\in\mathbb{S},\\
\infty,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

then the proximal operator (
7
) reduces to the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{S}
, i.e.,

\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}}}({\bm{a}})=\arg\min\left\{\|x-{\bm{a}}\|^{2}\;|\;x\in\mathbb{S}\right\}=\mathrm{\bf Proj}_{\mathbb{S}}({\bm{a}}).

•

\theta(x)=\|x\|_{1}
, then the proximal operator (
7
) becomes the well-known shrinkage (or soft-thresholding) operator (see
[
32
]
), i.e.,

\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{t\|\cdot\|_{1}}({\bm{a}})=\mathrm{\bf shrink}({\bm{a}},t)\equiv\text{\rm sign}({\bm{a}})\odot\max\{|{\bm{a}}|-t,0\},

where ‘
\text{\rm sign}(\cdot)
’ is the sign function, and ‘
\odot
’ represents the component-wise product.

•

\theta(X)=\|X\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm of matrix
X
, then the proximal operator (
7
) corresponds to the singular value thresholding operator (see
[
23
]
), i.e.,

\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{t\|\cdot\|_{*}}(A)=\arg\min_{X}\left\{t\|X\|_{*}+\frac{1}{2}\|X-A\|^{2}_{F}\right\}=\mathrm{\bf SVT}(A,t)\equiv U\mathrm{\bf shrink}(\Sigma,t)V^{\top},

where
U\Sigma V^{\top}
is the singular value decomposition of matrix
A
.

To end this section, we recall the definition of Bregman distance and its properties (see
[
22
]
and also
[
11
, Chapter 9]
), which are useful tools for our algorithmic design and convergence analysis.

Definition 10
(Bregman distance).

Let
\psi(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
be a proper, closed and convex function that is differentiable over
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}
. The Bregman distance associated with the kernel
\psi(\cdot)
is the function
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot):\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)}\times\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)=\psi(x)-\psi(y)-\langle\nabla\psi(y),x-y\rangle,\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)},y\in\mathrm{\bf int}\,{(\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)})}.

Generally speaking, the Bregman distance does not necessarily enjoy the symmetry and the triangle inequality property. However, such a measurement covers the standard Euclidean distance as its special case, i.e.,
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
if the kernel function is taken as
\psi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
. More generally, letting
M
be a positive definite matrix and
\psi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{M}^{2}
, we obtain
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}_{M}
, which is useful for solving quadratic programming. Besides, when taking the kernel function as
\psi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\log x_{i}
, the Bregman distance is specified as

\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\log\frac{x_{i}}{y_{i}}+y_{i}-x_{i},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\;\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++},

which plays an important role in solving optimization problems over unit simplex

\Delta^{n}:=\left\{\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\Big|\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1,\;x_{i}\geq 0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\;\right\},

(8)

since such a Bregman function can simplify the proximal subproblems so that they enjoy closed-form solutions (see
[
7
]
and our experiments in Section
5.3
). Below, we summarize some properties of Bregman distance (also see
[
10
,
11
]
).

Lemma 11
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is nonempty closed and convex, and the function
\psi(\cdot)
is proper closed convex and differentiable over
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}
. If
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\psi)}
and
\psi(\cdot)+\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{S}}(\cdot)
is
\varrho
-strongly convex (
\varrho>0
), then the Bregman distance
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(\cdot,\cdot)
associated with
\psi(\cdot)
has the following properties:

(i)

\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)\geq\frac{\varrho}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}
and
y\in\mathbb{S}\cap\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}
;

(ii)

Let
x\in\mathbb{S}
and
y\in\mathbb{S}\cap\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}
. Then
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)\geq 0
, and in particular, the equality holds if and only if
x=y
;

(iii)

If
\nabla\psi
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
L_{\psi}
, it holds that
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,y)\leq\frac{L_{\psi}}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}
and
y\in\mathbb{S}\cap\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial\psi)}
.

In the literature, the Bregman distance has been widely used to design efficient algorithms for optimization problems, we here refer the reader to
[
10
,
13
,
26
,
37
,
61
,
76
]
and references therein for more properties and applications.

3
The Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm

In this section, we first introduce the unified Bregman alternating minimization algorithm for generalized DC programming (
1
). Then, we will show that our algorithmic framework covers some state-of-the-art algorithms.

As we know, those seminal DC algorithms for DC programming often require the second DC parts being differentiable. However, many real-world problems usually possess two nonsmooth DC components. In this paper, we follow the spirit of majorization minimization and then employ the Fenchel-Young inequality (
6
) to approximate
-g_{1}(x)
and
-g_{2}(y)
iteratively. More specifically, at the
k
-th iterate
(x^{k},y^{k})
, we always have

g_{1}^{*}(\xi)-\langle x^{k},\xi\rangle\geq-g_{1}(x^{k})\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}^{*}(\eta)-\langle y^{k},\eta\rangle\geq-g_{2}(y^{k}).

Comparatively, the above approximations provide us a flexible way to exploit the favorable structure of the conjugate functions of
g_{1}(x)
and
g_{2}(y)
for some real-world applications.

In addition, the coupling function
h(x,y)
often makes the
x
- and
y
-subproblems difficult in the sense that both subproblems neither have closed-form solutions, even when the proximal operators of
f_{1}(x)
and
f_{2}(y)
can be expressed explicitly, nor can be accurately calculated via fast solvers. So, we employ the popular linearization strategy to approximate the underlying subproblems. However, we notice that the coupling function
h(x,y)
is assumed to have two parts, i.e.,
h(x,y)=h^{+}(x,y)-h^{-}(x,y)
. In particular, when
h(x,\cdot)
(resp.
h(\cdot,y)
) is a DC function on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp.
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
), we call
h(\cdot,\cdot)
a generalized partial DC function (see
[
65
]
). Here, we just assume that
h(x,y)
consists of two parts, but do not strictly assume
h(x,y)
being a generalized partial DC function. For this situation, directly linearizing
h(x,y)
will ignore its favorable split nature, which encourages us to consider the linearization approximation to
h^{-}(x,y)
. Actually, we will show below that the split form of
h(x,y)
is of benefit for designing customized variants of our algorithm.

As shown in the literature, the uniqueness of optimal solutions to
x
- and
y
-subproblems is of importance for convergence analysis. Here we shall incorporate Bregman proximal regularization terms into the
x
- and
y
-subproblems, thereby ensuring that both subproblems are strongly convex as long as the proximal parameters can be chosen appropriately. Consequently, on the one hand, each subproblem has a unique solution. On the other hand, the Bregman proximal terms provide us a flexible framework to design customized algorithms, and meanwhile to understand some novel existing algorithms. Our unified algorithm is formally described in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm for (
1
).

Take a starting point
(x^{0},y^{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and strongly convex functions
\psi_{k}(\cdot)
and
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)
.

for

k=0,1,2,\cdots

do

Compute
\xi^{k+1}
and
\eta^{k+1}
:

\xi^{k+1}\in\arg\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{g_{1}^{*}(\xi)-\langle x^{k},\xi\rangle\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{k+1}\in\arg\min_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{g_{2}^{*}(\eta)-\langle y^{k},\eta\rangle\right\}.

(9)

Update
x^{k+1}
and
y^{k+1}
in an alternating order as follows:

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f_{1}(x)+h^{+}(x,y^{k})-\langle x-x^{k},\bm{u}^{k}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x,x^{k})\right\},

(10)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{f_{2}(y)+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y)-\langle y-y^{k},\bm{v}^{k}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y,y^{k})\right\},

(11)

where
\bm{u}^{k}=\xi^{k+1}+\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})
and
\bm{v}^{k}=\eta^{k+1}+\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})
.

end

for

Remark 12
.

Roughly speaking, both subproblems (
10
) and (
11
) dominate the main computational cost of Algorithm
1
. When taking some specific Bregman kernel functions such as
\psi_{k}(\cdot)=\varphi_{k}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
, we can see that (
10
) and (
11
) amount to evaluating the following proximal operator:

\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f+\hat{h}}({\bm{a}}^{k})=\arg\min_{w}\left\{f(w)+\hat{h}(w)+\frac{1}{2}\|w-{\bm{a}}^{k}\|^{2}\right\},

where
f(\cdot)
represents
f_{1}(\cdot)
or
f_{2}(\cdot)
,
\hat{h}(\cdot)
corresponds to
h^{+}(\cdot,y^{k})
or
h^{+}(x^{k+1},\cdot)
, and
{\bm{a}}^{k}
is a point associated with
x^{k}
or
y^{k}
. In this situation, the efficiency of Algorithm
1
, to a large extent, depends on the evaluation of the proximal operator
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f+\hat{h}}({\bm{a}}^{k})
. Generally, the appearance of
\hat{h}(\cdot)
coupling with a given
y^{k}
or
x^{k+1}
possibly leads to the proximal operator
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f+\hat{h}}({\bm{a}}^{k})
losing its closed-form solution. Therefore, how to maximally explore the explicit proximal operators is crucial for the application of Algorithm
1
. Indeed, we can easily handle the difficulty caused by
\hat{h}(\cdot)
in many cases. Note that we do not impose strict limitations on
h^{+}(x,y)
and
h^{-}(x,y)
. Therefore, we can freely rewrite the form of
h(x,y)
according to actual needs such that
\hat{h}(\cdot)
vanishes from
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f+\hat{h}}({\bm{a}}^{k})
for the purpose of exploiting the explicit form of
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f}({\bm{a}}^{k})
(e.g., see Remarks
13
-
16
and applications in Section
5
). Of course, when the evaluation of proximal operator
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f+\hat{h}}({\bm{a}}^{k})
(or
\mathrm{\bf Prox}_{f}({\bm{a}}^{k})
) is inevitable without a closed-form solution, we suggest employing some solvers equipped with inexact strategies (e.g.,
[
9
,
11
]
) to improve the performance of Algorithm
1
.

Below, we present some remarks (i.e., Remarks
13
-
16
) to show that our Algorithm
1
can produce some existing novel iterative schemes, when reformulating some specific nonconvex optimization models as special cases of (
1
). Here, we shall emphasize that one of the main goals of these remarks is to help us understand those state-of-the-art algorithms tailored for nonsmooth and nonconvex programming (e.g.,
[
4
,
19
,
62
,
65
,
74
,
75
]
) in a unified framework, so that those algorithms can be concatenated together via Algorithm
1
. Another main goal is to tell the reader how to design customized variants of Algorithm
1
for solving some real-world problems. So, we only show how to employ Algorithm
1
to derive those existing iterative schemes developed in the literature. It is noteworthy that our convergence results are not necessarily sufficient for the resulting algorithms, and the reader is referred to the original papers for their tightest convergence analysis or is requested to reanalyze their tighter convergence when the required conditions could be weakened.

Remark 13
.

When the generalized DC programming (
1
) reduces to the standard DC programming of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\theta_{1}(x):=f_{1}(x)-g_{1}(x)\right\},

(12)

it is clear that, by setting
\psi_{k}(x)=0
, an application of Algorithm
1
to (
12
) yields the iterative scheme of the standard DC algorithm
[
50
,
66
]
. When we take
\psi_{k}(x)=\frac{c_{k}}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, our Algorithm
1
for (
12
) reads as

x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f_{1}(x)-\langle x,\xi^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{c_{k}}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\}\quad\text{ with }\quad\xi^{k+1}\in\partial g_{1}(x^{k}),

which immediately coincides with the iterative scheme of the method introduced in
[
74
]
. Moreover, when we further consider a more general case of (
12
) as follows

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\theta(x):=f_{1}(x)-g_{1}(x)+h_{1}(x)\right\},

(13)

we can rewrite (
13
) as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{\Phi(x,y):=f_{1}(x)-g_{1}(x)-\left(-h_{1}(x)\right)\right\},

where the
h(x,y)
in (
1
) can be specified as
h(x,y)=0-\left(-h_{1}(x)\right)
. In this situation, when assuming that
h_{1}(x)
is differentiable, we have
\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x,y)=-\nabla h_{1}(x)
, and Algorithm
1
immediately reads as

x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{f_{1}(x)+\langle x,\nabla h_{1}(x^{k})-\xi^{k+1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x,x^{k})\right\}\quad\text{ with }\quad\xi^{k+1}\in\partial g_{1}(x^{k}),

which corresponds to the iterative scheme of the method introduced in
[
75
]
. Particularly, we can also reformulate some constrained convex optimization problems as the form of (
13
), then the iterative schemes of some classical methods, such as proximal (projected) gradient method and mirror descent method (e.g., see
[
11
, Chapters 8-10]
), can be derived under the framework of our Algorithm
1
.

Remark 14
.

When considering a simplified version of model (
1
) without the first two DC parts but with setting
h^{-}(x,y)=-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)
and assuming that
f_{1}(x)
and
f_{2}(y)
are continuously differentiable, model (
1
) is specified as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\;\Phi(x,y):=h^{+}(x,y)-\underbrace{\left(-f_{1}(x)-f_{2}(y)\right)}_{h^{-}(x,y)}\;\right\}.

Clearly,
\bm{u}^{k}=\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})=-\nabla f_{1}(x^{k})
and
\bm{v}^{k}=\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})=-\nabla f_{2}(y^{k})
. When the Bregman proximal terms
\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x,x^{k})
and
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y,y^{k})
are further taken as
\frac{1}{2\tau}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}
and
\frac{1}{2\sigma}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}
(i.e., by setting
\psi_{k}(x)=\frac{1}{2\tau}\|x\|^{2}
and
\varphi_{k}(y)=\frac{1}{2\sigma}\|y\|^{2}
), respectively, our Algorithm
1
immediately is specified as

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{h^{+}(x,y^{k})+\langle x,\nabla f_{1}(x^{k})\rangle+\frac{1}{2\tau}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{h^{+}(x^{k+1},y)+\langle y,\nabla f_{2}(y^{k})\rangle+\frac{1}{2\sigma}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

which is precisely the same as the iterative scheme of the so-named Alternating Structured-Adapted Proximal Gradient Descent (ASAPGD) algorithm proposed in
[
62
]
. Actually, we can derive some other variants of the ASAPGD algorithm by taking different Bregman proximal terms so that the potentially favorable structure of
h^{+}(x,y)
can be efficiently exploited.

Remark 15
.

As discussed in Remark
14
, when we consider the case of (
1
) with the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\;h(x,y):=h^{+}(x,y)-h^{-}(x,y)\;\right\},

and assume
h(x,y)
being a generalized partial DC function, our Algorithm
1
immediately reduces to

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{h^{+}(x,y^{k})-\langle x,\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x,x^{k})\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left\{h^{+}(x^{k+1},y)-\langle y,\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y,y^{k})\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Apparently, when setting
\psi_{k}(x)=0
and
\varphi_{k}(y)=0
, the resulting iterative scheme is essentially the same as the one proposed in the most recent work
[
65
]
when dealing with differentiable cases. More generally, reformulating our model (
1
) as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\;\left\{\;\Phi(x,y):=\underbrace{\left(f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(y)+h^{+}(x,y)\right)}_{h_{1}(x,y)}-\underbrace{\left(g_{1}(x)+g_{2}(y)+h^{-}(x,y)\right)}_{h_{2}(x,y)}\;\right\},

(14)

then applying the alternating DC algorithm
[
65
]
to (
14
) also falls into the special case of Algorithm
1
without the Bregman proximal terms. Comparatively, our Algorithm
1
possesses two universal proximal regularization terms so that the underlying subproblems usually have a unique solution.

Remark 16
.

Interestingly, when considering the special case of (

) where
g_{1}(x)
and

]

)) and

4
Convergence analysis

In this section, we are concerned with the convergence properties of the proposed Algorithm
1
. We begin this section with stating some standard assumptions on model (
1
) as assumed in
[
67
]
, which will be used in convergence analysis.

Assumption 17
.

The components
f_{i}

(i=1,2)
are proper lower semicontinuous functions, and
g_{i}

(i=1,2)
are continuous and convex functions.

Assumption 18
.

\inf_{x,y}\;\Phi(x,y)=\inf_{x,y}\;\left\{\theta_{1}(x)+\theta_{2}(y)+h(x,y)\right\}>-\infty.

Assumption 19
.

The coupling function
h(x,y)=h^{+}(x,y)-h^{-}(x,y)
satisfies the following conditions.

(i)

For any fixed
y
, the function
h^{-}(\cdot,y)
is
C_{L_{1}^{-}(y)}^{1,1}
, namely the partial gradient
\nabla_{x}h^{-}(\cdot,y)
is globally Lipschitz with moduli
L_{1}^{-}(y)
, that is

\left\|\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x_{1},y)-\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x_{2},y)\right\|\leq L_{1}^{-}(y)\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,\;\forall x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Likewise, for any fixed
x
, the function
h^{-}(x,\cdot)
is assumed to be
C_{L_{2}^{-}(x)}^{1,1}
.

(ii)

For
i=1,2
, there exist
\lambda_{i}^{-}
and
\lambda_{i}^{+}>0
such that

\inf\{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}\geq\lambda_{1}^{-}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\inf\{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}\geq\lambda_{2}^{-},

(15)

and

\sup\{L^{-}_{1}(y^{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}\leq\lambda_{1}^{+}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\sup\{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}\}\leq\lambda_{2}^{+}.

(16)

(iii)

h(x,y)
is a continuously differentiable function and its gradient
\nabla h(x,y)=\nabla h^{+}(x,y)-\nabla h^{-}(x,y)
is Lipschitz continuous on bounded subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
. That is, for each bounded subsets
\mathbb{B}_{1}\times\mathbb{B}_{2}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there exists
L>0
(or
L^{+}
and
L^{-}
satisfying
L=L^{+}+L^{-}
) such that

\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}h(x_{1},y_{1})\\
\nabla_{y}h(x_{1},y_{1})\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}h(x_{2},y_{2})\\
\nabla_{y}h(x_{2},y_{2})\end{pmatrix}\right\|\leq L\left\|\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
y_{1}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}\\
y_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|

for all
(x_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{B}_{1}\times\mathbb{B}_{2}

(i=1,2)
.

Before the convergence analysis, for notational simplicity, we let

In what follows, we denote

and

for notational simplicity. Besides, we use

throughout this paper, and each iteration-varying parameter and the strongly convex constant of the corresponding Bregman kernel function will be merged into one for simplicity. Now, we first show that
\{\Psi(x^{k},\xi^{k},y^{k},\eta^{k})\}\equiv\{\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})\}

Lemma 20
.

Suppose Assumptions
17
and
19
hold. Let
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}:=(x^{k},y^{k})\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm

. Then, both sequences
\left\{\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})\right\}
and
\left\{\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})\right\}
are decreasing, and there exists a constant

such that

\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})\leq\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}.

(19)

Proof.

Invoking the first-order optimality condition of (

0\in\partial g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k+1})-x^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad 0\in\partial g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k+1})-y^{k}.

(20)

It then follows from the Fenchel-Young inequality (

) that

\xi^{k+1}\in\partial g_{1}(x^{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\eta^{k+1}\in\partial g_{2}(y^{k}),

(21)

which further implies that

g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k+1})=\langle x^{k},\xi^{k+1}\rangle-g_{1}(x^{k})\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k+1})=\langle y^{k},\eta^{k+1}\rangle-g_{2}(y^{k}).

(22)

Consequently, it follows from (
22
) and the notion of
\Psi(\bm{w})
given by (
17
) that

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(x^{k+1})+g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k+1})-\langle x^{k+1},\xi^{k+1}\rangle+f_{2}(y^{k+1})+g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k+1})-\langle y^{k+1},\eta^{k+1}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{1}(x^{k+1})-\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\xi^{k+1}\rangle-g_{1}(x^{k})+f_{2}(y^{k+1})-\langle y^{k+1}-y^{k},\eta^{k+1}\rangle-g_{2}(y^{k})

(23)

\displaystyle\qquad+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1}).

According to Assumption
19
(i), it follows from Lemma

that

-h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\leq-h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})-\left\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right\rangle+\frac{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k})}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

where

is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x,y^{k})
at
x^{k}
. Similarly, we have

-h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})\leq-h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})-\left\langle y^{k+1}-y^{k},\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\rangle+\frac{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})}{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2},

is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y)
at
y^{k}
. Consequently, substituting the above two inequalities into (

) leads to

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x^{k+1})-\left\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\xi^{k+1}+\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right\rangle-g_{1}(x^{k})

(24)

\displaystyle\quad+f_{2}(y^{k+1})-\left\langle y^{k+1}-y^{k},\eta^{k+1}+\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\rangle-g_{2}(y^{k})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k})}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})}{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k}).

Using the updating schemes of
x^{k+1}
and
y^{k+1}
in (
10
) and (

), respectively, we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(x^{k+1})+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k})-

\displaystyle\left\langle x^{k+1}-x^{k},\xi^{k+1}+\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right\rangle

(25)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\leq f_{1}(x^{k})+h^{+}(x^{k},y^{k})

and

\displaystyle f_{2}(y^{k+1})+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-

\displaystyle\left\langle y^{k+1}-y^{k},\eta^{k+1}+\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\rangle

(26)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\leq f_{2}(y^{k})+h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k}).

Plugging the two inequalities (

) and (

) into (

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x^{k})-g_{1}(x^{k})+f_{2}(y^{k})-g_{2}(y^{k})+h^{+}(x^{k},y^{k})-h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k})}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+\frac{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})}{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k})

\displaystyle=\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\frac{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k})}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle\hskip 42.67912pt-\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k})+\frac{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})}{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}.

Recalling the strong convexity of
\psi_{k}(\cdot)
and
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)
and Lemma

\psi_{k}(\cdot)
and
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)
with strongly convex modulus

and

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k}):=\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k})-\frac{L_{1}^{-}(y^{k})}{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k}):=\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k})-\frac{L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})}{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2},

\widetilde{\psi}_{k}(\cdot)
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}(\cdot)
that there exists
\gamma_{k}:=\min\{\rho_{1}-L_{1}^{-}(y^{k}),\rho_{2}-L_{2}^{-}(x^{k+1})\}>0

Therefore, combining (

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\left(\mathscr{B}_{\widetilde{\psi}_{k}}(x^{k+1},x^{k})+\mathscr{B}_{\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\widehat{\mathscr{B}_{k}}(\bm{z}^{k+1},\bm{z}^{k})

\displaystyle\leq\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}.

where the last inequality follows from (
18
). Such an inequality immediately means that the sequence
\{\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})\}
is decreasing. Moreover, using (
18
) again to the left hand side of (

) yields

\Phi(\bm{z}^{k+1})\leq\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1})\leq\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2},

(30)

which shows that
\{\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})\}
is also decreasing. The proof is completed.

Hereafter, we will show that two adjacent points of the sequence
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}=(x^{k},y^{k})\right\}

Lemma 21
.

Suppose that Assumptions
17
-
19
hold. Let
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}=(x^{k},y^{k})\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Then, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|=0

According to (
15
), we can expect
\mu=\inf_{k}\{\gamma_{k}\}>0
. Then, it follows from (

) that

\Phi(\bm{z}^{k+1})\leq\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\Phi(\bm{z}^{k})-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}

(31)

Let

be a positive integer. Summing (

) from

to

leads to

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\mu}\left(\Phi(\bm{z}^{0})-\Phi(\bm{z}^{N})\right).

By Assumption

, i.e.,
\inf_{\bm{z}}\{\Phi(\bm{z}):=\Phi(x,y)\}>-\infty
, taking the limit as

immediately arrives at

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty.

Hence, we conclude that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\|=0
.

Below, we present an inequality characterizing the relationship between the sequences
\{\bm{w}^{k}\}
and
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}

Lemma 22
.

Suppose Assumptions
17
and
19
hold. Let
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}:=(x^{k},y^{k})\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
which is assumed to be bounded. Then, there exists

such that for any

, we have

\mathrm{\bf dist}(0,\partial\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1}))\leq\tau\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|.

Writing the first-order optimality conditions of (
10
) and (

) arrives at

0\in\partial f_{1}(x^{k+1})-\xi^{k+1}+\nabla_{x}h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k})-\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})+\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k})

(32)

and

(33)

On the other hand, calculating
\partial\Psi(\bm{w})
at
\bm{w}^{k+1}:=(x^{k+1},\xi^{k+1},y^{k+1},\eta^{k+1})
leads to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial f_{1}(x^{k+1})-\xi^{k+1}+\nabla_{x}h(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})&=\partial_{x}\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1}),\\
-x^{k+1}+\partial g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k+1})&=\partial_{\xi}\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1}),\\

(34)

Consequently, substituting (

) and (
33

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}(0,\partial\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1}))

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\nabla_{x}h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-\nabla_{x}h^{+}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\|+\left\|\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k})\right\|+\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|+\left\|\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq(L_{+}+2L_{-}+2+L_{\psi_{k}}+L_{\varphi_{k}})\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|,

where the first inequality follows from the definition of distance function and the triangle inequality (i.e.,
\|a+b\|\leq\|a\|+\|b\|
for all

), and the second inequality is due to the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\psi_{k}
and
\nabla\varphi_{k}
with Lipschitz constants
L_{\psi_{k}}
and
L_{\varphi_{k}}
, respectively. We can take
\tau=\sup\{L_{+}+2L_{-}+1+L_{\psi_{k}}+L_{\varphi_{k}}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}<\infty
from (
16
) and immediately conclude that

\mathrm{\bf dist}(0,\partial\Psi(\bm{w}^{k+1}))\leq\tau\left\|\bm{z}^{k+1}-\bm{z}^{k}\right\|.

(35)

The proof is complete.

Note that the boundedness of the sequence is a standard assumption for many nonconvex optimization algorithms. It is documented in
[
4
, Remark 3.3]
that the boundedness assumption on the sequence
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}\right\}
automatically holds when the corresponding lower level set
\{\bm{z}\;|\;\Phi(\bm{z})\leq\alpha_{0}\}
is compact for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
h(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
. Although checking the aforementioned boundedness is not an easy task in general, a large number of nonconvex optimization algorithms have received great successes in the fields of data sciences, machine learning and image processing. To a certain extent, those successful applications imply that the required conditions such as the boundedness of the sequences generated by nonconvex optimization algorithms could be reached with a high probability, or some theoretical assumptions could be further weakened.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose that Assumptions
17
–
19
hold. Let
\left\{\bm{z}^{k}:=(x^{k},y^{k})\right\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
which is assumed to be bounded. The following assertions hold.

(i)

The set of accumulation points of the sequence
\left\{\bm{w}^{k}:=(x^{k},\xi^{k},y^{k},\eta^{k})\right\}
, denoted by
\mathbb{W}^{*}
, is a nonempty compact set and contained in
\mathrm{\bf crit}\,{(\Psi)}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{W}^{*}\subset\mathrm{\bf crit}\,{(\Psi)}
.

(ii)

The limit
\Psi^{\infty}:=\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})
exists.

(iii)

\Psi(\bm{w})\equiv\Psi^{\infty}
for all
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{W}^{*}
.

Proof.

We first prove Item (i). The boundedness assumption on
\{\bm{z}^{k}=(x^{k},y^{k})\}
, which, together with the continuity and convexity of
g_{i}(\cdot)
(
i=1,2
), implies that the sequence
\{(\xi^{k},\eta^{k})\}
is also bounded. As a consequence, the sequence
\left\{\bm{w}^{k}=(x^{k},\xi^{k},y^{k},\eta^{k})\right\}
is also bounded, which, together with the closedness of the set
\mathbb{W}^{*}
, immediately shows that
\mathbb{W}^{*}
is a nonempty compact set. Given any
\bm{w}^{*}\in\mathbb{W}^{*}
being a limit point of some subsequence, denoted by
\left\{\bm{w}^{k_{j}}=(x^{k_{j}},\xi^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}},\eta^{k_{j}})\right\}
, we will prove that
\bm{w}^{*}=(x^{*},\xi^{*},y^{*},\eta^{*})
is a critical point of
\Psi(\cdot)
.
Letting
k=k_{j}-1
, it follows from the optimal condition of (
10
) that

\displaystyle f_{1}(x^{k_{j}})\;+\;

\displaystyle h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle x^{k_{j}}-x^{k_{j}-1},\bm{u}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k_{j}},x^{k_{j}-1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x^{*})+h^{+}(x^{*},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle x^{*}-x^{k_{j}-1},\bm{u}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{*},x^{k_{j}-1}),

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle f_{1}(x^{k_{j}})\;+\;

\displaystyle h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}-1})+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k_{j}},x^{k_{j}-1})

(36)

\displaystyle\leq f_{1}(x^{*})+h^{+}(x^{*},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle x^{*}-x^{k_{j}},\bm{u}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{*},x^{k_{j}-1}).

Similarly, it follows from the optimal condition of (
11
) that

\displaystyle f_{2}(y^{k_{j}})\;+\;

\displaystyle h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}-1})

(37)

\displaystyle\leq f_{2}(y^{*})+h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{*})-\langle y^{*}-y^{k_{j}},\bm{v}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{*},y^{k_{j}-1}).

Hence, it follows from the nonnegativity of Bregman distance and the two inequalities (
36
) and (

) that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}\Psi(\bm{w}^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{1}(x^{k_{j}})-\langle x^{k_{j}},\xi^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k_{j}})+f_{2}(y^{k_{j}})-\langle y^{k_{j}},\eta^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k_{j}})+h(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{j\to\infty}f_{1}(x^{k_{j}})+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{k_{j}},x^{k_{j}-1})-\langle x^{k_{j}},\xi^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+f_{2}(y^{k_{j}})+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle y^{k_{j}},\eta^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k_{j}})+h(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{j\to\infty}\left\{f_{1}(x^{*})+h^{+}(x^{*},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle x^{*}-x^{k_{j}},\bm{u}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x^{*},x^{k_{j}-1})\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\langle x^{k_{j}},\xi^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{1}^{*}(\xi^{k_{j}})+f_{2}(y^{*})+h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{*})-\langle y^{*}-y^{k_{j}},\bm{v}^{k_{j}-1}\rangle

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\left.+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y^{*},y^{k_{j}-1})-\langle y^{k_{j}},\eta^{k_{j}}\rangle+g_{2}^{*}(\eta^{k_{j}})-h^{+}(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}-1})-h^{-}(x^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})\right\}

(38)

Consequently,

. On the other hand, it follows from Lemmas

is a critical point of

Now, we turn our attention to Item (ii). According to Assumption

\left\{\Psi(\bm{w}^{k})\right\}
is bounded below. Besides, we see that the sequence

. From Items (i) and (ii), we have

Thus, we conclude by the arbitrariness of

With the above preparations, we are now proving that the sequence
\{\bm{z}^{k}\}

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, we aim to show that our Algorithm
1
(denoted by ‘UBAMA’) is a customized solver for generalized DC programming (
1

As tested in the nonconvex literature, here we will not check the conditions required in theoretical analysis for the coming models, since the quality of these obtained solutions can be seen from the recovered images. 5.1
Image reconstruction

Image deconvolution and inpainting are prototypical image reconstruction problems. In this subsection, we consider the task of reconstructing an image from the one which is convoluted (or blurred) and suffered some noise and loss of information, i.e., reconstructing image

[
72

[

introduced a weighted difference of anisotropic and isotropic TV model, and showed numerically that such a model is powerful for image denoising, image deblurring, and magnetic resonance imaging reconstruction. Here, we follow this idea to tackle (

\displaystyle=\left(K^{\top}S^{\top}SK+c_{k}I\right)^{-1}\left(K^{\top}S^{\top}b+c_{k}x^{k}-\beta\mathbf{D}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}x^{k}-y^{k}\right)\right),

which precisely corresponds to the
x
-subproblem of the PALM algorithm (
4
). Besides, the

-subproblem of the PALM algorithm for (
52

(56)

which in general requires an optimization solver to find an approximation instead of its accurate solution. In our experiments, by utilizing the simple DC structure, we employ one step approximation (

5.2
Robust principal component analysis

The model (
52
) in Section
5.1
has only one DC part. Now, in this subsection, we will consider a more general case where there exist two separable DC parts in the objective function. More concretely, we consider the well-known Robust Principal Component Analysis (RPCA, see
[
24
]
), which is a general framework to extract a low-rank matrix and a sparse one from an incomplete and noisy observation. Mathematically, the most general form of the RPCA problem can be expressed as follows:

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\;\left\{~\text{rank}(X)+\tau\|Y\|_{0}\;\;|\;\;\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(X+Y-B)\|_{F}\leq\delta~\right\},

(57)

where
\text{rank}(X)
is the rank function,
\|Y\|_{0}
represents the number of nonzero components of
Y
,
B
is the observed data matrix,
\delta
represents the standard deviation of Gaussian white noise,
\Omega
is a subset of the index set of entries
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}\times\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the orthogonal projection onto the span of matrices vanishing outside of
\Omega
so that the
ij
-th entry of
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(B)
is
B_{ij}
if
(i,j)\in\Omega
and zero otherwise. Such a problem has been proven to be NP-hard. Therefore, one of the most popular ways is the convex relaxation approach (see
[
24
]
), which employs
\|X\|_{*}
and
\|Y\|_{1}
to approximate
\text{rank}(X)
and
\|Y\|_{0}
, respectively. In
[
73
]
, the authors introduced a novel nonconvex formulation for the RPCA problem using the capped trace norm and the capped
\ell_{1}
-norm. Here, we accordingly follow their idea to reformulate (
57
) as follows:

\min_{X,Y}\left\{\underbrace{\left\|X\right\|_{*}-g_{1}\left(X;\kappa_{1}\right)}_{\theta_{1}(X):=\left\|X\right\|_{*,\kappa_{1}}}+\underbrace{\tau\left(\left\|Y\right\|_{1}-g_{2}\left(Y;\kappa_{2}\right)\right)}_{\theta_{2}(Y)=\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\kappa_{2}}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}(X+Y-B)\right\|_{F}^{2}}_{h^{+}(X,Y)}\right\},

(58)

where

g_{1}\left(X;\kappa_{1}\right)=\sum_{i}^{\min\{m,n\}}\max\left\{\sigma_{i}(X)-\kappa_{1},0\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{2}\left(Y;\kappa_{2}\right)=\sum_{i}^{m}\sum_{j}^{n}\max\left\{|Y_{ij}|-\kappa_{2},0\right\},

and
\left\|X\right\|_{*,\kappa_{1}}
and
\left\|Y\right\|_{1,\kappa_{2}}
are the so-called capped trace norm and the capped
\ell_{1}
-norm, respectively.
Obviously, (
58
) falls into the case of model (
1
) with two separable DC parts, and our UBAMA is applicable to such a problem. According to
[
73
]
, we note that the subdifferential of
g_{1}\left(X;\kappa_{1}\right)
is given by

\partial g_{1}(X;\kappa_{1}):=\left\{U\text{diag}(w)V^{\top}\;|\;w\in W^{*}\right\}

(59)

where
U
and
V
are the left and right singular vectors of
X
, respectively, and

W^{*}=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\;\Big|\;w_{i}\in\begin{cases}\{1\},&\text{if }\sigma_{i}(X)>\kappa_{1},\\
\{0\},&\text{if }\sigma_{i}(X)<\kappa_{1},\\
[0,1],&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\right\}

with
r
being the rank of
X
. Besides, we also have the subdifferential of
g_{2}\left(Y;\kappa_{2}\right)
given by

\partial g_{2}\left(Y;\kappa_{2}\right)=\left\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\;\Big|\;V_{ij}\in\begin{cases}\{1\},&\text{if }Y_{ij}>\kappa_{2},\\
[0,1],&\text{if }Y_{ij}=\kappa_{2},\\
\{0\},&\text{if }|Y_{ij}|<\kappa_{2},\\
[-1,0],&\text{if }Y_{ij}=-\kappa_{2},\\
\{-1\},&\text{if }Y_{ij}<-\kappa_{2}.\end{cases}\quad\right\}.

(60)

Due to the appearance of
\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}
, we accordingly take the kernel functions as
\psi_{k}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{M_{k}}^{2}
and
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}_{N_{k}}
for the Bregman proximal terms
\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}
and
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}
, where
M_{k}
and
N_{k}
are specified as
M_{k}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\mu_{k}I-\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}\right)
and
N_{k}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\nu_{k}I-\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}^{\top}\mathcal{P}_{\Omega}\right)
with appropriate
\mu_{k}>0
and
\nu_{k}>0
, respectively, such that
M_{k}
and
N_{k}
are positive definite. With the above preparations, the algorithmic details of applying our UBAMA to (
58
) are summarized in Algorithm
3
.

5.3
Blind image deconvolution

It is not difficult to observe that the nonconvexity of the above two models (
52
) and (
58
) comes from their DC parts, while their coupling functions are indeed convex. In this subsection, we consider the well-studied yet challenging Blind Image Deconvolution (BID) problem (e.g., see
[
27
,
44
]
), which has a nonconvex coupling function. Concretely, the task of BID is to recover both a sharp image
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(
m=m_{1}\times m_{2}
is the number of pixels of an image) and the unknown point spread function
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(a small 2D blur kernel of size
n=n_{1}\times n_{2}
pixels) from a given blurry and possibly noisy image
b
, i.e., solving the following inverse problem:

b=x\star y+\varepsilon,

(64)

where
\varepsilon
represents the noise and the operation
x\star y
denotes the usual 2D modulo-
m_{1},m_{2}
discrete circular convolution operation defined by (and interpreting the image
x
and the blur kernel
y
as 2D arrays)

(x\star y)_{ij}=\sum_{s=0}^{n_{1}}\sum_{t=0}^{n_{2}}y_{st}x_{(i-s)_{{\text{mod}(m_{1})}},(j-t)_{\text{mod}(m_{2})}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq m_{1},\;1\leq j\leq m_{2},

which can also be expressed as the 2D discrete convolution by the matrix vector products of the form

u=x\star y\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;u=K(y)x\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;u=K(x)y

with
K(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
being a sparse matrix (each row holds the values of the blur kernel
y
) and
K(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
being a dense matrix (each column is given by a circularly shifted version of the image
x
). Generally, the blur kernel is assumed to be normalized, i.e.,
y\in\Delta^{n}
, where
\Delta^{n}
is the standard unit simplex given by (
8
). Moreover, the pixel intensities of the unknown sharp image
x
are assumed to be normalized to the interval
[0,1]
, i.e.,
x\in\mathbb{B}^{m}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\;|\;x_{i}\in[0,1],\;i=1,2\ldots,m\}
.
In what follows, we consider a classical BID model (e.g., see
[
64
,
67
]
), which reads as

\min_{x,y}\;\left\{\sum_{p=1}^{8}\phi\left(\nabla_{p}x;\tau\right)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|x\star y-b\right\|^{2}\;\Big|\;x\in\mathbb{B}^{m},\;y\in\Delta^{n}\right\},

(65)

where the function
\phi(\cdot~;\tau)
is a differentiable robust error function promoting sparsity in its argument, which is defined by

\phi(x;\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\left(1+\tau x_{i}^{2}\right),\quad\text{for}\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\;\;\tau>0,

and the linear operators
\nabla_{p}
(
p=1,2,\ldots,8
) are the finite differences approximation to directional image gradients, which are given in
[
67
]
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lll}(\nabla_{1}x)_{i,j}=x_{i+1,j}-x_{i,j},&\qquad(\nabla_{2}x)_{i,j}=x_{i,j+1}-x_{i,j},&\\
(\nabla_{3}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i+1,j+1}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{2}},&\qquad(\nabla_{4}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i+1,j-1}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{2}},&\quad 1\leq i\leq m_{1},\\
(\nabla_{5}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i+2,j+1}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{5}},&\qquad(\nabla_{6}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i+2,j-1}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{5}},&\quad 1\leq j\leq m_{2}.\\
(\nabla_{7}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i+1,j+2}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{5}},&\qquad(\nabla_{8}x)_{i,j}=\frac{x_{i-1,j+2}-x_{i,j}}{\sqrt{5}},&\end{array}

In our experiments, we assume natural boundary conditions, i.e.,
(\nabla_{p})_{i,j}=0
, whenever the operator references a pixel location that lies outside the domain. However, it has been emphasized in
[
67
]
that model (
65
) performs well for favor sharp images, while it could possibly be a bad choice for textured images.

By invoking the indicator functions associated to
\mathbb{B}^{m}
and
\Delta^{n}
, we can easily reformulate (
65
) as

\min_{x,y}\;\left\{\;\underbrace{\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}^{m}}(x)}_{f_{1}(x)}\;+\;\underbrace{\mathcal{I}_{\Delta^{n}}(y)}_{f_{2}(y)}\;-\;\underbrace{\left(-\sum_{p=1}^{8}\phi\left(\nabla_{p}x;\tau\right)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|x\star y-b\right\|^{2}\right)}_{h^{-}(x,y)}\;\right\},

(66)

which is clearly a special case of model (
1
). Moreover, it has been shown in
[
67
]
that the coupling function
h^{-}(x,y)
defined in (
66
) is smooth with block Lipschitz continuous gradients given by

\begin{cases}\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x,y)=-2\tau\sum_{p=1}^{8}\nabla_{p}^{\top}\text{\bf vec}\left(\frac{(\nabla_{p}x)_{i,j}}{1+\tau(\nabla_{p}x)_{i,j}^{2}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{m_{1},m_{2}}-\lambda K^{\top}(y)\left(K(y)x-b\right),\\
\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x,y)=-\lambda K^{\top}(x)\left(K(x)y-b\right),\end{cases}

where the operation “
\text{\bf vec}(\cdot)
” corresponds to the formation of a vector from the values passed to its argument. In
[
67
]
, we notice that their method requires a solver to compute the projection onto the unit simplex with
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
time complexity. In this section, we shall consider two different Bregman proximal terms for the
y
-subproblem for the purpose of highlighting the flexibility of our algorithmic framework, especially of showing that one of our choices can avoid computing the projection onto the unit simplex. Below, we elaborate the details of applying UBAMA to (
65
) as follows.

•

Firstly, the
x
-subproblem is specified as

x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{B}^{m}}\left\{\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{B}^{m}}(x)-\left\langle x-x^{k},\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\psi_{k}}(x,x^{k})\right\}.

(67)

Considering the simple structure of
\mathbb{B}^{m}
, we take
\psi_{k}(x)=\frac{c_{k}}{2}\|x\|^{2}
in (
67
). Then, the updating scheme of
x
immediately reads as

\displaystyle x^{k+1}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{\bf Proj}}_{\mathbb{B}^{m}}\left(x^{k}+\frac{1}{c_{k}}\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\min\left\{x^{k}+\frac{1}{c_{k}}\nabla_{x}h^{-}(x^{k},y^{k}),1\right\},0\right\}.

(68)

•

Secondly, the
y
-subproblem is specified as

y^{k+1}=\arg\min_{y\in\Delta^{n}}\left\{\mathcal{I}_{\Delta^{n}}(y)-\left\langle y-y^{k},\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right\rangle+\mathscr{B}_{\varphi_{k}}(y,y^{k})\right\}.

(69)

For the above subproblem, we have two options on
\varphi_{k}(\cdot)
.

–

When taking
\varphi_{k}(y)=\frac{d_{k}}{2}\|y\|^{2}
, the iterative scheme (
69
) immediately is specified as

y^{k+1}=\mathrm{\bf Proj}_{\Delta^{n}}\left(y^{k}+\frac{1}{d_{k}}\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})\right),

(70)

which can be solved by the method introduced in
[
36
]
.

–

When taking
\varphi_{k}(y)=d_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\log(y_{i})
, the iterative scheme (
69
) reads as

y^{k+1}=\frac{y^{k}\odot e^{-\varsigma^{k}/d_{k}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}^{k}e^{-\varsigma^{k}_{j}/d_{k}}},

(71)

where
\varsigma^{k}:=\nabla_{y}h^{-}(x^{k+1},y^{k})
and “
\odot
” is the component-wise product of vectors.

Clearly, we can see from (
70
) and (
71
) that the latter has an explicit form, which is simpler than the former. So, (
71
) can save some computational cost to accelerate our algorithm.

Now, we turn our attention to the numerical behaviors of the UBAMA equipped with different Bregman kernel functions. Here, we consider two blurry images
Peppers
(
384\times 512
) and
Books
(
512\times 340
), which are convoluted by blur kernels of size
27\times 13
and
31\times 31
, respectively. Empirically, we choose the model parameters as
\lambda=5\times 10^{5}
and
\tau=10^{4}
for this experiment. The UBAMA starts from
x^{0}
and
y^{0}
, which are initialized to be the blurry image
b
and the average filters of size
n
, respectively. We set the maximal iterate number as
2000
and the algorithmic parameters
c_{k}
and
d_{k}
are determined by the backtracking line search. In Figure
6
, we present the original images, blurred images, and the recovered images by our UBAMA equipped with two different Bregman kernel functions. It is easy to see that both UBAMA variants could recover blur kernels and piecewise smooth images. Note that the BID problem is usually extremely ill-posed and highly nonconvex. In this situation, the accuracy of solving subproblems and the proximal parameters possibly affects the numerical behaviors of the UBAMA significantly. As an illustration, we see from Figure

that although the UBAMA equipped with two (

) achieves almost the same objective values, (

) and (

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we considered a class of structured nonconvex optimization problem, which is called generalized DC programming. The main computational challenges are the nonsmoothness, nonconvexity, and nonseparability appeared in the objective function. Designing an algorithm to circumvent the above difficulty is very important to efficiently solving the problem under consideration. So, we proposed a Unified Bregman Alternating Minimization Algorithm (UBAMA), which combines the novel spirits of DCA, alternating minimization, and Bregman regularization. Our UBAMA provides a flexible way to design customized algorithms for some real-world problems so that they often enjoy easy subproblems, which also is supported by some applications in image processing. Moreover, a series of numerical experiments demonstrated that our UBAMA performs well on solving generalized DC programming. In recent years, the inertial technique is widely used to accelerate many first-order optimization methods. So, we will consider some acceleration on our UBAMA by using the inertial technique in future. On the other hand, although our UBAMA enjoys easy subproblems for many sparse and low-rank optimization problems, there are some cases that we need to call solvers for finding solutions of subproblems. Therefore, designing inexact variants of UBAMA is also one of our future concerns. Finally, the stability of nonconvex optimization methods is very important for applications. So, we will also pay our attention on the stability of UBAMA in future.
