Needless to say, the second-order partial differential equations equations with degenerate or unbounded coefficients have been extensively studied for a long time.

With the degeneracy in the equation, it is hard to expect to obtain full regularity estimates of solutions unless there are weights involved in estimates. For instance, by taking the leading coefficients
a^{ij}(t)=0
for all
i,j,t
, we see that it is not possible to obtain the unweighted

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{xx}(t,x)|^{p}dtdx\leq N\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}dtdx.

(1.1)

Hence, weights have been commonly used to controls the degeneracy or unboundeness (singularity) of the coefficients.
However, most results in the literature focus on degeneracy or singularity near the boundary of a domain.
If we consider the whole space, it is naturally not expected that there is a regularity gain of a solution in general due to the extreme case such as

u_{t}=f
, which could be understood as one of equation (
0.1
) with coefficients
a^{ij}(t)=0
for all

Hence when it comes to the solvability of second-order equations with degeneracy in the whole space, people used to only prove the existence and uniqueness of a weak solution without considering regularity gain from the equations.

Nonetheless, there is a way to express an
L_{p}
-norm of second derivatives of a solution
u
with a weight which could be singular even in the whole space.
For instance, assume that the degeneracy happens on a time interval
(a,b)
, then
\delta(t)=0
for all
t\in(a,b)
.
Then we cannot expect the smoothing gain from the diffusion equations and the Sobolev second derivatives
u_{xx}
fails to exist.
However, since there is the weight
\delta(t)
in the first line of (
0.2

0.2
) as an improper integral. To the best of our knowledge, this type estimate is firstly introduced by the author and collaborator in
[
24
,
25
]

L_{p}
-estimates to
L_{q}(L_{p})
-estimates with lower-order terms.

It is well-known that probabilistic methods are very powerfully working for leading coefficients which are unbounded and have degeneracy (cf.
[
29

4

We remark that probabilistic tools play very important roles to obtain our results.
Especially, to obtain (
0.2

N
, the degeneracy, and the unboundedness of coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
.
Maximal
L_{p}
-regularity estimates such as (
1.1

L_{p}
-boundedness of singular integral operators.
However, the exact relation among parameters related to coefficients is hard to obtain from singular integral theories since all parameters are combined in a complicated way to control singularities of operators.
We found that this relation could be more clear by applying probabilistic representations of solutions (see Theorem
4.3

We believe that our result could initiate various interesting weighted estimates for degenerate second-order equations with space dependent coefficients
or domain problems.

2.
Setting and main result

Throughout the paper, we fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
to denote the dimension of the space variable and all functions are real-valued if there is no special comment.
We study the following degenerate second-order evolution equation

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)u_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)u(t,x)+f(t,x),

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.1)

We emphasize that our coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
do not satisfy any regularity conditions.
More importantly, our coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
can be unbounded and degenerate.
Here are more concrete conditions on the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
.

Assumption 2.1
.

(i)

Assume that there exists a measurable mapping
\delta(t)
from
(0,\infty)
to
[0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq\delta(t)|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall t\in[0,\infty)~\text{and}~\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(ii)

Assume that the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
,
b^{i}(t)
, and
c(t)
are locally integrable, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(|a^{ij}(t)|+|b^{i}(t)|+|c(t)|\right)dt<\infty\qquad\forall T\in(0,\infty)~\text{and}~\forall i,j.

(2.2)

For
T\in(0,\infty)
and a measurable function
u
on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that
u
is locally integrable if

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|<c}|u(t,x)|dxdt<\infty\quad\forall t\in(0,T)~\text{and}~\forall c>0.

Definition 2.2

(Solution)
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
and
f
be a locally integrable function on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that a locally integrable function
u
is a solution to (
2.1
) if for any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

\displaystyle(u(t,\cdot),\varphi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}x^{j}}+b^{i}(s)\varphi_{x^{i}}+c(s)\varphi\right)ds

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left(f(s,\cdot),\varphi\right)ds\quad\forall t\in(0,T),

(2.3)

where
(u(t,\cdot),\varphi)
denotes the
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-inner product, i.e.

(u(t,\cdot),\varphi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,x)\varphi(x)dx.

Remark 2.3
.

Due to the definition of a solution, it is obvious that

a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}=\frac{a^{ij}(t)+a^{ji}(t)}{2}u_{x^{i}x^{j}}.

Thus without loss of generality, we may assume that our coefficient matrix
(a^{ij}(t))_{d\times d}
is nonnegative symmetric for all
t
.
Additionally,
\delta(t)
in Assumption
2.1
(i) can be chosen by the smallest eigenvalue of
(a^{ij}(t))_{d\times d}
.

We recall the definition of Muckenhoupt’s weights.

Definition 2.4

(Muckenhoupt’s weight)
.

For
q\in(1,\infty)
, let
A_{q}(\mathbb{R})
be the class of all nonnegative and locally integrable functions
w
on
\mathbb{R}
satisfying

[w]_{A_{q}(\mathbb{R})}:=\sup_{-\infty<a<b<\infty}\left(-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{(a,b)}w(t)dt\right)\left(-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{(a,b)}w(t)^{-1/(q-1)}dt\right)^{q-1}<\infty,

where

-\hskip-10.81218pt\int_{(a,b)}w(t)dt=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}w(t)dt}{b-a}.

Finally, we introduce our main result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
,
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, and

.
Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.

) such that

\displaystyle\leq\left[\int_{0}^{\alpha(T)}w(t)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}dt\right]^{q-1}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(t))|\delta(t)|^{1-q}dt,

\displaystyle\leq[w]_{A_{q}(\mathbb{R})}[\alpha(T)]^{q}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(t))|\delta(t)|^{1-q}dt,

and

and

\displaystyle\leq\left[\frac{q-1}{q-1-\beta}\right]^{q-1}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\delta(t)dt\right]^{q-1-\beta}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\delta(s)ds\right|^{\beta}(\delta(t))^{1-q}dt,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\leq[|t|^{\beta}]_{A_{p}(\mathbb{R})}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\delta(t)dt\right]^{q}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\delta(s)ds\right|^{\beta}(\delta(t))^{1-q}dt,

(2.8)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|u_{xx}\left(t,x\right)(t,x)\right|^{p}\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\delta(s)ds\right|^{\beta}\delta(t)dt

Remark 2.6
.

(i)

For
t\in[0,T]
so that
\delta(t)=0
, the existence of the Sobolev derivatives

is not guaranteed by (
2.6
).
Moreover,
\delta(t)
can be zero on a set with a positive Lebesgue measure, which is far from Muckenhoupt’s weight.

(ii)

Since
\delta(t)
can be zero on a set with a positive measure, the integral

\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(t))(\delta(t))^{1-q}dt

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(t))(\delta(t))^{1-q}dt

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(t)+\varepsilon t)(\delta(t)+\varepsilon)^{1-q}dt.

(2.10)

(iii)

If

\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(s,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}w(\alpha(s))|\delta(s)|^{1-q}ds<\infty,

then the local integrability condition on

is not necessary in Theorem
2.5
.
In other words, the finiteness condition implies the local integrability of
f
.
To investigate this fact, let
t\in(0,T)
and

.
Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, applying Hölder’s inequality and the change of variable
\alpha(t)+\varepsilon t\to t
,
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{|x|<c}|f(t,x)|dxds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|1_{|x|<c}dx1_{0<s<t}ds

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{1/p}1_{0<s<t}ds

\displaystyle\leq N\left[\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(s,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}e^{-q\int_{0}^{s}c(\rho)d\rho}w(\alpha(s)+\varepsilon s)|\delta(s)+\varepsilon|^{1-q}ds\right]^{1/q}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{q}{q-1}\int_{0}^{s}c(\rho)d\rho}w^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}(\alpha(s)+\varepsilon s)(\delta(s)+\varepsilon)ds\right]^{(q-1)/q}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\quad\times e^{\frac{q}{q-1}\int_{0}^{t}|c(s)|ds}\left[\int_{0}^{\alpha(t)+t}w^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}(s)ds\right]^{(q-1)/q}.

It is obvious that

since the function

is locally integrable.
Moreover, since

Therefore, taking

.
Then we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|u_{xx}\left(t,x\right)(t,x)\right|^{p}w_{0}(x)dx\right)^{q/p}w(\alpha(t))\delta(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{p}w_{0}(x)dx\right)^{q/p}w(\alpha(t))|\delta(t)|^{1-q}dt.

(2.13)

Then the function
v(t,x)=u\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)
becomes a solution to

\displaystyle v_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)v_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)v_{x^{i}}(t,x)+f\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right),

\displaystyle v(0,x)=0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thus by (
2.13
) with
p=q=2
and
\delta(t)=1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|v_{xx}\left(t,x\right)(t,x)\right|^{2}w_{0}\left(x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)w(t)dxdt

\displaystyle\leq N\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|f\left(t,x\right)\right|^{2}w_{0}\left(x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)w(t)dxdt.

Observe that

(t,x)\mapsto w(t)w_{0}\left(x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)\notin A_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})

unless
\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds
is a constant vector uniformly for all
t
since
w
has a singularity or a degeneracy in general.
Therefore we cannot expect (
2.12
) if there is a non-trivial coefficient
b(t)
in the equation.
Moreover, our main tool is the probabilistic solution representation such as (
3.2
).
We use the translation invariant property of
L_{p}
-norms with this representation in many parts of proofs of the main theorem.
Thus (
2.12
) is impossible to obtain by our method even for the case
b(t)=0
for all
t
(see Remark
4.4
).

(vi)

All constants in estimates in Theorem
2.5
do not depend on the integrals of coefficients
a^{ij}
,
b^{i}
, and

.
Thus for a fixed time
T\in(0,\infty)
, the integrability condition on the coefficients (
2.2
) can be relaxed to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(|a^{ij}(t)|+|b^{i}(t)|+|c(t)|\right)dt<\infty\qquad\forall t\in(0,T)~\text{and}~\forall i,j.

3.
Probabilistic solution representations

In this section, we consider equations without lower-order terms first, i.e.

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+f(t,x)\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0.

(3.1)

Consider a Brownian motion
B_{t}
in a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{t},\mathbb{P})
with the usual condition.
It is well-known that any predictable function
\sigma(t):\Omega\times(0,T)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma(s)|^{2}ds<\infty\quad(a.s.)\quad\forall t\in[0,t],

the Itô integral

X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dB_{s}

is well-defined and Itô’s formula works for the stochastic process
f(X_{t})
with a smooth function
f
(cf.
[
30
, Chapter 5]
).
Moreover, our solution
u
to equation (
3.1
) can be derived from the expectation of a composition of a function
f
and the stochastic process
X_{t}
. Here is a more explicit statement.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
and
f
be a locally integrable function on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Assume that the function
t\in(0,\infty)\mapsto A(t):=\left(a^{ij}(t)\right)_{d\times d}
is locally integrable, i.e. for each
i
and
j
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}a^{ij}(t)dt<\infty

and the coefficients
\left(a^{ij}(t)\right)_{d\times d}
are nonnegative, i.e.

a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq 0\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~\text{and}~\forall t\in(0,T].

Then there exists a unique solution
u
to (
3.1
) and this solution
u
is given by

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s})\right]ds,

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle X_{t}:=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{A}^{ij}(s)dB_{s}^{j},

and
B_{t}=(B_{t}^{1},\ldots,B_{t}^{d})
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion (Wiener process) and the integral is Itô’s stochastic integral.
Moreover, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, we have

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}ds\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

(3.3)

and for any functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
on
[0,T]
which are positive (a.e.), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{1}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left[h_{1}(t)\left[\int_{0}^{t}|h_{2}(s)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}ds\right]^{q-1}\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(s)ds\right]dt.

(3.4)

Proof.

Part I.
(Uniqueness)

Even though the coefficients can be unbounded or degenerate,
the uniqueness of a solution can be easily obtained from a classical Fourier transform method with Grönwall’s inequality.
To give a rigorous detail, choose a
\varphi
which is a nonnegative function in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a unit integral.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, denote

\varphi^{\varepsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d}}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right).

Let
u
be a solution to

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+f(t,x)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0

and define

u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(t,y)\varphi^{\varepsilon}(x-y)dy.

It is sufficient to show that for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t\in(0,T)
,
u(t,x)=0
for almost every
x\ in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
t\in(0,T)
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Recalling the definition of a solution and putting
\varphi^{\varepsilon}(x-\cdot)
in (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(s)\left(u^{\varepsilon}\right)_{x^{i}x^{j}}(s,x)ds.

(3.5)

For each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
t\in(0,T)
, (
3.5
) holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Take the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform with respect to
x
in (
3.5
) and absolute value. Then we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{F}[u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)](\xi)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|a^{ij}(s)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\right|\left|\left[\mathcal{F}[u^{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot)\right](\xi)\right|ds

(3.6)

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Note that for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, (
3.6
) holds for all
t\in(0,T)
. Thus finally applying Grönwall’s inequality, we have

|\mathcal{F}[u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)](\xi)|=0

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
t\in(0,T)
, and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which completes the uniqueness of a solution
u
.

Part II.
(Existence)

The existence of a solution
u
cannot be shown based on a classical Fourier transform method since the coefficients can be degenerate.
Thus we choose a probabilistic method to show the existence of a solution.
Since it is a well-known fact if the inhomogeneous term
f
is smooth (even for more general
f
in a
L_{p}
-class).
However, it is not easy to find an appropriate reference which exactly fit to our setting (cf.
[
25
, Section 3]
), we give a proof with a detail.
Our main tools are Itô’s formula and a smooth approximation.
We divide the proof into three steps.

Step 1.
(Smooth case) In this step, we assume that for each
t\in(0,T)
,
f(t,x)
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
x
.

Recall that for each
t
,
\left(a^{ij}(t)\right)_{d\times d}
is a nonnegative symmetric matrix.
Then there exists a
d\times d
matrix
\sqrt{A}(t)
such that

A(t)=\sqrt{A}(t)\times\sqrt{A}^{\ast}(t),

where
\sqrt{A}^{\ast}
denotes the transpose matrix of
\sqrt{A}
.
Recall

X_{t}=\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{A}(s)dB_{s}.

We claim that the function

\displaystyle u(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s})\right]ds

(3.7)

becomes a solution to (
2.1
).
As mentioned before, it is not easy to show that
u
defined in (
3.2
) becomes a solution to (
2.1
) based on an analytic method such as the Fourier transform since the degeneracy of
a^{ij}(t)
makes the Fourier transform of
u
lose the integrability. However, it is still possible to apply Itô’s formula. Fix
s\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Apply Itô’s formula to

f\left(s,x+\sqrt{2}\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{A}(r)dB_{r}\right).

Then we have

\displaystyle f\left(s,x+\sqrt{2}\int_{s}^{t}\sqrt{A}(r)dB_{r}\right)

\displaystyle=f(s,x)+\int_{s}^{t}f_{x^{i}}\left(s,\int_{s}^{\rho}\sqrt{A}(r)dB_{r}\right)dB_{\rho}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(\rho)f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+\sqrt{2}\int_{s}^{\rho}\sqrt{A}(r)dB_{r}\right)d\rho

(3.8)

for all
s\leq t<T

(a.s)
.
Taking the expectations in (
3.8
), using the property of the Itô integral that

\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}f_{x^{i}}\left(s,\int_{s}^{\rho}\sqrt{A}(r)dB_{r}\right)dB_{\rho}\right]=0,

and recalling the definition of
X_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=f(s,x)+\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(\rho)f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{\rho}-X_{s}\right)d\rho\right]

(3.9)

for all
0<s\leq t<T
.
Taking the integration
\int_{0}^{t}\cdot~ds
to both sides of (
3.9
) and applying the Fubini Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s}\right)\right]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}a^{ij}(\rho)f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{\rho}-X_{s}\right)d\rho\right]ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x)ds+\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(\rho)\int_{0}^{\rho}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(s,x+X_{\rho}-X_{s}\right)\right]dsd\rho.

Finally due to the definition of
u
in (
3.7
), we have

\displaystyle u_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+f(t,x)

for all
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Step 2.
(Bounded case) In this step, we assume that
f
is bounded.

We use Sobolev’s mollifiers.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, denote

f^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x-\varepsilon y)\varphi(y)dy,

and

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{\varepsilon}(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s})\right]ds

for all
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then by the result in Step 1, we have

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)u^{\varepsilon}_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+f^{\varepsilon}(t,x).

In particular, applying the integration by parts, for any
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle(u^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot),\phi)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u^{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\phi_{x^{i}x^{j}}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left(f^{\varepsilon}(s,\cdot),\phi\right)ds\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

Since
f
is bounded, applying the dominate convergence theorem one can easily check that

u(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s})\right]ds=\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)~(a.e.)

and it becomes a solution to (
3.1
).

Step 3.
(General case)

It suffices to remove the condition that
f
is bounded.
Due to the linearity of equation (
3.1
) and the trivial decomposition
f(t,x)=f^{+}(t,x)-f^{-}(t,x)
,
we may assume that
f
is nonnegative, where
f^{+}(t,x)=\frac{|f(t,x)|+f(t,x)}{2}
and
f^{-}(t,x)=\frac{|f(t,x)|-f(t,x)}{2}
.
For
M>0
, define
f^{M}(t,x):=f(t,x)\wedge M:=\min\{f(t,x),M\}
and denote

\displaystyle u^{M}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{M}(s,x+X_{t}-X_{s})\right]ds.

Then by the result of step 2, for any
M>0
, we have

\displaystyle(u^{M}(t,\cdot),\phi)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(u^{M}(s,\cdot),a^{ij}(s)\phi_{x^{i}x^{j}}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left(f^{M}(s,\cdot),\phi\right)ds\quad\forall t\in(0,T).

(3.10)

It is obvious that
u^{M}(t,x)\to u(t,x)
for all
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
M\to\infty
.
Finally, taking
M\to\infty
and applying the monotone and dominate convergence theorems in (
3.10
), we show that
u
is a solution to (
3.1
).

Part III.
(Estimate)

We prove (
3.3
) and (
3.4
).
By (
3.2
), the generalized Minkowski inequality, and the translation invariant property of the
L_{p}
-space,

\displaystyle\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}ds.

Moreover, applying Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{1}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}h_{1}(t)\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(s)ds\left[\int_{0}^{t}|h_{2}(s)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}ds\right]^{q-1}dt.

\Box

Remark 3.2
.

Assume that

\int_{0}^{T}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}dt<\infty.

Then due to (
3.3
) and the linearity of (
3.1
), one can easily find a continuous modification of
u
so that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|f(s,\cdot)\|_{L_{p}}ds\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, and
q\in(1,\infty)
.
Suppose that Assumption
2.1
holds.
Additionally, assume that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are functions on
[0,T]
which are positive (a.e.).
Then for any locally integrable function
f
on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is a unique solution
u
to equation (
2.1
) such that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left[\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left[\int_{0}^{T}|h_{2}(t)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}dt\right]^{q-1}\int_{0}^{T}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(t)dt.

(3.11)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}h_{1}(t)dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\left[h_{1}(t)\left[\int_{0}^{t}|h_{2}(s)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}ds\right]^{q-1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-q\int_{0}^{s}c(\rho)d\rho}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(s)ds\right]dt.

(3.12)

Proof.

Let
v
be a solution to the equation

\displaystyle v_{t}(t,x)=a^{ij}(t)v_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}f\left(t,x-\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right),

\displaystyle v(0,x)=0,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Define
U(t,x)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}v\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)
, where
b(t)=(b^{1}(t),\ldots,b^{d}(t))
.
Then

\displaystyle U_{t}(t,x)

\displaystyle=c(t)U(t,x)+e^{\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left(v_{t}\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)+b^{i}(t)v_{x^{i}}\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c(t)U(t,x)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left(a^{ij}(t)v_{x^{i}x^{j}}\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)+e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}f(t,x)\right)

\displaystyle\quad+e^{\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\left(b^{i}(t)v_{x^{i}}\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)\right)

\displaystyle=a^{ij}(t)U_{x^{i}x^{j}}(t,x)+b^{i}(t)U_{x^{i}}(t,x)+c(t)U(t,x)+f(t,x)

and

\displaystyle U(0,x)=0.

Thus by the uniqueness of a solution, the solution
u
to (
2.1
) is given by

u(t,x)=e^{\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}v\left(t,x+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right)

and obviously

v(t,x)=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}u\left(t,x-\int_{0}^{t}b(s)ds\right).

Applying (
3.4
) to
v
and using the translation invariant property of

-norms, we obtain (
3.12
).
Moreover, by (
3.3
) and Hölder’s inequality, for any
0\leq t\leq T
, we have

\displaystyle e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\|u(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle=\|v(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-q\int_{0}^{s}c(\rho)d\rho}\|f(s,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(s)ds\left[\int_{0}^{t}|h_{2}(s)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}ds\right]^{q-1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}e^{-q\int_{0}^{t}c(s)ds}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{q}_{L_{p}}h_{2}(t)dt\left[\int_{0}^{T}|h_{2}(s)|^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}ds\right]^{q-1},

which obviously implies (

4.
Estimates for non-degenerate equations

We start the section by reviewing previous weighted estimates with uniform elliptic and bounded coefficients and apply these estimates to our model equation (
3.1
).
We denote

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{p,q}(T,w)}=\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\right)^{1/q}.

As usual,

L_{p,q}(T,w)
denote the spaces of all locally integrable functions
f
on
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\|f\|_{L_{p,q}(T,w)}<\infty
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
,
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, and
w\in A_{q}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
are uniformly bounded and elliptic, i.e. there exist positive constants
M
and
\delta
such that

\displaystyle M|\xi|^{2}\geq a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq\delta|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(4.1)

Then for any
f\in L_{p,q}(T,w)
, there exists a unique solution
u
to (
3.1
)
such that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{xx}(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq N\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\right)^{1/q},

(4.2)

where

N=N\left(p,q,M,\delta,[w]_{A_{q}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

Proof.

It is a well-known result which could be easily obtained by combining some classical results.
However, it is not easy to find a paper covering the result directly.
Thus we refer two recent papers
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
handling more general coefficients and
[
3
, Theorem 2.14]
studying time measurable pseudo-differential operators.

\Box

Remark 4.2
.

Theorem
4.1
is enough for our application.
However, as shown in
[
5
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
3
, Theorem 2.14]
,
w_{0}(x)\in A_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be inside (
4.2
) if (
4.1
) holds.
In other words, we can find a positive constant
N
such that
such that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{xx}(t,x)|^{p}w_{0}(x)dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq N\left(\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}w_{0}(x)dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\right)^{1/q},

where

N=N\left(p,q,M,\delta,[w]_{A_{q}(\mathbb{R})},[w_{0}]_{A_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right).

Next we want to enhance Theorem
4.1
.
Specifically, we show the constant
N
in (
4.2
) is independent of the upper bound
M
of the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
and more precise relation between the constant
N
and the elliptic constant
\delta
.
However, it seems to be almost impossible to prove it with only analytic tools.
Thus we recall probabilistic representations of solutions to upgrade Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
,
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, and
w\in A_{q}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
are uniformly elliptic, i.e. there exists a positive constant
\delta
such that

\displaystyle a^{ij}(t)\xi^{i}\xi^{j}\geq\delta|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(4.3)

Additionally, we assume that the coefficients
a^{ij}(t)
are locally integrable, i.e.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}a^{ij}(s)ds<\infty\qquad\forall t\in(0,T).

Then for any
f\in L_{p,q}(T,w)
, there exists a unique solution
u
to (
3.1
)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{xx}(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt\leq\frac{N}{\delta^{q}}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(t,x)|^{p}dx\right)^{q/p}w(t)dt,

(4.4)

where

N=N\left(p,q,[w]_{A_{q}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

Proof.

(Step 1)
a^{ij}(t)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}=\delta\Delta u
.

For this simple case, we use a basic scaling property of the equation.
Put
