On the Evolution of States in a Quantum-Mechanical Model of Experiments
Jürg Fröhlich, Zhou Gang

Abstract
The postulates of von Neumann and Lüders concerning measurements in quantum mechanics are discussed and criticized in the context of a simple model proposed in [ 1 ] . The main purpose of our paper is to analyze some mathematical aspects of that model and to draw some general lessons on the so-called “measurement problem” in quantum mechanics pointing towards the need to introduce general principles that determine the law for the stochastic time evolution of states of individual physical systems.

Introduction: A concrete example in the quantum theory of experiments

In this paper the theoretical basis of the postulate of wave-function collapse and of Born’s rule
used in descriptions of measurements, following the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics,
is reconsidered. We begin by sketching the following interesting example of an indirect (“non-demolition”)
measurement of a physical quantity in cavity quantum electrodynamics, which, besides clarifying the basis
of those postulates, also provides an illustration of the “non-locality” of quantum mechanics.

A little more than fifteen years ago, a group of physicists around S. Haroche

A simple model unifying the von Neumann- and Lüders postulates in the quantum theory
of experiments and measurements

Consider a physical system,
S
, whose states are described by density matrices,
\rho
, (i.e., positive trace-class
operators of trace one) acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{N}
is a self-adjoint operator
acting on
\mathcal{H}
that represents a physical quantity characteristic of

. (In the example discussed in Section 1,

\mathcal{N}
is the photon number operator counting how many photons of frequency
\Omega
are stored
in the cavity.) For simplicity, we henceforth assume that
\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{m}
is finite-dimensional
and bestowed with the standard Euclidean scalar product
\langle\cdot,\ \cdot\rangle
and the norm
\|\cdot\|
induced
by it. The spectrum of the “observable”
\mathcal{N}
is then necessarily discrete. Let

\mathcal{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\nu_{n}P_{n},\qquad N+1\leq m<\infty,

(7)

be the spectral decomposition of
\mathcal{N}
,
\nu_{0},\nu_{1}\dots,\nu_{N}
being its eigenvalues and

P_{0},P_{1},\dots,P_{N}
the corresponding eigenprojections. (In the example of Section 1,
\nu_{n}=n
is
the number of photons in any state of
S
that belongs to the range of the spectral projection
P_{n}
.)
Clearly

\displaystyle P_{l}P_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{l}&\text{if}\ k=l,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

and

\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}=\mathbf{1}\,.

(10)

Von Neumann’s Postulate
:
We consider an
ensemble
of systems all isomorphic to
S
,
and we propose to describe the statistics of measurements of the “observable”
\mathcal{N}
on all the systems
in the ensemble. Let
\rho_{in}
be the density matrix describing the ensemble average of the states of all the
systems belonging to the ensemble right before a measurement of
\mathcal{N}
is made. In his book on
the foundations of quantum mechanics
[
7
]
, von Neumann postulated that, after averaging over the
ensemble of identical systems, the effect of measuring
\mathcal{N}
on all the systems in
the ensemble amounts to replacing the state
\rho_{in}
by the state
\rho_{out}
given by

\rho_{out}:=\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}\,\rho_{in}\,P_{n}\,,

(11)

and
Born’s rule
holds, namely

p_{n}[\rho_{in}]:=\text{Tr}\big(\rho_{in}\cdot P_{n}\big)

(12)

is the probability of finding the value
\nu_{n}
in measurements of the observable
\mathcal{N}

for all the systems in the ensemble, given the initial state
\rho_{in}
, for every
n=0,1,\dots,N.

A more appropriate formulation of von Neumann’s postulate also applying to measurements that
are
not
instantaneous but take some extended amount of time is as follows: The measurement
of
\mathcal{N}
has the effect to replace the density matrix
\rho_{in}
describing the ensemble
average of states of
S
when measurements of
\mathcal{N}
start by a density matrix

\rho_{out}
, describing the ensemble average of states of
S
right
after
these measurements
of
\mathcal{N}
have been completed, which has the following properties.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{in}\mapsto\rho_{out},&\quad\text{where }\,\,\big[\rho_{out},P_{n}\big]=0,\,\,\text{ and }\\
\text{Tr}(\rho_{in}\cdot P_{n})&=\text{Tr}(\rho_{out}\cdot P_{n}),\,\,\forall\,n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}\,,\end{split}

(13)

in particular, using (
13
) and
\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}=\mathbf{1}
, one has that

\rho_{out}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}\,\rho_{out}\,P_{n}.

Lüders’ postulate
:
Going beyond von Neumann, G. Lüders postulated
in
[
8
]
that, for an
individual system
isomorphic to
S
prepared in a pure state
given by a unit vector
\psi_{in}\in\mathcal{H}
just before the “observable”
\mathcal{N}

is measured, the state of the system right
after
the measurement of
\mathcal{N}
is given by
one of the states

\psi_{out}:=\frac{P_{n}\psi_{in}}{\|P_{n}\psi_{in}\|}\,,\quad\text{for some }\,\,n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}\,.

(14)

When considering experiments that take an extended amount of time to be carried out,
such as a Stern-Gerlach measurement of the spin of a silver atom moving through a magnetic field,
Lüders’ postulate clearly does
not
hold in the form of Eq. (
14
). It must be amended
as follows: In a measurement of the physical quantity
\mathcal{N}
the state
\psi_{in}
of the system
just before the meaurement of
\mathcal{N}
begins is mapped to some state
\psi_{out}
in the range
of
one
of the spectral projections
P_{n}
, for some
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\},
immediately after the end
of the measurement; i.e.,

\psi_{in}\mapsto\psi_{out}\in\text{Ran}(P_{n}),\quad\text{for some }\,\,n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}\,,

(15)

where Ran
(P)
is the range (or image) of a projection
P
. If, after the measurement of
\mathcal{N}
,
the state of the system belongs to the range of the spectral projection
P_{n}
, with

\text{dim}(\text{Ran}P_{n})>1
, for some
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\},
then it is usually
not
possible
to say in exactly
which
state
\psi_{out}\in\text{Ran}(P_{n})
the system will be found at the
end of the measurement of
\mathcal{N}
.
Text-book quantum mechanics – if it were clear about this point – only claims that, at the end of the
experiment measuring
\mathcal{N}
, the system is found in
some
state
\psi_{out}
belonging
to
some
subspace
\text{Ran}P_{n}
; but it does
not
predict
which

n
will be
observed. However, quantum mechanics claims to predict the
probability,

p_{n}[\psi_{in}]
,
of finding the system in a state belonging to the range of the spectral projection
P_{n}
when the
measurement of
\mathcal{N}
is repeated many times, for any
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
. This probability
is given by
Born’s rule
, namely by

p_{n}[\psi_{in}]=\langle\psi_{in},P_{n}\,\psi_{in}\rangle\,,\quad n=0,1,\dots,N\,.

(16)

Thus, according to Lüders, the evolution of the state of an
individual
physical system is described
by a
non-linear stochastic process
whenever experiments/measurements are carried out on the
system. Alas, Lüders’ postulate clearly neither predicts
which
experiments will ever be made, nor does
it predict the
times
when the experiments succeed. Of course, this cannot be accepted as the true story!
For, Lüders’ postulate presupposes that there is an “observer” who decides which experiments will be made and at
which times they will begin, rather than providing an “observer”-independent, intrinsically quantum-mechanical
description of
events,
including experiments and measurements, and a precise law governing the
stochastic evolution of states.
In this paper we describe a simple model first proposed in
[
1
]
that is designed to overcome these
shortcomings in very special situations. The model
does
provide a precise law for the stochastic evolution
of states, unifying von Neumann’s and Lüders’ postulates. However, it is really quite ad hoc. In order to justify
its use one would have to derive it from a fundamental approach towards describing events and, in particular,
measurements in quantum mechanics, or, more precisely, from a
general law
for the stochastic evolution
of states, such as the one proposed in
[
6
]
. But the model considered in the following
does

yield quite a satisfactory description of measurements of the photon number in a cavity considered in Section 1,
placing the Heisenberg cut at the system consisting only of the electromagnetic field stored in the cavity and,
hence, not describing the probes explicitly.

In von Neumann’s formalism, states in quantum mechanics only serve to predict
ensemble averages

of the behavior of identical physical systems, rather than the behavior of individual systems. For this
reason, von Neumann’s states are typically
not
pure states but density matrices. In the
Schrödinger picture, these density matrices evolve in time according to a
deterministic, linear
equation
, the Liouville-von Neumann equation (for closed systems) or, e.g., a Lindblad equation
[
9
]

(for open systems). This state of affairs has an analogy in diffusion theory. The diffusion equation

\frac{\partial\rho_{t}(x)}{\partial t}=D\big(\Delta\rho_{t}\big)(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{E}^{3},\quad D=\text{ diffusion constant},

is a determinstic, linear evolution equation for the probability density,
\rho_{t}
, of finding a diffusing particle
at a certain position in physical space
\mathbb{E}^{3}
, at a given time
t
, when an ensemble average is taken.
However, we have learned from Einstein, Smoluchovsky and Wiener that the diffusion equation can be
“unravelled” in the form of a stochastic evolution of the position of a particle given by
Brownian motion.

In classical physics, a point particle has a
precise position
in space, at every time, and this fact is correctly
accounted for in the theory of Brownian motion. However, this position is not predictable from initial conditions.
What
is
predictable concerns various qualitative properties of the trajectory the particle follows,
3
3

3

for
example, that the trajectory is Hölder continuous of index 1/2
as well as
the probabilities of finding the particle in certain subsets of physical space at various times, given its initial position.
The description of a particle exhibiting diffusive motion in terms of Brownian motion is analogous to a putative
quantum-mechanical description of a physical micro-system in terms of a stochastic non-linear evolution
of the pure states it occupies at different times. Lüders’ postulate has drawn attention to the desirability
of such a description, but without actually providing one. The model discussed in this paper (see
[
1
]

for the original proposal)
does
provide such a description, albeit one that looks, unfortunately, quite ad hoc.
Its merit is that it describes the experiment sketched in Section 1 quite accurately without relying on a study
of the quantum mechanics of the probes.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{t}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\Big\{-i\varepsilon_{n}[P_{n},\rho_{t}]-\frac{1}{2}(P_{n}\rho_{t}+\rho_{t}P_{n})+P_{n}\rho_{t}P_{n}\Big\},\quad\rho_{t=0}=\rho_{0},

\displaystyle\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle=1\,,

(24)

for almost every
\omega
and all times
t
.

Before stating further conservation laws we derive stochastic differential equations for the functions

p_{n},n=0,1,\dots,N,
which have been defined in (
19
). These equations are given by

\displaystyle dp_{n}(t,\omega)=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big(p_{k}(t,\omega)p_{n}(t,\omega)-p_{k,n}(t,\omega)\Big)dB_{t,k}(\omega),

(25)

where
p_{k,n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{k,n}(t,\omega):=&\frac{\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),P_{k}P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle}{\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{n}(t,\omega)&\ \text{if}\ k=n\\
0&\ \text{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(26)

It is not hard to derive (
25
). One verifies that (
21
) and (
23
)
imply the equation (we use abbreviated notation)

\displaystyle dp_{n}=\frac{D_{1}+D_{2}+D_{3}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle},

(27)

where
D_{1}
,
D_{2}
and
D_{3}
are defined as

\displaystyle D_{1}:=

\displaystyle 2Re\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big\langle\Big(-i\varepsilon_{k}-\frac{1}{2}+p_{k}\Big)P_{k}\psi-\frac{1}{2}p^{2}_{k}\psi,P_{n}\psi\Big\rangle\ dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\,\Big(-1+2p_{n}-\sum_{k=0}^{N}p_{k}^{2}\Big)\Big\langle\psi,P_{n}\psi\Big\rangle\ dt,

\displaystyle D_{2}:=

\displaystyle 2Re\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big\langle(p_{k}-P_{k})\psi\ dB_{t,k}(\omega),P_{n}\psi\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big(p_{k}\Big\langle\psi,P_{n}\psi\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\psi,P_{n}P_{k}\psi\Big\rangle\Big)dB_{t,k}(\omega),

\displaystyle D_{3}:=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big\langle(p_{k}-P_{k})\psi\ dB_{t,k}(\omega),P_{n}\ (p_{k}-P_{k})\psi\ dB_{t,k}(\omega)\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Big\langle(p_{k}-P_{k})\psi,P_{n}\ (p_{k}-P_{k})\psi\Big\rangle\ dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{N}p_{k}^{2}-2p_{n}+1\Big)\Big\langle\psi,P_{n}\psi\Big\rangle\ dt.

We have simplified some expressions by using that
P_{k}P_{n}=0
if
k\not=n
, and
P_{k}P_{n}=P_{n}
if
k=n
.
Since
D_{1}+D_{3}=0,
equation (
27
) takes the form

\displaystyle dp_{n}=\frac{D_{2}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}.

(28)

This equation and the definitions of
p_{n}
and
p_{n,k}
imply equation (
25
).

Equation (
25
) implies an important conservation law.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[p_{n}(t,\cdot)]=&\,p_{n}(0)=\frac{\langle\psi_{0},P_{n}\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle}.\end{split}

(29)

The purpose of this paper is to prove that, in the model considered here, von Neumann’s postulate
(
11
) and Lüders’ postulate (
14
) are almost surely consistent
in the limit as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In the next section, this result is stated with more precision. It is proven in Section 4.

3
The main result

We recall that (
24
) says that

\displaystyle\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\ \psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\Big\rangle=1\,,

(30)

for arbitrary
t
and almost every
\omega
.
We also remind the reader of the definitions of
p_{n}
and
p_{k,n}
in (
19
) and (
26
),
respectively.

We are now prepared to state our main result.

Main Theorem.

1.

There exist positive functions
\epsilon(t)
and
\delta(t)
satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\big[\epsilon(t)+\delta(t)\big]=0

(31)

such that, for an arbitrary
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq 1-p_{n}(t,\omega)\leq\epsilon(t),

(32)

holds with probability
p_{n}(0)+O(\delta(t))
, for all times
t>0
, and if (
32
) holds for all times then

\text{lim}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\psi(t,\omega)-P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\|=0,

(33)

while, with probability
1-p_{n}(0)+O(\delta(t))
, one has that

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{n}(t,\omega)\leq\epsilon(t),

(34)

and hence

\text{lim}_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\|=0
.

2.

Defining the density matrix
\rho_{t}
by

\rho_{t}:=\mathbb{E}\big[P_{\psi(t,\omega)}\equiv|\psi(t,\omega)\rangle\langle\psi(t,\omega)|\big]\,,

one has that

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\text{lim}}\,\|\rho_{t}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}P_{n}\rho_{t}P_{n}\|=0,

(35)

with

\text{Tr}(\rho_{t}\cdot P_{n})=\langle\psi_{0},P_{n}\,\psi_{0}\rangle,\quad\forall\,\,n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\};

i.e., von Neumann’s postulate holds in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
.

This theorem tells us that, with probability 1, every solution of equation (
18
)
tends to a vector in the range of
one
of the projections
P_{n},n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, as time
t

tends to
\infty
. For any
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, with probability
p_{n}(0)=\langle\psi_{0},P_{n}\,\psi_{0}\rangle
,
the solution
\psi(t,\omega)
of (
18
) with initial condition
\psi(t=0,\omega)=\psi_{0},\|\psi_{0}\|=1,

tends to a limiting random vector,
\psi_{\infty}(\omega)
, belonging to the range of the projection
P_{n}
.
This confirms that Lüders’ postulate for measurements of the “observable”
\mathcal{N}
, amended as
indicated in Eq. (
15
) of Section 2, holds. As claimed in Part 2 of the Main Theorem, this implies the
amended form of von Neumann’s postulate.

The proof of this theorem is presented in the next section.

4
Proof of the Main Theorem, conclusions

For every
n\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, we define a function
h_{n}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle h_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\big[p_{n}(t,\cdot)(1-p_{n}(t,\cdot))\big]\,.

(36)

From (
25
) we infer that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h_{n}(t)=-4\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}\big[\big(p_{k}(t,\cdot)p_{n}(t,\cdot)-p_{k,n}(t,\cdot)\big)^{2}\big]\,.

(37)

The most important term on the right side of this equation is

\mathbb{E}\big[(p_{k}p_{n}-p_{k,n})^{2}\big]\Big|_{k=n}=\mathbb{E}\big[(p_{n}^{2}-p_{n})^{2}\big],

where we have used that
p_{k,n}=p_{n}
when
k=n
.

It follows from the definition that
p_{n}\geq p_{n}^{2}
. The Schwartz inequality implies that

\displaystyle E((p_{n}-p_{n}^{2})^{2})\geq h_{n}^{2}.

(38)

With (
37
), this inequality implies the following crucial inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h_{n}(t)\leq-4h_{n}(t)^{2},\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(39)

Since
0\leq h_{n}(t)\leq 1
, for all times
t\geq 0
, there exists a positive constant
C_{n}
such that

\displaystyle 0\leq h_{n}(t)\leq C_{n}(1+t)^{-1}.

(40)

We are ready to state a first result.

Lemma.

There exist positive functions
\delta_{n}(t)
and
\epsilon_{n}(t)
satisfying

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\delta_{n}(t)+\epsilon_{n}(t)=0,

(41)

such that, with probability
\geq 1-\delta_{n}(t)
, one and only one of the following two possibilities arises,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}=&\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\ P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle\leq\epsilon_{n}(t),\\
\|(1-P_{n})\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}=&\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\ (1-P_{n})\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle\leq\epsilon_{n}(t).\end{split}

(42)

Proof.

Inequality (
40
) and the fact that
0\leq p_{n}(1-p_{n})\leq 1
imply that, for any
\epsilon>0
,
there exists a large constant
M
such that, for any
t\geq M
,

\displaystyle p_{n}(t,\omega)\big(1-p_{n}(t,\omega)\big)\leq\epsilon\,,

(43)

with probability
1-\epsilon
.
The result then follows by using that
0\leq p_{n}\leq 1
and the following obvious fact: if
\beta\in[0,1]
satisfies
the inequality
\beta(1-\beta)\leq\kappa
, for some
\kappa\ll 1
, then

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\min\{\beta,\,1-\beta\}\leq 2\kappa.\hskip 85.35826pt\square

(44)

We are now prepared to prove statements (
32
) and (
34
) in the Main Theorem. The
Lemma just proven says that, as
t\rightarrow\infty,
with probability 1,
p_{n}(t)
approaches only two possible values,

0
or
1
. This fact and (
29
) imply claims (
32
) and (
34
):
the probability of
p_{n}(t,\omega)
approaching 1 is given by
\langle\psi_{0},P_{n}\psi_{0}\rangle
, and
the probability that it approaches
0
is given by
\langle\psi_{0},(1-P_{n})\psi_{0}\rangle.

The proof of (
33
) is straightforward after noticing the following fact:

0\leq p_{n}(t,\omega)=\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle=\|P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}\leq\|\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}=1.

Thus if
|p_{n}(t,\omega)-1|\ll 1
, then

\displaystyle\|P_{n}\psi(t,\omega)-\psi(t,\omega)\|\ll 1.

(45)

This implies inequality (
33
).

Next we prove (
35
).
Since
P_{k_{1}}P_{k_{2}}=0
when
k_{1}\not=k_{2}
, and the projections
P_{k}
are nonnegative operators,
it is easy to see that, when
k_{1}\not=k_{2},

\displaystyle P_{k_{2}}\leq 1-P_{k_{1}}

(46)

and

\displaystyle P_{k_{2}}(1-P_{k_{1}})=P_{k_{2}}.

(47)

Moreover since one and only one of the two possibilities in (
42
) occurs, we find that, if
k_{1}\not=k_{2}
, then with probability great than
1-\delta_{k_{1}}(t)-\delta_{k_{2}}(t)
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|P_{k_{1}}\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}\ \|P_{k_{2}}\psi(t,\omega)\|^{2}\\
=&\Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\ P_{k_{1}}\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle\ \Big\langle\psi(t,\omega),\ P_{k_{2}}\psi(t,\omega)\Big\rangle\leq\epsilon_{k_{1}}(t)+\epsilon_{k_{2}}(t).\end{split}

(48)

This provides a bound for the operator
|P_{k_{1}}\psi\rangle\ \langle P_{k_{2}}\psi|
,
k_{1}\not=k_{2},

\displaystyle\Big\|\big|P_{k_{1}}\psi\big\rangle\big\langle P_{k_{2}}\psi\big|\Big\|\leq\sqrt{\epsilon_{k_{1}}(t)+\epsilon_{k_{2}}(t)}\rightarrow 0,\ \text{as}\ t\rightarrow\infty.

(49)

This implies the claim in Eq. (
35
).

4.1
Concluding remarks

I.

Consider an ensemble of identical quantum systems of interest interacting with a macroscopic environment.
As described for the model studied in Section 2, the evolution of the ensemble average of their states, after tracing
out the degrees of freedom of the environment, can often be described
approximately
by some
Lindblad equation (or some non-Markovian generalization thereof). This is a consequence of

“decoherence”

[
11
]
. In examples, it may then be possible to derive
von Neumann’s postulate

on measurements from properties of the systems that can be derived from decoherence.

This does, however,
not
explain why something like
Lüders’ postulate
may hold for
individual

systems. More precisely, it does not determine an appropriate notion of
states of individual systems
and a
law for the stochastic time evolution of those states. In particular, it does not imply the occurrence of any kind of
wave-function collapse.

II.

The effective evolution of density matrices describing ensemble averages of states of identical systems
coupled to a macroscopic environment can usually be “unravelled” in a way generalizing the passage
from Eq. (
17
) to Eq. (
18
) described in Section 2; (for general results, see

[
12
]
and references given there). Unfortunately, though, “unraveling” some effective
dynamics is usually
not
a unique process. Without some fundamental guiding principles, the ambiguties
encountered in “unraveling” effective evolution equations for ensemble averages of states of identical systems
cannot be removed. It leaves one with the feeling that some fundamental aspects of the time evolution of states
of individual systems remain to be deciphered.

III.

Although it appears to be fairly widely appreciated that, in quantum mechanics, the time evolution of
states of individual systems featuring events (including measurements) is
not
described by a linear
deterministic Schrödinger equation or some linear deterministic generalization thereof, there appears to
be much disagreement and confusion as to what the correct
law of stochastic evolution of states

of such systems might look like. There are plenty of proposals of
ad-hoc
such laws, but there is
no agreement on a
general principle
determining a precise law of this kind.

This unsatisfactory situation is analyzed in
[
6
]
(and references given there), where a precise
general principle determining the stochastic evolution of states of individual systems and leading, in particular,
to an understanding of
“projective measurements”
, is proposed and some concrete examples
are worked out in some detail.
