Consistency of MLE for partially observed diffusions, with application in market microstructure modeling
Sergey Nadtochiy, Yuan Yin

Abstract
This paper presents a tractable sufficient condition for the consistency of maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) in partially observed diffusion models, stated in terms of stationary distribution of the associated fully observed diffusion, under the assumption that the set of unknown parameter values is finite. This sufficient condition is then verified in the context of a latent price model of market microstructure, yielding consistency of maximum likelihood estimators of the unknown parameters in this model. Finally, we compute the latter estimators using historical financial data taken from the NASDAQ exchange.

Introduction

This paper is concerned with the large-sample properties of maximum likelihood estimators (MLEs) for partially observed diffusions, as well as their application in market microstructure modeling.
The need for such estimation arises when, in addition to the unobserved dynamic components, a partially observed dynamical system has parameters that are assumed to be constant but not known.
A fairly general form of a partially observed diffusion with unknown parameters is given by (
1
)–(
2
), and a more specific model, motivated by financial application, is discussed in Section
3
.

The choice of MLE as an estimator is justified by its large-sample properties. It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
New94
]
) that the MLE is consistent (i.e., it converges to the true parameter value as the sample size grows) and asymptotically efficient (i.e., its asymptotic variance attains the famous lower bound due to Cramer-Rao) in the cases where the observed sample consists of independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables. In this sense, MLE has the best large-sample properties. Some of these asymptotic properties of MLE have been extended to the settings where the observations are not i.i.d. but are given by a sample path of a Markov process: see
[
Bag81
]
,
[
Bag84
]
, and the references therein, for the case of fully observed diffusions with linear coefficients.
A general approach for analyzing the asymptotic properties of MLE (as well as of other statistical methods) is described, e.g., in
[
IH81
]
and
[
Kut04
]
. For the partially observed discrete-time Markov chains, there exist general results on the consistency of MLE (see, e.g.,
[
DMOvH11
]
,
[
GCL06
]
,
[
CMR05
]
), but it is not straightforward to extend the discrete-time methods and results to continuous-time processes. To date, with the exception of the present work and to the best of our knowledge, there exists only one general result on the (large-sample) consistency of MLE for partially observed diffusions, stated in
[
Chi09
]
.

The above literature review is focused on the large-sample properties of MLE for partially observed Markov processes, as such is the focus of the present paper. However, it is worth mentioning the related (though different) results on the asymptotic properties of MLEs for Markov systems with vanishing noise (cf.
[
Kut94
]
,
[
Kut19
]
,
[
JLG95
]
, and the references therein), on the large-sample properties of the so-called one-step MLE process (cf.
[
LC55
]
,
[
KK18
]
, and the references therein), and on the EM algorithm that can be used to efficiently compute MLEs (cf.
[
DZ86
]
).

The analysis of MLE for partially observed Markov systems is intimately connected to the problem of stochastic filtering: i.e., the computation of conditional distribution of the unobserved components given the information available thus far – this conditional distribution is referred to as the filter (see, e.g.,
[
BC09
]
, and the references therein).
Indeed, one may express the likelihood function and MLE via the filter and the observed component and notice that the latter form a new (fully observed) Markov process. Then, one may try to apply the abstract results (see, e.g.,
[
IH81
]
,
[
Kut04
]
) to establish the desired consistency and asymptotic efficiency of MLE in this fully observed Markov system. This is precisely the approach taken in
[
Chi09
]
, which provides sufficient conditions for the consistency and asymptotic efficiency of MLE in partially observed finite-state continuous-time Markov models.
However, with very few exceptions (e.g., Kalman-Bucy filter), the filter in a general nonlinear partially observed Markov system is not available in a closed form. Moreover, the joint dynamics of the (typically, infinite-dimensional) filter and the observation are convoluted and degenerate.
For this reason, the aforementioned approach based on the classical abstract results leads to assumptions that are difficult to verify in practice: e.g., stated in terms of the quantitative properties of the likelihood function or of the stationary distribution of the filter, for which there is no tractable characterization in general. The latter motivates Theorem
1
whose assumptions are stated in terms of stationary distribution of the original (finite-dimensional) diffusion, which has a tractable characterization, e.g., via the associated elliptic partial differential equation (PDE).

The main theoretical contribution of this work is two-fold. First, we derive a tractable sufficient condition for the consistency of MLE for partially observed diffusions (Theorem
1
, Section
2
). Second, we verify this sufficient condition for a specific model motivated by a financial application (Theorem
2
, Section
3
). The sufficient condition of Theorem
1
requires one to find an appropriate set of separating test functions and test processes, in order to distinguish between any two values of the unknown parameter. The main technical effort of this paper is in the proof of Theorem
2
, where we illustrate how to choose such test functions and test processes, and how to verify that they possess the desired separating property, in the context of a latent price model of market microstructure. This verification is based on a careful asymptotic analysis of certain functionals of the associated diffusion (see Proposition
4
). We believe that the methods presented in the proof of Theorem
2
can be applied to other models of interest, and we discuss the general approach to verifying the conditions of Theorem
1
in the discussion following the latter theorem. It is worth mentioning that Theorem
1
requires the set of the unknown parameter values to be finite. A relaxation of this assumption (via an appropriate continuity assumption on the diffusion coefficients) is the subject of ongoing research in
[
ENss
]
, which uses the finite-parameter result proved herein as an important building block.

The theoretical results developed in the present paper are motivated by a specific financial application. It is a notorious problem in market microstructure modeling to find the right notion of price for a given asset, that would be appropriate in a setting where the observations are made at a very high (time) frequency. For example, the quoted bid and ask prices of even the most liquid stocks change only once in several seconds, whereas other relevant events in the limit order book occur on a scale of milliseconds. Thus, any notion of a price based on the observed bid and ask quotes (e.g., the midprice, or the last transaction price) would remain constant most of the time and would not reflect with sufficient accuracy the consensus of market participants about the true value of the asset. As a result, it is very challenging to define and estimate the volatility of midprice (or of the last transaction price) in a high-frequency regime. The fact that the changes of midprice and the changes of other relevant market factors occur on two very different time scales also makes it difficult to build a predictive model for the future price. In particular, it is challenging to estimate the price impact (i.e., the expected change in the price caused by a purchase or a sale of one unit of the asset) using high frequency observations. Indeed, it takes a relatively large number of trades to move the quoted prices (which stay constant most of the time), hence the standard linear regression model for the two does not work well in the high frequency regime. This issue is documented in
[
CKS14
]
,
[
MCLL21
]
, whose empirical results indicate that the estimation of expected impact of trades on midprice only works well if the observations are taken at a fairly low frequency (tens of seconds), which means that a large fraction of observations have to be ignored in such analysis.
To address these challenges, and to obtain a “more dynamic” notion of price, the latent price models are used. Namely, instead of modeling the quoted prices directly, one assumes the existence of an unobserved (latent) price process, which changes at a high frequency, such that the quoted (observed) prices are functions (e.g., roundings to the nearest integer number of cents) of the latent price: see, e.g.,
[
DR15
]
, and the references therein. The advantage of a latent price model is that the price and other relevant market factors evolve on the same time scale. The main disadvantage is that the true price is hidden, which leads to a system with partial observations.
The latent price model analyzed herein is a version of the model proposed in
[
Nad21
]
to explain the concavity of expected price impact of a large sequence of co-directed trades – a phenomenon that has received much attention from both practitioners and academics (see the discussion and references in
[
Nad21
]
).
This model is a mixture of the uncertainty-zone model of
[
DR15
]
and a linear price impact model (see, e.g.,
[
AC01
]
), but it also prescribes an additional micro-drift for the latent price process (which is responsible for the aforementioned concavity of price impact). A more detailed description of the proposed model is presented at the beginning of Section
3
, which also contains the proof of consistency of MLE in this model (via verification of the conditions of Theorem
1
). The empirical analysis described in Section
4
shows how to compute MLE in the proposed latent price model numerically, using historical financial data. The results of this analysis, in particular, provide high-frequency estimates of the price impact coefficient and of the volatility of latent price. In addition, these empirical results support the presence of the aforementioned micro-drift in the latent price process (i.e., the estimated magnitude of the micro-drift is non-negligible), which validates the main novel feature of the proposed model.

Remark 1
.

Note that the continuous-time setting used herein, in fact, does not complicate the analysis as compared to the discrete-time setting. On contrary, it turns out that the diffusion models naturally lead to a more convenient choice of a reference measure with respect to which the likelihood is computed (i.e., a measure with respect to which the density of the observation is computed). The existing results on discrete-time Markov processes (see the introduction for a list of such results) express the sufficient condition for consistency in terms of the transition kernel of the Markov process, which naturally leads to the (product) Lebesgue measure as a reference measure for the likelihood. We believe that the sufficient condition of Theorem
1
is more tractable than the existing conditions for discrete-time Markov processes (and the arguments in our proof are simpler), because the continuous-time framework leads us naturally to the (scaled) Wiener measure as a reference measure for the likelihood, which turns out to be more convenient for analyzing the consistency of MLE.

\mathbb{P}^{\theta}

The first part of this clam is obvious and the second follows from the observation that the set of mass points of the distribution of
\xi
is at most countable, and from the application of the continuous mapping theorem.
Moreover, noticing that
M^{-1}
is a positive supermartingale under
\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}
, we conclude that there exist
\epsilon>0
and
\eta
such that

\lim_{T\to\infty}M^{-1}_{T}=\eta,\quad\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{M_{T}\geq\epsilon\}}=\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta\leq\epsilon^{-1}\}},

(8)

a.s. under
\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}
.

Then, with the notation
A:=\{\xi\geq\epsilon\}
, we claim that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|^{2}dt=0\quad\mathbb{P}^{\theta}\mbox{-a.s. on $A$}

(9)

To prove (
9
), we notice that, if it doesn’t hold, there exists an event
A^{\prime}\subset A
, s.t.
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(A^{\prime})>0
and

\liminf_{T\to\infty}-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|^{2}dt<0\quad\mbox{on $A^{\prime}$}

Then, on
A^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{T\to\infty}M_{T}=\liminf_{T\to\infty}\exp\left(\frac{T}{2}\left[-\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|^{2}dt+\frac{2}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}dW^{\theta}_{t}\right]\right)=0,

as the term
\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}dW^{\theta}_{t}
converges to zero in probability (under
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
) and hence a.s. along a subsequence of any sequence of
\{T_{n}\}
.
The above is a contradiction as
A^{\prime}\subset A
and
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(A^{\prime})>0
. Thus, we conclude that (
9
) holds.

Next, using the dominated convergence theorem, we deduce from (
9
) that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|^{2}dt\,\mathbf{1}_{A}=0.

The above, along with the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, yields the following for any bounded test function
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and any test process
Y^{\lambda}
satisfying the assumptions of the theorem:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{A}\,\left|f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|^{2}dt\,\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\lim_{T\to\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\right|^{2}dt\mathbf{1}_{A}\right)^{1/2}=0.

In addition, using the notation

A_{t}:=\{M_{t}\geq\epsilon\},

and recalling (
7
), as well as the dominated convergence theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\left|(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}-\mathbf{1}_{A})\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right|dt=0,

which yields

\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right)dt=0.

(10)

Let us expand the integrand in (
10
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left[h(\theta^{\prime};X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]\right)-{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[h(\theta;X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left[M^{-1}_{t}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta^{\prime};X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta;X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left(M^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta^{\prime};X_{t})\right)-\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta;X_{t})\right),

where we used the fact that
M_{t}=d\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}_{[0,t]}/d\mathbb{P}^{\theta}_{[0,t]}
.
Note that
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}
is an expectation under
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
, which is defined on a larger probability space than
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
, hence the right hand side of the above may not equal zero even if
h(\theta;x)
does not depend on
\theta
.

From the above, we deduce

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}^{\theta}\left(\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot\Delta h_{t}^{\theta^{\prime},\theta}\right)dt

(11)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\left[\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}M^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta^{\prime};X_{t})dt-\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta;X_{t})dt\right].

Recalling that
(h(\theta;X),Y^{\lambda})
is strongly ergodic under
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
and (
7
), we obtain
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
-a.s.:

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta;X_{t})dt=\mathbf{1}_{\{\xi\geq\epsilon\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta,\lambda}(dx,dy).

Using the dominated convergence, we deduce from the above that

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta;X_{t})dt=\mathbb{P}(\xi\geq\epsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta,\lambda}(dz,dy),

where we also noticed that
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
coincide on
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{\infty}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{\infty}
.
Similarly, recalling (
8
) and using the strong ergodicity along with the dominated convergence, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}M^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}}\,f\left(Y^{\lambda}_{t}\right)\cdot h(\theta^{\prime};X_{t})dt=\mathbb{E}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left(\eta\mathbf{1}_{\{\eta\leq\epsilon^{-1}\}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta^{\prime},\lambda}(dz,dy)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\theta^{\prime}}\left(M^{-1}_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{M_{t}\geq\epsilon\}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta^{\prime},\lambda}(dz,dy)=\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\xi\geq\epsilon)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta^{\prime},\lambda}(dz,dy)\,

where we used again the fact that
M_{t}=d\mathbb{P}^{\theta^{\prime}}_{[0,t]}/d\mathbb{P}^{\theta}_{[0,t]}
.

Collecting the above and recalling (
11
), as well as
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}(\xi\geq\epsilon)>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta,\lambda}(dz,dy)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}}z\cdot f(y)\nu^{\theta^{\prime},\lambda}(dz,dy).

The above, in view of the arbitrary choice of
f
and
\lambda
, yields
\theta=\theta^{\prime}
which leads to a desired contradiction.

To prove Theorem
2
it remains to notice that, due to Proposition
1
and the finiteness of
\Theta
,
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
-a.s., for all large enough

, the unique maximizer of

is given by
\theta
.

3
Latent price model of market microstructure

We denote by
X
the latent price process (a.k.a. the unobserved fundamental price process) and by
Y
the order flow process, whose value at time
t
is given by the total volume of all the limit buy orders at time
t
less the total volume of all limit sell orders.
6
6

6

This notion of order flow is sometimes called the total order imbalance. Note that it implicitly incorporates the changes in both limit and market orders (as any market order eliminates the corresponding number of limit orders).

Instead of postulating what
X_{t}
should be, as a function of observed market factors, we list the natural properties required of
X
and then build a simple model that reflects these properties. The three properties we focus on herein are: (i) on a macroscopic scale,
X
and
Y
should evolve as random walks, (ii) there is a positive correlation between the local increments of
X
and
Y
(referred to as price impact), and (iii)
X
has a microscopic drift that pushes it away from the midprice (i.e., from the middle point between the best bid and ask prices). The first two properties are standard in the existing literature and are confirmed empirically.
The third property appeared recently in
[
Nad21
]
, where the presence of such microscopic drift is used as an explanation of the concavity of expected price impact of a large meta-order. The presence of such microscopic drift is also confirmed empirically in
[
Nad21
]
for
X
given by the “microprice” (an observed quantity that is another candidate for the notion of price in a high frequency regime).
Assuming that the prices are measured in cents (i.e., in
\$0.01
) and that the bid-ask spread stays at one cent (its smallest possible value) most of the time, the above properties translate into the following diffusion model for
(X,Y)
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{dX_{t}=\alpha\beta\mu(X_{t})dt+\sigma dB_{t},}\\
{dY_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}dX_{t}+\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}/\alpha^{2}}d\tilde{W}_{t}=\beta\mu(X_{t})dt+\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}dB_{t}+\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}/\alpha^{2}}d\tilde{W}_{t},\quad Y_{0}=0,}\end{array}\right.

(12)

where

\displaystyle\mu(x)=x\,\mathrm{mod}\,1-1/2,\quad\alpha,\beta,\sigma,\bar{\sigma}\in(0,\infty),\quad\sigma<\alpha\bar{\sigma},

B
and
\tilde{W}
are independent one-dimensional Brownian motions, and
X_{0}
is a random variable independent of
(B,\tilde{W})
.
Using Girsanov’s theorem, it is easy to show that there exists a weak solution to (
12
), that this solution is unique in law, and that
X
and
\tilde{W}
are independent.
The best bid and ask prices at time
t
are given by
P^{b}_{t}:=B(X_{t})
and
P^{a}_{t}:=A(X_{t})
respectively, with the associated functions defined as follows:

\displaystyle B(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\lfloor x\rfloor,}&{\text{if }\nexists i\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ s.t. }|x-i|\leq\epsilon,}\\
{i-1,}&{\text{if }\exists i\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ s.t. }|x-i|\leq\epsilon,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle A(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\lceil x\rceil,}&{\text{if }\nexists i\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ s.t. }|x-i|\leq\epsilon,}\\
{i+1,}&{\text{if }\exists i\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ s.t. }|x-i|\leq\epsilon,}\end{array}\right.

for a constant
\epsilon\in(0,1/2)
.

For
\beta=0
, the system (
12
) yields a classical model (in the spirit of
[
AC01
]
) where the latent price and the order flow are modeled as correlated random walks, and where each share purchased moves the latent price up by
\alpha
(the latter is the price impact coefficient). When
\beta>0
, the latent price process obtains an additional micro-drift (which is also transferred to the order flow via the price impact) that pushes the latent price away from the midprice.

Our observations are given by the continuous-time processes

\displaystyle P^{b}_{t},P^{a}_{t},Y_{t},\quad t\in[0,T].

Note that the present setting does not immediately fit the one considered in Section
2
, because the observation process herein includes certain (non-invertible) functions of
X
, in addition to the coupled diffusion process
Y
. In what follows, we show how to reduce the estimation problem in the above model to the one considered in Section
2
.

We start by noticing that the constant
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
can be estimated precisely from the above sample, for any
T>0
, as

\displaystyle\frac{[Y,Y]_{T}}{T}=\bar{\sigma}^{2},

(17)

we assume that
\bar{\sigma}^{2}
is known.
Our primary goal is to find a consistent estimator for the unknown vector of parameters
\theta:=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})
, where the consistency is understood in the regime
T\rightarrow\infty
.
7
7

7

It is also shown in Subsection
4.1
how to construct a consistent estimator of
\epsilon
. However,
\epsilon
does not appear in the estimation algorithm for the rest of the parameters – this is why the dependence on
\epsilon
is omitted in most of the remainder of the paper.

We assume that
\theta
belongs to a given (known) set
\Theta\subset(0,\infty)^{3}
. We construct our estimator on the space
(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{\otimes[0,\infty)}\times C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
of the paths of
(P^{b},P^{a},Y)
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
, we equip the latter space with the measure
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
which is defined as the distribution of
(B(X),A(X),Y)
, where
(X,Y)
are given by (
12
).

The estimation procedure consists of two parts. First, we obtain the consistent estimator of

\displaystyle\Sigma=\Sigma(\theta):=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\phi(\gamma(\theta))},

(18)

where

\displaystyle\phi(z):=2\int_{0}^{1}\exp(-z(x-1/2)^{2})\int_{0}^{x}\exp(z(y-1/2)^{2})dydx,\quad\gamma(\theta):=\frac{\alpha\beta}{\sigma^{2}}.

(19)

Proposition 2
.

Assume that
M:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is such that
M(T),T/M(T)\rightarrow\infty
as
T\rightarrow\infty
. Then, for any
\theta=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\hat{\Sigma}_{T}=\Sigma(\theta),

in probability under
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
, where

\hat{\Sigma}_{T}:=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=1}^{M(T)}\left(\hat{X}_{kT/M(T)}-\hat{X}_{(k-1)T/M(T)}\right)^{2},\quad\hat{X}_{t}:=\frac{1}{2}(P^{b}_{t}+P^{a}_{t}).

The proof of this proposition is given in the Appendix.

The second, and most important, part of the estimation is performed by applying the maximum likelihood method to the partially observed diffusion (
12
), as described in Section
2
. Note that the notation used herein is consistent with the one used in Section
2
:

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}W=\frac{\sigma}{\alpha}B+\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}/\alpha^{2}}\,\tilde{W},\quad b(\theta;x)=\alpha\beta\mu(x),\quad h(\theta;x)=\beta\mu(x),\quad\sigma(\theta;x)=\sigma,\quad q=m=d=1.

Recall that
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
denotes the distribution of
(X,Y)
, given by (
12
) for any fixed
\theta\in\Theta
, that
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
is the second marginal of
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
, and that
\mathbb{P}
is the distribution of
\bar{\sigma}W
(i.e., a scaled Wiener measure).
Recall also that
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}_{T},\mathbb{P}^{\theta}_{T},\mathbb{P}_{T}
denote the restrictions of respective measures to the time interval
[0,T]
.

The likelihood function, in this case, becomes:

\displaystyle L_{T}(\theta)=\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mu^{\theta}_{t}\right)^{2}dt+\frac{\beta}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\mu^{\theta}_{t}dY_{t}\right),\quad\mu^{\theta}_{t}:=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\mu(X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right).

(20)

Notice that the moment-matching condition
\hat{\Sigma}_{T}=\Sigma(\theta)
, effectively, reduces the dimension of unknown
\theta
.
Thus, for any given
T>0
and
s>0
, we define MLE
\hat{\theta}_{T}(s)
of
\theta
for a reduced parameter set as any random variable satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}_{T}(s)\in\text{argmax}_{\theta\in\Theta(s)}\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mu^{\theta}_{t}\right)^{2}dt+\int_{0}^{T}\mu^{\theta}_{t}dY_{t}\right),

(21)

where

\displaystyle\Theta(s):=\text{argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(|\Sigma(\theta)-s|\right).

More precisely, we define
\hat{\theta}_{T}(s,\omega)
as a selector from the above argmax that is
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{T}
-measurable in
(s,\omega)
(see
[
AB06
, Thm 18.13]
for the existence of such a measurable selector).

The above MLE for a reduced parameter set is consistent under the following technical assumption (in addition to the assumption
|\Theta|<\infty
).

Assumption 1
.

The function

\displaystyle z\mapsto\frac{1-1/\phi(z)}{z(1/\psi(z)-1)\phi(z)}

(22)

is invertible on the domain
\{z=\alpha\beta/\sigma^{2}:\,(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta\}
,
where

\displaystyle\phi(z)=2\int_{0}^{1}\exp(-z(x-1/2)^{2})\int_{0}^{x}\exp(z(y-1/2)^{2})dydx,\quad\psi(z):=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(zy^{2}-zy)dy.

Remark 2
.

Assumption
1
, in principle, needs to be verified for every given set
\Theta
, which is not hard to do if the latter set is finite. The numerical experiments indicate that
\phi(z)
is strictly increasing for all
z\geq 0
(see Figure
1
). Such strict monotonicity of (
22
) would clearly imply that Assumption
1
is always satisfied. However, at this time we are not able to prove this monotonicity rigorously for the entire range
z\geq 0
, hence it remains a conjecture.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
\Theta
is finite and let Assumption
1
hold.
Then, for any
\theta=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta
and
s=\Sigma(\theta)
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\hat{\theta}_{T}(s)=\theta,

in probability (and a.s.) under
\mathbb{P}^{\theta}
.

The above theorem says that, if we know the true value of
\Sigma(\theta)
, denoted by
s
(without necessarily knowing the true value of
\theta
itself), then a MLE for the reduced parameter set
\Theta(s)=\{\theta\in\Theta:\,\Sigma(\theta)=s\}
is consistent. The proof of Theorem
2
consists of verification of the assumptions of Theorem
1
, and it is presented in the next subsection.

Finally, we combine the above results to obtain a consistent estimator of
\theta
.
To state this final result, we note that
\hat{\theta}_{T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{T})
is a random variable on the probability space
(\mathbb{Z}^{2})^{\otimes[0,\infty)}\times C([0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
of the paths of
(P^{b},P^{a},Y)
.
8
8

8

To avoid confusion, we note that
\hat{\theta}_{T}(s)
is constructed as an estimator in a model where the observation is artificially restricted to the path of
Y
only: i.e.,
\hat{\theta}_{T}(s)
is a random variable on the canonical space consisting of the paths of
Y
. On the other hand,
\hat{\theta}_{T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{T})
is an estimator in a model with actual observations, given by
(P^{b},P^{a},Y)
: i.e.,
\hat{\theta}_{T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{T})
is a random variable on the canonical space consisting of the paths of the latter processes.

Corollary 1
.

Assume that
\Theta
is finite and let Assumption
1
hold.
Then, for any
\theta=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta
, we have

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\hat{\theta}_{T}\left(\hat{\Sigma}_{T}\right)=\theta,

in probability (and a.s.) under
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
.

Proof:

We only need to note that, since
\Theta
is finite and
\hat{\Sigma}_{T}\rightarrow\Sigma(\theta)
, we have
\bar{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
-a.s.: for large enough
T
,

\displaystyle\Theta(\hat{\Sigma}_{T})=\{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta:\,\Sigma(\theta^{\prime})=\Sigma(\theta)\}.

Then, for such
T
, we have
\hat{\theta}_{T}(\hat{\Sigma}_{T})=\hat{\theta}_{T}(\Sigma(\theta))
, and the result follows from Theorem
2
.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
2

Our goal is to deduce the statement of Theorem
2
from Theorem
1
. First, we define

\displaystyle dY^{\lambda}_{t}=-\lambda Y^{\lambda}_{t}dt+dY_{t},\quad Y^{\lambda}_{0}=0=Y_{0},

for all
\lambda\in(0,\infty)=:\Lambda
.
It is clear that
Y^{\lambda}
is adapted to
\mathbb{F}^{Y}
.
The following proposition shows that
(X\,\mathrm{mod}\,1,Y^{\lambda})
is strongly ergodic, which verifies the first assumption of Theorem
1
. In this proposition, we recall the notation
H^{1}([a,b],\mathbb{R})
which denotes the Sobolev space of square-integrable functions from
[a,b]
to
\mathbb{R}
whose first weak derivative is also square-integrable. The latter space is equipped with its natural Sobolev norm.

Proposition 3
.

For every
\theta=(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^{2})\in\Theta
and
\lambda>0
there exists a probability measure
\nu^{\theta,\lambda}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\phi(z,y)\nu^{\theta,\lambda}(dz,dy)=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\phi(\beta\mu(X_{t}),Y^{\lambda}_{t})dt=\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\phi(\beta\mu(X_{t}),Y^{\lambda}_{t})dt,\quad\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}\text{-a.s.},

(23)

for any bounded Borel
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, the density
q(t,\cdot)
of
X_{t}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1
under
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{t}^{t+u}\|q(s,\cdot)-\chi\|_{H^{1}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}ds=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|q(t,\cdot)-\chi\|_{L^{2}([0,1],\mathbb{R})}=0,

where

\displaystyle\chi(x):=\frac{\exp(\gamma x^{2}-\gamma x)}{\psi(\gamma)}\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]}(x),\quad\psi(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(\gamma y^{2}-\gamma y)dy,\quad\gamma=\gamma(\theta)=\alpha\beta/\sigma^{2},

(24)

and the above convergence is uniform over all distributions of
X_{0}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1
and locally uniform over
u\geq 0
.

Proof:

Step 1
.
In this step, we prove the first equality in (
23
). To this end, we apply Itô’s formula to
(Y^{\lambda})^{2}
,

\displaystyle d\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})^{2}=\left[2\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})Y^{\lambda}_{t}-\lambda\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}\right]dt,

and use Young’s inequality to conclude that
\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})^{2}
is bounded from above uniformly over all
t\geq 1
. This yields tightness of the laws of
(\beta\mu(X_{t}),Y^{\lambda}_{t})
over
t\geq 0
, which in turn yields tightness of
\{\nu_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
, where

\displaystyle\nu_{T}(A):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}^{\theta}\left((\beta\mu(X_{t}),Y^{\lambda}_{t})\in A\right)dt,

for any Borel
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, we conclude that any sequence
T_{n}\rightarrow\infty
contains a subsequence along which
\nu_{T}
converges weakly to a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Any such limiting measure is invariant for the Markov process
(\beta\mu(X),Y^{\lambda})
, as is easy to see from the definition of
\nu_{T}
.

Let us show that there exists only one invariant measure of
(\beta\mu(X),Y^{\lambda})
. We argue by contradiction and assume that there exist two distinct invariant measures
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
.
Notice that

\displaystyle Y^{\lambda}_{t}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}dX_{s}+\sqrt{\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}/\alpha^{2}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}d\tilde{W}_{s},

where the Brownian motion
\tilde{W}
is independent of
X
. Therefore, fixing any deterministic initial condition for

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})\,|\,X_{t}\right]=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\right]\,|\,X_{t}\right],

\displaystyle(b-a)C_{1}\leq\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(Y^{\lambda}_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{X}_{t}\right]\leq(b-a)C_{2},

for some constants

Thus, the conditional distribution of

has the same properties (see Step 3), we conclude that the transition kernel of the Markov process

Then,

must have densities

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(z,y,t,z^{\prime},y^{\prime})g(z,y)dzdy=g(z^{\prime},y^{\prime}),\quad g:=p-\tilde{p}.

Notice that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|g(z^{\prime},y^{\prime})|dz^{\prime}dy^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(z,y,t,z^{\prime},y^{\prime})g(z,y)dzdy\right|dz^{\prime}dy^{\prime}

\displaystyle>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}K(z,y,t,z^{\prime},y^{\prime})|g(z,y)|dzdydz^{\prime}dy^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|g(z,y)|dzdy,

Thus, the invariant measure of

is unique and, therefore,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(\mu(X_{s})-\mu(X_{s-u}))(W_{s}-W_{s-u})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(\mu_{n}(X_{s})-\mu_{n}(X_{s-u}))(W_{s}-W_{s-u}).

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}(\mu_{n}(X_{s})-\mu_{n}(X_{s-u}))(W_{s}-W_{s-u})

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{s-u}^{s}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu_{n}^{\prime}(X_{r})dr+\sigma\int_{s-u}^{s}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[(X_{r}-X_{s-u})(\mu_{n}^{\prime}(X_{r})\mu(X_{r})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mu_{n}^{\prime\prime}(X_{r}))\right]dr

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}|\mu_{n}(X_{s})-\mu_{n}(X_{s-u})|=\int_{0}^{1}q(s-u,x)\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mu_{n}(y)-\mu_{n}(x)|\bar{q}(x,u,y)dydx

Next, we estimate the second term in the right hand side of (

\displaystyle\phantom{??????????????????????????}\forall\,r-s+u\in(0,1],\,\,x\in[0,1],\,\,n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[(X_{r}-X_{s-u})(\mu_{n}^{\prime}(X_{r})\mu(X_{r})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mu_{n}^{\prime\prime}(X_{r}))\right]\right|\leq C_{4}(r-s+u)^{-1/2},

\displaystyle\phantom{??????????????????????????}\forall\,r-s+u\in(0,1],\,\,n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[(X_{r}-X_{s-u})(\mu_{n}^{\prime}(X_{r})\mu(X_{r})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\mu_{n}^{\prime\prime}(X_{r}))\right]\right|\leq C_{5}(r-s+u)^{1/2},

\displaystyle\phantom{??????????????????????????}\forall\,r-s+u\in(0,1],

Next, we consider the first term in the right hand side of (

\displaystyle=\sigma u\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{x}\chi(x)\mu(x)dx+g(s,u)+h(s,u)\right)=\sigma u\left(1-\chi(1)+g(s,u)+h(s,u)\right),

\displaystyle 2\alpha\beta\sigma\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\lambda^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\mu(X_{s})ds\,\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}dW_{s}\,dt

Repeating the above for

which is the main result of Step 1.

Step 2
. Herein, we analyze

.
It is easy to see that, for any finite

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X^{\lambda}_{t}=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X_{t},\quad\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\mu(X_{t})X^{\lambda}_{t}=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta^{\prime}}\mu(X_{t})X_{t}.

The following lemma shows that we can interchange the limits in

Lemma 1
.

For any
\theta\in\Theta
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\sup_{t\geq 1}\left|\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X^{\lambda}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X_{t}\right|=0

Proof:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X^{\lambda}_{t}-\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X_{t}=-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\mu(X_{t})X_{s}ds=-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mu(X_{t})\,|\,X_{s}\right]X_{s}\right)ds

\displaystyle=:-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\tilde{g}(t-s,X_{s})X_{s}\right)ds.

Then, to prove the statement of the lemma it suffices to show that

\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{s\geq 1}|\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\tilde{g}(u,X_{s})X_{s}\right)|=0.

To this end we notice that
\tilde{g}(u,\cdot)
is 1-periodic and that
\tilde{g}(u,x)=-\tilde{g}(u,1-x)
,
\tilde{g}(u,0)=\tilde{g}(u,1)=0
, and recall from Lemma
3
that

\displaystyle\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|\tilde{g}(u,x)|=0.

(38)

Next, we recall the scale transform of
X
:

\displaystyle Z_{t}=S(X_{t}),\quad S(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\exp\left(-2\int_{1/2}^{y}\mu(z)/\sigma^{2}dz\right)dy=\int_{0}^{x}\exp\left(-\mu^{2}(y)/\sigma^{2}\right)dy,

\displaystyle dZ_{t}=\exp\left(-2\int_{1/2}^{X_{t}}\mu(z)/\sigma^{2}(z)dz\right)dX_{t}-\exp\left(-2\int_{1/2}^{X_{t}}\mu(z)/\sigma^{2}(z)dz\right)\mu(X_{t})dt

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}(Z_{t})dW_{t},\quad\tilde{\sigma}(z)=S^{\prime}(S^{-1}(z))\sigma.

Notice that

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(u,X_{s})X_{s}=g(u,Z_{s})S^{-1}(Z_{s}),\quad g(u,z):=\tilde{g}(u,S(z)),

where

satisfies the same properties as
\tilde{g}(u,\cdot)
, except that its period is

.
Thus, our goal is to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{u\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{s\geq 1}|\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\gamma}\left(g(u,Z_{s})S^{-1}(Z_{s})\right)|=0.

To show the above, we recall some classical function-analytic results.
Denote by

the Sobolev space of functions

with square integrable first and second derivatives and such that
v(x)=-v(p-x)
,

. This space is endowed with its natural Sobolev norm.
The generator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(x)\partial^{2}_{xx}

which is compactly and densely embedded in the Hilbert space

. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{L}
is self-adjoint w.r.t. the cross-product of the latter space.
It is also well known (and can be verified directly) that the inverse
\mathcal{L}^{-1}

(notice that
\tilde{\sigma}(x)=\tilde{\sigma}(1-x)
). Then,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
is compact and the Riesz-Schauder theorem implies that there exists an orthonormal basis
\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
in

and a sequence
\{-\lambda_{i}\}
, s.t.
\{|\lambda_{i}|\}
is non-decreasing and converges to
\infty

\mathcal{L}v_{i}=-\lambda_{i}v_{i}
(in particular,

). It is easy to deduce from the definition of
\mathcal{L}
that
\lambda_{i}>0
.

As
g(u,\cdot)\in L^{2}([0,p],\tilde{\sigma}^{-2},\mathbb{R})
, we have:

\displaystyle g(u,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}(u)v_{i}(z),\quad\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}g(u,Z_{t})S^{-1}(Z_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}(u)\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}v_{i}(Z_{t}\,\mathrm{mod}\,p)S^{-1}(Z_{t}).

Extending

\tilde{\sigma}

-periodic functions on

\displaystyle d\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}v_{i}(Z_{t})S^{-1}(Z_{t})=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})\partial^{2}_{zz}\left[v_{i}(z)S^{-1}(z)\right](Z_{t})\right)dt

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})v_{i}^{\prime\prime}(Z_{t})S^{-1}(Z_{t})+\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})v_{i}^{\prime}(Z_{t})(S^{-1})^{\prime}(Z_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})v_{i}(Z_{t})(S^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(Z_{t})\right)dt

\displaystyle=\left[-\lambda_{i}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}v_{i}(Z_{t})S^{-1}(Z_{t})+\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})v_{i}^{\prime}(Z_{t})(S^{-1})^{\prime}(Z_{t})+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{t})v_{i}(Z_{t})(S^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(Z_{t})\right)\right]dt.

\tilde{q}(s,\cdot)

.
In addition, we notice that
(S^{-1})^{\prime}

Using these observations, we continue:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}v_{i}(Z_{t})S^{-1}(Z_{t})=e^{-\lambda_{i}(t-1)}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}v_{i}(Z_{1})S^{-1}(Z_{1})

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{t}e^{-\lambda_{i}(t-s)}\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{s})v_{i}^{\prime}(Z_{s})(S^{-1})^{\prime}(Z_{s})+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(Z_{s})v_{i}(Z_{s})(S^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(Z_{s})\right)ds

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{t}e^{-\lambda_{i}(t-s)}\int_{0}^{p}v_{i}(x)\left[\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(x)(S^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(x)\tilde{q}(s,x)-\partial_{x}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(x)(S^{-1})^{\prime}(x)\tilde{q}(s,x)\right)\right]dxds.

\displaystyle u(s,x):=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{4}(x)(S^{-1})^{\prime\prime}(x)\tilde{q}(s,x)-\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\left(\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(x)(S^{-1})^{\prime}(x)\tilde{q}(s,x)\right),\quad x\in[0,p].

4
Implementation and empirical analysis

Let us discuss a numerical algorithm for approximating
L_{T}(\theta)
in the financial example of Section
3
. Note that the observed data is always discrete, even if it is observed at a very high frequency. Therefore, we aim to construct an approximation method that converges to the true
L_{T}(\theta)
as the observation frequency increases.
The main component in the computation of
L_{T}(\theta)
(recall (
20
)) is

\displaystyle\mu^{\theta}_{t}=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left(\mu(X_{t})\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right)=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mu\left(\kappa Y_{t}+\tilde{X}_{t}\right)\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right],

(42)

where
\tilde{X}_{t}:=X_{t}-\kappa Y_{t}
and
\kappa:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\alpha\bar{\sigma}^{2}}
.
It is easy to see that

\displaystyle d\tilde{X}_{t}=\beta\frac{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\alpha\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\mu(\kappa Y_{t}+\tilde{X}_{t})dt+\sigma\frac{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}dB_{t}-\sigma^{2}\frac{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}}{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}d\tilde{W}_{t},

(43)

that the quadratic covariation
\langle\tilde{X},Y\rangle
between
\tilde{X}
and
Y
is zero,
and that
\langle\tilde{X},\tilde{X}\rangle_{t}=t\sigma^{2}\left(1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\right)

Then, the standard results in stochastic filtering (see
[
LR17
, Section 5.6.1]
, and
[
BC09
]
for more) imply that the conditional distribution of
\tilde{X}_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}
has a density that can be computed by normalizing the function

which solves the Zakai equation:

\displaystyle du=\left[\frac{1}{2}A^{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}u-C\partial_{x}\left(\mu(x+\kappa Y_{t})u\right)\right]dt+\frac{\beta}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\mu(x+\kappa Y_{t})udY_{t},

\displaystyle A^{2}:=\sigma^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}},\quad C:=\beta\frac{\alpha^{2}\bar{\sigma}^{2}-\sigma^{2}}{\alpha\bar{\sigma}^{2}}.

Note that, to compute
L_{T}
, it suffices to have the conditional density of
\tilde{X}_{t}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1
given
\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}

x=0,1

the conditional density of
\tilde{X}_{t}\,\mathrm{mod}\,1

\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}

Next, we need to approximate

To avoid stability issues and keep the implementation simple, we use the operator splitting method to approximate

\displaystyle du=\left[\frac{1}{2}A^{2}\partial^{2}_{xx}u-C\partial_{x}\left(\mu(x+\kappa Y_{t})u\right)\right]dt,

\displaystyle du=\frac{\beta}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\mu(x+\kappa Y_{t})udY_{t},

(45)

Equation (

\displaystyle G^{\Delta t}_{2}[u_{0}](x):=u_{0}(x)\exp\left[-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mu^{2}(x+\kappa Y_{t})dt+\frac{\beta}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mu(x+\kappa Y_{t})dY_{t}\right],

To approximate the solution to (

\displaystyle G^{\Delta t,Y_{t}}_{1}[u_{0}](x):=\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{K}^{\Delta t}\left(\left|z-b(x,\Delta t,Y_{t})\right|\right)u_{0}\left(z\right)dz,

The proposed approximation of

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=T/\Delta t-1}^{0}G^{\Delta t}_{2}G^{\Delta t,Y_{i\Delta t}}_{1}\right)[u_{0}],

Recalling (

\displaystyle\mu^{\theta}_{t}=\tilde{\mathbb{E}}^{\theta}\left[\mu\left(\kappa Y_{t}+\tilde{X}_{t}\right)\,|\,\mathcal{F}^{Y}_{t}\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\mu(\kappa Y_{t}+x)u(t,x)dx,

(46)

and compute

Once

The computation of
