A derivation of first variation formulas from the strain-displacement relations in thin shell theory
Yoshiki Jikumaru

Abstract
. In this paper, we derive the first variation formulas for surfaces in 3-dimensional Euclidean space by using the “strain-displacement relations” known in thin shell theory. For applications to architectural surface design, we focus on the objective function which has linear Weingarten surfaces as stationary points. This article aims to provide an elementary and cross-disciplinary exposition for applications without any tensor calculus, and thus, we do not give any new mathematical results.



1.
Introduction

In recent years, the application of differential geometry in architectural surface design has been actively studied, forming a large field called
architectural geometry
.
This paper focuses on linear Weingarten surfaces, one of the important surface classes in architectural surface design, from the viewpoint of the variational principle.
The mechanical properties of linear Weingarten surfaces in the context of shell membrane theory can be found in
[
7
,
10
]
.
The energy functional which has linear Weingarten surfaces as stationary points and related studies are classically well known, see for example
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
6
,
8
,
9
]
and the references therein.
Thus, this paper does not provide any new mathematical results.
However, it is essential to create a cross-disciplinary environment in which results in mathematics can be applied to architectural surface design.
Therefore, this paper is written to be accessible to structural engineering researchers with basic knowledge of differential geometry of surfaces, avoiding abstract Riemannian geometry or tensor calculus, and completing calculations as elementary as possible.
In the calculations, we show how the strain-displacement relation found in standard textbooks on classical thin shell theory can be used to derive the first variation formulas.

2.
Preliminaries

In the theory of continuum mechanics, thin shells can be studied using classical differential geometry of surfaces by considering the middle surface of the shell as a smooth surface in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space.
For basic terminologies, see for example
[
3
,
5
,
7
]
.
In this paper, we use the symbols in
[
7
,
10
]
.

Let us consider a surface
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}(\alpha,\beta)
in the 3-dimensional Euclidean space parametrized by the curvature line coordinates
(\alpha,\beta)
,
that is, the first and second fundamental forms of the surface are diagonalized.
We denote the first fundamental form
I
by

(1)

I=A_{1}^{2}\,d\alpha^{2}+A_{2}^{2}\,d\beta^{2},\quad A_{1}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha},\quad A_{2}^{2}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta},

where the subscripts represent the partial derivatives.
Let
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}
be the unit tangent vectors on the surface defined by the following relations:

(2)

\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}=A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}=A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}.

Since the second fundamental form is also diagonalized, we have
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha\beta}\perp\mbox{\boldmath$N$}
, where
N
is the unit normal vector on the surface.
A direct calculation shows that the integrability condition
(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha})_{\beta}=(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})_{\alpha}
for the linear system (
2
) is given by

(3)

(A_{1})_{\beta}=pA_{2},\quad(A_{2})_{\alpha}=qA_{1},\quad(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})_{\beta}=q\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\quad(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})_{\alpha}=p\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},

where the first and second equations define
p
and
q
.
Since
N
is the unit vector, we have the relations

(4)

\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}=H_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}=K_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},

for some scalar values
H_{\circ}
and
K_{\circ}
.
Then, the
principal curvatures

\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}
and the
principal curvature radii

R_{1},R_{2}
are defined as follows:

(5)

H_{\circ}=-\kappa_{1}A_{1}=\frac{A_{1}}{R_{1}},\quad K_{\circ}=-\kappa_{2}A_{2}=\frac{A_{2}}{R_{2}}.

In particular, we have

(6)

\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}=-\kappa_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}=-\kappa_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}.

This formula is called the
Weingarten formula
or
Rodrigues formula
.
With these notations, the
Gauss formula
is given by

(7)

(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})_{\alpha}=-p\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-H_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\quad(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})_{\alpha}=p\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\quad(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})_{\beta}=q\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\quad(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})_{\beta}=-q\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}-K_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$},

Or equivalently, we have the
Gauss-Weingarten formula
in the matrix form:

(8)

\displaystyle(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$N$})_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\begin{pmatrix}0&p&H_{\circ}\\
-p&0&0\\
-H_{\circ}&0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$N$})_{\beta}

\displaystyle=(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\begin{pmatrix}0&-q&0\\
q&0&K_{\circ}\\
0&-K_{\circ}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The integrability condition for the Gauss-Weingarten system is called the
Gauss-Mainardi-Codazzi equations
, which can be represented as follows:

(9)

p_{\beta}+q_{\alpha}+H_{\circ}K_{\circ}=0,\quad(H_{\circ})_{\beta}=pK_{\circ},\quad(K_{\circ})_{\alpha}=qH_{\circ}.

3.
The strain-displacement relations

If we consider the surface
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}(\alpha,\beta)
as the middle surface of a thin shell model, then we can consider the deformation of the middle surface as follows:

(10)

\mbox{\boldmath$R$}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+\Delta,\quad\Delta=u\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+v\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+w\mbox{\boldmath$N$},

where
\Delta
is called the
displacement vector
.
For the deformed surface
R
, we have the following relations:

(11)

\frac{1}{A_{1}}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}=(1+\varepsilon_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\omega_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\vartheta\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\quad\frac{1}{A_{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}=\omega_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(1+\varepsilon_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\psi\mbox{\boldmath$N$},

where

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varepsilon_{1}&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}(u_{\alpha}+pv+H_{\circ}w),\quad\varepsilon_{2}=\frac{1}{A_{2}}(v_{\beta}+qu+K_{\circ}w),\\
\omega_{1}&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}(v_{\alpha}-pu),\quad\omega_{2}=\frac{1}{A_{2}}(u_{\beta}-qv),\\
\vartheta&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}(-w_{\alpha}+H_{\circ}u),\quad\psi=\frac{1}{A_{2}}(-w_{\beta}+K_{\circ}v).\end{aligned}

The above relations are called the
strain-displacement relations
.
Here,
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are called the
normal strains
, and
\omega=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
is called the
shear strain
.

Next, we introduce the “infinitesimal” version of the strain-displacement relations.
We replace the displacement vector
\Delta
with the variation vector
V
:

(13)

\mbox{\boldmath$R$}=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+t\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$V$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$V$}=v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\quad|t|\ll 1,

where
t
is the deformation parameter.
For convenience, we decompose the variation vector
V
to the tangential component
\eta
and the normal component as follows:

(14)

\mbox{\boldmath$V$}=\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$N$},\quad\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}=v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}.

In the same way as before, we introduce the following symbols:

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overline{\varepsilon}_{1}&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}+pv_{2}+H_{\circ}v_{n}),\quad\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}=\frac{1}{A_{2}}((v_{2})_{\beta}+qv_{1}+K_{\circ}v_{n}),\\
\overline{\omega}_{1}&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((v_{2})_{\alpha}-pv_{1}),\quad\overline{\omega}_{2}=\frac{1}{A_{2}}((v_{1})_{\beta}-qv_{2}),\\
\overline{\vartheta}&=\frac{1}{A_{1}}(-(v_{n})_{\alpha}+H_{\circ}v_{1}),\quad\overline{\psi}=\frac{1}{A_{2}}(-(v_{n})_{\beta}+K_{\circ}v_{2}).\end{aligned}

In the following, we will refer to these relations as the
infinitesimal strain-displacement relations
.

4.
The first variation of the area

Let
M
be a
2
-dimensional manifold with boundary
\partial M
and
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth immersion.
Then the area of the immersion can be defined as follows:

(16)

A=\int_{M}dA,

where the area element
dA
is defined by
dA=\|\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}\|\,d\alpha d\beta
in the local coordinates
(\alpha,\beta)
.
We denote the first fundamental form
I^{\prime}
of the deformed surface
R
as follows:

(17)

I^{\prime}=A_{1}^{\prime 2}\,d\alpha^{2}+A_{2}^{\prime 2}\,d\beta^{2},\quad A_{1}^{\prime 2}=\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha},\quad A_{2}^{\prime 2}=\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}.

Let
dA^{\prime}
be the area element of
R
.

Lemma 4.1
.

The first variation of the area element can be written as the summation of the infinitesimal normal strains:

(18)

\delta dA=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=0}dA=(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})\,dA.

Proof.

By using the relation (
11
), we have

(19)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}(\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta})=\vartheta\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\psi\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+(1+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}).

Therefore we have

(20)

\displaystyle dA^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\|\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}\|\,d\alpha d\beta

\displaystyle=(\vartheta^{2}+\psi^{2}+(1+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})^{2})^{1/2}\,A_{1}A_{2}\,d\alpha d\beta+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{2}(2(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})+(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})^{2}+\vartheta^{2}+\psi^{2})\right)dA+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\,dA+O(t^{2}).

Here if we replace
(u,v,w)
with
(tv_{1},tv_{2},tv_{n})
, then we have

(21)

dA^{\prime}=dA+t(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})\,dA+O(t^{2})\iff\frac{dA^{\prime}-dA}{t}=(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})\,dA+O(t),

which proves the statement.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

The summation of the infinitesimal normal strains is given by

(22)

\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}={\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}v_{n},\quad\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}),

where
\mathcal{H}
is the mean curvature of the middle surface
r
.

Proof.

Note that the divergence of
\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}=v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}
is defined by

(23)

{\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}\left((v_{1}A_{2})_{\alpha}+(v_{2}A_{1})_{\beta}\right).

By using the relations
(A_{1})_{\beta}=pA_{2}
,
(A_{2})_{\alpha}=qA_{1}
, we have

(24)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}A_{2}+v_{1}qA_{1}+(v_{2})_{\beta}A_{1}+v_{2}pA_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}+pv_{2})+\frac{1}{A_{2}}((v_{2})_{\beta}+qv_{1}).

Since
H_{\circ}=-\kappa_{1}A_{1}
,
K_{\circ}=-\kappa_{2}A_{2}
, we have

(25)

\displaystyle\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}+pv_{2}+H_{\circ}v_{n})+\frac{1}{A_{2}}((v_{2})_{\beta}+qv_{1}+K_{\circ}v_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}+pv_{2})+\frac{1}{A_{2}}((v_{2})_{\beta}+qv_{1})-(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})v_{n}

\displaystyle={\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}v_{n}.

This proves the statement.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

The first variation of the area is given by

(26)

\delta\int_{M}dA=-2\int_{M}\mathcal{H}(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\,dA+\int_{\partial M}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds,

where
t
is the outward-pointing unit conormal vector and
ds
is the line element of the boundary
\partial M
, respectively.

Proof.

The divergence theorem on the surface

(27)

\int_{M}{\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\,dA=\int_{\partial M}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds,

immediately shows the statement.
∎

5.
The first variation of the volume

The (algebraic) volume
V
enclosed by the surface
r
is defined by

(28)

V=\frac{1}{3}\int_{M}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\,dA,

where the integrand represents the volume of the “infinitesimal cone”.
In the local coordinates
(\alpha,\beta)
, the integrand can be written as

(29)

(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\,dA=\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})\,d\alpha d\beta,

To derive the first variation formula of the volume, we will show two lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

The following relation holds

(30)

{\rm div}\,(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}-(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$N$})=2(1+\mathcal{H}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})).

Proof.

Using the Gauss formula, we have

(31)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle div}\,(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}-(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$N$})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})_{\alpha}A_{2}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})qA_{1}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})_{\beta}A_{1}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})pA_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}(A_{1}A_{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(-p\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-H_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$})A_{2}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1})qA_{1}

\displaystyle+A_{2}A_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(-q\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}-K_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$})A_{1}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})pA_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}(2A_{1}A_{2}-(H_{\circ}A_{2}+K_{\circ}A_{1})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$}))

\displaystyle=2+(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})=2(1+\mathcal{H}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

In the local coordinates
(\alpha,\beta)
, the following relation holds

(32)

\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})\,d\alpha d\beta=({\rm div}\,(-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})+2v_{n})\,dA.

Proof.

We first show the following formula:

(33)

\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})=-A_{1}A_{2}\nabla v_{n}+({\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}v_{n})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})+v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}+v_{2}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}.

Using the equation (
2
) and
\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}=A_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}
, we have

(34)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}=t\overline{\vartheta}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}+t\overline{\psi}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}+t(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}),

\displaystyle\iff\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$R$}_{\beta}-\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}}{t}=\overline{\vartheta}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}+\overline{\psi}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}+(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}).

Therefore we have

(35)

\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})=\overline{\vartheta}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}+\overline{\psi}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}+(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}).

Since

(36)

\displaystyle\nabla v_{n}=\frac{(v_{n})_{\alpha}}{A_{1}}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\frac{(v_{n})_{\beta}}{A_{2}}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}=(-\overline{\vartheta}-\kappa_{1}v_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(-\overline{\psi}-\kappa_{2}v_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},

\displaystyle\iff A_{1}A_{2}\nabla v_{n}=-\overline{\vartheta}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}-\overline{\psi}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}+v_{1}A_{2}(-\kappa_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha})+v_{2}A_{1}(-\kappa_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}),

the Weingarten formula (
6
) shows

(37)

\overline{\vartheta}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}+\overline{\psi}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}=-A_{1}A_{2}\nabla v_{n}+v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}+v_{2}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}.

This proves the equation (
33
).
Next, we show the equation:

(38)

\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}+v_{2}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta})=A_{1}A_{2}\nabla(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}.

A direct calculation shows

(39)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}+v_{2}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=(v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})_{\alpha}-(v_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$})_{\alpha}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+(v_{2}A_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$r$})_{\beta}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$}

\displaystyle=A_{1}A_{2}{\rm div}\,((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})-((v_{1}A_{2})_{\alpha}+(v_{2}A_{1})_{\beta})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})

\displaystyle=A_{1}A_{2}({\rm div}\,((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})-({\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$}))=A_{1}A_{2}(\nabla(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}),

which proves the equation (
38
).
Combining the equations (
33
) and (
38
), we conclude

(40)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=-A_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\nabla v_{n}+({\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}v_{n})\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})

\displaystyle+A_{1}A_{2}{\rm div}\,((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})-({\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})

\displaystyle=-A_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\nabla v_{n}-(2\mathcal{H}(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$}))A_{1}A_{2}v_{n}+A_{1}A_{2}{\rm div}\,((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})

\displaystyle=-A_{1}A_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\nabla v_{n}-({\rm div}\,(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}-(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$N$})-2)A_{1}A_{2}v_{n}+A_{1}A_{2}{\rm div}\,((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})

\displaystyle=A_{1}A_{2}({\rm div}\,(-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})+2v_{n}).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 5.3
.

The first variation of the volume
V
is given by

(41)

\delta V=\int_{M}v_{n}\,dA+\frac{1}{3}\int_{\partial M}((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
5.1
and
5.2
that we have

(42)

\displaystyle\delta((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\,dA)

\displaystyle=\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})\,d\alpha d\beta)

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$V$}\cdot(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta})+\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\delta(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\alpha}\times\mbox{\boldmath$r$}_{\beta}))\,d\alpha d\beta

\displaystyle=(3v_{n}+{\rm div}\,(-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}))\,dA,

in the local coordinates
(\alpha,\beta)
.
By using the divergence theorem, we have

(43)

\displaystyle\delta V

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\int_{M}(3v_{n}+{\rm div}\,(-(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}))\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{M}v_{n}\,dA+\frac{1}{3}\int_{M}{\rm div}\,(-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{M}v_{n}\,dA+\frac{1}{3}\int_{\partial M}(-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$}+(\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$})\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds

which proves the statement.
∎

6.
The first variation of the mean curvature

Lemma 6.1
.

The first variations of the principal curvatures are given by

(44)

\delta\kappa_{1}=-\frac{\overline{\vartheta}_{\alpha}+p\overline{\psi}}{A_{1}}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{1},\quad\delta\kappa_{2}=-\frac{\overline{\psi}_{\beta}+q\overline{\vartheta}}{A_{2}}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{2}.

Proof.

The frame
(\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$N$}^{\prime})
on the deformed surface
R
is given by (see
[
7
]
, p.17):

(45)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\omega_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\vartheta\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\psi\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$N$}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\vartheta\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\psi\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}+\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}).

The principal curvatures
\kappa_{1}^{\prime},\kappa_{2}^{\prime}
for the deformed surface
R
can be computed by

(46)

\kappa_{1}^{\prime}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}^{\prime}}{A_{1}^{\prime}},\quad\kappa_{2}^{\prime}=-\frac{\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}^{\prime}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}^{\prime}}{A_{2}^{\prime}}.

By using the Gauss-Weingarten formula, we have

(47)

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi+H_{\circ})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(\psi_{\alpha}-p\vartheta)\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\vartheta H_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}),

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\vartheta_{\beta}-q\psi)\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta+K_{\circ})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}-\psi K_{\circ}\mbox{\boldmath$N$}+O(t^{2}).

Then we have

(48)

\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\alpha}^{\prime}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}^{\prime}=\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi+H_{\circ}+O(t^{2}),\quad\mbox{\boldmath$N$}_{\beta}^{\prime}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2}^{\prime}=\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta+K_{\circ}+O(t^{2}).

Therefore

(49)

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}^{\prime}=-\frac{\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi+H_{\circ}}{A_{1}(1+\varepsilon_{1})}+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi+H_{\circ}}{A_{1}}(1-\varepsilon_{1})+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi+H_{\circ}}{A_{1}}+\varepsilon_{1}\frac{H_{\circ}}{A_{1}}+O(t^{2}),

\displaystyle\kappa_{2}^{\prime}=-\frac{\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta+K_{\circ}}{A_{2}(1+\varepsilon_{2})}+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta+K_{\circ}}{A_{2}}(1-\varepsilon_{2})+O(t^{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta+K_{\circ}}{A_{2}}+\varepsilon_{2}\frac{K_{\circ}}{A_{2}}+O(t^{2}).

Or equivalently,

(50)

\kappa_{1}^{\prime}-\kappa_{1}=-\frac{\vartheta_{\alpha}+p\psi}{A_{1}}-\varepsilon_{1}\kappa_{1}+O(t^{2}),\quad\kappa_{2}^{\prime}-\kappa_{2}=-\frac{\psi_{\beta}+q\vartheta}{A_{2}}-\varepsilon_{2}\kappa_{2}+O(t^{2}).

The statement follows from the replacement
(u,v,w)\to(tv_{1},tv_{2},tv_{n})
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

The first variation of the mean curvature is given by

(51)

\delta\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm div}\,\nabla v_{n}+\nabla\mathcal{H}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+(2\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K})v_{n},

where
\mathcal{K}=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
is the Gaussian curvature.

Proof.

If we note the relation

(52)

\nabla v_{n}=(-\overline{\vartheta}-\kappa_{1}v_{1})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+(-\overline{\psi}-\kappa_{2}v_{2})\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2},

then we have

(53)

\displaystyle\delta(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\overline{\vartheta}_{\alpha}+p\overline{\psi}}{A_{1}}-\frac{\overline{\psi}_{\beta}+q\overline{\vartheta}}{A_{2}}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=-{\rm div}\,(\overline{\vartheta}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\overline{\psi}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})-(\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2})(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})+\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle={\rm div}\,(\nabla v_{n}+\kappa_{1}v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\kappa_{2}v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})-2\mathcal{H}({\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}v_{n})+\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle={\rm div}\,(\nabla v_{n}+2\mathcal{H}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-\kappa_{2}v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}-\kappa_{1}v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})-2\mathcal{H}{\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+4\mathcal{H}^{2}v_{n}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle={\rm div}\,\nabla v_{n}+2\nabla\mathcal{H}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+4\mathcal{H}^{2}v_{n}-{\rm div}\,(\kappa_{2}v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\kappa_{1}v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})+\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}+\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}.

If we show the equation

(54)

{\rm div}\,(\kappa_{2}v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\kappa_{1}v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})-\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}=2\mathcal{K}v_{n},

then

(55)

\delta(\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2})={\rm div}\,\nabla v_{n}+2\nabla\mathcal{H}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+(4\mathcal{H}^{2}-2\mathcal{K})v_{n},

which proves the statement.
Applying the Mainardi-Codazzi equations, we have

(56)

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle div}\,(\kappa_{2}v_{1}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{1}+\kappa_{1}v_{2}\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{2})-\overline{\varepsilon}_{1}\kappa_{2}-\overline{\varepsilon}_{2}\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}}((\kappa_{2}v_{1})_{\alpha}+p\kappa_{1}v_{2})+\frac{1}{A_{2}}((\kappa_{1}v_{2})_{\beta}+q\kappa_{2}v_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa_{2}}{A_{1}}((v_{1})_{\alpha}+pv_{2}-\kappa_{1}A_{1}v_{n})-\frac{\kappa_{1}}{A_{2}}((v_{2})_{\beta}+qv_{1}-\kappa_{2}A_{2}v_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}((\kappa_{2})_{\alpha}A_{2}v_{1}+p(K_{\circ}+\kappa_{1}A_{2})v_{2}+(\kappa_{1})_{\beta}A_{1}v_{2}+q(H_{\circ}+\kappa_{2}A_{1})v_{1})+2\mathcal{K}v_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{1}A_{2}}((qH_{\circ}-(K_{\circ})_{\alpha})v_{1}+(pK_{\circ}-(H_{\circ})_{\beta})v_{2})+2\mathcal{K}v_{n}=2\mathcal{K}v_{n}.

This shows the equation (
54
) and proves the lemma.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

The first variation of the integral of the mean curvature is given by

(57)

\delta\int_{M}\mathcal{H}\,dA=-\int_{M}\mathcal{K}v_{n}\,dA+\int_{\partial M}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{n}+\mathcal{H}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds.

Proof.

It follows from Lemma
6.3
and the divergence theorem that we have

(58)

\displaystyle\delta\int_{M}\mathcal{H}\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{M}(\delta\mathcal{H})\,dA+\mathcal{H}\,\delta dA

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\rm div}\,\nabla v_{n}+\nabla\mathcal{H}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+(2\mathcal{H}^{2}-\mathcal{K})v_{n}+\mathcal{H}{\rm div}\,\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-2\mathcal{H}^{2}v_{n}\right)\,dA

\displaystyle=\int_{M}\left({\rm div}\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{n}+\mathcal{H}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\right)-\mathcal{K}v_{n}\right)\,dA

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}\mathcal{K}v_{n}\,dA+\int\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{n}+\mathcal{H}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\right)\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds.

∎

4.3
,
5.3
and
6.4
, we have the following result:

Theorem 6.5
.

For constants
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the functional
E
as follows:

(59)

E=\int_{M}(a\mathcal{H}+b)\,dA+cV.

Then the first variation formula is given by

(60)

\displaystyle\delta E

\displaystyle=-\int_{M}(a\mathcal{K}+2b\mathcal{H}-c)v_{n}\,dA

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial M}\left[a\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla v_{n}+\mathcal{H}\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}\right)+b\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}+\frac{c}{3}((\mbox{\boldmath$r$}\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$N$})\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}-v_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$r$})\right]\cdot\mbox{\boldmath$t$}\,ds.

In particular, the equilibrium condition away from the boundary is given by

(61)

a\mathcal{K}+2b\mathcal{H}=c,

that is, we have the linear Weingarten condition.
