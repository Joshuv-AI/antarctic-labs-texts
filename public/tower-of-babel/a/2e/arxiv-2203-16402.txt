A Unified Cosmological Dark Sector from a Bose-Einstein Condensate Corresponding Author. Email: sourav.sur@gmail.com, sourav@physics.du.ac.in
Saurya Das

Abstract
We examine the viability of cosmological solution(s) describing a unified picture of the dark side of the universe from a Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) of light bosons. The energy density of the BEC, together with its quantum potential, can indeed account for such a unification, in the sense that the (dust-like) cold dark matter and the dark energy components emerge from the same source. In particular, the bulk of the dark energy can be attributed to the quantum potential, in the quantum corrected Raychaudhuri-Friedmann equation, when the ‘macroscopic’ BEC wave-function is taken to be such that the corresponding probability density is construed as the energy density of the dusty fluid. However, there arises a purely quantum mechanical back-reaction, of even the visible baryons, on the effective dark energy and dark matter contents, which crucially determines the mass of the BEC. We determine the constraint on such a back-reaction, and hence on the BEC mass, from physical considerations, as well as estimate the same using recent observational data.



Keywords:
Bose-Einstein condensate, dark energy, cold dark matter, cosmology of theories beyond the SM, quantum Raychaudhuri equation.

2
Cosmology with a BEC: the General Formalism

Let us resort to the formal aspects of the standard (homogeneous and
isotropic) FRW cosmology, by first referring to the usual perfect fluid
description, in terms of the fluid velocity
u^{\alpha}
, the energy density

\rho
and pressure
p
. In principle, this may be realized by a
collection of perfect fluids as well, in which case their individual
energy densities and pressures would sum up to give
\rho
and
p

respectively. The classical general relativistic principles imply that
the cosmic evolution is governed by the Friedmann equations, which,
under the assumption of spatial flatness, read as

\displaystyle H^{2}(t)=\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{3}\,\rho(t)\,\,,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\dot{H}(t)+\,H^{2}(t)=\,-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\Big[\rho(t)+\,3p(t)\Big]\,,

(2.2)

where
\kappa^{2}=8\pi G
, and
H(t)=\dot{a}(t)/a(t)
is the Hubble
parameter, with
a(t)
being the FRW scale factor, and the overhead
dot
\{\cdot\}
denoting derivative with respect to the cosmic time
t
.

Nevertheless, what would happen when a constituent fluid is inherently
quantum in nature, say for e.g. a condensate of bosonic particles (i.e.
a BEC) of rest mass
m
? The answer to this could be revealed from the
explicit computation of the quantum corrections to the Raychaudhuri
equation carried out by one of us (SD) in an earlier work

[
67
]
,
by considering the Bohmian (quantal) particle trajectories

[
68
,
69
,
70
]
,
which are relevant in this case, in lieu of classical geodesics, as the
bosons in the BEC are themselves quantum particles.
In particular, taking the ‘quantum’ fluid (or the BEC) wave-function in
the generic form

\Psi(\vec{\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\,\mathcal{R}(\vec{\boldsymbol{x}},t)\,e^{i\,S(\vec{\boldsymbol{x}},t)}\,,\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\mathcal{R},S\in\mathbb{R}\,\,,

(2.3)

the modified (or, quantum corrected) Raychaudhuri equation, describing
the flow of the quantal trajectories parametrized by an affine
parameter
\lambda
, is shown to be

\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda}=\,-\,\frac{\theta^{2}}{3}-\,R_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\,\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\,h^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{\square\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)_{\!;\mu\,;\nu}\,\,,

(2.4)

where
R_{\mu\nu}
is the Ricci tensor,
\square\mathcal{R}\equiv\mathcal{R}^{;\alpha}_{~;\alpha}\,
, and
\theta=h^{\mu\nu}u_{\mu;\nu}
is the expansion
scalar, with
\,h_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}+u_{\mu}u_{\nu}\,
denoting the
induced metric (or the projection tensor onto the hyperplanes
orthogonal to
u^{\mu}
). Of course, the shear and vorticity terms are
ignored here, as they have no relevance to the homogeneous-isotropic
cosmology. Hence, with the usual identifications

\theta=\,3H\quad\mbox{and}\quad R_{\mu\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\nu}=\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\big(\rho+\,p\big)\,,

(2.5)

the above equation (
2.4
) reduces to

\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}\equiv\,\dot{H}+\,H^{2}=\,-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\Big(\rho+\,3p\Big)+\,\frac{1}{3}\,U_{{}_{Q}}\,\,,

(2.6)

where the quantity

U_{{}_{Q}}=\,\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\,h^{\mu\nu}\,\left(\frac{\square\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{R}}\right)_{\!;\mu\,;\nu}\,\,,

(2.7)

is interpreted as a
quantum potential

[
68
,
69
,
70
,
67
]
.

2.1
The semi-classical dark fluid description

It is natural to identify Eq. (
2.6
) as the semi-classical version of the second order Friedmann equation (
2.2
), simply under the replacement

\,p\rightarrow p-2U_{{}_{Q}}/(3\kappa^{2})
.
However, one needs to be cautious in making physical interpretations out of such a replacement. In fact, marking the

U_{{}_{Q}}
-term
solely as the effective pressure of the quantum fluid is not quite tenable,
because there is in principle nothing that can prevent the potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
to make a contribution to the system’s total energy density
\rho
as well. After all, even in a semi-classical approach, the compatibility of the Raychaudhuri equation with the Einstein’s equations implies that the conservation relation

\dot{\rho}=\,-\,3\,H\left(\rho+\,p\right)\,,

(2.8)

is not independent of the cosmic evolution equations, which must therefore be the Friedmann equations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). In other words, one may reckon the semi-classical cosmic evolution to be described by Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), despite the following:

•

The total energy density
\rho
is not simply given by that of the classical fluid matter content (the visible baryons, background radiation, etc.) plus that of the BEC (perceived semi-classically as the corresponding probability density
|\Psi|^{2}=|\mathcal{R}|^{2}
).

•

The total pressure
p
is not just the classical fluid pressure (if any)
augmented with
\,-2U_{{}_{Q}}/(3\kappa^{2})
.

As such, a
unified
cosmic dark sector can emerge naturally,
since by all means, the (quantum) fluid description of the BEC and
the associated quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
can determine the evolution
profile of a
dark fluid
, that supposedly encodes the
effective DE and DM components of the universe, and interaction(s)
thereof. In other words, the dark fluid can be thought of as
having energy density given by
\rho
minus the classical fluid
density, and pressure given by
p
minus the classical fluid
pressure (if any). Therefore, if the classical fluid obeys the
energy-momentum conservation law, so would the dark fluid,
regardless of its constituents – the effective DE and DM –
not being conserved.

2.2
Criteria and assertions for a consistent picture

Let us enlist the criteria, and subsequent assertions for a
concrete BEC cosmological formulation, particularly from the
point of view of the dark fluid description:

•

The foremost criterion is, of course, the large scale isotropy
of the universe, which requires the embedded BEC wave-function to be
spherically symmetric, i.e.
\Psi(\vec{\boldsymbol{x}},t)\equiv\Psi(r,t)\,
, where
the variable

1

Note that

However, we are leaving this for a future work, and focus only on the background cosmological evolution herein.

Next comes the question of suggesting the form of
\Psi(r,t)

which can be recast in the form of that of a harmonic oscillator,

\ddot{r}(t)+\,\omega^{2}(t)\,r(t)=\,0\,\,,

\omega(t)=\,\frac{4\pi\,G}{3}\,\rho^{(B)}(t)\,\,.

(2.11)

Effective Scenarios with a Unified Cosmic Dark Sector

Since our interest in this paper is in the late-time cosmological evolution in
the standard FRW framework, we resort to a scenario in which the universe is
assumed to be constituted only by the BEC, which emulates a dust-like fluid of
energy density given by Eq. (
2.13
), and the visible matter, also in the form of a dust (of baryons) of energy density

\rho^{(b)}(t)=\,\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}\,a^{-3}(t)\,\,,

(3.16)

where
\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}
denotes the corresponding value at the present epoch (
t=t_{{}_{0}}
,
whence
a=1
).

3.1
BEC cosmological equations

Eqs. (
2.13
) and (
3.16
) imply that the Raychaudhuri equation
(
2.6
) can be written as

\frac{\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)}=\,-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}\big[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\big]}{6\,a^{3}(t)}+\,\frac{1}{3}\,U_{{}_{Q}}(t)\,\,.

(3.17)

While this is identified as the second Friedmann equation (
2.2
) in the semi-classical picture, we have the total energy density of the universe
expressed as

\rho\,(t)=\,\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}(t)}-\,\frac{2}{\kappa^{2}}\,U_{{}_{Q}}(t)-\,3p\,(t)\,\,.

(3.18)

Note that
U_{{}_{Q}}(t)
is the background (or homogeneous) level
quantum potential, mentioned above, i.e. the homogeneous part
of the expression (
2.7
).
For the BEC wave-function (
2.12
), with the wave amplitude

R(t)
given by Eq. (
2.15
), this turns out to be

U_{{}_{Q}}(t)=\,\frac{24\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}\sigma^{4}}\left[1+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{12}\left\{\Big[1+\frac{3\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{8}\,a(t)\,p^{\prime}(t)\Big]\rho(t)-3p(t)\right\}\right]\,,

(3.19)

where the prime
\{^{\prime}\}
denotes
d/da
(see ref.

[
32
]

for the detailed steps in the computation).

In fact, rather conveniently, one may resort to the following
form of the quantum potential, obtained as a function of the
scale factor
a
, by substituting Eq. (
3.18
) in
Eq. (
3.19
) and re-arranging the terms:

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\frac{6\,\alpha^{2}}{1+\,\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\,f(a)}\bigg[1\,+\,\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{12}\bigg\{\left[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right]\frac{f(a)}{a^{3}}-\,3\left[1+f(a)\right]p(a)\bigg\}\bigg]\,,

(3.20)

where

\alpha=\,\frac{2\hbar}{m\sigma^{2}}\,,

(3.21)

and

f(a):=\,1+\,\frac{3\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\,a\,p^{\prime}(a)}{8}

(3.22)

are, respectively, a constant of dimension
\kappa^{-1}
, and a dimensionless function of
a
.

On the other hand, substituting Eq. (
3.18
) in the conservation
relation (
2.8
) one derives the equation

\frac{2}{a}\,\Big[a^{3}U_{{}_{Q}}(a)\Big]^{\prime}+\,3\kappa^{2}\Big[a^{2}p(a)\Big]^{\prime}=\,0\,,

(3.23)

which requires to be solved simultaneously with Eq. (
3.20
) in
order to determine the Hubble rate
H(a)
, and hence the cosmic expansion
history.
This is nevertheless a difficult proposition in general, since the potential

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
in Eq. (
3.20
) is given in terms of both
p(a)
and
p^{\prime}(a)
,
as is evident from the definition (
3.22
) of the function
f(a)
.
Substituting for
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
in the first order differential equation
(
3.23
), one therefore finds a high degree of non-linearity of the
resulting higher order differential equation for
p(a)
.

3.2
Exact solution describing an effective
\Lambda
CDM evolution

Quite remarkably, however, an exact solution can be obtained simply by
resorting to the ansatz

p=\,-\,\Lambda\,\,,

(3.24)

where
\Lambda
is a (hitherto unspecified) constant

[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
Such an ansatz is of course legitimate, and more importantly, the simplest possible one that renders the function
f
in Eq. (
3.22
) just a constant (
=1
), and thereby leads to a great deal of technical simplification while looking for a cosmological solution, in the sense that Eq. (
3.20
) becomes an algebraic equation, instead of being a differential equation when
p^{\prime}\neq 0
. Consequently, there is no non-linearity in Eq. (
3.23
) when Eq. (
3.20
) is substituted in it, and this really works !
One can easily verify that the corresponding cosmological solution is the one that describes an effective
\Lambda
CDM evolution, since the total energy density of the universe, given by Eq. (
3.18
), gets decomposed as

\rho(a)=\,\rho^{(m)}(a)+\,\Lambda\,,

(3.25)

where

\rho^{(m)}(a)=\,\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}a^{-3}\,,

(3.26)

can be treated as the total dust-like matter density (with value
\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}
at the present epoch), and
\Lambda
as the effective DE density in the form of a cosmological constant.

Now, it is natural to identify the total effective CDM density as

\rho^{(c)}(a)=\,\rho^{(m)}(a)-\,\rho^{(b)}(a)\,,

(3.27)

where
\rho^{(b)}=\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}a^{-3}\,
is the visible (baryonic) matter density. However, quite interestingly, neither
\rho^{(c)}
is equal to the BEC density
\rho^{(B)}=\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}a^{-3}
, nor the cosmological constant
\Lambda
equals the quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
(up to an appropriate dimensional scaling).
To be more specific, the constancy of pressure (
3.24
), which implies
f=1
, makes Eqs. (
3.20
) and (
3.23
) easily solvable, simultaneously, to yield
U_{{}_{Q}}
as the sum of a constant and a term proportional to
a^{-3}
. While the latter contributes to the total dust-like matter density of the universe,
\rho^{(m)}
, the former (i.e. the constant part of
U_{{}_{Q}}
) is equal to
\kappa^{2}\Lambda
, under the stipulation

\Lambda=\,\frac{6\,\kappa^{-2}\alpha^{2}}{1-\,2\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}\,\,.

(3.28)

Explicit computations consequently reveal that
\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}
is not equal to
\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}
, i.e. the sum of the (visible) baryon and BEC densities at the present epoch. Instead, one can express it as

[
32
]

\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}\,=\left(1-\,\varepsilon\right)\left[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right]\,,

(3.29)

upon defining a dimensionless and positive definite constant parameter

\varepsilon\,:=\,\frac{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}{1\,+\,\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}\,=\,\frac{H_{{}_{0}}^{2}\,\sigma^{2}\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}{2\,+\,3H_{{}_{0}}^{2}\,\sigma^{2}\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}\,\,,

(3.30)

where
\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}
is the value of the
\Lambda
-density parameter
\,\Omega^{(\Lambda)}=\Lambda/\rho\,
at the present epoch
3
3

3

Note that the last
equality in Eq. (
3.30
) is obtained by eliminating
\alpha
using
Eq. (
3.28
), and from the definition
\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}=\Lambda/\rho_{{}_{0}}
,
where
\rho_{{}_{0}}^{2}=3H_{0}^{2}/\kappa^{2}
is the critical density at the
present epoch.
.

The BEC density
\rho^{(B)}
is thus not accountable for the effective CDM
density
\rho^{(c)}
in entirety. In fact, from Eqs. (
3.27
) and
(
3.29
) it follows that
\rho^{(c)}
is always less than
\rho^{(B)}
, with
the value at the present epoch determined as

\rho^{(c)}_{{}_{0}}\,\equiv\,\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}-\,\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}=\left(1-\,\varepsilon\right)\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}-\,\varepsilon\,\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}\,\,.

(3.31)

3.3
Quantum back-reaction and the BEC mass bound

The net reduction in
\rho^{(c)}_{{}_{0}}
from
\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}
is certainly a quantum effect, in
the sense that it can be interpreted as the
quantum back-reaction

(QB) of the entire bulk of physical constituents of the universe (i.e. the
BEC and the visible baryons) on the metric structure of space-time, by
virtue of the quantum correction to the Raychaudhuri-Friedmann equation.
The parameter
\varepsilon
(defined above) is therefore dubbed as the QB parameter
in ref.

[
32
]
.
An estimation of this parameter, or at least the assertion of the feasible
range of its values, is crucial for constraining the BEC mass parameter
which can be expressed as

m\,=\,\frac{m_{{}_{H}}}{2\varepsilon}\sqrt{\left(1-\,\varepsilon\right)\left(1-\,3\varepsilon\right)\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}\,\,,

(3.32)

by recalling that
\,\alpha=2\hbar/(m\sigma^{2})\,
and using Eq. (
3.30
).
Here,
\,m_{{}_{H}}=2\sqrt{2}\,\hbar H_{{}_{0}}\,
is regarded as the ‘Hubble value’
of the BEC mass, which one obtains in a Newtonian cosmological formulation,
and under the consideration that all the bosons in the BEC are ultralight and
neutral (for e.g. gravitons), with the Gaussian spread of the corresponding
wave-function taken as
\sigma\simeq H_{{}_{0}}^{-1}
. As the Hubble constant
H_{{}_{0}}\simeq 10^{-42}\,
GeV, in the units
c=1=\hbar
, one gets
m_{{}_{H}}\simeq 10^{-32}\,
eV

[
30
,
31
,
32
]

Now, the parameter
\varepsilon
being positive definite, its feasible range can be obtained from certain physical considerations:

•

Firstly, the positive definiteness of
\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}
, and that of
\rho^{(b)}
and

\rho^{(B)}
, implies
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, as is evident from Eq. (
3.29
).

•

Secondly, the real-valuedness of

, given by Eq. (
3.32
),
restricts the domain further to
\varepsilon\in\left(0,{}^{\scriptstyle{1}}\!\!\diagup_{\!\!\scriptstyle{3}}\right)
.

•

Finally, an even tighter upper bound on
\varepsilon
can be obtained from
the legitimate demand that the BEC density
\rho^{(B)}
must not exceed the total

\rho^{(m)}
at any phase of evolution of the universe. This
amounts to imposing the condition
\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}<\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}\,
at the present epoch

\varepsilon\,<\,\frac{\Omega^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}}{1-\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}+\,\Omega^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}}\,\,,

where
\Omega^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}

[

Note that we have excluded the possibility

On the other hand, the small upper limit of
\varepsilon

implies that

by at least a numerical factor of

However, the viability of the models with ultralight bosonic dark matter

Nevertheless, these mass bounds, which are generally obtained under

may still argue that the small scale crisis for the

\varepsilon
,

However, such an estimation, in an analysis with the commonly used

\Lambda

does not involve the parameter
\varepsilon
explicitly. Hence there is a

\Lambda

\varepsilon

3.4
A unified scenario of dark matter and a dynamical dark energy

Let us recall the expression (
3.20
) for the quantum potential

U_{{}_{Q}}\,
, and re-write it as follows, in terms of the
parameters
\Lambda
and
\varepsilon
defined by Eqs. (
3.28
) and
(
3.30
) respectively:

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\frac{\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)\kappa^{2}}{1-\varepsilon\left[1-f(a)\right]}\bigg[\Lambda+\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)}\bigg\{\left[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right]\frac{f(a)}{a^{3}}-\,3\big[1+f(a)\big]p(a)\bigg\}\bigg]\,,

(3.34)

where

f(a)=\,1+\,\frac{9\varepsilon\,a\,p^{\prime}(a)}{4\left(1-\,3\varepsilon\right)\Lambda}

(3.35)

is the function given by Eq. (
3.22
), re-expressed accordingly.

Plugging Eq. (
3.34
) in the differential equation (
3.23
),
we can in principle solve for the pressure
p\,(a)
, and thereby
determine the expansion history of the universe (
\rho\,(a)
or
H(a)
)
using Eq. (
3.18
). However, given the complicated form of
Eq. (
3.34
), it is very difficult to get the general solution
analytically. Numerical techniques, on the other hand, require
certain fiducial parametric range specifications, i.e. suitable priors
on some of the parameters in the set
\left(\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}},\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}},\Lambda,\varepsilon\right)
,
apart from the appropriate usage of the initial conditions. Such
priors are bound to have some arbitrariness in their selection, and
the numerical solution would be far from being a general solution
under the given circumstances. Nevertheless, deciding on the extent
of the generality is our prerogative, in the sense that we forbid any
large deviation from
\Lambda
CDM, in view of the general observational
concordance on the latter. To be more specific, the general solution,
or any particular solution more general than
\Lambda
CDM, essentially
implies a dynamical evolution of the effective DE component. However,
we demand the dynamics to be
mild
enough, so that the cosmic
parametric estimates do not breach the corresponding
\Lambda
CDM margins
very significantly. Now, with such a demand, we may still reckon the
effectiveness of the quantum back-reaction we have had for the

\Lambda
CDM case in the previous subsection, and the smallness of the
respective parameter
\varepsilon
therein. It then suffices to look for a
solution of the cosmological equations, which may be regarded as the
general solution, but only up to, say, the leading order in the
powers of
\varepsilon
. This is the very reason why we have recast
U_{{}_{Q}}

explicitly in terms of
\varepsilon
and
\Lambda
in Eq. (
3.34
). The
following is the power series expansion for the same:

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\kappa^{2}\left[\Lambda+\,\varepsilon\left\{\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{2a^{3}}\,-\,3\Big[p(a)+\,\Lambda\Big]\right\}+\,\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon^{2}\big)\right]\,.

(3.36)

This is certainly a lot simpler to handle, compared to the full
expression (
3.34
).

Keeping terms up to
\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big)
in
U_{{}_{Q}}
, we solve the
differential equation (
3.23
) to obtain

p(a)\,\approx\,p_{{}_{0}}a^{-2s}-\,\Lambda\Big(1-\,a^{-2s}\Big)\,\,,

(3.37)

where
p_{{}_{0}}=p\big|_{t=t_{{}_{0}}}
is the pressure at the present
epoch, and

s=\,\frac{1-3\varepsilon}{1-2\varepsilon}\,\approx\,1-\varepsilon\,.

(3.38)

Substituting Eq. (
3.37
) back in Eq. (
3.36
),
we get the approximated quantum potential

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)\,\approx\,\kappa^{2}\left[\Lambda+\,\varepsilon\left\{\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{2a^{3}}\,-\,\frac{3\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\,\Lambda\right)}{a^{2s}}\right\}\right]\,,

(3.39)

which when plugged, alongwith Eq. (
3.37

3.18

\rho\,(a)\,\approx\,\frac{\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}\,+\,\Lambda\,-\,\frac{3\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\,\Lambda\right)}{a^{2s}}\,,

(3.40)

where the first term is the total dust-like matter density of the universe, whose value at the present epoch is
\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\left(\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right)\,
[

3.29

3.40

\rho_{\!{}_{X}}(a)
,
whence the corresponding (DE) equation of state (EoS) parameter can be expressed as

\mathsf{w}_{\!{}_{X}}(a)\,:=\,\frac{p\,(a)}{\rho_{\!{}_{X}}(a)}\,\approx\,-\,1\,+\,\frac{2\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\,\Lambda\right)}{3\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\,\Lambda\right)-\,\Lambda a^{2s}}\,.

(3.41)

The overall cosmological evolution is therefore quintessance-like (
\mathsf{w}_{\!{}_{X}}>-1
), as long as
p_{{}_{0}}>-\Lambda

Substituting Eq. (
3.40
) in the Friedmann equation (
2.1
) we have

\left[\frac{H(a)}{H_{{}_{0}}}\right]^{2}\,\approx\,\frac{\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}+\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}-\,\frac{3\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)\left[\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}+\,\mathsf{w}_{{}_{0}}\right]}{a^{2s}}\,,

(3.42)

where
\mathsf{w}_{{}_{0}}
is the value of the total EoS parameter of the system,
\mathsf{w}=p/\rho
, at the present epoch (
a=1
),
\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}
and
\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}
are, respectively, the matter density parameter and the
\Lambda
-density parameter at
a=1
, and
H_{{}_{0}}
is the Hubble constant. In fact, since
H=H_{{}_{0}}
at
a=1
, we have the constraint

\mathsf{w}_{{}_{0}}\approx\,-\,\frac{1-\,\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}+\,2\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}{3\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)}\,.

(3.43)

Using this, Eq. (
3.42
) can be re-expressed as

\left[\frac{H(a)}{H_{{}_{0}}}\right]^{2}\,\approx\,\frac{\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}+\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}+\frac{1-\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}-\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}{a^{2s}}\,,

(3.44)

or more conveniently, as

\left[\frac{H(z)}{H_{{}_{0}}}\right]^{2}\,\approx\,\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}\left(1+z\right)^{3}+\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}+\left[1-\,\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}-\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}\right]\left(1+z\right)^{2\left(1-\varepsilon\right)}\,,

(3.45)

where

s\approx 1-\varepsilon\,
[
cf
. Eq. (
3.38
)].

The crucial point to note here is that the parameter
\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}

\,\Omega^{(X)}_{{}_{0}}:=\left[\rho_{\!{}_{X}}/\rho\right]_{z=0}\,

\,1-\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}

It is also worth pointing out that a full perturbative approach, i.e. the consideration of the series expansion not only of
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
, but also of

and

\varepsilon

\displaystyle U_{{}_{Q}}(a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}\left[\Lambda+\,\varepsilon\left\{\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{2a^{3}}\,-\,\frac{3\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\,\Lambda\right)}{a^{2}}\right\}\right]\,,

(3.46)

\displaystyle p(a)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\,\Lambda+\,\frac{\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\Lambda\right)\left(1+2\varepsilon N\right)}{a^{2}}\,,

(3.47)

\displaystyle\rho\,(a)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}+\,\Lambda-\,\frac{3\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\Lambda\right)\left[1+\,2\varepsilon\left(N-1\right)\right]}{a^{2}}\,,

5
A BEC Dark Universe with a Spatial Curvature

While the parametric estimations in previous section, for the cosmological solution obtained in subsection
3.4
, show a very small upper limit of the BEC mass
m
, the question that naturally arises is
whether it would still be so, if the assumption of spatial flatness is relaxed
. After all, remember that it is the quantum back-reaction parameter
\varepsilon
which is solely responsible for the dynamics of the effective DE component of the universe. However, such a DE dynamics only mildly distorts the
\Lambda
CDM evolution, because of the smallness of the
\varepsilon
that has been demonstrated in subsection
3.3
from purely physical considerations. Therefore, since the smallness of
\varepsilon
holds the key here, it is natural to look for its overall effect without assuming the universe to be spatially flat beforehand. In fact, note that with the spatial flatness assumption, the estimated
\varepsilon
, although small (up to
1\sigma
), is not negligible enough to enhance the BEC mass
m
(which roughly varies as
\varepsilon^{-1}
) from the Hubble value
m_{{}_{H}}
, to a great extent. As such,
m
is left in the range of that for a scalar field DE, which is of course quite contrary to the common consideration of the BEC as a typical DM candidate. It is therefore imperative to examine whether this would still be the case, when the density parameter
\Omega^{(k)}=-k/(a^{2}H^{2})
, corresponding to the spatial curvature constant
k
, is not assumed to be ignorable at any epoch.

On the other hand, given the smallness of
\varepsilon
, the approximate solution (
3.40
) for the total energy density
\rho
, in subsection
3.4
, has the striking feature of distorting
\Lambda
CDM only to the extent of that due to almost an effective spatial curvature (as noticed from the last term therein, which very nearly varies as
a^{-2}
, since the parameter
s\approx 1-\varepsilon\,
[
cf
. Eq. (
3.38
)]). In other words, we almost have an effective
k\Lambda
CDM evolution in the BEC cosmological setup, and that too when
k=0\,
! This is in fact even more clear from the full perturbative solution (
3.40
) for
\rho
, shown at the end of subsection
3.4
. One is therefore urged further to examine the nature of the solutions of the Friedmann equations in such a setup, for a non-vanishing
k
, and see how the latter affects the already very nearly
k\Lambda
CDM evolution found in its absence.

A generalization of the BEC cosmological formalism in sections
2
and
3
, in presence of a non-zero
k
or
\Omega^{(k)}
, is not an easy task though. In fact, given a specific functional form of the BEC wave-function, such as that in Eq. (
2.12
), the computation of the quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
is complicated by itself, as the general form of
U_{{}_{Q}}
, given by Eq. (
2.7
), involves as many as four space-time derivatives — two in the
\square
operator and two outside of it. Nevertheless, for
k=0
, one can have a good deal of simplification in such a computation, as the form of the (spatially flat) FRW line element is simple enough, especially in the Cartesian basis. For
k\neq 0
, the steps in the computation, although complicated, can be made reasonably tractable if, as we shall see below, the Cartesian basis is used as well.

The general (spatially non-flat) FRW line element is expressed in the Cartesian basis as

ds^{2}=\,-\,dt^{2}+\,a^{2}(t)\,\gamma_{ij}(x)\,dx^{i}dx^{j}\,,

(5.51)

with

\gamma_{ij}(x)=\left(1+\frac{kx^{2}}{4}\right)^{-2}\!\delta_{ij}\,\,,\quad\mbox{where $\,\,x=\sqrt{\delta_{ij}\,x^{i}x^{j}}$}\,.

(5.52)

Therefore, while resorting to the BEC wave-function (
2.12
),
it is to be kept in mind that the variable
r
, or the proper radial coordinate distance, now has not only an implicit time-dependence, but also a non-linear dependence on the comoving distance
x
as well:

r(x,t)=\,\sqrt{h_{ij}(x,t)\,x^{i}x^{j}}=\,x\left(1+\frac{kx^{2}}{4}\right)^{-1}a(t)\,.

(5.53)

This obviously makes the computation of the quantum potential very tedious and complicated, in general. However, our task is relatively easier in this paper, as we focus on the study of the background level BEC cosmological evolution, for which it suffices to extract out only the purely time-dependent (or homogeneous) part of the general (
x,t
dependent) quantum potential. Nevertheless, the computation still involves a large number of rigorous mathematical steps, upon carrying out which, and making certain algebraic simplifications, we obtain the following form of the ‘homogeneous’ quantum potential:

U_{{}_{Q}}(t)=\,\frac{3\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}}\left[H(t)\,\dot{Y}(t)\,+\,\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}\left\{F(t)+\,\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}+\,\frac{4k}{a^{2}(t)}\right\}\right]\,,

(5.54)

where

\displaystyle Y(t)=\,\frac{\ddot{R}(t)}{R(t)}+\,\frac{3H(t)\,\dot{R}(t)}{R(t)}\,\,,

(5.55)

\displaystyle F(t)=\,\dot{H}(t)+\,5H^{2}(t)+\,\frac{2H(t)\,\dot{R}(t)}{R(t)}\,\,,

(5.56)

with
R(t)
and
\sigma
being, respectively, the temporal modulating factor and the spread of Gaussian in the BEC wave-function (
2.12
).

The last term of Eq. (
5.54
) shows the spatially non-flat generalization of the expression for
U_{{}_{Q}}(t)
obtained in ref.

[
32
]

(see the Eq. (9) therein). In fact, the generalization is rather profound, as one may notice from the Friedmann equations, which, as illustrated earlier, stand as the cosmological evolution equations in the semi-classical picture as well, and which, for a non-zero
k
, read as:

\displaystyle H^{2}(t)=\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{3}\,\rho(t)-\,\frac{k}{a^{2}(t)}\,\equiv\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{3}\,\widetilde{\rho}(t)\,,

(5.57)

\displaystyle\dot{H}(t)+\,H^{2}(t)=\,-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\Big[\rho(t)+\,3p(t)\Big]\equiv\,-\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\Big[\widetilde{\rho}(t)+\,3\widetilde{p}(t)\Big]\,,

(5.58)

where, for convenience, we have defined

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(t):=\,\rho(t)-\,\frac{3k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}(t)}\,,

(5.59)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(t):=\,p(t)+\,\frac{k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}(t)}\,.

(5.60)

Note that
\widetilde{\rho}(t)
is nothing but the critical density of the universe.

With the stipulated wave amplitude,
\,R(t)=R_{\!{}_{0}}a^{-3/2}(t)\,
[
cf
. Eq. (
2.15
)], we therefore have

\displaystyle Y(t)=\,-\,\frac{3}{2}\left[\dot{H}(t)+\,\frac{3}{2}\,H^{2}(t)\right]=\,\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{4}\,\widetilde{p}(t)\,,

(5.61)

\displaystyle F(t)=\,\dot{H}(t)+\,2H^{2}(t)=\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{6}\Big[\widetilde{\rho}(t)-\,3\widetilde{p}(t)\Big]\,.

(5.62)

Substituting these in Eq. (
5.54
), and using Eq. (
5.57
), we obtain

U_{{}_{Q}}(t)=\,\frac{24\hbar^{2}}{m^{2}\sigma^{4}}\left[1+\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{12}\left\{\Big[1+\frac{3\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{8}\,a(t)\,\widetilde{p}\,^{\prime}(t)\Big]\widetilde{\rho}(t)-3\widetilde{p}(t)\right\}+\,\frac{2k\sigma^{2}}{a^{2}(t)}\right]\,,

(5.63)

where the prime
\{^{\prime}\}
denotes
d/da
. Eq. (
5.63
) is the generalization of Eq. (
3.19
) in the subsection
3.1
.

Now, the Raychaudhuri equation, or the second Friedmann equation (
5.58
), being independent of the value of
k
, we have the same expression (
3.18
) for the total energy density
\rho
, as in subsection
3.1
, in terms of the total pressure
p
, the quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
, and the BEC and baryon densities,
\rho^{(B)}
and
\rho^{(b)}
, given by Eqs. (
2.13
) and (
3.16
) respectively. Since, by definition,
\widetilde{\rho}+3\widetilde{p}=\rho+3p
, Eq. (
3.18
) can be recast as

\widetilde{\rho}\,(t)=\,\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}(t)}-\,\frac{2}{\kappa^{2}}\,U_{{}_{Q}}(t)-\,3\widetilde{p}\,(t)\,.

(5.64)

Using this, we derive from Eq. (
5.63
) the following expression for
U_{{}_{Q}}
as a function of the scale factor
a
:

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\frac{6\,\alpha^{2}}{1+\,\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\,\widetilde{f}(a)}\bigg[1\,+\,\frac{\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}}{12}\bigg\{\left[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right]\frac{\widetilde{f}(a)}{a^{3}}-\,3\left[1+\widetilde{f}(a)\right]\widetilde{p}(a)\bigg\}+\,\frac{2k\sigma^{2}}{a^{2}}\bigg]\,,

(5.65)

upon denoting, as before,
\alpha=2\hbar/(m\sigma^{2})
[
cf
. Eq. (
3.21
)], and defining

\widetilde{f}(a):=\,1+\,\frac{3\kappa^{2}\sigma^{2}\,a\,\widetilde{p}\,^{\prime}(a)}{8}\,.

(5.66)

Eq. (
5.65
) is the generalization of Eq. (
3.20
) obtained in subsection
3.1
.

On the other hand, the equation (
3.23
), which is derived in subsection
3.1
from the conservation relation (
2.8
) satisfied by
\rho
and
p
, holds for a non-vanishing
k
as well. Remember that (
3.23
)
is the equation that determines the cosmic evolution profile, when solved simultaneously with that for
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
, which is the equation (
5.65
) now. Working out the solution is of course difficult enough, and even more so, compared to the
k=0
case, as the high degree of non-linearity arising from the substitution of Eq. (
5.65
) in Eq. (
3.23
) gets compounded with the technical complication posed by the last term in the former. In particular, a straightforward inspection reveals that, unlike in the
k=0
case, no specific ansatz (such as
p^{\prime}=0
) will lead to an exact
k\Lambda
CDM solution in the generalized picture.

Nevertheless, we can consider taking the approach as before, that is to re-express Eq. (
5.65
) in terms of the parameters
\Lambda
and
\varepsilon
, defined respectively by Eqs. (
3.28
) and (
3.30
) in subsection
3.2
, and have the following generalization of Eq.(
3.34
) derived in subsection
3.4
:

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\frac{\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)\kappa^{2}}{1-\varepsilon\left[1-\widetilde{f}(a)\right]}\bigg[\Lambda+\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2\left(1-3\varepsilon\right)}\bigg\{\left[\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right]\frac{\widetilde{f}(a)}{a^{3}}-\,3\big[1+\widetilde{f}(a)\big]\widetilde{p}(a)+\,\frac{12k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}}\bigg\}\bigg]\,,

(5.67)

with

\widetilde{f}(a)=\,1+\,\frac{9\varepsilon\,a\,\widetilde{p}\,^{\prime}(a)}{4\left(1-\,3\varepsilon\right)\Lambda}

(5.68)

being the function given by Eq. (
5.66
), re-written accordingly. The purpose is to exploit the smallness of
\varepsilon
, by expanding
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
in powers of the latter and work out the solutions for the total energy density and pressure,
\rho(a)
and
p(a)
, either iteratively or perturbatively (in which case,
\rho(a)
and
p(a)
are also expanded in powers of
\varepsilon
). After all, the smallness of
\varepsilon
is beyond question in the limiting
\Lambda
CDM case in subsections
3.2
and
3.3
, as it follows from a few merely physical considerations therein, such as that the BEC density must not exceed the total matter density of the universe.
Although this relies on the presumption
k=0
, one cannot expect a cosmological solution with a small
\varepsilon
to be affected much by a non-zero
k
either, since wherever
k
appears in the generalized expression (
5.67
) for
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
, in the last term or in the terms involving
\widetilde{f}
and
\widetilde{p}
, it always carries a multiplicative factor of
\varepsilon
. Moreover, a non-vanishing spatial curvature only implies
|k|=1
and not anything large enough to affect the order of magnitude of
\varepsilon
which multiplies it in every
k
-involving term in Eq. (
5.67
) for
U_{{}_{Q}}(a)
that determines the evolution profile of the universe.

An expansion of the right hand side of Eq. (
5.67
) in powers of
\varepsilon
leads to

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)=\,\kappa^{2}\left[\Lambda+\,\varepsilon\left\{\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\,\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{2a^{3}}\,-\,3\Big[\widetilde{p}(a)+\,\Lambda\Big]+\,\frac{12k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}}\right\}+\,\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon^{2}\big)\right]\,,

(5.69)

whence, keeping terms up to
\mathcal{O}\big(\varepsilon\big)
, and using the definition (
5.60
) of
\widetilde{p}(a)
, we solve Eq. (
3.23
) to obtain

p(a)\,\approx\,p_{{}_{0}}a^{-2s}-\,\Lambda\Big(1-a^{-2s}\Big)+\,\frac{3k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}}\Big(1-a^{2\varepsilon}\Big)\,,

(5.70)

where
p_{{}_{0}}
is the value of
p
at the present epoch, and
s\approx 1-\varepsilon\,
[
cf
. Eq. (
3.38
)]. Substituting Eq. (
5.70
) back in Eq. (
5.69
), we get

U_{{}_{Q}}(a)\,\approx\,\kappa^{2}\left[\Lambda+\,\varepsilon\left\{\frac{\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}}{2a^{3}}\,-\,\frac{3\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\Lambda-3\kappa^{-2}k\right)}{a^{2s}}\right\}\right]\,,

(5.71)

which when plugged, alongwith Eq. (
5.70
), in Eq. (
5.64
), yields

\rho\,(a)\,\approx\,\frac{\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}+\,\Lambda\,-\,\frac{3\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)\left(p_{{}_{0}}+\Lambda\right)}{a^{2s}}-\,\frac{9k}{\kappa^{2}a^{2}}\Big[1-\left(1-2\varepsilon\right)a^{2\varepsilon}\Big]\,,

(5.72)

where
\rho^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}=\left(1-\varepsilon\right)\left(\rho^{(b)}_{{}_{0}}+\rho^{(B)}_{{}_{0}}\right)\,
[
cf
. Eq. (
3.29
)] is the total matter density at the present epoch.

Consequently, eliminating the parameter
p_{{}_{0}}
via the constraint arising by virtue of setting
H=H_{{}_{0}}
at the present epoch (
a=1
), we obtain from the Friedmann equation (
5.57
),

\left[\frac{H(a)}{H_{{}_{0}}}\right]^{2}\,\approx\,\frac{\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{3}}+\,\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}+\frac{1-\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}-\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}}{a^{2s}}+\,4\left(1-a^{2\varepsilon}\right)\frac{\Omega^{(k)}_{{}_{0}}}{a^{2}}\,,

(5.73)

where
\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}
,
\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}
and
\Omega^{(k)}_{{}_{0}}
respectively denote the matter density parameter, the
\Lambda
-density parameter and the
k
-density parameter at
a=1
.
Note that, while the equation (
3.44
) obtained for
k=0
in subsection
3.4
gets modified by the last term in Eq. (
5.73
), the factor
\left(1-a^{2\varepsilon}\right)
in the latter is quite small, for a small
\varepsilon
, at all epochs, i.e. for all finite values of the scale factor
a
. This is regardless of the value of
\Omega^{(k)}_{{}_{0}}
, which is a free parameter here, and not constrained to be equal to
\left(1-\Omega^{(m)}_{{}_{0}}-\Omega_{{}_{0}}^{(\Lambda)}\right)
, as in the standard
k\Lambda
CDM scenario. However, since
\Omega^{(k)}_{{}_{0}}
cannot exceed unity (as otherwise the total energy density of the universe is negative) we can safely infer that the last term in Eq. (
5.73
) is quite insignificant compared to the other terms therein, which constitute Eq. (
3.44
) and for which the parameter
\varepsilon
is already estimated to be very small (albeit not with a small enough
1\sigma
margin that can highly enhance the BEC mass
m
from its Hubble value
m_{{}_{H}}
). In other words, the near-
k\Lambda
CDM solution obtained in subsection
3.4
, albeit for
k=0
, prevails in presence of a non-zero
k
as well. Hence, taking the argument around, the parametric estimations done in the preceding section still stand, and so do the outcome that the BEC mass is comparable to that of a scalar field DE, rather than that of an axion-like DM.

6
Conclusion

We have thus explored the possible ways of realizing a semi-classical picture of a
unified
cosmic dark sector, from a BEC of ultralight bosons stretching across a very large length scale, close to the Hubble scale.
While this implies a
quantum
‘dark fluid’ description of the universe being sought from the BEC, our primary objective in this paper has been a self-consistent theoretical formulation, from the foundations laid in some of our earlier works

[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.
In particular, we have pondered on the essential criteria for realistic scenarios in the standard paradigm of the spatially flat FRW cosmology, such as the conditions for the formation of the BEC in the early universe and the spherical symmetry of the corresponding wave-function
\Psi
, apart from suggesting the functional form of the latter, so that the BEC may source the CDM content of the universe, albeit having interactions with the effective DE component in a unified picture.

The key role in such a unification is shown to be played by the associated quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
, derived from the amplitude of
\Psi
, as the bosons in the BEC being (supposedly) quantum particles, follow the (Bohmian) quantal trajectories

[
68
,
69
,
70
]
.
In fact, it is this quantum potential
U_{{}_{Q}}
which leads to a quantum correction to the Raychaudhuri-Friedmann equation

[
67
]
,
and thereby makes an effective DE component perceptible in the formalism. While this has been pointed out in the earlier papers

[
29
,

,

,
32
]
,
quite in contrast with a variety of BEC-DM models in the literature
