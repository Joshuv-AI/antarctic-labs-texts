Sampling strategies for the Herman–Kluk propagator of the wavefunction Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Fabian Kröninger, Caroline Lasser, Jiří J. L. Vaníček

Abstract
When the semiclassical Herman–Kluk propagator is used for evaluating quantum-mechanical observables or time-correlation functions, the initial conditions for the guiding trajectories are typically sampled from the Husimi density. Here, we employ this propagator to evolve the wavefunction itself. We investigate two grid-free strategies for the initial sampling of the Herman–Kluk propagator applied to the wavefunction and validate the resulting time-dependent wavefunctions evolved in harmonic and anharmonic potentials. In particular, we consider Monte Carlo quadratures based either on the initial Husimi density or on its square root as possible and most natural sampling densities. We prove analytical convergence error estimates and validate them with numerical experiments on the harmonic oscillator and on a series of Morse potentials with increasing anharmonicity. In all cases, sampling from the square root of Husimi density leads to faster convergence of the wavefunction.

In particular, converged computations might require an extremely large number of trajectories. Yet, the number of guiding trajectories can already be significantly reduced simply by choosing the sampling density of the initial conditions wisely. Specifically, we investigate two mesh-free discretization approaches for the initial sampling—first analytically and then numerically on the examples of harmonic and Morse oscillators with increasing anharmonicity.

II
Discretising the Herman–Kluk propagator

II.1
Herman–Kluk propagator

Evolution of a quantum state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is governed by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\Ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{H}\Ket{\psi(t)},\quad\Ket{\psi(0)}=\Ket{\psi_{0}}

(1)

where
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant and
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian operator. Here, we assume the Hamiltonian to be the time-independent operator

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\hat{p}^{T}\cdot\hat{p}+V(\hat{q}),

(2)

where
m
is the mass (after mass-scaling coordinates), and
q
and
p
are
D
-dimensional position and momentum vectors. Under the usual regularity and growth assumptions on the potential energy function
V
, the spectral-theorem
Hall 2013
provides for all times
t\in\mathbb{R}
a well-defined unitary propagator

\hat{U}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\exp{({-i\hat{H}t/\hbar})},

(3)

in terms of which the solution of (
1
) can be expressed as

\Ket{\psi(t)}=\hat{U}_{t}\Ket{\psi_{0}}

(4)

for all square integrable initial data
\psi_{0}(x)=\langle x\ket{\psi_{0}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
.

Solving the Schrödinger equation numerically is notoriously difficult for various reasons. With respect to the atomic scale, the nuclear mass is rather large, so that the presence of the factor
m^{-1}
in the kinetic energy operator induces highly oscillatory motion both with respect to time and space. More importantly, for most molecular systems the dimension
D
of the configuration space is so large that grid-based integration methods are very expensive if not infeasible. Therefore, one often resorts to mesh-free discretization methods or semiclassical approximations, both of which alleviate this “curse of dimensionality” at least partially.

The semiclassical Herman–Kluk propagator utilizes frozen Gaussian functions

\displaystyle\begin{split}&g_{z}^{\gamma}(x)=\left(\frac{\det{\gamma}}{\pi^{D}\hbar^{D}}\right)^{1/4}\\
&\times\exp{\left\{-\left[(x-q)^{T}\cdot\gamma\cdot(x-q)/2+ip^{T}\cdot(x-q)\right]/\hbar\right\}}\end{split}

(5)

with a centre at the phase-space point
z=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
and with a fixed,
real, symmetric, positive-definite width-matrix
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
. The frozen Gaussians
\{\ket{g_{z}^{\gamma}}:z\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}\}
form an over-complete subset of the Hilbert space of square integrable functions. They have the striking property that any
\ket{\psi}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
can be decomposed as
Martinez 2002

\Ket{\psi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi\rangle\Ket{g_{z}^{\gamma}}d\nu,

(6)

with the scaled phase-space measure
d\nu=dz/(2\pi\hbar)^{D}.

Using this decomposition for our solution of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, we obtain

\hat{U}_{t}\Ket{\psi_{0}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle\hat{U}_{t}\Ket{g_{z}^{\gamma}}\,d\nu.

(7)

Based on the continuous superposition (
7
), the state obtained by applying the Herman–Kluk propagator to
\ket{\psi_{0}}
can be expressed as

\Ket{\hat{U}^{\rm HK}_{t}\psi_{0}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}R(t,z)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(t,z)}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle\ket{g_{z(t)}^{\gamma}}d\nu,

(8)

where
z(t)=(q(t),p(t))
is the solution to the underlying classical Hamiltonian system

\dot{w}=J\cdot\nabla h(w),\hskip 14.22636ptw(0)=z,

(9)

for the Hamiltonian function
h(q,p)=T(p)+V(q)
, where

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{Id}_{D}\\
-\mathrm{Id}_{D}&0\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D\times 2D}

(10)

is the standard symplectic matrix.
S
denotes the classical action integral

S(t,z)=\int_{0}^{t}\left[\frac{d}{d\tau}q(\tau)\cdot p(\tau)-h(z(\tau))\right]d\tau

(11)

which solves the initial value problem

\displaystyle\dot{S}(t,z)=T(p(t))-V(q(t)),\hskip 14.22636ptS(0,z)=0,

(12)

for all
z=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.

In (
8
), the Herman–Kluk prefactor

\displaystyle\begin{split}&R(t,z)=2^{-D/2}\\
&\cdot\det{\left(M_{qq}+\gamma^{-1}\cdot M_{pp}\cdot\gamma-iM_{qp}\cdot\gamma+i\gamma^{-1}\cdot M_{pq}\right)^{1/2}}\end{split}

(13)

depends on the matrices

M_{\alpha\beta}=\partial\alpha_{t}/\partial\beta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}\ \ \ (\alpha,\beta\in\{q,p\}),

(14)

i.e., the four
D\times D
block components of the
2D\times 2D
stability matrix
M.

Stability matrix, defined as the Jacobian
M(t)=\partial z(t)/\partial z
of the flow map, is the solution to the variational equation

\dot{M}(t)=J\cdot\mathrm{Hess}\,h(z(t))\cdot M(t),\hskip 14.22636ptM(0)=\textrm{Id}_{2D},

(15)

with
\mathrm{Hess}\,h
the Hessian of the Hamiltonian function
h
.

The Herman–Kluk approximation has been mathematically justified in different works.
Kay 2006
;
Lasser and Lubich 2020
;
Swart and Rousse 2008
;
Robert 2010
It has been shown that
the exactly evolved quantum state is approximated by the state

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}:=\ket{\hat{U}^{\rm HK}_{t}\psi_{0}},

(16)

obtained by applying the Herman–Kluk propagator to the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
,
with an error of the order of
\hbar.
More precisely,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\hat{U}_{t}\Ket{\psi_{0}}-\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq C(T)\hskip 2.84544pt\hbar,

(17)

for all initial data
\ket{\psi_{0}}
of norm one, where
T>0
is a fixed time and
C(T)>0
is a constant independent of
\hbar.
If the potential is at most harmonic, then the approximation is exact.
Lasser and Lubich 2020

II.2
Discretisation

Evolving the wavefunction (
8
) with the Herman–Kluk propagator requires evaluating an integral over the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2D}
and the overlap of the initial state with a frozen Gaussian. Furthermore, the algorithm needs to propagate the trajectories
z(t)=(q(t),p(t)),
the classical action
S(t,z)
, and the Herman–Kluk prefactor
R(t,z)
according to Hamilton’s equations of motion for all chosen quadrature points
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}.
The latter can be achieved using symplectic integration methods to preserve also the symplectic structure of the classical Hamiltonian system. Due to the curse of dimensionality, for high
D
the integral on
\mathbb{R}^{2D}
must be evaluated using mesh-free discretization, such as Monte Carlo methods.
Caflisch 1998

To evaluate the Herman–Kluk wavefunction by Monte Carlo sampling, we rewrite the integral from
Eq.

8
as

\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}=\left\langle r(z)\Ket{\phi(t,z)}\right\rangle_{\rho(z)},

(18)

where we introduced the notation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle\Ket{\psi(t,z)}\ \right\rangle_{\rho(z)}&\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\Ket{\psi(t,z)}\,d\mu\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\Ket{\psi(t,z)}\rho(z)\,d\nu\end{split}

(19)

for the phase-space average of
\ket{\psi(t,z)}
with respect to a probability measure
\mu
with density
\rho(z)=d\mu/d\nu
and defined a time-dependent state

\Ket{\phi(t,z)}:=R(t,z)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(t,z)}\ket{g^{\gamma}_{z(t)}},

(20)

i.e., a propagated frozen Gaussian multiplied by the Herman–Kluk and phase prefactors, and a time-independent function

r(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle\,\rho(z)^{-1}.

(21)

The Monte Carlo estimator is then given by

\displaystyle\Ket{\psi_{N}(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Ket{\psi(t,z_{j})},

(22)

where
\psi(t,z_{j})
is the state obtained from initial condition
z_{j}
and
z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{N}
are sampled from probability density
\rho(z)
.

As for any importance sampling, there are infinitely many ways to decompose the time-independent part of the phase space integrand in
Eq.

8
into the product
\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle=r(z)\rho(z)

of a prefactor
r
with a normalized sampling density
\rho
.
If one computes observables and correlation functions, which are quadratic in the initial state,
\rho(z)
is typically taken to be the Husimi probability density

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm H}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}|\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}

(23)

of the initial state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
. For Husimi distribution, the prefactor
r(z)
becomes

r_{\rm H}(z)=\langle\psi_{0}|g_{z}^{\gamma}\rangle^{-1}.

(24)

However, since the wavefunction evolved with the Herman–Kluk propagator is first- and not second-order in
\ket{\psi_{0}}
, it is natural to also test the square root of the Husimi density and to consider
the probability density

\tilde{\rho}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\frac{|\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle|}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|\langle g_{w}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle|d\nu}

(25)

with a bounded prefactor

\tilde{r}(z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle\,\tilde{\rho}(z)^{-1}.

(26)

The two probability densities
\rho_{\rm H}(z)
and
\tilde{\rho}(z)
can now be used to compute the phase-space integral by Monte Carlo integration. To sum up, we have two cases, in which we evaluate
\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}
either as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle r_{\rm H}(z)\Ket{\phi(t,z)}\right\rangle_{\rho_{\rm H}(z)}

(Case H)

or as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}=\left\langle\tilde{r}(z)\Ket{\phi(t,z)}\right\rangle_{\tilde{\rho}(z)}.

(Case sqrt-H)

In general, the integral over
\mathbb{R}^{D}
defining the overlap of the initial wavefunction with a Gaussian has to be computed by numerical quadrature. However, for important specific cases analytical formulas are available.
If the initial wavefunction is a Gaussian wavepacket
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{g_{z_{0}}^{\gamma}}
centred at some phase-space point
z_{0}=(q_{0},p_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D})}&=\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{4\hbar}(z-z_{0})^{T}\cdot\Sigma_{0}\cdot(z-z_{0})\right]}\\
&\cdot\exp{\left[\frac{i}{2\hbar}(p+p_{0})^{T}\cdot(q-q_{0})\right]},\end{split}

(27)

where
\Sigma_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}\!\!=}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\
0&\gamma^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
is the matrix containing the width parameters of the initial coherent state. Then, the Husimi density is given by

\rho_{\rm H}(z)=\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{2\hbar}(z-z_{0})^{T}\cdot\Sigma_{0}\cdot(z-z_{0})\right]},

(28)

whereas the second approach provides the density

\tilde{\rho}(z)=2^{-D}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{4\hbar}(z-z_{0})^{T}\cdot\Sigma_{0}\cdot(z-z_{0})\right]}

(29)

and prefactor

\tilde{r}(z)=2^{D}\exp{\left[\frac{i}{2\hbar}(p+p_{0})^{T}\cdot(q-q_{0})\right]}.

(30)

II.3
Summary of the numerical algorithm

Taking into account all the previous considerations, we slightly extend the natural numerical algorithm (described, e.g., in Ref.
Lasser and Sattlegger 2017
, Sec. 4
]) for finding the Herman–Kluk approximation to the wavefunction at time
t
:

Algorithm 1
.

(Herman–Kluk propagation)

1.

Draw independent samples
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2D}
from a distribution with density
\rho_{\rm H}(z)
or
\tilde{\rho}(z)
given by Eqs. (
28
) and (
29
).

2.

For all
j\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}:

2.1

Set initial values
z(0)=z_{j},

M(0)=\textrm{Id}_{2D}
and
S(0)=0.

2.2

Compute approximate solutions to
Eqs.

9
,
12
and
15
up to time
t
with a symplectic integration method
Brewer et al. 1997
based on the Störmer–Verlet scheme.
Hairer et al. 2006
;
Hairer et al. 2003

2.3

Compute the Herman–Kluk prefactor
R(t,z_{j})
from
M(t)
while choosing the correct branch of the complex square root,
Stewart and Tall 2018
which guarantees continuity of
R(t,z_{j})
as a function of
t
.

3.

Calculate
\ket{\psi_{N}(t)}
by means of formula (
22
) with
\ket{\psi(t,z_{j})}
replaced with either

\displaystyle\Ket{\psi_{\rm H}(t,z_{j})}=r_{\rm H}(z_{j})R(t,z_{j})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(t,z_{j})}\Ket{g^{\gamma}_{z_{j}(t)}}

(31)

or

\displaystyle\Ket{\tilde{\psi}(t,z_{j})}=\tilde{r}(z_{j})R(t,z_{j})e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}S(t,z_{j})}\Ket{g^{\gamma}_{z_{j}(t)}},

(32)

where
r_{\rm H}(z_{j})
and
\tilde{r}(z_{j})
are given by Eqs. (
24
), (
27
), and (
30
).

\hfill\qed

We note that
Eqs.

9
,
12
and
15
can be evaluated simultaneously with a single numerical integrator. To increase the accuracy of the time integration one can use higher-order composition methods.
Kahan and Li 1997
;
Suzuki 1992
They increase the order of the time integrator, but also its numerical cost. A higher-order time integrator is not necessarily useful since the phase-space error occurring from the Monte Carlo quadrature usually dominates the time integration error.

III
Phase space error analysis

Algorithm
1
relies on the discretisation of the phase-space integrals and of the system of ordinary differential equations. Here we focus only on the phase-space discretisation error.

III.1
Moments of the integrand

To assess the accuracy of the Monte Carlo estimator (
22
), we examine the moments of the two different integrands:
\ket{\psi_{\rm H}(t)}
and
\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}.

First, we observe that for any
s>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\tilde{\psi}(t)\rVert^{s}\big]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|R(t,z)|^{s}\,|\tilde{r}(z)|^{s}\,d\tilde{\mu}(z)<\infty,

(33)

since
\tilde{r}(z)
and the Herman–Kluk prefactor
R(t,z)
are both bounded functions. For a discussion of the boundedness of
R(t,z)
, see
Appendix

A
. In contrast,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\psi_{\rm H}(t)\rVert^{s}\right]&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|R(t,z)|^{s}\,|r_{\rm H}(z)|^{s}\ d\mu_{\rm H}(z)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|R(t,z)|^{s}\ |\langle g_{z}^{\gamma}|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2-s}\ d\nu\end{split}

(34)

ceases to be finite for
s\geq 2
. However, both integrands have a finite first moment, so that the Strong Law of Large Numbers (Theorem 2.4.1 in Ref.
Durrett 2019
) provides convergence of the estimator,

\displaystyle\Ket{\psi_{N}(t)}\to\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty,

(35)

with probability one. The non-existing second moment for (
Case H
) results in a slightly worse convergence rate than the one for (
Case sqrt-H
). Numerical results in Sec.
IV
confirm this expectation.

III.2
Mean squared error

For (
Case sqrt-H
) the second moment is finite, so that
the mean squared error of the Monte Carlo estimator (
22
) is well-defined and satisfies

\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert^{2}\big]=\frac{\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}\big]}{N},

(36)

where the expectation value and the variance are with respect to the density
\tilde{\rho}(z)
. Moreover,
Lasser and Sattlegger 2017

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}\big]\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}\lVert\tilde{\psi}(t)-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\psi}(t)]\rVert^{2}\,d\tilde{\mu}(z)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|R(t,z)|^{2}\,|\tilde{r}(z)|^{2}\,d\tilde{\mu}(z)-\lVert\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

(37)

In the special case of an initial Gaussian initial wavepacket
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{g_{z_{0}}^{\gamma}}
, this simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}\big]=4^{D}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2D}}|R(t,z)|^{2}\,d\tilde{\mu}(z)-\lVert\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert^{2},

(38)

and at initial time
t=0
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(0)}\big]=4^{D}-1,

(39)

since the Herman–Kluk prefactor satisfies
R(0,z)=1
.

For a more general assessment of the variance, numerical experiments in Sec.
IV
consider the error between the approximations with
N
and
2N
samples. By the linearity of the expectation value and the triangle inequality, this error can be estimated by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi_{2N}(t)\rVert^{2}\big]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert^{2}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\lVert\psi^{\rm HK}(t)-\psi_{2N}(t)\rVert^{2}\big]\\
&=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mathbb{V}\big[\Ket{\psi(t)}\big]}{N}.\end{split}

(40)

Note that
Eq.

36
gives only the expected convergence error, i.e., the convergence error averaged over infinitely many independent simulations, each using
N
trajectories. The actual convergence error for any specific simulation with
N
trajectories may deviate from this analytical estimate substantially due to statistical noise. Nevertheless, in
Appendix

B
, we explain how
Eq.

36
can also provide a rigorous lower bound and asymptotic estimate of the number of trajectories needed for convergence of a single simulation.

III.3
Other sampling densities

For the special case of an initial Gaussian wavepacket
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{g_{z_{0}}^{\gamma}}
, the
two proposed sampling densities
\rho_{\rm H}(z)
and
\tilde{\rho}(z)
belong to a family of normal distributions with probability density functions

\displaystyle\rho_{a}(z)=(a\pi\hbar)^{-D}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{a\hbar}(z-z_{0})^{T}\cdot\Sigma_{0}\cdot(z-z_{0})\right]}

(41)

with
a\geq 2
.
In the spirit of importance sampling, the Herman–Kluk wavefunction can accordingly be written as a phase-space average

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{\rm HK}(t)}=\left\langle\ket{\psi_{a}(t,z)}\right\rangle_{\rho_{a}(z)}.

(42)

At time
t=0
, the norm of the integrand satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lVert\psi_{a}(0,z)\rVert\\
&=\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^{D}\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{a-4}{4a\hbar}\right)(z-z_{0})^{T}\cdot\Sigma_{0}\cdot(z-z_{0})\right]},\end{split}

(43)

which implies for the variance

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{V}[\Ket{\psi_{a}(0)}]\\
&=\left(\frac{a}{4\pi\hbar}\right)^{D}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\exp{\left[-\left(\frac{a-2}{2a\hbar}\right)|z|^{2}\right]}dz-1\\
&=\left(\frac{a^{2}}{2(a-2)}\right)^{D}-1.\end{split}

(44)

For
a>2
, the function
a\mapsto a^{2}/(2a-4)
attains its minimum at
a=4
, which corresponds to the sampling density
\tilde{\rho}
. In other words, (
Case sqrt-H
) is optimal as far as the mean squared error is concerned.

III.4
Harmonic motion

The one-dimensional harmonic oscillator

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x^{2}

(45)

is one of the rare examples for which explicit expressions for the solution of the Schrödinger equation and for the Herman–Kluk prefactor exist. For an initial Gaussian wavepacket
\ket{\psi_{0}}=\ket{g^{\gamma}_{z_{0}}}
with position and momentum
q_{0},p_{0}\in\mathbb{R},
the exact wavefunction is given by
Tannor 2007

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm ex}(x,t)=\exp{\left\{\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2}(x-q_{t})^{2}+p_{t}(x-q_{t})+\beta_{t}\right]\right\}}

(46)

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}

\displaystyle=b\frac{\alpha_{0}\cos(\omega t)-b\sin(\omega t)}{b\cos(\omega t)+\alpha_{0}\sin(\omega t)},

(47)

\displaystyle q_{t}

\displaystyle=q_{0}\cos(\omega t)+\frac{p_{0}}{b}\sin(\omega t),

(48)

\displaystyle p_{t}

\displaystyle=p_{0}\cos(\omega t)-bq_{0}\sin(\omega t)\text{ and }

(49)

\displaystyle\beta_{t}

\displaystyle=\beta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\left[q_{t}p_{t}-q_{0}p_{0}+i\hbar\ln{\left(\frac{z_{t}}{b}\right)}\right]

(50)

with the abbreviations
z_{t}=b\cos(\omega t)+\alpha_{0}\sin(\omega t),

\alpha_{0}=i\gamma
and
b=m\omega.
Here,
\beta_{0}
includes the normalization constant for the wavefunction at time
t=0.
The classical action is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}&S(t,q_{0},p_{0})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sin(\omega t)\left[\left(\frac{p_{0}^{2}}{b}-bq_{0}^{2}\right)\cos(\omega t)-q_{0}p_{0}\sin(\omega t)\right].\end{split}

(51)

The four components of stability matrix

can be obtained, from their definition (

q_{t}
and

and

\displaystyle M(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\left(\omega t\right)}&b^{-1}\sin{\left(\omega t\right)}\\
-b\sin{\left(\omega t\right)}&\cos{\left(\omega t\right)}\end{pmatrix}.

(52)

The Herman–Kluk prefactor satisfies

\displaystyle R(t)=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left[2\cos{\omega t}-i\sin(\omega t)\left(\frac{\gamma}{b}+\frac{b}{\gamma}\right)\right]\right\}^{1/2},

(53)

so that the variance (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}\big]\\
&=2\left[4\cos^{2}(\omega t)+\left(\frac{\gamma}{b}+\frac{b}{\gamma}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}(\omega t)\right]^{1/2}-1.\end{split}

(54)

This implies that for (
Case sqrt-H
) applied to a harmonic oscillator, the mean squared error of our Monte Carlo estimator oscillates with a period of
\pi/\omega
between

\displaystyle 3\leq\mathbb{V}\big[\ket{\tilde{\psi}(t)}\big]\leq 2\left(\frac{\gamma}{m\omega}+\frac{m\omega}{\gamma}\right)-1.

(55)

We compared two different sampling strategies for evaluating the semiclassical wavefunction evolved with the Herman–Kluk propagator. For the initial phase-space sampling, we either used the Husimi density (
Case H
) or its square root (
Case sqrt-H
). We showed that the square root sampling produces a Monte Carlo integrand with finite second
moment, while the Husimi sampling comes with an undesirable infinite second moment. The numerical experiments
for the harmonic oscillator and two Morse oscillators with different extents of anharmonicity confirm that the infinite second moment results in a slower convergence of the Monte Carlo estimator.
Therefore, we recommend the square root approach (
Case sqrt-H
) whenever the Herman–Kluk propagator is used directly for approximating the quantum-mechanical wavefunction and the
L^{2}
-error
of the wavefunction approximation is the relevant accuracy measure. A follow-up paper applying a similar analysis to the autocorrelation function as well as to the expectation values computed with the Herman–Kluk propagator is in preparation.

Appendix A
Boundedness of Herman–Kluk prefactor

For notational simplicity we consider the case
\gamma=\mathrm{Id}_{D}.
A general
\gamma
does not change the argument, but it requires to exchange
M
by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{D}&0\\
0&\gamma^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{D}&0\\
0&\gamma\end{pmatrix}

(68)

in the following. We recall that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&R(t,z)\\
&=\sqrt{2^{-D}\det{\left(M_{qq}+M_{pp}-iM_{qp}+iM_{pq}\right)}}\\
&=:\sqrt{\det{F}}\end{split}

(69)

with

\displaystyle F=(\mathrm{Id}_{2D}+M)-iJ\cdot(\mathrm{Id}_{2D}-M).

(70)

Following [
Lasser and Lubich 2020
, Lemma 5.6
], for
F
one calculates

\displaystyle F^{\dagger}\cdot F=2(\mathrm{Id}_{2D}+M^{T}\cdot M).

(71)

Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{split}|R(t,z)|^{2}&=\sqrt{\det(F^{\dagger}\cdot F)}\\
&=\sqrt{2^{D}\det{(\mathrm{Id}_{2D}+M^{T}\cdot M)}}.\end{split}

(72)

With this representation, we can link growth and boundedness of
R(t,z)
directly with growth and boundedness of the stability matrix, and it is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian
\textrm{Hess}\,h
that ultimately gives upper bounds on
R(t,z)
.

Appendix B
Number of trajectories needed for convergence

In the following, we give a lower bound as well as an asymptotic estimate for the number of trajectories
N
needed “for convergence,” or, more precisely, needed to guarantee that the probability of the convergence error exceeding a specified threshold
\epsilon
is below a prescribed value
p
. The following analysis holds for any potential
V
in any number of dimensions
D
, but assumes that the variance
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{V}[\ket{\psi(t)}]
of the estimator is finite.

On one hand, Chebyshev’s inequality
Durrett 2019

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N\epsilon^{2}}

(73)

implies that the number of trajectories
N
must obey the inequality

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{p\epsilon^{2}}

(74)

in order that the probability of the convergence error
\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert
exceeding
\epsilon
be at most
p
.

On the other hand, the central limit theorem
Durrett 2019
states that the random variable

\displaystyle Z_{N}:=\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sigma}\left(\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\right)

(75)

converges in distribution to the standard normally distributed random variable. Hence, the probability for the convergence error to be above this threshold is

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\lVert\psi_{N}(t)-\psi^{\rm HK}(t)\rVert\geq\epsilon\right)\\
&\approx(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sigma}\epsilon}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}/2}\,dt.\end{split}

(76)

For a given probability
p
that
\epsilon
is exceeded, this leads to the asymptotic behaviour

\displaystyle N\approx\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\epsilon^{2}}\left[\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(2p)\right]^{2},

(77)

where
\operatorname{erfc}
is the complementary error function.
Dyke 2013
