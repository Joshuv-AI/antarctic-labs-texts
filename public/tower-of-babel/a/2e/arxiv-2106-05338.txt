Introduction

The
BAU
is defined and measured
Planck:2015fie
;
Tanabashi:2018oca
to be

\displaystyle Y_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{n_{B}-n_{\bar{B}}}{s}\approx\left(8.6\pm 0.1\right)\cdot 10^{-11}\equiv Y_{B}^{\text{obs}}\,,

(1)

where
n_{(\bar{B})B}
is the (anti-)baryon number density and
s
is the entropy density of the Universe. A non-vanishing value can be either the result of initial conditions, or dynamically generated during the early Universe. The former requires fine tuning and is inconsistent with inflation. The latter, which is the more acceptable mechanism to address the asymmetry, is called Baryogenesis (See
Cline:2006ts
;
Morrissey:2012db
, for reviews).

There are three necessary conditions, known as the Sakharov conditions
Sakharov:1967dj
, that are required from any theory in order to explain such an imbalance: Baryon number violation,
\mathcal{C}
-symmetry and
\mathcal{CP}
-symmetry violation and interactions out of thermodynamic equilibrium. Although the
SM
(
SM

Gavela:1993ts
;
Huet:1994jb
due to two factors: the smooth crossover of the electroweak phase transition and the suppression from the Kobayashi-Maskawa (KM) mechanism of
\mathcal{CP}
violation.
Thus, if the baryon asymmetry was generated via electroweak baryogenesis, the electroweak phase transition had to be strongly first order and a new sources of
\mathcal{CP}
violation must exist at, or at least not far above, the electroweak scale.

NP
(
NP
) beyond the
SM
is highly motivated by several open questions in Physics (e.g. dark matter, neutrino masses). However, despite the efforts made to discover new particles, none were found up to the TeV scale
CMS:2019ybf
;
ATLAS:2019fgd
. It is then plausible that
NP
is above the electroweak scale, and thus could be integrated out. This allows us to use
SMEFT
(
SMEFT
) tools to explore higher order terms, without being model-dependent.

We add a
CPV
(
CPV
) phase using a
dim-$6$
(
dim-$6$
) coupling of three Higgs fields to the
SM
charged fermions. The
BAU
is then proportional to the
CPV
source, which could be constrained by the
EDM
(
EDM
) of both the electron and neutron and by the Higgs boson decay and production rates. This was previously done for the third generation particles
deVries:2017ncy
;
deVries:2018tgs
;
Fuchs:2020uoc
and the muon
Fuchs:2019ore
. Of that list, it was shown that the

BAU

deVries:2018tgs
;
Fuchs:2020uoc
. We applied this procedure to evaluate the contribution from all of the
SM
particles, including the light quarks (charm, strange, down, up) and the electron, and discuss the results here.

This Letter is organized as follows. First, we describe the
SMEFT

dim-$6$
term and the
CPV

Electroweak baryogenesis

By adding a complex effective Yukawa coupling to one of the fermions of the
SM
we introduce a
CPV
source to the model. The source depends on the background Higgs boson field
h
, which acquires a
VEV
. Here we assume that the electroweak phase transition is strongly first order, and describe
h
by the kink solution (Eqn. (
36
)). This choice results with a
CPV
source that peaks mostly inside the non-vanishing
VEV
bubble. A schematic plot is given in Fig.
1
. Namely, the
CPV
source generates a chiral asymmetry, mainly inside the broken phase.
That asymmetry can be transformed into an abundance of baryons via the weak sphaleron, which is a non-perturbative effect of the
SM
. The rate of the weak sphaleron process is given by
\Gamma_{ws}\sim e^{-\langle h\rangle/T}T

Kuzmin:1985mm
, where
T
is the temperature. Although the rate is exponentially suppressed inside the bubble, it is fast outside the bubble, during the early Universe. Therefore, for baryogenesis to occur at the electroweak phase transition, the chiral asymmetry should have propagated outside the bubble and into the symmetric phase
Joyce:1994zn
. Finally, as the bubble continues to expand, it eventually captures the resulting baryon asymmetry.

Two-step approach

The
dim-$6$
term described in Eqn. (
2
) will affect the dynamics of the number densities, which are defined as the difference between the number densities of particles and anti-particles. This effect is the first step of the approach and is described via a set of transport equations. We generalize the set given in
deVries:2018tgs
to include all the fermions of the
SM
in addition to the Higgs boson,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}U_{i}^{\mu}\equiv\partial U_{i}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{M}^{U_{i}}\mu_{M}^{U_{i}}-\Gamma_{Y}^{U_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{U_{i}}+\Gamma_{ss}\mu_{ss}+S_{U_{i}}\,,

\displaystyle\partial D_{i}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{M}^{D_{i}}\mu_{M}^{D_{i}}-\Gamma_{Y}^{D_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{D_{i}}+\Gamma_{ss}\mu_{ss}+S_{D_{i}}\,,

\displaystyle\partial Q_{i}

\displaystyle=-\partial U_{i}-\partial D_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\partial E_{i}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma_{M}^{E_{i}}\mu_{M}^{E_{i}}-\Gamma_{Y}^{E_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{E_{i}}+S_{E_{i}}\,,

\displaystyle\partial L_{i}

\displaystyle=-\partial E_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\partial h

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma_{Y}^{U_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{U_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma_{Y}^{D_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{D_{i}}-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Gamma_{Y}^{E_{i}}\mu_{Y}^{E_{i}}\,,

(10)

where
U_{R},D_{R},Q_{L}
are the
SM
quark fields,
E_{R},L_{L}
are the
SM
charged lepton fields (the chirality is implicit hereafter),
\Gamma_{M,Y}
are the relaxation and Yukawa rates, respectively, and
S_{f}
are the
CPV
sources. The chemical potentials are given by

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}^{U_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{U_{i}}}{k_{U_{i}}}-\frac{{Q_{i}}}{k_{Q_{i}}}-\frac{h}{k_{h}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{M}^{U_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{U_{i}}}{k_{U_{i}}}-\frac{{Q_{i}}}{k_{Q_{i}}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}^{D_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{D_{i}}}{k_{D_{i}}}-\frac{Q_{i}}{k_{Q_{i}}}+\frac{h}{k_{h}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{M}^{D_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{D_{i}}}{k_{D_{i}}}-\frac{{Q_{i}}}{k_{Q_{i}}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{Y}^{E_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{E_{i}}}{k_{E_{i}}}-\frac{{L_{i}}}{k_{L_{i}}}+\frac{h}{k_{h}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{M}^{E_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{E_{i}}}{k_{E_{i}}}-\frac{{L_{i}}}{k_{L_{i}}}\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{ss}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\frac{2Q_{i}}{k_{Q_{i}}}-\frac{U_{i}}{k_{U_{i}}}-\frac{D_{i}}{k_{D_{i}}}\right)\,,

(11)

where

Conclusions

We consider the
CPV
source resulting from a dim-
6

SMEFT
term which couples three Higgs fields to the
SM
fermions. We apply the procedure described in Ref.
Fuchs:2020pun
to evaluate the complete set of single flavor modifications from all of the
SM
charged fermions.

We deduce that although a larger
dim-$4$
Yukawa coupling enhances the
CPV
source, quarks have more washout than leptons and are therefore less favorable candidates to produce the
BAU
. Moreover, to saturate
Y_{B}^{\text{obs}}
, some of the particles require non-perturbative
dim-$4$
Yukawa couplings, e.g. the up and down quarks, and are therefore unequivocally ruled out as sole-contributors via this mechanism.

Constrained by
U.B.
s of the electron and neutron
EDM
s and measurements of various Higgs boson processes, we evaluate the maximal contribution from each single flavor modification (See Table
1
). We conclude that the
\tau
is the only candidate able to produce the observed
BAU

deVries:2018tgs
;
Fuchs:2020uoc
. Other than the
\mu
, which could provide up to
16\%\ Y_{B}^{\text{obs}}

Fuchs:2019ore
, the rest of the charged fermions produce negligible contributions (less than
6\%\ Y_{B}^{\text{obs}}
).

An interplay of different flavors could relax current bounds to allow the observed baryon asymmetry be accounted for by a
CPV
Yukawa coupling. Specifically, the interplay of the top and the electron could allow the top to saturate
Y_{B}^{\text{obs}}
.

Appendix B
Solving the set of transport equations: the two-step approach

In this appendix we summarize the analytic techniques used to calculate the produced baryon asymmetry, similarly to Ref.
Fuchs:2020pun
. We used the two-step approach: First we solve the set of transport equations, given in Eqn. (
Two-step approach
). Then, the
BAU
is obtained by summing over the left-handed number densities and considering the weak sphaleron process.

Reduction of dimensions

The left hand side of Eqn. (
Two-step approach
) can be written as a one dimensional - second order differential equation with respect to the bubble wall dimension denoted
z

Cohen:1994ss
:

\displaystyle\partial f\equiv\partial_{\mu}f^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial f^{0}}{\partial t}-\vec{\nabla}\cdot\vec{f}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial t}}_{\equiv v_{w}}\underbrace{\frac{\partial f^{0}}{\partial z}}_{f^{\prime}}+\vec{\nabla}\cdot\left(-D_{f}\vec{\nabla}f^{0}\right)

\displaystyle=v_{w}f^{\prime}-D_{f}\underbrace{\laplacian{f^0}}_{\equiv f^{\prime\prime}}=v_{w}f^{\prime}-D_{f}f^{\prime\prime}\,,

(40)

where we used Fick’s first law and the diffusion approximation.

Reduction of order

We can solve this set of
N=16
- second order differential equations by reduction of order:

\displaystyle g_{f_{i}}

\displaystyle\equiv f^{\prime}_{i}\,,

\displaystyle\vec{f}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{U}_{R}&\vec{D}_{R}&\vec{Q}_{L}&\vec{E}_{R}&\vec{L}_{L}&h\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\vec{\chi}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\vec{f}&\vec{g}_{f}\end{pmatrix}^{T}\,.

\displaystyle\vec{\chi}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbbm{1}_{NxN}\\
\hat{\Gamma}&\hat{V}\end{pmatrix}\vec{\chi}+\vec{S}\equiv\hat{K}\vec{\chi}+\vec{S}\,.

(41)

We are left with
2N
-first order differential equations. Then,
\hat{K}
can be diagonalized numerically, e.g. by using MATLAB.

Numerical diagonalization

The solution for the symmetric phase is given by

\displaystyle{\vec{\chi}}^{S}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{2N}C_{i}^{S}e^{\lambda_{i}^{S}z}\vec{u}_{i}^{S}\equiv\hat{\Phi}^{S}(z)\vec{C}^{S}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}^{X}_{i,j}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda_{jj}^{X}z}\left(\vec{u}_{j}^{X}\right)_{i}\,.

(42)

where
C_{j}
’s are constants,
\lambda_{j}
are the eigenvalues, and
\vec{u}_{j}
’s are the eigenvectors. We define

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{i}\right)

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=[1:2N]\,.

(43)

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\vec{u}_{1}&\vec{u}_{2}&\dots&\vec{u}_{2N}\\
|&|&&|\end{pmatrix}

(44)

Accordingly,

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}\left(z\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}_{ij}e^{\hat{\lambda}_{jj}z}=\begin{pmatrix}|&|&&|\\
e^{\lambda_{1}z}\vec{u}_{1}&e^{\lambda_{2}z}\vec{u}_{2}&\dots&e^{\lambda_{2N}z}\vec{u}_{2N}\\
|&|&&|\end{pmatrix}

(45)

The full solution in the broken phase is obtained by variation of parameters to be

\displaystyle{\vec{\chi}}^{B}

\displaystyle=\hat{\Phi}^{B}(z)\vec{C}^{B}+\hat{\Phi}^{B}(z)\int\limits_{0}^{z}\left(\hat{\Phi}^{B}(x)\right)^{-1}\vec{S}(x)\ dx\,.

(46)

Boundary conditions

•

The integration constants of the divergent modes in the symmetric phase (correspond to
\lambda^{S}_{j}\leq 0
) are set to zero,

\displaystyle C^{S}_{0-}

\displaystyle=0\,.

(47)

•

The positive eigenvalues in the broken phase,
C^{B+}_{j}
(correspond to
\lambda^{B}_{j}>0
), are chosen such that they cancel the divergent part of the full solution at infinity:

\displaystyle\vec{C}^{B}_{+}

\displaystyle=-\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\hat{\Phi}^{B}_{+}(x)\right)^{-1}\vec{S}(x)\ dx\,.

(48)

•

We demand continuity at
z=0
.

–

In the symmetric phase we have

\displaystyle\vec{\chi}^{S}_{i}\left(z\to 0^{-}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}^{S}_{ij}\vec{C}^{S}_{j}=\hat{\phi}^{S}_{i+}\vec{C}^{S}_{+}\,.

(49)

–

In the broken phase we have

\displaystyle\vec{\chi}^{B}_{i}\left(z\to 0^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}^{B}_{ij}\vec{C}^{B}_{j}=\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i(0-)}\vec{C}^{B}_{0-}+\underbrace{\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i+}\vec{C}^{B}_{+}}_{\equiv b_{i}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i(0-)}\vec{C}^{B}_{0-}+b_{i}\,.

(50)

Continuity is then

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{S}_{i+}\vec{C}^{S}_{+}

\displaystyle\overset{!}{=}\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i(0-)}\vec{C}^{B}_{0-}+b_{i}\longrightarrow\hat{\phi}^{S}_{i+}\vec{C}^{S}_{+}-\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i(0-)}\vec{C}^{B}_{0-}=b_{i}\,.

(51)

We obtain a linear set of equations,

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{SB}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}|&|\\
\hat{\phi}^{S}_{i+}&\hat{\phi}^{B}_{i(0-)}\\
|&|\end{array}\right)\,,

\displaystyle\vec{C}_{SB}

\displaystyle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\vec{C}^{S}_{+}\\
-\vec{C}^{B}_{0-}\end{pmatrix}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{SB}\ \vec{C}_{SB}\overset{!}{=}b_{i}\,.

(55)

Solving it sets the rest of the coefficients.

The solution

The
BAU
is then given by

\displaystyle Y_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\Gamma_{\text{ws}}}{2D_{q}\alpha_{+}s}\int\limits_{0}^{-\infty}e^{-\alpha_{-}x}n_{L}(x)\ dx\,,

(56)

where

is the density of left handed particles in the symmetric phase (where the weak sphaleron process is efficient),

\displaystyle n_{L}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(Q_{Li}(z)+L_{Li}(z)\right)\,,

(57)

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2D_{q}}\left(v_{w}\pm\sqrt{4D_{q}\Gamma_{\text{ws}}\mathcal{R}+v_{w}^{2}}\right)\,.

(58)
