Limiting spectral distribution of random self-adjoint quantum channels
Cécilia Lancien and Patrick Oliveira Santos and Pierre Youssef

Abstract
. We study the limiting spectral distribution of quantum channels whose Kraus operators are sampled as n × n n\times n random Hermitian matrices satisfying certain assumptions. We show that when the Kraus rank goes to infinity with n n , the limiting spectral distribution (suitably rescaled) of the corresponding quantum channel coincides with the semi-circle distribution. When the Kraus rank is fixed, the limiting spectral distribution is no longer the semi-circle distribution. It corresponds to an explicit law, which can also be described using tools from free probability.



1.
Introduction

In quantum physics, the state of an
n
-dimensional system is described by a density operator on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e.
\rho\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
a Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix with trace
1
:
\rho\succeq 0
and
\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1
. A transformation of such a quantum system is described by a quantum channel on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, i.e.
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
a completely positive and trace-preserving linear map. We recall that a linear map
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is said to be

•

positive if it preserves the fact of being Hermitian and positive semidefinite: for all
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
X\succeq 0
implies that
\Phi(X)\succeq 0
;

•

completely positive if
\Phi\otimes\operatorname{Id}:M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
is positive (where
\operatorname{Id}:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the identity map);

•

trace-preserving if it preserves the trace: for all
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\operatorname{tr}(\Phi(X))=\operatorname{tr}(X)
.

A quantum channel
\Phi
thus maps quantum states to quantum states (and so does
\Phi\otimes\mathrm{id}
).

The action of a completely positive map
\Phi
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
can always be described in the following (non-unique) way, called a Kraus representation of
\Phi
(see e.g.
[
3
, Section 2.3.2]
or
[
25
, Chapter 2]
): There exist
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, called Kraus operators of
\Phi
, such that

(1)

\Phi:X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}XK_{i}^{*}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),

where
K_{i}^{*}
stands for the adjoint of
K_{i}
(and where we have used the shorthand notation
[d]
for the set of integers
\{1,\ldots,d\}
). The fact that
\Phi
is trace-preserving is equivalent to the following constraint on the Kraus operators
K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}
:

\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}^{*}K_{i}=\operatorname{Id}.

The smallest
d
such that an expression of the form of equation (
1
) for
\Phi
exists is called the Kraus rank of
\Phi
. It is always at most
n^{2}
for a completely positive map on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Given a completely positive map
\Phi
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, its adjoint (or dual) is defined by duality with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, i.e. as the map
\Phi^{*}
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
(which is completely positive as well) such that, for all
X,Y\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,

(2)

\operatorname{tr}\left(X\Phi^{*}(Y)\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Phi(X)Y\right).

\Phi
being trace-preserving is equivalent to
\Phi^{*}
being unital, i.e. such that
\Phi^{*}(\operatorname{Id})=\operatorname{Id}
.

Note that, identifying
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, a linear map
\Phi:M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\to M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
can equivalently be seen as a linear map
M_{\Phi}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
, i.e. an element of
M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
. Concretely, a completely positive linear map

\Phi:X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}XK_{i}^{*}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

can be identified with

(3)

M_{\Phi}=\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}\otimes\overline{K}_{i}\in M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C}),

where
\overline{K}_{i}
stands for the entry-wise conjugate of
K_{i}
, in the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This identification preserves the spectrum, i.e.
\mathrm{spec}(\Phi)=\mathrm{spec}(M_{\Phi})
. Moreover, the matrix version of the adjoint
\Phi^{*}
of
\Phi
is simply the adjoint of
M_{\Phi}
, i.e.
M_{\Phi^{*}}=M_{\Phi}^{*}
.

In this paper, we will consider quantum channels
\Phi
whose Kraus operators are Hermitian matrices, which ensures that
\Phi
itself is Hermitian (in the sense that it is equal to its adjoint
\Phi^{*}
, as defined by equation (
2
)), or equivalently that its matrix version
M_{\Phi}
is Hermitian. This may look like a restrictive setting, but it, in fact, encompasses all Hermitian quantum channels. Indeed, it is not hard to see that any Hermitian completely positive map
\Phi
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
admits a Kraus representation with Hermitian Kraus operators, where we can additionally guarantee that the number of such operators is at most twice the Kraus rank of
\Phi
(i.e. in general at most
2n^{2}
). Concretely, if
K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are
d
Kraus operators for
\Phi
, then
K_{1,R},K_{1,I},\ldots,K_{d,R},K_{d,I}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are
2d
Hermitian Kraus operators for
\Phi
, where given
K\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we set
K_{R}=(K+K^{*})/2,\,K_{I}=-i(K-K^{*})/2
, so that
K=K_{R}+iK_{I}
and
K_{R}^{*}=K_{R},\,K_{I}^{*}=K_{I}
. The latter claim follows from the observation that,if
M_{\Phi}^{*}=M_{\Phi}
, then we can re-write
M_{\Phi}=(M_{\Phi}+M_{\Phi}^{*})/2
, and thus

M_{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(K_{j}\otimes\overline{K}_{j}+K_{j}^{*}\otimes\overline{K}_{j}^{*}\right)=\sum_{j\in[d]}\left(K_{j,R}\otimes\overline{K}_{j,R}+K_{j,I}\otimes\overline{K}_{j,I}\right).

Now, many interesting and well-studied classes of quantum channels are Hermitian: depolarizing channels, dephasing channels, and Pauli channels, to name just a few. Note that such channels are, in particular, unital.

This work aims to study the spectrum of a randomly generated quantum channel as the underlying dimension
n
goes to infinity. More precisely, given a quantum channel
\Phi
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
whose Kraus operators
K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
have been randomly sampled, we aim to characterize its asymptotic spectrum (i.e. its spectrum in the limit where
n
goes to infinity).
Our study will involve two regimes, one where the Kraus rank
d
is fixed and only
n
grows, and the other one where
d=d(n)
is a growing function of
n
.

Previous related works were mostly concerned with identifying the spectral gap of a random quantum channel, i.e. the difference between its largest and second largest eigenvalues. Indeed, it is known that a quantum channel
\Phi
on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
always has its largest (in modulus) eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(\Phi)
equal to
1
(with an associated eigenvector which is a positive semidefinite matrix), implying that
\Phi
always has a fixed state. It was established that random quantum channels generically have their largest eigenvalue
1
isolated from the rest of the spectrum. All other eigenvalues are of order at most
1/\sqrt{d}
(in modulus). This was shown first for specific models, where Kraus operators were sampled either as independent Haar unitaries
[
16
,
22
]
, or as blocks of a Haar isometry
[
15
]
, or as independent Ginibre matrices (i.e. matrices having i.i.d. Gaussian entries)
[
18
]
, and recently in greater generality
[
19
]
.

On the other hand, much less is known concerning the asymptotic distribution of the bulk of the spectrum. To understand this, one studies the empirical spectral distribution, which, given a matrix
M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{M}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\in[n]}\delta_{\lambda_{k}(M)},

where
\lambda_{k}(M)
is the
k
th eigenvalue of
M
.
In
[
8
]
, a model where Kraus operators are sampled as blocks of a Haar isometry was studied. It was conjectured, from heuristic arguments and numerical simulations, that in the regime where
d=d(n)=n^{2}
, the empirical spectral distribution (rescaled by a factor
\sqrt{d}
) of such random quantum channel converges towards a circular distribution as
n
grows. It was later shown in
[
2
]
that, in the same regime
d=d(n)
of order
n^{2}
, the empirical singular value distribution (again rescaled by a factor
\sqrt{d}
) of a random quantum channel whose Kraus operators are sampled as independent Ginibre matrices converges towards a quarter-circular distribution as
n
grows.
We believe that for both models (and for more general ones) and in any regime
d=d(n)\to\infty
, the limiting spectral distribution coincides with the circular law.

In this work, we embark on the investigation of the limiting spectral distribution of quantum channels in the Hermitian setting. This serves as a precursor to our broader research goals involving the non-Hermitian case and the conjecture presented above. Classical problems in Random Matrix Theory have traditionally prioritized the exploration of Hermitian matrices before delving into their non-Hermitian counterparts, partly due to the inherent technical complexities introduced by non-Hermitian systems, notably the instability of the spectrum under perturbations (see
[
4
, Chapter 11]
). For instance, while the limiting spectral distribution of an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix with i.i.d. (up to symmetry) centered entries of variance
1/n
was shown to be Wigner’s semi-circle distribution, it is almost half a century later that the analogous result in the non-Hermitian case, Girko’s circular law theorem, was established in full generality
[
23
]
. Understanding the limiting spectral distribution in the non-Hermitian case follows the Hermitization technique invented by Girko
[
13
,
14
]
. The latter requires a quantitative control on the smallest singular value of the corresponding random matrix model (see
[
6
]
for an introduction to the method), making the problem significantly more involved. In the context of quantum channels, the associated random matrix model exhibits dependencies among its entries, which adds a layer of difficulty compared to classical random matrix models, even in the Hermitian setting.

Now, let us shift our focus back to the specific context of this paper. Here, we will consider the case where the Kraus operators of the quantum channel
\Phi
are chosen to be random Hermitian operators. This ensures that the resulting completely positive map
\Phi
, or equivalently its matrix version
M_{\Phi}
, is Hermitian. Conversely, as explained earlier, any Hermitian completely positive map
\Phi
can be written with Hermitian Kraus operators. Moreover, we aim to keep our assumptions regarding the distribution of these random Kraus operators as minimal as possible. Our objective is to gain a comprehensive understanding of the spectrum of
M_{\Phi}
as the dimension
n
increases without imposing specific constraints on the scaling relationship between
d
and
n
.

To keep the introduction light, we state an informal version of our main result and differ from the rigorous statement, requiring notions from free probability, to Section
2
(see Theorem
2.2
).

Theorem 1.1

(Informal Statement)
.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be independent Hermitian random matrices such that, for each
i\in[d]
,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i})=0
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}^{2})=\operatorname{Id}
. Suppose additionally that, for each
i\in[d]
, the spectral distribution of
W_{i}
converges to some distribution
\mu_{i}
as
n\to\infty
and that the joint asymptotic distribution of the family
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
is determined by the family
(\mu_{i})_{i\in[d]}
. Next, set
K_{i}=W_{i}/\sqrt{d}
for each
i\in[d]
and define
\Phi
as the random completely positive map on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
having
K_{1},\ldots,K_{d}
as Kraus operators, i.e.

\Phi:X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}XK_{i}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C}).

First,
\Phi
is, on average, trace-preserving and unital. And second,

•

If
d
is fixed, then the spectral distribution of
\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi)
converges to a specific distribution, depending only on
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}
, as
n\to\infty
.

•

If
d=d(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
and
W_{i}
are i.i.d, then the spectral distribution of
\sqrt{d}(\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi))
converges to the semicircular distribution as
n\to\infty
.

The specific assumption underlying the family of independent matrices
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
is precisely asymptotic freeness, a concept introduced by Voiculescu
[
24
]
within the framework of Free Probability. Asymptotic freeness proves instrumental in a systematical examination of the asymptotic behavior of random matrices. Importantly, this assumption is not restrictive as many classical independent random matrices are known to be asymptotically free. However, this property is no longer valid in the context of tensors (see
[
10
]
), preventing a direct derivation of the asymptotic joint spectrum of these random tensors, which is the setting of interest in this paper. To overcome this, we analyze the moment method directly on our model in order to derive the limiting distribution.

The required notions from Free Probability are presented in Section
2
, where the main rigorous theorem is stated. The proofs are carried out in Section
3
.
Section
4
regroups a variety of examples for which our result applies, while Section
5
offers perspectives for future work.

2.
Preliminaries and rigorous statement of the main result

2.1.
Preliminaries

We begin by recalling some notation from Free Probability; see
[
21
]
. A noncommutative probability space is a pair
(\mathcal{A},\tau)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a unital algebra equipped with a tracial state
\tau
, that is,
\tau
is linear,
\tau(1)=1
and
\tau(ab)=\tau(ba)
. We say that subalgebras
\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{d}
are free if

(4)

\displaystyle\tau(a_{1}\ldots a_{p})=0

whenever
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}_{j_{i}}
,
\tau(a_{i})=0
for all
i\in[p]
and
j_{1}\neq j_{2}\neq\cdots\neq j_{p}
. We say that random variables
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\in\mathcal{A}
are free if their algebras are free. The distribution of a self-adjoint variable
a=a^{*}
is defined as the collection of moments

\displaystyle\{\tau(a^{p}):\,p\in\mathbb{N}\}.

In particular, there always exists a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\tau(a^{p})=\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\mu,

for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Such a measure is also called the distribution of
a
. A particular example of free variables is the free semicircular system
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})
, whose moments satisfy

\displaystyle\tau(s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{p}})=\sum_{\pi\in NC_{2}(p)}\prod_{(l,k)\in\pi}\delta_{i_{l}i_{k}}.

Here,
NC_{2}(p)
is the set of all noncrossing pair partitions of
[p]
, namely, each block of
\pi\in NC_{2}(p)
has cardinality
2
and
\pi
does not contain two blocks
\{i,j\},\{k,l\}
such that
i<k<j<l
. In particular, the moments of the semicircular variable are

\displaystyle\tau(s^{p})=|NC_{2}(p)|=\int x^{p}f_{sc}(x)\,\text{d}x,

where
f_{sc}
denotes the density of the semi-circle distribution
\mu_{sc}

(5)

\displaystyle f_{sc}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq 2}.

Given a matrix
M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we define its normalized trace as

\displaystyle\tau^{(n)}(M):=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{tr}(M).

In particular, for two matrices
M,N\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\displaystyle\tau^{(n^{2})}(M\otimes N)=\tau^{(n)}\otimes\tau^{(n)}(M\otimes N)=\tau^{(n)}(M)\tau^{(n)}(N).

We say that a random Hermitian matrix
M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is
normalized
if

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(M)\right)=0;

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(M^{2})\right)=1.

We say that a sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random Hermitian matrices
M_{n}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\operatorname{tr}(M_{n}^{p})
is integrable for all
p\geq 1
converges weakly in probability (resp. in expectation) to
\mu
, if
(\mu_{M_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
\mu
in probability (resp. in expectation). Equivalently, for any
p\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\tau^{(n)}(M_{n}^{p})=\frac{1}{n}\text{tr}(M_{n}^{p})\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\mu\

in probability and

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(M_{n}^{p})\right)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\text{tr}(M_{n}^{p})\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\mu

for the convergence in expectation. Whenever
(\mu_{M_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
\mu
, we denote it by
\mu_{M_{n}}\Rightarrow\mu
, or simply
M_{n}\Rightarrow\mu
as
n\to\infty
. In particular, we can find a noncommutative random variable
a
such that
a
has distribution
\mu
and
M_{n}
converges to
a
.

Finally, we say that
d
random Hermitian matrices
M_{1},\ldots,M_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are asymptotically free in probability if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau^{(n)}\Big(\left(M_{i_{1}}^{p_{1}}-\tau^{(n)}(M_{i_{1}}^{p_{1}})\right)\cdots\left(M_{i_{m}}^{p_{m}}-\tau^{(n)}(M_{i_{m}}^{p_{m}})\right)\Big)=0

in probability, for all
m\geq 1
,
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq\cdots\neq i_{m}\in[d]
and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\geq 1
. They are asymptotically free in expectation if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\tau^{(n)}\Big(\left(M_{i_{1}}^{p_{1}}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(M_{i_{1}}^{p_{1}})\right)\right)\cdots\left(M_{i_{m}}^{p_{m}}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(M_{i_{m}}^{p_{m}})\right)\right)\Big)\Big]=0,

for all
m\geq 1
,
i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq\cdots\neq i_{m}\in[d]
and
p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\geq 1
. This is equivalent to (
4
) in the limit for the algebras
\mathcal{A}(M_{i})
generated by each
M_{i}
. As usual, we write
M+\lambda:=M+\lambda\operatorname{Id}
, where
M\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 2.1
.

We have defined weak convergence as the convergence of the moments of all order. In particular, this requires that
\operatorname{tr}(M_{n}^{p})
is integrable for all
p\geq 1
. In several cases, however, such strong integrability is not needed, and similar results can be proved for a larger class of random matrices via truncation techniques
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.21]
.

2.2.
Rigorous results

Let
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{d}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
be free variables with distribution
\mu_{i}
, respectively. We define the tensor measure
\mu_{i}\star\mu_{i}
as the measure associated with
a_{i}\otimes a_{i}
and the tensor convolution
\mu_{1}\circledast\cdots\circledast\mu_{d}
as the distribution of

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[d]}a_{i}\otimes a_{i}=a_{1}\otimes a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}\otimes a_{d}.

In other words, we have

\displaystyle\int x^{k}\,\text{d}\mu_{i}\star\mu_{i}=\tau\otimes\tau(a_{i}^{k}\otimes a_{i}^{k})=\tau^{2}(a_{i}^{k}),

and

\displaystyle\int x^{k}\,\text{d}\mu_{1}\circledast\cdots\circledast\mu_{d}=\tau\otimes\tau\left(\sum_{i\in[d]}a_{i}\otimes a_{i}\right)^{k}=\sum_{i\in[d]^{k}}\tau^{2}(a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{k}}).

Note that
\mu_{1}\circledast\cdots\circledast\mu_{d}
is not necessarily the free convolution of
\mu_{i}\star\mu_{i}
. Indeed, it was proved in
[
10
]
that freeness for tensor products
a_{i}\otimes a_{i}
does not follow from freeness of
a_{i}
.

In the sequel, we will be considering random Hermitian matrices
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
satisfying the following assumptions:

(
A.1
)

For each
i\in[d]
,
W_{i}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
\mu_{i}
;

(
A.2
)

The family
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
is in probability and in expectation asymptotically free, for each
d
fixed.

(
A.3
)

For each
i\in[d]
,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}})\in M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
converges weakly to
0
.

(
A.4
)

The matrices
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
are independent and identically distributed.

As we will see in Section
4
, a variety of classical random matrix models satisfy those assumptions.
We are ready to state our main result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be centered Hermitian random matrices satisfying Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
and
d=d(n)
. Then, the following holds.

(
2.2
.i)

If
d
is fixed, then

\displaystyle\Delta:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i})\right)

converges weakly in probability and in expectation to the tensor convolution of
\tilde{\mu}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\mu}_{d}
, where
\tilde{\mu}
denotes the dilation of
\mu
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\tilde{\mu}:=\frac{1}{d^{p/2}}\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\mu.

This means that

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\tilde{\mu}_{1}\circledast\cdots\circledast\tilde{\mu}_{d},

in probability and in expectation.

(
2.2
.ii)

If
d=d(n)
diverges,
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
are also normalized and satisfy Assumption
(A.4)
, then
\Delta

converges weakly in probability and in expectation to the semicircular distribution
\mu_{sc}
. This means that

\displaystyle\mu_{\Delta}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\mu_{sc},

in probability and in expectation.

3.
Proofs

We start with the following observation.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
(A.1)
and
(A.3)
hold. For each
i\in[d]
,
B_{i}=W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}\right)
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
a_{i}\otimes a_{i}
, where
a_{i}
has distribution
\mu_{i}
.

Proof.

For Hermitian matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, such that
M_{1}\Rightarrow\mu_{1}
and
M_{2}\Rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle M_{1}+M_{2}\Rightarrow\mu_{1}.

Indeed, using Holder’s Inequality, we can bound the trace of a product of the matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
by

(6)

\displaystyle\left|\tau^{(n)}\left(\prod_{j\in[m]}M_{i_{j}}\right)\right|\leq\prod_{j\in[m]}\left(\tau^{(n)}|M_{i_{j}}|^{p_{j}}\right)^{1/p_{j}},

where
i\in\{1,2\}^{m}
and
(p_{j})_{j\in[m]}
are conjugate exponents. If there is at least one
j\in[m]
such that
i_{j}=2
, then the right-hand side of (
6
) goes to
0
. In particular, since

\displaystyle\tau^{(n)}\left((M_{1}+M_{2})^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}^{p}}\tau^{(n)}\left(\prod_{j\in[p]}M_{i_{j}}\right),

any term associated with
i\in\{1,2\}^{p}
having at least one index
i_{j}=2
will asymptotically vanish. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau^{(n)}\left((M_{1}+M_{2})^{p}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tau^{(n)}\left(M_{1}^{p}\right).

The result of the lemma follows by taking
M_{1}=W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}}
and
M_{2}=-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}})
, for each
i\in[d]
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\tau^{(n)}\otimes\tau^{(n)}\left(\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}\right)^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\tau^{(n)}\left(W_{i}^{p}\right)\right)^{2},

since the eigenvalues of
W_{i}
are real. Hence, the weak convergence in probability and in expectation of
W_{i}
to
\mu_{i}
implies that

\displaystyle\tau^{(n)}\otimes\tau^{(n)}\left(\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}\right)^{p}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\left(\int x^{p}\,\text{d}\mu_{i}\right)^{2}

in probability and in expectation. This finishes the proof.
∎

As an immediate consequence, we have the following.

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
, and
(A.3)
hold. Let
B_{i}=W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}})
. Then, for each
d
fixed,
(B_{i})_{i\in[d]}
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
(a_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in[d]}
, where
a_{i}
are free variables with distribution
\mu_{i}
.

Proof.

By Assumption
(A.2)
, we can assume
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
(a_{i})_{i\in[d]}
, where
a_{i}
are free. The result follows by Lemma
3.1
.
∎

We are ready to prove Theorem
(.i)
.

Proof of Theorem
(.i)
.

By Corollary
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[d]}B_{i}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\sum_{i\in[d]}a_{i}\otimes a_{i},

where
a_{i}
are free with distribution
\mu_{i}
. To conclude, it suffices to note that
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
is the distribution of
a_{i}\otimes a_{i}/\sqrt{d}
.
∎

Before proving Theorem
(.ii)
, let us give a sufficient condition for Assumption
(A.3)
to hold.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}
satisfying Assumption
(A.1)
and assume that

(7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\tau(W_{i}))\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}0,

for all
i\in[d]
. Let
(W_{i}^{(l)})_{l\in[L]}
be independent copies of
W_{i}
. If for each
i\in[d]
and
L\geq 1
,
(W_{i}^{(l)})_{l\in[L]}
are asymptotically free in probability and in expectation, then Assumption
(A.3)
holds.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[d]
. Then, we can easily see that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}}\right)\right)^{p}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\operatorname{tr}^{2}\left(W_{i}^{(1)}\cdots W_{i}^{(p)}\right)\right).

Since
(W_{i}^{(l)})_{l\in[p]}
are asymptotically free in probability and in expectation, we get that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W_{i}}\right)\right)^{p}\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\tau^{2}(a_{i}^{(1)}\cdots a_{i}^{(p)}),

where
(a_{i}^{(l)})_{l\in[p]}
are free i.i.d with distribution
\mu_{i}
. The centered Assumption (
7
) implies that
\mu_{i}
is centered, hence
\tau(a_{i}^{(1)}\cdots a_{i}^{(p)})=0
by freeness and the result follows.
∎

3.1.
The asymptotic free Central Limit Theorem

In order to prove Theorem
(.ii)
, we begin with a simple lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that Assumption
(A.4)
holds. For each
i\in[d]
, set
B_{i}:=W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i})
. Let
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{p})\in[d]^{p}
and suppose that there exists
k\in[p]
such that, for all
l\in[p]
with
l\neq k
,
i_{l}\neq i_{k}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\operatorname{tr}(B_{i_{1}}\cdots B_{i_{p}})\right)=0.

Proof.

We can assume without loss of generality that
k=1
, i.e.
i_{1}\notin\{i_{2},\ldots,i_{p}\}
. In this case, notice that
B_{i_{1}}
is independent of
B:=B_{i_{2}}\cdots B_{i_{p}}
by Assumption
(A.4)
, hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\operatorname{tr}(B_{i_{1}}B)\right)=\sum_{k_{1},k_{2}\in[n]^{2}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(B_{i_{1}}(k_{1},k_{2})\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(B(k_{2},k_{1})\right).

Since
B_{i_{1}}
is centered, we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(B_{i_{1}}(k_{1},k_{2}))=0
and the result follows.
∎

Although Lemma
3.4
is a simple consequence of independence and centering, it is a powerful property that will allow us to obtain a limit distribution for
\Delta
.
We are now ready to prove Theorem
(.ii)
.

Proof of Theorem
(.ii)
.

We begin by writing
\Delta
as

\displaystyle\Delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}B_{i},

where
B_{i}=W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}\otimes\overline{W}_{i})
. We recall that, given
X\in M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{C})
a random matrix, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(X)\right)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\operatorname{tr}(X)\right).

Then, we can compute the moments of
\Delta
by

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=d^{-p/2}\sum_{i\in[d]^{p}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}\left(B_{i_{1}}\cdots B_{i_{p}}\right)\right).

For every
i\in[d]^{p}
, we associate a partition
\pi(i)
of
[p]
defined by placing any
k,\ell\in[p]
in the same block of
\pi(i)
whenever
i_{k}=i_{l}
. We denote by
P(p)
the set of all partitions of
[p]
.
Notice that for every
i\in[d]^{p}
,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(B_{i_{1}}\cdots B_{i_{p}})\right)
only depends on the partition
\pi(i)
, since
B_{i}
are identically distributed by Assumption
(A.4)
. In particular, let
\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)
be the common value of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(B_{i_{1}}\cdots B_{i_{p}})\right)
for
\pi(i)=\pi
. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=d^{-p/2}\sum_{\pi\in P(p)}\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)\left|\{i\in[d]^{p}:\pi(i)=\pi\}\right|.

The cardinality can be computed by simply choosing an index for each block of
\pi
, namely,

\displaystyle\left|\{i\in[d]^{p}:\pi(i)=\pi\}\right|=d(d-1)\cdots(d-|\pi|+1)=d^{|\pi|}\left(1+O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\right),

where
f=O_{p}(1/d)
means that
f\leq C_{p}/d
, for some constant
C_{p}
that depends only on
p
. We then have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=\sum_{\pi\in P(p)}\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)d^{|\pi|-p/2}\left(1+O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\right).

By Lemma
3.4
, if
\pi\in P(p)
has a block
V
of size
1
, then we would have
\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)=0
. Hence, we can restrict to partitions without single blocks, yielding

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in P(p)\\
\forall V\in\pi,\,|V|\geq 2\end{subarray}}\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)d^{|\pi|-p/2}\left(1+O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{d}\right)\right).

Using Assumption
(A.1)
and Holder’s Inequality, we have that

(8)

\displaystyle\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)\leq\max_{i\in[d]}\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(|B_{i}|^{p})\right)\right]^{1/p}\leq C_{p},

where
C_{p}<\infty
is a constant independent of
n
.
Hence, whenever
\pi
has a block of size
|V|\geq 3
, we have
|\pi|<p/2
and

\displaystyle\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)d^{|\pi|-p/2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

In particular, the only partitions that contribute to the dominating term are the pair partitions; that is, every block has cardinality two. Let
P_{2}(p)
be the set of all pair partitions, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=\sum_{\pi\in P_{2}(p)}\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)+O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Note that
\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)
depends only on
B_{1},\ldots,B_{p}
. Corollary
3.2
implies that
(B_{i})_{i\in[d]}
converges to
(a_{i}\otimes a_{i})_{i\in[d]}
in probability and in expectation, where
a_{i}
are free variables and the limit of
W_{i}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\tau^{(n^{2})}(\pi)\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\tau\otimes\tau(\pi):=\tau^{2}(a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{p}}),

In particular, freeness implies that
\tau\otimes\tau(\pi)=0
if and only if
\pi\in P_{2}(p)\setminus NC_{2}(p)
, where
NC_{2}(p)
is the set of noncrossing pair partitions. If
\pi\in NC_{2}(p)
, then
\tau\otimes\tau(\pi)=1
because the matrices are normalized. We deduce that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=|NC_{2}(p)|+o_{p}(1)+O_{p}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

where
f=o_{p}(1)
denotes a function that depends on
p
and
f\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This proves the convergence in expectation. To get convergence in probability, set

\displaystyle\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)=\tau^{(n^{2})}(B_{j_{1}}\cdots B_{j_{p}}),

for each
j\in[d]^{p}
. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=\frac{1}{d^{p}}\sum_{i,j\in[d]^{p}}\left\{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)\right\}.

Now, each index
i_{l},j_{l}
must appear at least twice in
i\cup j
, as the matrices are centered and independent. In particular, the summation has at most
d^{p}
indices. We thus have

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)\leq\max_{i,j\in[d]^{p}}\left\{\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)\right\}.

Assumption
(A.1)
implies that
(B_{i})_{i\in[p]}
are uniformly integrable, then the Dominated Convergence Theorem
[
11
, Theorem 1.5.8]
and Corollary
3.2
imply that

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}\tau\otimes\tau(\pi(i))\tau\otimes\tau(\pi(j)),

The same limit holds for
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(i)\right)\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(j)\right)
, and therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left(\tau^{(n^{2})}(\Delta^{p})\right)=o_{p}(1),

from which convergence in probability follows.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

As we saw in the proof, the only assumption for the existence of a limit of
\Delta
is the centering assumption in Lemma
3.4
. In particular, the proof of Theorem
(.ii)
works verbatim for noncommutative variables. If
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
are centered exchangeable variables such that Lemma
3.4
holds, that is,
\tau(a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{p}})=0
whenever there exists an index different than the others, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\tau\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}a_{i}\right)^{p}=\sum_{\pi\in P_{2}(p)}\tau(\pi).

If in addition
(a_{i})_{i\in[d]}=(a_{i}^{(n)})_{i\in[d]}
depends on some parameter
n
and are asymptotically free, as
n
grows, we get an asymptotic free Central Limit Theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{n,d\to\infty}\tau^{(n)}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}a_{i}^{(n)}\right)^{p}=|NC_{2}(p)|.

4.
Examples

We begin with an almost surely unital and trace-preserving Hermitian quantum channel.

Example 4.1

(Rademacher diagonal matrices)
.

Let
R=\mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_{k})_{k\in[n]}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be a Rademacher diagonal matrix, i.e.
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}
are independent Rademacher (or symmetric Bernoulli) random variables with parameter
1/2
. Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be independent Haar unitary matrices independent of
R
as well and set
W_{i}=U_{i}RU_{i}^{*}
for each
i\in[d]
. It follows from
[
24
]
that
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
are asymptotically free and Lemma
3.3
yields Assumption
(A.3)
. Theorem
(.i)
thus implies that

\displaystyle\Delta\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}r_{i}\otimes r_{i},

in probability and in expectation, where
(r_{i})_{i\in[d]}
are free with Rademacher distribution. In particular, for a polynomial

\displaystyle p(x):=b_{0}+\sum_{l\in[m]}b_{l}x^{l},

we immediately compute

\displaystyle p(r_{i})-\tau(p(r_{i}))=\left(\sum_{l\in[\lceil m/2\rceil]}b_{2l-1}\right)r_{i};

\displaystyle p(r_{i}\otimes r_{i})-\tau\otimes\tau(p(r_{i}\otimes r_{i}))=\left(\sum_{l\in[\lceil m/2\rceil]}b_{2l-1}\right)r_{i}\otimes r_{i}.

Since
\tau(r_{i})=0
, we can readily see that the freeness of
(r_{i})_{i\in[d]}
is equivalent to the freeness of
(r_{i}\otimes r_{i})_{i\in[d]}
in this case. Hence
(r_{i}\otimes r_{i})_{i\in[d]}\overset{d}{=}(r_{i})_{i\in[d]}
where the equality holds in distribution. If we denote the limit of
\Delta
by
z
, we then have

\displaystyle z\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}r_{i}.

For
d
even, we can precisely compute this limit. Indeed,
[
21
, Example 12.8.1]
shows that the free convolution
(r_{1}+r_{2})
has arcsine distribution. Moreover, such a distribution was proved to be the law of
u+u^{*}
in
[
21
, Example 1.14]
, where
u
is a Haar unitary. We then deduce that

\displaystyle z\overset{d}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d/2]}(u_{i}+u_{i}^{*}).

The distribution of the free sum of
u_{i}+u_{i}^{*}
is called the Kesten-McKay distribution with parameter
d
; see
[
17
]
,
[
21
, Exercise 12.21]
. Its density is given by

\displaystyle f_{KM(d)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d^{2}-x^{2}}\sqrt{4(d-1)-x^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}}.

It is also the limit spectral distribution of random
d
-regular graphs
[
20
]
. Therefore, the density of
b
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{KM(d)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d}{d-x^{2}}\sqrt{4\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)-x^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq 2\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}}}.

A direct computation shows that, for all
x\in(-2,2)
,
\tilde{f}_{KM(d)}(x)\to f_{sc}(x)
as
d\to\infty
. This is a local version of Theorem
(.ii)
.

Let us interpret Example
4.1
above in terms of the corresponding random quantum channel. Given
W=URU^{*}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
a uniformly rotated Rademacher matrix, we have
W^{2}=R^{2}=\operatorname{Id}
. Hence, sampling
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
independently and uniformly rotated Rademacher matrices and setting
K_{i}=W_{i}/\sqrt{d}
for each
i\in[d]
, the random completely positive map

\Phi:X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}XK_{i}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

is exactly trace-preserving and unital (not just on average), i.e. it is a random unital quantum channel. More precisely, it is a mixture of unitary conjugations. What is more, by what precedes, the spectral distribution of
\sqrt{d}(\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi))
converges weakly almost surely to
\tilde{\mu}_{KM(d)}
(for
d
even) as
n\to\infty
. And the spectral distribution of
\sqrt{d}(\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi))
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
\mu_{sc}
as
n,d\to\infty
.

Remark 4.2
.

There is a straightforward generalization of the previous example to deterministic matrices. Let
M_{1},\ldots,M_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be deterministic Hermitian matrices such that, for each
i\in[d]
,
M_{i}\Rightarrow\mu_{i}
as
n\to\infty
and
\mu
is centered. A natural way to make them asymptotically free is by conjugating them with independent Haar unitaries. In particular, if we set, for each
i\in[d]
,
W_{i}=U_{i}M_{i}U_{i}^{*}
, where
U_{1},\ldots,U_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
are independent uniformly chosen unitaries, then
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
satisfies Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
and
(A.4)
. Indeed, for instance, Assumption
(A.3)
follows by Lemma
3.3
. So that we can apply Theorem
2.2
to this kind of matrices.

Example 4.3

(Wigner matrices)
.

A random Hermitian matrix
W=(W_{kl})_{k,l\in[n]}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a Wigner matrix if
W_{kl}
are i.i.d. centered with variance
1/n
for
k,l\in[n]
such that
k\leq l
and
W_{kl}=\overline{W_{lk}}
for
k,l\in[n]
such that
l<k
. It is well-known
[
1
,
12
,
24
]
that independent Wigner matrices
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}
satisfy Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
(by Lemma
3.3
) and
(A.4)
. Their joint law
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
converges weakly in probability to a free semicircular family
(s_{i})_{i\in[d]}
. Therefore, Theorem
2.2
holds and the limit in Theorem
(.i)
is expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}s_{i}\otimes s_{i},

in probability and in expectation.

Let us interpret Example
4.3
above in terms of the corresponding random quantum channel. Given
W\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
a Wigner matrix, we have
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{kl})=0
and
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}|W_{kl}|^{2}=1/n
for all
k,l\in[n]
and
(W_{kl})_{k\leq l\in[n]}
independent. Therefore,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(W^{2}\right)=\sum_{k\in[n]}E_{kk}=\operatorname{Id}.

So we can conclude that, sampling
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
independent Wigner matrices and setting
K_{i}=W_{i}/\sqrt{d}
for each
i\in[d]
, the random completely positive map

\Phi:X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\sum_{i\in[d]}K_{i}XK_{i}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})

is, on average, trace-preserving and unital. Moreover, the spectral distribution of
d(\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi))
converges weakly almost surely to
\mu_{sc}^{\circledast d}
as
n\to\infty
, and the spectral distribution of
\sqrt{d}(\Phi-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi))
converges weakly in probability and in expectation to
\mu_{sc}
as
n,d\to\infty
.
In this case, we can actually explicitly compute
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi)
. Indeed, denoting by
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and setting
E_{kl}=e_{k}e_{l}^{*}
for each
k,l\in[n]
, we have

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(W\otimes\overline{W}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k,l\in[n]}E_{kl}\otimes E_{kl}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k\neq l\in[n]}E_{kl}\otimes E_{lk}=\psi\psi^{*}+\frac{1}{n}\left(F-\operatorname{diag}(F)\right),

where
\psi=\sum_{k\in[n]}(e_{k}\otimes e_{k})/\sqrt{n}
is a maximally entangled unit vector,
F=\sum_{k,l\in[n]}E_{kl}\otimes E_{lk}
is the flip operator and
\operatorname{diag}(F)=\sum_{k\in[n]}E_{kk}\otimes E_{kk}
is its diagonal part (with respect to the canonical product basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
).
F-\operatorname{diag}(F)
has spectrum
\{1,-1,0\}
, where
1
and
-1
have multiplicities
n(n-1)/2
(with associated eigenvectors
\{(e_{k}\otimes e_{l}+e_{l}\otimes e_{k})/\sqrt{2},\ k<l\in[n]\}
and
\{(e_{k}\otimes e_{l}-e_{l}\otimes e_{k})/\sqrt{2},\ k<l\in[n]\}
respectively) and
0
has multiplicity
n
. Hence,

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(M_{\Phi})=\psi\psi^{*}+\frac{1}{n}\left(F-\operatorname{diag}(F)\right).

This can be re-written at the level of
\Phi
as

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi):X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(X)\frac{\operatorname{Id}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\left(X^{T}-\operatorname{diag}(X)\right)\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),

where
X^{T}
denotes the transposition of
X
and
\operatorname{diag}(X)
its diagonal part, both with respect to the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This means that, up to a correction that vanishes as
n
grows,
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(\Phi)
is the so-called fully randomizing channel
\Pi:X\mapsto\operatorname{tr}(X)\operatorname{Id}/n
, which has one eigenvalue equal to
1
, with associated eigenvector the maximally mixed state
\operatorname{Id}/n
, and all the other eigenvalues equal to
0
. Our result thus gives a precise understanding of how the asymptotic spectrum of
\Phi
deviates from the flat one of
\Pi
, for
d
either fixed or growing.

We can also consider non-homogeneous matrices as in
[
19
,
5
]
.

Example 4.4

(Non-homogeneous matrices)
.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be independent Gaussian matrices such that, for each
i\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i})\|\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}0;

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(W_{i}^{2})-\text{Id}\|\underset{n\to\infty}{\to}0.

Then it was proven in
[
5
, Theorem 2.10]
that
(W_{i})_{i\in[d]}
are asymptotically free in expectation and almost surely as long as the covariance structure of each one goes to
0
sufficiently fast. Concretely, we have to impose that, for each
i\in[d]
,

\displaystyle v(W_{i}):=\|\operatorname{cov}(W_{i})\|=o\left(\log^{-3/2}n\right).

The limit is a free semicircular family
(s_{i})_{i\in[d]}
. So Theorem
(.i)
implies that

\displaystyle\Delta\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i\in[d]}s_{i}\otimes s_{i},

in probability and in expectation. As a matter of fact, similar results hold for Hermitian random matrices with bounded entries (see
[
7
, Theorem 3.25]
).

Another classical example is Wishart-type matrices.

Example 4.5

(Wishart matrices)
.

A random Hermitian matrix
W\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a (centered) Wishart matrix if

\displaystyle W=XX^{*}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}(XX^{*})=XX^{*}-\operatorname{Id},

where
X\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
has i.i.d. centered entries with variance
1/n
. Independent Whishart matrices
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}
also satisfy Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
(by Lemma
3.3
) and
(A.4)
; see
[
9
]
. The limit of a Wishart matrix
XX^{*}
is equal to
cc^{*}
, where
c
is a circular element, i.e.

\displaystyle c=\frac{s+is^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}},

with
s,s^{\prime}
free semicircular variables, and it is called the quarter circular law or Marchenko-Pastur law. The limit law in
(.i)
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\underset{n\to\infty}{\Rightarrow}\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in[d]}(c_{j}c_{j}^{*}-1)\otimes(c_{j}c_{j}^{*}-1),

in probability and in expectation, where
(c_{j})_{j\in[d]}
are free copies of
c
.

5.
Perspectives

The results presented so far are general, and several natural classes of random matrices satisfy Assumptions
(A.1)
,
(A.2)
,
(A.3)
, and
(A.4)
. A natural extension would be to remove assumption
(A.3)
. It turns out, however, that such an assumption is necessary in order for the limit in Theorem
(.ii)
to be semicircular. To understand this problem, recall that the tensor convolution
\mu_{1}\circledast\mu_{2}
is not the free convolution of
\mu_{1}\star\mu_{1}
with
\mu_{2}\star\mu_{2}
. This is because if
a
and
b
are free variables, then their tensor products
a\otimes a
and
b\otimes b
are not necessarily free. This was proven in
[
10
]
. In particular, it is surprising that the semicircular law can still be recovered for tensor products under only the mean zero assumption. However, a deep understanding of the structure of tensor products is still lacking, and while classical freeness is lost, tensor products still enjoy “partial” freeness properties. In a work in progress, we aim to decrypt such a structure in the framework of Free Probability.

A second extension of our results would be the non-Hermitian case, addressing, in particular, the conjecture mentioned in the introduction. Indeed, the methods presented here can be easily generalized to capture the convergence of the
*
-moments of non-Hermitian matrices
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, that is, moments of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}\left(\tau^{(n)}(W_{i_{1}}^{w_{1}}\cdots W_{i_{k}}^{w_{k}})\right),

where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[d]
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in\{1,*\}
. However, the spectral distribution of non-Hermitian matrices is no longer characterized by the
*
-moments convergence or singular value convergence. A second ingredient, which is in general more involved, is to lower bound the smallest singular value of
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d}
uniformly; see
[
6
]
and references within. The first setting we might want to study is when the Kraus operators of the random quantum channel are sampled as independent Haar unitaries. In future work, we aim to establish the quantitative invertibility of the model by deriving lower bounds for the corresponding smallest singular value.
