A 2-component Camassa-Holm equation, Euler–Bernoulli Beam Problem and Non-Commutative Continued Fractions
Richard Beals, Jacek Szmigielski

Abstract
A new approach to the Euler-Bernoulli beam based on an inhomogeneous matrix string problem is presented. Three ramifications of the approach are developed: 1. motivated by an analogy with the Camassa-Holm equation a class of isospectral deformations of the beam problem is formulated; 2. a reformulation of the matrix string problem in terms of a certain compact operator is used to obtain basic spectral properties of the inhomogeneous matrix string problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions; 3. the inverse problem is solved for the special case of a discrete Euler-Bernoulli beam. The solution involves a non-commutative generalization of Stieltjes’ continued fractions, leading to the inverse formulas expressed in terms of ratios of Hankel-like determinants.



1
Introduction

In 1993, Camassa and Holm

[
5
]
discovered the shallow water equation

m_{t}+(um)_{x}+u_{x}m=0,\qquad m=u-u_{xx},

(1.1)

where subscripts denote partial derivatives. The most attractive novel property of
(
1.1
) is that it supports non-smooth soliton solutions.
These peaked solitons (
peakons
) are obtained from the ansatz

u(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}m_{j}(t)\,e^{-\left\lvert x-x_{j}(t)\right\rvert},

(1.2)

involving amplitudes
m_{j}(t)
and positions
x_{j}(t)
depending smoothly on time. It was shown
in
[
5
]
that
u
given by (
1.2
) is a weak solution to the
Camassa–Holm equation (
1.1
) if and only if the positions
x_{j}(t)
and
amplitudes
m_{j}(t)
satisfy the Hamiltonian system

\dot{x}_{j}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial m_{j}}=u(x_{j}),\qquad\dot{m}_{j}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x_{j}}=-m_{j}u_{x}(x_{j}),

(1.3)

with Hamiltonian

H(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},m_{1},\dots,m_{d})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}m_{i}m_{j}e^{-\left\lvert x_{i}-x_{j}\right\rvert},

and the convention that
u_{x}(x_{i})=\bigl\langle D_{x}u\bigr\rangle(x_{i})
where
\bigl\langle f\bigr\rangle(x_{i})
means the arithmetic
mean of the left and right hand limits of
f
at
x_{i}
.

The general solution of (
1.3
) (for arbitrary
d
)
was constructed in
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
using inverse spectral methods.
The main premise of these papers was the realization that the CH equation can be viewed
as an isospectral deformation of the classical inhomogeneous string problem studied in the
1950
s
by M.G. Krein
[
15
,
14
,
13
]
and, subsequently, by Dym and
McKean in
[
7
]
; for a review see
[
8
]
.
In particular, the peakon solutions
(
1.2
) were shown in
[
3
]
to be directly linked to an isospectral deformation of discrete
strings, i.e. those strings for which the mass density is a linear combination of point masses.
Krein had observed long ago that, in the case of discrete strings, the inverse string problem can be
solved explicitly by using results of Stieltjes on continued fractions
[
22
]
.
Stieltjes’ methods were applied in
[
3
]

to derive the determinantal formulas for the amplitudes and positions of
peakons and, in turn, to determine the asymptotic behaviour of peakon solutions.

It has been known at least since the fundamental paper
[
16
]
by Lax
on the Korteweg–de Vries equation that the isospectral deformations of boundary value problems
may lead to interesting non-linear
equations. The boundary value problem for the KdV equation is given by the Schrödinger equation
on the whole real axis, in which case the spectrum is a union of continuous and discrete spectra.

In the late
1970
s, in a series of interesting papers
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
, Sabatier put forward an idea that spectral problems with just discrete spectrum
can also be a source of interesting non-linear problems derived
via
isospectral deformations.
In particular, the inhomogeneous string boundary value problem was singled out as a potential
source of interesting boundary problems with the discrete spectrum, to be subjected to isospectral
deformations. Sabatier studied a very limited class of spectral deformations, applicable only to
strictly positive, smooth densities. The situation changed with the discovery of the CH equation
(
1.1
) and subsequent realization in

[
2
,
3
,
4
]
that the
CH equation is, in disguise, an isospectral deformation of an inhomogeneous string boundary value
problem. This line of research was later generalized to other equations from the CH family
(
[
17
,
6
,
11
]
). In the introduction to

[
4
]
, the authors speculated,
guided by the work of Barcilon
[
1
]
on the Euler-Bernoulli beam problem,
that higher order boundary value problems might provide an equally rich environment for
isospectral deformations. This task required a new look at the beam boundary
value problem that would be naturally amenable to a Lax type deformation. The present paper aims
to offer a new approach to the beam boundary value problem by rephrasing it as a matrix inhomogeneous
string problem.

In the remainder of this section we outline the main results of this paper and provide a context
for some of the techniques used in our arguments.

In
Section 2
we propose a simple derivation of a system of nonlinear equations that generalizes
the CH equation to a new two component equation which is structurally of the same type as the CH equation.
We are keenly aware that there exist other two-component generalizations of the CH equation, for example,

\displaystyle m_{t}=(um)_{x}+u_{x}m+\rho\rho_{x},

\displaystyle m=u-u_{xx},

\displaystyle\rho_{t}=(\rho u)_{x},

first derived using tri-hamiltonian methods by P.J. Olver and Rosenau in
[
18
]

and studied, for example, in
[
12
]
. Other generalizations have been proposed as well

[
25
]
.

The equation we propose (see (
2.5
)) takes the form

\displaystyle n_{t}=(un)_{x}+u_{x}n+vn,

\displaystyle m_{t}=(um)_{x}+u_{x}m-vm,

\displaystyle u_{xx}-4u=n+m,

\displaystyle v_{x}=n-m,

and comes from a matrix valued Lax pair, structurally identical to the original CH case,
but involving two measures
m
and
n
rather than one.

In
Section 3
, using a Liouville transformation, we map the problem to a finite
interval, following a similar procedure used in
[
2
]
to study an acoustic scattering
problem. We note that the transformed
x
-member of the Lax pair (see (
3.1
)) is a matrix
version of an inhomogeneous string boundary value problem.

In
Section 4
we review the pertinent facts about the Euler–Bernoulli beam problem, which
we show can be reformulated as a string problem with a matrix density – the same system already
encountered in
Section 3
. We then study
the basic spectral properties of that matrix string problem.

In
Section 5
we reformulate the Euler–Bernoulli beam problem as a standard spectral
problem for a compact (in fact trace-class) operator
T
and we study the properties of that
operator on an appropriate Hilbert space. In particular, we establish basic properties of
the resolvent of that operator.

In
Section 6
, in
Theorem 6.4
, we derive a closed-form expression for the resolvent
of
T
and introduce a special element of the resolvent, the Weyl function, which plays a key role in
the formulation of the inverse problem.

In
Section 7
we analyze the spectral problem for a discrete Euler-Bernoulli beam,
that is, a beam in which both measures
m
and
n
are chosen to be finite sums of point masses..
This is a beam counterpart of Stietljes’ string. Similar to what occurs for the string problem, the
Weyl function admits a continued fraction expansion (
Section 7
), albeit with
non-commuting coefficients. The general concept of continued fractions over non-commutative rings
goes back to Wedderburn
[
24
]
, but our special non-commutative case is a direct
generalization of continued fractions of Stieltjes’ type, this time associated to a discrete inverse
beam problem, rather than a string problem.
The continued fraction expansion can be rephrased in terms of non-commutative Padé approximations and
we formulate Padé approximation conditions needed for the inverse problem.

In
Section 8
we explicitly solve the inverse problem for the discrete
Euler-Bernoulli beam. To this end we construct a sequence of non-commutative Padé approximations
using the Weyl function, or, to be more precise, the spectral measure, as an input data and,
in the end, we recover the discrete measures
m
and
n
.
In the process of solving the inverse problem we are prompted to introduce several variations on the
Hankel moment matrix that are germane to the
inverse beam problem. The final formulas bear a remarkable resemblance to string formulas (see

[
4
]
) with the proviso that in the beam problem the usual
Hankel determinants of the moment matrix are replaced by determinants of suitably reduced
Hankel matrices of moments.

The paper concludes with (Appendix)
Section 9
, in which we provide
a detailed analysis of the Lax pair parametrization giving rise to (
2.5
).

2
A
2
-Component CH equation

The Camassa-Holm (CH) equation (
1.1
) is the compatibility condition for a pair
of scalar equations on the line, which we take for simplicity to have the form

\psi_{xx}=(1+\lambda m)\psi,\qquad\psi_{t}=a\psi+b\psi_{x},

(2.1)

where
-\infty<x<\infty
, and the subscripts in
x
and
t
represent distribution derivatives
in
x,t
respectively.

Remark 2.1
.

In general, the derivatives we consider are distribution derivatives.
It will sometimes be convenient to use
D_{x}
,
D_{t}
, or simply
D
in the case of one variable.

The CH flow corresponds to the choice

b=u+\frac{1}{\lambda},\quad a=-\frac{u_{x}}{2}.

In this case the compatibility condition reads

m_{t}=(um)_{x}+u_{x}m,\qquad(u_{xx}-4u)_{x}=2m_{x}.

(2.2)

This compatibility condition holds even if
m
is a measure,
in which case
u_{x}
is of bounded variation,
u
is continuous, and
the term
u_{x}m
means that on the singular support of
m
the multiplier is
taken to be
u_{x}(a)=\bigl\langle u_{x}\bigr\rangle(a)
, where
\bigl\langle f\bigr\rangle(a)
is
\frac{1}{2}[f(a_{-})+f(a_{+})]
.

Remark 2.2
.

Our choice of the coefficients in (
2.1
) differs slightly from the original
Lax pair in
[
5
]
. This change results in a different relation between

u
and
m
as one can see by comparing
(
1.1
) and
(
2.2
)
.

Now, we consider a two-component version

\displaystyle\Psi_{xx}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{1}+\lambda M)\Psi,\qquad M=\begin{bmatrix}0&n\\
m&0\end{bmatrix},

(2.3a)

\displaystyle\Psi_{t}

\displaystyle=a\Psi+b\Psi_{x},

(2.3b)

where
\Psi,a
and
b
are
2\times 2
matrix functions of
x,t,\lambda
and
{\bf 1}

denotes the
2\times 2
identity matrix. We assume for now that
a,b
have only terms
of degree
0,-1
in
\lambda
. As shown in the Appendix, if we add the
assumption that the matrices
a
and
b
are bounded as
x\to\pm\infty
, then,
up to a normalization, they have the form

b=\begin{bmatrix}u&0\\
0&u\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\qquad a=-\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}u_{x}-v&0\\
0&u_{x}+v\end{bmatrix}.

(2.4)

The compatibility conditions split into two constraints

(u_{xx}-4u)_{x}=(n+m)_{x},\qquad v_{x}=n-m,

and a system of evolution equations

n_{t}=(un)_{x}+u_{x}n+vn,\qquad m_{t}=(um)_{x}+u_{x}m-vm.

(2.5)

Furthermore, if we are interested in bounded
u
and compactly supported
m,n
, then we
can replace one of the constraints with a more restrictive
one, keeping the other constraint intact,

u_{xx}-4u=n+m,\qquad v_{x}=n-m.

(2.6)

We assume that
M
is compactly supported and take as solutions of (
2.6
)
the particular choices

\displaystyle u(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert}[m(y,t)+n(y,t)]\,dy,

(2.7)

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sgn(x-y)[n(y,t)-m(y,t)]\,dy.

(2.8)

It follows that

\begin{matrix}\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}u(x,t)=0=\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}u_{x}(x,t)\\
\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}v(x,t)=\pm\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[n(y,t)-m(y,t)]\,dy.\end{matrix}

(2.9)

Remark 2.3
.

The integrals appearing in this paper are all Stieltjes integrals, but we find it
more convenient to write them as
\int f(x)m(x,t)dx
, or, later in the paper, as
\int f(x)dm(x,t)
. The former
notation is analogous to writing the point mass at the origin as
\delta(x)dx
.

Proposition 2.4
.

The integral

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[m(y,t)+n(y,t)]\,dy

is independent of
t
.

Proof.

According to (
2.5
), (
2.9
),

\begin{split}D_{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[m(y,t)+n(y,t)]\,dy&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{[u(n+m)]_{y}+u_{y}(n+m)+v(n-m)\}\,dy\\
&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[u_{y}u_{yy}-4u_{y}u+vv_{y}]\,dy\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[u_{y}^{2}-4u^{2}+v^{2}]_{y}\,dy=0.\end{split}

∎

Remark 2.5
.

Note, however, that separately
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}mdy
and

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ndy
are not conserved. Indeed, it follows from
(
2.6
) that

D_{t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}[n-m]\,dy=-4\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}uv\,dy.

The integral

4
The Dirichlet problem for a beam

Vibrations of a beam parametrized by the interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
are characterized
by the equation

D^{2}[rD^{2}\phi]\ =\ \lambda^{2}m\phi,\qquad D=D_{x};

(4.1)

(see
[
1
]
or, for a comprehensive view of vibration problems in engineering,

[
10
]
).
What we refer to as a beam problem is often referred to as an
Euler–Bernoulli
beam problem
. The two functions (or positive measures)
r,m
are the flexural
rigidity and mass density. The spectral parameter
\lambda^{2}
denotes the square of the
frequency.

Setting

D^{2}\varphi_{1}\ =\ \lambda n\varphi_{2},

(4.2)

where
n=1/r
, (
4.1
) becomes

D^{2}\varphi_{1}\ =\ \lambda n\varphi_{2},\qquad D^{2}\varphi_{2}\ =\ \lambda m\varphi_{1}.

(4.3)

The matrix form is

D^{2}\varphi\ =\ \lambda M\varphi,\qquad\varphi\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{1}\\
\varphi_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad M\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}0&n\\
m&0\end{bmatrix}.

(4.4)

We require that
\varphi
be continuous. We want to allow
m
and
n
to be finite positive measures
on the interval
-1\leq x\leq 1
. We assume

\begin{matrix}\hbox{(a)\quad The endpoints $x=\pm 1$ are not atoms for $m$ or $n$.}\\
\hbox{(b)\quad The measures $m$ and $n$ have the same support.}\end{matrix}

(4.5)

Then
M\varphi
should be interpreted as

M\varphi\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}\varphi_{2}n\\
\varphi_{1}m\end{matrix}\right].

Remark 4.1
.

Throughout this section and the next
it is convenient to adopt the (more correct) way
of writing integrals with respect to
m
and
n
, using
dm(x)
,
dn(x)
rather than
the symbolic
m(x)dx
,
n(x)dx
.

A partial fundamental solution

\Phi(x,\lambda)
with the property
\Phi(-1,\lambda)=0,\,

D\Phi(-1,\lambda)=~~{\bf 1}
,
the identity matrix, can be constructed in the form

\Phi(x,\lambda)\ =\ \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\Phi_{k}(x)

(4.6)

with

\Phi_{0}(x)\ =\ (1+x){\bf 1},\qquad\Phi_{k+1}(x)\ =\ \int_{-1}^{x}\left[\int_{-1}^{y}M(z)\Phi_{k}(z)\,dz\right]\,dy.

To be specific, we take the integrals to run on the intervals
[-1,x)
and
[-1,y)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The functions
\Phi_{k}(x)
are diagonal for even
k
, off-diagonal for
odd
k
. The non-zero entries
\Phi_{k1}
,
\Phi_{k2}
are non-negative, positive at
x=1
, and
satisfy the estimate

0\ \leq\ \Phi_{kj}(x)\ \leq\ 2\frac{(1+x)^{k}(\overline{m}+\overline{n})^{k}}{k^{k}\,k\,!}.\qquad\ j=1,2.

(4.7)

where
\overline{m}
and
\overline{n}
denote the total mass of
m
and
n
.

Proof.

The first assertions follow by induction from the construction (
4.6
) and the
assumption that
m
and
n
are positive measures. To prove the estimate, let

p=m+n
, and define
\psi_{k}
by

\psi_{0}(x)\ =\ 1+x,\qquad\psi_{k+1}(x)\ =\ \int_{-1}^{x}\int_{-1}^{y}\psi_{k}(z)\,dp(z)\,dy.

Each
\Phi_{kj}(x)
is bounded by
\psi_{k}(x)
. Changing the order of integration and integrating
first with respect to
y
, the result can be written as

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(x)\ =\ \int_{-1}^{x}(x-y_{k})\psi_{k-1}dp(y_{k})

\displaystyle\ =\ \int_{-1}^{x}\int_{-1}^{y_{k}}\left[(x-y_{k})(y_{k}-y_{k-1})\right]\psi_{k-2}(y_{k-1})\,dp(y_{k-1})\,dp(y_{k})

\displaystyle\ =\ \int_{-1}^{x}\cdots\int_{-1}^{y_{2}}\left[(x-y_{k})(y_{k}-y_{k-1})\cdots(y_{2}-y_{1})\right]\psi_{0}(y_{1})\,dp(y_{1})\,dp(y_{2})\cdots\,dp(y_{k}).

The product in brackets is maximized when the
k

interval
[y_{1},x]
has length at most
1+x
, and
\psi_{0}(x)\leq 2
, so

0\ \leq\ \psi_{k}(x)\leq 2\left[\frac{1+x}{k}\right]^{k}\int_{0\leq y_{1}<\dots<y_{k}<x}dp^{(k)}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{k}),

(4.8)

where
p^{(k)}
denotes the product measure on the
k
-cube
\{(x_{1},\dots x_{k}):|x_{j}|\leq 1\}

in
\mathbf{R}^{k}
. The domain of integration is one of
k\,!
pairwise disjoint domains in the
cube that are obtained by permuting the indices. Each domain has the same measure, so

0\ \leq\ \int_{0\leq y_{1}<\dots<y_{k}<x}dp^{(k)}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{k})\ \leq\ \frac{\left[\int_{-1}^{x}dp(y)\right]^{k}}{k\,!}.

(4.9)

Since
\int^{1}_{-1}dp(x)\ =\ \overline{m}+\overline{n}
, the estimates (
4.8
) and
(
4.9
) imply (
4.7
).
∎
.

We are interested in the Dirichlet problem for solutions of (
4.4
):

\varphi_{1}(\pm 1,\lambda)\ =\ 0\ =\ \varphi_{2}(\pm 1,\lambda).

(4.11)

A value
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
for which a non-zero solution of (
4.4
),
(
4.11
) exists will be referred to as a Dirichlet eigenvalue. Note that
zero is not an eigenvalue.

Proposition 4.4
.

The Dirichlet eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{\nu}\}
are precisely the zeros
of
\Delta
, where

\Delta(\lambda)\ =\ \det\Phi(1,\lambda).

They satisfy

\sum_{\nu}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{\nu}|}\ <\ \infty.

(4.12)

Proof.

Any solution
\varphi
of the Dirichlet problem with eigenvalue
\lambda
is a linear combination
of the two columns of
\Phi(\cdot,\lambda)
:

\varphi(x)\ =\ \Phi(x,\lambda)\,v

where
v
is a constant 2-vector. The condition at
x=1
implies that
\Phi(1,\lambda)v=0
.
Thus the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of a non-zero
solution (“eigenfunction”) of the Dirichlet problem
(
4.4
), (
4.11
) is that
\det\Phi(1,\lambda)=0
.

Corollary
4
implies that
|\Delta(\lambda)|
is dominated by
\exp(4a|\lambda|^{1/2})
.
Therefore the zeros
\lambda_{\nu}
, if numbered with
|\lambda_{\nu}|
non-decreasing, satisfy

|\lambda_{\nu}|\ \geq\ c\nu^{2}

(4.13)

for some constant
c>0
.
[
23
, §8.21]
. In particular,
(
4.12
) is true.
∎

At the end of this section we will show that the zeros of
\Delta
are simple.

If
\varphi
is a solution of the Dirichlet problem (
4.4
), (
4.11
),
then
f=D\varphi
is a solution of

Df\ =\ \lambda M\varphi.

(4.14)

It follows that
f
is a function of bounded variation which is continuous at any
point that is not an atom. In particular,
f
is continuous at the endpoints
\pm 1
.
This fact, for
f
and similar functions, justifies the various integration-by-parts
formulas in this and later sections.

The following identity is fundamental to our discussion of the Dirichlet problem.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\varphi
is a solution of the Dirichlet problem with
eigenvalue
\lambda
, and
f=D\varphi
, then

\int^{1}_{-1}[f_{1}\overline{f}_{2}+f_{2}\overline{f}_{1}]\,dx\ =\ -\lambda\int^{1}_{-1}[|\varphi_{1}|^{2}\,dm+|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dn]\ \neq\ 0.

(4.15)

Proof.

It is convenient to write the left side in the form
(D\varphi,\sigma D\varphi)
, where
the inner product
(\ ,\ )
is the
L^{2}
inner product for vector-valued functions:

(f,g)\ =\ \int^{1}_{-1}f(x)\cdot\overline{g(x)}\,dx\ =\ \int^{1}_{-1}[f_{1}(x)\overline{g_{1}(x)}+f_{2}(x)\overline{g_{2}(x)}]\,dx,

(4.16)

and
\sigma
is the matrix

\sigma\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}.

(4.17)

Then integration by parts gives

(D\varphi,\sigma D\varphi)\ =\ -(D^{2}\varphi,\sigma\varphi)\ =\ -\lambda(M\varphi,\sigma\varphi),

(4.18)

which is (
4.15
).

If the right side of (
4.15
) is zero, then
\varphi_{1}=0
on the
support of
m
and
\varphi_{2}=0
on the support of
n
. As a consequence

(D\varphi,D\varphi)\ =\ -\lambda(M\varphi,\varphi)\ =\ -\lambda\int^{1}_{-1}[\varphi_{1}\overline{\varphi}_{2}\,dm+\varphi_{2}\overline{\varphi}_{1}\,dn]\ =\ 0,

implying that
\varphi
is constant, hence zero, a contradiction.  ∎

Theorem 4.6
.

The Dirichlet eigenvalues satisfy the
following conditions:

(a) If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue, so is
-\lambda
.

(b) Each eigenvalue
\lambda
is real and its eigenspace has
dimension one.

Proof.

If
\varphi=[\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}]^{t}
is an eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, it
follows immediately from (
4.3
) that
[\varphi_{1},-\varphi_{2}]^{t}
has eigenvalue

-\lambda
. The complex conjugate
\overline{\varphi}
has eigenvalue
\overline{\lambda}
. Integration
by parts shows that

(D^{2}\varphi,\varphi)\ =\ (\varphi,D^{2}\varphi)\ =\ \overline{(D^{2}\varphi,\varphi)},

so Lemma (
4
) and (
4.18
) show that

0\ \neq\ \lambda\int^{1}_{-1}[|\varphi_{1}|^{2}\,dm+|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dn]\ =\ \overline{\lambda}\int^{1}_{-1}[|\varphi_{1}|^{2}\,dm+|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dn].

Therefore
\lambda=\overline{\lambda}
. Then the singular matrix
\Phi(1,\lambda)
has positive diagonal entries,
so it has two eigenvalues, namely
0
and
{\rm tr}\,\Phi(1,\lambda)>0
.
Thus the eigenspace for
\lambda
has dimension 1.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

The zeros of
\Delta=\det\Phi(1,\lambda)
are simple.

Proof.

Write

\Phi(1,\lambda)\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}a(\lambda)&b(\lambda)\\
c(\lambda)&d(\lambda)\end{bmatrix}\qquad v(\lambda)\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}d(\lambda)\\
-c(\lambda)\end{bmatrix},

and let
\varphi(x,\lambda)=\Phi(x,\lambda)v(\lambda)
. The entries of
\Phi(1,\lambda)
are non-zero,
so
\varphi\neq 0
. We have
\varphi(-1,\lambda)={\bf 0}
and

\varphi(1,\lambda)\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{1}(1,\lambda)\\
\varphi_{2}(1,\lambda)\end{bmatrix}\ =\ \begin{bmatrix}\Delta(\lambda)\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

We want to prove that the derivative
D_{\lambda}\varphi(1,\lambda)
does not vanish if
\lambda

is an eigenvalue. Differentiating with respect to
\lambda
shows that at an
eigenvalue

(D_{x}^{2}-\lambda M)D_{\lambda}\varphi\ =\ M\varphi.

Therefore, by Lemma (
4
), and because all terms here are real,

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{-1}[|\varphi_{1}|^{2}\,dm+|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dn]\ =\ (M\varphi,\sigma\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ (D_{x}^{2}D_{\lambda}\varphi,\sigma\varphi)-(\lambda MD_{\lambda}\varphi,\sigma\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ -(D_{x}D_{\lambda}\varphi,\sigma D_{x}\varphi)-\lambda(M\varphi,\sigma D_{\lambda}\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ -D_{\lambda}\varphi\cdot\sigma D_{x}\varphi\big|^{1}_{-1}+[(D_{\lambda}\varphi,\sigma D_{x}^{2}\varphi)-(\lambda M\varphi,\sigma D_{\lambda}\varphi)]

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ -D_{\lambda}\varphi_{1}(1,\lambda)D_{x}\varphi_{2}(1,\lambda).

Therefore
D_{\lambda}\Delta(\lambda)=D_{\lambda}\varphi_{1}(1,\lambda)\neq 0
.  ∎

5
Spectral theory

In this section we rephrase the Dirichlet problem for the Euler-Bernoulli beam as a standard
eigenvalue problem for a compact operator, whose eigenfunctions are the derivatives
D\varphi
.

Let
{\bf H}
be the
L^{2}
space of vector-valued
functions on the interval
[-1,1]
, with the inner product
4.16
and norm

||f||\ =\ (f,f)^{1/2}.

We also introduce an indefinite form

\langle f,g\rangle\ =\ \int^{1}_{-1}[f_{1}(x)\overline{g_{2}(x)}+f_{2}(x)\overline{g_{1}(x)}]\,dx\ =\ (\sigma f,g)\ =\ (f,\sigma g)

(5.1)

where
\sigma
is the matrix (
4.17
).

We know that the Dirichlet eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{\nu}\}
are real, and we take their
eigenvectors
\{\varphi_{\nu}\}
to be real as well. Lemma
4
can be rephrased as

\langle f_{\nu},f_{\nu}\rangle\ =\ -\lambda_{\nu}\int^{1}_{-1}[\varphi_{\nu,1}^{2}\,dm+\varphi_{\nu,2}^{2}\,dn]\neq 0.

(5.2)

Thus
\langle f_{\nu},f_{\nu}\rangle
and
\lambda_{\nu}
have opposite signs. Because of this we index the
eigenvalues with

\dots\ <\ \lambda_{2}\ <\ \lambda_{1}\ <\ 0\ <\lambda_{-1}\ <\ \lambda_{-2}\ <\ \dots\ .

(5.3)

The calculation that led to (
5.2
) leads to two formulations for

\langle f_{\nu},f_{\mu}\rangle
that show

\langle f_{\nu},f_{\mu}\rangle\ =\ 0\qquad\hbox{if\ \ $\mu\neq\nu$.}

(5.4)

Let
{\bf H}_{0}\subset{\bf H}
consist of the constant functions, and let
{\bf H}_{1}
be the orthogonal
complement:

{\bf H}_{1}\ =\ \left\{f\in{\bf H}\,:\,\int^{1}_{-1}f=0\right\}.

Note that each
f_{\nu}
belongs to
{\bf H}_{1}
, since
\int^{1}_{-1}f_{\nu}=\varphi_{\nu}(1)-\varphi_{\nu}(0)=0
.

This discussion leads us to introduce two inverses of
D=D_{x}
that map
{\bf H}
to
{\bf H}
:

D_{0}^{-1}g(x)\ =\ \begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{x}g(y)\,dy-\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{1}g(y)\,dy,&g\in{\bf H}_{1},\\
0,&g\in{\bf H}_{0},\end{cases}

(5.5)

and

D_{1}^{-1}g(x)\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{x}(y+1)g(y)\,dy+\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}^{1}(y-1)g(y)\,dy.

(5.6)

Lemma 5.1
.

For any
g\in{\bf H}
,

D_{0}^{-1}g(-1)\ =\ D_{0}^{-1}g(1)\ =\ 0;\qquad D_{1}^{-1}g\in{\bf H}_{1}.

Proof.

The first pair of identities is clear for
g\in{\bf H}_{1}
and true by definition
for
g\in{\bf H}_{0}
. The third identity follows from an easy calculation.

∎

In view of these remarks, the Dirichlet eigenvalue problem can be reformulated as

Df\ =\ \lambda MD_{0}^{-1}f,\qquad f\in{\bf H}_{1}.

or, equivalently,

f\in{\bf H}_{1},\qquad f\ =\ \lambda Tf,\qquad T\ =\ D_{1}^{-1}MD_{0}^{-1}.

(5.7)

Thus the problem (
4.4
), (
4.11
) is
equivalent to a standard eigenvalue problem for the operator
T
mapping

{\bf H}_{1}
to
{\bf H}_{1}
or
{\bf H}
to
{\bf H}
.
Note that
T=0
on
{\bf H}_{0}
, since this is true, by definition of
D_{0}^{-1}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

The operator
T
is a compact operator in
{\bf H}
. If
f
is in
the image of
T
, then
f
has bounded variation and is continuous at the
endpoints
x=\pm 1
.

Proof.

Any inverse
D^{-1}
takes
L^{2}
functions to functions that satisfy
a Hölder condition: if
u=D^{-1}f
and
x<y
, then
Du=f
so

|u(y)-u(x)|\ \leq\ \int_{x}^{y}|f(t)|\,dy\ \leq\ \left[\int_{x}^{y}|f|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\,\left[\int_{x}^{y}dt\right]^{1/2}\ \leq\ ||f||\,(y-x)^{1/2}.

It follows that the image under
D_{0}^{-1}
of a bounded sequence in
{\bf H}
is uniformly
equicontinuous. The same argument applied to the two summands in (
5.5
)
shows that
|D_{0}^{-1}f(x)|\leq 2||f||
, so the image is also uniformly bounded.
By the theorem of Ascoli–Arzelá there is a uniformly
convergent subsequence,
\{g_{n}\}
. Thus
D_{0}^{-1}
is compact from
{\bf H}
to the space of bounded
continuous functions. The map
g\to D_{1}^{-1}gM
from this space to
{\bf H}
is bounded, so
T
is compact.
Moreover
g\to D_{1}^{-1}gM
maps to
functions of bounded variation; continuity of
Tf
at the endpoints follows from assumption
(a) of (
4.5
).

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

If
K\neq(0)
is a closed subspace of
\,{\bf H}
that
is invariant under
T
, then
K
contains an eigenfunction of
T
.

Proof.

Since
T
is compact, every non-zero point of the spectrum is an eigenvalue.
This applies also to the restriction of
T
to the invariant subspace
K
.
Compactness implies that the operator
I-\lambda T
is Fredholm with index zero.
If the restriction to
K
has no null space, for all
\lambda\in\mathbf{C}
, then it is invertible,
and the inverse is entire and bounded at
\lambda=\infty
, leading to a contradiction. Therefore

T
has an eigenvector in
K
.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

The operator
T
is symmetric with respect to the indefinite
form
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
.

Proof.

We may assume that
f
and

and are smooth. Since

and

\displaystyle\langle Tf,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Tf,\sigma DD_{0}^{-1}g\rangle\ =\ -(DTf,\sigma D_{0}^{-1}g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(MD_{0}^{-1}f,\sigma D_{0}^{-1}g)\ =\ -(D_{0}^{-1}f,M^{t}\sigma D_{0}^{-1}g).

Since

is diagonal, the last expression is symmetric in

and

.

∎

Let
{\bf H}_{T}
be the closure in
{\bf H}
of the range of

. Any solution of
(

{\bf H}_{T}

Proposition 5.5
.

The span of the eigenfunctions

Proof.

Let
N_{T}\in{\bf H}_{T}
be the orthogonal complement of

is orthogonal

{\bf H}_{T}

.

∎

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
N
be the null space of
T
, i.e.
N=\{f\in{\bf H};Tf=0\}
.
Then
N=\sigma N
. Moreover,
N
coincides with each of
the subspaces

(a) The orthogonal complement of
{\bf H}_{T}
with respect to
(\ ,\ )
;

(b) The orthogonal complement of
{\bf H}_{T}
with respect to
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
.

Proof.

Note that
Tf=0
if and only if either

, in which case
\sigma H_{0}=H_{0}
,
or
f\in H_{1}
and

, where

.
This, in turn, is equivalent to the conditions that

vanish on the

and
\varphi_{2}
vanish on the support of

. In view of

.

The relations between

and the spaces (a), (b) follow from the identities,
valid for every

\displaystyle(f,Tg)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\sigma f,Tg\rangle\ =\ \langle T(\sigma f),g\rangle;

\displaystyle\langle Tf,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f,Tg\rangle.

The first identity shows that

is in space (a) if and only if

,
which is equivalent to

. The second shows that

is in space (b) if and
only if

.
∎

There is a natural decomposition of
{\bf H}_{T}
into two subspaces that are orthogonal

{\bf H}_{T}^{\pm}\ =\ \hbox{closure of the span of $\{f_{\nu}\,:\,\pm\nu>0\}$.}

We introduce a new inner product in
{\bf H}_{T}
by setting

(f,g)_{T}\ =\ \begin{cases}\ \langle f,g\rangle,\quad f,g\in{\bf H}_{T}^{+};\\
-\langle f,g\rangle,\quad f,g\in{\bf H}_{T}^{-};\\
\ 0,\quad\qquad f\in{\bf H}_{T}^{+},\ g\in{\bf H}_{T}^{-}.\end{cases}

(5.9)

Let
\widehat{\bf H}_{T}
be the completion of
{\bf H}_{T}
with respect to the

||f||_{T}^{2}\ =\ (f,f)_{T}
. Note that in all cases

(f,f)_{T}\ =\ |\langle f,f\rangle|\ =\ |(f,\sigma f)|\ \leq||f||\,||\sigma f||\ =\ ||f||^{2}.

(5.10)

The

are clearly an orthonormal basis for this space.
Since
Tf_{\nu}=\lambda_{\nu}^{-1}f_{\nu}
, the restriction of

to
{\bf H}_{T}
extends to a compact

with eigenvalues (in the usual sense)

.

The orthogonal projection of

onto the span of the eigenfunction

is

(5.11)

Abusing notation, we write

also for the kernel of the operator (

\displaystyle E_{\nu}f(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{-1}E_{\nu}(x,y)f(y)\,dy,\qquad f\in{\bf H}_{T}

\displaystyle E_{\nu}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\langle f_{\nu},f_{\nu}\rangle}\left[\begin{matrix}f_{\nu,1}(x)f_{\nu,2}(y)&f_{\nu,1}(x)f_{\nu,1}(y)\\
f_{\nu,2}(x)f_{\nu,2}(y)&f_{\nu,2}(x)f_{\nu,1}(y)\end{matrix}\right].

(5.12)

We may also view the operator
E_{\nu}
as a projection of
{\bf H}
onto the span of
f_{\nu}
.
We view the kernels
E_{\nu}
as belonging to the Hilbert space

{\bf H}^{(2)}\ =\ L^{2}(I\times I;M(2,\mathbf{C}))

of
complex

Let us write the integral kernel for the operator

Since

Suppose that

This follows by applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to

Proposition 5.8
.

Each element of the kernel (

(\overline{m}+\overline{n})|\lambda_{\nu}|
, where

By Lemma

|f_{\nu,1}(x)|^{2}\ \leq\\
\lambda_{\nu}^{2}\overline{n}\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,2}^{2}\,dn\ \leq\ \overline{n}|\lambda_{\nu}\langle f_{\nu},f_{\nu}\rangle|.

Similarly,

Therefore each entry of

For later use we introduce the operator

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\frac{\lambda}{\lambda-\lambda_{\nu}}E_{\nu}(x,y)

For each

. By Proposition

the corresponding summand is

as an element of

(5.17)

Lemma 5.10

Under assumption (

The first inequality is (

To prove the second inequality, let

\displaystyle(M\varphi_{\nu},\varphi_{\nu})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,1}\varphi_{\nu,2}d(m+n)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,1}^{2}d(m+n)\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,2}^{2}d(m+n)

\displaystyle C^{2}\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,1}^{2}\,dm\int^{1}_{-1}\varphi_{\nu,2}^{2}\,dn

7
The discrete beam

The discrete beam is characterized by measures
m
and
n
that are supported on discrete
points

-1\ <\ x_{1}\ <\ x_{2}\ <\ \dots\ <\ x_{d-1}\ <\ x_{d}\ <\ 1,

with masses
m_{j}
,
n_{j}
. For convenience we also define

x_{0}\ =\ -1,\quad\ \ x_{d+1}\ =\ 1,\quad\ \ l_{j}\ =\ x_{j+1}-x_{j},\quad\ \ M_{0}\ =\ {\bf 0}.

Here conditions (
4.5
) and (
5.17
) both
reduce to the assumption that
m_{j}n_{j}>0
,
j=1,\dots d
.

The partial fundamental
solution
\Phi(x,\lambda)
satisfies
D^{2}\Phi=0
except at the
x_{j}
, so it is piecewise
linear in
x
, and the derivative
D\Phi
is piecewise constant. Thus for any given

\lambda
the function
\Phi
is characterized by its values

\Phi_{j}\ =\ \Phi_{j}(\lambda)\ =\ \Phi(x_{j},\lambda),\quad j=0,\dots d+1.

(7.1)

Similarly,
D\Phi=\Phi_{j}^{\prime}
is characterized by its one-sided values

\Phi_{j}^{\prime}\ =\ D\Phi_{j}(\lambda)\ =\ D\Phi(x_{j}-,\lambda),\quad j=1,\dots d+1.

(7.2)

The beam equation
D^{2}\Phi=\lambda M\Phi
, with initial conditions

\Phi(-1,\lambda)\ =\ {\bf 0},\qquad D\Phi(-1,\lambda)={\bf 1},

translates to the conditions

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf 0},\qquad\Phi_{j+1}\ =\ \Phi_{j}+l_{j}\Phi^{\prime}_{j+1},

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bf 1},\qquad\Phi^{\prime}_{j+1}\ =\ \Phi^{\prime}_{j}+\lambda M_{j}\Phi_{j}.

These relations can be put in two forms:

\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi_{j+1}\\
\Phi^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{matrix}\right]\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}&l_{j}{\bf 1}\\
{\bf 0}&{\bf 1}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi_{j}\\
\Phi^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{matrix}\right]

(7.3)

and

\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi_{j+1}\\
\Phi^{\prime}_{j+1}\end{matrix}\right]\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}+\lambda l_{j}M_{j}&l_{j}{\bf 1}\\
\lambda M_{j}&{\bf 1}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi_{j}\\
\Phi^{\prime}_{j}\end{matrix}\right]\ =\ T_{j}\left[\begin{matrix}\Phi_{j}\\
\Phi^{\prime}_{j}\end{matrix}\right].

(7.4)

Lemma 7.1
.

Each of
\Phi_{j}
and
\Phi^{\prime}_{j}
is a polynomial of degree

has

Proof.

The first statement follows by induction from the recursion relations (
7.4
).
A consequence is that the determinant
\Delta(\lambda)=\det\Phi_{d+1}
is a
polynomial of degree
d
in

, so the eigenvalues come in
d
pairs.

∎

As shown in the general case, the eigenvalues are distinct and real.

The Weyl function (
6.17
) in the discrete case is

W(\lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{\lambda}\Phi^{\prime}_{d+1}\Phi_{d+1}^{-1}.

(7.5)

The recursion relations (
7.3
)
imply that
W(\lambda)
has a continued fraction expansion involving

[
22

24

Proposition 7.2
.

W(\lambda)=\cfrac{1}{\lambda l_{d}\mathbf{1}+\cfrac{1}{M_{d}+\cfrac{1}{\lambda l_{d-1}\mathbf{1}+\cfrac{1}{M_{d-1}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{\lambda l_{0}\mathbf{1}}}}}}}

Proof.

Let
W_{j}=\lambda^{-1}\Phi^{\prime}_{j}\Phi_{j}^{-1}
.
Note that, for
\lambda
large enough, all
\Phi^{\prime}_{j}
and

are invertible.
The relations

imply that

\Phi_{d+1}(\Phi^{\prime}_{d+1})^{-1}=(\lambda M_{d}+\Phi^{\prime}_{d}\Phi_{d}^{-1})^{-1}+l_{d}\mathbf{1},

hence

Inverting this expression we obtain

Iterating down to

\displaystyle=

Let

\displaystyle=

Proof.

,

(7.22)

8
The inverse problem for the discrete beam

We shall show that the Weyl function

has an asymptotic expansion

W(\lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{\lambda}\,C_{0}+\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\,C_{1}+\dots\ \frac{1}{\lambda^{n+1}}\,C_{n}+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{n+2}}\right)\quad\hbox{as\ \ $\lambda\to\infty$.}

(8.1)

The denominators
Q_{k}
of the Padé approximants to

can be recovered
from this asymptotic expansion of

. For example, subsitute the
expansion (
8.1
) for
W
in (
7.18
) and expand.
Since
Q_{2j-1}
has no constant term and the constant term of

is
{\bf 1}
, the term of order
0
in the expansion is
{\bf 1}
and the terms of order
-1,\dots,1-j

in the expansion are zero. Writing

(8.2)

the resulting system of equations can be written

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}Q^{(j-)}_{1}&Q^{(j-)}_{2}&\dots&Q^{(j-)}_{j}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}C_{0}&C_{1}&\dots&C_{j-1}\\
C_{1}&C_{2}&\dots&C_{j}\\
&&\dots&\\
C_{j-1}&C_{j}&\dots&C_{2j-2}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}&0&\dots&0\end{matrix}\right].

(8.3)

Write

Q_{2j}\ =\ \lambda^{j}Q^{(j+)}_{j}+\dots+\lambda Q^{(j+)}_{1}+{\bf 1}.

(8.4)

Since
Q_{2j}(0)={\bf 1}
, the same argument leads to the system

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}Q^{(j+)}_{1}&Q^{(j+)}_{2}&\dots&Q^{(j+)}_{j}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}C_{1}&C_{2}&\dots&C_{j}\\
C_{2}&C_{3}&\dots&C_{j+1}\\
&&\dots&\\
C_{j}&C_{j+1}&\dots&C_{2j-1}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\ =\ -\left[\begin{matrix}C_{0}&C_{1}&\dots&C_{j-1}\end{matrix}\right].

(8.5)

8.3
) and (
8.5
), considered as

4j

4j
unknowns. However we know

) and

j

To understand the

, we return to the formula (

) for

:

W(\lambda)\ =\ \frac{1}{2\lambda}{\bf 1}+\sum_{\nu=-d}^{-1}\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda_{\nu}}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}&-\alpha_{\nu}^{2}\\
-\beta_{\nu}^{2}&\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\end{matrix}\right]+\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda-\lambda_{\nu}}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}&\alpha_{\nu}^{2}\\
\beta_{\nu}^{2}&\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\end{matrix}\right],

where
\alpha_{\nu}=\alpha_{-\nu}
and
\beta_{\nu}=\beta_{-\nu}
are positive.

For large

,
(\lambda-\lambda_{\nu})^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\lambda_{\nu}^{k}/\lambda^{k+1}
, so

C_{0}\ =\ \left[\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}2\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\right]{\bf 1},

(8.6)

and

C_{k}\ =\ \sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\left\{\lambda_{-\nu}^{k}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}&-\alpha_{\nu}^{2}\\
-\beta_{\nu}^{2}&\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\end{matrix}\right]+\lambda_{\nu}^{k}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}&\alpha_{\nu}^{2}\\
\beta_{\nu}^{2}&\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\end{matrix}\right]\right\},\qquad k\geq 1.

Recall that
\nu
and
\lambda_{\nu}
have opposite signs, so

C_{k}\ =\ \begin{cases}\ \ \sum_{\nu=1}^{d}2|\lambda_{\nu}|^{k}\left[\begin{matrix}0&-\alpha_{\nu}^{2}\\
-\beta_{\nu}^{2}&0\end{matrix}\right],&\ \hbox{$k$\ \ odd};\\
&\\
\ \ \sum_{\nu=1}^{d}2|\lambda_{\nu}|^{k}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}&0\\
0&\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu}\end{matrix}\right],&\ \hbox{$k$\ \ even,\ \ \ $k\geq 2$.}\end{cases}

C_{k}\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}a_{k}&0\\
0&a_{k}\end{matrix}\right],\quad k\ \ \hbox{even};\qquad C_{k}\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}0&b_{k}\\
c_{k}&0\end{matrix}\right],\quad k\ \ \hbox{odd},

(8.7)

\displaystyle a_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+2\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu};\qquad a_{k}\ =\ 2\sum_{\nu=1}^{d}|\lambda_{\nu}|^{k}\alpha_{\nu}\beta_{\nu},\quad k\ {\rm even},\,k\geq 2;

\displaystyle-\sum_{\nu>0}2|\lambda_{\nu}|^{k}\alpha_{\nu}^{2};\qquad c_{k}=-\sum_{\nu>0}2|\lambda_{\nu}|^{k}\beta_{\nu}^{2},\quad\ \ \ k\ \ {\rm odd.}

Appendix: The consistency conditions; the smooth case

Under an additional assumption of smoothness, the compatibility conditions that relate

D_{x}^{2}\Phi\ =\ ({\bf 1}+\lambda M)\Phi,\qquad M\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}0&n\\
m&0\end{matrix}\right].

(A.1)

and

D_{t}\Phi\ =\ [bD_{x}+a]\Phi,

(A.2)

namely

D_{t}D_{x}^{2}\Phi\ =\ D_{x}^{2}D_{t}\Phi\

lead to

\displaystyle\lambda M_{t}\Phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{b_{xx}+2a_{x}+\lambda[b,M]\right\}D_{x}\Phi

(A.3)

\displaystyle\ +\left\{a_{xx}+2b_{x}+\lambda(bM)_{x}+\lambda b_{x}M+\lambda[a,M]\right\}\Phi.

At a given value of
t
, this is a differential equation for
\Phi
of order at most one.
We are assuming that
\Phi
is a solution of a nontrivial second–order equation.
We assume that the differential operator in (
A.3
)
trivializes:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{xx}+2a_{x}+\lambda[b,M],

(A.4)

\displaystyle\lambda M_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{xx}+2b_{x}+\lambda(bM)_{x}+\lambda b_{x}M+\lambda[a,M],

(A.5)

since otherwise the system is degenerate.

As in Section
2
we suppose that

a\ =\ a_{0}+\lambda^{-1}a_{1},\qquad b\ =\ b_{0}+\lambda^{-1}b_{1},

and that
a_{j}
and
b_{j}
are bounded,
j=0,1
. Each equation in (
A.4
), (
A.5
)
leads to three equations, for the coefficients of the powers
\lambda^{k}
,
k=-1,0,1
.

For
k=-1
the equations are

(a_{1})_{xx}+2(b_{1})_{x}\ =\ 0\,;\qquad(b_{1})_{xx}+2(a_{1})_{x}\ =0.

(A.6)

Thus

(a_{1})_{x}=-\frac{(b_{1})_{xx}}{2},\qquad(b_{1})_{xxx}-4(b_{1})_{x}=0.

(A.7)

The second equation implies that
b_{1}=C_{1}e^{2x}+C_{2}e^{-2x}+C_{3}
and since

b_{1}
is bounded,
b_{1}
is a constant matrix, and by the first equation so is
a_{1}
.

For
k=1
the equations are

0\ =\ [b_{0},M]\,;\qquad M_{t}\ =\ (b_{0}M)_{x}+(b_{0})_{x}M+[a_{0},M].

(A.8)

We assume that
m\neq n
, so first equation in (
A.8
) implies that the diagonal part
of
b_{0}
is a multiple of the identity matrix, and the off-diagonal part is a multiple of
M
:

b_{0}\ =\ uI+pM.

(A.9)

Therefore the diagonal part of the second equation in (
A.8
)
gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(pmn)_{x}+(pn)_{x}m+(a_{0})_{12}m-(a_{0})_{21}n;

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(pmn)_{x}+(pm)_{x}n+(a_{0})_{21}n-(a_{0})_{12}m.

(A.10)

Adding these two equations gives

0\ =\ 4p_{x}(mn)+3p(mn)_{x}.

Multiplying by
p^{3}(mn)^{2}
gives
0=[p^{4}(mn)^{3}]_{x}
, so
p^{4}(mn)^{3}
is constant.
If
p\neq 0
then this is a nontrivial

a priori
relationship between
m
and
n
. Therefore we assume
p=0
. With this
assumption, equations (
A.10
) imply that the off-diagonal part of
a_{0}
is proportional
to
M
. We can write

b_{0}\ =\ u{\bf 1},\qquad a_{0}\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{matrix}w(x)+v(x)&0\\
0&w(x)-v(x)\end{matrix}\right]+qM.

(A.11)

The remaining information from equations (
A.4
),
(
A.5
)
is contained in the equations for the
k=0
part:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b_{0})_{xx}+2(a_{0})_{x}+[b_{1},M]\,;

(A.12)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(a_{0})_{xx}+2(b_{0})_{x}+b_{1}M_{x}+[a_{1},M].,

(A.13)

since
a_{1}
,
b_{1}
are constant. Write

a_{1}\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}\\
\alpha_{3}&\alpha_{4}\end{matrix}\right]\,;\qquad b_{1}\ =\ \left[\begin{matrix}\beta_{1}&\beta_{2}\\
\beta_{3}&\beta_{4}\end{matrix}\right].

Looking at the diagonal terms, then the off-diagonal terms, in (
A.12
), we find

\displaystyle u_{xx}+w_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0;\qquad v_{x}\ =\ \beta_{3}n-\beta_{2}m;

(A.14)

\displaystyle 2(qn)_{x}+(\beta_{1}-\beta_{4})n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ 0\ =\ 2(qm)_{x}+(\beta_{4}-\beta_{1})m.

(A.15)

Multiply the left side of (
A.15
) by
n
, the right side by
m
, and add, to obtain:

0\ =\ 2\left[(qn)_{x}m+(qm)_{x}n\right]\ =\ 2\left[2q_{x}(mn)+q(mn)_{x}\right].

As above, unless
q=0
this gives an
a priori
relation
q^{2}(mn)=
constant.
Taking
q=0
, (
A.15
) implies
\beta_{1}=\beta_{4}
.

Looking at the off-diagonal terms in (
A.13
), we obtain equations for
n
and for
m
with
constant coefficients:

\beta_{1}n_{x}\ =\ (\alpha_{4}-\alpha_{1})n;\qquad\beta_{1}m_{x}\ =\ (\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{4})m.

In order to avoid trivial cases, we must assume that
\beta_{1}=0
and
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{4}
.
Computing the diagonal part of (
A.13
), taking into account
(
A.14
) gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}u_{xxx}+2u_{x}+(\beta_{2}m+\beta_{3}n)_{x}]\,;

(A.16)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}m-\alpha_{3}n.

(A.17)

To avoid a trivial linear relation
between
m
and
n
we need the off–diagonal terms
\alpha_{2}
,
\alpha_{3}
of
a_{1}
to vanish.

Summing up to this point:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{matrix}\gamma-u_{x}+v&0\\
0&\gamma-u_{x}-v\end{matrix}\right]+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left[\begin{matrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\alpha\end{matrix}\right]\,;

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}u&0\\
0&u\end{matrix}\right]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[\begin{matrix}0&\beta_{2}\\
\beta_{3}&0\end{matrix}\right]\,,

where
\gamma
,
\alpha
,
\beta_{2}
, and
\beta_{3}
are constant.

Keeping in mind the obvious
symmetry between
(\varphi_{1},m)
on one hand and
(\varphi_{2},n)
on the other, we symmetrize
by taking
\beta_{2}=\beta_{3}=\beta
. Moreover, the first Lax equation (
A.1
) has an additional gauge
symmetry
\Phi\rightarrow\omega(t,\lambda)\Phi
. Under this gauge transformation

a\rightarrow\omega_{t}\omega^{-1}+a,

and thus, by choosing
\omega
to satisfy
\omega_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\gamma+\frac{\alpha}{\lambda})\omega=0
, we can eliminate
both
\alpha
and
\gamma
from the parametrization of
a
, obtaining:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{matrix}u_{x}-v&0\\
0&\-u_{x}+v\end{matrix}\right];

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}u&0\\
0&u\end{matrix}\right]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[\begin{matrix}0&\beta\\
\beta&0\end{matrix}\right].

Finally, we note that this form of
a,b
implies that the Lax pair has a scaling symmetry:

\lambda\rightarrow s\lambda,M\rightarrow\frac{1}{s}M,\beta\rightarrow s\beta
. Choosing the scale to be

s=\frac{1}{\beta}
fixes
\beta=1
and we obtain the final form (see (
2.4
))

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{matrix}u_{x}-v&0\\
0&\-u_{x}+v\end{matrix}\right];

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}u&0\\
0&u\end{matrix}\right]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[\begin{matrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{matrix}\right],

used in Section
2
.
