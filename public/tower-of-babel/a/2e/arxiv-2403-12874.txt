Solutions to a Romanoff type problem
Yuchen Ding

Abstract
. We answer negatively a 2014 problem of Yang and Chen on Romanoff type representations. Sharp results involving their problem were also obtained in this article.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11P32, 11A41; Secondary 11B13.

1.
Introduction

In 1849, de Polignac
[
12
]
made the conjecture that any odd number greater than 3 is the sum of a prime and a power of 2. But soon, he
[
13
]
recognized that 127 and 959 are two counterexamples. Actually, as de Polignac mentioned, these two counterexamples were already pointed out in a 1752 letter from Euler to Goldbach. Along the positive line, Romanoff
[
14
]
proved that there is a positive proportion of the odd numbers which can be represented by the sum of a prime and a power of 2. To answer a question of Romanoff, in 1950 Erdős
[
8
]
constructed an arithmetic progression, none of whose member can be written as the sum of a prime and a power of 2. This immediately leads to a theorem of
van der Corput
[
4
]
, which states that the odd numbers not with the form
p+2^{m}
, where
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, also possess positive lower density.
During the past decades, a large number of variants of the Romanoff theorem were established. To name only a few of them, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
11
]
and the references listed therein.

For any set
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{A}(x)=\left|\mathcal{A}\cap[1,x]\right|
. A subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{N}
is said to satisfy
c
-condition if
\mathcal{B}(cx)\gg\mathcal{B}(x)
for some positive constant
c<1
. In the article of Yang and Chen
[
15
]
, the following sumset

\mathcal{S}=\left\{p+b:p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}

is considered, where
\mathcal{B}
is a subset of
\mathbb{N}
with
c
-condition. They proved that

\frac{x}{\log x}\min\left\{\mathcal{B}(x),\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}\right\}\ll\mathcal{S}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\min\left\{\mathcal{B}(x),\log x\right\}.

(1.1)

As an application, Chen and Yang showed that

\#\left\{n\leq x:n=p+2^{a^{2}}+2^{b^{2}},p\in\mathcal{P},a,b\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\gg\frac{x}{\log\log x}.

Besides the above result, they also constructed a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathcal{B}(x)=\frac{1+o(1)}{m+1}\left(\frac{\log x}{\log\log x}\right)^{m+1}

(1.2)

and

\mathcal{S}(x)\ll\frac{x}{\log\log x},

where
m
is an arbitrarily given integer. Yang and Chen
[
15
]
then posed the following two problems for further research.

Problem 1
.

Does there exist a real number
\alpha>0
and a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{N}
with
c
-condition such that
\mathcal{B}(x)\gg x^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x/\log\log x
?

Problem 2
.

Does there exist a positive integer
k
such that the set of positive integers which can be represented as
p+\sum_{i=1}^{k}2^{m_{i}^{2}}
with
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
has a positive lower density? If such
k
exists, what is the minimal value of such
k
?

Recently, the author
[
5
]
gave a complete solution to
Problem 2
by showing that
k=2
is admissible. In this subsequent note, we turn to the investigations of
Problem 1
. We give a negative answer to it via the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
be arbitrarily small given number and
\mathcal{B}
a subset of
\mathbb{N}
with
c
-condition such that
\mathcal{B}(x)\gg x^{\alpha}
. Then

\mathcal{S}(x)=\#\left\{n\leq x:n=p+b,p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}\gg x/\log\log\log x.

It would be of interest to show that the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
is in fact optimistic in some sense. Let’s explain this by the following Theorem
1.2
.

From now on, let
\exp(y)
denote
e^{y}
for any real number
y
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any positive integer
m\geq 2
, there exists a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathbb{N}
with
c
-condition
such that

\mathcal{B}(x)\gg x\exp\left(-\frac{2}{m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right)

and

\mathcal{S}(x)=\#\left\{p+b\leq x:p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}\ll x/\log\log\log x.

It is clear that

x\exp\left(-\frac{2}{m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right)>x^{1-\varepsilon}

for any given
\varepsilon>0
, provided that
x
is sufficiently large. Thus,
Theorem
1.2
surpasses our requirement significantly.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The standard method in the proof of the Romanoff type theorems lies in the investigations on moments of the representation function defined as

f(n)=\#\left\{(p,b):n=p+b,p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}.

But, as it was already shown by Yang and Chen
[
15
]
, this could only lead to the bound

\mathcal{S}(x)\gg x/\log\log x.

Our new idea is, instead of investigating
f(n)
, studying a certain truncated representation function

f_{\alpha}(n)=\#\left\{(p,b):n=p+b,p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B},b<(\log n)^{1/\alpha}\right\}.

(2.1)

We now proceed the details below.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
\mathcal{B}
is a subset of
\mathbb{N}
with
c
-condition, there is a constant
c<1
such that
\mathcal{B}(cx)\gg\mathcal{B}(x)
. For any given
\alpha>0
, there exists some
0<\delta<1
(depending on
c
and
\alpha
) so that

\delta^{1/\alpha}>c.

Let
f_{\alpha}(n)
be defined as the one in Eq. (
2.1
). Noting that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(x)

\displaystyle=\#\left\{n\leq x:n=p+b,p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\#\left\{n\leq x:n=p+b,p\in\mathcal{P},b\in\mathcal{B},b<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x),\quad\text{say},

it suffices to prove
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)\gg x/\log\log\log x
. Employing the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we would obtain that

\displaystyle\Bigg(\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}(n)\Bigg)^{2}\leq\Bigg(\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}^{2}(n)\Bigg)\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)

(2.2)

since any number
n\leq x
with
f_{\alpha}(n)\geq 1
will clearly be counted exactly once by
\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)
. It can be seen for sufficiently large
x
that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}(n)\geq\sum_{x^{\delta}<n\leq x}f_{\alpha}(n)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x^{\delta}<p+b\leq x\\
b<\left(\log x^{\delta}\right)^{1/\alpha}\end{subarray}}1\geq\sum_{x^{\delta}<p<x/2}1\sum_{b<(\log x^{\delta})^{1/\alpha}}1.

Thus, we deduce from above and the prime number theorem that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}(n)\gg_{c,\alpha}\frac{x}{\log x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x^{\delta})^{1/\alpha}\right)=\frac{x}{\log x}\mathcal{B}\left(\delta^{1/\alpha}(\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right),

provided that
x
is sufficiently large (in terms of
c
and
\alpha
).
Recall that
\delta^{1/\alpha}>c
, we know

\mathcal{B}\left(\delta^{1/\alpha}(\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)\geq\mathcal{B}\left(c(\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)\gg_{c,\alpha}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right),

where the last inequality comes from the
c
-condition of
\mathcal{B}
.
It then follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}(n)\gg_{c,\alpha}\frac{x}{\log x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right).

(2.3)

We are now in a position to study the second moment of
f_{\alpha}(n)
on looking at Eq. (
2.2
).

It is clear that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}^{2}(n)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}+b_{1}=p_{2}+b_{2}\leq x\\
b_{1},b_{2}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}\end{subarray}}1.

By separating the cases that
b_{1}=b_{2}
or not, one can observe easily that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}^{2}(n)\leq\sum_{b_{1}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{p_{1}\leq x}1+2\sum_{b_{1}<b_{2}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{2}<p_{1}\leq x\\
p_{1}-p_{2}=b_{2}-b_{1}\end{subarray}}1.

(2.4)

Using again the prime number theorem, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{b_{1}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{p_{1}\leq x}1\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right).

(2.5)

For even
h\neq 0
, let
\pi_{2}(x,h)
be the number of prime pairs
p
and
q
with
q-p=h
not exceeding
x
. It is well known (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorem 7.3]
) that

\displaystyle\pi_{2}(x,h)\ll\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\prod_{p|h}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(2.6)

It is also well–known (see e.g.
[
9
]
) that

\displaystyle\prod_{p|h}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)\ll\log\log h.

(2.7)

From Eqs. (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) we have

\displaystyle\sum_{b_{1}<b_{2}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{2}<p_{1}\leq x\\
p_{1}-p_{2}=b_{2}-b_{1}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}\sum_{b_{1}<b_{2}<(\log x)^{1/\alpha}}\prod_{p|b_{2}-b_{1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle\ll_{\alpha}\frac{x\log\log\log x}{\log^{2}x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{2}.

(2.8)

Taking Eqs. (
2.5
) and (
2.8
) into Eq. (
2.4
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}f_{\alpha}^{2}(n)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)+\frac{x\log\log\log x}{\log^{2}x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x\log\log\log x}{\log^{2}x}\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{2}

(2.9)

since
\mathcal{B}\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)\gg\left((\log x)^{1/\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}=\log x
. Now, it follows immediately from Eqs. (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.9
) that

\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(x)\gg_{c,\alpha}x/\log\log\log x,

which completes the proof of our theorem.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
p_{i}
be the
i
-th prime and
d_{t}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdot\cdot\cdot p_{t}
for any positive integer
t
.
For any positive integer
j
, set

\mathcal{B}_{j}=\left\{n:n\in\mathbb{N},d_{j}|n\right\}\cap\left[\exp\left(j^{m}\right),\exp\left((j+1)^{m}\right)\right)

and

\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{j}.

It is not difficult to see that there is some positive integer
j_{0}
such that

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{j_{0}}|<|\mathcal{B}_{j_{0}+1}|<|\mathcal{B}_{j_{0}+2}|<|\mathcal{B}_{j_{0}+3}|<\cdot\cdot\cdot

(3.1)

via the prime number theorem.
Let
x
be a sufficiently large number. Suppose that

\displaystyle\exp\left(\ell^{m}\right)\leq x<\exp\left((\ell+1)^{m}\right),

(3.2)

then

\displaystyle(\log x)^{1/m}-1<\ell\leq(\log x)^{1/m}.

(3.3)

By Eq. (
3.3
) together with the Chebyshev estimate, we have

\displaystyle d_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq p_{\ell}}\log p\right)<\exp\left(2\ell\log\ell\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{2}{m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right),

(3.4)

\displaystyle d_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigg(\sum_{p\leq p_{\ell-1}}\log p\Bigg)>\exp\left(\frac{2}{3}\ell\log\ell\right)\geq\exp\left(\frac{1}{2m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right),

(3.5)

and

\displaystyle\exp\left((\ell-1)^{m}\right)<\exp\left(\ell^{m}-(m-1/2)\ell^{m-1}\right)<x\exp\left(-(m-1)(\log x)^{\frac{m-1}{m}}\right).

(3.6)

By Eqs. (
3.4
), (
3.6
) and definition of
\mathcal{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathcal{B}_{\ell}(x)+\left|\mathcal{B}_{\ell-1}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{x-\exp\left(\ell^{m}\right)}{d_{\ell}}+\frac{\exp\left(\ell^{m}\right)-\exp\left((\ell-1)^{m}\right)}{d_{\ell-1}}-3

\displaystyle\geq\frac{x-\exp\left((\ell-1)^{m}\right)}{d_{\ell}}-3

\displaystyle\gg x\exp\left(-\frac{2}{m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right).

The subset
\mathcal{B}
is the one satisfying
c
-condition, since the elements of
\mathcal{B}
lying in the interval
[x/2,x)
are divisible by more (or at least equal) primes than those in the interval
[1,x/2)
, which leads to the fact that there are more elements of
\mathcal{B}
located in
[1,x/2)
than in
[x/2,x)
. Therefore, we have
\mathcal{B}(x/2)\geq\mathcal{B}(x)/2
. It remains to prove

\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x/\log\log\log x.

To this aim, let
s
be a sufficiently large integer depending on
x
and
m
which shall be decided later.
The sums
p+b~(b\in\mathcal{B})
up to
x
will divided into the following three parts:

Part I are the sums
p+b
with
p>p_{\ell}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}_{j}~(s\leq j\leq\ell)
. The sums
p+b
in this part cannot be divisible by primes
p_{j}~(1\leqslant j\leqslant s)
, thus the number of the sums
p+b
within this part is not large than

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\left(n,\prod_{p\leq p_{s}}p\right)=1\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{k|\prod_{p\leq p_{s}}p}\mu(k)\left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq x\prod_{p\leq p_{s}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(2^{s}\right)

\displaystyle\ll x(\log p_{s})^{-1}+2^{s}

\displaystyle\ll x(\log s)^{-1}+2^{s},

(3.7)

where the last but one step follows from the Mertens estimate.

Part II are the sums
p+b
with
p\leq p_{\ell}
and
b\in\mathcal{B}_{j}~(s\leq j\leq\ell).
The number of these sums
p+b
up to
x
can be bounded by Eqs. (
3.1
), (
3.3
), (
3.5
) and the Chebyshev estimate as

\displaystyle\left(|\mathcal{B}_{s}|+\cdot\cdot\cdot+|\mathcal{B}_{\ell-1}|+\mathcal{B}_{\ell}(x)\right)\ell

\displaystyle\leq\left(\ell|\mathcal{B}_{\ell-1}|+\mathcal{B}_{\ell}(x)\right)\ell

\displaystyle\leq\left((\log x)^{1/m}\frac{\exp\left(\ell^{m}\right)}{d_{\ell-1}}+\frac{x}{d_{\ell}}\right)(\log x)^{1/m}

\displaystyle\leq 2x(\log x)^{2/m}/d_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\ll x(\log x)^{2/m}\exp\left(\!-\frac{1}{2m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\!\right).

(3.8)

Part III are the remaining sums
p+b
with
b\in\mathcal{B}_{j}~(1\leq j\leq s)
. These remaining sums are trivially bounded by

\displaystyle s|\mathcal{B}_{s}|\pi(x)\ll s\frac{\exp\left((s+1)^{m}\right)}{d_{s}}\frac{x}{\log x}\ll\frac{x}{\log x}\exp\left((s+1)^{m}\right).

(3.9)

Gathering together Eqs. (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we would have

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(x)\ll

\displaystyle x(\log x)^{2/m}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2m}(\log x)^{1/m}\log\log x\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+2^{s}+x(\log s)^{-1}+\frac{x}{\log x}\exp\left((s+1)^{m}\right).

(3.10)

Now we choose
s
to be the integer
\left\lfloor(\log\log x)^{\frac{1}{2m}}\right\rfloor-1
, then Eq. (
3.10
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x(\log\log\log x)^{-1}.

(3.11)

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
∎
