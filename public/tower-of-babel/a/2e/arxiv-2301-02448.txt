Optimal subsampling algorithm for composite quantile regression with distributed data
Xiaohui Yuan, Shiting Zhou, Yue Wang

Abstract
For massive data stored at multiple machines, we propose a distributed subsampling procedure for the composite quantile regression. By establishing the consistency and asymptotic normality of the composite quantile regression estimator from a general subsampling algorithm, we derive the optimal subsampling probabilities and the optimal allocation sizes under the L-optimality criteria. A two-step algorithm to approximate the optimal subsampling procedure is developed. The proposed methods are illustrated through numerical experiments on simulated and real datasets.

Introduction

With the rapid development of science and technology, extremely large datasets are ubiquitous and lays heavy burden on storage and computation facilities.
Many efforts have been made to deal with these challenge. There are three main directions from the view of statistical applications: divide-and-conquer, online updating, and subsampling. Among them, subsampling has been found to be useful for reducing computational burden and extracting information from massive data.

The idea of subsampling was first proposed by Jones (1956)
jones1956
. A key tactic of subsampling methods is to specify nonuniform sampling probabilities to include more informative data points with higher probabilities. For example, the leverage score-based subsampling in Ma et al. (2015)
Ma2015
, the information based optimal subdata selection in Wang et al. (2019)
Wang2019
, and the optimal subsampling method under the A-optimality criterion in Wang et al. (2018)
wang2018
. Recently, Fang et al. (2021)
fang2021
applied subsampling to a weak-signal-assisted procedure for variable selection and statistical inference. Ai et al. (2021)
ai2021
studied the optimal subsampling method for generalized linear models under the A-optimality criterion.
Shao et al. (2022)
ShaoL2022
employed the optimal subsampling method to ordinary quantile regression.

Due to the large scale and fast arrival speed of data stream, massive data are often partitioned across multiple servers. For example, Walmart stores produce a large number of data sets from different locations around the world, which need to be processed. However, it is difficult to transmit these datasets to a central location. For these datasets, it is common to analyze them on multiple machines. Qiu et al. (2020)
qiu2020
constructed a data stream classification model based on distributed processing. Sun et al. (2021)
sun2021
proposed a data mining scheme for edge computing based on distributed integration strategy. Zhang and Wang (2021)
zhand2021
proposed a distributed subdata selection method for big data linear regression model. Zuo et al. (2021)
zuo2021
proposed a distributed subsampling procedure for the logistic regression. Yu et al. (2022)
yu2022
derived a optimal distributed Poisson subsampling procedure for the maximum quasi-likelihood estimators with massive data.

In the paper, we investigate the optimal distributed subsampling for composite quantile regression (CQR; Zou and Yuan (2008)
zou2008
) in massive data.
In a linear model, composite quantile regression can uniformly estimate the regression coefficients under heavy tail error. Moreover, since the asymptotic variance of the composite quantile regression estimate does not depend on the moment of the error distribution, the CQR estimator is robust. The CQR method is widely used in many fields. For massive data, Jiang et al. (2018)
jiang2018
proposed a divide-and-conquer CQR method. Jin and Zhao (2021)
jin2021
proposed a divide-and-conquer CQR neural network method. Wang et al. (2021)
wang2021
proposed a distributed CQR method for the massive data. Shao and Wang (2022)
ShaoY2021
and Yuan et al. (2022)
yuan2022
developed the subsampling for composite quantile regression. To the best of our knowledge, there is almost no work on random subsampling for composite quantile regression with distributed data.

Based on the above motivation, we investigate the optimal subsampling for the composite quantile regression in massive data when the datasets are stored at different sites. We propose a distributed subsampling method in the context of CQR, and then study the optimal subsampling technology for data in each machine.
The main advantages of our method are as follows: First, we establish the convergence rate of the subsample-based estimator, which ensures the consistency of our proposed method. Second, it avoids the impact of different intercept items in data sets stored at different sites. Third, the computational speed of our subsampling method is much faster than the full data approach.

The rest of this article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we propose the distributed subsampling algorithm based on composite quantile regression. The asymptotic properties of estimators based on subsamples are also established. We present a subsampling strategy with optimal subsampling probability and optimal allocation size. The simulation studies are given in Section 3. In Section 4, we study the real data sets. The content of the article is summarized in Section 5. All proofs are given in the Appendix.

2.2
Subsampling algorithm and asymptotic properties

In this subsection, we propose a distributed subsampling algorithm to approximate the

Algorithm 1
Distributed Subsampling Algorithm£º

•

Sampling:
Assign subsampling probabilities
\{\pi_{ik}\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}

k

•

Estimation:

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}=(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{s},\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{s})=\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}}Q^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\rho_{\tau_{m}}(y_{ik}^{\ast}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\textsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\ast}-b_{m})}{\pi_{ik}^{\ast}}.

To establish asymptotic properties of the subsample-based estimator
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}
, we need the following assumptions:

(A.1)
Assume that
f(t)
is continuous with respect to
t
and
0<f(b_{0m})<+\infty
for
1\leq m\leq M
. Let
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}=(\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\textsf{T}},\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{m}^{\textsf{T}})^{\textsf{T}}
, where
\mbox{\boldmath$e$}_{m}
denotes a
M\times 1
vector, which has a one only in its

Define

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}f(b_{0m})\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m})^{\textsf{T}}.

(3)

Assume that there exist positive definite matrices
E
, such that

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}\longrightarrow\mbox{\boldmath$E$},\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \max_{1\leq k\leq K,1\leq i\leq n_{k}}\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(n^{1/2}).

(A.2)
Assume that, for

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq k\leq K,1\leq i\leq n_{k}}\frac{\|\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}\|+1}{r_{k}\pi_{ik}}=o_{p}\left(\frac{n}{r^{1/2}}\right).

(4)

Define

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{1}{\pi_{ik}}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\right]^{\otimes 2},

(5)

where for a vector
a

\mbox{\boldmath$a$}^{\otimes 2}=\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\mbox{\boldmath$a$}^{\textsf{T}}

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mbox{\boldmath$V$},

where

Theorem 1
. If Assumptions (A.1) and (A.2) hold, conditional on

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$I$}),

(6)

2.3
Optimal subsampling strategy

Given
r
, we specify the subsampling probablities
\{\pi_{ik}\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
, and the allocation sizes
\{r_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
in Algorithm 1. A naive choice is the uniform subsampling strategy with

\{\pi_{ik}=1/n_{k}\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
and
\{r_{k}=[rn_{k}/n]\}_{k=1}^{K}
, where
[\cdot]
denotes the rounding operation. However, this uniform subsampling method is not optimal. As suggested by Wang et al. (2018)
wang2018
, we adopted the nonuniform subsampling strategy to determine the optimal allocation sizes and optimal subsampling probabilities by minimizing the trace of
\Sigma
in Theorem 1.

Since
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}
, the optimal allocation sizes and subsampling probabilities require the calculation of
\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}
, which depend on the unknown density function
f(\cdot)
. Following Wang and Ma (2021)
wangM2021
, we derive optimal subsampling probabilities under the L-optimality criterion. Note that
\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}
are nonnegative definite. Simple matrix algebra yields that
tr(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})=tr(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-2})=tr(\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-2})tr(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi})
.
\Sigma
depends on
r_{k}
and
\pi_{ik}
only through
\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}
, and
\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}
is free of
r_{k}
and
\pi_{ik}
. Hence, we suggest to determine the optimal allocation sizes and optimal subsampling probabilites by directly minimizing
tr(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi})
rather than
tr(\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$})
, which can effectively speed up our subsampling algorithm.

Theorem 2
. If
r_{k}
and
\pi_{ik}
,
i=1,\cdots,n_{k}
,
k=1,\cdots,K
, are chosen as

\displaystyle\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}=\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})=\frac{\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel},

(7)

and

\displaystyle r_{k}^{Lopt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel},

(8)

then
tr(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi})/n
attains its minimum.

2.4
Two-step algorithm

Note that the optimal subsampling probabilities and allocation sizes depend depends on
\varepsilon_{ik}=y_{ik}-\mbox{\boldmath$x$}^{T}_{ik}\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}_{0}
and
b_{0m}
,
m=1,\cdots,M
.
The L-optimal weight result is not directly implementable. To deal with this problem, we use a pilot estimator
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
to replace
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
. In the following, we propose a two-step subsampling procedure in Algorithm 2.

Algorithm 2
Two-Step Algorithm£º

•

Step 1:
Given
r_{0}
, we run Algorithm 1 with subsampling size
r_{k}=[r_{0}\frac{n_{k}}{n}]
to obtain a pilot estimator
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}
, using
\pi_{ik}=1/n_{k}
, where
[\cdot]
denotes the rounding operation. Replace
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
with
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{0}
in (
7
) and (
8
) to get the allocation sizes
r_{k}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
and subsampling probabilities
\pi_{ik}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})
, for
i=1,\cdots,n_{k}
and
k=1,\cdots,K
, respectively.

•

Step 2:
Based on
\{r_{k}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\{\pi_{ik}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
in Step 1, we can select a subsample
\{(y_{ik}^{\ast},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\ast},\pi_{ik}^{\ast}):i=1,\cdots,r_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
from the full data
D_{n}
. Minimizes the following weighted function

\displaystyle Q^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}(\tilde{\text{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}})}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\rho_{\tau_{m}}(y_{ik}^{\ast}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\textsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\ast}-b_{m})}{\pi_{ik}^{\ast}},

to get a two-step subsample estimate
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt}
, where
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt}=(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{Lopt},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{Lopt})=\arg\min Q^{\ast}(\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$})
.

For the subsample-based estimator
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt}
in Algorithm 2, we give its asymptotic distribution in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
. If Assumptions (A.1) and (A.2) hold, then as
r_{0}\rightarrow\infty
,
r\rightarrow\infty
, and
n\rightarrow\infty
, then we have

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$I$}),

(9)

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}
denotes convergence in distribution,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}=\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}
. Here

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}^{Lopt}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{1}{\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}}\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\right]^{\otimes 2},

(10)

where

\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}=\frac{\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel},

and

r_{k}^{Lopt}=r\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\parallel\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{\tau_{m}-I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\parallel}.

For the statistical inference about
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
, to avoid estimating
f(b_{0m})
, we propose the following iterative sampling procedure.

Firstly, using
\{\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\}_{i=1}^{n_{k}}
proposed in Algorithm 2, we sample with replacement to obtain
B
subsamples,
\{(y_{ik}^{\ast,j},\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\ast,j},\pi_{ik}^{\ast,j}),i=1,\cdots,r_{k}^{Lopt}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}),k=1,\cdots,K\}
for
j=1,\cdots,B
. Next, we calculate the
j
th estimate of
\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0}
through

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{Lopt,j},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{Lopt,j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\text{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}^{Lopt}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{k}^{Lopt}(\tilde{\text{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}})}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{\rho_{\tau_{m}}(y_{ik}^{\ast,j}-\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\textsf{T}}\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{ik}^{\ast,j}-b_{m})}{\pi_{ik}^{\ast,j}}.

The combined estimate can be obtained by

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L}=(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{L}^{\textsf{T}},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$b$}}_{L}^{\textsf{T}})^{\textsf{T}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt,j}

(11)

and its variance-covariance matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}=\mbox{cov}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L})
can be estimated by

\displaystyle\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{ef}B(B-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{B}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt,j}-\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L})^{\otimes 2},

(12)

where
r_{ef}
is the effective subsample size ratio (Wang & Ma, 2021
wangM2021
) given by

\displaystyle r_{ef}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(1-\frac{r_{k}B-1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\{\pi_{ik}^{Lopt}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}})\}^{2}\right).

From Theorem 3, for any fixed
B
, the conditional distribution of
\sqrt{rB}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\sqrt{rB}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}

\displaystyle N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$I$}).

The distribution of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt}
can be approximated by the empirical distribution of
\{\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{Lopt,j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
. For
s=1,\cdots,p+K
, the
100\times(1-\alpha)
% confidence interval of
\theta_{0s}
can be approximated by
[\hat{\theta}_{L,s}-\hat{\omega}_{ss}^{1/2}z_{1-\alpha/2},\hat{\theta}_{L,s}+\hat{\omega}_{ss}^{1/2}z_{1-\alpha/2}]
, where
\hat{\theta}_{L,s}
is the
s
th element of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{L}
,
\hat{\omega}_{ss}
is the
(s,s)
th element of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}}
and
z_{1-\alpha/2}
is the
1-\alpha/2
quantile of the standard normal distribution.

\displaystyle\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})=-\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{r}^{*}+o_{p}(1).

Based on the above results, we can prove that

\displaystyle\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}^{-1}_{n}\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}_{r}^{*}+o_{p}(1).

By Slutsky’s Theorem, for any
a\in\mathbf{R}^{p+M}
, from (
17
) we have that

\displaystyle P[\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\leq\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\mid D_{n}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Phi_{p+M}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}),

(28)

where
\Phi_{p+M}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$})
denotes the standard
p+M
dimensional multivariate normal distribution function. And the conditional probability in (
28
) is a bounded random variable, then convergence in probability to a constant implies convergence in the mean.
Therefore, for any
\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\in R^{p+M}
,

\displaystyle P[\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\leq\mbox{\boldmath$a$}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E(P[\{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi}\mbox{\boldmath$E$}_{n}^{-1}\}^{-1/2}\sqrt{r}(\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{s}-\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}_{0})\leq\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\mid D_{n}])

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\Phi_{p+M}(\mbox{\boldmath$a$}).

We complete the proof of Theorem 1.

Proof the Theorem 2

We can prove that

\displaystyle tr(\mbox{\boldmath$V$}_{\pi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{1}{\pi_{ik}}tr\left(\left[\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\right]^{\otimes 2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}}\biggr(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\pi_{ik}\biggr)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\frac{1}{\pi_{ik}}\biggr\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}[I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}]\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\biggr\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{r}{r_{k}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\biggr\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\biggr\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_{k}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{r_{k}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\biggr\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}[I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}]\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\biggr\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\biggr\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}\{I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}\}\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\biggr\|^{2},

with Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the equality in it holds if and only if when
\pi_{ik}\propto\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}[I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}]\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\|
and
r_{k}\propto\sum_{i=1}^{n_{k}}\|\sum_{m=1}^{M}[I(\varepsilon_{ik}<b_{0m})-\tau_{m}]\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{ik,m}\|
, respectively. We complete the proof of Theorem 2.
