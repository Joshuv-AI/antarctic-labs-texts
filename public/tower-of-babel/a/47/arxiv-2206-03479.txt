Abstract




Abstract:

We study certain aspects of the Selberg sieve, in particular when sifting by rather thin sets of primes. We derive new results for the lower bound sieve suited especially for this setup and we apply them in particular to give a new sieve-propelled proof of Linnik’s theorem on the least prime in an arithmetic progression in the case of the presence of exceptional zeros.

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})
be a finite sequence of numbers
a_{n}\geqslant 0
. Our goal is to estimate the sifting function

(1.1)

S(\mathcal{A},z)=\sum_{(n,P(z))=1}a_{n}

where
P(z)
is the product of all primes
p<z
.

To this end we assmue that the congruence sums

(1.2)

A_{d}=\sum_{n\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}d)}a_{n}

are well-approximated by

(1.3)

A_{d}=g(d)X+r_{d}\quad{\rm if}\,\,d\mid P(z)

where
g(d)
is a multiplicative function (called the density) satisfying

(1.4)

0\leqslant g(p)<1

and
X>0
is a fixed number such that the error terms
r_{d}
are small
on average.

We assume that the “remainder”

(1.5)

R(y)=\sum_{d<y}\tau_{3}(d)|r_{d}|

is considerably smaller than the contribution to
S(\mathcal{A},z)
from the main
terms in (
1.3
), so it is negligible. The larger the value of
y

that can be used, the stronger the estimates that can be obtained.
Here, the mysterious looking presence of the divisor function
\tau_{3}
is
due to our use of the Selberg sieve weights.

The
\Lambda^{2}
-sieve of Selberg yields a lovely upper bound

(1.6)

S(\mathcal{A},z)\leqslant XJ^{-1}+R(\Delta^{2})

where

(1.7)

J=\sum_{d\leqslant\Delta,\,d|P(z)}h(d)

and
h(d)
is the multiplicative function with

(1.8)

h(p)=\frac{g(p)}{1-g(p)}.

Obviously,

(1.9)

J\leqslant\prod_{p|P(z)}(1+h(p))=V(z)^{-1}

where

(1.10)

V(z)=\prod_{p|P(z)}(1-g(p)).

Hence, the main term in the upper bound (
1.6
) exceeds
XV(z)
.
Therefore, the remainder is insignificant if, say,

(1.11)

R(\Delta^{2})\ll XV(z)(\log\Delta)^{-1}.

But, we still need a lower bound for
J
in (
1.6
). A strong
and useful bound is difficult to establish unless we make some further
assumptions about the density function
g(d)
or its companion
h(d)
. It is
quite often the case that
h(d)
behaves nicely in the sense that the
Dirichlet series

(1.12)

D(s)=\sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{d\geqslant 1}h(d)d^{1-s}

admits an analytic continuation to
{\rm Re}\,s>\frac{1}{2}
, with only
a pole at
s=1
.

Here and in what follows, the superscript
\flat
indicates that the summation
variable is restricted to squarefree numbers.

Chapter 7 of [Opera] provides a plethora of major examples with proofs. In
Section 7.9 an asymptotic formula for
J
is given under the assumption

(1.13)

\sum_{p\leqslant x}g(p)\log p=\kappa\log x+O(1)

for every
x\geqslant 2
, where
\kappa
is a positive constant called the sieve
dimension. This approximation tells us that
g(p)p
is
\kappa
on average
provided that
p
is sufficiently large in terms of some defining parameters
of
g
, so that the error term in (
1.13
) can be ignored.

However, in some practical sieve applications, the function
g(p)
appears in
segments in which it is not uniformly distributed. For example, in Section 24.2
of [Opera] we have

(1.14)

\begin{cases}g(p)p=1+\chi(p)\bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigr)\quad{\rm if}\,\,p\nmid q\\
g(p)=0\quad{\rm if}\,\,p\mid q,\end{cases}

where
\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)
is a real, non-principal character. In this
case (
1.13
) holds with
\kappa=1
but, so far as we know,
with a terribly poor error term.

Fortunately, sieve methods can also
produce useful estimates when one has access only to upper bounds for
g(p)
.
In the above example, we can use the trivial bound
g(p)p\ <2
giving

(1.15)

\sum_{p\leqslant x}g(p)\log p\leqslant 2\log x+O(1).

To get an absolutely bounded error term we have here sacrificed the
sieve dimension so the results are weaker. Still, if
z
is small in the
logarithmic scale (as in the Fundamental Lemma of sieve theory) compromising

\kappa
does not significantly affect the output.

Even if
z
is relatively large there can be significant consequences in
the case of upper bounds. Unfortunately, the sieve of superficially enlarged
dimension may yield a negative lower bound for
S(\mathcal{A},z)
in a range of
z

where it is expected to be positive.

In these notes we are concerned with
g(p)p
fluctuating unpredictably
within the segment

(1.16)

0\leqslant g(p)p<2.

We shall establish a positive lower bound

(1.17)

S(\mathcal{A},z)\gg XV(z)

for
z
quite large, provided that
g(p)p
is small on average. To this end
we could go through the recurrence formula of Buchstab via the Fundamental
Lemma. However, we can derive very explicit and neat results by means of
Selberg’s lower-bound sieve method.

2.
Selberg’s Lower-Bound Sieve

Following Selberg [S], we have

(2.1)

S(\mathcal{A},z)\geqslant S^{-}(\mathcal{A},z)

where

(2.2)

S^{-}(\mathcal{A},z)=\sum_{n}a_{n}\bigl(1-\sum_{p|n,\,p<z}1\bigr)\bigl(\sum_{d|n}\rho_{d}\bigr)^{2}

with any real numbers
\rho_{d}
,
\rho_{1}=1
. We assume that
\rho_{d}
are
supported on squarefree
d\leqslant\Delta
. Opening the square and applying the
approximations (
1.3
) we obtain

(2.3)

S^{-}(\mathcal{A},z)=XW+\mathcal{R}

where

(2.4)

W=\sum_{d_{1}}\sum_{d_{2}}\rho_{d_{1}}\rho_{d_{2}}\bigl(g([d_{1},d_{2}])-\sum_{p<z}g([p,d_{1},d_{2}])\bigr)

and
\mathcal{R}
is the corresponding remainder

(2.5)

\mathcal{R}=\sum_{d_{1}}\sum_{d_{2}}\rho_{d_{1}}\rho_{d_{2}}\bigl(r_{[d_{1},d_{2}]}-\sum_{p<z}r_{[p,d_{1},d_{2}]}\bigr).

In the quadratic form
W
in the variables
\rho_{d}
we make a linear
change of variables, specifically setting

(2.6)

y_{d}=\frac{\mu(d)}{h(d)}\sum_{m\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}d)}g(m)\rho_{m}.

Applying Möbius inversion, we find

(2.7)

\rho_{\ell}=\frac{\mu(\ell)}{g(\ell)}\sum_{d\equiv 0(\mathop{\rm{mod}}\ell)}g(d)y_{d}.

The quadratic form (
2.4
) in the new variables becomes

(2.8)

W=\sum_{d}h(d)y_{d}^{2}-\sum_{p<z}g(p)\sum_{(d,p)=1}h(d)(y_{d}-y_{pd})^{2};

see the formula (7.109) in [Opera].

Remarks:
We have tacitly assumed that
g(\ell)
,
h(d)
do not vanish
to justify the transformations (
2.6
), (
2.7
). However,
after obtaining (
2.8
) we no longer need this slight detour.

It is clear that the support conditions for
\rho_{d}
and
y_{d}
are the
same, namely

(2.9)

d\leqslant\Delta,\quad d\,\,{\rm squarefree}.

We could apply the stronger conditions
d\leqslant\Delta
,
d|P(z)
which we
omit for technical simplifications of our arguments.

The normalization
\rho_{1}=1
becomes

(2.10)

\sum_{d}h(d)y_{d}=1.

Now we choose

(2.11)

y_{d}=\frac{1}{H}\log\frac{\Delta}{d}

for
d\leqslant\Delta
,
d
squarefree,
where

(2.12)

H=\sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{d\leqslant\Delta}h(d)\log\frac{\Delta}{d}.

Recall this means we are restricting
d
to squarefree variables.

The sieve constituents
\rho_{d}
are bounded. Precisely, as in the
pure
\Lambda^{2}
-sieve of Selberg, for every squarefree
\ell\leqslant\Delta
we
argue as follows:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{k|\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<\Delta\\
(d,\ell)=k\end{subarray}}h(d)\log\frac{\Delta}{d}=\sum_{k|\ell}h(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\Delta/k\\
(m,\ell)=1\end{subarray}}h(m)\log\frac{\Delta}{mk}

\displaystyle\geqslant\bigl(\sum_{k|\ell}h(k)\bigr)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\Delta/\ell\\
(m,\ell)=1\end{subarray}}h(m)\log\frac{\Delta}{m\ell}=\frac{h(\ell)}{g(\ell)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\Delta/\ell\\
(m,\ell)=1\end{subarray}}h(m)\log\frac{\Delta}{m\ell}.

On the other hand, pulling the factor
h(\ell)
out from the
sum (
2.7
), we get

\mu(\ell)\rho_{\ell}=\frac{h(\ell)}{g(\ell)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m<\Delta/\ell\\
(m,\ell)=1\end{subarray}}h(m)\log\frac{\Delta}{m\ell}.

Combining these results we find that

(2.13)

|\rho_{\ell}|\leqslant 1.

Now, we can estimate the remainder (
2.5
). We obtain

(2.14)

|\mathcal{R}|\leqslant R(\Delta^{2})+2(\log\Delta)R(z\Delta^{2}).

Next, we proceed to an estimation of our main term
W
.
For our choice of
y_{d}
given by (
2.11
) we find that

(2.15)

y_{p}-y_{pd}=H^{-1}\min\bigl(\log p,\log\frac{\Delta}{d}\bigr)

and the formula (
2.8
) becomes

(2.16)

H^{2}W=K(\Delta)-\sum_{p<z}g(p)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<\Delta\\
(d,p)=1\end{subarray}}h(d)\bigl\{\min\bigl(\log p,\log\frac{\Delta}{d}\bigr)\bigr\}^{2}

where, for any
u\leqslant\Delta

(2.17)

K(u)=\sum_{d\leqslant u}h(d)\bigl(\log\frac{\Delta}{d}\bigr)^{2}.

Note that, abbreviating
K=K(\Delta)
, we have
H^{2}\leqslant JK

by Cauchy’s inequality.

Having in mind that
g(p)
is unstable at small primes we split the
range of
p
in (
2.16
) into two segments
p\leqslant w
and
w<p<z

with some
2\leqslant w<z
at our disposal. Then we estimate
\min(..,..)

by
\log p
and
\log(\Delta/d)
, respectively. We get

(2.18)

H^{2}W\geqslant K(\Delta)\bigl(1-\sum_{w<p<z}g(p)\bigr)-\sum_{p\leqslant w}g(p)(\log p)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d<\Delta\\
(d,p)=1\end{subarray}}h(d).

We estimate the contribution of
p\leqslant w
,
d\leqslant w
,
(d,p)=1
as follows
(note that
g(p)h(d)\leqslant h(pd)
).

\mathop{\sum\!\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,d\leqslant w\\
(p,d)=1\end{subarray}}h(pd)(\log p)^{2}\leqslant(\log w)\sum_{d\leqslant w^{2}}h(d)\log d.

If
\Delta\geqslant w^{3}
we have

(\log w)(\log d)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(3\alpha\log\Delta/d)^{2}
, where

(2.19)

\alpha=(\log w)/\log\Delta.

Hence, this contribution is bounded by
\frac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}K(w^{2})
where, as
in (
2.17
),

(2.20)

K(w^{2})=\sum_{d\leqslant w^{2}}h(d)(\log\Delta/d)^{2}.

We drop the condition
(d,p)=1
in the remaining sum

(2.21)

J(w,\Delta)=\sum_{w<d\leqslant\Delta}h(d).

Introducing the above estimates into (
2.18
), we obtain

LEMMA 2.1
.

Let
z>w\geqslant 2
and
\Delta\geqslant w^{3}
. We have

(2.22)

H^{2}W\geqslant\bigl(1-\sum_{w<p\leqslant z}g(p)\bigr)K-\tfrac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}K(w^{2})-J(w,\Delta)G(w)

where

(2.23)

G(w)=\sum_{p\leqslant w}g(p)(\log p)^{2}.

3.
Two Assumptions

So far, the inequality (
2.22
) holds without severe restrictions
on the density functions
g(d)
,
h(d)
. To proceed further we accept two
assumptions.

Assumption 1.
If
w
is larger than some absolute constant, then

(3.1)

G(w)\leqslant\tfrac{3}{2}(\log w)^{2}.

Note that if
g(p)p
fluctuates in the interval
[0,2]
, then

G(w)\leqslant\sum_{p\leqslant w}\tfrac{2}{p}(\log p)^{2}=(\log w)^{2}+O(\log w),

so in this case we need
w
to be sufficiently large to make the error
term
O(\log w)
strictly smaller than
\frac{1}{2}(\log w)^{2}
.

Let
K(w^{2},\Delta)=K(\Delta)-K(w^{2})
denote the part of (
2.17
)
that is complementary to (
2.20
);

(3.2)

K(w^{2},\Delta)=\sum_{w^{2}<d\leqslant\Delta}h(d)(\log\Delta/d)^{2}.

Assumption 2.
For
\Delta\geqslant w^{3}
we have

(3.3)

J(w,\Delta)(\log\Delta)^{2}\leqslant 3K(w^{2},\Delta).

We shall illustrate how to verify (
3.3
) in special circumstances.
But first we enjoy using both assumptions. By (
3.1
)
and (
3.3
) we have

(3.4)

J(w,\Delta)G(w)\leqslant\tfrac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}K(w^{2},\Delta).

Recall that
\alpha=\log w/\log\Delta\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}
. Hence Lemma 2.1 yields

(3.5)

H^{2}W\geqslant\nu K

where

(3.6)

\nu=1-\sum_{w<p\leqslant z}g(p)-\tfrac{9}{2}\alpha^{2}.

This lower bound is only interesting if
\nu
is positive. Then
W
is
positive and we can apply the inequality
H^{2}\leqslant JK
, getting

THEOREM 1
.

Let
\Delta\geqslant w^{3}
and
g(d)
be such that (
3.1
)
and (
3.3
) hold. If
z>w
and
\nu>0
, then

(3.7)

JW\geqslant\nu.

Remarks
One is unlikely to obtain
\nu>0
in normal situations.
However, this can happen in exceptional circumstances, as for
g(p)
given
by (
1.14
) with an excepional real character
\chi(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)
. These
notes were designed mainly to handle such exceptional cases.

4.
Verification of Assumption 2

Although
h(p)p
is unpredictable at small primes,
h(d)d
can be quite
regular for large
d
in the sense that

(4.1)

\sum_{d\leqslant x}h(d)d=cx+O\bigl(q^{\frac{1}{4}}x^{\frac{3}{4}}\bigr)

holds for every
x\geqslant 2
with some constants
c>0
,
q\geqslant 2
and an
absolute implied constant in the error term. Having this formula, we can
establish (
3.3
) by asymptotic evaluation of both sides.

Recall that
\alpha=\log w/\log\Delta\leqslant\tfrac{1}{3}
. On the left side we get

\displaystyle J(w,\Delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{w}^{\Delta}x^{-1}d\bigl(cx+O(q^{\frac{1}{4}}x^{\frac{3}{4}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=c\log\tfrac{\Delta}{w}+O(q^{\frac{1}{4}}w^{-\frac{1}{4}})=(1-\alpha)c\bigl(\log\Delta+O(1)\bigr)

provided that
w\gg qc^{-4}
. On the right side we get

\displaystyle K(w^{2},\Delta)

\displaystyle=\int_{w^{2}}^{\Delta}x^{-1}\bigl(\log\tfrac{\Delta}{x}\bigr)^{2}d\bigl(cx+O(q^{\frac{1}{4}}x^{\frac{3}{4}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{c}{3}\bigl((\log\Delta)^{3}-(\log w^{2})^{3}\bigr)+O\bigl(q^{\frac{1}{4}}w^{-\frac{1}{4}}(\log\Delta)^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{c}{3}(1-8\alpha^{3})(\log\Delta)^{2}\bigl(\log\Delta+O(1)\bigr).

Hence, the ratio of the right side to the left side of (
3.3
) is

\tfrac{1-8\alpha^{3}}{1-\alpha}+O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\log\Delta}\bigr)\geqslant 1+\tfrac{\alpha}{6}+O\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\log\Delta}\bigr)>1

provided that
w
is sufficiently large to compensate for
the error term
O(1/\log\Delta)
. This proves (
3.3
) if

\Delta\geqslant w^{3}
,
w\gg qc^{-4}
.

Example
Let
g(p)p
be given by (
1.14
). Then

(4.2)

h(p)p=\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{p}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(1-\tfrac{\chi(p)}{p}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(1+\chi(p)(1-\tfrac{1}{p})\bigr)

if
p\nmid q
and
h(p)=0
if
p|q
.
Hence, the series

D(s)=\sum_{d}h(d)d^{1-s}=\prod_{p}\bigl(1+h(p)p^{1-s}\bigr)=\zeta(s)L(s,\chi)E(s)

has analytic continuation to
{\rm Re}s>\tfrac{1}{2}
. The local factors

\displaystyle E_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-p^{-s}\bigr)\bigl(1-\chi(p)p^{-s}\bigr)\bigl(1+h(p)p^{1-s}\bigr)

\displaystyle=1+a_{1}p^{-s-1}+a_{2}p^{-2s}+a_{3}p^{-3s}

have
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
bounded. Hence (
4.1
) follows by standard
contour integration with the constant

c=\mathop{\rm{res}}_{s=1}D(s)=L(1,\chi)E(1).

We have

1+h(p)=\bigl(1-g(p)\bigr)^{-1}=\bigl(1-\tfrac{1}{p}\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(1-\tfrac{\chi(p)}{p}\bigr)^{-1}

if
p\nmid q
and
1
if
p|q
. Hence
E(1)=\varphi(q)/q
so the residue is

(4.3)

c=L(1,\chi)\varphi(q)/q\gg\varphi(q)q^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(\log q)^{-2},

by the Dirichlet class number formula. Therefore, we have proved
that (
3.3
) holds as long as

(4.4)

\Delta\geqslant w^{3},\quad w\geqslant q^{3},

and
q
is sufficiently large.

5.
Exceptional Primes

We are going to apply the theorem to estimate the sum of
a_{p}
over primes

p\leqslant x
. In the context of these notes we say that the density function

g(d)
is “exceptional” if

(5.1)

\delta(w,z)=\sum_{w<p\leqslant z}g(p)

is bounded, smaller than
1
. Specifically, we take
\Delta=w^{3}
,

z=\sqrt{x}\geqslant w^{3}
and we make the following

Assumption 3.

(5.2)

\delta=(w,\sqrt{x})\leqslant\tfrac{1}{4}.

We then have

(5.3)

\nu=1-\delta(w,\sqrt{x})-\tfrac{1}{2}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{4}.

so that (
3.7
) implies
W\geqslant\tfrac{1}{4}V
, where

(5.4)

V=\prod_{p<w^{3}}\bigl(1-g(p)\bigr).

If
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})
is supported on
\sqrt{x}<n\leqslant x
, then

(5.5)

\sum_{p}a_{p}=S(\mathcal{A},z)\geqslant S^{-}(\mathcal{A},z)=XW+\mathcal{R}

where the remainder
\mathcal{R}
is bounded by (see (
1.5
) and (
2.14
))

(5.6)

\mathcal{R}\ll R\log x=(\log x)\sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{d<\sqrt{x}w^{6}}\tau_{3}(d)|r_{d}|.

Hence we conclude the following

PROPOSITION 5.1
.

If
g
is exceptional, that is (
5.2
) holds, then

(5.7)

\sum_{\sqrt{x}<p\leqslant x}a_{p}\geqslant\tfrac{1}{4}XV-R\log x.

Recall that we work under Assumptions 1, 2, 3. The first two assumptions
are verified in the case of
g(p)
given by (
1.14
). The required
conditions are those in (
4.4
). We choose

(5.8)

w=q^{3}.

We obtain

V=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<w^{3}\\
p\nmid q\end{subarray}}\bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigr)\bigl(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p}\bigr)>\tfrac{1}{21}V(q)\frac{q}{\varphi(q)}

where

(5.9)

V(q)=\prod_{p<q^{2}}\bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\bigr)\bigl(1-\frac{\chi(p)}{p}\bigr).

6.
The Exceptional Character

Let
\lambda=1*\chi
, so
\lambda(p)=1+\chi(p)
. We
have proved in (24.20) of [Opera] that

(6.1)

\sum_{q^{3}<p\leqslant\sqrt{x}}\lambda(p)p^{-1}<(1-\beta)\log x+O(q^{-3/4})

if
x\geqslant q^{6}
, where
\beta
is any real zero of
L(s,\chi)
and the
implied constant is absolute. Note that

(6.2)

1-\beta\gg q^{-1/2}(\log q)^{-2},

so the error term in the inequality (
6.1
) is negligible.
Now, our exceptional condition (
5.2
) holds in the segment

(6.3)

q^{6}\leqslant x\leqslant e^{1/4(1-\beta)}

which is non-empty if

(6.4)

(1-\beta)\log q\leqslant\tfrac{1}{24}.

Therefore, we shall say that the real primitive character
\chi
is
exceptional if
L(s,\chi)
has a real zero
\beta
satisfying (
6.4
).
If also
\chi(a)=1
its effect pulls in an unfavourable direction.

Under this exceptional situation we are able to estimate the least prime
in an arithmetic progression,

(6.5)

p\equiv a(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)\quad{\rm with}\,\,\,\chi(a)=1.

To this end, consider the sequence

(6.6)

a_{n}=\lambda(n)

for
n\equiv a(\mathop{\rm{mod}}q)
,
1\leqslant n\leqslant x
. We set
a_{n}=0
otherwise. We have
shown in Section 24.2 of [Opera] that
\mathcal{A}
has our sieve properties
with the density function (
1.14
) and

(6.7)

X=2L(1,\chi)xq^{-1}.

The individual error terms satisfy

(6.8)

r_{d}\ll\tau_{3}(d)\sqrt{x/d}

if
d\leqslant x
, where the implied constant is absolute. Hence, our
remainder (
5.6
) satisfies

(6.9)

R\log x\ll w^{3}x^{\tfrac{3}{4}}(\log x)^{10}.

We want this bound to be insignificant by comparison with the main term

(6.10)

\tfrac{1}{4}XV\gg xq^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(\log q)^{-2}.

This is the case if
x\geqslant q^{43}
. We obtain

COROLLARY 6.1
.

Suppose
L(s,\chi)
has a real zero
\beta
with

(6.11)

(1-\beta)\log q\leqslant\tfrac{1}{172}.

Let
\chi(a)=1
. Then, for
q^{43}\leqslant x\leqslant e^{1/4(1-\beta)}
, we have

(6.12)

\pi(x;q,a)\geqslant L(1,\chi)\frac{V(q)}{\varphi(q)}\frac{x}{168},

where
V(q)
is the product (
5.9
).
