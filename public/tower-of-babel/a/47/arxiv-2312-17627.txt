Sociohydrodynamics: data-driven modelling of social behavior
Daniel S. Seara, Jonathan Colen, Michel Fruchart, Yael Avni, David Martin, Vincenzo Vitelli

Abstract
Living systems display complex behaviors driven by physical forces as well as decision-making. Hydrodynamic theories hold promise for simplified universal descriptions of socially-generated collective behaviors. However, the construction of such theories is often divorced from the data they should describe. Here, we develop and apply a data-driven pipeline that links micromotives to macrobehavior by augmenting hydrodynamics with individual preferences that guide motion. We illustrate this pipeline on a case study of residential dynamics in the United States, for which census and sociological data is available. Guided by Census data, sociological surveys, and neural network analysis, we systematically assess standard hydrodynamic assumptions to construct a sociohydrodynamic model. Solving our simple hydrodynamic model, calibrated using statistical inference, qualitatively captures key features of residential dynamics at the level of individual US counties. We highlight that a social memory, akin to hysteresis in magnets, emerges in the segregation-integration transition even with memory-less agents. This suggests an explanation for the phenomenon of neighborhood tipping, whereby a small change in a neighborhood’s population leads to a rapid demographic shift. Beyond residential segregation, our work paves the way for systematic investigations of decision-guided motility in real space, from micro-organisms to humans, as well as fitness-mediated motion in more abstract genomic spaces.

I
The sociohydrodynamic pipeline

Figure
1
summarizes our sociohydrodynamic pipeline.
First, we identify candidate hydrodynamic variables that evolve slowly both in time and space (Fig.
1
A).
To be useful, these variables must contain enough information to forecast their own future values – they must be
self-predictive
.
To test this self-predictability in a model-agnostic way, we train a neural network to forecast the evolution of the candidate hydrodynamic variables and examine whether the forecasting is possible.
Examining further the trained neural network allows us to assess whether the dynamics are local, i.e. whether hydrodynamic variables are only instantaneously influenced by their immediate surroundings (Fig.
1
B).
This locality, when it holds, considerably simplifies the models we have to consider next.
We then construct an analytical, phenomenological model that relates micromotives to macrobehavior using a combination of physics and economic theory (Fig.
1
C).
Finally, we apply this model to predict real data by first inferring the equation’s coefficients and then checking their numerical solution against experimentally measured trajectories (Fig.
1
D).

In the sections below, we detail how we apply this pipeline to the case of human residential dynamics.
There, we make precise the variables we use, how we check predictability and locality, and the specific model we build to describe the data.

I.1
Identifying hydrodynamic variables in social behavior

Before building a hydrodynamic theory, we must first find suitable hydrodynamic variables.
When the collective variables are not easy to guess, data-driven techniques can be used to propose candidates
Schmitt et al. 2023a
;
Schmitt et al. 2023b
;
Lefebvre et al. 2023
.
In our example of residential dynamics, a reasonable guess for collective variables are the populations of different groups.
Here, we focus on non-Hispanic White and non-Hispanic Black residents.
Data from the decennial US Census
Manson et al. 2022
gives us access to population densities of each group
a
at position
\mathbf{r}
and time
t
,
\rho^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)
.
From these densities, we define a proxy for the local housing capacity
h(\mathbf{r})
by finding the highest density of people who have lived at each location within a county over time.
In this work, we take fill fractions as our hydrodynamic variables, defined as the population densities normalized by the maximum housing availability in the county –
\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)=\rho^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)/\mathrm{max}(h)
(Methods).

In order to apply the usual tools of hydrodynamics, we must check that the collective variables are slowly varying both in space and time, compared to the space and time scales we are able to resolve.
Qualitatively, we observe that the main features of the population distributions appear unaffected by spatial smoothing over the length-scale of single counties.
Quantitatively, fill fractions are correlated over distances four to seven times larger than the typical census tract
\ell
in the region (Methods, Fig.
6
A,B).
In the following analysis, we smooth the fill fractions using a Gaussian filter with a kernel width of 3 km (SI Fig. 1).

We also require hydrodynamic variables to vary slowly in time.
We assess the temporal change in populations via measurements of segregation using the entropy index, which measures how local demographic distribution differs from the global composition
Duncan and Duncan 1955
;
Reardon and O’Sullivan 2004
;
Roberto 2015
(Methods).
Qualitatively, maps of the entropy index appear similar over a span of 40 years.
Quantitatively, its overall magnitude decreases slowly between the years of 1980-2020, as measured previously
Hwang and McDaniel 2022
(Fig.
6
C,D).

Together, these two results indicate that human residential dynamics may indeed be described by hydrodynamic variables that evolve slowly over the scale of individual counties and over a time-scale of decades.

I.2.1
Self-predictability

We next test how well the candidate hydrodynamic variables
\vec{\phi}
identified in the previous section can predict their own future states (Fig.
1
B).
Formally, we check whether
\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)
depends only on
\vec{\phi}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}},t)
(for all
\mathbf{r^{\prime}}
).
Testing the self-predictability of collective variables requires a reliable rule for dynamics, which we do not have
a priori
.
To resolve this, we train a neural network on US Census data to map the fields
\phi^{a}
to the time derivatives of the fields
\partial_{t}\phi^{a}
(Methods).
Our dynamical system

{\partial}_{t}\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)=f_{\mathchoice{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}}^{a}\left[\vec{\phi};\,h\right](\mathbf{r}),

(1)

uses a machine-learned dynamical rule
\vec{f}_{\mathchoice{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}}
for
\vec{\phi}
that depends on both
\vec{\phi}
and the local housing approximation

introduced earlier (Fig.
2

The predicted population dynamics, obtained by integrating (

Fig.

Similar results are seen for both White and Black populations in 3 additional counties dispersed throughout the US (SI Fig. 2).
In the SI, we also quantify the prediction accuracy and show that the neural network out-performs several alternative dynamical rules, including no dynamics, linear growth, and exponential growth (SI Fig. 3).

I.2.2
Locality

Then, we evaluate whether the dynamics are generated locally (Fig.
1
B).
Locality allows us to approximate
\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)
as a function of
\vec{\phi}
and its gradients,
f^{a}[\vec{\phi}]=f^{a}(\vec{\phi},\nabla\vec{\phi},...)
evaluated at
\mathbf{r}
.
To evaluate locality, we consider the saliency

\mathcal{K}^{ab}\left(r\right)=\bigg\langle\dfrac{\partial f^{a}_{\mathchoice{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}}(\mathbf{r})}{\partial\phi^{b}(\mathbf{r^{\prime}})}\bigg\rangle_{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r^{\prime}}|=r}

(2)

of the machine-learned dynamics.
In essence,
\mathcal{K}^{ab}(r)
measures how strongly the predicted dynamics of
\phi^{a}
at a point
\mathbf{r}
depends on the value of the field
\phi^{b}
at another point a distance
r
away (Fig.
2
C, see Methods)
Simonyan et al. 2014
;
Schmitt et al. 2023a
. A
\mathcal{K}^{ab}(r)
that decays rapidly to zero indicates that
f_{{\mathchoice{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}{\includegraphics[height]{figures/NN.jpg}}}}
uses information within a very narrow region to generate its dynamics.
Note that correlation functions would not be sufficient to assess whether the dynamics is local as they do not directly address the evolution rules of the system.

Indeed, the trained neural network identifies a local rule (Fig.
2

\mathcal{K}^{ab}
exhibits a similar structure for all predicted regions and is narrowly peaked at
r<5\ell
, where
\ell
is the typical size of a census tract within that county (Fig.
2

Furthermore, the cross-saliencies (
a\neq b
) may echo the preferences underlying residential decision-making.
Below, we codify these preferences in
“utility functions”
.
We find that saliencies extracted from a neural network trained on agent-based simulations based on the Schelling model
Schelling 1971
support this claim.
Namely, the sign of the saliencies at
r=0

Microscopically, nonlocality can both arise from the fact that agents are influenced by what happens away from them and from the fact that they can move far away.
However, microscopic sources of nonlocality do not necessarily lead to a nonlocal effective description.
For example, a variant of the Ising model with local interactions but infinite-range displacements
Conti et al. 2002
is described by a hydrodynamic theory with local motility at a sufficiently coarse-grained level (SI Fig. 5).
Indeed, human residential dynamics feature non-local displacements at the individual level: according to the US Census Bureau, 53% of moves were within the same county in 2022, down from 64% in 2012
US Census
Bureau 2023a
.

I.3
Constructing a hydrodynamic theory

Using the insights and elements extracted from data in the initial steps of our pipeline, we now develop a hydrodynamic model designed to capture universal features of residential dynamics found across US cities.

I.3.1
General theory

Having identified the collective variables
\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)
and verified that they are predictive of their own future, we write a general equation of motion for these variables

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}^{a}+S^{a}

(3a)

in which we have separated the dynamics into two parts: the divergence of a flux
\mathbf{J}^{a}
that redistributes
\phi^{a}
in space, and a source term
S^{a}
that changes the total populations. The self-predictability and locality of the dynamics for
\vec{\phi}
(Fig.
2
) motivates us to write
\mathbf{J}^{a}=\mathbf{J}^{a}(\vec{\phi})
and
S^{a}=S^{a}(\vec{\phi})
.

The source term
S^{a}
in (
3a
) describes how
\phi^{a}
changes locally due to, for example, reproduction or immigration (SI Fig. 6).
In addition to human populations, these processes play a crucial role in contexts such as microbiology and ecology
Fisher 1937
;
Hallatschek et al. 2023
.
Growth can be related to a local evolutionary
“fitness function”
,
f^{a}
, that describes to what extent the environment promotes the growth of a certain group
Crow and Kimura 1970
;
Kimura 1983
;
Tsimring et al. 1996
;
Mustonen and Lässig 2009
.
Although the fitness and utility functions share similarities, they have no reason to be identical
Cremer et al. 2019
.

We write the flux
\mathbf{J}^{a}
using a gradient expansion as

\mathbf{J}^{a}(\vec{\phi})=\phi^{a}\mathbf{v}^{a}-D^{ab}\nabla\phi^{b}+\Gamma^{ab}\nabla^{3}\phi^{b}

(3b)

in which we have assumed isotropy, and where
D^{ab}[\vec{\phi}]
accounts for diffusion of
\phi^{a}
down the gradients of
\phi^{b}
and
\Gamma^{ab}[\vec{\phi}]
acts like a surface tension, penalizing spatial gradients in
\phi^{a}

Cahn and Hilliard 1958
;
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. Higher order terms in the gradients have been neglected.
The first term in (
3b
) describes advection of
\phi^{a}
at a velocity
\mathbf{v}^{a}
.
We assume that the velocity is proportional to the gradient of a
utility function

\pi^{a}
,

\mathbf{v}^{a}\propto\nabla\pi^{a}.

(3c)

The proportionality factor will be determined below based on a microscopic, agent-based model.
The utility function quantifies the preference of an
a
individual for the location
\mathbf{r}
at time
t
,
providing a link between motility and socioeconomical incentives.
The gradient reflects the propensity of individuals to move towards regions they prefer, i.e. up gradients in their utility.
We call this behavior
“utility-taxis”
, in reference to other guided navigation strategies such as chemotaxis
Keller and Segel 1971
;
Adler 1975
;
Berg 1975
or infotaxis
Vergassola et al. 2007
.

I.3.2
Utility functions

The question remains – what is
\pi^{a}
?
Although many socioeconomic and personal factors may contribute
Charles 2003
;
Hwang and McDaniel 2022
, here we focus on the impact of neighborhood demographic preferences in driving residential dynamics.
In other words, we seek a utility function written as

\pi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)=\pi^{a}\left(\vec{\phi}(\mathbf{r},t)\right).

(4)

This is the key feature of sociohydrodynamics: it establishes a feedback loop between the slowly-evolving hydrodynamic variables and the decision-making processes that lead to motility in the first place.

To model these utility functions in the case at hand, we turn to the social science literature measuring neighborhood demographic preferences (Fig.
3
).
Social scientists have found that residential preferences remained consistent across time over a span of 16 years between 1976 and 1992 in the Detroit metropolitan area
Farley et al. 1993
, and across space over several major US metropolitan areas
Clark 2002
(Fig.
3
).
White residents show a monotonic decrease in their preference of neighborhoods with increasing proportion of Black residents.
On the other hand, Black residents show a marked preference for mixed neighborhoods, which remains consistent between the two surveys.
Qualitatively similar results were obtained in other US-based surveys
Farley et al. 1978
;
Clark 1991
;
Farley et al. 1993
;
Clark 2002
;
Clark and Fossett 2008
(SI Fig. 7).
Thus, we assume that each group
a
has a distinct, time-invariant utility function written as a non-linear function of
\vec{\phi}
.

I.3.3
Coarse-graining the Schelling model

To further constrain our equations of motion, we construct and coarse-grain an agent-based model for residential dynamics, based on the Schelling model

Schelling 1971
;
Schelling 1980
;
Card et al. 2008
;
Vinković and Kirman 2006
;
Grauwin et al. 2009
;
Grauwin et al. 2012
;
Zhang 2011
;
Clark and Fossett 2008
;
Fossett 2006
;
Gauvin et al. 2009
;
Zakine et al. 2024
.
The same approach has been independently developed in Refs.
Zakine et al. 2024
;
Garnier-Brun et al. 2024
, resulting in hydrodynamic equations similar to ours.
In short, it models agents on a lattice that randomly move to adjacent sites with a bias towards increasing their utility (SI).
Within a mean-field approximation, coarse-graining this agent-based model leads to (
3a
) with
S^{a}=0
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}^{a}(\vec{\phi})

\displaystyle=\phi^{0}\nabla\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi})

(5a)

\displaystyle D^{ab}(\vec{\phi})

\displaystyle=T^{a}(\phi^{a}+\delta^{ab}\phi^{0})

(5b)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{ab}(\vec{\phi})

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{a}\delta^{ab}\phi^{b}\phi^{0}.

(5c)

In the above,
\phi^{0}=1-\sum_{b}\phi^{b}
is a vacancy fraction that arises because each lattice site has a maximum carrying capacity

, mimicking the availability of housing.
The parameters

and

control the rate of hopping and penalize spatial gradients of

To validate our model, we simulate (
51
) and compare against the observed evolution of human populations in the US (Fig.
4
).
To do so, we use linear regression to infer the coefficients of our model focusing on the same metropolitan areas as used to train the neural network in Sec.
I.2
(SI Fig. 9).

In light of the responses to the social surveys in Fig.
3
, we parameterize
\pi^{a}
as

\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi})=\sum_{b,c}\kappa^{ab}\phi^{b}+\nu^{abc}\phi^{b}\phi^{c}.

(6)

This is a Taylor expansion of a non-constant
\pi^{a}
around
\phi^{a}=0
.
Therefore, we fit 5 coefficients for the utility function of each field
\left(\kappa^{aW},\kappa^{aB},\nu^{aWW},\nu^{aWB},\nu^{aBB}\right)
, as well as the diffusion constant
T^{a}
and the surface tension
\Gamma^{a}
.

The growth term
S^{a}
in (
51
) is fixed by fitting the total population change assuming an exponential growth model,
S^{a}=r^{a}\phi^{a}
.
This simple model captures the roughly exponential growth rates we empirically measure across US counties (SI Fig. 6), although it neglects growth that may occur from immigration outside of the region.
With this growth rate in hand, we fit the mass-conserving portion of (
51

-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}^{a}=\partial_{t}\phi^{a}-S^{a}
.

We then numerically solve (
51
) using the learned coefficients.
We take
\vec{\phi}(\mathbf{r},1980)
as our initial condition and evolve in time until 2020.
As shown in Fig.

For example, we predict the increase of the Black in the southwest of Harris county, as well as a decrease in the White population in the southeaster portion of Los Angeles county (see SI Fig. 3 for results from other counties).

The fitted coefficients are given in Figs.

Across multiple counties, we observed that the fitted diffusion constant of the Black population is larger than that of the White population,

For compatible utilities, meaning

The coexistence regions in Fig.

We note that the region of phase space exhibiting this hysteresis can qualitatively change if utility functions are non-linear (SI. Fig 12).

III
Conclusion and outlook

To sum up, we introduced a data-driven pipeline to construct hydrodynamic descriptions of socially-driven residential motility.
We illustrated this framework on the example of racial segregation in human residential dynamics.
After testing the mathematical assumptions that enter a hydrodynamic theory, we validated our model by comparing its predictions to the evolution of demographic distributions in the US Census.
We observed that our sociohydrodynamic model is sufficient to capture certain trends of residential dynamics in the US, even though our model neglects many important personal, sociological, and geographical aspects of residential choices.
We showed that segregation can persist even in the absence of external influence due to an emergent memory in the population of memoryless agents.

More broadly, our work illustrates how to incorporate individual choices in a hydrodynamic theory to provide a precise mathematical mapping from micromotives to macrobehavior
Schelling 1980
.
Beyond humans, it could be applied to motile micro-organisms
Kerr et al. 2002
;
Velicer 2003
;
Hibbing et al. 2009
;
Kelsic et al. 2015
;
Van Dyken et al. 2013
;
Ratzke et al. 2020
;
Miller and Bassler 2001
;
Waters and Bassler 2005
evolving
Mills et al. 1967
;
Smith 1985
;
Chen and Arnold 1991
or adapting
Balaban et al. 2004
;
Kussell and Leibler 2005
;
Murugan et al. 2021
in spatially extended time-varying environments.
Further, our method may also be applied to motion through abstract rather than physical spaces, such as the chase and escape dynamics in antigenic space during hosts and pathogens coevolution
Marchi et al. 2021
;
Lässig et al. 2017
or cell fate decisions during development
Turing, Alan 1952
;
Gilmour et al. 2017
;
Lefebvre et al. 2023
.

IV.2
Hydrodynamic variables

We use US Census data at the census tract level to find population densities of each group
a\in\{W,B\}
at position
\mathbf{r}
at time
t
, denoted as
\rho^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)
.
We approximate the housing availability as
h(\mathbf{r})=\underset{t}{\mathrm{max}}\left(\sum_{a}\rho^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)\right)
.
Finally, we define the fill fraction as
\phi^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)=\rho^{a}(\mathbf{r},t)/\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}=\underset{\mathbf{r}}{\mathrm{max}}\left(h(\mathbf{r})\right)
acts as a carrying capacity.
One could consider fill fractions that are normalized by a spatiotemporal capacity to capture how the dynamics of housing availability impacts residential dynamics
Becharat et al. 2024
.
This is beyond the scope of this work.

IV.3
Measuring segregation

We define the local demographic distribution of census tract
i
using the probability vector
\mathbf{p}_{i}
, whose
m^{\mathrm{th}}
element gives the proportion of the population at location
i
belonging to group
m
.
Similarly, we define the demographic distribution of the county using the probability vector
\mathbf{p}
, whose
m^{\mathrm{th}}
element gives the proportion of the county population belonging to group
m
.
We measure the local segregation using the entropy index
Reardon and O’Sullivan 2004
,defined as

h_{i}=\dfrac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}||\mathbf{p}\right)}{H\left(\mathbf{p}\right)}\geq 0

(8)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathbf{p}_{i}||\mathbf{p}\right)
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence and
H(\mathbf{p})
is the Shannon entropy. The condition for perfect integration is
h_{i}=0
, which occurs if a census tract’s demographic distribution matches that of the county,
\mathbf{p}_{i}=\mathbf{p}
,
not
when demographic groups are present in equal numbers.
In order to quantify the segregation present in both Census data and simulations, we utilize the entropy index, also called a divergence index
Roberto 2015
, which measures how distinct the demographic distribution in a local area (e.g. a census tract) is from the demographic distribution of the entire region (e.g. a county).

Let
i~\in~[1,2,\ldots,N]
index local areas and
m~\in~[1,2,\ldots,M]
index demographic groups.
We define
p_{i}^{m}
to be the proportion of the population in the local area
i
that is composed of individuals from group
m
, and thereby define the probability vector
\mathbf{P}_{i}~=~\left(p_{i}^{1},p_{i}^{2},\ldots\right)
.
Similarly, we define
p^{m}
to be the proportion of the population in the entire region that is composed of individuals from group
m
, and thereby define the probability vector
\mathbf{P}~=~\left(p^{1},p^{2},\ldots\right)
.

With these two quantities, we construct the local measure of segregation at location
i
,
h_{i}
, that is shown in Fig.
6
C,

h_{i}=\dfrac{\sum\limits_{m}p_{i}^{m}\ln\left(\dfrac{p_{i}^{m}}{p^{m}}\right)}{-\sum\limits_{m}p^{m}\ln p^{m}}=\dfrac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathbf{P}_{i}||\mathbf{P}\right)}{H\left(\mathbf{P}\right)}

(9a)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\mathbf{P}_{i}||\mathbf{P})\geq 0
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between
\mathbf{P}_{i}
and
\mathbf{P}
, and
H(\mathbf{P})
is the Shannon entropy of
\mathbf{P}
.

We define a single scalar measure of a region’s segregation, the entropy index,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H}}
, using a weighted mean of
h_{i}
. Specifically, we weight
h_{i}
by the fraction of the total population that lives in local area
i
,
f_{i}
, giving

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{H}}=\sum_{i}f_{i}h_{i}.

(9b)

IV.4
Sociohydrodynamic diffusion matrix

We provide the full derivation of our sociohydrodynamic theory starting from an agent-based model in the SI, including the effects of altruism. Here, we simply report the diffusion matrix in (
51
). Assuming that the utility does not have an explicit spatial dependence, i.e.
\pi^{a}=\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi})
, we have

\displaystyle J^{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{b}D^{ab}\partial_{x}\phi^{b}+\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\Gamma^{ab}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{b}

(10)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{b}\mathsf{D}^{ab}\partial_{x}\phi^{b}+\Gamma^{ab}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{b}

(11)

where
\phi^{0}=1-\sum_{b}\phi^{b}
is the vacancy fraction and
\mathsf{D}^{ab}
are elements of the matrix

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}(\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle-T\begin{pmatrix}1-\phi^{Y}&\phi^{X}\\
\phi^{Y}&1-\phi^{X}\end{pmatrix}+\phi^{0}\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{X}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}}{\partial\phi^{X}}&\phi^{X}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}}{\partial\phi^{Y}}\\
\phi^{Y}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}}{\partial\phi^{X}}&\phi^{Y}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}}{\partial\phi^{Y}}\end{pmatrix}

(12)

The first term arises due to volume exclusion effects with finite carrying capacity of the lattice sites in the agent-based model. The second term arises from the utility functions.
Similarly, we find

\Gamma^{ab}=\delta^{ab}\phi^{b}\phi^{0},

(13)

where
\delta^{ab}
is the Kronecker delta.

III.4
Saliency of a non-local Ising model

Figure 5:

Coarse-graining non-local motion leads to local dynamics.

We implement a 2D Ising model whose dynamics are given by exchanging spins. Any two spins in the lattice can be exchanged, but the energy is computed locally.
The resulting dynamics are then smoothied, and used inputs into a convolutional neural network to predict the dynamics and compute a saliency.
The result of the saliency is shown on the right side of the text, and shows that the NN function is highly localized around
r=0
.

What would the saliency give if the underlying dynamics exhibit non-local displacements?
A simple model for this is a 2D Ising model whose dynamics involve swapping any two arbitrarily chosen spins, i.e. Kawasaki dynamics with infinite range exchanges
Conti et al. 2002
.
More precisely, consider a set of spins
\mathbf{s}
on a 2D square lattice. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by

H(\mathbf{s})=-\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}Js_{i}s_{j},

where the coupling strength
J>0
and
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}
indicates a sum over nearest neighbor pairs of spins.
The dynamics are generated by swapping any two spins
i
and
j
. If
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{i},\ldots,s_{j},\ldots)
, we define the swapped state
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{j},\ldots,s_{i},\ldots)
.
The system is evolved using Glauber dynamics, where the probability of accepting a proposed spin swap is given by

w(\mathbf{s}^{\prime}|\mathbf{s})=\dfrac{1}{1+\exp[\beta(H(\mathbf{s}^{\prime})-H(\mathbf{s}))]},

where
\beta
is an inverse temperature.
Thus, this system has local interactions, and long-range displacements (Fig.
5
)

Simulating this system gives us a trajectory
\mathbf{s}(t)
.
We smooth the spin field with a Gaussian kernel and use the smoothed fields as an input into the same neural network architecture used to predict the dynamics of the Census data.
We then measure the saliency of the trained system, which is shown on the right-hand side of Fig.
5
.
We see that the neural network learns a highly localized dynamical rule, despite the infinite-range motility of the underlying spins.

These results show that a coarse-grained field, even with long-range mobility, can evolve according to a local set of dynamics.
This analysis further supports our use of a local PDE when simulations residential dynamics whose microscopic origins are surely non-local.

IV
The sociohydrodynamic equation

Our sociohydrodynamic equation is

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}=\nabla\cdot\left(D^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\nabla\phi^{b}-\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\nabla\pi^{a}-\Gamma^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\nabla^{3}\phi^{b}\right)+S^{a}(\vec{\phi})

(8)

This is an equation for advection-diffusion plus growth, where the advection velocity is given by
\mathbf{v}^{a}=\phi^{0}\nabla\pi^{a}
and we require
\Gamma^{ab}>0
for stability.
In the sections below, we discuss the phenomenological origins of this model, in particular for the forms of the growth
S^{a}
and utility functions
\pi^{a}
considered in the main text.
We also discuss the origin of the
\nabla^{3}
term as a necessary, stabilizing term.
Finally, we describe a microscopic, agent-based model that we explicitly coarse-grain to obtain (
8
), including all prefactors of the vacancy
\phi^{0}
.

Our census data, once smoothed, does not exhibit fast variations at small length-scales.
Thus, (
8
) should be linearly stable at small wavelengths (high wavenumber,

).
Diffusion and (local) advection alone are insufficient to ensure this.
A natural way to regain stability at high-

Below, we will show that this comes at the expense of a more complicated theory.
The
\nabla^{3}
term qualitatively mimics the effect of non-local fitness functions while maintaining analytical simplicity, and is therefore added to the model as a phenomenological term.

To illustrate, we work with a simplified version of our theory in the case of only one group, denoted by the fill fraction
\phi(x,t)\in[0,1]
. We introduce the spatially smoothed fill fraction

\varphi(x,t)=(K_{\sigma}*\phi)(x,t)=\int dyK_{\sigma}(x-y)\phi(y,t)

(9)

where
K_{\sigma}(x)
is a normalized convolution kernel in

that is non-increasing in
|x|
with a characteristic width
\sigma

to be an even function,
K_{\sigma}(-x)=K_{\sigma}(x)

Examples are Gaussian, decaying exponentials, or top hat functions (see Fig.

). The model presented in the main text and SM considered the special case

.

The non-local interactions manifest by the utility functions becoming functions of
\varphi
(equivalently, functionals of
\phi
),
\pi=\pi(\varphi(x,t))
. In this case, we can write the sociohydrodynamic equations as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot j

(10a)

\displaystyle j(x,t)

\displaystyle=-D\nabla\phi+\phi\phi^{0}\nabla\pi(\varphi)

(10b)

where the diffusion constant
D>0
and vacancy fraction
\phi^{0}=1-\phi
. Linearizing around a uniform solution,
\phi(x)=\phi_{0}+\delta\phi(x)
,

\partial_{t}\delta\phi=-\nabla\cdot\left(-D\nabla\delta\phi+\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}\nabla\delta\varphi\right)\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\Rightarrow}\partial_{t}\delta\phi=s(q)\delta\phi,

(11)

where
\delta\varphi=K_{\sigma}*\delta\phi
,
\pi^{\prime}_{0}=(\partial\pi/\partial\varphi)|_{\varphi=\phi_{0}}
is the derivative of the utility function with respect to smoothed field
\varphi
evaluated at
\phi=\phi_{0}
, and the symbol
\overset{\mathcal{F}}{\Rightarrow}
indicates moving to Fourier space. The stability of the homogeneous solution is given by the eigenfrequency
s(q)
,

s(q)=-q^{2}\left(D-\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}K_{\sigma}(q)\right).

(12)

An instability arises if
D-\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}K_{\sigma}(q)<0
, or more simply if

D-\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}<0.

(13)

This simplification arises due to the fact that we considered
K_{\sigma}(x)
to be normalized and monotonically decreasing and therefore its Fourier transform has a maximum
K_{\sigma}(q=0)=1
. Given
K_{\sigma}(x)\in L^{2}
, we also have
K_{\sigma}(q)\in L^{2}
, meaning
|K_{\sigma}(q)|
decays to zero as

. This means that, even if Eq. (
13

q
, above some

it will no longer be satisfied and the system is stable at short wavelengths (Fig.
8
).

If we Taylor expand
K_{\sigma}(q)
around
q=0

s(q)=-q^{2}\left(D-\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{2n}q^{2n}\right),

(14)

where
c_{n}=K^{(n)}(0)/n!
, and we only have even powers of
q
due to
K_{\sigma}(x)
being an even function in
x
. If we truncate the Taylor series at
q^{2}
, we have

s(q)=-q^{2}\left(D-\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}\right)+q^{4}\left(\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}\pi^{\prime}_{0}c_{2}\right)

(15)

The resulting
q^{4}
term can be seen as the result of a
\nabla^{3}
term in a flux

. This truncation will always stabilize an unstable system at large

, as we will now explain.

If Eq. (
13
) holds, we must have
\pi^{\prime}_{0}>\frac{D}{\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}}>0
, and the
q^{2}
term in Eq. (
15
) becomes unstable (i.e. it has a positive coefficient).
At large
q
, the
q^{4}
term dominates and always has a negative coefficient. As
\phi_{0}\phi^{0}_{0}>0
and we are working in a regime where
\pi^{\prime}_{0}>0
to satisfy Eq. (
13
), we are saved by the fact that
c_{2}=K^{\prime\prime}_{\sigma}(0)/2<0
, because
K_{\sigma}(0)
is a local maximum.

There remains one subtlety. Suppose our linearization is stable because
\pi^{\prime}_{0}<0
, thus violating Eq. (
13
). In this case, our truncation at
\mathcal{O}(q^{2})
actually
introduces
an instability at large
q
, as
\pi^{\prime}_{0}c_{2}>0
. In this case, we would need to truncate at a higher order of
q
, such as
\mathcal{O}(q^{4})
.
We therefore conclude that there is no consistent way to truncate the non-local interaction model to ensure stability for all kernels and fitness functions.
One must keep the fully non-local interactions, making the theory more complicated to study analytically.

In light of this, we can achieve the same stabilizing effect of non-local interactions by introducing a
\nabla^{3}
term in our flux with a fixed sign to ensure stability, in the spirit of Ginzburg-Landau theory.
While quantitatively different from the non-local interactions (see Fig.
8
), the effect is qualitatively similar and does not require the specification of the kernel
K_{\sigma}(x)
when attempting to fit to data.

IV.4
The Schelling model

Here we describe a set of microscopic dynamics that we will find coarse-grains to our conjectured sociohydrodynamic equations.
We note that the ability to write the form of (
8
).
However, having the microscopic model gives more insight into the origin of otherwise phenomenological coefficients, especially the diffusion matrix
D^{ab}
and surface tension
\Gamma^{ab}
.

The set up amounts to a bounded-neighborhood variant of the Schelling model
Schelling 1971
;
Grauwin et al. 2009
;
Zhang 2011
;
Grauwin et al. 2012
;
Bertin 2021
;
Zakine et al. 2024
.
We consider a system composed of two types of particles (agents), labeled by
(X,Y)
, distributed over a periodic lattice with
M
sites and lattice spacing
l
. Each lattice site can contain at most
\mathcal{N}
total particles. At a time
t
, lattice site
i
’s composition is given by the vector
\vec{n}_{i}(t)=(n^{X}_{i}(t),n^{Y}_{i}(t))
giving the number of particles of both types at site
i
. The capacity constraint is
n_{i}^{X}(t)+n_{i}^{Y}(t)\leq\mathcal{N}
for all
i
and all
t
. We use
\underline{n}(t)\equiv\left(\vec{n}_{1}(t),\vec{n}_{2}(t),\ldots,\vec{n}_{M}(t)\right)
to indicate the full configuration of the system. Dividing the components of
\underline{n}(t)
by
\mathcal{N}
, we further define the (discrete for now) density field configuration
\underline{\phi}(t)\equiv\left(\vec{\phi}_{1}(t),\vec{\phi}_{2}(t),\ldots,\vec{\phi}_{M}(t)\right)
where
\vec{\phi}_{i}(t)=(\phi^{X}_{i}(t),\phi^{Y}_{i}(t))
with
\phi_{i}^{m}(t)=n_{i}^{m}(t)/\mathcal{N}
.

IV.4.1
The utility function

An agent of type
a
at lattice site
i
has a measure of the
utility
of the configuration in which it finds itself,
\pi_{i}^{a}(\underline{\phi}(t))
. The utility
\pi_{i}^{a}
generically depends on the configuration and on the lattice site
i
.
While we do not consider utility functions that explicitly depend on the latter in this work, we note that adding this feature may be necessary for a more realistic description of human residential dynamics.

As noted by Schelling himself
Schelling 1971
, any model for residential dynamics that is built only on neighborhood composition preferences ignores at least 2 other factors largely responsible for observed residential patterns – discrimination (e.g. redlining) and socioeconomic inequality (e.g. income inequality).
In either case, the end result is that certain areas in space are more or less accessible to certain groups as imposed by factors other than their own personal preference.
These effects can be at least partially captured by adding an explicit, spatially dependent term to the utility functions which acts like an externally imposed potential function restricting movement.
For example, one could consider
\pi^{a}_{i}(\underline{\phi})=\alpha i+f(\underline{\phi})
, where
\alpha
is a constant that determines the utility of a location
i
, in addition to a term
f(\underline{\phi})
that captures the individuals preferences.
For the rest of this work, however, we only consider utilities that are functions of the system configuration,
\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{i})=\pi^{a}(\phi^{X}_{i},\phi^{Y}_{i})
.
Finally, we note that each individual does not count itself as a neighbor in the evaluation of its utility, e.g. an individual of type
X
calculates its utility as
\pi^{X}(\vec{\phi}_{i})=\pi^{X}(\phi^{X}_{i}-\mathcal{N}^{-1},\phi^{Y}_{i})
.

IV.4.2
The transition rates

An individual of type
b
moves by jumping from site
j
to site
k
. This changes the configuration as

\underline{\phi}\to\underline{\phi}^{\prime}=D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{\phi}\quad\text{such that}\quad(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})^{a}_{i}=\phi_{i}^{a}+\delta^{ab}\left(\delta_{ik}-\delta_{ij}\right)\delta\phi,

(16)

where
\delta\phi=\mathcal{N}^{-1}
. We have defined the
diffusion operator

D^{b}_{k|j}
that acts on the configuration
\underline{\phi}
by removing an individual of type
b
from site
j
and adding it to site
k
.

An individual makes this jump with a probability depending on a gain function,
g^{a}(k|j)
, which measures the variation of its utility after moving from site
j
to site
k
.

The rate we consider is similar to those used in Glauber dynamics of spin lattices
Glauber 1963
, as well as in other quantitative socioeconomic models where it is known as a logit function
Grauwin et al. 2009
;
Bouchaud 2013
,

w^{a}(k|j)=w^{a}(g^{a}(k|j))=\dfrac{\tau^{-1}}{1+\exp(-g^{a}(k|j)/T)},

(17)

where
\tau
gives a time scale for the jumps and
T
is akin to a temperature that quantifies the uncertainty of a move decreasing the gain function.
We note that Eq.
17
is not the only choice possible. Generically, the transition rate should satisfy certain properties, namely
w^{a}(g\to\infty)\to 1
and
w^{a}(g\to-\infty)=0
. The logit transition rate, in addition of satisfying these properties, also obeys detailed balance when
g^{a}(k|j)=G(k)-G(j)
,
i.e.
when the gain can be written as the difference of some function
G
that only depends on the system configuration. If the latter is verified, detailed balance holds and the system will settle into a steady state given by the Boltzmann Distribution of statistical physics,
p(\underline{\phi})\propto e^{G/T}
. This was pointed out for socioeconomic agents in
Grauwin et al. 2009
.

In this work, we take the gain to be given by

g^{a}(k|j)=g^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{k},\vec{\phi}_{j})=(1-\alpha)\left[\pi^{a}\left(\vec{\phi}_{k}^{\prime}\right)-\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{j})\right]+\alpha\left[U[\underline{\phi}^{\prime}]-U[\underline{\phi}]\right]+\Gamma\left[\Sigma[\underline{\phi}^{\prime}]-\Sigma[\underline{\phi}]\right].

(18)

The first term in Eq. (
18
) denotes the change in the moving particle’s own utility before and after the move. The second term denotes the change in the global utility given by the functional
U[\underline{\phi}]
,

U[\underline{\phi}]=\mathcal{N}\sum_{b=X,Y}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\phi^{b}_{j}\pi^{b}(\vec{\phi}_{j}).

(19)

From here, we see that the parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
denotes the relative weight that a particle gives to its own utility with respect to the utility of the entire system. For this reason, we follow
Grauwin et al. 2009
and call
\alpha
an
altruism parameter
. Completely selfish particles (
\alpha=0
) only consider their own utility, while completely altruistic particles (
\alpha=1
) only consider the utility of the global configuration. We note that the main text only considered
\alpha=0
.

The functional
\Sigma[\underline{\phi}]
denotes a contribution to the gain that comes from spatial gradients of the density field,

\Sigma[\underline{\phi}]=\mathcal{N}\sum_{b=X,Y}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\phi_{j}^{b}\left(\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi}\right)^{b}_{j}.

(20)

Here,
\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi}
is a discrete Laplacian operator defined as

(\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi})^{b}_{j}=\dfrac{\phi^{b}_{j+1}-2\phi^{b}_{j}+\phi^{b}_{j-1}}{\ell^{2}}.

(21)

This function indicates that particles like being at a minimum of the density field, where
\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi}>0
. We will see that this term gives the system a surface tension that promotes spatial uniformity in the density field. Intuitively, this comes from the fact that particles are more likely to move into local minima of the density field (
\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi}>0
) and away from local maxima of the density field (
\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi}<0
), thereby reducing local gradients in the density field. This will prove crucial to obtain a well-behaved hydrodynamic theory.

IV.5
Coarse-graining the Schelling model

Here we coarse-grain our agent-based model and show that it leads to the sociohydrodynamic equations in the main text.

The stochastic evolution of the system is described by the probability distribution
P(\underline{n},t)
, which is assumed to obey the master equation

\partial_{t}P(\underline{n},t)=\sum_{\underline{n}^{\prime}}W(\underline{n}|\underline{n}^{\prime})P(\underline{n}^{\prime},t)-W(\underline{n}^{\prime}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t),

(22)

where
W(\underline{n}^{\prime}|\underline{n})
is the transition rate from state
\underline{n}
to state
\underline{n}^{\prime}
and
\sum_{\underline{n}^{\prime}}
is a sum over all system configurations.

The allowed transitions are given by Eq. (
16
), which reduce the sum over configurations to sums over particle types and pairs of sites
jk

\partial_{t}P(\underline{n},t)=\sum_{b=X,Y}\sum_{jk}W(\underline{n}|D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n})P(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n},t)-W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t).

(23)

We can now find the dynamics for the average number of particles of type
a
at lattice site
i
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle n^{a}_{i}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{n}}n_{i}^{a}\partial_{t}P(\underline{n},t)

(24a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{n}}n^{a}_{i}\left(\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}W(\underline{n}|D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n})P(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n},t)-W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t)\right)

(24b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{n}}\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}n^{a}_{i}W(\underline{n}|D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n})P(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n},t)-n^{a}_{i}W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t).

(24c)

We wish to make the right hand side the average over some function of
n^{a}_{i}
, i.e.
\partial_{t}\langle n^{a}_{i}\rangle=\langle f(n^{a}_{i})\rangle=\sum_{\underline{n}}P(\underline{n},t)f(n^{a}_{i})
in order to apply the mean-field approximation,
i.e.

\partial_{t}\langle n_{i}\rangle=f(\langle n_{i}\rangle)
. To this end, we perform a change of variables in the first term of Eq. (
24c
), taking

\underline{n}\to D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n}=\left(D^{b}_{k|j}\right)^{-1}\underline{n}

This change of variables leaves the sum over configurations unchanged as both
D^{b}_{k|j}
and
D^{b}_{j|k}
preserve particle number. This gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle n_{i}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{n}}\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n})^{a}_{i}W(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t)-n_{i}^{a}W(D_{k|j}^{b}\underline{n}|\underline{n})P(\underline{n},t)

(25a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{n}}P(\underline{n},t)\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n})^{a}_{i}W(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n}|\underline{n})-n_{i}^{a}W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})

(25b)

\displaystyle=\left<\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n})^{a}_{i}W(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n}|\underline{n})-n_{i}^{a}W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})\right>.

(25c)

In the following, we take the mean-field approximation and drop the angle brackets.

As written,
W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})
gives the rate for
some
particle of type
b
to move from site
j
to site
k
in the configuration
\underline{n}
. This is not the single particle jump rate given by Eq.
17
,
w^{b}(k|j)
. Rather, the two are related by

W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})=n_{j}^{b}\left(1-\sum_{c}\phi_{k}^{c}\right)w^{b}(k|j)(\delta_{k,j+1}+\delta_{k,j-1}).

(26)

Intuitively, the above formula states that the rate at which a
b
particle moves from site
j
to site
k
is given by the rate at which a single
b
particle makes that transition (
w^{b}(k|j)
) scaled by the number of
b
particles at
j
(
n_{j}^{b}
) and the fraction of vacant sites at
k
(
1-\sum_{c}\phi_{k}^{c})
. This prevents particles from entering full sites. In the following, we denote the total vacancy fraction by

\phi^{0}_{k}=1-\sum_{c}\phi^{c}_{k}.

Finally, we have also restricted motion to neighboring lattice sites, specifying that
k=j\pm 1
with the Kronecker deltas.

Plugging this decomposition of
W(D^{b}_{k|j}\underline{n}|\underline{n})
into the mean-field equation (
25c
) and writing
(D^{b}_{j|k}\underline{n})^{a}_{i}
explicitly, we obtain

\begin{split}\partial_{t}n_{i}^{a}=\sum_{b}\sum_{jk}&\left(n_{i}^{a}+\delta^{ab}(\delta_{ij}-\delta_{ik})\right)n_{k}^{b}\phi^{0}_{j}w^{b}(j|k)\left(\delta_{j,k+1}+\delta_{j,k-1}\right)\\
&-n_{i}^{a}n_{j}^{b}\phi^{0}_{k}w^{b}(k|j)\left(\delta_{k,j+1}+\delta_{k,j-1}\right).\end{split}

(27)

The first and last terms cancel as they are identical up to a relabeling of
j\leftrightarrow k
and are summed over all
j,k
. After summing over
b
in the middle term, we have

\partial_{t}n_{i}^{a}=\sum_{jk}n_{k}^{a}\phi^{0}_{j}w^{a}(j|k)\left(\delta_{ij}-\delta_{ik}\right)\left(\delta_{j,k+1}+\delta_{j,k-1}\right).

(28)

The sum over
jk
is significantly simplified due to the two sets of
\delta
-functions and gives

\partial_{t}n_{i}^{a}=n^{a}_{i-1}\phi^{0}_{i}w^{a}(i|i-1)+n^{a}_{i+1}\phi^{0}_{i}w^{a}(i|i+1)-n_{i}^{a}\left[\phi^{0}_{i-1}w^{a}(i-1|i)+\phi^{0}_{i+1}w^{a}(i+1|i)\right].

(29)

This has the form of a conservation law,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle=J_{i-1}^{a}-J_{i}^{a}

(30)

\displaystyle J_{i}^{a}

\displaystyle=n_{i}^{a}\phi^{0}_{i+1}w^{a}(i+1|i)-n_{i+1}^{a}\phi^{0}_{i}w^{a}(i+1|i)

Moving to continuous space, the lattice site
i
is at position
x=i\ell
, where
\ell
is the lattice spacing. Upon taking
\ell\to 0
and dividing by
\mathcal{N}
, we can approximate the above as

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(x,t)=j^{a}(x-\ell)-j^{a}(x)\approx-\ell\partial_{x}j^{a}(x)

(31)

Now we are left to specify the particle current,

j^{a}(x)=\phi^{a}(x)\phi^{0}(x+\ell)w^{a}(x+\ell|x)-\phi^{a}(x+\ell)\phi^{0}(x)w^{a}(x|x+\ell)

(32)

Performing a Taylor expansion for
\ell\to 0
up to
\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})
gives

\displaystyle j^{a}(x)=

\displaystyle\phi^{a}\left(\phi^{0}+\ell\partial_{x}\phi^{0}\right)\left(w^{a}(x|x)+\ell\partial_{x}\vec{\phi}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{\phi}}^{(1|0)}w^{a}(x|x)\right)-(\phi^{a}+\ell\partial_{x}\phi^{a})\phi^{0}\left(w^{a}(x|x)+\ell\partial_{x}\vec{\phi}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{\phi}}^{(0|1)}w^{a}(x|x)\right)+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2})

(33)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell\left[\left(\phi^{a}\partial_{x}\phi^{0}-\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\phi^{a}\right)w^{a}(x|x)+\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\vec{\phi}\cdot\left(\nabla_{\vec{\phi}}^{(1|0)}w^{a}(x|x)-\nabla_{\vec{\phi}}^{(0|1)}w^{a}(x|x)\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}),

where we have canceled all terms that do not depend on
\ell
. Furthermore, we have used the notation
\partial_{x}\vec{\phi}=\left(\partial_{x}\phi^{X},\partial_{x}\phi^{Y}\right)
,

\nabla_{\vec{\phi}}^{(\mu|\nu)}w^{a}(y|x)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\phi^{X}}^{(\mu|\nu)}w^{a}(y|x)\\
\partial_{\phi^{Y}}^{(\mu|\nu)}w^{a}(y|x)\end{pmatrix}

(34)

and

\partial_{\phi^{b}}^{(\mu|\nu)}w^{a}(y|x)=\dfrac{\partial^{(\mu+\nu)}w^{a}(y|x)}{\partial\phi^{b}(y)^{\mu}\partial\phi^{b}(x)^{\nu}}.

(35)

Finally, we take
f(x|x)=\lim_{y,z\to x}f(y|z)
for any function
f(y|z)
.

We are left to evaluate
w^{a}(x|x)
and its derivatives. We begin with the former, which amounts to calculating
g^{a}(x|x)
. Recalling Eq.
18
, we rewrite it in continuous space variables as

\displaystyle g^{a}(y|x)=(1-\alpha)

\displaystyle\left(\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}(y)+\delta\phi\hat{e}_{a})-\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}(x))\right)

(36)

\displaystyle+\alpha

\displaystyle\left(U[D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]-U[\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]\right)

\displaystyle+\Gamma

\displaystyle\left(\Sigma[D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]-\Sigma[\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]\right),

where we have introduced the notation
\hat{e}_{a}
which is a vector with one in the
a
component, and zero otherwise. This is simply a convenient way to specify that the fraction only changes in the
a
species when considering
g^{a}
. Furthermore, there is a dummy index
x^{\prime}
in the arguments of
U
and
\Sigma
, reflecting the fact that these functionals will be integrated over
x^{\prime}
.

The continuous version of the diffusion operator takes the form

D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})=\phi^{b}(x^{\prime})+\delta\phi\delta^{ab}\left(\delta(x^{\prime}-y)-\delta(x^{\prime}-x)\right)

The second term of the gain is given by

\displaystyle U[D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]-U[\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\sum_{b=X,Y}\int dx^{\prime}\left[\left(D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})\right)\pi^{b}\left(D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})\right)-\phi^{b}(x^{\prime})\pi^{b}(\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime}))\right]

(37)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\sum_{b}\left(\phi^{b}(y)+\delta\phi\delta^{ab}\right)\pi^{b}\left(\vec{\phi}(y)+\delta\phi\hat{e}^{a}\right)-\phi^{b}(y)\pi^{b}(\vec{\phi}(y))

\displaystyle+\left(\phi^{b}(x)-\delta\phi\delta^{ab}\right)\pi^{b}\left(\vec{\phi}(x)-\delta\phi\hat{e}^{a}\right)-\phi^{b}(x)\pi^{b}(\vec{\phi}(x)).

A similar calculation can be done for the third term proportional to
\Gamma
. With great care taken to keep track of all the correct indices, we only report the result,

\displaystyle\Sigma[D^{a}(y|x)\vec{\phi}(x^{\prime})]-\Sigma[\vec{\phi}(x)]

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}(y)-\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}(x)-3\delta\phi.

(38)

Combining the above equations, we arrive at

g^{a}(x|x)\approx\delta\phi\left[(1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{a}}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{(\partial\phi^{a})^{2}}-3\right]

(39)

where the Laplacian terms in
\Sigma
cancelled when
y=x
. In the limit where
\delta\phi\to 0
, this term goes to zero, making

\lim_{\delta\phi\to 0}w^{a}(x|x)=w^{a}(g^{a}=0)=\dfrac{\tau^{-1}}{2}.

(40)

We now turn to computing the derivatives of the rate functions,

\partial_{\phi^{b}}^{(1|0)}w^{a}(y|x)=\dfrac{\partial w^{a}(y|x)}{\partial\phi^{b}(y)}=\dfrac{dw^{a}(g^{a})}{dg^{a}}\dfrac{\partial g^{a}(\vec{\phi}(y),\vec{\phi}(x))}{\partial\phi^{b}(y)}.

(41)

The first factor in the above equation is given by

\dfrac{dw^{a}}{dg^{a}}=\dfrac{\tau^{-1}}{(1+e^{-g^{a}/T})^{2}}\dfrac{e^{-g^{a}/T}}{T},

(42)

and in the limit of vanishing
\delta\phi
it reduces to

\dfrac{dw^{a}}{dg^{a}}\underset{\delta\phi\to 0}{\to}\dfrac{\tau^{-1}}{4T}.

(43)

To close (
41
), we need to compute the derivatives of the gain
g^{a}
with respect to the fields using expression (
36
).
Substiting the final form of (
41
) into (
33
) will then give the expression of the hydrodynamic current.
We first consider the case
\Gamma=0
and will discuss how to include the
\Gamma
-dependent contributions to the current later.
Retaining only the terms proportional to
\alpha
and
(1-\alpha)
in Eq.
36
, we derive them with respect to
\phi^{b}(y)
before taking the limit
\delta\phi\to 0
to obtain

\dfrac{\partial g^{a}(y|x)}{\partial\phi^{b}(y)}\Big|_{y=x}\underset{\delta\phi\to 0}{\to}(1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{a}}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{a}\partial\phi^{b}},

(44)

where
u(x,t)=\phi^{X}\pi^{X}(\vec{\phi}(x))+\phi^{Y}\pi^{Y}(\vec{\phi}(x))
.
Performing a similar computation, we also obtain

\lim_{\delta\phi\to 0}\dfrac{\partial g^{a}(y|x)}{\partial\phi^{b}(x)}\Big|_{y=x}=-\lim_{\delta\phi\to 0}\dfrac{\partial g^{a}(y|x)}{\partial\phi^{b}(y)}\Big|_{y=x}.

(45)

Inserting (
44
) into (
41
), we obtain the derivative of the jump rate as

-\partial_{\phi^{b}}^{(0|1)}w^{a}(x|x)=\partial_{\phi^{b}}^{(1|0)}w^{a}(x|x)=\dfrac{\tau^{-1}}{4T}\left((1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{a}}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{a}\partial\phi^{b}}\right)\;.

(46)

We can now inject (
46
) and (
40
) in expression (
33
) to obtain the particle current
j^{a}_{0}
in the
\Gamma=0
case as

\displaystyle j^{a}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ell\tau^{-1}}{2}\left[(\phi^{a}\partial_{x}\phi^{0}-\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\phi^{a})+T^{-1}\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\left((1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\alpha\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}\right)\right]\;.

(47)

When
\Gamma\neq 0
, additional contributions have to be taken into account in (
47
).
Instead of deriving them by using expression (
41
) together with (
36
), which is not a straightforward derivation due to the presence of both functional and spatial gradients, we will use arguments drawn from equilibrium physics.
First, we note that the term proportional to
\Gamma
in (
36
) is a purely relaxational, equilibrium contribution to the gain. Indeed, it is written as a difference of a single function evaluated before and after the microscopic jump. Having a gain function with this property necessarily gives rise to a Boltzmann-like steady-state distribution with a well-defined potential function
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]
. In the case of the
\Sigma
functional, which penalizes gradients, this potential function is given by
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]=\int dx\ \Gamma^{a}\frac{|\partial_{x}\phi^{a}|^{2}}{2}
. Taking into account volume exclusions, the contribution
j^{a}_{\Gamma}
to the current due to the gradient-penalizing terms is given by the equilibrium formula
j^{a}_{\Gamma}=M^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\partial_{x}\delta F/\delta\phi^{b}
, with a fraction-dependent mobility
M^{ab}(\vec{\phi})=\delta^{ab}\phi^{b}\phi^{0}
. Inserting the expression of
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]
in the latter formula for
j^{a}_{\Gamma}
gives

j^{a}_{\Gamma}=\Gamma^{a}\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}.

(48)

Adding the above contribution to (
47
) gives us the final form of the current as

\displaystyle j^{a}(x)=j^{a}_{0}(x)+j^{a}_{\Gamma}(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ell\tau^{-1}}{2}\left[(\phi^{a}\partial_{x}\phi^{0}-\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\phi^{a})+T^{-1}\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\left((1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\alpha\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\Gamma^{a}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}\right)\right]\;.

(49)

We now make an assumption about the rate of jumping
\tau
: we assume that it follows an Eyring equation
Eyring 1935
with
\tau^{-1}\propto T
.
The latter indicates that reactions happen faster as the temperature increases. In particular, we assume
\tau^{-1}=2T\tau_{0}^{-1}
, where
\tau_{0}
is a time-scale.
In this case, the current becomes

j^{a}(x)=\ell\tau_{0}^{-1}\left[T\left(\phi^{a}\partial_{x}\phi^{0}-\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\phi^{a}\right)+\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\left((1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\Gamma^{a}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}\right)\right].

(50)

Putting (
50
) into (
31
) and setting
\ell=1
, and
\tau_{0}^{-1}=1
, we arrive at our (non-dimensionalized) sociohydrodynamics equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=T\left(\phi^{0}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}-\phi^{a}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{0}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\left[(1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\alpha\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\Gamma^{a}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}\right]\right)

(51)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}\left(\sum_{b}\mathsf{D}^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\partial_{x}\phi^{b}+\Gamma^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}\right)

where
\mathsf{D}^{ab}(\vec{\phi})
are the elements of the density dependent diffusion matrix, written for 2 groups explicitly as

\mathsf{D}(\vec{\phi})=-T\begin{pmatrix}1-\phi^{Y}&\phi^{X}\\
\phi^{Y}&1-\phi^{X}\end{pmatrix}+\phi^{0}\begin{pmatrix}\phi^{X}\left((1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}}{\partial\phi^{X}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{(\partial\phi^{X})^{2}}\right)&\phi^{X}\left((1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}}{\partial\phi^{Y}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{X}\partial\phi^{Y}}\right)\\
\phi^{Y}\left((1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}}{\partial\phi^{X}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{X}\partial\phi^{Y}}\right)&\phi^{Y}\left((1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}}{\partial\phi^{Y}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{(\partial\phi^{Y})^{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(52)

The fourth-order derivative has coefficients
\Gamma^{ab}=\sum_{b}\delta^{ab}\Gamma^{b}\phi^{b}\phi^{0}
, which are components of a diagonal matrix given our assumptions on the underlying dynamics.

Generically, the diffusion matrix can be written as

\mathsf{D}(\vec{\phi})=\begin{pmatrix}D^{XX}&D^{XY}\\
D^{YX}&D^{YY}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}D^{XX}&D_{+}-D_{-}\\
D_{+}+D_{-}&D^{YY}\end{pmatrix}

(53)

In terms of
D_{\pm}
, the off-diagonal terms are given by

D_{\pm}=\dfrac{D^{YX}\pm D^{XY}}{2}=-T\dfrac{\phi^{Y}\pm\phi^{X}}{2}+\dfrac{\phi^{0}}{2}\left[(1-\alpha)\left(\phi^{Y}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}}{\partial\phi^{X}}\pm\phi^{X}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}}{\partial\phi^{Y}}\right)+\alpha(\phi^{Y}\pm\phi^{X})\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{X}\partial\phi^{Y}}\right]

(54)

From (
54
), we see that
D_{-}
measures the breaking of the compatibility condition. This becomes especially clear when and
\alpha=0
, giving

D_{-}=\dfrac{\phi^{0}}{2}\left(\dfrac{\partial(\phi^{Y}\pi^{Y})}{\partial\phi^{X}}-\dfrac{\partial(\phi^{X}\pi^{X})}{\partial\phi^{Y}}\right)

V.2
Solving PDEs on counties

Here, we give further details about the methods used to solve our equations over individual counties.
We create our mesh and solve our equations of motion in Python using
FiPy

Guyer et al. 2009
.

In order to minimize the effects of boundary conditions, we simulate dynamics over a region
1.5\times
the size of each individual county.
We simplify the external boundaries by buffering by 1 km and simplifying the boundary to have line segments of a minimum of 1 km using the
geopandas
functions
buffer
and
simplify
, respectively.
This process is especially helpful to eliminate small-scale details along coasts.
We only simulate the largest contiguous geographic area left after this process, mostly to avoid needing to consider the dynamics between islands and the mainland (for example, Catalina Island off the coast of Los Angeles).
We note that this process will often merge areas separated by rivers (e.g. the island of Manhattan with the boroughs of Queens and Brooklyn in New York).
This allows us to approximate dynamics between areas that are physically separated but connected by bridges and tunnels.

We use
FiPy

gmsh

2
.
We then interpolate the population densities over time to the generated mesh using areal-weighted interpolation (see Fig.

) and convert the densities to fill fractions as discussed above.
Finally, we smooth fill fractions using a Gaussian filter with standard deviation of 3 km.
We then solve the sociohydrodynamic equations over the mesh while enforcing no-flux boundary conditions.

While we simulate areas larger than our counties of interest, we evaluate errors only within each county.

VI
Linear Stability Analysis

We now make predictions about the onset of patterns and their subsequent dynamics using linear stability analysis. We consider perturbing around a spatially uniform state densities,
\vec{\phi}(x,t)=\vec{\phi}_{0}+\delta\vec{\phi}(x,t)
, where the components
\phi_{0}^{a}
satisfy
\phi_{0}^{a}>0
and
\sum_{c}\phi_{0}^{c}\leq 1
. Writing the perturbation as a plane wave,
\delta\vec{\phi}(x,t)=e^{st-iqx}\delta\vec{\phi}_{0}
, where
s=\sigma+i\omega
is a complex number describing the growth rate
\sigma
and oscillation frequency
\omega
of the perturbation with wavenumber
q
, our equations of motion reduce to the eigenvalue problem

s\ \delta\phi_{0}^{a}=J^{ab}(\vec{\phi}_{0})\delta\phi_{0}^{b}

(56)

with the Jacobian matrix with elements

J^{ab}(\vec{\phi}_{0})=q^{2}D^{ab}(\vec{\phi}_{0})-q^{4}\Gamma^{ab}(\vec{\phi}_{0}),

(57)

Given
\Gamma^{ab}
is a diagonal matrix with positive elements (which we will always assume to be true), the
q^{4}
term acts to stabilize patterns at large wavenumber
q
, or short wavelengths.
The system’s stability is entirely determined by the eigenvalues
s
of the Jacobian matrix
\mathsf{J}
.
In the case with two groups
X
and
Y
,

\mathsf{J}=\begin{pmatrix}J^{XX}&J^{XY}\\
J^{YX}&J^{YY}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}J^{XX}&(J_{+}-J_{-})/2\\
(J_{+}+J_{-})/2&J^{YY}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(58)

The eigenvalues take the usual form for
2\times 2
matrices,

s_{\pm}=\dfrac{\mathrm{Tr}\ \mathsf{J}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Tr}\ \mathsf{J}}{2}\right)^{2}-\det{\mathsf{J}}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(J^{XX}+J^{YY}\pm\sqrt{(J^{XX}-J^{YY})^{2}+J_{+}^{2}-J_{-}^{2}}\right).

(59)

From here, we see that
\omega=\mathrm{Im}(s)\neq 0
only when
J_{-}^{2}>J_{+}^{2}+(J^{XX}-J^{YY})^{2}
.
As the
q^{4}
term in (
57
) is diagonal, this condition is controlled entirely by
\mathsf{D}
.

VIII
The potential function

In this section, we make connections with the potential function for the Schelling model discovered in Ref.
Grauwin et al. 2009
;
Grauwin et al. 2012
.
In particular, we rederive the
“link function”
, the non-trivial addition to a potential function that shows that the equilibrium state of bounded-neighborhood Schelling models need not coincide with the state that maximizes the utility function of all agents.

The advantage of using Eq. (
17
) as the transition rate is its connection to detailed balance. Namely, if one can show that

g^{a}(k|j)=F[\underline{\phi}^{\prime}]-F[\underline{\phi}],

then the system obeys detailed balance and will settle into a steady state given by the Boltzmann distribution,
P(\underline{\phi})\propto e^{F[\underline{\phi}]/T}

Evans and Hanney 2005
. To find this potential function,
F[\underline{\phi}]
, we will proceed term by term in Eq. (
18
) and show that they can each be written as the difference of some function depending on
\underline{\phi}
. The full potential function will then be a sum of these individual contributions.

This condition is manifestly satisfied by the second and third terms in Eq. (
18
), which are already written in the requisited form of a difference of a function of
\underline{\phi}
. However, it is not immediately obvious that the first term can be written as a function of the full configuration, as it explicitly depends on the type of particle moving (i.e. has a dependence on the index
a
) and on the specific lattice sites in consideration (i.e. has dependence on the indices
j,k
). Luckily,
Grauwin et al. 2009
showed that it is indeed possible if the utilities obey a specific restriction. Specifically, the function is given by

\Lambda(\underline{\phi})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{n^{X}_{i}-1}\left(\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}_{i}-1}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)\right)+\sum_{m=0}^{n^{Y}_{i}-1}\left(\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}_{i}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\right)\right],

(72)

and the restriction on the utilities is

\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}+1}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)=\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}+1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right).

(73)

This is the discrete version of the compatibility condition introduced in the main text. In the next section, we explicitly show that
\Lambda(\underline{\phi})
, which we call a
“link”
function, is indeed a potential function for the first term of Eq. (
18
).

We are now in a position to give the form of our potential function,
F[\underline{\phi}]
, where we follow
\lx@sectionsign~4.1.1
in
Bertin 2021
. As stated above, given the satisfaction of Eq. (
73
), our system will reach a steady state given by

P[\underline{\phi}]=\dfrac{1}{Z}\exp\left(\dfrac{\mathcal{N}}{T}\tilde{F}[\underline{\phi}]\right)

where
Z
is a normalization factor. The potential function can be written as a sum over lattice sites,

\tilde{F}[\underline{\phi}]=\sum_{i}\tilde{f}(\vec{\phi}_{i}),

with the density

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

\displaystyle=T\tilde{S}(\vec{\phi}_{i})+(1-\alpha)\tilde{\lambda}(\vec{\phi}_{i})+\alpha\tilde{u}(\vec{\phi}_{i})+\Gamma\tilde{\sigma}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

(74a)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\ln\left(\dfrac{\mathcal{N}!}{n_{i}^{X}!n_{i}^{Y}!\left(\mathcal{N}-n_{i}^{X}-n_{i}^{Y}\right)!}\right)

(74b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\lambda}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\mathcal{N}}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{n^{X}_{i}-1}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}_{i}-1}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)+\sum_{m=0}^{n^{Y}_{i}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}_{i}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\right]

(74c)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=X,Y}\phi^{b}\pi^{b}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

(74d)

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}(\vec{\phi}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=X,Y}\dfrac{\phi^{b}_{i}(\partial_{x}^{2}\underline{\phi})^{b}_{i}}{2}.

(74e)

The term
S(\vec{\phi}_{i})
comes from counting the number of ways of arranging
n^{X}
and
n^{Y}
particles in a lattice site with
\mathcal{N}
total sites. Taking the limit
\{\mathcal{N},n_{i}^{a}\}\to\infty
while keeping
\phi^{a}_{i}=n_{i}^{a}/\mathcal{N}
constant, we can expand the factorials in
\tilde{S}
using Stirling’s approximation,
\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n
, to get

\tilde{S}(\vec{\phi}_{i})\to S(\vec{\phi}_{i})=-\phi^{X}_{i}\ln\phi^{X}_{i}-\phi^{Y}_{i}\ln\phi^{Y}_{i}-\phi^{0}_{i}\ln\phi^{0}_{i},

(75)

where
\phi^{0}_{i}=1-\phi^{X}_{i}-\phi^{Y}_{i}
is the number of vacancies at site
i
. This is the entropy of mixing.

Furthermore, we can convert the sums in
\lambda(\vec{\phi}_{i})
to integrals, giving

\tilde{\lambda}(\vec{\phi}_{i})\to\lambda(\vec{\phi}_{i})=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\int_{0}^{\phi^{X}_{i}}d\psi\ \pi^{X}\left(\psi,\phi^{Y}_{i}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\psi,0\right)+\int_{0}^{\phi^{Y}_{i}}d\psi^{\prime}\ \pi^{Y}\left(\phi^{X}_{i},\psi^{\prime}\right)+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\psi^{\prime}\right)\right].

(76)

Finally, we also take the continuum limit,
\ell\to 0
, while keeping the total density,
\sum_{i}\phi^{a}_{i}\to\ell^{-1}\int\phi^{a}(x)dx
constant. For all the local terms, this amounts to converting discrete
i
indices to a continuous
x
coordinate. In addition, the discrete Laplacian operator in
\sigma(\vec{\phi}_{i})
becomes the usual differential Laplacian operator.

We now finally arrive at our potential function,

F[\vec{\phi}(x)]=\int f(\vec{\phi}(x))dx

with the potential density

\displaystyle f(\vec{\phi},\partial_{x}\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle TS(\vec{\phi})+(1-\alpha)\lambda(\vec{\phi})+\alpha u(\vec{\phi})-\Gamma\sigma(\vec{\phi})

(77a)

\displaystyle S(\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle-\phi^{X}\ln(\phi^{X})-\phi^{Y}\ln(\phi^{Y})-\phi^{0}\ln\phi^{0}

(77b)

\displaystyle\lambda(\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\Bigg[\int_{0}^{\phi^{X}}d\phi^{\prime}\left(\pi^{X}(\phi^{\prime},\phi^{Y})+\pi^{X}(\phi^{\prime},0)\right)+\int_{0}^{\phi^{Y}}d\phi^{\prime\prime}\left(\pi^{Y}(\phi^{X},\phi^{\prime\prime})+\pi^{Y}(0,\phi^{\prime\prime})\right)\Bigg]

(77c)

\displaystyle u(\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=X,Y}\phi^{a}\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi})

(77d)

\displaystyle\sigma(\vec{\phi})=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=X,Y}\dfrac{|\partial_{x}\phi^{a}(x)|^{2}}{2}

(77e)

Note that we have used integration by parts on
\int\sigma(\vec{\phi}(x))dx
to obtain the minus sign in front of
\Gamma
. In the large
\mathcal{N}
limit, the compatibility condition Eq.
73
is now given by

\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})}{\partial\phi^{Y}}=\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})}{\partial\phi^{X}}

(78)

VIII.1
Demonstration of
\Lambda(\underline{\phi})
as a potential function

Here, we will explicitly show that the difference of the utility function of an individual agent is given by the difference of the link function defined over the entire population, i.e.

\Lambda(\underline{\phi}^{\prime})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi})=\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}^{\prime}_{k})-\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{j})=\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{k})-\pi^{a}(\vec{\phi}_{j}-\delta\phi\hat{e}_{a}),

where
\underline{\phi}^{\prime}=D^{a}_{k|j}\underline{\phi}
. For concreteness, let us set
a=X
. The calculation for
a=Y
proceeds identically. In this case,

\Lambda(D^{X}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\left[\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n^{X}_{i}-1+\delta_{ik}+\delta_{ij}}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n^{Y}_{i}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)+\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{n^{Y}_{i}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n^{X}_{i}-1+\delta_{ik}-\delta_{ij}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\right].

(79)

Each term in the sum over
i
in
\Lambda(\underline{\phi}^{\prime})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi})
is zero unless
i=\{j,k\}
. We are left with

\begin{split}2(\Lambda(D^{X}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi}))=&{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0}\sum_{n_{k}^{\prime}=0}^{n_{k}^{X}}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{k}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{k}^{\prime}=0}^{n_{k}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}+1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m_{k}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\\
-&{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0}\sum_{n_{k}=0}^{n_{k}^{X}-1}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{k}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{k}=0}^{n_{k}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\\
+&{\color[rgb]{0,0.392,0}\sum_{n_{j}^{\prime}=0}^{n_{j}^{X}-2}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{j}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}+{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{j}^{\prime}=0}^{n_{j}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)\\
-&{\color[rgb]{0,0.392,0}\sum_{n_{j}=0}^{n_{j}^{X}-1}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{j}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{\prime}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}-{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{n_{j}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}+\pi^{Y}\left(0,\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right).\end{split}

(80)

The first two lines come from the
i=k
term of
\Lambda(\underline{\phi}^{\prime})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi})
, while the final two lines from the
i=j
term of
\Lambda(\underline{\phi}^{\prime})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi})
, respectively. We have color-coded the different terms for clarity in what follows.

To proceed, we notice that the very last terms in the first two lines cancel, as do the very last terms in the final two lines. Combining the remaining terms with the same colors, we have

\begin{split}2(\Lambda(D^{X}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi}))=&{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{k}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.392,0}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{j}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}\\
+&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{k}=0}^{n_{k}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}+1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}+{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\sum_{m_{j}=0}^{n_{j}^{Y}-1}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}.\end{split}

(81)

Here, we are forced to use Eq. (
73
) to change the last two summands as

\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}+1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}&={\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}+1}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{k}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}\\
{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{Y}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}&={\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{m_{j}+1}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}.\end{split}

Evaluating the sums, we find

\begin{split}2(\Lambda(D^{X}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi}))=&{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{k}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)+\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}-{\color[rgb]{0,0.392,0}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{j}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}\\
+&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.545}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{k}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{k}^{X}}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)}+{\color[rgb]{0.545,0,0.545}\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},0\right)-\pi^{X}\left(\dfrac{n_{j}^{X}-1}{\mathcal{N}},\dfrac{n_{j}^{Y}}{\mathcal{N}}\right)}.\end{split}

(82)

Finally, combining terms and canceling others, we arrive at our desired result

\Lambda(D^{X}_{k|j}\underline{\phi})-\Lambda(\underline{\phi})=\pi^{X}(\vec{\phi}_{k})-\pi^{X}(\vec{\phi}_{j}-\delta\phi\hat{e}^{X}).

(83)

VIII.2
Relaxational dynamics

Given the existence of the utility potential function
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]
, one can define a dynamics for
\phi^{a}(x,t)
such that
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]
plays the role of a Lyapunov function ensuring the relaxation of the system toward a stationary state, as done for physical systems
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. One important difference is that the utility potential function will be
maximized
, as contrasted with a physical free energy which is minimized.
We note that this is not the general case for sociohydrodyanmics. It is a special case where the incompatibility condition is satisfied.

As the microscopic dynamics consist solely of particles moving, the macroscopic dynamics must conserve the total particle number,

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(x,t)=-\partial_{x}J^{a}(x,t).

(84)

where
\partial_{x}=\partial/\partial x
. Analogously to physical system, we take the particle current
J^{a}(x,t)
to obey the constitutive relation

J^{a}(x,t)=+M^{ab}(\vec{\phi})\partial_{x}\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta\phi^{b}}.

(85)

M^{ab}(\vec{\phi})
is a density dependent mobility matrix, which we take to be given by

M^{ab}(\vec{\phi})=\delta^{ab}\phi^{b}\phi^{0}.

(86)

Recalling
\phi^{0}=1-\sum_{c}\phi^{c}
, this form enforces that both empty (
\phi^{b}=0
) and full (
\phi^{0}=0
) locations give zero-flux.

\delta F/\delta\phi^{b}
is the functional derivative of the potential
F[\vec{\phi}(x)]
that plays the role of a chemical potential
Lemoy et al. 2011
. Eq. (
85
) says that the particle current flows
up
spatial gradients of the chemical potential. We now evaluate the chemical potential as

\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta\phi^{b}}=-T\left(\ln\phi^{b}-\ln\phi^{0}\right)+(1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\Gamma\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{b}.

(87)

Taking the gradient, we obtain

\partial_{x}\dfrac{\delta F}{\delta\phi^{b}}=-T\dfrac{\phi^{0}\partial_{x}\phi^{b}+\phi^{b}\partial_{x}\phi^{0}}{\phi^{b}\phi^{0}}+(1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\alpha\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{b}}+\Gamma\partial_{x}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{b}.

(88)

Finally multiplying by the mobility matrix
M^{ab}=\delta^{ab}\phi^{b}\phi^{0}
, we get our general form for relaxational sociohydrodynamics

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(x,t)=T\left((1-\phi^{b})\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}+\phi^{a}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{b}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\ \partial_{x}\left[(1-\alpha)\dfrac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\alpha\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\Gamma^{a}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}\right]\right).

(89)

In the special case where the compatibility condition Eq.
78
holds, the derivative of the link function
\lambda(\vec{\phi}(x))
is given by (here, we take
b=X
. The calculation for
b=Y
proceeds analogously)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi^{X}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})+\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},0)+\int_{0}^{\phi^{Y}}d\phi^{\prime}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{Y}(\phi^{X},\phi^{\prime})}{\partial\phi^{X}}\right)

(90)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})+\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},0)+\int_{0}^{\phi^{Y}}d\phi^{\prime}\dfrac{\partial\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{\prime})}{\partial\phi^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})+\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},0)+\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y})-\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},0)\right)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\phi^{X}}

\displaystyle=\pi^{X}(\phi^{X},\phi^{Y}).

where we used the compatibility condition Eq. (
78
) to go from the first to the second line. In this case, Eq. (
89
) becomes

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}(x,t)=T\left((1-\phi^{b})\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{a}+\phi^{a}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi^{b}\right)-\partial_{x}\left(\phi^{a}\phi^{0}\left[(1-\alpha)\partial_{x}\pi^{a}+\partial_{x}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial\phi^{a}}+\Gamma^{a}\partial_{x}^{3}\phi^{a}\right]\right).

(91)

where
b\neq a
. This is the same equation as Eq.
51
, showing that our coarse-grained equations are equivalent to the relaxational dynamics when the compatibility condition holds.

We now consider a microscopic model for growth that is built in the same spirit as agent-based model above. This would lead to a form for
S^{a}
which we set to zero in the main text.

We consider individuals within a lattice site can spontaneously die, or reproduce if there is space available in the lattice site, with a rate
k
dependent on the fitness
f^{a}
,
k=k(f^{a})
. We denote these processes by the chemical reactions

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\overset{k(-f^{a})}{\to}\emptyset

(92a)

\displaystyle a+\emptyset

\displaystyle\overset{k(f^{a})}{\to}a+a.

(92b)

The reaction rates are given by

k(f^{a})=\dfrac{2r}{1+e^{-\gamma f^{a}}},

(93)

where
r
is a rate,
\gamma
measures the effects of stochasticity in the death/reproduction processes, and the factor of 2 is included for simplicity. Here, we have assumed that both processes have the same
r
and
\gamma
. The form of
k(f^{a})
assures that reproduction (death) occurs more (less) rapidly with increasing fitness.

Assuming mass action kinetics, the density of population
a
will obey

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}=\phi^{a}\left(\phi^{\emptyset}k(f^{a})-k(-f^{a})\right),

(94)

where
\phi^{\emptyset}=1-\sum_{b}\phi^{b}
measures the density of empty sites available to be occupied by reproduction.
In the limit of weak selection,
\gamma\to 0
, we can expand
k(f^{a})
to find

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}=r\phi^{a}\left(\gamma f^{a}-\left(\sum_{b}\phi^{b}\right)\left(1+\dfrac{\gamma f^{a}}{2}\right)\right)=S^{a}.

(95)
