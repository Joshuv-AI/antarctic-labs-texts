Abstract
. Personalized PageRank (PPR) is a critical measure of the importance of a node t t to a source node s s in a graph. The Single-Source PPR (SSPPR) query computes the PPR’s of all the nodes with respect to s s on a directed graph G G with n n nodes and m m edges; and it is an essential operation widely used in graph applications. In this paper, we propose novel algorithms for answering two variants of SSPPR queries: (i) high-precision queries and (ii) approximate queries. For high-precision queries, Power Iteration ( ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ I ​ t ​ r \emph{PowItr} ) and Forward Push ( ​ F ​ w ​ d ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{FwdPush} ) are two fundamental approaches. Given an absolute error threshold λ \lambda (which is typically set to as small as 10 − 8 10^{-8} ), the only known bound of ​ F ​ w ​ d ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{FwdPush} is O ⁡ ( m λ ) O(\frac{m}{\lambda}) , much worse than the O ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ 1 λ ) O(m\log\frac{1}{\lambda}) -bound of ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ I ​ t ​ r \emph{PowItr} . Whether ​ F ​ w ​ d ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{FwdPush} can achieve the same running time bound as ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ I ​ t ​ r \emph{PowItr} does still remains an open question in the research community. We give a positive answer to this question. We show that the running time of a common implementation of ​ F ​ w ​ d ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{FwdPush} is actually bounded by O ⁡ ( m ⋅ log ⁡ 1 λ ) O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda}) . Based on this finding, we propose a new algorithm, called Power Iteration with Forward Push ( ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ e ​ r ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{PowerPush} ), which incorporates the strengths of both ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ I ​ t ​ r \emph{PowItr} and ​ F ​ w ​ d ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{FwdPush} . For approximate queries (with a relative error ϵ \epsilon ), we propose a new algorithm, called ​ S ​ p ​ e ​ e ​ d ​ P ​ P ​ R \emph{SpeedPPR} , with overall expected time bounded by O ⁡ ( n ⋅ log ⁡ n ⋅ log ⁡ 1 ϵ ) O(n\cdot\log n\cdot\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}) on scale-free graphs. This improves the state-of-the-art O ⁡ ( n ⋅ log ⁡ n ϵ ) O(\frac{n\cdot\log n}{\epsilon}) bound. We conduct extensive experiments on six real datasets. The experimental results show that ​ P ​ o ​ w ​ e ​ r ​ P ​ u ​ s ​ h \emph{PowerPush} outperforms the state-of-the-art high-precision algorithm ​ B ​ e ​ P ​ I \emph{BePI} by up to an order of magnitude in both efficiency and accuracy. Furthermore, our ​ S ​ p ​ e ​ e ​ d ​ P ​ P ​ R \emph{SpeedPPR} also outperforms the state-of-the-art approximate algorithm ​ F ​ O ​ R ​ A \emph{FORA} by up to an order of magnitude in all aspects including query time, accuracy, pre-processing time as well as index size.



1.
Introduction

As a natural data model, graphs are playing a more and more important role in real-world applications nowadays.
In a graph, it is often useful to measure the
relevance
between nodes.
One of the most important relevance measurements is the
importance
of a node
t
to a node
s
,
for which the
Personalised PageRank
(PPR) is a widely adopted indicator.

Consider a directed graph
G=\langle V,E\rangle
with
n
nodes and
m
edges, a source node
s
and a target node
t
in
V
;
the
PPR
of
t
with respect to
s
, denoted by
\pi(s,t)
,
is the probability that an
\alpha
-random walk from
s
stops at
t
.
Specifically, an
\alpha
-random walk (for some constant
\alpha\in[0,1)
, e.g.,
\alpha=0.2
) from
s
is proceeded as follows:
starting from
s
, the walk may stop at the current node
v
(initially
v=s
) with the probability of
\alpha
, or with the probability of
1-\alpha
, the walk may move to one of
v
’s out-neighbors uniformly at random.

Of particular interest is the
Single Source PPR
(SSPPR) query; its goal is to compute
\pi(s,v)
for every node
v\in V
with respect to a given source node
s
.
The answer to a SSPPR query is a
vector
in
\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
, denoted by
\vec{\pi_{s}}
, of which the
i
-th coordinate is the PPR
\pi(s,v_{i})
, where
v_{i}
is the
i
-th node in
G
.
The SSPPR query has many important traditional applications such as computing PageRank and Who-to-Follow recommendation in social networks (e.g., Twitter). Moreover, the SSPPR query provides essential and primitive features widely used in representation learning for graphs, which is attracting huge attention in the machine learning community at the moment.
For example, the PPR information has been adopted in graph embedding methods such as HOPE
(
Ou
et al. 2016
)
, STRAP
(
Yin and Wei 2019
)
and Verse
(
Tsitsulin et al. 2018
)
, and
graph attention networks such as ADSF
(
Zhang
et al. 2020
)
.

Therefore, it is imperative to have highly efficient algorithms for answering SSPPR queries.
It is known that an SSPPR query can be
precisely
solved by solving the following linear equation system
(
Page
et al. 1999
)
:

(1)

\displaystyle\vec{\pi_{s}}=\alpha\cdot\vec{e_{s}}+(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{\pi_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{P}\,,

where
\vec{e_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
is an indicator vector which has
1
on the
s
-th dimension and
0
for others,
and
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the so-called
transition matrix
of
G
.
However, solving Equation (
1
) requires to compute the inverse of an
n\times n
matrix related to
\mathbf{P}
, which is expensive.
In practice, to trade for better efficiency, people instead compute an estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
of
\vec{\pi_{s}}
, which meets a certain error criteria.
Along this direction, SSPPR queries can be categorized into two variants: (i)
High-Precision SSPPR
queries and (ii)
Approximate SSPPR
queries.

In this paper, we propose novel algorithms for answering these two types of queries.
Our algorithms are efficient both in theory and in practice.
Before illustrating our results, we first set up the context of the relevant state-of-the-art algorithms.

High-Precision SSPPR.

The goal of this type of queries is to compute a
high-precision
estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
of
\vec{\pi_{s}}
such that the
\ell_{1}
-error
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq\lambda
,
where
\lambda
is a specified threshold and
\lambda
is often set to as small as
10^{-8}
.

Power Iteration
(
\emph{PowItr}
) and
Forward Push
(
\emph{FwdPush}
) are two fundamental approaches to answer high-precision SSPPR queries.

Power Iteration (
\emph{PowItr}
).

\emph{PowItr}
is an iterative algorithm for solving Equation (
1
).
More specifically, it refines an estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
of
\vec{\pi_{s}}
iteration by iteration;
in each iteration,
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}
decreases by a factor of
(1-\alpha)
.
It is known that the overall running time of
\emph{PowItr}
is bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})

(
Berkhin 2005
)
.

Forward Push (
\emph{FwdPush}
).

\emph{FwdPush}
is another feasible approach to answer high-precision SSPPR queries.
It is well-known that the running time of
\emph{FwdPush}
is bounded by
O(\frac{1}{r_{\max}})
, where
r_{\max}
is a parameter that controls the stop condition of the algorithm.
However,
at the time when
\emph{FwdPush}
was first proposed in 2006
(
Andersen
et al. 2006
)
, the
\ell_{1}
-error bound of this approach was unclear.
In 2017, Wang et. al
(
Wang
et al. 2017
)
officially documented that the
\ell_{1}
-error is bounded by

(2)

\displaystyle\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq m\cdot r_{\max}\,.

Therefore, in order to guarantee
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq\lambda
, one needs to set
r_{\max}=\lambda/m
leading to an overall time complexity
O(m/\lambda)
.
Unfortunately, this bound is not quite useful. Given that
\lambda
is often as small as
10^{-8}
, this bound would imply a huge cost when the graph is large, e.g., on the billion-edge Twitter graph.
However, interestingly, despite of the
O(m/\lambda)
-bound,
\emph{FwdPush}
is found to
be more efficient
than the bound suggests in certain applications (e.g., the Approximate SSPPR queries as discussed below).

Therefore, a significant knowledge gap still exists between the practical use and the theoretical understanding of
\emph{FwdPush}
.
In particular, the following question:

Does
\emph{FwdPush}
admit a tighter running time bound with a weaker dependency on the
\ell_{1}
-error threshold
\lambda
?

remains open to the research community.

Approximate SSPPR.

The aim of approximate SSPPR is to compute an estimation
\hat{\pi}(s,v)
bounded by a relative error
\epsilon
, i.e.,
|\hat{\pi}(s,v)-\pi(s,v)|\leq\epsilon\cdot\pi(s,v)
,
for every node
v
whose
\pi(s,v)\geq 1/n
, and the algorithm must be correct with probability of at least
1-{1}/{n}
.

FORA.

\emph{FORA}

(
Wang
et al. 2016
)
is a representative of the state-of-the-art approximate SSPPR algorithms.
The basic idea of
\emph{FORA}
is to combine
\emph{FwdPush}
and the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method.
Specifically, there are two phases:
in the first phase,

\emph{FORA}
runs
\emph{FwdPush}
to obtain an estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
such that
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq m\cdot r_{\max}
.
In the second phase, the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method based on
\hat{\pi_{s}}
is adopted to refine the estimations to
be within a relative error
\epsilon
for every node
v
with
\pi(s,v)\geq 1/n
.
The overall expected running time is bounded by
O(\frac{1}{r_{\max}}+m\cdot r_{\max}\cdot\frac{n\log n}{\epsilon^{2}})
.
By setting
r_{max}
carefully to “balance” the two terms and assuming the graph is scale-free, i.e.,
m=O(n\log n)
,
the complexity is minimized to
O(\frac{n\log n}{\epsilon})
.
In the literature, no existing work
(
Wang
et al. 2016
;
Wei
et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Lin
et al. 2020
)
can overcome this
O(\frac{n\log n}{\epsilon})
-barrier.

Besides,
\emph{FORA}
admits an index version, called
\emph{FORA}
+, where the results of the
\alpha
-random walks that would be needed in the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
phase are pre-generated.
With the index, the actual running time of
\emph{FORA}
+ can be further reduced.
However,
since
\emph{FORA}
has to set
r_{\max}
to minimize the complexity,
the number of random walks required to be pre-generated in
\emph{FORA}
+ depends on the relative error
\epsilon
.
Thus, the index constructed for one
\epsilon
value may not be sufficient for answering a query with another smaller
\epsilon
value.
This weakness significantly limits the applicability of
\emph{FORA}
+.

Our Contributions.
We make the following contributions:

•

An Equivalence Connection.

We show that there essentially exists an equivalence connection between the global-approach
\emph{PowItr}
and the local-approach
\emph{FwdPush}
.

•

A Positive Answer to the Open Question.

Embarking from this connection, we prove that
the running time of a common
\emph{FwdPush}
implementation is actually bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
with
r_{\max}=\lambda/m
,
rather than the widely accepted
O(\frac{m}{\lambda})
-bound.

•

A New Algorithm for High-Precision SSPPR.

Based on our finding, we
propose a new implementation for
\emph{PowItr}
(and hence, also for
\emph{FwdPush}
), called
Power Iteration with Forward Push (
\emph{PowerPush}
).
Our
\emph{PowerPush}
is carefully designed such that it incorporates both the strengths of
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
(detailed discussions are in Section
5
).
Therefore, it outperforms
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
in all cases in our experiments while still achieving the
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
theoretical bound.

Moreover, unlike the state-of-the-art algorithm,
\emph{BePI}

(
Jung
et al. 2017
)
,
which requires a substantial pre-processing time and space for index storage,

\emph{PowerPush}
is completely on-the-fly without needing any pre-processing or index pre-computation.
Even though the advantage of pre-processing is taken,
in our experiment, on a medium-size data, Orkut,

\emph{BePI}
requires
672
seconds for a query.
Our
\emph{PowerPush}
answers the same query in less than
40
seconds,
17
times faster than
\emph{BePI}
.

Besides, although
\emph{PowerPush}
is a high-precision algorithm, in our experiments, in some cases, it even outperforms the state-of-the-art approximate SSPPR algorithms in running time.

Finally, given the fact that
\emph{PowItr}
is an important fundamental method, we believe that our
\emph{PowerPush}
would be of independent interests in other applications beyond the SSPPR queries.

•

A New Algorithm for Approximate SSPPR.

Based on the support of
\emph{PowerPush}
, we further design a new algorithm, called SpeedPPR, for answering approximate SSPPR queries.
On scale-free graphs with
m=O(n\cdot\log n)
, the overall expected time of
\emph{SpeedPPR}
is bounded by
O(n\cdot\log n\cdot\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})
,
improving the state-of-the-art
O(\frac{n\cdot\log n}{\epsilon})
-bound.
Furthermore,
\emph{SpeedPPR}
always admits an index of at most
m

\alpha
-random walk results.
Hence, the space consumption of the index is at most as large as the graph itself.
More importantly, the index size of SpeedPPR is independent to the values of
\epsilon
.
In other words, once the index is built, it suffices to answer queries with any
\epsilon
.
This feature of SpeedPPR is considered as an important improvement over
\emph{FORA}
+.
In particular, for small
\epsilon
values, SpeedPPR consumes
10
times less space than
\emph{FORA}
+ does for index storage.

•

Extensive Experiments.

We conduct extensive experiments on six real datasets which are widely adopted in the literature.
The experimental results show that our
\emph{PowerPush}
outperforms the state-of-the-art high-precision SSPPR algorithms by up to an order of magnitude. Our SpeedPPR outperforms all the state-of-the-art competitors for approximate SSPPR by up to an order of magnitude in terms of
query efficiency and result accuracy; for index-based version,
\emph{SpeedPPR}
also achieves up to
10
times improvements on both pre-processing time and index size.

Problem Formulation

Consider a directed graph
G=\langle V,E\rangle
with
n=|V|
nodes and
m=|E|
edges.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the nodes in
V
are in order such that
v_{i}
is the
i
-th node in
V
, where
i\in[n]
and
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
For a node
v\in V
, denote the set of the out-neighbors of
v

by
N_{out}(v)=\{u\mid(v,u)\in E\}
, and
d_{v}=|N_{out}(v)|
is defined as the out-degree of
v
.
Clearly,
m=\sum_{v\in V}d_{v}
.
In this paper, we assume that there is no “dead-end” nodes, i.e.,
d_{v}\geq 1
holds for all
v\in V
, in the graph
G
.
As we explain below, this assumption is without loss of generality.

Indicator Vector.

Denote by
\vec{e}_{v_{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
the indicator vector which has coordinate
1
on the
i
-th dimension and
0
on the others, where
v_{i}\in V
.
It is easy to verify that for any
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{M}
, the result of
\vec{e}_{v_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{M}
is exactly the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{M}
.

\ell_{1}
-Norm.

For any
n
-dimensional vector
\vec{x}
, the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
\vec{x}
is computed as
\|\vec{x}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|
, where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th coordinate of
\vec{x}
.

Adjacent Matrix.

The adjacent matrix
\mathbf{A}
of a directed graph
G
is an
n\times n
matrix,
where the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
, denoted by
\vec{A}_{v_{i}}
, is a row vector which has coordinate
1
on the
j
-th dimension if
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E
and
0
otherwise, for
j\in[n]
.

Transition Matrix.

The transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
of a directed graph
G
with an adjacent matrix
\mathbf{A}
is
an
n\times n
matrix,
where the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{P}
, denoted by
\vec{P}_{v_{i}}
, satisfies
\vec{P}_{v_{i}}=\frac{1}{d_{v_{i}}}\cdot\vec{A}_{v_{i}}
,
and hence,
\|\vec{P}_{v_{i}}\|_{1}=1
for all
i\in[n]
.
Furthermore, it can be verified that for any vector
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
, it holds that
\|\vec{x}\cdot\mathbf{P}^{k}\|_{1}=\|\vec{x}\|_{1}
for all integer
k\geq 1
. An example of a transition matrix is shown in Figure
1
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1/2&1/2&0&0\\
1/4&0&1/4&1/4&1/4\\
0&1/2&0&1/2&0\\
1/3&1/3&1/3&0&0\\
0&1/2&1/2&0&0\end{bmatrix}

.
A directed graph
G
and its transition matrix
\mathbf{P}

\alpha
-Random Walk.

Consider a constant parameter
\alpha\in[0,1)
which is set to
0.2
by default in the literature;
an
\alpha
-random walk from a node
s\in V
is defined as follows:
let
v
be the current node and initially the current node
v
is
s
;
at every step, the walk stops at
v
with probability
\alpha
,
and with probability
1-\alpha
, the walk moves one-step forward depending on either of the following two cases:
(i) if
N_{out}\neq\emptyset
, the walk uniformly at random, i.e., with equal probability
\frac{1}{d_{v}}
, moves to an out-neighbor
u\in N_{out}(v)

(that is, the current node
v
now becomes
u
);
(ii) otherwise (i.e.,
N_{out}=\emptyset
), the walk jumps back to
s
(the current node
v
becomes
s
).
Effectively, this is equivalent to conceptually add an edge from each “dead-end” node (whose out-degree is
0
) to the source node
s
,
and hence, one can assume that no dead-end node exists in the graph.
Moreover, without stated otherwise, all the random walks considered in this paper are
\alpha
-random walks.

Alive Random Walk.

If an
\alpha
-random walk at the current node
v
does not stop yet, then we say this random walk is alive at
v
.

Personalized PageRank (PPR).
Consider a node
s\in V
and a node
t\in V
; the PPR of
t
with respect to
s
, denoted by
\pi(s,t)
,
is defined as the probability that an
\alpha
-random walk from
s
stops at
t
.

Single Source Personalized PageRank (SSPPR).

Given a source node
s\in V
, the goal of a SSPPR query is to compute
the PPR vector
\vec{\pi_{s}}
, where the
i
-th coordinate in
\vec{\pi_{s}}
is the PPR
\pi(s,v_{i})
of
v_{i}
.
Essentially,
\vec{\pi_{s}}
is the probability distribution over all the nodes that an
\alpha
-random walk from
s
stops at a node.
Thus,
\|\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}=1
.

High-Precision SSPPR (HP-SSPPR).

Given an
\ell_{1}
-error threshold
\lambda\in(0,1]
, the goal of a High-Precision SSPPR query is to compute an estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
of
\vec{\pi_{s}}
such that

\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq\lambda
.
In general, the value of
\lambda
is set to
\min\{\frac{1}{m},10^{-8}\}
.

Approximate SSPPR (Approx-SSPPR).

Given an relative error threshold
\epsilon>0
and a PPR value threshold
\mu\in(0,1]
, an Approximate SSPPR query aims to compute
an estimation
\hat{\pi}(s,v)
for each node
v
with
\pi(s,v)\geq\mu
such that
|\hat{\pi}(s,v)-\pi(s,v)|\leq\epsilon\cdot\pi(s,v)

with high probability
1-\frac{1}{n}
.
In the literature,
\mu
is conventionally set to the average over all the PPR values with respect to
s
, i.e.,
\frac{1}{n}
.

.

3.
Preliminaries of HP-SSPPR

In this section, we introduce the details of the two most relevant existing approaches to this paper for answering Hight-Precision SSPPR queries:
Power Iteration (
\emph{PowItr}
) and Forward Push (
\emph{FwdPush}
).
In the literature,
they are often considered as two different types of methods,
respectively as global and local approaches.
Thus, in the previous work, these two approaches are usually understood and explained from different perspectives.
Next, we explain both
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
from a unified perspective of alive random walks.
Our explanations would hint some ideas on the equivalence connection between these two approaches as discussed in Section
4
.

3.1.
The Power Iteration Approach

Define
\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
as the vector, of which the
i
-th coordinate is the probability that
an
\alpha
-random walk from
s
is alive at
v_{i}
at the
j
-th step.
Clearly,
\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(0)}=\vec{e_{s}}
: a random walk from
s
can be alive only at
s
at the initial state, i.e., at the
0
-th step.
According to the definition of
\alpha
-random walks, it can be verified that

(3)

\small\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{P}=(1-\alpha)^{j+1}\cdot\vec{e_{s}}\cdot\mathbf{P}^{j+1}\,.

Therefore, the
i
-th coordinate of the vector

\alpha\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}

is the probability that
a random walk from
s
stops at
v_{i}
at exactly the
j
-th step.

Intuitively, by the definition of PPR,
\pi(s,t)
can be computed as the sum of the probabilities that
an
\alpha
-random walk from
s
stops at
t
with exactly
j
steps, for all possible length
j=0,1,2,\cdots
.
That is,

(4)

\small\vec{\pi_{s}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}\,.

The basic idea of
\emph{PowItr}
is to iteratively maintain an underestimate
\hat{\pi_{s}}=\vec{\pi}_{s}^{(j+1)}
in the
(j+1)
-th iteration (for
j=0,1,2,\cdots
) such that:

(5)

\small\vec{\pi}_{s}^{(j+1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\alpha\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(k)}\,,

where the
i
-th coordinate of
\vec{\pi}_{s}^{(j+1)}
is the probability that a random walk from
s
stops at
v_{i}
with at most
j
steps.

The
\ell_{1}
-Error Bound.

The
\ell_{1}
-error at the end of the
(j+1)
-th iteration is given by:

\displaystyle\small\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\sum_{k=j+1}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(k)}\|_{1}=\sum_{k=j+1}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot(1-\alpha)^{k}\cdot\|\vec{e}_{s}\cdot\mathbf{P}^{k}\|_{1}

(6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=j+1}^{\infty}\alpha\cdot(1-\alpha)^{k}\cdot\|\vec{e}_{s}\|_{1}=(1-\alpha)^{j+1}\,.

Two observations follow immediately from Equation (
3.1
).
First, the
\ell_{1}
-error
(1-\alpha)^{j+1}
is exactly equal to
\|\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}\|_{1}
,
that is, the total probability mass of a random walk alive at the
(j+1)
-th steps.
This is intuitive because this is exactly the total amount of the probability mass
that is not yet converted to the PPR values.
Second, the
\ell_{1}
-error decreases by a
factor of
(1-\alpha)
in each iteration; after at most
O(\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
iterations,
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}\leq\lambda
.
Since it is easy to verify that the computational cost of each iteration is bounded by
O(m)
,
the overall running time of
\emph{PowItr}
is thus bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
.

3.2.
The Forward Push Approach

\emph{FwdPush}
conceptually considers a random walk from
s
and observes the state of this walk in terms of probability mass.
Given a specified parameter
r_{\max}\in[0,1]
,
the basic idea of
\emph{FwdPush}
is to maintain, for each node
v\in V
, the following information:

•

a reserve
\hat{\pi}(s,v)
: it is an underestimate of
\pi(s,v)
and

•

a residue
r(s,v)
:
it is the unprocessed probability mass of the random walk from
s
alive at
v
at the current state.

Initially,
\hat{\pi}(s,v)=0
for all
v\in V
and
r(s,v)=0
for all
v\neq s
while
r(s,s)=1
:
at the initial state,
the unprocessed probability mass of the random walk from
s
alive at
s
is
1
.

Active Nodes.

A node
v
is active if it satisfies
r(s,v)>d_{v}\cdot r_{\max}
; otherwise, it is inactive.

The Push Operation.

A crucial primitive in
\emph{FwdPush}
is the push operation, which is to
process a node’s residue.
Specifically, a push operation on a node
v
works as follows:

•

First,
\alpha
portion of
v
’s residue
r(s,v)
is converted to
\hat{\pi}(s,v)
, i.e.,
\hat{\pi}(s,v)\leftarrow\hat{\pi}(s,v)+\alpha\cdot r(s,v)
.
This represents the fact that
with probability
\alpha
, the alive random walk at
v
stops at
v
.

•

Second, the rest
(1-\alpha)
portion of
r(s,v)
is evenly distributed to the residues of
v
’s out-neighbors.
That is, the residue of each out-neighbor of
v
is increased by
\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r(s,v)}{d_{v}}
,
which is the probability that, conditioned on
r(s,v)
, the random walk at
v
moves to this out-neighbor and is alive at this out-neighbor at the current state.

•

Third, after the residue of
v
is processed,
r(s,v)\leftarrow 0
, indicating that currently there is no unprocessed probability mass of the random walk from
s
currently alive at
v
.

The process of
\emph{FwdPush}
is to repeatedly pick an arbitrary active node,
and perform a push operation on it.
The algorithm terminates until there is no active node.

The

When

The Open Question.

The only known time complexity of

\emph{PowItr}
.
Despite of its practicality in certain applications, it still remains an open question:
Does

, just like what
\emph{PowItr}
does?

4.
A Tighter Analysis of Forward Push

Figure 3

In this section, we give a positive answer to the open question regarding to the running time of
\emph{FwdPush}
.
More specifically, we prove that under a proper strategy to pick active nodes to perform push operations,
the overall running time of
\emph{FwdPush}
can be bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
with
r_{\max}=\lambda/m
.
This finding stems from an observation on a subtle equivalence connection between
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
as we discuss next.

4.1.
Equivalence Connection to Power Iteration

Recall that in each iteration,

\emph{PowItr}
essentially computes
\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{P}
.
Thus, the alive random walks considered in the same iteration are all with the same lengths.
Such a well structured process makes the error bound analysis of
\emph{PowItr}
relatively clear.
In contrast, the process of
\emph{FwdPush}
is a lot less structured.
Due to the fact that
\emph{FwdPush}
allows to perform push operations on arbitrary active nodes,
the residues of the nodes actually mix up the probability mass of the alive random walk from
s
at the states of different lengths.
Despite of the similar rationale of moving alive random walks one-step forward in both of the algorithms,
the arbitrary push operation ordering of
\emph{FwdPush}

makes the analysis of the error bound during the algorithm very challenging.
To overcome this challenge, we conceptually restrict
\emph{FwdPush}
to perform push operations in iterations.

A Special
\emph{FwdPush}
Variant.

As the first step, we reveal the subtle equivalence connection between
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
.
In the following, we show a special variant of
\emph{FwdPush}
which can perform exactly the same computation for
\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}
and
\vec{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}
in
\emph{PowItr}
.
This variant is called Simultaneous Forward Push (
\emph{SimFwdPush}
), and has the following modifications on Algorithm
1
:

•

All the nodes with non-zero residues are active, i.e.,
r_{\max}=0
.

•

The
\emph{SimFwdPush}
algorithm works in iterations:

–

At the beginning of the
(j+1)
-th iteration (for integer
j\geq 0
), the residue of node
v
is denoted by
r^{(j)}(s,v)
.

–

In each iteration, the algorithm performs a push operation on every active node simultaneously based on
r^{(j)}(s,v)
.

•

At the end of the
(j+1)
-th iteration, the algorithm terminates if the
\ell_{1}
-error
r_{sum}=\sum_{v\in V}r^{(j+1)}(s,v)\leq\lambda
.

A Running Example.

Figure
3
shows a running example of
\emph{SimFwdPush}
.
At the beginning of the first iteration, only
v_{1}
has non-zero residue, i.e.,
r^{(0)}(s,v_{1})=1
, and thus, it is the only active node in this iteration.
After the push operation on
v_{1}

r^{(1)}(s,v_{2})=r^{(1)}(s,v_{3})=\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(0)}(s,v_{1})}{d_{v_{1}}}=0.4
.
Hence,
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
are the two active nodes in the second iteration.
The algorithm then performs push operations simultaneously on both
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
,
where the operation on
v_{2}
pushes
\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(1)}(s,v_{2})}{d_{v_{2}}}=0.08
probability mass to each
v_{2}
’s out-neighbor,
while the operation on
v_{3}
pushes
\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(1)}(s,v_{3})}{d_{v_{3}}}=0.16
to its out-neighbors accordingly.
The resulted residue of each node is shown in the figure.

The Connection.

Define
\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}
as the residue vector of all the nodes, whose the
i
-th coordinate is
r^{(j)}(s,v_{i})
.
The crucial observation on
\emph{SimFwdPush}
is that performing simultaneous push operations on all the active nodes in the
(j+1)
-th iteration is equivalent to the following computation:

\displaystyle\vec{r_{s}}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\text{active}\,v}\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)}{d_{v}}\cdot\vec{A}_{v}=\sum_{v\in V}\frac{(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)}{d_{v}}\cdot\vec{A}_{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V}(1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)\cdot\vec{P}_{v}=\sum_{v\in V}\left((1-\alpha)\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)\cdot\vec{e}_{v}\cdot\mathbf{P}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\cdot\left(\sum_{v\in V}r^{(j)}(s,v)\cdot\vec{e}_{v}\right)\cdot\mathbf{P}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{P}\,.

We have the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.1.

The residue vector
\vec{r_{s}}^{(j+1)}
and underestmate PPR vector
\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}
obtained by
\emph{SimFwdPush}
in the
(j+1)
-th iteration are exactly the same as
\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}
and
\vec{\pi}_{s}^{(j+1)}
computed in the
(j+1)
-th iteration
in
\emph{PowItr}
, for all integer
j\geq 0
.

Proof.

We prove this lemma with a mathematical induction argument.
Clearly, the base case
\vec{r_{s}}^{(0)}=\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(0)}=\vec{e}_{s}
and
\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(0)}=\vec{\pi_{s}}^{(0)}=\vec{0}
holds.
For the inductive case, assuming that
\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}=\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}
and
\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j)}=\vec{\pi_{s}}^{(j)}
holds,
by Equation (
4.1
), we have:

\displaystyle\vec{r_{s}}^{(j+1)}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{P}=(1-\alpha)\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j)}\cdot\mathbf{P}=\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(j+1)}\,;

and according to the push operations,

\displaystyle\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}=\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j)}+\alpha\cdot\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\alpha\cdot\vec{r_{s}}^{(k)}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\alpha\cdot\vec{\gamma_{s}}^{(k)}=\vec{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}\,.

Therefore, the inductive case holds, and the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 4.2.

The overall running time of
\emph{SimFwdPush}
is bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
.

Proof.

The cost of each push operation on a node
v
is
O(d_{v})
.
Thus, in each iteration, the total cost is bounded by the total degree
O(\sum_{v\in V}d_{v})=O(m)
.
According to the analysis of
\emph{PowItr}
and Lemma
4.1
, after at most
O(\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
iterations,
the
\ell_{1}
-error
r_{sum}\leq\lambda
.
∎

Putting Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
together,
we conclude that:

\emph{SimFwdPush}
is equivalent to
\emph{PowItr}
.

4.2.
A Tighter Analysis

Unfortunately, the equivalence between
\emph{SimFwdPush}
and
\emph{PowItr}

is not sufficient to answer the open question regarding to the running time of
\emph{FwdPush}
.
The reasons are as follows:

•

First, the push operations in
\emph{SimFwdPush}
are performed simultaneously in each iteration,
while in
\emph{FwdPush}
, they are performed in an asynchronous way.

•

Second, the crucial parameter
r_{\max}
does not make much effect in
\emph{SimFwdPush}
, but it determines which node is eligible for a push operation in
\emph{FwdPush}
.

•

Third, the stop condition in
\emph{SimFwdPush}
that requires
r_{sum}\leq\lambda
is not a sufficient condition to achieve

r(s,v)\leq d_{v}\cdot r_{\max}
for all
v\in V
, where the latter is the original stop condition in
\emph{FwdPush}
.

In this subsection, we remove all these restrictions.
The only requirement in our analysis for
\emph{FwdPush}
is that the algorithm is performed in iterations (just as what
\emph{SimFwdPush}
does and as defined below).
We note that considering the algorithm in iterations makes the entire process more structured
and thus allows us to bound the decrease rate of the
\ell_{1}
-error.
Nonetheless, as mentioned earlier,
such a requirement is not a strong restriction;
and it indeed can be implemented as simple as with a First-In-First-Out queue to organize the active nodes during the algorithm.
Interestingly enough, this is actually a common implementation of
\emph{FwdPush}
in practice –
people have unconsciously implemented
\emph{FwdPush}
in an efficient way!
From our analysis, it explains why
\emph{FwdPush}
is often found to have a weaker dependency on
\lambda
than as what its previous running time complexity suggested in applications.

In the following, we analyse the running time of an implementation of

We answer the open question regarding to the running time of
\emph{FwdPush}

Theorem 4.3.

Given
0<r_{\max}<\frac{1}{2\cdot m}
and

\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}

It should be noted that when
r_{\max}\geq\frac{1}{2\cdot m}

O(\frac{1}{r_{\max}})=O(m)
is already good enough.
Furthermore, as aforementioned, the goal is to obtain high-precision results; the
\lambda

and thus,

is often far smaller than
\frac{1}{2\cdot m}
in practice.

The Iterations.
For the ease of analysis, we first define the iterations of
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
based on Algorithm

.
In particular, we define
S^{(j)}
as the set of all the active nodes at the beginning of the
(j+1)
-th iteration, where
j=0,1,2,\cdots
.
Specifically, we define

in an inductive way:

•

Initially,
S^{(0)}=\{s\}
: the source node is the only active node at the beginning of the first iteration.

•

S^{(j+1)}
is the set of all the nodes appended to
Q
at Line
11

2
when processing the nodes in
S^{(j)}
.

Furthermore, in the
(j+1)
-th iteration, the algorithm performs a push operation for every active node in
S^{(j)}
.

Under this definition, the iterations are exactly the same as those we considered in
\emph{SimFwdPush}
, except that the push operations are now performed in an asynchronous way.
Consider the example in Figure

and assume

is sufficiently small, e.g.,
0.001
;

S^{(0)}

only.
After the push operation on

v_{2}
and

are appended to
Q
; thus,
S^{(1)}=\{v_{2},v_{3}\}
.
In the second iteration, during the push operations on
v_{2}
and
v_{3}
,
all the five nodes are appended to
Q
.
Hence,
S^{(2)}=\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4},v_{5}\}
.

An Overview of the Analysis.

Let
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}=\|\vec{r_{s}}^{(j)}\|_{1}
, the total residues of all the nodes at the beginning of the
(j+1)
-th iteration.
Initially,
r_{\text{sum}}^{(0)}=1
.
According to the analysis for
\emph{FwdPush}
,
we know that
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}
is exactly the
\ell_{1}
-error after the
(j+1)
-th iteration.
When the iteration number is not important, we use
r_{\text{sum}}
to denote the
\ell_{1}
-error at the current state.

Our analysis on the overall running time of
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
consists of two main steps.
Firstly, we show that the following lemma:

Lemma 4.4.

In
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda}+m)
time,
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
can make the
\ell_{1}
-error
r_{\text{sum}}\leq\lambda
.

As aforementioned,
r_{\text{sum}}\leq\lambda
is not sufficient to guarantee that
r(s,v)\leq d_{v}\cdot r_{\max}
holds for all
v\in V
.
Thus, the
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
algorithm may not stop and keep running until there is no more active node.
To bound the running time of this part, in the second step, we prove:

Lemma 4.5.

Starting from the state of
r_{\text{sum}}\leq\lambda
,
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
stops in
O(m)
time.

Theorem
4.3
follows immediately from these two lemmas.
In the rest of this subsection, we prove Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
, respectively.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Consider the
(j+1)
-th iteration;
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
performs a push operation on each node
v\in S^{(j)}
.
For each such push operation, an amount of
\alpha\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)
probability mass is converted to
\hat{\pi}^{(j+1)}(s,v)
, and hence,

r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}
is decreased by
\alpha\cdot r^{(j)}(s,v)
.
Therefore, at the end of this
(j+1)
-th iteration,
the net decrease of
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}
is:

(9)

\small r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}-r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}\geq\alpha\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}r^{(j)}(s,v)\,.

The key in our proof is to show
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}\leq(1-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}d_{v})\cdot r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}
.
To achieve this, we show the following observation.

Observation 1.

\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}r^{(j)}(s,v)\geq\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}d_{v}\cdot r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}\,
.

Proof.

In the following calculation, we omit all the superscripts in
S^{(j)}
,
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}
and the residues
r^{(j)}(s,v)

as they are all with respect to
j
.
Clearly, when
\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}=0
or
\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}=m
, the observation holds. Otherwise, by the definition of active nodes, we have:

\frac{\sum_{v\in S}r(s,v)}{\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}}\geq r_{\max}\geq\frac{\sum_{v\not\in S}r(s,v)}{\sum_{v\not\in S}d_{v}}\,.

Therefore, it follows that:

\frac{\sum_{v\in S}r(s,v)}{\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}}\geq\frac{\sum_{v\in S}r(s,v)+\sum_{v\notin S}r(s,v)}{\sum_{v\in S}d_{v}+\sum_{v\notin S}d_{v}}=\frac{r_{sum}}{m}\,.

The observation follows.
∎

Substituting Observation
1
to Equation (
9
), we have:

\displaystyle r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}

\displaystyle\leq r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}-\alpha\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}r^{(j)}(s,v)\leq(1-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}d_{v})\cdot r_{\text{sum}}^{(j)}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=0}^{j}(1-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(k)}}d_{v})\cdot r_{\text{sum}}^{(0)}

(10)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{k=0}^{j}(\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot\sum_{v\in S^{(k)}}d_{v})\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot(\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{v\in S^{(k)}}d_{v})\right)\,,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
1-x\leq e^{-x}
holds for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
T^{(j+1)}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\sum_{v\in S^{(k)}}d_{v}
be the total degree of the node in each push operation performed in the first
(j+1)
iterations.
By Equation (
4.2
), in order to make
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}\leq\lambda
, it suffices to find the smallest
j
such that

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot T^{(j+1)}\right)\leq\lambda\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{m}\cdot T^{(j)}\right)\,.

Thus, we have:

\displaystyle T^{(j)}\leq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\ln\frac{1}{\lambda}\leq T^{(j+1)}\,.

By the fact that
T^{(j+1)}-T^{(j)}=\sum_{v\in S^{(j)}}d_{v}\leq m
, we further have:

(11)

\displaystyle T^{(j+1)}\leq T^{(j)}+m\leq\frac{m}{\alpha}\cdot\ln\frac{1}{\lambda}+m\,.

Finally, since the cost of a push operation on
v
is bounded by
O(d_{v})
,
thus
O(T^{(j+1)})
is actually an upper bound on the overall cost in the first
(j+1)
iterations.
Therefore, the overall running time to achieve
r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}\leq\lambda
is bounded by
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda}+m)
.
This completes the whole proof for Lemma
4.4
.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Let
r_{\text{sum}}^{\text{before}}=r_{\text{sum}}^{(j+1)}\leq\lambda
be the
r_{\text{sum}}
at the current state, and
r_{\text{sum}}^{\text{after}}
be the
r_{\text{sum}}
when the algorithm terminates.
Recall that each push operation on an active node
v
decreases
r_{\text{sum}}
by
\alpha\cdot r(s,v)\geq\alpha\cdot d_{v}\cdot r_{\max}
, and
the corresponding running time cost is
O(d_{v})
.
Therefore, after paying a total running time cost of
O(T)
, the net decrease of
r_{\text{sum}}
is at least
\alpha\cdot T\cdot r_{\max}
.
As the net decrease is at most
r_{\text{sum}}^{\text{before}}-r_{\text{sum}}^{\text{after}}\leq\lambda
,
it follows that
\alpha\cdot T\cdot r_{\max}
cannot be greater than
\lambda
.
Hence,
T\leq\frac{\lambda}{\alpha\cdot r_{\max}}=O(m)
.
Thus, the largest possible running time of
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
starting from the state of
r_{\text{sum}}\leq\lambda
is bounded by
O(m)
.
Lemma
4.5
thus follows.

5.
A New Efficient Power Iteration

In the previous section, we show that: (i)
\emph{PowItr}
is equivalent to a special variant of
\emph{FwdPush}
,
and (ii) a simple implementation
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
of
\emph{FwdPush}
can achieve time complexity
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
.
Based on these theoretical findings, in this section, we design an efficient implementation of
\emph{PowItr}
, call Power Iteration with Forward Push (
\emph{PowerPush}
), from an engineering point of view.
Our optimizations in the design of
\emph{PowerPush}
unifies the global-approach
\emph{PowItr}
and local-approach
\emph{FwdPush}
and incorporates both their strengths.

We introduce some crucial optimizations in
\emph{PowerPush}

Asynchronous Pushes.

Unlike
\emph{PowItr}
,
our
\emph{PowerPush}
uses asynchronous push operations.
We note that asynchronous push operations can be possibly more effective.
This is because during the
(j+1)
-th iteration, if there is a push operation on an in-neighbor
u
of a node
v
before the push of
v
,
when
v
pushes, its current residue is greater than
r^{(j)}(s,v)
, and hence, this push operation can send out more residue.
To see this, in the second iteration in Figure

is performed based on a residue of
0.4

but in the same iteration in Figure

v_{2}
is based on a residue of
0.56
.
This is because
v_{3}
pushed before
v_{2}

v_{2}

0.16

Moreover, after this asynchronous push, the residue of
v_{2}
becomes
0
in the next iteration, while in contrast,
v_{2}
still has
0.16
(obtained from
v_{3}
) under the simultaneous pushes.
In other words, this asynchronous push on
v_{2}
has equivalently processed the residues of
v_{2}
in two iterations under simultaneous pushes.

Global Sequential Scan v.s. Local Random Access.

One of the biggest optimizations in
\emph{PowerPush}
is the strategy of switching to a global sequential scan from using the queue to access active nodes.
The key observation is that after a few iterations, in
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
, there would be a large number of active nodes which are stored in the queue according to their “append-to-queue” order.
As a result, to perform push operations on these nodes, it requires a large number of random access in both the node list and the edge list, incurring a substantial overhead.

To remedy this, in
\emph{PowerPush}
, when the current number of active nodes is greater than a specified
scanThreshold
, it switches to sequential scan the node list to perform push operations on the active nodes (as shown in Algorithm
3
Line
15
-
24
).
Moreover, to further facilitate this idea,
\emph{PowerPush}
stores all the nodes sorted by id’s and concatenates the adjacent lists of the nodes in the same order (i.e., sorted by id’s) in a large array.
Thanks to this storage format, in each iteration,
\emph{PowerPush}
can perform push operations on active nodes via a sequential scan on this edge array, which in turn has largely make the memory access patterns become cache-friendly.
Interestingly, this idea is borrowed from the implementation of
\emph{PowItr}
as a global-approach.

Dynamic
\ell_{1}
-Error Threshold.

Another optimization worth mentioning is the strategy of using dynamic
\ell_{1}
-error threshold (see Line

The rationale here is that with a larger

We note that

To see this, recall that a push operation on

by
\alpha\cdot r(s,v)
.
Thus,
\alpha\cdot r(s,v)/d_{v}
can be considered as the unit-cost benefit of this operation.
By definition, a node becomes active only if a push operation on it has unit-cost benefit
\geq\alpha\cdot r_{\max}
.
The good thing of performing push operations with higher unit-cost benefits first is that
it allows other nodes to accumulate their residues before pushing.
In this way, the number of push operations to achieve

Motivated by this, we perform

6.
Improved Approx-SSPPR Algorithm

In this section, we propose a new algorithm, called
\emph{SpeedPPR}
, for answering approximate SSPPR queries.

6.1.
Preliminaries on Approx-SSPPR

In this subsection, we first introduce some preliminaries on two relevant algorithms:

\emph{MonteCarlo}
and
\emph{FORA}
.
The ideas of these two algorithms would help understand the key idea of the design of our
\emph{SpeedPPR}
.
Recall that an Approx-SSPPR query aims to compute an estimation
\hat{\pi}(s,v)
for every node
v
with
\pi(s,v)\geq\mu
within relative error
\epsilon
with a succeed probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
.

The Monte Carlo Method.

Perhaps, one of the most straightforward ways to answer Approx-SSPPR query is the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method.
The basic idea is to generate
W
independent
\alpha
-random walks from
s
, and utilise the empirical number
f(s,v)
out of these random walks that stop at a node
v
to estimate its expectation
\pi(s,v)\cdot W
.
Thus,
\hat{\pi}(s,v)=\frac{f(s,v)}{W}
gives an estimation of
\pi(s,v)
.
By the standard Chernoff Bound
(
Chung and Lu 2006
)
, it is known that setting

(12)

\displaystyle W=\frac{2\cdot(2\cdot\epsilon/3+2)\cdot\log n}{\epsilon^{2}\cdot\mu}=O(\frac{\log n}{\epsilon^{2}\cdot\mu})

suffices to obtain a correct estimation for every node
v
with
\pi(s,v)\geq\mu
with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
.
Furthermore, as the expected length of an
\alpha
-random walk is at most
\frac{1}{\alpha}
,
the overall expected running time of
\emph{MonteCarlo}
is bounded by
O(\frac{\log n}{\epsilon^{2}\cdot\mu})
.
When
\mu=\frac{1}{n}
, this bound can be written as
O(\frac{n\cdot\log n}{\epsilon^{2}})
.

In the rest of this section, without loss of generality, we assume that
m<W
, because otherwise, i.e.,
m\geq W
,
one can always switch their algorithm to the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method and guarantee a time complexity
O(W)
no worse than
O(m)
.

FORA.

\emph{FORA}

(
Wang
et al. 2017
)
is a state-of-the-art representative algorithm for answering Approx-SSPPR queries.
It adopts a two-phase framework and combines
\emph{FwdPush}
and
\emph{MonteCarlo}
.
In the first phase, it runs
\emph{FwdPush}
with a specified
r_{\max}
(whose value is to be determined shortly)
to obtain an estimation
\hat{\pi_{s}}
of
\vec{\pi_{s}}
with
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}-\vec{\pi_{s}}\|_{1}=r_{\text{sum}}=\sum_{v\in V}r(s,v)\leq m\cdot r_{\max}
.
In the second phase, it performs the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method.
Specifically, it works as follows.
For each node
v
with
r(s,v)>0
,
\emph{FORA}
generates
W_{v}=\lceil r(s,v)\cdot W\rceil
random walks from
v
,
where
W
is set by Equation (
12
).
Among these
W_{v}
random walks from
v
, if
f(v,u)
out of them had stopped at a node
u
, then increase
\hat{\pi}(s,u)
by:

(13)

\displaystyle\frac{r(s,v)\cdot W}{W_{v}}\cdot\frac{f(v,u)}{W}=r(s,v)\cdot\frac{f(v,u)}{W_{v}}\,.

In summary, the final estimation
\hat{\pi}^{\prime}(s,u)
of
\pi(s,u)
is computed as:

(14)

\hat{\pi}^{\prime}(s,u)=\hat{\pi}(s,u)+\sum_{v\in V:r(s,v)>0}r(s,v)\cdot\frac{f(v,u)}{W_{v}}\,,

where
\hat{\pi}(s,u)
is obtained in the first
\emph{FwdPush}
phase, and the second term is the net increase based on
\hat{\pi}(s,u)
in the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
phase.

Running Time Analysis.

According to the previous bound on the running time of
\emph{FwdPush}
, the cost of the first phase in
\emph{FORA}
is bounded by
O(\frac{1}{r_{\max}})
;
and in the second phase,
\emph{FORA}
needs to generate at most
\sum_{v\in V:r(s,v)>0}\lceil r(s,v)\cdot W\rceil\leq r_{\text{sum}}\cdot W+n\leq m\cdot r_{\max}\cdot W+n
random walks.
Therefore, the overall expected time of
\emph{FORA}
is bounded by
O(\frac{1}{r_{\max}}+m\cdot r_{\max}\cdot W+n)
,
which can be minimized to
O(\sqrt{m\cdot W}+n)
by setting
r_{\max}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m\cdot W}}
.
When
\mu=\frac{1}{n}
and the graph is scale-free, i.e.,
m=O(n\cdot\log n)
,
this bound can be further simplified to
O(\frac{n\cdot\log n}{\epsilon})
.
In this case,
\emph{FORA}
improves the
\emph{MonteCarlo}
method by a factor of
\frac{1}{\epsilon}
,
Furthermore, this
O(\frac{n\cdot\log n}{\epsilon})
-bound is actually state-of-the-art;
none of the existing algorithms can overcome this barrier.

Pre-Computing the Random Walks.

An optimization of
\emph{FORA}
is to pre-compute
K_{v}
random walks for each node
v\in V
, where
K_{v}=d_{v}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{W}{m}}+1\geq W_{v}
;
when answering a query, it just needs to read the pre-computed random walk results to perform the second
\emph{MonteCarlo}
phase.
Therefore, the actual query cost can be further reduced.
Such an index-based variant is called
\emph{FORA}
+.
The space consumption of all the pre-computed random walk results is
\sum_{v\in V}K_{v}=\sqrt{m\cdot W}+n
.
When
m=O(n\cdot\log n)
, this gives the overall space consumption bound
O(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cdot n\cdot\log n)
.
Unfortunately, as the number of pre-computed random walks for each node depends on
W
and hence on the relative error
\epsilon
,
the index of
\emph{FORA}
+ constructed for an
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}
is not sufficient to answer queries with relative error
\epsilon_{2}<\epsilon_{1}
.
Moreover, to support queries with small
\epsilon
, the index requires a substantial space consumption.
These drawbacks have significantly limited the applicability of
\emph{FORA}
+.

6.2.
Our Improved Algorithm

Next, we propose a new Approx-SSPPR algorithm, called
\emph{SpeedPPR}
, which not only improves
\emph{FORA}
’s running time complexity, but also admits an index with size independent to
\epsilon

While it eventually turns out that

is as simple as substituting
\emph{PowerPush}
along with a
O(m)

the first phase of
\emph{FORA}

it is our new

\emph{FORA}

8.1.
Evaluations of High-Precision SSPPR

In this experiment, we evaluate the high-precision SSPPR algorithms.
For
\emph{PowItr}
,
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
and
\emph{PowerPush}
, we set the
\ell_{1}
-error threshold
\lambda=\min\{10^{-8},1/m\}
.

\emph{BePI}
adopts a different error measurement, which is to compute the
\ell_{2}
distance between the obtained results in two consecutive iterations, namely,
\|\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}-\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j)}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{v\in V}\left(\hat{\pi}^{(j+1)}(s,v)-\hat{\pi}^{(j)}(s,v))\right)^{2}}
;
when this
\ell_{2}
distance is no more than a specified convergence parameter
\Delta
, it considers the current result
\hat{\pi_{s}}^{(j+1)}
converges and thus stops.
For
\emph{BePI}
, we set
\Delta=\min\{10^{-8},1/m\}
.
It should be noted that under this setting of
\Delta
,
the results obtained by
\emph{BePI}
do not necessarily meet the requirement that
the
\ell_{1}
-error (with respect to the ground truth
\vec{\pi_{s}}
) is at most
\lambda
.
Therefore, its running time reported in the following experiments is an underestimate of
\emph{BePI}
’s actual time to achieve the
\ell_{1}
-error
\lambda
.

Moreover, among all these four competitors,
\emph{BePI}
is the only one that requires pre-computed index.
Table
2
shows the pre-processing time and the index space consumption of
\emph{BePI}

.

takes
57,988
seconds (over
15
hours) to compute the index on Orkut and
6,180
seconds on Twitter, which consume
54.5

This is because
\emph{BePI}
is a matrix-based algorithm and thus affected heavily by the density of the graph.
As shown in Table

35.3
.
Hence, the pre-processing time (rsp. index size) of the former is significantly longer (rsp. larger) than that of the latter.

Figure

Among the three algorithms, the proposed
\emph{PowerPush}

This is because the dynamic threshold optimization enables
\emph{PowerPush}
to “accumulate” the residues of the nodes before pushing.

Of interest is Orkut, in which
\emph{PowerPush}

\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}

\emph{PowerPush}
requires much less time than
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
on the same dataset.
The reason is that the global sequential scan technique makes the memory access pattern in
\emph{PowerPush}
more cache-friendly and hence more efficient to perform pushes.
Similar observation can also be found in the comparison between
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FIFO-FwdPush}
on Orkut, where

For the index-based

Actual
\ell_{1}
-Error v.s.
\epsilon
.

Finally, we study the solution quality of the approximate algorithms.
Figure
8
shows the
\ell_{1}
-error with respect to the ground truth
\vec{\pi_{s}}
which is computed with
\emph{PowerPush}
by setting
\lambda=10^{-17}

Except on the dataset web-Stanford,
\emph{SpeedPPR}
offers the best solution quality.
When
\epsilon
is small, its solution quality could be an order of magnitude better than other algorithms.
This is impressive, considering it just takes comparable running time of
\emph{FORA-Index}
.
Another observation is that both
\emph{SpeedPPR-Index}
and
\emph{FORA-Index}
provide inferior solutions compared to the index-free algorithms.
The reason for this is that the index-based algorithms tend to use more random walks as the walks can be performed with a relatively small cost.
These algorithms thus spend less time on the local push phase, which actually computes the estimation deterministically.
As a result, the random walks are performed based on a larger
r_{\text{sum}}
leading to a larger variance in the estimations.

9.
Conclusion

In this paper, we show an equivalent connection between the two fundamental algorithms
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
.
Embarking from this connection, we further prove that the time complexity of a common
\emph{FwdPush}
implementation is
O(m\cdot\log\frac{1}{\lambda})
, where
\lambda
is the
\ell_{1}
-error threshold.
This answers the long-standing open question regarding the time complexity of
\emph{FwdPush}
in the dependency on
\lambda
.
Based on this finding, we propose a new implementation of
\emph{PowItr}
, called
\emph{PowerPush}
, which incorporates both the strengths of
\emph{PowItr}
and
\emph{FwdPush}
.
Furthermore, we propose a new algorithm, called
\emph{SpeedPPR}
for answering approximate single-source PPR queries.
The expected time complexity of
\emph{SpeedPPR}
is
O(n\log n\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})
on scale-free graphs, improving the state-of-the-art
O(\frac{n\log n}{\epsilon})
-bound.
In addition,
\emph{SpeedPPR}
admits an index with size always at most
O(m)
independent on
\epsilon
.
Our experimental results show that our
\emph{PowerPush}
and
\emph{SpeedPPR}
outperform their state-of-the-art competitors by up to an order of magnitude in all evaluation metrics.
