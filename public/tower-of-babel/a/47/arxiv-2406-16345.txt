Abstract
. Highly localized kernels based on orthogonal polynomials have been studied and utilized over several regular domains. Much of the results deduced via these kernels can be treated uniformly in the framework of localizable spaces of homogeneous type. We clarify the concept of localness and provide a list of such localizable spaces.

41A10, 41A63, 42C10, 42C40

1.
Introduction

Highly localized kernels based on orthogonal polynomials have been instrumental in studying various problems
in approximation theory and computational analysis over regular domains in the past two decades. Such kernels
have near-exponential decay off the diagonal and also serve as kernels for integral operators that are polynomials
of near-best approximation on the domain, which makes them ideal tools for studying several problems in
approximation theory and harmonic analysis. They have been established for weighted function spaces on the
interval, the unit sphere, the unit ball, and the simplex in the Euclidean spaces

[
3
,
13
,
14
,
9
,
10
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
as well as on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

[
12
,
22
]
, and more recently, on the conic domains
[
28
,
30
]
.

In the literature, much of the analysis based on highly localized kernels is carried out domain by domain, relying on
the geometry of each domain. Recently, in
[
28
]
, a framework was developed that provides a unified treatment
for all compact domains that are equipped with highly localized kernels. In this expository paper, we aim to clarify
the concept of localization and explain the essence of the framework.

The setup is on the space of homogeneous type, which is a measure space
(\Omega,\mu,{\mathsf{d}})
on the set
\Omega

with a positive measure
\mu
and a metric
{\mathsf{d}}
such that all open balls

B(x,r)=\{y\in\Omega:{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)<r\}

are measurable and
\mu
is a regular measure satisfying the doubling property

\mu(B(x,2r))\leq c\mu(B(x,r)),\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,\quad\forall r>0,

where
c
is independent of
x
and
r
. Such a measure
\mu
is called a doubling measure. In our setup, the
domain
\Omega
will be either an algebraic surface or a domain with a non-empty interior, and

\mathrm{d}\mu={\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}x
with
{\mathsf{w}}
being a nonnegative weight function defined on
\Omega
that defines a
well-established inner product

(1.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{w}}}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}x

on the space of polynomials restricted to
\Omega
. We shall write the homogeneous space as
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})

with
{\mathsf{w}}
being a doubling weight, and we write
{\mathsf{w}}(E)=\int_{E}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{w}}
for the subset
E\subset\Omega
.

We need kernels defined in terms of orthogonal polynomials. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathsf{w}})
be the space of orthogonal
polynomials of degree
n
with respect to this inner product. Let
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
be the reproducing kernel
of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathsf{w}})
. The orthogonal projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}:L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathsf{w}})

can be written as

(1.2)

(\operatorname{proj}_{n}f)(x)=\int_{\Omega}P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)f(y){\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}y,\qquad f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}}).

Let
\widehat{a}
be a cut-off function, defined as a compactly supported nonnegative function in
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
such that

\widehat{a}(t)=1
for
0\leq t\leq 1
and
\widehat{a}(t)=0
for
t\geq 2
. Then our highly localized kernels are defined by

(1.3)

L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y):=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)P_{j}({\mathsf{w}};x,y).

The kernel is regarded as highly localized if it satisfies an upper-bound estimate that contains a factor

(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{-{\kappa}}
for any given
{\kappa}>0
, which means that the kernel decays faster than any polynomial
power away from the diagonal
x=y
; see the next section for a precise definition.

We call
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
a
localizable
space of homogeneous type if it possesses highly localized
kernels. For such a space, the framework in
[
28
]
allows us to establish several results in approximation
theory and harmonic analysis in weighted function spaces, such as
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
, that include a
characterization of best approximation by polynomials and localized tight frames, among other results. In other words,
several results can be regarded as properties of localizable space of homogeneous type. The definition of
highly localized kernels in
[
28
]
requires three assertions that need to hold. As we shall show in the next section,
one of the assertions is redundant as it holds for every doubling weight.

At the moment, the localizable spaces of homogenous type are known only for a few regular domains. This is
not surprising given the complexity of multivariate orthogonal polynomials and, even more so, the kernel

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
. Most highly localized kernels are established when a closed-form formula for the
reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
is known, which we call an
addition formula
, taking a cure
from the addition formula for the spherical harmonics. The existence of such a formula is rare and reflects
something fundamental about the localizable space of homogeneous type. We shall provide a list of examples
of localizable spaces of homogeneous type.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide a precise definition of highly localized
kernels and, as a consequence, localizable space of homogeneous type, and a sketch of some results that
can be established with the help of such kernels. In the third section, we provide a list of examples of known
localizable spaces.

2.
Localized space of homogeneous type

2.1.
Orthogonal structure and localized kernels

Let
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
be a space of homogeneous type associated with the inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}

defined in (
1.1
). The dimension of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathsf{w}})
of orthogonal polynomials depends on the
geometry of
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For our purpose, we only consider two cases. The first case is when the interior
of
\Omega
is an open set, such as the unit ball or the simplex in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for which

(2.1)

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})=\binom{n+d-1}{n},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

The second case is when the domain
\Omega
is a quadratic algebraic surface of the form
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\phi(x)=0\}
,
where
\phi
is a quadratic polynomial, such as the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
with
\phi(x)=1-\|x\|^{2}
, for which

(2.2)

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})=\binom{n+d-2}{n}+\binom{n+d-3}{n-1},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots.

We assume that
{\mathsf{w}}
is regular so that the orthogonal decomposition

L^{2}(\Omega,\varpi)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}}):\qquad f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}f

holds, where
\operatorname{proj}_{n}
is the orthogonal projection operator. This gives the Fourier orthogonal decomposition of
f
since,
if
P_{\nu,n}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\varpi)
, then

\operatorname{proj}_{n}f=\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})}\widehat{f}_{\nu,n}P_{\nu,n},\qquad\widehat{f}_{\nu,n}:={\langle}f,P_{\nu,n}{\rangle}_{\mathsf{w}}.

The reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
is the kernel of the operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}
,
as shown in (
1.2
), which can be written as

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})}P_{\nu,n}(x)P_{\nu,n}(y)

in terms of any orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\varpi)
. In the multidimensional setting, orthogonal polynomials are
complicated and described by multiple indices even when they can be written down, and the kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)

is a sum of multiple layers of products of polynomials. Such a sum can hardly be useful for obtaining estimates
of kernels like
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})
defined in (
1.3
).

In all cases when highly localized kernels are known, their desired upper bound is established with the help of
an addition formula for the reproducing kernel. In most of the cases, the addition formula is of the form

(2.3)

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}Z_{n}(\xi(x,y;u))\mathrm{d}\mu(u)

where
Z_{n}
is a polynomial, often orthogonal polynomial, of one variable,
\xi:(x,y;z)\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
for

x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\mathrm{d}\mu
is a measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, which can degenerate to point evaluations. The
simplest example is the unit sphere with orthogonal polynomials being spherical harmonics with respect to the
surface measure, for which the closed-form formula is given by

(2.4)

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle})\quad\hbox{with}\quad{\lambda}=\frac{d-2}{2},

where
C_{n}^{\lambda}
is the Gegenbauer polynomial of degree
n
(see Subsection
3.2
below). This
formula is called the
addition formula
for spherical harmonics, a name that we adopt for closed-formula of
reproducing kernels in all other cases. If an addition formula of the form (
2.3
) exists, then the kernel

L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
defined in (
1.3
) can be written as

L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}L_{n}\big(\xi(x,y;\mu)\big)\mathrm{d}\mu(u),

where
L_{n}
on the right-hand side is a function of one variable given by

L_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)Z_{j}(t),

which allows us to deduce an upper bound of the kernel
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
from that of
L_{n}
.

We should emphasize that the addition formula is something special. If it is of the form (
2.3
), then it
indicates that the reproducing kernel, hence the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}f
, has an implicit one-dimensional
structure, which allows us to relate the study of the Fourier orthogonal series to that of the Fourier orthogonal series
in one variable. For example, it is well-known that a substantial portion of the Fourier series on the unit sphere
is reduced to that of the Fourier-Gegenbauer series. The same holds for other regular domains and for any
space of homogeneous type that possesses an addition formula (cf.
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
).

2.2.
Highly localized kernels

Let
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
be a space of homogeneous type. The existing examples of the highly localized
kernel suggests the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

The kernels
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, are called highly localized if they satisfy the following
assertions:

Assertion 1
. For
{\kappa}>0
and
x,y\in\Omega
,

|L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x,n^{-1})\right)}\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(y,n^{-1})\right)}\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\right)^{\kappa}}.

Assertion 2
. For
0<\delta\leq\delta_{0}
with some
\delta_{0}<1
and
x_{1}\in B(x_{2},\frac{\delta}{n})
,

\left|L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x_{1},y)-L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x_{2},y)\right|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{n{\mathsf{d}}(x_{1},x_{2})}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x_{1},n^{-1})\right)}\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x_{2},n^{-1})\right)}\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x_{2},y)\right)^{\kappa}}.

This definition contains one less assertion in comparison with the definition given in
[
28
]
. The third
assertion contained in
[
28
]
implies that the first two assertions are sharp. For example, it implies that

(2.5)

\int_{\Omega}\left|L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)\right|^{p}{\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)\leq c\left[{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B\!\left(x,n^{-1}\right)\right)\right]^{1-p},

where
\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\Omega
, which is frequently needed when we utilize
the highly localized kernels. The third assertion imposes a condition on the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
:

Assertion 3
. For sufficient large
{\kappa}>0
, there is a constant
c_{\kappa}>0
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{{\mathsf{w}}(y)}{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(y,n^{-1})\right)\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{{\kappa}}}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)\leq c_{\kappa}.

This assertion is stated in
[
28
]
as part of the definition for
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})
being a highly localized kernel.
It is, however, redundant since it holds for all doubling weight.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
is a space of homogeneous type, then Assertion 3 holds.

Proof.

It is known that the doubling weight
{\mathsf{w}}
satisfies

(2.6)

\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{-{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})}\leq\frac{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B\left(y,n^{-1}\right)\right)}{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B\left(x,n^{-1}\right)\right)}\leq\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})},

where
{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})>0
is a positive constant. Fix
n
and define
\Omega_{0,n}(x)=B(x,n^{-1})
and

\Omega_{m,n}(x)=\left\{y:2^{m-1}n^{-1}\leq{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\leq 2^{m}n^{-1}\right\},\qquad m=1,2,\ldots.

Then
\Omega=\cup_{m=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{m,n}(x)
. Let
J_{n}
denote the left-hand side of the inequality in Assertion 3.
Using (
2.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{n}\,

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega_{m,n}(x)}\frac{{\mathsf{w}}(y)}{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(y,n^{-1})\right)\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{{\kappa}}}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x,n^{-1})\right)}\int_{\Omega_{m,n}(x)}\frac{{\mathsf{w}}(y)}{\big(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\big)^{{\kappa}-{\alpha}(w)}}\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y).

Since
{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\geq 2^{m-1}n^{-1}
and
\Omega_{m,n}(x)=B(x,2^{m}n^{-1})\setminus B(x,2^{m-1}n^{-1})
, it follows that

\displaystyle J_{n}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x,n^{-1})\right)}\frac{{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(x,2^{m}n^{-1})\right)}{(2^{m-1})^{{\kappa}-{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})}}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(c_{\mathsf{w}})^{m}}{(1+2^{m-1})^{{\kappa}-{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})}}\leq\frac{1}{2^{{\kappa}-{\alpha}({\mathsf{w}})}-c_{\mathsf{w}}}

where we have used the doubling condition of
{\mathsf{w}}
, with
c_{\mathsf{w}}
denoting the smallest constant for the doubling condition,
in the second inequality. Choosing
{\kappa}
large shows that
J_{n}
is bounded.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

A space
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
of homogeneous type is called localizable if it possesses highly localized kernels for
one doubling weight
\varpi
on
\Omega
.

This definition is given in
[
28
]
, where several problems are studied in a localizable space of homogeneous type,
with few additional assumptions, and it provides a uniform treatment of analysis on several regular domains that
are derived domain by domain in the literature. As a consequence, as soon as we can show a space of homogeneous
type is localizable, a variety of results will follow right away. This is the motivation for the study in
[
28
]
, where the
framework is developed and applied to new spaces over conic domains.

It is worthwhile to emphasize that we need highly localized kernel only for
one
doubling weight
\varpi
on

\Omega
in order for
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
being localizable. Indeed, most of the results in
[
28
,
30
]
hold
for all doubling weight in a localizable space.

2.3.
Analysis in localizable space of homogeneous type

The highly localized kernels have been an important tool for several problems in approximation theory and harmonic
analysis. Here we briefly outline a few of them to illustrate their usefulness.

2.3.1.
Best approximation by polynomials

Let
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
be the operator defined in (
1.3
) via a cut-off function
\widehat{a}
, which
is a resampling of the kernel
P_{2n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
. We can define an integral operator that has

L_{n}\big(\varpi;\cdot,\cdot)
as its kernel. For convenience, we denote the operator by
L_{n}(\varpi)*f
in the
notation of a pseudo convolution and define it as

(2.7)

L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})*f(x)=\int_{\Omega}f(y)L_{n}\big({\mathsf{w}};x,y){\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y).

The definition of the cut-off function
\widehat{a}
shows that
L_{n}*f=f
, whenever
f
is a polynomial of degree at most

n
, and
L_{n}*f
is a polynomial of degree at most
2n
. If
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})
were highly localized, then
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})*f

should be a good approximation to the function
f
. This is indeed the case. Let
E_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
denote the
error of the best polynomial approximation of degree at most
n
to
f
,

E_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}:=\int_{P\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)}\|f-P\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\qquad 1\leq p\leq\infty,

where
\|\cdot\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
denotes the norma of
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and
\Pi_{n}(\Omega)
denote the space of
polynomials restricted on
\Omega
. Then
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})*f
is a near-best polynomial
of best approximation, as specified in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
be a localizable space of homogeneous type. Let
f\in L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
,
1\leq p<\infty

and
f\in C(\Omega)
for
p=\infty
. Then the operator
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})*f
is bounded in
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
and

\|f-L_{n}*f\|_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}\leq c_{p}E_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}},\quad 1\leq p\leq\infty.

If
Z_{n}
in the addition formula (
2.3
) is a normalized Jacobi polynomial given by

Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)}{h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}},

where
h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is the
L^{2}
norm of
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
, which holds for several domains as will be
seen in the next section, then we can define a translation operator
S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}
by

(2.8)

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};S_{{\theta},{\mathsf{w}}}f)=\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(\cos{\theta})}{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};f),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

This is a bounded operator and satisfies, in particular, that

\|S_{{\theta},\varpi}f\|_{p,\varpi}\leq\|f\|_{p,\varpi}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\lim_{{\theta}\to 0}\|S_{{\theta},\varpi}f-f\|_{\varpi,p}=0.

for
f\in L^{p}(\varpi;\Omega)
if
1\leq p\leq\infty
, or
f\in C(\Omega)
if
p=\infty
. These properties allow us to
define a modulus of smoothness in the localizable space of homogeneous type. For
r>0
, we define the
r
-th
difference operator

\triangle_{{\theta},\varpi}^{r}=\left(I-S_{{\theta},\varpi}\right)^{r/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\binom{r/2}{n}(S_{{\theta},\varpi})^{n},

where
I
denotes the identity operator, in the distribution sense. Then the modulus smoothness is defined by

(2.9)

\omega_{r}(f,t)_{p,\varpi}:=\sup_{0<\theta\leq t}\left\|\triangle_{{\theta},\varpi}^{r}f\right\|_{p,\varpi},\quad 0<t<\pi,

which satisfies the usual properties of the modulus of smoothness. Furthermore, it can be used to prove both
direct and inverse theorems in a characterization of the best approximation
E_{n}(f)_{p,{\mathsf{w}}}
by polynomials.
We refer to
[
28
]
for details.

2.3.2.
Positive cubature rules and Christoffel functions

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight on
\Omega
. A cubature rule of degree
n
for
{\mathsf{w}}
is a finite linear
combination of point evaluations that satisfies

\int_{\Omega}f(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\lambda}_{k}f(x_{k}),\qquad\forall f\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega),

where
{\lambda}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
x_{k}\in\Omega
. The cubature rule is called positive if all
{\lambda}_{k}
are positive.
The positivity is important for numerical stability and for many applications. Positive cubature rules exist
if the set of its nodes is well separated as defined below.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Xi
be a discrete set in
\Omega
.

(a)

A finite collection of subsets
\{S_{z}:z\in\Xi\}
is called a partition of
\Omega
if
S_{z}^{\circ}\cap S_{y}^{\circ}=\emptyset
when
z\neq y
and
\Omega=\bigcup_{z\in\Xi}S_{z}
.

(b)

Let
{\varepsilon}>0
. A discrete subset
\Xi
of
\Omega
is called
{\varepsilon}
-separated if
{\mathsf{d}}(x,y)\geq{\varepsilon}

for every two distinct points
x,y\in\Xi
.

(c)

\Xi
is called maximal if there is a constant
c_{d}>1
such that

1\leq\sum_{z\in\Xi}\chi_{B(z,{\varepsilon})}(x)\leq c_{d},\qquad\forall x\in\Omega,

where
\chi_{E}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
E
.

The item (c) implies
\Omega=\bigcup_{z\in\Xi}B(z,{\varepsilon})
. The positive cubature rules have been studied
by many authors and in various domains. It takes the following form.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight on
\Omega
. Let
\Xi

be a maximum
\frac{\delta}{n}
-separated subset of
\Omega
. There is a
\delta_{0}>0

such that for
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
there exist positive numbers
{\lambda}_{z}
,
z\in\Xi
, so that

(2.10)

\int_{\Omega}f(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)=\sum_{z\in\Xi}{\lambda}_{z}f(z),\qquad\forall f\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega).

Moreover, the weight
{\lambda}_{z}
satisfies
{\lambda}_{z}\geq c_{1}{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(z,\tfrac{\delta}{n})\right)
.

This theorem is first proved for the unit sphere
[
17
,
18
]
. The proof is standard by now and
can be easily adapted to any space of homogeneous type. The existence of positive
{\lambda}_{z}
in (
2.10
)
is based on the Farkas lemma and uses the Marcinkiewicz-Zygmund inequality. The proof latter
inequality does not rely on highly localized kernels, nor is the proof of the above theorem. Thus,
these results are properties for the space of homogeneous type. With such generality, they have
become a useful tool for the discretization of
L^{p}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
for fairly general
\Omega
and
{\mathsf{w}}
.

In the case that the space is localizable, we can utilize the highly localized kernels to provide a matching
upper bound for the coefficients
{\lambda}_{z}
via the Christoffel function, which plays an important role in
studying the tight polynomial frame in the next subsection. Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight on
\Omega
.
The Christoffel function
{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot)
is defined by

(2.11)

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}g(x)=1\\
g\in\Pi_{n}(\Omega)\end{subarray}}\int_{\Omega}|g(x)|^{2}{\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x).

The function is of interest in its own right and has various applications. Recall that
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)

denote the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
. it is known
[
5
, Theorem 3.6.6.]
that, for

n=0,1,2,\ldots
,

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x)=\frac{1}{K_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,x)},\qquad K_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{k}({\mathsf{w}};x,y).

We need to obtain lower and upper bound for
{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x)
. If
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
is localizable, then the
lower bound follows immediately from the highly localized kernel
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}})
, since

K_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,x)\leq L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,x)\leq c\,{\mathsf{w}}\big(B(x,n^{-1})\big)

where the second inequality follows from setting
y=x
in Assertion 1, which is equivalent to

{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x)\geq c{\mathsf{w}}\big(B(x,n^{-1})\big)
by (
2.12
). For the upper bound, we can use
(
2.11
) by selecting a particular function
g(y)
that is localized at
x
, which we choose as
a fast decaying polynomial. In one variable, fast decaying polynomials are studied in
[
11
]
, see also

[
23
]
, which leads to fast decaying polynomials on the unit sphere by the addition formula. For the
unit ball, such polynomials are constructed in
[
21
]
. In the space of homogeneous type, we assume
that such polynomials exist by making the following assertion.

Assertion 4
.
Let
\Omega
be compact. For each
x\in\Omega
, there is a nonnegative
polynomial
T_{x}
of degree at most
n
that satisfies

(1)

T_{x}(x)=1
,
T_{x}(y)\geq\delta>0
for
y\in B(x,\frac{1}{n})
for some
\delta
independent of
n
,
and, for each
{\gamma}>1
,

0\leq T_{x}(y)\leq c_{\gamma}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{-{\gamma}},\qquad y\in\Omega;

(2)

there is a polynomial
q_{n}
such that
q_{n}(x)T_{x}(y)
is a polynomial of degree at most
rn
,
for some positive integer
r
, in
x
-variable and
c_{1}\leq q_{n}(x)\leq c_{2}
for
x\in\Omega
for some
positive numbers
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

This was formulated in
[
28
]
and used to establish the following
[
28
, Lemma 2.1.5]
:

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume Assertion 4. Let
{\alpha}>0
be a positive number and let
x\in\Omega
be fixed. For a doubling
weight
{\mathsf{w}}
on
\Omega
, there is a polynomial
Q_{x}
of degree
n
such that, for all
y\in\Omega
,

(2.13)

c_{1}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{{\alpha}}{\mathsf{w}}(B(y,\tfrac{1}{n}))\leq Q_{x}(y)\leq c_{2}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}(x,y))^{{\alpha}}{\mathsf{w}}(B(y,\tfrac{1}{n})),

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive constant independent of
n
,
x
and
y
.

The inequality (
2.13
) shows that
Q_{x}
is highly localized and decays near-exponentially
away from
y=x
. Using this lemma, we obtain an upper bound of
{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}})
and, in particular, a matching
upper bound for
{\lambda}_{z}
in Theorem
2.6
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
be a localizable space of homogeneous type and assume Assertion 4. Then

{\lambda}_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x)\sim{\mathsf{w}}\big(B(y,\tfrac{1}{n})\big).

Moreover, the coefficients
{\lambda}_{z}
in the cubature rule (
2.10
) satisfies

{\lambda}_{z}\sim{\mathsf{w}}\!\left(B(z,\tfrac{\delta}{n})\right)
.

It is shown in
[
28
,
30
]
that this theorem holds for conic domains.

2.3.3.
Localized polynomial tight frames

The study of such tight frames starts with the unit sphere in
[
18
]
, where they are called
needlelits
,
and then studied for several regular domains, such as (cf.
[
3
,
4
,
12
,
9
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
), and they
have been applied in both computational analysis (cf.
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
,
15
,
24
]
) and in
decomposition of functions spaces (cf.
[
13
,
14
,
10
,
19
,
22
]
). We follow the framework
in
[
28
]
.

Let
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})
be a localizable homogeneous space. We consider a highly localized kernel

L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};x,y)
with its cut-off function
\widehat{a}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{a}(t)\geq\rho>0,\qquad&\mbox{if $t\in[3/5,5/3]$},\\
[\widehat{a}(t)]^{2}+[\widehat{a}(2t)]^{2}=1,\qquad&\mbox{if $t\in[1/2,1]$.}\end{split}

Such a cut-off function can be easily constructed. The last assumption on
\widehat{a}
implies

(2.14)

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left[\widehat{a}\left(\frac{t}{2^{j}}\right)\right]^{2}=1,\quad t\in[1,\infty).

In terms of the kernel
L_{n}({\mathsf{w}};\cdot,\cdot)
defined via the above cut-off function
\widehat{a}
, we define

F_{0}(x,z):=1,\quad\hbox{and}\quad F_{j}(x,z):=L_{2^{j-1}}({\mathsf{w}};x,z),\quad j=1,2,3,\ldots.

and define, accordingly,
F_{j}*f=L_{2^{j-1}}({\mathsf{w}})*f
, that is,

F_{j}*f(x):=\int_{\Omega}f(y)F_{j}(x,y){\mathsf{w}}(y)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(y),\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots.

The
*
operation is associative. By the orthogonality of the reproducing kernel, it follows readily that

\displaystyle F_{j}*F_{j}*f=\sum_{k=1}^{2^{j}}\left|\widehat{a}\left(\frac{k}{2^{j-1}}\right)\right|^{2}\operatorname{proj}_{k}({\mathsf{w}};f).

Hence, the following semi-discrete Calderón type decomposition follows from (
2.14
),

(2.15)

f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{k}({\mathsf{w}};f)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}F_{j}*F_{j}*f,\qquad f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}}).

Since
F_{j}(x,\cdot)F_{k}(\cdot,y)
is a polynomial of degree
2^{j}
, discretization by a cubature rule of degree
2^{j}

puts
F_{j}*F_{k}
as a sum, which leads to a tight frame if the cubature rule has positive coefficients. We use the
positive cubature rule in Theorem
2.6
. For
j=0,1,\ldots,
let
{\varepsilon}_{j}=\frac{\delta}{2^{j}}
and
let
\Xi_{j}
be a maximal
{\varepsilon}_{j}
-separated subset in
\Omega
, where
\delta
is chosen so that the cubature rule
(
2.10
) holds. Thus, there are
{\lambda}_{z,j}>0
for
z\in\Xi_{j}
such that

\int_{\Omega}f(x){\mathsf{w}}(x)\mathrm{d}{\mathsf{m}}(x)=\sum_{z\in\Xi_{j}}{\lambda}_{z,j}f(z),\qquad f\in\Pi_{2^{j}}(\Omega);

moreover,
{\lambda}_{z,j}\sim{\mathsf{w}}(B(z,2^{-j}))
since we assume that Assertion 4 holds for
{\mathsf{w}}
. We use this cubature
to discretize
F_{j}*F_{k}
, which leads to a tight frame.

For
z\in\Xi_{j}
and
j=1,2,\ldots
, we define our frame elements by

\psi_{z,j}(x):=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{z,j}}F_{j}(x,z).

Then
\Psi:=\big\{\psi_{z,j}:z\in\Xi_{j},\quad 1\leq j\leq\infty\big\}
is a frame system
[
28
, Theorem 2.20]
:

Theorem 2.9
.

Assume that
\Omega
admits a localizable homogenous space. Let
{\mathsf{w}}
be a doubling weight
satisfying Assertion 4. If
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})
, then

(2.16)

f=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z\in\Xi_{j}}\langle f,\psi_{z,j}\rangle_{\mathsf{w}}\psi_{z,j}\qquad\mbox{in $L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}})$}

and

(2.17)

\|f\|_{2,{\mathsf{w}}}=\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{z\in\Xi_{j}}|\langle f,\psi_{z,j}\rangle_{\mathsf{w}}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

The frame element
\psi_{z,j}
has a near exponential rate of decay away from its center with respect to the
distance
{\mathsf{d}}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\Omega
.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
(\Omega,\varpi,{\mathsf{d}})
be a localizable space of homogeneous type. There, there is a constant
c_{\kappa}>0

depending only on
{\kappa}
,
d
,
\varpi
and
\widehat{a}
such that for
z\in\Xi_{j}
,
j=0,1,\dots
,

(2.18)

|\psi_{z,j}(x)|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varpi(B(z,2^{-j}))}(1+2^{j}{\mathsf{d}}(x,z))^{\kappa}},\quad x\in\Omega.

Besides the regular domains mentioned before, these localized tight frames hold on conic domains as
shown in
[
28
]
.

3.
Examples of localizable space of homogeneous type

We state several examples of
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},\mathrm{d})
that are localizable spaces of homogeneous type. In each
case, we also include the addition formula utilized to prove Assertions 1 and 2.

3.1.
The interval
[-1,1]
with the Jacobi weight

For
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
, the Jacobi weight function is defined by

w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t):=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\qquad-1<x<1.

The intrinsic distance of the interval
[-1,1]
is defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)=\arccos\left(ts+\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-s^{2}}\right),

which is the projection of the distance
|{\theta}-\phi|
on the upper half of the unit circle if we set
t=\cos{\theta}

and
s=\cos\phi
. The space
([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}},{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]})
is a localizable homogeneous space if

{\alpha},{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Let
P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
be the usual Jacobi polynomials. Then the kernel
L_{n}
, denoted
by
L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
, is of the form

(3.1)

L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\widehat{a}\Big(\frac{j}{n}\Big)\frac{P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x)P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(y)}{h_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}},

where
\widehat{a}
is an admissible cutoff function and
h_{j}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is the norm square of
P_{j}^{(\\
a,{\beta})}
in
the
L^{2}([-1,1],w_{{\alpha},{\beta}})
. Let the ball
B(x,r)
be defined with respect to this distance. Then the Jacobi weight
is a doubling weight and satisfies,

w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(B(x,n^{-1}))\sim n^{-1}(1-t+n^{-2})^{{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2}}(1+t+n^{-2})^{{\beta}+\frac{1}{2}}=:w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(n;t).

The first Assertion of the highly localized kernel is established in
[
20
]
, which states: for any
{\kappa}>0
and
n\geq 1
,

|L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t,s)|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(n;t)}\sqrt{w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(n;s)}}\left(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{[-1,1]}(t,s)\right)^{-{\kappa}},

where
\alpha,\beta\geq-1/2
and
0<{\varepsilon}\leq 1
. The second assertion is stated in
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
.

In this one-dimensional case, there is no addition formula but a product formula that writes the product of two
Jacobi polynomials of the same parameters as an integral of one Jacobi polynomial of the same parameters,
which allows us to write
L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(x,y)
as an integral of
L_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(z,1)
.

3.2.
Unit sphere

For the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we consider the space
({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathrm{d}\sigma,{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{S}})
, where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
is the
surface measure and
{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{S}}
is the geodesic distance defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{S}}(\xi,\eta)=\arccos{\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle},\qquad\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}.

The orthogonal polynomials are the classical spherical harmonics, which are homogeneous harmonic polynomials restricted
to the unit sphere. The space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},\mathrm{d}\sigma)
is usually denote by
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
and its dimension is given by
(
2.2
). Let
\{Y_{\nu,n}:1\leq\nu\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
. The classical
addition formula for the spherical harmonics gives a closed-form formula for the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
,

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}(\xi,\eta):=\sum_{1\leq\nu\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}}Y_{\nu,n}(\xi)Y_{\nu,n}(\eta)=Z_{n}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),\quad\forall\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

where we use
Z_{n}^{\lambda}
to denote a multiple of the Gagenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{\lambda}
,

Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t):=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t),\qquad{\lambda}\geq 0,

where the case
{\lambda}=0
holds under the limit so that
Z_{n}^{0}(t)=T_{n}(t)
, the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind.
The addition formula reduces the estimate of the highly localized kernel to the kernel of the Jacobi polynomials

P_{n}^{(\mu,\mu)}(1,z)
with
\mu=\frac{d-3}{2}
. In this case, the Assertion 1 is of a particularly simple form

\left|L_{n}(\xi,\eta)\right|\leq c_{\kappa}\frac{n^{d-1}}{(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{S}}(\xi,\eta))^{{\kappa}}},

which is also the first highly localized kernel known in the literature (
[
18
]
).

The space
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
has another important property worth mentioning: it is the eigenspace of the Laplace-Beltrami
operator
\Delta_{0}
, which is the restriction of the Laplace operator
\Delta
on the unit sphere; see, for example,

[
4
, Section 1.4]
for its explicit expression. More precisely, it is known
[
4
, (1.4.9)]
that

(3.2)

\Delta_{0}Y=-n(n+d-2)Y,\qquad Y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}.

We shall call
\Delta_{0}
the spectral operator. The two properties, the addition formula and the spectral operator, are
fundamental for approximation and harmonic analysis on the unit sphere.

3.3.
Unit ball

For the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we consider the space
({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu},{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}})
, where
{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}
is the
weight function defined by

{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(x)=(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mu>-\frac{1}{2},\quad x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},

and
{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}}
is the intrinsic distance on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}}(x,y)=\arccos\Big({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+u\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\Big),\quad x,y\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}.

Several orthogonal basis of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu})
can be given explicitly. The
reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{\mu};\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies an addition formula
[
25
]
:
for
\mu\geq 0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{\mu};x,y)=c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\Big({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+u\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\Big)(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u,

where
\mu>0
and it holds for
\mu=0
under the limit

(3.3)

\lim_{\mu\to 0+}c_{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{f(1)+f(-1)}{2}.

It is known that
{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}
is a doubling weight on the unit ball and

{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}\big(B(x,n^{-1})\big)\sim n^{-d}\left(\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}+n^{-1}\right)^{2\mu}=:{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(n;x).

In this case, the Assertion 1 takes the form

\left|L_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{\mu};x,y)\right|\leq c_{k}\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(n;x)}\sqrt{{\mathsf{w}}_{\mu}(n,y)}(1+n{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{B}}(x,u))^{\kappa}}.

This inequality and Assertion 2 are established in
[
21
]
. Consequently, the space
({\mathbb{B}}^{d},{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
is a
localizable space of homogeneous type.

3.4.
Simplex

For the simplex
\triangle^{d}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defined by

\triangle^{d}:=\left\{x:x_{1},\geq 0,\ldots,x_{d}\geq 0,\,|x|\leq 1\right\},\qquad|x|:=x_{1}+\ldots+x_{d},

we consider the space
(\triangle,{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma},{\mathsf{d}}_{\triangle})
, where
{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma}
is the classical Jacobi weight function

{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma}(x)=x_{1}^{{\gamma}_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{{\gamma}_{d}}(1-|x|)^{{\gamma}_{d+1}},\qquad{\gamma}_{i}>-1,\quad x\in\triangle^{d},

and
{\mathsf{d}}_{\triangle}
is the intrinsic distance on
\triangle^{d}
defined by

{\mathsf{d}}_{\triangle}(x,y)=\arccos\Big(\sqrt{x_{1}y_{1}}+\cdots+\sqrt{x_{d}y_{d}}+\sqrt{1-|x|}\sqrt{1-|y|}\Big).

Like the case of the unit ball, several orthogonal bases of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(\triangle^{d},{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma})
can be given explicitly.
The reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma};\cdot,\cdot)
also satisfies an addition formula
[
5
, Theorem 5.2.4]
:
for
{\alpha}_{i}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
and
x,y\in\triangle^{d}
,

(3.4)

P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma};x,y)=c_{\alpha}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{(|{\gamma}|+d-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}\left(2\xi(x,y;t)^{2}-1\right)\prod_{i=1}^{d+1}(1-t_{i}^{2})^{{\gamma}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t,

where
Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is a constant multiple of the Jacobi polynomial

Z_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\frac{P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(1)P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)}{h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}}

and
\xi(x,y,t)
is a function defined by

\xi(x,y;t)=\sqrt{x_{1}y_{1}}t_{1}+\cdots+\sqrt{x_{d}y_{d}}t_{d}+\sqrt{x_{d+1}y_{d+1}}t_{d+1}.

In this case,
{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma}
is known to be a doubling weight and it satisfies, for
{\gamma}_{i}\geq 0
,
1\leq i\leq d+1
,

{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma}(B(x,n^{-1}))\sim n^{-d}\prod_{i=1}^{d+1}(\sqrt{x_{i}}+n^{-1})^{2{\gamma}_{i}+1},\quad x\in\triangle^{d},

which follows, for example, from
[
4
, Lemma 11.3.6]
by making a change of variables
x_{j}\mapsto\sqrt{x_{j}}
.
In this case, Assertion 1 was established in
[
9
]
and Assertion 2 appeared more recently in
[
6
]
for

{\mathsf{w}}_{\gamma}
. Thus, the space
(\triangle^{d},{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}}_{\triangle})
is a localizable space of homogeneous type.

3.5.
Conic surface

For the conic surface
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
defined by

{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|=t,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,0\leq t\leq 1\right\}

we consider the space
({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}})
, where the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
is the
Jacobi weight function with one parameter equal to
-1
,

{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}(t)=t^{-1}(1-t)^{{\gamma}},\qquad{\gamma}>-1,

and
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}
in the intrinsic distance function on the conic surface
[
28
, Definition 4.1]
,

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}((x,t),(y,s)):=\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+ts}{2}}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right).

Orthogonal polynomials in this setting were first studied in
[
26
]
, where it is shown, in particular, that
the reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};\cdot,\cdot)
for the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}})
satisfies
the addition formula

(3.5)

\displaystyle P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}};(x,t),(y,s))=c_{{\gamma}}\int_{[-1,1]^{2}}

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{({\gamma}+d-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2})}\big(2\zeta(x,t,y,s;v)^{2}-1\big)

\displaystyle\times(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}-1}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}v

for
{\gamma}>-\frac{1}{2}
and under the limit when
{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}
. The weight
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
is an integrable doubling weight with
respect to
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}}
and it satisfies
[
28
, (4.4)]

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\big({\mathsf{c}}((x,t),n^{-1})\big)\sim r^{d}(t+n^{-2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}(1-t+n^{-2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}.

Both Assertions 1 and 2 are established in
[
28
]
with the help of the addition formula (
3.5
)
for
{\mathsf{w}}_{-1,{\gamma}}
, where the work of carrying out the estimates is fairly involved. Consequently, the space

({\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}_{0}})
is a localizable space of homogeneous type.

3.6.
Solid cone

For the solid cone
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
bounded by
{\mathbb{V}}_{0}^{d+1}
and the hyperplane
t=1
,

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}:=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq t,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\,0\leq t\leq 1\right\}

we consider the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu}
defined by

{\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{{\gamma}},\qquad{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2},\quad{\gamma}>-1,

and
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}
is the intrinsic distance function on the conic surface
[
28
, Definition 4.1]
,

{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}((x,t),(y,s)):=\arccos\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}+ts}{2}}+\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}\right).

Orthogonal polynomials in this setting were first studied in
[
26
]
, where it is shown, in particular, that
the reproducing kernel
P_{n}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu};\cdot,\cdot)
for the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu})
satisfies
the addition formula

(3.6)

\displaystyle P_{n}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu};(x,t),(y,s)\right)=c

\displaystyle\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}Z_{n}^{2\mu+{\gamma}+d}\big(2\zeta(x,t,y,s;u,v)\big)

\displaystyle\times(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\mu+\frac{d-3}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

where
\zeta(x,t,y,s;u,v)\in[-1,1]
is defined by

\zeta(x,t,y,s;t)=v_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(ts+{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}u\right)}+v_{1}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s}.

The
{\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu}
is an integrable doubling weight with respect to
{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}}
and it satisfies
[
28
, (4.4)]

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\gamma},\mu}\big(B((x,t),n^{-1})\big)\sim n^{-d+1}(t+n^{-2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}(1-t+n^{-2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{1}{2}}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}+n^{-2})^{\mu}.

Both Assertions 1 and 2 are established in
[
28
]
, so that the space
({\mathbb{V}}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{V}}})
is a
localizable space of homogeneous type.

3.7.
Double conic and hyperbolic surface

For
\varrho\geq 0
, we consider the quadratic surface defined by

{\mathbb{X}}_{0}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}=t^{2}-\varrho^{2},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\varrho\leq|t|\leq\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+1}\right\},

which is a hyperbolic surface if
\varrho>0
and a double conic surface if
\varrho=0
. We consider
the space
({\mathbb{X}}_{0},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}_{0}})

3.9.
Other double domains of conic and hyperbolic type

Orthogonal structure on several solid domains, beyond the domains bounded by quadratic surfaces, is preserved
under an appropriate structure transformation on the hyperboloid
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, as shown in
[
31
]
.
This includes the addition formula and highly localized kernels so that we obtain localized space of homogeneous
type on such domains. We state one example to give a flavor.

For
0<\mathfrak{b}<1
, we consider the domain inside the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d+1}
but outside the ellipsoid

\{(x,t):\mathfrak{b}(1-\|x\|^{2})\leq t^{2}\}\subset{\mathbb{B}}^{d+1}
, which is defined by

\mathbb{X}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\sqrt{1-\tfrac{t^{2}}{\mathfrak{b}}}\leq\|x\|\leq\sqrt{1-t^{2}},\,\,|t|\leq 1\right\}.

We consider the space
({\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}})
, where the weigh function is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}(x,t)=\big(1-\|x\|^{2}-t^{2}\big)^{\beta}\big(t^{2}-\mathfrak{b}(1-\|x\|^{2})\big)^{\gamma}\big(t^{2}-\mathfrak{b}+\|x\|^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and the distance function
{\mathsf{d}}_{X}X
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}}((x,t),(y,s))=\arccos

\displaystyle\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sqrt{\frac{1-t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}{1-\mathfrak{b}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}{1-\mathfrak{b}}}\right).

Then the corresponding kernel
L_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{w}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}})
is highly localized with respect to the distance
{\mathsf{d}}_{\mathbb{X}}
.
Consequently,
({\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1},{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}}_{{\mathbb{X}}})
is a localizable space of homogeneous type if
{\mathsf{w}}
is even in the

t
variable.
