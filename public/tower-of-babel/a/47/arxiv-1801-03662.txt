A value distribution result related to Hayman’s alternative
Kuldeep Singh Charak, Anil Singh

Abstract
. Motivated by Bloch’s Principle, we prove a value distribution result for meromorphic functions which is related to Hayman’s alternative in certain sense.

1.
Introduction and Main Result

The reader is assumed to be familiar with the standard notations of Nevanlinna value distribution theory of meromorphic functions (one may refer to
[
4
,
6
]
) such as
T\left(r,f\right)
,
m\left(r,f\right)
,
N\left(r,f\right),
etc. We shall denote the class of all meromorphic functions on a domain
D
in
\mathbb{C}
by
\mathcal{M}(D)
and we shall write, ‘
\left\langle f,D\right\rangle\in\mathcal{P}
’ for
‘
f\in\mathcal{M}(D)
satisfies the property
\mathcal{P}
on
D
’ .

We say that
\phi\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is a small function of
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
if
T(r,\phi)=S(r,f)
as
r\to\infty
possibly outside a set of
r
of finite linear measure.

W.K. Hayman proved the following ‘Picard type’ theorem, also known as Hayman’s alternative:

Theorem 1.1
.

[
7
]
Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
and let
l\geq 1.
Suppose that
f(z)\neq 0,\mbox{ and }f^{(l)}(z)-1\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}.
Then
f
is constant.

A subfamily
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{M}(D)
is said to be normal in
D
if every sequence of members of
\mathcal{F}
contains a subsequence that converges locally uniformly (w.r.t. the spherical metric) in
D
. Recall Bloch’s Principle (see
[
10
,
11
]
):
A subfamily
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{M}(D)
with
\left\langle f,D\right\rangle\in\mathcal{P}
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
is likely to be normal on
D
if
\mathcal{P}
reduces every
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
to a constant
. Neither Bloch’s Principle nor its converse is true (see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
8
,
10
]
).

According to Bloch’s Principle, to every ‘Picard type’ theorem there corresponds a normality criterion. A normality criterion corresponding to Theorem
1.1
was proved by Y.Gu as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

[
5
]
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(D)
and let
l\geq 1.
Suppose that
f(z)\neq 0,\mbox{ and }f^{(l)}(z)-1\neq 0
for all
z\in D
and
f\in\mathcal{F}.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

The constants
0\mbox{ and }1
in Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
can be replaced by arbitrary constants
a\mbox{ and }b\neq 0
:

Theorem 1.3
.

[
7
]
Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(D)
and let
l\geq 1.
Suppose that
f(z)\neq a,\mbox{ and }f^{(l)}(z)-b\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{C},
where
a,b\in\mathbb{C},~b\neq 0.
Then
f
is constant.

Theorem 1.4
.

[
5
]
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(D)
and let
l\geq 1.
Suppose that
f(z)\neq a,\mbox{ and }f^{(l)}(z)-b\neq 0
for all
z\in D,

f\in\mathcal{F}
, where
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
with
~b\neq 0.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

Note that if
l\geq 1
and
b\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
, then there is a polynomial
P(z)
such that
P^{(l)}(z)=b.
Using this observation, Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
can be restated as:

Theorem 1.5
.

Suppose that
P(z)
is a polynomial of degree
l\geq 1
and
a\in\mathbb{C}.
If
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is such that
f(z)\neq a\mbox{ and }\left(f(z)-P(z)\right)^{(l)}\neq 0,
then
f
is constant.

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that
P(z)
is a polynomial of degree
l\geq 1
and
a\in\mathbb{C}.
If
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(D)
is such that each
f\in\mathcal{F}
satisfies:

f(z)\neq a\mbox{ and }\left(f(z)-P(z)\right)^{(l)}\neq 0,

then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

Remark 1.7
.

Put
g=f-P\mbox{ and }R=Q-P
, where
P\mbox{ and }Q
are polynomials with
deg(P-Q)=deg(Q)=l
and
Q
is non-constant. If
f(z)-P(z)\neq 0
and
\left(f(z)-Q(z)\right)^{(l)}\neq 0,
then by using Theorem
1.5
, we find that
f(z)=P(z)+c
, for some constant
c\neq 0.

Remark
1.7
shows that Theorem
1.3
does not hold if
a
is replaced by some non-constant function.

Remark 1.8
.

Suppose
f
is an entire function such that
f-g
has only finitely many zeros in the plane, where
g
is some non-constant entire function. Further, let

F(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}(z)\left(f-g\right)^{(k)}

omits
1,
where
a_{k}(z)
are small functions of
f.
Then by using Theorem 3.2 in
[
6
]
, we find that

f(z)=g(z)+p(z)
, for some polynomial
p(z).
Indeed,

T(r,f-g)<\overline{N}(r,f-g)+N(r,{1\over f-g})+\overline{N}\left(r,{1\over F-1}\right)-N_{0}\left(r,{1\over F^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f),

where
N_{0}\left(r,{1\over F^{\prime}}\right)
is the counting function of the zeros of
F^{\prime}
which are not zeros of
F-1.

Since
f-g
is entire and has only finitely many zeros, it follows that

\displaystyle T(r,f-g)<\overline{N}\left(r,{1\over F-1}\right)-N_{0}\left(r,{1\over F^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow T(r,f-g)<\overline{N}\left(r,{1\over F-1}\right)+S(r,f).

If
f-g
is transcendental, then
F(z)=1
must have infinitely many roots, which is a contradiction and hence
f-g
must be a polynomial, say
p(z);
that is,
f(z)=g(z)+p(z).

Let
P\mbox{ and }Q
be polynomials with
1\leq deg(P)<deg(Q)=l
and
\mathcal{P}
be the property defined as follows:

``\left\langle f,D\right\rangle\in\mathcal{P}\Leftrightarrow f-P\neq 0\mbox{ and }\left(f-Q\right)^{(l)}\neq 0\mbox{ on }D
”. That is,
f
satisfies the property
\mathcal{P}
if, and only if
f-P\mbox{ and }\left(f-Q\right)^{(l)}
have no zeros in
D.
With this
\mathcal{P}
, Theorem
1.6
immediately yields:

Theorem 1.9
.

The family
\mathcal{F}:=\left\{f\in\mathcal{M}\left(D\right):\left\langle f,D\right\rangle\in\mathcal{P}\right\}
is normal in
D.

Note that Remark
1.7
and Theorem
1.9
provide a counterexample to the converse of Bloch’s Principle.

W. Schwick generalized Theorem
1.2
:

Theorem 1.10
.

[
12
]
Let
g\not\equiv 0
be in
\mathcal{M}(D)
and let
l\in\mathbb{N}.
Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(D)
be such that
f\neq 0,

f^{(l)}\neq g,
and
f\mbox{ and }g
have no common poles for each
f\in\mathcal{F}.
Then
\mathcal{F}
is normal in
D.

According to the converse of Bloch’s Principle, one may find a ‘Picard type’ theorem corresponding to Theorem
1.10
, and this is the purpose of this paper. In fact, we prove the following value distribution result corresponding to Theorem
1.10
which is related to Hayman’s alternative in certain sense:

Theorem 1.11
.

Suppose that
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
is transcendental and
\phi
is a small function of
f
such that
f\mbox{ and }\phi
have no common poles. Let
l\in\mathbb{N}
and
\psi(z)=f^{(l)}(z)
. If
f(z)\neq 0\mbox{ and }\psi(z)\neq\phi(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{C},
then

\psi^{\prime}(z)=\phi(z)
and
\psi^{\prime}(z)=\phi^{\prime}(z)
have infinitely many solutions.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.11

Since the proof of Theorem
1.11
is based on Milloux techniques (see
[
6
]
p.60), we need to prove some key lemmas for the proof of Theorem
1.11
. Throughout this paper, we shall denote
f^{(l)}(z)
by
\psi(z)
, where
l\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{C})
and let
\phi
be a small function of
f.
Then for
r\to\infty
outside a set of finite linear measure,

T\left(r,f\right)\leq\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)-N^{0}_{2}\left(r,\psi\right)+S(r,f),

(2.1)

where
N^{0}_{2}\left(r,\psi\right)=N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)-N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)
and
N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)
is the counting function of zeros of
\psi^{\prime}
which are not zeros of
\psi
.

Proof.

By the second fundamental theorem of Nevanlinna for three small functions (see
[
6
]
Theorem. 2.5, also see
[
4
]
Theorem. 5.9.1) with
a_{1}=0,a_{2}=\infty\mbox{ and }a_{3}=\phi
, we have

T(r,f)\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,{f}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)+S(r,f).

(2.2)

as
r\to\infty
outside a set of
r
of finite linear measure.

Since

\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)={N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)-{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right)+{N}_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right),

~~~~\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)={N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)-{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+{N}_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)

and

\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)=N\left(r,f^{\prime}\right)-N\left(r,f\right),

therefore (
2.2
) yeilds (after adding
m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)
to both sides)

\displaystyle T\left(r,f\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)

\displaystyle+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)+N\left(r,{f^{\prime}}\right)-N\left(r,{f}\right)

\displaystyle-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow m\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{f-\phi}\right)+m\left(r,{f}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2T\left(r,f\right)-N_{1}\left(r,f\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f),

(2.3)

where
N_{1}\left(r,f\right)=N\left(r,\frac{1}{f^{\prime}}\right)+2N\left(r,f\right)-N\left(r,f^{\prime}\right).

Applying (
2
) to
\psi=f^{(l)}\mbox{ and put }g=\psi-\phi,
we have

\displaystyle m\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)+m\left(r,{\psi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2T\left(r,\psi\right)-N_{1}\left(r,\psi\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{g^{\prime}}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{g^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,\psi)

(2.4)

as
r\to\infty
outside a set of
r
of finite linear measure.

Since

N\left(r,\psi^{\prime}\right)-N\left(r,\psi\right)=\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)

and

N\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{g^{\prime}}\right)=\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)-N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{g^{\prime}}\right)

and using the first fundamental theorem of Nevanlinna, we have

\displaystyle 2T\left(r,\psi\right)-N_{1}\left(r,\psi\right)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,{\psi}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)+N\left(r,\psi\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)

\displaystyle-N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)-2N\left(r,\psi\right)+N\left(r,\psi^{\prime}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,{\psi}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle+\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)+S(r,f)

and hence (
2
) reduces to

m\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f).

(2.5)

Also

\displaystyle T\left(r,f\right)

\displaystyle=T\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,\frac{\psi}{f\psi}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq m\left(r,\frac{\psi}{f}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle=m\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+S(r,f).

(2.6)

Now by using (
2.5
) in (
2
), we get

\displaystyle T\left(r,f\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{g}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f)

\displaystyle=\bar{N}\left(r,f\right)+N\left(r,\frac{1}{f}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)-N^{0}_{2}\left(r,{\psi}\right)+S(r,f)

(2.7)

as
r\to\infty
outside a set of
r
of finite linear measure, where

N^{0}_{2}\left(r,\psi\right)=N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)-N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}}\right)=N\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)-\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi}\right)

counts only repeated zeros of
\psi
with multiplicity reduced by
1
. ∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
and let
\phi
be a small function of
f
such that
f\mbox{ and }\phi
have no common poles. Then

lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)\leq\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right)+S(r,f),

(2.8)

where
N_{1}\left(r,f\right)
is the counting function of simple poles of
f
and
\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)
is the counting function of multiple poles of
f
counted once.

The proof of Lemma
2.2
is carried out by following the proof of Lemma 3.1 in
[
6
]
with certain modifications.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Put

G(z)={\left\{\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)\right\}^{l+1}\over{\left\{\phi(z)-\psi(z)\right\}^{l+2}}}.

If
z_{0}
is a simple pole of
f(z),
then near
z_{0}
we have

f(z)={a\over z-z_{0}}+O(1),

and

\psi(z)={(-1)^{l}a(l!)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{l+1}}+O(1)

near
z_{0}
, and hence

\psi^{\prime}={(-1)^{l+1}a(l+1)!\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{l+2}}\left\{1+O(z-z_{0})^{l+2}\right\}.

Since
f
and
\phi
have no common poles, therefore near
z_{0},
we have

G(z)={(-1)^{l+1}(l+1)^{l+1}\over{al!}}\left\{1+O(z-z_{0})^{l+1}\right\}

which implies that
G(z_{0})\neq 0,\infty,
and
G^{\prime}(z)
has a zero of order atleast
l
at
z_{0}
and so

\displaystyle lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)\leq N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{G^{\prime}}\right).

(2.9)

Applying Jensen’s formula to
{G^{\prime}/{G}}
we get

N\left(r,\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)-N\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)=m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)-m\left(r,\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)+O(1).

(2.10)

Since the only zeros of
{G^{\prime}}/{G}
are the zeros of
G^{\prime}
which are not zeros of
G,
we have

\displaystyle N\left(r,\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)=N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{G^{\prime}}\right).

(2.11)

Also,
{G^{\prime}}/{G}
has only simple poles at the zeros and poles of
G,
so

\displaystyle N\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)=\bar{N}\left(r,G\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right).

(2.12)

Using (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) in (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{G^{\prime}}\right)-\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)-\bar{N}\left(r,{G}\right)=m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)-m\left(r,\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)+O(1).

(2.13)

Now, from (
2.9
), (
2.10
) and (
2.13
), we have

\displaystyle lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)

\displaystyle\leq N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{G^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,{G}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)-m\left(r,\frac{G}{G^{\prime}}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,{G}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)+O(1).

(2.14)

Let
z_{0}
be a pole of
\psi(z)-\phi(z)
of order
m
, say. Then near
z_{0}

\psi(z)-\phi(z)={s_{0}(z)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{m}}

for some function
s_{0}(z)
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}\mbox{ such that }s_{0}(z_{0})\neq 0.

Now, there are two cases:

Case 1:

z_{0}
is a pole of
f(z)
. Then
m=k+l
, where
k>1
is the multiplicity of
z_{0}
as a pole of
f
. Since
z_{0}
is not a pole of
\phi,
we see that
z_{0}
is a pole of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)
of multiplicity
m+1.
Therefore, near
z_{0}

G(z)=t_{0}(z)\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{k-1},

for some function
t_{0}(z)
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}\mbox{ such that }t_{0}(z_{0})\neq 0.
So
z_{0}
is a zero of order
k-1
of
G(z)
.

Case 2:

z_{0}
is a pole of
\phi(z).
Then
z_{0}
is also a pole of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)
of multiplicity
m.
Therefore, near
z_{0}

G(z)=t_{1}(z)\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{m},

for some function
t_{1}(z)
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}\mbox{ such that }t_{1}(z_{0})\neq 0.
This shows that
z_{0}
is a pole of
G(z)
of the same multiplicity as that of
\phi(z).

Similarly, looking at the poles of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z),
we obtain the same conclusion as in the case of poles of
\psi(z)-\phi(z).

Next, correspnding to the zeros of
\psi(z)-\phi(z)
and
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z),
we have the following three cases:

Case 1:

z_{0}
is a zero of
\psi(z)-\phi(z)
but it is not a zero of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z).
Then
z_{0}
is a pole of
G(z)
.

Case 2:

z_{0}
is zero of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)
but it is not a zero of
\psi(z)-\phi(z).
Then
z_{0}
is a zero of
G(z)
.

Case 3:

z_{0}
is a common zero of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)\mbox{ and }\psi(z)-\phi(z)
. Let
j
and
k
be the multiplicities of
z_{0}
as a zero of
\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi(z)\mbox{ and }\psi(z)-\phi(z)
, respectively. Then near
z_{0}
,

G(z)=t_{2}(z)\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{(l+1)j-(l+2)k}

for some function
t_{2}(z)
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}\mbox{ such that }t_{2}(z_{0})\neq 0
.

Thus
z_{0}
is a pole of
G(z)
if
k>\frac{l+1}{l+2}j
and
z_{0}
is a zero of
G(z)
if
k<\frac{l+1}{l+2}j
.

Let
N(r,\frac{1}{f},\frac{1}{g^{0}})
be the counting function of zeros of
f
which are not zeros of
g
,
N(r,\frac{1}{f},\frac{1}{g})
be the counting function corresponding to the common zeros of
f\mbox{ and }g
and
N^{(\alpha)}(r,\frac{1}{f},\frac{1}{g})
be the counting function corresponding to the common zeros of
f
and
g
, such that the
m(f,z_{0})>\alpha m(g,z_{0})
, where by
m(f,z_{0})
we denote the multiplicity of
z_{0}
as a zero of
f
. With these notations and the preceding arguments , we find that

\displaystyle\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}_{2}(r,f)+\bar{N}(r,\phi)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi},\frac{1}{(\psi-\phi)^{0}}\right)

\displaystyle+\bar{N}^{\left(\frac{l+2}{l+1}\right)}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi},\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)

(2.15)

and

\displaystyle\bar{N}(r,G)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi},\frac{1}{(\psi^{\prime}-\phi)^{0}}\right)+\bar{N}^{\left(\frac{l+1}{l+2}\right)}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi},\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right).

(2.16)

Note that

\displaystyle\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi},\frac{1}{(\psi^{\prime}-\phi)^{0}}\right)+\bar{N}^{\left(\frac{l+1}{l+2}\right)}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi},\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right).

Therefore, using (
2
) and (
2.16
) in (

\displaystyle lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}_{2}(r,f)+\bar{N}(r,\phi)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right)+m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)+O(1).

(2.17)

Since

]

Thus from (
2.17
), it follows that

\displaystyle lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{N}_{2}(r,f)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi}\right)+S(r,f).

(2.18)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{M}\left(\mathbb{C}\right)
and let
\phi
be a small function of
f
such that
f\mbox{ and }\phi
have no common poles. Then

lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)\leq\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f),

(2.19)

where
N_{1}\left(r,f\right)
is the counting function of simple poles of
f
,
\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)
is the counting function of multiple poles of
f
counted once and
N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)
is the counting function of zeros of
\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}
which are not repeated zeros of
\psi-\phi
.

Proof.

Define

G(z)={\left\{\psi^{\prime}(z)-\phi^{\prime}(z)\right\}^{l+1}\over{\left\{\phi(z)-\psi(z)\right\}^{l+2}}}.

Then as in the proof of Lemma
2.2
above, we
again arrive at (
2
).

Next, to find the distribution of poles and zeros of
G(z)
, we proceed as follows;

Put,

h(z)=\psi(z)-\phi(z).

If
z_{0}
is a pole of
h(z)
of order
m,
then near
z_{0},

h(z)={s(z)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{m}}\mbox{ and }h^{\prime}(z)={t(z)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{m+1}}~,

where
s(z)\mbox{ and }t(z)
are functions analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}
and both have no zeros at
z_{0}.
So,

\displaystyle G(z)={w(z)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{l+1-m}}

(2.20)

for some function
w(z)
analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}\mbox{ such that }w(z_{0})\neq 0.

Next if
z_{0}
is a zero of
h(z),
then near
z_{0}
,
h(z)={l(z)\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{m}}
and so

\displaystyle G(z)={m(z)\over\left(z-z_{0}\right)^{l+1+m}}

(2.21)

where
l(z)\mbox{ and }m(z)
are functions analytic in a neighborhood of
z_{0}
and both have no zeros at
z_{0}.

From (
2.21
) and (
2.20
) we see that the only poles of
G(z)
occur at

(i)

the roots of
h(z)=0
and

(ii)

the poles of
\phi(z)
of multiplicity less than
l+1
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{N}\left(r,G\right)\leq\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\phi\right)-\bar{N}_{l+1}\left(r,\phi\right),

(2.22)

where
\bar{N}_{k}\left(r,\phi\right)
is the counting function of poles of
\phi(z)
which have multiplicity atleast
k
, each pole is counted once.

Since the zeros of
G(z)
occur at

(i)

the roots of
h^{\prime}(z)=0
which are not the roots of
h(z)=0

(ii)

multiple poles of
f(z)
and

(iii)

poles of
\phi
of multiplicity greater than
l+1
,

therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)\leq\bar{N}_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+\bar{N}_{2}\left(r,f\right)+\bar{N}_{l+1}\left(r,\phi\right).

(2.23)

Adding (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we have;

\displaystyle\bar{N}\left(r,G\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{G}\right)\leq\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\phi\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right).

(2.24)

Since
T(r,\phi)=S(r,f)
and
S(r,\psi)=S(r,f)
, by Theorem
3.1
in
[
6
]
, we have

m\left(r,\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\right)=S(r,f).

Thus, from (
2
) and (
2.24
), it follows that

lN_{1}\left(r,f\right)\leq\bar{N_{2}}\left(r,f\right)+\bar{N}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi-\phi}\right)+N_{0}\left(r,\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}-\phi^{\prime}}\right)+S(r,f).

(2.25)
