Negative APE dissipation as the fundamental criterion for double diffusive instabilities
R. Tailleux 1

Abstract
Understanding the energetics and mechanisms of double diffusive instabilities in stratified fluids remains challenging. These instabilities can arise from a mechanically unforced stably stratified state with no available potential energy (APE), thus requiring the background potential energy (BPE) to be dynamically active rather than passive. Middleton and Taylor proposed a criterion linking BPE extraction to APE with the diapycnal component of the diffusive buoyancy flux, but it only predicts diffusive convection instability, not salt finger instability. We argue that the extraction of BPE into APE is better determined by the sign of the net APE dissipation rate. Our theory predicts that in doubly-diffusive fluids, the APE dissipation rate can be enhanced, suppressed, or even have the opposite sign compared to a simple fluid, depending on the diffusivity ratio, density ratio, and a spiciness parameter measuring density-compensated thermohaline variations. In the laminar or weakly-turbulent regime, negative APE dissipation predicts the occurrence of both salt finger and diffusive convection instability. However, only diffusive convection instability persists in the turbulent regime. To sustain salt finger instability in turbulent conditions, density-compensated thermohaline (spiciness) variations are essential. We derive new criteria for the range of density ratios where double diffusive instabilities are active, resembling those in laminar regimes where the Prandtl number is replaced by the inverse of the mixing efficiency. Interestingly, the APE dissipation rate can be made to appear as one of the terms contributing to the entropy budget, which establishes that it should be regarded as irreversible only when positive.

Introduction

Stratified binary fluids, in which the stratifying agents diffuse at different rates, exhibit a range of intriguing phenomena not observed in single-component stratified fluids. One such phenomenon are double diffusive instabilities, which arise when one of the stratifying agents act to destabilise the stratification. In the context of ocean mixing, this leads to the emergence of new types of flows, including diffusive convection, salt fingering, thermohaline intrusions, and thermohaline staircases (Turner, 1985; Schmitt, 1994; Radko, 2013). Theoretical analysis categorises the different dynamically relevant types of stratification based on the density ratio
(R_{\rho})
of the fluid: 1) the doubly stable case
(R_{\rho}<0)
, when both temperature and salinity are stabilising; 2) salt fingers favourable
(R_{\rho}>1)
, with salinity as the destabilising agent; 3) diffusive convection favourable
(0<R_{\rho}<1)
, where temperature acts as the destabiliser. Understanding how double diffusion modulates mixing in the doubly stable case and characterising double diffusive instabilities in both laminar and turbulent regimes are key questions of interest.

To date, linear stability analysis has been the primary approach for identifying the physical parameters that control the development of double diffusive instabilities from a quiescent sate. Such an analysis reveals, for instance, that molecular viscosity and diffusion can severely restrict the range of density ratios at which these instabilities can occur, viz.,

\begin{split}\frac{P_{r}+\tau}{P_{r}+1}<R_{\rho}<1,&\qquad{\rm for\,\,diffusive\,\,convection},\\
1<R_{\rho}<\frac{1}{\tau},&\qquad{\rm for\,\,salt\,\,fingers},\end{split}

(1)

e.g.,
Radko 2013
, where
\tau=\kappa_{S}/\kappa_{T}
represents the ratio of salt diffusivity to temperature diffusivity,
P_{r}=\nu/\kappa_{T}
denotes the Prandtl number, and
\nu
is the molecular viscosity. For example, using the typical oceanic values
\tau\approx 0.01
and
P_{r}\approx 7
yield a reduced density ratio range of
1<R_{\rho}<100
for salt fingers and
0.8076<R_{\rho}<1
for diffusive convection.

While linear stability theory provides valuable insights into double diffusive instabilities, other approaches are necessary to understand their energy source and whether they can persist in a turbulent environment like the oceans. In this regard, the theory of available potential energy (APE) introduced by
Lorenz 1955
and applied to the study of turbulent stratified fluids by
Winters et al. 1995
(referred to as W95 thereafter) appears to be one of the most promising avenues of research. According to APE theory, the energetics of a stratified fluid can be separated into two main budgets, one for the total mechanical energy (APE+kinetic energy), and one for the background potential energy (BPE). Physically, the BPE represents the potential energy (PE) associated with the state of minimum potential energy achievable from the actual state through an adiabatic (and isohaline in seawater) mass re-arrangement. The generic form of these budget equations is

\frac{d(APE+KE)}{dt}={\rm Forcing}-\int_{V}\rho(\varepsilon_{k}+\varepsilon_{p})\,{\rm d}V

(2)

\frac{d(BPE)}{dt}=\int_{V}\rho(\varepsilon_{k}+\varepsilon_{p})\,{\rm d}V

(3)

where
\varepsilon_{k}
is the positive definite viscous dissipation rate, and
\varepsilon_{p}
the diffusive rate of APE dissipation. As is well known, viscous dissipation is an irreversible process that converts KE into ‘heat’, which in APE theory is played by the BPE.

In a single-component compressible fluid,
Tailleux 2013c
established that the APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}
may be broken down into three components:

\varepsilon_{p}=\varepsilon_{p,lam}+\varepsilon_{p,tur}+\varepsilon_{p,eos},

(4)

with
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
and
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
representing the laminar and turbulent parts of
\varepsilon_{p}
respectively, and
\varepsilon_{p,eos}
representing the part of
\varepsilon_{p}
arising from the nonlinearities of the equation of state.
To date, the APE dissipation rate has been primarily defined and studied for single-component Boussinesq fluids with a linear equation of state, for wich only
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
and
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
subsist. As clarified further in the text,
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
and
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
relate to the terms
-\Phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{d}
in W95’s framework. Physically,
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
is the fundamental quantity for defining the turbulent diapycnal diffusivity

K_{\rho}=\frac{\varepsilon_{p,tur}}{N^{2}}=\frac{\Gamma\varepsilon_{k}}{N^{2}}

(5)

e.g.,
Osborn & Cox 1972
;
Osborn 1980
;
Lindborg & Brethouwer 2008
, often quantified by the dissipation ratio or ‘mixing efficiency’
\Gamma=\varepsilon_{p,tur}/\varepsilon_{k}
. Typically,
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
is estimated from microstructure measurements using formula such as the widely used expression:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon_{p,tur}}=\frac{g\alpha\kappa_{T}|\nabla\theta^{\prime}|^{2}}{d\overline{\theta}/dz},

(6)

(
Oakey 1982
;
Gargett & Holloway 1984
)
, where
\alpha
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
g
is the acceleration due to gravity, and
\overline{\theta}
represents the mean potential temperature profile. Consequently,
\varepsilon_{p}
, like the viscous dissipation rate, is generally considered a positive quantity that also converts mechanical energy into BPE or ‘heat’ (for further views on this, see
Tailleux 2009
).

In a double diffusive fluid, double diffusive instabilities can develop even in the absence of mechanical forcing. Within the framework of APE theory, this is only possible if BPE, like APE, can become a source of kinetic energy, and hence if we accept the notion that not only can
\varepsilon_{p}
become negative, but it can also be large enough to overcome viscous dissipation
\varepsilon_{k}
. In this paper, we derive a theoretical expression for the ratio
{\cal F}=\varepsilon_{p}^{dd}/\varepsilon_{p}^{std}
of the APE dissipation rates for a double diffusive case over that for a simple fluid.
We demonstrate that
\varepsilon_{p}^{dd}
is negative under the conditions corresponding to the linear stability analysis for both salt finger and diffusive convection regimes. Recently,
Middleton & Taylor 2020
proposed to characterise double diffusive instabilities in terms of the sign of the diapycnal component of the molecular buoyancy flux, which is related to the turbulent part
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
of
\varepsilon_{p}
(equivalently
\Phi_{d}
), rather than
\varepsilon_{p}
.
However, their criterion only succeeds in predicting the occurrence of the diffusive convection regime but not the salt finger regime, which suggests that it is the sign of the net APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p,lam}+\varepsilon_{p,tur}
(equivalently
\Phi_{d}-\Phi_{i}
) that represents the most fundamental measure of double diffusive instabilities. Additionally, we show that considering the APE dissipation rate sheds light on other aspects of double diffusive instabilities in turbulent regimes. For example, it correctly predicts the regime associated with diffusive interleaving in the presence of density-compensated isopycnal gradients of temperature and salinity, which are thought to be essential for the formation of thermohaline staircases
(
Merryfield 2000
)
.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows: Section 2 establishes the general form of the local APE dissipation rate for a double diffusive fluid. Section 3 outlines the conditions necessary for negative APE dissipation. Section 4 provides a comparison of the local and APE frameworks, as well as a discussion of the nature of the APE dissipation rate and background potential energy. Section 5 discusses the practical issues related to the computation of the reference temperature and salinity profiles used in our definition of spiciness. Finally, Section 6 discusses the results and provides future perspectives.

Local APE theory and APE dissipation rate

Similarly as
Middleton & Taylor 2020
, we study
the energetics of double diffusive instabilities for a
Boussinesq fluid with a linear equation of state for density governed by the system of equations:

\frac{D{\bf v}}{Dt}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\star}}\nabla\delta p=-\frac{g\delta\rho}{\rho_{\star}}{\bf k}+\nu\nabla^{2}{\bf v},

(7)

\nabla\cdot{\bf v}=0,

(8)

\frac{D\theta}{Dt}=-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}_{\theta},\qquad\frac{DS}{Dt}=-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}_{S},

(9)

\rho=\rho_{\star}\left[1-\alpha(\theta-\theta_{\star})+\beta(S-S_{\star})\right],

(10)

where
\rho
is density,
p
is pressure,
g
is gravitational acceleration,

S
is salinity,
\theta
is potential temperature,
\alpha
is the thermal expansion coefficient,
\beta
is the haline contraction coefficients, while
\rho_{\star}
,
S_{\star}
and
\theta_{\star}
are constant reference values for
\rho
,
S
and
\theta
respectively.
Moreover,
\delta p=p-p_{0}(z)
and
\delta\rho=\rho-\rho_{0}(z)
denote the pressure and
density anomalies defined relative to the reference pressure and density profiles

p_{0}(z)
and
\rho_{0}(z)=-g^{-1}dp_{0}/dz
characterising Lorenz reference state.
Simple diffusive laws are assumed for
\theta
and
S
so that
{\bf J}_{\theta}=-\kappa_{T}\nabla\theta
and
{\bf J}_{s}=-\kappa_{S}\nabla S
, where
\kappa_{T}
and
\kappa_{S}
are the molecular diffusivities of heat and salt respectively.
The tracer equations (
9
)
may be combined with the equation of state (
10
) to form an equation
for the Boussinesq buoyancy
b_{bou}=g[\alpha(\theta-\theta_{\star})-\beta(S-S_{\star})]
,

\frac{Db_{bou}}{Dt}=-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}_{b}

(11)

where

{\bf J}_{b}=g(\alpha{\bf J}_{\theta}-\beta{\bf J}_{s})=-g(\kappa_{T}\alpha\nabla\theta-\kappa_{S}\beta\nabla S)

(12)

is the molecular buoyancy flux. Note that in the absence of salinity,
{\bf J}_{b}=-\kappa_{T}\nabla b_{bou}
is down the gradient of the Boussinesq buoyancy, but not
in the general case.

To understand how double diffusion affects APE dissipation, the local APE framework
(
Tailleux 2013b
;
Tailleux 2018
;
Novak & Tailleux 2018
)
is preferred over the
Winters et al. 1995
’s global APE framework used by
Middleton & Taylor 2020
. Indeed, we show in Section
4
that defining the total APE of a fluid
as the volume integral of non-sign definite
integrand makes the global APE framework problematic in several respects; for instance,
it introduces unphysical terms in its budget. To avoid such difficulties, it is
therefore preferable to define the APE of a fluid as the volume integral of the APE density

e_{a}=e_{a}(S,\theta,z)=-\int_{z_{r}}^{z}b(S,\theta,z^{\prime})\,{\rm d}z^{\prime},

(13)

where
b(S,\theta,z)=-(g/\rho_{\star})(\rho(S,\theta)-\rho_{0}(z))
is the buoyancy defined relative to Lorenz reference density profile
\rho_{0}(z)
characterising the notional reference state of minimum potential energy obtainable from the actual state by means of an adiabatic and volume-conserving re-arrangement. This definition of buoyancy
has the advantage of vanishing in Lorenz reference state of rest, which is not the case of
the Boussinesq buoyancy. As is well known, the APE density (
13
)
represents the work against buoyancy forces that a parcel needs to counteract to move from its reference position at
z_{r}
,
solution of the level of neutral buoyancy equation

b(S,\theta,z_{r})=0,

(14)

to its actual position at
z
.
For simplicity, we disregard the temporal dependence of
\rho_{0}(z)
as the APE dissipation only depends on spatial gradients of the tracer fields. Inversion of Eq. (
14
) establishes that
z_{r}=z_{r}(\rho)=z_{r}(S,\theta)
is a material function of
S
and
\theta
and therefore a Lagrangian invariant following fluid parcels in the absence of diffusive sources/sinks of heat and salt.

A local budget equation for the APE density is easily obtained by taking the
material derivative of (
13
), which yields

\begin{split}\frac{De_{a}}{Dt}=&-b\frac{Dz}{Dt}+\underbrace{b(S,\theta,z_{r})}_{=0}\frac{Dz_{r}}{Dt}-(z-z_{r})\frac{Db_{ou}}{Dt}\\
=&-bw-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}_{a}-\varepsilon_{p},\end{split}

(15)

where
{\bf J}_{a}
is the diffusive flux of APE and
\varepsilon_{p}
the APE dissipation rate,
given by

{\bf J}_{a}=-(z-z_{r}){\bf J}_{b},

(16)

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{p}=&{\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla(z-z_{r})\\
=&\underbrace{-g\left(\alpha\kappa_{T}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z}-\beta\kappa_{S}\frac{\partial S}{\partial z}\right)}_{\varepsilon_{p,lam}}+\underbrace{g(\alpha\kappa_{T}\nabla\theta-\beta\kappa_{S}\nabla S)\cdot\nabla z_{r}}_{\varepsilon_{p,tur}}.\end{split}

(17)

As indicated by (
17
),
\varepsilon_{p}
can be decomposed into
a ‘laminar’ and ‘turbulent’ components
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
and
\varepsilon_{p,tur}

whose volume integrals

\int_{V}\varepsilon_{p,lam}\rho_{\star}{\rm d}V=-\Phi_{i},\qquad\int_{V}\varepsilon_{p,tur}\rho_{\star}{\rm d}V=\Phi_{d},

(18)

can easily be shown to
correspond to
Winters et al. 1995
global energy conversions
-\Phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{d}
in
the global APE framework. So far, the turbulent mixing community has generally assumed

\Phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{d}
to represent
distinct types of energy conversions, the former with internal energy (IE) and the latter with
the background potential energy (BPE); however, the analysis of

4

clearly indicates that there is no physical basis for such a view, and that in reality,
\varepsilon_{p}
,
\Phi_{d}
, and
\Phi_{i}
all represent conversions with internal energy

Tailleux 2009

\varepsilon_{p,lam}
and

Physically, Eq. (
15

can be modified through: 1) conversion with kinetic energy via the buoyancy flux

(19)

In their study,
Middleton & Taylor 2020

(denoted

which is not the case of
\tau

present case, this can be implemented in practice by decomposing

and

as

\theta=\theta_{0}(z_{r})+\theta_{\xi},\qquad S=S_{0}(z_{r})+S_{\xi},

(20)

denoting the reference position of a fluid parcel as before. Provided that

are defined as the isopycnal mean temperature and salinity respectively, they contain all necessary information to compute density

and

in terms of isopycnal means can be justified on the ground that such a state
is the one that would be expected to result in the idealised limit of infinitely fast (resp.
infinitely slow) isopycnal (resp. diapycnal) mixing. How to estimate these in practice are discussed in Section
5
. As shown below, defining spiciness in this way allows

\varepsilon_{p}

In the following,

\varepsilon_{p}
in the following form

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{p}=&g\left(\kappa_{T}\alpha\frac{\partial\tilde{\theta}}{\partial z_{r}}-\kappa_{S}\beta\frac{\partial\tilde{S}}{\partial z_{r}}\right)\left(|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}-\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial z}\right)+\frac{g(\kappa_{T}-\kappa_{S})}{2\rho_{\star}}\left(\nabla z_{r}\cdot\nabla\xi-\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial z}\right)\\

(21)

which depends on the following parameters:

\begin{split}{\cal M}\equiv\frac{g}{2\rho_{\star}N_{0}^{2}(z_{r})}\frac{(\nabla z_{r}\cdot\nabla\xi-\partial_{z}\xi)}{|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}-\partial_{z}z_{r}}\qquad&{\rm Spiciness\,\,parameter}\\
\\

\\
N_{0}^{2}(z_{r})\equiv-\frac{g}{\rho_{\star}}\frac{d\rho_{0}}{dz_{r}}(z_{r})=g\left(\alpha\frac{d\theta_{0}}{dz_{r}}-\beta\frac{dS_{0}}{dz_{r}}\right)(z_{r})\qquad&{\rm Reference\,\,N^{2}}\\
\\
R_{\rho}\equiv R_{\rho}(z_{r})=\left.\alpha\frac{d\theta_{0}}{dz_{r}}\right/\beta\frac{dS_{0}}{dz_{r}}\qquad&{\rm Density\,\,Ratio}\\
\\
\tau\equiv\frac{\kappa_{S}}{\kappa_{T}}\qquad&{\rm Diffusivity\,\,Ratio}\end{split}

(22)

3
APE dissipation and diffusive stability

3.1
Single component fluid

Even in the case of a single component fluid, the APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}
, unlike the viscous dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{k}
, is not guaranteed to be always positive definite. To see this, let us first set
{\cal M}=0
and
\tau=1
in (
21
), thus leading to

\varepsilon_{p}\equiv\varepsilon_{p}^{\rm std}=K_{\rm eff}N_{0}^{2}(z_{r})\left(1-\frac{\partial_{z}z_{r}}{|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}}\right)=\kappa_{T}\left(|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}-\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial z}\right)N_{0}^{2}.

(23)

Since
\kappa_{T}
and
N_{0}^{2}
are positive by construction, (
23
) shows that
\varepsilon_{p}
can occasionally be negative
provided that

{\cal F}\equiv|\nabla z_{r}|^{2}-\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial z}=|\nabla_{h}z_{r}|^{2}+\left(\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial z}\right)^{2}-\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial z}<0,

(24)

where
\nabla_{h}
denotes the horizontal gradient. If viewed as a quadratic polynomial in
\partial z_{r}/\partial z
with discriminant
\Delta=1-4|\nabla_{h}z_{r}|^{2}|
,
{\cal F}
can only be negative when possessing two real roots, that is when
\Delta<0
, or equivalently when

|\nabla_{h}z_{r}|<\frac{1}{2}.

(25)

In that case,
{\cal F}
and
\varepsilon_{p}^{\rm std}
achieve their maximally negative values for
\partial z_{r}/\partial z=1/2
,

\left|\varepsilon_{p,min}^{\rm std}\right|=\kappa_{T}N_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}-|\nabla_{h}z_{r}|^{2}\right)<\frac{\kappa_{T}N_{0}^{2}}{4},

(26)

which is bounded from above by
\kappa_{T}N_{0}^{2}/4
, which is laminar like in character and therefore extremely small relative to turbulent values. Nevertheless, because a negative
\varepsilon_{p}
can potentially cause perturbations to grow with time, it seems of interest to estimate the time scale
\tau_{ape}=e_{a}/\varepsilon_{p}
of the latter. Using the fact that the APE density scales as
N_{0}^{2}\zeta^{2}/2
, where
\zeta=z-z_{r}
, this leads to

\tau_{ape}=\frac{N_{0}^{2}\zeta^{2}/2}{\kappa_{T}N_{0}^{2}/4}=\frac{2\zeta^{2}}{\kappa_{T}},

(27)

For illustration, using
\kappa_{T}=10^{-7}{\rm m^{2}.s^{-1}}
and
\zeta=1\,{\rm cm}=10^{-2}{\rm m}
yields
\tau=2\times 10^{-4}/10^{-7}=2\times 10^{3}{\rm s}\approx 33\,{\rm min}
, which is fast enough to be observable in the laboratory, at least in principle. Eq. (
25
), which may be rewritten as
|\nabla_{h}\zeta|<1/2
, also requires that the horizontal scales of the unstable perturbations be larger than their vertical scales. A priori, perturbations with the required characteristics cannot occur spontaneously, they can only be created with some form of appropriate gentle mechanical stirring. These considerations appear to be consistent with the experimental observation of steps formation by
Park et al. 1994

Since

(35)

This equation shows that the single-component APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}^{std}=K_{\rm eff}N_{0}^{2}(z_{r})

4
Comparison of APE frameworks

Holliday & McIntyre 1981
and
Andrews 1981
had already shown the possibility of building Lorenz APE theory from a local principle by the time W95 published their study in the mid-1990s. However, the local APE framework was still rudimentary and had not been used for any concrete applications. This situation has improved in the recent years, as the local APE framework has progressed rapidly and achieved full maturity, being able to deal with a fully compressible multi-component stratified fluid
(
Tailleux 2018
)
. In this paper, we prefer the local APE framework to the global APE framework, because it is objectively simpler and more physical, even though this is not necessarily widely appreciated or acknowledged. The next subsection intends to reveal some of the unphysical aspects of the global APE framework that are commonly overlooked, but easily uncovered by the local APE framework. The subsequent subsection contrasts the Boussinesq APE framework with its exact counterpart to elucidate the nature of APE dissipation and background potential energy, which we believe are still misinterpreted in the literature.

4.1
Boussinesq local versus global APE frameworks

We first start by reviewing the main resuls of the global APE framework as first derived by W95. Following
Lorenz 1955
, W95 defined the APE of a fluid as the
potential energy of the actual state minus that of the adiabatically sorted state

E_{a}^{W95}=\int_{V}\rho gz\,{\rm d}V-\int_{V}\rho_{r}gz_{r}\,{\rm d}V=\int_{V}\rho g(z-z_{r})\,{\rm d}V

(39)

and showed that its budget equation may be written in the form

\frac{dE_{a}^{W95}}{dt}=\underbrace{\int_{V}\rho gw\,{\rm d}V}_{\Phi_{z}}\,\,\underbrace{-g\oint_{S}(z\rho-\psi){\bf v}\cdot{\bf n}{\rm d}S}_{S_{APE}^{adv}}+\underbrace{\kappa g\oint_{S}(z-z_{r})\nabla\rho\cdot{\bf n}\,{\rm d}S}_{S_{APE}^{diff}}-(\Phi_{d}-\Phi_{i})

(40)

where
\Phi_{z}
represents the conversion with kinetic energy,
S_{ADV}^{adv}
represents the
advective flux of APE through the boundaries enclosing the control volume considered,
S_{APE}^{dif}
represents the diffusive flux of APE through the same boundaries. The expressions for
\Phi_{i}
and

\Phi_{d}
are given by

\Phi_{i}=-\kappa gA(\overline{\rho}_{top}-\overline{\rho}_{bottom})

(41)

\Phi_{d}=-\kappa g\int_{V}\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial\rho}|\nabla\rho|^{2}\,{\rm d}V

(42)

where
A
is the cross-section of the domain, assumed constant, the overbar denoting
surface-average, while the quantity
\psi
entering the expression of the advective flux is given by

\psi=\int^{\rho}z_{r}(\tilde{\rho},t)\,{\rm d}\tilde{\rho}.

(43)

For the most part, the derivation of (
40
) is straightforward,
except for the demonstration that

\int_{V}\rho\frac{\partial z_{r}}{\partial t}\,{\rm d}V=0.

(44)

which is non-trivial. A key point of this paper concerns the proper interpretation of
\Phi_{i}
and

\Phi_{d}
. Following W95,
\Phi_{d}
and
\Phi_{i}
have been interpreted as representing two different
types of energy conversion, with BPE and IE respectively. The need for revisiting this interpretation is explained below.

The global APE budget (
40
) is now compared with that obtained by
integrating the local APE density budget. This is done here for a two-constituent fluid
with time-dependent Lorenz reference state, for which the local APE density takes the form

e_{a}=e_{a}(S,\theta,z,t)=-\int_{z_{r}}^{z}b(S,\theta,\tilde{z},t)\,{\rm d}\tilde{z}=\frac{g}{\rho_{\star}}\int_{z_{r}}^{z}[\rho(S,\theta)-\rho_{0}(\tilde{z},t)]\,{\rm d}\tilde{z},

(45)

with the global APE being now defined as the volume-integrated APE density

E_{a}=\int_{V}\rho_{\star}e_{a}\,{\rm d}V.

(46)

For a time-dependent Lorenz reference state, the local APE budget (
15
)
derived earlier needs is easily shown to be given by

\frac{\partial e_{a}}{\partial t}=-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla e_{a}-bw-\nabla\cdot{\bf J}_{a}-\varepsilon_{p}-\frac{g}{\rho_{\star}}\int_{z_{r}}^{z}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial t}(\tilde{z},t)\,{\rm d}\tilde{z}

(47)

where
{\bf J}_{a}=-(z-z_{r}){\bf J}_{b}
and
\varepsilon_{p}={\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla(z-z_{r})
as before.
By taking the volume integral of (
47
), the evolution
equation for the global APE
E_{a}
is easily shown to be

\frac{dE_{a}}{dt}=\underbrace{-\int_{V}\rho_{\star}bw\,{\rm d}V}_{-\Phi_{z}}\underbrace{-\oint_{S}\rho_{\star}e_{a}{\bf v}\cdot{\bf n}dS}_{S_{APE}^{adv}}+\underbrace{\oint_{S}\rho_{\star}(z-z_{r}){\bf J}_{b}\cdot{\bf n}\,{\rm d}S}_{S_{APE}^{diff}}-\underbrace{\int_{V}\rho_{\star}\varepsilon_{p}\,{\rm d}V}_{\Phi_{d}-\Phi_{i}},

(48)

where the expressions for
\Phi_{i}
and
\Phi_{d}
are

\begin{split}\Phi_{i}=&-\int_{V}\rho_{\star}\varepsilon_{p,lam}=\rho_{\star}gA\left[\alpha\kappa_{T}\Delta\theta-\beta\kappa_{S}\Delta S\right]\\
=&\rho_{\star}gA\kappa_{T}\beta\Delta S\left[\frac{\alpha\Delta\theta}{\beta\Delta S}-\frac{\kappa_{S}}{\kappa_{T}}\right]=\rho_{\star}gA\kappa_{T}\beta\Delta S\left(R_{\rho}^{bulk}-\tau\right)\end{split}

(49)

\Phi_{d}=\int_{V}\rho_{\star}\varepsilon_{p,tur}=\int_{V}g(\alpha\kappa_{T}\nabla\theta-\beta\kappa_{S}\nabla S)\cdot\nabla z_{r}\rho_{\star}\,{\rm d}V

(50)

where
\Delta\theta=\overline{\theta}_{top}-\overline{\theta}_{bottom}
and
\Delta S=\overline{S}_{top}-\overline{S}_{bottom}
,
while
R_{\rho}^{bulk}
is a bulk density ratio. For canonical salt finger instability,

\Phi_{i}>0
as required to act as a source of APE. Note that the last term in (
47
) may be written in the form
F(z,t)-F(z_{r},t)
, with
F
such that
\partial F/\partial z(z,t)=\partial\rho_{0}/\partial t(z,t)
. Since the adiabatic
re-arrangement
{\bf x}\rightarrow{\bf x}_{r}
is isochoric so that
{\rm d}V={\rm d}V_{r}
,
one must have
\int_{V}(F(z,t)-F(z_{r},t))\,{\rm d}V=\int_{V}F(z)\,{\rm d}V=\int_{V_{r}}F(z_{r})\,{\rm d}V_{r}=
provided that the volume
V
remains unaffected by the re-arrangement as also
assumed by W95.

Eqs. (
48
) and (
40
) exhibit two key differences that establishes that despite
E_{a}^{W95}
and
E_{a}
being equal, their respective
budgets are neither mathematically nor physically equivalent:
1) in W95, the reversible conversion with kinetic energy
\Phi_{z}
is associated with the density flux
\rho gw
but with the buoyancy flux
-bw
in the local APE framework; 2) in W95, the advective flux of APE through boundaries
S_{APE}^{adv}
is associated with the flux of the bizarre quantity

g(z\rho-\psi)
but with the flux of APE density
e_{a}
in the local framework,
which makes much more sense. The unphysical character of the advective flux of APE in W95 highlights
the well known problematic character of the global APE framework for regional studies, which was one of the key motivations for the quest of a local available energy framework
(
Tailleux 2013a
)
.
For this reason, we believe that the use of the global APE framework should be discontinued and
replaced by the local APE framework.

4.2
Thermodynamics foundations of the APE dissipation rate and BPE

The possibility for the BPE to occasionally act as a source of APE when the APE dissipation rate
becomes negative is thermodynamically puzzling and raises important questions about the nature of
both quantities. The main aim of this section is to clarify this issue in the context of the local
APE theory for a fully compressible multi-component stratified fluid recently developed by
Tailleux 2018
, in order to understand how Boussinesq expressions and interpretations
compare with their exact counterparts. In particular, we aim to show that contrary to
popular wisdom: 1)
\Phi_{d}
is
of the same energy conversion type as
\Phi_{i}
; 2) the quantity taken to be the BPE in the
standard Boussinesq model is not solely made up of gravitational potential energy, but
primarily thermodynamic in nature.

In the local APE theory for a single-component compressible fluid, the expressions for the
local APE and BPE take the form

\Pi=h(\eta,p)-h(\eta,p_{0}(z_{r}))+g(z-z_{r})+\frac{p_{0}(z)-p}{\rho},

(51)

B_{r}=h(\eta,p_{0}(z_{r}))+gz_{r},

(52)

(
Tailleux 2018
)
, where
\eta
, the specific entropy, is assumed to be
governed by the standard evolution equation

\rho\frac{D\eta}{Dt}=-\frac{\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf J}_{q})}{T}+\frac{\rho\varepsilon_{k}}{T}

(53)

where
{\bf J}_{q}=-\kappa_{T}\rho c_{p}\nabla T
is Fourier’s law for
the molecular diffusive flux of heat.
For a compressible fluid, the reference position of a fluid parcel
is solution of level of neutral buoyancy (LNB) equation
\rho(\eta,p_{0}(z_{r}))=\rho_{0}(z_{r})
.
Evolution equations for
\Pi
and
B_{r}
have been derived in
Tailleux 2018
. In the
following, we focus exclusively on the effects of diabatism. For simplicity, the time variations
of the reference state are ignored, as they do not matter for the discussion that follows.
Taking the material derivative of (
51
) yields

\begin{split}\rho\frac{D\Pi}{Dt}=&\rho(T-T_{r})\frac{D\eta}{Dt}+C(\Pi,E_{k})\\
=&-\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right)\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf J}_{q})+\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right)\rho\varepsilon_{k}+C(\Pi,E_{k})\\
=&-\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf J}_{a})-\rho\varepsilon_{p}+C(\Pi,E_{k}),\end{split}

(54)

in which
C(\Pi,E_{k})
represents the conversion with kinetic energy, while

{\bf J}_{a}
and
\varepsilon_{p}
are the diffusive flux of
\Pi
and APE dissipation rate
respectively, whose expressions are easily shown to be

{\bf J}_{a}=\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right){\bf J}_{q}

(55)

\varepsilon_{p}={\bf J}_{q}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right)-\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right)\varepsilon_{k},

(56)

e.g.,
Tailleux 2013c
. The budget of local background potential energy is given by

\rho\frac{DB_{r}}{Dt}=\rho T_{r}\frac{D\eta}{Dt}\\
=-\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf J}_{r})+\rho(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{k}),

(57)

where
{\bf J}_{r}=T_{r}{\bf J}_{q}/T
is the diffusive flux of BPE. The Carnot-like thermodynamic
efficiency
(T-T_{r})/T
featuring in the above expressions highlights the thermodynamic
character of APE theory. This thermodynamic efficiency
separates the molecular diffusive heat transfer into the part that participates in the dynamics
from the one that only diffuses heat passively through the reference state.

How to relate the exact APE dissipation rate (
56
) to its Boussinesq approximation was clarified by
Tailleux 2013c
, to which the reader is referred to for
details. In short,
Tailleux 2013c
showed that
\varepsilon_{p}
can be broken down into three components:

\varepsilon_{p}=\varepsilon_{p,lam}+\varepsilon_{p,tur}+\varepsilon_{p,eos}

(58)

a laminar component
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
that relate to W95’s
\Phi_{i}
; a turbulent component

\varepsilon_{p,tur}
that relates to W95’s
\phi_{d}
; and a component that arises for a nonlinear
equation of state, which has no counterpart in a Boussinesq fluid. Physically, these three
different components do not exist as separate identities and therefore must have the same
energy conversion type as that of
\varepsilon_{p}
, which clearly refutes the notion that

\Phi_{d}
and
\Phi_{i}
should be interpreted as different energy conversion types.

From a thermodynamic perspective, it is crucial to establish if
\varepsilon_{p}
is reversible or irreversible. This can be clarified by recalling that a process is considered irreversible if it leads to an irreversible increase in the system’s entropy, as stated by the second law of thermodynamics. Both the viscous dissipation of KE and molecular diffusive heat transfer are
known to be irreversible for they meet this criterion.
However, the irreversibility of APE dissipation is less certain and more disputed. This is primarily because whether APE dissipation increases entropy has remained unproven, and also because
APE dissipation is generally considered fundamentally different from KE dissipation and commonly attributed with characteristics typically associated with reversibility. Indeed, while
viscous processes are accepted to irreversibly and irrecoverably dissipate KE into the IE component of BPE, the prevailing opinion in the turbulent mixing literature is that molecular diffusion irreversibly dissipates APE into the GPE component of the BPE, but not irrecoverably, as it is also assumed that the APE thus dissipated can be recovered and released back into APE by surface cooling, e.g.,
Hughes et al. 2009
.
This view significantly deviates from the traditional concept of irreversibility, which generally equates irreversibility and unrecoverability. This interpretation of APE dissipation has been contested for not being traceable to the full compressible Navier Stokes equations and for being incompatible with standard thermodynamics
(
Tailleux 2009
;
Tailleux 2013c
)
.
A major hindrance to acceptance is that the incompressibility approximation underlying the standard Boussinesq model decouples the dynamics from the thermodynamics. This seems to explain why
the most investigators in the turbulent mixing community tend
to favour interpretations that minimise the role of thermodynamics and internal energy,
even if they contravene the second law or are inconsistent with standard thermodynamics.

We can use the local budget of BPE (
57
) to demonstrate that the dissipation
of KE and APE are physically the same kind of process, at least when they are both positive.
This budget links the APE dissipation rate to the entropy budget in the following way:

\rho\frac{D\eta}{Dt}=\frac{\rho(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{k}+\dot{q}_{r})}{T_{r}}

(59)

where
\rho\dot{q}_{r}=-\nabla\cdot(\rho{\bf J}_{r})
is the local diabatic or cooling
caused by the diffusive heat transfer through the background reference state.
We can also express
\rho\dot{q}_{r}/T_{r}
as

\rho\dot{q}_{r}/T_{r}=-\nabla(\rho{\bf J}_{r}/T_{r})+\rho{\bf J}_{r}\cdot\nabla(1/T_{r})
to rewrite the entropy budget as

\rho\frac{D\eta}{Dt}=\frac{\rho(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{k})}{T_{r}}-\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{\rho{\bf J}_{r}}{T_{r}}\right)+\frac{\rho\kappa c_{p}\nabla T\cdot\nabla T_{r}}{TT_{r}},

(60)

which, after integrating over the volume and assuming no diffusive flux of
B_{r}
through
boundaries, leads to

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\int_{V}\rho\eta\,{\rm d}V=\int_{V}\frac{\rho(\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{k})}{T_{r}}\,{\rm d}V+\int_{V}\frac{\rho\kappa c_{p}\nabla T\cdot\nabla T_{r}}{TT_{r}}\,{\rm d}V.

(61)

This equation is important, because it clearly reveals that the dissipation of KE and APE, as well
as the molecular diffusive heat transfer, are irreversible processes, at least when the APE
dissipation rate is positive, as they then all contribute to increase entropy.
Conversely, the APE dissipation rate should be regarded as reversible when negative,
according to thermodynamics.
Eqs. (
59
-
61
) also indicate that from the viewpoint of the entropy budget,

\varepsilon_{p}
behaves like a source or sink of diabatic heating, similar to
\varepsilon_{k}

or
\dot{q}_{r}
, which means that there is no physical basis for treating
\varepsilon_{p}

differently from
\varepsilon_{k}
. There is considerable evidence that in most turbulent mixing
events, the dissipation ratio
\varepsilon_{p}/\varepsilon_{k}
is less than one and often
close to 20 percent
(
Oakey 1982
)
. As for
\varepsilon_{k}
, it is generally much smaller than
the local rate of heating due to molecular heat transfer, so that

|\varepsilon_{p}|<|\varepsilon_{k}|\ll|\dot{q}|.

(62)

Physically, diabatic effects affect the centre of gravity of the fluid by expanding or contracting the fluid parcels. When both
\varepsilon_{p}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
are positive, they
contribute to raising the centre of gravity by expanding the fluid, but the effect is typically
utterly small.
The only source of diabatic heating large enough to do that is
\dot{q}_{r}
. For

\dot{q}_{r}
to raise the centre of gravity, heating must occur at higher pressure than cooling.
This is exactly what happens in both laminar and turbulent stratified fluids, since molecular and
turbulent mixing both tend to make the temperature or specific entropy homogeneous, which warms up
and cool down the lower and upper layers of the fluid respectively.

To conclude this section, we briefly discuss the relationship between the BPE in standard Boussinesq model and its exact counterpart, namely

B_{r}=e_{r}+\frac{p_{0}(z_{r})}{\rho_{0}(z_{r})}+gz_{r}

(63)

where
e_{r}=h(\eta,p_{0}(z_{r}))-p_{0}(z_{r})/\rho_{0}(z_{r})
and

\rho(\eta,p_{0}(z_{r}))=\rho_{0}(z_{r})
by definition.
Note here that the exact BPE (
63
) is
not just the sum of background IE and GPE, contrary to what is usually assumed, but also includes the term
p_{0}(z_{r})/\rho_{0}(z_{r})
whose presence in the BPE was only recently revealed by
Tailleux 2018
. In the standard Boussinesq approximation, the pressure is assumed to vary linearly with height at leading order,
p_{0}(z_{r})\approx-\rho_{\star}gz_{r}
, and the relative density difference
(\rho-\rho_{\star})/\rho_{\star}
is assumed to be small,
which leads to the following approximation of
B_{r}
:

B_{r}\approx e_{r}+g\left(1-\frac{\rho_{\star}}{\rho_{0}(z_{r})}\right)z_{r}\approx e_{r}+g\left(\frac{\rho_{0}(z_{r})-\rho_{\star}}{\rho_{\star}}\right)z_{r}.

(64)

Since
\rho_{0}(z_{r})
concides with the Boussinesq density
\rho
in the standard Boussinesq approximation, we can also write

B_{r}\approx e_{r}-b_{bou}z_{r}

(65)

where
b_{bou}=-g(\rho-\rho_{\star})/\rho_{\star}
is the classical Boussinesq buoyancy. This establishes reassuringly
that the Boussinesq BPE can easily be linked to its exact counterpart.
However, what is also implies is that the term
-b_{bou}z
in a Boussinesq fluid does not approximate the background GPE, but rather the quantity

\frac{p_{0}(z_{r})}{\rho_{0}(z_{r})}+gz_{r}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad-b_{bou}z_{r}.

(66)

As shown by W95, the rate of change of
\int_{V}-b_{bou}z_{r}\rho_{\star}dV
is given by
the term
\Phi_{d}
, which is positive in a single-component fluid, and is generally interpreted
as implying that irreversible mixing increases the centre of gravity. However, what this argument really means is that it is the quantity on the left-hand side of (
66
)
that increases. Since the material derivative of this quantity can be verified to be

\frac{D}{Dt}\left(\frac{p_{0}(z_{r})}{\rho_{0}(z_{r})}+gz_{r}\right)=p_{r}\frac{D\upsilon_{r}}{Dt},

(67)

it follows that the term
\Phi_{d}
actually approximates the work of expansion/contraction

\int_{V}p_{r}\frac{D\upsilon_{r}}{Dt}\,\rho\,{\rm d}V\quad\leftrightarrow\quad\Phi_{d}

(68)

thus confirming that it should be interpreted as a conversion with internal energy, similarly as
\Phi_{i}
. Indeed, since

{\rm d}e_{r}=T_{r}D\eta/Dt-p_{r}D\upsilon_{r}/Dt
, it follows that what the global APE budget of W95 actually demonstrates is that mixing acts as a net sink of background IE. Since
\varepsilon_{p}
and
\varepsilon_{k}
are too small sources of diabatic heating to contribute significantly to
D\upsilon_{r}/Dt
, it follows that
\Phi_{d}
must represent the rate at which background IE is converted into background GPE due to the downward heat transfer. In other words, turbulent mixing accelerates the IE to GPE conversion associated with the downward heat transfer, as first advocated by
Tailleux 2009
.

Recently,
Tailleux & Dubos 2023
showed that it is always possible to construct the Boussinesq approximation so as to retain the exact form of BPE (
63
). When the
exact form is retained, there is no ambiguity that
\varepsilon_{p}
, like
\varepsilon_{k}
,
represents an energy conversion between APE and the IE part of the BPE.
Tailleux & Dubos 2023

also argued that the standard Boussinesq approximations make un-necessary simplifications that
only deterioriate their energetics without any benefits. Because most of the difficulties associated with correctly interpreting the energetics of the standard Boussinesq model stems from such a model hiding the thermodynamic character of
B_{r}
, efforts are currently underway to develop a new
energetically complete Boussinesq model based on the findings of
Tailleux & Dubos 2023

for which all the above developments take the same form as that for a compressible fluid. We hope that when such a model becomes available, the above derivations and interpretations can become more widely accepted by the turbulent mixing community.

5
Reference state variables

The practical implementation of the above theoretical results require the computation of Lorenz reference density profile
\rho_{0}(z,t)
, as well as of the associated isopycnal temperature and salinity averages necessary for defining spiciness. Here, we provide the mathematical foundations for defining the latter in terms of probability density functions (p.d.f.), which allows one to compute these in practice without the use of sorting. These results pertain to arbitrary domains and unify the p.d.f approaches of
heng Tseng & Ferziger 2001
and
Saenz et al. 2015
, while also making use of previous ideas by
Hochet & Tailleux 2019
and
Hochet et al. 2021
.

5.1
Reference position
z_{r}(\rho,t)
and density profile
\rho_{0}(z,t)

The classical interpretation of Lorenz reference state as an isochoric re-arrangement of fluid parcels is easy to understand and implement algorithmically. However, it does not easily lend
itself to mathematical analysis. Here, we derive expressions for defining and constructing Lorenz reference density profile
\rho_{0}(z,t)
and its inverse function
z_{r}(\rho,t)
[such that
\rho_{0}(z_{r}(\rho,t),t)=\rho
at all times]
in a way that greatly facilitates the study of its mathematical properties. Our approach is not
entirely new, as elements of it can be found in
Winters et al. 1995
or in the p.d.f. approaches
of
heng Tseng & Ferziger 2001
,
Saenz et al. 2015
or
Hochet & Tailleux 2019
;
Hochet et al. 2021
, but is useful to
introduce the approaches discussed next. To that end, let us
assume that the domain containing the fluid is bounded at the top by a rigid lid at
z=0
and at its bottom by a spatially varying bottom topography at
z=-H(x,y)
. We denote by
\hat{V}(z)
the volume between an arbitrary depth
z
and the surface, and by
A(z)=-{\rm d}V/{\rm d}z(z)

the cross-sectional area of the domain at the depth
z
.

Figure 2:
Mapping of physical space (a) onto reference state space (b).

Physically, the reference depth
z=z_{r}(\rho,t)
of a fluid parcel must be such that the volume of water between
z_{r}(\rho,t)
and the surfafe, viz.,

\hat{V}(z_{r})=\int_{z_{r}}^{0}A(z)\,{\rm d}z

(69)

must equal the volume of water in physical space made up of all the fluid parcels of densities
less than
\rho
, viz.,

V(\rho,t)=\int_{V(\rho({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\leq\rho)}{\rm d}V^{\prime}=\int_{V}H(\rho-\rho({\bf x}^{\prime},t))\,{\rm d}V^{\prime}

(70)

where
H
denotes the Heaviside step function defined as in
Winters et al. 1995
,

H(\rho({\bf x},t)-\rho({\bf x}_{0},t))=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\rho({\bf x},t)<\rho({\bf x}_{0},t)\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2},&\rho({\bf x},t)=\rho({\bf x}_{0},t)\\[2.0pt]
1,&\rho({\bf x},t)>\rho({\bf x}_{0},t)\end{array}\right..

Equating the two volumes
\hat{V}(z_{r})=V(\rho,t)
with
V(\rho,t)
yields an implicit
equation for
z_{r}(\rho,t)
, which may be inverted to yield

z_{r}(\rho,t)=\hat{V}^{-1}[V(\rho,t)],

(71)

which in the case where
A(z)={\rm constant}
reduces to

z_{r}(\rho,t)=-\frac{V(\rho,t)}{A}

(72)

as previously derived by
Winters et al. 1995
. As an illustration, Fig.
2
(a) shows the constant Lorenz reference depth
z_{r}(\rho,t)=-1000\,{\rm m}
for a realistic ocean, with panel (b) showing the corresponding position in
reference state space. Construction of Lorenz reference state for a realistic ocean has been discussed in
Saenz et al. 2015
;
Tailleux 2016
;
Tailleux 2021
. Once
z_{r}(\rho,t)
has been determined for a number of target densities, the reference density profile
\rho_{0}(z,t)
may be obtained simply
by solving the implicit relation
\rho_{0}(z_{r}(\rho,t),t)=\rho
at a number of discrete reference
depths, from which an interpolator may be constructed to infer the reference density for other values. Alternatively, using the fact that
\rho=\rho_{0}(z_{r},t)
by definition, one may also solve the equation
\hat{V}(z_{r})=V(\rho,t)
in the form
\hat{V}(z_{r})=V(\rho_{0}(z_{r},t),t)
to infer
\rho_{0}(z_{r},t)
at a number of predetermined target reference depths.

Note that by differentiating the equality
\hat{V}(z_{r})=V(\rho,t)=V(\rho_{0}(z_{r},t),t)
with respect to
z_{r}
, the following useful expression for

\partial\rho_{0}/\partial z_{r}
may be obtained,

\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z}(z_{r},t)=-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial\rho}\right)^{-1}A(z_{r}).

(73)

The above expressions establish that the practical computation of
z_{r}(\rho,t)
and
\rho_{0}(z,t)

can be achieved without resorting to a sorting algorithm.
Indeed, all one has to do is to define a number of target densities
\rho_{i},{i=1,\cdots,N}
at which to compute the corresponding volumes
V(\rho_{i},t)
. Having defined the inverse function
\hat{V}^{-1}(z)
, one may simply compute the corresponding target reference depths
z_{r}(\rho_{i},t)
at which
\rho_{0}(z,t)
may be evaluated. Once all quantities have been determined at the discrete target depths, interpolators can be constructed to find values at other points.

5.2
Construction of isopycnal mean profiles

Physically, the isopycnally-averaged temperature and salinity profiles
\theta_{0}(z,t)
and

S_{0}(z,t)
needed to isolate the
passive spice component need to be obtained as thickness-weighted isopycnal averages,
as illustrated in Fig.
3
, as this is the only physically meaningful way to
define an isopycnal average
(
Young 2012
)
. Mathematically,
\theta_{0}(z,t)
can be
defined as a solution of the following equation:

\int_{z_{r}(\rho,t)}^{0}A(z)\theta_{0}(z,t)\,{\rm d}z=\underbrace{\int_{V(\rho,t)}\theta({\bf x},t)\,{\rm d}V}_{H(\rho,t)}.

(74)

Physically, the right-hand side of (
74
) represents the volume integrated
temperature over the body of water made up of fluid parcels with densities less than
\rho
,
which we denote
H(\rho,t)
. The right-hand side is the volume integral of the thickness-weighted
isopycnally averaged temperature between
z_{r}(\rho,t)
and the surface. An explicit expression
for
\theta_{0}(z,t)
at
z=z_{r}
may be obtained by differentiating (
74
)
with respect to
z_{r}
, which leads to

-A(z_{r})\theta_{0}(z_{r},t)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial\rho}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z_{r}}(z_{r},t)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\theta_{0}(z_{r},t)=\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial\rho}\right)^{-1}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\rho}

(75)

which made use of (
73
).

) is easily implemented in practice. As an illustration,

5.3
Evolution of the reference density profile

By definition,
\rho_{0}(z,t)
must coincide with its thickness-weighted isopycnal average and therefore satisfy (
74
), viz.,

\int_{z_{r}}^{0}A(z)\rho_{0}(z,t)\,{\rm d}z=\underbrace{\int_{V(\rho,t)}\rho({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\,{\rm d}V^{\prime}}_{R(\rho,t)}.

(76)

We now show that this equation can be manipulated to yield an exact expression for the evolution equation satisfied by
\rho_{0}(z,t)
as as well as for the effective diffusivity first introduced by
Winters & d’Asaro 1996
and
Nakamura 1996
. For simplicity, we focus only on the case where the buoyancy flux vanishes through all solid boundaries as well as the surface, but the derivation can easily be extended to account for a non-zero surface buoyancy flux.

To obtain the sought-for evolution equation for
\rho_{0}
, let us take the time derivative of (
76
), which yields

\int_{z_{r}}^{0}A(z)\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial t}(z,t)\,{\rm d}z=\int_{V(\rho,t)}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}({\bf x}^{\prime},t)\,{\rm d}V^{\prime}.

(77)

Now, using the fact that the evolution equation for density may be written as

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\star}{\bf J}_{b}/g),

(78)

(
77
) may be rewritten as follows

\begin{split}\int_{z_{r}}^{0}A(z)\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial t}(z,t)\,{\rm d}z=&\int_{V(\rho,t)}[\nabla\cdot(\rho_{\star}{\bf J}_{b}/g)-{\bf v}\cdot\nabla\rho]\,{\rm d}V^{\prime}\\
=&\oint_{S_{\rho}}(\rho_{\star}/g){\bf J}_{b}\cdot{\bf n}\,{\rm d}S=\oint_{S_{\rho}}(\rho_{\star}/g){\bf J}_{b}\cdot\frac{\nabla\rho}{|\nabla\rho|}\,{\rm d}S\\
=&\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z}(z_{r},t)\oint_{S_{\rho}}(\rho_{\star}/g)\frac{{\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla z_{r}}{|\nabla\rho|}\,{\rm d}S\\
=&-\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z}(z_{r},t)\oint_{S_{\rho}}(\rho_{\star}/g)\frac{\varepsilon_{p,tur}}{|\nabla\rho|}\,{\rm d}S.\end{split}

(79)

In the second line,
S_{\rho}
refers to the isopycnal surface
\rho={\rm constant}
, meaning that the outward normal unit vector
{\bf n}=\nabla\rho/|\nabla\rho|=-\nabla z_{r}/|\nabla z_{r}|
as schematically illustrated in Fig.
2
(a). To go from the second to third line we used the fact that as
\rho=\rho_{0}(z_{r},t)
, we have
\nabla\rho=\partial\rho_{0}/\partial z_{r}(z_{r},t)\nabla z_{r}
. To go from the third to fourth line, we used the fact that by definition
{\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla z_{r}=-\varepsilon_{p,tur}
.

By defining the reference squared buoyancy profile as

N_{0}^{2}(z_{r},t)=-\frac{g}{\rho_{\star}}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z}(z_{r},t)

(80)

and the locally-defined effective diffusivity as

\kappa_{\rm eff}=\frac{\varepsilon_{p,turb}}{N_{0}^{2}(z_{r})},

(81)

(
79
) may be rewritten as

\int_{z_{r}}^{0}A(z)\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial t}(z,t)\,{\rm d}z=-A(z_{r})K_{\rm eff}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z_{r}}(z_{r},t)=\frac{\partial R}{\partial t},

(82)

where the globally defined effective diffusivity is related to the locally-defined one by

K_{\rm eff}=\frac{1}{A(z_{r})}\oint_{S_{\rho}}\frac{\kappa_{\rm eff}}{|\nabla z_{r}|}\,{\rm d}S.

(83)

as previously established by
Hochet et al. 2019
.
The final step of the derivation consists in differentiating
(
82
) with respect to
z_{r}
, which yields

\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial t}(z_{r},t)=\frac{1}{A(z_{r})}\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{r}}\left[A(z_{r})K_{\rm eff}\frac{\partial\rho_{0}}{\partial z_{r}}\right](z_{r},t).

(84)

Note that Eq. (
83
) is impractical for calculating the effective diffusivity
K_{\rm eff}(z_{r},t)
. In practice,
K_{\rm eff}
can be determined by inverting the diffusion equation (
84
) from the knowledge of the temporal variations of the sorted density profile
\rho_{0}(z,t)
. Alternatively, one may combine Eqs. (
73
) and (
82
) to verify that the globally-defined effective diffusivity may be written as

K_{\rm eff}(z_{r},t)=\frac{1}{A^{2}(z_{r})}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\rho}(\rho,t)\frac{\partial R}{\partial t}(\rho,t)

(85)

which in principle allows one to calculate
K_{\rm eff}
using simple binning techniques. The above results establish that the effective diffusivity only depends on the turbulent part
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
of
\varepsilon_{p}
, consistent with
established results.

6
Summary, discussion, and conclusions

Double diffusive instabilities are mysterious as they can seemingly develop from a state with zero initial mechanical energy, without the need for any external mechanical source(s) of energy (except for that of the initial perturbation triggering the instability). In the context of APE theory, such instabilities are possible only if the mechanical energy KE+APE can grow at the expense of BPE. This can happen only if the net mechanical energy dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}+\varepsilon_{k}
— the net energy conversion between KE+APE and BPE — occasionally becomes locally negative. Since
\varepsilon_{k}
is positive definite, this requires that the APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}
be not only negative but also large enough to overcome viscous processes in parts of the fluid. Therefore, it is more logical and physically plausible to consider the sign of the APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}={\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla(z-z_{r})
, rather than the sign of the diapycnal component of the buoyancy flux proposed by
Middleton & Taylor 2020
, as the most fundamental criterion for characterising double diffusive instabilities. In the context of
Winters et al. 1995
framework, this is equivalent to associating the development of double diffusive instabilities with the sign of
\Phi_{d}-\Phi_{i}
rather than that of
\Phi_{d}
only. This is justified by the fact that
\Phi_{d}
is only negative for diffusive convection but not for salt finger instability, while
\Phi_{d}-\Phi_{i}
is negative for both. We were also able to link the APE dissipation rate to the entropy budget explicitly; this is an important result, which confirms that the APE dissipation rate should be regarded as irreversible when positive, but reversible when negative. It also confirms that the APE dissipation rate should be regarded as a regular form of diabatic heating or cooling, converting APE into the IE component of BPE.

In our study, we opted for the local APE framework over
Winters et al. 1995
global APE framework to address these issues. Despite its mathematical correctness, the global APE framework has several unphysical aspects that can sometimes lead to erroneous conclusions and/or interpretations. Examples include its unsuitability for regional studies and the inability to meaningfully decompose the sign-indefinite integrand
\rho g(z-z_{r})
into mean and eddy components. These issues, which tend to be overlooked in the turbulent mixing community, are what prompted the quest for local available energy principles, as discussed in
Tailleux 2013a
. Now that the local APE theory has reached a mature stage, in would seem preferable to discontinue the use of the global APE framework to avoid the difficulties attached to it.

The Boussinesq APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}={\bf J}_{b}\cdot\nabla(z-z_{r})
has been proven to represent the Boussinesq approximation of its exact counterpart. For a single-component compressible fluid, the expression for the APE dissipation rate is

\rho\varepsilon_{p}=\kappa c_{p}\nabla T\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{T-T_{r}}{T}\right)

(86)

(
Tailleux 2009
;
Tailleux 2013c
)
. Eq. (
86
) features a Carnot-like thermodynamic efficiency
(T-T_{r})/T
underscoring the inherent thermodynamic character of turbulent stratified mixing and APE dissipation, which is hidden by the Boussinesq approximation.
Tailleux 2013c
demonstrated that (
86
) could be broken down into three parts: a laminar component
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
related to the term
-\Phi_{i}
in W95; a turbulent term
\varepsilon_{p,turb}
related to the term
\Phi_{d}
in W95; and a term
\varepsilon_{p,eos}
that arises from the nonlinearities of the equation of state and has no equivalent in the standard Boussinesq model and W95. However, these components do not represent separate energy conversions as they do not exist as separate physical entities; they are all of the same nature as
\varepsilon_{p}
, which goes against W95’s view that
\Phi_{d}
and
\Phi_{i}
are distinct energy conversion types, the former with BPE and the latter with IE. In the literature,
\Phi_{i}
or
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
are often neglected based on the assumption that they are negligible in fully turbulent flows. However, this may not be the case, as
\Phi_{i}
appears to be a significant term in many published studies. For example,
\Phi_{i}
is comparable to
\Phi_{d}
in the simulations discussed by W95. It is also ignored by
Middleton & Taylor 2020
on the ground that it could be hypothetically small, when diagnosing
\Phi_{i}
in their canonical salt finger instability would have shown its dominant role. It is regrettable that
Middleton & Taylor 2020
did not diagnose
\Phi_{i}
, which would have undoubtedly demonstrated its fundamental importance. We hope to illustrate this behaviour in the future by using an energy complete Boussinesq approximation currently under development and based on the findings of
Tailleux & Dubos 2023
.

Middleton & Taylor 2020
characterised
\Phi_{d}
in terms of three physical parameters that could also be used to characterise
\varepsilon_{p}
. However, these parameters (save for the diffusivity ratio
\kappa_{S}/\kappa_{T}
) are strongly affected by turbulence and the micro-structures of
\theta
and
S
, so diagnosing them from observational data or environmental parameters, or even linking them to parameters used in previous studies (such as the density ratio), is not straightforward. To overcome some of these difficulties,
Middleton et al. 2021
subsequently reformulated the problem in terms of density/spiciness variable, using a linear spiciness variable to achieve a more physical and useful characterisation of
\Phi_{d}
. Nevertheless, this approach has its own limitations, as this spiciness variable is suboptimal for measuring spiciness because it is not properly calibrated to vanish in a spiceless fluid, as shown by
Tailleux 2021
. By defining spiciness as an isopycnal anomaly instead, as recommended by
Tailleux 2021
, we came up with a new set of physical parameters that we used to characterise
\varepsilon_{p}
: a density ratio and a new spiciness parameter more in line with the parameters used in previous studies. However, estimating these parameters in numerical simulations or in the field requires the computation of thickness-weighted isopycnal averages, which most non-oceanographers are likely to be unfamiliar with.
For this reason and to facilitate the adoption of our ideas, we provided explicit mathematical expressions and practical examples to show how to implement this form of averaging in practice, valid even for a fully compressible and nonlinear ocean, and illustrated it on a realistic oceanographic example with real data.

While the presence of double diffusive instabilities is determined by the sign of the full APE dissipation rate
\varepsilon_{p}=\varepsilon_{p,lam}+\varepsilon_{p,tur}
, the effective diapycnal diffusivity controlling the evolution of the sorted density profile is only determined by the turbulent part
\varepsilon_{p,tur}
. Consequently, the conventional expressions for turbulent diapycnal diffusivity and dissipation ratio should be formulated as
K_{v}=\varepsilon_{p,turb}/N^{2}
and
\Gamma=\varepsilon_{p}/\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon_{p,turb}/\varepsilon_{k}
rather than in terms of the full
\varepsilon_{p}
. This is consistent with the common practice, but it may not be necessarily obvious if one considers that
\varepsilon_{p,lam}
(equivalently
\Phi_{i}
) is part of the definition of the net APE dissipation rate, which departs from the prevailing view that only
\varepsilon_{p,turb}
or
\Phi_{d}
contributes to it. As a consequence, not all double-diffusive instabilities can be expected to exhibit negative effective diffusivities, which, for instance, are not present in the initial stages of the canonical salt finger instability discussed by
Middleton & Taylor 2020
.
Our theory shows that double diffusion can enhance, reduce, or even change the sign of
K_{v}
, whose consequences for the study of mixing in the oceans need to be further investigated. The implications for the theoretical understanding of thermohaline staircases is unclear, as recent theories tend to emphasise the spatial variations (linked to the functional dependence on some turbulent parameters such as the buoyancy Reynolds number) of an otherwise positive effective diffusivity as the main causee, e.g., see
Radko 2005
,
Taylor & Zhou 2017
or
Ma & Peltier 2022b
;
Ma & Peltier 2022a
and references therein for some recent discussion. Further research is clearly needed to clarify the issue, but beyond the scope of this paper.

In the study of standard turbulent stratified mixing where both
\varepsilon_{k}
and
\varepsilon_{p}
are positive, the BPE primarily acts as a passive recipient for the dissipated APE and KE, and is therefore essentially irrelevant. It does not really matter whether APE dissipates into the internal or gravitational potential energy part of the BPE. In practice, mixing and the effective diapycnal diffusivity can be calculated from the turbulent APE dissipation rate
\Phi_{d}
or inferred from the evolution of the sorted density profile; neither require knowledge of the BPE as such. The BPE becomes centrally important, however, when it is the mechanical energy source for double-diffusive instabilities. In the context of moist energetics,
Harris et al. 2022
have referred to the part of the BPE that can occasionally become convertible into APE as latent APE (LAPE).
Smyth et al. 2005
and
Ma & Peltier 2021
have tried to link the latter to the APE of the individually sorted salinity and temperature profiles, but such an approach is ad-hoc and its physical justification unclear. Moreover, the possibility that the IE part of the BPE, not the GPE part, is the energy source for double diffusive instabilities, raises the question of how the conversions with an energy reservoir that is not explicit in the standard Boussinesq model can be correctly represented. We hope to address this question in future studies, using energetically and thermodynamically complete Boussinesq models based on the recent findings of
Tailleux & Dubos 2023
.
