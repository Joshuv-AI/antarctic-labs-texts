A Framework for Combining Entity Resolution and Query Answering in Knowledge Bases
Ronald Fagin, Phokion G. Kolaitis, Domenico Lembo, Lucian Popa, Federico Scafoglieri

Abstract
We propose a new framework for combining entity resolution and query answering in knowledge bases (KBs) with tuple-generating dependencies (tgds) and equality-generating dependencies (egds) as rules. We define the semantics of the KB in terms of special instances that involve equivalence classes of entities and sets of values. Intuitively, the former collect all entities denoting the same real-world object, while the latter collect all alternative values for an attribute. This approach allows us to both resolve entities and bypass possible inconsistencies in the data. We then design a chase procedure that is tailored to this new framework and has the feature that it never fails; moreover, when the chase procedure terminates, it produces a universal solution, which in turn can be used to obtain the certain answers to conjunctive queries. We finally discuss challenges arising when the chase does not terminate.

Introduction

Entity resolution is the problem of determining whether different data records refer to the same real-world object, such as the same individual or the same organization, and so on
[
\citeauthoryearBenjelloun et al.2009
,
\citeauthoryearPapadakis et al.2021
]
.
In this paper, we study entity resolution in combination with query answering in the context of knowledge bases (KBs) consisting of ground atoms and rules specified as tuple-generating dependencies (tgds) and equality-generating dependencies (egds).
These rules have been widely investigates in databases and knowledge representation, e.g. in
[
\citeauthoryearBeeri and Vardi1984
,
\citeauthoryearCalì et al.2004
,
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
,
\citeauthoryearBaget et al.2011
,
\citeauthoryearCuenca Grau et al.2013
,
\citeauthoryearKrötzsch, Marx, and
Rudolph2019
]
; in particular, they allow expressing both data transformations and classical axioms used in Description Logic (DL)
[
\citeauthoryearBaader et al.2007
,
\citeauthoryearArtale et al.2009
]
and Datalog +/- ontologies
[
\citeauthoryearCalì, Gottlob, and
Lukasiewicz2009
]
.
In addition, egds are employed to express typical entity resolution rules that one may write in practice, i.e., rules that enforce equality between two entities, as in
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Cima, and

]
.

To capture entity resolution rules, we allow egds to contain atoms involving built-in predicates, such as
\mathit{JaccSim}
. In the example, rule
s_{1}
states that two names (i.e., strings) with Jaccard similarity above
0.6
must belong to the same individual. Rules
s_{2}
and
s_{3}
stipulate that an individual has at most one name and at most one landline home phone number, respectively. We call
entity-egds
rules that impose equality on two entities (e.g.,
s_{1}
), and we call
value-egds
rules that impose equality on two values (e.g.,
s_{2}
and
s_{3}
).

It is now easy to see that, according to the standard semantics, the database in Fig.
1(a)
does not satisfy the entity-egd
s_{1}
(note that the Jaccard similarity of
John Doe
and
J. Doe
is
0.625
). Thus, the main challenge one has in practice is to come up with a consistent way to complete or modify the original KB, while respecting all its rules.

In this paper, we develop a framework for entity resolution and query answering in KBs, where the valid models, called
KB solutions
, must satisfy all entity resolution rules along with all other KB rules; furthermore, the solutions must include all original data (i.e., no information is ever dropped or altered). Our approach is guided by the intuitive principle that for each real-world object there must be a single node in the solution that represents all “equivalent” entities denoting the object.
To achieve this, we use equivalence classes of entities, which become first-class citizens in our framework.
In addition, we relax
the standard way in which value-egds are satisfied by allowing solutions to use sets of values, thus collecting together all possible values for a given attribute (e.g., the second argument of
\mathit{Name}
or
\mathit{HPhone}
).
Continuing with the above example, Fig.
1(b)
shows a new set of ground atoms that uses equivalence classes and sets of values.

We remark that the use of equivalence classes of entities and sets of values in KB solutions requires a drastic revision of the classical notion of satisfaction for tgds and egds.
Intuitively, we interpret egds as matching dependencies
[
\citeauthoryearBertossi, Kolahi, and
Lakshmanan2013
,
\citeauthoryearFan2008
]
. That is, when the conditions expressed by (the body of) an entity-egd or a value-egd hold in the data (and thus two entities or two values must be made equal),
we combine them through a merging function. We adopt a general and common merging function that takes the union of the entities or the union of the values. This function actually belongs to the
Union class of match and merge functions
analyzed in
[
\citeauthoryearBenjelloun et al.2009
,
\citeauthoryearBertossi, Kolahi, and
Lakshmanan2013
]
.
In this way, we group all possible alternatives for denoting an object into a unique set of entities. Similarly, when two
values exist where only one value is instead allowed according to the TBox, we explicitly form their union.
Note that we consider the union of entities a “global” feature for a solution, that is, an entity
e
may belong to exactly one equivalence class; in contrast, the union of values is “local” to the context in which a value occurs, that is, a particular value may belong to more than one set of values. For instance, let us add
\mathit{HPhone}(\textnormal{{Doe}}_{3},\texttt{358})
to the KB of our example. Then, in a solution satisfying the KB,
358
belongs to two different sets: the one shown in Fig.
1(b)
(i.e.,
\{\texttt{358},\texttt{635}\}
) and the singleton
\{\texttt{358}\}
, which is the set of landline home phone numbers of
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{3}
.

It is worth noting that the semantics we adopt for value-egds allows us to always have a solution (that is, a model) for a KB, even when there are no models according to the standard first-order semantics; for example,
if a value-egd enforces the equality of two different values, then first-order logic concludes that the KB is inconsistent. We thus may say that our semantics is inconsistency tolerant with respect to violations of value-egds. Indeed, we collect together all possible alternative values for individual attributes, whereas in data cleaning the task is to choose one of the alternatives.

We also point out that value-egds may substantially affect entity resolution. As an example, consider the database in Fig.
1(a)
and on top of it the new set of rules given below

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}(s^{\prime}_{1})&\mathit{Name}(p_{1},n_{1})\wedge\mathit{HPhone}(p_{1},f)\wedge\mathit{Name}(p_{2},n_{2})\wedge\\
&\mathit{HPhone}(p_{2},f)\wedge\mathit{JaccSim}(n_{1},n_{2},0.6)\rightarrow p_{1}=p_{2}\\
(s^{\prime}_{2})&\mathit{Name}(p_{1},n_{1})\wedge\mathit{Name}(p_{2},n_{2})\wedge\mathit{HPhone}(p_{1},f_{1})\wedge\\
&\mathit{HPhone}(p_{2},f_{2})\wedge\mathit{JaccSim}(n_{1},n_{2},0.6)\rightarrow f_{1}=f_{2}\\[8.53581pt]
\end{array}

Rule
s^{\prime}_{1}
states that two entities having similar names and the same landline home phone number denote the same real-world individual, while rule
s^{\prime}_{2}
says that two entities with similar names must have the same phone number. It is easy to see that a TBox having only rule
s^{\prime}_{1}
would not lead to inferring that
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{1}
and
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{2}
denote the same individual.
However, by virtue of rule
s^{\prime}_{2}
, a solution has to group together the two telephone numbers into the same set, thus saying that
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{1}
and
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{2}
have both the landline home phone numbers
\{\texttt{635},\texttt{358}\}
. This union “fires” rule
s^{\prime}_{1}
, and thus entities are resolved, i.e., are put together into the same equivalence class of the solution.

We finally note that, in order to maximize entity resolution, our semantics allows the body a rule to be satisfied by assignments in which different occurrences of the same value-variable are replaced by different sets of values, as long as these sets have a non-empty intersection (instead of requiring that all occurrences are replaced by the same set of values).

For instance, if we add to the rules in Fig.
2
the tgd

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{HPhone}(p_{1},f)\wedge\mathit{HPhone}(p_{2},f)\rightarrow\mathit{SameHouse}(p_{1},p_{2},f)\\[8.53581pt]
\end{array}

stating that two entities with the same home phone number live in the same house, and to the database in Fig.
1(a)
the atom
\mathit{HPhone}(\textnormal{{Doe}}_{3},\texttt{358})
, we can conclude that the individual denoted by
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{3}
and the individual denoted by
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{1}
and
\textnormal{{Doe}}_{2}
live in the same house (since they share one phone number).onsistently with this choice, we also require tgds to “propagate” such intersections. Then,
through the above tgd, we also infer that the phone number of the house is
\{\texttt{358}\}
,which we denote with a fact of the form
\mathit{SameHouse}([\textnormal{{Doe}}_{1},\textnormal{{Doe}}_{2}],[\textnormal{{Doe}}_{3}],\{\texttt{358}\})
.
Note that the behaviour we described differs from the standard one only for value-variables, since two equivalence classes of entities are always either disjoint or the same class.

In this paper, we formalize the aforementioned ideas and investigate query answering, with focus on conjunctive queries (CQs).
The main contributions are as follows.

•

We propose a new framework for entity resolution in KBs consisting of tgds and egds, and give rigorous semantics.

•

We define
universal solutions
and show that, as for standard tgd and egd semantics, universal solutions can be used to obtain the certain answers of CQs.

•

We propose a variant of the classical
chase
procedure
[
\citeauthoryearBeeri and Vardi1984
,
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
tailored to our approach.
An important feature of our chase procedure is that it never fails, even in the presence of egds. At the same time, as in other frameworks, our chase procedure might not terminate.

•

We show that, when the chase procedure terminates, it returns a universal solution (and thus we have an algorithm for computing the certain answers to conjunctive queries).

•

When the chase procedure does not terminate, defining the result of the chase becomes a more intricate task.

We point out that the strategy proposed in
[
\citeauthoryearBeeri and Vardi1984
]
does not work in our framework. Thus a different notion of the result of the chase is needed, which we leave for future study.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give some background notions useful for the following treatment. In Section
3
we propose our framework and give the notions of instance, solution and universal solution for a KB. In Section
4
we define query answering and show that for CQs it can be solved through query evaluation over a universal solution. In Section
5
we first define the chase procedure in our setting, and discuss the case of terminating chase sequences, for which the chase procedure returns a universal solution; then we attack the challenging case of non-terminating chase sequences, for which we show that previously proposed approaches cannot directly adapted to our setting. In Section
6
we discuss some related work, and in Section
7
we conclude the paper.

Basic Notions

Equivalence relations.

If
\mathcal{Z}
is a set, then an
equivalence relation on
\mathcal{Z}
is a binary relation

\theta\subseteq\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
that is reflexive (i.e.,
x\>\theta\>x
, for every
x\in\mathcal{Z}
), symmetric (i.e.,
x\>\theta\>y
implies
y\>\theta\>x
, for every
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}
), and transitive (i.e.,
x\>\theta\>y
and
y\>\theta\>z
imply
x\>\theta\>z
, for every
x,y,z\in\mathcal{Z}
).

If
x\in\mathcal{Z}
, then the
equivalence class of
x
w.r.t.
\theta
is the set
[x]_{\theta}=\{y\in\mathcal{Z}\>\mid\>y\>\theta\>x\>\}
. Sometimes, we will write
[x]
instead of
[x]_{\theta}
, if
\theta
is clear from the context. Moreover, we may write, e.g.,
[a,b,c]_{\theta}
(or simply
[a,b,c]
) to denote the equivalence class consisting of the elements
a
,
b
, and
c
.

The equivalence classes of an equivalence relation
\theta
have the following well known properties: (i) each equivalence class is non-empty; (ii) for all
x,y\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have that
x\theta y
if and only if
[x]_{\theta}=[y]_{\theta}
; (iii) two equivalence classes are either equal sets or disjoint sets. Consequently, the equivalence classes of
\theta
form a partition of
\mathcal{Z}
. The
quotient set
\mathcal{Z}/\theta
of
\theta
on
\mathcal{Z}
is the set of all equivalence classes over
\mathcal{Z}
w.r.t.
\theta
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Z}/\theta=\{[x]_{\theta}\>|\>x\in\mathcal{Z}\}
. Obviously, given a quotient set
\mathcal{Z}/\theta
, we have that
\mathcal{Z}=\underset{[x]_{\theta}\in\mathcal{Z}/\theta}{\bigcup}[x]_{\theta}
.

Alphabets and conjunctions of atoms.
We consider four pairwise disjoint alphabets
\mathcal{S}_{P}
,
\mathcal{S}_{E}
,
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
. The set
\mathcal{S}_{P}
is a finite alphabet for predicates; it is partitioned into the sets
\mathcal{S}_{O}
and
\mathcal{S}_{B}
, which are the alphabets for
KB
predicates and
built-in
predicates, respectively. The sets
\mathcal{S}_{E}
,
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
are countable infinite alphabets for
entities
,
values
, and
variables
, respectively. For ease of exposition, we do not distinguish between different data types, thus
\mathcal{S}_{V}
is a single set containing all possible values.

The number of arguments of a predicate

P\in\mathcal{S}_{P}

is the
arity
of
P
, denoted with
\mathit{arity}(P)
. With each
n
-ary predicate
P
we associate a tuple
\mathit{type}(P)=\langle\rho_{1},\ldots\rho_{n}\rangle
, such that, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, either
\rho_{i}=\mathsf{e}
or
\rho_{i}=\mathsf{v}
. This tuple specifies the
types of the arguments of
P
, i.e., whether each argument of
P
ranges over entities (
\mathsf{e}
) or values (
\mathsf{v}
). We also write
\mathit{type}(P,i)
for the
type

\rho_{i}
of the
i
-th argument of
P
.

Note that the built-in predicates from
\mathcal{S}_{B}
are special, pre-interpreted predicates, whose arguments range only over values, i.e., if
B
is an
n
-ary built-in predicate, then
\mathit{type}(B)
is the
n
-ary tuple
\langle\mathsf{v},\ldots,\mathsf{v}\rangle
.
The examples in Sec.
1

use the Jaccard similarity
\mathit{JaccSim}
as a built-in predicate.

An
atom
is an expression
P(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
, where
P\in\mathcal{S}_{P}
, and each
t_{i}
is either a variable from
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
or a constant, which in turn is either an entity from
\mathcal{S}_{E}
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{e}
, or a value from
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{v}
. When
t_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
, we call it an
entity-variable
if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{e}
, or a
value-variable
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{v}
. A
ground
atom is an atom with no variables.

A
conjunction
\phi(\mathbf{x})
of atoms
is an expression
P_{1}(\mathbf{t}_{1})\land\ldots\land P_{m}(\mathbf{t}_{m})
,

where each
P_{j}(\mathbf{t}_{j})
is an atom such that each variable in
\mathbf{t}_{j}
is among those in
\mathbf{x}
.
We also require that every variable
x
in
\mathbf{x}
is either an entity-variable or a value-variable and that, if
x
occurs in an atom whose predicate is built-in (note that thus
x
is a value-variable), then there exists some other atom
P_{j}(\mathbf{t}_{j})
in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
such that
P_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{O}
and
x
is in
\mathbf{t}_{j}
.
If a conjunction contains no built-in predicates, we say that it is
built-in free
;
if it contains no entities or values, we say that it is
constant-free
.

Remark on notation.
As done in this section, in the formulas appearing throughout the paper, we use
e
to denote an entity from
\mathcal{S}_{E}
, we use
v
to denote a value from
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, we use
c
to denote a constant (i.e.,
c\in\mathcal{S}_{E}\cup\mathcal{S}_{V}
), we use

x
,
y
,
w
, and
z
to denote variables from
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}
, and we use
t
to denote terms (i.e.,
t\in\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{V}}\cup\mathcal{S}_{E}\cup\mathcal{S}_{V}
). Typically, we use
P
to denote a predicate from
\mathcal{S}_{P}
. All the above symbols may appear with subscripts.
In the examples, we use self-explanatory symbols, and we write entities in bold font and values in true type font.

3
Framework

In this section, we present the syntax and the semantics of a framework for entity resolution in knowledge bases.

Syntax.
A
knowledge base
(KB)
\mathcal{K}
is a pair
(\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D})
, consisting of a TBox
\mathcal{T}
and a database
\mathbf{D}
.
The TBox is a finite set of
tuple-generating dependencies
(tgds) and
equality-generating dependencies
(egds).
A
tgd
is a formula

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\forall\mathbf{x}(\phi(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\exists\mathbf{y}\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})),\end{array}

where
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
are conjunctions of atoms, such that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
have no variables in common and
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is built-in free and, for simplicity, constant-free. As in
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
, we assume that all variables in
\mathbf{x}
appear in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
, but not necessarily in
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. We call
\phi(\mathbf{x})
the body of the tgd, and
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
the head of it.

An
egd
is a formula

\forall\mathbf{x}(\phi(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow y=z),

(2)

where
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is a conjunction of atoms and
y
and
z
are distinct variables occurring in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
, such that either both
y
and
z
are entity-variables (in which case we have an
entity-egd
) or both
y
and
z
are value-variables (in which case we have a
value-egd
). We call
\phi(\mathbf{x})
the body of the egd. For
value-egds
, we require that neither
y
nor
z
occur in atoms having a built-in as predicate. This ensures that the meaning of built-ins remains fixed.
We will write
\mathsf{body}(r)
to denote the body of a tgd or an egd
r
; furthermore, we may write
r
with no quantifiers.

Example 1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be the TBox consisting of the rules

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}(r_{1})&\mathit{CI}(p_{1},\mathit{name}_{1},\mathit{phone}_{1})\wedge\mathit{CI}(p_{2},\mathit{name}_{2},\mathit{phone}_{2})\wedge\\
&\mathit{JaccSim}(\mathit{name}_{1},\mathit{name}_{2},0.6)\rightarrow p_{1}=p_{2}\\
(r_{2})&\mathit{CI}(p,\mathit{name}_{1},\mathit{phone}_{1})\wedge\mathit{CI}(p,\mathit{name}_{2},\mathit{phone}_{2})\\
&\rightarrow\mathit{name}_{1}=\mathit{name}_{2}\\
(r_{3})&\mathit{CI}(p,\mathit{name}_{1},\mathit{phone}_{1})\wedge\mathit{CI}(p,\mathit{name}_{2},\mathit{phone}_{2})\\
&\rightarrow\mathit{phone}_{1}=\mathit{phone}_{2}\\
(r_{4})&\mathit{CI}(p,\mathit{name},\mathit{phone})\rightarrow\mathit{Emp}(p,\mathit{comp})\wedge\mathit{CEO}(\mathit{comp},\mathit{dir})\\
(r_{5})&\mathit{Emp}(p,\mathit{comp}_{1})\wedge\mathit{Emp}(p,\mathit{comp}_{2})\rightarrow\mathit{comp}_{1}=\mathit{comp}_{2}\\
(r_{6})&\mathit{CI}(p_{1},\mathit{name}_{1},\mathit{phone})\wedge\mathit{CI}(p_{2},\mathit{name}_{2},\mathit{phone})\\
&\rightarrow\mathit{SameHouse}(p_{1},p_{2},\mathit{phone})\end{array}

Here,

,

The predicates have the meaning suggested by their names. In particular, the predicate

maintains contact information of individuals, whereas

Each of the six rules makes an assertion about the predicates. In particular,

states that if the Jaccard similarity of two names is higher then

The TBox also says that everyone can have only one name (

) and only one the landline phone number (

); everyone having contact information is also an employee in some company which in turn has a CEO (

); everyone can work for only one company (
r_{5}
); two individuals with the same landline phone number live in the same house (
r_{6}
). ∎

The database
\mathbf{D}
of a KB
\mathcal{K}
is a finite set of ground atoms of the form

over the alphabets
\mathcal{S}_{P}
,
\mathcal{S}_{E}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, where

is an entity from
\mathcal{S}_{E}
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{e}
, or a value from
\mathcal{S}_{V}
, if

.

To ensure that built-in predicates have the same semantics in every KB,

and

Given a KB

\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{O}\cup\mathbf{D}_{B}
, where
\mathbf{D}_{O}
contains only ground atoms with predicate from
\mathcal{S}_{O}
and
\mathbf{D}_{B}
is the (finite) set of all atoms in
GB
whose built-in predicates and values are mentioned in
\mathcal{T}
and in
\mathbf{D}_{O}
.

Semantics.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{EN}
and
\mathcal{S}_{VN}
be two
infinite, countable, disjoint sets
that are also disjoint from the alphabets introduced in Sec.
2
. We call
\mathcal{S}_{EN}
the set of entity-nulls and
\mathcal{S}_{VN}
the set of value-nulls; their union is referred to as the set of nulls.
We use

\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K})
to denote the signature of a KB
\mathcal{K}
, i.e., the set of symbols of
\mathcal{S}_{P}
,
\mathcal{S}_{E}
and
\mathcal{S}_{V}
occurring in
\mathcal{K}
.

The semantics of a KB is given using
special databases, called KB
instances
, whose ground atoms have components that are either equivalence classes of entities and entity-nulls or non-empty sets of values and value-nulls.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB,
\mathcal{S}
a subset of
(\mathcal{S}_{E}\cap\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K}))\cup\mathcal{S}_{EN}
, and
\sim
an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{S}
, called the equivalence relation
associated
with
\mathcal{K}
. An
instance
\mathcal{I}
for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t.
\sim
is a set of
facts

P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
such that
P\in\mathcal{S}_{P}\cap\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K})
,
\mathit{arity}(P)=n
, and, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have that
T_{i}\neq\emptyset
and either
T_{i}\in{\mathcal{S}/\hskip-3.0pt\sim}
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{e}
, or
T_{i}\subseteq(\mathcal{S}_{V}\cap\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K}))\cup\mathcal{S}_{VN}
, if
\mathit{type}(P,i)=\mathsf{v}
.

To denote an equivalence class in
\mathcal{I}
, we may use the symbol
E
(possibly with a subscript).
Note that an equivalence class
E
in
\mathcal{I}
may contain both entities and entity-nulls. In the sequel, we may simply refer to
E
as an equivalence class of entities. Similarly, a non-empty subset of
(\mathcal{S}_{V}\cap\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K}))\cup\mathcal{S}_{VN}
in
\mathcal{I}
can be simply referred to as a set of values, denoted with the symbol
V
(possibly with a subscript). We will use
T
(possibly with a subscript) to denote a set that can be either an equivalence class of entities or a set of values.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB and let
\mathcal{I}
be an instance for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t. to an equivalence relation
\sim
.

The
active domain of
\mathcal{I}
, denoted by
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I})
, is the set

\{T\mid\mbox{there are $P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})\in\mathcal{I}$ and $i\leq n$ with $T=T_{i}$}\}
.

We write
\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
and
\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
to denote the set of all equivalence classes of entities and the set of all sets of values contained in
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I})
, respectively. Obviously,
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I})=\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})\cup\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
.

The
underlying domain of
\mathcal{I}
, denoted by
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I})
, is the set

\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})\cup\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
, where

\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})=\{e\mid\mbox{there is $E\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})$ and $e\in E$}\}

and

\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I})=\{v\mid\mbox{there is $V\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})$ and $v\in V$}\}
.

If
\mathcal{I}
is an instance of
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t.
\sim
,
then it is easy to see that
\mathcal{I}
is an instance of
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t. the equivalence relation
\sim^{\prime}
induced on
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
by
\sim
. In what follows, we will consider only instances w.r.t. equivalence relations over
\mathcal{S}=\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
.

Furthermore, we may simply call
\mathcal{I}
an instance for
\mathcal{K}
and leave the equivalence relation associated with
\mathcal{I}
implicit.

To define the notions of satisfaction of tgds and egds by an instance, we first introduce the notion of an
assignment
from a conjunction
\phi(\mathbf{x})
of atoms to an instance
\mathcal{I}
of a KB
\mathcal{K}
. To formalize this notion, we need a preliminary transformation of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
that substitutes each occurrence of a value-variable in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
with a fresh variable. We call such fresh variables
set-variables
and denote the result of the transformation
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
. If
x
is a value-variable in
\mathbf{x}
, we write
\mathit{SetVar}(x,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))
to denote the set of fresh variables used in
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
to replace the occurrences of
x
in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
.

For example, if
\phi(\mathbf{x})=P_{1}(x,y,z)\land P_{2}(y,z)\land P_{3}(x,w)
, where
\mathit{type}(P_{1})=\langle\mathsf{e},\mathsf{e},\mathsf{v}\rangle
,
\mathit{type}(P_{2})=\langle\mathsf{e},\mathsf{v}\rangle
, and
\mathit{type}(P_{3})=\langle\mathsf{e},\mathsf{v}\rangle
, then
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))=P_{1}(x,y,S_{1}^{z})\land P_{2}(y,S_{2}^{z})\land P_{3}(x,S_{1}^{w})
, where
S_{1}^{z}
,
S_{2}^{z}
,
S_{1}^{w}
are the fresh set-variables introduced by
\tau
. Also,
\mathit{SetVar}(z,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))=\{S_{1}^{z},S_{2}^{z}\}
and
\mathit{SetVar}(w,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))=\{S_{1}^{w}\}
.
In what follows, if
x
is a value-variable, then each set-variable in
\mathit{SetVar}(x,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))
will have
x
as a superscript.

We are now ready to formally define assignments.

Definition 3
.

Let
\phi(\mathbf{x})
be a conjunction of atoms
and let
\mathcal{I}
be an instance for a KB
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t.
\sim
. An
assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
is a mapping
\mu
from the variables and values in
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
to
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I})
, defined as follows:

1.

\mu(x)
is an equivalence class in
\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
, for every entity-variable
x
;

2.

\mu(v)=V
such that

V\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
and
v\in V
, for every value
v
;

3.

\mu(S)
is a set of values in
\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
, for every set-variable
S
;

4.

\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mu(S_{i}^{x})\neq\emptyset
, for every value-variable
x
such that
\mathit{SetVar}(x,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))=\{S_{1}^{x},\ldots,S_{k}^{x}\}
;

5.

\mathcal{I}
contains a fact of the form
P(\mu(x),\mu(S),[e]_{\sim},\mu(v))
, for each atom
P(x,S,e,v)
of
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
, where
x
is an entity-variable,
S
is a set-variable,
e
is an entity in
\mathcal{S}_{E}
and
v
is a value in
\mathcal{S}_{V}
(the definition generalizes in the obvious way for atoms of different form).

In words, the above definition says that an assignment maps every entity-variable to an equivalence class of entities (Condition 1), every value to a set of values containing it (Condition 2), and every occurrence of a value-variable to a set of values (Condition 3), in such a way that multiple occurrences of the same value-variable are mapped to sets with a non-empty intersection (Condition 4). This captures the intuition that, since predicate arguments ranging over values are interpreted through sets of values, a join between such arguments holds when such sets have a non-empty intersection. Finally, Condition 5 states that
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
is “realized” in
\mathcal{I}
.
Note that an assignment also maps values to sets of values (Condition 2). This allows an assignment to map atoms in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to facts in
\mathcal{I}
, since predicate arguments ranging over values are instantiated in
\mathcal{I}
by sets of values.

If
P(\mathbf{t})
is an atom of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
, we write
\mu(P(\mathbf{t}))
for the fact of
\mathcal{I}
in Condition 5
and call it the
\mu
-image (in
\mathcal{I}
)
of
P(\mathbf{t})
. We write
\mu(\phi(\mathbf{x}))
for the set
\{\mu(P(\mathbf{t}))~|~P(\mathbf{t})\textrm{ occurs in }\phi(\mathbf{x})\}
, and call it the
\mu
-image (in
\mathcal{I}
)
of
\phi(\mathbf{x})
.

We are now ready to define the semantics of tgds and egds.

Definition 4
.

An instance
\mathcal{I}
for a KB
\mathcal{K}
satisfies:

•

a tgd of the form (
3
), if for each assignment
\mu
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
there is an assignment
\mu^{\prime}
from
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to
\mathcal{I}
such that, for each
x
in
\mathbf{x}
occurring in both
\phi(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
:

–

\mu(x)=\mu^{\prime}(x)
, if
x
is an entity-variable;

–

\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mu(S_{i}^{x})\subseteq\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\mu^{\prime}(R_{i}^{x})
, where
\{S_{1}^{x},\ldots,S_{k}^{x}\}=\mathit{SetVar}(x,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))
and
\{R_{1}^{x},\ldots,R_{\ell}^{x}\}=\mathit{SetVar}(x,\tau(\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})))
, if
x
is a value-variable.

Each such assignment
\mu^{\prime}
is called a
head-compatible tgd-extension
of
\mu
to
\mathcal{I}
(or simply a tgd-extension of
\mu
to
\mathcal{I}
).

•

an entity-egd of the form (
2
), if each assignment
\mu
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
is such that
\mu(y)=\mu(z)
;

•

a value-egd of the form (
2
), if each assignment
\mu
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
is such that

\mu(S^{y})=\mu(S^{z})
for all set-variables
S^{y},S^{z}\in\mathit{SetVar}(y,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))\cup\mathit{SetVar}(z,\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x})))
.

In words, Def.
4
expresses the following for each assignment
\mu
from the body of a rule
r
to
\mathcal{I}
:

(1)
If
r
is a tgd, the definition stipulates two different behaviours for frontier variables (i.e., variables occurring in both the body and the head of the tgd).
Specifically, for the tgd to be satisfied, it should be possible to extend
\mu
so that
(i) every frontier entity-variable is assigned to exactly the same equivalence class both in the body and the head of the rule (this is in line with the standard semantics for frontier variables in tgds); and (ii) the intersection of the sets of values assigned in the body of the tgd to the various occurrences of a frontier value-variable
x
is contained in the intersection of the sets of values assigned to the occurrences of
x
in the head of the tgd. Note that, while
\mu
maps all the occurrences of an entity-variable in
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to the same equivalence class, it may map multiple occurrences of a value-variable to different sets of values with non empty-intersection; thus, it is natural that this intersection is “propagated” to the head.

(2)
If
r
is an entity-egd, the notion of satisfaction is standard, since it requires that
\mu
assigns
y
and
z
to the same equivalence class of entities.

(3)
If
r
is a value-egd, we have again to take into account that each occurrence of
y
in the rule body can be assigned by
\mu
to a different set of values (provided that all such sets have a non-empty intersection), and analogously for each occurrence of
z
. The definition stipulates that, for the value-egd to be satisfied, all such sets of values are equal.

. ∎

.

\mathcal{I}

Universal solutions.
It is well known that the
universal
solutions exhibit good properties that make them to be the preferred solutions (see, e.g.,

[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
,
\citeauthoryearCalì, Gottlob, and
Kifer2013
,
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
]
).
To introduce the notion of a universal solution in our framework, we first need to adapt the notion of homomorphism.
We begin with some auxiliary definitions and notation.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathbf{T}=\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
and

\mathbf{T^{\prime}}=\langle T^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,T^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
be two tuples such that each
T_{i}
and each
T^{\prime}_{i}
is either a set of entities and entity-nulls or a set of values and value-nulls.

•

\mathbf{T^{\prime}}

dominates

\mathbf{T}
,
denoted
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
, if
T_{i}\subseteq T^{\prime}_{i}
, for all
i
.

•

\mathbf{T^{\prime}}

strictly dominates

\mathbf{T}
, denoted
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{T}^{\prime}
,
if
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
and
\mathbf{T}\neq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
.

•

Let
P(\mathbf{T})
and
P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})
be facts.

P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})

dominates

P(\mathbf{T})
,
denoted
P(\mathbf{T})\leq P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})
, if
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
;

P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})

strictly dominates

P(\mathbf{T})
, denoted
P(\mathbf{T})<P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})
, if
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
.

Definition 7
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
be two instances of a KB
\mathcal{K}
.
A homomorphism
h:\mathcal{I}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{2}
is a mapping from the elements of
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
to elements of
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
such that:

1.

h(e)=e
, for every entity
e
in
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{E}
;

2.

h(e_{\bot})
belongs to
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
, for every entity-null
e_{\bot}
in
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{EN}
;

3.

h(v)=v
, for every value
v
in
\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{V}
;

4.

h(v_{\bot})
belongs to
\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
, for every value-null
v_{\bot}
in
\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})\cap\mathcal{S}_{VN}
;

5.

for every
P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, there is a
P(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}_{2}
such that
P(h(T_{1}),\ldots,h(T_{n}))\leq P(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
, where
h(T_{i})=\{h(x)\mid x\in T_{i})\}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
.

In the sequel, we may write
h(P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}))
instead of
P(h(T_{1}),\ldots,h(T_{n}))
, and may use
h(\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle)
to denote the tuple
\langle h(T_{1}),\ldots,h(T_{n})\rangle
.

We now define the key notion of a universal solution.

Definition 8
.

A solution
\mathcal{U}
for a KB
\mathcal{K}
is
universal
if, for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathit{Sol}(\mathcal{K})
, there is a homomorphism
h:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
.

The instance
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
in Ex.
3
is a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
, as is the instance obtained by eliminating
\textnormal{{e}}_{1}^{\bot}
from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
.

Two instances
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
are
homomorphically equivalent
if there are homomorphisms
h:\mathcal{I}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{2}
and
h^{\prime}:\mathcal{I}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
All universal solutions
are homomorphically equivalent.

4
Query answering

A conjunctive query (CQ)
q
is a formula
\exists\mathbf{y}\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,
where

\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a built-in free conjunction of atoms.
The
arity
of
q
is the number
of its free variables in
\mathbf{x}
; we will often write
q(\mathbf{x})
, instead of just
q
, to indicate the free variables of
q
.

Let
q(\mathbf{x}):\exists\mathbf{x}\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
be a CQ, where
\mathbf{x}=x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
.
Given a KB
\mathcal{K}
and an instance
\mathcal{I}
for
\mathcal{K}
,
the
answer to
q
on
\mathcal{I}
, denoted by
q^{\mathcal{I}}
, is the set of all tuples

\langle T_{1},\ldots,T_{n}\rangle
such that there is an assignment
\mu
from
\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to
\mathcal{I}
for which

•

T_{i}=\mu(x_{i})
, if
x_{i}
is an entity-variable;

•

The next results tell that if two sets of entities appear in a certain answer or in different certain answers, then either they are the same set or they are disjoint. In particular, the sets of entities that appear in certain answers can be viewed as equivalence classes of some equivalence relation.

Proposition 2
.

Let
q
be a CQ of arity
n
, let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB, and let
\mathbf{T}=\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
and
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}=\langle T^{\prime}_{1},\dots,T^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
be two certain answers to
q
w.r.t.
\mathcal{K}
. If
T_{i}
and
T^{\prime}_{j}
are sets of entities, then either
T_{i}=T^{\prime}_{j}
or
T_{i}\cap T^{\prime}_{j}=\emptyset
, where
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

Proof.

Towards a contradiction, assume that
T_{i}
,
T^{\prime}_{j}
are two non-empty sets of entities such that
T_{i}\neq T^{\prime}_{j}
and
T_{i}\cap T^{\prime}_{j}\neq\emptyset
. Then
T_{i}\subsetneqq T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}
or
T^{\prime}_{j}\subsetneqq T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}
. Let’s assume
T_{i}\subsetneqq T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}
. Since
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
and
\langle T^{\prime}_{1},\dots,T^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
are certain answers of
q
, for every solution
\mathcal{I}
, there are tuples
\langle L_{1},\dots,L_{n}\rangle
,
\langle L^{\prime}_{1},\dots,L^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
in
q^{\mathcal{I}}
such that for every
k
we have
T_{k}\subseteq L_{k}
and
T^{\prime}_{k}\subseteq L^{\prime}_{k}
. Thus we have that
T_{i}\subseteq L_{i}
and
T^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq L^{\prime}_{j}
. Since
T_{i}\cap T^{\prime}_{j}\neq\emptyset
, it follows that
L_{i}\cap L^{\prime}_{j}\neq\emptyset
. However,
L_{i}
,
L^{\prime}_{j}
are equivalence classes in the solution
\mathcal{I}
, hence they must coincide, i.e.
L_{i}=L^{\prime}_{j}
. It follows that
T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq L_{i}=L^{\prime}_{j}
. Consider now the tuple
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{i-1},T_{i}\cup T_{j},T_{i+1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
, which is obtained from
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
by replacing
T_{i}
by
T_{i}\cup T_{j}
. Note that
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{i-1},T_{i},T_{i+1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle<\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{i-1},T_{i}\cup T_{j},T_{i+1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
because
T_{i}\subsetneqq T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}
. However for every solution
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{K}
, we have that
T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq L_{i}
, and for all
1\leq k\leq n
such that
k\neq i
, we have that
T_{k}\subseteq L_{k}
. This means that
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{i-1},T_{i}\cup T_{j},T_{i+1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
satisfies Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
, and therefore
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
does not respect Condition
(ii)
of Definition
9
), and thus it is not a certain answer, which is a contradiction. Note finally that if we assume
T^{\prime}_{j}\subsetneqq T_{i}\cup T^{\prime}_{j}
we contradict that
\langle T^{\prime}_{1},\dots,T^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
is a certain answer by using an analogous argument.
∎

when

In data exchange and related areas, the certain answers to CQs can be obtained by evaluating the query on a universal solution and then applying an operator
\downarrow
that eliminates the tuples that contain nulls (see, e.g.,
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
,
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
]
).
The operator
\downarrow
can easily be adapted to our framework as follows.
If
E
is a set of entities and entity-nulls,
then
E{\downarrow}=\{e~|~e\in\mathcal{S}_{E}\cap E\}
. Similarly, if
V
is
a non-empty set of values and value-nulls, then
V{\downarrow}=\{v~|~v\in\mathcal{S}_{V}\cap V\}
. In words,
\downarrow
removes all nulls from
E
and from
V
. If
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
is a tuple such that each
T_{i}
is either a set of entities and entity-nulls or a set of values and value-nulls, then we set
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle{\downarrow}=\langle T_{1}{\downarrow},\dots,T_{n}{\downarrow}\rangle
.
Finally, given a set
\Theta
of tuples of the above form,

\Theta{\downarrow}
is the set obtained from
\Theta
by removing all tuples in
\Theta
containing a
T_{i}
such that
T_{i}{\downarrow}=\emptyset
, and replacing every other tuple
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle
in
\Theta
by the tuple
\langle T_{1},\dots,T_{n}\rangle{\downarrow}
.

The next example shows that, in our framework, the certain answers to a CQ

cannot always be obtained by evaluating
q
on a universal solution and then applying
\downarrow
.

Example 9
.

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be
such that
\mathit{type}(P_{1})=\mathit{type}(P_{2})=\langle\mathsf{e},\mathsf{v}\rangle
,

be the TBox consisting of the rules

\begin{array}[]{l}P_{1}(x,y)\rightarrow P_{2}(x,y),~~P_{1}(x,y)\land P_{1}(x,z)\rightarrow y=z\end{array}

and

Consider the following universal solutions for
\mathcal{K}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathbf{D}^{\prime})

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{I}_{1}&=&\{P_{1}([\textnormal{{e}}],\{1,2\}),P_{2}([\textnormal{{e}}],\{1,2\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}_{2}&=&\{P_{1}([\textnormal{{e}}],\{1,2\}),P_{2}([\textnormal{{e}}],\{1\}),P_{2}([\textnormal{{e}}],\{1,2\})\}\end{array}

and the query

.
Then

, and

Intuitively, in the above example the universal solution

is not “minimal”, in the sense that the fact

in

is dominated by another fact of
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, namely

. This behaviour causes that the answer to the query over

contains also tuples that are not maximal with respect to tuple dominance (cf. Def.

).
Note also that the situation is even more complicated in the presence of nulls. For example, we might have an instance containing both facts

and

, where

and

are two different value-nulls. In this case, no fact in the solution is dominated by another fact, still “non-minimality” shows up if we eliminate the nulls.his suggests that we need some additional processing besides elimination of nulls. We modify the operator
\downarrow
by performing a further

Definition 10
.

Given a query
q
and an instance
\mathcal{I}
for a KB
\mathcal{K}
, we write

to denote the set of null-free tuples obtained by removing from

\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
are two tuples in
q^{\mathcal{I}}{\downarrow}
such that
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
, then remove
\mathbf{T}
from
q^{\mathcal{I}}{\downarrow}
.

The following property is needed to prove the last theorem of this section. It easily follows from Prop.
1
and Def.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB,
q
be a CQ,
\mathcal{U}
be a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
, and
\mathbf{T}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}
. Then, for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathit{Sol}(\mathcal{K})
there is a tuple
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\in q^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
\mathbf{T}\downarrow\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\downarrow\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is universal, there is a homomorphism

from
\mathcal{U}
to
\mathcal{I}

\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\in q^{\mathcal{I}}

We are now able to present the main result of this section, which asserts that universal solutions can be used to compute the certain answers to CQs in our framework.

Theorem 1
.

Let
q
be a CQ, let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB, and let
\mathcal{U}
be a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
. Then
\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})=q^{\mathcal{U}}{{\downarrow^{\rho}}}
.

Proof.

We first show that
q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}\subseteq\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})
. Let
\mathbf{T}\in{q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}}
, which of course implies
\mathbf{T}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}
. By Corollary
2
we have that for every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathit{Sol}(\mathcal{K})
there is a tuple
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\in q^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
\mathbf{T}{\downarrow}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
. Since
\mathbf{T}\in{q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}}
, it is null-free and thus
\mathbf{T}{\downarrow}=\mathbf{T}
. Hence, Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
(certain answers) is satisfied by
\mathbf{T}
.
Let us now assume by contradiction that
\mathbf{T}
does not satisfy Condition
(ii)
of Definition
9
. This means that there exists a null-free tuple
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
satisfying Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
such that
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
. Since
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
satisfies Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
, there must exist a tuple
\mathbf{U}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\leq\mathbf{U}
, and thus obviously
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{U}
. We can apply the
\downarrow
operator to both
\mathbf{T}
and
\mathbf{U}
and get
\mathbf{T}{\downarrow}<\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
, and since, as said,
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}{\downarrow}
, we have that
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
, which is a contradiction. Indeed,
\mathbf{T}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}
(by hypothesis) and
\mathbf{U}\downarrow\in q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow}
, and thus it is not possible that
\mathbf{U}
dominates
\mathbf{T}
(cf. Step 3 of the definition of the
\downarrow^{\rho}
operator).

We now show that
\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})\subseteq q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}
. Let
\mathbf{T}\in\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})
. By Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
there exists a tuple
\mathbf{U}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{U}
, which implies
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
(since
\mathbf{T}
is null-free). By Corollary
2
, we have that for each
\mathcal{I}\in\mathit{Sol}(\mathcal{K})
there is a tuple
\mathbf{T^{\prime}}\in q^{\mathcal{I}}
such that
\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
. This last fact implies that
\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
is a null-free tuple satisfying Condition
(i)
of Definition
9
. Thus, if
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
then
\mathbf{T}
is not a certain answer, which contradicts the assumption. Therefore, it must be
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{U}{\downarrow}
. This shows that
\mathbf{T}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow}
. Let us assume by contradiction that
\mathbf{T}\not\in q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}
. By definition of the
\downarrow^{\rho}
operator, the above assumption implies that there exists a tuple
\mathbf{X}\in q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}
such that
\mathbf{T}<\mathbf{X}
, and since
q^{\mathcal{U}}{\downarrow^{\rho}}\subseteq\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})
, as shown in the first part of this proof,

satisfies Condition
(i)
of Definition

, which in turn implies that
\mathbf{T}\not\in\mathit{cert}(q,\mathcal{K})

5
Computing a universal model

In this section, we adapt the well-known notion of restricted chase
[
\citeauthoryearBeeri and Vardi1984
,
\citeauthoryearJohnson and Klug1984
,
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
,
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
]

to our framework.

Interestingly, the chase procedure we define never produces a failure,
unlike, e.g., the restricted chase procedure in the case of standard data exchange, where the application of egds may cause a failure when two different constants have to be made equal.
Instead, in our framework, when an entity-egd forces two different equivalence classes of entities to be equated, we combine the two equivalence classes into a bigger equivalence class. Similarly, when a value-egd forces two different sets of values to be equated, we take the union of the two sets and modify the instance accordingly. However, it is possible that the chase procedure may have infinitely many steps, each producing a new instance. As a consequence, some care is required in defining the result of the application of this (potentially infinite) procedure to a KB
\mathcal{K}
, so that we can obtain an instance that can be used (at least in principle) for query answering. We call such instance
the result of the chase of the KB
\mathcal{K}
, and distinguish the case in which the chase terminates from the case in which it does not. In the former case, the result of the chase of
\mathcal{K}
is simply the instance produced in the last step of the chase procedure, and we show that this instance is a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
. In the latter case, we point out that previous approaches from the literature for infinite standard chase sequences under tgds and egds cannot be smoothly adapted to our framework, and we leave it open for this case how to define the result of the chase so that it is a universal solution.

We start with the notion of the
base instance
for a KB.
Given a KB
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D})
, we define the set

\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}=\{P(\{c_{1}\},\ldots,\{c_{n}\})~|~P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbf{D}\}.

\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
is an instance for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t. the identity relation
\mathsf{id}
over the set
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{E}\cap\mathit{sig}(\mathcal{K})
. We call
\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
the
base instance
for
\mathcal{K}
.
Note that the base instance for
\mathcal{K}
is also a base instance for every KB having
\mathbf{D}
as database, and is a solution for
(\emptyset,\mathbf{D})
.
As an example, note that the instance
\mathcal{I}
of Ex.
3
is the base instance for the KB
\mathcal{K}
defined in Ex.
1
and in Ex.

.

We now present some basic properties of the instances in a chase sequence.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB.

•

If
\mathcal{I}
is an instance for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t. an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
and if
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is such that
\mathcal{I}\xrightarrow{r,\mu}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}

\sim^{\prime}
such that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
is an instance for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t.
\sim^{\prime}
on
\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
.

•

Every
\mathcal{I}_{i}
occurring in a chase sequence for a KB

is an instance for
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

We prove the thesis at the first bullet by considering the three possible cases for the rule
r
and the corresponding chase steps of Definition
11
.

Case of tgd chase step:
Let
r
be a tgd of the form (
3
). Let
\mathcal{S}=\mathit{under}_{E}(\mathcal{I})\cup\textsf{N}^{f_{e}}
, where
\textsf{N}^{f_{e}}=\{f_{e}^{1},\ldots,f_{e}^{h}\}

11
for the tgd case. Let

(notice that
\textsf{N}^{f_{e}}

). It is easy to see that

is an equivalence relation over
\mathcal{S}
, and that

11
and thus for each

and each

such that

, either

, and thus

w.r.t.

over

Let

2

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB, let
\sigma
be a chase sequence for
\mathcal{K}
, and let
\mathcal{I}_{i}
and
\mathcal{I}_{j}
, with
i\leq j
, be two instances for
\mathcal{K}
w.r.t. equivalence relations
\sim^{i}
and
\sim^{j}
, respectively, such that
\mathcal{I}_{i}
and
\mathcal{I}_{j}
belong to
\sigma
. Then the following holds:

•

\sim^{i}\subseteq\sim^{j}

and
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{i})\subseteq\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{j})
;

•

If
P(\mathbf{T})
is a fact in
{\mathcal{I}_{i}}
, then there is
a fact
P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})
in
\mathcal{I}_{j}
such that
P(\mathbf{T})\leq P(\mathbf{T}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We recall that
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D})
, where
\mathcal{T}
is the TBox of
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathbf{D}
the database of
\mathcal{K}
. The properties can be easily proved by induction.

Base case.
Let be
i=0
and
j=1
, that is, consider
\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, where
\mathcal{I}_{0}
is the base instance
\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
of
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{I}_{1}
is the instance produced by the application of a chase step to
\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
. The inclusions
\sim^{0}\subseteq\sim^{1}
and
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{0})\subseteq\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
can be proved by using the same construction adopted in the proof of Proposition
3
, where
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{0}
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}_{1}
. In particular, for the case in which
r
is a tgd, by construction, we have that
\sim_{0}=\sim\subseteq\sim^{\prime}=\sim_{1}
. Moreover,
\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})=\mathit{under}_{V}(\mathcal{I})\cup\{f_{v}^{1},\ldots,f_{v}^{j}\}
and thus
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
(that is,
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{0})\subseteq\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
. The cases in which

is a value-egd or an entity-egd are even more straightforward.

It remains to prove that for each fact
P(\mathbf{T})\in\mathcal{I}_{0}
there exists
P(\mathbf{T^{\prime}})\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
such that
\mathbf{T}\leq\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
. Let us consider the three possible chase steps.
(tgd)
: this step does not modify facts in
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, thus
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T^{\prime}}
.
(entity-egd)
: every equivalence class

in
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I}_{0})
, i.e., the active domain of
\mathcal{I}_{0}
, remains as is or it is substituted by a class

such that

. Moreover,
\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{0})=\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
. These facts easily show the thesis in this case.
(value-egd)
: the argument is similar as the previous case, that is, every set of values
V
in
\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{0})
remains as is or it is substituted by a set of values

such that

. Moreover,
\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{0})=\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
.

Inductive case.
we assume that the thesis holds for a certain integer
\ell
, i.e., that properties
(i)
,
(ii)
, and
(iii)
hold for each

such that

, and prove that they hold also for each

such that

, because the other cases already hold by the inductive hypothesis.
Of course, if

the condition is trivially satisfied. For

the three properties can be proved exactly as done for the base case. Then, all cases in which

∎

Prop.

When a chase sequence for a KB is infinite, there might be rules applicable to some instance
\mathcal{I}_{i}
in the sequence that are never applied, even though they remain applicable in subsequent chase steps. It is however always possible to establish a suitable order in the application of the rules so that the above situation never occurs. Chase sequences enjoying this property are called

. In what follows, we assume that all chase sequences considered are fair.

We now investigate the possible outcomes of the chase for a KB
\mathcal{K}
.

We first give the definition of the result of the chase for a finite chase sequence.

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D})
be a KB.

•

A
finite chase of
\mathcal{K}
is a finite chase sequence
\sigma=\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{m}
, for which there is no rule
r
in
\mathcal{T}
and no assignment
\mu
such that
r
can be applied to
\mathcal{I}_{m}
with
\mu
.

•

We say that
\mathcal{I}_{m}
is the
result of the finite chase
\sigma
for
\mathcal{K}
, and denote it by
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
.

Prop.
3
implies that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is an instance for
\mathcal{K}
. Actually, it turns out to be a solution for
\mathcal{K}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a KB and let
\sigma=\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{m}
be a finite chase for
\mathcal{K}
. Then
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is a solution for
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

We need to prove that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
satisfies all facts in
\mathbf{D}
, all tgds, entity-egds and value-egds in
\mathcal{T}
. We show each point separately:

(i)

The property that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
satisfies all facts in
\mathbf{D}
follows from
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
and from Proposition
4
. Indeed,
\mathcal{I}^{\mathbf{D}}
satisfies all facts in
\mathbf{D}
, by definition of base instance, which means that for each ground atom
P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
in
\mathbf{D}
there is a fact
P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}_{0}
such that
c_{i}\in T_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. By Proposition
4
it follows that in
\mathcal{I}_{m}
there is a tuple
\langle T_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,T_{n}^{\prime}\rangle
such that
T_{1}\subseteq T_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,T_{n}\subseteq T_{n}^{\prime}
, and thus
P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
is satisfied also in
\mathcal{I}_{m}=\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
.

(ii)

To prove that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
satisfies all tgds in
\mathcal{T}
, let us assume by contradiction that there is a tgd
d=\forall\mathbf{x}(\phi(\mathbf{x})\rightarrow\exists\mathbf{y}\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))
that is not satisfied by
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
. This means that there is an assignment
\mu
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
such that there is no
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
-compatible tgd-extension
\mu^{\prime}
of
\mu
to
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
. But thus

would be applicable to
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
with assignment
\mu
, which contradicts the hypothesis that
\sigma
is a finite chase.

(iii)

The fact that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
satisfies all the entity-egds and value-egds can be proved as done in (ii) for satisfaction of tgds in
\mathcal{T}
.

∎

The following lemma is used to prove that if
\sigma
is a finite chase, then
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is not just a solution for
\mathcal{K}
, but also a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
. A similar result, known as the Triangle Lemma, was used in
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
in the context of data exchange.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1}\xrightarrow{r,\mu}\mathcal{I}_{2}
be a chase step.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be an instance for
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
r
and there exists a homomorphism
h_{1}:\mathcal{I}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
. Then there is a homomorphism
h_{2}:\mathcal{I}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}
such that
h_{2}
extends
h_{1}
.

Proof.

The proof follows the line of the proof of Lemma 3.4 in
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
. However, since our structures are based on equivalence classes and sets of values, the various steps of the proof are more involved. In particular, differently from
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
, in our framework the composition of an assignment with an homomorphism does not directly yield a new assignment. Nonetheless, an assignment can be constructed on the basis of such composition.

We prove the thesis by considering the three possible cases for the rule
d
and the corresponding chase steps of Definition
11
.

Case of tgd chase step:
Let
r
be a tgd of the form (
3
). For ease of exposition, we assume
\phi(\mathbf{x})=P_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\land P_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=P_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})\land P_{4}(x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
, where
x_{1}
and
y_{2}
are entity variables and
x_{2}
and
y_{1}
are value-variables.
The case of generic tgds can be managed in a similar way.
If we apply the operator
\tau
to both the body and the head of the tgd, we obtain the following formulas:

\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))=P_{1}(x_{1},S_{1}^{x_{2}})\land P_{2}(x_{1},S_{2}^{x_{2}})

\tau(\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=P_{3}(x_{1},R_{1}^{x_{2}},R_{1}^{y_{1}},y_{2})\land P_{4}(R_{2}^{x_{2}},R_{2}^{y_{1}},y_{2})

We first construct an assignment
\hat{\mu}
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
. By the definition of chase step,
\mu
is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Then, there exist an equivalence class
E_{1}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
and two sets of values
V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
such that
V_{1}\cap V_{2}\neq\emptyset
,
\mu(x_{1})=E_{1}
,
\mu(S_{1}^{x_{2}})=V_{1}
,
\mu(S_{2}^{x_{2}})=V_{2}
,
P_{1}(E_{1},V_{1})
and
P_{2}(E_{1},V_{2})
are facts in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
Since
h_{1}
is homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{1}
to
\mathcal{I}
, there exist facts
P_{1}(E_{1}^{\prime},V_{1}^{\prime})
,
P_{2}(E_{2}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{I}
,
such that

h_{1}(P_{1}(E_{1},V_{1}))\leq P_{1}(E_{1}^{\prime},V_{1}^{\prime})
and
h_{1}(P_{2}(E_{1},V_{2}))\leq P_{2}(E_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
, that is,

h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{1})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{2})\subseteq V_{2}^{\prime}
.
Since
V_{1}\cap V_{2}\neq\emptyset
, then also
V_{1}^{\prime}\cap V2^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, since
E_{1}^{\prime}
and
E_{2}^{\prime}
are equivalence classes containing the same non-empty subset, they must be the same equivalence class, i.e.,
E_{1}^{\prime}=E_{2}^{\prime}
. We then define the mapping
\hat{\mu}
as follows:
\hat{\mu}(x_{1})=E_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(S_{1}^{x_{2}})=V_{1}^{\prime}
, and
\hat{\mu}(S_{2}^{x_{2}})=V_{2}^{\prime}
. It is easy to see that
\hat{\mu}
satisfies the conditions in Definition
3
, and thus it is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
.

Since
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
r
, there is an assignment
\mu^{\prime}
that is a
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
-compatible extension of
\hat{\mu}
to
\mathcal{I}
. By definition we have that:

•

\mu^{\prime}(x_{1})=\hat{\mu}(x_{1})=E_{1}^{\prime}

•

\mu^{\prime}(R_{1}^{x_{2}})=V_{3}^{\prime}
,
\mu^{\prime}(R_{2}^{x_{2}})=V_{4}^{\prime}
, where
V_{3}^{\prime},V_{4}^{\prime}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
are such that
V_{1}^{\prime}\cap V_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}\cap V_{4}^{\prime}
,

•

\mu^{\prime}(R_{1}^{y_{1}})=V_{5}^{\prime}
,
\mu^{\prime}(R_{2}^{y_{1}})=V_{6}^{\prime}
, where
V_{5}^{\prime},V_{6}^{\prime}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
are such that
V_{5}^{\prime}\cap V_{6}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
,

•

\mu^{\prime}(y_{2})=E_{2}^{\prime}
, where
E_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
,

•

\mathcal{I}
contains the facts
P_{3}(E_{1}^{\prime},V_{3}^{\prime},V_{5}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime})
and
P_{4}(V_{4}^{\prime},V_{6}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime})
.

Then, by definition of tgd chase step it follows that there is also an assignment
\mu_{2}
from
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to
\mathcal{I}_{2}
defined as follows

•

\mu_{2}(x_{1})=\mu(x_{1})=E_{1}

•

\mu_{2}(R_{1}^{x_{2}})=\mu_{2}(R_{2}^{x_{2}})=V_{1}\cap V_{2}

•

\mu_{2}(R_{1}^{y_{1}})=\mu_{2}(R_{2}^{y_{1}})=\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\}

•

\mu_{2}(y_{2})=[f_{e}^{y_{2}}]

where
f_{v}^{y_{1}}
and
f_{e}^{y_{2}}
are the fresh value-null and entity-null, respectively, introduced by the chase step. We remark that
\mu_{2}
is a
\psi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
-compatible extension of
\mu
to
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, and that
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})=\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})\cup\{f_{v}^{y_{1}},f_{e}^{y_{2}}\}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{I}_{2}=\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\{P_{3}(E_{1},V_{1}\cap V_{2},\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\},[f_{e}^{y_{2}}]),P_{4}(V_{1}\cap V_{2},\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\},[f_{e}^{y_{2}}])\}
.

We now construct a mapping
h_{2}
and show that it is an homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{2}
to
\mathcal{I}
.
We define
h_{2}
as follows:

•

h_{2}(o)=h_{1}(o)
for each
o\in\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
;

•

h_{2}(f_{v}^{y_{1}})=v
, where
v
is any element in
V_{5}^{\prime}\cap V_{6}^{\prime}
;

•

h_{2}(f_{e}^{y_{2}})=e
, where
e
is any element in
E_{2}^{\prime}
.

Note that, as stated before,
V_{5}^{\prime}\cap V_{6}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover,
E_{2}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
, since
E_{2}^{\prime}
is an equivalence class in
\mathit{active}(\mathcal{I})
. Thus
v
and
e
above always exist. Note also that
h_{2}
extends
h_{1}
.

By construction,
h_{2}
is a mapping from the elements of
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
to the elements of
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I})
. Moreover, Conditions 1-4 of Definition
7
(homomorphism) are trivially satisfied.
To prove that
h_{2}
is homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{2}
to
\mathcal{I}
it remains to prove that also Condition 5 of Definition
7
holds.
Such condition is obviously satisfied by all facts in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
(which is contained in
\mathcal{I}_{2}
), since
h_{2}
over such facts behaves as
h_{1}
, and
h_{1}
is an homomorphism to
\mathcal{I}
.
Consider now the fact
P_{3}(E_{1},V_{1}\cap V_{2},\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\},[f_{e}^{y_{2}}])
, which is in
\mathcal{I}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{1}
. We have to show that there exists a fact in
\mathcal{I}
of the form
P_{3}(\hat{E_{1}},\hat{V_{1}},\hat{V_{2}},\hat{E_{2}})
, where
\hat{E_{1}},\hat{E_{2}}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
and
\hat{V_{1}},\hat{V_{2}}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I})
,
such that

h_{2}(P_{3}(E_{1},V_{1}\cap V_{2},\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\},[f_{e}^{y_{2}}]))\leq P_{3}(\hat{E_{1}},\hat{V_{1}},\hat{V_{2}},\hat{E_{2}})
, i.e.,

h_{2}(E_{1})\subseteq\hat{E_{1}}
,
h_{2}(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\subseteq\hat{V_{1}}
,
h_{2}(\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\})\subseteq\hat{V_{2}}
,
h_{2}([f_{e}^{y_{2}}])\subseteq\hat{E_{2}}
.
To this aim we set
\hat{E_{1}}=E_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{V_{1}}=V_{3}^{\prime}
,
\hat{V_{2}}=V_{5}^{\prime}
,
\hat{E_{2}}=E_{2}^{\prime}
, and show that the above mentioned containments do indeed hold.

•

h_{2}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
follows from
h_{2}(E_{1})=h_{1}(E_{1})
and
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
;

•

h_{2}(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}
is a consequence of
h_{2}(V_{1}\cap V_{2})=h_{1}(V_{1}\cap V_{2})\subseteq h_{1}(V_{1})\cap h_{1}(V_{2})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}\cap V_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}\cap V_{4}^{\prime}\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}
.

•

h_{2}(\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\})\subseteq V_{5}^{\prime}
holds because
h_{2}(f_{v}^{y_{1}})=v
and
v\in V_{5}^{\prime}\cap V_{6}^{\prime}
, and thus
h_{2}(\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\})=\{v\}\subseteq V_{5}^{\prime}
.

•

h_{2}([f_{e}^{y_{2}}])\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
holds because
h_{2}(f_{v}^{y_{1}})=e
and
e\in E_{2}^{\prime}
, and thus
h_{2}([f_{e}^{y_{2}}])=[e]\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
.

We can proceed in analogously to show that Condition 5 of Definition
7
is satisfied also for the other fact,
P_{4}(V_{1}\cap V_{2},\{f_{v}^{y_{1}}\},[f_{e}^{y_{2}}])
, which is contained in
\mathcal{I}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{1}
.

Case of entity-egd chase step:

Let
r
be an entity-egd of the form (
2
).
By the definition of the chase step,
\mu
is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and in particular there exist equivalence classes
E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
such that
\mu(y)=E_{1}
and
\mu(z)=E_{2}
.
It is then possible to construct an assignment
\hat{\mu}
from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
such that
\hat{\mu}(y)=E_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(z)=E_{2}^{\prime}
,
E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
,
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
and
h_{1}(E_{2})\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
.
The construction of
\hat{\mu}
is similar to the analogous construction described for the case of tgd chase step. For the sake of completeness we describe this procedure also for the entity-egd chase step (note that in this case we need to also deal with entities and values possibly occurring in the rule body
\phi(\mathbf{x})
). For ease of exposition, and without loss of generality, we assume that
\phi(\mathbf{x})=P_{1}(x,y,e,v)\land P_{2}(x,y,z)
, where
x
is a value-variable,
y,z
are entity-variables,
e\in\mathcal{S}_{E}
, and
v\in\mathcal{S}_{V}
. By applying the operator
\tau
to
\phi(\mathbf{x})
we obtain

\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))=P_{1}(S_{1}^{x},y,e,v)\land P_{2}(S_{2}^{x},y,z)
. Since
\mu
is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, there exist
E,E_{1},E_{2}\in\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
and
V,V_{1},V_{2}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
such that
e\in E
,
V_{1}\cap V_{2}\neq\emptyset
,
v\in V
,
P_{1}(V_{1},E_{1},E,V)
and
P_{2}(V_{2},E_{1},E_{2})
are two facts in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and
\mu(S_{1}^{x})=V_{1}
,
\mu(y)=E_{1}
,
\mu(v)=V
,
\mu(S_{2}^{x})=V_{2}
, and
\mu(z)=E_{2}
.
Since
h_{1}
is homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{1}
to
\mathcal{I}
, there exist
P_{1}(V_{1}^{\prime},E_{1}^{\prime},E^{\prime},V^{\prime})
and
P_{2}(V_{2}^{\prime},E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{I}
such that
h_{1}(P_{1}(V_{1},E_{1},E,V))\leq P_{1}(V_{1}^{\prime},E_{1}^{\prime},E^{\prime},V^{\prime})
and
h_{1}(P_{2}(V_{2},E_{1},E_{2}))\leq P_{2}(V_{2}^{\prime},E_{1}^{\prime},E_{2}^{\prime})
, i.e.,

h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{1})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(E)\subseteq E^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V)\subseteq V^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{2})\subseteq V_{2}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(E_{2})\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
and . We then define
\hat{\mu}
as follows:
\hat{\mu}(S_{1}^{x})=V_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(y)=E_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(v)=V
,
\hat{\mu}(S_{2}^{x})=V_{2}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(z)=E_{2}^{\prime}
.
It is easy to see that
\hat{\mu}
is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
. In particular, since
e\in E
and
h(e)=e
it obviously holds
e\in h_{1}(E)\subseteq E^{\prime}
, which implies
e\in E^{\prime}
. Similarly, since
v\in\mu(v)=V
and
v=h_{1}(v)
we have that
v\in h_{1}(V)\subseteq V^{\prime}
, which implies
v\in V^{\prime}
.

We then take
h_{2}
to be
h_{1}
and show that
h_{1}
is still a homomorphism when considered from
\mathcal{I}_{2}
to
\mathcal{I}
.
We soon note that
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})=\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
, and thus
h_{1}
is a mapping from
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
as well. Moreover, since
h_{1}
is an homomorphism, then Conditions 1-4 of Definition
7
are obviously satisfied. It remains to prove that Condition 5 of Definition
7
is also satisfied, i.e., we have to prove that for each fact
P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}_{2}
there exists a fact
P(U_{1},\ldots,U_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}
such that
h_{1}(T_{i})\subseteq U_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq n
.
We recall that
\mathcal{I}_{2}
is obtained by replacing in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
every occurrence of
E_{1}
and every occurrence of
E_{2}
with
E_{1}\cup E_{2}
. Therefore, two cases are possible.
(a)

T_{i}\neq E_{1}\cup E_{2}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. In this case, the thesis holds since
P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{n})
is also in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
h_{1}
is an homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{1}
to
\mathcal{I}
.
(b)
there is
j
such that
1\leq j\leq n
and
T_{j}=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
. Without loss of generality we can also assume that
T_{i}\neq E_{1}\cup E_{2}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
i\neq j
. Since two equivalence classes are either disjoint or are the same class, we have that each
T_{i}
such that
i\neq j
is either a set of values or an equivalence class disjoint from
E_{1}\cup E_{2}
.
Then, there is a fact
P(T_{1},\ldots,T_{j-1},T^{\prime}_{j},T_{j+1}\ldots,T_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
where it is either
T^{\prime}_{j}=E_{1}
or
T^{\prime}_{j}=E_{2}
, and, for every
i\neq j
,
T_{i}\neq E_{1}
and
T_{i}\neq E_{2}
. Let us consider
T^{\prime}_{j}=E_{1}
(the other choice can be treated analogously). Then, there must exist a fact
P(U_{1},\ldots,U_{j-1},U_{j},U_{j+1}\ldots,U_{n})
in
\mathcal{I}
such that
h_{1}(T_{i})\subseteq U_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. In particular, this means that
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq U_{j}
, with
U_{j}
an equivalence class in
\mathit{active}_{E}(\mathcal{I})
.
Since
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
, it must be
U_{j}=E_{1}^{\prime}
. Indeed, for the two equivalence classes
U_{j}
and
E_{1}^{\prime}
it holds either
U_{j}=E_{1}^{\prime}
or
U_{j}\cap E_{1}^{\prime}=\emptyset
, but the second option is impossible, since
h_{1}(E_{1})
is a common non-empty subset of
U_{j}
and
E_{1}^{\prime}
. Then, since
\mathcal{I}
satisfies the entity-egd
d
, which in
d
is triggered by
\hat{\mu}
, we have that
E_{1}^{\prime}=E_{2}^{\prime}
and thus from
h_{1}(E_{1})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
and
h_{1}(E_{2})\subseteq E_{2}^{\prime}
it follows
h_{1}(E_{1})\cup h_{1}(E_{2})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
and thus
h_{1}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}
, that is
h_{1}(T_{j})\subseteq E_{1}^{\prime}=U_{j}
. This shows that
P(U_{1},\ldots,U_{j-1},U_{j},U_{j+1}\ldots,U_{n})
is the fact in
\mathcal{I}
we were looking for, thus proving Condition 5 of Definition
7
.

Case of value-egd chase step:
The proof is similar to the one used for the entity-egd chase step.
Let
r
be an entity-egd of the form (
2
), and, Without loss of generality, assume that
\phi(\mathbf{x})=P_{1}(y,z)\land P_{2}(z)
, where
y,z
are value-variables (the possible presence of entity-variables, entities and values can be dealt with as shown in the previous cases of this proof). Then,
\tau(\phi(\mathbf{x}))=P_{1}(S_{1}^{y},S_{1}^{z})\land P_{2}(S_{2}^{z})
. Since
\mu
is assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, then there exist
V_{1},V_{2},V_{3}\in\mathit{active}_{V}(\mathcal{I}_{1})
such that
\mu(S_{1}^{y})=V_{1}
,
\mu(S_{1}^{z})=V_{2}
,
\mu(S_{2}^{z})=V_{3}
,
V_{2}\cap V_{3}\neq\emptyset
,
P_{1}(V_{1},V_{2})
and
P_{2}(V_{3})
are facts in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.
Since
h_{1}
is homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{1}
to
\mathcal{I}
, then there are facts in
P_{1}(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
and
P_{2}(V_{3}^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{I}
such that

h_{1}(P_{1}(V_{1},V_{2}))\leq P_{1}(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
and
h_{1}(P_{2}(V_{3}))\leq P_{2}(V_{3}^{\prime})
, i.e.,

h_{1}(V_{1})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{2})\subseteq V_{2}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{3})\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}
. Since
V_{2}\cap V_{3}\neq\emptyset
, then
V_{2}^{\prime}\cap V_{3}^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
. We define a mapping
\hat{\mu}
such that
\hat{\mu}(S_{1}^{y})=V_{1}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(S_{1}^{z})=V_{2}^{\prime}
,
\hat{\mu}(S_{2}^{z})=V_{3}^{\prime}
. It is easy to see that
\hat{\mu}
is an assignment from
\phi(\mathbf{x})
to
\mathcal{I}
.

We then take
h_{2}
to be
h_{1}
and show that
h_{1}
is still a homomorphism when considered from
\mathcal{I}_{2}
to
\mathcal{I}
.
We soon note that
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{1})=\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
, and thus
h_{1}
is a mapping from
\mathit{under}(\mathcal{I}_{2})
as well. Moreover, since
h_{1}
is an homomorphism, then condition 1-4 of Definition
7
are obviously satisfied.
Condition 5 of Definition
7
is also already satisfied for all facts in
\mathcal{I}_{2}
that are also in
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. It remains to prove that such condition holds also for facts in
\mathcal{I}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{1}
. The only two such facts are
P_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3},V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})
and
P_{2}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})
(see the value-egd chase step in Definition
11
). We have to show that there are facts
P_{1}(\hat{V_{1}},\hat{V_{2}})
and
P_{2}(\hat{V_{3}})
in
\mathcal{I}
such that

h_{1}(P_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3},V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}))\leq P_{1}(\hat{V_{1}},\hat{V_{2}})
and
h_{1}(P_{2}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3}))\leq P_{2}(\hat{V_{3}})
, i.e.,

h_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})\subseteq\hat{V_{1}}
,
h_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})\subseteq\hat{V_{2}}
, and
h_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})\subseteq\hat{V_{3}}
.
It is not difficult to see that such facts are
P_{1}(V_{1}^{\prime},V_{2}^{\prime})
and
P_{2}(V_{3}^{\prime})
. Indeed, we know that
h_{1}(V_{1})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}
,
h_{1}(V_{2})\subseteq V_{2}^{\prime}
and
h_{1}(V_{3})\subseteq V_{3}^{\prime}
. Then, since
\mathcal{I}
satisfies
d
, which is triggered in
\mathcal{I}
by
\hat{\mu}
, it must be
V_{1}^{\prime}=V_{2}^{\prime}=V_{3}^{\prime}
. Thus, we have that
h_{1}(V_{1})\cup h_{1}(V_{2})\cup h_{1}(V_{3})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}=V_{2}^{\prime}=V_{3}^{\prime}
, and thus
h_{1}(V_{1}\cup V_{2}\cup V_{3})\subseteq V_{1}^{\prime}=V_{2}^{\prime}=V_{3}^{\prime}
, which shows the thesis.
∎

The following theorem is the main result of this section.

Theorem 2
.

If
\mathcal{K}
is a KB and
\sigma=\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{m}
is a finite chase for
\mathcal{K}
, then
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is a universal solution for
\mathcal{K}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
1
it follows that
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
is a model for
\mathcal{K}
. It remains to prove that it is universal, i.e., that there exists an homomorphism from
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)=\mathcal{I}_{m}
to every solution for
\mathcal{K}
. The proof is based on Lemma
2
and on the observation that the identity mapping
\mathsf{id}
is a homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{0}
to every
\mathcal{I}\in\mathit{Sol}(\mathcal{K})
. The thesis can be thus proved by applying Lemma
2
to each
\mathcal{I}_{i},\mathcal{I}_{i+1}
in
\sigma
, starting from
\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1}
, with
h_{1}=\mathsf{id}
as homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{0}
to a solution
\mathcal{I}
for
\mathcal{K}
.
∎

Theorem
2
implies that if both
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are finite chases for
\mathcal{K}
, then
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
and

\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma^{\prime})
are homomorphically equivalent. Thus, the result of a finite chase for
\mathcal{K}
is unique up to homomorphic equivalence.

It is easy to verify that if
\mathcal{K}=(\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D})
is a KB in which every tgd in
\mathcal{T}
is full (i.e., there are no existential quantifiers in the heads of the tgds in
\mathcal{T}
), then every chase sequence for
\mathcal{K}

is finite. In particular, this holds true if
\mathcal{T}
consists of egds only, which covers all entity resolution settings.

For infinite chase sequences, the definition of the result of the chase requires some care. The typical approach adopted when only tgds are present
[
\citeauthoryearKrötzsch, Marx, and
Rudolph2019
,
\citeauthoryearGrahne and Onet2018
]
, or for settings involving
separable
tgds and egds
[
\citeauthoryearJohnson and Klug1984
,
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
,
\citeauthoryearCalì, Gottlob, and
Kifer2013
]
, is to define the result of an infinite chase sequence as the union of all facts generated in the various chase steps. This approach does not work for arbitrary tgds and egds since the resulting instance needs not satisfy all the rules and thus needs not be a solution in our terminology. Moreover, in our framework the union of all instances in an infinite chase sequence is not even an instance for the KB at hand, because the sets of entities in the result does not correspond to equivalence classes with respect to an equivalence relation.

An alternative approach, which might be better suited for a setting with arbitrary tgds and egds, is to define the result of an infinite chase sequence as the instance containing all
persistent
facts, i.e.,
all facts that are introduced in some step in the chase sequence and are never modified in subsequent chase steps. This notion was introduced in
[
\citeauthoryearBeeri and Vardi1984
]
. In our setting, given a KB
\mathcal{K}
and an infinite chase sequence
\sigma=\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots
, this means that we define the result
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
of the infinite chase
\sigma
of
\mathcal{K}
as follows:

\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)=\{f\mid\textrm{there is some }i\geq 0\textrm{ such that }\\
f\in\mathcal{I}_{j}\textrm{ for each }j\geq i\}.

(3)

The following example shows that this definition does not work in our framework, because the above set might be empty (even if the database
\mathbf{D}
is non-empty).

Example 12
.

Let
\mathcal{K}=\langle\mathcal{T},\mathbf{D}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{D}=\{P(1,2)\}
and
\mathcal{T}
consists of the two rules

(r_{1})~P(x,y)\rightarrow P(y,z);~~r_{2})~P(x,y)\land P(y,z)\rightarrow y=z,

with
\mathit{type}(P)=\langle\mathsf{v},\mathsf{v}\rangle
. We construct an infinite chase sequence starting with
\mathcal{I}_{0}=\{P(\{1\},\{2\})\}
and by repeatedly applying the above rules with suitable assignments in the following order:
r_{1},r_{1},r_{2},r_{1},r_{2},r_{1},r_{2},r_{1}\ldots
.

We obtain the infinite chase sequence
\sigma=\mathcal{I}_{0},\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{I}_{0}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}_{1}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2\}),P(\{2\},\{v_{\bot}^{1}\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}_{2}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2\}),P(\{2\},\{v_{\bot}^{1}\}),P(\{v_{\bot}^{1}\},\{v_{\bot}^{2}\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}_{3}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2,v_{\bot}^{1}\}),P(\{2,v_{\bot}^{1}\},\{2,v_{\bot}^{1}\}),\\
&&P(\{v_{\bot}^{1}\},\{v_{\bot}^{2}\})\}\\
\ldots\\
\mathcal{I}_{7}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2,v_{\bot}^{1},v_{\bot}^{2}\}),P(\{2,v_{\bot}^{1}\},\{2,v_{\bot}^{1},v_{\bot}^{2}\}),\\
&&P(\{2,v_{\bot}^{1},v_{\bot}^{2}\},\{2,v_{\bot}^{1},v_{\bot}^{2}\}),P(\{v_{\bot}^{2}\},\{v_{\bot}^{3}\}),\\
&&P(\{v_{\bot}^{3}\},\{v_{\bot}^{4}\})\}\\
\ldots\end{array}

It is not difficult to see that the set
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma)
defined in (
3
) above is empty, i.e.,
no
persistent facts occur in
\sigma
.
∎

The infinite chase sequence
\sigma
in Ex.
12
is fair. At the same time, there are finite fair sequences for the KB
\mathcal{K}
in this example. Indeed, if we apply rule
r_{1}
and then rule
r_{2}
,
we get a finite chase sequence
\sigma^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{0},\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}
, where

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{0}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{1}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2\}),P(\{2\},\{v_{\bot}^{1}\})\}\\
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}&=&\{P(\{1\},\{2,v_{\bot}\}),P(\{2,v_{\bot}\},\{2,v_{\bot}\})\}.\end{array}

Consequently,
\mathit{chase}(\mathcal{K},\sigma^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}
.
Thus, further investigation is needed when both finite and infinite chase sequences exist for a KB. We leave this as a topic for future work.

6
Related Work

From the extensive literature on entity resolution, we comment briefly on only a small subset of earlier work that is related to ours.

Swoosh
[
\citeauthoryearBenjelloun et al.2009
]
is a generic approach to entity resolution in which the functions used to compare and merge records are “black boxes”.
In our framework, the match function is determined by entity-egds and value-egds, whereas the merge function is implemented via the union operation - an important special case of merge functions in the Swoosh approach. In this sense, our framework is less general than Swoosh. In another sense, our framework is more general as it supports tgds, differentiates between entities and values, and incorporates query answering.

As in the work on matching dependencies (MDs)
[
\citeauthoryearFan2008
,
\citeauthoryearBertossi, Kolahi, and
Lakshmanan2013
]
, we consider variables in the head of egds to be matched and merged rather than to be made equal.
In
[
\citeauthoryearBertossi, Kolahi, and
Lakshmanan2013
]
, generic functions obeying some natural properties are used to compute the result of a match, while we use the union of the sets of values or sets of entities involved in the match. In the MDs framework,
a version of the chase procedure is used to resolve dependency violations.
This chase
acts locally on a pair of tuples at each step, whereas our chase procedure matches entities in a global fashion and values in a local fashion. Furthermore, we support tgds, which are not in the framework of MDs.

There is a body of work on declarative entity resolution and its variants that is related to our framework; relevant references include the Dedupalog framework
[
\citeauthoryearArasu, Ré, and Suciu2009
]
, the declarative framework for entity linking in
[
\citeauthoryearBurdick et al.2016
,
\citeauthoryearBurdick et al.2019
]
, and the more recent LACE framework
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu, Cima, and
Gutiérrez-Basulto2022
]
. In both Dedupalog and LACE, there is a distinction between hard and soft entity resolution rules. LACE supports global merges and creates equivalence classes as we do, but it does not support local merges; also, LACE combines entity resolution rules with denial constraints, while our framework combines entity resolution rules with tgds.
The declarative framework for entity linking in
[
\citeauthoryearBurdick et al.2016
,
\citeauthoryearBurdick et al.2019
]
uses key dependencies and disjunctive constraints with “weighted” semantics that measure the strength of the links.
The key dependencies are interpreted as hard rules that the solutions must satisfy in the standard sense. Consequently, two conflicting links cannot co-exist in the same solution, hence that approach uses repairs to define the notion of the certain links. In contrast, in our framework we carry along via the chase all the alternatives as either sets of values or equivalence classes of entities. Another feature of our framework is the focus on the certain answers of queries.
Finally, the aforementioned declarative framework for entity linking uses more general link relations without rules for an equivalence relation of entities; thus, it is less focused on entity resolution.

7
Conclusions and Future Work

The main contribution of this paper is the development of a new declarative framework that combines entity resolution and query answering in knowledge bases. This is largely a conceptual contribution because the development of the new declarative framework entailed rethinking from first principles the definitions of such central notions as assignment, homomorphism, satisfaction of tgds and egds in a model, and universal solution. At the technical level, we designed a chase procedure that never fails, and showed that, when it terminates, it produces a universal solution that, in turn, can be used in query answering.

As regards future directions, perhaps the most pressing issue is to identify a “good” notion of the result of the chase when the chase procedure does not terminate. This may lead to extending the framework presented here to settings where all universal solutions are infinite.
While infinite universal solutions cannot be materialized,
such solutions have been used to obtain rewritability results
[
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2007
]
, occasionally combined with partial materialization of the result of an infinite chase
[
\citeauthoryearLutz, Toman, and Wolter2009
]
. In parallel, it is important to identify structural conditions on the tgds and the egds of the TBox that guarantee termination of the chase procedure in polynomial time and, thus, yield tractable conjunctive query answering. It is also worthwhile exploring whether other variants of the chase procedure, such as the semi-oblivious (a.k.a. Skolem) chase
[
\citeauthoryearMarnette2009
,
\citeauthoryearCalautti and Pieris2021
]
or the core chase
[
\citeauthoryearDeutsch, Nash, and Remmel2008
]
, can be suitably adapted to our framework so that their desirable properties carry over.

Finally, we note that there are several different areas, including data exchange
[
\citeauthoryearFagin et al.2005
]
, data integration
[
\citeauthoryearLenzerini2002
,
\citeauthoryearDoan, Halevy, and Ives2012
]
, ontology-mediated query answering
[
\citeauthoryearBienvenu and Ortiz2015
]
, and ontology-based data access
[
\citeauthoryearCalvanese et al.2018
]
,
in which tgds and egds play a crucial role. We believe that the framework presented here makes it possible to infuse entity resolution into these areas in a principled way.
