On the chromatic number of random regular hypergraphs
Patrick Bennett, Alan Frieze

Abstract
We estimate the likely values of the chromatic and independence numbers of the random r r -uniform d d -regular hypergraph on n n vertices for fixed r r , large fixed d d , and n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty .

\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\lambda)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2c^{2}t}\right).

Upper bound on the independence number

We will apply Theorem

Fix

z_{2}=\frac{c}{1-c}+O\left(c^{r}\right)

Proof.

After some algebra, we re-write (

and the claim follows.
∎

Now we check (

\displaystyle h\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)+h(dc)+h(d(1-c))-h(c)-h(1-c)-h(d)-\frac{d}{r}\log z_{1}-dc\log z_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\log\left(\frac{d}{r}\right)+dc\log(dc)+d(1-c)\log(d(1-c))-c\log c-(1-c)\log(1-c)

\displaystyle\qquad-d\log d-\frac{d}{r}\log z_{1}-dc\log z_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle dc\log\left[\frac{c}{(1-c)z_{2}}\right]+\frac{d}{r}\log\left[(z_{2}+1)^{r}-z_{2}^{r}\right]+d\log(1-c)-c\log c-(1-c)\log(1-c).

(7)

Now note that the first term of (

The second term of (

\displaystyle\frac{d}{r}\log\left[(z_{2}+1)^{r}-z_{2}^{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{r}\log\left[\left(\frac{1}{1-c}+O\left(c^{r+1}\right)\right)^{r}-\left(\frac{c}{1-c}+O\left(c^{r+1}\right)\right)^{r}\right]

\displaystyle=-d\log(1-c)-\frac{d}{r}c^{r}+O\left(dc^{r+1}\right).

The last term of (

Therefore (

\displaystyle-\frac{d}{r}c^{r}-c\log c+O\left(c+dc^{r+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c\left[\frac{d}{r}(1+\varepsilon)^{r-1}\frac{r\log d}{(r-1)d}+\log\left((1+\varepsilon)\left(\frac{r\log d}{(r-1)d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}\right)\right]+O\left(c+dc^{r+1}\right)

3.2
Upper bound on the chromatic number

Our proof of the upper bound uses the method of Frieze and Łuczak
[
12
]
. We will generate
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,d)
in a somewhat complicated way. The way we generate it will allow us to use known results on
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p)
due to Krivelevich and Sudakov
[
14
]
.

Set

m:=\left(\frac{d-d^{1/2}\log d}{r}\right)n.

(9)

Let
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{r}(n,m)
be an
r
-uniform multi-hypergraph with
m
edges, where each multi-edge consists of
r
independent uniformly random vertices chosen with replacement. We will generate
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{r}(n,m)
as follows. We have
n
sets (“buckets” )
V_{1},\ldots V_{n}
and a set of
rm
points
P:=\{p_{1},\ldots p_{rm}\}
. We put each point
p_{i}
into a uniform random bucket
V_{\phi(i)}
independently. We let
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{m}\}
be a uniform random partition of
P
into sets of size
r
. Of course, the idea here is that the buckets
V_{i}
represent vertices and the parts of the partition
\mathcal{R}
represent edges. Thus
R_{i}
defines a hyper-edge
\left\{\phi(j):j\in R_{i}\right\}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. We denote the hypergraph defined by
\mathcal{R}
by
H_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Note that since
r\geq 3
the expected number of pairs of multi-edges in
\mathcal{H}^{*}_{r}(n,m)
is at most

\binom{n}{r}\binom{m}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\binom{n}{r}}\right)^{2}=O\left(\frac{m^{2}}{n^{r}}\right)=O(n^{-1}).

Thus, w.h.p. there are no multi-edges. Now the expected number of “loops” (edges containing the same vertex twice) is at most

nm\binom{r}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2}=O(1).

Thus w.h.p. there are at most
\log n
loops. We now remove all multi-edges and loops, and say that
M
is the (random) number of edges remaining, where
m-\log n\leq M\leq m
. The remaining hypergraph is distributed as
\mathcal{H}(n,M)
, the random hypergraph with
M
edges chosen uniformly at random without replacement. Next we estimate the chromatic number of
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,M)
.

Claim 1
.

W.h.p. we have

\left|\frac{\chi(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,M))-\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad\qquad\left|\frac{\alpha(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,M))-\left(\frac{r\log D}{(r-1)D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}n}{\left(\frac{r\log D}{(r-1)D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}n}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Proof.

We will use Theorem
5
together with a standard argument for comparing
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p)
with
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,m)
. Set
p:=m/\binom{n}{r}
and apply Theorem
5
with
\varepsilon
replaced with
\varepsilon/4
so we get

\left|\frac{\chi(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p))-\left(\frac{(r-1)D}{r\log D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{\left(\frac{(r-1)D}{r\log D}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

(10)

with probability at least
1-o(1/n)
. Note that here

D=\binom{n-1}{r-1}p=\binom{n-1}{r-1}m/\binom{n}{r}=rm/n=d-d^{1/2}\log d.

Now since
d,D
can be chosen to be arbitrarily large and
d=D+O(D^{1/2}\log D)
we can replace
D
with
d
in (

) without changing the left hand side by more than

to obtain

\left|\frac{\chi(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p))-\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

(11)

with probability at least
1-o(1/n)
. But now note that with probability

the number of edges in

is precisely

. Thus we have that

\left|\frac{\chi(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,M))-\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}{\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

with probability at least
1-o(n^{-1/2})
. This proves the first inequality, and the second one follows similarly.
∎

\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,m)
to the random regular hypergraph

d
, and then adding some edges to vertices of degree less than
d
. We define the

. We form a new set of points

and a partition

of

as follows. For any

containing a point with rank more than

, we delete

from

and delete all points of

from

. Note that each bucket contains at most
r
points of
P^{\prime}
. Note also that

is a uniform random partition of
P^{\prime}
. We let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
be the natural hypergraph associated with
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
.

Now we would like to put some more points into the buckets until each bucket has exactly
d

. We would also like a uniform partition
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
of

into sets of size
r
, and we would like
\mathcal{R}^{\prime\prime}
to have many of the same parts as

(in other words
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{j}

is defined as follows.

.

Consider a random partition of

.

We claim that

3.2.1
Bounding the number of low degree vertices in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}

We define some sets of buckets. We show that w.h.p. there are few small buckets i.e few vertices of low degree in the hypregraph
H_{\mathcal{R^{\prime}}}
. Let
S_{0}
be the buckets with at most
d-3d^{1/2}\log d
points of
P^{\prime}
, and let
S_{1}
be the buckets with at most
d-2d^{1/2}\log d
points of
P
. Let
S_{2}
be the set of buckets that, when we remove points from
P^{\prime}
to get
P
, have at least
d^{1/2}\log d
points removed. Then
S_{0}\subseteq S_{1}\cup S_{2}
. Our goal is to bound the probability that
S_{0}
is too large.

Fix a bucket
V_{j}
and let
X\sim\textrm{Bin}\left(rm,\frac{1}{n}\right)
be the number of points of
P
in
V_{j}
. Then the probability that
V_{j}
is in
S_{1}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}[V_{j}\in S_{1}]=\mathbb{P}\left[X\leq d-2d^{1/2}\log d\right]=\mathbb{P}\left[X-\frac{rm}{n}\leq-d^{1/2}\log d\right]\\
\leq\exp\left[-\frac{d\log^{2}d}{3(d-d^{1/2}\log d)}\right]=\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right],

where for our inequality we have used the Chernoff bound (Lemma
2
). Therefore
\mathbb{E}[|S_{0}|]\leq\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]n
. Now we argue that
|S_{1}|
is concentrated using McDiarmid’s inequality (Lemma
3
). For our application we let
X=|S_{1}|
which is a function (say
f
) of the vector
(Z_{1},\ldots Z_{rm})
where
Z_{i}
tells us which bucket the
i^{th}
point of
P
went into. Moving a point from one bucket to another can only change

by at most 1 so we use

. Thus we get the bound

\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq n^{2/3})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{4/3}}{2rm}\right)=o(1).

(12)

Now we handle
S_{2}
. For
1\leq j\leq n
let
Y_{j}
be the number of parts
R_{k}\in\mathcal{R}
such that
R_{k}
contains a point in the bucket
V_{j}
as well as a point in some bucket
V_{j^{\prime}}
where
|V_{j^{\prime}}|>d
. Note that if
V_{j}\in S_{2}
then
Y_{j}\geq d^{1/2}\log d.
We view
R_{k}
as a set of
r
points, say
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{r}\}
each going into a uniform random bucket. Say
q_{i}
goes to bucket
V_{j_{i}}
. The probability that
R_{k}
is counted by

is at most

\displaystyle r\mathbb{P}[j_{1}=j\mbox{ and }|V_{j_{1}}|>d]+r(r-1)\mathbb{P}[j_{1}=j\mbox{ and }|V_{j_{2}}|>d]

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{n}\mathbb{P}[|V_{j_{1}}|>d\big|j_{1}=j]+\frac{r(r-1)}{n}\mathbb{P}[|V_{j_{2}}|>d\big|j_{1}=j]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2}}{n}\mathbb{P}[|V_{j_{1}}|>d\big|j_{1}=j]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r^{2}}{n}\mathbb{P}[\textrm{Bin}(rm-1,1/n)\geq d]=\frac{r^{2}}{n}\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right].

Thus we have

\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]=m\cdot\frac{r^{2}}{n}\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]\leq rd\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]=rd^{1/2}\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]

and so Markov’s inequality gives us

\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{j}\geq d^{1/2}\log d\right]\leq\frac{rd\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]}{d^{1/2}\log d}=\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right]

and so
\mathbb{E}[|S_{2}|]=n\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right].
We use McDiarmid’s inequality once more, this time with
X=|S_{2}|
. A change in choice of bucket changes
|S_{2}|
by at most one and so (
12
) continues to hold. Thus

|S_{0}|=n\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right].\quad\text{w.h.p.}

3.2.2
A property of independent subsets of
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,m)

Fix
1\leq j\leq r-1
. Set

a:=\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\left(\frac{r\log d}{(r-1)d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}},\qquad\kappa_{j}:=\frac{10d}{r}\binom{r}{j}a^{j},\qquad p:=\frac{d(r-1)!}{n^{r-1}}.

The expected number of independent sets
A
in
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p)
of size at most
an
such that there are
\kappa_{j}n
edges each having
j
vertices in
A
is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{an}\binom{n}{s}(1-p)^{\binom{s}{r}}\binom{\binom{s}{j}\binom{n}{r-j}}{\kappa_{j}n}p^{\kappa_{j}n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{an}\exp\left\{s\log\left(\frac{en}{s}\right)-\binom{s}{r}p+\kappa_{j}n\log\left(\frac{e\frac{(an)^{j}}{j!}\frac{n^{r-j}}{(r-j)!}p}{\kappa_{j}n}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{an}\exp\left\{s\log\left(\frac{en}{s}\right)-\binom{s}{r}p+\kappa_{j}n\log\left(\frac{ea^{j}}{10}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq an\cdot\exp\left\{\left[\log\left(\frac{e}{a}\right)-\frac{10d}{r}\binom{r}{j}a^{j-1}\log\left(\frac{10}{e}\right)\right]an\right\}

\displaystyle=o(1/n)

where the last line follows since as
d\rightarrow\infty
we have

\log\left(\frac{e}{a}\right)\sim\frac{1}{r-1}\log d

and

\frac{10d}{r}\binom{r}{j}a^{j-1}\log\left(\frac{10}{e}\right)=\Omega\left(d^{\frac{r-j}{j-1}}\log^{-\frac{j-1}{r-1}}d\right)\gg\log d.

Thus with probability
1-o(1/n)
,
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,p)
has a coloring using
(1+\varepsilon/2)\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}
colors such that for each color class
A
and for each
1\leq j\leq r-1
there are at most
\kappa_{j}n
edges with
j
vertices in
A
. The hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,m)
,
m=\binom{n}{r}p
will have this property w.h.p..

3.2.3
Transforming
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
into
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,d)

Now we will complete the transformation to the random regular hypergraph
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,d)
. We are open to the possibility that doing so will render our coloring no longer proper, since this process will involve changing some edges which might then be contained in a color class. We will keep track of how many such “bad” edges there are and then repair our coloring at the end.

We have to add at most
(3d^{1/2}\log d+d\exp\left[-\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)\right])n<(4d^{1/2}\log d)n
points, which takes at most as many steps. For each color class
A
of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
define
X_{A,j}=X_{A,j}(i)
to be the number of edges with
j
vertices in
A
at step
i
. We have already established that
X_{A,j}(0)\leq\kappa_{j}n
. This follows from Section
3.2.2
and the fact that we have removed edges from
\mathcal{H}(n,m)
to obtain
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{i}
be the event that at step
i
we have that for each color class
A
and for each
1\leq j\leq r-1
we have
X_{A,j}(i)\leq 2\kappa_{j}n
. Then, assuming
\mathcal{E}_{i}
holds, the probability that
X_{A,j}
increases at step
i
is at most

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq r,\;\;j_{\ell}\geq 1\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\frac{2\kappa_{j_{\ell}}n}{nd/r}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq r,\;\;j_{\ell}\geq 1\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j\end{subarray}}\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq k}20\binom{r}{j_{k}}a^{j_{k}}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq r,\;\;j_{\ell}\geq 1\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{k}=j\end{subarray}}20^{r}2^{r^{2}}a^{j}\leq 40^{r}2^{r^{2}}a^{j}.

Also, the largest possible increase in
X_{A,j}
in one step is
r
. Thus, the final value of
X_{A,j}
after at most
(4d^{1/2}\log d)n
steps is stochastically dominated by
\kappa_{j}n+rY
where
Y\sim\textrm{Bin}\big((4d^{1/2}\log d)n,40^{r}2^{r^{2}}a^{j}\big)
. An easy application of the Chernoff bound tells us

\mathbb{P}\left(Y>2\mathbb{E}[Y]\right)\leq\exp(-\Omega(n)).

(13)

Note that here

\frac{2\mathbb{E}[Y]}{\kappa_{j}n}=\frac{8d^{1/2}\log d\cdot 40^{r}2^{r^{2}}a^{j}n}{10d\binom{r}{j}a^{j}n/r}=O(d^{-1/2}\log d)<1

for sufficiently large
d
. Thus, using (
13
) and the union bound over all color classes
A
, we have w.h.p. the final value of
X_{A,j}
is at most
\kappa_{j}n+2\mathbb{E}[Y]\leq 2\kappa_{j}n
for all
1\leq j\leq r-1
.

Now we address “bad” edges, i.e. edges contained in a color class. Assuming
\mathcal{E}_{i}
holds, the expected number of new edges contained in any color class at step
i
is at most
r(40)^{r}2^{r^{2}+2r}a^{r}=O\left(\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{\frac{r}{r-1}}\right)
(because it would have to be one of the colors of one of the vertices we are adding points to). Thus the expected number of bad edges created in
(4d^{1/2}\log d)n
steps is stochastically dominated by
Z\sim r\cdot\textrm{Bin}\big((4d^{1/2}\log d)n,O\left(\left(\frac{\log d}{d}\right)^{\frac{r}{r-1}}\right)\big)
. Another easy application of Chernoff shows that w.h.p.
Z\leq 2\mathbb{E}[Z]=O(d^{-1/2}n)
.

We repair the coloring as follows. First we uncolor one vertex from each bad edge, and let the set of uncolored vertices be
U
where
|U|=u=O\left(d^{-1/2}n\right)
. Let

\delta:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}.

We claim that for every
S\subseteq U,|S|=s
, the hypergraph induced on
S
has at most
\delta s/r
edges. This will complete our proof since it implies that the minimum degree is at most
\delta
and so
U
can be recolored using a fresh set of
\delta
colors, yielding a coloring of
\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,d)
using at most

\chi(\mathcal{H}_{r}(n,M))+\delta\leq\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}=\left(1+\varepsilon\right)\left(\frac{(r-1)d}{r\log d}\right)^{\frac{1}{r-1}}

colors.
The expected number of sets
S
with more than
\delta s/r
edges is at most

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq s\leq u}\binom{n}{s}\binom{\binom{ds}{r}}{\delta s/r}\frac{1}{\binom{dn}{r}\binom{dn-r}{r}\ldots\binom{dn-\delta s+r}{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq s\leq u}\left(\frac{ne}{s}\right)^{s}\left(\frac{(dse/r)^{r}e}{\delta s/r}\right)^{\delta s/r}\frac{(r!)^{\delta s/r}}{(dn-\delta s)^{\delta s}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq s\leq u}\left[\frac{ne}{s}\left(\frac{dse}{(dn-\delta s)r}\right)^{\delta}\left(\frac{er\cdot r!}{\delta s}\right)^{\delta/r}\right]^{s}.

(14)

Now for
1\leq s\leq\sqrt{n}
the term in (
14
) is at most

\left[O(n)\cdot\left(O(n^{-1/2})\right)^{\delta}\cdot O(1)\right]^{s}=o(1/n)

since
\delta
can be made arbitrarily large by choosing
d
large. Meanwhile for
\sqrt{n}\leq s\leq u
we have that the term in (
14
) is at most

\left[O(n^{1/2})\cdot O(1)\cdot\left(O(n^{-1/2})\right)^{\delta/r}\right]^{s}=o(1/n).

Now since (
14
) has
O(n)
terms the whole sum is
o(1)
and we are done. This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

4
Summary

We have asymptotically computed the chromatic number of random
r
-uniform,
d
-regular hypergraphs when proper colorings mean that no edge is mono-chromatic. It would seem likely that the approach we took would extend to other definitions of proper coloring. We have not attempted to use second moment calculations to further narrow our estimates. These would seem to be two natural lines of further research.
