Ethical Framework for Responsible Foundational Models in Medical Imaging
Abhijit Das, Debesh Jha, Jasmer Sanjotra, Onkar Susladkar, Suramyaa Sarkar

Abstract
Foundational models (FMs) have tremendous potential to revolutionize medical imaging. However, their deployment in real-world clinical settings demands extensive ethical considerations. This paper aims to highlight the ethical concerns related to FMs and propose a framework to guide their responsible development and implementation within medicine. We meticulously examine ethical issues such as privacy of patient data, bias mitigation, algorithmic transparency, explainability and accountability. The proposed framework is designed to prioritize patient welfare, mitigate potential risks, and foster trust in AI-assisted healthcare.

Challenges of foundation models: Overview

FMs have the potential to revolutionize healthcare workflows, yet they face significant challenges, both domain-specific (related to Medical Imaging) and general
[
20
]
.
(i) Data scarcity.
One primary challenge is the bottleneck of data scarcity and the lack of high-quality annotated images for model training and testing. This scarcity hampers the ability to develop accurate and reliable models.
(ii) Variation.
Medical volumetric data is extremely high resolution and exhibits inter-person anatomical variability, making it challenging for models to generalize effectively
[
21
]
.
(iii) Heterogeneous data.
The heterogeneity of vision data, consisting of different modalities acquired from diverse devices and imaging protocols, further complicates model training and deployment.
(iv) Computational cost.
Another issue is the scalability of FMs in medical imaging. These models tend to be computationally expensive, resulting in longer inference times and higher cost requirements.
(v) Ethics and reliability.
The requirement for patient data raises concerns about privacy, security, and integrity. Whereas the output of FMs is often questioned about the explainability and reliability.
(vi) Susceptibility.
The accuracy of medical decisions is paramount, as they can have life-defining consequences. Therefore, the susceptibility of FMs to adversarial attacks is a major concern
[
22
]
. This not only compromises patient safety but also raises ethical concerns, as the leakage of highly sensitive patient data through adversarial attacks can lead to confidentiality breaches. We dive deep into the problems in the following sections.

Methods for measuring fairness, bias, privacy, and diversity of generations

Evaluation of the equality of results in generative FMs that produces identical results for individuals showing similar characteristics irrespective of a person’s gender, race or other traits should be favoured. Fairness measures such as statistical tests or disparate impacts are used to confirm forecasts to match each other’s actual results. The training data is checked carefully for biases using analytical techniques examining demographic data and determining connections between sensitive features and outputs from the model by utilizing algorithms. Algorithmic audits are used to identify possible bias in the model. To preserve privacy and prevent data breaches, noise is added to the training data while maintaining general patterns. 8
Towards ethical and responsible use

Promoting transparency, fairness, and bias avoidance involves clearly documenting how data is collected, preprocessed, and customized to train a model. It’s important to identify and rectify biases in the dataset. Regularly monitoring the model’s performance and ensuring it works well for all users is crucial. Involving diverse stakeholders with different viewpoints is essential. Techniques like data augmentation and debiasing can help mitigate biases during training
[
57
]
.
Prioritizing privacy and security measures is essential in handling information. Adhering to data protection regulations is crucial. Techniques like differential privacy and federated learning help minimize counter-attacks and safely manage risk-prone user data. Regular auditing to identify security loopholes in the model and implementing measures to protect data from breaches and threats are important practices to follow. Accountability for decisions made by FMs and their consequences, openness to feedback, and responsible development and deployment for external entities are crucial. Engaging with communities, ordinary people, and stakeholders to address feedback and concerns raised by marginalized groups is crucial.

9
Conclusion

In conclusion, foundational models are set to transform medical imaging, enhancing accuracy and treatment strategies. However, their clinical integration presents significant ethical challenges, from data privacy concerns to the risks of bias and the demands for transparency and accountability. We studied a comprehensive ethical framework to guide the responsible development and use of these models, emphasizing a balanced approach that combines technology with ethical principles. This paper highlights the importance of a collaborative, multidisciplinary effort to address these ethical considerations, ensuring foundational models advance healthcare with the utmost integrity and a steadfast focus on patient well-being.
