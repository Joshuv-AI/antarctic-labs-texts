Randomized Block-Coordinate Optimistic Gradient Algorithms for Root-Finding Problems
Quoc Tran-Dinh and Yang Luo

Abstract
In this paper, we develop two new randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient algorithms to approximate a solution of nonlinear equations in large-scale settings, which are known as root-finding problems . Our first algorithm is non-accelerated with constant stepsizes, and achieves a 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k ) \mathcal{O}(1/k) best-iterate convergence rate on 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ G ​ x k ‖ 2 ] \mathbb{E}\left[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\right] when the underlying operator G G is Lipschitz continuous and possesses a weak Minty solution, where 𝔼 ⁡ [ ⋅ ] \mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right] is the expectation and k k is the iteration counter. Our second method is a new accelerated randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient algorithm. We establish both 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) \mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right) and o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o\left(1/k^{2}\right) last-iterate convergence rates on both 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ G ​ x k ‖ 2 ] \mathbb{E}\left[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\right] and 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ x k + 1 − x k ‖ 2 ] \mathbb{E}\left[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\right] for this algorithm under the co-coerciveness of G G . In addition, we prove that the iterate sequence { x k } \{x^{k}\} converges to a solution almost surely and k ​ ‖ G ​ x k ‖ k\|Gx^{k}\| attains a o ⁡ ( 1 / k ) o\left(1/k\right) almost sure convergence rate. Then, we apply our methods to a class of large-scale finite-sum inclusions, which covers prominent applications in machine learning, statistical learning, and network optimization, especially in federated learning. We obtain two new federated learning-type algorithms and their convergence rate guarantees for solving this problem class. We test our algorithms on four numerical examples using both synthetic and real data, and compare them with related methods. Our numerical experiments show some promising performance of the proposed methods against their competitors.

Introduction.

In this paper, we first develop two new randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient-type algorithms to solve the following [non]linear equation:

{\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)$ such that:}\quad Gx^{\star}=0,}\vskip-2.15277pt

(NE)

where
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a single-valued operator satisfying a given assumption: either Assumption
3
in Section
3
or Assumption
4
in Section
4
, and
\mathrm{dom}(G)
is the domain of
G
.

Next, we apply our methods to solve the following finite-sum [non]linear inclusion (FNI):

{\text{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}\ 0\in Gx^{\star}+Tx^{\star}\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}x^{\star}+Tx^{\star},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(FNI)

where
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
is maximally monotone and
G_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
(
\forall i\in[n]
) satisfy given conditions (possibly nonmonotone) specified later in Section
5
.
If
n=1
, then (
FNI
) is just a classical [non]linear inclusion
[
7
]
.
Both (
NE
) and (
FNI
) are called
root-finding problems
in the literature.

Since we will focus on algorithmic development and convergence analysis, we assume throughout this paper that the solution sets
\mathrm{zer}(G):=\{x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Gx^{\star}=0\}
of (
NE
) and
\mathrm{zer}(G+T):=\{x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:0\in Gx^{\star}+Tx^{\star}\}
of (
FNI
) are nonempty.

Though (
NE
) and (
FNI
) look simple, it is well-known that (
NE
) is equivalent to the problem of finding a fixed-point
x^{\star}
of the operator
T:=\mathbb{I}-\eta G
, i.e.
x^{\star}=Tx^{\star}
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity operator, and
\eta>0
is any positive constant.
Similarly, (
FNI
) also covers variational inequality problems as special cases (see Subsection
2.4
for more concrete examples).
Therefore, (
NE
) and (
FNI
) cope with many fundamental problems across different fields by appropriately reformulating them into special cases of (
NE
) or (
FNI
) (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
25
,
15
,
32
,
73
,
76
,
82
,
81
,
84
]
).
In addition, in this paper, we can handle a class of nonmonotone operators
G
(i.e.
G
possesses a weak Minty solution), which allows us to tackle a broader class of problems than recent works in
[
25
,
73
]
.
Note that the finite-sum structure in (
FNI
) provides a unified template to model various optimization, game theory, and minimax problems in networks and distributed systems, statistical learning, and machine learning, including federated learning (FL), see, e.g.,
[
11
,
25
,
61
,
64
,
83
]
.

Motivation.

This work is motivated by the following aspects.

Large-scale applications.

We focus on settings where the operator
G
in (
NE
) resides in a high-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
(
p\gg 1
) making operations on full-dimensional vectors computationally intractable.
Such models often arise in modern applications of machine learning, statistics, and data science, especially when model parameters are matrices or even tensors
[
14
,
45
,
86
]
.
One common approach to tackle these problems is block-coordinate methods (BC), which iteratively update one or a small number of block-coordinates instead of the full-dimensional vector or matrix of the model parameters.
This approach is though very classical
[
6
,
71
]
, it has garnered widespread attention in recent years in optimization, monotone inclusions, and fixed-point problems, see, e.g.,
[
8
,
22
,
46
,
69
,
70
,
73
,
79
,
94
]
.
As a concrete example,
[
37
]
illustrated that block-coordinate methods can solve large-scale inverse covariance estimation problems involving millions of variables (i.e.
p
in a range of
10^{12}
).
Such a large-scale problem is very hard or prohibited to handle by classical optimization techniques.
This aspect motivates us to develop block-coordinate methods for (
NE
).

Limitation of current BC methods.

While deterministic methods for solving both (
NE
) and (
FNI
) are well developed under both monotone and certain nonmonotone settings
[
7
,
23
,
26
,
32
,
51
,
56
,
77
,
93
]
, designing efficient block-coordinate variants of these methods for (
NE
) and (
FNI
) remains , especially when the underlying operators are nonmonotone, see
[
20
,
22
,
73
]
as a few examples.
Most existing works focus on special cases of (
NE
) and (
FNI
) such as optimization, convex-concave minimax, and supervised learning problems, see e.g.,
[
8
,
46
,
70
,
69
,
79
,
94
]
.
Moreover, developing Nesterov’s accelerated coordinate methods for (
NE
) and (
FNI
) is still open.

Limitation of convergence analysis.

In deterministic methods for (
NE
), we often use the residual norm
\|Gx^{k}\|
to characterize an approximate solution
x^{k}
, making it difficult to adapt this analysis to randomized block-coordinate (RBC) schemes, especially in accelerated variants.
In fact, we can see from
[
4
,
96
]
that generalizing Nesterov’s accelerated methods from convex optimization to root-finding problems is not straightforward, especially for explicit methods such as forward or gradient-type schemes.
Apart from a momentum term, accelerated methods for (
NE
) are often aided by a “gradient” correction term
Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1}
, see
[
89
]
as an example.
Recent attempts on designing deterministic accelerated methods for monotone inclusions and monotone VIPs have been made such as in
[
4
,
17
,
38
,
55
,
91
]
.
These algorithms often achieve faster convergence rates than their classical counterparts on an appropriate residual norm.
However, it remains unclear if one can still adapt the theoretical analysis of these algorithms to BC methods for (
NE
) and (
FNI
).

Better convergence rates.

In terms of convergence rates, classical methods for solving (
NE
) and (
FNI
) such as dual averaging, extragradient-type, and splitting schemes can achieve up to
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)
(or sometimes
o\left(1/k\right)
) convergence rates on the squared residual norm or on a gap function under the monotonicity of
G
or some special nonmonotone extensions, where
k
is the iteration counter, see, e.g.,
[
12
,
26
,
29
,
32
,
65
,
68
,
74
]
.
In accelerated methods, these rates can be boosted up to
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
(or faster,
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)
) under the monotonicity.
The
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
rate matches the lower bound of convergence rates in different settings, see
[
39
,
67
,
72
]
.
Moreover, a
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
rate can be achieved if
G
satisfies a co-hypomonotone condition as shown in
[
47
,
88
]
.
Nevertheless, we are not aware of any accelerated RBC optimistic gradient-type method for (
NE
) that achieves similar convergence rates as ours.

Broader applications.

As mentioned earlier, since (
NE
) can be reformulated equivalently to a fixed-point problem, theory and solution methods from one field can be applied to another and vice versa.
This relation expands the applicable scope of our methods.
Note that the
nonexpansiveness
in the fixed-point theory is equivalent to the co-coerciveness of
G
in (
NE
), see
[
7
, Proposition 4.11]
.
Furthermore, both (
NE
) and (
FNI
) can cover many common applications in scientific computing beyond optimization models as discussed in
[
73
,
84
]
.
For instance, they can be specified to handle linear systems, [composite] smooth and nonsmooth optimization, feasibility problems, decentralized optimization, federated learning, adversarial training and learning, and [distributionally] robust optimization as discussed in Subsection
2.4
, see also
[
9
,
53
,
73
,
84
]
.

Our goal and challenges.

Our goal in this paper is to advance a recent development in variational inequality problems (VIPs) and apply it to design new randomized block-coordinate (RBC) schemes for solving (
NE
) and (
FNI
).
Our approach relies on Popov’s past-extragradient method
[
77
]
(also equivalent to the optimistic gradient (OG) method from
[
62
]
in our context).
We note that developing RBC methods for (
NE
) and (
FNI
) faces at least three fundamental challenges.

First, unlike the gradient method in convex or nonconvex optimization, which guarantees to converge under only the Lipschitz continuity of the objective gradient, its counterpart, the forward or fixed-point scheme for (
NE
) may not converge when
G
is monotone and Lipschitz continuous, see, e.g.,
[
74
]
.
This issue prevents us from adopting RBC methods for optimization to (
NE
).

Second, RBC methods for an optimization or a minimax problem often use its objective function to build a Lyapunov function for convergence analysis, while this object does not exist in (
NE
).
Hitherto, most convergence analysis of existing methods for (
NE
) rely on a squared distance to solution
\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
, a Bregman distance, or a gap or restricted gap function
[
22
,
43
,
73
]
.
Nevertheless, it remains unclear if any of these metrics can play a similar role as the objective function in optimization to analyze the convergence of RBC methods for (
NE
).

Third, it is difficult to apply existing convergence analysis techniques from deterministic algorithms such as extragradient methods
[
42
]
and Halpern’s fixed-point iteration
[
35
]
to RBC variants for (
NE
) due to the dependence on the operator value
Gx^{k+1}
(but not on the iterate
x^{k+1}
alone) of the underlying potential or Lyapunov function, where
x^{k+1}
is random.
This dependence prevents us from passing the expectation operator
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
between
Gx^{k+1}
and
x^{k+1}
.
In this paper, we aim at addressing these challenges by tackling two settings of (
NE
) in the high-dimensional regime.

Our contribution.

Our main contribution in this paper can be summarized as follows.

(a)

First, we propose a new RBC optimistic gradient scheme (called
RCOG
) to approximate a solution of (
NE
) when
G
is Lipschitz continuous and possesses a weak Minty solution (
cf.
Assumption
3
) as in
[
29
]
.
Under an appropriate condition on the problem parameters, we establish a
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)
best-iterate convergence rate on
\mathbb{E}[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}]
.

(b)

Second, we develop a novel accelerated RBC optimistic gradient algorithm (called
ARCOG
) to solve (
NE
).
This method achieves both
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
and
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)
last-iterate convergence rates on
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]
under the co-coerciveness of
G
(
cf.
Assumption
4
).
In addition, we establish that
\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
attains a
o\left(1/k\right)
almost sure convergence rate, and the iterate sequence
\{x^{k}\}
converges to
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G)
almost surely.
We also utilize a change of variable to derive a practical variant of
ARCOG
, which avoids full dimensional operations on the iterates.

(c)

Third, we apply our methods from (a) and (b) to solve (
FNI
) which forms a basic model of many supervised machine learning tasks, including federated learning
[
40
,
48
,
60
,
61
]
.
We obtain two new federated learning-type algorithms.
The first one achieves a
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)
convergence rate under the existence of a weak Minty-type solution (
cf.
Assumption
5.2
).
The second algorithm is an accelerated RBC variant of the Douglas-Rachford splitting method, which achieves both
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
and
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)
last-iterate convergence rates, as well as almost sure convergence results.

Related work and comparison.

Let us compare our contribution with the most related works.

First, our algorithms are very simple to implement due to their single loop and simple operations.
They also significantly differ from existing methods.
Indeed, one of the most related works to our methods is
[
73
]
, which extends the asynchronous RBC method to (
NE
).
Though this algorithm is asynchronous, it is non-accelerated, and therefore, in our context, achieves
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)
and at most
o\left(1/k\right)
convergence rates on
\mathbb{E}[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}]
under a quasi-co-coerciveness of
G
.
Moreover, the form of our algorithm is also different from
[
73
]
due to a “double forward” step and a momentum term (see

), while achieving
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
and
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)

Second, our algorithms are also different from the RBC fixed-point-type method in
[
22
]
, which renders from fixed-point iteration

or its variants.
These methods use a stronger assumption (i.e. averaged operators
[

) than Assumption

in

and achieve asymptotically convergence results

.
Under Assumption

, the method in
[

does not have a convergence guarantee (or not known yet).
Note that
[
22

uses equivalent assumptions as
ARCOG
, but it only has asymptotic convergence guarantees.
In contrast, our

\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)

to a solution of (

).

Third, compared to a recent work
[

]
, our non-accelerated scheme,

, has a similar form as Algorithm 3 in
[

]
.
However,
[

requires two strong assumptions which exclude our class of problems covered by Assumption

Theorem 4.2 in

Therefore, the analysis in

is not applicable to our

Fourth, unlike methods for convex problems, convergence analysis of algorithms for nonlinear equations such as (

Moreover, it is still unclear if some recent analysis techniques for VIPs and minimax problems, e.g., in

Notations and terminologies.

We work with a finite dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
equipped with the standard inner product
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle
and Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
.
For a multivalued mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
,
\mathrm{dom}(G)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Gx\not=\emptyset\right\}
denotes its domain,
\mathrm{gra}(G)=\left\{(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:u\in Gx\right\}
denotes its graph, where
2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
is the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Furthermore, we assume that the variable
x
of (
NE
) is decomposed into
n
-blocks as
x=[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}]
, where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
is the
i
-th block subvector for
i\in[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
,
1\leq n\leq p
, and
p_{1}+\cdots+p_{n}=p
.
Given
G
in (
NE
), we denote
[Gx]_{i}
the
i
-th block coordinate of
Gx
such that
Gx=[[Gx]_{1},\cdots,[Gx]_{n}]
.
We also denote
G_{[i]}x=[0,\cdots,0,[Gx]_{i},0,\cdots,0]
so that only the
i
-th block is presented and other blocks are zero.
For any vector
x=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define a block-weighted norm of
x
as
\|x\|_{\sigma}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
associated with a weight vector
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
(the set of all positive real-valued vectors). We also use the conventions
\sigma\circ x:=[\sigma_{1}x_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n}x_{n}]
and
\sigma\circ\|x\|^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\|x_{i}\|^{2}
.
For a symmetric matrix
Q
,
\lambda_{\min}(Q)
stands for the smallest eigenvalue of
Q
.
We denote
\mathcal{F}_{k}
to be the smallest
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\{x^{0},x^{1},\cdots,x^{k}\}
collecting all iterate vectors up to the
k
-th iteration of our algorithms.
We also use
\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\cdot\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{i_{k}}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
for the conditional expectation and
\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot\right]
for the total or full expectation.

Background, Preliminary Results, and Examples.

We first review necessary background on monotone operators and related concepts used in this paper.
Then, we recall the optimistic gradient method and its variants, including a Nesterov’s accelerated variant.
Finally, we present the equivalence between (
NE
) and (
FNI
) and some concrete examples of (
NE
) and (
FNI
).

2.1
Monotone operators and related concepts.

For a multivalued mapping
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
, we say that
G
is monotone if
\langle{u-v,x-y}\rangle\geq 0
for all
(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathrm{gra}(G)
.

G
is said to be
\rho
-co-hypomonotone if
\langle{u-v,x-y}\rangle\geq-\rho\|u-v\|^{2}
for all
(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathrm{gra}(G)
, where
\rho>0
is a given parameter.
Note that a
\rho
-co-hypomonotone operator is not necessarily monotone.
If
G
is single-valued, then these conditions reduce to
\langle{Gx-Gy,x-y}\rangle\geq 0
and
\langle{Gx-Gy,x-y}\rangle\geq-\rho\|Gx-Gy\|^{2}
, respectively.
We say that
G
is maximally [co-hypo]monotone if
\mathrm{gra}(G)
is not properly contained in the graph of any other [co-hypo]monotone operator.

A single-valued operator
G
is said to be
L
-Lipschitz continuous if
\|Gx-Gy\|\leq L\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, where
L\geq 0
is a Lipschitz constant.
If
L=1
, then we say that
G
is nonexpansive. We say that
G
is
\beta
-co-coercive if
\langle{u-v,x-y}\rangle\geq\beta\|u-v\|^{2}
for all
(x,u),(y,v)\in\mathrm{gra}(G)
.
If
G
is
\beta
-co-coercive, then by using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
G
is also monotone and
\frac{1}{\beta}
-Lipschitz continuous, but the reverse statement is not true in general.

The operator
J_{G}x:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:x\in y+Gy\right\}
is called the resolvent of
G
, often denoted by
J_{G}x=(\mathbb{I}+G)^{-1}x
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity mapping.
If
G
is monotone, then
J_{G}
is singled-valued, and if
G
is maximally monotone, then
J_{G}
is singled-valued and
\mathrm{dom}(J_{G})=\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

2.2
The optimistic gradient method and its variants.

Popov’s past extragradient method in
[
77
]
(also equivalent to the optimistic gradient (
OG
) scheme in
[
62
]
) for solving (
NE
) can be written as follows.
Starting from
x^{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, set
x^{-1}:=x^{0}
, and for
k\geq 0
, we update

{x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\eta_{k}\big(Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}\big),}\vskip-2.15277pt

(OG)

where
\eta_{k}>0
and
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
are given parameters.
Though
OG
is a “double forward” scheme, it only requires one evaluation
Gx^{k}
of
G
per iteration.
If we denote
\hat{\eta}_{k}:=\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}
and choose
\gamma_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}
, then
OG
becomes
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\hat{\eta}_{k}(2Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1})
, which exactly reduces to the forward-reflected-backward splitting method proposed in
[
57
]
for solving (
NE
) (the backward step is identical) when
G
is monotone and
L
-Lipschitz continuous, where
\hat{\eta}_{k}\in(0,\frac{1}{2L})
.
If
\hat{\eta}_{k}:=\hat{\eta}
is fixed, then
OG
can also be rewritten equivalently to
y^{k+1}=y^{k}-\hat{\eta}G(2y^{k}-y^{k-1})
with
y^{k}:=x^{k}+\hat{\eta}Gx^{k-1}
, which is known as the reflected gradient method in
[
56
]
.
The
OG
scheme can be rewritten as
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\alpha_{k}Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}\eta_{k}(Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1})
, where
\alpha_{k}:=\eta_{k}(1-\gamma_{k})>0
.
This form can be viewed as a gradient/forward method with a “gradient” correction term
\gamma_{k}\eta_{k}(Gx^{k}-Gx^{k-1})
to solve (
NE
).

We can modify
OG
by adding a momentum term
\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})
to obtain the following momentum OG (or accelerated OG) method to solve (
NE
):

{x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\eta_{k}\big(Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}\big),}\vskip-2.15277pt

(AOG)

where
\theta_{k}\geq 0
is a given parameter.
If we additionally introduce
y^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\alpha_{k}Gx^{k}
, then we can equivalently transform
AOG
into the following form:

{\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}y^{k+1}&:=&x^{k}-\alpha_{k}Gx^{k},\\
x^{k+1}&:=&y^{k+1}+\theta_{k}(y^{k+1}-y^{k})+\nu_{k}(x^{k}-y^{k+1}),\end{array}\right.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(NesOG)

where
\alpha_{k}:=\frac{\gamma_{k+1}\eta_{k+1}}{\theta_{k+1}}
and
\nu_{k}:=1+\theta_{k}-\frac{\theta_{k+1}\eta_{k}}{\gamma_{k+1}\eta_{k+1}}
.

If
\nu_{k}=0
, then
NesOG
is identical to Nesterov’s accelerated methods in convex optimization with
G(\cdot)=\nabla{f}(\cdot)
, see
[
66
,
67
]
.
However, as discussed in
[
4
,
54
]
, the term
\nu_{k}(x^{k}-y^{k+1})
plays a crucial role to establish the convergence of accelerated methods for solving (
NE
).

2.3
The equivalence between (
NE
) and (
FNI
).

It is clear that (
FNI
) covers (
NE
) as a special case when
T=0
.
However, under appropriate assumptions, (
FNI
) can equivalently be reformulated into (
NE
).
Let us discuss some common reformations below.

The first common equivalent form of (
FNI
) is the resolvent equation
J_{\lambda(G+T)}x=0
, where
J_{\lambda(G+T)}:=(\mathbb{I}+\lambda(G+T))^{-1}
is the resolvent of
\lambda(G+T)
for any
\lambda>0
.
This reformulation is used to develop proximal-point methods for solving (
FNI
).
However, evaluating the resolvent
J_{\lambda(G+T)}
is usually challenging in most cases, limiting the applicability of proximal-point methods in practice.

Next, if
G
is
\beta
-co-coercive, then finding a solution
x^{\star}
of (
FNI
) is equivalent to solving

{F_{\lambda}x^{\star}=0,\qquad\text{where}\quad F_{\lambda}x:=\lambda^{-1}(x-J_{\lambda T}(x-\lambda Gx))\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda>0.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(1)

The operator
F_{\lambda}
is called the forward-backward splitting (FBS) residual.
It is well-known
[
7
]
that
F_{\lambda}
is
\frac{\lambda(4\beta-\lambda)}{4\beta}
-co-coercive, provided that
0<\lambda<4\beta
.
Hence,
F_{\lambda}
fulfills Assumption
4
below.
Alternatively, we can also reformulate (
FNI
) equivalently to
H_{\lambda}y^{\star}=0
, where
H_{\lambda}(\cdot):=G(J_{\lambda T}(\cdot))+\lambda^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-J_{\lambda T})(\cdot)
is the backward-forward splitting (BFS) operator (see Proposition 2.4. in
[
5
]
).
Then,
x^{\star}
solves (
FNI
) iff
x^{\star}:=J_{\lambda T}y^{\star}
solves
H_{\lambda}y^{\star}=0
.
This reformulation is very useful to develop efficient stochastic algorithms for solving the finite-sum setting (
FNI
), see
[
25
]
as an example.

If both
G
and
T
are maximally monotone, then we can reformulate (
FNI
) equivalently to

{V_{\beta}u^{\star}=0,\qquad\text{where}\quad V_{\beta}u:=\beta^{-1}(J_{\beta T}u-J_{\beta T}(2J_{\beta G}u-u))\quad\text{and}\quad\beta>0.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(2)

The operator
V_{\beta}
is called the Douglas-Rachford splitting (DRS) residual, which is
\beta
-co-coercive.
Note that
u^{\star}
solves (
2
) iff
x^{\star}:=J_{\beta T}u^{\star}
solves (
FNI
).
Furthermore, if
T:=C+\hat{T}
, where
\hat{T}
is maximally monotone and
C
is
\beta
-co-coercive, then we can reformulate (
FNI
) equivalently to (
NE
) using a three-operator splitting residual in
[
26
]
.

Finally, if
G
is possibly nonmonotone, e.g.,
G
satisfies a variant of Assumption
3
below, then we can reformulate (
FNI
) equivalently to (
NE
) using Tseng’s forward-backward-forward splitting (FBFS) residual from
[
93
]
.
We refer to Subsection
5.2
below for more details.

2.4
Examples.

Both settings (
NE
) and (
FNI
) cover many fundamental problems in practice.
Let us recall some important special cases of these problems here.
We also refer to
[
25
,
73
,
84
]
for further details and additional examples.

2.4.1
Optimization problems.

Consider the following composite optimization problem:

{\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\big\{\phi(x):=f(x)+g(x)\big\},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(3)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is given and
g:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed, and convex.
Problem (
3
) covers many applications such as linear and nonlinear least-squares, logistic regression, optimization over networks, and distributed optimization
[
7
,
14
,
19
,
21
,
86
,
95
]
.

As a concrete example, if
f(x):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\langle{Z_{i},x}\rangle;y_{i})
represents an empirical loss associated with a dataset
\{(Z_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
g(x):=\tau R(x)
is a regularizer to promote desired structures of
x
(e.g., sparsity or low-rankness), then (
3
) covers many learning problems such as linear regression with
\ell(\tau,s):=\frac{(\tau-s)^{2}}{2}
, logistic regression with
\ell(\tau,s):=\log(1+\exp(-s\tau))
), and soft-margin support vector machine (SVM) with the hinge loss
\ell(\tau,s):=\max\{0,1-\tau s\}
.

Let us recall
\partial{f}
and
\partial{g}
as the subdifferentials or gradients of
f
and
g
, respectively.
Then, under appropriate regularity assumptions
[
7
,
82
]
, the optimality condition of (
3
) is

{0\in\partial{f}(x^{\star})+\partial{g}(x^{\star}).}\vskip-2.15277pt

(4)

If (
3
) is convex, then
x^{\star}
solves (
4
) iff it solves (
3
).
Clearly, (
3
) is a special case of (
FNI
) with
G:=\partial{f}
and
T:=\partial{g}
.
If
g=\delta_{\mathcal{X}}
, the indicator of a nonempty and closed set
\mathcal{X}
, then (
3
) also covers constrained optimization problems.
If
g=0
and
f
is differentiable, then (
4
) reduces to
\nabla{f}(x^{\star})=0
, which is a special case of (
NE
) with
G:=\nabla{f}
.
Our methods developed in this paper will reduce to new randomized block-coordinate gradient-type algorithms for solving (
3
).

2.4.2
Minimax problems.

Consider the following minimax problem:

{\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\Big\{\mathcal{L}(u,v):=f(u)+\Psi(u,v)-g(v)\Big\},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(5)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are proper, closed, and convex, and
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given joint objective function.
This minimax problem is a central model in robust and distributionally robust optimization
[
10
,
78
,
63
]
, two-person games
[
36
,
44
]
, fairness machine learning
[
31
,
58
]
, and generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
3
,
24
,
34
]
, among others.

As a concrete example, if
f(u):=\delta_{\Delta_{p_{1}}}(u)
and
g(v):=\delta_{\Delta_{p_{2}}}(v)
are the indicators of the standard simplexes
\Delta_{p_{1}}
and
\Delta_{p_{2}}
, respectively, and
\Psi(u,v):=\langle{Au,v}\rangle
is a bilinear form, where
A
is a pay-off matrix, then (
5
) covers the classical bilinear game problem.
Another common example is the following non-probabilistic robust optimization model obtained from Wald’s minimax model:
\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}}\big\{\phi(u):=\psi(u)+f(u)\equiv\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\Psi(u,v)+f(u)\big\}
, where
u
is a decision variable,
v
represents an uncertainty vector on
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
, and the objective term
\psi(u):=\max_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\Psi(u,v)
is the worst-case risk over all possible candidates
v
in
\mathcal{V}
(see
[
10
]
for concrete instances of this model).

Again, under appropriate regularity conditions
[
7
,
82
]
, the optimality condition of (
5
) becomes

{0\in\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{u}{\Psi}(u^{\star},v^{\star})\\
-\partial_{v}{\Psi}(u^{\star},v^{\star})\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}\partial{f}(u^{\star})\\
\partial{g}(v^{\star})\end{bmatrix}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(6)

If
\Psi
is convex-concave, then (
6
) is necessary and sufficient for
(u^{\star},v^{\star})
to be an optimal solution of (
5
).
Otherwise, it is only a necessary condition.
If
f
and
g
are the indicators of given convex sets, then (
5
) also covers constrained minimax problems.
Clearly, if we define
x:=[u,v]
,
G:=[\partial_{u}{\Psi},-\partial_{v}{\Psi}]
, and
T:=[\partial{f},\partial{g}]
, then (
6
) can be viewed as a special case of (
FNI
).
If
f=0
and
g=0
, then the optimality condition (
6
) reduces to
Gx^{\star}=0
, a special case of (
NE
).

2.4.3
Variational Inequality Problems (VIP).

If
T=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}
, the normal cone of a nonempty, closed, and convex set
\mathcal{X}
, then (
FNI
) reduces to

{\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathcal{X}$ such that:}\ \langle{Gx^{\star},x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq 0,\ \textrm{for all}\ x\in\mathcal{X}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(VIP)

More generally, if
T=\partial{g}
, the subdifferential of a convex function
g
, then (
FNI
) reduces to a mixed VIP.
The formulation (
VIP
) covers many well-known problems in practice, including unconstrained and constrained optimization, minimax problems, complementarity problems, and Nash’s equilibria, see also
[
32
,
41
]
for concrete examples in traffic networks and economics.

2.4.4
Fixed-point problems.

The fixed-point problem is fundamental in computational mathematics, which has various applications in numerical analysis, ordinary and partial differential equations, engineering, and physics
[
1
,
7
,
18
]
.
The classical fixed-point problem is written as

{\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}\quad x^{\star}=Fx^{\star},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(FP)

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a given mapping.
This problem is equivalent to (
NE
) with
G:=\mathbb{I}-F
, where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity mapping.
Our algorithms developed in this paper for (
NE
) can be applied to solve certain classes of (
FP
).
We can also generalize (
FP
) to a multivalued mapping fixed-point problem
x^{\star}\in Fx^{\star}
(known as Kakutani’s fixed-point problem), which is also equivalent to (
FNI
).

3
The Non-Accelerated Randomized Block-Coordinate OG Method.

In this section, we develop a new randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient (RCOG) method to solve (
NE
) that satisfies the following assumption.

{assumption}

The operator
G
in (
NE
) is
L_{i}
-block coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuous, i.e. there exists
L:=(L_{1},\cdots,L_{n})\in[0,+\infty)^{n}
such that for any
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
:

{\|[Gx]_{i}-[Gy]_{i}\|\leq L_{i}\|x_{i}-y_{i}\|,\quad\text{for all $i\in[n]$}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(7)

Weak-Minty solution:
There exist
\rho:=(\rho_{1},\cdots,\rho_{n})\in[0,L]
and
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G)
of (
NE
) such that:

{\begin{array}[]{ll}\langle{Gx,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq-\rho\circ\|Gx\|^{2},\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)$}.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(8)

The expression (
7
) shows that
G
is block-coordinate-wise Lipschitz continuous.
It also implies that
G
is
\bar{L}
-Lipschitz continuous with
\bar{L}:=\max\{L_{i}:i\in[n]\}
.
Conversely, if
G
is
\bar{L}
-Lipschitz continuous, then it also satisfies (
7
) with
L_{i}:=\bar{L}
for all
i\in[n]
.
The Lipschitz continuity of
G
is required in most numerical methods for solving (
NE
) and its generalizations, see, e.g.,
[
32
]
.

The condition (
8
) is also referred to as a weak Minty solution of (
NE
), see
[
29
]
for more details and examples.
If
\rho_{i}=\hat{\rho}\geq 0
for all
i\in[n]
, then it exactly reduces to the weak Minty condition
\langle{Gx,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq-\hat{\rho}\|Gx\|^{2}
used in many recent works, including
[
12
,
29
,
74
]
.
It is weaker than the
\hat{\rho}
-co-hypomonotonicity of
G

[
47
,
88
]
.
We can refer to (
8
) as a
\hat{\rho}
-“star” co-hypomonotonicity of
G
.
Note that under condition (
8
),
G
is not necessarily monotone.
If
\rho=0
, then
G
is star-monotone, i.e.
\langle{Gx,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
.
As a concrete example, if we consider a linear mapping
Gx:=\mathbf{G}x+\mathbf{g}
for an invertible symmetric matrix
\mathbf{G}
with
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}^{-1})<0
, and a given
\mathbf{g}
, then
G
is not monotone, but
\hat{\rho}
-co-hypomonotone with
\hat{\rho}:=-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{G}^{-1})>0
. Hence,
G
satisfies (
8
).

In contrast to (
7
), many works such as
[
43
,
73
]
rely on a quasi-strongly monotone condition
\langle{Gx,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq\mu\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
for
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, where
\mu>0
.
This assumption is slightly weaker than the strong monotonicity of
G
, but significantly different from (
8
) due to different right-hand side.
In convex optimization, if
Gx:=\nabla{f}(x)
is the gradient of the objective function, then the quasi-strong monotonicity is equivalent to the quasi-strong convexity of
f
, i.e.
f(x)\geq f(x^{\star})+\langle{\nabla{f}(x^{\star}),x-x^{\star}}\rangle+\mu\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
.
Note that the quasi-strong monotonicity of
G
also implies the error bound condition
\|x-x^{\star}\|\leq\mu^{-1}\|Gx\|
as in
[
52
]
.
However, error bound conditions are often imposed locally to establish a linear convergence of optimization methods, see, e.g.,
[
30
]
for further discussion.

3.1
The derivation of algorithm.

Since our method is randomized, we introduce a random variable
i_{k}
on
[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
to select the
i
-th block coordinate with the following probability:

{\mathbf{Prob}\left(i_{k}=i\right)=\mathbf{p}_{i},\quad\text{for all}~i\in[n],}\vskip-2.15277pt

(9)

where
\mathbf{p}_{i}>0
for all
i\in[n]
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i}=1
is a given probability distribution.
We also denote
\mathbf{p}_{\min}:=\min_{i\in[n]}\mathbf{p}_{i}>0
.
If
\mathbf{p}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
, then
i_{k}
is a uniform random variable.
Otherwise, we also cover non-uniform randomized block-coordinate methods.

Our
randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient
(RCOG) method is described as follows.

Given an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, we set
x^{-1}:=x^{0}
, and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, we randomly sample a block-coordinate
i_{k}\in[n]
satisfying (
9
), and update:

{x^{k+1}:=x^{k}-\tfrac{\eta_{i_{k}}}{\mathbf{p}_{i_{k}}}\left(G_{[i_{k}]}x^{k}-\gamma_{i_{k}}G_{[i_{k}]}x^{k-1}\right),}\vskip-2.15277pt

(RCOG)

where
\eta_{i_{k}}>0
and
\gamma_{i_{k}}\in(0,1)
are given parameters corresponding to the
i_{k}
-th block coordinate
(
determined later
)
, and
G_{[i]}x=[\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\cdots,\mathbf{0},[Gx]_{i},\mathbf{0},\cdots,\mathbf{0}]
such that only the
i
th-block is
[Gx]_{i}
, and other blocks are zero.

If
n=1
(i.e. one block), then
RCOG
reduces to
OG
in the deterministic case.
For each block-coordinate
i
, we use different constant parameters
\eta_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
at all iterations.
In terms of algorithmic form,
RCOG
uses two block-coordinate evaluations
[Gx^{k}]_{i_{k}}
and
[Gx^{k-1}]_{i_{k}}
of
G
evaluated at the two consecutive iterates
x^{k}
and
x^{k-1}
, respectively.

3.2
Lyapunov function and descent lemma.

We introduce a new Lyapunov function:

\mathcal{P}_{k}:=\|x^{k}-x^{\star}+\gamma\eta\circ Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}+\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|_{\sigma}^{2},

(10)

where
\sigma:=(\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
is a given weight vector.
Then, the following lemma provides a key descent property to analyze the convergence of
RCOG
, whose proof is in Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 3.1

Suppose that
\mathrm{zer}(G)\neq\emptyset
and Assumption
3
holds.
Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by
RCOG
to solve (
NE
) and
\mathcal{P}_{k}
be defined by (
10
).
If
L_{i}^{2}\gamma_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}\big(2-\mathbf{p}_{i}+2\sigma_{i}\big)\leq\mathbf{p}_{i}\sigma_{i}
for all
i\in[n]
, then

{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\big]&\leq&\mathcal{P}_{k}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}2\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})\big[\eta_{i}-\frac{\rho_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\eta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})(1+\sigma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big]\cdot\|[Gx^{k}]_{i}\|^{2}.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(11)

3.3
Main result 1: Convergence of
RCOG
.

Utilizing Lemma
3.1
, we prove our first main result in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2

Suppose that
\mathrm{zer}(G)\neq\emptyset
and Assumption
3
holds for (
NE
).
Let
\mathcal{P}_{k}
be defined by (
10
) for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
and
\gamma_{i}
is chosen such that
\frac{1+\sigma_{i}}{1+\sigma_{i}+\mathbf{p}_{i}}<\gamma_{i}<1
for
i\in[n]
.
Suppose that

{L_{i}\rho_{i}<\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}\left[1-\tfrac{(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad 0<\eta_{i}\leq\tfrac{\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}}{\gamma_{i}L_{i}},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(12)

for all
i\in[n]
, where
\kappa_{i}^{2}:=\frac{\sigma_{i}}{2(1+\sigma_{i})}
.
Then, the following estimate holds:

{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\big]&\leq&\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{P}_{k}\big]-\psi\circ\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big],\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(13)

where
\psi_{i}:=2\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})\big[\eta_{i}-\frac{\rho_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\eta_{i}(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big]>0
and
\psi:=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{n})
.
Moreover, for
\psi_{\min}:=\min\{\psi_{i}:i\in[n]\}>0
, the following convergence bound also holds:

{\frac{\psi_{\min}}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\psi\circ\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{3\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}}{K+1}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(14)

Proof 3.3

Proof.

First, we denote
\kappa_{i}^{2}:=\frac{\sigma_{i}}{2(1+\sigma_{i})}
.
Since
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in(0,1)
, if
L_{i}\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}\leq\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
for all
i\in[n]
, then the condition
L_{i}^{2}\gamma_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}[2(1+\sigma_{i})-\mathbf{p}_{i}]\leq\mathbf{p}_{i}\sigma_{i}
in Lemma
3.1
holds.
Moreover, the condition
L_{i}\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}\leq\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}
leads to
0<\eta_{i}\leq\frac{\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}}{\gamma_{i}L_{i}}
as our choice of
\eta_{i}
in (
12
) of Theorem
3.2
.

Next, let us denote
\psi_{i}:=2\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})\big[\eta_{i}-\frac{\rho_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\eta_{i}(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big]
.
To guarantee
\psi_{i}>0
, we first need to impose that
\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}>(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})
and then choose
\eta_{i}>\frac{\rho_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}-(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}
.
The first condition holds if we choose
\frac{1+\sigma_{i}}{1+\sigma_{i}+\mathbf{p}_{i}}<\gamma_{i}<1
as we stated.
Clearly, combining the second condition and
0<\eta_{i}\leq\frac{\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}}{\gamma_{i}L_{i}}
, we have
L_{i}\rho_{i}<\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big[1-\frac{(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big]
as stated in (
12
) of Theorem
3.2
.

Now, using
\psi_{i}:=2\gamma_{i}\eta_{i}(1-\gamma_{i})\big[\eta_{i}-\frac{\rho_{i}}{\gamma_{i}}-\frac{\eta_{i}(1+\sigma_{i})(1-\gamma_{i})}{\gamma_{i}\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big]>0
and
\psi:=(\psi_{1},\cdots,\psi_{n})
, we obtain
\mathbb{E}_{k}\big[\mathcal{P}_{k+1}\big]\leq\mathcal{P}_{k}-\psi\circ\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
from (
11
).
Taking the full expectation of this inequality, we get (
13
).
Since
\mathcal{P}_{k}\geq 0
, letting
\psi_{\min}:=\min_{i\in[n]}\psi_{i}
, and then averaging (
13
) from
k:=0
to
k:=K
, we get

{\frac{\psi_{\min}}{K+1}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{1}{K+1}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\psi\circ\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}]\|^{2}\big]\leq\frac{\mathcal{P}_{0}}{K+1}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

Finally, since
x^{-1}=x^{0}
and
Gx^{\star}=0
, by the Lipschitz continuity of
G
and
2\gamma_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}L_{i}^{2}\leq 2\kappa_{i}\sqrt{\mathbf{p}_{i}}\leq 1
, we have
\mathcal{P}_{0}=\|x^{0}+\gamma\eta\circ Gx^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq 2\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}\eta^{2}\circ\|Gx^{0}-Gx^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2+2\gamma_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}^{2}L_{i}^{2})\|x_{i}^{0}-x_{i}^{\star}\|^{2}\leq 3\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
.
Substituting this bound of
\mathcal{P}_{0}
into the last inequality, we obtain (

).

Remark 3.4

(
Simple stepsizes and distribution

One simple choice of

and

4
The Accelerated Randomized Block-Coordinate OG Method.

Alternative to the non-accelerated method in Section
3
, in this section, we develop an accelerated variant of
RCOG
for solving (
NE
) under the following assumption.

{assumption}

{\langle{Gx-Gy,x-y}\rangle\geq\bar{\beta}\circ\|Gx-Gy\|^{2}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(CP)

Assumption
4
states that
G
is
1
-co-coercive w.r.t. a weighted norm
\|\cdot\|_{\bar{\beta}}
.
Clearly, under (
CP
),
\langle{Gx-Gy,x-y}\rangle\geq\bar{\beta}_{\min}\|Gx-Gy\|^{2}
, showing that
G
is
\bar{\beta}_{\min}
-co-coercive, where
\bar{\beta}_{\min}:=\min\{\bar{\beta}_{i}:i\in[n]\}
.
Conversely, if
G
is
\hat{\beta}
-co-coercive, then it satisfies (
CP
) with
\bar{\beta}_{i}=\hat{\beta}
for all
i\in[n]
.
Note that (
CP
) is weaker than the block-coordinate-wise co-coerciveness of
G
, i.e.
\langle{[Gx]_{i}-[Gy]_{i},x_{i}-y_{i}}\rangle\geq\bar{\beta}_{i}\|[Gx]_{i}-[Gy]_{i}\|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
i\in[n]
.
The condition (
CP
) also implies that
G
is monotone and
\bar{L}
-Lipschitz continuous with
\bar{L}:=\big(\min\left\{\bar{\beta}_{i}:i\in[n]\right\}\big)^{-1}
.
It is stronger than Assumption
3
.

As shown in Subsection
2.4
, (
NE
) is equivalent to the fixed-point problem (
FP
) of
F:=\mathbb{I}-\eta G
for any
\eta>0
.
Moreover, the co-coerciveness of
G
is equivalent to the non-expansiveness of
F

[
7
, Proposition 4.11]
.
Therefore, the problem of finding a fixed-point of a non-expansive operator
F
, can be cast into (
NE
) under Assumption
4
, which covers a broad range of applications.

In smooth and convex optimization, if
Gx=\nabla{f}(x)
, the gradient of the objective function
f
, then the convexity of
f
and the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla{f}
is equivalent to the
\frac{1}{L}
-co-coerciveness of
G
, see
[
67
, Theorem 2.1.5]
.
Thus Assumption
4
is not restrictive.

4.1
The derivation of algorithm.

Our
accelerated randomized block-coordinate optimistic gradient
(
ARCOG
) scheme for solving (
NE
) is presented as follows:

Starting from
x^{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, we set
x^{-1}:=x^{0}
, and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, we randomly sample a block-coordinate
i_{k}\in[n]
using (
9
) and update

{x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\frac{\eta_{k}}{\mathbf{p}_{i_{k}}}\big(G_{[i_{k}]}x^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{[i_{k}]}x^{k-1}\big),}\vskip-2.15277pt

(ARCOG)

where
\theta_{k}\in(0,1)
,
\eta_{k}>0
, and
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
are given parameters at each iteration
k
and for all block-coordinates
(
determined later
)
, and
G_{[i]}x^{k}=[\mathbf{0},\cdots,\mathbf{0},[Gx^{k}]_{i},\mathbf{0},\cdots,\mathbf{0}]
such that
[Gx^{k}]_{i}
is the

-the block of
Gx^{k}
(
i\in[n]
).

Compared to
RCOG
, our

still requires to use simultaneously two block-coordinates
[Gx^{k}]_{i_{k}}
and
[Gx^{k-1}]_{i_{k}}
evaluated at the two consecutive iterates
x^{k}
and
x^{k-1}

\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})
.
These two features make it different from existing randomized [block]-coordinate methods in
[
22
,
73
]
for monotone inclusions as well as randomized coordinate-type methods for optimization such as
[
33
,
46
,
69
,
70
,
79
,
94
]
.

Practical variant of

Given

Remark 4.4

5
Application to Finite-Sum Root-Finding Problems.

The finite-sum inclusion (
FNI
) provides a unified template to cope with many applications in different fields, including networks, distributed systems, statistical learning, and machine learning as mentioned earlier.
In this work, we are interested in instances of (
FNI
) arising from
federated learning
(FL), where the number of users
n
is sufficiently large.

Briefly, FL is a recent paradigm in machine learning to train a learning algorithm that coordinates different users (also called workers or agents) to perform a given task without sharing users’ raw data.
A key point that distinguishes FL from traditional distributed or parallel algorithms is the privacy of data, where the raw data of users must not be shared.
Moreover, due to a large number of users and a heterogeneity of the system, only one or a small number of users can participate in each communication round (i.e. each iteration
k
).
Existing FL algorithms are often designed to solve FL optimization problems such as
[
40
,
48
,
60
,
61
]
.
There is only a limited number of works studying minimax problems and other extensions, e.g.,
[
31
,
85
,
87
]
but these works often require strong assumptions and generally do not cover our model (
FNI
).

Our goal is to apply both
RCOG
and
ARCOG
to develop new FL-type algorithms for solving (
FNI
).
Our algorithms can solve a more general class of problems covered by (
FNI
) beyond optimization under milder assumptions than many previous works.
Moreover, they only require exchanging certain intermediate vectors between users, which fulfills the privacy requirement.
They also do not require the full participation of all users at each communication round
k
.

6.1
Minimax problems.

Our first mathematical model for numerical experiments is the following minimax problem, which is possibly nonconvex-nonconcave:

{\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}}\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}\Big\{\mathcal{L}(u,v):=f(u)+\tfrac{1}{2}u^{\top}Pu+b^{\top}u+u^{\top}Hv-\tfrac{1}{2}v^{\top}Qv-c^{\top}v-g(v)\Big\},}\vskip-2.15277pt

(47)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
are two proper, closed, and convex functions, often used to handle constraints or regularizers,
P\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{1}}
and
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{2}}
are two given symmetric matrices,
H\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
is a given matrix, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
are given vectors.

We denote
x:=[u,v]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
p:=p_{1}+p_{2}
as the concatenation of the primal variable
u
and its dual variable
v
.
We also define
\mathbf{G}:=[[P,H],[-H,Q]]
as the KKT matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
formed from 4 blocks
P
,
H
,
-H
, and
Q
, and
\mathbf{g}:=[b,c]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as the concatenation of
b
and

.
When

and

are present, we denote

as a maximally monotone mapping formed from the subdifferentials of

and
g
.
Then, the optimality condition of (
47
) can be written as

, which is a special form of (
FNI
), where
Gx:=\mathbf{G}x+\mathbf{g}
is a linear mapping.
If

, i.e.

and
g
are absent in (
47
), then this optimality condition reduces to
Gx=0
(i.e.
\mathbf{G}x+\mathbf{g}=0
– a linear system) as a special case of (
NE
).
Since

,
\mathbf{G}
is non-symmetric.
In addition,

and
Q
are possibly not positive semidefinite, implying that

may not be monotone.

Lemma A.1

Let
\mathcal{L}:=\{\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{np}:x_{i}=x_{1},\forall i=2,\cdots,n\}
and
\mathbf{T}_{1}\mathbf{x}:=[nTx_{1},0,\cdots,0]
for a given maximally monotone operator
T
.
Then, for any
\beta>0
and
\mathbf{u}:=[u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}]
, we have

{J_{\beta(\mathbf{T}_{1}+\partial{\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})}\mathbf{u}=[\hat{u},\cdots,\hat{u}],\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{u}:=J_{\beta T}\Big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\Big).}\vskip-2.15277pt

Proof A.2

Proof.

Computing the resolvent
J_{\beta(\mathbf{T}_{1}+\partial{\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})}\mathbf{u}
of
\beta(\mathbf{T}_{1}+\partial{\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})
at
\mathbf{u}:=[u_{1},\cdots,u_{n}]
is equivalent to solving the following system in
\tilde{u}_{i}
and
\tilde{s}_{i}
(
i\in[n]
):

{\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}0\in n\beta T\tilde{u}_{1}+\beta\tilde{s}_{1}+\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1},\\
0=\beta\tilde{s}_{i}+\tilde{u}_{i}-u_{i},\quad i=2,\cdots,n,\end{array}\right.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(51)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{s}}:=[\tilde{s}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{s}_{n}]\in\partial{\delta}_{\mathcal{L}}(\tilde{\mathbf{u}})\equiv\mathcal{L}^{\perp}:=\{\tilde{\mathbf{s}}:=[\tilde{s}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{s}_{n}]:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{s}_{i}=0\}
.
The last line of (
51
) leads to
\tilde{u}_{i}=u_{i}-\beta\tilde{s}_{i}
for
i=2,\cdots,n
.
Therefore, since
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathcal{L}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{s}_{i}=0
, we have
\tilde{u}_{i}=\tilde{u}_{1}
for
i=2,\cdots,n
and

{(n-1)\tilde{u}_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}u_{i}-\beta\sum_{i=2}^{n}\tilde{s}_{i}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}u_{i}-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{s}_{i}+\beta\tilde{s}_{1}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}u_{i}+\beta\tilde{s}_{1}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

This equation implies that
\tilde{u}_{1}+\beta\tilde{s}_{1}=n\tilde{u}_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}u_{i}
.
Substituting this expression into the first line of (
51
), we get
0\in n\beta T\tilde{u}_{1}+n\tilde{u}_{1}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}
, or equivalently,
0\in\beta T\tilde{u}_{1}+\tilde{u}_{1}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}
.
Solving this inclusion, we obtain
\tilde{u}_{1}=J_{\beta T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\right)
.
Let us define
\hat{u}:=\tilde{u}_{1}=J_{\beta T}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\right)
.
Then, since
\tilde{u}_{i}=\tilde{u}_{1}=\hat{u}
for
i=2,\cdots,n
, we have
J_{\beta(\mathbf{T}_{1}+\partial{\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})}\mathbf{u}=[\hat{u},\cdots,\hat{u}]
as desired.

\square

Lemma A.3

Let
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
be maximally monotone and
G:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be
L
-Lipschitz continuous, and
\langle{u,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq-\rho\|u\|^{2}
for any
(x,u)\in\mathrm{gra}(G+T)
and
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G+T)
, where
L>0
and
\rho\geq 0
are given.
For some
\lambda>0
, we consider the following Tseng’s FBFS operator

{S_{\lambda}x:=x-J_{\lambda T}(x-\lambda Gx)-\lambda\left(Gx-G\circ J_{\lambda T}(x-\lambda Gx)\right).}\vskip-2.15277pt

(52)

Then,
S_{\lambda}
is
L_{s}
-Lipschitz continuous with
L_{s}:=(1+\lambda L)(2+\lambda L)
.
Moreover,
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G+T)
iff
S_{\lambda}x^{\star}=0
.
For
\hat{\rho}:=\frac{1-\lambda L}{(1+\lambda L)^{2}}-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}
, the following property holds:

{\langle{S_{\lambda}x,x-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq\hat{\rho}\|S_{\lambda}x\|^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathrm{dom}(G+T).}\vskip-2.15277pt

(53)

If
8L\rho\leq 1
, then for any
\lambda
such that
0<\frac{1-2L\rho-\sqrt{1-8L\rho}}{2L(1+L\rho)}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1-2L\rho+\sqrt{1-8L\rho}}{2L(1+L\rho)}
, we have
\hat{\rho}\geq 0
.

Proof A.4

Proof.

The statement
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G+T)
iff
S_{\lambda}x^{\star}=0
and the
L_{s}
-Lipschitz continuity of
S_{\lambda}
were proven in
[
13
, Proposition 1]
.
For any
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G+T)
, if we denote
z:=J_{\lambda T}(x-\lambda Gx)
, then by (
52
), we have
S_{\lambda}x=x-z-\lambda(Gx-Gz)
.
Moreover,
u:=\frac{1}{\lambda}S_{\lambda}x=\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-z)-(Gx-Gz)\in(G+T)z
, which shows that
(z,u)\in\mathrm{gra}(G+T)
.
In addition, by
[
13
, Proposition 1]
, we have
\|S_{\lambda}x\|\leq(1+\lambda L)\|x-z\|
.

Now, for any
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G+T)
, since
u:=\frac{1}{\lambda}S_{\lambda}x\in(G+T)z
, by our assumption
\langle{u,z-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq-\rho\|u\|^{2}
, we have
\langle{S_{\lambda}x,z-x^{\star}}\rangle\geq-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\|S_{\lambda}x\|^{2}
.
This inequality leads to

{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\langle{S_{\lambda}x,x-x^{\star}}\rangle&\geq&\langle{x-z-\lambda(Gx-Gz),x-z}\rangle-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\|S_{\lambda}x\|^{2}\\
&\geq&(1-\lambda L)\|x-z\|^{2}-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\|S_{\lambda}x\|^{2}\\
&\geq&\left(\frac{1-\lambda L}{(1+\lambda L)^{2}}-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\right)\|S_{\lambda}x\|^{2},\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

which proves (
53
).
Finally, since
\hat{\rho}:=\frac{1-\lambda L}{(1+\lambda L)^{2}}-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}
, if
L(1+L\rho)\lambda^{2}-(1-2L\rho)\lambda+\rho\leq 0
, then
\hat{\rho}\geq 0
.
The last condition holds if
0<\frac{1-2L\rho-\sqrt{1-8L\rho}}{2L(1+L\rho)}\leq\lambda\leq\frac{1-2L\rho+\sqrt{1-8L\rho}}{2L(1+L\rho)}
, provided that
8L\rho\leq 1
.

\square

exists almost surely.
Moreover, almost surely, we also have

{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+r+1)\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+r)^{2}\|Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(70)

These prove the first two summable bounds in (
69
).

Step 2
: The third summable bound in (
69
).
Next, similar to the proof of (
32
), but without taking the full expectation, with
v^{k}:=x^{k+1}-x^{k}
and
\psi:=\max_{i\in[n]}\big(\frac{1-\mathbf{p}_{i}}{\mathbf{p}_{i}}\big)
, we have

{\hskip-12.91663pt\begin{array}[]{ll}(k+r-1)(k+r+2)&\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|v^{k}+\eta_{k}Gx^{k}\|^{2}\right]\leq(k+r-2)(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&-{~}(r-2)(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&+{~}\psi\omega^{2}(k+r)^{2}\cdot\|Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}+\frac{4(k+r+1)}{r}\cdot\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}.\end{array}\hskip-25.83325pt}\vskip-2.15277pt

Since the last two terms are summable almost surely as proven in (
70
), applying again
[
80
, Theorem 1]
, we conclude that
\lim_{k\to\infty}(k+r-2)(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}
exists almost surely.
Moreover, we also obtain
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty
almost surely.
Combining both statements, almost surely, we conclude that

{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\lim_{k\to\infty}(k+r-2)(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}=0,\\
&\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+r+1)\|v^{k-1}+\eta_{k-1}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(71)

Utilizing (
35
), the first summable bound in (
70
), and the second line of (
71
), we can easily show that
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(k+r+1)\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}<+\infty
almost surely, which proves the third line of (
69
).

Step 3
: The last two limits in (
69
).
Now, similar to the proof of (
37
) but without taking the full expectation, we get

{\hskip-8.61108pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}(k+r+2)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\right]&\leq&(k+r+1)^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}-(r+2)(k+r)\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&+{~}\frac{\omega^{2}(k+r)^{2}}{\mathbf{p}_{\min}}\cdot\|Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}+\omega^{2}rk\cdot\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}\hskip-17.22217pt}\vskip-2.15277pt

Since the last two terms are summable almost surely due to (
70
) and (
71
), applying one more time
[
80
, Theorem 1]
, we obtain that
\lim_{k\to\infty}(k+r+1)^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}
exists almost surely and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k+r)\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}<+\infty
almost surely.
These two statements imply the first limit in the last line of (
69
), that is

{\begin{array}[]{ll}&\lim_{k\to\infty}(k+r+1)^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}=0\quad\textrm{almost surely}.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

(72)

Combining the limit in the first line of (
71
), the limit in (
72
), and (
35
), we can prove that
\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{2}\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}=0
almost surely, which proves the second limit in the last line of (
69
).

Step 4
: The existence of
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
.
Furthermore, for
\mu=1
, it is obvious to see that
r\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
.
Combining this relation and the existence of
\lim_{k\to\infty}\hat{P}_{k}
almost surely, there exists
M>0
such that almost surely

{\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\leq M,\quad\textrm{for all}\ k\geq 0.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(73)

Since
\frac{\omega r}{r+2}\leq\eta_{k}\leq\omega
for all
k\geq 0
due to (
23
) and
t_{k}:=k+r+1
, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
73
), and the last limit of (
69
), we can show that almost surely

{\begin{array}[]{lcl}|t_{k}\eta_{k-1}\langle{Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}}\rangle|^{2}&\leq&|t_{k}\langle{Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}}\rangle|^{2}\\
&\leq&\|x^{k-1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\cdot t_{k}^{2}\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&\overset{\tiny\eqref{eq:lmA3_proof1}}{\leq}&Mt_{k}^{2}\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\overset{\tiny\eqref{eq:ARCOG_almost_sure}}{\to}0\quad\textrm{as}\ k\to\infty.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

Thus we get
\lim_{k\to\infty}|t_{k}\eta_{k-1}\langle{Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}}\rangle|=0
almost surely.
Moreover, from the third line of (
69
), we have
\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}\eta_{k-1}\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}=0
almost surely.
Combining the last two limits yields

{\lim_{k\to\infty}t_{k}\eta_{k-1}\big[\langle{Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}}\rangle-\beta\circ\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\big]=0\quad\textrm{almost surely}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(74)

Similarly, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
73
), and the last line of (
69
), we have, almost surely

{\begin{array}[]{lcl}|t_{k}\langle{x^{k-1}-x^{\star},x^{k}-x^{k-1}}\rangle|^{2}&\leq&\|x^{k-1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}\cdot t_{k}^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&\overset{\tiny\eqref{eq:lmA3_proof1}}{\leq}&Mt_{k}^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\overset{\tiny\eqref{eq:ARCOG_almost_sure}}{\to}0\quad\textrm{as}\ k\to\infty.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

Hence, we conclude that

{\lim_{k\to\infty}|t_{k}\langle{x^{k-1}-x^{\star},x^{k}-x^{k-1}}\rangle|=0\quad\textrm{almost surely}.}\vskip-2.15277pt

(75)

Finally, from (
18
), with
\mu=1
, we can rewrite
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{k}
as

{\begin{array}[]{lcll}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{k}&=&2rt_{k}\eta_{k-1}\big[\langle{Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}}\rangle-\beta\circ\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\big]+2rt_{k}\langle{x^{k-1}-x^{\star},x^{k}-x^{k-1}}\rangle\\
&&+{~}t_{k}^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}+r(r+1)\|x^{k-1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.\end{array}}\vskip-2.15277pt

Since, almost surely, the limit on the left-hand side of this expression exists, the limit of the first two terms on the right-hand side is zero due to (
74
) and (
75
), respectively.
The limit of the third term
t_{k}^{2}\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}
on the right-hand side is zero almost surely due to the last line of (
69
).
Therefore, we conclude that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|x^{k-1}-x^{\star}\|^{2}
exists almost surely, which proves our claim.

\square
