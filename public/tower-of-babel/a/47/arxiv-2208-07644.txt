Asynchronous Functional Sessions: Cyclic and Concurrent (Extended Version ) Thanks: This is an extended version of our workshop paper [ 15 ] , with technical details in the appendix. Affiliation: University of Groningen, The Netherlands
Bas van den Heuvel Jorge A. Pérez

Abstract
We present Concurrent GV (CGV), a functional calculus with message-passing concurrency governed by session types. With respect to prior calculi, CGV has increased support for concurrent evaluation and for cyclic network topologies. The design of CGV draws on APCP, a session-typed asynchronous π \pi -calculus developed in prior work. Technical contributions are (i) the syntax, semantics, and type system of CGV; (ii) a correct translation of CGV into APCP; (iii) a technique for establishing deadlock-free CGV programs, by resorting to APCP’s priority-based type system.

Introduction

The goal of this paper is to introduce a new functional calculus with message-passing concurrency governed by linearity and session types.
Our work contributes to a research line initiated by Gay and Vasconcelos
[
9
]
, who proposed a functional calculus with sessions here referred to as
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
; this line of work has received much recent attention thanks to Wadler’s GV calculus
[
27
]
, which is a variation of
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
.

Our new calculus is dubbed Concurrent GV (CGV);
with respect to previous work, it
presents three intertwined novelties: asynchronous (buffered) communication; a highly concurrent reduction strategy; and thread configurations with cyclic topologies.
The design of CGV rests upon a solid basis: an operationally correct translation into APCP (Asynchronous Priority-based Classical Processes), a session-typed
\pi
-calculus in which asynchronous processes communicate by forming cyclic networks
[
14
]
.

We discuss the salient features of CGV by example, using a simplified syntax.
As in
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
, communication in CGV is asynchronous: send operations place their messages in buffers, and receive operations read the messages from these buffers.
Let us write
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{send}~(u,x)}
to denote the output of message
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}u}
along channel
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{recv}~y}
to denote an input on
y
.
The following program expresses the parallel composition (
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\parallel}
) of two threads:

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\left.\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathsf{let}\,x=\mathsf{send}~(u,x)\,\mathsf{in}\\
\quad\mathsf{let}\,(v,y)=\mathsf{recv}~y\,\mathsf{in}\,()\end{array}\qquad\middle|\!\middle|\qquad\mathsf{let}\,y=\underline{\mathsf{send}~(w,y)}\,\mathsf{in}\,()\right.}

In variants of
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
with
synchronous
communication,
such as GV and Kokke and Dardha’s PGV
[
18
,
19
]
,
this program is stuck: the send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
(underlined, on the right) cannot synchronize with the receive on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
(on the left): it is blocked by the send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
(on the left), and there is no receive on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
.
In contrast, in CGV the send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
can be buffered after which the communication on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
can take place.

In CGV, reduction is “more concurrent” than usual call-by-value or call-by-name strategies.
Consider the following program:

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}l@{}}\lambda x\mathbin{.}&\mathsf{let}\,(u,y)=\mathsf{recv}~y\,\mathsf{in}\\
&\quad\mathsf{let}\,x=\mathsf{send}~(u,x)\,\mathsf{in}()\end{array}\right)\quad\big(\mathsf{send}~(v,z)\big)}

In
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
, reduction is call-by-value and so the function on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
can only be applied on a value.
However, the function’s parameter (send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}z}
) is not a value, so it needs to be evaluated before the function on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
can be applied.
Hence, this program can only be evaluated in one order: first the send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}z}
, then the receive on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
.
In contrast, the semantics of CGV evaluates a function and its parameters
concurrently
: the send on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}z}
and the receive on
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
can be evaluated in any order.
Note that asynchrony plays no role here: both buffering a message and synchronous communication entail a reduction in the function’s parameter.

The third novelty is cyclic thread configurations: threads can be connected by channels to form cyclic networks.
Consider the following program:

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\left.\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathsf{let}\,(u,x)=\mathsf{recv}~x\,\mathsf{in}\\
\quad\mathsf{let}\,y=\mathsf{send}~(u,y)\,\mathsf{in}\,()\end{array}\qquad\middle|\!\middle|\qquad\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\mathsf{let}\,x=\mathsf{send}~(v,x)\,\mathsf{in}\\
\quad\mathsf{let}\,(w,y)=\mathsf{recv}~y\,\mathsf{in}\,()\end{array}\right.}

Here we have two threads connected on channels
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
, thus forming a cyclic thread configuration.
Clearly, this program is deadlock-free.
In
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
, the program is well-typed, but there is no deadlock-freedom guarantee: the type system of
\lambda^{\mkern-5.0mu\text{sess}}
admits deadlocked cyclic thread configurations.
In GV and Fowler
et al.
’s EGV
[
8
]
(an extension of Fowler’s AGV
[
7
]
) there is a deadlock-freedom guarantee for well-typed programs; however, their type systems only support
tree-shaped
thread configurations—this limitation is studied in
[
5
,
6
]
. Hence, the program above is not well-typed in GV and EGV.

These novelties are
intertwined
, in the following sense.
Asynchronous communication reduces the synchronization points in programs (as output-like operations are non-blocking), therefore increasing concurrent evaluation.
In turn, reduced synchronization points can streamline verification techniques for deadlock-freedom based on
priorities

[
17
,
23
,
24
,
4
]
, which unlock the analysis of process networks with cyclic topologies.
Indeed, in an asynchronous setting only input-like operations require priorities.

We endow CGV with a type system with functional types and session types; we opted for a design in which well-typed terms enjoy subject reduction / type preservation but not deadlock-freedom.
To validate our semantic design and attain the three novelties motivated above, we resort to APCP.
In our developments, APCP operates as a “low-level” reference programming model.
We give a typed translation of CGV into APCP, which satisfies strong correctness properties, in the sense of Gorla
[
11
]
. In particular, it enjoys operational correspondence, which provides a significant sanity check to justify our key design decisions in CGV’s operational semantics.
Interestingly, using our correct translation and the deadlock-freedom guarantees for well-typed processes in APCP, we obtain a technique for transferring the deadlock-freedom property to CGV programs.
That is,
given a CGV program
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
,
we prove that if the APCP translation of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
is typable (and hence, deadlock-free), then
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
itself is deadlock-free.
This result thus delineates a class of deadlock-free CGV programs that includes cyclic thread configurations.

In summary, this paper presents the following technical contributions:
(1) CGV, a new functional calculus with session-based asynchronous concurrency;
(2) A typed translation of CGV into APCP, which is proven to satisfy well-studied encodability criteria;
(3) A transference result for the deadlock-freedom property from APCP to CGV programs.
An extended version contains omitted technical details
[
report/vdHeuvelP22
]
.

The CGV configuration language.

The main syntactic entities in CGV are
terms
,
runtime terms
, and
configurations
.
Intuitively, terms reduce to runtime terms; configurations correspond to the parallel composition of a main thread and several child threads, each executing a runtime term. Buffered messages are part of configurations. We define two reduction relations: one is on terms, which is then subsumed by reduction on configurations.

The syntax of terms (
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}L},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}N}
) is given and described in
Figure

1
. We use
x,y,\ldots
for
variables
; we write
endpoint
to refer to a variable used for session operations (send, receive, select, offer).
Let
\mathrm{fn}({\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M})
denote the free variables of a term.
All variables are free unless bound:
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\lambda x\mathbin{.}M}
binds
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
in
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{let}\,(x,y)=M\,\mathsf{in}\,N}
binds
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}
in
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}N}
.
We introduce syntactic sugar for applications of abstractions:
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{let}\,x=M\,\mathsf{in}\,N}
denotes
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}(\lambda x\mathbin{.}N)~M}
.
For
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}(\lambda x\mathbin{.}M)~N}
, we assume
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}\notin\mathrm{fn}({\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}N})
, and for
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{let}\,(x,y)=M\,\mathsf{in}\,N}
, we assume
x\neq y
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}y}\notin\mathrm{fn}({\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M})
.

Figure

1
also gives the reduction semantics of CGV terms (
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{M}}
), which relies on runtime terms (
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{L}},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{M}},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{N}}
), reduction contexts (
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathcal{R}}
), and structural congruence (
\equiv_{\mathtt{M}}
).
Note that this semantics comprises the functional fragment of CGV; we define the concurrent semantics of CGV hereafter.

Runtime terms, whose syntax extends that of terms, guide the evaluation strategy of CGV; we discuss an example evaluation of a term using runtime terms after introducing the reduction rules (
Example

2.1
).
Explicit substitution
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{M}{\lBrace\mathbb{N}/x\rBrace}}
enables the concurrent execution of a function and its parameters.
The intermediate primitive
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{send}^{\prime}(\mathbb{M},\mathbb{N})}
enables
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{N}}
to reduce to an endpoint; the
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{send}}
primitive takes a pair of terms as an argument, inside which reduction is not permitted (
cf.

]
).
Reduction contexts define the
non-blocking
parts of terms, where subterms may reduce.
We write
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathcal{R}[\mathbb{M}]}
to denote the runtime term obtained by replacing the hole

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{M}}

We discuss the reduction rules.
The Structural congruence rule
SC-SubExt
allows the scope extrusion of explicit substitutions along reduction contexts.
Rule

Rule

unpacks the elements of a pair into two explicit substitutions (arbitrarily ordered, due to the syntactical assumptions introduced above).
Rules
E-SubstName
and

convert explicit substitutions of or on variables into standard substitutions.
Rule

reduces a

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathsf{send}^{\prime}}

Rules

and

We write
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{M}}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{M}}^{k}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\mathbb{N}}

Concurrency in CGV allows the parallel execution of terms that communicate through buffers.
The syntax of configurations (
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D},{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}E}

Figure

.
The configuration

We comment on some of the congruences and reduction rules.

Rule
SC-ResSwap
allows to swap the direction of an empty buffer; this way, the endpoint that could read from the buffer before the swap can now write to it.

Rule
SC-ResComm
allows to interchange buffers, and
Rule
SC-ResExt
allows to extrude their scope.
Rule
SC-ResNil
garbage collects buffers of closed sessions.

Rule
SC-ConfSubst
lifts explicit substitution at the level of terms to the level of threads, and Rule
SC-ConfSubstExt
allows the scope extrusion of explicit substitution along configuration contexts.
Notably, putting messages in buffers is not a reduction: Rules
SC-Send’
and
SC-Select

equate
sends and selects on an endpoint
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
with terms and labels in the buffer for
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}x}
, as asynchronous outputs are computationally equivalent to messages in buffers.

Reduction rule
E-New
creates a new buffer, leaving a reference to the newly created endpoints in the thread.
Rule
E-Spawn
spawns a child thread (the parameter pair’s first element) and continues (as the pair’s second element) inside the calling thread.
Rule
E-Recv
retrieves a term from a buffer, resulting in a pair containing the term and a reference to the receiving endpoint.
Rule
E-Case
retrieves a label from a buffer, resulting in a function application of the label’s corresponding branch to a reference to the receiving endpoint.
There are no reduction rules for closing sessions, as they are closed silently.
We write
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}^{k}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
to denote that
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
reduces to
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
in
k
steps.
Also, we write
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}^{+}
to denote the transitive closure of
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}
(i.e., reduction in at least one step).

Syntax and Semantics

We write
x,y,z,\ldots
to denote
endpoints
(or
names
), and write
\tilde{x},\tilde{y},\tilde{z},\ldots
to denote sequences of endpoints.
Also, we write
i,j,k,\ldots
to denote
labels
and
I,J,K,\ldots
to denote sets of labels.

Figure

5
(top) gives the syntax and meaning of processes.
In APCP, all endpoints are used strictly linearly: each endpoint can be used for exactly one communication only.
However, we want to assign session types to endpoints, so we have to be able to implement sequences of communications.
Therefore, each communication action carries an additional
continuation endpoint
to continue the session on.

The output action
x[y,z]
sends a message endpoint
y
and a continuation endpoint
z
along
x
.
The input prefix
x(y,z)\mathbin{.}P
blocks until a message and a continuation endpoint are received on
x
, binding
y
and
z
in
P
.
The selection action
x[z]\mathbin{\triangleleft}i
sends a label
i
and a continuation endpoint
z
along
x
.
The branching prefix
x(z)\mathbin{\triangleright}\{i:P_{i}\}_{i\in I}
blocks until it receives a label
i\in I
and a continuation endpoint
z
on
x
, binding
z
in each
P_{i}
.
Restriction
(\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\nu}xy)P
binds
x
and
y
in
P
to form a channel for communication.
The process
P\mathbin{|}Q
denotes parallel composition.
The process
\bm{0}
denotes inaction.
The forwarder process
x\mathbin{\leftrightarrow}y
is a primitive copycat process that links together
x
and
y
.

Endpoints are free unless they are bound somehow.
We write
\mathrm{fn}(P)
for the set of free names of
P
.
Also, we write
P\{x/y\}
to denote the capture-avoiding substitution of the free occurrences of
y
in
P
for
x

The Type System

APCP types processes by assigning binary session types to channel endpoints.
Following Curry-Howard interpretations, we present session types as linear logic propositions (cf. Caires
et al.

[
3
]
and Wadler
[
27
]
)
extended with
priority
annotations.
Intuitively, actions typed with lower priority cannot be blocked by those with higher priority.

We write
\mathsf{o},\kappa,\pi,\rho,\ldots
to denote priorities, and
\omega
to denote the ultimate priority that is greater than all other priorities and cannot be increased further.
That is,
\forall\mathsf{o}\in\mathbb{N}.~\omega>\mathsf{o}
and
\forall\mathsf{o}\in\mathbb{N}.~\omega+\mathsf{o}=\omega
.

Definition 3.1
.

The following grammar defines the syntax of
session types

A,B
.
Let

\displaystyle A,B

\displaystyle::=A\mathbin{\otimes}^{\mathsf{o}}B

(output)

\displaystyle\;\mbox{\large{$\mid$}}\;A\mathbin{\mathchoice{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\displaystyle{\&}$}}{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\textstyle{\&}$}}{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\scriptstyle{\&}$}}{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\&}$}}}^{\mathsf{o}}B

(input)

\displaystyle\;\mbox{\large{$\mid$}}\;\oplus^{\mathsf{o}}\{i:A\}_{i\in I}

(select)

\displaystyle\;\mbox{\large{$\mid$}}\;\&^{\mathsf{o}}\{i:A\}_{i\in I}

(branch)

\displaystyle\;\mbox{\large{$\mid$}}\;\bullet

(end)

Note that type

Duality

Furthermore, dual types must have matching priority annotations.

The typing rules of APCP ensure that actions with lower priority are not blocked by those with higher priority (cf. Dardha and Gay

1.

An action with priority

4
Translating CGV into APCP

4.1
The Translation

In this section, we translate CGV into APCP.
We translate entire typing derivations, following, e.g., Wadler
[
27
]
.
Given the structure of CGV and its type system, the translation is defined in parts: for (runtime) terms, for configurations, and for buffers.
The translation is defined on well-typed configurations which may be deadlocked, so our translation does not consider priority requirements.
As we will see, typability in APCP will enable us to identify deadlock-free configurations in CGV (cf. Sec.
4.3
).

The translation is informed by the semantics of CGV.
It is crucial that subterms may only reduce when they occur in reduction contexts.
For example,
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{2}}
may not reduce if they appear in a pair
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}(M_{1},M_{2})}
.
The translation must thus ensure that subterms are blocked when they do not occur in reduction contexts.
Translations such as Wadler’s hinge on blocking outputs and inputs;
for example, the pair
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}(M_{1},M_{2})}
is translated as an output that blocks the translations of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{2}}
.
However, outputs in APCP are non-blocking and so
we use additional inputs to disable the reduction of subterms.
For example, the translation of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}(M_{1},M_{2})}
adds extra inputs to block the translations of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{1}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}M_{2}}
.

The translation of buffers requires care: each message in the buffer is translated as an output in APCP, where the output of the following messages is on the former output’s continuation endpoint.
Once there are no more messages in the buffer, the translation uses a typed APCP process—a parameter of the translation—to provide the behavior of the continuation of the lastmost output.

Following Gorla

With the soundness result, our translation is stronger than related prior translations

Our completeness result states that the reductions of a well-typed configuration can be mimicked by its translation in zero or more steps.

Theorem 4.6

(Completeness)
.

Given
{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\Gamma}\vdash_{\mathtt{C}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\phi}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}:{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}T}

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}^{\ast}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}
.

Proof (Sketch).

By induction on the derivation of the configuration’s reduction.
In each case, we infer the shape of the configuration from the reduction and well-typedness.
We then consider the translation of the configuration, and show that the resulting process reduces in zero or more steps to the translation of the reduced configuration.
See the extended version of this paper
[
report/vdHeuvelP22
]
for a full proof.
∎

Soundness states that any sequence of reductions from the translation of a well-typed configuration eventually leads to the translation of another configuration, which the initial configuration also reduces to.
Asynchrony in APCP requires us to be careful, specifically concerning the semantics of variables in CGV.
Variables can only cause reductions under specific circumstances.
On the other hand, variables translate to forwarders in APCP, which reduce as soon as they are bound by restriction.
This semantics for forwarders turns out to be too eager for soundness.
As a result, soundness only holds for an alternative, so-called
lazy semantics
for APCP, denoted
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}
, in which forwarders may only cause reductions under specific circumstances.
It is here that the forwarder-enabled restrictions
(\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\bm{\nu}}xy)
anticipated in
Section

4.1
come into play.
As we will see in
Section

4.3
, this alternative semantics does not prevent us from identifying a class of deadlock-free CGV configurations through the translation into APCP.
Due to space limitations, the definitions of the lazy semantics only appears in the extended version of this paper
[
report/vdHeuvelP22
]
.

k
of steps
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{k}Q
.
We then consider which reductions might occur from
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}
to
Q
.
Considering the structure of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
, we then isolate a sequence of
k^{\prime}
possible steps, such that
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{k^{\prime}}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}
for some
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}
where
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}
.
Since
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{k-k^{\prime}}Q
, it then follows from the induction hypothesis that there exists
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}^{\ast}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D^{\prime}}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{\ast}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}
.

Key here is the independence of reductions in APCP:
if two or more reductions are enabled from a (well-typed) process, they must originate from independent parts of the process, and so they do not interfere with each other.
This essentially means that the order in which independent reductions occur does not affect the resulting process.
Hence, we can pick “desirable” sequences of reductions, postponing other possible reductions.
See the extended version of this paper
[
report/vdHeuvelP22
]
for a full proof of soundness.
∎

From the proof above we can deduce that if the translation takes at least one step, then so does the source:

Corollary 4.8
.

Given
{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\Gamma}\vdash_{\mathtt{C}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}\phi}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}:{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}T}
, if
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{+}Q
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}^{+}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
and
Q\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}^{\ast}{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}
for some
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
.

4.3
Transferring Deadlock-freedom from APCP to CGV

In APCP, well-typed processes typable under empty contexts (
P\vdash\emptyset
) are deadlock-free.
By appealing to the operational correctness of our translation, we transfer this result to CGV configurations.
Each deadlock-free configuration in CGV obtained via transference satisfies two requirements:

•

The configuration is typable
{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\emptyset}\vdash_{\mathtt{C}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}{\mathchoice{\scriptstyle}{\scriptstyle}{\scriptscriptstyle}{}\blacklozenge}}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}:{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\bm{1}}
: it needs no external resources and has no external behavior.

•

The typed translation of the configuration satisfies APCP’s priority requirements: it is well-typed under ‘
\vdash
’, not only under ‘
\vdash^{\mkern-3.0mu\ast}
’ (cf. Def.
4.3
).

We rely on soundness (
Theorem

4.7
) to transfer deadlock-freedom to configurations.
However, APCP’s deadlock-freedom (
Theorem

3.5
) considers standard semantics (
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}
), whereas soundness considers the lazy semantics (
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}
).
Therefore, we first must show that if the translation of a configuration satisfying the requirements above reduces under
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}
, it also reduces under
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}
; this is
Theorem

4.9
below.
The deadlock-freedom of these configurations (
Theorem

4.10
) then follows from
Theorems

3.5
and
4.9
.
See the extended version of this paper
[
report/vdHeuvelP22
]
for detailed proofs of these results.

Theorem 4.9
.

Given
{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\emptyset}\vdash_{\mathtt{C}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}{\mathchoice{\scriptstyle}{\scriptstyle}{\scriptscriptstyle}{}\blacklozenge}}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}:{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\bm{1}}
, if
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\vdash\Gamma
for some
\Gamma
and
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}Q
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}Q^{\prime}
, for some
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof (Sketch).

By inspecting the derivation of
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}Q
.
If the reduction is not derived from
\shortrightarrow_{\text{Id}}
, it can be directly replicated under
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}
.
Otherwise, we analyze the possible shapes of
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}
and show that a different reduction under
\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{L}}
is possible.
∎

Theorem 4.10

(Deadlock-freedom for CGV)
.

​Given
{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\emptyset}\vdash_{\mathtt{C}}^{{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}{\mathchoice{\scriptstyle}{\scriptstyle}{\scriptscriptstyle}{}\blacklozenge}}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}:{\color[rgb]{0.7422,0.2891,0}\bm{1}}
, if
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\vdash\Gamma
for some
\Gamma
, then
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\equiv{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}{\mathchoice{\scriptstyle}{\scriptstyle}{\scriptscriptstyle}{}\blacklozenge}\,()}
or
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}_{\mathtt{C}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
for some
{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}D}
.

Proof (Sketch).

By assumption and
Theorem

4.4
,
{\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\vdash z:\bullet
.
Then
(\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\nu}z\_){\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\vdash\emptyset
.
By
Theorem

3.5
, (i)
(\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\nu}z\_){\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\equiv\bm{0}
or (ii)
(\mkern-1.0mu\bm{\nu}z\_){\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\llbracket{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\color[rgb]{0.6055,0,0.0117}\rrbracket}{z}\mathbin{\longrightarrow}Q
for some

.
In case (i) it follows from the well-typedness and translation of

{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}C}\equiv_{\mathtt{C}}{\color[rgb]{0.0977,0,0.543}{\mathchoice{\scriptstyle}{\scriptstyle}{\scriptscriptstyle}{}\blacklozenge}\,()}

5
Conclusion

We have presented CGV, a new functional language with asynchronous session-typed communication.
As illustrated in
Section

1
, CGV is strictly more expressive than its predecessors, thanks to a highly asynchronous semantics (compared to GV and PGV), its support for cyclic thread configurations (compared to EGV), and the ability to send whole terms and not just values (compared to all the mentioned calculi).

An operationally correct translation into APCP solidifies the design of CGV, and enables identifying a class of deadlock-free CGV programs.
Interestingly, the asynchronous semantics of CGV is reminiscent of
future
/
promise
programming paradigms (see, e.g.,
[
12

22
,
26
]
), which have been little studied in the context of session-typed communication.

The alternative to establishing deadlock-freedom in CGV via translation into APCP would be to enhance CGV’s type system with priorities (in the spirit of, e.g., work by Padovani and Novara
[
24
]
).
Another useful addition concerns recursion / recursive types.
We leave these extensions to future work.
