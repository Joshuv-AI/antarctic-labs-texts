Abstract
. In this paper we extend the interior regularity results for stable solutions in [Cabré, Figalli, Ros-Oton, and Serra, Acta Math. 224 (2020)] to operators with variable coefficients. We show that stable solutions to the semilinear elliptic equation a i ​ j ​ ( x ) ​ u i ​ j + b i ​ ( x ) ​ u i + f ⁡ ( u ) = 0 a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}+b_{i}(x)u_{i}+f(u)=0 are Hölder continuous in the optimal range of dimensions n ≤ 9 n\leq 9 . Our bounds are independent of the nonlinearity f ∈ C 1 f\in C^{1} , which we assume to be non-negative. The main achievement of our work is to make the constants in our estimates depend on the C 1 C^{1} norm of a i ​ j a_{ij} and the C 0 C^{0} norm of b i b_{i} , instead of their C 2 C^{2} and C 1 C^{1} norms, respectively, which arise in a first approach to the computations.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain and
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a
C^{1}
function.
We consider stable solutions

u\colon\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}

to the semilinear equation

(1.1)

-Lu=f(u)\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

where
L
is a
second order linear elliptic differential operator
of the form

(1.2)

Lu=a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}+b_{i}(x)u_{i},\quad a_{ij}(x)=a_{ji}(x).

The purpose of this paper is to
extend the
recent results
of Cabré, Figalli, Ros-Oton, and Serra
in
[
2
]
and of Cabré in
[
5
]

for the Laplacian
to the above operators with variable coefficients.
In
[
2
]
,
the authors solved a
long-standing conjecture concerning the regularity of stable solutions to semilinear problems.
They showed that stable solutions are bounded (and hence smooth)
in dimension
n\leq 9
.
This result
is optimal, since there are examples of singular stable solutions for
n\geq 10
.

In the papers
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
,
the authors obtain universal a priori estimates that do not depend on the nonlinearity
f
.
They prove interior regularity bounds, assuming
f\geq 0
,
and boundary regularity estimates on
C^{3}
domains, assuming
f\geq 0
,
f^{\prime}\geq 0
, and
f^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
.
The boundary result applies only to solutions vanishing on the boundary.

The first main interest in extending these results to operators with variable coefficients
(besides possible future applications to nonlinear problems)
is to simplify the boundary regularity arguments, even for the Laplacian.
Indeed, starting from a curved boundary, the proof of regularity in
[
2
]

requires a delicate blow-up and Liouville theorem argument,
which is needed in order to apply a result of theirs only available on a flat boundary.
In addition, this proof is by contradiction-compactness and does not allow to quantify the constants in the estimates.
Recently in
[
5
]
, Cabré has given a quantitative proof of
this result for the Laplacian in the case of a flat boundary.
On the other hand, a curved boundary can be flattened out by a change of variables.
Note now that, in the new coordinates, the Laplacian is written as an operator of the form (
1.2
).
It is therefore natural to establish quantitative a priori estimates for our family of equations in the half-ball.
By extending the techniques in
[
5
]
to operators with variable coefficients,
we will avoid the
intricate blow-up and Liouville theorem result form
[
2
]

as well as the compactness part.

An important feature of our estimates is that they depend only on the ellipticity constants and on the norms
\|a_{ij}\|_{C^{1}}
and
\|b_{i}\|_{C^{0}}
of the coefficients.
The main difficulty in our proofs will be to
obtain this dependence instead of on
\|a_{ij}\|_{C^{2}}
and
\|b_{i}\|_{C^{1}}
,
which are the norms that appear naturally
in a first approach to the computations.
This will be especially relevant for boundary regularity,
since it will allow us to relax the
C^{3}
regularity requirement of the domain in
[
2
]
.
The key point here is that,
as mentioned before,
flattening the boundary
transforms the Laplacian into an operator of the form (
1.2
),
with coefficients

a_{ij}
and
b_{i}

involving first and second derivatives of the boundary surface, respectively.
Thus, a
C^{1}
,
C^{0}
bound of the coefficients
a_{ij}
,
b_{i}
would correspond to a
C^{2}
bound of the boundary.
The actual regularity of the boundary that is needed is under current investigation.

Moreover, our methods will also be useful in a future work
where we treat the case
of the Laplacian
with non-homogeneous boundary conditions.
Recall that the previous papers
[
2
]
and
[
5
]

require strongly that the solutions vanish on the boundary.
Flattening the boundary, we will be able to reduce the
problem to
an equation on the half-space for an operator of the form (
1.2
)
with zero boundary conditions and an additional source term.

The study of the regularity of stable solutions
was initiated
in the seventies
by Crandall and Rabinowitz in
[
6
]
.
There,
they showed the boundedness of stable solutions
when
n\leq 9
for exponential and power-type nonlinearities.
Their work was motivated by problems in combustion
[
9
]
, commonly known as “Gelfand-type problems”;
for more information on these problems, we refer the reader to the monograph of Dupaigne
[
7
]
.
Later, in the mid-nineties, Brezis
[
1
]

asked for an extension of this regularity result to a larger class of nonlinearities.
The boundedness of stable solutions
was proven by Nedev
[
12
]
for
n\leq 3
,
and by Cabré
[
3
]
for
n=4
.
The optimal dimension

n\leq 9

remained open
until
it was finally reached by
Cabré, Figalli, Ros-Oton, and Serra
in
[
2
]
.

1.1.
The setting. Stability.

We are interested in the class of stable solutions to the semilinear equation (
1.1
).
We say that
u
is a
stable
solution of (
1.1
) if there exists a function

\varphi\in C^{2}(\Omega)\cap C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})

such that

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlll}-L\varphi&\geq&f^{\prime}(u)\varphi&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\varphi&>&0&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\varphi&=&0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Equivalently, a solution is stable when
the principal Dirichlet eigenvalue of the linearized equation is nonnegative.
We denote the linearized equation at
u
, the
Jacobi operator
, by

(1.4)

J_{u}\varphi=L\varphi+f^{\prime}(u)\varphi.

Throughout the paper we assume the coefficients of
L
to be smooth.
Thus, our operator (
1.2
) can be written in divergence form as

(1.5)

\mathcal{L}u=\partial_{i}\left(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u\right)+d_{i}(x)\partial_{i}u,

for certain appropriate coefficients
d_{i}
.
Now, recall that our bounds for non-divergence operators depend on the norms
\|a_{ij}\|_{C^{1}}
and
\|b_{i}\|_{C^{0}}
of the coefficients.
As a consequence, our results continue to hold for every divergence-form operator
\mathcal{L}
as in (
1.5
),
with constants depending on
\|a_{ij}\|_{C^{1}}
and
\|d_{i}\|_{C^{0}}
instead.

We assume that the symmetric coefficient matrix
A(x)=\left(a_{ij}(x)\right)
is
uniformly elliptic
in
\Omega
, i.e.,
there are
constants
\lambda
and
\Lambda
such that

(1.6)

0<\lambda\leq a_{ij}(x)p_{i}p_{j}\leq\Lambda\quad\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\text{ and }p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ such that }|p|=1.

This condition will be written as
\lambda\leq A(x)\leq\Lambda
.
In particular, the matrix
A(x)
is positive definite and defines a norm

(1.7)

|p|_{A(x)}:=\left(a_{ij}(x)p_{i}p_{j}\right)^{1/2}

on vectors
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For variational equations
-\partial_{i}\left(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}u\right)=f(u)

stability is equivalent to the nonnegativity of the second variation of the associated energy functional.
This provides the useful integral inequality

(1.8)

\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi|_{A(x)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

satisfied by all test functions
\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
.
A key
strategy to derive a priori estimates
in that setting
is to choose appropriate test functions in (
1.8
).
When chosen correctly in terms of the Jacobi operator,
the test functions allow to get rid of the nonlinearity within the proofs.
This is what is done for the Laplacian in
[
2
]
.

Since our operator
L
does not have variational structure, (
1.8
) is not available.
Nevertheless, we are able to exploit the pointwise stability condition (
1.3
)
for
\varphi
to obtain a convenient
integral inequality which does not involve the function
\varphi
.
We will use it as replacement of (
1.8
) in our non-variational setting.
To derive the integral inequality,
we first write the operator
L
in divergence form as in (
1.5
) with
d_{i}(x)=\widehat{b}_{i}(x)
,
where
\widehat{b}
is the vector field given by

(1.9)

\widehat{b}_{i}(x)=b_{i}(x)-\partial_{k}a_{ki}(x),

hence
Lu=\partial_{i}\left(a_{ij}(x)u_{j}\right)+\widehat{b}_{i}(x)u_{i}
.
Now, for a test function
\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
,
multiply (
1.3
) by
\xi^{2}/\varphi
and integrate by parts
to obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(A(x)\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\xi^{2}}{\varphi}\right)-\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\frac{\xi^{2}}{\varphi}\nabla\varphi\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(-|\xi\nabla\log{\varphi}|_{A(x)}^{2}+2A(x)\xi\,\nabla\log{\varphi}\cdot\nabla\xi-\xi\,\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\xi\,\nabla\log{\varphi}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Using that

2A(x)\xi\,\nabla\log{\varphi}\cdot\left(\nabla\xi-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\xi A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right)-|\xi\nabla\log{\varphi}|_{A(x)}^{2}\leq\left|\nabla\xi-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\xi A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)},

we deduce

(1.10)

\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla\xi-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\xi A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega).

We remark that, in general, (
1.10
) is not equivalent to the stability condition (
1.3
).
The main reason is that our equation
-Lu=f(u)
is not variational due to the presence of
\widehat{b}
when written in
the divergence form (
1.5
).
In Appendix
A
,
we give an example of the non-equivalence,
and, at the same time, we characterize the drifts
\widehat{b}
for which the equivalence holds.

1.2.
Main results

This paper concerns the interior regularity of stable solutions.
Boundary regularity results will be treated in a forthcoming work.
Therefore, it suffices to consider stable solutions
to
-Lu=f(u)
in the unit ball
B_{1}
.
A constant depending only on
n
,
\lambda
, and
\Lambda

will be called
universal
,
a terminology that we use throughout the paper.

The following is our main result,
which provides interior a priori estimates for stable solutions:
a Hölder bound when
n\leq 9
,
and a
W^{1,2+\gamma}
estimate in every dimension.
The only requirement for the nonlinearity is
f\geq 0
, as in
[
2
]
.
An important accomplishment in our estimates is that they involve the norms

\|A\|_{C^{1}}
and
\|b\|_{C^{0}}
,
while a first approach to the problem leads to computations including
second derivatives of
A
and first derivatives of
b
.
On the other hand, our bounds are independent of
f
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})
be a stable solution of
-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
for some nonnegative function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Then

(1.11)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2+\gamma}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
\gamma=\gamma(n)>0

and
C=C(n,\lambda,\|A\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B}_{1})},\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})})
.
In addition,

(1.12)

\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{B}_{1/2})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}\quad\text{ if }n\leq 9,

where

\alpha=\alpha(n,\lambda,\Lambda)>0

and
C=C(n,\lambda,\|A\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B}_{1})},\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})})
.

For applications, it may be useful to point out that
the result only needs
A
to be Lipschitz and
b
to be bounded.
Our direct computations within the proofs assume

A\in C^{1}
and
b\in C^{1}

in order to evaluate
certain identities pointwise.
However, we only need these to be meaningful in a weaker sense;
see Remarks
3.1
and
5.2
.
Similarly,
we only need

u
to be
C^{2}
and to have weak third derivatives.
These last conditions seem to require more regularity of the drift
b
; see Remark
3.1
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
will rely
on our second main result,
Theorem
1.2
below, and its consequences.
It consists of
two types of Hessian estimates.
The first one, (
1.13
),
is an extension of
the geometric stability condition due to Sternberg and Zumbrun
[
16
]

to operators with variable coefficients.
The second one, (
1.15
)-(
1.16
),
controls the
L^{1}
norm of the “Hessian times the gradient”,
|D^{2}u||\nabla u|
,
in balls and annuli
by the
L^{2}
norm squared of the gradient
whenever the lower order coefficients are small.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}\big(\overline{B}_{1}\big)
be a stable solution of

-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}
,
for some function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then

(1.13)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}\mathcal{A}^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}\left(|\nabla\eta|^{2}_{A(x)}+C\varepsilon|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+C\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

for all
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
,
where
C
is a universal constant
and

(1.14)

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big({\rm tr}(A(x)D^{2}uA(0)D^{2}u)-|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{-2}|D^{2}uA(0)\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}\Big)^{1/2}&\text{ if }\nabla u\neq 0\\
0&\text{ if }\nabla u=0.\end{array}\right.

Assume in addition that
f\geq 0
.
Then there is a universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
with the following property:
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
then

(1.15)

\|\,|\nabla u|\,D^{2}u\,\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})}\leq C\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{1})}

and

(1.16)

\|\,|\nabla u|\,D^{2}u\,\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4})}\leq C\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})},

where
C
is a universal constant.

Our first inequality (
1.13
)
generalizes the Sternberg-Zumbrun estimate for the Laplacian,
which corresponds to the case
\varepsilon=0
and
A(0)=I
.
The peculiar form of the function
\mathcal{A}
in (
1.14
)
(the coefficients of
A
are evaluated both at
x
and
0
)
will guarantee that the direct computations within our proofs give dependence on the norm
\|A\|_{C^{1}}
, instead of
\|A\|_{C^{2}}

for other choices of
\mathcal{A}
.
In this direction,
it is worth noting that
the classical Sternberg-Zumbrun result and the function
\mathcal{A}

have a Riemannian analogue
(found by Farina, Sire, and Valdinoci
[
8
]
)
which can be related
to our Euclidean setting with variable coefficients.
The estimate from the Riemannian framework leads to bounds depending
on
\|A\|_{C^{2}}
.
We elaborate on these topics further in Remarks
3.2
and
3.3
.

The “Hessian times the gradient” estimates (
1.15
)-(
1.16
)
rely on the inequality (
1.13
)
with sufficiently small errors
\varepsilon
,
and will require the assumption
f\geq 0
.
While the bound on annuli (
1.16
) can be deduced from the one in balls (
1.15
)
by a standard scaling and covering argument,
we include it in the statement since it will be crucial in the proof of the Hölder estimate in Theorem
1.1
.

1.3.
Structure of the proof

By a scaling and covering argument, it suffices to obtain the a priori estimates from Theorem
1.1
in small balls.
There, the problem can be written as an equation in the unit ball
involving an operator
L
that is close to the Laplacian, i.e.,
whose coefficients satisfy
A(0)=I
and
\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon
, with
\varepsilon
small.
We explain this in more detail in Section
2
below.

The key estimates leading to Theorem
1.1

are contained in
Propositions
1.3
,
1.4
, and
1.5
below.
Our proofs are all quantitative as in the paper
[
5
]

and avoid the compactness argument from the previous work
[
2
]
.
The proofs of the first two propositions
use the Hessian estimates of Theorem
1.2
above.
In particular, this forces us to prove the Sternberg-Zumbrun inequality
before the crucial weighted
L^{2}
estimate for the radial derivative (Proposition
1.4
).
It is worth noting that,
for the Laplacian, these two results are independent from each other
(and hence can be obtained in any order, as in the works
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
),
while this is no longer the case for operators with variable coefficients.

In the first proposition,
we control the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient by the
L^{1}
norm of the solution
when the error
\varepsilon
of the coefficients is sufficiently small.
This is a direct consequence of Theorem
1.2

and the interpolation inequalities of Cabré in
[
5
]
.
We recall these inequalities in Appendix
C
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}\big(\overline{B}_{1}\big)
be a stable solution of

-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}
,
for some nonnegative function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some

\varepsilon>0
.

Then there exists a universal
\varepsilon_{0}>0

with the following property:
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then

(1.17)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
C
is a universal constant.

The second proposition
is a weighted
L^{2}
estimate for the radial derivative in a ball
by
the full gradient in an annulus.
It is here that we need
n\leq 9
.
Again, we will assume that the coefficient error
\varepsilon
is small
and that the nonlinearity is nonnegative
f\geq 0
.
Here and throughout the paper we use the notation

r=|x|,\quad u_{r}=\dfrac{x}{|x|}\cdot\nabla u

for the radial derivative.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}\big(\overline{B}_{1}\big)

be a stable solution of

-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}
,
for some nonnegative
function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then there is a universal
\varepsilon_{0}>0
with the following property:
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
3\leq n\leq 9
, then

(1.18)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}\setminus B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

for all
\rho\leq 1/4
, where
C
is a universal constant.

Notice that this result requires
n\geq 3
.
However, adding superfluous variables to the solution,
we can also use it when
n\leq 2
.

Our inequality (
1.18
) in Proposition
1.4

is an analogue of Lemma 2.1 in
[
2
]
, where the authors obtain
a similar
bound for the Laplacian (
\varepsilon=0
and
A(0)=I
)
without the nonnegativity assumption on
f
.
Recall that this assumption is needed in the Hessian estimates (
1.15
)-(
1.16
) in Theorem
1.2
above,
which will allow us to treat a weighted
|D^{2}u||\nabla u|
error term
which does not appear for the Laplacian.
We will be able to control this error by writing it as an infinite sum on dyadic annuli,
pulling the weight out of the integral in each annulus,
and applying the bound (
1.16
).

Finally in the third proposition
we show that,
under the assumption that
A(0)=I
,
(generalized) superharmonic functions
are controlled by the radial derivative
plus an error involving the full gradient in
L^{1}
.
This is an extension of Lemma 4.1 in Cabré
[
5
]

to operators with variable coefficients.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})
be
superharmonic in the sense that

Lu\leq 0
in
B_{1}
.
Assume that

A(0)=I\quad\text{ and }\quad\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then
there exists a constant
t
, which depends on
u
,
such that

\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}\leq C\|u_{r}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
C
is a constant depending only on
n
and
\lambda
.

Our proof of Proposition
1.5

is by comparison with harmonic functions.
The required
L^{1}
estimates for harmonic functions
will follow by duality from the
L^{\infty}
bounds of a Neumann problem.
Such
L^{\infty}
estimates are the most technical part of the argument,
where we use a Moser iteration in the spirit of Winkert
[
17
]
to deduce the uniform bounds.

The
W^{1,2+\gamma}

result (
1.11
) in Theorem
1.1

will follow from the Hessian estimate on balls (
1.15
) in Theorem
1.2

together with Proposition
1.3
.
To show it,
we first control the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient uniformly on level sets by
the Dirichlet integral in a ball, and hence by the
L^{1}
norm of the solution.
A device from
[
2
]
will then allow us to deduce the higher integrability.

To prove the Hölder estimate (
1.12
) in Theorem
1.1
,
we will show that the scale-invariant weighted integral
\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}
decays algebraically.
In the previous works
[
2
]
and
[
5
]
,
the authors proved the decay of the weighted radial derivative instead.
They could later deduce the
C^{\alpha}
estimate
by either averaging or applying a version of Morrey’s embedding for radial derivatives.
Here we will obtain the decay of the full gradient directly for the first time.
For this,
combining Propositions
1.3
and
1.5
,
we are able to bound the full gradient by the radial derivative on annuli in
L^{2}
.
This, together with the dyadic decomposition explained above, allows us to control the weighted integral of the gradient by that of the radial derivative (up to gradient errors).
Now, Proposition
1.4

will
yield a control of the weighted integral of the gradient in the ball by the same quantity on an annulus.
Finally, a standard iteration will lead to decay.

Our integral stability inequality (
1.10
)
will be crucial in the proofs of both Theorem
1.2
and Proposition
1.4
.
These will follow from (
1.10
), with
\Omega=B_{1}
,
by choosing appropriate test functions in terms of the Jacobi operator,
as we explain next.
Taking a test function of the form
\xi=\textrm{\bf c}\eta
, where
c
and
\eta
are smooth and
\,{\rm supp}\,\eta\subset B_{1}
,
the integrand on the right-hand side of (
1.10
) becomes

(1.19)

\begin{split}&\left|\nabla\xi-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\xi A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}=\left|\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}+\textrm{\bf c}\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\textrm{\bf c}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\\
&\quad=\left|\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}\right|_{A(x)}^{2}+2A(x)\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}\cdot\textrm{\bf c}\nabla\eta-\eta^{2}\textrm{\bf c}\,\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla\textrm{\bf c}+\textrm{\bf c}^{2}\left|\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}.\end{split}

Integrating in
B_{1}
, the first term
\int_{B_{1}}|\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
in (
1.19
) can be integrated by parts as

\int_{B_{1}}\left|\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}\right|_{A(x)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{1}}\Big(-{\rm div}\left(A(x)\nabla\textrm{\bf c}\right)\,\textrm{\bf c}\eta^{2}-2A(x)\eta\nabla\textrm{\bf c}\cdot\textrm{\bf c}\nabla\eta\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

and hence,
by (
1.19
), (
1.10
), and rearranging terms,
it follows that

(1.20)

\int_{B_{1}}(J_{u}\textrm{\bf c})\,\textrm{\bf c}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B_{1}}\textrm{\bf c}^{2}\left|\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The key idea now is to choose the
c
function
in a way such that

J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}
becomes independent of the nonlinearity.
This will yield universal a priori estimates for stable solutions.
Since
u
solves the equation
-Lu=f(u)
,
taking a derivative, we have that

f^{\prime}(u)\nabla u=-\nabla Lu

and hence

J_{u}\nabla u=L\nabla u-\nabla Lu

no longer involves
f
.
This computation suggests that we choose

as
a function of the gradient of

.
Thus,
to prove the estimate for
\mathcal{A}
in Theorem
1.2
, we will make the choice

\textrm{\bf c}(x)=|\nabla u(x)|_{A(0)}=\big(a_{ij}(0)u_{i}u_{j}\big)^{1/2}.

On the other hand, the weighted
L^{2}
bound
in Proposition
1.4

will also require to choose the auxiliary function
\eta
above carefully.
The test functions leading to this estimate are

\textrm{\bf c}(x)=x\cdot\nabla u=ru_{r}\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta(x)=|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\zeta,

where
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
is a cut-off.

We note that
our
test functions
above
are the
ones used in the paper
[
2
]

under the linear transformation
x\mapsto A^{1/2}(0)x
,
where
A^{1/2}(0)
is the positive square root of the matrix
A(0)
.
These seem to be
the simplest functions
leading to a priori estimates
in the variable coefficients framework.
Moreover,
thanks to the particular form of these functions,
all direct computations within our proofs will only involve first derivatives of the coefficients
A
and
b

while other choices of functions require two derivatives of

A suitable integration by parts will yield bounds in terms of the norms

and

2.
Preliminaries: Invariance under affine transformations

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we will analyze the semilinear equation
-Lu=f(u)

in small balls.
Since the class of stable solutions is invariant under affine transformations,
the question reduces to studying an equation in the unit ball involving
an operator that is close to the Laplacian.
After proving
the necessary estimates in this setting,
the theorem will follow from a scaling and covering argument.
It is worth mentioning that the nonnegativity of the nonlinearity,
which is required in our main results,
is preserved under these transformations.

We now explain this invariance with more detail in different particular situations.
First we study the equation under translations and scalings.
These simple yet important transformations will be used several times throughout the paper.
Secondly,
we consider the equation under general linear transformations.
These allow us to reduce ourselves to the case where the coefficient matrix is the identity at the origin.
Notice that this is only required in
Proposition
1.5
, but will be crucial in the proof of the
C^{\alpha}
bound (
1.12
) from Theorem
1.1
given in Section
7
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the bounds in our a priori estimates depend only on the ellipticity constants
\lambda
and
\Lambda

and on the quantity

(2.1)

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}

concerning the coefficients.
As we will see now, the two norms
in (
2.1
)
have the same scaling.
It is therefore natural to state our results in terms of this quantity.

(i)

Translation and scale invariance
.
If
u
is a stable solution of
-Lu=f(u)
in a ball
B_{\rho}(y)
,
then the function
u^{y,\rho}:=u(y+\rho\,\cdot)

is a solution of

-L^{y,\rho}u^{y,\rho}=\rho^{2}f(u^{y,\rho})
in
B_{1}
,
where
L^{y,\rho}
is the operator

L^{y,\rho}v={\rm tr}\left(A^{y,\rho}(x)D^{2}v\right)+b^{y,\rho}(x)\cdot\nabla v,

with coefficients

A^{y,\rho}(x)=A(y+\rho\,x)\quad\text{ and }\quad b^{y,\rho}(x)=\rho\,b(y+\rho\,x).

The stability condition (
1.3
) in
B_{\rho}(y)

becomes
-L^{y,\rho}\varphi^{y,\rho}\leq\rho^{2}f^{\prime}(u^{y,\rho})\varphi^{y,\rho}
in
B_{1}
,
where
\varphi^{y,\rho}=\varphi(y+\rho\,\cdot)

satisfies the assumptions in (
1.3
),
and hence
u^{y,\rho}
is stable.

Since the coefficients satisfy the bounds

\|DA^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\rho\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{\rho}(y))}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|b^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\rho\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{\rho}(y))},

whenever
B_{\rho}(y)\subset B_{R}
for some
R>0

and
L
is defined in this larger ball, we have

(2.2)

\|DA^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\rho\left(\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{R})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{R})}\right),

which can be made small for
\rho
small.
In particular,
assuming that
L
is close to the Laplacian as in the statements of our propositions and
by (
2.2
), we deduce the following property:
if
\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{R})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{R})}\leq\varepsilon
, then

\|DA^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\rho\varepsilon\quad\text{ for all }B_{\rho}(y)\subset B_{R}.

This elementary observation will be used throughout the paper.

(ii)

Invariance under linear transformations
.
Given a symmetric positive definite matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
if
u
is a stable solution of
-Lu=f(u)
in the unit ball
B_{1}
,
then the function

u^{M}:=u(M\,\cdot)

is a solution of

-L^{M}u^{M}=f(u^{M})
in
M^{-1}(B_{1})
,
where
L^{M}
is the operator

L^{M}v={\rm tr}\big(A^{M}(x)D^{2}v\big)+b^{M}(x)\cdot\nabla v,

with coefficients

A^{M}(x)=M^{-1}A(Mx)M^{-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad b^{M}(x)=M^{-1}b(Mx).

As above, the stability condition (
1.3
) in
B_{1}
becomes
-L^{M}\varphi^{M}\leq f^{\prime}(u^{M})\varphi^{M}
in
M^{-1}(B_{1})
, where
\varphi^{M}=\varphi(M\cdot)
,
and hence
u^{M}
is a stable solution.

If
M
satisfies
\sqrt{\lambda}\leq M\leq\sqrt{\Lambda}
, then the new coefficients
A^{M}
are uniformly elliptic with

\lambda/\Lambda\leq A^{M}(x)\leq\Lambda/\lambda
.
Moreover, we have the bounds

|DA^{M}(x)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}}{\lambda}|DA(Mx)|\quad\text{ and }\quad|b^{M}(x)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}|b(Mx)|,

and taking the supremum in
x\in M^{-1}(B_{1})
,
using that

B_{1/\sqrt{\Lambda}}\subset M^{-1}(B_{1})
, we deduce

(2.3)

\begin{split}\|DA^{M}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1/\sqrt{\Lambda}})}+\|b^{M}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1/\sqrt{\Lambda}})}&\leq\frac{\sqrt{\Lambda}}{\lambda}\left(\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\right).\end{split}

In particular,
taking
M=A^{1/2}(0)
as the unique positive square root of
A(0)
,
we see that
u^{A^{1/2}(0)}
solves an elliptic equation in the ball
B_{1/\sqrt{\Lambda}}

with coefficients
(A^{A^{1/2}(0)})(x)
satisfying
(A^{A^{1/2}(0)})(0)=I
, i.e.,
equal to the identity at the origin.
By the monotonicity of the principal eigenvalue with respect to the domain,
it follows that
u^{A^{1/2}(0)}
is also a stable solution in this ball.

It is now easy to combine
these transformations with the ones given in the first part
(i)
.
For each ball
B_{\rho}(y)\subset B_{1}
,
the function

\widetilde{u}=u\big(y+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}A^{1/2}(y)\,\cdot\big)

is a stable solution of an elliptic equation

-\widetilde{L}\widetilde{u}=\widetilde{f}(\widetilde{u})

in
B_{1}
.
Here,
\widetilde{f}
is the nonlinearity

\widetilde{f}=\frac{\rho^{2}}{\Lambda}f
,
while

\widetilde{L}
is an operator of the form (
1.2
) with coefficients

\textstyle\widetilde{A}(x)=A^{-1/2}(y)A\big(y+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}A^{1/2}(y)x\big)A^{-1/2}(y)

and

\textstyle\widetilde{b}(x)=\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}A^{-1/2}(y)b\big(y+\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\Lambda}}A^{1/2}(y)x\big).

Notice that the matrix
\widetilde{A}(x)

is uniformly elliptic with
\lambda/\Lambda\leq\widetilde{A}(x)\leq\Lambda/\lambda

and is equal to the identity at the origin.
Furthermore,
combining (
2.3
) and (
2.2
),
the coefficients can be bounded by

\|D\widetilde{A}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|\widetilde{b}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\left(\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\right).

As mentioned above,
this observation will be important in the proof of the Hölder estimate
1.12

in Section
7
below.

3.
Hessian and

estimates

The goal of this section is to prove
Theorem
1.2
and the energy estimate Proposition

.

Recall the function
\mathcal{A}\colon\overline{B}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}
introduced in (
1.14
) in the statement of Theorem
1.2
.
This function can also be written as

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(\|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\|_{\rm HS}^{2}-|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\textrm{\bf n}(x)|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}&\text{if }\nabla u\neq 0\\
0&\text{if }\nabla u=0,\end{array}\right.

where
\|\cdot\|_{\rm HS}
denotes the Euclidean Hilbert-Schmidt norm for matrices
1
1

1

Recall that, for a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, this norm squared is
\|M\|^{2}_{\rm HS}={\rm tr}(M^{T}M)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}M_{ij}^{2}
.

and
\textrm{\bf n}(x)
is the unit vector field

\textrm{\bf n}\colon\overline{B}_{1}\cap\{\nabla u\neq 0\}\to\mathbb{R}

given by

(3.2)

\textrm{\bf n}(x):=|\nabla u|^{-1}_{A(0)}A^{1/2}(0)\nabla u(x).

The equivalence between the expressions (
1.14

3.1
) follows from the identities

\|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\|_{\rm HS}^{2}={\rm tr}\left(A(x)D^{2}uA(0)D^{2}u\right)

|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\textrm{\bf n}(x)|^{2}=|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{-2}|D^{2}uA(0)\nabla u|_{A(x)}^{2},

which are easy to check.

We start by proving the bound (
1.13
) in Theorem
1.2
,
which is a generalization of
the geometric stability inequality due to Sternberg and Zumbrun
[
16
]

for stable solutions to
-\Delta u=f(u)
.
For this, we will test the integral stability inequality (
1.20
) with the function

\textrm{\bf c}(x)=|\nabla u|_{A(0)}

and a cut-off
\eta
.
The proof of the remaining estimates in
Theorem
1.2

will rely on this preliminary inequality.

Two comments are in order.
First, with this choice of
c
,
our result requires an appropriate integration by parts to allow dependence of the bounds on only
\|b\|_{C^{0}}
.
Secondly,
after the proof,
in Remarks
3.2
and
3.3
we will comment on alternative choices of
c
and of the function
\mathcal{A}
.

We originally took
|\nabla u|_{A(x)}
as our

a choice that required the regularity

and
b\in C^{1}

when computing
J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}
in the stability inequality (

).
With that choice,
a further integration by parts was needed to obtain bounds depending only on
\|A\|_{C^{1}}
.
Instead,
our function
|\nabla u|_{A(0)}
only needs
A\in C^{1}
and
b\in C^{1}
.
Moreover,
the proof with our choice
is easier and we only need an integration by parts to get rid of the first derivatives of
b
.
Note that
the function
|\nabla u|_{A(x)}
is motivated by geometric considerations
and had already appeared in the Riemannian analogue of the Sternberg-Zumbrun estimates, as explained in Remark
3.3
.

Proof of (
1.13
) in Theorem
1.2
.

Since
|\nabla u|_{A(0)}
is not necessarily smooth when
\nabla u=0
,
we consider the smooth function

\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}:=\sqrt{|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}+\delta^{2}}

instead.
We will apply the stability inequality (
1.20
) with
\textrm{\bf c}=\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}
.
In the end we will let
\delta\to 0
, which will yield the claim.
Throughout this proof, the letter
C
denotes a generic universal constant.

By the stability inequality
(
1.20
), we have the upper bound

(3.3)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)\left|\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)\left(|\nabla\eta|^{2}_{A(x)}+C\varepsilon|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+C\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where in the last line we have expanded the quadratic expression and applied the bounds of the coefficients.

We will bound the
expression

\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}=\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,L\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}+f^{\prime}(u)\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{2}

from below.
Since

\begin{split}\partial_{i}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}&=\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{-1}\,u_{ik}a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\partial^{2}_{ij}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}&=\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{-1}\,u_{ijk}a_{kl}(0)u_{l}+\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{-1}u_{ik}a_{kl}(0)u_{jl}-\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{-3}\,u_{ik}a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,u_{jp}a_{pq}(0)u_{q},\end{split}

we deduce

(3.4)

\begin{split}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,a_{ij}(x)\partial^{2}_{ij}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}&=a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{ijk}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\|^{2}_{\rm HS}-\textstyle\frac{|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(0)}}{|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(0)}+\delta^{2}}|A^{1/2}(x)D^{2}uA^{1/2}(0)\textrm{\bf n}|^{2}\\
&\geq a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{ijk}+\mathcal{A}^{2}\end{split}

and

(3.5)

\begin{split}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,b_{i}(x)\partial_{i}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}=a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,b_{i}(x)u_{ik}.\end{split}

Adding (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we obtain

(3.6)

\begin{split}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}&\geq a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,Lu_{k}+\mathcal{A}^{2}+f^{\prime}(u)\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{2}.\end{split}

Differentiating the equation
-Lu=f(u)

in the direction of
A(0)\nabla u
,
we have

(3.7)

A(0)\nabla(Lu)\cdot\nabla u=-f^{\prime}(u)|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}.

The first term on the right-hand side of (
3.6
) can be written in terms of this derivative as

a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,Lu_{k}=A(0)\nabla(Lu)\cdot\nabla u-a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}-a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}b_{i}(x)u_{i},

hence, by (
3.7
) and
the coefficient estimates,
we can bound this expression from below as

(3.8)

a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\,Lu_{k}\geq-f^{\prime}(u)|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}-C\varepsilon|D^{2}u||\nabla u|-a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}b_{i}(x)u_{i}.

Applying (
3.8
) in (
3.6
),
since
\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}^{2}-|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}=\delta^{2}
, we obtain

(3.9)

\begin{split}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}\,J_{u}\textrm{\bf c}_{\delta}&\geq\mathcal{A}^{2}+\delta^{2}f^{\prime}(u)-C\varepsilon|D^{2}u||\nabla u|-a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}b_{i}(x)u_{i}.\end{split}

Multiplying (
3.9
) by
\eta^{2}
and integrating,
the last term
-\int_{B_{1}}a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}b_{i}(x)u_{i}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

can be integrated by parts as

(3.10)

\begin{split}\left|-\int_{B_{1}}a_{kl}(0)u_{l}\partial_{k}b_{i}(x)u_{i}\,\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|&=\left|\int_{B_{1}}b_{i}(x)\partial_{k}(a_{kl}(0)u_{l}u_{i}\,\eta^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
&\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla(\eta^{2})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Combining (
3.9
), (
3.10
), and (
3.3
), rearranging terms, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1}}\left(\mathcal{A}^{2}+\delta f^{\prime}(u)\right)\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\leq\int_{B_{1}}\left(|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)\left(|\nabla\eta|^{2}_{A(x)}+C\varepsilon|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+C\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

and letting

Remark 3.1
.

In (

to get rid of the dependence on the nonlinearity.
Instead, we could have multiplied the equation

In the argument above, we need

In

This fact allows them to carry out a similar argument to the one explained above.
2
2

2

In fact, they are able to deduce the estimate without computing

directly, but they still need to have three derivatives of the solution; see the proof of Lemma 2.1 in

]
.

For an operator with variable coefficients

,
the regularity of the solution depends on that of the coefficients.
Assuming

,

,
and

Now, for

To see this, taking a derivative of the equation we have

and the right hand side is in

.
This is somewhat surprising,

.

.

Remark 3.3
.

Our result (
1.13
) is related to a Riemannian analogue of the Sternberg and Zumbrun estimate
found by Farina, Sire, and Valdinoci in
[
8
]
.
It states that stable solutions to the equation
-\Delta_{\rm LB}u=f(u)
in a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
,
where
\Delta_{\rm LB}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator, satisfy the inequality

(3.12)

\int_{M}\mathcal{A}_{\rm Riem}^{2}\,\eta^{2}+\int_{M}{\rm Ric}(\rm\nabla_{g}u,\rm\nabla_{g}u)\,\eta^{2}\leq\int_{M}|\rm\nabla_{g}u|_{g}^{2}|\rm\nabla_{g}\eta|_{g}^{2}.

Here,

\mathcal{A}_{\rm Riem}
(given by (
3.13
)) is a Riemannian analogue of the function
\mathcal{A}
in Theorem
1.2
,

{\rm Ric}
denotes the Ricci tensor,
and all the norms, gradients, and integrals are intrinsic to the metric
g
.

When expressed in coordinates, these Riemannian quantities
fit within our Euclidean setting with variable coefficients.
For instance, the operator
\Delta_{\rm LB}
can be written in coordinates as

Lu={\rm div}\left(A(x)\nabla u\right)+\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u
.
Here
A(x)=\left(a_{ij}(x)\right)=\left(g^{ij}(x)\right)
is the inverse of the metric and
\widehat{b}_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{2}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{j}\log|g|

involves the volume density
|g|=\det(g_{ij}(x))^{1/2}
.
Moreover, with our notation for matrices, the function
\mathcal{A}_{\rm Riem}
in (
3.12
) can be written locally in
\{\rm\nabla_{g}u\neq 0\}
as

(3.13)

\mathcal{A}_{\rm Riem}=\Big(\|A^{1/2}(x)HuA^{1/2}(x)\|_{\rm HS}^{2}-\big|A^{1/2}(x)HuA^{1/2}(x)\textrm{\bf n}_{A}(x)\big|^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

where

\textrm{\bf n}_{A}(x)=|\nabla u|_{A^{(}x)}^{-1}A^{1/2}(x)\nabla u

has appeared in the definition (
3.11
) of
\mathcal{A}_{x}
in Remark
3.2

and

Hu=\left((Hu)_{ij}\right)

is the Riemannian Hessian matrix given by
(Hu)_{ij}=u_{ij}-\Gamma_{ij}^{k}u_{k}
, where
\Gamma_{ij}^{k}
are the Christoffel symbols of the metric.

By this identification of
\Delta_{\rm LB}
with
L
, applying the Riemannian result in
[
8
]
,
collecting all lower order terms,
and estimating the derivatives of the metric,
we are led to an a priori bound
for the function
\mathcal{A}_{x}
in (
3.11
)
which involves
errors of the same type as in (
1.13
).
Due to the presence of the Ricci tensor in (
3.12
),
this estimate derived from the Riemannian inequality (
3.12
)
depends on the norm
\|A\|_{C^{2}(\overline{B}_{1})}
, i.e.,
it requires two derivatives of the metric.
Nevertheless, integrating the unwanted coefficient derivatives by parts as we did in our proof of (
1.13
),
we could deduce an estimate depending only on
\|A\|_{C^{1}(\overline{B}_{1})}
.

The authors in
[
8
]
obtain (
3.12
)
by choosing the test function
\textrm{\bf c}=|\rm\nabla_{g}u|_{g}
in their stability inequality.
In our coordinates, this function reads as
\textrm{\bf c}(x)=|\nabla u|_{A(x)}
.
As explained in Remark
3.2
,
this choice of
c
and our integral stability inequality (
1.20
)
lead to a similar estimate for
\mathcal{A}_{x}
by using the ideas from the proof of (
1.13
) above.

We emphasize that both approaches (the Riemannian one and ours)
give an estimate for
\mathcal{A}_{x}
which contains an error term involving the product
|D^{2}u||\nabla u|
.
This error arises from the interaction between the second and first order terms in the Riemannian Hessian
Hu

when squaring
\mathcal{A}_{\rm Riem}
, and thus squaring
Hu=D^{2}u-\Gamma\nabla u=D^{2}u+O(\varepsilon|\nabla u|)
.

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\

&\quad\quad\leq C\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\big(|\nabla\eta|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\big(|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+\varepsilon\eta^{2}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

The inequalities (
1.15
) and (
1.16
) in Theorem
1.2
follow easily from (
3.33
) by choosing appropriate cut-off functions
and using that
\varepsilon
is bounded by a universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}
.
Choosing

\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
such that
\eta=1
in
B_{3/4}
and
0\leq\eta\leq 1
in
B_{1}

leads to the estimate in balls (
1.15
).
The second estimate in annuli (
1.16
)
follows by choosing

\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1}\setminus\overline{B}_{1/8})
with
\eta=1
in
B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4}
and
0\leq\eta\leq 1
in
B_{1}
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

By the proof above,
we can also deduce an interior a priori estimate for the
L^{1}
norm of the Hessian.
For this, assuming
\varepsilon
to be small,
recalling that
\mathcal{A}_{0}\leq C\mathcal{A}
by (
3.15
),
and absorbing the Hessian term in Step 1, we obtain

|D^{2}u|\leq-C{\rm div}\left(A(0)\nabla u\right)+C\mathcal{A}+C|\nabla u|\quad\text{a.e. in }B_{1}.

Multiplying this inequality by a cut-off function and integrating by parts,
using the bound for
\mathcal{A}
in Step
3
and applying Cauchy-Schwarz,
we deduce an estimate
for the
L^{1}
norm of the Hessian in terms of the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient in a larger ball.

We conclude this section by proving Proposition
1.3
.
To show that the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient is controlled by the
L^{1}
norm of the function in a larger ball,
we use the interpolation inequalities of Cabré
[
5
]

combined with the Hessian estimates from Theorem
1.2
.
The errors in larger balls can then be absorbed thanks to a celebrated lemma of Simon
[
15
]
.
We recall the interpolation inequalities of Cabré in Appendix
C

and Simon’s lemma in Appendix
D
.

Proof of Proposition
1.3
.

We cover
B_{1/2}

(except for a set of measure zero)
with a family of disjoint open cubes
Q_{j}

of the same side-length and small enough so that
Q_{j}\subset B_{3/4}
.
The side-length and the number of cubes depend only on
n
.
Combining the interpolation inequalities of Propositions
C.1
and
C.2
, rescaled from the unit cube to
Q_{j}
,
with

\tilde{\delta}=\delta^{3/2}
for a given
\delta\in(0,1)
,
we obtain

\int_{Q_{j}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq C\delta\int_{Q_{j}}\lvert D^{2}u\rvert|\nabla u|\,dx+C\delta\int_{Q_{j}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+C\delta^{-2-\frac{3n}{2}}\left(\int_{Q_{j}}|u|\,dx\right)^{2}.

Since
Q_{j}\subset B_{3/4}
,
applying (
1.15
) from Theorem
1.2
, for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
we have

\int_{Q_{j}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx\leq C\delta\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+C\delta^{-2-\frac{3n}{2}}\left(\int_{B_{1}}|u|\,dx\right)^{2}.

Adding up these inequalities, we obtain

(3.34)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2})}^{2}\leq C\delta\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}+C\delta^{-2-\frac{3n}{2}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}^{2}\quad\text{ for }\delta\in(0,1)\text{ and }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

As explained in Section
2
,
for
B_{\rho}(y)\subset B_{1}
,
the function

u^{y,\rho}:=u(y+\rho\,\cdot)

is a stable solution to a semilinear equation with coefficients
A^{y,\rho}=A(y+\rho\,\cdot)
and
b^{y,\rho}=\rho\,b(y+\rho\,\cdot)
.
In particular, since
\rho\leq 1
, for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
we have that

\|DA^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b^{y,\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\rho\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},

and we can apply (
3.34
) to
u^{y,\rho}
, which yields

\begin{split}\rho^{n+2}\int_{B_{\rho/2}(y)}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx&\leq C\delta\rho^{n+2}\int_{B_{\rho}(y)}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+C\delta^{-2-\frac{3n}{2}}\left(\int_{B_{\rho}(y)}|u|\,dx\right)^{2}\\
&\leq C\delta\rho^{n+2}\int_{B_{\rho}(y)}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+C\delta^{-2-\frac{3n}{2}}\left(\int_{B_{1}}|u|\,dx\right)^{2}.\end{split}

By Lemma
D.1
with

\sigma(B):=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B)}^{2}
,
the claim follows.
∎

4.
The
W^{1,2+\gamma}
estimate

Here we prove the higher integrability estimate (
1.11
) in Theorem
1.1
.
The strategy of proof is the same as for the Laplacian in
[
2
]
.
First we bound the
L^{2+\gamma}
norm in terms of the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient when the coefficients are small.
This will follow from a uniform estimate of the Dirichlet norm on level sets,
which relies on the Hessian estimates in Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}\big(\overline{B}_{1}\big)
be a stable solution of

-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}
,
for some nonnegative function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then there exists a universal constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0

with the following property:
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2+\gamma}(B_{1/2})}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})},

where
\gamma>0

depends only on
n
,
and

C
is a universal constant.

Proof.

The proof is divided in two steps.

Step 1:

We prove that, if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\int_{\{u=t\}\cap B_{1/2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2},

where
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
C
are universal.

Since
\left|{\rm div}\big(|\nabla u|\nabla u\big)\right|\leq C|D^{2}u||\nabla u|
,
by (
1.15
) in Theorem
1.2
, for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}

we have

(4.1)

\begin{split}\left\|{\rm div}\big(|\nabla u|\nabla u\big)\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4})}\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}.\end{split}

Consider a cut-off function
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4})
with
\eta=1
in
B_{1/2}
and
0\leq\eta\leq 1
.
By the divergence theorem, for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\begin{split}\int_{\{u=t\}\cap B_{1/2}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\leq\int_{\{u=t\}\cap B_{1}\cap\{\nabla u\neq 0\}}|\nabla u|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&=-\int_{\{u>t\}\cap B_{1}\cap\{\nabla u\neq 0\}}{\rm div}\big(|\nabla u|\nabla u\,\eta^{2}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla(\eta^{2})|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{B_{1}}\big|{\rm div}\big(|\nabla u|\nabla u\big)\big|\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

and applying (
4.1
) we obtain the claim.

Step 2:

Conclusion.

Let

v:=\dfrac{u-(u)_{B_{1}}}{\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}},

where

(u)_{B_{1}}:=\displaystyle\frac{1}{|B_{1}|}\int_{B_{1}}u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
.
In particular
\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}=1

and by the Sobolev-Poincaré inequality, for some dimensional
p>2,
we have

(4.2)

\left(\int_{B_{1}}|v|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq C\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla v|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=C.

By the coarea formula and (
4.2
), we have

(4.3)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}|t|^{p}|\nabla v|^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{B_{1}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}|v|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C.\end{split}

Since
p>2,
we can choose dimensional constants
q>1
and
\theta\in(0,1/3)
such that
p/q=(1-\theta)/\theta.

We define

h(t):=\max\{1,|t|\}.

Using the coarea formula and the Hölder inequality (note that
p\theta-q(1-\theta)=0
), we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1/2}}|\nabla v|^{3-3\theta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1/2}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}h(t)^{p\theta-q(1-\theta)}|\nabla v|^{-\theta+2(1-\theta)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}h(t)^{p}|\nabla v|^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right)^{\theta}\cdot\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1/2}}h(t)^{-q}|\nabla v|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right)^{1-\theta}.\end{split}

Thanks to (
4.3
) and the definition of
h(t),
we have

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}h(t)^{p}|\nabla v|^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\
&\leq\int_{-1}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1}\cap\{|\nabla v|\neq 0\}}|\nabla v|^{-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+C\leq|B_{1}|+C\leq C.\end{split}

Since
q>1,

it follows that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}h(t)^{-q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t
is finite
and by Step 1, for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, we have

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\,h(t)^{-q}\int_{\{v=t\}\cap B_{1/2}}|\nabla v|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C.\end{split}

Finally, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1/2}}|\nabla v|^{3-3\theta}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C\end{split}

which gives the claim, since
\nabla v\equiv\nabla u/\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}
.
∎

To deduce the
L^{2+\gamma}
estimate (
1.11
) in Theorem
1.1
, we will combine Proposition
1.3
with Lemma
4.1
,
and apply a scaling and covering argument.

Proof of (
1.11
) in Theorem
1.1
.

Combining Proposition
1.3

and Lemma
4.1
, applied to
u(\cdot/2)
,
we deduce that there is a universal
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that, if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then

(4.4)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2+\gamma}(B_{1/4})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
\gamma>0
depends only on
n
, and
C
is universal.

Now (
1.11
) will follow easily from (
4.4
) by a scaling and covering argument.
Let
\rho\in(0,1)
to be chosen later.
We cover the ball
B_{1/2}
by a finite number of balls
B_{\rho/4}(y_{j})

with

B_{\rho}(y_{j})\subset B_{1}
.
The number balls depends only on
n
and
\rho
.
As explained in Section
2
,
the functions
u^{y_{j},\rho}=u(y_{j}+\rho\,\cdot)

are stable solutions to a semilinear equation with coefficients

A^{y_{j},\rho}=A(y_{j}+\rho\,\cdot)
and
b^{y_{j},\rho}=\rho\,b(y_{j}+\rho\,\cdot)
.
Choosing
\rho
small enough so that

\rho\big(\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\big)\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,
it follows that

\|DA^{y_{j},\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b^{y_{j},\rho}\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon_{0},

and we can apply (
4.4
)
to each
u^{y_{j},\rho}
,
which yields

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2+\gamma}(B_{1/2})}\leq\sum_{j}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2+\gamma}(B_{\rho/4}(y_{j}))}\leq C\sum_{j}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho}(y_{j}))}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

for some
C
depending only on

n
,
\lambda
,
\Lambda
, and
\rho
.
Since
\rho
depends only on

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}
,
\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}
,
and
\varepsilon_{0}
, which is universal,
this concludes the proof.
∎

5.
The weighted
L^{2}
estimate for radial derivatives

Our goal in this section is to prove Proposition
1.4
,
where we bound the weighted
L^{2}
norm of the radial derivative in balls
by the
L^{2}
norm of the full gradient in annuli.
To prove the estimate,
we will first apply the integral stability inequality with the test functions

\textrm{\bf c}(x)=x\cdot\nabla u\quad\text{ and }\quad\eta=|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\zeta,

where
\zeta
is a cut-off.
Our choice will yield the desired bound with an additional error term involving a weighted integral of the “Hessian times the gradient”,
which we will be able to treat thanks to the a priori estimates on annuli from Theorem
1.2
.

We start by choosing
\textrm{\bf c}=x\cdot\nabla u
and a generic test function
\eta

in the integral stability inequality:

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}\big(\overline{B}_{1}\big)
be a stable solution of

-Lu=f(u)
in
B_{1}
,
for some function
f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that

\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|_{A(x)}^{2}\Big((n-2)\eta^{2}+x\cdot\nabla(\eta^{2})\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{B_{1}}\Big(-2(x\cdot\nabla u)A(x)\nabla u\cdot\nabla(\eta^{2})-|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|_{A(x)}^{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u||x|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\Big(|x|^{2}|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+\left(|x|+|x|^{2}\varepsilon\right)\eta^{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\end{split}

for all
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1})
,
where
C
is a universal constant.

Proof.

Throughout the proof,
C
denotes a generic universal constant.
Testing the integral stability inequality (
1.20
) with
\eta
and
\textrm{\bf c}=x\cdot\nabla u
,
we deduce

(5.1)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}(x\cdot\nabla u)J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)\,\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{B_{1}}|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}\left|\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

The quadratic term on the right-hand side of (
5.1
) can be bounded by

\left|\nabla\eta-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\eta A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\right|^{2}_{A(x)}\leq|\nabla\eta|^{2}_{A(x)}+C\varepsilon|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+C\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2},

and hence

(5.2)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1}}(x\cdot\nabla u)J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)\,\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq\int_{B_{1}}|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|_{A(x)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}|x|^{2}\big(|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+\varepsilon\eta^{2}\big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

To compute the Jacobi operator
J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)=L(x\cdot\nabla u)+f^{\prime}(u)(x\cdot\nabla u)
,
we differentiate the equation
-Lu=f(u)
in the direction of
x
,
which yields

(5.3)

-x\cdot\nabla(Lu)=f^{\prime}(u)\left(x\cdot\nabla u\right),

and hence

(5.4)

\begin{split}J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)&=L\left(x\cdot\nabla u\right)-x\cdot\nabla\left(Lu\right)\\
&=2a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}-x_{k}\partial_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}+b_{i}(x)u_{i}-x_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}(x)u_{i}.\end{split}

From (
5.4
), by the coefficient bounds, it follows that

(5.5)

\begin{split}(x\cdot\nabla u)J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)&\geq 2x_{k}u_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}-x_{k}u_{k}x_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}(x)u_{i}\\
&\quad\quad\quad-C\varepsilon|x|^{2}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|-C\varepsilon|x||\nabla u|^{2}.\end{split}

The idea now is to integrate by parts to get rid of the highest order terms on the left-hand side of (
5.2
).
For this we must rewrite the term
2x_{k}u_{k}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}
in (
5.5
) in divergence form.
By the chain rule, we have

(5.6)

x_{k}u_{k}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}=\partial_{i}\big(x_{k}u_{k}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{j}\big)-x_{k}u_{k}\partial_{i}a_{ij}(x)u_{j}-a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j}-x_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{ik}u_{j}.

Using that
a_{ij}(x)=a_{ji}(x)
,
the last term in (
5.6
) can be written as

x_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{ik}u_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{k}\big(a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j}x_{k}\big)-\frac{n}{2}a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j}-\frac{1}{2}x_{k}\partial_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j},

and hence

(5.7)

\begin{split}2x_{k}u_{k}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{ij}&=\partial_{i}\Big(2x_{k}u_{k}\,a_{ij}(x)u_{j}-a_{jk}(x)u_{j}u_{k}x_{i}\Big)+(n-2)a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j}\\
&\quad\quad-2x_{k}u_{k}\partial_{i}a_{ij}(x)u_{j}+x_{k}\partial_{k}a_{ij}(x)u_{i}u_{j}\\
&\geq{\rm div}\left(2(x\cdot\nabla u)A(x)\nabla u-|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}x\right)+(n-2)|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}\\
&\quad\quad-C\varepsilon|x||\nabla u|^{2},\end{split}

where in the last inequality we have used the estimates for the coefficients.
Combining (
5.7
) and (
5.5
),
we obtain

(5.8)

\begin{split}&(x\cdot\nabla u)J_{u}(x\cdot\nabla u)\\
&\quad\quad\geq{\rm div}\left(2(x\cdot\nabla u)A(x)\nabla u-|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}x\right)+(n-2)|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-x_{k}u_{k}x_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}(x)u_{i}-C\varepsilon|x|^{2}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|-C\varepsilon|x||\nabla u|^{2}.\end{split}

Multiplying (
5.8
) by
\eta^{2}
and integrating, the third term
-\int_{B_{1}}x_{k}u_{k}x_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}(x)u_{i}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
can be integrated by parts
and estimated by

(5.9)

\begin{split}\left|-\int_{B_{1}}x_{k}u_{k}x_{j}\partial_{j}b_{i}(x)u_{i}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|&=\left|\int_{B_{1}}b_{i}(x)\partial_{j}(x_{k}u_{k}x_{j}u_{i}\eta^{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\\
&\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u||x|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\left(|x|^{2}|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+|x|\eta^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Substituting (
5.8
) in the inequality (
5.2
),
rearranging terms and
by the error bound (
5.9
),
it follows that

(5.10)

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1}}\left((n-2)|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}\eta^{2}-|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|^{2}_{A(x)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{B_{1}}{\rm div}\Big(2(x\cdot\nabla u)A(x)\nabla u-|\nabla u|^{2}_{A(x)}x\Big)\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u||x|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\Big(|x|^{2}|\nabla(\eta^{2})|+(|x|+\varepsilon|x|^{2})\eta^{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Integrating by parts the divergence term on the left-hand side of (
5.10
) yields the claim.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

In (
5.3
) we took a derivative of the equation in the
x
direction
to get rid of the dependence on the nonlinearity.
Instead, we could have multiplied the equation
by the test function
\xi={\rm div}\left(x\,(x\cdot\nabla u)\eta^{2}\right)

and integrated by parts.
Thanks to this, we avoid having to take any derivatives of
b
,
since the term involving it can be bounded directly.
Notice also that we need
u
to have three derivatives to be able to compute
L\textrm{\bf c}
.
This is the same phenomenon as in the proof of Theorem
1.2
;
see the discussion in Remark
3.1
.

Remark 5.3
.

Since
|A(x)-A(0)|\leq C\varepsilon|x|
,
the inequality in Lemma
5.1
also holds
if we replace
A(x)
by the constant matrix
A(0)

and we add an additional error term
C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}|x|^{3}|\nabla\eta|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

on the right-hand side.
For future use, the final estimate involving
A(0)
instead of
A(x)
reads as

(5.11)

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}\Big((n-2)\eta^{2}+x\cdot\nabla(\eta^{2})\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&+\int_{B_{1}}\Big(-2(x\cdot\nabla u)A(0)\nabla u\cdot\nabla(\eta^{2})-|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}|\nabla\eta|_{A(0)}^{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u||x|^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla u|^{2}\Big(|x|^{3}|\nabla\eta|^{2}+(|x|+\varepsilon|x|^{2})\eta^{2}\Big)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

Next, we choose the singular test function

Lemma 5.4
.

Let

If

(5.13)

\begin{split}&(n-2-a)\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{-a}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{a(8-a)}{4}\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{-a}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\

Proof.

By approximation,

Since

From the identities (

\begin{split}&(n-2-a)\int_{B_{1}}|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{-a}|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}\zeta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\dfrac{a(8-a)}{4}\int_{B_{1}}|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{-a-2}|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}\zeta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\

for some universal constant

,
where we have controlled the remainder terms
thanks to the uniform ellipticity
and the fact that

is bounded by a dimensional constant.

For
0<\rho\leq 1/2

as in the statement,
we consider a Lipschitz function

,

\begin{split}&(n-2-a)\int_{B_{1}}|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{-a}|\nabla u|_{A(0)}^{2}\zeta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\dfrac{a(8-a)}{4}\int_{B_{1}}|x|_{A^{-1}(0)}^{-a-2}|x\cdot\nabla u|^{2}\zeta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}\setminus B_{\rho}}r^{-a}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{2\rho}}r^{2-a}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{2\rho}}\big(r^{1-a}+\varepsilon r^{2-a}\big)|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Since

(5.18)

\begin{split}&\lambda^{a/2+1}\left((n-2-a)\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{-a}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{a(8-a)}{4}\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{-a}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\\

∎

We can finally prove Proposition

.
For this, we will apply Lemma

by

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

Since
3\leq n\leq 9
, we have that

in Lemma

\begin{split}&\frac{(n-2)(10-n)}{4}\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}\setminus B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{2\rho}}r^{4-n}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{2\rho}}\big(r^{3-n}+\varepsilon r^{4-n}\big)|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

To bound the Hessian term
\int_{B_{2\rho}}r^{4-n}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
on the right-hand side of (

we will apply the a priori estimate on annuli (

) from Theorem

Let

.
As explained in Section

Hence,

) in Theorem

,
there is a universal

with the following property:

, then

\int_{B_{r_{j+1}}\setminus B_{r_{j+2}}}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq Cr_{j}^{-1}\int_{B_{r_{j}}\setminus B_{r_{j+3}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }j\geq 0,

,

\begin{split}&\frac{(n-2)(10-n)}{4}\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\leq C\int_{B_{2\rho}\setminus B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}\quad\text{ for }\rho\leq 1/4\text{ and }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},

Remark 5.5
.

Our proof
in Section
7

of the

bound (

controls the weighted integral

As a consequence, we will also obtain a bound for the less singular error terms
\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

Here we point out that this last quantity can be estimated directly
from our previous Lemma
5.4
,
without the use of Section
6
.
This is done as follows.

Letting
a=n-3
in Lemma
5.4
,
since

for
3\leq n\leq 11
,
we can drop the radial term in (

)
and the left-hand side becomes

.
The right-hand side now includes an error term

,

\varepsilon\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{4-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

.
Hence, we obtain

Making

smaller if necessary, since

in

, we can absorb the rightmost term into the left-hand side.
This leads to an expression which can be hole-filled,

\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C\rho^{2\alpha}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}\quad\text{ for all }\rho\leq 1/4,

and

are universal constants.
We note that,

.

In general,
this strategy allows to prove decay
of the weighted integrals

for exponents

and

.
As an application,
we could extend the optimal regularity

[
13
]
for stable solutions in dimensions

in (

Remark 5.6
.

The key estimate in the proof of Proposition
1.4
is an inequality for weighted integrals,

\int_{B_{2\rho}}r^{4-n}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

\begin{split}\int_{B_{2\rho}}r^{4-n}|D^{2}u||\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&\leq C\left(\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{B_{4\rho}}r^{4-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}\\

which avoids the use of annuli
and which involves a very recent test function of
Cabré
[
4
]
and Peng, Zhang, and Zhou
[
14
]
.

To obtain (
5.28
),
one uses the inequality (
3.33
)
from the proof of Theorem
1.2

with the singular test function

\eta=r^{-n/2}\zeta
,
where
\zeta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
is a cut-off.
It is worth noting
that this inequality relies on the Sternberg-Zumbrun estimate for
\int_{B_{1}}\mathcal{A}^{2}\eta^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
,
which comes from choosing the test function

in the integral stability inequality (

).
A function of this form had already appeared in
[
4
]
,

.
Interestingly,
our choice
\xi=|\nabla u|r^{-n/2}

coincides with the
test function used by the authors in
[
14
]
,
where they obtained an a priori Hölder estimate
for stable solutions when
n\leq 5
.
While their strategy involves integrating by parts an expression that is already quadratic in the gradient,
expressing the new “coupled” Hessian errors

as the uncoupled expression

and an error term related to

.

6.
The radial derivative controls the function in
L^{1}

The goal of this section is to prove Proposition
1.5
,
where we control the
L^{1}
norm of (generalized) superharmonic functions
Lu\leq 0
by the
L^{1}
norm of the radial derivative on annuli.
By a comparison argument, it will suffice to derive the analogue
L^{1}
estimate on spheres for harmonic functions
Lv=0
,
which are obtained by duality from the
L^{\infty}
estimates of a Neumann problem.

Let
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})
,
and consider the Neumann problem in divergence form

(6.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla\varphi)=0&\text{ in }B_{1}\\
A(x)\nabla\varphi\cdot\nu=g&\text{ on }\partial B_{1},\end{array}\right.

which
admits solutions if and only if
\int_{\partial B_{1}}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0
.
Recall that the solutions of (
6.1
) are unique up to addition of constants.
We will derive an
L^{\infty}
a priori estimate for the zero mean solutions of (
6.1
)
in terms of the conormal derivative

(6.2)

N\varphi:=A(x)\nabla\varphi\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}.

This is achieved by a Moser iteration
based on the following Sobolev trace inequality

(6.3)

\|u\|_{L^{2^{\star}}(\partial B_{1})}^{2}\leq C\big(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}^{2}\big)

for
u\in W^{1,2}(B_{1})
,
where
C
depends only on
n
and

(6.4)

2^{\star}:=\frac{n-1}{n-2}2

is the Sobolev trace exponent.
We give a short proof of this inequality in Appendix
B
.

Our proof by Moser iteration is inspired by the one of Winkert in
[
17
]
,
where
he
obtains
L^{\infty}
estimates for general quasilinear Neumann problems.
While the author employs certain technical interpolation and trace inequalities from the theory of Besov and Lizorkin-Triebel spaces,
we only need the basic trace inequality (
6.3
),
for which we give an elementary proof.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\varphi
be the unique solution of (
6.1
) with
\int_{B_{1}}\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0
.
Then

\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})},

where
C
depends only on
n
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

Dividing the solution by the norm
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}
, we may assume that
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}=1
.
By the maximum principle, it suffices to bound the
L^{\infty}
norm of
\varphi
on the sphere
\partial B_{1}
.
In this proof,
C
always denotes a generic constant depending only on
n
and
\lambda
.

First, we obtain a basic energy estimate.
Multiplying the equation (
6.1
) by
\varphi
and integrating by parts, we have

(6.5)

\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B_{1}}g\varphi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Combining the standard trace inequality
\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C\big(\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{2})}^{2}\big)

with the Poincaré inequality in the ball (recall that
\int_{B_{1}}\varphi=0
),
we also have

(6.6)

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}.

Hence, applying Cauchy-Schwarz in (
6.5
),
by (
6.6
) we obtain

(6.7)

\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}_{A(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}.

By uniform ellipticity and the bound
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}=1
, from (
6.7
) it follows that

(6.8)

\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\leq C.

Therefore, by (
6.3
), (
6.6
), and (
6.8
) we deduce the a priori estimate

(6.9)

\|\varphi\|_{L^{2^{\star}}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C.

Next, we derive an
L^{\infty}
bound for the positive part of
the solution
by Moser iteration.
Let
m\geq 2
.
Multiplying the equation by
the power
(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}

and integrating by parts

(6.10)

(m-1)\int_{B_{1}}(\varphi^{+})^{m-2}|\nabla\varphi^{+}|_{A(x)}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B_{1}}(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Since

|\nabla(\varphi^{+})^{\frac{m}{2}}|^{2}_{A(x)}=\frac{m^{2}}{4}(\varphi^{+})^{m-2}|\nabla(\varphi^{+})|^{2}_{A(x)},

by (
6.10
) and the uniform ellipticity, using that
\frac{m}{m-1}\leq 2
for
m\geq 2
, we have

(6.11)

\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla(\varphi^{+})^{\frac{m}{2}}|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq Cm\int_{\partial B_{1}}(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Adding the integral
\int_{\partial B_{1}}(\varphi^{+})^{m}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
to both sides of (
6.11
), we have

\|\nabla(\varphi^{+})^{\frac{m}{2}}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}^{2}+\|(\varphi^{+})^{\frac{m}{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}^{2}\leq Cm\|(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{1})}+\|(\varphi^{+})^{\frac{m}{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{1})}^{2}

and applying the Sobolev trace inequality (
6.3
) on the left-hand side yields

(6.12)

\|\varphi^{+}\|^{m}_{L^{\frac{2^{\star}}{2}m}(\partial B_{1})}\leq Cm\|(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{1})}+C\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}^{m}.

By Hölder and since
m\geq 2
,
the
L^{m-1}
norm in (
6.12
) can be bounded by

\|(\varphi^{+})^{m-1}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{1})}\leq|\partial B_{1}|^{\frac{1}{m}}\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}^{m-1}\leq C\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}^{m-1},

and hence

(6.13)

\|\varphi^{+}\|^{m}_{L^{\frac{2^{\star}}{2}m}(\partial B_{1})}\leq Cm\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}^{m-1}+C\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}^{m}.

Since
\|\varphi^{+}\|^{m-1}_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}\leq\max\{1,\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}\}^{m}
,
from (
6.13
)
it follows that

(6.14)

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{\frac{2^{\star}}{2}m}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C^{\frac{1}{m}}m^{\frac{1}{m}}\max\{1,\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m}(\partial B_{1})}\}.

We wish to iterate (
6.14
).
Let
m_{0}:=2^{\star}

and,
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let

m_{k}:=\left(\frac{2^{\star}}{2}\right)^{k}m_{0}.

By (
6.14
) and the definition of
m_{k}
, we have

(6.15)

\begin{split}\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}&\leq C^{\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}}m_{k-1}^{\frac{1}{m_{k-1}}}\max\{1,\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k-1}}(\partial B_{1})}\}\\
&=(Cm_{0})^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{k-1}}\left(\frac{2^{\star}}{2}\right)^{\frac{k-1}{m_{0}}\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{k-1}}\max\{1,\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k-1}}(\partial B_{1})}\}.\end{split}

We have exactly one of the following three cases:

•

Case 1
:

(6.16)

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}\leq 1

•

Case 2
:
there is an
l\in\{1,2,\ldots,k-1\}
such that

(6.17)

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k+1-i}}(\partial B_{1})}>1\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq l,\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k-l}}(\partial B_{1})}\leq 1.

•

Case 3
:

(6.18)

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k+1-i}}(\partial B_{1})}>1\quad\text{ for }1\leq i\leq k.

Case
1
already yields a uniform bound for
\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(B_{1})}
.
If Case
2
holds then, iterating (
6.15
)
l-1
times, we arrive at

(6.19)

\begin{split}\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}&\leq(Cm_{0})^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=k-l}^{k-1}\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}}\left(\frac{2^{\star}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=k-l}^{k-1}j\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}}.\end{split}

The right-hand side of (
6.19
) is nondecreasing in
l
for
(say)
C\geq 1
,
which we can always assume.
Finally, if Case 3 holds
then, iterating (
6.15
), we obtain

(6.20)

\begin{split}\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}&\leq(Cm_{0})^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}}\left(\frac{2^{\star}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}j\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}}\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{0}}(\partial B_{1})}.\end{split}

By the monotonicity of (
6.19
) in
l

and using the a priori estimate (
6.9
) for
\|\varphi\|_{L^{m_{0}}(\partial B_{1})}
in (
6.20
),
we see that in all three cases above we have

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C(Cm_{0})^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}}\left(\frac{2^{\star}}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{m_{0}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}j\left(\frac{2}{2^{\star}}\right)^{j}},

and since the exponent on the right-hand side is uniformly bounded, we deduce

(6.21)

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{m_{k}}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C.

Taking the limit as
k\to\infty
in (
6.21
) now yields

\|\varphi^{+}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C,

which is the desired
L^{\infty}
estimate for the positive part of the solutions.
The same argument gives an a priori estimate for the negative part
\varphi^{-}
and yields the claim.
∎

By duality, from the
L^{\infty}
estimate in Lemma
6.1

we deduce an
L^{1}
bound for the elliptic problem with a source:

Lemma 6.2
.

Given
h\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B_{1}})
,
let
v\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})

satisfy

{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla v)+h(x)=0\quad\text{ in }B_{1}.

Then

\|v-t\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C\|Nv\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{1})}+C\|h\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where

t:=\inf\{\overline{t}\,\colon\,|\{v>\overline{t}\}\cap\partial B_{1}|\leq|\partial B_{1}|/2\}

and

C
depends only on
n
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

Replacing
v
by
v-t
we may assume that
t=0
, therefore

|\{v>0\}\cap\partial B_{1}|\leq|\partial B_{1}|/2

and

|\{v<0\}\cap\partial B_{1}|\leq|\partial B_{1}|/2
.
The function
{\rm sgn}(v)=v/|v|
in
v\neq 0
can then be extended to
\{v=0\}\cap\partial B_{1}
,
taking values
\pm 1

and in such a way that
\int_{\partial B_{1}}{\rm sgn}(v)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0
.
In particular,
|v|=v\,{\rm sgn}(v)
on
\partial B_{1}
.

We define the convolutions on
\partial B_{1}

g_{k}:={\rm sgn}(v)\star\eta_{k},

where
\{\eta_{k}\}
is a sequence of smooth mollifiers on
\partial B_{1}
.
We have
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})
,
|g_{k}|\leq 1
, and
\int_{\partial B_{1}}g_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0
since
{\rm sgn}(v)
has zero average on
\partial B_{1}
.
Moreover, it holds that

(6.22)

\int_{\partial B_{1}}|v|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\lim_{k}\int_{\partial B_{1}}vg_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Since
g_{k}
has zero average on
\partial B_{1}
, we can uniquely solve the Neumann problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla\varphi_{k})=0&\text{ in }B_{1}\\
N\varphi_{k}=g_{k}&\text{ on }\partial B_{1}\end{array}\right.

imposing additionally that
\int_{B_{1}}\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0
.
By Lemma
6.1
, we deduce

(6.23)

\|\varphi_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{1})}\leq C,

where
C
depends only on
n
and
\lambda
.
Notice that, integrating by parts, we have

\begin{split}\int_{\partial B_{1}}vg_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&=\int_{\partial B_{1}}vN\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial B_{1}}(Nv)\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{B_{1}}{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla v)\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&=\int_{\partial B_{1}}(Nv)\,\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{B_{1}}h\,\varphi_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\end{split}

where in the last equality we have used the equation satisfied by
v
.
Hence, by (
6.23
)

\left|\int_{\partial B_{1}}vg_{k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|\leq C\int_{\partial B_{1}}|Nv|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+C\int_{B_{1}}|h|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

and the claim follows by (
6.22
).
∎

We now use the previous estimates on spheres
to obtain
L^{1}
bounds on annuli for a divergence-form operator with drift.
The drift term will be treated as a source,
which will appear as an error in the right-hand side of the estimate.
If
the coefficient matrix
A(x)
is close to the identity,
then the conormal derivative
Nu

is
close to the radial derivative
u_{r}
.
Hence,
we will obtain Proposition
1.5
as a corollary of the following:

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})
be a supersolution

\mathcal{L}u\leq 0
in
B_{1}
,
where
\mathcal{L}
is the operator

\mathcal{L}u={\rm div}(A(x)\nabla u)+d(x)\cdot\nabla u
.
Assume that

\|d\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Then
there exists a constant
t
, which depends on
u
,
such that

\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}\leq C\|u_{r}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}+C\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})},

where
C
is a constant depending only on
n
and
\lambda
.

Proof.

Since
\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}=\int_{1/8}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\int_{\partial B_{r}}|Nu|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
,
by the mean value theorem

(6.24)

\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}=\frac{7}{8}\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}|Nu|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

for some
\rho\in[1/8,1]
.
Let
v
be the unique solution of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla v)+d(x)\cdot\nabla u=0&\text{ in }B_{\rho}\\
v=u&\text{ on }\partial B_{\rho}.\end{array}\right.

Since
{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla(u-v))\leq 0
in
B_{\rho}
,
by the comparison principle
u\geq v
in
B_{\rho}
.
Moreover, using that
u=v
on
\partial B_{\rho}
, we deduce that
Nu\leq Nv
on
\partial B_{\rho}
.
In particular, this gives

(6.25)

(Nv)^{-}\leq(Nu)^{-}\text{ on }\partial B_{\rho}.

Notice also that, integrating the equation

{\rm div}(A(x)\nabla v)=-d(x)\cdot\nabla u
in
B_{\rho}
,
by the divergence theorem
we have

\left|\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}Nv\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|=\left|\int_{B_{\rho}}{\rm div}\left(A(x)\nabla v\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|=\left|-\int_{B_{\rho}}d(x)\cdot\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}

and since
\int_{\partial B_{\rho}}Nv\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\|(Nv)^{+}\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}-\|(Nv)^{-}\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}
, we deduce

(6.26)

\|(Nv)^{+}\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}\leq\|(Nv)^{-}\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}+\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}.

Using (
6.26
) and (
6.25
) we have

(6.27)

\begin{split}\|Nv\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}&=\|(Nv)^{-}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+\|(Nv)^{+}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}\leq 2\|(Nv)^{-}\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}\\
&\leq 2\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}.\end{split}

Applying Lemma
6.2

with coefficients
A(\rho\,\cdot)
and source
h(x)=\rho^{2}d(\rho x)\cdot\nabla u(\rho x)

to the function
v(\rho\,\cdot)
yields the estimate

(6.28)

\|v-t\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}\leq C\rho\|Nv\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+C\rho\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})}.

Since

u-t=v-t
on
\partial B_{\rho}
,
combining (
6.28
) and (
6.27
), we obtain

\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}\leq C\rho\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+C\rho\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{\rho})},

and since
\rho\in[1/8,1]
, by (
6.24
), we deduce that

(6.29)

\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}\leq C\|Nu\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}.

To conclude the proof,
it suffices to show that

(6.30)

\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}\leq C\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{\rho})}+C\|u_{r}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}.

Since

(u-t)(s\sigma)=(u-t)(\rho\sigma)-\int_{s}^{\rho}u_{r}(r\sigma)\,dr

for every
s\in(1/8,1)
and
\sigma\in\partial B_{1}
, we have

s^{n-1}|(u-t)(s\sigma)|\leq 8^{n-1}\rho^{n-1}|(u-t)(\rho\sigma)|+8^{n-1}\int_{1/8}^{1}r^{n-1}|u_{r}(r\sigma)|\,dr.

Integrating in
\sigma\in\partial B_{1}
, and then in
s\in(1/8,1)
, we deduce (
6.30
).
Combining (
6.29
) and (
6.30
) yields the claim.
∎

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

We consider the operator
\mathcal{L}
with
d(x)=\widehat{b}(x)
given by (
1.9
),
so that
\mathcal{L}u=Lu={\rm div}(A(x)\nabla u)+\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u
.
Since
A(0)=I
, writing the conormal derivative (
6.2
) as

Nu=u_{r}+(A(x)-I)\nabla u\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}

and by the mean value theorem,
we have

|Nu|\leq|u_{r}|+C\varepsilon|\nabla u|
in
B_{1}
.
Applying Proposition
6.3
now,
the conormal term on the right-hand side of the estimate can be bounded by

,

7.
Proof of the
C^{\alpha}
estimate

This section is devoted to proving the Hölder regularity estimate (
1.12
) in Theorem
1.1
.
The main goal will be to show that the scale-invariant weighted integral
\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}
decays like a power
\rho^{2\alpha}
,
since this gives a
C^{\alpha}
bound.
We will show this property
under the additional assumption that the operator
L
is close to the Laplacian,
i.e.,
assuming
A(0)=I
and
\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon
sufficiently small.
An affine transformation will then lead to an estimate that is valid for all operators, with bounds depending on the norms of the coefficients.

The key idea is
to write the weighted integral of the gradient as an infinite sum on dyadic annuli,
pulling out the weights,
and applying Propositions
1.3
and
1.5
in each annulus.
This allows to control the weighted
L^{2}
norm of the gradient by a weighted
L^{2}
norm of the radial derivative.
Once
we have this bound,
Proposition
1.4
will lead directly to the decay by a standard iteration argument.
This will yield a bound of the
C^{\alpha}
norm in terms of the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient,
which can be controlled by the
L^{1}
norm of the solutions thanks to Proposition
1.3
.

Proof of the Hölder estimate (
1.12
) in Theorem
1.1
.

We may assume that
3\leq n\leq 9
.
Indeed, when
n=2
,
we recover the estimate by applying Theorem
1.1
to the function
\widetilde{u}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=u(x_{1},x_{2})
,
which is a stable solution to the elliptic equation
L\widetilde{u}+\lambda\widetilde{u}_{x_{3}x_{3}}=f(\widetilde{u})
in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Similarly, when
n=1
, one considers the function
\widetilde{u}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}):=u(x_{1})
.

Throughout the proof,
C
denotes a generic universal constant unless stated otherwise.
The proof is divided in three steps.

Step 1:

Under the assumption that

A(0)=I\quad\text{ and }\quad\|DA\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}+\|b\|_{C^{0}(\overline{B}_{1})}\leq\varepsilon,

we prove that there is a universal
\varepsilon_{0}>0
with the following property:
if
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, then

(7.1)

\int_{B_{\rho}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\rho^{2\alpha}\quad\text{ for all }\rho\leq 1/8,

where
\alpha>0
and
C
are universal constants.

As explained before,
we will write the weighted Dirichlet integral as an infinite sum on dyadic annuli,
similarly to what we did for the weighted Hessian estimates in the proof of Proposition
1.4
.
We treat the case
\rho=1/2
first,
and then apply the scaling of the problem.

Let
r_{j}:=2^{-j}
with
j\geq 0
.
We have

(7.2)

\begin{split}\int_{B_{1/2}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x&=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{B_{r_{j+1}}\setminus B_{r_{j+2}}}r^{2-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}^{2-n}\int_{B_{r_{j+1}}\setminus B_{r_{j+2}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

We want to apply Proposition
1.3
on annuli
to control the Dirichlet integrals in (
7.2
) by the
L^{1}
norm of the solution,
and then Proposition
1.5
to obtain bounds in terms of the radial derivative.

We cover the annulus
B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4}

by a finite number of balls
B_{d/2}(y_{j})
,
where
d=d(n)
is small enough so that

B_{d}(y_{j})\subset B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8}
.
The number of balls depends only on
n
.
As explained in Section
2
,
the functions
u(y_{j}+d\cdot)
are stable solutions to a semilinear equation with coefficients
A(y_{j}+d\cdot)
and
d\,b(y_{j}.+d\cdot)
.
Applying Proposition
1.3
to each
u(y_{j}+d\cdot)
,
there is a universal
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that,
for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
, we have

(7.3)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4})}^{2}\leq\sum_{j}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{d/2}(y_{j}))}^{2}\leq C\sum_{j}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{2d}(y_{j}))}^{2}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}^{2}.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
u-t
is a stable solution to
-L\widetilde{u}=f(\widetilde{u}+t)
in
B_{1}
.
Hence,
by (
7.3
),
it follows that

(7.4)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4})}\leq C\|u-t\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}\quad\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Since
A(0)=I
,
we can choose
t
in (
7.4
) to be the constant in the conclusion of Proposition
1.5
,
and by this result we deduce

(7.5)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4})}\leq C\|u_{r}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}+C\varepsilon\|\nabla u\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}\quad\text{ for all }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Squaring (
7.5
) and by Cauchy-Schwarz,
we also have the weaker

(7.6)

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(B_{1/2}\setminus B_{1/4})}^{2}\leq C\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(B_{1}\setminus B_{1/8})}^{2}+C\varepsilon^{2}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(B_{1})}\quad\text{ for all }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Now we apply (
7.6
) to the rescaled functions
u(r_{j}\cdot)
,
which gives (see the comments in
(i)
in Section
2
)

(7.7)

\int_{B_{r_{j+1}}\setminus B_{r_{j+2}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq C\int_{B_{r_{j}}\setminus B_{r_{j+3}}}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon^{2}r_{j}^{2}\int_{B_{r_{j}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Hence, multiplying (
7.7
) by
r_{j}^{2-n}
and summing in
j

(7.8)

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}^{2-n}\int_{B_{r_{j+1}}\setminus B_{r_{j+2}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}^{2-n}\int_{B_{r_{j}}\setminus B_{r_{j+3}}}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}^{4-n}\int_{B_{r_{j}}}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{B_{r_{j}}\setminus B_{r_{j+3}}}r^{2-n}u_{r}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r_{j}\int_{B_{r_{j}}}r^{3-n}|\nabla u|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0},\end{split}

where in the last line we have used that
r_{j}^{3-n}\leq r^{3-n}
in
B_{r_{j}}
for
n\geq 3
.
Since
r_{j}=2^{-j}

Appendix A
Stability is not equivalent to the integral inequality

Let
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution to
-Lu=f(u)
in
\Omega
with
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
Recall that
u
is a stable solution if

(A.1)

J_{u}\varphi=L\varphi+f^{\prime}(u)\varphi\leq 0\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

for some function
\varphi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
with
\varphi>0
in
\Omega
and
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
This is the stability condition (
1.3
)
presented in the Introduction
and is equivalent to the nonnegativity of the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
J_{u}
(with the sign convention
J_{u}\varphi=-\mu\varphi
).
There, we also showed
that stable solutions satisfy the integral inequality (
1.10
), which reads

(A.2)

\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\xi-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)\xi|^{2}_{A(x)}\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

Our goal in this appendix is to show that the integral inequality (
A.2
)
does not imply the stability condition (
A.1
) in general.
The main reason is that
the problem is not variational, due to the drift in
L
.
We also give conditions under which the equivalence holds.
Namely, writing the operator in divergence form
Lu={\rm div}(A(x)\nabla u)+\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u
, we show that if
A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)

is the gradient of a scalar function, then the problem is variational and the two conditions are equivalent.

First we write the integrals in (
A.2
) as the quadratic form associated to a linear self-adjoint operator.
Integrating by parts, we have

(A.3)

\int_{\Omega}\left(|\nabla\xi-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\xi A^{-1}(x)\widehat{b}(x)|_{A(x)}^{2}-f^{\prime}(u)\xi^{2}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\xi\widetilde{J_{u}}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where
\widetilde{J_{u}}
is the operator

(A.4)

\widetilde{J_{u}}\xi:={\rm div}(A(x)\nabla\xi)-\Big\{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}{\rm div}(\widehat{b}(x))+\textstyle\frac{1}{4}|\widehat{b}(x)|_{A^{-1}(x)}^{2}\Big\}\xi+f^{\prime}(u)\xi.

Hence,
by the variational characterization of eigenvalues,
(
A.2
) amounts to the nonnegativity of the principal eigenvalue of
\widetilde{J_{u}}
.

We can now state our example
of a solution satisfying (
A.2
) but not (
A.1
):

Example 1
.

Consider the operator

Lv=\Delta v+\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla v

with vector field

\widehat{b}(x)=\frac{-x_{2}e_{1}+x_{1}e_{2}}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}}.

For each constant
c>0
, we let
f(u)=(\lambda_{1}+c)u+1
,
where
\lambda_{1}
denotes the least Dirichlet eigenvalue of the Laplacian in the unit ball
B_{1}
.

If

c>0
is sufficiently small, then the unique solution
u
to the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-Lu=f(u)&\text{ in }B_{1}\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial B_{1}\end{array}\right.

satisfies the integral stability condition (
A.2
) but is not a stable solution, i.e., the stability condition (
A.1
) does not hold.
5
5

5

The function
u
can be given explicitly in terms of Bessel functions of the first kind

as

u(x)=\frac{1}{(\lambda_{1}+c)\mathcal{J}_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}+c}\right)}|x|^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\mathcal{J}_{\frac{n-2}{2}}\left(\sqrt{\lambda_{1}+c}\,|x|\right)-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}+c}.

Proof.

The problem for

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\Delta u-\widehat{b}(x)\cdot\nabla u-(\lambda_{1}+c)u=1&\text{ in }B_{1}\\

Notice that the drift

above,
we have

\mu_{1}\int_{\Omega}e^{w(x)}\varphi_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}(e^{w/2}\varphi_{1})\widetilde{J_{u}}(e^{w/2}\varphi_{1})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq\widetilde{\mu_{1}}\int_{\Omega}e^{w(x)}\varphi_{1}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

and we obtain the reverse inequality
\mu_{1}\geq\widetilde{\mu_{1}}
.

Appendix B
A trace inequality

First we prove a simple lemma to control the
L^{p}
norm in the ball by the
L^{p}
norms of the trace and the gradient:

Lemma B.1
.

For
p\geq 1
and
u\in W^{1,p}(B_{1})
, we have

\|u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}\leq 2^{p-1}\left(\|u\|_{L^{p}(\partial B_{1})}^{p}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}\right).

Proof.

By approximation, we may assume that
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})
.
For
r\in(0,1)
and
\sigma\in\partial B_{1}
, we have

u(r\sigma)=u(\sigma)-\int_{r}^{1}\sigma\cdot\nabla u(t\sigma)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

and hence

(B.1)

\begin{split}r^{n-1}|u(r\sigma)|^{p}&\leq 2^{p-1}r^{n-1}|u(\sigma)|^{p}+2^{p-1}r^{n-1}\int_{r}^{1}|\nabla u(t\sigma)|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\\
&\leq 2^{p-1}|u(\sigma)|^{p}+2^{p-1}\int_{0}^{1}t^{n-1}|\nabla u(t\sigma)|^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

Integrating (
B.1
) in
\int_{0}^{1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\int_{\partial B_{1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)
now yields the claim.
∎

We prove a Sobolev trace inequality with best exponent:

Proposition B.2
.

For
1<p<n
,
let
p^{\star}:=\frac{n-1}{n-p}p
.
Then

\|u\|_{L^{p^{\star}}(\partial B_{1})}^{p}\leq C\left(\|u\|_{L^{p}(\partial B_{1})}^{p}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}\right)

for all
u\in W^{1,p}(B_{1})
,
where
C
is a constant depending only on
n
and
p
.

Proof.

By approximation, we may assume that
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{1})
.
Recall
the standard Sobolev inequality

(B.2)

\|u\|_{L^{p_{S}}(B_{1})}^{p}\leq C\big(\|u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}\big),

where
p_{S}:=\frac{n}{n-p}p

is the Sobolev exponent
and
C
depends only on
n
and
p
.

By the divergence theorem we have

\int_{\partial B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{B_{1}}{\rm div}(x|u|^{p^{\star}})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=n\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+p^{\star}\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}-2}u(x\cdot\nabla u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

whence

(B.3)

\int_{\partial B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq n\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+p^{\star}\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}-1}|\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The last term in (
B.3
) can be bounded by the Hölder inequality as

\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{p^{\star}-1}|\nabla u|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\int_{B_{1}}|u|^{(p^{\star}-1)\frac{p}{p-1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})},

and noticing that
(p^{\star}-1)\frac{p}{p-1}=p_{S}

we deduce

(B.4)

\|u\|_{L^{p^{\star}}(\partial B_{1})}^{p^{\star}}\leq n\|u\|_{L^{p^{\star}}(B_{1})}^{p^{\star}}+p^{\star}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}\|u\|_{L^{p_{S}}(B_{1})}^{p^{\star}-1}.

Since
p^{\star}<p_{S}
,
by Hölder we have
\|u\|_{L^{p^{\star}}(B_{1})}\leq C\|u\|_{L^{p_{S}}(B_{1})}
,
and applying the Sobolev inequality (
B.2
)
in (
B.4
), we obtain the trace Sobolev inequality

(B.5)

\|u\|_{L^{p^{\star}}(\partial B_{1})}^{p}\leq C\big(\|u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}+\|\nabla u\|_{L^{p}(B_{1})}^{p}\big),

where
C
depends only on
n
and
p
.
Applying Lemma
B.1
in (
B.5
) now yields the claim.
∎

Appendix C
Two interpolation inequalities

We recall two interpolation inequalities in cubes by Cabré
[
5
]
.
The first one states that the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient
can be bounded in terms of the
L^{1}
norm of
the “Hessian times the gradient” and the
L^{2}
norm of the function.
In the second one, the
L^{2}
norm of the function is controlled by the
L^{2}
norm of the gradient and the
L^{1}
norm of the function.

Proposition C.1

(X. Cabré
[
5
]
)
.

Let
Q=(0,1)^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u\in C^{2}(\overline{Q})
.

Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq 2n\delta\|\,|\nabla u|\,D^{2}u\,\|_{L^{1}(Q)}+n\left(\frac{18}{\delta}\right)^{2}\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}.

Proposition C.2

(X. Cabré
[
5
]
)
.

Let
Q=(0,1)^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u\in C^{2}(\overline{Q})
.

Then, for every
\widetilde{\delta}\in(0,1)
,

\|u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\leq 2n^{2}\widetilde{\delta}^{2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}+2^{n+1}\widetilde{\delta}^{-n}\|u\|_{L^{1}(Q)}^{2}.

Appendix D
Absorbing errors in larger balls

We recall the following well-known lemma of Simon
[
15
]
,
which allows to absorb errors in larger balls when controlling quantities in smaller balls:

Lemma D.1

(L. Simon
[
15
]
)
.

Let
\beta\geq 0
and
C_{0}>0
.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the class of all open balls
B
contained in the unit ball
B_{1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let

\sigma\colon\mathcal{B}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
satisfy the following subadditivity property:

\sigma(B)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma(B^{j})\quad\mbox{ whenever }N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+},\{B^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{B},\text{ and }B\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}B^{j}.

It follows that there exists a constant
\delta>0
, which depends only on
n
and
\beta
, such that if

\rho^{\beta}\sigma\left(B_{\rho/2}(y)\right)\leq\delta\rho^{\beta}\sigma\left(B_{\rho}(y)\right)+C_{0}\quad\mbox{whenever }B_{\rho}(y)\subset B_{1},

then

\sigma(B_{1/2})\leq CC_{0}

for some constant
C
which depends only on
n
and
\beta
.
