Absolute Triangulation Algorithms for Space Exploration
Sébastien Henry and John A. Christian

Abstract
Images are an important source of information for spacecraft navigation and for three-dimensional reconstruction of observed space objects. Both of these applications take the form of a triangulation problem when the camera has a known attitude and the measurements extracted from the image are line of sight (LOS) directions. This work provides a comprehensive review of the history and theoretical foundations of triangulation. A variety of classical triangulation algorithms are reviewed, including a number of suboptimal linear methods (many LOS measurements) and the optimal method of Hartley and Sturm (only two LOS measurements). It is shown that the optimal many-measurement case may be solved without iteration as a linear system using the new Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) method. Both LOST and the polynomial method of Hartley and Sturm provide the same result in the case of only two measurements. The various triangulation algorithms are assessed with a few numerical examples, including planetary terrain relative navigation, angles-only optical navigation at Uranus, 3-D reconstruction of Notre-Dame de Paris, and angles-only relative navigation.



1
Introduction

Cameras, telescopes, and other optical instruments are an important source of information for modern space vehicles
[
1
]
. Information extracted from the images produced by these sensors may be used for many different purposes, with shape modeling
[
2
,
3
]
and spacecraft navigation
[
4
,
5
]
being two of the most common. In both of these applications, the objective is achieved by exploiting the geometry that relates the three-dimensional (3-D) location of objects in the observed scene to their apparent pixel locations in a digital image.

There are a variety of observables one might extract from an image and a variety of ways in which these observables may be interpreted. Perhaps the simplest situation is when one obtains line of sight (LOS) measurements from an optical instrument to a point in 3-D space. Oftentimes, the absolute location of either the camera or the observed point is known. When the camera location is known and the observed point’s location is unknown, then this is a shape modeling (i.e., 3-D reconstruction) problem. Conversely, when the observed point’s location is known and the camera location is unknown, this is a navigation problem. The geometry of these two problems is identical and the algorithms developed for one are equally applicable to the other. For the sake of brevity and clarity, this work primarily develops algorithms from the navigation perspective (though all results are also valid for 3-D reconstruction).

Point localization using a collection of LOS measurements (or angles) is generically referred to as triangulation [for the specific application of spacecraft navigation this is also sometimes called “angles-only” optical navigation (OPNAV)].
In recent years, there has been substantial interest in the utility of triangulation methods for spacecraft localization in cislunar space using sightings of other well-known satellites
[
6
,
7
]
or for interplanetary exploration using sightings of asteroids/planets
[
8
,
9
,
7
,
10
,
11
]
. The concepts of triangulation, however, apply to a much broader class of spaceflight navigation problems. For example, some of these other applications include terrain relative navigation (TRN) with known landmarks
[
12
]
, angles-only relative navigation (RelNav)
[
13
]
, and star-based interstellar navigation
[
14
]
.

This work reviews the present state of triangulation algorithms with applicability to space exploration. Special consideration is given to the relationships between the different algorithms, and some algorithms are shown to be equivalent under certain conditions. Many of the most popular methods (e.g., the Direct Linear Transform) are geometrically exact, but do not produce statistically optimal localization estimates when provided noisy LOS measurements. When there are only two LOS measurements, the classic method of Hartley and Sturm
[
15
]
finds the statistically optimal answer as the solution to a polynomial of degree six. This work shows how Hartley and Sturm’s solution can be simplified to a polynomial of degree two when all of the LOS measurements come from a single image (as happens with monocular vision-based navigation). Unfortunately, the extension of Hartley and Sturm’s method to three or more LOS measurements is not practical
[
16
]
and past work has relied on iterative numerical schemes to perform statistically optimal triangulation with many measurements. To address this problem, the new Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) method is introduced in Section
5.2
. The LOST method solves the statistically optimal triangulation problem without iteration as the solution to a linear system. When only two measurements are available the error covariance of the triangulated point is exactly the same for the LOST method and the Hartley and Sturm method. Moreover, as compared to the Hartley and Sturm method, the LOST method is faster (small linear system instead of a polynomial of degree six) and scalable (can directly find optimal solution for any number of measurements as a linear system). This work concludes with a number of examples to illustrate the similarities, advantages, and disadvantages of the various triangulation methods.

2
Background and the Lexicon of Triangulation

The idea of using optical observations to find an object’s location is not new. A sophisticated understanding of the underlying geometry was known in antiquity, and has been used for over two thousand years by navigators, cartographers, astronomers, civil engineers, and many others. Unfortunately, many critical ideas have been forgotten and rediscovered over the centuries—with this apparent forgetfulness being strangely prevalent today. Harry S. Truman famously said “There is nothing new in the world except the history you do not know.” It will soon become apparent that Truman’s astute observation certainly applies to the field of triangulation.

To begin, let the term
triangulation
refer to the task of localizing a point using the angles that describe a triangle (see Fig.
1
). In the simplest formulation there are two points of known position (forming two vertices of the triangle), such that the angles describing a triangle uniquely determine the position of an unknown point (the third vertex of the triangle). Note that the “tri” in triangulation refers to the use of triangles and not to the number of points. Thus, the term triangulation remains precise when observations of many known points are used to localize a single unknown point, since the solution then consists of many triangles.

The origins of triangulation are difficult to establish, largely due to the loss of primary sources from antiquity. Indeed, attribution of primacy seems to depend as much on whose works survived to the modern era as it does on who was truly the first to “discover” an idea. Regardless, a few things are known. The concept of using triangles to estimate distance and size was known as early as the 16th century BC, as can be seen by the problem/solution sets (especially P57-P60) in the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus
[
17
]
from the Second Intermediate Period of ancient Egypt. Sophistication of understanding increased significantly by 6th centuty BC, when the Greek mathematician Thales purportedly used triangles to estimate the height of the Egyptian pyramids and the distance to ships at sea
[
18
]
. All of the geometric principles necessary for point localization by triangulation were known by the 3rd century BC, when they were clearly laid out in Euclid’s
Elements

[
19
,
20
]
. Moreover, by the 2nd century BC, Hipparchus (amongst the first known to posses a trigonometric table
[
21
]
) used triangulation principles to estimate the distance to the Sun and Moon (as described by Ptolemy in the Almagest Book V)
[
22
,
23
]
. Thus, there can be little doubt that the classical Greek geometers were quite familiar with how triangles and a known baseline could be used to infer the location of a point at a distance. While this line of study continued to flourish in the Islamic world during the first millennium CE
[
24
]
, the knowledge was lost to European scholars for over a thousand years (until Euclid’s
Elements
was translated from Arabic into Latin in the 12th century CE
[
24
,
20
]
). The modern understanding of triangulation (e.g., Fig.
2
) can be traced to a booklet published in 1533 by Gemma Frisius
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
. Notable improvements in the practice were made by Snellius in 1617 as part of his effort to measure the circumference of the Earth
[
27
,
28
]
, and triangulation was put into a least squares framework by Gauss himself while conducting a survey of Hanover in the 1820s
[
29
,
30
]
. At a conceptual level, the work of Frisius, Snellius, and Gauss brings us to the modern era—where new sensors, computational resources, and applications have made room for additional innovations.

The modern triangulation problem takes on one of two forms: intersection or resection
[
31
]
. This is illustrated in Fig.
1
. The first form is
intersection
, which occurs when angle measurements collected by observers at two (or more) known locations are used to estimate the location of an unknown point. The intersection form of triangulation has many practical applications, such as satellite orbit determination
[
32
,
33
]
and 3-D scene reconstruction from multiple images
[
15
,
34
,
35
]
. The second form is
resection
, which occurs when an observer estimates its unknown location using angle measurements to two (or more) points at known locations. The resection form of triangulation describes the vehicle (e.g., robot, ship, aircraft, spacecraft) localization problem that commonly appears in navigation applications
[
9
,
36
]
. The intersection and resection problems have the same underlying geometry, with the primary difference being whether the triangle vertex of unknown location is the observer or is being observed.

Both the intersection and resection forms of the triangulation problem find the location of the unknown triangle vertex (denoted as
r
in Fig.
1
) relative to the locations of the known triangle vertices (denoted as
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{2}
in Fig.
1
). Thus, one typically chooses to express the known locations of
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{1}
and
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{2}
in a common frame and then triangulation usually results in an estimate of
r
this same frame. The frame in which all this occurs is generically called the
localization frame
. The choice of the localization frame depends on the application (see Section
7
for three different choices), but common examples for spacecraft localization include an inertial frame (e.g., International Celestial Reference Frame
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
), planet-fixed frame, spacecraft-fixed frame, or the orbital local-vertical, local-horizontal (LVLH) frame.

The angles in the triangulation problem often come from optical instruments.
For example, in land surveying and laboratory metrology applications the optical instrument is commonly a theodolite. In spacecraft navigation applications the angles usually come from camera or telescope images. The angles themselves are often referred to as
bearings
and come in pairs for 3-D problems (e.g., azimuth and elevation, right ascension and declination). Regardless of the application, the angle measurements (i.e., the bearings) produced by such optical instruments describe the direction from the sensor to an observed point (i.e., the direction from one triangle vertex to another). This direction corresponds to a straight line connecting two points that is commonly referred to as the
line of sight
(LOS).

Crucially important is the frame(s) in which the measured LOS directions may be expressed. Most optical instruments fundamentally measure the LOS direction in a frame fixed to the sensor, referred to here as the
sensor frame
. If the orientation of this sensor frame relative to the localization frame is known, then it is possible to transform the LOS direction from the sensor frame (where it is measured) into the localization frame (where the locations of the reference points are known). When expressed in the localization frame, these measurements are referred to as
absolute
LOS directions. For usual optical instruments, the absolute LOS is coincident with the
line of position
(LOP)—where the LOP describes a straight line in the localization frame on which the unknown point must lie. Moreover, following the terminology of Kaplan
[
40
]
, define
absolute triangulation
as triangulation performed exclusively with absolute LOS directions and having a solution at the intersection of two (or more) LOPs. Most spacecraft have excellent attitude knowledge (e.g., from star trackers
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
) and so the absolute triangulation problem occurs frequently in spaceflight applications. Absolute triangulation is the primary topic of this manuscript and numerous algorithms are presented to accomplish this task (see Sections
4
and
5
).

Although absolute triangulation is the subject of the technical discussions that follow, additional insight may be gained (and common mistakes avoided) by also understanding what absolute triangulation is not. Thus, consider the alternative case where the orientation of the optical instrument is not known and the absolute LOS directions cannot be found—instead, only the triangle interior angles may be measured. In this case, the differences between the intersection and resection forms of the triangulation problem are especially important. For example, consider a two-dimensional (2-D) system. In this example, the intersection form of the triangulation problem may be solved with a single triangle using observations of the unknown point from two known observer locations. This is because measurement of the angles
\phi
and
\psi
(see top diagram in Fig.
1
) produce two LOS directions relative to the known observer baseline that intersect at a unique 2-D point. This, however, is not the case for the resection problem with two known points. If absolute orientation is unknown and the only measurement is the angle
\theta
, then the observer’s unknown location is somewhere along a circular arc (see Fig.
3
) called the
circle of position
(COP). This is a well-known result. The observer position may be made unique in the 2-D resection problem by measuring angle pairs between three points, which is classically referred to as the
three-point resection problem
or the
Snellius-Pothenot problem
(named after Snellius’s seminal 1617 work mentioned earlier
[
28
,
27
]
). As an example, consider Fig.
4
where Snellius documented how he found the location of point
o
(roof of his house) using only measured angles
\angle yoi=32^{\circ}57^{\prime}
and
\angle you=64^{\circ}40^{\prime}
to the known points
y
(Pieterskerk),
i
(Town Hall),
u
(Hooglandse Kerk) in Leiden. Clearly shown are the two COPs (one from the baseline
yi
and the other from the baseline
yu
) that intersect at the unique location of
o
. Even after 400 years, the Snellius-Pothenot problem remains an important problem in land surveying, with various algorithms still being published today
[
44
]
.

Spacecraft and other aerospace vehicles operate in a 3-D world, and so it is instructive to expand upon the intuitive 2-D results from the Snellius-Pothenot problem.
The 3-D case of resection, where a sensor of unknown attitude lies at the point of unknown location, has sometimes been called the
space resection problem
in land surveying and photogrammetry applications. The astute roboticist, engineer, or computer scientist will recognize this as identical to the
perspective-n-point (PnP) problem
. When
n=3
one has the famous P3P problem, which admits 4 unique solutions with a direct (non-iterative) method existing as early as 1841
[
45
]
. Since then, driven in large part by interest from the computer vision community, there have been great improvements in algorithms for the P3P
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
, P4P
[
49
]
, and PnP
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
problems. Similar problems (i.e., position and attitude from LOS measurements to points) have been considered within the space community for some time
[
54
,
55
]
. Note that the PnP problem simply describes a particular means of solving the
pose estimation problem
. Pose estimation is the general problem of finding the position and attitude of an object, which may be achieved with optical observations of points (e.g., PnP problem) or with other sensors (e.g., LIDAR point cloud registration
[
56
,
57
]
). Finally, the reader should note that the pose estimation problem cannot be solved for the intersection form of the triangulation problem since the measured angles (collected by observers at the triangle’s known points) do not depend on the orientation of the unknown point. Hence, the unknown point’s location may be found, but the attitude of the object residing at the unknown point is unobservable by triangulation alone.

The triangulation problems discussed so far imply that the point of unknown location is either (1) stationary or (2) that the LOS measurements are obtained at the same instant in time. Spacecraft, however, are not stationary vehicles. Moreover, while it is possible in many situations to obtain (nearly) simultaneous LOS measurements, this is not always the case. Fortunately, the tools of triangulation may also be used for a moving observer—a concept referred to here as
dynamic triangulation
. The ideas of dynamic triangulation were pioneered for maritime applications, such as ship navigation
[
36
]
and bearings-only target motion analysis (BO-TMA)
[
58
]
. The topic gained much attention after the wide-spread deployment of radar systems in the 1940s and 1950s, where it immediately became apparent that a ship moving along a straight line cannot navigate by bearings to a single station
[
59
]
. Likewise, a single observer that is either stationary or moving along a straight line cannot uniquely determine the trajectory of a target ship with bearings alone. In these cases, it is impossible to distinguish between the object’s true trajectory and an infinite number of
homothetic trajectories
. Any one of the homothetic trajectories is related to the true trajectory by a homothety
[
60
]
, which is described by a contraction or expansion of the true trajectory about the homothetic center. Thus, the observer is left with scale ambiguity on the trajectory, as illustrated on Fig.
5
. The usual solution is to collect LOS observations from multiple stations or to maneuver the observer
[
61
,
62
]
.

If the motion between two particles is governed by linear dynamics, then then LOS measurements from one particle to the other can be explained by an infinite number of homothetic trajectories.

Dynamic triangulation plays a central role in spacecraft “angles-only” relative navigation (RelNav). Recall that two spacecraft in near circular orbits that are close to one another exhibit nearly linear dynamics (e.g., as described with the Clohessy-Wiltshire equations
[
63
]
). Due to the linear dynamics, the exact same observability challenges found for the BO-TMA problem were found to exist for problems of spacecraft angles-only RelNav
[
64
,
13
]
. Unsurprisingly, the solutions (e.g., maneuvering the observer) for space applications are largely the same as for maritime applications. Indeed, many of the novel approaches for spacecraft angles-only RelNav are actually rediscoveries of the dynamic triangulation techniques known for decades within the context maritime BO-TMA.

3
Projective Geometry, Camera Models, and Images

Proper development and analysis of triangulation methods requires a model of the instrument used to acquire the LOS measurements. This work assumes an optical instrument obeying projective geometry and its mathematical representation is referred to here as the
camera model
. The subsections that follow first develop the camera model from the perspective of algebraic projective geometry
[
65
]
and then consider the measurement statistics.

3.1
Ideal Camera Model

Suppose a spacecraft at position
r
views an object located as position
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
with an optical instrument. In many cases the sensor only provides a measurement of the LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}

(1)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
is of arbitrary scale and describes a point in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. For additional information on projective space (e.g.,
{\mathbb{P}}^{2},{\mathbb{P}}^{3},{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
), see Refs.
[
5
,
50
,
66
]
. The relationship in Eq. (
1
) is true in any consistent frame.

When the optical instrument is a camera (or telescope) obeying projective geometry, the LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
is produced by back-projecting a ray out of the camera that corresponds to a specific pixel coordinate
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{T}_{i}=[u_{i},v_{i}]
in a digital image.
Although formation of the LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
may be accomplished in any frame, it is convenient to choose the sensor frame (which, in this case, is the
camera frame
). The most popular camera frame convention—and the one chosen here—places the origin at the center of the camera’s entrance pupil (i.e., apparent pinhole location to an observer outside the camera). The camera frame
z

- and

[
5
]
that aligns the

-axis with the image

y
-axis with the image
v
-direction. Details and diagrams are provided in Ref.
[
5
]
.

Now, in the case where the camera attitude is known, it is possible to express everything in the camera frame. Different normalizations of the direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
in the camera frame (denoted by the subscript
C
) describe the pinhole camera geometry
[
5
]
, such that

\begin{bmatrix}x_{i}\\
y_{i}\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{a}}_{C_{i}}\propto\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}\propto\textbf{\emph{p}}_{C_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{C}

(2)

where
\textbf{\emph{a}}_{C_{i}}=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}/\|\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}\|
is a unit vector pointing from the camera to the observed object and where
\textbf{\emph{x}}^{T}_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i}]
are the 2-D coordinates where the direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}
pierces the camera frame’s
z=1
plane (which is sometimes called the image plane). The 3-D camera frame coordinates of the

\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}
written in homogeneous coordinates, which is denoted here as
\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}^{T}_{i}=[\textbf{\emph{x}}^{T}_{i},1]

Contemporary space cameras most commonly produce a digital image. A 2-D point
\textbf{\emph{x}}^{T}_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i}]

\textbf{\emph{u}}^{T}_{i}=[u_{i},v_{i}]

(4)

In most cases

and this transformation simply shifts the origin (
u_{p}
and

) and converts from units of length to units of pixels (

3.2
LOS Representations and Measurement Models

The choice of LOS representation and its covariance model is one of the most important decisions the designer of a triangulation algorithm must make. A good choice will often allow for an elegant and statistically optimal solution. Conversely, a poor choice will lead to a complicated (and usually iterative) solution.

The most common choice of LOS representation for spacecraft navigation is a unit vector. The popularity of this choice can be traced to the great success of unit vectors for star-based attitude determination. The problem of spacecraft attitude determination from unit vectors in correspondence was posed by Grace Wahba in 1965
[
69
]
and a number of elegant solutions have been proposed since then
[
70
,
71
,
72
,
43
]
. These algorithms are the workhorse of modern star-based attitude determination and play a central role in commercially available star trackers.

Proceeding in a similar way, consider the LOS direction represented by the unit vector
a
. Let the noisy measurement of that unit vector be
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{a}}}=\textbf{\emph{a}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}
, where
\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\textbf{0}_{3\times 1},\textbf{\emph{R}}_{QMM})
. In what is now known as the QUEST measurement model (QMM), the covariance of a unit vector (to first order) with isotropic direction uncertainty is
[
71
]

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{QMM}=E[\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{T}]=\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\left(\textbf{\emph{I}}_{3\times 3}-\textbf{\emph{a}}\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}\right)

(6)

where
\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
is the variance of the unit vector pointing direction (in radians). This is true regardless of the frame in which
a
is expressed. Since both
a
and
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{a}}}
are constrained to be unit vectors, the covariance matrix
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{QMM}
is rank two and describes a plane tangent to the unit sphere at the point
a
. Importantly, solutions to Wahba’s problem are known to provide a maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of the attitude when unit vector measurements are assumed to follow the QMM
[
73
]
. This justifiably led to widespread adoption of the unit vector and QMM convention.

Building on past successes with spacecraft attitude determination, it would seem logical to represent LOS directions for spacecraft triangulation with the unit vector
a
and covariance from Eq. (
6
). However, these are generally poor choices in practice. There are two problems. The first problem is that the QMM constrains
\boldsymbol{\eta}
to lie on the unit sphere’s tangent plane such that
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{a}}}
is not a unit vector (specifically,
\|\tilde{\textbf{\emph{a}}}\|>1
). Although it is a second-order effect, these difficulties with additive noise are well-known and have led some to consider a multiplicative error model
[
74
]
. Avoiding the (usually) artificial unit vector normalization eliminates the need for the multiplicative error model. The second (and more important) problem is that the QMM from Eq. (
6
) is not the correct noise model when the sensor is a camera or telescope.

To build the correct error covariance for an LOS measurement, begin by considering how these measurements are constructed from a digital image.
Recall that LOS directions originate as image points with pixel coordinates
\{u_{i},v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. Depending on the type of object and image processing technique used (e.g., centroiding of an unresolved celestial body
[
75
]
, cross-correlation of a terrain maplet
[
2
,
76
]
, localization of a SIFT keypoint
[
77
]
), there will be some uncertainty in the pixel coordinates
[u_{i},v_{i}]
used to create the LOS measurement. Therefore, let the pixel coordinate
\textbf{\emph{u}}^{T}_{i}=[u_{i},v_{i}]
produce the camera frame LOS measurement
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}
using Eq. (
5
). Describe the zero-mean, Gaussian measurement noise in the image point location by
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}})
, such that

\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(7)

where the measurement covariance
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}=E[\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{T}]
is a
2\times 2
matrix.
Thus, following Eq. (
5
),

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{C_{i}}\propto\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}+\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(8)

where

\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{u}_{i}\\
\tilde{v}_{i}\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}=\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}+\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(9)

\textbf{\emph{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{I}}_{2\times 2}&\textbf{0}_{2\times 1}\end{bmatrix}

(10)

Moreover, defining
\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}
, one has

\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}=\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}+\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}

(11)

Thus, making use of Eq. (
8
), it follows that the error covariance of
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}
is

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}=E[\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}\textbf{\emph{w}}^{T}_{i}]=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{S}}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-T}

(12)

Since
\textbf{\emph{x}}=\textbf{\emph{S}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}
, the
2\times 2
covariance on the image plane is

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}=\textbf{\emph{S}}\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}=\textbf{\emph{S}}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{S}}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-T}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}

(13)

Finally, as was shown in Ref.
[
78
]
, the covariance of the unit vector LOS direction is given by

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}}=\left(\frac{\partial\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}}{\partial\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\right)\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\left(\frac{\partial\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}}{\partial\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\right)^{T}

(14)

where the Jacobian
\partial\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}/\partial\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}
is the
3\times 2
matrix

\frac{\partial\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}}{\partial\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}=\left(\frac{1}{\|\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\|}\textbf{\emph{I}}_{3\times 3}-\frac{1}{\|\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\|^{3}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}^{T}_{i}\right)\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}=\frac{-1}{\|\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\|}\left[\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}\times\right]^{2}\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}

(15)

and where
\left[\,\cdot\,\times\right]
is the skew-symmetric cross-product matrix,
\left[\textbf{\emph{a}}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{b}}=\textbf{\emph{a}}\times\textbf{\emph{b}}
.
It was shown in Ref.
[
78
]
that
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{QMM}\geq\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{a}}}
, suggesting that the QMM provides a conservative bound of the measurement uncertainty. However, to use the QMM is to assume measurement statistics that are inconsistent with the optical instrument’s actual behavior—ultimately leading to reduced state estimation performance.

The development of the triangulation methods that follow can be equivalently achieved with any normalization of the LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
and its corresponding error covariance. The authors prefer to use
\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
in almost all cases, since this is often easier than working with the unit vector
\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}
.

4
Trigonometric Solutions

Simple trigonometric relations may be used to develop two of the classical solutions to the triangulation problem. Referred to here as the
trigonometric solutions
, one solution method follows from the Law of Sines and the other follows from the Law of Cosines. Both of these solutions have found widespread use in computer vision.

In the discussions that follow, the development for both of the trigonometric solutions are first presented from a vector analysis perspective. This is done since the vector interpretation has clear algorithmic advantages. These solutions are then shown to be equivalent to either the Law of Sines or Law of Cosines. The reader is reminded that idea behind the Law of Sines was understood by the classical Greek geometers (Euclid’s
Elements
, Prop. I.18 and I.19
[
19
]
) and was known in its modern form by at least the 13th century CE (when it appears in the work of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, “Treatise on the Quadrilateral”
[
79
]
). Similarly, the idea underpinning the Law of Cosines is also found in Euclid’s
Elements
(Prop II.12 and II.13
[
19
]
) and appeared in its modern form by at least the 15th century CE (notably in the work Jamshīd al-Kāshī
[
80
]
). Thus, while the vector notation used here is modern, the fundamental relationships necessary to construct the trigonometric solutions to the triangulation problem would have been quite familiar to classical Greek, medieval Islamic, and Renaissance European geometers.

The trigonometric solutions are geometrically exact and produce a perfect solution when provided perfect (noise free) measurements. They have the advantage of not requiring any
a priori
position knowledge, admitting a closed-form solution, and being easily scalable to many (
n>2
) measurements (with the methods based on Law of Sines being considerably better at scaling than the Law of Cosines). The classical trigonometric solutions both have the disadvantage of being statistically suboptimal when challenged with noisy measurements obtained from a camera, telescope, or other optical sensor. This suboptimality arises because they are conventionally written as an ordinary (unweighted) least squares problems, though the Law of Sines method may be easily modified to overcome this drawback (see Section
5.2
). Finally, under consistent normalization assumptions with two LOS measurements (
n=2
), the two trigonometric solutions produce identical results. Thus, of the two methods, the Law of Sines approach is usually preferred since it is simpler and scales better with many LOS measurements.

The discussions that follow are primarily written from the perspective of a resection problem (e.g., spacecraft navigation). If desired, a simple change in notation will transform these results into the intersection problem (e.g., 3-D reconstruction).

4.1
Law of Sines: The Direct Linear Transform (DLT)

Optical instruments such as cameras and telescopes measure LOS directions
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}
. However, the distance between the instrument and the observed object along that LOS direction is not directly measured—thus giving rise to the proportional relationship in Eq. (
1
). Rather than attempting to explicitly solve for these unknown distances, it is sometimes easier to remove them entirely by use of the Direct Linear Transform (DLT)
[
50
,
81
]
. In recent years, this approach has been considered for both camera-based navigation with planetary landmarks (a resection problem)
[
82
]
and for orbit determination from ground-based telescope observations (an intersection problem)
[
40
]
.

4.1.1
Development by Vector Analysis

The DLT is the simplest of the triangulation algorithms and its development consists of a few steps. To begin, consider the case where one obtains
n\geq 2
LOS measurements
\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
to points with known 3-D locations
\{\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
. Now, to remove the scale (i.e., range) ambiguity in Eq. (
1
), take the cross-product of both sides with the known LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}\right)=\textbf{0}_{3\times 1}

(16)

Both Eq. (
1
) and Eq. (
16
) are true in any consistent frame, and so no frame-specific notation is required. Thus, after simplification,

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{r}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(17)

If many LOS measurements
\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are available, they may be stacked into a linear system as

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}\times\right]\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{2}\times\right]\\
\vdots\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{n}\times\right]\end{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{1}\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(18)

This linear system may be solved in the least squares sense for the estimated position
\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}
. The DLT equations [e.g., Eq. (
17
) or Eq. (
18
)] are the same for both resection and intersection forms of the triangulation problem since
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}\propto\textbf{\emph{r}}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
. Though Eq. (
18
) is true in any frame, some frame choices have algorithmic advantages in certain situations.

It is desirable for a triangulation algorithm to produce a consistent solution regardless of the choice of localization frame
[
15
]
. This condition is satisfied for the DLT regardless of the frame chosen. To see this, suppose that one knows the locations of the
n
observed points
\{\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as expressed in some localization frame denoted by
I
. Further suppose the triangulation
\tau
produces the solution
\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}=\tau(\{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{I_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n})
, where
\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}^{T}=[\textbf{\emph{r}}^{T}_{I},1]
and
\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}^{T}_{I_{i}}=[\textbf{\emph{p}}^{T}_{I_{i}},1]
. If
\textbf{\emph{M}}^{I}_{A}
is a
4\times 4
matrix describing a general frame transformation (rotation and translation) between two different localization frames
I
and
A
, then a consistent triangulation solution would imply
\textbf{\emph{M}}^{I}_{A}\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}=\tau(\{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\textbf{\emph{M}}^{I}_{A}\{\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{I_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n})
. Simple substitution will show this condition to be satisfied for Eq. (
18
).

Since almost any reasonable frame convention may be used with Eq. (
18
), a simple convention is usually best. As an example, the specific convention preferred by the authors is now provided. Following the sensor model from Section
3.1
, the LOS measurements originate as pixel coordinates
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
in a digital image. These image points produce LOS measurements in the camera frame as described by Eq. (
5
). Recognizing that different LOS measurements may come from different cameras, denote the camera frame producing image point
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
as
C_{i}
and the corresponding camera calibration matrix as
\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}
. If all the measurements come from one image, then they all come from the same camera and
C=C_{i},\,\textbf{\emph{K}}=\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}\;\forall i
. Thus, taking the most straightforward approach, one may write

\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}

(19)

where
\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}
is the attitude transformation matrix that transforms a vector expressed in frame
I
to that same vector expressed in frame
C_{i}
. The result in Eq. (
19
) makes use of the LOS normalization
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}\propto\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
. Although any LOS normalization may be used, some care is required since (re)normalization acts as a weighting on the affected rows of the linear system from Eq. (
18
). Inconsistent normalization can result in inconsistent weighting of the measurements, which can result in poor triangulation performance. This observation is the motivation for the statistically optimal algorithm presented in Section
5.2
.

If many image points
\{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are available, the corresponding vector equations from Eq. (
19
) may be stacked into a linear system as

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{1}}\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{n}}\end{bmatrix}

(20)

Similar to Eq. (
18
), the linear system in Eq. (
20
) may be solved in the least squares sense (or total least squares sense) for the estimated position
\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}
. An analytic expression for the covariance of this position estimate is given in the Appendix.

4.1.2
Relationship to Law of Sines

It was shown in Ref.
[
82
]
that the key relation from Eq. (
17
) is simply the Law of Sines in disguise. Making use of the angles from Fig.
6
, write the Law of Sines for the triangle formed by the localization frame origin,
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
, and
r
,

Figure 6:
The DLT is a vector representation of the Law of Sines using angles
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
.

\frac{\sin\alpha_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{r}}\|}=\frac{\sin\beta_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\|}

(21)

which is the same as

\|\textbf{\emph{r}}\|\sin\beta_{i}=\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\|\sin\alpha_{i}

(22)

This expression is true regardless of the frame in which the vector quantities are expressed. Recalling that
\|\textbf{\emph{a}}\times\textbf{\emph{b}}\|=\|\textbf{\emph{a}}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{b}}\|\sin\theta
and accounting for the sign of the cross-product,

\|\textbf{\emph{r}}\|\sin\beta_{i}=\|\textbf{\emph{r}}\|\frac{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{r}}}{\|\textbf{\emph{r}}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\|}=\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\|\frac{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}}{\|\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\|}=\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\|\sin\alpha_{i}

(23)

Simplifying the middle two terms yields

\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{r}}=\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(24)

which is exactly the same as Eq. (
17
). Hence, the familiar DLT solution is a vector representation of the Law of Sines.

4.1.3
Relationship to the Collinearity Equations

There are other ways of representing the triangulation problem as a linear system, and some of these other representations are the same as the DLT. For example, consider triangulation with the collinearity equations.

To arrive at the collinearity equations, rewrite the pinhole camera model from Eq. (
2
) in homogeneous coordinates and remove the proportionality constraint by introducing the scaling
\alpha_{i}\neq 0

\alpha_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})

(25)

or, equivalently,

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{i}x_{i}\\
\alpha_{i}y_{i}\\
\alpha_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}&\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}\\
1\end{bmatrix}

(26)

Define the rows of
\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}
as

\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}\\
\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\\
\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\end{bmatrix}

(27)

and the elements of
\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}
as

\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{1}&p_{2}&p_{3}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(28)

The three rows of the pinhole camera model are now

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}x_{i}

\displaystyle=-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{1}

(29a)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}y_{i}

\displaystyle=-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{2}

(29b)

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{3}

(29c)

Dividing Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
) by Eq. (
29c
) gives the classical collinearity equations seen in textbooks and widely-used in a variety of applications (e.g., the so-called CAHVOR model for planetary photogrammetry
[
83
,
84
]
)

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{1}}{-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{3}}=\frac{\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}{\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}

(30a)

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{2}}{-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{3}}=\frac{\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}{\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}

(30b)

Within the context of triangulation, the collinearity equations may be used to construct a linear system for
r
. To do this, substitute Eq. (
29c
) into Eqs. (
29a
) and (
29b
)

\displaystyle x_{i}(-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{3})

\displaystyle=-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{1}

(31a)

\displaystyle y_{i}(-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{3})

\displaystyle=-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}+p_{2}

(31b)

Rearranging this pair of equations and stacking them into matrix form yields (writing the equation for
y_{i}
first) a linear system that may be solved for
\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}

\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\\
\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}-x_{i}\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}p_{3}-p_{2}\\
p_{1}-x_{i}p_{3}\end{bmatrix}

(32)

Which is easily verified to be the same as

\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}\\
\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-x_{i}\textbf{\emph{t}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}p_{3}-p_{2}\\
p_{1}-x_{i}p_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}

(33)

where
S
is from Eq. (
10
). Hence, comparing this result to Eq. (
19
) and recalling that
\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
, the collinearty equations for each measured image point are exactly the first two rows of the corresponding part of the DLT. Since the skew-symmetric matrix
\left[\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}\times\right]
is only rank two, the third row is redundant (linearly dependent on the first two). Thus, solving the DLT is equivalent to solving the collinearity equations (and vice versa).

4.1.4
Relationship to Plücker Coordinates

Given the algebraic projective geometry framework considered here, it is logical to consider triangulation through the use of Plücker coordinates. Introduced in 1865
[
85
]
, the so-called Plücker coordinates are a way of representing a 3-D line in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
using the ratios between six parameters (a point in
{\mathbb{P}}^{5}
). This representation of 3-D lines has become popular for a variety of computer vision applications
[
50
,
86
]
.

The LOS direction
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
is formed by the line passing through the origin (camera location in the camera frame) and the camera-relative point location
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}
. The corresponding LOP is a 3-D line (not necessarily passing through the origin) passing through two points
\textbf{\emph{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Therefore, consider the triangulation problem as expressed in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. In this case, let the spacecraft position be denoted by
\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}^{T}=[\textbf{\emph{r}}^{T},1]
and the known point’s position be denoted by
\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{i}^{T}=[\textbf{\emph{r}}^{T}_{i},1]
. Describe the LOP as the line in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
formed by the join of the points
\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
and
\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
, which may be written directly using the Plücker matrix
\textbf{\emph{L}}_{i}

[
50
]

\textbf{\emph{L}}_{i}\propto\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{i}^{T}-\bar{\textbf{\emph{p}}}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}^{T}

(34)

The present application requires the dual representation
\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}
, which may be computed as

\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}\propto\begin{bmatrix}\left[(\textbf{\emph{r}}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i})\times\right]&(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{r}})\\
(\textbf{\emph{r}}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i})^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}

(35)

Since the same line is formed by any two points along the line, one may use the relation from Eq. (
1
) to find

\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}\propto\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]&(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\times\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i})\\
(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i})^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}

(36)

This solution is valid in any frame, and one may easily choose to express this problem in the camera frame, localization frame, or any other frame.
Now, recall that any point
\bar{\textbf{\emph{q}}}^{T}\propto[\textbf{\emph{q}}^{T},1]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
lies on the line when
\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{q}}}=\textbf{0}_{4\times 1}
. Thus, it follows that

\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}=\textbf{0}_{4\times 1}

(37)

The matrix
\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{i}
has rank two. Hence when
n\geq 2
unique LOS measurements are available one may construct the linear system

\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{\emph{L}}^{\ast}_{n}\end{bmatrix}\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}=\textbf{0}_{4n\times 1}

(38)

The result in Eq. (
38
) is a null space problem and the least squares solution for
\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}
may be found directly via the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). The resulting
\hat{\bar{\textbf{\emph{r}}}}\in{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
is a
4\times 1
vector with ambiguous scale (it represents the null space direction) and one may recover the camera location
\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by scaling the fourth component to unity.

It is now observed that the Plücker coordinate solution shown here is the same as the DLT solution. To see this, begin by noting that the first three rows of Eq. (
37
) are

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]&(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\times\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}\\
1\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{0}_{3\times 1}

(39)

which is the same as

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{r}}+(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\times\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i})=\textbf{0}_{3\times 1}

(40)

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{r}}=\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(41)

which, in turn, is identical to the foundational DLT expression from Eq. (
17
). Moreover, the fourth row in Eq. (
37
) is linearly dependent on the first three rows and simply says that
r
must lie in the plane spanned by the origin and the 3D LOS (i.e., the line connecting
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
and
r
). To arrive at this result, observe that the normal to this plane as expressed in the camera frame is given by
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
. Since the camera location
r
lies along the LOS, it must lie in this plane. Hence
(\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i})^{T}\textbf{\emph{r}}=0
, which is the fourth row in Eq. (
37
).

4.2
Law of Cosines: Explicit Range Estimation

The missing piece of information for each measurement is the unknown range
\rho_{i}=\|\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}\|
, and so it is understandable that one might wish to explicitly estimate these unknown ranges. Following this line of reasoning, the unknown scale in Eq. (
1
) may be made explicit by introducing the range,

\rho_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}\propto\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}

(42)

which is true in any consistent frame. Solving the triangulation problem by finding the unknown ranges
\{\rho_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
has been suggested within the context of spacecraft navigation by a number or authors
[
8
,
87
,
11
]
.

4.2.1
Development by Vector Analysis

As before, consider the case where one obtains
n\geq 2
LOS measurements
\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
to points with known 3-D locations
\{\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
. Choose to normalize the LOS measurements to be the unit vectors
\{\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
. Thus, by rearrangement of Eq. (
42
),

\textbf{\emph{r}}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\rho_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}

(43)

where the objective is to find
r
without any
a priori
knowledge of
\rho_{i}
.
Since
r
is the same for all measurements, the relation from Eq. (
43
) may be used to equate any pair of measurements
i\neq j
,

\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\rho_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{r}}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{j}-\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(44)

which can be rearranged to find

\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}-\rho_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{j}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(45)

Now, let
\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}
be the vector from
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
to
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{j}
,

\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{j}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(46)

Substituting for
\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}
and left-multiplying by either
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}
or
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{j}
leads to a pair of equations that are linear in the unknown ranges
\rho_{i}
and
\rho_{j}

(\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j})\rho_{j}-\rho_{i}=\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}

(47)

\rho_{j}-(\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i})\rho_{i}=\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{j}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}

(48)

It is possible to construct such a pair of equations for every
\{i,j\}
satisfying

, ultimately producing a total of
2\binom{n}{2}
relationships with
n
unknown range values. The resulting equations may be stacked to create a linear least squares problem that may be solved for the
n
unknown ranges. For example, the case of
n=3
leads to the following linear system

\begin{bmatrix}-1&\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{2}&0\\
-\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{1}&1&0\\
-1&0&\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{3}\\
-\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{1}&0&1\\
0&-1&\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{3}\\
0&-\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{2}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}\\
\rho_{2}\\
\rho_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{12}}\\
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{12}}\\
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{1}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{13}}\\
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{13}}\\
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{23}}\\
\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{3}\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{23}}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(49)

which may be solved in the least squares sense for the estimated ranges
\{\hat{\rho}_{i}\}^{n}_{i=1}
. Of note is that Eq. (
49
) is true in any consistent frame.

With the ranges known, it is possible to solve for

directly using Eq. (
43

\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\hat{\rho}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{I_{i}}\right)

An analytic expression for the covariance of this position estimate is given in the Appendix.

4.2.2
Relationship to Law of Cosines

It may be shown that Eq. (
47
) and Eq. (
48
) [and, hence, the rows of the linear system in Eq. (
49
)] are nothing more than the Law of Cosines in disguise. Therefore, begin by writing the Law of Cosines as for each of the interior angles belonging to the triangle described by edges
\rho_{i}
,
\rho_{j}
, and
\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|
:

\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|^{2}=\rho_{i}^{2}+\rho_{j}^{2}-2\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(51)

\rho_{i}^{2}=\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|^{2}+\rho_{j}^{2}-2\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}_{ij}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(52)

\rho_{j}^{2}=\rho_{i}^{2}+\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|^{2}+2\rho_{i}\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}_{ij}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}

(53)

Use Eq. (
51
) to substitute for
\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|
in Eq. (
52
) to find

\rho_{i}^{2}=\left(\rho_{i}^{2}+\rho_{j}^{2}-2\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}\right)+\rho_{j}^{2}-2\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}_{ij}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(54)

Next, after eliminating
\rho^{2}_{i}
,

2\rho_{j}^{2}-2\rho_{i}\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}=2\rho_{j}\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}_{ij}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(55)

This simplifies to

\rho_{j}-\rho_{i}(\textbf{\emph{a}}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j})=\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}_{ij}\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}

(56)

and is identical to Eq. (
48
). Likewise, substitution of Eq. (
51
) into Eq. (
53
) will reproduce Eq. (
47
). Hence, the explicit range solution shown here is a vector representation of the Law of Cosines.

4.2.3
Difficulty with Scalability

Compared with other triangulation methods, the explicit range method does not scale well with a growing number of LOS measurements.
In this method, the length of the state vector is dependent on the number of LOS measurements since the unknown range must be estimated for each measurement.
Moreover, because the explicit range method depends on measurement pairs, the number of equations grows as
2\binom{n}{2}
—which is nearly exponential for
n\gg 2
—when every pairwise LOS combination is considered. For example, the consideration of only 20 LOS measurements would require the solution to a least squares problem with a matrix of size
380\times 20
. To consider 192 measurements (which is the model point with the maximum number of measurements in the 3-D reconstruction example in Section
7.3
) leads to a matrix of size
36,672\times 192
. At best, the explicit range method would require inversion of a
192\times 192
matrix in this example—as compared to inversion of only a
3\times 3
matrix for either the DLT (Section
4.1
) or the statistically optimal LOST algorithm (Section
5.2
). This rapid growth in problem size makes the explicit range estimation method comparatively undesirable for some applications.

4.3
Comparison of the Trigonometric Solutions

Both of the trigonometric solutions are exact when provided perfect measurements. Interestingly, when provided with noisy measurements, both the DLT method (Section
4.1
) and the explicit range method (Section
4.2
) produce an identical estimate of
\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}
when a consistent LOS normalization is used with two LOS measurements (
n=2
).
Specifically, this occurs when the LOS measurements in the DLT are scaled to be unit vectors [i.e., choose
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}
in Eq. (
18
)]. This can be shown analytically for
two LOS
measurements, and a proof is provided in the Appendix. The results can also be observed in numerical experiments (see Section
7.1
, especially Fig.
11
). As compared to the DLT solution suggested in Eq. (
20
), this normalization weights points near the image center more and points near the image edges less (since
\|\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\|^{-1}
is larger near the image center). While point localization performance may indeed be better near the image center and worse near the image edges (e.g., empirical focal plane model from Ref.
[
88
]
), the relative weighting of these observations should be derived from the measurement statistics and not from an arbitrary normalization scheme. Proper accounting of this fact tends to favor the DLT solution over the explicit range solution.

From a practical standpoint, there are a few reasons to prefer the DLT solution over the explicit range solution. The DLT is (1) much simpler, (2) scales more elegantly with many measurements, and (3) allows for easy control of the normalization scheme. The DLT also forms the foundation for statistically optimal triangulation (i.e., LOST algorithm in Section
5.2
) and dynamic triangulation (Section
6
). For these reasons, the DLT method (Law of Sines method) is almost always preferable to the explicit range method (Law of Cosines method).

5
Optimal Triangulation

The trigonometric solutions from Section
4
do not usually provide a statistically optimal estimate of the unknown point’s location. Though both of these solutions do solve a least squares optimization problem, they do not minimize the correct cost function. Proper formulation of the cost function requires consideration how LOS measurement errors are related to state estimation errors and a realization that some measurements provide better information than others. One appropriate way to proceed is with a maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) framework
[
89
,
90
]
. The two-measurement case admits some interesting optimal solutions, though the classical approach requires finding the roots of a polynomial of degree six
[
15
]
. After first reviewing this special case, a more general approach—referred to here as the Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) algorithm—is shown to provide an equivalent estimate as the solution to a linear system. The new LOST algorithm is generally preferable to the classical polynomial solutions in that (1) it provides an equivalent solution without the need to solve a degree six polynomial and (2) it provides the MLE for an arbitrary number (
n\geq 2
) of LOS measurements as the solution to a linear system (with no iteration).

5.1
Two LOS Measurements: The Polynomial Methods

At least two absolute LOS measurements are necessary to triangulate the location of a camera and some interesting algorithms arise when exactly two LOS measurements exist. There are two different scenarios to consider here. The first scenario is when the two LOS measurements are collected from different cameras and extracted from different images, and so the two measured points generally lie in two different image planes. The second scenario is when both of the LOS measurements are collected by the same camera and extracted from the same image, and so the two measured points lie in the same image plane. These two scenarios lead to two different (but equivalent) optimal algorithms. The solution for two images involves solving a polynomial of degree six, while the solution for a single image involves solving only a quadratic equation. A small numerical example is provided in the supplemental material for this article.

Consider a collection of LOS measurements to
n
objects that were produced by optical instruments.
Applying the measurement model from Section
3.2
,

\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{S}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}=h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})=\frac{\textbf{\emph{S}}\textbf{\emph{K}}\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}{\textbf{\emph{k}}^{T}\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})}

(57)

and a noisy measurement of

\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}=h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})+\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}

(58)

As before, assuming
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\textbf{0}_{2\times 1},\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}})
and that the additive noise on measurements
i
and
j
are uncorrelated (i.e.,
E[\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}]=\textbf{0}_{2\times 2}
), the MLE statement is

\max\;J(\textbf{\emph{r}})=C\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]^{T}\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]\right\}

(59)

which is the same as the minimization problem

\min\;J(\textbf{\emph{r}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]^{T}\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]

(60)

Thus, the MLE solution seeks to find the location
r
that minimizes the reprojection errors as weighted by the measurement covariance.
In many cases, the image point errors are isotropic and the measurement covariance is
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\approx\sigma^{2}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{I}}_{2\times 2}
. In this case,

\min\;J(\textbf{\emph{r}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}^{2}}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]^{T}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}-h_{i}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\right]

(61)

Moreover, when there are only two LOS measurements (
n=2
) and assuming similar uncertainty in each measurement (i.e.,
\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{1}}=\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{2}}
), the cost function simplifies further to

\min_{\textbf{\emph{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\;J(\textbf{\emph{r}})=\|\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}-h_{1}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\|^{2}+\|\tilde{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{2}-h_{2}(\textbf{\emph{r}})\|^{2}

(62)

which is simply the sum of the squared reprojection errors. Since the two reprojected points
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{1}=h_{1}(\textbf{\emph{r}})
and
\textbf{\emph{u}}_{2}=h_{2}(\textbf{\emph{r}})
must satisfy the epipolar constraint, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min\;J(u_{1},v_{1},u_{2},v_{2})=

\displaystyle\left[(\tilde{u}_{1}-u_{1})^{2}+(\tilde{v}_{1}-v_{1})^{2}\right]+\left[(\tilde{u}_{2}-u_{2})^{2}+(\tilde{v}_{2}-v_{2})^{2}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{F}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}=0

where
F
is the fundamental matrix. This is the cost function suggested in the majority of the contemporary literature when seeking an “optimal” triangulation with two LOS measurements
[
15
]
.

Further, for a calibrated camera with a known calibration matrix
K
, the cost function may be rewritten in terms of the image plane coordinates. Assuming square pixels (
d_{x}=d_{y}
) and no detector skewness (
\alpha=0
) the matrix
K
simplifies and

\displaystyle\min\;J(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})

\displaystyle=w_{1}\left[(\tilde{x}_{1}-x_{1})^{2}+(\tilde{y}_{1}-y_{1})^{2}\right]+w_{2}\left[(\tilde{x}_{2}-x_{2})^{2}+(\tilde{y}_{2}-y_{2})^{2}\right]

(64)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}^{T}_{2}\textbf{\emph{E}}\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{1}=0

where
w_{i}=1/\sigma^{2}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}=d^{2}_{x_{i}}/\sigma^{2}_{\textbf{\emph{u}}_{i}}
and where
E
is the essential matrix. Note that this form has reintroduced the possibility of a different uncertainty for the two different LOS measurements. This cost function can be used to produce optimal triangulation algorithms for special case of two LOS measurements.

5.1.1
Measurements from Different Images

Consider the situation where each LOS measurement comes from a different image. This is a necessary condition for the intersection form of the triangulation problem. The situation is less common for resection, but does still occur for a few important problems (e.g., triangulation with two objects whose angular separation is larger than a single optical instrument’s field of view). The optimal solution for the intersection problem was developed by Hartley and Sturm in 1997
[
15

and involves finding the roots of a polynomial of degree six. It is possible to show that the resection problem is identical to the intersection problem, and that the optimal solution from Ref.

The first task is to establish the equivalence between the intersection and resection form of the two-image triangulation problem. This may be accomplished by considering the geometry illustrated in Fig.

5.1.2
Measurements from the Same Image

A simpler solution is possible in the case when measurements come from the same camera (for resection) or from two cameras of the same attitude (for intersection). Under these conditions the optimal two-LOS triangulation problem may be solved by the solution to a quadratic equation (polynomial of degree two), instead of the usual polynomial of degree six from Section
5.1.1
. This reduction to a quadratic equation has been known since at least the mid-1990s, when it was discussed in early work by both Armstrong
[
92
]
and Sturm
[
93
]
. What follows is a different—but ultimately equivalent—derivation of this fact.

To simplify the discussion, and without loss of generality, focus on the resection form of the triangulation problem. To find the triangulation constraint, first recognize that
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C}
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{2}
, and
\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{12}}
are linearly dependent and all lie in a common plane. Thus, since the vector
\textbf{\emph{d}}_{{12}}\times\boldsymbol{\ell}_{2}
is perpendicular to the this plane, it follows that

\boldsymbol{\ell}^{T}_{1}\left[\textbf{\emph{d}}_{12}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\ell}_{2}=0

(80)

Recalling that in the camera frame
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}\propto\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}
, one may also write

\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{1}^{T}\left[\textbf{\emph{d}}_{C_{12}}\times\right]\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{2}=0

(81)

which is the same thing as the epipolar constraint. Recalling
\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}^{T}_{i}=[x_{i},y_{i},1]
and defining
\textbf{\emph{d}}^{T}=[d,e,f]
, the constraint develops as

\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{1}^{T}\left[\textbf{\emph{d}}_{C_{12}}\times\right]\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{2}=x_{1}\left(e-fy_{2}\right)+y_{1}\left(fx_{2}-d\right)+\left(dy_{2}-ex_{2}\right)=0

(82)

Thus, the triangulation (epipolar) constraint may be adjoined to the cost function from Eq. (
64
) with a Lagrange multiplier,
\lambda

\min\;J(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2},\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}w_{i}\left[(\tilde{x}_{i}-x_{i})^{2}+(\tilde{y}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}\right]+\lambda\left(\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{1}^{T}\left[\textbf{\emph{d}}_{C_{12}}\times\right]\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{2}\right)

(83)

Applying the first differential condition yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial x_{1}}=2w_{1}\left(x_{1}-\tilde{x}_{1}\right)-\lambda\left(fy_{2}-e\right)=0

(84a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial y_{1}}=2w_{1}\left(y_{1}-\tilde{y}_{1}\right)+\lambda\left(fx_{2}-d\right)=0

(84b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial x_{2}}=2w_{2}\left(x_{2}-\tilde{x}_{2}\right)+\lambda\left(fy_{1}-e\right)=0

(84c)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J}{\partial y_{2}}=2w_{2}\left(y_{2}-\tilde{y}_{2}\right)-\lambda\left(fx_{1}-d\right)=0

(84d)

Additionally,
{\partial J}/{\partial\lambda}=0
gives the constraint at Eq. (

Solving Eq. (
84a

(\cdot)=\frac{N_{(\cdot)}}{D}=\frac{\gamma_{2}\lambda^{2}+\gamma_{1}\lambda+\gamma_{0}}{\gamma_{2}\lambda^{2}+\gamma_{1}\lambda+\gamma_{0}}

(85)

with coefficients from Table

\displaystyle=\frac{2w_{1}w_{2}\left[e(\tilde{x}_{1}-\tilde{x}_{2})+d(\tilde{y}_{2}-\tilde{y}_{1})\right]}{(d^{2}+e^{2})(w_{1}+w_{2})}

5.2
More than Two LOS Measurements: The Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) Method

Solving the MLE triangulation problem by explicit minimization of the reprojection errors does not easily scale to more than two LOS measurements. This was attempted in Ref.
[
16
]
for three LOS measurements (
n=3
) and was found to require the solution to a system of polynomials possessing 47 roots. To attempt this for
n\geq 4
is completely unreasonable. In light of these challenges, it is commonly argued that to obtain the MLE solution for
n
views requires one to iteratively solve a non-linear least squares problem (so-called
bundle adjustment
). This, however, is not true. It is possible to create a MLE solution to
r
as the solution to a linear system by a double applications of the Law of Sines. The resulting method is referred to here as the Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) algorithm.

Begin with the first application of the Law of Sines as written in the form of the DLT. Rewrite Eq. (
19
) as

\left[\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}})=\textbf{0}_{3\times 1}

(95)

This condition is only satisfied with perfect measurements. When only the noisy measurements
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}
are available, the right-hand side is no longer exactly zero. Instead, one finds that

\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}})=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}

(96)

Recalling from Eq. (
11
) that
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}=\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}+\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}
, the residual vector
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
is found to be

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}=\left[\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})\times\right]\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}

(97)

with covariance

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}=E[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}^{T}]=-\left[\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})\times\right]\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})\times\right]

(98)

where
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}=E[\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}\textbf{\emph{w}}_{i}^{T}]
is from Eq. (
12
). The leading negative sign in Eq. (
98
) is a result of the identity
[\,\cdot\,\times]^{T}=-[\,\cdot\,\times]
.
It is possible to rewrite
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}
explicitly in terms of the corresponding measurement
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
and unknown range
\rho_{i}
by substitution from Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
42
)

\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}=-\frac{\rho^{2}_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\|^{2}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]

(99)

Therefore, one may write the MLE problem as

\min J(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{T}_{i}\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}

(100)

which, after substituting for
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
using Eq. (
96
), becomes

\min J(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}-(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}})^{T}\textbf{\emph{T}}_{I}^{C_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}})

(101)

There are three apparent issues here—all of which relate to the practical computation of
\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
. First is that
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
depends on the ideal pixel coordinates
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
when only the measured pixel coordinates
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}
are available in practice. Second is that the
3\times 3
matrix
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
is only rank two and, therefore, not invertable. Third is that
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
depends on the unknown distance
\rho_{i}
between points
r
and
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}
. All three of these issues may be avoided.

Begin with addressing the use of the noisy measurements
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}
in place of the perfect
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
in Eq. (
99
). This is a common problem in navigation. Indeed, a measurement’s error covariance is dependent on the measurement itself for all but the simplest sensor models. When approximating the error covariance of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
with the measurement
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}
, denote the noisy covariance as
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
, where

\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}=-\frac{\tilde{\rho}^{2}_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|^{2}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]

(102)

Using the measurement in place of the truth when computing the covariance is correct to first order and has been studied before for image-based LOS measurements
[
78
,
88
]
. Moreover, using measurements or estimates is the only choice since the true values will never be known. This approximation is acceptable in practice since the covariance
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
is only used to weight the different residual vectors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
. The numerical results in Section
7
confirm this assertion. Thus, proceed using
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
in place of
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
.

Next, address the computation of
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
. Observe from Eq. (
102
) that the null space of
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
is in the direction of
\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}=\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}
. Observe also that
\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]
has a null space in the same direction. Thus, because of the alignment of the null spaces, the central term in the cost function of Eq. (
101
) may be replaced with the identity

\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]=\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]

(103)

where the superscript
\dagger
indicates the pseudoinverse. It is always possible to find pseudoinverse numerically with the SVD of the
3\times 3
matrix
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
. In most cases, however, this is not the preferred approach. Instead, the pseudoinverse may usually be computed analytically as discussed in the Appendix.

Finally, address the issue of the unknown range
\rho_{i}
. Dealing with this unknown range was a central issue for the trigonometric solutions presented in Section
4
, where it was either avoided [e.g., DLT: see Eq. (
16
)] or directly estimated [e.g., explicit range: see Eq. (
49
)].
Thus, the unmitigated dependence on
\rho_{i}
(or on
r
) would seem to suggest an iterative algorithm, where the unknown range is derived from the previous iteration. Another option is to attempt some kind of approximation in special situations
[
94
]
. However, the unknown range
\rho_{i}
may be constructed directly from the measurements themselves.
Interestingly, the solution for how to obtain the unknown range was suggested by Frisius in 1533 in the same booklet
[
25
]
that (re)introduced the idea of triangulation to the modern world. Making use of Haasbroek’s translation of the original Latin, Frisius observes “that the distances can also be computed ‘with the tables of sine, but I omitted this intentionally as it is too difficult for the common man’ ”
[
27
]
. To the modern (and
un
common!) [wo]man, Frisius’s suggestion is most straightforwardly mechanized by employing the Law of Sines,

\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|}{\sin\theta_{ij}}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{\sin\psi_{ij}}

(104)

with angles as defined in Fig.
6
. Recall that
\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}
may be computed from Eq. (
46
) and is a known quantity. Thus, the sine functions in Eq. (
104
) may be rewritten in terms of the cross-product of known vectors, and it follows that the distance
\rho_{i}
is given by

\rho_{i}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\|\sin\psi_{ij}}{\sin\theta_{ij}}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{ij}\times\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}\|}{\|\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}\|}

(105)

where everything on the right-hand side is known. This expression for
\rho_{i}
is true in any consistent frame. Since LOS unit vectors
\textbf{\emph{a}}_{i}
and
\textbf{\emph{a}}_{j}
may come from different cameras having different frames (
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
), one may perform the cross products in the localization frame

\tilde{\rho}_{i}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{I_{ij}}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}{\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|\,\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{I_{ij}}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}{\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}

(106)

where, once again, the measured
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}
is used in place of the true
\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
(this is acceptable when the approximation is
only
used for approximating the covariance
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
). Thus, applying these observations to Eq. (
99
) yields an expression for
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
that is written entirely in terms of known (or measured) quantities

\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}=-\gamma^{2}_{i}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]

(107)

where

\gamma_{i}=\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{I_{ij}}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}{\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}

(108)

Finally, for any given any LOS measurement
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}
, there is some ambiguity in which additional LOS measurement
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_{j}\propto\textbf{\emph{K}}_{j}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}
(for
i\neq j
) to use for computing
\gamma_{i}
with Eq. (
108
). Experience has shown that any reasonable scheme (even a random one) provides essentially the same triangulation performance in practice (i.e., small variations in
\gamma_{i}
do not change the relative measurement weightings in the MLE enough to matter). Efforts to find the “optimal” choice of a companion measurement
j
do not appear to be worth the additional computational effort.

Now, to find the optimal solution, return to Eq. (
101
) and apply the first differential condition

\sum_{i=1}^{n}-2(\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}-\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}})^{T}\textbf{\emph{T}}_{I}^{C_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}=0

(109)

Substituting for
\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{x}}}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}
and rearranging gives

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{\emph{T}}_{I}^{C_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\right)\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{\emph{T}}_{I}^{C_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}

(110)

As expected, this final result is nothing more than the normal equations for an optimally weighted DLT (with weighting
\textbf{\emph{W}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{R}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\sim\textbf{\emph{R}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}
). Following the usual approach for solving any least squares system
[
95
]
, the numerical stability may be improved by avoiding explicit formation of the normal equations and instead solving (e.g., with QR factorization)

\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{B}}_{1}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\\
\textbf{\emph{B}}_{2}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{\emph{B}}_{n}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{B}}_{1}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{1}}\\
\textbf{\emph{B}}_{2}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
\textbf{\emph{B}}_{n}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{n}}\end{bmatrix}

(111)

which makes use of the factorization
\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}=\textbf{\emph{B}}_{i}^{T}\textbf{\emph{B}}_{i}
. In the case that
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}=\sigma^{2}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{I}}_{2\times 2}
, the results of the Appendix show that

\textbf{\emph{B}}_{i}=\frac{q_{i}}{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|^{2}}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]^{2}

(112)

where

q_{i}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\gamma_{i}}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|}{\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\rho_{i}}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}{\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\|\textbf{\emph{d}}_{I_{ij}}\times\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{j}}_{I}\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{j}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{j}\|}

(113)

Recalling the identity
[\textbf{\emph{b}}\times]^{3}=-\|\textbf{\emph{b}}\|^{2}[\textbf{\emph{b}}\times]
, it follows that the above simplifies to

\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\\
q_{2}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
q_{n}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\end{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{I}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{1}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{1}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{1}}\\
q_{2}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{2}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{2}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
q_{n}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{n}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{n}}\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{n}}\end{bmatrix}

(114)

Note here that the matrix
\textbf{\emph{S}}=[\textbf{\emph{I}}_{2\times 2},\textbf{0}_{2\times 1}]
removes the redundant third row for each of the measurements.
The reader is reminded that the two remaining rows were shown in Section
4.1.3
to be equivalent to the collinearity equations. Moreover, substituting from Eq. (
113
), one finds that

q_{i}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}=\frac{\|\textbf{\emph{K}}_{i}^{-1}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\rho_{i}}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\tilde{\textbf{\emph{a}}}_{C_{i}}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}

(115)

It is observed that a particular LOS measurement is weighted less as either the measurement error (
\sigma_{i}
) or the range (
\rho_{i}
) increase.

Whether it is ultimately best to solve for
\hat{\textbf{\emph{r}}}_{I}
via the normal equations [Eq. (
110
)] or by QR factorization [Eq. (
114
)] depends on the problem at hand and the priorities of the analyst. The normal equations solution requires less memory and fewer computations (when
n
is large), while the QR factorization tends to have slightly better numerical stability. The numerical stability of the QR factorization approach becomes more important as the angle(s) between the LOS measurements become smaller. It is also straightforward to write Eq. (
114
) as a total least squares problem, which may be solved via singular value decomposition.

Since the weighting is statistically optimal, the covariance may be computed as

\textbf{\emph{P}}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textbf{\emph{T}}_{I}^{C_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}\times\right]\tilde{\textbf{\emph{R}}}^{\dagger}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\right)^{-1}

(116)

where, again, the leading negative sign is a result of the identity
[\,\cdot\,\times]^{T}=-[\,\cdot\,\times]
. In the case of
\textbf{\emph{R}}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}=\sigma^{2}_{\textbf{\emph{x}}_{i}}\textbf{\emph{I}}_{2\times 2}
computing the pseudoinverse can be avoided and

\textbf{\emph{P}}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q^{2}_{i}\textbf{\emph{T}}^{C_{i}}_{I}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{S}}^{T}\textbf{\emph{S}}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}\tilde{\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{I}_{C_{i}}\right)^{-1}

(117)

where
q_{i}
is from Eq. (
113
). Since
\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}_{i}
may be written directly (see Ref.
[
5
]
), computing the covariance
\textbf{\emph{P}}_{r}
only requires a single matrix inverse. This is fast to compute since the inverse is of a symmetric
3\times 3
matrix.

Finally, though the authors are unaware of prior weighted DLT methods that are both optimal and direct (non-iterative), the LOST method proposed here will likely seem obvious to the contemporary practitioner of estimation theory. What becomes clear, however, is that the widespread claims of suboptimality of the DLT are often misplaced. The suboptimality is not a shortcoming of the DLT itself, but a shortcoming of the choice to solve the resulting equations as an unweighted least squares problem. Accounting for the different uncertainty in different measurements is essential to any estimation problem—be that triangulation with the DLT, navigating a spacecraft, or estimating anything else. Indeed, handling the the relative weighting of observations was one of Fisher’s original complaints against the method of ordinary least squares that led to the development of the MLE approach between 1912 and 1922
[
96
]
. Thus, the authors make no claim that the concepts behind the LOST method are especially novel. The LOST algorithm is simply the natural consequence of applying the MLE framework to the DLT algorithm, while recognizing the Law of Sines may be used to efficiently acquire the measurement covariance.

6
Dynamic Triangulation

The concepts from triangulation are not confined to concurrent measurements (or to static systems). Triangulation with moving objects was pioneered for maritime navigation and tracking after widespread deployment of radar systems in the 1940s—largely under the name bearings-only target motion analysis (BO-TMA)
[
58
]
. Similar concepts were considered for applications in spacecraft orbit determination by Kaplan
[
40
]
, though these historical presentations often obscure elegant relations to triangulation (especially the DLT from Section
4.1
). As with other triangulation methods, there is no meaningful difference between the algorithms to address the intersection (e.g., orbit determination) and resection (e.g., navigation) forms of the dynamic triangulation problem.

Consider a camera aboard a vehicle whose state
\boldsymbol{\xi}
evolves according the linear dynamics

\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(t)=\textbf{\emph{A}}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)

(118)

Such a dynamical system exhibits a solution of the form
[
97
]

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}

(119)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})
is the state transition matrix (STM) that advances the state from
t_{0}
to
t_{i}
. Suppose that one of the dynamical states within
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the vehicle’s position
r
. Let the rows of the STM
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
corresponding to
r
be denoted by
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}
such that

\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}

(120)

Thus the LOS measurement at any point in time is given by

\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}=\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}

(121)

Following the same approach as for the DLT in Section
4.1
, take the cross product with the measured LOS direction,

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\textbf{0}_{3\times 1}

(122)

such that

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}

(123)

Stacking these equations for many measurements leads to a modified version of the DLT,

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{1},t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{n}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{n},t_{0})\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{n}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{p}}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(124)

which is true in any consistent frame. This formulation is nothing more than a specialized form of classical batch estimation, which is widely used in astrodynamics applications
[
90
]
.

For a STM
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})
of general form and LOS measurements to a variety of different points
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}\neq 0
, one may usually solve for the full state vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
. However, under certain degeneracies in dynamics, sampling times, or point configurations, it is sometimes not possible to solve for
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
without ambiguity. The variations of degeneracies are many and their full enumeration is not practical. However, the most common degeneracy occurs when all LOS measurements are between a moving point and a single reference point at the origin [or a reference point that can be shifted to the origin while maintaining the linear dynamics of Eq. (
119
)], which leads to a family of homothetic solutions characterized by an unobservable scale (e.g., Fig.
5
). As an example of this, the common case of spacecraft relative navigation is discussed in Section
7.4
.

Interplanetary Exploration

Triangulation is expected to play an important role in future exploration missions to the outer planets. Both the gas giants (Jupiter and Saturn) and the ice giants (Uranus and Neptune) have a large number of moons, and it is possible to use LOS measurements to these moons to triangulate a spacecraft’s location.

The second use of the Monte Carlo analysis is further investigate the statistical equivalence of the HS and LOST methods. The covariance of these two methods has already been shown to be equivalent—to within machine precision for the analytic result and to within the expected error for the Monte Carlo result. Having an equivalent covariance, however, does not imply that the HS and LOST methods provide the exact same estimate for any particular realization of measurement noise. Two additional analyses are preformed to address this question. First, the total standard deviation of the residual
\Delta\textbf{\emph{r}}=\textbf{\emph{r}}_{LOST}-\textbf{\emph{r}}_{HS}
is computed and then contours of the ratio
\sigma_{\Delta\textbf{\emph{r}}}/\sigma_{LOST}

\sigma_{LOST}

17

\sigma_{\Delta\textbf{\emph{r}}}
is usually 3–4 orders of magnitude smaller than the total standard deviation
\sigma_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}
(and at least one order of magnitude smaller everywhere). Second, there is no apparent structure in which method is closer to the truth, suggesting that there is no viewing geometry that systematically makes the LOST method better (or worse) than HS method. Moreover, each algorithm was found to be “best” with about the same frequency (the LOST algorithm was better for about 50.1% of the points evaluated). Thus, not only do the LOST and HS methods have exactly the same covariance, but neither of the methods is more likely to produce a better estimate than the other.

While the HS and LOST algorithms have equivalent localization performance, the LOST algorithm has a few notable advantages. First, for the two-measurement case, the solution to LOST takes the form of a small linear system, which is easier (and faster) to solve than the polynomial of degree six in the HS method. Second, the LOST method retains its linear form for
n\geq 3

By comparison, the optimal HS method does not scale to more than two LOS measurements.

7.4
Rendezvous, Proximity Operations, and Docking (RPOD)

Rendezvous, Proximity Operations, and Docking (RPOD) is a critical capability for a variety of spaceflight applications
[
114
]
, including human exploration
[
115
,
116
,
117
]
, interplanetary exploration
[
118
]
, and satellite servicing
[
119
]
. When two spacecraft in nearly circular orbits are close to one another, their relative dynamics are nearly linear and may be described by the Clohessy-Wiltshire (CW) equations
[
63
]
. It has been known for some time that the relative state between two particles is unobservable under CW dynamics when only LOS measurements are available
[
64
,
13
]
. There are two ways to reintroduce observability during coasting flight (i.e., with no maneuvers). The first is to consider the nonlinear dynamics
[
120
]
. The second is to alter the triangulation geometry. This brief example focuses only on the latter of these these solutions.

Consider the situation where the origin of an LVLH frame is fixed to a chief spacecraft in a circular orbit. The relative motion of a nearby deputy spacecraft in this same LVLH frame is governed by the CW equations having a solution of the form:

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}\\
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})&\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}\textbf{\emph{v}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\\
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{v}}\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})&\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{v}}\textbf{\emph{v}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{0}\\
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}

(126)

where
\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}
and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{i}
are LVLH position and velocity of the deputy relative to the chief at time
t_{i}
. The equations for
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})
may be found in Ref.
[
63
]
or a contemporary astrodynamics textbook (e.g.,
[
121
]
).

Using the notation from Section
6
, let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}
denote the rows of the STM that produce the position vector,

\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{i},t_{0})&\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}\textbf{\emph{v}}}(t_{i},t_{0})\end{bmatrix}

(127)

which is a
3\times 6
matrix in this case.

Suppose for a moment that LOS measurements are collected between the chief and deputy spacecraft (the same equations result regardless of the vehicle on which the camera resides). Since the chief spacecraft is at the origin, this implies that
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{I_{i}}=0,\;\forall t_{i}
. Hence, substituting this scenario into Eq. (
124
), one arrives at

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{1},t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{n}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textbf{\emph{r}}}(t_{n},t_{0})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{\emph{r}}_{0}\\
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{0}\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{0}_{3n\times 1}

(128)

Since the result is a homogeneous linear system, it follows that both
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
and
\alpha\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
(for
\alpha\neq 0
) provide equally acceptable solutions to the linear system. There are infinitely many homothetic solutions
\alpha\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}
that represent an expansion or contraction of the figure (i.e., true deputy’s trajectory) about the homothetic center (i.e., the chief’s location at the origin of the LVLH frame). This is nothing more than a triangulation interpretation of the well-known scale ambiguity associated with angles-only RelNav that is usually shown by other means (e.g., Ref.
[
64
]
).

It is obvious now that scale observability may be introduced by making the right-hand side of Eq. (
128
) non-zero. This may be practically achieved by introducing a known offset in the location of either the camera (on the deputy) or the observed point (on the chief). Such a situation may occur when either of the spacecraft are not small when compared to the scale of the relative motion.

To see this, suppose there is a camera aboard the deputy spacecraft with a known offset of
\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}
at time
t_{i}
. In this case, the LOS measurement is

\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\propto\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-(\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}+\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i})

(129)

and so the DLT equation becomes

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}=\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}\right)

(130)

Therefore, after mapping
\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}
to time
t_{0}
,

\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\left[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{i}\times\right]\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{i}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{i}\right)

(131)

Letting the localization frame be the LVLH frame (denoted by
L
) and using the normalization
\boldsymbol{\ell}_{C_{i}}\propto\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}
,

\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{i}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{i}}\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{L_{i}}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{L_{i}}\right)

(132)

When more than one measurement is available at more than one time, this expression may be stacked in the usual way to form a linear system

\begin{bmatrix}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{1}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{1},t_{0})\\
\vdots\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{i}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_{i},t_{0})\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{1}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{1}}\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{L_{1}}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{L_{1}}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{i}}\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{L_{i}}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{L_{i}}\right)\end{bmatrix}

(133)

So long as the offsets (and measurement times) are chosen to avoid
\left[\textbf{\emph{K}}^{-1}\bar{\textbf{\emph{u}}}_{i}\times\right]\textbf{\emph{T}}^{L}_{C_{i}}\left(\textbf{\emph{p}}_{C_{i}}-\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{C_{i}}\right)=0
for all of the observation times then the problem becomes observable. In most cases this condition is the same as ensuring
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{C_{i}}\neq\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{C_{i}}
. The idea of introducing observability by an offset in just the point aboard the deputy (i.e.,
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{C_{i}}=0
and
\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{C_{i}}\neq 0
) was explored in Ref.
[
122
]
. The result above demonstrates that an equivalent solution could be achieved by introducing an offset on the chief (i.e.,
\textbf{\emph{p}}_{C_{i}}\neq 0
and
\delta\textbf{\emph{r}}_{C_{i}}=0
).

8
Conclusions

Absolute line of sight (LOS) measurements from images may be used to solve both spacecraft navigation and three-dimensional (3-D) reconstruction problems via triangulation. The mathematical foundations necessary for triangulation have been understood since antiquity, and knowledge of this history enhances the versatility of the modern practitioner. This manuscript reviews the historical evolution of triangulation algorithms that ultimately led to the embodiments of the trigonometric solutions that are in widespread use today. Many variations on these trigonometric solutions [e.g, with the Direct Linear Transform (DLT), collinearity equations, or Plücker coordinates] are shown to be equivalent to one another. In the special case of two LOS observations, the statistically optimal solution of Hartley and Sturm
[
15
]
is summarized and the resulting polynomial of degree six is considered. When the two LOS observations come from the same camera (e.g., with monocular navigation), the derivation can be modified to produce a much simpler polynomial of degree two. Moreover, in the general case of many measurements (from one or many cameras), it is shown that a Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) may be developed as the solution to a small linear system (of size
2n\times 3
for
n
LOS measurements)—resulting in the so-called Linear Optimal Sine Triangulation (LOST) method. The non-iterative LOST method is shown to provide an equivalent solution to the classical Hartley and Sturm method, but has the comparative advantages of (1) being scalable to many measurements and (2) not requiring the solution to a polynomial of degree six. Finally, many of the triangulation results are also shown to be valid for dynamic systems, especially when the dynamics are linear. The various algorithms and analytic insights are validated through a variety of examples, including planetary terrain relative navigation (TRN), interplanetary optical navigation (OPNAV), 3-D reconstruction of Notre-Dame de Paris, and angles-only relative navigation (RelNav).
