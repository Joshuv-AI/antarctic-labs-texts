To be or not to be stable, that is the question: understanding neural networks for inverse problems
Davide Evangelista, Elena Loli Piccolomini, Elena Morotti, James Nagy

Abstract
The solution of linear inverse problems arising, for example, in signal and image processing is a challenging problem since the ill-conditioning amplifies, in the solution, the noise present in the data. Recently introduced algorithms based on deep learning overwhelm the more traditional model-based approaches in performance, but they typically suffer from instability with respect to data perturbation. In this paper, we theoretically analyze the trade-off between stability and accuracy of neural networks, when used to solve linear imaging inverse problems for not under-determined cases. Moreover, we propose different supervised and unsupervised solutions to increase the network stability and maintain a good accuracy, by means of regularization properties inherited from a model-based iterative scheme during the network training and pre-processing stabilizing operator in the neural networks. Extensive numerical experiments on image deblurring confirm the theoretical results and the effectiveness of the proposed deep learning-based approaches to handle noise on the data.

Introduction

Linear inverse problems of the form:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a full-rank matrix discretizing a linear operator,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
m\geq n
, arise in various image processing tasks, such as deblurring or tomographic reconstruction
[
28
,
29
,
40
]
.
It is well-known that in these applications, equation (
1
) represents the discretization of an ill-posed problem.
Following the well-known Hadamard definition, a problem is ill-posed if either a solution does not exist, the solution is not unique or it does not continuously depend on the data
\boldsymbol{y}
.
In the case considered in (
1
) the third condition holds, thereby the computation of the solution becomes very challenging when noise affects the data.
In this work, we consider data corrupted by Gaussian noise, i.e.:

\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e},\qquad\boldsymbol{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{I});

(2)

where
\delta
denotes the standard deviation of the white additive Gaussian noise,
\boldsymbol{I}
is the identity matrix, and
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
is the ground truth, clean image.

Traditional regularization approaches tackle problem (
2
) as the minimization of an objective function containing a data-fit term and a regularization prior, with possible further constraints on the solution
[
9
,
18
]
.
These terms theoretically grant stability, but, in general, the computational time required by solvers is high and it may be necessary to choose many parameters, tuning them by trial and error on the data.

In the last few years, neural networks have been introduced with great success for the solution of problem (
2
), since they are capable of achieving greater accuracy than iterative regularized methods
[
2
,
13
,
33
]
.
However, noise-related issues still persist, as their high accuracy is obtained at the expense of robustness against noise in the input data. Specifically, these networks frequently yield suboptimal results when applied to data contaminated with noise that differs from that encountered during the training phase. This tendency is commonly referred to as network
instability
.

Some authors have already studied the behavior of neural networks in the presence of noise on the data, focusing on the solution of under-determined imaging inverse problems (i.e. when
m<n
in equation (
1
))
[
22
,
53
,
54
,
3
,
16
,
32
,
34
,
38
,
39
,
43
,
48
,
55
,
56
,
24
,
15
]
.
We note that the paper
[
24
]
offers a comprehensive bibliography on this topic, with the authors remarking that“
stability implies a universal barrier on performance
”.
However, to the best of our knowledge, a mathematically grounded understanding is still lacking and no works address the case of
m\geq n
.

Contributions

In this work, we look at neural networks as solvers of discrete ill-posed problems, and we contribute to the state-of-art as follows.

Firstly, we adapt the regularization theory presented by Engl at al. in
[
17
]
for solving discrete inverse problems through neural networks. It is noteworthy that Engl et al. examined regularization in Hilbert spaces, while our focus is on discrete inverse problems. Prior to introducing neural networks as solvers, we present a more general theory encompassing a broader class of functions, termed
reconstructors
, designed for addressing problem (
2
). Within this framework, we first formalize the two fundamental concepts of
\eta^{-1}
-accuracy and
\epsilon
-stability, and then we present significant findings for a class of functions called
stabilizers
. We establish a mathematical relationship that quantifies the trade-off between stability and accuracy, demonstrating that enhancing a solver’s stability is impossible without compromising its accuracy.
In this theoretical approach, neural networks have been analyzed as formal mathematical operators, shedding light on their wildly discussed ’black-box’ nature/misinterpretation.

Secondly, we propose a new ground truth-free scheme for reconstructors based on neural networks. We refer to this approach as the REgularized Neural Network (ReNN), as the target images used in the training procedure are solutions of (
2
) computed through a regularization method.
Beyond being more stable than commonly used neural networks as reconstructors, it is applicable in scenarios where collecting a set of ground-truth solutions is challenging or impossible, such as in medical imaging.

Finally, we present a novel stabilization strategy tailored for deep learning-based solvers, which incorporates a stabilizer within a pre-processing operator plugged into a neural network reconstructor. This approach demonstrates substantial efficacy in handling elevated noise levels in data. We have termed this methodology STabilized Neural Network (StNN). Furthermore, when integrated with the ReNN scheme, it evolves into the StReNN framework.

Structure of the paper

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2

2
). In Section
3

4

5
, we describe our experimental setup, whereas Section
6
showcases numerical results pertaining to deblurring and denoising. Finally, Section
7
comprises conclusions and outlines potential directions for future work.

2
Reconstructors for the solution of linear inverse problems

This section establishes the theoretical background of the manuscript, providing essential definitions and preliminary results.
To improve the readability of the work, however, we start by introducing the notation we will use throughout the paper.
We always consider
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
to lie in a subset
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the set of admissible data. We denote as
\mathcal{Y}=Rg(\boldsymbol{A},\mathcal{X})
the range of
\boldsymbol{A}
over
\mathcal{X}
, where
\boldsymbol{A}
is a continuous linear operator. We assume
\mathcal{Y}
to be dense-in-itself (i.e. with no isolated point) so that, for any admissible
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
and any neighborhood
V
of
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
, there is at least an
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
,
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
, such that
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in V
. When
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, then
||\boldsymbol{x}||
and
||\boldsymbol{y}||
will be Euclidian norms. For any
\epsilon>0
, we also define
\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}=\{\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{e};\>\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y},||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\}
.
With the following definitions, we can formalize the concept of reconstructor to solve problem (
1
) accurately.

Definition 2.1
.

Any continuous function
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, mapping
\boldsymbol{y}
to
\boldsymbol{x}=\Psi(\boldsymbol{y})
, is called a reconstructor.

Definition 2.2
.

A reconstructor
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
\eta^{-1}
-accurate, with
\eta>0
, if:

\displaystyle\eta=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}}||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||.

We define the set

\ \mathcal{R}_{\eta}=\{\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n};\Psi\text{ is a reconstructor with accuracy }\eta^{-1}\}.

We observe that without any other restriction,
\eta
could be infinite. To avoid any issue, we will always consider reconstructors with finite
\eta
in the following.

Example 2.1
.

An
\infty-
accurate reconstructor of problem (
1
) is given by:

\Psi^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{y}=(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y},

where
\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}
is the pseudo-inverse matrix,
\boldsymbol{A}^{*}
is the transpose of
\boldsymbol{A}
, and the last equality holds since
\boldsymbol{A}
is assumed to be full-rank. In this case
\Psi^{\dagger}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\infty
-accurate, as:

||\Psi^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=||(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=||(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=0.

However, reconstructors are rarely applied to noise-free data, hence a focus on the robustness of reconstructors with respect to noise is necessary.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\Psi
be an
\eta^{-1}
-accurate reconstructor applied to problem (
2
). We define the
\epsilon
-stability constant
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}
of
\Psi
as:

C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}.

We will consider in the following the realistic case of
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}<\infty
.

Definition 2.4
.

The reconstructor
\Psi
is said to be
\epsilon
-stable for a given
\epsilon>0
if
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}\in[0,1)
. Otherwise,
\Psi
is said to be
\epsilon
-unstable.

An
\epsilon
-stable reconstructor
\Psi
does not amplify corruptions having norm less than
\epsilon
(as graphically represented in Figure
1
), since (
2.3
) implies:

||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta+C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}||\boldsymbol{e}||\qquad\forall\>\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X},\forall\>\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon.

Definition 2.5
.

We define the stability radius
\rho
of
\Psi
as:

\rho=\sup\{\epsilon>0;\>C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\in[0,1)\}\,.

Figure 1:
Graphical representation of the
\epsilon
-stability and
\epsilon
-instability for an
\eta^{-1}
-accurate reconstructor.

Example 2.2
.

A reconstructor with an infinite stability radius is the following. Given
\epsilon>0
, if
\mu
is a probability distribution over
\mathcal{X}
(for example,
\mu
is the normalized Lebesgue distribution over
\mathcal{X}
), the reconstructor defined as:

\Psi^{\mathcal{X},\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\boldsymbol{x}\mu(d\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}

is
\epsilon
-stable independently from the value of
\epsilon>0
. Indeed:

||\Psi^{\mathcal{X},\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=\Bigl\|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\boldsymbol{x}\mu(d\boldsymbol{x})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\Bigr\|\leq\rho(\mathcal{X}),

where
\rho(\mathcal{X})
is the radius of
\mathcal{X}
, defined as
\rho(\mathcal{X})=\inf\{r>0:\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{B}(\int_{\mathcal{X}}\boldsymbol{x}\mu(d\boldsymbol{x});r)\}
. As a consequence the stability constant is infinite regardless
\epsilon
, and
\Psi^{\mathcal{X}(\boldsymbol{y}),\epsilon}
has accuracy
\rho(\mathcal{X})^{-1}
.

Example 2.3
.

The pseudo-inverse reconstructor
\Psi^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{y})
in (
2.1
) is unstable for any
\epsilon>0
when
\boldsymbol{A}
is ill-conditioned. Indeed:

\displaystyle||\Psi^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=||(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||

\displaystyle=||(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}||.

If
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
is the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of
\boldsymbol{A}
, then:

(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}=(\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{*})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{U}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{e}}{\sigma_{i}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i},

which implies that
||(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{e}||\gg||\boldsymbol{e}||
when
\boldsymbol{A}
has singular values close to zero.

These examples shed light on a possible conflict between accuracy and stability for a given reconstructor
\Psi
.
In the next paragraphs, we study this relationship.

2.1
Accuracy vs. stability trade-off

We can derive a relation between accuracy and stability, which becomes particularly interesting when
\boldsymbol{A}
is ill-conditioned.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an
\eta^{-1}
-accurate reconstructor. Then, for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
and for any
\epsilon>0
,
\exists\>\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
such that:

||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\geq||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-\eta\,.

(3)

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{Y}
for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
, and since
\mathcal{Y}
has no isolated points, then for any
\epsilon>0
there is an
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
such that
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{Y}
. Thus,
\exists\>\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
. Consequently:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}||

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta\,.

Since
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
by construction, it holds that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
, which implies that
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}
. To conclude:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\geq||\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta=||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-\eta\,.

∎

Since the corruption
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}
such that the relationship (
3
) holds for some
\epsilon>0
depends on
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
, for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
, we will consider the set:

E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})=\{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m};\>\text{Equation \eqref{eq:bad_corruption} holds for }\boldsymbol{e},\text{ for some }\epsilon>0\}.

(4)

Theorem 2.2

(Trade-off Theorem)
.

Under the assumptions of Lemma
2.1
it holds that, for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
and for any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
,

C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\geq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-2\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}.

(5)

Proof.

For any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
,

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\geq\sup_{||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}.

If
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
,
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
, is a perturbation defined on Lemma
2.1
, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}&\geq\sup_{||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\\
&\geq\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}\\
&\geq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-2\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||},\end{split}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.2.1
.

Given the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there is a constant
C(\boldsymbol{A})>0
which depends only on
\boldsymbol{A}
, such that:

\rho\leq\frac{2}{\eta^{-1}C(\boldsymbol{A})}.

Proof.

Consider a reconstructor
\Psi
. By Theorem
2.2
, for any
\epsilon>0
, any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
, and any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
,

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\geq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-2\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}.

(6)

We first observe that, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})=\mathcal{Y}:=Rg(\boldsymbol{A},\mathcal{X})
for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
. Indeed,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
if and only if there exists
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
. Since
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is closed under addition, then
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, which implies that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, thus
E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
. Conversely, if
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, then by definition there exists
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}
. By defining
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}
, then
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})
, which implies that
\boldsymbol{y}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
and consequently
E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})=\mathcal{Y}
.

Now, let
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
be the SVD of
\boldsymbol{A}
and define
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right)
, where
\sigma_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
are the smallest singular value of
\boldsymbol{A}
and its associated right-singular vector, respectively. Note that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in\mathcal{Y}=E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
by definition. Moreover:

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{A}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right)=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right)=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\sigma_{n}\boldsymbol{u}_{n}=\epsilon\boldsymbol{u}_{n},

from which
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||=\epsilon||\boldsymbol{u}_{n}||=\epsilon\leq\epsilon
.
Consequently, (
5
) holds for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}
. Additionally:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{A}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n}\right)=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}\boldsymbol{v}_{n},

hence
||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}
. Given that, (
5
) reads:

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\geq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-2\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}=\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}-2\eta}{\epsilon}.

As a consequence of the above relationship, if

\frac{\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_{n}}-2\eta}{\epsilon}>1,
then
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}>1
, i.e.
\rho\leq\epsilon
. A simple computation shows that this holds if:

\displaystyle\epsilon<\frac{2}{\eta^{-1}\left(\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\right)}=\frac{2}{\eta^{-1}C(\boldsymbol{A})},

concluding the proof by calling
C(\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
.
∎

The relation in Corollary
2.2.1
between the stability radius
\rho
and the accuracy
\eta^{-1}
suggests that there exists a trade-off between accuracy and stability, showing that a very accurate reconstructor is unstable for noise corruption larger than
\frac{2}{\eta^{-1}C(\boldsymbol{A})}
. We remark that for ill-conditioned problems
C(\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
can be very large, making the radius potentially very small.

Similarly, Theorem
2.2
shows that a reconstructor
\Psi
can be
\epsilon
-stable only if its accuracy is bounded.

Corollary 2.2.2
.

Given the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
, there exists
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, such that any reconstructor
\Psi
with accuracy
\eta^{-1}\geq\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})^{-1}
is
\epsilon
-unstable, i.e.
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}\geq 1
.

Moreover, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\eta^{-1}\geq\frac{2}{C(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon}
, where
C(\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
, then
\Psi
is
\epsilon
-unstable.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.2
,

\Psi
is
\epsilon
-unstable for a given
\epsilon>0
if

\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-2\eta}{||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}\geq 1.

Such condition holds if and only if:

\eta\leq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}{2}.

Thus, if
\exists\>\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
such that
\eta\leq\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}{2}
, then
\Psi
is
\epsilon
-unstable. In particular, if we define:

\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})\\
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||}{2},

(7)

we get the result. Note that, in general,
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
could be infinite.

In the assumption of
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we proved in Corollary
2.2.1
that for any
\epsilon>0
and any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
, we can always choose
\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}\in E(\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
with
||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||\leq\epsilon
such that
||\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||-||\tilde{\boldsymbol{e}}||=\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}\epsilon=C(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon
. Thus,
\Psi
is
\epsilon
-unstable if:

\eta\leq\frac{C(\boldsymbol{A})\epsilon}{2},

which proves the corollary.
∎

2.2
A sufficient condition for stability

Whenever a reconstructor is (locally) Lipschitz continuous, we can also derive conditions assessing its stability. First of all, we recall the definition of locally Lipschitz continuous reconstructors.

Definition 2.6
.

Given
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\epsilon>0
, we define the
\epsilon
-Lipschitz (also called local Lipschitz) constant of
\Psi
over
\mathcal{Y}
as:

\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\\
||\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{y}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{z})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y})||}{||\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{y}||}.

If
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})<\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
, then
\Psi
is said to be locally Lipschitz continuous.

Focusing on our problem (
2
), we remark we are interested in the cases where
\mathcal{Y}=Rg(\boldsymbol{A},\mathcal{X})
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
implies that
\exists\>\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
and each
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{y}||\leq\epsilon
can be characterized by
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e}
for some
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
.
Thus, the definition of
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}.

The importance of the local Lipschitz constant
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
lies in its strong relationship to the stability constant
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}
of the reconstructor. Indeed, if
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{Y}
is corrupted by additional noise
\boldsymbol{e}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
, then
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
represents the maximum possible variation of the reconstruction obtained by
\Psi
around the corrupted
\boldsymbol{y}
, as stated by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\Psi\in\mathcal{R}_{\eta}
has local Lipschitz constant
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
, then, for any
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
, it holds:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta+L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{e}||\,.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, it follows that:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||+||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||.

Since
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
, the definition of local Lipschitz constant implies that:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||\leq L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{e}||,

whereas the accuracy of
\Psi
gives:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta.

Thus, we can conclude:

\displaystyle||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{e}||+\eta.

∎

Corollary 2.3.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, it holds:

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y}).

Proof.

From the inequality in Theorem
2.3
, we have:

||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta+L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{e}||\iff L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})\geq\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}

for any
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
and any
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
. Consequently,
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
is a majorant of the set:

\left\{\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||};\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X},||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\right\}.

Since
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}
is defined as the supremum of this set, by the minimality of the supremum we have
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
.
∎

We remark that Corollary
2.3.1
proves that
\Psi
is
\epsilon
-stable if
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})<1
, yielding a useful sufficient condition to the assessment of stability.

Example 2.4
.

Under suitable parameter choices, the Tikhonov reconstructor is
\epsilon
-stable for any
\epsilon>0
.
The Tikhonov reconstructor is built on the Tikhonov method
[
47
,
50
]
and defined as:

\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}||\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}||^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}||\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{x}||^{2},

(8)

where
\lambda>0
is the regularization parameter and
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is a matrix such that
\ker(\boldsymbol{A})\cap\ker(\boldsymbol{L})=\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
\boldsymbol{L}
is usually chosen as the identity or the forward-difference operator.
We can prove the following proposition regarding Tikhonov stability.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
. Then
\exists\>\lambda>0
such that:

\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}(\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}},\mathcal{Y})<1.

Proof.

For any
\lambda>0
and any
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}
, it can be shown, by considering the normal equations of (
8
), that:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\Bigl(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}+\lambda\boldsymbol{L}^{*}\boldsymbol{L}\Bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{L}^{*}\boldsymbol{L}\Bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\,.

Consequently, for any
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}
, it holds that

\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})\to 0
for
\lambda\to\infty.
Then:

\displaystyle L^{\epsilon}(\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}},\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\to 0\quad\text{for}\quad\lambda\to\infty,

which implies that, for all
\alpha>0
, there exists
\bar{\lambda}>0
such that for any
\lambda>\bar{\lambda}
,
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}},\mathcal{Y})<\alpha
. Choosing
\alpha=1
we obtain the required result.
∎

Corollary
2.3.1
and Proposition
2.4
demonstrate that it is always possible to build a stable Tikhonov reconstructor. Such property will play a crucial role in Subsection
4.3
where we will explain our proposed ReNN approach.

3
Stabilizers in the solution of linear inverse problems

In this section, we delve into additional properties pertinent to stable reconstructors, by introducing the novel concept of
stabilizer
which will be exploited in Subsection
4.4
to define our StNN and StReNN approaches.

3.1
Stabilizers and properties

Definition 3.1
.

A continuous functions
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{t}
is an
\epsilon
-stabilizer of a reconstructor
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
if:

1.

\forall\>\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
,
\exists\ C^{\epsilon}_{\phi}\in[0,1)
and
\exists\ \boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}||=C^{\epsilon}_{\phi}||\boldsymbol{e}||
such that:

\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e})=\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}.

2.

\exists\ \gamma:\mathbb{R}^{t}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi
.

The smallest constant
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi}
for which the definition holds is defined as the stability constant of the stabilizer
\phi
.

We also define the set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}=\{\Psi\in\mathcal{R}_{\eta};\>\exists\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{t}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\exists\>\phi\>\epsilon\text{-stabilizer,}\text{ s.t. }\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi\}.

Whenever
t=m
and
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a reconstructor, the reconstructor
\Psi
is said to be
\epsilon
-stabilized with respect to
\gamma
.

Note that, in the definition of
\epsilon
-stabilizer, we only require a stability condition for
\phi
in the first item. Interestingly, given a reconstructor
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi
, we can estimate its
\epsilon
-stability constant
C_{\Psi}^{\epsilon}
by means of the constant
C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}
and the local Lipschitz constant of
\gamma
, as proved in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi
, with
\phi
being an
\epsilon
-stabilizer. If
C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}
is the constant mentioned in Definition
3.1
,
L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})
is the local Lipschitz constant of
\gamma
with
\mathcal{T}=\phi(\mathcal{Y})
, it holds:

C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
and
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
. Then:

||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=||\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e}))-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||.

Since
\phi
is a stabilizer,
\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})=\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})+\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}||\leq C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}||\boldsymbol{e}||
. Thus:

\begin{split}||(\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e}))-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||&=||\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})+\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\\
&\leq||\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})+\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime})-\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}))||+||\gamma(\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}))-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\\
&\leq\eta+L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})||\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}||=\eta+L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}||\boldsymbol{e}||,\end{split}

which implies that
L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}
is a majorant of the set:

\left\{\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||};\>\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X},||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\right\}.

Since
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}
is defined as the supremum of the same set, by the minimality of the supremum we have
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{T})C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}
.
∎

Theorem
3.1
implies the following important result.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0
, let
\Psi_{1}=\gamma_{1}\circ\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{1}}^{\epsilon}
, and
\Psi_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{\eta_{2}}
. If:

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{1}}\in\left[0,\frac{C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{2}}}{L^{\epsilon}(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{T})}\right],

(9)

then:

C_{\Psi_{1}}^{\epsilon}\leq C_{\Psi_{2}}^{\epsilon}.

Proof.

Since (
9
) holds by hypothesis
and
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{1}}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{T})C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{1}}
for
\Psi_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta_{1}}^{\epsilon}
by Theorem
3.1
, we get:

C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{1}}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{T})C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{1}}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{T})\frac{C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{2}}}{L^{\epsilon}(\gamma_{1},\mathcal{T})}=C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{2}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

The theorem yields interesting consequences for the special case where
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
share the same accuracy.
For instance, when
\Psi_{1}=\gamma_{1}\circ\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
and
\Psi_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{\eta}
, if (
9
) holds, the theorem suggests that
\Psi_{1}
is preferable to
\Psi_{2}
, as
\Psi_{1}
is more stable than
\Psi_{2}
.
In addition, we can state the following result, whose proof is trivial.

Corollary 3.2.1
.

Let
\Psi_{1}=\gamma_{1}\circ\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
and
\Psi_{2}=\gamma_{2}\circ\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
.
If (
9
) holds,
then
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{1}}\leq C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{2}}
.

In the next proposition, we show a result linking the accuracy of a reconstructor
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
to a characterization of its
\epsilon
-stabilizer
\phi
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
. Let:

\displaystyle\sigma(\phi):=\sup\{||\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||;\>\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})\}\,.

(10)

Then:

\eta^{-1}\leq\frac{2}{\sigma(\phi)}.

Proof.

Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
. Then:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||

\displaystyle\leq||\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}||+||\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||

\displaystyle=||\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}||+||\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||\leq 2\eta,

which implies that:

\eta\geq\frac{||\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}||}{2}.

Since the estimation above holds for any
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2}
with
\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{1})=\phi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}_{2})
, it holds for
\sigma(\phi)
, thus concluding the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
3.3
, if
\phi
is the constant operator (having
C_{\phi}^{\epsilon}=0
as observed in Example
2.2
), it gets
\sigma(\phi)=\infty
, which implies that for any
\gamma
, the accuracy of
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi
will be zero, whenever
\mathcal{X}
is unbounded.

Now, in the following proposition, we show that a sequence of functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
approximating
\Psi\in\mathcal{R}_{\eta}
, i.e.:

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}}||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})||=0.

can be exploited to construct a good stabilizer.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given a reconstructor
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with local Lipschitz constant
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})<1
and a sequence of functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
approximating
\Psi
, there exists
K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq K
,
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{k}}<1
.

Proof.

Consider
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in X
and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
. To prove the result, we need to show that:

\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})=\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})+\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}\ {\mbox{f}or}\ k\geq K,

with
||\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}||=C^{\epsilon}_{\phi}||\boldsymbol{e}||
and
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi}\in[0,1)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}:=\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})
, then:

\displaystyle||\boldsymbol{e}^{\prime}||

\displaystyle=||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||

\displaystyle\leq L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||=L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})||\boldsymbol{e}||,

which implies that
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{k}}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})
. Since
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of approximators of
\Psi
, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
there is a constant
c_{k}
such that
||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})||\leq c_{k}
and
c_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Consequently, it holds:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\\
&\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})||+||\phi_{k}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||+||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\\
&\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||+2c_{k}}{||\boldsymbol{e}||},\end{split}

which implies that
L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})\to L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})
as
k\to\infty
. Since
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})<1
,
\exists\>K\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq K
,
L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})<1
. For those values of
k
,
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{k}}\leq L^{\epsilon}(\phi_{k},\mathcal{Y})<1
.
∎

3.2
Tikhonov stabilizers

If we now consider the Tikhonov reconstructor
\Psi=\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}
introduced in Example
2.4
, it is possible to construct a sequence
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\epsilon
-stabilizers.
In fact, recalling that
L^{\epsilon}(\Psi,\mathcal{Y})<1
for suitable
\lambda>0
as stated in Proposition
2.4
, a simple way to generate the sequence
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is the following.
Consider a convergent iterative algorithm for the solution of (
8
):

\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{k+1}=\mathcal{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}),\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}^{k},\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})
models the application of the
k
-th iterate of the algorithm, starting from
\boldsymbol{x}^{k}
and with datum
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
.
To set an example, the Conjugate Gradient for Least Squares (CGLS) algorithm is an iterative method solving the normal equations associated with (
8
).
Now, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we can define the
Tikhonov stabilizers

\phi_{k}
to be the composition of the first
k
iterations of the algorithm, i.e.:

\phi_{k}(\cdot)=\bigcircop_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{T}_{i}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}).

(11)

For the convergence property of the algorithm,
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of functions approximating
\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}
and with
C^{\epsilon}_{\phi_{k}}<1
for suitable
k\geq K
. Such property will be fundamental for the stabilization technique we propose in Subsection
4.4
.

In this section, the theoretical results previously outlined are applied to scenarios where reconstructors are operationalized through neural networks. Concurrently, we delineate our methodologies aimed at advancing current state-of-the-art approaches. Figure
2
offers a detailed schematic that encapsulates all the approaches considered within this study. The ’Tik’ label refers to the Tikhonov reconstructor
\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}
, defined in Example
2.4
.

A schematic representation of the proposed methods.

4.1
Parameter-dependent families of reconstructors

We now consider a family of reconstructors
\{\Psi_{\Theta}\}_{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}
, depending on a vector of parameters
\Theta
, approximating a reconstructor
\Psi
to solve problem (
1
).
We prove in the following theorem that the stability of
\Psi_{\Theta}
is strongly related to the stability of
\Psi
.

Theorem 4.1

(Approximation Theorem for Reconstructors)
.

Let
\Psi
be an
\eta^{-1}
-accurate reconstructor and let
\{\Psi_{\Theta}\}_{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}
be a set of reconstructors with accuracy
\eta_{\Theta}^{-1}
for any
\Theta
. We define, for any
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
:

\Delta(\Theta):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||

and:

\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||<\epsilon\end{subarray}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})||.

If
\Delta(\Theta)\to 0
when
\Theta\to\Theta^{*}
, then:

\lim_{\Delta(\Theta)\to 0}\eta_{\Theta}=\eta.

(12)

Moreover, if
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)\to 0
when
\Theta\to\Theta_{\epsilon}^{*}
, then:

\lim_{\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)\to 0}C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{\Theta}}=C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}.

(13)

Proof.

Consider
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
. Since:

\displaystyle||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||+||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||

and:

\displaystyle||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\geq||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||,

it holds that:

|\>||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\>|\leq||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||\leq\Delta(\Theta),

which implies that
||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\to||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||
as
\Delta(\Theta)\to 0
and consequently,
\eta_{\Theta}\to\eta
as
\Delta(\Theta)\to 0
.

Now, consider
\epsilon>0
and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon
. A similar computation shows that:

\displaystyle|\>||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\>|\leq\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta),

which implies that
||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\to||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+\boldsymbol{e})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||
for
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)\to 0
. Consequently, for
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)\to 0
,

\displaystyle C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{\Theta}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+e)-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta_{\Theta}}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}\to\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}\\
||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\end{subarray}}\frac{||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}+e)-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||-\eta}{||\boldsymbol{e}||}=C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.1.1
.

For any
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, it holds:

\eta_{\Theta}\leq\eta+\Delta(\Theta).

Proof.

Consider
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then:

\displaystyle||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||+||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||.

Since
||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})||\leq\Delta(\Theta)
by hypothesis and
||\Psi(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta
since
\Psi
is
\eta^{-1}
-accurate, then:

||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||\leq\eta+\Delta(\Theta),

which shows that
\eta_{\Theta}\leq\eta+\Delta(\Theta)
.
∎

Note that
\Delta(\Theta)
and
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
are, in general, not independent, as proved in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, let
\Delta(\Theta)
and
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
be the quantities defined in Theorem
4.1
. Then:

\Delta(\Theta)\leq\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta).

Proof.

Observe that, by definition of
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}
,
\Delta(\Theta)
and
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\Delta(\Theta)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y})||,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y})-\Psi(\boldsymbol{y})||,

where
\mathcal{Y}^{\epsilon}=\{\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{e};\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y},||\boldsymbol{e}||\leq\epsilon\}\supseteq\mathcal{Y}
. The result follows from the property that the supremum of a set must be larger than the supremum of its subsets.
∎

An insight on the stability properties of
\Psi_{\Theta}
can be obtained by the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\Psi_{\Theta}
be a reconstructor parameterized by
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, approximating a reconstructor
\Psi
with error
\Delta(\Theta)>0
. Let
\eta_{\Theta}^{-1}
and
\eta^{-1}
be the accuracy of
\Psi_{\Theta}
and
\Psi
, respectively. If:

\Delta(\Theta)\leq\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})-\eta

(14)

for a fixed
\epsilon>0
, where
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
is the constant defined in Corollary
2.2.2
, then
C_{\Psi_{\Theta}}^{\epsilon}\geq 1
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. By Corollary
4.1.1
, the accuracy of
\Psi_{\Theta}
can be estimated as
\eta_{\Theta}\leq\eta+\Delta(\Theta)
. Consequently, by Corollary
2.2.2
, if
\eta+\Delta(\Theta)\leq\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
, then
\eta_{\Theta}\leq\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
, which implies that
C_{\Psi_{\Theta}}^{\epsilon}\geq 1
.
∎

In the following paragraphs, we will analyze two particular families of reconstructors
\{\Psi_{\Theta}\}_{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}
.

4.2
Neural Networks as reconstructors: the NN approach

Now we consider the set of neural networks defined by a fixed architecture as the family
\{\Psi_{\Theta}\}_{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}}
.

Definition 4.1
.

Given a neural network architecture
\mathcal{A}=(\nu,S)
where
\nu=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{L})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
,
\nu_{0}=m,\nu_{L}=n
, defines the width of each layer and
S=(S_{1,1},\dots,S_{L,L}),S_{j,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu_{j}\times\nu_{k}}
is the set of matrices representing the skip connections, we define the parametric family of neural network reconstructors with architecture
\mathcal{A}
, parameterized by
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
, as

\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}=\{\Psi_{\Theta}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n};\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\},

where
\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})=\boldsymbol{z}^{L}
is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{z}^{0}=\boldsymbol{y}\\
\boldsymbol{z}^{l+1}=\rho(W^{l}\boldsymbol{z}^{l}+b^{l}+\sum_{k=1}^{l}S_{l,k}\boldsymbol{z}^{k})\quad\forall\>l=0,\dots,L-1\end{cases}

(15)

and

W^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu_{l+1}\times\nu_{l}}
is the weights matrix,
b^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{\nu_{l+1}}
is the bias vector.

Given
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, consider the dataset
\mathbb{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\delta},\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i});\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}\in\mathcal{D}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}
of images according to (
2
).
Training a neural network to solve the inverse problem (
2
) results in finding the parameters
\Theta^{*}
such that the associated reconstructor
\Psi_{\Theta^{*}}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies:

\Psi_{\Theta^{*}}\in\arg\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}}\frac{1}{N_{\mathbb{D}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}\ell(\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\delta}),\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}),

(16)

where
\delta\geq 0
and
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the loss function.

In this work, we consider as reconstructors
\Psi_{\Theta}
the neural networks trained with the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss. We will name this family as NN, in the following.
We first apply NN onto noiseless data (
\delta=0
), thereby (
16
) corresponds to:

\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}||_{2}^{2}=\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i})-\Psi^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i})||_{2}^{2},

(17)

which results in the minimization of
\Delta(\Theta)
as introduced in Theorem
4.1
with
\Psi=\Psi^{\dagger}
.

We observe that when
\boldsymbol{A}
is ill-conditioned,
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
is large. This becomes particularly apparent when
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as under these circumstances,
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})
is bounded below by a quantity depending on
C(\boldsymbol{A})=\frac{1-\sigma_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
. Additionally, the value of
\Delta(\Theta^{*})
derived from NN training likely meets the established inequality in Proposition
4.3
, which leads to instability. This confirms that effective neural network training can produce a very accurate but unstable reconstructor
\Psi_{\Theta}
.

A widely adopted strategy to bolster robustness in neural networks is known as noise injection. This technique involves adding noise to the input of the network during its training phase. In this context, the set of reconstructors
\Psi_{\Theta}
, referred to as iNN, is defined by a neural network trained through the following equation:

\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}\Theta^{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\delta})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}||_{2}^{2},

(18)

where
\delta>0
. Research detailed in
[
10
]
has demonstrated that this approach effectively introduces a Tikhonov regularization term into the loss function. Although this technique, as described in
[
4
]
, enhances the stability of the resultant network, the impact of noise injection on the accuracy of the model remains somewhat ambiguous. Furthermore, the optimal amount of noise to be added to each input to optimize the balance between stability and accuracy is still a subject of investigation.

4.3
Regularized NN-based reconstructors: the ReNN approach

To develop a reconstructor with improved stability compared to standard neural networks (NN), we harness the properties of Tikhonov regularization. It is important to note that a Tikhonov regularized reconstructor
\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}
achieves stability for an appropriately chosen regularization parameter, as delineated in Proposition
2.4
. This methodology will be referred to as the Regularized Neural Network (ReNN), denoted as
\Psi_{\Theta}^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}
.
ReNN is defined by training a neural network with a new loss
\ell
as:

\Psi_{\Theta}^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}\in\arg\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{i})-\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{i})||_{2}^{2},

(19)

with
\delta>0
. We underline that ReNN does not require any ground-truth solutions
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}
since the target is computed from the corrupted datum
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}
via the Tikhonov-regularized reconstructor.
Furthermore, in the training of ReNN, noise is present not solely to the input of the neural network model, as is the case with iNN, but also to the input of the Tikhonov-regularized reconstructor, which is responsible for generating the target.
In the following, we consider for simplicity the case
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but similar results hold for a general
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Starting from inequality (
14
) it is easy to notice that (
19
) corresponds to the minimization of
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
in Theorem
4.1
. Moreover, by Theorem
4.2
, if
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
is small, as it is common when
\Psi_{\Theta}
is a neural network, then
\Delta(\Theta)\in[0,\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)]
is also small. Regarding the right hand side
\bar{\eta}(\boldsymbol{A},\epsilon,\mathcal{X})-\eta
of (
14
), it is noted that in this instance
\eta=\eta(\lambda)
and
\eta(\lambda)\to\infty
for
\lambda\to\infty
. Consequently, for sufficiently large values of
\lambda
, it is probable that ReNN does not fulfill the conditions of (
14
).

Moreover, minimizing
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)

is crucial for enforcing the method’s stability, as proven by Theorem
4.1
, where we have shown that in our hypothesis the stability constant
C^{\epsilon}_{\Psi_{\Theta}^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}}<1
for sufficiently small
\Delta_{\epsilon}(\Theta)
.
Hence, effective training of ReNN should produce an accurate and stable reconstructor. The pseudocode to compute
\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}_{\Theta}
is given in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Regularized Neural Network (ReNN)

input
a collection
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
of data points, a noise level
\delta>0
,
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and a stable reconstrctor
\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}

for

i\leftarrow 1:N_{\mathbb{D}}

do

Sample
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{I})

Compute
\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{i}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{i}

end

for

Solve

\min_{\Psi_{\Theta}\in\mathcal{F}_{\Theta}^{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathbb{D}}}||\Psi_{\Theta}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{i})-\Psi^{\lambda,\boldsymbol{L}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta}_{i})||_{2}^{2}.

return
a trained ReNN
\Psi_{\Theta}

4.4
Stabilization on NN and ReNN: St- approaches

In the remainder of this section, we discuss an application of the stabilizers, introduced in Section
3
, to improve the stability of neural network-based reconstructors.
We propose new reconstructors
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\eta}^{\epsilon}
,
\Psi=\gamma\circ\phi
where
\gamma
is a neural network based reconstructor.
In particular, we consider
\phi
as the Tikhonov
\epsilon
-stabilizer
\phi_{k}
defined in Subsection
3.2
and obtained by
k
iterations of the CGLS algorithm, with a suitable
k
. When
\gamma
is chosen as NN, iNN, ReNN we obtain the
\epsilon
-stabilized reconstructors StNN, StiNN, and StReNN, respectively.

Note that, in this case, we can apply Theorem
3.2
with
\Psi_{1}=\gamma\circ\phi_{k}
and
\Psi_{2}=\gamma
, and whenever we choose
\phi_{k}
such that:

\displaystyle C_{\phi_{k}}^{\epsilon}\leq\frac{C_{\gamma}^{\epsilon}}{L^{\epsilon}(\gamma,\mathcal{Y})},

(20)

the
\epsilon
-stabilized reconstructor
\Psi_{1}
gets more stable than its unstabilized version
\Psi_{2}
.
We remark that it is always possible to find a Tikhonov stabilizer
\phi_{k}
fitting (
20
), by suitably tuning
\lambda
and
k
.
Clearly, this comes at the expense of accuracy as discussed in Proposition
3.3
, but we will show that the accuracy does not suffer excessively, as evidenced by empirical results in Section
6
.

5.2
Results evaluation

In order to estimate in our experiments the accuracy and the stability constants of a given reconstructor
\Psi
we compute the
empirical
accuracy
\hat{\eta}^{-1}
and the
empirical

\hat{C}^{\epsilon}_{\Psi}

\mathcal{TS}

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\delta^{2}\boldsymbol{I}
) differs for each datum
\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{TS}
).
Finally, we compute the
empirical reconstruction error
on the test set as:

\mathcal{E}(\Psi,\delta)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}\in\mathcal{TS}}||\Psi(\boldsymbol{y}^{\delta})-\boldsymbol{x}^{gt}||.

To evaluate a single image reconstruction, we also compute the widely used Structural Similarity Index (SSIM)
[

, taking values in
[0,1]

and

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we conducted a comprehensive theoretical analysis of a broad spectrum of reconstructors for addressing a discrete ill-posed inverse problem with noisy data. Our findings, particularly encapsulated in Theorem
2.2
, establish that enhancing stability in these reconstructors invariably leads to a decrease in accuracy. Our focus was primarily on reconstructors that leverage neural networks.

In consideration of the trade-off theorem, our objective was to enhance the stability of reconstructors based on deep learning, while preserving their accuracy as much as possible. We based our analysis on the reconstructors represented by the popular end-to-end NN approach for image restoration and we also considered the extensively utilized noise injection stabilization technique, here referred to as iNN.
As is commonly understood, these approaches are trained using datasets that include images with known ground truth.

We have proposed new deep learning-based approaches: (i) an additional reconstructor, ReNN, which is trained on noisy images and increases the stability of NN by inheriting regularization from a model-based scheme in its training; (ii) a stabilization technique which stabilizes the solving process by reducing the impact of the noise with few iterations of a model-based algorithm and it is applied to all the proposed reconstructors resulting in StNN, StiNN and StReNN.

We performed extensive numerical experiments on image deblurring and denoising, with results serving to substantiate the theoretical framework presented in our study.
