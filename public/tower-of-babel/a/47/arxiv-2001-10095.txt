{Abstract}

In this work, we solve the eigenvalues problem with the Bohr collective Hamiltonian for triaxial nuclei within Deformation-Dependent Mass formalism (DDM) using the Hulthén potential . We shall call the solution developed here Z(5)-HDDM. Analytical expressions for energy spectra are derived by means of a recent version of the Asymptotic Iteration Method. The calculated numerical results are compared with the experimental data, and the model Z(5)-H using the Hulthén potential without DDM formalism as well as theoretical predictions of Z(5)-DDDM model with Davidson potential.

1
Introduction

Usually, in Bohr’s Hamiltonian, the mass parameter is considered as a constant. However, there is a growing evidence that this approximation may be inadequate. Several comparisons with experimental data have recently pointed out that the mass tensor of the collective Hamiltonian cannot be considered as a constant and should be taken as a function of the collective coordinates. Based on these proofs, a Bohr Hamiltonian with a mass depending on the collective variable can be treated. It should be noticed that the concept of a non-constant mass has been used long ago in quantum physics. But, it has been introduced for the first time in nuclear physics by D. Bonatsos et al
[
1
]

Atomic nuclei exhibit phase transitions as a function of the number of
nucleons. These phase transitions are of quantum type. Several critical
points of symmetries namely E(5), X(5), X(3), Z(5), Z(4) have been introduced.
In this work, we will focus on the critical point of symmetry Z(5) which
represents the transition from prolate axially symmetric SU(3) nuclei to oblate shapes. We will consider a Bohr Hamiltonian with the Hulthén potential including a mass parameter depending on the collective coordinate
\beta
.

The structure of the present work is as follows. In Section 2, the theoretical background of the elaborated model Z(5)-HDDM is briefly presented. In section 3, we give the obtained analytical expressions for the energy levels by means of Asymptotic Iteration Method. Section 4 contains results and discussion about the effect of DDM on the energy spectra of Xe and Pt isotopes.

2
The Z(5)-HDDM model

in the framework of Z(5), the original Bohr Hamiltonian
[
2
]
is

\begin{split}H_{B}=-\dfrac{\hbar^{2}}{2B}\bigg[\frac{1}{\beta^{4}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta^{4}\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}sin3\gamma}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\sin 3\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\\
-\frac{1}{4\beta^{2}}\sum^{3}_{k=1}\frac{Q_{k}^{2}}{sin^{2}(\gamma-\frac{2\pi}{3}k)}\bigg]+V(\beta,\gamma)\end{split}

(1)

Where B is the mass parameter, which is usually considered constant,
\beta
and
\gamma
are the usual collective coordinates (
\beta
being a deformation coordinate measuring departure from spherical shape, and
\gamma
being an angle measuring departure from axial symmetry), while
Q_{k}
(k = 1, 2, 3) are the components of angular momentum in the intrinsic frame.

Using a mass depending on the deformation coordinate
\beta
,

B(\beta)=\frac{B_{0}}{(f(\beta))^{2}}

(2)

where
B_{0}
is the constant mass and
f(\beta)
the deformation function. The Schrodinger equation corresponding to the Hamiltonian (
1
) is given by
[
1
]

\begin{split}H\Psi(\beta,\gamma,\theta_{i})=\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\sqrt{f}}{\beta^{4}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta^{4}f\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\sqrt{f}-\dfrac{f^{2}}{2\beta^{2}sin3\gamma}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}sin3\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\\
+\;\frac{f^{2}}{8\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1,2,3}\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{sin^{2}(\gamma-\frac{2}{3}\pi k)}+V_{eff}\bigg]\Psi(\beta,\gamma,\theta_{i})=\varepsilon\Psi(\beta,\gamma,\theta_{i})\end{split}

(3)

Where
\theta_{i}
are the Euler angles and the reduced energies
\varepsilon
, reduced potential
v(\beta,\gamma)
, effective potential
V_{eff}(\beta,\gamma)
are respectively

\varepsilon=\frac{B_{0}}{\hbar^{2}}\;E
,

v(\beta,\gamma)=\frac{B_{0}}{\hbar^{2}}\;V(\beta,\gamma)

V_{eff}(\beta,\gamma)=v(\beta,\gamma)+\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta-\lambda)f\nabla^{2}f+\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\delta)(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)(\nabla f)^{2}

The function
f(\beta)
depends only on the radial coordinate
\beta
, so only the
\beta
part of the above equation is affected.

3
Separable form of the Hamiltonian

In order to achieve a separation of variables, we assume that the reduced potential
v(\beta,\gamma)
depends on the variables
\beta
and
\gamma
and has the form
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]

\hskip 56.9055ptv(\beta,\gamma)=u(\beta)+\frac{f^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\;w(\gamma)

(4)

with
w(\gamma)
having a deep minimum at
\gamma
=
\frac{\pi}{6}
and the wave functions have the form

\hskip 56.9055pt\Psi(\beta,\gamma,\theta_{i})=\xi(\beta)\;\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})

(5)

The separation of variables gives

\begin{split}\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\sqrt{f}}{\beta^{4}}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\beta^{4}f\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\sqrt{f}+\frac{f^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\Lambda+\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta-\lambda)f\nabla^{2}f\\
+\;\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\delta)(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)(\nabla f)^{2}+u(\beta)\bigg]\;\xi(\beta)=\varepsilon\;\xi(\beta)\end{split}

(6)

and

\begin{split}\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{\sin 3\gamma}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}sin3\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1,2,3}\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{sin^{2}(\gamma-\frac{2}{3}\pi k)}\\
+\;w(\gamma)\bigg]\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})=\Lambda\;\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})\end{split}

(7)

where
\Lambda
is the separation constant and equation (
6
) can be simplified by performing the derivations

\frac{1}{2}f^{2}\xi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+\bigg(ff^{{}^{\prime}}+\frac{2f^{2}}{\beta}\bigg)\xi^{{}^{\prime}}+\bigg(\frac{(f^{{}^{\prime}})^{2}}{8}+\frac{ff^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{4}+\frac{ff^{{}^{\prime}}}{\beta}-\frac{f^{2}}{2\beta^{2}}\Lambda+\varepsilon-v_{eff}\bigg)\xi=0

(8)

with

v_{eff}=u(\beta)+\frac{1}{4}(1-\delta-\lambda)f(\frac{4f^{{}^{\prime}}}{\beta}+f^{{}^{\prime\prime}})+\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\delta)(\frac{1}{2}-\lambda)(f^{{}^{\prime}})^{2}

(9)

In the present work, we use the Hulthén potential
[
7
,
8
]
with a unit depth as in
[
9
,
10
]

u(\beta)=-\frac{1}{e^{\tau\beta}-1}

(10)

where
\tau=\frac{1}{b}
is a screening parameter, and
b
is the range of the potential. This potential has some properties, namely it behaves as a short-range potential for small values of
\beta
and decreases exponentially for very large values of
\beta
. By inserting the function
R(\beta)=\beta^{2}\;\xi(\beta)
in the radial equation (
8
), one obtains

f^{2}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+2ff^{{}^{\prime}}R^{{}^{\prime}}+\bigg(2\varepsilon-2(v_{eff}+\frac{f^{2}+\beta ff^{{}^{\prime}}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{f^{2}\Lambda}{2\beta^{2}}-\frac{(f^{{}^{\prime}})^{2}}{8}-\frac{ff^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{4})\bigg)R=0

(11)

In order to make connection between our results and those obtained in ref.
[
11
]
, we have replaced
2\varepsilon
by
\epsilon
in the the above equation, so one obtains

f^{2}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}+2ff^{{}^{\prime}}R^{{}^{\prime}}+(\epsilon-2u_{eff})R=0

(12)

where

u_{eff}=v_{eff}+\frac{f^{2}+\beta ff^{{}^{\prime}}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{f^{2}\Lambda}{2\beta^{2}}-\frac{(f^{{}^{\prime}})^{2}}{8}-\frac{ff^{{}^{\prime\prime}}}{4}

(13)

The special form for the deformation function is

f(\beta)=1+a\beta^{2},\;\;\;a<<1

(14)

Using these forms for the potential and the deformation function in Eq. (
13
), one obtains

2u_{eff}=k_{1}\beta^{2}+k_{0}+\frac{k_{-1}}{\beta^{2}}-\frac{1}{e^{\tau\beta}-1}

(15)

Where

\displaystyle k_{1}

\displaystyle=a^{2}\bigg(5(1-\delta-\lambda)+(1-2\delta)(1-2\lambda)+4+\Lambda\bigg)

(16a)

\displaystyle k_{0}

\displaystyle=a\bigg(5(1-\delta-\lambda)+7+2\Lambda\bigg)

(16b)

\displaystyle k_{-1}

\displaystyle=2+\Lambda

(16c)

Equation (
12
) becomes

f^{2}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\beta)+2ff^{{}^{\prime}}R^{{}^{\prime}}(\beta)+\bigg(\epsilon-k_{1}\beta^{2}-k_{0}-\frac{k_{-1}}{\beta^{2}}+\frac{1}{e^{\tau\beta}-1}\bigg)R(\beta)=0

(17)

To simplify equation (
17
), we will proceed to a change of the function R(
\beta
) by

R(\beta)=\frac{R(\beta)}{1+a\beta^{2}}

(18)

So equation (
17
), becomes

\begin{split}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(\beta)+\bigg(-\frac{k_{1}\beta^{2}}{(1+a\beta^{2})^{2}}-\frac{2a}{1+a\beta^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{(1+a\beta^{2})^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1+a\beta^{2})^{2}(e^{\tau\beta}-1)}\\
-\frac{k_{0}}{(1+a\beta^{2})}-\frac{k_{-1}}{(1+a\beta^{2})\beta^{2}}\bigg)R(\beta)=0\end{split}

(19)

From this equation, if we set the deformation parameter
a=0
, we recover the equation (7) of ref.
[
11
]
. Because of the centrifugal potential and the form of the Hulthén one, the Schrodinger equation (
19
) cannot be solved analytically. So we will proceed to a rigorous approximation that allows to tackle this problem. For a small
\beta
deformation, the centrifugal potential could be approximated by the following expression, as in refs.
[
12
,
13
,
14
]

\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\approx\tau^{2}\frac{e^{-\tau\beta}}{(e^{-\tau\beta}-1)^{2}}

(20)

This approximation is also valid for small values of the
screening parameter
\tau
. By using the new variable
y=e^{-\tau\beta}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{e^{\tau\beta}-1}=\frac{y}{1-y}\;\;,\;\;\;\;\beta=\frac{1-y}{\sqrt{y}\;\tau}\;\;,\;\;\;1+a\beta^{2}=\frac{a(1-y)^{2}+y\tau^{2}}{y\tau^{2}}

(21)

Rewriting equation (
19
) by using the new variable
y
, we obtain

\begin{split}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)+\frac{1}{y}R^{{}^{\prime}}(y)+\bigg(-\frac{(\epsilon-k_{0})\tau^{2}+(2-y)k_{1}}{(ay^{2}+(\tau^{2}-2a)y+a)^{2}}-\frac{k_{1}+2a}{y(ay^{2}+(\tau^{2}-2a)y+a)}\\
+\frac{\tau^{2}\;y}{(1-y)(ay^{2}+(\tau^{2}-2a)y+a)^{2}}+\frac{\tau^{4}k_{-1}\;y}{(1-y)^{2}(ay^{2}+(\tau^{2}-2a)y+a)^{2}}\bigg)R(\beta)=0\end{split}

(22)

If
a=0
, the dependence of the mass on the deformation is canceled, then we easily check that we get the equation (9) of ref.
[
11
]
.

The Schrodinger equation (
22
) cannot yet be solved analytically because of some terms. So, in the absence of a rigorous solution to this equation,
we can use a further approximation. For a small deformation parameter
a
(
a<<1
), as a first approximation, we can neglected the following terms:
ay^{2}-2ay+a
,
k_{1}+2a
and
\frac{k_{1}}{\tau^{4}y}
. So equation (
22
) becomes

\begin{split}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)+\frac{1}{y}R^{{}^{\prime}}(y)+\left[\frac{(\epsilon-k_{0})\tau^{2}+2k_{1}}{\tau^{4}y^{2}}-\frac{k_{-1}}{y(1-y)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}y(1-y)}\right]R(y)=0\end{split}

(23)

In order to transform the above differential equation to a more compact one, we use the following variables

\mu^{2}=-\frac{(\epsilon-k_{0})\tau^{2}+2k_{1}}{\tau^{4}},\;\;\;\;\nu=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+4k_{-1}})

(24)

So, the differential equation (
23
) becomes

\begin{split}R^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)+\frac{1}{y}R^{{}^{\prime}}(y)-\left[\frac{\mu^{2}}{y^{2}}+\frac{\nu^{2}-\nu}{y(1-y)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\tau^{2}y(1-y)}\right]R(y)=0\end{split}

(25)

To apply the asymptotic iteration method of refs.
[
15
,
16
]
, the reasonable physical wave function that we propose is as follows

R(y)=y^{\mu}(1-y)^{\nu}\chi(y)

(26)

For this form of the radial wave function, eq. (
25
) reads

\chi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)=-\frac{\omega(y)}{\sigma(y)}\;\;\chi^{{}^{\prime}}(y)-\frac{\kappa_{n}}{\sigma(y)}\;\;\chi(y)

(27)

with

\displaystyle\omega(y)

\displaystyle=(2\mu+1)-(2\mu+2\nu+1)y

(28a)

\displaystyle\sigma(y)

\displaystyle=y(1-y)

(28b)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}-\nu(2\mu+\nu)

(28c)

Equation (
27
) leads us directly to the energy eigenvalues using the new generalized formula
[
17
]
which replaced the iterative calculations in the original AIM formulation
[
18
]
.

\kappa_{n}=-n\;\omega^{{}^{\prime}}(y)-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\;\;\sigma^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(y)

(29)

The above formulation gives the energy spectrum of the
\beta
equation

\begin{split}\epsilon_{n}=-\left(\frac{\tau^{2}(n+\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+k_{-1}})^{2}-1}{2\tau(n+\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+k_{-1}})}\right)^{2}-2\;\frac{k_{1}}{\tau^{2}}+k_{0}\end{split}

(30)

where
n
is the principal quantum number and
k_{-1}
is defined previously as a function of
\Lambda
, which represents the eigenvalues of the
\gamma
-vibrational plus rotational part of the Hamiltonian for triaxial nuclei. If we set the deformation parameter
a=0
, our energy spectrum formula eq. (
30
) is in agreement with the energy formula obtained in previous works refs.
[
11
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
.

For eq.(
7
), which represents the
\gamma
variable, we use a new generalized potential proposed in
[
22
]
that is inspired by a ring-shaped potential

w(\gamma)=\frac{c+s\;cos^{2}(3\gamma)}{sin^{2}(3\gamma)}

(31)

where
c
and
s
are free parameters. Inserting this form of the potential in equation (
7
), we get

\begin{split}\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{\sin 3\gamma}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}sin3\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1,2,3}\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{sin^{2}(\gamma-\frac{2}{3}\pi k)}\\
+\;\frac{c+s\;cos^{2}(3\gamma)}{sin^{2}(3\gamma)}\bigg]\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})=\Lambda\;\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})\end{split}

(32)

Since the potential is minimal at
\gamma=\frac{\pi}{6}
, then the angular momentum term can be written as the form
[
23
,
24
]

\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1,2,3}\frac{Q^{2}_{k}}{sin^{2}(\gamma-\frac{2}{3}\pi k)}\approx{\bf Q}^{2}-\frac{3}{4}Q_{1}^{2}

(33)

With
{\bf Q}^{2}=Q_{1}^{2}+Q_{2}^{2}+Q_{3}^{2}

One then take wave functions of the form

\Phi(\gamma,\theta_{i})=\Gamma(\gamma)\;D_{M,\alpha}^{L}(\theta_{i})

(34)

Thus, the separation of variables leads to the following set of differential equations

\bigg[-\dfrac{1}{\sin 3\gamma}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}sin3\gamma\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}+\frac{c+s\;cos^{2}(3\gamma)}{sin^{2}(3\gamma)}\bigg]\;\Gamma(\gamma)=\Lambda^{{}^{\prime}}\;\Gamma(\gamma)

(35)

[Q^{2}-\frac{3}{4}Q_{1}^{2}]\;D_{M,\alpha}^{L}(\theta_{i})=\bar{\Lambda}\;D_{M,\alpha}^{L}(\theta_{i})

(36)

where
D(\theta_{i})
denotes Wigner functions of the Euler angles
\theta_{i}(i=1,2,3)
,
L
is the total angular momentum quantum number, while
M
and
\alpha
are the quantum numbers of the projections of angular momentum on the laboratory fixed
z
-axis and the body-fixed
x^{{}^{\prime}}
-axis, respectively.

Since the deformation function
f
depends only on the radial coordinate
\beta
, only the
\beta
part of the resulting equation was affected, the solution of the angular equation (see ref.
[
11
]
for details ) gives

\Lambda^{{}^{\prime}}=9n_{\gamma}(n_{\gamma}+1)+3\sqrt{c+s}(2n_{\gamma}+1)+c

(37)

\bar{\Lambda}=\frac{L(L+4)+3n_{w}(2L-n_{w})}{4}

(38)

where
n_{\gamma}
is the quantum number related to
\gamma
-excitation, and
n_{w}
the wobbling quantum number.

Finally, the analytical expression of
\Lambda
, which represents the eigenvalues of the
\gamma
-vibrational plus rotational part of the Hamiltonian for triaxial nuclei is

\Lambda=9n_{\gamma}(n_{\gamma}+1)+3\sqrt{c+s}(2n_{\gamma}+1)+c+\frac{L(L+4)+3n_{w}(2L-n_{w})}{4}

(39)

Conclusion

In this work, we have solved the eigenvalues problem with the Bohr collective Hamiltonian for triaxial nuclei within deformation-dependent mass formalism.
Using the potential of Hulthén, we have improved the accuracy of the results obtained in reference
[
11
]
except for isotopes with the deformation parameter
a
equal to
0
. From the comparison with the results obtained in Ref
[
1
]
using the potential of Davidson, one can conclude that the results for
{}^{126,128,130}Xe
isotopes are less accurate but for
{}^{132,134}Xe
and
{}^{192,194,196}Pt
isotopes, we have an improvement in accuracy. Our results confirm those obtained in Ref
[
1
]
concerning the vibratory nature of the isotopes
{}^{128,130,132,134}Xe
.

It has been shown that, whether with Hulthén’s potential or with Davidson’s potential, the results have been improved in general, compared whith the infinite square well. Consequently, for a better description of the experimental data or to enlarge the palette of applications, it is important to test other more flexible potentials.
