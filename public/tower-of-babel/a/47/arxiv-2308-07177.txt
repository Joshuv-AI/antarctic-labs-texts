Conformance Checking for Pushdown Reactive Systems based on Visibly Pushdown Languages
Adilson Luiz Bonifacio

Abstract
Testing pushdown reactive systems is deemed important to guarantee a precise and robust software development process. Usually, such systems can be specified by the formalism of Input/Output Visibly Pushdown Labeled Transition System (IOVPTS), where the interaction with the environment is regulated by a pushdown memory. Hence a conformance checking can be applied in a testing process to verify whether an implementation is in compliance to a specification using an appropriate conformance relation. In this work we establish a novelty conformance relation based on Visibly Pushdown Languages (VPLs) that can model sets of desirable and undesirable behaviors of systems. Further, we show that test suites with a complete fault coverage can be generated using this conformance relation for pushdown reactive systems.

Introduction

A large number of real-world systems, such as communication protocols (internet), vehicle and aircraft control systems, and most of industrial control systems, are indeed reactive systems, where their behavior is dictated by their interaction with the environment.
That is, reactive systems receive input stimuli from an external environment and, eventually, send back outputs in responses.

Reactive systems, in general, are critical applications and then their development process must be precise and robust.
Automatic supporting techniques are then deemed important, particularly in the testing activity, where high costs are associated to maintenance when the testing step is poorly conducted during the system development process.
Model-based testing meets such requirement relying on formal methods, then supporting several models and testing approaches for reactive systems.

Usually, reactive behaviors can be appropriately specified by the formalism of Input/Output Labeled Transition Systems (IOLTSs)
[
11
]
, where the exchange of input and output stimuli can occur asynchronously.
Hence a conformance checking can be applied in a testing process to verify whether an implementation is in compliance to a specification

.
This conformance checking process is established over a certain conformance relation
[

]
.

In this work we turn into a more complex reactive systems, where the interaction with the environment is regulated by a pushdown memory through the communication channel.
We study aspects of conformance testing and test suite generation for pushdown reactive systems that can be treated by Input/Output Visibly Pushdown Labeled Transition Systems (IOVPTSs)
[
4
]
and also by Visibly Pushdown Automata (VPAs)

.
In this setting a more general conformance relation has been given in the same spirit of the classical

.

Here we go further and address this problem in a more general setting, where particular desirable and undesirable behaviors can be specified by Visibly Pushdown Languages (VPLs)

The class of VPLs is strict more powerful than the classical regular languages treated by

We then propose and prove the correctness of an efficient algorithm that can check conformance in this scenario.
The language-based approach is established on a white-box testing scenario, where the structure of implementations under test (IUTs) are previously known by the tester, and the participating models have an auxiliary stack memory.

2.1
Visibly Pushdown Automata

A Visibly Pushdown Automaton (VPA)
[
1
]
is, basically, a Pushdown Automaton (PDA)
[
6
]
, with a transition relation over an alphabet and a pushdown stack (or just a stack, for short) associated to it.
Thus a VPA can make use of a potentially infinite memory as with PDA models.
Any alphabet

Elements in the set

are “call symbols”, or “push symbols”, and specify push actions on the stack.
Elements in
L_{r}
are “return symbols”, or “pop symbols”, and indicate pop actions, and in
L_{i}
we find ‘‘simple symbols’’, that do not change the stack
1

1

In
[
1
]
symbols in
L_{i}
are called “internal action symbols”.
In this text we will reserve that denomination for another special symbol as will be apparent later.
.

[

]
.
Here, we also allow
\varepsilon
-moves, that is, the VPA can change states without reading any symbol from the input.

Definition 1
.

A
Visibly Pushdown Automaton
(VPA)
[
1
]
over a finite input alphabet
A
is a tuple
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
, where:

—

A=A_{c}\cup A_{r}\cup A_{i}
and
A_{c}
,
A_{r}
,
A_{i}
are pairwise disjoint;

—

S
is a finite set of
states
;

—

S_{in}\subseteq S
is set of
initial states
;

—

\Gamma
is a finite
stack alphabet
, with
\bot\not\in\Gamma
the
initial stack symbol
;

—

The
transition relation
is
\rho=\rho_{c}\cup\rho_{r}\cup\rho_{i}
, where
\rho_{c}\subseteq S\times A_{c}\times\Gamma\times S
,
\rho_{r}\subseteq S\times A_{r}\times\Gamma_{\bot}\times S
, and

\rho_{i}\subseteq S\times(A_{i}\cup\{\varepsilon\})\times\{\sharp\}\times S
, where
\sharp\not\in\Gamma_{\bot}
is a place-holder symbol;

—

F\subseteq S
is the set of
final states
.

A transition
(p,\varepsilon,\sharp,q)\in\rho_{i}
is called an
\varepsilon
-move
of
\mathscr{A}
.
A
configuration
of
\mathscr{A}
is any triple
(p,\sigma,\alpha)\in S\times A^{\star}\times(\Gamma^{\star}\{\bot\})
, and the set of all configurations of
\mathscr{A}
it is indicated by
\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}
.

A tuple
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{c}
specifies a

.
We have a

if
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{r}
, and a

if
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{i}
.
The intended meaning for a push-transition
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{c}
is that
\mathscr{S}
, in state
p
and reading input

, changes to state
q
and pushes
Z
in the stack.
A pop-transition
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{r}
makes
\mathscr{S}
, in state
p
and reading

, pop
Z
from the stack and change to state
q
.
A simple-transition
(p,a,\sharp,q)\in\rho_{i}
has the intended meaning of reading

to state

, no matter what the topmost stack symbol is.
An

indicates that

must move from state

We can now define the relation
\underset{}{\mapsto}\subseteq\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}\times\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}
which captures simple moves of a VPA
\mathscr{A}
.
Note that the model can make pop moves when the stack contains only the initial stack symbol

.
The semantics of a VPA is the language comprised by all input strings it accepts.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a VPA.
For all
\sigma\in A^{\star}
:

1.

If
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{c}
then
(p,a\sigma,\alpha\bot)\underset{}{\mapsto}(q,\sigma,Z\alpha\bot)
;

2.

If
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho_{r}
then
(p,a\sigma,Z\alpha\bot)\underset{}{\mapsto}(q,\sigma,\alpha\bot)
when
Z\neq\bot
, or
(p,a\sigma,\bot)\underset{}{\mapsto}(q,\sigma,\bot)
when
Z=\bot
;

3.

If
(p,a,\sharp,q)\in\rho_{i}
then
(p,a\sigma,\alpha\bot)\underset{}{\mapsto}(q,\sigma,\alpha\bot)
.

The set
L(\mathscr{A})=\big\{\sigma\in A^{\star}\,|\,(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\beta),\,\,s_{0}\in S_{in},\,\,p\in F\big\}

is the
language accepted
by
\mathscr{A}
.
Two VPAs
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
are said to be

when
L(\mathscr{A})=L(\mathscr{B})
.

We may also write
(p,a\sigma,\alpha)\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}(q,\sigma,\gamma)
if it is important to explicitly mention the VPA
\mathscr{A}
.
It is clear that when
(p,a\sigma,\alpha)\underset{}{\mapsto}(q,\mu,\beta)
then we get
(q,\mu,\beta)\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}
, so that
\mapsto
is a relation on the set
\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}
.
The
n
-th power of the relation
\underset{}{\mapsto}
will be indicated by
\overset{n}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}
for all

, and its reflexive and transitive closure will be indicated by

.

A language is said to be a Visibly Pushdown Language (VPL) when it is accepted by a VPA.
That is, let

be an alphabet and let

be a language over

is a

Given any VPA, we can always construct and equivalent VPA with no
\varepsilon
-moves and the same number of states as the original VPA.

Proposition 4
.

For any VPA we can effectively construct an equivalent VPA with no
\varepsilon
-moves and with the same number of states.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
.
Define the mapping
E:S\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(S)
by

E(s)=\{p\,|\,(s,\varepsilon,\bot)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\bot),n\geq 0\},

that is,
E(s)
is the set of all states that can be reached from
s
through
\varepsilon
-moves.
A simple inductive algorithm guarantees that we can effectively compute
E
.

Consider the VPA
\mathscr{B}=\langle S,Q_{in},A,\Gamma,\mu,F\rangle
where

Q_{in}=\bigcup\limits_{s\in S_{in}}E(s)
, and
the set of transitions
\mu
is obtained from
\rho
by removing all
\varepsilon
-transitions from
\rho
, and then adding to
\mu
all transitions
(r,a,Z,p)
where
a\neq\varepsilon
,
(s,a,Z,q)
is a transition in
\rho
and, by means of
\varepsilon
-transitions alone, we can reach
s
from
r
and
p
from
q
, that is,

\mu=\left[\rho-\{(s,\varepsilon,\sharp,p)\,|\,s,p\in S\}\right]\bigcup\left\{(r,a,Z,p)\,|\,(s,a,Z,q)\in\rho,a\neq\varepsilon,s\in E(r),p\in E(q)\right\}.

Clearly,
\mathscr{B}
has no
\varepsilon
-moves, and has the same number of states as
\mathscr{A}
.

The next two claims will be used to show that
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
are equivalent VPAs.

Claim 1:

If
\sigma\neq\varepsilon
and
(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
, then

(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
.

Proof:
Let
(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
,
n\geq 1
and
\sigma=a\delta
where
a\in A
.
When
n=1
, we have
\delta=\varepsilon
and there is a transition
(s,a,Z,t)
in
\rho
.
Since
s\in E(s)
and
t\in E(t)
, the construction gives
(s,a,Z,t)
in
\mu
and the result follows immediately.
When
n>1
we may write

(s,\sigma,\alpha)=(s,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{k}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(r,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(q,\delta,\gamma)\overset{m}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta),

where
k\geq 0
and
k+1+m=n
, that is, the first
k
moves of
\mathscr{A}
are
\varepsilon
-moves, while the next one is a move over
a
, through a transition
(r,a,Z,q)\in\rho
, for some
Z\in\Gamma_{\bot}
.
Then,
r\in E(s)
.
If
\delta=\varepsilon
, the last
m
moves of
\mathscr{A}
are also
\varepsilon
-moves, and we get
\gamma=\beta
and
t\in E(q)
.
In this case, the construction gives
(s,a,Z,t)\in\mu
.
Since
\sigma=a\delta=a
we have

(s,\sigma,\alpha)=(s,a,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\gamma)=(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
, as desired.
Now let
\delta\neq\varepsilon
. Since
q\in E(q)
and we already have
r\in E(s)
, the construction gives
(s,a,Z,q)\in\mu
, so that
(s,\sigma,\alpha)=(s,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q,\delta,\gamma)
.
But
m<n
, and since
\delta\neq\varepsilon
, the induction gives
(q,\delta,\gamma)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
.
Hence,
(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\gamma)
and the claim holds.

Now let
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{A})
.
Then,
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\gamma)
where
s_{0}\in S_{in}
and
f\in F
.
When
\sigma=\varepsilon
we get
\gamma=\bot
and
f\in E(s_{0})
.
Since
s_{0}\in S_{in}
we get
f\in Q_{in}
.
Clearly,
(f,\varepsilon,\bot)\overset{0}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\bot)
and so
\sigma=\varepsilon\in L(\mathscr{B})
.
When
\sigma\neq\varepsilon
, Claim 1 gives
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\gamma)
.
Since
s_{0}\in E(s_{0})
, the construction gives
s_{0}\in Q_{in}
.
Hence,
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{B})
.
We may now conclude that
L(\mathscr{A})\subseteq L(\mathscr{B})
.

For the converse, we state

Claim 2:

If
(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
with
n\geq 0
, then

(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
.

Proof:
When
n=0
the result follows immediately. Let
n>0
.
Since
\mathscr{B}
has no
\varepsilon
-moves, we must have
\sigma=a\delta
with
a\in A
, and

(s,\sigma,\alpha)=(s,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(r,\delta,\gamma)\overset{n-1}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta),

where the first move was through a transition
(s,a,Z,r)\in\mu
.
By the construction, we must have
(p,a,Z,q)\in\rho
with
p\in E(s)
and
r\in E(q)
.
This gives
(s,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,a\delta,\alpha)
and
(q,\delta,\gamma)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(r,\delta,\gamma)
.
Composing we get

(s,\sigma,\alpha)=(s,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,a\delta,\alpha)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(q,\delta,\gamma)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(r,\delta,\gamma).

Using the induction hypothesis we get
(r,\delta,\gamma)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
.
So,
(s,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\beta)
and the claim holds.

Now let
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{B})
, so that
(q_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\gamma)
, with
q_{0}\in Q_{in}
and
t\in F
.
Using Claim 2, we may write
(q_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\gamma)
.
Also, the construction gives some
s_{0}\in S_{in}
with
q_{0}\in E(s_{0})
.
Hence,
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(q_{0},\sigma,\bot)
.
Composing we get
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(t,\varepsilon,\gamma)
, that is
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{A})
.
This shows that
L(\mathscr{B})\subseteq L(\mathscr{A})
.

We now have
L(\mathscr{A})=L(\mathscr{B})
, and the proof is complete. ∎

Next we define deterministic VPAs.
Determinism captures the idea that there is at most one computation for a given input string.
Since our notion of a VPA extends the original
[
1
]
, we also have to deal with
\varepsilon
-moves.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a VPA.
We say that
\mathscr{A}
is
deterministic
if
|S_{in}|\leq 1
, and the following conditions hold:

1.

(p,x,Z_{i},q_{i})\in\rho_{c}
with
i=1,2
, implies
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
and
q_{1}=q_{2}
;

2.

(p,x,Z,q_{i})\in\rho_{r}\cup\rho_{i}
with
i=1,2
, implies
q_{1}=q_{2}
;

3.

(p,x,Z,q_{1})\in\rho
with
x\neq\varepsilon
, implies
(p,\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{2})\not\in\rho
for all
q_{2}\in S
.

A language
L
is a
deterministic VPL
if
L=L(\mathscr{A})
for some deterministic VPA
\mathscr{A}
.

It is worth noticing that Definition
5
does not prohibit
\varepsilon
-moves in deterministic VPAs.
With deterministic VPAs, however, computations are always unique, as expected.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a deterministic VPA, let
(p,\sigma,\alpha)\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{A}}
, and take
n\geq 0
.
Then
(p,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{n}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(q_{i},\mu_{i},\gamma_{i})
,
i=1,2
, implies

(q_{1},\mu_{1},\gamma_{1})=(q_{2},\mu_{2},\gamma_{2})
.

Proof.

By induction on
n\geq 0
. When
n=0
the result is immediate.
Take
n>0
.
Using the induction hypothesis we can write
(p,\sigma,\alpha)\overset{n-1}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(q,\mu,\gamma)\overset{1}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(q_{i},\mu_{i},\gamma_{i})
, where the last single steps
used the transitions
(q,x_{i},Z_{i},q_{i})\in\rho
,
i=1,2
.

First assume that
x_{1}=\varepsilon
. Then Definition
1
gives
(q,x_{1},Z_{1},q_{1})=(q,\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{1})\in\rho_{i}
.
From
(q,\mu,\gamma)\overset{}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(q_{1},\mu_{1},\gamma_{1})
and Definition
3
, we conclude that
\mu_{1}=\mu
and
\gamma=\gamma_{1}
.
Since
\mathscr{A}
is deterministic, Definition
5
(3) forces

x_{2}=\varepsilon=x_{1}
, and then Definition
1
gives
(q,x_{2},Z_{2},q_{2})=(q,\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{2})\in\rho_{i}
.
So, from
(q,\mu,\gamma)\overset{}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(q_{2},\mu_{2},\gamma_{2})
we now get

\gamma=\gamma_{2}

5

, and then we have

Likewise when

x_{1}
,

It suffices to verify that

The next result shows that we can remove
\varepsilon
-moves, while still preserving determinism.

Proposition 7
.

Given a deterministic VPA
\mathscr{A}
, we can obtain an equivalent deterministic VPA
\mathscr{B}
with no
\varepsilon
-moves and with the same number of states.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
.
First we eliminate cycles of
\varepsilon
-transitions, and in a second step we eliminate the remaining
\varepsilon
-transitions.
Let

\displaystyle(s_{1},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{2}),(s_{2},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{3}),\ldots,(s_{k-1},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{k}),(s_{k},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{k+1}),

(1)

with
s_{k+1}=s_{1}
and
k\geq 1
, be a
\varepsilon
-cycle in
\mathscr{A}
.
We construct the new VPA
\mathscr{B}=\langle Q,Q_{in},A,\Gamma,\mu,E\rangle

s_{1}
.
Let
T=\{(s_{j},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{j+1}):j=1,\ldots,k\}
,
J=\{s_{j}:j=1,\ldots,k\}
.
Start with
\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{A}
and transform
\mathscr{B}
as follows:

(a)

Transitions: (i) remove

from
\mu
; (ii) for all
(p,x,Z,q)\in\rho

and
q\in J

(p,x,Z,q)

\mu

(b)

s_{1}
to

We have to show that
\mathscr{B}
is equivalent to
\mathscr{A}
, and that
\mathscr{B}
is deterministic.

Claim 1:

\mathscr{B}
is deterministic.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{A}
is deterministic,
|S_{in}|\leq 1
.
Hence, it is clear that
|Q_{in}|\leq 1
.
We now look at each conditions in Definition
5
.

Suppose that

,

.
If

, the determinism of

immediately gives

and so condition (1) in Definition
5
holds.
With no loss of generality assume

.
Then item (a) of the construction forces
p\not\in J

, and
t^{\prime}_{1}=(p,x,Z_{1},s_{j})\in\rho

s_{j}\in J
.
If

is deterministic, condition (1) in Definition

and
q_{2}=s_{j}
, so that
t_{2}=(p,x,Z_{2},s_{j})\in\mu_{c}
.
If

Hence,
j=1

q_{2}=s_{j}=s_{1}=q_{1}
.
Together with

5
holds.
Assume now
t_{2}\not\in\rho
.
We obtain again
q_{2}=s_{1}
and
t^{\prime}_{2}=(p,x,Z_{2},s_{\ell})\in\rho

s_{\ell}\in J
.
Now
t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}\in\rho
and the determinism of
\mathscr{A}
forces
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
.
We now have
q_{1}=s_{1}=q_{2}
,
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
and condition (1) in Definition
5
holds again.

Suppose that
t_{i}=(p,x,Z,q_{i})\in\mu_{r}\cup\mu_{i}
,
i=1,2
.
An argument entirely similar to the one in the preceding paragraph shows that condition (2) in Definition
5
holds.

Now let
t_{1}=(p,x,Z,q_{1})\in\mu
and
t_{2}=(p,\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{2})\in\mu
, with
x\neq\varepsilon
.
If
t_{1},t_{2}\in\rho
we get an immediate contradiction to the determinism of
\mathscr{A}
.
Assume
t_{1}\not\in\rho
.
As before the construction gives
t^{\prime}_{1}=(p,x,Z,s_{j})\in\rho
for some
s_{j}\in J
.
Since
x\neq\varepsilon
, if

we get a contradiction to the determinism of
\mathscr{A}
.
Hence,
t_{2}\not\in\rho
implies, by the construction, that

Again,

\mathscr{A}
.
Finally, when

\mathscr{A}
will force
t_{2}\not\in\rho
, and the construction will give

for some
s_{\ell}\in J

Then,

contradicts the determinism of
\mathscr{A}
again because

.
We conclude that
t_{1}=(p,x,Z,q_{1})\in\mu
and
t_{2}=(p,\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{2})\in\mu

5
also holds.

Next, we want to argue for language equivalence. First we show that
\mathscr{A}

.

Claim 2:

If
(q,\sigma,\alpha_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha_{2}\bot)

(q,\sigma,\alpha_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha_{2}\bot)

2.2
The Synchronous Product of VPAs

The product of two VPAs captures their synchronous behavior.
It will be useful when testing conformance between pushdown memory models.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
and
\mathscr{Q}=\langle Q,Q_{in},A,\Delta,\mu,G\rangle
be VPAs with a common alphabet.
Their product is the VPA

\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}=\langle S\times Q,S_{in}\times Q_{in},A,\Gamma\times\Delta,\nu,F\times G\rangle
, where
((s_{1},q_{1}),a,Z,(s_{2},q_{2}))\in\nu
if and only if either:

1.

a\neq\varepsilon
and we have
(s_{1},a,Z_{1},s_{2})\in\rho
and
(q_{1},a,Z_{2},q_{2})\in\mu
with
Z=(Z_{1},Z_{2})
or
Z=Z_{1}=Z_{2}\in\{\bot,\sharp\}
; or

2.

a=\varepsilon
,
Z=\sharp
and we have either
s_{1}=s_{2}
and
(q_{1},\varepsilon,\sharp,q_{2})\in\mu
; or
q_{1}=q_{2}
and
(s_{1},\varepsilon,\sharp,s_{2})\in\rho
.

\varepsilon
-moves when the original VPAs satisfy such conditions.

((t,q),\delta,\gamma_{0})\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma)
.
From Eq. (

((s,q),\sigma,\gamma_{0})\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma)
.
Recalling the definitions of

,

,
\beta_{n}
, and of
\gamma_{0}
,
\gamma
, we see that the result holds in this case.

We now turn to the case

.
If

.
But then

Thus,

.
This gives

(q,a\delta,\beta_{0})\overset{1}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(u,\delta,\beta_{1})\overset{m-1}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta_{m}),

(12)

and we must have a transition
(q,a,W,u)\in\mu

There are three cases.

Case 3:

a\in A_{i}
.
Then, Eqs. (

,

.
From the same equations, we now obtain

The induction hypothesis now implies
((t,u),\delta,\gamma_{0})\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma)
.
Since
(s,a,\sharp,t)\in\rho

8
item (1)

, and we may write
((s,q),a\delta,\gamma_{0})\overset{1}{\underset{\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}((t,q),\delta,\gamma_{0})
.

Case 4:

a\in A_{c}
.
From Eq. (

(s,a,X,t)\in\rho

And from Eq. (

((s,q),a\delta,\gamma_{0})\overset{1}{\underset{\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}((t,u),\delta,(X,Y)\gamma_{0})

Inductively, Eqs. (

Case 5:

a\in A_{r}
, and recall that
\alpha_{0}=X_{1}\ldots X_{i}\bot

When

Let

.
From Eq. (

,

.
Because

,

.
Since

2.3
Closure Properties over Visibly Pushdown Languages

Now we look at some closure properties involving VPLs.
Similar results appeared elsewhere
[
1
]
, but here the VPA models are somewhat more general because they allow for
\varepsilon
-moves,
which can make a difference in the results.
Moreover, it will prove important to investigate if determinism, when present in the participant VPAs, can also be guaranteed for the resulting VPAs.
Further, since later on we will be analyzing the complexity of certain constructions, we also note how the sizes of the resulting models vary
as a function of the size of the given models.

First we report on a simple result on the stack size during runs of VPAs.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
and
\mathscr{B}=\langle Q,Q_{in},A,\Delta,\mu,G\rangle
be VPAs over a common alphabet
A
.
Consider starting configurations
(s_{1},\sigma,\alpha_{1}\bot)
and
(q_{1},\sigma,\beta_{1}\bot)
of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, respectively,
and where
|\alpha_{1}|=|\beta_{1}|
.
If
(s_{1},\sigma,\alpha_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(s_{2},\varepsilon,\alpha_{2}\bot)
and
(q_{1},\sigma,\beta_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q_{2},\varepsilon,\beta_{2}\bot)
, then we must have
|\alpha_{2}|=|\beta_{2}|
.

Proof.

A simple induction on
n=|\sigma|
.
When
n=0
we get
\sigma=\varepsilon
.
According to Definition
3
,
\varepsilon
-moves do not change the stack, and so we get
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}
and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
, and the result follows.

Assume
n\geq 1
and
\sigma=\delta a
, where
a\in A
and
|\delta|=n-1
.
Then computations can be separated thus

\displaystyle(s_{1},\delta a,\alpha_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(s_{3},a,\alpha_{3}\bot)\overset{1}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(s_{4},\varepsilon,\alpha_{4}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(s_{2},\varepsilon,\alpha_{2}\bot)

\displaystyle(q_{1},\delta a,\beta_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q_{3},a,\beta_{3}\bot)\overset{1}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q_{4},\varepsilon,\beta_{4}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q_{2},\varepsilon,\beta_{2}\bot).

Clearly, we get
(s_{1},\delta,\alpha_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(s_{3},\varepsilon,\alpha_{3}\bot)
and
(q_{1},\delta,\beta_{1}\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{B}}{\mapsto}}(q_{3},\varepsilon,\beta_{3}\bot)
.
The induction hypothesis implies
|\alpha_{3}|=|\beta_{3}|
.
The proof will be complete if we can show that
|\alpha_{4}|=|\beta_{4}|
, because from these configurations onward we have only
\varepsilon
-moves, which would imply that
|\alpha_{2}|=|\beta_{2}|
.

Let
(s_{3},a,Z,s_{4})
and
(q_{3},a,W,q_{4})
be the
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
transitions, respectively, used in the one-step
computations, as indicated above.
From Definition
3
we have three simple cases: (i) when
a\in A_{i}\cup\{\varepsilon\}
we get
\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{4}
and
\beta_{3}=\beta_{4}
;
(ii) when
a\in A_{c}
we get
\alpha_{4}=Z\alpha_{3}
and
\beta_{4}=W\beta_{3}
;
and (iii) when
a\in A_{r}
, since
|\alpha_{3}|=|\beta_{3}|
, we get either
\alpha_{3}\neq\varepsilon\neq\beta_{3}
and then
\alpha_{3}=Z\alpha_{4}
and
\beta_{3}=W\beta_{4}
, or

\alpha_{3}=\varepsilon=\beta_{3}
and then
\alpha_{4}=\varepsilon=\beta_{4}
.
In any case we see that
|\alpha_{4}|=|\beta_{4}|
, because we already have
|\alpha_{3}|=|\beta_{3}|
.
∎

Using the product construction, we can show that VPLs are also closed under intersection.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
and
\mathscr{Q}=\langle Q,Q_{in},A,\Delta,\mu,G\rangle
be VPAs with
n
and
m
states, respectively.
Then
L(\mathscr{S})\cap L(\mathscr{Q})
can be accepted by a VPA
\mathscr{P}
with
mn
states.
Moreover, if
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
are deterministic, then
\mathscr{P}
is also deterministic.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{Q}=\langle S\times Q,S_{in}\times Q_{in},A,\Gamma\times\Delta,\nu,F\times G\rangle

be the product of
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
.
See Definition
8
.
It is clear that
\mathscr{P}
has
nm
states.

For the language equivalence, assume that
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{P})
, so that

((s,q),\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{P}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma\bot)
with
(s,q)\in S_{in}\times Q_{in}
and

(p,r)\in F\times G
, for some
\gamma\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
Using Proposition
10
we get
(s,\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)

for some
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
.
Since
s\in S_{in}
and
p\in F
, we conclude that
\sigma\in\ L(\mathscr{S})
.
Likewise,
\sigma\in\ L(\mathscr{Q})
, so that
\sigma\in\ L(\mathscr{S})\cap L(\mathscr{Q})
.
For the converse, assume that
s\in S_{in}
,
p\in F
and
(s,\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)

for some
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
. Likewise, let
q\in Q_{in}
,
r\in G
and
(q,\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)

for some
\beta\in\Delta^{\star}
.
From Proposition
11
we get
|\alpha|=|\beta|
.
We can now apply Proposition
10
and write

((s,q),\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{P}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma\bot)
for some
\gamma\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
Since
(p,r)\in F\times G
we get
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{P})
, and the equivalence holds.

Finally, Proposition
9
guarantees that
\mathscr{P}
is deterministic when
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
are deterministic.
∎

Now, we investigate the closure of VPLs under union.
But first, in order to complete the argument for the union, we need to consider VPAs that can always read any string of input symbols when started at any state and with any stack configuration.

Definition 13
.

Let
\mathscr{A}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a VPA.
We say that
\mathscr{A}
is a
non-blocking
VPA if, for all
s\in S
, all
\sigma\in A^{\star}
and all
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
, there are
p\in S
and
\beta\in\Gamma^{\star}
such that
(s,\sigma,\alpha\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{A}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
.

Any VPA can be easily turned into a non-blocking VPA, with almost no cost in the number of states.

Proposition 14
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a VPA with
n
states.
Then we can construct an equivalent VPA
\mathscr{Q}
with at most
n+1
states and which is also a non-blocking VPA.
Moreover,
\mathscr{Q}
is deterministic if
\mathscr{S}
is deterministic, and
\mathscr{Q}
has no
\varepsilon
-moves if so does
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{Q}=\langle S\cup\{p\},S_{in},A,\Gamma,\mu,F\rangle
where
p\not\in S
is a new state.
In order to construct the new transition set
\mu
as an extension of
\rho
, first pick some stack symbol
Z\in\Gamma
.
Then, for all
s\in S
such that there is no
\varepsilon
-transition out of
s
, that is
(s,\varepsilon,\sharp,q)
is not in
\rho
for any
q\in S
, we proceed as follows.
For any input symbol
a\in A
:

1.

a\in A_{i}
: if
(s,a,\sharp,r)\not\in\rho
for all
r\in S
, add the
transition
(s,a,\sharp,p)
to
\mu
;

2.

a\in A_{c}
: if
(s,a,W,r)\not\in\rho
for all
W\in\Gamma
and all
r\in S
, add the
transition
(s,a,Z,p)
to
\mu
;

3.

a\in A_{r}
: if
(s,a,W,r)\not\in\rho
for some
W\in\Gamma_{\bot}
and all
r\in S
, add the
transition
(s,a,W,p)
to
\mu
.

Finally, add to
\mu
the self-loops
(p,a,\sharp,p)
for all
a\in A_{i}
,
(p,a,Z,p)
for all
a\in A_{c}
, and

(p,a,W,p)
for all
a\in A_{r}
and all
W\in\Gamma_{\bot}
.

It is clear now that
\mathscr{S}
has
n+1
states.
Moreover, for all
s\in S
and all
a\in A
the construction readily allows for a move
(s,a,\alpha\bot)\overset{}{\underset{}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
for all
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
.
Hence, an easy induction on
|\sigma|\geq 0
shows that for all
s\in S
and all
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
there will always be a computation
(s,\sigma,\alpha\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
, for some
r\in S
and some
\beta\in\Gamma^{\star}
.
That is, the modified version is a non-blocking VPA.

The construction adds no
\varepsilon
-moves, so it is clear that
\mathscr{Q}
has no
\varepsilon
-moves when we start with a VPA
\mathscr{S}
that already has no
\varepsilon
-moves.

Assume that
\mathscr{S}
is deterministic.
Since the construction adds no
\varepsilon
-moves, the new VPA
\mathscr{Q}
does not violate condition (3) of Definition
5
.
Also, none of the self-loops added at the new state
p
violate any of the conditions of Definition
5
.
When
a\in A_{c}
a new transition
(s,a,Z,p)
is only added to
\mathscr{Q}
when there are no transition
(s,a,X,r)
already in
\mathscr{S}
, for any
X\in\Gamma
,
r\in S
.
Hence,
\mathscr{Q}
does not violate condition (1) of Definition
5
.
Likewise, we only add
(s,a,\sharp,p)
, or
(s,a,W,p)
, to
\mu
when we find no
(s,a,\sharp,r)
, respectively we find no
(s,a,W,r)
, in
\rho
for any
r
in
S
.
Hence,
\mathscr{Q}
does not violate condition (2) of Definition
5
.
We conclude that
\mathscr{Q}
is deterministic when
\mathscr{S}
is already deterministic.

If
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
, with
s_{0}\in S_{in}
,
f\in F
,
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
then we also have
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
, because all transitions in
\rho
are also in
\mu
.
Hence,
L(\mathscr{S})\subseteq L(\mathscr{Q})
.
For the converse, assume
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
, with
s_{0}\in S_{in}
,
f\in F
,
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
.
Note that all new transitions added to
\mu
have the new state
p
as a target state.
Thus, since the new state
p
is not in
S_{in}
nor in
F
, we see that all transitions used in this run over
\mathscr{Q}
are also in
\rho
, and
we then get
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(f,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
.
Thus we also have
L(\mathscr{Q})\subseteq L(\mathscr{S})
, showing that
L(\mathscr{S})=L(\mathscr{Q})
.
∎

Now the closure of VPLs under union is at hand.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
be two VPAs over an alphabet
A
, with
n
and
m
states, respectively.
Then, we can construct a non-blocking VPA
\mathscr{P}
over
A
with at most
(n+1)(m+1)
states and such that
L(\mathscr{P})=L(\mathscr{S})\cup L(\mathscr{Q})
.
Moreover, if
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
are deterministic, then
\mathscr{P}
is also deterministic and has no
\varepsilon
-moves.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
and
\mathscr{Q}=\langle Q,Q_{in},A,\Delta,\mu,G\rangle
.
Using Proposition
14
we can assume that
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
are non-blocking VPAs with
n+1
and
m+1
states, respectively.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be the product of
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
as in Definition
8
, except that we redefine the final states of
\mathscr{P}
as
(F\times Q)\cup(S\times G)
.
Clearly,
\mathscr{P}
has
(n+1)(m+1)
states.

We now argue that
\mathscr{P}
is also a non-blocking VPA.
Let
\sigma\in A^{\star}
,
(s,q)\in S\times Q
, and let
\gamma=(Z_{1},W_{1})\ldots(Z_{k},W_{k})\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
Since
\mathscr{S}
is a non-blocking VPA, we get
n\geq 0
,
p\in S
,
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
such that

(s,\sigma,Z_{1}\ldots Z_{k}\bot)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
.
Likewise,
(q,\sigma,W_{1}\ldots W_{k}\bot)\overset{m}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
, for some

m\geq 0
,
r\in Q
,
\beta\in\Delta^{\star}
.
Applying Proposition
11
we get
|\alpha|=|\beta|
, and then using Proposition
10
we have

((s,q),\sigma,\gamma\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{P}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\delta\bot)
where
\gamma=(Z_{1},W_{1})\ldots(Z_{k},W_{k})
and
\delta\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
This shows that
\mathscr{P}
is a non-blocking VPA.

Now suppose that
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{P})
, that is
((s_{0},q_{0}),\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{P}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
, where
(s_{0},q_{0})\in S_{in}\times Q_{in}
,
(p,r)\in(F\times Q)\cup(S\times G)
, and
\alpha\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
Take the case when
(p,r)\in(F\times Q)
.
We get
p\in F
and
s_{0}\in S_{in}
.
Using Proposition
10
we can also write
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
, for some
\beta\in\Gamma^{\star}
.
This shows that
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{S})
.
By a similar reasoning, when
(p,r)\in(S\times G)
we get
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{Q})
.
Thus,
L(\mathscr{P})\subseteq L(\mathscr{S})\cup L(\mathscr{Q})
.

Now let
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{S})\cup L(\mathscr{Q})
.
Take the case
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{S})
, the case
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{Q})
being similar.
Then we must have
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
for some
n\geq 0
, some
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
, and some
p\in F
.
Pick any
q_{0}\in Q_{in}
.
Since
\mathscr{Q}
is a non-blocking VPA, Definition
13
gives some
r\in Q
and some
\beta\in\Delta^{\star}
such that
(q_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{m}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\beta\bot)
for some
m\geq 0
, some
r\in Q
and some
\beta\in\Delta^{\star}
.
Using Proposition
11
we conclude that
|\alpha|=|\beta|
.
The only-if part of Proposition
10
now yields

((s_{0},q_{0}),\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{P}}{\mapsto}}((p,r),\varepsilon,\gamma\bot)
, where
\gamma\in(\Gamma\times\Delta)^{\star}
.
Clearly,
(s_{0},q_{0})\in S_{in}\times Q_{in}
is an initial state of
\mathscr{P}
and
(p,r)\in(F\times Q)\cup(S\times G)
is a final state of
\mathscr{P}
.
Hence
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{P})
.
Thus
L(\mathscr{S})\cup L(\mathscr{Q})\subseteq L(\mathscr{P})
, and so
L(\mathscr{S})\cup L(\mathscr{Q})=L(\mathscr{P})
.

Applying Proposition
9
(2) we see that when
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
are deterministic, then
\mathscr{P}
is also deterministic and has no
\varepsilon
-moves.

The proof is now complete.
∎

We can also show that deterministic VPLs are closed under complementation.
A related result appeared in
[
1
]
, but here we also allow for arbitrary
\varepsilon
-moves in any model.

Proposition 16
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,F\rangle
be a deterministic VPA with
n
states. Then, we can construct a non-blocking and deterministic VPA
\mathscr{Q}
over
A
with no
\varepsilon
-moves,
n+1
states, and such that
L(\mathscr{Q})=\overline{L(\mathscr{S})}=\Sigma^{\star}-L(\mathscr{S})
.

Proof.

Applying Propositions
7
and
14
, we can assume that
\mathscr{S}
is a non-blocking and deterministic VPA with
n+1
states and no
\varepsilon
-moves.

Let
\mathscr{Q}=\langle S,S_{in},A,\Gamma,\rho,S-F\rangle
, that is, we switch the final states of
\mathscr{S}
.
Clearly, since
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{Q}
have the same set of initial states and the same transition relation,
we see that
\mathscr{Q}
is also a non-blocking and deterministic VPA with
n+1
states and no
\varepsilon
-moves.

Pick any
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{Q})
, and let
n=|\sigma|
.
Since
\mathscr{Q}
has no
\varepsilon
-moves, we must have
(q_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{n}{\underset{\mathscr{Q}}{\mapsto}}(r,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
for some
q_{0}\in S_{in}
,
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
, and some
r\in S-F
.
For the sake of contradiction, assume that
\sigma\in L(\mathscr{S})

Then, because

Since

have exactly the same set of transitions, we can now write

.

Now let

.
Since

is non-blocking, we have
(s_{0},\sigma,\bot)\overset{\star}{\underset{\mathscr{S}}{\mapsto}}(p,\varepsilon,\alpha\bot)
for some

and
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}

Because

we need

.
Since

and
\mathscr{Q}
have the same set of transitions, we can write

Thus,

.
Hence,
\overline{L(\mathscr{S})}\subseteq L(\mathscr{Q})

3
Reactive Pushdown Models

In this section we introduce the Visibly Pushdown Labeled Transition System (VPTS), and its variation Input/Output VPTS, both models that can properly specify pushdown reactive systems.
We also discuss the notion of contracted VPTSs, an important property to allow the model-based testing process, and state the relationship with its associated VPA.

3.1
The formalism of VPTS

Visibly Pushdown Labeled Transition System (VPTS) is an appropriate formalism with a potentially infinite memory that allow us to model pushdown reactive systems.
VPTS models can specify the asynchronous exchange of messages between a system and the environment, where the outputs do not have to occur synchronously with inputs,
i.e.
, output messages are generated as separated events.
Next we formally define VPTS models.

Definition 17
.

A
Visibly Pushdown Labeled Transition System
(VPTS) over an input alphabet
L
is a tuple
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
, where:

—

S
is a finite set of
states
or
locations
;

—

S_{in}\subseteq S
is the set of
initial states
;

—

There is a special symbol
\varsigma\notin L
, the
internal action symbol
;

—

\Gamma
is a set of
stack symbols
.
There is a special symbol
\bot\not\in\Gamma
, the
bottom-of-stack symbol
;

—

T=T_{c}\cup T_{r}\cup T_{i}
, where
T_{c}\subseteq S\times L_{c}\times\Gamma\times S
,
T_{r}\subseteq S\times L_{r}\times\Gamma_{\bot}\times S
, and
T_{i}\subseteq S\times(L_{i}\cup\{\varsigma\})\times\{\sharp\}\times S
, where
\sharp\not\in\Gamma_{\bot}
is a place-holder symbol.

Assume that
t=(p,x,Z,q)
is a transition of
T
.
We call by
push-transition
when
t\in T_{c}
and the meaning is reading an input
x
when moving from the state
p
to
q
in
\mathscr{S}
, and pushes
Z
onto the stack.
We also call
pop-transition
if
t\in T_{r}
, and in this case, the intended meaning is that, changing
\mathscr{S}
from
p
to
q
,
x\in L_{r}
is read in
\mathscr{S}
, and pops
Z
from the stack.
Notice that a pop move can be taken when the stack is empty, leaving the stack unchanged, if the pop symbol is
\bot
.
Finally, we call by a
simple-transition
when
t\in T_{i}
and
x\in L_{i}
,
and by an
internal-transition
when
t\in T_{i}
and
x=\varsigma
.
A simple-transition
t
that reads
x
when moving from
p
to
q
does not change the stack.
Likewise an internal-transition does not change the stack, but further does not read any symbol from the input.

Next we give the notion of configurations and precisely define elementary moves in VPTS models.

Definition 18
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle

A

.

1.

\ell\in L_{c}
, and
\beta=Z\alpha
;

2.

\ell\in L_{r}

\alpha=Z\beta
, or (ii)

3.

\ell\in L_{i}\cup\{\varsigma\}
and

Then an

of
\mathscr{S}
is represented by
(p,\alpha)\overset{\ell}{\rightarrow}(q,\beta)
when the transition

Further, after any elementary move

\mathscr{S}
.

next to the corresponding arc from

to

will be indicated by the label

to
q

When a sequence of events is induced over a VPTS model we can observe its behavior.
That is, we can obtain the semantics of a VPTS by its traces, or behaviors.

Definition 20
.

Let

Definition 21
.

Let

1.

.

.

2.

The

of

, and the

of

is

\bigcup\limits_{q\in S_{in}}\!\!\!\!otr(q,\bot)
.

We will also indicate the semantics and, respectively, the observable semantics, of

by

and
otr(\mathscr{S})
.
If
(s,\alpha)\overset{}{\Rightarrow}(p,\beta)

(s,\alpha)\overset{}{\rightarrow}(p,\beta)
in
\mathscr{S}
, for all
(s,\alpha)
,
(p,\beta)\in\mathscr{C}_{\mathscr{S}}
.
Moreover,

and when

has no internal transitions we already have

.

We also note that
\varsigma
-labeled self-loops can also be eliminated in a VPTS since they play no role when considering any system behaviors.
So given a VPTS
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
, for any

we postulate that
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\sigma}{\rightarrow}(s,\alpha\bot)

\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
,
\sigma\in L_{\varsigma}^{\star}
, and

.
Further, if

then

.
In general,
\varsigma
-moves indicate that a VPTS can autonomously move along
\varsigma
-transitions, without consuming any input symbol.
But in some cases such moves may be undesirable, or simply we might want no observable behavior leading to two distinct states.
Then we need the notion of determinism in VPTS models.

Definition 22
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
be a VPTS.
We say that
\mathscr{S}
is

,
p\in S_{in}
,
s_{1}
,
s_{2}\in S
,

, and

we have that
(s,\bot)\overset{\sigma}{\underset{}{\Rightarrow}}(s_{1},\beta_{1}\bot)
and

imply

and
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}
.

Net we show that deterministic VPTSs do not have internal moves.

Proposition 23
.

Let
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
be a deterministic VPTS.
Then
\mathscr{S}
has no
\varsigma
-labeled transitions.

Proof.

By contradiction, assume that
(s,\varsigma,\sharp,q)\in T
.
Since there is no self-loop with label
\varsigma
we get
s\neq q
and then
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
,
\sigma\in\ L^{\star}
such that
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\sigma}{\rightarrow}(s,\alpha\bot)
, with
s_{0}\in S_{in}
.
Hence,
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\sigma}{\rightarrow}(s,\alpha\bot)\overset{\varsigma}{\rightarrow}(q,\alpha\bot)

Using Definition
21
we get
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\mu}{\Rightarrow}(s,\alpha\bot)
and
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\mu}{\Rightarrow}(q,\alpha\bot)

\mu=h_{\varsigma}(\sigma)
.
Since

∎

3.2
Contracted VPTSs

The syntactic description of VPTSs can also be reduced, without loosing any semantic capability, by removing states that are not reachable from any initial state.
Moreover, we can remove transitions in a VPTS model that cannot be exercised by some trace.
Since every transition, except possibly for pop transitions, can always be taken, we concentrate on the pop transitions.

Definition 24
.

We say that a VPTS
\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
is
contracted
if for every transition
(p,b,Z,r)\in T
with

s_{0}\in S_{in}
,
\alpha\in\Gamma^{\star}
and
\sigma\in L^{\star}
such that
(s_{0},\bot)\overset{\sigma}{\Rightarrow}(p,\alpha\bot)

\alpha=Z\beta
for some
\beta\in\Gamma^{\star}
, or (ii)
\alpha=\varepsilon
and
Z=\bot
.

We can obtain contracted VPTSs using the next Proposition
25

The idea is to construct a context free grammar (CFG) based on the given VPTS, in such a way that the CFG generates strings where terminals represent VPTS transitions.
The productions of the CFG will indicate the set of transitions that can be effectively used in a trace over the VPTS.

\mathscr{S}=\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle

3.4
Input Output Pushdown Transition Systems

The VPTS formalism can be used to model systems with a potentially infinite memory and with a capacity to interact asynchronously with an external environment.
In such situations, we may want to treat some action labels as symbols that the VPTS “receives” from the environment, and some other action labels as symbols that the VPTS “sends back” to the environment.
The next VPTS variation differentiates between input action symbols and output action symbols.

Definition 29
.

An Input/Output Visibly Pushdown Transition System (IOVPTS) over an alphabet

, or

;

•

output actions
, or
output labels

•

, and

is the set of
actions
or
labels
; and

•

\langle S,S_{in},L,\Gamma,T\rangle
is an

over

.

In this section we define a more general conformance relation based on Visibly Pushdown Languages
[

, a proper subset of the more general class of context-free languages
[

In sequel we give a

4.1
A General Conformance Relation for VPTS models

The more general conformance relation is defined on subset of words specified by a tester.
Informally, consider a language
D

F
, the set of “forbidden” behaviors, of a system.
If we have a specification VPTS

\mathscr{S}

\mathscr{I}

\mathscr{S}

Definition 32
.

Let

and

\sigma\in otr(\mathscr{I})\cap F

2.

\sigma\in otr(\mathscr{I})\cap D

\sigma\in otr(\mathscr{S})
.

We note an equivalent way of expressing these conditions that may also be useful.

.

Proof.

From Definition
32
we readily get

4.2
Fault Model over Visibly Pushdown Languages

When testing pushdown reactive systems we have notice that not only their natural reactive behavior with the environment must considered but also we need to deal with a potentially infinite memory.
A fault model for systems of this nature must then be able to find faults in IUTs according to their corresponding specification modeled by VPTSs,
i.e.
,
the a fault model must provide a fault detection in the testing process.
A sequence of symbols that can detect faults in VPTS models is called a
test case
, and a set of test cases gives the notion of a
test suite
.
So, a test suite
T
over an alphabet
L
is a language over
L
,
i.e.

T\subseteq L^{\star}
, should be engineered to detect faulty observable behavior of any given IUT, when compared to what has been determined by a specification.
In this case,
T
can be seen as specifying a fault model, in the sense that test cases in
T
represent faulty observable behaviors.
In particular, if
T
is a VPL, then it can be specified by a VPA
\mathscr{A}
.
Alternatively, we could specify
T
by a finite set of VPAs, so that the union of all the undesirable behaviors specified by these VPAs comprise the fault model.

Next we say that an implementation
\mathscr{I}
passes a test suite
T
if no observable behavior of
\mathscr{I}
is a harmful behavior present in
T
.

Definition 35
.

Let
T
be a test suite over an alphabet
L
.
A VPTS
\mathscr{Q}
over
L

passes
to
T
if
\sigma\not\in T
for all
\sigma\in otr(\mathscr{Q})
.
Further, an IOVPTS
\mathscr{I}
over
L
passes to
T
if its underlying VPTS passes to
T
.

In a testing process we also desire test suites to be sound,
i.e.
, when
\mathscr{I}
passes a test suite

\mathscr{I}
-visibly conforms to
\mathscr{S}
.
The opposite direction is also desirable, that is, if
\mathscr{I}
-visibly conforms to
\mathscr{S}
then
\mathscr{I}
passes the test suite
T
.

Definition 36
.

Let
L
be an alphabet and let
T
be a test suite over
L
.
Also let
\mathscr{S}
be a specification VPTS over
L

D,F\subseteq L^{\star}
be languages over
L
.

1.

is

for
\mathscr{S}
and
(D,F)
if

\mathscr{I}
passes to

\mathscr{I}\,\text{\bf vconf}_{D,F}\,\,\mathscr{S}
, for all VPTS
\mathscr{I}
over
L
.

2.

is

for
\mathscr{S}
and
(D,F)
if
\mathscr{I}\,\text{\bf vconf}_{D,F}\,\,\mathscr{S}

passes to

\mathscr{I}
over
L

\mathscr{S}

(D,F)

Notice that an IUT

5
Conclusion

Pushdown reactive systems are more complex because their behaviors are given by phrase structure rules in addition to their asynchronous interactions with the environment.
Therefore testing activities are also more intricate when applied to systems of this nature.
Several methods have been designed to treat the problem of conformance checking and test suite generation for regular reactive systems that can be specified by memoryless formal models.
Some other previous approaches have addressed these same problems for pushdown reactive systems that have access to an infinite stack memory.

Here we also study the latter class of reactive systems that can make use of a potentially infinite memory, but we deviated from previous works in the sense that we allow a more wide range of possibilities to define a fault model.
In this case we make use of visibly pushdown languages to define desirable and undesirable behaviors to be checked over an implementation against to its corresponding specification.
That is, we treated the problem of conformance checking for asynchronous system with a stack memory using the formalism of IOVPTS, and defined a more general conformance relation for these systems.
So we gave a method to generate test suites that can verify whether this more general conformance relation holds between a specification and a given IUT.
Also we have shown that these test suites are complete, always giving a conclusive verdict, and also can be generated in polynomial time complexity.

Our method was developed, and proved correct, using a reduction from the previous work based on
ioco-like
theory, where a balanced run property is applied to obtain verdicts of conformance.
Therefore our approach exhibits an asymptotic worst case time complexity that can be bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}+m^{2})
, where
n
and
m
are proportional to the number of states and transitions from the product between implementations and specification models, respectively.
