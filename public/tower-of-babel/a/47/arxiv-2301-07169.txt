On ring-like event systems in quantum logic
Dietmar Dorninger, Helmut Länger

Abstract
A class of ring-like event systems (RLSEs) is studied that generalizes Boolean rings. Quantum logics represented by orthomodular lattices are characterized within this class and the correspondence between Boolean algebras and Boolean rings is enlarged to orthomodular lattices. The structure of RLSEs and various subclasses is analysed and classical logics are especially identified. Moreover, sets of numerical events within different contexts of physical problems are described. A numerical event is defined as a function p p from a set S S of states of a physical system to [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] such that p ⁡ ( s ) p(s) is the probability of the occurrence of an event when the system is in state s ∈ S s\in S . In particular, the question is answered whether a given (small) set of numerical events will give rise to the assumption that one deals with a classical physical system or a quantum mechanical one.

Introduction

In quantum mechanics so-called quantum logics, also referred to as event systems, are an essential tool for theoretical reasoning and practical computations. The most common event systems are orthomodular lattices (and generalizations of them), in particular, the lattices of closed subspaces of a separable Hilbert space, known as Hilbert logics. Orthomodular lattices can be viewed as a generalization of Boolean algebras, which are characteristic for event systems in classical physics. So the question arises whether the logic behind an experiment might be a Boolean algebra, which, being in one-to-one correspondence to a Boolean ring, can also be understood as a Boolean ring. As often common with electrical engineering, calculations within rings are sometimes preferred to carrying out calculations within lattices. Then the question is whether a logic is a Boolean ring which entails the problem to find an appropriate generalization of Boolean rings corresponding to orthomodular lattices, a problem we will answer in this paper.

For this end we first recall the definition of ring-like structures of events (RLSEs), which we will later on reformulate for a wide class of RLSEs
\mathbf{R}=(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
of characteristic
2
(which means that they satisfy the identity
x+x\approx 0
) showing that these structures can be simply obtained by weakening customary axioms of Boolean rings or also by more suggestive other laws.

Definition 1.1
.

(
cf.
[
9
]
)
A ring-like structure of events (RLSE) is an algebra
(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
of type
(2,2,0,0)
such that
(R,\cdot,0,1)
is a bounded meet-semilattice and satisfying the following identities:

(R1)

x+y\approx y+x
,

(R2)

(xy+1)(x+1)+1\approx x
,

(R3)

\big((xy+1)x+1\big)x\approx xy
,

(R4)

xy+(x+1)\approx(xy+1)x+1
.

As one can easily verify, all Boolean rings are RLSEs.

Definition 1.2
.

An
RLSE

(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
is called specific if it satisfies the identity

(R5)

x+y\approx x(y+1)+(x+1)y
.

As we will note below, specific RLSEs are of characteristic
2
(which, in general, is not the case). Moreover, we will show that there is a one-to-one correspondence between orthomodular lattices and specific RLSEs. This means that the class of all RLSEs is larger than the class of specific RLSEs.

In this paper we will consider various classes of RLSEs, answer the question when an RLSE is a Boolean ring, study structural properties of RLSEs and link RLSEs to so-called
algebras of numerical events
(which are sets of probabilities that can be gained by measurements – cf.
[

]
and
[

+
of RLSEs will coincide with the summation of real functions and the order of the elements of an RLSE with the order of functions. Further, we will give answers to the question, whether a (small) set of numerical events obtained by measurements will justify that one deals with a classical physical system or not. Finally we will weaken the concept of RLSEs by omitting axiom (R3) and associate these structures to sets of numerical events endowed with operations which are relevant for experiments.

3
Structure theory of RLSEs

Definition 3.1
.

An
RLSE

(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
is called weakly distributive if it satisfies the identity

(R6)

(xy+1)x\approx xy+x
.

Obviously, any specific RLSE
(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
is weakly distributive, because according to Proposition
2.2
(ii) and (R5) we have

(xy+1)x\approx xy(x+1)+(xy+1)x\approx xy+x.

Moreover, any weakly distributive RLSE is of characteristic
2
since

x+x\approx x1+x\approx(x1+1)x\approx(x+1)x\approx 0

according to Proposition
2.2
(ii).

Example 3.2
.

The specific
RLSE
corresponding to the orthomodular lattice
\mathbf{MO}_{2}
is weakly distributive.

According to Corollary
2.6
this example shows that in general a specific RLSE
\mathbf{R}
is not a Boolean ring.

The next theorem explains how the initially introduced axioms for RLSEs can be rephrased by weakening the customary axioms of associativity and distributivity known from Boolean rings in case of weakly distributive RLSEs.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{R}=(R,+,\cdot,0,1)
be an algebra of type
(2,2,0,0)
such that
(R,\cdot,0,1)
is a bounded meet-semilattice. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\mathbf{R}
is a weakly distributive

(ii)

\mathbf{R}
satisfies the following identities:

(W1)

(W2)

(W3)

(xy+x)+1\approx xy+(x+1)

(W4)

(xy+x)+x\approx xy+(x+x)

(W5)

(xy+1)x\approx xy+x

(W6)

(xy+1)(x+1)\approx xy(x+1)+(x+1)
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii):

(W1)

follows from Proposition
2.2
(iii) and (R1).

(W2)

equals (R1).

(W3)

We have

according to (R6) and (R4).

(W4)

We find

(xy+x)+x\approx(xy+1)x+x\approx\big((xy+1)x+1\big)x\approx xy\approx xy+0\approx xy+(x+x)

according to (R6), (R3), Proposition
2.2
(iii) and the fact that every weakly distributive RLSE is of characteristic
2
.

(W5)

equals (R6).

(W6)

We notice

(xy+1)(x+1)\approx\big((xy+1)(x+1)+1\big)+1\approx x+1\approx 0+(x+1)\approx xy(x+1)+(x+1)

according to Proposition
2.2
(i), (R2), Proposition
2.2
(iii), (R1) and Proposition
2.2
(ii).

(ii)

Putting

(R1)

We see that

We close this section with a purely algebraic remark about the structure of RLSEs.

Let
\mathbf{A}=(A,F)

is called

if

for all

,

•

if

is distributive,

•

•

congruence regular
if for all

and
\Theta,\Phi\in\Con\mathbf{A}
,

implies
\Theta=\Phi
,

•

if for every

all classes of
\Theta
have the same cardinality.

Remark 3.6
.

RLSEs
are arithmetical, congruence regular and congruence uniform.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{R}
be an RLSE. Since the fundamental operations of
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})
are terms in
\mathbf{R}
we have
\Con\mathbf{R}\subseteq\Con\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})
. Now the theorem follows from the fact (see e.g.
[
4
]
) that orthomodular lattices are arithmetical, congruence regular and congruence uniform.

4
Algebras of numerical events

Let
S
be a set of states of a physical system and
p(s)
the probability of the occurrence of an event when the system is in state
s\in S
. The function
p
from
S
to
[0,1]
is called a
numerical event
, or more precisely, an
S-probability
(cf.
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
).

Let
P
be a set of S-probabilities including the constant functions
0
and
1
. We denote the order of real functions by
\leq
, write
p^{\prime}:=1-p
for the counter probability of
p\in P
and
p\perp q
if
p
and
q
are orthogonal in
P
, i.e.
p\leq q^{\prime}
. If the infimum or supremum of
p,q\in P
exists in
P
, we denote this by
p\wedge q
and
p\vee q
, respectively. Finally we agree to write
p+q
,
p-q
and
pq
for the sum, difference and product of functions
p,q\in P
. Not to mix up the sum and product of functions with the sum and product within RLSEs, we will use with RLSEs
\oplus
and
\odot
instead of
+
and
\cdot
, respectively.

Definition 4.1
.

(
cf.
[
2
]
)

A set
P
of S-probabilities is called an algebra of S-probabilities if

(S1)

0,1\in P
,

(S2)

p^{\prime}\in P
for every
p\in P
,

(S3)

if
p\perp q\perp r\perp p
for
p,q,r\in P
then
p+q+r\in P
.

Putting
r=0
in axiom (S3) one obtains that
p\perp q
implies
p+q\in P
in which case one can show that
p+q=p\vee q
(in respect to the order
\leq
of the functions of
P
).

In general,
(P,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
is an orthomodular poset in respect to the partial order of functions, but from now on we will assume with good cause that
P
is a lattice.

That an algebra of S-probabilities is a lattice and hence an orthomodular lattice, is a typical feature of many quantum logics. In particular, every Hilbert-space logic can be considered as a lattice-ordered algebra of S-probabilities (cf.
[
3
]
), and in the important case that
|S|=2
every algebra of S-probabilities is a lattice (cf.
[
5
]
). Of course, also all classical logics whose order
\leq
correspond to Boolean algebras (cf.
[
13
]
)can be understood as lattice-ordered algebras of numerical events.

If measurements are available in the context of a set
P_{n}
of numerical events it is often crucial to get to know whether one deals with a classical situation or a quantum-mechanical one which means that one has to decide whether
P_{n}
can be embedded into an algebra of S-probabilities
P
in such a way that the elements of
P_{n}
lie within a Boolean subalgebra of
P
. If this is the case,
P_{n}
is called
Boolean embeddable
, or for short, only
embeddable
(cf.
[
11
]
).

Let
P
be a lattice-ordered algebra of S-probabilities and

\displaystyle p\oplus q

\displaystyle:=(p\wedge q^{\prime})\vee(p^{\prime}\wedge q),

\displaystyle p\odot q

\displaystyle:=p\wedge q

for all
p,q\in P
. Then, as shown in
[
9
]
,
\mathbf{R}=(P,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
is an RLSE with
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})=P
. We call
\mathbf{R}
the RLSE
associated to P
.
\mathbf{R}
has characteristic
2
and is weakly distributive, because
\mathbf{R}
obviously satisfies identity (R5). If a set
P_{n}
of numerical events is Boolean embeddable into
P
we will also say that it is Boolean embeddable into
\mathbf{R}
.

Next we express
\oplus
,
\odot
and
\leq
of RLSEs associated to algebras of S-probabilities by the sum, difference and
\leq
of real functions.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{R}
be the RLSE associated to a lattice-ordered algebra of S-probabilities
P
and
p,q\in P
. Then

(i)

p\oplus q=p\odot(1-q)+(1-p)\odot q
,

(ii)

p\oplus q=q-p
if
p\leq q
,

(iii)

p\oplus 1=1-p
,

(iv)

p\oplus q=p+q
if
p\perp q
.

Proof.

(i)

holds since the operations
\odot
and
\wedge
coincide,
p^{\prime}=1-p
and
(p\odot q^{\prime})\perp(p^{\prime}\odot q)
.

(ii)

is a consequence of Theorem
3.4
and Proposition 2.1 in
[
5
]
.

(iii)

follows from (ii).

(iv)

results from Proposition
2.2
(vii).

∎

When we say that a set
P_{n}
of S-probabilities is Boolean embeddable into an RLSE
\mathbf{R}=(R,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
we assume that there exists an (arbitrary) lattice-ordered algebra
P
of S-probabilities such that
P=\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})
. If the elements of
P_{n}
can only have two values, namely
0
and
1
, then we also assume this for the elements of
P
. Such an algebra of S-probabilities then is a so-called concrete logic, that is a quantum logic which can be represented by sets.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
P_{2}=\{p,q\}
. Then the following holds:

(i)

P_{2}
is Boolean embeddable if and only if
p\odot(1-q)=p-p\odot q
.

(ii)

If
p
and
q
are two-valued then
P_{2}
is Boolean if and only if
p\odot q=pq
.

Proof.

(i)

p
,
q
are Boolean embeddable into an orthomodular lattice if and only if
p\mathrel{\C}q
. According to Corollary
2.7
this is equivalent to
p\odot(q\oplus 1)=p\odot q\oplus p
which by Proposition
4.2
means
p\odot(1-q)=p-p\odot q
.

(ii)

We assume that
p
and
q
can only have the values
0
and
1
. For orthomodular lattices
p\mathrel{\C}q
is equivalent to
p\mathrel{\C}q^{\prime}
, hence by what we have already proved
p
and
q
are Boolean embeddable if and only if
p\odot q=p-p\odot(1-q)
. Within a Boolean subalgebra
p(s)\wedge q(s)=\min\big(p(s),q(s)\big)=p(s)q(s)
with
\min
short for minimum. Hence
pq
must be the element
p\odot q
of
\mathbf{R}
. Conversely, if
pq=p\odot q
then
p\odot q\oplus p=p-p\odot q=p-pq=p(1-q)=p\odot(1-q)
since
p(1-q)
is an element of
\mathbf{R}
that coincides with
p\wedge(1-q)
.

∎

Let
A
be a finite subset of an RLSE
\mathbf{R}
. We denote by
\prod_{\bf R}A
the product in
\mathbf{R}
of all elements of
A
and by
\bigwedge A
the infimum of these elements in
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})
. Moreover, we will denote the product within the reals of all functions belonging to
A
by
\prod A
and the set-theoretic union of
A
and
B
by
A\cup B
. As proven in
[
8
]
for
n>1
an
n
-element subset
T
of an orthomodular poset is Boolean embeddable if and only if
\bigwedge A
and
\bigwedge B
commute for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and every
k
-element subset
A
and
B
of
T
. Taking this into account one can derive from Theorem
4.3
a rough procedure to find out whether a set
P_{n}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}
of S-probabilities is Boolean embeddable, namely

For
k=1
to
n-1
:

Check for every
k
-element subsets
A
and
B
of
P_{n}
whether
\prod_{\bf R}A\odot(1-\prod_{\bf R}B)=\prod_{\bf R}A-\prod_{\bf R}A\odot\prod_{\bf R}B
, or rather whether
\prod_{\bf R}A\odot\prod_{\bf R}B=\prod(A\cup B)
, if all S-probabilities are two-valued.

We conclude this paragraph by weakening two former concepts.

Definition 4.4
.

Omitting axioms
(R3)
and
(R4)
in the definition of an
RLSE
we will call the structure arising this way a near-RLSE, and if a near-RLSE satisfies axiom
(R5)
we will call it specific. Moreover, substituting axiom
(S3)
in the definition of algebras of S-probabilities by its special case
r=0
, i.e. if
p\perp q
for
p,q\in P
then
p+q\in P
, we obtain a so-called generalized field of events
(GFE)

(
cf.
[
7
]
)
.

If
\mathbf{R}=(R,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
is a near-RLSE then
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{R})
is a lattice with an antitone involution
′
that in general is not a complementation. Such a lattice could be considered as a quantum logic, however, we will now focus on a different approach to near-RLSEs.

We consider a set
Q
of S-probabilities containing
0
and
1
endowed by the operations
\oplus
and
\odot
defined for
p,q\in Q
by

\displaystyle(p\oplus q)(s)

\displaystyle:=\max\big(p(s),q(s)\big)-\min\big(p(s),q(s)\big),

\displaystyle(p\odot q)(s)

\displaystyle:=\min\big(p(s),q(s)\big)

for all
s\in S
with
\min
and
\max
having the obvious meanings.

As for these operations one could think of repeating an experiment several times for the same states
s\in S
,
p\oplus q
giving the bandwidth between the lowest and highest values of two repetitions
p
and
q
,
p\odot q
the obtained lowest values and
p\oplus 1=1-p
providing the counter probability to
p
.

In the proof of the next theorem we use the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
p,q\in Q
and
s\in S
and assume
p(s),q(s)\in\{0,1\}
. Then

\displaystyle p(s)\oplus q(s)

\displaystyle=p(s)+q(s)-2p(s)q(s),

\displaystyle p(s)\odot q(s)

\displaystyle=p(s)q(s).

Proof.

Consider the four cases
\big(p(s),q(s)\big)\in\{0,1\}^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Put

\displaystyle x\oplus y

\displaystyle:=\max(x,y)-\min(x,y),

\displaystyle x\odot y

\displaystyle:=\min(x,y)

for all
x,y\in[0,1]
and let
a,b\in[0,1]
. Then the following holds:

(i)

a\oplus b=b\oplus a
,

(ii)

(a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot(a\oplus 1)\oplus 1=a
,

(iii)

\big((a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1\big)\odot a=a\odot b
if and only if
b\geq\min(a,1-a)
,

(iv)

a\odot b\oplus(a\oplus 1)=(a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1
if and only if
a\in\{0,1\}
or
b=0
,

(v)

a\oplus b=a\odot(b\oplus 1)\oplus(a\oplus 1)\odot b
.

Remark 4.7
.

The equalities in
(i)
–
(v)
correspond exactly to the identities
(R1)
–
(R5)
.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

(i)

a\oplus b=\max(a,b)-\min(a,b)=\max(b,a)-\min(b,a)=b\oplus a

(ii)

(a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot(a\oplus 1)\oplus 1=1-\min\big(1-\min(a,b),1-a\big)=\max(\min(a,b),a)=a

(iii)

If
b\geq a
then

\displaystyle\big((a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1\big)\odot a

\displaystyle=\min\big(1-\min(1-a,a),a\big)=\min\big(\max(a,1-a),a\big)=

\displaystyle=a=\min(a,b)=a\odot b.

If
b<a
and
b\geq 1-a
then

\big((a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1\big)\odot a=\min(b,a)=\min(a,b)=a\odot b.

If, finally,
b<a
and
b<1-a
then

\big((a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1\big)\odot a=\min(1-a,a)>b=\min(a,b)=a\odot b.

(iv)

Because of

\displaystyle a\odot b\oplus(a\oplus 1)

\displaystyle=\max\big(\min(a,b),1-a\big)-\min\big(\min(a,b),1-a\big)=

\displaystyle=\max\big(\min(a,b),1-a\big)-\min(a,b,1-a)

and

(a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1=1-\min\big(1-\min(a,b),a\big)=\max\big(\min(a,b),1-a\big),

a\odot b\oplus(a\oplus 1)=(a\odot b\oplus 1)\odot a\oplus 1
if and only if
\min(a,b,1-a)=0
which means
a\in\{0,1\}
or
b=0
.

(v)

If
a\leq 1-b
then

\displaystyle a\oplus b

\displaystyle=\max(a,b)-\min(a,b)=

\displaystyle=\max\big(\min(a,1-b),\min(1-a,b)\big)-\min\big(\min(a,1-b),\min(1-a,b)\big)=

\displaystyle=a\odot(b\oplus 1)\oplus(a\oplus 1)\odot b.

If
a\geq 1-b
then

\displaystyle a\oplus b

\displaystyle=\max(a,b)-\min(a,b)=1-\min(a,b)-\big(1-\max(a,b)\big)=

\displaystyle=\max(1-b,1-a)-\min(1-b,1-a)=

\displaystyle=\max\big(\min(a,1-b),\min(1-a,b)\big)-\min\big(\min(a,1-b),\min(1-a,b)\big)=

\displaystyle=a\odot(b\oplus 1)\oplus(a\oplus 1)\odot b.

∎

Now we can prove our final result.

Theorem 4.8
.

For
\mathbf{Q}=(Q,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
the following hold:

(i)

\mathbf{Q}
is a specific near-
RLSE
,

(ii)

\mathbf{Q}
is a
GFE
in respect to the order
\leq
of functions,

(iii)

the following are equivalent:

(a)

Q\subseteq\{0,1\}^{S}
,

(b)

\mathbf{Q}
satisfies identity
(R3)
,

(c)

\mathbf{Q}
satisfies identity
(R4)
,

(d)

\mathbf{Q}
is an
RLSE
,

(e)

\mathbf{Q}
is a Boolean ring.

Proof.

Let
p,q\in Q
.

(i)

follows immediately from Lemma
4.6
.

(ii)

If
p\perp q
(in respect to
\leq
, not in the sense of RLSEs) then
p\leq 1-q
and hence

p+q=1-(1-q-p)=1-\big(\max(p,1-q)-\min(p,1-q)\big)=\big(p\oplus(q\oplus 1)\big)\oplus 1\in Q.

(iii)

The equivalence of (a) – (d) can be directly derived from Lemma
4.6
.

(
(a) and (d)
)

\Rightarrow
(e):

Using Lemma
4.5
we obtain

p\odot(q\oplus 1)=p(1-q)=p-pq=pq+p-2pqp=p\odot q\oplus p

which by Corollary
2.8
is equivalent to (e).

(e)
\Rightarrow
(d):

This is already well-known.
