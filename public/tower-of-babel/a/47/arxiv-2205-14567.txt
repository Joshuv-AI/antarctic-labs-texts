Abstract
Safe longitudinal control is discussed for a connected automated truck traveling behind a preceding connected vehicle. A controller is proposed based on control barrier function theory and predictor feedback for provably safe, collision-free behavior by taking into account the significant response time of the truck as input delay and the uncertainty of its dynamical model as input disturbance. The benefits of the proposed controller compared to control designs that neglect the delay or treat the delay as disturbance are shown by numerical simulations.

Introduction

Control systems are often subject to strict safety requirements that must be met before deployment in practice.
As such, safety is of primary importance in vehicle control applications, where collisions must be avoided
(
Vasudevan et al. 2012
)
.

Control barrier functions
(CBFs)
(
Ames et al. 2017
)
provide provable means to achieve safety, similarly to how Lyapunov theory yields stability.
Remarkably, one of the first applications of CBFs was adaptive cruise control
(
Ames et al. 2014
)
.
Still, challenges continue to arise in safe vehicle control due to the interplay of significant time delays (the response time of vehicles, their drivers and controllers), complicated nonlinear dynamics, disturbances, and dynamically changing environments.

Figure 1:

Safety-critical control of a connected automated truck (CAT) with input delay behind a connected vehicle (CV) during emergency braking.
The proposed predictor feedback controller maintains safety, while a baseline controller without predictor fails to do so.

In this work, we address safe vehicle control in the presence of both input delay and input disturbance.
Recently, safety-critical control with input delay has been addressed in discrete time
(
Liu et al. 2020
;
Singletary et al. 2020
)
and in continuous time for linear systems
(
Jankovic 2018a
;
Abel et al. 2019
)
and nonlinear systems
(
Molnar et al. 2021
)
, including multiple and time-varying input delays
(
Abel et al. 2020
;
Abel et al. 2021
)
.
Furthermore, the safety of delay-free systems with input disturbance have been investigated by robust control barrier functions
(
Jankovic 2018b
)
and using the notion of input-to-state safety
(
Kolathaya and Ames 2019
;
Alan et al. 2022
)
.
While real-life control systems may involve both delays and disturbances, their combined effect has not yet been addressed in safety-critical control — only
Seiler et al. 2022
approached this problem by treating the delay as part of the disturbance.

The contribution of our work is twofold.
First, we propose a safety-critical control method using CBFs that handles both input delay and input disturbance.
We achieve this by integrating the approaches of
Molnar et al. 2021
and
Alan et al. 2022
that separately address delay and disturbance by relying on the concepts of predictor feedback
(
Michiels and Niculescu 2007
;
Bekiaris-Liberis and Krstic 2013
;
Karafyllis and Krstic 2017
)
and input-to-state safety
(
Kolathaya and Ames 2019
)
, respectively.
Second, we apply the proposed method to safely control a connected automated truck in the presence of a significant response time (input delay) and uncertainties in its dynamics (input disturbance).
Simulations show that the proposed approach outperforms controllers designed by neglecting the delay or treating the delay as disturbance.

Motivation: Safety-critical Control of a Connected Automated Truck

Consider the scenario in Fig.
1
where a connected automated truck (CAT) is controlled to follow a connected vehicle (CV) that shares information via vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication.
Our goal is to design a controller that allows the CAT to match its speed
v
to the speed
v_{\rm L}
of the lead vehicle while maintaining a safe distance
D
.

To design the controller, we consider the dynamical model:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{D}(t)&=v_{\rm L}(t)-v(t),\\
\dot{v}(t)&=u(t-\tau)+d(t),\\
\dot{v}_{\rm L}(t)&=a_{\rm L}(t),\\
\end{split}

(1)

where
u(t)
is the control input (commanded acceleration) of the CAT and
a_{\rm L}(t)
is the lead vehicle’s acceleration at time
t
.
Since heavy-duty vehicles have large response time due to their size and inertia, we include the input delay (powertrain delay)
\tau

Furthermore, the high-fidelity dynamics of the CAT consist of several nontrivial elements (engine and powertrain behavior, gear schedule, resistance forces, tire dynamics, etc.) that are missing from the model above.
Thus, we capture model mismatches and real-life uncertainties via the disturbance
d(t)
in (
1

Our goal is to maintain safety for the CAT despite the presence of the significant delay
\tau
and disturbance

in its dynamics.
Specifically, we wish to keep the distance

over a minimal value

{D_{\rm sf}+Tv}
,

and time headway

{h(D(t),v(t)):=D(t)-D_{\rm sf}-Tv(t)\geq 0}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.
We state this problem more generally below.

\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t))+g(t,x(t))(u(t-\tau)+d(t)),

(2)

design a controller that achieves safety in that
{h(x(t))\geq 0}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
holds along the solutions of the corresponding closed-loop system for a given safety certificate

We highlight that for the CAT these quantities read:

x=\begin{bmatrix}D\\

0\end{bmatrix},

(3)

and

h(x)=D-D_{\rm sf}-Tv.

(4)

Safety with Input Delay

The problem statement contains two main challenges for safety-critical control: input delay and input disturbance.
This section is dedicated to addressing the delay without disturbance, and disturbance is added in the next section.

3.1
Delay-free Scenario

First, we revisit safety-critical control for delay-free systems.
Specifically, consider a nonlinear control-affine system with state
{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
, input
{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
and dynamics:

\dot{x}=f(t,x)+g(t,x)u,

(5)

where
{f:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
{g:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}
are locally Lipschitz continuous.
We consider Lipschitz continuous controllers
{k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
,
{u=k(t,x)}
, and we assume that given an initial condition
{x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
the solution
x(t)
of the closed-loop system exists for all
{t\geq 0}
.

To guarantee safe behavior, we define the
safe set

S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that represents the set of states in which the system is considered safe.
We seek to design controller
k
such that it enforces safety by rendering
S
forward invariant, meaning that
{x(0)\in S\implies x(t)\in S}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
holds for the closed-loop system.
Specifically, we consider safe sets of the form:

S=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(x)\geq 0\},

(6)

where
{h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}}
denotes a continuously differentiable function throughout this paper.

We endow the system with safety guarantees using control barrier functions (CBFs) that provide a practical method to synthesize safety-critical controllers
(
Ames et al. 2017
)
.
The main idea behind CBFs is lower-bounding the derivative of
h
w.r.t. time, which keeps
h(x(t))
nonnegative (i.e.,
{x(t)\in S}
).
This derivative along the system (
5
) reads:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{h}(t,x,u)&=L_{f}h(t,x)+L_{g}h(t,x)u,\\
L_{f}h(t,x)&=\nabla h(x)f(t,x),\\
L_{g}h(t,x)&=\nabla h(x)g(t,x),\end{split}

(7)

where
L_{f}h
and
L_{g}h
denote the Lie derivatives of
h
along
f
and
g
.
With these preliminaries, we formally define CBFs.

Definition 1

(
Ames et al. 2017
)

Function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a control barrier function (CBF) for (
5
) if there exists
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
1
1

1

Function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}
is of class-
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
(
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}_{\infty}}
) if it is continuous, strictly monotonically increasing,
{\alpha(0)=0}
and
{\lim_{r\to\infty}\alpha(r)=\infty}
. Function
\alpha:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is of extended class-
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
(
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
) if it is of class-
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
and
{\lim_{r\to-\infty}\alpha(r)=-\infty}
.
such that
{\forall t\geq 0}
and
{\forall x\in S}
:

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(t,x,u)>-\alpha(h(x)).

(8)

The main result from
Ames et al. 2017
specifies a condition on the control input
u
that provides formal safety guarantees, as summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(
Ames et al. 2017
)

If
h
is a CBF for (
5
), then any locally Lipschitz continuous controller
k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying:

\dot{h}(t,x,k(t,x))\geq-\alpha(h(x)),

(9)

{\forall t\geq 0}
,
{\forall x\in S}
renders
S
forward invariant (safe) such that
{x(0)\in S\implies x(t)\in S}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.

The proof can be constructed based on
Ames et al. 2017
that considers
f
and
g
independent of
t
.
The same proof applies here, since it is based on the theorem of
Nagumo 1942
that includes time-variant systems.
Per Theorem
2
, controllers satisfying (
9
) yield safe behavior, while Definition
1
ensures that such controllers exist.
Thus, (
9
) can be used as condition to synthesize safety-critical controllers.
This is often achieved by incorporating (
9
) as constraint into optimization problems
(
Ames et al. 2017
)
.

3.2
Control with Input Delay

Figure 2:

Illustration of predictor feedback.
The state
x(t)
(purple) and input history
u_{t}
(green) are used to calculate the predicted state
x_{\rm p}(t)
(blue) that is used as feedback to synthesize the control input
u(t)
(red).

Now we revisit the notion of CBFs with input delay, that was introduced by
Molnar et al. 2021
for time-invariant systems via the idea of predictor feedback
(
Bekiaris-Liberis and Krstic 2013
;
Karafyllis and Krstic 2017
;
Michiels and Niculescu 2007
, ch. 15)
.
We extend these results to the time-variant system (
5
) with input delay
{\tau>0}
:

\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t))+g(t,x(t))u(t-\tau).

(10)

To overcome the delay in
{u(t-\tau)}
we seek to use a prediction of the future state
{x(t+\tau)}
as feedback.
To formalize this process, first, we characterize the solution of (
10
).
We begin by noting that the solution over
[t,t+\tau]
evolves according to the history of the control input over
[t-\tau,t)
; see the illustration in Fig.
2
.
We formally define the input history by the function
{u_{t}:[-\tau,0)\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
:

u_{t}(\theta)=u(t+\theta),\quad\theta\in[-\tau,0),

(11)

which is assumed to be bounded and continuous almost everywhere.
We shortly denote the space of such functions with
\mathcal{B}
.
As shown below, predictor feedback leads to controllers that depend on the input history
{u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
, in the form
{k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
,
{u=k(t,x,u_{t})}
.
Throughout the paper, we assume that given an initial condition
{x(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and initial input history
{u_{0}\in\mathcal{B}}
, the solution
x(t)
of the closed-loop system exists for all
{t\geq 0}
.

For given
{u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
, system (
10
) can be considered as a forced ordinary differential equation (ODE) over
[t,t+\tau]
.
The solution of the ODE over
[t,t+\tau]
can be written as:

x(t+\vartheta)=\Psi(\vartheta,t,x(t),u_{t}),\quad\vartheta\in[0,\tau].

(12)

That is, the solution depends on time
t
, the current state
x(t)
, and the input history
u_{t}
; see Fig.
2
.
Formally, it is given by the semi-flow

{\Psi:[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}
:

\Psi(\vartheta,t,x,u_{t})=x+\int_{0}^{\vartheta}\Big(f\big(t+s,\Psi(s,t,x,u_{t})\big)\\
+g\big(t+s,\Psi(s,t,x,u_{t})\big)u_{t}(s-\tau)\Big){\rm d}s,

(13)

which can be calculated by numerical ODE solvers in practice.
Specifically, predictor feedback relies on the predicted state
x_{\rm p}(t)
at prediction time
t_{\rm p}
:

t_{\rm p}\triangleq t+\tau,\quad x_{\rm p}(t)\triangleq x(t+\tau)=\Psi(\tau,t,x(t),u_{t}).

(14)

Note that the predicted state depends on
{f(t+s,.)}
and
{g(t+s,.)}
, i.e., on the future expressions of the dynamics in (
13
).
If such information is unknown, the predicted state needs to be approximated.
This leads to prediction errors, whose effect is treated as disturbance in the next section.
The predicted state is used in the following definition of CBFs for input delay systems from
Molnar et al. 2021
.

Definition 3

(
Molnar et al. 2021
)

Function
{h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}}
is a control barrier function (CBF) for (
10
) if there exists
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
such that
{\forall t\geq 0}
,
{\forall x\in S}
and
{\forall u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
:

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p},u)>-\alpha(h(x_{\rm p})),

(15)

where
{t_{\rm p}=t+\tau}
,
{x_{\rm p}=\Psi(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
with
\Psi
given by (
13
).

This definition leads to the main result in
Molnar et al. 2021
that endows controllers with safety guarantees, as summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 4

(
Molnar et al. 2021
)

If
h
is a CBF for (
10
), then any locally Lipschitz continuous controller
{k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
satisfying:

\dot{h}(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p},k(t,x,u_{t}))\geq-\alpha(h(x_{\rm p})),

(16)

with
{t_{\rm p}=t+\tau}
,
{x_{\rm p}=\Psi(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
,
{\forall x\in S}
and
{\forall u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
renders
S
forward invariant such that
{x(t)\in S}
,
{\forall t\in[0,\tau]}

{\implies x(t)\in S}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.

The proof can be constructed based on
Molnar et al. 2021
.
Note that safety is conditioned on
{x(t)\in S}
,
{\forall t\in[0,\tau]}
instead of
{x(0)\in S}
, as the solution over
[0,\tau]
is determined by the initial input history
u_{0}
; cf. (
12
).
If
{\tau=0}
, these two conditions coincide and Theorem
4
reduces to Theorem
2
, since
{t_{\rm p}=t}
,
{x_{\rm p}=x}
.

3.3
Application to Vehicle Control

Now we apply the above theory to design a safe longitudinal controller for the connected automated truck (CAT) in Fig.
1
.
We describe the dynamics of the CAT by (
3
,
10
), first without considering disturbance (
{d(t)\equiv 0}
), and we rely on CBF (
4
).
This system was studied from safety perspective in
He and Orosz 2018
without time delay, wherein the following nominal controller was used to execute car following:

k_{\rm n}(x)=A(V(D)-v)+B(W(v_{\rm L})-v).

(17)

The term with gain
B
makes the CAT match its speed
v
to the speed
v_{\rm L}
of the lead vehicle or the speed limit
v_{\max}
, whichever is smaller, as expressed by the speed policy
W
:

W(v_{\rm L})=\min\{v_{\rm L},v_{\max}\}.

(18)

The term with gain
A
allows the CAT to maintain appropriate distance
D
by the help of the range policy
V
:

V(D)=\min\{\kappa(D-D_{\rm st}),v_{\max}\},

(19)

meaning that the CAT
shall increase its speed
v
linearly (with gradient
\kappa
) for distances above a standstill distance
D_{\rm st}
, until reaching the speed limit
v_{\max}
.

Here we endow controller (
17
) with safety guarantees by choosing its parameters safely.
When
{\tau=0}
, we choose
{D_{\rm st}\geq D_{\rm sf}}
and
{B=\kappa=1/T}
, which makes the controller
{k(t,x)=k_{\rm n}(x)}
provably safe since it satisfies (
9
) with
{\alpha(r)=Ar}
.
When
{\tau>0}
, we maintain safety guarantees by using the predictor feedback controller
{k(t,x,u_{t})=k_{\rm n}(x_{\rm p})}
that satisfies (
16
).
First, we demonstrate this controller for the case where the predicted state
{x_{\rm p}=\Psi(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
is calculated accurately.
This requires information from the lead vehicle about its future intent (namely,
{a_{\rm L}(t+s)}
,
{s\in[0,\tau]}
that shows up in
{f(t+s,.)}
in (
13
)), which may be provided by vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) connectivity.
This intent requirement will be relaxed in the next section.

4
Robust Safety with Disturbance

In this section, we provide robust safety guarantees against disturbances via the notion of input-to-state safety.
We also address prediction errors as a part of disturbances.

4.1
Delay-free Scenario

Consider control-affine systems with input disturbance:

\dot{x}=f(t,x)+g(t,x)\big(u+d(t)\big),

(20)

where
{d:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
is bounded: there exists
{\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
such that
{\|d(t)\|\leq\delta}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.
The disturbance affects safety as:

\dot{h}(t,x,u+d)=\dot{h}(t,x,u)+L_{g}h(t,x)d,

(21)

cf. (
7
).
Since
L_{g}h(t,x)
characterizes the impact of the disturbance on safety, we use
L_{g}h(t,x)
to provide robustness against the input disturbance.
Instead of accounting for the worst-case disturbance as described by
Jankovic 2018b
we rely on
input-to-state safety

(
Kolathaya and Ames 2019
)
.
When a system is input-to-state safe, trajectories are kept within a neighborhood
{S_{\delta}\supseteq S}
of the safe set
S
:

S_{\delta}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:h_{\delta}(x)\geq 0\},

(22)

defined with:

h_{\delta}(x)=h(x)+\gamma(h(x),\delta).

(23)

The size of the neighborhood
S_{\delta}
depends on the bound
\delta
of the disturbance, and it is characterized by function
{\gamma:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}}
that is continuously differentiable in its first argument and is of class-
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
in its second argument (see the expression of
\gamma
later in (
26
)).
Below we outline a method that enables us to make this neighborhood as small as desired and keep safety violations arbitrarily small in the presence of disturbance.
We achieve this by using tunable input-to-state safe control barrier functions (TISSf-CBFs) from
Alan et al. 2022
.

Definition 5

(
Alan et al. 2022
)

Function
{h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}}
is a tunable input-to-state safe control barrier function (TISSf-CBF) for (
20
) with a continuously differentiable function
{\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
if there exists
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
such that
{\forall t\geq 0}
and
{\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
:

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(t,x,u)>-\alpha(h(x))+\sigma(h(x))\|L_{g}h(t,x)\|^{2}.

(24)

Note the difference from the CBF definition (
8
): an additional term with the Euclidean norm of
L_{g}h(t,x)
appears, which provides robustness against disturbance.
By tuning the coefficient
\sigma(h(x))
, one may choose to increase this robustness closer to the safe set boundary or outside the safe set without introducing conservativeness farther inside the safe set.
To achieve this, we choose
{\sigma(h)=\sigma_{0}\exp(-\lambda h)}
with parameters
{\sigma_{0},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
so that
\sigma(h(x))
gets larger as
h(x)
gets smaller.
We remark that a reciprocal coefficient
\epsilon(h)
is utilized in
Alan et al. 2022
, which can be translated to the form given in this study by simply choosing
{\sigma(h)=1/\epsilon(h)}
.
Furthermore,
{\sigma(h)\equiv 0}
would reduce to the CBF definition (
8
).

The following theorem from
Alan et al. 2022
states the robust safety guarantees provided by TISSf-CBFs.

Theorem 6

(
Alan et al. 2022
)

If
h
is a TISSf-CBF for (
20
) with
{\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
satisfying
{\sigma^{\prime}(r)\leq 0}
,
{\forall r\in\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\alpha^{-1}\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
is continuously differentiable, then any locally Lipschitz continuous controller
{k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
satisfying:

\dot{h}(t,x,k(t,x))\geq-\alpha(h(x))+\sigma(h(x))\|L_{g}h(t,x)\|^{2},

(25)

{\forall t\geq 0}
and
{\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
renders
S_{\delta}
with

\gamma(h,\delta)=-\alpha^{-1}\bigg(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{4\sigma(h)}\bigg)

(26)

forward invariant such that
{x(0)\!\in\!S_{\delta}\!\implies\!x(t)\!\in\!S_{\delta}}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.

The proof can be generalized from
Alan et al. 2022
, and the proof with input delay is given in the next section.
Note that if a controller
{u=k(t,x)}
satisfies (
9
), then robustness to disturbance can be achieved by a simple modification that satisfies (
25
):
{u=k(t,x)+\sigma(h(x))L_{g}h^{\top}(t,x)}
.

4.2
Control with Input Delay

Having the machinery to address input delay and input disturbance separately, now we combine our two approaches to handle both simultaneously.
Consider the control-affine system with input delay and disturbance:

\dot{x}(t)=f(t,x(t))+g(t,x(t))\big(u(t-\tau)+d(t)\big),

(27)

cf.​ (
10
) and​ (
20
).
We use TISSf-CBFs and predictor feedback to address the disturbance and delay.

The disturbance affects the solution of (
27
) and hence the predicted state
{x_{\rm p}(t)=x(t+\tau)}
.
The predicted state is now given as
{x_{\rm p}(t)=\Psi_{d}(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
using the semi-flow
{\Psi_{d}:[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}}
:

\Psi_{d}(\vartheta,t,x,u_{t})=x+\int_{0}^{\vartheta}\Big(f\big(t+s,\Psi_{d}(s,t,x,u_{t})\big)\\
+g\big(t+s,\Psi_{d}(s,t,x,u_{t})\big)\big(u_{t}(s-\tau)+d(t+s)\big)\Big){\rm d}s.

(28)

We remark that when the disturbance
{d(t+s)}
or the future dynamics
{f(t+s,.)}
or
{g(t+s,.)}
are unknown,
{x_{\rm p}(t)=\Psi_{d}(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
is also unknown and needs to be approximated, which inevitably leads to prediction errors.
First, we provide safety guarantees with the theoretical assumption that
x_{\rm p}(t)
is accurately available, and second, we address prediction errors.
We use the following definition.

Definition 7

Function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a tunable input-to-state safe control barrier function (TISSf-CBF) for (
27
) with a continuously differentiable function
{\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
if there exists
{\alpha\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
such that
{\forall t\geq 0}
,
{\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
{\forall u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
:

\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p},u)>-\alpha(h(x_{\rm p}))+\sigma(h(x_{\rm p}))\|L_{g}h(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p})\|^{2},

(29)

where
{t_{\rm p}=t+\tau}
,
{x_{\rm p}=\Psi_{d}(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
with
\Psi_{d}
given by (
28
).

This definition leads to our main result that ensures safety in the presence of both input delay and disturbance.

Theorem 8

If
h
is a TISSf-CBF for (
27
) with
{\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}}
satisfying
{\sigma^{\prime}(r)\leq 0}
,
{\forall r\in\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\alpha^{-1}\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}_{\infty}}
is continuously differentiable, then any locally Lipschitz continuous controller
{k:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}}
satisfying:

\dot{h}(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p},k(t,x,u_{t}))\geq-\alpha(h(x_{\rm p}))+\sigma(h(x_{\rm p}))\|L_{g}h(t_{\rm p},x_{\rm p})\|^{2},

(30)

with
{t_{\rm p}=t+\tau}
,
{x_{\rm p}=\Psi_{d}(\tau,t,x,u_{t})}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
,
{\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
{\forall u_{t}\in\mathcal{B}}
renders
S_{\delta}
with (
26
) forward invariant such that
{x(t)\in S_{\delta}}
,
{\forall t\in[0,\tau]}

{\implies x(t)\in S_{\delta}}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
.

The proof is given in Appendix

.
Inequality (
30

Remark 9

Since the semi-flow
\Psi_{d}
in (
28
) depends on the disturbance and future dynamics, the future state
{x_{\rm p}(t)=\Psi_{d}(\tau,t,x(t),u_{t})}
may be unknown.
One may approximate
x_{\rm p}(t)
by some
\hat{x}_{\rm p}(t)
, for example, as
\hat{x}_{\rm p}(t)=\Psi(\tau,t,x(t),u_{t})
via the semi-flow (
13
) without disturbance.
Similarly, one may also approximate
t_{\rm p}
by
\hat{t}_{\rm p}
(e.g.
{\hat{t}_{\rm p}=t}
) if quantities in (
30
) are unavailable at the future time
t_{\rm p}
.
These yield prediction errors, and the corresponding controller
{\hat{u}=\hat{k}(t,x,u_{t})}
synthesized via
\hat{x}_{\rm p}
,
\hat{t}_{\rm p}
will differ from
{u=k(t,x,u_{t})}
synthesized via
x_{\rm p}
,
t_{\rm p}
.
The discrepancy contributes to the disturbance and yields the effective disturbance
{\hat{d}(t)=d(t)+\hat{u}(t-\tau)-u(t-\tau)}
.
However, controllers synthesized via (
30
) are robust to disturbances by design.
If
\hat{d}(t)
is bounded and
{\exists\hat{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
such that
{\|\hat{d}(t)\|\leq\hat{\delta}}
,
{\forall t\geq 0}
(e.g. in case of bounded prediction error and Lipschitz continuous controller), then the robust safety guarantees of Theorem
8
hold with
\hat{\delta}
instead of
\delta
.

Remark 10

Input-to-state safety allows for safety violations, but they can be made arbitrarily small with large enough
\sigma(h)
and control effort.
This holds for both Theorems
6
and
8
without and with delay.
As such, one may be tempted to use the delay-free control design (
25
) and treat the entire effect of delay as disturbance, which corresponds to using
{\hat{x}_{\rm p}(t)=x(t)}
and
{\hat{t}_{\rm p}=t}
.
While this solution exhibits a level of robustness and was successfully implemented in
Alan et al. 2022
, the size
\hat{\delta}
of the disturbance may be large and it may take a large control effort to keep safety violations minimal.
Control effort and safety violations can be significantly decreased by predictors that estimate
x_{\rm p}(t)
better than
{x(t)}
; see the example below.

5
Conclusion

This work addressed the robust safety-critical control of systems with input delay and input disturbance.
Predictor feedback and tunable input-to-state safe control barrier functions were integrated to achieve provable safety guarantees.
The proposed control method was applied to the longitudinal control of a connected automated truck, and showed better performance than delay-free control designs or controllers treating the delay as disturbance.
Future work may involve safety for systems with state delays and robust safety with disturbance observers.
