A transfer theorem for multivariate Δ \Delta -analytic functions with a power-law singularity
Linxiao Chen

Abstract
This paper presents a multivariate generalization of Flajolet and Odlyzko’s transfer theorem. Similarly to the univariate version, the theorem assumes Δ \Delta -analyticity (defined coordinate-wise) of a function A ⁡ ( z 1 , … , z d ) A(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}) at a unique dominant singularity ( ρ 1 , … , ρ d ) ∈ ( ℂ ∗ ) d (\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d})\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{d} , and allows one to translate, on a term-by-term basis, an asymptotic expansion of A ⁡ ( z 1 , … , z d ) A(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}) around ( ρ 1 , … , ρ d ) (\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d}) into a corresponding asymptotic expansion of its Taylor coefficients a n 1 , … , n d a_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}} . We treat the case where the asymptotic expansion of A ⁡ ( z 1 , … , z d ) A(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}) contains only power-law type terms, and where the indices n 1 , … , n d n_{1},\ldots,n_{d} tend to infinity in some polynomially stretched diagonal limit. The resulting asymptotic expansion of a n 1 , … , n d a_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}} is a sum of terms of the form I ( λ 1 , … , λ d ) ⋅ n 0 − Θ ⋅ ρ 1 − n 1 ⋯ ρ d − n d , I(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\cdot n_{0}^{-\Theta}\cdot\rho_{1}^{-n_{1}}\cdots\rho_{d}^{-n_{d}}, where ( λ 1 , … , λ d ) ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) d (\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in(0,\infty)^{d} is the direction vector of the stretched diagonal limit for ( n 1 , … , n d ) (n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}) , the parameter n 0 n_{0} tends to ∞ \infty at similar speed as n 1 , … , n d n_{1},\ldots,n_{d} , while Θ ∈ \Theta\in\real and I : ( 0 , ∞ ) d → ℂ I:(0,\infty)^{d}\to\mathbb{C} are determined by the asymptotic expansion of A A .

Introduction

Univariate transfer theorems.

In their pioneering work
[
5
]
, Flajolet and Odlyzko developped a method, known as the transfer theorems, that allows one to compute a precise asymptotic expansion of a sequence
a_{n}
as
n\to\infty
, from an asymptotic expansion of its generating function
A(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
around some singularity
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\equiv\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. We use the notation
[z^{n}]A(z)
for the coefficient
a_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}A^{(n)}(0)
.
One distinctive feature of the transfer theorem is that it applies to generating functions that are
\Delta
-analytic
, that is, analytic on a
\Delta
-domain
of the form
\rho\cdot\Delta_{\delta}\equiv\left\{\rho z\,\middle|\,z\in\Delta_{\delta}\right\}
for some
\delta>0
, where

\Delta_{\delta}:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|<1+\delta,z\neq 1\text{ and }\arg(1-z)\in(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\delta,\tfrac{\pi}{2}+\delta)\right\}\,.

(1)

See Figure
1
(a)
.
(Simple extensions of the transfer theorem also apply to functions analytic in a finite intersection of
\Delta
-domains of the form
(\rho_{1}\!\cdot\!\Delta_{\delta})\hskip-1.0pt\cap\hskip-0.5pt\cdots\hskip-0.5pt\cap\hskip-1.0pt(\rho_{n}\!\cdot\!\Delta_{\delta})
, but we shall not discuss that further here.)

Under the
\Delta
-analyticity assumption,
\rho
is the unique dominant singularity of the function
A(z)
. By the change of variable
z^{\prime}=z/\rho
, we can bring this singularity to
z=1
. In the following, we will assume without loss of generality that
\rho=1
.

In its most general form, Flajolet and Odlyzko’s transfer theorem applies to functions whose asymptotic expansion is composed of any regular varying functions taken from a large class of “standard scale functions”, such as
f(z)=(1-z)^{\alpha}(\log(1-z))^{\beta}
for
\alpha,\beta\in\real
. Here we focus on functions whose asymptotic expansion contains only power-law terms of the form
(1-z)^{\alpha}
. In this simple case, the transfer theorem can be formulated as follows.

Theorem A

(Univariate transfer theorem)
.

Let
\alpha\in\real
and let
A(z)
be an analytic function on
\Delta_{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
.

1.

(Analytic terms have exponentially small contribution)

If
A(z)
is also analytic at
1
, then there exists
r\in(0,1)
such that
[z^{n}]A(z)=O(r^{n})
when
n\to\infty
.
(In this case,
z=1
is actually
not
the dominant singularity of
A(z)
.)

2.

(Coefficient asymptotics of power functions)

When
A(z)=(1-z)^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\in\real
, we have the following asymptotic expansion as
n\to\infty
,

[z^{n}](1-z)^{\alpha}=\frac{n^{-\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{e_{k}(\alpha)}{n^{k}}\equiv\frac{n^{-\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}\left(1+\frac{e_{1}(\alpha)}{n}+\frac{e_{2}(\alpha)}{n^{2}}+\cdots\right)

(2)

where
e_{k}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q}[\alpha]
is a polynomial of degree
2k
in
\alpha
. More precisely,
e_{k}(\alpha)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}g_{k,l}\,\frac{\Gamma(-\alpha)}{\Gamma(-\alpha-k-l)}
, where
g_{k,l}
is defined by the Taylor expansion

G(x,y):=\exp\left(-y-\left(\frac{y}{x}+1\right)\log(1-x)\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}g_{k,l}\,y^{l}\right)x^{k}\,.

(3)

3.

(Big-O transfer and little-o transfer)

If
A(z)=O((1-z)^{\alpha})
when
z\to 1
in
\Delta_{\delta}
, then

[z^{n}]A(z)=O(n^{-\alpha-1})
when
n\to\infty
.

If
A(z)=\,o\,((1-z)^{\alpha})
when
z\to 1
in
\Delta_{\delta}
, then

[z^{n}]A(z)=\,o\,(n^{-\alpha-1})
when
n\to\infty
.

In practice, the transfer theorem is usually applied to functions
A(z)
which are linear combinations of the three cases above, as in the following statement.

Theorem
A
′

(Univariate transfer theorem, integrated form)
.

Let
A(z)
be an analytic function on
\Delta_{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
such that when
z\to 1
in
\Delta_{\delta}
, we have

A(z)=A_{\mathtt{reg}}(z)+h_{0}(1-z)^{\alpha_{0}}+\cdots h_{m}(1-z)^{\alpha_{m}}+o\left((1-z)^{\alpha_{m}}\right)\,,

(4)

where
A_{\mathtt{reg}}(z)
is defined and analytic in a neighborhood of
1
in
\mathbb{C}
, and
h_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha_{j}\in\real
for all
0\leq j\leq m
.
Then, the coefficients of
A(z)
has the following expansion when
n\to\infty

[z^{n}]A(z)=h_{0}\cdot[z^{n}](1-z)^{\alpha_{0}}+\cdots+h_{m}\cdot[z^{n}](1-z)^{\alpha_{m}}+o(n^{-\alpha_{m}-1})

(5)

where each term
[z^{n}](1-z)^{\alpha_{j}}
has the asymptotic expansion given in Theorem
A
(2)
.

The same result holds if the little-o estimates in both (
4
) and (
5
) are replaced by big-O estimates.

Remark
.

1.

A
and
Theorem

′
are not exactly equivalent, for the following reasons:
When
A(z)
is analytic at
1
, Theorem
A
asserts that
[z^{n}]A(z)
decays exponentially as
n\to\infty
, while Theorem
′
‣
1
only implies a super-polynomial decay.
On the other hand, one cannot prove Theorem
′
‣
1
by simply applying Theorem
A
to each term of the expansion (
4
), because the terms
A_{\mathtt{reg}}(z)
and
o((1-\alpha)^{\alpha_{m}})
in (
4
) have
a priori
no analytic continuations on the whole domain
\Delta_{\delta}
.

2.

For a sequence of functions
s_{k}(n)
, the asymptotic expansion
S(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}s_{k}(n)
ususally means that for all
m\geq 0
, we have
S(n)=s_{0}(n)+\cdots+s_{m-1}(n)+O(s_{m}(n))
as
n\to\infty
.
Theorem
A
(2)
uses a slightly modified notion of asymptotic expansion: we choose implicitly the family
s_{\beta}(n)=n^{-\beta}
(
\beta\in\real
) as the reference asymptotic scale, and view each asymptotic expansion as a weighted sum of the form
S(n)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\cdot n^{-\beta_{k}}
with some
\beta_{0}<\beta_{1}<\cdots
. The difference is that now the prefactors
c_{k}
may vanish for some or even all
k\geq 0
, and the expansion should be read as: for all
m\geq 0
, we have
S(n)=c_{0}n^{-\beta_{0}}+\cdots+c_{m-1}n^{-\beta_{m-1}}+O(n^{-\beta_{m}})
as
n\to\infty
.

3.

It is also possible to include
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\real
in
A
and
′
‣
1
. The asymptotic expansion (
2
) would hold unmodified. But complex exponents
\alpha
rarely appear in applications and they complicate the asymptotic scale
s_{\beta}(n)=n^{-\beta}
(since
\mathbb{C}
is not totally ordered). We restrict ourselves to
\alpha\in\real
for the sake of simplicity.

4.

Theorem
A
(1)–(2)
together imply that the prefactor
\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}
in (
2
) must vanish when
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed,
\alpha\mapsto\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}
is an entire function and we have
\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\alpha)}=0

if and only if

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
.

The transfer theorems apply to a large class of naturally occuring generating functions. For example, it is well known that all D-finite functions analytic at
0
are linear combinations of
\Delta
-analytic functions. Among them, the algebraic functions always have singularities of power-law type, to which
Theorem

′
applies.
Compared to alternative methods such as Darboux’s method or the Tauberian theorems, the transfer theorem has the advantage of giving a transparent correspondance between the asymptotic expansion of the generating function and that of its coefficients. See
[
6
, Sec. 5]
for a detailed discussion about this comparison.

In this paper, we present a generalization of the transfer theorem to the multivariate setting. Below, we start by recalling some basic notations about multivariate generating functions, and then define the regimes of coefficient asymptotics with which our transfer theorem will be concerned.

Multivariate generating functions.

In many practical problems, the relevant information is captured naturally by a multidimensional infinite array
(a_{\bm{n}})_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{N}^{d}}
.
Such multidimensional arrays and the multivariate generating functions which encode them will be this paper’s central objects.
To make the formulas compact, we will use the following multi-index notations: For any formal or complex vectors
\bm{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
and
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})
, we denote

\bm{\theta}\bm{z}=(\theta_{1}z_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}z_{d})\,,\qquad\bm{\theta\cdot z}=\theta_{1}z_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d}z_{d}\,,\qquad\bm{z}^{\bm{\theta}}=z_{1}^{\theta_{1}}\cdots z_{d}^{\theta_{d}}\,,\qquad\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=\mathrm{d}z_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}z_{d}\,.

(6)

And, for any scalar
\sigma
and
integer vector
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, let

\sigma^{\bm{\theta}}=(\sigma^{\theta_{1}},\ldots,\sigma^{\theta_{d}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{\partial^{m}}=\partial_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots\partial_{d}^{m_{d}}\equiv\frac{\partial^{m_{1}}}{\partial\!\left.\!z_{1}\!\right.^{m_{1}}}\cdots\frac{\partial^{m_{d}}}{\partial\!\left.\!z_{d}\right.^{m_{d}}}.

(7)

With these notations, the multivariate generating function of the array
(a_{\bm{n}})_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}
can be written as

A(\bm{z})=\sum_{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}a_{\bm{n}}\bm{z^{n}}\equiv\sum_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{d}=0}^{\infty}a_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}}\,z_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots z_{d}^{n_{d}}.

(8)

Similarly to the univariate case, we denote the coefficient
a_{\bm{n}}
by
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
.
We assume that every generating function in this paper is absolutely convergent in an open neighborhood of
\bm{0}\equiv(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, so that it defines an analytic function there.

We refer to
[
7
]
for the general theory on power series and analytic functions in several variables.
One particular fact that we will use without further mention is the uniqueness of analytic continuation: if
A
and
B
are analytic functions on an open connected domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
A(\bm{z})=B(\bm{z})
on any open subset of
\Omega
, then
A(\bm{z})=B(\bm{z})
for all
\bm{z}\in\Omega
.

Stretched diagonal limits.

One central problem of analytic combinatorics in several variables is to understand the asymptotics of the coefficients
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
when the components
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
of the multi-index tend to
\infty

simultaneously
.
In general, one needs to put some constraint on the relative speeds at which
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
grow in order to get a useful asymptotic formula, and the interesting regimes are to a large extent dictated by the structure of the singularities of
A(\bm{z})
.

In this work, we will be interested in the
stretched diagonal limits
, where
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d}
grow at polynomial speeds relative to each other. In other words, we require that for each
j=2,\ldots,d
, there exists a constant
\theta_{j}>0
such that the ratio
n_{j}/n_{1}^{\theta_{j}}
remains in some compact interval
\mathcal{I}\subset\real_{>0}\equiv(0,\infty)
when
n_{1}\to\infty
.
A symmetrized definition of this limit regime goes as follows:

Definition

(Stretched diagonal limit)
.

We say that the multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
tends to
\bm{\infty}
in the
stretched diagonal regime
if there exist an exponent vector
\bm{\theta}\in\real_{>0}^{d}
and an auxiliary variable
n_{0}>0
, such that

\bm{n}=\bm{\lambda}n_{0}^{\bm{\theta}}\equiv\left(\lambda_{1}n_{0}^{\theta_{1}},\ldots,\lambda_{d}n_{0}^{\theta_{d}}\right)

(9)

for some prefactor
\bm{\lambda}=\bm{\lambda}(n_{0})
that remains in a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\real_{>0}^{d}
when
n_{0}\to\infty
.
In this case, we will also say that
\bm{n}\to\bm{\infty}
in the
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal limit
.

Remark
.

If we require in addition that
\bm{\lambda}(n_{0})\to\bm{\lambda}_{*}\in\real_{>0}^{d}
as
n_{0}\to\infty
, then the point
\bm{n}=\bm{\lambda}n_{0}^{\bm{\theta}}
would tend to
\bm{\infty}
roughly along the curve
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta}}=(\bm{\lambda}_{*}t^{\bm{\theta}},t>0)
. But we only require
\bm{\lambda}(n_{0})
to stay in some compact set. Intuitively this means that
\bm{n}
can jump between the curves
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{\lambda}_{*},\bm{\theta}}
for different values of
\bm{\lambda}_{*}
.
Consequently, the asymptotics that we write in the stretched diagonal limit should be understood as uniform with respect to
\bm{\lambda}_{*}
on every compact subset of
\real{}_{>0}^{d}
.

Notice that for any
\tau>0
, the
(\tau\bm{\theta})
-diagonal limit is the same as the
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal limit: it suffices to replace the variable
n_{0}
by
n_{0}^{\tau}
or
n_{0}^{1/\tau}
to pass from one to the other. The classical notion of
diagonal limit
, e.g. as defined in
[
9
]
, corresponds to the
(1,\cdots,1)
-diagonal limit in our terminology.

Apart from being the relevant limit regime for multivariate
\Delta
-analytic function with power-law singularity (to be defined below), the stretch diagonal limits also arise naturally from the study of critical phenomena in probability and mathematical physics.

Preliminary definitions.

Let us define some domains and function classes needed for stating the main theorems.
Let
\mathbb{C}_{*}=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
\real{}_{>0}=(0,\infty)
.
For
\delta>0
, we write

K_{\delta}=\left\{u\in\mathbb{C}_{*}:\,|\arg(u)|<\delta\right\}
and denote
\Omega_{\delta}=K_{\pi/2+\delta}
. The multivariate versions of these cones are denoted
\bm{K}_{\delta}=K_{\delta}^{d}
and
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}=\Omega_{\delta}^{d}
.
For
\epsilon>0
,
w\in\mathbb{C}
and
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, let
B_{\epsilon}(w)=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z-w|<\epsilon\right\}
and
\bm{B}_{\epsilon}(\bm{w})=B_{\epsilon}(w_{1})\times\cdots\times B_{\epsilon}(w_{d})
.
Notice that
\Omega_{\delta}
is related to the
\Delta
-domain
\Delta_{\delta}
by
\Omega_{\delta}=\left\{\mu(1-z)\,\middle|\,\mu>0\text{ and }z\in\Delta_{\delta}\right\}
.

Definition

(Multivariate
\Delta
-analytic functions)
.

Let
\bm{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}_{*}^{d}
. We say that a multivariate function
A(\bm{z})
is
\Delta
-analytic at
\bm{\rho}
if it has an analytic continuation on the product domain
\bm{\rho}{\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}}:=(\rho_{1}\Delta_{\delta})\times\cdots\times(\rho_{d}\Delta_{\delta})
for some
\delta>0
, where
\Delta_{\delta}
is the univariate
\Delta
-domain defined in (
1
).

Like in the univariate case, one can make the change of variable
\bm{z}^{\prime}=\bm{z}/\bm{\rho}\equiv(z_{1}/\rho_{1},\ldots,z_{d}/\rho_{d})
to bring the point
\bm{\rho}
to
\bm{1}\equiv(1,\ldots,1)
. In the following, we will focus without loss of generality on functions which are
\Delta
-analytic at
\bm{1}
.

Definition

(Demi-analytic functions)
.

Let
\bm{\Omega}=\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{d}
be an open product domain in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and let
\bm{\rho}\in(\partial\Omega_{1})\times\cdots\times(\partial\Omega_{d})
. For
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, denote
\bm{\Omega}_{\hat{j}}=\Omega_{1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{j-1}\times\mathbb{C}\times\Omega_{j+1}\times\cdots\times\Omega_{d}
.
We say that a function
A:\bm{\Omega}\to\mathbb{C}
is
demi-analytic at
\bm{\rho}\in\bm{\Omega}
if
A
is analytic on
\bm{\Omega}
and there exist
\epsilon>0
and a decomposition
A=A_{1}+\cdots+A_{d}
such that
A_{j}
is analytic in
B_{\epsilon}(\bm{\rho})\cap\bm{\Omega}_{\hat{j}}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
If each term
A_{j}
in the above decomposition is analytic on
\bm{\Omega}_{\hat{j}}
, then we say that
A
is
demi-entire
.

Definition

(Generalized homogeneous functions)
.

Let
\bm{K}
be a cone in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, i.e. a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
\left\{\sigma\bm{z}\,\middle|\,\bm{z}\in\bm{K}\right\}=\bm{K}
for all
\sigma>0
.
For
\theta_{0}\in\real
and
\bm{\theta}\in\real_{>0}^{d}
, we say that a function
H:\bm{K}\to\mathbb{C}
is
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous
if

H(\sigma^{\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})\equiv H(\sigma^{\theta_{1}}u_{1},\ldots,\sigma^{\theta_{d}}u_{d})=\sigma^{\theta_{0}}H(\bm{u})

(10)

for all
\bm{u}\in\bm{K}
and
\sigma>0
.

It is clear that a
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function is also
(\tau\theta_{0},\tau\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous for all
\tau>0
.
The classical notion of
homogeneous functions of degree
D
becomes
(D,\bm{1})
-homogeneous
in our terminology.

The three definitions above generalize respectively the notions of
\Delta
-analytic functions, locally analytic functions (for the term
A_{\mathtt{reg}}
in
Theorem

′
), and power functions (for the term
h\cdot(1-z)^{\alpha}
) used in the univariate transfer theorems.
To state the multivariate transfer theorem, we need one more definition whose counterpart does not appear explicitly in the univariate setting:

Definition

(Functions of polynomial type)
.

For any
\delta>0
,
we say that a function
F
is
of polynomial type (globally) on
\bm{K}_{\delta}
if there exist
C,M>0
such that

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{K}_{\delta},\quad\left|F(\bm{u})\right|\leq C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{-M}+|u_{1}|^{M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{-M}+|u_{d}|^{M}\right)

(11)

We say that
F
is
of polynomial type locally at
\bm{0}\in\bm{K}_{\delta}
if the above bound only holds in a neighborhood of
\bm{0}\in\bm{K}_{\delta}
. Equivalently,
F
is
of polynomial type locally at
\bm{0}\in\bm{K}_{\delta}
if there exist
\epsilon,C,M>0
such that

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{K}_{\delta}\cap\bm{B}_{\bm{0},\epsilon}\,,\quad\left|F(\bm{u})\right|\leq C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{-M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{-M}\right).

(12)

Similarly, we say that a function
A
is
of polynomial type on
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
if there exist
C,M>0
such that

\forall\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta},\quad\left|A(\bm{z})\right|\leq C\cdot\left(|z_{1}-1|^{-M}+\cdots+|z_{d}-1|^{-M}\right)

(13)

(here we do not need the terms
|z_{j}-1|^{M}
on the right hand side because
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
is bounded),
and we say that
A
is
of polynomial type locally at
\bm{1}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
if it satisfies the above bound in
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\cap\bm{B}_{\bm{1},\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
.
For
\bm{S}=\bm{K}_{\delta}
,
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}
or
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
, we write
\mathcal{P}(\bm{S})=\left\{f:\bm{S}\to\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,f\text{ is analytic and of polynomial type on }\bm{S}\right\}
.

Up to decreasing
\delta
, a continuous function on
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
is always bounded by a constant on
(\Delta_{\delta}\setminus U)^{d}
for any neighborhood
U
of
1\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus, the condition that
A
is of polynomial type (
globally
) on
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
is essentially an upper bound for
\left|A(\bm{z})\right|
when
z_{j}\to 1
in
\Delta_{\delta}
for
some

j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
In contrast, the condition of being of polynomial type
locally
at
\bm{1}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
only gives a bound for
|A(\bm{z})|
when
z_{j}\to 1
for
all

j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
When
d=1
, the two conditions are essentially the same. They do not appear explicitly in the statement of the univariarte transfer theorem because a function having an asymptotic expansion of the form (
4
) is automatically of polynomial type on
\Delta_{\delta}
.

Main results.

We are now ready to state the multivariate transfer theorem. Like in the univariate case, we formulate it in two ways, one discussing the building blocks of the coefficient asymptotics piece by piece, and the other showing how the theorem would be applied in practice.

Theorem 1

(Multivariate transfer theorem)
.

Let
\theta_{0}\in\real
,
\bm{\theta}\in\real_{>0}^{d}
and
A\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})
for some
\delta>0
.
We consider the asymptotics of the coefficients
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
when
\bm{n}\to\bm{\infty}
in the
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal regime.

1.

(Demi-analytic terms have exponentially small contribution)

If
A
is demi-analytic at
\bm{1}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
, then there exist
r\in(0,1)
and
\sigma>0
such that
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})=O(r^{n_{0}^{\sigma}})
.

2.

(Coefficient asymptotics of generalized homogeneous functions)

When
A(\bm{z})=H(\bm{1}-\bm{z})
and
H
is
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous (
\theta_{0}\in\real
), we have the asymptotic expansion

[\bm{z^{n}}]H(\bm{1}-\bm{z})=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\Theta}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{D_{\bm{k}}I(\bm{\lambda})}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}\equiv\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\Theta}}\left(I(\bm{\lambda})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial_{j}I(\bm{\lambda})+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{j}\partial_{j}^{2}I(\bm{\lambda})}{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}+\cdots\right).

(14)

. Together with the growth bounds (

) and (

\forall\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{C}}\setminus\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O},\quad\left|\frac{A(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\right|\leq\frac{dC\cdot n_{0}^{M\theta_{\mathtt{max}}}}{\tilde{c}^{\,d-1}\cdot(1+\delta/2)^{n_{j^{*}}+1}}\leq\frac{\tilde{C}\cdot n_{0}^{M\theta_{\mathtt{max}}}}{(1+\delta/2)^{\lambda_{\mathtt{min}}\cdot n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}}}\leq\mathtt{C}\cdot r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}}

for some constants

\mathtt{C}
independent of
n_{0}
. It follows that

[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})=I_{\mathtt{loc}}+O\big(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}}\big)

(30)

for some constant
r\in(0,1)
as

Step 2. Removing the demi-analytic term.

Let

I_{\mathtt{loc}}=I_{\mathtt{reg}}+I_{\mathtt{sing}}:=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\frac{A_{\mathtt{reg}}(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}+\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\frac{A_{\mathtt{sing}}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}

(31)

Recall that the demi-analytic term

A_{\mathtt{reg}}=A_{\mathtt{reg,\mathrm{1}}}+\cdots+A_{\mathtt{reg,\mathnormal{d}}}
, where each
A_{\mathtt{reg,\mathnormal{j}}}
is analytic in
(\Delta_{\delta}^{j-1}\times\mathbb{C}\times\Delta_{\delta}^{d-j})\cap\bm{B}_{\bm{1},\epsilon}

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}^{O}

\mathcal{C}_{j}^{O}

\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\frac{A_{\mathtt{reg,\mathnormal{j}}}(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{1}^{O}\times\cdots\times\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{j}^{O}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{C}_{d}^{O}}\frac{A_{\mathtt{reg,\mathnormal{j}}}(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}z=O(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}})

(32)

for some
r\in(0,1)
. Summing over

gives
I_{\mathtt{reg}}=O(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}})
. Together with Step 1, this implies

[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})=I_{\mathtt{sing}}+O(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}})

(33)

for some constant
r\in(0,1)
as
n_{0}\to\infty
.
In particular, when

Theorem

Step 3. Transfer.

By linearity, the expansion (
16

I_{\mathtt{sing}}=I_{0}+\cdots+I_{m}
, where

I_{k}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\!\!\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\!\!\frac{H_{k}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}\quad\text{for }0\leq k<m\qquad\text{and}\qquad I_{m}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\!\!\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\!\!\frac{o(H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z}))}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}

(34)

Thanks to the analyticity of

[\bm{z^{n}}]H_{k}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})=I_{k}+O(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}})\qquad\text{for all }0\leq k<m.

(35)

Now let us show that

\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}

For a function
\varepsilon(x)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{0.79727pt}{$\scriptstyle x\to\infty$}]{}0

\mathcal{C}_{j}^{O}
and
\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}

\mathcal{C}_{j}^{o}=\{z\in\mathcal{C}_{j}^{O}:\,|z-1|<\varepsilon(n_{0})\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{o}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{C}_{d}^{o}\,.

(36)

Contrary to
\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}
​, which has a small but fixed size
\epsilon

\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}

\bm{1}
when

According to the above definition, for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}\setminus\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}

j^{*}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
|z_{j^{*}}|\geq 1+\sin(\delta/2)\cdot\varepsilon(n_{0})

Then, similarly to (

\forall\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}\setminus\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o},\quad\left|\frac{H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\right|\leq\frac{\tilde{C}n_{0}^{M\theta_{\mathtt{max}}}}{\big(1+\sin(\delta/2)\cdot\varepsilon(n_{0})\big)^{\lambda_{\mathtt{min}}\cdot n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}}}\leq\mathtt{C}\cdot r^{\varepsilon(n_{0})\cdot n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}}

(37)

for some

when

\varepsilon(x)=x^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}/2}

\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}\setminus\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}}\frac{o(H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z}))}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=o\left(n_{0}^{-\Theta_{m}}\right).

(38)

as

On the other hand, since the contour
\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}

\forall\bm{z}\in\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o},\quad\left|o(H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z}))\right|\ \leq\ \tau\left|H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})\right|

(39)

\bm{u}=n_{0}^{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{1}-\bm{z})\equiv(\mathchoice{n_{0}^{\theta_{1}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{\theta_{1}}$}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{1}}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{1}}}(1-z_{1}),\ldots,\mathchoice{n_{0}^{\theta_{d}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{\theta_{d}}$}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{d}}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{d}}}(1-z_{d}))

\left|\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}}\frac{o(H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z}))}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}\right|\leq\tau\int_{\bm{V}^{o}}\left|\frac{H_{m}(n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})}{\big(\bm{1}-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u}\big)^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\right|\frac{\left|\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\right|}{n_{0}^{\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d}}}=\frac{\tau}{\,\,n_{0}^{\Theta_{m}}}\int_{\bm{V}^{o}}\bigg|\frac{H_{m}(\bm{u})}{\big(\bm{1}-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u}\big)^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\bigg|\left|\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\right|

(40)

for
n_{0}\geq n_{0}^{*}
, where
\bm{V}^{o}
is the image of
\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}
under the change of variable, and the last equality used the
(\theta_{0}^{(m)},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneity of
H_{m}
.
The bounds (
25
) and (

\frac{C|u_{1}\cdots u_{d}|^{M}}{c^{d}e^{-\mu\cdot\mathfrak{Re}(u_{1}+\cdots+u_{d})}}\,=\,c^{-d}C\prod_{j=1}^{d}|u_{j}|^{M}e^{\mu\mathfrak{Re}(u_{j})}\,,

(41)

which is integrable on
\bm{V}\supset\bm{V}^{o}
. Hence the inegral on
\bm{V}^{o}
is bounded by a constant independent of
n_{0}
. It follows that
\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{o}}\frac{o(H_{m}(\bm{1}-\bm{z}))}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}=o(n^{-\Theta_{m}})
,
Adding this to (
38

I_{m}=o(n_{0}^{-\Theta_{m}})
.
Together with the conclusions of Steps 2 and 3, this implies the asymptotic expansion (
17
) in Theorem
′
‣
1

When the little-o estimate in (

to

17

Step 4. Coefficient asymptotics of homogeneous functions.

It remains to prove the asymptotic expansion (
14

(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function
H
satisfying the assumptions of
Theorem

1
. With the same argument as in Step 3, there exists
r\in(0,1)
such that

[\bm{z^{n}}]H(\bm{1}-\bm{z})=J+O(r^{n_{0}^{\theta_{\mathtt{min}}}})\qquad\text{with}\qquad J:=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{O}}\frac{H(\bm{1}-\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}

(42)

We perform the same change of variable as in Step 3, which gives

J=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{V}^{O}}\frac{H(n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})}{(1-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{u}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{1}+\cdots+\theta_{d}}}=\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\Theta}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{V}^{O}}\frac{H(\bm{u})}{(1-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}

(43)

where
\Theta=\theta_{0}+\cdots+\theta_{d}
as defined in
Theorem

1
.
According to the definition (
3
) of the coefficients
g_{k,l}
, we have

\left(1-n_{0}^{-\theta}u\right)^{-\lambda n_{0}^{\theta}-1}=\ e^{\lambda u}\,G\left(n_{0}^{-\theta}u,\lambda u\right)\ =\ e^{\lambda u}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}g_{k,l}\,\lambda^{l}u^{k+l}\right)\frac{1}{n_{0}^{k\cdot\theta}}\,.

(44)

Applying the above formula to each factor of the product

\prod_{j=1}^{d}\big(1-\mathchoice{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}u_{j}\big)^{-n_{j}-1}

\frac{1}{(\bm{1}-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}=\ e^{\bm{\lambda\cdot u}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\Bigg(\sum_{\bm{l}\leq\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k},\bm{l}}\,\bm{\lambda^{l}}\bm{u}^{\bm{k}+\bm{l}}\Bigg)\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}\,.

(45)

If we treat the right hand side as a formal sum over
\bm{k}
, and approximate the contour
\bm{V}^{O}
by its limit
\bm{V}
, then (
43
) would imply heuristically that:

\displaystyle J\,

\displaystyle\approx\,\frac{1}{n^{\Theta}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{V}}\Bigg(\sum_{\bm{l}\leq\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k},\bm{l}}\,\bm{\lambda^{l}}\bm{u}^{\bm{k}+\bm{l}}\Bigg)e^{\bm{\lambda\cdot u}}H(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{n^{\Theta}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}\cdot\sum_{\bm{l}\leq\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k},\bm{l}}\,\bm{\lambda^{l}}\bm{\partial_{\lambda}}^{\bm{k}+\bm{l}}\left(\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{V}}e^{\bm{\lambda\cdot u}}H(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{n^{\Theta}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}\cdot D_{\bm{k}}I(\bm{\lambda})\,.

where the function
I(\bm{\lambda})
and the differential operator
D_{\bm{k}}
are defined as in
Theorem

1
.

Now let us show that the last line is indeed an asymptotic expansion of

. In other words, for any

n_{0}^{\Theta}\cdot J=\,\sum_{\bm{k\cdot\theta}<N}\frac{D_{\bm{k}}I(\bm{\lambda})}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}^{N}}\right)

(46)

when
n_{0}\to\infty
. For this, let us go back to (
44
). It can be seen as the Taylor series expansion of the function
f_{y}(x)=(1-x)^{-y/x-1}
around
x=0
evaluated at

and
y=\lambda u
. The corresponding Taylor expansion with remainder term writes

f_{y}(x)=e^{y}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}g_{k,l}\,y^{l}\right)x^{k}+R_{m}(x,y)\quad\text{with}\quad R_{m}(x,y)=\frac{1}{m!}\int_{0}^{x}(x-\xi)^{m}f_{y}^{(m+1)}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\,.

(47)

It is not hard to show by induction that there are functions
\varphi_{k,m}
continuous on the unit disk, such that

f_{y}^{(m)}(x)=f_{y}(x)\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{m}\varphi_{k,m}(x)y^{k}\,,

(48)

It follows that for each
m
, there exists a constant
C_{m}>0
such that
\big|f_{y}^{(m)}(x)\big|\leq C_{m}(1+|y|^{m})\cdot\big|f_{y}(x)\big|
for all
|x|\leq 1/2
and
y\in\mathbb{C}
. Plugging this into the definition of
R_{m}(x,y)
gives that

\forall|x|\leq 1/2,\,\forall y\in\mathbb{C},\quad|R_{m}(x,y)|\leq\frac{C_{m}}{(m+1)!}(1+|y|^{m})\cdot|x|^{m+1}\sup_{\xi\in[0,x]}\big|f_{y}(\xi)\big|

(49)

Consider the case where
(x,y)=(\mathchoice{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}u,\lambda u)
with
n_{0}>0
,
\lambda\in[\lambda_{\mathtt{min}},\lambda_{\mathtt{max}}]
and

. In this case the condition
|x|\leq 1/2
is satisfied because
|u|\leq\epsilon\mathchoice{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}
on
V_{j}^{O}
and
\epsilon=\epsilon(\delta)
is assumed to be small enough.
Since
|u|\geq 1
on
V_{j}^{O}
, the term
(1+|y|^{m})
is bounded by a constant times
|u|^{m}
uniformly for
u\in V_{j}^{O}
. Moreover, for all
\xi\in[0,x]
, there exists
\tilde{n}_{0}>0
such that
\xi=\tilde{n}_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}u
. Thus we can apply the bound (
25
) to see that
|f_{y}(\xi)|=\big|(1-\tilde{n}_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}u)^{-\lambda u-1}\big|\leq c^{-1}e^{\mu\cdot\mathfrak{Re}(u)}
for all
\xi\in[0,x]
. It follows that

\forall u\in V_{j}^{O},\quad\left|R_{m}(\mathchoice{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}u,\lambda u)\right|\leq\tilde{C}_{m}\frac{|u|^{2m+1}e^{\mu\cdot\mathfrak{Re}(u)}}{n_{0}^{(m+1)\theta_{j}}}

(50)

for some constant
\tilde{C}_{m}
depending only on
\delta
,
\lambda_{\mathtt{min}}
,
\lambda_{\mathtt{max}}
and
m
. Then, the Taylor expansion (
47
) becomes

(1-\mathchoice{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}u)^{-\lambda\mathchoice{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{\theta_{j}}}-1}=e^{\lambda u}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k}g_{k,l}\,\lambda^{l}u^{k+l}\right)\frac{1}{n_{0}^{k\cdot\theta_{j}}}+O\left(\frac{|u|^{2m+1}e^{\mu\cdot\mathfrak{Re}(u)}}{n_{0}^{(m+1)\theta_{j}}}\right)\,,

(51)

where the big-O estimate is uniform with respect to
u\in V_{j}^{O}
as
n_{0}\to\infty
. Now take
m>N/\theta_{\mathtt{min}}
and replace each factor in
\prod_{j=1}^{d}\big(1-\mathchoice{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}\raisebox{1.0pt}{$\!\!{}^{-\theta_{j}}$}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}{n_{0}^{-\theta_{j}}}u_{j}\big)^{-n_{j}-1}
by the right hand side of the above formula. After expanding the resulting product, we obtain a
finite
sum. By collecting all the terms of order
O(1/n_{0}^{N})
together, we obtain an expansion of the form

\frac{1}{(\bm{1}-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}=\ e^{\bm{\lambda\cdot u}}\sum_{\bm{k\cdot\theta}<N}\Bigg(\sum_{\bm{l}\leq\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k},\bm{l}}\,\bm{\lambda^{l}}\bm{u}^{\bm{k}+\bm{l}}\Bigg)\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}+O\left(\frac{R(\bm{u})}{n_{0}^{N}}\right),

(52)

where the big-O estimate is uniform on
\bm{u}\in\bm{V}^{O}
. By following the above calculation more closely, it is not hard to see that we can choose

for some
\mu,\tilde{M}>0

With the bound (
26
) for

, it follows that
H(\bm{u})R(\bm{u})
is integrable on
\bm{V}\supset\bm{V}^{O}
. So we can integrable the previous display term by term with respect to
\bm{u}\in\bm{V}^{O}
to obtain

\int_{\bm{V}^{O}}\frac{H(\bm{u})}{(\bm{1}-n_{0}^{-\bm{\theta}}\bm{u})^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}=\sum_{\bm{k\cdot\theta}<N}\int_{\bm{V}^{O}}H(\bm{u})e^{\bm{\lambda\cdot u}}\Bigg(\sum_{\bm{l}\leq\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k},\bm{l}}\,\bm{\lambda^{l}}\bm{u}^{\bm{k}+\bm{l}}\Bigg)\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\cdot\frac{1}{n_{0}^{\bm{k\cdot\theta}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}^{N}}\right).

(53)

Like in Step 1, replacing the contour
\bm{V}^{O}
by
\bm{V}
in each term of the above equation only produces an error of order

as

. The resulting equation divided by

gives exactly (
46

The expansion (
14

3
The Borel-Laplace transforms

In this section, we prove the three statements in
Theorem

2
.

Proof of
Theorem

2
(1)
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and
H\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Omega}_{\delta})
.
Recall that
\mathcal{B}[H]
is defined as the integral

I_{\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}}(\bm{\lambda}):=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\int_{\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}}e^{\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{u}}H(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},

(54)

on a contour
\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}
chosen from the class of contours

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta^{\prime}}:=\big\{V_{1}\times\cdots\times V_{d}\,\big|\,

\displaystyle\,\forall 1\leq j\leq d,\,V_{j}\text{ is a piecewise smooth curve in }\Omega_{\delta}\text{ }

\displaystyle\ \text{which coincide with }\partial\Omega_{\delta^{\prime}}\text{ outside a bounded set, as in \lx@cref{creftypecap}{fig:Borel-Laplace proof}\penalty\ \hyperref@@ii[fig:Borel-Laplace proof]{\ref*{fig:Borel-Laplace proof}(a)}}\big\}\,.

Thanks to the analyticity of
H
on the domain
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}=(\Omega_{\delta})^{d}
, it is clear that for each fixed
\delta^{\prime}
, the value of the integral
I_{\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}}(\bm{\lambda})
does not depend on the choice of the contour
\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}
within the class
\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta^{\prime}}
.

First, let us fix
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
and
\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta^{\prime}}
and show that the integral
I_{\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}}(\bm{\lambda})
is absolutely convergent and analytic with respect to
\bm{\lambda}\in\bm{K}_{\delta^{\prime}}
.
Let
U_{\delta^{\prime}\!,r}
be the closure of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\Omega_{\delta^{\prime}})\cup B_{0,r}
, where
B_{0,r}\subset\mathbb{C}
is the disk of radius
r
around the origin.
It is a simple exercise to show that for
\delta^{\circ}\in(0,\delta^{\prime})
and
r>0
, we have

\forall u\in U_{\delta^{\prime}\!,r},\ \forall\lambda\in K_{\delta^{\circ}},\quad\big|e^{\lambda u}\big|\,\leq\,C_{*}^{r|\lambda|}e^{-\sigma_{*}|\lambda|\cdot|u|}\,,

(55)

with
\sigma_{*}=\sin(\delta^{\prime}-\delta^{\circ})>0
and
C_{*}=e^{1+\sigma_{*}}
.
Since each component of the contour
\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}\in\bm{\mathcal{V}}_{\delta^{\prime}}
is bounded away from the origin and
H
is of polynomial type, there exist
\tilde{C},M>0
such that

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}},\quad|H(\bm{u})|\leq\tilde{C}\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{M}\right)\,.

(56)

On the other hand, there exists
r>0
such that
V_{\delta^{\prime}}\subset U_{\delta^{\prime}\!,r}
. So the bound (
55
) implies that for any bounded set
\bm{S}\subseteq\bm{K}_{\delta^{\circ}}
, there exists
C,\sigma>0
, such that

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}},\ \forall\bm{\lambda}\in\bm{S},\quad\big|e^{\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{u}}\big|\leq C\cdot e^{-\sigma(|u_{1}|+\cdots+|u_{d}|)}\,.

(57)

Up to increasing the value of
C
and decreasing
\sigma
, the polynomial bound on the right hand side of (
56
) can be absorbed by the exponential decay in the above display. Therefore we have

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}},\ \forall\bm{\lambda}\in\bm{S},\quad\big|e^{\bm{\lambda}\cdot\bm{u}}H(\bm{u})\big|\leq C\cdot e^{-\sigma(|u_{1}|+\cdots+|u_{d}|)}

(58)

for some
C,\sigma>0
.
Since the right hand side is independent of
\bm{\lambda}\in\bm{S}
and integrable on
\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}
, it follows that the integral
I_{\bm{V}_{\delta^{\prime}}}(\bm{\lambda})
is absolutely convergent and analytic with respect to

Notice that the Borel transform

where

(77)

The above decompositions allow us to reduce the proof of

2
(3)
to the univariate case. In the following, we assume

and fix some

and

Let

. Let us show that
\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{L}_{c}[I](\lambda)=I(\lambda)
for all
\lambda>c
.

\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
and let
V_{\delta^{\prime}}=\partial\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}
with the domain
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}
defined above (

).
Let
V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{+}
(resp.

V_{\delta^{\prime}}
in the upper half-plane (resp. lower half-plane).
Recall from the proof of
Theorem

2
(2)

\mathcal{L}_{c}[I]
can be expressed as an integral over

for any
\varphi\in(-\delta,\delta)
, where

is the contour shown in
Figure

2
(3)

\varphi\in(\delta^{\prime},\delta)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\circ\mathcal{L}_{c}[I](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}}e^{\lambda u}\mathcal{L}_{c}[I](u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{+}}e^{\lambda u}\left(\int_{R_{c,-\varphi}}e^{-\tau u}I(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{-}}e^{\lambda u}\left(\int_{R_{c,\varphi}}e^{-\tau u}I(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\mathrm{d}u\right).

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left(\int_{R_{c,-\varphi}}I(\tau)\left(\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{+}}e^{\lambda u-\tau u}\mathrm{d}u\right)\mathrm{d}\tau+\int_{R_{c,\varphi}}I(\tau)\left(\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}^{-}}e^{\lambda u-\tau u}\mathrm{d}u\right)\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

(\lambda,v)\mapsto e^{-\lambda u}e^{\lambda v}H(v)
is integrable on
[c,\infty)\times V_{\delta^{\prime}}
. By Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{c}\circ\mathcal{B}[H](u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda u}\left(\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}}e^{\lambda v}H(v)\mathrm{d}v)\right)\mathrm{d}\lambda

(79)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}}H(v)\left(\int_{c}^{\infty}e^{-(u-v)\lambda}\mathrm{d}\lambda\right)\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}}H(v)\frac{e^{-(u-v)c}}{u-v}\mathrm{d}v\,.

(80)

For each
u\in\mathbb{C}
, let

E_{c}(u)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\partial U_{\delta^{\prime}\!,R}}H(v)\frac{e^{-(u-v)c}}{u-v}\mathrm{d}v\,.

(81)

It is not hard to see that the above limit stablizes when
R>|u|
, and defines an entire function of
u
. Actually, by the residue theorem, we have for all
R>|u|
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial U_{\delta^{\prime}\!,R}}H(v)\frac{e^{-(u-v)c}}{u-v}\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{V_{\delta^{\prime}}}H(v)\frac{e^{-(u-v)c}}{u-v}\mathrm{d}v+\res_{v\to u}H(v)\frac{e^{-(u-v)c}}{u-v}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{c}\circ\mathcal{B}[H](u)-H(u)\,.

It follows that
\mathcal{L}_{c}\circ\mathcal{B}[H](u)=H(u)+E_{c}(u)
for all
u>0
. By analytic continuation, the same is true for all
u\in\Omega_{\delta}
.
To recover the case of general dimension
d
, we apply this formula of
\mathcal{L}_{c}\circ\mathcal{B}[H]
to each factor in the decomposition (
77
). To simplify notation, we write
\mathcal{I}_{j}=\mathcal{L}_{c_{j}}^{(j)}\circ\mathcal{B}^{(j)}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\bm{c}}\circ\mathcal{B}[H]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{I}_{d-1}\circ\mathcal{I}_{d}\right)[H]

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{I}_{d-1}\right)[H]+\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{I}_{d-1}\right)[E_{\bm{c}}^{(d)}]

\displaystyle=\qquad\cdots\qquad\qquad\cdots\qquad\qquad\cdots\qquad

\displaystyle=H+E_{\bm{c}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{I}_{1}[E_{\bm{c}}^{(2)}]+\cdots+\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{I}_{d-1}\right)[E_{\bm{c}}^{(d)}]\,.

where each
E_{\bm{c}}^{(j)}
is an analytic function on
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}
which is entire with respect to the variable
u_{j}
. The same is true for

\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\mathcal{I}_{j-1}[E_{\bm{c}}^{(j)}]
, because the integral transform
\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\mathcal{I}_{j-1}
does not affect the variable
u_{j}
.
It follows that
E_{\bm{c}}^{(1)}+\mathcal{I}_{1}[E_{\bm{c}}^{(2)}]+\cdots+\left(\mathcal{I}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal{I}_{d-1}\right)[E_{\bm{c}}^{(d)}]
is a demi-entire function on
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta}
. This proves the decomposition
\mathcal{L}_{\bm{c}}\circ\mathcal{B}[H]=H+E_{\bm{c}}
in any dimension
d
, and concludes the proof of
Theorem

2
.

4
Discussions

In this section, we discuss some additional properties of the classes of multivariate functions used in the statement of the multivariate transfer theorems.

\Delta
-analytic functions.

Like in the univariate case, the multivariate transfer theorems relies fundamentally on the
\Delta
-analyticity of the generating function. But unlike the univariate case,
\Delta
-analyticity is a rather restrictive condition in the multivariate setting. In particular, it implies that the dominant singularity (for any reasonable definition of the term) is unique and independent of the exponent
\bm{\theta}
and the direction
\bm{\lambda}
of the
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal limit taken.
This is in stark contrast with the case of rational functions, where the dominant singularities (a.k.a. contributing critical points) generically depend on the direction of the diagonal limit taken.
In fact, a multivariate rational function is never
\Delta
-analytic at any of its poles, unless its denominator has a univariate linear factor which vanishes at this pole.

Proposition 5

(Genuinely multivariate rational functions are never
\Delta
-analytic)
.

If a rational function is
\Delta
-analytic at
\bm{1}
and has a pole at
\bm{1}
, then its denominator is divisible by
z_{j}-1
for some
1\leq j\leq d
.

Remark
.

The proposition as well as its proof given below can be easily generalized to meromorphic functions. We shall not enter into the details here and refer to
[
8
, Section 3.1.1]
for an introduction to meromorphic functions in several variable.

Proof.

Consider a rational function
A=\frac{F}{G}
, where
F,G
are coprime polynomials. The proposition follows directly from
Lemma

6
below about the local geometry of the zero set of a polynomial: it suffices to apply
Lemma

6
to the polynomial
H(\bm{u})=G(\bm{1}-\bm{u})
.
∎

For
\delta,\epsilon>0
, let
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta,\epsilon}=(\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon})^{d}
with

\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon}:=\Omega_{\delta}\cap B_{0,\epsilon}\equiv\left\{u\in\mathbb{C}:\,0<|u|<\epsilon\text{ and }|\arg(u)|<\frac{\pi}{2}+\delta\right\}
.

Lemma 6

(Local version of
Proposition

5
)
.

If a polynomial
H\in\mathbb{C}[\bm{u}]
has a zero at
\bm{0}
, but no zero on
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta,\epsilon}
for some
\delta,\epsilon>0
, then
H(\bm{u})
is divisible by
u_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq d
.

Proof.

When
d=1
, the lemma is trivial: a univariate polynomial
H(u)
has a zero at
0
if and only if it is divisible by
u
.

When
d=2
, consider a polynomial
H\in\mathbb{C}[u,v]

not
divisible by
u
nor
v
, such that
H(0,0)=0
. According to the Newton-Puiseux theorem (see e.g.
[
2
, Corollary 1.5.5, Theorem 1.7.2]
), there exist an integer
n\geq 1
and a nonzero analytic function
s
defined on a neighborhood of
0
, such that for every determination of the
n
-th root, the mapping
\varphi(u)=s(u^{1/n})
satisfies
H(u,\varphi(u))=0
for all
u
in some neighborhood of
0
. Up to swapping the variables
u
and
v
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\varphi(u)=O(u)
as
u\to 0
. Since
s
is analytic at
0
and is not identically zero, there exist
c\neq 0
and
\alpha\geq 1
such that
\varphi(u)\sim c\cdot u^{\alpha}
as
u\to 0
. Geometrically, this means that the mapping
\varphi
multiplies the angles at
u=0
by
\alpha
. In particular, for any
\delta,\epsilon>0
with
\epsilon
small enough, the image
\varphi(\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon})
conains an angle of
\alpha(\pi+2\delta)
at
0
. Since
\alpha\geq 1
, we have
\alpha(\pi+2\delta)+(\pi+2\delta)>2\pi
. This implies that
\varphi(\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon})\cap\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon}\neq\varnothing
. In other words, the graph of the mapping
\varphi
intersects
(\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon})^{2}
. It follows that the polynomial
H
has zeros on
(\Omega_{\delta,\epsilon})^{2}
for any
\delta,\epsilon>0
. By contraposition, this proves
Lemma

6
when
d=2
.

For
d\geq 3
, we give a proof by contradiction based on the result of the case
d=2
: Let
H\in\mathbb{C}[\bm{u}]
be a polynomial with a zero at
\bm{0}
, no zero on
\bm{\Omega}_{\delta,\epsilon}
for some
\delta,\epsilon>0
, and such that
H(\bm{u})
is
not
divisible by
u_{j}
for any
1\leq j\leq d
.
For
1\leq j\leq d
and
m\geq 0
, define
E_{m}^{(j)}=\left\{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\,\middle|\,(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{d})-n_{j}=m\right\}
. Let us prove the following statement by induction on
m
:

\forall j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},\,\forall\bm{n}\in E_{m}^{(j)},\quad[\bm{u^{n}}]H(\bm{u})=0\,.

(
\mathcal{H}_{m}
)

For
\bm{x}\in\real_{>0}^{d-1}
, let
H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=H(u,vx_{1},\ldots,vx_{d-1})
. One can check that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

[v^{k}]H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=\sum_{\bm{n}\in E_{k}^{(1)}}[\bm{u^{n}}]H(\bm{u})\cdot u^{n_{1}}\cdot x_{1}^{n_{2}}\cdots x_{d-1}^{n_{d}}\,.

(82)

Now fix some
m\geq 0
and assume that
(\mathcal{H}_{k})
is true for all
0\leq k<m
. By (
82
), we have
[v^{k}]H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=0
for all
k<m
. Hence
H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(u,v):=v^{-m}H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)
is a polynomial in
(u,v)
. Moreover,
H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(0,0)=0
: For
m=0
, we have
H_{\bm{x}}^{(0)}(0,0)=H_{\bm{x}}(0,0)=H(\bm{0})=0
by assumption. When
m\geq 1
, one can check that

\big\{\bm{n}\in E_{m}^{(1)}\,\big|\,n_{1}=0\big\}\ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{k<m}\Bigg(\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}E_{k}^{(j)}\Bigg)\,.

(83)

Hence the hypotheses
(\mathcal{H}_{k})_{k<m}
and (
82
) imply that
H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(0,0)\equiv[u^{0}v^{m}]H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=0
as well.
On the other hand, since
H(\bm{u})\neq 0
for all
\bm{u}\in\bm{\Omega}_{\delta,\epsilon}
, we have
H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(u,v)=v^{-m}H(u,vx_{1},\ldots,vx_{d-1})\neq 0
for all
(u,v)\in(\Omega_{\delta,\tilde{\epsilon}})^{2}
, where
\tilde{\epsilon}=\min\left(1,x_{1}^{-1},\ldots,x_{d-1}^{-1}\right)\cdot\epsilon>0
.
So, according to the result of the case
d=2
, the polynomial
H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(u,v)
is either divisible by
u
or divisible by
v
.
In particular, we have

H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(0,1)\cdot H_{\bm{x}}^{(m)}(u,0)\equiv H(0,\bm{x})\cdot[v^{m}]H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=0

(84)

for all
\bm{x}\in\real_{>0}^{d-1}
. By analytic continuation, the above identity is valid for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{d-1}
. Because
H(\bm{u})
is not divisible by
u_{1}
, the polynomial
\bm{x}\mapsto H(0,\bm{x})
is not identically zero. Since the ring of polynomials is an integral domain, this implies that
[v^{m}]H_{\bm{x}}(u,v)=0
as a polynomial in
\bm{x}
. Comparing this to (
82
), we see that
[\bm{u^{n}}]H(\bm{u})=0
for all
\bm{n}\in E_{m}^{(1)}
.
The same argument works for
E^{(j)}_{m}
for any
1\leq j\leq d
. Therefore
(\mathcal{H}_{m})
is true.

By induction,
(\mathcal{H}_{m})
holds for all
m\geq 0
. But this implies that
H(\bm{u})\equiv 0
, which contradicts the assumption that
H(\bm{u})
is not divisible by
u_{j}
. This completes the proof of
Lemma

6
for general
d\geq 3
.
∎

Demi-analytic functions.

A univariate function
A
is demi-analytic at
\rho
if and only if it is analytic in a neighborhood of
\rho
, and it is demi-entire if and only if it is an entire function. Thus the names “demi-analytic” and “demi-entire”.

It is easy to check that a
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function is demi-entire with respect to a cone if and only if it is demi-analytic at
\bm{0}
with respect to the same cone.

Given the form of the asymptotic expansion (
17
) and the analyticity of the scaling function
I(\bm{\lambda})
, it is not hard to see that the coefficients
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
decays exponentially as
n_{0}\to\infty
if and only if
I
is identically zero. By
Corollary

3
, this happens if and only if the homogeneous component
H(\bm{u})
is demi-analytic. In this sense, the demi-analyticity condition in
Theorem

1
(1)
is optimal.

Generalized homogeneous functions.

If
H
is a
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function, then for all
u_{1}>0
, we have

H(\bm{u})=u_{1}^{\theta_{0}/\theta_{1}}\cdot H\left(1,\frac{u_{2}}{u_{1}^{\theta_{2}/\theta_{1}}\,},\cdots,\frac{u_{d}}{\,u_{1}^{\theta_{d}/\theta_{1}}}\right)\,.

(85)

Inversely, for any function
h(r_{2},\ldots,r_{d})
of
d-1
variables,
H(\bm{u})=u^{\theta_{0}/\theta_{1}}\cdot h\big(u_{1}^{-\theta_{2}/\theta_{1}}u_{2},\cdots,u_{1}^{-\theta_{d}/\theta_{1}}u_{d}\big)
is a
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function satisfying
h(r_{2},\ldots,r_{d})=H(1,r_{2},\ldots,r_{d})
.
In particular, when
d=1
, the only
(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
-homogeneous analytic functions are the power functions
H(u)=C\,u^{\theta_{0}/\theta_{1}}
(
C\in\mathbb{C}
).

For each
\bm{\alpha}\in\real^{d}
, the monomial
\bm{u}\mapsto\bm{u^{\alpha}}
is
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous for all
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
such that
\bm{\alpha\cdot\theta}=\theta_{0}
.
Clearly, a linear combination of finitely many such monomials is also
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous, as long as their multi-exponents
\bm{\alpha}
satisfy the above linear relation for the same
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
.
Such polynomials are obviously
not
all the homogeneous functions, but they provide a good intuition for how a non-homogeneous function could be decomposed into homogeneous components.

Functions of polynomial type.

In
Theorem

′
, the remainder term in the asymptotic expansion of the generating function
A(\bm{z})
was expressed as a big-O of some
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function of polynomial type. The following lemma tells us that every such remainder term can also be bounded by finitely many
monomials
with the same homogeneity.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\bm{K}
be a cone in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
If
H:\bm{K}\to\mathbb{C}
is a
(\theta_{0},\bm{\theta})
-homogeneous function of polynomial type locally at
\bm{0}
, then there exist a constant
C
and finitely many vectors
\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\in\real^{d}
, such that
\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}\bm{\cdot\theta}=\theta_{0}
for all
k
, and

\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{K},\quad|H(\bm{u})|\leq C\cdot\sum_{k}\big|\bm{u}^{\bm{\alpha}^{(k)}}\big|\,.

(86)

Proof.

By considering
\mathcal{H}(\bm{s})=H(\bm{s^{\theta}})
instead of
H
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\bm{\theta}=\bm{1}
.
By assumption, there exist constants
C,M,\epsilon>0
such that on
\left\{\bm{u}\in\bm{K}\,\middle|\,\forall 1\leq j\leq d,\,|u_{j}|<\epsilon\right\}
, we have

|H(\bm{u})|\leq C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{-M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{-M}\right)\,.

(87)

Let
i
be such that
|u_{i}|=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|u_{j}|
. Then we can rescale the vector
\bm{u}
by
\frac{\epsilon}{|u_{i}|}
to place it in the above set. Since
H
is
(\theta_{0},\bm{1})
-homogeneous, we have
H(\bm{u})=\epsilon^{-\theta_{0}}|u_{i}|^{\theta_{0}}H\left(\epsilon\frac{u_{1}}{|u_{i}|},\,\ldots\,,\epsilon\frac{u_{d}}{|u_{i}|}\right)
, and therefore

\displaystyle\left|H(\bm{u})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{-\theta_{0}}|u_{i}|^{\theta_{0}}C\cdot\Big(\epsilon^{-M}\Big|\frac{u_{1}}{u_{i}}\Big|^{-M}+\cdots+\epsilon^{-M}\Big|\frac{u_{d}}{u_{i}}\Big|^{-M}\Big)

\displaystyle=C\epsilon^{-\theta_{0}-M}\left(\big|u_{i}^{\theta_{0}+M}u_{2}^{-M}\big|+\cdots+\big|u_{i}^{\theta_{0}+M}u_{d}^{-M}\big|\right)

Summing the right hand side over
i
gives a bound of
H(\bm{u})
of the form (
86
) for all
\bm{u}\in\bm{K}
.
∎

Lemma

7
was briefly used in the proof of
Theorem

′
to obtain the bound (
26
). It implies (
26
) because the variable
\bm{u}\in\bm{V}
in (
26
) satisfies
|u_{j}|\geq 1
for all
1\leq j\leq d
.

In practical examples, it is usually not hard to check that a function is of polynomial type, in particular thanks to the following lemma.

Lemma 8

(Closure properties of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
and
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})
)
.

For any
\delta\in(0,\pi)
, the space
\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
forms a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra with respect to pointwise addition and multiplication of functions.

Moreover, if
f
is any function such that
|f(x)|
is bounded by a polynomial of
|x|
(e.g.
f(x)=x^{\beta}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
), then for all
h\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
such that
f\circ h
is well-defined and analytic on
\bm{K}_{\delta}
, we have
f\circ h\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
.

The same is true for
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})
with
\delta\in(0,\pi/2)
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,\pi)
and let
g,h\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
. By definition there exist
C,M>0
such that

\displaystyle\left|g(\bm{u})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{M}+|u_{1}|^{-M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{M}+|u_{d}|^{-M}\right)

(88)

\displaystyle\text{and}\qquad\left|h(\bm{u})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{M}+|u_{1}|^{-M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{M}+|u_{d}|^{-M}\right)

(89)

for all
\bm{u}\in\bm{K}_{\delta}
.
It is clear that any linear combination of
g
and
h
satisfies a bound of the same form. So
\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
is a
\mathbb{C}
-vector space. In addition, since
ab\leq\frac{1}{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})
for all
a,b\in\real
, we have

\displaystyle|g(\bm{u})h(\bm{u})|

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\Big(|u_{i}|^{M}|u_{j}|^{M}+|u_{i}|^{M}|u_{j}|^{-M}+|u_{i}|^{-M}|u_{j}|^{M}+|u_{i}|^{-M}|u_{j}|^{-M}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\Big(|u_{i}|^{2M}+|u_{j}|^{2M}+|u_{i}|^{-2M}+|u_{j}|^{-2M}\Big)\,.

So
\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
is also closed under multiplication, and therefore forms a
\mathbb{C}
-algebra.

Now let
f
be a function such that
|f(x)|
is bounded by a polynomial of
|x|
. Then there exist
c>0
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
|f(x)|\leq c\cdot(1+|x|^{m})
. If
h\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
and
f\circ h
is well-defined and analytic on
\bm{K}_{\delta}
, then we have
h^{m}\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
by the closure of
\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
under multiplication, and therefore

\left|f\circ h(\bm{u})\right|\leq c\cdot(1+|h(\bm{u})^{m}|)\leq c\cdot\Big(1+C\cdot\left(|u_{1}|^{M}+|u_{1}|^{-M}+\cdots+|u_{d}|^{M}+|u_{d}|^{-M}\right)\Big)

(90)

for some
C,M>0
. It follows that
f\circ h\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{K}_{\delta})
.
The same proof works for
\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})
.
∎

Let
\bm{\Omega}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{d}
. A function
A:\bm{\Omega}\to\mathbb{C}
is
algebraic
if there exists a polynomial
E\in\mathbb{C}[a,\bm{z}]
, such that
E(A(\bm{z}),\bm{z})=0
for all
\bm{z}\in\bm{\Omega}
.

Lemma 9

(Algebraic functions are of polynomial type)
.

If
A
is an algebraic function analytic on
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
, then
A\in\mathcal{P}(\bm{\Delta}_{\delta^{\prime}})
for all
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
.

Proof.

Let
A
be an algebraic function which is analytic on
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
. For
\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
, let

h(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{dist}(\bm{z},\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})}\equiv\left(\inf_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}}\left\|\bm{z}-\bm{w}\right\|\right)^{-1},

(91)

where
\left\|\bm{z}-\bm{w}\right\|
is the Euclidean distance between
\bm{z}
and
\bm{w}
, viewed as points in
2d
. Let us show that there exist
C,M>0
such that

\forall\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta},\quad\big|A(\bm{z})\big|\leq C\cdot h(\bm{z})^{M}\,.

(92)

This is a variant of the
Łojasiewicz inequality
in semi-algebraic geometry. See e.g.
[
1
, Chapter 2]
for an introduction.
Recall that a set
S\subseteq\real^{n}
is semi-algebraic if and only if it can be defined by a first order logic formula involving only polynomial conditions (i.e. equations or inequalities) and quantifiers
\forall/\exists
over . And a function
f:S\to\real^{m}
is semi-algebraic if and only if
\mathtt{Graph}(f):=\left\{(\bm{x},f(\bm{x}))\in\real^{n+m}\,\middle|\,\bm{x}\in S\right\}
is a semi-algebraic set.

It is not hard to write a first order formula that describes the set
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
(viewed as a subset of
2d
). Therefore
\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}
is a semi-algebraic set.
It is well-known that a continuous algebraic function defined on an open semi-algebraic set is always semi-algebraic (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorem 11]
). Hence the function
A:\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\to\mathbb{C}\equiv\real^{2}
is semi-algebraic.
Thanks to general closure properties of the class of semi-algebraic functions (c.f.
[
1
, Section 2.2]
), we deduce from the above facts that the functions
h:\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\to\real
and
|A|:\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\to\real
are also semi-algebraic.

For each
t>0
, let
G_{t}=\left\{\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}:\,h(\bm{z})=t\right\}
and
g(t)=\sup_{\bm{z}\in G_{t}}|A(\bm{z})|
, with the convention that
\sup\varnothing=0
. The graph of the function
g:\real_{>0}\to\real
can be described by a first order formula as follows:

\displaystyle\mathtt{Graph}(g)=\Big\{\,(t,y)\in\real^{2}\ \Big|\ t>0\text{ and }

\displaystyle\left(\forall\bm{z}\in G_{t},\,|A(\bm{z})|\leq y\right)

and

\displaystyle\big((G_{t}=\varnothing\text{ and }y=0)\text{ or }(\exists\bm{z}\in G_{t},\,|A(\bm{z})|=y)\big)\,\Big\}

where

\displaystyle\Big(\forall\bm{z}\in G_{t},\,|A(\bm{z})|\leq y\Big)\ \Leftrightarrow\ \Big(\,\forall\bm{z}\in\real^{2d},\,

\displaystyle\big(\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\text{ and }(\bm{z},t)\in\mathtt{Graph}(h)\big)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\big(\exists w\in\real,\,(\bm{z},w)\in\mathtt{Graph}(|A|)\text{ and }w\leq y\big)\Big)\,,

\displaystyle\Big(G_{t}=\varnothing\text{ and }y=0\Big)\Leftrightarrow\ \Big(\,\forall\bm{z}\in\real^{2d},\,

\displaystyle\big(\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\Rightarrow(\bm{z},t)\not\in\mathtt{Graph}(h)\big)\Big)\,,

\displaystyle\Big(\exists\bm{z}\in G_{t},\,|A(\bm{z})|=y\Big)\Leftrightarrow\ \Big(\,\exists\bm{z}\in\real^{2d},\,

\displaystyle\big(\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta}\text{ and }(\bm{z},t)\in\mathtt{Graph}(h)\text{ and }(\bm{z},y)\in\mathtt{Graph}(|A|)\big)\Big)\,.

Since
h
and
|A|
are semi-algebraic functions, the conditions
(\bm{z},t)\in\mathtt{Graph}(h)
and
(\bm{z},y)\in\mathtt{Graph}(|A|)
can be further expanded into first order logic formulas involving only polynomial conditions and quantifiers over . It follows that
g
is a semi-algebraic
function. A classical result on the growth rate of univariate semi-algebraic functions
[
1
, Proposition 2.6.1]
states that there exist constants
C,M,t_{0}>0
such that
g(t)\leq C\cdot t^{M}
for all
t>t_{0}
. The definition of
g
implies that it is bounded on
(0,t_{0}]
. Hence the bound
g(t)\leq C\cdot t^{M}
extends to all
t>0
. This proves (
92
).

Finally, notice that for all
\delta^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\forall\bm{z}\in\bm{\Delta}_{\delta^{\prime}},\quad c^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\bm{z},\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})\ \leq\ \min_{1\leq j\leq d}|z_{j}-1|\ \leq\ c\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\bm{z},\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\bm{\Delta}_{\delta})\,.

(93)

Moreover, we have

\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq d}|z_{j}|\right)^{-M}\!\leq\ |z_{1}-1|^{-M}+\cdots+|z_{d}-1|^{-M}\,\leq\,d\cdot\left(\min_{1\leq j\leq d}|z_{j}|\right)^{-M}.

(94)

It follows that

on

for some

Background on ACSV and its general strategy.

The results in paper fall under the topic of
analytic combinatorics in several variables
(ACSV). The remaining paragraphs provide some background on ACSV and where this work stands relative to the others.

In general, analytic combinatorics aims at understanding the enumerative properties of large combinatorial structures through the analytic properties of their generating functions. This is usually done in two steps: First, some (possibly implicit) expression of the generating function must be derived from the definition of the combinatorial structure that it encodes. Then, one studies the generating function as an complex analytic function to derive asymptotic formulas of its coefficients.

Compared to analytic combinatorics in one variable, to which
the transfer theorems
A
and
′
‣
1
belong
, analytic combinatorics in several variables is a much less mature theory. The difference is especially stark when it comes to derving coefficient asymptotics from the generating functions (i.e. the second step outlined above). This process is often known as
singularity analysis
, since the asymptotic expansion of the coefficients of a generating function is mostly determined by the properties of the function near its singularities.
For instance, if a function
A(z)
has a unique dominant singularity at
\rho\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, then thanks to the analyticity of
A
everywhere else inside and on the circle of radius
|\rho|
, we can deform the contour of integration in the Cauchy integral formula (
27
) to a curve
\mathcal{C}
that coincides with a circle of radius
r>|\rho|
everywhere except in a neighborhood
\mathcal{N}
of
\rho
.
By splitting the parts of the contour inside and outside
\mathcal{N}
, we get
[z^{n}]A(z)=I_{\mathtt{loc}}+I_{\mathtt{rem}}
with

I_{\mathtt{loc}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}\,\cap\,\mathcal{N}}\frac{A(z)}{z^{n+1}}\mathrm{d}z\qquad\text{and}\qquad I_{\mathtt{rem}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}\,\setminus\,\mathcal{N}}\frac{A(z)}{z^{n+1}}\mathrm{d}z\,.

(95)

Since
|z|=r
on
\mathcal{C\setminus N}
, we have
I_{\mathtt{rem}}=O(r^{-n})
, which is exponentially small compared to
|\rho|^{-n}
. On the other hand,
\limsup_{n\to\infty}([z^{n}]A(z))^{1/n}=|\rho|^{-1}
by the root test of radius of convergence. Hence the asymptotics of
[z^{n}]A(z)
is dominated by the term
I_{\mathtt{loc}}
.
Since
I_{\mathtt{loc}}
only depends on
A(z)
in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of
\rho
, its asymptotics can be further studied via asymptotic expansions of the function
A(z)
near its singularity
z=\rho
. (This is basically the beginning of a proof of Theorem
′
‣
1
.)

At first glance, it is not clear how the above approach of singularity analysis could be generalized to multivariate functions. Indeed, the singularities of a multivariate complex function
A(\bm{z})
always form a continuous set with no isolated points (c.f. Hartog’s extension theorem). The crucial remark here is that in general, not all singularities of
A(\bm{z})
contribute to the dominant asymptotics of its coeffcients. In nice cases, one can even expect to find a finite number of
contributing singularities
, so that the asymptotics of
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
is dominanted by the values of
A(\bm{z})
in arbitrarily small neighborhoods of these points. In practice, one would like deform the torus
\bm{T}_{r}
in the Cauchy integral formula (
27
) to a cycle (i.e.
d
-chain without boundary)
\bm{\mathcal{C}}
homologous to
\bm{T}_{r}
in the domain of analyticity of the integrant
\frac{A(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}
, such that the denominator
\left|\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}\right|
attains its mimimum only at a finite number of points
\bm{\rho}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\rho}^{(m)}
on
\bm{C}
. By Stokes’ theorem (see e.g.
[
9
, Appendix A.2]
), such a deformation does not change the value of the integral. One can then take arbitrarily small neighborhoods
\bm{\mathcal{N}}^{(1)},\ldots\bm{\mathcal{N}}^{(m)}
of the points
\bm{\rho}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\rho}^{(m)}
, and split the integral inside and outside these neighborhoods as in the univariate setting. This gives the decomposition

[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})=I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(1)}+\cdots+I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(m)}+I_{\mathtt{rem}}
, where

I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(s)}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\!\int_{\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{(s)}}\frac{A(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}\qquad\text{and}\qquad I_{\mathtt{rem}}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\right)^{d}\!\int_{\bm{C}\setminus\left(\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{(1)}\cup\cdots\cup\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{(m)}\right)}\frac{A(\bm{z})}{\bm{z}^{\bm{n}+\bm{1}}}\mathrm{d}\bm{z}\,,

(96)

with
\bm{\mathcal{C}}^{(s)}:=\bm{\mathcal{C}}\cap\bm{\mathcal{N}}^{(s)}
.
For a well chosen cycle
\bm{\mathcal{C}}
, we expect the
non-local
term
I_{\mathtt{rem}}
to be exponentially small compared to the
local
terms
I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(s)}
, so that the latter dominates the the asymptotics of
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
. After this, one still needs to expand the localized integrals
I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(s)}
to obtain a simple asymptotic expansion of
[\bm{z^{n}}]A(\bm{z})
. How this can be done depends on the form of the function
A(\bm{z})
. But usually this is a simpler problem, since there are a lot more tools at our disposal for the local analysis of a function.

The above general strategy to the multivariate singularity analysis has been outlined by Pemantle and Wilson in
[
9
]
. Over the past twenty years, they and their collaborators developped an impressive theory that treats the singularity analysis of multivariate rational-type functions in an algorithmic way. The results of their project are collected at
http://acsvproject.com/
. More precisely, they consider general rational functions in several variables (and also some other functions whose singularities form algebraic varieties) and compute their coefficient asymptotics in the diagonal limit (i.e.
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal limit with
\bm{\theta}=(1,\ldots,1)
). In this broad setting, they carry out the strategy described in the previous paragraph in a systematic way, identifying the contributing singularities
\bm{\rho}^{(s)}
and simplifying the localized integrals
I_{\mathtt{loc}}^{(s)}
, using powerful tools from algebraic topology and Morse theory.
The reason that they need such advanced tools is that the singular set of a general rational function can have a quite complicated geometry. In particular, the location of the dominant singularities
\bm{\rho}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{\rho}^{(m)}
(or
contributing critical points
, as they are called in
[
9
]
) depends on the direction
\bm{\lambda}
of the diagonal limit. And in general, they cannot be reached by the easy-to-visualize cycles of product form
\bm{\mathcal{C}}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{C}_{d}
.

This paper explores the case of
\Delta
-analytic generating functions, following the same general strategy of singularity analysis. As shown by
Proposition

5
, this case is essentially disjoint from that of rational functions.
\Delta
-analytic
functions have a simpler singularity structure, in the sense that they always have a unique, fixed dominant singularity reachable by a cycle of product form. This allows us to study their coefficients in the
\bm{\theta}
-diagonal limits for general
\bm{\theta}\in\real_{>0}^{d}
, while using mostly elementary tools from univariate complex analysis. We will discuss more the relation between our case and the case of rational functions in
Section

4
. For more background on ACSV, we refer to the historical accounts in
[
9
, Chapter 1]
and
[
8
, Section 1.2.2]
.
