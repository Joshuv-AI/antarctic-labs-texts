Denoising-based Turbo Message Passing for Compressed Video Background Subtraction
Zhipeng Xue, Xiaojun Yuan, Yang Yang

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the compressed video background subtraction problem that separates the background and foreground of a video from its compressed measurements. The background of a video usually lies in a low dimensional space and the foreground is usually sparse. More importantly, each video frame is a natural image that has textural patterns. By exploiting these properties, we develop a message passing algorithm termed offline denoising-based turbo message passing (DTMP). We show that these structural properties can be efficiently handled by the existing denoising techniques under the turbo message passing framework. We further extend the DTMP algorithm to the online scenario where the video data is collected in an online manner. The extension is based on the similarity/continuity between adjacent video frames. We adopt the optical flow method to refine the estimation of the foreground. We also adopt the sliding window based background estimation to reduce complexity. By exploiting the Gaussianity of messages, we develop the state evolution to characterize the per-iteration performance of offline and online DTMP. Comparing to the existing algorithms, DTMP can work at much lower compression rates, and can subtract the background successfully with a lower mean squared error and better visual quality for both offline and online compressed video background subtraction.

Introduction

Video background subtraction (VBS) is important for many applications including video surveillance, object detection, visual hull computation, hunman-machine interaction, background substitution, gesture recognition, etc.

fuzzy background modeling is introduced that models the background using fuzzy running average
[

, type-2 fuzzy mixture of Gaussian
[

, Sugeno integral
[

or Choquet integral
[
14

. Recently, deep learning based approaches have been extensive studied
[

[

,

,

,
10

provides a robust model for separating video background and foreground. A video modeled as a data matrix can be decomposed as an addition of two component matrices, namely the background and the foreground. The background is modeled by a low-rank subspace since the background is usually static or changes slowly over time, while the moving foreground is modeled as a sparse matrix. Besides, the enormous success of information retrieval methods such as compressive sensing
[
19
]
and low-rank matrix completion
[
20
]
shows that structured data can be recovered from highly incomplete measurements. This inspires us to study the compressed VBS problem, i.e., to separate the background and the foreground of a video based on its compressed measurements.

The compressed VBS problem can be formulated as a compressed RPCA problem. As a generalization of the RPCA problem, compressed RPCA
[
21
]
aims to recover a low-rank matrix and a sparse matrix from the compressed measurements of their sum. Several algorithms for compressed RPCA have been proposed recently
[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. In
[
21
]
, a variant of the principal component pursuit (PCP) method is proposed for noiseless compressed RPCA. In
[
22
]
, a greedy algorithm is proposed to iteratively estimate the low-rank component and the sparse component. In
[
23
]
, a stable PCP method is proposed to handle the measurement noise. In
[
24
]
a message passing based algorithm is proposed to achieve low-complexity recovery with fewer measurements.

For real applications, video data are mostly delivered in the form of video streams. This leads to a more challenging problem of online compressed VBS, i.e., to separate the background and the foreground of a video whenever a compressed data frame arrives. In
[
25
]
, the authors proposed an online algorithm by taking the similarities of the foreground frames into consideration. In
[
26
]
, the authors proposed an online algorithm that leverages the information from the previously separated foreground frames by using optical flow.

In the above mentioned approaches, the compressed VBS problem is formulated as an optimization problem to induce the low-rankness of the background by using the nuclear norm and the sparsity of the foreground by using the
l_{1}
-norm. However, besides low-rankness and sparsity, video frames possess far richer local and global structural properties (such as textures, edges, etc.) since each frame is a natural image. These image structures, if not appropriately exploited, may cause significant performance loss in solving the compressed VBS problem.

In this paper, we incorporate the image structural information into the formulation of the offline and online compressed VBS problems. We first design a message passing based algorithm termed offline denoising based turbo message passing (DTMP) for the offline compressed VBS problem. More specifically, we establish a probability model for the offline compressed VBS problem by considering the image structure information in addition to the low-rankness of the background and the sparsity of the foreground. Based on that, we develop a factor graph representation of the offline compressed VBS problem, and design the message passing algorithm by basically following the sum-product rule. To reduce computational complexity, appropriate approximations are introduced based on the turbo message passing principle
[
27
]
. We further extend the DTMP algorithm to the online scenario. We use the sliding-window based background estimation to reduce the computation complexity, and exploit the continuity of the foreground frames by using the optical flow method. We characterize the performance of both the offline and online DTMP algorithms using two scalar functions termed state evolution. Simulation on real video datasets demonstrates that the offline and online DTMP achieve significant performance gains compared to their counterpart algorithms in terms of both visual quality and normalized mean-square error.

In our prior work
[
24
]
, we proposed to solve the compressed RPCA problem by using the turbo message passing (TMP) framework, where low-rank and sparsity denoisers are adopted for the estimation of the low-rank background matrix and the sparse foreground matrix. The TMP algorithm can be applied to the offline compressed VBS problem. Compared with
[
24
]
, the work in this paper has the following novelties and contributions.

•

We introduce appropriate approximations to the joint probability density function by taking into account the sparsity of the foreground, the low-rankness of the background, and more importantly the image structural information, based on which a factor graph of the offline compressed VBS problem is constructed.

•

Based on the factor graph, we develop the offline DTMP algorithm by using the sum-product rule and Gaussian message approximations. Compared to the TMP algorithm in
[
24
]
, the main difference of the DTMP algorithm is the inclusion of an image denoiser to handle the image structural information. To ensure good performance, we carefully design the input of the image denoiser (by dropping a non-Gaussian input component), based on which the extrinsic denoising principle developed in
[
27
]
can be applied.

•

We further consider the online scenario and develop the online DTMP algorithm. In specific, we employ a sliding-window based background estimation approach to reduce the computation complexity, and exploit the continuity of the foreground frames by using the optical flow method.

•

We characterize the behavior of both the offline and online DTMP algorithms by using the state evolution. We show that the performances of the DTMP algorithms can be accurately predicted by the state evolution.

II
Related Work

A large amount of work in the field of video background subtraction has been published in the literature. In this section, we give a brief review of the developments in this field from three aspects.

II-A

Video Background Subtraction Methods

Background subtraction methods can be mainly classified into the following categories:

•

Conventional background subtraction methods
use the average
[
3
]
, or the median
[
4
]
, or the histogram over time
[
5
]
to represent the video background. These methods can be easily implemented. However, modeling background with a single image requires a fixed background without noise and artifacts, which makes the performance of these methods not robust to real applications.

•

Statistical background subtraction methods
basicaly model the background pixels by using a probability density function (PDF) and learn the PDF from the video frames. The single-Gaussian method
[
6
]
assumes that the intensity values of a pixel over time can be modeled by a single Gaussian distribution. To handle dynamic backgrounds, a mixture-of-Gaussian method
[
7
]
is proposed. However, a fast varying background cannot be modeled accurately by a few Gaussians. To solve this problem, the kernel density estimation (KDE) method
[
8
]
was proposed. The KDE method is time-consuming which limits its application. Recently, principal component analysis based subspace learning
[
9
,
10
]
are widely used to construct a background model and RPCA based methods
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
10
]
provide a robust model for video background and foreground seperation. Other methods such as support vector based methods, and subspace learning methods also fall into this category.

•

Fuzzy background subtraction methods
use fuzzy running average
[
11
]
or type-2 fuzzy mixture of Gaussian
[
12
]
to model the video background. The forground is detcted using the Sugeno integral
[
13
]
or Choquet integral
[
14
]
.

•

Neural network background subtraction methods
model the background as the weights of a neural network which can be trained by using training video frames. The network is trained to classify each pixel of the input frame as background or foreground
[
15
]
. In
[
28
]
, a neural network which forms an unsupervised Bayesian classifier for background modeling and foreground detection is proposed. In
[
29
]
, a multivalued discrete neural network is used to detect and correct the deficiencies and errors of the Mixture of Gaussian algorithm. In
[
30
]
, an unsupervised competitive neural network to represent the background is constructed based on adaptive neighborhoods.

•

Clustering based background subtraction methods
suppose that pixels in the input frame can be represented by clusters. The K-means based method
[
31
]
, the codebook based method
[
32
]
and the sequential clustering method
[
33
]
belongs to this category.

Interested readers may refer to
[
34
,
35
]
for a more detailed review of the video background subtraction methods.

II-C

Message Passing Based Video Background Subtraction Methods

Message passing based algorithms have been widely used in many areas including compressed sensing
[
43
]
, and low-rank matrix recovery
[
44
]
. Recently, the message passing approach has been extended for solving the compressed RPCA problem. In
[
44

[

The TMP algorithm can be applied to the offline compressed VBS problem considered in this paper, and will be used as a baseline for comparison.

III
Offline Denoising-Based Turbo Message Passing

III-A

Problem Formulation

Consider a video with a sequence of frames. Let
w
and
h
be the width and the height of a video frame, respectively. Each frame of a video can be represented by a vector of length
n_{1}=hw
. Denote by
\bm{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times 1}
the
i
-th frame of the video, by
\bm{s}_{i}
the foreground of the
i
-th frame, and by
\bm{l}_{i}
the background of the
i
-th frame. Denote by
n_{2}
the total number of frames. Let
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\cdots,\bm{x}_{n_{2}}]=\bm{L}+\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
be the video matrix, where
\bm{L}=[\bm{l}_{1},\bm{l}_{2},\cdots,\bm{l}_{n_{2}}]
is the background matrix, and
\bm{S}=[\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2},\cdots,\bm{s}_{n_{2}}]
is the foreground matrix.

A linear measurement vector of the video is given by

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{X})+\bm{n}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{L}+\bm{S})+\bm{n}

(1)

where
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is an independent Gaussian measurement noise with zero mean and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\bm{I}
, and
\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is a linear measurement operator. Then, the offline compressed VBS problem is defined as to recover the foreground component
\bm{S}
and the background component
\bm{L}
from the noisy observation
\bm{y}
. Here, “offline” means that the foreground and background separation is carried out after collecting all the
n_{2}
frames of the video.

Existing RPCA based approaches to solving the offline compressed VBS problem mostly exploit the following two properties of the video background and foreground: The background vectors
\{\bm{l}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
reside in a low dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
, i.e.,
\bm{L}
is a low-rank matrix; and the foreground vectors
\{\bm{s}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{2}}
are sparse, i.e.
\bm{S}
is a sparse matrix. However, these approaches do not take into account the fact that each frame
\bm{x}_{i}
is a natural image. The textural features of
\bm{x}_{i}
as an image can be exploited to enhance compressed VBS problem. In what follows, we aim to develop a novel message-passing algorithm for solving the compressed VBS problem by incorporating the textural features of each
\bm{x}_{i}
as an image.

To start with, suppose that the prior distribution of the low-rank component
\bm{L}
is
p(\bm{L})
and that the prior distribution of the sparse component
\bm{S}
is
p(\bm{S})
. Then, the joint probability density of
\bm{y}
,
\bm{X}
,
\bm{L}
, and
\bm{S}
is given by

\displaystyle p(\bm{y},\bm{X},\bm{L},\bm{S})=p(\bm{y}|\bm{X})p(\bm{X}|\bm{L},\bm{S})p(\bm{L})p(\bm{S}),

(2)

where
\bm{L}
and
\bm{S}
are assumed to be independent of each other. Then the offline compressed VBS problem can be expressed as a statistical inference problem:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{L}}=\mathrm{E}[\bm{L}|\bm{y}]\text{\ and\ }\hat{\bm{S}}=\mathrm{E}[\bm{S}|\bm{y}].

(3)

However, the conditional expectations in (
3
) are difficult to calculate since the prior distributions
p(\bm{L})
and
p(\bm{S})
are usually unavailable for real-world images and videos. Even if
p(\bm{L})
and
p(\bm{S})
are available, the evaluation of these conditional means is still computationally infeasible since high-dimensional integrals are involved. Message passing is a powerful low-complexity tool to provide near-optimal statistical inference. As inspired by the recent successful development of message passing algorithms for structured signal reconstruction problems
[
48
,
49
]
, we propose a message-passing based solution in the following subsections.

III-B

Factor Graph Representation

We first describe the factor graph representation of
p(\bm{y},\bm{X},\bm{L},\bm{S})
, based on which the message passing algorithm is developed. The factorization of
p(\bm{y},\bm{X},\bm{L},\bm{S})
in (
2
) cannot be used directly since the prior distributions
p(\bm{L})
and
p(\bm{S})
are difficult to acquire for real-world images and videos. To avoid this difficulty, we replace
p(\bm{L})
and
p(\bm{S})
by three separable constraints, namely,
f_{L}(\bm{L})
for
\bm{L}
being low-rank,
f_{S}(\bm{S})
for
\bm{S}
being sparse, and
f_{X}(\bm{X})
for
\bm{X}
being a collection of natural images. Then, by noting
p(\bm{y}|\bm{X})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{y};\mathcal{A}(\bm{X}),\sigma^{2}\bm{I})
and
p(\bm{X}|\bm{L},\bm{S})=\delta(\bm{X}-\bm{L}-\bm{S})
, we approximate the joint distribution of
\bm{y}
,
\bm{X}
,
\bm{L}
, and
\bm{S}
in (
2
) as

\displaystyle p(\bm{y},\bm{X},\bm{L},\bm{S})\approx

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{N}(\bm{y};\mathcal{A}(\bm{X}),\sigma^{2}\bm{I})\delta(\bm{X}-\bm{L}-\bm{S})

\displaystyle\times f_{X}(\bm{X})f_{L}(\bm{L})f_{S}(\bm{S})

(4)

where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta function. Note that the explicit expressions of
f_{L}(\bm{L})
,
f_{S}(\bm{S})
, and
f_{X}(\bm{X})
are not available in practice. We will discuss how to approximately evaluate the messages involving
f_{L}(\bm{L})
,
f_{S}(\bm{S})
, and
f_{X}(\bm{X})
in the next subsection.

Based on the factorization (

Derivation of Offline DTMP

\displaystyle m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j}),

where from the sum-product rule, the message from node

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\bm{X}_{/(i,j)}}p(\bm{y}|\bm{X})m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})}{m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})}

(6a)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{\bm{y}}(X_{i,j})}{m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})},\forall i,j,

(6b)

with

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{v_{\bm{y}}}-\frac{1}{v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}}\right)^{-1}.

(12b)

For the sake of low computational complexity, we usually choose
\mathcal{A}
as a partial orthogonal linear operator, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{A}^{T}(\bm{a}))=\bm{a}
for an arbitrary vector
\bm{a}
. Equivalently, the matrix form of
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the following property:

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{A}^{T}=\bm{I}.

(13)

Then

\displaystyle v_{\bm{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Tr}\left(v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}\bm{I}\!-\!v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}^{2}\bm{A}^{T}(v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{T}\!+\!\sigma^{2}\bm{I})^{-1}\bm{A}\right)}{n_{1}n_{2}}

(14a)

\displaystyle=v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}-\frac{m}{n_{1}n_{2}}\frac{v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}^{2}}{v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}+\sigma^{2}}.

(14b)

Combining (
11
)-(
14
), we obtain a simplified form of
\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
and
v_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
given by

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}+\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{m}\mathcal{A}^{T}(\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}))

(15a)

\displaystyle v_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{m}(v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}+\sigma^{2})-v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}.

(15b)

III-C
2
Message from variable node
\bm{X}
to factor node
f_{X}

Similarly to (
5
), define the message from variable node
\bm{X}
to factor node
f_{X}
by

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})

(16)

where the message
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})
is given by

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})=m_{\delta\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j}).

(17)

By combining (
16
) and (
17
), we have

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}m_{\delta\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})\prod_{i,j}m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})

(18a)

\displaystyle=m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X}).

(18b)

Recall from (
12
) that the mean of
m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
is the LMMSE estimator by treating
m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
as the prior. It is known that, based on the central limit theorem, the estimation error of the LMMSE estimator is approximately Gaussian, and hence
m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
can be approximated well as a Gaussian message.However, due to the lack of explicit expressions of
f_{L}(\cdot)
and
f_{S}(\cdot)
, it is generally difficult to determine the expression of
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
. From numerical simulations, we observe that
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
is empirically quite far from a Gaussian distribution. As such, it is difficult to evaluate
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
by combining
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
and
m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
as in (
18
). Instead, in the algorithm, we ignore
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
by letting

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})=m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X}).

(19)

Indeed, this treatment may lose some information in message update since the message from node
\delta
is ignored. However, this treatment facilitates the message update involved in factor node
f_{X}
. Particularly, when we ignore message
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
, the message passing from
f_{X}
to
\bm{X}
can be calculated using the turbo principle, as elaborated in what follows.

III-C
3
Message from factor node
f_{X}
to variable node
\bm{X}

Similarly to (
5
), define the message from factor node
f_{X}
to variable node
\bm{X}
by

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i,j}m_{f_{X}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})

(20)

where the sum-product rule gives

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\bm{X}_{/(i,j)}}f_{X}(\bm{X})m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})}{m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{f_{X}}(X_{i,j})}{m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})}

(21)

with

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}}(X_{i,j})=\int_{\bm{X}_{/(i,j)}}f_{X}(\bm{X})m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X}).

(22)

Further define

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}}(\bm{X})=\prod_{i,j}m_{f_{X}}(X_{i,j}).

(23)

Then, from (
20
)-(
23
), we have

\displaystyle m_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})=\frac{m_{f_{X}}(\bm{X})}{m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})}.

(24)

Recall that an explicit expression of
f_{X}(\bm{X})
that encodes the image structural information of frames of video matrix
\bm{X}
is unavailable. We next describe how to approximately evaluate the message in (
24
). Instead of calculating the integral in (
22
), we obtain the mean of message
m_{f_{X}}(\bm{X})
by using an image denoiser that handles the image structural information, such as the wavelet denoiser
[
51
]
, the sliding-window transform denoiser
[
52
]
, or the block matching and 3D filtering (BM3D) denoiser
[
53
]
.
Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{x},v)
an image denoiser that takes vector
\bm{x}
as a noisy input with noise power
v
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{X}(\bm{X},v)=[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{x}_{1},v),\cdots,\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{x}_{n_{2}},v)]
. Then, the estimated mean of
m_{f_{X}}(\bm{X})
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{f_{X}}=D_{X}(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}),

(25)

where
\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}
and
v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}
are respectively the mean and variance of message
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
. From (
12
a), the mean of message
m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
is a linear combination of the mean matrices of messages
m_{\bm{y}}(\bm{X})
and
m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
. Similarly, we construct
\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
as a linear combination of the mean matrices of messages
m_{f_{X}}
and
m_{\bm{X}\rightarrow f_{X}}
:

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}=c_{X}(\mathcal{D}_{X}(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}})\!-\!\alpha_{X}\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}})

(26)

where
\alpha_{X}
and
c_{X}
are combination coefficients to be determined. The variance of message
m_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
can be estimated by following
[
24
, Eq. 20]
as

\displaystyle v_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\left\|\bm{y}-\mathcal{A}\left(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}\right)\right\|_{F}^{2}-\sigma^{2}.

(27)

We next determine the coefficients in (
26
). Note that
m_{f_{X}}(X_{i,j})
and
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})
can be respectively regarded as the posterior and prior messages of node
X_{i,j}
in the processing of the constraint
f_{X}
. Then, (
21
) can be interpreted as the calculation of extrinsic message
m_{f_{X}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})
by excluding the prior message from the posterior message. In other words, the extrinsic message
m_{f_{X}\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})
is independent of the prior message
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})
(since the latter does not appear in the calculation of the former according to the sum-product rule). Independence implies uncorrelatedness, yielding

\displaystyle\left<\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}-\bm{X},\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}-\bm{X}\right>=0,

(28)

where
\left<\bm{X}_{1},\bm{X}_{2}\right>=\text{trace}(\bm{X}_{1}^{T}\bm{X}_{2})
. Further, we require that the output MSE is minimized. Then,
\alpha_{x}
and
c_{X}
can be determined by solving

\displaystyle\minimize_{\alpha_{X},c_{X}}

\displaystyle\|\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}-\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}

(29a)

\displaystyle\st

\displaystyle\left<\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}-\bm{X},\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}-\bm{X}\right>=0

(29b)

where
\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}
is given by (
26
). By using Stein’s unbiased risk estimate and Stein’s lemma, it can be shown by following the approach of
[
27
]
that the two coefficients in (
26
) are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{div}(\mathcal{D}_{X}(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}))}{n}

(30a)

\displaystyle c_{X}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left<\mathcal{D}_{X}(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}})-\alpha_{X}\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}\right>}{\|\mathcal{D}_{X}(\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}},v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}})-\alpha_{X}\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}\|_{2}^{2}}

(30b)

where
\text{div}(\cdot)
denotes the divergence. We note that a prerequisite to invoke Stein’s lemma is that the input of the denoiser
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
is a Gaussian message, or more specifically, the input mean
\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}
can be modelled as
\bm{X}_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}=\bm{X}+\bm{W}
, where
\bm{W}
is an additive Gaussian noise matrix with the elements drawn from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,v_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}})
. From (
19
) and the discussions therein, the Gaussianity of message
m_{f_{X}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
in (
19
) is ensured by the LMMSE estimation involved in the calculation of message
m_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
.

4
Messages between variable node
\bm{X}
and factor node
\delta

The message from
\bm{X}
to
\delta
is given by

\displaystyle m_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})=m_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})m_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X}).

(31)

Following
[
49
, Eq. 11]
, the mean and variance of message
m_{\delta\rightarrow X}(\bm{X})
are respectively given by

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{X}}\left(\frac{\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}{v_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}+\frac{\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}{v_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}\right)

(32a)

\displaystyle v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{v_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}+\frac{1}{v_{\bm{y}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}\right)^{-1}.

(32b)

The message in the opposite direction is given by

\displaystyle m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})=\prod_{i,j}m_{\delta\rightarrow X_{i,j}}(X_{i,j})

(33)

where

\displaystyle m_{\delta\!\rightarrow\!X_{i,j}}(\!X_{i,j}\!)\!=\!\!\!\int_{L_{i,j},S_{i,j}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\delta(X_{i,j}\!-\!L_{i,j}\!-\!S_{i,j})m_{\delta\!\leftarrow\!L_{i,j}}\!(L_{i,j})m_{\delta\!\leftarrow\!S_{i,j}}\!(S_{i,j}).

(34)

Denote by
\bm{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}
and
\bm{S}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{S}}
the means of messages
m_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})
and
m_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{S}}(\bm{S})
respectively, and by
v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}
and
v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{S}}
the variances of messages
m_{\bm{L}\rightarrow\delta}(\bm{L})
and
m_{\bm{S}\rightarrow\delta}(\bm{S})
respectively. Then, the mean and variance of message
m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
are respectively given by

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=\bm{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}+\bm{S}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{S}}

(35a)

\displaystyle v_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}+v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{S}}.

(35b)

), the mean and variance of

III-C
7
Messages between variable node
\bm{L}
and factor node
f_{L}

From the sum-product rule, the message from
\bm{L}
to
f_{L}(\bm{L})
is given by

\displaystyle m_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})=m_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L}),

(42)

and the message from
f_{L}(\bm{L})
to
\bm{L}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{f_{L}\rightarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})=\prod_{i,j}m_{f_{L}\rightarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})

(43)

where

\displaystyle m_{f_{L}\rightarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\bm{L}_{/(i,j)}}m_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})f_{L}(\bm{L})}{m_{f_{L}\leftarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})}

(44a)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{f_{L}}(L_{i,j})}{m_{f_{L}\leftarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})}.

(44b)

Note that
m_{f_{L}\rightarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})
in (
44
) is difficult to determine since
f_{L}(\bm{L})
is not available. We next calculate the mean and variance of
m_{f_{L}\rightarrow L_{i,j}}(L_{i,j})
by mimicking the approach to the approximation of
m_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}(\bm{X})
.

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
the denoiser for low-rank matrix estimation. When the rank of
\bm{L}
is not available,
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be chosen as the singular value soft thresholding (SVST) denosier or the singular value hard thresholding (SVHT) denoiser
[
54
]
; when the rank of
\bm{L}
is known,
\mathcal{D}_{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
can be chosen as the best-rank-
r
denoiser
[
55
]
. The estimated mean of message
m_{f_{L}\rightarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{f_{L}\rightarrow\bm{L}}

\displaystyle=c_{L}(\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})-\alpha_{L}\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})

(45)

where coefficients
c_{L}
and
\alpha_{L}
are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{div}(\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}))}{n},

(46a)

\displaystyle c_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left<\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})-\alpha_{L}\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\right>}{\|\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})-\alpha_{L}\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\|_{2}^{2}}.

(46b)

When
\bm{D}_{L}(\cdot,\cdot)
is chosen as the best-rank-
r
denoiser, the variance of message
m_{f_{L}\rightarrow\bm{L}}(\bm{L})
can be estimated by
[
47
, Eq. 54(a)]

\displaystyle v_{f_{L}\rightarrow\bm{L}}=v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\left(\left(1-\frac{r}{n_{1}}\left(1+\frac{k}{n_{1}}\right)\right)\frac{1}{(1-\alpha_{L})^{2}}-1\right).

(47)

Alternatively, the variance can be estimated by
[
47
, Eq. 54(c)]

\displaystyle v_{f_{L}\!\rightarrow\!\bm{L}}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\left<\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})-\alpha_{L}\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\right>^{2}}{n\|\mathcal{D}_{L}(\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}},v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}})-\alpha_{L}\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\|_{F}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\|\bm{L}_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}\|_{F}^{2}}{n}-v_{f_{L}\leftarrow\bm{L}}.

(48)

(50b)

Note that
m_{f_{S}\rightarrow S_{i,j}}(S_{i,j})

is not available. Following the treatment for the low-rank denoiser described above, we calculate the mean and variance of

Denote by
\mathcal{D}_{S}(\cdot,\cdot)

]

. The estimated mean of message

where

and

(52b)

\mathcal{D}_{S}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the variance of message
m_{f_{S}\rightarrow\bm{S}}(\bm{S})
can be estimated by
[

\displaystyle v_{f_{S}\rightarrow\bm{S}}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\|\bm{y}\!-\!\mathcal{A}(\bm{S}_{f_{S}\rightarrow\bm{S}}\!+\!\bm{L}_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}))\|_{2}^{2}\!-\!v_{\delta\leftarrow\bm{L}}\!-\!\sigma^{2}.

\bm{X}
to factor node
\bm{y}

From the sum-product rule, we have

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}

\displaystyle=v_{\bm{y}\leftarrow\bm{X}}\left(\frac{\bm{X}_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}{v_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}+\frac{\bm{X}_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}}{v_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{v_{f_{X}\rightarrow\bm{X}}}+\frac{1}{v_{\delta\rightarrow\bm{X}}}\right)^{-1}

). In specific, the approximation of

III-D

Overall Algorithm

Based on the discussions in the preceding subsections, we summarize the offline denoising-based turbo message passing (DTMP) algorithm in Algorithm
1
. Note that a partial orthogonal linear operator
\mathcal{A}
is employed in Algorithm
1
. Specifically, Lines 2-3 of Algorithm

IV
Online Denoising-Based Turbo Message Passing

IV-A

Problem Formulation

As discussed in the preceding section, the complexity of the offline DTMP algorithm is linear in the total number of video frames. This complexity is not affordable for online processing of video data, where real-time foreground background separation is required. Specifically, the online version of the compressed VBS problem is defined as to recover the background component
\bm{l}_{t}
and foreground component
\bm{s}_{t}
of the current frame
\bm{x}_{t}
given the current compressed measurement
\bm{y}_{t}
and the separation results from the previous frames:
\bm{S}^{pre}_{t}=[\hat{\bm{s}}_{t-k_{S}},\cdots,\hat{\bm{s}}_{t-1}]
and
\bm{L}^{pre}_{t}=[\hat{\bm{l}}_{t-k_{L}},\cdots,\hat{\bm{l}}_{t-1}]
, where
k_{S}
and
k_{L}
are respectively the window sizes of the recovered foregrounds and backgrounds, and
\hat{\bm{s}}_{i}
and
\hat{\bm{l}}_{i}
are respectively the recovered foreground and background of the
i
-th frame. In addition, each
\bm{y}_{i}
is modelled by

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})+\bm{n}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{l}_{i}+\bm{s}_{i})+\bm{n}_{i},i=1,\cdots,t

(57)

where
\bm{n}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is the Gaussian measurement noise with mean zero and covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\bm{I}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{hw\times 1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m\times 1}
is a linear measurement operator applied to the
i
-th frame.

We aim to extend the offline DTMP algorithm to the online scenario. There are three issues to be addressed in the extension:

1.

First, in the online scenario, the linear measurement is conducted in a frame-by-frame manner rather than after collecting all the frames. That is, the linear estimation constraints in (
1
) need to be decoupled as in (
57
).

2.

Second, in the offline scenario, the background frames together are treated as a low-rank matrix, and the singular value decomposition (SVD) is applied in the estimation of the low-rank matrix. However, in the online case, the video frames arrive in a one-by-one manner. As such, it will be very time-consuming if the SVD operation is applied to the whole set of the collected frames whenever a frame arrives.

3.

Third, in offline DTMP, each foreground frame is estimated separately, and the continuity of the foregrounds in adjacent frames is not considered in the algorithm design. Such continuity, if appropriately exploited, can improve the performance of VBS.

To address the above issues, we start with the probability model of the online problem in what follows.

IV-B

Factor Graph Representation

For the online VBS problem, the joint probability density of
\bm{y}_{t}
,
\bm{x}_{t}
,
\bm{l}_{t}
, and
\bm{s}_{t}
conditioned on
\bm{L}^{pre}_{t}
and
\bm{S}^{pre}_{t}
is given by

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{t},\bm{x}_{t},\bm{l}_{t},\bm{s}_{t}|\bm{L}^{pre}_{t},\bm{S}^{pre}_{t})=

\displaystyle\,\,p(\bm{y}|\bm{x}_{t})p(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{l}_{t},\bm{s}_{t})

\displaystyle\times p(\bm{l}_{t}|\bm{L}^{pre}_{t})p(\bm{s}_{t}|\bm{S}^{pre}_{t})

(58)

where
p(\bm{l}_{t}|\bm{L}^{pre}_{t})
and
p(\bm{s}_{t}|\bm{S}^{pre}_{t})
are respectively the probability densities of the background and the foreground of the
t
-th frame conditioned on the previously recovered results, and

\displaystyle p(\bm{y}_{t}|\bm{x}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}_{t};\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}_{t}),\sigma^{2}\bm{I})

(59a)

\displaystyle p(\bm{x}_{t}|\bm{s}_{t},\bm{l}_{t})

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{x}_{t}-\bm{s}_{t}-\bm{l}_{t}).

(59b)

The conditional probability densities
p(\bm{l}_{t}|\bm{L}^{pre}_{t})
and
p(\bm{s}_{t}|\bm{S}^{pre}_{t})
are difficult to acquire in practice. Similarly to the approach in (
4

\displaystyle\ p(\bm{y}_{t},\bm{x}_{t},\bm{l}_{t},\bm{s}_{t}|\bm{L}^{pre}_{t},\bm{S}^{pre}_{t})\approx

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{N}(\bm{y}_{t};\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}_{t}),\sigma^{2}\bm{I})\delta(\bm{x}_{t}\!-\!\bm{l}_{t}\!-\!\bm{s}_{t})

\displaystyle\times\!f_{x}(\bm{x}_{t})f_{l}(\bm{l}_{t};\bm{L}^{pre}_{t})f_{s}(\bm{s}_{t};\bm{S}^{pre}_{t}),

(60)

where
f_{x}(\bm{x}_{t})
represents the constraint of
\bm{x}_{t}
as a natural image,
f_{l}(\bm{l}_{t};\bm{L}_{t}^{pre})
represents the correlation between
\bm{l}_{t}
and the previous backgrounds
\bm{L}_{t}^{pre}
, and
f_{s}(\bm{s}_{t};\bm{S}_{t}^{pre})
represents the correlation between
\bm{s}_{t}
and the previous foregrounds
\bm{S}_{t}^{pre}
.

The factor graph of the joint probability distribution in (

IV-D

Complexity Analysis

The computational complexity of the online DTMP algorithm are dominated by the operations in Lines 2, 4, 10 and 14. The computational complexity of Line 2 is
\mathcal{O}(mn_{1})
for a general linear opearator
\mathcal{A}
. For a partial orthogonal DCT operator, the complexity can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(n_{1}\log(n_{1}))
. The complexity of Line 4 concentrates on the processing of image denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{x}
which is usually linear to the size of input image. The complexity of the operation in Line 10 mainly concentrates on the step of calculation of
\mathcal{D}_{l}(\bm{l}_{f_{l}\leftarrow\bm{l}},v_{f_{l}\leftarrow\bm{l}})
which involves the computation of the truncated SVD of
[\bm{L}_{t}^{pre};\hat{\bm{l}}_{t}]
with complexity
\mathcal{O}(rn_{1}k_{L})
(or using incincremental SVD which has
\mathcal{O}(rn_{1}k_{L})
complexity). The complexity of the operation in Line 14 mainly concentrates on the calculation of
\mathcal{D}_{s}(\bm{s}_{f_{s}\leftarrow\bm{s}},v_{f_{s}\leftarrow\bm{s}})
. When the SURE-LET denoiser with the kernel given by
[
27
, Eqs. 28-30]
is chosen, the complexity is
\mathcal{O}(n_{1})
. Thus, the complexity of the online DTMP algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(rn_{1}k_{L})+\mathcal{O}(n_{1}\log(n_{1}))
flops when a partial orthogonal DCT operator is adopted.

V-B

State Evolution of Online DTMP

Different from the offline DTMP algorithm, in the online DTMP algorithm, the previous recovered backgrounds and foregrounds are available when we process the current frame which makes it difficult to track the performance of online DTMP on a given frame since the recovery process depends on previous recovery results. To track the performance of online-DTMP, we assume that for the current frame, the previous recovery results are known and the information provided to the current frame is fixed. Based on the assumption, the SE of online-DTMP can be established which has a similar form to the SE of given as follows:

\displaystyle\tau_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}(v_{t})=\frac{n_{1}}{m}(v_{t}+\sigma^{2})-v_{t}

(69a)

\displaystyle v_{t+1}

\displaystyle=g_{t}(\tau_{t})=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathrm{E}\left[\|\mathcal{D}_{r}(\bm{x}_{t}+\sqrt{\tau_{t}}\bm{n};\bm{L}_{t}^{pre},\bm{S}_{t}^{pre})-\bm{x}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(69b)

where
\mathcal{D}_{r}
is the right nonlinear part of the online DTMP algorithm. Numerical results will be presented in the next section to verify the effectiveness of the developed state evolution.

VI-A

Simulation Settings

The offline and online DTMP algorithms can be applied to compressed VBS with various linear operators such as Gaussian random linear operator and partial orthogonal linear operator. In practice, a general random linear operator may impose high storage and computation requirements on video implementations. To reduce the computational complexity and storage requirement, we choose the partial discrete cosine transform (DCT) operator in the experiments since there is no need to store the DCT operator in implementation and the fast DCT algorithm can be adopted in the computation. We generate the partial DCT operator

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\bm{P}\bm{D}

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a denoising-based message passing framework termed DTMP for compressed video background and foreground separation in both offline and online measurement modes. DTMP exploits the textual feature of a natural image and the continuity of the foreground in a video to improve the background and foreground separation performance. We also developed the state evolution to accurately characterize the behavior of the proposed algorithms. Numerical results demonstrate that DTMP can separate the background and foreground of the compressed video with a much higher resulting PSNR and exhibits a substantially better visual quality compared to the counterpart algorithms. The work in this paper can be potentially improved in a number of aspects. For example, from Fig.
6

7
,
9
, we see that the foreground of an image usually exhibits block sparsity, i.e. the non-zeros appear in clusters. This block sparsity, however, has not been appropriately exploited in our proposed algorithms. In addition, some key parameters of the proposed algorithms, such as the rank of the matrix
\bm{L}
and the noise level
\sigma^{2}
, are set empirically in simulations. These parameters can be learned automatically by using data-driven machine learning techniques. We will explore these possibilities in our future work.
