eXtended Hybridizable Discontinous Galerkin (X-HDG) Method for Linear Convection-Diffusion Equations on Unfitted Domains Journal: arXiv
Haroon Ahmad, Ceren Gürkan

Abstract
In this work, we propose a novel strategy for the numerical solution of linear convection diffusion equation (CDE) over unfitted domains. In the proposed numerical scheme, strategies from high order Hybridized Discontinuous Galerkin method and eXtended Finite Element method is combined with the level set definition of the boundaries. The proposed scheme and hence, is named as eXtended Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (XHDG) method. In this regard, the Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method is eXtended to the unfitted domains; i.e, the computational mesh does not need to fit to the domain boundary; instead, the boundary is defined by a level set function and cuts through the background mesh arbitrarily. The original unknown structure of HDG and its hybrid nature ensuring the local conservation of fluxes is kept, while developing a modified bilinear form for the elements cut by the boundary. At every cut element, an auxiliary nodal trace variable on the boundary is introduced, which is eliminated afterwards while imposing the boundary conditions. Both stationary and time dependent CDEs are studied over a range of flow regimes from diffusion to convection dominated; using high order ( p ≤ 4 ) (p\leq 4) XHDG through benchmark numerical examples over arbitrary unfitted domains. Results proved that XHDG inherits optimal ( p + 1 ) (p+1) and super ( p + 2 ) (p+2) convergence properties of HDG while removing the fitting mesh restriction.



1
Introduction

Linear convection diffusion (CD) equation can be of interest for all physical phenomena that involves a passive scalar being transported with a flowing medium. This can be interpreted as pollutant transport in a river or plumes carried away after a volcanic eruption. Numerical solution of convection diffusion equation and hence is of interest for scientists and engineers. Because of their discontinuous nature, convection dominated convection-diffusion problems; when numerically analyzed using classical continuous Galerkin (CG) methods, shows very poor stability properties and the numerical solution is often polluted by non-physical oscillations. For this reason CG methods, when used to solve convection dominated flow problems, often used together with a stabilization strategy. Among those stabilization strategies, the streamline upwind Petrov–Galerkin (SUPG) method
[
27
]
, residual-free bubble method
[
4
]
or adaptive mesh refinement techniques
[
16
]
are commonly used. For a through review on CDEs and their solution with stabilized continuous Galerkin methods the reader is referred to
[
32
,
36
]
.

Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods on the other hand; are seen to be a more suitable alternative to solve CDEs especially because of their favorable stability properties. The earliest work in DG front is presented in
[
35
]
for neutron transport and the recent developments can be found in
[
13
]
. Error estimates, convergence and accuracy of various DG methods for pure diffusion
[
3
,
28
,
25
]
; for stationary CD
[
37
,
18
]
and for non stationary CD problems
[
17
,
26
,
19
]
are well understood in the community. Despite their advantageous stability properties, DG methods are often criticized because the number of degrees of freedom at their final system is more than its continuous counterpart for the same mesh and the same approximation degree.

Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) method
[
11
,
12
]
is proposed as a possible remedy to that chokepoint. HDG inherits all advantageous properties of standard DG methods such as local conservation, built in stabilization, suitability for adaptivity and parallel computation while outperforming them with its hybrid and super convergent nature. Its hybrid nature reduces the number of degrees of freedom at the final system, similar to static condensation in CG methods
[
20
]
. Moreover, HDG is based on a mixed formulation where both primal unknown and its derivative are approximated using same polynomial degree
p
and this brings a
p+1
convergence order in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
error norm not only for the primal unknown but also for its derivative. Consequently, a simple element by element post processing of the derivative leads to a super convergent approximation of order
p+2
for the primal unknown
[
14
]
. HDG method is used to solve linear convection-diffusion equations successfully over standard fitted meshes in
[
33
]
and on general polyhedral fitted meshes in
[
34
]
.

The novelty of the strategy proposed here lays on without losing the favorable stability properties; optimal and super convergence and hybrid nature of the HDG method into an
unfitted
setting; removing the mesh-fitting restriction and costs.

1.1
Earlier work

The essential precondition for any numerical analysis is a high quality mesh resolving all the geometric features of the domain, ensuring an accurate enough numerical solution. Regardless of increasing computer power and ever growing research on accurate mesh generation; for complex stationary domains, mesh generation is still a challenge. For the problems involving complex domains or non-stationary interfaces; the majority of the computational cost is originated from accurate mesh creation or re-meshing. Independent of the numerical technique used, whenever a fitted mesh is on the table, scientists and engineers had to cope with these meshing/remeshing costs. To soothe this problem, so called,
unfitted
discretization techniques are proposed. In an
unfitted
setting, the mesh does not need to fit to the domain boundary, instead, a simple, cheap and stationary background mesh is created and the boundary or interface of interest is defined -generally- by a level set function
[
38
]
so that it can
cut
through this background mesh. With unfitted discretizations, complex domains or non stationary interfaces can be studied avoiding the accurate fitted mesh creation or re-meshing costs. In the context of CG methods, several alternative unfitted/cut discretization techniques are proposed. Under the name of CutFEM
[
5
]
, elliptic interface problems
[
8
]
, fluid structure interaction type coupled problems
[
30
]
, Stokes equations
[
6
]
and Oseen equations
[
31
]
are treated successfully. Unfitted discretizations are also used for the numerical solution of differential equations defined on surfaces
[
7
]
or on surface-bulk coupled problems
[
23
]
.

Unfitted discretizations are as well adapted to DG framework to treat interface or evolving boundary problems, among many others
in
[
1
]
scalar elliptic equation, in
[
2
]
advection-diffusion equation, in
[
24
]
incompressible Navier Stokes equations and in
[
22
]
advection-reaction problem with a high order CutDG method is analyzed. For the solution of surface and coupled surface-bulk PDEs using unfitted DG methods, the reader is referred to
[
29
]
and the references therein.

Building upon the HDG method and extending it to an unfitted setting; the XHDG method was initially proposed to treat void boundaries in
[
21
]
and then implemented for the solution of elliptic bimaterial interface
[
10
]
and Stokes interface problems
[
9
]
, respectively.

Animated by the efficiency of HDG on one hand and the effectiveness of unfitted discretizations on the other, the goal of novel XHDG method is to outreach the already existing analysis done over CDEs over an unfitted DG setting. There is a limited number of research done on the solution of CDEs using unfitted DG methods and the work presented here aims to outperform previous analysis from various aspects. The unfitted DG analysis done on CDEs in
[
2
]
differs from the work presented here, since authors showed convergence only up to
p\leq 2
while using second order approximation functions
p=2
. This means, the scheme employed was only
p
order accurate when using
p
order approximation, moreover, it did not have hybridization. Being different from steady unfitted HDG analysis for advection-reaction problems in
[
15
]
and for steady Oseen equations in
[
39
]
; in this work with XHDG, no extrapolations or mesh-distance-to-boundary restrictions are at stake; leading to a more robust method, while studying both steady and time dependent problems. Furthermore, in both of the analysis, the authors showed convergence only up to
p\leq 3
. This work presents the novel XHDG method for the solution of CDEs, surpassing the previous work done in this field with its high order, unfitted, hybrid, robust and superconvergent nature with no mesh-distance limitations.

1.2
Contributions and outline of this paper

In this work, we extend the XHDG discretization for the solution of steady and time dependent linear convection diffusion equations. Together with the level set description of the boundary, novel XHDG discretization for linear CD equation is proposed. Extension to time dependent CDEs is as well presented, using Backward Euler scheme for time discretization. At the elements
cut
by the boundary, numerical integrations are handled using high order modified quadratures. Both diffusion and convection dominated regimes are studied while implementing two alternative built in flux stabilizations, namely, centered and upwind flux. When approximation functions of order
p
is used, optimal
p+1
convergence rate is reached both for primal unknown and its derivative; moreover, after an easy post process; super
p+2
convergence rate is reached for the primal unknown.

This paper is organized as follows. The details of the XHDG discretization is presented in section
2
. In subsection
2.1
, local XHDG discretization of the elements that are not cut by the boundary is shown. For the elements not cut by the boundary, the element-wise formulation is equivalent to the standard HDG formulation. In subsection
2.2
, the novel local weak form for the elements cut by the boundary when Neumann boundary conditions are imposed at the cut boundary is presented. The local problem is then assembled into the so called global problem in subsection
2.3
where the hybridization happens by means of creating the system matrix in terms of the unknown only defined at element faces. Four numerical examples to prove the accuracy and convergence of XHDG is presented in section
3
. For the first two examples the cut boundary is in simple circular shape; diffusion and convection dominated regimes examined respectively. For the third example a more complex "peanut" shaped cut boundary is studied. Lastly, the performance of XHDG for time dependent CDE is proved through the transport of a decaying Gaussian pulse. All numerical examples proved that XHDG reaches optimal and super convergence without computational mesh fitting to the domain boundary. This work is concluded with section
4
stating the conclusions, final remarks and future work.

where,

\overline{\Omega}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\texttt{n}_{\texttt{el}}}}\overline{K}_{i},\quad K_{i}\cap K_{j}=\emptyset;\quad\text{for $i\neq j$,}\quad\partial\Omega^{ext}\subset\partial\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{{\texttt{n}_{\texttt{el}}}}\overline{K}_{i}\right]

The convection diffusion equation given in (

In this subsection, the local XHDG formulation for an element

\hskip 105.00015pt\displaystyle\int_{\partial K_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widehat{u}v{\,dS}=\displaystyle\int_{K_{i}}vf{\,dV}\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(K_{i})\\

\begin{array}[]{rl}\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}\right]\mathbf{u}^{i}&+\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uq}\mathbf{q}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widehat{u}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}}\mathbf{\widetilde{u}}^{i}=\mathbf{f}_{u}^{\Omega_{i}}\\[5.0pt]
\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qu}\mathbf{u}^{i}&+\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qq}\mathbf{q}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widehat{u}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}}\mathbf{\widetilde{u}}^{i}=\mathbf{0}\\
\end{array}

(2.15)

similar to (
2.10
), but with three new matrices corresponding to integrals on the inner boundary
\mathcal{I}_{i}
, and the nodal values for the new trace variable,
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
. To be able to obtain the
local solver
similar to (
2.12
) we need to eliminate the new unknown variable
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
from (
2.15
). By imposing the Neumann boundary condition at the inner boundary
\mathcal{I}_{i}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
can be eliminated from (
2.15
).
The Neumann boundary condition at the inner boundary is stated as:

(\widehat{\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u}+\widehat{\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}})\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}=g_{N}\quad\text{on }\mathcal{I}_{i}

Replacing the numerical flux with the expression given in (
2.9
), the weak form of the Neumann condition can be written as: given
u\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\Omega_{i})
,
\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}\in[\mathcal{P}_{k}(\Omega_{i})]^{d}
, find
\widetilde{u}^{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{I}_{i})
such that

\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\widetilde{u}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widetilde{v}+\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widetilde{v}{\,dS}+\tau\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}\widetilde{v}\left(u-\widetilde{u}\right){\,dS}=\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}\widetilde{v}g_{N}{\,dS}\quad\forall\widetilde{v}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\mathcal{I}_{i})

(2.16)

Equation (
2.16
) can be written in matrix form as follows

\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}u}\mathbf{u}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}q}\mathbf{q}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}\widetilde{u}^{i}}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}=\mathbf{g_{N}},

(2.17)

Using (
2.17
) the new variable
\widetilde{u}^{i}
defined on inner boundary
\mathcal{I}
can be written in terms of
u
and
q
as follows

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}=\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}\mathbf{u}^{i}+\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}\mathbf{q}^{i}-\mathbf{t}^{i}.

(2.18)

where,

\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}=-[\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}\widetilde{u}^{i}}]^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}u},\quad\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}=-[\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}\widetilde{u}^{i}}]^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}q},\quad\mathbf{t}^{i}=-[\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{\widetilde{u}^{i}\widetilde{u}^{i}}]^{-1}\mathbf{g_{N}}.

If in the local problem (
2.15
)
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{i}
is replaced with the expression (
2.18
) the updated local problem in matrix form can be written as follows

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}\right]\mathbf{u}^{i}+\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uq}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}\right]\mathbf{q}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widehat{u}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}\\[5.0pt]
\hskip 242.13278pt=\mathbf{f}_{u}^{\Omega_{i}}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{t}^{i}\\[5.0pt]
\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}\right]\mathbf{u}^{i}+\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qq}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}\right]\mathbf{q}^{i}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widehat{u}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}=\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{t}^{i}\\
\end{array}

After eliminating the new variable
\widetilde{u}^{i}
from local problem using Neumann boundary condition; identical to (
2.12
),
local solver
for a cut element can be written as:

\mathbf{u}^{i}=\mathbf{U}^{K_{i}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}+\mathbf{f}^{K_{i}}_{U},\quad\mathbf{q}^{i}=\mathbf{Q}^{K_{i}}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}+\mathbf{f}^{K_{i}}_{Q},

(2.19)

with

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{U}^{K_{i}}\\[5.0pt]
\mathbf{Q}^{K_{i}}\end{array}\right]=-\mathbf{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widehat{u}}\\[5.0pt]
\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widehat{u}}\end{array}\right],\;\;\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{f}^{K_{i}}_{U}\\[5.0pt]
\mathbf{f}^{K_{i}}_{Q}\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{\mathbb{A}}^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{f}_{u}^{\Omega_{i}}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{t}^{i}\\[5.0pt]
\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{t}^{i}\end{array}\right]

(2.20)

and

\mathbf{\mathbb{A}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{uu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}\right]&\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{uq}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{u\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}\right]\\[5.0pt]
\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qu}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{u}\right]&\left[\mathbf{A}^{\Omega_{i}}_{qq}+\mathbf{A}^{\mathcal{I}_{i}}_{q\widetilde{u}^{i}}\mathbf{T}^{i}_{q}\right]\end{array}\right]

It is important to understand that the structure of the local solver is similar to the one for non-cut elements (
2.12
). In the local solver unknown variables
u
and
q
are once more expressed only in terms of the trace unknown
\widehat{u}
, which is the essence of hybrid discretization.

Remark 2.1

The main difference between unfitted XHDG discretization and fitted HDG discretization lays in the local problem for cut elements. To calculate the partial integrals over cut elements and cut faces, a high order modified quadrature is introduced. The details of this implementation can be found in
[
21
]
, section 4.

Remark 2.2

The implementation of Dirichlet boundary conditions on internal boundary is straightforward. Starting from the mixed strong form:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{\boldmath$\nabla\cdot$\unboldmath}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u+\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath})=f&\text{in }\Omega_{i}\\
\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}+\nu\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}u=0&\text{in }\Omega_{i}\\
u=u_{I}&\text{on }\mathcal{I}_{i}\\
u=\widehat{u}&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}\end{array}

And multiplying it with test functions, doing integration by parts, replacing the flux with the numerical flux
\widehat{\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u}+\text{\boldmath$\hat{q}$\unboldmath}:=\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}(\hat{u}+u_{I})+\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}+\tau(u-\hat{u})\text{\boldmath$\hat{n}$\unboldmath}+\tau(u-u_{I})\text{\boldmath$\hat{n}$\unboldmath}
and undoing integration by parts (only for first equation); one can obtain the weak form below for every cut element when Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed at the cut boundary.

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}v{\,dV}+\int_{\Omega_{i}}v\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}{\,dV}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}\tau(u-\widehat{u})v{\,dS}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widehat{u}v{\,dS}\\
+\displaystyle\int_{I_{i}}\tau uv{\,dS}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}vf{\,dV}-\displaystyle\int_{I_{i}}\tau u_{I}v{\,dS}-\int_{I_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})u_{I}v{\,dS}\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(K_{i})\\[10.00002pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\frac{\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}}{\nu}\cdot\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}{\,dV}-\int_{\Omega_{i}}u\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}{\,dV}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}\widehat{u}(\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}){\,dS}\\
\qquad=-\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}u_{I}(\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}){\,dS}\qquad\forall\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\in[\mathcal{P}_{k}(K_{i})]^{d}\end{array}

By close inspection, the reader can realize that the integral weak form given above for Dirichlet boundaries are quite similar to the one of Neumann boundaries; the only difference is that now, the integrals over cut boundary
\mathcal{I}
with unknown
\widetilde{u}
do appear at the right hand side of the equations with known nodal Dirichlet values
u_{I}
.

Remark 2.3

The built in stabilization of XHDG is inherited from HDG. The built in stabilization is embedded in the numerical flux definition (
2.9
). In
[
33
]
it is shown that the equations (
2.8
) and (
2.14
) together with the global problem have a unique solution if
\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\geq 0
and when stabilization parameter
\tau
satisfies the condition
\tau>\frac{1}{2}\hskip 2.84544pt(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})
.
The stabilization parameter
\tau
here is chosen following the strategy presented in
[
33
]
. There are two possible choices, one leading to centered and the other to upwind flux definition. First, lets define
\tau=:\tau_{d}+\tau_{c}
where
\tau_{d}
represents diffusive and
\tau_{c}
represents convective flux. When
\tau_{d}^{+}=\tau_{d}^{-}=\eta_{d}
and
\tau_{c}^{+}=\tau_{c}^{-}=\eta_{c}
with
\eta_{d}=\frac{\nu}{\textit{l}}
and
\eta_{c}=|\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}|
the flux scheme is named as centered where
l
denotes the diffusive length scale. In the centered flux definition we have a single-valued
\tau
, that is
\tau^{+}=\tau^{-}
. On the other hand, when we choose
\tau_{d}^{\pm}
and
\tau_{c}^{\pm}
as

(\tau_{d}^{\pm},\tau_{c}^{\pm})=(\eta_{d},\eta_{c})\frac{|\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}^{+}|\pm\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}^{+}}{2|\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}^{+}|}

the scheme leads to the upwind flux definition. In the numerical examples, both centered and upwind flux definitions are used.

2.3
Global problem

Element by element contributions both from standard and cut elements, leads to the local solver (
2.12
) and (
2.19
). The local solver defines the unknowns in the element,
u
and
q
, in terms of the trace unknown at element boundary,
\widehat{u}
.
Therefore, the problem can be reduced to determine the trace unknowns
\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{f}\}_{f=1}^{{\texttt{n}_{\texttt{fc}}}}
on the mesh skeleton
\Gamma
. To create the system matrix, element by element contributions are assembled using the so-called
global problem
. Global problem corresponds to the discretization of the conservativity of flux on
\Gamma
, stated in (
2.5
).

Replacing flux by the numerical flux (
2.9
), the weak form of the conservativity condition can be stated as: find
\widehat{u}\in\Lambda^{h}
such that
\widehat{u}=u_{D}
on
\partial\Omega^{ext}
and

\int_{\Gamma}\widehat{v}\llbracket\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}\rrbracket{\,dS}+2\int_{\Gamma}\widehat{v}\{\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}.\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}\}\widehat{u}{\,dS}+2\int_{\Gamma}\widehat{v}(\{\tau u\}-\{\tau\}\widehat{u}){\,dS}=0\quad\forall\widehat{v}\in\Lambda^{h},

where
\{\cdot\}
is the mean operator on the faces defined as,

\{\circledcirc\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\circledcirc_{L(f)}+\circledcirc_{R(f)}\right)\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{f}.

Discrete form of the conservativity condition leads to the following matrix system

\mathbf{A}^{f,L}_{\widehat{u}u}\mathbf{u}^{L(f)}+\mathbf{A}^{f,L}_{\widehat{u}q}\mathbf{q}^{L(f)}\;+\;\mathbf{A}^{f,R}_{\widehat{u}u}\mathbf{u}^{R(f)}+\mathbf{A}^{f,R}_{\widehat{u}q}\mathbf{q}^{R(f)}\;+\;\mathbf{A}^{f}_{\widehat{u}\widehat{u}}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{f}=0.

(2.21)

Elemental contributions from both left
\mathbf{u}^{L(f)}\text{, }\mathbf{q}^{L(f)}
and right
\mathbf{u}^{R(f)}\text{, }\mathbf{q}^{R(f)}
side of the element face are assembled into (
2.21
) using the local solver. In (
2.21
) when
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{q}
are replaced with local solver from standard (
2.12
) or from cut element (
2.19
), the final system then can be obtained only in terms of the trace variable
\mathbf{\Lambda}^{i}
.

XHDG implementation, being similar to HDG implementation; involves a loop over elements. For each element, the matrices and vectors of the local solver (
2.12
) and (
2.19
) are computed, and then those contributions are assembled into the system matrix using the global solver (
2.21
).
Once the system is assembled the Dirichlet boundary conditions on the outer boundary (
2.6
) (when applicable) are imposed and then the system can be solved. Since in the global system the only unknown variable is trace variable
\mathbf{\Lambda}
defined on mesh skeleton
\Gamma
; with the solution of this system, the nodal values of the trace variable is obtained. After the nodal trace values are obtained, with a simple put-back operation using the local solver, the elemental unknowns
\mathbf{u}^{i}
and
\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}^{i}
, can be computed.
It is important to note that in X-HDG implementation standard HDG structure is kept. The basic differences are the derivation of modified weak forms over cut elements, modified integral calculations at cut elements, the new unknowns defined at cut boundary
\widetilde{u}
and their elimination.

Remark 2.4

For the simplest XHDG implementation; all faces are assembled in the global problem even if they do not intersect the domain i.e. when they are inside the void part of the domain. The matrices assembled for the faces not intersecting the domain lead to null rows and columns in the global system. Those rows and columns are then removed from the system, reducing its size and obtaining a system with unique solution, together with imposing the Dirichlet boundary condition (
2.6
).

Remark 2.5

An element-by-element postprocessing can be performed to compute a superconvergent numerical solution from original numerical solution obtained after solving the global system. The superconvergence is one of the most distinguished properties of HDG, making it outperform other DG methods. This supercovergence is as well inherited by XHDG. Similar to standard HDG; the superconvergent solution can be computed in every element
K_{i}
to find
u^{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\Omega_{i})
such that:

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\nu\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}u^{*}\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}v{\,dV}=-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}v{\,dV}\quad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k+1}(\Omega_{i}),\\
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}u^{*}{\,dV}=\int_{\Omega_{i}}u{\,dV},\end{array}

with
\Omega_{i}=K_{i}
for standard elements, and
\Omega_{i}=K_{i}\cap\Omega
for cut elements. The solution of this element-by-element computation,
u^{*}
, converges with order
p+2
in the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm. See
[
11
,
14
]
for details and other possible computations of a superconvergent solution.

2.4
Extension to time dependent problems

The extension of steady XHDG formulation to unsteady problems is straightforward. Here, the unsteady XHDG formulation is presented for a cut element, the unsteady weak form for a standard element can be derived following the same steps. The mixed strong form for a Neumann cut element can be defined as:

\begin{array}[]{rl}\dfrac{du}{dt}+\text{\boldmath$\nabla\cdot$\unboldmath}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u+\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath})=f&\text{in }\Omega_{i}\\
\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}+\nu\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}u=0&\text{in }\Omega_{i}\\
(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u+\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath})\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}=g_{N}&\text{on }\mathcal{I}_{i}\\
u=\widehat{u}&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}\end{array}

when time dependent term is discretized using Backward Euler method:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\dfrac{du}{dt}v{\,dV}=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}u^{t}v{\,dV}-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}u^{t-1}v{\,dV}\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(\Omega_{i})

The local unsteady weak form for a Neumann cut element can be stated as follows with two additional terms coming from time discretization:

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}u^{t}v{\,dV}\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega_{i}}\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}u\cdot\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}v{\,dV}+\int_{\Omega_{i}}v\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}{\,dV}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}\tau(u-\widehat{u})v{\,dS}+\\
\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widehat{u}v{\,dS}+\displaystyle\int_{I_{i}}\tau(u-\widetilde{u})v{\,dS}+\int_{I_{i}}(\text{\boldmath$c$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath})\widetilde{u}v{\,dS}\\
\hskip 42.67912pt=\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\dfrac{1}{\Delta t}u^{t-1}v{\,dV}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}vf{\,dV}\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(K_{i})\\[10.00002pt]
\vskip 8.5359pt\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{i}}\frac{\text{\boldmath$q$\unboldmath}}{\nu}\cdot\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}{\,dV}-\int_{\Omega_{i}}u\text{\boldmath$\nabla$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}{\,dV}+\int_{\partial\Omega_{i}\backslash\mathcal{I}_{i}}\widehat{u}(\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}){\,dS}\\
+\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{I}_{i}}\widetilde{u}(\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\cdot\text{\boldmath$n$\unboldmath}){\,dS}=0\qquad\forall\text{\boldmath$w$\unboldmath}\in[\mathcal{P}_{k}(K_{i})]^{d}\end{array}

the local solver then can be obtained as explained in subsection
2.2
and the global problem can be formed as stated in subsection
2.3
.

3
Numerical tests

The performance of X-HDG method is tested over four numerical examples where the boundary is defined by a level set function, cutting through the background mesh. In the first example, square background geometry with circular void boundary is considered on diffusion domainated flow regime. In the second example, the geometry and the boundary kept the same while changing the flow regime to be convection dominated. For the third example, an arbitrary cut boundary in peanut shape is solved on convection dominated regime; to demonstrate the capability of XHDG to handle arbitrary boundary cuts. Last but not least, transport of a decaying Gaussian pulse is studied. All tests prove that removing the boundary-fitting-mesh restriction and using a cut mesh did not effect the optimal and super convergence or accuracy of the XHDG when compared to its mesh-fitting counterpart, HDG.

Remark 3.1

In Tables
3.1
,

3.2
,

3.3
and
3.4
the reader can notice that for a high approximation degree and fine mesh, although the optimal and super convergence is reached, there might be a jump in error value and hence, the convergence order is not shown. This is the main bottleneck of any unfitted discretization, namely, the ill conditioning because of so called bad-cut situations. Bad-cut situation occurs when the boundary cuts the element in the background mesh very close to its vertex or edge, leading to a very small contribution from that element in the global system. At the end, these small contributions lead to a global system matrix with a high condition number, effecting the error and convergence of the cut discretization scheme. It is not in the context of this work to propose a remedy to this ill conditioning problem, however, the reader is referred to
[
10
]
to a node-moving type quick-fix or to
[
22
]
to a ghost penalty type solution to this ill conditioning problem.

4
Conclusions and final remarks

We have presented novel XHDG formulation for the solution of steady and time dependent solution of convection diffusion equations over unfitted domains. With unfitted XHDG discretization; the background mesh is cheap and easy to create and the domain of interest is defined with a level set function such that it can cut through the background mesh arbitrarily. With this unfitted strategy, the advantageous properties of HDG method, such as local and built in stabilization, element by element fashion, reduced number of degrees of freedom, optimal and super convergence is kept while removing the mesh-fitting-to-domain-boundary restriction; saving substantial mesh adaptation costs.

In XHDG discretization, for the elements not cut by the domain boundary, standard HDG discretization is followed, while for cut elements a novel modified weak form is derived. Keeping the original unknown structure, to calculate the integrals over the cut elements and faces, a modified quadrature rule is used. A new unknown variable at the cut boundary is first introduced and then eliminated from the weak form imposing the boundary conditions. Extension to time dependent problems are as well presented, using first order Backward Euler time discretization scheme.

Being different than other unfitted HDG strategies, with XHDG; the unknown structure of HDG is kept, high order
p\leq 4
convergence is proven, unsteady problems are treated and no extensions from domain or limitations on extension distance needed to be discussed.

The XHDG discretization is tested over three steady and one unsteady problem where the cut boundary is either circular or in rather complex peanut shape. The convergence results of the numerical examples prove that while removing the mesh adaptation costs, XHDG shows optimal and super convergence for flow problems that is either diffusion or convection dominated.

Currently, XHDG formulation is being adapted for the solution of non-linear convection diffusion and Navier Stokes equations; together with a stabilization strategy to handle the ill conditioning issue at bad cut elements.
