The Carleman convexification method for Hamilton-Jacobi equations on the whole space
Huynh P.N. Le, Thuy T. Le, Loc H. Nguyen

Abstract
We propose a new globally convergent numerical method to solve Hamilton-Jacobi equations in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 . This method is named as the Carleman convexification method. By Carleman convexification, we mean that we use a Carleman weight function to convexify the conventional least squares mismatch functional. We will prove a new version of the convexification theorem guaranteeing that the mismatch functional involving the Carleman weight function is strictly convex and, therefore, has a unique minimizer. Moreover, a consequence of our convexification theorem guarantees that the minimizer of the Carleman weighted mismatch functional is an approximation of the viscosity solution we want to compute. Some numerical results in 1D and 2D will be presented.



Key words: numerical methods; Carleman estimate;
Hamilton-Jacobi equations; viscosity solutions; vanishing viscosity process.

35D40, 35F21

However, due to the nonlinearity of the Hamiltonian, the least squares cost functional is nonconvex and might have multiple local minima and ravines.
Hence, the methods based on optimization can provide reliable numerical solutions if good initial guesses of the true solutions are given.
The key point of the convexification method in this paper is to include in such least squares mismatch functionals some Carleman weight functions to make these functionals convex.

On the other hand, we especially draw the reader attention to

Although strong, these methods might not be applicable to solve (

1.2

\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
one can compute the restriction of the solution to (
1.2

Recently, we, in
[
23
,
32
]
, developed the convexification method to solve (1) the inverse scattering problems and (2) a general class of Hamilton-Jacobi equations in a bounded domain. The efficiency of the convexification method was rigorously proved. However, the version of the convexification method above requires both Neumann and Dirichlet boundary conditions of the solution, which are not always available. Therefore, in order to apply the convexification method in
[
23
,
32
]
without requesting the knowledge of the boundary conditions, we have to develop a new version.
The key of the success involves a new piece-wise Carleman estimate and a new mismatch functional with a suitable Carleman weight function.
Our method to solve (
1.2
) consists of two stages.
In stage 1, we apply a truncation technique to reduce the problem of solving (
1.2
) on the whole
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to the problem of computing viscosity of another Hamilton-Jacobi equation on a bounded domain. The boundary conditions of the new Hamilton-Jacobi equation are unknown but we can estimate them in term of the cut-off function.
Then, in stage 2, we minimize a mismatch functional with a special Carleman weight function involved, called the Carleman weighted mismatch functional.
The presence of the Carleman weight function is extremely important in the sense that it guarantees the strict convexity of the Carleman weighted mismatch functional. As a result, our Carleman weighted mismatch functional has a unique minimizer in any bounded set of the functional space under consideration.
We will apply a Carleman estimate to prove this theoretical result, called a convexification theorem.
Besides guaranteeing the strict convexity of the cost functional, the convexification theorem can be used to prove that the minimizer is an approximation of the desired viscosity solution.

2
A change of variable

Rather than computing the solution
u
to (
1.2
) on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we compute the restriction of
u
on an arbitrary bounded domain
G
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Without lost of generality, we assume that
G
is compactly contained inside the cube

\Omega=(-R,R)^{d}
for some
R>0.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a small number.
Let
\chi_{\delta}
be a cut off function in the class
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>c>0&{\bf x}\in G\\
\in(\delta,c)&{\bf x}\in\Omega\setminus G,\\
=\delta&{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

for some constant
c>0
.
Define

v({\bf x})=\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})u({\bf x})\quad\mbox{or equivalently}\quad u({\bf x})=\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}

(2.2)

for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since

\displaystyle\lambda u({\bf x})+H({\bf x},\nabla u({\bf x}))

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}}+H\left({\bf x},\nabla\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\left({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\right),

it follows from (
1.2
) that

\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\left({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\right)=0

(2.3)

for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Multiplying
\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})
to both sides of (
2.3
), we derive an equation for
v
, read as

F({\bf x},v({\bf x}),\nabla v({\bf x})):=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})\left[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\left({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\right)\right]=0

(2.4)

for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Remark 2.1
.

The presence of
\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})
in the the right hand side of (
2.4
) helps us remove the blow-up behavior of the term
\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
. In fact, by (
1.1
),

\displaystyle\left|\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})H\left({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})\left|\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\right|^{k}

\displaystyle=C|\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla v({\bf x})-v({\bf x})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})|^{k},

which is uniformly bounded provided that
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

This step is crucial in numerical computation.

We have the proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

The function
u
is a viscosity solution to (
1.2
) if and only if the function
v
is a viscosity solution to (
2.4
).

Proof.

We prove that if
u
is a viscosity subsolution to (
1.2
) then
v
is a viscosity solution to (
2.4
).
In fact, fix an arbitrary point
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\varphi
be a test function such that
v-\varphi
has a strict maximum at
{\bf x}_{0}
.
We have

v({\bf x})-\varphi({\bf x})-[v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0})]<0,\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf x}_{0}\}.

(2.5)

Define

\phi({\bf x})=\frac{\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.6)

Then,
\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
A simple algebra yields that for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})

\displaystyle\Big[\lambda u({\bf x})+H({\bf x},\nabla\phi({\bf x}))\Big]=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})\Big[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\Big({\bf x},\nabla\frac{\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})\Big[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\Big({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla\big(\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\big)-\big(\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\big)\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\Big)

\displaystyle=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x})\Big[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}+H\Big({\bf x},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\nabla(\varphi({\bf x}))-\big(\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\big)\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\Big)\Big].

In particular, when
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x}_{0})\Big[\lambda u({\bf x}_{0})

\displaystyle+H({\bf x}_{0},\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{0}))\Big]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x}_{0})\Big[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x}_{0})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})}+H\Big({\bf x}_{0},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})\nabla(\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))-\big(\varphi({\bf x}_{0})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\big)\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x}_{0})\Big[\lambda\frac{v({\bf x}_{0})}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})}+H\Big({\bf x}_{0},\frac{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})\nabla(\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))-v({\bf x}_{0})\nabla\chi_{\delta}({\bf x}_{0})}{\chi_{\delta}^{2}({\bf x})}\Big)\Big]

Hence, by (
2.4
),

F({\bf x}_{0},v({\bf x}_{0}),\nabla\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))=\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x}_{0})\Big[\lambda u({\bf x}_{0})+H({\bf x}_{0},\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{0}))\Big].

(2.7)

Due to (
2.2
) and (
2.6
), for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf x}_{0}\}
,

\displaystyle u({\bf x})-\phi({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{v({\bf x})-\big(\varphi({\bf x})+(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\big)}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}

\displaystyle=\frac{v({\bf x})-\varphi({\bf x})-(v({\bf x}_{0})-\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))}{\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})}<0.

It is obvious that
u({\bf x}_{0})-\phi({\bf x}_{0})=0.

Hence,
u-\phi
attains a strict maximum at
{\bf x}_{0}
.
Since
u
is a viscosity subsolution to (
1.2
),

\chi_{\delta}^{2k}({\bf x}_{0})\Big[\lambda u({\bf x}_{0})+H({\bf x}_{0},\nabla\phi({\bf x}_{0}))\Big]\leq 0.

(2.8)

Combining (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), we obtain

F({\bf x}_{0},v({\bf x}_{0}),\nabla\varphi({\bf x}_{0}))\leq 0.

Hence
v
is a viscosity subsolution to (
2.4
).
We can repeat the proof above to show that if
u
is a viscosity supersolution to (
1.2
) then
v
is a viscosity supersolution to (
2.4
). The reverse direction of Theorem
2.1
can be proved in the same manner.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

A direct consequence of Proposition
2.1
is that we can compute the viscosity solution
u^{*}
to (
1.2
) by finding the viscosity solution
v^{*}
to (
2.4
) and then setting
u^{*}=v^{*}/\chi_{\delta}
.
Although the formula
u^{*}=v^{*}/\chi_{\delta}
holds true for all
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this formula is reliable only in the domain
G
where
\chi_{\delta}>c>0
. Outside
G
, the function
\chi_{\delta}
is close to zero. In this case, the “artificial” error in computation, due to discretization with positive step size, the presence of the viscosity term and regularization term, is magnified.

It is well-known from the vanishing viscosity process that
v^{*}
can be approximated by the solution to

-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v_{\epsilon_{0}}+F({\bf x},v_{\epsilon_{0}},\nabla v_{\epsilon_{0}})=0\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2.9)

In computation, it is inconvenient to compute the function
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We only find
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
in the bounded domain
\Omega
on which
\chi_{\delta}>\delta.

In order to solve PDEs of the form (
2.9
) on a bounded domain, we have to approximate the boundary conditions.
By (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), for all
{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega
,

|v({\bf x})|=\delta|u({\bf x})|<C\delta

(2.10)

and

|\partial_{\nu}v({\bf x})|=|\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x})+u({\bf x})\partial_{\nu}\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})|=|\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x})|<C\delta.

(2.11)

Here, we have used the fact that
\partial_{\nu}\chi_{\delta}({\bf x})|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.
Since the functions
u|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}
are unknown, neither are
v|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\partial_{\nu}v|_{\partial\Omega}
.
We are unable to compute the exact Cauchy information of
v
on
\partial\Omega
. However,
since
\delta
is a small number, due to (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), both
|v|
and
|\nabla v|
on
\partial\Omega
are small.
Therefore, we can impose the conditions that both
v_{\epsilon_{0}}|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\nabla v_{\epsilon_{0}}|_{\partial\Omega}
satisfy

|v_{\epsilon_{0}}|<C\delta\quad\mbox{and }|\nabla v_{\epsilon_{0}}|<C\delta

(2.12)

for all
{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega.

Since the exact boundary condition for
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
cannot be retrieved, numerical methods to compute it is not yet developed.
Conventional methods compute a function
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
that satisfies (
2.9
) and (
2.12
) are based on least squares optimization.
That means we minimize a cost functional and then set the minimizer, named as
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
, as the computed solution.
A typical example of such a functional is

v\mapsto J(v)=\int_{\Omega}|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)|^{2}d{\bf x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}|v|^{2}d{\bf x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}|\nabla v|^{2}d\sigma({\bf x})+\mbox{a regularization term}.

(2.13)

This approach is effective in many cases. It is widely used in the scientific community.
However, it has several drawbacks.
The most important drawback is that finding the global minimizer
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
is extremely challenging unless a good initial guess is given.
This is because the functional
J
might not be convex and might have multiple local minima.
The second drawback is that, in general, the distance between the true solution
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
to (
2.4
) and the computed solution
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
is not known.
In this paper, we generalize the convexification method in
[
23
,
32
]
to compute the “best fit” solution to (
2.9
) and (
2.12
).
By convexification, we mean that we let a Carleman weight function involve in the functional
J
, defined in (
2.13
).
The presence of the Carleman weight function remove both significant drawbacks of the least squares optimization approach above.
As mentioned in Section
1
, the idea of using Carleman weight function to convexify the functional
J
was originally introduce in
[
28
]
and then was investigated intensively by our research group, see e.g.,
[
3
,
23
,
32
,
35
]
.

3
A piece-wise Carleman estimate

The key tool for us to rigorously prove the convexifying phenomenon is the Carleman estimate established in this section.
Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth boundary.
Let
A:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a
d\times d
matrix valued function in the class
C^{2}
.
Assume that

1.

A
is symmetric; i.e.,
A^{\rm T}=A
or equivalently
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
, where
a_{ij}
is the entry on row
i
and column
j
of
A
;

2.

A
is positive definite; i.e., there exists a positive number
\Lambda
such that

\Lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq A({\bf x})\xi\cdot\xi\leq\Lambda|\xi|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega},\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(3.1)

Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega.
For each
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define

r({\bf x})=|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega}.

(3.2)

We have the theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\lambda>0
and
u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}).

Then, there exists a positive constant
\beta_{0}
depending only on
\|A\|_{C_{1}(\overline{\Omega})}
and
\Lambda

such that if
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
and
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}=2R^{\beta}
, where
R=\max_{{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega}}\{|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|\}
, then

r^{\beta+2}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|{\rm div}(A\nabla u)|^{2}\geq C\Big[{\rm div}(U)+\lambda^{3}\beta^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}r^{-2\beta-2}|u|^{2}+\lambda\beta e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|\nabla u|^{2}\Big].

(3.3)

Here,
U
is a vector valued function satisfying

|U|\leq Ce^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(\lambda^{3}\beta^{3}r^{-2\beta-2}|u|^{2}+\lambda\beta|\nabla u|^{2})

(3.4)

and
C
is a constant depending only on
{\bf x}_{0},

\Omega
,
\|A\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})}
,
\Lambda
and
d
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

In the proof, we denote by
C_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,\dots\}
, positive constants depending only on
\|A\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})},

{\bf x}_{0},

\Omega
,
\Lambda
and
d.

We split the proof into several steps.

Step 1
.
For
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, recall
r=|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|.

Set

v=e^{\lambda r^{-\beta}}u\quad\mbox{equivalently}\quad u=e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}v.

(3.5)

By the product rule in differentiation and the symmetry of
A
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm div}(A\nabla u)

\displaystyle={\rm div}(A\nabla(e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}v))

\displaystyle=2A\nabla v\cdot\nabla(e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}})+e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}{\rm div}(A\nabla v)+v{\rm div}(A\nabla e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}})

\displaystyle=2\lambda\beta r^{-\beta-2}e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}A\nabla v\cdot({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0})+e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}{\rm div}(A\nabla v)+v{\rm div}(A\nabla e^{-\lambda r^{-\beta}}).

Using the inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\geq 2a(b+c),
we have for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega

\frac{r^{\beta+2}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|{\rm div}(A\nabla u)|^{2}}{2\lambda\beta}\geq 2({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0})\cdot A\nabla v{\rm div}(A\nabla v)+2e^{\lambda r^{-\beta}}[({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0})\cdot A\nabla v]v{\rm div}(A\nabla e^{\lambda r^{-\beta}}).

(3.6)

Denote by

4
The Carleman convexification theorem

Let
p>\lceil d/2\rceil+2.

We have
H^{p}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded into
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}).

Fix
\beta=\beta_{0}
. For all
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
and for
\eta\in(0,1),
define the Carleman weighted mismatch functional
J_{\lambda,\eta}:H^{p}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as follows

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)=\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)|^{2}d{\bf x}+\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v|^{2}+|\nabla v|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}.

(4.1)

The Carleman weighted mismatch functional
J_{\lambda,\eta}
in (
4.1
) is different from the ones used in our research group’s previous papers
[
23
,
32
,
35
]
. The main differences is that in (
4.1
), we include the integral on
\partial\Omega
.
We add this boundary integral to the mismatch functional because we do not know the exact boundary information of the function
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
on
\partial\Omega.

The presence of this boundary integral somewhat guarantees that the values of
v^{\epsilon_{0}}_{\rm min}|_{\partial\Omega}
and
\nabla v^{\epsilon_{0}}_{\rm min}|_{\partial\Omega}
are small where
v^{\epsilon_{0}}_{\rm min}
is the minimizer of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
.
Also, since we will
minimize
J_{\lambda,\eta}
without boundary constraints,
the earlier versions of the Carleman convexification method
[
3
,
23
,
32
,
35
]
, which require some boundary conditions on the minimizer, are not applicable.
We modify the use of the Carleman estimate in those theorem to obtain the convexification theorem below.

Theorem 4.1

(The convexification theorem)
.

Assume that the function
F
is of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}).
We have:

1.

For all
\lambda>1
and
\eta>0
,
the functional
J_{\lambda,\nu}
is Frétchet differentiable. The derivative of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
is given by

DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v)h=2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)][-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h]d{\bf x}\\
+2\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[vh+\nabla v\cdot\nabla h]d\sigma({\bf x})+2\eta\langle v,h\rangle_{H^{p}(\Omega)}

(4.2)

for all
v,h\in H^{p}(\Omega).

Here,
\partial_{s}F
is the partial differential derivative of the function
F({\bf x},s,{\bf p})
,
({\bf x},s,{\bf p})\in\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with respect to the second variable and
\nabla_{{\bf p}}F
is the gradient vector of
F
with respect to the third variable
{\bf p}.

2.

Let
M
be an arbitrarily large number.
For each
\beta>1
,

\lambda>\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(\epsilon_{0},M,b,d,r,F,\beta)>1
,
\eta>0
,
u,v\in\overline{B(M)}
, we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(u)-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v)(u-v)\geq C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|u-v|^{2}+|\nabla(u-v)|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+C\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|u-v|^{2}+|\nabla(u-v)|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|u-v\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.3)

Here, the constant
C
depends only on
\lambda,

\beta
,
R,

r,

d
,
M
,
F
and
\epsilon_{0}
.

3.

The functional
J_{\lambda,\beta,\eta}
has a unique minimizer in
\overline{B(M)}
.

Remark 4.1
.

An intuition for the convexity of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
is that one can apply the convexification theorem in
[
32
]
to obtain the convexity of the functional

v\mapsto I_{\lambda,\eta}(v)=\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)|^{2}d{\bf x}+\eta\|v\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}.

By adding the convex term
\displaystyle\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[|v|^{2}d\sigma({\bf x})+|\nabla v|^{2}]d\sigma({\bf x})
to this functional, we obtain the desired convexity of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
.
However, the convexity of
I_{\lambda,\eta}
is valid only on a set of functions that satisfy some Cauchy boundary data.
Hence, the informal argument above is not rigorous.
We present the proof of Theorem
4.1
here.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

The first part of Theorem
4.1
can be proved by a straight forward computation similarly in the first part of
[
32
, Theorem 4.1]
. We now discuss part 2 of Theorem
4.1
.
Let
u
and
v
be two functions in
H^{p}(\Omega).
Let
h=u-v
.
We have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(u)-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v)(u-v)\\
=\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta u+F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)|^{2}-|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[u^{2}-v^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}-|\nabla v|^{2}\big]d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\big[\|u\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}-\|v\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\big]\\
-2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)][-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h]d{\bf x}\\
-2\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[vh+\nabla v\cdot\nabla h]d\sigma({\bf x})-2\eta\langle v,h\rangle_{H^{p}(\Omega)}.

(4.4)

Using the identity
a^{2}-b^{2}=(a-b)(a+b)
, we deduce from (
4.4
) that

J_{\lambda,\eta}(u)-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(u-v)=\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\\
-2\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+2F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big]\big[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big]d{\bf x}\\
+\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[(u+v)h+\nabla(u+v)\cdot\nabla h]d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\langle u+v,h\rangle_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\\
-2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)][-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h]d{\bf x}\\
-2\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[vh+\nabla v\cdot\nabla h]d\sigma({\bf x})-2\eta\langle v,h\rangle_{H^{p}(\Omega)}.

(4.5)

Expending the right hand side of (
4.5
), we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(u)-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(u-v)=I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|h\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.6)

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big|-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big|^{2}d{\bf x},

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big]\big[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big]d{\bf x},

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)][-\epsilon_{0}\Delta h+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h]d{\bf x}.

Using the inequality
(a-b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}-b^{2}
and recalling that
u
and
v
are in the bounded set
\overline{B(M)}
, we can find a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|\Delta h|^{2}d{\bf x}-\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)|^{2}d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|\Delta h|^{2}d{\bf x}-C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2}]d{\bf x}.

(4.7)

On the other hand,

I_{2}+I_{3}=-2\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[-\epsilon_{0}\Delta v+F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\big]\\
\times\big[F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h\big]d{\bf x}

(4.8)

Since both
u
and
v
are in the set
\overline{B(M)}
, we have

\big|F({\bf x},u,\nabla u)-F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)+\partial_{s}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)h+\nabla_{{\bf p}}F({\bf x},v,\nabla v)\cdot\nabla h\big|\leq C[|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2}].

Thus,

I_{2}+I_{3}\geq-C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2}]d{\bf x}.

(4.9)

Combining (
4.6
), (
4.7
), (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(u)-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v)-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(u-v)\geq\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|\Delta h|^{2}d{\bf x}-C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}[|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2}]d{\bf x}\\
+\lambda^{4}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|h|^{2}+|\nabla h|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|h\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(4.10)

In order to prove the convexity of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
, we need to show that the right hand side (
4.10
) is nonnegative.
This is the main reason why the Carleman estimate in (
3.40
) plays the key role in this proof.
Applying (
3.40
) for the function
h
with
A=\textrm{Id}
, we have

\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}|\Delta h|^{2}d{\bf x}\geq\frac{C\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[\lambda^{3}|h|^{2}+\lambda|\nabla h|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}-\frac{C\epsilon_{0}^{2}}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[\lambda^{3}|h|^{2}+\lambda|\nabla h|^{2}\big]d\sigma({\bf x}).

(4.11)

Letting
\lambda
be sufficiently large, allowing
C
to depend on
\epsilon_{0}
and
\lambda
, combining (
4.10
) and (
4.11
), and recalling that
h=u-v
, we get (
4.3
).

We next show that
J_{\lambda,\eta}
has a unique minimizer by using the arguments in
[
3
]
. Assume
J_{\lambda,\eta}
has two minimizers
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
\overline{B(M)}
.
Applying (
4.3
) for
u=v_{1}
and
v=v_{2}
, we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{1})-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})(v_{1}-v_{2})\geq C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+C\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.12)

By
[
3
, Lemma 2]
, since
v_{2}
is a minimizer of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
in
\overline{B(M)},

DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})(v_{1}-v_{2})\geq 0,\quad\mbox{or }\quad-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})(v_{1}-v_{2})\leq 0

(4.13)

Combining (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{1})-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})\geq C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+C\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(4.14)

Similarly, interchanging the roles of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{2})-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{1})\geq C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+C\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v_{1}-v_{2}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{1}-v_{2})|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(4.15)

Adding (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), we obtain
v_{1}=v_{2}.

∎

The unique minimizer of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
can be obtained by the the conventional gradient descent method. We refer the reader to
[
35
, Theorem 2]
and
[
32
, Theorem 4.2]
for this fact. Let
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
be the minimizer of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
, one can repeat the proof in
[
35
]
.
We next estimate the distance of minimizer
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
and
v_{\epsilon_{0}}.

We have the theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
be a function satisfying (
2.9
) and (
2.12
).
Assume that
v_{\epsilon_{0}}\in\overline{B(M)}
for some large number
M
.
Let
\beta,\lambda
be such that (
4.3
) holds true.
Let
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
be the unique minimizer of

J_{\lambda,\eta}
in
\overline{B(M)}
.
We have

\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})\\
+\eta\|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\Big[\lambda^{4}\delta^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{\epsilon_{0}}\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\Big].

(4.16)

Proof.

Applying (
4.3
) for
u=v_{\epsilon_{0}}
and
v=v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
, we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\epsilon_{0}})-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})\geq C\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}\big[|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})|^{2}\big]d{\bf x}\\
+C\int_{\partial\Omega}\lambda^{4}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}(|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}|^{2}+|\nabla(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})|^{2})d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}^{2}

(4.17)

Since
v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}}
is the minimizer of
J_{\lambda,\eta}
in
\overline{B(M)}
, by
[
3
, Lemma 2]
,

DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})\geq 0,\quad\mbox{or }\quad-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})\leq 0,

we have

-J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})-DJ_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})(v_{\epsilon_{0}}-v_{\rm min}^{\epsilon_{0}})\leq 0.

(4.18)

On the other hand, recalling that
v_{\epsilon_{0}}
satisfies (
2.9
) and (
2.12
), we have

J_{\lambda,\eta}(v_{\epsilon_{0}})\leq\lambda^{4}C\delta^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{2\lambda r^{-\beta}}d\sigma({\bf x})+\eta\|v_{\epsilon_{0}}\|^{2}_{H^{p}(\Omega)}.

(4.19)

Combining (
4.17
), (
4.18
) and (
4.19
) yields (
4.16
).
The proof is complete.
∎

The procedure to compute the numerical solution to (

) on a domain

1:

. Choose a cut-off function

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have developed a new version of the Carleman based convexification method to compute the viscosity solutions to Hamilton-Jacobi equations on the whole space.
Our procedure consists of two main stages. In Stage 1, we derive from the given Hamilton-Jacobi equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
another Hamilton-Jacobi equation on a bounded domain by applying a truncation technique and a simple change of variable.
It is important to mention that the boundary conditions for the Hamilton-Jacobi equation obtained Stage 1 cannot be exactly computed.
Only approximations ones are derived.
This feature makes the original convexification method is not applicable.
In Stage 2, we develop the new version of the Carleman-based convexification method to solve the new Hamilton-Jacobi equation with approximated boundary conditions.
The main theorems in this paper guarantee that the Carleman-based convexification method in Stage 2 delivers reliable numerical solutions to nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi equations without requiring good initial guess.
