An asymptotic behaviour near the crest of waves of extreme form on water of finite depth
Vladimir Kozlov 1 , Evgeniy Lokharu 1

Abstract
. We prove local higher-order asymptotics for extreme water waves with vorticity near stagnation points. We obtain that the behaviour of solutions and their regularity depend substantially on the vorticity. In particular, we show that extreme waves with a negative vorticity distribution have concave profiles near the crest. Our approach is based on new regularity results and asymptotic analysis of the corresponding nonlinear problem in a half-strip. Our main result is local and therefore is valid for a broad range of problems, such as for waves with a piecewise constant vorticity, stratified waves, flows with counter-currents or waves on infinite depth.



1.
Introduction

Extreme waves or also known as waves of greatest height is an important phenomena in the mathematical theory of water waves. The story goes back to Sir George Stokes
[
19
]
who in 1880s studied periodic solutions of the water wave problem when the wavelength is fixed. He assumed that such waves can be parametrized by the wave height
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x)-\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\eta(x)
, where
y=\eta(x)
is the surface profile. In
[
20
]
Stokes conjectured that the family of periodic waves contains “
the wave of greatest height
” distinguished by sharp crests of included angle
120^{\circ}
, see Figure 1. That was a remarkable hypothesis for the time and it took about two centuries before it was rigorously justified.

The Stokes conjecture might be divided into two independent parts:

(i)

there exists a sufficiently regular travelling wave solution of the water wave problem that enjoys stagnation at every crest (where the horizontal and vertical components of the relative velocity fields vanish);

(ii)

every solution from (i) with surface profile
\eta
must satisfy

\lim_{x\to x_{0}\pm}\eta_{x}(x)=\mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}

at every stagnation point
(x_{0},\eta(x_{0}))
; this corresponds to the included angle
120^{\circ}
.

The main difficulty about the Stokes conjecture is that waves with surface stagnations in (i) are normally large-amplitude solutions with low regularity. That makes their analysis complicated and requires different tools compared to perturbation methods for small-amplitude nonlinear water waves. The first construction of large-amplitude waves is due to Krasovskii in
[
14
]
, who proved the following statement about irrotational water waves in deep water. It was shown that for any given flux
Q
, wavelength
L
and
\beta\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{6})
there exists a Stokes wave with
\max\theta=\beta
, where
\theta
is the inclination angle to the horizontal. Even so this is a beautiful statement with a clear geometrical interpretation it does not explain if such waves are close to stagnation or not. About two decades later Keady and Norbury
[
10
]
used a different approach based on the global bifurcation theory for the Nekrasov equation. They proved that there exist Stokes waves (symmetric periodic profiles having exactly one crest and trough in every minimal period) that are arbitrary close to the stagnation. The limiting wave with surface stagnation was obtained by Toland
[
21
]
in the case of infinite depth. For the corresponding results about extreme (by extreme waves we mean solutions with surface stagnation points as in (i)) Stokes and solitary waves on finite depth we refer to Amick and Toland
[
4
,
5
]
.

The second part (ii) of the Stokes conjecture was verified independently by Amick, Fraenkel, and Toland in
[
3
]
and by Plotnikov
[
17
]
. Much later, Plotnikov and Toland
[
18
]
proved the existence of extreme periodic waves that are convex everywhere outside crests. The Stokes conjecture in the irrotational case was refined by Varvaruca and Weiss in
[
23
]
, who proved (ii) for solutions under weak regularity assumptions and without any symmetry or monotonicity constraints. In particular, (ii) turned out to be a local property and is valid for the extreme solitary wave found in
[
5
]
.

So far all mentioned results concerned with water waves on the surface of irrotational flows, while the rotational theory is much less developed. The subject has attracted a significant interest with the pioneering study by Constantin and Strauss
[
7
]
. The authors used bifurcation and degree theories to construct global connected sets of large-amplitude periodic waves with vorticity that can be arbitrary close to the stagnation. Thus, one could think of constructing an extreme wave by passing to the limit along a sequence of waves approaching the stagnation. This was formally done in
[
22
]
under certain assumptions on the vorticity. We say formally because it is not known if the limiting wave is trivial or not. By a trivial extreme wave we mean a laminar flow whose surface or bottom consists of stagnation points. To overcome this difficulty a different approach was proposed in
[
12
]
and extreme waves subject to (i) were constructed.

The second part (ii) of the Stokes conjecture is more complicated for waves with vorticity. In their study
[
24
]
Varvaruca and Weiss found (without proving the existence) that surface profiles near stagnation points are either (a1) Stokes corners (
120^{\circ}
), (a2) horizontally flat with
\eta_{x}(0)=0
, or (a3) overhanging horizontal cusps. So far it is not known if options (a2) and (a3) are possible, though (a2) is always true for extreme laminar flows.

Beside the questions (i) and (ii) one can also ask about the regularity of extreme waves near stagnation points. The only results of this type are
[
2
]
and
[
16
]
for irrotational waves on infinite depth. An asymptotic expansion for the inclination angle in the conformal variables was obtained in
[
2
]
, while
[
16
]
contains an analysis of the leading order coefficient. In the present paper we investigate these questions about extreme water waves with vorticity. Assuming (ii) we obtain local asymptotics for the surface profile in the right neighbourhood of the stagnation point. The latter asymptotics essentially depend on values of the vorticity function near the surface. As a consequence we obtain a surprising result: the surface profile can be concave near the stagnation point when the vorticity has the right sign. This observation is confirmed by several numerical studies, such as
[
11
]
and
[
8
]
. In some sense our results improve the statement of
[
2
]
for waves on infinite depth, which is obtained only in conformal variables, while we provide expansions in physical variables.

2.
Statement of the problem

We consider the classical water wave problem for two-dimensional steady waves with vorticity on water of finite depth. An infinite fluid region is occupied with an ideal fluid of constant unit density, separated from the air by an unknown free surface, where the effects of surface tension and air motion are neglected. Assuming the motion of the fluid is steady we can find an appropriate coordinate system moving with a constant speed
c>0
in which the flow is stationary and governed by Euler equations:

(2.1a)

\displaystyle(u-c)u_{x}+vu_{y}

\displaystyle=-P_{x},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle(u-c)v_{x}+vv_{y}

\displaystyle=-P_{y}-g,

(2.1c)

\displaystyle u_{x}+v_{y}

\displaystyle=0,

which hold true in a two-dimensional fluid domain
D_{\eta}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:0<y<\eta(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Here
(u,v)
are components of the relative velocity field,
y=\eta(x)
is the surface profile,
c
is the wave speed,
P
is the pressure and
g
is the gravitational constant. The corresponding boundary conditions are

(2.1d)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }y=0,

(2.1e)

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=(u-c)\eta_{x}

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.1f)

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=P_{\mathrm{atm}}

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta.

It is often assumed in the literature that the flow is irrotational, that is
v_{x}-u_{y}
is zero everywhere in the fluid domain. Under this assumption components of the velocity field are harmonic functions, which allows to apply methods of complex analysis. Being a convenient simplification it forbids modeling of non-uniform currents, commonly occurring in nature. In the present paper we will consider rotational flows, where the vorticity function is defined by

(2.2)

\omega=v_{x}-u_{y}.

Throughout the paper we assume that the flow is free from stagnation points and the horizontal component of the relative velocity field does not change sign, that is

(2.3)

u-c<0

everywhere in the fluid. We call such flows unidirectional.

In the two-dimensional setup relation (
2.1c
) allows to reformulate the problem in terms of a stream function
\psi
, defined implicitly by relations

\psi_{y}=c-u,\ \ \psi_{x}=v.

This determines
\psi
up to an additive constant, while relations (
2.1d
),(
2.1d
) force
\psi
to be constant along the boundaries. Thus, by subtracting a suitable constant, we can always assume that

\psi=m,\ \ y=\eta;\ \ \psi=0,\ \ y=0.

Here
m
is the mass flux, defined by

m=\int_{0}^{\eta}(u-c)dy.

In what follows we will use non-dimensional variables proposed by Keady & Norbury
[
10
]
, where lengths and velocities are scaled by
(m^{2}/g)^{1/3}
and
(mg)^{1/3}
respectively; in new units
m=1
and
g=1
. For simplicity we keep the same notations for
\eta
and
\psi
.

Taking the curl of Euler equations (
2.1a
)-(
2.1c
) one checks that the vorticity function
\omega
defined by (
2.2
) is constant along paths tangent everywhere to the relative velocity field
(u-c,v)
; see
[
6
]
for more details. Having the same property by the definition, stream function
\psi
is strictly monotone by (
2.3
) on every vertical interval inside the fluid region. These observations together show that
\omega
depends only on values of the stream function, that is

\omega=\omega(\psi).

This property and Bernoulli’s law allow to express the pressure
P
as

(2.4)

\displaystyle P-P_{\mathrm{atm}}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}+y+\Omega(\psi)-\Omega(1)=const,

where

\displaystyle\Omega(\psi)=\int_{0}^{\psi}\omega(p)\,dp

is a primitive of the vorticity function
\omega(\psi)
. Thus, we can eliminate the pressure from equations and obtain the following problem:

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\Delta\psi+\omega(\psi)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{for }0<y<\eta,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}+y

\displaystyle=r

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.5c)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=1

\displaystyle\text{on }y=\eta,

(2.5d)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on }y=0.

At the crest located at
(0,r)
the flow has a stagnation point, that is

(2.5e)

\psi_{y}(0,r)=\psi_{x}(0,r)=0,\ \ \eta(0)=r.

In what follows we will consider a local solution of the problem, which unidirectional in a neighbourhood of the stagnation point. Thus, the boundary condition (
2.5d
) might be omitted. Therefore, our results will be valid for waves on infinite depth and for flows with counter-currents.

We assume that the surface profile
\eta
is defined on an open interval
I=(-\delta,\delta)
and is an even function there. Furthermore, we assume that
\eta_{x}\in C^{1}([0,\delta])
, while

(2.5f)

\lim_{x\to 0+}\eta_{x}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.

The stream function
\psi
is defined over the set

D_{\eta}^{\delta}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ x^{2}+(y-r)^{2}\leq\delta^{2},\ \ -\delta<x<+\delta,\ \ y\leq\eta(x)\}.

As for regularity of
\psi
we assume that
\psi\in C^{1}(\overline{D_{\eta}^{\delta}})\cap C^{2}(D_{\eta}^{\delta})
. We additionally require that
\psi
is even in the
x
-variable.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(\psi,\eta)
be as above and solve (
2.5
) in
D_{\eta}^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
. Assume that
\omega\in C^{1}([1-\delta,1])
and
\psi_{y}>0
for
(x,y)\in D_{\eta}^{\delta},y\neq r
. Then

(2.6)

\eta_{x}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{2^{\tfrac{3}{2}}}{3^{\tfrac{5}{4}}}\omega(1)\sqrt{x}+a_{1}\omega^{2}(1)x+f(x)

for some explicit
a_{1}>0
, where
f=O(x^{\tfrac{3}{2}(\tau_{1}-1)}),\ f_{x}=O(x^{\tfrac{3}{2}(\tau_{1}-1)-1})
as
x\to 0+
and
\tau_{1}\approx 1.8
is the smallest root of
\tau_{1}=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\cot(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tau_{1})
.

It follows from the theorem that
\eta\in C^{2,\gamma}([0,\delta])
for some small
\gamma>0
, provided
\omega(1)=0
. Beside (
2.6
) one can also obtain the corresponding asymptotics for the stream function, where the leading order term is determined by the Stokes corner flow. For more details see Section
5
.

Our proof of Theorem
2.1
is given in the next sections.

3.
Hölder regularity

Our aim in this section is to obtain the optimal global regularity of an extreme wave.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
(\psi,\eta)
satisfies assumptions of Theorem
2.1
. Then there exists
0<\delta_{1}<\delta
such that

\|\nabla\psi\|_{C^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Omega}_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}})}\leq C,

where the constant
C
depends only on
\delta,\delta_{1},\omega
and
r
.

Note that the Hölder exponent
1/2
is optimal and can not be improved. Thus, the Hölder space
C^{1,1/2}
is the natural choice for the stream function
\psi
of an extreme wave. Our proof consists of several lemmas. The key property of the stream function is stated in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exist
\delta_{1}>0
and constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

(3.1)

C_{1}\rho^{\tfrac{1}{2}}<\psi_{y}(x,y)<C_{2}\rho^{\tfrac{1}{2}}

for all
(x,y)\in\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}
, where
\rho=(x^{2}+(r-y)^{2})^{\tfrac{1}{2}}
and
C_{1},C_{2}
are independent of
\rho
.

Proof.

Note that (
3.1
) holds true along the surface in
\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta}
, which is a direct consequence of the Bernoulli equation (
2.5b
) and (
2.5f
). Now let
0<x<\delta_{1}
be given, where
\delta_{1}>0
is such that

\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}<|\eta_{x}|<\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}

for all
|x|<\delta_{1}
. Then we put
R=\tfrac{1}{10}x
and apply Theorem 8.26 in
[
9
]
in
B_{\rho}(x,\eta(x))
for the function
\psi_{y}
solving

\Delta\psi_{y}+\omega^{\prime}(\psi)\psi_{y}=0.

This shows that the left inequality in (
3.1
) is valid in
B_{R}(x,\eta(x))
for any
|x|<\delta_{1}
. For an interior estimate one can use the Harnack principle from Theorem 8.20 in
[
9
]
. It remains to prove the upper bound for
\psi_{y}
. For that purpose we consider the function

f=\psi_{y}^{2}-A(r-y)+B(r-y)^{2},

where

B=\sup_{\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}}|\psi_{y}|^{2}|\omega^{\prime}(\psi)|,\ \ \ A=\sup_{\partial\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}}\psi_{y}^{2}(r-y)^{-1}+B(r-y).

The choice of
B
implies that
\Delta f\geq 2|\nabla\psi_{y}|^{2}\geq 0
so that
f
attains its maximum at the boundary of
\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}
, where
f
is nonpositive by the choice of
A
. Note that the constant
A
depends on
\delta_{1}
and might be large though it is finite since (
3.1
) is valid along the upper boundary. Thus, by the maximum principle we have
f\leq 0
in
\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}
so that

\psi_{y}^{2}\leq Ay\leq CA\rho\ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}.

This finished the proof of the lemma.
∎

Figure 2
.
Domains
\Omega_{x}
and
B_{R}

Now using Lemma
3.2
we can obtain local estimates in the domain

\Omega_{x}=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}):\in\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}:\ \ \tfrac{1}{2}x<x^{\prime}<\tfrac{3}{2}x,\ \ y^{\prime}>r-\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{3}}x^{\prime}\},

where
\delta_{1}
is the constant from Lemma
3.2
and
0<x<\tfrac{2}{3}\delta_{1}
(see Figure 1). Note that

(3.2)

C_{1}x<\rho<C_{2}x

for all
(x,y)\in\Omega_{x}
with some absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
.

Lemma 3.3
.

There exist
C>0
and
x_{0}>0
such that

|\nabla\psi(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla\psi(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq Cx^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|)

for all
0<x<x_{0}
and for all
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\Omega_{x}
. The constant
C
is independent of
x
. Furthermore, we have

(3.3)

\|\psi\|_{C^{2,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega_{x}})}\leq Cx^{-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\gamma\right)}

for
0<x<x_{0}
and any
\gamma\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to perform the partial hodograph transform in
\Omega_{x}
obtaining a nonlinear equation with good scaling properties. More precisely, we put

q=x,\ \ p=1-\psi(x,y),\ \ h(q,p)=r-y.

A direct computation shows that
h
solves an elliptic equation

\frac{1+h_{q}^{2}}{h_{p}^{2}}h_{pp}-2\frac{h_{q}}{h_{p}}h_{qp}+h_{qq}+h_{p}\omega(1-p)=0\ \ \text{in}\ \ S_{x},

where
S_{x}
is the corresponding to
\Omega_{x}
domain in the
(q,p)
-variables given by

S_{x}=\{(q,p):\tfrac{1}{2}x<q<\tfrac{3}{2}x,\ \ C(q)x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}<p<0\},

where
C_{1}<C(q)<C_{2}
for some absolute constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. Thus, we scale variables as follows:

q=x\hat{q},\ \ p=x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\hat{p},\ \ h(q,p)=x\hat{h}(\hat{q},\hat{p}).

Another computation gives

h_{p}=x^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\hat{h}_{\hat{p}},\ \ h_{q}=\hat{h}_{\hat{q}},\ \ h_{pp}=x^{-2}\hat{h}_{\hat{p}\hat{p}},\ \ h_{qq}=x^{-1}\hat{h}_{\hat{q}\hat{q}},

so that
\hat{h}
solves

\frac{1+\hat{h}_{\hat{q}}^{2}}{\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}^{2}}\hat{h}_{\hat{p}\hat{p}}-2\frac{\hat{h}_{\hat{q}}}{\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}}\hat{h}_{\hat{q}\hat{p}}+\hat{h}_{\hat{q}\hat{q}}+x^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}\omega(1-x^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\hat{p})=0\ \ \text{in}\ \ \hat{S}_{x}.

Note that

C_{1}<\hat{h},\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}<C_{2}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \hat{S}_{x}

for some constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, which follows from Lemma
3.2
since
\hat{h}_{p}=x^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\psi_{y}^{-1}
and
x
is comparable with
\rho
by (
3.2
). Along the upper boundary of
\hat{S}_{x}
we have

\frac{1+\hat{h}_{\hat{q}}^{2}}{2\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}^{2}}-\hat{h}=0,\ \ \hat{p}=0,

which follows from the Bernoulli equation after the scaling. Thus,
\hat{h}
solves a uniformly elliptic nonlinear boundary problem and from Theorem 1.1
[
15
]
we conclude that

(3.4)

\|\hat{h}\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\hat{S}_{x}})}\leq C

for some constant
C>0
independent of
x
(we even get a higher regularity by it is not essential for our purposes). Now we can estimate

\begin{split}|\psi_{y}(x_{1},y_{1})-\psi_{y}(x_{2},y_{2})|&\leq Cx|h_{p}(q_{1},p_{1})-h_{p}(q_{2},p_{2})|\leq Cx^{\tfrac{1}{2}}|\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}(\hat{q}_{1},\hat{p}_{1})-\hat{h}_{\hat{p}}(\hat{q}_{2},\hat{p}_{2})|\\
&\leq Cx^{\tfrac{1}{2}}(|\hat{q}_{1}-\hat{q}_{2}|+|\hat{p}_{1}-\hat{p}_{2}|)\leq Cx^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|)\end{split}

for all
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\Omega_{x}
. Similarly, we obtain

|\psi_{x}(x_{1},y_{1})-\psi_{x}(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq Cx^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|).

The remaining inequality (
3.3
) can be obtained the same way from (
3.4
). This finishes the proof.
∎

Now we consider an interior region
B_{R}\subset\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta}
, where
B_{R}
is a ball of radius
R
centred at
(0,y_{R})\in\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}
, where
r-y_{R}>\tfrac{6}{5}R
(see Figure 1). Thus, the distance from
B_{R}
to the upper boundary of
\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta}
is of order
R
. From Lemma
3.2
we find that

(3.5)

C_{1}R^{\tfrac{3}{2}}<1-\psi<C_{2}R^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\ \ \text{in}\ \ B_{R}.

Next we perform the following scaling of variables in
B_{R}
:

x=R\hat{x},\ \ y=r-R\hat{y},\ \ \psi(x,y)=1-R^{\tfrac{3}{2}}\hat{\psi}(\hat{x},\hat{y}).

Thus, the new function
\hat{\psi}
is defined in a ball
\hat{B}
of radius
1
and satisfy

C_{1}<\hat{\psi}<C_{2}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \hat{B}

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants independent of
R
, which follows from (
3.5
). Now the classical elliptic theory yields that

\|\hat{\psi}\|_{C^{2,\gamma}(\hat{B})}\leq C.

Scaling back, we obtain

(3.6)

|\nabla\psi(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla\psi(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq CR^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}(|x_{1}-x_{2}|+|y_{1}-y_{2}|)

for all
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in B_{R}
. Furthermore, we have

(3.7)

\|\psi\|_{C^{2,\gamma}(B_{R})}\leq CR^{-\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma}.

Now we can complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
by establishing interior estimates. Let us consider two points
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\Omega_{\eta}^{\delta_{1}}
. Let
\rho_{j}
be the distance from
(x_{j},y_{j})
to
(0,r)
and let
\rho_{0}=\min(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
. Furthermore, we put
\rho_{12}
to be the distance between
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
. If
\rho_{0}<\rho_{12}
, then we obtain

|\psi_{y}(x_{1},y_{1})-\psi_{y}(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq|\psi_{y}(x_{1},y_{1})|+|\psi_{y}(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq C\rho_{12}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}

by (
3.1
) as desired. Assume that
\rho_{0}>\rho_{12}
. Then without loss of generality we can assume that both points
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
belong to one of the regions studied above:
B_{\rho_{0}}
or
\Omega_{x}
, where
C_{1}\rho_{0}<|x|<C_{2}\rho_{0}
. In both cases we conclude from (
3.6
) and Lemma
3.3
that

|\psi_{y}(x_{1},y_{1})-\psi_{y}(x_{2},y_{2})|\leq C\rho_{0}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\rho_{12}\leq C\rho_{12}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.

In a similar way one obtains an estimate for
\psi_{x}
. The only difference is in the case when
\rho_{0}<\rho_{12}
. We need to show that
|\psi_{x}(x_{j},y_{j})|<C\rho_{j}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}
. But this follows from the previous interior and boundary estimates (
3.6
) and Lemma
3.3
which imply that
|\psi_{x}|\leq C\psi_{y}
. This finishes the proof of the theorem.

4.
Reformulation of the problem

4.1.
Logarithmic transformation

Figure 3
.
Transformation of the fluid domain near a stagnation point into a curved half-strip
\hat{S}
; the vertical
x=0
becomes the bottom boundary
\theta=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}
and the surface
y=\eta(x)
turns into
\theta=\zeta(t)
.

The problem (
2.5
) in a corner can be transformed into the one in a strip through the following logarithmic transformation:

x=e^{-t}\cos\theta,\ \ y=r+e^{-t}\sin\theta,\ \ t>-\ln\delta_{1},\ \ -\tfrac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\zeta(t).

Under this transformation the corresponding part of the fluid domain becomes a curved half-strip
\hat{S}
, infinite from the right; see Figure
3
. The upper boundary of the ”strip” corresponds to the surface profile, while the flat bottom is the image of the vertical line
x=0
. The new unknown function is

\hat{\psi}(t,\theta)=1-\psi(x,y),

which solves

\hat{\psi}_{tt}+\hat{\psi}_{\theta\theta}-e^{-2t}\hat{\omega}(\hat{\psi})=0\ \ \text{in}\ \ \hat{S}=\{(t,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:t>-\ln\delta_{1},\ \ -\tfrac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\zeta(t)\},

where
\hat{\omega}(p)=\omega(1-p)
. The latter is a direct consequence of (
2.5a
). According to our definitions we have

(4.1)

\hat{\psi}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \theta=\zeta,

while the formula
\hat{\psi}_{\theta}=e^{-t}\sin\theta\psi_{x}-e^{-t}\cos\theta\psi_{y}
shows that

\hat{\psi}_{\theta}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ \theta=-\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

On the surface
\theta=\zeta(t)
we also have

(4.2)

\tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{\psi}_{t}^{2}+\hat{\psi}_{\theta}^{2})=-e^{-3t}\sin\theta\ \ \text{on}\ \ \theta=\zeta(t),

which is a direct consequence of (
2.5c
) and (
2.5b
). Beside the boundary relations from above we also know from (
2.5f
) that

(4.3)

\zeta(t)\to-\tfrac{1}{6}\pi\ \ \text{as}\ \ t\to+\infty.

Furthermore, from (
4.1
) we find

(4.4)

\zeta_{t}=-\frac{\hat{\psi}_{t}}{\hat{\psi}_{\theta}}=-\frac{\psi_{x}\cos{\zeta}+\psi_{y}\sin{\zeta}}{\psi_{x}\sin{\zeta}-\psi_{y}\cos{\zeta}}=-\frac{\eta_{x}\cos{\zeta}-\sin{\zeta}}{\eta_{x}\sin{\zeta}+\cos{\zeta}}.

Thus, from (
4.3
) and (
2.5f
) we conclude that

(4.5)

\zeta_{t}\to 0\ \ \text{as}\ \ t\to+\infty.

Using (
4.4
) we can recover the surface profile
\eta
as

(4.6)

\eta_{x}=\frac{\sin\zeta-\zeta_{t}\cos\zeta}{\cos\zeta+\zeta_{t}\sin\zeta},

which will be useful later.

A direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
is the following

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
there exists
C>0
such that the inequality

\|\hat{\psi}\|_{C^{2,\gamma}(\hat{S}_{t})}\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}t},

is valid for all
t\geq-\ln{\delta_{1}}
, where
\hat{S}_{t}=\{(t^{\prime},\theta)\in\overline{\hat{S}}:\ t\leq t^{\prime}\leq t+1\}
.

Proof.

It follows from definitions that

(4.7)

\hat{\psi}_{\theta}=e^{-t}\sin\theta\psi_{x}-e^{-t}\cos\theta\psi_{y},\ \ \hat{\psi}_{t}=e^{-t}\cos\theta\psi_{x}+e^{-t}\sin\theta\psi_{y}.

On the other hand, by Theorem
3.1
since
\nabla\psi(0,r)=(0,0)
we conclude

|\nabla\psi|\leq C\rho^{\tfrac{1}{2}}=Ce^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t}.

Combining this with (
4.7
) we obtain

|\nabla\hat{\psi}|\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}t}\ \ \text{in}\ \ \hat{S}.

Now let
(t_{1},\theta_{1}),(t_{2},\theta_{2})\in\hat{S}
be given such that
t\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq t+1
. Then from (
4.7
) we find

\begin{split}|\nabla\hat{\psi}(t_{1},\theta_{1})-\nabla\hat{\psi}(t_{2},\theta_{2})|&\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|)+Ce^{-t}|\nabla\psi(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla\psi(x_{2},y_{2})|\\
&\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|)+Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\\
&\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{3}{2}}(|t_{1}-t_{2}|+|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}|)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

A similar argument is valid for the second-order derivatives and the claim follows from Lemma
3.3
and (
3.7
). This finishes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Flattening of the domain

The flattening transformation

q=t,\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}\frac{\theta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\zeta(t)+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}

maps
\hat{S}
onto the half-strip
S=\{(q,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \ q\geq-\ln\delta_{1},\ \ 0<z<\tfrac{\pi}{2}\}
. Thus, the corresponding stream function in new variables is

\bar{\psi}(q,z)=\hat{\psi}(t,\theta),

which solves

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left[\bar{\psi}_{q}-\frac{z\zeta_{q}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\bar{\psi}_{z}\right]_{q}-\frac{z\zeta_{q}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\left[\bar{\psi}_{q}-\frac{z\zeta_{q}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\bar{\psi}_{z}\right]_{z}+\left(\frac{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)^{2}\bar{\psi}_{zz}-e^{-2q}\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})=0\ \ \text{in}\ S,

(4.9)

\displaystyle\ \bar{\psi}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2};\ \ \bar{\psi}_{z}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=0.

The remaining nonlinear boundary relation (
4.2
) becomes

(4.10)

\left[\bar{\psi}_{q}-\frac{z\zeta_{q}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\bar{\psi}_{z}\right]^{2}+\left(\frac{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)^{2}\bar{\psi}_{z}^{2}=-2e^{-3q}\sin(\zeta)\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

In order to reformulate this problem as a first-order system, we introduce an axillary function

(4.11)

\hat{\Psi}=\frac{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\left[\bar{\psi}_{q}-\frac{z\zeta_{q}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\bar{\psi}_{z}\right].

Thus, we can rewrite (
4.8
) as

(4.12)

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{q}=\frac{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\hat{\Psi}+\frac{z}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\zeta_{q}\bar{\psi}_{z},

(4.13)

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{q}=\frac{1}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\zeta_{q}(z\hat{\Psi})_{z}-\frac{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\bar{\psi}_{zz}+e^{-2q}\frac{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi}).

Note that we consider
\zeta
and
\zeta_{q}
as coefficients, so that the presence of
q
-derivative of
\zeta
is not a problem.

The boundary condition (
4.10
) becomes

(4.14)

\hat{\Psi}^{2}+\bar{\psi}_{z}^{2}=-2\left(\frac{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)^{2}e^{-3q}\sin(\zeta)\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}

and (
4.9
) turns into

(4.15)

\bar{\psi}_{z}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=0,\ \ \bar{\psi}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

In the next section we will linearize equations (
4.12
)-(
4.15
) near an appropriate solution of the model problem.

4.3.
Linearization near the Stokes corner flow

In order to determine the behaviour of
\bar{\psi}
near the positive infinity we need to examine the corresponding problem, which is obtained from (
4.12
)-(
4.15
) by setting
\zeta=-\tfrac{\pi}{6}
; one can also think about passing to the limit
q\to+\infty
for the coefficients in (
4.12
)-(
4.15
). Thus, taking the limit in (
4.11
) one obtains the relation
\hat{\Psi}=\tfrac{2}{3}\bar{\psi}_{q}
. Therefore, the limiting problem for
\bar{\psi}=\bar{U}
and
\hat{\Psi}=\tfrac{2}{3}\bar{U}_{q}
is the following

\displaystyle\tfrac{2}{3}\bar{U}_{qq}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\bar{U}_{zz},\ \

\displaystyle\text{for}\ \ (q,z)\in\mathbb{R}\times(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}),

\displaystyle\tfrac{4}{9}\bar{U}_{q}^{2}(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})+\bar{U}_{z}^{2}(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})=\tfrac{4}{9}e^{-3q},\ \

\displaystyle\text{for}\ \ q\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{z}(q,0)=\bar{U}(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})=0,\ \

\displaystyle\text{for}\ \ q\in\mathbb{R}.

Separating variables and solving the equations, we find two decaying solutions

\bar{U}_{+}(q,z)=\tfrac{2}{3}e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}\cos{z},\ \ \bar{U}_{-}(q,z)=-\tfrac{2}{3}e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}\cos{z}.

These are the candidates for the leading term in the asymptotics for
\bar{\psi}
. Note that
\bar{\psi}=\bar{U}_{+}
and
\zeta=-\tfrac{\pi}{6}
determine a solution known as the Stokes corner flow.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\bar{U}_{+}
be defined as above. Then

\bar{\psi}_{z}=(\bar{U}_{+})_{z}+o(1)e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2},

where
o(1)\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
. Furthermore, we have
\hat{\Psi}(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})=o(1)e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}
, where
o(1)\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
.

Proof.

The latter is a direct consequence of (
4.14
). Note that by the definition (
4.11
) of
\hat{\Psi}
we have

(4.16)

\hat{\Psi}=-\zeta_{q}\bar{\psi}_{z}\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

Using this relation in the Bernoulli equation (
4.14
) we obtain

\begin{split}(1+\zeta_{q}^{2})\bar{\psi}_{z}^{2}&=-2\left(\frac{\tfrac{\pi}{3}+(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6})}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)^{2}e^{-3q}\sin(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6}-\tfrac{\pi}{6})\\
&=-\tfrac{8}{9}(1+\tfrac{2}{\pi}(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6}))^{2}e^{-3q}\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\sin(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6})-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\cos(\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6}))\right).\end{split}

Now using the fact that
\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{6},\zeta_{q}\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
, we conclude that

(4.17)

\bar{\psi}_{z}^{2}=\tfrac{4}{9}e^{-3q}(1+o(1))\ \ \text{for}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

Note that
\bar{\psi}_{z}(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
is negative for large
q
, which follows from the first formula in (
4.7
) (since
\psi_{x}=-\eta_{x}\psi_{y}
at the boundary). Taking that into account we obtain the desired asymptotics for
\bar{\phi}_{z}
by taking the square root in (
4.17
).
∎

Lemma
4.2
shows that
\bar{\psi}
is close to
\bar{U}
at the boundary of the domain. As we will justify in next sections this information is enough to obtain higher order asymptotics for
\bar{\psi}
.

Now we can linearize equations for
\bar{\psi}
and
\hat{\Psi}
near
\bar{U}=\bar{U}_{+}
by setting

\bar{\psi}=\bar{U}+\bar{\Phi},\ \ \hat{\Psi}=\tfrac{2}{3}\bar{U}_{q}+\bar{\Psi},\ \ \zeta=-\tfrac{1}{6}\pi+\xi.

This leads to the following problem for
\bar{\Phi},\bar{\Psi}
and
\xi
, where we extract linear terms:

(4.18)

\displaystyle\bar{\Phi}_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}\bar{\Psi}+\frac{9}{2\pi}\bar{U}\xi+\frac{3}{\pi}z\bar{U}_{z}\xi_{q}+\bar{N}_{1},

(4.19)

\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}_{q}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\bar{\Phi}_{zz}-\frac{9}{2\pi}\bar{U}\xi-\frac{3}{\pi}(z\bar{U})_{z}\xi_{q}+\bar{N}_{2}.

Here

\begin{split}\bar{N}_{1}&=-\tfrac{3}{2}\frac{\xi}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\hat{\Psi}+\tfrac{3}{\pi}\frac{\xi^{2}}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\bar{U}_{q}+\frac{z\xi_{q}}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\bar{\Phi}_{z}-\tfrac{3}{\pi}\frac{z\bar{U}_{z}\xi_{q}}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\xi,\\
\bar{N}_{2}&=\frac{\xi_{q}}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}(z\bar{\Psi})_{z}+\tfrac{3}{\pi}\frac{(z\bar{U})_{z}\xi_{q}}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\xi+\tfrac{3}{2}\frac{\xi}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\bar{\Phi}_{zz}+\tfrac{9}{2\pi}\frac{\bar{U}\xi}{\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\xi+\tfrac{2}{\pi}e^{-2q}(\xi+\tfrac{\pi}{3})\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi}).\end{split}

The Bernoulli equation (
4.14
) becomes

2\bar{U}_{z}\bar{\Phi}_{z}-\tfrac{8}{3\pi}\xi e^{-3q}+\tfrac{4}{3\sqrt{3}}\xi e^{-3q}=\bar{N}_{3}

with

\begin{split}\bar{N}_{3}=&-\bar{\Psi}^{2}-\bar{\Phi}_{z}^{2}-2\left(\frac{\zeta+\tfrac{\pi}{2}}{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\right)^{2}e^{-3q}\left\{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(\sin\xi-\xi)+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\cos\xi)\right\}\\
&-\tfrac{8}{\pi^{2}}\left\{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\xi-\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right\}\xi^{2}e^{-3q},\end{split}

while

\bar{\Phi}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2},\ \ \bar{\Phi}_{z}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=0.

The linear part of this system can be significantly simplified by introducing new functions
\Phi
and
\Psi
from the relations

(4.20)

\bar{\Phi}=\Phi+\frac{3}{\pi}z\bar{U}_{z}\xi,\ \ \bar{\Psi}=\Psi-\frac{3}{\pi}(z\bar{U})_{z}\xi.

The latter transformation allows to eliminate linear terms with
\xi
and
\xi_{q}
that are not included in
\bar{N}_{1},\bar{N}_{2}
and
\bar{N}_{3}
. Thus, plugging (
4.20
) into (
4.18
)-(
4.19
), we obtain

(4.21)

\displaystyle\Phi_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}\Psi+N_{1},

(4.22)

\displaystyle\Psi_{q}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\Phi_{zz}+N_{2},

where

\begin{split}N_{1}&=-\frac{9}{2(\pi+3\xi)}\Psi\xi+\frac{3z}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}(\tfrac{9}{2}\bar{U}_{z}\xi^{2}-3z\bar{U}\xi\xi_{q})+\frac{3z}{(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi_{z}\xi_{q},\\
N_{2}&=\frac{2\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q}-\frac{27}{2\pi(\pi+3\xi)}(\bar{U}+z\bar{U}_{z})\xi^{2}+\frac{3\xi_{q}(z\Psi)_{z}}{(\pi+3\xi)}-\frac{9z(z\bar{U})_{zz}}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}\xi\xi_{q}+\frac{9\Phi_{zz}\xi}{2(\pi+3\xi)}.\end{split}

The Bernoulli equation takes the following form

(4.23)

\begin{split}2\bar{U}_{z}\Phi_{z}+\frac{4\sqrt{3}}{9}e^{-3q}\xi&=\frac{4\sqrt{3}}{9\pi^{2}}e^{-3q}(3\xi+\pi)^{2}(\sin\xi-\xi)\\
&+\frac{4}{9\pi^{2}}e^{-3q}(9\xi^{2}\cos\xi+(\cos\xi-1)(\pi^{2}+6\pi\xi))\\
&-(\Psi-\tfrac{3}{2}\bar{U}_{z}\xi)^{2}-(\tfrac{3}{\pi}\bar{U}_{z}\xi+\Phi_{z})^{2},\end{split}

while the boundary condition at the bottom is unchanged:

\bar{\Phi}_{z}=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=0.

The transformation (
4.20
) has another advantage. Using the homogenous boundary relation
\bar{\Phi}=0
at the surface one can recover
\xi
in terms of the new function
\Phi
as

(4.24)

\xi=-\tfrac{2}{3}(\bar{U}_{z})^{-1}\Phi=e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}\Phi\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}.

This allows to express

(4.25)

\begin{split}N_{1}=&-\frac{9\xi}{2(\pi+3\xi)}\Psi-\frac{9z\sin z\xi}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}-\frac{6z^{2}\cos z\xi_{q}}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}+\frac{3z\xi_{q}}{(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi_{z},\\
N_{2}=&-\frac{9\xi(\cos z-z\sin z)}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}+\frac{3\xi_{q}}{(\pi+3\xi)}(z\Psi)_{z}+\frac{6z(\cos z+z\sin z)\xi_{q}}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\\
&+\frac{9\xi}{2(\pi+3\xi)}\Phi_{zz}+\frac{2\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q}}{\pi}\Phi|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}+\tfrac{2}{3}\omega(1)e^{-2q}+\frac{2(\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})-\hat{\omega}(0))(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q}.\end{split}

Thus, except the last two terms in
N_{2}
, we can think
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
as linear operators of
\Phi
and
\Psi
. Below we will formalize this idea.

Similarly, we write (
4.23
) as

(4.26)

\Phi_{z}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\Phi=N_{3},

where

(4.27)

\begin{split}N_{3}=&\left(\left(\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{3}{\pi}\right)e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}\frac{(3\xi+\pi)^{2}(\sin\xi-\xi)}{\xi}-\frac{9\xi^{2}\cos\xi+(\cos\xi-1)(\pi^{2}+6\pi\xi)}{3\pi^{2}\xi}\right)\Phi\\
&+\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\Psi e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}+\tfrac{3}{2}\xi\right)\Psi+\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\Phi_{z}e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{3}{\pi}\xi\right)\Phi_{z}.\end{split}

As before it can be seen as a linear operator of
\Phi
and
\Psi
with decaying coefficients.

In what follows we will examine the decay rate of
\Phi
,
\Psi
and
\xi
. Note that Lemma
4.1
only shows that
\Phi
,
\Psi
are
O(e^{-(3/2)q})
, which is insufficient since this is the order of the leading term
\bar{U}
. Furthermore, we have no information about the decay rate of
\xi
and
\xi_{q}
. Such scant information makes the proof quite technical, though the idea is very clear. Let us outline the main steps of the proof.

Weak decay of
\Phi
and
\Psi
. This is the main idea of the proof. Roughly speaking the decay of
\Phi
and
\Psi
is determined by the forcing term with
\omega(1)
in the definition of
N_{2}
in (
4.25
). To formalize that we will define appropriate weighted spaces (with an exponential function as the weight) and will study the model linear operator

{\mathcal{L}}(\Phi,\Psi)=\left(\Phi_{q}-\tfrac{3}{2}\Psi,\Psi_{q}+\tfrac{3}{2}\Phi_{zz}\right).

Thus, using the invertibility of
{\mathcal{L}}
we will obtain that

\int_{q<q^{\prime}<q+1}\int_{0}^{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\Phi^{2}(q^{\prime},z)+\Phi^{2}_{z}(q^{\prime},z)+\Psi^{2}(q^{\prime},z)dzdq^{\prime}=O(e^{-2\beta q})

as
q\to+\infty
for any
\beta\in(\tfrac{3}{2},2)
. In particular this will give an exponential decay for
\xi
. An exponential decay for
\xi_{q}
and
\xi_{qq}
requires additional arguments.

An exponential decay of Hölder norms
\|\Phi\|_{C^{1,\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\pi/2])}
. For this purpose we will apply Shauder estimates for
\Phi
, solving the second-order elliptic boundary problem. The difficulty here is that we can not exclude
\xi_{q}
in terms of
\Phi_{q}
by using (
4.4
) since this would give
e^{(3/2)q}
as a coefficient. Instead of that we will show that
\|\xi_{q}\|_{C^{\gamma}([q,q+1])}\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
, which is enough to apply Shauder estimates (with the right-hand side in a divergence form). Next we will obtain a similar statement for the norms
\|\Phi\|_{C^{2,\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\pi/2])}
. This will give the decay for
\xi_{q}
and
\xi_{qq}
.

Higher order asymptotics.
Once we have the decay for
\Phi
and
\xi
and their derivatives we can obtain higher order asymptotics. The leading order term for
\Phi
is determined by the expression with
\omega(1)
in (
4.25
) and can be found explicitly, which gives

\Phi(q,z)=\tfrac{1}{12}(3-2\cos{(\tfrac{4}{3}z)})\hat{\omega}(0)e^{-2q}+\Phi_{err}(q,z).

Thus, we need to establish the decay properties for
\Phi_{err}
and this can be done the same way as before. Though an exponential decay for the function
\Phi_{err}
by itself can be obtained by using the invertibility of
{\mathcal{L}}
, the decay for higher order derivatives will require additional arguments.

4.4.
A weak decay for
\Phi
and
\Phi

It follows from (
4.2
) that
\Phi(q,\tfrac{\pi}{2})e^{(3/2)q}
decays to zero as
q\to+\infty
. Below we will prove the following statement:

Proposition 4.3
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
we have that

\|\Phi\|_{C^{1,\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])},\ \|\Psi\|_{C^{\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}=e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}o(1),

where
o(1)\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
.

This improves the statement of Lemma
4.2
. Before proving the claim we need to obtain a similar decay property for
\xi
:

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
the norms
\|\xi\|_{C^{1,1/2}([q,q+1])}
are uniformly bounded and

(4.28)

\|\xi\|_{C^{1,\gamma}([q,q+1])}\to 0

as
q\to+\infty
.

Proof.

To prove the claim it is enough to show that the norm
\|\xi_{q}\|_{C^{1/2}([q,q+1])}
is bounded by a constant independent of
q
. Indeed, assuming that is true we can use the inequality

\begin{split}\frac{|\xi_{q}(q_{1})-\xi_{q}(q_{2})|}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|^{\gamma}}&=\frac{|\xi_{q}(q_{1})-\xi_{q}(q_{2})|}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}|q_{1}-q_{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma}\\
&\leq\begin{cases}&\|\xi_{q}\|_{C^{\tfrac{1}{2}}([q,q+1])}\|\xi_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}[q,q+1]}^{\tfrac{1}{2}-\gamma},\ \ |q_{1}-q_{2}|\leq|\xi_{q}(q_{1})-\xi_{q}(q_{2})|\\
&2\|\xi_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}[q,q+1]}^{1-\gamma},\ \ |q_{1}-q_{2}|>|\xi_{q}(q_{1})-\xi_{q}(q_{2})|\end{cases}\end{split}

Thus, if
\|\xi_{q}\|_{C^{1/2}([q,q+1])}
are uniformly bounded, then the right-hand side from the above estimate tends to zero since we already known that
\xi_{q}\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
. Note that
\xi_{q}(q)=\zeta_{t}(q)
so we need to estimate the quantity
|\zeta_{t}(t_{1})-\zeta_{t}(t_{2})|
for arbitrary
t_{1},t_{2}\in[t,t+1]
. For that purpose we use the formula (
4.4
) to obtain

(4.29)

\begin{split}|\zeta_{t}(t_{1})-\zeta_{t}(t_{2})|&\leq C(|\nabla\psi(x_{1},\eta(x_{1}))-\nabla\psi(x_{2},\eta(x_{2}))|\\
&+C\max_{x_{1}\leq x\leq x_{2}}|\nabla\psi(x,\eta(x))||\zeta(t_{1})-\zeta(t_{2})|)e^{\tfrac{1}{2}t}.\end{split}

Here we used that the quantity

-\psi_{x}\sin{\zeta}+\psi_{y}\cos{\zeta}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}+\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}+o(1)\right)e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t},

where
o(1)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
. Note that
|\nabla\psi(x,\eta(x))|=O(e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t})
by Theorem
3.1
and

(4.30)

|\nabla\psi(x_{1},\eta(x_{1}))-\nabla\psi(x_{2},\eta(x_{2}))|\leq Ce^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t}|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.

Finally, since

|\zeta(t_{1})-\zeta(t_{2})|\leq C|t_{1}-t_{2}|

we obtain from (
4.29
) and (
4.30
) that

|\zeta_{t}(t_{1})-\zeta_{t}(t_{2})|\leq C|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.

This shows that (
4.28
) is true for
\xi_{q}
. The remaining estimate for
\xi
is now trivial.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
be given and assume that the claim is false so that there exists a sequence
\{q_{j}\}_{j=1}^{+\infty}
accumulating at
+\infty
and such that

(4.31)

\|\Phi\|_{C^{1,\gamma}([q_{j},q_{j}+1]\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}\geq e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q_{j}}\epsilon

for some
\epsilon>0
and all
j\geq 1
. Then we consider functions

\Phi^{(j)}(q,z)=\Phi(q+q_{j},z)e^{\tfrac{3}{2}(q+q_{j})},\ \ \Psi^{(j)}(q,z)=\Psi(q+q_{j},z)e^{\tfrac{3}{2}(q+q_{j})},\ \ \xi^{(j)}(q)=\xi(q+q_{j}).

Note that by the definition we have

\Phi=\bar{\psi}-\bar{U}-\tfrac{3}{\pi}z\bar{U}_{z}\xi

so that Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.4
give

(4.32)

\|\Phi^{(j)}\|_{C^{1,\tfrac{1}{2}}([0,+\infty)\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])},\ \|\Psi^{(j)}\|_{C^{\tfrac{1}{2}}([0,+\infty)\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])},\ \|\xi^{(j)}\|_{C^{1,\tfrac{1}{2}}([0,+\infty))}<+\infty.

Then by the compactness we can find a subsequence
\{j_{k}\}
such that functions
\Phi^{(j_{k})}
,
\Psi^{(j_{k})}
and
\xi^{(j_{k})}
converge in every space
C^{1,\gamma}(I\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])
,
C^{\gamma}(I\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])
and
C^{1,\gamma}(I)
respectively for all compact intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]
(though some finite number of functions may not be defined). We denote the limiting functions by
\Phi^{\infty}
,
\Psi^{\infty}
and
\xi^{\infty}
. Note that
\xi^{\infty}
is identically zero. These limiting functions are subject to certain equations that we will derive below. For this purpose we need to exploit a weak form of equations (
4.21
)-(
4.22
).

Let us multiply equations (
4.21
)-(
4.22
) by
e^{3/2q}
first, and then by some test functions
\varPhi(q+q_{j},z)
and
\varPsi_{j}(q+q_{j},z)
with compact supports. This leads to the equations

\begin{split}-\iint\Phi^{(j)}(q,z)\varPhi_{q}(q,z)dqdz&=\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\left\{\Phi^{(j)}(q,z)+\Psi^{(j)}(q,z)\right\}\varPhi(q,z)dqdz\\
&+\iint N_{1}(q,z)e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}\varPhi(q+q_{j},z)dqdz,\\
-\iint\Psi^{(j)}(q,z)\varPsi_{q}(q,z)dqdz&=\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\Psi^{(j)}(q,z)dqdz+\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\Phi^{(j)}_{z}(q,z)\varPsi_{z}(q,z)dqdz\\
&+\iint N_{2}(q,z)e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}\varPsi(q+q_{j},z)dqdz.\end{split}

The definitions of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
and (
4.32
) imply that the integral terms with
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
tend to zero as
j\to+\infty
. Thus, the limiting functions solve

\begin{split}-\iint\Phi^{\infty}(q,z)\varPhi_{q}(q,z)dqdz&=\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\left\{\Phi^{\infty}(q,z)+\Psi^{\infty}(q,z)\right\}\varPhi(q,z)dqdz\\
-\iint\Psi^{\infty}(q,z)\varPsi_{q}(q,z)dqdz&=\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\Psi^{\infty}(q,z)dqdz+\tfrac{3}{2}\iint\Phi^{\infty}_{z}(q,z)\varPsi_{z}(q,z)dqdz.\end{split}

This shows that
\Phi^{\infty}
and
\Psi^{\infty}
are infinitely smooth in
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\pi/2)
and
\Phi^{\infty}
is subject to

\Phi^{\infty}_{qq}-3\Phi^{\infty}_{q}+\tfrac{9}{4}\Phi^{\infty}+\tfrac{9}{4}\Phi^{\infty}_{zz}=0.

On the other hand
\Phi_{z}^{\infty}=0
on
z=0
, while
\Phi_{z}^{\infty}=\Phi^{\infty}=0
on
z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}
, which follows from (
4.26
) and Lemma
4.2
. Together this guarantees that
\Phi^{\infty}
is zero identically, though (
4.31
) shows the opposite, leading to a contradiction. In a similar way one proves the claim about
\Psi
.
∎

4.5.
Reduction to homogenous boundary conditions

For a further analysis of (
4.21
)-(
4.22
) we need to replace (
4.26
) by a homogenous relation (with
N_{3}=0
). For this purpose we introduce

(4.33)

\Phi^{\star}(q,z)=\Phi(q,z)+\tfrac{2}{\pi}\int_{z}^{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tau N_{3}^{\star}(q)(\Phi(q,\cdot),\Psi(q,\cdot))(\tau)d\tau,\ \ \Psi^{\star}(q,z)=\Psi(q,z),

where

\begin{split}N_{3}^{\star}(q)(\varPhi,\varPsi)=&\left(\left(\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{3}{\pi}\right)e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}\frac{(3\xi+\pi)^{2}(\sin\xi-\xi)}{\xi}-\frac{9\xi^{2}\cos\xi+(\cos\xi-1)(\pi^{2}+6\pi\xi)}{3\pi^{2}\xi}\right)\varPhi\\
&+\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\varPsi e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}+\tfrac{3}{2}\xi\right)\varPsi+\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\varPhi_{z}e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{3}{\pi}\xi\right)\varPhi_{z}.\end{split}

The main purpose of (
4.33
) is that the nonlinear boundary relation (
4.26
) is transformed to

(4.34)

\begin{split}\Phi^{\star}_{z}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\Phi^{\star}&=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=\tfrac{\pi}{2},\\
\Phi^{\star}_{z}&=0\ \ \text{on}\ \ z=0.\end{split}

Note that
N_{3}^{\star}(\Phi,\Psi)=N_{3}(\Phi,\Psi)
for
z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}
. At the same time
N_{3}^{\star}
is defined as a nonlinear operator from
H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
to
H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
. Thus, for all sufficiently large
q
our definition (
4.33
) determines a near-identical transformation in
H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
, which follows from Proposition
4.3
. This allows to express

(4.35)

(\Phi(q,z),\Psi(q,z))=(B^{(1)}(q)(\Phi^{\star}(q,\cdot),\Psi^{\star}(q,\cdot))(z),B^{(2)}(q)(\Phi^{\star}(q,\cdot),\Psi^{\star}(q,\cdot))(z)),

where

\begin{split}&B^{(1)}(q):H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\to H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}),\\
&B^{(2)}(q):H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\to H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\end{split}

are bounded nonlinear operators and their norms are uniformly bounded for large
q
.

Let us differentiate (
4.33
) with respect to
q
-variable, which leads to

(4.36)

\Phi^{\star}_{q}=\Phi_{q}+\tfrac{2}{\pi}\int_{z}^{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\tau\frac{\partial N_{3}^{\star}}{\partial q}(\Phi,\Psi)+N_{4}^{\star}d\tau,

where

\begin{split}N_{4}^{\star}=&\left(\left(\tfrac{3}{4}+\tfrac{3}{\pi}\right)e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}\pi^{2}}\frac{(3\xi+\pi)^{2}(\sin\xi-\xi)}{\xi}-\frac{9\xi^{2}\cos\xi+(\cos\xi-1)(\pi^{2}+6\pi\xi)}{3\pi^{2}\xi}\right)\Phi_{q}\\
&+\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\Psi e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}+\tfrac{3}{2}\xi e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}\right)\Psi_{q}+\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\Phi_{z}e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}-\tfrac{4}{\pi}e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}\right)\Phi_{zq}.\end{split}

Now we can replace
\Phi_{q}
and
\Psi_{q}
in the latter formula by using (
4.21
) and (
4.22
). Furthermore, in the corresponding expressions for
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
given by (
4.25
) we replace
\Phi
and
\Psi
by using (
4.35
). In the same way we replace all remaining occurrences of
\Phi
and
\Psi
in (
4.36
). After the same procedure for
\Psi^{\star}_{q}
we obtain that

(4.37)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\star}_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}\Psi^{\star}+N_{1}^{\star}(q)(\Phi^{\star}(q,\cdot),\Psi^{\star}(q,\cdot)),

(4.38)

\displaystyle\Psi_{q}^{\star}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\Phi_{zz}^{\star}+N_{2}^{\star}(q)(\Phi^{\star}(q,\cdot),\Psi^{\star}(q,\cdot))+f,

where
N_{1}^{\star}(q):H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\to H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
and
N_{2}^{\star}(q):H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\times H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\to L^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})
are bounded nonlinear operators for all sufficiently large
q
and the corresponding norms satisfy

(4.39)

\|N_{1}^{\star}(q)\|,\|N_{2}^{\star}(q)\|\to 0\ \ \text{as}\ \ q\to+\infty.

More precisely,
N_{1}^{\star}
,
N_{2}^{\star}
and
f
are given by

(4.40)

\begin{split}N_{1}^{\star}&=-\frac{9\xi}{2(\pi+3\xi)}B^{(2)}-\frac{9z\sin z\xi}{\pi+3\xi}(B^{(1)})|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}-\frac{6z^{2}\cos z\xi_{q}}{\pi+3\xi}(B^{(1)})|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}+\frac{3z\xi_{q}}{(\pi+3\xi)}(B^{(1)})_{z},\\
N_{2}^{\star}&=-\frac{9\xi(\cos z-z\sin z)}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}(B^{(1)})|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}+\frac{3\xi_{q}}{(\pi+3\xi)}(zB^{(2)})_{z}+\frac{6z(\cos z+z\sin z)\xi_{q}}{\pi(\pi+3\xi)}(B^{(1)})|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\\
&+\frac{9\xi}{2(\pi+3\xi)}(B^{(1)})_{zz}+\frac{2\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q}}{\pi}(B^{(1)})|_{z=\tfrac{\pi}{2}},\\
f&=\tfrac{2}{3}\omega(1)e^{-2q}+\frac{2(\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})-\hat{\omega}(0))(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q}.\end{split}

Note that the system (
4.37
)-(
4.38
) is equipped with the homogenious boundary relations (
4.34
).

4.6.
The model linear problem

At the positive infinity the nonlinear system (
4.37
)-(
4.38
) is reduced to the linear problem

\displaystyle\varPhi_{q}^{\star}=\tfrac{3}{2}\varPsi^{\star},

\displaystyle\varPsi_{q}^{\star}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\varPhi^{\star}_{zz},

where
\varPhi^{\star}
is subject to the boundary relations (
4.34
). The corresponding spaces for
\varPhi^{\star}
and
\varPsi^{\star}
are

\begin{split}&X_{1}=\{\varPhi\in H^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}):\ \varPhi_{z}(0)=0,\ \ \varPhi_{z}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\varPhi(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=0\},\\
&X_{2}=H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}).\end{split}

Furthermore, we define the range spaces as

Y_{1}=H^{1}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}),\ \ Y_{2}=L^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}).

Next we consider the corresponding linear operator
{\mathcal{L}}:W^{1,2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
given by

{\mathcal{L}}(\varPhi,\varPsi)=\left(\varPhi_{q}-\tfrac{3}{2}\varPsi,\varPsi_{q}+\tfrac{3}{2}\varPhi_{zz}\right).

For a given
\beta>0
and an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
we define weighted spaces
W^{1,2}_{\beta}(I;X_{1}\times X_{2})
and
L^{2}_{\beta}(I;Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
as subspaces of
W^{1,2}_{loc}(I;X_{1}\times X_{2})
and
L^{2}_{loc}(I;Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
of functions with finite norms

\begin{split}&\|x\|_{W^{1,2}_{\beta}(I)}=\left(\int_{I}e^{2\beta q}\left[\|x(q)\|_{X_{1}\times X_{2}}^{2}+\|D_{q}x(q)\|_{Y_{1}\times Y_{2}}^{2}\right]dq\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}\\
&\|x\|_{L^{2}_{\beta}(I)}=\left(\int_{I}e^{2\beta q}\|x(q)\|_{Y_{1}\times Y_{2}}^{2}dq\right)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Let us study the kernel of
{\mathcal{L}}:W^{1,2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R};Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
. Separating variables one finds that the kernel is spanned by the following functions

\varphi_{j}(z)e^{\pm\tfrac{3}{2}\tau_{j}q},\ \ j=0,1,2,...,

where
\varphi_{j}
and
\mu_{j}:=\tau_{j}^{2}
are the corresponding eigenpairs of the following Sturm–Liouville problem

(4.41)

-\varphi^{\prime\prime}=\mu_{j}\varphi,\ \ z\in(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2});\ \ \varphi_{z}(0)=0,\ \ \varphi_{z}(\tfrac{\pi}{2})-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\varphi(\tfrac{\pi}{2})=0.

The latter has a discrete spectrum accumulating at the positive infinity, while the first eigenvalue
\mu_{0}
is negative. The second eigenvalue
\mu_{1}=\tau_{1}^{2}
can be found as the smallest positive solution to

\tau_{1}=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\cot(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\tau_{1})

and is approximately given by

\tau_{1}\approx 1.8.

Note that the numbers
\tau_{j}
are the same as
1+\beta_{j}
in Amick and Fraenkel
[
2
]
, where similar asymptotics were studied in the infinite depth case.

Our proof will be based on the following basic fact about
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Proposition 4.5
.

The operator
{\mathcal{L}}:W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
is invertible for any
\beta>0
, provided
\beta\neq\tfrac{3}{2}\tau_{j}
,
j\geq 1
.

The statement follows directly from
[
13
, Theorem 2.4.1]
.

4.7.
A higher-order exponential decay

In order to employ Proposition
4.5
we need the following preliminary result.

Proposition 4.6
.

There exists
\beta_{0}\in(0,3/2)
such that
(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta_{0}}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
for some cut-off function
\chi
.

By a cut-off function we mean a smooth function
\chi(q)
such that
\chi(q)=1
for
q>q_{0}+1
and
\chi(q)=0
for
q<q_{0}
with some
q_{0}>0
.

Proof.

To prove the claim we apply Shauder type estimates to the system (
4.37
)-(
4.38
). Thus, for intervals
I=[q,q+1]
and
I_{1}=[q-1,q+1]
we apply
[
13
, Lemma 2.9.1]
and obtain a local estimate

\begin{split}\|(\Phi^{\star},\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}(I;X_{1}\times X_{2})}\leq C\Big\{&\|N_{1}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(I_{1};Y_{1})}+\|N_{2}^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(I_{1};Y_{2})}+\|f\|_{W^{1,2}(I_{1};Y_{2})}+\\
&\|\Phi^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(I_{1};L^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})))}+\|\Psi^{\star}\|_{L^{2}(I_{1};L^{2}(0,\tfrac{\pi}{2})))}\Big\}.\end{split}

Let
\chi
and
\beta_{0}\in(0,3/2)
be given. Then using the latter inequality we can estimate

(4.42)

\begin{split}\|(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}_{\beta_{0}}}^{2}&\leq C\int_{q_{0}-1}^{+\infty}\|(\Phi^{\star},\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}(I;X_{1}\times X_{2})}^{2}e^{2\beta_{0}q}dq\\
&\leq C\int_{q_{0}-1}^{+\infty}\max_{I_{1}}\left\{\|N_{1}^{\star}(q)\|^{2}+\|N_{2}^{\star}(q)\|^{2}\right\}\cdot\|(\Phi^{\star},\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}(I_{1};X_{1}\times X_{2})}^{2}\\
&+Ce^{-(\tfrac{3}{2}-\beta_{0})q_{0}}.\end{split}

Here we used the fact that

\|\Phi^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])},\|\Psi^{\star}\|_{L^{\infty}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}=O(e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}q}),

which is a consequence of Lemma (
4.1
). Note that in view of (
4.39
) we find from (
4.42
) that

\|(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}_{\beta_{0}}}^{2}\leq C+C\epsilon\|(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})\|_{W^{1,2}_{\beta_{0}}}^{2},

where
\epsilon\to 0
as
q_{0}\to+\infty
. Thus, choosing
\epsilon
to be small enough and subtracting the corresponding term we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Now we are ready to establish a higher-order weak decay for
\Phi^{\star}
and
\Psi^{\star}
. More precisely we prove

Proposition 4.7
.

For any
\beta\in(0,2)
there exists a cut-off function
\chi
such that
(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
.

Proof.

Let us multiply (
4.37
)-(
4.38
) by some cut-off function
\chi
and write the corresponding equations as

{\mathcal{L}}(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})-{\mathcal{N}}(\chi\Phi^{\star},\chi\Psi^{\star})=(\chi_{1},\chi_{2}+\chi f),

where
\chi_{1}
and
\chi_{2}
are cut-off functions and

f=\tfrac{2}{3}\omega(1)e^{-2q}+\frac{2(\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})-\hat{\omega}(0))(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q};

the nonlinear operator
N
is defined as

{\mathcal{N}}(\varPhi,\varPsi)=\big(\chi N_{1}^{\star}(\varPhi,\varPsi),\chi N_{2}^{\star}(\varPhi,\varPsi)\big).

Given
\beta\in(0,2)
the operator
{\mathcal{L}}:W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};Y_{1}\times Y_{2})
is invertible by Proposition
4.5
since the interval
(0,2)
is free from eigenvalues
\mu_{j}=(3/2)\tau_{j}
. On the other hand, the norm of the operator

{\mathcal{N}}:W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})\to L^{2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};Y_{1}\times Y_{2})

is small, provided
q_{0}
(in the definition of the cut-off function) is large enough. Therefore, the operator
{\mathcal{L}}-{\mathcal{N}}
is invertible in the latter spaces so that the equation

{\mathcal{L}}(\varPhi^{\star},\varPsi^{\star})-{\mathcal{N}}(\varPhi^{\star},\varPsi^{\star})=(\chi_{1},\chi_{2}+\chi f)

has a unique solution in
(\varPhi^{\star},\varPsi^{\star})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
. Finally, the unique solubility in
W^{1,2}_{\beta_{0}}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
(with
\beta_{0}
from Proposition
4.6
) and Proposition
4.6
give that
\varPhi^{\star}=\chi\Phi^{\star}
and
\varPsi^{\star}=\chi\Psi^{\star}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

It immediately follows from Proposition
4.7
that
(\chi\Phi,\chi\Psi)\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};H^{2}(0,\pi/2)\times H^{1}(0,\pi/2))
for any
\beta\in(0,2)
. This shows that

(4.43)

\Phi(q,z)=O(e^{-\beta q})\ \ \text{as}\ \ q\to+\infty

uniformly in
z
. Thus, we find from (
4.24
) that

(4.44)

\xi(q)=O(e^{-\beta_{0}q})

for any
\beta_{0}\in(0,1/2)
.

Proposition 4.8
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
and
\beta\in(0,2)
there exist
C>0
and
q_{0}>0
such that

\|\Phi\|_{C^{2,\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}\leq Ce^{-\beta q}

for all
q\geq q_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
and
0<\beta<2
be given. First we derive a second-order equation for the function
\Phi
by differentiating (
4.21
) and using (
4.22
) to replace
\Psi_{q}
. This gives

\Phi_{qq}+\tfrac{9}{2}\Phi=(N_{1})_{q}+\tfrac{3}{2}N_{2}.

Note that
N_{2}
is given in a divergence from:
N_{2}=(N_{2}^{\circ})_{z}
. For a given
q
we consider intervals
I=[q+1]
and
I_{1}=[q-1,q+1]
and apply Theorem 9.3 from
[
1
]
to conclude

(4.45)

\begin{split}\|\Phi\|_{C^{2,\gamma}(I\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}\leq C\Big\{&\|N_{1}\|_{C^{1,\gamma}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}+\|N_{2}\|_{C^{\gamma}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}+\\
&\|N_{3}\|_{C^{1,\gamma}(I_{1})}+\|\Phi\|_{L^{2}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}\Big\},\end{split}

where the constant is independent of
q
. To simplify the notations we will use the following convention:

\|\cdot\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(I_{1}\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}=\|\cdot\|_{k,\gamma;I_{1}},\ \ k=0,1,2.

Let us estimate the right-hand side in (
4.45
). First, we note that (
4.21
) implies

\|\Psi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\leq C\big\{\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I_{1}}+\|\xi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I_{1}}+\|\xi_{q}\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\|\Phi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\big\}.

On the other hand, we find from (
4.24
) that

\|\xi_{q}\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\leq\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I_{1}}e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}.

Note that
\|\Phi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}e^{\tfrac{3}{2}q}\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
by Proposition
4.3
, while
\|\xi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\to 0
by Lemma
4.4
so that

\|\Psi\|_{1,\gamma;I_{1}}\leq C\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I_{1}}.

In a similar fashion we estimate the right-hand side in (
4.45
) and conclude

(4.46)

\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I}\leq C\Big\{o(1)\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I_{1}}+e^{-\beta q}\Big\},

where
o(1)
is bounded and
o(1)\to 0
as
q\to+\infty
. Here we used (
4.43
) to estimate the corresponding
L^{2}
-norm. Taking into account that the norms
\|\Phi\|_{2,\gamma;I}
are uniformly bounded by (
4.1
) we conclude the desired estimate from (
4.46
) by the iteration.
∎

4.8.
Explicit asymptotics for
\Phi
and
\Psi

To find the next leading term for
\Phi^{\star}
and
\Psi^{\star}
we need to consider an inhomogeneous problem which is obtain from (
4.37
)-(
4.38
) by setting
N_{1}^{\star}
and
N_{2}^{\star}
to zero and
f
to
\tfrac{2}{3}\omega(1)e^{-2q}
. The corresponding equations are

\displaystyle U^{\star}_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}V^{\star},

\displaystyle V^{\star}_{q}=-\tfrac{3}{2}U^{\star}_{zz}+\tfrac{2}{3}\hat{\omega}(0)e^{-2q}.

Separating variables one finds a solution

U^{\star}=\tfrac{1}{12}(3-2\cos{(\tfrac{4}{3}z)})\hat{\omega}(0)e^{-2q}.

We will show below that
U^{\star}
determines
\Phi^{\star}
up to the leading order. For this purpose we put

\Phi^{\star}=U^{\star}+\Phi^{\dagger},\ \ \Psi^{\star}=\tfrac{2}{3}U^{\star}+\Psi^{\dagger}

and obtain from (
4.37
)-(
4.38
) equations for the next order terms
\Phi^{\dagger}
and
\Psi^{\dagger}
:

(4.47)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\dagger}_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}\Psi^{\dagger}+N_{1}^{\star}(q)(\Phi^{\dagger},\Psi^{\dagger})+f_{1}^{\dagger},

(4.48)

\displaystyle\Psi^{\dagger}_{q}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\Phi^{\dagger}_{zz}+N_{2}^{\star}(q)(\Phi^{\dagger},\Psi^{\dagger})+f_{2}^{\dagger},

where

f_{1}^{\dagger}=N_{1}^{\star}(q)(U^{\star},V^{\star}),\ \ f_{2}^{\dagger}=N_{2}^{\star}(q)(U^{\star},V^{\star})+\frac{2(\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})-\hat{\omega}(0))(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q}.

Lemma 4.9
.

For any
\beta\in(0,5/2)
there exists a cut-off function
\chi
such that
(\chi\Phi^{\dagger},\chi\Psi^{\dagger})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
.

Proof.

One argues the same way as in the proof of Proposition
4.7
. We only note that
(f_{1}^{\dagger},f_{2}^{\dagger})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
for any
\beta\in(0,5/2)
, while
5/2<(3/2)\tau_{1}
.
∎

Note that we can not apply Shauder estimates directly for the function
\Phi^{\dagger}
in order to establish a decay for the first- and second-order derivatives. Instead of that we express

(4.49)

\Phi=U^{\star}+\tilde{\Phi},\ \ \Psi=V^{\star}+\tilde{\Psi}.

It follows from (
4.33
) and Proposition
4.8
that
(\chi\tilde{\Phi},\chi\tilde{\Psi})\in W^{1,2}_{\beta}(\mathbb{R};X_{1}\times X_{2})
for any
\beta\in(0,5/2)
. On the other hand,
\tilde{\Phi}
and
\tilde{\Psi}
solve the problem

(4.50)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{q}=\tfrac{3}{2}\tilde{\Psi}+N_{1}(q)(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Psi})+\tilde{f}_{1},

(4.51)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}_{q}=-\tfrac{3}{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{zz}+N_{2}(q)(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Psi})+\tilde{f}_{2},

where

\tilde{f}_{1}=N_{1}(q)(U^{\star},V^{\star}),\ \ \tilde{f}_{2}=N_{2}(q)(U^{\star},V^{\star})+\frac{2(\hat{\omega}(\bar{\psi})-\hat{\omega}(0))(\pi+3\xi)}{3\pi}e^{-2q}.

Furthermore, we find form (
4.26
) that

\tilde{\Phi}_{z}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\tilde{\Phi}=N_{3}(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Psi})+\tilde{f}_{3},

where

\tilde{f}_{3}=N_{3}(U^{\star},V^{\star}).

Applying Shauder estimates as in Proposition
4.8
we conclude with

Proposition 4.10
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1/2)
and
\beta\in(0,2)
there exist
C>0
and
q_{0}>0
such that

\|\tilde{\Phi}\|_{C^{2,\gamma}([q,q+1]\times[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}])}\leq Ce^{-\beta q}

for all
q\geq q_{0}
.

In fact, we can specify the asymptotics for
\Phi
even further. For that purpose one needs to solve (
4.50
)-(
4.51
), where
N_{j}(q)(\tilde{\Phi},\tilde{\Psi})
are set to zero and
\tilde{f}_{j}
contain only terms of order
e^{-(5/2)q}
, where
\xi
is replaced by its approximation

\tilde{\xi}=\frac{\omega(1)}{3}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q},

which is obtained from (
4.24
). After a long and tedious calculation one solves reduced equations for
\tilde{\Phi}
and finds that

(4.52)

\xi(q)=\frac{\omega(1)}{3}e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}q}+\lambda\omega^{2}(1)e^{-q}+O(e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}(\tau_{1}-1)q}).

The constant
\lambda\approx 1.1869
is found numerically, though it can also be done analytically. Furthermore, similar asymptotics are valid for
\xi_{q}
and
\xi_{qq}
and can be obtained by differentiating the latter formula.

5.
Asymptotics for
\eta
and
\psi

Based on (
4.49
) we can obtain the corresponding expansions for
\eta_{x}
and
\psi
. First, using (
4.52
) and (
4.6
) we find

(5.1)

\begin{split}\eta_{x}&=\frac{\sin(-(\pi/6)+\xi)-\xi_{t}\cos(-(\pi/6)+\xi)}{\cos(-(\pi/6)+\xi)+\xi_{t}\sin(-(\pi/6)+\xi)}\\
&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{4}{3}\left(\xi-\xi_{t}\right)-\frac{4\left(-2\xi\xi_{t}+\xi_{t}^{2}+\xi^{2}\right)}{3\sqrt{3}}+O(\xi^{3}+\xi_{t}^{3})\\
&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{2}{3}\omega(1)e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t}-\frac{\sqrt{3}-24\lambda}{9}\omega^{2}(1)e^{-t}+O\left(e^{-\tfrac{3}{2}(\tau_{1}-1)t}\right).\end{split}

Note that

(5.2)

e^{-t}=\sqrt{x^{2}+(r-\eta(x))}=\frac{2}{3^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}x(1+O(x^{\tfrac{1}{2}})),

so that

\eta_{x}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\kappa\sqrt{x}+O(x),\ \ \kappa=\frac{2^{\tfrac{3}{2}}}{3^{\tfrac{5}{4}}}\omega(1).

We can use this information in order to specify the error term in (
5.2
), which gives

e^{-t}=\frac{2}{3^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}x\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{2\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{x}+O(x)\right),\ \ e^{-\tfrac{1}{2}t}=\frac{2^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}{3^{\tfrac{1}{4}}}\sqrt{x}\left(1-\frac{\kappa}{4\sqrt{3}}\sqrt{x}+O(x)\right).

Finally, using this in (
5.1
) we conclude

\eta_{x}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}+\kappa\sqrt{x}-\left\{\frac{1}{18}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sqrt{3}-24\lambda}{9}\right\}\omega^{2}(1)x+O\left(x^{-\tfrac{3}{2}(\tau_{1}-1)}\right)

as
x\to 0+
. Note that the coefficient

-\left\{\frac{1}{18}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\sqrt{3}-24\lambda}{9}\right\}\approx 3.37

is positive.

In a similar way one obtains asymptotics for the stream function
\psi
:

\psi(x,y)=1-\cos{\tfrac{3}{2}(\theta+\tfrac{\pi}{2})}(x^{2}+(r-y)^{2})^{3/2}+O(x^{2}+y^{2}),

where the next order terms can be found explicitly, although the formulas are more complicated.
