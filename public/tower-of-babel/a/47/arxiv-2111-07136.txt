The Tri-Pants Graph of the Twice-Punctured Torus
Katherine Betts, Troy Larsen, Jeffrey Utley, Avalon Vanis

Abstract
. We investigate the structure of the tri-pants graph, a simplicial graph introduced by Maloni and Palesi, whose vertices correspond to particular collections of homotopy classes of simple closed curves of the twice-punctured torus, called tri-pants, and whose edges connect two vertices whenever the corresponding pants differ by suitable elementary moves. In particular, by examining the relationship between the tri-pants graph and the dual of the Farey complex, we prove that the tri-pants graph is connected and it has infinite diameter.



Introduction

Let
T^{2}
be a fixed orientable closed surface of genus
1
, and let
\bullet,\circ
be two distinct marked points of
T^{2}
. We will call the
twice-punctured torus
the punctured surface
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. The aim of this paper is to study the structure of the
tri-pants graph
, a simplicial graph introduced by Maloni and Palesi and defined in terms of particular sets of essential simple closed curves on the twice punctured torus, called
tri-pants
. A tri-pant can be described as a collection
T=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}
of six distinct homotopy classes of non-separating, essential, simple closed curves on the twice-punctured torus that satisfy the following properties:

(1)

every pair
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
,
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}
,
\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}
describes a
pants decomposition
of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
, i.e. it can be represented by disjoint simple closed curves that cut the surface into two once-punctured annuli;

(2)

every pair of homotopy classes of curves belonging to distinct pants decompositions have minimal intersection number equal to
1
.

We say that two tri-pants
T
and
T^{\prime}

differ by an elementary move
if they share two out of three pants decompositions. The tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
can then be described as the simplicial graph that has a vertex for each tri-pant of the twice-punctured torus, and has an edge joining two vertices when the corresponding tri-pants differ by an elementary move.

The tri-pants graph can be considered as a higher complexity analogue of a similar simplicial graph defined in terms of systems of curves on the once-punctured torus, namely the
dual of the Farey complex

\mathcal{F}^{*}
, in light of the following observations: a pants decomposition of the once-punctured torus is given by (the homotopy class of) a single essential simple closed curve; each vertex of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
can be interpreted as a triple of homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves intersecting exactly once, and therefore as the data of three pants decompositions with minimal intersection; two vertices of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
are connected by an edge whenever the corresponding triples of homotopy classes differ by exactly one element. The first result that we will describe concerns the topology of the tri-pants graph. In particular, we will prove:

Theorem A
.

The tri-pants graph is connected.

Equivalently, every pair of tri-pants of the twice-punctured torus is connected by a finite sequence of elementary moves.

In fact, we can provide a more detailed description of the structure of the tri-pants graph in terms of a natural projection
\pi:\mathcal{TP}\to\mathcal{F}^{*}
onto the dual of the Farey complex. Let
\eta:T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}\to T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
denote the natural inclusion map. It turns out that, if
T=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}
is a tri-pant, then the images of the curves in
T
under
\eta
provide a triple of essential simple closed curves of the once-punctured torus that intersect exactly once, up to homotopy. In particular, there is a natural projection from the set of vertices of
\mathcal{TP}
into
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. As we will describe in Section 3, adjacent vertices of the tri-pants graph are mapped under the projection onto vertices of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
that are either equal, or adjacent. This allows us to construct a continuous surjective map
\pi:\mathcal{TP}\to\mathcal{F}^{*}
between the graphs, naturally associated to the inclusion
\eta:T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}\to T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. We will deduce Theorem
A
from the following result:

Theorem B
.

For every vertex
v
of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(v)
is a connected subgraph of
\mathcal{TP}
.

Our proof of Theorem
B
relies on the properties of the
pure mapping class class group

\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})
, the group of homeomorphisms of the torus that fix the set
\{\circ,\bullet\}
pointwise, up to isotopy relative to the punctures. In particular, the main technical tool that we will deploy is the Birman’s exact sequence
[
1
]
(see also
[
3
]
), which relates the pure mapping class group of the twice-punctured torus to the mapping class group of the once-punctured torus.

Theorem
B
allows us to deduce information also about the metric structure of the tri-pants graph. If
T
and
T^{\prime}
are two vertices of
\mathcal{TP}
, we define their distance to be the minimum number of elementary moves required to go from
T
to
T^{\prime}
. With respect to such metric, we will show the following:

Theorem C
.

The tri-pants graph has infinite diameter.

Both Theorems
A
and
C
extend properties that are well known for the dual of the Farey complex to the case of the tri-pants graph. Additional analogies can be drawn between the two graphs, in particular in relation to the topological interpretation of their edges.

A vertex of the dual of the Farey complex can be interpreted as the data of an
ideal triangulation
of the once-punctured torus
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, namely a triangulation of
T^{2}
with exactly one vertex at the puncture
\circ
. Two vertices of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
are then connected by an edge if and only if the associated ideal triangulations differ by a diagonal exchange. A similar while more sophisticated phenomenon happens also for the tri-pants graph. The data of a tri-pant turns out to be equivalent to a system of three homotopy classes of essential simple arcs on the twice-punctured torus based at the puncture
\bullet
and satisfying certain topological constraints. In the study of
\mathcal{TP}
, we will observe that two tri-pants
T
and
T^{\prime}
differ by an elementary move if and only if their corresponding tri-arcs differ by suitable elementary flips. In this case, the elementary flips can be divided into two types in relation to the map
\pi
: flips between the tri-arcs associated to two adjacent tri-pants
T
and
T^{\prime}
are called
big flips
when
\pi(T)=\pi(T^{\prime})
, while are called
small flips
when
\pi(T)
and
\pi(T^{\prime})
are distinct adjacent vertices in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. Theorem
B
can then equivalently phrased as: every pair of tri-pants
T
and
T^{\prime}
lying in the same fiber of
\pi
have associated tri-arcs that differ by a finite sequence of big flips.

As a major difference between the two graphs and their topological structure, we will show that
\mathcal{TP}
has non-trivial simple closed loops, phenomenon that does not occur in the dual of the Farey complex, which is a infinite tri-valent tree. As a bi-product of our study, we will also provide an alternative characterization of the notion of tri-pant:

Theorem D
.

A set of homotopy classes of non-separating essential simple closed curves on the twice-punctured torus that intersect at most once is maximal with respect to the inclusion if and only if it is a tri-pant.

In particular, every set of homotopy classes of non-separating essential simple closed curves on the twice-punctured torus that intersect at most once has cardinality
\leq 6
, and it is equal to
6
if and only if it is a tri-pant.

An underlying motivation for the introduction of the tri-pants graph and the investigation of its properties comes from the connection between the notion of tri-pant and the character variety of
\mathbb{F}_{3}\cong\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})
, the free group in
3
generators, into
\textrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. In particular, the tri-pants graph constitutes a promising analogue of the decorated versions of the complex of curves deployed in the works of Bowditch, Maloni, Palesi, Tan, and Yang (see
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
7
]
) in the study of the action of the mapping class group of
S
on the
\textrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
-character variety
\mathfrak{X}(\pi_{1}(S),\textrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}))
, for
S
equal to the once-punctured torus, the four-holed sphere, and the three-holed punctured plane.

Outline of the paper.
We rely on numerous definitions and structures to analyze the tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
. In Section
1
we provide preliminary insights on closed curves, proper arcs, and their respective homotopies, outlining some of their properties and introducing a standard notation.

Section
2
is dedicated to the study of pants decompositions of the twice-punctured torus, and the notion of tri-pant. Throughout our exposition, a pants decomposition will always be assumed to be composed of
non-separating
simple closed curves. We then describe the notion of
tri-arcs
of the twice-punctured torus in Section
2.3
, and we illustrate a one-to-one correspondence between tri-pants and tri-arcs in Lemma
2.17
. Section
2.4
will then focus on the characterization of tri-pants as maximal families of non-separating simple closed curves that intersect at most once, as described by Theorem
D
.

The study of the tri-pants graph and the proofs of Theorems
B
,
A
, and
C
are the central subjects of Section
3
. We first recall the main properties of the Farey complex and its dual graph
\mathcal{F}^{*}
in Section
3.1
. Section 3 focuses on the investigation of elementary moves between tri-pants and the construction of the tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
. By means of the bijective correspondence between tri-arcs and tri-pants described by Lemma
2.17
, we characterize elementary moves in terms of small and big flips between tri-arcs (see Section
3.2.1
). We show that every vertex of
\mathcal{TP}
has degree 9 in Lemma
3.11
, and introduce the projection map
\pi:\mathcal{TP}\to\mathcal{F}^{*}
in Section
3.2.3
. In the final part of the paper (Section
3.2.4
) we recall the properties of the pure mapping class group of the twice-punctured torus and its associated Birman’s exact sequence, leading us to the proof of the connectedness of the fibers of the map
\pi
(Theorem
B
). We conclude our exposition by showing that the tri-pants graph is connected (Theorem
A
) and it has infinite diameter (Theorem
C
).

1.
Preliminaries

1.1.
Curves and arcs on the twice-punctured torus

In this paper, we focus on how curves behave within punctured surfaces. We define a surface with punctures to be as follows.

Definition 1.1

(Surface with Punctures)
.

Let
\overline{S}
be a compact, connected, orientable surface, possibly with boundary, and let
P
be a finite set of marked points in the interior of
\overline{S}
. Then
S=\overline{S}\setminus P
is a
surface with punctures
and is often also denoted
(\overline{S},P)
.

Throughout the remainder of this paper, we will use the term “surface” to mean a compact, orientable, connected surface possibly with punctures and with (possibly empty) boundary. We define homotopy of closed curves in the following way, in order to specify how
\alpha
and
\beta
relate or differ in both surfaces:

Definition 1.2

(Homotopy of Closed Curves)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta:S^{1}\to S
be two closed curves in a surface
S
. We say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
homotopic as closed curves
if there exists a continuous map
F:[0,1]\times S^{1}\to S
that satisfies the following:

(1)

For every
s\in S^{1}
, we have
F(0,s)=\alpha(s)
.

(2)

For every
s\in S^{1}
, we have
F(1,s)=\beta(s)
.

When two curves
\alpha
and
\beta
are homotopic, we write
\alpha\simeq\beta
. Part of our motivation in studying punctured surfaces comes from understanding these homotopy classes. We now define some relevant properties of closed curves for the purposes of our paper.

Definition 1.3

(Properties of Closed Curves)
.

Let
\alpha:S^{1}\to S
be a closed curve in any surface
S
.

(a)

\alpha
is
simple
if (the map) is injective. Items (b)-(d) assume that
\alpha
is simple.

(b)

\alpha
is
parallel to the boundary
if it is homotopic to one of the boundary components.

(c)

A simple closed curve
\alpha
is
essential
if it does not bound a disk or a punctured disk and is not parallel to the boundary.

(d)

A simple closed curve
\alpha
is
separating
if
S\setminus\alpha(S^{1})
is not connected and
\alpha
is
non-separating
if
S\setminus\alpha(S^{1})
is connected.

Definition 1.4

(Isotopy of Closed Curves)
.

Let
\alpha
,
\beta
be simple closed curves such that
\alpha\simeq\beta
, and let
F:[0,1]\times S^{1}\to S
be the continuous map such that
F(0,s)=\alpha(s),F(1,s)=\beta(s)
for every
s\in S^{1}
. If
F(t,\cdot)
is injective for all
t\in[0,1]
, then we say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
isotopic as closed curves
.

As outlined in the following section, we will be able to define a one-to-one correspondence between specific pairs of homotopy classes of essential, non-separating simple closed curves on the twice-punctured torus
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
and homotopy classes of essential simple arcs on the once-punctured torus, based at the puncture. This will give us a better intuition of how these curves behave within our surface.
1
Thus, we define proper arcs within punctured surfaces as well as their properties and the notion of homotopy of arcs.

Definition 1.5

(Proper Arcs)
.

Let
S
be a surface, with set of punctures
P
. A
proper arc
in
S
(or in
(\overline{S},P)
) is a continuous map
a:[0,1]\to\overline{S}
such that
a^{-1}(P\cup\partial S)=\{0,1\}
.

Definition 1.6

(Homotopy of Arcs)
.

Let
S=(\overline{S},P)
be a surface with punctures
P
. Let
a,b:[0,1]\to\overline{S}
be two proper arcs. We say that
a
and
b
are
homotopic as proper arcs
if there is a continuous map
F:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\overline{S}
that satisfies the following:

(1)

F(t,\cdot)
is a proper arc for all
t\in[0,1]
.

(2)

F(0,s)=a(s)
for every
s\in[0,1]
.

(3)

F(1,s)=b(s)
for every
s\in[0,1]
.

We will continue to use the notation
a\simeq b
if
a
and
b
are homotopic as proper arcs. Additionally, we say that
a
and
b
are
isotopic as proper arcs
if
F(t,\cdot)
is injective for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Definition 1.7

(Properties of Arcs)
.

Let

a:[0,1]\to\overline{S}
be a proper arc in a surface
S
.

(a)

a
is called
simple
if
\alpha|_{(0,1)}
is injective. Items (b)-(c) assume that
\alpha
is simple.

(b)

A simple proper arc
a
is
parallel to the boundary
if it is homotopic into a boundary component.

(c)

A simple proper arc
a
is
essential
if it is not parallel to the boundary, not homotopic to a marked point of
S
, and does not bound a punctured disk.

(d)

If
b:[0,1]\to\overline{S}
is also a simple proper arc and
F:[0,1]\times[0,1]\to\overline{S}
is a homotopy from
a
to
b
such that
F(t,\cdot)
is injective for all
t\in[0,1]
then
a
and
b
are isotopic as proper arcs.

A note on notation

For the sake of consistency and transparency throughout the remainder of this paper, we feel the need to outline a standard notation for curves and arcs. When discussing curves, we make use of the Greek alphabet. We denote homotopy classes of simple closed curves with unaccented characters;
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
, for example. We indicate simple representatives of these classes by “hatting” their characters. For instance, we may choose
\hat{\alpha}
as a simple representative of the set
\alpha
. A similar pattern occurs when describing arcs, though we make use of the Latin alphabet. We denote homotopy classes of proper arcs by
a
,
b
,
c
, for example, and choose their respective simple representatives
\hat{a}
,
\hat{b}
, and
\hat{c}
. Additionally, although closed curves and proper arcs are defined to be continuous maps, when referencing either object we will identify the map with its image. That is, for a proper arc
\hat{a}:[0,1]\to\overline{S}
and a closed curve
\hat{\alpha}:S^{1}\to\overline{S}
, we will let
a
denote the image
\hat{a}[0,1]
and
\hat{\alpha}
denote the image
\hat{\alpha}(S^{1})
.

Definition 1.8

(Intersection Number)
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be homotopy classes of simple closed curves in the torus
T^{2}
. For representatives
\hat{\alpha}\in\alpha,\hat{\beta}\in\beta
, define
n(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})
to be the number of times
\hat{\alpha}
and
\hat{\beta}
intersect. Then we define the
(geometric) intersection number
of
\alpha
and
\beta
by

i(\alpha,\beta):=\min\{n(\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta})\>|\>\hat{\alpha}\in\alpha,\hat{\beta}\in\beta\}.

Thus, the intersection number is the minimum number of times that representatives of the two classes intersect. More results on intersection number can be found in
[
3
]
.

Now that we have compiled a list of important properties of how closed curves and proper arcs behave within a punctured surface, we introduce the surface that will be the focal point of the remainder of our paper: the twice-punctured torus.

Definition 1.9

(Twice Punctured Torus)
.

Let
T^{2}
be the torus, and let
P=\{\bullet,\circ\}
be a set of two marked points in the interior of
T^{2}
. We define
S=T^{2}\setminus P
to be the twice-punctured torus and denote it as
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
.

2.
(Tri-)Pants and Decompositions

2.1.
Pants decompositions

In this section, we investigate the relationship between the twice-punctured torus and collections of pairs of pants through the construction of pants decompositions. We will first present formal definitions of pants decompositions and then proceed to outline some of their important properties.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S_{g,b,n}
denote the homeomorphism class of surfaces with genus
g
, number of boundary components
b
, and number of punctures
n
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
pair of pants
is a surface
P
in the homeomorphism class
S_{0,b,n}
, where
b+n=3
.

In most extant literature,
P
has three boundary components and no punctures. We extend this definition by allowing punctures to take the place of boundary components. In this less rigid construction, the sum of boundary components and punctures must equal three. Note that a pair of pants
P
does not contain any non-separating simple closed curves, nor does it contain an essential simple closed curve
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.3

(Pants Decomposition)
.

A
pants decomposition
of a surface
S
is a collection of disjoint simple closed curves
(\gamma_{i})_{i}
so that every connected component of
S\setminus\bigcup_{i}\gamma_{i}
is homeomorphic to the interior of a pair of pants.

Note that we say that a collection
(\alpha_{i})_{i}
of homotopy classes of simple closed curves give a pants decomposition of
S
if there exists a set of representatives from each class
(\hat{\alpha}_{i})_{i}
which form a pants decomposition of the surface.

This definition is equivalent to saying that a pants decomposition is a maximal collection of disjoint essential simple closed curves in
S
that are pairwise non-homotopic, as proven in Section 8.3.1 of
[
3
]
. Although a pants decomposition may include separating curves in other settings, we will focus only on pants decompositions composed of non-separating curves in this paper. It is important to note that a surface admits a pants decomposition if and only if
\chi(S)<0
. This result follows from the fact that
\chi(S)=-n
for any surface
S
which may be decomposed into
n
disjoint pairs of pants, as shown in Section 8.3.1 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
S
be a surface so that
\chi(S)<0
and with genus
g
,
b
boundary components, and
n
punctures. Any pants decomposition of
S
divides the surface into
2g-2+b+n
pairs of pants and consists of
3g-3+b+n
curves.

A proof of Lemma
2.4
(among many other results pertaining to pants decompositions) can be found in
[
3
]
. From now on, this paper will primarily focus on the twice-punctured torus. Lemma
2.4
shows that a pants decomposition of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
consists of exactly two curves and splits
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
into two pairs of pants. We will further restrict our study of pants decompositions to only those satisfying the following definition.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all pairs of distinct homotopy classes of non-separating, essential simple closed curves
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
such that
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
determines a pants decomposition.

Lemma 2.6
.

Every pants decomposition
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
separates the twice-punctured torus into two surfaces, both homeomorphic to a once-punctured annulus.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
, we may be sure that a pants decomposition of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
consists of two homotopy classes
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
and divides the surface into two pairs of pants,
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
. For sake of contradiction, suppose that
P_{1}
contains both
\bullet
and
\circ
. Then
P_{1}
must have exactly one boundary component as
b(P_{1})+n(P_{1})=3
. Since
b(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})=0
, this boundary component arises from cutting along a representative of
\alpha
or
\alpha^{\prime}
. Without loss of generality, assume that it arises from cutting along
\hat{\alpha}\in\alpha
. However, this implies that
\hat{\alpha}
is separating, contradicting the fact that
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
. Then
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
each must contain exactly one of the punctures, since applying the same argument to
P_{2}
gives the same contradiction. Therefore
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
each have two boundary components and one puncture, and thus both are homeomorphic to a punctured annulus.
∎

.

T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
that determine a pants decomposition.

Corollary 2.7
.

Suppose that two curves
\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
determine a pants decomposition of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. Then
\hat{\alpha}\simeq\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
when viewed as curves on
T^{2}
, but
\hat{\alpha}\not\simeq\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
on the twice-punctured torus.

This corollary will be crucial in our study of tri-pants and follows directly from Lemma
2.6
. Further information about pants decompositions and most omitted proofs from this section can be found in Section 8.3.1 of
[
3
]
. For the following lemmas, we define
\eta:T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}\to T^{2}
to be the inclusion map and
\eta_{*}
the induced map the sends homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
to those in
T^{2}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be homotopy classes in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
such that
i(\alpha,\beta)<2
. Then
i(\alpha,\beta)=i(\eta_{*}(\alpha),\eta_{*}(\beta))
.

Proof.

We approach this claim from the contrapositive. Let
\alpha,\beta
be distinct homotopy classes in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
and let
\hat{\alpha}\in\alpha
and
\hat{\beta}\in\beta
be representatives of these classes that are in minimal position. Suppose that
i(\eta_{*}(\alpha),\eta_{*}(\beta))\neq i(\alpha,\beta)
. Then
\eta\circ\hat{\alpha}
and
\eta\circ\hat{\beta}
must bound a bigon on
T^{2}
in order to reduce intersection number under
\eta_{*}
without changing either homotopy class. It follows that
\hat{\alpha}
and
\hat{\beta}
bound a punctured bigon on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
, and
i(\alpha,\beta)\geq 2
. We thus conclude that if
i(\alpha,\beta)<2
, then
i(\alpha,\beta)=i(\eta_{*}(\alpha),\eta_{*}(\beta))
.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

If
X
is a set of homotopy classes of essential non-separating simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
that pairwise intersect at most once, then for any homotopy class of simple closed curves
\alpha\in T^{2}
, the cardinality of
\{\beta\in X|\eta_{*}(\beta)=\alpha\}
does not exceed 2.

Proof.

First, let
\delta
be a homotopy class of simple closed curves in
T^{2}
and define the set
x=\eta_{*}^{-1}(\{\delta\})\cap X
. Proving our desired result is equivalent to proving that
card(x)\leq 2
. It follows from Definition
1.3
that non-separating closed curves are essential in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
if and only if they are in
T^{2}
. Thus, if
\delta
is not a homotopy class of essential curves, then
card(x)=0
. Therefore, we assume that
\delta
is a class of essential curves. For sake of contradiction, assume
x=\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
. Choose
\hat{\alpha}
,
\hat{\beta}
and
\hat{\gamma}
as their respective representatives so that they pairwise intersect minimally. Using the previous lemma, we claim that
i(\alpha,\beta)=i(\beta,\gamma)=i(\alpha,\gamma)=0
. Indeed,
i(\alpha,\beta)=i(\eta_{*}(\alpha),\eta_{*}(\beta))=i(\delta,\delta)=0
. Thus,
\hat{\alpha},\hat{\beta},
and
\hat{\gamma}
are disjoint. Cutting
T^{2}
along
\eta\circ\hat{\alpha}
and
\eta\circ\hat{\beta}
thus yields two annuli, which we will denote
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\eta\circ\hat{\gamma}\subset A_{1}
. Since
\eta\circ\hat{\alpha}\simeq\eta\circ\hat{\beta}\simeq\eta\circ\hat{\gamma}
, we see that
\eta\circ\hat{\gamma}
is parallel to the boundary of
A_{1}
. Then cutting along
\eta\circ\hat{\gamma}
separates
A_{1}
into two annuli, and we see that cutting along
\eta\circ\hat{\alpha}
,
\eta\circ\hat{\beta}
, and
\eta\circ\hat{\gamma}
separates
T^{2}
into three annuli. Note that if we remove exactly two points from this disjoint union of annuli, then at least one of the annuli will remain unchanged. Thus, we can conclude that at least two of these curves bound an annulus in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
and are therefore homotopic in this surface. Therefore, at least two of the homotopy classes in
x
have to be identical, so
card(x)\leq 2
.
∎

2.2.
Tri-Pants and their constructions

As their names suggest, the study of tri-pants and the tri-pants graph are inherently related; specifically, one cannot expect to digest any results pertaining to the tri-pants graph without first comprehending tri-pants. In this section, we outline the criteria satisfied by tri-pants, describe some of their properties, and begin to discuss the relationships between differing tri-pants.

Definition 2.10
.

A
tri-pant
on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
is a collection of six homotopy classes of simple closed curves
T=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}
satisfying the following criteria:

(1)

All curves in
T
are essential and non-separating.

(2)

Without loss of generality, we have
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
,
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}
,
\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
.

(3)

For every pair of distinct elements
\zeta,\xi\in T
, we have
i(\zeta,\xi)=1
unless
\{\zeta,\xi\}
is equal to
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
,
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}
, or
\{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\}
.

Another note on notation

We denote tri-pants by
T
and
T^{\prime}
most frequently. Later, in Section
2.3
, we define tri-arcs to be analogous sets of homotopy classes of proper arcs. To illustrate the bijection between tri-pants and tri-arcs (which we will investigate fully), we indicate tri-arcs with an asterisk in subscript;
T_{*}
and
T_{*}^{\prime}
thus appear most frequently in our paper. Lastly, in Section
3.2
, we introduce an oriented tri-arc which we label
T_{\pi_{1}},T_{\pi_{1}}^{\prime}
to emphasize that this object is a subset of the fundamental group of the surface.

Now that we have built more intuition on the properties of a tri-pant, we see that there are infinitely many choices of a tri-pant on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. At times, it can seem daunting to differentiate between them, so for the next portion of this paper we discuss similarity.

Definition 2.11
.

Two tri-pants
T
and
T^{\prime}

differ by an elementary move
if they share two pants decompositions.

While this definition gives us a concrete way to relate two tri-pants, it can be difficult to check if this property holds for a wide variety of reasons. If we view
T
and
T^{\prime}
pictorially, the number (and complexity) of the curves often clouds our understanding. Alternatively, if we view
T
and
T^{\prime}
algebraically as elements of the fundamental group of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
, we quickly encounter the difficulties of working in a more complicated group structure. Thus, we seek simpler, but equally valid, representations of tri-pants to ease our study of their properties. In the next section, we outline one such representation and its relationship with tri-pants.

2.3.
The Tri-Arc presentation

Is there a way to represent tri-pants that simultaneously simplifies their pictorial and algebriac analysis? In this section, we answer this question with a resounding “yes!” by introducing tri-arcs to our discussion. We will outline a one-to-one correspondence between pants decompositions in
\mathcal{P}
and isotopy classes of simple, essential, unoriented arcs of the twice-punctured torus based at
\bullet
. In the process of studying tri-arcs, we reduce the number of pictorial components from six to three and reduce the complexity of working in the fundamental group by a factor of
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition 2.12
.

Define
\mathcal{A}
to be the set of isotopy classes of essential, unoriented simple arcs on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
based at
\bullet
(that is,
a(0)=a(1)=\bullet
).

Definition 2.13
.

We say that two homotopy (or isotopy) classes of arcs
a
and
b
based at the same point in a surface
S

bound a cylinder
if there exist representatives
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
so that a connected component of
S\setminus(\hat{a}\cup\hat{b})
is homeomorphic to
S^{1}\times(0,1)
, the interior of a cylinder.

Definition 2.14
.

A
tri-arc

T_{*}=\{a,b,c\}
is a collection of three distinct elements of
\mathcal{A}
that pairwise intersect only at
\bullet
and do not bound a cylinder in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
.

We now begin to investigate the relationship between tri-pants and tri-arcs. Define the map
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{P}
where
a\mapsto\{\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}\}
as follows: for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, choose a simple representative
\hat{a}
and consider the simple closed curve
\hat{a}\cup\{\bullet\}\subset T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. Take an open tubular neighborhood
N_{\hat{a}}
of
\hat{a}\cup\{\bullet\}
homeomorphic to
S^{1}\times(0,1)
. Then
\partial N_{\hat{a}}\subset T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
consists of two simple closed curves
\hat{\alpha}_{a},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{a}
, by the construction of
N_{\hat{a}}
. We define
\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}
to be the homotopy classes (as simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
) of
\hat{\alpha}_{a},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{a}
, respectively.

Claim 2.15
.

We claim that
\{\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}\}
is an element of
\mathcal{P}
, independent of the chosen neighborhood of
a
(see e. g.
[
4
, Theorem 5.3]
for the uniqueness of tubular neighborhoods up to isotopy), and the map
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{P}
where
a\mapsto\{\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}\}
is a bijection. A proof of this statement follows from the two subsequent lemmas.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then
\Phi(a)=\{\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}\}
is an element of
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

We must first prove that there exist representatives
\hat{\alpha}_{a},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{a}
of
\alpha_{a},\alpha_{a}^{\prime}
, respectively, which are non-separating and non-peripheral. To do this, first fix a representative
\hat{a}\in a
. Note that there is only one homotopy class of non-trivial simple arcs in the punctured annulus
N_{\hat{a}}\setminus\{\bullet\}
, based at
\bullet
. We may take a simple representative
\hat{a}
and then define the simple closed curve
\tilde{a}:=\hat{a}\cup\{\bullet\}\subset T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, which is homotopic to both representatives
\hat{\alpha}_{a}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. Since
\hat{a}
is essential as an arc in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
, it does not bound a disk or a punctured disk within this surface, implying that
\tilde{a}
is also essential as a simple closed curve in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
and therefore
\hat{\alpha}_{a},\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
are as well. Note that all simple separating curves on
T^{2}
bound disks, so all simple separating curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
are non-essential. Thus,
\hat{\alpha}_{a}
and
\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{a}
are non-separating in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, so
(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\})\setminus\hat{\alpha}_{a}
is connected. Because connectedness of a surface is not changed by removing a single point,
(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})\setminus\hat{\alpha}_{a}
is also connected. The same argument applies to
\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
, so both representatives are non-separating in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. Now, note that if a simple curve is peripheral in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
, then it either bounds a disk or a punctured disk in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. However, because
\hat{\alpha}_{a}
and
\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}_{a}
are essential in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, this cannot be the case. Therefore,
\hat{\alpha}_{a}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
are also non-peripheral in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
.

Now, we prove that
\hat{\alpha}_{a},\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
gives a pants decomposition of
S
. Because
N_{\hat{a}}\setminus\{\bullet\}
is homeomorphic to the interior of a punctured annulus, if we let
\overline{N_{\hat{a}}}
denote
N_{\hat{a}}\cup\partial N_{\hat{a}}
, then
\chi(\overline{N_{\hat{a}}}\setminus\{\bullet\})=-1
. Let
M:=T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}\setminus N_{\hat{a}}
. Then

\chi(\overline{N_{\hat{a}}})+\chi(M)=\chi(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})=-2,

so
\chi(M)=-1
. We know that
M
must have two boundary components,
\hat{\alpha}_{a}
and
\hat{\alpha}_{a}^{\prime}
, as well as one puncture,
\circ
, implying that
M
has genus 0 and is thus also homeomorphic to a punctured annulus. Because punctured annuli are pairs of pants,
\{\hat{\alpha}_{a},\alpha_{a}^{\prime}\}
gives a pants decomposition of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. Therefore,
\{\alpha_{a},\alpha_{a}^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Lemma 2.17
.

The map
\Phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{P}
where
a\mapsto\{\alpha_{a},\alpha^{\prime}_{a}\}
is a bijection.

Proof.

To prove bijectivity, we introduce a candidate inverse
\Psi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{A}
. Let
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
. By definition, cutting along disjoint representatives
\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
of these two homotopy classes yields two disjoint pairs of pants denoted
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
, each containing a single puncture. Without loss of generality, assume that
\bullet\in P_{1}
and consider
P_{1}
as a once-punctured annulus. Inside this annulus, there exists a unique homotopy class of simple and non-trivial unoriented arcs based at
\bullet
. Let
a
be this class of arcs and note that we can find a representative
\hat{a}\in a
which is parallel to the boundary. That is,
\hat{a}\cup\{\bullet\}
is homotopic as a simple closed curve to
\hat{\alpha}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, so it is also essential as a simple closed curve in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. It follows that
\hat{a}
is essential as an arc, as it does not bound a disk or a punctured disk. Therefore, we may conclude that
a\in\mathcal{A}
and define
\Psi(\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}):=a
. We can now show that
(\Phi\circ\Psi)(\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\})=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
and
(\Psi\circ\Phi)(a)=a
for any
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. First, consider
(\Phi\circ\Psi)(\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\})
. We denote
a:=\Psi(\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\})
. Taking any tubular neighborhood
N_{\hat{a}}
of a representative
\hat{a}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
we obtain an element
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{P}
(by Lemma
2.16
) such that
\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}=\Phi(a)
. However, as shown above,
\hat{a}\cup\bullet\simeq\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
as simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
since
\hat{\alpha},\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}
bound a tubular neighborhood of
\hat{a}
. Because the same is true for representatives
\hat{\beta},\hat{\beta}^{\prime}
, these curves are also homotopic to
\hat{a}\cup\bullet
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. Because
\circ
is not in either neighborhood by construction, this implies that
\hat{\alpha}\simeq\hat{\beta}
and
\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}\simeq\hat{\beta}^{\prime}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
. Thus,
\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}=\{\beta,\beta^{\prime}\}
so
(\Psi\circ\Phi)(\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\})=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}
. Now consider
(\Psi\circ\Phi)(a)
. Taking a tubular neighborhood
N_{\hat{a}}
of a representative
\hat{a}
in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
gives boundary curves
\partial N_{\hat{a}}=\{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\}=\Phi(a)\in\mathcal{P}
by Lemma
2.16
. By construction, representatives of
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
bound a punctured annulus containing
\hat{a}
. Going through the process outlined for
\Psi
, we locate the unique homotopy class of non-trivial arcs based at the puncture
\bullet
. By uniqueness, this class must be
a
. Thus
(\Psi\circ\Phi)(a)=a
. It follows now that
\Psi=\Phi^{-1}
, and thus
\Psi
must be bijective.
∎

Therefore, Claim
2.15
holds. However, the existence of a bijection between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{P}
is not enough to give us our desired one-to-one correspondence between tri-arcs and tri-pants. In order to prove this correspondence, we must prove that minimal intersection of the arcs

b
, and

corresponds to minimal intersection of the associated pants decompositions
\Phi(a)
,
\Phi(b)
, and
\Phi(c)
.

Claim 2.18
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between the sets of tri-arcs and tri-pants.

2.4.
Tri-Pants and Maximality

There are many other ways to understand a tri-pant; we provide an example below with an alternate, less restrictive definition.

A

2.5.
The inclusion map and rational numbers

Can we find an even more accessible representation of tri-pants that retains bijectivity? The answer to this question appears to be “no”; however, we can describe a beautiful relationship between tri-pants and the rational numbers using the inclusion map. Let
\eta:T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}\to T^{2}
be the inclusion map. We investigate its induced homomorphism
\eta_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)\to\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
, or perhaps more simply,

\eta_{*}:\mathbb{Z}\cdot a\ast\mathbb{Z}\cdot b\to\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}.

We note that
\eta_{*}
maps “words” written with the letters
a,a^{-1},b
, and
b^{-1}
into tuples of integers
(p,q)
, where
p
and
q
correspond to the number of times a given curve travels about the meridian and longitude of the torus, respectively.

We see that this map is not bijecive. For example, we know that
aba^{-1}
and
b
are distinct elements of
\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)
, but
\eta_{*}(aba^{-1})=\eta_{*}(b)=(0,1)
. We should not let this lack of bijectivity discourage us though. If, instead of the tuple
(p,q)
, we represent elements in the image of
\eta_{*}
by the rational numbers
\frac{p}{q}
, we introduce a powerful connection between tri-pants and
\mathbb{Q}
. In the next section, we thoroughly investigate this correspondence and extend it to relate the tri-pants graph with the dual of the Farey complex.

3.
A Tale of Two Graphs

3.1.
The Farey graph and its dual

As shown in the previous section, we can relate curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
to those on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
via the inclusion map,
\eta
. This relationship will provide implications for our main results, as set the homotopy classes of essential curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the vertices of the Farey complex. This graph, denoted
\mathcal{F}
, provides a tessellation of the upper half-plane model of the hyperbolic plane,
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, which corresponds to the union of the set of complex numbers that have positive imaginary part with the set containing only infinity.

Definition 3.1
.

The
Farey graph
is contained in
\overline{\mathbb{H}^{2}}
, with vertex set
V=\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
. The points
\frac{p}{q}
and
\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
are connected by an edge if and only if
|pq^{\prime}-p^{\prime}q|=1
. If
q=0
, then
\frac{p}{q}=\infty
.

Figure 11
.
The Farey graph.

If three vertices in
\mathcal{F}
are pairwise connected by an edge, then those vertices create a
triangle
in the Farey graph, as seen by the red edges in Figure
11
. We say that two triangles are
adjacent
if they share an edge. We define a metric
d_{\mathcal{F}}(\frac{p}{q},\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}})
to be the minimum number
n\in\mathbb{Z}
of consecutive edges connecting
\frac{p}{q}
and
\frac{p^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}
. It is simple to see that the Farey Graph is connected.

As discussed in Section
2.5
, the inclusion map induces a homomorphism
\eta_{*}
that allows us to associate pairs of integers with simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. Then, two vertices in
\mathcal{F}
are connected by an edge if the associated simple closed curves intersect exactly once. The relationships between the Farey graph and simple closed curves in
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
are as follows:

•

Let
\alpha\in\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)
such that
\eta_{*}(\alpha)=(p,q)
. Then
\alpha
relates to the vertex
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathcal{F}
. It follows that if
\eta_{*}(\alpha)=(1,0)
, then
\alpha
relates to the point at infinity in
\mathcal{F}
. Note that for all
(p,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
with
p,q
coprime, there exists a unique
\alpha\in\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)
such that
i_{*}(\alpha)=(p,q)
and
\alpha
is represented as a simple closed curve.

•

The map
\eta_{*}
maps tri-arcs to triangles in
\mathcal{F}
. We see that applying
\eta_{*}
to the arcs of a tri-arc yields three adjacent vertices on
\mathcal{F}
, due to the condition that the arcs intersect pairwise once. Thus, each pair of vertices in
\mathcal{F}
is connected by an edge, so the tri-arc corresponds to a unique triangle in
\mathcal{F}
. We note that adjacent triangles in
\mathcal{F}
correspond to tri-arcs that differ by exactly one arc. This conclusion follows from the fact that they share two vertices on
\mathcal{F}
when applying
\eta_{*}
to their arcs.

Definition 3.2
.

Two tri-arcs are
adjacent
if they differ by exactly one arc.

We will now extend this connection between homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
and
\mathcal{F}
to a correspondence between curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
and the dual of the Farey complex, denoted
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.

Figure 12
.
The dual of the Farey graph (blue) is superimposed atop
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 3.3
.

The
dual of the Farey graph
, denoted
\mathcal{F}^{*}
, is the graph associated
\mathcal{F}
having a vertex
T^{*}
per each triangle
T
in
\mathcal{F}
and whose edges connect two vertices
v
,

if and only if their corresponding triangles
T^{*}
and
(T^{*})^{\prime}
are adjacent in
\mathcal{F}
.

It is important to note that the dual of the Farey graph is a connected, trivalent, infinite tree and therefore has infinite diameter. For the remainder of this paper, we will examine powerful connections between
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and the tri-pants graph.

3.2.
The Tri-Pants Graph

In this section, we will define, construct, and build intuition on the tri-pants graph. We will then state and prove the central results of this paper; namely, that the tri-pants graph is connected and has infinite diameter. First, though, one must understand the structure of the graph itself.

3.2.1.
Elementary moves

As stated in the previous section, tri-arcs that share two of their arcs that project into in adjacent triangles in
\mathcal{F}
. We now seek to understand how adjacent tri-arcs relate to one another. In fact, there are two distinct operations for moving between adjacent tri-arcs; tri-pants that correspond to adjacent tri-arcs differ by an elementary move.

Lemma 3.6

Let

By defining this algebraic relationship between tri-arcs differing only by a big flip, we are able to prove the following properties.

Lemma 3.7
.

If

contains the arc

Lemma 3.8
.

The composition of big flips along distinct arcs does not commute.

Proof.

Let
T_{\pi_{1}}=\{a,b,c\}
be a tri-arc representation of a tri-pant. We will show that

, we can assume without loss of generality that

B_{a}(T_{\pi_{1}})=\{[b,c]a,b,c\}=\{bcb^{-1}c^{-1}cb,b,c\}=\{bc,b,c\}

\displaystyle B_{b}(B_{a}(T_{\pi_{1}}))

\displaystyle=\{bc,[c^{-1},bc]b,c\}

\displaystyle=\{bc,c^{-1}bc(cc^{-1})(b^{-1}b),c\}

\displaystyle=\{bc,c^{-1}bc,c\}.

We also have

B_{b}(T_{\pi_{1}})=\{a,[a,c^{-1}]b,c\}=\{a,ac^{-1}a^{-1}c(c^{-1}a),c\}=\{a,ac^{-1},c\}

\displaystyle B_{a}(B_{b}(T_{\pi_{1}}))

\displaystyle=\{[c,ac^{-1}]a,ac^{-1},c\}

\displaystyle=\{cac^{-1}(c^{-1}c)(a^{-1}a),ac^{-1},c\}

\displaystyle=\{cac^{-1},ac^{-1},c\}.

Substituting

B_{a}(B_{b}(T_{\pi_{1}}))=\{ccbc^{-1},cbc^{-1},c\}

∎

3.2.4.
Connectedness

With our goal being to understand how two tri-pants can relate, it is important to see if we can use elementary moves to change any tri-pant into another. This problem translates into proving one of our main results, that the tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
is connected. To tackle this proof, we will focus on the study of big and small flips. Because big flips connect two tri-arcs within the same fiber and small flips connect two tri-arcs within different fibers, it is sufficient to prove the following statements:

(1)

Proving that any two tri-arcs in a fiber can be related by a sequence of big flips.

(2)

Proving that any two fibers are connected via a sequence of small flips (and possibly big flips within intermediate fibers).

It turns out that proving
(2)
is not such a difficult task, given the relationship that the map
\pi
gives between the set of fibers and the vertices of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. However, proving
(1)
is more difficult and requires us to define homeomorphisms of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
that perform big flips on tri-arcs. To locate these homeomorphisms we look within the surface’s mapping class group.

Definition 3.15
.

Consider a surface with punctures
S=(\overline{S},\mathcal{P})
and let Homeo
{}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})
be the set of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms
f:\overline{S}\to\overline{S}
such that
f|_{\partial S\cup\mathcal{P}}
is the identity map. Additionally, let Homeo
{}_{0}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})
be the set of maps
f\in
Homeo
{}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})
for which there exists a homotopy
F:[0,1]\times\overline{S}\to\overline{S}
from
f
to the identity maps and so that

Homeo
{}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})
for all

(that is,
f
is isotopic to the identity in Homeo
{}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})
). We define the
pure mapping class group

\text{PMod}(S):={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\displaystyle{\text{Homeo}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\text{Homeo}_{0}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}}{\raisebox{3.98112pt}{$\textstyle{\text{Homeo}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\text{Homeo}_{0}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}}{\raisebox{3.17558pt}{$\scriptstyle{\text{Homeo}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\text{Homeo}_{0}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}}{\raisebox{2.5739pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Homeo}^{+}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Homeo}_{0}(S,\partial S\cup\mathcal{P})}$}}}.

(S)
are isotopy classes of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms on

which fix the boundary points and punctures. Within PMod

The map Push

For any tri-arc

since
\circ
need not be fixed by the intermediate homeomorphisms. Then, we can travel along
a^{-1}
, which will bring the images of all curves intersecting
a
back to their pre-images, yielding the identity map. This implies that Image(Push)
\subseteq
Ker(Forget). However, we wish to determine if push maps describe all homeomorphisms which are isotopic to the identity in Homeo
{}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})
, a result that is not at all obvious to show. Luckily,
[
3
]
discusses construction of the Birman exact sequence,

1\to\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\},\circ)\overset{Push}{\longrightarrow}\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})\overset{Forget}{\longrightarrow}\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})\to 1.

As proven in Section 4.2.3 of
[
3
]
, this sequence is exact (that is, Image(Push) = Ker(Forget). Within this source, we can also find a proof of our desired result.

Lemma 3.20
.

Image(Push)
=
Ker(Forget).

Thus, push maps do describe all maps in
\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})
which are isotopic to the identity in Homeo
{}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\},\{\bullet\})
. While this result is not only a nice characterization of push maps, it also gives us another way to locate then. Since push maps are difficult to explicitly construct, it is useful to study tools which allow us to “find” push maps within
\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})
. Lemma
3.21
gives us one such tool. Note that the proof of the lemma’s statement also depends on a result from
[
3
]
. There is an isomorphism
\sigma:\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})\to\text{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
defined as follows:

First, take any
[f]\in\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})
and a representative
f
. By definition of PMod
(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})
,
f
is an isomorphism of
T^{2}
and thus induces an isomorphism
f_{*}:\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Note that group of isomorphisms on
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
is itself isomorphic to SL
(2,\mathbb{Z})
by taking an isomorphism
g
and constructing a matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
with
g(1,0)=(a,c)
and
g(0,1)=(b,d)
. We apply this process to
f_{*}
in order to find the matrix
\sigma[f]
. The proof that this map is an isomorphism is discussed in Section 2.2.4 of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 3.21
.

If a map
\phi\in\text{Homeo}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\},\{\circ,\bullet\})
has induced group morphism
\phi_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)\to\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
that is the identity, then
[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})}=
Push
(\theta)
for some
\theta\in\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\},\circ)
.

Proof.

Note that, by definition of
\text{Homeo}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\},\{\circ,\bullet\})
,
\phi
is a homeomorphism of
T^{2}
which fixes
\bullet
, and is thus an element of Homeo
{}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\},\{\bullet\})
. By appealing to the construction of
\sigma
defined above,
\sigma([\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})})=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
, where
(a,c)=\phi_{*}(1,0)
and
(b,d)=\phi_{*}(0,1)
. However, since
\phi_{*}
is the identity,
\sigma([\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
. Because
\sigma
is group isomorphism which maps
[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})}
to the identity element,
[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})}
must be the identity element of
\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})
. Note now that by definition of the Forget map,

[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\})}=\text{Forget}([\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})}),

which implies that

[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})}\in\text{Ker(Forget)}=\text{Image(Push)}.

That is, there exists a
\theta\in\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\bullet\},\circ)
so that
[\phi]_{\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})}=
Push
(\theta)
.
∎

We then reach yet another description of push maps: the elements of
\text{PMod}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\})
whose representatives all induce the identity morphism on
\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. However, it is still not easy to find many homeomorphisms of the torus which act as identities on homotopy classes. In fact, we are not used to defining homeomorphisms based on their effect on curves at all. For this, we appeal again to
[
3
]
in order to define homeomorphisms based on mappings of curves, giving us the following result.

Lemma 3.22

(Change of Coordinates Principle for Pairs Intersecting Once)
.

Suppose that
a,b
and
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
are pairs of simple closed curves in surface
S
which intersect exactly once. There exists an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\phi:S\to S
such that
\phi(a)=a^{\prime}
and
\phi(b)=b^{\prime}
.

The proof of this statement is discussed in Sections 1.3.1-1.3.2 of
[
3
]
. The change of coordinates principle gives us a nice way to find homeomorphisms in
\text{Homeo}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\},\{\circ,\bullet\})
that act as we wish on curves.

Note that for this proof, we will be considering both homotopy classes of unoriented arcs in
\mathcal{A}
and homotopy classes of oriented arcs in the fundamental group. For the sake of notation, we will notate homotopy classes in
\mathcal{A}
by
a_{\mathcal{A}}
, with homotopy classes in the fundamental group being notated simply by
a
. Further, it will make things easier if we define a new set

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}={\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.32pt}{$\displaystyle{\{a:[0,1]\to T^{2}:a\text{ unoriented essential simple arc with }a(0)=a(1)=\bullet\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.31873pt}{$\displaystyle{\simeq}$}}{\raisebox{4.07pt}{$\textstyle{\{a:[0,1]\to T^{2}:a\text{ unoriented essential simple arc with }a(0)=a(1)=\bullet\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.31873pt}{$\textstyle{\simeq}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\{a:[0,1]\to T^{2}:a\text{ unoriented essential simple arc with }a(0)=a(1)=\bullet\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.72855pt}{$\scriptstyle{\simeq}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\{a:[0,1]\to T^{2}:a\text{ unoriented essential simple arc with }a(0)=a(1)=\bullet\}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.287pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\simeq}$}}}

which only differs from
\mathcal{A}
by considering homotopy of arcs in
T^{2}
instead of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
. We will denote elements of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
by
a_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
. For
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we will be considering the homotopy class
[i(\hat{a})]_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
, where
i
is the inclusion map
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}\hookrightarrow T^{2}
and
\hat{a}
is a representative of
a
. We will let
i(a)_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
denote this homotopy class, where
i(a)
denotes an oriented homotopy class in
\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. However, for a representative
\hat{a}\in a
,
i(\hat{a})\subset T^{2}
still denotes the image of
\hat{a}
in
T^{2}
.

Theorem
B
.

For any
v\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(v)
is connected.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
, and assume
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{TP}
such that
T,T^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(v)

T_{*}=\{a_{\mathcal{A}},b_{\mathcal{A}},c_{\mathcal{A}}\}

T_{*}^{\prime}=\{a^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},b^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},c^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}}\}
be the corresponding tri-arcs. We will prove that there is a finite sequence of big flips separating
T_{*}
and
T_{*}^{\prime}
, implying the existence of a sequence of edges in
\pi^{-1}(v)
connecting the vertex
T_{*}
to
T_{*}^{\prime}
. Choose representatives
\hat{a},\hat{b},\hat{c},\hat{a}^{\prime},\hat{b}^{\prime},\hat{c}^{\prime}
that realize the tri-arcs
T_{*}
and
T_{*}^{\prime}
. Note first that the hypothesis implies that

i(a)_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=i(a^{\prime})_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},\>\>i(b)_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=i(b^{\prime})_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},\;\;i(c)_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=i(c^{\prime})_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},

up to relabeling. Applying Lemma
3.22
for
S=T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, we get a homeomorphism
\phi\in\text{Homeo}^{+}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\},\{\circ,\bullet\})
such that
\phi(\hat{a})=\hat{a}^{\prime}
and
\phi(\hat{b})=\hat{b}^{\prime}
. Note that
\phi:T^{2}\to T^{2}
fixes
\circ
, so
\psi:=\phi|_{T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}}
is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism which fixes
\bullet
(since it sends the intersection point of
\hat{a},\hat{b}
to the intersection point of
\hat{a}^{\prime},\hat{b}^{\prime}
) and has the property
\phi\circ i\equiv i\circ\psi
. Thus,
\psi(\hat{a})=\phi(\hat{a})=\hat{a}^{\prime}
and
\psi(\hat{b})=\phi(\hat{b})=\hat{b}^{\prime}
. Further, these maps generate isomorphisms
\phi_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)\to\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
and
\psi_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)\to\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)
. In order to study these isomorphisms, we must choose orientations to obtain an orientated tri-arc
T_{\pi_{1}}=\{a,b,c\}\subset\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)
, which gives us natural orientations for the inclusions of these classes
i(a),i(b),i(c)\in\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. Note that we can define orientations for
a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}
(and thus
i(a^{\prime}),i(b^{\prime}),i(c^{\prime})
) by composing parameterizations of these oriented curves with
\psi
. It follows that
i(a)=i(a^{\prime}),i(b)=i(b^{\prime})
, and
i(c)=i(c^{\prime})
as elements of
\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. We first study
\phi_{*}
and choose a generating set
i(a),i(b)
of
\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. Because

\phi(i(a))=[\phi(i(\hat{a}))]=i(a^{\prime})\text{ and }\phi(i(b))=[\phi(i(\hat{b}))]=i(b^{\prime}),

we see that
\phi_{*}
fixes a generating set and is thus the identity map on
\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. Applying Lemma
3.21
and then Lemma
3.18

tells us that

\phi(T_{*})=\{a^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},b^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},\phi(c)_{\mathcal{A}}\}=\{a^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},b^{\prime}_{\mathcal{A}},\psi(c)_{\mathcal{A}}\}=\psi(T_{*})

differs from

be an even number of big flips. Note that

is necessarily a tri-arc since it is the image of a tri-arc by a homeomorphism. Note now that

being the identity implies that
\phi(i(c))_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=i(c^{\prime})_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}

i(\psi(c))_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=i(c^{\prime})_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}.

Note that since
\psi(T_{*})
is a tri-arc, this equation implies that
\pi(\psi(T_{*}))=\pi(T_{*}^{\prime})

or
\psi(c)_{\mathcal{A}}
is a big flip of

differs by

by a finite sequence of big flips.

With connectedness of the fibers, we have tackled the first part of the proof. We are now able to prove Theorem
A
by appealing to connectedness of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
.

Theorem
A
.

The tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
is connected.

Proof.

Let
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{TP}
be two tri-pants. We will prove that there is a sequence of edges in
\mathcal{TP}
which connects
T
to
T^{\prime}
. Let
v=\pi(T),v^{\prime}=\pi(T^{\prime})\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
be two vertices. Since
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is connected, there is a sequence of vertices
v_{0},v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{k+1}
(where
v=v_{0},v_{k+1}=v^{\prime}
) and a sequence of edges
e_{0},e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
both contained in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and so that
e_{i}
connects
v_{i}
to
v_{i+1}
for all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,k\}
. Because
\pi
is surjective by Lemma
3.14
, for each edge
e_{i}
in
\mathcal{TP}
, there is at least one corresponding edge
w_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(e_{i})\subset\mathcal{TP}
which connects some tri-pant
T_{i}\in\pi^{-1}(v_{i})
to another
T_{i+1}\in\pi^{-1}(v_{i+1})
. Starting with
T\in\pi^{-1}(v_{0})
, we find an edge
w_{0}\in\pi^{-1}(e_{0})
which connects some
T_{0}\in\pi^{-1}(v_{0})
to a
T_{1}\in\pi^{-1}(v_{1})
. By Theorem
B
, there is a sequence of edges in the fiber
\pi^{-1}(v_{0})
which connects
T
to
T_{0}
. Adding the edge
w_{0}
at the end of this sequence gives a path from
T
to
T_{1}
. Continuing with this pattern, after adding the edge
w_{k}
we have found a path in
\mathcal{TP}
from
T
to some
T_{k+1}\in\pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
. Because
T^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
as well, Theorem
B
implies the existence of a path within the fiber
\pi^{-1}(v^{\prime})
connecting
T_{k+1}
to
T^{\prime}
. Therefore, there is a path in
\mathcal{TP}
connecting
T
to
T^{\prime}
.
∎

3.2.5.
Infinite Diameter

We will again use the connection between the tri-pants graph and the Farey graph to prove Theorem
C
.

Theorem
C
.

The tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
has infinite diameter.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{F}^{*}
is a connected tree with infinite diameter. Thus, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
there exists vertices
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
such that
d_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}(v_{1},v_{2})\geq n
. Let
T\in\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(v_{1})
and
T^{\prime}\in\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(v_{2})
. We claim that any path
\gamma
connecting
T
and
T^{\prime}
will have length at least
n
. For the sake of contradiction, assume that there exists some path
\delta
between
T
and
T^{\prime}
with length less then
n
. Then
\pi(\delta)
provides a map between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
with length less than or equal to
n
since
\pi
sends edges in
\mathcal{TP}
to either vertices or edges in
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. If this length is less than
n
, we contradict the statement that
\mathcal{F}^{*}
has infinite diameter. Thus, for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
there exists
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{TP}
with distance at least
n
, hence the tri-pants graph has infinite diameter.
∎

Conclusion

Throughout this paper, we have investigated the properties of homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
which form pants decompositions, with a large focus on how different decompositions can relate to one another. In this study, the object of a tri-pant arose when considering triples of pants decompositions which pairwise intersect once. After presenting the primary definition of a tri-pant, Definition
2.10
, we were able to prove equivalence with a second definition: a maximal collection of homotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ,\bullet\}
which pairwise intersect at most once. Since tri-pants still proved difficult to picture on the twice-punctured torus, we found it easier to relate these structures to collections of homotopy classes of essential simple arcs on the once-punctured torus which intersect only at the base point and do not pairwise bound cylinders. Luckily for us, we were able to prove a one-to-one correspondence between tri-pants and these new objects, tri-arcs. Because we were able to view tri-arcs on the punctured square representation of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, this correspondence gave a great pictorial view of tri-pants.

In fact, to our delight, we could then also relate these new structures to the Farey graph
\mathcal{F}
by utilizing the map
i_{*}:\pi_{1}(T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\},\bullet)\to\pi_{1}(T^{2},\bullet)
. By mapping the three representative arcs of a tri-arc to three corresponding simple closed curves on
T^{2}
, we were able to relate a tri-pant to a triangle on the Farey graph, or a vertex on its dual
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. Mapping between sets of tri-pants and the Farey graph motivated us to create a graph whose vertices are tri-pants. However, as it was unclear when vertices of two tri-pants should be connected by an edge, we introduced elementary moves. Because two adjacent triangles in
\mathcal{F}
correspond to triples which shared two of the same curves, it made sense for two tri-pants to be connected by an edge if they shared two pants decompositions. In the process of defining elementary moves, we also introduced big and small flips to explain elementary moves between two tri-pants as viewed on their associated tri-arcs. With this understanding, we could then define the tri-pants graph
\mathcal{TP}
.

In our study of
\mathcal{TP}
, we were able to formally define their association with
\mathcal{F}^{*}
by introducing the map
\pi:\mathcal{TP}\to\mathcal{F}^{*}
. Although
\pi
is not a bijection, it is a surjective map. Further, studying
\pi
allowed us to prove, among other results, that every vertex in the graph has valence equal to
9
and that
\mathcal{TP}
is connected, is not a tree, and has infinite diameter. We hope that this paper provides a robust exposition to the tri-pants graph, although many questions pertaining to
\mathcal{TP}
remain unanswered. In particular, we conjecture that
each fiber of the map
\pi
is isomorphic to the dual of the Farey graph
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. There is a fundamental similarity between big flips and diagonal exchanges on triangulations of
T^{2}\setminus\{\circ\}
, which we believe may prove that each fiber of the map
\pi
is infinite.

Finally, we hope to inspire similar work on other “small” surfaces. For instance, does such a graph structure exist atop the thrice-punctured torus, and if so, is there a relationship between this construction and the tri-pants graph? This area of study is relatively unexplored, and we eagerly await future findings.
