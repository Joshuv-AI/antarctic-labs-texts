The Daniell Integral: Integration without measure
Adriaan de Clercq

Abstract
. In his 1918 paper “A General Form of Integral”, Percy John Daniell developed a theory of integration capable of dealing with functions on arbitrary sets. Daniell’s method differs from the measure-theoretic notion of integration. Linear functionals over vector lattices were considered as the fundamental objects on which he built the theory, rather than measures over sets. In this document, we explore Daniell’s concept of integration and how his theory relates to the measure-theoretic notion of integration. We paint a picture of the historical context surrounding Daniell’s ideas. Furthermore, we present examples due to Norbert Wiener, where the Daniell integral was employed on spaces too general for the standard integration techniques of the time.

Introduction

In this document, we review the theory of integration developed by Percy John Daniell (1889-1946).
His developments came in the period following
the success of Lebesgue’s theory of integration based on measure.
However, measure theory was still in its
infancy, and an abstract theory of measure had not yet been developed.
Consequently, as an attempt to generalise the theory of integration,
Daniell looked at the integral, rather than measure, as the principle object of study.
In his series
of papers which began with the 1918 paper “A General Form of Integral”
[

, Daniell provided a
method for extending the ideas of integration to sets of utmost generality.

We start with, in Section

We explore, in Section

Daniell was also interested in generalising the Stieltjies integral. We

2.1.
Definition of a function

The concept of integration and differentiation was understood
on an intuitive level since the works of
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) and Isaac Newton (1642-1726/27).
However, they did not use the term functions.
Rather, they referred to quantities and the rates of changes of these quantities.
Furthermore, intuitive geometrical notions of limit procedures sufficed as a justification for
the theory.
Therefore, the notion of integral was synonymous with area,
and the notion of derivative was synonymous with tangent. We refer the readers to
[
2
, Chapter 3]
for
more details surrounding the development of calculus.

It was not until Leonhard Euler (1707-1783), that the idea of a function was beginning to form.
In his 1748 book
Introductio in analysin infinitorum
, he defined a function to be an analytical expression
containing constants and variables. Later on, in his 1755 text

Institutiones calculi differentialis
, he defined a function to be
a quantity which depends on another.
Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789-1857) later used Euler’s definition
when he started a new age of rigour with his
textbook
Cours d’analyse de l’École Royale Polytechnique
written in 1821.
A year later in 1822,
Joseph Fourier (1768-1830) published his own definition of a function in his main work

Théorie analytique de la Chaleur
. The definition was as follows:

“In general, the function
f(x)
represents a succession of values or ordinates each of
which is arbitrary. An infinity of values being given to the abscissa
x
, there are an
equal number of ordinates
f(x)
. All have actual numerical values, either positive
or negative or nul. We do not suppose these ordinates to be subject to a common
law; they succeed each other in any manner whatever, and each of them is given
as it were a single quantity.” (Fourier, 1822.)

In essence, Fourier defined a function in terms of its graph. Although this still had the
geometrical flavour of the previous century, this definition is general enough
as to include the stranger counterexamples that drove mathematicians of the time to begin treating the
subject with the necessary care.

In his work, Fourier decomposed a function
f
into the series

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{2}a_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n}\sin(nx)+b_{n}\cos(nx))

where the coefficients are given by

a_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx,

a_{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx,\text{ and }b_{n}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx,\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N}.

In his derivations, he assumed that the interchange between an infinite series and integration
is valid, i.e.that the following can be done to a series of functions

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}(x)
:

\int_{a}^{b}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}s_{n}(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{a}^{b}s_{n}(x)\,dx.

This brought up two questions. Firstly, what is the meaning of
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx
for
‘arbitrary’ functions
f
as given by Fourier’s definition? Secondly, when can the limit
of a sequence of functions be interchanged with the integral?

2.3.
Development of Riemann’s ideas

Enter the French mathematician
Jean-Gaston Darboux (1842-1917). It is important to

\displaystyle\Delta(P):=M(P)-m(P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(M_{i}-m_{i})(x_{i}-x_{i-1}).

The second problem with the Riemann integral has to do with the original
assumption made by Fourier: the exchange of the limit and integral.
This, in general, is not possible, even with the power of the
modern Lebesgue integral. An example of this was given by Darboux.
Consider the telescoping series given by

f(x)=-2xe^{-x^{2}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[-2n^{2}xe^{-n^{2}x^{2}}+2(n+1)^{2}xe^{-(n+1)^{2}x^{2}}\right].

Note that

\int_{0}^{1}f(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{1}-2xe^{-x^{2}}\,dx=e^{-x^{2}}\bigg|_{0}^{1}=\frac{1}{e}-1,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left(-2n^{2}xe^{-n^{2}x^{2}}+2(n+1)^{2}xe^{-(n+1)^{2}x^{2}}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[(e^{-n^{2}}-1)-(e^{-(n+1)^{2}}-1)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[e^{-n^{2}}-e^{-(n+1)^{2}}\right]=\frac{1}{e}.

This holds regardless of which integral we use,
and hence we cannot expect to exchange the integral and
sum in all cases. However, it was shown by Cesare Arzelà (1847-1912) in
1885 (we refer the readers to

the limit and integral may be interchanged. It is again important to note
the requirement that

sequence of Riemann integrable functions may not
converge to a Riemann integrable one.

The final important problem with the Riemann integral comes up when one tries to extend the ideas to
higher dimensions. Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

Given a domain

The French mathematician Marie Ennemond Camille

In his 1892 work

After a successful

“If a set consists of a countably infinite totality of disjoint intervals

From this definition, Borel noted four important properties of his measure.

•

The measure is countably additive.

•

The measure of a difference
m(E\setminus E^{\prime})
(assuming
E\subset E^{\prime}
)
is the difference of the measures
m(E)-m(E^{\prime})
.

•

The measure is always nonnegative.

•

A set of positive measure is uncountable.

A student at the
École Normale
during the time of Borel’s lecture series
was Henri Lebesgue.
Lebesgue studied at the
École Normale
from 1894 to 1897,
and it is likely that
he attended the lectures given by Borel.
Lebesgue completed his doctoral dissertation in 1902, where
he developed the Lebesgue theory of measure as we know it today.
In the introduction of his dissertation
he made it clear that he attempted to address the known
shortcomings of the Riemann integral:

“It is known that there are derivatives which are not integrable,
if one accepts Riemann’s definition of the integral; the kind of
integration as defined by Riemann does not allow in all cases to
solve the fundamental problem of calculus:

Find a function with a given derivative.

It thus seems to be natural to search for a definition of the integral
which makes integration the inverse operation of differentiation
in as large a range as possible.” (Lebesgue, 1902.)

It is worth noting that Lebesgue was the first mathematician to criticise
Riemann’s definition (
[
2
, Subsection 9.3.1, p. 272]
). Although
there were many publications exploring the weak points of the Riemann integral,
these publications were not intended nor interpreted as criticisms of the Riemann
integral and Riemann’s ideas were still viewed as sufficiently general.

In writing his dissertation, Lebesgue, like Jordan from
whom Lebesgue took inspiration,
first considered the problem
of defining measures of sets. Referring to Borel, he required his measure

m
to be countably additive. To be explicit, if
\left\{E_{i}:i\in I\right\}
is a collection
of at most countably many disjoint measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, then

m\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}\right)=\sum_{i\in I}m(E_{i}).

Furthermore, if
\left\{I_{j}:j\in J\right\}
is an at most countably many
set of intervals such that

E\subset\bigcup_{j\in I}
, then one would want the following to hold:

m(E)\leq m\left(\bigcup_{j\in J}I_{j}\right)\leq\sum_{j\in J}l(I_{j}),

with
l(I_{j})
denoting the length of the interval
I_{j}
.
Consequently, Lebesgue defined the outer measure
m_{e}(E)
as

m_{e}(E):=\inf\left\{\sum_{j\in J}l(I_{j}):E\subset\bigcup_{j\in J}I_{j}\right\}.

It was also known at the time that if
E\subset[a,b]
was
measurable in the sense of Jordan (recall
that Jordan’s ideas of measurability could also be
applied to one dimensional sets), then

\mathscr{I}(E)+\mathscr{I}([a,b]\setminus E)=b-a.

This motivated Lebesgue in defining the inner measure
of a set
E\subset[a,b]
to be

m_{i}(E):=b-a-m_{e}([a,b]\setminus E).

Note that
m_{i}(E)=m_{e}(E)
if and only if
m_{e}(E)+m_{e}([a,b]\setminus E)=b-a
,
and so Lebesgue defined the
set
E
to be measurable precisely when this condition holds.
From this definition, Lebesgue noted,
as the Jordan inner and outer contents were essentially
coverings by a finite number of intervals, that

\underline{\mathscr{I}}(E)\leq m_{i}(E)\leq m_{e}(E)\leq\overline{\mathscr{I}}(E)

and concluded that all Jordan measurable sets were also measurable by his new measure.
Using this more powerful tool for measuring sets, he went further than Borel and applied this generalised
idea to develop a stronger theory of integration. For a function
f
defined on
[a,b]

with range in
[m,M]
, Lebesgue partitioned
the range
[m,M]
instead of partitioning the domain as Riemann had done.
Let
P=\{a_{0},a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}
be a partition

m=a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{n}=M

of
[m,M]
. Define the sums

S(P)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}m(f^{-1}([a_{k-1},a_{k}])),

and

s(P)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k-1}m(f^{-1}([a_{k-1},a_{k}])).

Note that if
\left\lVert P\right\rVert
denotes the maximum of

a_{i}-a_{i-1}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
in the partition
P
then

0\leq S(P)-s(P)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(a_{k}-a_{k-1})m(f^{-1}[a_{k-1},a_{k}])\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lVert P\right\rVert m(f^{-1}[a_{k-1},a_{k}])\leq\left\lVert P\right\rVert(b-a).

Hence, as the partition is refined and
\left\lVert P\right\rVert\rightarrow 0
,
the sums
S(P)
and
s(P)
both converge to the same value.
Lebesgue defined the integral to be the value to which these sums converge.
The only condition
required for a function
f
to be (Lebesgue) integrable is that

f^{-1}([u,v])=\left\{x:u\leq f(x)\leq v\right\}

needs to be a measurable set for each
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
,
which Lebesgue showed to be a weak requirement.
He therefore showed that this integral is
an extension of Riemann’s. Furthermore,
as the concept of measure is easily generalised to higher
dimensions, his definition of the integral
also easily carries over to the multi-variable case.
In his dissertation he proved the following theorems
6
6

6

Once again we are paraphrasing the statement of the original results.
:

Theorem 2.3
.

If a function
f
on
[a,b]
has bounded
derivative
f^{\prime}
, then
f^{\prime}
is (Lebesgue) integrable
and
\int_{a}^{b}f^{\prime}(x)\,dx=f(b)-f(a)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If a sequence of functions
(f_{n})
whose absolute
value is uniformly bounded from above has limit
f
,
then the integral of
f
is the limit of the integrals of the functions
f_{n}
.

Recall that the big problems with Riemann’s integral were:

(i)

the definition does not extend to higher dimensions,

(ii)

there are differentiable functions with bounded derivative whose derivative is not Riemann integrable,

(iii)

and the limit of a sequence of Riemann integrable functions may not be Riemann integrable.

With these theorems Lebesgue, addressed these weakpoints of Riemann’s integral.

2.5.
The context of Daniell’s papers

In the period following Lebesgue’s dissertation, his work
was developed by himself and many other mathematicians.
Pierre Joseph Louis Fatou (1878-1929)
proved the Parseval’s equality for
the Fourier coefficients for general measurable functions in 1906.
Guido Fubini (1879-1943)
published his results pertaining to iterated integration in 1907.
Most important
of all, Frigyes Riesz (1880-1956) solidified Lebesgue’s
theory in the heart of modern analysis
by proving the Riesz-Fischer representation theorem in 1907 and introducing the
L^{p}
-spaces in 1910.

During this time, the notion of measure on the real line started becoming more
abstract; the underlying properties required of the sets and the set functions in the theory of
integration were starting to become clear. Furthermore, mathematicians in France and Italy became interested
in what we today would call ‘functionals’ (
[
3
, Epilogue, p. 182]
).
The Stieltjies integral was known at the time and Riesz proved in 1909 that
it could be extended to functions of bounded variation. Riesz
showed that if
U
is a linear functional such that

U(f_{n})\rightarrow U(f)
whenever
f_{n}
tends to
f
uniformly, then
one can find a function
g
of bounded variation such that
U
is equal to the
Stieltjies integral

U(f)=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,dg(x).

Beyond this, in 1910 Lebesgue published a paper where he extended his ideas
to multiple dimensions. He made the important observation that integrals may
just as well be considered as additive set functions.
If
E
is a measurable set in
n
-dimensional space, and
f
is a fixed measurable
function, one can define a set function

F(E):=\int_{E}f\,dA.

Lebesgue made the following important observations:
If
(E_{n})
is a sequence of subsets, then

•

if
m(E_{n})\rightarrow 0
, then
F(E_{n})\rightarrow 0
; and

•

if the sets
E_{n}
are pairwise disjoint, then

F(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}F(E_{n})
.

This observation was the key for the Austrian
mathematician Johann Karl August Radon (1887-1956) in 1913 to
merge the Stieltjies integral with the ideas of Lebesgue. He abstracted
the requirements needed in Lebesgue’s theory, only requiring an additive
set function, and by doing so generalised the Lebesgue theory even further.

The development of the idea of ‘functionals’ and
the movement towards abstraction during this period motivated
Percy John Daniell (1889-1946) towards a more general
form of integral, first introduced in 1918.
Daniell explicitly makes
reference to the publication of Radon in 1913 and his
generalisation of the Stieltjies integral.
He states in his introductory paragraph that his work is an
attempt to abstract the notion of integration even further,
to functions of elements of arbitrary nature.

“In this paper a theory is developed which is independent of the nature
of the elements. They may be points in space of a denumerable number
of dimensions or curves in general or classes of events so far as the
theory is concerned.” (Daniell, 1918.)

In his series of papers, Daniell developed a notion of integration that did not
rely on a measure theory of the underlying sets, and only used properties of
functionals and spaces of functions.
From this he derived many of the important integration results,
including
all the standard integration theorems due to Lebesgue, and even an analogue of
the Radon-Nikodym theorem
[
4
, Theorem 3.4]
. It is worth noting that Daniell, in his paper

[
5
]
, was the first to produce examples of sufficiently general integrals of functions
on a denumerably
infinite number of dimensions. This development by Daniell was later rediscovered by
the Soviet mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov (1903-1987) in the context of probability theory and
stochastic processes
and is now known as the Kolmogorov extension theorem which was proved in 1933.

The application of Daniell’s integral to stochastic processes was noted long before Kolmogorov.
Norbert Wiener (1894-1964) in
[
6
]
and

[
7
]
, used Daniell’s theory as the framework on which
he developed the rigorous treatment
of Brownian Motion. We discuss this in detail in Section
6
.

In a more modern setting, the Daniell integral
finds applications in the theory of measure on compact
topological groups. Vladimir Bogachev, in his
book on measure theory
[
8
, p. 445]
, states

“Daniell’s construction turned out to be very
efficient in the theory of integration on locally compact spaces. It enabled
one to construct the integral without prior constructing measures, which is
convenient when the corresponding measures are not
\sigma
-finite. This was manifested
especially by the theory of Haar measures. In that case, it turned
out to be preferable to regard measures as functionals on spaces of continuous
functions.” (Bogachev, 2007.)

In the years following Daniell’s papers there
was a widespread point of view that the
Daniell integral is the more appropriate way of
teaching the subject of integration
[
8
, p. 446]
.
This is due to the fact that using Daniell’s method, the
standard theorems quickly arises.
Teaching in this way was thus perceived to be more economical.
However, in applications,
measures turn out to be the principle object of study,
and thus in a Daniell-based treatment of the subject
one needs to prove these theorems in any case.
It is therefore that today the pedagogical
treatment of the Daniell integral
is less important than that of measure.

Today the Daniell integral serves as a useful tool
when considering measures as functionals on spaces
of continuous functions. In the words of Bogachev in
[
8
, p. 446]
:

“Certainly, for the researchers in measure theory and functional
analysis, acquaintance with Daniell’s method is necessary for broadening the
technical arsenal.” (Bogachev, 2007.)

3.
Daniell’s Integral and Extension

In this section, we review the extension procedure as
described in the first of Daniell’s papers on discussing his theory of integration
[
1
]
.
Let
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}

be the extended real numbers.
Let
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
be given by

{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}:=\left\{x:x\text{ is a function from }X\text{ to }\overline{\mathbb{R}}\right\}.

For
x,y\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
we say
x\leq y
if
x(t)\leq y(t)

for all
t\in X
.
Thus
({\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X},\leq)
forms a partially ordered set. In fact, it
forms a lattice with the meet and join given as follows:
for
x,y\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}

(x\land y)(t)=\min\{x(t),y(t)\},\text{ for }t\in X,

and

(x\lor y)(t)=\max\{x(t),y(t)\},\text{ for }t\in X.

Remark 3.1
.

In the source material, meet
\land
and join
\lor
is
referred to as
logical addition
and

logical product
respectively. We keep the
terminology meet and join throughout this
document.

Let
T_{0}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
be a class of functions
that is closed under
\land
,
\lor
, and linear combinations.
More explicitly, if
x,y\in T_{0}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
then
\alpha x+\beta y
,
x\lor y
, and

x\wedge y
are also in
T_{0}
.

Remark 3.2
.

If it were the case that all the functions are
finite-valued, this class would be called a
vector lattice
. Care is taken to
allow functions which possibly take on infinite values. In the
book
Real Analysis
by H. Royden
[
9
]
such a set
is also called a vector lattice. However, we use the terminology
extended vector lattice
to make
explicit note of the difference.
We thus need clarification in the ambiguous case when the
addition is of the form “
\infty-\infty
”.
Let
x,y
be functions in
T_{0}
. Let
P_{x+y}
be the set
where addition is ambiguous. More explicitly,

P_{x+y}:=\{t\in X:x(t)=\infty\text{ and }y(t)=-\infty\text{, or }x(t)=-\infty\text{ and }y(t)=\infty\}.

For any
c\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, we require the following to be in
T_{0}
:

f(t):=\begin{cases}x(t)+y(t),&\text{ if }t\not\in P_{x+y}\\
c,&\text{ if }t\in P_{x+y}\\
\end{cases}.

Furthermore, for
x,y\in T_{0}
we define
x+y
as follows: For
t\in X

(x+y)(t):=\begin{cases}x(t)+y(t),&\text{ if }t\not\in P_{x+y}\\
0,&\text{ if }t\in P_{x+y}\\
\end{cases}.

If
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
\left({\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X},\leq\right)

and
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
, then we
use the notation
(x_{n})\searrow x
(respectively
(x_{n})\nearrow x
)
to mean a pointwise decreasing (respectively increasing)
sequence with a pointwise limit
x
. Similarly, if
(\alpha_{n})

is a sequence in
\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we
use the notation
(\alpha_{n})\searrow\alpha
(respectively
(\alpha_{n})\nearrow\alpha
)
to mean a decreasing (respectively increasing)
sequence with limit
\alpha
.

If
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
\left({\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X},\leq\right)
,
then
\bigvee\left\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
and

\bigwedge\left\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
always exists. We define

\sup_{n}x_{n}:=\bigvee\left\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\};

and

\inf_{n}x_{n}:=\bigwedge\left\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

Note that the supremum and infimum is given by the following:

\left(\sup_{n}x_{n}\right)(t)=\sup_{n}x_{n}(t)\text{ for }t\in X;

and

\left(\inf_{n}x_{n}\right)(t)=\inf_{n}x_{n}(t)\text{ for }t\in X.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
I:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We call
I
an
I
-integral
if for all
x,y\in T_{0}
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and sequences
(x_{n})\subset T_{0}
:

(D1)

I(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha I(x)+\beta I(y)
;

(D2)

(x_{n})\searrow 0
implies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})=0
; and

(D3)

if
0\leq x
then
0\leq I(x)
.

3.1.
Extending the
I
-integral

Throughout this section, let
T_{0}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
be
an extended vector lattice and

I:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be an
I
-integral.

Definition 3.4
.

Define
T_{1}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
as follows:

T_{1}:=\{x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}:\text{ there exists a sequence }(x_{n})\subset T_{0}\text{ such that }(x_{n})\nearrow x\}.

We aim to show that:

(A1)

I
extends to
T_{1}
; and

(A2)

if
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
T_{1}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
, then
x\in T_{1}
.

Note that
T_{1}
is not an extended vector lattice since
T_{1}

is only closed under nonnegative scalar multiplication.
However,
T_{1}
is closed under addition,
\land
, and
\lor
.
For if
x
and
y
are in
T_{1}
with

(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
sequences in
T_{0}
such that

(x_{n})\nearrow x
and
(y_{n})\nearrow y
,
then
(x_{n}+y_{n})\nearrow x+y,(x_{n}\lor y_{n})\nearrow x\lor y,\text{ and }(x_{n}\wedge y_{n})\nearrow x\wedge y.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x\in T_{1}
and
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
T_{0}

such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
. If
h\in T_{0}

such that
h\leq x
, then
I(h)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})

(where
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})
might possibly be infinite).

Proof.

Let
x\in T_{1}
and
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
. Let
h\in T_{0}

such that
h\leq x
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
h_{n}:=x_{n}\wedge h
. Then for
each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

h_{n}\in T_{0}
and
h_{n}\leq x_{n}
. Also note that
(h_{n})
is an increasing sequence.
We then have

\sup_{n}h_{n}=\sup_{n}(x_{n}\wedge h)=\left(\sup_{n}x_{n}\right)\wedge h=x\wedge h=h,

and thus
(h_{n})\nearrow h
.
Therefore,
(h-h_{n})\searrow 0
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(h-h_{n})=0

by (D2) of Definition
3.3
. But

0=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(h-h_{n})=\inf_{n}[I(h)-I(h_{n})]=I(h)-\sup_{n}I(h_{n}),

and so
I(h)=\sup_{n}I(h_{n})
.
For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
h_{n}\leq x_{n}

and thus by (D3) of Definition
3.3
that

I(h_{n})\leq I(x_{n})
. Hence

I(h)=\sup_{n}I(h_{n})\leq\sup_{n}I(x_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n}),

proving the result.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
x,y\in T_{1}
with
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
sequences in
T_{0}

such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
and
(y_{n})\nearrow y
. If
y\leq x
then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(y_{n})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n}).

Proof.

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have
y_{m}\in T_{0}
and
y_{m}\leq y\leq x
. By Lemma
3.5
,
we know that
I(y_{m})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})
. Thus

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}I(y_{m})=\sup_{m}I(y_{m})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n}),

proving the result.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})
are both
sequences in
T_{0}
that increase to the same vector
x\in T_{1}
,
then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(y_{n})
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
I_{1}:T_{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be defined as follows:
if
x\in T_{1}
with
(x_{n})
a sequence in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
, then define

I_{1}(x):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})
.

Note that if
x\in T_{0}
, then
x\in T_{1}
and
I_{1}(x)=I(x)
.
This completes our first goal (A1)
of extending
I
to
T_{1}
.
We proceed to our second goal (A2) with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

If
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
and
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
T_{1}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
,
then
x\in T_{1}
and
I_{1}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(x_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
x_{r}\in T_{1}
. Therefore, there exists
a sequence
(x_{r,k})
in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{r,k})\nearrow x_{r}
.
Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

g_{n}:=\sup\{x_{r,k}:r,k\leq n\}.

Note that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
g_{n}\leq x
, and since
g_{n}
is a finite join of functions in
T_{0}
,
g_{n}\in T_{0}
.
Also note that
(g_{n})\subset T_{0}
is increasing,
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}\in T_{1}
by definition of
T_{1}
.
We also have
g_{n}\leq x_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus the following limits hold pointwise:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=x.

If
r,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
r\leq n
, then we have that
x_{r,n}\leq g_{n}

and so the following limits hold pointwise:

(3.2)

\displaystyle x_{r}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{r,n}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}.

This holds for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
and so, combining (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we have

x=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}x_{r}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=x,

and the inequalities becomes equalities. Therefore
(g_{n})
is a sequence in
T_{0}
increasing to
x
, hence
x\in T_{1}
.
Since
g_{n}\leq x_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Lemma
3.6
implies
that
I(g_{n})=I_{1}(g_{n})\leq I_{1}(x_{n})
and so

(3.3)

\displaystyle I_{1}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(g_{n})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(x_{n}).

If we fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
, then
x_{r,n}\leq g_{n}
for all
n\geq r
and it follows
that

I_{1}(x_{r})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{r,n})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(g_{n})=I_{1}(x).

Thus
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(x_{r})\leq I_{1}(x)
. This, together with (
3.3
),
allows us to conclude that
I_{1}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(x_{n})
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

Recall that
T_{1}
is closed with respect to addition and
positive scalar multiplication. We see that
I_{1}
also respects these operations.
If
x,y\in T_{1}
with
(x_{n})
and
(y_{n})

sequences in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
and
(y_{n})\nearrow y
, then
(x_{n}+y_{n})\nearrow x+y
and so

Furthermore if

with

, then
(cx_{n})\nearrow cx

3.2.
The class of integrable functions

Let
T_{0}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
once again denote an extended vector lattice and

I:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
an
I
-integral. Furthermore, we denote by
T_{1}
the
extension of
T_{0}
as in Definition
3.4
and
I_{1}
the extension of
I
to
T_{1}
.

Definition 3.11
.

For
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
define:

\displaystyle\overline{I}(x):=\inf\{I_{1}(\varphi):x\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}\text{ and }\underline{I}(x):=-\overline{I}(-x)\text{.}

We say
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
is
I
-integrable
if
\overline{I}(x)=\underline{I}(x)
and this value is finite.
We denote the common value by
\int x
and the class of integrable functions by
\mathscr{L}
.
Thus we have

\mathscr{L}:=\left\{x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}:-\infty<\underline{I}(x)=\overline{I}(x)<\infty\right\}

and
\int:\mathscr{L}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\int x

\displaystyle:=\underline{I}(x)=\overline{I}(x).

Note that if
x\in T_{0}
, then
x\in T_{1}
and so

\overline{I}(x)=I_{1}(x)=I(x).

Furthermore, since
T_{0}
is an extended vector lattice,
-x\in T_{0}
and

\underline{I}(x)=-\overline{I}(-x)=-I_{1}(-x)=-I(-x)=I(x).

Consequently
\overline{I}(x)=\underline{I}(x)=I(x)
and we see that
x\in\mathscr{L}
with
\int x=I(x).
Thus
\int

extends
I
.

Lemma 3.12
.

If
c\in\real
with
c\geq 0
and
x_{1},x_{2}\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{X}
,
then:

(i)

\overline{I}(cx_{1})=c\overline{I}(x_{1})
;

(ii)

\overline{I}(x_{1}+x_{2})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})+\overline{I}(x_{2})
;

(iii)

if
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
then
\overline{I}(x_{1})\leq\overline{I}(x_{2})
;

(iv)

if
\underline{I}(x_{1})
is finite, then
\underline{I}(x_{1})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})
;

(v)

\overline{I}(x_{1}\vee x_{2})+\overline{I}(x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})+\overline{I}(x_{2})
; and

(vi)

0\leq\overline{I}(|x_{1}|)-\underline{I}(|x_{1}|)\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})-\underline{I}(x_{1})
.

Proof.

(i)

If
c\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle\overline{I}(cx_{1})

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{I_{1}(\varphi):cx\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{I_{1}(c\varphi):cx\leq c\varphi,c\varphi\in T_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=c\inf\left\{I_{1}(\varphi):x\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\right\}

\displaystyle=c\overline{I}(x_{1})\text{.}

(ii)

If one of the sets
\{I_{1}(\varphi):x_{1}\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}
or

\{I_{1}(\varphi):x_{2}\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}
is empty, then one of

\overline{I}(x_{1})
or
\overline{I}(x_{2})
is infinite and the inequality holds.

Assume
\{I_{1}(\varphi):x_{1}\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}
and

\{I_{1}(\varphi):x_{2}\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}

are both non-empty. Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in T_{1}
be arbitrary such that

x_{1}\leq\varphi_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq\varphi_{2}
.
Then
\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}\in T_{1}
with

x_{1}+x_{2}\leq\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
and therefore

\overline{I}(x_{1}+x_{2})\leq I_{1}(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})=I_{1}(\varphi_{1})+I_{1}(\varphi_{2}).

Hence we have that

\overline{I}(x_{1}+x_{2})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})+\overline{I}(x_{2})
.

(iii)

Assume
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
. If
\varphi\in T_{1}
such that
x_{2}\leq\varphi
, then

x_{1}\leq\varphi
and
\overline{I}(x_{1})\leq I_{1}(\varphi)
.
Thus
\overline{I}(x_{1})
is a lower bound for
\{I_{1}(\varphi):x_{2}\leq\varphi,\varphi\in T_{1}\}

and
\overline{I}(x_{1})\leq\overline{I}(x_{2})
.

(iv)

Assume
\underline{I}(x_{1})
is finite. Then
0=\overline{I}(0)=\overline{I}(x_{1}-x_{1})
and by (ii) we know that

0\leq\overline{I}(x_{1}-x_{1})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})+\overline{I}(-x_{1})=\overline{I}(x_{1})-\underline{I}(x_{1}).

It follows that
\underline{I}(x_{1})\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})
.

(v)

Let
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in T_{1}
such that
x_{1}\leq\varphi_{1}
and

x_{2}\leq\varphi_{2}
. Then
x_{1}\vee x_{2}\leq\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2}
and
x_{1}\wedge x_{2}\leq\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
.
Thus

\overline{I}(x_{1}\vee x_{2})+\overline{I}(x_{1}\wedge x_{2})\leq I_{1}(\varphi_{1}\vee\varphi_{2})+I_{1}(\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2})=I_{1}(\varphi_{1})+I_{1}(\varphi_{2}).

Since this holds for any
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in T_{1}
such that
x_{1}\leq\varphi_{1}

and
x_{2}\leq\varphi_{2}
, we have the result.

(vi)

We know that
|x_{1}|=x_{1}\vee(-x_{1})
and
-|x_{1}|=x_{1}\wedge(-x_{1})
. Thus by (v) above

\overline{I}(|x_{1}|)+\overline{I}(-|x_{1}|)=\overline{I}(x_{1}\vee(-x_{1}))+\overline{I}(x_{1}\wedge(-x_{1}))\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})+\overline{I}(-x_{1}),

whence

\overline{I}(|x_{1}|)-\underline{I}(|x_{1}|)\leq\overline{I}(x_{1})-\underline{I}(x_{1}).

By (iv) this is greater than 0. ∎

Remark 3.13
.

We are now in a position to prove that
\mathscr{L}
is an extended vector lattice of extended real-valued functions
and that
\int
is an
I
-integral on
\mathscr{L}
. Before we do so, we mention the following vector lattice
identities:

(3.4)

\displaystyle x\vee y=\frac{1}{2}(x+y+|x-y|),

and

(3.5)

\displaystyle x\wedge y=\frac{1}{2}(x+y-|x-y|).

Identities (
3.4
) and (
3.5
)
also hold in extended vector lattices from Remark
3.2
.
Recall that, since we are dealing with possibly infinite-valued functions, the
right hand sides of (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) may not be defined.
This is solved by letting any ambiguity equal 0.
To illustrate, consider the case when
x(t_{0})=\infty
, and
y(t_{0})=-\infty
for some
t_{0}\in X
.
Then
(x\lor y)(t_{0})=\infty
. Now
x(t_{0})+y(t_{0})
is not defined, however if
f
is
the function from Remark
3.2
with
f(t)=0
on
P_{x+y}
and

f(t)=x(t)+y(t)
elsewhere, then we have

\frac{1}{2}(f(t_{0})+|x(t_{0})-y(t_{0})|)=(x\lor y)(t_{0})=\infty.

The other cases can be treated similarly.

Proposition 3.14
.

The class
\mathscr{L}
is an extended vector lattice of functions and
\int
is linear.

Proof.

From (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) it is sufficient for us to show

\mathscr{L}
to be closed under addition, scalar multiplication,
and absolute value to conclude
that
\mathscr{L}
is an extended vector lattice.

We first prove that
\mathscr{L}
is closed with respect to scalar multiplication.
Let
x
be integrable and
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Recall from Lemma
3.12
(i)
that
\overline{I}(cx)=c\overline{I}(x)
for nonnegative scalars
c
.

Case 1:
If
c
is a nonnegative scalar then

\overline{I}(cx)=c\overline{I}(x)=c\int x,

and

\underline{I}(cx)=-\overline{I}(c(-x))=c(-\overline{I}(-x))=c\underline{I}(x)=c\int x.

Thus
cx\in\mathscr{L}
and

\int cx=c\int x
.

Case 2:
If
c
is negative,

\overline{I}(cx)=(-c)\overline{I}(-x)=c\underline{I}(x)=c\int x,

and

\underline{I}(cx)=-\overline{I}(-cx)=c\overline{I}(x)=c\int x.

Thus
cx\in\mathscr{L}

and
\int cx=c\int x
.

We now prove
\mathscr{L}
to be closed under addition.
Let
x,y\in\mathscr{L}
. Then by Lemma
3.12
(ii) we have

\overline{I}(x+y)\leq\overline{I}(x)+\overline{I}(y)=\int x+\int y.

Also, again using Lemma
3.12
(ii) we have

-\underline{I}(x+y)=\overline{I}(-x-y)\leq\overline{I}(-x)+\overline{I}(-y)=-\underline{I}(x)-\underline{I}(y).

Thus
\underline{I}(x)+\underline{I}(y)\leq\underline{I}(x+y)
and we have

\int x+\int y\leq\underline{I}(x+y)\leq\overline{I}(x+y)\leq\int x+\int y.

Thus
x+y\in\mathscr{L}
and
\int(x+y)=\int x+\int y
.

The fact that
x\in\mathscr{L}
implies
|x|\in\mathscr{L}
follows
readily from Lemma
3.12
(vi). Since if
x\in\mathscr{L}
, then we have

0\leq\overline{I}(|x|)-\underline{I}(|x|)\leq\overline{I}(x)-\underline{I}(x)=0,

hence
\overline{I}(|x|)=\underline{I}(|x|)
and we conclude
|x|\in\mathscr{L}
.
∎

Definition 3.15
.

We call
\int
on the class
\mathscr{L}
the Daniell Integral induced by
I
on
T_{0}
.

Proper care needs to be taken in order to ensure
that the Daniell Integral is in fact an
I
-integral (we treat this
in Remark
3.17
below).
In order to do so, we first prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.16

(Monotone Convergence Theorem for the Daniell Integral)
.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
\mathscr{L}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
. If
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}

is finite, then
x\in\mathscr{L}
and
\int x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
\mathscr{L}
such that
(x_{n})\nearrow x
and assume

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}
is finite.
We have
x_{1}\leq x_{2}\leq x_{3}\leq\cdots\leq x
. Then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
-x\leq-x_{n}

and so
\overline{I}(-x)\leq\int-x_{n}
which is the same as

\int x_{n}\leq\underline{I}(x)
.
Thus
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}\leq\underline{I}(x)
.

To prove the reverse inequality, let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary.
Then using the definition of infimum on Definition
3.11

there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})
in
T_{1}

such that,

x_{1}\leq\varphi_{1},\quad 0\leq x_{2}-x_{1}\leq\varphi_{2},\quad\cdots,\quad 0\leq x_{n}-x_{n-1}\leq\varphi_{n}

with the property that
I_{1}(\varphi_{1})\leq\int x_{1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

and
I_{1}(\varphi_{n})\leq\int(x_{n}-x_{n-1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N},n>1
.

Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the function
\rho_{n}:=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}+\cdots+\varphi_{n}
.
Note, since
0\leq\varphi_{n}
for
n\geq 2
, that
(\rho_{n})
is an increasing sequence in
T_{1}
.
Also note that

x_{n}=x_{1}+(x_{2}-x_{1})+\cdots+(x_{n}-x_{n-1})\leq\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}+\cdots+\varphi_{n}\leq\rho_{n}.

Let
\psi\in T_{1}

such that
x\leq\psi
, and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, set
\psi_{n}:=\psi\wedge\rho_{n}
.
Then
(\psi_{n})
is an increasing sequence in
T_{1}

and
x_{n}\leq\psi_{n}\leq\psi
.
Thus the pointwise limit
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}
exists and is in

T_{1}
by Lemma
3.9
.
We also have

x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n},

and thus, again using Lemma
3.9
, we conclude

\overline{I}(x)\leq I_{1}\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(\psi_{n}).

Note that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that

I_{1}(\rho_{n})=I_{1}(\varphi_{1})+\cdots+I_{1}(\varphi_{n})\leq\left(\int x_{1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)+\left(\int(x_{2}-x_{1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right)+\cdots+\left(\int(x_{n}-x_{n-1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}\right)

and consequently,

I_{1}(\psi_{n})\leq I_{1}(\rho_{n})\leq\int x_{n}+\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\cdots+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}\right)\leq\int x_{n}+\varepsilon\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}+\varepsilon.

Therefore

\overline{I}(x)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(\psi_{n})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}+\varepsilon,

and this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Thus
\overline{I}(x)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}

and we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}\leq\underline{I}(x)\leq\overline{I}(x)\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}.

Thus
x\in\mathscr{L}
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}=\int x
as desired.
∎

We now state and prove analogues of standard results from measure theory viz. Fatou’s Lemma and
Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem. For this purpose, we need the following lemma.

Lemma 3.18
.

If
(x_{n})
is a sequence of nonnegative functions in
\mathscr{L}
, then
\inf_{n}x_{n}\in\mathscr{L}
.

Proof.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative functions in
\mathscr{L}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

\varphi_{n}:=x_{1}\wedge x_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{n}.

Then by Theorem
3.14
,
\varphi_{n}\in\mathscr{L}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Note also that

(\varphi_{n})
is decreasing and nonnegative and thus
(-\varphi_{n})
is an increasing sequence bounded above by 0.
Therefore

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int-\varphi_{n}\leq 0<\infty,

and by the Theorem
3.16
we have

\inf_{n}x_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\varphi_{n}\in\mathscr{L}.

∎

Theorem 3.19

(Fatou’s Lemma for the Daniell Integral)
.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence of non-negative functions in
\mathscr{L}
. If
\liminf\int x_{n}<\infty
, then
\liminf x_{n}
is
in
\mathscr{L}
and

\int\liminf x_{n}\leq\liminf\int x_{n}.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{n}:=\inf_{k\geq n}x_{k}
. From Lemma
3.18
,
\psi_{n}\in\mathscr{L}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore,
(\psi_{n})
is an increasing sequence and
\psi_{n}\leq x_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus
\int\psi_{n}\leq\int x_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and so

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\psi_{n}=\liminf\int\psi_{n}\leq\liminf\int x_{n}<\infty.

Thus by the Theorem
3.16
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}\in\mathscr{L}
and

\int\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\psi_{n}\leq\liminf\int x_{n}.

But
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\psi_{n}=\liminf x_{n}
and we conclude
\int\liminf x_{n}\leq\liminf\int x_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 3.20

(Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem for the Daniell Integral)
.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
\mathscr{L}
such that
\left|x_{n}\right|\leq z
for some
z\in\mathscr{L}
.
If
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}=x
pointwise, then
x\in\mathscr{L}

and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}=\int x
.

Proof.

The proof is the same as that of the standard theorem in measure theory. We provide it for completeness.
Note
0\leq x_{n}+z\leq 2z
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Now
2z\in\mathscr{L}
,
hence
\liminf\int(x_{n}+z)\leq\int 2z<\infty
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus by Theorem
3.19
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int x+\int z=\int(x+z)=\int\liminf(x_{n}+z)\leq\liminf\int(x_{n}+z)=\liminf\int x_{n}+\int z.

Therefore, after subtracting
\int z
from both sides,
\int x\leq\liminf\int x_{n}
.
Using the same argument on
-x_{n}+z
, we get

\displaystyle-\int x+\int z=\int(-x+z)

\displaystyle=\int\liminf(-x_{n}+z)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf\int(-x_{n}+z)=-\limsup\int x_{n}+\int z.

Therefore, after subtracting
\int z
from both sides,
-\int x\leq-\limsup\int x_{n}
which is the same as
\limsup\int x_{n}\leq\int x
.

Finally, combining (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) we have

\int x\leq\liminf\int x_{n}\leq\limsup\int x_{n}\leq\int x.

This shows that these inequalities are, in fact, equalities and we conclude that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int x_{n}=\int x.

∎

Another result of interest gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a function
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}

to be integrable.

Theorem 3.21
.

For any
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
we have
x\in\mathscr{L}
if and only if for every

\varepsilon>0
there exists an
x_{\varepsilon}\in T_{0}
such that
\overline{I}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|)<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Let
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
.
First assume for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
x_{\varepsilon}\in T_{0}
such that
\overline{I}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|)<\varepsilon
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
x_{\varepsilon}\in T_{0}
as in the hypothesis. Then

x=x_{\varepsilon}+x-x_{\varepsilon}\leq x_{\varepsilon}+|x-x_{\varepsilon}|,

and so, using Lemma
3.12
, we obtain

(3.8)

\displaystyle\overline{I}(x)\leq I(x_{\varepsilon})+\overline{I}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|)<I(x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon.

Similarly, we have

-x=-x_{\varepsilon}-x+x_{\varepsilon}\leq-x_{\varepsilon}+|x-x_{\varepsilon}|,

and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\overline{I}(-x)\leq I(-x_{\varepsilon})+\overline{I}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|)<I(-x_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon.

Adding together (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we obtain

0\leq\overline{I}(x)-\underline{I}(x)=\overline{I}(x)+\overline{I}(-x)\leq I(x_{\varepsilon})+I(-x_{\varepsilon})+2\varepsilon=2\varepsilon.

Since this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
we have that
x
is integrable.

Conversely, assume that
x\in\mathscr{L}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\varphi\in T_{1}
with
x\leq\varphi
such that

\overline{I}(x)\leq I_{1}(\varphi)<\overline{I}(x)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Thus, using the fact that
x
is integrable and that

x\leq\varphi
, we have from Lemma
3.12

(3.10)

\displaystyle\overline{I}(|\varphi-x|)=\overline{I}(\varphi-x)\leq I_{1}(\varphi)+\overline{I}(-x)=I_{1}(\varphi)-\underline{I}(x)

Since
x\in\mathscr{L}
we know that
\overline{I}(x)=\underline{I}(x)
and so

(3.11)

\displaystyle\overline{I}(|\varphi-x|)\leq I_{1}(\varphi)-\underline{I}(x)=I_{1}(\varphi)-\overline{I}(x)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Since
\varphi\in T_{1}
there exists an
x_{\varepsilon}\in T_{0}
such that
x_{\varepsilon}\leq\varphi
and

I_{1}(\varphi)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<I(x_{\varepsilon})

Therefore

I_{1}(|\varphi-x_{\varepsilon}|)=I_{1}(\varphi-x_{\varepsilon})=I_{1}(\varphi)-I_{1}(x_{\varepsilon})=I_{1}(\varphi-x_{\varepsilon})=I_{1}(|\varphi-x_{\varepsilon}|)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Using Lemma
3.12
together with (
3.11
) we have that

\overline{I}(|x-x_{\varepsilon}|)\leq\overline{I}(|x-\varphi|+|\varphi-x_{\varepsilon}|)\leq\overline{I}(|x-\varphi|)+I_{1}(|\varphi-x_{\varepsilon}|)<\varepsilon.

This proves the result.
∎

To end this section, we describe what the functions in
\mathscr{L}
‘look like’. This can be seen as an
analogue to the regularity theorem for the Lebesgue measure (see Appendix, Theorem
7.11
).

Definition 3.22
.

We say
x\in\mathscr{L}
is a null function if
\int|x|=0
.

Proposition 3.23
.

If
x\in\mathscr{L}
is a null function and
y\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}

is such that
|y|\leq|x|
, then
y
is also a null function.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathscr{L}
be a null function and
y\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}

is such that
|y|\leq|x|
.
Note that

0\leq\underline{I}(y\lor 0)\leq\overline{I}(y\lor 0)\leq\overline{I}(|y|)\leq\overline{I}(|x|)=\int|x|=0.

Thus
y\lor 0\in\mathscr{L}
. Similarly,
(-y)\lor 0\in\mathscr{L}
and
y=[y\lor 0]-[(-y)\lor 0]\in\mathscr{L}
with

\int y=\int(y\lor 0)-\int((-y)\lor 0)=0
. Therefore
y
is also a null function.

∎

Definition 3.24
.

Define
T_{2}\subset\mathscr{L}
as follows:

T_{2}:=\left\{x\in\mathscr{L}:\text{ there exists }(x_{n})\subset T_{1}\text{ such that }(x_{n})\searrow x\text{ and }-\infty<\int x<\infty\right\}.

Up until this point we have defined multiple sets of functions:
T_{0}
,
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
, and
\mathscr{L}
.
The relationship between these sets are

Proposition 3.25
.

If
x\in T_{2}
, then there exists a
sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset T_{1}
with
I_{1}(\varphi_{n})<\infty
for

such that
(\varphi_{n})\searrow x
.

Assume
x\in T_{2}
. Then by Definition
3.24

there exists a sequence
(\psi_{n})\subset T_{1}
such that

(\psi_{n})\searrow x
. Also, since

and there

\varphi\in T_{1}

We thus have for all

.
∎

Theorem 3.26

(Characterisation Theorem)
.

For any function
x\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
, we have that

x\in\mathscr{L}
if and only if
x=y-z
where
y\in T_{2}
and
z
is a nonnegative null function.

Proof.

Assume firstly that
x=y-z
with
y\in T_{2}
and
z\geq 0
a null function.
Then both
z,y\in\mathscr{L}
and so
x\in\mathscr{L}
by Theorem
3.14
.

Now assume that
x\in\mathscr{L}
. Then
\int x=\overline{I}(x)<\infty
and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists

\varphi_{n}\in T_{1}
such that
x\leq\varphi_{n}
and

\overline{I}(x)\leq I_{1}(\varphi_{n})\leq\overline{I}(x)+\frac{1}{n}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

y_{n}:=\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}\land\cdots\land\varphi_{n}.

Note that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y_{n}\leq\varphi_{n}
and that
y_{n}\in T_{1}
.
Also
(y_{n})
is a decreasing sequence with
x\leq y_{n}

and

-\infty<\overline{I}(x)\leq I_{1}(y_{n})\leq\overline{I}(x)+\frac{1}{n}<\infty

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
I_{1}(y_{n})<\infty
we have that
y_{n}\in\mathscr{L}

and
I_{1}(y_{n})=\int y_{n}
.
Furthermore,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int y_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(y_{n})
is finite.
Let
y:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}y_{n}
pointwise. Then by Theorem

3.16
we have
y\in\mathscr{L}
, hence also in
T_{2}
,
and
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int y_{n}=\int y
.

We thus have

\int x=\overline{I}(x)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{1}(y_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int y_{n}=\int y.

We also know
x\leq y
so if we set
z:=y-x
then it follows that
0\leq z
.
Furthermore,
z\in\mathscr{L}
and

\int|z|=\int z=\int y-\int x=0.

Hence
z
is a null function and
x=y-z
where
y\in T_{2}
and

z
is a null function. This completes the proof.
∎

Measure from the Integral

We described a procedure that extends a linear functional to one that
allows the classical measure-theoretic limit theorems in

We now show that this extension is, in fact, the same as the measure-theoretic construction.
The material of this section

Throughout this section, we let
X
be a set,
T_{0}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}

denote an extended vector lattice, and

I:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
an

Let

be the extension of

If

Explicitly, for

To begin with, we define the Daniell analogue of measurable functions.

Definition 4.1
.

We say a nonnegative function

is

if for all

we have
\varphi\land x\in\mathscr{L}

if its characteristic function

Using this definition we have:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
x,y\in{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
. Then

(i)

if
x
and
y
are Daniell measurable functions, then so are
x\lor y
and
x\land y
; and

(ii)

if
(x_{n})
is a sequence of nonnegative Daniell measurable
functions converging pointwise to a function

, then

is also Daniell measurable.

Proof.

To prove (i), let
x
and
y
be nonnegative and measurable and let
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}
.
Then because
\mathscr{L}
is closed under
\land
with both

x\land\varphi
and

in
\mathscr{L}

(x\land y)\land\varphi=(x\land\varphi)\land(y\land\varphi)\in\mathscr{L}.

Similarly, because
\mathscr{L}
is closed under
\lor
with both

y\land\varphi
and
x\land\varphi
in
\mathscr{L}

(x\lor y)\land\varphi=(x\land\varphi)\lor(y\land\varphi)\in\mathscr{L}.

To prove (ii), let
(x_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative Daniell measurable functions.
If
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}

then since

for

|x_{n}\land\varphi|\leq|\varphi|
.
We also know that

Hence by Theorem

, we have that

As this holds for all

is measurable. ∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let

be nonnegative.
We have

for each

Proof.

Assume

We claim that

for each

for each integrable
\varphi\in T_{1}

We claim that

By Proposition

and so
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}
.
Since

(4.1)

\displaystyle\varphi\land 0\leq\varphi\land x\leq\varphi_{n}\land x\leq\varphi_{1}\land x,

for each

. We know by Proposition

Hence from (

for all

If

\varphi=\psi-z

We then have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\psi\land x-\varphi\land x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\psi+x-|\psi-x|)-\frac{1}{2}(\varphi+x-|\varphi-x|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\psi-\varphi+|\varphi-x|-|\psi-x|)

\displaystyle=\psi-\varphi=z.

is a Daniell measurable function.

The converse follows from the fact that

.
Hence if

∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be the class of all Daniell measurable sets.
Then
\mathscr{A}
is a
\sigma
-ring. Furthermore if
\chi_{X}
is a Daniell measurable function, then

\mathscr{A}
is a
\sigma
-algebra.

Proof.

To show that
\mathscr{A}
is a
\sigma
-ring, it suffices to show that,
\mathscr{A}

is closed under

(i)

relative difference, and

(ii)

countable union.

To prove (i), let
A,B\in\mathscr{A}
.

Claim.
For all
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}
,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B}=\varphi\land\chi_{A}-\varphi\land(\chi_{A}\land\chi_{B})+\varphi\land 0

Proof of Claim.
Let
t\in X
. If
\varphi(t)\leq 0
, since characteristic functions are all
nonnegative, then we have

(\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B})(t)=\varphi(t)=(\varphi\land\chi_{A})(t)-(\varphi\land(\chi_{A}\land\chi_{B}))(t)+(\varphi\land 0)(t).

If
\varphi(t)\geq 0
and
t\in A\setminus B
, then

(\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B})(t)=\min\{\varphi(t),1\},

and

(\varphi\land\chi_{A})(t)-(\varphi\land(\chi_{A}\land\chi_{B}))(t)+(\varphi\land 0)(t)=\min\{\varphi(t),1\}-0+0=\min\{\varphi(t),1\}.

If
\varphi(t)\geq 0
and

, then

(\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B})(t)=0,

and

(\varphi\land\chi_{A})(t)-(\varphi\land(\chi_{A}\land\chi_{B}))(t)+(\varphi\land 0)(t)=\min\{\varphi(t),1\}-\min\{\varphi(t),1\}+0=0.

Finally, if
\varphi(t)\geq 0
and
t\notin A
, then

(\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B})(t)=0,

and

(\varphi\land\chi_{A})(t)-(\varphi\land(\chi_{A}\land\chi_{B}))(t)+(\varphi\land 0)(t)=0.

Thus, for all

, we have that (
4.2
) holds. This proves the claim.

Thus, if
\varphi\in\mathscr{L}
then each term on the right side of (
4.2
) is in
\mathscr{L}
.
We conclude that
\varphi\land\chi_{A\setminus B}
is a Daniell measurable function
and
A\setminus B
is a Daniell measurable set.

To show (ii), let
(A_{n})
be a sequence of sets in
\mathscr{A}
. Then for each

we have that

\chi_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}}=\chi_{A_{1}}\lor\chi_{A_{2}}\lor\cdots\lor\chi_{A_{n}}

and this is Daniell measurable by Lemma

.
Furthermore
(\chi_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}})\nearrow\chi_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}}
and

\chi_{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}}
is a
Daniell measurable function.
Thus
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}
is a Daniell measurable set.

These properties show that
\mathscr{A}
is a
\sigma
-ring.

If
\chi_{X}
is a Daniell measurable function, then

X
is a Daniell measurable set. Hence

and
\mathscr{A}
is a

\sigma
-algebra.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Assume that
\chi_{X}
is Daniell measurable.
Let
\mu:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be defined
for
E\in\mathscr{A}
by

\mu(E):=\begin{cases}\int\chi_{E}&\text{ if $\chi_{E}$ is integrable}\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise. }\\
\end{cases}

Then
\mu
is a measure.

Proof.

We first show that
\mu
is nonnegative and that
\mu(\emptyset)=0
.
If
E\in\mathscr{A}
, then we have that
0\leq\chi_{E}
.
If
\mu(E)
is finite, then since
0\leq\chi_{E}
we have

0\leq\int\chi_{E}=\mu(E).

Furthermore,
\chi_{\emptyset}=0
and so
Since
\int
is linear by Proposition
3.14
, we have that

\mu(\emptyset)=\int\chi_{\emptyset}=\int 0=0.

We now show that
\mu
is countably additive.
First assume that
(E_{n})
is a sequence of disjoint sets
in
\mathscr{A}
such that all of
\mu(E_{n})
are finite.
Since these sets are disjoint we have

\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi_{E_{n}}
.
If
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\chi_{E_{n}}<\infty
, then by Theorem
3.16
, we have

\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi_{E_{n}}\in\mathscr{L},

and

\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\int\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}}=\int\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi_{E_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\chi_{E_{n}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(E_{n}).

If
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\chi_{E_{n}}=\infty
,
then
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}\right)=\infty
. For if this is not the case, then
\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}}\in\mathscr{L}
,
and for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sum_{n=1}^{N}\int\chi_{E_{n}}=\int\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}E_{n}}\leq\int\chi_{\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}}<\infty,

contradicting the assumption that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int\chi_{E_{n}}=\infty
.
We conclude that
\mu
is a measure.
∎

We defined a measure on our set

and a Daniell Integral

on
the class

of

connects the two and finalises the connection between the Daniell Integral and a measure.

Theorem 4.6

(Daniell-Stone)
.

Let

be an extended vector lattice of functions on a set

with the
property that
\chi_{X}\land\varphi\in T_{0}
whenever
\varphi\in T_{0}
. Let

be an

-integral on

. Then there is a
\sigma
-algebra

\mathscr{A}
on

and a measure
\mu
on
\mathscr{A}
such that

is integrable with respect to
\mu
if and only
if it is
I
-integrable.
Furthermore if
\varphi
is both
I
- and
\mu
-integrable then

\int\varphi=\int\varphi\,d\mu.

Proof.

Our assumption that
\chi_{X}\land\varphi\in T_{0}
for each

\varphi\in T_{0}
together with
Lemma
4.3
allows us to conclude that

\chi_{X}
is a Daniell measurable function.
Theorems
4.4
and

guarantee the
existence of the
\sigma
-algebra

\mathscr{A}
and measure
\mu
on
X
.

Claim.
If
x\in\mathscr{L}
, then
x
is
\mu
-measurable.

Proof of Claim.
Since every function in
\mathscr{L}

is the difference of two nonnegative functions, it is sufficient to
consider nonnegative functions in
\mathscr{L}
.
Without loss of generality let
x\in\mathscr{L}
with
x\geq 0

and let

E_{\alpha}:=\{t\in X:x(t)>\alpha\}.

If
\alpha\leq 0
then
E_{\alpha}=X\in\mathscr{A}
. So assume
\alpha>0
.
Let

y=\frac{1}{\alpha}x-\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}x\right)\land\chi_{X}.

Since
\chi_{X}
is Daniell measurable,

\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}x\right)\land\chi_{X}\in\mathscr{L}

and so

is also in
\mathscr{L}
.
Let
t\in X
. If
t\in E_{\alpha}
then
\alpha<x(t)
. This implies that
\chi_{X}(t)<\frac{1}{\alpha}x(t)
and
thus

. If
t\notin E_{\alpha}
, then
x(t)\leq\alpha
and
\frac{1}{\alpha}x(t)\leq\chi_{X}(t)
.
Hence
y(t)=0
. In other words
0<y(t)
if and only if
t\in E_{\alpha}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

(4.3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}:=\chi_{X}\land ny

(see Figure
4
for an illustration). Then
(\varphi_{n})
is a sequence of Daniell measurable
functions such that
(\varphi_{n})\nearrow\chi_{E_{\alpha}}
.
Thus by Lemma
4.2

\chi_{E_{\alpha}}
is Daniell measurable
and

. This proves the claim.

An illustration of
\varphi_{n}
in (

).

Let
x
be nonnegative in
\mathscr{L}
. We aim to show that

is integrable with respect to
\mu
.
For
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

(4.4)

\displaystyle E_{k,n}:=\{t\in X:x(t)>k2^{-n}\}.

From the proven claim above we know

and
\chi_{E_{k,n}}
is Daniell measurable.
Furthermore,

\chi_{E_{k,n}}=\chi_{E_{k,n}}\land(2^{n}k^{-1}x)\in\mathscr{L}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset\mathscr{L}
by

(4.5)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}:=2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\chi_{E_{k,n}}.

Claim.
The sequence
(\varphi_{n})
is increasing.

Proof of Claim.
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Note that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n+1}=2^{-(n+1)}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2(n+1)}}\chi_{E_{k,n+1}}=2^{-n}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{2(n+1)-1}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{E_{2j,n+1}}+\chi_{E_{2j-1,n+1}}\right).

Also, if

, then

and so

.
Similarly, if

, then

The sequence

.

If

and
x(t)

.

\displaystyle\int x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int 2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\chi_{E_{k,n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\int\chi_{E_{k,n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\mu(E_{k,n}).

The measure-theoretic definition of the integral for simple functions gives us

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\mu(E_{k,n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi_{n}\,d\mu.

Thus

We now show the converse of the theorem for nonnegative functions.
Assume

.
We aim to show that

As in (
4.9
) for

\displaystyle E_{k,n}:=\{t\in X:x(t)>k2^{-n}\},

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}:=2^{-n}\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2n}}\chi_{E_{k,n}}.

Since the (measure-theoretic) integral with respect to

.

In the same way as in (

and applying Theorem
3.16
we have

\int x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi_{n}\,d\mu=\int x\,d\mu.

We conclude that
x
is
I
-integrable and that the integrals coincide.

If
x\in\mathscr{L}
is an arbitrary
I
-integrable function, not necessarily
nonnegative, then we can write
x
as a difference of two
nonnegative functions

x=x\lor 0-(-x)\lor 0

which are
I
-integrable by Proposition
3.14
and see that
x
is also
integrable with respect to
\mu
. Similarly if
x
is an arbitrary
\mu
-integrable
function, not necessarily nonnegative, then
x
can be written as a
difference between two nonnegative
\mu
-integrable functions and so
x
is
I
-integrable.
This completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of Theorem
4.6
, we obtain Carathéodory’s Extension Theorem.
We refer the readers to the Appendix for the definitions of
pre-measure (Definition
7.3
), and the notion of a
\sigma
-algebra
generated by a ring (Definition
7.7
).
Before we prove Carathéodory’s Extension Theorem, we first consider a lemma.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\mathscr{R}
be a ring of subsets of
X
.
Let
\mu:\mathscr{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a pre-measure.
We define

T:=\text{span}\left\{\chi_{R}:R\in\mathscr{R}\text{ and }\mu(R)<\infty\right\}.

Define
I:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:
For each
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
let

I\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu(R_{i}).

Then

(i)

T
is an extended vector lattice; and

(ii)

I
is an
I
-integral.

Proof.

Before we continue with the proof, we first make the following claim.

Claim.
If
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
, then there exists
a collection of pairwise disjoint sets

\{B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{n}\}
in
\mathscr{R}
and real numbers

\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n}\}
such that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{l}c_{k}\chi_{B_{k}}
.

We provide the proof in the Appendix (Lemma
7.14
).

We prove (i). By definition,
T
is closed under linear combinations.
We thus only need to show that
T
is closed
under
\land
and
\lor
. By the identities in Remark
3.13
,
it is sufficient to show that
T
is closed under
absolute value. Let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
. Using the above claim, we may assume
without loss of generality that the sets
R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{n}
are pairwise disjoint.
We then get the following representation: for
t\in X

x(t):=\begin{cases}c_{i}&\text{ if }t\in R_{i}\\
0&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

From this it is seen that
|x|
is given by

|x(t)|:=\begin{cases}|c_{i}|&\text{ if }t\in R_{i}\\
0&\text{ else,}\end{cases}

for
t\in X
. Thus
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_{i}|\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
. Hence
T
is a vector
lattice, and thus an extended vector lattice.

We now show (ii). The proof that
I
is well-defined and linear,
and hence satisfies (D1) from Definition
3.3
, is a standard
result in measure theory and we defer the proof to another source
[
9
, p. 78-79]
.

Assume that
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T_{0}
such that
x\geq 0
.
Using the claim we may assume without loss of
generality that the sets
R_{i}
for
i=1,2,\cdots,n
are pairwise disjoint.
For each
i=1,2,\cdots,n
we pick a
t_{i}\in R_{i}
. Then we get that for
i=1,2,\cdots,n

c_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}(t_{i})=x(t_{i})\geq 0

and so

I(x)=I\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu(R_{i})\geq 0.

Since this holds for all
x\in T
with
x\geq 0
, we conclude that
I
is satisfies (D3) from Definition

3.3
.

Recall that
T
consists of linear combinations of characteristic function of
sets in
\mathscr{R}
.
Assume
(x_{n})\subset T
is a sequence such that
(x_{n})\searrow 0
and fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Let

A:=\left\{t\in X:x_{1}(t)>0\right\}.

Note that, since
A
is the finite union of
the sets in that characteristic functions making up
x_{1}
,
A\in\mathscr{R}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

A_{n}:=\left\{t\in X:x_{n}(t)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}\right\}
.
Note once again that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
because
A_{n}
is the finite union of
some of the sets in that characteristic functions making up
x_{n}
, we have
that
A_{n}\in\mathscr{R}
. Since
(x_{n})
is a decreasing sequence,
we have the inclusions

A\supset A_{1}\supset A_{2}\supset A_{3}\supset\cdots.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
B_{n}:=A_{n}\backslash A_{n+1}
. Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary. We then have

\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}B_{n}=\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}A_{n}\backslash A_{n+1}=A_{N}\backslash\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}.

Since we assumed that
(x_{n})\searrow 0
, we have for all
t\in X
, that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}x_{n}(t)=0
, and so
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}=\emptyset
.
Consequently,
A_{N}=\bigcup_{n=N}^{\infty}B_{n}
. Since the sets
B_{n}
are disjoint,

\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\mu(B_{n})=\mu(A_{N})\leq\mu(A)<\infty.

If
x_{1}=0
, then since
(x_{n})\searrow 0
, we have that
x_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

and the result holds. Therefore, without loss of generality, we may assume that
x_{1}\neq 0
and
thus, that
\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}>0
.
Choose
N
large enough so that

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mu(A_{N})=\sum_{n=N}^{\infty}\mu(B_{n})<\frac{\varepsilon}{2\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}}.

If
n>N
and
x_{n}
is given by
x_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}c_{k}\chi_{R_{k}}

where all the
R_{k}
’s are disjoint
(note that we are hiding the
R_{k}
’s and
c_{k}
’s dependence on
n
), then we may write
x_{n}

in the folllowing way:

x_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\chi_{R_{k}\backslash A_{n}}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\chi_{R_{k}\cap A_{n}}.

Note that if
c_{k}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}
, then for all
t\in R_{k}
we have
x_{n}(t)>\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}
.
Hence if
c_{k}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}
, then
R_{k}\subset A_{n}
and
R_{k}\backslash A_{n}=\emptyset
.
We thus have

\displaystyle I(x_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mu(R_{k}\backslash A_{n})+\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\mu(R_{k}\cap A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mu(R_{k}\backslash A_{n})+\left(\max_{k}c_{k}\right)\mu(A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2\mu(A)}\mu(A)+\left(\max_{k}c_{k}\right)\mu(A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(\max\left\{x_{n}(t):t\in X\right\}\right)\mu(A_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}\right)\mu(A_{N}).

Thus, in light of (
4.11
), we have

I(x_{n})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}\mu(A_{N})\right)<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left(\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}\right)\frac{\varepsilon}{2\max\left\{x_{1}(t):t\in X\right\}}=\varepsilon.

Thus, we have shown that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I(x_{n})=0
, proving (D2) of Definition
3.3
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.8
.

(Carathéodory’s Extension Theorem)
Let
\mathscr{R}
be a ring of subsets of
X
.
Let
\mu:\mathscr{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a pre-measure.
Let
\sigma(\mathscr{R})
be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathscr{R}
.
Then
\mu
extends to a measure

\mu^{\prime}:\sigma(\mathscr{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Proof.

We want to apply Theorem
4.6
. Let
T
and
I:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be
as defined in Lemma
4.7
.
By Lemma
4.7
,

T
is an extended vector lattice and

I
is an
I
-integral. We thus only need to prove that

\chi_{X}\land\varphi\in T
for each
\varphi\in T
.
Note that if
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
then

\chi_{X}\land\varphi=\chi_{X}\land\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}\min(c_{i},1)\chi_{R_{i}}\in T.

Hence the condition for Theorem
4.6
holds
and we conclude the existence of a
\sigma
-algebra

\mathscr{A}
a measure
\mu^{\prime}
on
X
. We show
that
\mathscr{R}\subset\mathscr{A}
and that

\mu^{\prime}=\mu
on
\mathscr{R}
.
If it is the case that
R\in\mathscr{R}

with
\mu(R)<\infty
we have

\mu^{\prime}(R)=\int\chi_{R}=I(\chi_{R})=\mu(R),

and so
R\in\mathscr{A}
.

If it is the case that
R\in\mathscr{R}
such that
\mu(R)=\infty
,
then let
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
.
Without loss of generality, by Lemma
7.14
, we may assume that the
sets
R_{i}
for
i=0,1,\dots,n
are disjoint. We then have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\varphi\land\chi_{R}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right)\land\chi_{R}=\sum_{c_{i}<0}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}+\sum_{c_{i}\geq 0}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}\cap R}\in T.

Since
\varphi\in T
was arbitrary, we know that

\varphi\land\chi_{R}\in T
for each
\varphi\in T

and we conclude, using Lemma
4.3

that
\chi_{R}
is a Daniell measurable function
and hence that
R\in\mathscr{A}
.

Claim.
We have
\mu^{\prime}(R)=\mu(R)=\infty
.

Proof of Claim.
Suppose, to the contrary, that
\mu^{\prime}(R)<\infty
.
Then, by the definition of the measure obtained from Theorem
4.6
,

\mu^{\prime}(R)=\int\chi_{R}=\overline{I}(\chi_{R}),

and there exists a
\varphi\in T_{1}
such that
\chi_{R}\leq\varphi
and

\overline{I}(\chi_{R})\leq I_{1}(\varphi)\leq\overline{I}(\chi_{R})+1=\mu^{\prime}(R)+1<\infty.

Since
\varphi\in T_{1}
, there exists a sequence
(\varphi_{n})\subset T
such that

(\varphi_{n})\nearrow\varphi
. Since
\chi_{R}\leq\varphi
, we have that

(\varphi_{n}\land\chi_{R})\nearrow\chi_{R}
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In the same way as
was done in (
4.12
), we write

\varphi_{n}\land\chi_{R}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right)\land\chi_{R}=\sum_{c_{i}<0}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}+\sum_{c_{i}\geq 0}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}\cap R},

with the
R_{i}
’s disjoint and conclude that
\varphi_{n}\land\chi_{R}\in T

(once again, we hide the dependence of the
R_{i}
’s and
c_{i}
’s on
n
).
If we replace all the positive
c_{i}
’s with 1, and drop all the negative
c_{i}
’s,
we are left with

\sum_{c_{i}\geq 0}\chi_{R_{i}\cap R}=\chi_{\bigcup_{c_{i}\geq 0}R_{i}}\in T.

Define
R_{n}:=\bigcup_{c_{i}\geq 0}R_{i}
.
Note that, since
\chi_{R_{n}}\in T
,
we have that
\mu(R_{n})<\infty
. Also note that we have

\varphi_{n}\land\chi_{R}\leq\chi_{R_{n}}\leq\chi_{R}.

Therefore, since
(\varphi_{n}\land\chi_{R})
is increasing,
so is
(\chi_{R_{n}})
and we have that
(\chi_{R_{n}})\nearrow\chi_{R}
.
Thus
R=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{n}
with
R_{1}\subset R_{2}\subset\cdots\subset R
,
and we have

\mu(R)=\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}R_{n}\right)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mu(R_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\chi_{R_{n}}\leq\int\chi_{R}=\mu^{\prime}(R)<\infty.

This contradicts our assumption that
\mu(R)=\infty
, and we conclude that
\mu(R)=\mu^{\prime}(R)=\infty
.
This proves the claim.

We conclude that

\mathscr{R}\subset\mathscr{A}
and hence that

\sigma(\mathscr{R})\subset\mathscr{A}
and that the measure

\mu=\mu^{\prime}
on
\mathscr{R}
.
∎

4.1.
Application to
X=\mathbb{R}

We aim to construct the Lebesgue integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
T_{0}
be the continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}
with compact support.
For the purposes
of this section, denote the Riemann integral of a function
f

by
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx
.
From elementary analysis, we know the Riemann integral to be linear
and positive. Hence the Riemann integral satisfies (D1) and (D3) of
Definition
3.15
. To show (D3) of Definition
3.15
,
we state it as a proposition.

Proposition 4.9
.

If we have a sequence
(f_{n})
of continuous functions with compact support such that

(f_{n})\searrow 0
, then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)\,dx=0
.

Proof.

Let
(f_{n})
be a decreasing sequence of continuous functions with compact support such that

(f_{n})\searrow 0
.
Then since
f_{1}
has compact support, there exists

a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{1}
is zero outside of
[a,b]
. Since for all

n\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
0\leq f_{n}\leq f_{1}
,
the functions
f_{n}
are all zero outside
of
[a,b]
.
We thus have for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

0\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(x)\,dx\leq\int_{a}^{b}\left(\sup_{t\in[a,b]}f_{n}(t)\right)\,dx=(b-a)\sup_{t\in[a,b]}f_{n}(t).

It thus suffices to show that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{t\in[a,b]}f_{n}(t)=0
, i.e.
that
(f_{n})
converges to 0 uniformly. This follows from the standard result in analysis
known as Dini’s theorem which we state fully in the Appendix (Theorem
7.13
).
∎

Consequently, the Riemann integral is a Daniell integral on
T_{0}
.
From Theorem
4.4

and Theorem
4.5
, we know there exists a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{A}
and
a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
.
We claim that
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure.

Denote the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
by
m
.
We first show that
\mathscr{A}
contains the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
It is sufficient to show that
\mathscr{A}

contains all the open intervals of finite length.
Let
J=(a,b)
be an open interval.
We construct a sequence of continuous functions that increase toward
\chi_{J}
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
f_{n}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be

(4.13)

\displaystyle f_{n}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{2n}{b-a}(x-a)&\text{ if }x\in\left[a,a+\frac{b-a}{2n}\right]\\
1&\text{ if }x\in\left[a+\frac{b-a}{2n},b-\frac{b-a}{2n}\right]\\
\frac{2n}{b-a}(b-x)&\text{ if }x\in\left[b-\frac{b-a}{2n},b\right]\\
0&\text{ else. }\end{cases}

Figure 5.
An illustration of
f_{n}
in (
4.13
).

Then
(f_{n})\nearrow\chi_{J}
(see Figure
5
) and
\chi_{J}\in T_{1}
.
Furthermore, if
I_{1}
denotes the extension from
Definition
3.8
of the Riemann integral to
T_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(\chi_{J})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{n}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\int_{a}^{a+\frac{b-a}{2n}}\frac{2n}{b-a}(x-a)\,dx+\int_{a+\frac{b-a}{2n}}^{b-\frac{b-a}{2n}}1\,dx+\int_{b-\frac{b-a}{2n}}^{b}\frac{2n}{b-a}(b-x)\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left[\left(\frac{b-a}{4n}\right)+\left(b-a-\frac{b-a}{n}\right)+\left(\frac{b-a}{4n}\right)\right]=b-a.

Thus
\chi_{J}\in\mathscr{L}
and is a Daniell measurable function.
Hence
J\in\mathscr{A}
and since this holds for an arbitrary open interval
J
,
\mathscr{A}

contains the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
Furthermore, the measure
\mu
obtained from Theorem
4.5
satisfies

\mu(J)=\int\chi_{J}=b-a=m(J).

Thus
\mu
corresponds to
m
when restricted to the open intervals, and hence the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

Using a result in measure theory (Theorem
7.12
in the
Appendix), to show
\mathscr{A}
contains the
Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebra and that
\mu=m
on the Lebesgue measurable sets, it is
sufficient to prove that
\mu
is complete (for the definition of completeness, we refer the readers to
Definition
7.6
in the Appendix).

Lemma 4.10
.

The measure
\mu
given by Theorem
4.6
is complete.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathscr{A}
such that
\mu(A)=0
and
B\subset A
.
Since
B\subset A
, we have
0\leq\chi_{B}\leq\chi_{A}
and

0\leq\underline{I}(\chi_{B})\leq\overline{I}(\chi_{B})\leq\overline{I}(\chi_{A})=\int\chi_{A}=0.

Hence the inequality is, in fact, an equality and

\chi_{B}\in\mathscr{L}
with
\mu(B)=\int\chi_{B}=0
.
Thus
B\in\mathscr{A}
and
B
is a null set. We conclude
\mu
is complete.
∎

We conclude that
\mu
coincides with the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}

and that the Daniell Integral constructed in this way is the Lebesgue integral.

5.
S
-Integral

As in Section
3
, let
X
be a set and
let
T_{0}\subset{\overline{\mathbb{R}}}^{X}
be an extended vector lattice.
We discussed Daniell’s method of extending an
I
-integral on
T_{0}
to a
larger class. Daniell mentioned another integral in his paper
[
1
]

which generalises the notion of the Stieltjies Integral.
In this section, denote the set of all nonnegative functions in
T_{0}
by

T_{0}^{+}:=\left\{x\in T_{0}:0\leq x\right\}.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
S:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We call
S
an
S
-integral
if for all
x,y\in T_{0}
,

\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, and sequences
(x_{n})\subset T_{0}
:

(S1)

S(\alpha x+\beta y)=\alpha S(x)+\beta S(y)
;

(S2)

(x_{n})\searrow 0
implies
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S(x_{n})=0
; and

(S3)

there exists a function
M:T_{0}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with the properties that
if
\varphi,\psi\in T_{0}^{+}
such that
\varphi\leq\psi

then
M(\varphi)\leq M(\psi)
and for all

x\in T_{0}
we have
|S(x)|\leq M(|x|)
.

Proposition 5.2
.

An
I
-integral is an
S
-integral.

Proof.

Any
I
-integral satisfies (S1) and (S2), as these are
(D1) and (D2) of Definition
3.3
.
For all
x\in T_{0}
we have
-|x|\leq x\leq|x|
and
thus by (D3) we have
-I(|x|)\leq I(x)\leq I(|x|)
.
Thus
|I(x)|\leq I(|x|)
and
I
takes the role of
M
in (S3).
∎

We now discuss the relationship between the
S
-integral and the
I
-integral. We aim to show that an
S
-integral can be
expressed as the difference between two
I
-integrals.

Definition 5.3
.

If
x\in T_{0}^{+}
define

P(x):=\sup\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}.

Recall that a function
x\in T_{0}
, can be expressed as
x=x\lor 0-(-x)\lor 0

where both
x\lor 0
and
(-x)\lor 0
are nonnegative.
Then for
x\in T_{0}\setminus T_{0}^{+}
define

S^{+}(x):=P(x\lor 0)-P((-x)\lor 0).

Remark 5.4
.

In Definition
5.3
, the supremum of the set

\left\{S(\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}:\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}

exists.
For if
x\in T_{0}^{+}
and
\varphi\in T_{0}
with

0\leq\varphi\leq x
we have

S(\varphi)\leq M(|\varphi|)=M(\varphi)\leq M(x)
.
Thus the set
\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}
is bounded above by
M(x)
and the supremum exists.
Also note that
S^{+}
corresponds with
P
on the set
T_{0}^{+}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

S^{+}
is an
I
-integral.

We break the proof into a sequence of lemmas.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in T_{0}^{+}
. Then
P(x_{1}+x_{2})=P(x_{1})+P(x_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in T_{0}
such that
0\leq x_{1}
and
0\leq x_{2}
.
If
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in T_{0}
with
0\leq\varphi_{1}\leq x_{1}
and
0\leq\varphi_{2}\leq x_{2}
then

0\leq\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}\leq x_{1}+x_{2}
and so

S(\varphi_{1})+S(\varphi_{2})=S(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})\leq P(x_{1}+x_{2}).

Since this holds for all

\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in T_{0}
such that
0\leq\varphi_{1}\leq x_{1}
and
0\leq\varphi_{2}\leq x_{2}

we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle P(x_{1})+P(x_{2})\leq P(x_{1}+x_{2}).

To show the reverse inequality, let
\varphi\in T_{0}
such that
0\leq\varphi\leq x_{1}+x_{2}
.
Then
\varphi-x_{1}\leq x_{2}
and since
0\leq x_{2}
we have

(5.2)

\displaystyle(\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0\leq x_{2}.

Furthermore,

\varphi+x_{1}=\varphi\land x_{1}+\varphi\lor x_{1}=\varphi\land x_{1}+(\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0+x_{1}.

Subtracting
x_{1}
from both sides we get
\varphi=\varphi\land x_{1}+(\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0
.
Using (
5.2
) and the fact that

0\leq\varphi\land x_{1}\leq x_{1}
, we have

S(\varphi\land x_{1})\leq P(x_{1})

and

S((\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0)\leq P(x_{2}).

Consequently, we get that

\displaystyle S(\varphi)

\displaystyle=S(\varphi\land x_{1}+(\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0)

\displaystyle=S(\varphi\land x_{1})+S((\varphi-x_{1})\lor 0)

\displaystyle\leq P(x_{1})+P(x_{2}).

Since this holds for all
\varphi\in T_{0}
with
0\leq\varphi\leq x_{1}+x_{2}
we have

P(x_{1}+x_{2})\leq P(x_{1})+P(x_{2}).

This, together with (
5.1
) allows us to conclude
that for
x_{1},x_{2}\in T_{0}^{+}
we have

P(x_{1}+x_{2})=P(x_{1})+P(x_{2}).

∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
x\in T_{0}^{+}
, then
P(cx)=cP(x)
for each nonnegative scalar
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in T_{0}^{+}
and
c
a nonnegative scalar.
Then

\displaystyle P(cx)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq cx\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{S(c\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}

\displaystyle=c\,\sup\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}

\displaystyle=cP(x).\qed

We have shown that
P
is additive and preserves
positive scalar multiplication on
T_{0}^{+}
.
We now proceed to prove linearity of
S^{+}
on
T_{0}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

We have:

(i)

For all
x\in T_{0}
, if
x=\varphi-\psi
with

\varphi\geq 0
and
\psi\geq 0
, then
S^{+}(x)=P(\varphi)-P(\psi)
.

(ii)

For
x_{1},x_{2}\in T_{0}
,
S^{+}(x_{1}+x_{2})=S^{+}(x_{1})+S^{+}(x_{2})
.

(iii)

For all
x\in T_{0}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
S^{+}(cx)=cS^{+}(x)
.

Proof.

We prove (i).
Let
x\in T_{0}
and
\varphi,\psi\in T_{0}
both nonnegative
such that
x=\varphi-\psi
. Recall that we can write
x=x\lor 0-(-x)\lor 0
. Thus

\varphi-\psi=x=x\lor 0-(-x)\lor 0,

and adding
\psi+(-x)\lor 0
to the equality we have

\varphi+(-x)\lor 0=x\lor 0+\psi.

Because all these functions are nonnegative, using Lemma
5.6
, we have

\displaystyle P(x\lor 0)+P(\psi)

\displaystyle=P(x\lor 0+\psi)

\displaystyle=P(\varphi+(-x)\lor 0)

\displaystyle=P(\varphi)+P((-x)\lor 0).

Rearranging, we have

S^{+}(x)=P(x\lor 0)-P((-x)\lor 0)=P(\varphi)-P(\psi).

This proves (i) and we conclude that the way we decompose
x
as the difference
between two positive functions does not matter in the definition of
S^{+}
.

We prove (ii).
Let
x_{1},x_{2}\in T_{0}
and
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in T_{0}
all nonnegative such that

x_{1}=\varphi_{1}-\psi_{1}
and
x_{2}=\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2}
.
Then we have
x_{1}+x_{2}=(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})-(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})
. Hence, using Lemma
5.6
,

\displaystyle S^{+}(x_{1}+x_{2})

\displaystyle=P(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})-P(\psi_{1}+\psi_{2})

\displaystyle=P(\varphi_{1})+P(\varphi_{2})-P(\psi_{1})-P(\psi_{2})

\displaystyle=P(\varphi_{1})-P(\psi_{1})+P(\varphi_{2})-P(\psi_{2}).

Using (i), we know that
S^{+}(x_{1})=P(\varphi_{1})-P(\psi_{1})
and

S^{+}(x_{2})=P(\varphi_{2})-P(\psi_{2})
. Thus we have

S^{+}(x_{1}+x_{2})=P(\varphi_{1})-P(\psi_{1})+P(\varphi_{2})-P(\psi_{2})=S^{+}(x_{1})+S^{+}(x_{2}).

This shows that
S^{+}
is additive.

We prove (iii).
Let
x\in T_{0}
. If
c\in\mathbb{R}
is a nonnegative scalar, then we have

\displaystyle S^{+}(cx)

\displaystyle=P((cx)\lor 0)-P((-cx)\lor 0)

\displaystyle=cP(x\lor 0)-cP((-x)\lor 0)

\displaystyle=cS^{+}(x).

If
c
is a negative scalar, then

\displaystyle S^{+}(cx)

\displaystyle=P((cx)\lor 0)-P((-cx)\lor 0)

\displaystyle=(-c)P((-x)\lor 0)-(-c)P(x\lor 0)

\displaystyle=cP(x\lor 0)-cP((-x)\lor 0)

\displaystyle=cS^{+}(x).

We conclude that
S^{+}(cx)=cS^{+}(x)
for all scalars
c
,
and thus
S^{+}
is linear.
∎

Lemma
5.7
(ii) and (iii) is (D1) of Definition
3.3
.

Remark 5.9
.

Property (D3) of Definition
3.3
is satisfied by
S^{+}
,
for if
x\in T_{0}
with
x\geq 0
,
then
S(0)\in\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x\right\}

and so
0=S(0)\leq S^{+}(x)
.

What remains is to show that
S^{+}
satisfies (D2) of Definition
3.3
.

Lemma 5.10
.

If
(x_{n})\subset T_{0}
is a sequence such that
(x_{n})\searrow 0
then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{+}(x_{n})=0.

Proof.

Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{n})\searrow 0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Then, using the definition of supremum, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
there exists a
\varphi_{n}\in T_{0}
such that
0\leq\varphi_{n}\leq x_{n}
and

(5.3)

\displaystyle P(x_{n})=\sup\left\{S(\varphi):\varphi\in T_{0}\text{ such that }0\leq\varphi\leq x_{n}\right\}\leq S(\varphi_{n})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n}}.

Define the sequence
(\psi_{n})\subset T_{0}
by

\psi_{n}:=\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}\land\cdots\land\varphi_{n},

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We show inductively that
P(x_{n})\leq S(\psi_{n})+(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}})\varepsilon
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We know
\psi_{1}=\varphi_{1}
and so the inequality

P(x_{1})\leq S(\psi_{1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}

holds. Hence the base case is satisfied.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. As the induction hypothesis, assume that

(5.4)

\displaystyle P(x_{n})\leq S(\psi_{n})+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\varepsilon.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
0\leq\psi_{n}\leq x_{n}
and
0\leq\varphi_{n+1}\leq x_{n+1}\leq x_{n}
.
Consequently, we get the following inequality

0\leq\psi_{n}\lor\varphi_{n+1}\leq x_{n}

and, applying the induction hypothesis, we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle S(\psi_{n}\lor\varphi_{n+1})\leq P(x_{n})\leq S(\psi_{n})+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\varepsilon.

Also, recall the vector lattice identity

\varphi_{n+1}+\psi_{n}=\varphi_{n+1}\lor\psi_{n}+\varphi_{n+1}\land\psi_{n},

and note that
\varphi_{n+1}\land\psi_{n}
is equal to

\psi_{n+1}
by definition. We thus have the equality

\varphi_{n+1}+\psi_{n}=\varphi_{n+1}\lor\psi_{n}+\psi_{n+1}.

Isolating
\psi_{n+1}
in the above equation, we get

\psi_{n+1}=\varphi_{n+1}+\psi_{n}-\varphi_{n+1}\lor\psi_{n}.

From our choice of
\varphi_{n+1}
in (
5.3
) we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle P(x_{n+1})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2^{n+1}}\leq S(\varphi_{n+1}),

and from (
5.5
) we have

(5.7)

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\varepsilon\leq S(\psi_{n})-S(\psi_{n}\lor\varphi_{n+1}).

Adding (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) together we obtain

P(x_{n+1})-\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\varepsilon-\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\varepsilon\leq S(\varphi_{n+1})+S(\psi_{n})-S(\psi_{n}\lor\varphi_{n+1})=S(\psi_{n+1}),

and so

P(x_{n+1})\leq S(\psi_{n+1})+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\right)\varepsilon.

This concludes the inductive step and we have shown (
5.4
) to hold for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence

(5.8)

\displaystyle S^{+}(x_{n})=P(x_{n})\leq S(\psi_{n})+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\cdots+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right)\varepsilon<S(\psi_{n})+\varepsilon

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Property (D3) from Definition
3.3
for
S^{+}

was treated in Remark
5.9
.
This allows us to conclude that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}

we have
0\leq S^{+}(x_{n})
. Furthermore, Property (D3) from Definition
3.3

implies that the sequence
(S^{+}(x_{n}))
is decreasing.
Thus
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{+}(x_{n})
exists and
is nonnegative. Recall that
\psi_{n}\leq x_{n}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, since

(x_{n})\searrow 0
, we have
(\psi_{n})\searrow 0
.
Using (
5.8
) and (S2) from Definition
5.1

we know that the limits satisfy the following:

0\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{+}(x_{n})\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S(\psi_{n})+\varepsilon=\varepsilon.

This holds for every
\varepsilon>0
and so

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{+}(x_{n})=0
. We therefore conclude property (D2) of Definition

3.3
as well.
∎

We have shown that
S^{+}
satisfies (D1), (D2), and (D3) of Definition
3.3

and hence that
S^{+}
is an
I
-integral.
Associated with
S
there are two other
I
-integrals.

Definition 5.11
.

Define
S^{-}:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

S^{-}(x):=S^{+}(x)-S(x).

Define
|S|:T_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

|S|(x):=S^{+}(x)+S^{-}(x)=2S^{+}(x)-S(x).

Proposition 5.12
.

Both
S^{-}
and
|S|
are
I
-integrals.

Proof.

The fact that
S^{-}
and
|S|
are linear follows
as both
S^{+}
and
S
are linear. If
x\in T_{0}
with
0\leq x
,
then
S(x)
is in the set over which we take supremum in Definition

5.3
. Thus we have
S(x)\leq S^{+}(x)

and
0\leq S^{+}(x)-S(x)=S^{-}(x)
. Hence we also have
0\leq S^{+}(x)+S^{-}(x)=|S|(x)
.
If
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
T_{0}
such that
(x_{n})\searrow 0
,
then
S^{-}(x_{n})
and
|S|(x_{n})
are both decreasing (we have shown them to preserve order).
Furthermore

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{-}(x_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S^{+}(x_{n})-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S(x_{n})=0,

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|S|(x_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}2S^{+}(x_{n})-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}S(x_{n})=0.

∎

Remark 5.13
.

From Definition
5.11
, one has
S=S^{+}-S^{-}
and that using Proposition

5.12
both
S^{+}
and
S^{-}
are
I
-integrals. If one phrases this
in the language of Riesz theory, we say
S
has been decomposed into the difference
between two positive linear functionals. This result is stated formally as:

If
M
and
N
are Riesz spaces with
N
Dedekind complete, and if

is an order bounded linear functional, then there exists positive linear functionals

T^{+}:M\rightarrow N
and
T^{-}:M\rightarrow N
such that
T=T^{+}-T^{-}
.

Today we trace back the origins of Riesz theory to the work by
Frigyes Riesz around the time when he delivered the presentation
“Sur la décomposition des opérations fonctionelles linéaires” at the International
Congress of Mathematics, Bologna in 1928. However, as pointed out by Koos Grobler
in his plenary talk “101 years of vector lattice theory” given at the Positivity conference
in Pretoria in 2019, Daniell preempted much of this work by almost a decade.

In Section
3
we discussed how to extend a
I
-integral from class
T_{0}
.
We thus have three
I
-integrals:

, and

We showed in Section

6.
The applications by Norbert Wiener

One of the mathematicians who saw the value in Daniell’s
ideas was the American mathematician and philosopher Norbert Wiener (1894-1964).
We present in this section a brief summary of the development by Wiener. Te keep the
material in this section brief, we do not treat everything in the fullest detail.

6.1.
Extending Dirichlet problems

In 1923, Wiener published the article
“Discontinuous Boundary Conditions and the Dirichlet Problem” detailing an application
of Daniell’s integral to extending the family of solutions to the Dirichlet
problem for the Laplace equation
[
10
]
. This application provides a context
where the integral, rather than the measure, is the most natural tool to use.

Problem 6.1

(The Dirichlet Problem in two dimensions)
.

Let
D
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
We denote the boundary of
D
by
\partial D
.
Let
g:\partial D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be continuous.
For a twice differentiable function
u:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let

\Delta
denote the Laplacian operator, i.e.

\Delta u:=\partial_{1}^{2}u+\partial_{2}^{2}u.

Note that
\Delta
is linear.
Find a function
u\in C^{2}(D)\cap C(\overline{D})
such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\Delta u=0\text{ on }D;\text{ and }

u=g\text{ on }\partial D.

Definition 6.2
.

A function
u\in C(D)
that satisfies

\Delta u=0\text{ on }D

is called
harmonic
.

Definition 6.3
.

For the rest of this section, let
D
be a bounded domain
(a connected, bounded, open set) such that for every continuous function
g
on
\partial D
,
we have a solution
u_{g}\in C^{2}(D)\cap C(\overline{D})
satisfying Problem
6.1
.
Let
x\in D
be arbitrary and define the functional
I_{x}:C(\partial D)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

(6.2)

\displaystyle I_{x}(g):=u_{g}(x).

As
C(\partial D)
is a vector
lattice, it therefore satisfies the conditions needed for
T_{0}
.
We now need to show that
I_{x}
is an
I
-integral, i.e.

I_{x}
satisfies the conditions (D1)-(D3) of Definition
3.3
.
To this end we recall a well-known property of solutions to Problem
6.1
:

Theorem 6.4
.

(Strong Maximal Principle,
[
11
, Ch. 2, Theorem 4]
)

Suppose
u\in C^{2}(D)\cap C(\overline{D})
is a solution to Problem
6.1
,
then

\max_{\overline{D}}u=\max_{\partial D}u.

From this, one notes that
if
u
attains its maximum in
D
then
u
must be constant on
\overline{D}
.
It also follows
by replacing
u
with
-u
that

\min_{\overline{D}}u=\min_{\partial D}u.

Furthermore, if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
both satisfies Problem
6.1
with the same
boundary condition
g
, then
u_{1}=u_{2}
. This is
because, by linearity,
v=u_{1}-u_{2}
will satisfy
Problem
6.1
with boundary condition 0
and so
v=0
. Consequently,
u_{1}=u_{2}
and
we have uniqueness of the solutions to Problem
6.1
.
Therefore
I_{x}
from Definition
6.3
is indeed well-defined.

Theorem 6.5
.

For each
x\in D
, the map
I_{x}
from Definition
6.3
is an
I
-integral.

Proof.

Let
x\in D
.
To verify (D1) of Definition
3.3
for
I_{x}
,
consider arbitrary
g_{1},g_{2}\in C(\partial D)
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}

with
u_{1}
being the solution to Problem
6.1
with boundary condition
g_{1}
and

u_{2}
being the solution corresponding to
g_{2}
. Then by linearity of
\Delta
we have

\Delta(\alpha u_{1}+\beta u_{2})=\alpha\Delta u_{1}+\beta\Delta u_{2}=0\text{ on }D,

and

\alpha u_{1}+\beta u_{2}=\alpha g_{1}+\beta g_{2}\text{ on }\partial D.

By uniqueness of the solution we then have
u_{\alpha g_{1}+\beta g_{2}}=\alpha u_{g_{1}}+\beta u_{g_{2}}
,
and consequently

I_{x}(\alpha g_{1}+\beta g_{2})=u_{\alpha g_{1}+\beta g_{2}}(x)=\alpha u_{g_{1}}(x)+\beta u_{g_{2}}(x)=\alpha I_{x}(g_{1})+\beta I_{x}(g_{2}).

Since
g_{1},g_{2}\in C(\partial D)
were arbitrary, we have that

I_{x}
is linear, proving (D1) of Definition
3.3
.

To verify (D3) of Definition
3.3
, let
g\in C(\partial D)
be such that
g\geq 0
.
We then have

0\leq\min_{\partial D}g=\min_{\partial D}u_{g}=\min_{\overline{D}}u_{g}\leq u_{g}(x)=I_{x}(g),

which shows (D3) of Definition
3.3
.

Finally, if
(g_{n})\subset C(\partial D)
such that
(g_{n})\searrow 0
then
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
0\leq g_{n}-g_{n+1}
,
and hence that

0\leq I_{x}(g_{n}-g_{n+1})=I_{x}(g_{n})-I_{x}(g_{n+1}).

Note also that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
the function
g_{n}\geq 0
and hence by what we have
already shown,
0\leq I_{x}(g_{n})
.
Consequently, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(6.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq I_{x}(g_{n+1})\leq I_{x}(g_{n}).

To show that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{x}(g_{n})=0
, note that

0\leq I_{x}(g_{n})=u_{g_{n}}(x)\leq\max_{\overline{D}}u_{g_{n}}=\max_{\partial D}u_{g_{n}}=\max_{\partial D}g_{n}.

As
(g_{n})\searrow 0
, we have that
(\max_{\partial D}g_{n})\searrow 0
and therefore

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{x}(g_{n})=0
. This together with (
6.3
) proves (D2) of Definition
3.3
.
∎

The theory of Daniell now applies. For each
x\in D
there
exists an extended vector lattice of functions
\mathscr{L}_{x}
on
\partial D
.
Furthermore, the functional
I_{x}
extends to a Daniell Integral
\int_{x}:\mathscr{L}_{x}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We now define a function that may be viewed as a solution to Problem
6.1

with more general boundary conditions.

Definition 6.6
.

If
f\in\bigcap_{x\in D}\mathscr{L}_{x}
, define the function
v_{f}:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

by

v_{f}(x):=\int_{x}f.

Note that the condition
f\in\bigcap_{x\in D}\mathscr{L}_{x}
means that
f
is

I_{x}
-integrable for each
x\in D
. We now prove a theorem which says that the requirement

f\in\bigcap_{x\in D}\mathscr{L}_{x}

is superficial and that we only require
f
to be
I_{x}
-integrable for
one element in
D
.

To do this, we first cite two theorems due to Harnack:

Theorem 6.7
.

(Harnack’s Inequality,
[
11
, Ch. 2, Theorem 11]
)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a connected domain and let
u:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be harmonic and nonnegative. If
V\subset\Omega
is open such that the closure

\overline{V}\subset\Omega
, then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on

\Omega
and
V
, such that
\sup_{x\in V}u(x)\leq C\inf_{x\in V}u(x)
.

Theorem 6.8
.

(Harnack’s Theorem,
[
12
, Ch. 11, Theorem 11.11]
)

Let
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a bounded and connected domain.
If
u_{n}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an increasing sequence of harmonic functions
on
G
, then either
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}(x)
is
finite for every
x\in G
or
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}(x)
is infinite
for every
x\in G
. Furthermore, in the case when the limits are all finite, then

u:=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{n}

is harmonic.

These results are used in the proof of the following theorem.

Theorem 6.9
.

If
x\in D
and
f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
, then
f\in\mathscr{L}_{y}
for all

y\in D
. Furthermore, in this case
v_{f}
is harmonic.

Proof.

Let
x\in D
and assume that
f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
.
Let
T_{1,x}
be as in Definition
3.4
. Note that there is no real dependence
on
x
in this definition, however we keep the subscript as a reminder that we are specifically working
with the
I
-integral
I_{x}
.
We first treat the case when
f\in T_{1,x}
. There exists
a sequence
(g_{n})\subset C(\partial D)
such that
(g_{n})\nearrow f
. In this case

\int_{x}f=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{x}(g_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{g_{n}}(x).

Since we assumed that
f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
, we know that
\int_{x}f<\infty
.
Using Theorem
6.8
, since
f
is (Daniell) integrable with respect to
I_{x}
,
we have that
\int_{x}f=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{g_{n}}(x)
is finite
and we get that
\int_{y}f=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u_{g_{n}}(y)
is finite for each
y\in D
.
Hence
f\in\mathscr{L}_{y}
for each
y\in D
. Also note that the pointwise limit

v_{f}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}
is harmonic in
D
.

We now consider the general case where
f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
I_{1,x}:T_{1,x}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be as in
Definition
3.8
.
Recall that
f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
if and only if

\overline{I}_{x}(f)=\underline{I}_{x}(f)=-\overline{I}_{x}(-f),

and that this common value is denoted
\int_{x}f
. Hence, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there
exists
g_{n}\in T_{1,x}
with
f\leq g_{n}
and

(6.4)

\displaystyle I_{1,x}(g_{n})\leq\int_{x}f+\frac{1}{n},

and there exists
h_{n}
such that
-h_{n}\in T_{1}
with
-f\leq-h_{n}
and

(6.5)

\displaystyle I_{1,x}(-h_{n})\leq\int_{x}(-f)+\frac{1}{n}.

Since
f
is integrable,
I_{1,x}(g_{n})
and
I_{1,x}(-h_{n})
are both finite.
Furthermore, since
g_{n}
and
-h_{n}
are both in
T_{1,x}
, we may conclude
that both
h_{n}
and
g_{n}
are
I_{x}
-integrable. We may therefore write

I_{1,x}(g_{n})=\int_{x}g_{n}\text{ and }I_{1,x}(-h_{n})=\int_{x}-h_{n}=-\int_{x}h_{n}.

Since
T_{1,x}
closed with respect to
\land
and
\lor
,
we may assume without loss of generality that the sequence
(g_{n})
is decreasing,
and that the sequence
(h_{n})
is increasing.
Note that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

h_{n}\leq f\leq g_{n},

and from what we have shown in the special case, that both

v_{g_{n}}
and
v_{h_{n}}
are harmonic. Furthermore, it follows that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}

and each
y\in D
we have

v_{h_{n}}(y)=\int_{y}h_{n}\leq\int_{y}g_{n}=v_{g_{n}}(y).

Hence
v_{h_{n}}\leq v_{g_{n}}
.

Fix an open ball
B(x,a)
in
D
of radius
a>0
around
x
such that the closure

\overline{B(x,a)}\subset D
.
Using Theorem
6.7
we conclude that
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in V
we therefore have that
v_{g_{n}}-v_{h_{n}}
is harmonic
on
D
and that

(6.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{g_{n}}(y)-v_{h_{n}}(y)\leq\sup_{x\in B(x,a)}(v_{g_{n}}-v_{h_{n}})\leq C\inf_{x\in B(x,a)}(v_{g_{n}}-v_{h_{n}})\leq C(v_{g_{n}}(x)-v_{h_{n}}(x)).

From (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) we know that

0\leq v_{g_{n}}(x)-v_{h_{n}}(x)=\int_{x}g_{n}-\int_{x}h_{n}\leq\int_{x}f+\int_{x}(-f)+\frac{2}{n}=\frac{2}{n}.

Combining this with (
6.6
) we get for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
y\in B(x,a)

that

0\leq v_{g_{n}}(y)-v_{h_{n}}(y)\leq\frac{2C}{n}.

Therefore, there exists a
v:B(x,a)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

such that for each
y\in B(x,a)
we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{h_{n}}(y)=v(y)

and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}v_{g_{n}}(y)=v(y).

Furthermore, on
B(x,a)
this convergence
is uniform. Therefore, we may conclude that
v
is a harmonic function on
B(x,a)
.
Also for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, since
h_{n}\leq f\leq g_{n}
, for every
y\in B(x,a)
we have

v_{h_{n}}(y)\leq\underline{I}_{y}(f)\leq\overline{I}_{y}(f)\leq v_{g_{n}}(y).

Since both
v_{h_{n}}(y)
and
v_{g_{n}}(y)
converge to the same limit
v(y)
as
n
tends to infinity,
we conclude that
f
is
I_{y}
-integrable and that
v_{f}(y)=\int_{y}f=v(y)
. Since
y\in B(x,a)

is arbitrary we conclude that
v_{f}
is defined and equal to
v
on
B(x,a)
, and hence
that
v_{f}
is harmonic on
B(x,a)
.

Let
z\in D
be arbitrary. Since
D
, being a domain, is path connected, we pick a path from

x
to
z
. A path in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a compact set, and hence we may pick a finite string of overlapping open balls covering the path and repeat the argument
on each of them. As a consequence, we conclude that
v_{f}
is harmonic on the neighbourhood around
the path connecting

x
and
z
and also,
f
is (Daniell) integrable with respect to
I_{z}
. We may conclude that

f
is (Daniell) integrable with respect to
I_{z}
for every
z\in D
. Thus, we have proved that

f\in\mathscr{L}_{x}
for some
x\in D
implies
f\in\mathscr{L}_{y}
for all
y\in D
. Furthermore, since

v_{f}
is harmonic in the neighbourhood of any path between two points in
D
,
we conclude that
v_{f}
is harmonic in
D
.
∎

This justifies our remark that the function
v_{f}
can be seen as a solution to Problem
6.1
.
However, of note is that

might not any longer be continuous on the boundary.
This is discussed further in Wiener’s paper

Explicitly, we let

Let

Define the function

Let

\displaystyle\mu D(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(t_{i}-t_{i-1})}}\int_{B_{1}}\int_{B_{2}}\cdots\int_{B_{n}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{t_{1}}}e^{-\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdots e^{-\frac{(x_{n}-x_{n-1})^{2}}{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{n}

Proposition 6.10
.

The collection
\mathscr{R}
is a ring of subsets of
X
.

Proof.

We prove that
\mathscr{R}
contains
X
and that
\mathscr{R}

is closed under intersection and relative difference.
This would mean that
\mathscr{R}
is an algebra of sets,
which is sufficient to conclude that
\mathscr{R}
is a ring.

To prove that
\mathscr{R}
is closed under intersection,
let

P_{n}=\{t_{0},\cdots,t_{n}\}
and
Q_{m}=\{s_{0},\cdots,s_{m}\}
be
partitions of
[0,1]
and

\mathscr{B}_{n}=(B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{n})
and

\mathscr{C}_{m}=(C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{m})

tuples of Borel sets and consider

D(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n}),D(Q_{m},\mathscr{C}_{m})\in\mathscr{R}
.
Then
P_{n}\cup Q_{m}
is also a partition of

[0,1]
. Let the partition
P_{n}\cup Q_{m}
be
\{r_{0},r_{1},\cdots,r_{l}\}
.
Define the tuple
\mathscr{D}_{l}=(D_{1},D_{2},\cdots,D_{l})
by

D_{i}=\begin{cases}B_{j}&\text{ if }r_{i}=t_{j}\in P_{n}\backslash Q_{m}\\
C_{j}&\text{ if }r_{i}=s_{j}\in Q_{m}\backslash P_{n}\\
B_{j}\cap C_{k}&\text{ if }r_{i}=t_{j}=s_{k}\in P_{n}\cap Q_{m}\end{cases}

for
i=1,2,\cdots l
. Then

D(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n})\cap D(Q_{m},\mathscr{C}_{m})=D(P_{n}\cup Q_{m},\mathscr{D}_{l})\in\mathscr{R}.

To prove that
\mathscr{R}
is closed under relative difference,
let
D(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n}),D(Q_{m},\mathscr{C}_{m})\in\mathscr{R}
be as in the previous case.
Define the tuple
\mathscr{D}_{l}=(D_{1},D_{2},\cdots,D_{l})
by

D_{i}=\begin{cases}B_{j}&\text{ if }r_{i}=t_{j}\in P_{n}\backslash Q_{m}\\
\mathbb{R}\backslash C_{j}&\text{ if }r_{i}=s_{j}\in Q_{m}\backslash P_{n}\\
B_{j}\backslash C_{k}&\text{ if }r_{i}=t_{j}=s_{k}\in P_{n}\cap Q_{m}\end{cases}

for

D(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n})\backslash D(Q_{m},\mathscr{C}_{m})=D(P_{n}\cup Q_{m},\mathscr{D}_{l})\in\mathscr{R}.

Finally, note that
X=D(\{0,1\},(\mathbb{R}))\in\mathscr{R}
.

is an algebra of sets.

Proposition 6.11
.

The function
\mu
is a pre-measure.

Proof.

From the definition, it is clear that
\mu(\emptyset)=0
, and that
\mu
is nonnegative. Let

D=(P_{n},\mathscr{B}_{n})\in\mathscr{R}
be the disjoint union of the countable collection

(D_{k})\subset\mathscr{R}
. Since for all
k
,
D_{k}\subset D
we may assume, without loss of generality,
that the partitions for the
D_{k}
’s are all the same. Hence we may write
D_{k}=D(P_{n},\mathscr{A}_{n}^{(k)})

with
\mathscr{A}_{n}^{(k)}=(A_{1}^{k},A_{2}^{(k)},\cdots,A_{n}^{(k)})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The assumption that the
D_{k}
’s are disjoint means that the
sets
A_{1}^{(k)}\times A_{2}^{(k)}\times\cdots\times A_{n}^{(k)}
are all disjoint and hence
that the iterated integral in (
6.7
) may be written

\displaystyle\int_{B_{1}}\int_{B_{2}}\cdots\int_{B_{n}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{t_{1}}}e^{-\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdots e^{-\frac{(x_{n}-x_{n-1})^{2}}{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{\bigcup_{k}A_{1}^{(k)}}\int_{\bigcup_{k}A_{2}^{(k)}}\cdots\int_{\bigcup_{k}A_{n}^{(k)}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{t_{1}}}e^{-\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdots e^{-\frac{(x_{n}-x_{n-1})^{2}}{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\int_{A_{1}^{(k)}}\int_{A_{2}^{(k)}}\cdots\int_{A_{n}^{(k)}}e^{-\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{t_{1}}}e^{-\frac{(x_{2}-x_{1})^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}}\cdots e^{-\frac{(x_{n}-x_{n-1})^{2}}{t_{n}-t_{n-1}}}\,dx_{1}dx_{2}\cdots dx_{n}.

From this it follows that

\mu(D)=\mu\left(\bigcup_{k}D_{k}\right)=\sum_{k}\mu(D_{k}).

Hence we conclude that
\mu
is a pre-measure.

∎

Theorem 7.8

(Carathéodory Extension Theorem)
.

Let

\mathscr{R}
be a ring of subsets of

.
Let

There exists an extension of

\sigma
-algebra generated by
\mathscr{R}
. Furthermore,

\sigma
-finite, then this extension is unique.

Definition 7.10

If

Theorem 7.11

(Regularity of the Lebesgue Measure)
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be the
\sigma
-algebra of Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}
.
Let
m:\mathscr{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be the Lebesgue measure,
and denote the Lebesgue outer measure by
m^{*}
.
The following are equivalent for a set
E\subset\mathbb{R}
.

(i)

E\in\mathscr{M}
.

(ii)

For all
\varepsilon>0
there exists an open set
O\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

E\subset O
and
m^{*}(O\backslash E)\leq\varepsilon
.

(iii)

There exists a
G_{\delta}
-set
G
such that
E\subset G
and
m^{*}(G\backslash E)=0
.

(iv)

For all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a closed set
F\subset\mathbb{R}
such that

F\subset E
and
m^{*}(E\backslash F)\leq\varepsilon
.

(v)

There exists a
F_{\sigma}
-set
F
such that
F\subset E
and
m^{*}(E\backslash F)=0
.

Theorem 7.12
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be the Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
and let
\mathscr{B}
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra.
The Lebesgue measure
m:\mathscr{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

is complete. Furthermore, if
\mathscr{A}
is another
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbb{R}
and
if
\mu:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}

is another complete measure such that
\mu=m
on
\mathscr{B}
, then
\mathscr{M}\subset\mathscr{A}

and
\mu=m
on
\mathscr{M}
.

Proof.

We defer the completeness of
m
to
[
14
, Example 4, p. 30]
.

Let
E\in\mathscr{M}
. Then there exists a
G_{\delta}
-set
G
such that
E\subset G
and
m(G\backslash E)=0
.
There also exists a
G_{\delta}
-set
D
such that
G\backslash E\subset D
and
m(D)=0
.
Note that since
G\backslash E
and
E
are disjoint,

m(G)=m(G\backslash E)+m(E)=m(E).

Since
D
is a
G_{\delta}
-set,
D\in\mathscr{B}
, and we have that
m(D)=\mu(D)=0
.
By completeness of
\mu
we conclude that
G\backslash E\in\mathscr{A}
.
Since
G
is a
G_{\delta}
-set, we also
conclude that
G\in\mathscr{B}
and that
m(G)=\mu(G)
. Hence,

E=G\backslash(G\backslash E)\in\mathscr{A}
and

\mu(E)=\mu(G)-\mu(G\backslash E)=m(G)-0=m(E).

Thus we conclude that
\mathscr{M}\subset\mathscr{A}
and that the measures coincide on
\mathscr{M}
.
∎

Theorem 7.13

(Dini’s Theorem)
.

If

(f_{n})
is a monotone sequence of continuous

Lemma 7.14
.

Let
\mathscr{R}
be a ring of subsets of
X
.
Let
\mu:\mathscr{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be a pre-measure.
Let

T:=\text{span}\left\{\chi_{R}:R\in\mathscr{R}\text{ and }\mu(R)<\infty\right\}.

Define
I:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:
For each
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
let

I\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\right):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mu(R_{i}).

If
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
, then there exists
a collection of pairwise disjoint sets

\{B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{n}\}
in
\mathscr{R}
and real numbers

\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{n}\}
such that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{l}c_{k}\chi_{B_{k}}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction.
If
n=1
, then we
x=c\chi_{R}
for some
R\in\mathscr{R}
and
some scalar
c
and we are done.
Hence assume the claim is valid for an integer
n\geq 1
.
Let
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}\in T
. Note that

\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}+c_{n+1}\chi_{R_{n+1}},

and hence we apply the inductive hypothesis on

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\chi_{R_{i}}
to get disjoint
sets
\{B_{1},B_{2},\cdots,B_{l}\}
in
\mathscr{R}

and real numbers
\{b_{1},b_{2},\cdots,b_{l}\}
such that

x=\sum_{i=1}^{l}b_{k}\chi_{B_{k}}+c_{n+1}\chi_{R_{n+1}}.

Now consider the collection of sets

\left\{B_{1}\backslash R_{n+1},B_{2}\backslash R_{n+1},\cdots,B_{l}\backslash R_{n+1}\right\}\bigcup\left\{B_{1}\cap R_{n+1},B_{2}\cap R_{n+1},\cdots,B_{l}\cap R_{n+1}\right\}\bigcup\left\{R_{n+1}\backslash\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}B_{k}\right\}.

Note that the sets in this collection are pairwise disjoint,
and that by the properties of a ring, all of these
are in
\mathscr{R}
. For convenience, denote for

k=1,2,\cdots,l
the set
D_{k}=B_{k}\backslash R_{n+1}
,
the set
E_{k}=B_{k}\cap R_{n+1}
, and denote by

F=R_{n+1}\backslash\bigcup_{k=1}^{l}B_{k}
. Then we have the representation

x=\sum_{k=1}^{l}b_{k}\chi_{D_{k}}+\sum_{k=1}^{l}(b_{k}+c_{n+1})\chi_{E_{k}}+c_{n+1}\chi_{F},

as a linear combination over disjoint sets.
We conclude that the claim holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎
