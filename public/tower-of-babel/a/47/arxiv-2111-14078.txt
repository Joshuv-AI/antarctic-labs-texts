A simple ill-posedness proof for incompressible Euler equations in critical Sobolev spaces
Junha Kim, In-Jee Jeong

Abstract
We provide a simple proof that the Cauchy problem for the incompressible Euler equations in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with any d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 is ill-posed in critical Sobolev spaces, extending an earlier work of Bourgain–Li (Int. Math. Res. Not. 2021) in the case d = 3 d=3 . The ill-posedness is shown for certain critical Lorentz spaces as well.

1
Introduction

1.1
Main results

In this paper, we are concerned with the Cauchy problem for the incompressible Euler equations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\geq 3
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}{\bf u}+{\bf u}\cdot\nabla{\bf u}+\nabla p&=0,\\
\nabla\cdot{\bf u}&=0,\\
{\bf u}(t=0)&={\bf u}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1.1)

We shall provide a simple proof that (
1.1
) is
ill-posed
in the critical Sobolev space
H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in the strongest sense of Hadamard. The space
H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is critical since one can obtain local well-posedness of (
1.1
) in
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with any
s>d/2+1
(
[
15
,
16
]
), while
{\bf u}\in H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
does not guarantee that
\|\nabla{\bf u}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
, which is an essential ingredient in local regularity proof. Our first main result gives the existence of a
H^{d/2+1}
–regular data without any solutions in the same space.

Theorem 1.1

(Nonexistence)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
{\bf u}_{0}\in H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying

\begin{split}\|{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon\end{split}

such that there is
no
corresponding solution to (
1.1
) belonging to
L^{\infty}([0,\delta];H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for
any

\delta>0
.

Furthermore, we are able to prove that a large growth of the
H^{d/2+1}
–norm occurs for certain infinitely smooth initial data.

Theorem 1.2

(Strong norm inflation)
.

For any
\delta
,
\varepsilon
,
A>0
, there exists
{\bf u}_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\varepsilon
such that the unique local-in-time smooth solution
{\bf u}
to (
1.1
) exists on
[0,\delta^{*}]
for some
0<\delta^{*}<\delta
and satisfies

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,\delta^{*}]}\|{\bf u}(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{d/2+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}>A.\end{split}

We remark that the above statements for the two and three-dimensional cases have been already proved by Bourgain–Li in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, respectively. We refer the interested readers to these works for an extensive list of literature on the Cauchy problem for the Euler equations. As Bourgain and Li are emphasizing in the introduction of
[
5
]
, the 3D case is much more involved than the 2D case due to the presence of the vortex stretching term, lack of
L^{p}
conservation of the vorticity, absence of Yudovich theory, and the issues arising from dealing with the non-local norm
H^{5/2}
. A short proof for the 2D case was given in
[
10
]
, and this simplified argument became a basis for further developments in the well-posedness theory, including continuous-in-time loss of Sobolev regularity (
[
11
]
), enstrophy growth in the Navier–Stokes equations (
[
14
,
13
]
), and strong ill-posedness for active scalar equations (
[
12
]
). Some further developments in this direction are given in
[
19
,
7
]
. Therefore, we believe that the simplified argument presented in the current paper for three and higher dimensions will be useful in the further study of well-posedness for the high-dimensional Euler and related systems. Indeed, the ill-posed mechanism in critical spaces is closely related with recent groundbreaking works on finite-time singularity formation for the 3D Euler equations (
[
9
,
6
]
). Moreover, our simple proof is robust enough to handle all dimensions
d\geq 3
at once. Lastly, we prove strong ill-posedness in critial Lorentz spaces which does not seem to follow from the approach of
[
5
]
, see technical discussion in §
2.4
below.

1.2
Axisymmetric Euler without swirl

The aforementioned ill-posedness results will be obtained by considering a class of solutions referred to as
axisymmetric without swirl
: if the velocity takes the form

\begin{split}{\bf u}(t,x)=u^{r}(t,r,x_{d})e^{r}+u^{d}(t,r,x_{d})e^{d}\end{split}

(1.2)

where
x=(x_{h},x_{d})
with
r=|x_{h}|
, then it can be shown that this ansatz propagates in time for the evolution of (
1.1
), upon having the uniqueness of a solution to (
1.1
). In this case, introducing the scalar vorticity

\begin{split}\omega(t,x)=\partial_{r}u^{d}-\partial_{d}u^{r}\end{split}

(1.3)

which is again a function of
r
and
x_{d}
, the Euler equations (
1.1
) reduce to a simple evolution equation for
\omega
: we have

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\frac{\omega}{r^{d-2}}+(u^{r}\partial_{r}+u^{d}\partial_{d})\frac{\omega}{r^{d-2}}=0.\end{split}

(1.4)

The components
u^{r}
and
u^{d}
can be recovered from
\omega
at each instant of time via the axisymmetric Biot–Savart law:

\begin{split}u^{r}(t,x)=-\frac{1}{d|B_{d}|}\frac{1}{|x_{h}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(x_{d}-y_{d})\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}x_{j}y_{j}}{|y_{h}||x-y|^{d}}\omega(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

(1.5)

\begin{split}u^{d}(t,x)=\frac{1}{d|B_{d}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}(x_{j}-y_{j})y_{j}}{|y_{h}||x-y|^{d}}\omega(t,y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

(1.6)

where
|B_{d}|
denotes the volume of the
d
-dimensional unit ball. Introducing the flow
\Phi=\Phi^{r}e^{r}+\Phi^{d}e^{d}
by

\begin{split}\Phi(0,x)=x\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Phi(t,x)={\bf u}(t,\Phi(t,x)),\end{split}

(1.7)

we see that solutions to (
1.4
) satisfy

\begin{split}\frac{\omega(t,\Phi(t,x))}{\Phi^{r}(t,x)^{d-2}}=\frac{\omega_{0}(x)}{r^{d-2}}\end{split}

(1.8)

along the flow. Note that along the flow,
\omega
could either increase or decrease by the factor
(\Phi^{r}/r)^{d-2}
. This
vortex stretching
effect can be alternatively seen from the equation for
\omega
, rather than
\omega/r^{d-2}
:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}{\omega}+(u^{r}\partial_{r}+u^{d}\partial_{d}){\omega}=(d-2)\frac{u^{r}}{r}\omega.\end{split}

Regarding the axisymmetric without swirl equation (
1.4
), we have local well-posedness for any initial data satisfying
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
r^{-(d-2)}\omega_{0}\in L^{d,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
: there exists a unique corresponding local-in-time solution to (
1.1
) satisfying
\omega\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
r^{-(d-2)}\omega\in L^{d,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for some time interval. The solution is global in the case
d=3
. This was proved by Danchin (
[
8
]
) and can be viewed as a sharp extension of the celebrated Yudovich theory
[
20
]
in two dimensions. Unlike the two-dimensional case, the extra condition
r^{-(d-2)}\omega_{0}\in L^{d,1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is necessary to control vortex stretching, and seems to be
sharp
(see
[
9
]
). Indeed, as a byproduct of our analysis, we were able to obtain the following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

For each
d\geq 3
, there exists
1<\bar{q}_{d}<\infty
such that the axisymmetric equation (
1.4
) is
ill-posed
for
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
with
r^{-(d-2)}\omega_{0}\in L^{d,q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with any
\infty\geq q>\bar{q}_{d}
.

The precise ill-posedness statement is given in Proposition
4.1
below.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Choice of initial data

We demonstrate the choice of initial data that will be used in the proof of main theorems. To begin with, let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a smooth bump function with the following properties:

•

\phi
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth and radial;

•

\phi
is supported in
B(0;\frac{1}{8})
and
\phi=1
in
B(0;\frac{1}{32})
.

For some
n_{0}<m\leq\infty
and
0<\alpha<3/4
to be determined later, we take

\begin{split}\omega_{0}:=\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{m}n^{-\alpha}\omega^{(n)}_{0,loc},\end{split}

(2.1)

where

\begin{split}\omega^{(n)}_{0,loc}(r,x_{d}):=\phi(8^{n}(r-8^{-n+1},x_{d}-8^{-n}))-\phi(8^{n}(r-8^{-n+1},x_{d}+8^{-n})).\end{split}

(2.2)

The precise value of
\alpha
(which determines the regularity of (
2.1
)) will be specified in the proofs of each theorems. We observe that
\omega_{0}
is an odd function with respect to
x_{d}
, and has the form of a weighted sum of normalized
bubbles
; we shall sometimes (informally) refer to
n^{-\alpha}\omega^{(n)}_{0,loc}
as the
n
-th bubble.

2.2
The Key Lemma

We now state the main technical tool of this paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

We impose the following assumptions on
\omega\in H^{\frac{d}{2}}\cup(L^{\infty}\cap L^{2})(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

•

Odd with respect to the last coordinate:

\begin{split}\omega(y_{h},y_{d})=-\omega(y_{h},-y_{d});\end{split}

(2.3)

•

For any
y\neq 0
satisfying either
|y_{h}|=0
or
y_{d}=0
, there exists an open neighborhood of
y
such that
\omega
vanishes.

Then, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
r=|x_{h}|\geq x_{d}>0
, we have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{u^{r}(x)}{r}-{\frac{1}{(d-1)|B_{d}|}}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{d}}{|y|^{d+2}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq CB_{1}(x)\end{split}

(2.4)

and

\begin{split}\left|\frac{u^{d}(x)}{x_{d}}+{\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{d}}{|y|^{d+2}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq CB_{2}(x),\end{split}

(2.5)

where
Q(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d};|y_{h}|\geq 4|x_{h}|\}
and
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
are non-negative functions with the upper bound

\begin{split}B_{1}(x)\leq\min\left\{\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right\},\end{split}

\begin{split}B_{2}(x)\leq\min\left\{\left(1+\log\frac{r}{x_{d}}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\left(1+\log\frac{r}{x_{d}}\right)\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right\}.\end{split}

Remark 2.2
.

Gagliardo–Nirenberg interpolation inequality implies that

\begin{split}\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq C\|\Lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}\omega\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.\end{split}

The “Key Lemma” has first appeared in a celebrated work of Kiselev–Sverak
[
18
]
for the two-dimensional Euler equations, although Bourgain and Li were using similar formulas in
[
4
]
; see also
[
21
,
17
,
14
]
. For the three-dimensional Euler equations, a similar result with a different remainder bound was used in
[
9
,
6
]
. Our version of Key Lemma is sharp in that the remainder is bounded using only critical norms of the Euler equations. Therefore, we believe that this lemma will be useful for other purposes, for instance in the construction of smooth solutions with rapid Sobolev norm growth. While we defer the (somewhat tedious) proof later to §
7
, one may observe from the axi-symmetric Biot–Savart law that in the limit
x\to 0
, we exactly have

\begin{split}\partial_{r}u^{r}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{u^{r}(x)}{r}={\frac{1}{(d-1)|B_{d}|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{d}}{|y|^{d+2}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\quad\partial_{d}u^{d}(0)=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{u^{d}(x)}{x_{d}}=-{\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{d}}{|y|^{d+2}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

2.3
Hardy’s inequality

We now recall the famous Hardy’s inequality. We omit the proof since it can be easily obtained using an integration by parts.

Lemma 2.3

(Hardy’s inequality)
.

Let
f
be a positive smooth function defined on the interval
(0,1)
with
f(0)=0
. Then for any
p>1
, we have

\begin{split}\|x^{-1}f(x)\|_{L^{p}(0,1)}\leq C\|\partial_{x}f(x)\|_{L^{p}(0,1)}.\end{split}

2.4
Ideas of the proof

Let us illustrate the main ideas of the proof, which have been inspired by the previous works
[
10
,
14
,
12
]
. The interested reader can find more information there; in the current setup, the main difference is the presence of vortex stretching. We consider the case of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

•

Formal computation and mechanism of illposedness.
With a few simple and formal computations, let us explain the reasoning behind the choice of our initial data. To begin with, at the initial time, the velocity is roughly given by

\begin{split}\begin{pmatrix}u^{r}_{0}\\
u^{z}_{0}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}r\\
-2z\end{pmatrix}(\ln\frac{1}{|x|})^{1-\alpha}\varphi(\frac{x}{|x|}),\end{split}

when
n_{0}=1
and
m=\infty
in (
2.1
), which can be seen by the main term of the Key Lemma. Here,
\varphi
is a bounded function supported in the region
r,|z|\simeq|x|
. Then, using that the variable
\xi:=\omega/r
is simply being transported by the flow, we compute

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\xi^{2}}{z}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{-u^{z}_{0}}{z}\frac{\xi_{0}^{2}}{z}\,\mathrm{d}x\simeq\int_{\{|x|\leq 1\}\cap\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\ln\frac{1}{|x|})^{1-3\alpha}\frac{1}{|x|^{3}}\,\mathrm{d}x=+\infty\end{split}

when
\alpha<2/3
. That is, the quantity
\|z^{-\frac{1}{2}}r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{2}}
immediately blows up for
t>0
, which implies the blow up of the critical norm
\|\omega\|_{H^{3/2}}
as it controls
\|z^{-\frac{1}{2}}r^{-1}\omega\|_{L^{2}}
thanks to the Hardy’s inequality. Indeed, when
\alpha
is slightly larger than
1/2
, the initial vorticity “barely” belongs to the space
H^{3/2}
in a way that the corresponding velocity is not Lipschitz continuous in space. Geometrically, the ill-posedness comes from the squeezing of the vorticity in the
z
-direction by the strong hyperbolic flow. In the case of
L^{\infty}
, we can similarly compute

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}}=\|r^{-1}u_{0}^{r}\omega_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}\simeq\sup_{|x|\leq 1}(\ln\frac{1}{|x|})^{1-2\alpha}=+\infty\end{split}

when
\alpha<1/2
, and one clearly sees that the ill-posedness comes from vortex stretching.

•

Monotonicity and stability.
While the above computation shows that ill-posedness occurs for the linear advection equation obtained by replacing
u(t)
by
u_{0}
, for the nonlinear problem we need to understand the effects on the velocity caused by evolution of the vorticity, and in particular need to make sure that the velocity gradient (given by the main terms in the Key Lemma) remains large enough to create norm inflation. There are two competing effects; while the squeezing of the vorticity weakens the induced velocity gradient, the vortex stretching term strengthens it. As in our previous work
[
12
]
, we identify a timescale for each of the bubbles during which it essentially remains the same, and obtain a lower bound on the velocity gradient based on such a stability statement. It turns out that,
together with the help of the vortex stretching term
, such a lower bound is sufficient to prove strong ill-posedness results.

•

Contradiction hypothesis and Lagrangian property.
Throughout the proof, we heavily rely on the Lagrangian analysis, and in particular on the fact that the solutions of the axisymmetric Euler equations satisfy the Cauchy formula (
1.8
). The Lagrangian analysis is not only essential in proving the stability statement described in the above but also allows us to obtain sharp bounds on the critical norms. Fortunately, existence and uniqueness of the flow map in our case is guaranteed by the contradiction hypothesis that there is a local solution in the critical space.

Remark 2.4
.

At this point, let us point out the main difference of our argument with the original proof of Bourgain–Li: in
[
5
]
, to prove ill-posedness in
H^{3/2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, the authors very carefully identify a class of initial data such that there is local well-posedness in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
(yet escapes
H^{3/2}
). In their work, a significant portion is devoted to proving propagation of the
L^{\infty}
–norm with such data. After having
L^{\infty}
, one can then try to repeat the strategy developed in the 2D case. On the other hand, we proceed by a direct contradiction argument which does not require the hypothetical solution to be bounded in
L^{\infty}
. Furthermore, we are able to prove ill-posedness in
L^{\infty}
as well: the vortex stretching effect is crucial in Theorem
1.3
.

Remark 2.5
.

Very recently, An–Chen–Yin
[
1
,
2
]
proved strong ill-posedness of the
compressible
Euler equations in critical Sobolev spaces for dimensions 2 and 3. The ill-posedness in the compressible Euler case seems to be driven by instantaneous shock formation.

3
Short time dynamics

We consider
\omega_{0}
of the form (
2.1
) and proceed under the assumption that there exists an associated solution to (
1.4
)

\begin{split}\omega\in L^{\infty}([0,T];H^{\frac{d}{2}}\cup(L^{\infty}\cap L^{2})(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\end{split}

(3.1)

with

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\min\left\{\|\Lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}\omega(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\|\omega(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\right\}\leq A\end{split}

(3.2)

for some
T>0
and
A>0
. By this assumption, we can find a constant
B>0
such that

\begin{split}\|\nabla u(t)\|_{BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\|\omega(t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq B,\qquad t\in[0,T].\end{split}

This follows from the embedding
H^{d/2}\subset BMO
and the singular integral operator bound
L^{\infty}\to BMO
. Moreover, the
BMO
bound implies that the equation (
1.4
) possesses at most one solution belonging to the class (
3.1
) and the velocity
u(t,\cdot)
is log-Lipschitz (see
[
12
,
3
]
): there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|u(t,x)-u(t,y)|\leq CB|x-y|\log\left(10+\frac{1}{|x-y|}\right),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{split}

This guarantees that the flow map
\Phi
is well-defined on
t\in[0,T]
as the unique solution of (
1.7
). Thus, we can verfiy that the unique solution
\omega
is given by (
1.8
) and in particular Lemma
2.1
applies to
\omega(t,\cdot)
on the time interval
[0,T]
. We shall let
T
be small so that
AT\leq c_{0}
for some absolute constant
c_{0}>0
, which is determined at the end of this section. We begin with the simple result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
\omega
be a solution to (
1.4
) satisfying (
3.2
) with initial data (
2.1
). Then, we have
\omega(t,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
0\leq|x_{h}|<|x_{d}|
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

We recall Lemma
2.1
with
x
on the region
\{(r,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0};1/2\leq x_{d}/r\leq 1\}

\begin{split}\frac{u^{r}(t,x)}{r}\geq-CA,\qquad\frac{u^{d}(t,x)}{x_{d}}\leq CA.\end{split}

Using these inequalities, we show that
\Phi^{d}(t,x)/\Phi^{r}(t,x)\leq 1
for all
x
with
x_{d}/r\leq 1/2
and
t\in[0,T]
. We don’t need to consider
x
with
x_{d}/r>1/2
due to the definition of
\omega_{0}
. Suppose that
\Phi(t,x)
enters the region at
t=T^{*}\leq T
. Until escaping the area, the trajectory complies with

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{\Phi^{r}(t,x)}\leq CA.\end{split}

Then for
CAT\leq Cc_{0}\leq\log 2
, it follows

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{\Phi^{r}(t,x)}\leq\log\frac{1}{2}+CAT\leq 0.\end{split}

From this, we conclude that
\Phi(t,x)
cannot touch the line
r=x_{d}
. This completes the proof.
∎

This lemma guarantees that
\Phi(t,x)
with
x\in{\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega_{0})
can be traced by Lemma
2.1
up to
t\in[0,T]
. For simplicity, let us write
n
-th bubble as
\Omega_{n}:={\mathrm{supp}}\,(\omega^{(n)}_{0,loc})
. The next result provides that the bubbles are “well-ordered”: each size of bubble and distance of adjacent bubbles in direction
r
are maintained to some extent.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
\omega
be a solution to (
1.4
) satisfying (
3.2
) with initial data (
2.1
). Then, we have

\begin{split}\sup_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\Phi^{r}(t,x)\leq 4\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\Phi^{r}(t,x)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad 4\sup_{x\in\Omega_{n+1}}\Phi^{r}(t,x)\leq\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\Phi^{r}(t,x)\end{split}

(3.3)

for all
n\geq n_{0}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

We first show a generic proerty, which works even for
\omega_{0}
without (
2.1
): let
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with
r\geq r^{\prime}>0
. Then, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\Phi^{r}(t,x^{\prime})\leq\Phi^{r}(t,x)\leq\frac{2r}{r^{\prime}}\Phi^{r}(t,x^{\prime}),\qquad t\in[0,T].\end{split}

(3.4)

To show it, we make a simple observation

\begin{split}\frac{u^{r}(t,x)}{r}-\frac{u^{r}(t,x^{\prime})}{r^{\prime}}\leq CA,\qquad r\geq r^{\prime}>0.\end{split}

This is directly obtained from (
2.4
) with
y_{d}\omega(y)\geq 0
. Then, we have

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\Phi^{r}(t,x)-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\Phi^{r}(t,x^{\prime})\leq CA\end{split}

when
\Phi^{r}(t,x)\geq\Phi^{r}(t,x^{\prime})
. Integrating it over time and using the smallness of
c_{0}
, we can infer the claim.

Now, we consider
\omega_{0}
with (
2.1
). Then, we clearly have from (

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\partial_{d}\omega^{(n)}_{loc}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\omega^{(n)}_{loc}(t,x)|^{2}}{|x_{d}|^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|^{2(d-2)}}{|\Phi^{d}(t,x)|^{2}}\frac{|\omega_{0}(x)|^{2}}{|x_{h}|^{2(d-2)}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq c\left(\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|}{r}\right)^{2(d-2)}\left(\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|x_{d}|}{|\Phi^{d}(t,x)|}\right)^{2}8^{2n}\int_{\Omega_{n}}|\omega_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\omega^{(n)}_{loc}(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{n}}|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|^{2(d-2)}\frac{|\omega_{0}(x)|^{2}}{|x_{h}|^{2(d-2)}}\,\mathrm{d}x\leq\left(\sup_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|}{r}\right)^{2(d-2)}\int_{\Omega_{n}}|\omega_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Combining the above estimates with

\begin{split}\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|}{r}\simeq\sup_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|}{r},\end{split}

a direct consequence of (
3.3
), we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}\omega(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq c\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}\left(\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(t,x)|}{r}\right)^{2(d-2)}\left(\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|x_{d}|}{|\Phi^{d}(t,x)|}\right)^{d}8^{dn}\int_{\Omega_{n}}|\omega_{0}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.\end{split}

Recalling the definition of
\omega_{0}
, we obtain (
5.3
). This completes the proof.
∎

To make
\dot{H}^{\frac{d}{2}}
norm inflation, it is essential to control the position of each bubble in the direction of
x_{d}
. The following lemma provides a bound.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that
\omega
be a solution to (
1.4
) satisfying (
3.2
) with initial data (
2.1
). Then, there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{x_{d}}\leq-\frac{1}{4|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CAt\end{split}

(5.4)

for all
x\in\Omega_{n}
with
n>n_{0}
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\Omega_{n}
with
n>n_{0}
and
x_{d}>0
. From (
2.4
), we clearly have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\Phi^{r}(t,x)-\frac{1}{(d-1)|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}I_{k}(t)\right|\leq CA\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Phi^{r}(t,x)}{r}\leq C\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CAt.\end{split}

On the other hand, we show from (
2.5
) that

\begin{split}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\Phi^{d}(t,x)+\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}I_{k}(t)\right|\leq CA\left(1+\log\frac{\Phi^{r}(t,x)}{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{d}},\end{split}

hence,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\Phi^{d}(t,x)\leq-\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}I_{k}(t)+CA\left(1+\log\frac{\Phi^{r}(t,x)}{r}-\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{x^{d}}\right).\end{split}

(5.5)

Suppose that
T^{*}\in(0,T]
be the first time such that

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{x_{d}}\geq-\frac{1}{2|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CAt.\end{split}

Note that we can ensure
T^{*}\neq 0
by taking the constant
C
in the above assumption large. Integrating (
5.5
) over time and combining the above, we can have

\displaystyle\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(t,x)}{x_{d}}=\log\frac{\Phi^{d}(T^{*},x)}{x_{d}}-\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{T^{*}}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CA(t-T^{*})\left(1+\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CAT^{*}-\frac{1}{|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{T^{*}}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CA(t-T^{*})\left(1+\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CA(t-T^{*})\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{t}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau+CAt

until the assumption is satisfied. We impose the condition
CAT\leq Cc_{0}\leq 1/4|B_{d}|
. Then, we can infer (
5.4
). This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
. For given
\varepsilon>0
, we take
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large, so that by Proposition
5.1
, we have

\begin{split}\|\omega_{0}\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<c\varepsilon\end{split}

for any
c>0
. Then, as we have remarked after Proposition
5.1
, this implies that
{\bf u}_{0}\in H^{\frac{d}{2}+1}
with
\|{\bf u}_{0}\|_{H^{\frac{d}{2}+1}}<\varepsilon
by choosing small

.
Let
\ell\geq n_{0}
be given in (
3.10
) and
x\in\Omega_{n}
with
\sqrt{n}>\ell
. Then, from (

) we have

\begin{split}\log\frac{\Phi^{r}(T,x)}{r}\geq\frac{1}{(d-1)|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{T}I_{k}(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau-CAT\geq\log n^{c_{2}}-CAT,\end{split}

and from Lemma

,

\begin{split}\log\frac{|x_{d}|}{|\Phi^{d}(T,x)|}\geq\frac{1}{4|B_{d}|}\sum_{k<n}\int_{0}^{T}I_{k}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t-CAT\geq\log n^{c_{2}}-CAT.\end{split}

Thus, we have

\begin{split}\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|\Phi^{r}(T,x)|}{r}\geq\frac{1}{2}n^{c_{2}},\qquad\inf_{x\in\Omega_{n}}\frac{|x_{d}|}{|\Phi_{d}(T,x)|}\geq\frac{1}{2}n^{c_{2}}.\end{split}

Applying them to (

), we obtain

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Lambda^{\frac{d}{2}}\omega(T)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq c_{3}\sum_{n\geq\ell^{2}}n^{c_{4}-2\alpha}\end{split}

for some

and

. Here, we take

with

. This clearly leads to a contradiction. Taking

large and

Proposition 6.1

7
Proof of the Key Lemma

We only treat the case

), obtaining

\begin{split}\left|\frac{u^{r}(x)}{r}-\frac{3}{8\pi}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{3}}{|y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq C\|\nabla\omega\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\end{split}

(7.1)

and

\begin{split}\left|\frac{u^{r}(x)}{r}-\frac{3}{8\pi}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{|y_{h}|y_{3}}{|y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq C\|\omega\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\end{split}

(7.2)

respectively. We recall (
1.5
)

\begin{split}u^{r}(x)=-\frac{1}{4\pi|x_{h}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{(x_{3}-y_{3})(x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2})}{|y_{h}||x-y|^{3}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

We fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
|x_{h}|\geq x_{3}>0
and define the sets
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\displaystyle R(x)

\displaystyle:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3};|y_{h}|\leq 4|x_{h}|,\,|y_{3}|\geq 4|x_{h}|\},\qquad S(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{3};|y_{h}|\leq 4|x_{h}|,\,|y_{3}|\leq 4|x_{h}|\}.

Using
\mathbb{R}^{3}=Q(x)\cup R(x)\cup S(x)
, we write

\begin{split}u^{r}(x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}(I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}),\end{split}

(7.3)

where
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}
refer to the integral over

, respectively. Due to
|y|^{2}\geq 8|x|^{2}
for

, we can perform the Taylor expansion and obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{3}}=\frac{1}{|y|^{3}}\left(1-\frac{2(x\cdot y)}{|y|^{2}}+\frac{|x|^{2}}{|y|^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}=\frac{1}{|y|^{3}}+\frac{3(x\cdot y)}{|y|^{5}}+O\left(\frac{|x|^{2}}{|y|^{5}}\right).\end{split}

(7.4)

Thanks to the following estimate

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{|(x_{3}-y_{3})(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})|}{|y_{h}|}\frac{|x|^{2}}{|y|^{5}}|\omega(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C|x|^{2}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{1}{|y|^{3}}\frac{|\omega(y)|}{|y|}\,\mathrm{d}y\leq C|x|\left\|\frac{\omega(y)}{|y_{h}|}\right\|_{L^{3}(Q(x))},\end{split}

\begin{split}|I_{1}-(I_{11}+I_{12}+I_{13})|\leq C|x|\left\|\frac{\omega(y)}{|y_{h}|}\right\|_{L^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},\end{split}

where

\displaystyle I_{11}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{(x_{3}-y_{3})(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})}{|y_{h}||y|^{3}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle I_{12}

\displaystyle:=-\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{x_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})(x\cdot y)}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle I_{13}

\displaystyle:=\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{y_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})(x\cdot y)}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

To begin with, the property

\begin{split}\omega(y_{h},y_{3})=\omega(-y_{h},y_{3})\end{split}

(7.5)

shows that
I_{11}=0.
Next, writing
I_{12}

\begin{split}I_{12}=-\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{x_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})^{2}}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y-\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{x_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})(x_{3}y_{3})}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

we use (
2.3

7.5
) respectively to get
I_{12}=0.

Similarly, we separate
I_{13}

\begin{split}I_{13}=\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{y_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})^{2}}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{y_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})(x_{3}y_{3})}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{split}

We clearly have

\begin{split}\frac{3}{|x_{h}|}\int_{Q(x)}\frac{y_{3}(x_{h}\cdot y_{h})(x_{3}y_{3})}{|y_{h}||y|^{5}}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y=0.\end{split}

By

\displaystyle J_{12}

We write

\displaystyle J_{122}

\displaystyle:=-\int_{Q(x)\cap\{y_{3}\geq 0\}}\frac{y_{3}|y_{h}||x-y||x-\bar{y}|}{|x-y|^{3}|x-\bar{y}|^{3}(|x-y|+|x-\bar{y}|)}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,

We only prove the case

\displaystyle=-\int_{Q(x)\cap\{y_{3}\geq 0\}}|y_{h}|y_{3}\frac{2|y|^{5}-|x-y||x-\bar{y}|^{3}(|x-y|+|x-\bar{y}|)}{2|y|^{5}|x-y||x-\tilde{y}|^{3}(|x-y|+|x-\tilde{y}|)}\omega(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

We note

\begin{split}2|y|^{5}-|x-y||x-\bar{y}|^{3}(|x-y|+|x-\bar{y}|)=|y|^{5}-|x-y|^{2}|x-\bar{y}|^{3}+|y|^{5}-|x-y||x-\bar{y}|^{4}\end{split}

Applying

With Hardy’s inequality, we deduce (

). Thus, we obtain (

). As estimating

\displaystyle J_{32}
