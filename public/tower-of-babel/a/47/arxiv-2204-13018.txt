New invariants of Gromov-Hausdorff limits of Riemannian surfaces with curvature bounded below
Semyon Alesker, Mikhail G. Katz, Roman Prosanov

Abstract
Let { X i } \{X_{i}\} be a sequence of compact n n -dimensional Alexandrov spaces (e.g. Riemannian manifolds) with curvature uniformly bounded below which converges in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense to a compact Alexandrov space X X . The paper [ 1 ] outlined (without a proof) a construction of an integer-valued function on X X ; this function carries additional geometric information on the sequence such as the limit of intrinsic volumes of X i X_{i} ’s. In this paper we consider sequences of closed 2-surfaces and (1) prove the existence of such a function in this situation; and (2) classify the functions which may arise from the construction.

Before describing the main results, let us remind a few well known facts on the topology and geometry of 2-surfaces. Any topological closed 2-surface is homeomorphic
to exactly one surface from the following list:

(1) the 2-sphere;

(2) the connected sum of
g

(3) the connected sum of
k

k\geq 1

The surfaces of types (1) and (2) are orientable, while of type (3) are not orientable. It follows that a surface is defined uniquely up to a homeomorphism
by its Euler characteristic and whether it is orientable or not.

Consider now surfaces with metrics with curvature bounded below in the sense of Alexandrov (see Definition

1.4
Relation to the work of Shioya and Yamaguchi.

Shioya and Yamaguchi
[
33
]
studied the properties of the Yamaguchi fibration and collapse in the 3-dimensional case. In particular, they studied the homeomorphism types of the metric balls inside collapsing 3-dimensional manifolds. Their techniques could be applied also in the easier 2-dimensional case, and could be used to give an alternative proof of the results of our paper. Note, however, that the methods of our paper are more elementary. Thus their approach relies on deep (even in dimension 2) facts from the Alexandrov geometry such as the generalized soul theorem and the parameterized stability theorem. The former is not used in our paper, the latter is used only in the no-collapse case.
In our study of collapse to the segment we exploit the Alexandrov realization theorem, a totally different tool from those used in
[
33
]
.

Convexity in hyperbolic space.

In this section we review a few facts on the geometry of convex subsets of the hyperbolic space, CBB(

2.1
Busemann-Feller lemma.

The following result due to Milka
[
22
]
is a hyperbolic version of the Busemann-Feller lemma in Euclidean space.

2.1 Theorem
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a closed convex subset of the hyperbolic space. Then

(1) for any point
x\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
there exists a unique nearest point from
K
;

(2) the map
\mathbb{H}^{n}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}K
sending a point from
\mathbb{H}^{n}
to its nearest point from
K
is a 1-Lipschitz map.

The following corollary should be well known but we have no reference.

2.2 Corollary
.

Let
K_{1}\subset K_{2}\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}
be 2-dimensional convex compact subsets of the hyperbolic plane. Then

length(\partial K_{1})\leq length(\partial K_{2}).

Proof.
Let
r\colon\partial K_{2}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\partial K_{1}
be the restriction to
\partial K_{2}
of the nearest point map to
K_{1}
. By the Busemann-Feller
lemma it is 1-Lipschitz. It is easy to see that
r
is onto. The result follows. Q.E.D.

2.2
Klein model of hyperbolic space.

The Klein model of the
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the open unit Euclidean ball

\mathbb{B}^{n}=\{x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}<1\}
equipped with the Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}^{2}}{1-|x|^{2}}+\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}dx_{i}\right)^{2}}{(1-|x|^{2})^{2}},

where
|x|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
is the square of the Euclidean norm.

The relevant property of this metric to be used later is that its geodesic lines are precisely the intersections of affine lines in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the open ball
\mathbb{B}^{n}
.

We say that

2.5
Isometric imbedding of 2-sphere with antipodal involution.

The following result is the Alexandrov imbedding theorem.

2.9 Theorem

(Alexandrov
[
4
]
, Ch. XII, §2)
.

Given the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with CBB(
-1
) metric
d
. Then there exists a compact convex set
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
of dimension either 3 or 2 such that
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d)
is isometric to
\partial K
in the former case, or it is isometric to the double cover
DK
in the latter case.

In this section we are going to prove the following its variation:

2.10 Theorem
.

Let
d
be a CBB(
-1
) metric on the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
invariant with respect to the antipodal involution
\iota:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Then there exists a compact convex set
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
of dimension either 3 or 2 such that
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d)
is isometric to its boundary (in the former case),
or it is isometric to the double cover
DK
(in the latter case) and
K
is symmetric with respect to a point so that this symmetry
I:\mathbb{H}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{3}
induces
\iota
.

This result follows from the combination of Theorem
2.9
and Pogorelov’s rigidity theorem in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. The latter says that the realization is unique in a strong sense. For 3-dimensional convex bodies in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
this means that every isometry of the boundaries is induced by an isometry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. We do not put further restrictions on the boundaries, so they may be neither smooth, nor polyhedral. One needs to be more careful to include also the 2-dimensional cases, compare with the polyhedral version below, Theorem
2.13
. However, proofs of Pogorelov’s rigidity theorem are notoriously difficult even in the Euclidean space, see
[
30
]
. For the hyperbolic case Pogorelov provided in
[
30
]
an intricate outline how to reduce it to the Euclidean case, this outline was completed by Milka in
[
23
]
.

We would like to point out that this seems to be slightly excessive for Theorem
2.10
. Its proof can be obtained from the classic approach of Alexandrov to Theorem
2.9
, see
[
4
, Chapter VII]
, combined with the rigidity of convex polyhedra in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, which seem to us much more accessible rather than the rigidity of general convex surfaces in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
(and the proofs do not differ much between the Euclidean and hyperbolic cases). Hence, here we sketch a proof of Theorem
2.10
using these tools. Some steps of Alexandrov’s approach to the approximation of CBB metrics by cone-metrics were also verified in CBB(
-1
) case by Richard in
[
31
, Annex A]
.

Let us have a few preparations.

2.11 Definition
.

A
hyperbolic cone-metric

d
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is locally isometric to the metric of hyperbolic plane except finitely many points called
conical points
. At a conical point
v
the metric
d
is locally isometric to the metric of a hyperbolic cone with angle
\lambda_{v}\neq 2\pi
. A hyperbolic cone-metric is called
convex
if for every conical point
v
we have
\lambda_{v}<2\pi
.

We will use the following results.

2.12 Theorem

(Alexandrov’s realization theorem)
.

Let
d
be a convex hyperbolic cone-metric on the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then there exists a closed convex polyhedron
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{3}
of dimension either 3 or 2 such that
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d)
is isometric to its boundary (in the former case), or it is isometric to the double cover
DK
(in the latter case).

In the Euclidean case this is Theorem in
[
3
, Section 4.3]
. The proof works just the same in the hyperbolic 3-space, as it is noted in
[
3
, Section 5.3]
.

.

.

,

to

, hence in particular each copy of

The isometry

such that

Again, in the Euclidean case this is the Theorem in

2.15 Theorem

(Blaschke Selection Theorem)
.

A sequence of convex compact sets

in

of uniformly bounded diameters passing through the same point contains a subsequence converging to a compact convex set

in the Hausdorff sense.

This version follows easily from
[

]
, Theorem 7.3.8.

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

From Theorem
2.14
we consider a
\iota
-invariant triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{i}
of
(\mathbb{S}^{2},d)
sufficiently fine so that each angle of each triangle is not less than the angle of the respective hyperbolic triangle. One can do this due to the compactness of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The sum of the angles of all triangles at each vertex is at most
2\pi
. See
[
31
, Lemma A.2.1]
, which adapts the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
4
, Chapter VII.4]
from the CBB(0) case. Thus, if we replace each triangle of
\mathcal{T}_{i}
by the respective hyperbolic comparison triangle, we obtain a
\iota
-invariant convex hyperbolic cone-metric

.

The metrics

converge to

uniformly. In the Euclidean case this is shown in
[
4
, Chapter VII.6]
. In the CBB(
-1
) case this is done in
[
31
, Annex A.2]
. In particular, the diameters of

are bounded.

Due to Theorem
2.12
, there exists a polyhedron

such that

is isometric to its boundary (if the dimension of

is 3) or to

(if the dimension is 2). In the former case, due to Theorem
2.13
, the action by
\iota
is induced by an isometry

of order two fixing
\partial K^{\prime}_{i}
as a set, but having no fixed points in it. Due to the classification of isometries of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,
I_{i}
is a symmetry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
around a point. In the latter case, Theorem
2.13
implies that

is a centrally-symmetric polygon. Then by
I_{i}
we consider the central symmetry of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
around the center of the polygon. We compose each

with another isometry to obtain compact convex polyhedra

symmetric with respect to the same isometry

, which is the central symmetry around a point

.

As the diameters of

are uniformly bounded, the diameters of

in

are also uniformly bounded. Thus, by Theorem
2.15
there exists a subsequence converging in the Hausdorff metric to a compact convex set

Due to Proposition
2.5
(3),

shows that if

Q.E.D.

3
Collapse of
n
-dimensional convex bodies in hyperbolic
n
-space.

The main results of this subsection are Propositions
3.3
and
3.5
.

Let a sequence
\{K_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
of
n
-dimensional convex compact sets converge to a convex compact set
K
with
1\leq\dim K\leq n-2
.
We note that for our applications we need only the case
n=3
and
K
be a non-degenerate segment, but parts of the proof remain the same in a greater generality, so we consider it as an additional support for the validity of our construction.
By
L
we denote the geodesic subspace of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
spanned by
K
, i.e., having the same dimension and containing
K
, by
q:\mathbb{H}^{n}\rightarrow L
we denote the nearest-point map to
L
.

Let us fix a point
x\in K
. We denote by
dist
the distance on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Set

{\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta)=\{z\in\partial K_{i}|\,\,dist(z,x)<\delta\}.

Denote

{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta)=\{z\in\partial K_{i}|\,\,dist(q(z),x)<\delta\}.

3.1 Lemma
.

Let
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}
. Then there exists
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
i>i_{0}
and for any
x\in K
one has

{\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2})\subset{\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1})\subset{\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{2})\subset{\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2})\subset{\cal A}_{i,x}(2\delta_{2}).

Proof.

If the points
z
,
q(z)
and
x
are pairwise distinct, the triangle formed by them has right angle at
q(z)
. Thus,
dist(q(z),x)\leq dist(z,x)
(this is also clearly true if some of these points coincide) and for any
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}
we have
{\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta)\subset{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta^{\prime})
. Next, for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
i>i_{0}
we have
K_{i}\subset L_{\varepsilon}
, where
L_{\varepsilon}
is the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
L
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. For
\delta>0
by
B(x,\delta)
we denote the
\delta
-ball in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
centered at
x
, by
A(x,\delta)
we denote the set of points
z\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
dist(q(z),x)<\delta
. For every
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}
there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\big(A(x,\delta)\cap L_{\varepsilon}\big)\subset B(x,\delta^{\prime}).

Indeed, one can take
\varepsilon
to be the distance between
L
and any point from
\big(\partial A(x,\delta)\big)\cap\big(\partial B(x,\delta^{\prime})\big)
. Hence, it follows that for any
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}
there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
i>i_{0}
we have

{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta)=\big(A(x,\delta)\cap\partial K_{i}\big)=\big(A(x,\delta)\cap L_{\varepsilon}\cap\partial K_{i}\big)\subset\big(B(x,\delta^{\prime})\cap\partial K_{i}\big)={\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta^{\prime}).

Q.E.D.

3.2 Lemma
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be an n-dimensional convex compact subset of the hyperbolic n-space,
L\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a totally geodesic subset of dimension
k
,
1\leq k\leq n-2
,
q:\mathbb{H}^{n}\rightarrow L
be the nearest-point map, and
A:=int(q(K))
. Then the restriction of
q
to
\partial K
is a topological fibre bundle over
A
with fiber homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-k}
.

Proof.

This could be easier to perceive in the Klein model. We note that if in the Klein model
L
passes through the origin, then orthogonality to
L
is the same both in hyperbolic and in Euclidean metrics.

Let
R:=\max_{x\in K}dist(x,L)
and
L_{R}
be the closed
R
-neighborhood of
L
in
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, hence
K\subset L_{R}
. Then
\partial L_{R}
is homeomorphic to
L\times\mathbb{S}^{n-k}
so that the restriction of
q
to
\partial L_{R}
is the projection to the first component. For
x\in\partial K\cap q^{-1}(A)
by
l_{x}
denote the line passing through
x
orthogonal to
L
. Note that as
l_{x}\cap L=q(x)\in A
,
l_{x}
does not belong to any supporting hyperplane to
K
, hence
l_{x}
intersects
int(K)
, thus it intersects
\partial K
exactly in two points contained in the segment
l_{x}\cap L_{R}
. The ray belonging to
l_{x}
starting at
x
outwards
K
intersects
\partial L_{R}
in a unique point, which we denote
f(x)
. The map
f:\partial K\cap q^{-1}(A)\rightarrow\partial L_{\varepsilon}
is injective and continuous. Therefore, it is a homeomorphism onto the image, which is
q^{-1}(A)\cong A\times\mathbb{S}^{n-k}
. By construction, the composition of
f
with the projection to the first component equals
q
. Now the lemma follows.
Q.E.D.

3.3 Proposition
.

Let
\{K_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a sequence of
n
-dimensional convex compact sets converging in the Hausdorff metric to a convex compact set
K
of dimension
k
,
1\leq k\leq n-2
.
Let
\kappa>0
be a constant. Let
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\frac{\kappa}{2}
.
Then there exists
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
i>i_{0}
, for any
x\in int(K)
with
dist(x,\partial K)>\kappa
and for an arbitrary commutative group
A
one has

Im(H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2});A)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1});A))\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&\mbox{if}&a=0,n-k\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}&\end{array}.\right.

Proof.
Consider the natural maps in cohomology induced by inclusions in Lemma
3.1

\displaystyle H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}))\overset{a_{1}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1}))\overset{a_{2}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{2}))\overset{a_{3}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2}))\overset{a_{4}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(2\delta_{2})).

(3.0.1)

Step 1.
Let us show that the maps
a_{1}\circ a_{2}
and
a_{3}\circ a_{4}
are isomorphisms. For it suffices to show that for any
0<\delta<\kappa
the natural map

H^{*}(q^{-1}(x)\cap\partial K_{i})\leftarrow H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta))

is an isomorphism for large
i
. But for large
i
the set
\{x\in K|\,dist(x,\partial K)>\kappa\}
belongs to the interior of the image
q(K_{i})
. Thus, for large
i
the restriction
q|_{\partial K_{i}}
is a topological fiber bundle over this set with fiber homeomorphic to the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-k}
by Lemma
3.2
.

Step 2.
It follows from Step 1 and the diagram (
3.0.1
) that the image

Im(H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2}))\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1})))
is isomorphic to
H^{a}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}))\simeq H^{a}(q^{-1}(x)\cap\partial K_{i})=H^{a}(\mathbb{S}^{n-k})
. The result follows. Q.E.D.

Now we need to study points at
\partial K
. Here for simplicity we consider only the case when
K
is a non-degenerate segment
I
. Let
x\in\partial I
be a point and
0<\delta<length(I)
be a real number.
The boundary of the set
{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta)
are two totally geodesic hyperplanes at distance
\delta
from
x
orthogonal to the line
L
. We denote them by
{\cal H}_{\delta}
and
{\cal G}_{\delta}
such that
{\cal H}_{\delta}
intersects
I
.

3.4 Lemma
.

Let
x\in\partial I
and
0<\delta<length(I)
. Then for large
i
the set
{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta)
is homeomorphic to a disk. In particular for any commutative group
A
one has

H^{a}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta);A)\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&\mbox{if}&a=0\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}&\end{array}\right.

Proof.
In the Klein model
{\cal H}_{\delta}
is the usual Euclidean hyperplane intersected with the unit Euclidean ball. For large
i
the hyperplane
{\cal H}_{\delta}
intersects the interior of
K_{i}
since both open halfspaces bounded by
{\cal H}_{\delta}
must contain points of
K_{i}
. Also for large
i
the hyperplane
{\cal G}_{\delta}
does not intersect
K_{i}
. Let
{\cal H}_{\delta}^{+}
be the open halfspace bounded by
{\cal H}_{\delta}
and containing
x
.
Then for large
i
we have
{\cal A}_{i,x}(\delta)={\cal H}_{\delta}^{+}\cap\partial K_{i}
, which is an open cap homeomorphic to a disc. Q.E.D.

3.5 Proposition
.

Let
\{K_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a sequence of
n
-dimensional convex bodies converging in the Hausdorff metric to a (non-degenerate) segment
I
.
Let
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\frac{length(I)}{2}
. Let
x\in\partial I
. Then there exists
i_{0}
such that for any
i>i_{0}
and any commutative group
A
one has

Im(H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2});A)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1});A))\simeq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&\mbox{if}&a=0\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}&\end{array}.\right.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
for large
i
the inclusions hold

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{i,a}(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2})\subset{\cal B}_{i,a}(\delta_{1})\subset{\cal A}_{i,a}(\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{2})\subset{\cal B}_{i,a}(\delta_{2})\subset{\cal A}_{i,a}(2\delta_{2}).

(3.0.2)

Let us consider the sequence in cohomology induced by inclusions (
3.0.2
)

H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}))\overset{a_{1}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{1}))\overset{a_{2}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(\frac{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}{2}))\overset{a_{3}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\delta_{2}))\overset{a_{4}}{\leftarrow}H^{*}({\cal A}_{i,x}(2\delta_{2})).

It follows from Lemma

that

and

are isomorphisms. Then it follows from the above diagram that

4
Riemannian submersions and the Yamaguchi map.

4.1
Riemannian submersions

Let us review a few facts on Riemannian submersions. Let
M,N
be smooth complete Riemannian manifolds. Let
\pi\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a Riemannian submersion.
At any point of
M
the tangent space of it splits into the direct sum of the vertical subspace, i.e. the tangent space to the fiber of
\pi
, and the horizontal subspace, i.e.
the orthogonal complement to the vertical subspace.

Let
C\colon[a,b]\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a smooth curve. Fix
c\in[a,b]
and
\tilde{c}\in\pi^{-1}(c)
. Then there exists a unique smooth curve
\tilde{C}\colon[a,b]\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}M
, called a horizontal lift of
C
, such that

C=\pi\circ\tilde{C},

\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{C}(t)
is horizontal for any
t\in[a,b]
, and
\tilde{C}(c)=\tilde{c}
.

4.1 Lemma

(
[
8
]
, Proposition 3.31)
.

In the above notation
C
is a geodesic if and only if its horizontal lift
\tilde{C}
is.

4.2 Lemma

(
[
21
]
, Corollary 26.12)
.

Let
\pi\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a Riemannian submersion. Let
\gamma
be a geodesic in
M
. Assume
\gamma
is orthogonal to a fiber of
\pi
at some point. Then
\gamma

is orthogonal to each fiber it intersects.

A geodesic in
M
orthogonal to all fibers it intersects will be called
horizontal
geodesics. It follows from Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
that a geodesic in
M
is horizontal if and only if it is
a horizontal lift of its image under
\pi
. It is easy to see that the length of any horizontal geodesic and of its image under
\pi
are equal.

Below
inj(N)
denotes the injectivity radius of
N
.

4.3 Lemma
.

Let
\pi\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a Riemannian submersion. Let
x\in N
. Let
0<\delta<inj(N)
.
Let
{\cal N}_{\delta}
denote the
\delta
-neighborhood of zero section of the normal bundle of the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
.

(1) For any
x\in N,p\in M
one has

dist(p,\pi^{-1}(x))=dist(\pi(p),x).

(2) For any point from
\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
there is exactly one path minimizing the distance from this point to the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
.
This path is necessarily a horizontal geodesic.

(3) For any vector
v\in{\cal N}_{\delta}
one has

dist(\exp(v),\pi^{-1}(x))=|v|.

(4) If
\pi
is proper then the exponential map
\exp\colon{\cal N}_{\delta}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}M
is a homeomorphism onto
\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
.

Proof.

(1) First since
\pi
is 1-Lipschitz one has

dist(p,\pi^{-1}(x))\geq dist(\pi(p),x).

To prove the opposite inequality let
\gamma
be a geodesic in
N
minimizing the distance between
\pi(p)
and
x
. Let
\tilde{\gamma}
be its horizontal lift started at
p
.
\tilde{\gamma}
ends necessarily on
\pi^{-1}(x)
.

\gamma
and
\tilde{\gamma}
have equal length. Hence the opposite inequality follows.

(2) Let now
p\in\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
. Let us assume that there exist two geodesics
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
connecting
p
to
\pi^{-1}(x)
and having length
dist(p,\pi^{-1}(x))
.
By minimality both have to be orthogonal to the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
. Hence they are horizontal and by Lemma
4.1
their images
\pi\circ\gamma_{1},\pi\circ\gamma_{2}
are geodesics in
N
; they have the same length

dist(p,\pi^{-1}(x))\overset{\mbox{part }(1)}{=}dist(\pi(p),x)<\delta
. Since
\delta<inj(N)
the two geodesics must coincide
\pi\circ\gamma_{1}=\pi\circ\gamma_{2}
. Since
\gamma_{i}
is the horizontal lift of
\pi\circ\gamma_{i}
,
i=1,2
,
with the common end point
p
it follows that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
.

(3) Let
v\in{\cal N}_{\delta}
.
Let
\gamma(t):=\exp(tv),\,t\in[0,1]
. Then
\gamma
is a horizontal geodesic since it is orthogonal to the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
. When
t\in[0,1]
its length is equal to the length of
\pi\circ\gamma
on the one hand, and equals to
|v|
on the other hand.
Clearly
length(\gamma)=|v|<\delta
. Hence
\gamma\subset\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
. There is a path
\gamma_{0}
minimizing the distance between
\exp(v)
and
\pi^{-1}(x)
; it is necessarily a horizontal geodesic by part (2).
Then the images
\pi\circ\gamma
and
\pi\circ\gamma_{0}
are geodesics contained in
B(x,\delta)
with equal endpoint
x
and
\pi(\exp(v))
. Since
\delta<inj(N)
it follows
\pi\circ\gamma=\pi\circ\gamma_{0}
. Hence their
horizontal lifts are equal:
\gamma=\gamma_{0}
. Part (3) follows.

(4) Since
\pi
is 1-Lipschitz,
\exp({\cal N}_{\delta})\subset\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
.

Let us show the opposite inclusion. Let
p\in\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
. Let
\gamma
be a normal geodesic starting at
p
and minimizing distance from
p
to
\pi^{-1}(x)
. By part (3)
\gamma
is a horizontal geodesic.
Let us denote its end point by
z\in\pi^{-1}(x)
. By part (1)

l:=length(\gamma)=dist(p,z)=dist(p,\pi^{-1}(x))=dist(\pi(p),x)<\delta.

Then
v:=\frac{d\gamma}{dt}\big|_{z}
belongs to the unit normal bundle to
\pi^{-1}(x)
. Then
\gamma(t)=\exp(tv)
,
t\in[0,l]
. In particular
p=\exp(lv)
.
Since
l<\delta
the converse inclusion follows and hence

\exp({\cal N}_{\delta})=\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta)).

Let us prove the injectivity of
\exp
on
{\cal N}_{\delta}
. Otherwise there exist
p\in\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
and two horizontal geodesics
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
starting on
\pi^{-1}(x)
, ending at
p
, contained in
\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
.
Then their images
\pi\circ\gamma_{1}
and
\pi\circ\gamma_{2}
are geodesics contained in
B(x,\delta)
, both start at
x
and end at
\pi(p)
. Since
\delta<inj(N)
,
\pi\circ\gamma_{1}=\pi\circ\gamma_{2}
.
Hence
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
.

Let us show that the inverse map of
\exp
is continuous. Let
p_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}p
be a sequence in
\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))
. Set

\displaystyle(z_{i},v_{i}):=\exp^{-1}(p_{i})\in{\cal N}_{\delta},

\displaystyle(z,v):=\exp^{-1}(p)\in{\cal N}_{\delta},

where
z_{i},z\in\pi^{-1}(x)
and
v_{i}\in{\cal N}_{\delta}|_{z_{i}},\,v\in{\cal N}_{\delta}|_{z}
. Since
\pi
is proper after a choice of subsequence we may assume that
(z_{i},v_{i})\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}(y,w)\in{\cal N}_{\delta}
.
Then by continuity of
\exp
one has

\exp_{y}(w)=\exp_{z}(v).

Since
\exp
is bijective on
{\cal N}_{\delta}
it follows that
(y,w)=(z,v)
. Q.E.D.

4.4 Corollary
.

Let
\pi\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a proper smooth Riemannian submersion. Let
\delta<inj(N)
. For any point
x\in N
the natural imbedding

\pi^{-1}(x)\hookrightarrow\pi^{-1}(B(x,\delta))

is a homotopy retraction.

Proof.
The following diagram is commutative

where the horizontal arrow are homeomorphisms by Lemma
4.3
(4) and the vertical ones are closed imbeddings. Since the left arrow is a homotopy
retraction, so is the right one. Q.E.D.

4.5 Proposition
.

Let
(M,g)
,
(N,h)
be smooth Riemannian manifolds. Let
\pi\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
be a smooth proper submersion. Let
0<\delta_{1}<\delta_{2}<\frac{1}{10}\min\{inj(N),1\}
.
Let
0<\varepsilon<\frac{\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}-\delta_{1}\}}{100(1+diam(N))}
. Let us assume that
\pi
is
\varepsilon
-almost Riemannian submersion, i.e.

e^{-\varepsilon}<\frac{|d\pi(v)|}{|v|}<e^{\varepsilon}

for any non-zero tangent to
M
vector
v
which is horizonal, i.e. orthogonal to the corresponding fiber of
\pi
.

Let

be a metric on

Most probably the results of this section are folklore. The main result of this section is Theorem

below.
Let us state a consequence of it:

Let a sequence

5.1
Equivariant GH-distance.

Let us fix throughout this subsection a finite group

5.1 Definition
.

Let

and
(Y,d_{Y})

G
on

and

Define the

-equivariant GH-distance between them as

where the infimum is taken over all
G
-invariant semi-metrics on the disjoint union
X\bigsqcup Y
extending the original semi-metrics on
X
and
Y
and such that

d(x,y)>0
for any
x\in X,y\in Y
.
5
5

5

The only difference between this definition and the
original GH-distance is that

has to be
G
-invariant.

5.2 Remark
.

If
(X,d)
is a semi-metric space acted by
G
by isometries then the canonical quotient metric space
(\bar{X},\bar{d})
carries the induced action of

by isometries and

5.3 Lemma

Let

(X,d_{X})
, and

(1) Then

(2)

is a semi-metric on the class of semi-metric spaces with an isometric
G
-action as in Definition

.

(3) Assume in addition that
(X,d_{X})
and

are metric (rather than semi-metric) spaces. Then

if and only if there exists a

Proof.
(1) The first inequality is obvious. For the second one, fix a constant

(2) The symmetry of

Given

, disregarding the action.
Then

(1) there exists an action of
G
on
X
by isometries and a subsequence of
\{X_{i}\}
which converges to
X
in the
G
-equivariant sense, i.e. in
{\cal X}^{G}
;

(2) For this subsequence
G\backslash X_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}G\backslash X
in GH-sense.

5.2
A simple lemma on finite-group quotients.

Let a finite group
G
act on a metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
by isometries. Let

\pi\colon Z\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}G\backslash Z

be the canonical quotient map; it is a 1-Lipschitz map when the target is equipped with the quotient metric
\bar{d}_{Z}
.

Let
\bar{z}\in G\backslash Z
. Let

\pi^{-1}(\bar{z})=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\}.

Let
\bar{A}\subset G\backslash Z
be a subset such that

\displaystyle\alpha:=\sup_{\bar{a}\in\bar{A}}dist(\bar{z},\bar{a})<\frac{1}{10}\min_{i\neq j}d_{Z}(z_{i},z_{j}).

(5.2.1)

Let

A_{p}:=\pi^{-1}(\bar{A})\cap\{x\in Z|\,d(z_{p},x)\leq\alpha\}.

5.8 Lemma
.

In the above notation we have

(1)
A_{p}\cap A_{q}=\emptyset
for
p\neq q
.

(2) For any
1\leq p\leq k
and any
g\in G
there exists
1\leq q\leq k
such that
g(A_{p})=A_{q}
.

(3)
\pi^{-1}(\bar{A})=\bigsqcup_{p=1}^{k}A_{p}
.

(4)
\pi(A_{p})=\bar{A}
for any
p=1,\dots,k
.

(5) The natural map

Stab(x_{p})\backslash A_{p}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bar{A}

is an isometry for any
p=1,\dots,k
.

Proof.
(1) Let
z\in A_{p}\cap A_{q}
,
p\neq q
. Then
d_{Z}(z_{p},z_{q})\leq 2\alpha\overset{(\ref{E:A-distance})}{<}d_{Z}(z_{p},z_{q})
; this is a contradiction.

(2) Let
q
be such that
g(z_{p})=z_{q}
. Let
x\in A_{p}
. Then
d_{Z}(g(x),z_{q})=d_{Z}(x,z_{p})\leq\alpha
. Hence
g(A_{p})\subset A_{q}
.
By the symmetry the opposite inclusion also holds.

(3) Let
x\in\pi^{-1}(\bar{A})
. By the definition of
\bar{d}_{Z}
we have

\min_{i}d_{Z}(x,z_{i})=\bar{d}_{Z}(\pi(x),\bar{z})\leq\alpha.

Hence there exists
p
such that
d_{Z}(x,z_{p})\leq\alpha
, i.e.
x\in A_{p}
.

(4) Let
\bar{x}\in A
. Let
\pi(x)=\bar{x}
. By (2) there exists
q
such that
x\in A_{q}
. There exists
g\in G
such that
g(z_{q})=z_{p}
. By part (3)

g(A_{q})=A_{p}
. Hence
g(x)\in A_{p}
and
\pi(g(x))=\bar{x}
. Hence
\pi(A_{p})=\bar{A}
.

(5) Let
x,y\in A_{p}
and
\pi(x)=\pi(y)
. Then there exists
g\in G
such that
y=g(x)
. Due to disjointness of different
A_{i}
’s, one has
g(A_{p})=A_{p}
.
Hence
g\in Stab(z_{p})
. Hence the map
Stab(z_{p})\backslash A_{p}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\bar{A}
is injective and due to part (4) it is bijective. It remains to show that this map preserves distances.

Let
x,y\in A_{p}
be arbitrary points. Let
g\in G
. Assume that
g(A_{p})=A_{q}
with
q\neq p
. Then

\displaystyle d_{Z}(x,g(y))\geq

\displaystyle d_{Z}(z_{p},z_{q})-d_{Z}(x,z_{p})-d_{Z}(g(y),z_{q})\geq d_{Z}(z_{p},z_{q})-d_{Z}(x,z_{p})-d_{Z}(y,z_{p})>

\displaystyle>8\alpha.

On the other hand if
g(A_{p})=A_{p}
, or equivalently
g(z_{p})=z_{p}
,

d_{Z}(x,g(y))\leq d_{Z}(x,z_{p})+d_{Z}(g(y),z_{p})\leq 2\alpha.

Consequently

\bar{d}_{Z}(\pi(x),\pi(y))=\min_{g\in G}d_{Z}(x,g(y))=\min_{g\in Stab(z_{p})}d_{Z}(x,g(y)).

Part (5) follows. Q.E.D.

5.3
G
-equivariant Yamaguchi map.

Let
G
be a finite group. We would like to have a
G
-equivariant version of the Yamaguchi theorem
4.6
which we prove here only in a very special case needed below, namely when
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and the target manifold
N
is a circle.
Let us formulate the general question.

5.9 Question
.

Let a sequence
\{M_{i}^{m}\}
of closed smooth Riemannian manifolds with uniformly bounded below sectional curvature and equipped with a
G
-action by isometries, converge in
{\cal X}^{G}
to a smooth closed Riemannian manifold
N
acted by
G
by isometries.
Let
d_{i}
be
G
-invariant metrics on
M_{i}\bigsqcup N
extending the original metrics on
M_{i}
and
N
and such that
d_{i,H}(M_{i},N)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0
.

The question is whether there exist positive numbers
\varepsilon_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0
and smooth maps
f_{i}\colon M_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
such that for large
i

(1)
f_{i}
is
\varepsilon_{i}
-almost Riemannian submersions;

(2)
f_{i}
is
G
-equivariant;

(3)
d_{i}(x,f_{i}(x))<\varepsilon_{i}
for any
x\in M_{i}
;

(4) the fibers of
f_{i}
are connected.

5.10 Remark
.

One can show that (4) follows from (1)-(3).

We are going to show that this question has positive answer in a very special situation sufficient for applications in this paper:

5.11 Proposition
.

In the notation of Question
5.9
let us assume that
N
is a circle (imbedded standardly to a the plane),
the group
G=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
acts on
N
by reflection with respect to a line passing through the center of the circle
N
.
Then Question
5.9
has positive answer, i.e. there exist
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-equivariant maps
f_{i}\colon M_{i}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
satisfying (1)-(4) there.

The proof of this proposition will be reduced to the proof of Yamaguchi’s Theorem
4.6
. Let us remind the main construction used in the proof of the latter theorem.

Let
M,N
be, as in Theorem
4.6
, smooth closed Riemannian manifolds such that
\dim M=m\geq\dim N
. Let
sec(M)\geq-1
,
|sec(N)|\leq 1
, and the injectivity radius
inj(N)>\mu
, where
\mu>0
is a fixed number.
Let
0<\varepsilon\ll\sigma\ll\frac{1}{2}\min\{\mu,\pi/2\}
.
Let
d
be a metric on
M\bigsqcup N

extending the original metrics on
M
and
N
and such that

.

One can show (see
[
37
]
and references therein) that there exists a finite set (of indices)
{\cal I}
and two maps
\mathfrak{m}\colon{\cal I}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}M,\,\mathfrak{n}\colon{\cal I}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}N
such that

\displaystyle M\subset(Im(\mathfrak{m}))_{5\varepsilon},\,\,N\subset(Im(\mathfrak{n}))_{5\varepsilon}

(5.3.1)

\displaystyle d(\mathfrak{m}(i),\mathfrak{m}(j))>\varepsilon,\,d(\mathfrak{n}(i),\mathfrak{n}(j))>\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }i\neq j\in{\cal I}

(5.3.2)

\displaystyle d(\mathfrak{m}(i),\mathfrak{n}(i))<\varepsilon.

(5.3.3)

In addition to these subsets let us fix a
C^{\infty}
-smooth function
h\colon\mathbb{R}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}h(0)=1&&\\
h(t)=0&\mbox{if}&t\geq\sigma\\
-\frac{4}{\sigma}<h^{\prime}(t)<-\frac{3}{\sigma}&\mbox{if}&\frac{3\sigma}{8}<t<\frac{5\sigma}{8}\\
-\frac{4}{\sigma}h^{\prime}(t)<0&\mbox{if}&\frac{2\sigma}{8}<t<\frac{3\sigma}{8}\mbox{ or }\frac{5\sigma}{8}<t<\frac{6\sigma}{8}\\
-\sigma<h^{\prime}(t)<0&\mbox{if}&0<t<\frac{2\sigma}{8}\mbox{ or }\frac{6\sigma}{8}<t<\sigma\\
|h^{\prime\prime}(t)|<\frac{50}{\sigma}&\mbox{for all }t.\end{array}\right.

Define the map
\Phi_{N}\colon N\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathbb{R}^{{\cal I}}

\Phi_{N}(x)=[i\mapsto h(d(x,\mathfrak{n}(i)))].

In was shown in
[
16
]
that if
\sigma
and

are smaller than a constant depending on

and

then

satisfies the following properties:

(i)

(ii) Let us denote by

the normal bundle of

in

is the maximal number of points of

in balls of radius
\sigma
in
N
. Denote by

the subset of normal vectors of Euclidean norm at most
C
. The claim is that
the restriction to

{\cal U}
, where

m,\mu,\sigma
. We denote by

P\colon{\cal U}\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\Phi_{N}(N)

Let us define a

\Phi_{M}\colon M\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathbb{R}^{\cal I}

.

It is not hard to check that

. Finally define

The claim is that this

Let us now discuss how the construction of the map

-equivariant situation where

5.12 Lemma
.

Let us assume that there exist a finite set

Proof of Proposition
5.11
.

[

:

\iota\colon N\hookrightarrow V.

Let
{\cal N}_{N}
be the normal bundle of

6.1
Reduction between sequences of metrics.

The goal of this subsection is to prove a technical lemma, which allows to reduce the study of the nearby cycle construction from a sequence of metrics to a sequence of metrics with more feasible behavior.

Let us introduce notation. Let
\{X_{i}\}
be a sequence of sets equipped with two sequences of metrics
\{d_{X_{i}}\}
and
\{d^{\prime}_{X_{i}}\}
each of them making
X_{i}
a compact space. Let us assume that

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in X_{i}}|d_{X_{i}}(x,y)-d^{\prime}_{X_{i}}(x,y)|\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0.

(6.1.1)

Further assume that
\{(X_{i},d_{X_{i}})\}
GH-converges to a compact metric space
X
. This and the condition (
6.1.1
) imply that
\{(X_{i},d^{\prime}_{X_{i}})\}
GH-converges to
X
.
Let
d_{i}
(resp.
d_{i}^{\prime}
) be a semi-metric on
X_{i}\coprod X
extending the original metric on
X
and the metric
d_{X_{i}}
(resp.
d^{\prime}_{X_{i}}
) on
X_{i}
.
Assume that

\displaystyle d_{i,H}(X_{i},X)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0\mbox{ and }d^{\prime}_{i,H}(X_{i},X)\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0.

(6.1.2)

Fix a subset

, and

.

Denote

\displaystyle{\cal B}_{i,x}(\varepsilon):=\{y\in X_{i}|\,\,d_{i}(x,y)<\varepsilon\},

(6.1.3)

\displaystyle{\cal B}^{\prime}_{i,x}(\varepsilon):=\{y\in X_{i}|\,\,d^{\prime}_{i}(x,y)<\varepsilon\}.

(6.1.4)

In the next lemma the cohomology is taken with coefficients in a field.

6.1 Lemma
.

6.1.2

be a non-negative integer. Assume that for any

) implies that there exists

\dim Im[X\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}Y]\geq\dim Im[A\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}B]

\displaystyle\dim Im[H^{a}({\cal B}^{\prime}_{i,x}(2\varepsilon_{2}))\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}^{\prime}_{i,x}(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}}{2}))]\leq\dim Im[H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\varepsilon_{2}))\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}_{i,x}(\varepsilon_{1}))]\leq

\displaystyle\dim Im[H^{a}({\cal B}^{\prime}_{i,x}(\delta_{2}))\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}H^{a}({\cal B}^{\prime}_{i,x}(\delta_{1}))].

But the first and the third dimensions are equal to each other by the initial assumption. Hence the result follows. Q.E.D.

6.2
Which surfaces may collapse.

The main result of this subsection is Proposition
6.4
which seems to be well known.
For the lack of reference and for the sake of completeness we present a proof.
We will need a few preparations which will also play a role later.
The following result is a combination of Lemmas 1.13 and 1.14 in
[
34
]
.

6.2 Lemma

(Slutskiy,
[
34
]
)
.

Let
S
be a closed 2-dimensional surface with a metric with curvature at least -1 in the sense of Alexandrov.
Then there exists a sequence of
C^{\infty}
-smooth metrics on
S
with the Gaussian curvature ar least -1 which converges uniformly to the given metric.

6.3 Lemma
.

Given a
C^{\infty}
-smooth metric on the sphere
S^{2}
with Gaussian curvature at least -1. Then its isometric imbedding into
\mathbb{H}^{3}
cannot be a double cover of a convex set.

Proof.
Assume the opposite. Then the Gaussian curvature
K=-1
everywhere: indeed, this is clearly the case outside of the boundary, and since the boundary has measure 0 the same is true
on the boundary by the
C^{\infty}
-smoothness. By the Gauss-Bonnet formula we have

4\pi=2\pi\chi(S^{2})=\int_{S^{2}}KdA=-area(S^{2})<0.

This is a contradiction. Q.E.D.

6.4 Proposition
.

Let
\{X_{i}\}
be a sequence of closed 2-surfaces of a given homeomorphism type with CBB(
-1
) metrics which GH-converges to a compact metric space of dimension less than 2.
Then
X_{i}
are homeomorphic to one of the following surfaces: sphere, real projective plane, torus, Klein bottle.

Proof.
By Lemma
6.2
we may assume that the metrics on
X_{i}
are
C^{\infty}
-smooth. By the Gauss-Bonnet formula

\chi(X_{i})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{X_{i}}KdA\geq-area(X_{i}).

Since
\{X_{i}\}
collapses,
area(X_{i})\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}0
by
[
7
]
, Theorem 10.8. Hence
\chi(X_{i})\geq 0
. In the orientable case that means that
X_{i}
are homeomorphic either to
the 2-sphere or 2-torus. In the non-orientable case
X_{i}
is homeomorphic to a connected sum of
k
copies of
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The Euler characteristic of it is
2-k\geq 0
.
Thus either
k=1
, i.e.
X_{i}
is
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
, or
k=2
, i.e.
X_{i}
is the Klein bottle. Q.E.D.

6.8
An application to integration with respect to the Euler characteristic.

As an application of our main results of closed surfaces let us prove the property (
) for intrinsic volumes. As we have mentioned in the introduction,
the only relevant cases of intrinsic volumes are
V_{0}=\chi
and
V_{2}=area
. For the latter the result follows from
[
7
]
, Theorem 10.8.
Let us consider the former case of the Euler characteristic
\chi

First we have to provide more explanations on the right hand side of (
). We need to briefly remind the notion of integration of a ’constructible’
function with respect to the Euler characteristic. The technical definition of constructibility depends on the context and will not be specified here. However in the application
in this subsection this will not lead to misunderstanding due to simplicity of the situation. Let
X
be a ’nice’ topological space, e.g. it might be a real analytic manifold. For the general conjectures in
[
1
]

X
should be a compact finite dimensional Alexandrov space. In this subsection it suffices to consider the case of closed topological surfaces, segments, and points.

Let
F\colon X\allowbreak\mathrel{\mathop{\hbox to11.99998pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits}\mathbb{C}
be a compactly supported function with finitely many values and ’nice’ level sets. Under broad assumptions satisfied in relevant for us case
F
can be written as
a finite linear combination

\displaystyle F=\sum_{i}c_{i}{1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{Z_{i}}

(6.8.1)

where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
Z_{i}
are compact sets which are ’nice’ enough to have a well defined Euler characteristic (thus e.g. the Cantor set is excluded).
One defines the integral of
F
with respect to the Euler characteristic by

\int Fd\chi:=\sum_{i}c_{i}\chi(Z_{i}).

In a number of technical setups one can show that this integral is well defined, i.e. is independent of the presentation (
6.8.1
).

6.15 Remark
.

(1) The fact that the integral is well defined was proved by Groemer
[
13
]
for the class of functions on
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the level sets are finite unions of convex compact sets.
When
X
is a complex analytic manifold and the level sets of functions are complex analytic subvarieties this fact is due to Viro
[
35
]
.
For a real analytic manifold
X
and functions with level sets being subanalytic this fact was proven by Schapira
[
32
]
, while a more special situation was previously considered
by Khovanskii and Pukhlikov
[
20
]
.

(2) For the purposes of general conjectures
[
1
]
the relevant class of constructible functions consists of functions on a compact Alexandrov space
X
which are constant on
the strata of the Perelman-Petrunin stratification. In the present paper a more elementary situation will be sufficient: constant functions on topological surfaces and functions on a closed segment which
are constant in its interior. In the former case the integral with respect to the Euler characteristic is trivially well defined once we consider only constant functions.
The latter case is a special case either of the Khovanskii-Pukhlikov
[
20
]
or the Schapira
[
32
]
approaches, although it can be treated directly by elementary methods.

Proof.

Case 1.

. Hence
F=\alpha
, and
\int_{X}Fd\chi=\alpha
.

Case 2.
Let
\dim X=2
. By the Perelman stability theorem
[
25
]
(see also
[
18
]
) all
X_{i}
are homeomorphic to
X
. By Theorem
6.13
one has

F\equiv 1
. Hence
\int_{X}Fd\chi=\chi(X)=\alpha
.

Case 3.
Let
\dim X=1
.

Subcase 3a.
Assume that
\{X_{i}\}
are homeomorphic to the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. By Theorem
6.7
(2)
X
is a segment.
By Theorem
6.5
one has
F={1\hskip-2.5pt{\rm l}}_{\partial X}
. Clearly

\int_{X}Fd\chi=2=\chi(\mathbb{S}^{2})

as necessary.

Subcase 3b.
Assume that
\{X_{i}\}
are homeomorphic to the real projective plane
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2}
. By Theorem
6.7
(2)
X
is a segment.
By Theorem
6.8
the function
F
vanishes everywhere on
X
but one point. Hence

\int_{X}Fd\chi=1=\chi(\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{2})

as required.

Subcase 3c.
Assume that
\{X_{i}\}
are homeomorphic to the 2-torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Since by
[
17
]
and
[
38
]
torus cannot collapse to a segment,
X
is a circle.
By Theorem
6.7
(1) one has
F\equiv 0
. Then

\int_{X}Fd\chi=0=\chi(\mathbb{T}^{2}).

Subcase 3d.
Assume that
\{X_{i}\}
are homeomorphic to the Klein bottle
\mathbb{K}
. Then
X
could be both circle and segment.
If
X
is a circle then by Theorem
6.7
(1)
F\equiv 0
, and if
X
is a segment then by Theorem
6.11
one has
F=0
again. Hence in either case

\int_{X}Fd\chi=0=\chi(\mathbb{K}).

Q.E.D.
