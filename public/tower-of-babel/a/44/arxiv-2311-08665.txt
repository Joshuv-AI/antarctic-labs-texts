Friedrichs extensions for a class of singular discrete linear Hamiltonian systems
Guojing Ren, Guixin Xu

Abstract
. This paper is concerned with the characterizations of the Friedrichs extension for a class of singular discrete linear Hamiltonian systems. The existence of recessive solutions and the existence of the Friedrichs extension are proved under some conditions. The self-adjoint boundary conditions are obtained by applying the recessive solutions and then the characterization of the Friedrichs extension is obtained in terms of boundary conditions via linear independently recessive solutions.



Keywords
: Discrete Hamiltonian system; Friedrichs extension; Disconjugacy; Recessive solution.

2020
Mathematics Subject Classification
: 34B20, 39A70.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the following singular discrete linear Hamiltonian system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta u(t)=A(t)u(t+1)+B(t)v(t),\\
\Delta v(t)=(C(t)-\lambda W(t))u(t+1)-A^{*}(t)v(t),\quad t\in\mathcal{I},\end{array}\right.

where
\Delta
is the forward difference operator, i.e.,
\Delta u(t)=u(t+1)-u(t)
;

A(t)
is
n\times n
complex matrix,
B(t)
,
C(t)
and
W(t)
are
n\times n
Hermitian matrices satisfying

B(t)\geq 0,\quad W(t)\geq 0;

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is a spectral parameter; and
\mathcal{I}:=[0,\infty)\cap\mathbb{Z}=\{t\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
.
To ensure the existence and uniqueness of the solution of any
initial value problem for
(1.1)
, we always assume that

I_{n}-A(t)\;{\rm is\;invertible\;on\;}\mathcal{I},

where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
unit matrix.

Self-adjoint extension problems are most fundamental in
the study of spectral problems for differential operators.
There are two important theories available.
One is the Weyl-Titchmarsh theory, which was started with Weyl¡¯s work in 1910 and
it has been generalized to linear Hamiltonian differential systems
(cf.
[
2
,
11
,
18
,
33
]
and their references).
The other is the GKN theory, which was established by Glazman, Krein, and Naimark in 1950’s .
Based on these two theories, the self-adjoint extensions for linear differential
operators have been widely studied
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

Among all the self-adjoint extensions, there is a particular one which preserves
the same lower bound when the associated minimal operator is bounded from below.
This self-adjoint extension is known as the Friedrichs extension,
which was initially constructed by K. Friedrichs for a densely defined operator in 1930’s.
His work has been widely developed by many authors to singular differential operators using various approaches
(cf.
[
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
20
]
).
By using the existence of the principal solutions of linear Hamiltonian differential systems
[
21
]
,
a characterization of the Friedrichs extension of a class of singular Hamiltonian differential operators
was given in terms of principal solutions
[
35
]
.
Recently, the Friedrichs extension of a class of singular differential systems including non-symmetric cases
was characterized
[
34
]
.

Difference equations are usually regarded as the discretization of the corresponding differential equations.
It has been found that the maximal operator corresponding to a formally self-adjoint difference equation
is multi-valued, and the corresponding minimal operator is non-densely defined in general case
[
23
,
26
]
.
Not only that, this case can happen for general linear Hamilton differential systems
[
12
]
.
Therefore, the classical spectral theory for symmetric operators, i.e.,
densely defined and Hermitian single-valued operators, are not available in the studying
of the spectral properties of differential and difference equations in general case.

Due to the above reasons, some researchers began to focus on extending the theory of linear operators to
linear non-densely defined or multi-valued operators (which are called linear relations or linear subspaces),
and many good results have been obtained (cf.
[
8
,
10
,
28
]
and their references).
E. A. Coddington successfully extended the von Neumann self-adjoint
extension theory for symmetric operators to Hermitian subspaces
[
7
]
.
Y. Shi extended the classical GKN theory for symmetric operators to Hermitian subspaces
[
27
]
.
Based on the above, a complete characterization of all the self-adjoint extensions for a class of
discrete linear Hamiltonian systems are obtained in
terms of boundary conditions via linear independent square summable
solutions
[
24
]
.

The spectral properties of discrete linear Hamiltonian systems have been widely discussed
(cf.
[
1
,
5
,
9
,
22
,
25
]
and their references).
It is worth mentioning that
M. Bohner and his coauthors discussed the disconjugacy of discrete linear Hamiltonian systems
and proved the Reid Roundabout Theorem
[
5
, Theorem 2]
.
In
[
1
]
, some properties of recessive solutions of (1.1) were discussed.
The Friedrichs extension of semi-bounded second-order difference operators was discussed in
[
4
]
.
To the best of our knowlege, the existence of the recessive solutions of (1.1) has not be given and the
Friedrichs extension of the minimal subspace corresponding to (1.1) has not been established.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, the basic concepts and useful results on the linear relations and the sesquilinear forms
are recalled. In addition, several important difference equations related with (1.1) are listed at the end of this section.
In Section 3, the existence of the recessive solutions of (1.1) and the existence of the Friedrichs extension
of the minimal subspace generated by (1.1) are proved under some conditions, respectively.
In addition, a characterization of the recessive solutions of (1.1) is obtained.
In Section 4, a characterization of matrix
\Theta
is obtained by using the recessive solutions
and then a characterization of the Friedrichs extension is established in terms of boundary condition via the recessive solutions.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Linear relations

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a complex Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Let
T
and
S
be two linear relations (briefly, relations or named linear subspaces) in
\mathcal{X}^{2}:=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.
The domain and the range of
T
are defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(T):=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(T):=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T\}.

The adjoint of
T
and the sum of two relations are defined as

\displaystyle T^{*}:=\{(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}^{2}:\;\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle=\langle x^{\prime},y\rangle\;{\rm for\;all}\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T\},

\displaystyle T^{-1}:=\{(x^{\prime},x):(x,x^{\prime})\in T\},

\displaystyle T+S:=\{(x,x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime}):\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T,\;(x,x^{\prime\prime})\in S\}.

It has been shown that
T
is densely defined if and only if
T^{*}

is single-valued
[
23
, Theorem 3.1]
.

T
is said to be
Hermitian
if
T\subset T^{*}
.

T
is said to be
symmetric
if
T\subset T^{*}
and
\mathcal{D}(T)
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
.

T
is said to be
self-adjoint
if
T=T^{*}
.

Denote

T(x)=\{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}:\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T\}.

It is clear that
T(0)=\{0\}
if and only if there exists
a unique linear operator
A_{T}
from
\mathcal{D}(T)
into
\mathcal{X}

such that its graph is equal to
T
, i.e.,
G(A_{T})=T
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. The subspace
\mathcal{R}(T-\lambda I))^{\bot}
and the number

d_{\lambda}(T):=\dim\mathcal{R}(T-\lambda I)^{\bot}
are called the
deficiency
space
and
deficiency index
of
T
with
\lambda
, respectively.
By
\overline{T}
denote the closure of
T
in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
.
It can be easily verified that
d_{\lambda}(T)=d_{\lambda}(\overline{T})
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Let
T
be a relation in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
. The set

\Gamma(T):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\exists\,c(\lambda)>0\;{\rm s.t.}\;\|x^{\prime}-\lambda x\|\geq c(\lambda)\|x\|,\forall(x,x^{\prime})\in T\}

is called the regularity domain of
T
.
It has been shown by
[
27
, Theorem 2.3]
that the deficiency index
d_{\lambda}(T)

is constant in each connected subset of
\Gamma(T)
.
If
T
is Hermitian, then
d_{\lambda}(T)
is constant in the upper and lower half-planes.
Denote

d_{\pm}(T):=d_{\pm i}(T)

for an Hermitian linear relation
T
,
and call
d_{\pm}(T)
the positive and negative deficiency indices of
T
, respectively.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
7
, Theorem 15]

Let
T
be a closed Hermitian subspace in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
. Then
T
has self-adjoint extensions if and only if

d_{+}(T)=d_{-}(T)
.

Next, we introduce a form on
\mathcal{X}^{2}\times\mathcal{X}^{2}
by

\displaystyle[(x,x^{\prime}):(y,y^{\prime})]:=\langle x^{\prime},y\rangle-\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle,\quad(x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}^{2}.

It can be easily verified that
[:]
is a conjugate bilinear and
skew-Hermitian map from
\mathcal{X}^{2}\times\mathcal{X}^{2}
into
\mathbb{C}
.
Let
T
be a closed Hermitian subspace in
X^{2}
and satisfy

d_{+}(T)=d_{-}(T)=d
. A set
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
is called a GKN-set
for the pairs of subspaces
\{T,T^{*}\}
if it satisfies

(1)

\beta_{j}\in T^{*}
,
1\leq j\leq d
;

(2)

\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{d}
are linearly independent in

T^{*}
(modulo
T
);

(3)

[\beta_{j}:\beta_{k}]=0
,
1\leq j,k\leq d
.

Lemma 2.2
.

[
27
, Theorem 4.7]

Let
T
be a closed Hermitian subspace in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
and satisfy

d_{+}(T)=d_{-}(T)=d
. A subspace
T_{1}
in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
is a self-adjoint extension of
T
if and
only if there exists a GKN-set
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
for
\{T,T^{*}\}

such that
T_{1}
is determined by

\displaystyle T_{1}=\{\gamma\in T^{*}:\;[\gamma:\beta_{j}]=0,\,1\leq j\leq d\}.

A subspace
T
is said to be bounded from below if there exists a number
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.1)

\langle x^{\prime},x\rangle\geq\gamma\|x\|^{2},\quad\forall\;(x,x^{\prime})\in T.

The lower bound of
T
is the largest number
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
for which
(
2.1
) holds.
It can be easily verified that
T
is Hermitian if it is bounded from below.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
3
, Proposition 1.4.6]

Let
T
be an Hermitian subspace and be bounded from below with lower bound
\gamma
. Then

\mathbb{C}\setminus[\gamma,\infty)\subset\Gamma(T)
, and the deficiency index
d_{\lambda}(T)
is constant for all

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[\gamma,\infty)
.

2.2.
Sesquilinear forms

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a complex Hilbert space and let
\mathcal{D}
be a
linear subspace (not necessarily closed or dense) of
\mathcal{X}
.
A sesquilinear form
{\bf t}[\cdot,\cdot]
is a complex-valued mapping
from
\mathcal{D}\times\mathcal{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle{\bf t}[x,ay+bz]=\bar{a}\;{\bf t}[x,y]+\bar{b}\;{\bf t}[x,z],

\displaystyle{\bf t}[ay+bz,x]=a\;{\bf t}[y,x]+b\;{\bf t}[z,x],

for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{D}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. The set
\mathcal{D}({\bf t})=\mathcal{D}
is called the domain of
{\bf t}
.
Define the quadratic form
{\bf t}[\cdot]
associated with
\bf t[\cdot,\cdot]
as
{\bf t}[x]:={\bf t}[x,x]

with the same domain
\mathcal{D}
.

{\bf t}
is said to be sectorial with vertex at the origin and
semiangle
\alpha
,
\alpha\in[0,\pi/2)
, if

{\bf t_{i}}[x]\leq\tan\alpha{\bf t_{r}}[x],\quad x\in\mathcal{D}({\bf t})=\mathcal{D}({\bf t_{i}})=\mathcal{D}({\bf t_{r}}),

where
{\bf t_{r}}
and
{\bf t_{i}}
stand for the real and imaginary parts of
{\bf t}
.

{\bf t}
is said to be Hermitian if
{\bf t}[x]
is real for all
x\in\mathcal{D}({\bf t})
.
It is clear that
{\bf t}
is sectorial if it is Hermitian.

A sequence
\{x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{D}(\bf t)
is said to be
{\bf t}
-convergent to
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
in symbol

x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf t}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;x,

if
{x_{n}}\to x
in
\mathcal{X}
and
{\bf t}[x_{n}-x_{m}]\to 0
as
n,m\to\infty
.
An Hermitian sesquilinear form
{\bf t}
is said to be closed if
\{x_{n}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf t}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;x
implies that

x\in\mathcal{D}(\bf t)
and
{\bf t}[x_{n}-x]\to 0
;
and
{\bf t}
is said to be closable if it has a closed extension.

The following is a direct consequence of
[
15
, Chapter VI, Theorem 1.17]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

An Hermitian sesquilinear form
{\bf t}
is closable if and only if
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf t}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;0

implies
{\bf t}[x_{n}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
When this condition is satisfied,
{\bf t}
in
\mathcal{X}
has
the closure (the smallest closed extension)
{\bf\bar{t}}
which defined in the following way.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}({\bf\bar{t}}):=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:\exists\;\{x_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{D}({\bf t})\;{\rm s.t.}\;x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf t}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;x\right\},

\displaystyle{\bf\bar{t}}[x_{1},x_{2}]:=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\bf t}[x_{1n},x_{2n}],\quad\forall\;x_{jn}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf t}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;x_{j},\quad j=1,2.

The following result is a direct consequence of
[
10
, Lemma 4.2]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
T
be a Hermitian relation in
\mathcal{X}^{2}
. The form
{\bf t}_{T}
generated by
T
as the following

{\bf t}_{T}[x_{1},x_{2}]:=\langle x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}\rangle,\quad\forall\;(x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\in T,\quad j=1,2,

with
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{t})=\mathcal{D}(T)
is well-defined, Hermitian and closable.

The following result is a direct consequence of
[
10
, Proposition 2.8, Theorem 4.3]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
{\bf t}
be a closed and Hermitian form in
\mathcal{X}
with lower bound
\gamma>0
.
Then there exists a unique self-adjoint linear relation
T_{\bf t}
with the same lower bound
\gamma>0
such that

(2.2)

\mathcal{D}(T_{\bf t})\subset\mathcal{D}(\bf t)

and for every
(x,x^{\prime})\in T_{\bf t}
and
y\in\mathcal{D}(\bf t)
one has

(2.3)

{\bf t}[x,y]=\langle x^{\prime},y\rangle.

Conversely, for every self-adjoint relation
T_{\bf t}
with the lower bound
\gamma>0
, there exists a unique
closed and Hermitian form
{\bf t}
in
\mathcal{X}
such that
(\ref{form and relation 1})
and
(\ref{form and relation 2})
are satisfied.

2.3.
Several difference equations related to (1.1)

System (1.1) contains the following two important models. One is the
formally self-adjoint scalar difference equation with complex coefficients

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\Delta^{j}(p_{j}\nabla^{j}z(t))+i\sum_{k=1}^{n}[(-1)^{k+1}\Delta^{k}(q_{k}z(t))+q_{k}\nabla^{k}z(t)]=\lambda w(t)z(t),

where
p_{j}(t)
and
q_{k}(t)
are all real-valued,
p_{n}(t)\neq 0
on
\mathcal{I}
, and
i=\sqrt{-1}
.
In fact, by letting

u=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n})^{T}
and
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})^{T}
with

\displaystyle u_{j}(t)=\Delta^{j-1}z(t-j),

\displaystyle v_{j}(t)=\sum_{k=j}^{n}(-1)^{k+j}\Delta^{k-j}(p_{k}(t)\nabla^{k}z(t))+i\sum_{k=j}^{n}(-1)^{k+1}\Delta^{k-j}(q_{k}(t)z(t)),

for
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, we can convert (
2.4
) into (1.1) with

\displaystyle A(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{n-1}\\
iq_{n}(t)/p_{n}(t)&0\end{pmatrix},\quad C(t)=\begin{pmatrix}p_{0}(t)+q_{n}(t)/p_{n}(t)&\alpha(t)\\
\alpha^{*}(t),&\beta(t)\end{pmatrix},

and

\displaystyle B(t)={\rm diag}\{0,\ldots,0,p_{n}^{-1}(t)\},

\displaystyle\alpha(t)=i(q_{n-1}(t),q_{n-2}(t),\ldots,q_{1}(t))^{T},

\displaystyle\beta(t)={\rm diag}\{p_{1}(t),p_{2}(t),\ldots,p_{n-1}(t)\},

\displaystyle W(t)={\rm diag}\{w(t),0,\ldots,0\}.

The other is the second-order vector Sturm-Liouville difference equation

(2.5)

-\nabla(P(t)\Delta u(t))+Q(t)u(t)=\lambda W(t)u(t),\quad t\in\mathcal{I},

where
P(t)
,
Q(t)
and
W(t)
are
n\times n
Hermitian matrices,

W(t)\geq 0
and
P(t)>0
. In fact, by letting
v(t)=P(t)\Delta u(t)
, we can convert (
2.5
)
into the following form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Delta u(t)=P^{-1}(t)v(t),\\
\Delta v(t)=(Q(t+1)-\lambda W(t+1))u(t+1),\end{array}\right.\quad t\in\mathcal{I}.

The general singular discrete linear Hamiltonian system is in the form

(2.7)

\displaystyle J\Delta y(t)=(\tilde{P}(t)+\lambda\tilde{W}(t))R(y)(t),\quad t\in\mathcal{I},

where
\tilde{W}(t)\geq 0
and
\tilde{P}(t)
are
2n\times 2n
Hermitian matrices;

J
is the
2n\times 2n
canonical symplectic matrix, that is,

J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{array}\right),

y(t)=(u^{T}(t),v^{T}(t))^{T}
with
u(t),v(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

and
R(y)(t)
is the partial right shift operator

R(y)(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u(t+1)\\
v(t)\\
\end{array}\right).

System (1.1) is a special case of (
2.7
) with

\tilde{W}(t)={\rm diag}\{W(t),0\}.

Since
I_{n}-A(t)
is invertible, system (1.1) is equivalent to the following system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}u(t+1)=\tilde{A}(t)u(t)+\tilde{A}(t)B(t)v(t),\\
v(t+1)=\tilde{C}(t,\lambda)\tilde{A}(t)u(t)+D(t,\lambda)v(t),\quad t\in\mathcal{I},\end{array}\right.

where

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}&\tilde{A}(t)=(I_{n}-A(t))^{-1},\quad\tilde{C}(t,\lambda)=C(t)-\lambda W(t),\\
&D(t,\lambda)=\tilde{C}(t,\lambda)\tilde{A}(t)B(t)+I_{n}-A^{*}(t).\end{array}

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, it can be easily verified that

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{A}(t)&\tilde{A}(t)B(t)\\
\tilde{C}(t,\lambda)\tilde{A}(t)&D(t,\lambda)\\
\end{array}\right)^{*}J\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\tilde{A}(t)&\tilde{A}(t)B(t)\\
\tilde{C}(t,\lambda)\tilde{A}(t)&D(t,\lambda)\\
\end{array}\right)\equiv J.

This implies that (
2.3
), as well as (1.1), is a symplectic system when
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.
The existence of the recessive solutions and the Friedrichs extension

In the first subsection, some variational properties of the solutions of
(1.1)
are recalled,
and then the existence of the recessive solutions of
(\ref{HS1})
is proved.
In the second subsection, by introducing a quadratic form associated with (1.1)

3.1.
Existence of the recessive solutions

Denote

\displaystyle l(\mathcal{I}):=\{y:\;y=\{y(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{I}},\;y(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}\}.

For any two
y_{1},y_{2}\in l(\mathcal{I})
, denote

(y_{1},y_{2})(t):=y_{2}^{*}(t)Jy_{1}(t),

where
J
is the canonical symplectic matrix.
If the limit

\lim_{t\to\infty}(y_{1},y_{2})(t)

exists and is finite, then
its limit is denoted by
(y_{1},y_{2})(\infty)
.
The natural difference operator corresponding to system
(1.1)
is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(y)(t)

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}(I_{n}-A^{*}(t))v(t)-v(t+1)+C(t)u(t+1)\\
(I_{n}-A(t))u(t+1)-u(t)-B(t)v(t)\end{array}\right),\quad y\in l(\mathcal{I}).

Lemma 3.1
.

[
25
, Lemma 2.1]

For any two
y_{1},y_{2}\in l(\mathcal{I})
and any
s,k\in\mathcal{I}
with
s<k
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=s}^{k}[R(y_{2})^{*}(t)\mathcal{L}(y_{1})(t)-\mathcal{L}(y_{2})^{*}(t)R(y_{1})(t)]=(y_{1},y_{2})(k+1)-(y_{1},y_{2})(s).

By small letters
y=(u;v)

we denote a vector-valued solution of (1.1) and by capital letter
Y=(U;V)
denote a
2n\times n
matrix-valued solution of (1.1).
Let
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
be two solutions of (1.1). It follows from
Lemma
3.1
that

\displaystyle(y_{1},y_{2})(t)\equiv(y_{1},y_{2})(0)=c,\quad t\in\mathcal{I}.

where
c
is a constant. Further, we call
y
a
prepared (or conjoined) solution
if

(y,y)(t)\equiv 0.

Similarly,
Y(t)=(U(t);V(t))
is called a
prepared (or conjoined) solution
if

Y^{*}(t)JY(t)\equiv 0.

It follows from Lemma
3.1
that
Y(t)
is a prepared solution of (1.1) if and only if

U^{*}(t)V(t)
is Hermitian for some
t\in\mathcal{I}
.

As it has been shown in Section 2.3, system (1.1) can be converted into (
2.3
), which is a symplectic system when
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
So, the results in
[
1
, Section 3]
can be applied to system (1.1) when
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
\tilde{A}(t)=(I_{n}-A(t))^{-1}
in (
2.3
).

Lemma 3.2
.

[
1
, Theorems 3.32, 3.33]

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, and
Y_{0}=(U_{0};V_{0})
be a prepared solution of
(1.1)
such that

U_{0}(t)
is invertible for
t\geq t_{1}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle D(t)=U_{0}^{-1}(t+1)\tilde{A}(t)B(t)(U_{0}^{-1})^{*}(t),\quad t\geq t_{1}

is Hermitian, and consequently

\displaystyle S_{0}(t_{1}):=0,\quad S_{0}(t):=\sum_{s=t_{1}}^{t-1}U_{0}^{-1}(s+1)\tilde{A}(s)B(s)(U_{0}^{-1})^{*}(s),\quad t\geq t_{1}+1

is Hermitian for
t\geq t_{1}
.
Let
Y=(U;V)
be another solution of
(1.1)
. Then it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}U(t)=U_{0}(t)(P+S_{0}(t)Q),&\\
