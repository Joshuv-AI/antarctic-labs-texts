Holonomy of complex projective structures on surfaces with prescribed branch data
Thomas Le Fils

Abstract
. We characterize the representations of the fundamental group of a closed surface to PSL 2 ​ ( ℂ ) \mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}) that arise as the holonomy of a branched complex projective structure with fixed branch divisor. In particular, we compute the holonomies of the spherical metrics with prescribed integral conical angles and the holonomies of affine structures with fixed conical angles on closed surfaces.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Holonomy of branched projective structures

For each
g\geqslant 0
and
n\geqslant 0
, we fix
\Sigma_{g,n}
a connected compact oriented surface of genus
g
with
n
boundary components and
\Gamma_{g,n}
a fundamental group of
\Sigma_{g,n}
. We will be most interested in the case
n=0
and we let
\Gamma=\Gamma_{g,0}
and
\Sigma=\Sigma_{g,0}
.

A
branched projective structure
on
\Sigma
is the datum of a branched atlas with values in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and transition maps in
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. By branched we mean that our charts have the local form
z\mapsto z^{n+1}
for some integer
n\geqslant 0
, called the
order
of the point corresponding to
0
in this chart. A point of order at least
1
is called a
branch point
. The analytic continuation of one of the charts in the universal cover of
\Sigma
yields a
developing map
that is
\rho
-equivariant for a unique homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
called the
holonomy
of the projective structure. The conjugacy class of this holonomy does not depend on the choice of the chart and thus gives a map defined on the set
\mathcal{BP}
of branched projective structures on
\Sigma

\mathrm{Hol}:\mathcal{BP}\to\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}).

The map
\mathrm{Hol}
and in particular its restriction
\mathrm{Hol}_{|\mathcal{P}}
to the set
\mathcal{P}
of projective structures without any branch point has been extensively studied. For example, it is known that
\mathrm{Hol}_{|\mathcal{P}}
is a local homeomorphism but not a covering map, see
[
Hej75
]
. It is also known since Poincaré
[
Poi84
]
, see also
[
LMP09
]
, that the restriction of
\mathrm{Hol}
to the subset of
\mathcal{P}
of projective structures with a fixed underlying complex structure is injective.
A celebrated theorem of Gallo, Kapovich and Marden
[
GKM00
]
characterizes the image of
\mathrm{Hol}_{|\mathcal{P}}
, see also
[
LF19
]
.

Gallo Kapovich Marden
.

Suppose
g\geqslant 2
.
The image of
\mathcal{P}
by
\mathrm{Hol}
is the set of nonelementary representations that lift to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. The image of the set of projective structures with a single branch point, of order
1
, is the set of nonelementary representations that do not lift to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

In this article, we adress a problem raised in
[
GKM00
, Problem 12.6.1]
: “make precise and optimize the connection between branching divisor and monodromy”.
The set
\mathcal{BP}
is naturally stratified by the sets
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
of branched projective structure with
k
branch points of orders
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
.
The aim of this article is thus to characterize the image of the strata
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
by
\mathrm{Hol}
.

Recall that an elementary representation is a representation
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
yielding an action with a finite orbit on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
or on
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. In the first case we say that
\rho
is
spherical
and we will assume without loss of generality up to conjugating
\rho
, that it takes its values in
\mathrm{PSU}_{2}\simeq\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
. In the second case, if
\rho(\Gamma)
fixes a point in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
we say that
\rho
is
affine
and if it moreover fixes a horosphere we say that
\rho
is
Euclidean
. We will assume without loss of generality that this point is
\infty\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
and thus that
\rho
takes its values in
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})=\{az+b\mid a\in\mathbb{C}^{*},b\in\mathbb{C}\}
. If
\rho(\Gamma)
fixes globally a pair of points in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, we say that
\rho
is
dihedral
and we will assume that this pair is
\{0,\infty\}
. We refer to
[
Rat06
, Chapter 5]
for more information on elementary subgroups of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

We wish to
geometrize
a given
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
, that is to find a projective structure
X\in\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
such that
\rho=\mathrm{Hol}(X)
. There are some obstructions to being the holonomy of such a
X
that we will now explicit. Indeed suppose that
\rho=\mathrm{Hol}(X)
.
It is proven in
[
GKM00
]
that
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if
\sum_{i}n_{i}
is even.

Obstruction 1
.

The sum
\sum_{i}n_{i}
must be even if
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and odd otherwise.

There exist some surgeries on projective structures, such as bubbling (see
5.7
), that allow us to add an even number of branch points to some projective structures. In particular we will see in
Section

6.5
that it is a consequence of the theorem of Gallo, Kapovich and Marden cited above that a nonelementary representation of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is in
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if
\sum_{i}n_{i}
has the required parity.
Thus we now focus on elementary representations.
As a consequence of the Gauss-Bonnet formula, we have another obstruction on
\sum_{i}n_{i}
when
\rho
is elementary.

Obstruction 2
.

If
\rho
is elementary, then we must have
\sum_{i}n_{i}\geqslant 2g-2
.
If moreover
\rho
is spherical, then
\sum_{i}n_{i}\geqslant 2g-1
.

The image of
\rho
might be a finite group. In this case the developing map induces a branched cover
S\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
defined on the cover
S
of
\Sigma
associated with the subgroup
\ker\rho
of
\Gamma
. The Riemann-Hurwitz formula then gives another obstruction.

Obstruction 3
.

If the image of
\rho
is a finite group of order
n
, then

n(\chi(\Sigma)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i})\geqslant 2(\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}n_{i}+1).

Let us define an
affine structure
on
\Sigma
as a branched projective structure on
\Sigma
with affine holonomy, such that the image of its developing map does not contain
\infty\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.
Adapting an argument of Kapovich in
[
Kap20
]
, we will see in
6.18
that if
\rho
is affine, then
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2
if and only if
X
is an affine structure.
If moreover
\rho
is Euclidean, then we may define the volume
\mathrm{Vol}(\rho)
of
\rho
, see
Section

4.2
. The volume of
\rho
must be positive, as we shall see in
Section

6.3.2
.

Obstruction 4
.

If
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2
and
\rho
is Euclidean, then
\mathrm{Vol}(\rho)>0
.

Haupt
[
Hau20
]
observed that there is another obstruction to being the holonomy of a
translation surface
, that is an affine structure with holonomy in the group of translations
\mathbb{C}
. Indeed if
g\geqslant 2
and
\chi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})
is the holonomy of a translation surface such that
\Lambda=\chi(\Gamma)
is a lattice, then
d=\mathrm{Vol}(\chi)/\mathrm{Area}(\Lambda)
must be at least
2
because the developing map induces a branched cover
\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
of degree
d
. This obstruction was generalized by
[
CDF15
]
for translation surfaces with prescribed singularities, where the authors observed that
d
must satisfy
d\geqslant\max_{i}n_{i}+1
and asked if these were the only obstructions to being the holonomy of a translation surface with prescribed singularities. This latter question was answered in the positive in
[
LF20
,
BJJP20
]
.
The
linear part
of an Euclidean representation
\rho
is the representation
\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
, where
\mathrm{Li}:\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
is defined by
az+b\mapsto a
. If the linear part of
\rho
takes its values in a finite group, then the cover
S
of
\Sigma
associated with the subgroup
\ker\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
of
\Gamma
inherits a translation surface structure. Therefore the obstruction of Haupt gives the following.

Obstruction 5
.

If
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2
and
\rho
is Euclidean such that the image of its linear part is a finite group of order
n
and the group
\Lambda=\{z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\mid z+z_{0}\in\rho(\Gamma)\}
is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
, then we must have

n\mathrm{Vol}(\rho)\geqslant(\max_{i}n_{i}+1)\mathrm{Area}(\mathbb{C}/\Lambda).

As we will see,
\Lambda
can be a lattice only if
n\in\{1,2,3,4,6\}
.

If
\rho
is dihedral there is an obstruction that only occurs in genus
2
, when
\sum_{i}n_{i}
is minimal: that is when
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2
.
This new obstruction comes from the observation that every such structure is the exponential of a half-translation structure. We observe thanks to
5
that a half-translation structure in genus
2
with a single branch point is in fact a translation surface structure, see
6.26
. Therefore we show that there is no branched projective structure with holonomy
\rho
and a single branch point in genus
2
.

Obstruction 6
.

Suppose
g=2
.
If
\rho
is a dihedral representation that is not affine and that lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
, then
X
cannot have a single branch point of order
2
.

Our main result is that these are the only obstructions to being in
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
. We have
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if and only if
\rho
satisfies the conditions of the 6 obstructions above.

In particular for a given
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
we can compute the number
d(\rho)
defined as the minimal value of
\sum_{i}n_{i}
such that there exists a branched projective structure in
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
with holonomy
\rho
.

Corollary 1.2
.

The image of the function
d:\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\to\mathbb{Z}
is the set
\{0,1,2g-2,2g-1,2g,2g+2\}
and we explicit
d
, see
Figure

1
at the end of this introduction.

Our proof of
1.1
consists in the case-by-case study of the possible
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
such that
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
, for each form that
\rho(\Gamma)
can take. In some of these cases we get back to notions that were already studied in the literature.
For example if
\rho(\Gamma)\subset\mathrm{PSU}_{2}
, then we are characterizing the holonomies of conical spherical metrics on
\Sigma
.
These results in some special cases that we now explicit have their own interest and form the pieces of our proof of
1.1
.

1.1.1.
Spherical structures.

Let us first focus on spherical representations.
1.1
consists in this case in computing the holonomies of spherical metrics with conical singularities of integral angles. For more information on spherical metrics with conical singularities we refer to
[
Ere19
,
MP19
]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))
. The representation
\rho
is the holonomy of a spherical metric with conical singularities of angles
2\pi(n_{1}+1),\ldots,2\pi(n_{k}+1)
if and only if the following conditions are met.

(1)

\sum_{i}n_{i}
is even if
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SU}_{2}
and odd otherwise.

(2)

\sum_{i}n_{i}\geqslant 2g-1
.

(3)

If the image of
\rho
is finite of order
n\geqslant 1
, then

n(\chi(\Sigma)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i})\geqslant 2(\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}n_{i}+1).

Observe that a branched projective structure with trivial holonomy is just a branched cover of the sphere.

Proposition 1.4
.

There exists a branched cover
\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
with branch points of order
1\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant n_{k}
if and only if
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2(g+d-1)
for an integer
d\geqslant 1
such that
1\leqslant n_{i}\leqslant d-1
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
.

Note that our convention differs in this setting from the usual one: by a branch point of order
n
we mean that the branched cover is locally
z\mapsto z^{n+1}
while this is often called a branch point of order
n+1
.
While the present article was being written, an independent proof of
Proposition 1.4 appeared in the work of Chenakkod, Faraco and Gupta

[
CFG21
, Corollary D]
. Their proof, as well as ours, consists in showing directly
that some branched data are realizable. We do it by following the
techniques of
[
EKS84
]
, while their proof is more geometric.
The case
g=0
was also proven, with different methods, independently
by Kapovich and Tomasini in
[
Kap17
,
Tom15
]
.

1.1.2.
Affine holonomy.

If we consider affine representations and
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2
then we compute the holonomies of affine structures with prescribed singularities.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
1\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant n_{k}
be such that
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2
. Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
.

(1)

If
\rho
is not Euclidean then
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
.

(2)

If
\rho
is Euclidean, then
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if and only if both
\mathrm{Vol}(\rho)>0

and
\rho
satisfies the condition of
5
if the image of its linear part is finite and has order in
\{1,2,3,4,6\}
.

This answers a question raised in
[
CFG21
, Question 1.3]
. In fact
1.5
is due to a large extent to Ghazouani
[
Gha18
]
, where he studies the action of the mapping class group on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
and characterizes the representations of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
that are the holonomy of an affine structure.
Ghazouani does not address the problem of characterizing the representations that are holonomies of affine structures with prescribed branch points.
Let us observe that
1.5
gives a new proof of the fact that the strata of quadratic differentials
\mathcal{Q}(2n_{1},\ldots,2n_{k})
are nonempty except if
g=1
and
k=0
, or
g=2
and
k=1
, see
6.28
. This was proven by Masur and Smilie in
[
MS93
]
, where they study the nonemptiness of all the strata of quadratic differentials. See also
[
Lan08
]
for another proof and the study of the connectedness of these strata.

In the case where
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2d
for
d\geqslant g
, we will see that every
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
that is not spherical is in
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
.

1.2.
Action of the mapping class group on some representation spaces

We will prove a Ehresmann-Thurston principle, following an argument of Kapovich
[
Kap20
, Theorem 2.7]
.

Proposition 1.6
.

The sets
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
are open.

The mapping class group
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)=\pi_{0}(\mathrm{Homeo}_{+}(\Sigma))
acts on the space of conjugacy classes of representations
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and this action preserves the sets
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
. This group identifies with the positive outer automorphisms of
\Gamma
,
\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Gamma)=\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)/\mathrm{Inn}(\Gamma)
and acts on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
by precomposition (see
Section

2
) where
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
is the index
2
subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
of automorphisms induced by orientation preserving homeomorphisms.
In order to geometrize a representation with prescribed branch data, it suffices by
1.6
to geometrize any element of the closure of its orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
. Therefore our proof of
1.1
is based on the description of the closure of the orbits of this action. Indeed this description will leave us with a small number of explicit representations to geometrize.

The study of the mapping class group action on character varieties
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G
, where
G
is a group, was pioneered by Goldman and is a wide and active research area. We refer to
[
Gol03
]
for an introduction to this subject and
[
Can15
]
and references therein for more information on this domain of research.
In particular Goldman proved in
[
Gol97
]
that the action of the mapping class group on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SU}_{2})/\mathrm{SU}_{2}
is ergodic. While this result provides a description of the closure of almost every orbit, it does not allow us to understand the closure of a given orbit.
The description of the closure of the orbit of every representation in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SU}_{2})/\mathrm{SU}_{2}
with dense image was achieved by Previte and Xia in
[
PX00
,
PX02
]
. A consequence of their work is that the orbit of a representation in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))
with image a dense subgroup of
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
is dense in its connected component of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))/\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
, see
Lemma

4.4
. We study the other orbits of representations in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))
and give a complete description of their closures. This somehow complements the main result of
[
PX02
]
in the case of closed surfaces.
Let
\epsilon:\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{O_{2}}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
be the quotient homomorphism. Goldman showed in
[
Gol88
]
that
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
endowed with the pointwise convergence topology has two connected components. Representations in one of these connected components lift to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
while the others do not. Let
\mathrm{sw}:\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))\to\{0,1\}
be the map that assigns
0
to the representations that lift to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
and
1
to the others. The proper closed subgroups of
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
are isomorphic to
\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathrm{O}(2)
, the cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, the dihedral groups
\mathrm{D}_{n}
of order
2n
, the alternating groups
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
and
\mathfrak{A}_{5}
and the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
. Let us start with
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))
with infinite image. After conjugating
\rho
, we may assume that
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.

Proposition 1.7
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})))
with infinite image. The closure of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\cdot\rho
is the set of representations
\rho_{\infty}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
such that
\epsilon\circ\rho_{\infty}
has the same image as
\epsilon\circ\rho
and
\mathrm{sw}(\rho_{\infty})=\mathrm{sw}(\rho)
.

We also describe the orbits of representations with finite image in
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

Proposition 1.8
.

Suppose

.
The action

that if

2.1.
Fundamental groups of surfaces

For each
g,n\geqslant 0
, we fix
\Sigma_{g,n}
a compact oriented surface of genus
g
with
n
boundary components. We also choose a basepoint in each of these surfaces and thus a fundamental group
\Gamma_{g,n}
. Let us fix standard generators
a_{1},\ldots,b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
for
\Gamma_{g,n}
, as in
Figure

2
.

Figure 2
.
Standard generators of
\Gamma_{2,1}
.

These standard generators yield the following presentation of
\Gamma_{g,n}

\Gamma_{g,n}=\langle a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}\mid\prod_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]=c_{1}\ldots c_{n}\rangle.

In particular for
n=0
, we have

\Gamma=\langle a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{g},b_{g}\mid\prod_{i=1}^{g}[a_{i},b_{i}]=\mathrm{id}\rangle.

Recall that every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
is induced by a continuous map

preserving the basepoint of
\Gamma
. We denote by
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
the index
2
subgroup of
\mathrm{Aut}(\Gamma)
induced by the
f
preserving the orientation of
\Sigma
.
Let us denote by
\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(\Sigma)

the connected component of the identity of
\mathrm{Homeo}_{+}(\Sigma)
, the set of positive homeomorphisms of
\Sigma
, equipped with the compact-open topology. It is the set of homeomorphisms of
\Sigma
isotopic to the identity, see
[
FM01
, Chapter 2]
.
The mapping class group is the group of
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{Homeo}_{+}(\Sigma)/\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(\Sigma)
of isotopy class of positive homeomorphisms of
\Sigma
.
Every
f\in\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
induces an outer automorphism of
\Gamma
by the action on
\Gamma
of one of its representatives that fixes the basepoint of
\Gamma
. The theorem of Dehn, Nielsen and Baer, see
[
FM01
, Chapter 6]
, states that this homomorphism
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)\to\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Sigma)
is an isomorphism, where
\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Gamma)=\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)/\mathrm{Inn}(\Gamma)
and
\mathrm{Inn}(\Gamma)
denotes the set of inner automorphisms of
\Gamma
.

2.2.
Action on representation spaces

Let
G
be a group.
The group
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
acts on the set of representations
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
by precomposition. The group
G
also acts by conjugation on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
and these two actions commute. Therefore
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times G
acts on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
. The group
\mathrm{Inn}(\Gamma)
of inner automorphisms of
\Gamma
acts trivially on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G
. Hence we have an action of
\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Gamma)
on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G
.
Therefore the group
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
acts on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G
. Note that we may restrict both the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
and the action of
\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of (conjugacy classes) of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
.

Remark 2.1
.

Two surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
are in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times G
if and only if they are in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
.

In our study of the
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
action on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
, we will often consider the restriction of a homomorphism
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
such that
\rho
kills
c=\prod_{i=1}^{k}[a_{i},b_{i}]
to
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g-k},G)
. The following lemma ensures that we may then study the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma_{g-k})
on this restriction to understand the orbit of
\rho
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
and
c
be a simple separating curve in
\Sigma
such that
\rho(c)=\mathrm{id}
.
The curve
c
induces a splitting
\Sigma=\Sigma_{h}\sharp\Sigma_{l}
, where
h+l=g
. For every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma_{h})
, the representation that we denote by
\sigma\cdot\rho
satisfying:

\sigma\cdot\rho(\gamma)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho(\sigma\cdot\gamma)&\mbox{if }\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{h})\\
\rho(\gamma)&\mbox{if }\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{l})\end{array}\right.

lies in
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\cdot\rho
.

Proof.

Let us fix such a curve
c
.
The set
\mathcal{A}
of
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma_{h})
satisfying this property for every
\rho
such that
\rho(c)=\mathrm{id}
is stable by multiplication. It contains the automorphisms induced by Dehn twists along nonseparating simple closed curves contained in
\Sigma_{h}
. The set
\mathcal{A}
also contains the inner automorphisms of
\Gamma_{h}
: if
\delta
is a nonseparating simple closed curve in
\Sigma_{h}
, then the composition of a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
\delta
and of a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
c^{\pm 1}\delta
conjugates
\rho(\gamma)
with
\rho(\delta)
for
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{h})
and leaves
\rho(\gamma)
unchanged for
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{l})
.
Since the Dehn twists along nonseparating simple closed curves generate
\mathrm{Out}^{+}(\Gamma_{h})
, see
[
FM01
, Chapter 4]
, we have
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma_{h})
.
∎

Observe that if
G
is an abelian group, then any homomorphism
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
factors through a homomorphim in
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathrm{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z}),G)
since the abelianization of
\Gamma
is
\mathrm{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
. The group
\mathrm{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})
is the free abelian group on the generators
[a_{i}],[b_{i}]
. Therefore, we may identify
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
with
G^{2g}
, by the map
\rho\mapsto(\rho(a_{1}),\ldots,\rho(b_{g}))
.
The group
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
acts on
\mathrm{H}_{1}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
and it is well known that this action is made by the full symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
, see
[
FM01
, Chapter 6]
. The action of
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)\simeq\mathrm{Out^{+}(\Gamma)}
on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\simeq G^{2g}
is thus by
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
that acts on
G^{2g}
seen as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module.

3.
Mapping class group orbits on representation spaces into some finite groups

In this section we study the orbits of the
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
action on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
for some finite groups
G
.
Observe that the projection
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\to\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G
induces a bijection between the
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
orbits of surjective homomorphisms and the
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
orbits of conjugacy class of surjective homomorphisms by
2.1
.
Let us recall the classification of these orbits when the genus is large enough, from
[
DT06
, Theorem 6.23]
.
There is a natural map
\Phi:\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\to\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
defined as follows. Every
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
induces a map in homology
\rho_{\star}:\mathrm{H}_{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
and we define
\Phi(\rho)
to be
\rho_{\star}([\Sigma])\in\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
, where
[\Sigma]\in\mathrm{H}_{2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{Z})=\mathrm{H_{2}}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
is the fundamental class of
\Sigma
. This map is invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
, since every positive automorphism of
\Gamma
maps
[\Sigma]
to itself.

Theorem 3.1

(Dunfield-Thurston)
.

Let
G
be a finite group.
There exists
g_{0}\geqslant 0
such that the natural map from the orbits of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g},G)
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
to
\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is a bijection for every
g\geqslant g_{0}
.

The proof of Dunfield and Thurston is not constructive and does not give any bound on the minimal
g_{0}
required. We will see that
g_{0}=1
in the case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and that
g_{0}=2
in the case
G\in\{\mathrm{D}_{n},\mathfrak{A}_{4},\mathfrak{S}_{4},\mathfrak{A}_{5}\}
.
There does not exist any surjective homomorphism in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{1},G)
for
G\in\{\mathfrak{A}_{4},\mathfrak{S}_{4},\mathfrak{A}_{5}\}
and for
G=\mathrm{D}_{n}
,
n\geqslant 3
, since these groups are not abelian. Moreover, the image of the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{1},\mathrm{D}_{2})
by
\Phi
is not the whole group
\mathrm{H}_{2}(\mathrm{D}_{2},\mathbb{Z})
. Hence
g_{0}\geqslant 2
for these groups. However for
g\geqslant 2
and these groups
G
, the image of the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
by
\Phi
is surjective. We may check that
|\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})|\leqslant 2
. We are going to show that the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms with fixed image by
\Phi
is transitive.

3.1.
Cyclic groups

Let us start with the case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the cyclic group of order
n\geqslant 2
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{n})
is transitive.

This follows from the work of Edmonds in
[
Edm82
]
, since
\mathrm{H}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\mathbb{Z})
is trivial. Indeed the main result of
[
Edm82
]
is the following.

Theorem 3.3

(Edmonds)
.

If
G
is a finite abelian group, then the natural map from the orbits of the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
to
\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is injective.

This result for the special case
G=\mathbb{Z}_{n}
even goes back to Nielsen
[
Nie37
]
.
Let us nevertheless give a short proof of
3.2
.

Proof.

Recall from
Section

2
that
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
acts as
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{n})\simeq(\mathbb{Z}_{n})^{2g}
. Let us lift
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{n})
to
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}^{2g})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
. It is well known that the orbits of the
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
action on
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
are classified by the greatest common divisor of each of its elements, see for example
[
FM01
, Proposition 6.2]
. Hence we may assume that
\tilde{\rho}=(k,0,\ldots,0)
with
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Note that
\gcd(k,n)=1
, since
\rho
is surjective. The homomorphism
\rho^{\prime}=(k,n,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
is another lift of
\rho
and with the action of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
, one can change it to
\rho^{\prime}=(1,0,\ldots,0)
. We now have
\rho(a_{1})=1
,
\rho(b_{1})=0
and
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=0
for all
i\geqslant 2
.
∎

3.2.
Dihedral groups

In this section we consider the case where
G=\mathrm{D}_{n}
is the dihedral group of order
2n
, where
n\geqslant 2
, generated by a rotation of the plane of order
n
and a symmetry
s
along an axis of the Euclidean plane. Recall that
\epsilon\in\mathrm{Hom}(\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\mathbb{Z}_{2})
denotes the quotient homomorphism
\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Observe that there exists a natural isomorphism
\mathrm{D}_{n}\simeq\mathbb{Z}_{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and that
\epsilon
restricted to
\mathrm{D}_{n}
is the composition of this isomorphism with the projection
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
Note that for every
x\in\ker\epsilon
and every
y\in\mathrm{D}_{n}\setminus\ker\epsilon
, we have
y^{-1}xy=x^{-1}
and
y=y^{-1}
.

Let us first deal with the case
g=1
.

Proposition 3.4
.

If
g=1
, then there is no surjective homomorphism
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
unless
n=2
, in which case the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
is transitive.

Proof.

If there exists a surjective homomorphism in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
then
\mathrm{D}_{n}
must be abelian and
n=2
.
The set
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{2})
has cardinality
16
. Among those
16
representations,
6
are surjective. Indeed this set is in bijection with the bases of the
\mathbb{F}_{2}
vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{2}
since
\mathrm{D}_{2}\simeq\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}^{2}
. We leave it to the reader to check that the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on this set is transitive.
∎

We now suppose until the end of this section that
g\geqslant 2
.

Proposition 3.5
.

The action of the group
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
has two orbits if
n
is even and one if
n
is odd.

This follows from this theorem of Edmonds which is the main result of
[
Edm83
]
.

Theorem 3.6

(Edmonds)
.

Let
G
be a split metacyclic group.
The natural map from the
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
orbits of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
to
\mathrm{H}_{2}(G,\mathbb{Z})
is injective.

The group
\mathrm{D}_{n}
is indeed a semi-direct product of cyclic groups: it inserts in the split exact sequence

1\to\mathbb{Z}_{n}\to\mathrm{D}_{n}\xrightarrow{\epsilon}\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to 1.

However for the sake of completeness we will give a direct proof of
3.5
.
Let us describe these orbits. We define
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
as follows.
Let
\rho(a_{1})\in\ker\epsilon
be an order
n
rotation and
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
.
We let
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=\mathrm{id}
for
1<i<g
and
\rho(a_{g})=\mathrm{id}
and
\rho(b_{g})=s
. If
n
is even, we define
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
by
\rho^{\prime}(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\{a_{1},\ldots,b_{g}\}\setminus\{a_{g}\}
and by
\rho^{\prime}(a_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{n/2}
. Observe that
s
and
\rho(a_{1})^{n/2}
commute since
s\rho(a_{1})^{n/2}=\rho(a_{1})^{-n/2}s=\rho(a_{1})^{n/2}s
. Therefore
\rho^{\prime}
is well-defined.
The representations
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are not in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
since
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
while
\rho^{\prime}
does not because of the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
be order
2
elements that commute. Either
\alpha=\beta
or
[\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}]=-\mathrm{Id}
, where
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\beta}
are lifts of
\alpha
and
\beta
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\alpha\neq\beta
. We may conjugate
\alpha
and
\beta
so that
\alpha=\pm\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\\
\end{pmatrix}
. The map
\beta
must interchange the fixed points of
\alpha
in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, thus
\beta=\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}
. We have
[\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}]=-\mathrm{Id}
.
∎

We turn to the proof of
3.5
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
with image
\mathrm{D}_{n}
.
The homomorphism
\epsilon\circ\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is onto, hence by
3.2
, we may assume that
\epsilon\circ\rho(b_{g})=1
,
\epsilon\circ\rho(a_{g})=0
and
\epsilon\circ\rho(a_{i})=\epsilon\circ\rho(b_{i})=0
for
i<g
. For
i<g
, both
\rho(a_{i})
and
\rho(b_{i})
are in
\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, hence
\rho([a_{i},b_{i}])=\mathrm{id}
. Therefore
\rho
restricts to a homomorphism
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g-1},\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
with finite image. By
3.2
again, we may assume that
\rho(a_{1})
generates the group
\rho(\Gamma_{g-1})
, that
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
and that
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=\mathrm{id}
for
1<i<g
. We claim that
\rho(a_{1})
and
\rho(a_{g})
generate
\ker\epsilon
. Indeed every
x\in\ker\epsilon
is a word in
\rho(a_{1})
,
\rho(a_{g})
and
\rho(b_{g})
since
\rho
is onto. Such a word contains an even number of
\rho(b_{g})
because
x\in\ker\epsilon
. Since
\rho(b_{g})^{2}=\mathrm{id}
and
\rho(b_{g})\rho(a_{i})=\rho(a_{i})^{-1}\rho(b_{g})
for all
i
, we can write
x
without any
\rho(b_{g})
. We may apply a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
a_{1}a_{g}
to replace
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
with
\rho(a_{1})\rho(a_{g})
. Applying again an automorphism of
\Gamma_{g-1}
, we may assume that
\rho(a_{1})
is a chosen generator of
\ker\epsilon
, while
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
and
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=\mathrm{id}
for
1<i<g
.
Let us write
\rho(b_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{k}s
. Let us show that we may assume that
k
is even. If
k
is odd and
\rho(a_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{l}
with
\ell
odd, then we apply a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
a_{g}
so that
\rho(b_{g})
is replaced with
\rho(a_{g})\rho(b_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{k+l}s
. If
\rho(a_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{l}
with
\ell
even, then apply a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
a_{g}a_{1}
. It replaces
\rho(b_{g})
with
\rho(b_{g})\rho(a_{g})\rho(a_{1})=\rho(a_{1})^{k+l+1}s
. We may apply an automorphism of
\Gamma_{g-1}
so that
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
again.
We now assume that
\rho(b_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{k}s
, with
k=2k^{\prime}
.
We conjugate
\rho
with
\rho(a_{1})^{k^{\prime}}
so that
\rho(b_{g})=s
.
Since
\rho([a_{g},b_{g}])=\mathrm{id}
, we have
\rho(b_{g})^{-1}\rho(a_{g})^{-1}\rho(b_{g})\rho(a_{g})=\rho(a_{g})^{2}=\mathrm{id}
. Since
\rho(a_{g})
is in the cyclic group
\ker\epsilon
, either
\rho(a_{g})=\mathrm{id}
, or
n
is even and
\rho(a_{g})=\rho(a_{1})^{n/2}
.
∎

3.3.
The group
\mathfrak{A}_{4}

Let us now classify the orbits of the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,G)
where
G=\mathfrak{A}_{4}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

The action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathfrak{A}_{4})
has two orbits.

Recall that the group
\mathrm{K}=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{A_{4}}\mid\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{id}\}
is an order
4
normal subgroup of
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. The quotient
\mathfrak{A}_{4}/\mathrm{K}
has order
3
; hence there exists a surjective homomorphism
\varphi:\mathfrak{A}_{4}\to\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

Let us describe a representative of one of these two orbits. Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathfrak{A}_{4})
be such that
\rho(a_{1})
is
3
-cycle,
\rho(b_{1})=\rho(a_{2})=\mathrm{id}
and
\rho(b_{2})\in\mathrm{K}\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}
and
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=\mathrm{id}
for
3\leqslant i\leqslant g
.
As a first step to prove
3.8
, we show that all the representations of this form lie in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 3.9
.

For every
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
of this form, there exists
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
such that
\sigma\cdot\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}
.

Proof.

By
2.1
it suffices to show that they are in the same orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathfrak{A}_{4}
.
We may conjugate
\rho_{0}
so that
\rho_{0}(a_{1})\in\{\rho_{1}(a_{1}),\rho_{1}(a_{1})^{-1}\}
.
We can even suppose
\rho_{0}(a_{1})=\rho_{1}(a_{1})
, replacing
\rho_{0}
with
\sigma\cdot\rho_{0}
by
3.2
, where
\sigma\in\Gamma_{1}
following the notation of
Lemma

2.2
. Conjugating
\rho_{0}
with a power of
\rho_{0}(a_{1})
replaces
\rho_{0}
with
\rho_{2}
. Indeed we have

\{\rho_{0}(a_{1})^{k}\rho_{0}(b_{2})\rho_{0}(a_{1})^{-k}\mid 0\leqslant k\leqslant 2\}=\mathrm{K}\setminus\{\mathrm{id}\}.\qed

Let us now describe the other orbit.
Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathfrak{A}_{4})
be such that
\rho(a_{1})
is a
3
-cycle,
\rho(b_{1})=\mathrm{id}
and
\rho(a_{2})
and
\rho(b_{2})
are distinct double transpositions and
\rho(a_{i})=\rho(b_{i})=\mathrm{id}
for
3\leqslant i\leqslant g
. Observe that such a representation cannot be in the same orbit as the previous one, since it does not lift to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
by
Lemma

3.7
.

Lemma 3.10
.

If
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
have this form, then there exists
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
such that
\sigma\cdot\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}
.

Proof.

As before we can assume that
\rho_{0}(a_{1})=\rho_{1}(a_{1})
.
The action of
\Gamma_{1}
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{1},\mathrm{K})
is transitive, since
\mathrm{K}\simeq\mathrm{D}_{2}
. Therefore, we may replace
\rho_{0}
with
\sigma\cdot\rho_{0}
so that
\rho_{0}=\rho_{1}
, where
\sigma\in\Gamma_{1}
, following the notations of
Lemma

2.2
.
∎

We now show that any representation may be taken to the form of
Lemma

3.9
or
Lemma

3.10
. This completes the proof of
3.8
.

Proof.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathfrak{A}_{4})
with image
\mathfrak{A}_{4}
. The homomorphism
\varphi\circ\rho
in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{3})
is surjective. We have seen in
3.2
that the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
on the set of surjective homomorphisms of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{3})
is transitive. Hence we may assume that
\varphi\circ\rho(a_{i})=\varphi\circ\rho(b_{i})=0
for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant g
and that
\varphi\circ\rho(a_{1})=1
and
\varphi\circ\rho(b_{1})=0
. For all
2\leqslant i\leqslant g
,
\rho([a_{i},b_{i}])=\mathrm{id}
since
\rho(a_{i})
and
\rho(b_{i})
are in the abelian group
\mathrm{K}
. Since
\varphi\circ\rho(a_{1})
has order
3
,
\rho(a_{1})
itself has order
3
and
\rho(a_{1})
is a

\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
, see
Section

2
.

Proof.

Let
\rho_{n}=(\frac{1}{n},0,\ldots,0)\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Let us consider a lift
\tilde{\rho}
of
\rho
to
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2g}
. Let
x_{i}=\tilde{\rho}(a_{i})
and
y_{i}=\tilde{\rho}(b_{i})
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant g
. One of
x_{1},\ldots,y_{g}
is not rational and we can assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}
is as such: we can replace
\rho
with some
\sigma\cdot\rho
, exchanging the handles so that
x_{1}
or
y_{1}
is not rational and we may then apply the symplectic transformation
(x_{1},y_{1})\mapsto(y_{1},-x_{1})
if necessary. We can apply the transformation that replaces
(x_{1},y_{1})
with
(x_{1},y_{1}-y_{i})
and
(x_{i},y_{i})
with
(x_{1}+x_{i},y_{i})
for each
i
if necessary so that
x_{i}\notin\mathbb{Q}
. For every
i
such that
x_{i}\mathbb{Z}+y_{i}\mathbb{Z}
is not dense in
\mathbb{R}
, replace
y_{i}
with
y_{i}+1
. The new
\tilde{\rho}
is still a lift of
\rho
and now
x_{i}\mathbb{Z}+y_{i}\mathbb{Z}
is dense in
\mathbb{R}
because
\frac{y_{i}}{x_{i}}
and
\frac{y_{i}+1}{x_{i}}
cannot both be rationals. We may assume that each
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are nonnegative, applying
(x_{i},y_{i})\mapsto(x_{i}+ky_{i},y_{i})
and
(x_{i},y_{i})\mapsto(x_{i},y_{i}+lx_{i})
, for some
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
. We now apply the Euclidean algorithm in each handle: if
x_{i}>y_{i}
, replace
x_{i}
with
x_{i}-y_{i}
and otherwise replace
y_{i}
with
y_{i}-x_{i}
. Iterating this process, we can assume that
0<x_{i},y_{i}<\epsilon
for every
i
, where
\epsilon>0
is fixed. Finally replacing
x_{1}
with
x_{1}+my_{1}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we can assume that
|x_{1}-\frac{1}{n}|<\epsilon
and that the other
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
are in
[0,\epsilon]
.

We have shown that each
\rho_{n}
is in the closure of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\cdot\rho
. Since this closure is invariant under
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
, it contains every representation in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
with finite image by
3.2
. Therefore it contains the closure of
(\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})^{2g}
, hence is equal to
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.
∎

4.1.2.
The group
\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))

\rho
has infinite image in
\mathrm{O}_{2}
and is not contained in
\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})

under the action of

is included in the closed set

\rho^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
such that

is surjective and such that
\mathrm{sw}(\rho^{\prime})=\mathrm{sw}(\rho)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The orbit
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\cdot\rho
is dense in

.

Proof.

It suffices to show that every representation
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{D}_{n})
with finite image such that
\epsilon\circ\rho^{\prime}
is surjective and
\mathrm{sw}(\rho)=\mathrm{sw}(\rho^{\prime})
is in
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\cdot\rho
.
The representation
\epsilon\circ\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{2})
is surjective, hence we may assume by
3.2
that

for

and that

[\rho(a_{i}),\rho(b_{i})]=\mathrm{id}
for every

. Therefore
\rho(b_{1})
is either
\mathrm{id}
or the rotation of order
2
in
\mathrm{SO}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let us consider a representation
\rho^{\prime}
with finite image in
\mathrm{O_{2}}
, such that
\epsilon\circ\rho
is surjective and
\mathrm{sw}(\rho)=\mathrm{sw}(\rho^{\prime})
.
By
3.5
, we may assume that
\rho^{\prime}(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma)
for

. Since the image of

is dense in
\mathrm{O}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, one of the

has infinite order, where

By

, there exists a sequence

in

∎

4.1.3.
The group
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

In this subsection, we consider representations
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))
such that the image of

is dense in
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let us recall the main result of
[
PX02
]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

[Previte-Xia]
Let
g^{\prime}\geqslant 1
and

.
Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g^{\prime},b},\mathrm{SU}_{2})

then the closure of the orbit

Let us recall that the representation space
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))/\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
has two connected components: one of which is the set of conjugacy classes of representations that lift to
\mathrm{SU}_{2}

.
It follows from

that the orbit of a representation in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))

\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))/\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

Lemma 4.4
.

The
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
orbit of
[\rho]\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R}))/\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

Proof.

Let us see
\rho
in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g,1},\mathrm{P}\mathrm{SU}(2))
where

. There exists a lift
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma_{g,1},\mathrm{SU}(2))
such that
\tilde{\rho}(\partial\Sigma_{g,1})=\epsilon\mathrm{Id}

∎

4.2.
Affine holonomy

In this subsection we consider representations
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
, where the group
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
that fix
\infty\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. In other words,
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})=\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&t\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*},t\in\mathbb{C}\}
. The map
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{C}^{*}\rtimes\mathbb{C}
defined by
\pm\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&t\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto(\lambda^{2},\lambda^{-1}t)
is an isomorphism. We denote by
az+b
the element of
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
corresponding to
(a,b)\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\rtimes\mathbb{C}
.
We identify a representation
\rho
with the
2g
-tuple
(\rho(\gamma))_{\gamma\in\{a_{1},\ldots,b_{g}\}}
.
Let us first observe that a representation in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Every representation
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof.

Let us pick lifts
\tilde{\rho}(\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\lambda}_{\gamma}&\tilde{t}_{\gamma}\\
0&\tilde{\lambda}_{\gamma}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
in
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
of
\rho(\gamma)
for each
\gamma\in\{a_{1},\ldots,b_{g}\}
. The product of the commutators
[\tilde{\rho}(a_{i}),\tilde{\rho}(b_{i})]
is
\mathrm{Id}
.
∎

We denote by
\mathrm{Li}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\rtimes\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
the projection on the first coordinate and by
\mathrm{Tr}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\rtimes\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
the projection on the second coordinate. Note that
\mathrm{Li}
is a homomorphism while
\mathrm{Tr}
satisfies
\mathrm{Tr}(h_{1}h_{2})=\mathrm{Tr}(h_{1})+\mathrm{Li}(h_{1})\mathrm{Tr}(h_{2})
for every
h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
.
Recall that an affine representation
\rho
induces a representation in
\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
its linear part

\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
. We also define the
translation part
of
\rho
to be the map
\mathrm{Tr}\circ\rho
.

We identify
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{*})
with the set of affine representations
\rho
such that
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)=0
.
The linear part of a representation is in the closure of its orbit under the action of
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
by conjugation.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
. The representation
\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
is in the closure of
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})\cdot\rho
.

Proof.

The sequence
(g_{n}^{-1}\rho g_{n})_{n}
tends to
\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
, where for every
n\geqslant 1
,

g_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}n&0\\
0&1/n\end{pmatrix}.

∎

Let us denote by
\mathbb{S}^{1}
the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mid z\overline{z}=1\}
. The group
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2})
is the subgroup
\mathbb{S}^{1}\rtimes\mathbb{C}
of
\mathbb{C}^{*}\rtimes\mathbb{C}=\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
. It is the group of positive isometries of the Euclidean plane.

Let us suppose for now that
\rho
is Euclidean:
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2}))
.
The representation
\rho
acquires a notion of
volume
, see also
[
Gha16
, Section 6]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

There exists a smooth
\rho
-equivariant function
f:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, the pullback of
dx\wedge dy
by
f
induces a form
\omega
on
\Sigma
whose volume
\int_{\Sigma}\omega
does not depend on
f
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2}))
is path-connected, the bundle
\tilde{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{C}/\Gamma
is topologically trivial, where
\Gamma
acts by
\gamma\cdot(\tilde{x},z)=(\gamma\cdot\tilde{x},\rho(\gamma)\cdot z)
. Hence it admits smooth sections. We can lift such a section to a function
F:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\tilde{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{C}
of the form
F(\tilde{x})=(\tilde{x},f(z))
. The function
f:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{C}
is smooth and
\rho
-equivariant.

Given two smooth
\rho
-equivariant functions
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
, the smooth function
H:(x,t)\in\tilde{\Sigma}\times[0,1]\mapsto tf_{1}(x)+(1-t)f_{0}(x)
is also
\rho
-equivariant. The pullback
H^{*}(dx\wedge dy)
is
\Gamma
-invariant and thus induces a form
\omega
on
\Sigma\times[0,1]
. Observe that this form is closed since
dx\wedge dy
is closed. Therefore Stokes’ theorem gives:

0=\int_{\Sigma\times[0,1]}d\omega=\int_{\Sigma\times\{1\}}\omega-\int_{\Sigma\times\{0\}}\omega.

The restriction of
\omega
to
\Sigma\times\{i\}
is the pullback of
dx\wedge dy
by
f_{i}
for
i=0,1
.
∎

Definition 4.8
.

The volume of
\rho
is the number
\mathrm{Vol}(\rho)=\int_{\Sigma}\omega
, where
\omega
is the induced volume form on
\Sigma
by the pullback of
dx\wedge dy
by a smooth
\rho
-equivariant function
f:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{C}
.

Remark 4.9
.

The volume is invariant under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
and under the action of
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2})
by conjugation.

4.2.1.
Finite linear part

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2}))
be such that
\alpha=\mathrm{Li}\circ\rho
has finite image of order
n\geqslant 1
. We also suppose that
\rho
is not spherical.

We exhibit some representations in the closure of the orbit of
\rho
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
. Using our classification of the orbits of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}_{n})
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
, we provide a simple form of
\rho
if

.

Lemma 4.10
.

If
n\geqslant 2
, then there exists
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathbb{C}\cdot\rho
such that

\rho^{\prime}=(\zeta z,z,z+z_{3},z+z_{4},\ldots,z+z_{2g}).

Where
\mathbb{C}
is the subgroup of
\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2})
of translations, and
\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}
.

Proof.

There exists
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
such that
\sigma\cdot\alpha=(\zeta,1,\ldots,1)
by
3.2
.
Therefore, replacing
\rho
with
\sigma\cdot\rho
we have

\rho=(\zeta z+z_{1},z+z_{2},z+z_{3},\ldots,z+z_{2g}).

Now we can conjugate
\rho
by a translation so that
z_{1}=0
. The product of the commutators
[\rho(a_{i}),\rho(b_{i})]
is
[\zeta z,z+z_{2}]
and must be trivial. Thus
\zeta z
commutes with
z+z_{2}
hence
z_{2}=0
.
∎

There are even simpler representations in the closure of the orbit of
\rho
under the action of
\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
\rho_{\infty}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Isom}^{+}(\mathbb{E}^{2}))
be the representation defined by

\rho_{\infty}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(z+1,z,z,\ldots z)&\mbox{if }n=1\\
(\zeta z,z,z+1,z,\ldots,z,z)&\mbox{if }n\geqslant 2.\end{array}\right.

We have
\rho_{\infty}\in\overline{\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)\times\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})\cdot\rho}

We first suppose that
n=1
.
Let us write
\rho=(z+z_{1},\ldots,z+z_{2g})
.
We have supposed that
\rho
is not conjugated in
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
, hence there exists
1\leqslant i\leqslant 2g
such that
z_{i}\neq 0
. Exchanging the handles if necessary we can assume that

. Applying a Dehn twist in the first handle if needed, we may even assume that

\rho
with

, we may assume that
z_{2}=1
. A power of a Dehn twist along a curve homotopic to
b_{1}
replaces
\rho
with

\rho_{N}=(z+N+z_{1},z+1,z+z_{3},\ldots z+z_{2g}).

The representation

converges to
\rho_{\infty}
, where
h_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{N}&0\\
0&1/\sqrt{N}\end{pmatrix}
.
We now suppose that
n\geqslant 2
.
We may start with
\rho
as in
Lemma

4.10
.
Since
\rho
is not conjugated in
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})

such that

.
∎

If

then the group
\mathrm{GL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{R})

Let

Let

5.
Branched projective structures

5.1.
Definitions

Definition 5.1
.

A branched projective atlas on
\Sigma
is the datum of an open covering
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of
\Sigma
together with maps, called
charts
,
\varphi_{\alpha}:U_{\alpha}\to V_{\alpha}
, where
V_{\alpha}
is an open set of
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
. We require that the
\varphi_{\alpha}
are topologically conjugated to the map from the unit disk to itself
z\mapsto z^{n}
, for some
n\geqslant 1
. We also require that when
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\neq\emptyset
, there exists
h\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that
\varphi_{\alpha}=h\circ\varphi_{\beta}
on
U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}
.

Two branched atlases are said to be equivalent if their union is still a branched atlas.
Let us denote by
\mathbf{BP}(\Sigma)
the set of equivalence classes of branched projective atlases on
\Sigma
, or equivalently the set of maximal atlases.
Given a maximal atlas we may associate to each point
p\in\Sigma
an integer
n\geqslant 0
as follows. We can pick a chart
\varphi_{\alpha}
around
p
that is topologically conjuated to
z\mapsto z^{n+1}
and such that
p
correspond to
0
. This integer
n\geqslant 0
does not depend on the chart and vanishes everywhere but on a discrete, hence finite, set. We thus define a divisor on
\Sigma
called the
branch divisor
. A point where this integer
n
is at least
1
is called a branch point, or a conical point. We say that
n
is the order of this branch point, or that this conical point has angle
2\pi(n+1)
.
The group
\mathrm{Homeo}_{+}(\Sigma)
acts on
\mathbf{BP}(\Sigma)
by precomposing the charts.

Definition 5.2
.

A branched projective structure on
\Sigma
is an element of
\mathcal{BP}(\Sigma)=\mathbf{BP}(\Sigma)/\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(\Sigma)
.

The branch divisor of a branched projective structure is not well-defined but the datum of the orders of the branch points is. Hence
\mathcal{BP}
decomposes as the union of
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
of branched projective structures with
k
branch points of order
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
.

Given
X\in\mathbf{BP}(\Sigma)
, we may choose a chart
U_{\alpha}
and consider its analytic continuation
f:\widetilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
as usual with geometric structures, see
[
Thu97
, Chapter 3]
. There exists a unique
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
such that
f
is
\rho
-equivariant:
f(\gamma\cdot z)=\rho(\gamma)\cdot f(z)
for all
z\in\widetilde{\Sigma}
, and
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Choosing another chart would change
f
to
h\circ f
and
\rho
to
h\rho h^{-1}
for some
h\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. The map
f
is called a developing map of
X
and
\rho
the holonomy of
X
.
Note that one can give an element of
\mathbf{BP}(\Sigma)
by giving its developing map.
Observe that the holonomy
\mathrm{Hol}(X)=[\rho]\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
satisfies
\mathrm{Hol}(h\cdot X)=\mathrm{Hol}(X)
for every
h\in\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(\Sigma)
. Indeed if
h
is isotopic to the identity, then it admits a
\Gamma
-equivariant lift
H:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\tilde{\Sigma}
. A developing map for
g\cdot X
is thus
\mathrm{dev}(X)\circ H
, that is
\rho
-equivariant.
Therefore we have a well-defined map

\mathrm{Hol}:\mathcal{BP}\to\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}).

In the rest of the paper, we will abuse notation and identify
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
with the subset of
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
of representations
\rho
whose conjugacy class is in
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
.

Let us give some examples of branched projective surfaces.

Example 5.3
.

(1)

A (possibly branched) hyperbolic structure on
\Sigma
gives a developing map
\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
that is
\rho
-equivariant for some
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.

(2)

Suppose
S
is a surface endowed with a branched projective structure
X
. Let us consider
f:\Sigma\to S
a branched cover. We define the
pullback
of
X
by
f
, that we denote
f^{*}X
, as follows. A developing map for
f^{*}X
is given by
\mathrm{dev}\circ\tilde{f}
, where
\mathrm{dev}:\tilde{S}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
is a developing map for
X
and
\tilde{f}:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\tilde{S}
is a lift of
f
.

5.2.
The mapping class group action on
\mathcal{BP}

The mapping class group
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)=\mathrm{Homeo}_{+}(\Sigma)/\mathrm{Homeo}_{0}(\Sigma)
acts naturally on
\mathcal{BP}
. This action preserves the sets
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
. The map
\mathrm{Hol}
is equivariant with respect to the actions of
\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma)
on
\mathcal{BP}
and on
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))/\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Aut}^{+}(\Gamma)
and
h\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
. For every
1\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant n_{k}

\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))\iff h(\sigma\cdot\rho)h^{-1}\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})).

5.3.
The Ehresmann-Thurston principle

We prove a version of the Ehresman-Thurston principle,

[

.
We first show that given a projective structure on

Let us consider the compact set

Lemma 5.5
.

Let us consider a branched projective structure

\Sigma
.
There exists a triangulation

\Sigma

(1)

Every conical point of

(2)

Every edge of

(3)

Each lift

Let us start with an arbitrary triangulation of

5.5
.

6.
Geometrization

The aim of this section is to prove
1.1
. We will consider representations
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
that satisfy the conditions of the obstructions and prove that they are
geometric
, that is, that they are the holonomy of a projective structure with the given datum of branch points. Along the way we will also explain the obstructions that we did not explain in the introduction.

6.1.
Branched cover of the sphere

Let us fix
g\geqslant 0
. We consider the special case where
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is the trivial representation:
\rho(\gamma)=\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
. The aim of this part is to prove the following.

Proposition 6.1
.

The trivial representation
\rho
is in
\mathcal{\mathrm{Hol}}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if and only if
\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g+2l
for some integer
l\geqslant 1
such that
\max_{i}n_{i}\leqslant l
.

The developing map
f:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
of a spherical structure with holonomy
\rho
induces a branched cover
\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Conversely, any branched cover
\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
gives a branched projective structure on
\Sigma
with trivial holonomy. Therefore we are reduced to the classical problem of the existence of branched covers of the sphere with prescribed branching data. With this point of view it is clear that
6.1
is equivalent to
1.4
.
As observed in
[
MP19
]
, it is the
g=0
case a necessary and sufficient condition to the existence of a spherical metric with conical angles
2\pi(n_{1}+1),\ldots,2\pi(n_{k}+1)
since its holonomy must be trivial.

Let us recall the classical statement of the Hurwitz realization problem, following the notations of
[
EKS84
]
.
A
branch data
is a set of partitions of an integer
d>0
. A branched cover
\Sigma\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
of degree
d
induces a branch data in the following way: for each point
p\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
which is the image of a branch point, the orders
a_{i}
of its pre-images give a partition of
d
. A branch data that arises in this way is said to be
realizable
. The Hurwitz realization problem is to characterize the branch data that are realizable.
The Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives obstructions to realizability. For a partition
A=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}]
, let
v(A)=\sum_{i}(a_{i}-1)
, and for a branch data
\mathcal{D}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
, let
v(\mathcal{D})=\sum_{i}v(A_{i})
. If
\mathcal{D}
is realizable, then the Riemann-Hurwitz formula gives

\chi(\Sigma)=2d-v(\mathcal{D}).

In particular,
v(\mathcal{D})
must be even and at least
2d-2
. However it is well known that these conditions are not sufficient, see for example
[
EKS84
,
PP06
]
. This problem was considered by Hurwitz in
[
Hur91
]
where he showed the following theorem. We identify the conjugacy classes of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{d}
with the partitions of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
as follows: the lengths of the orbits of the action of the group generated by
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{d}
on
\{1,\ldots,d\}
determine a partition of
d
.

Hurwitz conditions
.

The branch data
\mathcal{D}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
is realizable if and only if there exists permutations
\alpha_{1}\in A_{1},\ldots\alpha_{k}\in A_{k}
such that

(1)

\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\ldots\alpha_{k}=\mathrm{id}

(2)

The group generated by the
\alpha_{i}
acts transitively on
\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

We show the following statement, that implies
6.1
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let

and

\mathcal{D}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}

6.2.
Spherical structures

In this section we consider representations that fix a point in

6.2.1.
General obstructions

Suppose we have a branched projective structure

on
\Sigma

\gamma^{*}f^{*}\omega=(f\circ\gamma)^{*}\omega=(\rho(\gamma)\circ f)^{*}\omega=f^{*}\rho(\gamma)^{*}\omega=f^{*}\omega.

Therefore it induces a form
\omega_{\Sigma}
on

, Section 1.2]

\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Sigma}\omega=2g-2.

Therefore, we must have
\sum_{i}n_{i}>2g-2

2
in the spherical case.

Let us suppose that

6.3.
Affine holonomy

In this subsection we geometrize affine representations.
An example of a branched projective structure with affine holonomy is an affine structure, as studied for example in
[
Gha18
]
.

Definition 6.14
.

An
affine structure
is a branched projective structure with affine holonomy whose developing map takes its values in
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

This definition generalizes for example the notion of translation surface.

Definition 6.15
.

A translation surface structure
X
on
\Sigma
is an affine structure with holonomy in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C})
, where
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathrm{Aff(\mathbb{C})}
is the subgroup of translations. An even half-translation structure is an affine structure with holonomy in
\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\{\pm 1\}\rtimes\mathbb{C})
.

Remark 6.16
.

This amounts to requiring that
\alpha=\mathrm{Li\circ\rho}
is the trivial homomorphism for the translation surface case, and that
\alpha
has its image in
\{\pm 1\}
for the half-translation case.

Remark 6.17
.

Our definition of even half-translation surface is equivalent to the usual definitions of half-translation surfaces, see for example
[
Zor06
,
Lan08
]
, with the extra requirement that the total angle of every conical point is even in the following sense: it is of the form
k\pi
, with
k\in 2\mathbb{N}
. In other words, following the notations of
[
Lan08
]
, we are looking at the half-translation surfaces that are either translation surfaces or correspond to quadratic differentials in

\bigcup_{n_{i}\geqslant 1\mid\sum_{i}n_{i}=2g-2}\mathcal{Q}(2n_{1},2n_{2},\ldots,2n_{k}).

This extra requirement is necessary to obtain a holonomy defined on
\Gamma
, and not on the fundamental group of a punctured surface.

6.3.1.
General obstructions

Let us show that the divisor of a branched projective structure with affine holonomy has degree at least
2g-2
and that we may read in this degree whether such a structure is an affine one or not.

Proposition 6.18
.

If
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C}))
is the holonomy of a projective structure in
\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
with developing map
f
, then
\sum_{i}n_{i}\geqslant 2g-2
. Moreover
\sum n_{i}=2g-2
if and only if
f(\tilde{\Sigma})\subset\mathbb{C}
.

The following proof of
6.18
is an adaptation of
[
Kap20
, Section 6]
, where Kapovich shows the same result in the realm of holonomies with values in the subgroup of translations
\mathbb{C}\subset\mathrm{Aff(\mathbb{C})}
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\rho
is the holonomy of a branched projective structure with holomorphic developing map
f:\tilde{\Sigma}\to\mathbb{CP}^{1}
for a complex structure on
\Sigma
.
Let us choose a lift
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
of
\rho
and define
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\star})
by
\varphi(\gamma)=\lambda_{\gamma}
where
\tilde{\rho}(\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\gamma}^{-1}&t_{\gamma}\\
0&\lambda_{\gamma}\end{pmatrix}
. Let us consider the vector bundles
V=\tilde{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
and
\Lambda=\tilde{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{C}/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
acts by
\gamma\cdot(\tilde{x},v)=(\gamma\cdot\tilde{x},\tilde{\rho}(\gamma)\cdot v)
and
\gamma\cdot(\tilde{x},z)=(\gamma\cdot\tilde{x},\varphi(\gamma)\cdot z)
. The projection on the second coordinate gives a morphism of holomorphic bundles
\phi:V\to\Lambda
. The developing map gives for each point
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{\Sigma}
a line
f(\tilde{x})\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and by
\rho
-equivariance induces a line subbundle
L\subset V
. We restrict
\phi
to get a morphism of line bundles
\phi:L\to\Lambda
, which is injective if and only if
f(\tilde{\Sigma})
does not contain
\infty\in\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.

Lemma 6.19
.

We have
\deg L\leqslant\deg\Lambda
with equality if and only if
\phi
is injective.

Proof.

Let us pick a smooth section
s:\Sigma\to L
transverse to the zero section whose zeroes are different from those of
\phi:L\to\Lambda
. We also assume that
\phi\circ s
is transverse to the zero section. The degree of
L
is the intersection number between
s(\Sigma)
and the zero section:
\deg L=\sum_{p\in s^{-1}(0)}\mathrm{sign}_{p}(s)
, where
\mathrm{sign}_{p}(s)
is the sign of
\det(ds_{p})
where
s
is viewed as
s:U\subset\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
in a local chart around
p
, and a local trivialization of
L
.
The zeroes of the section
\phi\circ s
of
\Lambda
are of two types: the zeroes of
s
and the zeroes of
\phi
. Each zero of
\phi
has sign
1
, because in a local chart centered at
0
and a local trivialization, we have
\phi\circ s:z\mapsto\alpha(z)s(z)
where
\alpha
is holomorphic,
\alpha(0)=0
and
s(0)\neq 0
. Therefore
d(\phi\circ s)=s(0)\cdot d\alpha_{0}
has positive determinant. If
p
is a zero of
s
, we have
\alpha(0)\neq 0
and
s(0)=0
, thus
d(\phi\circ s)_{0}=\alpha(0)\cdot ds_{0}
and
\mathrm{sign}_{p}(\phi\circ s)=\mathrm{sign}_{p}(s)
. We have

\deg\Lambda=\sum_{p\in(\phi\circ s)^{-1}(0)}\mathrm{sign}_{p}(\phi\circ s)\\
=\sum_{p\in s^{-1}(0)}\mathrm{sign}_{p}(\phi\circ s)+\#\{p\in\Sigma\mid\phi=0\}.

Therefore,
\deg\Lambda=\deg L+\#\{p\in\Sigma\mid\phi=0\}\geqslant\deg L
with equality if and only if
\phi
is injective.
∎

Lemma 6.20
.

We have
\deg\Lambda=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that this degree does not depend on the homomorphism
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathbb{C}^{\star})

\gamma\in\{a_{1},\ldots,b_{g}\}
. We thus have
\exp(\chi)=\alpha
. We may now replace
\chi(b_{1})
with
\chi(b_{1})+2\pi ik
where

so that
\det(\chi(a_{1}),\chi(b_{1}))\neq 0
. We can then replace
\chi(b_{2})
with
\chi(b_{2})+2\pi ik

\mathrm{Vol}(\chi)\geqslant 2g|\det(\chi(a_{1}),\chi(b_{1}))|

generated by the

is the holonomy of a translation surface with a single conical point of angle
2\pi(2g-1)

∎

The explicit constructions made in

show that if

is the holonomy of a translation surface with a single conical point, then

is also the holonomy of another translation surface with a single conical point such that there exists a simple closed curve

that develops injectively on a line that is not parallel to the imaginary axis. The exponential of the associated developing map gives a translation surface with a single conical point and holonomy

if

is even and at least

6.4.
Dihedral holonomy

In this section, we suppose that
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
is
dihedral
but not affine, that is its image
\rho(\Gamma)
fixes globally a pair of points in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
, but not pointwise. After conjugating
\rho
, we may assume that
\rho(\Gamma)
is included in the group
\{z\mapsto cz^{\epsilon}\mid c\in\mathbb{C},\epsilon=\pm 1\}
. We require that
\rho
is not conjugated into
\mathrm{SO}_{3}(\mathbb{R})
nor in
\mathrm{Aff}(\mathbb{C})
, that is there exist
\gamma
and
\delta
in
\Gamma
such that
\rho(\gamma)=c_{\gamma}z
with
|c_{\gamma}|\neq 1
and
\rho(\delta)=c_{\delta}z^{\epsilon(\delta)}
with
\epsilon(\delta)=-1
.

Observe that
\epsilon:\Gamma\to\{\pm 1\}
is a group homomorphism. Thus we may assume that it satisfies
\epsilon(a_{1})=-1
and
\epsilon(\gamma)=1
for
\gamma\in\{b_{1},a_{2},\ldots b_{g}\}
by
3.2
. We may conjugate
\rho
with an isometry of the form
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
so that
c_{\gamma}=1
for
\gamma=a_{1}
. Since
\rho([a_{i},b_{i}])=\mathrm{id}
for
i\geqslant 2
, we must have
\rho([a_{1},b_{1}])=\mathrm{id}
. Therefore
c_{\gamma}\in\{1,-1\}
for
\gamma=b_{1}
.
It follows from
Lemma

3.7
that
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
if and only if
c_{\gamma}=1
, where
\gamma=b_{1}
.
Note that
\rho(\Gamma)
is not abelian. Therefore we assume that

in this section.

6.4.1.
Even Stiefel Whitney class

We first assume that
\rho
lifts to
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
:
c_{\gamma}=1
for
\gamma=b_{1}

We may extend the definition of the exponential of a representation to the representations of the form

Definition 6.35

(1)

The exponential of

(2)

The exponential of a half-translation surface

Remark 6.36

We have

Proposition 6.37
.

Let

Proof.

Let us consider a developing map

the double-cover of
\Sigma
associated with
\ker\epsilon\triangleleft\Gamma

with a branching divisor of degree
-2\chi(\Sigma)=-\chi(S)

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\star}
such that

does not contain

Now let
\tilde{f}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}
be the lift of

to the universal cover
\mathbb{C}
of
\mathbb{C}^{\star}

\exp:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}^{\star}
is the associated covering map.
For every

, and for every

\exp(\tilde{f}(\gamma\cdot z))=f(\gamma\cdot z)=c_{\gamma}f(z)^{\epsilon(\gamma)}

\tilde{f}(\gamma\cdot z)=\epsilon(\gamma)\tilde{f}(z)+l_{\gamma}

6.5.
Nonelementary holonomy

The celebrated theorem of Gallo, Kapovich and Marden proven in
[
GKM00
]
is in fact enhanced in the same paper, see
[
GKM00
, Theorem 11.2.4]
.

Theorem 6.41

(Gallo-Kapovich-Marden)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
be a nonelementary representation. We have
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if and only if
\sum_{i}n_{i}
has the same parity as
\mathrm{sw}(\rho)
.

Their proof is based on the existence of Schottky decompositions of nonelementary representations, shown in
[
GKM00
, Part A]
.
By a Schottky decomposition of
\rho
we mean a pants decomposition
\Sigma=\cup_{i}P_{i}
such that each restriction
\rho_{|P_{i}}:\pi_{1}(P_{i})\to\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is an isomorphism onto a Schottky group.
Indeed, the techniques of construction of projective structures with given holonomy of Gallo, Kapovich and Marden allow them to prove directly this theorem when
\rho
has a Schottky decomposition; see
[
GKM00
, Part B, Theorem 11.2.4]
.
In genus
g=2
, there exist nonelementary representations
\rho\in\mathrm{Hom}(\Gamma,\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}))
that do not admit a Schottky decomposition: the
pentagon representations
, see
[
LF19
]
.
In the case where
\rho
is a pentagon representation, there exists a branched projective structure on
\Sigma_{2}
with a single conical point of total angle
2\pi(1+1)
and holonomy
\rho
, see
[
LF19
, Section 5]
. Moreover we can assume that there exists a curve based at the conical point that develops injectively, see the red curve in
[
LF19
, Figure 8]
, whose exponential develops injectively. Thus we have
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(2d+1))
for every
d\geqslant 0
by
5.7
and
\rho\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
if
\sum_{i}n_{i}
is odd by
5.9
.
Therefore
1
is the only obstruction to being in
\mathrm{Hol}(\mathcal{P}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}))
for nonelementary representations.
