Proof of the transverse instability of Stokes waves Thanks: MSC Classification: 76B07, 35Q35, 35R35, 35C07, 35B35.
Ryan P. Creedon, Huy Q. Nguyen, Walter A. Strauss

Abstract
. A Stokes wave is a traveling free-surface periodic water wave that is constant in the direction transverse to the direction of propagation. In 1981 McLean discovered via numerical methods that Stokes waves at infinite depth are unstable with respect to transverse perturbations of the initial data. Even for a Stokes wave that has very small amplitude ε \varepsilon , we prove rigorously that transverse perturbations, after linearization, will lead to exponential growth in time. To observe this instability, extensive calculations are required all the way up to order O ⁡ ( ε 3 ) O(\varepsilon^{3}) . All previous rigorous results of this type were merely two-dimensional, in the sense that they only treated long-wave perturbations in the longitudinal direction. This is the first rigorous proof of three-dimensional instabilities of Stokes waves.



1.
Introduction

We consider classical water waves that are irrotational, incompressible, and inviscid.
The water lies below an unknown free surface
S
.
Such waves have been studied for over two centuries, notably by Stokes
[
32
]
.
A
Stokes wave
is a two-dimensional steady wave traveling in a fixed horizontal direction at a fixed speed
c
. It has been known for a century that
a curve of small-amplitude Stokes waves exists
[
27
,
23
,
33
]
.
Several decades ago it was proven that the curve extends to large amplitudes as well
[
21
]
.

In 1967 Benjamin and Feir
[
3
]
discovered, to the general surprise of the fluids community, that a
small

long-wave perturbation of a
small
Stokes wave in the same direction of propagation will lead to exponential instability.
This is known as the
modulational
(or Benjamin-Feir or sideband)
instability
, a phenomenon
whereby deviations from a periodic wave are reinforced by the nonlinearity, leading to the eventual breakup of the wave into a train of pulses. Rigorous proofs of the modulational instability were discovered by Bridges and Mielke
[
10
]
in the case of finite depth, provided the depth is larger than a critical depth
d_{0}
, and by two of the current authors
[
28
]
for infinite depth.
A more detailed description of the instability, including the figure-8 pattern of the
unstable eigenvalues, was found numerically in
[
16
]
and asymptotically by another of the current authors
[
15
]
.
This detailed description was proven rigorously by Berti
et al
, first in the deep water case
[
4
]

and then in the finite depth case
[
6
]
when the depth is larger than
d_{0}
. Recently the much more subtle
critical depth case was treated in
[
7
]
.

A different type of instability due to perturbations in the same direction of propagation (
i.e.
, the longitudinal direction)
was detected in the numerical work of McLean
[
24
,
25
]
. They are called
high-frequency instabilities
because they develop away from the origin of the complex plane, appearing as small isolas (bubbles)
centered on the imaginary axis. In contrast to modulational instabilities, high-frequency instabilities occur at all values of the depth. The first plot of the high-frequency instabilities was due to Deconinck and Oliveras
[
16
]
, thirty years after McLean’s work. Among the challenges in plotting these instabilities was to find the longitudinal wave numbers of the perturbation that correspond to each high-frequency isola, which exist in narrow intervals that drift as the amplitude of the Stokes wave increases. In
[
14
]
, a perturbation method was developed to obtain an asymptotic expansion of these intervals in addition to an asymptotic expansion of the maximum growth rates of the high-frequency instabilities. This revealed for the first time analytically that such instabilities can grow faster than the modulational instability at certain finite depths. These high-frequency results have since been made rigorous in the recent work
[
19
]
.

Both modulational and high-frequency instabilities are created by longitudinal perturbations that have
different periods
compared to that of the Stokes waves.
On the other hand, what was unanswered was
whether a small Stokes wave could be unstable when perturbed in both horizontal directions but keeping the longitudinal period unperturbed. This
transverse instability
problem was studied numerically first by Bryant
[
11
]
and was
followed by much more detailed work of McLean
et al

[
26
,
24
,
25
]
.
While these remarkable papers did detect transverse instabilities, a mathematical proof has been missing ever since. This problem is truly three-dimensional.

The purpose of the current paper is to provide the first rigorous proof of transverse instabilities of small Stokes waves.

Before discussing this paper, it is important to note that there are many other models of water waves for which the transverse instability has been studied rigorously.
One such model includes the presence of surface tension, that is, gravity-capillary waves.

However, it should be kept in mind that the presence of surface tension
drastically changes the mathematical problem.

The transverse instability for solitary (non-periodic) waves in such a model was rigorously
discussed by a number of authors, including Bridges
[
9
]
,
Pego and Sun
[
30
]
and Rousset and Tzvetkov
[
31
]
.
The transverse instability for periodic waves in this model was recently studied
by Haragus, Truong and Wahlen
[
18
]
.

With these results in mind, we now specify the parameters of our problem. Let
x
and
y
denote the horizontal variables
and
z
the vertical one.
For simplicity, we assume here that the depth is infinite.
We are confident that our proof generalizes to the finite-depth case. Consider the curve of Stokes waves traveling in the
x
-direction and with a given period, say
2\pi
without loss of generality. This curve is parametrized by a small parameter
\varepsilon
which represents the wave amplitude of the Stokes waves.
Such a steady wave can be described in the moving
(x,z)
plane
(where
x-ct
is replaced by
x
)
by its free surface

S=\{(x,y,z)\ |\ z=\eta^{*}(x;\varepsilon)\}
and by its velocity potential
\psi^{*}(x;\varepsilon)
restricted to
S
.

The perturbation of
\eta^{*}
takes the form
\overline{\eta}(x)e^{\lambda t+i\alpha y}
, where
\overline{\eta}
has the
same period

2\pi
as the Stokes wave,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is the growth rate of the perturbation, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is the transverse wave number of the perturbation. The problem is to find at least one value of
\alpha
that leads to instability, that is,
\text{Re}\lambda>0
.
After linearizing the nonlinear water wave system about a Stokes wave, introducing a “good-unknown,” and performing a conformal mapping change of variables, we find that the exponents
\lambda
are eigenvalues of a linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
, where
\beta=\alpha^{2}
. Motivated by
[
26
]
, we first determine a
resonant transverse wave number

\alpha_{*}
so that the unperturbed operator
\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}
with
\beta_{*}=\alpha_{*}^{2}
has an imaginary double eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=i\sigma
.
This eigenvalue corresponds to the lowest-possible resonance that generates a Type II transverse instability according to McLean
[
26
]
, of which there are infinitely many higher-order resonances that have potential to generate higher-order transverse instabilities. We expect however that higher-order transverse instabilities have smaller growth rates for small Stokes waves. In order to capture the transverse instabilities we introduce a new small parameter
\delta
for the perturbation of
\beta
about
\beta_{*}
. Our main result is that the perturbed operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}

with non-zero real parts that bifurcate from
\lambda_{0}
, stated more precisely in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exist
\varepsilon_{\textrm{max}}>0
and
\delta_{\text{max}}>0
such that
for all
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{\textrm{max}},\varepsilon_{\textrm{max}})
and
\delta\in(-\delta_{\text{max}},\delta_{\text{max}})
,
the operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
has a pair of eigenvalues

(1.1)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}

\displaystyle=i\Big(\sigma+\frac{1}{2}T(\varepsilon,\delta)\Big)\pm\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Delta(\varepsilon,\delta)},

where
T
and
\Delta
are real-valued, real-analytic functions such that
T(\varepsilon,\delta)=O\left(\delta\right)
and
\Delta(\varepsilon,\delta)=O\left(\delta^{2}\right)
as
(\varepsilon,\delta)\rightarrow(0,0)
. Furthermore, there exist
\kappa_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\kappa_{1}>0
such that for

(1.2)

\displaystyle\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\theta)=\kappa_{0}\varepsilon^{2}+\theta\varepsilon^{3}\hskip 10.00002pt\textrm{with}\hskip 10.00002pt|\theta|<\kappa_{1},

we have
\Delta(\varepsilon,\delta(\varepsilon,\theta))>0
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.
Thus, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{+}
has positive real part provided
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\theta)
with
|\theta|<\kappa_{1}
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. Moreover,
\text{Re}\lambda_{+}=O\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
for each
\theta
.
This means that there exist transverse perturbations of the given Stokes wave whose amplitudes
grow temporally like
e^{t\,\text{Re}\lambda_{+}}
.

Substituting
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\theta)
into (
1.1
) and dropping terms of
O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
and smaller, we obtain an asymptotic expansion of the unstable eigenvalues. By eliminating
\theta
from this expansion in favor of its real and imaginary parts, denoted
\lambda_{r}
and
\lambda_{i}
, respectively, we find that the eigenvalues lie approximately on the ellipse

(1.3)

\frac{4.085\lambda_{r}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{6}}+\frac{86.059\left(\lambda_{i}+0.389-0.467\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{2}}{\varepsilon^{6}}=1,

where we have numerically evaluated coefficients for ease of readability. The center of this ellipse drifts from the double eigenvalue
i\sigma\approx-0.389i
along the imaginary axis like
O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, while its semi-major and semi-minor axes scale like
O\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
. We refer to Corollary
6.3
for the precise statement and the left panel of Figure
1
for a schematic.

We compare (
1.3
) with numerical computations of the unstable eigenvalues obtained from the Floquet-Fourier-Hill method applied to the Ablowitz-Fokas-Musslimani formulation of the transverse spectral problem, see
[
17
]
for details. The right panel of Figure
1
shows the results of these numerical computations on a Stokes wave with amplitude parameter
\varepsilon=0.01
. Also plotted is the corresponding asymptotic ellipse (
1.3
).
The difference between the asymptotic and numerical results is
O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
, demonstrating agreement between the theoretical results and the numerical computations to
O\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
, as desired. Even better agreement can be found by retaining higher-order corrections of the unstable eigenvalues in a manner similar to
[
14
]
.

The isola of unstable eigenvalues found above is reminiscent of the high-frequency isolas that appear in the longitudinal stability spectrum. It is therefore natural to compare the growth rates of the transverse instability obtained in this work to the known growth rates of the longitudinal instabilities of Stokes waves, including the high-frequency and Benjamin-Feir instabilities. In the infinite depth longitudinal case, the largest high-frequency isola has semi-major and semi-minor axes that scale like
O\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)

[
14
]
. Thus our transverse instability grows at a
faster
rate
O(\varepsilon^{3})
for sufficiently small
amplitude waves.
On the other hand, our instability grows
slower
than the Benjamin-Feir instability rate, which is

O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
in both finite and infinite depth

[
10
,
28
,
4
,
6
,
19
,
15
]
. Moreover, our instability grows
slower
than the largest high-frequency instability in finite depth, which grows like
O(\varepsilon^{2})

[
14
,
19
]
.

(Left) A schematic of the transverse instability isolas (orange curves) of width
O\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
drifting from
\lambda_{0}
like
O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
. Here,
\lambda_{*}
represents the center of the isola. (Right) A comparison of the asymptotic approximation of the transverse instability isola (

) (orange curve) and numerical computations of the unstable eigenvalues (blue dots) when

.

With the preceding discussion in mind, we now turn to the main ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
It begins by finding an expression for

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
by a method analogous to that in
[
28
]
. However, for the present three-dimensional instability problem,
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
involves a genuine pseudo-differential operator as opposed to the Fourier multiplier in the two-dimensional problem considered in
[
28
,
4
]
. The proof continues by following the method of
[
4
]
that uses a Kato similarity transformation
to reduce the relevant spectral data of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
to a
2\times 2
matrix
\textrm{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
with the property that
i\textrm{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is real and skew-adjoint. Then we show that the entries of this matrix are real analytic functions of
\varepsilon

and
\delta
and we obtain convenient functional expressions for its eigenvalues, resulting in (
1.1
). In order to conclude that
\Delta(\varepsilon,\beta)>0
for
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\theta)
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon
,
we must expand the entries of the matrix in a power series up to third order in the pair

(\varepsilon,\delta)
. If the expansions are terminated before third order,
one finds
\Delta(\varepsilon,\delta)\leq 0
for any choice of
\delta
, which is insufficient for eigenvalues with positive real part. With the third-order expansions, however, we are able to show that
\Delta(\varepsilon,\delta)>0
if
\delta=O(\varepsilon^{2})
is chosen appropriately.

The problem solved in this paper turned out to be considerably more difficult than we had anticipated.
Originally we began by attempting to take the transverse perturbation at a fixed period, that is,
\delta=0
.
For the reasons stated above, that approach did not yield an instability.
We also found that certain residue calculations related to the eigenfunction expansions of the unstable eigenvalues led to more non-zero terms than we had initially thought. This, coupled with the introduction of the small parameter
\delta
, led to extremely arduous calculations,
so we took advantage of Mathematica to carry out the longest ones. They can be found in the companion Mathematica file
CompanionToTransverseInstabilities.nb
.

In Section 2 we introduce the Stokes waves and proceed with the linearization and then the flattening by means of a conformal mapping. The main result here is Theorem
2.7
, which is devoted to the three-dimensional Dirichlet-Neumann operator under a two-dimensional conformal mapping, and the proof of analyticity in
\varepsilon
and
\delta
. Theorem
1.1
is then reduced to studying the eigenvalues of the linearized operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
, which has a Hamiltonian form and is reversible.
Section 3 is devoted to a discussion of the first resonance on the imaginary axis when
\varepsilon=0
, the introduction of Kato’s perturbed basis,
and the reduction to the study of the eigenvalues of a
2\times 2
matrix
\textrm{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.
In Section 4 we perform lengthy expansions of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
out to third order in both
\varepsilon
and
\delta
; this is a major new difficulty compared to the two-dimensional instabilities studied in
[
28
,
4
]
.
In Section 5 we use the expansions of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
to compute the expansions of the Kato basis vectors and of the matrix
\textrm{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
.
Finally in Section 6 we analyze the leading terms in the characteristic discriminant of
\textrm{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
. An important step is to prove that a key coefficient, which we call
b_{3,0}
, does not vanish. This implies the existence of instabilities. The full derivation of the ellipse (
1.3
) is given in Corollary
6.3
.

2.
Transverse perturbations of Stokes waves

2.1.
Stokes waves

We consider the fluid domain

(2.1)

D(t)=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ :\ ~z<\eta(x,y,t)\}.

below the free surface
S=\{(x,y,\eta(x,y,t)):(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}
to have infinite depth. Assuming that the fluid is incompressible, inviscid and irrotational, the velocity field admits a harmonic
potential
\phi(x,y,t):D(t)\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
\phi
and
\eta
satisfy the water wave system

(2.2)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x,y,z}\phi=0\quad\text{ in }D(t),\\
\partial_{t}\phi+\tfrac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x,y,z}\phi|^{2}=-g\eta+P\quad\text{ on }S,\\
\partial_{t}\eta+\partial_{x}\phi\partial_{x}\eta=\partial_{y}\phi\quad\text{ on }S,\\
\nabla_{x,y,z}\phi\to 0\text{ as }z\to-\infty,\end{cases}

where
P
is the Bernoulli constant and
g>0
is the constant acceleration due to gravity. The second equation is Bernoulli’s, which follows from the
pressure being constant along the free surface; the third equation expresses the kinematic boundary condition that particles on the surface remain there;
the last condition asserts that the water is quiescent at great depths. For convenience we will take
P=0
.

In order to reduce the system to the free surface
S
, we introduce the Dirichlet-Neumann operator
G(\eta)

associated to
\Omega
, namely,

(2.3)

(G(\eta)f)(x,y)=\partial_{z}\vartheta(x,y,\eta(x,y))-\nabla_{x,y}\vartheta(x,y,\eta(x,y))\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\eta(x,y),

where
\vartheta(x,y,z)
solves the elliptic problem

(2.4)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x,y,z}\vartheta=0\quad\text{in}~D,\\
\vartheta|_{z=\eta(x,y)}=f(x,y),\quad\nabla_{x,y,z}\vartheta\in L^{2}(D).\end{cases}

We define
\psi
as the trace of the velocity potential on the free surface,
\psi(t,x,y)=\phi(t,x,y,\eta(t,x,y))
. Then, in the moving frame with speed
c\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the gravity water wave system
written in the Zakharov-Craig-Sulem formulation
[
34
,
13
]
is

(2.5)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\eta=c\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\eta+G(\eta)\psi,\\
\partial_{t}\psi=c\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\psi-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x,y}\psi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\big(G(\eta)\psi+\nabla_{x,y}\psi\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\eta\big)^{2}}{1+|\nabla_{x,y}\eta|^{2}}-g\eta,\end{cases}

By a
Stokes wave
we mean a periodic steady solution of (
2.5
), i.e.,

(2.6)

\begin{cases}F_{1}(\eta,\psi,c):=c\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\eta+G(\eta)\psi=0,\\
F_{2}(\eta,\psi,c):=c\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\psi-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla_{x,y}\psi|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\big(G(\eta)\psi+\nabla_{x,y}\psi\cdot\nabla_{x,y}\eta\big)^{2}}{1+|\nabla_{x,y}\eta|^{2}}-g\eta=0,\end{cases}

where
\eta,~\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Consider now a Stokes wave traveling in the
x
-direction.
It is given by a pair of periodic functions
(\eta(x),\psi(x))
independent of
y
and a speed
c=(c_{1},0)
solving (
2.6
). Without loss of generality, we henceforth consider
2\pi
-periodic Stokes waves. As has been known for over a century, there exists a curve of small Stokes waves
parametrized analytically by the small amplitude
\varepsilon
.

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 1.3,
[
5
]
)
.

For any
s>5/2
, there exists
\varepsilon_{St}(s)>0
and a unique family of solutions

\big(\eta(x;\varepsilon),\psi(x;\varepsilon),c(\varepsilon)\big)\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\times(\mathbb{R}\times\{0\})

to the problem (
2.6
), parametrized by
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{St}(s)
, such that

•

(i) the mapping
(-\varepsilon_{St}(s),\varepsilon_{St}(s))\ni\varepsilon\to\big(\eta(\cdot;\varepsilon),\psi(\cdot;\varepsilon),c_{1}(\varepsilon)\big)\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\times H^{s}(\mathbb{T})\times\mathbb{R}
is analytic;

•

(ii)
\eta(\cdot,\varepsilon)
is even and has average zero, and
\psi(\cdot,\varepsilon)
is odd.

The following fourth-order expansion, basically already known by Stokes, will be needed in our proof.
We derive it in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 2.2

(Theorem 1.3,
[
5
]
)
.

The unique family of Stokes waves, given by Theorem
2.1
, has the expansions

(2.7)

\displaystyle\eta(x;\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos x+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\cos(2x)+\varepsilon^{3}\Big\{\frac{1}{8}\cos x+\frac{3}{8}\cos(3x)\Big\}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{4}\left\{\frac{5}{6}\cos(2x)+\frac{1}{3}\cos(4x)\right\}+O(\varepsilon^{5}),

\displaystyle\psi(x,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sqrt{g}\sin x+\frac{\sqrt{g}}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x)+\frac{\sqrt{g}}{4}\varepsilon^{3}\big\{3\sin x\cos(2x)+\sin x\big\}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{4}\sqrt{g}\left\{\frac{5}{12}\sin(2x)+\frac{1}{3}\sin(4x)\right\}+O(\varepsilon^{5}),

\displaystyle c_{1}(\varepsilon)

2.2.
Transverse perturbations, linearization, and transformations

Let us fix a Stokes wave
(\eta^{*},\psi^{*},c^{*})
with amplitude
\varepsilon
and perturb it by a two-dimensional perturbation:

(2.8)

\eta(x,y)=\eta^{*}(x)+\nu\overline{\eta}(x,y),\hskip 10.00002pt\psi(x,y)=\psi^{*}(x)+\nu\overline{\psi}(x,y),\hskip 10.00002pt|\nu|\ll 1.

Before linearizing the dynamics of the full water wave equations using (
2.8
), we recall the shape-derivative formula for the derivative of
G(\eta)\psi
with respect to
\eta
.

Theorem 2.3

(Theorem 3.20,
[
22
]
, and Proposition 2.11,
[
1
]
)
.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. The shape-derivative of
G(\eta)\psi
with respect to
\eta
is denoted by

(2.9)

G^{\prime}(\eta)\overline{\eta}\psi=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\left(G(\eta+h\overline{\eta})\psi-G(\eta)\psi\right).

We have

(2.10)

G^{\prime}(\eta)\overline{\eta}\psi=-G(\eta)(\overline{\eta}B(\eta)\psi)-\cnx(\overline{\eta}V(\eta)\psi),

where

(2.11)

B(\eta)\psi:=\frac{G(\eta)\psi+\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla\psi}{1+|\nabla\eta|^{2}},\hskip 10.00002ptV(\eta)\psi:=\nabla\psi-B\nabla\psi.

Using the formulas (
2.10
), (
2.11
), and the fact that the Stokes wave is independent of the transverse variable
y
, we can
linearize
(
2.6
) around it to obtain

(2.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\eta}=\frac{\delta F_{1}(\eta^{*},\psi^{*},c^{*})}{\delta(\eta,\psi)}(\overline{\eta},\overline{\psi})=\partial_{x}\big((c_{1}^{*}-V^{*})\overline{\eta}\big)+G(\eta^{*})(\overline{\psi}-{B^{*}}\overline{\eta}),

(2.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\overline{\psi}=\frac{\delta F_{2}(\eta^{*},\psi^{*},c^{*},P^{*})}{\delta(\eta,\psi)}(\overline{\eta},\overline{\psi})=(c_{1}^{*}-{V^{*}})\partial_{x}\overline{\psi}+{B^{*}}G(\eta^{*})(\overline{\psi}-{B^{*}}\overline{\eta})-{B^{*}}\partial_{x}{V^{*}}\overline{\eta}-g\overline{\eta},

where

(2.14)

B^{*}:=B(\eta^{*})\psi^{*}=\frac{G(\eta^{*})\psi^{*}+\partial_{x}\psi^{*}\partial_{x}\eta^{*}}{1+|\partial_{x}\eta^{*}|^{2}},\hskip 20.00003ptV^{*}:=V(\eta^{*})\psi^{*}=\partial_{x}\psi^{*}-B^{*}\partial_{x}\eta^{*}.

A similar derivation was done in Lemma 3.2,
[
28
]
for perturbations
\overline{\eta}
and
\overline{\psi}
depending only on
x
.
It is worthwhile noting that in fact
B^{*}
and
V^{*}
are the vertical and horizontal
components, respectively, of the fluid velocity of the Stoke wave at the free surface
\{z=\eta^{*}(x)\}
.
Where
G(\eta^{*})
acts on functions of
(x,y)
we consider
\eta^{*}(x,y)\equiv\eta^{*}(x)
.
We change variables to the so-called
good unknowns
(à la Alinhac)

(2.15)

v_{1}(x,y)=\overline{\eta}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}~v_{2}(x,y)=\overline{\psi}-{B^{*}}\overline{\eta}

satisfying

(2.16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{1}=\partial_{x}\big((c_{1}^{*}-{V^{*}})v_{1}\big)+G(\eta^{*})v_{2},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{2}=-\big(g+({V^{*}}-c_{1}^{*})\partial_{x}{B^{*}}\big)v_{1}+(c_{1}^{*}-{V^{*}})\partial_{x}v_{2}.

Choosing a simple form for the transverse perturbations, we consider perturbations
\overline{\eta}
and
\overline{\psi}
that have wave number
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
in the transverse variable
y
:

(2.18)

\overline{\eta}(x,y)=\widetilde{\eta}(x)e^{i\alpha y},\hskip 10.00002pt\overline{\psi}(x,y)=\widetilde{\psi}(x)e^{i\alpha y}.

Consequently, the good unknowns have the form

(2.19)

v_{1}(x,y)=\widetilde{\eta}(x)e^{i\alpha y}:=\widetilde{v_{1}}(x)e^{i\alpha y},\hskip 10.00002ptv_{2}(x,y)=(\widetilde{\psi}-{B^{*}}\widetilde{\eta})e^{i\alpha y}:=\widetilde{v_{2}}(x)e^{i\alpha y}.

Then, in the linearized system (

1.
In order to calculate
G(\eta^{*})(f_{0}e^{i\alpha y})
for

\begin{cases}\Delta_{X,y,Z}\vartheta=0\quad\text{in }\{Z<\eta^{*}(X)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
\vartheta(X,y,\eta^{*}(X))=f_{0}(X)e^{i\alpha y},\\
\nabla_{X,Z}\vartheta\to 0\quad\text{as }Z\to-\infty.\end{cases}

Of course,
the solution
\vartheta
has the form
\vartheta(X,y,Z)=\theta(X,Z)e^{i\alpha y}
, where
\theta
satisfies

(2.30)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{X,Z}\theta-\alpha^{2}\theta=0\quad\text{in }\{Z<\eta^{*}(X)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},\\
\theta(X,\eta^{*}(X))=f_{0}(X),\\
\nabla_{X,Z}\theta\to 0\quad\text{as }Z\to-\infty.\end{cases}

We use the Riemann mapping
(X,Z)
in Proposition
2.4
to flatten the

\{Z<\eta_{*}(X)\}
. It has the Jacobian

\mathcal{J}=|\partial_{x}X|^{2}+|\partial_{z}X|^{2}.

For an arbitrary function

, we will define function
\Theta
by solving

(2.31)

\begin{cases}\Delta_{x,z}\Theta-\beta\mathcal{J}\Theta=0\quad\text{in }\{(x,z):z<0\},\\
\Theta(x,0)=f(x),\\
\nabla_{x,z}\Theta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}).\end{cases}

In order to specify
f
, we will denote
\Theta=\Theta_{f}
. Then
\theta(X(x,z),Z(x,z))=\Theta_{f_{0}\circ\zeta}(x,z)
is defined on the

\{(x,z):\ z<0\}
.
We note that by virtue of Theorem
2.1
,
\eta^{*}

can be taken as smooth as we wish, and hence so can
\zeta
, provided
\varepsilon
is small enough.

To be precise, we now consider
\varepsilon\in(-\iota,\iota)
where
\iota>0
is sufficiently small so that
\mathcal{J}
is bounded with bounded inverse independent of
\varepsilon
. By classical elliptic regularity, (
2.31
) has a unique solution
\Theta\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})
for any

. Consequently
\Delta_{x,z}\Theta=\beta\mathcal{J}\Theta\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})
. Since both the vector field
\nabla_{x,z}\Theta
and its divergence are in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})
, Proposition
2.6
implies the trace of the normal component
\nabla_{x,z}\Theta(\cdot,0)\cdot(0,1)\equiv\partial_{z}\Theta(\cdot,0)

makes sense in

and

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}\Theta(\cdot,0)\|_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C\Big(\|\Theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}+\|\nabla_{x,z}\Theta\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C(\beta)\|f\|_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})},\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon\in(-\iota,\iota).

Now, if
f\in H^{2}(\mathbb{T})
and
\iota
is chosen smaller if necessary,
we know that
\Theta\in H^{\frac{5}{2}}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})
.
Hence the trace theorem applied to
\partial_{z}\Theta\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-})

yields
\partial_{z}\Theta(\cdot,0)\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
with

(2.33)

\|\partial_{z}\Theta(\cdot,0)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leq C(\beta)\|f\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{T})},\hskip 10.00002pt\varepsilon\in(-\iota,\iota).

In view of (
2.32
) and (

defined by

(2.34)

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\beta}f=\partial_{z}\Theta_{f}(\cdot,0)

for all

and
\varepsilon\in(-\iota,\iota)
.
Using Theorem
2.1
, elliptic regularity,
and the trace theorem for the range

(2.35)

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\beta}\in\mathcal{L}(H^{s}(\mathbb{T}),H^{s-1}(\mathbb{T}))\hskip 10.00002pt\forall s\geq\tfrac{1}{2},~\forall\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0}(s),\varepsilon_{0}(s))

is invertible, as proven for example in Proposition 2.4 in
[
29
]
. For the preceding interpolation argument we have chosen
\varepsilon_{0}(s)=\varepsilon(2)
for all
s\in[\frac{1}{2},2]
. In what follows,
\varepsilon_{0}(s)
may shrink from one line to another.
Additionally, if
\varepsilon=0
, the system (
2.31

2.26
).

Next, we prove (
2.25
). By Proposition
2.4
(i),

is odd in

is also even. Therefore,
\Theta_{\bar{f}(-\cdot)}(x,0)-=\overline{\Theta_{f}(-x,0)}

For the self-adjointness, we fix

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{G}f,m\rangle_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}},H^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}}\nabla\Theta_{f}\cdot\nabla\Theta_{m}dxdz+\int_{\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}_{-}}\beta J\Theta_{m}\Theta_{f}dxdz.

Since the right-hand side is symmetric in

and

, we deduce that

\langle\mathcal{G}f,m\rangle_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}},H^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\langle\mathcal{G}m,f\rangle_{H^{-\frac{1}{2}},H^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

By (

\mathcal{G}f
,
\mathcal{G}m\in L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
, whence

. Thus

.
Then
(\mathcal{G}f,\ell)=(f,\mathcal{G}m)=(\mathcal{G}f,m)

.
Since
\mathcal{G}f
is an arbitrary function in

\ell=m\in H^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,
so that

On the other hand, for

2.3.
The reformulated instability problem

Theorem

allows us to precisely formulate the instability problem as follows.
Starting from the Riemann stretch (
2.20
),

(2.53)

\zeta_{\sharp}f(x)=(f\circ\zeta)(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\zeta_{*}f(x)=\zeta^{\prime}(x)\zeta_{\sharp}f(x).

Then we define the new unknowns

(2.54)

We apply
\zeta_{*}
to (

) and

to (

), and use Theorem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{1}=\partial_{x}\big(p(x)w_{1}\big)+\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon,\beta}w_{2},

)–(

Resonance condition and Kato’s perturbed basis

3.1.
Resonance condition

We begin with the basic case
\varepsilon=0
. Using the expansions (
2.62
) and (
2.63
) for
p
and
q
and the formula (
2.26
) for
\mathcal{G}(0,\beta)
, we find in view of (

(3.1)

\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta}=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x}&(|D|^{2}+\beta)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
-1&\partial_{x}\end{bmatrix}.

The
spectrum of

\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta}
consists of the purely imaginary eigenvalues

(3.2)

\lambda^{0}_{\pm}(k,\beta)=i[k\pm(k^{2}+\beta)^{\frac{1}{4}}],\hskip 10.00002ptk\in\mathbb{Z},

which are the roots of the quadratic characteristic polynomial

(3.3)

\Delta_{0}(\lambda;k,\beta)=(\lambda-ik)^{2}+(k^{2}+\beta)^{\frac{1}{2}}=[\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{+}(k,\beta)][\lambda-\lambda^{0}_{-}(k,\beta)].

We learn from
[
26
]
that the resonance condition is a double eigenvalue

\lambda^{0}_{+}(-(m+1),\beta)=\lambda^{0}_{-}(m,\beta)
. That is,

(3.4)

-(m+1)+((m+1)^{2}+\beta)^{\frac{1}{4}}=m-(m^{2}+\beta)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

For our purposes, we choose the
simplest resonance

m=1
. We define
\beta_{*}\approx 2.7275211479
to be the unique positive solution of

(3.5)

\lambda^{0}_{+}(-2,\beta_{*})=\lambda^{0}_{-}(1,\beta_{*})\iff-2+(\beta_{*}+4)^{\frac{1}{4}}=1-(\beta_{*}+1)^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Given
\beta_{*}
, we also define
\sigma
as

(3.6)

i\sigma=\lambda^{0}_{+}(-2,\beta_{*})=\lambda^{0}_{-}(1,\beta_{*}).

We have

(3.7)

\frac{d}{dk}\left(-i\lambda^{0}_{\pm}(k,\beta)\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\forall|k|\geq 1,

so the functions
\mathbb{Z}\ni k\mapsto-i\lambda^{0}_{\pm}(k,\beta)
are strictly increasing on
(-\infty,-1]
and
[1,\infty)
. In addition, it can be readily checked that

(3.8)

\displaystyle-i\lambda^{0}_{+}(k,\beta)(-1)<-i\lambda^{0}_{+}(0,\beta)<-i\lambda^{0}_{+}(1,\beta),

(3.9)

\displaystyle-i\lambda^{0}_{-}(-1,\beta)(-1)<-i\lambda^{0}_{-}(1,\beta)<-i\lambda^{0}_{-}(0,\beta).

Combining (
3.7
), (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), we deduce that

\begin{cases}\lambda^{0}_{+}(k,\beta_{*})=\lambda^{0}_{+}(-2,\beta_{*})\iff k=-2,\\
\lambda^{0}_{-}(k,\beta_{*})=\lambda^{0}_{+}(1,\beta_{*})\iff k=1.\end{cases}

Consequently

(3.10)

\Delta_{0}(i\sigma;k,\beta_{*})=0\iff k\in\{1,-2\},

and thus
i\sigma
is a
double eigenvalue
of
\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}
. Moreover, it follows from (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
) that
i\sigma
is
isolated
in the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}
. The eigenspace corresponding to
i\sigma
is

(3.11)

\mathcal{U}:=N(\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}-i\sigma\I)=\text{span}\{U_{1},U_{2}\},

where
\I
denotes the identity operator and

(3.12)

U_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}i(\beta_{*}+1)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{ix}\\
e^{ix}\end{bmatrix},\hskip 10.00002ptU_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}-i(\beta_{*}+4)^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-2ix}\\
e^{-2ix}\end{bmatrix}.

For ease of notation, we denote

(3.13)

\gamma_{j}=\Omega(j)^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\beta_{*}+j^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}},

so that

(3.14)

\sigma=1-\gamma_{1}=-2+\gamma_{2}.

3.2.
Kato’s perturbed basis

In order to prove the transverse instability, we will prove that the isolated double eigenvalue
i\sigma
of
\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}
leaves the imaginary axis as
\mathcal{L}_{0,\beta_{*}}
is perturbed to
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
for well-chosen
(\varepsilon,\delta)
near
(0,0)
. This will in turn be achieved by tracking how the eigenspace
\{U_{1},U_{2}\}
is perturbed accordingly. To that end, following Berti
et al

[
4
]
, we use Kato’s similarity transformation
[
20
]

(3.15)

\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}:=\{1-(P_{\varepsilon,\delta}-P_{0,0})^{2}\}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\{P_{\varepsilon,\delta}P_{0,0}+(1-P_{\varepsilon,\delta})(1-P_{0,0})\}.

Here,
P_{\varepsilon,\delta}:(L^{2}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}\to(H^{1}(\mathbb{T}))^{2}
is the spectral projection

(3.16)

P_{\varepsilon,\delta}=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}-\lambda)^{-1}d\lambda,

where
\Gamma
is a closed circle of sufficiently small radius around the isolated double eigenvalue
i\sigma
such that
\Gamma\subset\rho(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta})
for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
close to
(0,0)
. The analyticity (
2.65
) of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
implies that
P_{\varepsilon,\delta}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
are analytic in
(\varepsilon,\delta)
near
(0,0)
. Set

(3.17)

\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}:=\text{Range}(P_{\varepsilon,\delta}).

We will need the following properties:

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta},

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta})\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}~\text{inside~}\Gamma\}=\sigma(\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}|_{\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}}),

(3.20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\mathcal{V}_{0,0},

whose proof can be found in Lemma 3.1 in
[
4
]
.

Since
\mathcal{V}_{0,0}=\text{span}\{U_{1},U_{2}\}
, (
3.20
) implies

(3.21)

\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=\text{span}\{U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{1},U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{2}\},

where

(3.22)

U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{m}=\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}U_{m}\hskip 10.00002pt(m=1,2).

We note that
U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{m}
is analytic in
(\varepsilon,\delta)
near
(0,0)
because
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is so. The property (
3.18
) allows us to reduce the spectral analysis of
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta_{*}+\delta}
to that of a
2\times 2
matrix. To write down the matrix, we first compute the inner products

(3.23)

(JU_{1},U_{2})=(JU_{2},U_{1})=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(JU_{1},U_{1})=-i4\pi\gamma_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt(JU_{2},U_{2})=i4\pi\gamma_{2}.

By Lemma 3.2 in
[
4
]
,
\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
is symplectic,
i.e.

\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}^{*}J\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=J
. It follows that
(J\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}U,\mathcal{K}_{\varepsilon,\delta}V)=(JU,V)
and hence (
3.23
) yields

(3.24)

(JU_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=(JU_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(JU_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=-i4\pi\gamma_{1},\hskip 10.00002pt(JU_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=i4\pi\gamma_{2}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Using our notation
\beta=\beta_{*}+\delta
, the
2\times 2
matrix
that represents the linear operator
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}=J\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon,\beta}:\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}\to\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
(by (
3.18
)) with respect to the basis
\{U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta}\}
is

(3.25)

\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{i}{4\pi\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta})&\frac{i}{4\pi\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta})\\
-\frac{i}{4\pi\gamma_{1}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{1}^{\varepsilon,\delta})&\frac{i}{4\pi\gamma_{2}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta})\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

For any
U\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
, we write
U=a_{1}U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{1}+a_{2}U_{2}^{\varepsilon,\delta}
. Then

(JU,U_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=a_{k}(JU_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta})=i(-1)^{k}a_{k}4\pi\gamma_{k}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for }k=1,2

in view of (
3.24
). Consequently,

U=-i\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{4\pi\gamma_{k}}(JU,U_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta})U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{k}.

For any
U\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}=R(P_{\varepsilon,\delta})
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U\in\mathcal{V}_{\varepsilon,\delta}
since
\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}P_{\varepsilon,\delta}=P_{\varepsilon,\delta}\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}
. Substituting
U=\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{j}
, we have

\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\beta}U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{j}=-i\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{4\pi\gamma_{k}}(J\mathcal{L}_{\varepsilon,\delta}U_{j}^{\varepsilon},U_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta})U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{k}=i\sum_{k=1}^{2}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{4\pi\gamma_{k}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}U_{j}^{\varepsilon,\delta},U_{k}^{\varepsilon,\delta})U^{\varepsilon,\delta}_{k}.

This concludes the proof of (
3.25
).

\displaystyle+|D|\left\{\overline{\eta}|D|(\widetilde{\eta}|D|\psi)\right\}+|D|\left\{\overline{\eta}\partial_{x}(\widetilde{\eta}\partial_{x}\psi)\right\}-|D|\left\{\overline{\eta}\partial_{x}\widetilde{\eta}\partial_{x}\psi\right\}+\partial_{x}\{\overline{\eta}|D|\psi\partial_{x}\widetilde{\eta}\}.

Simplifying, we arrive at

(B.9)

G^{\prime\prime}(0)[\overline{\eta},\widetilde{\eta}]\psi=|D|\{\widetilde{\eta}|D|(\overline{\eta}|D|\psi)\}+|D|\left\{\overline{\eta}|D|(\widetilde{\eta}|D|\psi)\right\}+|D|\left\{\overline{\eta}\widetilde{\eta}\partial^{2}_{x}\psi\right\}+\partial^{2}_{x}\{\overline{\eta}\widetilde{\eta}|D|\psi\}.

B.2.
Expansion of
p

We use (
B.7
) to expand
B^{*}
up to
\varepsilon^{2}
:

(B.10)

\displaystyle B^{*}(x):

\displaystyle=B(\eta^{*})\psi^{*}=B(0)\psi^{*}+B^{\prime}(0)\eta^{*}\psi^{*}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=|D|\psi^{*}-|D|(\eta^{*}|D|\psi^{*})-\partial_{x}(\eta^{*}\partial_{x}\psi^{*})+\partial_{x}\eta^{*}\partial_{x}\psi^{*}+O(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=|D|(\varepsilon\sin x+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x))-|D|\big((\varepsilon\cos x)(\varepsilon\sin x)\big)-\partial_{x}\big((\varepsilon\cos x)(\varepsilon\cos x)\big)

\displaystyle-(\varepsilon\sin x)(\varepsilon\cos x)+O(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon\sin x+\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x))-\varepsilon^{2}\big\{\sin(2x)-\sin(2x)+\frac{1}{2}\sin(2x)\big\}+O(\varepsilon^{3})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\sin x+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x)+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

This implies the expansion for
V^{*}
up to
\varepsilon^{3}
:

(B.11)

\displaystyle V^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\partial_{x}\psi^{*}-\partial_{x}\eta^{*}B^{*}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos x+\varepsilon^{2}\cos(2x)+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon^{3}\big\{3\cos x\cos(2x)-6\sin x\sin(2x)+\cos x\big\}

\displaystyle-\{-\varepsilon\sin x-\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x)\}\{\varepsilon\sin x+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x)\}+O_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos x+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}(1+\cos(2x))+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{8}(5\cos x+3\cos(3x))+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

Regarding
\zeta
, we write

\zeta=x+\varepsilon\zeta^{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}\zeta^{3}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),\hskip 11.74988pt\eta^{*}=\varepsilon\eta^{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\eta^{2}+\varepsilon^{3}\eta^{3}+O(\varepsilon^{4}),

so that by Taylor expansion,

(B.12)

\displaystyle\eta^{*}(\zeta(x))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\eta^{1}(x)+\varepsilon^{2}(\zeta^{1}\partial_{x}\eta^{1}(x)+\eta^{2}(x))

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{3}\left\{\zeta^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\eta^{1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{1}(x))^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}\eta^{1}(x)+\zeta^{1}(x)\partial_{x}\eta^{2}(x)+\eta^{3}(x)\right\}+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

It follows from (
2.21
) that

(B.13)

\zeta(x)=X(x,0)=x-\frac{i}{2\pi}\sum_{k\neq 0}e^{ikx}\mathrm{sign}(k)\widehat{\eta^{*}\circ\zeta}(k).

We equate the coefficients of
\varepsilon^{j}
on both sides to determine
\zeta^{j}
. It was already found in
[
28
]
that
\zeta^{1}(x)=\sin x
and
\zeta^{2}(x)=\sin(2x)
. At the order
\varepsilon^{3}
we have

\displaystyle\zeta^{3}(x)=-\frac{i}{2\pi}\sum_{k\neq 0}e^{ikx}\mathrm{sign}(k)\widehat{g}(k),

where

\displaystyle g(x)

\displaystyle=\zeta^{2}(x)\partial_{x}\eta^{1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{1}(x))^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}\eta^{1}(x)+\zeta^{1}(x)\partial_{x}\eta^{2}(x)+\eta^{3}(x)=-\cos x+\frac{3}{2}\cos(3x).

Direct calculations give
\zeta^{3}(x)=-\sin x+\frac{3}{2}\sin(3x)
and thus

(B.14)

\zeta(x)=x+\varepsilon\sin x+\varepsilon^{2}\sin(2x)+\varepsilon^{3}\big(-\sin x+\frac{3}{2}\sin(3x)\big)+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

Inserting (
B.14
) back in (
B.12
) gives

(B.15)

\eta^{*}(\zeta(x))=\varepsilon\cos x+\varepsilon^{2}\big(\cos(2x)-\frac{1}{2}\big)+\varepsilon^{3}\big(\frac{3}{2}\cos(3x)-\cos x\big)+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

Using (
B.12
) with
V^{*}
in place of
\eta^{*}
we obtain

(B.16)

\displaystyle V^{*}(\zeta(x))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon V^{1}(x)+\varepsilon^{2}(\zeta^{1}\partial_{x}V^{1}(x)+V^{2}(x))

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{3}\left\{\zeta^{2}(x)\partial_{x}V^{1}(x)+\frac{1}{2}(\zeta^{1}(x))^{2}\partial^{2}_{x}V^{1}(x)+\zeta^{1}(x)\partial_{x}V^{2}(x)+V^{3}(x)\right\}+O(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos x+\varepsilon^{2}\cos(2x)+\varepsilon^{3}\left\{\sin(2x)(-\sin x)+\frac{1}{2}\sin^{2}x(-\cos x)+\sin x(-\sin(2x))\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{5}{8}\cos x+\frac{3}{8}\cos(3x)\right\}+O(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos x+\varepsilon^{2}\cos(2x)+\varepsilon^{3}\big(-\frac{1}{2}\cos x+\frac{3}{2}\cos(3x)\big)+O(\varepsilon^{4}).

Combining (

) and (
