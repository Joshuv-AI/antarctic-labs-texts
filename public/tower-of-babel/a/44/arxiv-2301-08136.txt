Input-Output Analysis: New Results From Markov Chain Theory (To appear in Economie Appliquée Review)
Nizar Riane, Claire David

Abstract
In this work, we propose a new lecture of input-output model reconciliation Markov chain and the dominance theory, in the field of interindustrial poles interactions. A deeper lecture of Leontieff table in term of Markov chain is given, exploiting spectral properties and time to absorption to characterize production processes, then the dualities local-global/dominance-Sensitivity analysis are established, allowing a better understanding of economic poles arrangement. An application to the Moroccan economy is given.

1
Introduction

In his seminal work of 1967, C. Ponsard
[
Pon67
]
proposed to apply graph theory to the analysis of interregional economic flows. Five years later, R. Lantner
[
Lan72
]
introduced his economic dominance theory based on Leontieff input-output model, where interdependencies are at stake. Under the Leontieff prism, for instance, a translation (of an interaction) enables one to measure the vulnerability of each patner.

The economic dominance theory makes use of input-output matrices, the coefficients of which correspond to quantitative measures of dependence versus interdependance. One can make a connection between minimal values of the determinant of those matrices, and situations of complete autarky,while maximal values correspond to a situation of perfect dominance. Yet, one thus misses local tools, which would enable one to better understand constantly changing situations.

In 1982, an analogy was made by B. Peterson and M. Olinick in
[
PO82
]
between Leontieff models and Markov chains, by considering the input-output matrix as a transition probability one. Thus, a stationary production vector is related to the case of a closed (no final expenditure) model, while a productivity condition occurs in the situation of open model. The analysis brought by the authors contain significant algebraic results, but the economic implications are not so clear ; moreover, the link between Markov chains and input-output analysis is not completely exploited.

Recently, D. Lebert
[
Leb19
]
generalized this theory to such fields as: international trade in industrial goods, the productive structuring of companies in the United States
and in Western Europe, the economics of innovation and territorial cognitive dynamics.

We presently propose to revisit the input-output model as a Markov chains process. We then establish local results, in term of sensitivity analysis. Our study relies on the same assumptions that can be found in the work of R. Lantner
[
Lan72
]
:

i
.

Homogeneity of poles activity.

ii
.

No substitution phenomena.

iii
.

Constant returns to scale.

Our results establish a close relationship between
dominance phenomena
and
spillover effects
.

2
Structural analysis : the economic dominance school

The theory of dominance, as a branch of structural analysis theory, is based on influence graphs. By representing the interaction web between economic poles, one can thus study the interdependence between poles by extracting, from the resulting graph, the intrinsic underlying information.

Starting from the following observation
[
Lan72
]
: “The importance of a transaction between a supplier and a requestor is measured less by its absolute value than by the degree of vulnerability it implies for one or the other”, a global measure of dominance might, first, be deduced from the influence graph.

Further developments can be reached then: according to R. Lantner
[
Lan72
]
, the macroscopic analysis of the structure as a whole can be achieved by means of the determinant of the input-output matrix. The resulting analysis yields structural indicators, such as: autarky (or independence), hierarchy (or dependence), circularity (or interdependence).

In
[
Lan15
]
, the author distinguishes three ways to design dominance analysis:

i
.

By respecting the linearity assumption, one can dissociate the direct effect of dominance from indirect ones, and specify the importance of one in relation to the other, as established b R. Lantner and D. Lebert in
[
LL15
]
. The stability assumption thus makes it possible to forecast the development of the activity of divisions for all the moments to come.

ii
.

The second approach consists in selecting a certain number of indicators associated to the arrangement of the structure. For instance, it was shown in
[
LD01
]
that the more numerous and more intense were the short circuits in a structure, the weaker was the determinant of its representative matrix. In
[
LY15
]
, the authors exploit this property to construct and analyze the changes induced on a small number of global indicators by the elimination of some studied poles.

iii
.

The third approach exploits properties of Boolean matrices, associated with graphs valuable for studying multiple properties of sets. In
[
HEYZ15
]
, H. El Younsi et al. thus analyze the matrix of the world’s largest firms holding blocks of technological knowledge, which enable them to enlighten complex multinational strategies associated to the web of patents.

4
Input-output model : a Markov chain formulation

We now consider an economy with
n\,\in\,{\mathbb{N}}^{\star}
poles, where, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, the production of the
i^{th}
pole is denoted by
X_{i}
, while its final consumption is
Y_{i}
. The production is shared, with intermediary consumption
x_{ij}
between the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}
poles,
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
. The input-output system can be then written as:

\forall\,(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}\,:\quad X_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ij}=Y_{i}\\

Such a design is called
direct orientation
of the flow. The
indirect
one is obtained by considering the origin (supply) instead of the destination (demand):

\forall\,(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}\,:\quad X_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{ji}=W_{i}

where
W_{i}
denotes the added value of the
i^{th}
pole.

For
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
, we respectively define the
technical
and
trade
coefficients, as:

\theta_{ij}=\displaystyle\frac{x_{ij}}{X_{j}}\quad,\quad\alpha_{ij}=\displaystyle\frac{x_{ij}}{X_{i}}

One may then rewrite the model under the following matrix form:

\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{Y}\quad,\quad\mathbf{X}^{T}-\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{W}^{T}\\

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}=\left(\begin{matrix}\theta_{11}&\ldots&\theta_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\ldots&\theta_{nn}\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad\mathbf{A}=\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{11}&\ldots&\alpha_{1n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\alpha_{n1}&\ldots&\alpha_{nn}\\
\end{matrix}\right),\quad\mathbf{X}=\left(\begin{matrix}X_{1}\\
\vdots\\
X_{n}\end{matrix}\right),\quad\mathbf{Y}=\left(\begin{matrix}Y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
Y_{n}\end{matrix}\right),\quad\mathbf{W}=\left(\begin{matrix}W_{1}\\
\vdots\\
W_{n}\end{matrix}\right)

Let us set:

Y=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}

Y
represents the total final expenditure, while
W
stands for the total added value.

Let us then introduce the following augmented matrices:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{\Theta}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{w}^{T}&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}{W_{1}}/{X_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
{W_{n}}/{X_{n}}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
W\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{Y}\\
-W\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}{Y_{1}}/{X_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
{Y_{n}}/{X_{n}}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
Y\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{W}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{W}\\
-Y\end{matrix}\right)

This yields:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{X}}-\tilde{\mathbf{\Theta}}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
W\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{\Theta}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{w}^{T}&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
W\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
W\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{X}+W\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
W\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{\Theta}\mathbf{X}\\
2W\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}

and:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}-\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}&Y\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}&Y\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{y}\\
\mathbf{0}&1\\
\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}&Y\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}&\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}+Y\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}&Y\end{matrix}\right)-\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{A}&2Y\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{W}}^{T}

This artifact enables one to transform the model into an
absorbing Markov chain
with
n+1
states, where
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
play the role of the transition matrices (one may note that
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}^{T}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
are stochastic matrices,while
\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}
and
\mathbf{A}
are substochastic ones).

Remark 4.1
.

It is possible to obtain the final expenditure related to each pole, but with no significant interest in our situation. In the case of the indirect scheme, the matrices have the form:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{A}&\mathbb{Y}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\\
\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}{Y_{1}}/{X_{1}}&&0\\
&\ddots&\\
0&&{Y_{n}}/{X_{n}}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{X}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{X}\\
\mathbf{Y}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{W}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{W}\\
-\mathbf{Y}\end{matrix}\right)

By construction, the substochastic matrices
\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}
and
\mathbf{A}
are such that:

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Theta}_{ij}=1-\mathbf{w}_{j}\leqslant 1\quad\text{and}\quad 0\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ij}=1-\mathbf{y}_{i}\leqslant 1

It follows from Theorem
3.8
that:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\left(1-\mathbf{w}_{j}\right)\leqslant\rho(\mathbf{\Theta})\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}\left(1-\mathbf{w}_{j}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\left(1-\mathbf{y}_{i}\right)\leqslant\rho(\mathbf{A})\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\left(1-\mathbf{y}_{i}\right)

We have thus proved the following result:

Corollary 4.1
.

One has:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}=\lim_{k\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbf{\Theta}^{k}=\mathbf{0}

and:

\mathbf{O}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{\Theta}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{\Theta}^{k}\geqslant 0\quad,\quad\mathbf{N}=\left(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}\geqslant 0

Remark 4.2
.

The spectrum of the block triangular matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
(resp.
\widehat{\mathbf{\Theta}}
) is the same as the one of
\mathbf{A}
(resp.
\mathbf{\Theta}
) and
1
.

Remark 4.3
.

Let us consider
the intermediary expenditures matrix

\mathbf{\Omega}
, and the diagonal one
\mathbb{X}
such that:

\forall\,i\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\,:\quad\mathbb{X}_{ii}=X_{i}

One has then:

\mathbf{\Theta}=\mathbf{\Omega}\,\mathbb{X}^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{A}=\mathbb{X}^{-1}\,\mathbf{\Omega}

One might thus deduce that the direct and the indirect schemes have the same connection and spectral properties. Indeed, given an eigenvalue-eigenvector couple
(\lambda,v)
of
\mathbf{\Theta}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Theta}v

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbb{X}^{-1}v=\lambda v

Multiplication of each side by
\mathbb{X}^{-1}
yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}^{-1}\mathbf{\Theta}v

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbb{X}^{-1}\mathbf{\Omega}\right)\left(\mathbb{X}^{-1}v\right)=\mathbf{A}\left(\mathbb{X}^{-1}v\right)=\lambda\left(\mathbb{X}^{-1}v\right)

which means that
(\lambda,\mathbb{X}^{-1}\,v)
is the corresponding eigenvalue-eigenvector couple of
\mathbf{A}
.

4.1
Dual interpretation

Let us consider again our economy with
n\,\in\,{\mathbb{N}}^{\star}
poles: for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, the production flow
X_{i}
of the
i^{th}
pole is equivalent to a monetary flow in the opposite direction (demand)
M^{d}_{i}
. For
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
, the intermediary expenditure
x_{ij}
induces an intermediate monetary supply
m_{ji}
, while the final consumption
Y_{i}
induces a final monetary supply
K_{i}
. The dual monetary input-output system can thus be written as:

\forall\,(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}\,:\quad M^{d}_{i}-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{ji}=K_{i}\\

For
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
, we define the
monetary supply coefficients
:

\gamma_{ij}=\displaystyle\frac{m_{ij}}{M^{d}_{i}}

One gets then the
dual monetary problem
in term of monetary flows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M^{d}}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{G})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{T}

where

\forall\,(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}\,:\quad\gamma_{ij}=\displaystyle\frac{m_{ij}}{M^{d}_{i}}=\displaystyle\frac{x_{ji}}{X_{i}}=\theta_{ji}

which means that
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{\Theta}^{T}
. One may then write:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M^{d}}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{\Theta}^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{T}

In particular, the matrix
\mathbf{G}
is substochastic.

5
Sensitivity analysis

Using the fundamental matrices
\mathbf{O}
and
\mathbf{N}
, one has:

\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{O}\mathbf{Y}\quad,\quad\mathbf{X}^{T}=\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{N}

where:

\forall\,(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}\,:\quad\mathbf{X}_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{O}_{ij}\mathbf{Y}_{j}\quad,\quad\mathbf{X}_{j}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{W}_{i}\mathbf{N}_{ij}

which yield:

\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{j}}=\mathbf{O}_{ij}\quad,\quad\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{W}_{i}}=\mathbf{N}_{ij}

In the monetary sphere, one may write:

\mathbf{M^{d}}^{T}=\mathbf{K}^{T}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{G})^{-1}\\
=\mathbf{Y}^{T}\mathbf{Q}

For
j\,\in\,\{1,\ldots,n\}
, one has:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M^{d}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\mathbf{Q}_{ij}

In particular:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{M^{d}}_{j}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}_{ij}

From the Markov chains theory, one knows that
\mathbf{O}^{T}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{N}_{ij}
and
\mathbf{Q}_{ij}
represent the expected number of visits to
j
starting from
i
. In the specific case of the indirect scheme, may one compute the expected time

\mathbf{t}_{i}

related to the production of the
i^{th}
pole before been absorbed by final demand, one gets:

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{t}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{t}_{n}\end{matrix}\right)=\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{1}

which is not more than production process duration vector of each pole.

5.1
Long run effects and relaxation time

In the first section, we have introduced the relaxation time as the inverse of the spectral gap, which is the difference between the absolute values of the smallest and the greatest eigenvalue
\lambda^{\star}
.

May one consider the indirect scheme, it follows from remark
4.1
that the maximal spectral radius of
\mathbf{A}
is:

\lambda^{\star}=\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}(1-y_{i})

One can thus question the situation where
\lambda^{\star}=1
.

In an economy with no final expenditure, each pole shares its production with the other ones, the final expenditure state is thus connected to itself with probability
1
. The transition matrix rank is
2
, with a basis of two eigenvectors for the eigenvalue
1
, corresponding to two recurrent classes. The spectral gap is thus minimized (null). This situation is an utopia.

In the situation of non-null final expenditure, let
\mathbf{v}
be the non-negative eigenvector with respect to the spectral radius of the substochastic matrix
\mathbf{A}
, as defined in theorem
3.8
. The vector
\mathbf{v}
cannot be constant unless:

\forall\,i\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\,:\quad(1-y_{i})=(1-y)=\lambda^{\star}

in which case:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=v\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ij}=\lambda^{\star}v

In the situation where
\mathbf{v}
is not constant, let us set:

i^{\star}=\arg\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}(1-y_{i})\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{i}=\arg\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mathbf{v}_{i}

One has then:

i
.

If
i^{\star}\neq\overline{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j}<\lambda^{\star}\mathbf{v}_{\overline{i}}

since
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\right)<1
, which contradict the spectral identity.

ii
.

If
i^{\star}=\overline{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\lambda^{\star}\mathbf{v}_{\overline{i}}

which is not possible for a convex combination unless:

\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}\overline{i}}=\lambda^{\star}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{A}_{\overline{i}j}=0\quad\text{for }j\neq\overline{i}

This result describes an
almost-infinite loop
. This corresponds to an economy where the pole with the least final expenditures rate is in (supply) autarky. The production is then reinjected in the system, and creates spillover effects, while the speed of absorption by the final expenditure is at the slowest level. The pole at stake is the
transformation
one.

Let us consider now the situation where the whole production is consumed as final expenditure. The transition matrix
\mathbf{P}
will have rank
1
, with one eigenvector of the eigenvalue
1
corresponding to the absorbing class, the other eigenvalues are null and the spectral gap will be maximized (equals to
1
). This situation is also an utopia.

With non-zero final expenditure, we proceed in the same way as before. We set

\lambda^{\star}=\displaystyle\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}(1-y_{i})

and:

i_{\star}=\displaystyle\arg\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant n}(1-y_{i})\quad,\quad\underline{i}=\arg\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mathbf{v}_{i}

One has then:

i
.

If
i_{\star}\neq\underline{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\right)\mathbf{v}_{j}>\lambda^{\star}\mathbf{v}_{\underline{i}}

since
\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\right)>1
, contradiction.

ii
.

If
i_{\star}=\underline{i}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}}{\lambda^{\star}}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\lambda^{\star}\mathbf{v}_{\underline{i}}

which is not possible for a convex combination unless
\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}\underline{i}}=\lambda^{\star}
and

\mathbf{A}_{\underline{i}j}=0
for
j\neq\underline{i}
.

To ensure that, for the other eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
,
i=2,\ldots,n
, one has:

\lambda_{i}\leqslant\lambda^{\star}

we apply the maximal spectral radius resonating to the matrix resulting from
\mathbf{A}
by suppressing the line and the column
\underline{i}
, this matrix is substochastic, so we can apply Perron-Frobenius theorem
3.8
for non-negative matrices to get the condition

\displaystyle\min_{i\neq\underline{i}}\sum_{j\neq\underline{i}}\mathbf{A}_{ij}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda^{\star}

This solution describes an
almost-pyramidal structure
, where the pole
i_{\star}
of the highest final expenditure rate shares the remaining production proportion
\lambda^{\star}
with himself, one of the other poles at least shares a production proportion not exceeding
\lambda^{\star}
, the surplus
(1-y_{i})-\lambda^{\star}
goes to the pole
i_{\star}
. The spillover effects in this situation is minimized. We will call this pole the
outlet pole
.

A particular case emerges when the production is fairly shared with the other poles. For
(i,j)\,\in\,\{1,\ldots,n\}^{2}
, the
j^{th}
pole shares a production
\displaystyle\frac{(1-y_{i})}{n}
with the the
i^{th}
one The matrix
\mathbf{A}
is singular with rank
1
, and its spectrum contains only one non-zero eigenvalue, corresponding to:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-y_{i})}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\lambda^{\star}\mathbf{v}_{i}

Summation over
i
yields:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{ij}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-y_{i})}{n}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{v}_{j}=\lambda^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{v}_{j}

\displaystyle\lambda^{\star}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-y_{i})}{n}

This is a situation of
fair division
, the connection between poles is maximal, no pole gets a special treatment.

5.2
Marginal effects and fundamental matrix

Let us consider
(i,j)\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}
. We recall the marginal effects identity:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{O}_{ij}

or, expressed in terms of an infinite sum

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{\Theta}^{k}

The minimal marginal effect is:

\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\delta_{ii}(ij)

This corresponds to a situation where there is no walk from
i
to
j
. In other words, the output of pole
i
never integer the production process of pole
j
.

The maximal marginal effect is:

\mathbf{O}_{ii}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(1-y_{i})^{k}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{i}}\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbf{\Theta}_{ij}=(1-y_{i})\,\delta_{ii}(ij)

and :

\mathbf{O}_{ij}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}(1-y_{i})\,(1-y_{j})^{k}=\displaystyle\frac{(1-y_{i})}{y_{j}}\quad\text{for $j\neq i$ if $\mathbf{\Theta}_{ij}=(1-y_{i})$, $\mathbf{\Theta}_{jj}=(1-y_{j})$ and $\mathbf{\Theta}_{ik}=\mathbf{\Theta}_{jk}=0$ for $k\neq j$}

the output of pole
i
is totally used in the production of pole
j
, and pole
j
is in autarky.

5.3
Production cycle

The production process duration is defined by means of the expected time before absorption vector:

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathbf{t}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{t}_{n}\end{matrix}\right)=\mathbf{N}\,\mathbf{1}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A})^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}

By analogy with previous section, the minimal value:

\displaystyle\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mathbf{t}_{i}

with non-zero final expenditure, is reached when the whole output of the
i^{th}
pole is destined to the outlet one in autarky
i_{\star}
. One has then:

\underline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}=\displaystyle 1+\frac{(1-y_{i})}{y_{i_{\star}}}\quad\text{if }i\neq i_{\star}\quad\text{or }\underline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{i_{\star}}}\text{ if }i=i_{\star}

In the same way, the maximal value of
\mathbf{t}_{i}
,
i=,\ldots,n
, is reached when all the output of the
i^{th}
pole is destined to the transformation pole
i^{\star}
in autarky, in which case:

\overline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}=\displaystyle 1+\frac{(1-y_{i})}{y_{i^{\star}}}\quad\text{ if }i\neq i^{\star}

or

\overline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{y_{i^{\star}}}\quad\text{ if }i=i^{\star}

The proof is obtained by induction, since:
\mathbf{A}^{k}=\mathbf{A}^{k-1}\mathbf{A}
, and:

\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{I}+\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}

One has thus:

\displaystyle(1-y_{i})(1-y_{i_{\star}})\leqslant(1-y_{i})\,\min_{1\leqslant l\leqslant n}\mathbf{A}_{lj}\leqslant\mathbf{A}^{2}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\mathbf{A}_{il}\,\mathbf{A}_{lj}

\displaystyle=(1-y_{i})\,\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\mathbf{A}_{il}}{(1-y_{i})}\right)\mathbf{A}_{lj}

\displaystyle\leqslant(1-y_{i})\,\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant n}\mathbf{A}_{lj}\leqslant(1-y_{i})(1-y_{i^{\star}})

and

\displaystyle\frac{1-y_{i}}{y_{i^{\star}}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{N}_{ij}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{A}^{k}_{ij}\leqslant\frac{1-y_{i}}{y_{i_{\star}}}

In order to quantify the relative duration of a production process
i
, we introduce, for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, the ratio

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\Delta\mathbf{t}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{t}_{i}-\underline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}}{\overline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}-\underline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}}\leqslant 1

5.4
Discussion

The analysis toolbox presented in this section considers the input-output model from two point of view: a first one, through a
global vision
based on the spectral radius and the relaxation time, measuring the spillover effect and the return time to equilibrium ; a second one, through a
local vision
defined by the marginal effects and the time to absorption, measuring the interactions between poles and the production processes duration.

The three measures are deeply related: the almost-infinite loop structure describes a maximal spillover effect induced by the maximal spectral radius. Such a situation corresponds to the longest time to absorption, and the maximum self-marginal effect for the transformation pole.

The almost-pyramidal structure is produced when the spillover effect is minimal, with a spectral radius minimal too. In this case, the absorption time reaches its minimum for the outlet pole.

One may summarize those implications as follows:

Almost-infinite loop

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\forall\,i\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\,:\quad\mathbf{t}_{i^{\star}}=\overline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}\Leftrightarrow\max_{i,j}\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{i}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{j}}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{i^{\star}}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{i^{\star}}}=\frac{1}{y_{i^{\star}}}

Almost-pyramidal structure

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\forall\,i\,\in\,\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\,:\quad\mathbf{t}_{i_{\star}}=\underline{\mathbf{t}_{i}}\Leftrightarrow\frac{\partial\mathbf{X}_{i_{\star}}}{\partial\mathbf{Y}_{i_{\star}}}=\frac{1}{y_{i_{\star}}}

7
Conclusion

This paper had a threefold purpose. First, we aimed at introducing a local measure of dominance based on the spectral properties of the input-output matrix. This has highlighted a local-global duality, in so far as the determinant is nothing more than the product of the eigenvalues.

Second, we wanted to reconcile sensitivity analysis and dominance theory - which was not possible without a local measure of dominance.

Third, we wished to give a new lecture of the input-output matrix in term of Markov chains, with a deeper interpretation of the underlying dynamics. One must of course bear in mind that the classical lecture is reduced to the analysis of mechanical transition, and does not enable one to determine the intrinsic and fundamental properties of the production process: interdependency and long run effects.

With respect to Lantner’s classification evoked at the beginning of our study, we thus place ourselves in the line of the first category of contributions, where we plan to bring further developments.
