The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm and the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick Model at Infinite Size
Edward Farhi, Jeffrey Goldstone, Sam Gutmann, Leo Zhou

Abstract
The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is a general-purpose algorithm for combinatorial optimization problems whose performance can only improve with the number of layers p p . While QAOA holds promise as an algorithm that can be run on near-term quantum computers, its computational power has not been fully explored. In this work, we study the QAOA applied to the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model, which can be understood as energy minimization of n n spins with all-to-all random signed couplings. There is a recent classical algorithm [ 1 ] that, assuming a widely believed conjecture, can efficiently find an approximate solution for a typical instance of the SK model to within ( 1 − ϵ ) (1-\epsilon) times the ground state energy. We hope to match its performance with the QAOA. Our main result is a novel technique that allows us to evaluate the typical-instance energy of the QAOA applied to the SK model. We produce a formula for the expected value of the energy, as a function of the 2 ​ p 2p QAOA parameters, in the infinite size limit that can be evaluated on a computer with O ⁡ ( 16 p ) O(16^{p}) complexity. We evaluate the formula up to p = 12 p=12 , and find that the QAOA at p = 11 p=11 outperforms the standard semidefinite programming algorithm. Moreover, we show concentration: With probability tending to one as n → ∞ n\to\infty , measurements of the QAOA will produce strings whose energies concentrate at our calculated value. As an algorithm running on a quantum computer, there is no need to search for optimal parameters on an instance-by-instance basis since we can determine them in advance. What we have here is a new framework for analyzing the QAOA, and our techniques can be of broad interest for evaluating its performance on more general problems where classical algorithms may fail.

Introduction

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm
[
2
]
, QAOA, consists of a shallow depth quantum circuit with
p
layers and
2p
parameters. It is designed to find approximate solutions to combinatorial search problems, and like simulated annealing can be applied to almost any problem. As
p

p=1
, worst case performance guarantees have been established
[
2
,
3

. These beat random guessing but not the best classical algorithms. Aside from worst case we can ask how the QAOA performs on typical instances where the problem instances are drawn from a specified distribution. For example, for the combinatorial search problem E3LIN2, a worst case performance guarantee was established but the typical performance is better
[
3
]
. For similar results on other problems see Lin and Zhu
[
4

.

A key insight for typical instances, drawn from a specified distribution, is the landscape independence
[
5
]
of the expected value of the cost function in the parameter dependent quantum state: Given an instance of a combinatorial search problem and a set of control parameters, the QAOA quantum state depends both on the instance and the parameters. However for large systems, the expected value of the cost function in the quantum state depends on the parameters but not the instance, up to finite size effects. This means that for each value of
p
, the optimal parameters are the same for typical instances coming from the distribution. Although the optimal parameters may be known, one still needs to run the quantum computer for each instance to find a string which achieves the optimal cost function value attainable at a given
p
.

In this paper, we introduce a novel technique for evaluating the typical-instance energy of the QAOA applied to a combinatorial search problem for arbitrary fixed
p
as the problem size goes to infinity.
We apply the QAOA to the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model.
This model can be seen as a combinatorial search problem on a complete graph with random couplings. For typical instances, the value of the lowest energy is known
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9

.
Assuming a widely believed conjecture about the model, there is also a recent polynomial-time classical algorithm for finding a string whose energy is between

[

.
What we have accomplished is a method for calculating, in advance, what energy the QAOA will produce for given parameters at any fixed

We also show concentration,

This concentration result implies landscape independence for this model, similar to what was found for bounded degree graphs in

This means that optimal parameters found for one large instance will also be good for other large instances.
The complexity of our calculation scales as

Without much trouble, we have found optimal parameters up to

2
The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)

We start by reviewing the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA).
Given a classical cost function
C({\boldsymbol{z}})
defined on
n
-bit strings
{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})\in\{+1,-1\}^{n}
, the QAOA is a quantum algorithm that aims to find a string
{\boldsymbol{z}}
such that
C({\boldsymbol{z}})
is close to its absolute minimum.
The cost function
C
can be written as an operator that is diagonal in the computational basis, defined as

C\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}}=C({\boldsymbol{z}})\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}}.

(1)

We introduce a unitary operator that depends on
C
and a parameter
\gamma

U(C,\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma C}.

(2)

Additionally, we introduce the operator

B=\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j},

(3)

where
X_{j}
is the Pauli
X
operator acting on qubit
j
, and an associated unitary operator that depends on a parameter
\beta

U(B,\beta)=e^{-i\beta B}=\prod_{j=1}^{n}e^{-i\beta X_{j}}.

(4)

In the QAOA circuit, we initialize the system of qubits in the state

\ket{s}=\ket{+}^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}},

(5)

and alternately apply
p
layers of
U(C,\gamma)
and
U(B,\beta)
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{p}
; then
the QAOA circuit prepares the following state

\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=U(B,\beta_{p})U(C,\gamma_{p})\cdots U(B,\beta_{1})U(C,\gamma_{1})\ket{s}.

(6)

For a given cost function
C
, the associated QAOA objective function is

\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

(7)

By measuring the quantum state
\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
in the computational basis, one will find a bit string
{\boldsymbol{z}}
such that
C({\boldsymbol{z}})
is near (
7
) or better.
Different strategies have been developed to find optimal
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
for any given instance
[
5
,
10
,
11
]
.
In this paper, however, we will show how to find, in advance, the optimal parameters of the QAOA at fixed
p
for typical instances of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model.

3
The Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (SK) model

In this paper, we apply the QAOA to instances of the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, which is central to many important results in the theory of spin glass and disordered systems (see
[
7
]
for a review).
This model describes a classical spin system with all-to-all couplings between the
n
spins.
The classical cost function is

C({\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}z_{j}z_{k}.

(8)

Each instance is specified by the
J_{jk}
’s which are independently chosen from a distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.
For example they could be
+1
or
-1
each with probability 1/2, or drawn from the standard normal distribution.
In this paper, we assume
J_{jk}
comes from a symmetrical distribution (i.e.,
J_{jk}
and
-J_{jk}
have the same distribution), although we believe generalization to any subgaussian distribution is not too difficult.
As
n
goes to infinity, the classical results as well as our quantum results are independent of which distribution the
J_{jk}
’s come from. In a celebrated result, Parisi calculated the lowest energy of (
8
) for typical instances as
n
goes to infinity. The first step is to consider the partition function

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\exp(-C({\boldsymbol{z}})/T)

(9)

which is dominated by the low energy configurations for low temperature
T
. Averaging this quantity over the
J_{jk}
’s is easy but gives the wrong description at low temperature. One needs to first take the log and then average:

\frac{T}{n}\EV_{J}\Big[\log\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\exp(-C({\boldsymbol{z}})/T)\Big)\Big].

(10)

In the limit of
n\to\infty
, this quantity has been proven to exist, although evaluating this quantity is extremely challenging. Using the method of replica symmetry breaking, Parisi
[
6
]
provides a formula (for proof, see
[
7
]
) that, when numerically evaluated
[
8
,
9
,
1
]
, shows for typical instances,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\min_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}\frac{C({\boldsymbol{z}})}{n}=-0.763166\ldots.

(11)

We note that this model has a phase transition at
T=1
, and that at this temperature,
C/n
is
-0.5
for typical instances.

The result just given tells us what the lowest energy of the SK model is, but it does not tell us how to find the associated configuration.
How hard is this?
Simulated annealing does rather well in getting a string whose energy is close to the minimum.
For example running simulated annealing starting at a temperature above the phase transition, say
T=1.3
, and bringing it down to near
T=0
in 10 million steps on a 10000 bit instance achieves
-0.754
.
It is not believed that simulated annealing can go all the way to the lowest energy configuration
[
12
]
.
We can also ask how zero-temperature simulated annealing performs.
Here the update rule is flip a spin if it lowers the energy, and don’t flip if it does not.
This algorithm comes down to around
-0.71
.

Another natural approach to solve this problem is a convex relaxation method such as semidefinite programming. The simplest such method is called spectral relaxation, where the solution is obtained by taking the lowest eigenvector of the
n\times n
matrix
\boldsymbol{J}
(whose elements are given by
J_{jk}
), and rounding each entry to
\pm 1
based on its sign. This yields
C/n=-2/\pi+o(1)\approx-0.6366

[
13
]
, where
o(1)
is a number that vanishes as
n\to\infty
. The standard semidefinite programming algorithm with “sign rounding” yields the same result
[
14
,
15
]
.

Recently, assuming a widely believed yet unproven conjecture that the SK model has “full replica symmetry breaking,” Montanari gave an algorithm
[
1
]
that finds a string whose energy is below
(1-\epsilon)

times the lowest energy for typical instances. The run time of this algorithm is
c(\epsilon)n^{2}
, and the function
c(\epsilon)
is an inverse polynomial of
\epsilon
.

4
Summary of Results

We analyze how the QAOA performs on the SK model by evaluating

\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]

(12)

in the infinite size
n\to\infty
limit.
Note that both
C
and
\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
depend on
J
.
Our main result, which will be proved in Section
6
, is the following:

Theorem
.

For any
p
and any parameters
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}),

(13)

where
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
has an explicit formula that we give below in Eq. (
33
).
Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]=[V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})]^{2}.

(14)

Note the above theorem easily implies the following corollary which shows that the QAOA exhibits strong concentration properties:

Corollary

(Concentration)
.

At any fixed
p
and for any parameters
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
, the energy produced by the QAOA on almost every instance of the SK model
concentrates
at
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
in the limit as
n\to\infty
.
Specifically, we have
concentration over instances
, i.e., for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{J}\left(\Big|\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}-V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})\Big|>\epsilon\right)\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty.

(15)

We also have
concentration over measurements
, i.e., for every
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\Big[\mathbb{P}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}\textnormal{ measured from }\ket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\left(\Big|C({\boldsymbol{z}})/n-\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big|>\epsilon\right)\Big]\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty.

(16)

Proof of Corollary.

Combining (
13
) and (
14
), we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\EV_{J}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}]-\EV_{J}^{2}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}]\right)=0.

(17)

Since for any operator
O
,

\displaystyle\braket{O}^{2}\leq\braket{O^{2}},

(18)

we have

\displaystyle\EV_{J}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}]-\EV_{J}^{2}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}]

\displaystyle\leq\EV_{J}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}]-\EV_{J}^{2}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}].

(19)

Note that as
n\to\infty
, the right hand side vanishes, which implies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\EV_{J}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}]-\EV_{J}^{2}[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}]\right)=0.

(20)

By Chebyshev’s inequality, this implies (
15
), which proves landscape independence
[
5
]
for the SK model.

To show concentration over measurements for fixed
J
, we subtract (
20
) from (
17
) to give us

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\Big[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad n\to\infty.

(21)

By (
18
) for any
J

\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|(C/n)^{2}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{2}\geq 0,

(22)

Then (
21
)
shows that this variance vanishes typically as
n\to\infty
.
This implies (
16
) by Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

This corollary means that as
n\to\infty
, for typical instances of the SK model, upon applying the QAOA and measuring in the computational basis, we will obtain a string
{\boldsymbol{z}}
that has energy
C({\boldsymbol{z}})/n
close to
\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|C/n|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, which is itself close to
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.

Formula for
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})

—
We now provide instructions on how to evaluate
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
.
Start by defining

A=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},a_{-p},\ldots,a_{-1}):a_{\pm j}=+1,-1\}

(23)

as the set of all configurations.
We partition into
A=A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p}\cup A_{p+1}
, where

A_{\ell}=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}:a_{-k}=a_{k}\text{ for }p-\ell+1<k\leq p,\text{ and }a_{-p+\ell-1}=-a_{p-\ell+1}\}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\ell\leq p,

(24)

and

A_{p+1}=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}:a_{-k}=a_{k}\text{ for }1\leq k\leq p\}\,.

(25)

We define a
*
operation which transforms configuration
{\boldsymbol{a}}
into
{\boldsymbol{a}}^{*}
given by

a^{*}_{r}=a_{r}a_{r+1}\cdots a_{p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad a^{*}_{-r}=a_{-r}a_{-r-1}\cdots a_{-p}\quad\text{for }1\leq r\leq p\,.

(26)

For any
{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A
, let

\displaystyle Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\cos\beta_{j})^{1+(a_{j}+a_{-j})/2}(\sin\beta_{j})^{1-(a_{j}+a_{-j})/2}(i)^{(a_{-j}-a_{j})/2},

(27)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\sum_{r=1}^{p}\gamma_{r}(a^{*}_{r}-a^{*}_{-r}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad X_{\boldsymbol{b}}=Q_{\boldsymbol{b}}\exp\Big({-}\frac{1}{2}{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{p+1}}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}\Phi^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}}\Big).

(28)

Furthermore, for any
{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A\setminus A_{p+1}
, let

\displaystyle\Delta_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi^{2}_{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}-\Phi^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}})

(29)

where the
\bar{{\boldsymbol{a}}}
operation takes configuration
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell}
to
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell}
for
1\leq\ell\leq p
via

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\pm r}=\begin{cases}a_{\pm r},&r\neq p-\ell+1\\
-a_{\pm r}&r=p-\ell+1\end{cases}\,.

(30)

We then bipartition
B=A\setminus A_{p+1}
into two sets
D\cup D^{c}
, such that if
{\boldsymbol{b}}\in D
then
\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}\in D^{c}
.
Note
|D|=|B|/2=(2^{2p}-2^{p})/2
.
For concreteness, we can use the convention where all configurations of
D
are chosen so that their first
p
elements have even parity.
Furthermore, for each element
{\boldsymbol{b}}\in D
we assign an index
j({\boldsymbol{b}})\in\{1,2,\ldots,|D|\}
, such that if
j({\boldsymbol{b}})\leq j({\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime})
then
\Phi_{\bar{\boldsymbol{b}}{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}}^{2}=\Phi^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{b}}{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}}
(and we say “
{\boldsymbol{b}}
plays well with
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}
”).
This index can be efficiently assigned since
{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A_{\ell}
plays well with
{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\prime}\in A_{\ell^{\prime}}
if
\ell\leq\ell^{\prime}
(see Lemma
1
in Section
6.1
).
With this index assignment, we have
\Delta_{k,j}=0
for
k\leq j
.

Next, for every element of
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in D
we assign a value
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
which can be iteratively obtained as follows:
Start with
W_{|D|}=X_{|D|}
, and for
j=|D|-1,\ldots,2,1
compute

W_{j}=X_{j}\exp\left({\textstyle\sum_{k=j+1}^{|D|}W_{k}\Delta_{k,j}}\right).

(31)

Then for other elements
{\boldsymbol{u}}\not\in D
we assign

W_{\boldsymbol{u}}=Q_{\boldsymbol{u}}\quad\text{when}\quad{\boldsymbol{u}}\in A_{p+1},\qquad\text{ and }\qquad W_{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}=-W_{\boldsymbol{u}}\quad\text{when}\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\in D^{c}.

(32)

Finally,
V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})
is obtained from all the
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
’s via the following formula:

V_{p}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\gamma_{r}\left[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in A}(u^{*}_{r}+u^{*}_{-r})W_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right]\left[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in A}(v^{*}_{r}-v^{*}_{-r})W_{\boldsymbol{v}}\right]\,.

(33)

The memory complexity of the above computation is at most
{O}(4^{p})
for storing the vector of
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
’s which has dimension
|D|=(4^{p}-2^{p})/2
.
Computing all the
W_{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
’s requires evaluating (
31
)
|D|
times, each involving a sum of at most
|D|
terms.
Hence, we only need at most
O(|D|^{2})=O(16^{p})
time to compute the
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
’s.
Once we have an array of
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
in memory, computing
V_{p}
takes at most
O(p\,4^{p})
extra time as we simply add up
W_{\boldsymbol{u}}
for some
p
pairs of subsets of
A
and multiply the results.
The time complexity for computing
V_{p}
is thus at most
O(16^{p})
.

5
The QAOA applied to the SK model at
p=1

For the remainder of the paper we explain how to prove our main Theorem.
We start by proving it for the simplest case of the QAOA at
p=1
where results for any problem size
n
can be obtained.
Since there is only one parameter in each of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we denote them without boldface.
We start by evaluating

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\EV_{J}\left[\braket{s|e^{i\gamma C}e^{i\beta B}e^{i\lambda C/n}e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}|s}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\EV_{J}\left[\penalty\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\braket{s|e^{i\gamma C}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}|e^{i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}e^{i\lambda C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})/n}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{-i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}|e^{-i\gamma C}|s}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\EV_{J}\left[\exp\Big({\footnotesize i\gamma[C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})-C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})]+i\frac{\lambda}{n}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})}\Big)\right]\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}|e^{i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{-i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}},

(36)

where we have inserted complete sets of basis vectors
\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}},\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}},\ket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}
, each of which runs through all
2^{n}
elements of
\{+1,-1\}^{n}
.
Note that
J_{jk}
only appears in
C({\boldsymbol{z}})=(1/\sqrt{n})\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}z_{j}z_{k}
.
For every string
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
, we can redefine

{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
, where the product of strings
\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{z}
is understood as bit-wise product (i.e.,
[\boldsymbol{y}\boldsymbol{z}]_{k}=y_{k}z_{k}
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\penalty\ \penalty\ =\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\EV_{J}\left[\exp\Big({\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}z^{\rm m}_{j}z^{\rm m}_{k}\left[\gamma(z^{1}_{j}z^{1}_{k}-z^{2}_{j}z^{2}_{k})+\lambda/n\right]}\Big)\right]\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{-i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}.

(37)

Observe that
\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}=\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}|e^{i\beta B}|\boldsymbol{1}}
, which is independent of
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
.
Since we have assumed
J_{jk}
follows a symmetrical distribution, averaging over
J_{jk}
is the same as averaging over
J_{jk}z^{\rm m}_{j}z^{\rm m}_{k}
.
(Note this is the only place we use the assumption that the distribution of
J
is symmetrical.)
Hence, we can drop the dependence on
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
, and the sum over
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
kills the
1/2^{n}
factor to yield

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\EV_{J}\left[\exp\Big({\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}\left[\gamma(z^{1}_{j}z^{1}_{k}-z^{2}_{j}z^{2}_{k})+\lambda/n\right]}\Big)\right]\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}|e^{i\beta B}|\boldsymbol{1}}\braket{\boldsymbol{1}|e^{-i\beta B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\prod_{j<k}\EV_{J}\left[\exp\Big({\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}J_{jk}[\phi_{jk}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})+\lambda/n]}\Big)\right]f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})f_{\beta}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}),

(38)

where we have denoted

\displaystyle\phi_{jk}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})

\displaystyle\equiv\gamma(z^{1}_{j}z^{1}_{k}-z^{2}_{j}z^{2}_{k}),

(39)

and

\displaystyle f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}})

\displaystyle\equiv\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}|e^{i\beta B}|\boldsymbol{1}}=(\cos\beta)^{\#1\text{'s in }{\boldsymbol{z}}}(i\sin\beta)^{\#-1\text{'s in }{\boldsymbol{z}}}.

(40)

Now, let us evaluate
\EV_{J}\left[\cdots\right]
, where the choices of
J_{jk}
are averaged over.
While in principle
J_{jk}
can be drawn from any symmetrical distribution with mean 0 and variance 1, here we consider the case of the standard normal distribution, because it is more convenient when
J_{jk}
is in the exponential.
Then we have
\EV_{J}\left[e^{iJ_{jk}x}\right]=e^{-x^{2}/2}
and so

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\prod_{j<k}\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2n}(\phi_{jk}+\lambda/n)^{2}\Big]f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})f_{\beta}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}}\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2n^{3}}\binom{n}{2}\Big]f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})f_{\beta}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}).

(41)

Changing from string to configuration basis

—
Instead of summing over all
(2^{n})^{2}
possible strings
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}
in (
41
), we can switch to a more convenient basis that we call the
configuration basis
.
For a given choice of strings
({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})
, if we look at the
k
-th index bit of both strings,
(z^{1}_{k},z^{2}_{k})
, it can only be one of four possible configurations from the following set

\displaystyle A=\{(+1,+1),\penalty\ (+1,-1),\penalty\ (-1,+1),\penalty\ (-1,-1)\}.

(42)

We denote the number of times the
(+1,+1)
configuration appears among the
n
possible bits as
n_{++}
, etc.
In other words, we want to change the basis from

\{({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}):{\boldsymbol{z}}^{j}\in\{\pm 1\}^{2n}\}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\{(n_{++},n_{+-},n_{-+},n_{--}):\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}=n\}.

(43)

We wish to express (
41
) in the configuration basis.
We start by observing that

\displaystyle f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})

\displaystyle=(\cos\beta)^{n_{++}+n_{+-}}(i\sin\beta)^{n_{-+}+n_{--}},

(44)

and

\displaystyle f_{\beta}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})

\displaystyle=(\cos\beta)^{n_{++}+n_{-+}}(-i\sin\beta)^{n_{+-}+n_{--}}.

(45)

Therefore,

\displaystyle f_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})f_{\beta}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})=(\cos^{2}\beta)^{n_{++}}(i\sin\beta\cos\beta)^{n_{-+}}(-i\sin\beta\cos\beta)^{n_{+-}}(\sin^{2}\beta)^{n_{--}}\equiv\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

(46)

where

\displaystyle Q_{++}=\cos^{2}\beta,\qquad Q_{--}=\sin^{2}\beta,\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_{-+}=-Q_{+-}=i\sin\beta\cos\beta.

(47)

Now we want to perform the basis change on the part that depends on
\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}^{q}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})
, where
q=1,2
.
Since
\phi_{jk}=\phi_{kj}
and
\phi_{kk}=0
, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}^{q}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\phi_{jk}^{q}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{q}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}

(48)

where the product of configurations
{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}
is understood as bit-wise product (i.e.,
[{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}]_{j}=a_{j}b_{j}
), and

\displaystyle\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}\equiv\gamma(a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}b_{2}).

(49)

We can now rewrite (
41
) in the configuration basis, yielding

\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]=e^{\large-\frac{\lambda^{2}(n-1)}{4n^{2}}}\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\exp\Big[{-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}-\frac{\lambda}{2n^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

(50)

where the multinomial coefficient is defined in general as

\displaystyle\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}=\binom{n}{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3},\ldots}=\frac{n!}{n_{1}!n_{2}!n_{3}!\cdots}\qquad\text{subject to}\qquad n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+\cdots=n.

(51)

Expressions of moments

—
The desired moments
\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|C/n|\gamma,\beta}\right]
and
\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|(C/n)^{2}|\gamma,\beta}\right]
can be calculated by differentiating with respect to
\lambda
and then setting
\lambda=0
.
For the first moment, we get

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|C/n|\gamma,\beta}\right]=-i\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\gamma,\beta}\right]\Big|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\left(\frac{i}{2n^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}\right)\exp\Big({-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big)\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

(52)

Similarly, the second moment is

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|(C/n)^{2}|\gamma,\beta}\right]=(-i)^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}\EV_{J}[\braket{e^{i\lambda C/n}}]\Big|_{\lambda=0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\bigg[\Big(\frac{i}{2n^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}\Big)^{2}+\frac{n-1}{2n^{2}}\bigg]\exp\Big({-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big)\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2n^{2}}+\sum_{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\Big(\frac{i}{2n^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}\Big)^{2}\exp\Big({-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big)\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}},

(53)

where we pulled out
(n-1)/(2n^{2})
out of the sum as the rest of the summand
corresponds to setting
\lambda=0
on the LHS of (
50
), which yields
\EV_{J}[\braket{\mathds{1}}]=1
.
To simplify the expressions of the moments further, note that

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\gamma(a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}b_{2})n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}\,.

(54)

Now the only non-zero coefficient in front of
n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}
is
2\gamma a_{1}b_{1}
when
a_{1}b_{1}=-a_{2}b_{2}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}=4\gamma(n_{++}-n_{--})(n_{+-}-n_{-+}).

(55)

Furthermore, since
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}
can only be 0 or
(2\gamma)^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}

\displaystyle=2(2\gamma)^{2}(n_{++}+n_{--})(n_{+-}+n_{-+}),

(56)

where the extra factor of 2 comes from double counting in the sum.

Evaluating the first moment
\braket{C/n}

—
Plugging (
55
) and (
56
) into (
52
), we get that the first moment is

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i2\gamma}{n^{2}}\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}(n_{++}-n_{--})(n_{+-}-n_{-+})\exp\Big[{-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{n}(n_{++}+n_{--})(n_{+-}+n_{-+})}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

(57)

To evaluate this, let
t=n_{-+}+n_{+-}
and
n-t=n_{++}+n_{--}
.
We then sum on
t
to get

\displaystyle M_{1}=\frac{i2\gamma}{n^{2}}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\binom{n}{t}\exp\Big[{-2\gamma^{2}\frac{t(n-t)}{n}}\Big]\sum_{n_{+-}+n_{-+}=t}

\displaystyle\binom{t}{n_{+-},n_{-+}}(n_{+-}-n_{-+})Q_{+-}^{n_{+-}}Q_{-+}^{n_{-+}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n_{++}+n_{--}=n-t}

\displaystyle\binom{n-t}{n_{++},n_{--}}(n_{++}-n_{--})Q_{++}^{n_{++}}Q_{--}^{n_{--}}.

(58)

Observe the following identity

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q=s}\binom{s}{p,q}(p-q)x^{p}y^{q}=s(x-y)(x+y)^{s-1}.

(59)

We can then evaluate
the sum over
n_{++}
and
n_{--}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{++}+n_{--}=n-t}\binom{n-t}{n_{++},n_{--}}(n_{++}-n_{--})Q_{++}^{n_{++}}Q_{--}^{n_{--}}=(n-t)(\cos^{2}\beta-\sin^{2}\beta)=(n-t)\cos 2\beta,

(60)

where we used the fact that
Q_{++}=\cos^{2}\beta
and
Q_{--}=\sin^{2}\beta
.

Now for the sum over
n_{+-}
and
n_{-+}
, we have that
Q_{+-}=-Q_{-+}=-i\sin\beta\cos\beta
.
Thus when applying (
59
), where
x=-y
, it is only nonzero for
s=1
, in which case it is
2x
.
For the
n_{+-}
and
n_{-+}
sum we set
t=1
and get

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{+-}+n_{-+}=t}\binom{t}{n_{+-},n_{-+}}(n_{+-}-n_{-+})Q_{+-}^{n_{+-}}Q_{-+}^{n_{-+}}=2(-i\sin\beta\cos\beta)=-i\sin 2\beta.

(61)

Now, returning to (
58
), we get with
t=1

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|C/n|\gamma,\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=M_{1}=\frac{i2\gamma}{n^{2}}n\exp\big({-2\gamma^{2}\frac{n-1}{n}}\big)[-i(n-1)\sin 2\beta\cos 2\beta]

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{n}\gamma\exp\big({-2\gamma^{2}\frac{n-1}{n}}\big)\sin 4\beta.

(62)

Here at
p=1
, we have obtained a formula for any finite
n
when
J_{jk}
are drawn from the standard normal distribution.
We can carry out a similar calculation for when
J_{jk}
are uniformly drawn from
\{+1,-1\}
, and obtain a slightly different answer.
Nevertheless, in the infinite size limit, both answers agree and become the following

\displaystyle\boxed{V_{1}(\gamma,\beta)\equiv\lim_{n\to\infty}\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|C/n|\gamma,\beta}\right]=\gamma e^{-2\gamma^{2}}\sin 4\beta.}

(63)

This is minimized at
\gamma=1/2
and
\beta=-\pi/8
, where

\displaystyle V_{1}(1/2,-\pi/8)=-1/\sqrt{4e}\approx-0.303265.

(64)

Evaluating the second moment
\braket{(C/n)^{2}}

—
To evaluate the second moment, we plug (
55
) and (
56
) into (
53
) to obtain

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2n^{2}}-\frac{4\gamma^{2}}{n^{4}}\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}(n_{++}-n_{--})^{2}(n_{+-}-n_{-+})^{2}\exp\Big[{-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{n}(n_{++}+n_{--})(n_{+-}+n_{-+})}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}}.

(65)

Note that the sum we need to do is very similar to that for the first moment (
58
).
We perform the same change of variable with
t=n_{-+}+n_{+-}
and
n-t=n_{++}+n_{--}
to get

\displaystyle M_{2}=\frac{n-1}{2n^{2}}-\frac{4\gamma^{2}}{n^{4}}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\binom{n}{t}e^{-2\gamma^{2}t(n-t)/n}\sum_{n_{+-}+n_{-+}=t}

\displaystyle\binom{t}{n_{+-},n_{-+}}(n_{+-}-n_{-+})^{2}Q_{+-}^{n_{+-}}Q_{-+}^{n_{-+}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{n_{++}+n_{--}=n-t}

\displaystyle\binom{n-t}{n_{++},n_{--}}(n_{++}-n_{--})^{2}Q_{++}^{n_{++}}Q_{--}^{n_{--}}.

(66)

Using another version of the identity in (
59
),

\displaystyle\sum_{p+q=s}\binom{s}{p,q}(p-q)^{2}x^{p}y^{q}=s\big[s(x-y)^{2}+4xy\big](x+y)^{s-2},

(67)

we can then evaluate the sum over
n_{++}
and
n_{--}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{++}+n_{--}=n-t}\binom{n-t}{n_{++},n_{--}}(n_{++}-n_{--})^{2}Q_{++}^{n_{++}}Q_{--}^{n_{--}}=\frac{1}{2}(n-t)[n-t+1+(n-t-1)\cos 4\beta],

(68)

where we plugged in
Q_{++}=\cos^{2}\beta
and
Q_{--}=\sin^{2}\beta
.

Now for the sum over
n_{+-}
and
n_{-+}
, we again have
Q_{+-}=-Q_{-+}=-i\sin\beta\cos\beta
.
And so in (
67
) we have
x=-y
, and it is only nonzero for
s=2
, which which case it is
8x^{2}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{+-}+n_{-+}=t}\binom{t}{n_{+-},n_{-+}}(n_{+-}-n_{-+})^{2}Q_{+-}^{n_{+-}}Q_{-+}^{n_{-+}}=8(-i\sin\beta\cos\beta)^{2}=-2\sin^{2}2\beta.

(69)

Returning to our expression in (
66
), we set
t=2
and get

\displaystyle M_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2n^{2}}+\frac{2\gamma^{2}(n-1)(n-2)[n-1+(n-3)\cos 4\beta]}{n^{3}}e^{-4\gamma^{2}(n-2)/n}\sin^{2}2\beta.

(70)

In the infinite size limit, this has a much simpler form

\displaystyle\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|(C/n)^{2}|\gamma,\beta}\right]=\gamma^{2}e^{-4\gamma^{2}}\sin^{2}4\beta=V_{1}^{2}(\gamma,\beta).}

(71)

This is exactly equal to the first moment squared in the infinite size limit.
As discussed in Section
4
, this implies concentration over instances and measurements.

Finite-Size Effect

—
To get a sense of the effect from finite
n
, we subtract the square of (
62
) from (
70
) to get

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\gamma,\beta|(C/n)^{2}|\gamma,\beta}\right]-\EV_{J}^{2}[\braket{\gamma,\beta|C/n|\gamma,\beta}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\frac{1}{2}+2\gamma^{2}e^{-4\gamma^{2}}\big[\gamma^{2}-(\gamma^{2}-1)\cos 8\beta-\cos 4\beta\big]\Big)+O\Big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big).

(72)

This implies that the combined variance over instances and measurements is of order
1/n
.
So the standard deviation over each is
O(1/\sqrt{n})
.

We also note that we could find optimal parameters for finite
n
by optimizing
\gamma
and
\beta
in (
62
).
We see that the finite-size optimal
\gamma
at
p=1
differ from the infinite size limit by
O(1/n)
, while
\beta
remains the same.

Generic QAOA states

—
Looking at (
63
) one concludes that for any values of
\gamma
and
\beta
the quantum expectation of the cost function is of order
n
.
However, as we now explain, in a generic QAOA state the expected value of the cost function is exponentially small in
n
.
To understand this apparent inconsistency, return to (
8
) where you see the factor of
1/\sqrt{n}
in front.
Without this factor and with the
J_{jk}
’s chosen from
\{+1,-1\}
,
\gamma
is an angle between
-\pi
and
\pi
.
With this convention the expected value of the cost function scales like the right hand side of (
63
) with
\gamma
replaced by
\sqrt{n}\gamma
. Then, a random
\gamma
between
-\pi
and
\pi
gives an exponentially small quantum expected value.
Under this convention the expected value of the cost function is only of order
n
in a vanishingly small subset of parameter space (i.e., the corner where
\gamma
is of order
1/\sqrt{n}
), and exponentially small in
n
elsewhere.
This observation is consistent with the “barren plateau” prediction
[
16
]
arguing that under certain conditions, the parameter search in variational quantum algorithms may be challenged by the exponentially small gradients seen at generic parameters.
What we have shown is that this issue can be addressed by avoiding random initialization in our parameter search, since we can know in advance in which corner to look.

6
General
p

In this section we go beyond the
p=1
calculations done in Section
5
to prove our main Theorem for arbitrary
p
.
We will be able to evaluate the first and second moment of
C/n
in an arbitrary QAOA state averaged over instances in the
n\to\infty
limit, for any given
2p
parameters.
The form of our answer will involve an iterative procedure that can be run on a classical computer with
O(16^{p})
complexity, corresponding to the formula given in Section
4
.

To begin, we look at

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\braket{s|e^{i\gamma_{1}C}e^{i\beta_{1}B}\cdots e^{i\gamma_{p}C}e^{i\beta_{p}B}e^{i\lambda C/n}e^{-i\beta_{p}B}e^{-i\gamma_{p}C}\cdots e^{-i\beta_{1}B}e^{-i\gamma_{1}C}|s}

(73)

and now insert
2p+1
complete sets to get

\displaystyle\braket{e^{i\lambda C/n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm 1]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}\braket{s|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]}}e^{i\gamma_{1}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]})}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]}|e^{i\beta_{1}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]}}\cdots e^{i\gamma_{p}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]})}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]}|e^{i\beta_{p}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}e^{i\lambda C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})/n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}|e^{-i\beta_{p}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]}}e^{-i\gamma_{p}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]})}\cdots\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]}|e^{-i\beta_{1}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]}}e^{-i\gamma_{1}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]})}\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]}|s}.

(74)

Here we label the
2p+1
strings as
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]}
.
This labelling is convenient because
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[j]}
will often be paired with
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-j]}
in the calculations that follow.
Note each of the terms of the form say
\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]}|e^{i\beta_{3}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}}
only depends on the bit-wise product
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}
.
Hence, we define

\displaystyle f_{j}({\boldsymbol{z}}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\prime})\equiv\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}|e^{i\beta_{j}B}|{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\prime}}.

(75)

Then we get

\displaystyle\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm 1]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}}

\displaystyle\exp\Big[{i\sum_{r=1}^{p}\gamma_{r}[C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[r]})-C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-r]})]+i\frac{\lambda}{n}C({\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})}\Big]

\displaystyle\times f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]})\cdots f_{p-1}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p-1]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]})f_{p}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})

\displaystyle\times f_{1}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]})\cdots f_{p-1}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1-p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]})f_{p}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}).

(76)

We are going to transform the
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[j]}
’s to achieve two results.
We will make each of the
f
factors depends on only one
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[j]}
.
We will also be able to absorb
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
into other variables so it disappears.
To this end, for every
r=1,2,\ldots,p
, we simultaneously transform

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[r]}&\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[r]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[r+1]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m},\\
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-r]}&\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-r]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-r-1]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}\end{split}

(77)

where the product of strings is understood as bit-wise product.
In the
f
factors, we see for example

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}&\to({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[4]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})={\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]},\\
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-3]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-4]}&\to({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-3]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-4]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-4]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-5]}\cdots{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m})={\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-3]}.\end{split}

(78)

In general, the products
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm r]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm(r+1)]}\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm r]}
for
1\leq r\leq p-1
, so the first goal is achieved.
Since
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]}{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}\to{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]}
, the second goal is also achieved.
Recall that

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{z}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}z_{j}z_{k}\,.

(79)

Then after the transformation,
J_{jk}
always appear together with
z^{\rm m}_{j}z^{\rm m}_{k}
as
J_{jk}z^{\rm m}_{j}z^{\rm m}_{k}
.
Now, note that averaging over
J_{jk}
is the same as averaging over
J_{jk}z^{\rm m}_{j}z^{\rm m}_{k}
, so we get

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm 1]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]}}\EV_{J}\Big[\exp\big({\frac{i}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{j<k}J_{jk}(\phi_{jk}+\frac{\lambda}{n})}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]})f_{2}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]})\cdots f_{p}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]})f_{p}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]})\cdots f_{2}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]})f_{1}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]})

(80)

where

\displaystyle\phi_{jk}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\left(z^{[1]}_{j}z^{[2]}_{j}\cdots z^{[p]}_{j}z^{[1]}_{k}z^{[2]}_{k}\cdots z^{[p]}_{k}\penalty\ -\penalty\ z^{[-1]}_{j}z^{[-2]}_{j}\cdots z^{[-p]}_{j}z^{[-1]}_{k}z^{[-2]}_{k}\cdots z^{[-p]}_{k}\right)+

\displaystyle\quad\penalty\ \gamma_{2}\left(z^{[2]}_{j}\cdots z^{[p]}_{j}z^{[2]}_{k}\cdots z^{[p]}_{k}\penalty\ -\penalty\ z^{[-2]}_{j}\cdots z^{[-p]}_{j}z^{[-2]}_{k}\cdots z^{[-p]}_{k}\right)+\cdots+

\displaystyle\quad\penalty\ \gamma_{p}\left(z^{[p]}_{j}z^{[p]}_{k}-z^{[-p]}_{j}z^{[-p]}_{k}\right).

(81)

The sum over
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{\rm m}
, which no longer appears, killed the
1/2^{n}
factor in front of (
6
).

For convenience, we assume each
J_{jk}
comes from the standard normal distribution, which means
\EV_{J}[e^{iJ_{jk}x}]=e^{-x^{2}/2}
.
We now average over all
J_{jk}
to get

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm 1]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[\pm p]}}\prod_{j<k}\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2n}(\phi_{jk}+\frac{\lambda}{n})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]})f_{2}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]})\cdots f_{p}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]})f_{p}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]})\cdots f_{2}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]})f_{1}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]}).

(82)

Changing from string to configuration basis

—
Instead of summing over all
(2^{n})^{2p}
possible string values of
({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]})
in (
82
), we will sum over a configuration basis as we did at

.
To define this configuration basis, let

\displaystyle A=\{+1,-1\}^{2p}=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{p},a_{-p},\ldots,a_{-2},a_{-1}):a_{\pm j}\in\{+1,-1\}\},

(83)

where we index the
2p
bits in each
{\boldsymbol{a}}
as
1,2,\ldots,p,-p,\ldots,-2,-1
.
Now look at the
k
-th indexed bit of all the strings, that is

(z^{[1]}_{k},z^{[2]}_{k}\ldots,z^{[p]}_{k},z^{[-p]}_{k},\ldots,z^{[-2]}_{k},z^{[-1]}_{k})\in A.

(84)

We denote the number of times a given configuration
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A
occurs among the
n
possible bits as
n_{\boldsymbol{a}}
, where

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}=n.

(85)

Note that
{\boldsymbol{a}}
takes
2^{2p}
possible values, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}=\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{2^{2p}}}\binom{n}{n_{1},\ldots,n_{2^{2p}}}=(2^{2p})^{n}=(2^{n})^{2p},

(86)

where the multinomial coefficient is defined in (
51
), so we have everything covered in the configuration basis.

We want to express the sum in (
82
) in terms of the
{\boldsymbol{a}}
’s and the
n_{\boldsymbol{a}}
’s.
Let us start with the
f
’s, for example

\displaystyle f_{3}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]})=\braket{{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}|e^{i\beta_{3}B}|\boldsymbol{1}}=(\cos\beta_{3})^{\#\text{ of 1's in }{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}}(i\sin\beta_{3})^{\#\text{ of $-1$'s in }{\boldsymbol{z}}^{[3]}}.

(87)

Let
{\boldsymbol{a}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},a_{-p},\ldots,a_{-1})
. Then the
\cos\beta_{3}
term fires when
a_{3}=1
while the
i\sin\beta_{3}
term fires when
a_{3}=-1
.
So this
f_{3}
term can be written as

\displaystyle f_{3}=(\cos\beta_{3})^{\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}(1+a_{3})/2}(i\sin\beta_{3})^{\sum_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}(1-a_{3})/2}.

(88)

Therefore, the product of all the
f
’s is

\displaystyle f_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[1]})f_{2}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[2]})\cdots f_{p}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[p]})f_{p}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-p]})\cdots f_{2}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-2]})f_{1}^{*}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{[-1]})=\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}}

(89)

where

\displaystyle Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}(\cos\beta_{j})^{1+(a_{j}+a_{-j})/2}(\sin\beta_{j})^{1-(a_{j}+a_{-j})/2}(i)^{(a_{-j}-a_{j})/2}\,.

(90)

Return to (
82
) where we want to put the
\phi_{jk}
terms into the configuration basis.
Note for
q=1,2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}^{q}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\phi_{jk}^{q}

(91)

since
\phi_{jk}=\phi_{kj}
and
\phi_{kk}=0
.
For any configuration
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in A
, let

\displaystyle\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{p}\gamma_{r}(c_{r}^{*}-c_{-r}^{*}),

(92)

where
{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*}
is a transformation of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
defined as

\displaystyle c^{*}_{r}=c_{r}c_{r+1}\cdots c_{p}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c^{*}_{-r}=c_{-r}c_{-r-1}\cdots c_{-p}\quad\text{for }1\leq r\leq p\,.

(93)

For example, for
p=3
,

\begin{array}[]{lclllrrr}{\boldsymbol{c}}&=&(c_{1},&c_{2},&c_{3},&c_{-3},&c_{-2},&c_{-1}),\\
{\boldsymbol{c}}^{*}&=&(c_{1}c_{2}c_{3},&c_{2}c_{3},&c_{3},&c_{-3},&c_{-2}c_{-3},&c_{-1}c_{-2}c_{-3}).\end{array}

(94)

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j<k}\phi_{jk}^{q}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{q}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}

(95)

where
{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}
is understood as bit-wise product (i.e.,
[{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}]_{k}=a_{k}b_{k}
).
Thus, (
82
) in the configuration basis is

\displaystyle\EV_{J}\left[\braket{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}|e^{i\lambda C/n}|\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right]=\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}

\displaystyle\exp\Big[{-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}-\frac{\lambda}{2n^{2}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2n^{3}}\binom{n}{2}}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}}.

(96)

which is the same as (
50
) in the

case, except with expanded meaning of
\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}
,
Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}
, etc.

6.1
Organizing the sum by showing
1=1

We are going to expand (
96
) in
\lambda
to get the first two moments as the coefficients of
\lambda^{1}
and
\lambda^{2}
.
To organize our calculation we first look at the
\lambda^{0}
term, which yields 1 on the left hand side of (

).
This means

\displaystyle 1=\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}\binom{n}{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}}

\displaystyle\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}}.

(97)

However, it is not obvious how the sum on right hand side evaluates to 1. Understanding why this is true will help us evaluate the sum for the higher moments.

To make it work, we organize the sum over
\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}\}
to get cancellations.
To this end, we divide the set of configurations
A
into
p+1
subsets,

A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\cdots\cup A_{p+1},

(98)

where

\displaystyle A_{\ell}=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}:a_{-k}=a_{k}\text{ for }p-\ell+1<k\leq p,\text{ and }a_{-p+\ell-1}=-a_{p-\ell+1}\}\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq\ell\leq p,

(99)

and

A_{p+1}=\{{\boldsymbol{a}}:a_{-k}=a_{k}\text{ for }1\leq k\leq p\}\,.

(100)

Recall that we index the entries of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
as
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},a_{-p},\ldots,a_{-1})
.
Subset
A_{1}
has
a_{-p}=-a_{p}
, so there are
2^{2p-1}
elements.
Subset
A_{2}
has
a_{-p}=a_{p}
and

, with

elements, etc.
We illustrate this for

,

\begin{array}[]{lclllrrrr}A_{1}&:&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&-a_{3},&a_{-2},&a_{-1})&\quad 32\text{ elements}\\
A_{2}&:&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&a_{3},&-a_{2},&a_{-1})&\quad 16\text{ elements}\\
A_{p}&:&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&a_{3},&a_{2},&-a_{1})&\quad 8\text{ elements}\\
A_{p+1}&:&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&a_{3},&a_{2},&a_{1})&\quad 8\text{ elements}\end{array}

(101)

We now define a “bar” operation that takes configuration
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell}
to
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell}
for

via

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{\pm r}=\begin{cases}a_{\pm r},&r\neq p-\ell+1\\
-a_{\pm r}&r=p-\ell+1\end{cases}\quad\text{ for }\quad{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell},\quad 1\leq\ell\leq p\,.

(102)

Note the bar operation is its own inverse. We also never bar elements of
A_{p+1}
.
For example, for the form of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
’s given in (

), the corresponding

’s are

\begin{array}[]{lclrrrrrrr}A_{1}&:&(\hskip 8.0pta_{1},&a_{2},&-a_{3},&a_{3},&a_{-2},&a_{-1})&\\
A_{2}&:&(\hskip 8.0pta_{1},&-a_{2},&a_{3},&a_{3},&a_{2},&a_{-1})&\\

(103)

Note

flips the signs of two elements of
{\boldsymbol{a}}
.
If you look at

defined in (

\displaystyle Q_{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}}=-Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}\,.

(104)

Furthermore, we note that

\displaystyle\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}}=0\,.

(105)

We now introduce a notion we call “plays well with”:

Definition 1
.

Given two configurations
{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A
, we say

plays well with

\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}=\Phi_{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}.

.

For any

, if
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{\ell}
and

, where

We show how to prove for the case of

, and the generalization to other

First, let us show that all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{1}
plays well with all
{\boldsymbol{b}}
. Using

as an example, then
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}
are of the form

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}{\boldsymbol{a}}&=&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&-a_{3},&a_{-2},&a_{-1}),\\
{\boldsymbol{b}}&=&(b_{1},&b_{2},&b_{3},&b_{-3},&b_{-2},&b_{-1}).\end{array}

(106)

Then

depends on the following

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}})^{*}=(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}b_{1}b_{2}b_{3},\quad a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3},\quad a_{3}b_{3},\quad-a_{3}b_{-3},\quad-a_{-2}a_{3}b_{-2}b_{-3},\quad-a_{-1}a_{-2}a_{3}b_{-1}b_{-2}b_{-3}).

Note every term is proportional to

. Taking

,

We see that indeed all
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{1}

.

Now consider
{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A_{2}

\begin{array}[]{ccccccccc}{\boldsymbol{a}}&=&(a_{1},&a_{2},&a_{3},&a_{3},&-a_{2},&a_{-1}),\\

So

\displaystyle({\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}})^{*}=(a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}b_{1}b_{2}b_{3},\quad a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3},\quad a_{3}b_{3},\quad a_{3}b_{3},\quad-a_{2}a_{3}b_{2}b_{3},\quad a_{-1}a_{2}a_{3}b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}).

Now the coefficients of

in

.

Return to our task of showing

as in (

).
Pick one

We can write the RHS of (

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{d}=0}^{n}\penalty\ \sum_{n_{\boldsymbol{d}}+n_{\bar{\boldsymbol{d}}}=t_{d}}\sum_{\{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}:\penalty\ {\boldsymbol{a}}\in A^{c}\}}

\displaystyle\binom{n}{t_{d}}\binom{t_{d}}{n_{\boldsymbol{d}},n_{\bar{\boldsymbol{d}}}}\binom{n-t_{d}}{\{n_{{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}}\exp\Big[{-\frac{1}{4n}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in A^{c}}\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{2}n_{\boldsymbol{a}}n_{\boldsymbol{b}}}\Big]\prod_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in A^{c}}Q_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n_{\boldsymbol{a}}}

Now note the identity

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{{\boldsymbol{d}}}+n_{\bar{\boldsymbol{d}}}=t_{d}}\binom{t_{d}}{n_{{\boldsymbol{d}}},n_{\bar{\boldsymbol{d}}}}x^{n_{{\boldsymbol{d}}}}y^{n_{\bar{\boldsymbol{d}}}}=(x+y)^{t_{d}}.

If

7
Discussion

The QAOA is a general purpose quantum optimizer whose computational power has not been fully explored. Early applications were to problems such as MaxCut on bounded degree graphs. Here it is known that for fixed depth
p
, as the graph size increases there can be upper bounds on how well the algorithm can perform.
For example, for MaxCut on 3-regular bipartite graphs (i.e., completely satisfiable) with
o(n)
subgraphs that are triangles, squares or pentagons,
the achieved approximation ratio at
p=2
is 0.7559
[
2
]
. (Poor reader, don’t think this has anything to do with the Parisi constant of 0.763…in (
11
).)
For fixed degree graphs we need
p
to increase at least logarithmically so that each edge sees the whole graph. What we mean by “sees” is this: Consider a cost function that is a sum of local terms described in terms of a graph problem. Pick one term say
C_{\alpha}
corresponding to clause
\alpha
. Then in evaluating (
7
) we look at

U^{\dagger}(C,\gamma_{1})\cdots U^{\dagger}(B,\beta_{p})C_{\alpha}U(B,\beta_{p})\cdots U(C,\gamma_{1})

(225)

as in (
6
).
The locality of this operator is found by taking each qubit in
C_{\alpha}
and moving out a distance
p
on the instance graph to see which other qubits are involved. If
p
does not grow with
n
, then for large
n
, not all qubits are “seen” by the clause. Without seeing the whole graph the algorithm can not detect contradictions from say large loops.
Therefore, to have hope of success on bounded degree graphs, we need
p
to grow at least as a big enough constant times
\log(n)
. For near term quantum computers with even 1000 qubits, this is easily achievable since for say 3-regular graphs at
p=8
, each clause is guaranteed to see the whole graph.

For problems on graphs whose degree grows with the number of bits, the previous arguments do not apply. For the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, the associated graph is the complete graph.
Therefore, each clause sees all the qubits at
p=1
, and sees all the qubits as well as all the edges at
p=2
.
Hence, one may imagine that for large fixed
p
, that in the large
n
limit, the QAOA will perform well.
We have explored this prospect in this work by obtaining a formula for the expected value of the cost function for an arbitrary QAOA state in the limit as
n\to\infty
.
The complexity of our formula grows as
O(16^{p})
, so without too much trouble we could optimize up to
p=8
and evaluate up to
p=12
, and our results are shown in Figure
1
.
(A recent result in Ref.
[
17
]
improves the complexity to
O(p^{2}4^{p})
which enables evaluation up to
p=20
.)
It is currently not possible to know for certain if for large
p
the performance asymptotes to the Parisi value (
11
) or something less optimal.
At
p=4
, we have crossed the energy at the phase transition which is
-0.5n
.
At
p=11
, we have surpassed the performance of the spectral relaxation and the standard semi-definite programming algorithms which yield
-2n/\pi
.
Nevertheless, as we discussed in Section
3
, the recent classical algorithm by Montanari
[
1
]
can efficiently find a string whose energy is between
(1-\epsilon)
and 1 times the lowest energy, assuming a widely believed conjecture that the SK model has “full replica symmetry breaking.”
Hence, we can only hope to match this with the QAOA.
We want to improve our techniques to determine the asymptotic behavior of the QAOA’s performance.
Furthermore, we can apply our techniques to other problems where we average over instances.
For example, we can consider generalizations of the SK model where multi-spin couplings are allowed. Since some of these models are shown to have no “full replica symmetry breaking”
[
18
]
, it is believed that classical algorithms like Montanari’s will fail to find near-optimal solutions
[
19
,
20
]
.
It would be interesting to see how the QAOA performs for those problems, and whether it will outperform the classical algorithms. We also imagine the possibility of extending our techniques to
p
growing with
n
.
