Classification of differentiable structures on the non-Hausdorff line with two origins
Mykola Lysynskyi, Sergiy Maksymenko

Abstract
. We classify differentiable structures on a line 𝕃 \mathbb{L} with two origins being a non-Hausdorff but T 1 T_{1} one-dimensional manifold obtained by ‘‘doubling’’ 0 0 . For k ∈ ℕ ∪ { ∞ } k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\} let H H be the group of homeomorphisms h h of ℝ \mathbb{R} such that h ⁡ ( 0 ) = 0 h(0)=0 and the restriction of h h to ℝ ∖ 0 \mathbb{R}\setminus 0 is a 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -diffeomorphism. Let also D D be the subgroup of H H consisting of 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -diffeomorphisms of ℝ \mathbb{R} also fixing 0 0 . It is shown that there is a natural bijection between 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -structures on 𝕃 \mathbb{L} (up to a 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -diffeomorphism fixing both origins) and double D D -coset classes D ∖ H / D = { D ​ h ​ D ∣ h ∈ H } D\setminus H/D=\{DhD\mid h\in H\} . Moreover, the set of all 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -structures on 𝕃 \mathbb{L} (up to a 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -diffeomorphism which may also exchange origins) are in one-to-one correspondence with the set of double ( D , ± ) (D,\pm) -coset classes D ∖ H ± / D = { D ​ h ​ D ∪ D ​ h − 1 ​ D ∣ h ∈ H } D\setminus H^{\pm}/D=\{DhD\cup Dh^{-1}D\mid h\in H\} . In particular, in contrast with the real line, the line with two origins 𝕃 \mathbb{L} admits uncountably many pair-wise non-diffeomorphic 𝒞 k \mathcal{C}^{k} -structures for each k = 1 , 2 , … , ∞ k=1,2,\ldots,\infty .

\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L})=\mathcal{D}^{+,\mathrm{ex}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L})\sqcup\mathcal{D}^{-,\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L}),

so every
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism from
\mathfrak{S}(a)
to
\mathfrak{S}(1/a)
either fixes
0
and
\bar{0}
and
reverses
orientation, or exchanges
0
and
\bar{0}
and
preserves
orientation.
Moreover, for every
h\in\mathcal{D}^{-,\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L})
and
g\in\mathcal{D}^{+,\mathrm{ex}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L})
we have the following bijections:

\mathcal{D}^{+,\mathrm{ex}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L})\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \penalty\ k\,\mapsto\,g^{-1}\circ k\penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathcal{D}^{+,\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a)}(\mathbb{L})\xrightarrow{\penalty\ \penalty\ k\,\mapsto\,h\circ k\penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathcal{D}^{-,\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(1/a)}(\mathbb{L}).

(4)

Finally, if
a\neq b,1/b
, then
\mathcal{D}_{\mathfrak{S}(a),\mathfrak{S}(b)}(\mathbb{L})=\varnothing
, i.e. these
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures are not diffeomorphic.
In particular,
\mathbb{L}
has uncountably many pair-wise non-diffeomorphic
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures of each
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\infty
.

Remark 1.2.4
.

Notice that we have a surjective homomorphism
j^{1}\colon\mathcal{E}^{k,+}(\mathbb{R},0)\to\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
,
j^{1}(h)=h^{\prime}(0)
.
Let
G_{k}
be its kernel.
Then
G_{k}
consists of diffeomorphisms
k
-tangent to the identity and was studied in
[
34
]
.
Moreover, the group
G_{\infty}
plays an important role in the theory of foliations, see
[
35
,
31
,
16
]
.

.
In Section

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper the arrows
\lhook\joinrel\rightarrow
and
\rightarrow\!\!\!\!\!\to
mean
monomorphism
and
epimorphism
respectively.
We will also regard the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of order
2
as the multiplicative group
\{\pm 1\}
.

2.1.
Semidirect products

Let
H
be a group with a unit
e
and
A
be some subgroup of
H
.
A homomorphism
s\colon H\to A
is called
retraction
if
s(a)=a
for all
a\in A
.

Suppose
s\colon H\to A
is a homomorphism-retraction, and let
K
be its kernel.
Then
A
acts on
K
by conjugations, and this allows to define the following group
K\rtimes_{s}A
called the
semidirect product
of
K
and
A
corresponding to the retraction
s
.
Namely,
K\rtimes_{s}A
is a cartesian product of
sets

K\times A
with the following multiplication:

(k,a)(l,b):=(kala^{-1},ab),\qquad(k,a),(l,b)\in K\times A.

Moreover, the map
\gamma\colon K\rtimes_{s}A\to H
,
\gamma(k,a)=ka
, is an isomorphism of groups making commutative the following diagram:

\textstyle{K\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{k\,\mapsto\,(k,e)}
\textstyle{K\rtimes_{s}A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{(k,a)\,\mapsto\,a}
\scriptstyle{\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{A\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{K\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{H\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{s}
\textstyle{A}

We will exploit this construction several times.

2.2.
Wreath product
D\wr\mathbb{Z}_{2}

Let
D
be a group and

\phi\colon D\times D\to D\times D,\qquad\phi(a,b)=(b,a),

be the automorphism of exchanging coordinates.
It has the order
2
, and therefore defines a monomorphism
\bar{\phi}\colon\mathbb{Z}_{2}\to\mathrm{Aut}(D\times D)
, given by
\bar{\phi}(1)=\mathrm{id}_{D\times D}
and
\bar{\phi}(-1)=\phi
.

The corresponding semidirect product
(D\times D)\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is called the
wreath product
of
D
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and denoted by
D\wr\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, see e.g.
[
22
]
.
Thus,
D\wr\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is the direct product of
sets

D\times D\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with the following binary operation:

(a,b,\delta)(c,d,\varepsilon)=\begin{cases}(ac,bd,\delta\varepsilon),&\delta=+1,\\
(ad,bc,\delta\varepsilon),&\delta=-1.\end{cases}

2.3.
Double cosets
C\setminus H\,/\,D

We recall here the definition of double cosets, see e.g.
[
11
, Section 1.7]
.
Let
H
be a group and
C
,
D
be two subgroups.
Then we have a natural
left
action of the product
C\times D
on
H
:

C\times D\times H\to H,\qquad(c,d)\cdot h=chd^{-1}.

The corresponding orbit of
h\in H
is called the
(C,D)
-double coset
of
h
and is denoted by
ChD
.
If
C=D
, then
DhD
is also called the
D
-double coset
of
h
.

Notice that

ChD=\{chd^{-1}\mid c\in C,d\in D\}=\{chd\mid c\in C,d\in D\}=\mathop{\cup}\limits_{c\in C}chD=\mathop{\cup}\limits_{d\in D}Chd.

In particular, it is the union of left cosets of
D
corresponding to elements of the right coset
Ch
, as well as the union of right cosets of
C
corresponding to elements of the left coset
hD
.
The partition of
H
into
(C,D)
-double cosets is denoted by

C\setminus H\,/\,D.

Partitions into double cosets are not so ‘‘regular’’ as partitions into left or right cosets, and they are often hard to compute, see e.g.
[
30
,
13
,
12
,
20
]
, and references therein.
For instance, the double coset classes may have distinct sizes.
The reason is that the above action of
C\times D
on
H
is not always free.

Example 2.3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{D}_{3}=\langle r,s\mid r^{3}=s^{2}=(rs)^{2}=1\rangle
be the dihedral group consisting of six elements
e,r,r^{2},s,sr,sr^{2}
.
Consider two subgroups
A=\{e,s\}
and
B=\{e,sr\}
of
\mathbb{D}_{3}
.
One easily checks that

\displaystyle A\setminus\mathbb{D}_{3}\,/\,B=\bigl\{\{e,s,r,sr\},\ \{r^{2},sr^{2}\}\bigr\},

\displaystyle A\setminus\mathbb{D}_{3}\,/\,A=A\setminus\mathbb{D}_{3}^{\pm 1}\,/\,A=\bigl\{\{e,s\},\{r,r^{2},sr,sr^{2}\}\bigr\}.

The following lemma is trivial.
We will refer to it several times, and therefore it will be convenient to put it as a separate statement:

Lemma 2.3.2
.

If
C=D
and this group is normal in
H
, then
C\setminus H\,/\,D
coincides with the partition
H/D
into
(
no matter left or right
)
cosets of
D
in
H
.
∎

Let us mention one more description of
C\setminus H\,/\,D
which will be useful in this paper.

Lemma 2.3.3
.

Let
g,h\in H
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

g\in ChD
, which is the same as
CgD=ChD
;

(2)

C\cap gDh^{-1}\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2)

Suppose
g\in ChD
, so
g=chd^{-1}
for some
c\in C
and
d\in D
.
Then
c=gdh^{-1}\in C\cap gDh^{-1}\neq\varnothing
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)

Conversely, if
C\cap gDh^{-1}
contains some element
c
, i.e.
c=gdh^{-1}
for some
d\in D
, then
g=chd^{-1}\in ChD
.
∎

2.4.
Double cosets
D\setminus H^{\pm 1}\,/\,D

Suppose
C=D
.
Then for each
h\in H
, we have that
(DhD)^{-1}=Dh^{-1}D
is the
D
-double coset of
h^{-1}
, see e.g. the discussion in
[
4
, §3]
.
The following set

Dh^{\pm 1}D:=DhD\cup Dh^{-1}D=\{chd,ch^{-1}d\mid c,d\in D\}

will be called
(D,\pm)
-double coset of
h
.
Thus, if
DhD
mutually contains some
g\in H
and its inverse, then
Dh^{\pm 1}D=DhD=Dh^{-1}D
.
Otherwise,
Dh^{\pm 1}D=DhD\sqcup Dh^{-1}D
.

It follows that
(D,\pm)
-double cosets yield a partition on
H
which will be denoted by

D\setminus H^{\pm 1}\,/\,D.

One can give another description of
D\setminus H^{\pm 1}\,/\,D
.
Note that we have a natural
left
action

(D\wr\mathbb{Z}_{2})\times H\to H,\qquad(a,b,\delta)\cdot h:=ah^{\delta}b^{-1}.

Indeed, if
a,b,c,d\in D
and
h\in H
, then

\displaystyle(a,b,1)\bigl((c,d,1)\cdot h\bigr)

\displaystyle=a(chd^{-1})b^{-1}=(ac,bd,1)\cdot h=\bigl((a,b,1)(c,d,1)\bigr)\cdot h,

\displaystyle(a,b,-1)\bigl((c,d,1)\cdot h\bigr)

\displaystyle=a(chd^{-1})^{-1}b^{-1}=(ad,bc,-1)\cdot h=\bigl((a,b,-1)(c,d,1)\bigr)\cdot h,

\displaystyle(a,b,1)\bigl((c,d,-1)\cdot h\bigr)

\displaystyle=ach^{-1}d^{-1}b^{-1}=(ac,bd,-1)\cdot h=\bigl((a,b,1)(c,d,-1)\bigr)\cdot h,

\displaystyle(a,b,-1)\bigl((c,d,-1)\cdot h\bigr)

\displaystyle=a(ch^{-1}d^{-1})^{-1}b^{-1}=(ad,bc,1)\cdot h=\bigl((a,b,-1)(c,d,-1)\bigr)\cdot h.

It is easy to see that
D\setminus H^{\pm 1}\,/\,D
coincides with the partition into orbits of that action of
D\wr\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

2.5.
Branch points

Let
M
be a topological space with topology
\eta
, and
x\in M
be a point.
Denote by
\mathsf{hcl}(x):=\mathop{\cap}\limits_{x\in W\in\eta}\overline{W}
the intersection of closures of all open neighborhoods of
x
, we will call it the
Hausdorff closure of
x
.
Evidently,
x\in\mathsf{hcl}(x)
.
Also note that
\mathsf{hcl}(x)
consists of
y\in M
such that
x
and
y
have no disjoint neighborhoods.

Say that
x
is a
branch
point of
M
.
It is well known and is easy to see that
M
is Hausdorff iff
\{x\}=\mathsf{hcl}(x)
for all
x\in M
, i.e.
M
has no branch points.
There are not standard terms for such points.
For instance in
[
27
]
they are called
Hausdorff-violating
.

The following simple lemma is left for the reader.

Lemma 2.5.1
.

Let
h\colon M\to M
be a homeomorphism of
M
.
Then
h(\mathsf{hcl}(x))=\mathsf{hcl}(h(x))
for all
x\in M
.
In particular, the set of branch points of
M
is invariant under homeomorphisms.

3.
Differentiable structures on locally euclidean spaces

This section briefly recalls definition of differentiable structures on locally Euclidean spaces.
We refer the reader to the book by J. Lee
[
21
, Chapters 1,2]
, and also to the paper of F. Takens
[
36
]
for an equivalent approach.

Usually a
manifold
is a Hausdorff second countable locally Euclidean topological space.
The first two conditions are necessary and sufficient for metrizability of manifolds and for the possibility to embed them into some Euclidean spaces.
However, for defining differentiable structures only the property of being locally Euclidean is enough.
Therefore we will assume here only that property.
For simplicity we also do not consider manifolds with boundary.

For each
m\geq 0
the real
m
-dimensional linear space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the usual topology is called
Euclidean
.
Note that
\mathbb{R}^{0}=\{0\}
is a one-point space being a
0
-dimensional linear space.
Also recall that a homeomorphism
h\colon U\to V
between open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is called a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, if both
h
and
h^{-1}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-maps.

3.1.
Locally Euclidean spaces

Let
M
be a topological space.
A
chart
on
M
is an open embedding (i.e. a homeomorphism onto open subset)
\varphi\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
of some open subset
U\subset M
for some
m\geq 0
.
In this case we will say that this chart is
m
-dimensional
and
U
is its
domain
.
It will often be convenient to regard such a chart as a pair
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
.

Notice that then for every open subset
U^{\prime}\subset U
the restriction
\varphi|_{U^{\prime}}\colon U^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is also an
m
-dimensional chart on
M
.

Any collection
\mathsf{A}=\{(U_{i},\varphi_{i})\}_{i\in\Lambda}
of charts on
M
will be called a
partial atlas
.
If their domains cover
M
, i.e.
M=\mathop{\cup}\limits_{i\in\Lambda}U_{i}
, then
\mathsf{A}
is called an
atlas
.
Evidently, the union of any collection of (partial) atlases is a (partial) atlas.
It will also be convenient to regard each chart
\mathsf{u}
as a partial atlas
\{\mathsf{u}\}
consisting of that chart only.
This will never lead to confusion.

Say that
M
is
locally Euclidean
if either of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(LE1)

every point
x\in M
has an open neighborhood homeomorphic to some open subset of some Euclidean space (whose dimension may depend on
x
);

(LE2)

M
admits an atlas.

3.2.
Differentiable structures

Let
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
be two
overlapping
charts on
M
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e.
U\cap V\neq\varnothing
.
Then the following well-defined homeomorphism between open subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}

(3.1)

\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}\colon\varphi(U\cap V)\to\psi(U\cap V)

is called the
transition map
from
\mathsf{u}
to
\mathsf{v}
.
Note that its inverse,
\varphi\circ\psi^{-1}\colon\psi(U\cap V)\to\varphi(U\cap V)
, is then the transition map from
\mathsf{v}
to
\mathsf{u}
.

In what follows fix once and for all some
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Then the charts
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible
if either
U\cap V=\varnothing
or the transition map
\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism.

A partial atlas
\mathsf{A}=\{(U_{i},\varphi_{i})\}_{i\in\Lambda}
on
M
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
if all its charts are pair-wise
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible.

Further, two partial
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlases
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible
if their union
\mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B}
is still a partial
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas, i.e. the charts from
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are pair-wise
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible as well.

Notice that the relation
‘‘to be
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible’’
on the set of all
partial atlases
on
M
is not an equivalence: it is reflexive and symmetric, but not transitive.
However, it is easy to show that it is transitive on the set of all
atlases
:

Lemma 3.2.1
.

The relation
‘‘to be
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible’’
on the set of all atlases on
M
is an equivalence relation.
In particular, the union of any collection of pair-wise
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlases is still a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas.

The corresponding equivalence classes of pair-wise
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible atlases on
M
are called
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
.
It will be convenient to say that an atlas
\mathsf{A}
on
M

represents
a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
if
\mathsf{A}\in\mathfrak{A}
.
Also, a partial atlas
\mathsf{A}
on
M
, e.g. a chart, is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible
with
\mathfrak{A}
if it is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with some atlas from
\mathfrak{A}
.

Note also that taking the union of all atlases from the same
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure
\mathfrak{A}
, we will get a
maximal atlas
of
\mathfrak{A}
, in the sense that it contains any other
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible atlas.
Hence, each
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure is represented by a unique maximal atlas
.
However, such a description is not very useful from the ‘‘practical’’ point of view, since the maximal
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlases are too large to be ‘‘
observable
’’.
In many cases it is more convenient to work with atlases having as small number of charts as possible.
See also Lemma
3.4.1
below for another characterization of differentiable structures.

Moreover, the number of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
on
M
is still too large, and in order to get some reasonable classification one should consider those structures up to a homeomorphism being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism between them.

3.3.
Orientations

The transition map (
3.1
)
preserves orientation
if the determinant of the Jacobi matrix of
\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}
is positive at each point
x\in\varphi(U\cap V)
.
A
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas
\mathsf{A}=\{(U_{i},\varphi_{i})\}_{i\in\Lambda}
on
M
is called
orientable
if all its transition maps preserve orientation.
A
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure on
M
is
orientable
, if it contains an orientable atlas.

3.4.
Differentiable maps between locally Euclidean spaces

Till the end of this section we assume that
M
and
N
are locally Euclidean spaces endowed with some
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
respectively.

Let
h\colon M\to N
be a continuous map and
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
be charts in
M
and
N
respectively such that
h(U)\subset V
.
Then the induced map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces:

(3.2)

\psi\circ h\circ\varphi^{-1}\colon\varphi(U)\to\psi(V)

is called a (
local
)
presentation
of
h
in the charts
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
.

Say that
h
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}

(
with respect to
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
), if for each
x\in M
there exist local charts
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
on
M
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
on
N
such that

•

x\in U
and
\mathsf{u}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathfrak{A}
;

•

h(x)\in V
and
\mathsf{v}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathfrak{B}
;

•

h(U)\subset V
and the presentation
\psi\circ h\circ\varphi^{-1}\colon\varphi(U)\to\psi(V)
of
h
in
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
at the point
\varphi(x)
.

In this case we will also say use the notation
h\colon(M,\mathfrak{A})\to(N,\mathfrak{B})
to indicate that
h
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-map with respect to
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
, and denote by
\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(M,N)
the set of all such maps.

A homeomorphism
h\colon M\to N
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism (with respect to
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
)
or
between
(M,\mathfrak{A})
and
(N,\mathfrak{B})
, if
h\in\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(M,N)
and
h^{-1}\in\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\mathfrak{B},\mathfrak{A}}(N,M)
.

The set of all
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphisms
h\colon(M,\mathfrak{A})\to(N,\mathfrak{B})
will be denoted by
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(M,N)
.
In particular, if
M=N
, and
\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{B}
, then
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{A}}(M,M)
will also be abbreviated to
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A}}(M)
or even to
\mathcal{D}^{k}(M)
if the corresponding
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure is assumed from the context.

The following easy lemma gives another characterization of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures.

Lemma 3.4.1
.

Two
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
on
M
coincide iff
\mathrm{id}_{M}\in\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(M,M)
, i.e. the identity map of
M
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism with respect to them.

Example 3.4.2
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an open subset.
Then
U
admits an atlas
\mathsf{Id}_{U}:=\{(U,\mathrm{id}_{U})\}
consisting of a unique chart being just the inclusion map
\mathrm{id}_{U}\colon U\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
This atlas is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
The corresponding
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure on
U
will be called
canonical
and denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{U}
.

Also, if
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is another open subset, then a map
h\colon U\to V
belongs to

\mathcal{C}^{k}_{\mathcal{I}_{U},\mathcal{I}_{V}}(U,V)
iff
h
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
as a map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces in the usual sense.

Example 3.4.3
.

Let

4.
Uniqueness of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures on
\mathbb{R}
up to a diffeomorphism

In this section
M
is a one-dimensional locally Euclidean space and
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
We will describe a way of joining charts on
M
.
This technique is mentioned in
[
15
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
be two charts on
M
.
Say that
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable
if they are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible and

\displaystyle\varphi(U)=(a;c),

\displaystyle\varphi(U\cap V)=\psi(U\cap V)=(b;c),

\displaystyle\psi(V)=(b;d)

for some
a<b<c<d\in\mathbb{R}
.

In this case every open embedding
\sigma\colon U\cup V\to\mathbb{R}
, regarded as a chart
\mathsf{w}=(U\cup V,\sigma)
on
M
, will be called a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join
of
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
if, see Figure
4.1
,

(1)

\sigma(U)=\varphi(U)=(a;c)
and
\sigma(V)=\psi(V)=(b;d)
;

(2)

\mathsf{w}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
;

(3)

\sigma|_{U\setminus V}=\varphi|_{U\setminus V}\colon U\setminus V\to(a;c]
and
\sigma|_{V\setminus U}=\psi|_{V\setminus U}\colon V\setminus U\to[b;d)
.

Figure 4.1.
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable charts

The first condition implies that
\sigma(U\cap V)=(c;b)
, while the latter condition can be formulated so that
\sigma
differs from
\varphi
and
\psi
only on the intersection
U\cap V
, or equivalently, that the transition maps
\sigma\circ\varphi^{-1}
and
\sigma\circ\psi^{-1}
are identity on
U\setminus V
and
V\setminus U
respectively.

Our aim is to establish the following theorem

Theorem 4.2
.

(1)

Every pair of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable charts
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
has a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join.

(2)

Let
\mathsf{A}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on
M
having a pair of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable charts
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
such that for every other chart
\mathsf{y}=(Y,\xi)
from
\mathsf{A}
we have that

Y\cap U\cap V=\varnothing.

Then for any
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join
\mathsf{w}
of
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
the following collection of charts

\mathsf{B}=\bigl(\mathsf{A}\setminus\{\mathsf{u},\mathsf{v}\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}\},

obtained by replacing
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
with
\mathsf{w}
, is still a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on
M
being also
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{A}
.

Proof.

First we establish the following simple lemma.

Lemma 4.2.1
.

Let

b<c\in\mathbb{R}
and

g\colon(b;c)\to(b;c)
be a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism preserving orientation.
Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
and a diffeomorphism
p\colon(b;c)\to(b;c)
such that

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=x\ \text{for}\ x\in(b;b+\varepsilon],

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=g(x)\ \text{for}\ x\in[c-\varepsilon;c).

Proof.

It suffices to find
\varepsilon>0
and a strictly positive function
\gamma\colon[b;c)\to(0;+\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\gamma(x)=1\ \text{for}\ x\in[b;b+\varepsilon],

\displaystyle\gamma(x)=g^{\prime}(x)\ \text{for}\ x\in[c-\varepsilon;c),

\displaystyle\smallint_{b}^{c}\gamma(s)ds=c-b,

and then put
p(x)=b+\int_{b}^{x}\gamma(s)ds
.

As
g
is a preserving orientation homeomorphism of
(b;c)
, we have that

b=\lim\limits_{x\to b+0}g(x)<\lim\limits_{x\to c-0}g(x)=c.

Then one can easily find
\varepsilon>0
and two
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
functions
\alpha,\beta\colon[b;c]\to[0;+\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\alpha=1\ \text{on}\ [b;b+\varepsilon],\qquad\alpha=0\ \text{on}\ [b+2\varepsilon;c-2\varepsilon],\qquad\alpha=g^{\prime}\ \text{on}\ [c-\varepsilon;c],

\displaystyle A:=c-b-\smallint_{b}^{c}\alpha(s)ds>0,\qquad\beta^{-1}(0)=[b;b+\varepsilon]\cup[c-\varepsilon;c].

Denote
B=\smallint_{b}^{c}\beta(s)ds
.
Then the function
\gamma=\alpha+(A/B)\beta
has the above properties.
∎

Now we can prove Theorem
4.2
.

(1)

Let
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
be two
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable charts on

.
Not loosing generality, assume that

\displaystyle\varphi(U)=(0;2),

\displaystyle\varphi(U\cap V)=\psi(U\cap V)=(1;2),

\displaystyle\psi(V)=(1;3).

.
We need to construct a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join

Let

\mathsf{u}
to
\mathsf{v}
.
Then by Lemma

one can find
\varepsilon>0
and a

-diffeomorphism

such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=x\ \text{for}\ x\in(0;1+\varepsilon),

(4.2)

\displaystyle p(x)

\displaystyle=g(x)\ \text{for}\ x\in(2-\varepsilon;2).

Consider the homeomorphism

Then by (

Since

Note that

\displaystyle\sigma\circ\varphi^{-1}

-diffeomorphisms,

is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join of
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
.

(2)

Let
\mathsf{u}=(U,\varphi)
and
\mathsf{v}=(V,\psi)
be two

-joinable charts in the atlas

such that for every other chart

from

.
Let also
\mathsf{w}
be any

-join of
\mathsf{u}
and
\mathsf{v}
.
We need to prove that

\mathsf{B}=\bigl(\mathsf{A}\setminus\{\mathsf{u},\mathsf{v}\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}\},

-atlas on

-compatible with

.

Let us show that

4.3.
Chain-like atlases

Let
\mathsf{B}=\{\mathsf{v}_{j}=(V_{j},\psi_{j})\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a countable
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on the real line
\mathbb{R}
.
Say that
\mathsf{B}
is
chain-like
if for each
j\in\mathbb{Z}
the following conditions hold:

(G1)

the charts
\mathsf{v}_{j}
and
\mathsf{v}_{j+1}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable;

(G2)

V_{i}\cap V_{j}\cap V_{j+1}=\varnothing
for all
i\neq j,j+1
.

Equivalently, suppose that
\psi_{j}(V_{j})=(a_{j};b_{j})
for some
a_{j}<b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
,

.
Then
\mathsf{B}
is

if for every

we have that

•

are finite,

•

a_{j}<b_{j-1}<a_{j+1}<b_{j}
,

•

\psi_{j}(V_{j}\cap V_{j+1})=\psi_{j+1}(V_{j}\cap V_{j+1})=(a_{j+1};b_{j})
.

Notice that for any pairs of strictly increasing two-sided sequences

and
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
\mathbb{R}
there exists a
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
-diffeomorphism

such that

for

.
Hence one can always assume that for such a chain-like atlas we have that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to-\infty}a_{j}=\lim_{j\to-\infty}b_{j}=-\infty,

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to+\infty}a_{j}=\lim_{j\to+\infty}b_{j}=+\infty,

which is equivalent to the assumption that

(G3)

\mathop{\cup}\limits_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{j}(V_{j})=\mathbb{R}
.

.

(1)

For every
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas
\mathsf{A}=\{(U_{i},\varphi_{i})\}_{i\in\Lambda}

\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas
\mathsf{B}
being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{B}
.

(2)

Let
\mathsf{B}
be a chain-like
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on
\mathbb{R}
.
Then there exists a homeomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the chart

\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{B}

Proof.

(1)

Since
\mathbb{R}
is one-dimensional and paracompact, and the domains of
\mathsf{A}
constitute an open cover of
\mathbb{R}

\{V_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}

\mathbb{R}
such that for every

(a)

V_{j}=(a_{j};b_{j})
for some

such that
a_{j}<b_{j-1}<a_{j+1}<b_{j}

(4.5)

V_{i}\cap V_{j}\cap V_{j+1}=\varnothing,\qquad i\neq j,j+1;

(b)

Note that for any
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-embdding

\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{A}
.
In particular, one can choose

so that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(V_{j})=(a_{j};b_{j}),

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(V_{j-1}\cap V_{j})=(a_{j};b_{j-1})

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(V_{j}\cap V_{j+1})=(a_{j+1};b_{j}),

Indeed, note
\varphi_{j}(V_{j})=(c_{j};d_{j})
for some

Take

Suppose now that

Then by Theorem

there exists a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join
\mathsf{w}_{0,1}=(V_{0}\cup V_{1},\sigma_{0,1})
of
\mathsf{v}_{0}
and
\mathsf{v}_{1}
, such that the collection of charts

\mathsf{B}_{0,1}:=\bigl(\mathsf{B}\setminus\{\mathsf{v}_{0},\mathsf{v}_{1}\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}_{0,1}\}

is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{B}
.
By definition,
\mathsf{w}_{0,1}
differs from
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{1}
only on the intersection

which is disjoint from other chars from

.
This easily implies that
\mathsf{B}_{0,1}
is still chain-like.

In particular, the charts
\mathsf{v}_{-1}
and
\mathsf{w}_{0,1}
are
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-joinable, and again by Theorem

for any of their
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-join

\mathsf{w}_{-1,1}=\bigl(V_{-1}\cup W_{0,1}=V_{-1}\cup V_{0}\cup V_{1},\ \sigma_{-1,1}\bigr)

the collection of charts

\mathsf{B}_{-1,1}:=\bigl(\mathsf{B}\setminus\{\mathsf{v}_{-1},\mathsf{w}_{0,1}\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}_{-1,1}\}=\bigl(\mathsf{B}\setminus\{\mathsf{v}_{-1},\mathsf{v}_{0},\mathsf{v}_{1}\}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}_{-1,1}\}

is a chain-like
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{B}_{0,1}
and therefore with
\mathsf{B}
.
Again notice that
\sigma_{-1,1}
differs from with
\psi_{-1}
and
\sigma_{0,1}
only on the intersection

V_{-1}\cap(V_{0}\cap V_{1})=V_{-1}\cap V_{0}.

In particular,
\sigma_{-1,1}=\psi_{0}
on the compact segment

Thus, by induction for each

we can construct a sequence of chain-like
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlases

\mathsf{B}_{-n,n}:=\bigl(\mathsf{B}\setminus\{\mathsf{v}_{j}\}_{j=-n}^{n}\bigr)\cup\{\mathsf{w}_{-n,n}\}

where
\mathsf{w}_{-n,n}=\bigl(\mathop{\cup}\limits_{j=-n}^{n}V_{j},\sigma_{-n,n}\bigr)
is a chart such that
\sigma_{-n,n}\bigl(\mathop{\cup}\limits_{j=-n}^{n}V_{j}\bigr)=(a_{-n};b_{n})
and
\sigma_{-n,n}=\sigma_{-n+1,n-1}
on the compact segment

A_{n}:=\bigl(\mathop{\cup}\limits_{j=-n}^{n}V_{j}\bigr)\setminus\bigl(V_{-n}\cup V_{n}\bigr).

Since
A_{n}\subset\mathrm{Int}{A_{n+1}}
and
\mathbb{R}=\mathop{\cup}\limits_{n=0}^{+\infty}A_{n}
, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
there exists

such that

\sigma_{-i,i}(x)=\sigma_{-j,j}(x)\ \text{for all}\ i,j\geq n.

Then due to (
4.4
), we get a well-defined homeomorphism
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(x)=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sigma_{-n,n}(x)
, which is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with all the above atlases and, in particular, with
\mathsf{B}
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Every
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure
\mathfrak{B}
on
\mathbb{R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphic with the canonical one given by the atlas
\mathsf{Id}_{\mathbb{R}}=\{(\mathbb{R},\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}})\}
with a single chart.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.3.1
,
\mathfrak{B}
is represented by an atlas
\mathsf{B}=\{(\mathbb{R},\psi)\}
consisting of a single chart given by some homeomorphism
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
On the other hand, the canonical
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{R}}
is also given by the atlas with a single chart whose coordinate homeomorphism
\mathrm{id}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
has the same image
\mathbb{R}
as
\psi
.
Then by Lemma
3.5.1
, the map
\psi=\psi\circ\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}}^{-1}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism between
(\mathbb{R},\mathfrak{B})
and
(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{R}})
.
∎

Corollary 4.4.1
.

Let
M
be a one-dimensional locally Euclidean topological space,
\mathsf{A}
a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on
M
, and
U\subset M
be an open subset homeomorphic with
\mathbb{R}
.
Then there exists a homeomorphism
\varphi\colon U\to\mathbb{R}
being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{A}
.
In other words,
\mathsf{A}\cup\{(U,U)\}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-atlas on
M
being
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-compatible with
\mathsf{A}
.

We finish this section with the following well-known statement which could be proved in using the approach of similar to Theorem
4.3.1
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
M
be a connected Hausdorff one-dimensional locally Euclidean topological space.
Then for any two
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
on
M
there exists a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism

.
In other words, the action of the group of homeomorphisms

on the set of all
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures on

is transitive.

5.

5.2.
Group
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)

From now on fix some

\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
be the group of
monotone

\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphisms of
\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
(i.e. either increasing or decreasing).

Example 5.2.1
.

The diffeomorphism
h\colon\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\to\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
,
h(x)=1/x
, is
not monotone
and therefore does not belong to
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
.

Notice that for each
h\in\mathcal{W}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)
its restriction
h|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 0}\colon\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\to\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
is a monotone
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism.

Lemma 5.2.2
.

The map
\gamma\colon\mathcal{W}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)\to\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
,
\gamma(h)=h|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 0}
, is an isomorphism of groups.

Proof.

Evidently,
\gamma
is a monomorphism, and we need to show that
\gamma
is surjective.
Let
g\in\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
.
Since
g
is monotone,
\lim\limits_{x\to 0}g(x)=0
, whence
g
extends to a homeomorphism
\hat{g}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by
\hat{g}=g
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
and
\hat{g}(0)=0
.
It follows, that
\hat{g}\in\mathcal{W}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)
and
\gamma(\hat{g})=g
, i.e.
\gamma
is surjective as well.
∎

For each

the homeomorphism
\gamma^{-1}(g)
will be called the
0
-extension
on

.
Note also that we have the following inclusion

\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)\ \subset\ \mathcal{W}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\gamma^{-1}}}{{\equiv}}\ \mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0).

Thus, one can regard
\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)
as a subgroup of
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
.
It is easy to check that
\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)
is not normal in
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{mon}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus 0)
.

Lemma 5.2.3
.

The group
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{R},0)
is normal in
\mathcal{W}^{k}(\mathbb{R},0)
.

Proof.

Let
h\in\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{R},0)
and

.
Then

is fixed near
0\in\mathbb{R}
and its restriction to
\mathbb{R}\setminus 0
is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism.
Hence

is a
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}
fixed near
0
, i.e. it belongs to
\mathcal{D}^{k}_{\mathrm{nb}}(\mathbb{R},0)
.
∎

5.3.

\mathbb{L}

A

and

Notice that for every such an atlas, its transition map from
(U,\varphi)
to

\psi\circ\varphi^{-1}\colon\mathbb{R}\setminus 0\to\mathbb{R}\setminus 0

-diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}\setminus 0

5.5.
Special minimal atlases

Our next aim is to provide examples of
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
on
\mathbb{L}
for which either of sets
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{fix}}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{L})
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B}}(\mathbb{L})
can be empty or non-empty.
For that reason it will be convenient to work only with
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structures admitting
special
minimal atlases defined as follows.

Recall that
U=\mathbb{R}
and
V=(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\cup\{\bar{0}\}
by the construction of
\mathbb{L}
.
Hence, we have the following homeomorphisms: the identity map
\mathrm{id}_{u}\colon U\to\mathbb{R}

\mathrm{id}_{v}\colon V\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathrm{id}_{v}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x=\bar{0},\\
x=\mathrm{id}_{u}(x),&x\in V\setminus\bar{0}.\end{cases}

In particular,
\mathrm{id}_{u}=\mathrm{id}_{v}
on
U\setminus 0=V\setminus\bar{0}

Then for each

one can define the following minimal atlas

\mathsf{S}(h):=\{(U,\mathrm{id}_{u}),(V,h\circ\mathrm{id}_{v})\}

whose transition map is

h\circ\mathrm{id}_{v}\circ\mathrm{id}_{u}^{-1}|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 0}=h|_{\mathbb{R}\setminus 0}.

Hence, its
0
-extension is
h
, i.e.
g_{\mathsf{S}(h)}=h
.

The atlas
\mathsf{S}(h)
will be called the

Denote by
\mathfrak{S}(h)
the corresponding
\mathcal{C}^{k}
-structure on
\mathbb{L}
defined by the atlas
\mathsf{S}(h)
, so it is determined by
h
.
