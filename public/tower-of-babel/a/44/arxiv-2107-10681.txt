Abstract
. We consider the algebra Σ ˙ ​ ( ℒ ) \dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}}) generated by the inner-limit derivations over the GICAR {\rm GICAR} algebra of a fermion gas populating an aperiodic Delone set ℒ {\mathcal{L}} . Under standard physical assumptions such as finite interaction range, Galilean invariance of the theories and continuity with respect to the deformations of the aperiodic lattices, we demonstrate that the image of Σ ˙ ​ ( ℒ ) \dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}}) through the Fock representation can be completed to a groupoid-solvable pro- C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebra. Our result is the first step towards unlocking the K K -theoretic tools available for separable C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebras for applications in the context of interacting fermions.

Introduction and Main Statements

The physical observables of a self-interacting Fermi gas populating a uniformly discrete lattice
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
generate the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of canonical anti-commutation relations (CAR) over the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})

[
18
]
, denoted here by
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. Our interest is in the dynamics of these observables, formalized as a point-wise continuous one-parameter group

\bm{\alpha}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\rm Aut}\big({\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})\big).

We will refer to
\bm{\alpha}
as the time evolution. Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
be the set of elements
A\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
for which
\bm{\alpha}_{t}(A)
is first order differentiable w.r.t.
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
is stable under addition and multiplication, hence it is actually a sub-algebra of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. Furthermore,

\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}(A):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\tfrac{1}{t}\big(\bm{\alpha}_{t}(A)-A\big)

defines a
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
-valued linear map over
{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\alpha}}
, which obeys Leibniz’s rule

\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}(AA^{\prime})=\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}(A)A^{\prime}+A\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}(A^{\prime}),\hskip 8.19447ptA,A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\bm{\alpha}},

hence
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
is a (possibly unbounded) derivation
[
17
]
. We will refer to
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
as the generator of
{\bm{\alpha}}
. Consider now a family of time evolutions whose
{\mathcal{D}}
’s contain a fixed dense
\ast
-subalgebra
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})\subset{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, which is invariant under the action of all the generators of the family. Under such conditions, these generators can be composed and they form a sub-algebra of the algebra of linear maps over
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
, which we refer to as the
core
algebra associated with the family of time evolutions. In this work, we investigate the core algebra associated with a large family of time evolutions, which we claim it contains
all
physically reasonable time evolutions over a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}
. The goal is to complete this and an associated core algebra of physical Hamiltonians to pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebras (in the sense of
[
40
]
), and to supply fine characterizations of these completions in a manner that unlocks the
K
-theoretic tools available for separable
C^{\ast}
-algebras. To our knowledge, such tools are not yet available in the context of interacting many-fermion systems.

Before elaborating any further, let us acknowledge that, for the 1-fermion sector, the program outlined above has been in place for almost three decades. Indeed, a fundamental result by Bellissard and Kellendonk
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
30
]
states that, for Galilean invariant theories, the generators of the 1-fermion dynamics over a uniformly discrete pattern form a separable groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra, canonically associated to the given pattern. By identifying this separable
C^{\ast}
-algebra, Bellissard and Kellendonk essentially completed the stably-homotopic classification of all the available Galilean invariant gapped 1-fermion Hamiltonians over a given pattern, which now can be enumerated via the
K
-theories of the Bellissard-Kellendonk groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra. In
[
13
,
42
]
and
[
14
]
, the reader can find models for implementing this program in the context of disordered and, respectively, generic lattices in arbitrary dimensions. At the practical level, the program pioneered by Bellissard and Kellendonk spurred new directions in materials science, resulting in a high-throughput of novel topological materials and meta-materials
[
4
,
7
,
20
,
21
,
22
,
35
,
38
,
45
,
46
,
56
]
, based on the sampling of different classes of Delone sets. The results in the present can facilitate similar
developments for arbitrary
N
-fermion sectors.

We will be dealing exclusively with time evolutions that conserve the number of fermions, hence with the ones that drop to time evolutions over the
{\rm GICAR}
-subalgebra of gauge invariant physical observables. We will enforce two natural physical conditions: 1) the generators of the time evolutions depend continuously on the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
when the latter is deformed inside a fixed class of Delone sets and 2) the theories are Galilean invariant (see section
5.2
and below). A large portion of our work is dedicated to formulating these two properties in a rigorous and natural framework and to identifying the most general expression of the generators that deliver such time evolutions. Prototypical examples are supplied by Hamiltonians generated from many-body potentials, such as

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}}\sum_{\{x_{i},x^{\prime}_{i}\}\in{\mathcal{L}}}\ w_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k})\,a^{\ast}_{x_{1}}\cdots a^{\ast}_{x_{k}}\,a_{x^{\prime}_{k}}\cdots a_{x^{\prime}_{1}},

(1.1)

where the
k
-body potentials
w_{k}:({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{k}\to{\mathbb{C}}
obey several constraints, such as continuity, anti-symmetry against permutations and invariance under global shifts

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k})\mapsto(x_{1}-x,\ldots,x_{k}-x;x^{\prime}_{1}-x,\ldots,x^{\prime}_{k}-x).

The
a
’s in Eq. (
1.1
) are the generators of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. If the
k
-body potentials have finite interaction range,
i.e.

w_{k}
’s vanish whenever the diameter of the sets
\{x_{i}\}\cup\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}
exceed a fixed value, then
H_{\mathcal{L}}
defines a generator for a dynamics, which comes in the form of a closable unbounded derivation

{\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}(A)=\imath[A,H_{\mathcal{L}}],\hskip 8.19447ptA\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})\subset{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}),

with a fixed dense and invariant domain (see section
5.1
). Note that a many-body potential defines an entire correspondence
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto H_{\mathcal{L}}
, which is tacitly assumed to be continuous w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}
. Now, if
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
happen to enter a relation like
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{L}}-x
for some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then

{\rm Ad}_{H_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}}=S_{x}\circ{\rm Ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}\circ S_{x}^{-1},

(1.2)

where
S_{x}:{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})\to{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}-x)
are the obvious
C^{*}
-algebra isomorphisms (which properly map the domains). Eq. (
1.2
) reflects the Galilean invariance of the theory, which was already encoded in the many-body potentials.

At first sight, formalizing such Hamiltonians seem straightforward, but a fundamental difficulty presents itself. As we do not have a topology on the set of our models, the only way to define the continuity w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}
is through the coefficients of the Hamiltonians, such as the
w_{k}
’s in Eq. (
1.1
). Therefore, from both the physical and mathematical points of view, identifying the correct domain of these coefficients as a topological space is paramount. Note that, besides the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
, the orderings of the sets
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}
in Eq. (
1.1
) are part of the data defining the coefficients and, for a generic Delone set, there is no canonical choice for these orderings. Furthermore, under continuous cyclic deformations of the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
, the orderings of the sets
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}
can change and this can happen even when the deformations occur inside the transversal of a single pattern (see Definition
3.10
), as it is the case for topological lattice defects
[
43
]
or for patterns like in our Example
3.13
. Such phenomena present a challenge because, while the Hamiltonian (
1.1
) returns to itself under such cyclic deformations, the coefficients
do not
.

In Section
5
, we construct the natural domains of the Hamiltonian coefficients as covering spaces of the topological space of all Delone sets. First, we introduce the set of pairs
({\mathcal{L}},V)
, where
{\mathcal{L}}
is a Delone set and
V
is a subset of
{\mathcal{L}}
of cardinal
|V|=k
, and we topologize this set such that it becomes an (infinite) cover of the space of Delone sets. Then we introduce a second, finite cover over the topological space of pairs
({\mathcal{L}},V)
, which we call the
order cover
, such that the group of deck transformations of this covering space is the full permutation group
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}
on
k
elements. A point in this cover is a triple
\xi=({\mathcal{L}},V_{\xi},\chi_{\xi})
, where
\chi_{\xi}:\{1,\ldots,k\}\to V_{\xi}
is a bijection, hence an ordering of the subset
V_{\xi}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
(see section
5.3
). There is one such space for each
k>1
and we call
\mathfrak{a}_{k}(\xi):={\mathcal{L}}
the
k
-body covering map of the space of Delone sets.

A generic finite interaction range Hamiltonian evaluated at
{\mathcal{L}}
then takes the form

H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}}\tfrac{1}{k!}\sum_{\xi,\zeta\in\mathfrak{a}^{-1}_{k}({\mathcal{L}})}h_{k}(\xi,\zeta)\,\bm{a}^{\ast}(\xi)\bm{a}(\zeta),

(1.3)

where

\bm{a}(\xi):=a_{\chi_{\xi}(k)}\cdots a_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}),

and the
h_{k}
are bi-equivariant coefficients w.r.t. to the deck transformations of the order cover, invariant under the simultaneous translation of the arguments, continuous in the arguments and supported on pairs
(\xi,\zeta)
for which the diameter of
V_{\xi}\cup V_{\zeta}
is smaller than a fixed positive number (see subsection
5.4
for complete details). The derivations
{\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}
corresponding to Hamiltonians of the type (
1.3
) generate the core algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
we mentioned in the first paragraph.

Of course, the Hamiltonian coefficients can be also defined over a twofold orientation cover of the space of Delone sets. However, it turns out that the full order covers over the space of Delone sets not only supply natural domains for the Hamiltonian coefficients, but also hold the key to our generalization of the Bellissard-Kellendonk groupoid algebras to the
N
-fermion sectors. As a topological space, for each
N
, the groupoid
\mathcal{G}_{N}
appears naturally as a transversal to the translation action of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on the
N
-order cover. The ordering
\chi_{\xi}
give us a handle to equip these transversals with the algebraic structure of a groupoid. Indeed, viewing each subset
V_{\xi}
as anchored at
\chi_{\xi}(1)
allows us to pull
V_{\xi}
to the origin of the physical space and also gives us the means to define the set of composable pairs (see section
6.3
for details). The permutation group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
acts on
\mathcal{G}_{N}
by bisections and this actions are non-trivial as they involve permutations
and
translations of the points (see sections
2.3
and
6.4
).

We now describe the process that enables us to characterize the algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
. The key observation is that these are inner-limit derivations
[
17
]
and such derivations have a special relation with the double sided ideals: They leave them invariant and, as such, the inner-limit derivations descend to derivations on quotients of double sided ideals. The lattice of double sided ideals of
{\rm GICAR}
algebra was worked out by Bratteli, who uncover the following structure (see section
4.5
):

Theorem 1.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

The
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
algebra is a solvable
C^{\ast}
-algebra (of infinite length), in the sense of Dynin
[
25
]
. Specifically, it accepts a filtration by primitive ideals

\cdots\rightarrowtail{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\rightarrowtail\cdots\rightarrowtail{\rm GI}_{1}({\mathcal{L}})\rightarrowtail{\rm GI}_{0}({\mathcal{L}})={\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})

(1.4)

and

{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})/{\rm GI}_{N+1}({\mathcal{L}})\simeq{\mathbb{K}},

(1.5)

for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
{\mathbb{K}}
is the algebra of compact operators.

Since the derivations descend on the quotients (
1.5
), we obtain a sequence of representations of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
by (bounded) derivations on the algebra of compact operators. The bounded derivations over the algebra of compact operators have been already completely characterized, cf.
[
17
, Example 1.6.4]
, specifically, they always take the form of commutators with bounded operators over the underlying Hilbert space, which in the present context happens to be the
N
-fermion sector
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}({\mathcal{L}})
of the Fock space. As a result, there are sub-algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
of the algebras of bounded operators over
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}({\mathcal{L}})
as well as a tower of representations of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
inside
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
. Furthermore, if
\eta
is the vacuum state and
\pi_{\eta}
is the associated Fock representation of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, then the latter induces a representation
\dot{\pi}_{\eta}
of
\dot{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{L}}
on a dense linear sub-space of
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}({\mathcal{L}})
and
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})/{\rm ker}\,\dot{\pi}_{\eta}\simeq\varprojlim\,\dot{\Gamma}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
, where
\dot{\Gamma}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}\mathfrak{H}_{N}
. Note that
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})/{\rm ker}\,\dot{\pi}_{\eta}
represents the core algebra of physical Hamiltonians (see section
5.5
for details and justification).

In a followup step, we compute the
C^{\ast}
-envelopes of
\mathfrak{H}_{N}
’s and promote the tower
\{\dot{\Gamma}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\}
to a projective tower of
C^{\ast}
-algebras
\{\Gamma_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\}
, whose inverse limit
\mathfrak{H}({\mathcal{L}})
is the pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra mentioned in the first paragraph of the section. This brings us to our first major result, stated in Theorem
6.24
, saying that the core algebra of physical Hamiltonians embeds into the pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra
\mathfrak{H}({\mathcal{L}})
. A similar statement holds for the algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
(see Theorem
6.27
). Our second major result states that
\mathfrak{H}({\mathcal{L}})
affords the following explicit characterization:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}_{N}:{\mathfrak{H}}({\mathcal{L}})\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
be the epimorphisms associated with the inverse limit and define the closed double sided ideals
{\mathcal{J}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}}):={\rm ker}\,\mathfrak{p}_{N}
. Then
\{{\mathcal{J}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\}_{N}
supplies a filtration of
\mathfrak{H}({\mathcal{L}})
and

{\mathcal{J}}_{N-1}({\mathcal{L}})/{\mathcal{J}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})=\pi_{\mathcal{L}}\Big({\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N},{\mathbb{C}})\big)\Big).

(1.6)

Here,
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
is the groupoid canonically associated to
{\mathcal{L}}
generalizing the Bellissard-Kellendonk groupoid from the single to the
N
-fermion sector,
C^{\ast}_{r,\mathcal{S}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N},{\mathbb{C}})
is the
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
-bi-equivariant groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra,
{\mathcal{M}}
indicates the essential extension to the multiplier algebra and
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
is a left regular representation of the groupoid
C^{*}
-algebra.

We will show that
{\mathcal{J}}_{N-1}({\mathcal{L}})/{\mathcal{J}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
are isomorphic to the images of
\mathfrak{H}({\mathcal{L}})
through its Fock representations on the
N
-fermion sectors. As such, the algebra of Galilean invariant Hamiltonians of
N
fermions over the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
coincides with multiplier algebra
{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N},{\mathbb{C}})\big)
. This reduces to the (unital) Bellissard-Kellendonk groupoid algebra for
N=1
. Our third major result states that the groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
continue to be second countable, Hausdorff and étale for
N>1
, hence its groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra is separable (see section
6.3
). Furthermore, as stated already, this groupoid accepts a (highly non-trivial) 2-action of the permutation group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
(see Definition
2.21
and section
6.4
).

In section
8.1
, we argue that the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of the groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
generates self-binding dynamics, in the sense that under the evolution generated by a Hamiltonian from
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N},{\mathbb{C}})
, the
N
fermions evolve in one single cluster. If the Hamiltonian comes from the corona of the multiplier extension, then the dynamics is scattered, in the sense that the
N
fermions evolve in two or more separate clusters. When the many-body covers are disconnected, as it is the case for periodic and quasi-periodic lattices, we show in section
7
that
C^{\ast}_{r,\mathcal{S}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N},{\mathbb{C}})
is Morita equivalent to the 1-fermion Bellissard-Kellendonk
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{1},{\mathbb{C}})
. Thus, in these special cases, the stably-homotopic classification of the self-binding states of
N
fermions is no more complicated than that of single fermion states. We point out that this has been already observed in numerical experiments (see
[
34
]
).

We follow up with pointed remarks in order to guide the reader through the significance of the above results, its relation to other works and to highlight important aspects that were left to future investigations:

Remark 1.3
.

A good portion of our program can be repeated for AF-algebras other than the
{\rm GICAR}
algebra, provided they have a rich lattice of ideals and the descended derivations on the quotients are by commutators with elements from their multiplier algebras. It is important to note that the pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra completions generated by the mechanism explained above are not universal, in the sense that they depend in an essential way on the chosen filtration. In the present context, the filtration mentioned in Theorem
1.1
makes the most physical sense and the associated pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra gives a satisfactory framework for the dynamics of fermions. Nevertheless, it will be very interesting to investigate the range of pro-
C^{\ast}
-completions that can be generated from other filtrations or even from other AF-algebras, and to study the relations among these different completions. Lastly, it will be interesting to see how all this fits into or benefits from the existing frameworks on representations of
\ast
-algebras by unbounded operators
[
36
,
48
]
.

{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})

Remark 1.5
.

The spaces of units of the groupoids
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
are only locally compact, except for
N=1
. Hence, the groupoid algebras for
N>1
do not have a unit and the extensions to the multiplier algebras are non-trivial. Since
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
does not contain the compacts, neither does its multiplier extension. As such, this extension is not the whole algebra of bounded operators over the
N
-sector of the Fock space and we expect
{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})\big)
to have a very interesting structure. A glimpse into this structure is supplied by Proposition
6.39
, where a continuous groupoid morphism
{\mathcal{G}}_{N+M}\to{\mathcal{G}}_{N}\times{\mathcal{G}}_{M}
is defined. This groupoid morphism is neither continuous nor does it preserve the Haar system. Nonetheless, its pullback map supplies a
{\mathbb{C}}
-module map
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})\otimes C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{M})\to{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{N+M})\big)
, which actually lands in the corona (see Remark
6.41
). We conjecture that the multiplier extensions can be fully characterized by such maps.

\Diamond

Remark 1.6
.

In Example 2.37 of
[
55
]
, Williams describes how to generate new groupoids by blowing up the unit space of an existing groupoid. As we shall see in Sec.
6.6
, the groupoids
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
,
N>1
, are all blow-ups of
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
, which automatically implies that the associated
C^{\ast}
-algebras are Morita equivalent (see Th. 2.52 in
[
55
]
). The
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
-bi-equivariant sub-algebras are, however,
not
expected to be Morita equivalent in general. In fact, the 2-action of the permutation group does not admit a straightforward interpretation when the groupoids
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
are presented as blow-ups of
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
. Nevertheless, as we already announced, there are special yet important cases when the
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
-bi-equivariant sub-algebra is in fact Morita equivalent to
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{1})
. As a result, for these special cases, we have precise
K
-theoretic statements.

.

In the light of the previous two remarks, we believe that our formalism supplies new tools and a fresh framework for the problem of the thermodynamic limit (

-sectors are equally interesting, from both a theoretical and a practical point of view. For example,

Remark 1.10
.

To our knowledge, the notion of bi-equivariant groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra has not been used before. We were naturally led to it once the analysis was lifted to the order cover and the Hamiltonians were expressed via bi-equivariant coefficients as in Eq. (
1.3
). The concept is developed in section
2.3
and is based on the notion of 2-actions of a group on groupoids and
C^{\ast}
-algebras. By viewing groupoids as categories, a 2-action is the natural 2-category analogue of the action of a group on a space. Historically, such structures arose under the nomer of crossed module of a pair of groups, originally introduced by Whitehead
[
54
]
in the context of homotopy 2-types. The actions of crossed modules on groupoids and
C^{*}
-algebras are described in detail in
[
19
, Sections 2 and 3]
. We believe that the bi-equivariant groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra for spin-less fermions introduced here can be generalized, for example, to generate dynamical models for particles with various statistics, including non-abelian anyons.

\Diamond

Remark 1.11
.

The CAR algebra itself accepts a presentation as a groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra (see Example 1.10 in
[

]
, Example 8.3.5 in
[

]

). This, in fact, represented the starting point for our investigation but we soon realized that this presentation is too rigid because it assumes a fixed pre-ordering of the lattice points. That, unfortunately, is incompatible with a context where the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
can be deformed and the points can be exchanged.

Remark 1.12
.

The completion of the algebra of physical Hamiltonians puts a topology on the physical models. Indeed, as for any pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra, the topology is given by the semi-norms supplied by the
C^{\ast}
-norms of the projective tower. Let us acknowledge that the completion includes physical Hamiltonians with infinite interaction range. As we shall see in Proposition
6.46
, the coefficients
h_{k}
in Eq (
1.3
) define compactly supported functions over
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
. The passage from finite to infinite interaction range happens when the algebra of functions with compact support on
{\mathcal{G}}_{k}
is completed to the groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{k})
.

C^{\ast}

Section

2.
Bi-Equivariant Groupoid
C^{\ast}
-Algebras

This section develops the notion of group 2-actions on groupoids and a bi-equivariant version of the
C^{\ast}
-algebra associated to a groupoid in the presence of such a group 2-actions. There will be several specialized groupoids appearing in this work and, as we already stated in the introduction, our main results point to a certain groupoid structure in the dynamics of interacting fermions. As such, the reader needs a minimal familiarity with these structures and the first two subsections are intended to supply just that. In parallel, these subsections introduce notation and examples that will be used throughout.

2.1.
Background: Étale groupoids

We follow here and throughout the notations and the conventions from
[
49
, Ch. 8]
. We start with the algebraic structure of a groupoid. Abstractly, a groupoid is a small category in which all morphisms are invertible. Concretely, we have the following:

Definition 2.1
.

A groupoid consists of

•

A set
{\mathcal{G}}
equipped with an inverse map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{-1}
;

•

A subset
{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}\subset{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{G}}
equipped with a multiplication map
(\alpha,\beta)\mapsto\alpha\beta\in{\mathcal{G}}
,

such that

1)

(\alpha^{-1})^{-1}=\alpha
for all
\alpha\in{\mathcal{G}}
;

2)

If
(\alpha,\beta)
,
(\beta,\gamma)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
, then
(\alpha\beta,\gamma)
,
(\alpha,\beta\gamma)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
;

3)

(\alpha,\alpha^{-1})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
for all
\alpha\in{\mathcal{G}}
;

4)

For all
(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
,
(\alpha\beta)\beta^{-1}=\alpha
and
\alpha^{-1}(\alpha\beta)=\beta
.

The set
{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
is said to contain the pairs of composable elements and the set

{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)}=\{\alpha^{-1}\alpha,\ \alpha\in{\mathcal{G}}\}=\{\alpha\alpha^{-1},\ \alpha\in{\mathcal{G}}\}

is referred to as the space of units. The maps

\mathfrak{r},\ \mathfrak{s}:{\mathcal{G}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathfrak{r}(\alpha):=\alpha\alpha^{-1},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathfrak{s}(\alpha):=\alpha^{-1}\alpha

(2.1)

are called the range and the source maps, respectively. Note that
{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
can be equivalently defined as

{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}=\big\{(\alpha,\beta)\in{\mathcal{G}}\times{\mathcal{G}},\ \mathfrak{s}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{r}(\beta)\big\}.

Remark 2.2
.

It follows that

\mathfrak{r}(\alpha^{-1})=\mathfrak{s}(\alpha),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathfrak{s}(\alpha^{-1})=\mathfrak{r}(\alpha).

Hence,
(\alpha,\beta^{-1})\in{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}
iff
\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{s}(\beta)
. This is relevant for the product (
2.2
) and for the left regular representations (
2.11
). Many other useful properties and identities can be found in
[
49
, Ch. 8]
.

\Diamond

Below, we introduce several standard groupoids, which will appear in our discussion of aperiodic physical systems.

Example 2.3
.

For an
arbitrary
set

, one defines the matrix groupoid over

to be the set
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}=X\times X
. The set
{\mathcal{M}}_{X}^{(2)}\subset{\mathcal{M}}_{X}\times{\mathcal{M}}_{X}
of composable elements consists of pairs
((x,y),(z,w))
such that

and the composition of two composable elements is
(x,y)\cdot(y,z)=(x,z)
. The inversion map acts as
(x,y)^{-1}=(y,x)
. The space of units

consists of
(x,x)
,

, hence it can be canonically identified with

or any other discrete and infinite topological space, then the groupoid relates to the algebra of compact operators over the separable Hilbert space

2)

[

(

Remark 2.10

Among all topological groupoids, the étale groupoids are the equivalent of the discrete groups within the topological groups. Indeed, an important characteristic of étale groupoids is that

2.3.
Bi-equivariant groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a topological groupoid. The group of bisections
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})
of
\mathcal{G}
is the space of continuous maps

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}):=\left\{b:\mathcal{G}^{(0)}\to\mathcal{G}:\mathfrak{s}\circ b=\textnormal{Id},\hskip 8.19447pt\mathfrak{r}\circ b\textnormal{ is a homeomorphism}\right\}.

The group structure on

is given by

b_{1}\cdot b_{2}(\alpha):=b_{1}\big(\mathfrak{r}\circ b_{2}(\alpha)\big)b_{2}(\alpha),\hskip 8.19447ptb^{-1}(\alpha):=b\big((\mathfrak{r}\circ b)^{-1}(\alpha)\big)^{-1},\hskip 8.19447pt\alpha\in\mathcal{G}^{(0)}.

(2.5)

Here,
b^{-1}
is the inverse of
b
in
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{G}})
,
(\mathfrak{r}\circ b)^{-1}
denotes the inverse homeomorphism to
\mathfrak{r}\circ b
, whereas

denotes the inverse of

in
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G})
is the inclusion

is a locally compact group in the compact open topology

b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2}:=b_{1}\big(\mathfrak{r}(\alpha)\big)\,\alpha\,b_{2}^{-1}\big(\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)\big)^{-1},\hskip 8.19447ptb\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}),\hskip 8.19447pt\alpha\in\mathcal{G}.

Lemma 2.19
.

The actions satisfy

\displaystyle(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{-1}\cdot\alpha^{-1}\cdot b_{1}^{-1},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{r}(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})

\displaystyle=b_{1}\cdot\mathfrak{r}(\alpha)\cdot b_{1}^{-1},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{-1}\cdot\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)\cdot b_{2}.

(2.8)

Proof.

Indeed:

\displaystyle(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}(\alpha))\,\alpha^{-1}\,b_{1}(\mathfrak{r}(\alpha))^{-1}=b_{2}^{-1}\cdot\alpha^{-1}\cdot b_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{r}(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{r}\circ b_{1}(\mathfrak{r}(\alpha))=b_{1}(\mathfrak{r}(\alpha))\,\mathfrak{r}(\alpha)\,b_{1}(\mathfrak{r}(\alpha))^{-1}=b_{1}\cdot\mathfrak{r}(\alpha)\cdot b_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{s}(b_{1}\cdot\alpha\cdot b_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{s}\circ b_{2}^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}(\alpha))^{-1}=b_{2}^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}(\alpha))\,\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)\,b_{2}^{-1}(\mathfrak{s}(\alpha))^{-1}=b_{2}^{-1}\cdot\mathfrak{s}(\alpha)\cdot b_{2},

which prove the statements.∎

If

3.
Single Fermion Dynamics by groupoid Methods

Our main goals for this section are to highlight the important role played by groupoid
C^{*}
-algebras in the analysis of the dynamics of a single fermion hopping over an aperiodic lattice and to formulate the problem from a perspective that affords, at the conceptual level, a straightforward generalization to the case of interacting fermions. For this, we start from an algebra of local physical observables available to an experimenter. As we shall see, the time evolutions of these observables are implemented by groups of outer authomorphisms. Then the specialized groupoid algebras constructed by Bellissard
[
8
,
9
]
and Kellendonk
[
30
]
can be seen as formalizing the generators of these groups of outer automorphisms.

3.1.
The algebra of local observables

The setting is that of a network
{\mathcal{L}}
of identical quantum resonators, such as atoms or quantum circuits, rendered in the physical space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and whose identical internal structures have been fixed. When placed next to each other, the resonators couple via potentials that are entirely determined by their internal structure and space arrangement. As such, under the stated constraints, the dynamics of the collective degrees of freedom is
fully
determined by the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In Fig.
3.1
, we show such a network of quantum resonators, together with an experimenter who excites the resonators, perhaps with a laser beam, and then observes the dynamics of the coupled degrees of freedom. The experimenter can only excite resonators and take measurements on resonators near its position. In other words, the experimenter can only probe the local degrees of freedom. Of course, we are referring to the idealized case of an infinite sample.

Figure 3.1.
Schematic of a local observation.

In the 1-fermion setting, the algebra of observables available to the observer is that of finite-rank operators over the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
. If the resonators have more than one internal degree of freedom, this Hilbert space is tensored by
{\mathbb{C}}^{N}
but this detail can be dealt with later. By a limiting procedure, one can close this algebra to the
C^{\ast}
-algebra
{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of compact operators over
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
, which represents the algebra of local observables in the 1-fermion setting. The main interest, however, is not in this algebra but in the time evolution of the observables, which is formalized by a one parameter group of
*
-automorphisms

\bm{\alpha}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\rm Aut}\Big({\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)\Big).

(3.1)

We assume from the beginning that the time evolution is point-wise continuous,
i.e.
the map
t\mapsto\bm{\alpha}_{t}(K)\in{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
is continuous for all
K\in{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
. The generator
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
of the dynamics is defined on the dense
*
-sub-algebra of elements
K\in{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
for which
\bm{\alpha}_{t}(K)
is first order differentiable in
t
. The derivations over the algebra of compact operators have been fully characterized in
[
17
, Example 1.6.4]
. In particular, all
bounded
derivations come in the form

{\rm ad}_{H}(K)=\imath[K,H],\hskip 8.19447pt(\imath=\sqrt{-1}),

for some
H
from the algebra
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of bounded operators over
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
, which will be referred to as the Hamiltonian.

3.2.
Galilean invariant theories

As a bounded operator over
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
, the Hamiltonian always affords a presentation of the form

H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}}w_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})\,|x\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|,\hskip 8.19447ptw_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})\in{\mathbb{C}},

(3.2)

with the sum converging in the strong operator topology. The notation in Eq.
3.2
emphasizes that the Hamiltonian coefficients
w_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})
are fully determined by the pattern itself. The experimenter can modify the pattern and map again the coefficients, hence, at least in principle, the entire map
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto\{w_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})\}
can be accessed experimentally. Physical considerations enable us to assume that the coefficients vary continuously with
{\mathcal{L}}
, in a sense that will be made precise later.

The Hamiltonians display additional structure steaming from the Galilean invariance of the non-relativistic physical laws. Indeed, in the absence of any background field or potential, suppose that two patterns
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
enter the relation
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{L}}-y
. Then necessarily

w_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})=w_{x-y,x^{\prime}-y}({\mathcal{L}}-y),\hskip 8.19447ptx,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}},

(3.3)

and, certainly, this is what the laboratory measurements will return. Now, we fix a pattern
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
and apply these facts to the corresponding Hamiltonian. We find

H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}w_{0,x^{\prime}-x}({\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x)\,|x\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|,

and, if we introduce
y=x^{\prime}-x\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x
and drop the trivial index, then

H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x}w_{y}({\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x)\,|x\rangle\langle x+y|,

or

(H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\psi)(x)=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x}w_{y}({\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x)\,\psi(x+y),\hskip 8.19447pt\psi\in\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}}_{0}).

(3.4)

Remark 3.1
.

We want to stress that the equivariance relation (
3.3
) is not a statement about the pattern but about the physical processes involved in the coupling of the quantum resonators. It is for this reason that we speak of Galilean invariance as opposed to translational invariance, as the latter is often an attribute of a pattern rather than of a physical law.

\Diamond

As it was already pointed out in
[
15

Proposition 3.19

\Diamond

Remark 3.21

It follows from their very definition (

(3.8)

Remark 3.22

We recall that all

\Diamond

We now turn our attention to the
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C_{r}^{\ast}({\mathcal{G}}_{1})
canonically associated to the groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and, hence, to the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}

We stress again that

{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
, which has trivial

-theory.

\Diamond

As discussed in section

{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and are carried by the Hilbert spaces

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
and

\displaystyle\big(({\mathcal{L}}_{0},x)^{-1}\big)=\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x}f({\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x,y)\varphi\big(({\mathcal{L}}_{0},x+y)^{-1}\big).

(3.9)

T_{a}^{\ast}\pi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(f)T_{a}=\pi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-a}(f),\hskip 8.19447ptf\in C_{r}^{\ast}({\mathcal{G}}_{1}).

4.
Interacting Fermions: The Algebra of Local Observables

In this section, we first describe the
C^{\ast}
-algebra
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
of local observables for a system of many-fermions hopping on a discrete lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
. As in the previous section, this local algebra plays a similar role as a Hilbert space does when we define operators: It will help us define an algebra of derivations associated with the dynamics of the local observables. While our final goal is to characterize this algebra of derivations, the success of that program rests on a fine characterization of the structure of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, which is supplied in this section. Of particular importance is our choice of presenting the elements in a symmetric manner that treats all possible orderings of the anti-commuting generators on equal footing. This leads to the notion of bi-equivariant coefficients w.r.t. permutation groups, which later will be connected with the material introduced in section
2.3
. The Fock representation of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is treated in the same spirit using frames that are stable against the actions of the permutation groups. One of our important observations is that the product of operators continues to manifest itself as a certain convolution of the “symmetrized” matrix elements. The reader will also find here the characterization
[
16
]
of the lattice of ideals of
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, the sub-algebra of gauge invariant elements. Of essential importance for our program is the observation that
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
admits a filtration by primitive ideals and that the quotient of two consecutive such ideals is isomorphic to the algebra of compact operators. In other words,
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is a solvable
C^{\ast}
-algebra in the sense of
[
25
]
.

4.1.
The CAR and
{\rm GICAR}
algebras over a lattice

The setting is that of a Delone pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
whose points are populated by spin-less fermions. The algebra of local observables is supplied by the algebra of canonical anti-commutation relations over
{\mathcal{L}}
, denoted here by
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})

[
18
]
. It is constructed from a net
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\}
of finite subsets of
{\mathcal{L}}
, with
{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\subset{\mathcal{L}}_{k+1}
and
\bigcup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathcal{L}}_{k}={\mathcal{L}}
. For each of these finite subsets, one defines
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})
as the finite
\ast
-algebra generated by
a_{x}
,
x\in{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
, and the relations

a_{x}a_{x^{\prime}}+a_{x^{\prime}}a_{x}=0,\hskip 8.19447pta_{x}^{\ast}a_{x^{\prime}}+a_{x^{\prime}}a_{x}^{\ast}=\delta_{x,x^{\prime}}.

(4.1)

Definition 4.1
.

The algebra
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is the limit of the inductive tower of finite algebras
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})
, supplied by the canonical embeddings
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})\rightarrowtail{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k+1})
.

Remark 4.2
.

We recall that the universal
{\rm CAR}
-algebra admits a unique faithful tracial state, which will be denoted by
{\mathcal{T}}
. It will play an essential role in section
5.4
(see Proposition
5.39
).

\Diamond

The local algebra of observables constructed this way formalizes the local measurements available to an experimenter dealing with a system of fermions over
{\mathcal{L}}
. As in subsection
3.1
, this experimenter analyses the dynamics of the available local observables and the main task is to figure out the group of automorphisms
\{\bm{\alpha}_{t}\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
that implements the time evolution, which is usually done by experimenting with many such local observables. As in subsection
3.1
, the experimenter will find that these automorphisms are outer, hence the Hamiltonians generating the dynamics do not belong to the local algebra. This central aspect will be addressed in section
5
.

4.2.
Symmetric presentation

Given the anti-commutation relations (
4.1
), a word constructed from the generators has many equivalent presentations. The formalism developed in sections
5
and
6
relies on our specific choice to treat all equivalent word presentations on equal footing. This leads to a specific presentation of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, which we call the symmetric presentation. We describe it here.

For a finite subset
J\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of cardinality
|J|=N
, we consider the set
\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{N},J)
of bijections from
{\mathcal{I}}_{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
to
J
. Any such bijection supplies a particular enumeration of the elements of
J
, hence, an order. The group
\mathcal{S}_{N}
of ordinary permutations of
N
objects can and will be identified with
\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{N},{\mathcal{I}}_{N})
. It has natural left and right actions on
\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{N},J)
via

s\cdot\chi_{J}:=\chi_{J}\circ s^{-1},\hskip 8.19447pt\chi_{J}\cdot s:=\chi_{J}\circ s,\hskip 8.19447pts\in\mathcal{S}_{N},\hskip 8.19447pt\chi_{J}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{N},J),

respectively. Consider now a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}
. We introduce the following relations

(J,\chi_{J})\mapsto a_{J}(\chi_{J}):=a_{\chi_{J}(|J|)}\cdots a_{\chi_{J}(|1|)}\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}),

(4.2)

for all
J\in{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{L}})
and
\chi_{J}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J|},J)
. Obviously,

a_{J}\big(s\cdot\chi_{J}\big)=a_{J}\big(\chi_{J}\cdot s\big)=(-1)^{s}a_{J}\big(\chi_{J}),\hskip 8.19447pt\forall\ s\in\mathcal{S}_{|J|},

and

a_{J}(\chi_{J})^{\ast}=a^{\ast}_{\chi_{J}(1)}\cdots a^{\ast}_{\chi_{J}(|J|)}.

(4.3)

It is also useful to introduce the special elements

n_{J}=a^{\ast}_{J}(\chi_{J})a_{J}(\chi_{J})=\tfrac{1}{|J|!}\sum_{\chi_{J}}a^{\ast}_{J}(\chi_{J})a_{J}(\chi_{J}).

Throughout, we will use the convention
a_{\emptyset}=a^{\ast}_{\emptyset}=1
, the unit of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Remark 4.3
.

Since
{\mathcal{L}}
is a discrete set, the compact subsets of
{\mathcal{L}}
are exactly the finite subsets. Throughout, we will only involve compact subsets, hence the cardinality of the subsets is finite, everywhere in our discussion.

\Diamond

One could argue that a choice for the enumeration of the subsets of
{\mathcal{L}}
can be made once and for all. This is certainly possible if the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
is fixed. However, we want and, in fact, are forced to allow for deformations of the pattern,
e.g.
at least those induced by the simple translations of
{\mathcal{L}}
, already encountered in section
3
. Under such deformations, two points of the pattern can be exchanged, as it was the case in Example
3.13
, and the enumeration of the points of a subset containing those two points becomes ambiguous. This example shows that the only acceptable option is to use all the available enumerations on equal footing and this is indeed what we will do when presenting the elements of the
{\rm CAR}
algebra. However, in doing so, we need to impose restrictions on the coefficients. It is at this point where the material from section
2.3
makes its entrance.

Definition 4.4
.

For a pair
(J,J^{\prime})
of compact subsets of
{\mathcal{L}}
, a bi-equivariant coefficient is a map
c_{J,J^{\prime}}:\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J|},J)\times\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J^{\prime}|},J^{\prime})\to\mathbb{C}
such that

c_{J,J^{\prime}}(s_{1}\cdot\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\cdot s_{2})=(-1)^{s_{1}}\ c_{J,J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}})\ (-1)^{s_{2}}.

Remark 4.5
.

Given the particular representations of the permutation groups entering in the above definition, one could argue that just an orientation of the subsets
J
and
J^{\prime}
will suffice. This is indeed the case for the presentation of the CAR algebra. However, the full orders on the sub-sets supply special points, namely
\chi_{J}(1)
, which will be used in an essential way in our construction of the groupoid associated with the dynamics of the fermions (see section
6.3
).

\Diamond

Proposition 4.6
.

Every element of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
can be uniquely presented as a norm convergent sum

A=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{L}})}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|J|!|J^{\prime}|!}}\sum_{\chi_{J},\chi_{J}^{\prime}}c_{J,J^{\prime}}\big(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big)a_{J}(\chi_{J})^{\ast}a_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}}),

(4.4)

where it is understood that the coefficients are bi-equivariant and that the second sum runs over the whole set
\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J|},J)\times\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J^{\prime}|},J^{\prime})
.

We will refer to Eq. (
4.4
) as the symmetric presentation of
A
. The consideration of the combinatorial factors in front of the second sum will be justified in Propositions
4.19
and
4.29
.

Remark 4.7
.

The first sum in (
4.4
) can include
J=\emptyset
,
J^{\prime}=\emptyset
or
J=J^{\prime}=\emptyset
. In these cases, we use our convention
a_{\emptyset}=1
.

\Diamond

Remark 4.8
.

It is important to acknowledge that the coefficients
c_{J,J^{\prime}}
in Eq.
4.4
must display a certain decay as the sets
J
or
J^{\prime}
are pushed to infinity. For example, a formal series with coefficients
c_{J,J}
that do not decay to zero as the diameter of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
sub-set
\{0\}\cup J\cup J^{\prime}
goes to infinity is not part of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
.

\Diamond

The gauge invariant (GI) elements are, by definition, elements that can be written as in Eq. (
4.4
), but with the first sum restricted to pairs of subsets with
|J|=|J^{\prime}|
. Such elements are invariant against the circle action
a_{x}\mapsto\lambda a_{x}
,
|\lambda|=1
, hence the name “gauge invariant”. They form a sub-algebra denoted here by
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. The symmetric presentation of such elements can be organized as

A=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{\chi_{J},\chi_{J}^{\prime}}c_{J,J^{\prime}}\big(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big)a_{J}(\chi_{J})^{\ast}a_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}}),

(4.5)

where we introduced the new notation
{\mathcal{K}}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})
for the finite un-ordered sub-sets of
{\mathcal{L}}
of cardinal
n
.

Remark 4.9
.

A groupoid presentation of the CAR algebra was supplied in Example 1.10 of
[
44
]
. Although not used in our work, we provide it here for completeness. The construction requires an ordering of the lattice points and it starts from the configuration space
S=\prod_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}S_{x}
, where
S_{x}=\{0,1\}
and
S
is equipped with the product topology. A configuration

singles out the sites
x
with
s_{x}=1
, which can be thought of as the sites of
{\mathcal{L}}
populated by fermions. Two configurations are declared equivalent iff they differ at at most a finite number of places. Algebraically, the CAR groupoid is the groupoid
{\mathcal{C}}
corresponding to this equivalence relation (see Example
2.4
). The étale topology on
{\mathcal{C}}
is strictly finer than the relative topology inherited from

. To describe this topology, we follow Example 8.3.5 in
[

]
. First, one identifies
{\mathcal{L}}
with
{\mathbb{N}}
by using the predefined order. Then, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any two finite words
v
and
w
from
\{0,1\}^{n}
, one defines the subset

Z_{n}(v,w)=\{(vs,ws)|s\in S\}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}},

where
vs
means concatenation of the words
v
and
s
. The collection

\{Z_{n}(v,w),\,n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,v,w\in\{0,1\}^{n}\},

is the basis of a topology that makes

into an étale groupoid. The associated groupoid algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{C}})
is isomorphic to the CAR algebra. In
[
44
]
, the isomorphism established by showing that the two algebras display common Elliot invariants. A direct proof of the isomorphism can be found in
[
49
, Example 9.2.7 ]
.

\Diamond

4.3.

The commutation relations (
4.1
) lead to somewhat complex relations between the monomials. This section lists several key algebraic relations with proofs omitted. They can be stated more effectively once some straightforward but essential notation is introduced.

Definition 4.10
.

For
J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{L}})
disjoint subsets and
\chi_{J}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J|},J)
,

In words,
\chi_{J}\vee\chi_{J^{\prime}}

enumerates first the elements of
J
and then continue with the enumeration of the elements of
J^{\prime}
, in the order set by
\chi_{J}
and
\chi_{J^{\prime}}

With this notation, for example, we have:

a_{J}(\chi_{J})^{\ast}\,a_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}})^{\ast}=a_{J\cup J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J}\vee\chi_{J^{\prime}})^{\ast},

(4.6)

whenever
J\cap J^{\prime}=\emptyset
. The following relations are useful for reducing products of generic

Proposition 4.11
.

For

, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle a_{J}(\chi_{J})a^{\ast}_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}})=(-1)^{\sigma}\sum_{K\subseteq J\cap J^{\prime}}(-1)^{|K|}a^{\ast}_{J^{\prime}\setminus J}(\chi_{J^{\prime}\setminus J})\,n_{K}\,a_{J\setminus J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J\setminus J^{\prime}}),

Remark 4.18

Since we want to avoid making any particular choice between the possible orderings of the sub-sets, we will work with the frame of

Proposition 4.19

We will refer to Eq. (

Proposition 4.20

Let

5.
Interacting Fermions: Dynamics

This section formalizes the dynamics of the gauge invariant local physical observables. Our main goal here is to identify a large enough set of derivations that can serve as the core of an algebra associated with the dynamics of the fermions. The starting point is the class of derivations with finite interaction range, which are defined over and return values in a fixed dense subalgebra
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})\subset{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. These derivations can be composed with each other, hence they generate a subalgebra of
{\rm End}({\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}}))
. Subsequently, we explore the dependence of the derivations on the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
and the constraints imposed by the (assumed) Galilean invariance. For guidance, we introduce a large class of Hamiltonians satisfying these constraints, inspired from the physics literature and generated from many-body potentials. In the process, we demonstrate that the lattice deformations and the fermion permutations cannot be separated. This leads us to the construction of the many-body covers of the space of Delone sets, which supply the natural and rightful domain for the Hamiltonian coefficients. It also enables us to give a precise formulation of a core
\ast
-algebra of derivations that can be actually mapped from real experiments. We show that this algebra admits representations by uniformly bounded operators on the Fock sectors with finite number of particles.

5.1.
Hamiltonians with finite interaction range

We consider a strongly continuous dynamics of the local physical observables

\bm{\alpha}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\rm Aut}\big({\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})\big)

and denote by
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
the generator of this dynamics. In the majority of the studied physical systems, the domain of
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
is

{\rm Dom}(\delta_{\bm{\alpha}})={\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}}):=\mathop{\cup}_{k}{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k}),

where
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\}
is the net of finite lattices used in section
4.1
to define
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. In the above conditions,
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}
is an inner-limit derivation
[
17
, p. 26]
and, throughout, we will restrict to these cases. We recall that we are seeking a core for the algebra of generators, which subsequently can be closed in many different ways (see section
6.1
). It is natural to start from the inner-limit derivations because the laboratory reality is that a finite team of experimenters can only map this type of generators in a finite amount of time.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
is not just a linear space but a dense subalgebra of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, closed under the
\ast
-operation. Obviously,
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
is not the whole
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
and, in general,
\delta_{\bm{\alpha}}

3
, where the generators were bounded.

\Diamond

Derivations are linear maps over
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
and a derivation that leaves
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
invariant is an element of the algebra
{\rm End}\big({\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of linear maps over
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
. A large class of such derivations is supplied by finite interaction range Hamiltonians:

Definition 5.2
.

A finite interaction range Hamiltonian is a formal sum

H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{L}})}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{|J|!|J^{\prime}|!}}\sum_{\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}}h_{J,J^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{L}}(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime\prime}})\,a^{\ast}_{J}(\chi_{J})a_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}}),

where the coefficients
h^{\mathcal{L}}_{J,J^{\prime}}
are bi-equivariant, uniformly bounded, obey the constraints

h^{\mathcal{L}}_{J,J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}})=\overline{h^{\mathcal{L}}_{J^{\prime},J}(\chi_{J^{\prime}},\chi_{J})},

(5.2)

and they vanish whenever the diameter of
J\cup J^{\prime}
exceeds a fixed value
{\rm R}_{\rm i}
, called here the interaction range. Also, the sum in Eq. (
5.5
) runs only over pairs of sub-sets with
|J\cup J^{\prime}|
even.

Remark 5.3
.

The expression (
5.1
) is quite involved because we need to keep track of the essential dependencies. Of course, if the lattice is fixed and a global orientation is chosen, then the notation can be simplified, but this is not at all the case here. Still, the notation will be simplified after we introduce the many-body covers of the space of Delone sets (see Remark
5.27
).

\Diamond

Remark 5.4

For reader’s convenience, we recall that the diameter of a subset

In Definition

with its Euclidean distance.

Note that in Definition

5.2.
Galilean invariant theories

As in section
3.2
, let us imagine an experimenter sitting at the origin of the physical space

h_{J,J^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{L}}
. The same experimenter then deforms the lattice, without changing the nature of the fermions or resonators, and maps again the Hamiltonian. After repeating this program for many Delone sets, the experimenter establishes a map

{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ni{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto H_{\mathcal{L}},

(5.6)

which is entirely determined by the nature of the fermions. The notation
H_{\mathcal{L}}
can be interpreted as the evaluation of a global Hamiltonian
H
at
{\mathcal{L}}
and this is the point of view we will adopt from now on. Throughout, our working assumption is that
H_{\mathcal{L}}
’s in Eq.
5.6
have finite interaction ranges.

The formalism faces two immediate challenges. The first one is how to properly define a continuity property for the maps in Eq.
5.6
. The second one is understanding the constraints imposed by the assumed Galilean invariance of the theory. The latter is investigated below, while the first challenge is addressed in the following subsections. For now, we will adopt the view that the map (
5.6
) has been generated by the experiment. Now, during the lengthy experimental process we just described, two lattices
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
may happen to enter the relation
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{L}}-x
for some
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. While the experimenter is pinned at the origin of the physical space at all times, just for this situation, we can imagine the experimenter and the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
being rigidly shifted until the experimenter sits at position
x
. Then the experimenter is dealing with the same lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
but from a difference location and, in the absence of background fields, Galilean invariance of the physical processes involved in the resonator couplings assures us that, up to a proper relabeling, the new experiments return the same coefficients. Therefore, the Hamiltonian coefficients must be subject to the following relations

h_{J-x,J^{\prime}-x}^{{\mathcal{L}}-x}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}\circ\chi_{J},\mathfrak{t}_{x}\circ\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big)=h_{J,J^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{L}}\big(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big),

(5.7)

for all
{\mathcal{L}}\in{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{L}})
. These relations can be expressed more concisely, as already explained in section
1
(see Eq. (
1.2
)). Let us specify that Remark
3.1
applies here as well.

Remark 5.10
.

Let us acknowledge that, among other things, Eq. (
5.7
) implies

h_{J,J^{\prime}}^{\mathcal{L}}\big(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big)=h_{J-x_{1},J^{\prime}-x_{1}}^{{\mathcal{L}}-x_{1}}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x_{1}}\circ\chi_{J},\mathfrak{t}_{x_{1}}\circ\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big),\hskip 8.19447ptx_{1}=\chi_{J}(1).

(5.8)

As one can see, the experimenter can archive the entire map
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto H_{\mathcal{L}}
by measuring just the coefficients
h_{J,J^{\prime}}^{{\mathcal{L}}}
with
J
and
J^{\prime}
inside a ball of radius
{\rm R}_{\rm i}
and centered at the origin of the physical space. In other words, by local experiments! Of course, the experimenter will need to sample many patterns
{\mathcal{L}}
and it is at this point where the continuity of the coefficients w.r.t.
{\mathcal{L}}
is essential. This is because it enables the experimentalist to extrapolate (aka connect the dots) the results generated by a finite number of observations.

\Diamond

So far, the coefficients of the Hamiltonians exist only in the tables generated by the experimenter. In the following, we describe an analytic method to generate Galilean invariant Hamiltonians that is often found in the physics literature. This class of Hamiltonians will serve as a stepping stone for our quest of the most general expression of global Hamiltonians displaying finite interaction range, Galilean invariance and continuity w.r.t. the underlying lattice.

It is instructive to start with a simple example, which actually represents the most common many-body Hamiltonian found in the physics literature:

Example 5.11
.

The Hamiltonian of a system of fermions over a discrete lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
and interacting pair-wise via a given potential
v:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
takes the form

H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}}w_{x,x^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}})\,a_{x}^{\ast}a_{x^{\prime}}+\sum_{x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}}v(x^{\prime}-x)\,a_{x^{\prime}}^{\ast}a_{x}^{\ast}a_{x^{\prime}}a_{x},

(5.9)

where
w
’s are as in section
3
. The Hamiltonian has a finite interaction range if the potential
v
has compact support.

\Diamond

Remark 5.12
.

Example
5.11
is exceptional in several ways. Firstly, note that it involves terms where
J
and
J^{\prime}
either contain just one point or they coincide. Because of this particularity, the ordering of the local observables is in fact irrelevant. Secondly, note that, when
x=x^{\prime}
in the second sum, the summands cancel. As such, the summation can be restricted to pairs with
x\neq x^{\prime}
. Since these pairs belong to a Delone set, they never come closer than a distance
r
. As such, the potential
v
can be multiplied by the function
(1-\phi)
, with
\phi
a continuous function with support inside
B(0,r)
, without producing any modifications to the Hamiltonian. This last remark will become relevant for the discussion in Remark
5.17
.

\Diamond

To define more general Hamiltonians, one needs to supply a whole family of bi-equivariant coefficients that are indexed by subsets of
{\mathcal{L}}
and, preferably, depend continuously on
{\mathcal{L}}
. This non-trivial task can be accomplished with the help of many-body potentials. For this, we consider the space
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}\times({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{n}
, together with the natural action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
induced by the diagonal translations,

\mathfrak{t}_{x}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n})=(x_{1}-x,\ldots,x_{n}-x;x^{\prime}_{1}-x,\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n}-x),

and of the group
\mathcal{S}_{n}\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
, which permutes the first and last
n
-variables, separately. Let
\big[({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\big]
be the space of orbits for the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
action, equipped with the quotient topology. Since the permutations and the diagonal translations commute, the action of
\mathcal{S}_{n}\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
descends to an action on
\big[({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\big]
.

Definition 5.13
.

We call
\hat{w}_{n}:\big[({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\big]\to{\mathbb{C}}
a seed for a bi-equivariant
n
-body potential if
\hat{w}_{n}
is continuous, has compact support and is odd relative to the action of
\mathcal{S}_{n}\times\mathcal{S}_{n}
. The
n
-body potential associated to such a seed is the function

w_{n}=\hat{w}_{n}\circ{\rm q}_{n}:({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\to{\mathbb{C}},

where
{\rm q}_{n}:({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\to\big[({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{2n}\big]
is the quotient map. In addition, we assume that

w_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n};x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n})=\overline{w_{n}(x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})}.

Definition 5.14
.

Assume that a system
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}}
of many-body potentials has been supplied. We then declare that the evaluation at
{\mathcal{L}}
of the global Hamiltonian corresponding to these potentials is

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}}\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})}\ \sum_{\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle w_{J,J^{\prime}}^{{\mathcal{L}}}(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}})a^{\ast}_{J}(\chi_{J})a_{J^{\prime}}(\chi_{J^{\prime}}),

(5.10)

where the bi-equivariant coefficients are given by

w_{J,J^{\prime}}^{{\mathcal{L}}}\big(\chi_{J},\chi_{J^{\prime}}\big):=w_{n}\big(\chi_{J}(1),\ldots,\chi_{J}(n);\chi_{J^{\prime}}(1),\ldots\chi_{J^{\prime}}(n)\big),

(5.11)

for
J,J^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Proposition 5.15
.

The Hamiltonians (
5.10
) are manifestly Galilean invariant.

Proof.

Indeed, the coefficients obey relation (
5.7
) because the potentials
w_{n}
are constant along the orbit induced by the diagonal translations. ∎

Example 5.16
.

For a
2
-body potential, Eq. (
5.10
) takes the form

\displaystyle H_{\mathcal{L}}=2\sum_{x_{1},x_{2};x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}}\;w_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})\;a_{x_{1}}^{\ast}a_{x_{2}}^{\ast}\,a_{x^{\prime}_{1}}a_{x^{\prime}_{2}}.

(5.12)

Consider now the following specific many-body potential

w_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2})=v_{1}(x_{2}-x_{1})v_{2}(x^{\prime}_{2}-x^{\prime}_{1})\varphi\big({\rm d}_{H}(\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\{x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\})\big),

(5.13)

where the two functions
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are odd,
v_{i}(-x)=-v_{i}(x)
, and
2v_{1}v_{2}=v
, the potential from Example
5.11
. Also,
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a function with
\varphi(0)=1
and with support in the interval
[0,r]
. Note that, if
{\mathcal{L}}\in{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, then the last factor in Eq.
5.13
takes non-zero values if only if
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}=\{x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2}\}
, as un-ordered sets. Then Eq. (
5.12
) reduces to the model Hamiltonian (
5.11
). Clearly, the potential (
5.13
) is constant along the orbit induced by the diagonal translations of the points, hence it descends to a seed on
\big[({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{4}\big]
, which can be checked to be continuous.

\Diamond

Remark 5.17
.

As is always with the case for odd functions,
v_{i}(0)=0
and, as such,
v(0)=0
. However, given the discussion in Remark
5.12
, this does not restrict the generality of the above construction.

\Diamond

Example 5.18
.

Explicit models of many-body potentials corresponding to various fractional Hall sequences can be found in
[
41
]
.

\Diamond

Remark 5.19
.

For finite interaction range Hamiltonians evaluated on a fixed class
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of Delone sets, there is an upper limit on the cardinals of the sub-sets
J
,
J^{\prime}
entering in Eq. (
5.10
). As such, a finite interaction range Hamiltonian as in Eq. (
5.10
) is always generated from a finite number of many-body potential seeds. Also, if the supports of these many-body potentials are contained inside the ball of radius
{\rm R}_{\rm i}
, then the range of the Hamiltonian (
5.10
) is
{\rm R}_{\rm i}
.

\Diamond

We recall that our ultimate goal is to define the most general class of finite interaction range Hamiltonians that are Galilean invariant and depend continuously on the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
. Since we do not yet have a topology on the algebra of derivations, at this point, the only available option is to formulate the continuity directly on the bi-equivariant coefficients. Clearly, the Hamiltonians constructed from many-body potentials provide guidance in this respect. However, even for these explicitly constructed Hamiltonians, there is still a difficulty in assessing the continuity of the coefficients (
5.11
), because they are also functions of the orderings. As elements of
\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{|J|},J)
,
J\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, these orderings change with the deformation of the pattern
{\mathcal{L}}
and they might not return to their initial value when
{\mathcal{L}}
is deformed back to its initial configuration. As such, there is a non-trivial topological space where the data
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
lives and this is investigated next.

5.3.
The many-body covers of the Delone space

We start by looking at the partial data
({\mathcal{L}},V)
,
|V|=n
, which generates the set

{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=\Big\{({\mathcal{L}},V),\ {\mathcal{L}}\in{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\ V\in{\mathcal{K}}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})\Big\}.

It is a subset of
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, hence it can be naturally equipped with the relative topology inherited from
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, with the latter endowed with the product topology. The following statements describe this topology more concretely.

Proposition 5.20
.

The family of subsets

\displaystyle U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)=\Big(U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}})\times\mathring{B}(V,\epsilon)\Big)\cap{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

(5.14)

form a neighborhood base for
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Here
\epsilon>0
,
M
is positive and large enough such that any
V^{\prime}\in\mathring{B}(V,\epsilon)
is contained in the ball
B(0,M)
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}})
is as defined in Eq. (
3.7
).

Proof.

According to the definition, the following family of sub-sets

\displaystyle U_{M}^{\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}},V)=\Big(U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}})\times\mathring{B}(V,\epsilon^{\prime})\Big)\cap{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

(5.15)

with
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
and
M>0
, is a basis of neighborhoods for the relative topology. Clearly

U_{M}^{\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}},V)\subseteq U_{M^{\prime}}^{\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}},V),\hskip 8.19447pt\forall\ M^{\prime}\leq M,

and, as such, we can trim the family to the subsets
U_{M}^{\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}},V)
with
M
larger than any threshold, without affecting the topology. In particular, we can restrict to
M
’s satisfying the stated constraints. Furthermore,

U_{M}^{\underline{\epsilon},\underline{\epsilon}}({\mathcal{L}},V)\subseteq U_{M}^{\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}({\mathcal{L}},V),\hskip 8.19447pt\underline{\epsilon}=\min(\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}),

hence the family can be further trimmed to members with
\epsilon=\epsilon^{\prime}
.∎

Remark 5.21
.

Note that, for any
({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},V^{\prime})\in U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)
, we have
V^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}[M]
.

\Diamond

Proposition 5.22
.

Let
({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},V^{\prime})
be any point from the open neighborhood
U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)
of
({\mathcal{L}},V)
. Then, if
\epsilon<r/2
, there exists a canonical bijective map
g:V\to V^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
{\rm d}_{\rm H}(V^{\prime},V)<\epsilon
, the open ball
\mathring{B}(v,\epsilon)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
necessarily contains at least one point of
V^{\prime}
, for each
v\in V
. Since both
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
are from
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\epsilon<r/2
, each
\mathring{B}(v,\epsilon)
can contain at most one point of
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. The conclusion is that each
\mathring{B}(v,\epsilon)
contains exactly one point
v^{\prime}
of
V^{\prime}
and, in fact, of the whole
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. The canonical map mentioned in the statement is defined to be the map corresponding to the graph

g=\{(v,v^{\prime})\in V\times V^{\prime},\ v^{\prime}\in\mathring{B}(v,\epsilon)\}.

The map is clearly invertible.∎

Corollary 5.23
.

The map

{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\hskip 8.19447pt({\mathcal{L}},V)\mapsto{\mathcal{L}},

is a local homeomorphism making
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
into an infinite cover of
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

We now define the order cover of the space
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which is the natural topological space for the data
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
. The following statements give the definition and supply a characterization of this cover.

Proposition 5.24
.

Consider the set

\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=\Big\{\big({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V}\big):\ ({\mathcal{L}},V)\in{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\ \chi_{V}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathcal{I}}_{n},V)\Big\}.

Then the subsets

U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})=\Big\{({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},V^{\prime},g\circ\chi_{V}):\ ({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},V^{\prime})\in U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)\Big\},

(5.16)

with
M>>0
,
0<\epsilon<r/2
and
g
the canonical bijection from Proposition
5.22
, form a neighborhood base for a topology on
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The map

\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\hskip 8.19447pt(\mathcal{L},V,\chi_{V})\mapsto(\mathcal{L},V),

is a local homeomorphism making
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
into an
n!
-cover of
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Proof.

Consider
({\mathcal{L}}_{1},V_{1})
and
({\mathcal{L}}_{2},V_{2})
from
{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and two overlapping neighborhoods
U_{M_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}}({\mathcal{L}}_{i},V_{i},\chi_{V_{i}})
,
i=1,2
. Let
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
be an element from their intersection and let
g_{j}:V_{j}\to V
,
j=1,2
, be the corresponding canonical bijections defined as in Proposition
5.22
. Then necessarily

g_{1}\circ\chi_{V_{1}}=g_{2}\circ\chi_{V_{2}}=\chi_{V},

(5.17)

which is a consequence of the very definition (
5.16
) of the open neighborhoods.
Clearly,
({\mathcal{L}},V)\in U_{M_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}({\mathcal{L}}_{1},V_{1})\cap U_{M_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}({\mathcal{L}}_{2},V_{2})
and, since the subsets defined in Eq. (
5.14
) form a neighborhood base, there exists a whole neighborhood
U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)
contained in this intersection.

We claim that

U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})\subset U_{M_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}({\mathcal{L}}_{1},V_{1},\chi_{V_{1}})\cap U_{M_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}({\mathcal{L}}_{2},V_{2},\chi_{V_{2}}),

(5.18)

where the neighborhood on the left is as in Eq. (
5.16
), with
\chi_{V}
as in Eq.
5.17
. Indeed, take
(\tilde{\mathcal{L}},\tilde{V})
from
U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)
and let
\tilde{g}_{j}:V_{j}\to\tilde{V}
,
j=1,2
and
\tilde{g}:V\to\tilde{V}
be the corresponding canonical bijection defined as in Proposition
5.22
. Then
\tilde{g}_{j}=\tilde{g}\circ g_{j}
,
j=1,2
, and, as a consequence,

\tilde{g}_{j}\circ\chi_{V_{j}}=\tilde{g}_{j}\circ\chi_{V_{j}}=\tilde{g}\circ g_{j}\circ\chi_{V_{j}}=\tilde{g}\circ\chi_{V},\hskip 8.19447ptj=1,2.

In other words,

(\tilde{\mathcal{L}},\tilde{V},\tilde{g}\circ\chi_{V})\in U_{M_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}}({\mathcal{L}}_{1},V_{1},\chi_{V_{1}})\cap U_{M_{2}}^{\epsilon_{2}}({\mathcal{L}}_{2},V_{2},\chi_{V_{2}}),

and the statement from Eq. (
5.18
) follows. Lastly, if
{\rm y}_{n}
is the projection
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})\mapsto({\mathcal{L}},V)
, then
{\rm y}_{n}^{-1}\big(U_{M}^{\epsilon}({\mathcal{L}},V)\big)
consist of
n!
disjoint open subsets of
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. ∎

Proposition 5.25
.

The order cover is path connected.

Proof.

Take a continuous path

[0,1]\ni t\xmapsto{\sigma}({\mathcal{L}}_{t},V_{t})\in{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

where just two points move and switch positions. Assume that these two points are inside
V_{t}
. Let
s:V\rightarrow V
be the permutation that encodes the exchange of the two points. Then,
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
and
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V}\circ s)
are connected by
{\rm y}_{n}^{-1}(\sigma[0,1])
. By further exchanging points of
V
, we can connect
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
with any other
({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi^{\prime}_{V})
from the order cover.∎

Remark 5.26
.

By combining the order cover and the map introduced in Corollary
5.23
, we obtain the covering map

\mathfrak{a}_{n}:\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\hskip 8.19447pt\mathfrak{a}_{n}({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V}):={\mathcal{L}},

which we call the
n
-body covering map of the space of Delone sets.

\Diamond

Remark 5.27
.

We now have the means to simplify the notation. The elements of
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
will be denoted by one letter, such as
\xi
,
\zeta
, etc.. If
\xi=({\mathcal{L}},V,\chi_{V})
and if any of those three pieces of information needs to be specified, we will do so using
{\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}
,
V_{\xi}
and
\chi_{\xi}
, as exemplified below.

\Diamond

The topological space
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
inherits two important group actions:

Proposition 5.28
.

The group action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by translations, defined in Eq. (
3.6
), extends to a group action on the many-body covers by

\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{x}(\xi):=\Big({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}-x,V_{\xi}-x,\mathfrak{t}_{x}\circ\chi_{\xi}\Big).

Proof.

It is enough to establish the validity of the statement for
x
in an open ball of the origin. For
|x|<r/2
, the canonical map
g:V_{\xi}\to\mathfrak{t}_{x}(V_{\xi})
coincides with the shift
V_{\xi}\mapsto V_{\xi}-x
and, as such,
\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{x}
is a homeomorphism in such cases. ∎.

Proposition 5.29
.

If
s\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is a permutation, then

s\mapsto\Lambda_{s},\hskip 8.19447pt\Lambda_{s}(\xi)=({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi},V_{\xi},\chi_{\xi}\circ s^{-1})

defines an isomorphism of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
onto the group of deck transformations of the order cover

\widehat{\rm Del}^{(n)}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to{\rm Del}^{(n)}_{(r,R)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Proof.

From its very definition,
\Lambda_{s}
acts fiber-wise and
\Lambda
is clearly a group action. Furthermore,

\Lambda_{s}\big(U_{M}^{\epsilon}(\xi)\big)=U_{M}^{\epsilon}\big(\Lambda_{s}(\xi)\big),

which means
\Lambda_{s}
are continuous maps with continuous inverses, hence homeomorphisms. The map
s\mapsto\Lambda_{s}
is injective and since the order of the group of deck transformations is at most
n!
, it is surjective as well.∎

5.4.
The algebra of finite-range Galilean invariant derivations

We are now ready to specify the most general Hamiltonians expected to come out of an actual laboratory investigation. The data entering the Hamiltonian coefficients generate the sets

\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n,m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})=\Big\{\big(\xi,\zeta\big)\in\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),\ {\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}={\mathcal{L}}_{\zeta}\Big\},

(5.19)

where
n
and
m
sample
{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}
. They can and will be naturally equipped with the topology inherited from
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

At this point, we introduce more efficient notation, which will be adopted throughout from now on:

\circ

We define the correspondence

\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ni\xi\mapsto\bm{a}(\xi)\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}),\hskip 8.19447pt\bm{a}(\xi)=a_{V_{\xi}}(\chi_{\xi}),

for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}
. It will be useful to append a point
\o
and declare that
\bm{a}(\o)=0
.

\circ

For
(\xi,\zeta)\in\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n,m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we write

\xi\vee\zeta=({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}={\mathcal{L}}_{\zeta},\ V_{\xi}\cup V_{\zeta},\ \chi_{\xi}\vee\chi_{\zeta})\in\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n+m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

if
V_{\xi}\cap V_{\zeta}=\emptyset
, and
\xi\vee\zeta=\o
otherwise.

\circ

For
(\xi,\zeta)\in\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n,m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we write
\zeta\leq\xi
if
V_{\zeta}\subseteq V_{\xi}
and
\chi_{\xi}=\chi_{\zeta}\vee\chi_{\Gamma}
, for some order
\chi_{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma=V_{\xi}\setminus V_{\zeta}
.

\circ

For
(\xi,\zeta)\in\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n,m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that
\zeta\leq\xi
, we denote by
\xi\setminus\zeta
the unique element of
\widehat{{\rm Del}}_{(r,R)}^{(n-m)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

Proof.

6.
Resolving the Algebra of Physical Hamiltonians

The ideals of
{\rm GICAR}
algebra are invariant by inner limit derivations, hence the latter descend on the quotient algebras generated from the ideals of the filtration introduced in section
4.5
. These quotients coincide with the algebras of compact operators over the Fock sectors, hence there are sub-algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\subset{\mathbb{B}}\big({\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
as well as a tower of representations of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
inside
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
. Furthermore, the algebra of physical Hamiltonians
\dot{\mathfrak{H}}({\mathcal{L}})=\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})/{\rm ker}\,\dot{\pi}_{\eta}
accepts a presentation as the inverse limit
\varprojlim\,\dot{\Gamma}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
, where
\dot{\Gamma}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}\mathfrak{H}_{N}
. The first part of the section formalizes these observations. The second part of the section shows that the algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
are contained in an essential extension of a specific
C^{\ast}
-subalgebra
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
of
{\mathbb{B}}\big({\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
, which excludes the compact operators. This enables us to complete
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
and
\dot{\mathfrak{H}}({\mathcal{L}})
to pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebras given by inverse limits of projective towers of
C^{\ast}
-algebras. In the third part, we introduce specialized étale groupoids and demonstrate that their left regular representations of its (bi-equivariant)
C^{\ast}
-algebra reproduce the algebras
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
. Among other things, these results give a complete characterization of the action of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
on the
N
-fermion sectors.

6.1.

{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(A)={\rm ad}_{Q_{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{n}}(A)
for some finite lattices
{\mathcal{L}}_{p}\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
. Since
{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}}_{p})\subseteq{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})
and the latter is an ideal of

,

{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(A)=\imath[A,Q_{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}^{n}]\in{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})\subset{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})

and the first statement follows.

ii) Let
[\cdot]_{N}
denote the quotient classes of the space in Eq. (
6.2
). Then the class
[A]_{N}
of

. Since
{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
is stable under addition,

automatically belongs to
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
, hence to
{\rm GI}_{N+1}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
. The latter is an ideal of the sub-algebra
{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
, hence the quotient space defined in Eq.
6.2
is well defined and the algebraic structure of
{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
descends on the quotient space (
6.2
). Since
{\rm GI}_{N+1}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})

\ast
-operation, the latter also descends to a
\ast
-operation on the quotient space (
6.2
).

iii) Given Eq. (
6.1
), we can be sure that
{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}
descends to a linear map on this algebra, which we denote by
\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}^{N}

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}^{N}\big([A]_{N}[B]_{N}\big)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}^{N}\big([AB]_{N}\big)=\big[{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(AB)\big]_{N}

\displaystyle=\big[{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(A)\,B+A\,{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(B)\big]_{N}.

Since both

and
{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(B)

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}^{N}\big([A]_{N}[B]_{N}\big)=\big[{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(A)\big]_{N}[B]_{N}+[A]_{N}\big[{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}(B)\big]_{N},

and the Leibniz rule follows.∎

We equip the ideals
{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})

{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
and the quotient algebras (
6.2

Proposition 6.2
.

The derivations
\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}}^{N}
are uniformly bounded.

Proof.

The quotient algebra (
6.2
) is spanned by the monomials
[\bm{a}(\xi)^{\ast}\bm{a}(\zeta)]_{N}
with
(\xi,\zeta)\in\mathfrak{b}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})

Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}
as in (

\displaystyle\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}}^{N}[\bm{a}(\xi)^{\ast}\bm{a}(\zeta)]_{N}

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{n!(N-n)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\xi^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{b}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})\\
V_{\zeta^{\prime}}\subseteq V_{\xi}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bar{\xi}\geq\zeta^{\prime}\\
V_{\bar{\xi}}=V_{\xi}\end{subarray}}q_{n}(\xi^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle(-1)^{\chi^{-1}_{\xi}\circ\chi_{\bar{\xi}}}\big[\bm{a}\big(\xi^{\prime}\vee(\bar{\xi}\setminus\zeta^{\prime})\big)^{\ast}\bm{a}(\zeta)\big]_{N}

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{n!(N-n)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\xi^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{b}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})\\

V_{\bar{\zeta}}=V_{\zeta}\end{subarray}}q_{n}(\xi^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})

\displaystyle(-1)^{\chi^{-1}_{\zeta}\circ\chi_{\bar{\zeta}}}\big[\bm{a}(\xi)^{\ast}\bm{a}\big((\bar{\zeta}\setminus\xi^{\prime})\vee\zeta^{\prime}\big)\big]_{N},

where we use our conventions on the
\vee

5.4
. There are finite numbers of non-zero terms in the two sums and these finite numbers have uniform upper bounds w.r.t.
\xi
and
\zeta
. Then the statement follows from the fact that the coefficients of

are uniformly bounded in
\xi^{\prime}
and
\zeta^{\prime}
.∎

Corollary 6.3

The derivations
\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}}^{N}

{\rm GI}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})/{\rm GI}_{N+1}({\mathcal{L}})\simeq{\mathbb{K}}\big({\mathcal{F}}^{(-)}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\big),\hskip 8.19447ptN\in{\mathbb{N}}.

[

generated by these bounded operators, more specifically:

Remark 6.6

Note that, although the Fock space appears above, the Fock representation has not been used so far. Indeed, the Fock space enter the picture simply because of its relation to the quotient algebra in Eq. (

\Diamond

Proposition 6.7
.

The algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
accepts canonical representations
\ddot{\pi}_{N}
inside the algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
, where
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
is the algebra opposite to
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
.

Proof.

Given that
\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}}^{N}
act as commutators with bounded operators, the action of a generic
{\mathcal{Q}}\in\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
, as descended on
{\mathbb{K}}\big({\mathcal{F}}^{(-)}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
, can always be expressed as

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{N}(K)=\sum_{i}B_{i}KC_{i},\hskip 8.19447ptB_{i},C_{i}\in{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\subset{\mathbb{B}}\big({\mathcal{F}}^{(-)}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\big),

for any
K\in{\mathbb{K}}\big({\mathcal{F}}^{(-)}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
. We claim that the rule

{\mathcal{Q}}\mapsto\ddot{\pi}_{N}({\mathcal{Q}}):=\sum_{i}B_{i}\otimes C_{i}\in{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}

(6.5)

supplies the representation mentioned in the statement. Indeed, the map is well defined because, if
\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{N}
accepts two equivalent expansions,

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{N}(K)=\sum_{i}B_{i}KC_{i}=\sum_{i}B^{\prime}_{i}KC^{\prime}_{i},\hskip 8.19447pt\forall\ K\in{\mathbb{K}}\big({\mathcal{F}}^{(-)}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})\big),

then one finds that both expansions are sent into the same element of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
by the map (
6.5
). Furthermore,

\big(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{{}^{\prime}N}\circ\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}^{N}\big)(K)=\sum_{i,j}B^{\prime}_{j}B_{i}KC_{i}C^{\prime}_{j},

hence the map (
6.5
) respects multiplication.∎

We recall that one of our goals is to complete
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
to a topological algebra and the representations
\ddot{\pi}_{N}
will supply the vehicle to reach this goal. The immediate task is to build an enveloping algebra for
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
out of the more manageable and computable algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
. One candidate could be the coproduct
\bigoplus_{N=0}^{\infty}{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}^{\rm op}
but, unfortunately, the topologized version of this coproduct will not fit the unbounded derivations we are dealing with here. The natural alternative is to consider the inverse limit of the tower of algebras

\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N+1}{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}^{\rm op}\twoheadrightarrow\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}^{\rm op},\hskip 8.19447ptN\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(6.6)

By the universal property of inverse limits, we can be assured that there exists an algebra morphism
\theta:\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\to\varprojlim\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}^{\rm op}
. The double sided ideal of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
supplied by the kernel of this morphism contains the linear maps on
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
that cannot be detected by examining the dynamics of finite but otherwise arbitrary number of fermions. Since this is the setting of any real laboratory experimentation, we can rightfully deem those linear maps as un-physical. The conclusion is that, modulo these un-physical elements, the algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
accepts an embedding into
\varprojlim\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}\otimes{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}^{\rm op}
. This embedding is the first step towards closing
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
to a pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra.

Our focus now shifts towards the algebras
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
, which now become the central objects of our program. Indeed, the computation of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
’s and the identification of suitable
C^{\ast}
-closures will provide a complete characterization of the algebra of derivations. In the same time, the
C^{\ast}
-closure of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
can be identified with the algebra of physical Hamiltonians generating the dynamics of
N
fermions. In fact, we are going to consider the following:

Definition 6.8
.

We declare the projective limit of algebras

\dot{\mathfrak{H}}({\mathcal{L}}):=\varprojlim\,\bigoplus_{n=0}^{N}{\mathfrak{H}}_{n}

(6.7)

to be the core algebra of physical GI-Hamiltonians for a system of self-interacting fermions populating the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Remark 6.9
.

Let us specify that, up to this point, our proposed program can be repeated for any AF-algebra that accepts a filtration by ideals. The computation and characterization of
\dot{\mathfrak{H}}({\mathcal{L}})
, however, will depend crucially on the particularities of the algebra and its filtration. Nevertheless, the case of the
{\rm GICAR}
algebra analyzed here can server as a model.

\widehat{\rm ad}_{Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}}^{N}=\imath[\cdot,\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n})]

The algebras

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N}
and
Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
be two derivations as in Eq. (

\pi_{\eta}^{N}\big(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}\circ Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N}\big)=\pi_{\eta}^{N}\big(\tilde{Q}_{\mathcal{L}}^{N}\big)

(6.10)

for some
\tilde{Q}_{\mathcal{L}}^{N}
as in Eq. (

Proof.

We will make use of Proposition
5.48
. If

can be taken as zero. If

, then, up to a normalization constant,

\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}\circ Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N})=\sum_{(\xi,\zeta)\in\mathfrak{b}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathfrak{a}_{N-n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})\\

and we can take

to be the derivation corresponding to the coefficient

\tilde{q}_{N}(\xi,\zeta)=\sum_{\zeta^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\mathfrak{a}_{N-n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})\\
\gamma\leq\xi\end{subarray}}\,q_{n}(\xi\setminus\gamma,\zeta^{\prime})q_{N}(\zeta^{\prime}\vee\gamma,\zeta).

Indeed,
\tilde{q}_{N}
is continuous, bi-equivariant and vanishes if the diameter of

is sufficiently large. ∎

Similar statements apply to the product

Proposition 6.20
.

Let
w:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to[0,1]

[0,1]
and to 0 over the interval
[2,\infty)

\epsilon>0

w_{\epsilon}(t)=w(\epsilon t)

1_{N}^{\epsilon}:=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}w_{\epsilon}(d_{\gamma})\,|\gamma\rangle\langle\gamma|,\hskip 8.19447pt\|1_{N}^{\epsilon}\|=1,

where

denotes the diameter of
V_{\gamma}
.

is a quasi-central approximate unit for
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Proof.

Example
5.41
assures us that the operator (
6.11
) belongs to
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
. By definition, any element from
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})

1_{N}\,\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N})-\ddot{\pi}_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N})=\sum_{(\xi,\zeta)\in\mathfrak{b}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\big(w_{\epsilon}(d_{\xi})-1\big)q_{N}(\xi,\zeta)|\xi\rangle\langle\zeta|.

The right hand side is a bounded operator that vanishes identically once

Corollary 6.21

{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}

\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})

Proof.

The net

converges to the identity of

\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})

is the norm closure of
\dot{\mathfrak{G}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
and the latter is an ideal of
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
. Then the smallest

-algebra containing
{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
must contain
{\mathfrak{G}}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})
as an ideal and be contained in the strong closure of
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})

{\mathfrak{H}}_{N}
is contained in an essential extension of
\mathfrak{G}_{N}({\mathcal{L}})

can be characterized as follows
[
1
]
. If

{\mathcal{A}}

can play the role of a physical Hamiltonian.

6.4.
The 2-action of the symmetric group and the bi-equivariant groupoid algebra

We saw in Proposition

on
\mathcal{G}_{N}

Proposition 6.42
.

Let
s\in\mathcal{S}_{N}
be a permutation. The formula

\tau_{s}(\xi):=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s^{-1}(1)}\big(\Lambda_{s}(\xi),\xi\big)

(6.21)

\tau:\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}_{N})
and thus a 2-action of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
on
\mathcal{G}_{N}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\tau_{s}
defines a section of the source map
\mathfrak{s}(\xi,\zeta)=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\zeta,\zeta)

\mathfrak{r}\circ\tau_{s}(\xi)=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s^{-1}(1)}\circ\Lambda_{s}(\xi)

\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}

\tau_{s_{1}}\cdot\tau_{s_{2}}
inside the group
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}_{N})

2.5
) and (
6.16

\displaystyle\tau_{s_{1}}\cdot\tau_{s_{2}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\tau_{s_{1}}(\mathfrak{r}\circ\tau_{s_{2}}(\xi))\tau_{s_{2}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\tau_{s_{1}}\left(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s_{2}^{-1}(1)}\circ\Lambda_{s_{2}}(\xi)\right)\tau_{s_{2}}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ(s_{1}s_{2})^{-1}(1)}\big(\Lambda_{s_{1}s_{2}}(\xi),\xi\big),

which proves that we have a group homomorphism
\mathcal{S}_{N}\to\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{G}_{N})

∎

The induced left and right actions (see Eq.
2.9
) of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
on
\mathcal{G}_{N}
will be denoted by
s_{1}\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot s_{2}

5.32
, which acts on a different space. We will need the explicit expression of the actions.

Proposition 6.43
.

The commuting left and right actions of
\mathcal{S}_{N}
on
\mathcal{G}_{N}

s_{1}\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot s_{2}=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s_{1}^{-1}(1)}\big(\Lambda_{s_{1}}(\xi),\Lambda_{s_{2}^{-1}}(\zeta)\big),

(6.22)

for
s_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{N}

Proof.

From the definition
2.9
), we have

\displaystyle s_{1}\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot s_{2}

\displaystyle=\tau_{s_{1}}\big(\xi\big)\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot\tau_{s_{2}^{-1}}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\zeta,\zeta)\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s_{1}^{-1}(1)}\big(\Lambda_{s_{1}}(\xi),\xi\big)\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}\circ s_{2}^{-1}(1)}\Lambda_{s_{2}^{-1}}\zeta,\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}\circ s_{2}^{-1}(1)}\zeta\big)^{-1}.

After we apply Eq. (

\displaystyle s_{1}\cdot(\xi,\zeta)\cdot s_{2}=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}\circ s_{1}^{-1}(1)}(\Lambda_{s_{1}}(\xi),\zeta)\cdot\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}\big(\zeta,\Lambda_{s_{2}^{-1}}(\zeta)\big).

Then one last application of Eq. (

.

2.3

6.5.
Left regular representations and the dynamics of fermions

We now have all the pieces in place and can complete the characterization of the algebra of Hamiltonians stated in Theorem

Proposition 6.45
.

The algebras

Proof.

\displaystyle\big((\xi_{0},\xi)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\xi_{0},\zeta)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{N}}f\big((\xi_{0},\xi)^{-1}\cdot(\xi_{0},\zeta)\big)\psi\big((\xi_{0},\zeta)^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\xi_{0},\zeta)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{N}}f\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}(\xi,\zeta)\big)\psi\big((\xi_{0},\zeta)^{-1}\big)

Proposition 6.46
.

The seed of an equivariant coefficient

6.6.

as a blow-up groupoid

We provide here an alternative description of the groupoids

through a topological procedure known as blowing up the unit space. This allows us to describe the structure of the

-algebras

Definition 6.47

(
[
55
]
p. 45)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a locally compact Hausdorff groupoid with open range map. Suppose that

through

\Diamond

Proposition 6.49
.

Recall the unit space of
{\mathcal{G}}_{N}
from Eq. (

Proof.

Indeed, the arguments from Proposition

The associated blow-up groupoid can be presented as

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{N}=\big\{\big(\xi,({\mathcal{L}},x),\zeta\big),\ ({\mathcal{L}},x)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{1},\ \xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}),\ \zeta\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}-x),\ \chi_{\xi}(1)=\chi_{\zeta}(1)=0\big\}.

Proposition 6.50
.

The map

{\mathcal{G}}_{N}\ni(\xi,\zeta)\mapsto\big(\xi,\big({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi},\chi_{\zeta}(1)\big),\mathfrak{t}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\zeta)\big)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{N}.

Indeed, the composable elements of

come as pairs

and
\mathfrak{t}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\zeta,\eta)

7.
Analysis of special cases with disconnected order covers

The periodic lattices
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and their small perturbations are very special because the order covers over the transversals of such patterns are disconnected. Indeed, as we shall see below, the subsets of these lattices and their translates accept canonical orderings that are compatible with the translations and the topology of the covers. As a consequence, the bi-equivariant groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
reduces to the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of a blow-up of
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and we can put forward a very strong statement saying that
K
-theory of
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
is identical to that of
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{1})
.

7.1.
Canonical ordering for perturbed periodic patterns

We start by introducing a large class of patterns displaying disconnected order covers.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}B(n,\varepsilon)
,
\varepsilon<1/2
, be a pattern of disconnected closed balls centered at the points of the periodic lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. We call
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a perturbation of the periodic lattice if, for any
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, there exists
\varepsilon<1/2
such that

1.

{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon}
;

2.

|{\mathcal{L}}\cap B(n,\varepsilon)|=1
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Example 7.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
be a point pattern such that
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\varepsilon_{0}}
for
\varepsilon_{0}<1/4
and
|{\mathcal{L}}\cap B(n,\varepsilon_{0})|=1
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Then
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is a perturbation of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Indeed, the points of a translate
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x
, for some arbitrary
x\in{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, are all at a distance less than
2\epsilon_{0}<1/2
from
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, hence the translates
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}-x
fulfill the conditions of the above definition.

\Diamond

The following statement highlights one of the main characteristics of a perturbed periodic lattice:

Proposition 7.3
.

If
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is a perturbation of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, then, for each
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, there exists a canonical bijection
\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
that labels a point
x\in{\mathcal{L}}
by an element of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}(x)=\big(n_{1}(x),\ldots,n_{d}(x)\big)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

This bijection is compatible with the translations,

\bm{l}_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}(x-y)=\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}(x)-\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}(y),\hskip 8.19447pt\forall\ x,y\in{\mathcal{L}}.

(7.1)

Proof.

Indeed, we can define
\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}
as the map corresponding to the graph

\big\{(y_{n},n)\in{\mathcal{L}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\ \ \{y_{n}\}={\mathcal{L}}\cap B(n,\varepsilon)\big\}.

With the stated assumptions, this is the graph of a bijection. The compatibility with the translations is obvious.∎

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
be a perturbation of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Then each compact subset
V
of
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
accepts a canonical order
\bar{\chi}_{V}:{\mathcal{I}}_{|V|}\to V
compatible with the translations,

\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}(V)}=\mathfrak{t}_{x}\circ\bar{\chi}_{V},\hskip 8.19447pt\forall\ x\in{\mathcal{L}}.

(7.2)

Proof.

Let
\bm{l}_{\mathcal{L}}:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
be the canonical bijection defined above. Given a subset
W\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, we define

W_{j}=\{x\in W\;|\;n_{j}(x)\leq n_{j}(y)\ \forall\ y\in W\}.

(7.3)

Then, for compact
V\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, we claim that the following set

\big(\cdots\big(\big(V_{1}\big)_{2}\big)_{3}\cdots\big)_{d}

(7.4)

contains one and only one point. Indeed, the set
W_{j}
in Eq. (
7.3
) is non-empty for any non-empty set
W
. As such,
V_{1}
,
\big(V_{1}\big)_{2}
, etc., are all non-empty. Assume that (

x_{1}
and
x_{2}

and, as such,
\bm{l}(x_{1})=\bm{l}(x_{2})
. Since
\bm{l}
is a bijection, this is impossible, hence (
7.4
) must contain a single point. We denote this point by

and declare
\bar{\chi}_{V}(1):=v_{1}
. By iteration, we devise the algorithm

\bar{\chi}_{V}(1)=v_{1},\ \bar{\chi}_{V}(2)=\bar{\chi}_{V\setminus\{\bar{\chi}_{V}(1)\}}(1),\ \bar{\chi}_{V}(3)=\bar{\chi}_{V\setminus\{\bar{\chi}_{V}(1),\bar{\chi}_{V}(2)\}}(1),\ {\rm etc.},

(7.5)

which supplies the desired canonical order. Indeed, since the algorithm uses the natural order of
{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is compatible with its group structure, the statement from Eq.

7.1
).∎

Remark 7.5

By permuting the indices in Eq. (

\Diamond

Remark 7.6

The pattern from Example

Proposition 7.10

{\rm j}:C^{\ast}_{r}(\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{N})\rightarrowtail C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{N}).

Proof.

Proposition 7.11

Proof.

8.
Discussion and Outlook

Our work proposes a framework for the dynamics of many fermions, but several other tools are yet to be developed in order to demonstrate its effectiveness. In this section, we point to several directions where, in our opinion, swift progress with such tools can be made.

8.1.
Self-binding versus scattered dynamics

Depending on the energy infused by an external excitation and on the type of self-interaction, the dynamics of
N
-fermions can display qualitatively distinct behaviors. Indeed, the
N
fermions can evolve together as one cluster or they can be split into several clusters. In the first scenario, one will say that the
N
-fermions are in a self-binding state, while for the second scenario that they are in a scattered state. For example, for the many-body quantum system simulated in Fig. 11 of
[
34
]
, the energy spectrum separates into several spectral islands and
[
34
]
found that the states associated with top spectral island display all the trades of self-binding dynamics. On the other hand, the states associated with the other spectral islands display all the trades of a scattered dynamics (see Fig. 23 in
[
34
]
for direct evidence of the self-binding/scattered characters). Furthermore, the spectral projection onto the top spectral island belongs to the groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebra, while the spectral projections onto the remaining spectral islands belong to the corona algebra.

We conjecture that any self-bound dynamics of
N
fermions can be generated from the bi-equivariant groupoid algebra
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
, while the scattering dynamics can be generated from the corona algebra
{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})\big)/C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
. Given the statement of Proposition
6.39
and Remark
6.41
, one can focus entirely on the separable
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
. In particular, computing various
K
-theories of
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
will shed light on the possible dynamical features that can be observed for
N
self-interacting fermions hopping on an aperiodic lattice. Given the statements from section
7
, the interesting cases are represented by the patterns with connected order covers.

8.2.
Thermodynamic limit

As we already indicated, a Hamiltonian with finite interaction range will contain a finite number of many-body potentials. Inherently, such Hamiltonians will fall into the corona algebra when sectors with large number of fermions are considered. At first sight, this seems to be an unavoidable phenomenon when considering the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. However, a Hamiltonian
H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
can be brought back into
C^{\ast}_{r,\mathcal{S}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
by sandwiching with the approximate unit introduced in Proposition
6.20
. According to our previous discussion,
\widetilde{H}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(\epsilon)=1_{N}^{\epsilon}H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}1_{N}^{\epsilon}
generates the dynamics of a “self-binding” cluster of
N
fermions and the size of the cluster can be adjusted by tuning the profile of the approximate unit. As such, we have a very convenient tool to enforce a desired density of fermions for such clusters. We recall that, traditionally, the fermion density is enforced during the thermodynamic limiting process by restricting the
N
fermions to a finite lattice of proper volume. The latter, however, destroys the Galilean invariance of the models and the motion of the center of mass.

The important conclusion is that we have put forward a new paradigm for taking the thermodynamic limit and the process can be set up entirely inside the separable
C^{\ast}
-algebras
C^{\ast}_{r,\mathcal{S}_{N}}({\mathcal{G}}_{N})
