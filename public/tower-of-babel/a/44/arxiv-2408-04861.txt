Dissipative fractional standard maps: Riemann-Liouville and Caputo
J. A. Méndez-Bermúdez, R. Aguilar-Sánchez

Abstract
In this study, given the inherent nature of dissipation in realistic dynamical systems, we explore the effects of dissipation within the context of fractional dynamics. Specifically, we consider the dissipative versions of two well known fractional maps: the Riemann-Liouville (RL) and the Caputo (C) fractional standard maps (fSMs). Both fSMs are two-dimensional nonlinear maps with memory given in action-angle variables ( I n , θ n ) (I_{n},\theta_{n}) ; n n being the discrete iteration time of the maps. In the dissipative versions these fSMs are parameterized by the strength of nonlinearity K K , the fractional order of the derivative α ∈ ( 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in(1,2] , and the dissipation strength γ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ] \gamma\in(0,1] . In this work we focus on the average action ⟨ I n ⟩ \left<I_{n}\right> and the average squared action ⟨ I n 2 ⟩ \left<I_{n}^{2}\right> when K ≫ 1 K\gg 1 , i.e. along strongly chaotic orbits. We first demonstrate, for | I 0 | > K |I_{0}|>K , that dissipation produces the exponential decay of the average action ⟨ I n ⟩ ≈ I 0 ​ exp ⁡ ( − γ ​ n ) \left<I_{n}\right>\approx I_{0}\exp(-\gamma n) in both dissipative fSMs. Then, we show that while ⟨ I n 2 ⟩ RL-fSM \left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny RL-fSM}} barely depends on α \alpha (effects are visible only when α → 1 \alpha\to 1 ), any α < 2 \alpha<2 strongly influences the behavior of ⟨ I n 2 ⟩ C-fSM \left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny C-fSM}} . We also derive an analytical expression able to describe ⟨ I n 2 ⟩ RL-fSM ​ ( K , α , γ ) \left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny RL-fSM}}(K,\alpha,\gamma) .



I
Preliminaries

Chirikov’s standard map (CSM)
C69

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=I_{n}-K\sin(\theta_{n}),\\
\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+I_{i+1},\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi),\end{array}

(1)

is known to represent the local dynamics of a large family of
Hamiltonian systems and is a paradigm model of the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) scenario;
that is, the generic transition to chaos; see e.g. Ref.
LL92
.
The CSM, a two-dimensional nonlinear map given in action-angle
variables, is the stroboscopic projection of the kicked rotor (KR), see e.g. Ref.
O08
, which
represents a free rotating stick in an inhomogeneous field that is periodically switched on in
instantaneous pulses. The KR is described by the second order differential equation

\frac{d^{2}\theta}{dt^{2}}+K\sin(\theta)\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\delta\left(t-j\right)=0.

(2)

Here,
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
is the angular position of the stick,
K
is the kicking strength, and

\delta
is the Dirac’s delta function. In the CSM,
I
corresponds to the angular momentum of the KR’s stick.

By replacing the second order derivative in the equation of motion of the KR by fractional
operators (fractional derivatives, fractional integrals or fractional integro-differential operators),
fractional versions of the KR are obtained.
Among the many fractional KRs (fKRs) reported in the literature we can mention:
the Riemann-Liouville fKR
TZ08
;
ET09
,
the Caputo fKR
E11
,
the Hadamard fKR
T21d
,
the Erdelyi-Kober fKR
T21e
, and
the Hilfer fKR
T21f
;
among others, see e.g.
T21b
;
T24
.
All the fKRs listed above, have stroboscopic versions which are known as fractional
standard maps (fSMs), in resemblance with the CSM.

Probably, the most studied fSMs are the Riemann-Liouville fSM
(RL-fSM)
ET09
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=I_{n}-K\sin(\theta_{n}),\\
\theta_{n+1}=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}I_{i+1}V^{1}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)},\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi),\end{array}

(3)

and the Caputo fSM (C-fSM)
E11
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=I_{n}\\
\quad\quad\ -\displaystyle\frac{K}{\Gamma(\alpha-1)}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}V^{2}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)\sin(\theta_{i})+\sin(\theta_{n})\right],\\
\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+I_{0}\\
\quad\quad\ -\displaystyle\frac{K}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}V^{1}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)\sin(\theta_{i}),\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi).\end{array}

(4)

Here,
\Gamma
is the Gamma function,
1<\alpha\leq 2
is assumed, and

V^{k}_{\alpha}(m)=m^{\alpha-k}-(m-1)^{\alpha-k}.

Note that the sums in the equations of maps (
3
) and (
4
) make the RL- fSM
and the C-fSM to have memory, meaning that the future
(n+1)-
state depends on the entire orbit and
not on the present
n-
state only.
Both, the RL-fSM and the C-fSM are parameterized by
K
and
\alpha
which control the strength of
nonlinearity and the fractional order of the derivative, respectively.
For
\alpha=2
, both the RL-fSM and the C-fSM reproduce the CSM
C69
;
E11
.

It is important to stress that all three maps defined above (the CSM, the RL-fSM and the C-fSM) are
energy-conservative maps. That is, they do not account for dissipation which is a fundamental concept
in dynamical systems, referring to the irreversible loss of energy over time due to the interaction of a
system with its environment, see e.g.
R06
.

Dissipation plays a crucial role in the
stability, behavior, and predictability of dynamical systems, particularly in real-world applications.
In complex dynamical systems, dissipation can occur through various mechanisms such as frictional
forces, viscous drag, turbulence, etc. These mechanisms dissipate the energy of a system, leading to
its stabilization and eventual decay.
The effect of dissipation on dynamical systems can be highly dependent on the nature of the system,
its parameters, and the dissipation mechanisms involved. For example, in some systems, dissipation
can lead to instability, while in others, it can promote stability.

Indeed, in order to explore the effects of dissipation in generic chaotic systems, Zaslavsky introduced
a dissipative map in Ref.
Z78
, which can also be written in the canonical form as

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=(1-\gamma)I_{n}-K\sin(\theta_{n}),\\
\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+I_{i+1},\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi).\end{array}

(5)

Map (
5
) is also referred to as the dissipative CSM
VZ89
.
Above, the dissipation is parametrized by
\gamma
, the dissipation strength.
If
\gamma
equals zero in (
5
), the area-preserving CSM is recovered.
Since the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of map (
5
) is
1-\gamma
, it is area-contracting
for any
\gamma\in(0,1]
.

Furthermore, Tarasov and Edelman
TE10
;
T11
already introduced the dissipative version of the RL-fSM
map as:

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=(1-\gamma)I_{n}-K\sin(\theta_{n}),\\
\theta_{n+1}=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}I_{i+1}V^{1}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)},\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi).\end{array}

(6)

Moreover, in analogy, here we introduce the dissipative version of the C-fSM as

\begin{array}[]{ll}I_{n+1}=(1-\gamma)I_{n}\\
\quad\quad\ -\displaystyle\frac{K}{\Gamma(\alpha-1)}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}V^{2}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)\sin(\theta_{i})+\sin(\theta_{n})\right],\\
\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}+I_{0}\\
\quad\quad\ -\displaystyle\frac{K}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\sum_{i=0}^{n}V^{1}_{\alpha}(n-i+1)\sin(\theta_{i}),\quad\mbox{mod}~(2\pi).\end{array}

(7)

Also note that the dissipative maps of Eqs. (
6
) and (
7
) are, respectively, the
Riemann-Liouville and the Caputo fractional versions of the dissipative CSM of Eq. (
5
).
We recall that both dissipative fractional maps are parametrized by: the strength of nonlinearity
K
,
the fractional order of the derivative
\alpha\in(1,2]
, and the dissipation strength
\gamma\in(0,1]
.

Therefore, the purpose of this work is twofold.
First, we numerically look for the effects of dissipation (parametrized by
\gamma
) in fractional
dynamical systems, represented by the RL-fSM and the C-fSM.
Specifically, we focus on the average action
\left<I_{n}\right>
and the
average squared action
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>
when
K\gg 1
, i.e. along strongly chaotic orbits.
Second, we obtain expressions for both
\left<I_{n}\right>
and
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>
which properly
incorporates the parameter set
(K,\alpha,\gamma)
.

From Fig.

we observe that
\left<I_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
decays with

;
the larger the dissipation strength the fastest the decay towards a chaotic attractor located
around
I\sim 0
. Indeed, the decay of
\left<I_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
is exponential
as it is demonstrated below; see also Refs.
ALM13

We start with an initial condition
I_{0}
and iterate the first equation of the dRL-fSM to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}I_{1}=(1-\gamma)~I_{0}-K\sin(\theta_{0}),\\
I_{2}=(1-\gamma)^{2}~I_{0}-K[(1-\gamma)\sin(\theta_{0})+\sin(\theta_{1})],\\
I_{3}=(1-\gamma)^{3}~I_{0}-K[(1-\gamma)^{2}\sin(\theta_{0})\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+(1-\gamma)\sin(\theta_{1})+\sin(\theta_{2})],\\
\quad\ \vdots\\
I_{n}=(1-\gamma)^{n}I_{0}+K\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(1-\gamma)^{n-1-i}\sin(\theta_{i}),\end{array}

which leads to

\left<I_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}=(1-\gamma)^{n}I_{0}+K\left<\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(1-\gamma)^{n-1-i}\sin(\theta_{i})\right>.

(9)

Given the periodicity of the sine function, the second term in the r.h.s. of Eq. (
9
) can
be neglected. Therefore, by expanding the first term of (
9
) in powers of
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left<I_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}\simeq I_{0}\left[\ln(1-\gamma)n+\frac{1}{2!}\ln(1-\gamma)^{2}n^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{3!}\ln(1-\gamma)^{3}n^{3}+\frac{1}{4!}\ln(1-\gamma)^{4}n^{4}+\ldots\right].

Now, considering a small value of
\gamma
and performing a Taylor expansion we obtain that

\left<I_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}\simeq I_{0}e^{-\gamma n},

(10)

meaning an exponential decay towards chaotic attractors where
\gamma
is the exponential
decay rate.

In Fig.
1

10

correspondence with the data, mainly for large dissipation strengths.
Note that in Fig.
1

I_{0}>0
only. However, we observe the same panorama
for
I<0
(not shown here), once
|I_{0}|>K
, where Eq. (
10

We want to notice that the panorama reported for

From the first equation of the dRL-fSM we have that

I^{2}_{n+1}=(1-\gamma)^{2}I_{n}^{2}+K^{2}\sin^{2}(\theta_{n})-2(1-\gamma)KI_{n}\sin(\theta_{n}),

so we can write

\left<I^{2}_{n+1}\right>=(1-\gamma)^{2}\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>+K^{2}\left<\sin^{2}(\theta_{n})\right>+\ 2(1-\gamma)K\left<I_{n}\right>\left<\sin(\theta_{n})\right>.

Since
\left<\sin(\theta_{n})\right>=0
, the term
2(1-\gamma)K\left<I_{n}\right>\left<\sin(\theta_{n})\right>

can be eliminated (moreover, given the symmetry of the phase space with respect to
I=0
,
also
\left<I_{n}\right>=0
). Therefore,

\left<I^{2}_{n+1}\right>=\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>+\frac{K^{2}}{2},

(11)

where we have already substituted
\left<\sin^{2}(\theta_{n})\right>=1/2
.
Then, by noticing that

\left<I^{2}_{n+1}\right>-\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>=\frac{\left<I^{2}_{n+1}\right>-\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>}{(n+1)-n}\approx\frac{dJ}{dn},

(12)

we rewrite Eq. (
11
) as the differential equation

\frac{dJ}{dn}=-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})J+\frac{K^{2}}{2},

(13)

where
J\equiv\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>
.
Note that Eq. (
13
) can be solved straightforwardly as

\int^{J}_{J_{0}}\frac{dJ^{\prime}}{-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})J^{\prime}+K^{2}/2}=\int^{n}_{n_{0}}dn^{\prime}\ ,

with
J_{0}=\left<I_{0}^{2}\right>=I_{0}^{2}
and
n_{0}=0
.
Therefore, we finally write

\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}=I_{0}^{2}e^{-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})n}+\frac{K^{2}}{2(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}\left[1-e^{-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})n}\right].

(14)

Then, in Fig.
2
we plot Eq. (
14
) as red-dashed lines and observe a
remarkably good correspondence with the data for both
I_{0}<K
and
I_{0}>K

In addition, Eq. (
14
) allows us to make the following observations:

(i)

For any
\gamma>0
the saturation of
\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
is observed in the

I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}\equiv\lim_{n\to\infty}\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}=\frac{K^{2}}{2(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})}\ .

(15)

(ii)

When
I_{0}<K
, case depicted in Figs.
2

of
\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>

14

\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}\approx I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}\left[1-e^{-(2\gamma-\gamma^{2})n}\right],

which provides the growth

(18)

I^{2}_{0}>I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}

\gamma_{\mbox{\tiny cr}}=1-\sqrt{1-\frac{K^{2}}{2I^{2}_{0}}}\ .

(21)

For the parameters used in Figs.
2
(d-f), Eq. (
21
) gives

\gamma_{\mbox{\tiny cr}}\approx 2.5\times 10^{-3}
, which coincides well with the behavior of the
data.

Finally, we want to note that the panorama reported above for the dRL-fSM is equivalent to that of
the dissipative CSM
MOL16
as well as for the dissipative DSM
MOL16
.
This, in fact, could have been anticipated since for the derivation of Eq. (
14
) we used the
equation for the action in map (
6
), which coincides with that of the dissipative CSM and the
dissipative DSM.

IV
Discussion and conclusions

In this work we have considered the dissipative versions of two well-known fractional maps:
the dissipative Riemann-Liouville fractional standard map (dRL-FSM) and
the (introduced here) dissipative Caputo fractional standard map (dC-FSM). So
we explored the effects of dissipation within the context of fractional dynamics.
Both, the dRL-fSM and the dC-fSM are parameterized by the strength of nonlinearity
K>0
,
the fractional order of the derivative
\alpha\in(1,2]
, and the dissipation strength
\gamma\in(0,1]
.
Specifically, we focused on the average action
\left<I_{n}\right>
and the average squared action

\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>
when
K\gg 1
, i.e. along strongly chaotic orbits.

First, we demonstrated, for
|I_{0}|>K
, that dissipation produces the exponential decay of the average
action in both dissipative fSMs, see Eqs. (
10
) and (
22
) as well as
Figs.
1
and
4
.

Second, we showed that
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny RL-fSM}}
barely depends on
\alpha
, see
Fig.
2
, and derived an analytical expression able to properly describe

\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny RL-fSM}}(K,\alpha,\gamma)
, see Eq. (
14
).
Also we observed for
n\to\infty
that
\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
converges to a saturation
value,
I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}
, and found an expression for it, see Eq. (
15
).
Moreover, from Eq. (
20
), which is written by substituting Eq. (
15
) into
Eq. (
14
), we can recognize
the following
universal
function of the variable
\overline{n}
:

\overline{\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>}_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}\equiv\frac{\left<I^{2}_{n}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}-I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}}{I_{0}^{2}-I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}}\approx e^{-\overline{n}}\ ,

(25)

which embraces all the scenarios reported in Fig.
2
.
Here
\overline{n}\equiv n/n_{\mbox{\tiny CO}}
where
n_{\mbox{\tiny CO}}
is given by Eq. (
18
).

Third, we showed that any
\alpha<2
strongly influences the behavior of
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dC-fSM}}
,
see Fig.
5
, and obtained a phenomenological expressions for
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dC-fSM}}
,
see Eq. (
23
), which works in certain parameter regimes.
Moreover, in contrast with
I^{2}_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}
, we found that the saturation value for the average
squared action of the dC-fSM strongly depends on
\alpha
, see Eq. (
24
) and Figs.
5

and
6
.

Finally, it is interesting to highlight that from the analysis of Eq. (
14
) we were able to
identify the condition, i.e. Eq. (
19
), for which dissipation and diffusion compensate each other
exactly so the squared average action of the dRL-fSM remains constant. That is, by setting
I_{0}=I_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-RL}}
,
the curves
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
vs.
n
are straight horizontal lines, except for

\alpha\to 1
where
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dRL-fSM}}
slightly decreases with
n
; see
Fig.
3
.
A pertinent question now is whether we can observe a similar behavior for

\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dC-fSM}}
. Thus, in Fig.
7
we report
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dC-fSM}}

vs.
n
, for several combinations of
\gamma
and
\alpha
, where we have used
I_{0}\approx I_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-C}}
,
where
I_{\mbox{\tiny SAT-C}}
has been computed numerically; see e.g. Fig.
6
.
It is clear from Fig.
7
that
\left<I_{n}^{2}\right>_{\mbox{\tiny dC-fSM}}
does not remain constant as
a function of time, instead it first decreases exponentially and approaches a
second
saturation value.
Indeed, Eq. (
23
) reproduces well the numerical data, see the green-dashed lines.
The reason for this difference between the dRL-fSM and the dC-fSM is the absence/presence of the
sum (which implies memory) in the equation for the action in the dRL-fSM/dC-fSM. So, to maintain
the saturation value in the dC-fSM the entire orbit is needed.

We hope our results may stimulate further numerical as well as analytical studies on
dissipative fractional dynamics.
