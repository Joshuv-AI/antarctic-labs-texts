Abstract
. Genome rearrangement is a common model for molecular evolution. In this paper, we consider the Pairwise Rearrangement problem, which takes as input two genomes and asks for the number of minimum-length sequences of permissible operations transforming the first genome into the second. In the Single Cut-and-Join model (Bergeron, Medvedev, & Stoye, J. Comput. Biol. 2010), Pairwise Rearrangement is # ​ P \#\textsf{P} -complete (Bailey, et. al., COCOON 2023), which implies that exact sampling is intractable. In order to cope with this intractability, we investigate the parameterized complexity of this problem. We exhibit a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm with respect to the number of components in the adjacency graph that are not cycles of length 2 2 or paths of length 1 1 . As a consequence, we obtain that Pairwise Rearrangement in the Single Cut-and-Join model is fixed-parameter tractable by distance. Our results suggest that the number of nontrivial components in the adjacency graph serves as the key obstacle for efficient sampling.

1.
Introduction

With the natural occurrence of mutations in genomes and the wide range of effects this can incite, scientists seek to understand the evolutionary relationship between species. Several discrete mathematical models have been proposed to model these mutations based on biological observations. Genome rearrangement models consider situations in which large-scale mutations alter the order of the genes within the genome. Sturtevant
[
17
,
18
]
observed the biological phenomenon of genome rearrangement in the study of strains of
Drosophila
(fruit flies), only a few years after he produced the first genetic map
[
16
]
. Palmer & Herbon
[
15
]
observed similar phenomenon in plants. McClintock
[
10
]
also found experimental evidence of genes rearranging themselves, or “transposing” themselves, within chromosomes.

Subsequent to his work on
Drosophila
, Sturtevant together with Novitski
[
19
]
introduced one of the first genome rearrangement problems, seeking a minimum length sequence of operations, called a
scenario
,
that would transform one genome into another. In investigating these questions, it is of key importance to balance biological relevance with computational tractability. One central issue is that of combinatorial explosion: the number of scenarios even between small genomes may be too large to handle, making it difficult to test hypotheses on all possible scenarios.
Thus, we desire a polynomial time algorithm to uniformly sample from the rearrangement scenarios. Since uniform sampling is no harder than exact enumeration
[
8
]
, we investigate the computational complexity of the
Pairwise Rearrangement
problem (Definition
2.3
) which asks for the number of minimum-length scenarios transforming one genome into another.

The
Pairwise Rearrangement
problem has received significant attention in several genome rearrangement models.

Pairwise Rearrangement
is known to be in
FP
for the Single Cut or Join model
[
11
]
, but is conjectured to be
#P
-complete for the Double Cut-and-Join model
[
14
]
. The Single Cut-and-Join model sits between these two models. Recently, Bailey, et al.
[
1
]
, showed that
Pairwise Rearrangement
is
#P
-complete in the Single Cut-and-Join model. However, in practice, the key structures that serve as obstacles to efficient sampling may not necessarily appear. In particular, the relevant obstacles for efficient sampling in the Single Cut-and-Join model remain opaque.

Main Results.
In this paper, we investigate the
Pairwise Rearrangement
problem in the Single Cut-and-Join model through the lens of parameterized complexity. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

In the Single Cut-and-Join model,
Pairwise Rearrangement
is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to the number of components in the adjacency graph (see Definition
2.4
) that are not trivial (cycles of length
2
or paths of length
1
).

Our parameterization in Theorem
1.1
has biological significance. Indeed, chromoanagenesis is a carcinogenic mechanism that involves massive chromosomal rearrangements, which may lead to fewer components in the adjacency graph
[
7
]
.

The
adjacency graph
(see Definition
2.4
) is a bipartite multigraph illustrating where two genomes differ. Bergeron, Medvedev, and Stoye
[
2
]
established a precise relationship between the adjacency graph and the distance between two genomes in the Single Cut-and-Join model. The operations in this model induce structural changes on the adjacency graph
[
1
, Observation 2.7]
. We leverage this crucially to establish Theorem
1.1
. Our precise technique involves developing a dynamic programming algorithm that, when the number of nontrivial components is bounded, the corresponding lookup table only has a polynomial number of entries. This establishes our claim of polynomial-time computation. We stress that arriving at the recurrence relations for the dynamic programming algorithm and proving their correctness is technical and nontrivial. Indeed this is not surprising, as
Pairwise Rearrangement
is
#P
-complete
[
1
]
. While our work is theoretical in nature, the algorithm underlying Theorem
1.1
in fact yields an efficient practical implementation (see
GitHub
).

We also note that if the distance (Equation (
1
)) between the two genomes is bounded
[
2
]
, then so is the number of nontrivial components. As a consequence, we obtain the following corollary:

Corollary 1.2
.

In the Single Cut-and-Join model,
Pairwise Rearrangement
is fixed parameter tractable with respect to the distance between two genomes.

To the best of our knowledge, parameterized complexity has received minimal attention in the genome rearrangement literature. For instance, a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm (parameterized by the number of components in the adjacency graph) for
Pairwise Rearrangement
in the Double Cut-and-Join model can easily be deduced from the work of
[
3
]
, though the authors do not explicitly investigate the parameterized complexity of this problem. Thus, beyond providing a means of coping with the intractability of
Pairwise Rearrangement
in the Single Cut-and-Join model, Theorem
1.1
(together with the results of
[
3
]
) makes precise that the number of components in the adjacency graph serves as a key obstacle towards efficient sampling, across multiple models of genome rearrangement.

In contrast, there has been significant algorithmic work on approximation and sampling (see, for instance,
[
11
,
12
,
4
,
5
,
13
,
9
]
), to cope with the intractability of enumeration. To the best of our knowledge, such approaches have not been fruitful against the Reversal model, for which the complexity of
Pairwise Rearrangement
is a longstanding open problem. We are not aware of any work on approximate counting or sampling for
Pairwise Rearrangement
in the Single Cut-and-Join model.

3.
Combinatorics of Genome Rearrangement

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.1
. Our strategy will be to build a lookup table for dynamic programming. In particular, our technique relies crucially on the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
1
, Lemma A.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
be a subset of components of an adjacency graph, and let
\Pi(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})
denote the set of all partitions of
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
. We have

\#\text{MPS}(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})}\binom{d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2}),\ldots,d(\pi_{p(\pi)})}\prod_{i=1}^{p(\pi)}\#\text{ST}(\pi_{i}),

where
\pi=\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{p(\pi)}\}
.

We will utilize Lemma
3.1
in the following manner. Fix an entry in the lookup table, and let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
denote the set of components being considered at said entry. Now fix a partition
\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
. In order to compute
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
, we will proceed as follows. For each
i\in[p(\pi)]
, we first check if
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})=0
. This step is computable in polynomial-time by checking whether there exists a permissible sorting operation (see Observation
2.7
). If
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})\neq 0
, then we access the entry in the lookup table for
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})
. This will allow us to compute

\binom{d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2}),\ldots,d(\pi_{p(\pi)})}\prod_{i=1}^{p(\pi)}\#\text{ST}(\pi_{i}).

We will show later that as the number of nontrivial crowns in the adjacency graph is bounded (by assumption), there are only a polynomial number of partitions
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{p(\pi)})\in\Pi(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})
such that
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})\neq 0
for all
i\in[p(\pi)]
.

We will now investigate how to compute
\#\text{ST}(\pi_{i})
, which requires studying which sets of components can and cannot sort together.

Proposition 3.2
.

Given a set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
of components of some adjacency graph
G
,
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=0
if
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
has any of the following properties:

(1)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
contains at least two components, at least one of which is a trivial crown.

(2)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

(3)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

-shaped component.

contains more than one path and at least one

-shaped component.

Proof.

We refer to Observation

and Figure

for the permissible sorting operations, from which the proof essentially follows. We provide full details below.

(1)

None of the sorting operations (a)-(h) sort a trivial crown with any other component. So if
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
contains at least one trivial crown and any other component, then
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=0
, as required.

(2)

\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
contains components
A
and

, where
A
is a

-shaped component. Note that

is only possible if

is an

is also a

-shaped component, any sequence of sorting operations applied to

and

will not yield components that can sort together. Therefore
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=0
.

(3)

Suppose
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
contains components
A
and
B
, where
A
is an
M
-shaped component. Note that
A\sim B
is only possible if
B
is a
W
-shaped component, using sorting operation (f). As seen in Figure
4
Diagram (II), sorting operations with
M
-shaped components cannot create
W
-shaped components. Therefore, if
B
is also an
M
-shaped component, any sequence of sorting operations applied to
A
and
B
will not yield components that can sort together. Therefore
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})=0

(4)

Suppose
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
contains components
A

∎

If
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
consists of only one trivial crown, then
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})=1
. Otherwise there are five possibilities for when
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})\neq 0
. Below we list each case and define a function along with each that we will use to simplify
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},M,W,N)
. In what follows,
\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}
denotes the set of finite multisets of integers in which each integer is at least
2
. Also, for this paper we take
\mathbb{N}
to include
0
. We will now list our cases:

•

The components are a single
N
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. Define
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}:\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C},\eta)=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},\emptyset,\emptyset,\{\eta\})
.

•

The components are a single
W
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. Define
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}:\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},\emptyset,\{w\},\emptyset)
.

•

The components are one or more nontrivial crowns. Define
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}:\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}-\{\emptyset\}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C})=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},\emptyset,\emptyset,\emptyset)
.

•

The components are a single
M
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. Define
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}:\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},\{m\},\emptyset,\emptyset)
.

•

The components are a single
M
-shaped component, a single

-shaped component, and zero or more nontrivial crowns. Define
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}:\mathcal{Z}_{\geq 2}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}\times\mathbb{Z}^{+}\to\mathbb{N}
as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w)=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{C},\{m\},\{w\},\emptyset)
.

We recall the following lemma, which allows us to compute the number of sorting scenarios for a single component.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
1
, Lemma 2.9]
)
.

•

For all
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\emptyset,\eta)=1
.

•

For all
w\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\emptyset,w)=2^{w-1}
.

•

For all
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\emptyset,m)=2^{m-1}
.

•

For all
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\left\{c\right\})=c\cdot 2^{c-1}
.

Our goal now is to enumerate the number of sorting scenarios in each of these cases. We first provide a recurrence relation for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C},\eta)
.

Proposition 3.4
.

We have the following recurrence relations for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}
:

\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C},\eta)=\begin{cases}1&:\mathcal{C}=\emptyset,\eta=0,\\
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(2c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},c)\right)&:\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset,\eta=0,\\
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C},\eta-1)+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(2c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},c+\eta)\right)&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\emptyset,0)
is the case of a single path of size
0
(a single edge). This represents that the given telomere has already been sorted, so there is only one way to sort this component (do nothing). On the other hand, when sorting a collection of nontrivial crowns together with an
N
-shaped component, the first operation either consists of applying a cut-join on the
N
-shaped component alone if it has size greater than
0
(
1
way to do this), or applying a cut-join of a nontrivial crown to the
N
-shaped component. If the crown being operated on has size
c
, there are
c
possible places to cut it, and there are
2
possible ways to join it to to the
N
-shaped component (either telomere of the newly cut crown). The result of this operation is one fewer crown and an increase in the size of the
N
-shaped component by
c
. These considerations lead to the recursive formulas for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C},\eta)
.
∎

We next provide a recurrence relation for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

We have the following recurrence relations for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}
:

\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)=\begin{cases}1&\hskip-3.61371pt:\mathcal{C}=\emptyset,w=1,\\
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(4c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},c+1)\right)&\hskip-3.61371pt:\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset,w=1,\\
2\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w-1)+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(4c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},c+w)\right)&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\emptyset,1)
is the case of a single
W
-shaped component of size
1
. To sort such a path, join the telomeres in the top genome. So, there is only one way to sort this component. On the other hand, when sorting a collection of nontrivial crowns together with a
W
-shaped component, the first operation either consists of a cut-join of one end of the
W
-shaped component if it has size greater than
1
(
2
ways to do this) or a cut-join of a nontrivial crown to the
W
-shaped component. If the crown being operated on has size
c
, there are
c
possible places to cut it, and there are
4
possible ways to join it to to the
W
-shaped component (either telomere of the newly cut crown could join with either telomere of the
W
-shaped component). The result of this operation is one fewer nontrivial crown and an increase in the size of the
W
-shaped component by
c
. These considerations lead to the recursive formulas for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)
.
∎

We next provide an expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C})
.

Proposition 3.6
.

We have the following expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}
:

\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},c)\right).

Note that
\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
is not in the domain of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}
, and so this expression is well-defined.

Proof.

When a collection of crowns sorts together, the first operation must be a cut. If the crown being operated on has size
c
, there are
c
possible places to cut it. The result of this operation is one fewer crown and a
W
-shaped component of size
c
. These considerations lead to the expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

A closed-form expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C})
was previously established in
[
1
, Corollary 3.2]
.

We have already discussed
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)
. Below we establish tools to show
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},w)
is the same as
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},w)
.
We have thus far considered
A(G_{1},G_{2})
, where operations act on
V_{1}
. We can also consider
A(G_{2},G_{1})
, which is the same as
A(G_{1},G_{2})
, but we now operate on
V_{2}
. Every component of
A(G_{1},G_{2})
corresponds to a component in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
(on the same vertices), though component types are not necessarily the same. For example, an
M
-shaped component
A_{12}
in
A(G_{1},G_{2})
corresponds to a
W
-shaped component
A_{21}
in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
.

Definition 3.8
.

For an operation
\alpha
, define the reverse operation
\alpha^{rev}
as follows. If
\alpha
is a cut at adjacency
ab
, then
\alpha^{rev}
is a join of
a
and
b
. Similarly, the reverse of a join is a cut. Further, if
\alpha
is a cut-join
ab,c
to
ac,b
, then
\alpha^{rev}
is a cut-join
ac,b
to
ab,c
. For a sequence
\sigma
of operations
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k}
, let the reverse sequence
\sigma^{rev}
be
\sigma^{rev}_{k},\dots,\sigma^{rev}_{1}
.

Observe that reversing an operation does not change the extremities operated on.
We now show that reversal preserves the sort together relation.

Proposition 3.9
.

For a sorting scenario
\sigma
and components
A_{12}
and
B_{12}
in
A(G_{1},G_{2})
, suppose we have that
A_{12}\sim B_{12}
. Then, for
\sigma^{rev}
and corresponding components
A_{21}
and
B_{21}
in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
, we have
A_{21}\sim B_{21}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be a sorting scenario for
A(G_{1},G_{2})
that consists of a sequence of operations
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k}
. Suppose further that
\sigma
sorts two components
A_{12}
and
B_{12}
together such that
A_{12}\sim B_{12}
. Since
\sigma
transforms
G_{1}
into
G_{2}
, the sequence
\sigma^{rev}
transforms
G_{2}
into
G_{1}
. Thus,
\sigma^{rev}
is a sorting scenario for
A(G_{2},G_{1})
.

Let
A_{21}
and
B_{21}
denote the two components in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
that correspond to
A_{12}
and
B_{12}
. We will show that
A_{21}\sim B_{21}
with respect to
\sigma^{rev}
. Since
A_{12}\sim B_{12}
with respect to
\sigma
, there is some
i
such that operation
\sigma_{i}
is a cut-join operation which joins an extremity
a
of
A_{12}
with an extremity
b
of
B_{12}
. Moreover,
\sigma^{rev}_{i}
cuts the adjacency
ab
. Since
a
and
b
come from different components, they must have been joined through operation
\sigma^{rev}_{j}
for some
j>i
to create adjacency
ab
(which is then cut by
\sigma^{rev}_{i}
). Thus,
A_{12}\sim B_{12}
in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
with respect to
\sigma^{rev}
, as required.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{12}^{\prime}
be a subset of the components of
A(G_{1},G_{2})
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{21}^{\prime}
be the corresponding subset of the components of
A(G_{2},G_{1})
. Then
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{12})=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{21})
. Consequently,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)=\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},m)
, and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w)=\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},w,m)
.

Proof.

Given a subset
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{12}
of components of
A(G_{1},G_{2})
, let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{21}
be the set of corresponding components in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
. Now, let
\Sigma_{12}
be the set of scenarios that sort the components of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{12}
together, and let
\Sigma_{21}
be the set of scenarios that sort the components of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{21}
together. By definition,
\left|\Sigma_{12}\right|=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{12})
and
\left|\Sigma_{21}\right|=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{21})
. Next, let
\Sigma_{12}^{rev}
and
\Sigma_{21}^{rev}
be the sets of reversals of the scenarios in
\Sigma_{12}
and
\Sigma_{21}
respectively. By Proposition
3.9
,
\Sigma_{12}^{rev}\subseteq\Sigma_{21}
. Since reversal is an involution, we also obtain that
\Sigma_{21}^{rev}\subseteq\Sigma_{12}
. Since
\left|\Sigma_{12}\right|=\left|\Sigma_{12}^{rev}\right|
and
\left|\Sigma_{21}\right|=\left|\Sigma_{21}^{rev}\right|
, this implies that
\left|\Sigma_{12}\right|\leq\left|\Sigma_{21}\right|
and
\left|\Sigma_{21}\right|\leq\left|\Sigma_{12}\right|
. Together, these imply that
\left|\Sigma_{12}\right|=\left|\Sigma_{21}\right|
, meaning
\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{12})=\operatorname{\#ST}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{21})
, as required.

We note that an
M
-shaped component in
A(G_{1},G_{2})
is a
W
-shaped component in
A(G_{2},G_{1})
. Thus, we obtain that
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)=\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},m)
and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w)=\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},w,m)
, as desired.
∎

By Corollary
3.10
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)=\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},m)
. But, here we present a different expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)
, which will be useful later when we examine
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}
. To simplify notation, we introduce a new definition. Denote:

\text{sum}(\mathcal{C}):=\sum_{c\in C}c.

Lemma 3.11
.

We have the following expression for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}(\mathcal{C},m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\binom{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m-1}{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta}\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m-1-\eta)

\displaystyle+\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m,c)\right).

Proof.

As we are sorting a collection of crowns with a single
M
shaped component, we have the following cases for the initial sorting operation.

Case 1: The initial sorting operation cuts a nontrivial crown.
Fix a nontrivial crown
C
of size
c\in\mathcal{C}
. This initial cut transforms
C
into a
W
-shaped component. There are
c
places to cut
C
. We now have a (possibly empty) collection
\mathcal{C}-\{c\}
of nontrivial crowns, a single
M
of size
m
, and a single
W
of size
c
. There are
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},m,c)
ways of sorting these components together. Summing over all such choices of which initial crown to cut yields the following number of sorting scenarios, as desired:

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\{c\},m,c).

Case 2: The initial sorting operation does not cut a nontrivial crown.
We must cut the
M
-shaped component into two
N
-shaped components:
N_{1}
of size
\eta
and
N_{2}
of size
m-1-\eta
. By Proposition
3.2
(d),
N_{1}
must sort together with some (possibly empty) subset of nontrivial crowns, and
N_{2}
must sort together with the remaining crowns.

In order to enumerate the desired sorting scenarios, we follow the strategy of Lemma
3.1
. Let
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
be the collection of components after cutting the
M
-shaped component into
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
, and let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
be a partition of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
where
\pi_{i}
consists of
N_{i}
and the crowns that sort together with
N_{i}
. From Equation (
2
) we have that:

d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m-1.

Now fix such a partition
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
as described above. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
denote the subset of
\mathcal{C}
corresponding to the sizes of the crowns in
\pi_{1}
.
Observe the following:

•

d(\pi_{1})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta

•

d(\pi_{2})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+m-1-\eta

•

\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{1})=\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)

•

\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{2})=\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m-1-\eta)
.

Now by Lemma
3.1
, we have

\sum_{\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\right)}\binom{d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2})}\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{1})\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{2})

sorting scenarios. By the above, this equals

\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\binom{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m-1}{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta}\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m-1-\eta)

for fixed
\eta
.

Summing over all such
\eta
yields the total number of sorting scenarios in this case:

\sum_{\eta=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\binom{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m-1}{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta}\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m-1-\eta).

We add the counts together for Case 1 and Case 2 to obtain the desired number of sorting scenarios.
∎

It now remains to define a recurrence relation for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w)
. We begin with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

We have the following recurrence relations for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(\mathcal{C},m,w)&:w=1,\\
2\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w-1)+f(\mathcal{C},m,w)&:w>1.\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle\hskip-7.22743ptf(\mathcal{C},m,w)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(4c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m,c+w)\right)

\displaystyle\hskip-43.36243pt+\sum_{\eta=0}^{m-1}4\cdot\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\binom{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m+w-1}{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta}\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m+w-\eta-1).

Proof.

When sorting a collection of nontrivial crowns together with an
M
-shaped component and a
W
-shaped component, we have the following cases for the initial sorting operation.

Case 1: Cut-join one end of the
W
-shaped component if it has size greater than
1
.
There are
2
ways to do this, one for each end of the
W
-shaped component. The result is a trivial crown (recall that trivial components don’t require further sorting) and a decrease in the size of the
W
-shaped component by
1
. This establishes the
2\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w-1)
term.

Case 2: Cut-join a nontrivial crown to the
W
-shaped component.
When applying a cut-join operation on a nontrivial crown of size
c
and
W
-shaped component, there are
c
possible places to cut the crown, and there are
4
possible ways to join it to the
W
-shaped component (join either telomere of the newly cut crown to either telomere of the
W
-shaped component). The result is one fewer crown and an increase in the size of the
W
-shaped component by
c
. This establishes the term
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(4c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m,c+w)\right)
.

Case 3: Cut-join the
M
-shaped component and
W
-shaped component into two
N
-shaped components.
There are
m
possible places that we can cut the
M
-shaped component; cutting at the
i^{\text{th}}
peak from the left end of the path yields an
N
-shaped component of size
i-1
from the left piece and an
N
-shaped component of size
m-i
from the right piece. Then there are four possibilities for joining: the
N
-shaped component of size
i-1
is joined to either of the two telomeres of the
W
-shaped component creating an
N
-shaped component of size
w+i-1
, or the
N
-shaped component of size
m-i
is joined to either telomere of the
W
-shaped component creating an
N
-shaped component of size
w+m-i
. We will determine the number of sorting scenarios for one of these possibilities, and then show that all four possibilities yield the same number of sorting scenarios.

Consider the first possibility and let
N_{1}
be the
N
-shaped component of size
\eta=w+i-1
, and
N_{2}
be the
N
-shaped component of size
m+w-\eta-1
. Similarly to the proof in Lemma
3.11
, let
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
be the collection of components in the adjacency graph after this cut-join occurred, and let
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
be a partition of
\mathcal{B^{\prime}}
where
\pi_{i}
consists of
N_{i}
and the crowns that sort together with
N_{i}
. Then from Equation (
2
) we have that:

d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m+w-1.

Fix a partition
\pi=(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
, and let
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
denote the sub-multiset of
\mathcal{C}
corresponding to the sizes of the crowns in
\pi_{1}
. Then:

•

d(\pi_{1})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta

•

d(\pi_{2})=\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+m+w-\eta-1

•

\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{1})=\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)

•

\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{2})=\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m+w-\eta-1)
.

By Lemma
3.1
, we have

\sum_{\left(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\right)}\binom{d(\mathcal{B^{\prime}})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2})}\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{1})\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{2})

sorting scenarios, or equivalently

\sum_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{C}}\binom{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C})+|\mathcal{C}|+m+w-1}{\operatorname{sum}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|+\eta}\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\eta)\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}^{\prime},m+w-\eta-1)

for fixed

\displaystyle+2\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},m+w)-\sum\limits_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\left(2c\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m+w,c)\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m-1,w)+\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w-1)+2\cdot\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C},m+w)

\displaystyle+\sum\limits_{c\in\mathcal{C}}2c\biggr(\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},c+m,w)+\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m,c+w)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C}-\left\{c\right\},m+w,c)\biggr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m-1,w)+\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C},m,w-1)+g(\mathcal{C},m,w).

∎

4.
Fixed-Parameter Tractability of
Pairwise Rearrangement

In this section, we will use the recurrences we have found to establish our main result.

Theorem
1.1
.

In the Single Cut-and-Join model,
Pairwise Rearrangement
is fixed-parameter tractable, with respect to the number of nontrivial components in the adjacency graph.

Proof.

Given two genomes with
n
edges each, the adjacency graph is easily constructed in polynomial time. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the set of components for this adjacency graph, and let
k
denote the number of nontrivial components in
\mathcal{B}
. It suffices to show that the number of scenarios
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{B})
can be determined in time
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot B_{k}+k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot n^{2})
, where
B_{k}
is the
k^{\text{th}}
Bell number.

Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N}
be multisets of nonnegative integers, denoting the sizes of the nontrivial crowns,
M
-shaped components,
W
-shaped components, and
N
-shaped components, respectively, of
\mathcal{B}
. Thus, we assume that every element of
\mathcal{C}
is at least 2, and every element of
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{W}
is at least 1.

We will proceed in two stages. In the first stage, we will build up a table of values for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{A},\eta)
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{A},w)
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{A})
, and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{A},m,w)
with
\mathcal{A}
any sub-multiset of
\mathcal{C}
and

\eta\geq 0
,
w\geq 1
, and
m\geq 1
. Then in the second stage, we will use this final table of values along with Lemma
3.1
to compute
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
.

Stage 1.
When
\eta\geq 0
we have by Lemma
3.3
that
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\emptyset,\eta)=1
. Similarly, for
w\geq 1
, we have that
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\emptyset,w)=2^{w-1}
, which is computable in time
O(n).

Now fix
m\in[n],w\in[n]
with
m+w=i
, and suppose that for each
1\leq m^{\prime}\leq m
and each
1\leq w^{\prime}\leq w
with
m^{\prime}+w^{\prime}<i
, that we have computed
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\emptyset,m^{\prime},w^{\prime})
. Using these values, together with the fact that
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\emptyset,w)=2^{w-1}
, we may apply Proposition
3.13
to compute
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\emptyset,m,w)
. There are at most
n^{2}
pairs
(m^{\prime},w^{\prime})\in[n]\times[n]
, and so this step takes time
O(n^{2})
.

Let
u
be an integer
0<u\leq|\mathcal{C}|\leq k
. First, let
0<x\leq n
.
Suppose we have a table of values of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{A},\eta)
and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{A},w)
for all sub-multisets
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{C}
with
|\mathcal{A}|<u
and
\eta,w\leq n
and also for all sub-multisets
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{C}
with
|\mathcal{A}|=u
and
\eta,w<x
.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be a sub-multiset of
\mathcal{C}
with
|\mathcal{C}^{\prime}|=u
.
We now proceed to fill in some of our lookup table from the bottom-up, using the recurrences from the previous section.

•

We may compute
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},x)
using Proposition
3.4
. There are at most
kn
cells in our lookup table, and so this step takes time
O(kn)
.

•

We may compute
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},x)
using Proposition
3.5
. There are at most
kn
cells in our lookup table, and so this step takes time
O(kn)
.

•

We now turn to computing
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})
. To do so, we apply Proposition
3.6
using the previously computed values of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{A},w)
for all
\mathcal{A}
of size less than
u
and all
1\leq w\leq n
. This step takes time
O(k)
.

Now, let
x
and
y
be nonnegative integers with
x+y\leq n
. Suppose we have a table of values of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{A},m,w)
for all sub-multisets
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{C}
satisfying one of the following:

•

|\mathcal{A}|<u
for any nonnegative integers
m
and
w
with
m+w\leq n
, or

•

|\mathcal{A}|=u
with
m+w<x+y
.

We now turn to computing
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},x,y)
. To do so, we apply Proposition
3.13
using the previously computed values of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{A},w)
for all
1\leq w\leq n
and of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{A},m,w)
for all sub-multisets
\mathcal{A}
of
\mathcal{C}
satisfying one of the conditions above.
There are at most
kn^{2}
many such cells in our lookup table, and so filling in these cells takes time
O(kn^{2})
.

To summarize, our first stage involves building up a table of values for
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}(\mathcal{A},\eta)
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}(\mathcal{A},w)
,
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}(\mathcal{A})
, and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}(\mathcal{A},m,w)
with
\mathcal{A}
any sub-multiset of
\mathcal{C}
and
\eta
,
w
, and
m
having values as above. This first stage is computable in time
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot n^{2})
by iterating through all sub-multisets of
\mathcal{C}
in non-decreasing order of cardinality.

Stage 2.
We will use the table of values computed in Stage 1, along with Lemma
3.1
, to compute
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
as follows. First, let
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
denote the set of all nontrivial components in
\mathcal{B}
, and let
t
denote the number of trivial
N
-shaped components in
\mathcal{B}
. As per Lemma
3.1
, we consider each partition
\pi
of
\mathcal{B}
, and examine
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})
for each part
\pi_{i}
of
\pi
. By Proposition
3.2
, we can determine in time
O(k)
if
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})=0
. In such a case, the term of
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{B})
corresponding to
\pi
contributes
0
, and so we may discard
\pi
. Call a partition
\pi

permissible
if
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})>0
for all parts. Recall the parts
\pi_{i}
from Proposition
3.2
for which
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})=0
. Note that the only part
\pi_{i}
with a combination of trivial and nontrivial components for which
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})>0
is the case in which
\pi_{i}
contains precisely nontrivial crowns and a single trivial
N
-shaped component.

So the permissible partitions of
\mathcal{B}
are precisely the partitions generated by the following procedure:

•

Start with a permissible partition
\pi^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

•

For at most
t
parts
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
of
\pi^{\prime}
that each consist of only nontrivial crowns, add one of the
t
trivial
N
-shaped components from
\mathcal{B}
to
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
.

•

Each of the remaining trivial
N
-shaped components and each trivial crown from
\mathcal{B}
become a singleton part.

Let
\pi^{\prime}=(\pi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{p(\pi^{\prime})}^{\prime})
be a permissible partition of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. We say that a permissible partition
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{p(\pi)})
of
\mathcal{B}

extends

\pi^{\prime}
if there exists an injective function
f:[p(\pi^{\prime})]\to[p(\pi)]
such that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\pi_{f(i)}
for all
i\in[p(\pi^{\prime})]
. Note that by Proposition
3.2
,
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
and
\pi_{f(i)}
may only be different if
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
contains only nontrivial crowns, and
\pi_{f(i)}
contains a nontrivial
N
-shaped component.

Note that while
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
has at most
k
components,
\mathcal{B}
may in general not have bounded size. Fix a permissible partition
\pi^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
. Our goal is to compute:

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi\in\Pi(\mathcal{B})\\
\pi\text{ extends }\pi^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\binom{d(\mathcal{B})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2}),\ldots,d(\pi_{p(\pi)})}\prod_{i=1}^{p(\pi)}\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i}).

As above, let
\pi^{\prime}
be a permissible partition of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, and let
\pi
be an extension of
\pi^{\prime}
. Recall, if
\pi_{i}
consists of only one trivial component, then
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})=1
.

Let
\pi_{i}
be a part of
\pi
that contains only nontrivial crowns and a single trivial
N
-shaped component. Let
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
be obtained from
\pi_{i}
by removing the trivial
N
-shaped component. We have by equation (
2
) that
d(\pi_{i}^{\prime})=d(\pi_{i})
. Thus, if
\pi
extends
\pi^{\prime}
, we have that:

\binom{d(\mathcal{B})}{d(\pi_{1}),d(\pi_{2}),\ldots,d(\pi_{p(\pi)})}=\binom{d(\mathcal{B}^{\prime})}{d(\pi_{1}^{\prime}),d(\pi_{2}^{\prime}),\ldots,d\!\left(\pi_{p(\pi^{\prime})}^{\prime}\right)}.

Proposition
3.2
and the above procedure for constructing permissible partitions yields precisely the following cases:

•

Case 1:
Suppose that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
consists of an
N
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. In this case, we can use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}
to count the number of ways to sort this part.

•

Case 2:
Suppose that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
consists of a
W
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. In this case, we can use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{W}
to count the number of ways to sort this part.

•

Case 3:
Suppose that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
consists of an
M
-shaped component and zero or more nontrivial crowns. In this case, we can also use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{M}
to count the number of ways to sort this part.

•

Case 4:
Suppose that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
consists of an
M
-shaped component, a
W
-shaped component, and zero or more nontrivial crowns. In this case, we can use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{MW}
to count the number of ways to sort this part.

•

Case 5:
If none of Cases 1-4 hold, then we necessarily have that
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
consists of only nontrivial crowns. Since the presence of such parts implies that
\pi^{\prime}
may not necessarily extend uniquely to a permissible partition of
\mathcal{B}
, we consider all such parts
\pi_{i}^{\prime}
simultaneously in order to handle all such permissible extensions of
\pi^{\prime}
. Let
\pi_{i_{1}}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{i_{\ell}}^{\prime}
be the parts of
\pi^{\prime}
that contain only nontrivial crowns. By Proposition
3.2
, we have that for
j\in[\ell]
, we can only add at most one trivial
N
-shaped component to
\pi_{i_{j}}^{\prime}
. As the trivial
N
-shaped components and the parts of
\pi^{\prime}
are both distinguishable, there are
P(t,v)=t!/(t-v)!
ways to assign
v\leq t
trivial
N
-shaped components to
v
parts drawn from
\pi_{i_{1}}^{\prime},\ldots,\pi_{i_{\ell}}^{\prime}
. The remaining trivial
N
-shaped components each form their own singleton component in the corresponding partition
\pi
of
\mathcal{B}
extending
\pi^{\prime}
. If part
\pi_{i_{j}}^{\prime}
(
j\in[\ell]
) has a trivial
N
-shaped component added, we can use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}
to count the number of ways to sort this part. If not, we can use a known value of
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}
to count the number of ways to sort this part. The number of ways to sort each part is independent of the number of ways to assign the trivial
N
-shaped components. Thus, multiply the value returned from the lookup table by
P(t,v)
. Finally, using Lemma
3.1
, we multiply together each of the
\operatorname{\#ST}_{N}
and
\operatorname{\#ST}_{C}
values that we accessed from the lookup table. This takes time
O(k)
since there are
O(k)
parts.

We now iterate over all integers
0\leq v\leq\min(t,\ell)
, where we consider all possible assignments of
v
trivial
N
-shaped components to the parts of
\pi^{\prime}
(where the assignment is as described in the preceding paragraph). As each
\pi_{i_{j}}^{\prime}
can only receive at most one trivial
N
-shaped component, we iterate over all
v
-subsets of
[\ell]
to fully enumerate each case. As
\ell\leq k
, there are at most
2^{k}
total subsets to consider across all values of
v
, and so computing the count in this case takes time
O(k\cdot 2^{k})
.

In the first four cases, we only look at a single part at a time. Since there are
O(k)
parts, Lemma
3.1
gives a complexity of
O(k)
for each of these cases. In Case 5 we already account for Lemma
3.1
, so we have a complexity of
O(k\cdot 2^{k}
). Thus for all cases the runtime is at most
O(k\cdot 2^{k})
using the existing values in the lookup table to compute
\operatorname{\#ST}(\pi_{i})
. As we are summing over all possible partitions, we are evaluating at most the number of partitions of a set of cardinality
k
, which is the
k^{\text{th}}
Bell number
B_{k}
. This gives a complexity of
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot B_{k})
for computing
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
from the pre-established lookup tables.

So, overall, we have a complexity of
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot n^{2})
for our first stage and a complexity of
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot B_{k})
for our second stage. Thus,
\operatorname{\#MPS}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{N})
is computable in time
O(k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot B_{k}+k\cdot 2^{k}\cdot n^{2})
, as required. The result now follows.
∎

5.
Conclusion

We investigated the computational complexity of the
Pairwise Rearrangement
problem in the Single Cut-and-Join model. In particular, while
Pairwise Rearrangement
was previously shown to be
#P
-complete under polynomial-time Turing reductions
[
1
]
, we proved it is fixed-parameter tractable when parameterized by the number of components in the adjacency graph that are not trivial crowns (Theorem
1.1
). In particular, our results show that the number of nontrivial components serves as a key barrier towards efficiently enumerating and sampling minimum length sorting scenarios. We conclude with some open questions.

Question 5.1
.

In the Single Cut-and-Join model, does
Pairwise Rearrangement
belong to
FPRAS
?

Question 5.2
.

In the Double Cut-and-Join model, is
Pairwise Rearrangement

#P
-complete?

The work of Braga and Stoye
[
3
]
immediately yields a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm for
Pairwise Rearrangement
in the Double Cut-and-Join model. Their algorithm is considerably simpler than our work in this paper.

The computational complexity of the
Pairwise Rearrangement
problem in the Reversal model is a long-standing open question. In particular, it is believed that this problem is
#P
-complete. Furthermore,
Pairwise Rearrangement
has been resistant to efficient sampling; membership in
FPRAS
remains open. Thus, perhaps the lens of parameterized complexity might shed new light on this problem. The following question is a natural place to start.

Question 5.3
.

In the Reversal model, is
Pairwise Rearrangement
fixed-parameter tractable by the number of components in the adjacency graph?
