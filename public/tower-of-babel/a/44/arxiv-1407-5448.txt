On large deviation properties of Brownian motion with dry friction
Yaming Chen, Wolfram Just

Abstract
We investigate piecewise-linear stochastic models as with regards to the probability distribution of functionals of the stochastic processes, a question which occurs frequently in large deviation theory. The functionals that we are looking into in detail are related to the time a stochastic process spends at a phase space point or in a phase space region, as well as to the motion with inertia. For a Langevin equation with discontinuous drift, we extend the so-called backward Fokker-Planck technique for nonnegative support functionals to arbitrary support functionals, to derive explicit expressions for the moments of the functional. Explicit solutions for the moments and for the distribution of the so-called local time, the occupation time and the displacement are derived for the Brownian motion with dry friction, including quantitative measures to characterize deviation from Gaussian behavior in the asymptotic long time limit.

I
Introduction

The study of functionals of stochastic processes
attracts considerable interest from various points of view,
most notably in the context of large deviation theory
where the probability distribution of integrals or sums of
random variables result in a potential characterizing large
deviations from the mean and extreme events
(see
Touchette 2009
for a recent review).
Some simple functionals can characterize the time a process spends at a phase space point
or in a phase space region, or more simply such integrals may just represent
physical quantities like the position of an object
Majumdar 2005
.
Here we consider these functionals in the context of piecewise-smooth
stochastic systems. We use the motion of a Brownian particle subjected
to dry friction as an illustrative example where explicit expressions for the
distribution of functionals can be derived in a formally exact manner, and
where we can quantify the deviation from Gaussian behavior which prevails
in the asymptotic limit.

Functionals of a process
v(t)
have been investigated intensively in the past and have found numerous applications in physics
Majumdar 2005
. For instance, two of the popular functionals are the local time
\int_{0}^{t}\delta(v(\tau))d\tau
and the occupation time
\int_{0}^{t}\theta(v(\tau))d\tau
(see, e.g.,
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
), which describe how much time the process
v(t)
has visited the origin and how long it has
taken positive values in the time window
[0,t]
, respectively. Here
\delta(v)
is the Dirac delta function and
\theta(v)
is the step function with
\theta(v)=1
for
v>0
and
\theta(v)=0
for
v\leqslant 0
. In addition, the area under the process, i.e.,
\int_{0}^{t}v(\tau)d\tau
, which is referred to as displacement if
v(t)
denotes the velocity of an object, is of particular importance due to its physical meaning.

To find statistical properties of functionals is usually nontrivial even for some of the simplest functionals, such as the local time and the occupation time. For the displacement, which is equivalent to solve a stochastic differential equation with inertial term, one often restricts the study to the overdamped case, in which limit the inertial term can be neglected
Risken 1989
. To the best of our knowledge, the distributions of the displacement are only available in closed analytic form for the cases of the pure diffusion process and the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
Kramers 1940
. To compute the
distribution of a functional there are two significant achievements, i.e., the celebrated Feynman-Kac formula developed by Kac
Kac 1949
and the so-called backward Fokker-Planck technique proposed by Majumdar and Comtet
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
.
The former enables one to obtain a corresponding Schrödinger equation for the distribution of a functional and the later results in a backward Fokker-Planck equation for the distribution.
The advantage of the later over the former is that the later depends only on the initial condition of the process, no extra integral over
v
is required to obtain the distribution of the functional.

In this paper, we attempt to derive analytic results for some functionals of the Brownian motion with dry (also called solid or Coulomb) friction,

\dot{v}(t)=-\mu\sigma(v(t))-\gamma v(t)+\sqrt{D}\xi(t),

(1)

which is used to describe the velocity of a solid object of unit mass subjected to a dry friction in addition to a viscous drag and a random noise (see, e.g.,
Kawarada and Hayakawa 2004
;
Hayakawa 2005
;
de Gennes 2005
;
Touchette et al. 2010
). Here

\sigma(v)
, denoting the sign of
v
, represents the dry friction force with coefficient
\mu>0
,
\gamma\geqslant 0
denotes the viscous coefficient and
D>0
is the strength of the Gaussian white noise
\xi(t)

characterized by

\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(2)

The notation
\langle\cdots\rangle
stands for the average over all possible realizations of the noise.
For this piecewise-linear stochastic model, the transition probability distribution or propagator can be obtained by using spectral decomposition methods
Touchette et al. 2010
or Laplace transforms
Touchette et al. 2012
. Especially, when
\gamma=0
, i.e., the model (
1
) with pure dry friction (also call Brownian motion with two-valued drift), the corresponding propagator is available in closed analytic form (see, e.g.,
Karatzas and Shreve 1984
;
Karatzas and Shreve 1991
;
Touchette et al. 2010
). The weak-noise approximation of the model (
1
) has also been investigated in detail by using a path integral approach
Baule et al. 2010
;
Baule et al. 2011
;
Chen et al. 2013
. In addition, the first-passage time distributions of Eq. (
1
) can be derived by using spectral decomposition methods or solving the backward Kolmogorov equation (see
Chen and Just 2014
). The dry friction effect in stochastic settings is also observed in experiments, such as those investigated recently by Chaudhury
et al.

Chaudhury and
Mettu 2008
;
Goohpattader et al. 2009
;
Goohpattader and
Chaudhury 2010
;
Goohpattader and
Chaudhury 2012
and Gnoli
et al.

Gnoli et al. 2013a
;
Gnoli et al. 2013b
.
Since the motion of two solid objects over each other is ubiquitous in nature
Li et al. 2011
;
Baule and Sollich 2012
;
Baule and Sollich 2013
;
Weymouth et al. 2013
;
Sano and Hayakawa 2014
, the dry friction model (
1
) plays a significant role at the interface between theory and experiment. Therefore, it is interesting to have a close look at stochastic properties of functionals whose underlying dynamics is governed by this model. In particular we will focus on the local time, the occupation time and the displacement in this paper.

It is worth noting that the local time and the occupation time of the pure dry friction case [i.e.,
\gamma=0
in Eq. (
1
)] have been investigated in
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
;
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
even though the authors did not attempt to study the dry friction effect in a stochastic setting.
An integral representation of the occupation time distribution of this simple model has also been derived in
Simpson and Kuske 2012
. But the recursive relations for the moments of the local time and the occupation time have not been
given explicitly. In addition, to the best of our knowledge analytic results of the displacement for the pure dry friction case and functionals of the full model (
1
) are not available in the literature.

The rest of this paper is arranged as follows. We first extend in Sec.
II
the backward Fokker-Planck technique for a positive support functional to an arbitrary support functional by replacing the double Laplace transform used in
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
with a Fourier-Laplace transform. Rather than solving the backward equation directly, we then derive from this equation a recursive ordinary differential equation (ODE) for the moments of the functional.
In Secs.
III
and
IV
, we show that the moments of the local time and the occupation time are given explicitly by the solution of the corresponding homogeneous ODE. Then we apply the results to solve the dry friction model (
1
).
In Sec.
V
, even though a simple formula is not available for a general drift, we show that the moments of the displacement can be obtained in the Laplace space for the dry friction model (
1
). Finally, results are summarized in Sec.
VI
.

II
Remarks on general piecewise-smooth stochastic systems

Let us consider the functional

T=\int_{0}^{t}U(v(\tau))d\tau

(3)

for an integrable kernel
U(v)
. The stochastic process

v(t)
with initial condition
v(0)=v_{0}

is assumed to obey the following Langevin equation

\dot{v}(t)=-\Phi^{\prime}(v(t))+\xi(t)

(4)

with potential
\Phi(v)
, where
\xi(t)
denotes the Gaussian white noise defined by Eq. (
2
).
In what follows, we will first discuss some general properties of the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})

of the functional (
3
). In the latter parts we are then going to derive
relations for the moments
M_{n}(t,v_{0})
of
T
which will be used in the applications.

II.1
Distribution of the functional

While it may be difficult to derive closed analytic expressions for the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})

of the functional (
3
), some statements can be made on the asymptotic properties in the limit of large
t

if we assume that the potential of the Langevin equation (
4
) is stable, i.e.,
\Phi(v)\rightarrow\infty

when
v\rightarrow\pm\infty
, and that the correlation of the corresponding process decays sufficiently fast. Then a simple heuristic argument suggests (see, e.g.,
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
) that
the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})
is simply Gaussian around its mean in the long time limit.
Thus the limiting distribution is characterized only by the first and the second moments,

p^{\text{asym}}(T,t,v_{0})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{Var}(T)}}\exp\left[-\frac{(T-M_{1}(t,v_{0}))^{2}}{2\mathrm{Var}(T)}\right],

(5)

where
M_{1}(t,v_{0})
and
\mathrm{Var}(T)
stand for the first moment and the variance of
T
, respectively.
As stated in
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
, the argument is as follows: At large time
t
, assuming the process

v(t)
to be mixing, the corresponding propagator
p(v,t|v_{0},0)
tends to the stationary distribution

\exp[-\Phi(v)]/Z
, where
Z
is a normalization factor. Hence, at time increments which exceed
the correlation time of the system, the random variables
U(v(\tau))-\langle U(v(\tau))\rangle
are only weakly
correlated. Therefore, from the definition (
3
)
we expect a central limit theorem to hold in the limit of
t
being much larger than the relaxation time
and the correlation time of the two aforementioned variables, resulting finally in a Gaussian distribution for the
functional
T
around its mean. Hence, in order to determine the limiting distribution of
T

around its mean we only need to obtain the first and the second moments, which can be attained by using the results
stated in the following subsections. Our arguments will be able to predict higher-order cumulants as well
so that we provide a quantitative tool to benchmark the aforementioned reasoning.

For the subsequent investigations we will need the boundary conditions of the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})

in the limit when
v_{0}
tends to infinity. These boundary conditions depend on the particular
function
U(v)
which enters the functional
T
. They can be derived from the observation
that a process starting at infinity cannot cross the origin in finite time. For simplicity let us discuss
two particular choices, namely
U(v)=\delta(v)
and
U(v)=\theta(v)
, corresponding to the so-called
local time

T_{\text{loc}}=\int_{0}^{t}\delta(v(\tau))d\tau

(6)

which measures the time that the process visits the origin, and the so-called occupation time

T_{\text{occ}}=\int_{0}^{t}\theta(v(\tau))d\tau

(7)

which measures the time that the process takes positive values.
Hence for the distribution of the local time we have

p_{\text{loc}}(T,t,v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty)=\delta(T),

(8)

and for the occupation time the boundary condition reads

\displaystyle p_{\text{occ}}(T,t,v_{0}\rightarrow\infty)=\delta(T-t),

\displaystyle p_{\text{occ}}(T,t,v_{0}\rightarrow-\infty)=\delta(T).

(9)

The third quantity we are going to discuss in some detail will be the displacement of the particle which
can also be written as a functional with the choice
U(v)=v
, i.e.,

T_{\text{dis}}=\int_{0}^{t}v(\tau)d\tau.

(10)

For the displacement, the argument of the boundary condition is slightly more subtle. We expect that for
|v_{0}|\gg t
the
velocity of the particle does not change considerably in the time window
[0,t]
and that the displacement should be at
the scale of
v_{0}t
. Therefore, the corresponding boundary condition can be formally approximated as

p_{\text{dis}}(T,t,v_{0})\sim\delta(T-v_{0}t)

in the asymptotic limit
v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Such a condition will be used later to obtain the boundary condition for the moments of the displacement.

II.2
Moments of the functional

We start to derive one of the main results by assuming the potential
\Phi(v)
to be continuous and to be smooth
everywhere apart from
v=0
where we allow the potential to be not differentiable, so that the deterministic part
of the Langevin equation (
4
) has a discontinuity at
v=0
. More general cases of
piecewise-smooth systems could be considered with ease but the discussion of this special case will be sufficient
to uncover the general structure. In addition, our particular choice also contains the dry friction model (
1
).
For a nonnegative support functional it has been shown by Majumdar and Comtet
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
that the
double Laplace transform of the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})
satisfies a backward equation (see Eq. (4) in
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
). In this paper we do not confine the discussion to nonnegative functionals, i.e.,
T
can be negative. Hence, instead of using the double Laplace transform we have to consider the Fourier-Laplace transform of the distribution
p(T,t,v_{0})
, i.e.,

r(k,s,v_{0})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ikT}dT\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}p(T,t,v_{0})dt.

(11)

Similarly, using the method stated in
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
(see also
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
)
it is straightforward to show that
r(k,s,v_{0})
satisfies the following backward equation

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{v_{0}}r(k,s,v_{0})-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{0})\partial_{v_{0}}r(k,s,v_{0})-[ikU(v_{0})+s]r(k,s,v_{0})+1=0.

(12)

Let us assume that the
n
-th moment
M_{n}(t,v_{0})
of
T
exists so that the moments of
T
in the
Laplace space are given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-st}M_{n}(t,v_{0})dt=\left.i^{n}\partial^{n}_{k}r(k,s,v_{0})\right|_{k=0}

\displaystyle\mbox{for }n\geqslant 1.

(13)

Thus by acting the operator
i^{n}\partial^{n}_{k}
on Eq. (
12
) and setting
k=0
,
we obtain a hierarchy of equations for
\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})
,

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{0})\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})-s\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})=-nU(v_{0})\widetilde{M}_{n-1}(s,v_{0}),\qquad\widetilde{M}_{0}(s,v_{0})=r(0,s,v_{0})=1/s,

(14)

which is easier to deal with than solving Eq. (
12
).

II.3
Boundary conditions

In order to solve Eq. (
14
), it is essential to know the boundary conditions for the moments in the
limit
v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty
. Using Eq. (
8
) and the uniform convergence of the Laplace transform
for
\mbox{Re}(s)>0
it follows that the moments of the local time obey

\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty)=0\qquad\mbox{for }\mbox{Re}(s)>0\mbox{ and }n\geqslant 1,

(15)

whereas a similar argument and Eq. (
9
) yield for the moments of the occupation time that

\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{n}(s,v_{0}\rightarrow\infty)=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\qquad\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{n}(s,v_{0}\rightarrow-\infty)=0

(16)

for
\mbox{Re}(s)>0
and
n\geqslant 1
.
For the displacement, the reasoning presented at the end of subsection

II.1
suggests that the
n
-th moment of the displacement as a function of
v_{0}
should be bounded by a polynomial of order
n
in
v_{0}
. We will see later that this condition is sufficient
to determine the solution of the dry friction model (
1
).

II.4
Matching conditions

Since we are here concerned with a piecewise-smooth potential
\Phi(v)
, say with a kink at
v=0
,
we have to solve Eq. (
14
) for
v_{0}>0
and
v_{0}<0
, respectively.
Even for a piecewise-smooth potential
\Phi(v)
the solution of Eq. (
14
)
has to be at least continuous, so that we require continuity of the moments
at
v_{0}=0
, resulting in the first matching condition

\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,0-)=\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,0+).

(17)

As for the first derivative we may obtain a matching condition by integrating
Eq. (
14
) across the discontinuity at
v_{0}=0
. Because of the continuity of the moments the right hand side will contribute only if the kernel
U(v)
has a
\delta
singularity at

v=0
. Thus for the local time (
6
) the matching condition reads

\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,0+)-\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,0-)=-n\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n-1}(s,0),

(18)

whereas for any other kernel, e.g., for the occupation time and for the displacement the matching condition just
simplifies to

\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}/\text{dis}}_{n}(s,0+)=\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}/\text{dis}}_{n}(s,0-).

(19)

Here we have used the shorthand notation
\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,0\pm)
to
denote
\partial_{v_{0}}\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})|_{v_{0}\rightarrow 0\pm}
.

II.5
Structure of the solution

Let us briefly discuss how we are going to approach the analytic solution of the hierarchy
(
14
) for a general potential
\Phi(v)
with nonanalyticity at
v=0
. Suppose
that the appropriate fundamental piecewise-smooth solution
\varphi(s,v_{0})

of the corresponding homogeneous ODE of Eq. (
14
) is known, which vanishes for
\mbox{Re}(s)>0

when
v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty
. The solution obeys

\left[\partial_{v_{0}}^{2}-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{0})\partial_{v_{0}}-s\right]\varphi(s,v_{0})=0

(20)

for
v_{0}\neq 0
and we assume
\varphi(s,v_{0})
to be continuous at
v_{0}=0
.
To start with, we consider the hierarchy (
14
) separately on the two domains
v_{0}>0
and

v_{0}<0
. Given
\widetilde{M}_{0}(s,v_{0})=1/s
[see Eq. (
14
)] we denote by
\widetilde{M}^{p}_{1}(s,v_{0})
a particular solution
of the inhomogeneous differential equation (for
n=1
) which fulfils the boundary condition at
infinity. By adding a multiple of the homogeneous solution
\varphi(s,v_{0})
we then obtain
two branches with two constants of integration which are adjusted according to the
matching conditions (see subsection
II.4
). Hence, at each level of
the hierarchy we determine a particular solution
\widetilde{M}^{p}_{n}(s,v_{0})
and construct the general
solution, which already satisfies the boundary condition at infinity (see subsection
II.3
), through

\widetilde{M}_{n}(s,v_{0})=\widetilde{M}_{n}^{p}(s,v_{0})+\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C^{+}_{n}\varphi(s,v_{0}),&v_{0}>0,\\
C^{-}_{n}\varphi(s,v_{0}),&v_{0}<0.\end{array}\right.

(21)

Finally we compute the coefficients
C^{\pm}_{n}
with the help of the matching conditions at
v_{0}=0

(see subsection
II.4
).
The relevance of the trivial Eq. (
21
) comes from the observation that
we just have to deal with the matching conditions caused by the nonanalyticities of the potential.
Furthermore, in most of the cases considered here, we will be able to express the particular solutions

\widetilde{M}^{p}_{n}(s,v_{0})
in terms of the homogeneous solution
\varphi(s,v_{0})
as well, so that the entire
structure just requires solving Eq. (
20
).

We may not be able to do the inverse Laplace transform for the moments analytically. However, in the long time limit
the behavior of the moments is dominated by the singular terms of the Laplace transform
and those are often not too difficult to evaluate. We can also resort to numerical Laplace inversion, such as
the so-called Talbot method
Abate and Valkó 2004
, which usually gives accurate results even at a very short time.

In the next three sections, we will apply the formula (
21
) to
the local time, the occupation time, and the displacement. The analysis of the local time
can be largely done for general potential while we are going to derive explicit formulas for
the other two observables for the dry friction model (
1
) without and with viscous friction, respectively.

III
Local time

To begin with we first consider the local time problem, i.e., the setup with kernel
U(v)=\delta(v)
. In this case
it is possible to derive closed form expressions for a general potential
\Phi(v)
with discontinuity at
v=0
.
We will use this simple case to illustrate our approach.

III.1
General drift

As the matching condition (
18
) takes care of the singularities at
v=0
we only need to
consider the hierarchy (
14
) for nonvanishing arguments, i.e., we can confine our study to the homogeneous equation
(
20
). Hence in Eq. (
21
) the particular solution vanishes and the moments are expressed
in terms of the fundamental solution
\varphi(s,v_{0})
. The coefficients follow immediately
from the matching conditions at
v_{0}=0
[see Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
)] and we arrive at
the recursive relation

\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0})=\frac{n\varphi(s,v_{0})\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n-1}(s,0)}{\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0-)-\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0+)}\qquad\mbox{for }n\geqslant 1.

(22)

Therefore, the higher moments are determined by the first moment via

\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0})=n!\big[s\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,0)\big]^{n-1}\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,v_{0}),

(23)

where

\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,v_{0})=\frac{\varphi(s,v_{0})/s}{\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0-)-\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0+)}.

(24)

This simple relation enables one to obtain the distribution of the local time explicitly in the Fourier-Laplace space, which reads [see Eq. (
11
)]

\displaystyle r(k,s,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-ik)^{n}}{n!}\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0})

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,v_{0})}{s\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,0)}\frac{1}{1+iks\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,0)}.

Then inverting the Fourier transform with respect to
k
yields the Laplace transform of the
distribution for the local time

\widetilde{p}_{\text{loc}}(T,s,v_{0})=\frac{\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,v_{0})}{s^{2}[\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,0)]^{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{T}{s\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{1}(s,0)}\right)

(26)

for
T\geqslant 0
, which indicates that the distribution is fully determined by the Laplace transform of
its first moment. The result (
26
) extends that obtained in
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
to be valid
for arbitrary initial condition
v_{0}
[see Eq. (30) therein]. In general, we may not be able to do the inverse
Laplace transform for Eq. (
26
) analytically. However, we can resort to numerical inversion
(e.g., the Talbot method
Abate and Valkó 2004
) to produce the distribution

p_{\text{loc}}(T,t,v_{0})
numerically, especially for short time
t
.

III.2
Pure dry friction

In order to make further progress and to illustrate the effectiveness of the approach
let us consider the particular model (
1
) with only dry friction (
\gamma=0
).
In this case, we let
\mu=D=1
. Other values can be covered by using the appropriate rescaling

x=\mu v/D,\qquad\tau=\mu^{2}t/D.

(27)

Hence the pure dry friction case can be written in the form (
4
)
with a stable potential

\Phi(v)=|v|.

(28)

The appropriate fundamental solution of the homogeneous ODE (
20
) corresponding to Eq. (
14
) is

\varphi(s,v_{0})=e^{(1-\sqrt{1+4s})|v_{0}|/2},

(29)

which vanishes for
\mbox{Re}(s)>0
when
v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty
.
Then, from Eqs. (
26
) and (
24
) we obtain
the local time distribution in the Laplace domain,

\widetilde{p}_{\text{loc}}(T,s,v_{0})=\frac{\sqrt{1+4s}-1}{s}e^{-(\sqrt{1+4s}-1)(T+|v_{0}|/2)}

(30)

for
T\geqslant 0
. In this case we can even perform the inverse Laplace transform explicitly.
From the table of Laplace transforms in
Abramowitz and Stegun 1968
we have the identity

\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left(\frac{e^{-k\sqrt{\alpha}}}{b+\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)=\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4t)}}{\sqrt{\pi t}}-be^{bk+b^{2}t}\mathrm{erfc}\left(b\sqrt{t}+\frac{k}{2\sqrt{t}}\right),

(31)

where
\mathrm{erfc}(z)
is the complementary error function.
Hence, using the shifting property of Laplace transform, we obtain from Eq. (
30
) the inverse Laplace transform,
i.e, the local time distribution

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003ptp_{\text{loc}}(T,t,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-(2T+|v_{0}|-t)^{2}/(4t)}

(32)

\displaystyle-e^{2T+|v_{0}|}\mathrm{erfc}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{2}+\frac{2T+|v_{0}|}{2\sqrt{t}}\right)

for
T\geqslant 0
, which generalizes the result in
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
to be valid for any
v_{0}
[see Eq. (A4) therein].

In most of cases the inverse Laplace transform cannot be performed analytically. Thus let us discuss
how the previous analysis based on moments [see, e.g., Eq. (
5
)] matches with the exact
expression (
32
). We will also use the opportunity to illustrate the method to derive
asymptotic results, which will be used later in cases where no exact distribution function is available.
Using Eqs. (
23
), (
24
) and (
29
) the Laplace transform of the moments of the local
time are explicitly given
by

\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0})=\frac{n!}{4^{n}s^{n+1}}(\sqrt{1+4s}+1)^{n}e^{(1-\sqrt{1+4s})|v_{0}|/2}.

(33)

The expressions have a pole at
s=0
and a branch cut on the real axis for
s<-1/4
.
As for the inverse transform we can use the standard asymptotic result (see, e.g.,
Murray 1974
) that the
time dependence of
M_{n}^{\text{loc}}(t,v_{0})
is determined by the pole at
s=0
up to
contributions of
O(e^{-(1/4-o)t})
. Here the notation
o
stands for an arbitrary small positive correction to
1/4
. This correction is due to a power-law correction to the leading exponential behavior. The same notation will be used in the rest of this paper. Hence the moments in the asymptotic long time
limit read

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003ptM^{\text{loc}}_{n}(t,v_{0})

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4^{-n}\left.\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}\left[(\sqrt{1+4s}+1)^{n}e^{(1-\sqrt{1+4s})|v_{0}|/2+st}\right]\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}).

In particular, for the first two moments we have the simple expressions

\displaystyle M^{\text{loc}}_{1}(t,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t}{2}+\frac{1-|v_{0}|}{2}+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

(35)

\displaystyle M^{\text{loc}}_{2}(t,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{4}+\frac{(2-|v_{0}|)t}{2}+\frac{v_{0}^{2}-2|v_{0}|-2}{4}

(36)

\displaystyle+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

which result in the variance

\text{Var}(T_{\text{loc}})=\frac{t}{2}-\frac{3}{4}+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}).

(37)

Finally the limiting distribution (
5
) reads

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006ptp_{\text{loc}}^{\text{asym}}(T,t,v_{0})

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(t-3/2)}}\exp\left[-\frac{(2T-t+|v_{0}|-1)^{2}}{4t-6}\right]

\displaystyle+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}).

As seen in Fig.
1
, this limiting distribution in leading order matches well
with the exact analytic expression (
32
) at large time
t
.

From the perspective of large deviation results it is of interest to look at the
asymptotic properties of the higher order
cumulamnts as well. Of course they can be worked out rather straightforwardly from the expression
(
34
). For instance, for the third and the fourth cumulants we obtain

\displaystyle K^{\text{loc}}_{3}=7/4+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

\displaystyle K^{\text{loc}}_{4}=-45/8+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

(39)

a result which shows that both are negligible compared to the first two moments
[see Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
)] in the asymptotic limit of large
t
.
Hence this simple indicator confirms that the large deviation
function of the local time, defined by
p_{\text{loc}}(t\vartheta,t,v_{0})\sim\exp(-t\phi(\vartheta))

is quadratic (see also Fig.
1
), a feature which of course can be confirmed as well
if one employs the exact analytic expression of the distribution function (
32
) and evaluates
t^{-1}\ln p_{\text{loc}}(t\vartheta,t,v_{0})

in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
(see also
Sabhapandit et al. 2006
).

III.3
Dry and viscous friction

Let us now add viscous friction to the setup discussed in the previous subsection. Intuitively one probably would not
expect any major change as a linear viscous force is unlikely to have any impact on the predominantly Gaussian
behavior found previously.
For the full force case (
1
), we let
\gamma=D=1
without loss of generality.
Other values are covered by the rescaling

x=(\gamma/D)^{1/2}v,\qquad\tau=\gamma t.

(40)

Hence the full model can be written in the form (
4
) with a stable potential

\Phi(v)=(|v|+\mu)^{2}/2.

(41)

The appropriate fundamental solution of the homogeneous ODE (
20
) is given by

\displaystyle\varphi(s,v_{0})=e^{(|v_{0}|+\mu)^{2}/4}D_{-s}(|v_{0}|+\mu),

(42)

which vanishes for
\mbox{Re}(s)>0
in the limit
v_{0}\rightarrow\pm\infty
.
Here we use the symbol
D_{\nu}(z)
to denote the parabolic cylinder functions
Buchholz 1969
.

As already stated in the previous subsection we now employ the general expression (
21
)
without any particular inhomogeneous solution and use the matching conditions (
17
) and (
18
) to compute
the Laplace transform of the moments. In fact the general expressions
Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
) immediately yield

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\text{loc}}_{n}(s,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n!}{2^{n}s^{n+1}}\left(\frac{D_{-s}(\mu)}{D_{-s-1}(\mu)}\right)^{n-1}

(43)

\displaystyle\times\frac{e^{(|v_{0}|+\mu)^{2}/4}D_{-s}(|v_{0}|+\mu)}{e^{\mu^{2}/4}D_{-s-1}(\mu)}.

For real argument
z
the parabolic cylinder function fulfils
D_{\nu}(z)\neq 0
for any
\nu
with nonvanishing imaginary part and
D_{\nu}(z)>0
for any negative real value of
\nu
. Hence, we conclude that all the singularities of Eq. (
43
) lie on the
nonpositive real axis as
\mu>0
.
In addition all these singularities are poles. The largest negative pole which determines
the asymptotic properties of the moments is given by

s_{0}=\max\{s:D_{-s-1}(\mu)=0\}<-1.

(44)

The dependence of this value on the dry friction coefficient
\mu
is displayed in Fig.
2
and turns out
to be a monotonically decreasing function. It is now again straightforward to write down the leading order asymptotic expansion
of the inverse Laplace transform, i.e., of the time dependent moment, resulting in

\displaystyle M^{\text{loc}}_{n}(t,v_{0})=\left.\frac{e^{(|v_{0}|+\mu)^{2}/4}}{2^{n}e^{\mu^{2}/4}}\frac{d^{n}}{ds^{n}}\left[\left(\frac{D_{-s}(\mu)}{D_{-s-1}(\mu)}\right)^{n-1}\frac{D_{-s}(|v_{0}|+\mu)}{D_{-s-1}(\mu)}e^{st}\right]\right|_{s=0}+O(e^{(s_{0}+o)t}),

(45)

where
s_{0}+o<0
.
In particular the leading terms of the the first two moments in the long time limit are given explicitly as follows,

\displaystyle M^{\text{loc}}_{1}(t,v_{0})=\frac{D_{0}(\mu)t}{2D_{-1}(\mu)}+\frac{D_{0}(\mu)}{2D_{-1}(\mu)}\left(\frac{D_{-1}^{(1,0)}(\mu)}{D_{-1}(\mu)}-\frac{D_{0}^{(1,0)}(\mu+|v_{0}|)}{D_{0}(\mu+|v_{0}|)}\right)+O(e^{(s_{0}+o)t}),

(46)

\displaystyle M^{\text{loc}}_{2}(t,v_{0})=\frac{D_{0}^{2}(\mu)t^{2}}{4D_{-1}^{2}(\mu)}+\frac{D_{0}^{2}(\mu)}{2D_{-1}^{2}(\mu)}\left(2\frac{D_{-1}^{(1,0)}(\mu)}{D_{-1}(\mu)}-\frac{D_{0}^{(1,0)}(\mu)}{D_{0}(\mu)}-\frac{D_{0}^{(1,0)}(\mu+|v_{0}|)}{D_{0}(\mu+|v_{0}|)}\right)t+O(1).

(47)

Here the symbol
D^{(1,0)}_{\Lambda}(z)
denotes the derivative of the parabolic cylinder function with
respect to its index, i.e.,
\left.\partial_{\nu}D_{\nu}(z)\right|_{\nu=\Lambda}
.
Thus we obtain for the leading order term of the variance the expression

\mathrm{Var}(T_{\text{loc}})=\frac{D_{0}^{2}(\mu)}{2D_{-1}^{2}(\mu)}\left(\frac{D_{-1}^{(1,0)}(\mu)}{D_{-1}(\mu)}-\frac{D_{0}^{(1,0)}(\mu)}{D_{0}(\mu)}\right)t+O(1).

(48)

Here we have stated the results for the second moment and the variance up to
O(1)
as the constant term is too cumbersome to be written down explicitly. Of course, including the constant term the expression is again correct up to
O(e^{(s_{0}+o)t})
.
As shown in Fig.
3
, the corresponding asymptotic distribution (
5
) matches well with the numerical evaluation of
Eq. (
26
) for times which are sufficiently large.

To quantify the accuracy of the asymptotic Gaussian distribution we can now check as well for the
size of the higher order cumulants. From Eq. (
43
) we can easily confirm that to leading order
the third and the fourth cumulants satisfy

K_{3}^{\text{loc}}=c_{3}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)t+O(1),\qquad K_{4}^{\text{loc}}=c_{4}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)t+O(1),

(49)

where both coefficients
c_{3}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)
and
c_{4}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)
are independent of the initial value
v_{0}
.
Intuitively, we expect that we recover the result of the previous subsection [see Eq. (
39
)]
if the dry friction term dominates the viscous friction force. That means that
the coefficients
c_{3}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)
and
c_{4}^{\text{loc}}(\mu)
in Eq. (
49
) which govern the linear time dependence
of the cumulants decay to zero for large
\mu
[see also Eq. (
39
)]. Indeed, numerical evaluation
of the coefficients confirms what one expect intuitively (see Fig.

). It is however remarkable and
somehow counterintuitive that the addition of a viscous force results in large cumulants as compared to the pure dry friction case [see Eq.
39
)]. The linear increase of the cumulants with time indicates that the entirely
Gaussian behavior of large fluctuations of the pure dry friction model, i.e., a strictly quadratic large deviation function,
is modified by non-Gaussian contributions due to viscous damping. In fact, Fig.
4
shows
a resonance like phenomenon, where the deviations from Gaussian behavior become maximal at some intermediate value
of
\mu
. The fourth order cumulant becomes maximal in absolute value if
the relative strength of dry and viscous friction attains a certain optimal value.

IV
Occupation time

As we have seen in the previous section, largely closed analytic expressions can be derived for the
distribution function of the local time. To check the robustness of the conclusions against
the change of the observable we now consider the occupation time (
7
). We
will see that we can again provide a largely analytic account if we still follow the
strategy outlined in the previous section.

IV.1
General drift

Let us first discuss to which extent we can derive properties of the occupation time distribution
and its moments without specifying the drift. As pointed out in Eq. (
21
) the construction
of particular solutions of the inhomogeneous moment hierarchy (
18
) is the key, if we assume
a fundamental solution of the homogeneous equation (
20
) to be given.

We first observe that the kernel
U(v)=\theta(v)
vanishes for
v<0
, so that for a particular solution
of the moment hierarchy (
18
), we may assume
\widetilde{M}_{n}^{p}(s,v_{0})=0
for
v_{0}<0
.
Furthermore, the first equation of the hierarchy (
n=1
) can be solved easily to yield

\widetilde{M}_{1}^{p}(s,v_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}1/s^{2}&&\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,\\
0&&\mbox{for }v_{0}<0.\end{array}\right.

(50)

Now to compute the higher order contributions
\widetilde{M}_{n}^{p}(s,v_{0})
recursively
we observe that the
m
-th order derivative of the
homogeneous equation (
20
) with respect to
s
just resembles the equations of the moment hierarchy, i.e.,

\left[\partial_{v_{0}}^{2}-\Phi^{\prime}(v_{0})\partial_{v_{0}}-s\right]\partial_{s}^{m}\varphi(s,v_{0})=m\partial_{s}^{m-1}\varphi(s,v_{0}).

(51)

Hence
\widetilde{M}_{n}^{p}(s,v_{0})
can be expressed as a linear combination of partial derivatives of the
fundamental solution. Using such an inductive argument and Eq. (
21
) we finally arrive at the expression for the Laplace transform of the
n
-th moment of the occupation time,

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{n}(s,v_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}n!/s^{n+1}+\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{m}{n\choose m}C^{+}_{n-m}\partial^{m}_{s}\varphi(s,v_{0})&&\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,\\
C^{-}_{n}\varphi(s,v_{0})&&\mbox{for }v_{0}<0.\end{array}\right.

The coefficients
C^{+}_{n}
and
C^{-}_{n}

are determined by the matching condition at
v_{0}=0
[see Eqs. (
17
) and (
19
)].
Thus, we obtain the explicit formulas

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{1}=\frac{\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0\mp)}{s^{2}\varphi(s,0)[\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0+)-\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0-)]},

(55)

and for
n>1

\displaystyle C^{\pm}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}(-1)^{m}{n\choose m}C^{+}_{n-m}[\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0\mp)\partial^{m}_{s}\varphi(s,0)

(56)

\displaystyle-\varphi(s,0)\partial^{m}_{s}\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0+)]+\frac{n!\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0\mp)}{s^{n+1}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\Big/\big\{\varphi(s,0)[\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0+)-\partial_{v_{0}}\varphi(s,0-)]\big\}.

While such expressions are certainly more involved than those for the local time problem we can still express the entire moment problem in terms of the fundamental solution of the homogeneous
equation (
20
).

IV.2
Pure dry friction

For the model with only dry friction, i.e., with the the potential (
28
), the fundamental solution of the
corresponding homogeneous system is given by Eq. (
29
). Hence, it is straightforward to obtain the moments of the occupation time
in the Laplace space by using the formula (
IV.1
). The first two moments are given explicitly by

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{1}(s,v_{0})=\frac{1}{2s^{2}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2-e^{-(\sqrt{1+4s}-1)v_{0}/2}&&\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,\\
e^{(\sqrt{1+4s}-1)v_{0}/2}&&\mbox{for }v_{0}<0,\end{array}\right.

\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{2}(s,v_{0})=\frac{1}{4s^{3}}\left\{\begin{split}&8-\frac{5\sqrt{4a+1}+4v_{0}s+1}{\sqrt{4s+1}}e^{-\left(\sqrt{4s+1}-1\right)v_{0}/2}&&\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,\\
&\frac{3\sqrt{4s+1}-1}{\sqrt{4s+1}}e^{\left(\sqrt{4s+1}-1\right)v_{0}/2}&&\mbox{for }v_{0}<0.\end{split}\right.

(59)

The analytic structure of these expressions is solely determined by the analytic structure of the fundamental solution and
thus coincides with what we found for the local time, namely a pole at
s=0
and a branch cut for
s<-1/4
. Thus the
asymptotic expansion of the inverse Laplace transform results in the long time behavior of the moments,

\displaystyle M^{\text{occ}}_{1}(t,v_{0})=\frac{t}{2}+\frac{v_{0}}{2}+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

(60)

\displaystyle M^{\text{occ}}_{2}(t,v_{0})=\frac{t^{2}}{4}+\frac{1+v_{0}}{2}t+\frac{v_{0}^{2}-6}{4}+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}).

(61)

The variance of the occupation time is easily computed as

\text{Var}(T_{\text{occ}})=\frac{t}{2}-\frac{3}{2}+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}),

(62)

and the limiting distribution (
5
) reads

\displaystyle p_{\text{occ}}^{\text{asym}}(T,t,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(t-3)}}\exp\left[-\frac{(2T-t-v_{0})^{2}}{4t-12}\right]

(63)

\displaystyle+O(e^{-(1/4-o)t}).

At large time, this limiting distribution matches well with the Monte Carlo simulation of the corresponding Langevin equation by
using the Euler-Maruyama scheme
Kloeden and Platen 1992
(see Fig.
5
). As shown in
Chen et al. 2013
, the application of this scheme is stable for piecewise-smooth stochastic differential equations and only requires that we choose the integration time-step small enough.

The inclusion of a viscous force results in the Langevin equation (

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{1}(s,v_{0})=\frac{1}{2s^{2}D_{-s}(\mu)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}2D_{-s}(\mu)-e^{v_{0}^{2}/4+\mu v_{0}/2}D_{-s}(\mu+v_{0})&&\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,\\

\displaystyle=

(68)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{s^{3}}-\frac{e^{v_{0}^{2}/4+\mu v_{0}/2}}{s^{2}D_{-s}(\mu)}D_{-s}^{(1,0)}(\mu+v_{0})\quad\mbox{for }v_{0}>0,

\displaystyle\widetilde{M}^{\text{occ}}_{2}(s,v_{0})=\frac{e^{v_{0}^{2}/4-\mu v_{0}/2}D_{-s}(\mu-v_{0})}{2s^{3}D_{-s-1}(\mu)D_{-s}^{2}(\mu)}\Big\{D_{-s}(\mu)\Big[sD^{(1,0)}_{-s-1}(\mu)+D_{-s-1}(\mu)\Big]-sD_{-s-1}(\mu)D^{(1,0)}_{-s}(\mu)\Big\}\quad\mbox{for }v_{0}<0.

(69)

analyze the singularities of these expressions. Poles appear at
s=0
and at values where the
parabolic cylinder functions vanish. It is a rather obvious consequence of the properties

) that all these singularities are poles

has been
introduced in Eq. (
45
) (see also Fig.

Displacement

Finally we investigate the distribution of the displacement of the object, which as well can be expressed
in terms of the functional (

) with the choice
U(v)=v
.
In this case, particular solutions
\widetilde{M}_{n}^{p}(s,v_{0})
of the corresponding inhomogeneous ODE
(
14

23
) or (
IV.1
)
is not available. Fortunately, for the dry friction model (
1
) we will see in the following subsections
that particular solutions can be constructed rather straightforwardly for the pure dry friction case and the full model,
respectively.

V.1
Pure dry friction

Let us consider Eq. (
14
) with the potential (
28
) and
U(v)=v
for
n=1
. On either of the domain

v_{0}>0
or
v_{0}<0
, it is obvious that a particular polynomial solution is given by

\widetilde{M}_{1}^{p}(s,v_{0})=\frac{sv_{0}-\sigma(v_{0})}{s^{3}}.

(74)

Hence, using Eq. (
29
) for the homogeneous solution we obtain from Eq. (
21
)
the expression for the first moment. The two constants are then determined by the two matching conditions
(
17
) and (
19
) so that we arrive at

\displaystyle\hskip-20.00003pt\widetilde{M}^{\text{dis}}_{1}(s,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{sv_{0}-\sigma(v_{0})}{s^{3}}+\frac{\sigma(v_{0})}{s^{3}}\varphi(s,v_{0})

(75)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{sv_{0}-\sigma(v_{0})}{s^{3}}+\frac{\sigma(v_{0})}{s^{3}}e^{(1-\sqrt{1+4s})|v_{0}|/2}.

For the moment of the next order we have to determine a particular solution of the inhomogeneous equation
(
14
) for
n=2
, i.e., where the right hand side is essentially given by Eq. (
75
). While that
is certainly a straightforward task we can uncover the underlying algebraic structure by reducing
all the manipulations to the properties of the fundamental solution (
29
).
Since

\displaystyle|v_{0}|\varphi(s,v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|v_{0}|e^{(1-\sqrt{1+4s})|v_{0}|/2}

VI
Conclusion

Large deviation properties of functionals, here integrals over a stochastic process,
have been analyzed for piecewise-smooth stochastic models. We have, in particular, considered the
Brownian motion with dry and viscous friction as an illustrative example for our approach,
where we have derived explicit results for three particular choices of a functional, the local time
which measures the crossing of the discontinuity, the occupation time measuring the
direction of movement of an object, and the spatial displacement.

To cope with general underlying stochastic models, we have extended the backward Fokker-Planck
technique developed in
Majumdar and Comtet 2002
to provide analytic solutions
for a general support functional. For a generic Langevin equation, the hierarchy of
differential equations for the moments of functionals have been derived and solutions have
been provided for the local time and the occupation time in terms of the solution of the
corresponding homogeneous equation.

Our study of a piecewise-linear model, i.e., the Brownian motion with pure dry friction serves as a simple
case study. For all three functionals the Laplace transform of the moments can be calculated analytically.
From such results asymptotic properties in the time domain are easily extracted. In principle one could
even invert the transform analytically as in this case the propagator of the corresponding
Fokker-Planck equation is known in closed analytic form (see, e.g.,
Touchette et al. 2010
). The results confirm that the distribution
of the functionals become strictly Gaussian in the asymptotic limit, for the local and for the occupation time, as
also clearly demonstrated by comparing the analytic results with Monte Carlo simulations. Higher order
cumulants contribute for the displacement in the long time limit, so that a strictly quadratic large deviation
function gets modified by these terms.

The inclusion of an additional viscous force introduces, somehow counterintuitively, non-Gaussian
behavior for the large deviation properties of the local time and the occupation time.
In addition, our analytic results confirm the numerical analysis provided in

Menzel and
Goldenfeld 2011
by solving the corresponding Fokker-Planck equation numerically.
For that purpose we need to specialize our general
expressions to the case
v_{0}=0
and translate our expressions, given in nondimensional units,
to the original scale (
1
) via (see
Touchette et al. 2010
)

\mu\rightarrow\mu/(\gamma D)^{1/2},\quad M_{2}(t,v_{0})\rightarrow DM_{2}(\gamma t,(\gamma/D)^{1/2}v_{0})/\gamma.

(94)

In Fig.
11
it is clearly visible that our analytic expressions confirm
the numerical results presented in
Menzel and
Goldenfeld 2011
(see Figs. 5 and 9 therein)
for the second moment and the distribution. As we have been able to work out higher order cumulants
as well we can even quantify deviations from the predominantly
observed Gaussian behavior.
