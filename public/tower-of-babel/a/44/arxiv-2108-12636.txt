Concentration inequalities for some negatively dependent binary random variables Thanks: Research partially supported by the National Science Centre, Poland, via the Sonata Bis grant no. 2015/18/E/ST1/00214 (RA) and the Preludium grant no. 2020/37/N/ST1/02667 (BP)
Radosław Adamczak, Bartłomiej Polaczyk

Abstract
. We investigate concentration properties of functions of random vectors with values in the discrete cube, satisfying the stochastic covering property (SCP) or the strong Rayleigh property (SRP). Our result for SCP measures include subgaussian inequalities of bounded-difference type extending classical results by Pemantle and Peres and their counterparts for matrix-valued setting strengthening recent estimates by Aoun, Banna and Youssef. Under a stronger assumption of the SRP we obtain Bernstein-type inequalities for matrix-valued functions, generalizing recent bounds for linear combinations of positive definite matrices due to Kyng and Song. We also treat in detail the special case of independent Bernoulli random variables conditioned on their sum for which we obtain strengthened estimates, deriving in particular modified log-Sobolev inequalities, Talagrand’s convex distance inequality and, as corollaries, concentration results for convex functions and polynomials, as well as improved estimates for matrix-valued functions. These results generalize inequalities for the uniform measure on slices of the discrete cube, studied extensively by many authors. Our approach is based on recent results by Hermon and Salez and a general framework involving modified log-Sobolev inequalities on the discrete cube, which is of independent interest. Keywords: concentration of measure, strong Rayleigh measure, stochastic covering property AMS Classification: 60E15, 60B20, 60J28



1.
Introduction

Investigating families of binary random variables with negatively dependent coordinates is an important problem from the point of view of computer science and combinatorics, which in the recent years has attracted considerable attention, see, e.g.,
[
43
,
49
,
14
,
44
,
34
,
26
,
10
,
6
,
8
,
32
]
.
A wide and important class of such variables is constituted by those satisfying the
strong Rayleigh property
(abbrev. SRP) introduced by Borcea et al.
[
14
]
.
More precisely, a probability measure
\pi
on the hypercube
\calBn:=\{0,1\}^{n}
satisfies the SRP if its generating polynomial

\mathbb{C}^{n}\ni z\mapsto\sum_{x\in\calBn}\pi(x)\prod_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{x_{i}}

has no roots
z
whose all coordinates lie in the (strict) upper half-plane.
The examples of such measures are, e.g., the law of independent Bernoulli random variables conditioned on their sum, determinantal measures, uniform measure on the bases of balanced matroids, laws of point processes or measures obtained by running exclusion dynamics on the cube, cf. Pemantle and Peres
[
44
]
.

The main purpose of this article is to deepen the understanding of the concentration of measure phenomenon in the context of strong Rayleigh distributions and related classes of probability measures on the discrete cube.
In some of our considerations we will exploit only a more general notion of the
stochastic covering property
(abbrev. SCP, cf. Definition
2.1
) introduced by Pemantle and Peres
[
44
]
, since this condition already turns out to provide a useful framework for proving concentration results
[
44
,
29
,
8
,
34
,
32
,
2
]
. On the other hand for some more specialized inequalities we will restrict our attention to independent Bernoulli variables conditioned on their sum taking some fixed value. Distributions of this type generalize the uniform measure on slices of the discrete cube, related to the Bernoulli–Laplace model, which has been extensively studied, e.g., by Lee–Yau, Bobkov–Tetali, Gao–Quastel
[
37
,
13
,
25
]
and more recently by Samson
[
48
]
and Sambale–Sinulis
[
46
]
. The non-uniform distribution given by conditioned Bernoulli variables has found applications, e.g., in survey sampling being a model of sampling without replacement from a finite population, with prescribed inclusion probabilities, which maximizes the entropy (often referred to as
conditional Poisson sampling
). We refer to
[
20
,
19
,
21
,
52
,
10
]
for properties and applications of this family of distributions.

1.1.
State of the art

The landmark paper that initiated the study of concentration phenomenon implied by the SCP is due to Pematle and Peres
[
44
]
who, using the martingale method, proved a sub-Gaussian concentration bound for measures satisfying the SCP and functions that are Lipschitz with respect to the Hamming distance
d_{H}(x,y)=\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}\neq y_{i}}
.
Recently, Hermon and Salez
[
29
]
, building on the works
[
40
,
31
]
, retrieved this estimate by proving that the SCP implies the modified log-Sobolev inequality.

These findings in terms of concentration of measure can be summarized as follows (we use the notation
\pi(f):=\int f\,d\pi
for a probability measure
\pi
on
\calBn
and
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
).

Theorem 1.1

(Pemantle–Peres
[
44
]
)
.

For a probability measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfying the SCP and any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d_{H}(x,y)\quad\forall\,x,y\in\calBn

the following estimate holds for all
t>0

(1.1)

\pi\big(f>\pi(f)+t\big)\leq\exp(-t^{2}/8n).

If
\pi
is
k
-homogeneous (i.e., it is supported on the set of binary vectors with exactly
k
coefficients equal to one), then
n
in the above expression can be replaced with
k
.

Recently, a sub-exponential version of Theorem
1.1
for matrix-valued functions has been shown by Aoun et al.
[
8
]
, who develop a general framework for deducing concentration bounds for matrix-valued functions from the Poincaré inequality.
A Bernstein-type bound for measures with the SRP, which in certain situations may give stronger concentration, has been also developed by Kyng and Song
[
34
]
for functions of the form
f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}C_{i}
, where
C_{i}
are nonnegative definite matrices (see Theorem
2.8
and Remark
2.10
below).

While concentration estimates and functional inequalities for general SCP measures are relatively recent, investigation of uniform measures on slices of the discrete cube in this context has much longer history. Such measures are of interest in relation to the Bernoulli–Laplace models of statistical physics and with uniform sampling without replacement. In particular Lee and Yau studied the Poincaré and log-Sobolev inequalities for such measures, whereas Bobkov–Tetali
[
13
]
and independently Gao–Quastel
[
25
]
investigated modified log-Sobolev inequalities relevant for concentration estimates. Strong concentration results for this case can be also obtained by projection from Talagrand’s convex distance inequality for uniform measure on the symmetric group
[
51
]
. Samson
[
48
]
complemented this approach by proving corresponding transportation inequalities. Very recently Sambale and Sinulis
[
46
]
, investigating general multislices, recovered convex distance inequalities by means of functional inequalities and also obtained concentration for polynomials. One should stress that concentration results for slices of the cube provided by the above references are substantially stronger than those coming from more general inequalities for SCP or SRP measures.

The uniform measure on slices of the cube can be seen as a special case of the distribution of independent Bernoulli random variables conditioned on their sum, when all the variables have the same probability of success. Such general distributions are known to be strong Rayleigh. To our best knowledge there has not been much work concerning refined concentration inequalities for general measures of this type. The only exception we are aware of is a recent article
[
10
]
by Bertail and Clémençon in which the authors, motivated by applications to survey sampling, obtain precise Bernstein-type inequalities for linear functionals.

1.2.
Our contribution

We develop two types of results.

Firstly, for general measures satisfying the SCP we extend the martingale argument from Pemantle and Peres
[
44
]
and generalize Theorem
1.1
to Lipschitz functions with respect to more general weighted Hamming distances
d_{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum\alpha_{i}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}\neq y_{i}}
obtaining a bounded-difference type inequality.
We use the approach developed for the scalar case together with matrix bounded-difference inequality due to Tropp
[
54
]
to get an analogous concentration for matrix-valued functions, strengthening the results of Aoun et al.
[
8
]
, in particular obtaining a subgaussian inequality in place of a subexponential one.
Under a stronger assumption of the SRP we are also able to strengthen the Bernstein-type inequality of Kyng and Song and extend it from linear combinations with coefficients in nonnegative definite matrices to general functions satisfying a matrix bounded-difference type assumptions.

Secondly, building up on the work of Hermon and Salez
[
29
]
, we develop an abstract condition which implies Talagrand’s convex distance inequality, matrix-Bernstein inequality and higher order concentration for tetrahedral polynomials.
Finally, we prove that this condition is satisfied for the distribution of Bernoulli random variables conditioned on their sum being equal to some constant, yielding all the aforementioned results in this case.

Organization of the article

In Section

Then, in Section
4
we formulate an abstract framework that allows to deduce the results of Section
3
.
Finally, all the proofs are presented in Sections
5
,
6
and
7
.

2.
Concentration under the SCP and SRP

In this section we present our concentration results for general measures satisfying the SCP or SRP.
Let us start with introducing some notation.
For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\calBn:=\{0,1\}^{n}
and any
S\subset\{1,2,\ldots,n\}=:[n]
we use the shorthand notation
x_{S}=(x_{i})_{i\in S}
. For any

we denote
x_{>r}=(x_{i})_{i>r}
(and analogously with relations other than
>
)

1

We adopt the convention that if
x\in\calBn
then
x_{>n}=\emptyset
and as a consequence, e.g.,
\mathbb{P}\left(\cdot\,|\,X_{>n}=\emptyset\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\cdot\right)
.
.
We also write
x^{i}
for the vector obtained from

by flipping its i-th coordinate and
x^{ij}
for the vector obtained by swapping the
i
-th and

-th coordinate, i.e.,
x^{i}=x\pm e_{i}
and if
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
then
x^{ij}=x\pm e_{i}\mp e_{j}
for

, where

is the vector with one on the

-th and zeros on the remaining coordinates; whereas

if
x_{i}=x_{j}
. We remark that the notation

should not be confused with
(x^{i})^{j}
.
The law of a random variable

is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(X)
, whereas

stands for the conditional law of

given an event

(with an analogous convention for conditioning with respect to

Below, we recall the definition of the SCP.

Definition 2.1

(Stochastic covering property)
.

For
x,y\in\calBn
, we say that
x
covers
y
, denoted
x\triangleright y
, if
x=y
or
x=y+e_{i}
for some
i\in[n]
.

A random variable
X
taking values in
\calBn
satisfies the SCP if for any
S\subset[n]
and any
x,y\in\calBn
satisfying
x_{S}\triangleright y_{S}
there exists a coupling
(U,V)
between the conditional distributions
\mathcal{L}(X_{S^{c}}\,|\,X_{S}=y_{S})
and
\mathcal{L}(X_{S^{c}}\,|\,X_{S}=x_{S})
such that
U\triangleright V
.
A measure
\pi
satisfies the SCP if
X
with law
\pi
does so.

Remark 2.2
.

As indicated in the introduction, the SCP is implied by the SRP, cf.
[
44
]
.
The opposite however is not true, as is demonstrated, e.g., by Cryan et al. in
[
22
, Appendix A]
, where the authors show that yet another possible generalization of the SRP, the strong log-concavity, is incomparable with the SCP.
In particular, they construct a distribution that is supported on the bases of a matroid, and that satisfies the SCP and violates the log-concavity (and whence the SRP as well).

For a finite sequence
x
, we denote by
x^{\downarrow}
the non-increasing rearrangement of the elements of
x
and for
\alpha\in[0,\infty)^{n}=:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
x,y\in\calBn
we define the
\alpha
-weighted Hamming distance
d_{\alpha}(x,y)=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{1}_{x_{i}\neq y_{i}}
.
Finally, for
p\in[1,\infty]
,
|\cdot|_{p}
is the
\ell_{p}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
|\cdot|:=|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm.

The first main result of this paper is the following generalization of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 2.3
.

For a probability measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfying the SCP, any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d_{\alpha}(x,y)\quad\forall\,x,y\in\calBn

the following estimate holds for all
t>0
:

\displaystyle\pi\big(f>\pi(f)+t\big)\leq\exp(-t^{2}/8|\alpha|^{2}).

If
\pi
is
k
-homogeneous then
8|\alpha|^{2}
in the above estimate can be replaced with
16\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}^{\downarrow})^{2}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
implies Theorem
1.1
(up to an absolute constant in the exponent) by taking
\alpha=(1,1,\ldots,1)
.
Moreover, by considering functions of the form
f(x)=\sum_{i}c_{i}x_{i}
with
|c|^{2}\ll n|c|^{2}_{\infty}
in the non-homogeneous or
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(c_{i}^{\downarrow})^{2}\ll k|c|^{2}_{\infty}
in the
k
-homogeneous case, one can see that Theorem
2.3
can give substantially better concentration estimates than Theorem
1.1
.

We now formulate the matrix analogue of Theorem
2.3
.
To this end, let us denote the space of
d
-dimensional Hermitian matrices by
\calHd
, the identity matrix in
\calHd
by
I_{d}
, the maximal eigenvalue of

by
\lambda_{max}(H)
and the operator norm of

by
\|H\|
.

Theorem 2.5
.

For a probability measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfying the SCP, any
f\colon\calBn\to\calHd
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|f(x)-f(y)\|\leq d_{\alpha}(x,y)\quad\forall\,x,y\in\calBn

the following estimate holds for all

\displaystyle\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\pi(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp(-t^{2}/32|\alpha|^{2}).

If
\pi
is
k
-homogeneous then

in the above estimate can be replaced with
64\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}^{\downarrow})^{2}
.

Remark 2.6
.

Recently, Aoun et al.
[
8
]
showed that for any

-homogeneous probability measure

\calBn
satisfying the SCP and any

\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\pi(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{8k+2t\sqrt{2k}}\Big).

The exponent in (

) is proportional to

for

Theorem

strengthens on (

) (and on an analogous result from

) as it yields a sub-Gaussian estimate.

Remark 2.7
.

Using semigroup techniques together with matrix concentration results implied by the Poincaré inequality due to Aoun et al.
[
8
]
, we are also able to derive a sub-exponential concentration inequality for general measures satisfying the SCP under weaker assumptions on

than those of Theorem

, cf. Remark

.

When comparing the inequality of Theorem
2.5
or the results from
[
8
]
with results for matrix-valued functions of independent random variables, one can ask if it is possible to weaken the assumptions on the function
f
and instead of the Lipschitz constant with respect to

use some weaker parameter, involving bounds on the increments of the function in terms of the positive semidefinite order. In many situations one encounters functions for which
(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{i}^{2}

C_{i}
are some positive semidefinite matrices and
\preccurlyeq
stands for the positive semidefinite order (note that considering arbitrary matrices

is a generalization of the condition (

), which corresponds to the special case

). The simplest, yet important situation of this type is given by

. Inequalities for such functions together with algorithmic applications were considered by Kyng and Song in

]
. It turns out that their approach can be adapted to the setting of general functions, yielding the following theorem.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\pi
be a
k
-homogeneous probability measure
\calBn
satisfying the strong Rayleigh property and

be such that there exists a sequence

(2.3)

\displaystyle(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{i}^{2}\quad\forall\,x\in\calBn,\,i\in[n].

Then for any
t>0
,

(2.4)

\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\pi(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{8\|\pi(\tilde{f})\|\log(ek)+\frac{4}{3}Kt}\Big),

where
\tilde{f}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}C^{2}_{i}
and

.

Remark 2.9
.

In fact, the only place in the proof of Theorem

where we use the SRP in its full strength is to get that
\PP(X_{i}=1\,|\,X_{i_{1}}=1,\ldots,X_{i_{l}}=1)\leq\PP(X_{i}=1)

.
Therefore, in Theorem

]
.

Remark 2.10
.

It is natural to expect that

in (

would lead to improvement of certain algorithmic constructions related to graph sparsifiers obtained via random spanning trees, cf.

Let us also point out that even though Theorem

3.
Concentration for conditional Bernoullis

In this section, we present our concentration results concerning Bernoulli random variables conditioned on their sum being constant.
These include Talagrand’s convex distance inequality, matrix-Bernstein inequality and concentration for polynomials.

We start with introducing the notation.
For a sequence
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\in(0,1)^{n}
, let
B=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})
be a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables with probabilities of success
p_{i}
, i.e.,
\PP(B_{i}=1)=1-\PP(B_{i}=0)=p_{i}
for
i\in[n]
.
Finally, set
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\sim\mathcal{L}\big(B\,|\sum_{i}B_{i}=k\big)
for some
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
and denote the distribution of
X
by
\pi(p,k)
.

Our first contribution is a counterpart of the celebrated convex distance inequality, introduced for the first time by Talagrand
[
50
]
for product measures on the cube.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\pi\sim\pi(p,k)
for some
p\in(0,1)^{n}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, then for any
A\subset\calBn
,

\pi(A)\pi\big(d_{T}^{2}(\cdot,A)/84\big)\leq 1,

where

d_{T}(x,A)=\sup_{\alpha\colon|\alpha|\leq 1}d_{\alpha}(x,A)\quad\text{for}\quad x\in\calBn,\,A\subset\calBn.

Let
\mathbb{M}_{\pi}f
denote any median of
f
with respect to the measure
\pi
.
A classical consequence of Theorem
3.1
is the following fact regarding the concentration around the median of convex functions
[
17
]
. Let us recall the classical observation that subgaussian concentration around median and mean for convex Lipschitz functions are equivalent up to the change of constants by a universal factor.

Corollary 3.2
.

If
\pi\sim\pi(p,k)
for some
p\in(0,1)^{n}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, then for any convex function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to the standard Euclidean distance on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any
t>0
,

\pi\big(|f-\mathbb{M}_{\pi}f|>t\big)\leq 4\exp\big(-t^{2}/84L^{2}\big).

Remark 3.3
.

If one is interested just in the lower tail of a convex function, then one can in fact replace the Lipschitz constant
L
by
\pi\big(|\nabla f|\big)
or even certain quantiles of
|\nabla f|
. We do not pursue this direction here and refer the reader to
[
3
]
.

Remark 3.4
.

If
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
is
d_{\alpha}
1-Lipschitz, then it can be extended to a function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is
|\alpha|
-Lipschitz with respect to the standard Euclidean distance.
Therefore, Corollary
3.2
counterparts Theorem
2.3
in the sense that it yields the same concentration profile while allowing for a weaker Lipschitz condition on
f
at the cost of assuming convexity.

Our next result concerns concentration for matrix-valued functions under weaker assumptions than those in Theorem
2.5
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\pi\sim\pi(p,k)
for some
p\in(0,1)^{n}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
.
Assume that
f\colon\calBn\to\calHd
is such that there is a sequence of positive semidefinite matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
satisfying

(3.1)

(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{i}^{2},\quad\forall\;x\in\calBn,\,i\in[n],

where
\preccurlyeq
denotes the partial ordering of the set of positive semidefinite matrices.
Define the variance proxy

\sigma^{2}=16\sup\Big\{\,\big\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{i}^{2}\big\|\,\colon\,|\mathcal{I}|=k,\,\mathcal{I}\subset[n]\,\Big\}.

Then for any
t>0
,

\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\mu(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp\big(-t^{2}/(\sigma^{2}+{\sigma}t)\big).

Remark 3.6
.

Condition (
3.1
) implies that
f
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to the distance
d_{\alpha}
with
\alpha_{i}={\|C_{i}\|}
.
On the other hand, for many choices of matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
it happens that
\sigma^{2}\ll\sum_{i=1}^{k}\big(\|C_{i}\|^{2}\big)^{\downarrow}
as
n,k\to\infty
.
Therefore, while yielding only sub-exponential concentration as opposed to the sub-Gaussian one given by Theorem
2.5
, Theorem
3.5
may improve significantly on Theorem
2.5
through better parameters in the exponent.

Remark 3.7
.

By an adaptation of the proof of Theorem
3.5
, one can obtain a similar result for general
k
-homogeneous measures satisfying the SCP condition with the variance proxy parameter

\sigma^{2}=8\sup\Big\{\,\big\|{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{i}^{2}}\big\|+k\max_{i\notin\mathcal{I}}\big\|{C_{i}^{2}}\big\|\colon|\mathcal{I}|\leq k,\,\mathcal{I}\subset[n]\,\Big\}.

Finally, let us turn to the higher order concentration.
By the Fourier–Walsh expansion (see e.g.,
[
41
]
), every function
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
can be written in a unique way as a tetrahedral polynomial, i.e., a polynomial which is affine with respect to every variable (in particular the degree of the polynomial is at most
n
).
Therefore in what follows we restrict our attention to this representation.
In particular, when we speak about the gradient
\nabla f=(\partial_{1}f,\ldots,\partial_{n}f)
or higher order derivatives
\nabla^{k}f
, we always think of the usual derivatives of the polynomial function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by the tetrahedral representation of
f
(sometimes referred to as the harmonic extension of
f
). We remark that the directional derivatives
\partial_{i}f
coincide on
\calBn
with the discrete derivatives of
f
given by
D_{i}f(x)=f(\max(x,x^{i}))-f(\min(x,x^{i}))
, where the maximum and minimum are taken coordinatewise.

In order to formulate concentration of measure estimates for tetrahedral polynomials, we need to introduce a family of injective tensor product norms on
d
-index matrices (
d
-tensors).
Let us recall the notation introduced by Latała in
[
35
]
.

Let
|I|
be the cardinality of a set
I
and for
{\bf i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}
let
|{\bf i}|=\max_{j\leq d}{i_{j}}
and
|{\bf i}_{I}|=\max_{j\in I}i_{j}
.
Denote by
P_{d}
the set of partitions of
[d]
into nonempty, pairwise disjoint sets. For a partition
\mathcal{I}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}\in P_{d}
, and a
d
-indexed matrix
A=(a_{\bf i})_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}
, define

\displaystyle\|A\|_{\mathcal{I}}=\sup\Big\{\sum_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}a_{{\bf i}}\prod_{l=1}^{k}x^{(l)}_{{\bf i}_{I_{l}}}\colon|(x^{(l)}_{{\bf i}_{I_{l}}})|\leq 1,1\leq l\leq k\Big\},

where
|(x_{{\bf i}_{I_{l}}})|=\sqrt{\sum_{|{\bf i}_{I_{l}}|\leq n}x_{{\bf i}_{I_{l}}}^{2}}
.
Therefore, for example,

\displaystyle\|(a_{ij})_{i,j\leq n}\|_{\{1,2\}}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\sum_{i,j\leq n}a_{ij}x_{ij}\colon\sum_{i,j\leq n}x_{ij}^{2}\leq 1\Big\}=\sqrt{\sum_{i,j\leq n}a_{ij}^{2}}=\|(a_{ij})_{i,j\leq n}\|_{HS},

\displaystyle\|(a_{ij})_{i,j\leq n}\|_{\{1\}\{2\}}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\sum_{i,j\leq n}a_{ij}x_{i}y_{j}\colon\sum_{i\leq n}x_{i}^{2}\leq 1,\sum_{j\leq n}y_{j}^{2}\leq 1\Big\}=\|(a_{ij})_{i,j\leq n}\|,

\displaystyle\|(a_{ijk})_{i,j,k\leq n}\|_{\{1,2\}\{3\}}

\displaystyle=\sup\Big\{\sum_{i,j,k\leq n}a_{ijk}x_{ij}y_{k}\colon\sum_{i,j\leq n}x_{ij}^{2}\leq 1,\sum_{k\leq n}y_{k}^{2}\leq 1\Big\},

where
\|\cdot\|_{HS}
and
\|\cdot\|
denote the Hilbert–Schmidt and the operator norm respectively.

Theorem 3.8
.

If
\pi\sim\pi(p,k)
for some
p\in(0,1)^{n}
and
k\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, then for any tetrahedral polynomial
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
d
,

\pi\big(\big|f-\pi(f)\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{C_{d}}\min_{1\leq r\leq d}\min_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{r}}\Big(\frac{t}{\|\pi(\nabla^{r}f)\|_{\mathcal{J}}}\Big)^{2/|\mathcal{J}|}\Big),

where
C_{d}
is a constant depending only on the degree
d
of
f
.

Inequalities of this type for polynomials of arbitrary degree were introduced for the first time by Latała
[
35
]
for tetrahedral polynomials in i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables. Subsequently they were extended to general polynomials in independent subgaussian random variables and to certain dependent situations related to Glauber dynamics (see
[
4
,
1
,
47
,
46
,
2
]
). We remark that in the independent, subgaussian case and
d=2
they reduce to the well known Hanson–Wright inequality for quadratic forms, which has proved useful in non-asymptotic analysis of random matrices and in asymptotic geometric analysis (see, e.g.,
[
55
, Chapter 6]
). It is worth mentioning that in the Gaussian case they may be reversed up to the value of the absolute constants, thus Theorem 3.8 shows that the measures
\pi(p,k)
exhibit Gaussian type concentration for polynomials.
While calculating the norms
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{J}}
is usually difficult, estimating them is sometimes possible, leading to applications involving subgraph counts (in the Erdős-Rényi case or for some models of random graphs with dependencies
[
4
,
47
,
46
]
) or to statistical applications, e.g., in testing Ising models
[
23
]
and signal processing
[
56
]
.

4.
Abstract formulations

In this section we recall some notions from the theory of Markov semigroups and formulate the abstract counterparts of the results of Section
3
and of Theorem
2.3
.
We believe that the results presented in this section might be of separate interest as they provide a general framework for proving concentration on the hypercube.
We stress that most of the proof techniques that we exploit were known previously – our main contribution is the abstract formulation of these results by means of the novel stability condition (cf. Definition
4.3
).

Throughout this section we will rely on the usual notions from the theory of Markov processes and Dirichlet forms specialized to finite state space. We will briefly recall them and refer to
[
39
,
9
,
13
]
for details.

4.1.
Modified log-Sobolev inequalities

Let
L
be the generator of a jump Markov process on some finite probability space
(M,\pi)
. In what follows we will sometimes treat
L
as a linear operator on
\mathbb{R}^{M}
and sometimes identify it with the corresponding matrix, indexed by the elements of
M
.

Assume that
L
satisfies the detailed-balance condition

(4.1)

\forall\;x,y\in M\quad\pi(x)L(x,y)=\pi(y)L(y,x),

which implies that
\pi
is a stationary measure for the Markov process and
L
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(\pi)
.
In this article we consider only Markov processes satisfying the above condition, which may not be stated explicitly in all the results.

For a given
L
, we define
\Delta(L):=\max_{x}-L(x,x)=\max_{x}\sum_{y\colon y\neq x}L(x,y)
and write
\calE(f,g)=-\pi(fLg)
for the Dirichlet form associated with
L
.
In particular
\calE(f,g)=\pi\big(\Gamma(f,g)\big)
, where
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
given by

(4.2)

\Gamma(f,g)(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in M}(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))L(x,y)

is the corresponding carré-du-champ operator.
We use shorthand notation
\Gamma(f,f)=:\Gamma(f)
and observe that by the detailed-balance condition (
4.1
) we have
\pi\big(\Gamma(f)\big)=\pi(\Gamma_{+}(f))
, where

(4.3)

\Gamma_{+}(f)(x)=\sum_{y\in M}(f(x)-f(y))_{+}^{2}L(x,y).

Finally, we denote by
\rho(L)
the best (the greatest) constant such that the following modified log-Sobolev inequality is satisfied

(4.4)

\rho(L)\Ent_{\pi}(f)\leq\calE(f,\log f)

for all non-constant functions
f\colon M\to[0,\infty)
, where
\Ent_{\pi}(f)=\pi(f\log f)-\pi(f)\log\pi(f)
is the entropy functional (we adopt the convention
0\log 0=0
).
We remark that
\rho(L)
is positive iff
L
is irreducible on the support of
\pi
(see the discussion in
[
13
]
and
[
38
, Chapter 12]
).
In what follows we will restrict our attention to this situation, without mentioning this assumption explicitly in each statement.

A classical observation, often referred to as Herbst’s argument (cf. the monographs
[
36
]
by Ledoux and
[
17
]
by Boucheron et al.), says that for any
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
,

(4.5)

\pi\big(f>\pi(f)+t\big)\leq\exp(-t^{2}\rho(L)/4\|\Gamma_{+}(f)\|_{\infty}),

where
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
stands for the norm in
L^{\infty}(\pi)
.

4.2.
Flip-swap random walks

After Hermon and Salez
[
29
]
, we say that a kernel
L
generates a flip-swap random walk if
L(x,y)>0
implies that
x=y^{i}
for some
i\in[n]
(i.e.,
x
and
y
differ by a flip) or
x=y^{ij}
for some
i\neq j
,
i,j\in[n]
(i.e.,
x
and
y
differ by a swap).
The main contribution of
[
29
]
can be stated in the following way.

Theorem 4.1

(Hermon–Salez
[
29
]
)
.

For any measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfying the SCP, there exists a kernel
L
generating a reversible flip-swap random walk with stationary measure
\pi
such that
\rho(L)\geq 1
and
\Delta(L)\leq n
.
If
\pi
is also
k
-homogeneous, then
\Delta(L)\leq 2k
as well.

Theorem
4.1
, by means of Herbst’s argument (
4.5
), implies (up to an absolute constant in the exponent) the estimate from Theorem
1.1
after observing that for a flip-swap random walk and any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
that is 1-Lipschitz with respect to the Hamming distance
d_{H}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|\Gamma_{+}(f)\|_{\infty}\leq\Delta(L)\cdot\max_{x,y\in\calBn}\{\,(f(y)-f(x))_{+}^{2}\colon L(x,y)>0\,\}\leq 4\Delta(L).

Finally, Theorem
4.1
is constructive – in Section
7
we revisit the inductive construction of
L
from
[
29
]
in the context of conditional Bernoulli distribution.

Remark 4.2
.

There are many examples of flip-swap random walks on the hypercube in the literature, including, e.g., the Bernoulli–Laplace model, Glauber dynamics or base exchange random walk on matroids, cf. e.g.,
[
13
,
28
,
47
,
22
]
.
We note that the results of this section apply to any flip-swap random walk as long as we have control of its stability (cf. Definition
4.3
) constant.

It turns out that for the proofs of all the statements of Section
3
it suffices to demonstrate that the following condition is true for some reversible generator
L
with stationary measure
\pi(p,k)
for which the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) is known.

Definition 4.3

(Stability condition)
.

Let
L
be a generator of a flip-swap random walk on
\calBn
with invariant probability distribution
\pi
.
We say that the pair
(L,\pi)
meets the stability condition with constant
R\geq 0
(i.e., is
R
-stable) if it satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) and

(4.7)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\supp\pi;\,i\in[n]}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)\leq R\rho(L).

If it is clear from the context which measure
\pi
is associated with
L
, we will often omit it in the discussion and simply say that
L
is
R
-stable.

Remark 4.4
.

If
\pi
is not concentrated on a single point, then a random walk on
\calBn
with a generator
L
that satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) may be at best
0.25
-stable (i.e.,
R\geq 0.25
).
Indeed, in this case there exists
i
such that
\pi(\{x_{i}=1\}),\pi(\{x_{i}=0\})>0
.
If
L
satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality, then it also satisfies the Poincaré inequality
\frac{1}{2}\rho(L)\Var_{\pi}(f)\leq\calE(f,f)
, see e.g.,
[
2
, Proposition B.5]
.
Therefore, by the stability condition (
4.7
) applied to the function
f(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}=1\}}
and reversibility of
L
we get that

\displaystyle R\rho(L)\pi(\{x_{i}=1\})

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{x\colon x_{i}=1}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}=0}L(x,y)\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}(x_{i}-y_{i})^{2}_{+}L(x,y)\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\calE(f,f)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\rho(L)\Var_{\pi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\rho(L)\pi(\{x_{i}=1\})\pi(\{x_{i}=0\}),

which gives
R\geq 0.5\cdot\pi(\{x_{i}=0\})
.
Similarly, by considering
f(x)=\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}=0\}}
we get that
R\geq 0.5\cdot\pi(\{x_{i}=1\})
as well, yielding
R\geq 0.25
.

This bound is optimal, as can be seen for
\pi
being the uniform measure on
\calBn
and
L(x,y)=1
if there exists
i
such that
y=x^{i}
,
L(x,y)=-n
if
y=x
and
L(x,y)=0
otherwise (this corresponds to the special case of Glauber dynamics, in which at rate
n
, a random coordinate is flipped). In this case
\rho(L)=4
(see
[
13
, Example 3.7]
, note a different normalization of both the Dirichlet form and the constant in the modified log-Sobolev inequality), whereas for all
x\in\calBn

\max_{i}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)=L(x,x^{i})=1=0.25\cdot\rho(L).

Let us illustrate the notion of
R
-stability with another classical example.

Example 4.5

(Bernoulli–Laplace model)
.

Let
\pi
be the uniform measure on the slice of
\calBn
consisting of elements with exactly
k
ones and let
L
be given by
Lf(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i<j}(f(x^{ij})-f(x))
(thus the corresponding Markov process at rate
(n-1)/2
swaps a uniformly chosen pair of coordinates). In the matrix form this corresponds to
L(x,y)=\frac{1}{n}
if
x\neq y
and
y=x^{ij}
,
L(x,x)=-k(n-k)/n
and
L(x,y)=0
otherwise. It has been proved in
[
25
]
and independently in
[
13
]
that
\rho_{0}(L)\geq 1/2
. At the same time
\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)
equals to
(n-k)/n
if
x_{i}=1
and to
k/n
otherwise. This shows that
L
is
2
-stable, independently of
n
and
k
. As mentioned in the introduction, the uniform measure on the slice of the discrete cube can be interpreted as the distribution of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables conditioned on their sum being equal to
k
. In Proposition
7.3
we generalize the above observation on stability and show that if
\mu
is the law of general independent Bernoulli variables conditioned on their sum being equal to a fixed constant, there exists a
2
-stable generator of a random walk reversible with respect to
\mu
.

Remark 4.6
.

Observe that the notion of stability is invariant under scaling of
L
(change of time), i.e., if
L
is
R
-stable then so is
aL
for any
a>0
.
This leads to a tensorization property for measures admitting an
R
-stable generator. More precisely, let
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{m}
be measures on
\mathcal{B}_{n_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{B}_{n_{m}}
, for which there exist reversible flip-swap random walks with
R
-stable generators
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
.
By changing time, we can assume without loss of generality that
\rho(L_{i})=\rho
for all
i\leq m
. Let
n=n_{1}+\ldots+n_{m}
and consider the product measure
\pi=\pi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi_{m}
on
\calBn
together with the generator
L=L_{1}+\ldots+L_{m}
, where we think of
L_{i}
as acting only on the
i
-th block of coordinates on
\calBn=\mathcal{B}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{n_{m}}
, i.e., we identify
L_{i}
with its tensor product with identity on
\otimes_{j\neq i}\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{B}_{n_{j}}}
. In the matrix form we have the representation

L(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}L_{i}(P_{i}x,P_{i}y)\prod_{j\neq i}\mathbf{1}_{\{P_{j}x=P_{j}y\}},

where
P_{j}\colon\calBn\to\mathcal{B}_{j}
is the projection onto the
j
-th factor in the product
\calBn=\mathcal{B}_{n_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{B}_{n_{m}}

Thanks to the well known tensorization property of the entropy (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Chapter 3]
) we have
\rho(L)=\rho
.
Moreover, for
i\in(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{j-1},n_{1}+\ldots+{n_{j}}]
,

\sum_{y\in\calBn\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{B}_{n_{j}}\colon y_{l}\neq(P_{j}x)_{l}}L_{j}((P_{j}x),y)\leq R\rho,

where
l=i-(n_{1}+\ldots+n_{j-1})
.
Thus
L
is indeed
R
-stable.

This observation allows in particular to extend all the theorems od Section
3
to product of measures
\pi(n,k)
allowing for more general conditioning of Bernoulli variables.

4.3.
Abstract formulations

Finally, let us present the counterparts of the results of Section
2
and of Theorem
2.3
from Section
3
in the abstract language of the stability condition (
4.7
).
We stress here that it is the sole property needed for their proofs, which are deferred to Section
7
.

We start with a bounded-difference type inequality for real valued functions.

Proposition 4.7
.

If a flip-swap random walk on
\calBn
with stationary distribution
\pi
and generator
L
satisfies the stability condition (
4.7
), then for any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq d_{\alpha}(x,y)\quad\forall\,x,y\in\calBn

the following estimate holds for all
t>0

\displaystyle\pi\big(f>\pi(f)+t\big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{8R|\alpha|^{2}}\Big).

In the above estimate one can also replace
8|\alpha|^{2}
with
16\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\rceil}(\alpha_{i}^{\downarrow})^{2}
.

Remark 4.8
.

Using the definitions of
R
-stability and of
\Delta(L)
one can see that
\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\leq n
and if
\pi
is
k
-homogeneous, then

\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\leq k
.

Let us now pass to the matrix-valued case.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let a flip-swap random walk on
\calBn
with stationary distribution
\pi
and generator
L
satisfy the stability condition (
4.7
).
Assume also that
f\colon\calBn\to\calHd
is such that there is a sequence of positive semidefinite matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{n}
satisfying

(4.8)

(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{i}^{2}\quad\forall\;x\in\calBn,\,i\in[n],

where
\preccurlyeq
denotes the positive semidefinite order on the set of symmetric matrices.
Set the variance proxy

\sigma^{2}=8R\cdot\sup\Big\{\,\big\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{i}^{2}\big\|\,\colon\,|\mathcal{I}|=\lceil\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\rceil,\,\mathcal{I}\subset[n]\,\Big\}.

Then for any
t>0
,

\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\pi(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp\big(-t^{2}/(\sigma^{2}+\sigma t)\big).

Our next proposition is the convex distance inequality under
R
-stability.

Proposition 4.10
.

If a flip-swap random walk on
\calBn
with some stationary distribution
\pi
and a generator
L
satisfies the stability condition (
4.7
), then for any set
A\subset\calBn

\pi(A)\pi\Big(\exp\Big(\frac{1}{40R+4}\cdot d_{T}^{2}(\cdot,A)\Big)\Big)\leq 1.

Finally, let us state the concentration result for polynomials in an abstract version.

Proposition 4.11
.

If a flip-swap random walk on
\calBn
with some stationary distribution
\pi
and a generator
L
satisfies the stability condition (
4.7
), then for any tetrahedral polynomial
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
d

\pi\big(\big|f-\pi(f)\big|\geq t\big)\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{C_{d}}\min_{1\leq r\leq d}\min_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{r}}\Big(\frac{t}{R^{r/2}\|\pi(\nabla^{r}f)\|_{\mathcal{J}}}\Big)^{2/|\mathcal{J}|}\Big),

where
C_{d}
is a constant depending only on the degree
d
of
f
.

Remark 4.12
.

Although Proposition
4.7
gives a worse constant in the exponent than Theorem
2.3
even in the case of conditional Bernoulli distributions
\pi(p,k)
, we state it here as in principle it does not assume that
\pi
satisfies the SCP and thus potentially can be applied in other settings.

Remark 4.13
.

The above propositions can be transferred to more general random walks that change at each step at most a fixed number of coordinates
N
(with
N=2
in case of flip-swap random walks).
We do not pursue this direction though and do not write all the theorems in full generality for the sake of readability.

Remark 4.14
.

Recently, Cryan et al.
[
22
]
have shown a version of Theorem
4.1
for
k
-homogeneous strongly log-concave measures.
Strong log-concavity is yet another possible generalization of the SRP, which is in general incomparable with the SCP
[
22
]
.
It is known, cf. Brändén and Huh
[
18
]
, that any
k
-homogeneous strongly log-concave measure is supported on the set of bases of some matroid of rank
k
.
Using this fact, and extending the previous results for uniform measures on the bases of matroids by Anari et al.
[
6
]
and Kaufman and Oppenheim
[
33
]
, Cryan et al.
[
22
]
explicitly construct a base-exchange random walk, which has any given strongly log-concave measure as a stationary distribution, and verify it satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
).

Since the base-exchange random walk proposed therein is a particular instance of a flip-swap random walk, a natural question is whether it satisfies the stability condition (
4.7
), which would allow to deduce concentration results presented in this section.
Unfortunately, the answer seems to be negative in full generality as can be seen already in the case of independent Bernoulli random variables
B=(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})
with different probabilities of success
\PP(B_{i}=1)=p_{i}
conditioned on their sum being
k
, i.e., for the distribution
\pi(p,k)\sim\mathcal{L}(B\,|\,\sum_{i}B_{i}=k)
.
If one chooses
p_{1}\to 1^{-}
and
p_{j}=c
for
j>1
and some
c\in(0,1)
, then it is straightforward to verify that the base-exchange random walk of
[
22
]
is at best
k
-stable.
Therefore, applying propositions of Section
4.3
to the base-exchange random walk gives much worse concentration constants than those of Section
3
.
On the other hand, the flip-swap random walk proposed by Hermon and Salez
[
29
]
with stationary measure
\pi(p,k)
, which we use to prove the results of Section
3
, turns out to be
2
-stable.

In view of the above, it is an interesting problem to analyze what other known kernels satisfy the stability condition (
4.7
) with good (dimension-independent) constant and to look for some other criteria that would allow to deduce this condition.

5.
Proofs of the results of Section
2

In this section we provide proofs of Theorems
2.3
,
2.5
and
2.8
.
All these results follow by modifications of the martingale argument due to Pemantle and Peres
[
44
]
.

Let
X\sim\pi
be a random variable with values in
\calBn
satisfying the SCP and
denote
\supp X=\{\,i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:X_{i}=1\,\}
.
In the non-homogeous case define a filtration
\calF=(\calF_{l})_{l=0}^{n}
by letting simply
\calF_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
and
\calF_{l}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{l})
for
l=1,\ldots,n
. In the
k
-homogeous case introduce a family of random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
given by the conditions

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y_{1}\,|\,X)=\Unif(\supp X\backslash\{1,\ldots,k\})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y_{l}\,|\,X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l-1})=\Unif(\supp X\setminus\{1,\ldots,k,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l-1}\}),\quad\text{for}\quad l=2,\ldots,k,

where
\Unif(A)
stands for the uniform distribution on the set
A
, and for notational simplicity we set
\Unif(\emptyset)
to be the Dirac mass at
0
and
X_{0}\equiv 1
(i.e., we add to
X
an additional coordinate providing no information and if the above sampling scheme yields all elements from
\supp X
before sampling some
Y_{l}
, we set
Y_{i}
to zero for all
i\geq l
).
Finally, define a filtration
\calG=(\calG_{l})_{l=0}^{2k}
setting
\calG_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
and
\calG_{l}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{l})
for
l\in[k]
,
\calG_{k+r}=\sigma(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}\}
for
r\in[k]
.

In other words in the first
k
-steps the subsequent values of
X
at the first
k
coordinates are revealed, while in the last
k
steps one reveals in a uniformly random order the remaining coordinates at which
X
takes the value
1
. Note that if
\alpha
is nonincreasing (which we may assume without loss of generality) and
f
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\alpha}
then the first part of this sampling scheme promotes the coordinates which may have the greatest impact on the value of
f(X)
. The construction can be thought of as a modification of the sampling scheme proposed by Pemantle and Peres in which one immediately starts revealing in a random order the coordinates at which
X
takes the value
1
.

The proof of Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
will be based on the following two lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
be nonincreasing and let
f\colon\calBn\to\calHd
be
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the distance
d_{\alpha}
.
Assume that
X
is a
\calBn
-valued random vector satisfying
the SCP. Let
M_{l}=\EE[f(X)\,|\,\calF_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,\calF_{l-1}]
for
l\in[n]
. Then for every
l\in[n]
,

(5.2)

M_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq 4\alpha_{l}^{2}I_{d}.

Lemma 5.2
.

In the setting of Lemma
5.1
assume additionally that
X
is
k
-homogeneous. For
l\in[2k]
define

N_{l}=\EE[f(X)\,|\,\calG_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,\calG_{l-1}]
. Then for
l\in[k]
,

(5.3)

N_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq 4\alpha_{l}^{2}I_{d},

while for
l=k+1,\ldots,2k
,

(5.4)

N_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq 4\alpha_{k}^{2}I_{d}.

We postpone for now the proof of the above lemmas and firstly show how they imply Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
.
To this end let us recall the matrix version of the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality due to Tropp
[
54
, Theorem 7.1]
, which asserts that if
D_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,n
are
\calHd
-valued martingale differences and
D_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{l}^{2}
for some deterministic matrices
C_{l}\in\calHd
, then for all
t\geq 0
,

\PP\Big(\lambda_{max}\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{n}D_{l}\Big)\geq t\Big)\leq de^{-t^{2}/8\sigma^{2}},

where
\sigma^{2}=\|\sum_{l=1}^{n}C_{l}^{2}\|
.
Note also that for
d=1
the classical Azuma-Hoeffding inequality (see, e.g.,
[
24
, Theorem 5.8]
) allows to replace the constant
1/8
by
1/2
.

Proof of Theorems
2.3
and
2.5
.

Since the SCP is invariant under permutations of coordinates of
X
, we may and do assume that
\alpha=\alpha^{\downarrow}
.
By Lemma
5.1
the martingale differences
M_{l}
satisfy
M_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq C_{l}^{2}:=4\alpha_{l}^{2}I_{d}
. Clearly

(5.5)

\Big\|\sum_{l=1}^{n}C_{l}^{2}\Big\|=4|\alpha|^{2}.

If
X
is
k
-homogeneous, then by Lemma
5.2
,
N_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq\widetilde{C}_{l}^{2}:=4\alpha_{\min(l,k)}^{2}I_{d}
. In this case

(5.6)

\Big\|\sum_{l=1}^{2k}\widetilde{C}_{l}^{2}\Big\|=4\Big[\Big(\sum_{l=1}^{k}\alpha_{l}^{2}\Big)+k\alpha_{k}^{2}\Big]\leq 8\sum_{l=1}^{k}\alpha_{l}^{2}.

We have
f(X)=\sum_{l=1}^{n}M_{l}
, whereas in the
k
-homogeneous case
f(X)=\sum_{l=1}^{2k}N_{l}
(observe that after
2k
-steps of the sampling procedure all the nonzero coordinates of
X
are revealed and so
X
is
\mathcal{G}_{2k}
-measurable). Thus the conclusion of Theorem
2.3
follows by applying estimates (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) for
d=1
together with the classical Azuma-Hoeffding inequality. Similarly, Theorem
2.5
follows from the matrix version of the Azuma-Hoeffding inequality.
∎

It remains to prove Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Let
A^{x}_{l}=\{X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{l}=x_{l}\}
for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\calBn
and
l=0,\ldots,n
.
Then, for
l=1,\ldots,n
and any
x\in\calBn
such that
\PP(A_{l}^{x})>0
,

\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1}]=\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1},X_{l}=x_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1}]\\
=\PP(X_{l}\neq x_{l}\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1})\big(\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1},X_{l}=x_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1},X_{l}\neq x_{l}]\big).

If
\PP(X_{l}\neq x_{l}\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1})\neq 0
, then by the SCP there exist a coupling
(\hat{X},\hat{Y})
between the distributions
\mathcal{L}(X\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1},X_{l}=x_{l})
and
\mathcal{L}(X\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1},X_{l}\neq x_{l})
that is supported on the set
\{\,(y,z)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{2}\,\colon\,d_{H}((y_{i})_{i>l},(z_{i})_{i>l})\leq 1\,\}
.
Using this coupling, the Lipschitz property of
f
, Jensen’s inequality and the fact that
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{l}
for any
i>l
, we get that

\|\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1}]\|\\
\leq\PP(X_{l}\neq x_{l}\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1})\EE\|f((x_{i})_{i\leq l},\hat{X}_{i>l})-f((x_{i})_{i<l},1-x_{l},\hat{Y}_{i>l})\|\\
\leq\PP(X_{l}\neq x_{l}\,|\,A^{x}_{l-1})\cdot 2\alpha_{l}\leq 2\alpha_{l},

which is equivalent to (
5.2
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

Note that for
l\leq k
, we have
\calG_{l}=\calF_{l}
. As a consequence
N_{l}=M_{l}
, where
M_{l}
are martingale increments defined in Lemma
5.1
, which implies (
5.3
).

Consider now
l>k
of the form
l=k+r
and for
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
and
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in(\{0\}\cup\{k+1,\ldots,n\})^{k}
set
A^{x,v}_{l}=\{X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{k}=x_{k},Y_{1}=v_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}=v_{r}\}
.
Then
\calF_{l}
is generated by the sets
A^{x,v}_{l}
. By the definition of the variables
Y_{r}
, we have
\{Y_{r}=i\}\subseteq\{X_{i}=1\}
and so for any
x,v
such that
\PP(A^{x,v}_{l})>0
,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x,v}_{l}]=\frac{\EE[f(X)\mathbf{1}_{A^{x,v}_{l-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{v_{r}}=1\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{r}=v_{r}\}}]}{\PP(A^{x,v}_{l-1},X_{v_{r}}=1,Y_{r}=v_{r})}.

For
s\in[r]
let
m_{s}=|\{i\in[k]\colon x_{i}=1\}|+|\{j\in[s-1]\colon v_{j}\neq 0\}|
be the number of ones sampled by the time
k+s-1
.
It follows from (
5.1
) that if
m_{s}<k
then
\PP(A^{x,v}_{k+s})>0
implies that
v_{s}\neq 0
and
\PP(Y_{s}=v_{s}|X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{s-1})=\frac{1}{k-m_{s}}
on
A^{x,v}_{k+s-1}\cap\{X_{v_{s}}=1\}
, whereas if
m_{s}=k
, then
\PP(A^{x,v}_{k+s})>0
implies that
v_{s}=0
and
\PP(Y_{s}=v_{s}\,|\,X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{s-1})=1
on
A^{x,v}_{k+s-1}\cap\{X_{v_{s}}=1\}=A^{x,v}_{k+s-1}
.
Going back to (
5.7
) and using this observation for
s=r,\ldots,1
, we obtain that

\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x,v}_{l}]=\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{x,v}_{l}],

where
B^{x,v}_{l}=\{X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{k}=x_{k},X_{v_{1}}=\ldots=X_{v_{l-k}}=1\}
. We thus obtain

\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x,v}_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{x,v}_{l-1}]\\
=\PP(X_{v_{r}}\neq 1\,|\,B^{x,v}_{l-1})(\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{x,v}_{l-1},X_{v_{r}}=1]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{x,v}_{l-1},X_{v_{r}}\neq 1]).

Note that the right-hand side may be non-zero only if
v_{r}\neq 0
. In this case
using the inequality
\alpha_{v_{s}}\leq\alpha_{k}
for
s\in[k]
we can conclude as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
.

∎

Let us now pass to the proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

We will rely on the martingale used in the article by Pemantle and Peres.
Let
X
be a random vector with law
\pi
and define the random variables
Y_{l}
for
l\leq n
as

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y_{1}\,|\,X)=\Unif(\supp X)\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(Y_{l}\,|\,X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l-1})=\Unif(\supp X\setminus\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l-1}\}),\quad\text{for}\quad l=2,\ldots,k,

i.e.,
Y_{l}^{\prime}s
reveal in a uniformly random order the elements of
\supp X
.
Let
\mathcal{H}_{0}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
and
\mathcal{H}_{l}=\sigma(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{l})
for
l=1,\ldots,k
.
Then
f(X)-\EE f(X)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}\EE[f(X)\,|\,\mathcal{H}_{l}]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,\mathcal{H}_{l-1}]=:\sum_{l=1}^{k}D_{l}
. We will use the matrix version of Freedman’s inequality due to Tropp
[
53
]
, which asserts (in a version specialized for our application) that if
\|D_{l}\|\leq a
a.s. for all
l
, and
\|\sum_{l=1}^{k}\EE[D_{l}^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{H}_{l-1}]\|\leq\sigma^{2}
a.s., then for any
t\geq 0
,

(5.9)

\PP(\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|\geq t)\leq 2d\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}+2at/3}\Big).

Consider thus a sequence of pairwise distinct
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in[n]
and denote
A^{v}_{l}=\{Y_{1}=v_{1},\ldots,Y_{l}=v_{l}\}
. Similarly as in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, if
\PP(A^{v}_{l})>0
, then we have

\EE[f(X)\,|\,A^{v}_{l}]=\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l}],

where
B_{l}^{v}=\{X_{v_{1}}=\ldots=X_{v_{l}}=1\}
. Therefore we have

(5.10)

D_{l}\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}=\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\big(\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=1]-\EE[f(X)|B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=0]\big)\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}.

Since the SRP implies the SCP, there exists a coupling
(\tilde{Z},\hat{Z})
between the distributions
\mathcal{L}(X\,|\,B^{v}_{l})
and
\mathcal{L}(X\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=0)
such that
\tilde{Z}
and
\hat{Z}
differ just at coordinate
v_{l}
and one additional coordinate (at which by
k
-homogeneity
\hat{Z}
necessarily takes the value one). Let
\tilde{Y}_{l}
be this coordinate.
We have

(5.11)

\displaystyle\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=1]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=0]=\EE[f(\tilde{Z})-f(\hat{Z})],

Since
\hat{Z}^{\tilde{Y}_{l}}=\tilde{Z}^{v_{l}}
, we have

(5.12)

\Big(\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=1]-\EE[f(X)\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1},X_{v_{l}}=0]\Big)^{2}=\Big(\EE[f(\tilde{Z})-f(\hat{Z})]\Big)^{2}\\
\preccurlyeq\EE\Big[\big(f(\tilde{Z})-f(\hat{Z})\big)^{2}\Big]=\EE\Big[\big(f(\tilde{Z})-f(\tilde{Z}^{v_{l}})+f(\hat{Z}^{\tilde{Y}_{l}})-f(\hat{Z})\big)^{2}\Big]\\
\preccurlyeq 2\EE\Big[\big(f(\tilde{Z})-f(\tilde{Z}^{v_{l}})\big)^{2}\Big]+2\EE\Big[\big(f(\hat{Z}^{\tilde{Y}_{l}})-f(\hat{Z})\big)^{2}\Big]\preccurlyeq 2C_{v_{l}}^{2}+2\EE C_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}^{2},

where in the first and second inequality we used the operator convexity of the function
x\mapsto x^{2}
(see
[
11
, Example V.1.3]
), and in the last inequality the assumption (
2.3
).

In particular, using (
5.10
), we obtain
\|D_{l}^{2}\|\leq 4\max_{i}\|C_{i}^{2}\|
, so
\|D_{l}\|\leq 2K
.
Moreover as on
A^{v}_{l}
we have
Y_{l}=v_{l}
, by (
5.10
) and (
5.12
) we get that

\displaystyle D_{l}^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}\preccurlyeq 2(C_{Y_{l}}^{2}+\EE C_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}^{2})\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}.

Let us now slightly change our notation and think of
\tilde{Y}_{l}
as of random variable defined on the same probability space as
X
, with conditional distribution with respect to the
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{H}_{l}
given on each of its atoms
A^{v}_{l}
by the above construction, using the corresponding coupling (which depends on
v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}
). Then the above inequality can be written as

(5.13)

\displaystyle D_{l}^{2}\preccurlyeq 2\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\big(C_{Y_{l}}^{2}+\EE[C_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}^{2}\,|\,A^{v}_{l}]\big)\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}.

Let us now go back to the equations (
5.10
) and (
5.11
) and let us apply them in the special case of the function
\tilde{f}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}C_{i}^{2}
, denoting the corresponding martingale increment by
\tilde{D}_{l}
. We obtain that

\tilde{D}_{l}\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}=\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\big(C^{2}_{Y_{l}}-\EE[C^{2}_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}\,|\,A^{v}_{l}]\big)\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}.

Thus we get that

0=\EE[\tilde{D}_{l}\,|\,A^{v}_{l-1}]=\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\EE\Big[\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}\big(C^{2}_{Y_{l}}-\EE[C^{2}_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}\,|\,A^{v}_{l}]\big)\,\Big|\,A^{v}_{l-1}\Big],

i.e.,

\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\EE\Big[\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}C^{2}_{Y_{l}}\,\Big|\,A^{v}_{l-1}\Big]\\
=\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\EE\Big[\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}\EE(C^{2}_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}\,|\,A^{v}_{l})\,\Big|\,A^{v}_{l-1}\Big],

which combined with the estimate (
5.13
) on
D_{l}^{2}
(replacing
\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})^{2}
by
\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})
) gives

\displaystyle\EE[D_{l}^{2}\,|\,A^{v}_{l-1}]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq 2\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\EE\Big[(C_{Y_{l}}^{2}+\EE[C_{\tilde{Y}_{l}}^{2}\,|\,A^{v}_{l}])\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}\,\Big|\,A^{v}_{l-1}\Big]

\displaystyle=4\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}\EE\Big[C_{Y_{l}}^{2}\PP(X_{v_{l}}=0\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})\mathbf{1}_{A^{v}_{l}}\,\Big|\,A^{v}_{l-1}\Big]

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq 4\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}C_{v_{l}}^{2}\PP(A^{v}_{l}\,|\,A^{v}_{l-1})

\displaystyle=4\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}C_{v_{l}}^{2}\frac{1}{k-l+1}\PP(X_{v_{l}}=1\,|\,B^{v}_{l-1})

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq 4\sum_{v_{l}\in[n]\setminus\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{l-1}\}}C_{v_{l}}^{2}\frac{1}{k-l+1}\PP(X_{v_{l}}=1),

where in the last inequality we used
[
34
, Lemma 1.10]
, which asserts that
\PP(X_{v_{l}}=1)\geq\PP(X_{v_{l}}=1|B^{v}_{l-1})
(we remark that this is the only place in the proof in which we use the full strength of the strong Rayleigh property).

Extending the summation to
[n]
, we thus obtain

\EE[D_{l}^{2}\,|\,\mathcal{H}_{l-1}]\preccurlyeq 4\sum_{v=1}^{n}C_{v}^{2}\PP(X_{v}=1)\frac{1}{k-l+1},

whence

\sum_{l=1}^{k}\EE[D_{l}^{2}|\mathcal{H}_{l-1}]\preccurlyeq 4\sum_{v=1}^{n}C_{v}^{2}\PP(X_{v}=1)\log(ek)\preccurlyeq 4\log(ek)\cdot\EE\Big[\sum_{v=1}^{n}X_{v}C_{v}^{2}\Big].

Combining this with the already obtained bound on
\|D_{l}\|
allows us to apply (
5.9
) with
a=2K
and
\sigma^{2}=4\|\EE\sum_{v=1}^{n}X_{v}C_{v}^{2}\|\log(ek)
, which ends the proof of the theorem.
∎

6.
Proofs of the results of Section
4

6.1.
Propositions
4.7
and
4.9

The main idea behind the proof of Proposition
4.7
is to find an estimate on
\|\Gamma_{+}(f)\|_{\infty}
in terms of
\alpha
, refining (
4.6
), and then to use the Herbst argument.
We will need the following lemma which we state in the matrix setting as it will be useful for the proof of Proposition
4.9
as well.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
be a sequence of nonnegative numbers and let
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}\in\calHd
be positive semidefinite matrices.
Then for any
T_{1}\geq|t|_{1}
and
T_{\infty}\geq|t|_{\infty}

(6.1)

\displaystyle\big\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}t_{i}D_{i}\big\|\leq T_{\infty}\cdot\sup\Big\{\,\big\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}D_{i}\big\|\colon\mathcal{I}\subset[n],\,|\mathcal{I}|\leq\lceil T_{1}/T_{\infty}\rceil\,\Big\}.

Proof.

By homogeneity we may assume without loss of generality that
T_{\infty}=1
. We may also assume that
T_{1}
is a positive integer. Let

\mathcal{X}=\Big\{\,x\in[0,1]^{n}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq T_{1}\,\Big\},\;\mathcal{Y}=\Big\{\,y\in\{0,1\}^{n}\colon\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq T_{1}\,\Big\}.

Since the right-hand side of (
6.1
) equals to
\max\{\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}D_{i}\|\colon y\in\mathcal{Y}\}
, whereas the left-hand side is a convex function of
t
, the lemma will follow once we prove that
\mathcal{X}\subset\conv\mathcal{Y}
. To this end, by the Krein–Milman theorem, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{Y}
is the set of all extreme points of the closed convex set
\mathcal{X}
. Consider any
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{Y}
.
Let
i_{0}\in[n]
be such that
x_{i_{0}}\in(0,1)
.
If
\sum_{i}x_{i}<T_{1}
then for
\varepsilon
sufficiently close to zero,
x+\varepsilon e_{i_{0}},x-\varepsilon e_{i_{0}}\in\mathcal{X}
and so
x=\frac{1}{2}(x+\varepsilon e_{i_{0}})+\frac{1}{2}(x-\varepsilon e_{i_{0}})
is not an extreme point of
\mathcal{X}
.
If
\sum_{i}x_{i}=T_{1}
, then since
T_{1}
is an integer, there exists
{i_{1}}\neq{i_{0}}
such that
x_{i_{1}}\in(0,1)
.
Then
x=\frac{1}{2}u+\frac{1}{2}v
, where
u=x+\varepsilon e_{i_{0}}-\varepsilon e_{i_{1}}
,
v=x-\varepsilon e_{i_{0}}+\varepsilon e_{i_{1}}
.
For
\varepsilon
close to zero
u,v\in\mathcal{X}
, thus again,
x
is not an extreme point.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.7
.

We recall that for
x\in\calBn
and
i,j\in[n]
,
x^{i}
and
x^{ij}
denote the vectors obtained from
x
by flipping the
i
-th and swapping the
i
-th and
j
-th coordinates respectively.
For any
x\in\calBn
, using the definition (
4.3
) of
\Gamma_{+}
, Lipschitz property of
f
and inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
we get

(6.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{+}(f)(x)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{i}))_{+}^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{ij}))_{+}^{2}L(x,x^{ij})\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{j})^{2}L(x,x^{ij})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\neq x^{ij}\}}\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}L(x,x^{i})+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}L(x,x^{ij})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\neq x^{ij}\}}\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}^{2}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y).\end{split}

Therefore, by the stability condition (
4.7
) we estimate
\|\Gamma_{+}(f)\|_{\infty}\leq 2R\rho(L)|\alpha|^{2}
.
Herbst’s argument (
4.5
) allows to conclude the first part.

The second part of the proposition follows by observing that for a flip-swap random walk

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)\leq 2\cdot\Delta(L)

so by (
6.2
), Lemma
6.1
applied in the scalar setting
d=1
with
t_{i}=2\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)
,
D_{i}=\alpha_{i}^{2}
,
T_{1}=4\Delta(L)
and
T_{\infty}=4R\rho(L)
we can estimate

\|\Gamma_{+}(f)\|_{\infty}\leq 4R\rho(L)\sum^{\lceil\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\rceil}_{i=1}(\alpha_{i}^{\downarrow})^{2}

and conclude again in virtue of Herbst’s argument (
4.5
).
∎

The proof of Proposition
4.9
follows along similar lines to the proof of Proposition
4.7
, the difference being that in the end, instead of Herbst’s argument, we apply the concentration result of Aoun et al.
[
8
]
, which asserts that if
L
satisfies the matrix Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{P}>0

(6.3)

\operatorname{Var}(f)\preccurlyeq-C_{P}\pi(fLf)\quad\forall\,f\colon\calBn\to\calHd,

(where
L
acts on the matrix-valued function
f
elementwise and
fLf
is the matrix product), then it satisfies the exponential concentration bound of the form

(6.4)

\pi\big(\lambda_{max}(f-\pi(f))>t\big)\leq d\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2C_{P}v_{f}+t\sqrt{2C_{P}v_{f}}}\Big),

where
v_{f}=\sup_{x}\|\Gamma(f)(x)\|
(where
\Gamma
is defined via (
4.2
), again with matrix multiplication, and
\|\cdot\|
stands for the operator norm).
Note that for
d=1
, (
6.3
) is just the usual scalar Poincaré inequality.

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

For any
x\in\calBn
and
i,j\in[n]
, using operator convexity of the function
x\mapsto x^{2}
(see
[
11
, Example V.1.3]
) we get that

(6.5)

\big(f(x)-f(x^{ij})\big)^{2}=\big[\big(f(x)-f(x^{i})\big)+\big(f(x^{i})-f(x^{ij})\big)\big]^{2}\\
\preccurlyeq 2\big(f(x)-f(x^{i})\big)^{2}+2\big(f(x^{i})-f(x^{ij})\big)^{2}.

Therefore, by the definition (
4.2
) of
\Gamma
, by the assumed Lipschitz property (
4.8
) of
f
and by (
6.5
), for any
x\in\calBn
,

(6.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma(f)(x)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{ij}))^{2}L(x,x^{ij})\\
&\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(C_{i}^{2}+C_{j}^{2})L(x,x^{ij})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\neq x^{ij}\}}\\
&\preccurlyeq\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_{i}^{2}\cdot\big[\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)\big].\end{split}

As both hand sides of (
6.6
) are positive semidefinite, their norms compare as well.
Therefore, as in the proof of Proposition
4.7
, by Lemma
6.1
with
t_{i}=\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)
,
T_{1}=2\Delta(L)
,
T_{\infty}=2R\rho(L)
and
D_{i}=C_{i}^{2}

\sup_{x\in\calBn}\|\Gamma(f)(x)\|\leq 2R\rho(L)\cdot\sup\Big\{\big\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}C_{i}^{2}\big\|\colon\mathcal{I}\subset[n],\,|\mathcal{I}|\leq\lceil\Delta(L)/R\rho(L)\rceil\Big\}.

Since
L
satisfies the (scalar) modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
), then it satisfies the (scalar) Poincaré inequality with constant
C_{P}=2/\rho(L)
(see, e.g.,
[
13
, p. 292]
, noting slightly different definitions of constants in functional inequalities used therein) and whence by
[
30
, Proposition 2.2]
or
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
it satisfies the matrix Poincaré inequality (
6.3
) with the same constant, which yields the conclusion in virtue of (
6.4
).
∎

6.2.
Proposition
4.10

The proof of Proposition
4.10
is based on the idea introduced by Boucheron et al.
[
16
]
and then developed by Paulin
[
42
]
.
We follow the exposition from the works of Sambale and Sinulis
[
45
,
46
]
.
We start with the following lemmas.

Lemma 6.2
.

For any flip-swap random walk with generator
L
satisfying the stability condition (
4.7
) and for any
A\subset\calBn

(6.7)

\Gamma_{+}(d_{T}^{2}(\cdot,A))(x)\leq 8R\rho(L)\cdot d_{T}^{2}(x,A).

Moreover, for any
x,y\in\calBn
and any set
A\subset\calBn
,

(6.8)

d_{T}^{2}(x,A)-d_{T}^{2}(y,A)\leq d_{H}(x,y).

Proof.

For
x\in\calBn
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a probability measure
\mu
on
\calBn
, let
h_{x}(\mu,\alpha)=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\mu(z\colon z_{i}\neq x_{i})
.
By Sion’s minmax theorem, cf.
[
17
, p. 227]
,

(6.9)

d_{T}(x,A)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}\sup_{\alpha\in B_{2}^{n}}h_{x}(\mu,\alpha),

where
\mathcal{M}(A)
is the set of probability measures on
A
and
B_{2}^{n}=\{\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|\leq 1\,\}
is the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
\alpha^{\ast}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}\cap B_{2}^{n}
,
\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}(A)
be such that
d_{T}(x,A)=h_{x}(\mu^{\ast},\alpha^{\ast})
and set
\nu_{y}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}h_{y}(\nu,\alpha^{\ast})
.
Then

\displaystyle\Gamma_{+}\big(d_{T}(\cdot,A)\big)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\big[h_{x}(\mu^{\ast},\alpha^{\ast})-\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}\sup_{\alpha\in B_{2}^{n}}h_{y}(\nu,\alpha)\big]_{+}^{2}L(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y}\big[h_{x}(\mu^{\ast},\alpha^{\ast})-h_{y}(\nu_{y},\alpha^{\ast})\big]_{+}^{2}L(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y}\big[h_{x}(\nu_{y},\alpha^{\ast})-h_{y}(\nu_{y},\alpha^{\ast})\big]_{+}^{2}L(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y}\big[\sum_{i}\alpha^{\ast}_{i}\big(\nu_{y}(z\colon z_{i}\neq x_{i})-\nu_{y}(z\colon z_{i}\neq y_{i})\big)\big]_{+}^{2}L(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y}\big[\sum_{i}\alpha^{\ast}_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}}\big]^{2}L(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i}(\alpha^{\ast}_{i})^{2}\sum_{y\colon y_{i}\neq x_{i}}L(x,y)\leq 2R\rho(L),

where the penultimate inequality follows since
L
is a flip-swap random walk and therefore
L(x,y)>0
implies that
d_{H}(x,y)\leq 2
and so at most two elements of the sum
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\ast}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}}
are non-zero, whence we may apply the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
. The last inequality is a consequence of the condition
\alpha^{\ast}\in B_{2}^{n}
and the stability condition (
4.7
).
We conclude (
6.7
) using the definition of
\Gamma_{+}
and estimating
(a-b)^{2}_{+}(a+b)_{+}^{2}\leq 4a^{2}(a-b)_{+}^{2}
.

To show the second part, note that (
6.9
) together with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality imply that

d_{T}^{2}(x,A)=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)}\sum_{i}\big(\mu(z\colon z_{i}\neq x_{i})\big)^{2}=\sum_{i}\big(\mu^{\ast}_{x}(z\colon z_{i}\neq x_{i})\big)^{2}

for some
\mu^{\ast}_{x}\in\mathcal{M}(A)
.
Therefore, for any
x,y\in\calBn

d_{T}^{2}(x,A)-d_{T}^{2}(y,A)\leq\sum_{i}\Big[\big(\mu^{\ast}_{x}(z\colon z_{i}\neq x_{i})\big)^{2}-\big(\mu^{\ast}_{x}(z\colon z_{i}\neq y_{i})\big)^{2}\Big]\leq\sum_{i}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}},

as desired.
∎

Using the inequality
1-e^{-z}\leq z
we observe that for any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\calE(e^{f},f)=\sum_{x}\pi(x)e^{f(x)}\Big[\sum_{y}(f(x)-f(y))_{+}(1-e^{f(y)-f(x)})L(x,y)\Big]\leq\pi\big(e^{f}\Gamma_{+}(f)\big).

Therefore, the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) implies the following inequality stated in Bobkov and Götze
[
12
]
:

(6.10)

\rho(L)\Ent_{\pi}(e^{f})\leq\pi\big(e^{f}\tilde{\Gamma}(f)^{2}\big)

with operator
\tilde{\Gamma}(f)=\sqrt{\Gamma_{+}(f)}
(note that in
[
12
]

\tilde{\Gamma}
is denoted by
\Gamma
, we use
\tilde{\Gamma}
to avoid a conflict of notation).
As a consequence, the hypothesis of
[
12
, Theorem 2.1]
(formula (1.1)) therein holds under the assumption of the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) (with
c=2/\rho(L)
).
As a result, the following lemma follows directly by the derivation of
[
12
, equation (2.4)]
with a slight adjustment of constants (see also
[
5
]
).

Lemma 6.3
.

If a measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) and
f\colon\calBn\to[0,\infty)
is such that
\Gamma_{+}(f)\leq Cf
for some constant
C>0
, then for all
t>C/\rho(L)
,

(6.11)

\pi\big(\exp(f/t)\big)\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\pi(f)}{t-C/\rho(L)}\Big).

We are finally in position to prove Proposition
4.10
.

Proof of Proposition
4.10
.

To lighten notation, denote
f(x)=d_{T}^{2}(x,A)
for
x\in\calBn
and some fixed set
A\subset\calBn
.
Denote also
h(z)=({e^{z}-1})/{z}
for
z\in[0,\infty)
and
Df_{y}(x)=f(x)-f(y)
for
x,y\in\calBn
, and note that
h
is an increasing function. Starting with the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) we have for all
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\Ent_{\pi}(e^{-\lambda f})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda/\rho(L)\cdot\calE(e^{-\lambda f},-f)

(by reversibility of
L
)

\displaystyle=\lambda/\rho(L)\sum_{x,y}\big(Df_{y}(x)\big)_{+}\big(e^{-\lambda f(y)}-e^{-\lambda f(x)}\big)L(x,y)\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}/\rho(L)\sum_{x}\pi(x)e^{-\lambda f(x)}\Big[\sum_{y}\big(Df_{y}(x)\big)_{+}^{2}h\big(\lambda Df_{y}(x)\big)L(x,y)\Big]

(by (
6.8
))

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{2}h(2\lambda)/\rho(L)\cdot\pi\big(e^{-\lambda f}\Gamma_{+}(f)\big)

(by (
6.7
))

\displaystyle\leq 8R\lambda^{2}h(2\lambda)\cdot\pi\big(e^{-\lambda f}f\big)

\displaystyle\leq 8R\lambda^{2}h(2\lambda)\cdot\pi(e^{-\lambda f})\pi(f),

where the last inequality follows by convexity of the function
t\mapsto t\log t
.
Therefore, using the entropy method (cf., e.g.,
[
17
, Chapter 6]
) and monotonicity of
h
, we have for every
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle\pi\big(\exp(\lambda(\pi(f)-f)\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\lambda\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{d}{ds}\Big[\frac{1}{s}\log\pi(e^{-sf})\Big]\,ds\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\lambda\int_{0}^{\lambda}\frac{\Ent_{\pi}(e^{-sf)}}{s^{2}\pi(e^{-sf})}\,ds\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(\lambda\cdot 8R\pi(f)\int_{0}^{\lambda}h(2s)\,ds\Big)\leq\exp\big(4R\lambda(e^{2\lambda}-1)\pi(f)\big).

By Chebyshev’s exponential inequality

\pi(A)=\pi\big(\pi(f)-f\geq\pi(f)\big)\leq\exp\Big(\lambda\big(4R(e^{2\lambda}-1)-1\big)\pi(f)\Big).

Taking
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\frac{1}{8R})
and estimating
\log(1+x)\geq x/(x+1)
for
x\geq 1
gives

(6.12)

\pi(A)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{4}\log\big(1+\frac{1}{8R}\big)\pi(f)\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{\pi(f)}{32R+4}\Big).

We conclude by dividing (
6.12
) by its right hand side and using Lemma
6.3
with
t=4+40R
and
C=8R\rho(L)
(in virtue of Lemma
6.2
).
∎

6.3.
Proposition
4.11

Before we move to the proof of Proposition
4.11
, let us comment a bit on a background result.
Using the equivalence between the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
) and the family of Beckner inequalities together with the approach developed by Boucheron et al.
[
15
]
, it was shown in
[
2
, Proposition 3.1]
that the following moment estimate is implied by the modified log-Sobolev inequality. Below we will denote by
\|\cdot\|_{p}
the norm in
L^{p}(\pi)
.

Proposition 6.4
.

If a probability measure
\pi
on
\calBn
satisfies the modified log-Sobolev inequality (
4.4
), then for any
p\geq 2
,

(6.13)

\|(f-\pi(f))_{+}\|_{p}\leq C\sqrt{p/{\rho(L)}}\|\sqrt{\Gamma_{+}(f)}\|_{p},

where
C=\sqrt{{3\sqrt{e}}/({\sqrt{e}-1})}
.

A general method of deriving estimates for polynomials from moment inequalities of the form (
6.13
) has been presented in
[
4
]
in the continuous case, and in
[
1
,
2
]
in the context of Glauber dynamics.
To obtain results for flip-swap random walks we will adapt a version of this method introduced recently by Sambale and Sinulis
[
46
]
for multislices.

Proof of Proposition
4.11
.

Below we write
C
to denote universal constants and
C_{a}
to denote constants depending only on the parameter
a
. In both cases the constants may change values between occurrences.
Let
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
be a tetrahedral polynomial.
By
\partial_{i}
we denote the partial derivative with respect to the
i
-th coordinate.
If
x,y\in\calBn
differ at the
i
-th coordinate only, then by the fact that
f
is linear in each coordinate

|f(x)-f(y)|=\big|\partial_{i}f(x)\big|.

Similarly, if
x
and
y
differ only by a swap of the
i
-th and
j
-th coordinate, we have

|f(x)-f(y)|=|\partial_{i}f(x)(y_{i}-x_{i})+\partial_{j}f(x)(y_{j}-x_{j})+\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(x)(y_{i}-x_{i})(y_{j}-x_{j})|\\
\leq|\partial_{i}f(x)|+|\partial_{j}f(x)|+|\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(x)|.

Thus

\Gamma(f)(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x)-f(x^{i}))^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(f(x)-f(x^{ij}))^{2}L(x,x^{ij})\\
\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\partial_{i}f(x)|^{2}L(x,x^{i})+\frac{3}{2}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i<j\leq n}}{{x^{ij}\neq x}}}(|\partial_{i}f(x)|^{2}+|\partial_{j}f(x)|^{2}+|\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(x)|^{2})L(x,x^{ij})\\
\leq R\rho(L)\Big(3.5\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\partial_{i}f(x)|^{2}+0.75\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f(x)|^{2}\Big),

where in the last inequality we used the stability condition (
4.7
). Note that since
f
is tetrahedral,
\partial_{i}\partial_{i}f(x)=0
for all
i
.

Combining the above equality with Proposition
6.4
we obtain that for every tetrahedral polynomial
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}

(6.14)

\displaystyle\|f-\pi(f)\|_{p}\leq C\sqrt{p}\sqrt{R}\Big(\big\||\nabla f|\big\|_{p}+\big\|\|\nabla^{2}f\|_{HS}\big\|_{p}\Big),

where
C
is a universal constant.

In the subsequent part of the proof we are going to need some auxiliary notation. For
d
-tensors
A=(a_{{\bf i}})_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}
,
B=(b_{\bf i})_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}
define

\langle A,B\rangle=\sum_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}a_{\bf i}b_{\bf i}.

Let us now consider a family of stochastically independent random tensors
\{G^{I}\colon I\subseteq\mathbb{N},|I|\in\{1,2\}\}
, given by
G^{\{m\}}=(g^{\{m\}}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
,
G^{\{l,k\}}=(g^{\{l,k\}}_{i,j})_{i,j\in[n]}
, with coefficients being i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables. Denote by
P_{d,\leq 2}
the family of all partitions of the set
[d]
into non-empty subsets of cardinality at most 2.
Finally, for any positive integers
d
and
l
and
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{l}\}\in P_{d,\leq 2}
define the random
d
-tensor
G_{\mathcal{J}}=(\prod_{j=1}^{l}g^{J_{j}}_{{\bf i}_{J_{j}}})_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{d}}
.
For instance
G_{\{\{1,3\},\{2\}\}}=(g^{\{1,3\}}_{i_{1}i_{3}}g^{\{2\}}_{i_{2}})_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}\in[n]}
.

Using the fact that the
p
-th moment of a mean zero Gaussian variable with variance
\sigma^{2}
is for
p\geq 2
comparable to
\sqrt{p}\sigma
up to universal constants, we can rewrite (
6.14
) as

(6.15)

\displaystyle\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq C\sqrt{R}\Big(\|\langle\nabla f(X),G^{\{1\}}\rangle\|_{p}+\|\langle\nabla^{2}f(X),G^{\{1,2\}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big),

where
X
is a random vector with law
\pi
, independent of the family
\{G^{I}\}
.

The inequality (
6.15
) constitutes a basis for the induction argument leading to the following inequality valid for any
f\colon\calBn\to\mathbb{R}
,
d\geq 1
and
p\geq 2
,

(6.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq&C_{d}\Big(\sum_{l=d}^{2d}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\\
&+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-2}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big).\end{split}

Before we prove the above estimate, let us show how it implies the statement of the proposition. If
f
is a tetrahedral polynomial of degree
d
, then
\nabla^{l}f=0
for
l>d
, moreover
\nabla^{d}f
is constant and so
\nabla^{d}f(X)=\EE\nabla^{d}f(X)
. Thus (
6.16
) reduces to

\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq C_{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}.

We can now use moment estimates for tetrahedral homogeneous polynomials in i.i.d. standard Gaussian variables due to Latała
[
35
]
, which assert that for any
l
-tensor
A=(a_{\bf i})_{{\bf i}\in[n]^{l}}
and
p\geq 2
,

\|\langle A,G_{\{\{1\},\ldots,\{l\}\}}\rangle\|_{p}\leq C_{l}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l}}p^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|A\|_{\mathcal{J}}.

Applying this inequality to
\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle
(we treat here
\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X)
as a
|\mathcal{J}|
-tensor by merging the indices according to the partition
\mathcal{J}
), we obtain

\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq C_{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in P_{l}\colon\mathcal{I}\succ\mathcal{J}}p^{|\mathcal{I}|/2}\|\EE\nabla^{l}f(X)\|_{\mathcal{I}},

where
\mathcal{I}\succ\mathcal{J}
if every element of
\mathcal{I}
is a union of certain elements of
\mathcal{J}
.
Rearranging the terms and taking into account that in a non-trivial case
R
is bounded away from zero by an absolute constant (see Remark
4.4
), which gives
R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\leq C_{d}R^{l/2}
for
\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}
, we get

\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq C_{d}\sum_{l=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathcal{I}\in P_{l}}R^{l/2}p^{|\mathcal{I}|/2}\|\EE\nabla^{l}f(X)\|_{\mathcal{I}}

for
p\geq 2
. This implies the tail inequality of the proposition in the standard way by the use of Chebyshev’s inequality
\PP(|f(X)-\EE f(X)|\geq e\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p})\leq e^{-p}
followed by an appropriate change of variables and adjustment of constants. We leave the details to the reader and turn to the proof of (
6.16
).

We will proceed by induction on
d
. For
d=1
, using the definitions of
G_{\{1\}}
and
G_{\{\{1,2\}\}}
one can easily see that (
6.16
) reads as

\displaystyle\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\sqrt{R}\|\langle\nabla f(X),G^{\{1\}}\rangle\|_{p}+\sqrt{R}\|\langle\nabla^{2}f(X),G^{\{1,2\}}\rangle\|_{p}

\displaystyle+R\|\langle\nabla^{2}f(X),G_{\{\{1\},\{2\}\}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big),

which is clearly weaker than (
6.15
). Let us thus assume that the inequality holds for all positive integers smaller than
d
. Applying the inequality with
d-1
and combining it with the triangle inequality in
L_{p}
we get (recall that the value of
C_{d}
may change between occurences)

(6.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|f(X)-\EE f(X)\|_{p}\leq&C_{d}\Big(\sum_{l=d-1}^{2d-2}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\\
&+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-4}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big)\\
\leq&C_{d}\Big(\sum_{l=d-1}^{2d-2}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle-\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\\
&+\sum_{l=1}^{2d-2}\sum_{\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}}2R^{|\mathcal{J}|/2}\|\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big).\end{split}

An application of inequality (
6.15
) conditionally on
G_{\mathcal{J}}
to the functions
g_{l,\mathcal{J}}(x)=\langle\nabla^{l}f(x),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle
for
l=d-1,\ldots,2d-2
and
\mathcal{J}\in P_{l,\leq 2}
(note that
g_{l,\mathcal{J}}
’s are tetrahedral polynomials), followed by the Fubini theorem, gives

\|\langle\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle-\langle\EE_{X}\nabla^{l}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}}\rangle\|_{p}\leq C\sqrt{R}\Big(\|\langle\nabla^{l+1}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}\cup\{\{l+1\}\}}\rangle\|_{p}\\
+\|\langle\nabla^{l+2}f(X),G_{\mathcal{J}\cup\{\{l+1,l+2\}\}}\rangle\|_{p}\Big),

which combined with (
6.17
) concludes the induction step, thus proving (
6.16
).
∎

Proofs of the results of Section

By virtue of the abstract results of Section

, all the results of Section

follow from the observation that there exist a flip-swap random walk on

with stationary measure

that satisfy the stability condition (

for all

and

).
The rest of this section is devoted to proving this fact.

We start with the following lemma, the proof of which is postponed until the end of this section.

Lemma 7.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in(0,1)^{n}
and
k\in[n]
, there exist a coupling
(Z,Z^{\prime})
of measures
