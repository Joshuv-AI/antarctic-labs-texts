Perfect Zero-Knowledge PCPs for # ​ 𝖯 \#\mathsf{P}
Tom Gur, Jack O’Connor, Nicholas Spooner

Abstract
We construct perfect zero-knowledge probabilistically checkable proofs (PZK-PCPs) for every language in # ​ 𝖯 \#\mathsf{P} . This is the first construction of a PZK-PCP for any language outside 𝖡𝖯𝖯 \mathsf{BPP} . Furthermore, unlike previous constructions of (statistical) zero-knowledge PCPs, our construction simultaneously achieves non-adaptivity and zero knowledge against arbitrary (adaptive) polynomial-time malicious verifiers. Our construction consists of a novel masked sumcheck PCP, which uses the combinatorial nullstellensatz to obtain antisymmetric structure within the hypercube and randomness outside of it. To prove zero knowledge, we introduce the notion of locally simulatable encodings: randomised encodings in which every local view of the encoding can be efficiently sampled given a local view of the message. We show that the code arising from the sumcheck protocol (the Reed–Muller code augmented with subcube sums) admits a locally simulatable encoding. This reduces the algebraic problem of simulating our masked sumcheck to a combinatorial property of antisymmetric functions.



1
Introduction

The notion of a
zero-knowledge (ZK) proof
, a proof that conveys no information except the truth of a statement, is one of the most influential ideas in cryptography and complexity theory of the past four decades.

Zero knowledge was originally defined by Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff
[
GMR89
]
in the context of interactive proofs (IPs). The deep and beautiful insight in this work is that it is possible to rigorously
prove
that an interaction does not convey information, by exhibiting an efficient algorithm called the
simulator
which can generate the distribution of protocol transcripts without interacting with the prover. In that work, the authors identify three different notions of zero knowledge, depending on the quality of the simulation. These are:

(1)

perfect
zero knowledge (PZK), where the simulator’s distribution is
identical
to the real distribution of transcripts;

(2)

statistical
zero knowledge (SZK), where the distributions are inverse-exponentially close; and

(3)

computational
zero knowledge (CZK), where the distributions are computationally indistinguishable.

This hierarchy led naturally to the study of the complexity classes
\mathsf{PZK},\mathsf{SZK},\mathsf{CZK}
of languages admitting ZK interactive proofs. Seminal results show that
\mathsf{PZK}
contains interesting “hard” languages, including quadratic residuosity and nonresiduosity
[
GMR89
]
and graph isomorphism and nonisomorphism
[
GMW91
]
. Despite this, the structure of the class
\mathsf{PZK}
, and its relation to other complexity classes, remains poorly understood. In light of this difficulty, the study of zero knowledge has followed two main routes:

(1)

studying the “relaxed” notions of
\mathsf{SZK}
and
\mathsf{CZK}
, and

(2)

studying zero knowledge in other models.

The former line of work has proved highly fruitful. A seminal result of
[
GMW91
]
showed that
\mathsf{CZK}=\mathsf{IP}=\mathsf{PSPACE}
, which launched the cryptographic study of ZK proofs, yielding a plethora of theoretical and practical results
[
Tha22
]
. The study of
\mathsf{SZK}
has revealed that this class has a rich structure, and deep connections within complexity theory (cf.
[
Vad99
]
).

The present work lies along the second route, also hugely influential. The seminal work of Ben-Or, Goldwasser, Kilian, and Wigderson
[
BGKW88
]
introduced the model of multi-prover interactive proofs (MIP) in order to achieve perfect zero-knowledge without any computational assumptions
[
LS95
,
DFKNS92
]
. This in turn inspired some of the most important models and results in contemporary theoretical computer science, including entangled-prover interactive proofs (MIP*), interactive oracle proofs (IOPs), and most notably, probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs). Indeed, the celebrated PCP theorem
[
ALMSS92
,
AS03
,
Din07
]
is widely recognised as one of the most important achievements of modern complexity theory
[
AAV13
]
.

Zero-knowledge PCPs.
Recall that a PCP is a proof which can be verified, with high probability, by a verifier that only makes a small number of queries (even
O(1)
) to the proof (cf.
[
AB09
]
). A zero-knowledge PCP is a
randomised
proof which, in addition to being locally checkable, satisfies a zero knowledge condition: the view of any efficient (malicious) verifier can be efficiently simulated (see
[
Wei22
]
for a survey). Similarly to the IP case, we can distinguish PCPs with perfect, statistical and computational zero knowledge. Note that there appears to be no formal relationship between ZK-IPs and ZK-PCPs: standard transformations from IP to PCP spoil zero knowledge.

The first
statistical
zero-knowledge PCPs appeared in the seminal work of Kilian, Petrank and Tardos
[
KPT97
]
. Later works
[
IMS12
,
IW14
]
simplified this construction, and extended it to PCPs of proximity. These constructions operate via a 2-step compilation of (non-zero-knowledge) PCP: first the PCP is endowed with honest-verifier statistical zero-knowledge (HVSZK), and then the latter property is boosted into full statistical zero knowledge by “forcing” the malicious verifier’s query pattern to be similarly distributed to that of the honest verifier, using an object called a locking scheme. Kilian, Petrank and Tardos use this transformation to prove that
\mathsf{NEXP}
has SZK-PCPs (with a polynomial-time verifier) that are zero knowledge against all polynomial-time malicious verifiers; i.e.,
\mathsf{SZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}[\mathrm{poly},\mathrm{poly}]=\mathsf{NEXP}
.

Unfortunately, locking schemes have two inherent drawbacks: they require the honest PCP verifier to be adaptive and they cannot achieve perfect zero knowledge. Another line of work, motivated by cryptographic applications, focuses on obtaining SZK-PCPs for NP with a
non-adaptive
honest verifier from leakage resilience. These results come with caveats, achieving either a weaker notion of zero knowledge known as
witness indistinguishability

[
IWY16
]
, or simulation against adversaries making only quadratically many more queries than the honest verifier
[
HVW22
]
.

The complexity landscape of perfect zero knowledge is subtle. As discussed above, we know that
\mathsf{PZK\text{-}MIP}=\mathsf{MIP}=\mathsf{NEXP}

[
BGKW88
]
, where
\mathsf{MIP}
is the class of languages with a multi-prover interactive proof. The quantum analogue of this result,
\mathsf{PZK\text{-}MIP}^{*}=\mathsf{MIP}^{*}=\mathsf{RE}
, is also known to hold
[
CFGS22
,
GSY19
]
. We know a similar result for interactive PCPs (IPCPs)
[
KR08
]
, an interactive generalisation of PCPs (and special case of IOPs):
\mathsf{PZK\text{-}IPCP}=\mathsf{IPCP}=\mathsf{NEXP}

[
CFS17
]
. For IPs, the picture is very different. We know that
\mathsf{PZK}\subseteq\mathsf{SZK}\subseteq\mathsf{AM}\cap\mathsf{coAM}

[
For87
,
AH91
]
, and so it is unlikely that
\mathsf{PZK}=\mathsf{IP}
(
=\mathsf{PSPACE}
), or even
\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{PZK}
. It is unknown whether
\mathsf{PZK}=\mathsf{SZK}
; indeed, it was recently shown that there is an oracle relative to which this equality does not hold
[
BCHTV20
]
.

In contrast,
nothing at all
is known about the class
\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}
except for the trivial inclusion
\mathsf{BPP}\subseteq\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}
. In particular, the following key question in the study of perfect zero knowledge remains open:

Do perfect zero-knowledge PCPs exist for
any
non-trivial language?

1.1
Our results

In this work, we give strong positive answer to this question, showing that there exist perfect zero-knowledge PCPs (PZK-PCPs) for all of
\#\mathsf{P}
.

Theorem 1

(Informally stated, see
Theorem

8.1
)
.

\#\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}[\mathrm{poly},\mathrm{poly}]
.

We prove
1
by constructing a PZK-PCP for the
\#\mathsf{P}
-complete language

\#\mathsf{SAT}=\{(\Phi,N):\text{$\Phi$ is a CNF, }\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Phi(x)=N\}.

This is the first construction of a PZK-PCP for a language (believed to be) outside
\mathsf{BPP}
. Furthermore, unlike previous constructions of zero-knowledge PCPs, our construction simultaneously achieves
non-adaptivity
for the honest verifier and zero knowledge against arbitrary (adaptive) polynomial-time malicious verifiers. We stress that
1
is unconditional and does not rely on any cryptographic assumptions.

Remark 1.1
.

As with its IP counterpart
\mathsf{PZK}

\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}
is a class of

problems, and so the inclusion
\#\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}
refers to the decision version of
\#\mathsf{P}
(for which
\#\mathsf{SAT}
is complete). This class contains
\mathsf{UP}
and
\mathsf{coNP}
(in a natural way) but may not contain
\mathsf{NP}
. Toda’s theorem, that
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{P}^{\#\mathsf{P}}
, does not directly imply that
\mathsf{PH}\subseteq\mathsf{PZK}\text{-}\mathsf{PCP}
, as the
\#\mathsf{P}
oracle in this inclusion is a function oracle.

On the way to proving
1
, we solve the following general algebraic-algorithmic problem, which we consider to be of independent interest.

Given oracle access to a function

of

\tilde{f}
drawn uniformly at random from the set of

2
Techniques

We start by outlining how to construct (non-ZK) PCPs for
\#\mathsf{SAT}
via the sumcheck protocol
[
LFKN92
]
. Recall that the prover and verifier in the sumcheck protocol have oracle access to an
m
-variate polynomial
P
(the arithmetisation of
\Phi
) of individual degree
d
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}
, and the prover wishes to convince the verifier that
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P(\vec{a})=\gamma
for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{F}
. This is achieved via a
2m
-message protocol, in which for every
i\in[m]
,

•

the
2i
-th message, sent by the verifier, is a uniformly random challenge
c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
; and

•

the
(2i-1)
-th message, sent by the prover, is the univariate polynomial

g_{i}(X)=\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i-1},X,a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m}).

The verifier accepts if

(a)

g_{1}(0)+g_{1}(1)=\gamma
;

(b)

for each
1\leq i\leq m-1
,
g_{i}(c_{i})=g_{i+1}(0)+g_{i+1}(1)
; and

(c)

g_{m}(c_{m})=P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})
.

This protocol is complete and sound for sufficiently large
\mathbb{F}
. Moreover, we can “unroll” the interactive sumcheck protocol into an (exponentially large) PCP by writing down the prover’s answers to each possible (sub-)sequence of challenges.

Unfortunately, the sumcheck PCP is
clearly not zero knowledge
: even the prover’s first message
g_{1}
reveals information about
P
which is
\#\mathsf{P}
-hard to compute. In this overview, we will explain how to modify the sumcheck PCP in order to achieve perfect zero knowledge.

Zero knowledge IOPs.

Our approach to constructing a PZK-PCP for sumcheck is inspired by the
[
BCFGRS17
]
construction of a perfect zero knowledge sumcheck in the
interactive oracle proof
(IOP) model. The IOP model is an interactive generalisation of PCPs, in which the prover and verifier interact across multiple rounds, with the prover sending a PCP oracle in each round.

In their IOP, the prover first sends the evaluation table of a uniformly random
m
-variate polynomial
R
such that
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}R(\vec{a})=0
, which will be used as a mask. Next, to ensure soundness in case
R
does not sum to zero, the verifier sends a challenge
\alpha
. Finally, the prover sends a sumcheck PCP for the statement
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\alpha P(\vec{a})+R(\vec{a})=\alpha\gamma
.

Intuitively, this protocol does not leak much information about
P
, because
\alpha P+R
is a
uniformly random
polynomial that sums to
\alpha\gamma
.
[
BCFGRS17
]
shows, using techniques from algebraic complexity theory, that this IOP is indeed perfect zero knowledge; we will make use of these techniques later in our construction.

Unfortunately, the interaction in this protocol is crucial in order to balance soundness and zero knowledge. Indeed, one could imagine “unrolling” this IOP into a PCP in the same way as for sumcheck itself: writing down, for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
, a sumcheck PCP
\Pi_{\alpha}
for
\alpha P+R
. This preserves soundness, but completely breaks zero knowledge: since the sumcheck PCP is a linear function of the underlying polynomial, given any
two

\Pi_{\alpha},\Pi_{\alpha^{\prime}}
for distinct
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}
, we can recover the sumcheck PCP for
P
as
(\Pi_{\alpha}-\Pi_{\alpha^{\prime}})/(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})
.

On the other hand, if we have the prover send only
one

\Pi_{\alpha}
, then soundness is lost, as the prover can easily cheat by sending
R
that does not sum to zero. We could attempt to fix the soundness issue by having the prover additionally
prove
, via another sumcheck PCP
\Pi_{R}
, that
\sum_{\vec{a}}R(\vec{a})=0
. While this would restore soundness, we once again lose zero knowledge, as the sumcheck PCP for
P
can be recovered as
(\Pi_{\alpha}-\Pi_{R})/\alpha
.

2.1
Structure versus randomness

From the above discussion, we see that the central obstacle to obtaining a zero knowledge PCP is that the prover can break soundness by sending some
R
with
\sum_{\vec{a}}R(\vec{a})\neq 0
, and known methods for detecting this strategy (without interaction) break zero knowledge.

Our approach is to
prevent
this malicious prover strategy by choosing a polynomial
R
so that
\sum_{\vec{a}}R(\vec{a})=0

by definition
. Of course, we must take care to ensure that
R
still hides certain information about
P
. But what kind of information? We observe that, without loss of generality, we can view the sumcheck PCP for
P+R
as an oracle
\Pi
such that

\Pi(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})=\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}(P+R)(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})~,

for all
i\in[m]
and
c_{1},\ldots,c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
. Computing the “subcube sum”
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}P(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})
requires
2^{m-i}
queries to
P
. Hence, in order to hope to simulate, we must ensure that
R
hides such subcube sums for any
1\leq i<m-O(\log m)
. So, at a minimum, it should be the case that for all such
i
, and all
c_{1},\ldots,c_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
, the subcube sum
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}R(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})
is marginally uniform.

In short:
(1) for soundness, we need that the
full
sum of the masking polynomial
R
over the hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
is fixed, and
(2) for zero knowledge, other than sum over the hypercube, we would like
R
to be distributed as random as possible.

We start by ensuring that any
partial
subcube sum of
R
is distributed uniformly at random. Our key observation here is that while the full sum is
invariant
under any reordering of the variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, any nontrivial subcube sum (
i<m
) is
not
. This leads us to the following choice for the masking polynomial:

R(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\ {:=}\ Q(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m})-Q(X_{m},X_{m-1},\ldots,X_{1})~,

where
Q
is a uniformly random
m
-variate polynomial of individual degree
d
.

Clearly, by the permutation invariance of the complete sum,
\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}R(\vec{a})=0
. On the other hand, consider the special case of a subcube sum of
R
on the hypercube, i.e., for
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})\in\{0,1\}^{i}
:

\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}Q(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})-\sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}Q(a_{m},\ldots,a_{i+1},c_{i},\ldots,c_{1})~.

This expression can only be identically zero for all
Q
if
\{c_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{c_{i}\}\times\{0,1\}^{m-i}=\{0,1\}^{m-i}\times\{c_{i}\}\times\cdots\times\{c_{1}\}
, which is clearly only possible if
i\in\{0,m\}
. By linearity, if this subcube sum is not identically zero for all
Q
, then it is uniform when
Q
is uniform.

Still, compared to the IOP case, the masking polynomial
R
has extra structure: it satisfies an “antisymmetry”
R(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})=-R(\alpha_{m},\ldots,\alpha_{1})
for all
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{F}^{m}
. This structure means that
P+R
is no longer uniformly random and may leak nontrivial information about
P
.

To mitigate this, we modify
R
to make it “as random as possible” while preserving antisymmetry on
\{0,1\}^{m}
and low-degree structure over
\mathbb{F}^{m}
. Our final choice of
R
is as follows:

R(\vec{X})=Q(\vec{X})-Q(\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}(1-X_{i})T_{i}(\vec{X})~,

(1)

where
Q
is as before,
\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}}\ {:=}\ (X_{m},\ldots,X_{1})
, and each
T_{i}
is a uniformly random
m
-variate polynomial where
\deg_{X_{i}}(T_{i})=d-2
and
\deg_{X_{j}}(T_{i})=d
for
j\neq i
. What is this extra term? It was observed by
[
CCGOS23
]
, via the combinatorial nullstellensatz
[
Alo99
]
, that
Z(\vec{X})\ {:=}\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}(1-X_{i})T_{i}(\vec{X})
is a uniformly random polynomial subject to the condition that, for all
\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,
Z(\vec{a})=0
. Adding this to
R
retains the antisymmetric structure on the hypercube, but has the effect of “masking out” any structure that appears outside of
\{0,1\}^{m}
; in particular,
R
is no longer antisymmetric over all of
\mathbb{F}^{m}
.

Key challenge: local simulation of combinatorial and algebraic structure.

Showing the completeness and soundness of the PCP obtained via the foregoing approach is straightforward. Hence, it remains to prove that the perfect zero-knowledge condition holds.

Starting with the trivial case, note that our observation about subcube sums in the discussion above provides a simple strategy for simulating any
single
query
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})

within the hypercube

\{0,1\}^{i}
to
\Pi
. Alas, this clearly does not suffice, as even the honest sumcheck verifier makes multiple queries to the proof, for
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})\notin\{0,1\}^{i}
, Hence, we must simulate multiple queries beyond the hypercube.

Indeed, the simulation of multiple queries to the low-degree extension of the hypercube is where the key challenge arises. More specifically, the difficulty is that our masking polynomial
R
has both
algebraic
structure arising from the polynomial degree bound and
combinatorial
structure arising from the antisymmeric reordering of the variables. In order to simulate, we must not only understand both types of structure, but crucially, also
how they interact
.

In the remainder of this proof overview, we explain how we design an efficient simulator that can simulate responses to any number of queries to
\Pi
(the sumcheck PCP for
P+R
), over the entire space
\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
. Towards this end, we introduce the notion of
locally-simulatable encodings
.

Loosely speaking, locally-simulatable encodings are randomised encodings in which every local view of the encoding can be efficiently sampled from a local view of the message. More precisely, we say that a randomised encoding function
\mathsf{ENC}\colon(H\to\mathbb{F})\to(D\to\mathbb{F})
is locally simulatable if there is an algorithm
\mathcal{S}
which, given oracle access to a message
m\colon H\to\mathbb{F}
and a set
S\subseteq D
, samples from the distribution of
\mathsf{ENC}(m)|_{S}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{S}
in time polynomial in
|S|
(which may be much less than
|H|
).

The notion of locally-simulatable encodings is tightly connected to ZKPCPs: we can view the mapping from instances to corresponding PCPs as a randomised encoding. A local simulator for this encoding is a zero knowledge simulator for the PCP system.

2.2
Combinatorial structure of antisymmetric functions

As discussed above, the response to any
single

(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})\in\{0,1\}^{i}
to
\Pi
can be perfectly simulated. In this section, we explain how to simulate any number of queries to
\Pi
, provided that those queries lie in the set
\{0,1\}^{\leq m}\ {:=}\ \cup_{i=1}^{m}\{0,1\}^{i}
. Note that evaluated over the hypercube, the last term of
Eq.

1
cancels, hence we can think of the masking polynomials as
R(\vec{X})=Q(\vec{X})-Q(\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}})
, where
\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}}\ {:=}\ (X_{m},\ldots,X_{1})
.

Observe that, when restricted to
\{0,1\}^{m}
, the distribution of
R
is well structured; namely, it is a uniformly random element of the vector space
\mathrm{AntiSym}
of ‘‘antisymmetric functions’’
2
2

2

This is terminology that we introduce, which is distinct from the usual notion of antisymmetry for polynomials.
, i.e., functions
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}
such that for all
\vec{c}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
,
f(\vec{c})=-f(\vec{c}_{\mathsf{rev}})
, where
\vec{c}_{\mathsf{rev}}=(c_{m},\ldots,c_{1})
is the reverse of
\vec{c}
. We can view the restriction of
\Pi
to
\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
as the output of a randomised encoding function
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}\colon(\{0,1\}^{m}\to\mathbb{F})\to(\{0,1\}^{m}\to\mathbb{F})
, applied to the restriction of
P
to
\{0,1\}^{m}
. The function
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}
has the following description.

\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}(f)
:

1.

Choose a uniformly random
r\in\mathrm{AntiSym}
.

2.

Output the function
\Sigma[f+r]
, given by

\Sigma[f+r](c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})\ {:=}\ \sum_{\vec{a}\in\{0,1\}^{m-i}}(f+r)(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i},a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{m})~,

(2)

for all
i\in[m]
; i.e., the function
f+r
, augmented with all of its subcube sums.

In this perspective, our task is now to provide a local simulator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}
. That is, for a query set
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
, we want to efficiently simulate
\Sigma[f+r]|_{S}
for a uniformly random
r\in\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}
, given oracle access to
f
.

Let
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{S}
denote the vector space
\{\Sigma[r]|_{S}:r\in\mathrm{AntiSym}\}
, and let
B
be a basis for its dual code
(\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{S})^{\perp}
; that is,
v\in\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{S}
if and only if
Bv=0
. Then, by linearity,
\Sigma[f+r]|_{S}
is distributed as a uniformly random vector
w\colon S\to\mathbb{F}
such that
Bw=B(\Sigma[f]|_{S})
.

With this in mind, our local simulator
\mathcal{S}
could work as follows.

\mathcal{S}^{f}(S)
:

1.

Compute a basis
B
for
(\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{S})^{\perp}
.

2.

Compute
y=B(\Sigma[f]|_{S})
by querying
f
as necessary.

3.

Output a random
w
such that
Bw=y
.

Correctness is straightforward. The only potential issue is efficiency: evaluating
\Sigma[f]
at a point in
S
may require exponentially many queries to
f
. Note, however, that if the column of
B
corresponding to
\vec{c}\ {:=}\ (c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})
is all zero, then we do not need to know

2.3
Local simulation of random low-degree extensions

In the previous section we addressed the
combinatorial
problem of simulating queries to
\Pi
that lie in the set
\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
, which exhibits antisymmetric strucutre. In this section we consider the
algebraic
problem of simulating queries to
\Pi
that also lie outside of the hypercube (i.e., general point queries), which exhibits pseudorandom structure. We then bring these two parts together in
Section

2.4
.

Recall that our choice of
R
is

R(\vec{X})=Q(\vec{X})-Q(\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}(1-X_{i})T_{i}(\vec{X})~,

where
Q
is as before,
\vec{X}_{\mathsf{rev}}\ {:=}\ (X_{m},\ldots,X_{1})
, and each
T_{i}
is a uniformly random
m
-variate polynomial where
\deg_{X_{i}}(T_{i})=d-2
and
\deg_{X_{j}}(T_{i})=d
for
j\neq i
.

Denote by
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}
the
Reed–Muller
code of evaluations of
m
-variate polynomials of individual degree
d
over
\mathbb{F}
. For a function

, denote by
\mathsf{LD}[f,d]\ {:=}\ \{\hat{f}\in\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]:\hat{f}(x)=f(x)\,\forall\vec{x}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\}
the set of
degree-

extensions
of

. With the above modification, we can describe the sumcheck PCP for
P+R
, restricted to
\mathbb{F}^{m}
, as the output of the following randomised encoding:

\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}\mathrm{AntiSym}}(P)
:

1.

Sample a random antisymmetric function
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}
.

2.

Sample
R
uniformly at random from
\mathsf{LD}[f,d]
.

3.

Output
P+R
.

Our problem now becomes to design a local simulator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}\mathrm{AntiSym}}
.

We will do this by solving a much more general problem:
we prove that random low-degree extensions are locally simulatable.
More precisely, let
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}}^{d}(f)
be the encoding function that outputs a uniformly random element of
\mathsf{LD}[f,d]
. We prove that
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}}^{d}
has a local simulator
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{RM}}^{d}
for any
d\geq 2
.
We can then easily obtain a simulator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}\mathrm{AntiSym}}
by composing
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{RM}}
with the local simulator for
\mathrm{AntiSym}
described in the previous section.

Our starting point.
We will build on the “stateful emulator” that was constructed in
[
CCGOS23
]
(for a particular oracle model that they introduced), which can be conceptualised as an
inefficient
local simulator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}}
.
To describe it, we first introduce some notation.

For
w\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, we denote by
\delta_{w}
the unique multilinear polynomial satisfying
\delta_{w}(w)=1
and
\delta_{w}(x)=0
for all
x\in\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus\{w\}
. For a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
, we say that
w
is
S
-good
if there exists an
m
-variate polynomial
q_{w}
of individual degree at most

such that

(i)

q_{w}(x)=0
for every
x\in\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus\{w\}
;

(ii)

q_{w}(z)=0
for every

; and

(iii)

q_{w}(w)=1

. We say that

is
S
-bad
if it is not
S
-good. Intuitively, when
\hat{f}
is a uniformly random low-degree extension of

,
\hat{f}|_{S}
only conveys information about

for

-bad points

. For example, any point in
\{0,1\}^{m}\cap S
is trivially
S
-bad. Less trivially, if
S
consists of sufficiently many points on a curve passing through

, then
w
may be
S
-bad even if
\{0,1\}^{m}\cap S
is empty.

The simulator of Chen et al. works roughly as follows:

However, they leave open the question of whether

. The issue is in

from

5

5

Chen et al. follow a different route: roughly, they show that because their

for any

is zero on

Step 1: solving the decision problem.
Our first step is to consider the
decision
variant of this problem: given a set
S
, does there
exist
an
S
-bad

? Our key insight here is that we can relate the existence of an
S
-bad

to the dimension of the vector space
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}

Z
is the constant zero function. We show that all

are

-good if and only if
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}=\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{S}
. Indeed, if every
w
is
S
-good, then for any degree-
d
polynomial

, the polynomial
P-\sum_{w\in\{0,1\}^{m}}P(w)\cdot q_{w}
is a degree-

extension of

that agrees with
P
on
S
. On the other hand, if
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}=\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{S}
, then for every

, there is a
\hat{Z}_{w}\in\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]
that agrees with
\delta_{w}
on

; hence we can take
q_{w}=\delta_{w}-\hat{Z}_{w}
, and so

is
S
-good.

Hence to solve the decision problem it suffices to compute (the dimensions of)
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{S}
and
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}
. The former can be achieved efficiently (in time
\mathrm{poly}(|S|,m,d,\log|\mathbb{F}|)
) using the
succinct constraint detector
for Reed–Muller discovered by
[
BCFGRS17
]
.

To compute the latter, we build on ideas introduced in
[
CCGOS23
]
. As discussed above, in that work the authors observe that to sample a vector

, it suffices to (lazily) sample random polynomials

,

, of the appropriate degrees, and then set
v(\vec{\alpha})\ {:=}\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}(1-\alpha_{i})T_{i}(\vec{\alpha})
for each
\vec{\alpha}\in S
. Similarly, we show how to compute a basis for
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}
by combining bases for the subspaces
\mathcal{P}_{i}\ {:=}\ \{T_{i}|_{S}\in\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},d,m]|_{S}:\deg_{X_{i}}(T_{i})=d-2\}

. These bases can also be computed using the succinct constraint detector for Reed–Muller; see

Step 2: search-to-decision reduction.
Next, we will build upon the techniques we developed in Step 1 to solve the original search problem. A natural strategy is to employ a binary search: for each
b\in\{0,1\}
, test if
\{b\}\times\{0,1\}^{m-1}
contains any
S
-bad points, and recurse if it does. Since the number of
S
-bad points is bounded by
|S|
, the recursion will terminate quickly. It therefore suffices to give an efficient algorithm that, given a set
A
of the form
\{a_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{a_{i}\}\times\{0,1\}^{m-i}
, determines whether
A
contains any
S
-bad point.

For
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
, let
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d]|_{S}\ {:=}\ \{P\in\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]:P(\vec{x})=0\,\forall\vec{x}\in A\}
. Our algorithm for computing a basis for
\mathsf{LD}[Z,d]|_{S}
can be straightforwardly extended to compute a basis for
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d]|_{S}
. We can also extend the reasoning from Step 1 to show that if every
w\in A
is
S
-good, then
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d]|_{S}=\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{S}
. Unfortunately, the converse does not hold: it may be that
A
contains
S
-bad points but
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d]|_{S}=\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{S}
. This can happen, for example, if
S
contains points on a curve passing through both
w\in A
and
w^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus A
. In particular, the argument from Step 1 fails: for
\hat{Z}_{w}\in\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d]
,
\delta_{w}-\hat{Z}_{w}
is not necessarily zero on
\{0,1\}^{m}\setminus A
.

To obtain our final algorithm, we will instead exploit the algebraic relationship between
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]
and its subcode
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d-1]
to derive a
sufficient
condition for every
w\in A
being
S
-good. Observe that if
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d-1]|_{S}=\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d-1]|_{S}
, then for every
w\in A
there is a
\hat{Z}_{w}\in\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d-1]
such that
p_{w}\ {:=}\ \delta_{w}-\hat{Z}_{w}
satisfies
p_{w}(w)=1
and
p_{w}(\vec{\alpha})=0
for all
\vec{\alpha}\in S
. It follows that all
w\in A
are
S
-good, as we can set
q_{w}=p_{w}\cdot\delta_{w}
.

Thus, our efficient test for whether
A
contains any
S
-bad point is as follows: compute bases for
\mathsf{LD}[Z_{A},d-1]|_{S}
and
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d-1]|_{S}
, and output “no” if they are of the same dimension; otherwise output “maybe”. By the above discussion, this test does not have any false negatives; however, there may be false positives. Provided there are not too many false positives, this does not cause a problem (we can either include them in
W
or perform an extra test to filter them out). We bound the number of false positives by noting that if the test says “maybe”, this in fact means that there exists an “
S
-bad” point in
A
with respect to polynomials of degree
d-1
. By
[
AW09
]
, provided
d\geq 2
, there are at most
|S|
such points.

2.4
Local simulation of subcube sums of LDEs

So far, we have built a simulator that can answer arbitrary queries to our sumcheck PCP
\Pi
, provided those queries lie within the set
\{0,1\}^{\leq m}\cup\mathbb{F}^{m}
, handling the combinatorial structure on the hypercube and the algebraic, pseudorandom algebraic structure outside of it. In this final part of the overview, we outline how this simulator can be extended to handle all queries; i.e., queries in the set
\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
.

To do this, it suffices to show that the code
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
admits a locally-simulatable encoding, where
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]=\{\Sigma[P]:P\in\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]\}
and
\Sigma[P]
is defined as in
Eq.

2
(where
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{i})
now ranges over
\mathbb{F}^{i}
). Specifically, we show that the following encoding function is locally simulatable:

\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}}^{d}(\Sigma[f]):

1.

Sample
\hat{f}
uniformly at random from
\mathsf{LD}[f,d]
.

2.

Output
\Sigma[\hat{f}]
.

Note that the
message
(input to
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}}
) is
\Sigma[f]
, even though
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}}
operates only on
f
. This is necessary for local simulation, since individual locations in
\Sigma[\hat{f}]
depend on partial sums of
f
which cannot be computed from few queries to
f
itself.

We would like to follow the strategy from the previous section: given a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
on which we want to simulate
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}}(\Sigma[f])
, compute the set of all “bad” points
W\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
, i.e.,
w\in W
if
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}}(\Sigma[f])|_{S}
conveys information about
\Sigma[f](w)
. Unfortunately, unlike in the “plain” Reed–Muller case, it is not clear how to even bound the
size
of
W
, let alone compute it.

The issue here is that a single evaluation of
\Sigma[\hat{f}](\vec{\alpha})
for
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{i}
depends on
\hat{f}(\vec{\beta})
for every point
\vec{\beta}\in\vec{\alpha}\times\{0,1\}^{m-i}
. Naively applying the lemma of
[
AW09
]
to this set of points yields a set
W\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
of size
2^{m-i}
. To bound
|W|
by a polynomial, we must therefore crucially make use of the fact that making a few queries to
\Sigma[\hat{f}]
can reveal only a few (possibly large)
partial sums
of
\hat{f}
— which we can hope to deduce from a small number of queries to
\Sigma[f]
.

To achieve this we will give a
decomposition
of
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
as a sequence of
\mathrm{RM}
codes on different numbers of variables, “tied together” with constraints that enforce summation structure.
In more detail, let
T\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
be a set with the following special structure, which we call “closed”: if
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i})\in S
, then its “parent”
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1})
and its “siblings”
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},0)
and
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{i-1},1)
are also in
S
. The set
\{0,1\}^{\leq m}
is closed; it is also easy to see that any set
T
can be made closed by adding at most
3m|T|
points. We prove the following theorem about the structure of restrictions of
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
to closed sets
T
:

Theorem 2.1

(Informally stated, see
Theorem

6.5
)
.

For any closed set
T
, any constraint
z\in(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{T})^{\perp}
lies in the span of the following two types of constraint:

•

summation constraints
, which ensure that
\Sigma[\hat{f}](t_{1},\ldots,t_{i})=\Sigma[\hat{f}](t_{1},\ldots,t_{i},0)+\Sigma[\hat{f}](t_{1},\ldots,t_{i},1)
for
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{i})\in T
,
i<m
; and

•

low-degree constraints
, which are given by, for each
i
, the
i
-variate Reed–Muller constraints on the set
T_{i}\ {:=}\ T\cap\mathbb{F}^{i}
; i.e.,
(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,d]|_{T_{i}})^{\perp}
.

If we take
T=\{0,1\}^{\leq m}\cup S
, then
T_{i}=\{0,1\}^{i}\cup(S\cap\mathbb{F}^{i})
(assuming
S
is closed). Using this decomposition, we show that we can take as the “bad” set
W\ {:=}\ \bigcup_{i=1}^{m}W_{i}
, where
W_{i}
is the set of
(S\cap\mathbb{F}^{i})
-bad points in
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,d]
. Each
W_{i}
can be computed efficiently using the algorithm described in
Section

2.3
.

We conclude by giving some brief intuition on how we prove
Theorem

2.1
. We show that any constraint
z
supported on
T_{i}\cup T_{i+1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{m}
can be “flattened” into a constraint supported on
T_{i}
only. Such constraints belong to
(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,d]|_{T_{i}})^{\perp}
. We then use a dimension-counting argument to show that these, augmented with the summation constraints, span
(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]|_{T})^{\perp}
.

\mathbb{F}

m\in\mathbb{N}
. Given
S_{1},\dots S_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
and a point

, we define the
Lagrange interpolating polynomial for

and

L_{\mathcal{S},w}(x)\ {:=}\ \prod_{i=1}^{m}\prod_{z\in S_{i}\setminus\{w_{i}\}}\frac{x_{i}-z_{i}}{w_{i}-z_{i}}\kern 5.0pt,

where denote
\mathcal{S}\ {:=}\ S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{m}
.
L_{\mathcal{S},w}
is of individual degree
|S_{i}|-1
in its

-th variable.

Fact 3.1
.

The set of Lagrange interpolators

form a basis for the space
\mathbb{F}^{\leq\vec{d}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
where
\vec{d}\ {:=}\ (|S_{1}|-1,\dots,|S_{m}|-1)
.

Vanishing functions.
Given a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}
we denote the function which is vanishing on

by
\mathcal{Z}_{S}(x)\ {:=}\ \prod_{s\in S}(x-s)
.

Vector reversal.
For a vector
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})

3.1
Coding theory

Let
\Sigma
an alphabet and let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. A
code

C
is a subset
C\subseteq\Sigma^{\ell}
. Given two strings
x,y\in\Sigma^{n}
, we denote the Hamming distance between
x
and
y
by
\Delta(x,y)\ {:=}\ \left|\{i\in[n]:x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}\right|
. We say that a vector
x
is
\varepsilon
-far from a set
S\subseteq\Sigma^{n}
if
\min_{y\in S}\Delta(x,y)/n\geq\varepsilon
.

Definition 3.2
.

Given a function
w\colon D\to\mathbb{F}
and a subset
I\subseteq D
, we denote by
w|_{I}
the restriction of
w
to
I
, that is the function
w|_{I}\colon I\to\mathbb{F}
such that
w(x)=w|_{I}(x)
for all
x\in I
.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a linear code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
and a subset
I\subseteq D
, we denote by
C|_{I}

the code consisting of codewords from
C
restricted to
I
, that is

C|_{I}=\{w|_{I}:w\in C\}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that
C|_{I}
is itself a linear code, as it is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{I}
.

Linear codes.
Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field. A code
C\colon\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
is
linear
if it is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear map from
\mathbb{F}^{k}
to
\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
.

Reed-Muller codes and low-degree extensions.
The Reed-Muller (RM) code is the code consisting of evaluations of multivariate low-degree polynomials over a finite field.

Definition 3.4
.

Given a field
\mathbb{F}
, a positive integer
m
, and a degree vector
\vec{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we denote by
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
the code of evaluations over
\mathbb{F}^{m}
of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}^{\leq\vec{d}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,d]\ {:=}\ \mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,(d,\ldots,d)]
.

Zero and sum codes.
A
zero code
is a subcode of a linear code that is zero on a given subset.

Definition 3.5

(Zero codes)
.

Given a
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
, and
S\subseteq D
, we define the
zero code

\mathbf{Z}_{S}(C)=\{w\in C:w|_{S}=\vec{0}\}.

A
sum code
is the extension of a linear code obtained by appending all sums of entries over a given subdomain.

Definition 3.6

(Sum codes)
.

For a product set
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
, we define
\mathcal{A}_{i}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots\times A_{i}
and
\mathcal{A}_{>i}\ {:=}\ A_{i+1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
;
\mathcal{A}_{0}\ {:=}\ \{\bot\}
. We denote
\bar{\mathcal{A}}\ {:=}\ \cup_{i=0}^{m}\mathcal{A}_{i}
.

Given a code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
where
D=D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{m}
and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq D
, we define the
sum code

\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\bar{D}}
to be the code consisting of all of the subcube sums over
\mathcal{A}
of the codewords in
C
. That is,

\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}C\ {:=}\ \{\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[w]:w\in C\}

where
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[w]\colon\mathbb{F}^{D}\to\mathbb{F}^{\bar{D}}
is given by

\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[w](\vec{x})\ {:=}\ \sum_{\vec{y}\in\mathcal{A}_{>\ell(\vec{x})}}w(\vec{x},\vec{y})~.

If
D=\mathcal{A}
, we will typically omit
\mathcal{A}
from the notation (i.e., we write
\Sigma C
,
\Sigma[w]
).

3.2
Linear-algebraic claims

We will make use of the following simple linear-algebraic algorithms and facts.

Claim 3.7
.

Let
U\leq\mathbb{F}^{n}
be a vector space. There is a polynomial-time algorithm which, given a basis for
U
, computes a basis for
U^{\perp}
.

Claim 3.8
.

Let
U\leq\mathbb{F}^{n},V\leq\mathbb{F}^{m}
be vector spaces. There is a polynomial-time algorithm which, given a linear map
M\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}
such that
\{Mu:u\in U\}=V
and basis
B\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
for
U^{\perp}
, outputs a basis for
V^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Use
3.7
to compute a basis
B^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times(n-k)}
for
U
. Then compute
A=MB^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times(n-k)}
; we have that
V=\colspan(A)
by assumption on
M
. Compute a basis for
V
from
A
by Gaussian elimination, and from this a basis for
V^{\perp}
by
3.7
.
∎

Claim 3.9
.

Let
T:V\to W
be a linear map. If
v\sim\mathcal{U}(V)
, then
T(v)\sim\mathcal{U}(T(V))
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\Pr_{v\leftarrow V}[T(v)=w]=\Pr_{v\leftarrow V}[v\in\ker(T)+w]=\frac{|\ker(T)|}{|V|}=\frac{1}{|T(V)|}.

∎

Definition 3.10
.

Let
(D,<)
be an ordered domain. For a vector
v\colon D\to\mathbb{F}
, we define
\lambda(v)\ {:=}\ \min\{i\in D:v(i)\neq 0\}
, where the minimum is taken with respect to
<
. A sequence of vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\colon D\to\mathbb{F}
is in
echelon form
if, for all
i<j
,
\lambda(v_{i})<\lambda(v_{j})
, and any all-zero vectors are at the end of the sequence.

It is a straightforward consequence of Gauss–Jordan elimination that every subspace of
(D\to\mathbb{F})
has a basis in echelon form. Conversely, a sequence of vectors in echelon form (not containing the all-zero vector) forms a linearly independent set.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
(R,<_{R})
and
(C,<_{C})
be ordered domains and let
A\in\mathbb{F}^{R\times C}
. The
leading entry
of a row
w\in\mathbb{F}^{C}
of
A
is
w(\lambda(w))
, i.e., the first non-zero entry in that row, with respect to the ordering
<_{C}
.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
(R,<_{R})
and
(C,<_{C})
be ordered domains. A matrix
A\in\mathbb{F}^{R\times C}
is said to be in
reduced row echelon form
if

(i)

the columns of
A
, listed in increasing order by their indices with respect to
<_{C}
are in row echelon form (with respect to
<_{D}
);

(ii)

all leading entries are 1; and

(iii)

all entries in the same column as a leading entry are 0.

Every matrix can be converted to reduced row echelon form via Gauss-Jordan elimination, and moreover, the resulting matrix is unique. We will often represent systems of linear equations in their matrix form. We refer to variables with a leading entry in their column of the reduced row echelon form matrix as
leading variables
, and the remaining variables as
free variables
.

3.3
The combinatorial nullstellensatz

The combinatorial nullstellensatz, due to Alon
[
Alo99
]
, is a powerful tool in combinatorial number theory. It is stated as follows.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be an arbitrary field, and let
f
be a polynomial in
\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
be nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{F}
and define
\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}}(x_{i})=\prod_{s\in S_{i}}(x_{i}-s)
. If
f
vanishes over all the common zeros of
\mathcal{Z}_{S_{1}},\ldots,\mathcal{Z}_{S_{n}}
(that is, if
f(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})=0
for all
s_{i}\in S_{i}
), then there are polynomials
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]
so that
\deg(h_{i})\leq\deg(f)-\deg(\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}})
so that

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{i}\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}}\kern 5.0pt.

4
Locally simulatable encodings

In this section we introduce the notion of locally simulatable encodings. First, we define randomised encoding functions, which are randomised mappings from a message space
\mathcal{M}
into a code
\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 4.1

(Randomised encoding function)
.

Let
\Sigma
be an alphabet,
S,D
be sets, and
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\Sigma^{S},\mathcal{C}\subseteq\Sigma^{D}
be the
message space
and
code space
, respectively. A
randomised encoding function
is a random variable
\mathsf{ENC}
taking values in
\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
.

Next, we define what it means for a randomised encoding function to be locally simulatable. At a high level, we require that the (conditional) distribution of any local view of the encoding can be efficiently simulated using only a local view of the message.

Definition 4.2

(Locally simulatable encoding)
.

Let
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
be a randomised encoding function, let
t\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
and let
\ell\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. We say that
\mathsf{ENC}
is
(t,\ell)
-locally simulatable
if there is a probabilistic algorithm
\mathcal{S}
, called a
local simulator
, which receives oracle access to a message
m\in\mathcal{M}
, a query-answer set
T\in(D\times\Sigma)^{n}
and a new query
\alpha\in D
, runs in time
t(n)
and makes at most
\ell(n)
queries to
m
, satisfying:

\Pr[\mathcal{S}^{m}(T,\alpha)=\beta]=\Pr\left[\mathsf{ENC}(m)_{\alpha}=\beta~|~\mathsf{ENC}(m)_{x}=y~\forall(x,y)\in T\right],

for all
m\in\mathcal{M},\alpha\in D,\beta\in\Sigma
, and for all
T
such that there exists
c\in\mathsf{supp}(\mathsf{ENC}_{m})
with
c_{x}=y
for all
(x,y)\in T
.

To give a sense of the definition, we consider two examples.

Example 4.3
.

The randomised encoding function which maps
m\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
,
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, to the evaluation of a random univariate polynomial
p
over
\mathbb{F}
of degree
2(|S|-1)
such that
p(x)=m(x)
for all
x\in S
, is
(\mathrm{poly}(n,d,\log|\mathbb{F}|),n)
-locally simulatable. Indeed, for
I\subseteq\mathbb{F}
,
|I|\leq|S|-1
,
p|_{I}
is distributed as
(m(x))_{x\in I\cap S}\|(\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{F}))_{x\in I\setminus S}
. For
|I|\geq|S|
, the simulator
\mathcal{S}
can simply fully determine
m
and directly conditionally sample
p
.

Example 4.4
.

The encoding function which maps
m\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
,
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, to the evaluation of the
unique
univariate polynomial
p
over
\mathbb{F}
of degree
|S|-1
such that
p(x)=m(x)
for all
x\in S
, is
not

(t,\ell)
-locally simulatable unless
\ell(n)\geq|S|
for all
n>0
. Indeed, determining
p(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{F}\setminus S
requires knowledge of the entire message
m
.

4.1
Linear randomised encoding functions

In this work we will focus on a special family of randomised encoding functions, for which the randomised encoding is given by appending a random vector to the message, and then applying a linear function.

Definition 4.5

(Linear randomised encoding function)
.

We say that
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
is a
linear randomised encoding function
if
\Sigma=\mathbb{F}
for some finite field
\mathbb{F}
, and
\mathsf{ENC}(m)
is distributed as
\mathsf{ENC}(m;\mu)
where
\mu\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{F}^{r})
and
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathcal{M}\times\mathbb{F}^{r}\to\mathcal{C}
is a (deterministic) linear function (of both message and randomness).

That is, we use
\mathsf{ENC}
to refer to both the fixed encoding function and the resulting random variable.

Linear randomised encodings have certain structural properties that make them easier to work with. One important property of linear randomised encodings is that the distribution of
\mathsf{ENC}(m)|_{I}
can be characterised as the solution space of an affine system. An
\ell
-constraint locator is an algorithm which outputs a description of this system, defined as follows.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
be a linear randomised encoding function, and let
\ell\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. We say that an algorithm
\mathsf{CL}
is an
\ell
-constraint locator for
\mathsf{ENC}
if, for every subdomain
I\subseteq D
, it satisfies

\mathsf{CL}(I)=(R,Z),

where
R\subseteq S
and
Z\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times(R\sqcup I)}
are such that
|R|=\ell(|I|)
, and for every message
m\in\mathcal{M}
and
\vec{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
,
(m|_{R},\vec{\beta})^{T}\in\ker(Z)\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{R\sqcup I}
if and only if there exists randomness
\mu\in\mathbb{F}^{r}
such that:

\mathsf{ENC}(m;\mu)|_{I}=\vec{\beta}.

Our notion of an
\ell
-constraint locator generalises the notion of a “constraint detector” introduced in
[
BCFGRS17
]
. A constraint detector for a linear code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
takes as input a subdomain
I\subseteq D
and outputs a matrix representing all linear relations that hold between codewords in
C|_{I}
. Setting the randomised encoding function for our
\ell
-constraint locator to be the uniform distribution over all codewords in
C
, we recover the definition of a constraint detector.

Definition 4.7

(Constraint detector)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
be a linear code. A
constraint detector for
\mathcal{C}
is a
0
-constraint locator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathcal{C}}\colon\varnothing\to\mathcal{C}
, the linear randomised encoding function given by
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathcal{C}}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{C})
.

Given an efficient constraint locator, it is straightforward to construct a local simulator.

Claim 4.8
.

Let
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{C}
be a linear randomised encoding with an
\ell
-constraint locator running in time
t
. Then
\mathsf{ENC}
is
(t,\ell)
-locally simulatable.

Construction 4.9
.

A local simulator
\mathcal{S}
for a linear randomised encoding function
\mathsf{ENC}\colon\mathbb{F}^{S}\to\mathbb{F}^{D}
given an
\ell
-constraint locator
\mathsf{CL}
for
\mathsf{ENC}
.

\mathcal{S}_{\mathsf{ENC}}^{m}(T,\alpha)
:

1.

Set
(R,Z)=\mathsf{CL}(\mathsf{supp}(T)\cup\{\alpha\})
.

2.

For each
\gamma\in R
, query the message and set
m_{\gamma}\ {:=}\ m(\gamma)
.

3.

If there exists a row
z\in\mathbb{F}^{R\cup\mathsf{supp}(T)\cup\{\alpha\}}
of the matrix
Z
with
z(\alpha)\neq 0
, output

\beta\ {:=}\ -\frac{1}{z(\alpha)}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{supp}(T)}y_{x}z(x)+\sum_{x\in R}m_{x}z(x)
, where
(x,y_{x})\in T
.

4.

Otherwise, output
\beta\leftarrow\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

First we introduce some notation. Denote
p_{\beta}\ {:=}\ \Pr[\mathsf{ENC}(m)_{\alpha}=\beta\mid\mathsf{ENC}(m)_{x}=y~\forall(x,y)\in T]
. Denote
I\ {:=}\ \mathsf{supp}(T)\cup\{\alpha\}
and denote
\kappa\ {:=}\ -\frac{1}{z(\alpha)}\sum_{x\in\mathsf{supp}(T)}y_{x}z(x)+\sum_{x\in R}m_{x}z(x)
. Define the vector
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathsf{supp}(T)}
to be the vector consisting of values
y_{x}
for each
(x,y_{x})\in T
.

We will break the proof into two parts, corresponding to the two possibilities for the output of the construction in
Item

3
and
Item

4
. First, we show that if there exists a row
z\in\mathbb{F}^{R\cup\mathsf{supp}(T)\cup\{\alpha\}}
of the matrix
Z
with
z(\alpha)\neq 0
(as in
Item

3
), then
p_{\beta}=1
if
\beta=\kappa
and
p_{\beta}=0
otherwise. If there exists such a row, then the vector
(\beta,\vec{y},m|_{R})\in\ker(Z)
if and only if
\beta=\kappa
. By the correctness of
\mathsf{CL}
,
(\beta,\vec{y},m|_{R})\in\ker(Z)
if and only if there exists randomness
\mu
such that
\mathsf{ENC}(m;\mu)|_{I}=(\beta,\vec{y})
. So for all randomness
\mu\in\mathbb{F}^{r}
,
\mathsf{ENC}(m;\mu)|_{I}=(\beta,\vec{y})
if and only if
\beta=\kappa
; hence
p_{\beta}=1
if
\beta=\kappa
and
p_{\beta}=0
otherwise.

Second, we show that if there does not exist a row
z
with
z(\alpha)\neq 0
(as in
Item

4
), then
p_{\beta}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{F}|}
, for all
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. For each
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
, consider the set of all possible choices of randomness which agree with
T\cup(\alpha,\beta)
, that is
Q_{\beta}\ {:=}\ \{\mu\in\mathbb{F}^{r}:\mathsf{ENC}(m,\mu)|_{\{\alpha\}\cup\mathsf{supp}(T)}=(\beta,\vec{y})\}
. We will show that
|Q_{\beta}|=|Q_{\beta^{\prime}}|
for each
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}
by giving a bijection, which implies that
p_{\beta}=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{F}|}
, since
\mu
is chosen uniformly at random.

In this case,
(\beta,\vec{y},m|_{R})\in\ker(Z)
for all
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. Then, by the correctness of
\mathsf{CL}
, for all
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
, there exists randomness
\mu\in\mathbb{F}^{r}
such that
\mathsf{ENC}(m,\mu)|_{I}=(\beta,\vec{y})
, that is,
Q_{\beta}
is non-empty for each
\beta\in\mathbb{F}
. Let
\mu_{\beta}\in Q_{\beta}
and let
\mu_{\beta^{\prime}}\in Q_{\beta^{\prime}}
. It is easy to verify that the map
M\colon Q_{\beta}\to Q_{\beta^{\prime}}
defined by
M(\mu)\ {:=}\ \mu+(\mu_{\beta^{\prime}}-\mu_{\beta})
is bijective, completing the proof.
∎

We prove that
\ell
-constraint locators satisfy a useful composition property. That is, given constraint locators for encodings
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}

\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}

\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}\circ\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}

Claim 4.10
.

Let
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}\colon\mathbb{F}^{S}\to\mathbb{F}^{D_{\text{in}}}
and
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}\colon\mathbb{F}^{D_{\text{in}}}\to\mathbb{F}^{D_{\text{out}}}

\ell_{\text{out}}
-constraint location respectively. Then
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}\circ\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}(M;\mu_{\text{in}},\mu_{\text{out}})\ {:=}\ \mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}(\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}(m;\mu_{\text{in}});\mu_{\text{out}})
admits
(\ell_{\text{in}}\circ\ell_{\text{out}})
-constraint location.

Construction 4.11
.

A constraint locator
\mathsf{CL}_{*}
for
\mathsf{ENC}^{*}
, which receives oracle access to the message
m
and is given constraint locators
\mathsf{CL}_{\text{in}}
and
\mathsf{CL}_{\text{out}}
for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}
and
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}
respectively. It receives as input a subdomain

\mathsf{CL}_{*}(I)

1.

Compute
(R_{\text{out}},Z_{\text{out}})\ {:=}\ \mathsf{CL}_{\text{out}}(I)
.

2.

Compute
(R_{\text{in}},Z_{\text{in}})\ {:=}\ \mathsf{CL}_{\text{in}}(R_{\text{out}})
.

3.

Define
Z_{*}
to the
(k_{\text{in}}+k_{\text{out}})\times(|I|+|R_{\text{out}}|+|R_{\text{in}}|)
matrix defined as

Z_{*}\ {:=}\ \left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}Z_{\text{out}}|_{I}&Z_{\text{out}}|_{R_{\text{out}}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&Z_{\text{in}}|_{R_{\text{out}}}&Z_{\text{in}}|_{R_{\text{in}}}\\
\end{array}\right),

where
k_{\text{in}}
and
k_{\text{out}}
are the number of rows in
Z_{\text{in}}
and
Z_{\text{out}}
respectively.

4.

Using Gaussian elimination, compute a basis
\mathcal{B}
for the space
V\ {:=}\ \ker(Z_{*})
.

5.

Compute a basis
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
for the space
(V|_{I\cup R_{\text{in}}})^{\perp}
, using
3.7
and
3.8
. Define
Z
to be the matrix whose rows are the elements of
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.

6.

Output
(R_{\text{in}},Z)
.

Proof.

Let
m\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
, and denote
w_{m}\ {:=}\ \mathsf{ENC}_{\text{in}}(m;\mu_{\text{in}})
and
c_{w}\ {:=}\ \mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}(w;\mu_{\text{out}})
.

By the correctness of
\mathsf{CL}_{\text{out}}
, it holds that
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
satisfies
(\vec{\alpha},w|_{R_{\text{out}}})\in\ker(Z_{\text{out}})
if and only if there exists randomness
\mu_{\text{out}}
such that for every message
w\in\mathbb{F}^{D_{\text{in}}}
,

. Similarly, we have that
\vec{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}^{R_{\text{out}}}
satisfies
(\vec{\beta},m|_{R_{\text{in}}})\in\ker(Z_{\text{in}})
if and only if there exists randomness
\mu_{\text{in}}
such that for every message
m\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
,
w_{m}|_{R_{\text{out}}}=\vec{\beta}
.

Then by definition of
Z_{*}
we must have that
(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},m|_{R_{\text{in}}})\in\ker(Z_{*})
if and only if there exists
\mu_{\text{out}},\mu_{\text{in}}
such that for all messages
m\in\mathbb{F}^{S}
, it holds that
w_{m}|_{R_{\text{out}}}=\vec{\beta}
and
c_{w_{m}}|_{I}=\vec{\alpha}
. Finally, as

is defined to be the matrix whose rows are the elements of a basis for
(V|_{I\cup R_{\text{in}}})^{\perp}=(\ker(Z_{*}|_{I\cup R_{\text{in}}}))^{\perp}

(\vec{\alpha},m|_{R_{\text{in}}})\in\ker(Z)
if and only if there exists randomness
\mu_{\text{in}},\mu_{\text{out}}
, for which
\mathsf{ENC}^{*}(m;\mu_{\text{in}},\mu_{\text{out}})|_{I}=\vec{\alpha}
.
∎

Note that this claim does not seem to generalise to permit composition of arbitrary locally simulatable encodings. Indeed, here we are implicitly using the fact that a constraint locator allows efficient sampling of a random input to
\mathsf{ENC}_{\text{out}}
consistent with (the restriction to
I
of) a given output. For general locally simulatable encodings, such an algorithm may not exist.

5
Constraint location for random low-degree extensions

In this section we construct an efficient constraint locator for random low-degree extensions of a given function. In
Section

5.1
, we construct an efficient constraint
detector
(cf.
Definition

4.7
) for polynomials which are zero on a given product set

, i.e., for the code

Section

, we efficiently reduce the task of (search) constraint location to the decision version.

In order to state the main theorem of this section, we first define a randomised encoding that maps a function
f
to a random extension of specified degree.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{S})}
to be the randomised encoding function that maps
f\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{F}
, where
\mathcal{S}\ {:=}\ S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
,
d_{i}\geq 2(|S_{i}|-1)
for all

, to a random degree-
\vec{d}
extension of

\mathsf{LD}_{\vec{d}}[f]

Note the restriction that
|d_{i}|\geq 2(|S_{i}|-1)

|S_{1}|=\cdots=|S_{m}|=2
. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.2
.

There is an
n
-constraint locator for

running in time
\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},n)
.

As a corollary, we obtain a local simulator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{S})}
: that is, we obtain an algorithm that can efficiently simulate a random low-degree extension of
f
given oracle access to
f
.

Corollary 5.3
.

\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{S})}
is
(t,n)
-locally simulatable for
t(n)=\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},n)

A constraint detector for random low-degree extensions of the zero function

We construct an efficient constraint detector for

Theorem 5.4

There is a constraint detector

We prove the following.

Lemma 5.5

5.6

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])

Construction 5.6
.

A constraint detector for

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])

Define the block diagonal matrix

Proof.

In order to prove that

using the fact that

(

3.13

Before we proceed, it will be very useful to write down an expression for an individual entry of

. For
\vec{\beta}\in I,\ell\in[k]

(\vec{\beta},\ell)
-entry of

G_{\ell}(\vec{\beta})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{\ell}(i,\vec{\beta})\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}}(\beta_{i}),

(3)

where

Correctness.

\mathrm{Im}(G)\subseteq\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])|_{I}
. Let

. Then, for some scalars

y(\vec{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}G_{j}(\vec{\beta})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{j}(i,\vec{\beta})\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}}(\beta_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{Z}_{S_{i}}(\beta_{i})\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}b_{j}(i,\vec{\beta}),

Eq.

3
. We also know that

Now we show

5.2
The decision problem

Before stating the main result of this section we define the notion of a constraint with respect to a linear code on a subset of its domain.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
be a linear code. A subset
I\subseteq D
is
constrained with respect to
C
if there exists a nonzero vector
z\in\mathbb{F}^{I}
such that, for every codeword
w\in C
,
z\cdot w|_{I}=0
(equivalently, if there exists
z\neq 0\in C^{\perp}
with
\mathsf{supp}(z)\subseteq I
); we refer to
z
as a
constraint with respect to
C
on
I
. We say that
I
is
unconstrained with respect to
C
if it is not constrained with respect to
C
.

We construct an algorithm
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}
which, given as input a (large) product set
\mathcal{S}
and a polynomial-size set
I
, efficiently determines whether
I\cup\mathcal{S}
is constrained with respect to the Reed-Muller code.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}\ {:=}\ S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{m}
,
I\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
and
\vec{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{m})
with
d_{i}\geq|S_{i}|
for all
i\in[m]
.
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d}}(I,\mathcal{S})
outputs

if and only if and
I\cup\mathcal{S}
is constrained with respect to
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
, and runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},|I|)
.

Construction 5.9
.

An algorithm which efficiently determines whether a set
I\cup\mathcal{S}
is constrained with respect to the Reed–Muller code, given an efficient constraint detector
\mathsf{CD}_{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])}(I)
for
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])
.

\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d}}~(I,\mathcal{S})
:

1.

Compute

(see
5.6
).

If

, output
no
, otherwise output
yes
.

To prove the correctness of

, we show an equivalence between constraint detection for
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM})
and deciding whether

is constrained with respect to
\mathrm{RM}
. In order to do so, we shall require the following two properties of linear codes, whose proofs we defer to
Section

A.1
and
Section

.

Claim 5.10
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
be a linear code and let

. Then

is unconstrained with respect to

if and only if for all
x\in I

there exists a codeword

satisfying

(i)

w_{x}(x)=1
and

(ii)

w_{x}(y)=0
for all
y\in I\setminus\{x\}
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
be a linear code, and let

. Suppose that

is unconstrained with respect to

is constrained with respect to

if and only if

is constrained with respect to

.

As a corollary, we show an equivalence between constraints with respect to the general Reed–Muller code and the Reed-Muller code fixed to be zero on a product set.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N},\vec{d}\in\mathbb{N}^{m},I\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
, let
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
, be such that
|S_{i}|\leq d_{i}+1
for all

, and denote
\mathcal{S}\ {:=}\ S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{m}
. Then

is constrained with respect to
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
if and only if

is constrained with respect to
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])
.

Proof.

By

5.11
, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{S}
is unconstrained with respect to
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
. Consider, for each

, the Lagrange polynomial

. As

for all

, the evaluation table of

is a codeword in
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
, for each

. These codewords satisfy

and

is unconstrained with respect to

∎

Proof of

.

The correctness follows from

5.3
Search-to-decision reduction

In this section we show a reduction from constraint location of random low-degree extensions (a search problem) to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}
. Before proving

5.2
, we define what it means for a subset
I
of the domain

of a code to

Definition 5.13
.

Let

be a linear code. We say that

with respect to

The following lemma is central to our reduction. It shows that points which are constrained with respect to the Reed–Muller code are determined with respect to another Reed–Muller code of lower degree. Thus, in our algorithm, it suffices to check the latter condition.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N},\vec{d}\ {:=}\ (d_{1},\dots,d_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m},I\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}

\mathsf{CD}_{\vec{d}}~(I\cup T)
will output a basis for the space of constraints on
I\cup T
, so the output is in the desired form.

Efficiency.
We show that the reduction is efficient by bounding the number of calls made to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d}}
. For
i\in[m]
, we say a point
(s_{1},\dots,s_{i})\in S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{i}
is
i
-accepting
if
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d^{\prime}}}(I,\{s_{1}\}\times\dots\times\{s_{i}\}\times S_{i+1}\times\dots\times S_{m})=\textsf{yes}
. Note that by correctness of
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}
and the fact that
I^{\prime}
is unconstrained, this means that a point
(s_{1},\dots,s_{i})\in S_{1}\times\dots\times S_{i}
is
i
-accepting if and only if there exists
(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{m})\in S_{i+1}\times\dots\times S_{m}
such that
(s_{1},\dots,s_{m})
is involved in a non-zero constraint with respect to
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d^{\prime}}]
. For all
i\in[m]
, let
a_{i}
denote the number of points which are
i
-accepting.

Now we count the number of calls our reduction makes to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}
in terms of
a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}
. Note that regardless of the value of
I
,
Algorithm

1
makes
|S_{1}|
calls to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d^{\prime}}}
at the beginning. Subsequently, for each
i\in[m-1]
,
Algorithm

1
makes
a_{i}|S_{i+1}|
many calls to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d^{\prime}}}
. Overall, the total number of calls that
Algorithm

1
makes to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}_{\vec{d^{\prime}}}
is:

N=|S_{1}|+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}a_{i}|S_{i+1}|.

Now we bound the size of
a_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
. As
d_{i}^{\prime}\geq|S_{i}|-1
for all
i\in[m]
,
Lemma

5.17
implies that there exists a set
G_{I}
with
|G_{I}|\geq|\mathcal{S}|-|I|
, such that for all
g\in G_{I}
there exists a polynomial
p_{g}(\vec{X})\in\mathbb{F}^{\leq\vec{d^{\prime}}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]
satisfying

(i)

p_{g}(x)=0
for all
x\in I\cup G_{I}\setminus\{g\}
and

(ii)

p_{g}(g)=1
.

Thus, by
5.10
, at most
|I|
many points in
\mathcal{S}
are constrained by
I
with respect to
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d^{\prime}}]
. In other words,
a_{i}\leq|I|
for all
i\in[m-1]
. Therefore

N\leq|S_{1}|+|I|\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|S_{i+1}|.

Thus, as each call to
\mathsf{CheckConstraints}
takes time
\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},n)
, and
|S_{i}|
is bounded by a polynomial in
d_{i}
for each
i
, we have that
t(n)=\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},n)
.

Finally, the fact that at most
|I|
many points in
\mathcal{S}
are constrained implies that
\ell(n)=n
.
∎

6
Constraint location for subcube sums of random low-degree extensions

In this section we extend the efficient constraint locator of the previous section to support queries to subcube sums. We begin by defining the corresponding randomised encoding.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
. We define
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}\colon\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{F})\to(\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}\to\mathbb{F})
as the randomised encoding
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[F])\sim\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[\mathsf{LD}_{\vec{d}}{[F]}])
, where
d_{i}\geq|A_{i}|-1
for all
i\in[m]
.

That is,
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}
encodes a message
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[F]\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{F})
by first choosing a random degree-
\vec{d}
extension
\hat{F}
of
F
, and then outputting the word
\Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}[\hat{F}]\colon\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}\to\mathbb{F}
obtained by augmenting
\hat{F}
with partial sums over
\mathcal{A}
(see
Definition

3.6
). Note that the input to
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}
includes partial sums of
F
; these are not necessary to define the encoding but are essential to permit local simulation.

The main theorem of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.2
.

There is a
(t,\ell)
-constraint locator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}
, where
\ell(n)=nm(m(a+1)+1)^{2}
, for
a\ {:=}\ \max_{i}|A_{i}|
and
t(n)=\mathrm{poly}(\log|\mathbb{F}|,m,\max_{i}d_{i},n)
.

We prove this theorem by giving, in
Section

6.1
, a local characterisation of
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
in terms of the plain Reed–Muller code. We give our construction based on this characterisation in
Section

6.2
.

6.1
Local characterisation of
\Sigma
RM

In this subsection we give a local characterisation of
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
in terms of the plain Reed–Muller code. We define the code
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
as follows.

Definition 6.3
.

Define
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}]\ {:=}\ \Sigma_{\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]\subseteq(\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}\to\mathbb{F})
.

Our local characterisation will hold for certain “nice” subsets of
\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
which we call “
\mathcal{A}
-closed”.

Definition 6.4
.

A set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed
if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

if
(s_{1},\dots,s_{t})\in S
, then
(s_{1},\dots,s_{t-1})\in S
; and

(ii)

if
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell})\in S
, then for all
a_{\ell}\in A_{\ell}
,
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell-1},a_{\ell})\in S
.

Given a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
, we refer to the smallest
\mathcal{A}
-closed set containing
X
as the
\mathcal{A}
-closure
of
X
, i.e.,
\hat{X}
is the
\mathcal{A}
-closure of
X
if
\hat{X}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed,
X\subseteq\hat{X}
, and for all
\mathcal{A}
-closed
Y\supseteq X
, it holds that
\hat{X}\subseteq Y
.

Globally the
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
code can be viewed as a collection of plain Reed–Muller codes on
1,2,\ldots,m
variables, related by summation constraints. The following theorem asserts that this characterisation also holds locally: over any
\mathcal{A}
-closed subdomain
S
,
(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{S})^{\perp}
is spanned by summation constraints
\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in S^{*}}
and the local constraints on a collection of Reed-Muller codes. For technical reasons, we show this with respect to both the “plain”
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
code and the subcode
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM})
of encodings of the zero word.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
,
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
and let
S
be an
\mathcal{A}
-closed set. Then for
X\in\{\varnothing,\mathcal{A}\}
, we have

(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}=\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in S^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}\big)

where
X_{i}\ {:=}\ \{\vec{x}\in X:\ell(\vec{x})=i\}
,
S_{i}\ {:=}\ \{\vec{s}\in S:\ell(\vec{s})=i\}
,
S^{*}\ {:=}\ \{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell-1})\in S:\exists s_{\ell}\text{ s.t. }(s_{1},\ldots,s_{\ell-1},s_{\ell})\in S\}
, and

z_{\vec{s}}(\vec{t})\ {:=}\ \begin{cases}1&\text{if $\vec{t}=\vec{s}$;}\\
-1&\text{if $\vec{t}=(\vec{s},a)$ for some $a\in A_{\ell(\vec{s})+1}$;}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We will use the above notation for
X_{i}
,
S_{i}
,
S^{*}
and
z_{\vec{s}}(\vec{t})
throughout the remainder of this section.

We prove
Theorem

6.5
by a dimensionality argument. In particular, we show that any basis for
(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
which is in echelon form can be mapped to a set of linearly independent vectors in the span of summation constraints and Reed–Muller constraints.

The key technical tool used here is a “flattening lemma” (
Lemma

6.8
). This lemma provides a means to map any constraint
z
on subcube sums of
m
-variate polynomials to a new constraint
z^{\prime}
on subcube sums of
(m-1)
-variate polynomials, while preserving the value of
\lambda(z)
. Crucially, in the case where
|\lambda(z)|=m-1
, all dependence of
z^{\prime}
on subcube sums vanishes, and we obtain a constraint with respect to the plain Reed–Muller code.

By repeated applications of the flattening lemma we map any constraint over subcube sums to a Reed–Muller constraint; we then show by a counting argument that these “flattened” constraints, along with the summation constraints, span the dual code.

Proof of
Theorem

6.5
.

Define
Z^{*}\ {:=}\ \{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in S^{*}}
, and
Z_{i}\ {:=}\ (\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
for
i\in[m]
. It follows easily from the definition of
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
that
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{m}\subseteq(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
. As
S
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed we also have that
Z^{*},\subseteq(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
. Hence it suffices to show that
\dim(\Span(Z^{*}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}))\geq\dim((\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp})
.

Let
\Delta\ {:=}\ \dim((\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp})
. Fix some ordering
<
on
S
so that

(i)

\ell(\vec{s})<\ell(\vec{t})
then
\vec{s}<\vec{t}
, and

(ii)

if
\ell(\vec{s})=\ell(\vec{t})
,
\vec{s}\notin S^{*}
and
\vec{t}\in S^{*}
then
\vec{s}<\vec{t}
.

Let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{\Delta}
be a basis for
(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
which is in echelon form with respect to
<
.

We show that
\dim(\Span(Z^{*}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}))\geq\Delta
by exhibiting a sequence of linearly independent vectors
b^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,b^{\prime}_{\Delta}\in Z^{*}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}Z_{i}
. These vectors are defined as follows:

b^{\prime}_{i}\ {:=}\ \begin{cases}z_{\vec{\lambda}(b_{i})}&\text{if $\lambda(b_{i})\in S^{*}$; and}\\
R(b_{i})&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
R
is as guaranteed by
6.6
below. To show that the
b^{\prime}_{i}
are linearly independent, it suffices to show that they are in echelon form with respect to
<
. This follows since
\lambda(b^{\prime}_{i})=\lambda(b_{i})
for all
i\in[\Delta]
: if
\lambda(b_{i})\in S^{*}
, then
\lambda(b^{\prime}_{i})=\lambda(z_{\lambda(b_{i})})=\lambda(b_{i})
; and if
\lambda(b_{i})\notin S^{*}
then
\lambda(b^{\prime}_{i})=\lambda(R(b_{i}))=\lambda(b_{i})
by
6.6
.
∎

It remains to prove the following technical claim.

Claim 6.6
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
,
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}
,
\vec{d}\ {:=}\ (d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
and
X\in\{\varnothing,\mathcal{A}\}
. If
d_{i}\geq|\mathcal{A}_{i}|-1
for all
i\in[m]
, then there is a function
T\colon(S\to\mathbb{F})\to\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(S_{i}\to\mathbb{F})
such that for all
z\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
,
T(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
for
i=|\lambda(z)|
, and if
\lambda(z)\notin S^{*}
,
\lambda(T(z))=\lambda(z)
.

This claim will be a consequence of
Lemma

6.8
(the “flattening lemma”), which provides a way to transform a constraint on an
m
-variate
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
code into a related constraint on an
(m-1)
-variate
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
code.

Definition 6.7
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
and
A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
. For
i\in[m]
and an element
a\in A_{i}
, we define the “flattening map”
Q_{i,a}\colon(S\to\mathbb{F})\to(S\setminus S_{i}\to\mathbb{F})
as follows, for
\vec{s}\in S\setminus S_{i}
:

Q_{i,a}(z)(\vec{s})\ {:=}\ \begin{cases}z(\vec{s})+\sum_{s_{i}\in S_{i}}z(\vec{s},s_{i})L_{A_{i},a}(s_{i})&\text{if $\vec{s}\in S_{i-1}\cap S^{*}$, and}\\
z(\vec{s})&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 6.8

(Flattening lemma)
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
,
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}
,
\vec{d}\ {:=}\ (d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
,
X\in\{\varnothing,\mathcal{A}\}
, and let
z\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
, where
d_{m}\geq|A_{m}|-1
.
For any point
a_{m}^{*}\in A_{m}
, the function
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}\colon(S\to\mathbb{F})\to(S\setminus S_{m}\to\mathbb{F})
(see
Definition

6.7
) satisfies the following properties:

1.

If
z
satisfies
|\lambda(z)|<m-1
, then
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{m-1}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A}_{m-1},(d_{1},\dots,d_{m-1})])|_{S\setminus S_{i}})^{\perp}
and
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.

2.

If
z
satisfies
|\lambda(z)|=m-1
, then
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{|\lambda(z)|}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},|\lambda(z)|,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{|\lambda(z)|})])|_{S_{|\lambda(z)|}})^{\perp}
, and furthermore if
\lambda(z)\notin S^{*}
, then
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.

Proof.

We will proceed by showing how a constraint on subcube sums of an
m
-variate polynomials induces a constraint on subcube sums of
(m-1)
-variate polynomials.

Let
z\in\left(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S}\right)^{\bot}
.
Define the set
P_{0}\ {:=}\ \{p\in\mathbb{F}^{\leq\vec{d}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}]:p(\vec{x})=0~\forall\vec{x}\in X\}
.
Then for all polynomials
p\in P_{0}
it holds that

\sum_{\vec{s}\in S}z(\vec{s})\sum_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}_{>\ell(\vec{s})}}p(\vec{s},\vec{a})=0.

(4)

Let
a_{m}^{*}\in A_{m}
and define the set of (
m-1
)-variate polynomials
Q_{0}\ {:=}\ \{q\in\mathbb{F}^{\leq\vec{d^{\prime}}}[X_{1},\ldots,X_{m-1}]:q(\vec{x})=0~\forall\vec{x}\in X_{m-1}\}
, where
\vec{d^{\prime}}\ {:=}\ (d_{1},\dots,d_{m-1})
. Then for any
q\in Q_{0}
, the polynomial defined by
q_{a_{m}^{*}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\ {:=}\ q(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})\cdot L_{A_{m},a_{m}^{*}}(x_{m})
satisfies
q_{a_{m}^{*}}(\vec{x})=0
for all
\vec{x}\in X
. So
q_{a_{m}^{*}}\in P_{0}
. Thus, substituting
q_{a_{m}^{*}}
into
Equation

4
implies that for all polynomials
q\in Q_{0}
,

\sum_{\vec{s}\in S\setminus(S_{m-1}\cup S_{m})}z(\vec{s})\sum_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{>\ell(\vec{s})}}q(\vec{s},\vec{a})+\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{m-1}}z(\vec{s})q(\vec{s})+\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{m-1}}q(\vec{s})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m}\\
(\vec{s},s_{m})\in S_{m}\end{subarray}}z(\vec{s},s_{m})L_{\mathcal{A}_{m},a_{m}^{*}}(s_{m})=0,

where
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\ {:=}\ \mathcal{A}_{m-1}
. Recall that for points
(s_{1},\dots,s_{m-1})\notin S^{*}
, there does not exist
s_{m}\in S_{m}
such that
(s_{1},\dots,s_{m-1},s_{m})\in S
. Thus we can rearrange this expression as:

\sum_{\vec{s}\in S\setminus(S_{m-1}\cup S_{m})}z(\vec{s})\sum_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{>\ell(\vec{s})}}q(\vec{s},\vec{a})+\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{m-1}\setminus S^{*}}z(\vec{s})q(\vec{s})+\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{m-1}\cap S^{*}}q(\vec{s})\left(z(\vec{s})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m}\\
(\vec{s},s_{m})\in S_{m}\end{subarray}}z(\vec{s},s_{m})L_{\mathcal{A}_{m},a_{m}^{*}}(s_{m})\right)=0.

(5)

Notice that the coefficients of
Eq.

5
are precisely the values of
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}
(
Definition

6.7
) at each point in
S\setminus S_{m}
. This demonstrates that for all
z\in\left(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S}\right)^{\bot}
,
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)\in\left(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{m-1}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\vec{d^{\prime}}])|_{S\setminus S_{m}}\right)^{\bot}
.

Now we will deal with the two cases of the lemma statement separately.

Case (i)
|\lambda(z)|+1<m
.
In this case we show that
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.

As
|\lambda(z)|+1<m
,
\lambda(z)\in S\setminus(S_{m}\cup S_{m-1})
. But we have that
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\vec{s})=z(\vec{s})
for all
\vec{s}\in S\setminus(S_{m}\cup S_{m-1})
, so in particular
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\vec{s})=z(\vec{s})=0
for all
\vec{s}<\lambda(z)
, and
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\lambda(z))=z(\lambda(z))\neq 0
. Thus
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.

Case (ii)
|\lambda(z)|+1=m
.
First, we show that
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
, where
i\ {:=}\ |\lambda(z)|
. Second, we show that if
\lambda(z)\notin S^{*}
, then
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.

For the first part, as
|\lambda(z)|=m-1
,
z(\vec{s})=0
for all
\vec{s}\in S\setminus(S_{m-1}\cup S_{m})
. As a result,
Equation

5
simplifies greatly and becomes:

\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{|\lambda(z)|}\setminus S^{*}}z(\vec{s})q(\vec{s})+\sum_{\vec{s}\in S_{|\lambda(z)|}\cap S^{*}}q(\vec{s})\left(z(\vec{s})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{m}\\
\vec{s}||s_{m}\in S_{m}\end{subarray}}z(\vec{s},s_{m})L_{\mathcal{A}_{m},a_{m}^{*}}(s_{m})\right)=0.

(6)

Note that
Equation

6
is a constraint with respect to the Reed-Muller code
(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
. So
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{R_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
.

For the second part, assume
\lambda(z)\notin S^{*}
. By the definition of
<
, if
\vec{s}<\lambda(z)
, then it must be that
\vec{s}\notin S^{*}
. However, if
\vec{s}\notin S^{*}
, then
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\vec{s})=z(\vec{s})
. In particular, for
\vec{s}<\lambda(z)
,
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\vec{s})=z(\vec{s})=0
, and
Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z)(\lambda(z))=z(\lambda(z))\neq 0
, so
\lambda(Q_{m,a_{m}^{*}}(z))=\lambda(z)
.
∎

We now apply
Lemma

6.8
to prove
6.6
, which will complete the proof of
Theorem

6.5
.

Proof of
6.6
.

For each
i\in\{|\lambda(z)|+1,\dots,m\}
arbitrarily chose an element
a_{i}\in A_{i}
, and for notational convenience, denote by
Q_{i}\ {:=}\ Q_{i,a_{i}}
the flattening map (
Definition

6.7
). For each
z\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{S})^{\perp}
, define the value of
T(z)\ {:=}\ (Q_{|\lambda(z)|+1}\circ\dots\circ Q_{m-1}\circ Q_{m})(z)
.

By part 1 of
Lemma

6.8
, we see that
(Q_{|\lambda(z)|+2}\circ\dots\circ Q_{m})(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i+1}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A}_{i+1},(d_{1},\dots,d_{i+1})])|_{S\setminus S_{m}})^{\perp}
, with
\lambda((Q_{|\lambda(z)|+2}\circ\dots\circ Q_{m})(z))=\lambda(z)
. Combining this with part 2 of
Lemma

6.8
we see that
T(z)\ {:=}\ (Q_{|\lambda(z)|+1}\circ\dots\circ Q_{m})(z)\in(\mathbf{Z}_{X_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{S_{i}})^{\perp}
, and provided that
\lambda(z)\notin S^{*}
, then
\lambda(T(z))=\lambda(z).

∎

6.2
An
\ell
-constraint locator for
\Sigma
RM

In this section we construct an constraint locator for
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
, by combining the structure implied by
Theorem

6.5
with the constraint locator we constructed in
Section

5
. We will often denote
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}\ {:=}\ \Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
when
\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}
are clear from context.

Construction 6.9
.

An
\ell
-constraint locator for

-constraint locator for

\mathsf{CL}_{\Sigma}(I)

-closure

2.

For each

Define the set

4.

For each

Construct the matrix

Construct the matrix

Output

Claim 6.10
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
and let
(\hat{R},B)\ {:=}\ \mathsf{CL}_{\Sigma}(I)
(cf.
6.9
), then
\ker(B)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\ker(Z)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}}
(for
Z
as in
6.9
). This is because
(\vec{r},\vec{\beta})\in\ker(B)
if and only if there exists
\vec{\gamma}\in\mathbb{F}^{\hat{I}\setminus I}

(\vec{r},\vec{\beta},\vec{\gamma})\in\ker(Z)

\ker(B)=\ker(Z)|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}.
If
\ker(Z)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}}

\hat{R}\sqcup I
, we have that
\ker(B)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}

To see that
\ker(Z)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}}

Z
consist of trivial constraints over
(\hat{I}\sqcup\hat{R})^{*}
and Reed-Muller constraints on the set

padded with zeros so as to be supported on
\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}
. That is,

\mathsf{rowspace}(Z)=\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in(\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I})^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}({\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})]}|_{{R_{i}\sqcup\hat{I}}_{i}})^{\perp}\times\{0\}^{\hat{R}_{i}\setminus R_{i}}\big).

By the correctness of each
\mathsf{CL}_{i}
, we know that for all
i\in[m]
, for all constraints
z\in(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\dots,d_{i})]|_{\mathcal{A}_{i}\sqcup\hat{I}})^{\perp}

z(\vec{\alpha})=0
for all
\vec{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}_{i}\setminus R_{i}
. Therefore as

, we have that
(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\dots,d_{i})]|_{\hat{R}_{i}\sqcup\hat{I}})^{\perp}=(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\dots,d_{i})]|_{R_{i}\sqcup\hat{I}})^{\perp}\times\{0\}^{\hat{R}_{i}\setminus R_{i}}

\mathsf{rowspace}(Z)=\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in(\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I})^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}({\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})]}|_{{\hat{R}_{i}\sqcup\hat{I}}_{i}})^{\perp}\big)=(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}})^{\perp},

where the final equality follows from
Theorem

6.5
and the fact that
\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed. In particular, we have shown that
Z
is a parity check matrix for
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}}
. In other words,
\ker(Z)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup\hat{I}}
.
∎

Now we focus on the second part of our proof of correctness: showing that
(\vec{r},\vec{\beta})\in\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}
if and only if for all messages

such that

Claim 6.11
.

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{D}
be a linear code, and let
U\subseteq V\subseteq D
,
I\subseteq D
be such that
\mathbf{Z}_{U}(C)|_{I}=\mathbf{Z}_{V}(C)|_{I}
. Then
(u,x)\in C|_{U\sqcup I}
if and only if for all

such that

,
(v,x)\in C|_{V\sqcup I}
.

We prove that
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM})|_{\hat{I}}=\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM})|_{\hat{I}}
where
\hat{I},\hat{R}
are as above. To do this, we first show an analogous statement for “plain”
\mathrm{RM}
, and then lift that result to
\Sigma\mathrm{RM}
via
Theorem

6.5
.

Claim 6.12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{m}
and let

. It holds that
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])|_{S}=\mathbf{Z}_{R}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])|_{S}
, where
(R,Z)\ {:=}\ \mathsf{CL}(S)
, for an
\ell
-constraint locator
\mathsf{CL}
for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}
.

Proof.

As
R\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, it is clear that
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])|_{S}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}_{R}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}])|_{S}
. We now argue the reverse containment. For brevity we write
\mathrm{RM}
for
\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},m,\vec{d}]
.

Let
w\in\mathrm{RM}
such that
w(r)=0
for all
r\in R
. Then
w|_{S}
is a general element of
\mathbf{Z}_{R}(\mathrm{RM})|_{S}
. We will show that there exists
w^{\prime}\in\mathrm{RM}
which agrees with
w
on
S
, but is zero on
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.,
w^{\prime}|_{S}=w|_{S}
and
w^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{A}}=\vec{0}
.

By the correctness of
\mathsf{CL}
, all constraints on
\mathcal{A}\sqcup S
with respect to
\mathrm{RM}
are supported only on
R\sqcup S
; in particular, they have no support on the set
\mathcal{A}\setminus R
. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus R
, define
w_{\alpha}\colon\mathcal{A}\sqcup S\to\mathbb{F}
by
w_{\alpha}(\alpha)\ {:=}\ 1
and
w_{\alpha}(\beta)\ {:=}\ 0
for all
\beta\in\mathcal{A}\sqcup S\setminus\{\alpha\}
. Then since

is only nonzero in
\mathcal{A}\setminus R

w_{\alpha}\in\mathrm{RM}|_{\mathcal{A}\sqcup S}
, and so
w_{\alpha}=w_{\alpha}^{\prime}|_{\mathcal{A}\sqcup S}
for

. Consider the codeword defined by

w^{\prime}\ {:=}\ w-\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\setminus R}w(\alpha)w_{\alpha}^{\prime}.

This choice of
w^{\prime}
satisfies
w^{\prime}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{RM})
, and
w^{\prime}|_{S}=w|_{S}
. Thus
w|_{S}\in\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{RM})|_{S}
.
∎

Corollary 6.13
.

For
\hat{I},R
as in
6.9
,
(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}=(\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
(\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}\subseteq(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}
, so we show the reverse inclusion.

For

as in
6.9
, we have that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d})|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in{\hat{I}}^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{\hat{I}_{i}})^{\perp}\big)

\displaystyle=\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in\hat{I}^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{Z}_{R_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{\hat{I}_{i}})^{\perp}\big)

\displaystyle\subseteq\Span\big(\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in\hat{I}^{*}}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(\mathbf{Z}_{{\hat{R}}_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{\hat{I}_{i}})^{\perp}\big)

The first equality is a consequence of

6.5
. The second equality is a consequence of
6.12
. The penultimate inclusion is true as

for each

. The final inclusion follows from the fact that trivially
(\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}_{i}}(\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},i,(d_{1},\ldots,d_{i})])|_{\hat{I}_{i}})^{\perp}\subseteq(\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}
for each

and as
\hat{R}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed, we also have that
\{z_{\vec{s}}\}_{\vec{s}\in\hat{I}^{*}}\subseteq(\mathbf{Z}_{\hat{R}}(\Sigma\mathrm{RM}[\mathbb{F},\mathcal{A},\vec{d}])|_{\hat{I}})^{\perp}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

6.2
.

Correctness.
By
6.10
, we have that
\ker(B)=\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}
. Then by

, setting

and

, noting that
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{RM}(\vec{d},\mathcal{A})}

), it follows that

if and only if for all messages

such that

, we have

. Thus, taking the restriction of both sides,
(\vec{r},\vec{\beta}|_{I})\in\Sigma\mathrm{RM}|_{\hat{R}\sqcup I}
if and only if for all messages

such that

, we have

. This is sufficient since, by definition of the encoding,

By

|\hat{R}|\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|+m+1)|R|

The runtime follows from

Claim 6.14
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
and
\mathcal{A}=A_{1}\times\dots\times A_{m}
. Then there exists a set
\hat{I}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\leq m}
such that
I\subseteq\hat{I}
and
\hat{I}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed, satisfying
|\hat{I}|\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|+m+1)|I|
. Moreover,
\hat{I}
can be computed in time
\mathrm{poly}(m,|I|,|A_{1}|,\dots,|A_{m}|)
.

Proof.

We construct
\hat{I}
as follows. Let
I_{i}\ {:=}\ \{x\in I:|x|=i\}
. Initialise
\hat{I}\ {:=}\ I
. For each
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\in I_{m}
, we add the point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1})
and for each
a_{m}\in A_{m}
we add the point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{m-1},a_{m})
to
\hat{I}
. Then repeat this process for

. By construction,
\hat{I}
is
\mathcal{A}
-closed.

Now we argue about the size of
\hat{I}
. Note that for each point in

at most
\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|+m
points are added to
\hat{I}
. Thus it holds that
|\hat{I}|\leq(\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}|+m+1)|I|
. The efficiency follows from this bound.
∎

7
Constraint location for
\Sigma
AntiSym

In this section we define the code
\mathrm{AntiSym}
of antisymmetric functions, and provide a constraint locator for an encoding function related to its sum code
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}
.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\ {:=}\ A_{1}\times\cdots\times A_{m}
be such that
A_{i}=A_{m-i+1}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
. We define

\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]\ {:=}\ \{w\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathcal{A}}:\forall\vec{x}\in\mathcal{A},w(\vec{x})+w(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})=0\}~.

The code
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}
is the sum code of
\mathrm{AntiSym}
(see
Definition

3.6
).

The main theorem of this section is the following, which gives an efficient constraint locator for
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}
with polynomial locality.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be as above. Let
\mathcal{M}\ {:=}\ \{F\colon\mathcal{A}\cup\{\bot\}\to\mathbb{F}\mid F(\bot)=\sum_{\vec{a}\in\mathcal{A}}F(\vec{a})\}
. Let
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{F}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be the following randomised encoding:

\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}(F)\ {:=}\ \Sigma[F|_{\mathcal{A}}+G],

where
G
is a codeword sampled uniformly at random from
\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]
.

\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}
has an
\ell
-constraint locator for
\ell(n)=n^{2}m^{2}+1+O(n^{4}m^{4}/|\mathcal{A}|)
, running in time
\mathrm{poly}(a,m,n)
, where
a\ {:=}\ \sum_{i}|A_{i}|
.

In
Section

7.1
we analyse the combinatorial structure of
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{I}^{\perp}
, obtaining a bound on the “complexity” of constraints in terms of the size of
I
. In
Section

7.2
, we show how this bound leads to an efficient constraint locator for
\mathsf{ENC}_{\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}}
.

7.1
Properties of
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}

We start by introducing some useful notation.

Definition 7.3
.

We say
\vec{a}\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is a
prefix
of
\vec{b}\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
if
\ell(\vec{a})\leq\ell(\vec{b})
and
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell(\vec{a})})=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{\ell(\vec{a})})
. A subset
J\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is
prefix-free
if for all
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in J
, if
\vec{a}
is a prefix of
\vec{b}
then
\vec{a}=\vec{b}
.

We associate with an element
\vec{a}\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
the set
\vec{a}\times\mathcal{A}_{>\ell(\vec{a})}
; we denote this set also by
\vec{a}
. In particular,
|\vec{a}|=|\mathcal{A}_{>\ell(\vec{a})}|=\prod_{i=\ell(\vec{a})+1}^{m}|A_{i}|
. (See
Definition

3.6
.)

It is straightforward to see that
\vec{b}\subseteq\vec{a}
if and only if
\vec{a}
is a prefix of
\vec{b}
. The following simple proposition gives a useful alternative characterisation of
\mathrm{AntiSym}
.

Proposition 7.4
.

w\in\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]
if and only if there exists
f\in\mathbb{F}^{\mathcal{A}}
such that
w(\vec{x})=f(\vec{x})-f(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})
.

Proof.

For the “if” direction, note that
w(\vec{x})+w(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})=f(\vec{x})-f(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})+f(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})-f(\vec{x})=0
for all
\vec{x}\in\mathcal{A}
. For the “only if” direction, let
w\in\mathrm{AntiSym}[\vec{A}]
, and consider some strict total ordering
<
on
\mathcal{A}
. Define

f(\vec{x})\ {:=}\ \begin{cases}w(\vec{x})&\text{if $\vec{x}<\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}}$, and}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then for all
\vec{x}
,

f(\vec{x})-f(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})=\begin{cases}w(\vec{x})&\text{ if $\vec{x}<\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}}$, and}\\
-w(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})&\text{ if $\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}}<\vec{x}$}\end{cases}=w(\vec{x})~.\qed

Definition 7.5
.

For
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell})\in\bar{\mathcal{A}}
, we define the
reverse set

\vec{a}_{\mathsf{rev}}\ {:=}\ \bar{\mathcal{A}}_{m-\ell}\times(a_{\ell},\ldots,a_{1})
. For a set
H\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
, we define
H_{\mathsf{rev}}\ {:=}\ \{\vec{a}_{\mathsf{rev}}:\vec{a}\in H\}
.

We say that
H
is
symmetric
if
\cup H=\cup H_{\mathsf{rev}}
, where
\cup H\ {:=}\ \cup_{\vec{h}\in H}\vec{h}
. We say that
H
is
minimal symmetric
if
H
is symmetric and for all nonempty
H^{\prime}\subsetneq H
,
H^{\prime}
is not symmetric.

Proposition 7.6
.

If
H\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
is symmetric and prefix-free, then
H=\cup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}
for
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\subseteq H
where each
H_{i}
is minimal symmetric and
H_{i}\cap H_{j}=\varnothing
for
i\neq j
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the size of
H
. Indeed, suppose that the statement is true for all sets
H^{\prime}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
of size less than
n
, and let
H\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
be a set of size
n
. If
H
is minimal symmetric, then the statement holds trivially, so suppose not. Then there is a nonempty symmetric
H^{\prime}\subsetneq H
. Since
H
is prefix-free,
\cup(H\setminus H^{\prime})=(\cup H)\setminus(\cup H^{\prime})=(\cup H_{\mathsf{rev}})\setminus(\cup H^{\prime}_{\mathsf{rev}})=\cup(H\setminus H^{\prime})_{\mathsf{rev}}
; hence
H\setminus H^{\prime}
is also symmetric. Since
H^{\prime}
and
H\setminus H^{\prime}
are strictly smaller than
H
, we can apply the inductive hypothesis to complete the proof.
∎

The following lemma shows that, for
G
prefix-free,
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}|_{G}^{\perp}
has a basis of constraints of the form “
\sum_{\vec{h}\in H}w(\vec{h})=0
”, taken over all minimal symmetric subsets
H\subseteq G
.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
G\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{A}}
be prefix-free. For
H\subseteq G
, let
1_{H}\colon G\to\mathbb{F}
be the indicator vector for
H
, i.e.,
1_{H}(\vec{h})=1
if
\vec{h}\in H
and
0
otherwise. A basis for
\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]|_{G}^{\perp}
is
\{1_{H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}}
where
\mathcal{H}
is the set of all minimal symmetric subsets of
G
.

Proof.

Let
B\ {:=}\ \{1_{H}\}_{H\in\mathcal{H}}
. First, note that
Proposition

7.6
implies that for all
H\neq H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
,
H\cap H^{\prime}=\varnothing
. Hence
B
is a linearly independent set. It is also straightforward to see that
B\subseteq\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]|_{G}^{\perp}
. It remains to show that
\Span(B)=\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]|_{G}^{\perp}
.

Suppose that
z\in\Sigma\mathrm{AntiSym}[\mathcal{A}]|_{G}^{\perp}
; equivalently, by
Proposition

7.4
, for all
f\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{F}
,

\sum_{\vec{a}\in G}z(\vec{a})\sum_{\vec{x}\in\vec{a}}f(\vec{x})=\sum_{\vec{a}\in G}z(\vec{a})\sum_{\vec{x}\in\vec{a}}f(\vec{x}_{\mathsf{rev}})~.

Let
S_{\gamma}\ {:=}\ \{\vec{a}\in G:z(\vec{a})=\gamma\}
. Then we can write

Go to

Lemma 7.13

ZKPCP for

In this section we prove our main theorem, that there exists a PZK-PCP for (decision)
\#\mathsf{SAT}
. First, we recall the definition:

\#\mathsf{SAT}=\{(\Phi,N):\text{$\Phi$ is a CNF, }\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Phi(x)=N\}.

Theorem 8.1
.

There exists a PZK-PCP for
\#\mathsf{SAT}
. The honest verifier is non-adaptive, and the zero-knowledge property holds against arbitrary (adaptive) polynomial-time malicious verifiers.

To formally obtain
1
, we must define what we mean by
\#\mathsf{P}
as a class of languages (typically,
\#\mathsf{P}
is treated as a class of function problems). One natural way is by reference to the number of accepting paths of a Turing machine, i.e.: a pair language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\{0,1\}^{*}\times\mathbb{N})
is in
\#\mathsf{P}
if and only if there is a polynomial-time nondeterministic Turing machine
M
such that
x\in\mathcal{L}
if and only if
M(x)
has
N
accepting paths.
\#\mathsf{SAT}
is complete for
\#\mathsf{P}
under this definition.

One can also adopt a more permissive definition of
\#\mathsf{P}
as the set of all languages that have a deterministic polynomial-time reduction to
\#\mathsf{SAT}
. This permits, for example, the inclusion
\mathsf{coNP}\subseteq\#\mathsf{P}
, via reduction from
\mathsf{UNSAT}
:
\Phi\mapsto(\Phi,0)
.
1
follows from
Theorem

8.1
also with respect to this broader definition.

Remark 8.2

(Sampling field elements)
.

Our PZK-PCP construction relies on sampling uniformly random field elements. This can be an issue if we demand perfect simulation, as typically PPT algorithms are given a tape of uniformly random
bits
, from which it is not possible to perfectly sample random field elements in strict polynomial time if the field has characteristic different from
2
. There are three ways to resolve this:

(1)

provide the simulator with uniformly random field elements on its random tape;

(2)

use rejection sampling, so that the simulator runs in expected polynomial time, or;

(3)

restrict attention to
\oplus P
, which can be verified by sumcheck over fields of characteristic
2
.

In this section we formally define the notion of a (perfect) zero-knowledge probabilistically-checkable proof.

For every
