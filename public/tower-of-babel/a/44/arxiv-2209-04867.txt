Abstract
This paper provides a comprehensive estimation framework for large covariance matrices via a log-det heuristics augmented by a nuclear norm plus ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -norm penalty. We develop the model framework, which includes high-dimensional approximate factor models with a sparse residual covariance. We prove that the aforementioned log-det heuristics is locally convex with a Lipschitz-continuous gradient, so that a proximal gradient algorithm may be stated to numerically solve the problem while controlling the threshold parameters. The proposed optimization strategy recovers in a single step both the covariance matrix components and the latent rank and the residual sparsity pattern with high probability, and performs systematically not worse than the corresponding estimators employing Frobenius loss in place of the log-det heuristics. The error bounds for the ensuing low rank and sparse covariance matrix estimators are established, and the identifiability conditions for the latent geometric manifolds are provided, improving existing literature. The validity of outlined results is highlighted by an exhaustive simulation study and a financial data example involving Euro Area banks.



Keywords:
covariance matrix, log-det heuristics, local convexity, nuclear norm, high dimension

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

Estimating high-dimensional covariance or precision matrices has become a
crucial task nowadays, due to the increasing availability of datasets composed of
a large number of variables
p
compared to the sample size
n
in many fields, like
economics, finance, biology, genetics, health, climatology, and social sciences.
The consistency of estimated covariance matrices is a prerequisite
to perform several statistical procedures in high dimensions like principal component analysis (PCA),
cluster analysis, graphical model inference, among others.
Recent books on this relevant topic are
Pourahmadi 2013
;
Zagidullina 2021
,
while recent comprehensive reviews include
Fan et al. 2016
;
Wainwright 2019
;
Lam 2020
;
Ledoit & Wolf 2021
.
The amplitude of techniques developed to overcome the estimation issues
in high dimensions now provides several state-of-the art solutions,
but also leaves some room to further improve the estimation process in many directions.

Although the theory of covariance matrix estimation for Gaussian data with low and fixed dimension
was developed in the fifties by pioneeristic contributions
(
Anderson 1958
)
,
it became soon apparent that the sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}

is not a reliable estimator of the true covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}

when the dimension
p
is large. As explained in
Ledoit & Wolf 2004
, in fact, sample eigenvalues may be
severely over-dispersed in that case,
as they follow the Mar
\check{\mathrm{c}}
enko-Pastur law

(
Marchenko & Pastur 1967
)
. This leads
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
to become numerically unstable as
p\to\infty
.
A large
p
also easily leads to identifiability issues for
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
,
as the number of parameters to be recovered grows quadratically in
p
in absence of further assumptions.
In addition, if
p/n\geq 1
,
p-n
sample eigenvalues are null,
thus irremediably affecting the invertibility of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
.

A relevant approach to large covariance matrix estimation passes by the assumption of a specific structure for
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
, in order to drastically reduce the number of parameters.
One option is to assume a factor model structure.
The spiked covariance model, introduced in
Johnstone 2001

and recovered in
Johnstone & Lu 2009
,
is a successful attempt of this kind.
Linear eigenvalue shrinkage under the same model has been proposed in
Donoho et al. 2018
.
Another option is to assume some type of sparsity.
Under that assumption, with respect to the underlying structure,
it has been proposed to recover
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
by
applying hard-thresholding
(
Bickel & Levina 2008b
)
, soft-thresholding
(
Bickel & Levina 2008a
)
,
generalized thresholding
(
Rothman et al. 2009
)
, or adaptive thresholding
(
Cai & Liu 2011
)
.
Penalized maximum likelihood (ML) has also been used to estimate a sparse covariance
(
Bien & Tibshirani 2011
)

or precision
(
Friedman et al. 2008
)
matrix.

These two types of algebraic structure enforced in
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
may both be too restrictive.
In fact, as pointed out by
Giannone et al. 2021
for economic data, it is likely that
the sparsity assumption is too strong in high dimensions, as the interrelation structure among the variables
is actually more dense than sparse.
At the same time, a strict factor model does not allow for any idiosyncratic covariance structure
that may catch specific pairs of extra-correlated variables beyond the factors.
As a consequence, it became clear that conditional sparsity with respect to an underlying approximate factor model
(
Chamberlain & Rothschild 1983
)

can effectively merge factor model and sparsity assumptions,
thus being simultaneously a parsimonious and flexible approach.
A covariance matrix estimator assuming conditional sparsity is POET
(
Fan et al. 2013
)
,
that proposes to threshold the principal orthogonal complement to obtain a consistent solution.

1.2
Approach

Conditional sparsity can be imposed by assuming for
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
a low rank plus sparse decomposition, that is

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathbf{L}^{*}+\mathbf{S}^{*}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}+\mathbf{S}^{*},

(1)

where
\mathbf{L}^{*}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}_{L}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{L}\mathbf{U}_{L}^{\prime}
,
with
\mathbf{U}_{L}

p\times r
semi-orthogonal matrix and
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{L}

r\times r
diagonal positive definite matrix, and

\mathbf{S}^{*}
is a positive definite and element-wise sparse matrix,
containing only
s\ll{p(p-1)}/{2}
off-diagonal non-zero elements.
Structure (
1
) has become the reference model
under which several high-dimensional covariance matrix estimators work.
It can be recovered by nuclear norm plus
l_{1}
penalization, that is by solving

\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})+\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
is a smooth loss function,
measuring the distance between
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
, which is an admissible covariance matrix estimate,
and the sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
, and

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\psi\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}
is a non-smooth composite penalty,
where
\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{L})
is the nuclear norm of
\mathbf{L}
,
i.e. the sum of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{L^{*}}
, and
\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\mathbf{S}_{ij}|

is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
\mathbf{S}
, while
\psi
and
\rho
are non-negative threshold parameters.

Heuristics (
2
) has generated a stream of literature where new covariance matrix estimators in high dimensions are derived.

Agarwal et al. 2012
ensures optimal rates for the solutions of (
2
) under model (
1
) via a purely analytical approach, providing the approximate recovery of
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
and a bounded non-identifiability radius for
\mathbf{L}^{*}
.
In
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
, a latent graphical model structure, based on a sparse minus low rank decomposition for
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
,
is learnt by solving a problem of type (
2
), providing both parametric and algebraic consistency
(see Section
3
for the definitions).

Luo 2011
derives LOREC estimator under model (
1
) for
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}

via the objective in (
2
) with
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
and

\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S})\|_{F}^{2}
. A similar heuristics
is employed in
Barigozzi & Farnè 2024
to recover the spectral density matrix in high dimensions.

In
Farnè & Montanari 2020
;
Farnè & Montanari 2024
, UNALCE estimator is proposed. UNALCE is based on problem (
2
)
with
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})

like LOREC, but overcomes LOREC deficiencies thanks to a random matrix theory result that
holds under a wide range of approximate factor models and accounts for the high-dimensional case
p\geq n
.
UNALCE is both algebraically consistent in the sense of
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
and
parametrically consistent in Frobenius and spectral norm.
Although UNALCE is the optimal estimator in finite sample
when
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})

in terms of Frobenius loss, there is room to further improve it by replacing

\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
with a different loss.

The Frobenius loss optimizes in fact the
entry by entry performance of the overall covariance matrix estimate

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
. A loss able to explicitly control
the spectrum estimation quality might be desirable, in order to ensure
the recovery of latent rank and residual sparsity pattern,
while simultaneously optimizing the estimated spectrum in terms of distance from the true one.
The loss
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
,
where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}
, is a possible one satisfying
these needs, because it is controlled by the individual eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
, since

\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})={\ln}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\ln(1+\lambda^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}),

(3)

thus providing an intrinsic eigenvalue correction.
Inequality (
3
) holds because
\ln(1+\lambda^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}))\leq\lambda^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}),
since
\ln(x)\leq x-1
for any
x>0
.
It trivially holds when
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}
is diagonal, and it can be proved in the general case by
diagonalizing the positive semi-definite symmetric matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}
.
Our estimator pair therefore becomes

\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}}\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})+\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}).

(4)

Problem (
4
) minimizes a loss controlled by the Euclidean norm of the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
, while simultaneously minimizing the latent rank and the residual support size, whose the nuclear norm and the
\ell_{1}
-norm are the respective tightest convex relaxations
(
Fazel 2002
)
. A relevant challenge is that
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
is locally convex, i.e. it is convex into a specific range for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
. In this paper, we exploit this fact to propose a proximal gradient algorithm to compute (
4
)
and to prove algebraic and parametric consistency for the estimates of
\mathbf{L}^{*}
,
\mathbf{S}^{*}
, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}

obtained in this way.

1.3
Contribution

Our model setting imposes for the data vector an approximate factor model with sparse residual covariance. Unlike the original approximate factor model of
Chamberlain & Rothschild 1983
,
the residual covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}^{*}
is assumed to be positive definite, and its estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
obtained through (
4
) also is with high probability.
We then assume
\mathbf{S}^{*}
to be element-wise sparse.
This means that, conditionally on the factors, some variables are assumed to be still correlated and their correlation is captured by the non-zero elements of
\mathbf{S}^{*}
.
This residual sparsity structure is very suitable in high dimensions and quite more general than the restrictions proposed in
Bai & Li 2012
and
Bai & Li 2016
.
Nevertheless, the sparsistency of
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
(i.e. the exact recovery of the sparsity pattern of
\mathbf{S}^{*}
) is ensured with high probability.

We follow the algebraic approach of
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
, consisting in solving a variety-constrained version of (
4
),
which is proven to be equivalent to the unconstrained version of (
4
). However, instead of learning a latent graphical model from the sample precision matrix as in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
,
here we learn a factor model from the sample covariance matrix. Problem (
4
) satisfies the Restricted Strong Convexity of
Negahban et al. 2012

for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
as
n\to\infty
, so that the error bounds in Frobenius norm of
Agarwal et al. 2012
also hold for
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}
(see the Supplement Section D).

An important feature of our approach is that we allow for weak factors, i.e. factors whose pervasiveness diverges slower than
O(p)
as
p\to\infty
,
unlike factor models in
Bai 2003
;
Bai & Li 2012
;
Bai & Li 2016
, for instance. A related crucial aspect regards the latent rank selection.
Some well-known criteria to select the latent rank under pervasive approximate factor models are proposed in
Bai & Ng 2002
.
Those criteria are widely used in the literature, for instance in
Fan et al. 2013
to construct POET estimator
and in
Bai & Ng 2019
, which explores pervasive approximate factor model estimation via nuclear norm minimization.
Recently, weak approximate factor model estimation by PCA has been explored in
Bai & Ng 2023
,
but no criterion to recover the latent rank is proposed therein. In this paper, the latent rank is instead consistently estimated
by solving the single optimization problem (
4
), thanks to the nuclear norm term.

Such feature was already present in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
to learn the number of latent variables driving the marginal graphical model.
It has later been exploited in
Farnè & Montanari 2020
;
Farnè & Montanari 2024
to learn the number of latent factors under a similar factor model setting.
However, compared to
Farnè & Montanari 2020
;
Farnè & Montanari 2024
, in this paper we replace the Frobenius loss
\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})

with the log-det loss
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
, which is proven to work not worse, due to its ability to approximate underlying eigenvalues.
This paper also generalizes the distributional assumptions on factors and residuals, only imposing the existence of second moments.

Summing up, our approach is able to retrieve both the latent rank and the residual sparsity pattern with probability tending to
1
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
as
n\to\infty
, starting from the sample covariance matrix generated from an approximate factor model, under generalized pervasiveness of latent factors and intermediate residual covariance sparsity. This is achieved by solving the single problem (
4
), which allows the simultaneous recovery of low rank and sparse components, as well of latent rank and residual sparsity pattern, without the need to apply a second-stage algorithm after a first stage recovering low rank component and latent rank.

Theoretical foundations

2.1
Model definition

We assume for the data the following factor model structure:

\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{f}_{k}+\mathbf{\epsilon}_{k},

(5)

where
\mathbf{B}
is a
p\times r
semi-orthogonal loading matrix
such that
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{r}
, with
\mathbf{\Lambda}_{r}

r\times r
diagonal matrix and
\mathbf{B}^{\prime}=[\mathbf{b}_{1}\;\ldots\;\mathbf{b}_{p}]
,

\mathbf{f}_{k}\sim(\mathbf{0}_{r},\mathbf{I}_{r})

\mathbf{\epsilon}_{k}\sim(\mathbf{0}_{p},\mathbf{S^{*}})

p\times 1

Assuming that

,

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}=\mathrm{E}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}+\mathbf{S^{*}}=\mathbf{L^{*}}+\mathbf{S^{*}},

where
\mathbf{L^{*}}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{B}^{\prime}

, and

) is the low rank plus sparse decomposition of the covariance matrix of

\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
.
We define the unbiased sample covariance matrix as
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\prime}
.

;

(iii)

p^{1-\delta_{1}}\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{min,off}}=o(1)
;

(iv)

\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbf{S}^{*}_{jj}}=o(p^{\alpha_{1}})
.

Assumption
3
explicitly controls the sparsity pattern of
\mathbf{S}^{*}
,
in that part (i) imposes a bound on the maximum number of non-zeros per row, part (ii) imposes the maximum element magnitude to be
O(1)
,
part (iii) requires the minimum magnitude of nonzero entries to disappear as
n\to\infty
, part (iv) ensures the factor model to be meaningful as
p\to\infty
,
i.e.
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}^{*})=o(\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))
, because

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{S}^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{p}{\mathbf{S}^{*}_{jj}}=o(p^{\alpha})=o(rp^{\alpha})
, and
\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{L}^{*})=O(rp^{\alpha})
.
Moreover, parts (i) and (ii) together establish the traditional eigengap between
\lambda_{r}(\mathbf{B}^{\prime}\mathbf{B})
and
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{S}^{*})
as
p\to\infty
(see
Chamberlain & Rothschild 1983
),
because
\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{1,v}\leq\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{0,v}\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{2}p^{\delta_{1}}
,
and
\delta_{1}\leq{1}/{2}<{\alpha_{r}}
by Assumption
2
(i), while
parts (iii) and (iv) are able to ensure that the impact of the factorial component in (
5
) always dominates over the residual component in the sample covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
.

2.3
Geometry

In order to ensure the effectiveness of the composite penalty

\psi\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}

in recovering the latent rank
\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{L}^{*})=r
and
the residual number of nonzeros
|\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{S}^{*})|=s

(where
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{S}^{*})
is the support of
\mathbf{S}^{*}
, denoting the location of nonzeros,
and
|\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{S}^{*})|
is its dimension),
we need to control the geometric manifolds containing

\mathbf{L}^{*}
and
\mathbf{S}^{*}
.
As in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2011
, we assume
\mathbf{L}^{*}\in\mathcal{L}(r)

and
\mathbf{S}^{*}\in\mathcal{S}(s)
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(r)=\{\mathbf{L}\mid\mathbf{L}\succeq 0,{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{U}^{\prime}},\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\mathrm{diagonal}\},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(s)=\{\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\mid\mathbf{S}\succ 0,|\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{S})|\leq s\}.

(8)

\mathcal{L}(r)
is the algebraic variety of matrices with at most rank
r
,

\mathcal{S}(s)
is the algebraic variety of (element-wise) sparse matrices with
at most
s
non-zero elements, and the two varieties

\mathcal{L}(r)
and
\mathcal{S}(s)

can be disentangled if
\mathbf{L}^{*}
is far from being sparse,
and
\mathbf{S}^{*}
is far from being low rank.
For this reason,
Chandrasekaran et al. 2011
defines
the tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
and
\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*})
to
\mathcal{L}(r)
and
\mathcal{S}(s)
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*})=\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\mid\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{Y}_{2}\mathbf{U}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r};

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{L}^{*}\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\mbox{diagonal}\},\qquad\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*})=\{\mathbf{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\mid\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{N})\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{S}^{*})\},

and proposes the following rank-sparsity measures:

\displaystyle\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))=\max_{\mathbf{M}\in\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}),\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}\leq 1}{\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\infty}},\qquad\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))=\max_{\mathbf{N}\in\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}),\|\mathbf{N}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\ {\|\mathbf{N}\|_{2}}.

Note that
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))
is normalized to attain maximum
1
, irrespectively of the underlying assumptions
on latent eigenvalues.

Then, according to
Chandrasekaran et al. 2011
, the identifiability condition to be satisfied
requires a bound on
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))
.
For this reason, recalling from
Chandrasekaran et al. 2011
that

\bullet

\mathrm{inc}(\mathbf{L}^{*})=\max_{j=1,\ldots,p}\|\mathbb{P}_{{L}^{*}}\mathbf{e}_{j}\|
, where
\mathbf{e}_{j}
is the
j
-th canonical basis vector, and
\mathbb{P}_{{L}^{*}}
is the projection operator onto the row–column space of
\mathbf{L}^{*}
, with
\sqrt{{r}/{p}}\leq\mathrm{inc}(\mathbf{L}^{*})\leq 1
;

\bullet

\mathrm{deg}_{min}(\mathbf{S}^{*})=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}(\mathbf{S}^{*}_{ij}\neq 0)
and

\mathrm{deg}_{max}(\mathbf{S}^{*})=\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{0,v}
,

with

\rm{inc}(\mathbf{L}^{*})\leq\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\leq 2\rm{inc}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
and
\rm{deg}_{min}(\mathbf{S}^{*})\leq\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\leq\rm{deg}_{max}(\mathbf{S}^{*})
,
we can control the degree of transversality between
\mathcal{L}(r)
and
\mathcal{S}(s)
by the following assumption.

Assumption 4
.

For all
p\in\mathrm{N}
, there exist
\kappa_{L},\kappa_{S}>0
independent of
p
, with
\kappa_{S}\leq\delta_{2}
,
\kappa_{S}{\sqrt{r}}/\kappa_{L}\leq 1/24
,
(\kappa_{L}p)^{\delta_{1}}\leq\sqrt{p}
,
such that
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))={\sqrt{r}}/(\kappa_{L}p)^{\delta_{1}}
and
\mu(\Omega({\mathbf{S^{*}}}))=\kappa_{S}p^{\delta_{1}}
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
.

Assumption
4
states that the maximum degree of
\mathbf{S^{*}}
is
O(p^{\delta_{1}})
,
where
\delta_{1}<\alpha_{r}
by Assumptions
2
(i) and
3
(i).
More, the incoherence of
\mathbf{L}^{*}
is assumed to scale to
O(p^{-\delta_{1}})
, in order to keep the product
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))
proportional to
O(1)
, which is crucial to prove algebraic consistency (see Section
3
). This assumption resembles in nature the approximate factor model of
Chamberlain & Rothschild 1983
, because
\delta_{1}<\alpha_{r}
, such that the number of residual nonzeros will become negligible with respect to latent eigenvalues, and the manifold underlying
\mathbf{L}^{*}

will be progressively easier to retrieve as
p\to\infty
.

We now define the function

f_{\delta_{\epsilon}}(p,n)={\frac{\max\left(p^{{2}/{(1+\delta_{\epsilon})}},\sqrt{\ln(p)}\right)}{n^{1/2}}},

with
\delta_{\epsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
as defined in Assumption
1
,
and the scalar
\psi_{0}(\delta_{\epsilon},p,n)={f_{\delta_{\epsilon}}(p,n)}/{\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))}

(hereafter, we will write
\psi_{0}
to avoid notation burden).

Assumption 5
.

There exist
\delta_{L},\delta_{S}>0
such that

(i)

the minimum eigenvalue of
\mathbf{L}^{*}
,
\lambda_{r}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
, is greater than

\delta_{L}{\psi_{0}}/{\xi^{2}(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))}

(ii)

the minimum absolute value of the non-zero off-diagonal entries of
\mathbf{S}^{*}
,

\|\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{min,off}}
, is greater than
\delta_{S}{\psi_{0}}/{\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))}
.

Assumption
5
is crucial for identifiability, as it guarantees
that the solution pair of (
4
) lies on the “right” manifolds, i.e. that
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}\in\mathcal{L}(r)
and

\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(s)
with high probability as
n\to\infty
.
We remark that
\psi_{0}
, which controls the error bound of the solution pair
\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)
,
directly depends on the moment condition imposed on the vector of residuals
\mathbf{\epsilon}
by Assumption
1
.

2.4
Main results

Suppose that model (
5
) is a strict factor model with pervasive factors, i.e., for all
p\in\mathrm{N}
:

(i)

tails
: the elements of factor score vectors
\mathbf{f}_{k}
and residual vectors
\mathbf{\epsilon}_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
, present sub-exponential tails (meaning
\delta_{f}=\delta_{\epsilon}=+\infty
),
i.e., there exist

b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2}>0
independent of
p
such that, for any
l>0
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
:

\displaystyle\Pr(|{f}^{i}_{k}|>l)\leq\exp\{-(l/{b_{1}})^{c_{1}}\},\qquad\Pr(|{\epsilon}^{j}_{k}|>l)\leq\exp\{-(l/b_{2})^{c_{2}}\};

(ii)

latent eigenvalues
:
\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{L}^{*})\simeq O(p)
for
i=1,\ldots,r
(which means
\alpha_{1}=1,\ldots,\alpha_{r}=1
),
r=O(\ln(p))
and
\|\mathbf{b}_{j}\|_{\infty}=O(1)
for all
j=1,\ldots,p
;

(iii)

residual component
:
\mathbf{S}^{*}
is diagonal, which implies that
\mu(\mathbf{S}^{*})=1
;

(iv)

incoherence
:
\mathrm{inc}(\mathbf{L}^{*})=(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)^{1/3}
, with
\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}\in\mathbb{R}
s.t.
(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)^{1/3}\geq\sqrt{r/p}
,
and
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))={(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)}^{1/3}
. Consequently,
\psi_{0}=\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}/{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)}^{1/3}
;

(v)

minimum latent eigenvalue
: there exist
\delta_{L}>0
such that the minimum eigenvalue of
\mathbf{L}^{*}
,
\lambda_{r}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
, is greater than
\delta_{L}{\psi_{0}}/{\xi^{2}(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))}
, i.e., greater than
\frac{\delta_{L}p\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r}
.

(vi)

sample size requirement
: since it must hold
\frac{\delta_{L}p\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r}=o(p)

to cope with
\alpha_{r}=1
, it holds
\ln(p)/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let us set
\psi_{0}=\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}/(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)^{1/3}
,

\gamma\in\left[2{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)^{1/3}},{1}/{4}\right]
,
\rho_{0}=\gamma\psi_{0}
,
with
p\geq 512\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r
,
\psi=p\psi_{0}
, and
\rho=\rho_{0}
,
where
\psi
and
\rho
are the thresholds in (
4
).
Suppose that conditions (i)–(vi) hold.
Then, for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\ln(p)/n\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
the pair of solutions defined in (
4
) satisfies:

(i)

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}^{*}\|_{2}}{{p^{4/3}}}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
;

(ii)

\mathrm{P}\left(\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
;

(iii)

\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{rk}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n})=\mathrm{rk}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\to 1
;

(iv)

\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{sgn}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n})=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\to 1
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
,
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, as
n\to\infty
it holds:

(i)

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{2}}{p{{}^{1/3}}}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
;

(ii)

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{n}-{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{*}\|_{2}}{{p^{4/3}}}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
;

(iii)

\mathrm{P}(\lambda_{p}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n})>0)\to 1
;

(iv)

\mathrm{P}(\lambda_{p}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n})>0)\to 1
.

In addition, supposing that
\lambda_{p}(\mathbf{S}^{*})=O(p^{-\varepsilon})
and
\lambda_{p}({\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{*})=O(p^{\-1-\varepsilon})
for some
\varepsilon>0
,
the following statements hold for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
as
n\to\infty
:

(v)

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{-1}-\mathbf{S}^{*-1}\|_{2}}{p^{1/3+2\varepsilon}}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
;

(vi)

\mathrm{P}\left(\frac{\|\widehat{{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{n}^{-1}-{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{*-1}\|_{2}}{p^{4/3+2\varepsilon}}\leq 5.3125\frac{\sqrt{\ln(p)/n}}{(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r)^{1/3}}\right)\to 1
.

Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.1
contain many powerful results.
First, problem (
4
) is able to ensure that
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}
is rank-consistent (part (iii) of Theorem
2.1
),
and
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
is sparsistent (part (iv) of Theorem
2.1
). This is particularly important in factor modelling,
because the latent rank is hard to identify in the presence of weak factors in high dimensions.
This identifiability result is obtained thanks to the control of the tangent spaces (via
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))
and
\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))
) to the low rank and the sparse matrix varieties containing
\mathbf{L}^{*}
and
\mathbf{S}^{*}
respectively, and is ensured under the mild condition
p\geq 512\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r
,
with
\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}
possibly smaller than
1
(as long as
(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)^{-1/3}\geq\sqrt{r/p}
). Note that the corresponding condition for identifiability in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012

is in the best case
p\geq 54^{2}\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r
with
\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}\geq 1
, which actually requires a super-large dimension.

Then, parts (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2.1
, as well as parts (i) and (ii) of Corollary
2.1
, contain convergence results in spectral norm
for the covariance matrix estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
,
which work under the mild condition
\ln(p)/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, a situation is configured where the sample size requirement is almost negligible, and a high dimension
p
is encouraged,
although identifiability still works for relatively small values of
p
, provided that the latent rank
r
is very small.
Parts (iii) and (iv) ensure that
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}
are positive definite, which means that their inverses can be
safely obtained when necessary (e.g., to estimate factor scores). Finally, we also obtain the convergence rates for the two inverses (parts (v) and (vi) of Corollary
2.1
).

All these results are obtained with a little inconvenience: an additional term
p^{1/3}
in the convergence rates in spectral norm,
which is due to the assumption
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))={(\widetilde{\kappa}_{L}r/p)}^{1/3}
. This is the price to pay to solve
a single-step problem like (
4
) and obtain all these results at the same time.

In the following, we extend Theorem
2.1
and Corollary
2.1
, by generalizing the divergence rates of latent eigenvalues (allowing for weak factors),
by permitting a non-diagonal residual component (element-wise sparse), by tolerating that the distributions of factors and residuals do not possess all moments,
and by exploring what happens to the identifiability of underlying manifolds and to the convergence rates when
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))
and
\mu(\mathbf{S}^{*})

present a more general, less favorable shape.

3
Consistency definitions

A distinctive feature of the nuclear norm plus
\ell_{1}
-norm penalization approach is to obtain a double type of consistency for covariance matrix estimators:
the usual parametric one (in a suitable norm) and the algebraic one, which is defined as follows.

Definition 1
.

A sequence of estimator pairs
\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)
, defined as in (
2
) and indexed in
n
,
is an algebraically consistent estimate of the low rank plus sparse decomposition (
6
)
for the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
if the following conditions hold with probability approaching
1
as
n\to\infty
:

1.

the low rank estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}
is positive semidefinite and rank-consistent, i.e.,
\mathrm{rk}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n})=r
;

2.

the residual estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
is positive definite and sparsistent, i.e.,
\mathrm{sgn}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n})=\mathrm{sgn}(\mathbf{S}^{*})
;

3.

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
is positive definite.

We now recall that

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\psi\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1},

where
\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}(\mathbf{L})
,

\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}|\mathbf{S}_{ij}|
,

\psi
and
\rho
are non-negative threshold parameters.
From Section
2.3
, we get that
\psi_{0}={f_{\delta_{\epsilon}}(p,n)}/{\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))}
,
and we set
\rho_{0}=\gamma\psi_{0}
,
\psi=p^{\alpha_{1}}\psi_{0}
, and
\rho=\rho_{0}
.
Then, we can rewrite
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
as

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=p^{\alpha_{1}}\psi_{0}{\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}}+\rho_{0}{\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}}
.
More, defining
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\psi^{-1}\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
, we obtain

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})={\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}}+p^{-\alpha_{1}}\gamma\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}
.
The dual norm of
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
can thus be defined as

g_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\max\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{L}\|_{2}}{p^{\alpha_{1}}},\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\|_{\infty}}{\gamma}\right)
.
Such definition is meaningful because, under Assumptions
2
(i) and
3
(i) with
\delta_{1}=0
,
we obtain that
\frac{\|\mathbf{L}\|_{2}}{p^{\alpha_{1}}}=O(1)
and
\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\|_{\infty}}{\gamma}=O(1)
,
which renders the two quantities perfectly comparable.

Relying on these considerations, we focus on the solution of the following equivalent version of problem (
4
):

\displaystyle\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})+\psi_{0}\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho_{0}\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}}{p^{\alpha_{1}}},

(9)

which may also be expressed as

\displaystyle\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})+\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\gamma\frac{\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}}{p^{\alpha_{1}}}.

(10)

The consistency norm
g_{\gamma}
,
with which the direct sum
\mathcal{L}(r)\oplus\mathcal{S}(s)
is naturally equipped (cfr. paragraph 3.3 in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
),
is defined as

g_{\gamma}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}^{*},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}^{*})=\max\left(\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}-{\mathbf{L}^{*}\|_{2}}}{p^{\alpha_{1}}},\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}^{*}\|_{\infty}}{\gamma}\right).

(11)

g_{\gamma}
-consistency is our target, which also implies
\ell_{2}
-consistency for
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
, as we later show.
For this reason, we define parametric consistency as follows.

Definition 2
.

A sequence of estimator pairs
\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)
, defined as in (
2
) and indexed in
n
,
is a parametrically consistent estimate of the low rank plus sparse decomposition (
6
) for the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
if the norm
g_{\gamma}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}-\mathbf{L}^{*},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}-\mathbf{S}^{*})
converges to
0
with probability approaching
1
as
n\to\infty
.

4
Algebraic consistency

4.1
Local convexity

Let us reconsider our optimization problem

\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}\bigr)=\argmin_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}}\phi(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),

where
\phi(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})+\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
,

\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
,

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\psi\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}
.

In order to optimize
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
, we need to prove that

\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
, with
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}
,
is convex within some range for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
.
In the univariate context, the function
0.5\ln\det(1+x^{2})
is convex if and only if
|x|<{1}/{\sqrt{2}}
.
In the multivariate context, it is therefore reasonable to suppose that a similar condition on
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}
ensures local convexity.
In other words, we aim to show that there exists a positive real
C
such that, whenever
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\|<C
,
the function
0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
is convex with high probability.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
\delta_{\phi}>0
the function
\ln\det\bigl(\delta_{\phi}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}\bigr)

is convex on the closed ball
\mathcal{C_{\delta_{\phi}}}=\{\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathrm{\penalty\ \penalty\ is\penalty\ a\penalty\ real\penalty\ \penalty\ }p\times p\mathrm{\penalty\ matrix\penalty\ },\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\|_{2}\leq(3\delta_{\phi}p)^{-1}\}
.

Proof

Proposition
4.1
is a direct specialization of
Bernardi & Farnè 2022
, Lemma 2. ∎

Proposition
4.1
evidences that the function
\ln\det\bigl(\delta_{\phi}^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}\bigr)

can be made locally convex in arbitrary ball near
0
, choosing a suitable
\delta_{\phi}
respecting the convexity radius

\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\|_{2}\leq(3\delta_{\phi}p)^{-1}
.
This proposition is necessary to study the random behaviour of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
around
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
with respect to convexity,
because
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
is a random matrix, and so the convexity condition
\|\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\|_{2}\leq(3\delta_{\phi}p)^{-1}

must be ensured for
\delta_{\phi}=1
. First and second derivative of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
, as well as its asymptotic behaviour,
are studied in the Supplement Section B.

4.2
Fisher information

The key to obtain the algebraic consistency of Definition
1

is to control the algebraic features of the low rank and sparse matrix varieties
\mathcal{L}(r)
and
\mathcal{S}(s)
containing
\mathbf{L}^{*}
and
\mathbf{S}^{*}
respectively, as well as their tangent spaces
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
and
\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*})
,
because the low rank variety
\mathcal{L}(r)
is locally curve, and so its recovery by using
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
in problem (
4
) may be very sensitive
to small perturbations in
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
(see
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
for reference, as well as
Chen et al. 2013
and
Hsu et al. 2011
for alternative approaches).
Let us define the following reshaped version of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
:

\widetilde{\phi}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}),

which can also be expressed as

\widetilde{\phi}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}^{\prime})
,
where
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
,
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}+\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}
, with
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{L}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{S}
.
We regard the function
-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})

as a nonlinear function of the squared sample covariance matrix,
which we need to maximize.
-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
is concave into
the convexity range of Proposition
4.1
.
Therefore, to ensure that
-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
is concave for
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}
,
once defined
\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
, we need that

p^{-\alpha_{1}}\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|\leq 1/(3p)
, which is dominated by the condition
\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|\leq 1/3
.

In this view, under its conditions, we can define

\mathcal{I}^{*}
, the operator associating to each pair
(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})

the Fisher information of
-\widetilde{\phi}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}[-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})]}{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}}\right\}=-\mathrm{E}\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}[-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})]}{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}}\right\}.

In the Supplement (Section B.3), it is proved that,
imposing the condition
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|\leq\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}
,
we can estimate from below the expectation

\Bigg\|\mathrm{E}\left(\frac{\partial{\widetilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})}}{\partial{\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}}}\right)\Bigg\|_{2}\geq\frac{(1-\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}^{2})^{2}}.

(12)

Analogously, imposing the condition
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta^{\infty}_{\widetilde{\Delta}}
, we can estimate from below the expectation

\Bigg\|\mathrm{E}\left(\frac{\partial{\widetilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})}}{\partial{\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}}}\right)\Bigg\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{(1-\delta^{\infty}_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta^{\infty 2}_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}.

Importantly, since
\|.\|_{\infty}\leq\|.\|_{2}
, it holds
\delta^{\infty}_{\widetilde{\Delta}}\leq\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}
.
Consequently, under the conditions of Proposition
4.1
, we can note that
\delta^{\infty}_{\widetilde{\Delta}}\leq\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}\leq 1/3p
,
so that

\frac{(1-\delta^{\infty}_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta^{\infty 2}_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}\geq\frac{(1-\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}^{2})^{2}}\geq\frac{9p^{2}(9p^{2}+1-6p)}{(9p^{2}+1)^{2}}\geq\frac{9}{25}.

(13)

This means that
\widetilde{\phi}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})
is strongly convex for all
p
and
n

in
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}
,
whenever the convexity condition
\|p^{-\alpha_{1}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}\|_{2}\leq 1/(3p)
of Proposition
4.1
,
dominated by
\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}\|_{2}\leq 1/3
, is satisfied.

4.3
Manifold retrieval

Let us define the following measure of transversality between two algebraic matrix varieties
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
as in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
, equation (2.7):

\varrho(\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2})=\max_{\|\mathbf{N}\|_{2}\leq 1}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\mathbf{N}-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}\mathbf{N}\|_{2},

(14)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
are the projection operators onto
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, respectively.
Hereafter, let
\Omega=\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*})
and

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*})
, where
{\Omega}
is the space tangent to
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(s)
(see (
8
)) at
\mathbf{S}^{*}
and
\mathcal{T}
is the space tangent to
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(r)
(see (
7
)) at
\mathbf{L}^{*}
.
We define the manifold
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
as the tangent space to a generic matrix
\mathbf{L}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L})=\{\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}\mid\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{Y}_{1}^{\prime}+\mathbf{Y}_{2}\mathbf{U}^{\prime},\mathbf{Y}_{1},\mathbf{Y}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r},\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{I}_{r},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\mbox{diagonal}\},

such that
\varrho(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\xi(\mathcal{T})
with
\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\in(0,1/2]
,
and we set the Cartesian product
\mathcal{Y}={\Omega}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
.
In order to assess the effect of a deviation from
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{*}
along the varieties
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
,
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}^{\perp}
,

\Omega
,
\Omega^{\perp}
under the constraints
\mathbf{L}\in\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
,

\varrho(\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathcal{T})\leq\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\xi(\mathcal{T})
with
\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\in(0,1/2]
, and
\mathbf{S}\in\Omega
,
we need to evaluate (
12
) under each case.

Following
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
and previous reasonings, we can define and compute the quantities

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathcal{T}}=\max_{\mathbf{M}\in{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\infty}=1}\|\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{S}^{*})\|_{\infty}=1\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\beta_{\Omega}=\max_{\mathbf{M}\in{\Omega},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L}^{*},\mathbf{M})\|_{2}=1.

Analogously, to study the behaviour of
\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
along the varieties
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}
,
\mathcal{T^{\prime}}^{\perp}
,
\Omega
, and
\Omega^{\perp}
,
following
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
we define:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathcal{T}}=\min_{\mathbf{M}\in{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}}(\mathbf{M})\|_{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\delta_{\mathcal{T}}=\max_{\mathbf{M}\in{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}^{\perp}},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{2}=1}\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}^{\perp}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}^{\perp}}(\mathbf{M})\|_{2};

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Omega}=\min_{\mathbf{M}\in{\Omega},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\infty}=1}\|\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}(\mathbf{M})\|_{\infty}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\delta_{\Omega}=\max_{\mathbf{M}\in{\Omega^{\perp}},\|\mathbf{M}\|_{\infty}=1}\|\mathbb{P}_{\Omega^{\perp}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathbb{P}_{\Omega^{\perp}}(\mathbf{M})\|_{\infty}.

Then, recalling that
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|\leq\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}
and exploiting (
12
), we can derive that:

\bullet

setting
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{S}^{*}))
, we get

\alpha_{\mathcal{T}}=\frac{(1-\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}^{2})^{2}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{T}}=\beta_{\mathcal{T}}-\alpha_{\mathcal{T}}
;

\bullet

setting
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}=p^{-\alpha_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}^{*}+\mathbf{M}))
, we get

\alpha_{\Omega}=\frac{(1-\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}})^{2}}{(1+\delta_{\widetilde{\Delta}}^{2})^{2}}
and

\delta_{\Omega}=\beta_{\Omega}-\alpha_{\Omega}
.

Since
\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
may be regarded as a map, we can consequently write

\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S})
;

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\mathbf{S})
;

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\perp}}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbb{P}_{\Omega^{\perp}}\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime\perp}}\mathbf{S})
.
These quantities need to be studied in order to ensure that the elements of the spaces
{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{T}
are individually identifiable
under the map
\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
, and that the sensitivity of
\mathcal{I}^{*}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
to small perturbations
has a limited impact on the recovery of the direct sum
\mathcal{L}(r)\oplus\mathcal{S}(s)
by problem (
4
).

Therefore, to ensure algebraic consistency,
we need to explicitly control the quantities
\alpha_{\Omega}
,
\alpha_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}}
,
\delta_{\Omega}
,
\delta_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}}
,
\beta_{\Omega}
,
\beta_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}}
.
Analogously to
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
, we define

\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}=\min(\alpha_{\Omega},\alpha_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}})
,
\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}=\max(\delta_{\Omega},\delta_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}})=1-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}
,

\beta_{\mathcal{Y}}=\max(\beta_{\Omega},\beta_{\mathcal{T^{\prime}}})
, and we assume the following.

Assumption 6
.

For some
\nu\in(0,{1}/{2}]
, it holds

{\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}{\delta_{\mathcal{Y}}}\leq 1-2\nu
.

Combining
\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}}=1-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}
and Assumption
6
, we get

{\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}}^{-1}{(1-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}})}\leq 1-2\nu
,
which leads to the condition
\nu\geq\frac{2\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}-1}{2\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}}
.
At this stage, we can state the following proposition, which improves and extends Proposition 3.3 in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
to the case of the log-det loss
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\|_{2}\leq 1/3
and Assumption
6
hold.
Let
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0.70711
,
\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\in(0,0.5]
,

\gamma\in\left[\frac{\frac{2(1-\kappa_{\mathcal{T}})}{(1+\kappa_{\mathcal{T}})}\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))(1-\nu)}{\nu\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}},\frac{\nu\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}}{4\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\beta_{\mathcal{Y}}(1-\nu)}\right],

\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*})\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\leq\frac{1-\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}}{8(1+\kappa_{\mathcal{T}})}\Biggl(\frac{\nu\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}}{\beta_{\mathcal{Y}}(1-\nu)}\Biggr)^{2},

with
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}},\beta_{\mathcal{Y}},\gamma,\nu
as previously defined.
Then, for all
(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L})\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
\mathcal{Y}={\Omega}\times\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
with
\varrho(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{T}^{\prime})\leq\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}\xi(\mathcal{T})
,
the following statements hold:

i)

\min_{(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})\in\mathcal{Y},\|\mathbf{L}\|_{2}=1,\|\mathbf{S}\|_{\infty}=\gamma}g_{\gamma}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}))\geq\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\Bigl(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\Bigr)g_{\gamma}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L})
;

ii)

g_{\gamma}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}^{\perp}}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}))\leq(1-\nu)g_{\gamma}(\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{A}^{{\dagger}}\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{A}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{L}))
.

Remark 1
.

Remarkably, the quantities
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}},\beta_{\mathcal{Y}},\gamma_{\mathcal{Y}},\nu
can now be explicitly computed, unlike in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
.
Comparing our identifiability condition with the one in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
, we can observe that, although we now need to impose the constraint
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0.70711
,
our condition is much weaker. In particular, imposing the maximum value
\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}=1/2
as they do, we get
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\leq 1/24
instead of

\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\leq 1/54
.
Considering the value
\kappa_{\mathcal{T}}=1/4
, which is obtained as part (iii) of Corollary C.1 in the Supplement,
our identifiability condition reads as
\xi(\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{L}^{*}))\mu(\Omega(\mathbf{S}^{*}))\leq 3/40
.

Remark 2
.

The condition
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0.70711
is explicitly assumed.
However, inequality (
13
) also shows that, as
p\to\infty
,
the actual lower bound for
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}
gets increasingly larger, progressively approaching
1
.
Moreover, the lower bound
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}(3/2-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}})
of part (i) cannot be smaller than
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}/2
(which is the lower bound in
Chandrasekaran et al. 2012
),
since
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\in(1/2,1]
by Assumption
6
, which leads to
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}(3/2-\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}})\geq 1/2
.

5
Parametric consistency

We now explicitly compare the rescaled heuristics of
Farnè & Montanari 2020
;
Farnè & Montanari 2024
, which is defined as

\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\frac{1}{2p^{\alpha_{1}}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S})\|_{F}^{2}+\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),

(15)

to the solution of problem (
10

We write

\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})+\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5p^{-\alpha_{1}}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S})\|_{F}^{2},

and

\widetilde{\phi}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\widetilde{\phi}^{(ld)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})+\mathbb{P}_{\gamma}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\widetilde{\phi}^{(ld)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}))^{2}).

The Supplement (Section B.1) shows that

\widetilde{\phi}_{D}^{{}^{\prime}(ld)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}p^{-\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n},

where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}))
. We can easily derive
that
\widetilde{\phi}_{D}^{{}^{\prime}(F)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=p^{-\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
.

Let us define the pair of solutions

(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(F)},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(F)})=\arg\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})

and
(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(ld)},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(ld)})=\arg\min_{\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}}\widetilde{\phi}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}),

with
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{(F)}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(F)}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(F)}
and

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{n}^{(ld)}=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(ld)}+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(ld)}
.
The following important theorem holds
(see Section C in the Supplement for the proof).

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
5
holds and
\alpha_{\mathcal{Y}}\geq 0.77155
.
Under the conditions of Proposition
4.2
,
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
it holds with probability
1-O(1/n^{2})
as
n\to\infty
:

\frac{\max g_{\gamma}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(ld)}-\mathbf{S}^{*},\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(ld)}-\mathbf{L}^{*})}{\max g_{\gamma}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(F)}-\mathbf{S}^{*},\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(F)}-\mathbf{L}^{*})}\leq 1.

Theorem
5.1
states that the minimax error bound in
g_{\gamma}
-norm of
(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(ld)},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(ld)})

is systematically not larger than the corresponding bound of
(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{n}^{(F)},\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{(F)})
.
This holds because the gradient of
\widetilde{\phi}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
exists if and only if the series whose limit is

(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}
converges, and any norm of
(\mathbf{I}_{p}+p^{-2\alpha_{1}}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}

is smaller than
1
if Proposition

holds. This implies that
g_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\phi}^{(ld)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}))\leq g_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\phi}^{(F)}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S}))
.

{p^{4/(1+\delta_{\epsilon})}}=o(n)
,
which equals to
{p^{2}}/{n}=o(1)
if
\delta_{\epsilon}=1
(fourth moment existence),
to
{p^{4/3}}/{n}=o(1)
if
\delta_{\epsilon}=2
(sixth moment existence),
to
{p}/{n}=o(1)
if
\delta_{\epsilon}=3
(eight moment existence).
The condition
p^{1-\delta_{1}}f_{\delta_{\epsilon}}(p,n)=o(1)
, equivalent to
{p^{(1-\delta_{1})+{4/(1+\delta_{\epsilon})}}}=o(n)
,
would instead rise to ensure the recovery of the residual sparsity pattern, i.e. the sparsistency of
\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
.

6
Computation

For each threshold pair
(\psi,\rho)
(we refer to Section E.2 in the Supplement for their initialisation), we can calculate

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)
from Algorithm 1 (see Section E.2)
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(F)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(F)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(F)}_{\rm{A}}(\psi,\rho)
from Algorithm 2 (see Section E.2).
Following
Farnè & Montanari 2020
;
Farnè & Montanari 2024
, we also perform the unshrinkage of estimated latent eigenvalues,
as this operation improves the sample total loss as much as possible in the finite sample. We thus get
the UNALCE (UNshrunk ALCE) estimates as:

\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\rm{U}}=\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm{A}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{\rm{A}}+\psi\mathbf{I}_{r})\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\rm{A}}^{\prime},\;\mathrm{diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm{U}})=\mathrm{diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\rm{A}})-\mathrm{diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\rm{U}}),\;\mathrm{off-diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm{U}})=\mathrm{off-diag}(\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{\rm{A}}),

where
\psi>0
is any chosen eigenvalue threshold parameter.
In this way, for each threshold pair
(\psi,\rho)
,
we can derive the pairs of estimates

\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho),\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)\bigr)
or

\bigl(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(F)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho),\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(F)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)\bigr)
,
and as a consequence, the overall UNALCE estimates as

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(ld)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)
and

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(F)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)=\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{(F)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)+\widehat{\mathbf{S}}^{(F)}_{\rm{U}}(\psi,\rho)
.

Then, given the latent variance proportions
\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}}=(\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}}))^{-1}{\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}})}
and

\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}}=(\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}}))^{-1}{\mathrm{tr}(\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}})}
,
we can select the optimal threshold pairs
(\psi_{U},\rho_{U})
and
(\psi_{A},\rho_{A})

by minimizing the MC criteria

\displaystyle MC(\psi,\rho)_{U}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{{\widehat{r}\|\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}}}\|_{2}}{\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}}},\frac{{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}}}\|_{1,v}}{{\gamma}(1-\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{U}})}\right\},

\displaystyle MC(\psi,\rho)_{A}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{{\widehat{r}\|\widehat{\mathbf{L}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}}}\|_{2}}{\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}}},\frac{{\|\widehat{\mathbf{S}}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}}}\|_{1,v}}{{\gamma}(1-\widehat{\theta}(\psi,\rho)_{\rm{A}})}\right\},

where

is the ratio between the sparsity and the latent eigenvalue threshold
(see
Farnè & Montanari 2020
for more details). In this way, we can select the optimal threshold pairs

(\psi_{A},\rho_{A})=\arg\min_{(\psi,\rho)}MC(\psi,\rho)_{A}
and

(\psi_{U},\rho_{U})=\arg\min_{(\psi,\rho)}MC(\psi,\rho)_{U}
.

Conclusions

In this paper, we study the estimation of large covariance matrices in high dimensions
under the low rank plus sparse assumption by minimizing a log-det heuristics augmented by a nuclear norm plus
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty.
In particular, we prove the local convexity and the Lipschitzianity of the proposed log-det heuristics,
which allows to solve the optimization problem via a proximal gradient algorithm. Then, we solve the low rank and sparse algebraic variety identification problem behind
the optimization problem, by showing that the log-det heuristics can be made locally convex and by controlling its Fisher information.
Relying on these results, we prove
the algebraic and parametric consistency of the ensuing pair of low rank and sparse covariance matrix estimators.
We also prove that the same pair of estimators performs systematically not
worse than the corresponding estimator obtained by nuclear norm plus
\ell_{1}
-norm penalized Frobenius loss minimization.
A new solution algorithm, that also permits to control for the input threshold parameters, is proposed,
and an ECB supervisory data example shows the usefulness of our approach on a real dataset.

Appendix B
Mathematical and probabilistic guarantees

The mathematical properties of
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
have been extensively studied in
Bernardi & Farnè 2022
.
This section extends the recalled mathematical analysis results related to

\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
.
To this end, we recall the first and the second derivatives of

\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
.

B.1
First derivative

Let us denote the spectral decomposition of the non-definite symmetric random matrix

\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
as
\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}
, so that the spectral decomposition of the semi-definite matrix

\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}
results to be
\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{2}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}
.
We study the first derivative of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
.

Proposition B.1
.

Define the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\Delta}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}-(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1})\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}
.
Assume the conditions of Proposition
4.1
(main paper) with
\delta_{\phi}=1
. Then, the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\frac{\partial\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}{\partial\mathbf{L}}=\frac{\partial\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}{\partial\mathbf{S}}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}

(36)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{D}_{\Delta}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}.

(37)

Proof

The proof of equation (
36
) is the proof of equation (6) in
Bernardi & Farnè 2022
,
provided that
(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}
exists.
Since it holds

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})^{(ld)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}^{{}^{\prime}}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}^{{}^{\prime}}_{n})^{j}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})^{2j+1},

(38)

we can observe that series (
38
) converges if and only if
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})^{2}<1
.
At this stage, since Proposition
4.1
with
\delta_{\phi}=1
ensures that
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})\leq(3p)^{-1}
,
the existence of
\phi^{\prime}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})^{(ld)}
is ensured.

To prove equation (
37
), recalling Woodbury formula, we write

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{2}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime})^{-1}=\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime},

because
\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}
is orthogonal.
Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime})\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}.

Going on, since
\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}
is orthogonal, we can write

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}-\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}[\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}-(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}]\mathbf{U}_{\Delta}^{\prime}

which proves the thesis because
\mathbf{D}_{\Delta}=(\mathbf{I}_{p}-(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}^{-2}+\mathbf{I}_{p})^{-1})\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta}
.
∎

Proposition
B.1
provides a direct link between the first derivative of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5\ln\det\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})
(where
\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
)
and the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\Delta}
, which is a
p\times p
diagonal matrix
whose
i
-th element,
i=1,\ldots,p
, is
\mathbf{D}_{ii}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta,ii}\bigl(1-{(1+\mathbf{\Lambda}_{\Delta,ii}^{-2})^{-1}}\bigr)
.
This result is of extreme interest, because the matrix
\mathbf{D}_{\Delta}
performs a sample eigenvalue correction: it shrinks down very large or very small sample eigenvalues,
i.e. those most affecting matrix inversion. This is why minimizing
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5\ln\det\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})

leads to eigenvalue-regularized covariance matrix estimates. This is a main difference between
\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S})\|_{F}^{2}=0.5\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})
, whose gradient is just
\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
,
and
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})
,
because the gradient of the smooth loss
\mathcal{L}^{(ld)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
is designed to avoid
rank deficiency by intrinsically regularizing sample eigenvalues, unlike
\mathcal{L}^{(F)}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n})
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\sigma_{ij}\partial\sigma_{hk}}0.5\ln\det\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\bigl(0.5\mathrm{Hess}\ln\det\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})\bigr)_{ijhk}.

Suppose that

Proof

Equation (

proved as equation (11) in

will be crucial to study the random behaviour of the Hessian of

around

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{I}^{*}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})\|

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\mathrm{E}\left(\frac{\partial{\widetilde{\phi}^{\prime}_{D}(\widetilde{\mathbf{L}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}})}}{\partial{\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}}}\right)\Bigg\|=\Bigg\|\mathrm{E}\left(\frac{\partial(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{n}}{\partial{\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}}}\right)\Bigg\|.

\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=\psi\|\mathbf{L}\|_{*}+\rho\|\mathbf{S}\|_{1}

The gradient of
\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})
with respect to
\mathbf{L}
and to
\mathbf{S}
is the same, and corresponds to

\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}
under the conditions of Proposition
B.1
.

We now define the
2p^{2}
-dimensional vectorized gradient

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{vec}\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}{\partial(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}\right)=\{\mathrm{vec}\bigl[\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\bigr]^{\prime}\mathrm{vec}\bigl[\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\bigr]^{\prime}\}^{\prime}.

The following proposition controls the effect on the vectorized gradient of a perturbation matrix
\mathbf{H}
.

Proposition E.1
.

Define two
p\times p
matrices
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}=\mathbf{L}_{2}+\mathbf{S}_{2}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}+\mathbf{H}
,
\epsilon>0
,
such that
\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
,
\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}-\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}
, and
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}-\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}
is a perturbation matrix.
Set the difference vector
\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})
.
Then, it holds:

\|\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})\|_{F}\leq\frac{5}{2}\|\mathbf{H}\|_{F}.

Proof

From the definition of
\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
and
\varphi(\mathbf{\Sigma})
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{d}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2})\|_{F}\leq 2\|(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}-(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}\|_{F}.

(68)

Therefore, to prove the thesis, we need to bound the
rhs
of (
68
).
To this purpose, we study the matrix
(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{1,n}-(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}
,
that is equal to

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I}_{p}+(\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})-(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{2,n}.

Then, we recall from
Bernardi & Farnè 2022
, Lemmas 3 and 4, the Lipschitzianity of the smooth function

\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})=0.5\ln\det(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime}),

and of its gradient function,

\frac{\partial\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}{\partial\mathbf{L}}=\frac{\partial\phi_{D}(\mathbf{L},\mathbf{S})}{\partial\mathbf{S}}=F(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}^{\prime})^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{n},

respectively. In particular, Lemma 4 in
Bernardi & Farnè 2022
shows that the gradient function is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant equal to
{5}/{4}
:

\|F(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})-F(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n})\|_{2}\leq\frac{5}{4}\epsilon\|\mathbf{H}\|_{2}+O(\epsilon^{2}),

(69)

with

F(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})=(\mathbf{I}_{p}+(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H})^{\prime})^{-1}(\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}+\epsilon\mathbf{H}),

for any
\epsilon>0
.
Therefore, from inequality (
68
), noticing that inequality (
69
) holds for the Frobenius norm as well and setting
\epsilon=1
, the thesis follows. ∎
