Algebraically closed σ \sigma -fields
Masood Aryapoor

Abstract
The concept of a skew root of a skew polynomial is used to introduce notions of algebraic closedness for σ \sigma -fields, that is, a field equipped with an endomorphism. It is shown that every σ \sigma -field can be embedded in algebraically closed σ \sigma -fields of different types.



1
Introduction

Skew polynomials were first studied by Ore
[
23
]
. Since Ore’s article was published in 1933, skew polynomials have been the subject of study by many authors. In
[
16
]
, Lam introduced the notion of a skew root of a skew polynomial in order to study a generalization of the Vandermonde matrix (see also
[
14
]
). This notion allows us to define a concept of algebraicity which has been used by Lam and Leroy to formulate and solve some problems regarding skew polynomials, see
[
15
,
17
,
18
]
. The main goal of this paper is to study this concept of algebraicity in the context of field extensions.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 deals with preliminaries and elementary problems such as interpolation for skew polynomials and Vieta’s formula for skew polynomials. In Section 3, we study field extensions from the perspective of skew polynomials. It is shown that for any nonconstant skew polynomial, there is an extension field in which the given skew polynomial has a skew root. Section 4 deals with the main subject of this paper, namely, algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields. It turns out that there are different notions of ”algebraic closedness” in the context of skew roots, some of which are introduced in this section, and moreover, it is shown that they exit. Finally, in Section 5, we discuss the notion of a partial fraction decomposition for skew polynomials. It is shown that an arbitrary ”skew rational functions” over an algebraically closed
\sigma
-field has a relatively simple partial fraction decomposition.

We conclude the introduction with some remarks. In this paper, all rings are assumed to be associative and contain an identity element. In general, a skew polynomial is an element of a skew polynomial ring
R[T;\sigma,\delta]
, where
R
is a (not necessarily commutative) ring,
\sigma:R\to R
is an endomorphism
and
\delta:R\to R
is a
\sigma
-derivation. However, in this paper, we work exclusively with skew polynomial rings
R[T;\sigma,\delta]
for which
R
is a commutative field and
\delta
is the zero map. Given a set
X
, we denote the identity map of
X
by
1_{X}
. For a subset
X
of a ring
R
, the notation
X^{*}
stands for
X\setminus\{0\}
.

2
Skew polynomials and skew roots

This section is devoted to preliminaries and some elementary results regarding skew polynomials and skew roots. We begin by reviewing basic facts about the ring of skew polynomials over a field, see
[
7
, Chapter 2]
for more details. Let
K
be a (commutative) field and
\sigma:K\to K
be an endomorphism. The pair
(K,\sigma)
is called a
\sigma
-field. A (nonzero) skew polynomial
P(T)
over
K
is a ”polynomial” of the form

P(T)=a_{0}+a_{1}T+\cdots+a_{n}T^{n},

where
n\geq 0
and
a_{0},...,a_{n}\in K
with
a_{n}\neq 0
. The number
n
, denoted by
\deg P
, is called the degree of
P(T)
. We usually use the word ”polynomial” instead of the expression ”skew polynomial” if there is no risk of confusion. The set of all skew polynomials over
K
is denoted by
K[T;\sigma]
. This set has a ring structure which resembles the ring of ordinary polynomials. A crucial difference is that
Ta=\sigma(a)T
for all
a\in K
. It is known that Euclidean algorithm for right division holds
K[T;\sigma]
, a fact which has a number of consequences:
K[T;\sigma]
is a left principal ideal domain;
K[T;\sigma]
is a left Ore domain whose field of fractions is denoted by
K(T;\sigma)
. Elements of
K(T;\sigma)
can be expressed as
P(T)^{-1}Q(T)
where
0\neq P(T),Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. Besides the rings
K[T;\sigma]
and
K(T;\sigma)
, one has the ring of ”skew power series”
K[[T;\sigma]]
, and if
\sigma
is an automorphism, the skew field
K((T;\sigma))
of Laurent series over
(K,\sigma)
whose elements are of the form

a_{0}+a_{1}T+\cdots+a_{n}T^{n}+\cdots,

and

a_{-m}T^{-m}+\cdots+a_{-1}T^{-1}+a_{0}+a_{1}T+\cdots+a_{n}T^{n}+\cdots,

respectively. We have the following inclusions:

\begin{matrix}K[T;\sigma]&\subset&K[[T;\sigma]]\\
\cap&&\cap\\
K(T;\sigma)&\subset&K((T;\sigma))\end{matrix}

If
\sigma:K\to K
is an automorphism, then
K[T;\sigma]
is a principal ideal domain, that is, it is both a left and a right principal ideal domain. Moreover, any
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
can be written as a ”right polynomial”:

P(T)=b_{0}+Tb_{1}+\cdots+T^{n}b_{n},

where
b_{0},...,b_{n}\in K
. In fact, if
P(T)
, as a left polynomial, has the expression

P(T)=a_{0}+a_{1}T+\cdots+a_{n}T^{n},

then
P(T)
, as a right polynomial, has the expression

P(T)=a_{0}+T\sigma^{-1}(a_{1})+\cdots+T^{n}\sigma^{-n}(a_{n}).

As an example of a ring of skew polynomials, we have the ring
\mathbb{C}[T;\,\bar{\,}\,]
of skew polynomials over
\mathbb{C}
, where the endomorphism
\bar{\,}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is the familiar map sending a complex number to its complex conjugate.

2.1
Preliminaries

In this subsection, we review some known facts regarding roots of skew polynomials. The main references for this part are the papers
[
16
,
14
]
. Throughout this subsection,
(K,\sigma)
is a fixed
\sigma
-field.

An element
a\in K
is called a
\sigma
-conjugate
of
b\in K

over a subset

X
of
K
if there exists
0\neq x\in X
such that
a=\sigma(x)bx^{-1}
. We drop the phrase ”over
X
” when
X=K
. It is easy to see that the notion of
\sigma
-conjugacy over a subgroup
X
of the multiplicative group
K^{*}
is an equivalence relation in which case the
\sigma
-conjugacy class containing
a\in K
is denoted by
C(a,X)
. When
X=K^{*}
, we write
C(a)
instead of
C(a,X)
.

The maps
N_{i}:K\to K
, where
i\geq 0
, are defined recursively as follows

N_{i}(a)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }i=0,\\
\sigma(N_{i-1}(a))\,a&\text{ if }i>0.\\
\end{cases}

Clearly,
N_{i}(a)=\sigma^{i-1}(a)\cdots\sigma(a)a
for all
i\geq 1
. It is easy to verify the following identities:

N_{i+j}(a)=\sigma^{i}(N_{j}(a))N_{i}(a),\text{ for all }a\in K\text{ and }i,j\geq 0,

(2.1)

N_{i}(\sigma(a)ba^{-1})=\sigma^{i}(a)N_{i}(b)a^{-1},\text{ for all }a\in K^{*},b\in K\text{ and }i\geq 0.

(2.2)

Note that
N_{i}(ab)=N_{i}(a)N_{i}(b)
for all
a,b\in K
. Furthermore, if
c\in K
is fixed by
\sigma
, then
N_{i}(c)=c^{i}
for all
i\geq 0
.

A polynomial
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}\in K[T;\sigma]
can be regarded as a function
P:K\to K
as follows:

P(a):=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}N_{i}(a).

Note that
P(c)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}c^{i}
for all
c
in the fixed field of
\sigma
, that is, the field
\{c\in K\,|\,\sigma(c)=c\}
. The element
P(a)
is called the value of
P
at
a
. This definition is justified by the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
and
a\in K
, there exists a unique polynomial
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
such that

P(T)=Q(T)(T-a)+P(a).

A simple consequence of this lemma is the following:

P(a)=0\iff P(T)\in K[T;\sigma](T-a).

A polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
is said to vanish on a subset
S
of
K
if
P(a)=0
for all
a\in S
. In particular, if
P(a)=0
, where
a\in K
, the element
a
is called a (right skew) root of the polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
.

The following formula is known as the product formula:

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
P(T),Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
, we have

(PQ)(a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }Q(a)=0,\\
P\left(\sigma(Q(a))\,a\,Q(a)^{-1}\right)Q(a)&\text{ if }Q(a)\neq 0,\\
\end{cases}

where
(PQ)(T)=P(T)Q(T)
, as elements of
K[T;\sigma]
.

One consequence of these lemmas is that a skew polynomial of degree
n
has roots in at most
n
different
\sigma
-conjugacy classes.

A subset
S
of
K
is called
\sigma
-algebraic (or simply algebraic when there is no risk of confusion) if there exists a nonzero
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
which vanishes on
S
. One can show that every finite subset of
K
is
\sigma
-algebraic. Moreover,
for any
\sigma
-algebraic subset
S
of
K
, there exists a unique monic polynomial
P_{S}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
which vanishes on
S
, and any skew polynomial, vanishing on
S
, belongs to the left ideal
K[T;\sigma]P_{S}(T)
. The polynomial
P_{S}(T)
is called the minimal (skew) polynomial of
S
and its degree, denoted by
rk(S)
, is called the rank of
S
. If
S
is not
\sigma
-algebraic, its rank is defined to be
\infty
. An element
a\in K
is called P-dependent on a
\sigma
-algebraic subset
S
of
K
if
P_{S}(a)=0
. A subset
S
of
K
is called P-independent if no
a\in S
is P-dependent on
S\setminus\{a\}
.

Given
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in K
, the
\sigma
-Vandermonde matrices
V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\in K^{n\times n}
and
V^{\sigma}_{\infty}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\in K^{\infty\times n}
are defined as follows

V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
N_{2}(a_{1})&N_{2}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{2}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(a_{1})&N_{n-1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{n-1}(a_{n})\\
\end{pmatrix},

V^{\sigma}_{\infty}(a_{1},...,a_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
N_{2}(a_{1})&N_{2}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{2}(a_{n})\\
N_{3}(a_{1})&N_{3}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{3}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\end{pmatrix}

The proof of the following result can be found in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in K
be arbitrary.

(a) The
\sigma
-Vandermonde matrices
V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})
and
V^{\sigma}_{\infty}(a_{1},...,a_{n})
have equal rank, and their rank is equal to the rank of the subset
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
of
K
. In particular,
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
is P-independent iff
V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})
is invertible.

(b) If
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}N_{j}(a_{i})=0
for
j=0,1,...,n-1
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}N_{j}(a_{i})=0
for all
j\geq 0
.

We remark that the conclusion of Part (b) holds for any skew polynomial, that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}P(a_{i})=0
for all
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
.

Theorem
2.3
can be used to analyze the concept of P-dependence in terms of linear dependence: Consider the
K
-vector space
K^{\infty}
of ”infinite” row vectors over
K
, that is,

K^{\infty}=\{(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},...)\,|\,a_{0},a_{1},...\in K\}.

Let
N:K^{*}\to K^{\infty}
be the map

N(a)=(N_{0}(a),N_{1}(a),N_{2}(a),...).

Using this map, we can reformulate Theorem
2.3
:

Proposition 2.4
.

1) A set
S\subset K^{*}
is
\sigma
-algebraic over
K
iff the vector subspace

N_{S}
of
K^{\infty}
which is generated by all
N(a)
, where
a\in S
, is finite-dimensional. Moreover, we have

rk(S)=\dim N_{S}
.

2) Elements
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in K^{*}
are P-independent iff the vectors
N(a_{1}),...,N(a_{n})
are linearly independent over
K
. In particular, an element
a\in K^{*}
is P-dependent on a subset
S
of
K^{*}
iff the row vector
N(a)
belongs to the vector space
N_{S}
.

The vector space
K^{\infty}
can be considered as a (commutative)
K
-algebra using the multiplication

(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},...)(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},...)=(a_{0}b_{0},a_{1}b_{1},a_{2}b_{2},...).

Since each
N_{i}:K\to K
satisfies the identity

N_{i}(ab)=N_{i}(a)N_{i}(b),\text{ where }a,b\in K,

we see that
N_{K^{*}}
is a subalgebra of the
K
-algebra
K^{\infty}
. More generally,
N_{S}
is a subalgebra of the
K
-algebra
K^{\infty}
if
S\subset K^{*}
is a multiplicative subset of
K
, that is,
1\in S
, and
ab\in S
when
a,b\in S
.

2.2
Left and right roots

In this part, we assume that
\sigma:K\to K
is a field automorphism. As mentioned at the beginning of this section, elements of
K[T;\sigma]
can also be considered as right polynomials. As a consequence, one has the ”left” analogues of the concepts and results of the preceding subsection. More precisely, any polynomial
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}\in K[T;\sigma]
gives rise to a left evaluation map
P_{l}:K\to K
defined by

P_{l}(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sigma^{-i}(a_{i})N_{-i}(a),

where
N_{-i}(a)=\sigma^{-i+1}(a)\cdots\sigma^{-1}(a)a
for
a\in K
and
i\geq 1
.
Moreover, the ”left” version of Lemma
3.8
holds. More precisely, for every polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]

and
a\in K
, there exists a unique polynomial
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
such that

P(T)=(T-a)Q(T)+P_{l}(a).

In particular,
P(T)\in(T-a)K[T;\sigma]
iff
P_{l}(a)=0
in which case
a
is called a left (skew) root of
P(T)
. We leave it to the reader to define and prove the left analogues of the concepts and results of the preceding subsection. We shall use the prefixes ”left” and ”right” to distinguish between the concepts defined using
P_{l}
and the ones presented in the preceding subsection. However, we usually drop the prefix ”right” when there is no risk for confusion. The following proposition gives a relationship between the concepts of left P-dependence and right P-dependence.

Proposition 2.5
.

For every
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in K^{*}
, the following are equivalent:

(1) The right rank of the set
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
is less than
n
, or equivalently, the elements
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are right P-dependent.

(2) There exist
b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, not all equal to zero, such that

b_{1}(1-a_{1}T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}(1-a_{n}T)^{-1}=0.

(3) There exist
b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, not all equal to zero, such that

b_{1}(1-a_{1}T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}(1-a_{n}T)^{-1}\in K[T;\sigma].

(4) The left rank of the set
\{\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})\}
is less than
n
, or equivalently, the elements
\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})
are left P-dependent.

Proof.

The equivalence of (1) and (2) follows from Theorem
2.3
and the fact that
(1-aT)^{-1}
, as an element of
K[[T;\sigma]]
, is the series

(1-aT)^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}N_{i}(a)T^{i}.

Clearly, (2) implies (3). To prove (3)
\implies
(4), let there be
b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, not all equal to zero, such that

b_{1}(1-a_{1}T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}(1-a_{n}T)^{-1}=P(T)\in K[T;\sigma].

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
b_{n}\neq 0
. This equality can be rewritten as follows

b_{1}\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1})(\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1})-T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})-T)^{-1}=P(T).

Let
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
have
\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})
as left zeros, that is,

Q_{l}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{i}^{-1}))=0,

for
1\leq i<n
. It follows that

\displaystyle b_{n}\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})-T)^{-1}Q(T)={}

\displaystyle P(T)Q(T)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_{i}\sigma^{-1}(a_{i}^{-1})(T-\sigma^{-1}(a_{i}^{-1}))^{-1}Q(T)

belongs to
K[T;\sigma]
. Therefore, we see that

Q_{l}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1}))=0
. Therefore, we conclude that
\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})
is left P-dependent on the set
\{\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n-1}^{-1})\}
. In particular, the left rank of the set
\{\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})\}
is less than
n
. This completes the proof of (3)
\implies
(4). It remains to prove (4)
\implies
(2). Let
\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})
be left P-dependent. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})
is P-dependent on the set
\{\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n-1}^{-1})\}
. Let
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
be the minimal left polynomial of
\{\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1}),...,\sigma^{-1}(a_{n-1}^{-1})\}
. Note that the degree of
Q(T)
is less than
n
. This fact, together with the fact that

(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})-T)^{-1}Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma],

for all
1\leq i\leq n
, imply that there are
b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, not all equal to zero, such that

b_{1}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1})-T)^{-1}Q(T)+\cdots+b_{n}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})-T)^{-1}Q(T)=0.

Since
K(T;\sigma)
is a skew field, it follows that

b_{1}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{1}^{-1})-T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}(\sigma^{-1}(a_{n}^{-1})-T)^{-1}=0,

or equivalently,

b_{1}\sigma^{-1}(a_{1})(1-a_{1}T)^{-1}+\cdots+b_{n}\sigma^{-1}(a_{n})(1-a_{n}T)^{-1}=0,

and we are done.
∎

As a consequence of this proposition, we have

Corollary 2.6
.

A set
S\subset K^{*}
is right
\sigma
-algebraic iff the set

\widehat{S}=\{\sigma^{-1}(a^{-1})\,|\,a\in S\},

is left
\sigma
-algebraic. Furthermore, the right rank of an arbitrary subset
S
of
K^{*}
is equal to the left rank of
\widehat{S}
.

In the case when
\sigma
is an involution, that is,
\sigma^{2}=1_{K}
, we have

\widehat{S}=\{\sigma(a^{-1})\,|\,a\in S\}.

As an example, a set
S\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}
is right
\sigma
-algebraic iff the set

\widehat{S}=\left\{\frac{1}{\bar{c}}\,|\,c\in S\right\},

is left
\sigma
-algebraic. Here,
\sigma:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is the complex conjugation map.

Although Proposition
2.5
gives a relationship between left and right ranks, it does not provide any relationship between minimal left and right polynomials. In order to derive such a relationship, we need the following result which deals with anti-automorphisms of rings of skew polynomials. Recall that a map
\alpha:R\to R
, where
R
is a ring, is called an anti-automorphism, if it is an additive group isomorphism,
\alpha(1)=1
, and
\alpha(ab)=\alpha(b)\alpha(a)
for all
a,b\in R
. In the following proposition, we give a characterization of anti-automorphisms of
K[T;\sigma]
. For the case of automorphisms of skew polynomial rings over (not necessarily commutative) rings, see
[
5
]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that
\sigma:K\to K
is an automorphism not equal to the identity map
1_{K}
. Let
\alpha:K[T;\sigma]\to K[T;\sigma]
be a map. If
\alpha
is an anti-automorphism, then the following hold:

(1)
\alpha(K)=K
, and the restriction
\alpha_{0}
of
\alpha
to
K
is an automorphism of
K
.

(2) There exists an element
a_{0}\in K^{*}
such that
\alpha(T)=a_{0}T
.

(3)
\sigma\alpha_{0}\sigma=\alpha_{0}
.

Conversely, if
\alpha_{0}:K\to K
is an automorphism of
K
which satisfies Property (3), then, for every
a_{0}\in K^{*}
, there exists a unique anti-automorphism

\alpha:K[T;\sigma]\to K[T;\sigma]
, with
\alpha(T)=a_{0}T
, whose restriction to
K
is
\alpha_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha:K[T;\alpha]\to K[T;\alpha]
be an anti-automorphism. First, we prove (1). Since the set of units of
K[T;\sigma]
is
K^{*}
, we see that
\alpha(K)\subset K
. Similarly, we have
\alpha^{-1}(K)\subset K
since
\alpha^{-1}
is an anti-automorphism of
K[T;\alpha]
. It follows that
\alpha(K)=K
. Clearly,
\alpha_{0}:K\to K
is an automorphism of
K
since
K
is commutative. This completes the proof of (1). A simple argument using the degree function reveals that
\alpha(T)=a_{0}T+b_{0}
for some
a_{0},b_{0}\in K
with
a_{0}\neq 0
.
For
a\in K
, we have

Ta=\sigma(a)T\implies\alpha(Ta)=\alpha(\sigma(a)T)\implies\alpha_{0}(a)\alpha(T)=\alpha(T)\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a)),

which in turn implies that

\alpha_{0}(a)a_{0}T+\alpha_{0}(a)b_{0}=a_{0}T\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a))+b_{0}\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a))=a_{0}\sigma(\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a)))T+b_{0}\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a)).

It follows that

\forall a\in K,\,\alpha_{0}(a)a_{0}=a_{0}\sigma(\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a)))\text{ and }\alpha_{0}(a)b_{0}=b_{0}\alpha_{0}(\sigma(a)).

The first relation proves (3) because
a_{0}\neq 0
. If
b_{0}\neq 0
, then
\alpha_{0}
must be equal to
\alpha_{0}\sigma
which implies that
\sigma=1_{K}
since
\alpha
is a bijection. This contradicts the assumption that
\sigma\neq 1_{K}
. This completes the proof of (2).

To prove the second statement, let
\alpha_{0}:K\to K
be an automorphism satisfying (1) and (3). Given
a_{0}\in K^{*}
, we define
\alpha:K[T;\alpha]\to K[T;\alpha]
, as follows

\alpha(\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}T^{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sigma^{i}(\alpha_{0}(b_{i}))N_{i}(a_{0})T^{i}.

It is easy to see that
\alpha
is an additive group isomorphism. Since
\alpha(1)=1
,
\alpha(T)=a_{0}T
, and the restriction of
\alpha
to
K
is
\alpha_{0}
, we only need to prove that

\alpha(P(T)Q(T))=\alpha(Q(T))\alpha(P(T)),

for all
P(T),Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. By the additivity of
\alpha
, it is enough to show that

\alpha\left((aT^{m})(bT^{n})\right)=\alpha\left(bT^{n}\right)\alpha\left(aT^{m}\right),

for all
a,b\in K
and
m,n\geq 0
. We have

\alpha\left((aT^{m})(bT^{n})\right)=\alpha\left(a\sigma^{m}(b)T^{m+n}\right)=\sigma^{m+n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(a\sigma^{m}(b)\right)\right)N_{m+n}(a_{0})T^{m+n},

and

\displaystyle\alpha\left(bT^{n}\right)\alpha\left(aT^{m}\right)=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(b\right)\right)N_{n}(a_{0})T^{n}\sigma^{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(a\right)\right)N_{m}(a_{0})T^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\sigma^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(b\right)\right)N_{n}(a_{0})s^{n}\left(\sigma^{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(a\right)\right)N_{m}(a_{0})\right)T^{m+n}.

Therefore, we need to show that

\sigma^{m+n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(a\sigma^{m}(b)\right)\right)N_{m+n}(a_{0})=\sigma^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(b\right)\right)N_{n}(a_{0})\sigma^{n}\left(\sigma^{m}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(a\right)\right)N_{m}(a_{0})\right),

for all
a,b\in K
and
m,n\geq 0
. Using Equation
2.1
and the fact that
\sigma
and
\alpha_{0}
are ring homomorphisms, we can simplify this equation to obtain

\sigma^{m+n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(\sigma^{m}(b)\right)\right)=\sigma^{n}\left(\alpha_{0}\left(b\right)\right).

It is now easy to use
\sigma\alpha_{0}\sigma=\alpha_{0}
to show that this relation holds for all
b\in K
and
m,n\geq 0
. The uniqueness part follows from the fact that
K
and
T
generate
K[T;\sigma]
as a ring. This completes the proof of the second statement.
∎

Let
AAut(K,\sigma)
denote the set of all anti-automorphisms of
K[T;\sigma]
and
Aut_{\sigma}(K)
denote the set of all automorphism
\alpha_{0}:K\to K
such that
\sigma\alpha_{0}\sigma=\alpha_{0}
. Using Proposition
2.7
, we obtain a 1-1 correspondence between
AAut(K,\sigma)
and the set
Aut_{\sigma}(K)\times K^{*}
, given by
\alpha\mapsto(\alpha|_{K},\alpha(T)T^{-1})
. It may happen that
Aut_{\sigma}(K)
is empty, in which case
AAut(K,\sigma)
is empty as well. As an example, the reader can verify that if
K
is any cyclic extension of the field of rational numbers, and
\sigma
is a generator of the Galois group of
K
over
\mathbb{Q}
, then
AAut(K,\sigma)
is nonempty iff
\sigma^{2}=1_{K}
(in the case
\sigma^{2}=1_{K}
, we have
1_{K}\in Aut_{\sigma}(K)
). If
AAut(K,\sigma)
is nonempty, then we have the following result concerning left and right roots:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha\in AAut(K,\sigma)
satisfy
\alpha(T)=T
, and set
\alpha_{0}=\alpha|_{K}
. Then, the following hold:

(1)
a\in K
is a left root of a polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
iff
\alpha_{0}(a)
is a right root of
\alpha(P(T))
.

(2) A polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
is the minimal left polynomial of a subset of
K
iff
\alpha(P(T))
is the minimal right polynomial of a subset of
K
.

(3) The assignment
S\mapsto\alpha(S)
establishes a 1-1 correspondence between the set of all left
\sigma
-algebraic subsets of
K
and the set of all right
\sigma
-algebraic subsets of
K
. Moreover, the left rank of
S
is equal to the right rank of
\alpha(S)
.

Proof.

(1) If
a
is a left root of
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
, then

P(T)=(T-a)Q(T),

for some
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. Applying
\alpha
to this relation gives

\alpha(P(T))=\alpha((T-a)Q(T))=\alpha(Q(T))\alpha(T-a)=\alpha(Q(T))(T-\alpha_{0}(a)),

showing that
\alpha(a_{0})
is a right root of
\alpha(P(T))
. The converse is proved in a similar fashion. Now, Parts
(2) and (3) follow easily from (1).
∎

In the case when
\sigma
is an involution, the identity map of
K[T;\sigma]
belongs to
AAut(K,\sigma)
. In this case, an application of this proposition gives the following: (1)

a
is a left root of
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}
iff
a
is a right root of
\sum_{i=0}^{n}T^{i}a_{i}
; (2)
S\subset K
is left
\sigma
-algebraic iff it is right
\sigma
-algebraic, and moreover, the left rank of
S
is equal to its right rank.

2.3
Values of skew polynomials

In this subsection, we present some elementary results concerning values of skew polynomials. As opposed to ordinary polynomials in an indeterminate over commutative fields, nonzero skew polynomials over commutative fields may have infinitely many roots. For example, the polynomial

T^{2}-1\in\mathbb{C}[T;\,\bar{\,}\,],

vanishes on the unit circle
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,|\,|z|=1\}
. However, we have the following result, which is well-known for ordinary polynomials over commutative fields. The result also holds for polynomials in a central indeterminate over a skew field (see
[
13
, Theorem 16.7]
).

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
(K,\sigma)
be an infinite
\sigma
-field and
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. If
P(a)=0
for all
a\in K
, then
P(T)=0
.

Proof.

Let
F
be the fixed field of
\sigma
. Suppose, on the contrary, that a skew polynomial
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}\neq 0
vanishes on
K
. Then, all the
\sigma
-conjugacy classes of
K
must be
\sigma
-algebraic. It follow from
[
18
, Theorem 5.17]
that the degree
[K:F]
of the extension
K/F
is finite. Therefore,
F
must be an infinite field. Since

P(c)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}c^{i},

where
c\in F
, the nonzero ordinary polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i}\in K[x],
must have infinitely many zeros over the commutative field
K
, a contradiction. ∎

This theorem does not hold for finite
\sigma
-fields: Since any finite subset of a
\sigma
-field is
\sigma
-algebraic, we see that there is a nonzero polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
vanishing on
K
if
(K,\sigma)
is a finite
\sigma
-field, in which case, the nonconstant polynomial
P(T)+1
has no roots in
K
. We note in passing that if
(K,\sigma)
is a finite
\sigma
-field, then the set of all
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
which vanish on
K
is a nonzero (two-sided) ideal of
K[T;\sigma]
.

Next, we prove a version of the Bray–Whaples theorem for skew polynomials, see
[
4
]
for the result which deals with polynomials in a central indeterminate over a skew field.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
a_{1},...,a_{n}\in K
be mutually nonconjugate over
K
. Then, there is a unique monic skew polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
of degree
n
whose roots are exactly
a_{1},...,a_{n}
. Moreover, if

Q(a_{1})=\cdots=Q(a_{n})=0,

where
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
, then
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]P(T)
.

Proof.

Let
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
be the minimal skew polynomial of
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
. Since
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are mutually nonconjugate, the degree of
P(T)
must be
n
. Therefore, it remains to show that
P(T)
has no roots other than
a_{1},...,a_{n}
. This holds trivially if
n=1
. So, let
n>1
. Suppose, on the contrary, that there exists
b\in K
not equal to
a_{1},...,a_{n}
such that
P(b)=0
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have

P(T)=Q(T)(T-b),

for some
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. Since
b\neq a_{i}
for all
i
,
it follows from Lemma
2.2
that the skew polynomial
Q(T)
has the following roots

c_{i}=\sigma(a_{i}-b)a_{i}(a_{i}-b)^{-1}\text{ where }i=1,...,n.

Clearly, each
c_{i}
is conjugate to
a_{i}
. Therefore, the roots
c_{1},...,c_{n}
of
Q(T)
are mutually nonconjugate, contradicting the fact that a skew polynomial of degree less than
n
cannot have roots in
n
different conjugacy classes. The second statement follows from the fact that
P(T)
is the minimal polynomial of
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
.
∎

In general, the classical Lagrange interpolation does not work for skew polynomials since an element
a\in K
can be P-dependent on a subset not containing
a
(see the discussion after Proposition
2.11
). However, we have the following version of interpolation for skew polynomials, which can be regarded as a generalization of Proposition
2.10
. The problem of interpolation for polynomials in a central indeterminate over a skew field is treated in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
S=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}\subset K
be P-independent. For any
b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, there exists a unique skew polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
of degree less than
n=rk(S)
such that
P(a_{i})=b_{i}
for all
i
. Moreover, a polynomial
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
satisfies
Q(a_{i})=b_{i}
for all
i
, iff

Q(T)\in P(T)+K[T;\sigma]P_{S}(T).

Proof.

We note that
rk(S)=n
by
[
16
, Lemma 12]
. Let
P_{i}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
be the minimal skew polynomial of
S\setminus\{a_{i}\}
. The degree of each
P_{i}(T)
is
rk(S)-1=n-1
. Since
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are P-independent,
P_{i}(a_{i})
cannot be zero. It is easy to verify that the polynomial

P(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}P_{i}(a_{i})^{-1}P_{i}(T),

satisfies the desired property. The uniqueness of
P(T)
follows from the fact that the degree of each
P_{i}(T)
is less than
rk(S)
.
∎

A consequence of this proposition is that if
a\in K
is P-dependent on a P-independent set
S=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
, then for any
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
, the value of
Q(a)
is completely determined by the values
Q(a_{1}),...,Q(a_{n})
. In fact, we have

Q(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Q(a_{i})P_{i}(a_{i})^{-1}P_{i}(a),

where
P_{i}(T)
is the minimal skew polynomial of
S\setminus\{a_{i}\}
. More generally, one can easily show that the value of
Q(a)
is completely determined by the values
Q(a),a\in S
, if
a
is P-dependent on a (not necessarily P-independent)
\sigma
-algebraic set
S\subset K
.

2.4
Vieta’s formulas for skew polynomials

The classical formulas of Vieta express the coefficients of an ordinary polynomial over a commutative field in terms of its roots.
Here, we present analogous formulas for skew polynomials. The formulas in the second part of the following result can be regarded as analogues of Vieta’s formulas (see
[
10
]
,
[
11
, Section 7.2]
and
[
12
]
for a more general discussion).

Theorem 2.12
.

Let
S=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
be P-independent with minimal polynomial
P_{S}(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}T^{n}\in K[T;\sigma]
.

(a) For every
a\in K
, we have

P_{S}(a)=\frac{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n},a)\right)}{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)}.

(b) For
i=0,...,n
,

b_{i}=\frac{(-1)^{n+i}}{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)}\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{i-1}(a_{1})&N_{i-1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{i-1}(a_{n})\\
N_{i+1}(a_{1})&N_{i+1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{i+1}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{n}(a_{1})&N_{n}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{n}(a_{n})\\
\end{vmatrix}

(2.3)

Moreover,

b_{0}=(-1)^{n}\,\frac{\sigma\left(\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)\right)}{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)}\,a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}.

Proof.

Using cofactor expansion along the last column of the matrix
V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n},a)
, we obtain

\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n},a)\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}N_{i}(a),

where

c_{i}=(-1)^{n+i}\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{i-1}(a_{1})&N_{i-1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{i-1}(a_{n})\\
N_{i+1}(a_{1})&N_{i+1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{i+1}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{n}(a_{1})&N_{n}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{n}(a_{n})\\
\end{vmatrix}.

Seting
Q(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}T^{n}
, we have

Q(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}c_{i}N_{i}(a)=\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n},a)\right),

for all
a\in K
. By Part (a) of Theorem
2.3
,
Q(a_{i})=0
for all
i=1,...,n
. It follows that

Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]P_{S}(T).

Since
P_{S}(T)
and
Q(T)
have the same degree, and
c_{n}=\det(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n}))
, we conclude that

Q(T)=V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})P_{S}(T),

and

P_{S}(a)=\frac{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n},a)\right)}{\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)},

for all
a\in K
.

(b) Formula
2.3
follows from (a). To prove the last formula, we set
i=0
in Formula
2.3
and use Identity
2.1
. We see that
(-1)^{n}b_{0}\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)
is equal to

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
N_{2}(a_{1})&N_{2}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{2}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{n}(a_{1})&N_{n}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{n}(a_{n})\\
\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n}\\
a_{1}\sigma(N_{1}(a_{1}))&a_{2}\sigma(N_{1}(a_{2}))&\cdots&a_{n}\sigma(N_{1}(a_{n}))\\
a_{1}\sigma(N_{2}(a_{1}))&a_{2}\sigma(N_{2}(a_{2}))&\cdots&a_{n}\sigma(N_{2}(a_{n}))\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
a_{1}\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{1}))&a_{2}\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{2}))&\cdots&a_{n}\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{n}))\\
\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
\sigma(N_{1}(a_{1}))&\sigma(N_{1}(a_{2}))&\cdots&\sigma(N_{1}(a_{n}))\\
\sigma(N_{2}(a_{1}))&a_{2}\sigma(N_{2}(a_{2}))&\cdots&\sigma(N_{2}(a_{n}))\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{1}))&\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{2}))&\cdots&\sigma(N_{n-1}(a_{n}))\\
\end{vmatrix}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
N_{1}(a_{1})&N_{1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{1}(a_{n})\\
N_{2}(a_{1})&N_{2}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{2}(a_{n})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(a_{1})&N_{n-1}(a_{2})&\cdots&N_{n-1}(a_{n})\\
\end{vmatrix}\right)a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\det\left(V^{\sigma}(a_{1},...,a_{n})\right)\right)a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n},

which proves the desired formula. ∎

We note that Part (a) of this theorem provides a method for finding the minimal polynomial of any finite P-independent set. In particular, one can apply this method to find the polynomials
P_{i}(T)
’s used in the proof of Proposition
2.11
.

3
\sigma
-Extensions

This section deals with the notion of
\sigma
-algebraicity in the context of
\sigma
-extensions. Since it is more convenient to use the language of difference rings, we begin by reviewing basic facts about difference rings. For a detailed account of the theory of difference rings, we refer the reader to the book
[
20
]
.

By a difference ring, we mean a pair
(R,\sigma_{R})
where
R
is a commutative ring and
\sigma_{R}:R\to R
is an injective endomorphism. The ring
R
is called the underlying ring of the difference ring
(R,\sigma)
and the endomorphism
\sigma_{R}
is called its structure map. A difference ring is also called a
\sigma
-ring, a term we mainly use in this paper. By abuse of notation, we often write
\sigma
in place of
\sigma_{R}
if there is no risk of confusion. Note that any ring equipped with its identity map gives rise to a
\sigma
-ring which we call a trivial
\sigma
-ring. In what follows, we present some definitions and facts regarding difference rings which are relevant to this work.

(1) A
\sigma
-ring is called a
\sigma
-field (resp.,
\sigma
-domain) if its underlying ring is a field (resp., an integral domain).

(2) By a
\sigma
-ideal of a
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
, we mean an ideal
I
of
R
which satisfies

\forall r\in R,\,r\in I\iff\sigma(r)\subset I.

For any
\sigma
-ideal
I
of a
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
, we can consider a
\sigma
-ring structure on
R/I
defined by
r+I\mapsto\sigma(r)+I
, which is called the quotient of
(R,\sigma)
modulo
I
, and denoted by
(R/I,\sigma)
.

(3) A
\sigma
-homomorphism
\phi:(R,\sigma_{R})\to(S,\sigma_{S})
between
\sigma
-rings is a ring homomorphism of
\phi:R\to S
between the underlying rings which satisfies
\phi\sigma_{R}=\sigma_{S}\phi
. It is easy to see that
\sigma
-rings and
\sigma
-homomorphisms form a category. If
I
is a
\sigma
-ideal of
(R,\sigma)
, then the quotient map
q:R\to R/I
gives rise to a
\sigma
-homomorphism
q:(R,\sigma)\to(R/I,\sigma)
. If
\phi:(R,\sigma_{R})\to(S,\sigma_{S})
is a
\sigma
-homomorphism, then
\ker\phi
is a
\sigma
-ideal of
(R,\sigma)
, and the map
R/\ker\phi\to S
is an (injective)
\sigma
-homomorphism.

(4) By a
\sigma
-subring of a
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
, we mean a subring
S
of
R
which satisfies
\sigma(S)\subset S
, in which case, the inclusion map becomes a
\sigma
-homomorphism
i:(S,\sigma)\to(R,\sigma)
. In particular, if both
S
and
R
are fields,
(S,\sigma)
is called a
\sigma
-subfield of
(R,\sigma)
, and
(R,\sigma)
is called an
extension
\sigma
-field
(or simply
\sigma
-extension
) of
(S,\sigma)
.

(5) Let
(S,\sigma)
be a
\sigma
-subring of a
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
. For a subset
X
of
(R,\sigma)
, the intersection of all
\sigma
-subrings of
(R,\sigma)
containing
S\cup X
, is itself a
\sigma
-subring of
(R,\sigma)
which is called the
\sigma
-subring of
(R,\sigma)
generated by
X
over
(S,\sigma)
, and denoted by
S[X]_{\sigma}
. In case,
X=\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
is finite, we denote
S[X]_{\sigma}
by
S[x_{1},...,x_{n}]_{\sigma}
.

(6) Let
(K,\sigma)
be a
\sigma
-subfield of a
\sigma
-field
(L,\sigma)
. For a subset
X
of
(L,\sigma)
, the intersection of all
\sigma
-subfields of
(L,\sigma)
containing
K\cup X
is itself a
\sigma
-field of
(L,\sigma)
which is called the
\sigma
-subfield of
(L,\sigma)
generated by
X
over
(K,\sigma)
, and denoted by
K\langle X\rangle
. In case,
X=\{x_{1},...,x_{n}\}
is finite, we denote
K\langle X\rangle
by
K\langle x_{1},...,x_{n}\rangle
.

(7) A
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
is called inversive if
\sigma:R\to R
is onto. It is known that for any
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
, there exists a
\sigma
-ring
(U,\sigma)
containing
(R,\sigma)
as a
\sigma
-subring such that any
\sigma
-homomorphism
\psi:(R,\sigma)\to(S,\sigma_{S})
, where
(S,\sigma_{S})
is inversive, has a unique extension to a
\sigma
-homomorphism
(U,\sigma)\to(S,\sigma_{S})
. The
\sigma
-ring
(U,\sigma)
, called a universal inversive closure of
(R,\sigma)
, is unique up to
\sigma
-isomorphism. Moreover,
(U,\sigma)
has the following property:

\forall u\in U,\,\exists n\geq 1,\,\sigma^{n}(u)\in R.

Also,
(U,\sigma)
is a
\sigma
-field (resp.,
\sigma
-domain) if
(R,\sigma)
is a
\sigma
-field (resp.,
\sigma
-domain).

(8) A multiplicative
\sigma
-subset of a
\sigma
-ring
(R,\sigma)
is a multiplicative subset
S
of
R
which satisfies

\forall r\in R,\,\left(r\in S\implies\sigma(r)\subset S\right).

Given a multiplicative
\sigma
-subset of a
\sigma
-domain
(R,\sigma)
, the map

rs^{-1}\mapsto\sigma(r)\sigma(s)^{-1},

is a well-defined map on the localization ring
S^{-1}R
, turning
S^{-1}R
into a
\sigma
-domain. Moreover, the ring homomorphism

R\to S^{-1}R,\,r\mapsto r1^{-1},

is a
\sigma
-homomorphism. In particular, if
(R,\sigma)
is a
\sigma
-domain, then the quotient field
Frac(R)
of
R
acquires a
\sigma
-field structure for which the inclusion map
R\to Frac(R)
is a
\sigma
-homomorphism. The resulting
\sigma
-field
(Frac(R),\sigma)
is called the
\sigma
-field of fractions of
(R,\sigma)
.

(9) Let
(R,\sigma)
be a
\sigma
-field. Consider the ring

R\{x\}:=R[x(0),x(1),x(2),...],

of polynomials over
R
in (commuting) indeterminates
x(0),x(1),...
. There is a unique endomorphism

\sigma:R\{x\}\to R\{x\}
which extends
\sigma:R\to R
and satisfies
\sigma(x(i))=x(i+1)
for
i\geq 0
. Clearly,
\sigma:R\{x\}\to R\{x\}
is injective. Therefore, the pair
(R\{x\},\sigma\})
defines a
\sigma
-ring which includes
(R,\sigma)
as a
\sigma
-subring. The

3.1
\sigma
-Algebraic elements in

In this subsection, we let

Lemma 3.1

For any element

(1)

\sigma
-algebraic over

(2) The

-vector subspace of

(3) There exists a finite-dimensional

-vector subspace

Proof.

(1)
\implies

c_{0}+c_{1}N_{1}(a)+\cdots+c_{n}N_{n}(a)=0,

\sigma(c_{0})a+\sigma(c_{1})a\sigma(N_{1}(a))+\cdots+\sigma(c_{n})a\sigma(N_{n}(a))=0.

Since

, this equation can be rewritten as

\sigma(c_{0})N_{1}(a)+\sigma(c_{1})N_{2}(a)+\cdots+\sigma(c_{n})N_{n+1}(a)=0,

Inductively, one can see that this holds for all

, where

(2)
\implies

(3)
\implies
(1): Let

are linearly dependent over

\sigma
-algebraic over

.
∎

(K[P],\sigma_{P})
is denoted by
(K(P),\sigma_{P})
. Note that
(K(P),\sigma_{P})
is a
\sigma
-extension of
(K,\sigma)
. Now, we show that
P(T)
is the minimal polynomial of an element in
(K(P),\sigma_{P})

Proposition 3.9
.

The element
y_{0}\in K(P)
is a nonzero root of
P(T)
in the
\sigma
-extension
(K(P),\sigma)
of
(K,\sigma)
. Moreover, the minimal skew polynomial of
y_{0}\in K(P)
over
(K,\sigma)
is
P(T)
.

Proof.

The fact that
y_{0}\in K(P)
is a root of
P(T)
follows from the fact that

P(y_{0})=y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}\frac{L(y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1})}{y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}}-c_{n}y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}-\cdots-c_{1}y_{0}-c_{0}=0.

If
y_{0}
is a root of a skew polynomial

d_{0}+d_{1}T+\cdots+d_{m}T^{m}\in K[T;\sigma],

where
m<\deg P
, then

0=d_{0}+d_{1}y_{0}+d_{2}y_{0}y_{1}+\cdots+d_{m}y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{m-1},

which implies
d_{0}=...=d_{m-1}=0
, since
y_{0},...,y_{n-1}
are algebraically independent over
K
. This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we show that the
\sigma
-domain
(K[P],\sigma_{P})
has the following universal property:

Theorem 3.10
.

An element
0\neq a
in a
\sigma
-extension
(L,\sigma)
of

has

as its minimal polynomial over
(K,\sigma)
iff there exists a
\sigma
-homomorphism

\psi_{a}:(K[P],\sigma_{P})\to(L,\sigma),

over
(K,\sigma)

\psi_{a}(y_{0})=a
. Moreover, such a
\sigma
-homomorphism is unique.

Proof.

First, suppose that
P(T)
is the minimal polynomial of

. By the universal property of

, there exists a unique
K
-algebra homomorphism

\psi:K[y_{0},...,y_{n-1}]\to L

such that

\psi(y_{i})=\sigma^{i}(a)
for

.
The condition

gives

\sigma^{n}(a)\cdots\sigma(a)a=c_{0}+c_{1}a+c_{2}a\sigma(a)+\cdots+c_{n}a\sigma(a)\sigma^{2}(a)\cdots\sigma^{n-1}(a),

from which it follows that

\sigma^{n}(a)\psi(y_{n-1})\cdots\psi(y_{0})=\psi(L(y_{0},...,y_{n-1})).

Localizing
\psi
at
L(y_{0},...,y_{n-1})
, we obtain
a

-algebra homomorphism

\psi_{1}:K[y_{0},...,y_{n-1}][L(y_{0},...,y_{n-1})^{-1}]\to L,

such that
\psi_{1}(\frac{L(y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1})}{y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}})=\sigma^{n}(a)
. For any

0\neq r=d_{0}+d_{1}y_{0}+d_{2}y_{0}y_{1}+\cdots+d_{n}y_{0}y_{1}\cdots y_{n-1}\in S,

we have

\psi_{1}(r)=d_{0}+d_{1}a+d_{2}a\sigma(a)+\cdots+d_{n}a\sigma(a)\cdots\sigma^{n-1}(a)\neq 0,

since the minimal polynomial of

Since

is a
\sigma
-homomorphism satisfying

. Conversely, suppose that

\psi_{0}:(K[P],\sigma_{P})\to(L,\sigma),

is a
\sigma

which satisfies

is a

by Proposition

and its restriction to

∎

4
Algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields

As is well-known, every (commutative) field has an algebraic closure which is unique up to isomorphism. This property of fields is based on the following two facts:

F1

Any irreducible polynomial over a field has a root in some extension of the field.

F2

Any polynomial over a field has a unique root up to isomorphism.

Thanks to F1, one can construct an algebraic closure of a given field by successively ”adjoining” roots of irreducible polynomials. The uniqueness of algebraic closure for fields is a consequence of F2.

More generally, the notion of root can be defined for other algebraic objects, using which one can define the corresponding concept of algebraic closedness. Generally speaking, if F1 holds for a particular algebraic object, then one can construct algebraic closures for the algebraic object under study by adjoining roots ”one after another”. However, if F2 does not hold, there may exist several notions of algebraic closedness. We refer the reader to
[
9
,
22
]
for the case of skew fields, and
[
21
,
25
]
for the case of groups, (see also
[
24
]
for a model-theoretic approach to this problem). In this section, we investigate the question of algebraic closedness for
\sigma
-fields. Note that F1 holds for skew roots, see Proposition
3.9
. However, F2 does not hold for skew roots, see Proposition
3.12
. The lack of F2 for
\sigma
-fields gives rise to different notions of algebraic closedness for
\sigma
-fields.

4.1
Some notions of algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields

The simplest notion of algebraic closedness for
\sigma
-field is the following: A
\sigma
-field
(L,\sigma)
is called
1
-algebraically closed
if every nonconstant polynomial in
L[T;\sigma]
has a root in
L
. In this part, we show that every
\sigma
-field has a
\sigma

1

[

Theorem 4.1
.

Every
\sigma
-field can be embedded in a
1
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-field.

Proof.

Let
(K,\sigma)

\sigma

Selecting and ordering polynomials (Step 1)

\alpha_{0}

\alpha

Adjoining roots (Step 2)

Using transfinite induction, we define a sequence

\sigma

(a) Using induction on the degree of

(b) If

4.2
Conjugacy classes in algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields

In order to determine whether a
\sigma
-field is
c
-algebraically closed for
c>1
, it is enough to study polynomials of degree two. More precisely, we have the following:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
c>1
be a cardinal number. A
\sigma
-field
(L,\sigma)
is
c
-algebraically closed iff it is
1
-algebraically closed and every skew polynomial
P(T)\in L[T;\sigma]
of degree
2
whose constant term is nonzero has at least
c
distinct roots in
L
.

Proof.

To prove the ”if” direction, let
P(T)\in L[T;\sigma]
be an arbitrary polynomial satisfying
\deg P>1
and
P(0)\neq 0
. Since
(L,\sigma)
is
1
-algebraically closed, it follows from Proposition
4.4
, that there are skew polynomials
P_{1}(T),P_{2}(T)\in L[T;\sigma]
such that
\deg P_{2}=2
and
P(T)=P_{1}(T)P_{2}(T)
. Since any root of
P_{2}(T)
is a root of
P_{1}(T)
, we see that
P(T)
has at least
c
distinct roots in
L
. The other direction is trivial.
∎

To present the next result, we need to review some facts regarding roots of skew polynomials in a fixed conjugacy class. For more details see
[
14
,
18
]
. Let
(K,\sigma)
be a
\sigma
-field with fixed field

.
Let
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}\in K[T;\sigma]
.
An element
\sigma(x)x^{-1}
, where

, is a root of
P(T)
iff

P(\sigma(x)x^{-1})=0\iff\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\sigma^{i}(x)x^{-1}=0\iff\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\sigma^{i}(x)=0.

The set
E(P,1)
of all
x\in K
for which
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\sigma^{i}(x)=0
is a vector space over
F
. One can see that the assignment
[x]\mapsto\sigma(x)x^{-1}
establishes a bijection between the projective space
\mathbb{P}(E(P,1))
over
F
and the set of roots of

P(T)
in the conjugacy class
C(1)
containing
1
. In particular, if
F
and
\dim_{F}E(P,1)
are finite, the number of roots of
P(T)
in
C(1)
is equal to

\frac{|F|^{\dim_{F}E(P,1)}-1}{|F|-1}.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(K,\sigma)
be a

\sigma
-field with fixed field

is

has exactly two distinct
\sigma
-conjugacy classes. Moreover,

is

is

(K,\sigma)
is
\aleph_{0}
-algebraically closed.

Proof.

Generally speaking, any
\sigma
-field has at least two distinct
\sigma
-conjugacy classes: the trivial conjugacy class
\{0\}
and the conjugacy class containing
1
. If
(K,\sigma)
has a nonzero element
a
which is not conjugate to
1
, then Proposition
2.10
implies that there is a skew polynomial of degree two whose roots in
K
are exactly
1
and
a
. Therefore, if
K
has at least three distinct conjugacy classes, then it cannot be
3
-algebraically closed. To prove the second statement, we assume that
K
has exactly two distinct
\sigma
-conjugacy classes. Let
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
be a polynomial of degree
>1
such that
P(0)\neq 0
. Since
(K,\sigma)
has only one nontrivial conjugacy classes, we see that the cardinality of the set of roots of
P(T)
is equal to the cardinality of the projective space
\mathbb{P}(E(P,1))
. Since
(K,\sigma)
is
2
-algebraically closed, we must have
\dim_{F}E(P,1)>1
form which it follows that the cardinality of
\mathbb{P}(E(P,1))
is at least
\geq 1+|F|
, and we are done.

∎

We remark that a
\sigma
-field has exactly two distinct
\sigma
-conjugacy classes iff the map
x\mapsto\sigma(x)x^{-1}
is a bijection of
K^{*}
. As an application, we have the following:

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
n>2
be a natural number. If
(K,\sigma)
is an
n
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-field which is not
(n+1)
-algebraically closed, then the characteristic
p
of
K
is nonzero, and moreover,
n
is of the form

\frac{p^{mk}-1}{p^{k}-1},

for some natural numbers
m,k
with
m>1
.

Proof.

Since
(K,\sigma)
is
3
-algebraically closed, it follows from Theorem
4.6
that the fixed field
F
of
K
is finite. In particular, the characteristic
p
of
K
is nonzero, and
|F|=p^{k}
for some natural number
k
. Since
(K,\sigma)
is not
(n+1)
-algebraically closed, there exists a nonconstant polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma^{n}]
of degree
>1
such that
P(0)\neq 0
and
P(T)
has exactly
n
distinct roots. Since the number of roots of
P(T)
is equal to

\frac{|F|^{\dim_{F}E(P,1)}-1}{|F|-1}=\frac{p^{k\dim_{F}E(P,1)}-1}{p^{k}-1},

the result follows. ∎

Another fact regarding algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields is given in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
(K,\sigma)
be a nontrivial
\sigma
-field, that is,
\sigma\neq 1_{K}
. If
(K,\sigma)
is
1
-algebraically closed, then
\sigma
is of infinite order.

Proof.

Assume on the contrary that
\sigma
is of finite order, say order
n
. Consider the skew polynomial
P_{a}(T)=T^{n}-a\in K[T;\sigma]
where
a\in K
. An element
x\in K
is a root of
P_{a}(T)
iff

N_{n}(x)=\sigma^{n-1}(x)\cdots\sigma(x)x=a.

Since
\sigma^{n}=1_{K}
, we see that
N_{n}(x)=N_{K/F}(x)
is the norm of
x
over the fixed field
F
of
K
. In particular, we must have
a\in F
if
P_{a}(T)
has a root in
K
. Since
(K,\sigma)
is
1
-algebraically closed, we conclude that
K=F
, contradicting the assumption that
\sigma\neq 1_{K}
.
∎

In the next section, we give examples of
n
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields.

4.3
Examples of algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields

We begin with a trivial example. Let
K
be an (ordinary) algebraically closed field. It is easy to see that the trivial
\sigma
-field
(K,1_{K})
is a
1
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-field. However, it is not
2
-algebraically closed because the skew polynomial
T^{2}-2T+1\in K[T;1_{K}]
has exactly one root in
K
.

To give a nontrivial example, we let
K
be an (ordinary) algebraically closed field of characteristic
p>0
. Let
\sigma:K\to K
be the Frobenius homomorphism, that is,
\sigma(a)=a^{p}
for all
a\in K
. Given a natural number
n
, we obtain the
\sigma
-field
(K,\sigma^{n})
. It is known that the fixed field of
(K,\sigma^{n})
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}
with
p^{n}
elements. Working over
(K,\sigma^{n})
, one can easily check that

N_{i}(a)=a^{\frac{p^{in}-1}{p^{n}-1}},

for all
a\in K
and natural numbers
i\geq 1
. Therefore, given a skew polynomial
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}T^{i}\in K[T;\sigma^{n}]
and
a\in K
, we obtain

P(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}N_{i}(a)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}a^{\frac{p^{in}-1}{p^{n}-1}}.

Setting

f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}x^{\frac{p^{in}-1}{p^{n}-1}}\in K[x],

we see that
P(a)=f(a)
for all
a\in K
. Since
K
is algebraically closed, we conclude that
(K,\sigma^{n})
is
1
-algebraically closed. In fact, more can be said. Assume that
P(0)\neq 0
. Then,
f(x)
is separable. Since the degree of
f
is

\frac{p^{nm}-1}{p^{n}-1}

where
m
is the degree of
P(T)
, we see that the number of distinct roots of the skew polynomial
P(T)
is

\frac{p^{nm}-1}{p^{n}-1}.

In particular, we see that
(K,\sigma^{n})
is
(p^{n}+1)
-algebraically closed, but not
(p^{n}+2)
-algebraically closed, since if
\deg P=2
, then
P(T)
has exactly
p^{n}+1
distinct roots.

4.4
c
-Algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-fields

In the preceding part, we proved the existence of various types of algebraically closed
\sigma
-field. Here, we prove the existence of
c
-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-fields. Recall that a
\sigma
-field
(K,\sigma)
is called inversive if
\sigma
is an automorphism.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
c
be a cardinal number and
(K,\sigma)
be
c
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-field. Then, the inversive closure of
(K,\sigma)
is
c
-algebraically closed.

Proof.

Let
(L,\sigma)
be the inversive closure of
(K,\sigma)
. Assume that a polynomial

P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}T^{i}\in L[T;\sigma],

satisfies
n>1
and
a_{0}\neq 0
.
Since
(L,\sigma)
is the inversive closure of
(K,\sigma)
,
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\sigma^{N}(a_{i})\in K
for all
i=0,1,...,n
. Then, the skew polynomial

Q(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sigma^{N}(a_{i})T^{i},

belongs to
K[T;\sigma]
. It is easy to see that if
a\in L
is a root of
Q(T)
, then
\sigma^{-N}(a)
is a root of
P(T)
. Since
Q(T)
has at least
c
distinct roots in
K
, we conclude that
P(T)
has at least
c
distinct roots in
L
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

As a consequence of this proposition, we have the following result:

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
c
be a cardinal number. Any
\sigma
-field
(K,\sigma)
can be embedded in a
c
-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-field.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
,
(K,\sigma)
can be embedded in a
c
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-field
(L,\sigma)
. Then, by Proposition
4.9
, the inversive closure of
(L,\sigma)
is
c
-algebraically closed. Since the inversive closure of
(L,\sigma)
contains
(K,\sigma)
as a
\sigma
-subfield, the result follows.
∎

5
Partial fraction decomposition

In this section, we present a version of a partial fraction decomposition for the field of fractions of the ring of skew polynomials. We begin with some standard definitions and facts regarding factorization in (not necessarily commutative) principal ideal domains, see
[
6
,
8
]
for more details. Let
R
be a (not necessarily commutative) principal ideal domain (or PID for short). A nonunit
a\in R
is called irreducible if
a=bc
, where
b,c\in R
, implies that
b
or
c
is a unit in
R
.
Elements
a,b\in R
are called similar if the left modules
R/Ra
and
R/Rb
are isomorphic, or equivalently, the right modules
R/aR
and
R/bR
are isomorphic. Elements
a,b\in R
are called left (resp., right) co-prime if
aR+bR=R
(reps.,
Ra+Rb=R
). They are called co-prime if they are both left and right co-prime. An element
a\in R
is called right decomposable if
a=b_{1}c_{1}=b_{2}c_{2}
for some nonunits
b_{1},b_{2},c_{1},c_{2}\in R
such that
Rc_{1}+Rc_{2}=R
. An element is called right indecomposable if it is not right decomposable. The notion of left decomposable (indecomposable) is defined similarly.
It is known that any PID
R
is a UFD, that is, every nonunit
a\in R
can be written as

a=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n},

where
a_{1},...,a_{n}
are irreducible, and moreover, if
a=b_{1}\cdots b_{m}
, where
b_{1},...,b_{m}
are irreducible, then
m=n
, and there is a permutation
\tau\in S_{n}
such that each
b_{i}
is similar to
a_{\tau(i)}
.

5.1
Partial fraction decomposition in a PID

In this subsection,
R
is a fixed PID. It is known that
R
is an Ore domain, hence has a field of fractions
F=Frac(R)
. A fraction
ab^{-1}\in F
, where
a,b\in R
, is called
reduced
if
a
and
b
are right co-prime, that is,
Ra+Rb=R
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Any
x\in F
has at least one reduced fraction
x=ab^{-1}
which is unique up to multiplication by units. More precisely, if
ab^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
are two reduced fractions, then
a_{1}=au
and
b_{1}=bu
for some unit
u\in R
. More generally, if
ab^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
and
ab^{-1}
is reduced, then there exists
r\in R
such that
a_{1}=ar
and
b_{1}=br

Proof.

Let
x=ab^{-1}
be an arbitrary element of
F
. We have
Ra+Rb=Rc
for some
c\in R
because
R
is a PID. It follows that

a=a_{1}c
and
b=b_{1}c
for some
a_{1},b_{1}\in R
.
Therefore,
Ra_{1}+Rb_{1}=R
which implies that
x=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
is a reduced fraction. This proves the existence part.
Now, let
ab^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
, where
ab^{-1}
is reduced, that is,

xa+yb=1

for some
x,y\in R
. Since
R
is an Ore domain,
bR\cap b_{1}R\neq\{0\}
from which it follows that there exist
c,c_{1}\in R^{*}
such that
bc=b_{1}c_{1}
. We have

ab^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}\implies(ab^{-1})(bc)=(a_{1}b_{1}^{-1})(b_{1}c_{1})\implies ac=a_{1}c_{1}.

Therefore, we obtain

c=(xa+yb)c=xac+ybc=xa_{1}c_{1}+yb_{1}c_{1}=(xa_{1}+yb_{1})c_{1}.

Setting
r=xa_{1}+yb_{1}
, we obtain

a_{1}c_{1}=ac=arc_{1}\implies a_{1}=ar.

In a similar way, one can show that
b_{1}=br
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following result can be regarded as a generalization of the method of partial fraction decomposition, see
[
19
]
for the case of a commutative prinicipal ideal domain.

Theorem 5.2
.

Every fraction of
R
can be be written as a sum of fractions of the form
ab^{-1}
, where
a,b\in R
with
0\neq b
being right indecomposable.

Proof.

Let
S
be the set of all fractions that cannot be written in the stated form. We need to show that
S
is empty. Assume, on the contrary, that
S
is nonempty. Consider the set
I
of all ideals of the form
Rb
where
ab^{-1}\in S
for some nonunits
a\in R
and
b\in R^{*}
. Since
R
is a PID,
I
has a maximal element
Rb_{0}
with respect to inclusion. Then
ab_{0}^{-1}\in S
for some
a\in R
. Note that
b_{0}
cannot be right indecomposable since otherwise
ab_{0}^{-1}\notin S
. So,
b_{0}
must be right decomposable, that is, we have
b_{0}=b_{1}c_{1}=b_{2}c_{2}
for some
b_{1},c_{1},b_{2},c_{2}\in R
such that
Rc_{1}+Rc_{2}=R
. In particular, there exist
a_{1},a_{2}\in R
such that

a_{1}c_{1}+a_{2}c_{2}=a
. Moreover,
b_{0}R\subsetneq b_{1}R
and
b_{0}R\subsetneq b_{2}R
since
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are non-units. Now, we have

a=a_{1}c_{1}+a_{2}c_{2}\implies ab_{0}^{-1}=a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}+a_{2}b_{2}^{-1}.

By the maximality of
Rb_{0}
, neither
a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
nor
a_{2}b_{2}^{-1}
can belong to
S
. Therefore, both
a_{1}b_{1}^{-1}
and
a_{2}b_{2}^{-1}
can be written in the desired form. Consequently,
ab^{-1}
which is the sum of these two fractions can be written in the desired form, a contradiction. This complete the proof.
∎

5.2
Partial fraction decomposition in
K(T;\sigma)

Let
(K,\sigma)
be an inversive
\sigma
-field. It is known that
K[T;\sigma]
is a PID. Its field of fractions is denoted by
K(T;\sigma)
. In particular, we can apply Theorem
5.2
to
K[T;\sigma]
. In fact, we have the following stronger result in the case of
K(T;\sigma)
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Every fraction
P(T)Q(T)^{-1}\in K(T;\sigma)
can be written as

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{0}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1},

where
P_{0}(T),P_{1}(T),Q_{1}(T),...,P_{n}(T),Q_{n}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
,
\deg P_{i}<\deg Q_{i}
for every
i
, each
Q_{i}(T)
is right indecomposable, and each fraction
P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1}
is reduced.

Proof.

Let
S
be the set of all partial fraction decompositions

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{0}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1},

where
P_{0}(T),P_{1}(T),Q_{1}(T),...,P_{m}(T),Q_{m}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
, and each
Q_{i}(T)
is right indecomposable of degree
>0
.
By Theorem
5.2
, the set
S
is nonempty. Therefore, we can choose

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{0}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1}

in
S
for which
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg P_{i}
is as small as possible. I claim that this decomposition satisfies the desired conditions. Assume, on the contrary, that
\deg P_{i}\geq\deg Q_{i}
for some
i
, say
i=1
.
Using the Euclidean division algorithm in
K[T;\sigma]
, we write

P_{1}(T)=P^{\prime}(T)Q_{1}(T)+R(T),

where
P^{\prime}(T),R(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
and
\deg R<\deg Q.

Then, the decomposition

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=\left(P_{0}(T)+P^{\prime}(T)\right)+\left(R(T)Q_{1}(T)^{-1}+\sum_{i=2}^{m}P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1}\right),

belongs to
S
, which is a contradiction since

\deg R+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\deg P_{i}<\deg Q+\sum_{i=2}^{m}\deg P_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\deg P_{i}.

This completes the proof of the claim. If some
P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1}
is not reduced, we can replace
P_{i}(T)Q_{i}(T)^{-1}
by a reduced fraction whose numerator has a smaller degree than the degree of
P_{i}(T)
, contradicting the choice of the partial fraction decomposition. This completes the proof. ∎

Next, we show that a stronger result is valid in the case of
2
-algebraically closed
\sigma
-fields.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
(K,\sigma)
be a
2
-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-field. Any fraction
P(T)Q(T)^{-1}\in K(T;\sigma)
has a partial fraction decomposition of the form

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{0}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}T^{-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(1-c_{i}T)^{-1},

where
P_{0}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
,
a_{1},...,a_{m}\in K
,
b_{1},...,b_{n},c_{1},...,c_{n}\in K^{*}
, and
c_{1},...,c_{n}
are (right) P-independent. Moreover, if

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{1}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}a^{\prime}_{i}T^{-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}b^{\prime}_{i}(1-c^{\prime}_{i}T)^{-1},

is another such decomposition, then we have

P_{0}(T)=P_{1}(T)
,
m=m_{1}
, and
a_{i}=a^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i
.

Proof.

First, we show that a nonconstant polynomial
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
is right indecomposable iff
P(T)=aT^{m}
for some
m>0
and
a\in K^{*}
, or
P(T)=a(1-cT)
for some
a,c\in K^{*}
. To prove the ”only if” part of this statement, assume that
P(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
is right indecomposable. There is
m\geq 0
and
Q(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
with a nonzero constant term such that
P(T)=Q(T)T^{m}
. Since
(K,\sigma)
is inversive, there exists
Q_{1}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
with a nonzero constant term such that

P(T)=Q(T)T^{m}=T^{m}Q_{1}(T).

Since
Q_{1}(T)
and
T^{m}
are right co-prime,
m
must be zero or
Q(T)
must be a constant. In the latter case, we have
P(T)=aT^{m}
for some
a\in K^{*}
. In the former case,
Q(T)
must be a linear skew polynomial, since otherwise the fact that
(K,\sigma)
is
2
-algebraically closed implies that
Q(T)
has at least two distinct roots, say
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
. It follows that

Q(T)=R_{1}(T)(T-a_{1})=R_{2}(T)(T-a_{2}),

for some polynomials
R_{1}(T),R_{2}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
. Since
T-a_{1}
and
T-a_{2}
are right co-prime,
Q(T)
must be right decomposable, a contradiction. This completes the proof of the ”only if” part. Conversely, it is clear that any linear skew polynomial is right indecomposable. Since
T^{m}=P(T)Q(T)
implies that
P(T)=aT^{n}
and
Q(T)=(1/a)T^{m-n}
for some
0\neq n\neq m
, we see that
T^{m}
is right indecomposable. This completes the proof of the statement. Now, we can prove the theorem: It follows from the above statement and Theorem
5.3
that any
P(T)Q(T)^{-1}\in K(T;\sigma)
has a partial fraction decomposition of the form

P(T)Q(T)^{-1}=P_{0}(T)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}T^{-i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(1-c_{i}T)^{-1},

where
P_{0}(T)\in K[T;\sigma]
,
a_{1},...,a_{m},b_{1},...,b_{n}\in K
, and the elements
c_{1},...,c_{n}
belong to
K^{*}
. Using Proposition
2.5
, it is now easy to show that there is such a partial fraction decomposition which, in addition, satisfies the requirement that
c_{1},...,c_{n}
be P-independent.

To prove the uniqueness part, we first note that the summand
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}T^{-i}
does not belong to
K[[T;\sigma]]^{*}
while the other summands belong to
K[[T;\sigma]]
, from which the equality
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}T^{-i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}a^{\prime}_{i}T^{-i}
follows. Finally, using the degree function on
K(T;\sigma)
, one can easily show that
P_{1}(T)
must be equal to
P_{0}(T)
.
∎

5.3
The twisted Hadamard algebra

We conclude this paper with an application for which we need to recall the definition of the twisted Hadamard algebra over a
\sigma
-field. For more details, see
[
1
]
. Let
(K,\sigma)
be an inversive
\sigma
-field. An element in
K[[T,\sigma]]
is called a rational twisted series if it belongs to the subring
K(T;\sigma)
. A rational twisted series is called regular if its degree is negative. The set of all regular rational series over
(K,\sigma)
is denoted by
H(T;\sigma)
. In
[
1
]
, it is shown that the set
H(T;\sigma)
equipped with the addition operation inherited from
K[[T,\sigma]]
and the Hadamard multiplication
\odot
, that is,

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}T^{i}\right)\odot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}b_{i}T^{i}\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}b_{i}T^{i},

is a
K
-algebra, called the twisted Hadamard algebra over
(K,\sigma)
. Here, we give a characterization of the twisted Hadamard algebra in the case when
(K,\sigma)
is 2-algebraically closed. For a characterization of the ordinary Hadamard algebra in terms of exponential polynomials, see
[
2
]
. In the following theorem, we use the
K
-algebra
N_{K^{*}}
introduced in Subsection
2.1
.

Theorem 5.5
.

There exists a unique
K
-algebra homomorphism

\alpha:N_{K^{*}}\to H(T;\sigma),

which satisfies
\alpha(N(a))=(1-aT)^{-1}
for all
a\in K^{*}
. Furthermore,
\alpha
is injective, and, if
K
is a 2-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-field,
\alpha
is an isomorphism of
K
-algebras.

Proof.

The vector space
K^{\infty}
, as defined in Subsection
2.1
, is a
K
-algebra under pointwise multiplication. Consider the map
\beta:K^{\infty}\to K[[T;\sigma]]
defined by

\beta(a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},...)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}T^{i}.

It is easy to see that
\beta
is a
K
-algebra homomorphism where
K[[T;\sigma]]
is equipped with the Hadamard product. Let
\alpha:N_{K^{*}}\to K[[T;\sigma]]
be the restriction of
\beta
to
N_{K^{*}}
. Note that
(1-aT)^{-1}\in H(T;\sigma)
, for all
a\in K^{*}
. Since
N_{K^{*}}
, as a vector space, is generated by
N(a)
’s, and
H(T;\sigma)
is closed under the Hadamard product, we see that the image of
\alpha
is contained in
H(T;\sigma)
. The uniqueness of
\alpha
follows from the fact that the elements
N(a)
,
a\in K
, generate
N_{K^{*}}
as a vector space over
K
. Clearly,
\alpha
is injective. The fact that
\alpha
is surjective when
K
is a
2
-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
-field follows from Theorem
5.4
.

∎

As a consequence of this result, we have the following:

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}T^{i}
be a rational twisted series over a
2
-algebraically closed inversive
\sigma
