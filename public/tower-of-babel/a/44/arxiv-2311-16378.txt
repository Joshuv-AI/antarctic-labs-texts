Bayesian Spectral Graph Denoising with Smoothness Prior
Sam Leone 1 † {\dagger} , Xingzhi Sun 2 † {\dagger} , Michael Perlmutter 3 and Smita Krishnaswamy 1,2,4,5,6,7,8

Abstract
Here we consider the problem of denoising features associated to complex data, modeled as signals on a graph, via a smoothness prior. This is motivated in part by settings such as single-cell RNA where the data is very high-dimensional, but its structure can be captured via an affinity graph. This allows us to utilize ideas from graph signal processing. In particular, we present algorithms for the cases where the signal is perturbed by Gaussian noise, dropout, and uniformly distributed noise. The signals are assumed to follow a prior distribution defined in the frequency domain which favors signals which are smooth across the edges of the graph. By pairing this prior distribution with our three models of noise generation, we propose Maximum A Posteriori (M.A.P.) estimates of the true signal in the presence of noisy data and provide algorithms for computing the M.A.P. Finally, we demonstrate the algorithms’ ability to effectively restore signals from white noise on image data and from severe dropout in single-cell RNA sequence data.



I
Introduction

Signals defined on modern large-scale, irregularly structured data sets are often corrupted by large amounts of noise such as measurement error or missing measurements. This motivates one to estimate the most likely
true, uncorrupted
values of the signal based on both the noisy observations and their prior beliefs about the signal, which often takes the form of a smoothness assumption. We shall present an approach for producing such
Maximum A Posteriori
(M.A.P.) estimates which utilizes tools from spectral graph theory.

Our method is motivated by the explosion in recent decades of complex high-dimensional data, and associated signals, with very high noise levels. Such data may explicitly reside on a graph, e.g., social, energy, transportation, sensor, or neuronal networks
[
1
]
, or it may implicitly have relationships between entities from which a graph can be built, for example, physical and biological systems, text, image, time series
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]

With the graph (either existing or built from data), we can treat features as signals (functions) on the graph, and apply methods in graph signal processing, especially spectral graph theory. Typically, a well-behaved signal defined on the vertices will take similar values at vertices that are more connected. This leads us to the prior that many functions of interest will be
smooth
on the graph, where the concept of smoothness can be quantified using tools from spectral graph theory and the eigendecomposition of the graph Laplacian.
This intuition motivates the following approach. First, we assume a priori that the signal of interest is likely “fairly smooth” on the graph. Then, we model the noise of the observations. Finally, we produce an estimate of the true signal with the highest likelihood based on our prior beliefs and the observed measurements. Importantly, we note that the assumption that the signal is smooth is very mild and we do not assume that the signal (or the data on which it is defined) has any specific form. We provide details on how to implement this approach under several noise models and then demonstrate the effectiveness of our method on real-world and synthetic data sets.
We also note that our method fills the gap of theoretical guarantees in the popular method MAGIC
[
4
]
, which it outperforms due to the specific modeling of noise types.

Background & Related Work

II-A

An example for high-dimensional data

We first motivate our method via an example of denoising features associated to complex high-dimensional data. Single-cell RNA sequence (scRNA-seq) provides high resolution information about gene expression and is of great interest in molecular biology, clinical studies, and biotechnology
[
7
,

. scRNA-seq data is high-dimensional, as it measures the expression of tens of thousands of genes on up to millions of cells
[
9
]
, and suffers from high noise levels due to multiple sources. Reducing this noise is a crucial step, which is needed prior to downstream analysis
[
10
]
.

In single-cell RNA sequence data, one obtains the gene-expression counts for a variety of genes in each cell. Each cell can then be viewed as a high-dimensional vector (whose
i
-th coordinate corresponds to the amount of gene
i
expressed). It is a common practice to turn this data, consisting of high-dimensional vectors (cells), into a graph by placing edges between cells which are close together in high-dimensional space, and viewing the expression of each gene, as a signal (function) defined on the cellular graph
[
11
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
.

II-B

Graph Signal Processing with Bayesian inference

Spectral graph theory concerns itself with the distribution of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
matrices associated with graphs. The set of eigenvalue-eigenvector pairs is known to uncover the geometric and algebraic properties of a graph. This is the observation that drives algorithms like spectral clustering and diffusion maps
[
6
]
, the main intuition being that low frequency eigenpairs capture low-resolution, key information about a graph’s structure.

Graph Signal Processing (GSP) utilizes tools from spectral graph theory to extend the Fourier transform from classical signal processing and time series analysis to the graph setting
[
1
]
. In the classical methods,
signals can be denoised by mapping the signal to the Fourier domain, reducing the high-frequency component of the function, and inverting the Fourier
transform to achieve a “smoother” version of the signal. In much the same way, GSP operates by
representing graph signals in a basis eigenvectors for the graph Laplacian (defined below), whose corresponding eigenvalues may be interpreted as (squared) frequencies,
and then reducing the high-frequency components. Filtering in this manner has been applied to use cases such as
point cloud data, biological imaging, sensor networks, and more
[
14
]
.

Bayesian inference is a fundamental method of parameter estimation. The typical form of this problem is that there is a random variable
\boldsymbol{x}
drawn from some prior distribution and another random variable
\boldsymbol{y}
whose distribution depends on
\boldsymbol{x}
. The ambition of Bayesian estimation is, given only
\boldsymbol{y}
, to estimate the underlying value of
\boldsymbol{x}
using both prior information on
\boldsymbol{x}
and the interaction between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Notably, two important, nonstandard aspects of our method are: (1) we do not have any explicit prior on a data on which the signals is defined, but rather directly build the graph from the data (if it does not already exist), and treat it as a deterministic structure; (2) we do not assume the signal has any specific form, but rather use a mild prior of its smoothness on the graph. These distinctions free us from the limitations of Bayesian models caused by model misspecification
[
15
]
, and make our method generally applicable to the vast range of data sets regardless of the data distributions.

II-C

MAGIC: Markov Affinity-based Graph Imputation of Cells

MAGIC
[
4
]
is a commonly used method for denoising single-cell data. It is based on the idea that the high-dimensional data lies on a low-dimensional manifold, represented by a nearest neighbor graph. After building the graph, it uses data diffusion, which is random-walk on the graph to denoise the model. Its has been tremendously successful; however, it lacks a solid theoretical model. Our method fills this gap with GSP and Bayesian inference. Furthermore, by specifying cases of common noise models, we are able to adjust our model accordingly, allowing us to outperform MAGIC in these cases.

II-D

Notation and Defininitions

Throughout, we shall let
G=(V,E,w)
denote a weighted, connected, and undirected graph with
|V|=n
and
|E|=m
. Without loss of generality, we will assume

V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We shall refer to functions
\boldsymbol{f}:V\to\mathbb{R}
as
graph signals
. In a slight abuse of notation, we will not distinguish between
\boldsymbol{f}
and the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose
a
-th entry is
\boldsymbol{f}(a)
,
1\leq a\leq n
. We shall let
\mathbf{A}
denote the
weighted
adjacency matrix and let
\boldsymbol{D}=\text{diag}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{1})
denote the corresponding diagonal degree matrix.

Given
\mathbf{A}
and
\boldsymbol{D}
, the
combinatorial Laplacian
is defined as
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{D}-\mathbf{A}
. Is is well-known that
\boldsymbol{L}
admits an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors,
\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i},

1\leq i\leq n,
where
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}=\boldsymbol{1}
and
0=\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
. It follows that
\boldsymbol{L}
is a positive semi-definite matrix whose null space is equal to
\textbf{span}\{\boldsymbol{1}\}
. One may compute that the quadratic form corresponding to
\boldsymbol{L}
is given by
\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{\{a,b\}\in E}w(a,b)(\boldsymbol{f}(a)-\boldsymbol{f}(b))^{2}
. Thus, setting
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
, the
\lambda_{i}
are interpreted as (squared) frequencies, representing the rate at which
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
oscillates over the graph, and the
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
are interpreted as
generalized Fourier modes
, where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})=\langle\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\rangle
represents the portion of
\boldsymbol{f}
at frequency
\lambda_{i}
. Since the
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
are an orthonormal basis, we have
\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
. Therefore, for a real-valued function
h
, we can define a corresponding filter by
h(\boldsymbol{f})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\lambda_{i})\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
.
We shall let
\boldsymbol{B}
denote the weighted
m\times n
incidence matrix, where rows correspond to edges and columns to vertices, whose entries are given by
\boldsymbol{B}(e,a)=-\boldsymbol{B}(e,b)=\sqrt{w(a,b)}
, if
e=(a,b)
and
\boldsymbol{B}(e,v)=0
for all
v\not\in\{a,b\}

[
16
]
. One may verify that the Laplacian can be factored as
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}
. (Here, we implicitly assume an arbitrary, but fixed, ordering of each edge
(a,b)
. This arbitrary choice does not affect the identity
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}
nor any of our analysis.)

We shall let
p(\boldsymbol{f})
denote the probability distribution of a random variable
\boldsymbol{f}
and shall let
p(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})
denote the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{f}
given another random variable
\boldsymbol{g}
. We shall make use of the fact that by Bayes’ theorem,
p(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})\propto p(\boldsymbol{f})p(\boldsymbol{g}|\boldsymbol{f})
, where
\propto
denotes proportionality and the implied constant depends on
\boldsymbol{g}
.

III
Methods

Our goal is to estimate an unknown signal
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
based on an observation
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which we interpret as a noisy version of
\boldsymbol{f}
under various settings. In each case, we will assume that an observed
\boldsymbol{g}
is obtained from a corruption of a true signal
\boldsymbol{f}
which lies within a corresponding admissibility class
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
. We shall then define the
maximum a posteriori
estimate of
\boldsymbol{f}
to be the most likely value of
\boldsymbol{f}
based on (i) the fact that
\boldsymbol{g}
was observed and (ii) our
a priori
beliefs on
\boldsymbol{f}
discussed in the following subsection.

III-A

A Prior Distribution Based on Smoothness

We define prior distributions on
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})
for
i=2,\ldots,n
, assuming that each
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})
follows the probability distribution:

p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}}(\lambda_{i}))\propto\exp\big(-\kappa\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\big)

where
\kappa
is a fixed smoothing parameter. We further assume that the
\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})
are independent which implies that, for any fixed value of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{1})
, the probability distribution of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
satisfies

\displaystyle p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}})\propto\prod_{i=2}^{n}\exp\big(-\kappa\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\big)=\exp\bigg(-\kappa\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\bigg).

We then give
\boldsymbol{f}
the probability distribution defined by taking the inverse GFT of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}
.
We note that since the
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
are an orthonormal eigenbasis and
\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{i})\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}
, we have

p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})\propto\exp\bigg(-\kappa\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\bigg)=\exp\big(-\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}\big).

Therefore, we see that this probability distribution is defined so that the likelihood of
\boldsymbol{f}
decreases with its variation across the graph and
\kappa
acts as a parameter controlling the tolerance towards fluctuation.
Notably, we do not assume any prior distribution on
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{1})
(although is some cases
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{1})
will be implicitly constrained by the admissibility class
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
). Therefore, our maximum a posteriori estimate is simply the most likely value of
\boldsymbol{f}
based on the
\boldsymbol{g}
and our prior beliefs about
\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{2}),\ldots,\widehat{\boldsymbol{f}}(\lambda_{n})
.

III-B

Gaussian Noise on the Graph

We first consider the setting where each of the Fourier coefficients is corrupted by Gaussian noise, i.e.,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})=\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})+z_{i}
,
2\leq i\leq n
, where each
z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
is an independent normal random variable. We will further assume that the total noise

\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{1})\}

\boldsymbol{g}
, we have
p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})\propto p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{g}|\boldsymbol{f})
, one may derive a maximum a posteriori estimate of
\boldsymbol{f}
given
\boldsymbol{g}

(Gaussian Denoising)
.

Let

\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{1})\}
. As above, assume that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})=\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})+z_{i},2\leq i\leq n

are as described in Section

\boldsymbol{f}
given
\boldsymbol{g}

.
Moreover,

can be computed, to within

-norm (

We note that the minimum in the term describing the time complexity arises from the existence of two possible methods of computation, both of which are algorithms for solving linear systems in an implicit matrix

\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{L})=\sum_{a\in V}\deg(a)=2\sum_{(a,b)\in E}w(a,b)

III-C

Uniformly Distributed Noise

Next, we consider the case when the noise is a random uniform scaling in the vertex domain:
\boldsymbol{g}(a)=u(a)\boldsymbol{f}(a)
, where each
u(a)\sim\text{Unif}[0,1]
is an independent uniform random variable. In this case, since
0\leq u(a)\leq 1
, we set the admissibility class
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:|\boldsymbol{f}(a)|\geq|\boldsymbol{g}(a)|,\text{sign}(\boldsymbol{f})=\text{sign}(\boldsymbol{g}),\forall a\in V\}
. For such an
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
, one may compute that the a posteriori likelihood of
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
given
\boldsymbol{g}
is

p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})=p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})\prod_{a\in V}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{f}(a)|}.

We will maximize the a posteriori likelihood by minimizing the negative log likelihood, which using basic properties of the logarithm leads us to the optimization problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}),\quad\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f})=\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\sum_{a\in V}\log|\boldsymbol{f}(a)|.

In order to (approximately) solve this problem, we adopt a constrained Convex-Concave Procedure (CCP)
[
18
]
for the above. The CCP operates by splitting a function of the form
f(x)=f_{\text{concave}}(x)+f_{\text{convex}}(x)
and approximating the concave portion linearly about the current solution; the relaxed problem is convex and can be solved more efficiently. The procedure is repeated until convergence, and it is known to be a descent algorithm. Applied this particular optimization, the CCP update of
\boldsymbol{f}^{t+1}
from
\boldsymbol{f}^{t}
is as follows:

\boldsymbol{f}^{t+1}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}}\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\sum_{a\in V}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}(a)}{|\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a)|}

We remark that
\boldsymbol{f}^{t+1}
can be computed as a quadratic program and that the update provides a descent algorithm -
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}^{t+1})\leq\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}^{t})
. This is because the loss function is a quadratic function of
\boldsymbol{f}
and the feasible region
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
is a convex polyhedron.

III-D

Partial Observations & Bernoulli Dropout

In our final two models, we consider two settings where the noise behaves differently at different vertices. We assume that there is some (possibly unknown) set
S\subseteq V
where
f(a)
is exactly equal to
g(a)
for all
a\in S
. We make no assumption regarding the relationship between
f(a)
and
g(a)
for
a\notin S
. This leads us to define the admissibility class
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:\boldsymbol{f}(a)=\boldsymbol{g}(a)\text{ for all }a\in S\}
. We consider two practically useful variations of this problem:

1.

Basic Interpolation: The set
S
is known.

2.

Bernoulli Dropout: There is a “set of suspicion”
\zeta
where we are unsure whether

or
a\in S^{c}

\zeta^{c}

and that

with probability

In this first scenario, the maximum a posteriori estimate of

\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}=\arg\max_{f\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}}p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})

Now, we consider the second, more challenging scenario where we observe a signal
\boldsymbol{g}
which is equal to
\boldsymbol{f}
, except in a set of suspicion
\zeta
where, with probability
p
,
g(a)
is corrupted (i.e., not equal to
f(a)
). Based on the observation
\boldsymbol{g}

S=\{a\in V:\boldsymbol{f}(a)=\boldsymbol{g}(a)\}
(although we do know
\zeta^{c}\subseteq S)
. However, we note that for
\boldsymbol{g}
to take a given value, there must be
\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}

|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}

p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{g}|\boldsymbol{f})\propto p^{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}(1-p)^{|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}

\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
, the negative log of the posterior likelihood of
\boldsymbol{f}
can be estimated as:

\displaystyle-\log p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})

\displaystyle=\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}\big(\log(1-p)-\log(p)\big)

\displaystyle+\text{constant}.

Therefore, if we define
\tau=\kappa^{-1}(\log(1-p)-\log(p))
, we observe the MAP is produced by the following minimization problem:

\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}\in\min_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\tau\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}.

Note that the sign of
\tau

\log(1-p)-\log(p)=\log(\frac{1}{p}-1)

p<1/2

\boldsymbol{f}

\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)

\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta^{c})

-based greedy algorithm
[
21
]
. Alternatively, we may consider a relaxed version of the minimization problem in which the
\ell_{0}
penalty term is replaced with an
\ell_{1}
penalty term. In this case, the relaxed
\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}
can be found via LASSO regression
[
23
]
, for which many efficient algorithms exist.

Finally, we draw special attention to the “no-trust” case where
\zeta=V
, i.e. we are skeptical of all observations. Then the optimization can be written more simply:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}(\zeta)

\displaystyle\in\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)+\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{x}}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{g}\|_{2}^{2}+\tau\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{0}

The benefit of the no-trust estimate is that it makes few assumptions about the nature of the noise and does not require the user to come up with
\zeta
.

V
Conclusion

We have introduced a method that denoises high-dimensional data by building a graph, treating the features as signals on the graph, and doing M.A.P estimation to recover the true features as denoised signals. We only rely on a mild prior of smoothness on the graph, making our model general and applicable to a vast variety of data modalities. We produce estimators and efficient algorithms for three types of noise common in real data: Gaussian, uniform-scaling, and Bernoulli dropout. Our model outperforms MAGIC and other methods, thanks to the modeling of the noise.

Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem 1

(Gaussian Denoising)
.

Suppose we observe some value
\boldsymbol{g}
and let
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{1})=\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{1})\}
. Suppose also that
\boldsymbol{g}
is generated from
\boldsymbol{f}
by additive Gaussian noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
in frequencies
\lambda_{2}\ldots\lambda_{n}
and that
\boldsymbol{f}
has density
p_{\kappa}
in
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
. Then if we let
h(\lambda_{i})=\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}
be the filter function, the the maximum a posteriori likelihood of
\boldsymbol{f}
given
\boldsymbol{g}
is,

\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}=h(\boldsymbol{g})

Furthermore, the MAP can be computed in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(m\log(\epsilon^{-1})\min\big\{\sqrt{\log(n)},\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{\max}+1/2\kappa\sigma^{2}}{\lambda_{\min}+1/2\kappa\sigma^{2}}}\big\}\big)
to
\epsilon
accuracy in the
\boldsymbol{L}
-norm.

Proof.

We first prove that the MAP is produced by the given filter. Notice first that the following expression holds for the likelihood of
\boldsymbol{g}
given
\boldsymbol{f}
, up to a multiplicative constant:

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{g}|\boldsymbol{f})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i=2}^{n}\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i}))^{2}\big)

independence

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=2}^{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i}))^{2}\big)

log properties

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i}))^{2}\big)

\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{1})=\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{1})
when
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}-\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\big)\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}

Parseval’s Identity

We can multiply this by the
a priori
probability of
\boldsymbol{f}
to obtain the
a posteriori
likelihood
p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})
:

\displaystyle p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})

\displaystyle\propto p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})\exp\big(-\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\exp\big(-\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}\big)

Of course, this relation holds among those
\boldsymbol{f}
whose means are the same as
\boldsymbol{g}
. To remedy this, we simply parameterize the set of
\boldsymbol{f}
who share a mean with
\boldsymbol{g}
. Let
\Pi
be the projection matrix onto
\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{1}\}^{\perp}
. We can write
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
where
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}=a\boldsymbol{1}
and
a=\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}
. Then
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\Pi)\boldsymbol{g}
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}
. Furthermore,
\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}=\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\Pi\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}
. Thus, by this observation and monotonicity of the exponent, it suffices to minimize the following loss function in
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\Pi\boldsymbol{g}\|^{2}+\kappa\boldsymbol{f}_{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}

Taking a gradient,

\nabla\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\Pi\boldsymbol{g})+2\kappa\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=\bigg(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Pi\boldsymbol{g}

Setting the gradient equal to zero, we obtain the necessary expression for
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}

\boldsymbol{f}_{1}=\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}\Pi\boldsymbol{g}

If we plug in our value for
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}

(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}
is mean preserving, then we finally obtain the following expression:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}\Pi\boldsymbol{g}+(\boldsymbol{I}-\Pi)\boldsymbol{g}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}

We now prove that this is produced by the given filter. Let
\boldsymbol{L}=\Psi\Lambda\Psi^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition of
\boldsymbol{L}
. Then we write,

\displaystyle\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\Psi\Psi^{\top}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\Psi\Lambda\Psi^{\top}\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle\Psi\Psi^{\top}=\boldsymbol{I}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\Psi\big(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\Lambda\big)\Psi^{\top}\bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Psi^{\top})^{-1}\big(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\Lambda\big)^{-1}\Psi^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Psi\text{diag}\big(1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}:i\in 1\ldots n\big)^{-1}\Psi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Psi\text{diag}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}:i\in 1\ldots n\bigg)\Psi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Psi h(\Lambda)\Psi^{\top}

Which demonstrates that the claimed filter. To prove that the filter can be computed efficiently, we make an appeal to the solver of

17

. Note that to compute

\boldsymbol{f}=\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{-1}\boldsymbol{g}

It suffices to solve,

\bigg(\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{g}

This can be done in time
\mathcal{\tilde{O}}(m\sqrt{\log(n)})
because
\boldsymbol{I}+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}
belongs to the class SDDM
0
. This is true because i. rescaling a Laplacian by a positive constant is still a Laplacian and ii. matrices of the form Laplacian + Diagonal belong to SDDM
0
.

The Gaussian Parameter Estimate

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{g}
is generated in the way that is described. For brevity, let
a=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\boldsymbol{1}^{\top}\boldsymbol{g}
be the component of
\boldsymbol{g}
in
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{1}
. Note that when
\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}
, then the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})
is
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}})
. Furthermore, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda-i)-\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})
is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
(it is known that if a signal is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I})
distributed, then its distribution is preserved under rotations). And thus, the distribution of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})
is
\mathcal{N}\big(0,\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}}\big)
. In this case, we can compute a moment of
\boldsymbol{g}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{L}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{g}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})^{2}]

linearity of expectation

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}\bigg(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}}\bigg)

normal distribution properties

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+\frac{1}{2\kappa}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{L})+\frac{1}{2\kappa}(n-1)

Similarly, we compute again,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{g}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}^{2}\boldsymbol{g}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\bigg(\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2\kappa}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{L}^{2})+\frac{1}{2\kappa}\text{tr}(\boldsymbol{L})

\tau
by
\hat{\tau}
is finding values of
\hat{\kappa}
and
\hat{\sigma^{2}}
for which these equations coincide with our observations and setting
\hat{\tau}=2\hat{\kappa}\hat{\sigma}^{2}
. Depending on the nature of our observations, it could be by chance that
\hat{\sigma^{2}}
or
\hat{\kappa}
are nonpositive.

\boldsymbol{x}

Additional Properties

We can also attempt to compute the distribution of the map estimate. Note,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}-\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\bigg(\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}_{\text{map}}(\lambda_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{g}}(\lambda_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})+z_{i})-\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\bigg(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})}_{\mathcal{N}(0,(2\kappa\lambda_{i})^{-1})}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\bigg)+\underbrace{z_{i}}_{\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})}\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\bigg(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\bigg)}_{\mathcal{N}\big(0,(2\kappa\lambda_{i})^{-1}\big(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\big)^{2}\big)}+\underbrace{z_{i}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}\bigg)}_{\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1)^{2}})}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}\bigg(\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\widehat{f}}(\lambda_{i})\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\bigg)+z_{i}\bigg(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}\bigg)}_{\mathcal{N}(0,(2\kappa\lambda_{i})^{-1}\big(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\big)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1)^{2}})}\bigg)

We then compute,

\displaystyle(2\kappa\lambda_{i})^{-1}\big(\frac{1}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}-1\big)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}}\bigg(\frac{2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}{1+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}}\bigg)^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1)^{2}}\bigg(\frac{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i})^{2}}{2\kappa\lambda_{i}}+\sigma^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1)^{2}}\bigg(2\kappa\sigma^{4}\lambda_{i}+\sigma^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\frac{2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1}{(2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1}

Therefore, there exists coefficients
c_{2}\ldots c_{n}
each distributed
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1})
. If we compile these coefficients into a vector
\boldsymbol{c}=(0,c_{2}\ldots c_{n})

\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}-\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{i}c_{i}=\Psi\boldsymbol{c}

As
\boldsymbol{c}
follows a multivariate normal distribution, so does
\Psi\boldsymbol{c}
. It is mean
0
and has covariance matrix,

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(\Psi\boldsymbol{c})

\displaystyle=\Psi\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{c})\Psi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Psi\text{diag}(0,c_{2}\ldots c_{n})\Psi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\Psi\text{diag}\big(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\kappa\sigma^{2}\lambda_{i}+1}:i\in 2\ldots n\big)\Psi^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\bigg(\Pi+2\kappa\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{L}\bigg)^{+}

From this expression, we can determine the behavior of the error for different values of
\sigma
and
\kappa
. When
\sigma=0
, we recover the exact signal, and the covariance matrix goes to zero. On the other hand, when
\kappa\to 0
, the covariance matrix approaches
\sigma^{2}\Pi
. This is because in the limit for such
\kappa
, we rely less and less on our prior information. In such a case, the map estimate is
\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}\to\boldsymbol{g}
, and the above formula is simply the covariance matrix of
z=\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{f}
.

It is worth beginning with an explanation of the harmonic interpolation algorithm suggested by

\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}
is minimal. The authors state that the optimal

is given by
\boldsymbol{L}(S^{c},S^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{A}(S^{c},S)\boldsymbol{g}(S)
.

Theorem 2

(Restated from
[
19
]
)
.

Suppose
S
has at least one edge going to
S^{c}
. Then there exists a unique solution to
\min_{\boldsymbol{f}\in\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}

\boldsymbol{f}
to

is given by

\boldsymbol{f}_{\text{map}}(S^{c})=\boldsymbol{L}(S^{c},S^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{A}(S^{c},S)\boldsymbol{g}(S).

We emphasize that the interpolation problem does not depend on the entirety of
S

\partial S

H

\hat{m}

\mathcal{O}(\hat{m})

\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}

(a subgraph of

\mathcal{O}(\hat{m})

Derivation of the M.A.P. Estimate

Thankfully, most of the computations involved in the Bernoulli model are fairly elementary. Suppose we have our “set of suspicion”
\zeta
and an estimate
\boldsymbol{f}
of the true signal. In order for
\boldsymbol{g}
to be produced by
\boldsymbol{f}
, it needs to be that
\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}
observations get sent to
S^{c}
, all independently and with probability
p
. Otherwise,
\boldsymbol{g}(a)=\boldsymbol{f}(a)
with probability
1-p
for each of
|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}
observations. The result is that the conditional likelihood of
\boldsymbol{g}
given
\boldsymbol{f}
is,

p^{\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}(1-p)^{|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}

Multiplying this by the prior probability
p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f})
, we obtain the conditional likelihood of
\boldsymbol{f}
given
\boldsymbol{g}
:

p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g})\propto\exp(-\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f})p^{|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}(1-p)^{|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}}

And thus,

\displaystyle\log(p_{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{f}|\boldsymbol{g}))

\displaystyle=-\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}\log(p)+(|\zeta|-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0})\log(1-p)+\text{constant}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}(\log(p)-\log(1-p))+\text{constant}

Thus, to maximize likelihood, we minimize,

\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}-\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}(\log(p)-\log(1-p))

Which is equivalent to minimizing,

\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}\underbrace{\frac{\log(1-p)-\log(p)}{\kappa}}_{\tau\text{ as defined}}

Again, the sign of
\tau
is dictated by the sign of
\log(1-p)-\log(p)
, which is positive when
1-p>p
(i.e.
p<1/2
), negative when
1-p<p
(i.e.
p>1/2
) and zero when
p=1/2
. When this is the case, the
\ell_{0}
penalty term is actually negative. Thus, we benefit from insisting that
\boldsymbol{f}(a)\in S^{c}
, since that increases the conditional likelihood of
\boldsymbol{g}
and allows us to vary
\boldsymbol{f}
over the smoothness term to optimality.

We stated an optimization for the Bernoulli model in terms of the incidence matrix. We make that formal now. First, we do assume the known property that
\boldsymbol{L}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta,\zeta)&\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta,\zeta^{c})\\
\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta^{c},\zeta)&\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta^{c},\zeta^{c})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{L}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}(E,\zeta)^{\top}\\

\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta^{c})\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta,\zeta)\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)+2\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta,\zeta^{c})\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta^{c})+\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta^{c})\boldsymbol{L}(\zeta^{c},\zeta^{c})\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta^{c})

\big\|\boldsymbol{B}(E,\zeta)\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{B}(E,\zeta^{c})\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta^{c})\big\|_{2}^{2}+\tau\|\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)-\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)\|_{0}

Among all

-E

The Update Rule of the CCP

The algorithm of the CCP is to approximate a function of the form convex + concave by taking the first-order Taylor expansion of the concave portion; as the sum of a convex function and a linear function is convex, so is the new problem. In this case, we write,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f})

\displaystyle=\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\sum_{a\in V}\log|\boldsymbol{f}(x)|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{vex}(\boldsymbol{f})+\mathcal{L}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f})

Note that,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{f}(a)}\mathcal{L}_{cave}=\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{f}(a)|}

Thus, a Taylor expansion of
\mathcal{L}_{cave}
about some center
\boldsymbol{f}^{t}
is,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f};\boldsymbol{f}^{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f}^{t})+(\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f}^{t})^{\top}\nabla\mathcal{L}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f}^{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f}^{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{|\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a)|}(\boldsymbol{f}(a)-\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}(a)}{|\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a)|}+\text{constant}

Thus, the function we try to minimize at each step of the iteration is,

\mathcal{L}_{vex}(\boldsymbol{f})+\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{cave}(\boldsymbol{f};\boldsymbol{f}^{t})=\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}(a)}{|\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a)|}+\text{constant}

Of course, it suffices to ignore the constant terms for the purpose of optimization. Thus, the iteration is as claimed,

\boldsymbol{f}^{t+1}\in\arg\min_{\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}}\kappa\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\boldsymbol{L}\boldsymbol{f}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\boldsymbol{f}(a)}{|\boldsymbol{f}^{t}(a)|}

(4)

Because the CCP is generally a descent algorithm, this special case is a descent algorithm as well. It remains to explain why this is a Quadratic Program. Of course, the loss function is quadratic in
\boldsymbol{f}
, so it remains to discuss the feasible region. One might recall that the feasible region is
\Omega_{\boldsymbol{g}}=\{\boldsymbol{f}:0\leq\boldsymbol{g}(a)\leq\boldsymbol{f}(a)\text{ or }\boldsymbol{f}(a)\leq\boldsymbol{g}(a)\leq 0\}
. This is a rectangular set and thus falls within the framework of QPs.

Finally, we give some discussion to when
\boldsymbol{g}(a)=0
exactly. This is a probability zero event according to our statistical model, and so there is some expectation that it doesn’t occur. Alternatively, we can simply insist that
\boldsymbol{f}(a)=0
and optimize over the remaining terms.

Appendix A
Additional Experiments

We validate the use of the CCP by a brief experimental comparison to projected gradient descent. To evaluate these models, we will run these algorithms on an artificial example using an image. First, we regard each color channel as a signal on a 50
\times
50 grid graph. Then, we artificially generate the uniform noise, independently for each pixel, to create our signal
\boldsymbol{g}
. We then run, for each channel, the projected gradient algorithm as well as the Convex-Concave Procedure. For the initialization, we use a bit of chemistry: although the observed signal
\boldsymbol{g}
is of course feasible, we perturb it each coordinate by a small amount so that
\boldsymbol{f}^{0}
is strictly feasible (heuristically, this seems to eliminate spotting in the output). For each algorithm, we use a stopping criteria that the error change by no more than
10^{-7}\times\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{f}^{0})
(we multiply by the initial loss to provide scale). Finally, a learning rate of
\gamma=1
is chosen for projected gradient.

Notice that this requires knowing the ground truth signal, so this is questionable in practice. But for the purposes of experimentation, since that estimation is not the focus, this will do. Both algorithms were run in python in the Yale Zoo. In order to solve the QP
4
, the package CVXOPT is used.

Comparison

We compare the two algorithms in terms of runtime (both in terms of number of steps and total time), rate of decay, and accuracy. For all three channels, the CCP converged within 10 iterations (impressive)! Each iteration took 1.63 seconds on average to complete. In contrast, projected gradient descent took 1950, 1425, and 985 iterations to meet the same convergence criteria. While the iterations for the CCP are more involved than those for gradient descent, the CCP is still faster overall. We find that the CCP took 15.6, 15.5, and 17.6 seconds to converge, while projected gradient required 75.9, 54.5, and 37.5 seconds.

What is much more interesting is the rate of decay. From figure

\mathcal{L}

Of course, the secondary question remains: how do the algorithms compare in accuracy? Here, we have two notions: final value of the objective function, and actual closeness to the ground truth signal. In all channels, the case is the same. Both algorithms pass the most natural benchmark: they are able to achieve a lower loss value than the ground truth.
