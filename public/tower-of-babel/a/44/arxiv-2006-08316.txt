Abstract
. We develop an algebraic language theory based on the notion of an Eilenberg–Moore algebra. In comparison to previous such frameworks the main contribution is the support for algebras with infinitely many sorts and the connection to logic in form of so-called ‘definable algebras’.

1.
Introduction

There are various approaches to formal language theory, each having
its own strengths and weaknesses.
We are here interested in the algebraic approach – in particular,
in its use in characterising subclasses of regular languages,
like the class of first-order definable languages.

The initial algebraic theory was developed for languages of finite words.
It has subsequently been generalised, first to infinite words (see, e.g.,
[
PP04
]
)
and then to finite trees (e.g.,
[
BW07
]
).
More recently, also a framework for dealing with infinite trees was developed

[
BI09
,
Blu11
,
BIS13
,
Blu13
,
Blu20
,
Blua
]
.
Each of these four theories comes in several different variants, depending on
which notion of a language or a logic they were designed for.
As usual when such a wealth of slightly different settings has been developed,
people have started to consolidate and unify them.
One well-known proposal of this kind, based on the formalism of Eilenberg–Moore algebras,
was put forward by Bojańczyk
[
Boj
]
.
Later on it was generalised by Salamanca
[
Sal
]
and by
Adámek, Chen, Milius and Urbat
[
UACM17
,
MU19
]
.
The present paper is built on these works.

The motivation for such generalisation initiatives originates in several places.
First of all, setting up a new algebraic framework for language theory
entails a lot of grunt work and usually results in papers that are quite long
and in large parts not very deep.
Having most of the common parts extracted into a general framework reduces
a lot of this work and allows one to focus on the parts that contain the ideas which are new.

Apart from potentially saving a lot of work, such a program can also lead to novel insights.
When proving a general result one is usually forced to isolate
the key properties and notions that are required for the proof
(such as
denseness
and
\bbM
-compositionality,
which we will introduce below).
This in turn provides insight into
how far the methods used can be extended and where their limits are.

Finally, in a concrete case there are often several possible variations
of the definitions that more-or-less work equally well.
Knowing which of them generalises helps one to evaluate their respective merits.

Besides hopefully improving upon the presentation,
the main contributions of the present article lie in two areas.
Firstly, we present the first framework that does support algebras with
infinitely many sorts, which is required when one wants to cover the
concrete frameworks that have been introduced for languages of infinite trees.
While it turns out that many results and proofs go through for infinitely many sorts with
nearly no changes, there are also a few places below where we are forced to make
substantial adjustments.
In particular, we introduce the notion of a
dense
morphism of monads
in Section
4
to prove the existence of syntactic algebras,
and we have to modify the definition of a pseudo-variety in Section
5

by adding closure under so-called
sort-accumulation points.

Secondly, the existing frameworks concentrate on the algebraic and
language-theoretic side of things, while mostly ignoring the connections to logic.
This is rather unfortunate, as logic is one of the main application areas
for algebraic language theory. We will therefore devote a substantial part
of the article to the connection between the algebraic theory and the study of logics.

The overview of the article is as follows.
In Section
2
we set up the basic toolkit of monads and
Eilenberg–Moore algebras which our algebraic framework is based on.
Our preparations continue in Section
3
with the development of
a theory of quotients and congruences for such algebras.

Our algebraic framework is set up in Sections
4
–
7
.
The central notions of a syntactic congruence and a syntactic algebra are introduced
in Section
4
.
Equipped with these tools, we study pseudo-varieties in Section
5

and derive our version of the Variety Theorem.
Section
6
contains technical material on profinitary monads,
which is needed to prove a version of Reiterman’s Theorem in Section
7
.

The second part of the article consists of Sections
8
–
10
.
We start by collecting a few basic notions from logic in Section
8
.
Section
9
contains the connection to language theory
in terms of algebras whose products are definable in a certain sense.
Finally in Section
10
,
we show how one can apply our framework to study monadic second-order logic
and first-order logic over infinite trees.

2.
Monads and algebras

We assume that the reader is reasonably familiar with basic notions of category theory.
But in order to make the article more accessible to readers from other fields,
we have tried not to rely on any concepts that are not covered by the usual introductory
text books.
As a consequence we will explicitly define any of the more specialised notions needed below
– such as that of a monad or a copresentable object.

Let me also make a philosophical remark.
In this article I have tried to strike a balance between the level of generality
of the framework and the technical overhead entailed by it.
For this reason, many of the results below will not be stated in the most general form possible.
Instead, I have adopted a level of generality that covers (most of) the intended applications
while not obscuring the proofs by pointless technicalities.
In particular, the framework below is not presented in a purely category-theoretical language,
but in a mixture of set theory and category theory.

In formal language theory one studies sets of labelled objects like words, trees, traces,
pictures, (hyper-)graphs, and so on.
To capture all these various settings we start by introducing
an operation
\bbM
mapping a given set
A
of labels to the set
\bbM A

of all
A
-labelled objects. A language in this context is then simply a subset
K\subseteq\bbM A
.
For instance, for languages of finite words we can define
\bbM A:=A^{+}
.
To accommodate more complicated settings like trees, it will be convenient to work
not with plain sets but with
many-sorted
ones.
For a given set
\Xi
of
sorts,
an
\Xi
-sorted set
is a family

A=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Xi}
of plain sets.
Then
\bbM
maps a
\Xi
-sorted set
A
of
labels
to a
\Xi
-sorted set

\bbM A=(\bbM_{\xi}A)_{\xi\in\Xi}
of
A
-labelled objects.

For instance, when working with infinite words it is convenient to use two sorts

\Xi=\{1,\infty\}
where sort
1
represents the ‘finite’ elements and sort
\infty

the ‘infinite’ ones. The operation
\bbM
maps
A=\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle
to

\bbM A=\langle\bbM_{1}A,\bbM_{\infty}A\rangle
where

\displaystyle\bbM_{1}A:=A_{1}^{+}\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM_{\infty}A:=A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\,.

(So a finite word is a finite sequence of finite elements, while an infinite word
can either be a finite sequence of finite elements followed by a single infinite element,
or an infinite sequence of finite elements.)
Our intended applications consist in deriving characterisation results for various logics.
To be able to handle logics that are not closed under negation, it will turn out to be
necessary to be slightly more general and consider
ordered many-sorted sets,

that is,
\Xi
-sorted sets
A=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Xi}
where each sort
A_{\xi}

is equipped with a partial order. Such sets form a category
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
if we take
as morphisms the
order-preserving
\Xi
-sorted functions,
that is,
a morphism
f:A\to B
consists of a family
f=(f_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Xi}
of functions
where each component
f_{\xi}:A_{\xi}\to B_{\xi}
is order-preserving.
We will frequently identify a sorted set
A=(A_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Xi}
with its
disjoint union
A=\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\displaystyle\mkern 1.0mu\cdot\hfil$\crcr$\displaystyle\bigcup$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\textstyle\mkern 1.0mu\cdot\hfil$\crcr$\textstyle\bigcup$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\scriptstyle\mkern 1.0mu\cdot\hfil$\crcr$\scriptstyle\bigcup$\crcr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\hfil\scriptscriptstyle\mkern 1.0mu\cdot\hfil$\crcr$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcup$\crcr}}}}_{\xi\in\Xi}A_{\xi}
.
Using this point of view, a morphism
f:A\to B
corresponds to a sort-preserving
and order-preserving function between the corresponding disjoint unions.

Before continuing let us introduce a bit of terminology.
From this point on, we will use the terms ‘set’ and ‘function’ as a short-hand for
’ordered
\Xi
-sorted set’ and ‘order-preserving
\Xi
-sorted function’.
If we mean any other kind of set or function, we will mention this explicitly.
We call a set
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

unordered
if its ordering is trivial,
i.e., any two distinct elements are incomparable.
For a property
P
, we say that
A
is
sort-wise
P
if each set
A_{\xi}
has property
P
.
In particular,
sort-wise finite
means that every
A_{\xi}
is finite.

Of course, the operation
\bbM
alone does not provide sufficient structure to build
a meaningful theory. Usually, the objects in a formal language are subject to
various composition operations, like concatenation of words, substitution for terms, etc..
To capture such operations we will employ the category-theoretical notion of a
monad.

Note that, in the cases of interest where
\bbM A
is a set of
A
-labelled objects
of some kind, every function
f:A\to B
induces an operation
\bbM f:\bbM A\to\bbM B

which applies the function
f
to each label.
This turns
\bbM
into a functor
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.

There are two other ingredients we will need.
Firstly, the concatenation operation in question is often of the form

\mathrm{flat}:\bbM\bbM A\to\bbM A
, that is, it takes an
\bbM A
-labelled object
s\in\bbM\bbM A

and assembles the appearing labels into a single large object.
We call
\mathrm{flat}(s)
the
flattening
of
s
.
Secondly, there is usually a
singleton operation

\mathrm{sing}:A\to\bbM A
that takes a label

a\in A
and produces an object with a single position which is labelled by
a
.
For instance, in the case of words
\mathrm{flat}:(A^{+})^{+}\to A^{+}
is simply the concatenation
operation and
\mathrm{sing}:A\to A^{+}
produces
1
-letter words.

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}(\langle w_{0},\dots,w_{n}\rangle)

\displaystyle:=w_{0}\dots w_{n}\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }w_{0},\dots,w_{n}\in A^{+},

\displaystyle\mathrm{sing}(a)

\displaystyle:=\langle a\rangle\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for }a\in A\,.

Usually, the flattening operation is associative, which makes the functor
\bbM
into a
monad.

{defi}

A
monad
consists of a functor
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
that is equipped with
two natural transformations
\mathrm{flat}:\bbM\circ\bbM\Rightarrow\bbM
and

\mathrm{sing}:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbM

(where
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity functor) satisfying the following equations.

\displaystyle\mathrm{flat}\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{id}\,,\qquad\mathrm{flat}\circ\bbM\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{id}\,,\qquad\mathrm{flat}\circ\mathrm{flat}=\mathrm{flat}\circ\bbM\mathrm{flat}\,.

\lrcorner

In algebraic language theory one equips the sets
\bbM A
with an algebraic structure
of some kind and then uses homomorphisms
\bbM A\to\frakB
into some other algebra
\frakB

to describe languages
K\subseteq\bbM A
.
If
\bbM
is a monad, there is a canonical way to define this algebraic structure:
we can equip a set
A
with a
product
operation of the form
\pi:\bbM A\to A
.
For instance, for words this product takes the form
\pi:A^{+}\to A
,
i.e., it multiplies a sequence of elements into a single element.
Hence,
\pi
can be seen as a semigroup product of variable arity.
But note that not every operation
\pi:A^{+}\to A
is of the form

\displaystyle\pi(\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{m}\rangle)=a_{0}\cdot a_{1}\cdot\cdots\cdot a_{m}

for some semigroup product
{}\cdot{}:A\times A\to A
.
If we want to exactly capture the notion of a semigroup,
we have to impose additional conditions on
\pi
.
It turns out, there are two such conditions:
associativity requires that

\displaystyle\pi(\pi(w_{0}),\dots,\pi(w_{m}))=\pi(w_{0}\dots w_{m})\,,\quad\text{for all }w_{0},\dots,w_{m}\in A^{+},

and the fact that the product of a single element should return that element again
requires that

\displaystyle\pi(\langle a\rangle)=a\,,\quad\text{for }a\in A\,.

These two conditions can be phrased more concisely as

\displaystyle\pi\circ\bbM\pi=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{id}\,.

This leads us to the following definition.

{defi}

Let
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
be a monad.

(a)
An
Eilenberg-Moore algebra
for
\bbM
, or
\bbM
-algebra
for short,
is a pair
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle
consisting of a set
A
and a function

\pi:\bbM A\to A
satisfying

\displaystyle\pi\circ\bbM\pi

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}\,,

\displaystyle\pi\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{id}\,.

The first of these equations is called the
associative law
for
\pi
,
the second one the
unit law.

(b) A
morphism

\varphi:\frakA\to\frakB
of
\bbM
-algebras is a function

\varphi:A\to B
commuting with the respective products in the sense that

\displaystyle\varphi\circ\pi=\pi\circ\bbM\varphi\,.

(c) We denote the category of all
\bbM
-algebras and their morphisms by
\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM)
.

(d) An algebra
\frakA
is
finitary
if its universe
A
is sort-wise finite
and
\frakA
is finitely generated, i.e., there exists a finite set
C\subseteq A

such that every element
a\in A
can be written as
a=\pi(s)
, for some
s\in\bbM C
.

\lrcorner

As a further example, let us take a look at the functor

\displaystyle\bbM\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle:=\langle A_{1}^{+},\ A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\rangle

for infinite words.
In this case an
\bbM
-algebra has two product functions

\displaystyle\pi_{1}:A_{1}^{+}\to A_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{\infty}:A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\to A_{\infty}\,.

The laws of an
\bbM
-algebra ensure that
\pi_{1}
corresponds to a semigroup product

A_{1}\times A_{1}\to A_{1}
and
\pi_{\infty}
correspond to the additional products

A_{1}\times A_{\infty}\to A_{\infty}
and
A_{1}^{\omega}\to A_{\infty}
of an
\omega
-semigroup.
Hence, in this case
\bbM
-algebras are nothing but
\omega
-semigroups.

There is a natural way to turn a set of the form
\bbM A
into an
\bbM
-algebra:
we can chose the function
\mathrm{flat}:\bbM\bbM A\to\bbM A
as the product.
It turns out that algebras of this form are exactly the
free algebras.

Proposition 1
.

For each ranked set
A
, there exists a free
\bbM
-algebra over
A
.
It has the form
\langle\bbM A,\mathrm{flat}\rangle
.

Proof 2.1
.

The fact that
\mathrm{flat}:\bbM\bbM A\to\bbM A

is the free
\bbM
-algebra is a standard result in category theory.
As the functor
\bbM
is a monad, it is left adjoint to the
forgetful functor
\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM)\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
which maps a
\bbM
-algebra
\frakB

to its universe
B
(see, e.g., Proposition 4.1.4 of
[
Bor94a
]
).
Consequently, for every
\bbM
-algebra
\frakB
and every function
f:A\to B
,
there exists a unique morphism
\varphi:\bbM A\to\frakB
such that
\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}=f
.

In order to obtain non-trivial results we have to put some mild restrictions
on the kind of monad
\bbM
we consider.
In the applications we have in mind,
\bbM
is always a polynomial functor of the form

\displaystyle\bbM A=\sum_{i<\lambda}A^{D_{i}},

for some cardinal
\lambda
and unordered sets
D_{i}\in\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
. For instance,
for the word functor
\bbM A=A^{+}
, we can take
\lambda=\aleph_{0}

and
D_{i}=\{0,\dots,i\}
.
Similarly, if we consider languages of trees, we can fix an enumeration

(t_{i})_{i<\lambda}
of all unlabelled trees and choose for
D_{i}
the
set of vertices of
t_{i}
.

For the results in this article, we do not need to assume that
\bbM
is polynomial.
A few weaker properties suffice. To state these, we have to introduce a bit of terminology.

{defi}

Let
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
be a functor.

(a) The
lift
of a relation
R\subseteq A\times B
is the relation

R^{\bbM}\subseteq\bbM A\times\bbM B
consisting of all pairs
\langle s,t\rangle

such that

\displaystyle s=\bbM p(u)\quad\text{and}\quad t=\bbM q(u)\,,\quad\text{for some }u\in\bbM R\,,

where
p:A\times B\to A
and
q:A\times B\to B
are the two projections.

(b)
We say that
\bbM

uses the standard ordering
if the ordering of
\bbM A

is the lift
\leq^{\bbM}
of the ordering
\leq
of
A
.

(c)
We say that
\bbM

preserves injectivity/surjectivity/bijectivity
if
it is the case that
\bbM
maps injective/surjective/bijective functions to functions of the same kind.

(d)

\bbM

preserves preimages
if, for every function
f:A\to B

and every subset
P\subseteq B
, we have

\displaystyle\bbM(f^{-1}[P])=(\bbM f)^{-1}[\bbM P]\,.

\lrcorner

For instance, the standard ordering for the word functor
\bbM A=A^{+}
is

\displaystyle\langle a_{0},\dots,a_{m}\rangle\leq\langle b_{0},\dots,b_{n}\rangle\quad\@iff\quad m=n\text{ and }a_{i}\leq b_{i}\text{ for all }i\leq m\,.

For our framework we require the following properties of
\bbM
,
which are clearly shared by every polynomial functor.

Convention.

In the following we will always tacitly assume that
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

is a monad which preserves injectivity, surjectivity, bijectivity, and preimages, and
that it uses the standard ordering.

An example of a monad that does not fit into this framework would be the functor
\bbD

mapping a set
A
to its set of downward closed sets, ordered by inclusion.
The multiplication of this monad maps a set of sets to its union,
and the singleton function maps an element
a\in A
to the set

\{\,b\in Ab\leq a\,\}
.
For a function
f:A\to B
we set

\displaystyle\bbD f(X)=\{\,b\in Bb\leq f(a)\text{ for some }a\in X\,\}\,.

This monad preserves surjectivity, but neither injectivity, bijectivity, nor preimages,
and it does not use the standard ordering.

Let us derive a first consequence of our assumptions.
A frequent problem we will have to deal with is the fact that in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

not every surjective morphism has a right inverse.
Therefore, we will sometimes be forced to make a detour through the category
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}

by ignoring the order of the sets involved.
For simplicity, we will treat
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
as a full subcategory of
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}

via the following embedding.

{defi}

(a) Let
\bbV:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
be the functor mapping a set
A
with order
\leq

to the same set, but with the trivial order
=
, and let

\iota:\bbV\Rightarrow\mathrm{Id}
the natural transformation induced
by the identity maps.

(b) A functor
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
is
order agnostic
if
there exists a natural isomorphism
\delta:\bbM\circ\bbV\Rightarrow\bbV\circ\bbM

satisfying

\displaystyle\bbM\iota

\displaystyle=\iota\circ\delta

and

\displaystyle\penalty\bbV\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\delta\circ\mathrm{sing}\,.

\lrcorner

Intuitively, being order agnostic means that
\bbM
does not make essential use
of the ordering of a set
A
when producing
\bbM A
. The ordering of
A
has no
influence on which elements
\bbM A
contains, only on the ordering between them.

Lemma 2
.

If
\bbM
satisfies the above assumption, it is order agnostic.

Proof 2.2
.

We start by showing that each set of the form
\bbM\bbV A
has the trivial order.
Hence, suppose that
s,t\in\bbM\bbV A
with
s\leq t
.
As
\bbM
uses the standard ordering, we can find some
u\in\bbM\Delta

with
s=\bbM p(u)
and
t=\bbM q(u)
, where
\Delta\subseteq A\times A

is the ordering of
\bbV A
and
p,q:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.
Note that
\Delta=\{\,\langle a,a\rangle a\in A\,\}
is the diagonal.
Consequently, we have
p(d)=q(d)
, for all
d\in\Delta
, which implies that

s=\bbM p(u)=\bbM q(u)=t
.

It follows that
\bbV\bbM\bbV A=\bbM\bbV A
and the respective identity maps
provide morphisms
i:\bbM\bbV A\to\bbV\bbM\bbV A
and
j:\bbV A\to\bbV\bbV A

that are inverse to the functions
\iota:\bbV\bbM\bbV A\to\bbM\bbV A

and
\iota:\bbV\bbV A\to\bbV A
.
We claim that the morphism
\delta:=\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i:\bbM\bbV A\to\bbV\bbM A

is the desired natural transformation.
First, note that
\delta
is bijective, as
i
and
\iota
are bijective and
both
\bbV
and
\bbM
preserve bijectivity.
Since the domain
\bbM\bbV A
and the codomain
\bbV\bbM A
both use the trivial order,

\delta
therefore has an inverse.
To conclude the proof it is hence sufficient to show that the following diagram commutes.

Since
\bbV\iota=\iota
it follows that

\displaystyle\iota\circ\delta

\displaystyle=\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\iota\circ\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota\circ\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\iota\circ\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota\circ\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\iota\circ\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota\circ\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\iota\circ\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota\circ\iota\circ i=\bbM\iota$\hss}}

and

\displaystyle\penalty\delta\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i\circ\mathrm{sing}\circ\iota\circ j

\displaystyle=\bbV\bbM\iota\circ i\circ\iota\circ\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ j

\displaystyle=\bbV\bbM\iota\circ\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ j

\displaystyle=\bbV(\mathrm{sing}\circ\iota)\circ j

\displaystyle=\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ(\bbV\iota\circ j)=\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ(\iota\circ j)=\bbV\mathrm{sing}\,.

3.
Congruences and quotients

We start by developing a theory of congruences for
\bbM
-algebras.
Most of the arguments in this section are quite standard,
but we did not find them worked out for Eilenberg–Moore algebras
anywhere in the literature.
We begin by looking at quotients of ordered sets. Then we will turn to
\bbM
-algebras.

{defi}

Let
A
be an ordered set and
{\sqsubseteq}\subseteq A\times A
a preorder with

{\leq}\subseteq{\sqsubseteq}
.

(a) The
kernel
of a function
f:A\to B
is the relation

\displaystyle\ker f:=\{\,\langle a,a^{\prime}\rangle\in A\times Af(a)\leq f(a^{\prime})\,\}\,.

(b) For
a\in A
and
X\subseteq A
, we set

\displaystyle\mathord{\Uparrow}a:=\{\,b\in Ab\geq a\,\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathord{\Uparrow}X:=\bigcup_{a\in X}\mathord{\Uparrow}a\,.

(c) The set of
\sqsubseteq
-classes
is

\displaystyle A/{\sqsubseteq}:=\{\,[a]_{\sqsubseteq}a\in A\,\}\quad\text{where}\quad[a]_{\sqsubseteq}:=\{\,b\in Ab\sqsubseteq a\text{ and }a\sqsubseteq b\,\}\,.

We equip it with the ordering

\displaystyle[a]_{\sqsubseteq}\leq[b]_{\sqsubseteq}\quad\@defiff\quad a\sqsubseteq b\,.

(d) The
quotient map

q:A\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
maps
a\in A
to
[a]_{\sqsubseteq}
.

(e) We say that
\sqsubseteq
has
finitary index
if
the quotient
A/{\sqsubseteq}
is sort-wise finite.

\lrcorner

A very useful tool to construct quotients is the following lemma from universal algebra.

Lemma 3

(Factorisation Lemma)
.

Let
f:A\to B
and
g:A\to C
be functions and assume that
f
is surjective.
Then
g=h\circ f
, for some
h:B\to C
, if and only if

\displaystyle\ker f\subseteq\ker g\,.

Moreover, the function
h
is unique, if it exists.

Proof 3.1
.

The uniqueness of
h
follows from the surjectivity of
f
,
since surjective functions are epimorphisms:

h\circ f=g=h^{\prime}\circ f
implies
h=h^{\prime}
.
Hence, it remains to consider existence.

(\Rightarrow)
If
g=h\circ f
, then

\displaystyle f(a)\leq f(b)\quad\text{implies}\quad g(a)=h(f(a))\leq h(f(b))=g(b)\,.

(\Leftarrow)

Suppose that
\ker f\subseteq\ker g
.
As
f
is surjective, it has a right inverse
r
(in
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
,
r
might not be monotone).
We claim that
h:=g\circ r
is the desired function.

For monotonicity, suppose that
a\leq b
in
B
.
Then

\displaystyle f(r(a))=a\leq b=f(r(b))\quad\text{implies}\quad\langle r(a),r(b)\rangle\in\ker f\subseteq\ker g\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle h(a)=g(r(a))\leq g(r(b))=h(b)\,.

To show that
g=h\circ f
, set
e:=r\circ f
.
For
a\in A
, it follows that

\displaystyle f(e(a))=(f\circ r\circ f)(a)=f(a)\,.

Hence,
\langle a,e(a)\rangle,\langle e(a),a\rangle\in\ker f\subseteq\ker g
,
which implies that
g(a)=g(e(a))
. Thus

g=g\circ e=g\circ r\circ f=h\circ f
.

In order to lift the statement of the Factorisation Lemma from functions to morphisms,
it is sufficient to prove that, if
f
and
g
are morphisms of
\bbM
-algebras, so is
h
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f:\frakA\to\frakB
and
g:\frakA\to\frakC
be morphisms of
\bbM
-algebras
and
h:B\to C
a function such that
g=h\circ f
.
If
f
is surjective, then
h
is also a morphism of
\bbM
-algebras.

Proof 3.2
.

Note that

\displaystyle h\circ\pi\circ\bbM f=h\circ f\circ\pi=g\circ\pi=\pi\circ\bbM g=\pi\circ\bbM h\circ\bbM f\,.

Since
f
is surjective, so is
\bbM f
.
Therefore, the above equation implies that
h\circ\pi=\pi\circ\bbM h
,
i.e., that
h
is a morphism of
\bbM
-algebras.

In
\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
there also exists a dual to the statement of the Factorisation Lemma,
but in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
this version only holds in special cases as, in general,
surjective functions do not have right inverses.
Let us record the following version for algebras of the form
\bbM X
where
X
is unordered.
In category-theoretical terminology
it states that such algebras are
projective
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\varphi:\bbM X\to\frakB
and
\psi:\frakA\to\frakB
be morphisms of
\bbM
-algebras
where
X
is an unordered set.
If
\psi
is surjective, there exists some morphism
\hat{\varphi}:\bbM X\to\frakA

such that
\varphi=\psi\circ\hat{\varphi}
.

Proof 3.3
.

If
\psi
is surjective, we can pick, for every
x\in X
some element

f(x)\in\psi^{-1}(\varphi(\mathrm{sing}(x)))
.
This defines a function
f:X\to A
with
\psi\circ f=\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}

(which is trivially monotone as
X
is unordered).
As
\bbM X
is freely generated by the range of
\mathrm{sing}
,
we can extend
f
to a unique morphism
\hat{\varphi}:\bbM X\to\frakA
with

\hat{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{sing}=f
. It follows that

\displaystyle\psi\circ\hat{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{sing}=\psi\circ f=\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}\,.

As the range of
\mathrm{sing}
generates
\bbM X
, this implies that
\psi\circ\hat{\varphi}=\varphi
.

Next, let us define quotients for algebras instead of sets.

{defi}

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra and
{\sqsubseteq}\subseteq A\times A

a preorder with
{\leq}\subseteq{\sqsubseteq}
.

(a) For
s,t\in\bbM A
, we set

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t\quad\@defiff\quad\bbM q(s)\leq\bbM q(t)\,,

where
q:A\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
is the quotient map.

(b) Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra.
The preorder
\sqsubseteq
is a
congruence ordering
on
\frakA
if

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t\quad\text{implies}\quad\pi(s)\sqsubseteq\pi(t)\,.

(c)
If
\sqsubseteq
is a congruence ordering on
\frakA
,
we define the
quotient

\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
as the algebra with universe
A/{\sqsubseteq}

where the product
\pi:\bbM(A/{\sqsubseteq})\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
is the unique function such that

\displaystyle\pi\circ\bbM q=q\circ\pi\,,

where
q:A\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
denotes the quotient map.

\lrcorner

Remark
.

It is straightforward to show that
{\sqsubseteq^{\bbM}}\subseteq{\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}}
.
For most monads
\bbM
, these two relations are actually equal,
but our assumptions on
\bbM
are not quite strong enough to prove this in general.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\sqsubseteq
be a congruence ordering on an
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA
.
The quotient
\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
is a well-defined
\bbM
-algebra and
the quotient map
q:\frakA\to\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
is a morphism of
\bbM
-algebras.

Proof 3.4
.

We have to check several properties.

(a) To see that
q
is monotone, note that

\displaystyle a\leq b\quad\Rightarrow\quad a\sqsubseteq b\quad\Rightarrow\quad q(a)\leq q(b)\,.

(b) To show that the product of
\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
is well-defined and monotone
we apply the Factorisation Lemma. Note that, for
s,t\in\bbM A
,

\displaystyle\bbM q(s)\leq\bbM q(t)\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t\quad\Rightarrow\quad\pi(s)\sqsubseteq\pi(t)\quad\Rightarrow\quad q(\pi(s))\leq q(\pi(t))\,,

where the second step follows from the fact that
\sqsubseteq
is a congruence ordering.
Thus,
\ker\bbM q\subseteq\ker{(q\circ\pi)}
and
the Factorisation Lemma implies that there is a unique function

\pi:\bbM(A/{\sqsubseteq})\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
with

\pi\circ\bbM q=q\circ\pi
.

(c) It remains to check the two axioms of an
\bbM
-algebra.
For the unit law, note that
\mathrm{sing}:\mathrm{Id}\Rightarrow\bbM
is a natural transformation.
Hence,

\displaystyle\pi\circ\mathrm{sing}\circ q=\pi\circ\bbM q\circ\mathrm{sing}=q\circ\pi\circ\mathrm{sing}=q\,.

As
q
is surjective, this implies that
\pi\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{id}
.
For the associative law, note that

\displaystyle\pi\circ\bbM\pi\circ\bbM\bbM q

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\bbM q\circ\bbM\pi

\displaystyle=q\circ\pi\circ\bbM\pi

\displaystyle=q\circ\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\bbM q\circ\mathrm{flat}=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}\circ\bbM\bbM q\,.

Hence, surjectivity of
\bbM\bbM q
implies that
\pi\circ\bbM\pi=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}
.

As usual, we have defined our notion of a congruence such that congruences correspond to
kernels of morphisms.
We will establish this correspondence in Proposition
8

below. But before doing so, let us take a closer look at the auxiliary relation
\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\sqsubseteq
be a preorder on
A
with
{\leq}\subseteq{\sqsubseteq}
. Then

\displaystyle\bbM{\sqsubseteq}=\langle\bbM p_{0},\bbM p_{1}\rangle^{-1}[{\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}}]\,,

where
p_{0},p_{1}:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.

Proof 3.5
.

Let
p^{\prime}_{0},p^{\prime}_{1}:A/{\sqsubseteq}\times A/{\sqsubseteq}\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
be the two projections,

q:A\to A/{\sqsubseteq}
the quotient map, and
let
\leq
be the ordering on
A/{\sqsubseteq}
.
As
\bbM
uses the standard ordering it follows that
\bbM(A/{\sqsubseteq})

is ordered by the lift
\leq^{\bbM}
.
We consider the following diagram

where the vertical arrows denote the respective inclusion maps.
Let us first explain why this diagram commutes.
Since in each square the vertical maps are inclusions and the top map is a
restriction of the bottom one,
it is sufficient to show that the maps in the upper trapezium
map the first of the given subsets to the second one.
That is, we have to show that

\displaystyle\bbM(q\times q)[\bbM{\sqsubseteq}]

\displaystyle\subseteq\bbM{\leq}\,,

\displaystyle\langle\bbM p^{\prime}_{0},\bbM p^{\prime}_{1}\rangle[\bbM{\leq}]

\displaystyle\subseteq{\leq}^{\bbM},

\displaystyle(\bbM q\times\bbM q)[{\sqsubseteq}_{\bbM}]

\displaystyle\subseteq{\leq}^{\bbM}.

The first inclusion follows from the fact that
q\times q
maps
\sqsubseteq
to
\leq

by simply applying the functor
\bbM
;
the second one holds by definition of the lift
\leq^{\bbM}
; and
the last inclusion follows immediately from the definition of
\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}
.

To conclude the proof, note that we have even the stronger statements

\displaystyle\bbM{\sqsubseteq}

\displaystyle=\bbM(q\times q)^{-1}[\bbM{\leq}]\,,

\displaystyle\bbM{\leq}

\displaystyle=\langle\bbM p^{\prime}_{0},\bbM p^{\prime}_{1}\rangle^{-1}[{\leq}^{\bbM}]\,,

\displaystyle{\sqsubseteq}_{\bbM}

\displaystyle=(\bbM q\times\bbM q)^{-1}[{\leq}^{\bbM}]\,.

The first equation follows from the fact that
\bbM
preserves preimages,
while the second one holds by definition of the lift
\leq^{\bbM}
.
To check the third equation, suppose that
\bbM q(s)\leq^{\bbM}\bbM q(t)
.
Considering the sets

\displaystyle X:=\{\,u\in\bbM(A/{\sqsubseteq})\bbM q(s)\leq^{\bbM}u\,\}\quad\text{and}\quad Y:=\{\,v\in\bbM As\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}v\,\}\,,

preservation of preimages implies that
Y=(\bbM q)^{-1}[X]
.
In particular,
t\in Y
and
s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t
.

It now follows that

\displaystyle\bbM{\sqsubseteq}

\displaystyle=\bbM(q\times q)^{-1}\bigl[\langle\bbM p^{\prime}_{0},\bbM p^{\prime}_{1}\rangle^{-1}[{\leq}^{\bbM}]\bigr]

\displaystyle=\langle\bbM p_{0},\bbM p_{1}\rangle^{-1}\bigl[(\bbM q\times\bbM q)^{-1}[{\leq}^{\bbM}]\bigr]=\langle\bbM p_{0},\bbM p_{1}\rangle^{-1}[{\sqsubseteq}_{\bbM}]\,.

We obtain the following characterisation of congruence orderings.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra and
{\sqsubseteq}\subseteq A\times A
a preorder that
contains the ordering of
A
. Let
p_{0},p_{1}:A\times A\to A
be the two projections.
The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

\sqsubseteq
is a congruence ordering on
\frakA
.

(2)

{\sqsubseteq}=\ker\varphi
, for some morphism
\varphi:\frakA\to\frakB
.

(3)

u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
implies
\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))
.

(4)

\sqsubseteq
induces a subalgebra of
\frakA\times\frakA
.

Proof 3.6
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2)
The quotient map
q:\frakA\to\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
has kernel
\sqsubseteq
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)
Clearly,
a\leq b
implies
\varphi(a)\leq\varphi(b)
.
Thus,
{\leq}\subseteq{\sqsubseteq}
.
For the other condition, consider two elements
s,t\in\bbM A
with
s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t
.
By definition, this means that
\bbM q(s)\leq\bbM q(t)
where
q:\frakA\to\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}

is the quotient map.
As
q
is surjective, we can use the Factorisation Lemma to find a function

f:A/{\sqsubseteq}\to B
with
\varphi=f\circ q
.
By monotonicity of
f
and
\pi
, it follows that

\displaystyle\varphi(\pi(s))=\pi(\bbM\varphi(s))

\displaystyle=\pi(\bbM f(\bbM q(s)))

\displaystyle\leq\pi(\bbM f(\bbM q(t)))=\pi(\bbM\varphi(t))=\varphi(\pi(t))\,.

Consequently,
\langle\pi(s),\pi(t)\rangle\in\ker\varphi={\sqsubseteq}
.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Let
u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
. Then

\displaystyle\bigl\langle\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u)),\ \pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle p_{0}(\pi(u)),\ p_{1}(\pi(u))\bigr\rangle=\pi(u)\in{\sqsubseteq}\,.

Hence,
\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(4)
Let
u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
. Then
\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))
implies that

\displaystyle\pi(u)=\bigl\langle p_{0}(\pi(u)),\ p_{1}(\pi(u))\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u)),\ \pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))\bigr\rangle\in{\sqsubseteq}\,.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1) To show that
{\sqsubseteq}
is a congruence ordering,
suppose that
s\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}t
.
By Lemma
7
, there is some

u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
with
s=\bbM p_{0}(u)
and
t=\bbM p_{1}(u)
.
Hence it follows by (3) that

\displaystyle\pi(s)=\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))=\pi(t)\,.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Given
u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
,
Lemma
7
implies that

\bbM p_{0}(u)\sqsubseteq_{\bbM}\bbM p_{1}(u)
.
Hence,
\bbM(q\circ p_{0})(u)\leq\bbM(q\circ p_{1})(u)
,
where
q:\frakA\to\frakA/{\sqsubseteq}
is the quotient map.
As the product
\pi
is monotone, it follows that

\displaystyle\pi(\bbM(q\circ p_{0})(u))\leq\pi(\bbM(q\circ p_{1})(u))\,.

Hence,

\displaystyle q(\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u)))=\pi(\bbM(q\circ p_{0})(u))\leq\pi(\bbM(q\circ p_{1})(u))=q(\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u)))\,,

which implies that
\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))
.

4.
Languages and syntactic algebras

Our main point of interest is to determine which
sets
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
are definable in a given logic.
We start by collecting the needed notions from language theory.

{defi}

(a) An
alphabet
is a finite
unordered
set
\Sigma\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.
We denote by
\mathsf{Alph}
the category of all alphabets with functions
as morphisms.

(b) A
language
over the alphabet
\Sigma
is a subset

K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
, for some sort
\xi
.

(c)
A
family of languages
is a function
\calK
mapping each alphabet
\Sigma

to a class
\calK[\Sigma]
of languages over
\Sigma
.

(d)
A function
f:\bbM\Sigma\to A

recognises

a language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
if

K=f^{-1}[P]
, for some
upwards closed
set
P\subseteq A_{\xi}
.

(e)
Let
f:\Sigma\to\Gamma
be a morphism of
\mathsf{Alph}
.
We call a morphism of the form
\bbM f:\bbM\Sigma\to\bbM\Gamma
a
relabelling

and, for a language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Gamma
, we call the set

\displaystyle(\bbM f)^{-1}[K]:=\{\,s\in\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma\bbM f(s)\in K\,\}

an
inverse relabelling
of
K
.

\lrcorner

Note that we always assume alphabets to be unordered.
This is required for the variety theorem in the next section.
But sometimes it is useful to also work with languages over ordered alphabets.
We do so by simply forgetting the order.
This leads to the following extension of the notion of a family of languages.

{defi}

Let
\calK
be a family of languages. For a finite
ordered
set
C
, we define

\displaystyle\calK[C]:=\bigl\{\,\bbM\iota[K]\bigm K\in\calK[\bbV C]\,\bigr\}\,,

where
\bbV
and
\iota
are the operations from Definition
2
.

\lrcorner

One of our main tools will be the following relation associated with a language.

{defi}

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra.

(a) A
context
is an element of
\bbM(A+\Box)
,
where
\Box
is considered as some special symbol of an arbitrary, but fixed sort
\zeta
.
For a context
p\in\bbM_{\xi}(A+\Box)
and an element
a\in A_{\zeta}
, we define

\displaystyle p[a]:=\sigma_{a}(p)\in A_{\xi}

where
\sigma_{a}:\bbM(A+\Box)\to\frakA
is the unique morphism that extends
the function
s_{a}:A+\Box\to A
given by

\displaystyle s_{a}(\Box):=a\quad\text{and}\quad s_{a}(c):=c\,,\quad\text{for }c\in A\,.

In the case where
\frakA=\bbM\Sigma
is a free
\bbM
-algebra,
we will also consider elements
p\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)
as contexts,
by identifying them with their image under
\bbM(\mathrm{sing}+1)

(the function
\mathrm{sing}+1
maps
a\in A
to
\mathrm{sing}(a)
and
\Box
to
\Box
).

(b) The
composition
of two contexts
p,q\in\bbM(A+\Box)
is the context

\displaystyle pq:=\hat{p}[q]\in\bbM(A+\Box)\,,

where
\hat{p}:=\bbM(\mathrm{sing}+1)(p)
and
\hat{p}[q]
is evaluated in the
\bbM
-algebra

\bbM(A+\Box)
.

(c) A
derivative
of a subset
K\subseteq A_{\xi}
is a set of the form

\displaystyle p^{-1}[K]:=\{\,a\in A_{\zeta}p[a]\in K\,\}\,,\quad\text{where }p\in\bbM_{\xi}(A+\Box)\text{ is a context.}

(d) The
syntactic congruence
of an upwards closed set
K\subseteq A_{\xi}

is the relation

\displaystyle a\preceq_{K}b\quad\@defiff\quad(p[a]\in K\Rightarrow p[b]\in K)\,,\quad\text{for all }p\in\bbM_{\xi}(A+\Box)\,,

for
a,b\in A
.

(e) We call the quotient

\mathrm{Syn}(K):=\frakA/{\preceq_{K}}
the
syntactic algebra
of
K
and the quotient map

\mathrm{syn}_{K}:\frakA\to\frakA/{\preceq_{K}}
the
syntactic morphism
of
K
.

(f) We say that a language
K

has a syntactic algebra
if

\preceq_{K}
is a congruence ordering with finitary index.

\lrcorner

Note that, in general, the syntactic congruence does not need to be a congruence ordering,
the syntactic algebra not an
\bbM
-algebra, and the syntactic morphism not a morphism of

\bbM
-algebras, but we will mainly be interested in the case where they are.
Hence the terminology.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra,
K\subseteq A_{\xi}
upwards closed,
a,b\in A
, and

p\in\bbM(A+\Box)
.

(a)

a\leq b\quad\text{implies}\quad p[a]\leq p[b]\,.

(b)

a\leq b\quad\text{implies}\quad a\preceq_{K}b\,.

(c)

a\preceq_{K}b\quad\text{implies}\quad p[a]\preceq_{K}p[b]\quad\text{and}\quad a\preceq_{p^{-1}[K]}b\,.

Proof 4.1
.

(a)
Let
g:A+\Box\to A\times A
be the function where

\displaystyle g(\Box):=\langle a,b\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad g(c)=\langle c,c\rangle\,,\quad\text{for }c\in A\,,

let
q,q^{\prime}:A\times A\to A
be the two projections, and set
u:=\bbM g(p)
.
Then
u\in\bbM{\leq}
(where
\leq
is the ordering of
A
) and

\displaystyle p[a]=\pi(\bbM q(u))\quad\text{and}\quad p[b]=\pi(\bbM q^{\prime}(u))\,.

Since
\bbM
uses the standard ordering, this implies that
p[a]\leq p[b]
.

(b) Suppose that
a\leq b
and let
p\in\bbM(A+\Box)
be a context.
By (a), we have
p[a]\leq p[b]
.
Consequently,
p[a]\in K
implies
p[b]\in K
.

(c)
Suppose that
a\preceq_{K}b
. To show that
p[a]\preceq_{K}p[b]
, consider
a context
q
with
q[p[a]]\in K
.
Then

a\preceq_{K}b

and

q[p[a]]=(qp)[a]\in K

implies that
q[p[b]]=(qp)[b]\in K
.

To show that
a\preceq_{p^{-1}[K]}b
, consider a context
q
with
q[a]\in p^{-1}[K]
.
Then

a\preceq_{K}b

and

(pq)[a]=p[q[a]]\in K

implies that
(pq)[b]=p[q[b]]\in K
. Thus
q[b]\in p^{-1}[K]
.

One consequence of this lemma is that
the quotient
A/{\preceq_{K}}
does exist at least as a set.

Corollary 10
.

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra and
K\subseteq A_{\xi}
upwards closed.
Then
\preceq_{K}
is a preorder with
{\leq}\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}
.

Proof 4.2
.

Reflexivity and transitivity of
\preceq_{K}
follow immediately from the definition.
The fact that
\preceq_{K}
contains
\leq
is part (a) of the preceding lemma.

Lemma 11
.

A morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
of
\bbM
-algebras recognises a
language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
if, and only if,
\ker\varphi\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}
.

Proof 4.3
.

(\Leftarrow)

We claim that
K=\varphi^{-1}[P]
where
P:=\mathord{\Uparrow}\varphi[K]
.
Clearly,
\varphi(t)\in P
, for all
t\in K
. Conversely,

\displaystyle\varphi(t)\in P

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varphi(s)\leq\varphi(t)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for some }s\in K\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\preceq_{K}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for some }s\in K\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad t\in K\,.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that
K=\varphi^{-1}[P]
, for an upwards closed set
P
,
and let
\varphi(s)\leq\varphi(t)
.
To show that
s\preceq_{K}t
, consider a context
p\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)

with
p[s]\in K
.
Set

\displaystyle\hat{p}:=\bbM(\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}+1)(p)\in\bbM(A+\Box)\,.

Then
\hat{p}[\varphi(s)]=\varphi(p[s])\in P
.
According to Lemma
9
(a),
we further have

\hat{p}[\varphi(s)]\leq\hat{p}[\varphi(t)]
.
Together, it follows that
\hat{p}[\varphi(t)]=\varphi(p[t])\in P
.
Hence,
p[t]\in K
.

A noteworthy consequence of this lemma is that the syntactic morphism of a language
K
is
the terminal object in the category of all morphisms recognising
K
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
be a language such that
\preceq_{K}
is a congruence ordering.
For every surjective morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
recognising
K
,
there exists a unique morphism
\varrho:\frakA\to\mathrm{Syn}(K)
such that

\mathrm{syn}_{K}=\varrho\circ\varphi
.

Proof 4.4
.

Suppose that

recognises
K
. By Lemma

we have

\displaystyle\ker\varphi\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}=\ker\mathrm{syn}_{K}\,.

Therefore, we can use the Factorisation Lemma to find a unique function

with

According to Lemma

, this function

is a morphism.

Let us take a look at what kind of languages are recognised by a syntactic algebra.

Proposition 13
.

Let
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
and

be languages such that

has a syntactic algebra.
The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L}}

(2)

\mathrm{syn}_{K}:\bbM\Sigma\to\mathrm{Syn}(K)
recognises
L
.

(3)

Every morphism recognising
K
also recognises
L
.

(4)

L
has the form

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i<m}\bigcap_{k<n_{i}}p_{ik}^{-1}[K]\,,\quad\text{for suitable }m,n_{i}<\omega\text{ and }p_{ik}\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)\,.

Proof 4.5
.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2)
follows directly by Lemma
11

since
{\preceq_{K}}=\ker\mathrm{syn}_{K}
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2) is trivial as
\mathrm{syn}_{K}
recognises
K
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Suppose that
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
recognises
K
.
By Lemma
11
, it follows that

\ker\varphi\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L}}
,
which, by the same lemma, implies that
\varphi
recognises
L
.

(4)
\Rightarrow
(1)
We have shown in Lemma
9
(b) that

\displaystyle{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{p^{-1}[K]}}\,,\quad\text{for every context }p\,.

To conclude the proof it is therefore sufficient to show that, for all languages
K,L_{0},L_{1}
,

\displaystyle{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L_{0}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L_{1}}}\quad\text{implies}\quad{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L_{0}\cup L_{1}}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\preceq_{K}}\subseteq{\preceq_{L_{1}\cap L_{1}}}\,.

For the first inclusion, suppose that
s\preceq_{K}t
and let
p
be a context such that

.
Then there is some
i<2
such that

.
Hence,

Similarly, if
s\preceq_{K}t
and

is a context with

, then

\displaystyle p[s]

\displaystyle\in L_{0}

and

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle p[t]

\displaystyle\in L_{0}\,,

\displaystyle p[s]

\displaystyle\in L_{1}

and

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\preceq_{L_{1}}t

\displaystyle p[t]

\displaystyle\in L_{1}\,.

Thus
p[t]\in L_{0}\cap L_{1}
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(4)
By definition of
\preceq_{K}
, for every pair of elements

with

, we can fix some context

such that

\displaystyle p_{ab}[s]\in K\quad\text{and}\quad p_{ab}[t]\notin K\,,\quad\text{for }s\in\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}(a)\text{ and }t\in\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}(b)\,.

and let

For

\displaystyle t\npreceq_{K}u\quad\text{implies}\quad p_{ab}[t]\in K\quad\text{where }a:=\mathrm{syn}_{K}(t)\text{ and }b:=\mathrm{syn}_{K}(u)\,.

Similarly,

\displaystyle s\npreceq_{K}t\quad\text{implies}\quad p_{ab}[t]\notin K\quad\text{where }a:=\mathrm{syn}_{K}(s)\text{ and }b:=\mathrm{syn}_{K}(t)\,.

Taken together it follows that

\displaystyle t\in\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[P]\quad\@iff\quad\text{there is some }a\in P\text{ with }p_{ab}[t]\in K\text{ for all }b\in Q\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[P]=\bigcup_{a\in P}\bigcap_{b\in Q}p_{ab}^{-1}[K]\,.

The next proposition describes languages recognised by syntactic algebras via arbitrary morphisms.

Proposition 14
.

Let
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
be a language with a syntactic algebra.
A language

is recognised by

if, and only if,

\displaystyle L=\varphi^{-1}\bigl[\bigcup_{i<m}\bigcap_{k<n_{i}}p_{ik}^{-1}[K]\bigr]\,,

, numbers

, and

.

Proof 4.6
.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose that

for some

.
By Lemma

, there exists a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Gamma\to\bbM\Sigma

with
\mathrm{syn}_{K}\circ\varphi=\psi
.
Consequently, it follows by Proposition

that

\displaystyle L=\psi^{-1}[P]=(\mathrm{syn}_{K}\circ\varphi)^{-1}[P]=\varphi^{-1}[\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[P]]=\varphi^{-1}\bigl[\bigcup_{i<m}\bigcap_{k<n_{i}}p_{ik}^{-1}[K]\bigr]\,,

for suitable contexts
p_{ik}
.

(\Leftarrow)

By Proposition

,
the morphism
\mathrm{syn}_{K}:\bbM\Sigma\to\mathrm{Syn}(K)
recognises the language

\displaystyle M:=\bigcup_{i<m}\bigcap_{k<n_{i}}p_{ik}^{-1}[K]\,.

Consequently,

.

In general, there is no reason why the syntactic congruence
\preceq_{K}

should be a congruence ordering. Let us isolate one property of the functor
\bbM

ensuring that this is in fact the case.

{defi}

A functor
\bbM:\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
is
finitary
if
it commutes with directed colimits, that is, if

\displaystyle\bbM(\varinjlim D)=\varinjlim(\bbM\circ D)\,,\quad\text{for every directed diagram }D:I\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}.

\lrcorner

Remark
.

(a)
In particular, if
\bbM
is finitary then
\bbM A
is equal to the directed colimit
of the diagram consisting of
\bbM C
, for all finite
C\subseteq A
.

(b)
The word functor
\bbM A:=A^{+}
is finitary as every finite word uses only finitely many labels.
The functor

\displaystyle\bbM\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle:=\langle A_{1}^{+},\ A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\rangle

for infinite words, on the other hand, is not finitary as
an infinite word can contain infinitely many different labels. Thus, in general

A^{\omega}\neq\bigcup{\{\,C^{\omega}C\subseteq A\text{ finite}\,\}}
.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\frakA
be a finitary
\bbM
-algebra and
K\subseteq A_{\xi}
a set.
If
\bbM
is finitary, then
\preceq_{K}
is a congruence ordering on
\frakA
.

Proof 4.7
.

We use the characterisation from Proposition
8
(3).
Hence, fix
u\in\bbM{\preceq_{K}}
.
We have to show that

\displaystyle\pi(\bbM q_{0}(u))\preceq_{K}\pi(\bbM q_{1}(u))\,,

where
q_{0},q_{1}:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.
As
\bbM
is finitary, there exists a finite relation
R\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}

such that
u\in\bbM R
.
Let
\langle a_{0},b_{0}\rangle,\dots,\langle a_{m-1},b_{m-1}\rangle
be an
enumeration of
R
and set

\displaystyle t_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bbM p_{k}(u)\,,

where

\displaystyle p_{k}(\langle a_{i},b_{i}\rangle)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}a_{i}&\text{if }i\geq k\,,\\
b_{i}&\text{if }i<k\,.\end{cases}

\displaystyle r_{k}

\displaystyle:=\bbM p^{\prime}_{k}(u)\,,

where

\displaystyle p^{\prime}_{k}(\langle a_{i},b_{i}\rangle)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}a_{i}&\text{if }i>k\,,\\
\Box&\text{if }i=k\,,\\
b_{i}&\text{if }i<k\,.\end{cases}

Then

\pi(t_{k})=r_{k}[a_{k}]
and
\pi(t_{k+1})=r_{k}[b_{k}]
,
and it follows by Lemma
9
that

\displaystyle a_{k}\preceq_{K}b_{k}\quad\text{implies}\quad\pi(t_{k})=r_{k}[a_{k}]\preceq_{K}r_{k}[b_{k}]=\pi(t_{k+1})\,.

Consequently,

\pi(\bbM q_{0}(u))=\pi(t_{0})\preceq_{K}\dots\preceq_{K}\pi(t_{m})=\pi(\bbM q_{1}(u))
,
as desired.

Unfortunately, not all the monads
\bbM
used in applications are finitary.
In particular those needed for languages of infinite words or infinite trees are not.
Therefore, we have to extend the preceding proposition to a larger class of functors.
It turns out that,
in all the known examples of a non-finitary functors where syntactic algebras exists,
the functor in question is ‘ruled’ in a certain sense by a subfunctor which
is
finitary.
The precise definitions are as follows.

{defi}

Let
\langle\bbM_{0},\mu_{0},\varepsilon_{0}\rangle
and

\langle\bbM_{1},\mu_{1},\varepsilon_{1}\rangle
be monads.

(a) A natural transformation
\varrho:\bbM_{0}\Rightarrow\bbM_{1}
is a

morphism of monads
if

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}=\varrho\circ\varepsilon_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{1}\circ(\varrho\circ\bbM_{0}\varrho)=\varrho\circ\mu_{0}\,.

In this case we say that
\bbM_{0}
is a
reduct
of
\bbM_{1}
.

(b) Let
\varrho:\bbM_{0}\Rightarrow\bbM_{1}
be a morphism of monads
and
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle
an
\bbM_{1}
-algebra.
The
\varrho
-reduct
of
\frakA
is the
\bbM_{0}
-algebra

\langle A,\pi\circ\varrho\rangle
.
If
\varrho
is understood, we also speak of an
\bbM_{0}
-reduct
of
\frakA
.

(c) A morphism
\varrho:\bbM^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbM
of monads is

dense
over a class
\calC
of
\bbM
-algebras if,
for all
\frakA\in\calC
,
C\subseteq A
, and
s\in\bbM C
,
there exists
s^{\circ}\in\bbM^{\circ}C
with
\pi(s^{\circ})=\pi(s)
.

(d) We say that a monad
\bbM
is
essentially finitary
over a class
\calC

if there exists a morphism
\varrho:\bbM^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbM
such that

\bbM^{\circ}
is finitary and
\varrho
is dense over the closure of
\calC
under binary products.

\lrcorner

Let us again consider the functor

\displaystyle\bbM\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle:=\langle A_{1}^{+},\ A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\rangle

for infinite words and let

\displaystyle\bbM^{\circ}\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle:=\langle A_{1}^{+},\ A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}\rangle\,,

where
A_{1}^{\mathrm{up}}
denotes the set of all ultimately periodic words in
A_{1}^{\omega}
.
Then the inclusion map
\bbM^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbM
is dense over the class of all finite

\omega
-semigroups since the infinite product of a finite
\omega
-semigroup is completely
determined by its restriction to all ultimately periodic words.
The case of infinite trees is similar and will be treated in detail in
Section
10
.

If
\bbM^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbM
is dense over
\calC
, every
\bbM
-algebra in
\calC

is uniquely determined by its
\bbM^{\circ}
-reduct.
This will be used below to prove the existence of syntactic algebras for essentially finitary
monads.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\varrho:\bbM^{\circ}\Rightarrow\bbM
be dense over a class
\calC
that is closed
under binary products.

(a)

Any two algebras in
\calC
with the same
\bbM^{\circ}
-reduct are isomorphic.

(b)

Let
\varphi:\frakA^{\circ}\to\frakB^{\circ}
be a morphism of
\bbM^{\circ}
-algebras
and assume that
\frakA^{\circ}
and
\frakB^{\circ}
are the
\bbM^{\circ}
-reducts of two

\bbM
-algebras
\frakA,\frakB\in\calC
.
Then
\varphi
is also a morphism
\frakA\to\frakB
of
\bbM
-algebras.

(c)

A relation
\sqsubseteq
is a congruence ordering on an
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA\in\calC

if, and only if, it is a congruence ordering on the
\bbM^{\circ}
-reduct
\frakA^{\circ}

of
\frakA
.

Proof 4.8
.

(a)
Suppose that
\calC
contains two
\bbM
-algebras
\frakA=\langle A,\pi\rangle
and

\frakA^{\prime}=\langle A,\pi^{\prime}\rangle
with the same
\bbM^{\circ}
-reduct

\frakA^{\circ}=\langle A,\pi^{\circ}\rangle
.
To show that
\pi=\pi^{\prime}
, fix an element
s\in\bbM A
.
Set
t:=\bbM d(s)\in\bbM\Delta
where

\Delta:=\{\,\langle a,a\rangle a\in A\,\}
is the diagonal of
A\times A
and

d:A\to\Delta
is the diagonal map.
By assumption, the product
\frakA\times\frakA^{\prime}
belongs to
\calC
.
As
\varrho
is dense, we can find some
t^{\circ}\in\bbM^{\circ}\Delta
with

\pi^{\circ}(t^{\circ})=\pi(t)
.
Note that
t^{\circ}\in\bbM^{\circ}\Delta
implies that

\bbM^{\circ}p(t^{\circ})=\bbM^{\circ}q(t^{\circ})

where
p,q:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\pi(s)=\pi(\bbM p(t))

\displaystyle=p(\pi(t))

\displaystyle=p(\pi^{\circ}(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=\pi^{\circ}(\bbM^{\circ}p(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=\pi^{\circ}(\bbM^{\circ}q(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=q(\pi^{\circ}(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=q(\pi(t))=\pi^{\prime}(\bbM q(t))=\pi^{\prime}(s)\,.

(b) Fix
s\in\bbM A
. To show that
\pi(\bbM\varphi(s))=\varphi(\pi(s))
,
we consider the graph

\displaystyle G:=\{\,\langle a,\varphi(a)\rangle a\in A\,\}

of
\varphi
.
Let
i:=\langle\mathrm{id},\varphi\rangle:A\to G
be the natural bijection and
set

.
Since
\frakA\times\frakB\in\calC
and
\varrho
is dense,
we can find some
t^{\circ}\in\bbM^{\circ}G
with
\pi(t^{\circ})=\pi(t)
.
Let
p:A\times B\to A
and
q:A\times B\to B
be the two projections.
Note that

\displaystyle\varphi=q\circ i\quad\text{and}\quad q(g)=\varphi(p(g))\,,\quad\text{for }g\in G\,,

which implies that
\bbM^{\circ}q(t^{\circ})=\bbM^{\circ}(\varphi\circ p)(t^{\circ})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\pi(\bbM\varphi(s))=\pi(\bbM q(t))

\displaystyle=q(\pi(t))

\displaystyle=q(\pi(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=\pi(\bbM^{\circ}q(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=\pi(\bbM^{\circ}(\varphi\circ p)(t^{\circ}))

\displaystyle=\varphi(p(\pi(t^{\circ})))

\displaystyle=\varphi(p(\pi(t)))=\varphi(\pi(\bbM p(t)))=\varphi(\pi(s))\,.

(c)
Clearly, every congruence ordering of
\frakA
is also one of
\frakA^{\circ}
.
Conversely, suppose that
\sqsubseteq
is a congruence ordering of
\frakA^{\circ}
.
We use the characterisation from Proposition
8
(3).
Thus, let
u\in\bbM{\sqsubseteq}
.
As the product
\frakA\times\frakA
belongs to
\calC
and
\varrho
is dense over
\calC
,
we can find some
u^{\circ}\in\bbM^{\circ}{\sqsubseteq}
with
\pi(u^{\circ})=\pi(u)
.
By assumption, we have
\pi(\bbM^{\circ}p_{0}(u^{\circ}))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM^{\circ}p_{1}(u^{\circ}))
,
where
p_{0},p_{1}:A\times A\to A
are the two projections.
Since

\displaystyle\pi(\bbM p_{i}(u))=p_{i}(\pi(u))=p_{i}(\pi(u^{\circ}))=\pi(\bbM^{\circ}p_{i}(u^{\circ}))\,,

this implies that
\pi(\bbM p_{0}(u))\sqsubseteq\pi(\bbM p_{1}(u))
, as desired.

Theorem 17
.

Suppose that
\bbM
is essentially finitary over
\calC
.
If a language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
is recognised by some morphism

\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
with
\frakA\in\calC
,
then
\preceq_{K}
is a congruence ordering on
\bbM\Sigma
.

Proof 4.9
.

Suppose that
K=\varphi^{-1}[P]
for
P\subseteq A_{\xi}
.
Let
\frakB\subseteq\frakA
be the subalgebra induced by
\mathop{rng}\nolimits\varphi
.
By Proposition
15
,

\preceq_{P}
is a congruence ordering on the
\bbM^{\circ}
-reduct
\frakA^{\circ}
of
\frakA
.
Hence, Lemma
16
(c) implies that it is also a congruence ordering on
\frakA
.
Consequently, its restriction is one on
\frakB
.
(If
\preceq_{P}
is the kernel of some morphism
q
, then its restriction to
B

is the kernel of
q\circ i
, where
i:\frakB\to\frakA
is the inclusion morphism.)
Thus,
{\preceq_{P}}=\ker\mathrm{syn}_{P}
where
\mathrm{syn}_{P}:\frakB\to\mathrm{Syn}(P)=\frakB/{\preceq_{P}}

is the quotient morphism. We will show that

\displaystyle{\preceq_{K}}=(\varphi\times\varphi)^{-1}[{\preceq_{P}}]\,.

It then follows that
{\preceq_{K}}=\ker(\mathrm{syn}_{P}\circ\varphi)

is also the kernel of a morphism and, thus, a congruence ordering.
Hence, it remains to prove the claim.

(\supseteq)

Let
f:=\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}+1:\Sigma+\Box\to B+\Box
be the function mapping

c\in\Sigma
to
\varphi(\mathrm{sing}(c))
and
\Box
to
\Box
.
For
s\in\bbM\Sigma
and
p\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)
, we have

\displaystyle p[s]\in K\quad\@iff\quad\varphi(p[s])\in P\quad\@iff\quad(\bbM f(p))[\varphi(s)]\in P\,.

If
\varphi(s)\preceq_{P}\varphi(t)
it therefore follows that

\displaystyle p[s]\in K\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\bbM f(p))[\varphi(s)]\in P\quad\Rightarrow\quad(\bbM f(p))[\varphi(t)]\in P\quad\Rightarrow\quad p[t]\in K\,,

for all
p\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)
.
Consequently,
s\preceq_{K}t
.

(\subseteq)

Suppose that
s\preceq_{K}t
and fix
p\in\bbM(B+\Box)
.
The morphism
\bbM(\varphi+1):\bbM(\bbM\Sigma+\Box)\to\bbM(B+\Box)

is surjective since

\varphi
is surjective and
\bbM
preserves surjectivity.
Thus, we can fix some context
\hat{p}\in(\bbM(\varphi+1))^{-1}(p)
.
It follows that

\displaystyle p[\varphi(s)]\in P

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varphi(\hat{p}[s])\in P

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat{p}[s]\in K

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat{p}[t]\in K

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varphi(\hat{p}[t])\in P\quad\Rightarrow\quad p[\varphi(t)]\in P\,.

Consequently,
\varphi(s)\preceq_{P}\varphi(t)
.

5.
Varieties

After these preparations, we come to the first of the central theorems of algebraic
language theory: the Variety Theorem. This theorem characterises which kind of language
families are amenable to our algebraic tools by
establishing a correspondence between language families and
the classes of algebras recognising them.

Before we can formally define the families and classes involved, we need to introduce a bit of
notation that allows us to transfer problems to a setting with only finitely many sorts.

{defi}

Let
\Delta\subseteq\Xi
be a set of sorts and
A
a
\Xi
-sorted set.

(a) We denote by
A|_{\Delta}
the subset of
A
containing only the elements with a sort in
\Delta
.
(Depending on the circumstances, we will treat
A|_{\Delta}
either as a set in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
, or as
a set in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
that just happens to have no element with a sort in
\Xi\setminus\Delta
.)

(b) For a function
f:A\to B
we denote the induced function
A|_{\Delta}\to B|_{\Delta}

by
f|_{\Delta}
.

(c) The corresponding restriction of the functor
\bbM
is defined by

\displaystyle\bbM|_{\Delta}A:=(\bbM(A|_{\Delta}))|_{\Delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM|_{\Delta}f:=(\bbM(f|_{\Delta}))|_{\Delta}\,.

(Again, depending on what is convenient, we will consider
\bbM|_{\Delta}

either as a functor
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
or
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
.)

(d) For an
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA
we denote by
\frakA|_{\Delta}
the
\bbM|_{\Delta}
-algebra
with domain
A|_{\Delta}
and product
\pi\restriction\bbM|_{\Delta}A
.
For a class
\calC
of
\bbM
-algebras we set

\calC|_{\Delta}:=\{\,\frakA|_{\Delta}\frakA\in\calC\,\}
.

(e) An
\bbM
-algebra
\frakB
is a
sort-accumulation point
of a class
\calA

of
\bbM
-algebras if, for every finite subset
\Delta\subseteq\Xi
,
there is some algebra
\frakA\in\calA
such that

\frakA|_{\Delta}
is a quotient of
\frakB|_{\Delta}
(as
\bbM|_{\Delta}
-algebras).

\lrcorner

Let us check that these notions are well-behaved.

Lemma 18
.

(a)

\langle\bbM|_{\Delta},\mathrm{flat}|_{\Delta},\mathrm{sing}|_{\Delta}\rangle
is a monad
on
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
.

(b)

If
\frakA
is an
\bbM
-algebra,
\frakA|_{\Delta}
is an
\bbM|_{\Delta}
-algebra.

Proof 5.1
.

(a)
It is sufficient to show that

\displaystyle\bbM|_{\Delta}A=(\bbM A)|_{\Delta}\quad\text{and}\quad\bbM|_{\Delta}f=(\bbM f)|_{\Delta}\,,\quad\text{for }f:A|_{\Delta}\to B|_{\Delta}\,.

Then it follows that every equation satisfied by
\bbM
is also satisfied by
\bbM|_{\Delta}
.

The second of these equations follows immediately from the definition as

f:A|_{\Delta}\to B|_{\Delta}
implies that
f|_{\Delta}=f
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\bbM|_{\Delta}f=(\bbM(f|_{\Delta}))|_{\Delta}=(\bbM f)|_{\Delta}\,.

For the first equation, let
\mathsf{1}
be the set containing exactly one element of each
sort
\xi
with
A_{\xi}\neq\emptyset
,
and let
f:A\to\mathsf{1}
be the unique function.
Since
\bbM
preserves preimages, it follows that

\displaystyle\bbM(A|_{\Delta})=(\bbM f)^{-1}[\bbM(\mathsf{1}|_{\Delta})]=(\bbM A)|_{\Delta}\,.

Consequently,
\bbM|_{\Delta}A=(\bbM(A|_{\Delta}))|_{\Delta}=(\bbM A)|_{\Delta}
.

(b) similar.

We will show below that there is a precise correspondence between the following families of
languages and classes of algebras.

{defi}

(a) A
(positive) variety of languages
is a family
\calK
of languages that is closed under
(i) finite unions and intersections, (ii) inverse morphisms, and (iii) derivatives.

(b)
A class
\calV
of finitary
\bbM

pseudo-variety

(i) quotients, (ii) finitary subalgebras of finite products, and
(iii) sort-accumulation points.

\lrcorner

Remark
.

(a) In the definition of a pseudo-variety, closure under quotients is superfluous
as it is implied by closure under sort-accumulation points. We have left it as
a requirement in the definition to emphasis the analogy to the usual definition
in the setting with finitely many sorts.

(b) The reason why we combine the operations of taking subalgebras and forming products into
a single one is that, in general, the product of two finitary algebras need not be
finitely generated (see
[
Blu20
]
for a counterexample).

(c) If the set of sorts
\Xi
is finite, our notion of a pseudo-variety coincides with
the standard one.

The aim of this section is to establish a one-to-one correspondence between
varieties of languages and pseudo-varieties of
\bbM
-algebras.
The arguments are mostly standard, except for some adjustments needed to support
infinitely many sorts.
We start with the following observation.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\calV
be a pseudo-variety and
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
a language with a syntactic algebra.
Then
K
is recognised by some algebra
\frakA\in\calV
if, and only if,
\mathrm{Syn}(K)\in\calV
.

Proof 5.3
.

(\Leftarrow)
is trivial since
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
recognises
K
.
For
(\Rightarrow)
, consider a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
recognising
K

with
\frakA\in\calV
.

\calV
is closed under finitary subalgebras, we may assume that

The first step in correlating varieties of languages and pseudo-varieties of algebras consists
in following the fact.

Proposition 21

If
\calK
is the family of languages recognised by the algebras of a pseudo-variety
\calV

of
\bbM
-algebras, then
\calK
is a variety of languages.

Proof 5.4
.

We have to prove three closure properties.

(1) We start with inverse morphisms.
Suppose that
K=\varphi^{-1}[P]
for a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Gamma\to\frakA
with

\frakA\in\calV
and
P\subseteq A_{\xi}
.
Let
\psi:\bbM\Sigma\to\bbM\Gamma
be a morphism. Then

\displaystyle\psi^{-1}[K]=\psi^{-1}[\varphi^{-1}[P]]=(\varphi\circ\psi)^{-1}[P]

is recognised by the morphism
\varphi\circ\psi
to
\frakA\in\calV
.

(2) Next, we consider closure under derivatives.
Let
K\in\calK
and fix a context
p
. By assumption,
there is a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
recognising
K
with
\frakA\in\calV
.
By Proposition
13
,
\varphi
also recognises
p^{-1}[K]
.
Hence,
p^{-1}[K]\in\calK
.

(3) It remains to prove closure under finite intersections and unions.
Clearly, the empty union
\emptyset
and the empty intersection
\bbM\Sigma
are
recognised by any morphism. Thus, it is sufficient to consider binary unions and intersections.
Consider two morphisms
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
and
\psi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakB

with
\frakA,\frakB\in\calV
,
and set
K:=\varphi^{-1}[P]
and

, for upwards closed
P\subseteq A_{\xi}
and

Then

\displaystyle K\cap L

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle^{-1}[P\times Q]\,,

\displaystyle K\cup L

\displaystyle=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle^{-1}[(P\times B_{\xi})\cup(A_{\xi}\times Q)]

are recognised by
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA\times\frakB

Let
\frakC
be the subalgebra of
\frakA\times\frakB
induced by the
range of
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle

Then
\frakC
is finitary and
\frakC\in\calV
.

It remains to prove the converse direction of the correspondence.
We start with two lemmas.

Lemma 22
.

Let
q:\frakA\to\frakB
be a surjective morphism.
Every language recognised by
\frakB
is also recognised by
\frakA
.

Proof 5.5
.

Suppose that
L=\psi^{-1}[P]
where
\psi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakB
and

is
upwards closed.
By Lemma

, there exists a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA

such that

. Setting

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}[Q]=\varphi^{-1}[q^{-1}[P]]=(q\circ\varphi)^{-1}[P]=\psi^{-1}[P]=L\,.

Lemma 23
.

Every language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
that is recognised by a finitary subalgebra

\frakC\subseteq\prod_{i\in I}\frakA^{i}
of a product of finitary
\bbM
-algebras
\frakA^{i}

can be written as a finite positive boolean combination of languages recognised by the factors
\frakA^{i}
.

Proof 5.6
.

As the statement also holds for infinite products and the proof of that case is not more complicated,
we prove the more general statement.
Let
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakC
be a morphism such that

K=\varphi^{-1}[P]
for some upwards closed
P\subseteq C\subseteq\prod_{i}A^{i}_{\xi}
.
Let
p_{k}:\prod_{i}\frakA^{i}\to\frakA^{k}
be the projection.
By the way the ordering of the product
\prod_{i}A^{i}
is defined, we can pick,
for every pair
a,b\in\prod_{i}A^{i}_{\xi}
of elements with
a\nleq b
,
some index

such that
p_{h}(a)\nleq p_{h}(b)
.
Let

be the finite set of such indices

that correspond to pairs
a,b\in C_{\xi}

For

and

, it follows that

\displaystyle a\nleq\varphi(s)\quad\@iff\quad p_{h}(a)\nleq p_{h}(\varphi(s))\,,\quad\text{for some }h\in H\,,

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\varphi(s)\geq a\quad\@iff\quad p_{h}(\varphi(s))\geq p_{h}(a)\,,\quad\text{for all }h\in H\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle K=\varphi^{-1}[P]=\bigcup_{a\in P}\varphi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]=\bigcup_{a\in P}\bigcap_{h\in H}(p_{h}\circ\varphi)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}p_{h}(a)]\,.

As the languages

As usual we start with a lemma.

6.
The profinitary term monad

The goal of this section and the next one is to derive an axiomatisation of pseudo-varieties
in terms of systems of inequalities.
We start by defining the kind of terms allowed in our axioms.
The actual axiomatisation will then be presented in Section
7
below.
A natural choice for the terms would be to take the elements of
\bbM X
,
for some set
X
of ‘variables’.
But it turns out that this does not work.
To capture the restriction to
finitary

\bbM
-algebras, we have to use
a more general notion of a term.
The classic result by Reiterman
[
Rei82
]
characterises the pseudo-varieties
of finite semigroups as exactly those axiomatisable by a set of
profinite
equations.
Analogously, we have to define
profinitary
\bbM
-terms
for our version of this
theorem. For this we follow the material in
[
CAMU16
,
UACM17
]
,
but with some adjustments that are needed to support infinitely many sorts.

To explain how we arrive at the definition below, let us collect our requirements on this
set of terms. We are looking for a functor
\widehat{\bbM}

mapping an (unordered) set
X
of ‘variables’ to some set
\widehat{\bbM}X
of ‘terms’.
These terms should generalise the ordinary terms from
\bbM X
, i.e.,
we need an embedding
\iota:\bbM X\to\widehat{\bbM}X
.
Furthermore, we should be able to ‘evaluate’ a term
t\in\widehat{\bbM}X
in a given finitary

\bbM
-algebra
\frakA
with respect to a given ‘variable assignment’
\beta:X\to A
.
Let us denote the resulting value by
\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)
.
For ordinary terms
t\in\bbM X
, this value should of course correspond to
the value of
t
in
\frakA
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(\iota(t);\beta)=\pi(\bbM\beta(t))\,,

where
\pi(\bbM\beta(t))
is the canonical extension of
\beta:X\to A
to
\bbM X\to A
.
Furthermore,
\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)
should be compatible with morphisms of
\bbM
-algebras.
That is,

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(t;\varphi\circ\beta)=\varphi(\mathrm{val}(t;\beta))\,,\quad\text{for every morphism }\varphi:\frakA\to\frakB\,.

This leads to the following construction.
We work in the category of all morphisms
\bbM X\to\frakA
.
In this category we consider the diagram of all
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA

where
\frakA
is finitary and we take for
\iota:\bbM X\to\widehat{\bbM}X

the limit. The morphisms
\widehat{\bbM}X\to\frakA
of the corresponding limiting cone
can then be taken as our evaluation maps.
The formal construction is as follows.

{defi}

Let
\calA\subseteq\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM)
be a subcategory of finitary
\bbM
-algebras and
X
a set.
We denote the comma category
(\bbM X\downarrow\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM))
by
\calC
,
the subcategory
(\bbM X\downarrow\calA)
by
\calC_{0}
, and the inclusion diagram by

D:\calC_{0}\to\penalty\calC
.

(a)
We denote by
\iota_{\calA}:\bbM X\to\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X
the limit
\iota_{\calA}:=\lim D
of
D
,
and the limiting cone by
(\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta))_{\beta\in\calC_{0}}
.
If
\calA
is the category of all finitary
\bbM
-algebras, we drop the subscript
and simply write
\widehat{\bbM}
,
\iota
, and
\mathrm{val}({-};\beta)
.

(b) We turn
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}
into a functor as follows. Given
f:X\to Y
,
the family
(\mathrm{val}({-};\beta\circ\bbM f))_{\beta}
(where
\beta
ranges over all
morphisms
\beta:\bbM Y\to\frakA\in\calA
) forms a cone from
\widehat{\bbM}X
to
D
.
As the cone
(\mathrm{val}({-};\beta))_{\beta}
is limiting, there exists a unique function

{defi}

(a) A

(one-sorted)

that is monotone and continuous.
We denote the category of all Priestley spaces and their morphisms by

(b) We denote by
\mathsf{Dist}
the category of all distributive lattices (with top and bottom elements)
and all lattice homomorphisms (preserving top and bottom).

(Priestley)

C\subseteq D(i)

such that
\mu_{i}(a)\in C
and
\mu_{i}(b)\notin C
.
The preimage
C^{\prime}:=\mu_{i}^{-1}[C]
is clopen in
B
and satisfies

a\in C^{\prime}
and
b\notin C^{\prime}
.
Suppose that
C^{\prime}
is not upwards-closed.
Then there are elements
c\leq d
with
c\in C^{\prime}
and
d\notin C^{\prime}
.
Consequently,
\mu_{i}(c)\leq\mu_{i}(d)
and
\mu_{i}(c)\in C
and
\mu_{i}(d)\notin C
.
This contradicts the fact that
C
is upwards-closed.

We have shown that
B
with the cone topology belongs to
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
.
Since
B
is the limit in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
, there exists a unique map

f:A\to B
(in
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Xi}
) such that
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}\circ f
, for all
i
.
Similarly, there exists a unique morphism
g:B\to A
of
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}

such that
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i}\circ g
.
We can see that the function
f
is continuous as follows.
Let
C=\mu_{i}^{-1}[K]
for a basic closed set
K\subseteq B
.
Then
f^{-1}[C]=(\mu_{i}\circ f)^{-1}[K]=(\lambda_{i})^{-1}[K]
.
Hence, continuity of
\lambda_{i}
implies that the preimage
f^{-1}[C]
is closed.

Consequently, we can applying the same universality argument two more times
to obtain
f\circ g=\mathrm{id}
and
g\circ f=\mathrm{id}
.
Therefore,
B
and
A
with the cone topology are isomorphic as topological space.

]
.

This is sufficient since Priestley spaces are Stone and, by the previous lemma,

Lemma 35

Let

(\mu_{i})_{i}

Our main technical tool in the next section is the following natural transformation

Theorem 36
.

Let
\calA\subseteq\calB\subseteq\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM)
.

(a)

There exists a unique morphism
\varrho:\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}\Rightarrow\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}

of monads that makes the following diagram commute, for all morphisms
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA

where
\frakA\in\calA

(b)

If
\calA

\calB

\varrho_{X}:\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X\to\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X

Proof 6.8
.

(a) For a given set
X
,
the family
(\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta))_{\beta\in(\bbM X\downarrow\calA)}
forms
a cone from
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X
to the diagram defining
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X
.
As the cone
(\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta))_{\beta\in(\bbM X\downarrow\calA)}
is limiting,
there exists a unique map
\varrho_{X}:\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X\to\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X

such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\varrho_{X}=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\,,\quad\text{for all }\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA\,.

As the equation
\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\iota_{\calA}=\beta
was already proved in
Lemma
28
(a),
it therefore remains to prove that the family
\varrho:=(\varrho_{X})_{X}
forms a morphism
of monads.
To see that it is a natural transformation, consider a function

.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\circ\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}f\circ\varrho

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta\circ\bbM f)\circ\varrho

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta\circ\bbM f)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}f=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\circ\varrho\circ\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}f\,.

By Corollary
29
, it follows that

\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}f\circ\varrho=\varrho\circ\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}f
,
as desired.

To check the two axioms of a morphism of monads,
let
\mu_{\calA}
and
\varepsilon_{\calA}
be the multiplication and unit map of
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}
,
and
\mu_{\calB}
and
\varepsilon_{\calB}
those of
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}
.
For every
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA
with
\frakA\in\calA
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\varrho\circ\mu_{\calB}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\circ\mu_{\calB}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}\bigl({-};\pi\circ\bbM\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}\bigl({-};\pi\circ\bbM\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\bigr)\circ\varrho

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}\bigl({-};\pi\circ\bbM\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\bbM\varrho\bigr)\circ\varrho

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}\bigl({-};\pi\circ\bbM\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\bigr)\circ\widehat{\bbM}\varrho\circ\varrho

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\mu_{\calA}\circ\widehat{\bbM}\varrho\circ\varrho

and

\displaystyle\penalty\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\varrho\circ\varepsilon_{\calB}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\varrho\circ\iota_{\calB}\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calB}({-};\beta)\circ\iota_{\calB}\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\beta\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\iota_{\calA}\circ\mathrm{sing}=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\circ\varepsilon_{\calA}\,.

By Corollary
29
, it follows that

\varrho\circ\mu_{\calB}=\mu_{\calA}\circ\widehat{\bbM}\varrho\circ\varrho

and
\varrho\circ\varepsilon_{\calB}=\varepsilon_{\calA}
.

(b)
To apply the topological machinery we have just set up, we translate the problem
into the category of Priestley spaces.
We equip each algebra in
\calB
with the discrete topology. As these algebras are
finitary, the resulting topologies are sort-wise Priestley.
According to Lemma
32
(b),
we can define the limits
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X
and
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X

in terms of only the surjective morphisms
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA

with
\frakA
in
\calA
or
\calB
.
Furthermore, it follows by Lemma
34
that

\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X
and
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X

are also sort-wise Priestley spaces when equipped with the cone topology.
In addition, the limits in the category
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
coincide with

\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X
and
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calB}X
.

Let
\Xi_{0}\subseteq\Xi
be the set of all sorts
\xi
such that
\bbM_{\xi}X\neq\emptyset
.
By Lemma
28
(d), it follows that
these are exactly the same sorts

,

and with

.
Consequently, we can perform the rest of the proof in the category

.
By the definition of the cone topology, all the maps

Furthermore, since we restricted the diagram to surjective maps
\beta

By Lemma

\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}X

Consequently, we can use Lemma

to show that

In the remainder of this section we will prove that
algebras of the form
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}|_{\Delta}X
are what is called

finitely copresentable
(at least if
X
and
\Delta
are finite).
This is another result requiring us to work with Priestly spaces.
Already the next lemma fails in
\mathsf{Set}
or
\mathsf{Pos}
.
Unfortunately, it also does only hold for finitely many sorts.

{defi}

An object
A
of a category
\calC
is
finitely copresentable
if,
for every cofiltered diagram
D:I\to\calC
with limit
C

and limiting cone
(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}
,
and for every morphism
f:C\to A
, there exists an index
k\in I
and
an essentially unique morphism
g:D(k)\to A
such that
f=g\circ\lambda_{k}
.
Essentially uniqueness here means that, if
g^{\prime}:D(k)\to A
is another morphism
with
f=g^{\prime}\circ\lambda_{k}
, then there exists an
I
-morphisms
h:l\to k
with

g\circ Dh=g^{\prime}\circ Dh
.

\lrcorner

Lemma 37
.

Let
\Xi
be a finite set of sorts.
Every finite Priestley space is finitely copresentable in
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
.

Proof 6.9
.

First, note that the duality theorem implies that
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
is equivalent
to
(\mathsf{Dist}^{\Xi})^{\mathrm{op}}
.
Furthermore, the corresponding translation maps finite spaces to finite lattices.
Consequently it is sufficient to show that every finite lattice is
finitely presentable in
\mathsf{Dist}^{\Xi}
.

It remains to transfer this result from
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
to
\mathsf{PAlg}(\widehat{\bbM})
.
We start with a variant of Remark 3.7 (a) from
[
CAMU16
]
.
As the proof is not included in the published version we have reproduced it here.

Proposition 38
.

Let
\calA
be a class of finitary
\bbM
-algebras.
The functor
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}
preserves cofiltered limits.

Proof 6.10
.

We obtain a very concise proof if we employ a bit of category-theoretical machinery.
For the following material on
right Kan extensions
and
ends
we
refer the reader to Sections X.3 and X.4 of
[
Lan98
]
.
A short introduction can also be found in Sections 1.1 and 1.2 of
[
Rie14
]
.
We have tried to present the proof in a way that it should be intelligible
without knowledge of the actual definitions of these terms.

As already noted above one can define
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}
as the

codensity monad
associated with the forgetful functor

I:\calA\to\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
maping an
\bbM
-algebra

\frakA\in\calA
to its universe
A
(equipped with the discrete topology).
By definition, this means that

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}=\mathrm{Ran}_{I}\,I

is the
right Kan extension
of
I
along itself.
(This equation follows immediately from Section X.3, Theorem 1 of
[
Lan98
]
.)
Furthermore, (according to the dual of Section X.4, Theorem 1 of
[
Lan98
]
)
we can compute a right Kan extension as

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Ran}_{I}\,I)(X)=\int_{\frakA\in\calA}\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(X,I\frakA)\pitchfork I\frakA=\int_{\frakA\in\calA}\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(X,A)\pitchfork A\,,

where the integral sign is a certain kind of limit called an
end

and the
power operator,
also called
cotensor,

{\pitchfork}:(\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi})^{\mathrm{op}}\times\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}\to\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
is defined as iterated product

\displaystyle X\pitchfork A=A^{X}=\prod_{x\in X}A\,.

The characteristic property of the power is the equation

\displaystyle\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(B,X\pitchfork A)\cong\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}(X,\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(B,A))\,,

for
X\in\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi}
and
A,B\in\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
.
For a fixed space
A
it follows that
{-}\pitchfork A:(\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi})^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}

is the right adjoint of the hom-functor
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}({-},A):\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}\to(\mathsf{Set}^{\Xi})^{\mathrm{op}}
.
This implies that
{-}\pitchfork Y
preserves all limits
(cf. Proposition 3.2.2 of
[
Bor94b
]
).
Furthermore, a space
A\in\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
is finitely copresentable if, and only if,
the hom-functor
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}({-},A)
preserves cofiltered surjective limits
(cf. Proposition 5.1.3 of
[
Bor94a
]
).
As we have seen in Lemma
37
that the universe
of a finitary
\bbM
-algebra is finitely copresentable in
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
, it follows that
the composition
\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}({-},A)\pitchfork A
preserves cofiltered limits,
for every
\frakA\in\calA
.

Given a cofiltered diagram
D:J\to\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}(\lim D)

\displaystyle=(\mathrm{Ran}_{I}\,I)(\lim D)

\displaystyle=\int_{\frakA\in\calA}\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(\lim_{j\in J}D(j),A)\pitchfork A

\displaystyle=\int_{\frakA\in\calA}\lim_{j\in J}{[\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(D(j),A)\pitchfork A]}

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\in J}\int_{\frakA\in\calA}{[\mathsf{PSp}^{\Xi}(D(j),A)\pitchfork A]}=\lim_{j\in J}\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}D(j)\,,

where the fourth step follows by the fact that an end is a limit and limits commute.

The following statement is the dual of Lemma 3.2 of
[
AP04
]
.

Lemma 39

Let

7.
Axiomatisations

After the preparations in the previous section we are now able to
define the type of inequalities we use to axiomatise pseudo-varieties
and to prove the characterisation theorem.

{defi}

Let
X
be a finite unordered set and
\calA
a class of finitary
\bbM
-algebras.

(a)
An
\bbM
-inequality over
X
is a statement of the form
s\leq t

with
s,t\in\widehat{\bbM}X
.

(b) A finitary
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA

satisfies
an
\bbM
-inequality
s\leq t

over
X
if

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(s;\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)\,,\qquad\text{for all }\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA\,.

We write
\frakA\models s\leq t
to denote this fact.

(c) The
\bbM
-theory

\mathrm{Th}(\calA)
of
\calA
is the set
of all
\bbM
-inequalities
s\leq t
satisfied by every algebra in
\calA
.
(We do
not
fix the set
X
these inequalities are over.)

(d) A set
\Phi
of
\bbM
-inequalities (possibly over several different sets
X
)

axiomatises
the following subclass of
\calA
.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi):=\bigl\{\,\frakA\in\calA\bigm\frakA\models s\leq t\text{ for all }s\leq t\in\Phi\,\bigr\}\,.

\lrcorner

Let us start with the following important property connecting
the theory of a class
\calA
to the morphism
\varrho_{\calA}

from Theorem
36
.

Lemma 41
.

Let
\calA
be a class of
\bbM
-algebras,
X
a finite set,
and
s\leq t
an
\bbM
-inequality over
X
.
Then

\displaystyle s\leq t\in\mathrm{Th}(\calA)\quad\@iff\quad\varrho_{\calA}(s)\leq\varrho_{\calA}(t)\,,

where
\varrho_{\calA}:\widehat{\bbM}\Rightarrow\widehat{\bbM}_{\calA}
is the morphism
from Theorem
36
.

Proof 7.1
.

By Lemma
28
(e), we have

\displaystyle\frakA\models s\leq t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }\frakA\in\calA

\@iff

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(s;\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA\in\calA

\@iff

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(\varrho_{\calA}(s);\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(\varrho_{\calA}(t);\beta)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA\in\calA

\@iff

\displaystyle\varrho_{\calA}(s)\leq\varrho_{\calA}(t)\,.

The easier direction is to show that every axiomatisable class is a pseudo-variety.

Proposition 42
.

Let
\calA
be a pseudo-variety and
\Phi
a set of
\bbM
-inequalities.
Then
\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
is a pseudo-variety.

Proof 7.2
.

We have to check three closure properties.
First, consider a finitary subalgebra
\frakA
of a product
\prod_{i\in I}\frakB^{i}

with
\frakB^{i}\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
. Let
p_{k}:\prod_{i}B^{i}\to B^{k}
be the projection.
For
s\leq t\in\Phi
over
X
and
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA
it follows that

\displaystyle p_{k}(\mathrm{val}(s;\beta))=\mathrm{val}(s;p_{k}\circ\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}(t;p_{k}\circ\beta)=p_{k}(\mathrm{val}(t;\beta))\,,

where the second step follows from the fact that
\frakB^{k}\models s\leq t
.
As the ordering of the product is defined component-wise, this implies that

\mathrm{val}(s;\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)
. Consequently,
\frakA\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
.

Next, consider a quotient
q:\frakB\to\frakA
with
\frakB\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
.
Fix
s\leq t\in\Phi
over
X
and
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA
.
Since
q
is surjective,
we can use Lemma
5

to find some
\gamma:\bbM X\to\frakA
with

\beta=q\circ\gamma
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}(s;\beta)=\mathrm{val}(s;q\circ\gamma)=q(\mathrm{val}(s;\gamma))\leq q(\mathrm{val}(t;\gamma))=\mathrm{val}(t;q\circ\gamma)=\mathrm{val}(t;\beta)\,,

where the third step follows by monotonicity of
q
and the fact that

\frakB\models s\leq t
. Consequently,
\frakA\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
.

Finally, suppose that
\frakA
is a sort-accumulation point of
\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
.
Fix
s\leq t\in\Phi
over
X
and
\beta:\bbM X\to\frakA
.
We have to show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(s;\beta)\leq\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(t;\beta)\,.

Suppose that
s,t\in\widehat{\bbM}_{\xi}X
and let
\Delta\subseteq\Xi
be a finite set of sorts
containing
\xi
and all sorts in
X
.
By assumption, there is some algebra
\frakB\in\mathrm{Mod}_{\calA}(\Phi)
and a surjective morphism

\mu:\frakB|_{\Delta}\to\frakA|_{\Delta}
.
By Lemma
5
, we can find a morphism

\gamma:\bbM|_{\Delta}X\to\frakB|_{\Delta}
with
\beta|_{\Delta}=\mu\circ\gamma
.
Since
\frakB\models s\leq t
and
s,t\in\widehat{\bbM}|_{\Delta}X
,
we have (working in the category
\mathsf{Pos}^{\Delta}
)

\displaystyle\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(s;\beta|_{\Delta})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(s;\mu\circ\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(s;\gamma))

\displaystyle\leq\mu(\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(t;\beta))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(t;\mu\circ\gamma)=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}(t;\beta|_{\Delta})\,.

Since

\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta|_{\Delta})=\mathrm{val}_{\calA}({-};\beta)\restriction\widehat{\bbM}|_{\Delta}X
,
it follows that
\frakA\models s\leq t
.

.

(a)
(\Rightarrow)

(\Leftarrow)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Th}(\calW)\subseteq\mathrm{Th}(\calV)\quad\text{implies}\quad\ker\varrho_{\calW,X}\subseteq\ker\varrho_{\calV,X}\,.

Hence, we can use the Factorisation Lemma to find a morphism

\varrho_{\calV,X}=q_{X}\circ\varrho_{\calW,X}
.
By Theorem

\varrho_{\calV,X}
is surjective.
Hence, so is
q_{X}
. That means that
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calV}X
is a quotient of
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calW}X
.
Consequently, every quotient of
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calV}X
is also a quotient of
\widehat{\bbM}_{\calW}X

and it follows by Proposition
43

that
\calV\subseteq\calW
.

(b)
We have seen in Proposition
42

that the class
\calW:=\mathrm{Mod}(\mathrm{Th}(\calV))
is a pseudo-variety.
We have to show that
\calV=\calW
.

(\subseteq)

Let
\frakA\in\calV
. Then we have
\frakA\models s\leq t
, for every
s\leq t
in
\mathrm{Th}(\calV)
.
This implies that
\frakA\in\mathrm{Mod}(\mathrm{Th}(\calV))=\calW
.

(\supseteq)

By (a) it is sufficient to prove that
\mathrm{Th}(\calW)\supseteq\mathrm{Th}(\calV)
.
Hence, let
s\leq t
be in
\mathrm{Th}(\calV)
.
Then
\frakA\models s\leq t
, for all
\frakA\in\mathrm{Mod}(\mathrm{Th}(\calV))=\calW
,
which implies that
s\leq t
belongs to
\mathrm{Th}(\calW)
.

We are finally able to state our Reiterman theorem for pseudo-varieties of
\bbM
-algebras.

Theorem 45
.

Let
\calF
be the class of all finitary
\bbM
-algebras.
A class
\calV
is a pseudo-variety if, and only if,
it is of the form
\calV=\mathrm{Mod}_{\calF}(\Phi)
, for some set
\Phi
of
\bbM
-inequalities.

Proof 7.5
.

(\Leftarrow)
was already proved in Proposition
42
,
and
(\Rightarrow)
follows by Corollary

since
\calV=\mathrm{Mod}_{\calF}(\mathrm{Th}(\calV))
.

8.
Logics

A major application of algebraic language theory consists in deriving criteria
for when a language is definable in a given logic.
In this section we will introduce an abstract framework covering
a large number of the logics used in practice.
Our focus will be on isolating some abstract properties of a logic
ensuring that the corresponding language family forms a variety and,
thus, fits into our framework.
In the next section we will then investigate what it means for a language
to be definable in a given logic.

Over the years several abstract logical frameworks have been in use, most of
them not developed enough to be ever published.
Among the major ones are the framework for abstract model theory proposed by
Barwise (see, e.g.,
[
BF85
]
),
the notion of an
abstract elementary class
introduced by Shelah
(see, e.g.,
[
Bal09
]
),
and the theory of
institutions
developed by
Goguen and Burstall (see, e.g.,
[
Dia08
]
).
The framework presented here is somewhat similar to the latter,
the main difference being that, in the following definition, we do not equip our class
of models with the structure of a category.

{defi}

(a) A
logic
is a triple
\langle L,\calM,{\models}\rangle

consisting of a (
\Xi
-sorted, unordered) set
L
of
formulae,

a (
\Xi
-sorted, unordered) class
\calM
of
models,

and a
satisfaction relation

{\models}\subseteq\calM\times L
.
To keep notation light, we usually identify a logic with its set of formulae
L
.

(b) A
morphism
of logics

\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle:\langle L,\calM,{\models}\rangle\to\langle L^{\prime},\calM^{\prime},{\models^{\prime}}\rangle

consists of two functions
\lambda:L\to L^{\prime}
and
\mu:\calM^{\prime}\to\calM
such that

\displaystyle M^{\prime}\models^{\prime}\lambda(\varphi)\quad\@iff\quad\mu(M^{\prime})\models\varphi\,,\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in L_{\xi}\text{ and }M^{\prime}\in\calM^{\prime}_{\xi}\,.

We denote the category of all logics and their morphisms by
\mathsf{Log}
.

(c) The
L
-theory
of a model
M\in\calM_{\xi}
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Th}_{L}(M):=\{\,\varphi\in L_{\xi}M\models\varphi\,\}\,.

For two models
M
and
N
, we define

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\sqsubseteq_{L}N

\displaystyle\quad\@defiff\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Th}_{L}(M)

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathrm{Th}_{L}(N)\,,

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\equiv_{L}N

\displaystyle\quad\@defiff\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{Th}_{L}(M)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Th}_{L}(N)\,.

(d) The class of
models
of a formula
\varphi\in L_{\xi}
is the set

\displaystyle\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi):=\{\,M\in\calM_{\xi}M\models\varphi\,\}\,.

A class
\calC\subseteq\calM_{\xi}
is
L
-definable
if

\calC=\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)
, for some
\varphi\in L_{\xi}
.

(e) A logic
L
is
lattice closed
if the collection of all
L
-definable
classes is closed under finite intersections and unions.

\lrcorner

As an example, for a given signature
\Sigma
, a set
X_{1}
of first-order variables,
and a set
X_{2}
of set variables, we can define
monadic second-order logic
as

\displaystyle\bigl\langle{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Sigma,X_{1},X_{2}],\,\mathsf{Alg}[\Sigma,X_{1},X_{2}],\,{\models}\bigr\rangle

where
{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Sigma,X_{1},X_{2}]
is the set of all monadic second-order formulae over the
signature
\Sigma
with free first-order variables in
X_{1}
and
free monadic second-order variables in
X_{2}
;
and
\mathsf{Alg}[\Sigma,X_{1},X_{2}]
is the set of all triples
\langle\frakA,\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\rangle

where
\frakA
is a
\Sigma
-structure and
\beta_{1}:X\to A
and
\beta_{2}:X\to\mathscr{P}(A)

are variable assignments.

Every
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-interpretation
\tau
(from the signature
\Sigma
to
\Gamma
) gives rise
to a morphism
{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Gamma,\emptyset,\emptyset]\to{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Sigma,\emptyset,\emptyset]

since, according to the Interpretation Lemma, we can construct, for every formula

\varphi\in{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Gamma,\emptyset,\emptyset]
,
some formula
\varphi^{\tau}\in{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\Sigma,\emptyset,\emptyset]
with

\displaystyle\tau(\frakA)\models\varphi\quad\@iff\quad\frakA\models\varphi^{\tau},\quad\text{for all $\Sigma$-structures }\frakA\,.

Let us isolate a few simple conditions for when a class of models is definable.

Lemma 46
.

Let
\langle L,\calM,{\models}\rangle
and
\langle L^{\prime},\calM^{\prime},{\models}\rangle
be lattice-closed
logics.

(a)

A class
\calC\subseteq\calM_{\xi}
is
L
-definable if, and only if,
there exists a finite subset
\Delta\subseteq L_{\xi}
such that

\displaystyle M\in\calC\quad\text{and}\quad M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N\quad\text{implies}\quad N\in\calC\,.

(b)

For sort-wise finite sets
\Delta\subseteq L
and
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq L^{\prime}
, and a function

f:\calM\to\calM^{\prime}
the following two statements are equivalent:

(1)

M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N\quad\text{implies}\quad f(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}f(N)\,.

(2)

The preimage
f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}]
of a
\Delta^{\prime}
-definable class
\calC^{\prime}\subseteq\calM^{\prime}

is
\Delta
-definable.

Proof 8.1
.

(a)

(\Rightarrow)

Let
\varphi\in L_{\xi}
be a formula defining
\calC
and set
\Delta:=\{\varphi\}
.
Suppose that
M\in\calC
and
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N
.
Then
M\models\varphi
, which implies that
N\models\varphi
.
Hence,
N\in\calC
.

(\Leftarrow)

Set

\displaystyle\varphi:=\bigvee{\bigl\{\,\bigwedge\mathrm{Th}_{\Delta}(M)\bigm M\in\calC\,\bigr\}}\,.

Note that this disjunction is finite since there are only finitely many subsets of
\Delta_{\xi}
.
For
N\in\calM
, it follows that

\displaystyle N\models\varphi

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad N\models\bigwedge\mathrm{Th}_{\Delta}(M)\,,\quad\text{for some }M\in\calC

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N\,,\quad\text{for some }M\in\calC

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad N\in\calC\,.

(b)
(1)\Rightarrow(2)

Suppose that
\calC^{\prime}\subseteq\calM^{\prime}
is
\Delta^{\prime}
-definable.
By (a) (applied to the logic
\Delta
instead of
L
), it is sufficient to show
that
M\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}]
and
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N
implies
N\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}]
.
Hence, let
M\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}]
and
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N
.
Then we have
f(M)\in\calC^{\prime}
and
f(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}f(N)
, by (1).
Consequently, (a) implies that
f(N)\in\calC^{\prime}
, that is,
N\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}]
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)

Suppose that
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N
.
To show that
f(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}f(N)
, we consider the class

\calC^{\prime}_{M}:=\{\,H\in\calM^{\prime}f(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}H\,\}
.
Note that
\calC^{\prime}_{M}
is
\Delta^{\prime}
-definable by (a).
By (2), we know that
f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}_{M}]
is
\Delta
-definable.
Consequently, it follows by (a) that

M\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}_{M}]
and
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}N
implies
N\in f^{-1}[\calC^{\prime}_{M}]
,
that is,
f(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}f(N)
.

Lemma 47
.

Let
\langle L,\calM,{\models}\rangle
and
\langle L^{\prime},\calM^{\prime},{\models}\rangle
be logics and

\mu:\calM^{\prime}\to\calM
a function.
The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

There exists a function
\lambda:L\to L^{\prime}
such that

\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle:\langle L,\calM,{\models}\rangle\to\langle L^{\prime},\calM^{\prime},{\models}\rangle

is a morphism of logics.

(2)

If
\calC\subseteq\calM
is
L
-definable, then
\mu^{-1}[\calC]
is
L^{\prime}
-definable.

If
L^{\prime}
is lattice closed, the following statement is also equivalent to those above.

(3)

For every sort-wise finite
\Delta\subseteq L
, there exists a sort-wise finite

\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq L^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}N\quad\text{implies}\quad\mu(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}\mu(N)\,.

Proof 8.2
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2)
Let
\varphi\in L
be a formula defining
\calC
. For
M\in\calM
, it follows that

\displaystyle M\in\mu^{-1}[\calC]\quad\@iff\quad\mu(M)\in\calC\quad\@iff\quad\mu(M)\models\varphi\quad\@iff\quad M\models\lambda(\varphi)\,.

Thus,
\lambda(\varphi)
defines
\mu^{-1}[\calC]
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1)
We define
\lambda:L\to L^{\prime}
as follows. For each
\varphi\in L
,
the class
\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)
is obviously
L
-definable.
By assumption it follows that the preimage
\mu^{-1}[\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)]

is defined by some formula
\varphi^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
.
We set
\lambda(\varphi):=\varphi^{\prime}
.

To see that
\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle
is a morphism of logics, fix
M\in\calM^{\prime}
and

\varphi\in L
. Then

\displaystyle\mu(M)\models\varphi

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad\mu(M)\in\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)

\displaystyle\quad\@iff\quad M\in\mu^{-1}[\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)]\quad\@iff\quad M\models\lambda(\varphi)\,.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Given
\Delta\subseteq L
, we set
\Delta^{\prime}:=\lambda[\Delta]
.
Suppose that
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}N
.
For every
\varphi\in\Delta
, we then have the implications

\displaystyle\mu(M)\models\varphi\quad\Rightarrow\quad M\models\lambda(\varphi)\quad\Rightarrow\quad N\models\lambda(\varphi)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mu(N)\models\varphi\,.

Consequently,
\mu(M)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}\mu(N)
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2)
Let
\calC\subseteq\calM
be defined by the formula
\varphi\in L
.
By assumption, there is some sort-finite set
\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq L^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}N\quad\text{implies}\quad\mu(M)\sqsubseteq_{\varphi}\mu(N)\,.

We use Lemma
46
(a) to show
that
\mu^{-1}[\calC]
is
\Delta^{\prime}
-definable.
Hence, suppose that
M\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}}N
and

.
Then
\mu(M)\sqsubseteq_{\varphi}\mu(N)
and, therefore,

\displaystyle M\in\mu^{-1}[\calC]\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mu(M)\models\varphi\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mu(N)\models\varphi\quad\Rightarrow\quad N\in\mu^{-1}[\calC]\,.

Here, we are mainly interested in logics whose set of models is of the form
\bbM\Sigma

with
\Sigma\in\mathsf{Alph}
, as these can be used to define languages.
As with families of languages, we also need to consider families of logics indexed by
the alphabet used.

{defi}

(a) A logic
L
is
over
an alphabet
\Sigma
if its class of models is
\bbM\Sigma
.

(b) A
family of logics
is a functor
L:\mathsf{Alph}\to\mathsf{Log}
such that

•

for every alphabet
\Sigma
, the image
L[\Sigma]
is a logic over
\Sigma
,

•

for every function
f:\Sigma\to\Gamma
, the image
L[f]

is a morphism
\langle\lambda,\mu\rangle
of logics with
\mu=\bbM f
.

(c) Let
L
be a family of logics.
A family of languages
\calK
is
L
-definable
if

\displaystyle\calK_{\xi}[\Sigma]\subseteq\{\,\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)\varphi\in L_{\xi}[\Sigma]\,\}\,,\quad\text{for all }\Sigma\in\mathsf{Alph}\text{ and }\xi\in\Xi\,.

(d) Let
L
be a family of logics and
A
a finite ordered set.
We call a subset
K\subseteq\bbM A

L
-definable,

if its unordered version
(\bbM\iota)^{-1}[K]\subseteq\bbM\bbV A
is
L
-definable.

(e) A family
L
of logics is

if the class of all
L
-definable
languages forms a variety of languages.

(f) We call a family of logics
L

(sort-wise) finite
if, for every alphabet
\Sigma
,
the set of formulae
L[\Sigma]
is (sort-wise) finite (up to logical equivalence).

(g) To keep notation light we will drop the signature from the notation
in cases where it is understood. Thus, we frequently write
L
instead of
L[\Sigma]
.

\lrcorner

For the word monad
\bbM A:=A^{+}
and monadic second-order logic, we can define
a family
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
that maps an alphabet
\Sigma
to the logic

{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}[\hat{\Sigma},\emptyset,\emptyset]
where

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}:=\{E,{\leq}\}\cup\{\,P_{a}a\in\Sigma\,\}

is the signature consisting of the successor relation
E
,
the ordering
\leq
, and predicates
P_{a}
for all letters in
\Sigma
.

As the notion of a logic is very general,
there is not much one can prove for an arbitrary logic.
To get non-trivial statements we need some kind of restriction.
As languages come equipped with a monadic composition operation,
it is natural to require our logics to be well-behaved under this form of composition.
This leads to the following definition.

{defi}

A family
L
of logics is
\bbM
-compositional
if, for every finite subfamily

\Phi\subseteq L
, there exists some sort-wise finite subfamily
\Phi\subseteq\Delta\subseteq L

such that, for all alphabets
\Sigma
,
the relation
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}
is a congruence ordering on
\bbM\Sigma
.

\lrcorner

For instance, for words
u,u^{\prime},v,v^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{+}
we have

\displaystyle u\equiv_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{m}}u^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad v\equiv_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{m}}v^{\prime}\quad\text{implies}\quad uv\equiv_{{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{m}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\,,

where
{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{m}
denotes the set of
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-formulae of quantifier rank at most
m
.
Consequently,
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
is
\bbM
-compositional for the word monad
\bbM A=A^{+}
.

The importance of
\bbM
-compositionality stems from the fact
the set of theories of such a logic forms an
\bbM
-algebra.

Proposition 48
.

A family of logics
L
is
\bbM
-compositional if, and only if, for every finite subfamily

\Phi\subseteq L
, there exist

•

a sort-wise finite subfamily
\Phi\subseteq\Delta\subseteq L
,

•

a functor
\Theta_{\Delta}:\mathsf{Alph}\to\mathsf{Alg}(\bbM)
, and

•

a surjective natural transformation

\theta_{\Delta}:(\bbM\restriction\mathsf{Alph})\Rightarrow\Theta_{\Delta}

such that

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}t\quad\@iff\quad\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\theta_{\Delta}(t)\,,\quad\text{for all }s,t\in\bbM\Sigma\,.

Proof 8.3
.

(\Leftarrow)

Given
\Phi\subseteq L
, choose
\Phi\subseteq\Delta\subseteq L
such that

s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}t
is equivalent to
\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\theta_{\Delta}(t)
.
This implies that the relation
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}
is equal to the kernel
of
\theta_{\Delta}
, which is a congruence ordering.

(\Rightarrow)

Given
\Phi\subseteq L
, choose
\Phi\subseteq\Delta\subseteq L
such that

\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}
is a congruence ordering.
We set
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma:=\bbM\Sigma/{\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}}
and choose for

\theta_{\Delta}:\bbM\Sigma\to\bbM\Sigma/{\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}}
the quotient map.
Given a function
f:\Sigma\to\Gamma
,
we define the morphism
\Theta_{\Delta}f:\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Gamma

as follows.
We will show that
\ker\theta_{\Delta}\subseteq\ker{(\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM f)}
.
Then we can apply the Factorisation Lemma to obtain a unique morphism

\psi:\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Gamma
with

\displaystyle\psi\circ\theta_{\Delta}=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM f\,,

and we set
\Theta_{\Delta}f:=\psi
.

To prove the above claim, note that,
by definition of a family of logics,
L[f]=\langle\lambda,\bbM f\rangle
is a morphism of logics.
Given
s,t\in\bbM\Sigma
with
s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Sigma]}t
and a formula

\varphi\in\Delta
, it therefore follows that

\displaystyle\bbM f(s)\models\varphi\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\models\lambda(\varphi)\quad\Rightarrow\quad t\models\lambda(\varphi)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bbM f(t)\models\varphi\,.

Hence,
\bbM f(s)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Gamma]}\bbM f(t)
, as desired.

From the definition, it immediately follows that
\theta_{\Delta}
is a natural transformation

\bbM\Rightarrow\Theta_{\Delta}
since

\Theta_{\Delta}f\circ\theta_{\Delta}=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM f
.
Hence, it remains to show that
\Theta_{\Delta}
is a functor.
Consider two functions
f:\Sigma\to\Gamma
and
g:\Gamma\to\Upsilon
.
By the equation we have just established, we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{\Delta}(g\circ f)\circ\theta_{\Delta}=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM(g\circ f)

\displaystyle=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM g\circ\bbM f

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\Delta}g\circ\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM f=\Theta_{\Delta}g\circ\Theta_{\Delta}f\circ\theta_{\Delta}\,.

By surjectivity of
\theta_{\Delta}
, this implies that

\Theta_{\Delta}(g\circ f)=\Theta_{\Delta}g\circ\Theta_{\Delta}f
.

It follows immediately from the definition that the theory algebra
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma

recognises every
\Delta
-definable language.

Lemma 49
.

The morphism
\theta_{\Delta}:\bbM\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
recognises
every
\Delta
-definable language
K\subseteq\bbM\Sigma
.

Proof 8.4
.

We claim that
K=\theta_{\Delta}^{-1}[P]
where

\displaystyle P:=\{\,\sigma\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\sigma\text{ for some }s\in K\,\}\,.

Clearly,
\theta_{\Delta}[K]\subseteq P
. Conversely, we have

\displaystyle\theta_{\Delta}(t)\in P

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\theta_{\Delta}(t)\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for some }s\in K\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for some }s\in K\,,

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad t\in K\,,

where the last step follows by Lemma
46
(a).

Next, let us take a look at the closure properties of definable languages.
Our first observation concerns closure under inverse relabellings,
which holds for every logic
L
.
Then we show that
\bbM
-compositionality implies, but is slightly stronger than,
closure under derivatives.

Lemma 50
.

Let
L
be a family of logics. The class of
L
-definable languages is closed
under inverse relabellings.

Proof 8.5
.

If
f:\Sigma\to\Gamma
is a morphism of
\mathsf{Alph}
,
it follows by the definition of a family of logics that there is some function
\lambda
such that

L[f]=\langle\lambda,\bbM f\rangle
is a morphism of logics.
Consequently, we can use Lemma
47

to show that
(\bbM f)^{-1}[K]
is
L
-definable, for every

L
-definable language
K\subseteq\bbM\Gamma
.

Lemma 51
.

Let
L
be an
\bbM
-compositional family of logics, and let
\Delta\subseteq L
be a subfamily
such that
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}
is a congruence ordering.
Then

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\quad\text{implies}\quad p[s]\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}p[t]\,,\quad\text{for all }s,t\in\bbM\Sigma\text{ and }p\in\bbM(\Sigma+\Box)\,.

Proof 8.6
.

Set
p^{\prime}:=\bbM(\theta_{\Delta}+1)(p)
.
By Lemma
9
(a),

\displaystyle a\leq b\quad\text{implies}\quad p^{\prime}[a]\leq p^{\prime}[b]\,,\quad\text{for }a,b\in\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\theta_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\theta_{\Delta}(p[s])=p^{\prime}(\theta_{\Delta}(s))\leq p^{\prime}(\theta_{\Delta}(t))=\theta_{\Delta}(p[t])

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad p[s]\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}p[t]\,.

Usually, the theory algebras
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
from Proposition

are not very well understood. (Otherwise, we would not need to introduce
a special algebraic framework to study definability questions.)
To shed a bit more light on what these algebras look like, we present an alternative
construction for the theory functor
\Theta
.

{defi}

Let
L
be a family of logics such that every
L
-definable language has a syntactic algebra.
The
syntactic theory morphism
(for an alphabet
\Sigma
) is

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{L}:=\langle\mathrm{syn}_{\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)}\rangle_{\varphi\in L[\Sigma]}:\bbM\Sigma\to\prod_{\varphi\in L[\Sigma]}\mathrm{Syn}(\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi))\,.

\lrcorner

Lemma 52
.

Let
L
be a family of lattice-closed logics such that every
L
-definable language has
a syntactic algebra, and let
\Delta\subseteq L
be sort-wise finite.
The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

The class of
\Delta
-definable languages is closed under derivatives.

(2)

s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\quad\@iff\quad\tilde{\theta}_{\Delta}(s)\leq\tilde{\theta}_{\Delta}(t)\,.

(3)

s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\quad\Rightarrow\quad p[s]\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}p[t]\,,\quad\text{for all contexts }p\,.

Proof 8.7
.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(3)
follows by
Lemma
46
(b).

(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)

First, note that in (3) we can replace the implication by an equivalence
since
p[s]\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}p[t]
implies
s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t
,
if we choose for
p
the empty context
\Box
.
Consequently, the equivalence of
(2)
and
(3)
follows from the fact that,
for
s,t\in\bbM\Sigma
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}_{\Delta}(s)\leq\tilde{\theta}_{\Delta}(t)

\displaystyle\@iff\quad

\displaystyle s\preceq_{\mathrm{Mod}(\varphi)}t\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in\Delta

\displaystyle\@iff\quad

\displaystyle p[s]\models\varphi\ \Rightarrow\ p[t]\models\varphi\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all contexts }p\text{ and all }\varphi\in\Delta

\displaystyle\@iff\quad

\displaystyle p[s]\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}p[t]\,,

\displaystyle\quad\text{for all contexts }p\,.

Theorem 53
.

Let
L
be a family of lattice-closed logics such that every
L
-definable language has
a syntactic algebra. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

L
is
\bbM
-compositional.

(2)

For every finite
\Phi\subseteq L
, there exists a sort-wise finite

\Phi\subseteq\Delta\subseteq L
such that the class of
\Delta
-definable languages
is closed under derivatives.

Proof 8.8
.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

This follows immediately from Lemma
46
(b)
together with Lemma
51
.

(2)\Rightarrow(1)

Given a subfamily
\Delta\subseteq L
with the above closure properties,
it follows by Lemma
52

that
{\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}}=\ker\tilde{\theta}_{\Delta}
.
In particular,
{\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}}
is a congruence ordering.

Apart from a criterion for
\bbM
-compositionality, this theorem also gives us an
explicit construction of the theory algebra
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
in
language-theoretic terms.
It therefore provides a more direct link between properties of a logic
L
and
properties of the class of
L
-definable languages.

9.
Definable algebras

We have finally arrived at the central part of this article where we combine algebra
and logic.
It follows from Theorem
26
that, to every varietal logic
L
,
there corresponds a unique pseudo-variety
\calV
of
\bbM
-algebras recognising the
family of
L
-definable languages.
We would like to use these
\bbM
-algebras to study the expressive power of our logic
L
.
To do so, we need to know as much as possible about how the algebras in
\calV
look like.
Unfortunately, Theorem
26
does not tell us very much about that.
The following definition provides a slightly more concrete description.

{defi}

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra and
L
a family of logics.

(a) A finite subset
C\subseteq A
is
L
-definably embedded
in
\frakA
if,
for every element
a\in A
, the preimage

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C\text{ is $L$-definable.}

(b)
\frakA
is
locally
L
-definable
if every finite subset
C\subseteq A

is
L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
.

(c)
\frakA
is
L
-definable
if it is finitary and locally
L
-definable.

\lrcorner

Remark
.

For the word functor
\bbM A=A^{+}
, every finite algebra (i.e., every finite semigroup)
is
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable since we can evaluate products in
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
.
(Just guess a labelling that associates with every position the product of the corresponding
prefix.)

The same is true for the functor

\bbM\langle A_{1},A_{\infty}\rangle=\langle A_{1}^{+},\ A_{1}^{+}A_{\infty}\cup A_{1}^{\omega}\rangle

for infinite words and for the functor for finite trees.
(For the former, one can use a reduction to the semigroup case
via a simple application of the Theorem of Ramsey; for the latter,
one can compute the product of a tree bottom-up similarly to the semigroup case.)

For infinite trees the situation is more complicated:
Bojańczyk and Klin
[
BK19
]
have constructed an example of a finitary
algebra that is not
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable.

If our logic
L
is sufficiently well-behaved, it immediately follows from this definition
that
L
-definable algebras only recognise
L
-definable languages.
(The converse, that every
L
-definable language is recognised by some
L
-definable algebra,
is harder to prove. We will do so later in this section.)
Note that this correspondence, besides being trivial, is also not that useful for
understanding the expressive power of
L
, as the definition makes essential use of

L
-definability. But the above definition can serve as a starting point for deriving
more useful descriptions – that of course will be specific to the logic in question.

Before proving that the
L
-definable algebras are exactly those that only recognise

L
-definable languages, let us start by looking at definably embedded sets.

Lemma 54
.

Let
\frakA
be a finitary
\bbM
-algebra and
L
a family of lattice-closed logics.
A finite set
C\subseteq A
is
L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
if, and only if,
there exists a sort-wise finite set
\Delta\subseteq L[C]
such that

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\quad\text{implies}\quad\pi(s)\leq\pi(t)\,,\quad\text{for all }s,t\in\bbM C\,.

Proof 9.1
.

(\Rightarrow)

Choose a function (of unordered sets)
\vartheta:A\to L
such that the
formula
\vartheta(a)
defines the set
\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C
.
We claim that the set
\Delta:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits\vartheta
has the desired properties.
Consider
s,t\in\bbM C
with
s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t
.
Then

\displaystyle s\models\vartheta(\pi(s))\quad\text{implies}\quad t\models\vartheta(\pi(s))\,.

Hence,
\pi(t)\geq\pi(s)
.

(\Leftarrow)

Let
\Delta
be a sort-wise finite set such that

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t\quad\text{implies}\quad\pi(s)\leq\pi(t)\,,\quad\text{for }s,t\in\bbM C\,.

Given
a\in A_{\xi}
, we set
K:=\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C
.
It follows that

\displaystyle s\in K\quad\text{and}\quad s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta_{\xi}}t\quad\text{implies}\quad t\in K\,.

Hence, we can use Lemma
46
(a) to show
that
K
is
L
-definable.

In general, the closure properties of definably embedded sets are rather weak.
To make them better behaved we have to impose some restriction on the logic
L
.

Lemma 55
.

Let
\frakA
be an
\bbM
-algebra,
L
a family of logics, and
C\subseteq A
finite set that is

L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
.

(a)

Every subset of
C
is
L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
.

(b)

If the class of
L
-definable languages is closed under inverse morphisms,
every finite subset
D\subseteq\langle C\rangle
is
L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
,
where
\langle C\rangle
denotes the subalgebra generated by
C
.

Proof 9.2
.

(a) Fix
D\subseteq C
and let
i:D\to C
be the inclusion map. Then

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM D=\bigl(\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C\bigr)\cap\bbM D=(\bbM i)^{-1}\bigl(\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C\bigr)

is the image of an
L
-definable set under an inverse relabelling
and, therefore,
L
-definable by Lemma
50
.

(b)
By (a) it is sufficient to consider the case where
D=\langle C\rangle
.
For every
d\in D
, we can find an element
f(d)\in\bbM C
such that
\pi(f(d))=d
.
This defines a function
f
with
\pi\circ f=\mathrm{id}_{D}
.
But note that, in general,
f
is not monotone.
Thus, we only obtain a function
f:\bbV D\to\bbM C
.
Let
\varphi:\bbM\bbV D\to\bbM C
be the (unique) extension of

f:\bbV D\to\bbM C
to
\bbM\bbV D
.
Let
\delta:\bbM\circ\bbV\Rightarrow\bbV\circ\bbM
be the natural isomorphism
obtained by the fact that
\bbM
is order agnostic.
Then

\displaystyle\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta\circ\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\bbV\mathrm{sing}

\displaystyle=\iota

\displaystyle=\pi\circ f

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}\,.

Note that
\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta
and
\pi\circ\varphi
are both morphisms
of
\bbM
-algebras. The latter is a composition of two morphism
\varphi:\bbM\bbV D\to\bbM C

and
\pi:\bbM C\to D
.
Concerning the former, we have

\displaystyle(\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta)\circ\pi

\displaystyle=(\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta)\circ\mathrm{flat}

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\iota\circ\delta\circ\mathrm{flat}

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\bbM\iota\circ\mathrm{flat}

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}\circ\bbM\bbM\iota

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\mathrm{flat}\circ\bbM(\iota\circ\delta)

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\bbM\pi\circ\bbM\iota\circ\bbM\delta=\pi\circ\bbM(\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta)\,.

As morphisms from a free
\bbM
-algebra are uniquely determined by their restriction
to
\mathop{rng}\nolimits\mathrm{sing}
, we therefore have
\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta=\pi\circ\varphi
.
For
a\in A
, it follows that

\displaystyle(\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM D]

\displaystyle=(\pi\circ\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbV\bbM D

\displaystyle=(\pi\circ\iota\circ\delta)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbV\bbM D

\displaystyle=(\iota\circ\bbV\pi\circ\delta)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbV\bbM D

\displaystyle=(\pi\circ\varphi)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbV\bbM D=\varphi^{-1}[\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C]\,,

which is the image of an
L
-definable language under an inverse morphism.
The way we defined
L
-definability for ordered sets, this implies that

\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM D
is also
L
-definable.

It follows immediately from the definition that an
L
-definable algebra only recognises

L
-definable languages. We start with a slightly more precise statement.

Theorem 56
.

Let
L
be a varietal family of logics.
A finitary
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA
is
L
-definable if, and only if,
every language recognised by
\frakA
is
L
-definable.

Proof 9.3
.

(\Leftarrow)

If some finite subset
C\subseteq A
is not
L
-definably embedded,
we can find an element
a\in A
such that
the preimage
K:=\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C
is not
L
-definable.
Thus, the restriction
\pi\restriction\bbM C:\bbM C\to\frakA
of the
product is a morphism recognising the non-
L
-definable language
K
.

(\Rightarrow)

Let
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\frakA
be a morphism and
P\subseteq A_{\xi}
an upwards closed set.
By assumption, the set
C:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits(\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing})
is
L
-definably embedded in
\frakA
.
For every
a\in A_{\xi}
, we can therefore fix an
L
-formula
\vartheta_{a}
defining the set

\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C
. Setting
\varphi_{0}:=\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}

it follows that

\displaystyle t\in\varphi^{-1}[P]\quad\@iff\quad\varphi(t)\in P\quad\@iff\quad\pi(\bbM\varphi_{0}(t))\in P\quad\@iff\quad\bbM\varphi_{0}(t)\models\bigvee_{a\in P}\vartheta_{a}\,.

(For the last step, note that
\bbM\varphi_{0}(t)\in\bbM C
.)
As the
L
-definable languages are closed under inverse morphisms,
we can find a formula
\chi\in L
such that

\displaystyle\bbM\varphi_{0}(t)\models\bigvee_{a\in P}\vartheta_{a}\quad\@iff\quad t\models\chi\,.

Consequently,
\chi
defines
\varphi^{-1}[P]
.

Obvious candidates for
L
-definable algebras are those of the form
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma

from Proposition
48
.
Below we will characterise under which conditions these are
L
-definable.
The proof rests on the following technical result.

Lemma 57
.

Let
L
be an
\bbM
-compositional family of lattice-closed logics.
For every sort-wise finite set
\Delta\subseteq L
such that
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}
is
a congruence ordering,
the set
\mathop{rng}\nolimits(\theta_{\Delta}\circ\mathrm{sing})
is
L
-definably embedded in
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
.

Proof 9.4
.

Set
f:=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\mathrm{sing}:\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
and
C:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits f
.
Choose a right inverse
g:\bbV C\to\bbV\Sigma
of
\bbV f:\bbV\Sigma\to\bbV C
and
let
\pi_{0}:\bbM C\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
be the restriction of the product
of
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
to
\bbM C
.
Recall the natural transformations
\delta
and
\iota
from Definition
2
.
Then

\displaystyle\bbV\pi_{0}\circ\delta

\displaystyle=\bbV\pi_{0}\circ\delta\circ\bbM(\bbV f\circ g)

\displaystyle=\bbV\pi\circ\delta\circ\bbM(\bbV\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ g)

\displaystyle=\bbV\pi\circ\delta\circ\bbM\bbV\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbM\bbV\mathrm{sing}\circ\bbM g

\displaystyle=\bbV\pi\circ\bbV\bbM\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbV\bbM\mathrm{sing}\circ\delta\circ\bbM g

\displaystyle=\bbV\theta_{\Delta}\circ\bbV\pi\circ\bbV\bbM\mathrm{sing}\circ\delta\circ\bbM g=\bbV\theta_{\Delta}\circ\delta\circ\bbM g\,.

To show that
C
is
L
-definably embedded in
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
,
we fix an element
a\in\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
. Then

\displaystyle(\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\pi_{0}^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]]

\displaystyle=(\pi_{0}\circ\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]

\displaystyle=(\pi_{0}\circ\iota\circ\delta)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]

\displaystyle=(\iota\circ\bbV\pi_{0}\circ\delta)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]

\displaystyle=(\iota\circ\bbV\theta_{\Delta}\circ\delta\circ\bbM g)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]

\displaystyle=(\theta_{\Delta}\circ\iota\circ\delta\circ\bbM g)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]=(\bbM\iota\circ\bbM g)^{-1}[\theta_{\Delta}^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]]\,.

We can use Lemma
46
to show that
the language
K:=\theta^{-1}_{\Delta}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]
is
L
-definable:
given
s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}t
and
s\in K
, we have

\theta_{\Delta}(s)\leq\theta_{\Delta}(t)
and
\theta_{\Delta}(s)\geq a
.
Thus,
\theta_{\Delta}(t)\geq a
, i.e.,
t\in K
.

To conclude the proof, note that we have shown in
Lemma
50
that the class of
L
-definable languages
is closed under inverse relabellings.
Consequently, the language

(\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\pi_{0}^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]]=(\bbM(\iota\circ g))^{-1}[K]
is
L
-definable and,
therefore, so is

\pi_{0}^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]=\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C
.

Theorem 58
.

Let
L
be an
\bbM
-compositional family of lattice-closed logics.
The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

L
is varietal.

(2)

Every algebra of the form
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
is
L
-definable.

(3)

The class of
L
-definable languages is closed under inverse morphisms.

Proof 9.5
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(3) is trivial.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1) Closure under inverse morphisms and under finite unions and intersections
holds by assumption, while closure under derivatives
was shown in Theorem
53
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(2)
First, note that
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
is finitary:
it is generated by the finite set
\mathop{rng}\nolimits(\theta_{\Delta}\circ\mathrm{sing})

and, for every sort
\xi\in\Xi
,
there are only finitely many elements in
(\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma)_{\xi}
,
since there are only finitely many subsets of
\Delta_{\xi}[\Sigma]
.

Hence, it remains to show that every finite subset is
L
-definably embedded.
Let
D\subseteq\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
be finite.
We have shown in the preceding lemma that the set
C:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits(\theta_{\Delta}\circ\mathrm{sing})

is
L
-definably embedded in
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
.
As
C
generates
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
, we have
D\subseteq\langle C\rangle
.
Consequently, it follows by Lemma
55
(b)
that
D
is also
L
-definably embedded.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3)
Fix a morphism
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\bbM\Gamma
and an
L
-definable language

K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Gamma
.
We will construct two sort-wise finite sets
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq L
such that

K
is
\Delta[\Gamma]
-definable and

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}[\Sigma]}t\quad\text{implies}\quad\varphi(s)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta[\Gamma]}\varphi(t)\,,\quad\text{for all }s,t\in\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma\,.

Then it follows by Lemma
46
(b)
that
\varphi^{-1}[K]
is
L
-definable.

Hence, it remains to find the sets
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
.
As
L
is
\bbM
-compositional, we can
choose a sort-wise finite subset
\Delta\subseteq L
such that
K
is
\Delta[\Gamma]
-definable
and
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}
is a congruence ordering.
Set

\displaystyle f:=\theta_{\Delta}\circ\varphi\circ\mathrm{sing}:\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Gamma\quad\text{and}\quad C:=\mathop{rng}\nolimits f\,.

By assumption,
C
is
L
-definably embedded in
\Theta_{\Delta}\Gamma
.
We can therefore use Lemma
54
to find a sort-wise
finite subset
\Psi\subseteq L
such that

\displaystyle u\sqsubseteq_{\Psi}v\quad\text{implies}\quad\pi(u)\leq\pi(v)\,,\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\bbM C\,.

Let
\Delta_{0}\subseteq\Delta
be the (finite) subset of all formulae whose sort is equal to the
sort of some element of
C
.
We have shown in Lemma
50

that
L
-definable languages are closed under inverse relabellings.
Therefore, we can use Lemma
47

to find a sort-wise finite set
\Psi_{\xi}\cup\Delta_{0}\subseteq\Delta^{\prime}\subseteq L
such that

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}[\Sigma]}t\quad\text{implies}\quad\bbM f(s)\sqsubseteq_{\Psi}\bbM f(t)\,.

For
s,t\in\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
, it follows that

\displaystyle s\sqsubseteq_{\Delta^{\prime}[\Sigma]}t

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\bbM f(s)\sqsubseteq_{\Psi}\bbM f(t)

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\theta_{\Delta}(\varphi(s))=\pi(\bbM f(s))\leq\pi(\bbM f(t))=\theta_{\Delta}(\varphi(t))

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\quad\varphi(s)\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}\varphi(t)\,.

As a consequence we obtain the following counterpart to
Theorem
56
.

Corollary 59
.

Let
L
be an
\bbM
-compositional, varietal family of logics.
A language
K\subseteq\bbM\Sigma
is
L
-definable if, and only if, it is recognised
by an
L
-definable algebra.

Proof 9.6
.

(\Leftarrow)
follows from Theorem
56
.
For
(\Rightarrow)
, fix some sort-wise finite
\Delta\subseteq L
such that

K
is
\Delta
-definable and
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
exists.
The claim follows as we have seen in Lemma
49

that the morphism
\theta_{\Delta}:\bbM\Sigma\to\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma

recognises
L
and in the preceding theorem that the algebra
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
is
L
-definable.

For syntactic algebras, we obtain similar statements.

Lemma 60
.

Let
L
be a varietal family of logics.
If
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Sigma
is an
L
-definable language with a syntactic algebra,
then
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
is
L
-definable.

Proof 9.7
.

Clearly,
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
is finitary. Hence, it remains to prove that it is locally
L
-definable.
Let
C\subseteq\mathrm{Syn}(K)
be finite.
Then
N:=\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C
is recognised by the restriction

\pi\restriction\bbM C:\bbM C\to\mathrm{Syn}(K)
.
By Proposition
14
it therefore follows that

N
is of the form

\displaystyle N=\varphi^{-1}\bigl[\bigcup_{i<m}\bigcap_{k<n_{i}}p_{ik}^{-1}[K]\bigr]\,,

for some morphism
\varphi:\bbM C\to\bbM\Sigma
and contexts
p_{ik}
.
By the assumed closure properties, all such languages are
L
-definable.
Consequently, it follows by
Theorem
56

that
C
is
L
-definably embedded in
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
.

Theorem 61
.

Let
L
be a family of lattice-closed logics such that every
L
-definable language
has a syntactic algebra. The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

L
is varietal.

(2)

For every
L
-definable language
K\subseteq\bbM\Sigma
,
the syntactic algebra
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
is
L
-definable.

Proof 9.8
.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) follows by
Lemma
60
.
For (2)
\Rightarrow
(1), fix an
L
-definable language
K\subseteq\bbM_{\xi}\Gamma
.
Then
K\subseteq\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[P]
where
P:=\mathrm{syn}_{K}[K]
.
For closure under inverse morphisms, consider
\varphi:\bbM\Sigma\to\bbM\Gamma
. Then

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}[K]=\varphi^{-1}[\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[P]]=(\mathrm{syn}_{K}\circ\varphi)^{-1}[P]\,,

which is
L
-definable by Theorem
56
.

For closure under derivatives, consider a context
p\in\bbM(\Gamma+\Box)
.
By Proposition
13
,
there exists an upwards closed set
Q\subseteq\mathrm{Syn}(K)
such that
p^{-1}[K]=\mathrm{syn}_{K}^{-1}[Q]
.
Consequently,
p^{-1}[K]
is recognised by
\mathrm{Syn}(K)
and, hence,
L
-definable by
Theorem
56
.

Next, let us take a look at the closure properties of
L
-definable algebras.

Proposition 62
.

Let
L
be an
\bbM
-compositional logic that is lattice closed.
The class of
L
-definable
\bbM
-algebras is a pseudo-variety.

Proof 9.9
.

We start by proving that the class of locally
L
-definable
\bbM
-algebras
is closed under arbitrary (a) subalgebras and (b) products.
This implies that that the class of
L
-definable
\bbM
-algebras is closed
under finitary subalgebras of finite products.
Having done so, it is then sufficient to show that the latter class is also
closed under (c) sort-accumulation points.
Hence, we have three statements to prove.

(a) Suppose that
\frakA\subseteq\frakB
where
\frakB
is locally
L
-definable.
Given a finite set
C\subseteq A
and an element
a\in A
, note that the set

K:=\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C
has the same value when evaluated in
\frakA

and in
\frakB
.
(To see this, note that
\pi[\bbM C]\subseteq A
as
A
is closed under
\pi
.
Hence,
\mathord{\Uparrow}a\cap\pi[\bbM C]
has the same value in both algebras.)
By our assumption on
\frakB
it thus follows that
K
is
L
-definable.

(b) First, note that the empty product
\frakA
has exactly one element
1_{\xi}
of each sort
\xi
.
Consequently,
\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}1_{\xi})\cap\bbM C=\bbM C
,
which is
L
-definable (by the empty conjunction).

It remains to consider the case of a non-empty product
\frakA=\prod_{i\in I}\frakB^{i}
.
Given a finite set
C\subseteq A
, we choose finite sets
D^{i}\subseteq B^{i}
, for
i\in I
,
such that
C\subseteq\prod_{i}D^{i}
.
Let
p_{k}:\prod_{i}B^{i}\to B^{k}
be the projections.
For
t\in\bbM\prod_{i}D^{i}
and
a\in A
, we have

\displaystyle\pi(t)\geq a\quad\@iff\quad\pi(\bbM p_{i}(t))=p_{i}(\pi(t))\geq p_{i}(a)\quad\text{for all }i\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C=\bigcap_{i\in I}{(\bbM p_{i})^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}p_{i}(a))\cap\bbM D^{i}\bigr]\cap\bbM C}\,.

For every pair of distinct elements
c,d\in C
, we fix one index
i\in I
with

p_{i}(c)\neq p_{i}(d)
. Let
H\subseteq I
be the (finite) set of these indices.
Then we have

\displaystyle c\neq d\quad\@iff\quad p_{i}(c)\neq p_{i}(d)\,,\text{ for some }i\in H\,.

It follows that

\displaystyle\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}a)\cap\bbM C

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{i\in I}{(\bbM p_{i})^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}p_{i}(a))\cap\bbM D^{i}\bigr]\cap\bbM C}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{h\in H}{(\bbM p_{h})^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}p_{h}(a))\cap\bbM D^{h}\bigr]\cap\bbM C}

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{h\in H}(\bbM j)^{-1}\bigl[(\bbM p_{h})^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}(\mathord{\Uparrow}p_{h}(a))\cap\bbM D^{h}\bigr]\bigr]\,,

where
j:C\to\prod_{i}D^{i}
is the inclusion map.
Note that we have seen in Lemma
50

that the
L
-definable languages are closed under inverse relabellings.
As the
\frakB^{i}
are
L
-definable and
L
-is closed under finite conjunctions,
the above set is therefore also
L
-definable.

(c)
Let
\frakA
be a sort-accumulation point of the class of
L
-definable algebras.
To show that
\frakA
is also locally
L
-definable, fix a finite set
C\subseteq A
and
an element
a\in A
. Let
\Delta\subseteq\Xi
be a finite set of sorts such that

C\cup\{a\}\subseteq A|_{\Delta}
.
By assumption, we can find an
L
-definable algebra
\frakB
such that

\frakA|_{\Delta}
is a quotient of
\frakB|_{\Delta}
.
Let
\mu:\frakB|_{\Delta}\to\frakA|_{\Delta}
be a surjective morphism.

Since
\mu
is surjective, there exists a function
f:\bbV A|_{\Delta}\to B|_{\Delta}
such that

\mu\circ f=\iota
.
Setting
D:=f[C]
, it follows that

\displaystyle(\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C]

\displaystyle=(\pi\circ\bbM\iota)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM\bbV C

\displaystyle=(\pi\circ\bbM\mu\circ\bbM f)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM\bbV C

\displaystyle=(\mu\circ\pi\circ\bbM f)^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM\bbV C

\displaystyle=(\bbM f)^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}[\mu^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]]\bigr]\cap\bbM\bbV C

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{b\in\mu^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]}(\bbM f)^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}b]\bigr]\cap\bbM\bbV C

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{b\in\mu^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]}(\bbM f)^{-1}\bigl[\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}b]\cap\bbM D\bigr]\cap\bbM\bbV C\,.

This set is
L
-definable since
each language of the form
\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}b]\cap\bbM D
is
L
-definable and
the class of
L
-definable languages is closed under finite unions and inverse relabellings.
Consequently,
\pi^{-1}[\mathord{\Uparrow}a]\cap\bbM C
is also
L
-definable.

The next theorem provides a more concrete description of this pseudo-variety:
it is generated by the theory algebras
\Theta_{\Delta}\Sigma
.

Theorem 63
.

Let
L
be a varietal
\bbM
-compositional logic.
An
\bbM
-algebra
\frakA
is
L
-definable if, and only if,
it belongs the the pseudo-variety
\calV
generated by all theory algebras of
the form
\Theta_{\Delta}X
where

is some finite set and
\Delta\subseteq L

a sort-wise finite subfamily such that
\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}

Proof 9.10
.

(\Leftarrow)

We have seen in Proposition
62

that the class of all
L
-definable algebras forms a pseudo-variety
\calW
,
and in Theorem
58

that
\calW
contains all theory algebras. Consequently,
\calV\subseteq\calW
.

(\Rightarrow)

Let
\frakA
be
L
-definable and fix a finite set
C\subseteq A
of generators.
It is sufficient to prove that
\frakA
is a sort-accumulation point of theory algebras.
Hence, fix a finite set

of sorts.
W.l.o.g. we may assume that

contains all the sorts in

Let

For

Consequently,

Let

The following theorem summarises our various characterisations of when a language

It can be considered the main result of this article.
Of these characterisations, (8) and (9) are the most useful.

-definable

Of course, for the latter one has to first determine the set of inequalities in question.

{\sqsubseteq_{\Delta}}\subseteq{\preceq_{K}}\,,
for some

10.
Applications

As an example, let us see how this abstract framework performs in the case
of languages of infinite trees. In this case, the functor
\bbM
maps a given set
A

to the set of all (finite and infinite)
A
-labelled trees.
There are several possible ways to chose the precise definition for
\bbM
.
We will present two of them denoted
\bbT
and
\bbT^{\times}
.
The latter is the more general one, while the former is a subfunctor.
Both operate on the category
\mathsf{Pos}^{\omega}
with sorts
\Xi:=\omega
.
We interpret a sort
n<\omega
as the
arity
of an element.
Given a set
A\in\mathsf{Pos}^{\omega}
, the set
\bbT^{\times}A
consists of all
(finite or infinite) trees
t
where each vertex
v
is labelled by an element
a
of
A

such that the arity of
a
matches the number of successors of
v
.
Hence, the elements of
A_{0}
appear at the leaves of
t
,
those of
A_{2}
at internal vertices with exactly two successors, and so on.
In addition to the elements of
A
we also allow as labels special variable symbols

x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots
, which are treated as elements of arity
0
and which are supposed
to be distinct from all elements of
A
.
Thus
\bbT^{\times}A
can be interpreted as the set of all (possibly infinite) non-closed terms over
the signature
A
.
The set
\bbT^{\times}_{n}A
consists of all trees
t
that use only the variable symbols

x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
.
Furthermore, we assume that the root is labelled by an element of
A
, not a variable.
(This is needed to define the flattening function below.)
Formally, we consider such a tree
t\in\bbT^{\times}_{n}A
as a function

t:\mathop{dom}\nolimits(t)\to A+\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}\}
where
\mathop{dom}\nolimits(t)
is the set of vertices of
t
.

Note that each variable
x_{i}
can be used once, several times, or not at all.
The subset
\bbT A\subseteq\bbT^{\times}A
consists of all those trees that use each variable
at most once. (Such trees are sometimes called
linear
in the literature.)

\bbT
and
\bbT^{\times}
are clearly polynomial functors. We turn them into monads as follows.
The singleton map
\mathrm{sing}:A\to\bbT^{\times}A
maps an element
a\in A_{n}
to the tree

a(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
consisting of a root with label
a
to which are attached
n

leaves with labels
x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1}
, respectively.
The flattening map
\mathrm{flat}:\bbT^{\times}\bbT^{\times}A\to\bbT^{\times}A
works as follows.
Given a tree
T\in\bbT^{\times}\bbT^{\times}A
where each vertex
v
is labelled by some tree

T(v)\in\bbT^{\times}A
,
we build a large tree assembled from the trees
T(v)
by

•

taking the disjoint union of all trees
T(v)
;

•

replacing each occurrence of a variable
x_{i}
in
T(v)
by an edge to the root
of
T(u)
, where
u
is the
(i+1)
-th successor of
v
;

•

unravelling the resulting directed acyclic graph into a tree.

For details, we refer the reader to
[
Blu20
,
Blua
]
.
It is now straightforward but a bit tedious to check that
\bbT^{\times}

together with these two operations forms a monad.
Hence, so is the restriction to
\bbT
.

The logics we are looking at are
first-order logic

\textstyle\mathrm{FO}
and

monadic second-order logic

\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
.
To define the satisfaction relation between formulae of these logics and
elements of
\bbT^{\times}\Sigma
, we encode a tree
t\in\bbT^{\times}_{n}\Sigma
as the structure

\displaystyle\frakT=\bigl\langle T,\,{\preceq},\,(S_{i})_{i<\omega},\,(P_{c})_{c\in\Sigma},\,(Q_{i})_{i<n},\,R\bigr\rangle

where
T=\mathop{dom}\nolimits(t)
,
\preceq
is the tree ordering (with the root as least element),

S_{i}
is the
i
-th successor relation,
P_{c}=t^{-1}(c)
the set of all vertices
v\in T

with label
c\in\Sigma
,
Q_{i}=t^{-1}(x_{i})
the vertices labelled by the variable
x_{i}
,
and
R
is an unary relation that only contains the root.
By necessity the proofs below assume some familiarity with tree automata
(more precisely, non-deterministic parity automata)
and back-and-forth arguments. Readers who want to refresh their knowledge
we refer to
[
Tho97
]
and
[
EF95
]
for an introduction.

We start by proving that
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable languages have syntactic algebras.

Theorem 66
.

\bbT^{\times}
is essentially finitary over the class of all
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras.

Proof 10.1
.

Let
\bbT^{\mathrm{reg}}A\subseteq\bbT^{\times}A
the set of all
regular
trees in
\bbT^{\times}A
,
i.e., those that, up to isomorphism, have only finitely many distinct subtrees.
We claim that the inclusion morphism
\bbT^{\mathrm{reg}}\Rightarrow\bbT^{\times}
is dense
over the class of all finite products of
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable
\bbT^{\times}
-algebras.

Let
\frakA_{0},\dots,\frakA_{n-1}
be
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-definable,

B\subseteq A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n-1}
, and
t\in\bbT^{\times}B
a tree with
\pi(t)=\bar{a}
.
We have to find a regular tree
t^{\circ}\in\bbT^{\mathrm{reg}}B
with
\pi(t^{\circ})=\bar{a}
.
Let
C_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
be a finite set of generators of
\frakA_{i}
and let

\calA_{i}
be a parity automaton recognising
\pi^{-1}(a_{i})\cap\bbT^{\times}C_{i}
.
Suppose that
Q_{i}
is the set of states of
\calA_{i}
,
K_{i}
the set of priorities
used by it, and
\Omega_{i}:Q_{i}\to K_{i}
the corresponding priority function.
For every
\bar{b}\in B
, we fix trees
\sigma_{i}(\bar{b})\in\bbT^{\times}C_{i}
with

\pi(\sigma_{i}(\bar{b}))=b_{i}
, for
i<n
.
This defines a function
\sigma_{i}:\bbV B\to\bbT^{\times}\bbV C_{i}
, which we can extend to a
morphism
\hat{\sigma}_{i}:\bbT^{\times}\bbV B\to\bbT^{\times}\bbV C_{i}
.

We construct the desired tree
t^{\circ}
by the following variant of the
usual Automaton–Pathfinder game (see, e.g.,
[
Tho97
]
).
In this game Automaton tries to construct a tree
s\in\bbT^{\times}B
such that,
for every
i<n
,
\hat{\sigma}_{i}(s)
is accepted by
\calA_{i}
,
while Pathfinder tries to prove that such a tree does not exist.
We will define the game in such a way that there is a correspondence between
winning strategies for Automaton and such trees
s
.
Note that these are exactly the trees
s
with
\pi(s)=\bar{a}
,
since

\displaystyle\pi(\hat{\sigma}_{i}(s))=\pi(\mathrm{flat}(\bbT^{\times}\sigma_{i}(s)))=\pi(\bbT^{\times}\pi(\bbT^{\times}\sigma_{i}(s)))=\pi(\bbT^{\times}p_{i}(s))=p_{i}(\pi(s))\,,

where
p_{i}:A_{0}\times\dots\times A_{n-1}\to A_{i}
is the
i
-th projection.
As
\pi(t)=\bar{a}
, it follows that Automaton indeed has a winning strategy for the game.
Furthermore, the winning condition of our game is regular. Therefore, it follows by
the Büchi-Landweber Theorem
[
BL69
]
that Automaton even has a winning strategy
that uses only a finite amount of memory.
As the trees
s
corresponding to finite-memory strategies via the above correspondence
are regular, the claim follows.

To conclude the proof, it therefore remains to define a regular game with the above properties.
In each round, Automaton picks the label
\bar{b}\in B
for the next vertex
v
of
s

and Pathfinder responds by choosing one of the successors of
v
.
While doing so, we have to keep track of all the states of the various automata from which
we want to accept the remaining subtree.

The positions for Automaton are of the form

\bar{U}\in\prod_{i<n}\mathscr{P}(K_{i}\times Q_{i})
,
while those for Pathfinder are tuples
\langle\bar{V}_{0},\dots,\bar{V}_{m-1}\rangle

where each component
\bar{V}_{i}
is a position for Automaton.
The initial position belongs to Automaton and consists of the tuple

\bigl\langle\bigl\{\langle 0,q^{i}_{0}\rangle\bigr\}\bigr\rangle_{i<n}
,
where
q^{i}_{0}
is the initial state of
\calA_{i}
.

In a position
\bar{U}
, Automaton chooses an element
\bar{b}\in B
and,
for every
i<n
and every pair
\langle k,q\rangle\in U_{i}
,
a partial run
\varrho_{q}
of
\calA_{i}
on the tree
\sigma_{i}(\bar{b})
that starts in the state
q
.
(It will turn out that Automaton can choose this run independently of
k
.
So we drop it to keep the notation light. We also assume that the sets
Q_{i}
are disjoint,
so we do not need to specify the index
i
.)
Suppose that
\bar{b}\in B_{m}
has arity
m
. For
i<n
and
j<m
,
let
H_{ij}
be the set of all vertices of
\sigma_{i}(\bar{b})
labelled by the variable
x_{j}
.
We denote by
W_{ij}(q)
the set of all pairs

\langle k^{\prime},q^{\prime}\rangle\in K_{i}\times Q_{i}
such that there is some
v\in H_{ij}
with

\displaystyle\varrho_{q}(v)=q^{\prime}\quad\text{and}\quad k^{\prime}:=\min{\{\,\Omega_{i}(\varrho_{q}(w))w\preceq v\,\}}\,.

The new position is
\langle\bar{V}^{0},\dots,\bar{V}^{m-1}\rangle
where

\displaystyle V^{j}_{i}:=\bigcup_{\langle k,q\rangle\in U_{i}}W_{ij}(q)\,.

Pathfinder responds by choosing some
j<m
after which the game proceeds to position
\bar{V}^{j}
.

Automaton wins a play of this game if either the play ends
in the position
\langle\rangle
where Pathfinder cannot make a move,
or if the play is infinite and satisfies the following variant of the parity condition.
Suppose that the play is
\bar{U}^{0},\bar{V}^{0},\bar{U}^{1},\bar{V}^{2},\dots
and let
W^{l}_{ij}(q)

be the sets used in the
l
-th turn by Automaton to determine the next position

\bar{V}^{l}=\langle\bar{V}^{l}_{0},\dots,\bar{V}^{l}_{m-1}\rangle
.
We call a sequence

We start with a bit of terminology.

•

The

Because of the translations between formulae and automata, there exists,

and each profile

\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-formula

stating that there is a partial run of
\calA
on the

.
Furthermore, every
\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-formula is equivalent to some formula of this kind.

For

, let
{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{(m)}

\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}
-formulae equivalent to a formula

\calA
is an automaton with at most

be the equivalence relation
which holds for two trees if they satisfy the same
{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{(m)}

We claim that

is a congruence ordering.
This means that, if

are trees with the same ‘shape’, i.e.,

\displaystyle S(v)\equiv_{(m)}T(v)\,,\quad\text{for all }v\,,\quad\text{implies}\quad\mathrm{flat}(S)\equiv_{(m)}\mathrm{flat}(T)\,.

For the proof, fix a formula
\varphi_{\calA,\tau}\in{\mathchoice{\mbox{$\displaystyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\textstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}{\mbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathrm{MSO}$}}}_{(m)}

.

\mathrm{flat}(T)

,

\calA
on
\mathrm{flat}(T)
with profile

.

For a given tree

,

\calA
on
\mathrm{flat}(T)

with profile
\tau
, while Pathfinder tries to disprove him.
The game starts in the position
\langle r,\tau\rangle

is the root of

.
In a position
\langle v,\upsilon\rangle

and
\upsilon

\varrho
on the subtree rooted at

with profile
\upsilon
.
He starts by choosing a partial run
\varrho
of
\calA
on the tree

starting in the same

\upsilon

\bar{\lambda}
for all the subtrees attached
to the copy of

in
\mathrm{flat}(T)

\upsilon

This is done as follows.

\mu=\langle p,\bar{U}\rangle

\varrho
.
For each component

of triples

\lambda

•

to the first two components.

•

•

\upsilon=\langle p,\bar{V}\rangle

Formally,

\displaystyle V_{i}=\bigl\{\,\langle l,q^{\prime}\rangle\bigm

\displaystyle\langle k,q,\lambda\rangle\in W_{i}\,,\ \lambda=\langle q,\bar{L}\rangle\,,\ \langle k^{\prime},q^{\prime}\rangle\in L\,,

\displaystyle l=\min{\{k,k^{\prime}\}}\,\bigr\}\,.

Let

be the successors of

Given

, Pathfinder responds by choosing a successor

of

\langle k,q,\lambda\rangle\in W_{i}
.
Then the game continues in the position
\langle u_{i},\lambda\rangle

If the game reaches a leaf of

If the leaf is labelled by a variable

and the current position is

In the case where the game is infinite, Automaton wins

\displaystyle\liminf_{i<\omega}k_{i}\quad\text{is even}\,.

It is straightforward to check that Automaton wins the game on a given tree

if, and only if, there exists a partial run of
\calA
on
\mathrm{flat}(T)
with profile
\tau
.
(Every partial run of
\calA
on
\mathrm{flat}(T)
with this profile gives rise to a winning strategy
in the game and, conversely, every winning strategy can be used to construct a partial run
with the desired profile.)

To conclude the proof we have to show that,
if

By construction, Automaton has a winning strategy

in the game on

We use it to define a winning strategy

in the game on

If
\sigma

on

(This is possible since

As only the profile of the chosen run is used by the game and

is winning,

is also winning.

we hit a leaf. For such a path it is trivial to check that its length is even.
Hence, our formula only needs to express
that the level parities computed in this way are consistent
and that the root is on an even level.

To express all this in first-order logic, we first define a few auxiliary formulae.

\displaystyle\mathrm{suc}(x,y):={}

\displaystyle S_{0}(x,y)\lor S_{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle\mathrm{zigzag}(x,y;u,v):={}

\displaystyle[S_{0}(x,y)\land S_{1}(u,v)]\lor[S_{1}(x,y)\land S_{0}(u,v)]

\displaystyle\mathrm{probe}(x,y):={}

\displaystyle x\preceq y\land\neg\exists z[\mathrm{suc}(y,z)]

\displaystyle{}\land{}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \forall u\forall v\forall w[&x\preceq u\land\mathrm{suc}(u,v)\land\mathrm{suc}(v,w)\land w\preceq y\\
&\quad{}\rightarrow\mathrm{zigzag}(u,v;v,w)]\,.\end{aligned}

The first one just states that
y
is a successor of
x
;
the second one says that
\langle x,y\rangle
and
\langle u,v\rangle
are two edges
that go into different directions, one to the left and one to the right;
and the last one states that
y
is one of the two leaves below
x
that are reached by
a zig-zag path consisting of alternatingly taking left and right successors.

Using these formulae we can express that a vertex
x
has an even distance from some leaf by

\displaystyle\mathrm{even}(x):=\exists y[

\displaystyle\mathrm{probe}(x,y)\land{}

\displaystyle\quad\exists u\exists v[\begin{aligned} x=y\lor[\mathrm{suc}(x,u)&\land u\preceq v\land\mathrm{suc}(v,y)\\
&\land\mathrm{zigzag}(x,u;v,y)]]]\,.\end{aligned}

Consequently, we can write the desired formula as

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y[\mathrm{suc}(x,y)\rightarrow[\mathrm{even}(x)\leftrightarrow\neg\mathrm{even}(y)]]\land\exists x\forall y[x\preceq y\land\mathrm{even}(x)]\,.
