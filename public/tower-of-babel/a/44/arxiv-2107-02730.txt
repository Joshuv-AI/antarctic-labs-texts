A provable two-stage algorithm for penalized hazards regression
Jianqing Fan, Wenyan Gong, Qiang Sun

Abstract
From an optimizer’s perspective, achieving the global optimum for a general nonconvex problem is often provably NP-hard using the classical worst-case analysis. In the case of Cox’s proportional hazards model, by taking its statistical model structures into account, we identify local strong convexity near the global optimum, motivated by which we propose to use two convex programs to optimize the folded-concave penalized Cox’s proportional hazards regression. Theoretically, we investigate the statistical and computational tradeoffs of the proposed algorithm and establish the strong oracle property of the resulting estimators. Numerical studies and a real data analysis lend further support to our algorithm and theory.



Keywords
: Cox’s proportional hazards model, counting process, NP-hardness, nonconvexity, sparsity, survival analysis, variable selection.

Introduction

An important goal of survival analysis is to identify possible risk factors or to evaluate treatment effects in epidemiological studies and clinical trials. To mitigate possible confounding bias, often a large number of covariates, such as clinical variables, imaging phenotypes and genetic markers, are collected and modeled, making the number of covariates far larger than that of observations. For inferential tractability and interpretability, a popular approach is to consider the regularized Cox’s proportional hazards regression

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta})\right\},

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})
is the negative log partial likelihood function depending on the observed data (to be introduced later) and
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta})
is a penalty function.

For linear models, there have been a surge of work on penalized regressions in the past two decades. When
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is taken to be the
\ell_{0}
-pseudo norm,
(
1.1
) corresponds to the best subset selection with different information criterions. For example, it includes the
C_{p}
-statistics, Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, minimum description length, and risk inflation criterion as special cases. Though the
\ell_{0}
-regularized regression maybe preferred statistically, it is discrete and thus is NP-hard to solve
(
Huo and Ni 2007
)
. To alleviate the computational challenge, some work has been conducted during the last two decades focusing on convex relaxations. A popular choice is the Lasso penalty
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
. In spite of the computational efficiency convex procedures may bring,
Fan and Li 2001
observed that convex surrogates introduce non-negligible estimation biases. Nonconvex penalties, such as the SCAD
(
Fan and Li 2001
)
penalty or MCP
(
Zhang 2010
)
, have been proposed to eliminate the estimation bias for large coefficients and to attain refined statistical rate of convergence under conditions on the minimal signal strength. Theoretical properties have been achieved for the hypothetical global optima
(or some local optima)
(
Fan and Li 2001
;
Kim et al. 2008
;
Zhang and Zhang 2012
)
which is not guaranteed to be achieved by a practical algorithm, such as the coordinate descent algorithm developed by
Breheny and Huang 2011
.

A natural question to ask is: is it possible to design a polynomial-time algorithm that can achieve the global optimum?
Chen et al. 2017
give a negative answer by providing a worst-case complexity analysis and showing that problem (
1.1
) with a general convex loss function and a folded concave penalty is strongly NP-hard. In other words, there does not exist a fully polynomial-time approximation algorithm for solving (
1.1
) with nonconvex penalties. Yet empirical studies have suggested that coordinate descent algorithms for nonconvex penalized regression work well, even better than those for the Lasso problems. So practice seems to contradict theory.

Fan et al. 2018
takes a step towards understanding this paradox by adapting the statistical analysis to the algorithmic framework. They propose a sequence of convex relaxation programs to approximate the original nonconvex optimization problem, and analyzing the statistical properties of the approximate solutions produced by these convex programs. They show that their obtained estimator can achieve the oracle rate as if the global optimum of the original nonconvex problem could be obtained. Numerical studies suggest that the proposed algorithm works more stable than the coordinate descent algorithm by
Breheny and Huang 2011
.

A closer examination at the geometry around the true regression coefficient
\bm{\beta}^{*}
demonstrates that the original nonconvex regression problem becomes (strictly) convex in a locally restricted neighborhood of
\bm{\beta}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\big\{\bm{\beta}\penalty\ \textnormal{is sparse}\penalty\ \textnormal{and}\penalty\ \|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}\leq r\big\},

(1.2)

for
r
sufficiently small, provided the loss function satisfies the local sparse strong convexity condition such that the convexity of the loss function can dominate the concavity of penalty function. Therefore, if we run an iterative algorithm starting from an initial estimator in the region of (
1.2
), the algorithm will finally converge to the (unique) global optimum. This observation suggests a two-stage optimization algorithmic framework for any nonconvex problem sharing a similar landscape:

1.

In the first stage, run a convex relaxation to find a good initial estimator in the locally restricted neighborhood of the underlying true regression parameter (we refer to this stage as the burn-in stage);

2.

In the second stage, run an iterative algorithm that can keep the solution sequence in the locally restricted neighborhood until convergence (to the global optimum).

Fan et al. 2018
’s algorithm happens to fall in this two-stage algorithmic framework as the first local linear approximation problem is used to burn in while all the remaining
\ell\geq 2
local linear approximation problems are used for global convergence. This encourages us to investigate the properties of this two-stage algorithmic framework for estimating the Cox’s proportional hazards regression model.

Inspired by the above intuition, we propose to directly optimize the second stage using the local adaptive majorization principle instead of adopting a sequence of convex programs. This helps to further reduce the iteration complexity of the algorithm developed in
Fan et al. 2018
. Theoretically, we prove the oracle properties of obtained estimators. Numerical studies suggest that our proposed algorithms work more stable than directly running the coordinate descent, thanks to the burn-in stage of the algorithm.

The rest of paper proceeds as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the Cox’s proportional hazards model and a two-stage algorithm called TLAMM. In Section
3
, we discuss some conditions on the localized eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix and prove the localized property of Cox’s Hessian.
Section
4
establishes the statistical and algorithmic property of the estimator resulting from TLAMM. Numerical experiments are used to examine the finite-sample performance of the proposed algorithm in Section
5
. In Section
6
, we apply TLAMM to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) skin cutaneous melanoma dataset to study the genes that are associated with the survival of melanoma patients. Section
7
concludes the paper with a brief discussion.

Related Work
:
Tibshirani 1997
proposed to use the Lasso penalty in Cox’s model for simultaneous parameter estimation and variable selection.
Gui and Li 2005
used the least angle regression
(
Efron et al. 2004
)
algorithm to compute the Lasso estimator in Cox’s model and applied to a microarray gene expression dataset. To reduce the bias of the Lasso estimator,
Fan and Li 2002
applied the SCAD regularization to Cox’s model which allows the addition of an frailty term and showed that the resulting estimator achieved the oracle performance when the dimensionality is fixed. Later,
Zhang and Lu 2007
proposed to use adaptive Lasso to estimate the Cox’s model, which achieved a similar oracle rate without nonconvexity issues.
Zou 2008
proposed a path-based variable selection method which is consistent for variable selection and efficient in estimation with a proper choice of shrinkage parameter.
Wang et al. 2009
developed an effective and flexible method for group selection in Cox’s model. In
Antoniadis et al. 2010
, the authors studied the statistical properties of the Dantzig selector in Cox’s model.
Du et al. 2010
studied the Cox’s model with semiparametric relative risk and proposed a procedure where two penalties are sequentially applied to achieve the oracle property. In
Bradic et al. 2011
, they focus on Cox’s model with ultrahigh dimensionality and establish strong oracle property for nonconcave penalized methods.
Huang et al. 2013
and
Kong and Nan 2014
both studied the oracle inequalities of Lasso estimators in Cox’s model under different set of conditions.

Methodology

Let
T,\,C\in\mathbb{R}
be the observed follow-up time and the censoring time respectively. Denote by
Z=\min\{T,C\}
the observed failure time and by
\delta=I(T\leq C)
the censoring indicator. We assume the following censoring mechanism:
T
and
C
are independent given the covariates
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Suppose we have collected
\left\{(\bm{X}_{i},Z_{i},\delta_{i}):i=1,2,...,n\right\}
such that they are i.i.d copies of
(\bm{X},Z,\delta)
.

Let
\lambda(t|\bm{x})
be the conditional hazard rate function at time
t
given the covariates
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
. The Cox’s proportional hazards model assumes that

\lambda(t|\bm{x}):=\lim\limits_{\Delta_{t}\rightarrow 0}P\left(t<T<t+\Delta_{t}|T>t,\bm{x}\right)/\Delta_{t}=\lambda_{0}(t)\exp(\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{\beta}),

(2.1)

where
\bm{\beta}
is the vector of log hazard ratios (HR) and
\lambda_{0}(\cdot)
is the baseline hazard function. Since some covariates can be time-dependent such as age, weight and blood pressure, we consider the time-varying version of (
2.1
) by assuming

\lambda(t|\bm{X}(t))=\lambda_{0}(t)\exp(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}(t)).

(2.2)

In this paper, we consider the general case where the covariates can be left-continuous.

Following
Fleming and Harrington 1991
, the negative log-partial likelihood is

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}(t)-\log\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}(t)\exp\left(\bm{\beta}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}(t)\right)\right)\right\}\mathrm{d}N_{i}(t),

(2.3)

where
N_{i}(t)=\mathds{1}\{Z_{i}\leq t,\delta_{i}=1\}
is the counting process corresponding to the observed failures and
Y_{i}(t)=\mathds{1}\{Z_{i}\geq t\}
is the at-risk process.

In high dimensions, we consider the following penalized partial-likelihood estimation problem

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{p}p_{\lambda}\left(\left\lvert\beta_{k}\right\rvert\right)\right\},

(2.4)

where
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a folded-concave penalty function such as SCAD and MCP, and
\lambda
is a non-zero regularization parameter.
We assume that the underlying regression coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}^{*}
is sparse with support set
\mathcal{O}
such that
\left\lvert\mathcal{O}\right\rvert=s
.

From the computational perspective, minimizing the nonvex penalized loss function (
2.4
) is challenging due to its intrinsic nonconvex structure.
Chen et al. 2017
exploited the worst-case analysis to show that solving (
2.4
) with a general convex loss function and a nonconvex penalty such as the SCAD and MCP is strongly NP-hard, indicating that, in general, there does not exist a polynomial-time algorithm for solving (
2.4
).
However, empirical studies have suggested that various algorithms, such as the local linear approximation
(
Zou and Li 2008
)
and the coordinate descent
(
Breheny and Huang 2011
)
, perform favorably despite the nonconvexity issues.

We examine this paradox closely in this section by looking at the landscape of the loss function around the true regression coefficient vector
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. Obviously, it is impossible to estimate
\bm{\beta}^{*}
without further conditions: the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})
is singular in high dimensions, rendering non-identifiability issues. A common remedy is to assume some local invertibility condition of the Hessian matrix
(
Candes and Tao 2007
;
Bickel et al. 2009
)
. Roughly speaking, we assume that, for some working sparsity
\bar{s}
and some radius
r
, the Hessian matrix is sparsely invertible in the following local zone

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r):=\left\{\bm{\beta}:\|\bm{\beta}\|_{0}\leq\bar{s},\,\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}\leq r\right\},

or more precisely, there exists some
\rho_{*}>0
such that

\displaystyle\min_{\|\mathbf{u}\|_{0}\leq\bar{s},\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)}\left\{\frac{\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}\mathbf{u}}\right\}\geq\rho_{*}.

When the nonconvex penalty is properly tuned such that
\rho_{*}
is larger than the maximum concavity of
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
, problem (
2.4
) becomes convex in the local restricted region of
\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
. For example, if
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is the SCAD penalty, we have

\displaystyle\max_{\beta}\{-p^{\prime\prime}_{\lambda}(\beta)\}=\frac{1}{a-1}.

By taking
a>1+1/\rho_{*}
, problem (
2.4
) becomes sparse strongly convex for any
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
. We define a shifted loss function as in
Loh et al. 2017
such that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+p_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta})-\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{1}
. To proceed, we rewrite the objective function as

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+p_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta})=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{1},

(2.5)

which is convex in
\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
. Therefore, if we can propose an algorithm that starts from an initialization in
\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
and keeps the solution sequence in
\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
, then optimizing (
2.4
) is equivalent to optimizing a convex problem. A natural question is: How shall we find a good initialization
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
such that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{1}\in\mathcal{C}(\bar{s},r)
? This can be done by solving a convex relaxation of problem (
2.4
) - the Lasso problem, which is the first stage of the algorithm:

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}}\left\{\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{p}\lambda\left\lvert\beta_{k}\right\rvert\right\}.

(2.6)

Note that this optimization is the same as the local linear approximation
(
Zou and Li 2008
)
to the problem (
2.4
) starting at the initial value
0
.
The first stage is a convex problem and thus can be solved efficiently. Then starting from
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
we can optimize (
2.4
) directly. In both stages, we apply the Local Adaptive Majorize-Minimization (LAMM) algorithm
(
Fan et al. 2018
)
to solve the corresponding optimization problem. The algorithm is thus referred to as the Two-stage LAMM (TLAMM) algorithm. Figure
1
shows an illustration of the TLAMM algorithm.

Figure 1:
An illustration of TLAMM.

To fix idea, we describe the second stage in details. The idea also applies to the first stage. At any working solution
\bm{\beta}^{2,k}
, we locally majorize
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})
at
\bm{\beta}^{2,k}
by the isotropic quadratic function

\Psi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\phi^{2,k+1}}(\bm{\beta},{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}):=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k})+\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}),\bm{\beta}-{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}\right\rangle+\frac{\phi^{2,k+1}}{2}||\bm{\beta}-{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}||_{2}^{2},

(2.7)

with
\phi^{2,k+1}
chosen (to be discussed below) such that the next-step update
\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi_{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\phi^{2,k+1}}(\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1},{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k})\geq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}).

(2.8)

With the majorization (
2.7
), the next-step update
\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}
is given by

\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k})+\left\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}),\bm{\beta}-{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}\right\rangle+\frac{\phi^{2,k+1}}{2}||\bm{\beta}-{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{1}\right\}.

(2.9)

The solution to (
2.9
) has a closed-form updating rule:

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}=S\left({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}-\frac{1}{\phi^{2,k+1}}\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\big({\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}\big),\lambda/\phi^{2,k+1}\right)=:T_{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}},\lambda,\phi^{2,k+1}}(\bm{\beta}^{2,k}),

(2.10)

and
S(x,\lambda)=\text{sign}(x)\cdot\max\left(\left\lvert x\right\rvert-\lambda,0\right)
is the soft-thresholding operator.

The quadratic coefficient
\phi^{2,k+1}
can be chosen by a line-search method
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
)
. We can start from a small factor
\phi_{0}=10^{-4}
, solve for (
2.10
) and plug it into (
2.8
) to check whether the local majorization condition hold. If yes, the algorithm outputs
\phi^{2,k+1}=\phi_{0}
and the corresponding
\bm{\beta}^{2,k+1}
; otherwise, we inflate its value by multiplying a fixed scale
\gamma_{u}>1
and repeat the above steps until the local majorization condition hold. Such a solution always exists since (2.7) with a sufficiently large
\phi
will eventually majorize
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})
. Following
Fan et al. 2018
, we refer to this line search method as the LAMM algorithm, which is summarized in the box of Algorithm
1
.

By repeating the LAMM algorithm, a sequence of solutions
\{{\bm{\beta}}^{2,k}:k=0,1,2,...\}
are generated. To stop the algorithm, we make use of the first order optimality condition. According to (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), if
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
is the second-stage minimizer, it must satisfy

\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2})+\lambda\bm{\xi}={\bf 0}\quad\text{for}\quad\bm{\xi}\in\partial||\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2}||_{1}.

(2.11)

Inspired by this, we stop the optimization algorithm when

\omega_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}^{2,k}):=\min\limits_{\bm{\xi}\in\partial||\bm{\beta}^{2,k}||_{1}}\left\{||\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{2,k})+\lambda\bm{\xi}||_{\infty}\right\}\leq\varepsilon,

(2.12)

where
\varepsilon
is a prefixed optimization error. We call
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}:=\bm{\beta}^{2,k}
an
\varepsilon
-optimal solution. A detailed description of TLAMM could be found in the box of Algorithm
2
.

3
Localized Sparse Eigenvalues

In this section, we study the local geometry of the Cox’s loss function by introducing the localized sparse eigenvalue (LSE) and the corresponding condition. We also verify that the loss function of Cox’s model satisfies an LSE condition which suggests the localized strong convexity around
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. Recall that
||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{0}=s
.

Definition 1
.

(Localized Sparse Eigenvalue (LSE))
The maximum and minimum localized sparse eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(m,r)=\sup\limits_{\bm{u},\bm{\beta}}\left\{\bm{u}_{\texttt{J}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})\bm{u}_{\texttt{J}}:||\bm{u}||_{2}^{2}=1,||\bm{u}||_{0}\leq m,||\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}\leq r\right\};

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(m,r)=\inf\limits_{\bm{u},\bm{\beta}}\left\{\bm{u}_{\texttt{J}}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})\bm{u}_{\texttt{J}}:||\bm{u}||_{2}^{2}=1,||\bm{u}||_{0}\leq m,||\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}\leq r\right\}.

We define the maximum and minimum localized sparse eigenvalues of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)
in the same way and denote them as
\kappa_{+}(m,r)
and
\kappa_{-}(m,r)
. Due to the concavity of the term
p_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta})-\lambda||\bm{\beta}||_{1}
, we have
\kappa_{-}(m,r)\leq\rho_{-}(m,r)\leq\kappa_{+}(m,r)\leq\rho_{+}(m,r)
.

Condition 1
.

We say the LSE condition holds if, for some given constant
C_{1}
and radius
r
, there exists an integer
\widetilde{s}\gtrsim s
such that

\displaystyle 0<\rho_{*}\leq\kappa_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)\leq\rho_{+}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)\leq\rho^{*}<+\infty,

\displaystyle\widetilde{s}/s>C_{1}\rho^{2}_{+}({2s}+2\widetilde{s},r)/\kappa^{2}_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r).

Empirically
\widetilde{s}
is of the same order as
s
. Our next theorem suggests that Condition
1
holds with high probability when the regularization is properly parametrized.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\{\bm{X}_{i}(t),Y_{i}(t),t\geq 0\}
are i.i.d. processes from
\{\bm{X}(t),Y(t),t\geq 0\}
with
\mathbb{P}\{\sup\limits_{t}||\bm{X}_{i}(t)||_{\infty}\leq M\}=1
for a constant
M>0
. Assume that the maximum event time
t^{*}<+\infty
and let
r_{*}=\mathbb{E}\left[Y(t^{*})\exp(\bm{\beta}^{*\top}\bm{X}(t^{*}))\right]
. Then, for any
s^{\prime}\leq p
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(s^{\prime},r)\leq\exp(4rM)\left\{C_{+}(s^{\prime})+4s^{\prime}M^{2}\left[\left(1+\Lambda_{0}(t^{*})\right)L_{n}\left(\frac{p(p+1)}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{2}{r_{*}}\Lambda_{0}(t^{*})t_{n,p,\varepsilon}^{2}\right]\right\},

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(s^{\prime},r)\geq\exp(-4rM)\left\{C_{-}(s^{\prime})-4s^{\prime}M^{2}\left[\left(1+\Lambda_{0}(t^{*})\right)L_{n}\left(\frac{p(p+1)}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{2}{r_{*}}\Lambda_{0}(t^{*})t_{n,p,\varepsilon}^{2}\right]\right\}

hold with probability at least
1-\exp(-nr_{*}^{2}/(8M^{2}))-2\varepsilon
. Here,
\Lambda_{0}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{0}(u)\mathrm{d}u
is the cumulative baseline hazard function,
C^{*}\geq C_{+}(s^{\prime})\geq C_{-}(s^{\prime})\geq C_{*}>0
where
C^{*}
and
C_{*}
are two constants depending on
\bm{\beta}^{*}
,
L_{n}(t)=\sqrt{(2/n)\log(t)}
and
t_{n,p,\varepsilon}
is the solution to
p(p+1)\exp(-nt_{n,p,\varepsilon}^{2}/(2+2t_{n,p,\varepsilon}/3))=\varepsilon/3
.

For any
s^{\prime}\leq p
, if
n\asymp s^{\prime 2}\log p
, then
L_{n}\left(p(p+1)/\varepsilon\right)\asymp 1/s^{\prime}
and
t_{n,p,\varepsilon}\lesssim 1/s^{\prime}
. Take
s^{\prime}=2s+2\widetilde{s}
and treat the cumulative baseline hazard
\Lambda_{0}(t^{*})
as a constant. Theorem
1
implies that if
n\geq C(2s+2\widetilde{s})^{2}\log p
for a sufficiently large constant
C
, then with high probability,
\rho_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)
is lower bounded by
C_{*}\exp(-4rM)/2
and
\rho_{+}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)
is upper bounded by
2C^{*}\exp(4rM)
. As long as the second order derivative of the regularization
p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is larger than
-C_{*}\exp(-4rM)/2
,
\kappa_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s})
is also bounded below by a positive value. Meanwhile, for large enough
\widetilde{s}
,
\rho_{+}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)/\kappa_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)
remains constant as
\widetilde{s}
grows. Thus there always exists an
\widetilde{s}
such that
\widetilde{s}/s\geq C_{1}\rho^{2}_{+}({2s}+2\widetilde{s},r)/\kappa^{2}_{-}(2s+2\widetilde{s},r)
. Therefore, Condition
1
holds with high probability.

4
Theoretical Results

To present the main theorem, we first need a condition on the folded concave penalty function.

Condition 2
.

The penalty function satisfies

1.

p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\cdot)
is a non-increasing continuous function defined on
[0,\infty)
;

2.

\lim_{x\rightarrow 0+}p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda
;

3.

There exists a constant
a_{1}>0
such that
p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\beta)=0
when
\beta>a_{1}\lambda
.

Condition
2
holds for two mainstream folded concave penalties: SCAD and MCP. For SCAD penalty,
a_{1}=a
; for MCP,
a_{1}=\gamma
. Here, both
a
and
\gamma
are user-picked parameters associated with the penalty functions, e.g.
a=3.7
for SCAD and
\gamma=3.0
for MCP are suggested in the papers where they were first proposed.

4.1
Statistical Properties

In TLAMM, we iteratively use LAMM to solve an optimization problem with localized linear approximation in (
2.6
) in the first stage and then directly optimize the penalized loss function (
2.4
) in the second stage. In this section, we prove statistical theory for the first-stage
\varepsilon_{1}
-optimal estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
and the second-stage
\varepsilon_{2}
-optimal estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
in Cox’s model. Indeed, when
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
are chosen properly, we shall prove that
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
is within
\mathcal{C}(s+\widetilde{s},r)
with high probability, based on which the second-stage estimator
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
can achieve the oracle property.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that Condition
1
holds. If
\lambda,\varepsilon_{1}
and
r
satisfy

2(||\nabla(\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*}))||_{\infty})+\varepsilon_{1}\leq\lambda\leq s^{-1}r\rho_{*}/36,

(4.1)

any
\varepsilon_{1}
-optimal solution
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
satisfies

||\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{2}\leq 18\rho_{*}^{-1}\lambda\sqrt{s}\quad\text{and}\quad||\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}\leq 36\rho_{*}^{-1}\lambda s.

(4.2)

This is a deterministic statement that bounds the estimation error after the first stage. With a properly selected
\lambda
,
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
is within an
\ell_{1}
ball with radius
r
around
\bm{\beta}^{*}
.
The following proposition characterizes the sparsity of
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
, which is also a deterministic result.

Proposition 2

Suppose that Condition

\lambda,\varepsilon_{1}
and

, i.e.
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
is
s+\widetilde{s}
sparse. Here
\widetilde{s}
is as described in Condition
1
.

To determine
\lambda
, we prove a tail probabilistic bound for
||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})||_{\infty}
as well
||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}||_{2}
.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup\limits_{t}||\bm{X}_{i}(t)||_{\infty}\leq M\right\}=1
,
then

\mathbb{P}\left(||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})||_{\infty}\geq 2M\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2p/\varepsilon_{0})}{n}}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{0},\quad\mathbb{P}\left(||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}||_{2}\geq 2M\sqrt{\frac{\left(\sqrt{-64\log\varepsilon_{0}}+1\right)s}{n}}\right)\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

Remark 4.1
.

From Proposition
3
, we know that with high probability
||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})||_{\infty}
in Cox’s model is at the order of
\sqrt{\log p/n}
. By taking
\lambda=C_{2}\sqrt{\log p/n}
with a large enough
C_{2}>0
and taking
\varepsilon_{1}\lesssim\sqrt{\log p/n}
, when the radius

in the LSE condition satisfies

, the condition in Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
holds.

Proposition
1
and Proposition
2
together suggest that
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
falls in
\mathcal{C}(s+\widetilde{s},r)
. This justifies the validity of directly optimizing the original nonconvex loss function (
2.4
) when starting from the warm initialization
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{1}
in the second stage.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Conditions
1
and
2
hold. Let
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{j:|\beta^{*}_{j}|\leq a_{1}\lambda\}
with
a_{1}
defined in Condition

. If
2\left(||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})||_{\infty}+\varepsilon_{2}\right)\leq\lambda\lesssim r/s
, then
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
satisfies the following inequality

||\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{2}\leq C_{3}\left(||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}||_{2}+\varepsilon_{2}\sqrt{s}+\lambda\sqrt{\left\lvert\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap S\right\rvert}\right)

where
C_{3}>0
is a constant.

Proposition
4
shows that the estimation error is upper bounded by the oracle rate, the optimization error and a bias term introduced by regularization. The bias only exists on
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cap S
where the signal strength is not strong enough. Based on this decomposition, we arrive at the following result, showing that under a condition on the true signal
\bm{\beta}^{*}
, the obtained estimator can achieve the weak oracle property.

Theorem 2

(Weak Oracle Property)
.

Suppose that Conditions
1
and
2
hold and
||\bm{\beta}^{*}_{S}||_{\min}\geq a_{1}\lambda
. Take
\lambda=4M\sqrt{2\log(2p/\varepsilon_{0})/n}
where

as in Proposition
3
,
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{1/n}
, then

\displaystyle||\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{2}\leq C_{4}\sqrt{\frac{s}{n}}

for some constant
C_{4}>0
with probability at least
1-\varepsilon_{0}
.

Before establishing the strong oracle property of
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
, we define the oracle estimator
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}
to be

\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{\text{supp}(\bm{\beta})=S}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}).

(4.3)

Theorem 3

(Strong Oracle Property)
.

Suppose that Conditions
1
and
2
hold,
||\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{\max}\leq\eta_{n}\lesssim\lambda

and
||\bm{\beta}_{S}^{*}||_{\min}\geq a_{1}\lambda+\eta_{n}
. Let
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2}
be the exact solution of the second stage, i.e.
\varepsilon_{2}=0
. Take
\lambda=2K\sqrt{2\log(2p/\varepsilon_{0})/n}
with some small

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{2}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}.

Remark 1
.

||\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{\max}\leq\eta_{n}\lesssim\lambda
is a mild condition since the order of the left hand side is related to the intrinsic dimension

while

grows with

. Theorem

4.2
Computational Theory

In this section, we study the computational complexity of TLAMM in terms of the number of iterations needed in each stage. We need an additional Lipschitz condition on the gradient of Cox’s loss function.

Condition 3

(Lipschitz Condition)
.

||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}_{1})-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}_{2})||_{2}\leq\rho_{c}||\bm{\beta}_{1}-\bm{\beta}_{2}||_{2}
, for
\bm{\beta}_{1},\bm{\beta}_{2}\in B_{2}(\bm{\beta}^{*},R/2)
, where
\rho_{c}
is a constant and
R\lesssim||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{2}+\lambda\sqrt{s}
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that Conditions
1
and
3
hold and take
\lambda\asymp\sqrt{\log p/n}
. With probability at least
1-p^{-1}
, we need at most
(1+\gamma_{u})^{2}R^{2}\rho_{c}^{2}/\varepsilon_{1}^{2}
LAMM iterations in the first stage to reach
\varepsilon_{1}
-optimal solution and at most
C_{5}\log(C_{6}\sqrt{s\log p/n}/\varepsilon_{2})
LAMM iterations in the second stage to reach
\varepsilon_{2}
-optimal solution, where
C_{5}>0
and
C_{6}>0
are two constants.

The sublinear rate in the first stage is due to the lack of strong convexity of the loss function, since TLAMM could tolerate an arbitrarily bad initialization. Once we enter the second stage, the strong convexity of the loss function allows the algorithm to admit a geometric convergence rate.

Fan et al. 2018
approximate the nonconvex loss function using a series of local linear approximations. In each stage, they solve an adaptive Lasso problem using the LAMM algorithm, and then update the tuning parameter using the gradient of the nonconvex penalty function at the latest solution for finer approximation in the next stage. The personalized tuning parameter for each entry gradually eliminates the shrinkage bias caused by the Lasso penalty. As a result, their algorithms requires
T\asymp\log\log p
stages after the first contraction stage to achieve good approximation and a total number of
(1+\gamma_{u})^{2}R^{2}\rho_{c}^{2}/\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+C_{5}\log\log p\cdot\log(C_{6}\sqrt{s\log p/n}/\varepsilon_{2})
LAMM iterations to complete the algorithm.

In comparison, TLAMM replaces the
T
approximation stages with one single stage that directly optimizes the nonconvex problem. As a consequence, the number of LAMM iterations needed is reduced to
(1+\gamma_{u})^{2}R^{2}\rho_{c}^{2}/\varepsilon_{1}^{2}+C_{5}\log(C_{6}\sqrt{s\log p/n}/\varepsilon_{2})
. This reduces the computational complexity while maintaining all the good statistical properties of the original I-LAMM algorithm.

7
Discussion

In this paper, we propose a two-stage algorithmic approach called TLAMM to fit the Cox’s proportional hazards model in high dimensions. The proposed algorithm achieves both statistical and computational guarantees: we show that the complexity of TLAMM is well controlled while the consistency of the estimator and the accuracy of the selection are guaranteed. Moreover, TLAMM has the potential to be extended to other common models such as linear regression and logistic regression, as long as the loss function shares a similar geometry locally around the true signal.

Lemma A.1

(
\ell_{1}
Cone Property For Approximate Solution in the Second Stage)
.

If
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon\leq\lambda
and
||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{\min}\geq a_{1}\lambda
, we must have

\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon}{\lambda-(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon)}\|{(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})}_{S}\|_{1},

(A.1)

where
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
is a stage estimator.

Proof of Lemma
A.1
.

Lemma
A.1
depicts the
\ell_{1}
cone property for the approximate solution in the second stage.

For any
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}\in\partial\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}
, let
\mathbf{u}=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})+p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})=\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})+\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
. By the Mean Value theory,there exists a
\gamma\in[0,1]
, such that
\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})-\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*})=\big[\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\big(\gamma\bm{\beta}^{*}+(1-\gamma)\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\big)\big]\big(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big)
. Write
\mathbf{H}=\nabla^{2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\big(\gamma\bm{\beta}^{*}+(1-\gamma)\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\big)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})\!+\!\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\!=\!\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\!+\!\bm{H}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})+\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle.

\displaystyle\!\leq\!\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\!-\!\bm{\beta}\|_{1}

Given that
||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{\min}\geq a_{1}\lambda
,
\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*})=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})-\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*}
where
\bm{\xi}^{*}\in\partial||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}
since
p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}^{*})=\bf 0
. Using the fact
(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})^{T}\mathbf{H}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})\geq 0
, we have

0\leq\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}-\underbrace{\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*}),\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle}_{\text{I}}-\underbrace{\big\langle\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle}_{\text{II}}.

(A.2)

Using a similar argument in the proof of Proposition
4
, we have

\text{I}\geq-\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}\|_{1},

and

\begin{split}\text{II}&=\big\langle\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*},\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle\\
&=\big\langle(\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*})_{S^{c}},(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\big\rangle+\big\langle(\lambda\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*})_{S},(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\big\rangle\\
&\geq\lambda\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}.\end{split}

(A.3)

In (
A.3
),
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\bm{\xi}^{*}=\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}
have the same sign as
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}=\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}
on
S^{c}
, making the first inner product equal to
\lambda\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}
while
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}-\bm{\xi}^{*}
have the same sign as
\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}
on
S
, making the second inner-product non-negative.
Plugging (
A.3
) into (
A.2
) and taking infimum with respect to
\widetilde{\bm{\xi}}\in\partial\|\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}
yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq-(\lambda-(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\omega_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})))\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\omega_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}))\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1},

or equivalently

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\omega_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})}{\lambda-(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\omega_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}))}\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}

Using the stopping criterion, i.e.
\omega_{\lambda}(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}})\leq\varepsilon
, we have that

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon}{\lambda-(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon)}\|(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}

Therefore we proved the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

If

Lemma A.4
.

We have

\displaystyle F(\bm{\beta}^{k},\lambda)-F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1},\lambda)\leq-\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}.

Proof of Lemma
A.3
.

Since
\bm{\beta}^{k}
is the exact solution to the
k
th iteration in the second stage,
the first order optimality condition holds: there exists a
\bm{\xi}^{k}\in\partial||\bm{\beta}^{k}||_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})+\phi(\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1})+\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k}=0.

Then for any
\mathbf{u}
such that
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1}=1
, we have

\begin{split}\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!+\!\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k},\mathbf{u}\rangle&\!=\!\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k}),\mathbf{u}\rangle\!-\!\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\!\phi(\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\mathbf{u}\big\rangle\\
&\!=\!\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\mathbf{u}\big\rangle\!-\!\big\langle\phi(\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\mathbf{u}\rangle\\
&\!\leq\!\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\|_{\infty}\!+\!\phi\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{\infty}\\
&\!\leq\!(\kappa_{+}+\phi)\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}\leq(1+\gamma_{u})\rho_{+}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}.\end{split}

(A.4)

In (
A.4
)
,\|\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\|_{\infty}\!
is upper bounded by
\kappa_{+}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}
because the estimators in the second stage are within the localized cone near
\bm{\beta}^{*}
where LSE holds with high probability.

The proof is completed by taking
\sup

\|\mathbf{u}\|_{1}\leq 1

Proof of Lemma
A.4
.

Recall the stopping criteria of the inflation of the quadratic isotropic parameter
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle F(\bm{\beta}^{k})-F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\leq\Psi_{\lambda,\phi}(\bm{\beta}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1})-F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}).

(A.5)

The convexity of
\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
implies

\displaystyle\lambda\|\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\|_{1}+\big\langle\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1}-\bm{\beta}^{k}\big\rangle.

Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle\!\!\!F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})

\displaystyle\!\geq\!\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\|_{1}+\big\langle\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1}-\bm{\beta}^{k}\big\rangle.

(A.6)

Given that

\begin{split}\Psi_{\lambda,\phi}(\bm{\beta}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1})=&\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\!\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\big\rangle+\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\!+p_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}^{k})\\

&+h(\bm{\beta}^{k})-h(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})-\!\big\langle h^{\prime}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\big\rangle\\
\leq&\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\!\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\big\rangle+\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\!+\lambda||\bm{\beta}^{k}||_{1}\end{split}

(A.7)

because of the concavity of function
h(\cdot)
, by
plugging (
A.6
) and (
A.7
) back into (
A.5
), we obtain

F(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!\leq\!-\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\!\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\!\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k},\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k}\rangle.

(A.8)

By the first order optimality condition, there exists some
\bm{\xi}^{k}
such that

\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})+\phi(\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1})+\lambda\bm{\xi}^{k}=0.

(A.9)

Plugging the optimality equation back to (

Lemma A.5

(Geometric Rate in the second stage)
.

Under the same conditions for Theorem
4
,
\{\bm{\beta}^{k}\}
converges geometrically,

\displaystyle F\big(\bm{\beta}^{k}\big)\!-\!F\big(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\Bigl(1\!-\!\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Bigr)^{k}\Big\{F(\bm{\beta}^{2,0})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\Big\}.

for some constant
\kappa>0
where
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is the global minimizer defined in (
2.4
).

Proof of Lemma
A.5
.

Define
\bm{\beta}(\alpha)=\alpha\widehat{\bm{\beta}}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\beta}^{k-1}
. Since
F(\bm{\beta}^{k})
is majorized at
\Psi(\bm{\beta}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1})
, we have

\displaystyle\!F(\bm{\beta}^{k})

\displaystyle\!\leq\!\Psi(\bm{\beta}^{k},\bm{\beta}^{k-1})

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\bm{\beta}(\alpha)}\Big\{{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!+\!\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1}),\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\rangle}\!+\!\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\!+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\Big\}

\displaystyle\!\leq\!\min_{\bm{\beta}(\alpha)}\Big\{F(\bm{\beta})+\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\},

where we restrict
\bm{\beta}
on the line segment
\alpha\widehat{\bm{\beta}}+(1-\alpha)\bm{\beta}^{k-1}
in the first inequality and the last inequality follows from the convexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})
.
Using the convexity of
F(\bm{\beta})
, we obtain that

\begin{split}F(\bm{\beta}^{k})&\!\leq\!\min_{\bm{\beta}(\alpha)}\Big\{F(\bm{\beta})+\frac{\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}\\
&\!\leq\!\min_{\alpha}\Big\{\alpha F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})+(1-\alpha)F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})+\frac{\alpha^{2}\phi}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k-1}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}\Big\}\\
&\!\leq\!\min_{\alpha}\Big\{F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!\alpha\big[F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\big]\!+\!\frac{\alpha^{2}\phi}{2}{\|\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\!-\!\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big\}.\end{split}

(A.10)

Next, we bound the last term in the inequality above. Applying Lemma
A.9
, we obtain

\displaystyle\|(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s},\penalty\ \|\bm{\beta}^{k-1}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}\leq C^{\prime}\lambda s\leq r,\penalty\ \|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq r,\penalty\ \mbox{and}\penalty\ \|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}.

Recall
\widehat{\bm{\xi}}
is some subgradient of
\|\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{1}
. Using the convexity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\cdot)
and the
\ell_{1}
-norm,

F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
can be bounded in the following way

\begin{split}F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})&\!\geq\!\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\!+\!\lambda\widehat{\bm{\xi}},\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\!-\!\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\big\rangle\!+\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})-\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}),\bm{\beta}^{k-1}-\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\rangle\right)\\
&\!\geq\!\frac{\kappa_{-}}{2}\|\bm{\beta}^{k-1}\!-\!\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2},\end{split}

(A.11)

where the last inequality is due to the first order optimality condition and the LSE condition.

Plugging (
A.11
) back to (
A.10
), we conclude that

\displaystyle F(\bm{\beta}^{k})

\displaystyle\!\leq\!\min\limits_{\alpha}\Big\{F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!\alpha\big[F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\!+\!\frac{\alpha^{2}\phi}{\kappa_{-}}\big[F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})\!-\!F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\big]\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})-\frac{\kappa_{-}}{4\phi}\big[F(\bm{\beta}^{k-1})-F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\big].

which, combining with the fact
\phi\leq\gamma_{u}\rho_{+}
, yields

\displaystyle F\big(\bm{\beta}^{k}\big)-F\big(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(1-\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Big)^{k}\Big\{F(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(0)})-F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\Big\},

in which
\kappa={\rho_{+}}/{\kappa_{-}}
.

Lemma A.6
.

Proof of Lemma
A.6
.

4
, we have

\displaystyle\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\lambda\sqrt{s}}{2\kappa_{-}}.

(A.12)

Next, we prove the desired bound for

\displaystyle F(\bm{\beta}^{*})\geq F(\bm{\beta})+\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}),\bm{\beta}^{*}-\bm{\beta}\big\rangle,

\displaystyle F(\bm{\beta})-F(\bm{\beta}^{*})\leq-\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta})+p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}),\bm{\beta}^{*}-\bm{\beta}\big\rangle\leq\varepsilon\|\bm{\beta}^{*}-\bm{\beta}\|_{1}.

On the other hand, we know from Lemma

\displaystyle\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}

(A.14)

Plugging (

Lemma A.7

(Basic Inequality)
.

Let
\lambda\!\geq\!2\left(\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}\!+\!\varepsilon\right)
. If
\|\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}
,
\bm{\beta}\in B_{2}(r,\bm{\beta}^{*})
and
F(\bm{\beta})-F(\bm{\beta}^{*})\leq C\lambda^{2}s
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{-}}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\leq\frac{5\lambda}{2}\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}+C\lambda^{2}s.

Proof of Lemma
A.7
.

Since
\|\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}
and
\|\bm{\beta}^{*}_{S^{c}}\|_{0}=0
, we have
\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}
. The localized sparse strong convexity implies that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*})+\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle+\frac{\kappa_{-}}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}).

(A.15)

Recall that
F(\bm{\beta})=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})+\lambda\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}
,

F(\bm{\beta})-F(\bm{\beta}^{*})\leq C\lambda^{2}s,

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})-\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*})+\lambda(\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}-\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1})\leq C\lambda^{2}s.

(A.16)

Plugging (
A.15
) into the left-hand side of (
A.16
), we immediately obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{-}}{2}\|\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\!C\lambda^{2}s-\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle\!+\!\lambda(\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}\!-\!\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1})

\displaystyle=\!C\lambda^{2}s\underbrace{-\big\langle\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\beta}\!-\!\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle}_{\text{I}}\!+\underbrace{\langle\lambda\bm{\xi}^{*},\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\rangle}_{\text{II}}+\!\underbrace{\lambda\left(\|\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{1}\!-\!\|\bm{\beta}\|_{1}\right)}_{\text{III}},

where
\bm{\xi}^{*}\in\partial||\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}
.
Following a similar argument in the proof of Proposition
4
, we have

\displaystyle\text{I}\leq\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty},

\displaystyle\text{II}\leq\lambda||(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}||_{1}

\displaystyle\text{III}\leq\lambda\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}-\lambda\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{-}}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+(\lambda-\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty})\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S^{c}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\leq(2\lambda+\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty})\|(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*})_{S}\|_{1}+C\lambda^{2}s.

The proof is finished by noticing that
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}\leq\lambda/2
.
∎

Lemma A.8
.

Let
\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\bm{\beta}^{*})\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon\leq\lambda/2
. If
\bm{\beta}\in B_{1}(r,\bm{\beta}^{*})
satisfies

\|\bm{\beta}_{S^{c}}\|_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}

F(\bm{\beta})-F(\bm{\beta}^{*})\leq C\lambda^{2}s,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\|_{2}\leq C^{\prime}\lambda\sqrt{s},

\displaystyle\big\langle\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta})-\nabla\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\bm{\beta}^{*}),\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}^{*}\big\rangle\leq C^{\prime 2}\kappa_{+}(s+\widetilde{s},r)\lambda^{2}s,

Proof of Lemma
A.8
.

Directly applying Lemma

.

We prove the theorem in three steps: first we show that
\left\{\bm{\beta}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to the global optimum
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
in (

); then we prove that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}

\bm{\beta}^{k}

According to Lemma
A.4

\displaystyle||\bm{\beta}^{k}-\bm{\beta}^{k-1}||_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\phi}\left(F(\bm{\beta}^{k})-F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\right)\leq\frac{2}{\phi}\left(1-\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\right)^{k}\left(F(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{(2,0)})-F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})\right).

Therefore
\left\{\bm{\beta}^{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converge as
k
grows and
\left\{F(\bm{\beta}^{k})\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
converges to
F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})
.

Then we show
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}=\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}
with contradiction. Since
||\nabla\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0})||_{\infty}<\lambda/2
and
\lim\limits_{x\rightarrow 0}\left\lvert p^{\prime}_{\lambda}(x)\right\rvert=\lambda
, we have
\nabla F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0})=\nabla\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0})+p_{\lambda}(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0})={\bf 0}
, which suggests that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}
is a local minimizer of (
2.4
).

4
and Proposition
2
, we derive that

||\widehat{\bm{\beta}}||_{0}\leq\widetilde{s}\quad\text{and}\quad||\widehat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}^{*}||_{1}\lesssim\lambda s\leq r,

i.e. the global minimizer of (
2.4
)
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
is also within the sparsity cone
\mathcal{C}(s+\widetilde{s},r)
around
\bm{\beta}^{*}
. Suppose that
\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0}\neq\widehat{\bm{\beta}}
and
F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}})<F(\widehat{\bm{\beta}}^{0})
. The convexity of

suggests that for any

\leq&\frac{5\lambda}{6}\end{split}

(F.2)

and

(F.3)

(

we have

. This also holds for all the steps after the

-th.

Therefore, an exact solution in the second stage would achieve the global minimum in the end.
∎

\rho_{-}\leq\phi\leq\gamma_{u}\phi
in the last inequality.
Since the sequence
\{F(\bm{\beta}^{k})\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
decrease monotonically, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{\beta}^{k+1})

\displaystyle\!\leq\!(1+\gamma_{u})\rho_{+}\sqrt{2\rho_{-}^{-1}(F(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!F(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2}))}

\displaystyle\!\leq\!(1+\gamma_{u})\rho_{+}\sqrt{2\rho_{-}^{-1}\Big(1\!-\!\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Big)^{k}\left(F(\bm{\beta}^{k})\!-\!F(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{2})\right)}

\displaystyle\!\leq\!(1+\gamma_{u})\rho_{+}\sqrt{3\kappa_{-}^{-1}\rho_{-}^{-1}\Big(1-\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Big)^{k}\lambda^{2}s}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{3}(1+\gamma_{u})\kappa\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Big)^{k}\lambda^{2}s}

where
\kappa=\rho_{+}/\kappa_{-}
, the second inequality is due to Lemma
A.5
, and the last one due to Lemma
A.9
.
Therefore, to ensure that
\bm{\beta}^{k+1}
satisfies
\omega_{\lambda}(\bm{\beta}^{k+1})\leq\varepsilon
, it suffices to choose
k
such that

\displaystyle\!\sqrt{3}(1+\gamma_{u})\kappa\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac{1}{4\gamma_{u}\kappa}\Big)^{k}\lambda^{2}s}\leq\varepsilon.

Equivalently, we obtain

\displaystyle k\geq 2C^{\prime}\log\Big(C^{\prime\prime}\frac{\lambda\sqrt{s}}{\varepsilon}\Big),

where

C^{\prime}=2/\log(4\gamma_{u}\kappa/\{4\gamma_{u}\kappa-1)\},\penalty\ C^{\prime\prime}=\sqrt{3}(1+\gamma_{u})\kappa.

∎
