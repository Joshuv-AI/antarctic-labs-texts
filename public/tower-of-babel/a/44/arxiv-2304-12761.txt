Focusing on Information Context for ITS using a Spatial Age of Information Model
Julian Heinovski, Jorge Torres Gómez, Falko Dressler

Abstract
New technologies for sensing and communication act as enablers for cooperative driving applications. Sensors are able to detect objects in the surrounding environment and information such as their current location is exchanged among vehicles. In order to cope with the vehicles’ mobility, such information is required to be as fresh as possible for proper operation of cooperative driving applications. The age of information has been proposed as a metric for evaluating freshness of information; recently also within the context of intelligent transportation systems. We investigate mechanisms to reduce the age of information of data transported in form of beacon messages while controlling their emission rate. We aim to balance packet collision probability and beacon frequency using the average peak age of information as a metric. This metric, however, only accounts for the generation time of the data but not for application-specific aspects, such as the location of the transmitting vehicle. We thus propose a new way of interpreting the age of information by considering information context, thereby incorporating vehicles’ locations. As an example, we characterize such importance using the orientation and the distance of the involved vehicles. In particular, we introduce a weighting coefficient used in combination with the peak age of information to evaluate the information freshness, thus emphasizing on information from more important neighbors. We further design the beaconing approach in a way to meet a given age of information requirement, thus, saving resources on the wireless channel while keeping the age of information minimal. We illustrate the effectiveness of our approach in Manhattan-like urban scenarios, reaching pre-specified targets for the age of information of beacon messages.

Introduction

Vehicles are becoming more capable in terms of sensors and computing power, we can already see first deployments of intelligent transportation systems.
They are able to do real-time monitoring of their surrounding environment, which allows detection of objects such as other vehicles or pedestrians.
Such a detection can help in taking driving decisions and might be performed collaboratively among vehicles.
Vehicle-to-everything communication technologies such as IEEE 802.11p (often referred to as distributed short-range communication), and cellular V2X allow exchanging of information with other vehicles or roadside infrastructure.
Such communication capability enables cooperative driving applications, which bring a set of new features and services for today’s driving, but also demands new communication strategies for proper operation
[
1
]
.

Such applications require fresh (up-do-date) information from other vehicles to operate properly.
Due to the inherent mobility of intelligent transportation systems, location-information may become outdated and eventually no longer relevant to an application because of position changes.
To ensure up-to-date information, vehicles exchange their location with regular
beacons
messages, such as cooperative awareness messages
[
2
]
.
Freshness of such information does not only depend on frequent beacons’ transmissions but also on the network capacities, which can lead to packet collisions and the loss of data.

Balancing requirements to update beacons regularly and network capacities, the concept of the age of information (AoI) provides a framework to evaluate information freshness
[
3
,
4
]
.
The age of information metric complements raw delay, loss, and throughput in the network, which in turn accounts for the process of emission and delays introduced in the communication chain, all-together
[
3
]
.
Inherently, this distinguishes age of information metrics from conventional delay metrics
[
5
]
, allowing to optimize of the network freshness as the best balance between throughput and delay.

Recent research illustrates the use of age of information metrics in the vehicular context.
The average age of information and the peak age of information are used to find the best strategy for the emission rate of beacon packets, see for instance
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
(further discussion in the next Section).
However, these metrics do not consider information context
[
13
]
; not all packets necessarily carry the same information’s importance, thereby not introducing the same level of freshness for the status update.
To illustrate the significance of information context, let us consider an intersection collision avoidance scenario as depicted in
Figure

Focusing on vehicle

Thus, it is preferable to collect updated information about vehicles

In this sense, we intent for an age of information-based metric to account for this application-dependent information context.

In the literature, some reported studies address the information’s content with the age of information.
Examples include techniques

In a different approach, we consider the transmitting vehicle’s location as a metric to quantify the importance of their information.
We emphasize such importance with weighting coefficients to evaluate the average peak age of information metric in the context of cooperative driving.
These coefficients inherently devise some filtering of received packets, which allows for treating vehicles’ information more selectively.
Using our model allows focusing on timely updates of relevant vehicles only for meeting a given age of information requirement.

In this paper, which is an extension of the previous work in

In addition, we demonstrate the practical use of the proposed model by applying it to an age of information-based beacon adaption algorithm.

Accounting for the age of information metrics, other works use analytic methods for the average age of information

The average age of information metric is formulated mainly in two different approaches.
On one hand,
Lyamin et al. 2020

Vinel et al. 2009
straightly formulate the average age of information as the average of the time duration between two consecutive received packets.
They assume that the time duration distributes according to the joint event where two transmissions do not collide in the channel.
The channel collision probability is evaluated according to the formulation provided by
Vinel et al. 2009
.

On the other hand, the average age of information is evaluated using the formula for the remaining service time in a queue
[
8
,
11
]
and considering the hidden
[
8
,
11
]
and non-hidden node problem scenario
[
10
]
.
In the hidden node scenario, the time duration of message transmissions is expanded, assuming that hidden nodes transmit independently with a random phase between
0
and the transmission duration parameter.
In the non-hidden scenario,
Andrea et al. 2021
also derive a formula for node and network levels.
The node-level accounts for the average age of information at any arbitrary node, assuming they only transmit the most recent packet.
The network level evaluates the case where nodes do not transmit new packets till the current one is sent.
In this case the network is modeled according to a Markov chain model, the transition probabilities can be derived
[
19
]
.

In a different direction, based on simulation results, the existence of a unique beacon period minimizing the average age of information is illustrated for a certain number of vehicles, and contention window sizes
[
6
]
.
Following these results, a rate control algorithm is derived from adapting the broadcast period based on local measurements of the average age of information.
The vehicle reduces or increases the beacon period by comparing it to the estimated average age of information metric looking for the maximum network freshness.

All the above studies conduct simulations based on the IEEE 802.11p standard for single-hop
[

,

,

,

,

and multi-hop

]

Besides, a variety of scenarios for the traffic of vehicles have been studied.
Examples include four lane roads
[

]

[

,

, platooning
[

,

[

[

[

Furthermore, the work in
[

However, these reported solutions are only addressing protocol parameters (e.g., beacon rate, contention window size) and the channel impact (e.g., collisions, noise) to formulate the average age of information metric.
Addressing information context,
Michalopoulou et al. 2021
seek to minimize the information aging in the spatial dimension when evaluating the product of the speed of the vehicle and the time duration between received packets.
The information age is reduced by stating the optimization problem in the spatial domain to minimize the predicted location error.
Also in the spatial domain,
Parella et al. 2022
implement a penalty function for the age of information, addressing a better prediction trajectory of neighboring vehicles.
The penalty function (dependent on the age of information of cooperative awareness message messages) is defined using the distance difference between the true and predicted position of the neighboring vehicles.
The importance of information can also be considered as a weight factor on the average age of information metric.

Zhang et al. 2022
evaluate the weight while balancing the message’s priority, persistence, and reliability.

In a different approach, we introduce a degree of importance in the age of information metrics concerning the intended direction of the vehicle and its surrounding.
In the form of weighting coefficients as formulated by
Sorkhoh et al. 2020
, we incorporate into the average peak age of information metric the vehicular context (direction, surrounding), looking for some meaning of received beacon packets
[
4
]
.
In doing so, we study the peak age of information metric weighting as more critical for those vehicles in the direction of movement (cf.
Figure

1
) and with less importance otherwise, when considering a safety application such as intersection collision avoidance
[
24
]
as an example use case.
We use this modified peak age of information metric and compare it to a similarly modified age of information requirement of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
, which is often used in literature as desired update interval
[
25
,
26
]
.
We thus identify how many vehicles have fresh information, similar to using the original age of information definition.

3
A Spatial Model for the AoI

Typically, the age of information metrics are measured per user irrespective of their location.
All the packets received from surrounding vehicles are treated with equal importance.
However, the level of importance is application dependent:
E.g., while in platooning only the members of the platoon itself are relevant, it is the surrounding vehicles within the direction of movement that are important for safety-related applications such as intersection collision avoidance.
As one example use case,
Figure

1
thus shows such an intersection scenario.
Here, the most valuable information for vehicle
1
will be located in the direction of its movement (shadowed area).
Thus, vehicles within this area (i.e., vehicles
23
) should be assigned a higher level of importance than other vehicles in the surrounding.
Beacon packets coming from vehicles in the rear of the intended direction will not be that informative about the traffic in the intended direction of vehicle
1
.
Therefore, vehicles in front will be more demanded to reduce the corresponding age of information metrics than the vehicles in the rear.
Since all vehicles are equal in the standard age of information, frequent beacon transmissions from the less important vehicles can lead to unnecessary channel load in this case.
If the communication protocol was aware of this application-specific level of importance, it could update the periodicity of the beacons in accordance and eventually reduce the load on the wireless channel.

3.1
Weighted Peak AoI

Figure 2:
Visual representation of our spatial model for calculating the weighting coefficient used for the age of information interpretation with an example configuration of
\alpha=0.75{},\beta=0.005{}
.

To consider the location of the transmitting vehicle as a metric of importance to the information, we propose a new way of interpreting the age of information.
To that end, we introduce a weighting coefficient that is applied to the peak age of information metric as well as to an age of information requirement, emphasizing on packets from important vehicles.
Thereby, we introduce some level of selectivity for the received packets which allows to treat vehicles’ information differently according to the importance to the application.
Our model can be easily adjusted to the requirement of a specific application through parameters and does not modify the underlying age of information metric itself.

We choose the weighting coefficient as a raised-cosine function with a decay factor as

\displaystyle\omega_{j,i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos(\alpha\theta_{i,j})\right)e^{-\beta\parallel\vec{d}_{i,j}\parallel}\quad,

(1)

where
\theta_{i,j}
is the angle between the transmitting vehicle
j
and the direction of movement of vehicle
i
and
\parallel\vec{d}_{i,j}\parallel
is the distance between both vehicles, while
\alpha
and
\beta
are two coefficients to select the degree of selectivity in the spatial domain.
The coefficient
\alpha
provides selectivity in the radial direction, while
\beta
in the azimuth direction.
The larger the value of
\alpha
or
\beta
is, the narrower is the beam of vehicle
i
.

Figure

2
shows a visual representation of our weighting coefficient with an example configuration of
\alpha=0.75{},\beta=0.005{}
.

To derive the angle and the distance between vehicles, we assume that vehicles are equipped with global positioning system receivers, and that this information is exchanged between vehicles in periodic beaconing messages.
Using the configuration in
Figure

2
,
\omega_{j,i}
is close to
1
whenever vehicle
j
is in the direction of movement of vehicle
i
.
Otherwise, it is close to
0
whenever vehicle
j
is away, being
0
when vehicle
j
is located at the rear of vehicle
i
.

Figure 3:
Visual representation of our spatial model for calculating the weighting coefficient used for the age of information interpretation with an example configuration of
\alpha=0.5,\beta=0
.
This configuration only takes the angle between vehicles
i
and
j
into account.

Figure 4:
Visual representation of our spatial model for calculating the weighting coefficient used for the age of information interpretation with an example configuration of
\alpha=0,\beta=0.0075
.
This configuration only takes the distance between vehicles
i
and
j
into account.

In order to parameterize our proposed model, one needs to select values for the model parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
.
Since these will determine the relevance of vehicles’ information, their values have to be selected carefully and application-dependent.
In order to give further intuition on the behavior of these,
Figures

3
and
4
show visual representations of two more example configurations.
In
Figure

3
, the distance between vehicles is not used for calculating the weighting coefficient
\omega_{j,i}
due to a value of
0
for
\beta
.
Thus, the area around the vehicle
i
is mapped to the coefficient
\omega_{j,i}
by the cosine-part of
Equation

1
, using the angle towards vehicle
j
.
Here, a close vehicle and a far-away vehicle will receive the same value for
\omega_{j,i}
if their angle towards vehicle
i
is the same.
In contrast, in
Figure

4
, the angle between vehicles is not used for calculating the weighting coefficient due to a value of
0
for
\alpha
.
Thus, the distance from vehicle
i
to other vehicles is mapped to the coefficient
\omega_{j,i}
by part with Euler’s number of
Equation

1
.

With this coefficient, we measure the importance of the introduced age per received packet using the average peak age of information metric as

\Delta^{(\omega)}_{j,i}=\omega_{j,i}\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}\quad,

(2)

where
\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}
denotes the average peak age of information metric for a link between vehicles
j
and
i
.
Correspondingly, the combined peak age of information per neighboring vehicle
j
at vehicle
i
will be calculated after averaging the perceived
\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}
as

\Delta^{(\omega)}_{i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}{\omega_{j,i}\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}}\quad.

(3)

Finally, we account for the network operation after averaging for the total of nodes as

\Delta^{(\omega)}=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}{\omega_{j,i}}\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}\quad.

(4)

The average peak age of information (
\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}
) can be derived by analytical or numerical means through simulators.
Analytically, it can be obtained after calculating the expected average of the inter-arrival (
Y_{n}
) and system time (
T_{n}
) as
\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}=\mathbb{E}\{Y\}+\mathbb{E}\{T\}
when all packets emitted are received
[
13
]
.
However, some packets will not be successfully received due to the system and channel conditions (e.g., collisions, replacement of the old beacon frames, low reliability during their reception, etc.)
[
19
]
.
Taking into account the impact of the system and channel effects in the packet reception process, as given by the successful probability
P_{sd}
, the average peak age of information can be calculated as

\Delta^{(p)}_{j,i}=\frac{1}{P_{sd}}\mathbb{E}\{Y\}+\mathbb{E}\{T\}\quad,

(5)

where the value for
P_{sd}
in
Equation

5
can be directly computed considering the impact of noise and collisions using the closed-form expressions in
[
19
, Eq. (7)]
, or through simulations.

The
Equation

5
can be directly computed by recalling the use of the negative binomial distribution as described in
[
27
]
.
However, it can be intuitively derived based on the meaning of the related variables in
Equation

5
.

P_{sd}
can be interpreted as the ratio of successfully transmitted packets; thus, its inverse will provide the total of attempts to have a successful transmission.
Therefore, the first term in
Equation

5
will provide the waiting period before a packet is successfully transmitted.
Adding the average time spent on the system (
\mathbb{E}\{T\}
) will thus provide the average peak age of information.

3.2
Remarks

The introduced coefficients in
Equation

3
provide a mean to “filter” packets according to their relevance.
For instance, in the intersection scenario depicted in
Figure

1
, the average peak age of information of packets from the vehicles
456
will be lowered as less relevant, thus emphasizing those packets from vehicles at the front side of vehicle
1
(vehicles
23
).
In this way, the resulting average peak age of information will be characterized the most by those links of interest according to the application context.

Equation

3
is also useful in different intelligent transportation system scenarios, and, also with a different dependency for the coefficients other than
Equation

1
.
Overall, the
Equation

3
comprises a mean to emphasize some communication links in contrast to others.
Once the links of interest are determined, they will shape the resulting average peak age of information whenever their corresponding coefficients are close to
1
.
In contrast, those links whose coefficients are close to
0
will not contribute to the age of information metric.

Besides, we selected the dependency of the coefficients with the spatial coordinates in
Equation

1
as a two-dimensional function in two separable terms.
One dimension for the azimuth direction defines the raised-cosine function
[
28
]
, which conveniently allows multiplying by
0
to those packets coming from the rear side of vehicle
i
.
The second dimension is in the radial direction and defines a decay factor, which decrements as long as the distance increases.
Overall, both terms let to a function that is also all-orders differentiable, which accounts for its mathematical tractability.

3.3
Weighted Target AoI

The derived weighted peak age of information metric can be used to fairly evaluate the freshness of the status updates with a given target, i.e., when the age of received packets is less than a given threshold.
This approach is particularly relevant when we want to save resources looking at the peak age of information metric just performing below a given threshold
T
(target).
In this way, we avoid the network to operate on the minimum average where demanding resources are higher.
After applying the same weighting coefficient (cf.
Equation

1
) to a given threshold
T
by

T^{(\omega)}_{j,i}={\omega_{j,i}}T\quad,

(6)

we can compare the derived average peak age of information with the weighted target
T^{(\omega)}_{j,i}

\Delta^{(\omega)}_{j,i}\leq T^{(\omega)}_{j,i}

(7)

for a link between vehicles
j
and
i
.
A single vehicle
i
can to this for all of its neighboring vehicles
j
by

T^{(\omega)}_{i}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}{\omega_{j,i}}T_{j,i}

(8)

and

\Delta^{(\omega)}_{i}\leq T^{(\omega)}_{i}\quad.

(9)

Correspondingly, we account for the network operation after averaging for the total of nodes as

T^{(\omega)}=\frac{1}{N(N-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}{\omega_{j,i}}T_{j,i}

(10)

and

\Delta^{(\omega)}\leq T^{(\omega)}\quad.

(11)

Further discussions on the utility of these expressions and their interpretation are given within
Section

5
.

4
Adaptive Beaconing based on AoI

In this section, we aim to show the practical use of our spatial model for cooperative driving by applying it to an algorithm for adapting the beacon rate based on the age of information.
We use a simple proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller-based algorithm that controls a vehicle’s beacon rate based on peak age of information measurements from neighboring vehicles.
In contrast to other work from the literature
[
6
,
29
]
, our goal is to keep peak age of information values below a fixed requirement (
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
), rather than achieving minimal age of information for all nodes.
Applying our spatial model should help to focus on relevant vehicles while selecting the beacon rates.

4.1
Communication Setup

Figure

\Delta^{(p)}_{i}
locally at vehicle

, according to

.
Since the information in the beacons of vehicle

are relevant to vehicles

and

, the age of information as well as the information’s relevance (given by the weighting coefficient) needs to be computed at vehicles

and

.
As only vehicle

.

4.2
Beacon Adaption Algorithm

We use a proportional-integral-derivative controller
[
30
]
on all vehicles to adjust their beacon rate such that peak age of information values converge to the target age of information.
To achieve this, we use the peak age of information as input and the target age of information as set-point for the proportional-integral-derivative controller.
A proportional-integral-derivative controller in general consists of
(1) a
P
roportional part, which produces a control output proportional to the error between the set-point and the actual output,
(2) an
I
ntegral part, which accumulates the error over time and generates an output proportional to the integrated error,
and (3) a
D
erivative part, which produces an output proportional to the rate of change of the error.
The controller output is given by

u(t)=G_{P}\cdot e(t)+G_{I}\cdot\int e(t)\penalty\ dt+G_{D}\cdot\frac{de(t)}{dt}\quad,

(12)

where

u(t)
is the controller output at time
t
,

e(t)
is the error (difference to the set-point) at time
t
,

\int e(t)\penalty\ dt
is the integral of the error over time (the accumulated error),
and
\frac{de(t)}{dt}
is the derivative of the error with respect to time (the rate of change of the error).

G_{P}
,
G_{I}
, and
G_{D}
are gain coefficients to control the impact of the individual parts of the proportional-integral-derivative controller towards the output.

As input and set-point for the proportional-integral-derivative controller, we specifically use the average weighted peak age of information measurement
\Delta^{(\omega)}_{i}
and the average weighted target age of information
T^{(\omega)}_{i}
, respectively.
Thereby, we expect the controller to eventually select beacon rates that result in the peak age of information measurements converging to the target age of information.
We implement the individual terms of
Equation

12
in the following way.
The error
e(t)
at vehicle
i
in iteration
t
is given by

e(t)=T^{(\omega)}_{i}-\Delta^{(\omega)}_{i}

(13)

and the derivate error
de(t)
evaluated in the discrete domain as

\frac{de(t)}{dt}\approx\frac{e(t-\Delta t)-e(t)}{\Delta t}\quad,

(14)

using only the error from the previous algorithm execution.
The gain coefficients for the individual parts of the proportional-integral-derivative controller are chosen by observing the stable system behavior.
We use
G_{p}=1.0
,
G_{I}=0
, thereby disabling the integral part, and
G_{D}=0.1
.

Using
Equation

12
, the adjustment
u(t)
of the current beacon interval
\lambda_{i}
at vehicle
i
in iteration
t
is calculated and added to the current beacon interval
\lambda_{i}
.
We update the new beacon interval within the limits of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
(
100\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
) and
100\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
(
0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
), such that only valid beacon rates are produced.
The algorithm is executed on a regular basis (i.e., at an interval of
2\penalty\ \times
target age of information) on all vehicles individually.

5.3
Impact of Link Distance on Standard AoI

In order to underline the issues with the standard age of information, we first have a look at the impact of the link distance on the age of information.
The peak age of information, as defined in
Equation

5
, is influenced by the beacon rate (cf.
\mathbb{E}\{Y\}
), system delay (cf.
\mathbb{E}\{T\}
), and probability of successful packet reception (cf.
\frac{1}{P_{sd}}
).
Thus, even if multiple vehicles use the same beacon rate for beacon transmission, the observed peak age of information metrics can be very different due to the effects of the wireless communication channel, especially over large distances.
Since the longest possible distance between two vehicles in our scenario is only about
780\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, we can neglect the system delay as an influencing factor.

The probability for successful reception of a packet, however, has to be considered.
It depends on the signal-to-noise-and-interference-ratio, which is, among others, influenced by scenario-related effects such as attenuation of the signal as well as interference from other vehicles.
In our scenario, the signal is attenuated by free-space path loss, which weakens its strength proportional to the link distance.
Also, at large distances, hidden nodes can introduce additional interference and collisions, which further degrades the signal-to-noise-and-interference-ratio.
At some point, a packet cannot be received successfully anymore and the age of information of the corresponding link increases further until the next successful reception.
Therefore, the link distance can have a huge impact on the peak age of information, especially for far away vehicles.

Figure 9:
Average peak age of information based on the link distance (rounded to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) with different beacon rates

Figure

9
shows the average peak age of information per link distance (rounded to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) with different beacon rates that we obtained from the simulation.
Indeed, we see that the link distance has an impact on the peak age of information according to our hypothesis described previously.

For small link distances (less than
50\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) and low beacon rates (e.g.,
1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
), the observed peak age of information closely follows the beacon interval (i.e., the multiplicative inverse of the beacon rate) as the signal distortion due to the impact of the wireless communication channel is minimal.
However, at higher beacon rates (e.g.,
16\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
), the effect is increased and becomes visible more clearly.
Latest at roughly
400\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, we start to see a massive increase in peak age of information, which is way above the beacon interval.
Here, the probability for a successful reception of a packet is so low that many updates are lost and the peak age of information increases a lot.
For very high rates (e.g.,
40\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
and above), the peak age of information already steeply rises at even low distances of below
100\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
.
As a result, we see that, even when using the same beacon rate, two links can have a very different peak age of information because of different node distances.
Hence, the freshness of the information from close vehicles is typically much better than from those far away, as expected.

5.4
Combined Standard AoI

Consider an arbitrary cooperative driving application that requires regular updates from surrounding vehicles (e.g., intersection collision avoidance, cf.
Section

3
).
This application will likely define a target age of information (i.e., maximum allowed age of information) that is required by the application to work successfully and reliably.
In order to determine whether certain information is fresh enough, the average peak age of information can be evaluated against this requirement.
A typical update interval requirement that is often found in literature is
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}

[
25
,
26
]
.
Following the observation from the previous section, far away vehicles will always suffer from a weakened signal-to-noise-and-interference-ratio and thus have a higher peak age of information compared to vehicles that are close.
Thus, when combining the peak age of information values from all surrounding vehicles using
Equation

3
, the far away vehicles will increase the average and thus distort the view on the overall information freshness.

.

Figure

shows the average standard peak age of information of short (i.e.,

) and long (i.e.,

) distance links for several beacon rates.
It also shows a static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
as well as the average of the peak age of information values from the two links (cf.
Equation

3
).
As expected, the average peak age of information of the short link distance (i.e.,
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) continuously decreases proportional to the increasing beacon rate.
Here, the minimum value, which indicates the best information freshness, is reached at the highest simulated beacon rate (i.e.,
100\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
.
At this distance, the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
is already reached at a beacon rate close to

This is expected, since
10\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}

When looking at the long link distance (i.e.,
500\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
), the situation is different:
First, the average peak age of information is decreasing similarly to the short link distance, following the increase of the beacon rate.
But it never reaches the target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
.
Instead, after reaching its minimum at
20\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
, the average peak age of information increases when beacons are transmitted at higher rates.
That is due to effects of the wireless communication channel described in
Section

5.3
.

When looking at the combination (i.e., average) of the two link distances, we can observe some interesting effects as well.
First, the average peak age of information value decreases as expected, but when increasing the beacon rate further, also its value decreases further, thus reaching the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
at roughly
20\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
(red circle).
The continuous decrease even at higher beacon rates (i.e., above
20\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
) is due to less received packets for the long distance link.
Thus, the combined peak age of information contains a lot more low values which have been observed from the short link distance.

When we now compare the beacon rates at which the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
is met, we see that twice the beacon rate of the short distance links is required when combining the peak age of information metrics from both distances.
Hence, in order to keep the combined freshness of the information from all surrounding vehicles below the given age of information requirement, beacons need to be transmitted at a higher rate than required for close vehicles only.
If vehicles now have different levels of relevance to the application, e.g., close vehicles are more important than far ones (cf.
Section

3
), the non-relevant vehicles (i.e., the far ones) will weaken the perceived combined information freshness.
In order to meet the age of information requirement, all vehicles need to transmit their beacons at a higher rate, which leads to unnecessary transmissions and channel load.

5.5
Combined Weighted AoI

In order to cope with the issues of the standard age of information (i.e., effects of the wireless communication channel and equal importance of all nodes), we now apply the proposed spatial model from
Section

3
to the age of information.
Using
Equation

1
, we calculate the weighting coefficient
\omega
for every peak age of information value that is observed for an arbitrary link between two vehicles
i,j
, producing a
weighted peak age of information
.
Additionally, we also apply the weighting coefficients to the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
on a per link bases, producing a
weighted target age of information
.
In this section, we use one exemplary parameterization (i.e.,
\alpha=0,\beta=0.01
) of our spatial model that uses only the distance between vehicles for calculating the weighting coefficient.
This focuses on the issue described in
Section

5.3
.

It also shows an average weighted target age of information as well as the average peak age of information values from the two links, which can be used by application as a view on the overall information freshness.
Since we selected fixed distances, the calculated weighting coefficient will be the same for all links of the same distance.
The resulting average weighted peak age of information is just a multiplication of the average standard peak age of information from

Due to the selected parameterization of our spatial model, a high (i.e., close to

As a result, the average peak age of information for the short distance links is very close to the one of the standard age of information from

Therefore, and due to less observations for the long distance links in general, the combination (i.e., average) of all peak age of information values from both distances is, in comparison to

Thus, the overall view on the information freshness is not distorted much by the long distance link, which is (in our parameterization) less relevant.

The average weighted target age of information is constructed by combining all weighted target age of information values from the two link distances, similarly to the average weighted peak age of information.
Since the same weighting coefficients are applied to the peak age of information and the target age of information, the target faces similar effects:
For the short distance, the target is close to the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
, whereas for the long distance, it is close to
0
due to the weighting coefficient being close to
0
.
The average weighted target age of information thus is close to the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
as it is mostly influenced by short distance links.
Note that the individual weighted target age of information for both distances is constant for all beacon rates as the link distance does not change and the beacon rate is not considered when calculating the weighting coefficient.
The average weighted target age of information, in contrast, is not constant due to the increasing number of lost packets and thus less values for the long link distance with high beacon rates.
The average therefore tends towards the value of the short link distance, when using a beacon rate
\geq$20\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
.

When comparing the average peak age of information with the target age of information, we see that now both link distances as well as their combination intersect with the target age of information at some point.
The short distance links meet the target at a beacon rate close to
20\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
.
The average peak age of information of the long distance links is below the weighted target age of information even for all beacon rates.
This is due to the average weighted target age of information mainly begin influenced by the short distance links, thus, tending towards the static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
.
Additionally, the weighting coefficient for the long distance links are close to
0
.
The combined weighted peak age of information reaches the weighted target age of information at a beacon rate close to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
, as indicated by the red circle.
This is a smaller beacon rate than required for only the short distance links due to the impact of the long distance links on the average.
However, we actually need to compare this situation with the values from using the standard peak age of information and the static target age of information in
Section

5.3
:
With the spatial model, we only need half of the previous beacon rate to reach the target age of information when combining all link distances.
Using our model allows to focus on timely updates of relevant vehicles for meeting a given age of information requirement instead, which saves channel resources.

5.9
Impact of the Scenario

Until now, we had disabled all movement of the vehicles as well as the attenuation of the wireless signal by buildings in our Manhattan grid simulation scenario.
We did this such that we can clearly observe the effects of our spatial model.
It is obvious that this is not realistic and that enabling both of these will influence the wireless channel available to the vehicles.

When considering buildings, the wireless signal will be attenuated, leading to unsuccessful transmissions for links with medium and long distances, thus increasing their age of information (if a message is received at all).
In contrast, the age of information for short link distances will likely be improved due to less interference from far-away vehicles.
We expect this effect in particular within our Manhattan grid scenario as line-of-sight between vehicles exists rarely, mainly only for vehicles on the same road or within the same road corridor.
Additionally, every building’s wall attenuates the signal of non line-of-sight links even more.
Thus, the interference domain for every receiving vehicles is reduced a lot in comparison to the same scenario without buildings.

When considering mobility, the distance as well as the signal quality of the links will change over time as vehicles drive around.
A former short distance link between two vehicles with a good signal quality can become a medium or even long distance one with worse quality (and age of information), and the other way around.
However, in a free-space scenario, we expect these changes and the differences between vehicles to be limited and the link quality to stay rather equal as vehicles can only drive a certain distance in a given time and continuously have a line-of-sight connection.

When considering the real-world scenario, i.e., combining attenuation by buildings and vehicle mobility, both of the aforementioned effects are mixed:
At one point in time, an arbitrary link can have a good quality with a low age of information, but at a different point in time, the same link can have a very high age of information or be blocked completely because vehicles changed their position.
The opposite can happen as well: vehicles that did not have a line-of-sight connection before, leading to bad link quality and age of information values, can suddenly be on the same road corridor leading to a good line-of-sight connection.

6
Discussion & Remarks

Throughout this work, we used a static target age of information of
100\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
, since this value is often used in literature as a desired update interval for information used by cooperative driving applications.
This value, however, is arbitrary and can freely be configured dependent on the specific needs of the chosen application.
Our proposed spatial model is independent of the actual value for this target age of information, since the same weighting coefficient is applied to both, the observed peak age of information values as well as the static target age of information.
When modifying the value of the target age of information, the intersection point with the average peak age of information will be shifted along the x-axes, leading to a different beacon rate that is sufficient for meeting the target.
When the target value is increased, this beacon rate decreases and vice versa.
In case both, a small target age of information and a low beacon rate, is desired, it can be beneficial to use a stricter configuration of our spatial model (i.e., lager values for
\alpha
and
\beta
).
This will impose a greater selectivity in the importance of vehicles and cope with effects of the wireless communication channel as described in
Section

5.3
.

We chose to aggregate peak age of information values from multiple neighboring vehicles using the average function instead of some high percentile (e.g.,
90
th or
99
th) due to multiple reasons.
The average peak age of information is a typical metric used in the field of age of information
[
3
]
.
When using high percentiles instead of an average in order to focus on neighbors with bad freshness (i.e., high peak age of information), the aggregated peak age of information value will be worse (i.e., higher) and the target age of information will not be reached at all for the standard case.
Applying our model in this case will result in an even better performance in comparison as the target age of information will still be reached (we do not show corresponding results within this manuscript).

The selection of the model parameters as well as the scenario play an important role in the resulting peak age of information and target age of information values.
A configuration which focuses mainly on the distance between vehicles is only useful if the interference domain is already large.
In contrast, if the interference domain is small and observed age of information values of neighboring vehicles are similar, a different configuration is useful.
The mobility of vehicles did not play a big role within the results of our simulation study, but it can have a big impact on the knowledge of neighboring vehicles in scenarios with buildings.

Putting our spatial model to practical use within an age of information-based beacon adaption algorithm helps reaching an age of information requirement while avoiding strong outliers for the beacon rate.
Accordingly, the channel usage is reduced and too infrequent updates are avoided.
It should be noted that we used a very simple approach for controlling the beacon rate based on a proportional-integral-derivative controller.
In fact, a proportional-integral-derivative controller is not well-suited for our use-case of keeping the peak age of information below the target age of information as it tries to reach the set-point (the target age of information) exactly and treats positive & negative deviation from this value equally.
A negative deviation from the set-point (i.e., peak age of information lower than target age of information) is better (and even desired) than a positive deviation (which indicates outdated information).
However, even with our simple approach, we are able to illustrate the benefit of applying the spatial model.

7
Conclusion

We explored the use of the age of information (AoI) in the context of intelligent transportation systems (ITS).
First approaches of using the age of information in intelligent transportation systems focused on the original definition only, i.e., to measure the peak age of informations for every received message and then to interpret the resulting values as they are.
We, however, observed that this is not adequate in this application scenario as effects from the wireless communication channel may lead to quite variable peak age of information measures for farther away vehicles – even though they play a less important role in many intelligent transportation systems applications.

In this paper, we proposed a new way of interpreting the age of information for arriving packets.
We focus on the location of the transmitting vehicle as a metric to assess the importance of the information.
Using a weighting coefficient applied to the peak age of information and also to an age of information requirement, we can add a priority measure.
As an example for intelligent transportation systems, we use the orientation and the distance of the corresponding vehicles for this process.
It should be noted that the underlying peak age of information metric is not changed in this procedure, i.e., compatibility with other approach is maintained.
The benefit of applying the model is dependent on its parameters as well as the scenario, which requires careful configuration.
Our spatial model allows to focus on timely updates of relevant vehicles for meeting a given age of information requirement, which helps saving resources on the wireless channel.
Applying the model to an age of information-based beacon adaption algorithm thus increases information freshness while keeping beacon rates reasonable and resource usage small.

In future work, we plan to apply our spatial model to various different intelligent transportation system applications to gain further insights to the advantages but also limits of the age of information metric in general.
Based on the case-study in this work, more sophisticated protocols for adaptively adjusting the beacon rate based on the importance of the information to other vehicles can be build.
