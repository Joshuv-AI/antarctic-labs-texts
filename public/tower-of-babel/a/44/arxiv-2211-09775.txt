A Lower Bound on Dark Matter Mass
Mustafa A. Amin, Mehrdad Mirbabayi

Abstract
We argue that there is a lower bound of order 10 − 19 10^{-19} eV on dark matter mass if it is produced after inflation via a process with finite correlation length. We rely on non-detection of free-streaming suppression and white-noise enhancement of density perturbations as the observational inputs.



I
Introduction

Dark matter is essential to our understanding of the cosmos – from the astrophysical scales relevant for dwarf galaxies to the cosmological scales in the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)
Drlica-Wagner et al. 2022
. Dark matter makes up approximately
84\%
of the non-relativistic matter in our cosmos
Aghanim et al. 2020
. Its detailed nature, however, is not well understood. For example, the mass or spin of dark matter particles is not known, and we have yet to confirm any non-gravitational interactions of dark matter. Furthermore, we do not have a unique formation mechanism for dark matter in the early universe. Given the relevance of dark matter to our understanding of the cosmos, any relatively model-independent constraint on some of its microscopic properties would be valuable. In this letter, we provide such a relatively model-independent lower bound on the mass of dark matter particles.

An approximately scale invariant initial power spectrum of dark matter density fluctuations for comoving wavenumbers
k<k_{\rm obs}\sim 10\,\rm{Mpc}^{-1}
is consistent with current observations
Iršič et al. 2017
;
Nadler et al. 2019
. We use two effects, (1) excess white noise power and (2) suppression of power due to free-steaming, to provide a relatively model-independent lower bound on the mass of the dark matter,
m\gtrsim 10^{-19}\,\rm eV
, assuming that the background dark matter density results from finite-momentum rather than the homogeneous oscillations of the dark matter field. The bound is independent of the nature of the field (scalar, vector, tensor etc.) and details of the production mechanism, but assumes this field constitutes all of dark matter and interacts only gravitationally after production. With more details of the production mechanism included, the bound can be strengthened further. Our lower bound is at least 1-2 orders of magnitude stronger than that due to the finite Jeans scale in fuzzy dark matter
Iršič et al. 2017
;
Rogers and Peiris 2021
. It is comparable to the recent bound due to dynamical heating of stars in ultra-faint dwarf galaxies
Dalal and Kravtsov 2022
. Our bound is more general, but weaker than the one of
Iršič et al. 2020
;
Feix et al. 2020
, who use a model-specific version of (1) alone. Based on inferred quasar spins and hence lack of superradiance,
Ünal et al. 2021
also claims a stronger bound on the mass than ours.

To demonstrate our idea, we provide a concrete example of scalar field dark matter. We set
\hbar=c=1
.

For instance, there is a finite, though extremely small, probability that after Peccei-Quinn symmetry breaking, everywhere in a finite-volume universe the axion field finds itself near the bottom of the would be axion potential.

V
Summary & Discussion

Post-inflationary production of light scalars
Iršič et al. 2020
;
Gorghetto et al. 2021b
, vectors
Agrawal et al. 2020
;
Co et al. 2019
;
Dror et al. 2019
;
Bastero-Gil et al. 2019
;
Long and Wang 2019
;
Co et al. 2021
, etc. naturally leads to a combination of white-noise and a free-streaming cutoff. We have used the absence of these effects in the observational data to provide a relatively model independent and conservative lower bound on the mass of dark matter particles. Of course, observing such features in future data would be a valuable hint regarding the nature of dark matter.

The generality of our bound arises from the following. An isocurvature white noise spectrum in the dark matter density at sufficiently small wavenumbers follows when the momentum integral that defines
P_{\delta}
is dominated by field modes on subhorizon scales. This is natural for a causal production mechanism with finite correlation length. Free-streaming follows from the assumption that dark matter is non-interacting (apart from gravity). The free streaming scale depends on the mass of the particle, and is also linked to the field momentum
k_{*}
that dominates the dark matter density. Our lower bound is only weakly sensitive to the details of the dark matter field power spectrum. While in some cases, the free streaming scale may also be sensitive to momenta higher than
k_{*}
, that would only make our bound stronger.

Although we have assumed post-inflationary production of dark matter, our bound also applies to many scenarios where the dark matter field is produced even during inflation. More generally, our bound is applicable when the density of dark matter is dominated by subhorizon field modes at late times, regardless of whether DM is produced during or after inflation. For example, the inflationary vector DM production scenario of
Graham et al. 2016
falls under the purview of our framework, so do
Redi and Tesi 2022
;
Harigaya and Wang 2022
because they lead to subhorizon field modes dominating the energy density at late times
5
5

5

However, in some of these cases the abundance constraints are stronger than those from free-streaming and white noise
. Our bound is
not applicable
when the zero mode of the field dominates the energy density at late times — the case for axions produced via PQ breaking
before
inflation.

We believe our analysis is sufficient for obtaining the quoted lower bound, however, a source of uncertainty in our bound are some of the analytic approximations we made to obtain (
6
) (which are quantified in the supplementary material). Obtaining more rigorous bounds for this class of dark matter models requires a new Boltzmann solver beyond, for example e.g.
Blas et al. 2011
, which assume a dominant homogeneous mode of the field. Due to an absence of the the zero mode of the field, the new solver will necessarily involve convolutions in Fourier space even if one only considers linearized evolution of the dark matter fields. A calculation in the nonlinear regime (including self-gravity of dark matter at sufficiently late times) will require carrying our
3+1
numerical simulations on a lattice. These are beyond the scope of the present letter.

Finally, note that free-streaming and white noise have opposite effects on the short scale power. A more quantitative bound requires fitting the spectrum given in (
6
) and marginalizing over
k_{*}
. Note that for
k_{\rm wn}\sim 10^{2}k_{\rm obs}
, our lower bound on mass [c.f. (
8
)] scales quadratically with
k_{\rm obs}
. Hence, as observations push to smaller scales
Valluri et al. 2022
, this bound will rapidly get stronger.

Supplementary Material

VI.1
Parametrization & generalization

It is useful to consider a fast formation process and relate
k_{*}
and
k^{*}_{\rm fs}
to the associated time and length scale. Suppose that the field is produced around
H_{\gamma}=\gamma m
, in a narrow window of wavenumbers around
k=\beta a_{\gamma}H_{\gamma}
, then
k_{*}\sim k_{\rm wn}\sim\beta\sqrt{\gamma}\sqrt{m/H_{\rm eq}}k_{\rm eq}
. The free-streaming suppression scale
k^{*}_{\rm fs}(t_{\rm eq})\sim k_{*}/[\beta^{2}\gamma\log\left(2k_{*}/(\beta^{2}\gamma k_{\rm eq})\right)]
. The lower bound on mass, corresponds to
\beta^{2}\gamma\sim 10
. This is consistent with subhorizon scales dominating at production, and/or the production happening before
H=m
. Note that
\beta^{2}\gamma
always appears in the same combination in
k_{\rm wn}
and
k^{*}_{\rm fs}
. Also note that for
\beta\gtrsim 1
, and
\beta^{2}\gamma\sim 10
, we get
a_{\gamma}\sim a_{\rm BBN}
, so our assumption that the universe has a standard expansion history after that point is justified.

The results in this paper are general expectations, though exceptions can of course be found. For example, the free-streaming scale can be affected by allowing for significant non-gravitational interactions of dark matter after production or by deviating from the standard expansion history. The bound will also be relaxed if post-inflationary production accounts for part of the total dark matter.

VI.2
Axions from a string network

A well motivated, but dynamically complex example of non-thermal post-inflationary production of light dark matter is that of axions produced by a string network due to Peccei-Quinn symmetry breaking after inflation

, suggesting

k_{*}\sim(\xi\log(f_{a}/m_{a}))^{1/4}m_{a}a(t_{*}).

(10)

We can then calculate

, we find

\displaystyle\left\langle{\rho_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}\right\rangle_{\zeta}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{\mathbf{q}}q^{2}[P_{A}(q)+P_{B}(q)]\cos(q-|{\mathbf{q}}-{\mathbf{k}}|)t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\int_{\mathbf{q}}q^{2}[P_{A}(q)+P_{B}(q)]\cos(\hat{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}\ t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\sin(kt)}{kt}\ \bar{\rho}.

(19)

This gives
\delta\rho_{\mathbf{k}}(t)/\bar{\rho}\approx\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}
when
kt\ll 1
, while it is negligible when
kt\gg 1
. This is the free-streaming cutoff.

VI.4
Cosmological perturbations

Now we analyze the evolution of perturbations during radiation dominance. Important limiting regimes are the superhorizon versus subhorizon, and relativistic versus nonrelativistic limits. At subhorizon scales, it is more convenient to work in the Newtonian gauge, where the metric perturbations remain small. In this gauge, the scalar perturbations of the metric are parametrized (at linear order) as

ds^{2}=-(1+2\Phi)dt^{2}+a^{2}(1+2\Psi)dx^{2}.

(20)

In the radiation era

a\propto\sqrt{t}\,,

(21)

and in terms of the conformal time
d\eta=dt/a
,

\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}=-\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=-2\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\sin(\nu\eta)-\nu\eta\cos(\nu\eta)}{\nu^{3}\eta^{3}},

(22)

where
\nu^{2}=k^{2}/3
, and
\zeta
is the scalar perturbation that remains conserved at superhorizon scales. One can also use a different gauge in which

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}e^{2\zeta}dx^{2}.

(23)

We will use this gauge for some arguments in the upcoming section, but for most of the appendix we work in the Newtonian gauge.

In the Newtonian gauge, the equation of motion for
\varphi
, at linear order in
\Phi
, reads

\begin{split}\frac{1}{a^{3}}\partial_{t}[a^{3}(1-\Phi+3\Psi)\dot{\varphi}]-&\frac{1}{a^{2}}\nabla\cdot[(1+\Phi+\Psi)\nabla\varphi]\\[10.0pt]
+&m^{2}(1+\Phi+3\Psi)\varphi=0.\end{split}

(24)

We are treating
\varphi
as an spectator field, namely, it feels the metric perturbations but does not source them. This is a good approximation well before matter-radiation equality.

VI.5
Adiabatic perturbations; superhorizon

To set the initial condition for the subsequent evolution, we need to find the effect of superhorizon adiabatic fluctuations on fast oscillating perturbations of
\varphi
. Neglecting gradients of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, we write the WKB solutions of eq. (
24
):

f^{\pm}_{k}(t)=\frac{e^{\mp i\int^{t}dt^{\prime}\omega(t^{\prime})}}{\sqrt{2a^{3}(1-\Phi+3\Psi)\omega(t)}},

(25)

where

\omega^{2}(t)\approx m^{2}(1+2\Phi)+\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}(1+2\Phi-2\Psi).

(26)

We have ignored
\dot{H}
and
H^{2}
terms in
\omega^{2}(t)
. We expand

\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\mathbf{q}}[c^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})f_{\mathbf{q}}^{+}(t,{\mathbf{x}})+c^{-}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})f_{\mathbf{q}}^{-}(t,{\mathbf{x}})]e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}},

(27)

where
c^{\pm}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})
are random variables, related by the reality of
\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})
,

c^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})=[c^{-}_{-{\mathbf{q}}}({\mathbf{x}})]^{*},

(28)

and the
{\mathbf{x}}
argument reminds us that their spectra depend on
\zeta({\mathbf{x}})
:

\left\langle{c^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})c^{+}_{{\mathbf{q}}}({\mathbf{x}})^{*}}\right\rangle^{\prime}=P(q,\zeta({\mathbf{x}})).

(29)

Equal and uncorrelated excitation of sine and cosine modes imply

\left\langle{c^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}({\mathbf{x}})c^{+}_{-{\mathbf{q}}}({\mathbf{x}})}\right\rangle^{\prime}=0.

(30)

The energy density
u^{\mu}u^{\nu}T_{\mu\nu}
at early times, when the field fluctuations are relativistic, is (up to linear order)

\begin{split}\left\langle{\rho_{r}}\right\rangle_{\zeta}&\approx\frac{1}{2}\left[(1-2\Phi)\left\langle{\dot{\varphi}^{2}}\right\rangle+\frac{(1-2\Psi)}{a^{2}}\left\langle{|\nabla\varphi|^{2}}\right\rangle\right],\\
&=\frac{1}{a^{4}}\int_{\mathbf{q}}qP(q,\zeta)(1-4\Psi({\mathbf{x}})).\end{split}

(31)

In order to get the expected result for radiation
\delta\rho_{r}/\rho_{r}=4\zeta/3
, we must have

P(q,\zeta)=P(e^{-\zeta}q,0).

(32)

We will derive this shortly. Before doing so, we can check its consistency by evaluating
\rho
after the fluctuations become non-relativistic:

\left\langle{\rho_{nr}}\right\rangle_{\zeta}\approx\frac{m}{a^{3}}\int_{\mathbf{q}}P(q,\zeta)(1-3\Psi({\mathbf{x}}))=\bar{\rho}_{nr}(1+\zeta),

(33)

which is the correct relation.

Equation (
32
) follows from the fact that in the presence of a superhorizon adiabatic perturbation different points on a constant
t
slice in gauge (
23
) are at the same moment along a common history, only the
x
coordinate has a different relation with the physical one. In particular, the energy density has to be uniform. In this gauge (and in the relativistic limit), we have

\left\langle{\rho_{r}^{\zeta-{\rm gauge}}}\right\rangle_{\zeta}=\frac{1}{a^{4}}\int_{\mathbf{q}}qP(q,\zeta)(1-4\zeta({\mathbf{x}})),

(34)

where we used the appropriate WKB solution (i.e. (
25
) with
\Phi\to 0
and
\Psi\to\zeta
). For this to be
\zeta
-independent (
32
) must hold. Of course, (
32
) is saying that power is the same in terms of the rescaled momentum
e^{-\zeta}k
.

Note that the WKB solutions in the
\zeta
gauge and the Newtonian gauge are related by a time-diffeomorphism:

t\to t(1-\frac{2}{3}\zeta).

(35)

Hence, the coefficients
c^{\pm}
(and their spectrum) remain the same.

VI.6
Adiabatic perturbations; subhorizon

We are ultimately interested in the effect of adiabatic fluctuations with momentum
k
on fluctuations of
\varphi
with a much larger momentum
q\gg k
. However, we start from a global misalignment scenario, which is simpler but conceptually similar.

VI.6.1
Inflationary/global misalignment

Constraints on the fuzzy dark matter mass come from the lowest
k
modes that are significantly modified compared to the CDM scenario. Hence, it is legitimate to assume
m\gg H
when
k\sim aH
. The
\varphi
equation can be treated with the WKB method, except the gradient and time-dependence of
\Phi
are no longer negligible. We write

\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\varphi_{0}\frac{[1+d(t,{\mathbf{x}})]^{1/2}}{\sqrt{2a^{3}}}e^{-im(t-S(t,{\mathbf{x}}))}+{\rm c.c.},

(36)

where
d,\partial{S}={\mathcal{O}}(\Phi)
. Linearizing in them, neglecting terms that are suppressed by
H^{2}/m^{2}
, and setting
\Psi=-\Phi
gives

\begin{split}\dot{d}_{\mathbf{k}}-\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}S_{\mathbf{k}}-\ddot{S}_{\mathbf{k}}&=4\dot{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}},\\[10.0pt]
\dot{S}_{\mathbf{k}}+\frac{k^{2}}{4m^{2}a^{2}}d_{\mathbf{k}}&=-\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}.\end{split}

(37)

When
k/a\ll H
, then
\dot{\Phi}\approx 0
, and we have

d_{\mathbf{k}}=\zeta_{\mathbf{k}},\qquad S_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{2}{3}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}t+{\rm const.}

(38)

where the integration constant was fixed by matching to the superhorizon solution.

Away from this limit, we can eliminate
S
to find

a^{2}\partial_{t}\left[a^{2}\partial_{t}\left[\left(1+\frac{k^{2}}{4m^{2}a^{2}}\right)d_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\right]+\frac{k^{4}}{4m^{2}}d_{\mathbf{k}}=J_{\mathbf{k}},

(39)

where

J_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv 3a^{2}\partial_{t}(a^{2}\dot{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}})-a^{2}k^{2}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}.

(40)

In the non-relativistic regime, the
k^{2}
term in the square brackets in (
39
) can be ignored. Let us define
t_{k}
as the time at which
k/a(t_{k})=H(t_{k})
. Explicitly,
t_{k}=a_{\rm eq}^{2}H_{\rm eq}/(2k^{2})
. In terms of the time variable

\tau\equiv\frac{1}{2a_{\rm eq}^{2}H_{\rm eq}}\log(t/t_{k}),

(41)

the non-relativistic regime corresponds to
a_{\rm eq}^{2}H_{\rm eq}\tau>\log(k^{2}/mH_{\rm eq}a_{\rm eq}^{2})
. In this regime, the solution is

d_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\!=\!\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\cos\!\left(\frac{k^{2}}{2m}\tau\!\right)+\int_{-\infty}^{\tau(t)}\!\!\!d\tilde{\tau}J_{\mathbf{k}}(\tilde{\tau})\frac{2m}{k^{2}}\sin\!\left[\frac{k^{2}}{2m}(\tau-\tilde{\tau})\right].

(42)

Since the source dominates around horizon crossing, we extended the lower limit of the integral to
-\infty
. At
t\gg t_{k}
, the gravitational potentials becomes negligible, and we can write

d_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\approx\alpha\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\sin\left[\beta_{k}\log(t/t_{*})\right]}{\beta_{k}},\qquad\beta_{k}\equiv\frac{k}{4ma(t_{k})}

(43)

where
\alpha=3
, and
t_{*}\approx 3t_{k}
. After horizon crossing, the perturbation grows logarithmically, as in the case of CDM. However, eventually the Jeans length becomes larger than the wavelength and the growth stops.

The perturbation to the phase of
\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})
follows from the first equation in (
37
). When
t\gg t_{k}

S_{\mathbf{k}}(t)\approx\frac{a^{2}}{k^{2}}\dot{d}_{\mathbf{k}}=\frac{2\alpha\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}}{H(t_{k})}\cos[\beta_{k}\log(t/t_{*})].

(44)

Note that even for
\zeta\ll 1
, a large phase can be accumulated because
m/H(t_{k})\gg 1
, though it evolves slowly when
t>t_{k}
. At linear order in
\zeta
, the contribution of this phase to
\rho_{\mathbf{k}}
is of order

\frac{d\rho_{S}}{\rho}\sim\dot{S}\sim\alpha\zeta\frac{H(t_{k})}{m}\sin[\beta_{k}\log(t/t_{*})],

(45)

which is negligible in our approximation. Therefore, at this order we find the expected result (with
d\rightarrow\delta
),

\rho(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{m^{2}\varphi_{0}^{2}}{a^{3}}[1+d(t,{\mathbf{x}})],

(46)

or equivalently,
\rho_{\mathbf{k}}(t)={m^{2}\varphi_{0}^{2}}{a^{-3}}d_{\mathbf{k}}
for
k\neq 0
.

VI.6.2
Post-inflation/local misalignment

Next we consider the case where
\varphi
fluctuations have a typical momentum
k_{*}
much bigger than the momentum
k
of the adiabatic perturbations of interest. The lowest
k
-modes that are dramatically altered in this case enter the horizon after
t_{\rm nr}
at which
k_{*}=a(t_{\rm nr})m
, i.e.
t_{k}\gg t_{\rm nr}
.

We use the following WKB ansatz

\varphi(t,{\mathbf{x}})=\int_{\mathbf{q}}\varphi_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})e^{i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}},

(47)

and decompose
\varphi_{\mathbf{q}}
in terms of positive and negative frequency modes

\displaystyle\varphi_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\!\equiv\,

\displaystyle c^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}\frac{[1+{d}^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})]^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\sqrt{2a^{3}}}e^{-im\left(t+\frac{q^{2}}{2m^{2}}\int\frac{dt}{a^{2}}+\cdots\right)+imS^{+}_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})}

\displaystyle+(+\leftrightarrow-).

(48)

Note that the subscript
{\mathbf{q}}
on the real functions
{d}^{\pm}_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
and
S^{\pm}_{\mathbf{q}}(t,{\mathbf{x}})
is just a label. The spatially-independent
c_{\mathbf{q}}^{\pm}
satisfy the statistical properties discussed in section
VI.5
. We will focus on the positive frequency part and drop the superscript
+
. The negative frequency transfer function can be obtained by sending
m\to-m
in the positive-frequency one. Neglecting terms that are suppressed by
H/m
or
Ha/q
, we find the following system

\begin{split}\dot{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}+i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{ma^{2}}{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}-\frac{k^{2}}{a^{2}}S_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}-\ddot{S}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}&=4\dot{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}},\\[10.0pt]
\dot{S}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}+i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{ma^{2}}S_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}+\frac{k^{2}}{4m^{2}a^{2}}{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}&=-\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}.\end{split}

(49)

In our approximation, we can replace in the first equation

\ddot{S}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\approx-\dot{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}+i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{ma^{2}}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}.

(50)

Then we can eliminate
S_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}
:

\!\!\!S_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\!=\!\frac{1}{k^{2}}\!\!\left[a^{2}\dot{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\!+\!i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{m}{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\!-\!3a^{2}\dot{\Phi}_{\mathbf{k}}\!-\!i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{m}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\!,

(51)

and obtain a second order equation for
{d}
, which after neglecting terms that are suppressed by
k/q
and
q/am
, reads

\left(a^{2}\partial_{t}+i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{m}\right)^{2}{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}+\frac{k^{4}}{4m^{2}}{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}=J_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}},

(52)

where

\!\!J_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\!\equiv\!3\left(\!a^{2}\partial_{t}\!+\!i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{m}\!\right)\left(\!a^{2}\partial_{t}\!+\!i\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{3m}\!\right)\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}\!-k^{2}a^{2}\Phi_{\mathbf{k}}.

(53)

To solve (
52
), we write

\!\!\!\!{d}_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}(t)\!=\!\psi_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}(t)e^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}({\mathbf{q}},t)},\quad\!\!{\bm{R}}({\mathbf{q}},t)\!\equiv\!\frac{{\mathbf{q}}}{m}\int_{t_{\rm nr}}^{t}\!\frac{d\tilde{t}}{a^{2}(\tilde{t})},\!\!

(54)

where
\psi_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}(t)
will now satisfy a similar equation as
{d}_{\mathbf{k}}(t)
of the last section apart from an extra phase
e^{i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}({\mathbf{q}},t)}
in the source. Note that the free streaming length
R({\mathbf{q}},t)\equiv|{\bm{R}}({\mathbf{q}},t)|
appears naturally in the solution for density perturbations. This length grows logarithmically until
t_{\rm eq}
. Therefore, for
k\sim k_{\rm fs}
, we can neglect the extra phase in the source and the
{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}/m
terms in (
53
) because the source contributes mainly around
t_{k}\ll t_{\rm eq}
, at which point
kR({\mathbf{q}},t_{k})\ll 1
. The form of the solution is the same as (
42
), and given by

\psi_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}(t)\!\approx\!\alpha_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\sin\left[\beta_{k}\log(t/t_{*})\right]}{\beta_{k}},\quad\beta_{k}\!\equiv\!\frac{k}{4ma(t_{k})},

(55)

where
t_{*}\approx 3t_{k}
, and
\alpha_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}=3+\mathcal{O}[kR({\mathbf{q}},t_{k})]
.

The factorization in (
54
) implies that the free-streaming suppression acts effectively as a multiplicative transfer function:

\begin{split}&\left\langle{\rho_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}\right\rangle_{\zeta}\\
&\approx 3\frac{\sin\left[\beta_{k}\log(t/t_{*})\right]}{\beta_{k}}\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}m^{2}\!\!\int_{\mathbf{q}}P_{\varphi}(t,q)\cos[{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\bm{R}}({\mathbf{q}},t)]\\
&=T_{\rm ad}(t,k)\zeta_{\mathbf{k}}m^{2}\int d\log q\frac{q^{3}}{2\pi^{2}}P_{\varphi}(t,q)\frac{\sin[kR({\mathbf{q}},t)]}{kR({\mathbf{q}},t)}.\end{split}

(56)

Note that the free-streaming length
R({\mathbf{q}},t)
is much larger than the “Jeans length”, because the logarithm in the argument of cosine is
\log(t/t_{\rm nr})\gg\log(t/t_{k})
. Hence, matter perturbations are still in the logarithmic growth phase when the free-streaming cutoff kicks in, and
T_{\rm ad}(t,k)
on the second line can be replaced with the CDM transfer function. In the regime of interest, i.e.
k_{*}>ma(t_{i})
the above equation results in eq. (
4
) in the main text. We expect relative corrections of order
kR(k_{*},t_{k})
, which for
k<k_{\rm fs}
is
<\log(t_{k_{\rm fs}}/t_{\rm nr})/\log(t_{\rm eq}/t_{\rm nr})\sim 0.1
.

From the first to the second line in (
56
), we used
\left\langle{\rho_{\mathbf{k}}(t)}\right\rangle_{\zeta}\approx m^{2}\int d^{3}xe^{-i{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{x}}}\langle\varphi^{2}(t,{\mathbf{x}})\rangle
with the form of
\varphi
from (
47
) and (
VI.6.2
), along with our solution for
d
. To calculate the expectation value, we used the previously defined properties of
c_{\mathbf{q}}^{\pm}
(see section (
VI.5
), but without the spatial dependence in the
c_{\mathbf{q}}^{\pm}
). In (
56
), we neglected the contribution from
S
, even though it can become large. Unlike the case of purely adiabatic perturbations, now the gradient term
a^{-2}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}
can also contribute at linear order in
\zeta
. So we use (
51
) to estimate its contribution. We find

\frac{\delta\rho_{S}}{\rho}\sim m\frac{{\mathbf{q}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}}{a^{2}}S\sim\alpha_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\zeta\frac{Hq}{mk},

(57)

which is much less than
\alpha_{{\mathbf{q}},{\mathbf{k}}}\zeta
, when
k\gg aH
and
q\ll am
.

VI.7
Isocurvature perturbations

Finally, suppose we neglect the small adiabatic fluctuations. Then the metric perturbations in (
24
) will only be sourced by the
\varphi
perturbations, and they are suppressed by
\rho_{\varphi}/\rho_{\rm tot}\ll 1
deep in the radiation era. Neglecting those,
\varphi
perturbations evolve according to (
47
), with
d=S=0
. Assuming equal power in the sine and cosine modes ((
29
) and (
30
)), we get in the nonrelativistic limit

P_{\varphi}(t,k)=\frac{1}{a^{3}}\left\langle{c_{\mathbf{k}}^{+}c_{-{\mathbf{k}}}^{-}}\right\rangle.

(58)

Then the density power spectrum reads

\left\langle{\rho_{\mathbf{k}}\rho_{-{\mathbf{k}}}}\right\rangle^{\prime}=m^{4}\int_{\bm{q}}P_{\varphi}(t,q)P_{\varphi}(t,|{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{q}}|).

(59)

Assuming that
P_{\varphi}(t,q)
is smooth around the peak momentum
k_{*}\gg k
, we can expand this expression in powers of
k^{2}
. The zeroth order term is the leading white-noise contribution in eq. (
2
) of the main text. Note that there is no free-streaming effect here.
