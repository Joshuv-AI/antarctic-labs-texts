Revisiting and Improving Scoring Fusion for Spoofing-aware Speaker Verification Using Compositional Data Analysis
Xin Wang, Tomi Kinnunen, Kong Aik Lee, Paul-Gauthier Noé, Junichi Yamagishi

Abstract
Fusing outputs from automatic speaker verification (ASV) and spoofing countermeasure (CM) is expected to make an integrated system robust to zero-effort imposters and synthesized spoofing attacks. Many score-level fusion methods have been proposed, but many remain heuristic. This paper revisits score-level fusion using tools from decision theory and presents three main findings. First, fusion by summing the ASV and CM scores can be interpreted on the basis of compositional data analysis, and score calibration before fusion is essential. Second, the interpretation leads to an improved fusion method that linearly combines the log-likelihood ratios of ASV and CM. However, as the third finding reveals, this linear combination is inferior to a non-linear one in making optimal decisions. The outcomes of these findings, namely, the score calibration before fusion, improved linear fusion, and better non-linear fusion, were found to be effective on the SASV challenge database.

Introduction

Automatic speaker verification (ASV) systems are vulnerable to spoofed synthetic speech data that clone the target speakers’ voices
[
1
]
.
By combining ASV and spoofing countermeasure (CM), which ideally should reject any spoofed data, we expect to be able to construct a spoofing-aware ASV (SASV) system that only accepts speech data from real (bona fide) humans who match the target speaker’s identity.

An SASV system can be a cascade of ASV and CM sub-systems
[
2
]
.
The ASV and CM make separate binary decisions, and the input is accepted if it is accepted by both sub-systems.
Another design used by many SASV systems is to produce one score and make one binary decision.
Let the input be
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}^{(p)},\bm{x}^{(r)})\in\mathcal{X}
, where
\bm{x}^{(p)}
and
\bm{x}^{(r)}
are the probe (test) and reference (enrollment) samples, respectively, and can be waveforms, features, etc.
An SASV system defines a scoring function
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that maps
\bm{x}
into a score
s_{\text{sasv}}
. If
s_{\text{sasv}}
surpasses a threshold, the system takes the action of
\mathtt{accept}
, claiming that
\bm{x}_{p}
is bona fide
and
matches the speaker identity in
\bm{x}_{r}
. Otherwise, it takes the action of
\mathtt{reject}
.

This paper focuses on the SASV systems that produce
s_{\text{sasv}}
using score-level fusion. The idea is to use ASV and CM sub-systems to obtain an ASV score
s_{\text{asv}}
and CM score
s_{\text{cm}}
then use a score-level fusion function
h:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to merge the scores into
s_{\text{sasv}}
.
Note that there are feature-level fusion and end-to-end methods
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
, which do not need to produce
\{s_{\text{asv}},s_{\text{cm}}\}
. We argue that score-level fusion is useful because
\{s_{\text{asv}},s_{\text{cm}}\}
provides invaluable extra explanations on the model decision. Some evaluation metrics, such as tandem equal error rate (t-EER)
[
6
]
, also require separated
s_{\text{asv}}
and
s_{\text{cm}}
.

An example SASV system using score-level fusion (baseline B1 of the SASV challenge
[
5
]
) is illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Its ASV sub-system extracts embeddings from
\bm{x}^{(p)}
and
\bm{x}^{(r)}
and computes their cosine similarity as
s_{\text{asv}}
, which indicates the degree that
\bm{x}^{(p)}
and
\bm{x}^{(r)}
match in terms of speaker identity.
The CM sub-system extracts an embedding from
\bm{x}^{(p)}
and produces a score
s_{\text{cm}}\in\mathbb{R}
using a neural network. A higher
s_{\text{cm}}
indicates that
\bm{x}^{(p)}
is more likely to be bona fide. Hence, it is intuitively reasonable to fuse the scores by
s_{\text{sasv}}=s_{\text{asv}}+s_{\text{cm}}
.

More advanced fusion functions have been proposed in the SASV and other biometrics fields
[
7
,
2
]
.
Although most are intuitively reasonable, for example, normalizing the numeric range of
s_{\text{asv}}
and
s_{\text{cm}}
before summation
[
5
]
, the link between practice and theory is not always clear. Therefore, the implicit assumptions, link to optimal decisions, and room for improvement remain elusive.

Starting from the summation of the ASV and CM scores, we scrutinize the score fusion using tools from decision theory.
After reformulating SASV as a classification task that involves three data classes but requires a binary decision,
we interpret SASV scoring from the perspective of compositional data analysis and show that the summation of
s_{\text{asv}}
and
s_{\text{cm}}
is a reasonable choice
if the ASV and CM scores are proper log-likelihood ratios (LLRs).
Hence, the interpretation indicates that score calibration before fusion is beneficial.
The interpretation also leads to an improved fusion method that uses probabilistic models to compute LLRs for summation. Finally, the summation-based fusion is analyzed from the perspective of optimal decision policy and found to be theoretically inferior to a non-linear fusion method.

2
Interpreting and improving SASV score fusion

2.1
SASV is not fusion of congruent binary classifiers

By definition, an SASV system should only
\mathtt{accept}
if the input is uttered by the bona fide target speaker; otherwise,
\mathtt{reject}
.
Fusing ASV and CM scores for a binary decision in the SASV system seems to be similar to the score fusion in multi-modal biometric verification
[
9
,
10
]
.

Let
\bm{\mathrm{X}}{}=\{\bm{x}{}_{1},\cdots,\bm{x}{}_{K}\}
be the inputs to a biometric verification system with
K
modalities (e.g., speech and face). The system determines whether inputs match the claimed identity (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar}{}}
) or not (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{non}{}}
).
Assuming that
\bm{x}_{k}
are statistically independent, we can use the Bayes’ formula
[
11
]
to obtain

\log\frac{{P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar}{}}|\bm{\mathrm{X}}{})}}{1-{P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar}{}}|\bm{\mathrm{X}}{})}}=\log\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}{1-\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar}{}}_{\mathsf{non}{}}(\bm{x}_{k}),

(1)

where

\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar}{}}_{\mathsf{non}{}}(\bm{x}_{k})\triangleq\log\frac{p(\bm{x}_{k}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar}{}})}{p(\bm{x}_{k}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non}{}})}
is an LLR produced by the
k
-th sub-system, and
\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}
is the prior probability of class
\mathsf{tar}{}
.
Assuming that the LLRs and priors are equal to the ground truth,
our decision strategy for minimizing the decision error rate is to accept
{\mathsf{tar}{}}
if and only if

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar}{}}_{\mathsf{non}{}}(\bm{x}_{k})+\log\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}{1-\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}>0

[
11
]
.
The sum of LLRs is the weight of evidence to favor
\mathsf{tar}{}
, which is known as
independent additivity
of LLRs
[
12
]
.

We may hastily treat SASV as biometric verification with two modalities, namely, ASV and CM. Thus, fusion by
s_{\text{cm}}+s_{\text{asv}}
can be interpreted as the sum of LLRs, given that the scores approximate the LLRs.
However, unlike Eq. (
1
) where all sub-systems deal with
\{{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar}{}},{H}{}_{\mathsf{non}{}}\}
,
ASV and CM have different hypotheses: for CM, the input is either bona fide (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{bona}{}}
) or spoofed (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{spoof}{}}
).
With four hypotheses involved, independent additivity of LLRs and Eq. (
1
) no longer hold
[
12
, sec.4.3]
.

2.2
Interpreting SASV using compositional data analysis

A better starting point is to have three exhaustive and mutually exclusive hypotheses
[
2
,
13
]
:
the input probe is bona fide and matches the reference (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}
), bona fide and unmatched (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}
), or spoofed (
{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}
).
2
2

2

It is unnecessary to separate
{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}
into matched and non-matched cases since both should be rejected
[
6
]
.

To interpret this classification task with three classes but two actions
\{\mathtt{accept},\mathtt{reject}\}
, we use
compositional data analysis

[
14
,
15
]
.

Let
\bm{P}=[P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})]^{\top}
and
\bm{\pi}=[\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}},\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}},\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}]^{\top}
be the vectors of posterior and prior probabilities, respectively. Let the vector of likelihoods be
\bm{w}=[p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}),p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}),p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})]^{\top}
.
Because
\sum_{i}\pi_{i}=\sum_{i}P_{i}=1
, where
i
is the index of the vector’s element,
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{P}
are compositional vectors on the probability simplex
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.
Hence,
\bm{P}
(and
\bm{\pi}
) can be equivalently represented by a vector in 2D Euclidean space
\tilde{\bm{P}}=[\tilde{{P}_{1}},\tilde{{P}_{2}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Among many possible choices for
\mathbb{S}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, an isometric-log-ratio (ILR) transformation
[
16
]

\tilde{\bm{P}}=\ilr(\bm{P})
defines

\displaystyle\tilde{{P}}_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\frac{P_{2}}{P_{1}},\quad\tilde{{P}}_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\log\frac{P_{3}P_{3}}{P_{1}P_{2}},

(2)

where the scalars
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}
are due to the Aitchison orthonormal basis
[
15
, Sec.10.2]
[
16
]
. The same transformation
\ilr(\cdot)
can be applied to
\bm{\pi}
and
\bm{w}
. Particularly, for
\tilde{\bm{w}}
, we have

\begin{split}\tilde{{w}}_{1}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\frac{w_{3}w_{2}}{w_{1}w_{3}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{x})\Big),\end{split}

(3)

\begin{split}\tilde{{w}}_{2}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\log\frac{w_{3}w_{3}}{w_{1}w_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Big(\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{x})+\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})\Big).\end{split}

(4)

With the
\tilde{\bm{P}},\tilde{\bm{\pi}}
, and
\tilde{\bm{w}}
, it is known that
\tilde{\bm{P}}=\tilde{\bm{\pi}}+\tilde{\bm{w}}

[
15
, Sec.3.3.3]
.
This equation has a similar form of “posterior log-odds = prior log-odds + LLR” to Eq. (
1
).
Similar to the sum of LLRs in Eq. (
1
),
\tilde{\bm{w}}=[\tilde{{w}_{1}},\tilde{{w}_{2}}]^{\top}
can be interpreted as weights of evidence, albeit in a hierarchical manner
[
15
]
as illustrated in Fig.
2
. A larger
\tilde{w}_{2}
favors
{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}
over the other two,
and it is the
\tilde{w}_{1}
that discriminates
{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}
from
{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}
.

The above description indicates that
\tilde{w}_{2}
is what an SASV system needs to produce. This observation paves the way for interpreting and improving the SASV score fusion methods.

2.3
Fusion method 1: summation of ASV and CM scores

Note that ASV is designed to discriminate
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
and
\mathsf{non.bf}{}
, while CM differentiates
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
from
\mathsf{spf}{}
.

If
their scores approximate the LLRs, i.e.,
s_{\text{asv}}\approx\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{x})
and
s_{\text{cm}}\approx\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})
,
3
3

3

For a speaker-independent CM, we can assume
p(\bm{x}|\mathsf{tar.bf}{})\approx p(\bm{x}|\mathsf{non.bf}{})\approx p(\bm{x}|\mathsf{bona}{})

[
17
]
and
\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})\approx\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{bona}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})
.
we can follow Eq. (
4
) and compute
s_{\text{sasv}}
as

\tilde{{w}}_{2}\approx s_{\text{sasv}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(s_{\text{asv}}+s_{\text{cm}}).

(5)

Hence, the summation-based score fusion is interpretable from the perspective of compositional data analysis.
Note that the factor
\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}
does not affect the discrimination power of
s_{\text{sasv}}
.

The interpretation may be disappointing since Eq. (
5
) is the same as what we have done, but it suggests a practice that is either ignored or conducted arbitrarily:
the ASV and CM scores need to well approximate the LLRs before summation.

One way to do so is score calibration
[
10
]
.
For example, let
\{f_{\text{asv}},f_{\text{cm}}\}
be calibration functions, both of which have a form of affine transformation
f(x)=ax+b
.
We can learn parameters of
\{f_{\text{asv}},f_{\text{cm}}\}
on a development set through logistic regression
[
18
]
, after which we compute
s_{\text{sasv}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\big[f_{\text{asv}}(s_{\text{asv}})+f_{\text{cm}}(s_{\text{cm}})\big]
.

2.4
Fusion method 2: summation of LLRs

Rather than calibrating the ASV and CM scores to produce the LLRs (i.e., discriminative calibration
[
19
]
), we can treat the scores as features and carry out generative calibration
[
19
]
.
Let us create a feature vector
\bm{s}=[s_{\text{asv}},s_{\text{cm}}]^{\top}
and
introduce likelihood functions
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})
,
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})
, and
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})
for the three classes. Assuming
\bm{s}
encodes all the information of
\bm{x}
and
\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{1})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{2})}=\frac{p(\bm{s}|\bm{\theta}_{1})}{p(\bm{s}|\bm{\theta}_{2})}
, based on Eq. (
4
), we can compute

s_{\text{sasv}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\Big(\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{s})+\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{s})\Big),

(6)

where
\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{s})\triangleq\log\frac{p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}{p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})}
and

\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}}(\bm{s})\triangleq\log\frac{p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}{p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})}
.
Each
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta})
can be a Gaussian or another parametric distribution, and its parameter set
\bm{\theta}

can be estimated by maximizing the likelihood on development data.

During testing, we compute
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})
,
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})
, and
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})
of a test sample and plug them into Eq. (
6
).
Since the selected parametric distributions may be different from the actual distribution of
\bm{s}
, the estimated LLRs may be inaccurate. Hence, we can optionally apply discriminative calibration on the LLRs before fusion.

2.5
Fusion method 3: nonlinear fusion of LLRs

The interpretation so far leads to the simple form of score fusion in Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
). Importantly, however, it also reveals a major limitation for making decisions.
Recall from Sec.
2.1
that, for a single ASV system (
K=1
), we accept
{\mathsf{tar}{}}
if and only if
\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar}{}}_{\mathsf{non}{}}(\bm{s})+\log\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}{1-\pi_{\mathsf{tar}{}}}>0
.
For SASV, a similar decision policy is to accept
{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}
if and only if
\tilde{{P}}_{2}=\tilde{{\pi}}_{2}+\tilde{{w}}_{2}>0
. Given
\tilde{{P}}_{2}
defined in Eq. (
2
), this condition can be written as

P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})^{2}>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x})P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),

(7)

or equivalently,

\begin{split}\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{s})+&\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{s})>\log\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}}{\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}^{2}}.\end{split}

(8)

However, this decision policy cannot minimize the decision cost even if when the true LLRs and priors are given.

It can be shown that, for SASV with equal decision costs on false rejection of
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
and false acceptance of
\mathsf{non.bf}
or
\mathsf{spf}
, the optimal decision policy is to accept
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
when

4
4

4

It is different from the condition
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x})
AND
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x})

[
15
, sec.10.3]
, which maximizes the decision utility
[
20
]
with a scalar diagonal utility matrix.

P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x})+P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),

(9)

or with Bayes’ formula and logarithm applied,

-\log[(1-\rho)e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}(\bm{s})}+\rho e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{s})}]>-\log\beta,

(10)

where
\beta\triangleq\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}}{\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}
and
\rho\triangleq\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}{\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}
.
5
5

5

\rho
is the same as the spoof prevalence prior in t-EER
[
6
]
.

Inequality. (
7
) is a necessary but not sufficient condition to achieve Ineq. (
9
).
For example, a sample with
[P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})]=[0.05,0.65,0.3]
will be rejected by Ineq. (
9
) but falsely accepted by (
7
).
The overall decision cost cannot be lower than that based on the optimal decision policy in Ineq. (
9
) (or (
10
)), given that we know the true LLRs and priors.

To support the above argument, we generated ASV and CM scores using Gaussian distributions and a uniform prior, which enables us to compute the true LLRs and compared the linear and non-linear decision boundaries.
As Fig.
3
indicates, the non-linear one based on Ineq. (
10
) leads to a lower decision cost, particularly, a lower false acceptance rate.
Note that, Ineqs. (
8
) and (
10
) are agnostic to the data distributions, and we use Gaussians in simulation only for convenience.

3.1
Model recipe and evaluation metrics

All eight experimental systems were based on the open-sourced SASV challenge baseline B1 (Fig.
1
) and used the pre-trained
ECAPA-TDNN-based ASV
[
22
]
and AASIST-based CM
[
23
]

•

B1

Results

Calibration before fusion was useful
:
As reported in the original paper
[
5
]
,
B1
achieved an SASV-EER of around 20%. As Fig.
4
(a) shows, due to the large difference in the dynamic ranges of ASV and CM scores, the latter dominated the fused SASV score.
The values of
\mathrm{Cllr}
and
\mathrm{Cllr}_{\text{calib}}
were higher than 1 bit, indicating that the SASV scores were not useful for making decisions.
By calibrating
s_{\text{asv}}
and
s_{\text{cm}}
before fusion,
B1c
reduced the SASV EER to 2.73%, and
\mathrm{Cllr}
-based metrics also improved. From Fig.
4
(b), we can observe that the distribution of
s_{\text{asv}}
was ‘stretched out’, and the fused
s_{\text{sasv}}
better separated the three classes of data.

When fusing LLRs, the system pairs {
L2
,
L2c
} and {
L3
,
L3c
} showed that the gain of the calibration was diminishing. This is understandable because the dynamic range of ASV and CM scores are handled by the covariance matrix of
p(\bm{s};\bm{\theta})
. However, calibrating before fusion was still helpful, especially in the case of linear fusion in Eq. (
6
). Compared with
L2
,
L2c
’s EER was reduced to 1.56%, and its
\mathrm{Cllr}
was only 0.14 bits.

Non-linear fusion of LLRs was better
:
Rather than fusing the ASV and CM scores,
L2
,
L3
, and their variants fused the estimated LLRs.
The nonlinear fusion of LLRs in
L3c
led to the best SASV-EER in our experiments.
The difference between
L3c
and
L2c
was small, possibly because the test data set of the SASV challenge can be well separated using a linear decision boundary in the space of LLRs, as Fig.
4
(c) and (d) show.

Compared with
B1v2
and
Post
, which treat the ASV and CM scores as posterior probabilities,
L2c
,
L3
, and
L3c
performed similarly or better in terms of SASV-EER, and their
\mathrm{Cllr}_{\text{calib}}
s were much lower. The results suggest that the fusion methods based on compositional data analysis are also practically useful.

Finally, the t-EER measures the discrimination power of
\{s_{\text{asv}},s_{\text{cm}}\}
or LLRs and is agnostic to their calibration and fusion. Hence, the t-EERs of
B1
and
B1c
are the same. Similarly, the four systems fusing LLRs have the same t-EER.
It was observed that using the LLRs led to a lower t-EER than raw or sigmoid-transformed
s_{\text{asv}}
and
s_{\text{cm}}
. This is evidence to encourage the sub-systems to produce LLRs.

4
Conclusions

This paper presented an alternative interpretation of SASV score fusion based on compositional data analysis.
We suggested practices and improved fusion methods that may have been ignored or used ad hoc. First, the calibration of scores or LLRs before fusion was found to be helpful, especially when linearly fusing raw scores or LLRs. Second, rather than fusing raw scores, estimating and fusing LLRs turned out to be better in experiments. Third, the linear fusion of LLRs was found to be inferior to the non-linear one in both analysis and experiments.
What is presented in this paper is by no means the only way to interpret SASV fusion, thus is expected to foster other studies.

A.1
Optimal decision policy

We elaborate on the decision policy and linear and non-linear decision boundaries presented in Sec.
2.5
.

A.1.1
SASV hypotheses and actions

In SASV, or the joint operation of ASV and CM, the input pair of the probe and reference
\bm{x}=(\bm{x}^{(p)},\bm{x}^{(r)})\in\mathcal{X}
, either as waveforms or feature vectors, can be from one of the three possible classes
\mathcal{Y}=\{{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{},{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{},{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}\}
[
2
]
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{tar.bf}{}:

\displaystyle\,\bm{x}_{p}\text{ is bona fide \emph{and} matches the speaker identity in }\bm{x}_{r},

\displaystyle\mathsf{non.bf}{}:

\displaystyle\,\bm{x}_{p}\text{ is bona fide \emph{but} the speaker identity does not match},

\displaystyle\mathsf{spf}{}:

\displaystyle\,\bm{x}_{p}\text{ is spoofed}.

In the last class, the SASV system does not need to differentiate whether a spoofed probe
\bm{x}_{p}
sounds like the target speaker. A more detailed discussion is given in Sec.
A.4
.

SASV is used to accept only bona fide speech uttered by the target speaker. Therefore, an SASV system can take one of two possible actions
\mathcal{A}=\{\mathtt{accept},\mathtt{reject}\}
:

\displaystyle\mathtt{accept}:

\displaystyle\,\bm{x}_{p}\text{ is claimed to be from }\mathsf{tar.bf}{}\text{ and accepted},

\displaystyle\mathtt{reject}:

\displaystyle\,\bm{x}_{p}\text{ is claimed to be NOT from }\mathsf{tar.bf}{}\text{ and rejected}.

In the case of
\mathtt{reject}
, SASV does not need to differentiate whether
\bm{x}_{p}
is
\mathsf{non.bf}{}
or
\mathsf{spf}{}
.

A.1.2
Decision cost and optimal decision policy

An SASV system may take wrong actions. A sample from
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
may be falsely rejected (missed), while samples from
\mathsf{non.bf}{}
or
\mathsf{spf}{}
may be falsely accepted.
We can assign a decision cost to each incorrect decision.
Given an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
and the ground-truth label
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we use
C(a,y)\geq 0
to denote the decision-cost function
c:\mathcal{A}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
A general definition of
C(a,y)
is given in Table
2
.

Following the Bayesian decision theory
[
11
]
, we can compute the conditional risk
R(a|\bm{x})
when taking an action
a
given the input sample
\bm{x}
as

R(a|\bm{x})=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}C(a,y)P(y|\bm{x}),

(12)

where
P(y|\bm{x})
is the posterior probability.
By plugging in the possible values of
C(a,y)
listed in Table
2
, we can compute the conditional risks when taking the two possible actions

\displaystyle R(\mathtt{accept}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})+C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x}),

(13)

\displaystyle R(\mathtt{reject}|\bm{x})

\displaystyle=C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x}).

(14)

To minimize the overall risk, the Bayes decision rule tells us to select the action with a smaller conditional risk
[
11
]
. Accordingly, the decision policy is

{a}=\begin{cases}\mathtt{accept},\quad\text{if }R(\mathtt{reject}|\bm{x})>R(\mathtt{accept}|\bm{x})\\
\mathtt{reject},\quad\text{if }R(\mathtt{reject}|\bm{x})\leq R(\mathtt{accept}|\bm{x})\\
\end{cases}.

(15)

In other words, the optimal decision policy for an SASV system is to
\mathtt{accept}
the input probe if the conditional risk
R(\mathtt{accept}|\bm{x})
is smaller than
R(\mathtt{reject}|\bm{x})
. Otherwise, the system should
\mathtt{reject}
the input.

) and (

), we re-write the condition to accept the input, i.e.,
R(\mathtt{reject}|\bm{x})>R(\mathtt{accept}|\bm{x})
, as

\begin{split}C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})&>\\
C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}&P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})+C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x}).\end{split}

(16)

Using the Bayes’ formula, we write the left and right hand sides as

C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})=C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}}{p(\bm{x})},

(17)

and

\begin{split}&C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})+C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x})\\
=&C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{})\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}}{p(\bm{x})}+C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{})\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}{p(\bm{x})}.\end{split}

(18)

Plugging the above two equations into Ineq. (
16
) and cancel
p(\bm{x})
on both sides of the inequality, Ineq. (
16
) can be written in terms of likelihoods and priors as

\begin{split}C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}&>\\
C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{})&\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{})\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}.\end{split}

(19)

By dividing both sides by
C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})
, we obtain

\begin{split}\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}>\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{})}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})}\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{})}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{})}\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}},\end{split}

(20)

which can be further written as

\begin{split}\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}>\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}},\end{split}

(21)

where the LLRs are defined as
\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})\triangleq\log\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})}
and
\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})\triangleq\log\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})}
.

Because the samples from
\mathsf{non.bf}{}
and
\mathsf{spf}{}
should be rejected, they can be conceptually grouped together as a “negative” class. The prior probability of having a sample from this “negative” class is therefore
\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}
, and, among the “negative” samples, the proportion of
\pi_{\mathsf{spf}}
is defined as

\rho\triangleq\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}{\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}.

(22)

The ratio
\rho
is referred to as
spoof prevalence prior
in the t-EER
[
6
]
. Furthermore, The ratio of the priors of the
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
against the “negative class” can be defined as

\beta\triangleq\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}}{\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}}=\frac{\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}}{1-\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}}.

(23)

By dividing both sides of Ineq. (
21
) with
\pi_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}+\pi_{\mathsf{spf}{}}
, we finally obtain

\begin{split}\beta>\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho,\end{split}

(24)

In short, to make the optimal decision, an SASV system computes the right hand side of Ineq. (
24
) given the LLRs and the priors. The value can be interpreted as a ‘score’. The system then compares the score with the left hand side to make a decision. If the score is smaller than
\beta
, the system
\mathtt{accept}
the input.

Note that the procedure assumes that a lower score indicates favors
\mathtt{accept}
more. In most implementations, it is more intuitive if a
higher
score indicates favors
\mathtt{accept}
more. This can be easily done re-written Ineq.(
24
), for example, as

\begin{split}-\Big[\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho\Big]>-\beta.\end{split}

(25)

We can further take
\log(\cdot)
on both sides. Notice that the left hand side increases if the two LLRs increase. In other words, if the LLRs give more weights to
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
, the decision favors
\mathtt{accept}
more.

A.1.3
On decoupling LLR and priors

Let us consider an extreme case without spoofed data, i.e.,
\rho=0
and
\pi_{\mathsf{spf}}{}=0
.
Ineq. (
24
) can be written as

\begin{split}\beta>\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})},\end{split}

(26)

or, with
-\log(\cdot)
applied to both sides of the inequality,

\begin{split}\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})>\log\frac{1-\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}}{\pi_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}}\frac{C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}}{C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}}.\end{split}

(27)

This is the optimal decision policy for ASV as a typical binary classification task
[
27
, (2.33)]
.

Note that the LLRs are on the left hand side, while the priors (and decision costs) are on the right hand side.
Because the LLRs are decoupled from the priors, the system can be designed to produce the LLRs without considering the priors. It is the user, who deploys the system into an application, assign the decision costs, asserted priors, and accordingly the decision threshold.
The decoupled LLRs and priors are preferred in a forensic context
[
28
,
27
]
. A similar decision policy can be derived when
\rho=1
, i.e., CM without ASV.

Unfortunately, when
\rho\in(0,1)
, the LLRs and priors cannot be decoupled. The
\rho
is needed to compute the score, and the SASV system designer has to estimate the value of the
\rho
, for example, on the basis of the statistics from the development data. In our experiment,
\rho
was found through grid search by minimizing the decision risks on the development data.

A.2
Optimal decision policy when decision costs are equal

For an actual application, we need to assign values to the decision costs on the basis of the application’s requirements. For example, the cost for missing a sample of
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
may be high for a smart-speech device since the user may be annoyed if their voice is falsely rejected. However, the costs for falsely accepting
\mathsf{spf}{}
and
\mathsf{non.bf}{}
may be high for online banking. False acceptance of imposters may lead to disastrous financial loss.

Following the common assumptions in other studies (e.g.,
[
21
]
), we now consider a particular case in which
C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}=C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}=C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}
.
Accordingly, the optimal policy of action for the SASV system is to
\mathtt{accept}
when

\begin{split}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})+P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x}),\end{split}

(28)

or, equivalently,

\begin{split}\beta>e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho.\end{split}

(29)

As mentioned above, the SASV system needs to compute the right hand side of Ineq. (
29
) as a score. The score is then compared with
\beta
to make a decision.

In practice, we assume that a higher SASV score indicates that the input is more likely from
\mathsf{tar.bf}{}
. Hence, we take the negative logarithm over the two sides of Ineq. (
29
) and obtain

\begin{split}-\log\big[e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho\big]>-\log\beta.\end{split}

(30)

Taking the logarithm makes the left hand side similar to conducting a log-sum-exp operation on the LLRs,
6
6

6

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LogSumExp
albeit a weighted summation with weights
\rho
and
1-\rho
.

In implementation, the SASV system computes the score
s_{\text{sasv}}=-\log\big[e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho\big]
for an input
\bm{x}
. It compares
s_{\text{sasv}}
with the threshold
-\log\beta
for making the decision.

Recall that if we assume the ASV and CM score vector
\bm{s}=[s_{\text{asv}},s_{\text{cm}}]^{\top}
encodes all the relevant information about the input sample
\bm{x}
, we can compute the LLRs given
\bm{s}
as the observed data. We then obtain the scoring function in Eq. (
11
) of Sec.
2.5
.

A.2.1
Link to Gaussian fusion back-end

Note that

\begin{split}s_{\text{sasv}}&=-\log\big[e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}(\bm{x})}(1-\rho)+e^{-\mathsf{llr}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}(\bm{x})}\rho\big]\\
&=-\log\Big[\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}(1-\rho)+\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}\rho\Big]\\
&=-\log\Big[\frac{(1-\rho)p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})+\rho p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})}{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}\Big]\\
&=\log\frac{p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}})}{(1-\rho)p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}})+\rho p(\bm{x}|{H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}})}.\end{split}

(31)

This equation has the same form as the so-called Gaussian fusion back-end
[
21
, Eq.(1)]
, but we have shown how it is derived from the optimal decision policy, given the decision-cost matrix defined in Table
2
and configuration
C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}{}=C_{\text{fa}}^{\mathsf{spf}{}}{}=C_{\text{miss}}^{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}{}
.

Note that the Gaussian fusion back-end parameterizes each likelihood function using a Gaussian distribution, but any other parametric distribution can be used.

A.2.2
Link to the sub-optimal decision policy

In Sec.
2.5
, we showed that the linear fusion of LLRs is linked to the decision policy in Ineqs. (
7
) and (
8
). For convenience, we write Ineqs. (
7
) again below

P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})^{2}>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x})P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),

(32)

It has been described that any vector of posterior probabilities
\bm{P}=[P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x}),P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})]^{\top}
that satisfies Ineq. (
28
) also satisfies Ineq. (
32
), but the reverse is not always true. In other words, Ineq. (
32
) is a necessary but not sufficient condition to achieve Ineq. (
28
).

Ineq. (
32
) is necessary to achieve Ineq. (
28
)
:
Note that
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}{}}|\bm{x})+P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}{}}|\bm{x})+P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}{}}|\bm{x})=1
. Therefore, Ineq. (
28
) can be written as

\begin{split}P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})&>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})+P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x})\\
&=1-P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x}),\end{split}

(33)

or, equivalently,
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})>0.5
. Therefore, for a
\bm{P}
that satisfies Ineq. (
28
), we know that
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})>0.5
,
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})<0.5
,
P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x})<0.5
, and

P({H}{}_{\mathsf{tar.bf}}{}|\bm{x})^{2}>0.25>P({H}{}_{\mathsf{non.bf}}{}|\bm{x})P({H}{}_{\mathsf{spf}}{}|\bm{x}).

(34)

Hence, Ineq. (
32
) is a necessary condition of Ineq. (
28
).

Ineq. (
32
) is not a sufficient condition of Ineq. (
28
)
:
In Seq.
2.5
, we provided an example
\bm{P}=[0.05,0.65,0.3]
that satisfies Ineq. (
32
) but not Ineq. (
28
):
0.3^{2}>0.05\times 0.65
but
0.3<0.05+0.65
. Hence, Ineq. (
32
) is not a sufficient condition of Ineq. (
28
).

A.3
Comparing with ternary classification

In Sec.
A.1
, we defined SASV as a classification task with two actions but three data classes. We may compare it with a ternary classification task. For convenience, let the three classes be
\mathcal{Y}=\{{H}{}_{1},{H}{}_{2},{H}{}_{3}\}
. The three possible actions are to assign the input to one of the three classes.

A.3.1
Minimizing the decision cost

We can define
C(a,y)
in Table
3
.
According to the Bayes decision rule, the system should classify the input sample
\bm{x}
to
{H}{}_{1}
if

\sum_{i}C_{i1}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})<\sum_{i}C_{i2}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})

(35)

and

\sum_{i}C_{i1}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})<\sum_{i}C_{i3}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x}).

(36)

Similar conditions can be written for accepting
{H}{}_{2}
and
{H}_{3}
.

If
C_{ii}=0
, i.e., no cost for correct action, the condition is

\begin{split}C_{21}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+&C_{31}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})\\
&<C_{12}P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})+C_{32}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})\end{split}

(37)

and

\begin{split}C_{21}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+&C_{31}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})\\
&<C_{23}p({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+C_{13}p({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x}).\end{split}

(38)

If we further set
C_{32}=C_{23}=0
as Table
4
shows, the condition becomes

\begin{split}C_{21}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+C_{31}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})<C_{12}P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})\end{split}

(39)

and

\begin{split}C_{21}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+C_{31}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})<C_{13}p({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x}).\end{split}

(40)

Merging the two sub-conditions, we further get

\begin{split}C_{21}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})+C_{31}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x})<\min(C_{13},C_{12})p({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x}).\end{split}

(41)

A.3.2
Maximizing the decision utility

In contrast to minimizing the decision cost, we may conduct a Bayesian decision by maximizing the utility
[
20
]
. Let us define a utility function
U(a,y)
that returns the utility (or reward) when the system the action
a
while the ground-truth label is
y
.
A generic value table of
U(a,y)
for a ternary classification task is shown in Table
5
.
Note that, different from the decision cost, we should set utilities so that
U_{ii}>U_{ij}\geq 0,i\neq{j}
.

The system accepts
{H}{}_{1}
if

\sum_{i}U_{i1}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})>\sum_{i}U_{i2}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})

(43)

and

\sum_{i}U_{i1}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x})>\sum_{i}U_{i3}P({H}{}_{i}|\bm{x}).

(44)

Similar conditions can be written for accepting
{H}{}_{2}
and
{H}_{3}
.

Although the condition looks similar to that for minimizing the decision cost, a nuance is that we can define a diagonal utility matrix, i.e.,
U_{ii}>0
and
U_{ij}=0,i\neq{j}
. This means that only the correct decision receives a reward.
In this case, the condition to accept
{H}{}_{1}
can be written as

U_{11}P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})>U_{22}P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})

(45)

and

U_{11}P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})>U_{33}P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x}).

(46)

If we further set
U_{ii}=U_{jj}=U,\forall{i,j}
as Table
6
shows, the condition becomes

P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{2}|\bm{x})\land P({H}{}_{1}|\bm{x})>P({H}{}_{3}|\bm{x}),

(47)

where
\land
is the logical operator AND.

In
[
15
, sec.10.3]
, Ineq. (
47
) and its relationship with compositional data analysis is discussed.
Inequality. (
47
) is different from Ineq. (
28
): the former is derived when maximizing the decision utility given a scalar utility matrix.

A.4
On spoofed sample

A spoofed sample should be rejected by SASV regardless of whether it sounds like the target speaker. If the spoofed probe is forged by an attacker (i.e., biometric imposters in ISO/IEC 30107), it is supposed to sound like the target speaker’s voice; however, the algorithm to create the spoofed probe may not be good enough to mimic the voice. Hence, the spoofed sample should be rejected regardless of its similarity to the target speaker’s voice.

Suppose the ‘spoofed’ sample is created by an authentic user who wants to make it less similar to their own voice (i.e., biometric concealers). In that case, this application is considered more relevant to voice anonymization
[
29
]
rather than SASV. Of course, an SASV system should also reject the ‘spoofed’ sample since it intended use is to conceal the speaker’s identity.
