Analysis of Regularized Learning in Banach Spaces for Linear-functional Data
*

Abstract
This article delves into the study of the theory of regularized learning in Banach spaces for linear-functional data. It encompasses discussions on representer theorems, pseudo-approximation theorems, and convergence theorems. Regularized learning is designed to minimize regularized empirical risks over a Banach space. The empirical risks are calculated by utilizing training data and multi-loss functions. The input training data are composed of linear functionals in a predual space of the Banach space to capture discrete local information from multimodal data and multiscale models. Through the regularized learning, approximations of the exact solution to an unidentified or uncertain original problem are globally achieved. In the convergence theorems, the convergence of the approximate solutions to the exact solution is established through the utilization of the weak* topology of the Banach space. The theorems of regularized learning are utilized in the interpretation of classical machine learning, such as support vector machines and artificial neural networks.

Introduction

In numerous practical problems, the main goal frequently involves determining an exact solution to minimize expected risks or errors over a Banach space, as exemplified in Optimization (
3.1

The exact solution will provide the optimal decision rule for ascertaining the most appropriate course of action.
Unfortunately, the expected risks are frequently unidentified or uncertain. Hence, it is difficult to determine the exact solution to the original problem directly.
In various mathematical models within the fields of physics and engineering, observed data are collected and measured from the original problem.
Moreover, the large-scale data are employed to approximate the exact solution.
Regularized learning provides an effective method for developing approximate solutions.
Regularized learning, as presented here, focuses on seeking an approximate solution to minimize regularized empirical risks that are the sum of empirical risks and regularization terms, as exemplified in Optimization (
3.4

The motivation for employing regularized learning is provided by the computation of empirical risks using finitely many training data and simplistic loss functions, as exemplified in Equation (

and the approximation of expected risks by empirical risks, as exemplified in Equation (

Regularized learning plays a vital role in various disciplines, including statistical learning, regression analysis, approximation theory, inverse problems, and signal processing.
Regularized learning is applied in various fields, such as engineering, computer science, psychology, intelligent medicine, and economic decision making.

The theory of regularized learning has already achieved a success in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS), exemplified by support vector machines in
SteinwartChristmann2008

The learning theory has recently been extended to reproducing kernel Banach spaces (RKBS) in

Regularized learning is commonly employed to analyze classical input data composed of regular vectors.

Our primary concept is that regularized learning is locally interpreted through linear-functional data and the exact solution is globally approximated through regularized learning.
This article delves into the entire theory of regularized learning through the utilization of the weak* topology of the Banach space.
It encompasses discussions on the representer theorems, pseudo-approximation theorems, and convergence theorems. The convergence theorems ensure that the approximate solutions converge to the exact solution, and the representer theorems or pseudo-approximation theorems ensure that the approximate solutions are computed either equivalently or approximately through finite-dimensional optimization.

2.2
Motivations of Linear-functional Data

For classical machine learning of regression and classification,
we will approximate an exact solution using classical input data

x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{N}\in\Omega,

where
\Omega
is a subset of

If the machine learning problem issues in

where
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is a RKHS and

The proof of the representer theorem in RKHS relies on the reproduction of RKHS which demonstrates that

is a

for a

\langle f,\delta_{x}\rangle=f(x)
for any

Recently, the representer theorem in RKBS in

\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)

p

Therefore, this concept prompts an exploration of machine learning problems through

This shows that linear-functional data can be a discretization of integral equations and differential equations, for example

For example of classical machine learning, we have

\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}\right)=\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{n}}L\left(\xi_{k}^{\ast},y_{k},t_{k}\right),\quad\text{for }\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ast}^{N_{n}},~\boldsymbol{y}\in Y^{N_{n}},~\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}},

(2.9)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}=\left(\xi_{1}^{\ast},\xi_{2}^{\ast},\cdots,\xi_{N_{n}}^{\ast}\right)
,

\boldsymbol{y}=\left(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{N_{n}}\right)
, and
\boldsymbol{t}=\left(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{N_{n}}\right)
.
We say that
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a
lower semi-continuous, continuous, or convex multi-loss function

if
\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\cdot\right)
is lower semi-continuous, continuous, or convex for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ast}^{N_{n}}
and all
\boldsymbol{y}\in Y^{N_{n}}
.
We say that
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a
local Lipschitz continuous multi-loss function

if for any
\theta>0
, there exists a
C_{n}^{\theta}>0
such that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ast}^{N_{n}},\boldsymbol{y}\in Y^{N_{n}}}\left\lvert\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}_{1}\right)-\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}_{2}\right)\right\rvert\leq C_{n}^{\theta}\lVert\boldsymbol{t}_{1}-\boldsymbol{t}_{2}\rVert_{\infty},

for all
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\boldsymbol{t}_{2}\in[-\theta,\theta]^{N_{n}}
.
We denote a collection of all multi-loss functions
by

\mathcal{L}:=\left\{\mathbb{L}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
uniformly local Lipschitz continuous
if
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}^{\theta}<\infty
for all
\theta
.
The local Lipschitz continuity is a prevalent condition of loss functions that is similar to
(
SteinwartChristmann2008
, Definition 2.18)
for support vector machines.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
has the formula as in Equation (
2.9
), then the following hold.

(a)

If
L
is a lower semi-continuous, continuous, convex, or local Lipschitz continuous loss function,
then
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a lower semi-continuous, continuous, convex, or local Lipschitz continuous multi-loss function.

(b)

If
L
is a local Lipschitz continuous loss function, then
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous.

3
Regularized Learning in Banach Spaces

In numerous machine learning problems, the primary objective is to identify an
exact solution

f^{0}
to minimize the expected risks (errors) over a Banach space
\mathcal{B}
, that is,

R\big(f^{0}\big)=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}}R(f),

(3.1)

where
R:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\infty)
is an
expected risk function
.
Let
\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
be the collection of all minimizers of Optimization (
3.1
).
Thus,
f^{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
.
Unfortunately,
R
is frequently unidentified or uncertain, which complicates the resolution of Optimization (
3.1
).
To address the abstract problem, we approximate the exact solution using training data.

Remark 3.1
.

In practical applications, any minimizer of Optimization (
3.1
) is a feasible solution.
Hence, the assumption of the singleton of
\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
is unnecessary.
If
\mathcal{B}
is a dense subspace of a topological vector space
\mathcal{X}
and
R
is continuously extended on
\mathcal{X}
, then Optimization (
3.1
) can be viewed as an equivalent problem of the minimization of
R
over

\mathcal{X}
.
Thus, the existence of
f^{0}
can be assumed in the following discussions.
In this article, our focus is solely on the minimization of
R
over
\mathcal{B}
that has a predual space
\mathcal{B}_{\ast}
ensuring the well-defined nature of the weak* topology of
\mathcal{B}
.

3.1
Regularized Learning for Linear-functional Data

For any
n
th approximation step, we utilize the training data
\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\right)
in Equation (
2.5
) and the multi-loss function
\mathbb{L}_{n}
in Equation (
2.8
) to construct an
empirical risk function

R_{n}
, that is,

R_{n}(f):=\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n},\langle f,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle\right),\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{B}.

(3.2)

Thus, the empirical risks are computable through the utilization of training data and multi-loss functions.
The output
\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
can be viewed as the values of the exact solution at the input
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}
without or with noises, for example of noiseless data,

\boldsymbol{y}_{n}=\langle f^{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle
in regression and

\boldsymbol{y}_{n}=\mathrm{sign}\left(\langle f^{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle\right)
in classification.
Roughly speaking, the approximate solution driven from the training data can be viewed as an equivalent element of the normal vector of a decision rule.
Furthermore, the multi-loss functions can be nonsmooth and nonconvex.

In the same manner as classical learning theory, empirical risks are employed to approximate expected risks, and empirical risks are considered as explicitly computable discretizations of expected risks.
Therefore,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{L}
are chosen such that

R_{n}
converges pointwise to
R
when
n\to\infty
, that is,

R(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}\left(f\right),\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{B}.

(3.3)

The pointwise convergence represents a weak condition that is applicable in practical problems, even in case where
R_{n}
is employed to approximate a simplified form of
R
.
In the field of learning theory, the viability of regularized learning derives from an approximation law using a large amount of data.
Therefore, according to Equation (
3.3
), the approximate solutions will be determined by employing the empirical risk functions.
In order to verify generalization and prevent overfitting,
we determine an
approximate solution

f_{n}^{\lambda}
to minimize the regularized empirical risks over
\mathcal{B}
, that is,

R_{n}\big(f_{n}^{\lambda}\big)+\lambda\Phi\big(\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda}\rVert\big)=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}}R_{n}(f)+\lambda\Phi\left(\lVert f\rVert\right),

(3.4)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter and

\Phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous strictly increasing function such that
\Phi\left(r\right)\to\infty
when
r\to\infty
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
be a collection of all minimizers of Optimization (
3.4
).
Since
\Phi
is fixed and unfocused, we do not index
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
for
\Phi
.
Thus,
f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
.
A specific example of regularized learning is a classical binary classifier for hinge loss, that is

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\max\left\{0,1-y_{k}f(x_{k})\right\}+\lambda\lVert f\rVert^{2},

where the binary data
\left\{(x_{k},y_{k}):k\in\mathbb{N}_{N}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\pm 1\}
.

Remark 3.2
.

By employing the identical method of classical regularization,
we consider the simplistic form of the regularization terms, which rely exclusively on the regularization parameters and the norms of Banach spaces.
There exist numerous alternative formulas for regularization terms aimed at mitigating the risk of overfitting.
For example, the regularization term
\lambda\Phi\left(\lVert f\rVert\right)
can be extended in a more general form

\tilde{\Phi}:\mathcal{B}\times[0,\infty)^{M}\to[0,\infty)
that satisfies the following conditions:

\bullet

For all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{M}
,
\tilde{\Phi}\left(f_{1},\lambda\right)>\tilde{\Phi}\left(f_{2},\lambda\right)
if and only if
\lVert f_{1}\rVert>\lVert f_{2}\rVert
,
\tilde{\Phi}\left(f,\lambda\right)\to\infty
when
\lVert f\rVert\to\infty
, and
\tilde{\Phi}(\cdot,\lambda)
is a weakly* lower semi-continuous function.

\bullet

For all
f\in\mathcal{B}
,
\tilde{\Phi}(f,0)=0
,

\tilde{\Phi}\left(f,\lambda\right)/\lVert\lambda\rVert_{2}\to\Phi\left(\lVert f\rVert\right)
when
\lambda\to 0
,
and
\tilde{\Phi}(f,\cdot)
is a concave function.

By a similar argument, the same conclusions still hold true for the extended
\tilde{\Phi}\left(f,\lambda\right)
.

Optimization (
3.4
) is a primary formula in regularized learning.
Regularized learning is evidently founded techniques of regularization and

R_{n}
plays a crucial role. We will study the characteristics of
R_{n}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

(a)

Then

R_{n}(f)=R_{n}(f+h),\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{B}\text{ and all }h\in ker\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\right).

(b)

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a lower semi-continuous, continuous, or convex multi-loss function, then
R_{n}
is weakly* lower semi-continuous, weakly* continuous, or convex.

(c)

If
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{B}_{\ast}
and
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous, then
\left\{R_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is weakly* equicontinuous on
rB_{\mathcal{B}}
for all
r>0
.

(d)

Suppose that all conditions of (c) hold true. If
R_{n}
converges pointwise to
R
when
n\to\infty
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{f\in rB_{\mathcal{B}}}\left\lvert R(f)-R_{n}(f)\right\rvert=0,\quad\text{for all }r>0.

(e)

Suppose that all conditions of (c) hold true. Let
\left(f_{\alpha}\right)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
be any weakly* convergent bounded net to an element
f_{0}
and
\left(\lambda_{\alpha}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
be any net. If

\lim_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\alpha}}\sup_{\xi^{\ast}\in\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}}\left\lvert\langle f_{0}-f_{\alpha},\xi^{\ast}\rangle\right\rvert=0,

then

\lim_{\alpha}\frac{1}{\lambda_{\alpha}}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lvert R_{n}\left(f_{0}\right)-R_{n}\left(f_{\alpha}\right)\right\rvert=0.

In the next section, we will explore the representer theorems, pseudo-approximation theorems, and convergence theorems of the approximate solutions.
Generally speaking, the convergence theorems ensure the approximation of regularized learning, and the representer theorems and pseudo-approximation theorems ensure the computation of regularized learning.

3.2
Approximate Approximation

Let
\mathcal{U}_{m}
be a subset of
\mathcal{B}
such that there exists a surjection
\Gamma_{m}
from
\Omega_{m}
onto
\mathcal{U}_{m}
,
where
\Omega_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
For example,
\mathcal{U}_{m}
is a
m
dimensional subspace, and
\mathcal{U}_{m}
is a collection of artificial neural networks with
m
weights and biases despite the possibility of it not being a linear space.
Now, we consider an approximately approximate solution
f_{nm}^{\lambda}
to minimize the regularized empirical risks over
\mathcal{U}_{m}
, that is,

R_{n}\big(f_{nm}^{\lambda}\big)+\lambda\Phi\big(\lVert f_{nm}^{\lambda}\rVert\big)=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{U}_{m}}R_{n}(f)+\lambda\Phi\left(\lVert f\rVert\right).

(3.5)

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{U}_{m}\right)
be a collection of all minimizers of Optimization (
3.5
).
Thus,
f_{nm}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{U}_{m}\right)
.

According to Remark
4.1
, if
\mathcal{B}_{\ast}
is smooth, then the representer theorems ensure that there exists a
\mathcal{U}_{N_{n}}
such that
f_{n}^{\lambda}
is equivalently solved by Optimization (
3.5
), such as support vector machines in RKHS and RKBS.
In numerous machine learning problems, the fast algorithms are developed by utilizing the specialized
\mathcal{U}_{m}
.
In this case,
f_{n}^{\lambda}
may not belong to any
\mathcal{U}_{m}
.
Therefore, we introduce another condition of
\mathcal{U}_{m}
known as universal approximation.
Under the condition of universal approximation,
the pseudo-approximation theorems ensure that
f_{n}^{\lambda}
is approximately solved by Optimization (
3.5
), such as artificial neural networks of using ReLU an Kan.

Moreover, we can solve a minimizer
\boldsymbol{w}_{m}
of the finite-dimensional optimization

\inf_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\Omega_{m}}\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y}_{n},\langle\Gamma_{m}(\boldsymbol{w}),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle\right)+\lambda\Phi\left(\lVert\Gamma_{m}(\boldsymbol{w})\rVert\right).

(3.6)

This shows that
f_{nm}^{\lambda}=\Gamma_{m}\left(\boldsymbol{w}_{m}\right)
.
According to the surjection from
\Omega_{m}
onto
\mathcal{U}_{m}
,
Optimization (

) is an equivalent transformation of Optimization (

3.3
Applications to Composite Algorithms

In current researches of machine learning, numerous algorithms focus on addressing a singular problem.
The theory of regularized learning provides a straightforward approach to integrating multiple models and data into a unified system. Now, we will illustrate the concept with a generic example.

Let
\mathcal{B}^{b},\mathcal{B}^{w}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
be two Banach spaces and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{b},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{w}
be their norms, respectively.
We assume that the exact solution
f^{0}\in\mathcal{B}^{b}\cap\mathcal{B}^{w}
is solved by the both abstract models

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{b}}R^{b}(f),\quad\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{w}}R^{w}(f),

where
R^{b}:\mathcal{B}^{b}\to[0,\infty)
and
R^{w}:\mathcal{B}^{w}\to[0,\infty)
are two expected risk functions.
Moreover,
R^{b},R^{w}
are approximated by the empirical risk functions
R_{n}^{b},R_{n}^{w}
, respectively. As shown in Equation (
3.2
),
R_{n}^{b},R_{n}^{w}
are constructed by the training data
\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{b\ast}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n}^{b}\big),\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{w\ast}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n}^{w}\big)
and multi-loss functions
\mathbb{L}_{n}^{b},\mathbb{L}_{n}^{w}
, respectively.
Thus,
f^{0}
is solved approximately by the both regaulairzed learning

\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{b}}R_{n}^{b}(f)+\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{b},\quad\inf_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{w}}R_{n}^{w}(f)+\lambda\lVert f\rVert_{w}.

Let
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}^{b}\cap\mathcal{B}^{w}
be a normed space endowed with the composite norm, that is,
\lVert f\rVert:=\lVert f\rVert_{b}+\lVert f\rVert_{w}
for
f\in\mathcal{B}^{b}\cap\mathcal{B}^{w}
.
Obviously,
\mathcal{B}
is a Banach space.
Since
\mathcal{B}^{b}\cap\mathcal{B}^{w}\cong\big(\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}\big)^{\ast}\cap\big(\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}\big)^{\ast}\cong\big(\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}+\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}\big)^{\ast}
, we have

\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}+\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}\cong\big(\mathcal{B}^{b}\cap\mathcal{B}^{w}\big)_{\ast}
.
Thus,
\mathcal{B}
has a predual space
\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}+\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}
, and

\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ast}
if
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}
or
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}
. This shows that the weak* convergence in
\sigma\left(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}_{\ast}\right)

implies the weak* convergence in
\sigma\big(\mathcal{B}^{b},\mathcal{B}^{b}_{\ast}\big)
and
\sigma\big(\mathcal{B}^{w},\mathcal{B}^{w}_{\ast}\big)
.
We construct the composite models, that is,

R:=R^{b}+R^{w},\quad R_{n}:=R_{n}^{b}+R_{n}^{w}.

Thus,
f^{0}
is a minimizer of the minimization of
R
over
\mathcal{B}
, and
f^{0}
is solved approximately by the composite regularized learning for minimizing
R_{n}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert
over
\mathcal{B}
.
If
R_{n}^{b},R_{n}^{w}
both satisfy the conditions of the representer theorems, pseudo-approximation theorems, and convergence theorems in Section
4
,
then it is easy to check that
R_{n}
satisfies the same conditions.
Therefore, the representer theorems, approximation theory, and convergence theorems hold true for the composite algorithms if they hold true for both individual algorithms.

Let
T^{b}_{n,\lambda}:=R_{n}^{b}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert_{b}
and

T^{w}_{n,\lambda}:=R_{n}^{w}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert_{w}
.
If
R_{n}^{b}
and
R_{n}^{w}
are weakly* lower semi-continuous and convex, then the minimizers of
the minimizations of
R_{n}^{b}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert_{b}
and
R_{n}^{w}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert_{w}
over
\mathcal{B}^{b}
and
\mathcal{B}^{w}
can be iteratively computed through the proximal gradient methods, respectively, that is,

g_{k+1}\in\text{prox}_{\theta T^{b}_{n,\lambda}}\left(g_{k}\right)\text{ or }g_{k+1}\in\text{prox}_{\theta T^{w}_{n,\lambda}}\left(g_{k}\right),~k=0,1,\ldots,

where
prox
represents a proximal operator and
\theta>0
is a step size.
Under the same hypotheses, the minimizer of the minimization of
R_{n}+\lambda\lVert\cdot\rVert
over
\mathcal{B}

can be iteratively computed through the Douglas-Rachford splitting methods, that is,

h^{b}_{k}\in\text{prox}_{\theta T^{b}_{n,\lambda}}\big(g_{k}\big),~h^{w}_{k}\in\text{prox}_{\theta T^{w}_{n,\lambda}}\big(2h^{b}_{k}-g_{k}\big),~g_{k+1}:=g_{k}+\sigma\big(h^{w}_{k}-h^{b}_{k}\big),

k=0,1,\ldots
, where
\sigma>0
is a relaxation parameter.
This shows that the composite algorithm can be formulated as an alternating iterative system that integrates the both algorithms.
In our forthcoming research, we intend to develop composite algorithms that integrate data-driven and model-driven methods.

In the following theorems, the conditions of
\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}_{n},\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\right)
and
\mathbb{L}_{n}
are consistent with Proposition

. We will explore the characteristics of
f^{0}
,

4.1
Representer Theorems

We improve the generalized representer theorems in Banach spaces in
HuangLiuTanYe2020

using alternative techniques from the unifying representer theorems for convex loss in
Unser2020
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a lower semi-continuous multi-loss function, then
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
is nonempty and weakly* compact.

Specifically, if
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a convex multi-loss function, which implies that
R_{n}
is convex by Proposition
3.1
(b), then
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
is also convex.
Moreover, if
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a convex multi-loss function and
\Phi
is strictly convex, then
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
has at most one element.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a lower semi-continuous multi-loss function,
then for any
f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
,
there exists a parameter
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}
such that

\text{(i) }\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\in\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert\big(f_{n}^{\lambda}\big),\quad\text{(ii) }\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\cdot\langle f_{n}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle=\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda}\rVert.

The “
\cdot
” represents the dot product, such as
\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\xi_{k}^{\ast}
,
where
\boldsymbol{c}:=\left(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{N}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}:=\left(\xi_{1}^{\ast},\xi_{2}^{\ast},\cdots,\xi_{N}^{\ast}\right)
.

Remark 4.1
.

The parameter
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}
in Theorem
4.2

is dependent on the different minimizer
f_{n}^{\lambda}
.
If
\mathcal{B}_{\ast}
is smooth, then
(
Megginson1998
, Proposition 5.4.2)
ensures that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)=\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{U}_{N_{n}}\right)
, where
\mathcal{U}_{N_{n}}
is a collection of all
f\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\in\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert\left(f\right)

for a
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}
.
Specifically, if
\mathcal{B}
is a Hilbert space, then
f_{n}^{\lambda}\cong\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda}\rVert\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}
.
If
f_{n}^{\lambda}\neq 0
,
then the characterizing subdifferential of norm in Equation (
2.1
) shows that

f_{n}^{\lambda}/\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda}\rVert\in\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ast}\left(\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\right)
.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}(\boldsymbol{a}):=\left\{\langle f,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle:f\in\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ast}\left(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\right)\right\}
for any
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}
.
Thus,
f_{n}^{\lambda}
can be solved as follows.

(i)

Solving
r_{n},\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
of the finite-dimensional optimization

\inf_{r\in\mathbb{R},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{n}}}\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y}_{n},r\boldsymbol{v}\right)+\lambda\Phi\left(r\right)\text{ subject to }r\geq 0,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}(\boldsymbol{a}).

(ii)

Choosing
g_{n}\in\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ast}\left(\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\right)
subject to
\langle g_{n},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle=\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
.

(iii)

Taking
f_{n}^{\lambda}:=r_{n}g_{n}
.

Since
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}=\boldsymbol{a}_{n}/\lVert\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rVert_{\ast}
and

r_{n}=\boldsymbol{c}_{n}\cdot\langle f_{n}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle
,
we also have
\lVert\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rVert_{\ast}=\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
.
If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a linear combination of convex loss functions as shown in Equation (
2.9
) and
\mathcal{B}
is a 1-norm RKBS, the proximity algorithm can be utilized to solve
\boldsymbol{c}_{n}
and
f_{n}^{\lambda}
by
(
WangXu2021
, Theorem 64)
.
In our forthcoming papers, we will study various numerical algorithms for specialized loss functions and Banach spaces, such as alternating direction methods of multipliers and composite optimization methods.

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a convex multi-loss function, then
the Krein-Milman theorem ensures that
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
is a closed convex hull of its extreme points.
If
\mathcal{B}
is strictly convex, then for any
f_{n}^{\lambda}\neq 0
, the element
f_{n}^{\lambda}/\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda}\rVert
is an extreme point of
B_{\mathcal{B}}
.
Next, we will verify that there exists a
\tilde{f}_{n}^{\lambda}
, which is
a linear combination of finitely many extreme points of
B_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is a lower semi-continuous multi-loss function,
then for any
f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
,
there exists a
\tilde{f}_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}\right)
such that

\text{(i) }\tilde{f}_{n}^{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{n},\quad\text{(ii) }\langle\tilde{f}_{n}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle=\langle f_{n}^{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\rangle,

where
M_{n}\leq N_{n}
,
the parameter
\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{M_{n}}
, and

\boldsymbol{e}_{n}:=\left(e_{n1},e_{n2},\cdots,e_{nM_{n}}\right)
composes of
extreme points
e_{n1},e_{n2},\ldots,e_{nM_{n}}
of
B_{\mathcal{B}}
.

The generalized representer theorems ensure that the approximate solutions can be computed by the numerical algorithms driven from the equivalent finite-dimensional optimizations.
We can also verify that the generalized representer theorems encompass the classical representer theorems in RKHS and RKBS.
More detailed information regarding the representer theorems is mentioned in

f^{0}=w^{\ast}-\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}},\quad\lVert f^{0}\rVert=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\rVert.

6
Examples of Regularized Learning

In the beginning, we study an example of noisy data.

Example 6.1
.

Let
\Omega:=[0,1]
and a Gaussian kernel

K(x,z):=\exp\big(-\theta^{2}\left\lvert x-z\right\rvert^{2}\big),\quad\text{for }x,z\in\Omega,

where the shape parameter
\theta>0
. As in
(
XuYe2019
, Section 4.4)
,
the RKBS
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
has a predual space
\left(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)\right)_{\ast}\cong\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
is embedded into the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
.
As in
(
FasshauerYe2011Dist
, Example 5.8)
,
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is embedded into the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{j}(\Omega)
of any order
j
. Thus, the Sobolev embedding theorem ensures that
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
is embedded into
\mathrm{C}^{0,1}(\Omega)
.

We consider an expected risk function

R(f):=\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert f(x)-f^{0}(x)\right\rvert\omega(x)\mathrm{d}x,\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega),

where the unknown function
f^{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
and the positive weight
\omega\in\mathrm{C}(\Omega)
.
This shows that
R\left(f^{0}\right)=0
and
f^{0}
is the unique minimizer of
R
over
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
, that is,
\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)\right)=\left\{f^{0}\right\}
.

Next, we have the points
x_{nk}\in((k-1)/n,k/n)
and the noise values
y_{nk}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\left\lvert f^{0}(x_{nk})-y_{nk}\right\rvert\leq\zeta_{nk}
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\zeta_{nk}>0
and
\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\zeta_{nk}\to 0
when
n\to\infty
.
Thus, there exists a
C>0
such that
\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left\lvert f^{0}(x)\right\rvert+\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\zeta_{nk}\leq C
.
This demonstrates that
f^{0}\left(x_{nk}\right),y_{nk}\in[-C,C]
.
By the reproducing property of
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
, the classical noisy data

\left(x_{n1},y_{n1}\right),\left(x_{n2},y_{n2}\right),\ldots,\left(x_{nn},y_{nn}\right)\in\Omega\times[-C,C],\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

are equivalently transferred to the data

\left(\delta_{x_{n1}},y_{n1}\right),\left(\delta_{x_{n2}},y_{n2}\right),\ldots,\left(\delta_{x_{nn}},y_{nn}\right)\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\infty}(\Omega)\times[-C,C],\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}:=\left(\delta_{x_{n1}},\delta_{x_{n2}},\cdots,\delta_{x_{nn}}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{y}_{n}:=\left(y_{n1},y_{n2},\cdots,y_{nn}\right),\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus,
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\delta_{x_{nk_{n}}}:k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{n},~n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and Lemma
2.2
ensures that

\mathfrak{F}_{D}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Let a multi-loss function

\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}L\left(\xi_{k}^{\ast},y_{k},t_{k}\right),\text{ for }\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\big(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\infty}(\Omega)\big)^{n},~\boldsymbol{y}\in[-C,C]^{n},~\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and absolute loss

L\left(\xi^{\ast},y,t\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left\lvert t-y\right\rvert\omega(x),&\text{if }\xi^{\ast}=\delta_{x}\text{ for }x\in\Omega\text{ and }y\in[-C,C],\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{\infty}(\Omega),~y\in[-C,C],~t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\omega
is bounded on
\Omega
,

L
is a local Lipschitz continuous loss function.
Thus, Lemma
2.3
(b) ensures that
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous.
This demonstrates that Condition (II) holds true.

Moreover, the empirical risk function is written as

R_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}L\left(\delta_{x_{nk}},y_{nk},\langle f,\delta_{x_{nk}}\rangle\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lvert f\left(x_{nk}\right)-y_{nk}\right\rvert\omega\left(x_{nk}\right),

for
f\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)
.
Since
\left\lvert f-f^{0}\right\rvert\omega
is continuous and
\omega
is bounded,
R_{n}
converges pointwise to
R
when
n\to\infty
. This demonstrates that Condition (I) holds true.

Let
\Phi(r):=r
for
r\in[0,\infty)
.
Since the extreme point of
B_{\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)}
has the formula as
\sqrt{\vartheta}\varphi
, Corollary
4.3
ensures that there exists an approximate solution

f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{1}(\Omega)\right)

such that
f_{n}^{\lambda}
is a linear combination of

\sqrt{\vartheta_{1}}\varphi_{1},\sqrt{\vartheta_{2}}\varphi_{2},\ldots,\sqrt{\vartheta_{M_{n}}}\varphi_{M_{n}},

where
M_{n}\leq N_{n}

and
\left(\vartheta,\varphi\right)
is a pair of eigenvalue and eigenfunction of
K
.

Finally, we choose a decrease sequence
\left(\lambda_{n}\right)
to
0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\zeta_{nk}}{\lambda_{n}}=0.

Since

R_{n}\big(f^{0}\big)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left\lvert f^{0}\left(x_{nk}\right)-y_{nk}\right\rvert\omega\left(x_{nk}\right)\leq 2\sup_{x\in\Omega}\omega(x)\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\zeta_{nk},

we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{R_{n}\left(f^{0}\right)}{\lambda_{n}}\leq 2\sup_{x\in\Omega}\omega(x)\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\zeta_{nk}}{\lambda_{n}}=0.

Thus, Theorem
4.8
ensures that
\big(f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\big)
is a weakly* convergent bounded sequence to
f^{0}
. This demonstrates that
f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}
converges pointwise to
f^{0}
when
n\to\infty
.
This result is consistent with the variational characterization of Tikhonov regularization.

In this article, we focus on deterministic convergence.
Actually, the pointwise convergence as shown in Equation (
3.3
) can be extended to stochastic convergence, such as
convergence almost surely and convergence in probability.
In the same manner, we can verify the conclusions of the convergence theorems for stochastic data.
Now, let us study an example of a binary classification for stochastic data.

Example 6.2
.

Let
\Omega:=[0,1]^{2}
and
a min kernel

K(x,z):=\min\left\{v_{1},w_{1}\right\}\min\left\{v_{2},w_{2}\right\},\text{ for }x=\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right),z=\left(w_{1},w_{2}\right)\in\Omega.

As stated in
(
XuYe2019
, Section 4.3)
, the RKBS
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)
is a reflexive Banach space
and its dual space is isometrically isomorphic to the RKBS
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
,
where
1<p\leq 2
and
q=p/(p-1)
.
As shown in
(
FasshauerYe2011DiffBound
, Example 5.1)
,
the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)

is equivalent to the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{1,1}_{mix}(\Omega)
of order
1,1
.
Since
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)
is embedded into
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
,
the Sobolev embedding theorem ensures that

\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)
is embedded into
\mathrm{C}^{0,1/2}(\Omega)
.

Given a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega\times\{\pm 1\}
,
we study an expected risk function

R(f):=\int_{\Omega\times\{\pm 1\}}\max\left\{0,1-yf(x)\right\}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P},\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega).

Thus, the binary classifier is constructed by a minimization of
R
over
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)
.

Next, we have the stochastic data
\left(x_{n},y_{n}\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\sim}i.i.d.\left(x,y\right)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By the reproducing property of
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)
, the classical data
\left(x_{n},y_{n}\right)

are equivalently transferred to the data

\left(\delta_{x_{n}},y_{n}\right)
.
Let

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}:=\left(\delta_{x_{1}},\delta_{x_{2}},\cdots,\delta_{x_{n}}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{y}_{n}:=\left(y_{1},y_{2},\cdots,y_{n}\right),\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus,
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\delta_{x_{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.
Lemma
2.2
ensures that

\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
.
Let a multi-loss function

\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}\right):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}L\left(\xi_{k}^{\ast},y_{k},t_{k}\right),\text{ for }\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\big(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)\big)^{n},~\boldsymbol{y}\in\{\pm 1\}^{n},~\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
hinge loss

L\left(\xi^{\ast},y,t\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\left\{0,1-yt\right\},&\text{if }\xi^{\ast}=\delta_{x}\text{ for }x\in\Omega\text{ and }y\in\left\{\pm 1\right\},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega),~y\in\{\pm 1\},~t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
L
is a local Lipschitz continuous loss function,
Lemma
2.3
(b) ensures that
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous.
This demonstrates that Condition (II) holds true.

Moreover, since
L\left(\delta_{x_{n}},y_{n},\langle f,\delta_{x_{n}}\rangle\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\sim}i.i.d.\max\left\{0,1-yf(x)\right\}
,
the strong law of large numbers shows that

R(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}L\left(\delta_{x_{n}},y_{n},\langle f,\delta_{x_{n}}\rangle\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}R_{n}(f)\text{ almost surely},

for all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega
.
This demonstrates that Condition (I) holds true almost surely.

Let
\Phi(r):=r^{p}
for
r\in[0,\infty)
.
If
p=2l/(2l-1)
for a
l\in\mathbb{N}
, then Theorem
4.2
ensures that
there exists an unique approximate solution
f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)\right)

such that
\partial\lVert\cdot\rVert\big(f_{n}^{\lambda}\big)
is a singleton including the unique element that is equivalent to a linear combination
of
K\left(x_{1},\cdot\right),K\left(x_{2},\cdot\right),\ldots,K\left(x_{n},\cdot\right)

Thus,
f_{n}^{\lambda}
is a linear combination of multi-kernel basis

K_{2l}\left(\cdot,x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\cdots,x_{i_{2l-1}}\right),\quad\text{for }i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{2l-1}\in\mathbb{N}_{n},

constructed by eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
K
, as shown in the proof of
(
XuYe2019
, Theorem 5.10)
.

In the same manner as stated in Section
5.4
, we exclude the null set of the unconvergent
R_{n}

to prove that Theorems
4.6
,
4.7
, and
4.8
still hold true almost surely.
Since
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
is separable and complete, the weakly* convergent bounded subnet of
\big(f_{n}^{\lambda}\big)
in Theorem
4.7
can be interchanged to a weakly* convergent sequence
\big(f_{n_{j}}^{\lambda_{j}}\big)
.
Since
\mathrm{span}\left\{\delta_{x}:x\in\Omega\right\}
is dense in
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
,

\left(f_{j}\right)
weakly* converges to
f_{0}
when
j\to\infty

if and only if
\left(f_{j}\right)
converges pointwise to
f_{0}
when
j\to\infty
,
where
\left(f_{j}\right)
is a sequence of
\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
.
In practical applications, if a stochastic sequence
\big(f_{n_{j}}^{\lambda_{j}}\big)
converges pointwise to a deterministic
f^{0}\in\mathcal{B}_{K}^{q}(\Omega)
when
j\to\infty
almost surely, then Theorem
4.7
ensures that
f^{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{B}_{K}^{p}(\Omega)\right)
.
Thus, the binary classifier is constructed by

\mathrm{sign}\left(f^{0}(x)\right)
or
\mathrm{sign}\big(f_{n_{j}}^{\lambda_{j}}(x)\big)
approximately for
x\in\Omega
.

Remark 6.1
.

For binary classification, we can still achieve similar results as shown in Example
6.2
by replacing the hinge loss with various other loss functions, such as truncated least squares loss, logistic loss, and even nonconvex loss.
According to Zhang’s inequality in
(
SteinwartChristmann2008
, Theorem 2.31)
, the exact binary classifier is the Bayes classifier
\mathrm{sign}\left(2\omega(x)-1\right)
,
where
\omega(x):=\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid x)
for
x\in\Omega
.
Unfortunately,
\omega
is usually unknown.
Since
\mathrm{C}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a predual space of
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we will utilize total variations to construct approximate classifiers using nonconvex and nonsmooth loss functions, when
2\omega-1\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in our forthcoming paper.

Next, we study a specific example involving two categories of linear-functional data induced by a Poisson equation.

Example 6.3
.

Let
\Omega:=[0,1]^{2}
and a Sobolev kernel

K(x,z):=\theta^{j-1}\lVert x-z\rVert^{j-1}_{2}\mathcal{K}_{1-j}\left(\theta\lVert x-z\rVert_{2}\right),\quad\text{for }x,z\in\Omega,

where
\theta>0
,
j\geq 4
, and
\mathcal{K}_{1-j}
is the modified Bessel function of the second kind of order
1-j
.
The RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is a reflexive Banach space.
As shown in
(
FasshauerYe2011Dist
, Example 5.7)
,
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is equivalent to the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{j}(\Omega)
of order
j
.
By the Sobolev embedding theorem,

\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is embedded into
\mathrm{C}^{2,1}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{C}^{0,1}(\partial\Omega)
, respectively.

We study a Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary, that is,

\Delta u=h\text{ in }\Omega,\quad u=g\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

where
h\in\mathrm{C}(\Omega)
and
g\in\mathrm{C}(\partial\Omega)
are given such that
the Poisson equation exists the unique solution
f^{0}\in\mathcal{H}^{j}(\Omega)\cong\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
.
Let an expected risk function

R(f):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\Delta f(x)-h(x)\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert f(z)-g(z)\right\rvert^{2}\mathrm{d}S,\quad\text{for }f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega).

Thus,
R\left(f^{0}\right)=0
and
\mathcal{S}^{0}\left(\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)\right)=\left\{f^{0}\right\}
.

Next, we have two categories of linear-functional data induced by the Poisson equation, that is,

\left(\delta_{x_{n1}}\circ\Delta,v_{n1}\right),\left(\delta_{x_{n2}}\circ\Delta,v_{n2}\right),\ldots,\left(\delta_{x_{nn^{2}}}\circ\Delta,v_{nn^{2}}\right),

and

\left(\delta_{z_{n1}},b_{n1}\right),\left(\delta_{z_{n2}},b_{n2}\right),\ldots,\left(\delta_{z_{nn}},b_{nn}\right),

where
v_{nk}:=h\left(x_{nk}\right)
,

x_{nk}
are Halton points in
\Omega
,
b_{nl}:=g\left(z_{nl}\right)
,
and
z_{nl}
are uniform grid points on
\partial\Omega
.
Let

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}:=\left(\delta_{x_{n1}}\circ\Delta,\delta_{x_{n2}}\circ\Delta,\cdots,\delta_{x_{nn^{2}}}\circ\Delta,\delta_{z_{n1}},\delta_{z_{n2}},\cdots,\delta_{z_{nn}}\right),

and

\boldsymbol{y}_{n}:=\left(v_{n1},v_{n2},\cdots,v_{nn^{2}},b_{n1},b_{n2},\cdots,b_{nn}\right),

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus,
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\delta_{x_{nk_{n}}}\circ\Delta,\delta_{z_{nl_{n}}}:k_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{n^{2}},l_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.
Since Halton points are deterministic, the linear-functional data are deterministic.
If we choose Sobol points, then the linear-functional data are stochastic.
Analysis similar to that in the proof of Lemma
2.2
shows that
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}
is relatively compact in
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
.
Let a multi-loss function

\mathbb{L}_{n}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{t}\right):=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{2}}L^{1}\left(\xi_{k}^{\ast},y_{k},t_{k}\right)+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}L^{2}\left(\xi_{n+l}^{\ast},y_{n+l},t_{n+l}\right),

for
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\ast}\in\left(\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)\right)^{n^{2}+n},~\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+n},~\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}+n}
, where
the square losses

L^{1}\left(\xi^{\ast},y,t\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left\lvert t-y\right\rvert^{2},&\text{if }\xi^{\ast}=\delta_{x}\circ\Delta\text{ for }x\in\Omega\text{ and }y\in range(h),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and

L^{2}\left(\xi^{\ast},y,t\right):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\left\lvert t-y\right\rvert^{2},&\text{if }\xi^{\ast}=\delta_{z}\text{ for }z\in\partial\Omega\text{ and }y\in range(g),\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

for
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega),~y\in\mathbb{R},~t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
h,g
are bounded on
\Omega
and
\partial\Omega
, respectively,
L^{1},L^{2}
are both local Lipschitz continuous loss functions.
In the same manner as Lemma
2.3
(b),
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous.
This demonstrates that Condition (II) holds true.

Since

R_{n}(f)=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n^{2}}\left\lvert\Delta f\left(x_{nk}\right)-h\left(x_{nk}\right)\right\rvert+\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\left\lvert f\left(z_{nl}\right)-g\left(z_{nl}\right)\right\rvert,\quad

for
f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
,
the Koksma–Hlawka inequality in quasi-Monte Carlo methods shows that

R_{n}
converges pointwise to
R
when
n\to\infty
.
This demonstrates that Condition (I) holds true.

Let
\Phi(r):=r^{2}
for
r\in[0,\infty)
.
The reproducing property of
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
shows that
\delta_{x}\circ\Delta\cong\Delta_{x}K(x,\cdot)

and
\delta_{z}\cong K(z,\cdot)
for
x\in\Omega
and
z\in\partial\Omega
.
Thus, Theorem
4.2
ensures that there exists the unique approximate solution

f_{n}^{\lambda}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\lambda}\left(\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)\right)

such that
f_{n}^{\lambda}
is a linear combination of

\Delta_{x}K\left(x_{n1},\cdot\right),\Delta_{x}K\left(x_{n2},\cdot\right),\ldots,\Delta_{x}K\left(x_{nn^{2}},\cdot\right),

and

K\left(z_{n1},\cdot\right),K\left(z_{n2},\cdot\right),\ldots,K\left(z_{nn},\cdot\right).

Finally,
we can verify that
R_{n}\left(f^{0}\right)=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore, for any decrease sequence
\left(\lambda_{n}\right)
to
0
,
Theorem
4.8
ensures that
f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}
weakly* converges to
f^{0}

and
\lVert f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\rVert
converges to
\lVert f^{0}\rVert
when
n\to\infty
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{K}(\Omega)
is a Radon-Riesz space,
we have
\lVert f^{0}-f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}\rVert\to 0
when
n\to\infty
.
This demonstrates that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left\lvert\partial^{\vartheta}f^{0}(x)-\partial^{\vartheta}f_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}(x)\right\rvert\to 0,\quad\text{for any }\left\lvert\vartheta\right\rvert\leq 2.

In Examples
6.1
,
6.2
, and
6.3
, we utilize the reproducing kernels to directly solve the approximate solutions according to the representer theorems.
Now, let us study a popular example of artificial neural networks.
Under the universal approximation of two-layer neural networks, we will use sigmoid functions to approximately compute the approximate solutions according to the pseudo-approximation theorems.

Example 6.4
.

Let
\Omega:=[-1,1]^{d}
. It is obvious that
\mathrm{L}_{1}(\Omega)
is a predual space of
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{m}
be a collection of all two-layer neural networks with
m
coefficients, that is,

f_{m}(x)=\mathsf{W}_{2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\left(\mathsf{W}_{1}x+\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\right)+\boldsymbol{b}_{2},\quad\text{for }x\in\Omega,

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is a continuous sigmoid function,
\mathsf{W}_{1},\mathsf{W}_{2}
are weight matrixes, and
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\boldsymbol{b}_{2}
are bias vectors as stated in
(
AnthonyBartlett1999
, Section 6.4)
.
Thus,
\mathcal{U}_{m}\subseteq\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)
and there exists a surjection
\Gamma_{m}
from
\mathbb{R}^{m}
onto
\mathcal{U}_{m}
.
It is easy to check that
\mathcal{U}_{m}
is weakly* closed if the coefficients are bounded.
Since
\left\{\mathcal{U}_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
satisfies the universal approximation in
\mathrm{C}(\Omega)
, as exemplified in
Cybenko1989
;
MhaskarMicchelli1994
,
\left\{\mathcal{U}_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
also satisfies the universal approximation in

\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Moreover,
\langle f,\xi^{\ast}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\xi^{\ast}(x)\mathrm{d}x
for
f\in\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\xi^{\ast}\in\mathrm{L}_{1}(\Omega)
. Thus, the input data for standard sigmoid neural networks or convolutional neural networks
can be equivalently transferred to the linear-functional data
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}^{\ast}\in\left(\mathrm{L}_{1}(\Omega)\right)^{N_{n}}
, such as mollifiers and convolutions.
In the same manner as Lemma
2.2
, we can verify that
\mathfrak{F}_{\mathcal{D}}
is relatively compact for various classical data, such as handwritten digit images.
The multi-loss function
\mathbb{L}_{n}
is represented by the classical loss function
L
as shown in Equation (
2.9
). Typically,
L
is chosen as a local Lipschitz continuous loss function for developing algorithms for multi-layer neural networks.
Thus, Lemma
2.3
(b) ensures that
\mathcal{L}
is uniformly local Lipschitz continuous.
This demonstrates that Condition (II) holds true.

For various problems of machine learning, the expected risks may be unidentified or uncertain, while the empirical risks are computable through two-layer neural networks based on
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}
and

Final Remark

In this article,
the theory of Banach spaces serves as fundamental basis for regularized learning in the analysis of linear-functional data.
Through the techniques of weak* topology, we finalize the proofs of the representer theorems, pseudo-approximation theorems, and convergence theorems in regularized learning.
My philosophical concept is to approximate unknown rules through explicit models and observed data using the theory of regularized learning.
The objective of regularized learning is to identify an approximate solution that can be effectively computed by a machine.
The field of regularized learning provides an additional pathway for investigating computational learning theory including:

\bullet

interpretability through approximation theory,

\bullet

nonconvexity and nonsmoothness through optimization theory,

\bullet

generalization and overfitting through regularization theory.

In my opinion, the exact solutions of the original problems are globally approximated by machine learning algorithms that provide local interpretability through linear-functional data.
According to the theorems of regularized learning,
the existence and convergence of the approximate solutions can be ensured by utilizing nonconvex and nonsmooth loss functions.
Hence, the techniques of nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization will be employed to develop the iterative algorithms.
Since weak* compactness is essential in the proof of the weak* convergence of the approximate solutions, the regularization terms play a crucial role in verifying generalization and preventing overfitting.
Based on the characteristics of weak* convergence in the convergence theorems, we will aim to introduce locally convergent rates and error bounds for the approximate solutions at certain observed inputs with specific structures, such as relative compactness.
In our forthcoming paper, we intend to investigate novel mathematical techniques and concepts to study the theory of regularized learning, such as sparse machine learning in
l_{1/2}
spaces using generalized dual topology.

Furthermore, the depictions of regularized learning exhibit similarities to the equations used in inverse problems.
By employing classical approaches to inverse problems,
convergent analysis typically necessitates a condition of uniform convergence.
However, verifying uniform convergence directly poses a significant challenge in numerous machine learning problems because the original problem is frequently unidentified or uncertain.
Indeed, pointwise convergence is considered to be a less stringent condition compared to uniform convergence.
Moreover, the pointwise convergence of empirical risks can be verified through test functions in practical applications or postulated through the representation of natural models.
Hence, in the convergence theorems, we focus on the condition of pointwise convergence, exemplified by Condition (I).
Unfortunately, the condition of pointwise convergence
is not sufficient to prove the convergence of the approximate solutions to the exact solutions.
Therefore, there arises a fundamental issue whether a robust convergence condition can be substituted by a weaker convergence condition together with an additional checkable condition.
Our proposition posits that the weaker condition and the additional condition can be independently checked.
Since linear-functional data is always known, the additional condition of linear-functional data is feasible to check as shown in Lemma
2.2
.
By the construction of linear-functional data, relative compactness emerges as a natural additional condition of linear-functional data as explained in Remark
2.2
.
The additional condition of loss functions is straightforward due to their typically simplistic forms.
Clearly, the additional condition of linear-functional data and loss functions is independent on the original problem, exemplified by Condition (II).
The additional condition also implies
the specific weak* equicontinuity of empirical risks as shown in Proposition
3.1
(c).
As stated in Section
5.4
, the weaker condition and the additional condition are sufficient to prove the convergence theorems.
