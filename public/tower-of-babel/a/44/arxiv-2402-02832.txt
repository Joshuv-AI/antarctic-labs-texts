On the Uniqueness of Kähler–Einstein Polygons in Mutation-Equivalence Classes
Thomas Hall

Abstract
. We study a subclass of Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons and how they behave under mutation. The polygons of interest are Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles and symmetric Fano polygons. In particular, we find an explicit bound for the number of these polygons in an arbitrary mutation-equivalence class. An important mutation-invariant of a Fano polygon is its singularity content. We extend the notion of singularity content and prove that it is still a mutation-invariant. We use this to show that if two symmetric Fano polygons are mutation-equivalent, then they are isomorphic. We further show that if two Kähler–Einstein triangles are mutation-equivalent, then they are isomorphic. Finally, we show that if a symmetric Fano polygon is mutation-equivalent to a Kähler–Einstein triangle, then they are isomorphic. Thus, each mutation-equivalence class has at most one Fano polygon which is either a Kähler–Einstein triangle or symmetric. A recent conjecture states that all Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons are either triangles or are symmetric. We provide a counterexample P P to this conjecture and discuss several of its properties. For instance, we compute iterated barycentric transformations of P P and find that (a) the Kähler–Einstein property is not preserved by the barycentric transformation, and (b) P P is of strict type B 2 B_{2} . Finally, we find examples of Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons which are not minimal.

1.
Introduction

A new approach to the classification of Fano manifolds was recently initiated by Coates–Corti–Galkin–Golyshev–Kasprzyk
[
11
]
. This has become known as the
Fanosearch programme
. Following this programme, a Laurent polynomial
f
is said to be
mirror dual
to a Fano manifold
X
if the classical period
\pi_{f}
of
f
agrees with the regularized quantum period
\widehat{G}_{X}
of
X
; see
[
11
]
for details. This correspondence is not unique: a Fano manifold can have (infinitely) many different mirror dual Laurent polynomials, and these Laurent polynomials are expected to be related via a combinatorial process called
mutation

[
2
]
. It is conjectured that Fano manifolds, up to
\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein-deformation, are in bijective correspondence with certain Laurent polynomials, up to mutation.

Crucial to this approach is the Newton polytope
P
of the Laurent polynomial
f
. This polytope
P
is
Fano
, i.e. it is a convex lattice polytope containing the origin in its strict interior, and has primitive vertices
[
20
]
. We thus enter the world of toric geometry. The polytope
P
corresponds to a (possibily singular) toric Fano variety
X_{P}
, which is expected to be a toric degeneration of the original Fano manifold
X
. The notion of mutation can be extended from Laurent polynomials to Fano polytopes. Ilten showed that if two Fano polytopes
P
and
Q
are related by mutation, then the corresponding toric varieties
X_{P}
and
X_{Q}
are deformation-equivalent
[
17
]
.

In
[
1
]
, the Fanosearch programme was specialised to orbifold del Pezzo surfaces; that is, del Pezzo surfaces with at worst cyclic quotient singularities. There are infinitely many of these surfaces, even up to
\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein-deformation; however, bounding the possible basket of singularities results in a finite classification. For example, an empty basket recovers the classical
10
smooth del Pezzo surfaces, up to
\mathbb{Q}
-Gorenstein-deformation.

Kasprzyk–Nill–Prince
[
21
]
introduced the notion of
minimality
for Fano polygons. A Fano polygon is called
minimal
if, amongst all polygons related to it by a single mutation, its volume is minimal. That is, minimality is a local property, and there can be multiple minimal polygons in a single mutation-equivalence class. Using this purely combinatorial definition, they found exactly
26
mutation-equivalence classes of Fano polygons whose baskets contain only
\frac{1}{3}(1,1)
singularities; these agree with
26
of the del Pezzo surfaces with
\frac{1}{3}(1,1)
singularities classified by Corti–Heuberger
[
13
]
.

One of the main results of
[
21
]
is the following:

Theorem 1.1

(​
[
21
, Theorem 6.3]
)
.

There are finitely many minimal Fano polygons, up to isomorphism, with a given basket of singularities.

As a consequence, the problem of classifying Fano polygons (up to mutation) with a given basket can be reduced to classifying finitely many minimal Fano polygons; this has been implemented algorithmically by Cavey–Kutas
[
8
]
. In this paper, we seek to further understand the behaviour of some of these minimal Fano polygons.

Another concept in algebraic geometry which has seen much recent interest is K-stability. It was initially formulated by Tian
[
26
]
as a way to characterise the existence of Kähler–Einstein metrics on Fano manifolds. K-stability has since been expanded by Donaldson
[
14
]
to polarised varieties. The Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture predicts that a Fano variety
X
is K-polystable if and only if it admits a Kähler–Einstein metric. This was proven in the smooth case by Chen–Donaldson–Sun
[
9
,
10
]
and Tian
[
27
]
; the singular case has seen recent progress
[
5
,
22
]
, but remains open. Its main application is the construction of well-behaved moduli spaces, called K-moduli spaces, of Fano varieties
[
23
,
18
,
6
]
.

We study toric Kähler–Einstein Fano varieties. Following Hwang–Kim
[
16
]
, a Fano polygon
P
is called
Kähler–Einstein
if its corresponding toric variety
X_{P}
admits a Kähler–Einstein metric. A related class, introduced by Batyrev–Selivanova
[
4
]
, are
symmetric polygons
: these are polygons whose automorphism group fixes only the origin.

Theorem 1.2

(​
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
)
.

All symmetric polytopes are Kähler–Einstein.

The converse to Theorem
1.2
holds for smooth Fano polytopes in dimension less than seven. Nill–Paffenholz
[
25
]
found that exactly one smooth Fano polytope in dimension seven, and exactly two smooth Fano polytopes in dimension eight, are Kähler–Einstein but not symmetric.

In this paper, we study symmetric Fano polygons and Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles. These were conjectured by Hwang–Kim
[
15
]
to constitute all Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons.

Conjecture 1.3

(​
[
15
, Conjecture 1.6]
)
.

All Kähler–Einstein polygons are either symmetric or triangles.

We prove that symmetric Fano polygons and Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles are minimal (see, respectively, Lemmas
3.15
and
4.3
). So, in each mutation-equivalence class, there is a finite number of these polygons. The main result of this paper is the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

In each mutation-equivalence class, there is at most one Fano polygon which is either symmetric or a Kähler–Einstein triangle.

Both behaviours are exhibited: there are examples of mutation-equivalence classes with no symmetric Fano polygons or Kähler–Einstein triangles (Example
3.2
) and examples with exactly one (Example
3.3
).

In §
5.1
we provide a counterexample to Conjecture
1.3
:

Proposition 1.5
.

The Fano polygon

P\coloneqq\conv\left\{(-9,-190),(19,27),(15,113),(-13,112)\right\}\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}

is a Kähler–Einstein Fano quadrilateral. In particular, Conjecture
1.3
does not hold.

All Fano quadrilaterals with barycentre zero are described in Proposition
5.5
. This allows us to present a method for systematically producing non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein quadrilaterals; see Remark
5.7
.

As noted above, both symmetric Fano polygons and Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles are minimal. Furthermore, all the non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein Fano quadrilateral examples we have found are minimal. It is natural to ask whether this holds more generally for all Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons, however this is not the case:

Proposition 1.6

(see Proposition
5.11
)
.

There exist Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons which are not minimal.

We now summarise the structure of this paper. In §
2
, we fix our notation for the objects of study: polygons and mutation. We then extend the notion of basket of singularities to suit our needs. The next two sections are dedicated to proving Theorem
1.4
. In §
3
, we prove Theorem
3.1
, which states that there is at most one symmetric Fano polygon in each mutation-equivalence class. In §
4
, we prove the rest of Theorem
1.4
. In particular, we prove that there is at most one Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle in each mutation-equivalence class and that if a symmetric Fano polygon is mutation-equivalent to a Kähler–Einstein triangle, then they are isomorphic. We conclude with §
5
, where we provide a counterexample to Conjecture
1.3
. We compute iterated barycentric transformations of
P
, and show that it has strict type
B_{2}
. Finally, we describe an alternative method for constructing non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons and use it to show that there are Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons which are not minimal (Proposition
5.11
).

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Lattice polygons and basic invariants

Throughout, we refer to the lattice
N\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and its dual lattice
M\coloneqq\Hom(N,\mathbb{Z})
. We also refer to their
\mathbb{Q}
-extensions as
N_{\mathbb{Q}}\coloneqq N\otimes\mathbb{Q}
and
M_{\mathbb{Q}}\coloneqq M\otimes\mathbb{Q}
. For elements
u\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\bm{x}\in N_{\mathbb{Q}}
, we denote their natural pairing as
u(\bm{x})
.

We often consider polygons up to
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence. Let
P,Q\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be lattice polytopes. If there exists some
U\in\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
such that
Q=UP
, then we say that
P
is
isomorphic
to
Q
. A subtle point used throughout this paper is to also consider polygons only up to
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence, since several properties we consider are only invariant under
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-transformations and not
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-transformations.

We first recall the definition of a Fano polygon. Each Fano polygon
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
(up to
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence) corresponds one-to-one with a normal toric Fano variety
X_{P}
(up to isomorphism).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a lattice polygon, i.e. a polygon whose vertices are in the lattice
N
. It is called
Fano
if the origin
\bm{0}
is in the strict interior
\strint(P)
of
P
and all its vertices
\bm{v}=(x,y)
are
primitive
, i.e.
\gcd(x,y)=1
.

It will be important later, when considering mutations, to have the notion of length and height of an edge of a Fano polygon.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
E
be an edge of a Fano polygon and let
u\in M
be the unique primitive inner normal vector to
E
. Then we say that
E
has
(lattice) length

\ell_{E}\coloneqq|E\cap N|-1
and
height

h_{E}\coloneqq|u(\bm{v})|
, where
\bm{v}
is a vertex of
E
. Note that the length and the height will be strictly positive integers.

A classical way to describe a cone or an edge of a Fano polygon is by its singularity type
\frac{1}{r}(1,a)
, which is equivalently a matrix in Hermite normal form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-a\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
It is invariant under
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-transformations. So, it throws away information; in particular, the orientation of the edge. But knowing how the edges of our polygons are oriented will be vital in several proofs in this paper. Thus, we modify the notion of Hermite normal form so that it keeps the orientation information.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a (pointed, full-dimensional) cone. Denote the primitive ray generators of
\sigma
by
\bm{v}_{0}
and
\bm{v}_{1}
, going anticlockwise. Let
A_{\sigma}
be the matrix with left column
\bm{v}_{0}
and right column
\bm{v}_{1}
. Let
H_{\sigma}
be the (row-style) Hermite normal form of
A_{\sigma}
. We call
H_{\sigma}
the
Hermite normal form
of the cone
\sigma
. Equivalently, we can speak of the
Hermite normal form

H_{E}
of an edge
E
of a Fano polygon by passing to the cone over
E
.

Note that the Hermite normal form of
\sigma
will be of the form
H_{\sigma}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, for some
r,a\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leq a<r
. We know that the first column of
H_{\sigma}
must be
(1,0)
, since the ray generators of
\sigma
are primitive. For the same reason,
a
and
r
must be coprime.

We now describe the exact behaviour of our orientation-preserving Hermite normal form; in particular, we describe how it behaves when we apply unimodular transformations to the cone (or edge).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a pointed, full-dimensional cone with Hermite normal form
H_{\sigma}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Let
G
be a unimodular matrix. If
G
has determinant
1
, then
G\sigma
has Hermite normal form
H_{G\sigma}=H_{\sigma}
. Instead suppose that
G
has determinant
-1
. Then
G\sigma
has Hermite normal form
H_{G\sigma}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a^{*}\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
aa^{*}\equiv 1\mod r
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
G
has determinant
1
. So,
G
preserves the orientation of the ray generators of
\sigma
. Thus,
A_{G\sigma}=GA_{\sigma}
. Since the Hermite normal form of matrices is invariant under unimodular transformation, it follows that
H_{G\sigma}=H_{\sigma}
.

Now, suppose that
G
has determinant
-1
. Consider the cone
\sigma^{\prime}\coloneqq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)G\sigma
. This is
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent to
\sigma
, and thus has the same Hermite normal form
H_{\sigma}
. In particular,
\sigma^{\prime}
can be transformed so that its primitive ray generators are the columns of
H_{\sigma}
, which are
(1,0)
and
(a,r)
. Applying the matrix
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
again, we see that
G\sigma
can be transformed so that it has primitive ray generators
(r,a)
and
(0,1)
.

We now aim to compute the Hermite normal form of the matrix
A\coloneqq\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r&0\\
a&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. There is some unimodular matrix
U
such that
A=U\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a^{*}\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some
a^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
0\leq a^{*}<r
. The first column of
U
must be
(r,a)
. So, if the second column of
U
is
(x,y)
, then we must satisfy
r(a^{*}+x)=0
and
aa^{*}+rw=1
. In particular, in the second equation, notice that this gives
aa^{*}\equiv 1\mod r
. Thus, since
G\sigma
is
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent to
A
, the Hermite normal form of
G\sigma
is as described.
∎

We can recover the singularity type of
\sigma
from its associated Hermite normal form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. The singularity type is simply
\frac{1}{r}(1,-a)
. Since cones are usually considered only up to
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-equivalence, we can also get a Hermite normal form of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a^{*}\\
0&r\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
aa^{*}\equiv 1\mod r
, which results in a singularity type of
\frac{1}{r}(1,-a^{*})
. This is consistent, since these two singularities are in fact indistinguishable in the world of algebraic geometry.

2.2.
Mutations of lattice polygons

Before we define the important notion of mutation, we introduce some notation. Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be Fano polytope and fix a primitive linear form
w\in M
. Denote by
N_{h}
the
h
-th graded piece of
N
with respect to
w
, i.e.
N_{h}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{x}\in N\ \colon\ w(\bm{x})=h\right\}
. Denote by
P_{h}
the
h
-th graded piece of
P
with respect to
w
, i.e.
P_{h}\coloneqq\conv(P\cap N_{h})
. We denote by
\mathcal{V}(P)
the set of vertices of
P
, and by
\mathcal{V}(P)_{h}
the set of vertices of
P
at height
h
with respect to
w
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}(P)_{h}\coloneqq\mathcal{V}(P)\cap N_{h}
. We write
h_{\min}\coloneqq\min_{\bm{x}\in P}w(\bm{x})
and
h_{\max}\coloneqq\max_{\bm{x}\in P}w(\bm{x})
so that all non-empty graded pieces of
P
with respect to
w
are contained in the list
P_{h_{\min}},P_{h_{\min}+1},\ldots,P_{h_{\max}}
.

Definition 2.5

(​
[
2
, Definition 3.3]
)
.

Let
F\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a lattice polytope. Then
F
is said to be a
factor of
P
with respect to
w
if
w(F)=\left\{0\right\}
and, for all integers in the range
h_{\min}\leq h<0
, there exists a (possibly empty) lattice polytope
R_{h}\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
such that
\mathcal{V}(P)_{h}\subseteq R_{h}+|h|F\subseteq P_{h}
. We refer to these lattice polytopes
R_{h}
as remainders.

Definition 2.6

(​
[
2
, Definition 3.3]
)
.

Let
F\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a factor of
P
with respect to
w
with choice of remainders
\mathcal{R}=\left\{R_{h_{\min}},\ldots,R_{-1}\right\}
. Then the
mutation
of
P
with respect to
w
and
F
(and the choice of remainders
\mathcal{R}
) is

\mut_{w}\left(P,F;\mathcal{R}\right)\coloneqq\conv\left(\bigcup_{h=h_{\min}}^{-1}R_{h}\ \ \cup\ \ \bigcup_{h=0}^{h_{\max}}(P_{h}+hF)\right)\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}.

By
[
2
, Proposition 3.8]
, all choices of remainders
\mathcal{R}
will produce the same polytope; thus, we simply write
\mut_{w}(P,F)
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
P
and
Q
be Fano polytopes in
N_{\mathbb{Q}}
. We say that
P
and
Q
are
mutation-equivalent
if there exists a sequence of mutations taking
P
to
Q
.

Later on, when studying mutation-equivalence classes of Fano polygons, it will be useful to have some invariants at hand. The first invariant of interest is the Ehrhart series, whose definition we now recall.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a polygon. The
Ehrhart series

\Ehr_{P}(t)
of
P
is defined as the formal power series

\Ehr_{P}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|kP\cap N|\,t^{k}.

But instead of taking the Ehrhart series of a Fano polygon, we are interested in the Ehrhart series of its dual polygon. We recall the definition of (polar) duality of polygons.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a polygon. Its
dual polyhedron
is defined to be the convex set

P^{*}\coloneqq\left\{u\in M_{\mathbb{Q}}\ \colon\ u(\bm{x})\geq-1,\ \forall\bm{x}\in P\right\}.

In
[
2
]
, it was shown that if we apply a mutation to a Fano polytope
P
, its dual polytope
P^{*}
is transformed by a piecewise linear function. Thus, it follows that the Ehrhart series of the dual polytope is invariant under mutation.

Proposition 2.10

(cf.
[
2
, Proposition 3.15]
)
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano polygon. Then the Ehrhart series
\Ehr_{P^{*}}(t)
of its dual polygon
P^{*}
is invariant under mutation of
P
.

In
[
19
]
, they introduced the important notion of singularity content of a Fano polygon. They proved that the singularity content of a Fano polygon is invariant under mutation. Here, we will introduce a modified version of this invariant. In particular, while the classical singularity content keeps track of singularity types of cones, we keep track of Hermite normal forms of cones. This modification will be vital to the success of later proofs.

We first define the notion locally, i.e. at the level of the edge (or cone).

Definition 2.11
.

Let
E
be an edge of a Fano polygon with length
\ell
and height
h
. Then
\ell=nh+\widetilde{\ell}
, for some integers
n\geq 0
and
0\leq\widetilde{\ell}<h
. So, we can subdivide
E
into
n
segments of length
h
and a (possibly empty) segment
\res(E)
of length
\widetilde{\ell}
. We call
\res(E)
the
residue
of
E
. Each segment of length and height
h
corresponds to a
primitive T-singularity
. If
\widetilde{\ell}>0
, then the segment
\res(E)
corresponds to an
R-singularity
. We define the
directed singularity content

\SC(E)
of
E
as the tuple
(n_{E},\res(H_{E}))
, where
\res(H_{E})
is the
residue
of the Hermite normal form of
E
, i.e. if
\widetilde{\ell}>0
, then
\res(H_{E})=H_{\res(E)}
; else,
\res(H_{E})=\varnothing
.

We now define the notion globally, i.e. at the level of the polygon.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano polygon. The number
n_{P}
of primitive T-singularities of
P
is defined as the sum over all edges
E
of
P
of the number
n_{E}
of primitive T-singularities of
E
. The
directed basket

\overrightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{P}
of
P
is defined as the cyclically ordered set of non-empty residues of the Hermite normal forms of the edges of
P
(ordered anticlockwise). We define the
directed singularity content

\SC(P)
of
P
as the tuple
(n_{P},\overrightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{P})
. The subscript
P
’s are often omitted when clear from context.

Next, we prove that this modified version of singularity content is indeed a mutation-invariant of Fano polygons.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
P,Q\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be Fano polygons. Suppose that they are related by a mutation, i.e.
Q=\mut_{w}(P,F)
for some
w\in M
and factor
F
of
P
with respect to
w
. Then, the directed singularity contents of
P
and
Q
coincide, i.e.
\SC(P)=\SC(Q)
.

Proof.

Let
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{P}\coloneqq\left\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}\right\}
be the directed basket of
P
and
\overrightarrow{\mathcal{B}}_{Q}\coloneqq\left\{H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,H_{m^{\prime}}^{\prime}\right\}
be the directed basket of
Q
. By
[
19
, Proposition 3.6]
, the usual singularity content is invariant under mutation; therefore, we have
m=m^{\prime}
and
H_{i}^{\prime}\in\left\{H_{i},H_{i}^{*}\right\}
, for all
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. It remains to show that
H_{i}^{\prime}=H_{i}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,m
. But this follows from the fact that Hermite normal forms of edges are invariant under
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-transformations; from
P
to
Q
, the normals of the edges undergo one of two linear transformations which have determinant
1
.
∎

In order to describe mutations, we introduce the notion of a long edge. This is already used implicitly in, for example,
[
21
, Lemma 2.3]
. It is defined so that we can mutate a polygon with respect to some edge if and only if it is long.

Definition 2.14
.

Let
E
be an edge with lattice length
\ell
and height
h
. We say that
E
is
long
if its length is greater than or equal to its height, i.e.
\ell\geq h
. Otherwise, we say that
E
is
short
. In the latter case, the cone
\sigma
over
E
is its own residue, i.e.
\sigma=\res(\sigma)
. Finally, we say that an edge is
pure
if its residue is empty. Note that a pure edge must be long.

We again recall the definition of minimality for Fano polygons, which we described in the introduction.

Definition 2.15

(​
[
21
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

A Fano polygon
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
is called
minimal
if, for every mutation
Q\coloneqq\mut_{w}(P,F)
of
P
, we have
|\partial P\cap N|\leq|\partial Q\cap N|
, i.e.
P
has the minimal number of boundary lattice points out of all polygons related to it by a single mutation.

There are several equivalent conditions for a Fano polygon to be minimal (see
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
). We summarise the main characterisation that we use.

Lemma 2.16

(​
[
21
, Corollary 4.5]
)
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano polygon. Then
P
is minimal if and only if
|h_{\min}|\leq h_{\max}
, for each long edge
E
of
P
, where
u_{E}
is the primitive normal to
E
.

An important class of Fano polygons are reflexive polygons.

Definition 2.17

(​
[
3
]
)
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a lattice polygon. We say that
P
is
reflexive
if each edge
E
of

has height

.

Remark 2.18
.

There are two properties of reflexive polygons relevant to minimality which we wish to highlight. First, it follows by Lemma
2.16
that all reflexive polygons are minimal (see
[
21
, Example 4.4]
). Second, their edges are all long and pure. In particular, all reflexive polygons have empty basket.

We finally introduce the notion of edge data, which is instrumental in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Definition 2.19
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano polygon. Order its edges anticlockwise:
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,E_{m}
. The
edge data

\mathcal{E}(P)
of

is defined as the cyclically ordered set
\left\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}\right\}
, where

is the Hermite normal form of the edge

.

In general, the edge data is not a mutation-invariant. Note that if we take the residue of the edge data, and remove the empty entries, then we recover the directed basket, which
is
invariant under mutation.

Example 2.20
.

Consider the Fano polygon

Finally, we remark that edge data is invariant under

-transformation. Further, applying a transformation in
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with determinant
-1
to the polygon

changes the edge data in a predictable way. Write the edge data of

as
\mathcal{E}(P)=\left\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}\right\}
and let

of

2.3.
Symmetric and Kähler–Einstein polygons

The main objects of study in this paper are symmetric and Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons. In this subsection, we state their definitions and several basic results concerning them.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano polygon. It’s called
Kähler–Einstein
if the barycentre of its dual polytope
P^{*}
is the origin. It’s called
symmetric
if its automorphism group
\Aut(P)
only fixes the origin, i.e.
\left\{\bm{x}\in N_{\mathbb{Q}}\ \colon\ G\cdot\bm{x}=\bm{x},\,\forall G\in\Aut(P)\right\}=\left\{\bm{0}\right\}
.

Definition 2.22
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a polygon. We call it
centrally symmetric
if for all
\bm{x}\in P
, we also have
-\bm{x}\in P
. We call it
3
-symmetric
if there exists an element
G\in\Aut(P)
of order
3
.

It is straightforward to see that if a polygon is centrally symmetric or
3
-symmetric, then it is also symmetric. We show that the converse also holds.

Proposition 2.23
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a symmetric polygon. Then
P
is either centrally symmetric or
3
-symmetric.

Proof.

Consider the automorphism group of
P
, which is a finite subgroup of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Since
P
is symmetric,
\Aut(P)
must contain a rotation
G
. By
[
24
, Theorem 3]
,
\Aut(P)
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
D_{4}
or
D_{6}
, the dihedral groups of order
8
and
12
, respectively. Thus, the rotation
G
has order in
\left\{2,3,4,6\right\}
. If
G
has order
3
, then
P
is
3
-symmetric. Otherwise,
G
has even order
2g
. Since the element
G^{g}
, which belongs to
\Aut(P)
, is
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, it follows that
P
is centrally symmetric.
∎

Remark 2.24
.

The polygon of Example
2.20
is centrally symmetric. For cyclically ordered sets like directed baskets and edge data, let us introduce the notation
\times g
to indicate that the objects are repeated
g
times. For example,
\left\{H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}\right\}\times 2
means
\left\{H_{1},H_{2},H_{3},H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}\right\}
. It is a fact that the directed basket (and edge data) of a centrally symmetric polygon can always be written as
\mathcal{C}\times 2
, for some cyclically ordered set
\mathcal{C}
. The analogous fact holds for
3
-symmetric polygons (replace
\times 2
with
\times 3
).

Next, let us recall the definition of weights and weight matrices of polygons. As we will immediately see, Kähler-Einstien Fano triangles have a nice describe in terms of weights. Further, we will give a description in §
5
of a Kähler–Einstein quadrilateral
P
in terms of the weight system of
P^{*}
.

Definition 2.25
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a polygon and fix an ordering of its vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{m}
. Let
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
be an integer vector of length
m
. Then
\bm{q}
is a
weight
of
P
if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{0}
. Let
W\in\mathbb{Z}^{(m-2)\times m}
be an integer matrix of rank
m-2
. Then
W
is a
weight matrix
for
P
if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}W_{ji}\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{0}
for all
j=1,2,\ldots,m-2
.

Remark 2.26
.

The toric variety corresponding to a Fano polygon
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
is a fake weighted projective plane
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{0}\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})/G
, where
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
are the weights of
P
and
G
is the group
N/N_{P}
and
N_{P}
is the sublattice of
N
generated by the vertices of
P
. Since we are in dimension two,
G
is a cyclic group of order
k
.

There are typically several different fake weighted projective planes with the same weights
(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and index
k
. In some cases, though, there is only one such variety up to isomorphism. In these cases, we are safe to write
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})/\mathbb{Z}_{k}
. For instance, throughout the paper we will write
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
when
P
is isomorphic to the triangle with vertices
(-1,-1)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,1)
. We will also write
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}
when
P
is isomorphic to the triangle with vertices
(-1,-1)
,
(2,-1)
, and
(-1,2)
.

We prove the following statement, which will be useful in both §
3.3
and §
4
.

Lemma 2.27
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle. Then
P
has weights
(1,1,1)
.

Proof.

It is well-known that the barycentre of a triangle is the average of its vertices. Therefore, a triangle has barycentre zero if and only if the sum of its vertices is the origin. Equivalently, a triangle has barycentre zero if and only if it has weights
(1,1,1)
. By
[
12
, Lemma 3.5]
, the weights of a Fano triangle coincide with the weights of its dual. Thus, a Fano triangle is Kähler–Einstein if and only if it has weights
(1,1,1)
.
∎

We can also use the above to give an alternative proof for the following statement, which was proven in
[
15
]
using direct computation of the barycentre of the dual.

Lemma 2.28

(​
[
15
, Proposition 4.1]
)
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Fano triangle. Then
P
is Kähler–Einstein if and only if
P
is isomorphic to a triangle with vertices
(-k,a-1)
,
(k,-a)
, and
(0,1)
, for some integers
k,a\geq 1
satisfying
\gcd(k,a)=\gcd(k,a-1)=1
.

Proof.

Since
P
is Fano, we may assume without loss of generality that one of its vertices
(0,1)
. We label its other vertices as
(-m,b)
and
(k,-a)
, for some integers
a,b,k,m\geq 1
. By Lemma
2.27
, since
P
is Kähler–Einstein, the sum of its vertices must be the origin. So,
(k-m,1+b-a)=(0,0)
. Thus,
m=k
,
b=a-1
, and the result follows.
∎

Finally, we must give the following lemma, which again is used in both §
3.3
and §
4
.

Lemma 2.29
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle. Suppose that all the edges of
P
have the same height
h
and are long. Then, either
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
or
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.28
, we may apply an appropriate transformation so that
P
has vertices
(-k,a-1)
,
(k,-a)
, and
(0,1)
, for some integers
a,k\geq 1
. For each edge
E
of
P
, consider the triangle with base
E
and peak
\bm{0}
. It has (normalised) volume
k
. It also has volume
\ell_{E}h_{E}
, where
\ell_{E}
is the length of
E
and
h_{E}
is the height of
E
. But by assumption, all the edges of
P
have the same height
h
. Thus, all edges have the same length
\ell
and so we obtain the following condition:

(2.1)

\ell=\gcd(k,a+1)=\gcd(k,a-2)=\gcd(2k,2a-1).

We see that
\ell
divides
a+1
and
a-2
; thus,
\ell
divides
3
.

If
\ell=1
then, since each edge of
P
is long, we have
h=1
. We may write
a=1
. It follows that
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Otherwise,
\ell=3
. We may write
a=3b-1
, for some integer
b\geq 1
. Plugging this into (
2.1
), we obtain:

(2.2)

1=\gcd(h,b)=\gcd(h,b-1)=\gcd(2h,2b-1).

Since the edges of
P
are long, we have
h\leq 3
. If
h=1
, then we may write
b=1
. It follows that
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}
. If
h=2
, then (
2.2
) implies that
b\not\equiv 0,1\mod 2
, which cannot hold. Else
h=3
, and (
2.2
) implies that
b\not\equiv 0,1,2\mod 3
, which also cannot hold.
∎

3.
At most one symmetric

We dedicate this section to proving part of Theorem
1.4
. Namely, we prove the following statement.

Theorem 3.1
.

There is at most one symmetric Fano polygon in each mutation-equivalence class.

As we will see later in Proposition
3.16
, the above theorem holds for symmetric Fano polygons with empty basket
\mathcal{B}
. Thus, the next few subsections will mainly focus on the case
\mathcal{B}\neq\varnothing
.

3.1.
Observation of both behaviours

Before we prove Theorem
3.1
, we will first demonstrate that both possibilities occur. More precisely, we show that there exists a mutation-equivalence class with no symmetric Fano polygons or Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles and that there exists a mutation-equivalence class with exactly one of them.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the Fano polygon
P\coloneqq\conv\left\{(2,-3),(1,5),(-1,-2)\right\}\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Since
P
is a triangle with weights
(3,7,13)
, it is not Kähler–Einstein by Lemma
2.27
. Further, the edges of
P
all have length
1
while their heights are
3
,
7
, and
13
— all greater than
1
. Thus, none of the edges of
P
are long. Therefore,
P
is the only polygon in its mutation-equivalence class. So, the mutation-equivalence class of
P
contains no Kähler–Einstein polygons. In particular, it contains no symmetric Fano polygons or Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles.

We now show that the other possibility can occur.

Example 3.3
.

Consider the Fano polygon
P\coloneqq\conv\left\{(\pm(1,-2),\pm(2,-1),\pm(1,1)\right\}\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
. Clearly,
P
is centrally symmetric (and thus symmetric). The edges of
P
all have length
1
and height
3
; thus, none of the edges of
P
are long. Therefore,
P
is the only polygon in its mutation-equivalence class. So, the mutation-equivalence class of
P
contains exactly one Kähler–Einstein polygon. In particular, it contains exactly one symmetric Fano polygon or Kähler–Einstein Fano polygon.

In the following subsections, we will show that for symmetric Fano polygons, no other possibility can occur, i.e. that if two symmetric Fano polygons are mutation-equivalent, then they are isomorphic.

3.2.
Constraints on centrally symmetric Fano polygons

When mutating polygons, it’s the long edges (in particular, the T-singularities) which move and change, while the short edges (i.e. the R-singularities) stay the same. Generally speaking, if a polygon has more long edges, then its behaviour under mutation will be richer. As we saw in Example
3.2
and Example
3.3
, if a polygon has no long edges, then its mutation-equivalence class is trivial; we can conclude that Theorem
1.4
holds in this case. For centrally symmetric polygons, the next simplest case is when the polygon has exactly one pair of parallel long edges.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a centrally symmetric polygon with exactly one pair of long edges
\pm E
. Let
Q\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a symmetric polygon. Suppose
P
is mutation-equivalent to
Q
. Then
P\cong Q
.

Proof.

Let
w\in M
be the primitive inner normal to
E
. Then
-w
is the primitive inner normal to
-E
. Consider
\mut_{w}(P,F)
, where
F
is a factor of
P
with respect to
w
. Then again, this polygon will have at most two long edges, whose primitive inner normals are in
\left\{w,-w\right\}
. Thus, by induction, all polygons mutation-equivalent to
P
will have at most two long edges with inner normals in
\left\{w,-w\right\}
.

Now consider the symmetric polygon
Q
, which is mutation-equivalent to
P
. Since the number of primitive T-singularities is invariant under mutation, and
P
has at least one primitive T-singularity, it follows that
Q
has at least one long edge. If
Q
were
3
-symmetric, then it would have at least three long edges. So, since
Q
has at most two long edges, it must be centrally symmetric. In particular, the number of primitive T-singularities on its two long edges must be equal. Thus,
Q
is isomorphic to
P
.
∎

Now, we are left to consider centrally symmetric Fano polygons with at least two pairs of parallel long edges. The rest of the subsection is dedicated to constraining the combinatorics of these polygons. In fact, in Corollary
3.10
, we discover that these polygons must have
exactly
two pairs of parallel long edges with at most
8
primitive T-singularities in total. This restricts the complexity of their behaviour under mutation, making the study of these objects under mutation realistic.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a centrally symmetric Fano polygon with non-empty basket
\mathcal{B}
and two pairs of opposite long edges
\pm E_{1}
and
\pm E_{2}
. Then the primitive normals to
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
span the dual lattice
M
.

Proof.

We may apply an appropriate unimodular transformation so that the primitive inner normal to
E_{1}
is
u_{1}=e^{*}_{1}
. We may also assume that the edge
E_{2}
is on the bottom. Thus, its primitive inner normal vector
u_{2}\coloneqq(a,b)^{t}\in M
will have
b>0
, as in Figure
1(a)
. We obtain
\det(u_{1},u_{2})=b
. Thus,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
span
M
if and only if
b=1
. So, assume towards a contradiction that
b\geq 2
.

Consider the primitive direction vector for
E_{2}
, which is
(b,-a)
. We can see that the starting vertex of
E_{2}
uses up one lattice point, and each unit segment of
E_{2}
uses up

lattice points. Since there are
2h_{1}+1
lattice points between

and
-E_{1}
for

to go through, we obtain the inequality
b\ell_{2}\leq 2h_{1}
. We can similarly derive that
b\ell_{1}\leq 2h_{2}
. Now, since
E_{2}
is a long edge and

has a smaller height than

, we obtain that

. Thus,

and the inequalities become equalities, giving
\ell_{1}=h_{1}=h_{2}=\ell_{2}
. Further, all

. This means that no more lattice points are left for any other edges going from

to
-E_{1}
to go through. So, no more pairs of edges exist other than
\pm E_{1}
and

. Thus,

is a centrally symmetric quadrilateral with only T-singularities, which contradicts our assumption that the basket

is non-empty.
∎

We now give the following lemma, which helps us to prove Proposition

.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a centrally symmetric Fano polygon with non-empty basket
\mathcal{B}\neq\varnothing
. Suppose that
P
has two pairs of opposite long edges
\pm E_{1}
and
\pm E_{2}
with heights
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
. Then,
2\leq h_{1}\leq h_{2}<2h_{1}
.

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that the primitive inner normals to
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are
e^{*}_{1}
and
e^{*}_{2}
, respectively. We first show that
h_{1}\geq 2
. Assume towards a contradiction that
h_{1}=1
. Then
P
is contained in the strip
\left\{-1\leq x\leq 1\right\}
. So,
E_{2}
has length at most
2
. Now, since
E_{2}
is long, it must have height
h_{2}=1
or
h_{2}=2
. We explore both possibilities.

Suppose that
h_{2}=1
. Then
P
is contained in the box
\left\{-1\leq x,y\leq 1\right\}
. Up to isomorphism, there are two polygons in this box which have long edges
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
with the prescribed heights: either
P=\conv\left\{\pm(1,1),\pm(-1,1)\right\}
or
P=\conv\left\{\pm(1,0),\pm(1,1),\pm(0,1)\right\}
. In either case,
P
has an empty basket
\mathcal{B}=\varnothing
, which is a contradiction.

Now suppose that
h_{2}=2
. Then
P
must be the quadrilateral
\conv\left\{\pm(1,2),\pm(-1,2)\right\}
. This has empty basket
\mathcal{B}=\varnothing
, so we again reach a contradiction. Thus, we have shown that
h_{1}\geq 2
.

Finally, we show that
h_{2}<2h_{1}
. Note that
P
is contained in the strip
\left\{-h_{1}\leq x\leq h_{1}\right\}
, which implies that
E_{2}
has length
\ell_{2}\leq 2h_{1}
. Since
E_{2}
is a long edge, we obtain that
h_{2}\leq 2h_{1}
. We see that it now remains to show that
h_{2}\neq 2h_{1}
.

Suppose towards a contradiction that
h_{2}=2h_{1}
. Then
P
must be the rectangle with vertices
\pm(h_{1},2h_{1})
and
\pm(-h_{1},2h_{1})
. Since
P
is Fano, its vertices must be primitive, i.e.
\gcd(h_{1},2h_{1})=1
. Thus,
h_{1}=1
. But we have already seen above that this results in a contradiction. Therefore,
h_{2}<2h_{1}
.
∎

In order to decide whether two polygons
P
and
Q
are mutation-equivalent, we can compare their mutation-invariants. One such invariant is the Ehrhart series of the dual polygon, i.e.
\Ehr_{P^{*}}(t)=\Ehr_{Q^{*}}(t)
. We find a striking pattern in the coefficients of the Ehrhart series.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a centrally symmetric Fano polygon with non-empty basket of R-singularities. Suppose
P
has two non-parallel long edges
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, which have primitive inner normals
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
and heights
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, respectively, with
h_{1}\leq h_{2}
. Then,

kP^{*}\cap M=\begin{cases}\left\{0\right\},&0\leq k<h_{1}\\
\left\{0,\pm u_{1}\right\},&h_{1}\leq k<h_{2}\\
\left\{0,\pm u_{1},\pm u_{2}\right\},&k=h_{2}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The inclusion “
\supseteq
” is clear; thus, it is enough to show that
h_{2}P^{*}\cap M\subseteq\left\{0,\pm u_{1},\pm u_{2}\right\}
. By Lemma
3.5
, we may assume that
u_{1}=(1,0)^{t}
and
u_{2}=(0,1)^{t}
. We first show that
\pm(1,1)^{t}\not\in h_{2}P^{*}
. This is equivalent to showing that there exists a point
(x,y)\in P
satisfying
x+y<-h_{2}
. Let
(a,-h_{2})
be the left vertex of
E_{2}
, where
a
is an integer satisfying
-h_{1}\leq a\leq h_{1}-h_{2}\leq 0
. In fact,
a<0
. Otherwise,
(0,-h_{2})
would be a vertex of

An immediate implication of Proposition

Corollary 3.10
.

Let

and

3.3.
Constraints on 3-symmetric Fano polygons

As stated at the beginning of §
3.2
, the more long edges a polygon has, the richer its behaviour under mutation will be. The simplest non-trivial case for
3
-symmetric polygons is when there is
at least one
triple of long edges. In fact, similarly to the centrally symmetric case, we see that these polygons must have
exactly one
triple of long edges (Corollary
3.13
).

Since we will reuse the following result in the general setting, we emphasise that
P
can have either empty or non-empty basket of R-singularities in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
3
-symmetric Fano polygon with
G\in\Aut(P)
having order
3
. Suppose
P
has a long edge
E
with primitive inner normal
u
. Then, one of the following must hold:

(i)

\det(u,uG)=3
and
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
;

(ii)

\det(u,uG)=1
.

Proof.

Denote by
h_{E}
the height of
E
. Consider the (not necessarily lattice) triangle
Q
which is the intersection of the supporting half-spaces of
E
,
GE
, and
G^{2}E
, i.e.

Q\coloneqq\left\{\bm{x}\in N_{\mathbb{Q}}\ \colon\ u(\bm{x})\geq-h_{E},(uG)(\bm{x})\geq-h_{E},(uG^{2})(\bm{x})\geq-h_{E}\right\}.

Due to the action of
G
on
Q
, there exists a unique
t\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
such that
tQ
has primitive vertices. Since
Q
contains the origin, we can conclude that
tQ
is Fano. Now, consider the sum of the vertices of
tQ
. This is fixed by
G
, so it must equal the origin. Thus,
tQ
has weights
(1,1,1)
.

Let
F
be an edge of
tQ
. Then the other two edges of
tQ
are
GF
and
G^{2}F
. In particular, the edges of
tQ
all have the same height. Further, since the original edge
E
of
P
is long, the edges
F
,
GF
, and
G^{2}F
must also be long. So, we may apply Lemma
2.29
to
tQ
. We obtain that either (i)
X_{tQ}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
or (ii)
X_{tQ}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{3}
.

In case (i), the result follows from the observation that
t=1
and
P=Q
. In case (ii), the result follows after noticing that
E
and
F
have the same primitive inner normal vector.
∎

The above lemma is enough to prove that

-symmetric Fano polygons are minimal, which we do later in Lemma

. For the rest of this subsection, we again focus on
3
-symmetric Fano polygons with

. As in §

Lemma 3.14
.

(ii)

The first vertex of

is the second vertex
\bm{v}_{1}
of

(iii)

The second vertex
\bm{v}_{2}
of

\bm{v}_{1}

. Thus,

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
P,Q\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be symmetric Fano polygons with non-empty basket of R-singularities. Suppose they have the same edge data, i.e.
\mathcal{E}(P)=\mathcal{E}(Q)
. Then

is isomorphic to
Q
.

Let
\mathcal{E}(P)=\mathcal{E}(Q)=\left\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{m}\right\}
. To get the desired result, we aim to repeatedly apply Lemma
3.17
. In order to do so, we must show that all the hypotheses of the lemma hold. Let
E
and
F
be two consecutive edges of
P
, ordered anticlockwise. Let
H
be the Hermite normal form of
F
. Since
P
is Fano, the vertices of
F
are primitive; hence,

satisfies the requirements of Lemma
3.17
. We next show that
F
belongs to the set
\mathcal{F}
of Lemma
3.17
.

Condition (i) is satisfied by the definition of

. Since
E
is adjacent to

, condition (ii) is satisfied. It remains to show condition (iii). Consider the second vertex
\bm{v}_{2}
of
F
. Since
\bm{v}_{2}
does not lie on
E
, the first inequality
u_{E}(\bm{v}_{2})>-h
holds. In order to prove the second inequality, we must use the fact that
P
is symmetric. By Lemma
3.18
, we have
u_{E}(\bm{v}_{2})\leq 2h
. Now, it remains to show that

, i.e. that
r\geq 3
.

We note that

. Since
P
is symmetric and has empty basket, it follows from Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.14
that

. If
h_{F}\geq 3
, then

and we are done. Otherwise,
F
has height

. Since the vertices of
P
are primitive, the length

of

must be even; hence,
\ell_{F}\geq 2
. So,

, and we are done.

We may now prove the statement of the proposition. Label anticlockwise the edges of

and

and

, respectively, so that

. So, there exists some

. The next edge of

. We may apply Lemma

to obtain

. By induction, we obtain that

for all

. Therefore,

, and we are done.

. Before we proceed with the final proposition of the section, we require the following small result.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let

4.
The behaviour of other Kähler–Einstein polygons under mutation

In this section, we aim to complete the proof of Theorem
1.4
. Previously, in §
3
, we proved that there is at most one symmetric Fano polygon in each mutation-equivalence class. Equivalently, we proved that if two symmetric Fano polygons are mutation-equivalent, then they are isomorphic. So, in order to complete the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we need to prove that (a) if two Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles are mutation-equivalent, then they are isomorphic and (b) if a symmetric Fano polygon is mutation-equivalent to a Kähler–Einstein triangle, then they are isomorphic. We prove (a) in Proposition
4.1
. Since the arguments for centrally symmetric and

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

be Kähler–Einstein Fano triangles. Suppose that

and

are mutation-equivalent. Then

is isomorphic to

.

Before we do the proof of Proposition

, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2

Let

Proof.

By Lemma

, we may apply an appropriate transformation so that

, and

is not primitive. So,

has a non-primitive vertex, which contradicts that

and

. Since

∎

Now that we have shown the triangle/triangle case, it remains to show the triangle/symmetric case. It will be useful to have the following lemma as it allows us to verify the case when the basket of R-singularities is empty.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle. Then
P
is minimal.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.27
,
P
has weights
(1,1,1)
. Let
\bm{v}_{0},\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}
be the vertices of
P
and
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}
be the vertices of
P^{*}
, ordered so that
u_{i}(\bm{v}_{j})=-1
if and only if

. In order to show minimality of

. To do this, we simply rearrange the identity

. We obtain
u_{i}(\bm{v}_{i})=2\geq 1
, and so we are done.
∎

We can now prove the remaining two propositions.

Proposition 4.4
.

No Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle is mutation-equivalent to a centrally symmetric Fano polygon.

Proof.

Let
P
be a centrally symmetric Fano polygon and
Q
be a Kähler–Einstein triangle. Suppose towards a contradiction that
P
and
Q
are mutation-equivalent. Due to the central symmetry of

, the number of R-singularities in the basket must be even. Since
Q
has three edges, there are at most three R-singularities. Thus, there are either zero or two R-singularities in the basket.

First suppose that there are no R-singularities in the basket. By Lemma
4.3
, the triangle
Q
will be minimal. Thus, the triangle
Q
appears in the list of
[
21
]
. The centrally symmetric
P
is also minimal and appears in that list. We see that there are no examples in the same mutation-equivalence class.

Otherwise, there are two copies of the same R-singularity in
\mathcal{B}
. Consider the edges of the triangle
Q
. Two of the edges
F_{1},F_{2}
host R-singularities and one edge
F_{0}
is a pure T-singularity. It follows from Lemma
2.27
that the determinant of each edge is
k
, for some positive integer
k
. So, the edges
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have the same number

of primitive T-singularities. Now, since
P
is centrally symmetric, it has an even number
2n
of primitive T-singularities. Thus, the pure edge
F_{0}
of

has
2n-2m
primitive T-singularities. Further, its determinant

must now be divisible by

. But this contradicts Lemma
4.2
.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
P\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a Kähler–Einstein Fano triangle and
Q\subset N_{\mathbb{Q}}
be a
3
-symmetric Fano polygon. Suppose that

is mutation-equivalent to

. Then

.

Proof.

First, we know that

and

are minimal. So, if

, then

and

must appear in the list of minimal polygons in Theorem 5.4 of

]
. We see that there is at most one Kähler–Einstein triangle or

-symmetric polygon for each number

.

Now, we have

. The number of R-singularities of

is a multiple of

. Further, the number of R-singularities of

is at most

. Since

and

are mutation-equivalent, they have the same number of R-singularities, which must be

is a triangle, each of its edges has an R-singularity. Due to the symmetry of order

. Since each edge of

Now we simply put all these results together to derive the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem

The result straightforwardly follows from Theorem

5.
Discussion of Kähler–Einstein polygons

Given Conjecture
1.3
, it would follow that there is at most one Kähler–Einstein Fano polygon in each mutation-equivalence class. However, in the first part of this section, we provide a counterexample to the conjecture. In particular, we show the existence of a Kähler–Einstein Fano polygon which is neither symmetric nor a triangle. This is enough to disprove the conjecture.

The second part of this section is dedicated to describing all Kähler–Einstein Fano quadrilaterals. In particular, we describe how quadrilaterals with barycentre as the origin must be constrained.

In the final part of this section, we construct a family of Kähler–EinsteinFano hexagons which are not minimal.

5.1.
A Kähler–Einstein Fano quadrilateral

So, as stated above, we will prove that the quadrilateral in Proposition

This means that it is still a wide-open question whether there is at most one Kähler–Einstein polygon per mutation-equivalence class.

Remark 5.1
.

We can ask whether the polygon

in the proof of Proposition

is mutation-equivalent to any other Kähler–Einstein polygon. Since the edges of

have lengths 1, 2, 7, 2 and heights 3149, 871, 481, 1739, respectively, it follows that none of its edges are long. Thus, the mutation-equivalence class consists only of

is not mutation-equivalent to any other other Kähler–Einstein polygon.

Having this example of a non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein quadrilateral allows us to test questions regarding barycentric transformations. These transformations were introduced in
[
16
]
as a way to measure how close a polytope is to being Kähler–Einstein. We recall the definition, specialised to dimension two.

Definition 5.2

(​
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
P
be a Fano polygon with vertices
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2},\ldots,\bm{v}_{m}
written in anticlockwise order. Then the

of

is defined as

B(P)\coloneqq\conv\left\{\frac{\bm{v}_{12}}{\gcd(\bm{v}_{12})},\frac{\bm{v}_{23}}{\gcd(\bm{v}_{23})},\ldots,\frac{\bm{v}_{m1}}{\gcd(\bm{v}_{m1})}\right\},

where
\bm{v}_{ij}=\bm{v}_{i}+\bm{v}_{j}
. We also write
B^{1}(P)\coloneqq B(P)
and
B^{k+1}(P)\coloneqq B^{k}(B(P))
, for integer

.

Note that the vertices of
B(P)
are guaranteed to be primitive. However, it isn’t certain that the origin is contained in the strict interior of

. So, the barycentric transformation of a Fano polygon might not be Fano itself.

Definition 5.3

(​
[
16
, Definition 2.4]
)
.

A Fano polygon

is said to be of

if

is of type

but not of type

In
[
16
]
, they expect that all smooth Kähler–Einstein (Fano) polytopes are of type

. They show that this expectation holds in all dimensions less than

, except for possibly one four-dimensional polytope
[
16
, Theorem 1.3]
. In two dimensions, they drop the smooth condition and prove that all Kähler–Einstein polygons are of type

[
16
, Theorem 1.6]
. They question whether Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons are all of type

. If so, they also question whether the Kähler–Einstein property is preserved under barycentric transformation. We answer both questions in the negative.

Example 5.4
.

We compute iterated barycentric transformations of the polygon

from Proposition

to determine its strict type. Consider

is not Kähler–Einstein. Next,

is not Fano. We can conclude that

is of strict type

.

Proposition 5.5

Let

As in the remark under Definition 2.3 in

5.3.
Non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein polygons coming from symmetric polygons

In the previous subsection, we showed one way to construct non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein polygons which are not triangles. By construction, all previous examples were quadrilaterals. In this subsection, we will construct a different type of non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein polygon. We first illustrate the main idea of the construction with the following example.

Example 5.9
.

Take the triangle
P
with vertices
(-1,-1)
,
(1,0)
, and
(0,1)
. This is Kähler–Einstein and symmetric, and has corresponding toric variety
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Consider
P
with respect to the lattice
N+\frac{1}{9}(1,2)\mathbb{Z}
. This is isomorphic to the triangle
P^{\prime}
with vertices
(-5,1)
,
(1,-2)
, and
(4,1)
, with respect to
N
, and whose corresponding toric variety is
X_{P^{\prime}}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{9}
. The weights of
P^{\prime}
are the same as those of

, so
P^{\prime}
is still Kähler–Einstein. However, the automorphism group of
P^{\prime}
is now only generated by a reflection. Thus,
P^{\prime}
is a Kähler–Einstein triangle which is not symmetric.

Remark 5.10
.

Note that a similar phenomenon was discussed in
[
15
, Example 1.7]
. They looked at a triangle
P
with
X_{P}=\mathbb{P}^{2}/\mathbb{Z}_{11}
. In this case,
P
had a trivial automorphism group.

We want to find more examples of non-symmetric Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons. To do this, we try to extend what we did in Example
5.9
to other symmetric polygons. In particular, we consider other symmetric polygons with respect to finer lattices in an attempt to destroy the symmetry but keep the Kähler–Einstein property. Of course, if a polygon is centrally symmetric with respect to one lattice, it will be centrally symmetric with respect to any other lattice. Thus, we restrict our attention to
3
-symmetric polygons.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let

be integers such that

and

is coprime to
h
,
h-1
, and

P_{h,k}\coloneqq\conv\begin{pmatrix}-hk&(1-h)k&(2h-1)k&(2h-1)k&(1-h)k&-hk\\

P_{h,k}
is of type

. If we further suppose that

, then

is not minimal.

Proof.

We first show that the polygon is Kähler–Einstein. The polygon
P_{h,1}
is
3
-symmetric, and thus Kähler–Einstein. The polygon
P_{h,k}
is isomorphic to
P_{h,1}
with respect to the lattice

. The weights of a polygon are the same, regardless of which lattice it is considered on. In particular,
P_{h,k}
has the same weights are
P_{h,1}
. Thus,
P_{h,k}
is also Kähler–Einstein.

Next, given the assumption that

is coprime to

,
h-1
, and
2h-1
, it easily follows that the vertices of
P_{h,k}
are primitive. Thus, since the origin is still contained in the strict interior, the polygon is Fano. It remains to show that
P_{h,k}
is not symmetric. To do this, we compare the lengths of its edges. Starting from the bottom-left edge of
P_{h,k}
and going anticlockwise, the lengths are

,
(3h-2)k
,
1
,
3h-2
,
k
, and

. Since

, the lengths don’t repeat three times. Thus,
P_{h,k}
is not

Now, we determine the type of

. We compute its barycentric transformation and obtain that

has vertices
\pm(k,-2)
,
\pm(2k,-1)
, and
\pm(k,1)
. In particular,

is centrally symmetric. Thus, by
[

is of type

.

Finally, we show that if

, then the polygon is not minimal. The bottom edge of

has length

and height

, so it is a long edge. The top edge has length

and height

is not minimal.
∎

So, we have found an infinite number of non-symmetric, non-triangle Kähler–Einstein Fano polygons.
