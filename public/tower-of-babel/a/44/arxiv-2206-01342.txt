Understanding the Role of Nonlinearity in Training Dynamics of Contrastive Learning
Yuandong Tian

Abstract
While the empirical success of self-supervised learning (SSL) heavily relies on the usage of deep nonlinear models, existing theoretical works on SSL understanding still focus on linear ones. In this paper, we study the role of nonlinearity in the training dynamics of contrastive learning (CL) on one and two-layer nonlinear networks with homogeneous activation h ​ ( x ) = h ′ ​ ( x ) ​ x h(x)=h^{\prime}(x)x . We have two major theoretical discoveries. First, the presence of nonlinearity can lead to many local optima even in 1-layer setting, each corresponding to certain patterns from the data distribution, while with linear activation, only one major pattern can be learned. This suggests that models with lots of parameters can be regarded as a brute-force way to find these local optima induced by nonlinearity. Second, in the 2-layer case, linear activation is proven not capable of learning specialized weights into diverse patterns, demonstrating the importance of nonlinearity. In addition, for 2-layer setting, we also discover global modulation : those local patterns discriminative from the perspective of global-level patterns are prioritized to learn, further characterizing the learning process. Simulation verifies our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Over the last few years, deep models have demonstrated impressive empirical performance in many disciplines, not only in supervised but also in recent self-supervised setting (SSL), in which models are trained with a surrogate loss (e.g., predictive
(
Devlin et al. 2018
;
He et al. 2021
)
, contrastive
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Caron et al. 2020
;
He et al. 2020
)
or noncontrastive loss
(
Grill et al. 2020
;
Chen & He 2020
)
) and its learned representation is then used for downstream tasks.

From the theoretical perspective, understanding the roles of nonlinearity in deep neural networks is one critical part of understanding how modern deep models work. Currently, most works focus on linear variants of deep models
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019a
;
Kawaguchi 2016
;
Jing et al. 2022
;
Tian et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2021
)
. When nonlinearity is involved, deep models are often treated as richer families of black-box functions than linear ones
(
Arora et al. 2019b
;
HaoChen et al. 2021
)
. The role played by nonlinearity is also studied, mostly on model expressibility
(
Gühring et al. 2020
;
Raghu et al. 2017
;
Lu et al. 2017
)
in which specific weights are found to fit the complicated structure of the data well, regardless of the training algorithm. However, many questions remain open: if model capacity is the key, why traditional models like
k
-NN
(
Fix & Hodges 1951
)
or kernel SVM
(
Cortes & Vapnik 1995
)
do not achieve comparable empirical performance, even if theoretically they can also fit any functions
(
Hammer & Gersmann 2003
;
Devroye et al. 1994
)
. Moreover, while traditional ML theory suggests carefully controlling model capacity to avoid overfitting, large neural models often generalize well in practice
(
Brown et al. 2020
;
Chowdhery et al. 2022
)
.

In this paper, we study the critical role of nonlinearity in the training dynamics of contrastive learning (CL). Specifically, by extending the recent
\alpha
-CL framework
(
Tian 2022
)
and linking it to kernels
(
Paulsen & Raghupathi 2016
)
, we show that even with 1-layer nonlinear networks, nonlinearity plays a critical role by creating many local optima. As a result, the more nonlinear nodes in 1-layer networks with different initialization, the more local optima are likely to be collected as learned patterns in the trained weights, and the richer the resulting representation becomes. Moreover, popular loss functions like InfoNCE tends to have more local optima than quadratic ones. In contrast, in the linear setting, contrastive learning becomes PCA under certain conditions
(
Tian 2022
)
, and only the most salient pattern (i.e., the maximal eigenvector of the data covariance matrix) is learned while other less salient ones are lost, regardless of the number of hidden nodes.

Based on this finding, we extend our analysis to 2-layer ReLU setting with non-overlapping receptive fields. In this setting, we prove the fundamental limitation of linear networks: the gradients of multiple weights at the same receptive field are always co-linear, preventing diverse pattern learning.

Finally, we also characterize the interaction between layers in 2-layer network: while in each receptive field, many patterns exist, those contributing to global patterns are prioritized to learn by the training dynamics. This
global modulation
changes the eigenstructure of the low-level covariance matrix so that relevant patterns are learned with higher probability.

In summary, through the lens of training dynamics, we discover unique roles played by nonlinearity which linear activation cannot do: (1) nonlinearity creates many local optima for different patterns of the data, and (2) nonlinearity enables weight specialization to diverse patterns. In addition, we also discover a mechanism for how global pattern prioritizes which local patterns to learn, shedding light on the role played by network depth. Preliminary experiments on simulated data verify our findings.

Related works
. Many works analyze network at initialization
(
Hayou et al. 2019
;
Roberts et al. 2021
)
and avoid the complicated training dynamics. Previous works
(

;

One-layer case

Now let us first consider 1-layer network with
K

{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}};\bm{\theta})=h(W{\bm{x}})
, where
W=[{\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{K}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times d}
,
\bm{\theta}=\{W\}
and
h(x)
is the activation. The

We consider per-filter normalization

Now let’s think about, which parameters

is. Therefore, the linear case will only learn the most salient single pattern due to the (overly-smooth) landscape of the objective function, a winner-take-all effect that neglects many patterns in the data.

3.1
Examples with multiple LMEs in ReLU setting

To see why the nonlinear activation leads to many LMEs in Eqn.
7

{\bm{x}}
that show Eqn.
7
has multiple critical points, then introduce more general cases. To make the examples simple and clear, we assume the condition of Corollary
1
(no augmentation and large batchsize), and let
\alpha_{ij}\equiv 1
. Notice that
\tilde{\bm{x}}^{{\bm{w}}}
is a deterministic function of
{\bm{x}}

Let

be orthonormal bases (

Latent categorical model
. Suppose

(8)

Now it is clear that

.

Latent summation model
. Suppose there is a latent variable
{\bm{y}}
so that
{\bm{x}}=U{\bm{y}}
, where
{\bm{y}}:=[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{M}]
. Each

is a standardized Bernoulli random variable:

3.2
Relate LMEs to Local Optima

Once LMEs are identified, the next step is to check whether they are attractive, or

. Let

4.1
Why nonlinearity is critical: linear activation fails

within one RF

Proof.

{\bm{y}}

{\bm{a}}

{\bm{b}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[h({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}),h^{\prime}({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}]+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[h^{\prime}({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}},h({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})]

1
) for activation

B.5
The effect of data augmentation on local optima

While the majority of the analysis focuses on the cases where there are no data augmentation (i.e., using Corollary
1
), the original formulation Lemma
2
can still handle contrastive learning in the presence of data augmentation.

In fact, data augmentation plays an important role by removing unnecessary local optima. First, Lemma
2
tells that the objective Eqn.
5
, when
K=1
, takes the following form:

2\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}({\bm{w}}):=\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[h({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})]\rightarrow\sum_{l}z_{l}^{2}\mathbb{V}_{{\bm{x}}_{0}\sim\tilde{p}_{l}(\cdot;\alpha)}\left[b({\bm{w}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})\right]

(105)

where
b({\bm{w}}|{\bm{x}}_{0}):=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{0})}[h({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{x}})|{\bm{x}}_{0}]
.

Now let us consider the following simple data augmentation of
{\bm{x}}_{0}
:

{\bm{x}}=R(t){\bm{x}}_{0},\quad t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(\mathcal{T})

(106)

where
R(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is some rotation parameterized by
t
, which is drawn uniformly from a parameter family
\mathcal{T}
.

We assume
\{R(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
forms a 1-dimensional
Lie group
parameterized by
\mathcal{T}
. This means that

•

Closeness
. For any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists
t^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
so that
R(t^{\prime\prime})=R(t)R(t^{\prime})
.

•

Existence of inverse element
. For each
R(t)
, there exists an inverse element
t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
so that
R(t^{\prime})=R^{-1}(t)=R^{\top}(t)
. The last equality is due to the fact that
R(t)
is a rotation.

•

Existence of identity map
.
R(0)=I
.

Then for any small transformation
R(t^{\prime})
applied to the weights
{\bm{w}}
(here “small” means
\|R(t^{\prime})-I\|_{2}
is small), we can write down
b(R(t^{\prime}){\bm{w}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})
using reparameterization trick:

\displaystyle b(R(t){\bm{w}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim p_{\mathrm{aug}}(\cdot|{\bm{x}}_{0})}[h((R(t){\bm{w}})^{\top}{\bm{x}})|{\bm{x}}_{0}]=\int h({\bm{w}}^{\top}R^{\top}(t)R(t^{\prime}){\bm{x}}_{0})\mathbb{P}[t^{\prime}]\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

(107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int h({\bm{w}}^{\top}R^{-1}(t)R(t^{\prime}){\bm{x}}_{0})\mathbb{P}[t^{\prime}]\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int h({\bm{w}}^{\top}R(t^{\prime\prime}){\bm{x}}_{0})\mathbb{P}[t^{\prime\prime}]\mathrm{d}t^{\prime\prime}=b({\bm{w}}|{\bm{x}}_{0})

(108)

Note that the last equality is due to the fact that
\{R(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
is a Lie group, so that
R^{-1}(t)R(t^{\prime})
always maps to another group element
R(t^{\prime\prime})
, and
t^{\prime\prime}
as the resulting parameterization is still uniform.

Due to stop gradient,
\alpha
and thus
\phi_{l}(\cdot;\alpha)
is treated as a constant term when checking the local property of the current parameters
{\bm{w}}
. This means that in the local neighborhood of
{\bm{w}}
,
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}({\bm{w}})=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(R(t^{\prime}){\bm{w}})
.

Now notice an important observation: if
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}:=R(t^{\prime}){\bm{w}}\neq{\bm{w}}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}({\bm{w}})=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}(R(t^{\prime}){\bm{w}})=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}({\bm{w}}^{\prime})
and therefore,
{\bm{w}}

cannot
be a local optimal.

Intuitively, this means that the data augmentation can
remove
certain local optima of
{\bm{w}}
, if they are not
locally invariant
(i.e.,
R(t^{\prime}){\bm{w}}\neq{\bm{w}}
) to the transformation of the data augmentation. Therefore, augmentation removes certain patterns in the input data and their local optima in the training, to only keep patterns (local optima) that are most relevant to the tasks.

Here we only use 1-dimensional rotation group as one simple example. In practice, the augmentation may not globally form a Lie group, and there could be multiple different types of augmentations, yielding high-dimensional transformation space. Therefore, we may use Lie algebra instead to capture the local transformation structure, without making assumptions about the global structure. We will give a formal study in the future work.

Proof.

Use variance decomposition, we have:

Remember that

is an abbreviation of gated input:

By conditional independence, we have

\mathrm{Cov}[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{km},\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k^{\prime}m^{\prime}}|z]=0\quad\quad\quad\forall k\neq k^{\prime}

(123)

This is because
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{km}
and

are deterministic functions of
{\bm{x}}_{k}
and
{\bm{x}}_{k^{\prime}}
and thus are also independent of each other.

Let

\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k1}\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k2}\\
\ldots\\
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{kM}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{Md}

(124)

and
L_{k}:=\mathbb{E}_{z}\mathbb{V}[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}|z]
. Then we know that
\mathbb{E}_{z}\mathbb{V}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]=\mathrm{diag}_{k}[L_{k}]
is a block diagonal matrix (See Fig.

).

On the other hand,

is a low-rank matrix:

\mathbb{V}_{z}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}])(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}])^{\top}\right]

(125)

Let

and
{\bm{q}}_{-c}:=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z\neq c]
, then we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z=c]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\bm{q}}_{c}-\sum_{c}p_{c}{\bm{q}}_{c}=(1-p_{c})\left({\bm{q}}_{c}-\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\frac{p_{c^{\prime}}}{1-p_{c}}{\bm{q}}_{c^{\prime}}\right)

(126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-p_{c})\left({\bm{q}}_{c}-\sum_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\mathbb{P}[z=c^{\prime}|z\neq c]{\bm{q}}_{c^{\prime}}\right)

(127)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-p_{c})({\bm{q}}_{c}-{\bm{q}}_{-c})

(128)

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{z}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}])(\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}])^{\top}\right]

(129)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c}p_{c}(1-p_{c})^{2}({\bm{q}}_{c}-{\bm{q}}_{-c})({\bm{q}}_{c}-{\bm{q}}_{-c})^{\top}

(130)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c}p_{c}(1-p_{c})^{2}\Delta(c)\Delta^{\top}(c)

(131)

where

\Delta(c):=\Delta(c;W):={\bm{q}}_{c}-{\bm{q}}_{-c}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta_{11}(c)\\
\ldots\\
\Delta_{KM}(c)\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{KMd}

(132)

and

\Delta_{km}(c):=\Delta_{km}(c;{\bm{w}}_{km}):=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{km}|z=c]-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{km}|z\neq c]

(133)

We can see that
\mathbb{V}_{z}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\bm{x}}|z]
is at most rank-
C
, since it is a summation of
C
rank-1 matrix.

In particular, when

and thus

and

Remark
.
Special case
. We start by assuming only one
\epsilon_{l}\neq 0
and all other
\epsilon_{l^{\prime}}=0
for

, and then we generalize to the case when all
\{\epsilon_{l}\}
are real numbers.

To quantify the probability that a random weight initialization leads to convergence of
{\bm{u}}_{l}
, we setup some notations. Let the event
E_{l}
be “a random weight initialization of
{\bm{y}}
leads to
{\bm{y}}\rightarrow{\bm{e}}_{l}
”, or equivalently
{\bm{w}}\rightarrow{\bm{u}}_{l}
. Let
Y_{l}
be the random variable that instantiates the initial value of
y_{l}(0)
due to random weight initialization. Then the convergence event
E_{l}
is equivalent to the following: (1)
Y_{l}>0
(so that the
l
-component has the opportunity to grow), and (2)
Y_{l}+\epsilon_{l}
is the maximum over all
Y_{l^{\prime}}
for any
l^{\prime}\neq l
, where
\epsilon_{l}
is an advantage (
>0
) or disadvantage (
<0
) achieved by having larger/smaller
b_{l}
due to global modulation (e.g.,
c
). Therefore, we also call
\epsilon_{l}
the
modulation factor
.

Here we discuss about a simple case that
Y_{l}\sim U[-1,1]
and for
l^{\prime}\neq l
,
Y_{l}
and
Y_{l^{\prime}}
are independent. In this case, for a given
l
,
\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}
is a random variable that is independent of
Y_{l}
, and has cumulative density function (CDF)
F_{\max}(x):=\mathbb{P}[\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}\leq x]=F^{d-1}(x)
, where
F(x)
is the CDF for
Y_{l}
.

Then we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{l}>0,Y_{l}+\epsilon_{l}\geq\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}\right]

(152)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}\leq Y_{l}+\epsilon_{l}\Big|Y_{l}=y_{l}\right]\mathbb{P}[Y_{l}=y_{l}]\mathrm{d}y_{l}

(153)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}F^{d-1}(y_{l}+\epsilon_{l})\mathrm{d}F(y_{l})

(154)

When
Y_{l}\sim U[-1,1]
,
F(x)=\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}(x+1),1\right\}
has a close form and we can compute the integral:

\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]=\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{l}>0,Y_{l}+\epsilon_{l}\geq\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}&\epsilon_{l}>1\\
\frac{1}{d}\left[1-\left(\frac{1+\epsilon_{l}}{2}\right)^{d}\right]+\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{2}&0\leq\epsilon_{l}\leq 1\\
\frac{1}{d}\left[(1+\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{2})^{d}-(\frac{1+\epsilon_{l}}{2})^{d}\right]&-1<\epsilon_{l}<0\end{array}\right.

(155)

We can see that the modulation factor
\epsilon_{l}
plays an important role in deciding the probability that
{\bm{w}}\rightarrow{\bm{u}}_{l}
:

•

No modulation
. If
\epsilon_{l}=0
, then
\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]\sim\frac{1}{d}
. This means that each dimension of
{\bm{y}}
has equal probability to be the dominant component after training;

•

Positive modulation
. If
\epsilon_{l}>0
, then
\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]\geq\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{2}
, and that particular
l
-th component has much higher probability to become the dominant component, independent of the dimensionality
d
. Furthermore, the stronger the modulation, the higher the probability becomes.

•

Negative modulation
. Finally, if
\epsilon_{l}<0
, since

,
\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]\leq\frac{1}{d}(1+\frac{\epsilon_{l}}{2})^{d}
decays exponentially w.r.t the dimensionality
d
.

General case
. We then analyze cases if all
\epsilon_{l}
are real numbers. Let

and

be the
k
-th index of
\epsilon_{l}

•

For

,
\epsilon_{l}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[E_{l}]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{l}\geq 0,Y_{l}+\epsilon_{l}\geq\max_{l^{\prime}\neq l}Y_{l^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{l^{\prime}}\right]

•

For

Appendix D
Analysis of Batch Normalization

From the previous analysis of global modulation, it is clear that the weight updating can be much slower for RF with small

(Eqn.

We consider BatchNorm right after

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i}f_{k}[i]+f_{k}[i^{\prime}]

(164)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i}(f_{k}[i]-\mu_{k})^{2}+(f_{k}[i^{\prime}]-\mu_{k})^{2}

(165)

When

Proof.

The proof is similar to Lemma

Remarks
In the presence of BatchNorm, Lemma
5
still holds, since it only depends on the generative structure of the data. Therefore, we have

\sigma^{2}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}[f_{k}]={\bm{w}}^{\top}_{k}\mathbb{V}[\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}]{\bm{w}}_{k}=d_{k}+p_{0}p_{1}\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}_{k}\Delta_{k}\right)^{2}

and thus

d^{\mathrm{bn}}_{k}=\sigma_{k}^{-2}d_{k}\rightarrow\frac{d_{k}}{d_{k}+p_{0}p_{1}\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}_{k}\Delta_{k}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+p_{0}p_{1}\left({\bm{w}}^{\top}_{k}\Delta_{k}/\sqrt{d_{k}}\right)^{2}}

becomes more uniform. This is because
d_{k}:={\bm{w}}^{\top}_{k}L_{k}{\bm{w}}_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{z}\mathbb{V}[f_{k}|z]\geq 0
(Eqn.
135
) is approximately the variance of
f_{k}
, and thus
{\bm{w}}_{k}^{\top}\Delta_{k}/\sqrt{d_{k}}
is normalized across different receptive field
k
, reducing the effect of magnitude of the input.

Since there is no much variation within
\{d^{\mathrm{bn}}_{k}\}
,
\lambda^{\mathrm{bn}}-d^{\mathrm{bn}}_{k}
becomes almost constant across different receptive field
R_{k}
and won’t lead to slowness of feature learning.

E.2
2-layer setting

We also do more experiments on the 2-layer setting, to further verify our theoretical findings.

Overall matching score
\bar{\chi}_{+}{}
and overall irrelevant-matching score
\bar{\chi}_{-}
. As defined in the main text (Eqn.
16
), the matching score
\chi_{+}(R_{k})
is the degree of matching between learned weights and the embeddings of the subset
R^{\mathrm{g}{}}_{k}
of tokens that are allowed in the global patterns at each receptive field
R_{k}
. And the overall matching score
\bar{\chi}_{+}{}
is
\bar{\chi}_{+}{}
averaged over all receptive fields:

\chi_{+}(R_{k})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{a\in R^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}}\max_{m}\frac{{\bm{w}}^{\top}_{km}{\bm{u}}_{a}}{\|{\bm{w}}_{km}\|_{2}\|{\bm{u}}_{a}\|_{2}},\quad\quad\quad\quad\bar{\chi}_{+}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\chi_{+}(R_{k})

(180)

Similarly, we can also define
irrelevant-matching score

\chi_{-}(R_{k})
which is the degree of matching between learned weights and the embeddings of the tokens that are NOT in the subset
R^{\mathrm{g}{}}_{k}
at each receptive field
R_{k}
. And the overall
irrelevant-matching
score
\bar{\chi}_{-}
is defined similarly.

\chi_{-}(R_{k})=\frac{1}{P}\sum_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}a\notin R^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}}}\max_{m}\frac{{\bm{w}}^{\top}_{km}{\bm{u}}_{a}}{\|{\bm{w}}_{km}\|_{2}\|{\bm{u}}_{a}\|_{2}},\quad\quad\quad\quad\bar{\chi}_{-}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}\chi_{-}(R_{k})

(181)

Ideally, we want to see high overall matching score
\bar{\chi}_{+}
and low overall irrelevant-matching score
\bar{\chi}_{-}{}
, which means that the important patterns in
R^{\mathrm{g}}_{k}
(i.e., the patterns that are allowed in the global generators) are learned, but noisy patterns that are not part of the global patterns (i.e., the generators) are not learned. Fig.
9
shows that this indeed is the case.

Non-uniformity
\zeta
and how BatchNorm interacts with it.

When the scale of input data varies a lot, BatchNorm starts to matter in discovering features with low magnitude (Sec.
D
). To model the scale non-uniformity, we set
\|{\bm{u}}_{a}\|_{2}=\zeta
for
\lfloor d/2\rfloor
tokens and
\|{\bm{u}}_{a}\|_{2}=1/\zeta
for the remaining tokens. Larger
\zeta
corresponds to higher non-uniformity across inputs.

Fig.
11
shows that BN with ReLU activations handles large non-uniformity (large
\zeta
) very well, compared to the case without BN. Specifically, BN yields higher
\bar{\chi}_{+}{}
in the presence of high non-uniformity (e.g.,
\zeta=10
) when the network is over-parameterized (
\beta>1
) and there are multiple candidates per
R_{k}
(
P>1
), a setting that is likely to hold in real-world scenarios.

Note that in the real-world scenario, features from different channels/modalities indeed will have very different scales, and some local features that turn out to be super important to global features, can have very small scale. In such cases, normalization techniques (e.g., BatchNorm) can be very useful and our formulation justifies it in a mathematically consistent way.

Appendix F
Context and Motivations of theorems

Here are the motivations and intuitions behind each major theoretical results:

•

First of all, Lemma
2
and Corollary
1
try to make connection between a relatively new (and somehow abstract) concept (i.e., contrastive covariance
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[\cdot]
) and a well-known concept (i.e., regular covariance
\mathbb{V}[\cdot]
). This will also enable us to leverage existing properties of covariance, in order to deepen our understanding of this concept, which seems to play an important role in contrastive learning (CL).

•

After that, we mainly focus on studying the CL energy function
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
, which can be represented in terms of the contrastive covariance
\mathbb{C}_{\alpha}[\cdot]
. One important property of the energy function is whether there exists any local optima and what are the properties of these local optima, since these local optima are the final destinations that network weights will converge into. Previous works in landscape analysis often talk about local optima in neural networks as abstract objects in high-dimensional space, but here, we would like to make them as concrete as possible.

•

For such analysis, we always start from the simplest case (e.g., one-layer). Therefore, naturally we have Lemma
3
that characterizes critical points (as a superset of local optima), a few examples in Sec.
3.2
, properties of these critical points and when they become local optima in Sec.
3
. Finally, Appendix
B.5
further gives a preliminary study on how the data augmentation affects the distribution of the local optima.

•

Then we extend our analysis to 2-layer setting. The key question is to study the additional benefits of 2-layer network compared to
K
independent 1-layer cases. Here the assumption of
disjoint
receptive fields is to make sure there is an apple-to-apple comparison, otherwise additional complexity would be involved, e.g., overlapping receptive fields. As demonstrated in Theorem
5
and Theorem
6
, we find the effect of
global modulation
in 2-layer case, which clearly tells that the interactions across different receptive fields lead to additional terms in the dynamics that favors patterns related to latent variable
z
that leads to conditional independence across the disjointed receptive fields.

•

As a side track, in 2-layer case, we also have Theorem
4
that shows linear activation does not learn distinct features, which is consistent with 1-layer case that linear activation
h(x)=x
only gives to maximal PCA directions (Sec.
6
).

7
Bound of
1-d_{t}
.

Define

\mu({\bm{w}}):=\frac{1+c({\bm{w}})}{2c^{2}({\bm{w}})}\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(A({\bm{w}}(t))}{\lambda_{1}(A({\bm{w}}(t))}\right)^{2}=\frac{1+c({\bm{w}})}{2c^{2}({\bm{w}})}\left[1-\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}(A(t))}{\lambda_{1}(A(t))}\right]^{2}\geq 0

(182)

and
\mu_{t}:=\mu({\bm{w}}(t))
. If
c_{t}>0
and
\lambda_{1}(t)>0
, then
1-d_{t}\leq\mu_{t}(1-c_{t})
.

Proof.

We could write
d_{t}
:

\displaystyle d_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\phi^{\top}_{1}(t)\tilde{\bm{w}}(t+1)}{\|\tilde{\bm{w}}(t+1)\|_{2}}=\frac{\lambda_{1}(t)\phi^{\top}_{1}(t){\bm{w}}(t)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda^{2}_{i}(t)\left(\phi^{\top}_{i}(t){\bm{w}}(t)\right)^{2}}}

(183)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}(t)c_{t}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{1}^{2}(t)c_{t}^{2}+\lambda_{2}^{2}(t)(1-c_{t}^{2})}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(t)}{\lambda_{1}(t)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{c_{t}^{2}}-1\right)}}

(184)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[1+\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(t)}{\lambda_{1}(t)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{c_{t}^{2}}-1\right)\right]^{-1/2}

(185)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}(t)}{\lambda_{1}(t)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{c_{t}^{2}}-1\right)=:1-\mu_{t}(1-c_{t})

(186)

The first inequality is due to the fact that
\sum_{i>1}\lambda^{2}_{i}(t)\left(\phi^{\top}_{i}(t){\bm{w}}(t)\right)^{2}=1-c^{2}_{t}
(Parseval’s identity).
The last inequality is due to the fact that for
x>-1
,
(1+x)^{\alpha}\geq 1+\alpha x
when
\alpha\geq 1
or
\alpha<0
(Bernoulli’s inequality). Therefore the conclusion holds.
∎

8

If
c_{t}>0
and
\lambda_{i}(t)>0
for all
i
, then
\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})}

Proof.

First, for
{\bm{w}}^{\top}(t+1){\bm{w}}(t)
, we have (notice that
\lambda_{i}(t)\geq 0
):

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{\top}(t+1){\bm{w}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}(t)\left(\phi^{\top}_{i}(t){\bm{w}}(t)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda^{2}_{i}(t)\left(\phi^{\top}_{i}(t){\bm{w}}(t)\right)^{2}}}

(187)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{1}(t)c_{t}^{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}_{1}(t)c_{t}^{2}+\lambda^{2}_{2}(t)(1-c_{t}^{2})}}\geq\left[1-\mu_{t}(1-c_{t})\right]c_{t}

(188)

Therefore,

\|{\bm{w}}(t+1)-{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}=\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1-{\bm{w}}^{\top}(t){\bm{w}}(t+1)}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})}

(189)

∎

9
.

Let
\delta A=A^{\prime}-A
, then the maximal eigenvector
\phi_{1}:=\phi_{1}(A)
and
\phi^{\prime}_{1}:=\phi_{1}(A^{\prime})
has the following Taylor expansion:

\phi^{\prime}_{1}=\phi_{1}+\Delta\phi_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\|\delta A\|_{2}^{2})

(190)

where
\lambda_{i}
is the
i
-th eigenvalue of
A
,
\Delta\phi_{1}:=\sum_{j>1}\frac{\phi^{\top}_{j}\delta A\phi_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\phi_{j}
is the first-order term of eigenvector perturbation. In terms of inequality, there exist
\kappa>0
so that:

\|\phi^{\prime}_{1}-(\phi_{1}+\Delta\phi_{1})\|_{2}\leq\kappa\|\delta A\|_{2}^{2}

(191)

Proof.

See time-independent perturbation theory in Quantum Mechanics
(
Fernández 2000
)
.
∎

10
.

Let
L
be the minimal Lipschitz constant of
A
so that
\|A({\bm{w}}^{\prime})-A({\bm{w}})\|_{2}\leq L\|{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}^{\prime}\|_{2}
holds. If
c_{t}>0
and
\lambda_{i}(t)>0
for all
i
, then we have:

|d_{t}-c_{t+1}|=\Big|\left(\phi_{1}(t)-\phi_{1}(t+1)\right)^{\top}{\bm{w}}(t+1)\Big|\leq\nu_{t}(1-c_{t})

(192)

where

\nu({\bm{w}}):=2\kappa L^{2}(1+\mu({\bm{w}})c({\bm{w}}))+2L\lambda^{-1}_{\mathrm{gap}}(A{\bm{w}}(t))\sqrt{\mu({\bm{w}})(1+\mu({\bm{w}})c({\bm{w}}))}\geq 0

(193)

and
\nu_{t}:=\nu({\bm{w}}(t))
.

Proof.

Using Lemma
9
and the fact that
\|{\bm{w}}(t+1)\|_{2}=1
, we have:

|d_{t}-c_{t+1}|=\Big|\left(\phi_{1}(t)-\phi_{1}(t+1)\right)^{\top}{\bm{w}}(t+1)\Big|\leq|\Delta\phi^{\top}_{1}(t){\bm{w}}(t+1)|+\kappa L^{2}\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}^{2}

(194)

where

\Delta\phi_{1}(t):=\sum_{j>1}\frac{\phi^{\top}(t)_{j}\delta A(t)\phi_{1}(t)}{\lambda_{1}(t)-\lambda_{j}(t)}\phi_{j}(t)

(195)

and
\delta A(t):=A(t+1)-A(t)
. For brevity, we omit all temporal notation if the quantity is evaluated at iteration
t
. E.g.,
\delta{\bm{w}}
means
\delta{\bm{w}}(t)
and
\phi_{1}
means
\phi_{1}(t)
.

Now we bound
|\Delta\phi_{1}^{\top}{\bm{w}}(t+1)|
. Using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle|\Delta\phi_{1}^{\top}{\bm{w}}(t+1)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg|\sum_{j>1}\left(\frac{\phi^{\top}_{j}\delta A\phi_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\right)\left(\phi^{\top}_{j}{\bm{w}}(t+1)\right)\Bigg|

(196)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{j>1}\left(\frac{\phi^{\top}_{j}\delta A\phi_{1}}{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{j}}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{j>1}\left(\phi^{\top}_{j}{\bm{w}}(t+1)\right)^{2}}

(197)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}(A)}\sqrt{\sum_{j>1}\left(\phi^{\top}_{j}\delta A\phi_{1}\right)^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{j>1}\left(\phi^{\top}_{j}{\bm{w}}(t+1)\right)^{2}}

(198)

Since
\{\phi_{j}\}
is a set of orthonormal bases, Parseval’s identity tells that for any vector
{\bm{v}}
, its energy under any orthonormal bases are preserved:
\sum_{j}(\phi^{\top}_{j}{\bm{v}})^{2}=\|{\bm{v}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle|\Delta\phi_{1}^{\top}{\bm{w}}(t+1)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}(A)}\|\delta A\phi_{1}\|_{2}\sqrt{1-d^{2}_{t}}

(199)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L}{\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}(A)}\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}\sqrt{1-d^{2}_{t}}

(200)

Note that using
-1\leq d_{t}\leq 1
and Lemma
7
, we have:

\sqrt{1-d_{t}^{2}}=\sqrt{1+d_{t}}\sqrt{1-d_{t}}\leq\sqrt{2(1-d_{t})}\leq\sqrt{2\mu_{t}(1-c_{t})}

(201)

Finally using bound of weight difference (Lemma
8
), we have:

\displaystyle|d_{t}-c_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\kappa L^{2}(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})+L\lambda_{\mathrm{gap}}^{-1}\sqrt{2(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})}\sqrt{1-d_{t}^{2}}

(202)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(1-c_{t})

(203)

Here
\nu_{t}:=2\kappa L^{2}(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})+2L\lambda^{-1}_{\mathrm{gap}}(A(t))\sqrt{\mu_{t}(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})}
.
∎

11
.

Let
c_{0}:=c({\bm{w}}(0))={\bm{w}}^{\top}(0)\phi_{1}(A({\bm{w}}(0)))>0
. Define local region
B_{\gamma}
:

B_{\gamma}:=\left\{{\bm{w}}:\|{\bm{w}}-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}}{1-\sqrt{\gamma}}\right\}

(204)

Define
\omega({\bm{w}}):=\mu({\bm{w}})+\nu({\bm{w}})
to be the irregularity (also defined in Def.
4
). If there exists
\gamma<1
so that

\sup_{{\bm{w}}\in B_{\gamma}}\omega({\bm{w}})\leq\gamma,

(205)

then

•

The sequence
\{c_{t}\}
increases monotonously and converges to
1
;

•

There exists
{\bm{w}}_{*}
so that
\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\bm{w}}(t)={\bm{w}}_{*}
.

•

{\bm{w}}_{*}
is the maximal eigenvector of
A({\bm{w}}_{*})
and thus a fixed point of gradient update (Eqn.
7
);

•

For any
t
,
\|{\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}}{1-\sqrt{\gamma}}
.

•

\|{\bm{w}}_{*}-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}}{1-\sqrt{\gamma}}
. That is,
{\bm{w}}_{*}
is in the vicinity of the initial weight
{\bm{w}}(0)
.

Proof.

We first prove by induction that the following
induction arguments
are true for any
t
:

•

c_{t+1}\geq c_{t}>0
;

•

1-c_{t}\leq\gamma^{t}(1-c_{0})
;

•

{\bm{w}}(t)
is not far away from its initial value
{\bm{w}}(0)
:

\|{\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\gamma^{t^{\prime}/2}

(206)

which suggests that
{\bm{w}}(t)\in B_{\gamma}
.

Base case (
t=1
)
. Since
1\geq c_{0}>0
,
\mu({\bm{w}})\geq 0
, and
A({\bm{w}})
is PD, applying Lemma
8
to
\|{\bm{w}}(1)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}
, it is clear that

\|{\bm{w}}(1)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}=\|\delta{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}\sqrt{(1+\mu_{0}c_{0})(1-c_{0})}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}

(207)

Note that the last inequality is due to
\mu_{0}\leq\gamma
. Note that

1-c_{1}=1-d_{0}+d_{0}-c_{1}\leq 1-d_{t}+|d_{0}-c_{1}|\leq(\mu_{0}+\nu_{0})(1-c_{0})\leq\gamma(1-c_{0})

(208)

and finally we have
c_{1}\geq 1-\gamma(1-c_{0})\geq c_{0}>0
. So the base case is satisfied.

Inductive step
. Assume for
t
, the induction argument is true and thus
{\bm{w}}(t)\in B_{\gamma}
. Therefore, by the condition, we know
\mu_{t}+\nu_{t}\leq\gamma
.

By Lemma
8
, we know that

\|{\bm{w}}(t+1)-{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}=\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\gamma^{t/2}

(209)

Therefore, we know that
{\bm{w}}(t+1)
also satisfies Eqn.
206
:

\displaystyle\|{\bm{w}}(t+1)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}+\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}

(210)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\gamma^{t^{\prime}/2}+\gamma^{t/2}\right]

(211)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}\gamma^{t^{\prime}/2}

(212)

Also we have:

\displaystyle 1-c_{t+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-d_{t}+d_{t}-c_{t+1}\leq 1-d_{t}+|d_{t}-c_{t+1}|

(213)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\mu_{t}+\nu_{t})(1-c_{t})\leq\gamma(1-c_{t})

(214)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma^{t+1}(1-c_{0})

(215)

and thus we have
c_{t+1}\geq 1-\gamma(1-c_{t})\geq c_{t}>0
.

Therefore, we have

1-c_{t}\leq\gamma^{t}(1-c_{0})\rightarrow 0

(216)

thus
c_{t}
is monotonously increasing to
1
. This means that:

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}c_{t}=\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\phi_{1}^{\top}(t){\bm{w}}(t)\rightarrow 1

(217)

Therefore, we can show that
{\bm{w}}(t)
is also convergent, by checking how fast
\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}
decays:

\|\delta{\bm{w}}(t)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\mu_{t}c_{t})(1-c_{t})}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\gamma^{t/2}

(218)

By Cauchy’s convergence test,
{\bm{w}}(t)={\bm{w}}(0)+\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\delta{\bm{w}}(t^{\prime})
also converges. Let

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}{\bm{w}}(t)={\bm{w}}_{*}

(219)

This means that
A({\bm{w}}_{*}){\bm{w}}_{*}=\lambda_{*}{\bm{w}}_{*}
and thus
P_{{\bm{w}}_{*}}^{\perp}A({\bm{w}}_{*}){\bm{w}}_{*}=0
, i.e.,
{\bm{w}}_{*}
is a fixed point of gradient update (Eqn.
7
). Finally,
we have:

\|{\bm{w}}(t)-{\bm{w}}(0)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t-1}\gamma^{t^{\prime}/2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2(1+\gamma)(1-c_{0})}}{1-\sqrt{\gamma}}

(220)

Since
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is continuous, we have the conclusion.
∎
