A CATEGORY OF ARROW ALGEBRAS FOR MODIFIED REALIZABILITY
Umberto Tarantino

Abstract
. In this paper we further the study of arrow algebras, simple algebraic structures inducing toposes through the tripos-to-topos construction, by defining appropriate notions of morphisms between them which correspond to morphisms of the associated triposes. Specializing to geometric inclusions, we characterize subtriposes of an arrow tripos in terms of nuclei on the underlying arrow algebra, recovering a classical locale-theoretic result. As an example of application, we lift modified realizability to the setting of arrow algebras, and we establish its functoriality.



The purpose of this paper is to develop the theory of arrow algebras as a framework to study realizability toposes from a more concrete, ‘algebraic’, point of view which can also take localic toposes into account.

Arrow algebras were introduced in
[
vdBB23
]
, of which this paper can be seen as a follow-up, generalizing Alexandre Miquel’s
implicative algebras

[
Miq20a
,
Miq20b
]
as algebraic structures which induce triposes and then toposes through the tripos-to-topos construction. The weakening of the axiom that distinguishes implicative algebras from arrow algebras allows the latter to perfectly factor through the construction of realizability triposes coming from partial combinatory algebras – from now on, PCAs – which are actually
partial
, whereas implicative algebras are the intermediate structure only in the case of
total
combinatory algebras. Indeed, in
[
vdBB23
]
it is shown how every frame can be seen as an arrow algebra in such a way that the induced
arrow tripos
coincides with the usual localic tripos; similarly, every PCA gives rise to an arrow algebra in such a way that the induced arrow tripos coincides with the usual realizability tripos. The aim of the following is then to define appropriate notions of morphisms between arrow algebras which correspond to morphisms of the associated triposes, so as to determine a category of arrow algebras factoring through the construction of both realizability and localic triposes in a 2-functorial way.

Related work

Earlier work in this area includes Hofstra’s
basic combinatory objects
(BCOs)
[
Hof06
]
, fundamental in advocating for the study of
relative
realizability which is implemented by the theory of both implicative and arrow algebras through the
separator
. The notion of a BCO appears quite different and more combinatorial in nature than the more algebraic notion of an arrow algebra, and we still don’t see how exactly the two structures relate: we hope to investigate this further in the future, possibly showing how to endow every arrow algebra with the structure of a BCO inducing the same tripos. A further generalization in this direction is given by Frey’s
uniform preorders

[
Fre13
,
Fre24
]
. Closely related to implicative algebras are instead Cohen, Miquey and Tate’s
evidenced frames

[
CMT21
]
. We leave for further research the question of how exactly arrow algebras compare with these other structures – now also with respect to morphisms.

Contents

The contents of this paper are as follows. In
section 1
we briefly review the theory of triposes and their morphisms. In
section 2
we summarize the theory of arrow algebras from
[
vdBB23
]
and we describe the two main examples: those coming from frames, and those coming from partial combinatory algebras. In
section 3
we introduce a first notion of morphism between arrow algebras, and in
section 4
we specialize it to the two main classes of arrow algebras described above recovering familiar notions in both cases. In
section 5
, we show how these morphisms correspond to appropriate transformations of the associated triposes, and we introduce the notion of
computational density
characterizing geometric morphisms of triposes; in
section 6
, we specialize once again to the two main examples. In
section 7
, we focus on geometric inclusions, characterizing subtriposes of triposes arising from arrow algebras as arrow triposes themselves. This recovers and extends
[
vdBB23
, Sec. 6]
, in particular by proving the converse of
[
vdBB23
, Prop. 6.3]
, and relies on a particularly simple construction on arrow algebras which does not seem to work for implicative algebras. As subtriposes exactly correspond to subtoposes, this means in particular that we can study subtoposes of realizability toposes by studying
nuclei
on the underlying arrow algebras. Finally, in
section 8
we apply the previous machinery to the study of Kreisel’s modified realizability on the level of arrow algebras, rephrasing and extending results partially known in the literature at the level of PCAs.

2-categorical notation

Following
[
Zoe22
]
, with
2-category
we mean a 2-dimensional category which is also an ordinary category, meaning that the unit and associativity laws for 1-cells hold on the nose. Instead, we speak of
bicategories
for 2-dimensional categories where the axioms of an ordinary category only hold up to invertible 2-cells.

A
preorder-enriched category
, i.e. a category enriched over the category
\Preord
of preordered sets and monotone functions, can then be seen as a locally small 2-category with at most one 2-cell between any pair of 1-cells.
\Preord
is itself preorder-enriched with respect to the pointwise order. We refer to weak 2-functors and strict 2-functors as
pseudofunctors
and
2-functors
, respectively.
Pseudonatural transformations
are defined in the usual way. With
pseudomonad
on a preorder-enriched category, we will refer to a (fully) weak 2-monad, that is, an endo-pseudofunctor endowed with pseudonatural unit and multiplication satisfying the usual monad axioms up to isomorphism. For more details on 2-categories and 2-monads, we refer the reader to
[
JY20
]
.

In a preorder-enriched category, a morphism
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is
left adjoint
to a morphism
g:Y\longrightarrow X
– equivalently,
g
is
right adjoint
to
f
– if
\id_{X}\leq gf
and
fg\leq\id_{Y}
, in which case we write
f\dashv g
. Two parallel morphisms
f,g
are
isomorphic
if
f\leq g
and
g\leq f
, in which case we write
f\simeq g
.

1.1.
Triposes

In this paper, we will only consider
\Sets
-based triposes.

Recall that a
Heyting prealgebra
is a preorder whose poset reflection is a Heyting algebra, and a morphism of Heyting prealgebras is a monotone function which is a morphism of Heyting algebras between the poset reflections of domain and codomain. The category
\HeytPre
of Heyting prealgebras is preorder-enriched with respect to the pointwise order.

Definition 1.1
.

A
(
\Sets
-)tripos
is a pseudofunctor
P:{\Sets}^{\operatorname{op}}\longrightarrow\HeytPre
satisfying the following axioms.

i.

For every function
f:X\longrightarrow Y
, the map
f^{*}\coloneqq P(f):P(Y)\longrightarrow P(X)
has both a left adjoint
\exists_{f}
and a right adjoint
\forall_{f}
in
\Preord
,
1
1

1

That is,
\exists_{f}
and
\forall_{f}
need not preserve the Heyting structure.
and they satisfy the Beck-Chevalley condition.

ii.

There exists a
generic element
in
P
, i.e. an element
\sigma\in P(\Sigma)
for some set
\Sigma
with the property that, for every set
X
and every element
\phi\in P(X)
, there exists a function
[\phi]:X\longrightarrow\Sigma
such that
\phi
and
[\phi]^{*}(\sigma)
are isomorphic elements of
P(X)
.

A tripos
P
such that
P(X)
is the set
\Sigma^{X}
of functions

for some set
\Sigma
and
P(f)
acts by precomposing with

is said to be

.

Example 1.2
.

Let
H
be a complete Heyting algebra. We define the
\Sets
-tripos of

as follows.

For every set

, we let
P_{H}(X)\coloneqq H^{X}
, which is a Heyting algebra under pointwise order and operations; for every function

, the precomposition map
f^{*}:P_{H}(Y)\longrightarrow P_{H}(X)
is then a morphism of Heyting algebras. Adjoints for
f^{*}
are provided by completeness as, for
\phi\in P_{H}(X)
and

\exists_{f}(\phi)(y)\coloneqq\bigveeop\displaylimits_{x\in{f}^{\kern 0.73616pt\text{-}1}(y)}\phi(x)\qquad\forall_{f}(\phi)(y)\coloneqq\bigwedgeop\displaylimits_{x\in{f}^{\kern 0.73616pt\text{-}1}(y)}\phi(x)

which also satisfy the Beck-Chevalley condition. A generic element is trivially given by
\id_{H}\in P_{H}(H)
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
P
and
Q
be triposes. A
transformation

\Phi:P\longrightarrow Q
is a pseudonatural transformation
P\Rightarrow Q
, seeing
P
and
Q
as pseudofunctors
{{\Sets}^{\operatorname{op}}\longrightarrow\Preord}
; explicitly, this means that each component
\Phi_{X}:P(X)\longrightarrow Q(X)
is an order-preserving function but not necessarily a morphism of Heyting prealgebras.

Transformations

can be ordered by letting
\Phi\leq\Psi
if
\Phi_{X}\leq{}_{X}
pointwise for every set
X
, therefore making triposes and transformations into a preorder-enriched category which we denote as
\mathsf{Trip}({\Sets})
.

A transformation
\Phi:P\longrightarrow Q
is an
equivalence
if there exists another transformation
\Psi:Q\longrightarrow P
such that
\Phi\circ\Psi\simeq\id_{Q}
and
\Psi\circ\Phi\simeq\id_{P}
.

Every tripos
P
gives rise to a topos
\Sets[P]
through the
tripos-to-topos construction
, which we will not describe here as it does not serve any purpose for the sake of this paper. The interested reader can find all the details in
[

]
.

Example 1.4
.

For a complete Heyting algebra
H
,
\Sets[P_{H}]
is the topos of
H
-valued sets, proved in
[

]
to be equivalent to the topos
\operatorname{Sh}(H)
of sheaves over
H
.

1.2.
Geometric morphisms of triposes

The most important notion of morphism between toposes is arguably that of a
geometric morphism
, which by now has a vast and standard theory. Much more niche, instead, is the theory of geometric morphisms of triposes, and how they relate with geometric morphisms of toposes: with no aim for a complete treatment, we review here what we will need in the following.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
P
and
Q
be triposes. A transformation
\Phi:P\longrightarrow Q
is
cartesian
if each component
\Phi_{X}:P(X)\longrightarrow Q(X)
preserves finite meets up to isomorphism. We denote with
\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})
the wide subcategory of
\mathsf{Trip}({\Sets})
on cartesian transformations.

A transformation is
geometric
if it is cartesian and admits a right adjoint in
\mathsf{Trip}({\Sets})
, that is, if there exists another transformation
\Phi_{+}:Q\longrightarrow P
such that
(\Phi^{+})_{X}\dashv(\Phi_{+})_{X}
in
\Preord
for every set
X
. We denote with
\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{geom}}({\Sets})
the wide subcategory of
\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})
on geometric transformations.

In this language, a
geometric morphism
of triposes
(\Phi^{+},\Phi_{+}):Q\longrightarrow P
is a geometric transformation
\Phi^{+}:P\longrightarrow Q
with right adjoint
\Phi_{+}:Q\longrightarrow P
, of which they constitute respectively the
inverse
and
direct image
.
2
2

2

The direction is conventional and agrees with the definition of geometric morphisms of toposes.

Remark 1.6
.

Cartesian transformations preserve the interpretation of
cartesian logic
, the fragment of finitary first-order logic defined by
\top
and
\land
.

Geometric transformations preserve the interpretation of
geometric logic
, the fragment of infinitary first-order logic defined by
\top
,
\bot
, finitary
\land
, infinitary
\lor
and
\exists
.

We will not go into details on the internal logic of triposes as it will not play any role in the paper; once again, we refer the reader to
[
vO08
]
.

Remark 1.7
.

Let
\Phi:P\longrightarrow Q
be an equivalence and let
\Psi:Q\longrightarrow P
be such that
\Phi\circ\Psi\simeq\id_{Q}
and
\Psi\circ\Phi\simeq\id_{P}
. Then,
(\Phi,\Psi):Q\longrightarrow P
and
(\Psi,\Phi):P\longrightarrow Q
are both geometric morphisms.

Theorem 1.8
.

Every geometric morphism of triposes
Q\longrightarrow P
induces a geometric morphism
\Sets[Q]\longrightarrow\Sets[P]
.

Remark 1.9
.

The converse is not true in general: a geometric morphism
\Sets[Q]\longrightarrow\Sets[P]
is induced by a geometric morphism of triposes
Q\longrightarrow P
if and only if its inverse image part
preserves constant objects
.

Example 1.10
.

Let
X,Y
be two complete Heyting algebras regarded as locales. Then, geometric morphisms
P_{X}\longrightarrow P_{Y}
correspond to locale homomorphisms
X\longrightarrow Y
.

More precisely, given any geometric morphism
\Phi=(\Phi^{+},\Phi_{+}):P_{X}\longrightarrow P_{Y}
, there exists an essentially unique morphism of locales
f:X\longrightarrow Y
such that, regarding
f
as a morphism of frames
f^{*}:\O(Y)\longrightarrow\O(X)
and letting
f_{*}:\O(X)\longrightarrow\O(Y)
be its right adjoint,
\Phi^{+}
is given by postcomposition with
f^{*}
and
\Phi_{+}
is given by postcomposition with
f_{*}
.

1.3.
Subtriposes

Geometric inclusions and surjections of toposes admit analogs on the level of triposes.

Definition 1.11
.

A geometric morphism of triposes
\Phi=(\Phi^{+},\Phi_{+}):Q\longrightarrow P
is an
inclusion
, in which case we also write
\Phi:Q\hookrightarrow P
, if either of the following equivalent conditions hold:

–

for every set
X
,
(\Phi_{+})_{X}
reflects the order;

–

\Phi^{+}\circ\Phi_{+}\simeq\id_{Q}
,

Dually,
\Phi
is a
surjection
, in which case we also write
\Phi:Q\twoheadrightarrow P
, if either of the following equivalent conditions hold:

–

for every set
X
,
(\Phi^{+})_{X}
reflects the order;

–

\Phi_{+}\circ\Phi^{+}\simeq\id_{P}
.

Proposition 1.12
.

Every geometric inclusion (resp. surjection) of triposes
Q\longrightarrow P
induces a geometric inclusion (resp. surjection)
\Sets[Q]\longrightarrow\Sets[P]
.

Definition 1.13
.

Let
\mathsf{SubTrip}({P})
be the set of
subtriposes
of
P
, that is, triposes endowed with a geometric inclusions into
P
.
3
3

3

For practical reasons, we identify a subtripos with the inclusion itself.
Given two geometric inclusions
\Phi:Q\hookrightarrow P
and
\Psi:R\hookrightarrow P
, we write
\Phi\subseteq\Psi
if there exists a geometric morphism
\Theta:Q\longrightarrow R
such that
\Phi\simeq\Psi\circ\Theta
– meaning that
\Phi_{+}\simeq{}_{+}\circ\Theta_{+}
or equivalently
\Phi^{+}\simeq\Theta^{+}\circ{}^{+}
–, in which case
\Theta
is an inclusion itself. This relation obviously makes
\mathsf{SubTrip}({P})
into a preorder.

Two subtriposes
\Phi:Q\hookrightarrow P
and
\Psi:R\hookrightarrow P
are
equivalent
if they are isomorphic elements of
\mathsf{SubTrip}({P})
, that is, if both
\Phi\subseteq\Psi
and
\Psi\subseteq\Phi
hold; equivalently, this means that there exists an equivalence
\Theta:Q\longrightarrow R
such that
\Phi\simeq\Psi\circ\Theta
.

As it is known, subtoposes of a topos
\E
correspond up to equivalence to
local operators
in
\E
, that is, morphisms

such that, in the internal logic of
\E
:

i.

j(\operatorname{t})=\operatorname{t}
;

ii.

jj=j
;

iii.

j(a\land b)=j(a)\land j(b)
,

In a topos of the form
\Sets[P]
for a canonically presented tripos
P\coloneqq\Sigma^{-}
, such a morphism corresponds to an essentially unique transformation
\Phi_{j}:P\longrightarrow P
which is:

i.

cartesian;

ii.

inflationary
, that is,
\id_{P}\leq\Phi_{j}
;

iii.

idempotent
, that is,
\Phi_{j}\Phi_{j}\simeq\Phi_{j}
.

Such
\Phi_{j}
is called a

on
P
; conversely, every closure transformation on

determines a local operator on
\Sets[P]
.

These correspondences lead to the following result.

.

Let

(1)

Geometric inclusions into

\mathsf{SubTrip}({P})\simeq{\mathsf{ClTrans}({P})}^{\operatorname{op}}

(2)

\mathsf{SubTop}({\Sets[P]})\simeq\mathsf{SubTrip}({P})

Note then that the poset reflection of

2.1.
Arrow algebras

We now briefly review part of the theory of arrow algebras presented in
[
vdBB23
]
so as to fix notations and make some remarks.

An
arrow algebra

\A=(A,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S)
is the datum of a complete meet-semilattice
(A,\preccurlyeq)
, a binary operation
\rightarrow:A\times A\longrightarrow A
called
implication
which is monotone in the second component and antitone in the first component, and a specified subset
S\subseteq A
called
separator
which is upward closed, closed under modus ponens, and contains appropriate
combinators

\mathbf{k}
,
\mathbf{s}
,
\mathbf{a}
.

Although an arrow algebra
\A
is defined in terms of the
evidential order

\preccurlyeq
, there is another order that can be defined in terms of implications and separators, that is, the
logical
order:

a\vdash b\iff a\rightarrow b\in S

(A,\vdash)
is a Heyting prealgebra, with
\rightarrow
being the Heyting implication. Through the logical order, we can also recover the separator as
\Set{a\in A}{\top\vdash a}
: in hindsight, this characterizes the separator as what Pitts calls the set of
designated truth values
of the induced arrow tripos.

For every set
I
, the set
A^{I}
of functions
I\longrightarrow A
can be made into an arrow algebra
\A^{I}
by choosing pointwise order and implication, and with the separator:

\phi\vdash_{I}\psi\iff\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i\in I}\phi(i)\rightarrow\psi(i)\in S

Remark 2.1
.

In the following, we will make use of a form of reasoning internal to an arrow algebra
\A=(A,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S)
, which is given by a combination of
[
vdBB23
, Prop. 3.5]
and
[
vdBB23
, Lem. 3.6]
and which we will refer to as
intuitionistic reasoning
.

Suppose we want to prove that
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A}\psi(a_{i}/p_{i})\rightarrow\chi(a_{i}/p_{i})\in S
for some propositional formulas
\psi,\chi
built from propositional variables
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
using implications only. Then, we will look for a propositional formula
\phi
also built from the same propositional variables using implications only, such that

(1)

\phi\rightarrow\psi\rightarrow\chi
is an intuitionistic tautology,

(2)

and
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A}\phi(a_{i}/p_{i})\in S
.

If such a
\phi
can be found, then by
[
vdBB23
, Prop. 3.5]
and (1) it will follow that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A}\phi(a_{i}/p_{i})\rightarrow\psi(a_{i}/p_{i})\rightarrow\chi(a_{i}/p_{i})\in S

and hence, by
[
vdBB23
, Lem. 3.6]
and (2):

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A}\psi(a_{i}/p_{i})\rightarrow\chi(a_{i}/p_{i})\in S

2.2.
Frames

Every frame
\O(X)
can be canonically seen as an arrow algebra
5
5

5

In particular, compatible with joins.
by using its order and its Heyting implication as the arrow structure, and
\{\top\}
as the separator. Note then that the logical order coincides with the evidential order, since
x\rightarrow y\in S
if and only if
\top\leq x\rightarrow y
, which is equivalent to
x\leq y
.

Similarly, the logical order
\vdash_{I}
on
\O(X)^{I}
reduces to the pointwise order,
6
6

6

We will see in
5.15
how this property characterizes frames among arrow algebras up to equivalence of the induced triposes.
which makes so that the arrow tripos
P_{\O(X)}
coincides with the localic tripos induced by
\O(X)
, and hence
\mathsf{AT}({\O(X)})
is equivalent to the topos
\operatorname{Sh}(\O(X))
of sheaves over
\O(X)
.

2.3.
Partial combinatory algebras

A main example of arrow algebras arises from PCAs, building blocks of realizability toposes. In this paper, we will continue with the not-entirely-standard definitions and conventions about PCAs given in
[
vdBB23
]
, which closely follow those of
[
Zoe22
]
. This means that, with PCA, we will always mean Hofstra’s notion of a
filtered ordered partial combinatory algebra

[
Hof06
]
. This allows us for the highest level of generality considered in the literature for what concerns appropriate notions of morphisms and their connections with morphisms of realizability triposes and toposes, as we will see in the next sections.

Let
\mathbb{P}=(P,\leq,\cdot,P^{\#})
be a PCA and consider the poset
DP
of downward-closed subsets of
P
. Recall from
[
Zoe22
, Ex. 2.1.7]
that
DP
can be given an application operation by defining, for
\alpha,\beta\in DP
:

\alpha\cdot\beta\coloneqq{\downarrow}{\Set{xy}{x\in\alpha,\ y\in\beta}}

in case
{xy}{\downarrow}
for all
x\in\alpha
and
y\in\beta
. Then, as seen in
[
Zoe22
, Ex. 2.1.18]
, the family of downward-closed subsets containing an element from
P^{\#}
is a filter on this partial applicative poset:

\displaystyle(DP)^{\#}\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\Set{\alpha\in DP}{\exists a\in\alpha\cap P^{\#}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Set{\alpha\in DP}{\exists a\in P^{\#}:{\downarrow}{\{a\}}\subseteq\alpha}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Set{\alpha\in DP}{\exists\beta\in D(P^{\#}):\exists b\in\beta\land\beta\subseteq\alpha}

and two combinators
\mathbf{k},\mathbf{s}\in P^{\#}
for
\mathbb{P}
yield corresponding combinators
{\downarrow}{\{\mathbf{k}\}},{\downarrow}{\{\mathbf{s}\}}\in(DP)^{\#}
making
(DP,\subseteq,\cdot,(DP)^{\#})
into a PCA which we denote as
D\mathbb{P}
. In particular, for a
discrete
and
absolute
PCA
\mathbb{P}
, we denote
D\mathbb{P}
also as
\operatorname{Pow}({\mathbb P})
.

Defining, for
\alpha,\beta\in DP
:

\alpha\rightarrow\beta\coloneqq\Set{a\in P}{{a\cdot\alpha}{\downarrow}\mbox{ and }a\cdot\alpha\subseteq\beta}

and letting
S_{DP}=(DP)^{\#}
,
[
vdBB23
, Thm. 3.9]
shows how
(DP,\subseteq,\rightarrow,S_{DP})
is an arrow algebra which is compatible with joins. We denote this arrow algebra as
D\mathbb{P}
as well, and in particular as
\operatorname{Pow}({\mathbb P})
in the discrete and absolute case.

This definition makes so that the arrow tripos
P_{D\mathbb{P}}
coincides with the realizability tripos induced by
\mathbb{P}
, and hence
\mathsf{AT}({D\mathbb{P}})
coincides with the realizability topos
\mathsf{RT}({\mathbb{P}})
.

3.
A first category of arrow algebras

In this section, we introduce the first notion of a morphism between arrow algebras we will see in this paper, namely that of
implicative morphisms
, and we set up some first results which will be useful in the following.

By definition, an arrow algebra is a poset endowed with an implication operation and a specified subset: therefore, it would be natural to define morphisms of arrow algebras as monotone functions preserving implications (in some sense) and the specified subset. This intuition, obviously also valid for implicative algebras, is what leads to the definition of
applicative morphisms
in
[
SM17
]
, which partially inspires our definition. However, for reasons which will become clear in the following, we will not define our morphisms to be monotone with respect to the evidential order,
7
7

7

In a way, the evidential order is not the most important feature of an arrow algebra: arrow triposes, in fact, are defined by the Heyting prealgebras determined by the logical order.
but we will see how this will not actually be an issue. The downside is that, in general, we will have to impose a third condition – automatically satisfied in case of monotonicity – involving both implications and separators.

3.1.
Implicative morphisms

Let
\mathcal{A}=(A,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S_{A})
and
\mathcal{B}=(B,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S_{B})
be two arrow algebras.

Definition 3.1
.

An
implicative morphism

f:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}
is a function
f:A\longrightarrow B
satisfying:

i.

f(a)\in S_{B}
for every
a\in S_{A}
;

ii.

there exists an element
r\in S_{B}
such that
r\preccurlyeq f(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})
for all
{a,a^{\prime}\in A}
;

iii.

for every subset
X\subseteq A\times A
,

\mbox{if }\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}a\rightarrow a^{\prime}\in S_{A}\;\mbox{ then }\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B},

in which case we say that
f
is
realized
by
r\in S_{B}
.

An order ‘up to a realizer’ can be defined on implicative morphisms as follows. Given two implicative morphisms
f,f^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}
, we write
f\vdash f^{\prime}
if there exists an element
u\in S_{B}
such that
u\preccurlyeq f(a)\rightarrow f^{\prime}(a)
for every
a\in A
, in which case we say that
f\vdash f^{\prime}
is
realized
by
u
. In other words, this means that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a\in A}f(a)\rightarrow f^{\prime}(a)\in S_{B}

i.e.
f\vdash_{A}f^{\prime}
seeing
f
and
f^{\prime}
as elements of the arrow algebra
\B^{A}
, so in particular it is also equivalent to
f\phi\vdash_{I}f^{\prime}\phi
for every set
I
and every function
\phi:I\longrightarrow A
.

Remark 3.2
.

If
f
happens to be monotone with respect to the evidential order, then (iii) is a consequence of (i) and (ii).

Indeed, given any
X\subseteq A\times A
such that
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}a\rightarrow a^{\prime}\in S_{A}
:

–

by (ii) we have:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}f(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B};

–

as
f(\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits P)\preccurlyeq\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits f(P)
for every subset
P\subseteq A
by monotonicity, and by (i) and upward-closure of
S_{B}
:

f\left(\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}a\rightarrow a^{\prime}\right)\preccurlyeq\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}f(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\in S_{B},

from which
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(a,a^{\prime})\in X}f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B}
by
[
vdBB23
, Lem. 3.6]
.

Therefore, to prove that a monotone function is an implicative morphism, we will systematically omit to check condition (iii).

Remark 3.3
.

Applicative morphisms of
[
SM17
]
only satisfy condition (ii) for
a,a^{\prime}\in A
such that
a\vdash a^{\prime}
, while they are monotone by definition. The two notions are hence incomparable in general.

Proposition 3.4
.

Arrow algebras, implicative morphisms and their order form a preorder-enriched category
\ArrAlg
.

Proof.

First, let
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
and
g:\B\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
be implicative morphisms; let us show that
gf:A\longrightarrow C
satisfies the definition of an implicative morphism
\A\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
. Condition (i) and (iii) are clearly compositional; to show condition (ii), instead, note that by (ii) for
f
we know that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}f(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B}

from which, by (iii) for
g
:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}gf(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow g(f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime}))\in S_{C}

Moreover, by (ii) for
g
we know that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}g(f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime}))\rightarrow gf(a)\rightarrow gf(a^{\prime})\in S_{C}

from which, by intuitionistic reasoning:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}gf(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow gf(a)\rightarrow gf(a^{\prime})\in S_{C}

Then, for every arrow algebra
\A
, the identity function
\id_{A}
is an implicative morphism
\A\longrightarrow\A
, trivially realized by
\mathbf{i}\in S_{A}
since we know that:

\mathbf{i}\preccurlyeq\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}\in A}(a\rightarrow a^{\prime})\rightarrow a\rightarrow a^{\prime}

This makes
\ArrAlg
into a category. The fact that
\vdash
is a preorder on each homset
\ArrAlg(\A,\B)
follows immediately as it is the subpreorder of
(B^{A},\vdash_{A})
on implicative morphisms. Therefore, to conclude, we simply need to show that composition of implicative morphisms is order-preserving:

–

for
f,f^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}
and
g:\mathcal{B}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that
f\vdash f^{\prime}
; explicitly, this means that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a\in A}f(a)\rightarrow f^{\prime}(a)\in S_{B}

from which, by (iii) in

4.
Examples of implicative morphisms I

Let us now consider the two main classes of implicative morphisms, corresponding to two main examples of arrow algebras: those arising from frame homomorphisms, and those arising from morphisms of PCAs.

4.1.
Frames

As we know, every frame can be canonically seen as an arrow algebra by choosing
\{\top\}
as the separator. Then, any morphism of frames

, a (necessarily monotone) function which preserves finite meets and arbitrary joins, is also an implicative morphism. Indeed:

i.

f(\top)=\top
as

preserves finite meets;

ii.

for

y\land(y\rightarrow y^{\prime})\leq y^{\prime}

f(y\rightarrow y^{\prime})\leq f(y)\rightarrow f(y^{\prime})

\top\leq f(y\rightarrow y^{\prime})\rightarrow f(y)\rightarrow f(y^{\prime})
.

Remark 4.1
.

As emerges from the above reasoning, more generally we have that any function
f:\O(Y)\longrightarrow\O(X)
which preserves finite meets is an implicative morphism.

Recall moreover that
\Frm
is preorder-enriched with respect to the pointwise order: therefore, given two frame homomorphisms

While obviously faithful, this inclusion is far from being full. Indeed, consider the initial frame
\Omega
and let

be a frame such that

5.

We have finally arrived at the heart of this paper. In this section, we further the study of implicative morphisms and their relations with transformations of arrow triposes, lifting the association

. Note then that by condition (i) for
f
we have:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}f(\top_{I}(i))=\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}f(\top)=f(\top)\in S_{B}

meaning that
f^{I}(\top)\in S^{I}_{B}
.

ii.

Let
r\in S_{B}
be a realizer for
f
and let
\rho:I\longrightarrow B
be the constant function at
r
; as
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}\rho(i)=r\in S_{B}
, we know that
\rho\in S^{I}_{B}
.

Then, for all
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in A^{I}
:

r\preccurlyeq f(\phi(i)\rightarrow\phi^{\prime}(i))\rightarrow f\phi(i)\rightarrow f\phi^{\prime}(i)\quad\forall i\in I

i.e., since order and implications are defined pointwise in
\B^{I}
:

\rho\preccurlyeq f^{I}(\phi\rightarrow\phi^{\prime})\rightarrow f^{I}\phi\rightarrow f^{I}\phi^{\prime}

meaning that
\rho
realizes
f^{I}
.

iii.

Let
X\subseteq A^{I}\times A^{I}
be such that
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{(\phi,\psi)\in X}\phi\rightarrow\psi\in S_{A}^{I}
. For the sake of notation, assume
X=\Set{(\phi_{j},\psi_{j})}{j\in J}
; then, since order (hence meets) and implications are defined pointwise in
\A^{I}
, we have:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{j}\phi_{j}(i)\rightarrow\psi_{j}(i)\in S_{A}

from which, by (iii) for
f
:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{i}\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{j}f\phi_{j}(i)\rightarrow f\psi_{j}(i)\in S_{A}

meaning that
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{j}f^{I}\phi_{j}\rightarrow f^{I}\psi_{j}\in S_{A}^{I}
.

∎

Fix now an implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
and define the transformation:

\Phi_{f}^{+}:P_{\A}\longrightarrow P_{\B}\qquad(\Phi_{f}^{+})_{I}(\phi)\coloneqq f^{I}\phi=f\circ\phi

Indeed, monotonicity of each component
(\Phi_{f}^{+})_{I}:P_{\A}(I)\longrightarrow P_{\B}(I)
precisely corresponds to condition (iii) in
3.1
, while naturality is obvious.

Let us now show that, for every set
I
,
(\Phi_{f}^{+})_{I}:P_{\A}(I)\longrightarrow P_{\B}(I)
preserves finite meets up to isomorphism. As
f^{I}(\top_{I})\in S_{B}^{I}
we know that
f^{I}(\top_{I})\dashv\vdash_{I}\top_{I}
, so we only have to show that for all
\phi,\psi\in A^{I}
:

f^{I}(\phi\land\psi)\dashv\vdash_{I}f^{I}\phi\land f^{I}\psi

where
f^{I}\phi\land f^{I}\psi
is the meet of
f^{I}\phi
and
f^{I}\psi
in
P_{\B}(I)
which, as we already recalled, can be assumed to be defined pointwise.

Of course
f^{I}(\phi\land\psi)\vdash_{I}f^{I}\phi\land f^{I}\psi
follows simply by monotonicity of
f^{I}
with respect to the logical order; on the other hand,
f^{I}\phi\land f^{I}\psi\vdash_{I}f^{I}(\phi\land\psi)
is ensured by the following lemma applied to
f^{I}:\A^{I}\longrightarrow\B^{I}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
be an implicative morphism. Then:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}(f(a)\land f(b))\rightarrow f(a\land b)\in S_{B}

Proof.

First, note that:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}a\rightarrow b\rightarrow(a\land b)\in S_{A}

from which, by (iii) in
3.1
:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}f(a)\rightarrow f(b\rightarrow(a\land b))\in S_{B}

Moreover, by (ii):

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}f(b\rightarrow(a\land b))\rightarrow f(b)\rightarrow f(a\land b)\in S_{B}

so by intuitionistic reasoning we conclude:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}f(a)\rightarrow f(b)\rightarrow f(a\land b)\in S_{B}

which means:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,b\in A}(f(a)\land f(b))\rightarrow f(a\land b)\in S_{B}

∎

Summing up, we have shown the following.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
be an implicative morphism. Then:

\Phi_{f}^{+}:P_{\A}\longrightarrow P_{\B}\qquad(\Phi_{f}^{+})_{I}(\phi)\coloneqq f\circ\phi

is a cartesian transformation of triposes.

As promised above, we can also prove the converse: up to isomorphism, every cartesian transformation of arrow triposes is induced in the sense of the previous proposition by an implicative morphism which is unique up to isomorphism.

Proposition 5.4
.

The association
f\mapsto\Phi_{f}^{+}
determines a 2-fully faithful 2-functor
\ArrAlg\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})
.

Explicitly, this means that for all arrow algebras
\A
and
\B
there is an equivalence of preorder categories:

{{\ArrAlg}({\A},{\B})}\simeq{{\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})}({P_{\A}},{P_{\B}})}

Proof.

By the previous discussion, we have a functor
\ArrAlg\longrightarrow\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})
; 2-functoriality amounts to showing that, given any two implicative morphisms
f,f^{\prime}:\A\longrightarrow\B
such that
f\vdash f^{\prime}
, then
\Phi^{+}_{f}\leq\Phi^{+}_{f^{\prime}}
. By definition,
f\vdash f^{\prime}
means that for every set
I
and every
\phi:I\longrightarrow A
then
f\phi\vdash_{I}f^{\prime}\phi
holds, i.e.
(\Phi^{+}_{f})_{I}(\phi)\vdash_{I}(\Phi^{+}_{f^{\prime}})_{I}(\phi)
holds in
P_{\B}(I)
, which means that
\Phi^{+}_{f}\leq\Phi^{+}_{f^{\prime}}
. Note then that the converse also holds: if
\Phi_{f}^{+}\leq\Phi_{f^{\prime}}^{+}
, then in particular we have that
(\Phi^{+}_{f})_{A}(\id_{A})\vdash_{A}(\Phi^{+}_{f^{\prime}})_{A}(\id_{A})
, which means that
f\vdash f^{\prime}
.

Let now
\Phi^{+}:P_{\A}\longrightarrow P_{\B}
be any cartesian transformation of arrow triposes. Recall that, up to isomorphism,
\Phi^{+}
is given by postcomposition with the function:

f\coloneqq(\Phi^{+})_{A}(\id_{A}):A\longrightarrow B

Let us now verify that
f
satisfies the three conditions
10
10

10

If we assumed implicative morphisms to be monotone, we would not be able to prove that
f
is one.
in
3.1
.

i.

To show condition (i), recall that we can equivalently prove that
f(\top)\in S_{B}
. By preservation of finite meets, we know that
(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\top_{I})\dashv\vdash_{I}\top_{I}
, which for
I\coloneqq 1
means that
f(\top)\dashv\vdash\top
, i.e.
f(\top)\in S_{B}
.

ii.

Let
I\coloneqq A\times A
; recall that condition (ii) can be rewritten as:

f(\pi_{1}\rightarrow\pi_{2})\vdash_{I}f\pi_{1}\rightarrow f\pi_{2}

where
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:I\longrightarrow A
are the two projections. In terms of
\Phi^{+}
, this means that we have to show:

(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{1}\rightarrow\pi_{2})\vdash_{I}(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{1})\rightarrow(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{2})

Through the Heyting adjunction in
P_{\B}(I)
, the previous is equivalent to:

(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{1}\rightarrow\pi_{2})\land(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{1})\vdash_{I}(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{2})

i.e., by preservation of finite meets:

(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{1}\rightarrow\pi_{2}\land\pi_{1})\vdash_{I}(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\pi_{2})

which is ensured by monotonicity since
\pi_{1}\rightarrow\pi_{2}\land\pi_{1}\vdash_{I}\pi_{2}
.

iii.

Condition (iii) precisely corresponds to the monotonicity of each component
(\Phi^{+})_{I}
.

Therefore, the association
\Phi^{+}\mapsto(\Phi^{+})_{A}(\id_{A})
realizes the desired inverse equivalence since obviously
(\Phi^{+}_{f})_{A}(\id_{A})=f
for every implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

In
[
Miq20b
]
it is shown that every canonically-presented (
\Sets
-)tripos is equivalent to an implicative one, and hence to an arrow one.

Therefore, assuming the Axiom of Choice, the 2-functor
\ArrAlg\longrightarrow\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{cart}}({\Sets})
is actually a 2-equivalence of 2-categories: in fact, under the Axiom of Choice, every tripos is equivalent to a canonically presented one (cfr.
[
HJP80
, Prop. 1.9]
), meaning that the above 2-functor is essentially surjective on objects; moreover, under the Axiom of Choice, a 2-functor is a 2-equivalence if and only if it is 2-fully faithful and essentially surjective on objects, see e.g.
[
JY20
, Thm. 7.4.1]
.

5.2.
Geometric morphisms of arrow triposes

Let us now move to geometric morphisms: as we will see in a moment, the existence of a right adjoint at the level of transformations of triposes can exactly be characterized by the existence of a right adjoint in
\ArrAlg
.

Definition 5.6
.

An implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
is
computationally dense
11
11

11

The name is obviously taken from the theory of PCAs, and it is also used in
[
SM17
]
for applicative morphisms.
if it admits a right adjoint in
\ArrAlg
, that is, if there exists an implicative morphism
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
such that
fh\vdash\id_{B}
and
\id_{A}\vdash hf
.

For every arrow algebra
\A
, the identity
\id_{A}:\A\longrightarrow\A
is computationally dense, as it is trivially right adjoint to itself. As the existence of a right adjoint in the preorder-enriched sense is clearly compositional, we can define the wide subcategory
\ArrAlgcd
of
\ArrAlg
on computationally dense morphisms.

Fix now a computationally dense implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
with right adjoint
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
and consider the cartesian transformation induced by
h
as in
5.3
:

\Phi_{+}:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}\qquad(\Phi_{+})_{I}(\phi)\coloneqq h\circ\phi

Lemma 5.7
.

For every set
I
,
(\Phi_{+})_{I}:P_{\B}(I)\longrightarrow P_{\A}(I)
is right adjoint to the map
(\Phi_{f}^{+})_{I}:\psi\mapsto f\circ\psi
.

Proof.

By the universal property of the counit of the adjunction, it suffices to show that for every
\phi:I\longrightarrow B
:

(1)

fh\phi\vdash_{I}\phi
in
P_{\B}(I)
;

(2)

for every
\psi:I\longrightarrow A
such that
f\psi\vdash_{I}\phi
in
P_{\B}(I)
, then
\psi\vdash_{I}h\phi
in
P_{\A}(I)
.

(1) clearly follows since
h
is right adjoint to
f
. To show (2), instead, suppose
f\psi\vdash_{I}\phi
; then,
hf\psi\vdash_{I}h\phi
as
h
is an implicative morphism, and hence
\psi\vdash_{I}h\psi
since
\id_{A}\vdash_{A}hf
.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

The 2-functor of

.

Explicitly, this means that for all arrow algebras

{{\ArrAlgcd}({\A},{\B})}\simeq{{\mathsf{Trip}_{\mathsf{geom}}({\Sets})}({P_{\A}},{P_{\B}})}

\Phi_{f}^{+}\leq\Phi_{f^{\prime}}^{+}
.

\Phi^{+}:P_{\A}\longrightarrow P_{\B}

\Phi_{+}:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}
. Recall that, up to isomorphism,
\Phi^{+}

5.3.
Equivalences of arrow triposes

Finally, let us characterize equivalences of arrow triposes on the level of arrow algebras.

With usual 2-categorical notation, we say that an implicative morphism

Lemma 5.11

Let

be an equivalence of arrow algebras.

Then,

is computationally dense, and the induced geometric morphism of triposes

Proof.

Let

be a quasi-inverse of

in
\ArrAlg

(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\psi)=f\circ\psi\qquad(\Phi_{+})_{I}(\phi)=g\circ\phi

In particular,
\Phi^{+}\Phi_{+}
and
\Phi_{+}\Phi^{+}
are isomorphic to identities since

and
gf\dashv\vdash\id_{A}
, meaning that
\Phi
is an equivalence.
∎

By the previous results, we can also easily address the converse.

6.1.

First, recall that frame homomorphisms are implicative morphisms, seeing frames as arrow algebras in the canonical way. More generally, as noted in

Proposition 6.1

Let

is a computationally dense implicative morphism.

Proof.

Let

is an implicative morphism

in
\ArrAlg

7.1.
Inclusions and surjections

Recall that a geometric morphism of arrow triposes
\Phi:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}
is an
inclusion
if
(\Phi_{+})_{I}
reflects the order for every set
I
, or equivalently if
(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\Phi_{+})_{I}(\phi)\dashv\vdash_{I}\phi
for every set
I
and every
\phi:I\longrightarrow B
. Dually,
\Phi
is a
surjection
if
(\Phi^{+})_{I}
reflects the order for every set
I
, or equivalently if
(\Phi_{+})_{I}(\Phi^{+})_{I}(\phi)\dashv\vdash_{I}\phi
for every set
I
and every
\phi:I\longrightarrow A
.

Recall moreover that, in any preorder-enriched category
\C
:

–

an arrow
f:A\longrightarrow B
is a
lax epimorphism
if, for every
C\in\C
, the map

-\circ f:\C(B,C)\longrightarrow\C(A,C)

is fully-faithful as a functor between preorder categories, which explicitly means that
p\leq q
for all
p,q:B\longrightarrow C
such that
pf\leq qf
;

–

an arrow
f:A\longrightarrow B
is a
lax monomorphism
if, for every
C\in\C
, the map

f\circ-:\C(C,A)\longrightarrow\C(C,B)

is fully-faithful as a functor between preorder categories, which explicitly means that
p\leq q
for all
p,q:C\longrightarrow A
such that
fp\leq fq
.

Specializing to
\ArrAlg
, we can then give the following definition.

Definition 7.1
.

A computationally dense implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
is an
implicative surjection
(resp.
implicative injection
) if it is a lax epimorphism (resp. lax monomorphism) in
\ArrAlg
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let

be a computationally dense implicative morphism with right adjoint

and let
\Phi:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}
be the induced geometric morphism of arrow triposes. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\Phi
is an inclusion;

(2)

fh\dashv\vdash_{B}\id_{B}
;

(3)

f
is an implicative surjection.

Dually, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\Phi
is a surjection;

(2)

hf\dashv\vdash_{A}\id_{A}
;

(3)

f
is an implicative injection.

Proof.

For (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2), recall that the inverse image
\Phi^{+}
is given by postcomposition with
f
, and the direct image
\Phi_{+}
is given by postcomposition with
h
: therefore,
\Phi
is an inclusion if and only if
fh\phi\dashv\vdash_{I}\phi
for every set

and every
\phi\in P_{\B}(I)
, which is equivalent to
fh\dashv\vdash_{B}\id_{B}
.

For (2)
\Rightarrow
(3), suppose

are such that

. Then,

, and hence
p\vdash q
.

For (3)

; conversely, to show that

, which is ensured by
\id_{A}\vdash hf
.

Combining

7.3.
A factorization theorem

As it is known, every geometric morphism of toposes can be factored as a geometric surjection followed by a geometric inclusion. Generalizing locale theory, let us recover the same result on the level of arrow algebras; in doing so, we will also make the correspondence between subtriposes and nuclei more explicit.

Let
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
be a computationally dense implicative morphism with right adjoint
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
; by
3.8
, up to isomorphism we can assume both
f
and
h
to be monotone with respect to the evidential order. First, observe the following.

Lemma 7.11
.

hf:\A\longrightarrow\A
is a nucleus on
\A
.

Proof.

Let us verify that
hf
satisfies the three conditions defining nuclei.

i.

hf
is monotone by composition.

ii.

Clearly
\id_{A}\vdash hf
as
h
is right adjoint to
f
.

iii.

Let
I\coloneqq A\times A
; note that condition (iii) can be rewritten as:

\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2}\vdash_{I}hf\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2}

where
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:I\longrightarrow A
are the obvious projections. Through the Heyting adjunction in
P_{\A}(I)
, this is equivalent to:

(\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2})\land hf\pi_{1}\vdash_{I}hf\pi_{2}

and hence, since
h\circ-
is right adjoint to
f\circ-
, which preserves finite meets, to:

f(\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2})\land fhf\pi_{1}\vdash_{I}f\pi_{2}

Therefore, since
fhf\pi_{1}\vdash_{I}f\pi_{1}
as
fh\vdash\id_{B}
, it suffices to show:

f(\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2})\land f\pi_{1}\vdash_{I}f\pi_{2}

i.e., again through the Heyting adjunction in
P_{\B}(I)
:

f(\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2})\vdash_{I}f\pi_{1}\rightarrow f\pi_{2}

which in turn follows since, by (ii) in
3.1
:

f(\pi_{1}\rightarrow hf\pi_{2})\vdash_{I}f\pi_{1}\rightarrow fhf\pi_{2}

and again
fhf\pi_{2}\vdash_{I}f\pi_{2}
.

∎

A natural question is then to relate
f
to
j\coloneqq hf
, and in particular the geometric morphism
\Phi_{f}:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}
induced by
f
with the inclusion
\Phi_{j}:P_{j}\hookrightarrow P_{\A}
induced by
j
. To this aim, recall here that
fh\vdash\id_{B}
and
\id_{A}\vdash hf
imply
fhf\dashv\vdash f
and
hfh\dashv\vdash h
.

Lemma 7.12
.

f
is an implicative injection
\A_{j}\longrightarrow\B
, with
h
as a right adjoint.

Proof.

Let us start by showing that
f
is an implicative morphism
\A_{j}\longrightarrow\B
.

i.

Let
a\in S_{j}
; by definition,
hf(a)\in S_{A}
, so
fhf(a)\in S_{B}
, and hence
f(a)\in S_{B}
since
fh\vdash\id_{B}
.

ii.

Condition (ii) explicitly reads as:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}f(a\rightarrow ja^{\prime})\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B}

which follows by intuitionistic reasoning from:

\displaystyle\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}f(a\rightarrow ja^{\prime})\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow fj(a^{\prime})\in S_{B}

\displaystyle\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a,a^{\prime}}(f(a)\rightarrow fhf(a^{\prime}))\rightarrow f(a)\rightarrow f(a^{\prime})\in S_{B}

where the latter follows since
fhf\vdash f
.

Note now that
h:B\longrightarrow A
is an implicative morphism
\B\longrightarrow\A_{j}
since it is an implicative morphism
\B\longrightarrow\A
and
\id_{A}
is an implicative morphism
\A\longrightarrow\A_{j}
. Then, we have that

is right adjoint to

:

–

clearly

;

–

on the other hand,
\id_{A}\vdash^{j}hf
explicitly reads as
\id_{A}\vdash_{A}hfhf
, which follows from
\id_{A}\vdash_{A}hf
.

Moreover, we also have that

as it explicitly reads as

.

that

factors through

8.
Arrow algebras for modified realizability

In this section, we will apply the theoretical framework developed above to lift the study of
modified realizability
to the level of arrow algebras. Modified realizability is a variant of Kleene’s number realizability introduced by Kreisel in 1959
[
Kre59
]
. On the semantical side, a topos for Kreisel’s modified realizability was first defined by Greyson in 1981
[
Gra81
]
; see also
[
vO97
,
Bv02
,
Joh17
]
.

The key feature of modified realizability lies in separating between a set of
potential realizers
and a subset thereof of
actual realizers
. On the level of triposes, this amounts to shifting from the ordinary realizability tripos
P_{\AA}
over a (traditionally, discrete and absolute) PCA
\AA
to a tripos whose predicates on a set
I
are functions from
I
to the set:

\Set{(\alpha,\beta)\in DA\times DA}{\alpha\subseteq\beta}

which are preordered by:

\phi\vdash_{I}\psi\iff\bigcapop\displaylimits_{i\in I}(\phi_{1}(i)\rightarrow\psi_{1}(i))\cap(\phi_{2}(i)\rightarrow\psi_{2}(i))\in(DA)^{\#}

where we denote with
\phi_{1}(i),\phi_{2}(i)
the two components of
\phi(i)
. This idea is what led, in
[
vdBB23
]
, to the definition of the
Sierpiński construction
on arrow algebras, which we will describe below. To do this, and for what follows, we will consider some
ad hoc
notions of arrow algebras which still encompass all the relevant cases and many others. This does not mean that we have counterexamples showing how the presented results may fail for more general classes of arrow algebras, but only that minimal assumptions suffice to ensure the desired properties.

Definition 8.1
.

An arrow algebra
\A=(A,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S)
is
binary implicative
if the equality:

\textstyle a\rightarrow(b\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits c)=a\rightarrow b\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits a\rightarrow c

holds for all
a,b,c\in A
, and it is
modifiable
if moreover the equality:

\bot\rightarrow a=\top

holds for every
a\in A
.

We denote with
\ArrAlgbi
and
\ArrAlgmod
the full subcategories of
\ArrAlg
on binary implicative and modifiable arrow algebras, respectively.

Example 8.2
.

Every frame, seen as an arrow algebra in the canonical way, is modifiable.

Example 8.3
.

For every PCA
\mathbb{A}
,
D\AA
is modifiable; in particular,
\operatorname{PER}\AA
(cfr.
[
vdBB23
, Thm. 3.10]
) is modifiable.

8.1.
The Sierpiński construction

Recall by
[
vdBB23
, Prop. 7.2]
that, starting from any binary implicative arrow algebra
\A=(A,\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,S)
, we can define a new arrow algebra
{\A}^{\rightarrow}=({A}^{\rightarrow},\preccurlyeq,\rightarrow,{S}^{\rightarrow})
, also binary implicative, by letting:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{x\in{A}^{\rightarrow}}\top\rightarrow Mf((cx)_{1})\in S_{B}

where
M:\ArrAlg\longrightarrow\ArrAlg
is the monotonization pseudofunctor of
3.9
, and hence since
Mf\dashv\vdash f
:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{x\in{A}^{\rightarrow}}\top\rightarrow f(((\bot,\top)\lor(x_{0},x_{1}))_{1})\in S_{B}

Note now that, in any arrow algebra of the form
{\A}^{\rightarrow}
, the logical join
a\lor a^{\prime}
has
a_{1}\lor a_{1}^{\prime}
as its second component. This can be seen using the explicit description of logical joins given in
[
vdBB23
, Prop. 5.1]
together with
5.13
, recalling also that (evidential) meets and implications in
{\A}^{\rightarrow}
are computed pointwise on the second component. Hence, the previous explicitly reads as:

\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{x\in{A}^{\rightarrow}}\top\rightarrow f(\top\lor x_{1})\in S_{B}

which follows from
\mathop{\vphantom{\sumop\displaylimits}\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{557066}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{655368}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{458758}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}{\vbox{\hbox{\resizebox{327684}{}{$\curlywedge$}}}}}\displaylimits_{a\in A}\top\rightarrow(\top\lor a)\in S_{A}
by the properties of implicative morphisms.
∎

Theorem 8.15
.

For every implicative morphism
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
in
\ArrAlgmod
, let
f^{m}:\A^{m}\longrightarrow\B^{m}
be the composite:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\B}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\B^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f}^{\rightarrow}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}}

where
{f}^{\rightarrow}:{\A}^{\rightarrow}\longrightarrow{\B}^{\rightarrow}
is the implicative morphism defined in
8.6
.

Then,
(-)^{m}
is a pseudofunctor
\ArrAlgmod\longrightarrow\ArrAlg
.

Moreover, if
f
is computationally dense with right adjoint
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
, then
f^{m}
is computationally dense as well, and a right adjoint is given by
h^{m}:\B^{m}\longrightarrow\A^{m}
. Furthermore, the square:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\B^{m}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A^{m}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\B}^{\rightarrow}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\A}^{\rightarrow}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{h^{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{h}^{\rightarrow}}}

commutes up to isomorphism.

Proof.

First, let us show that
(-)^{m}
preserves identities and compositions up to isomorphism.

–

By definition,
\id_{A}^{m}:\A^{m}\longrightarrow\A^{m}
is given by the composite:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A^{m}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\id_{A}}^{\rightarrow}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}}

which means that
\id_{A}^{m}=c:\A^{m}\longrightarrow\A^{m}
, and obviously
c\dashv\vdash^{c}\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}
.

–

By definition, for
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
and
g:\B\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
,
(gf)^{m}
is given by the composite:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}^{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(gf)}^{\rightarrow}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{{C}^{\rightarrow}}}

where of course
{(gf)}^{\rightarrow}\dashv\vdash{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}
, whereas
g^{m}f^{m}
is given by the composite:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\A^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\B}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\B^{m}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\B}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}}^{\rightarrow}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathcal{C}^{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{f}^{\rightarrow}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{g}^{\rightarrow}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id_{{C}^{\rightarrow}}}

which means that we need to show that
{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c\dashv\vdash^{c}{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}c
.

On one hand, using the fact that
\id\vdash c
both for
c\in\mathsf{N}({\B})
and
c\in\mathsf{N}({\mathcal{C}})
:

{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash c{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c

i.e.
{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash^{c}{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c
.

On the other, by the previous lemma we know that
c{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash{f}^{\rightarrow}c
, which implies
{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}c
by the properties of
{g}^{\rightarrow}
, and hence
{g}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash c{g}^{\rightarrow}{f}^{\rightarrow}c
since
\id_{{C}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash c
.

The pseudofunctoriality of
f\mapsto{f}^{\rightarrow}
then yields the pseudofunctoriality of
f\mapsto f^{m}
.

Suppose now
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
is right adjoint to
f
; let us show that
{h}^{\rightarrow}c
is right adjoint to
{f}^{\rightarrow}c:\A^{m}\longrightarrow\B^{m}
.

–

On one hand,
{f}^{\rightarrow}c{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}^{c}\id_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}
explicitly reads as
{f}^{\rightarrow}c{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}c
. By
8.6
, we know that
{h}^{\rightarrow}
is right adjoint to
{f}^{\rightarrow}
, so the previous is equivalent to
c{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}{h}^{\rightarrow}c
, which is ensured by the previous lemma.

–

On the other,
\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash^{c}_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}{h}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c
explicitly reads as
\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}c{h}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c
. As
\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash c
, this is ensured if
\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}{h}^{\rightarrow}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c
. This is again equivalent to
{f}^{\rightarrow}\vdash_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}c{f}^{\rightarrow}c
as
{h}^{\rightarrow}
is right adjoint to
{f}^{\rightarrow}
, which follows since
\id\vdash c
both for
c\in\mathsf{N}({{\A}^{\rightarrow}})
and
c\in\mathsf{N}({{\B}^{\rightarrow}})
.

Finally, to show that the square above commutes up to isomorphism, we need to show that
ch^{m}\dashv\vdash{h}^{\rightarrow}c
as morphisms
\B^{m}\longrightarrow{\A}^{\rightarrow}
. On one hand,
{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash ch^{m}
explicitly means
{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}c{h}^{\rightarrow}c
, which follows simply from
\id_{{A}^{\rightarrow}}\vdash c
. On the other,
ch^{m}\vdash{h}^{\rightarrow}c
explicitly means
c{h}^{\rightarrow}c\vdash_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}{h}^{\rightarrow}c
, which is again ensured by the previous lemma.
∎

Corollary 8.16
.

Let
\A
and
\B
be modifiable arrow algebras. Then, every geometric morphism
\Phi:P_{\B}\longrightarrow P_{\A}
induces a geometric morphism
\Phi^{m}:M_{\B}\longrightarrow M_{\A}
such that the diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M_{\B}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M_{\A}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{P_{{\B}^{\rightarrow}}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{P_{{\A}^{\rightarrow}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Phi^{m}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Phi}^{\rightarrow}}

is a pullback square of triposes and geometric morphisms.

In particular, the induced diagram of toposes and geometric morphisms:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{AT}({\B^{m}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{AT}({\A^{m}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{AT}({{\B}^{\rightarrow}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{AT}({{\A}^{\rightarrow}})}}

is a pullback square.

Proof.

The fact that the square commutes follows directly by the previous proposition. To show that it is a pullback, instead, recall from
[
Joh17
]
that, given a closed nucleus
kx\coloneqq x+u
on
{\A}^{\rightarrow}
, the pullback of the closed subtripos
P_{{\A}^{\rightarrow}_{k}}\hookrightarrow P_{{\A}^{\rightarrow}}
along
{\Phi}^{\rightarrow}
is the closed subtripos of
P_{{\B}^{\rightarrow}}
determined by the nucleus
k^{\prime}y\coloneqq y+({\Phi}^{\rightarrow})^{+}_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}(u)
. Therefore, the square above is a pullback if and only if
({\Phi}^{\rightarrow})^{+}_{{B}^{\rightarrow}}(\bot,\top)\dashv\vdash(\bot,\top)
in
{\B}^{\rightarrow}
.

To prove this, let
f:\A\longrightarrow\B
be an implicative morphism with right adjoint
h:\B\longrightarrow\A
inducing
\Phi
, so that
{\Phi}^{\rightarrow}
is induced by
{f}^{\rightarrow}
with right adjoint
{h}^{\rightarrow}
as in
8.6
; then, we need to show that
{f}^{\rightarrow}(\bot,\top)\dashv\vdash(\bot,\top)
in
{\B}^{\rightarrow}
. On one hand, by modifiability of
\B
,
(\bot,\top)\vdash{f}^{\rightarrow}(\bot,\top)
reduces simply to
\top\vdash f(\top)
, which is true as
f
is an implicative morphism. On the other,
{f}^{\rightarrow}(\bot,\top)\vdash(\bot,\top)
is equivalent to
(\bot,\top)\vdash{h}^{\rightarrow}(\bot,\top)
, which is true again as
\A
is modifiable and
h
is an implicative morphism.
∎
