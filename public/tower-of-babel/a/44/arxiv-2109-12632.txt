Weighted quadrature for hierarchical B-splines
Carlotta Giannelli, Tadej Kanduč, Massimiliano Martinelli, Giancarlo Sangalli, Mattia Tani

Abstract
We present weighted quadrature for hierarchical B-splines to address the fast formation of system matrices arising from adaptive isogeometric Galerkin methods with suitably graded hierarchical meshes. By exploiting a local tensor-product structure, we extend the construction of weighted rules from the tensor-product to the hierarchical spline setting. The proposed algorithm has a computational cost proportional to the number of degrees of freedom and advantageous properties with increasing spline degree. To illustrate the performance of the method and confirm the theoretical estimates, a selection of 2D and 3D numerical tests is provided.

Introduction

Local and adaptive mesh refinement methods in isogeometric analysis have gained a notable attention in the last years and their mathematical theory has recently been established, see
[
1

and references therein. One of the more prominent tool in this context is provided by hierarchical B-spline constructions

A local refinement step is governed by simple conditions that activate/deactivate basis functions from hierarchically nested sequence of spline spaces. The use of the hierarchical approach in isogeometric analysis was originally proposed in
[
2
]
and subsequently investigated in different directions, which range from the theory of adaptive methods
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
to engineering applications, see e.g.,
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
and references therein.

The efficient formation of matrices in isogeometric Galerkin methods is a topic of active research. In this paper we focus on the weighted quadrature (WQ) approach, introduced in
[
11
]
. Other recent results and methods in this area are integration by interpolation and look-up
[
12
,
13
]
, multiscale quadrature
[
14
]
, sum factorization
[
15
,
16
]
, the surrogate matrix method
[
17
]
, reduced integration at superconvergent points
[
18
]
and, beyond quadrature, the use of low-rank approximation
[
19
]
or GPUs
[
20
]
.

The aim of WQ is to reduce the number of
quadrature points that are needed to accurately compute integrals involving products of B-spline basis
functions. In combination with sum-factorization and other
implementational techniques, it reduces significantly the cost of
formation of isogeometric matrices.
The idea of WQ is that the test function plays the role of weight function in the
integration, and therefore the quadrature weights depends on the test function.
The advantage of this construction is that the number of exactness conditions to be
imposed is less than for Gauss quadrature, generalized Gauss
quadrature

[
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
or
reduced quadrature
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
.
Therefore, WQ requires less quadrature points, which mildly
depend on the spline degree.

In this paper we extend WQ to hierarchical B-splines with maximum regularity. Since the construction of the hierarchical basis is simply based on a suitable selection of standard B-splines at different levels of details, we can define hierarchical WQ as a linear combination of standard WQ on
different tensor-product levels.
The proposed algorithm has a computational cost proportional to the number of degrees of freedom and advantageous properties with increasing spline degree. To illustrate the performance of the method and confirm the theoretical estimates, a selection of 2D and 3D numerical tests is provided. For the sake of simplicity, we discuss the case of the mass matrix and
L^{2}
-projection. However, dealing with other matrices and with PDE problems is conceptually the same, see
[
11
]
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Preliminaries on hierarchical B-splines and weighted quadrature are recalled in Section
2

3

5

6

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Hierarchical B-splines

We consider a nested sequence of
L+1
multivariate tensor-product spline spaces
V^{\ell}
, for
\ell=0,\ldots,L,
of fixed degree
p
in any coordinate direction defined on a bounded closed hyper-rectangle
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By focusing on dyadic mesh refinement, we assume the spline spaces defined on a sequence of suitably refined knot vectors so that

V^{\ell}\subset V^{\ell+1}
, for
\ell=0,\ldots,L-1.
It should be noted however that the hierarchical B-spline model can be considered also in connection with more general (non-uniform) mesh refinement rules, where each mesh element is subdivided in an arbitrary number of children elements. Moreover, not only
h
-refinement but also
p
-refinement can be combined with the construction of the spline hierarchy as long as the spaces remain nested between each pair
(\ell,\ell+1)
of consecutive levels, for
\ell=0,\ldots,L-1
.
The considered choice is dyadic (uniform) refinement and fixed spline degree at all levels, which is the standard setting for adaptive isogeometric methods, based on hierarchical B-splines, and a suitable compromise between accuracy and efficiency for related application algorithms. Note that the design and development of fast assembly and efficient numerical integration rules tailored on hierarchical B-spline constructions are key ingredients for the subsequent development of more flexible adaptive approximation schemes.

In direction
k
of the domain
\Omega
, the level
\ell
basis
{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}_{k}
consists of
N^{\ell}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}
univariate B-splines
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
,

{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}_{k}=\bigl\{{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}\;:\;i_{k}=1,2,\dots,N^{\ell}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}\bigr\}.

The multivariate spline space
V^{\ell}
on
\Omega
can be defined as the span of the tensor-product B-spline basis functions
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
,

{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}:=\bigl\{b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}:=\prod_{k=1}^{d}{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}\;:\;{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}_{k}\bigl\}

with respect to the index set

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{i}:=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\;:\;1\leq i_{k}\leq N^{\ell}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}},\,k=1,\dots,d\bigr\}.

We denote the rectilinear mesh grid of level
\ell
by
{\cal M}^{\ell}
.
The dimension of
V^{\ell}
is then simply given by

\displaystyle N_{{\mathcal{B}}}^{\ell}:=\dim\bigl({\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}\bigr)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}\dim\bigl({\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}_{k}\bigr)=\prod_{k=1}^{d}N^{\ell}_{{\mathcal{B}}_{k}}\;.

To localize the refinement regions at different hierarchical levels, we also consider a nested sequence of closed subsets of
\Omega^{0}:=\Omega
given by

\displaystyle\Omega^{0}\supseteq\Omega^{1}\supseteq\ldots\supseteq\Omega^{L+1}=\emptyset.

The hierarchical mesh
{\cal M}
collects the grid elements
M\in\cal M^{\ell}
, which are not included in any refined region
\Omega^{m}
of higher level
m>\ell
,

\displaystyle{\cal M}:=\left\{M\in{\cal M}^{\ell}\;:\;M\subseteq\Omega^{\ell},M\not\subseteq\Omega^{\ell+1},\,\ell=0,\ldots,L\right\}.

We define the hierarchical B-spline basis
[
2
]
with respect to the hierarchical mesh
{\cal M}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}({\cal M}):=\left\{b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}\;:\;\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}},\,\ell=0,\ldots,L\right\},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{B}}\;:\;{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\subseteq\Omega^{\ell},{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\not\subseteq\Omega^{\ell+1}\right\}.

The cardinality is denoted by
N_{\mathcal{H}}
.
Each basis function
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\mathcal{H}
is uniquely identified by its level
\ell
and by the
multi-index
\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}
. Hence we can define the set of basis identifiers

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}:=\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{L}\{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\;:\;\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{H}}\}\;.

(1)

Hierarchical B-splines are non-negative, linear independent, and allow localized mesh refinement by suitably selecting basis functions with a varying level of resolution.

In order to limit the interaction between B-splines introduced at very different levels of the spline hierarchy, we consider
admissible
meshes. A hierarchical mesh
{\cal M}
is admissible of class
r
, with
2\leq r<L+1
, if the hierarchical B-splines taking non-zero values on any element
Q\in{\cal M}
belong to at most
r
successive levels. We refer to
[
5
,
1
]
for more details concerning admissible meshes and their properties, and to
[
28
]
for the presentation of the refinement algorithms which guarantee the construction of hierarchical mesh configurations with different class of admissibility. Note that the suitably graded meshes generated via these algorithms are characterized by a different (stronger) version of admissibility, which is easier to obtain via automatically-driven refinement rules.

2.2
WQ for tensor-product splines

The goal of WQ is to reduce the number of
quadrature points in the computation of Galerkin integrals for smooth B-spline basis
functions. In combination with the sum-factorization and replacement of the
element-wise assembly loop by a direct function-wise calculation of the matrix
entries, the cost for the formation of isogeometric Galerkin matrices
goes from
O(p^{3d}N)
FLOPS down to
O(p^{d+1}N)
FLOPS, where
p
and
N
denote the degree and
degrees-of-freedom number, respectively.

Consider
{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}
, the level
\ell
tensor product
B-spline basis, then we can directly apply the construction of

[
11
]
and introduce for each
\boldsymbol{i}\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}
, the following WQ

\displaystyle\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(v):=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\bar{w}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)\approx\int_{[0,1]^{d}}v(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}

(2)

where
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}
,
\bar{w}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}
are the quadrature weights and
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}_{\mathbf{q}}
the quadrature
points. For a better distinction between tensor-product and hierarchical objects, we use bars on the top of symbols for quadrature rules, points and weights in the former case.
Note that weights depend on
\boldsymbol{i}
, that is, on
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, which plays the role of a weight function for the integration.
The rule (
2
) can be used to approximate the
(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})
-entry of
the mass matrix at level
\ell
, indeed

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}c(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}\approx\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(cb^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{j}}),

(3)

where the given function
c
takes into account the determinant of
the Jacobian of the parametrization map. More generally, any matrix
arising in a isogeometric Galerkin method can be formed by suitable WQ, see
[
11
]
.
The accuracy of WQ is related to the exactness conditions that the rule satisfies.
In the case of (
2
)–(
3
), a typical request is

\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{j}})=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}\qquad\forall b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\ell}.

(4)

Though not necessary, following
[
11
]
, the quadrature points
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\ell}_{\mathbf{q}}

in (
2
) do not depend on
\boldsymbol{i}
.
The set of
d
-variate quadrature points is defined as the following tensor-product

\mathcal{Q}^{\ell}:=\mathcal{Q}^{\ell}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Q}^{\ell}_{d}\;,

(5)

where

\mathcal{Q}^{\ell}_{k}:=\{\bar{x}_{k,q_{k}}^{\ell}\}_{q_{k}=1}^{R^{\ell}_{k}}

is the set of univariate
quadrature points in the
k
-th direction and
R^{\ell}_{k}

is the number of quadrature points along that direction. In the case of splines of
of maximum regularity, these points can be selected as the
midpoints and endpoints of the knot spans, with the exception of the
first and last knot spans where
p+1
uniformly distributed quadrature
points can be selected at each edge of the interval, see
[
11
]
.
We also introduce the tensor product set of multi-indices, associated to
\mathcal{Q}^{\ell}
as

\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{Q}}:=\{\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\;:\;1\leq q_{k}\leq R^{\ell}_{k}\,,\,1\leq k\leq d\}\;.

Even though the quadrature points are defined globally, only those in the support of
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
are active for
\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, thus the active quadrature points of
\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
depend on
\boldsymbol{i}
.
This is formalized by setting to 0 the weights
\{\bar{w}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\}_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{Q}}}
corresponding to points that are outside the support of
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
. The nonzero weights are computed by imposing the univariate local exactness conditions, leading to linear problems, whose solution cost is not prevailing in the overall matrix formation cost.

WQ and mass matrix formation
for hierarchical B-splines

3.1
Definition of WQ for hierarchical B-splines

A weighted quadrature rule, associated to an active basis function
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\mathcal{H}
, is denoted by
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
. Its quadrature points and weights are jointly indexed with respect to an index set that is denoted by
\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}
.
Namely, let

\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}\coloneqq\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right\}_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}

(6)

be a set of quadrature points and the set of the corresponding weights are
\left\{w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right\}_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}
.
The quadrature rule
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
applied to an auxiliary function
v
has the following form

\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(v):=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right)\approx\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})v(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

(7)

A peculiar feature of this structure is that both the set of quadrature points and the set of quadrature weights depend on the considered test function
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
. However, as we will see in the following,
\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}
can be conveniently selected as a subset of a global tensor-product grid, which is chosen a priori.

Similarly as in the non-hierarchical case discussed in the previous section, the quadrature rules are characterized by exactness conditions.
More specifically, we require that the rules are exact for all functions in the spline space, or equivalently that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\;b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right)=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}\qquad\forall\;(m,\boldsymbol{j})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}.

(8)

For a given pair
\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}

we define
\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
as the finest level of a hierarchical basis function such
that its support has a nonempty intersection with the support of
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\coloneqq\max\left\{m\;:\;{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}\neq\emptyset,\;\forall b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{H}\right\}.

(9)

To make the notation lighter, the argument
(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
in
\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
will be sometimes omitted,
since the dependence on the basis identifier will be clear from the context.

Any active basis function
b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}
that interacts with
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}

(including itself) can be written as a linear combination of basis functions of
level
\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
, that is

\displaystyle b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}\;b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\qquad\forall\;(m,\boldsymbol{j})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{such that}\quad{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}\neq\emptyset,

(10)

with
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}>0
iff
{\suppoperator(b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}})}\subseteq{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}

and
\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}=0
otherwise.

In order to define the quadrature rule
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
for the hierarchical space
\mathcal{H}
,
we rely on the definitions and relations introduced in the previous section for tensor-product spaces.
In (
7
) we take

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{Q}}\;:\;\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\right\}\;

and let the quadrature points be
\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}

for every
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}
, hence (from definition (
6
)) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}=\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\;.

(11)

b^{1}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}

Proposition 1
.

For
(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
the quadrature rule
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
satisfies the exactness conditions (
8
) on the hierarchical space.

Proof.

Let
(m,\boldsymbol{j})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
If
{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}=\emptyset
, then the equation in
(
8
) is trivially satisfied since the quadrature points
\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}

belong to the support of
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.
On the other hand, if
{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}\neq\emptyset
, then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\left(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}\right)\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{j},\boldsymbol{t}}^{m,\nu}b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{t}}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

In the penultimate row we use the property that for each
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}

the set of points
\mathcal{Q}^{\nu}
and the set of weights
\left\{\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}\right\}_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{Q}}}

satisfy the exactness conditions (
4
) on the level
\nu=\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
.

Remark 1
.

Quadrature rule
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
in (
7
) of level
\ell
is actually
a linear combination of quadrature rules defined at level
\nu
.
Namely, if
{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(v)}\neq\emptyset
we can define

{\cal I}:=\{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathcal{I}^{\nu}_{\mathcal{B}}\;:\;{\suppoperator(b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}})}\subseteq{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\}

and derive

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\boldsymbol{x}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal I}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal I}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{(\nu,\boldsymbol{s})}_{\mathcal{Q}}}\bar{w}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s},\mathbf{q}}v\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{q}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal I}}\alpha_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{s}}^{\ell,\nu}\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}}(v).

In particular, the quadrature rule
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
is determined by those rules

\bar{\mathfrak{Q}}^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}}
whose
{\suppoperator(b^{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{s}})}
are in the support of
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
.

3.2
Preprocessing: computing the quadrature points and weights

Since the quadrature weights are not known in advance for every
possible mesh, degree, level and interaction, they need to be
computed efficiently in the preprocessing phase, before utilizing
them in the matrix formation phase. For computational
efficiency, we fully exploit the tensor-product structure of the
active basis functions
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
and of the quadrature
points for the full level
\ell
basis functions. Quadrature
weights are therefore obtained in two steps. First, we compute the
univariate quadrature weights of level
\nu
, defined in (
9
),
by solving the linear systems arising from the univariate exactness
conditions analogous to (
4
);
this is the same as in
[
11
]
.
Then, the univariate quadrature weights for the WQ associated to an
active basis function
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
are computed as linear
combination of level
\nu
quadrature weights, analogously to (
12
).

The quadrature points and weights for
d
-variate B-splines are stored
and used as
d
-tuples of the univariate points and univariate weights,
respectively, in order to be ready for the sum-factorization used in the
matrix formation.

To avoid redundant computations, all the active basis functions are
clustered with respect to the value of
\nu
so that the univariate
routines are engaged only ones for each level,
i.e., we classify the basis functions of
\mathcal{H}
with respect to
\nu
by
defining the
s
ets of level
n
interacting functions

F^{n}\coloneqq\{b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{i}}}\;:\;(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}\,,\,\nu({\ell},\boldsymbol{i})=n\}.

3.3
Matrix formation: algorithm

The rows and the columns of the mass matrix are associated to the test and trial functions, respectively.
In order to emphasize the hierarchical level of a given basis function,
we use row (or column) multi-index basis identifiers as in (
1
).
Therefore the single entry of the mass matrix is denoted as

\left[M\right]_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}),(m,\boldsymbol{j})}
and is defined as:

\left[M\right]_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}),(m,\boldsymbol{j})}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}c(\boldsymbol{x})b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(\boldsymbol{x})b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,d\boldsymbol{x}\,,

where the function
c\colon[0,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
incorporates the determinant of the
Jacobian of the mapping between the parametric domain
[0,1]^{d}
and the physical domain
\Omega
,
and in general it does not have a tensor-product structure.
Recalling the quadrature rule definition (
7
), and the fact that
w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}=0
for
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}}\notin{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}
, we
can write

\displaystyle\left[M\right]_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}),(m,\boldsymbol{j})}\approx\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(c\,b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})=\sum_{\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{\mathcal{Q}}}w^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}c(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}})b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}})\,d\boldsymbol{x},

(25)

where
n=\nu(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})
.
Using the sum-factorization approach, we exploit

w_{\boldsymbol{i},\mathbf{q}}^{n}=\prod_{k=1}^{d}w_{k,i_{k},\mathrm{q}_{k}}^{n}

and
b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}})=\prod_{k=1}^{d}{b}^{m}_{k,j_{k}}(\bar{x}_{k,\mathrm{q}_{k}}^{n})

and write (
25
) in terms of nested sums:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(cb^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{q}_{d}}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\left(w_{k,i_{k},\mathrm{q}_{k}}^{\ell}{b}^{m}_{k,j_{k}}(\bar{x}_{k,\mathrm{q}_{k}}^{n})\right)\,c(\bar{x}_{1,\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{n},\ldots,\bar{x}_{d,\mathrm{q}_{d}}^{n})

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{d}}w_{d,i_{d},\mathrm{q}_{d}}^{\ell}{b}^{m}_{d,j_{d}}(\bar{x}_{d,\mathrm{q}_{d}}^{n})\left(\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{d-1}}\ldots\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{1}}w_{1,i_{1},\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{\ell}{b}^{m}_{1,j_{1}}(\bar{x}_{1,\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{n})c(\bar{x}_{1,\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{n},\ldots,\bar{x}_{d,\mathrm{q}_{d}}^{n})\right)

where, in the summations above, each running index
\mathrm{q}_{k}
(
k=1,\dots,d
) belongs to the set

Q_{k,i_{k},j_{k}}^{n,\ell,m}\coloneqq\{\mathrm{q}_{k}\in\{1,\dots,R_{k}^{n}\}\colon\bar{x}_{k,\mathrm{q}_{k}}^{n}\in{\suppoperator({b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}})}\cap{\suppoperator({b}^{m}_{k,j_{k}})}\}\;.

(27)

Details are presented in the remaining part of this subsection, where, for the sake of notation simplicity,
we will systematically omit the set

Q_{k,i_{k},j_{k}}^{n,\ell,m}
for the running index
\mathrm{q}_{k}
in the summations.

In (
26
) we note that coefficient

c(\boldsymbol{x})
must be evaluated at the points of the quadrature rule of
level
n
. Moreover, from (
13
)–(
15
) we know
that the sets
\bigl\{F^{n}\bigr\}_{n=0}^{L}
(excluding the empty
sets) form a partition of the hierarchical basis
\mathcal{H}
. This suggests
to construct the matrix starting from an outer loop over
\bigl\{F^{n}\bigr\}_{n=0}^{L}
,
i.e., over the different levels of quadrature rules, then for a given level
n
,
compute the determinant of the Jacobian at
\mathcal{Q}^{n}_{\Psi}

(i.e., on the points that have non-empty intersection with the support of each basis
function in
F^{n}
) and set the values to be zero for the points

\mathcal{Q}^{n}\setminus\mathcal{Q}^{n}_{\Psi}
.

The key point here is that the evaluation of the non-tensor-product coefficient
c(\boldsymbol{x})
may be a costly operation, so we want to evaluate it just for the involved quadrature points, i.e., for each

\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{\mathcal{Q}}

we set:

C^{n}_{\mathbf{q}}=C^{n}_{(\mathrm{q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}})&\text{if}\;\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}^{n}_{\mathbf{q}}\in\mathcal{Q}^{n}_{\Psi}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

(28)

Remark 3
.

The coefficients
C^{n}_{\mathbf{q}}
will be used in the innermost loop of the sum-factorization algorithm,
so they should be stored in an efficient data structure for the data retrieval w.r.t. the loop index ordering
used in the sum-factorization.

Given a quadrature level
n\in\{0,\dots,L\}
such that
F^{n}\neq\emptyset
,
we loop over
\ell,m\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and compute the connectivity between
the test functions of
\mathcal{H}^{\ell}\cap F^{n}
and the trial functions of
\mathcal{H}^{m}
, i.e.,

K_{\ell,m}^{n}\coloneqq\{(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})\in\mathcal{I}^{\ell}_{\mathcal{B}}\times\mathcal{I}^{m}_{\mathcal{B}}\;:\;b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\ell}\cap F^{n}\,,\,b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}}\in\mathcal{H}^{m}\,,\,{\suppoperator(b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}})}\cap{\suppoperator(b^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})}\neq\emptyset\}\;.

(29)

At this point we can apply the sum-factorization algorithm that allows
us to evaluate the mass-matrix entries.

The sum-factorization algorithm in essence is a clever way to perform the nested
sum (
26
), that sequentially performs the integration along
the directions
k=1,\ldots,d
, considering for each
k
all pairs of indices

(i_{k},j_{k})
that identify the weight and trial function respectively.

The integration along direction
k=1
writes as

\displaystyle I^{(1)}_{(i_{1}),(j_{1});(\mathrm{q}_{2},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{1}}w^{\ell}_{1,i_{1},\mathrm{q}_{1}}{b}^{m}_{1,j_{1}}(\bar{x}^{n}_{1,q_{1}})C^{n}_{(\mathrm{q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{1}}w^{\ell}_{1,i_{1},\mathrm{q}_{1}}{b}^{m}_{1,j_{1}}(\bar{x}^{n}_{1,q_{1}})I^{(0)}_{(),();(\mathrm{q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}\;,

(30)

where we have defined
I^{(0)}_{(),();(\mathrm{q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}\equiv C^{n}_{(\mathrm{q}_{1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}
,
which only depends on the
d
-tuple of indices associated to the quadrature points.
Performing the summation over
\mathrm{q}_{1}
we have as result
I^{(1)}_{(i_{1}),(j_{1});(\mathrm{q}_{2},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}

that depends on the pair
(i_{1},j_{1})
(related to the univariate test and trial basis along
direction
1
) and on the
(d-1)
-tuple
(\mathrm{q}_{2},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})
(related to univariate quadrature points
along the directions
2,\dots,d
).
The integration along directions
k=2,\dots,d-1
then writes as:

I^{(k)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k});(\mathrm{q}_{k+1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{k}}w^{\ell}_{k,i_{k},\mathrm{q}_{k}}{b}^{m}_{k,j_{k}}(\bar{x}^{n}_{k,q_{k}})I^{(k-1)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1});(\mathrm{q}_{k},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}\;,

(31)

and finally for
k=d
:

I^{(d)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{d});()}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathrm{q}_{d}}w^{\ell}_{d,i_{d},\mathrm{q}_{d}}{b}^{m}_{d,j_{d}}(\bar{x}^{n}_{d,q_{d}})I^{(d-1)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d-1}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{d-1});(\mathrm{q}_{d})}=\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(cb^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})\;,

(32)

where the final expression in (
32

d
-tuple
((i_{1},\dots,i_{d}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{d}))
and is equal to
\mathfrak{Q}^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}(cb^{m}_{\boldsymbol{j}})
.

The key point (which allows to save computations) is that the value of

I^{(k-1)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1});(\mathrm{q}_{k},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}
in (
31
)
may be needed to compute multiple values of
I^{(k)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k});(\mathrm{q}_{k+1},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}
.
To exploit this fact, when we are integrating along a direction

we
must consider all the pairs of
k
-tuples
((i_{1},\dots,i_{k}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k}))
.

Accordingly, for each

we define the “projection” of the connectivity

along the first

4
Computational cost

We now want to estimate the total computational cost of the matrix formation. There are mainly three steps that contribute to this cost: the evaluation of the non-tensor product coefficient
c
, the computation of the weights, and the computation of the matrix entries via sum-factorization.

The coefficient
c
has to be evaluated for every active quadrature point. Quadrature points are more dense for elements that are adjacent to the boundary of
\Omega
. However the total number of active quadrature points is dominated from the interior part in all cases of interest. Recalling (
11
) and
\nu-\ell\leq r-1
, the number of quadrature points that belong to interior elements is bounded by

\displaystyle\sum_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}}\#\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}\leq\sum_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}}(2^{r-1}(p+1))^{d}=O(2^{dr}p^{d}N_{\mathcal{H}}).

(34)

We remark that bound above is not sharp especially for what concerns its dependence on
p
, since quadrature points in different
\mathcal{Q}^{\left(\ell,\boldsymbol{i}\right)}
may coincide.

As for the computation of the weights, we recall that we have to solve a system of the form (
19
) for every univariate index
j_{k}
, for
k=1,\ldots,d
. Since the number of univariate indices is bounded by the number of multi-indices
N_{\mathcal{H}}
, and since each of these linear system has
O(p)
unknown nonzero weights
the cost to compute them all using a direct solver is bounded by
O(p^{3}N_{\mathcal{H}})
flops.

If we compare the bound on this cost with the one related to the computation of the matrix entries (derived below), we see that they have the same order with respect to
p
for
d=2
and that the former has lower order for
d=3
. Note also that this bound does not depend on the admissibility parameter
r
.

We now discuss the computation of the matrix entries. Following the structure of Algorithm
5
, we fix
n\in\left\{0,\ldots,L\right\}
and
\ell,m\in\left\{0,\ldots,n\right\}
and consider the computation of the matrix entries (
25
) for all
(\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j})\in K_{\ell,m}^{n}
, as performed by Algorithm
4
.

As a preliminary step, we observe that for any fixed direction
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and any fixed index value
i_{k}
, the number of indices
j_{k}
that must be considered in (
26
) is clearly bounded by the number of basis functions of level
m
whose support intersects the support of
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
. It can be verified that the latter number is bounded by
2p+1
when
m\leq\ell
, and by
2^{m-\ell}(p+1)+p
when
m>\ell
. In both cases, this number is bounded by
2^{n-\ell+1}(p+1)
, since
n\geq\max\{m,\ell\}
.

Moreover,
again for any fixed direction
k
and index value
i_{k}
,
the active quadrature points
\bar{x}^{n}_{k,q_{k}}
are the ones belonging to the support of
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
; since we have
2
quadrature points on each interior element of level
n
, or
p+1
on the elements that touch the boundary, and the support of
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
contains at most
2^{n-\ell}(p+1)
elements of level
n
, we conclude that there are at most
2^{n-\ell+1}(p+1)
active quadrature points if
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
does not touch the boundary, or at most
\left(2^{n-\ell+1}+1\right)(p+1)
quadrature points if
{b}^{\ell}_{k,i_{k}}
touches the boundary. Typically, the cost is dominated by the quadrature at the interior, therefore we assume that the number of index values taken by
\mathrm{q}_{k}
in the
k
-th sum of (
26
) is roughly
2^{n-\ell+1}(p+1)
.

We are now ready to estimate the cost of computing (
26
). As a first step, we evaluate the innermost sum (
30
) for all relevant values of
i_{1}
,
j_{1}
and
\mathrm{q}_{2},\ldots,\mathrm{q}_{d}
. Of course in the sum we only need to consider the nonzero terms, and we observe that the term corresponding to a fixed
\mathrm{q}_{1}
is nonzero only for the
p+1
values of the index
j_{1}
such that
{b}^{m}_{1,j_{1}}(\bar{x}_{\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{n})\neq 0
.

Note that if we preliminary multiply
w_{1,i_{1},\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{\ell}{b}^{m}_{1,j_{1}}(\bar{x}_{\mathrm{q}_{1}}^{n})
for all such values of
\mathrm{q}_{1}
and
j_{1}
(which has a negligible cost), the compution of the sum (
30
) requires 2 flops for each of its nonzero terms.

Since each index
\mathrm{q}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{q}_{d}
, can take up to
2^{n-\ell+1}(p+1)
values, and the number of values taken by
i_{1}=\tau_{1}\boldsymbol{i}
is bounded by the number of multi-indices
\boldsymbol{i}
belonging to
F^{n}\cap\mathcal{H}^{\ell}
,
the cost of the first step is bounded by

\displaystyle 2\left(p+1\right)^{d+1}\,2^{d(n-\ell+1)}N_{n,\ell}\text{ flops}.

(35)

where

N_{n,\ell}:=|F^{n}\cap\mathcal{H}^{\ell}|.

For
k=2,\ldots,d-1
, the
k
-th step of the sum-factorization requires the computation of (
31
)
for all values of
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
,
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}
and
\mathrm{q}_{k+1},\ldots,\mathrm{q}_{d}
, where the inner sum
I^{(k-1)}_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}),(j_{1},\dots,j_{k-1});(\mathrm{q}_{k},\dots,\mathrm{q}_{d})}
has already been computed for all the relevant index values.

Since
\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\right)=\tau_{k}\boldsymbol{i}
the total number of

k
-tuples
\left(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\right)
that have to be considered is again bounded by the number of multi-indices
N_{n,\ell}
.

Moreover, again we observe that for each value of
\mathrm{q}_{k}
there are only
p+1
values of
j_{k}
that contribute to the sum, and since the number of values taken by each index
\mathrm{q}_{k},\ldots,\mathrm{q}_{d}
and
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k-1}
is bounded by
2^{n-\ell+1}(p+1)
, the cost of this step is again bounded by (
35
). With similar arguments, it can be shown that this is true also for the
d-
th step of the sum-factorization (
31
).

We conclude that the cost of the whole sum-factorization step is bounded by

\displaystyle 2d\left(p+1\right)^{d+1}\,2^{d(n-\ell+1)}N_{n,\ell}\text{ flops}.

We sum the above expression for all values of
n,\ell
and
m
, and observe that for a fixed level
\ell
the number of levels
m
that interact with it is at most
2r-1
. Thus, a bound on the total cost for the matrix entries computation is given by

\displaystyle 2d(2r-1)\left(p+1\right)^{d}\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell\leq n}2^{d(n-\ell+1)}N_{n,\ell}\text{ flops}.

(36)

We can derive a more explicit bound on the cost of the matrix entries computation if take a further step and observe that
n-\ell+1\leq r
and that

\sum_{n}\sum_{\ell\leq n}N_{n,\ell}=N_{\mathcal{H}}

Hence the total cost for the matrix entries computation (
36
) is bounded by

\displaystyle 2d(2r-1)2^{dr}\left(p+1\right)^{d+1}N_{\mathcal{H}}=O\left(dr2^{dr}p^{d+1}N_{\mathcal{H}}\right)\text{ flops}.

(37)

We observe that, similarly as in the bound on the active quadrature points (
34
), the latter expression grows exponentially with respct to the admissibilty parameter
r
, and this effect worsen with the increasing of the dimension
d
. This might seem unsatisfactory, but we emphasize that (
37
) is easily a rather pessimistic bound. Indeed, a careful analysis of the derivation of (
37
) reveals that we are essentially assuming that every hierarchical B-spline basis function
b^{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{i}}
, with
(\ell,\boldsymbol{i})\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{H}}
, interacts with all the admissible levels.
In many practical cases, however, refinement is perfomred only in specific regions of the domain, e.g., in the neighbourhood of low dimensional manifolds, and as a result the number of basis functions that interact with all the admissible levels is limited.

Numerical tests

The numerical tests comprise of the
L^{2}
-projection of the function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,

f(\mathbf{x})=\exp\biggl[-\Bigl(\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0}\|-1}{\beta}\Bigr)^{2}\biggr],

(38)

where the physical domain
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the parameter
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and the point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are specified below for the cases
d=2
and
d=3
, using a
r
-admissible hierarchical B-spline basis (see Section
2.1
) of degree
p
, with different values for the admissibility parameter
r
.

For each value of the admissibility parameter
r
, a nested sequence of hierarchical B-spline spaces is constructed
[
28
]
. The adaptive mesh refinement is steered by the “error estimator”, which is simply the
L^{2}
-error between the computed
L^{2}
projection and the function (
38
) and by using the Dörfler marking strategy
[
29
]
with parameter
\theta_{\ast}=0.2
.

For each refinement step, we perform a simulation using the standard element-base Gaussian quadrature (using
p+1
quadrature point along each direction of the element) to build the mass matrix (and right hand side) and then, using the same sequence of hierarchical spaces we compute the mass-matrix using the proposed hierarchical WQ algorithm.

Remark 4
.

All the numerical tests were performed using the IGATOOLS library
[
30
]
, on a single core of an Intel Xeon E5-2470 processor running at 2.3 GHz.
In order to alleviate the random fluctuations in the elapsed CPU time,
all plots involving CPU time refer to the average CPU time of multiple (5 for the 2D case and 3 for the 3D case) runs of the same simulation.

Remark 5

In all plots the lower limit of the CPU time is set to
10^{-1}
seconds to reduce the effect of random time fluctuations, due to the CPU scheduling.

6
Closure

A fast matrix formation technique for adaptive isogeometric Galerkin methods with multivariate hierarchical B-splines was presented by focusing on the efficient design of weighted quadrature rules. The theoretical estimates of the computational cost suitably exploit the limited number of basis functions which are non-zero on any element of an admissible hierarchical mesh. A selection of numerical examples confirm that the results obtained with the hierarchical weighted approach compare favorably with respect to standard Gaussian quadrature rules, specially in the three-dimensional case. Interesting topics for future research include for example the
combination of the proposed algorithm with matrix-free methods

[
31
]
as well as the extension to the case of truncated
hierarchical B-splines
[
3
,
4
]
, and the application to PDE problems of
applicative interest.
