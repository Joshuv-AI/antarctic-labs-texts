1
Introduction

The Moran model, describing the evolution via
resampling
of a population of individuals carrying genetic types, is a workhorse in population genetics. It is tractable because it is mean-field, i.e., all individuals exchange type with each other in the same manner, and because it has a simple dual, namely, the death process on the positive integers. It has been the basis for much more sophisticated models incorporating evolutionary forces such as mutation, selection, recombination and migration (see
[
Dur08
]
).

1.1
The two-type Moran model

A standard model for evolution of types in population genetics is the two-type Moran model (see
[
Dur08
, Section 1.5]
). Consider a population of
N\in\mathbb{N}
individuals. Each individual carries either type
1
or type
0
, and at rate
1
chooses an individual uniformly at random from the population (possibly itself) and adopts its type. Let
X^{N}(t)
be the number of individuals with type
1
at time
t
. For every
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(X^{N}(t)=0\text{ or }X^{N}(t)=N)=1,

(1.1)

because
\{0\}
and
\{N\}
are traps for
(X^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
: genetic diversity is lost forever when all individuals have type
0
or all individuals have type
1
, respectively.

To see what happens prior to trapping, scale space and time to get

Y^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N}X^{N}(Nt),

(1.2)

which is the fraction of individuals of type
1
at time
Nt
. It is well known that, if
\lim_{N\to\infty}Y^{N}(0)=y\in[0,1]
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{L}[Y^{N}]=\mathcal{L}[Y]\quad\text{in the Skorohod topology},

(1.3)

where
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
, and
\mathcal{L}
stands for law. The limit
Y
is the
Fisher-Wright diffusion

{{\mathrm{d}}}Y(t)=\sqrt{Y(t)\,(1-Y(t))}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}W(t),\qquad Y(0)=y,

(1.4)

where
W=(W(t))_{t\geq 0}
is standard Brownian motion. The convergence in (
1.3
) is proven by showing that the infinitesimal generator
L^{N}
of
Y^{N}
converges to the infinitesimal generator
L
of
Y
on a dense set of test functions (see
[
EK86
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
). The latter is given by

(Lf)(y)=y(1-y)f^{\prime\prime}(y),\qquad y\in[0,1],

(1.5)

for twice differentiable test functions
f\colon\,[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\tau=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\,Y(t)=0\text{ or }Y(t)=1\}
. It is know that
\tau<\infty
almost surely, i.e., genetic diversity is lost at time
\tau N+o(N)
as
N\to\infty
.

1.2
The two-type Moran model with random resampling rates

It is natural to allow for resampling rates that are
random
. Indeed, individuals typically live in different
environments
, causing them to have different
fitnesses
(e.g. due to lack or abundance of nutrients). In fact, the standard Moran model is an idealisation in which such natural variations are discarded. The situation is similar to what is seen in statistical physics, where disorder is introduced in the interaction between the constituent particles of a material, simply because almost all materials in nature are inhomogeneous at the microscopic level. The disorder typically affects the behaviour at the macroscopic level, in particular, the
transport coefficients
that capture physical phenomena like diffusion, viscosity or compressibility.

In the present paper we consider a version of the two-type Moran model in which each individual carries a
pre-assigned resampling rate
, drawn from a discrete probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}=(0,\infty)
. We will see that, in the large-population-size limit and after scaling time by the population size, the empirical distribution of the resampling rates of the individuals with type
1
converges to
\mathbb{P}
times a Fisher-Wright diffusion with a
diffusion constant
that depends on
\mathbb{P}
. The proof of this fact turns out to be somewhat delicate because the path of the empirical distribution
contracts to the submanifold

\{s\mathbb{P}\colon\,s\in[0,1]\}
at a rate that diverges with the population size (so that convergence of the infinitesimal generator away from the submanifold fails). This contraction, which is absent in the standard Moran model, requires us to work with a particular path topology, called the
Meyer-Zheng topology
, which roughly speaking induces weak convergence in “time
\times
space”. We may think of the latter as convergence outside a set of exceptional times.

Very little is known about population genetic models with disorder. The reason is that
the disorder destroys the exchangeability of the individuals
, especially in the quenched setting, and therefore makes duality, another workhorse in population genetics, much more involved. In fact, in the present paper we do not resort to duality and work with
Lyapunov functions
to control the contraction to the submanifold. How the contraction precisely occurs comes with a number of subtleties. Our proofs are restricted to discrete
\mathbb{P}
because the submanifold depends on
\mathbb{P}
. Perturbation arguments are delicate because they displace the submanifold.

1.3
Main theorem

Throughout the sequel we assume that
\mathbb{P}
satisfies the following assumption.

Assumption 1.1
.

(1)

\mathbb{P}
has countable support, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\delta_{r_{k}}
with
\mu=(\mu_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a probability distribution on
\mathbb{N}
and
(r_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Moreover,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}r_{k}^{-1}<\infty
.

(2)

Let
R=(R_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be i.i.d. drawn from
\mathbb{P}
, i.e.,
R
has law
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\hat{n}^{N}_{k}=\sum_{i\in[N]}1_{\{R_{i}=r_{k}\}},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},\qquad N_{R}=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\,\hat{n}_{k}^{N}>0\}.

(1.6)

Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k})\,(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k}^{-2})}{N\min_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k}}=0\qquad\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}\text{-a.s.}

(1.7)

Note that Assumption
1.1
holds for all
\mathbb{P}
with finite support and fails for all
\mathbb{P}
with uncountable support. In Section
2.3
we derive a sufficient condition on
\mathbb{P}
with countable support guaranteeing that (
1.7
) holds. For instance, it suffices that
\mu_{k}\asymp k^{-\chi}
,
k\to\infty
, with
\chi>2
(right tail condition) and
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,r_{k}^{-\alpha}<\infty
,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,r_{k}^{\beta}<\infty
for
\alpha
and
\beta
sufficiently large depending on
\chi
(left and right moment conditions).

In what follows, we consider the
quenched
process in which the resampling rates are chosen initially and are kept fixed while the process evolves. Let
R_{i}
denote the random resampling rate of individual
i
. Note that
R=(R_{i})_{i\in[N]}
has law
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes[N]}
. The empirical distribution of the resampling rates is

\mathcal{E}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}\delta_{R_{i}}.

(1.8)

Note that this is a probability distribution on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and that,
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
-a.s.,
\mathcal{E}^{N}
converges to
\mathbb{P}
as
N\to\infty
. Let
\tau_{i}(t)
denote the type of individual
i
at time
t
, and let

Y^{N}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}1_{\{\tau_{i}(Nt)=1\}}\delta_{R_{i}}

(1.9)

denote the
empirical distribution of the resampling rates of the individuals with type
1
at time
Nt
. Note that this is an element of
\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
, the set of sub-probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Our main result says that the process
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
converges in law, in the so-called
Meyer-Zheng topology
(explained in Section
2
), to a simple but interesting limiting process. We may rewrite
\mathcal{E}^{N}
as

\mathcal{E}^{N}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}n^{N}_{k}\delta_{r_{k}},\qquad n^{N}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}{\hat{n}^{N}_{k}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}1_{\{R_{i}=r_{k}\}}.

(1.10)

Note that
N_{R}=\{k\in\mathbb{N}\colon\,n^{N}_{k}>0\}
. Also note that
|N_{R}|\leq N
, and consequently the vector
n^{N}=(n^{N}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has at most
N
non-zero elements.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}
satisfies Assumption
1.1
. If
\lim_{N\to\infty}Y^{N}(0)=\nu
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathcal{L}[Y^{N}]=\mathcal{L}[Y]\quad\text{in the Meyer-Zheng topology}\quad\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}\text{-a.s.}

(1.11)

with
Y=(Y(t))_{t\geq 0}
given by

Y(t)=S(t)\,\mathbb{P},

(1.12)

where
S=(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is the Fisher-Wright diffusion on
[0,1]
given by

{{\mathrm{d}}}S(t)=\sqrt{D\,S(t)\,(1-S(t))}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}W(t),\qquad S(0)=\nu(\mathbb{R}_{+}),

(1.13)

with diffusion constant
D
defined as
1/D=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}(1/r)\,\mathbb{P}({{\mathrm{d}}}r)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,r_{k}^{-1}
.

Remark 1.3
.

Theorem
1.2
says that
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
collapses onto the submanifold
\{s\,\mathbb{P}\colon\,s\in[0,1]\}
in the limit as
N\to\infty
and on this submanifold performs an autonomous Fisher-Wright diffusion with a diffusion constant that is determined by
\mathbb{P}
. The contraction to the submanifold shows that
Y^{N}
exhibits
homogenisation
, i.e., the type and the resampling rate of the individuals are asymptotically independent as
N\to\infty
. The inverse of the diffusion constant
1/D
represents the average time between two successive resamplings of an individual. Section
2.3
contains examples where Assumption
1.1
holds. Assumption
1.1
excludes
\mathbb{P}
with uncountable support. We conjecture that Theorem
1.2
continues to hold in that setting, subject to appropriate tail and moment conditions, but we are unable to provide a proof. Along the way we will see what the hurdles are.

Remark 1.4
.

The diffusion constant
D
can be viewed a modulating an
effective population size
, a notion that is important in population genetics (see e.g.
[
Dur08
, Section 4.4]
). Since the time scale of the evolution is
N
, which is the population size, speeding up the evolution by a factor
D
means that the effective population size is
DN
.

Remark 1.5
.

Our model can be interpreted as a Moran model with a particular kind of selection by viewing the resampling as done in pairs. Pick a pair of individuals, decide randomly which of the two individuals adopts the type of the other individual. In the standard Moran model the latter is decided uniformly at random. In our model it is decided with probabilities that depend on the inverses of the resampling rates of the two individuals.

Remark 1.6
.

It is straightforward to extend Theorem
1.2
to the Moran model with finitely many types. It is more challenging to include the Fleming-Viot model with infinitely many types. In the latter setting, it would be interesting to see what happens when the resampling rate distribution has no finite inverse moment at zero. Slow rates may affect the property that the state of the Fleming-Viot diffusion after an arbitrary positive time becomes atomic (a property referred to as “coming down from infinity”).

2
Definitions and notation

In Section
2.1
we define the random process precisely and in Section
2.2
we recall the Meyer-Zheng topology. In Section
2.3
we derive some tail estimates for the random resampling rates and exhibit sufficient conditions under which Assumption
1.1
holds.

2.1
The random process

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be the size of the population. Individuals are labelled
[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
and carry either type
1
or type
0
. Each individual has a random resampling rate that is drawn from a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The resampling rates are assigned
independently
and before the process starts. Recall the definition of
R=(R_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, the resampling rates assigned to the individuals, and the definition of
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, the empirical distribution of the resampling rates of the individuals with type
1
at time
Nt
, both introduced in Section
1.3
.

For
\Delta\in\{-1,+1\}
,
r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
, put

T^{r,\Delta}(y)=y+\frac{\Delta}{N}\delta_{r}.

(2.1)

This operator encodes the event that an individual with resampling rate
r
changes its type. Namely, when
\Delta=-1
an individual with resampling rate
r
and type
1
chooses an individual with type
0
and adopts its type, while when
\Delta=+1
an individual with resampling rate
r
and type
0
chooses an individual with type
1
and adopts its type. Define

R^{r,-1}(y)\,{{\mathrm{d}}}r=ry({{\mathrm{d}}}r)[1-y(\mathbb{R}_{+})],\qquad R^{r,+1}(y)\,{{\mathrm{d}}}r=r[\mathcal{E}^{N}({{\mathrm{d}}}r)-y({{\mathrm{d}}}r)]y(\mathbb{R}_{+}).

(2.2)

The change
\hat{T}^{r,-1}(y)
happens at rate
\hat{R}^{r,-1}(y)
, because there are
y({{\mathrm{d}}}r)
individuals with type
1
that can choose a new individual from the population at rate
r
. In order for such an individual to change to
0
, it needs to choose an individual with type
0
. There is a fraction
1-y(\mathbb{R}_{+})
individuals of type
0
in total, and the individual chooses one of them uniformly. The explanation for
T^{r,+1}(y)
and
R^{r,+1}(y)
is analogous.

Note that the transition rates
R^{r,\Delta}(y)
and the transition operators
T^{r,\Delta}(y)
only depend on the current state
y
of the process. Hence
Y^{N}
is Markov. We can write down the infinitesimal generator
L^{N}
of
Y
. Let
f\colon\,\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded and continuous test function, and let
y\in\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
. Then

(L^{N}f)(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}{{\mathrm{d}}}r\sum_{\Delta\in\{-1,+1\}}R^{r,\Delta}(y)[f(T^{r,\Delta}(y))-f(y)]N^{2}.

(2.3)

The term
N^{2}
comes from the time scaling by
N
and the space scaling by
1/N
.

2.2
The Meyer-Zheng topology

For more background on what is written below, we refer the reader to
[
DM78
, Chapter III, no. 40–46]
,
[
MZ84
]
, and
[
GdHO22
, Appendix B]
. The Meyer-Zheng topology is a topology on path-space that is weaker than the Skorohod topology. Intuitively, it captures weak convergence in ‘space
\times
time’, i.e., it allows for deviations of the path on exceptional sets of time. In our context these exceptional times are those where
Y^{N}(t)
could potentially be away from the submanifold
\{s\mathbb{P}\colon\,s\in[0,1]\}
.

The Meyer-Zheng topology assigns to each
\bar{\mathbb{R}}
-valued Borel measurable function
w=(w(t))_{t\geq 0}
a probability measure on
[0,\infty]\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\psi_{w}(A)=\int_{[0,\infty]\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}}1_{A}(w(t))\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t,

(2.4)

i.e.,
\psi_{w}
is the image under the mapping
t\to(t,w(t))
of the probability measure
\mathrm{e}^{-t}{{\mathrm{d}}}t
. This image measure is called the
pseudopath
associated with
w
, and is simply the occupation measure of
w
. We say that a sequence of pseudopaths induced by a sequence of càdlàg paths
(w_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a pseudopath induced by a càdlàg path
w
if, for all continuous bounded function
f(t,w(t))
on
[0,\infty]\times\bar{\mathbb{R}}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{[0,\infty)}f(t,w_{N}(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t=\int_{[0,\infty)}f(t,w(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t.

(2.5)

Since a pseudopath is a measure, convergence of pseudopaths is convergence of measures. Let
\Xi
be the space of all pseudopaths. Endowed with the Meyer-Zheng topology,
\Xi
is a Polish space (see, for example,
[
Kur91
, Section 4]
).

Let
v_{N}\colon\,[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
denote the path of
Y^{N}
and
v\colon\,[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
the path of
Y
. For
f\in C_{b}(\Xi)
, define

\psi_{v_{N}}(f)=\int_{[0,\infty)}f(t,v_{N}(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t,\qquad\psi_{v}(f)=\int_{[0,\infty)}f(t,v(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t.

(2.6)

Suppose that
f(t,x)=1_{t\in[a,b]}\bar{f}(x)
, with
\bar{f}\colon\,\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}\to\mathbb{R}
bounded and continuous and
0\leq a<b<\infty
. The set of all functions of this form generates
C_{b}(\Xi)
. Write

\psi_{v_{N}}(f)=\int_{a}^{b}\bar{f}(v_{N}(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t,\qquad\psi_{v}(f)=\int_{a}^{b}\bar{f}(v(t))\ \mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t.

(2.7)

To prove convergence in the Meyer-Zheng topology, we need to show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\psi_{v_{N}}(f)]=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{v}(f)]\qquad\forall\,f\in C_{b}(\Xi),

(2.8)

which is equivalent to

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{a}^{b}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\bar{f}(Y^{N}(t))-\bar{f}(Y(t))}}\right|\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t}}\right]=0\qquad\forall\,\bar{f}\in C_{b}(\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}),\,0\leq a<b<\infty.

(2.9)

Suppose that
\bar{f}
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
for a given distance
d
on
\mathcal{M}_{\leq 1}
. By the Stone-Weierstrass theorem, the space of polynomial functions on the set of closed intervals in
[0,1]^{N}
is dense in the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]^{N}
. Furthermore, the space of Lipschitz functions contains the space of polynomial functions. This means that any result for Lipschitz functions can be generalised to continuous
\bar{f}
. We find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{a}^{b}\big|\bar{f}(Y^{N}(t))-\bar{f}(Y(t))\big|\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{a}^{b}d(Y^{N}(t),Y(t))\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t}}\right]

(2.10)

\displaystyle=C\int_{a}^{b}\mathbb{E}\big[d(Y^{N}(t),Y(t))\big]\,\mathrm{e}^{-t}\,{{\mathrm{d}}}t,

where we use Fubini’s theorem to interchange the integral and the expectation. Thus, if we manage to prove that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[d(Y^{N}(t),Y(t))\big]=0\text{ uniformly in $t$ over compact subsets of $(0,\infty)$},

(2.11)

then we have proven that
Y^{N}
converges to
Y
in the Meyer-Zheng topology. We will choose for
d
a suitable
l^{2}
-metric and establish (
2.11
) in Section
3.1
.

2.3
Examples satisfying Assumption
1.1

In this section we will identify examples satisfying Assumption
1.1
using tail and moment estimates. We will need the following conditions:

(I)

There are
\alpha,\beta\in(0,\infty)
such that

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,r_{k}^{-\alpha}<\infty,\qquad\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,r_{k}^{\beta}<\infty.

(2.12)

(II)

There are
\gamma,\delta\in(0,1]
such that

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{-(1-\gamma)}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,1_{\{\mu_{k}\leq\varepsilon\}}<\infty,\qquad\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon^{\delta}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}1_{\{\mu_{k}>\varepsilon\}}<\infty.

(2.13)

(III)

There is a
\chi>1
such that

\mu_{k}\asymp k^{-\chi},\qquad k\to\infty.

(2.14)

Lemma 2.1
.

Recall the definition of
(R_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
from Section
1.3
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let

M^{-}_{N}=\min_{i\in[N]}R_{i},\quad M^{+}_{N}=\max_{i\in[N]}R_{i},\quad Z_{N}={|N_{R}|.}

(2.15)

Write
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{E}
to denote probability and expectation with respect to the law
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
of
(R_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
.

If Condition
(I)
holds, then

(1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M^{-}_{N}N^{1/\alpha}\geq\varepsilon)=1
.

(2)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(M^{+}_{N}N^{-1/\beta}\leq\varepsilon^{-1})=1
.

If Condition
(II)
holds, then

(3)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}N^{-(\gamma\vee\delta)}\leq\varepsilon^{-1})=1
.

If Condition
(III)
holds, then

(4)

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{N}N^{-\frac{\upsilon}{\chi-1}}\leq\varepsilon^{-1})=1\,\,\forall\,v>1
.

Proof.

(1) Estimate, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M^{-}_{N}\geq\varepsilon N^{-1/\alpha})=\mathbb{P}\big(R_{i}\geq\varepsilon N^{-1/\alpha}\,\,\forall\,i\in[N]\big)=\mathbb{P}(R_{1}\geq\varepsilon N^{-1/\alpha})^{N}

(2.16)

\displaystyle=\big(1-\mathbb{P}((1/R_{1})^{\alpha}>\varepsilon^{-\alpha}N)\big)^{N}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(1/R_{1})^{\alpha}]}}\right)^{N}\to\exp(-\varepsilon^{\alpha}\mathbb{E}[(1/R_{1})^{\alpha}]).

By (
2.12
),
\mathbb{E}[(1/R_{1})^{\alpha}]<\infty
, and so the claim follows after letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

(2) Estimate, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M^{+}_{N}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}N^{1/\beta})=\mathbb{P}\big(R_{i}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}N^{1/\beta}\,\,\forall\,i\in[N]\big)=\mathbb{P}(R_{1}\leq\varepsilon^{-1}N^{1/\beta})^{N}

(2.17)

\displaystyle=\big(1-\mathbb{P}(R_{1}^{\beta}>\varepsilon^{-\beta}N)\big)^{N}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\varepsilon^{\beta}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(R_{1})^{\beta}]}}\right)^{N}\to\exp(-\varepsilon^{\beta}\mathbb{E}[(R_{1})^{\beta}]).

By (
2.12
),
\mathbb{E}[(R_{1})^{\beta}]<\infty
, and so the claim follows after letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

(3) Estimate

\mathbb{P}(N^{-(\gamma\vee\delta)}{Z_{N}}\leq\varepsilon^{-1})=1-\mathbb{P}(N^{-(\gamma\vee\delta)}{Z_{N}}>\varepsilon^{-1})\geq 1-\varepsilon N^{-(\gamma\vee\delta)}\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}].

(2.18)

Write

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(R_{i}=r_{k})\,\mathbb{P}(R_{i}\neq R_{j}\,\forall\,1\leq j<i\mid R_{i}=r_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[N]}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}(1-\mu_{k})^{i-1}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}\,\frac{1-(1-\mu_{k})^{N}}{1-(1-\mu_{k})}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[1-(1-\mu_{k})^{N}].

(2.20)

Then

as

<\infty

. Hence
\limsup_{N\to\infty}N^{-(\gamma\vee\delta)}\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}]<\infty
, and so the claim follows after letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
in (
2.18
).

(4) The proof of this part is inspired by the proof presented in

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}[1-(1-\mu_{k})^{N}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{{1\leq k\leq N_{0}}}[1-(1-\mu_{k})^{N}]+\sum_{k\geq N_{0}+1}[1-(1-\mu_{k})^{N}]

3
Proof of the main theorem

In Section
3.1
we state a key lemma (Lemma
3.1
below) that implies Theorem
1.2
. In Sections
3.2
–
3.5
we prove this lemma. The proof is based on the construction of a
Lyapunov function
together with a
projection argument
, showing that the scaled dynamics rapidly contracts to the submanifold
\{s\,\mathbb{P}\colon\,s\in[0,1]\}
. Outside the submanifold the generator of the scaled dynamics diverges as
N\to\infty
. We show that inside the manifold the generator converges to that of the Fisher-Wright diffusion in (
1.13
). Throughout this section, Assumption
1.1
is in force.

3.1
Definitions and a key lemma

Recall that we denoted by
(R_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the sequence of independent random variables with distribution
\mathbb{P}
, and
\mathcal{E}^{N}
the empirical distribution of the first
N
random variables, i.e.,

\mathcal{E}^{N}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}n^{N}_{k}\delta_{r_{k}},\qquad n^{N}_{k}=N^{-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}1_{\{R_{i}=r_{k}\}}.

(3.1)

Recall
N_{R}
from Section
1.3
. The measure-valued process
Y^{N}
can be represented as a vector-valued process
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, also with at most
N
non-zero elements. Each
Y^{N}_{k}
may be viewed as the fraction of
Y^{N}
that have the resampling rate
r_{k}
, and thus
Y^{N}_{k}>0
if and only if
k\in N_{R}
.

Convergence of Markov processes is usually proven by showing that the infinitesimal generators converge (see
[
EK86
, Chapter 4, Section 8]
. For convenience we first reformulate the infinitesimal generator of
Y^{N}
. Let
f\colon\,\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
be three times partially differentiable, and let

\lvert f\rvert_{3}=\sup_{i,j,k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial z_{i}\partial z_{j}\partial z_{k}}f}}\right\|_{\infty}.

(3.2)

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X_{r_{k}}(t)
be the number of individuals of type
1
with resampling rate
r_{k}
at time
t
, and

Y_{k}^{N}(t)=\frac{X_{r_{k}}^{N}(Nt)}{N}

(3.3)

the fraction of individuals at time
Nt
of type
1
with resampling rate
r_{k}
. The random process
Y^{N}=(Y^{N}(t))_{t\geq 0}
can now be viewed as
Y^{N}(t)=(Y^{N}_{k}(t))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
living on the state space

Q=\bigl\{y=(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\colon\text{$0\leq y_{k}\leq n_{k}^{N}\,\,\forall\,k\in\mathbb{N}$}\bigr\}.

(3.4)

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we can view (
2.1
) as

T^{k,\Delta}y=y+\Delta\frac{e_{k}}{N},

(3.5)

with
e_{k}
the unit vector in direction
k
, and the transition rates in (
2.2
) as

R^{k,-1}(y)=r_{k}y_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}(n_{l}^{N}-y_{l})}}\right),\qquad R^{k,+1}(y)=r_{k}(n_{k}^{N}-y_{k})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{N}}y_{l}}}\right),

(3.6)

and the generator
L_{N}
in (
2.3
) as

(L_{N}f)(y)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{\Delta\in\{-1,+1\}}R^{k,\Delta}(y)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[f(T^{k,\Delta}y)-f(y)}}\right]N^{2}.

(3.7)

A straightforward Taylor expansion yields

\displaystyle(L^{N}f)(y)

\displaystyle=-N\sum_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{k}-n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}}}\right)f_{k}(y)

(3.8)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{k}-2y_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}+n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}}}\right)f_{kk}(y)+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\biggl(N^{-1}\lvert f\rvert_{3}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}r_{k}\biggr),

where
f_{k}(y)=(\partial/\partial y_{k})f(y)
and
f_{kk}(y)=(\partial/\partial y_{k})^{2}f(y)
.

We see that the first term in the infinitesimal generator of the process
Y^{N}
has a prefactor
N
, and therefore diverges as
N\to\infty
on most of the state space. However, we will be able to show that
Y^{N}
is driven to a subspace on which the first term vanishes and the infinitesimal generator does converge. To account for this properly, we will prove that
Y^{N}
converges in the Meyer-Zheng topology (from Section
2.2
). We need to introduce some further quantities. Let

D^{N}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{n^{N}_{k}}{r_{k}}}}\right)^{-1},\qquad D=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mu_{k}}{r_{k}}}}\right)^{-1}.

(3.9)

By Assumption
1.1
, the strong law of large numbers gives (recall (
1.10

\lim_{N\to\infty}D^{N}=D\quad\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}\text{-a.s.}

(3.10)

Define

(3.11)

Let

be a Fisher-Wright diffusion with diffusion constant

(we will specify the coupling later). Using the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigl\|Y^{N}(t)-S(t)\mathbb{P}\bigr\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|Y^{N}(t)-S^{N}(t)n^{N}\bigr\|_{2}+\bigl\|S^{N}(t)n^{N}-{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)n^{N}\bigr\|_{2}

(3.12)

\displaystyle+\bigl\|{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)n^{N}-{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)\mathbb{P}\bigr\|_{2}+\bigl\|{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)\mathbb{P}-S(t)\mathbb{P}\bigr\|_{2},

is the
\ell_{2}
-norm for vectors. We will bound the right-hand side via a sequence of lemmas summarised as follows.

Lemma 3.1

\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
-a.s. the expectation of each of the four terms in the right-hand side of (

uniformly in

on compacts in
(0,\infty)

Lemma

and

directly, which is therefore not obviously close to Markov. In contrast, for the transformed quantity
Z^{N}(t)
with
Z^{N}_{k}(t)=Y^{N}_{k}(t)/r_{k}
,
k\in N_{R}
, its projection
\check{Z}^{N}(t)
and drift
do align
, making the motion of
\check{Z}^{N}(t)
on the submanifold close to Markov, i.e., the drift term of
\check{Z}^{N}(t)
vanishes (see (
3.34
) below).

(b)

Note that
\check{P}Y^{N}(t)
is just a rescaled version of
\check{Z}^{N}(t)
, and the scaling is chosen so as to bring
\check{P}Y^{N}(t)
back to the neighbourhood of
PY^{N}(t)
. Fortunately, the wind is not too strong, i.e., the direction of the main drift and the projection are close enough so long as
Y^{N}(t)
is close to the submanifold. Hence, the distance between
\check{P}Y^{N}(t)
and
PY^{N}(t)
can be controlled by means of the distance between
Y^{N}(t)
and
PY^{N}(t)
.

(c)

Figure
1
is drawn in correct proportions using the values
n^{N}=(0.37,0.63)
,
Y^{N}(t)=(0.17,0.61)
and
r=(0.8,3.5)
. However, note that the actual drift experienced by the processes is much larger due to the additional factor
N
appearing in their generators, which is not reflected in the figure.

3.4
Third term

Proof.

Since

\bigl\|{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)n^{N}-{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)\mathbb{P}\bigr\|_{2}\leq{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)\bigl\|n^{N}-\mathbb{P}\bigr\|_{2},

(3.30)

the result is a straightforward consequence of the strong law of large numbers for the multinomial distribution and the observation that
{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}(t)\leq 1
.
∎

3.5
Fourth term

The proof uses the following lemma.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
EK86
, Chapter 4, Corollary 8.4]
)
.

Let
(K_{N})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
be any increasing sequence of positive integers. Put
E=[0,1]
and
E_{N}=\{y\in E^{K_{N}}\colon\,y_{l}\leq n^{N}_{l},\,l\in[K_{N}]\}
, both endowed with the respective Euclidean distances. Let
A\subset C_{b}(E)\times C_{b}(E)
be linear and such that the closure of
A
generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on the domain
\mathcal{D}
of
A
(which is assumed to be separating). Let
X
be a Markov process with generator
A
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Y_{N}
be a progressive Markov process on
E_{N}
corresponding to a measurable contraction semigroup with full generator
{\hat{A}}_{N}\subset C_{b}(E_{N})\times C_{b}(E_{N})
. Let
\eta_{N}\colon\,E_{N}\to E
be measurable.

Suppose that, for each
(f,g)\in A
and
T>0
, there exist
(f_{N},g_{N})\in{\hat{A}}_{N}
such that

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{N}[\lvert f_{N}(Y_{N}(t))\rvert]<\infty,\qquad\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{N}[\lvert g_{N}(Y_{N}(t))\rvert]<\infty,

(3.31)

and

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{N}\big[\bigl\lvert f_{N}(Y_{N}(t))-f(\eta_{N}(Y_{N}(t)))\bigr\rvert\big]=0,

(3.32)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{N}\big[\bigl\lvert g_{N}(Y_{N}(t))-g(\eta_{N}(Y_{N}(t))\bigr\rvert\big]=0,

where
\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{N}
denotes expectation with respect to
Y_{N}(t)
. Then the finite-dimensional distributions of
\eta_{N}(Y_{N})
converge to those of
X
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

All spaces are compact, and hence separable and complete. Note that, for each
t
,
\{X_{N}(t)\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is relatively compact, since it is a subset of a compact set. The statement now follows from
[
EK86
, Chapter 4, Corollary 8.4]
after taking
M=\mathcal{D}
therein and using Eq. (8.12) therein.
∎

We are now ready to show that the fourth term vanishes in expectation.

Proof.

Let
{\mathaccent 28692{z}}
be defined by

{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}(y)=n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}\frac{y_{l}}{r_{l}}.

(3.33)

From (
3.8
) (or otherwise) it is not difficult to see that

\displaystyle(L^{N}f)({\mathaccent 28692{z}}(y))

(3.34)

\displaystyle=-N\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{k}-n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}}}\right)\sum_{m\in N_{R}}n^{N}_{m}f_{m}({\mathaccent 28692{z}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{1}{r_{k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{k}-2y_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}+n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}}}\right)\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in N_{R}}n^{N}_{m}n^{N}_{m^{\prime}}f_{mm^{\prime}}({\mathaccent 28692{z}})

\displaystyle+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(N^{-1}\lvert f\rvert_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}-2\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}+\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{n^{N}_{k}}{r_{k}}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}}}\right)\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in N_{R}}n^{N}_{m}n^{N}_{m^{\prime}}f_{mm^{\prime}}({\mathaccent 28692{z}})

\displaystyle+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}),

where we use that
\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}/r_{l}=\sum_{k}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}
. Abbreviate

\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)=y_{k}-n^{N}_{k}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}.

(3.35)

Note that

\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}=\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{y_{k}}{n_{k}}-\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{n^{N}_{k}}{r_{k}}\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l},

(3.36)

and, hence,

\sum_{l\in N_{R}}y_{l}=D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}-D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}.

(3.37)

We can therefore write

\displaystyle(L^{N}f)({\mathaccent 28692{z}}(y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}-2\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}-D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}}}\right)

(3.38)

\displaystyle+(D^{N})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}-D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}}}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k,l\in N_{R}}n^{N}_{k}n^{N}_{l}f_{kl}({\mathaccent 28692{z}})+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}}}\right)\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in N_{R}}n^{N}_{m}n^{N}_{m^{\prime}}f_{mm^{\prime}}({\mathaccent 28692{z}})+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}).

Hence, for
{\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N}(y)=D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle(L^{N}f)({\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N}(y))

(3.39)

\displaystyle=(D^{N})^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-2D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}{\mathaccent 28692{z}}_{k}}}\right)\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}}}\right)f^{\prime\prime}({\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N})

\displaystyle+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}(N^{-1}\lvert f\rvert_{3})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(D^{N}{\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N}(1-{\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N})-\frac{1}{2}D^{N}(1-2{\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N})\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)}{r_{k}}}}\right)f^{\prime\prime}({\mathaccent 28692{s}}^{N})+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\bigl(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}\bigr).

Recall
L
from (
1.5
). Now, let

A=\bigl\{(f,Lf)\colon\,f\in C^{3}([0,1])\bigr\}\subset C_{b}([0,1])\times C_{b}([0,1]).

(3.40)

The fact that the closure of
A
generates a strongly continuous contraction semigroup (i.e., Feller semigroup) corresponding to the Fisher-Wright diffusion on
[0,1]
is standard. Moreover, it is clear that
C^{3}([0,1])
is an algebra and separates points. The processes
Y_{N}
are right-continuous and measurable with respect to their own filtration, hence progressive. Define the linear operator

A_{N}=\bigl\{(f,L^{N}f)\colon\,f\in C^{3}(E_{N})\bigr\}\subset C_{b}(E_{N})\times C_{b}(E_{N}),

(3.41)

the closure of which generates the process
Y_{N}
(we will not need the flexibility of the full generator).

Write
\eta_{N}(y)={\mathaccent 28692{s}}(y)=D^{N}\sum y_{l}/r_{l}
. Let
f\in C^{3}([0,1])
be arbitrary, and let
g=Lf
. Set
f_{N}(y)=f(\eta_{N}(y))
, which clearly is an element of
C^{3}(E_{N})
, and hence an element of the domain of
A_{N}
. The first conditions of (
3.31
) and (
3.32
) are therefore trivially satisfied. With
g_{N}=L^{N}f_{N}
,

\bigl\lvert g(\eta_{N}(y))-g_{N}(y)\bigr\rvert\\
\leq\lvert D^{N}-D\rvert\lvert f\rvert_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\lvert f\rvert_{2}D^{N}\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\frac{1}{r_{k}}\lvert\Delta^{N}_{k}(y)\rvert+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\bigl(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}\bigr).

(3.42)

Consequently, from the above, taking expectations and using (
3.22
), we get

\displaystyle\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{y}\big[\bigl\lvert g(\eta_{N}(Y_{N}(t)))-g_{N}(Y_{N}(t))\bigr\rvert\big]

(3.43)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert D^{N}-D\rvert\lvert f\rvert_{2}+D^{N}\lvert f\rvert_{2}\biggl(M_{N}^{(-2)}\mathop{{}\mathbb{E}}\mathopen{}_{y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{k\in N_{R}}\Delta^{N}_{k}(Y_{N}(t))^{2}}}\right]\biggr)^{1/2}+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\bigl(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert D^{N}-D\rvert\lvert f\rvert_{2}

\displaystyle+D^{N}\lvert f\rvert_{2}\biggl(M_{N}^{(-2)}\bigg(\frac{\Sigma_{N}}{NM^{-}_{N}}+\biggl[g(0)-\frac{\Sigma_{N}}{NM^{-}_{N}}\biggr]\mathrm{e}^{-2NM^{-}_{N}t}\bigg)\biggr)^{1/2}+\mathop{{}\mathrm{O}}\mathopen{}\bigl(N^{-1}\Sigma_{N}\lvert f\rvert_{3}\bigr).

Now, the first term in the right-hand side of (
3.43
) converges to zero as
N\to\infty

\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
-a.s. because of (
3.10
). The second term can be treated in the same way as (
3.29
). Finally, note that there exists a
C
such that, for

, and hence

\frac{\Sigma_{N}}{N}=\frac{\Sigma_{N}M^{-}_{N}}{NM^{-}_{N}}\leq\frac{C\Sigma_{N}M_{N}^{(-2)}}{NM^{-}_{N}}.

(3.44)

Therefore, the second part of (
3.32
) holds
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
-a.s. Finally,

\lvert g_{N}(y)\rvert\leq\lvert Lf(\eta_{N}(y))\rvert+\lvert L^{N}(f\circ\eta_{N})(y)-Lf(\eta_{N}(y))\rvert.

(3.45)

The first term is bounded since
f\in C^{3}([0,1])
and the second term is bounded in expectation due to (
3.43
), which implies the second part of (
3.31
). This implies convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions on the real line.

From
[
BM16
, Theorem 2.1]
we have that, for real-valued stochastic processes, convergence of the finite-dimensional distributions on the real line implies weak convergence with respect to the topology induced by convergence of measure, which is equivalent to the Meyer-Zheng topology, hence no tightness is needed. We conclude that
{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}
converges to
S
weakly with respect to the Meyer-Zheng topology. Since
\Xi
is a Polish space, we can use Skorokhod’s representation theorem to couple
{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}
and
S
such that
{\mathaccent 28692{S}}^{N}
converges to
S
almost surely. As noted after Corollary 2.2 of
[
BM16
]
, convergence of uniformly bounded processes with respect to the convergence in measure topology, implies
L_{2}
convergence, which concludes the proof.
