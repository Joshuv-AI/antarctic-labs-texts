Generalized q q -Stirling numbers and normal ordering
Roberto B. Corcino, Richell O. Celeste, Ken Joffaniel M. Gonzales

Abstract
. The normal ordering coefficients of strings consisting of V , U V,U which satisfy U ​ V = q ​ V ​ U + h ​ V s UV=qVU+hV^{s} ( s ∈ ℕ s\in\mathbb{N} ) are considered. These coefficients are studied in two contexts: first, as a multiple of a sequence satisfying a generalized recurrence, and second, as q q -analogues of rook numbers under the row creation rule introduced by Goldman and Haglund. A number of properties are derived, including recurrences, expressions involving other q q -analogues and explicit formulas. We also give a Dobinsky-type formula for the associated Bell numbers and the corresponding extension of Spivey’s Bell number formula. The coefficients, viewed as rook numbers, are extended to the case s ∈ ℝ s\in\mathbb{R} via a modified rook model.

1.
Introduction

Let
V
and
U
be operators (or variables) that satisfy the commutation relation
[U,V]=UV-VU=1
. Given a string
w
consisting of
U
’s and
V
’s, the
normally ordered
form of
w
is an equivalent operator expressed as
\sum c_{i,j}V^{i}U^{j}
. The normally ordered form may be computed using the commutation relation alone, i.e., by replacing all occurrences of
UV
with
VU+1
, a task which can be cumbersome especially for long strings. It has been shown, however, that the normal ordering coefficients are numbers studied in combinatorics. In quantum physics, for instance, one encounters the boson operators
a
and
a^{\dagger}
which satisfy
[a,a^{\dagger}]=aa^{\dagger}-a^{\dagger}a=1
. It is known that
(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k){a^{\dagger}}^{k}a^{k}
, where the
S(n,k)
are the Stirling numbers of the second kind. Combinatorially, the number
S(n,k)
counts the number of partitions of
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
into
k
disjoint, non-empty subsets. Since only the commutation relation is considered, it follows that any pair of operators that satisfies the same commutation relation will have the same coefficients. One such pair of operators is given by
X
and
D
which act on the monomial
x^{n}
by
Vx^{n}=x^{n+1}
and
Ux^{n}=nx^{n-1}
. Since
[U,V]=1
, then
(VU)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k)V^{k}U^{k}
.

The connection between normal ordering and rook theory was demonstrated by Navon
[
15
]
who proved that the normal ordering coefficients are given by rook numbers on a Ferrers board. Varvak
[
19
]
derived explicit formulas for these coefficients using rook factorization. Blasiak
[
1
]
, and El-Desouky Cakić and Mansour
[
3
]
also computed explicit formulas using other methods. These coefficients may be considered as some form of generalized Stirling numbers.

Generalizations of the classical commutation relation have also been introduced and studied. Katriel and Kibler
[
7
]
considered the
q
-deformed commutation relation
[a,a^{\dagger}]_{q}=aa^{\dagger}-qa^{\dagger}a=1
and showed that the coefficients of
(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}
are a
q
-analogue of the Stirling numbers of the second kind, i.e.,
(a^{\dagger}a)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{q}[n,k]{a^{\dagger}}^{k}a^{k}
. By letting
q=1
, we recover the non-deformed commutation relation and the Stirling numbers of the second kind. Mansour and Schork
[
10
]
extensively studied normal ordering relations of the form
xy=qyx+hf(y)
. For the case where
f(y)=y^{s}
, they derived the normal ordering of the expression
(x+y)^{n}
, thereby extending Newton’s binomial formula. In
[
11
]
, they focused on the coefficients of
(VU)^{n}
, where
V,U
satisfy
UV-VU=hV^{s}
and obtained properties of the corresponding generalization of Stirling numbers and Bell numbers. They continued the study of these numbers in
[
12
]
where they obtained more properties and introduced a
q
-analogue through the relation
UV-qVU=hV^{s}
. (We also mention that the papers
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
also contain an excellent account of some literature on the subject.)

In this paper, we continue the study of normal ordering coefficients given the commutation relation
UV-qVU=hV^{s}
where
s\in\mathbb{N}
. The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we study the properties of the coefficients of
(VU)^{n}
in the context of a sequence satisfying a generalized recurrence relation. In Section
3
, we obtain explicit formulas for the normal ordering coefficients of arbitrary strings
H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}=V^{r_{n}}U^{s_{n}}\cdots V^{r_{2}}U^{s_{2}}V^{r_{1}}U^{s_{1}}
using an interpretation of normal ordering in terms of rook placements. In Section
4
, we resume to the coefficients of
(VU)^{n}
and further exploit the rook model to obtain identities for the associated Bell numbers. Finally, in Section
5
, we extend the coefficients to the case
s\in\mathbb{R}
using a modified rook model.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the
q
-analogue of
x
by
[x]_{q}=\frac{q^{x}-1}{q-1}

and

Let
h\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
. For
U,V
that satisfy the commutation relation
UV-qVU=hV^{s}
. Mansour, Schork and Shattuck
[
11
]
showed that the coefficients
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
in

(VU)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{s,h,q}[n,k]V^{sn-(s-1)k}U^{k}

satisfy the recurrence relation

(1)

S_{s,h,q}[n,k]=q^{s(n-1)-(s-1)(k-1)}S_{s,h,q}[n-1,k-1]+h[s(n-1)-(s-1)k]_{q}S_{s,h,q}[n-1,k]\,,

with initial conditions
S_{s,h,q}[n,0]=S_{s,h,q}[0,n]=\delta_{0,n}
. If
q=1
, we obtain the commutation relation
UV-VU=hV^{s}
and the numbers
S_{s,h}(n,k)
which were studied in
[
11
]
. Mansour, Schork and Shattuck
[
12
]
also showed that the number
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
can be expressed in terms of
b_{n,k}=q^{(s-1)\binom{k}{2}-s\binom{n}{2}}S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
where
b_{n,k}
satisfies

(2)

b_{n,k}=b_{n-1,k-1}+(h/q)([s(n-1)]_{1/q}-[(s-1)k]_{1/q})b_{n-1,k}\,.

The recurrence (
2
) is useful in deriving some basic properties. However, we will find it more convenient to work on a more general setting. Specifically, let
\textbf{v}=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots)
and
\textbf{w}=(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots)
be sequences from a ring
K
and define the numbers

A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}
by the recurrence relation

(3)

A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}=A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n-1,k-1}+(v_{n-1}+w_{k})A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n-1,k}\,,

with initial conditions
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{0,n}=\delta_{0,n}
and
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,0}=(v_{n-1}+w_{0})(v_{n-2}+w_{0})\cdots(v_{0}+w_{0})
. We also call
v
and
w
weight functions. When only the value of a weight function at
i=0,1,\ldots
is specified (for instance,
v_{i}
), it is understood that the corresponding weight function is the same letter in boldface without the subscripts. If
v_{i}=[si]_{1/q},w_{i}=-[(s-1)i]_{1/q}
, then by (
2
),
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}=(h/q)^{-(n-k)}q^{(s-1)\binom{k}{2}-s\binom{n}{2}}S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
.

We mention that for linear weight functions, the recurrence relation (
3
) has been considered by Xu
[
20
]
, Hsu and Shuie
[
5
]
and El-Desouky and Cakić
[
4
]
. Some
q
- and
p,q
-analogues have also been studied by Corcino, Hsu and Tan
[
2
]
, and Remmel and Wachs
[
16
]
(although we note that the type-II
p,q
-analogue in
[
16
]
does not seem to fall as
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}
). An explicit formula was obtained in
[
9
]
and
[
20
]
, which is given by

A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(w_{j}+v_{i})}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0,\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}(w_{j}-w_{i})}\,,

provided that the
w_{i}
’s are distinct. For
s\neq 1
, the formula above gives us (see
[
12
, Theorem 45]
)

S_{s,h,q}[n,k]=h^{n-k}q^{s\binom{n}{2}-(s-1)\binom{k}{2}-(n-k)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}([si]_{1/q}-[(s-1)j]_{1/q})}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0,\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}([(s-1)i]-[(s-1)j]_{1/q})}\,.

The theorem that follows gives different formulations for the numbers
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}
in terms of expressions that are analogous to elementary and complete symmetric functions.

Theorem 1
.

The following identities hold:

(4)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<...<i_{n-k}\leq n-1}~\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}(v_{i_{j}}+w_{i_{j}-j+1})

(5)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq...\leq i_{n-k}\leq k}~\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}(v_{i_{j}+j-1}+w_{i_{j}})

(6)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{0}+i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{k}=n-k}~\prod_{j=0}^{k}~\prod_{l=0}^{i_{j}-1}(v_{j+l+i_{0}+i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{j-1}}+w_{j})\,.

Proof.

It can be shown by partitioning the set of indices that the
RHS
of (
4
) and (
5
) satisfy the recursion (
3
). Identity (
6
) is a restatement of (
5
). ∎

Let
H
be a set. The
m
-th
elementary symmetric function

e
and
m
-th
complete symmetric function

are defined as follows:
e_{m}(H)
(resp.

) is the sum of all products of
m

0

4

A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n,k}=e_{n-k}(\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}\})
and by (

) or (
6

A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{v}}_{n,k}=h_{n-k}(\{v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\})
.

In the next theorem, we denote the matrix whose
(n,k)
-th entry is

by
\left[A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}\right]
.

Theorem 2
.

The following identities hold:

(1)

A matrix factorization

(7)

\displaystyle\left[A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}\right]=\left[A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{0}}}_{n,k}\right]\left[A^{\textnormal{{0}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}\right]\mbox{, or equivalently, }~~A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{0}}}_{n,j}A^{\textnormal{{0}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{j,k}

(2)

Orthogonality relation

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}A^{\textnormal{{-w}},\textnormal{{-v}}}_{k,m}

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,m}

(3)

A pair of inverse relations

(9)

\displaystyle(x+v_{0})(x+v_{1})\cdots(x+v_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}(x-w_{0})(x-w_{1})\cdots(x-w_{k-1})

(10)

\displaystyle(x-w_{0})(x-w_{1})\cdots(x-w_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{-w}},\textnormal{{-v}}}_{n,k}(x+v_{0})(x+v_{1})\cdots(x+v_{k-1})

(4)

Convolution formula

(11)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{l+m,n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{l,k}A^{\textnormal{{v}}_{+l},\textnormal{{w}}_{+k}}_{m,n-k}\,,\mbox{~where for any weight function $\textnormal{{f}}$, we define
$\textnormal{{f}}_{+m}=(f_{m},f_{m+1},\ldots)$}\,.

Proof.

We prove the second equation in (
7
) by showing that the quantity on the
RHS
satisfies the recurrence relation (
3

. By (

\displaystyle A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n,j}A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=(A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n-1,j-1}+v_{n-1}A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n-1,j})(A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j-1,k-1}+w_{k}A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j-1,k})

\displaystyle=A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n-1,j-1}A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j-1,k-1}+v_{n-1}A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n-1,j}A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j,k}+w_{k}A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n-1,j-1}A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{j-1,k}

Substituting this into (

For the orthogonality relation (
8
), we use
\left[A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{v}}_{n,k}\right]^{-1}=\left[(-1)^{n-k}A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n,k}\right]=\left[A^{\textbf{-v},\textbf{0}}_{n,k}\right]

\left[A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}\right]^{-1}=\left[A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{w}}_{n,k}\right]^{-1}\left[A^{\textbf{v},\textbf{0}}_{n,k}\right]^{-1}=\left[A^{\textbf{-w},\textbf{0}}_{n,k}\right]\left[A^{\textbf{0},\textbf{-v}}_{n,k}\right]=\left[A^{\textbf{-w},\textbf{-v}}_{n,k}\right]
.

An inductive argument and the recursion (
3
) proves (
9
). Identity (
8
) then establishes the equivalence of (
9
) with (
10
). For (
11

4
) and break each
(l+m-n)
-tuple of indices into two, namely, into those that satisfy

and

\displaystyle\sum_{l\leq i_{l-k+1}<\ldots<i_{l+m-n}\leq m+l-1}~\prod_{j=l-k+1}^{l+m-n}(v_{i_{j}}+w_{i_{j}-(j-1)})

\displaystyle=A^{\textbf{v}_{+l},\textbf{w}_{+k}}_{m,n-k}\,.

By Identity (
7

S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
in terms of other
q
-Stirling numbers. Note
that the second kind of this analogue is different from the

-analogue

S_{q}[n,k]=q^{k-1}S_{q}[n-1,k-1]+[k]_{q}S_{q}[n-1,k]\,.

On the other hand, the other

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{q}[n,k]

\displaystyle=\hat{S}_{q}[n-1,k-1]+[k]_{q}\hat{S}_{q}[n-1,k]

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{q}[n,k]

\displaystyle=\hat{c}_{q}[n-1,k-1]+[n-1]_{q}\hat{c}_{q}[n-1,k]\,.

with
\hat{S}_{q}[n,0]=\hat{S}_{q}[0,n]=\hat{c}_{q}[n,0]=\hat{c}_{q}[0,n]=\delta_{n,0}
. Using these recurrence relations, we see that when

,

and

Corollary 3
.

The numbers
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
satisfy

(12)

S_{s,h,q}[n,k]=h^{(n-k)}q^{s\binom{n}{2}-(s-1)\binom{k}{2}-n+k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}[s]_{1/q}^{n-j}(-[s-1]_{1/q})^{j-k}\hat{c}_{1/{q^{s}}}[n,j]\hat{S}_{1/{q^{s-1}}}[j,k]\,.

Consequently, the numbers
S_{s,h}(n,k)
are given by

S_{s,h}(n,k)=h^{n-k}\sum_{j=k}^{n}s^{n-j}(1-s)^{j-k}c(n,j)S(j,k)\,.

where
c(n,k)
and
S(n,k)
are the (classical) Stirling numbers of the first kind and second kind, respectively.

Proof.

The result follows from (
7
) and the property
[si]_{1/q}=[s]_{1/q}[i]_{1/q^{s}}
,
[(s-1)i]_{1/q}=[s-1]_{1/q}[i]_{1/q^{s-1}}
. ∎

Define
c_{s,h,q}[n,k]
by the recursion

c_{s,h,q}[n,k]=q^{(s-1)(n-1)-s(k-1)}c_{s,h,q}[n-1,k-1]+h[(s-1)(n-1)-sk]_{q}c_{s,h,q}[n-1,k]\,,

with initial conditions
c_{s,h,q}[n,0]=c_{s,h,q}[0,n]=\delta_{0,n}
. The orthogonality relation for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
involves
c_{s,h,q}[n,k]
and is given in the next corollary.

Corollary 4
.

For
m\leq n
, we have the following orthogonality relations

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m}^{n}S_{s,h,q}[n,k]c_{s,h,q}[k,m]

\displaystyle=\delta_{m,n}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m}^{n}c_{s,h,q}[n,k]S_{s,h,q}[k,m]

\displaystyle=\delta_{m,n}\,.

Proof.

Let
v_{i}=[si]_{1/q},w_{i}=-[(s-1)i]_{1/q}
. One can verify that
A^{\textbf{-w},\textbf{-v}}_{n,k}=(h/q)^{-(n-k)}q^{s\binom{k}{2}-(s-1)\binom{n}{2}}c_{s,h,q}[n,k]
. The orthogonality relation (
8
) implies that

\sum_{k=m}^{n}(h/q)^{-(n-k)}q^{(s-1)\binom{k}{2}-s\binom{n}{2}}S_{s,h,q}[n,k](h/q)^{-(k-m)}q^{s\binom{m}{2}-(s-1)\binom{k}{2}}c_{s,h,q}[k,m]=\delta_{m,n}\,.

After clearing the powers of
h
and
q
we obtain the desired result. ∎

The next theorem gives some recurrence relations for

Theorem 5
.

The following recurrence relations for
A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}
hold

(13)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{j-1,k-1}\prod_{i=j}^{n-1}(v_{i}+w_{k})

(14)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}(-1)^{j-k}A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n+1,j+1}\prod_{i=k+1}^{j}(v_{n}+w_{i})

(15)

\displaystyle A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(v_{n-j-1}+w_{k-j})A^{\textnormal{{v}},\textnormal{{w}}}_{n-j-1,k-j}\,.

Proof.

The proof of the three identities uses (
4
).

For (
13
), we use the fact that for every
(n-k)
-tuple of indices
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-k})
, there exists a unique

such that

and
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-k})
satisfies
0\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{j-k}\leq j-2
. A similar approach proves (
15
).

To prove identity (
14
), let
I_{j}
be the set of
(n-j)
-tuples of indices satisfying
0\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{n-j}\leq n
. Let

I^{\prime}_{j}=I_{j}\times(\underbrace{n,\ldots,n}_{j-k})
and
\mathcal{I}
be the multiset consisting of the union of all
I^{\prime}_{j}
’s,
k\leq j\leq n
. Assign the weight
(-1)^{j-k}
to each element of
\mathcal{I}
belonging to
I^{\prime}_{j}
. Then each
(n-k)
-tuple in
\mathcal{I}
which does not satisfy

occurs twice but with weights that are negatives of each other. ∎

Corollary 6
.

The following recurrence relations for

hold

(16)

\displaystyle S_{s,h,q}[n,k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}h^{n-j}q^{s(j-1)-(s-1)(k-1)}S_{s,h,q}[j-1,k-1]\prod_{i=j}^{n-1}[si-(s-1)k]_{q}

(17)

\displaystyle S_{s,h,q}[n,k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=k}^{n}(-h)^{j-k}q^{sn(k-j-1)+(s-1)\binom{j+1}{2}-(s-1)\binom{k}{2}}S_{s,h,q}[n+1,j+1]\prod_{i=k+1}^{j}[sn-(s-1)i]_{q}

\displaystyle S_{s,h,q}[n,k]

3.
Explicit Formulas

Let
\textbf{r}=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{n}),\textbf{s}=(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})
. We will use
|\cdot|
to denote the sum of the elements of a finite sequence. We now consider the normal ordering of strings of the form
H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}=V^{r_{n}}U^{s_{n}}\cdots V^{r_{2}}U^{s_{2}}V^{r_{1}}U^{s_{1}}
which can be written in the form

(22)

H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}=\sum_{k=s_{1}}^{|\textbf{s}|}S^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}_{s,h,q}[k]V^{|\textbf{r}|-(|\textbf{s}|-k)(1-s)}U^{k}\,.

In this section, we obtain explicit formulas for the numbers
S^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}_{s,h,q}[k]
.

Our first identity is a
q
-analogue of the following explicit formula for
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{0,1,1}(k)=S^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}(k)
which was computed by El-Desouky, Cakić and Mansour
[
3
]
by repeated application of the Leibniz formula

(23)

S^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}(k)=\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n-1}=s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n}-k}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{s_{i+1}}{j_{i}}\left(r_{1}+\cdots+r_{i}-(j_{1}+\cdots+j_{i-1})\right)^{(\underline{j_{i}})}

where
x^{(\underline{j})}=x(x-1)\cdots(x-(j-1))
denotes the falling factorial. Our proof uses a rook theoretic interpretation of normal ordering. This interpretation was pointed out by Varvak
[
19
, Section 7]
and is similar to the row creation rule introduced by Goldman and Haglund
[
6
]
.

The process of computing the normal ordering of a string
w
can be considered as forming the collection of finite sequences of two operations, namely the conversion
UV
to
qVU
and
UV
to
hV^{s}
, starting from the rightmost
UV
, such that successive applications of each element in the sequence produces a different string, until a string in normally ordered form is obtained. Let us denote the two operations by
\alpha
and
\beta
, respectively. Applying a sequence containing
k

\beta
’s results to an expression
cV^{|\textbf{r}|-k(1-s)}U^{|\textbf{s}|-k}
. The sum of all such coefficients
c
is then the coefficient of
V^{|\textbf{r}|-k(1-s)}U^{|\textbf{s}|-k}
in the normal ordering of
H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}
. The process we just described can be translated in terms of rook placements on a Ferrers board (or simply, board).

Let
U
correspond to a horizontal step and
V
a vertical step. Then
w
outlines a board which we denote by
B(w)
. The conversion
\alpha
corresponds to leaving a cell empty while the conversion
\beta
corresponds to placing a rook on a cell such that the rook cancels all cells on top of it and divides the row to its left into
s
rows. Note that if
s=0
, then a placement of a rook converts a row into zero rows, or equivalently, it cancels all the cells to its left. We denote a rook by marking a cell with “
\bullet
” and a canceled cell with “
\times
”. Since the
\beta
’s convert the rightmost
UV
, the rooks are placed in some chosen columns from right to left. We can think of a cell lying in a divided row as containing
subcells
, with each cell containing 1 subcell by default. A canceled cell is a assigned the weight 1 while a cell containing a rook is assigned the weight
h
. All other cells are assigned the weight
q^{a}
, where
a
is the number of subcells in a cell. The weight of the rook placement is the product of the weight of the cells. Finally, the normally ordered string resulting from a particular rook placement is
\omega V^{i}U^{j}
, where
\omega
is the weight of the rook placement,
i
is the number of rows in the leftmost column plus
s-1
if the leftmost column contains a rook, and
j
is the number of columns not containing rooks.
Note that the original model by Goldman and Haglund
[
6
]
involves creating
s
new rows to the left of a cell containing a rook and then canceling the original row. The model we described involves dividing the row into
s
rows, ie., creating
s-1
new rows without canceling the original row. It is apparent that these models are equivalent, the only other notable difference being the orientation of the boards. Alternatively, we also say the placement of a rook in a cell adds
s-1
subcells to every cell lying to its left. Let us call the rook placement rule we just described as the
row creation rule
.

Figure
1
shows a rook placement on
B(w)
, where
w=V^{2}U^{3}V^{3}U^{2},s=2
. This placement corresponds to applying the sequence

(\alpha,\alpha,\alpha,\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\alpha,\alpha,\beta,\alpha)
from right to left. The string
h^{2}q^{8}V^{7}U^{3}
is produced.

Let
B
be a board. Denote by
C_{k}(B;s)
the collection of all placements of
k
rooks on
B
under the row creation rule. For a rook placement
\phi\in C_{k}(B,s)
, denote the weight of
\phi
by
\omega(\phi)
. We define the
rook number

R_{s,h,q}[B,k]
by

(24)

R_{s,h,q}[B,k]=\sum_{\phi\in C_{k}(B;s)}\omega(\phi)\,.

One sees that the number of rooks completely determines the exponents of
V
and
U
in the word resulting from the rook placement. In particular, if
k
rooks are placed, then
k
columns are cancelled and
k(s-1)
rows are added. Hence, we can write
H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}
as

(25)

H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}=\sum_{k=0}^{|\textbf{s}|-s_{1}}R_{s,h,q}[B(H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}),k]V^{|\textbf{r}|-k(1-s)}U^{|\textbf{s}|-k}\,.

Comparing this with (
22
) gives
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]=R_{s,h,q}[B(H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}),|\textbf{s}|-k]
. It is important to note that
R_{s,h,q}[B(H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}),|\textbf{s}|-k]=0
when
k>|\textbf{s}|-s_{1}
since the number of rooks cannot exceed the number of rows of positive length. This is also reflected in the fact that the exponent of
U
in the normal ordering of
H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}
is between
s_{1}
and
|\textbf{s}|
, or equivalently, that
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]=0
when
k<s_{1}
or
k>|\textbf{s}|
.

The following lemma will be used in deriving an analogue of (

Lemma 9
.

We have

U^{s^{\prime}}V^{r^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=0}^{s^{\prime}}\left(h^{j}q^{r^{\prime}(s^{\prime}-j)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{s'}{j}_{q^{s-1}}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}[r^{\prime}+i(s-1)]_{q}\right)V^{r^{\prime}+j(s-1)}U^{s^{\prime}-j}\,.

Proof.

Suppose that
j
columns have been chosen where rooks will be placed. If the first rook is placed on the cell in the
i
th row,
1\leq i\leq r^{\prime}
, then the cells below the rook will contribute a weight of
q^{i-1}
. As
i
varies, a total weight of
h(1+q+\cdots+q^{r^{\prime}-1})=h[r^{\prime}]_{q}
will be contributed by all possible placements of the first rook. Since the placement of the first rook adds
s-1
subcells to every cell to its left, the total weight contributed by all possible placement of the second rook is
[r^{\prime}+(s-1)]_{q}
. Continuing this process with the other columns, we see that the weight contributed by all possible placements of
j
rooks in the chosen columns is
h^{j}\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}[r+i(s-1)]_{q}
, and that this weight is the same for any choice of
j
columns.

We now consider the weight contributed by the other columns in which no rooks are placed. For such a column, the weight is completely determined by the number of columns to its right that contains a rook, i.e., if there are
t
columns to its right containing a rook, then the column will assume a weight of
q^{r^{\prime}+t(s-1)}
. Note that
t
varies from
0
to
j
and that for a given placement of
j
rooks, the weight contributed by all the columns containing no rooks is
q^{r^{\prime}t_{0}}q^{(r^{\prime}+(s-1))t_{1}}q^{(r^{\prime}+2(s-1))t_{2}}\cdots q^{(r^{\prime}+j(s-1))t_{j}}
for some
t_{0}+t_{1}+\cdots+t_{j}=s^{\prime}-j
. Summing this up on all such possible collections
\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{i}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t_{0}+t_{1}+\cdots+t_{j}=s^{\prime}-j}

\displaystyle q^{r^{\prime}t_{0}}q^{(r^{\prime}+(s-1))t_{1}}q^{(r^{\prime}+2(s-1))t_{2}}\cdots q^{(r^{\prime}+j(s-1))t_{j}}

\displaystyle=q^{r^{\prime}(s^{\prime}-j)}\sum_{t_{0}+t_{1}+\cdots+t_{j}=s^{\prime}-j}q^{0(s-1)t_{0}}q^{1(s-1)t_{1}}q^{2(s-1)t_{2}}\cdots q^{j(s-1)t_{j}}

\displaystyle=q^{r^{\prime}(s^{\prime}-j)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{s'}{j}_{q^{s-1}}\,,

by (
6
) with
v_{i}=0
and
w_{i}=q^{i(s-1)}
. This proves the lemma. ∎

4.

The Bell polynomial
B_{n}(x)
is defined as the sum
\sum_{k=0}^{n}S(n,k)x^{k}
while the Bell number

\textbf{r}=(1,1,\ldots,1),\textbf{s}=(1,1,\ldots,1)

. The numbers

Corollary 14

By the property

when

\displaystyle B^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}_{s,h,q}[hx]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=s_{1}}^{|\textbf{s}|}S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]h^{k}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]h^{k}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{h^{|\textnormal{{s}}|}}{[k]_{q,1-s}!}(-1)^{k-j}q^{\binom{k-j}{2}(1-s)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}_{q^{1-s}}\Omega^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,q}[j]x^{k}\,.

Using
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}_{q^{1-s}}=\frac{[k]_{q^{1-s}}!}{[j]_{q^{1-s}}![k-j]_{q^{1-s}}!}=\frac{[k]_{q,1-s}!}{[j]_{q,1-s}![k-j]_{q,1-s}!}
and the Cauchy product rule,

\displaystyle B^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}_{s,h,q}[hx]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left(\frac{h^{|\textnormal{{s}}|}(-1)^{k-j}q^{\binom{k-j}{2}(1-s)}}{[k-j]_{q,1-s}!}\right)\left(\frac{\Omega^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,q}[j]}{[j]_{q,1-s}!}\right)x^{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h^{|\textbf{s}|}(-1)^{j}q^{\binom{j}{2}(1-s)}\frac{x^{j}}{[j]_{q,1-s}!}\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Omega^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,q}[j]\frac{x^{j}}{[j]_{q,1-s}!}\right)\,.

Our goal in the remainder of this section is to obtain

(32)

\displaystyle B_{s,h}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}h^{n-r-1}\binom{n-1}{r}B_{s,h}(r)\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}(1+si)

(33)

\displaystyle B_{s,h}(n+m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}h^{n-r}\binom{n}{r}S(m,j)B_{s,h}(r)\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-1}(j(1-s)+sm+si)\,.

B(n+m)=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{n}{r}S(m,j)B(r)j^{n-r}\,.

Lemma 15
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi\in C_{k}(J_{n};s)
. Then, there exists a unique (possibly empty) collection
\mathcal{C}
of columns in
\phi
such that if
|\mathcal{C}|=m+1
, then (a) each of these columns has a rook in the bottom
1,1+s,1+2s,\ldots,1+ms
subcells and (b) every column not in
\mathcal{C}
contains at least
1+st
uncanceled subcells not containing a rook, where
t
is the number of columns in
\mathcal{C}
to the right of that column.

Proof.

Let
\phi\in C_{k}(J_{n};s)
. The set
\mathcal{C}
may be obtained as follows. Let
c_{1}
be the first column of
\phi
from the right containing a rook in the bottom cell. If
c_{1}
does not exist, then
\mathcal{C}=\varnothing
and
m=-1
. If
c_{1}
exists, then all columns to the right of
c_{1}
have at least 1 uncanceled cell not containing a rook. Let
c_{2}
be the first column to the left of
c_{1}
containing a rook in the bottom
1+s
subcells. If
c_{2}
does not exist, then all columns to the left of
c_{1}
contain at least
1+s
uncanceled subcells not containing a rook. Hence,
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1}\}
satisfies (a) and (b) and
m=0
. Otherwise, if such a column
c_{2}
exists, then all columns to the right of
c_{2}
and to the left of
c_{1}
contain
1+s
uncanceled subcells not containing a rook. Let
c_{3}
be the first column to the left of
c_{2}
containing a rook in the bottom
1+2s
subcells. If
c_{3}
, does not exist, then
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
satisfies (a) and (b) and
m=1
. We repeat the process with the succeeding columns as long as needed until all the elements of
\mathcal{C}
are determined. This process shows both the existence and uniqueness of
\mathcal{C}
, which proves the lemma. ∎

Theorem 16
.

Let

. Then

(35)

S_{s,h,q}[n,k]=\sum_{r=k-1}^{n-1}h^{n-r-1}q^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r}_{q^{s}}S_{s,h,q}[r,k-1]\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}\,.

Furthermore, the numbers

are given by

(36)

B_{s,h,q}[n]=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}h^{n-r-1}q^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r}_{q^{s}}B_{s,h,q}[r]\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}\,.

Proof.

We first prove (
35
). The number
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
equals the sum of the weights of all rook placements in
C_{n-k}(J_{n};s)
. Let
R_{n-k}(J_{n};s;n-r-2)
be the subset of

consisting of rook placements that satisfy conditions (a) and (b) in Lemma
15
with
m=n-r-2
. Then, the same lemma implies that the collection
\{R_{n-k}(J_{n};s;n-r-2)\,|\,k-1\leq r<n-1\}
forms a partition of

. We want to show that

\sum_{\phi\in R_{n-k}(J_{n};s;n-r-2)}\omega(\phi)=h^{n-r-1}q^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r}_{q^{s}}S_{s,h,q}[r,k-1]\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}\,,

from which (
35
) follows.

For
\phi\in R_{n-k}(J_{n};s;n-r-2)
, denote its set of columns satisfying the conditions in Lemma
15
by
\mathcal{C}_{\phi}
. Let

\lambda_{\mathcal{C}_{\phi}}
be the cells of
\phi
consisting of its cells of the columns
\mathcal{C}_{\phi}
and the bottom
st+1
cells of the other columns, where
t
is the number of columns from
\mathcal{C}_{\phi}
that lie to the right of one such column. Also, let
\phi-\lambda_{\mathcal{C}_{\phi}}
be the cells of
\phi
not in
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}_{\phi}}
. One sees that the cells in

form a rook placement in
C_{r-k+1}(J_{r};s)
. We can therefore write every rook placement
\phi\in R_{n-k}(J_{n};s;n-r-2)
uniquely as a pair
(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}},\rho)
, for some set of columns
\mathcal{C}
satisfying Lemma
15
with
m=n-r-2
, and some rook placement
\rho
in
C_{r-k+1}(J_{r};s)
. The sum of the weights of rook placements in
C_{r-k+1}(J_{r};s)
is
S_{s,h,q}[r,k-1]
. We now compute the sum of the weights of the cells in
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
over all such possible set of columns
\mathcal{C}
, which we denote by
L_{n-r-2}
.

Clearly, the cells in
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
which contain rooks contribute a weight of
h^{n-r-1}\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}
. In addition,
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
contains
r
bottom cells, which collectively contribute a weight of
q^{r}
. The weight contributed by the remaining cells depends on the location of the rooks. To get a better picture of how the contribution by the remaining cells varies, let us distribute the cells of
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
so that the
s
subcells lie to the left of each rook. We illustrate this in Figure

for
n=9
and
n-r-2=3
. Here, the second column of
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
from the left lies to the left of three columns of
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
containing rooks. Hence, this column contains
1+3s
bottom cells. We moved each of the three

subcells (indicated by marking a cell with an

t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n-r-1}
be the number of cells marked

starting from bottommost row. Then the

0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\ldots\leq t_{n-r-1}\leq r
. Hence, if
\omega(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}})
denotes the product of the weights of the cells in
\lambda_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{C}\in L_{n-r-2}}\omega(\lambda_{\mathcal{C}})

\displaystyle=h^{n-r-1}q^{r}\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}\sum_{0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\ldots\leq t_{n-r-1}\leq r}q^{st_{1}}q^{st_{2}}\cdots q^{st_{n-r-1}}

where the second equality follows from (

To prove (

. ∎

.

Let

. We have

S_{s,h,q}[n+m,k]=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}h^{n-r}q^{r(j(1-s)+sm)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r}_{q^{s}}S_{s,h,q}[m,j]S_{s,h,q}[r,k-j]\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-1}[j(1-s)+sm+si]_{q}\,.

(38)

B_{s,h,q}[n+m]=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}h^{n-r}q^{r(j(1-s)+sm)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r}_{q^{s}}S_{s,h,q}[m,j]B_{s,h,q}[r]\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-1}[j(1-s)+sm+si]_{q}\,.

Corollary 19
.

The recurrence relation for the generalized
q
-Bell polynomials is given by

B_{q}[n+m;x]=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}h^{n-r}q^{r(j(1-s)+sm)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r}_{q^{s}}S_{s,h,q}[m,j]B_{q}[r;x]x^{j}\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-1}[j(1-s)+sm+si]_{q}\,.

In particular,

B_{s,h,q}[n;x]=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}h^{n-r-1}q^{r}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{r}_{q^{s}}B_{s,h,q}[r;x]x\prod_{i=0}^{n-r-2}[1+si]_{q}\,.

Moreover, a
q
-analogue of Spivey’s identity (
34
) is

B_{q}[n+m]=\sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{m}q^{rj}\binom{n}{r}S_{s,h,q}[m,j]B_{q}[r][j]_{q}^{n-r}

where we take
0^{0}=1
.

5.
The numbers
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]
and
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
for
s\in\mathbb{R}

Goldman and Haglund introduced, in the latter part of their paper
[
6
]
, a rook placement rule which is defined for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, such that the corresponding rook numbers agree with the rook numbers under the row creation rule when
s\in\mathbb{N}
. We give an presentation of this rule using the concept of
pre-weights
. As before, we place the rooks in some chosen columns from right to left such that at most one rook occupies each column. We say that every cell has a default pre-weight of 1 and a placement of a rook adds a pre-weight of
s-1
to every cell to its left. Every cell lying above a rook is assigned the weight 1. If a cell does not lie above a rook and has pre-weight
p
, then it is assigned the weight
h[p]_{q}
if it contains a rook, and
q^{p}
if otherwise. Let us call the rook placement rule we just described as the
pre-weight rule
. We denote the weight of a rook placement
\psi
by
\omega(\psi)
, which is defined as the product of the weights of the cells. Given a board
B
, we denote by
C^{*}_{k}(B;s)
the collection of all placement of
k
rooks on
B
under the pre-weight rule. When
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the pre-weight of a cell is exactly the number of its subcells. In this case, we have

R_{s,h,q}[B(w),k]=\sum_{\phi\in C_{k}(B;s)}\omega(\phi)=\sum_{\psi\in C^{*}_{k}(B;s)}\omega(\psi)\,.

We can therefore define
R[B(w),k]
as

R_{s,h,q}[B(w),k]=\sum_{\psi\in C^{*}_{k}(B;s)}\omega(\psi)\,.

when
s\in\mathbb{R}
without ambiguity. Using this definition, we can extend numbers the
S^{\textbf{r},\textbf{s}}_{s,h,q}[k]
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
by defining them as
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]=R_{s,h,q}[B(H_{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}),|\textbf{s}|-k]
. Analogously, we define
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]=R_{s,h,q}[B((VU)^{n}),|\textbf{s}|-k]
. It can be shown that under this definition,
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
satisfies the same recursion(
1
). Since the identities for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
in Section
2
were derived using this recursion, all of them also hold when
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that the numbers
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[n,k]
lose their interpretation as normal ordering coefficients when
s
is not a nonnegative integer.

The proof of Lemma
9
, and hence, of Theorem
10
(explicit formula for
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]
) and Corollary
11
(explicit formula for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
) extends readily under the pre-weight rule. We leave the details to the reader. On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
12
(explicit formula for
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]
), and hence, of Corollary
13
(explicit formula for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
) and Corollary
14
(Dobinsky-type formula), uses the rook factorization (
30
), which was computed using the normal ordering interpretation. The result
[
6
, Theorem 7.1]
furnishes the needed rook factorization for
h=1
, which will allow us to extend the said theorem and its corollaries. This result can be extended to arbitrary
h
without much difficulty.

To prove Theorem
16
(recursion for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
and
B_{s,h,q}[n]
), Theorem
18
(recursion for
S_{s,h,q}[n+m,k]
and
B_{s,h,q}[n+m]
) and Corollary
19
for the case
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we impose a modification in the assignment of pre-weights when the board is
J_{n}
. First, as usual, choose columns where we will place rooks. The first rook adds a pre-weight of
s-1
to each cell that lies above the bottom cell in every column to its left, the second rook adds a pre-weight of
s-1
to the cell that lies above the cell in every column to its left which has been added a pre-weight of
s-1
by the first rook, etc. We denote by
C^{\#}_{k}(J_{n};s)
the set of all rook placements on
J_{n}
under this rule, which we call the
modified pre-weight rule
. This modification preserves the sum of the weights of all rook placements given a choice of columns where rooks are to be placed. Hence,

(39)

\sum_{\psi\in C^{*}_{k}(J_{n};s)}\omega(\psi)=\sum_{\rho\in C^{\#}_{k}(J_{n};s)}\omega(\rho)\,.

6.
Conclusion

We have obtained a number of identities for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
, including an orthogonality relation, recurrence formulas and expressions involving other
q
-analogues. In particular, it was shown that
S_{s,h}(n,k)
can be written in terms of the classical Stirling numbers and
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
in terms of
q
-Stirling numbers. Explicit formulas for
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]
, and consequently,
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
, were also given. Recurrence relations for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
and
B_{s,h,q}[n]
have also been proved using rook placements. As a consequence, we have also provided alternative proofs of the original identities in
[
11
]
which were proved using weighted Laguerre configurations. It is possible that the identities for
S_{s,h,q}[n,k]
in Section
2
also have rook theoretic proofs. When viewed as normal ordering coefficients, the numbers
S^{\textnormal{{r}},\textnormal{{s}}}_{s,h,q}[k]
are defined only for
s\in\mathbb{N}
. These numbers, as well as the identities they possess which were derived in the earlier sections, were extended to the case
s\in\mathbb{R}
using a modified rook model.

We have not considered the corresponding boson operators and their action on coherent states that arise as a consequence of the generalized commutation relation. We note that the
q
-deformed case where
h=1,s=0
was studied by Schork
[
17
]
while Blasiak
[
1
]
studied the undeformed case and obtained the normal ordering of a more general class of expressions involving the boson operators.
