Robust Implicit Regularization via Weight Normalization
Hung-Hsu Chou, Holger Rauhut, Rachel Ward

Abstract
Overparameterized models may have many interpolating solutions; implicit regularization refers to the hidden preference of a particular optimization method towards a certain interpolating solution among the many. A by now established line of work has shown that (stochastic) gradient descent tends to have an implicit bias towards low rank and/or sparse solutions when used to train deep linear networks, explaining to some extent why overparameterized neural network models trained by gradient descent tend to have good generalization performance in practice. However, existing theory for square-loss objectives often requires very small initialization of the trainable weights, which is at odds with the larger scale at which weights are initialized in practice for faster convergence and better generalization performance. In this paper, we aim to close this gap by incorporating and analyzing gradient flow (continuous-time version of gradient descent) with weight normalization , where the weight vector is reparameterized in terms of polar coordinates, and gradient flow is applied to the polar coordinates. By analyzing key invariants of the gradient flow and using Lojasiewicz’s Theorem, we show that weight normalization also has an implicit bias towards sparse solutions in the diagonal linear model, but that in contrast to plain gradient flow, weight normalization enables a robust bias that persists even if the weights are initialized at practically large scale. Experiments suggest that the gains in both convergence speed and robustness of the implicit bias are improved dramatically by using weight normalization in overparameterized diagonal linear network models.



Keywords —
implicit regularization, weight normalization, gradient descent, overparameterization, linear neural network, vector factorization, L1 minimization, compressed sensing

Introduction

Unlike many classical models such as linear regression or kernel methods, recent machine learning breakthroughs are often based on overparameterized models, e.g. neural networks, where the number of data is less than the number of parameters. To develop theoretical understanding of modern machine learning, many researchers focus on analyzing the simplified model, the linear network

Implicit regularization refers to the hidden preference of the learning model, in contrast to explicit regularization which is explicitly specified in the training process. In particular, implicit regularization can be found in vector

, matrix

In these examples, we understand theoretically that gradient descent (GD) applied to simple overparameterized models exhibits implicit regularization for sparse/low-rank solutions, i.e. solutions of low complexity. Therefore, in applications where low complexity is desirable, the algorithm is guaranteed to perform well.

Yet, many of these theoretical guarantees only hold for GD with small

In practice, GD is initialized very differently. For example, a common setting for neural networks is the Xavier initialization

This gap between implicit bias theory (which requires small initialization) and practice (where initialization is often not small but normalized) indicates that the algorithm which has so far been the main focus of study for implicit bias – (stochastic) gradient descent, or (S)GD – might be too simplistic compared to the algorithms used in practice to train neural networks. As the authors in
[
18
,
35
]

\ell_{2}
loss. It was shown that GD on loss functions with exponential tails, such as exponential, logistic, and sigmoid losses, in general does not require small initialization. However, the optimization procedure requires certain notions of normalization, otherwise the iterates are likely to blow up. Hence it is natural to consider combining normalization with
\ell_{2}
loss to remove the constraints on initialization.

Indeed,
normalization
of some form is an important modification to plain (S)GD used in practice for accelerating convergence and generalization. Batch normalization
[
22
]
and layer normalization
[
4
]
are among the most popular choices, while weight normalization (WN)
[
34
]

In

\ell_{2}

Weight normalization re-parameterizes the weight vector in each layer in polar coordinates,

\displaystyle{\bf x}=\frac{r}{\|{\bf u}\|_{2}}{\bf u};

(1.1)

(S)GD is then implemented separately with respect to the vector

It is then natural to study GD with WN, and in particular to hope that WN can induce a more robust implicit bias towards low-complexity solutions. To be concise, we will be analyzing gradient flow (GF), the continuous-time version of gradient descent, in the following context. There are works establishing connections between GD and GF, e.g.
[
7
,
28

1.2
Formulation

We now introduce the two main models, gradient flow without normalization and gradient flow with normalization, and a key parameter
{\tilde{\eta}}
, which we call the learning rate ratio. They are defined as follows. Let
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
and
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be given. Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the following loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L}\|{\bf A}{\bf x}^{\odot L}-{\bf b}\|_{2}^{2}.

(1.10)

We say that
{\bf x}(t)
follows the gradient flow
(without WN)
if

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf x}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\mathcal{L}({\bf x}),\quad{\bf x}(0)={\bf x}_{0}.

(1.11)

This is the setting of many previous works on implicit bias in overparameterized models. In this paper, we define the loss function with WN as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(r,{\bf u})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{r}{\|{\bf u}\|_{2}}{\bf u}\right)

(1.12)

for
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
{\bf u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We say that
(r(t),{\bf u}(t))
follows the gradient flow
with WN
if

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r

\displaystyle=-\eta_{r}\nabla_{r}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(r,{\bf u}),\quad r(0)=r_{0}

(1.13)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf u}

\displaystyle=-\eta_{\bf u}\nabla_{{\bf u}}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(r,{\bf u}),\quad{\bf u}(0)={\bf u}_{0}

(1.14)

where
\eta_{r},\eta_{\bf u}>0
are the learning rates for the respective parameters. We can always assume
\eta_{\bf u}=1
without loss of generality (by Lemma
3.1
below). Unless otherwise specified, the default setting in this paper is that
\eta_{r}
equals to some positive time-independent constant
{\tilde{\eta}}
. We call this constant the
learning rate ratio
. The choice of
{\tilde{\eta}}
is important and will be discussed later on in the remarks after Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
. Roughly speaking, smaller
{\tilde{\eta}}
allows us to take larger initialization, but we cannot take it arbitrarily small because it will cause numerical instability.

Main Results

Here are some remarks on the scaling and generalization of Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.3
.

•

Error reduction due to
\rho
.

Consider the case where
L=2
and
r_{0}=1
. Let
{Q}=\min_{{\bf z}\in S_{+}}\|{\bf z}\|_{1}
. If the initialization takes the form
\alpha{\bf 1}
, then from Theorem
2.2
the error is given by

\varepsilon(\rho^{-L}\beta_{1},\rho^{-L}\beta_{\min})=\frac{\log(\beta_{1}/\beta_{\min})}{\log(\rho^{L}{Q}/\beta_{1})}.

Thus we can make the right hand side small by making
\rho
sufficiently large. Note that this is impossible without normalization.

•

Enlarged range of initialization for sparse recovery.

Based on the error reduction, below we show that there is a wider range of initialization that yields similar (or even smaller error) for weight normalized GF than the regular GF. The ratio is given by
\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}/\delta
, where
\delta\ll 1
is the small radius for GD to achieve sufficient implicit regularization. From (
1.8
) and (
1.9
), we know that the equivalent level sets for errors are given by

\begin{cases}&\text{GD: }\{\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}:\|\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}\|_{1}\leq\delta\}\\
&\text{WN-GD: }\{(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot L}:\rho^{-L}\|(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot L}\|_{1}\leq\delta\}.\end{cases}

(2.7)

Consider
L=2
. Since
\|{\bf u}_{0}\|_{2}=1
, if
{\bf z}=(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}
, then
\|{\bf z}\|_{1}=\|(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}\|_{1}=r_{0}^{2}
. Hence

\displaystyle\{(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}:\rho^{-2}\|(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}\|_{1}\leq\delta\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\{(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}:\frac{\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}{r_{0}^{2}}\exp\left(\frac{r_{0}^{2}-\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}{{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\|(r_{0}{\bf u}_{0})^{\odot 2}\|_{1}\leq\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\{{\bf z}:\frac{\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}{\|{\bf z}\|_{1}}\exp\left(\frac{\|{\bf z}\|_{1}-\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}{{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\|{\bf z}\|_{1}\leq\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\{{\bf z}:\exp\left(\frac{\|{\bf z}\|_{1}-\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}{{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad=\{{\bf z}:\|{\bf z}\|_{1}\leq\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}+{\tilde{\eta}}(\log\delta-\log(\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}))\}

Hence the radius of the
\ell_{1}
-ball increases from
\delta
to
\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}+{\tilde{\eta}}(\log\delta-\log(\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}))
, which leads to a huge improvement when
\delta\ll\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}
. Note that this is often the case because
\delta
is usually chosen to be small to have small error
\varepsilon
, whereas
\|{\bf A}^{\dagger}{\bf b}\|_{2}
is not small in general.

•

Assumption on kernel of
{\bf A}
.

One of the assumptions is that the kernel of
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
has non-empty intersection with the positive quadrant.
In general, the number of orthants in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
intersecting with a random subspace of dimension
K\geq 1
is
2\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\binom{N-1}{i}

[
10
]
. In our setting
K
is the dimension of the kernel of
{\bf A}
, and it is lower bounded by
N-M
. Suppose now that
{\bf A}
is chosen at random such that the kernel is a random subspace, whose distribution is invariant under rotation. For instance, this is the case for Gaussian random matrices
{\bf A}
as often considered in compressive sensing.
Then by symmetry, the probability that it intersects the positive orthant is given by
2\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\binom{N-1}{i}/2^{N}
. Thus our assumption holds with probability

p=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{K-1}\binom{N-1}{i}=1-\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{N-K-1}\binom{N-1}{i}.

For instance, when
M=1
,
K=N-1
and hence
p=1-(1/2)^{N-1}
. Furthermore, when
\lambda:=\frac{N-K-1}{N-1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
(small number of measurements) we can lower bound this probability via (
[
13
]
)

\displaystyle p\geq 1-2^{-(N-1)(1+H(\lambda))},

where
H(\lambda)=\lambda\log_{2}\lambda+(1-\lambda)\log_{2}(1-\lambda)
.

•

Extension beyond non-negative solutions

A common strategy to extend results from the positive solution set
S_{+}
to the full solution set
S:=\{{\bf z}:{\bf A}{\bf z}={\bf b}\}
is to introduce further parameters. For example, although gradient flow on the loss function

\mathcal{L}({\bf x})=\|{\bf A}{\bf x}^{\odot L}-{\bf b}\|_{2}^{2},\quad{\bf x}_{0}>0

can only lead to positive solutions, gradient flow on the modified loss function

\mathcal{L}_{\pm}({\bf u},{\bf v})=\|{\bf A}({\bf u}^{\odot L}-{\bf v}^{\odot L})-{\bf b}\|_{2}^{2},\quad{\bf u}_{0},{\bf v}_{0}>0

(2.8)

can lead to any solution
[
8
,
16
,
38
]
, since
{\bf u}
takes care of the positive part and
{\bf v}
takes care of the negative part. However, our key lemma (Lemma
3.11
), which depends on uniqueness, no longer holds in this regime. The intuitive reason is that

{\bf u}^{\odot L}-{\bf v}^{\odot L}=({\bf u}^{\odot L}+\zeta)-({\bf v}^{\odot L}+\zeta)

for any
\zeta
. In particular, since we cannot assume
{\bf u}
and
{\bf v}
have disjoint supports, it is unlikely that we can uniquely define
({\bf u},{\bf v})
based on the invariants in Lemma
3.8
. Therefore, our analysis only focuses on the case of positive solution set in this paper. We nevertheless include some simulation for gradient descent on the modified loss function (
2.8
) in Section
4.4
along with some discussion.

Theorem
2.2

Lemma 3.7

(Invariant, without normalization)

Let

{\bf x}
follows the dynamics in (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{0}

\displaystyle=(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\,c({\bf x}^{\odot 1-L}\odot\partial_{t}{\bf x})

Lemma 3.8

(Invariant, with normalization)
.

Let

,

Since

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\bf u}=-r\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(r{\bf u}\right)-\frac{r}{{\tilde{\eta}}}{\bf u}\partial_{t}r=-r\nabla\mathcal{L}\left(r{\bf u}\right)-\frac{1}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\partial_{t}(r^{2}){\bf u}.

Since

to (

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})({\bf u}^{\odot 1-L}\odot\partial_{t}{\bf u})=-\frac{1}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\partial_{t}(r^{2})(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}){\bf u}^{\odot 2-L}.

Rearranging terms we obtain

\displaystyle\log({\bf u})+\frac{r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\bm{1}=\log({\bf u})+\log\left(\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\bm{1}\right)\right)=\log\left({\bf u}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\right)

For

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\widetilde{{\bf u}})=-\frac{2-L}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\partial_{t}(r^{2})\cdot\widetilde{{\bf u}}.

The partial derivative of
\gamma
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\gamma(r_{0},r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r_{0}}\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\cdot\exp\left(\frac{r_{0}^{2}-r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\right).

Note that
\partial_{r}\gamma(r_{0},r)\leq 0
for

. By (

and hence

Thus

is an upper bound of

represents the improvement under weight normalization (larger

3.4
An example of time-dependent learning rate

In this section we study a particular example of time-dependent learning rate, given by
(\eta_{r},\eta_{\bf u})=(r^{2},1)
. Note that instead of a constant in time,
\eta_{r}=r^{2}(t)
is a function depends on time. In this case the dynamics is greatly simplified and is similar to gradient flow without normalization (

). Such simplification allows us to analyze the dynamics based on established methods, such as the argument with Bregman divergence in
[
8
]
, and completely bypass the need of invariants and uniqueness results proved in Section

.

However, gradient flow under this particular choice of learning rate (
(\eta_{r},\eta_{\bf u})=(r^{2},1)

(\eta_{r},\eta_{\bf u})=({\tilde{\eta}},1)
), and hence does not yield better bounds than the ones in previous works (
1.8
). It is nevertheless remarkable that the dynamics with certain choices of learning rate can be so different from the one with time-independent learning rate.

We will first prove a general reduction in dynamics. In fact, from Lemma

we can see why

is a natural choice of time-dependent learning rate.

Lemma 3.14

(Dynamics reduction)

Suppose

and
{\bf u}

) and (

\|{\bf u}(0)\|=1

(3.20)

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Observe that if

Note that without the additional factor

Before diving into the proof we would like to outline the general concepts. We consider the set of all non-negative solutions
S_{+}:=\{{\bf z}\geq 0:{\bf A}{\bf z}={\bf b}\}

Definition 3.16

(Bregman Divergence)
.

Let
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuously-differentiable, strictly convex function defined on a closed convex set
\Omega
. The Bregman divergence associated with
F
for points

is defined as

D_{F}(p,q)=F(p)-F(q)-\langle\nabla F(q),p-q\rangle.

(3.22)

Lemma 3.17

(
[
6
]
)
.

The Bregman divergence
D_{F}
is non-negative and, for any
q\in\Omega
, the function
p\mapsto D_{F}(p,q)
is strictly convex.

Lemma 3.18

(Non-increasing Bregman Divergence)
.

Let
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
L\geq 2
,
(\eta_{r},\eta_{\bf u})=(r^{2},1)
, and
\|{\bf u}(0)\|=1
. Denote
{\bf x}=\frac{r}{\|{\bf u}\|_{2}}{\bf u}
and
\tilde{{\bf x}}={\bf x}^{\odot L}

{\bf z}\geq 0
such that

\partial_{t}D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}})=-2L\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{L}({\bf x})

where

is the Bregman divergence associated with the function
F:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

F(\tilde{{\bf x}})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\langle\tilde{{\bf x}}\odot\log(\tilde{{\bf x}})-\tilde{{\bf x}},\bm{1}\rangle&\text{if }L=2\\
\frac{L}{2(2-L)}\langle\tilde{{\bf x}}^{\odot\frac{2}{L}},\bm{1}\rangle&\text{if }L>2.\end{cases}

(3.24)

Proof.

This directly comes from Lemma

5
Summary and Discussion

In this paper we initiate a study of the implicit bias of gradient descent with weight normalization beyond the linear regression setting. In the overparameterized diagonal linear neural network model, we show that weight normalization provably enables a robust implicit regularization towards sparse solutions that holds beyond the regime of small initialization. We moreover show a linear rate of convergence and an explicit dependence on the initialization scale, indicating that smaller initialization corresponds to slower convergence rate. Numerical experiments are consistent with our theory, and the key quantities such as invariants and the proof strategies can potentially be applied in more general settings.

There are still many remaining questions such as

1.

Do our results generalize to other settings which currently require small initialization to prove an implicit bias?

2.

Can we use the proof strategy to study the effect of weight normalization on neural networks with first-order homogeneous activation functions, such as ReLU?

3.

Can we extend the results here from gradient flow to gradient descent?

4.

Is there a reasonable choice of time-dependent rate that might outperform constant rate, or does the time-dependent rate necessarily lose the magnified implicit bias as shown in Theorem
2.4
?

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t),\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t))-\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t))\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\underbrace{\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}[\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t)]}_{\text{converges to }0}\,,\,\underbrace{\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t))-\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t))}_{\text{bounded}}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\underbrace{\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t)}_{\text{bounded}}\,,\,\underbrace{(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})[\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t))-\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t))]}_{\text{converges to }0}\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=0

Note that due to the monotonicity of
\log
, we have
(a-b)(\log(a)-\log(b))\geq 0
, and consequently

\bm{\xi}(t):=[\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t)]\odot[\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t))-\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t))]\geq 0.

A more compact way to express
\bm{\xi}
is via the difference

\bm{\Delta}:=|\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}|

and the expression

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Delta}\odot\log({\bf 1}+\bm{\Delta}\odot\min(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)},\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot-1})

\displaystyle\geq\bm{\Delta}\odot\log({\bf 1}+\bm{\Delta}\odot(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot-1}).

(A.6)

Since
\bm{\xi}\geq 0
and
\langle\bm{\xi}(t),{\bf 1}\rangle
converges to zero as
t\to\infty
, we can deduce that
\bm{\xi}
converges to zero as
t\to\infty
. Together with (
A.6
) and the assumption that
\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}
is uniformly bounded above, we conclude that
\bm{\Delta}
must also converges to zero. Since
\bm{\Delta}
converges to zero, our conclusion follows.

For
L>2
the analysis is similar. First use the assumption to deduce that

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t),(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t))^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1}-(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t))^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1}\rangle.

Since the vector

\bm{\xi}(t):=[\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t)]\odot[(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1}(t)-(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1}(t)]

is non-negative and
\langle\bm{\xi}(t),{\bf 1}\rangle
converges to zero, we can deduce that
\bm{\xi}
converges to zero. We will use the following fact: if
a=b+\delta
with
a,b,\delta\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}-\frac{1}{a}\geq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2b}&\text{if }\delta\geq b\\
\frac{\delta}{2b^{2}}&\text{if }\delta\leq b\end{cases}

and consequently

\frac{1}{b}-\frac{1}{a}\geq\frac{1}{2b^{2}}\cdot\min\left(b,\delta\right).

By substituting
{\bf a}=\max(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)},\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}
and
{\bf b}=\min(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)},\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}
into
\bm{\xi}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}

\displaystyle=|\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}|\odot[{\bf b}^{\odot-1}-{\bf a}^{\odot-1}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}|\odot\min\left({\bf b}^{\odot-1},|(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}-(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}|\odot{\bf b}^{\odot-2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}|\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}|\odot\min\left((\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1},|(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}-(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)})^{\odot 1-\frac{2}{L}}|\odot(\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)})^{\odot\frac{4}{L}-2}\right)

We again see that the difference
|\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)}(t)-\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}(t)|
converges to zero because
\bm{\xi}(t)
converges to zero and
\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(1)},\tilde{{\bf x}}^{(2)}
are uniformly bounded above and below. Hence the proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.12
.

Denote
\widetilde{r}=\exp(r^{2}/(2{\tilde{\eta}}))
. Without loss of generality we may assume
\|{\bf u}_{0}\|_{2}=1
. By Lemma
3.2
,
\|{\bf u}(t)\|_{2}=1
for all
t\geq 0
. According to Lemma
3.5
, the loss is non-increasing, and hence
\|{\bf A}(r{\bf u})^{\odot L}-{\bf b}\|_{\infty}
is upper bounded. Consequently,

\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}(r{\bf u})^{\odot L}\|_{\infty}\leq B_{1}

(A.7)

for some
B_{1}\geq 0
. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.8
, the quantity

(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\cdot\begin{cases}\log(\widetilde{r}{\bf u})&\text{if }L=2\\
(\widetilde{r}{\bf u})^{\odot 2-L}&\text{if }L\neq 2\end{cases}

(A.8)

equals its value at initialization for all
t\geq 0
. Thus its
\ell_{\infty}
-norm is upper bounded by some
B_{2}\geq 0
.

Let us first study the case where
L=1
by examining the inner product
\langle r{\bf u},\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\rangle
. By decomposing
I=\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}+(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle r{\bf u},\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle r{\bf u},\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle+\left\langle r{\bf u},(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}r{\bf u},\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle+\left\langle r{\bf u},(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq B_{1}\left\|\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\|_{1}+B_{2}\|r{\bf u}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(B_{1}\widetilde{r}+B_{2}r\right)\left\|{\bf u}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(B_{1}\widetilde{r}+B_{2}r\right)\sqrt{N},

where the last inequality comes from the fact that
\|{\bf u}\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{N}\|{\bf u}\|_{2}=\sqrt{N}
. Since the left hand side can be explicitly expressed as

\left\langle r{\bf u},\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right\rangle=r\widetilde{r}\|{\bf u}\|_{2}^{2}=r\widetilde{r},

we have

r\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)\leq\left(B_{1}\exp\left(\frac{r^{2}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\right)+B_{2}r\right)\sqrt{N}.

This implies that
r
is uniformly bounded, because otherwise the left hand side will eventually exceed the right hand side.

For
L=2
we use a similar strategy by examining the inner product
\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\log(\widetilde{r}{\bf u})\rangle
. We first obtain a lower bound of the inner product,

\displaystyle\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\log(\widetilde{r}{\bf u})\rangle

\displaystyle=r^{2}(\log(\widetilde{r})\langle{\bf u}^{\odot 2},{\bf 1}\rangle+\langle{\bf u}^{\odot 2},\log({\bf u})\rangle)

\displaystyle=r^{2}\left(\log(\widetilde{r})\|{\bf u}\|_{2}^{2}+\langle{\bf u}^{\odot 2},\log({\bf u})\rangle\right).

Since
\xi^{2}\log(\xi)\geq-\frac{1}{2e}
for all
\xi\geq 0
, we have

\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\log(\widetilde{r}{\bf u})\rangle\geq r^{2}\left(\log(\widetilde{r})-\frac{N}{2e}\right).

(A.9)

We now derive an upper bound. By decomposing
I=\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}+(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\rangle+\left\langle(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2},(I-\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}})\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq B_{1}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left(\widetilde{r}{\bf u}\right)\right\|_{1}+B_{2}\|(r{\bf u})^{\odot 2}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=B_{1}\left\|\log(\widetilde{r})\cdot\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}{\bf 1}+\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left({\bf u}\right)\right\|_{1}+B_{2}r^{2}\|{\bf u}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq B_{1}\log(\widetilde{r})\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}{\bf 1}\|_{1}+B_{1}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left({\bf u}\right)\right\|_{1}+B_{2}r^{2}.

Note that
B_{1}\left\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}\log\left({\bf u}\right)\right\|_{1}
is upper bounded by some constant
C
because the entries of
{\bf u}
are bounded both above and below. Combining this with (
A.9
), we have

\frac{r^{4}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}-\frac{eNr^{2}}{2}\leq\left(\frac{B_{1}}{2{\tilde{\eta}}}\|\mathcal{P}_{{\bf A}}{\bf 1}\|_{1}+B_{2}\right)r^{2}+C,

which implies that
r
is upper bounded because the left hand side scales like
r^{4}
while the right hand side scales like
r^{2}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.19
.

Denote
\tilde{{\bf x}}={\bf x}^{\odot L}
and
{\bf x}_{0}={\bf x}(0)
.
We prove the statement by contradiction. Suppose that
\mathcal{L}({\bf x}(t))
does not converge to zero. Since
\mathcal{L}
is non-increasing in
t
according to Lemma
3.5
,
\mathcal{L}
is bounded away from zero. Therefore, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{L}({\bf x}(t))\geq\varepsilon
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let
{\bf z}\in S_{+}
. Since
r(0)>0
and
{\bf u}(0)\geq 0
, we have
{\bf x}\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0
by Lemma
3.3
. Hence
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}(t))
is well-defined. By Lemma
3.18
,
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}(t))
is non-increasing in
t
and hence bounded above by
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0})
. By Lemma
3.17
,
D_{F}
is non-negative and hence bounded below. Therefore by (
3.23
),

\displaystyle D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0})\geq-\int_{0}^{\infty}\partial_{t}D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}(t))dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}2L\|{\bf x}(t)\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\mathcal{L}({\bf x}(t))dt\geq 2L\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\infty}\|{\bf x}(t)\|_{2}^{2}dt.

(A.10)

Since
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0})<\infty
, by (
A.10
)
\|{\bf x}(t)\|_{2}
cannot be bounded away from zero. This implies that there exists an increasing sequence
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in[\mathbb{N}]}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\|{\bf x}(t_{k})\|_{2}=0
. Together with the fact that
{\bf x}
is non-negative, we get that for all
n\in[N]
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{n}(t_{k})=0
, and consequently
\lim_{k\to\infty}\tilde{x}_{n}(t_{k})=0
.

We now prove that
D_{F}
eventually exceeds its initial value, i.e. there exists
T>0
such that
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}(T))>D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0})
, and hence the contradiction. Note that the Bregman divergence in our case is given by

D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\langle{\bf z}\odot\log({\bf z})-{\bf z}+\tilde{{\bf x}},\bm{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\log(\tilde{{\bf x}}),{\bf z}\rangle&\text{if }L=2,\\
\langle\frac{L}{2(2-L)}{\bf z}^{\odot\frac{2}{L}}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{{\bf x}}^{\odot\frac{2}{L}},\bm{1}\rangle-\frac{1}{2-L}\langle\tilde{{\bf x}}^{\odot\frac{2}{L}-1},{\bf z}\rangle&\text{if }L>2.\end{cases}

As
{\bf x}
converges to zero, all terms except the last one converge to zero, while the last term blows up to infinity because
{\bf z}\geq 0
and
{\bf z}
is not identically zero (as a consequence of
{\bf b}
being not identically zero). Thus there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}(t_{k}))>D_{F}({\bf z},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0})
. This contradicts Lemma
3.18
. Therefore
\mathcal{L}({\bf x}(t))
converges to zero.
∎
