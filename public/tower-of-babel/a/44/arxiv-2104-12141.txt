Coresets for k k -median clustering under Fréchet and Hausdorff distances
Abhinandan Nath

Abstract
We give algorithms for computing coresets for ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximate k k -median clustering of polygonal curves (under the discrete and continuous Fréchet distance) and point sets (under the Hausdorff distance), when the cluster centers are restricted to be of low complexity. Ours is the first such result, where the size of the coreset is independent of the number of input curves/point sets to be clustered (although it still depends on the maximum complexity of each input object). Specifically, the size of the coreset is Θ ⁡ ( k 3 ​ l ​ m δ ​ d ε 2 ​ log ⁡ ( k ​ l ε ) ) \Theta\left(\frac{k^{3}lm^{\delta}d}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{kl}{\varepsilon}\right)\right) for any δ > 0 \delta>0 , where d d is the ambient dimension, m m is the maximum number of points in an input curve/point set, and l l is the maximum number of points allowed in a cluster center. We formally characterize a general condition on the restricted space of cluster centers – this helps us to generalize and apply the importance sampling framework, that was used by Langberg and Schulman for computing coresets for k k -median clustering of d d -dimensional points on normed spaces in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , to the problem of clustering curves and point sets using the Fréchet and Hausdorff metrics. Roughly, the condition places an upper bound on the number of different combinations of metric balls that the restricted space of cluster centers can hit. We also derive lower bounds on the size of the coreset, given the restriction that the coreset must be a subset of the input objects.

Introduction

We study coresets for
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate
k
-median clustering of polygonal curves and finite point sets under the Fréchet and Hausdorff distances respectively, under the restriction that the cluster centers have a bounded number of points each. We frame it in the more general context of clustering points in a metric space
{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\mathtt{d})
where the cluster centers must come from a (possibly infinite) subset
C\subseteq X
– this has been dubbed as the
(k,C)
-median problem
[
27
]
. We prove a general condition on the subset
C
which allows us to efficiently compute small-sized
\varepsilon
-coresets using the sensitivity sampling framework of Langberg and Schulman
[
22
]
. This gives us the first such coresets for clustering curves and point sets, where the coreset size is independent of the number, but still dependent on the maximum complexity, of input curves/point sets; however the dependence can be made arbitrarily small (Theorem
11
). Specifically, the size of the coreset is
\Theta\left(\frac{k^{3}lm^{\delta}d}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{kl}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)
for any
\delta>0
, where
d
is the ambient dimension,
m
is the maximum number of points in an input curve/point set, and
l
is the maximum number of points allowed in a cluster center. As an easy corollary, we are able to give much better bounds on the VC dimension of the dual of the range space (whose ground set is a set of curves / point sets, and the ranges are metric balls under the Fréchet and Hausdorff distances) than those obtained by the results of
[
12
]
and the naive exponential bound for the dual range space (Corollary
8
). We also give a lower bound of
\Omega(k/\varepsilon)
(Theorem
13
) on the size of the coresets, provided the coreset must be a subset of the input set.

The
k
-median problem is well-known, where given a set
P
of
n
elements from a metric space, we need to select
k

centers
to minimize the sum of the distances of each input to its nearest center. Depending on the application, it is important to choose an appropriate distance function. In this paper, the elements to be clustered are polygonal curves and finite point sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which we use the Fréchet (both continuous and discrete versions) and Hausdorff distances repectively. Clustering is often a first step in data analysis, and the cluster centers can be thought of as representatives for the respective clusters. Often, real-life data is noisy, and restricting the complexity of the cluster centers (e.g., by bounding the number of points per center curve or point set) is an effective way to prevent them from overfitting to the input noise
[
27
,
4
,
5
,
11
,
7
]
.

With burgeoning data sizes, computational efficiency of clustering has become important. The
k
-median problem is computationally hard to solve exactly, even for the Euclidean metric
[
25
,
13
]
. A long line of work on approximation algorithms for the
k
-median problem has developed, with varying running times. The notion of
coresets
, a powerful data-reduction technique, is prominent among these. Vaguely speaking, a coreset is a small-sized proxy for the entire data set so that the cost of any solution on the whole data set and on the coreset are roughly equal. Hence, solving the clustering problem on the coreset (by, say, using a less efficient algorithm) gives us a solution for the entire data set.

Problem definition.

A
metric space

{\mathcal{X}}=(X,\mathtt{d})
consists of a set
X
and a distance function
\mathtt{d}:X\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that satisfies the following conditions – (i)
\mathtt{d}(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
; (ii)
\mathtt{d}(x,y)=\mathtt{d}(y,x)
for all
x,y\in X
; and (iii)
\mathtt{d}(x,z)\leq\mathtt{d}(x,y)+\mathtt{d}(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
.

Given
P,C\subseteq X
, the
(k,C)
-median
problem is to compute a set
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of
k

centers
that minimizes

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(P,C^{\prime})=\dfrac{1}{|P|}\sum_{p\in P}\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime})

where
\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime})=\min_{c\in C^{\prime}}\mathtt{d}(p,c)
. Here
P
is finite but
C
need not be.

The problem can be generalized to include weights
\mu:P\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for each point (without loss of generality, the weights
\mu
can be normalized to sum up to one), and the cost now becomes

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(P,C^{\prime})=\sum_{p\in P}\mu(p)\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime}).

Given such an instance
P
of the
(k,C)
-median problem, an
\varepsilon
-coreset
(for
\varepsilon>0
) is a subset
P^{\prime}\subseteq X
with weights
\omega:P^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for any
C^{\prime}\subseteq C
of size
k
, the following holds

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in P^{\prime}}\omega(p)\mathtt{d}(p,C^{\prime})\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,C^{\prime}).

Hence, we can solve the
(k,C)
-median problem on the (hopefully smaller)
P^{\prime}
, to get a good solution for
P
.

As special cases, we consider the metric spaces
(T^{m}_{poly},\mathtt{d}_{F}),(T^{m}_{poly},\mathtt{d}_{dF})
, and
(U^{m},\mathtt{d}_{H})
, where
T^{m}_{poly}
(resp.
U^{m}
) denotes the set of all polygonal curves (resp. point sets) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with at most
m
points each (
m>0
), and
\mathtt{d}_{F},\mathtt{d}_{dF}
, and
\mathtt{d}_{H}
denote the continuous Fréchet, discrete Fréchet, and Hausdorff distances respectively. Then, the cluster centers must lie in either
T^{l}_{poly}
or
U^{l}
for
l>0
, as the case may be.

Challenges and techniques.

The problem of
k
-median clustering under Fréchet and Hausdorff distances is challenging, not least due to the fact that these metric spaces do not have a constant doubling dimension
[
11
,
27
]
. In this sense, the continuous versions of the distance function (e.g., the continuous Fréchet distance) are more difficult than their discrete counterparts. This is because the doubling dimension for the discrete Fréchet distance, although not a constant, can be expressed as a function of the maximum complexity of the input curves, whereas the doubling dimension for the continuous Fréchet distance is unbounded even when the input curves’ complexities are bounded. Also, unlike the discrete Fréchet distance, the vertices of a median curve need not be anywhere near a vertex of an input curve, resulting in a huge search space. As far as coresets are concerned, a lot of earlier work on coresets involved various forms of geometric discretization of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
17
,
16
,
10
]
, something which seems difficult especially for the continuous Fréchet distance given the earlier statement about its doubling dimension and the huge search space.

Our main technique is the sensitivity sampling framework of Langberg and Schulman
[
22
]
– they use it to compute coresets for
k
-median clustering in normed spaces in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For the framework to be used in our setting, there are two major technical hurdles to overcome. First, showing that the total sensitivity bound of
O(k)
extends to the more general
(k,C)
-median problem, and that individual senstivities of input points can be estimated using a bicriteria approximation to the
(k,C)
-median problem. Second, generalizing the notion of well-behaved norms from
[
22
]
(which bounds the number of cells in any arrangement of centrally symmetric convex sets associated with the norm) to general metric spaces – to this end, we introduce the concept of
well-behaved subsets
of a metric space. Roughly, such well-behaved subsets can stab only a bounded number of different combinations of metric balls. We show that curves and point sets having small number of points are well-behaved for the Fréchet and Hausdorff metrics.

Coresets for the

-median problem have been studied extensively in Euclidean spaces. The first such coresets were given by Har-Peled and Mazumdar
[

[

]
introduced a different, more abstract approach to coreset construction by connecting it with VC-dimension-type results. This is done by associating a function with each

[

give coresets of size

-median problem (i.e., the cluster centers come from the input set) under the continuous Fréchet distance. For general metric spaces, a lower bound of

Preliminaries

Comparing curves and finite point sets.

In its most general form, a
curve
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be specified using a continuous parameterization
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A
polygonal curve
can also be specified using a finite sequence of points
\langle p_{1},p_{2},\ldots\rangle
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
called
vertices
, with each consecutive pair of vertices being joined by a segment
\overline{p_{i}p_{i+1}}
called an
edge
.

Given two parameterized curves
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
, the
Fréchet distance
between them is

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\min_{f,g:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]}\max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\left\lVert\gamma_{1}(f(\alpha))-\gamma_{2}(g(\alpha))\right\rVert

where
\left\lVert.\right\rVert
denotes the
l_{2}
norm, and
f,g
range over all continuous, non-decreasing functions with
f(0)=g(0)=0
and
f(1)=g(1)=1
. Intuitively, imagine a man on one curve walking a dog on the other while holding a fixed length leash, with both of them starting and ending at the start and end points of the respective curves, and none of them allowed to move backwards. The Fréchet distance gives the length of the shortest leash that makes such a walk possible
1

\mathtt{d}_{F}
is a pseudometric, i.e., we can have
\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=0

\gamma_{1}\neq\gamma_{2}

.

The

is defined for polygonal curves, and it only takes into account the vertices while disregarding the edges. Given two such curves
\gamma_{1}=\langle p_{1},\ldots\rangle

\gamma_{2}=\langle q_{1},\ldots\rangle

(i_{1},j_{1}),(i_{2},j_{2})\in{\mathcal{C}},i_{2}\geq i_{1}\Rightarrow j_{2}\geq j_{1}

\mathtt{d}_{dF}

\displaystyle\mathtt{d}_{dF}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=\min_{{\mathcal{C}}}\max_{(p,q)\in{\mathcal{C}}}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert

where
{\mathcal{C}}
ranges over all
monotone
correspondences between
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.

Let

Given two finite point sets

Range space, VC and shattering dimension.

A
range space

(X,\mathcal{R})
consists of a ground set
X
and a collection of ranges
\mathcal{R}
, where each range
R
is a subset of
X
. For
Y\subseteq X
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{|Y}=\{R\cap Y|R\in\mathcal{R}\}.

Then,
Y
is said to be

\mathcal{R}
if
\mathcal{R}_{|Y}
contains all subsets of
Y
. The
Vapnik-Chervonenkis (or VC) dimension

[
29
]
of
(X,\mathcal{R})
is the size of the largest shattered subset of
X
. The
shattering dimension
of
(X,\mathcal{R})
is the smallest

m

\displaystyle\max_{Y\subset X,|Y|=m}|\mathcal{R}_{|Y}|=O(m^{\delta}).

For a range space with VC-dimension
\nu
and shattering dimension
\delta
,
\nu=O(\delta\log\delta)
and
\delta=O(\nu)

[
15
]
.

Given a range space
(X,\mathcal{R})
, for any

, let
\mathcal{R}_{p}=\{R\in\mathcal{R}\mid p\in R\}
be the set of ranges containing

. The
dual range space
of
(X,\mathcal{R})
is the range space
(\mathcal{R},\{\mathcal{R}_{p}\mid p\in X\})
. If a range space has VC-dimension
\nu
, its dual range space has VC-dimension
\leq 2^{\nu+1}

[
15
]
.

Sensitivity-sampling framework.

We briefly describe the sensitivity-sampling framework introduced by Langberg and Schulman
[
22
]
in the more general setting of approximating integrals of functions.

Let
F
be a non-negative real-valued family of functions defined on some set
X
, and let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
X
. The goal is to approximate
\overline{f}=\int_{X}f(x)d\mu
for all
f\in F
, to within a factor of
(1\pm\varepsilon)
for some small
\varepsilon>0
, using a finite subset
R
of
X
with positive weights
\omega
, i.e.,
\sum_{x\in R}\omega(x)f(x)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\overline{f}
. Such an
R
is called an
\varepsilon
-approximator
of
F
. We next discuss how importance sampling can be used to compute
R
.

For any
x\in X
, its
sensitivity
is defined as
\sigma_{F,\mu}(x)=\sup_{f\in F}f(x)/\overline{f}
; we often drop the subscript from
\sigma_{F,\mu}
if it is clear from the context. The
total sensitivity
of
F
is then defined as
{\mathcal{S}}(F)=\sup_{\mu}\int\sigma_{F,\mu}(x)d\mu
. Further, let
s_{F,\mu}(x)
(or
s(x)
) be an upper bound on
\sigma_{F,\mu}(x)
(or
\sigma(x)
) for all
x\in X
, and let
S(F)=\sup_{\mu}\int s_{F,\mu}(x)d\mu(x)
.

Consider the probability distribution
q(x)=s(x)\mu(x)/S(F)
to be used for importance sampling while estimating
\overline{f}
. Then
G=f(x)\mu(x)/q(x)
is an unbiased estimator for
\overline{f}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{q}[G]=\overline{f}
, and its variance can be bounded as follows.

Lemma 1

(Theorem 2.1,
[
22
]
)
.

\textsc{Var}(G)\leq(S(F)-1)\overline{f}^{2}.

The following lemma then gives a concentration bound in terms of the number of independent samples needed from
q(x)
for estimating
\overline{f}
, upto a multiplicative factor.

Lemma 2

(Lemma 2.1,
[
22
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0,f\in F
, and
R
be a random sample of
X
of size
a\geq\frac{2(S-1)}{\varepsilon^{2}}
drawn according to the distribution
q
. Then

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|\overline{f}-\sum_{x\in R}\left(\tfrac{S(F)}{a\cdot s(x)}\right)f(x)\right|\geq\varepsilon\overline{f}\right]\leq 1/2.

Note that the above bound holds only for a fixed
f\in F
– if we want it to hold for all
f\in F
, we will have to use additional properties of
F
and
X
(similar to the VC-dimension arguments that have been used earlier
[
18
]
). We later show in our paper how to accomplish this for the
(k,C)
-median problem.

Bounded sensitivity for

For any

\displaystyle f_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}}(x)=\min_{c\in\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}}\mathtt{d}(x,c).

.

-median setting

Sensitivity upper bound via bicriteria approximation.

We show how to compute upper bounds on the individual sensitivity of each point in
X
, while still making sure that the corresponding upper bound on the total sensitivity of
F_{k,C}
is not too big, since the size of our coreset will depend on it.

Given
\alpha,\beta\geq 1
, an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation to an instance of the
(k,C)
-median problem is a set of at most
\beta k
centers from
C
such that cost of clustering using these centers is at most
\alpha
times the cost of the optimal clustering. Hence, the function
f
associated with these centers satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{f}=\int_{X}fd\mu\leq\alpha\Delta

where
\Delta
is the optimal cost of the
(k,C)
-median problem.

Lemma 4
.

Given an
(\alpha,\beta)
-approximation to an instance of the
(k,C)
-median problem, we can compute for all
x\in X
, a value
s(x)\geq\sigma(x)
satisfying
S=\int_{X}s(x)d\mu\leq 6\alpha+4\beta k
.

Proof.

Let
f=f_{\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{\beta k}\}}
be the function associated with the bicriteria approximation, i.e.,
\bar{f}\leq\alpha\Delta
. Let
\bar{f}=\Delta^{\prime}
, and let
{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})
be the Voronoi cell of
c_{i}
. Further, let
\mu_{i}=\mu({\mathcal{V}}(c_{i}))
, and
m_{i}=\frac{1}{\mu_{i}}\int_{{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})}\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i})d\mu
, so that
\Delta^{\prime}=\sum_{i}\mu_{i}m_{i}
. By Markov’s inequality, for each
i
,
\mu\left({\mathcal{B}}(c_{i},2m_{i})\cap{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})\right)\geq\mu_{i}/2
.

Next, we show that

\displaystyle s(x)=\frac{2\alpha(2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i}))}{\Delta^{\prime}}+\frac{4}{\mu_{i}}\geq\sigma(x).

Let
x\in{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})
, and let
f^{\prime}=f_{\{c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{k}\}}
for any set of
k
points
\{c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{k}\}\subseteq C
. Further, let
c^{\prime}
denote the closest point to
c_{i}
in
\{c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{k}\}
, and let
\mathtt{d}_{i}=\mathtt{d}(c^{\prime},c_{i})
. Then by triangle inequality and the preceding inequality

\displaystyle\bar{f^{\prime}}=\int\limits_{X}f^{\prime}d\mu\geq\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})}f^{\prime}d\mu\geq\int\limits_{{\mathcal{B}}(c_{i},2m_{i})\cap{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})}f^{\prime}d\mu\geq\max\{0,\mathtt{d}_{i}-2m_{i}\}\mu_{i}/2.

Also,
\bar{f^{\prime}}\geq\Delta^{\prime}/\alpha
. Hence,
\bar{f^{\prime}}\geq\max\{0,\mathtt{d}_{i}-2m_{i}\}\mu_{i}/4+\Delta^{\prime}/2\alpha
. We thus have

\displaystyle\sigma(x)

\displaystyle=\max_{f^{\prime}}f^{\prime}(x)/\bar{f^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\max_{f^{\prime}}\mathtt{d}(x,c^{\prime})/\bar{f^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{f^{\prime}}\frac{\mathtt{d}_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i})}{\max\{0,\mathtt{d}_{i}-2m_{i}\}\mu_{i}/4+\Delta^{\prime}/2\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\mathtt{d}_{i}\geq 2m_{i}}\frac{\mathtt{d}_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i})}{(\mathtt{d}_{i}-2m_{i})\mu_{i}/4+\Delta^{\prime}/2\alpha}.

The right hand side is maximized either at
\mathtt{d}_{i}=2m_{i}
or
\mathtt{d}_{i}=\infty
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\sigma(x)\leq\max\left\{\frac{2\alpha(2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i}))}{\Delta^{\prime}},\frac{4}{\mu_{i}}\right\}\leq\frac{2\alpha(2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i}))}{\Delta^{\prime}}+\frac{4}{\mu_{i}}.

Further,

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{X}s(x)d\mu=\sum_{i}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})}s(x)d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}}(c_{i})}\left(\frac{4\alpha m_{i}+2\alpha\mathtt{d}(x,c_{i})}{\Delta^{\prime}}+\frac{4}{\mu_{i}}\right)d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{4\alpha m_{i}\mu_{i}}{\Delta^{\prime}}+\frac{2\alpha m_{i}\mu_{i}}{\Delta^{\prime}}+4\right)

\displaystyle\leq 6\alpha+4\beta k.

∎

We formally characterize the subset

Well-behaved subsets.

Given a (possibly infinite) collection of sets
{\mathcal{A}}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots\}
from some universe

induced by
{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle x\sim y\Leftrightarrow\{i\mid x\in A_{i}\}=\{i\mid y\in A_{i}\}.

We denote the set of equivalence classes by
\Xi_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{U}
; note that the equivalence classes partition the universe
U
.

Let

be a non-decreasing function. Given a metric space

. Then,

has a non-empty intersection with at most

equivalence classes from
\Xi_{{\mathcal{B}}}^{X}
.
Roughly, it is a measure of the simplicity of

, in that it upper bounds the total number of different combinations of sets in
{\mathcal{B}}
that can be simultaneously intersected by
C
.

Small cover codes.

We discuss the notion of
cover codes
as introduced in
[
22
]
. Intuitively, a cover code is a subset of functions from the function family
F
that approximates the set
F
with respect to a finite subset of the support in
X
. A small-sized cover code plays a crucial role in sidestepping a naive union bound while extending Lemma
2
for all functions in F, analogous to the role that a bounded VC-dimension plays, e.g., in
[
18
]
.

We now formally define cover codes. Let
A\subseteq X
be a subset with
a
elements. For any
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, let
\nu_{A}(g)=(1/a)\sum_{x\in A}g(x)
. For
f,f^{\prime}\in F
and
x\in A

\hat{f}=\nu_{A}(f/s)
(where
s
is an upper bound on sensitivity) and

\displaystyle D_{A,x}(f,f^{\prime})=\left|\frac{f(x)}{\hat{f}\cdot s(x)}-\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{\hat{f^{\prime}}\cdot s(x)}\right|.

Let
S
be the upper bound on total sensitivity computed using
s
. Then,
F^{\prime}\subseteq F
is an
\varepsilon
-cover-code
for
(F,A,s)
for some
\varepsilon>0
iff for all
f\in F
there exists an
f^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
such that

(i)

\frac{\overline{f^{\prime}}}{\hat{f^{\prime}}}\leq\frac{\overline{f}}{\hat{f}}
, and

(ii)

For all
x\in A
,
D_{A,x}(f,f^{\prime})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{64S}\left(1+\frac{f(x)}{\hat{f}\cdot s(x)}+\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{\hat{f^{\prime}}\cdot s(x)}\right)
.

Coming back to our setting of the
(k,C)
-median problem, the following theorem is the main result of this section, and states that if
C
is simple enough, then
F_{k,C}
has small cover codes.

Theorem 9
.

Let
A\subseteq X
be of size
a
. If
{\mathcal{X}}
is

-well-behaved w.r.t.
C
, then

has an
\varepsilon
-cover-code of size
\left(h\left(\left(\frac{Sa}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\Theta(1)}\right)\right)^{k}

\varepsilon>0

We will prove this in two steps. First, we show that there exists a small set of functions

\displaystyle\left|\frac{f(x)}{s(x)}-\frac{c_{f}g(x)}{s(x)}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{256S}\hat{f}.

(1)

Second,

We now show the existence of

\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{r},\gamma_{i})=1
for all

\mathtt{d}_{F}(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j})=2
for all

. See Fig.

.

Suppose, to the contrary, that
P^{\prime}\subseteq P
is a coreset (with weight
w
) of size
<\frac{ck}{\varepsilon}
for some
c>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Define
W=\sum_{x\in P^{\prime},x\neq\gamma_{r}}w(x)=\sum_{x\in P^{\prime}}w(x)\mathtt{d}_{F}(x,\gamma_{r})
; the last inequality stems from the construction and distance values.

Consider a
k
-median solution where all centers lie on
\gamma_{r}
. We then have

\displaystyle W=\sum_{x\in P^{\prime}}w(x)\mathtt{d}_{F}(x,\gamma_{r})\geq(1-\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,\gamma_{r})=(1-\varepsilon)n,

where the inequality is because
P^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset. Let
C_{1}\subseteq P\setminus(P^{\prime}\cup\{\gamma_{r}\})
be of size
k
, and let
C_{2}
be the
k
curves in
P^{\prime}\setminus\{\gamma_{r}\}
with largest weight. By an averaging argument we have

\displaystyle W^{\prime}=\sum_{x\in C_{2}}w(x)\geq\frac{kW}{|P^{\prime}|}>\frac{\varepsilon W}{c}\geq\frac{\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)n}{c}.

Further,

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in P^{\prime}}w(x)\mathtt{d}_{F}(x,C_{1})=w(\gamma_{r})+2W\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,C_{1})\leq 2(2n+1).

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in P^{\prime}}w(x)\mathtt{d}_{F}(x,C_{2})=w(\gamma_{r})+2(W-W^{\prime})=\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})-2W^{\prime}.

Also, since
P^{\prime}
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset, and
\textsc{cost}(P,C_{1})=\textsc{cost}(P,C_{2})
by construction and our choice of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})}{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{2})}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,C_{1})}{(1-\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,C_{2})}=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})}{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{2})}=\frac{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})}{\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C_{1})-2W^{\prime}}>\frac{2(2n+1)}{2(2n+1)-\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon)n/c}>\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}

for large enough
n
, which is a contradiction.
∎

7
Conclusion

We give the first coresets for
k
-median clustering polygonal curves and finite point sets under the Fréchet and Hausdorff distances, whose sizes are independent of the number of input objects, when the cluster centers are restricted to have bounded complexity. In doing so, we precisely characterize a general condition on the restricted space of cluster centers that allows use to get small coresets. We also give a lower bound on the size of the coresets, when the coreset must be a subset of the input.

There are several interesting open problems. Can we extend our work to piecewise smooth curves (e.g., algebraic curves in the plane)? Can we use the work of Feldman and Langberg
[
14
]
to compute smaller coresets for our setting? As already mentioned, this requires a bound on the shattering dimension of range spaces induced by weighted distance functions. Can this be sidestepped using the probabilistic shattering dimension of Huang
et al.

[
19
]
? As far as lower bounds are concerned, can we give tighter lower bounds ? The lower bound of
\Omega(k/\varepsilon)
in this paper uses a very general construction, and does not depend on
m,l
. We hope our work will spur further research in these directions.

\displaystyle\sigma(x)\leq\max\left\{\frac{2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c^{*}_{i})}{(1-\alpha)\Delta},\frac{2}{\alpha\mu_{i}}\right\}\leq\frac{2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c^{*}_{i})}{(1-\alpha)\Delta}+\frac{2}{\alpha\mu_{i}}.

The total sensitivity can now be bounded by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(F_{k,C})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}}(c^{*}_{i})}\sigma d\mu\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\left(\int\limits_{{\mathcal{V}}(c^{*}_{i})}\left(\frac{2m_{i}+\mathtt{d}(x,c^{*}_{i})}{(1-\alpha)\Delta}+\frac{2}{\alpha\mu_{i}}\right)d\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{2m_{i}\mu_{i}}{(1-\alpha)\Delta}+\frac{m_{i}\mu_{i}}{(1-\alpha)\Delta}+\frac{2}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{1-\alpha}+\frac{2k}{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{2k})^{2}\;\;\;\;\text{(minimum of $a/(1-\alpha)+b/\alpha$ is $(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})^{2}$)}

\displaystyle\leq 4k+6.

∎
