A Faster Combinatorial Algorithm for Maximum Bipartite Matching
Julia Chuzhoy, Sanjeev Khanna

Abstract
The maximum bipartite matching problem is among the most fundamental and well-studied problems in combinatorial optimization. A beautiful and celebrated combinatorial algorithm of Hopcroft and Karp [ HK73 ] shows that maximum bipartite matching can be solved in O ⁡ ( m ​ n ) O(m\sqrt{n}) time on a graph with n n vertices and m m edges. For the case of very dense graphs, a different approach based on fast matrix multiplication was subsequently developed [ IM81 , MS04 ] , that achieves a running time of O ⁡ ( n 2.371 ) O(n^{2.371}) . For the next several decades, these results represented the fastest known algorithms for the problem until in 2013, a ground-breaking work of Madry [ Mad13 ] gave a significantly faster algorithm for sparse graphs. Subsequently, a sequence of works developed increasingly faster algorithms for solving maximum bipartite matching, and more generally directed maximum flow, culminating in a spectacular recent breakthrough [ CKL+22 ] that gives an m 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) m^{1+o(1)} time algorithm for maximum bipartite matching (and more generally, for min cost flows). These more recent developments collectively represented a departure from earlier combinatorial approaches: they all utilized continuous techniques based on interior-point methods for solving linear programs. This raises a natural question: are continuous techniques essential to obtaining fast algorithms for the bipartite matching problem? Our work makes progress on this question by presenting a new, purely combinatorial algorithm for bipartite matching, that, on moderately dense graphs outperforms both Hopkroft-Karp and the fast matrix multiplication based algorithms. Similar to the classical algorithms for bipartite matching, our approach is based on iteratively augmenting a current matching using augmenting paths in the (directed) residual flow network. A common method for designing fast algorithms for directed flow problems is via the multiplicative weights update (MWU) framework, that effectively reduces the flow problem to decremental single-source shortest paths (SSSP) in directed graphs. Our main observation is that a slight modification of this reduction results in a special case of SSSP that appears significantly easier than general decremental directed SSSP. Our main technical contribution is an efficient algorithm for this special case of SSSP, that outperforms the current state of the art algorithms for general decremental SSSP with adaptive adversary, leading to a deterministic algorithm for bipartite matching, whose running time is O ~ ​ ( m 1 / 3 ​ n 5 / 3 ) \tilde{O}(m^{1/3}n^{5/3}) . This new algorithm thus starts to outperform the Hopcroft-Karp algorithm in graphs with m = ω ⁡ ( n 7 / 4 ) m=\omega(n^{7/4}) , and it also outperforms the fast matrix multiplication based algorithms on dense graphs. We believe that this framework for obtaining faster combinatorial algorithms for bipartite matching by exploiting the special properties of the resulting decremental SSSP instances is one of the main conceptual contributions of our work. Finally, using a standard reduction from the maximum vertex-capacitated s s - t t flow problem in directed graphs to maximum bipartite matching, we also obtain an O ~ ​ ( m 1 / 3 ​ n 5 / 3 ) \tilde{O}(m^{1/3}n^{5/3}) -time deterministic algorithm for maximum vertex-capacitated s s - t t flow when all vertex capacities are identical.

Introduction

In the

G
with
n
vertices and
m
edges, and the goal is compute a matching of maximum size in
G
. This problem is among the most extensively studied in computer science, combinatorial optimization, and operations research, and holds a central place in graph algorithms with
intimate connections to many other fundamental graph optimization problems (see e.g.
[
Sch03
]
).

A standard textbook application of the maximum
s
-
t
flow problem shows that the task of finding an optimal bipartite matching can be reduced to finding a maximum
s
-
t
flow in a directed graph with unit edge capacities. This connection immediately gives an
O(mn)
-time algorithm for bipartite matching by simply invoking the basic Ford-Fulkerson algorithm
[
FF56
]
on the resulting flow network. The resulting algorithm is based on a simple idea: as long as the current matching is not optimal, it can be augmented by finding an
s
-
t
path in the residual flow network in
O(m)
time. A celebrated result of Hopcroft and Karp
[
HK73
]
showed an elegant way to speed up this simple algorithm. Instead of finding a single
s
-
t
path at a time, the Hopcroft-Karp (HK) algorithm finds a
maximal collection
of internally disjoint augmenting
s
-
t
paths of shortest possible length in the residual flow network, using a combination of breadth-first search (BFS) and depth-first search (DFS). This modification improves the runtime to
O(m\sqrt{n})
. For the ensuing several decades, the HK algorithm remained the fastest algorithm for bipartite matching except for the case of very dense graphs. For dense graphs, a subsequent work based on fast matrix multiplication was shown to give an
O(n^{\omega})
time algorithm for bipartite matching, beating the HK algorithm in the dense regime
[
IM81
,
MS04
]
.

After many decades of no further progress on bipartite matching, starting in the 2000s, a new paradigm started to emerge for solving combinatorial problems like bipartite matching and maximum
s
-
t
flows, using continuous techniques. A breakthrough result of Spielman and Teng
[
ST04
]
, achieving a near-linear time algorithm for solving Laplacian systems, paved the way for this new method, called the Laplacian paradigm. As a first illustration of this paradigm, Daitch and Spielman
[
DS08
]
showed that the task of computing maximum
s
-
t
flow in a directed graph can be reduced to solving
\widetilde{O}(m^{1/2})
Laplacian systems, thus obtaining an
\widetilde{O}(m^{3/2})
time algorithm for directed max flow.
Later, Madry
[
Mad13
]
showed that maximum flow in directed graphs, and hence bipartite matching, can be computed in
\tilde{O}(m^{10/7})
time using this paradigm. Exactly 40 years after the discovery of the HK algorithm, this became the first algorithm to substantially outperform it at least on sparse graphs. The following decade saw a sequence of remarkable improvements
[
Mad16
,
LS19
,
CMSV17
,
LS20
,
AMV20
]
with another big milestone arriving when van den Brand
et al.

[
vdBLN+20

gave an
\tilde{O}(m+n^{1.5})
time algorithm for bipartite matching. Finally, in another major breakthrough, Chen
et. al.

[
CKL+22
]
gave an
m^{1+o(1)}
-time algorithm for solving maximum flow in directed graphs, thereby obtaining an almost linear time algorithm for bipartite matching in all edge-density regimes.
At a high level, these developments are all based on formulating a directed flow problem as a linear program, solving the resulting linear program by efficiently utilizing interior-point methods (IPM), whereby each iteration of IPM reduces the directed graph problem to either solving a Laplacian system, or another efficiently solvable problem on undirected graphs (e.g., min ratio cycle in
[
CKL+22
]
). This general approach is then further combined with dynamic graph data structures to avoid re-computation from scratch in each successive IPM iteration, resulting in highly efficient new algorithms for problems like bipartite matching and maximum
s
-
t
flow.

This history of algorithmic developments for bipartite matching and related problems raises a natural question: are continuous techniques essential to obtaining fast algorithms for
maximum bipartite matching
? On one hand, the use of continuous techniques based on IPMs have led to an almost linear time algorithm for bipartite matching, strongly outperforming the HK algorithm in all edge density regimes. On the other hand, these improvements come at the expense of the simplicity and the transparent nature of the augmenting path based combinatorial algorithms.
In fact, this question can be asked more broadly for many other graph problems, where significant recent advances have come by strongly utilizing continuous techniques.

We make progress on the above-mentioned question for bipartite matching by presenting a new, purely combinatorial, augmenting path based algorithm, which on moderately dense graphs outperforms the Hopcroft-Karp algorithm, and on dense graphs outperforms the fast matrix multiplication based algorithms, proving the following theorem.

1.1
Our Techniques

It is well known that the
maximum bipartite matching
problem in a bipartite graph
G
can be equivalently cast as the problem of computing an integral maximum
s
-
t
flow in a directed flow network
G^{\prime}
with unit edge capacities.
We can view any given matching
M
in
G
as defining an
s
-
t
flow
f
in
G^{\prime}
of value is
|M|
. We let
H=G^{\prime}_{f}
be the corresponding residual flow network, that we refer to as the
residual flow network with respect to matching
M
. The residual network
H
contains an integral
s
-
t
flow of value
\Delta=\mathsf{OPT}-|M|
, where
\mathsf{OPT}
is the value of the maximum bipartite matching. Moreover, any integral
s
-
t
flow
f^{\prime}
in
H
that respects edge capacities, can be transformed into a collection
{\mathcal{Q}}
of
\operatorname{val}(f^{\prime})
internally disjoint
s
-
t
paths (augmenting paths), which can then in turn be used in order to obtain a new matching
M^{\prime}
in
G
, of cardinality
|M|+\operatorname{val}(f^{\prime})
. The task of augmenting
M
to a larger matching is then equivalent to recovering a large flow in
H
.

One standard approach for obtaining fast algorithms for various flow problems, due to
[
GK98
,
Fle00
,
AAP93
]
, is via the Multiplicative Weight Update (
MWU
) method. It was further observed by Madry
[
Mad10
]
that this approach can be viewed as reducing a given flow problem to a variant of decremental
SSSP
or
APSP
. While strong lower bounds are known for exact algorithms for decremental
SSSP
and
APSP
(see, e.g.
[
DHZ00
,
RZ11
,
HKNS15
,
ABKZ22
,
ABF23
]
), for our purposes, it is sufficient to only approximately solve the resulting decremental
SSSP
problem, and recover an approximate max flow in
H
. Specifically, we will iteratively augment the current matching, where the goal of a single iteration is to augment
M
to a new matching
M^{\prime}
that satisfies
|M^{\prime}|\geq|M|+\Omega(\Delta/\operatorname{poly}\log n)
, where
\Delta=\mathsf{OPT}-|M|
. This will ensure that the number of iterations is bounded by
O(\operatorname{poly}\log n)
. We now focus on the implementation of a single iteration.

In a single iteration, we use the
MWU
framework to find an
s
-
t
flow in the residual flow network
H
of value
\Omega(\Delta/\operatorname{poly}\log n)
with
O(\log n)
congestion. By scaling down, we may equivalently view this as giving a
fractional

s
-
t
flow in the residual flow network
H
of value
\Omega(\Delta/\operatorname{poly}\log n)
with no congestion.
The support of this flow is
\widetilde{O}(n)
edges, and we can now use the blocking flow approach of the Hopcroft-Karp
[
HK73
]
algorithm to recover an
integral

s
-
t
flow of value
\Omega(\Delta/\operatorname{poly}\log n)
in deterministic
\widetilde{O}(n^{3/2})
time.
We note that it is possible to round the fractional flow into an integral flow in
\widetilde{O}(n)
time using the random walk technique of
[
GKK10
,
LRS13
]
. However, the improved runtime for this step does not improve our overall runtime and comes at the expense of making the algorithm randomized, so we will rely on the deterministic rounding scheme. The remaining challenge is in the efficient implementation of the
MWU
framework, which in turn reduces to solving a special case of decremental
SSSP
in directed graphs.

At a high level, we follow the approach of
[
BGS20
]
, who designed a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation algorithm for directed decremental
SSSP
, with total update time
O\left(\frac{n^{8/3+o(1)}}{\epsilon}\right)

SSSP
; by adapting their algorithm to this setting, we obtain the desired faster running time. Unlike the general
SSSP
problem, the special case that we need to solve has some useful properties. First, all shortest-path queries are asked between a pre-specified pair
(s,t)
of vertices.
Additionally, we slightly modify the
MWU
method, so that the task of responding to shortest-path queries is even easier. Specifically, we can assume that we are given two parameters

and
\Delta
. In response to a shortest-path query, the algorithm needs to either return an

-
t

-
t

in the current graph is bounded by

s
-
t
paths in the current graph

. This property is crucially used by our algorithm to obtain a faster running time. Additionally, due to the specific setup provided by the
MWU
method, the only edges that are deleted from

SSSP
problem, where it is crucial that the time the algorithm takes to respond to each individual query is short, we only need to ensure that the total time the algorithm takes to respond to
all queries
is suitably bounded. We exploit all these advantages of the special case of the
SSSP
problem that we obtain in order to design an algorithm for it that is significantly faster than the algorithm of
[
BGS20
]
for general decremental
SSSP

appears before interval
I_{A}
, while in the latter case, we ensure that interval
I_{A}
appears before interval
I_{B}
. This placement of intervals ensures that there are relatively few right-to-left edges.

Lastly, we consider the contracted graph
H^{\prime}=H_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
, that is obtained from
H
by contracting every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}\setminus\left\{S,T\right\}
into a single vertex. As the time progresses, graph
H^{\prime}
undergoes edge deletions and vertex-splitting updates. The latter type of updates reflects the partitioning of the sets
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
of vertices into smaller subsets. At all times, the ordering
\sigma
of the intervals in
\left\{I_{X}\mid X\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}
that is maintained by the
\mathcal{ATO}
naturally defines an ordering of the vertices of
H^{\prime}
, that we denote by
\sigma^{\prime}
. Notice that, if we could ensure that no right-to-left edges are present, then graph
H^{\prime}
would be a DAG, and
\sigma^{\prime}
would define a topological ordering of its vertices. We could then use the algorithm of
[
BGWN20
]
for decremental
SSSP
in DAG’s, whose total update time is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
. In
[
BGWN20
]
it was further observed that, if we could ensure that the number of right-to-left edges with respect to the
\mathcal{ATO}
structure is low, and the average length of each such edge (with respect to the ordering
\sigma^{\prime}
of the vertices of
H^{\prime}
) is small, one can apply an algorithm similar to that for
SSSP
in DAG’s to the contracted graph
H^{\prime}
. The total update time of the resulting algorithm depends on the number of the right-to-left edges, and on their length with respect to
\sigma^{\prime}
. Exactly this approach was used in
[
BGS20
]
in order to obtain an algorithm for decremental
SSSP
in general directed graphs. We also employ the same approach, but we use the advantages of our setting, specifically, that all queries are between a pre-specified pair
s,t
of vertices, and that we only need to support the queries as long as there are many short internally disjoint
s
-
t
paths, in order to obtain a faster algorithm. Intuitively, since we can assume that the current graph
H
contains
\tilde{\Omega}(\Delta)
short internally disjoint
s
-
t
paths, and since the right-to-left edges must be distributed among such paths, at least one short
s
-
t
path must contain a very small number of right-to-left edges. It is this observation that leads to a faster running time of this part of our algorithm.

2
Preliminaries

In this paper we work with both directed and undirected graphs.
By default graphs are allowed to contain parallel edges but they may not contain loops. Graphs with no parallel edges are explicitly referred to as
simple
graphs.

We use standard graph theoretic notation. Given a graph
G
(that may be directed or undirected), and two disjoint subsets
A,B
of its vertices, we denote by
E_{G}(A)
the set of all edges of
G
with both endpoints in
A
, and by
E_{G}(A,B)
the set of all edges connecting a vertex of
A
to a vertex of
B
.
The degree of a vertex
v
in
G
, denoted by
\deg_{G}(v)
, is the number of edges incident to
v
in
G
; if graph
G
is directed, this includes both outgoing and incoming edges. Given a subset

of vertices of
G
, its

, denoted by
\operatorname{Vol}_{G}(A)
, is
\sum_{v\in A}\deg_{G}(v)
. When graph
G
is clear from context, we may omit the subscript.

Congestion of Paths and Flows.

Let
G
be a graph with capacities
c(e)\geq 0
on edges
e\in E(G)
, and let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a collection of simple paths in
G
. We say that the paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
cause
edge-congestion

\eta
, if every edge
e\in E(G)
participates in at most
\eta\cdot c(e)
paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
. When edge capacities are not explicitly given, we assume that they are unit.
If every edge of
G
belongs to at most one path in
{\mathcal{P}}
, then we say that the paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
are
edge-disjoint
.

Similarly, if we are given a flow value
f(e)\geq 0
for every edge
e\in E(G)
, we say that flow
f
causes edge-congestion
\eta
, if for every edge
e\in E(G)
,
f(e)\leq\eta\cdot c(e)
holds. If
f(e)\leq c(e)
holds for every edge
e\in E(G)
, we may say that
f
causes
no edge-congestion
.

Assume now that we are given a graph
G
, a pair
s,t
of its vertices, and a collection
{\mathcal{P}}
of simple paths connecting
s
to
t
. We say that the paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
cause
vertex-congestion

\eta
, if every vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus\left\{s,t\right\}
participates in at most
\eta
paths. If the paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
cause vertex-congestion
1
, then we say that they are
internally vertex-disjoint
.

Embedding of Graphs.

Let
H,G
be two graphs with
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
. An
embedding
of
H
into
G
is a collection
{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{P(e)\mid e\in E(H)\right\}
of paths in
G
, where for every edge
e=(u,v)\in E(H)
, path
P(e)
connects
u
to
v
in
G
. The
congestion
of the embedding is the maximum, over all edges
e^{\prime}\in E(G)
, of the number of paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
containing
e^{\prime}
; equivalently, it is the edge-congestion that the set
{\mathcal{P}}
of paths causes in
G
.

Cuts, Sparsity and Expanders for Directed Graphs.

Let
G
be a directed graph. A
cut
in
G
is a an ordered pair
(A,B)
of subsets of vertices of
G
, such that
A\cap B=\emptyset
,
A\cup B=V(G)
, and
A,B\neq\emptyset
. Note that we do not require that
|A|\leq|B|
in this definition. The
sparsity
of the cut is:

\Phi_{G}(A,B)=\frac{|E(A,B)|}{\min{\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}}}.

We say that a directed graph
G
is a
\varphi
-expander, for a given value
0<\varphi<1
, if the sparsity of every cut in
G
is at least
\varphi
. Notice that, if
G
is a
\varphi
-expander, then for every cut
(A,B)
, both
|E(A,B)|\geq\varphi\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}
and
|E(B,A)|\geq\varphi\cdot\min{\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}}
hold.

We also use the following theorem that provides a fast algorithm for an explicit construction of an expander, and is based on the results of Margulis
[
Mar73
]
and Gabber and Galil
[
GG81
]
. The proof for undirected graphs was shown in
[
CGL+19
]
.

Theorem 2.1
(Theorem 2.4 in
[
CGL+19
]
).

There is a constant
\alpha_{0}>0
and a deterministic algorithm, that we call
\mathsf{ConstructExpander}
, that, given an integer
n>1
, in time
O(n)
constructs a directed graph
H_{n}
with
|V(H_{n})|=n
, such that
H_{n}
is an
\alpha_{0}
-expander, and every vertex in
H_{n}
has at most
9
incoming and at most
9
outgoing edges.

The above theorem was proved in
[
CGL+19
]
for undirected expanders, with maximum vertex degree in the resulting graph bounded by
9
, but it is easy to adapt it to directed expanders. This is since, if a graph
G
is an undirected
\varphi
-expander, and a directed graph
G^{\prime}
is obtained from
G
by replacing every edge with a pair of bi-directed edges, then
G^{\prime}
is a directed
\varphi
-expander.

The following simple observation follows from the standard ball-growing technique. We provide its proof for completeness in Section
A
of Appendix.

Observation 2.2
.

There is a deterministic algorithm, that, given a directed
n
-vertex graph
G=(V,E)
with unit edge lengths and maximum vertex degree at most
\delta_{\mbox{{\footnotesize{max}}}}
, and two vertices
x,y\in V
with
\mbox{\sf dist}_{G}(x,y)\geq d
for some parameter

, computes a cut
(A,B)
of sparsity at most

in

. The running time of the algorithm is
O(\delta_{\mbox{{\footnotesize{max}}}}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\})
.

We will use the following simple observation a number of times. Intuitively, the observation allows us to convert a sequence of small sparse cuts into a single balanced sparse cut.

Observation 2.3
.

There is a deterministic algorithm, whose input consists of a directed
n
-vertex graph
G
, parameters
0<\alpha<1
and
0<\varphi<1
, and a sequence
{\mathcal{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
of non-empty subsets of vertices of
G
, such that the following hold:

•

Every vertex of
V(G)
lies in exactly one set in
\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\right\}
;

•

\max_{i}\left\{|X_{i}|\right\}=\alpha n
; and

•

for all

, if we denote by
X^{\prime}_{i}=\bigcup_{j=i+1}^{k}X_{j}
, then either
|E(X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i})|\leq\varphi\cdot|X_{i}|
, or
|E(X^{\prime}_{i},X_{i})|\leq\varphi\cdot|X_{i}|
.

The algorithm computes a cut
(A,B)
in
G
with
|A|,|B|\geq\min\left\{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot n,\frac{n}{4}\right\}
, and
|E_{G}(A,B)|\leq\varphi\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}|X_{i}|
. The running time of the algorithm is
O(|E(G)|)
.

Proof.

We will gradually construct an ordering
{\mathcal{O}}
of the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
, that will allow us to compute the cut. We compute the ordering in
k-1
iterations, where for all
1\leq i<k
, in iteration

, we compute an ordering
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
of the sets in
\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i}\right\}
; notice that these sets of vertices partition
V(G)
. We will also gradually construct a set
E^{\prime}
of edges, will ensure that, at the end of every iteration
i
, if an edge
e\in E(G)\setminus E^{\prime}
connects a vertex in set
Z
to a vertex in set
Z^{\prime}
, with
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i}\right\}
and
Z\neq Z^{\prime}
, then
Z
appears before
Z^{\prime}
in the ordering
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
(in other words, the edge is directed from left to right with respect to the ordering). We will also ensure that, at the end of each iteration
i
,
|E^{\prime}|\leq\varphi\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{i}|X_{j}|
.

We start by constructing the first ordering
{\mathcal{O}}_{1}
of the sets
X_{1},X^{\prime}_{1}
. If
|E(X_{1},X^{\prime}_{1})|\leq\varphi\cdot|X_{1}|
, then we place
X_{1}^{\prime}
before
X_{1}
in the ordering, and we set
E^{\prime}=E(X_{1},X^{\prime}_{1})
. Otherwise, we place
X_{1}
before
X_{1}^{\prime}
in the ordering (in this case,
|E(X^{\prime}_{1},X_{1})|\leq\varphi\cdot|X_{1}|
must hold), and we set

. Notice that, after the first iteration,

holds, and, if

whose endpoints lie in different sets of

, then

is directed from left to right with respect to the ordering

.

We now assume that the

th iteration successfully computed an ordering
{\mathcal{O}}_{i}
of sets

, together with a set
E^{\prime}
of edges, such that
|E^{\prime}|\leq\varphi\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{i}|X_{j}|
, and moreover, if an edge

connects a vertex in set
Z
to a vertex in set
Z^{\prime}
, with
Z,Z^{\prime}\in\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i}\right\}

to

. Otherwise,

must hold, and we obtain ordering

of

by replacing

in the ordering

with the sets

, so

.

3
High-Level Overview of the Algorithm

In this section we provide a high-level overview of our algorithm, define the main technical tool that we use, namely the restricted
SSSP
problem, state our main result for this problem, and show that the proof of
Theorem

1.1
follows from it.

Recall that, in the
maximum bipartite matching
problem, the input is a simple undirected
n
-vertex bipartite graph
G=(L,R,E)
, and the goal is to compute a matching of maximum cardinality in
G
.
Let
\mathsf{OPT}
denote the value of the optimal solution to the problem.
It is enough to design an algorithm, that, given an integer
0<C^{*}\leq\mathsf{OPT}
, computes a matching of cardinality at least
C^{*}
in
G
. This is since we can perform a binary search over the value
\mathsf{OPT}
. Given the current guess
C^{*}
on
\mathsf{OPT}
, we run the algorithm with this target value
C^{*}
. If the algorithm successfully returns a matching of cardinality at least
C^{*}
, then we adjust our guess
C^{*}
to a higher value, and otherwise we adjust it to a lower value. For convenience, we will denote this target value
C^{*}
by
\mathsf{OPT}
. Therefore, we assume from now on that we are given an integer
\mathsf{OPT}
, such that the input graph
G
contains a matching of value at least
\mathsf{OPT}
, and our goal is to find a matching of value at least
\mathsf{OPT}
.

It is well known that the
maximum bipartite matching
problem can be reduced to computing maximum flow in a directed graph with unit edge capacities. In order to do so, we start with the graph
G
, and direct all its edges from vertices of
L
towards vertices of
R
. We then add a source vertex
s
that connects with an edge to every vertex of
L
, and a destination vertex
t
, to which every vertex of
R
is connected. All edge capacities are set to
1
. Let
G^{\prime}
denote the resulting directed flow network. It is easy to verify that the value of the maximum
s
-
t
flow in
G^{\prime}
is equal to the cardinality of maximum matching in
G
. Moreover, given an integral flow
f
in
G^{\prime}
, we can compute a matching
M
of cardinality
\operatorname{val}(f)
in
G
, in time
O(n)
: we simply include in
M
all edges of
G
that carry
1
flow unit.

As observed by
[
GK98
,
Fle00
,
AAP93
]
, the Multiplicative Weight Update (
MWU
) framework can be adapted in order to efficiently compute approximate solutions for various flow problems. Later, Madry
[
Mad10
]
observed that an efficient implementation of this framework can be reduced to solving a special case of decremental
SSSP
or
APSP
. However, the use of this technique typically leads to algorithms that compute
approximate
and not
exact
maximum flow. Since our goal is to compute exact maximum matching, we cannot employ this technique directly. Instead, we will carefully combine the augmenting paths based approach for maximum flow with the
MWU
framework.

Our algorithm maintains a matching
M
in the input graph
G
, whose cardinality grows over time, starting with
M=\emptyset
. Given the current matching
M
, we denote
\Delta=\mathsf{OPT}-|M|
. Once
\Delta
becomes sufficiently small, we can use augmenting paths-based approach in a straightforward way, to compute a matching of cardinality
\mathsf{OPT}
in
G
in time
O(\Delta\cdot m)
; we provide the details later.
As long as
\Delta
remains sufficiently large, we perform iterations. If
M
is the matching that the algorithm maintains at the beginning of an iteration, and
M^{\prime}
is the matching obtained at the end of the iteration, then we will ensure that
|M^{\prime}|\geq|M|+\Omega(\Delta/\operatorname{poly}\log n)
, where
\Delta=\mathsf{OPT}-|M|
. This will ensure that the number of iterations is bounded by
O(\operatorname{poly}\log n)
.

We now proceed to describe a single iteration. We assume that
M
is the matching that is given at the beginning of the iteration, and let
\Delta=\mathsf{OPT}-|M|
. Note that matching
M
naturally defines an
s
-
t
flow
f
in
G^{\prime}
, of value
|M|
: for every edge
e=(u,v)\in M
, we set the flow on edges
(s,u)
,
(u,v)
and
(v,t)
to
1
; the flow on all other edges is set to
0
. We then consider the residual flow network
G^{\prime}_{f}
, that we denote for convenience by
H
. We will sometimes say that
H
is the
residual graph of
G
associated with the matching
M
. It is well known that the value of the maximum
s
-
t
flow in a residual flow network
G^{\prime}_{f}
is equal to the value of the maximum flow in the original flow network
G^{\prime}
minus the value of the flow
f
. In other words, the value of the maximum
s
-
t
flow in graph
H
is at least
\Delta
. Equivalently, there is a collection
{\mathcal{P}}
of
\Delta
edge-disjoint
s
-
t
paths in graph
H
. We note that graph
H
has a special structure that our algorithm will exploit extensively. Specifically, for every edge
e=(u,v)
of
G
with
u\in L
and
v\in R
, if
e\in M
, then edges
(u,s),(v,u)
, and
(t,v)
are present in
H
, and otherwise edge
(u,v)
is present in
H
.
Therefore, if
x
is a vertex of
L
, then the in-degree of
x
in
H
is
1
: if
x
is an endpoint of some edge
(x,y)
in
M
, then the only edge entering
x
in
H
is
(y,x)
, and otherwise the only edge entering
x
in
H
is
(s,x)
. Similarly, if
y
is a vertex of
R
, then its out-degree in
H
is
1
. This ensures that, for any collection
{\mathcal{Q}}
of edge-disjoint
s
-

3.2
The MWU Framework

Consider the residual flow graph
H
of
G
with respect to the current matching
M
. We
consider the problem of computing a largest cardinality collection
{\mathcal{P}}
of edge-disjoint paths connecting
s
to
t
in
H
. Let
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
denote the collection of all simple
s
-
t
paths in
H
. Then the problem can be expressed in a linear program, where every path
P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
is associated with a variable
f(P)
; we also provide its dual LP.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{\lx@text@underline{Primal}}&&\\
\text{Max}&\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}}f(P)&\\
\text{s.t.}&&\\
&\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}:}}{{e\in E(P)}}}f(P)\leq 1&\forall e\in E(H)\\
&f(P)\geq 0&\forall P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\\
\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{\lx@text@underline{Dual}}&&\\
\text{Min}&\sum_{e\in E(H)}\ell(e)\\
\text{s.t.}&&\\
&\sum_{e\in E(P)}\ell(e)\geq 1&\forall P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}\\
&\ell(e)\geq 0&\forall e\in E(H)\\
&&\\
\end{array}

In the dual LP, there is a variable
\ell(e)
for every edge
e\in E(H)
, that we will refer to as the
length
of the edge. We now consider the standard application of the
MWU
framework, that can be viewed as a primal-dual algorithm. The algorithm that we describe below is a simplified version of the original algorithm of
[
GK98
,
Fle00
]
; this variant achieves a slightly weaker approximation, but its analysis is much simpler and the higher approximation factor does not affect the overall performance of our algorithm.

Standard
MWU
-Based Algorithm.

For simplicity, in this informal description of the standard
MWU
-based algorithm, we assume that
m=|E(H)|
is an integral power of
2
.
We start with the graph
H
, and set the length of every edge
e
in
H
to
\ell(e)=1/m
. We initialize
{\mathcal{P}}=\emptyset
, and set
f(P)=0
for all
P\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
. Next, we perform iterations. In every iteration, we compute a simple
s
-
t
path
P
in
H
, whose length is bounded by
1
. We then add
P
to
{\mathcal{P}}
, set
f(P)=1
, double the length of every edge on
P
, and continue to the next iteration. The algorithm terminates once every
s
-
t
path in
H
has length greater than
1
. It is immediate to see that the final collection
{\mathcal{P}}
of paths is guaranteed to cause congestion at most
O(\log m)
. Indeed, whenever an edge
e
is used by a path in
{\mathcal{P}}
, we double its length, and once the length becomes greater than
1
, the edge may no longer be used in any paths that are subsequently added to
{\mathcal{P}}
. Since initially we set
\ell(e)=1/m
, at most
O(\log m)
paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
may use
e
. Moreover, if
\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime}
is the cardinality of the largest collection of edge-disjoint
s
-
t
paths in
H
, then at the end of the algorithm,
|{\mathcal{P}}|=\Omega(\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime})
holds.
This is since, at the beginning of the algorithm, the value of the dual solution is
1
, and the value of the primal solution is
0
. In every iteration, the value of the primal solution increases by
1
, and the value of the dual solution increases by at most
1
(since the total length of all edges whose lengths are being doubled is at most
1
). Therefore, if we denote by
C
the cost of the dual solution obtained at the end of the algorithm, then
C\leq 1+|{\mathcal{P}}|\leq 2|{\mathcal{P}}|
holds. As the algorithm terminates only when the length of every
s
-
t
path in
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
is at least
1
, we obtain a valid solution to the dual LP of value
C
at the end of the algorithm. By weak duality, the value of the optimal soluton to the primal LP (and hence
\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime}
) is upper-bounded by the value of the optimal solution to the dual LP, which is in turn upper-bounded by
C
. Therefore,
C\geq\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime}
, and, since
|{\mathcal{P}}|\geq C/2
, we get that
|{\mathcal{P}}|\geq\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime}/2
holds. In our case, since
\mathsf{OPT}^{\prime}\geq\Delta
, we are guaranteed that
|{\mathcal{P}}|=\Omega(\Delta)
.

In order to implement this framework, it is sufficient to implement an oracle that, in every iteration, computes a simple
s
-
t
path of length at most
1
in
H
, or correctly establishes that no such path exists, while the lengths of the edges in
H
increase over time.
As observed by Madry
[
Mad10
]
, the problem that the oracle needs to solve can be viewed as a special case of decremental
SSSP
. Indeed,
the increase in edge lengths can be easily implemented as edge-deletion updates. This is done by constructing another graph
\hat{H}
, that is obtained from graph
H
, by replacing every edge
e=(u,v)\in E(H)
with
\log m
parallel copies of lengths
1/m,2/m,\ldots,1
. Every time the length of
e
in graph
H
doubles, we delete the shortest copy of
e
that lies in
\hat{H}
. Implementing the oracle now reduces to solving the resulting instance of decremental
SSSP
. Unfortunately, this remains a challenging problem, since
exact
decremental
SSSP
is known to be a difficult problem, and it seems unlikely that one can get a fast algorithm even for the restricted special case that we obtain.

Modified
MWU
Framework.

We start with some intuition for the modified
MWU
framework. In order to overcome the difficulty mentioned above, we will slightly relax the requirements from the oracle: we still require that the paths that the oracle returns have lengths at most
1
, but now the oracle is allowed to terminate once
\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(s,t)\geq\frac{1}{32\log m}
holds (intuitively, this corresponds to solving the
SSSP
problem approximately). Even with this relaxed setting, we are still guaranteed that, at the end of the algorithm,
|{\mathcal{P}}|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\Delta}{\log m}\right)
holds, and as before, the paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
cause congestion at most
O(\log m)
: the bound on congestion follows from the same reasoning as before. In order to establish that
|{\mathcal{P}}|\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\Delta}{\log m}\right)
, we scale the final length of every edge in the dual LP up by factor
32\log m
, thereby obtaining a feasible dual solution. This proves that
|{\mathcal{P}}|
is within factor
O(\log m)
from the optimal solution to the primal LP.

We can relax the requirements from the oracle even more, and this relaxation will be crucial in reducing the running time of our algorithm. Specifically, we allow the algorithm to terminate, once the following condition is violated:

C1.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be the largest-cardinality collection of

-
t

.

In addition to this relaxation, we will also slightly change the initial lengths that are assigned to every edge. We use a parameter

.
In our algorithm, we will set the initial length

of every edge

to

.

(excluding the implementation of the oracle), appears in Figure

. The edges of
E(P)
are then deleted from
\hat{H}
and we proceed to the next iteration.

To summarize, we reduced
Theorem

3.1
(and generally the
maximum bipartite matching
problem) to a special case of (decremental)
SSSP
problem in directed graphs. Observe that the algorithm for this special case must be able to withstand an adaptive adversary, since the edges that are deleted from the graph depend on the paths that the algorithm returned in response to prior queries. While current state of the art algorithms for
SSSP
in directed graphs with an adaptive adversary are not so efficient, this special case of
SSSP
has some nice additional properties that we can exploit, as listed below:

•

The special structure of the input graph
\hat{H}\setminus\left\{s,t\right\}
: namely, for every vertex, either its in-degree or its out-degree is at most
1
, if we ignore parallel edges;

•

The algorithm is only required to return a short
s
-
t
path as long as there are many disjoint short
s
-
t
paths, and once this is not the case, it can be terminated;

•

We only need to respond to
shortest-path
query between a specific pair of vertices
s
and
t
that is known in advance. Moreover, the only edges that are deleted from the graph are edges that appear on the paths that the algorithm returns.

•

The number of times we are required to respond to
shortest-path
queries is not too large, and we only need to bound the
total time
required to both maintain the data structures and respond to queries.

•

Generally in the (decremental)
SSSP
problem we require that the time required to return a path in response to a
short-path
query (called
query time
) is proportional to the number of the edges on the path. In our case we could afford a higher query time.

•

While much of the work on
SSSP
focuses on factor
(1-\epsilon)
approximation, we can sustain a much higher approximation factor (the reduction we presented requires approximation factor
8
, but it can be easily adjusted to allow higher approximation factors, that may be as large as polynomial in
n
).

The algorithm that we present does not exploit all of the advantages listed above; this may suggest avenues for obtaining even faster algorithms for
maximum bipartite matching
via this general approach.

We abstract the special case of
SSSP
problem that we need to solve, that we call
restricted

SSSP
, in the following subsection.

3.3
The Restricted
SSSP
Problem

Well-Structured Graphs.

Throughout, we will work with graphs that have a special structure. Let
G
be the given instance of the Bipartite Matching problem. All graphs that we consider are subgraphs of the residual graph of
G
with respect to some matching
M
(except that we may add some parallel edges). Such graphs have special structural properties that we summarize in the following definition.

Definition 3.5
(Well-Structured Graph).

Let
H
be a directed graph, and let
m
be a parameter. We say that
H
is a
well-structured
graph of size
m
, if
|E(H)|\leq m\log m
, and
H
has two special vertices
s
and
t
. Additionally, if we denote
H^{\prime}=H\setminus\left\{s,t\right\}
, then the following hold.

•

V(H^{\prime})
is partitioned into two subsets
L
and
R
, and every edge of
H^{\prime}
has one endpoint in
L
and another in
R
. Edges directed from vertices of
R
to vertices of
L
are called
special edges
(they correspond to the edges of the current matching
M
), and the remaining edges of
H^{\prime}
are called
regular edges
.

•

Every vertex of
L\cup R
is incident to at most
\log m
special edges in
H^{\prime}
and all of them are parallel to each other, so the out-degree of every vertex in
R
(ignoring parallel edges) is at most
1
, and the in-degree of every vertex in
L
(also ignoring parallel edges) is at most
1
in
H^{\prime}
.

•

In graph
H
, vertex
s
has no incoming edges, and all edges leaving
s
connect it to vertices of
L
.

•

Similarly, in graph
H
, vertex
t
has no outgoing edges, and all edges entering
t
connect vertices of
R
to it.

•

Every edge of
H^{\prime}
has at most
\log m-1
edges that are parallel to it.

(We note that strictly speaking, a residual graph corresponding to a matching may contain edges entering
s
and/or edges leaving
t
, but since such edges will never be used in any augmenting path, we can delete them from the residual network).

Whenever an algorithm is given a well-structured graph as an input, we assume that it is also given the vertices
s
and
t
and the corresponding partition
(L,R)
of
V(H^{\prime})
.
We are now ready to define the restricted
SSSP
problem.

Definition 3.6
(Restricted
SSSP
problem).

The input to the restricted
SSSP
problem is a well-structured graph
G
of size
m
, where
m
is a given parameter, together with another parameter
0<\Delta\leq|V(G)|
. We denote
|V(G)|=n
and
\Lambda=\frac{8m\log m}{\Delta}
. For every edge
e\in E(G)
, we are also given a length
\ell(e)
, that is an integral power of
2
between
1
and
16\Lambda
.

The algorithm consists of at most
\Delta
iterations. At the beginning of every iteration, the algorithm must compute a simple
s
-
t
path
P
in the current graph
G
of length at most
8\Lambda
(we sometimes informally say that the algorithm responds to a
short-path
query between
s
and
t
). We assume that for each edge
e\in E(P)
, it is the shortest from among its parallel copies. Every edge of
E(P)
is then deleted from
G
, and the iteration ends.

The algorithm is allowed, at any time, to halt and return FAIL, but it may only do so if the largest-cardinality collection
{\mathcal{Q}}
of
s
-
t
paths in the current graph
G
, such that the paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
are edge-disjoint, and every path has length at most
\Lambda
, has
|{\mathcal{Q}}|<\frac{\Delta}{256\log^{2}m}
.

One of the main technical contributions of our paper is the proof of following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

There is a deterministic algorithm for the Restricted
SSSP
problem, that, given a well-structured graph
G
with
n
vertices and parameters
\Delta
and
m
, has running time
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{n^{2.5}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)
.

We note that the running time of the algorithm from
Theorem

3.7
includes both the time that is needed in order to maintain its data structures (total
update time
), and the time it takes to compute and return
s
-
t
paths (total
query time
).

The proof of
Theorem

3.1
immediately follows from
Theorem

3.7
: we use the algorithm from
Theorem

3.7
in order to implement the oracle for

ALG-MWU
. In order to do so, we first construct a graph
\hat{H}
from graph
H
, as described above. In every iteration, we use the algorithm for the restricted
SSSP
problem in order to compute a simple
s
-
t
path
P
in graph
\hat{H}
of length at most
8\Lambda
, which immediately defines a simple path in graph
H
of length at most
1
. The doubling of the length of every edge on
P
is implemented by deleting the shortest copy of each such edge from graph
\hat{H}
.
When the algorithm from
Theorem

3.7
terminates, we are guaranteed that Condition
C2
is violated in
\hat{H}
, and so Condition
C1
is violated in
H
.
It is easy to verify that the total running time of the algorithm is bounded by
O(m\log m)
plus the running time of the algorithm for the restricted
SSSP
problem, which is, in turn, bounded by
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{n^{2.5}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)
. Since
\Delta\leq n
, we can bound the total running time of the algorithm by
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{n^{2.5}}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\right)
.

In the remainder of this paper we focus on the proof of
Theorem

3.7
.
At a high level, the proof of the theorem follows the framework of
[
BGS20
]
for decremental
SSSP
in directed graphs. But since we only need to deal with a restricted special case of the problem, we obtain a faster running time, and also significantly simplify some parts of the algorithm and analysis.
In the following two sections, we present the two main tools that are used in the proof of
Theorem

3.7
: the
\mathcal{ATO}
framework, together with
SSSP
in “DAG-like” graphs, and expander-based techniques. We then provide the proof of
Theorem

3.7
in
Section

7
.

4
Overview of the
\mathcal{ATO}
framework

We now provide an overview of the Approximate Topological Order (
\mathcal{ATO}
) framework
that was developed in
[
BGWN20
]
and later used in
[
BGS20
]
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed
n
-vertex graph with two special vertices
s
and
t
, that undergoes edge deletions during a time interval
{\mathcal{T}}
.
Throughout, we refer to a collection
I\subseteq\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
of consecutive integers as an
interval
. The
size
of the interval
I
, denoted by
|I|
, is the number of distinct integers in it.

An
\mathcal{ATO}
consists of the following components:

•

a partition
{\mathcal{X}}
of the set
V
of vertices of
G
, such that there is a set
S=\left\{s\right\}
and
T=\left\{t\right\}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
; and

•

a map
\rho
, that maps every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
to an interval
I_{X}\subseteq\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
of size
|X|
, such that all intervals in
\left\{I_{X}\mid X\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}
are mutually disjoint.

For convenience, we will denote an
\mathcal{ATO}
by
({\mathcal{X}},\rho)
.
Both the partition
{\mathcal{X}}
and the map
\rho
evolve over time. We denote by
{\mathcal{X}}^{(\tau)}
the partition
{\mathcal{X}}
at time
\tau
, and by
\rho^{(\tau)}
the map
\rho
at time
\tau
. For every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}^{(\tau)}
, we denote by
I^{{}^{(\tau)}}_{X}
the interval
I_{X}
associated with
X
at time
\tau
.

We start initially with the collection
{\mathcal{X}}
of vertex subsets containing three sets,
S=\left\{s\right\}
,
T=\left\{t\right\}
, and
J=V\setminus\left\{s,t\right\}
. We set their corresponding intervals to
I_{S}=\left\{1\right\}
,
I_{T}=\left\{n\right\}
, and
I_{J}=\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}
.
The only type of changes that are allowed to the
\mathcal{ATO}
over time is the
splitting
of the sets
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
. In order to split a set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
, we select some partition
\Pi
of
X
, and replace the set
X
in
{\mathcal{X}}
with the sets in
\Pi
. Additionally, for every set
X^{\prime}\in\Pi
, we select an interval
I_{X^{\prime}}\subseteq I_{X}
of size
|X^{\prime}|
, such that the resulting intervals in
\left\{I_{X^{\prime}}\mid X^{\prime}\in\Pi\right\}
are mutually disjoint, and we set
\rho(X^{\prime})=I_{X^{\prime}}
for all
X^{\prime}\in\Pi
.

Notice that an
\mathcal{ATO}

(X,\rho)
naturally defines a left-to-right ordering of the intervals in
\left\{I_{X}\mid X\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}
: we say that interval
I_{X}
lies to the left of interval
I_{X^{\prime}}
, and denote
I_{X}\prec I_{X^{\prime}}
iff every integer in interval
I_{X}
is smaller than every integer in
I_{X^{\prime}}
. We will also sometimes denote
X\prec X^{\prime}
in such a case. Notice also that, if we are given an ordering
\sigma=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{k})
of the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
, then this ordering naturally defines the map
\rho
, where for all
1\leq i\leq k
, the first integer in
I_{X_{i}}
is
\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}|X_{j}|+1
, and the last integer is
\sum_{j=1}^{i}|X_{j}|
. For convenience, we will denote the map
\rho
associated with the ordering
\sigma
by
\rho(\sigma)
.

Given a vertex
v\in V
, we will sometimes denote by
X^{v}
the set of
{\mathcal{X}}
that contains
v
. Consider now some edge
e=(u,v)
in graph
G
, and assume that
X^{u}\neq X^{v}
. We say that
e
is a
left-to-right
edge if
X^{u}\prec X^{v}
, and we say that it is a
right-to-left
edge otherwise.

Lastly, we denote by
H=G_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
the
contracted graph
associated with
G
and
{\mathcal{X}}
– the graph that is obtained from
G
by contracting every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
into a vertex
v_{X}
, that we may call a
supernode
, to distinguish it from vertices of
G
. We keep parallel edges but discard loops.

Intuition.

For intuition, we could let
{\mathcal{X}}
be the set of strongly connected components of the graph
G
. The corresponding contracted graph
H=G_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
is then a DAG, so we can define a topological order of its vertices. This order, in turn, defines an ordering
\sigma
of the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
, and we can then let the corresponding
\mathcal{ATO}
be
({\mathcal{X}},\rho(\sigma))
.
Notice that in this setting, all edges of
G
that connect different sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
are left-to-right edges. However, we will not follow this scheme. Instead, like in
[
BGS20
]
, we will ensure that every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
corresponds to some expander-like graph, and we will allow both left-to-right and right-to-left edges. It would still be useful to ensure that the corresponding contracted graph
H
is close to being a DAG, since there are efficient algorithms for decremental
SSSP
on DAG’s and DAG-like graphs. Therefore, we will try to limit both the number of right-to-left edges, and their “span”, that we define below.

Skip and Span of Edges.

For every edge
e=(u,v)\in E(G)
we define two values: the
skip
of edge
e
, denoted by
\operatorname{skip}(e)
, and the
span
of
e
, denoted by
\operatorname{span}(e)
. Intuitively, the skip value of an edge
(u,v)
will represent the “distance” between the intervals
I_{X^{u}}
and
I_{X^{v}}
. Non-zero span values are only assigned to right-to-left edges. The span value also represents the distance between the corresponding intervals, but this distance is defined differently. We now formally define the span and skip values for each edge, and provide an intuition for them below.

Consider some edge
e=(x,y)\in E(G)
, and let
X,Y\in{\mathcal{X}}
be the sets containing
x
and
y
, respectively. If
X=Y
, then
\operatorname{skip}(e)=\operatorname{span}(e)=0
. Assume now that
X\prec Y
, that is
e
is a left-to-right edge. In this case, we set
\operatorname{span}(e)=0
, and we let
\operatorname{skip}(e)
be the distance between the last endpoint of
I_{X}
and the first endpoint of
I_{Y}
. In other words, if the largest integer in
I_{X}
is
z_{X}
, and the smallest integer in
I_{Y}
is
a_{Y}
, then
\operatorname{skip}(e)=a_{Y}-z_{X}
. Note that, as the algorithm progresses, sets
X
and
Y
may be partitioned into smaller subsets. It is then possible that
\operatorname{skip}(e)
may grow over time, but it may never decrease.

Lastly, assume that
Y\prec X
, so
e
is a right-to-left edge. In this case, we set
\operatorname{skip}(e)
to be the distance between the right endpoint of
I_{Y}
and the left endpoint of
I_{X}
, and we let
\operatorname{span}(e)
be the distance between the left endpoint of
I_{Y}
and the right endpoint of
I_{X}
. In other words, if
a_{X}
and
z_{X}
are the smallest and the largest integers in
X
respectively, and similarly
a_{Y}
and
z_{Y}
are the smallest and the largest integers in
I_{Y}
respectively, then
\operatorname{skip}(e)=a_{X}-z_{Y}
and
\operatorname{span}(e)=z_{X}-a_{Y}
. Notice that, as the algorithm progresses, and sets
X
and
Y
are partitioned into smaller subsets, the value
\operatorname{span}(e)
may only decrease, while the value
\operatorname{skip}(e)
may only grow. Moreover,
\operatorname{skip}(e)\leq\operatorname{span}(e)
always holds.

For intuition, consider again the setting where
{\mathcal{X}}
represents the strongly connected components of
G
, and
\rho=\rho(\sigma)
, where
\sigma
is an ordering of the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
corresponding to a topological ordering of the vertices of
H=G_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
. Then for any path
P
in
G
,
\sum_{e\in E(P)}\operatorname{skip}(e)\leq n
must hold. This is because the path must traverse the sets of
{\mathcal{X}}
in their left-to-right order. Consider now inserting a single right-to-left edge
e=(x,y)
into
G
, so
x\in X
,
y\in Y
, and
Y\prec X
. In this case, path
P
will generally traverse the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
from left to right, but it may use the edge
e
in order to “skip” back. It is not hard to see that
\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E(P)}\operatorname{skip}(e^{\prime})\leq n+\operatorname{skip}(e)+\operatorname{span}(e)

now holds, since, by traversing the edge
e
, path
P
is ‘set back’ by at most
\operatorname{span}(e)
units. In other words, path
P
traverses the sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
in their left-to-right order, except when it uses the edge
e
to “skip” to the left. The length of the interval it “skips” over is bounded by
\operatorname{span}(e)
, so this skip adds at most
\operatorname{skip}(e)+\operatorname{span}(e)\leq 2\operatorname{span}(e)
to
\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E(P)}\operatorname{skip}(e^{\prime})
.

Using the same intuitive reasoning, if we allow graph
G
to contain an arbitrary number of right-to-left edges, then for any simple path
P
,
\sum_{e\in E(P)}\operatorname{skip}(e)\leq n+\sum_{e\in E(G)}2\operatorname{span}(e)
holds. However, if we are given any collection
{\mathcal{P}}
of edge-disjoint paths in
G
, then there must be some path
P\in{\mathcal{P}}
with
\sum_{e\in E(P)}\operatorname{skip}(e)\leq n+\frac{\sum_{e\in E(G)}2\operatorname{span}(e)}{|{\mathcal{P}}|}
, since every edge
e\in E(G)
may contribute the value
2\operatorname{span}(e)
to at most one path in
{\mathcal{P}}
. We will use this fact extensively.

Overview of the Algorithm.

Like the algorithm of
[
BGS20
]
for decremental
SSSP
,
our algorithm relies on two main technical tools. The first tool allows us to maintain the partition
{\mathcal{X}}
of
V(G)
over time. Intuitively, we will ensure that, for every set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the corresponding induced subgraph
G[X]
of
G
is in some sense expander-like. In particular, we will be able to support short-path queries between pairs of vertices of
X
efficiently. When
G[X]
is no longer an expander-like graph, we will compute a sparse cut
(A,B)
in this graph, and we will replace
X
with
A
and
B
in
{\mathcal{X}}
. Some of the edges connecting the sets
A
and
B
may become right-to-left edges with respect to the current
\mathcal{ATO}

({\mathcal{X}},\rho)
that we maintain. We will control the number of such edges and their
\operatorname{span}
values by ensuring that the cut is
\varphi
-sparse, for an appropriately chosen parameter
\varphi
. This part is related to extensive past work on embedding expanders, maintaining expander embeddings in decremental graphs, and decremental
APSP
in expanders. However, due to the specifics of our setting, we obtain algorithms that are both significantly simpler than those used in past work, and lead to faster running times.

Given a dynamically evolving
\mathcal{ATO}

({\mathcal{X}},\rho)
for graph
G
, we can now construct the corresponding contracted graph
H=G_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
.
For convenience, the supernodes of
H
representing the sets
S=\left\{s\right\}
and
T=\left\{t\right\}
of
{\mathcal{X}}
are denoted by
s
and
t
, respectively.
Notice that, as the time progresses, graph
H
undergoes, in addition to edge-deletion, another type of updates, that we call
vertex-splitting
: whenever some set
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
is split into a collection
\Pi
of smaller subsets, we need to insert supernodes corresponding to the sets
Y\in\Pi
into
H
. We will always identify one of the sets in
\Pi
with
X
itself (so the supernode
v_{X}
corresponding to
X
may continue to serve as the supernode representing this one new set). We will say that the new supernodes corresponding to the remaining sets in
\Pi
were
split off
from
v_{X}
. Consider now any edge
e=(x,y)\in E(G)
, and assume that
x\in X
and
y\in Y
for some
X,Y\in{\mathcal{X}}
with
X\neq Y
. The length of the edge
e
in graph
H
remains the same, namely,
\ell(e)
. But we will also set the
weight
of the edge
e
to be the smallest integral power of
2
that is at least
\operatorname{skip}(e)
. As observed already, as the algorithm progresses, the value
\operatorname{skip}(e)
may grow. In order to avoid inserting edges into
H
overtime, we will create a number of parallel edges with different weight values corresponding to every edge of
G
whose endpoints lie in different sets of
{\mathcal{X}}
. Specifically, let
e=(x,y)
be any edge, with
x\in X
,
y\in Y
, and
X\neq Y
. For every integer
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, such that
2^{i}\geq\operatorname{skip}(e)
, we add an edge
(v_{X},v_{Y})
to
H
, with length
\ell(e)
and weight
2^{i}
. It is easy to verify that this construction ensures that, for every vertex
v
of
H
and integer
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, the number of vertices in the set
\left\{u\in V(H)\mid(v,u)\in E(H)\mbox{ and }w(v,u)=2^{i}\right\}
is bounded by
2^{i+2}
. Additionally, by carefully controlling the total span of all right-to-left edges in graph
G
, and using the fact that throughout the algorithm
G
contains a large number of edge-disjoint
s
-
t
paths, we will ensure that there is always a short
s
-
t
path
P
in
H
, such that
\sum_{e\in E(P)}w(e)
is relatively small. These two properties are crucial for obtaining a fast algorithm for computing short
s
-
t
paths in
H
, as it undergoes updates. The
\mathcal{ATO}
framework, combined with an algorithm for maintaining expander-like graphs, can now be viewed as reducing our problem to the problem of repeatedly computing short
s
-
t
paths in graph
H
, as it undergoes the updates that we have outlined above. We abstract this problem, that we call
DAG-like SSSP
, in
Section

5
, and provide an algorithm for it, that is essentially identical to the algorithm of
[
BGWN20
]
, and which was also used in exactly the same fashion by
[
BGS20
]
.

Overall, our algorithm for restricted
SSSP
departs from the algorithm of
[
BGS20
]
for decremental
SSSP
in two ways. First, we exploit the fact that
H
contains a large number of short
s
-
t
paths, in order to obtain better bounds on the running time of the algorithm of
[
BGWN20
]
for
DAG-like SSSP
. Second, our more relaxed setting allow us to obtain significantly simpler and faster algorithms for maintaining expander-like graphs, than, for example, those presented in
[
BGS20
]
. In particular, we do not need to use expander pruning, the recursive algorithm for
APSP
in expanders, and the recursive algorithm for the cut player in the cut-matching game. We provide simple implementation for the latter two algorithms.

We next describe the two main technical tools that we use: an algorithm for
DAG-like SSSP
and algorithms for maintaining expander-like graphs in the following two sections. We then complete the proof of
Theorem

3.7
, by providing an efficient algorithm for the Restricted
SSSP
problem, that exploits both these tools.

5
SSSP in DAG-like Graphs

In this section we define a problem that can be thought of as an abstraction for the types of
SSSP
instances that we obtain via
\mathcal{ATO}
. We call this problem
DAG-like SSSP
, and we provide an algorithm for this problem, that is essentially identical to the algorithm of
[
BGWN20
]
.

The input to the
DAG-like SSSP
problem is a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, with two designated vertices
s
and
t
, a distance parameter
d>0
, a precision parameter
0<\epsilon\leq 1/8
, such that
1/\epsilon
is an integer, and another parameter
\Gamma>0
.
The graph
G
may have parallel edges, but no self-loops. Every edge
e\in E
of
G
is associated with an integral
length

\ell(e)>0
, and an additional
weight

w(e)
, that must be an integral power of
2
between
1
and
2^{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}
. The graph undergoes the following two types of updates:

•

Edge deletion:
given an edge
e\in E
, delete
e
from
G
; and

•

Vertex splitting:
in this operation, we are given as input a vertex
v\not\in\left\{s,t\right\}
in the current graph, and new vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
that need to be inserted into
G
; we may sometimes say that vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
are
split-off from
v
. In addition to vertex
v
and vertices
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
, we are given a new collection
E^{\prime}
of edges that must be inserted into
G
, and for every edge
e\in E^{\prime}
, we are given an integral length
\ell(e)>0
and a weight
w(e)
that must be an integral power of
2
between
1
and
2^{\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}
. For each such edge
e\in E^{\prime}
, either both endpoints of
e
lie in
\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v\right\}
; or one endpoint lies in
\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right\}
, and the other in
V(G)\setminus\left\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right\}
. In the latter case, if
e=(u_{i},x)
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(v,x)
must currently lie in
G
, and both
w(e^{\prime})=w(e)
and
\ell(e^{\prime})=\ell(e)
must hold. Similarly, if
e=(x,u_{i})
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(x,v)
must currently lie in
G
, and both
w(e^{\prime})=w(e)
and
\ell(e^{\prime})=\ell(e)
must hold.

We let
{\mathcal{T}}
be the
time horizon
of the algorithm – that is, the time interval during which the graph
G
undergoes all updates.
We denote by
n
and by
m
the total number of vertices and edges, respectively, that were ever present in
G
during
{\mathcal{T}}
. We assume w.l.o.g. that for every vertex
v
that was ever present in
G
, some edge was incident to
v
at some time during the update sequence, so
n\leq O(m)
.

For every vertex
v\in V(G)
and integer
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, we define a set:

U_{i}(v)=\left\{u\in V(G)\mid\exists e=(v,u)\in E(G)\mbox{ with }w(e)=2^{i}\right\}.

Note that as the algorithm progresses, each such set
U_{i}(v)
may change, with vertices both leaving and joining the set. We require that
the following property must hold at all times
\tau\in{\mathcal{T}}
:

P1.

For every vertex
v\in V(G)\setminus\left\{t\right\}
, for every integer
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
,
|U_{i}(v)|\leq 2^{i+2}
.

As graph
G
undergoes edge-deletion and vertex-splitting updates, at any time the algorithm may be asked a query
path-query
. It is then required to return a
simple
path
P
connecting
s
to
t
, whose length
\sum_{e\in E(P)}\ell(e)
is bounded by
(1+10\epsilon)d
, in time
O(|E(P)|)
.

Lastly, the algorithm may return FAIL at any time and halt. It may only do so if the current graph
G
does not contain any
s
-
t
path
P
with
\sum_{e\in E(P)}\ell(e)\leq d
, and
\sum_{e\in E(P)}w(e)\leq\Gamma
.

The following theorem is one of the main technical tools that we use; it follows almost immediately from the results of
[
BGWN20
]
. Specifically, Theorem 5.1 of
[
BGWN20
]
provides a similar result with a slightly different setting of parameters, but their arguments can be easily extended to prove the theorem below. We provide the proof of the theorem for completeness in Section
C
of Appendix.

Theorem 5.1
.

There is a deterministic algorithm for the
DAG-like SSSP
problem with total update time
O\left(\frac{n^{2}+m+\Gamma\cdot n}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

6
Tools for Expander-Like Graphs

Well-Structured Cuts.

Suppose we are given a
simple
unweighted directed bipartite graph
H=(L,R,E)
, such that every vertex in
R
has out-degree at most
1
and every vertex in
L
has in-degree at most
1
. We call the edges that are directed from
R
to
L

special edges
, and the remaining edges are
regular
edges.

We say that a cut
(A,B)
in
H
is
well-structured
iff all edges of
E_{H}(A,B)
are special edges.

Observation 6.1
.

Suppose we are given a
simple directed bipartite graph
H=(L,R,E)
with no isolated vertices, such that every vertex in
R
has out-degree at most
1
and every vertex in
L
has in-degree at most
1
, together with and a cut
(A,B)
in
H
of sparsity at most
\varphi\leq\frac{1}{4}
. Then there is a well-structured cut
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
H
of sparsity at most
2\varphi
, such that
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
|A^{\prime}|\geq(1-\varphi)|A|
, and
\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A^{\prime})\geq\Omega(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A))
hold.

Moreover, there is an algorithm, that, given cut
(A,B)
, computes such a cut
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
, in time
O(\min\left\{\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A),\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B)\right\})
.

Proof.

We start with cut
(A,B)
and inspect every vertex in
A
. If
v
is any such vertex, and there is an edge
(v,u)
in
E_{H}(A,B)
that is a regular edge, we move
v
from
A
to
B
. Note that the only new edge that this move introduces into the cut
(A,B)
is a special edge that enters
v
, if such an edge exists. Vertex
v
is removed from
A
, and we say that edge
(v,u)
is
responsible
for this move. Notice that every regular edge of
E_{H}(A,B)
is responsible for moving at most
1
vertex from
A
to
B
.

Let
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
denote the final cut obtained at the end of this procedure. For convenience, denote
z=\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}
. It is easy to verify that
|B^{\prime}|\geq z
continues to hold, and
|A^{\prime}|\geq|A|-|E_{H}(A,B)|\geq|A|-\varphi\cdot z\geq\frac{|A|}{2}
. Therefore,
\min\left\{|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\right\}\geq\frac{\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}}{2}
.
From the above discussion,
|A^{\prime}|\geq|A|-\varphi\cdot z\geq(1-\varphi)|A|
also holds.

Notice also that
|E_{H}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})|\leq|E_{H}(A,B)|
, since every regular edge
e\in E(A,B)
may cause at most one additional special edge to be added to the cut. Altogether, we get that:

\frac{|E_{H}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime}|)}{\min\left\{|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\right\}}\leq\frac{2|E_{H}(A,B)|}{\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}}\leq 2\varphi.

Notice that for every edge
e\in E_{H}(A)
, at least one endpoint of
e
must remain in
A^{\prime}
. Indeed, consider any such edge
e=(x,y)
. The only way that
x
is moved from
A
to
B
is if there is a regular edge
(x,y^{\prime})\in E_{H}(A,B)
, which, in turn may only happen if
x\in L
. Using the same reasoning,
y
may only be moved to
B
if
y\in L
. But since
x
and
y
are connected by an edge, it is impossible that both vertices lie in
L
.
Let
A_{1}\subseteq A\setminus A^{\prime}
be the set of vertices
v\in A
, such that
v
was moved to
B^{\prime}
and
v
had no neighbors in
A
, and let
A_{2}=A\setminus A_{1}
. Then
|A_{1}|\leq|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\varphi\cdot|A|\leq\frac{|A|}{4}
, so in particular,
|A_{1}|\leq O(|A_{2}|)
.
Since
|E_{H}(A,B)|<|A|
, we get that:

\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A^{\prime})\geq\Omega(|E_{H}(A)|+|A_{2}|)\geq\Omega(|E_{H}(A)|+|A|)\geq\Omega(|E_{H}(A)|+|E_{H}(A,B)|)\geq\Omega(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A)).

(we have used the fact that
H
has no isolated vertices).

The running time of the algorithm is bounded by
O(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A))
. If
\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B)<\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A)
, then we can instead scan all vertices of
B
, and for each such vertex
u
try to identify a regular edge
(v,u)\in E(A,B)
. In this case, the running time is bounded by
O(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B))
. Notice that we can establish whether
\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A)\leq\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B)
in time
O\left(\min\left\{\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A),\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B)\right\}\right)
, and so the total running time of the algorithm is bounded by
O\left(\min\left\{\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(A),\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(B)\right\}\right)
.

6.1
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
Problem

We now define a new problem, called
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem. Intuitively, the goal in this problem is to maintain an expander-like subgraph of a given input graph, and to support short-path queries between pairs of its vertices.

Definition of
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
Problem.

The input to the problem is a
simple directed bipartite graph
H=(L,R,E)
, such that every vertex in
R
has out-degree at most
1
and every vertex in
L
has in-degree at most
1
, together with integral parameters
d^{*}>0
and
\Delta>0
.
All edge lengths in
H
are unit.
The algorithm consists of at most
\Delta
iterations. At the beginning of every iteration, the algorithm is given some pair
x,y\in V(H)
of vertices, and it must return a
simple
path
P
connecting
x
to
y
in
H
, whose length is at most
d^{*}
. After that, some edges of
P
may be deleted from
H
. At any time, the algorithm may provide a well-structured cut
(X,Y)
in
H
, whose sparsity is at most
O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d^{*}}\right)
. If
|X|\leq|Y|
, then the cut must be provided by listing the vertices of
X
, and otherwise it must be provided by listing the vertices of
Y
. After that the vertices of the smaller side of the cut are deleted from
H
. In other words, if
|X|\leq|Y|
, then we continue with the graph
H[Y]
, and otherwise we continue with the graph
H[X]
. Once the number of vertices in
H
falls below half the original number, the algorithm halts, even if fewer than
\Delta
iterations occurred.

We emphasize that the only updates that graph
H
undergoes are the deletion of some edges on the paths that the algorithm returns in response to queries; we think of these updates as performed by the adversary. Additionally, whenever the algorithm produces a sparse well-structured cut
(X,Y)
, it deletes the vertices of the smaller of the two sets from
H
. We think of this type of update as being performed by the algorithm itself. These are the only updates that graph
H
may undergo.

Below is our main theorem for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem; its proof is deferred to
Section

8
.

Theorem 6.2
.

There is an algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem, that, given an
n
-vertex graph
H
as in the problem definition and parameters
c^{*}\log^{6}n\leq d^{*}\leq n
, and
1\leq\Delta\leq n
, where
c^{*}
is a large enough constant, has running time at most:

\widetilde{O}(m\cdot d^{*}+n^{2})\cdot\max\left\{1,\frac{\Delta\cdot(d^{*})^{2}}{n}\right\},

where
m
is the number of edges in
H
at the beginning of the algorithm.

We note that the running time of the algorithm in the above theorem includes both the total update time of the data structure and the time needed to respond to queries.
We also note that some of the results from
[
BGS20
]
can be viewed as providing an algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem. In particular, by combining their Theorem 4.3 with Theorem 4.4 and Theorem 4.5, one can obtain such an algorithm (except for minor technical differences, e.g. the algorithm produces sparse vertex cuts and not edge cuts). The total update time of their algorithm is roughly
\widehat{O}(|E(H)|\cdot(d^{*})^{2})
. Our algorithm achieves both a better running time for the specific setting that we consider, where
\Delta\cdot(d^{*})^{2}\leq\widetilde{O}(n)
, and is also significantly simpler.

7
Algorithm for Restricted
SSSP
: Proof of
Theorem

3.7

In this section we provide an algorithm for the restricted
SSSP
problem, proving
Theorem

3.7
. Recall that we are given as input a well-structured graph
G
of size
m
(see
Definition

3.5
), where
m
is a given parameter, together with another parameter
\Delta>0
. We denote
|V(G)|=n
and
\Lambda=\frac{8m\log m}{\Delta}
. For every edge
e\in E(G)
, we are also given a length
\ell(e)
, that is an integral power of
2
between
1
and
16\Lambda
.

The algorithm consists of at most
\Delta
iterations. At the beginning of every iteration, the algorithm is required to return a simple
s
-
t
path
P
in the current graph
G
of length at most
8\Lambda
. We assume that for each edge
e\in E(P)
, edge
e
is the shortest among all its parallel copies. Every edge on
P
is then deleted from
G
, and the iteration ends.

The algorithm is allowed, at any time, to halt and return FAIL, but it may only do so if the following condition is violated:

C3.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be the largest-cardinality collection of
s
-
t
paths in the current graph
G
, such that the paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
are edge-disjoint, every path has length at most
\Lambda
in the current graph. Then
{|{\mathcal{Q}}|\geq\frac{\Delta}{256\log^{2}m}}
holds.

We use a parameter
{d=\sqrt{n/\Delta}}
.
We say that an edge
e\in E(G)
is
long
if
{\ell(e)\geq\frac{\Lambda}{64d\log m}}
, and otherwise we say that it is
short
.
We denote the set of all long edges by
E^{\mathsf{long}}
.

Our algorithm consists of two parts. The first part is responsible for maintaining an
\mathcal{ATO}

({\mathcal{X}},\rho)
of graph
G
, and the second part essentially executes the algorithm from
Theorem

5.1
on the corresponding contracted graph
G_{|{\mathcal{X}}}
. We now describe each of the two parts in turn.

7.1
Part 1: Maintaining the
\mathcal{ATO}
.

In this part we will work with a graph
\hat{G}
that is obtained from
G\setminus E^{\mathsf{long}}
, by setting the length of every edge to
1
, and removing parallel edges. Notice that
\hat{G}
is a dynamic graph that undergoes online edge deletions. We will maintain an
\mathcal{ATO}

({\mathcal{X}},\rho)
for this graph (that will naturally also define an
\mathcal{ATO}
for
G
).

We start with a partition
{\mathcal{X}}
of
V(\hat{G})
, containing three sets:
S=\left\{s\right\},T=\left\{t\right\}
, and
J=V(\hat{G})\setminus\left\{s,t\right\}
.
Their corresponding intervals are initialized to
I_{S}=\left\{1\right\}
,
I_{T}=\left\{n\right\}
, and
I_{J}=\left\{2,\ldots,n-1\right\}
. For convenience, we call the sets of vertices in
{\mathcal{X}}

clusters
.

Whenever a new cluster
X
joins
{\mathcal{X}}
(excluding the sets
S
and
T
), we set
{d_{X}=\frac{|X|}{n}\cdot d}
. If
d_{X}\geq c^{*}\log^{6}|X|
, where
c^{*}
is the constant from
Theorem

6.2
, then we say that
X
is a
non-leaf cluster
. We then immediately initialize the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem from
Theorem

6.2
on the corresponding graph
\hat{G}[X]
, with distance parameter
d^{*}=d_{X}
, and parameter
\Delta
remaining unchanged.
Notice that, since
d=\sqrt{n/\Delta}
, we get that
d_{X}=\frac{|X|}{n}\cdot d=\frac{|X|}{\sqrt{n\cdot\Delta}}\leq|X|
, and, since
d_{X}=\frac{|X|}{\sqrt{n\cdot\Delta}}\geq c^{*}\log^{6}|X|
, we get that
|X|\geq\sqrt{n\cdot\Delta}
, and so
\Delta\leq\frac{|X|^{2}}{n}\leq|X|
, as required from the definition of the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem and the conditions of
Theorem

6.2
.

If
d_{X}<c^{*}\log^{6}|X|
, then we say that
X
is a
leaf
cluster. In this case, we replace
X
in set
{\mathcal{X}}
with
|X|
new clusters, each of which consists of a single distinct vertex of
X
. For each such new cluster
\left\{v\right\}
, we assign an interval
I_{\left\{v\right\}}\subseteq I_{X}
, that consists of a single integer arbitrarily, but we ensure that the intervals assigned to vertices of
L
appear before intervals assigned to vertices of
R
. This ensures that all regular edges of
\hat{G}[X]
are left-to-right edges, and the special edges are right-to-left edges. We call all special edges of
\hat{G}[X]

bad edges
, and we say that
X

owns
these edges. Clearly,
X
owns at most
|X|
bad edges, and each such bad edge
e
has
\operatorname{span}(e)\leq|X|
. Observe that, since
d_{X}=\frac{|X|}{n}\cdot d
and
d_{X}<c^{*}\log^{6}|X|\leq c^{*}\log^{6}n
, it must be the case that
|X|\leq\frac{c^{*}n\log^{6}n}{d}
.

For convenience, for every cluster
X
, we denote by
X_{0}
the set
X
of vertices at the time when it first joined
{\mathcal{X}}
, and by
m_{0}(X_{0})
the number of edges in graph
\hat{G}[X_{0}]
at the time when
X_{0}
joined
{\mathcal{X}}
. We will also sometimes denote
d_{X}
by
d_{X_{0}}
.

Consider now some non-leaf cluster
X
.
Recall that the algorithm from
Theorem

6.2
, when applied to
X
, has running time at most:

\widetilde{O}(m_{0}(X_{0})\cdot d_{X_{0}}+|X_{0}|^{2})\cdot\max\left\{1,\frac{\Delta\cdot(d_{X_{0}})^{2}}{|X_{0}|}\right\}.

Since
d_{X_{0}}=\frac{|X_{0}|}{n}\cdot d
and
d=\sqrt{\frac{n}{\Delta}}
, we get that
\frac{\Delta\cdot d_{X_{0}}^{2}}{|X_{0}|}=\frac{\Delta\cdot|X_{0}|d^{2}}{n^{2}}=\frac{|X_{0}|}{n}\leq 1
. Therefore, the running time of the algorithm is bounded by:

\widetilde{O}(m_{0}(X_{0})\cdot d_{X_{0}}+|X_{0}|^{2}).

Once the number of vertices in
X
falls below
|X_{0}|/2
, the algorithm from
Theorem

6.2
terminates, and we update the distance parameter
d_{X}=\frac{|X|}{n}\cdot d
. If
d_{X}\geq c^{*}\log^{6}|X|
, then we apply the algorithm from
Theorem

6.2
to this new cluster

again, with the updated parameter

; otherwise cluster
X
becomes a leaf cluster and is treated as such. In either case, we will think of
X
as a set that newly joined
{\mathcal{X}}
.

Whenever the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem on graph
\hat{G}[X]
produces a cut
(A,B)
in
\hat{G}[X]
of sparsity at most
O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d_{X_{0}}}\right)
, we proceed as follows.
We partition inteval
I_{X}
into two intervals: interval

of size
|A|
and interval
I_{B}\subseteq I_{X}
of size

, so that

appears to the left of
I_{A}
. We say that set
X_{0}

owns
the edges in both
E_{\hat{G}}(A,B)
and
E_{\hat{G}}(B,A)
. Notice that edges of
E_{\hat{G}}(B,A)
are directed from left to right, and edges of
E_{\hat{G}}(A,B)
are directed from right to left. We will say that the edges of
E_{\hat{G}}(A,B)
are
bad
edges.
Recall that, if
|A|\leq|B|
, the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem deletes
A
from
X
and continues with the new set
X=B
.
We then assign interval
I_{B}
to the new set
X=B
, and we add the set
A
to the collection
{\mathcal{X}}
of vertex subsets that we maintain (after which the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem is applied to
\hat{G}[A]
if it is a non-leaf cluster, or it is processed as a leaf cluster otherwise). We say that set
X
of vertices just underwent
splitting
, and that cluster
A
was split off from it. Similarly, if
|B|<|A|
, the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem deletes
B
from

and continues with the new set
X=A
.
We then assign interval
I_{A}
to the new set
X=A
, and we add the set
B
to the collection
{\mathcal{X}}
of vertex subsets that we maintain. We say that set
X
of vertices just underwent splitting, and that cluster
B
was split off from it.

It is easy to verify by a simple charging argument that the total number of bad edges that cluster
X_{0}
owns at the end of the algorithm is bounded by
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{|X_{0}|}{d_{X_{0}}}\right)
. It is also easy to verify that each such bad edge

has
\operatorname{span}(e)\leq|X_{0}|
when it first becomes a bad edge, and from that point onward

may only decrease.
This completes the description of the first part of the algorithm. We now bound its total update time and the total span of all bad edges.

Bounding the Running Time of Part 1.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
be the collection of all non-leaf sets
X_{0}
that were ever added to
{\mathcal{X}}
, excluding
S
and
T
. For all
0\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\log n\right\rfloor
, let
{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}^{*}
be the collection of all sets
X_{0}
with
2^{i}\leq|X_{0}|<2^{i+1}
. It is easy to verify that all sets in the collection
{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
are mutually disjoint, and so
|{\mathcal{X}}_{i}|\leq\frac{n}{2^{i}}
.

Recall that for each set
X_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
, the total update time of the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem on
\hat{G}[X]
from
Theorem

6.2
is most:

\widetilde{O}(m_{0}(X_{0})\cdot d_{X_{0}}+|X_{0}|^{2}).

Since
d_{X_{0}}=\frac{|X_{0}|}{n}\cdot d
and
|X_{0}|\leq 2^{i+1}
, we get that this time is bounded by:

\begin{split}\widetilde{O}(m_{0}(X_{0})\cdot d_{X_{0}}+|X_{0}|^{2})&\leq\widetilde{O}\left(m_{0}(X_{0})\cdot\frac{|X_{0}|}{n}\cdot d+|X_{0}|^{2}\right)\\
&\leq\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{2^{i}\cdot m_{0}(X_{0})}{n}\cdot d+2^{2i}\right)\end{split}

Since
|{\mathcal{X}}_{i}|\leq\frac{n}{2^{i}}
, the total running time for all clusters
X_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
is bounded by:

\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{2^{i}\cdot m\cdot d}{n}+n\cdot 2^{i}\right),

where
m
is the number of edges in
G
at the beginning of the algorithm.

Summing over
0\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\log n\right\rfloor
, we get that the total update time over all invocations of the algorithm for the
\mathsf{MaintainCluster}
problem is bounded by
{\widetilde{O}\left(md+n^{2}\right)}
. The remaining time that is required in order to maintain the
\mathcal{ATO}
is subsumed by this time.

Bounding the Total Span of All Bad Edges.

We partition the set of all bad edges into two subsets. The first subset,
E_{1}
, contains all edges that are owned by non-leaf clusters.

Recall that for all
0\leq i\leq\left\lfloor\log n\right\rfloor
, every cluster
X_{0}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
may own at most
\widetilde{O}\left(\frac{|X_{0}|}{d_{X_{0}}}\right)
bad edges. Initially, when an edge
e
is added to the set of bad edges, it has
\operatorname{span}(e)\leq|X_{0}|
, and after that its
\operatorname{span}(e)
value may only decrease. In our calculations, for each bad edge
e
, we consider the initial value
\operatorname{span}(e)
, when the edge was just added to the set of bad edges. The summation of all
\operatorname{span}(e)
values of all bad edges owned by clusters in
{\mathcal{X}}_{i}
is then bounded by:

Observe first that, from our discussion so far,

into

We denote the number of vertices in

, so

Over the course of Step 2, some vertices may be deleted from

We also use a parameter

, and we will ensure that at all times, for every pair

\mbox{\sf dist}_{W^{*}\setminus F}(x,y),\mbox{\sf dist}_{W^{*}\setminus F}(y,x)\leq\hat{d}
.

We maintain two additional graphs: graph

We initialize the

data structure from
Theorem

2.4
for graph

, with source vertex

d+1
. We denote the corresponding trees by

and

, respectively.

We will ensure that, throughout the algorithm, every vertex
v
of

lies in both

and

, so that
\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(V(W^{*}),v),\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(v,V(W^{*}))\leq d

data structures to identify vertices

for which this no longer holds. Whenever we identify a vertex

\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(v,V(W^{*}))>d
, we use the algorithm from

in order to compute a well-structured cut
(A,B)
in

with

,

, and
|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\frac{2^{11}\log n}{d}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d^{*}}\right)\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}

, then we delete the vertices of

from

, (which includes deleting these vertices and their incident edges from graphs

, and

and

falls below

, then we delete the vertices of

from

, and terminate the current phase. Notice that in this case, at least
|V(W^{*})|\geq n^{\prime\prime}/4\geq n/64
vertices have been deleted from

in the current phase.

Similarly, whenever we identify a vertex
v
with
\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(V(W^{*}),v)>d
, we use the algorithm from

(but with reversed directions of edges) in order to compute a well-structured cut
(A,B)

,

, and
|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\frac{2^{11}\log n}{d}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d^{*}}\right)\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}

from

, and from both

from

In either case, if the set of vertices that was deleted from

is bounded by

. Therefore, the total time that the algorithm ever spends over the course of Step 2 to cut sparse cuts off from

For every edge

. We can initialize the sets

is subsumed by the time spent on constructing the embedding

of

into

A Cleanup Procedure

The purpose of the cleanup procedure is to ensure that every vertex of
H
lies in both the trees
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
, and additionally, that for every pair
x,y
of vertices of
W^{*}
,
\mbox{\sf dist}_{W^{*}\setminus F}(x,y),\mbox{\sf dist}_{W^{*}\setminus F}(y,x)\leq\hat{d}
holds. The cleanup procedure is executed before each query. In other words, we execute the procedure once at the beginning of Phase 2, before responding to any queries. Then, after every query, once graph
H
is updated with the relevant edge deletions, we execute the procedure again.

Consider for example a
short-path
query, and suppose the algorithm returned a path
P^{*}
in response to the query. Recall that some edges of
P^{*}
may be deleted from
H
. As a result, it is possible that some vertices of
H
no longer belong to one or both of the trees
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}
. As described already, we will compute a sparse cut in
H
in order to fix this issue, but this may in turn affect the expander
W^{*}
. Additionally, the deletion of the edges from
H
may lead to the deletion of some edges from the expander
W^{*}
. As a result, it is possible that the diameter of
W^{*}\setminus F
grows beyond
\hat{d}
. In such a case, we will compute a sparse cut in graph
W^{*}\setminus F
, but this may in turn affect the trees
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}
, since, whenever a vertex
x
leaves the set
V(W^{*})
of vertices, we delete edge
(s^{\prime},x)
from
T^{\prime}
and we delete edge
(s^{\prime\prime},x)
from
T^{\prime\prime}
. Similarly, it is possible that, at the beginning of Phase 2, not all vertices of
H
lie in the trees
T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime}
, or that the diameter of
W^{*}\setminus F
is greater than
\hat{d}
. Fixing each of these issues may in turn create new issues as described above. We now formally describe the cleanup procedure.

At the beginning of the cleanup procedure, we select an arbitrary vertex
v\in V(W^{*})
, and compute two shortest-paths trees
\hat{T}^{\prime}
and
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
rooted at
v
in graph
W^{*}\setminus F
, where the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime}
are directed away from
v
, and the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
are directed towards
v
. In other words, for every vertex
a\in V(W^{*})
, the unique
v
-
a
path in
\hat{T}^{\prime}
is a shortest
v
-
a
path in
W^{*}\setminus F
, and the unique
a
-
v
path in
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
is a shortest
a
-
v
path in
W^{*}\setminus F
. Note that both trees can be computed in time
O(n)
. We then perform a number of iterations. At the beginning of every iteration, we assume that we are given some vertex
v\in V(W^{*})
, and two shortest-paths trees
\hat{T}^{\prime}
and
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
in
W^{*}\setminus F
that are both rooted at
v
, with the edges of
\hat{T}^{\prime}
directed away from
v
, and the edges of
\hat{T}^{\prime}
directed towards
v
. The iterations are executed as long as at least one of the following two conditions hold: (i) some vertex
u\in V(H)
does not lie in
T^{\prime}
or in
T^{\prime\prime}
; or (ii) the depth of at least one of the trees
\hat{T}^{\prime},\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
is greater than
\hat{d}
. We now describe the execution of a single iteration.

Assume first that some vertex
u\in V(H)
no longer lies in either
T^{\prime}
or
T^{\prime\prime}
.
In this case, we say that the current iteration is a
type-1
iteration. Assume first that
u\not\in T^{\prime\prime}
,
in other words,
\mbox{\sf dist}_{H}(u,V(W^{*}))>d
holds. As we described already, we use the algorithm from
8.2
in order to compute a well-structured cut
(A,B)
in
H
with
u\in A
,
V(W^{*})\subseteq B
, and
|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\frac{2^{11}\log n}{d}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d^{*}}\right)\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}
. If
|A|\leq|B|
, then we delete the vertices of
A
from
H
, (which includes deleting these vertices and their incident edges from graphs
H,H^{\prime}
, and
H^{\prime\prime}
, and both
ES-Tree
data structures
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
). If
|V(H)|
falls below
n^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{1}{\hat{c}\log^{5}n}\right)
, then the algorithm is terminated, and otherwise we continue the algorithm. Alternatively, if
|A|>|B|
, then we delete the vertices of
B
from
H
, and terminate the current phase.
Similarly, if some vertex
u\in V(H)
no longer lies in
T^{\prime}
, then, as desribed above, we use the algorithm from
8.2
(but with reversed directions of edges) in order to compute a well-structured cut
(A,B)
in
H
with
V(W^{*})\subseteq A
,
u\in B
, and
|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\frac{2^{11}\log n}{d}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}\leq O\left(\frac{\log^{6}n}{d^{*}}\right)\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}
. As before, if
|B|\leq|A|
, then we delete the vertices of
B
from
H
, and from both
ES-Tree
data structures. If
|V(H)|
falls below
n^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{1}{\hat{c}\log^{5}n}\right)
, then the algorithm is terminated, and otherwise we continue the algorithm. Alternatively, if
|B|>|A|
, then we delete the vertices of
A
from
H
, and terminate the current phase. In the latter case, at least
n/64
vertices have been deleted from
H
in the current phase.
Let
Z
be the set of all vertices that were deleted from
H
in the current iteration, and let

\hat{E}=\bigcup_{e\in E_{H}(A,B)}S(e)
, that is, these are all edges
e^{\prime}\in E(W^{*})\setminus F
whose embedding path contains an edge of
E_{H}(A,B)
.
Since the embedding of
W^{*}\setminus F
into
H
causes congestion at most
O(d\log^{4}n)
, we get that
|\hat{E}|\leq O(d\log^{4}n)\cdot|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq O(|Z|\cdot\log^{6}n)
.
We delete the edges of
\hat{E}
from graph
W^{*}
. If
Z\cap V(W^{*})=\emptyset
, then we say that the current iteration is a
type-(1a) iteration
, and we continue to the next iteration. Notice that in this case, the running time of the current iteration is
\widetilde{O}(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(Z)\cdot d^{*})
, and we
charge
the running time of the current iteration to the set
Z
of vertices.

Assume now that
Z\cap V(W^{*})\neq\emptyset
. In this case, we say that the current iteration is a
type-(1b) iteration
. Let
X=Z\cap V(W^{*})
. So far the total running time of the iteration is
\tilde{O}(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(Z)\cdot d^{*})
, and it is charged to the set
Z
of vertices. Next, we delete the vertices of
X
from
W^{*}
. The remainder of the running time of the current iteration will be charged to set
X
. Notice that, if
Z=A
, and we denote
Z^{\prime}=B
, then graph
W^{*}\setminus F
no longer contains any edges connecting vertices of
Z\cap V(W^{*})
to vertices of
Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*})
. In other words,
(Z\cap V(W^{*}),Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*}))
is a cut of sparsity
0
in
W^{*}\setminus F
.
Similarly, if
Z=B
, and we denote
Z^{\prime}=A
, then cut
(Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*}),Z\cap V(W^{*}))
is a cut of sparsity
0
in
W^{*}\setminus F
. Once the vertices of
X
are deleted from
W^{*}
, we select an arbitrary vertex
v\in V(W^{*})
, and compute two shortest-paths trees
\hat{T}^{\prime}
and
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
rooted at
v
in graph
W^{*}\setminus F
, where the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime}
are directed away from
v
, and the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
are directed towards
v
. It is easy to verify that the total running time of a type-(1b) iteration is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(Z)\cdot d^{*})+O(n)
. We charge time
\widetilde{O}(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(Z)\cdot d^{*})
to the set
Z
of vertices that is deleted from
H
in the current iteration, and we charge time
O(n)
to the set
X
of vertices that is deleted from
W^{*}
in the current iteration.
Lastly, graphs
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime}
need to be updated to reflect the deletion of the vertices of
X
from
W^{*}
: namely, for all
u\in X
, we delete the edge
(s^{\prime},u)
from
H^{\prime}
, and we delete the edge
(s^{\prime\prime},u)
from
H^{\prime\prime}
. This, in turn, may require updating the
ES-Tree
data structures
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
. The time required to update the two trees is counted as part of the total update time of these data structures, and not as part of the running time of the current cleanup procedure.
This completes the description of a type-(1b) iteration.

If the depth of either of the trees
\hat{T}^{\prime},\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
is greater than
\hat{d}
, then we say that the current iteration is a type-2 iteration. In this case, we can compute, in time
O(1)
, a pair
a,b\in V(W^{*})
of vertices with
\mbox{\sf dist}_{W^{*}\setminus F}(a,b)>\hat{d}
.
Recall that
\hat{d}=\frac{2^{30}\log n}{\alpha_{0}}
, and the maximum vertex degree in
W^{*}
is bounded by
D=18
. We can now use the algorithm from
2.2
to compute, in time
O(n)
, a cut
(A,B)
in
W^{*}\setminus F
of sparsity at most
\varphi=\frac{32D\log n}{\hat{d}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{20}}
. If
|A|\leq|B|
, then we delete from
W^{*}
the vertices of
A
, and otherwise we delete from
W^{*}
the vertices of
B
.
If we denote by
X
the set of vertices deleted from
W^{*}
in the current iteration, then graphs
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime}
need to be updated accordingly (namely, for all
u\in X
, we delete the edge
(s^{\prime},u)
from
H^{\prime}
and we delete the edge
(s^{\prime\prime},u)
from
H^{\prime\prime}
). This, in turn, may require updating the
ES-Tree
data structures
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
. The time required to update the two trees is counted as part of the total update time of these data structures, and not as part of the running time of the current cleanup procedure.
We then select an arbitrary vertex
v\in V(W^{*})
, and compute two shortest-paths trees
\hat{T}^{\prime}
and
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
rooted at
v
in graph
W^{*}\setminus F
, where the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime}
are directed away from
v
, and the edges in
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
are directed towards
v
. Clearly, the running time of a type-2 iteration is
O(n)
, and we charge it to the set of vertices (either
A
or
B
) that was deleted from
W^{*}
over the course of the current iteration.

The algorithm for the cleanup procedure terminates once every vertex of
H
lies in both
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
, and the depths of the trees
\hat{T}^{\prime}
and
\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
are both bounded by
\hat{d}
.

We now bound the total time required to perform the cleanup procedure over the course of the entire Step 2.
Since we only need to respond to at most
N=O\left(\frac{n}{(d^{*})^{2}\log n}\right)
queries, the number of times that the cleanup procedure is called is bounded by
N
. The time that is required in order to compute the shortest-path trees
\hat{T}^{\prime},\hat{T}^{\prime\prime}
in
W^{*}\setminus F
for the first time at the beginning of each cleanup procedure is then bounded by
O(n\cdot N)\leq\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
.

If we denote by
Z
the set of all vertices that are deleted over the course of the phase from graph
H
, and by
{\mathcal{X}}
the collection of all subsets of vertices that were deleted from
V(W^{*})
over the course of the phase, then, from our charging scheme, the remaining running time that is spent on the repeated executions of the cleanup procedure can be bounded by
\widetilde{O}(\operatorname{Vol}_{H}(Z)\cdot d^{*})+O(|{\mathcal{X}}|\cdot n)\leq\widetilde{O}(md^{*}+n^{2})
. Therefore, the total time spent over the course of a single phase on executing the cleanup procedure is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(md^{*}+n^{2})
.

Updates to the Expander
W^{*}

Recall that, at the beginning of Step 2, we are given an
\alpha_{0}
-expander
W^{*}
, with
|V(W^{*})|=n^{\prime\prime}\geq n^{\prime}/8
and maximum vertex degree at most
18
, together with a subset
F\subseteq E(W^{*})
of at most
\frac{\alpha_{0}\cdot n^{\prime}}{500}
edges of
W^{*}
, and an embedding
{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
of
W^{*}\setminus F
into
H
via paths of length at most
O(d\log^{2}n)
, that cause congestion at most
O(d\log^{4}n)
.

Over the course of the algorithm, we may delete some edges from
W^{*}
, and we may also compute sparse cuts in
W^{*}
that decompose
W^{*}
into smaller subgraphs. For convenience, we will maintain a collection
{\mathcal{X}}
of subsets of vertices of
W^{*}
that partition the original set
V(W^{*})
. At the beginning of the algorithm,
{\mathcal{X}}
contains a single set
V(W^{*})
of vertices. Over the course of the algorithm, if we identify a sparse cut
(X,Y)
in the current graph
W^{*}\setminus F
, we replace
V(W^{*})
with
X
and
Y
in
{\mathcal{X}}
, and we delete the vertices of the smaller of the two sets from
W^{*}
. The key is to ensure that, since the cuts that we compute are sparse, and since the number of edges deleted from
W^{*}
over the course of the second step is relatively small,
|V(W^{*})|\geq n^{\prime\prime}/4
continues to hold throughout Step 2 of the phase.

There are three types of updates that expander
W^{*}
may undergo over the course of the algorithm, that we describe next.

First Type of Updates.

Suppose some edge
e
was deleted from
H
, as part of the update sequence that graph
H
undergoes. In this case, we delete every edge
e^{\prime}\in S(e)
from
W^{*}
. For each such edge
e^{\prime}
, we also delete
e^{\prime}
from the set
S(e^{\prime\prime})
of every edge
e^{\prime\prime}\in E(H)
that lies on the embedding path
P(e^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{P}}^{*}
. Recall that, over the course of the current phase, our algorithm will need to respond to at most
N

short-path
queries. If
Q
is a path that is returned in response to such a query, then
Q
may contain at most
d^{*}
edges, and the only edges that may be deleted from
H
over the course of the current phase as part of the update sequence are edges that lie on such paths. Therefore, the total number of edges that may be deleted from
H
as part of the update sequence during a single phase is at most
N\cdot d^{*}
. Since the embedding of
W^{*}\setminus F
causes congestion at most
O(d\log^{4}n)
in
H
, the number of corresponding edge deletions from
W^{*}
is bounded by:

O(N\cdot d^{*}\cdot d\log^{4}n)\leq N\cdot(d^{*})^{2}\cdot\log n\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{20}},

since
d=\frac{d^{*}}{c^{\prime}\log^{3}n}
, where
c^{\prime}
can be chosen as a large enough constant and
N=\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{20}(d^{*})^{2}\log n}
.
Throughout, we denote by
E^{\prime}
the set of edges that were deleted from
W^{*}
as the result of this type of updates to
W^{*}
, so
|E^{\prime}|\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{20}}
always holds. The time that is required to update graph
W^{*}
with this type of updates is subsumed by the time spent in Step 1 of the algorithm.

Second Type of Updates.

The second type of update happens when the algorithm for Step 2 identifies a well-structured cut
(A,B)
in
H
with
|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq\frac{2^{11}\log n}{d}\cdot\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}
. Denote sets
A
and
B
by
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
, so that
Z
is the smaller of the two sets, breaking ties arbitrarily. Recall that our algorithm then deletes the vertices of
Z
from
H
. If, as the result of this deletion, the number of vertices in
H
falls below
\left(1-\frac{1}{\hat{c}\log^{5}n}\right)n^{\prime}
, then the current phase terminates. If this did not happen, then we update graph
W^{*}
as follows. Let
\hat{E}=\bigcup_{e\in E_{H}(A,B)}S(e)
, that is, these are all edges
e^{\prime}\in E(W^{*})\setminus F
whose embedding path contains an edge of
E_{H}(A,B)
. Since the embedding of
W^{*}\setminus F
into
H
causes congestion at most
O(d\log^{4}n)
in
H
, we get that
|\hat{E}|\leq O(d\log^{4}n)\cdot|E_{H}(A,B)|\leq O(|Z|\cdot\log^{5}n)
.

We start by deleting the edges of
\hat{E}
from
W^{*}
. Note that, if
Z=A
and
Z^{\prime}=B
, then graph
W^{*}\setminus F
no longer contains any edges connecting vertices of
Z\cap V(W^{*})
to vertices of
Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*})
. In other words,
(Z\cap V(W^{*}),Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*}))
is a cut of sparsity
0
in
W^{*}\setminus F
. We replace the set
V(W^{*})
of vertices in
{\mathcal{X}}
by two sets:
Z\cap V(W^{*})
and
Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*})
. We also delete the vertices of
Z
from
W^{*}
. Similarly, if
Z=B
and
Z^{\prime}=A
, then
(Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*}),Z\cap V(W^{*}))
is a cut of sparsity
0
in
W^{*}\setminus F
. We replace the set
V(W^{*})
of vertices in
{\mathcal{X}}
by two sets:
Z\cap V(W^{*})
and
Z^{\prime}\cap V(W^{*})
, and we delete the vertices of
Z
from
W^{*}
.

We denote by
E^{\prime\prime}
the set of edges that were deleted from
W^{*}
as the result of this type of transformation: in other words, for each sparse cut
(A,B)
of
H
that our algorithm computes, we add all edges of
\bigcup_{e\in E_{H}(A,B)}S(e)
to
E^{\prime\prime}
. Recall that the number of edges added to

We use the Cut-Matching Game – a framework that was first proposed by

]
. The specific variant of the game is due to
[
KKOV07
]

[

[

]

We also note that
[

extended the original cut-matching game of
[

]
to directed graphs, though we do not use this variation here.
We now define the variant of the game that we use.

The game is played between two players: the Cut Player and the Matching Player.
The game starts with a directed graph
W
that contains
n
vertices and no edges, where
n
is an even integer, and proceeds in iterations. In the
i
th iteration, the Cut Player chooses a cut

in

of vertices with

[

]

[

]
for undirected graphs.

Theorem 8.4
(Lemma 7.4 in
[

]
; see also Theorem 7.3 and the following discussion).

The number of iterations in the above Cut-Matching Game is bounded by

.

in

must hold. Notice that the maximum vertex degree in the final graph

is bounded by

.

In order to implement the Cut-Matching Game, we design two algorithms: an algorithm for the Cut Player, and an algorithm for the Matching Player. We note that previous work, such as, e.g.

]

|\hat{F}|\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}\cdot n}{1000}
, we get that
|F|\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}\cdot n}{500}
.

It now remains to bound the running time of the algorithm. The algorithm has
O(\log n)
iterations. In every iteration we use the algorithm for the Cut Player from
Theorem

8.5
, whose running time is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
, and an algorithm for the Matching Player from
Theorem

8.6
, whose running time is
\widetilde{O}(md^{\prime})
.

Additionally, when computing the embedding of
W^{\prime}\setminus F
into
G
, we spend time at most
\tilde{O}(d^{\prime})
per edge of
W^{\prime}
, and, since
|E(W^{\prime})|\leq O(n)
, the computation of the embedding takes time
\tilde{O}(nd^{\prime})
.
Overall, the running time of the algorithm is
\widetilde{O}(md^{\prime}+n^{2})
.

Algorithm for the Cut Player – Proof of
Theorem

8.5

The proof of

Theorem 8.7
.

There is a deterministic algorithm, that, given an

(A,B)
in

another directed

of at most

We prove

below, after we complete the proof of

8.5
using it. We start with the graph

, and then perform iterations, as long as

from

In order to execute a single iteration, we apply the algorithm from

8.7
to the current graph

. Let

, that cause congestion at most

Assume now that the algorithm from
Theorem

8.7
returned a cut
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
in
\hat{W}
with
|E_{\hat{W}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})|\leq\frac{\min\left\{|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\right\}}{128}

Denote the sets

by

If the above algorithm does not terminate with an expander

that is embedded into

, then it must terminate once

holds. Let
{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{r}\right\}

We can now use the algorithm from

The running time of every iteration of the algorithm is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
, and the number of iterations is bounded by a constant. The running time of the algorithm from

is bounded by

.

In order to complete the proof of
theorem

8.5

8.7
.

Proof of
Theorem

8.7
.

Since we can afford a running time that is as high as
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
, we can use a straightforward algorithm, that is significantly simpler than other current deterministic algorithms for the cut player (see e.g. Theorem 7.3 in
[
BGS20
]
, that generalizes the results of
[
CGL+19
]
to directed graphs).

We let
c
be a sufficiently large constant, and we set the parameters

\tilde{d}=c\log^{2}n
and
\eta=c^{2}\log^{2}n
.

We start by using the algorithm from
Theorem

2.1
to construct, in time
O(n)
, a directed
n
-vertex graph
W^{\prime}
that is an
\alpha_{0}
-expander, with maximum vertex degree at most
18
.

Next, we start with a graph
\hat{W}=W
, and then perform
|E(W^{\prime})|
iterations. In every iteration we will attempt to embed some edge
e\in E(W^{\prime})
into
\hat{W}
, and we will delete from
\hat{W}
edges that participate in too many of the embedding paths. We will maintain a partition
(E^{\prime},E^{\prime\prime},F)
of the edges of
E(W^{\prime})
, where set
E^{\prime}
contains all edges
e\in E^{\prime}
for which an embedding path
P(e)
was already computed by the algorithm; set
F
contains all edges
e\in E^{\prime}
for which the algorithm failed to compute an embedding path; and
E^{\prime\prime}
contains all edges of
W^{\prime}
that were not considered by the algorithm yet. We also maintain a set
{\mathcal{P}}=\left\{P(e)\mid e\in E^{\prime}\right\}
of all embedding paths that the algorithm computed so far. For every edge
e\in E(W)
we will maintain a counter
\mu(e)
that counts the number of paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
containing
e
.

Initially, we set
E^{\prime}=F=\emptyset
,
E^{\prime\prime}=E(W^{\prime})
, and
{\mathcal{P}}=\emptyset
. We also set
\hat{W}=W
, and perform iterations as long as
E^{\prime\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

In order to perform an iteration, we consider any edge
e=(u,v)\in E^{\prime\prime}
. We compute the shortest path
P
in the current graph
\hat{W}
connecting
u
to
v
, by peforming a BFS from vertex
u
, in time
O(|E(\hat{W})|)\leq\widetilde{O}(n)
. If the length of
P
is greater than
\tilde{d}
, then we move edge
e
from
E^{\prime\prime}
to
F
. Otherwise, we set
P(e)=P
, move
e
from
E^{\prime\prime}
to
E^{\prime}
, and add path
P(e)
to set
{\mathcal{P}}
. Additionally, for every edge
e^{\prime}\in E(P)
we increase the counter
\mu(e^{\prime})
by
1
, and if the counter reaches
\eta
, we delete
e^{\prime}
from
\hat{W}
.

Once
E^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
holds, the algorithm terminates.
Notice that, since the algorithm spends at most
\widetilde{O}(n)
time on processing every edge of
W^{\prime}
, its running time so far is
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
.

We now consider two cases. Assume first that, at the end of the above procedure,
|F|\leq k
holds. In this case, we terminate the algorithm, and return the
\alpha_{0}
-expander
W^{\prime}
, the set
F
of its edges, and the embedding
{\mathcal{P}}
of
W^{\prime}\setminus F
into
W
that our algorithm has computed. It is easy to verify that every path in
{\mathcal{P}}
has length at most
\tilde{d}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}
causes congestion at most
\eta
in
W
.

From now on we assume that
|F|>k
. We now show an algorithm to compute a cut
(A,B)
in
W
of sparsity at most
\frac{1}{128}
, such that
|A|,|B|\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{14}}\cdot n
.

Let
E^{*}=E(W)\setminus E(\hat{W})
. Equivalently, set
E^{*}
contains all edges that participate in
\eta
paths in
{\mathcal{P}}
. Recall that
|{\mathcal{P}}|\leq|E(W^{\prime})|\leq 9n
, and the length of every path in
{\mathcal{P}}
is at most
\tilde{d}
. Therefore,
\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}}|E(P)|\leq 9\tilde{d}n
, and
|E^{*}|\leq\frac{\sum_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}}|E(P)|}{\eta}\leq\frac{9\tilde{d}n}{\eta}
.

In the remainder of the algorithm, we will iteratively cut off sparse cuts (that may be quite small) from graph
\hat{W}
. We maintain a set
{\mathcal{X}}
of subsets of vertices of
\hat{W}
, that is initially empty. The algorithm is iterative, and in every iteration a single set
Z
is added to
{\mathcal{X}}
, with the vertices of
Z
deleted from
\hat{W}
. The algorithm continues as long as
|V(\hat{W})|\geq n-k/2
holds.
We now describe the execution of a single iteration.

Since
|V(\hat{W})|\geq n-k/2
, there exists an edge
e=(x,y)\in F
with
x,y\in\hat{W}
. Moreover, since we were unable to embed
e
into
\hat{W}
when it was considered, and since we only deleted edges and vertices from
\hat{W}
since then, it must be the case that
\mbox{\sf dist}_{\hat{W}}(x,y)>\tilde{d}
holds right now. Let
\Delta=O(\log n)
denote the maximum vertex degree in
\hat{W}
. Since
\tilde{d}=c\log^{2}n
, where
c
is a large enough constant, we can ensure that
\tilde{d}\geq 64\Delta\log n
holds. We now use the algorithm from
2.2
to compute a cut
(Z,Z^{\prime})
in the current graph
\hat{W}
, whose sparsity is at most
\varphi^{\prime}=\frac{32\Delta\log n}{\tilde{d}}\leq\frac{O(\log^{2}n)}{\tilde{d}}
. Since
\tilde{d}=c\log^{2}n
, and we can set
c
to be a large enough constant, we can ensure that
\varphi^{\prime}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{22}}
. Denote the sets
Z,Z^{\prime}
by
X
and
Y
, such that
X
is the set of smaller cardinality, and
Y
is the other set (if the cardinalities of the two sets are equal, break the tie arbitrarily). We then add
X
to
{\mathcal{X}}
, delete the vertices of
X
from
\hat{W}
, and continue to the next iteration. Note that, if
X
is the set of vertices that was added to
{\mathcal{X}}
during the current iteration, then from
2.2
, the running time of the iteration is bounded by
\tilde{O}(|X|)
.

When the above algorithm terminates, we let
{\mathcal{X}}=\left\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{r}\right\}
be the final collection of vertex subsets, where the sets are indexed in the order in which they were added to
{\mathcal{X}}
. We also denote by
X_{r+1}
the set
V(\hat{W})
obtained at the end of the algorithm, and we add
X_{r+1}
to
{\mathcal{X}}
as well. For all
1\leq j\leq r
, denote
X^{\prime}_{j}=X_{j+1}\cup\cdots\cup X_{r+1}
. From our algorithm, for each such index
j
, either
|E_{\hat{W}}(X_{j},X^{\prime}_{j})|\leq\varphi^{\prime}\cdot|X_{j}|
, or
|E_{\hat{W}}(X^{\prime}_{j},X_{j})|\leq\varphi^{\prime}\cdot|X_{j}|
, where
\varphi^{\prime}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{22}}
.
From our discussion, the total time that the algorithm spent on constructing the sets of vertices in
{\mathcal{X}}
is bounded by
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\tilde{O}(|X_{j}|)\leq\tilde{O}(n)
.
Let
\alpha
be chosen such that the cardinality of largest set of vertices in
{\mathcal{X}}
is
\alpha\cdot n
. From our algorithm,
\alpha\leq 1-\frac{k}{2n}\leq 1-\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2000}
, since
k=\frac{\alpha_{0}\cdot n}{1000}
.

We can now use the algorithm from
2.3
to compute, in time
O(|E(\hat{W})|)\leq\widetilde{O}(n)
, a cut
(A,B)
in
\hat{W}
, with
|E_{\hat{W}}(A,B)|\leq\varphi^{\prime}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{r}|X_{i}|
, and
|A|,|B|\geq\min\left\{\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\cdot n,\frac{n}{4}\right\}\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{4000}
.

Recall that graph
\hat{W}
was obtained from
W
by deleting the edges of
E^{*}
from it, and that
|E^{*}|\leq\frac{9\tilde{d}n}{\eta}
. Subtituting
\tilde{d}=c\log^{2}n
and
\eta=c^{2}\log^{2}n
, where
c
is a sufficiently large constant, we get that:

|E^{*}|\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{22}}
.

Overall:

|E_{W}(A,B)|\leq|E_{\hat{W}}(A,B)|+|E^{*}|\leq\varphi^{\prime}n+\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{22}}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{21}}.

(we have used the fact that
\varphi^{\prime}\leq\frac{\alpha_{0}}{2^{22}}
). Lastly, since
|A|,|B|\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{4000}\geq\frac{\alpha_{0}n}{2^{14}}
, we get that
|E_{W}(A,B)|\leq\frac{\min\left\{|A|,|B|\right\}}{128}
, as required.

From our discussion, the running time of the entire algorithm is bounded by
\widetilde{O}(n^{2})
.

8.3
Algorithm for the Matching Player – Proof of
Theorem

8.6

As before, we call the edges of
E_{G}(R,L)

special edges
, and the edges of
E_{G}(L,R)

regular edges
. Notice that every vertex of
G
may be incident to at most one special edge.

As a preprocessing step, we construct a maximal set
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
of disjoint paths, where every path connects a distinct vertex of
A
to a distinct vertex of
B
, and consists of a single regular edge. We do so by employing a simple greedy algorithm. We start with
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}=\emptyset
. While there is a regular edge
e=(a,b)
with
a\in A
and
b\in B
, we add a path
Q=(e)
to
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
, and we delete
a
and
b
from
A
and
B
, respectively. Clearly, this preprocessing step can be executed in time
O(|E(G)|)
, and at the end of this step there is no regular edge in
G
connecting a vertex of
A
to a vertex of
B
.

Let
\hat{G}
be the graph that is obtained from
G
by replacing every edge
e
with
d^{\prime}
parallel copies. Edges that are copies of special edges in
G
are called special edges of
\hat{G}
, and similarly copies of regular edges in
G
are called regular edges of
\hat{G}
.

At the beginning of the algorithm, every special edge
e\in E(\hat{G})
is assigned a length
\ell(e)=1/d^{\prime}
, while every regular edge
e\in E(\hat{G})
is assigned length
0
.
Clearly, at the beginning of the algorithm,
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)\leq n
holds, as there are at most
nd^{\prime}
special edges in
\hat{G}
, each of which has length
1/d^{\prime}
.

Over the course of the algorithm, the lengths of special edges may grow, while the lengths of regular edges remain
0
. Additionally, vertices may be deleted from sets
A
and
B
. We will gradually construct a collection
{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}
of paths in graph
\hat{G}
, where every path connects a distinct vertex of
A
to a distinct vertex of
B
; the number of special edges on every path is at most
d^{\prime}
; and the paths cause edge-congestion at most
O(\log n)
in
\hat{G}
. Initially, we set
{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}=\emptyset
.

We assume for now that we are given an oracle, that, when prompted, either returns a simple path
Q
in the current graph
\hat{G}
connecting some vertex of
A
to some vertex of
B
, such that the length
\sum_{e\in E(Q)}\ell(e)
of the path is at most
1
; or correctly establishes that
such a path does not exist.

The algorithm proceeds in iterations. In every iteration, the oracle is asked to return a simple path
Q
in the current graph
\hat{G}
connecting some vertex of
A
to some vertex of
B
, such that the length
\sum_{e\in E(Q)}\ell(e)
of the path is at most
1
. If it successfully does so, then we add
Q
to
{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}
, double the length of every special edge on path
Q
, remove the endpoints of the path from sets
A
and
B
, respectively, and continue to the next iteration. Notice that in this case,
|{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}|
grows by
1
, while
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)
increases by at most
1
.
If the oracle establishes that no such path
Q
exists, then we terminate the algorithm.

At the end of the algorithm, we set
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathcal{Q}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}
.
If
|{\mathcal{Q}}|\geq n/2-z
, then we return the collection
{\mathcal{Q}}
of paths. It is immediate to verify that the paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
connect vertices of
A
to vertices of
B
, and that their endpoints are disjoint. Moreover, since the length of every special edge in
\hat{G}
is at least
\frac{1}{d^{\prime}}
, while the length of every path
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
is at most
1
, each path in
{\mathcal{Q}}
contains at most
d^{\prime}
special edges, and at most
2d^{\prime}+1
edges overall, since special and regular edges must alternate on every path. Lastly, due to the doubling of edge lengths on the paths that we add to
{\mathcal{Q}}
, every special edge of
\hat{G}
may participate in at most
\log d^{\prime}=O(\log n)
paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
, and so in total every special edge of
G
may participate in at most
O(d^{\prime}\log n)
paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
. Moreover, if
e=(u,v)
is a regular edge, then every path
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{2}
that contains
e
must also contain either the unique special edge incident to
u
, or the unique special edge incident to
v
, while the paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}_{1}
are disjoint. We conclude that the paths in
{\mathcal{Q}}
cause congestion at most
O(d^{\prime}\log n)
in
G
.

From now on we assume that, when the algorithm terminates,
|{\mathcal{Q}}|<n/2-z
holds. Let
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
be the sets of vertices that remain in
A
and
B
, respectively at the end of the algorithm. Since the algorithm terminated, in the current graph
\hat{G}
, the length of the shortest path connecting a vertex of
A^{\prime}
to a vertex of
B^{\prime}
is at least
1
. Since
|{\mathcal{Q}}|<n/2-z
and
|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|=n/2-|{\mathcal{Q}}|
, we get that
|A^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime}|\geq z
.

Recall that, at the beginning of the algorithm,
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)\leq n
held. Over the course of the algorithm, in every iteration,
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)
grew by at most
1
, and the number of iterations is bounded by
|A|\leq n
. Therefore, at the end of the algorithm,
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)\leq 2n
holds.

Let
e
be any edge of graph
G
, and let
S(e)
be the collection of
d
parallel edges representing
e
in graph
\hat{G}
. Let
e^{\prime}\in S(e)
be the copy of
e
with smallest current length
\ell(e^{\prime})
. Notice that, if we set the lengths of all edges in
S(e)
to
\ell(e^{\prime})
, then the length of the shortest path connecting a vertex of
A^{\prime}
to a vertex of
B^{\prime}
in
\hat{G}
will remain at least
1
. Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality, that, for every edge
e\in E(G)
, the lengths of all edges in set
S(e)
are identical; we denote this length by
\ell^{\prime}(e)
.
Notice that
\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)\leq 2n
continues to hold after this transformation.

Consider now the graph
G^{\prime}
that is obtained from
G
by
setting the length of every edge
e\in E(G)
to
\ell^{\prime}(e)
,
unifying all vertices of
A^{\prime}
into a source vertex
s
and all vertices of
B^{\prime}
into a destination vertex
t
. Since graph
\hat{G}
contained
d^{\prime}
parallel copies of every edge
e\in E(G)
, that all had the same length equal to
\ell^{\prime}(e)
, we now have that
\sum_{e\in E(G)}\ell^{\prime}(e)\leq\frac{\sum_{e\in E(\hat{G})}\ell(e)}{d^{\prime}}\leq\frac{2n}{d^{\prime}}
. At the same time, since
\hat{G}
did not contain an
A^{\prime}
-
B^{\prime}
path of length at most
1
, we get that
\mbox{\sf dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s,t)\geq 1
.

Consider the following thought experiment: we choose a threshold
\rho\in(0,1)
uniformly at random, and then define two sets
S=\left\{v\in V(G^{\prime})\mid\mbox{\sf dist}_{G^{\prime}}(s,v)\leq\rho\right\}
and
T=V(G^{\prime})\setminus S
of vertices. Clearly,
s\in S
and
t\in T
must hold.
By replacing
s
with the vertices of
A^{\prime}
in
S
and replacing
t
with the vertices of
B^{\prime}
in
T
, we obtain a cut
(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})
in graph
G
with
|E_{G}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})|=|E_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)|
. It is easy to verify that, for every edge
e=(u,v)\in E(G^{\prime})
, the probability that
u\in S
and
v\in T
is bounded by
\ell^{\prime}(e)
. Therefore, the expected value of
|E_{G^{\prime}}(S,T)|
is bounded by
\sum_{e\in E(G)}\ell^{\prime}(e)\leq\frac{2n}{d^{\prime}}
. By performing a BFS search from vertex
s
, we can then compute a cut
(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})
in
G^{\prime}
with
|E_{G^{\prime}}(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})|\leq\frac{2n}{d^{\prime}}
. Using the procedure outlined above, we can convert this cut into a cut
(S,T)
in
G
with
|E_{G}(S,T)|\leq\frac{2n}{d^{\prime}}
. Since
A^{\prime}\subseteq S
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq T
, we are guaranteed that
|S|,|T|\geq z

For all integers
i\geq 0
, let
S_{i}=B_{G}(x,i)
be the set of all vertices

with
\mbox{\sf dist}_{G}(x,u)\leq i
.

Let

. We claim that, if
|S_{L}|<n/2
, then there is some index

Indeed, assume otherwise. Then for all

Therefore, for all

|S_{i+1}|\geq\left(1+\frac{\varphi}{\delta_{\mbox{{\footnotesize{max}}}}}\right)|S_{i}|

For all integers
i\geq 0

S^{\prime}_{i}=\left\{u\in V(G)\mid\mbox{\sf dist}(u,y)\leq i\right\}

|S^{\prime}_{L}|\leq n/2
, then there is some index

|E_{G}(V\setminus S^{\prime}_{i},S^{\prime}_{i})|\leq\varphi\cdot|S^{\prime}_{i}|

We are now ready to describe our algorithm. We run in parallel two processes. The first process simply performs a BFS from vertex

\infty
. Additionally, for every edge
e=(v,u)
, vertex
u
is given an estimate
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
on
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)
(that may be outdated). More specifically, if
w(e)=2^{i}
, then vertex
v
only communicates its estimate
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
to
u
in increments of
{\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil}
(it is this modification of the standard Even-Shiloach tree algorithm that leads to the faster total update time). In other words, every time
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
passes a threshold of
{z\cdot\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil}
for some integer
z
, vertex
v
communicates its current estimate
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
to
u
, and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
is replaced with this amount.
Additionally, we maintain a directed tree
T\subseteq G
, that is rooted at vertex
s
, with all edges directed away from the root. The tree contains all vertices
v\in V(G)
with
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\neq\infty
. Whenever we refer to lengths of paths or distances in the tree
T
, it is with respect to edge lengths
\ell^{\prime}(e)
for
e\in E(T)
.

Additional Data Structures.

In addition to the tree
T
, the estimates
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
for vertices
v\in V
, and estimates
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
for edges
(v,u)\in E
, we maintain the following data structures. For every vertex
v\in V
, we maintain a heap
H(v)
that contains all vertices of
\left\{u\in V\mid(u,v)\in E\right\}
, with the key associated with each such vertex
u
being
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(u)+\ell^{\prime}(u,v)
. Additionally, for all
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, we maintain a set
U_{i}(v)
of vertices, that contains every vertex
u\in V(G)
, such that there is an edge
e=(v,u)
with
w(e)=2^{i}
in
G
.

Invariants.

We ensure that, throughout the algorithm, the following invariants hold.

I1.

For every vertex
v
, the value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
is an integral multiple of
\epsilon
, and it may only grow over the course of the algorithm; the same holds for values
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
for all edges
e=(v,u)
;

I2.

If
e=(x,y)
is an edge in the tree
T
, then vertex
x
is the vertex with the lowest key
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{y}(x)+\ell^{\prime}(x,y)
in
H(y)
, and moreover
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(y)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{y}(x)+\ell^{\prime}(x,y)
;

I3.

For every edge
e=(u,v)
, if
w(e)=2^{i}
, then
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)-\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(u)\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)
;

I4.

For all
v\in V(G)
, if
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}_{G}(s,v)\leq 2d
, then
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\neq\infty
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\leq\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)
; and

I5.

For all
v\in V(G)
,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\geq(1-\epsilon)\cdot\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)
holds at all times; moreover, if
v\in V(T)
, then the length of the
s
-
v
path in the current tree
T
is at most
\frac{\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)}{1-\epsilon}
.

Initialization.

At the beginning of the algorithm, we compute a shortest-path out-tree
T
rooted at vertex
s
, with respect to edge lengths
\ell^{\prime}(\cdot)
. For every vertex
v\in V
, we then set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)=\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)
– the distance from
s
to
v
in the tree
T
.
Next, we scan all vertices in the bottom-up order in the tree
T
. For each such vertex
v
, if
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)>2d
, then we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)=\infty
, and delete
v
from the tree. For every edge
e=(v,u)\in E
, we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
. Lastly, for every vertex
v\in V
, we initialize the heap
H(v)
with the vertices of
\left\{u\in V\mid(u,v)\in E\right\}
, and the sets
\left\{U_{i}(v)\right\}_{0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil}
of its out-neighbors. It is easy to verify that the initialization can be performed in time
O(m\log m)
, and that all invariants hold at the end of initialization.

Next we describe the algorithms for handling vertex-splits and edge deletions.

Algorithm for Handling Vertex Splits.

Consider a vertex split operation, in which we are given a vertex
v\in V
, and
a collection
U=\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right\}
of new vertices that need to be inserted into
G
. Recall that we are also given a collection
E^{\prime}
of new edges that must be inserted into
G
, and for every edge
e\in E^{\prime}
, we are given a length
\ell(e)
and a weight
w(e)
. For each such edge
e\in E^{\prime}
, either both its endpoints lie in
\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},v\right\}
; or one endpoint lies in
\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right\}
, and the other in
V(G)\setminus\left\{v,u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\right\}
. In the latter case, if
e=(u_{i},x)
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(v,x)
must currently lie in
G
, and both
w(e^{\prime})=w(e)
and
\ell(e^{\prime})=\ell(e)
must hold, so
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
. Similarly, if
e=(x,u_{i})
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(x,v)
must currently lie in
G
, and both
w(e^{\prime})=w(e)
and
\ell(e^{\prime})=\ell(e)
must hold, so
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
.

If
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\neq\infty
, then let
e=(x,v)
be the unique edge that is incoming into
v
in the tree
T
. We
temporarily
insert, into set
E^{\prime}
, edges
e_{i}=(x,u_{i})
with
\ell^{\prime}(e_{i})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
and
w(e_{i})=w(e)
for all
u_{i}\in U
.

Our algorithm consists of two steps.
In the first step,
we set, for every vertex
u_{i}\in U
,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u_{i})=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
. We then inspect the edges of
E^{\prime}
one by one.
Consider any such edge
e=(a,b)
.

•

If both endpoints of
e
lie in
U\cup\left\{v\right\}
, then we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
, and update the corresponding heap
H(b)
by inserting
a
into it.

•

If the second endpoint
b
of
e
lies in
U
and the first endpoint
a
lies in
V(G)\setminus(U\cup\left\{v\right\})
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(a,v)
with
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
must be present in
G
. In this case, we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(a)
, and update heap
H(b)
by inserting
a
into it.

•

Similarly, if the first endpoint
a
of
e
lies in
U
, and the second endpoint
b
lies in
V(G)\setminus(U\cup\left\{v\right\})
, then edge
e^{\prime}=(v,b)
with
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
must be present in
G
. In this case, we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(v)
, and update heap
H(b)
by inserting
a
into it.

Lastly, if
w(e)=2^{i}
, and
b\not\in U_{i}(a)
, then we insert
b
into
U_{i}(a)
.
We also update the graph
G
with the insertion of the vertices of
U
and edges of
E^{\prime}
.
Notice that all these updates can be done in time
O((|U|+|E^{\prime}|)\cdot\log m)
.

The second step only needs to be executed if
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\neq\infty
. In this step we delete from
G
the edges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
that were temporarily inserted. Our data structure is then updated using the algorithm that we describe below, for handling edge-deletion updates.

We now establish that all invariants hold after the first step.
Invariant
I1
continues to hold, as we did not update any
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
or
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
estimates except for the newly inserted vertices.

In order to establish Invariant
I2
, assume that
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\neq\infty
, and so edge
(x,v)
lies in tree
T
. Observe that for every vertex
u_{i}\in U
, all vertices in
H(u_{i})
lie in
H(v)
, except possibly for the vertices of
\left(U\cup v\right)\setminus\left\{u_{i}\right\}
. But for every vertex
a\in\left(U\cup v\right)\setminus\left\{u_{i}\right\}
,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)>\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(x)
currently holds, so the key of such a vertex
a
in the heap
H(u_{i})
is greater than or equal to
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u_{i}}(v)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(x)+\ell^{\prime}(x,v)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u_{i}}(x)+\ell^{\prime}(x,u_{i})
.
Since
u_{i}
is connected to
x
in the tree
T
, we get that Invariant
I2
holds for
u_{i}
. Consider now any vertex
a\not\in U
, and assume that some edge
(b,a)
lies in tree
T
. It is possible that we have inserted new vertices into
H(a)
: if an edge
e=(u_{i},a)
for some
u_{i}\in U
lies in
E^{\prime}
, then
u_{i}
was inserted into
H(a)
. However, in this case, edge
e^{\prime}=(v,a)
with
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
belonged to
G
before the current update, and so
v
already lied in
H(a)
. Since
\ell^{\prime}(e)=\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})
, and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(u_{i})=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(v)
, the key of
u_{i}
in heap
H(a)
is equal to the key of
v
, that remained unchanged. Therefore, Invariant
I2
continues to hold.

We now turn to prove that Invariants
I4
and
I5
continue to hold. Consider any vertex
a\in V(G)
. If
a\not\in U
, then it is easy to see that
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,a)
did not change after the current update. This is because for any path
P
connecting
s
to
a
in the current graph
G
, we can obtain a path
P^{\prime}
connecting
s
to
a
in the original graph
G
, whose length with respect to the modified edge lengths
\ell^{\prime}(\cdot)
is no higher than that of
P
, by replacing every vertex of
U
on
P
with the vertex
v
. Moreover, path
P^{\prime}
still belongs to the current graph since we did not delete any vertices or edges during the current update. Since
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
did not change, and the path connecting
s
to
a
in
T
did not undergo any changes either, both invariants continue to hold for
a
.

Assume now that
a\in U
. Let
P
be the path connecting
s
to
v
in the current tree
T
. Since path
P
did not change during the current update, from Invariant
I5
, the length of this path is at most
\frac{\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)}{1-\epsilon}
. Notice that the last edge on this path is
e=(x,v)
, and recall that we have inserted an edge
e^{\prime}=(x,a)
into
G
, with
\ell^{\prime}(e^{\prime})=\ell^{\prime}(e)
. By replacing edge
e
with
e^{\prime}
on path
P
, we obtain a path
P^{\prime}
, connecting
s
to
a
, which is the unique
s
–
a
path in tree
T
. Moreover, the length of
P^{\prime}
is equal to the length of
P
, which, in turn, is at most
\frac{\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)}{1-\epsilon}=\frac{\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)}{1-\epsilon}
. We conclude that
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,a)\leq\frac{\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)}{1-\epsilon}
, establishing Invariant
I5
for vertex
a
.

In order to establish Invariant
I4
for vertex
a
, let
Q
be the shortest
s
-
a
path in graph
G
, with respect to edge lengths
\ell^{\prime}(\cdot)
. By replacing every vertex of
U
on this path with vertex
v
, and removing loops, we obtain an
s
-
v
path
Q^{\prime}
in
G
, whose length is no higher than the length of
Q
. Therefore,
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)\leq\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,a)
. Since we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
, we get that, if
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,a)\neq\infty
, then
\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)\neq\infty
, and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\leq\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,v)\leq\mbox{\sf dist}^{\prime}(s,a)
. We conclude that Invariants
I4
and
I5
continue to hold.

It now remains to establish Invariant
I3
.
Consider any edge
e=(a,b)
. Notice that none of the values
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
of vertices
a\in V
changed, except for the vertices of
U
that were inserted into
G
. Similarly, value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
remained unchanged, except if
a
or
b
(or both) are vertices that were newly inserted into
G
. In other words, we only need to consider edges
e=(a,b)\in E^{\prime}
.

Let
e=(a,b)
be any such edge. As before, we need to consider three cases. The first case happens if both
a,b\in U\cup\left\{v\right\}
. In this case, we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
, so the invariant clearly holds. Assume now that
a\in U
and
b\not\in U\cup\left\{v\right\}
. In this case, edge
e^{\prime}=(v,b)
was present in
G
before the current update, and we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(v)
. Since the invariant held for edge
(v,b)
before the current update, and since
w(a,b)=w(v,b)
, the invariant holds for edge
(a,b)
at the end of the current update. Lastly, assume
that
a\not\in U\cup\left\{v\right\}
and
b\in U
. In this case, edge
(a,v)
was present in
G
before the current update, we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(a)
and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
remains unchanged. Since Invariant
I3
held for edge
(a,v)
at the beginning of the current update, it now holds for edge
(a,b)
at the end of the update.

Algorithm for Handling Edge Deletions.

We now provide an algorithm for updating the data structure with the deletion of some edge
e=(x,y)
. First, we may need to update the heap
H(y)
(by either deleting
x
from it, or changing the key of
x
in the heap, if another
(x,y)
edge is present in
G
). We may also need to delete
y
from the appropriate set
U_{i}(x)
; all this can be done in time
O(\log n)
. If
e\not\in T
, then no further updates are needed. We assume from now on that
e\in E(T)
. We start by deleting the edge
e
from the tree
T
.

Throughout the algorithm, we let
S
be the set of vertices that are not currently connected to the tree
T
. We will not maintain the set
S
explicitly, but it will be useful for the analysis. At the beginning of the algorithm, the set
S
contains all vertices that lied in the original subtree of
T
rooted at
y
.

Our algorithm maintains a heap
Q
that contains a subset of vertices of
S
that need to be examined. The key associated with each vertex
v
in
Q
is
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
. We will ensure that the following invariant holds throughout the update:

J1.

If
a
is the vertex with minimum value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
in
S
, then
a\in Q
holds.

We start with the heap
Q
containing only the vertex
y
. Then we perform iterations, as long as
Q\neq\emptyset
. In order to execute a single iteration, we let
a
be the vertex with smallest key
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
in the heap
Q
, and we let
b
be the vertex in heap
H(a)
with smallest key.
If
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)>2d
, then we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
to
\infty
and remove it from
Q
. We then set value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(x)
of every vertex that is currently not connected to the tree
T
to
\infty
and terminate the update algorithm. We assume from now on that
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)\leq 2d
. We then consider two cases.

The first case happens if
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)+\ell^{\prime}(b,a)\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
. Note that from Invariant
J1
, vertex
b
is currently attached to the tree
T
. In this case, we remove
a
from
Q
and attach it to the tree
T
as a child of vertex
b
, via the edge
(b,a)
. Every vertex that is currently a descendant of
a
also becomes automatically attached to the tree
T
and removed from the set
S
; vertex
a
itself is removed from
S
as well.

Consider now the second case, where
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)+\ell^{\prime}(b,a)>\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
. In this case, we increase
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
by (additive)
\epsilon
, and keep vertex
a
in the heap
Q
. Additionally, for every integer
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
, such that, for some integer
q
, threshold
q\cdot\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
lies between the old and the new value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
, we consider every vertex
z\in U_{i}(a)
. We set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{z}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
and update the key of vertex
a
in the heap
H(z)
. Additionally, if
z
is currently a child-vertex of
a
, then we disconnect
z
from
a
(so it stops being a child of
a
), and add
z
to the heap
Q
. This finishes the algorithm for handling edge-deletion updates.

We start by showing that Invariant
J1
holds throughout the algorithm.

Claim C.2
.

Invariant
J1
holds throughout the algorithm.

Proof.

Consider any time during the execution of the update procedure, and
let
a
be the vertex of
S
with smallest value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
. Assume for contradiction that
a\not\in Q
. Observe first that, whenever the value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
changes for any vertex of
S
, we add
v
to
Q
. Vertex
v
can only leave the heap
Q
when it is connected to the tree
T
, or if we set
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
to
\infty
. Therefore, if
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
ever changed over the course of the current update operation, then
a\in Q
must hold. We assume from now on that value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
did not change over the course of the current update operation. Let
b
be the parent-vertex of
a
at the beginning of the current update.
From our algorithm, if value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
ever changed over the course of the current update operation, then vertex
a
was immediately added to heap
Q
. Therefore, value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
remained unchanged throughout the current update.

Denote the weight of edge
(b,a)
by
2^{i}
.
In the next observation, we show that, at the beginning of the current update procedure,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)
was significantly lower than
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
. We will then conclude that
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)
must have grown significantly over the course of the current update procedure, and so
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
must have grown as well, leading to a contradiction.

Observation C.3
.

At the beginning of the current update procedure,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)-\frac{\epsilon\cdot d}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\cdot 2^{i-1}
held.

Proof.

From Invariant
I2
, at the beginning of the current update procedure,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)+\ell^{\prime}(b,a)
held.
Recall that
\ell^{\prime}(b,a)=\ell(b,a)+\epsilon\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\cdot 2^{i}\right\rceil
. Moreover, from Invariant
I3
,

\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)\geq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)-\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
held at the beginning of the procedure for processing the update.
Altogether, we get that, at the beginning of the current update procedure:

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)&=\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)+\ell^{\prime}(b,a)\\
&\geq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)-\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil+\ell(b,a)+\epsilon\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\cdot 2^{i}\right\rceil\\
&\geq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)+\frac{\epsilon\cdot d}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\cdot 2^{i-1}.\end{split}

Since vertex
a
is the vertex of
S
with smallest value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
, either vertex
b
is currently connected to the tree
T
, or the value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)
has grown to become at least
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
over the course of the current update procedure.

If vertex
b
remained attached to the tree
T
over the course of the entire update procedure, then
a=y
must hold; in other words, the edge that was deleted from
G
is
(b,a)
. In this case,
a
must have been added to
Q
immediately at the beginning of the update procedure.
Otherwise, if vertex
b
became attached to tree
T
at any time during the update procedure, then each of its children was either attached to tree
T
together with it, or added to
Q
. Since
a
was never added to
Q
, it must be the case that
b
was never attached to
T
, and so
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)
increased over the course of the current update procedure to become at least
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
. We claim that in this case, value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{b}(a)
must have been updated, leading to a contradiction.

Indeed, from the above calculations, over the course of the current update operation, value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(b)
must have increased by at least
\frac{\epsilon\cdot d}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\cdot 2^{i-1}>\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
units, (since
d\geq\Gamma\log n
and
\epsilon\leq 1/8
), and so value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{a}(b)
must have been updated, with vertex
a
added to
Q
, a contradiction.

We now verify that Invariants
I1
–
I3
continue to hold at the end of the update procedure, assuming that they held before the procedure started.

First, it is immediate to verify that values
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
for vertices
v\in V
and values
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
for edges
(v,u)
are never decreased, and they only increase by intergral multiples of
\epsilon
, so Invariant
I1
continues to hold.

Consider now some edge
e=(v,u)
with
w(e)=2^{i}
. Notice that our algorithm updates
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
to the current value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
whenever the latter passes a threshold that is an integral multiple of
\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
. This ensures that
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)-\epsilon^{2}\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{v}(u)\leq\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)
holds at all times and establishes Invariant
I3
.

In order to establish Invariant
I2
, we consider the time when some vertex

Analysis of Total Update Time.

From the discussion above, the initialization takes time
O(m\log m)
, and the total time spent on vertex-splitting updates (excluding the time spent on edge-deletion updates generated by them) is bounded by
O(m\log m)
.

We now turn to analyze the total time spent on edge-deletion updates. Consider the algorithm for processing the deletion of some edge
e=(x,y)
, that lies in the tree
T
. Initially, we add vertex
y
to the heap
Q
, and then inspect this vertex. Whenever any vertex
v\in Q
is inspected, we spend time
O(\log n)
in order to decide whether it needs to be reconnected to the tree
T
, and, if
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
increases, to calculate all indices
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
for which an integral multiple of
\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
lies between the old and the new value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
. We refer to this as
vertex inspection time
. Additionally, for every integer
i
, for which an integral multiple of
\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
lies between the old and the new value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
, we consider every vertex
u\in U_{i}(v)
, update
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}_{u}(v)
, and possibly insert
u
into
Q
. We call this procedure the
update of out-neighbor
u
, and the time required to perform this update, including the possible subsequent inspection of vertex
u
when it becomes the lowest-key vertex in
Q
, is counted as the time required to update the out-neighbor
u
. However, if such a vertex
u
is inspected several times (in which case its value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)
grows with every inspection), then only the first inspection is charged to the time that
v
spends on updating its out-neighbors. All subsequent times are charged to the increase of value
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(u)
.

To summarize, the total update time due to edge deletion updates can be bounded as follows.

•

Whenever an edge
e=(x,y)
is deleted from
G
, we may spend
O(\log m)
time on inspecting vertex
y
. In total this may contribute up to
O(m\log m)
to total update time.

•

Whenever, for some vertex
a\in V
,
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
increases, we may spend
O(\log m)
time on a subsequent inspection of
a
. Since, when
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(a)
reaches
2d
, it is set to be
\infty
, and since every increase is by
\epsilon
units, there can be at most
O(d/\epsilon)
such increases for each vertex
a
. In total this may contribute up to
O\left(\frac{nd\log m}{\epsilon}\right)
to total update time.

•

The remaining time contribution is due to out-neighbor update operations. Consider a vertex
v\in V
,
and consider an index
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
. Whenever
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
passes a threshold that is an integral multiple of

\epsilon^{2}\cdot\left\lceil\frac{d\cdot 2^{i}}{\Gamma\cdot\log n}\right\rceil
, we need to update all vertices in
U_{i}(v)
. Since
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)\leq 2d
, and
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(v)
only grows throughout the algorithm, there are in total at most
O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot\log n}{\epsilon^{2}\cdot 2^{i}}\right)
such update operations of vertices in
U_{i}(v)
.
Let
n_{i}(v)
be the largest number of vertices that are ever present at any given time in set
U_{i}(v)
. Then each such update operation takes time
O(n_{i}(v)\cdot\log n)
, and the total time spent on updating all out-neighbors of
v
in
U_{i}(v)
over the course of the algorithm is bounded by
O\left(\frac{n_{i}(v)\cdot\Gamma\cdot\log^{2}n}{2^{i}\cdot\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.
Summing this over all indices
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log m\right\rceil
, the total time required for all updates of out-neighbors of
v
is bounded by:

O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot\log^{2}n}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\left\lceil\log m\right\rceil}\frac{n_{i}(v)}{2^{i}}.

(1)

From Property
P1
, if
v\neq t
, then
n_{i}(v)\leq 2^{i+2}
, and so the total time required to update all out-neighbors of
v
is bounded by:

O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot\log^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

The total time required for all updates of out-neighbors of all vertices in
V(G)\setminus\left\{t\right\}
is bounded by
O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot n\cdot\log^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
.

Lastly, if
v=t
, then for all
0\leq i\leq\left\lceil\log m\right\rceil
,
n_{i}(v)\leq n
must hold. From Equation
1
, the total time required for updating all out-neighbors of
t
is bounded by
O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot n\cdot\log^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
. Altogether, the total time required for all updates of out-neighbors of all vertices of
G
is bounded by:

O\left(\frac{\Gamma\cdot n\cdot\log^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

We conclude that the total update time of the algorithm is bounded by:

O\left(\frac{nd\log m}{\epsilon}+m\log m+\frac{\Gamma\cdot n\log^{3}m}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)\leq\tilde{O}\left(\frac{n^{2}+m+\Gamma\cdot n}{\epsilon^{2}}\right),

since
d\leq\max\left\{4n/\epsilon,4\Gamma\cdot\log n\right\}
holds.

There are two minor points that we have ignored in our analysis so far. Consider a time when vertex
v
is split, and a collection
U=\left\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{r}\right\}
of new vertices is inserted into
G
. If
x
is the parent-vertex of
v
in the tree
T
, then we may temporarily insert edges
(x,u_{i})
into
G
, for all
u_{i}\in U
. These new edges are then immediately deleted from
G
, but between the time that they are inserted and until the time they are deleted, Property
P1
may not hold for
x
. However, it is easy to verify that, during the same time interval, we never update
\widetilde{\mathsf{dist}}(x)
, as the insertions and the deletions of these new edges do not affect the
s
-
x
path in the current tree, and so we do not need to update out-neighbors of

during this time.
Additionally, these edges that were temporarily inserted into

are not included in the total number of edges

that ever belong to
G
. However, every edge that ever lied in

contributes at most one such extra edge, so the total number of edges ever present in

is at most

, and the above asymptotic bound on total update time remains unchanged.
