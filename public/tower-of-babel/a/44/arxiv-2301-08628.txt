Wigner Gaussian dynamics: simulating the anharmonic and quantum ionic motion Preprint: APS/123-QED
Antonio Siciliano, Lorenzo Monacelli, Giovanni Caldarelli, Francesco Mauri

Abstract
The atomic motion controls important properties of materials, such as thermal transport, phase transitions, and vibrational spectra. However, simulating the ionic dynamics is exceptionally challenging when quantum fluctuations are relevant (e.g., at low temperatures or with light atoms) and the energy landscape is anharmonic. In this work, we present the Time-Dependent Self-Consistent Harmonic Approximation (TDSCHA) [ L. Monacelli and F. Mauri, Phys. Rev. B 103, 104305 (2021) ] in the Wigner framework, paving the way for the efficient computation of the nuclear motion in systems with sizable quantum and thermal anharmonic fluctuations. Besides the improved numerical efficiency, the Wigner formalism unveils the classical limit of TDSCHA and provides a link with the many-body perturbation theory of Feynman diagrams. We further extend the method to account for the non-linear couplings between phonons and photons, responsible, e.g., for a nonvanishing Raman signal in high-symmetry Raman inactive crystals, firstly discussed by Rasetti and Fermi. We benchmark the method in phase III of high-pressure hydrogen ab initio . The nonlinear photon-phonon coupling reshapes the IR spectra and explains the high-frequency shoulder of the H 2 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} vibron observed in experiments. The Wigner TDSCHA is computationally cheap and derived from first principles. It is unbiased by assumptions on the phonon-phonon and phonon-photon scattering and does not depend on empirical parameters. Therefore, the method can be adopted in unsupervised high-throughput calculations.

Introduction

The burst of computational resources accomplished during the last decades unlocked the path to material design: we can synthesize

new materials and
measure
their properties before the experimental realization. High-throughput simulations are driving the discovery of new cathode materials for batteries

, superconductor hydrides

, among others.
However, the available toolchain for high-throughput simulation fails when anharmonicity is strong. In these cases, the harmonic approximation and perturbative approaches are inadequate. This happens in hydrides where quantum fluctuations alter the free energy landscape
Errea et al. 2016
;
Errea et al. 2020
, phase diagram of hydrogen-rich compounds like high-pressure ice

;
Monacelli et al. 2018
and solid hydrogen

;

, and materials undergoing displacive phase transition like charge density wave (CDW) transition metal dichalcogenides

;

;

;

.

Lattice anharmonicity influences the vibrational spectra, the optical properties, and the conductivity of a crystal

. These consist in the main experimental signatures of the atomic structure and phase transitions when diffraction is not possible due to small sample size or low cross-section. Examples include solid hydrogen

;

, high-pressure water

Furthermore, accurate simulations of the ionic motion are fundamental in quantum paraelectric perovskites like

and
\text{SrTiO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

The Time-Dependent Self-consistent Harmonic Approximation (TDSCHA)

;
Lihm and Park 2021
provides an efficient numerical solution for finite-temperature nuclear dynamics with quantum and anharmonic fluctuations beyond perturbative approaches. This method has been successfully applied to simulate Raman and IR spectra of high-pressure molecular hydrogen
Monacelli et al. 2021a
and to characterize the quantum paraelectric transition in
\text{KTaO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

Ranalli et al. 2022
.

TDSCHA approximates the nuclear density matrix with the most general Gaussian. This leads to a more efficient technique than path-integral (PI) methods, where classical-like trajectories of different replicas are sampled
Cao and Voth 1994
;
Poulsen et al. 2003
;
Hele et al. 2015
;
Ceotto et al. 2017
.
Instead, it shares some similarities with the Linearized Semi-Classical Initial Value Representation method (LSC-IVR)
Plé et al. 2021
, in which an approximate quantum initial condition for the ionic system
Beutier et al. 2014
;
Plé et al. 2019
;
PoulsenWignerdistrSamplin
;
Shi and Geva 2003
is evolved subject to classical dynamics.
Among all these methods, TDSCHA is the computationally most efficient for medium-sized systems (containing hundreds of ions).

However, the exceptional complexity of the original TDSCHA formulation hampers its physical interpretations, and many questions remain unanswered. For example, how does it relate to other approaches employed in quantum chemistry
Plé et al. 2021
? Which phonon scattering mechanisms is the theory able to describe? What are the limitations of its applicability? And how does the theory behave in the classical limit?

In this work, we reformulate the TDSCHA in the Wigner formalism, where the density matrix is expressed as a function of position and momentum in a quantum-phase space
Wigner 1932
(Sec.
II
).
In this way, the TDSCHA equations are simplified, and the nuclear evolution is governed only by the position-momentum averages and correlators at a fixed time (Sec.
III
). The
\hbar
vanishes from the propagator, thus, the initial condition determines whether the evolution is quantum or classical, and they share the same computational cost.

The TDSCHA linear response in the Wigner formalism (Sec.
IV
) becomes very intuitive and compact. The propagator is reformulated in terms of Feynman diagrams, establishing a simple connection with other many-body approaches like the Self-Consistent Phonon (SCP) technique
Tadano and Tsuneyuki 2018
;
Tadano and Tsuneyuki 2015
. In Sec.
V
, we extend the formalism to allow for the simulation of nonlinear coupling between phonons and photons. Finally, we benchmark the theory on the IR spectrum of high-pressure hydrogen phase III (Sec.
VI
), where it is demonstrated that the high-frequency overtone observed in
Goncharov et al. 2001
is a result of the aforementioned nonlinear coupling between photons and phonons.

II

II
Nuclear dynamics

Here, we review the Wigner formalism for the exact nuclear evolution and compare the quantum and classical dynamics of
N
ions in a closed system. Nuclear spins and ionic exchange are neglected.

II.1
Classical nuclear evolution

In the classical limit, the nuclei behave like dimensionless particles that move according to the time-dependent Hamiltonian

H(t)=\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\frac{P^{2}_{a}}{2m_{a}}+V^{(\text{tot})}(\bm{R},t).

(1)

For brevity, we indicate with
a=(i,\alpha)
a composite index with the atomic index
i
and Cartesian index
\alpha
,
m_{a}=m_{i}
is the mass of atom
i
,
\small P_{a}=P_{i,\alpha}
is the momentum of atom
i
along the
\alpha
direction and
\small\bm{R}=\left(\bm{R}_{1},..,\bm{R}_{N}\right)
represents a configuration of atomic positions. The total potential can be divided into an internal static interaction and an external time-dependent perturbation

V^{(\text{tot})}(\bm{R},t)=V^{(\text{BO})}(\bm{R})+V^{(\text{ext})}(\bm{R},t),

(2)

where
\small V^{(\text{BO})}(\bm{R})
is the nuclear interaction mediated by the electrons within the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, i.e. the ground state electronic energy at fixed nuclear configurations. It can be evaluated as the total energy of an
ab-initio
calculation like density-functional theory (DFT), or an appropriately parametrized force field.
\small{V^{(\text{ext})}(\bm{R},t)}
is the external time-dependent potential. It encodes the interaction between the probe (usually an electromagnetic field) and the ions, mediated by the electrons.

Physical properties are computed as averages over the phase-space of the corresponding observable with the time-dependent probability distribution (normalized and positive-definite)
\rho_{\text{cl}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)
:

\left\langle O\right\rangle_{\rho_{\text{cl}}}=\int d\bm{R}\int d\bm{P}O(\bm{R},\bm{P})\rho_{\text{cl}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t).

(3)

In a closed system,
\rho_{\text{cl}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)
evolves according to the Liouville equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{\text{cl}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)+i\mathcal{L}^{\text{cl}}\rho_{\text{cl}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)=0.

(4)

The classical Liouville operator is defined as

i\mathcal{L}^{\text{cl}}\circ=-H(t)\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}\circ

(5)

and
\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}
is the Poisson brackets operator.

\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}=\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\left(\overset{\leftarrow}{\partialderivative{ R_a}}\overset{\rightarrow}{\partialderivative{ P_a}}-\overset{\leftarrow}{\partialderivative{ P_a}}\overset{\rightarrow}{\partialderivative{ R_a}}\right),

(6)

where the arrows indicate on which side the derivative is applied:

A\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}B=\{A,B\}=\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\left(\partialderivative{A}{ R_a}\partialderivative{B}{ P_a}-\partialderivative{A}{ P_a}\partialderivative{B}{ R_a}\right).

(7)

Eq. (
4
) preserves the phase-space volume, which behaves as an incompressible fluid, so probability can not be created nor destroyed leading to entropy conservation.

II.2
Quantum nuclear evolution

The Wigner formalism describes the quantum nuclear evolution in terms of position and momentum degrees of freedom, in a way analogous to the classical Liouville propagation discussed in Sec.
II.1
. Thus, the differences between quantum and classical dynamics are clear.

The Wigner quasi-distribution
Imre et al. 1967
, defined as a Fourier transform of the Von Neumann density operator
\hat{\rho}(t)
, is the quantum analogue of the classical probability distribution in the phase space. It is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)\hskip-1.42271pt=\hskip-2.27626pt\int\hskip-1.42271pt\frac{d\bm{R}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\bm{P}\cdot\bm{R}^{\prime}}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{3N}}\bra{\bm R + \frac{\bm R'}{2}}\hat{\rho}(t)\ket{\bm R - \frac{\bm R'}{2}}.

(8)

We remark that Eq. (
8
) is normalized, as in the classical case, but, in general, it is not positive-definite
Novaes 2003
, hence it can not be interpreted as a probability distribution. Still, it encodes all the information on the system.

Similarly, the Wigner expression for an operator
\hat{O}
is defined as

\small O_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P})=\hskip-1.42271pt\int\hskip-1.42271ptd\bm{R}^{\prime}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\bm{P}\cdot\bm{R}^{\prime}}\bra{\bm R + \frac{\bm R'}{2}}\hat{O}\ket{\bm R - \frac{\bm R'}{2}},

(9)

so that quantum averages in the Wigner formalism have the same expression as the classical ones (Eq. (
3
)):

\left\langle O_{\text{w}}\right\rangle_{\rho_{\text{w}}}=\int d\bm{R}\int d\bm{P}O_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P})\rho_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t).

(10)

The Wigner-Liouville equation controls the time evolution of
\rho_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)+i\mathcal{L}\rho_{\text{w}}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)=0,

(11)

where
i\mathcal{L}
is unitary and contains a classical (
cl
) and a quantum (
q
) propagator:

i\mathcal{L}=i\mathcal{L}^{\text{cl}}+i\mathcal{L}^{\text{q}}.

(12)

The classical propagator
i\mathcal{L}^{\text{cl}}
coincides with Eq. (
5
).
On the other hand, the quantum part depends explicitly on
\hbar
:

\small i\mathcal{L}^{\text{q}}\circ=-\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-\hbar^{2})^{n}}{2^{2n}(2n+1)!}H(t)\left(\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}\right)^{2n+1}\hskip-2.84544pt\circ.

(13)

Eq. (
13
) gives quantum corrections to the dynamics as odd powers of the Poisson brackets operator.

Interestingly, any quadratic potential has
i\mathcal{L}^{\text{q}}=0
, even when its coefficients are time-dependent, e.g.

\small H(t)=\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\frac{P^{2}_{a}}{2m_{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}(R-R_{0}(t))_{a}K_{0}(t)_{ab}(R-R_{0}(t))_{b}.

(14)

The density matrix propagates only according to
i\mathcal{L}^{\text{cl}}
, and the dynamic is independent on
\hbar
. The quantum/classical nature of the system is encoded only in the initial condition.

In the next Section, we will show that TDSCHA
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
shares
the same feature since it is based on a Hamiltonian with the same form of Eq. (
14
), where
\bm{K}_{0}(t)
and
\bm{R}_{0}(t)
are evaluated self-consistently from the distribution.

III
Gaussian dynamics in the Wigner framework

The TDSCHA constrains the quantum density matrix as the most general time-dependent Gaussian
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
. Since the Wigner transformation is equivalent to a Fourier transform, also the nuclear time-dependent Wigner quasi-distribution is a Gaussian

\displaystyle\small\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)\hskip-2.84544pt=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(t)\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}(R-\mathcal{R}(t))_{a}\alpha(t)_{ab}(R-\mathcal{R}(t))_{b}

\displaystyle\left.-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}(P-\mathcal{P}(t))_{a}\beta(t)_{ab}(P-\mathcal{P}(t))_{b}\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}(R-\mathcal{R}(t))_{a}\gamma(t)_{ab}(P-\mathcal{P}(t))_{b}\biggl],

(15)

where
\mathcal{N}(t)
is the normalization, defined such that

\int d\bm{R}\int d\bm{P}\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)=1.

(16)

\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t)
,
\bm{\mathcal{P}}(t)
,
\bm{\alpha}(t)
,
\bm{\beta}(t)
,
\bm{\gamma}(t)
are the time-dependent free parameters of the distribution. In contrast to the general Wigner distribution, Eq. (
15
) is positive-definite and can be interpreted as the quantum probability distribution. Thanks to the Wigner transformation, it is evident that
\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)
is the multidimensional generalization of the one reported in
Poulsen et al. 2017
for the 1D case. The position and momentum centroids
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t)
and
\bm{\mathcal{P}}(t)
are
3N
real vectors and represent, respectively, the instantaneous average position and momentum of the ions:

\vskip-7.11317pt\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t)=\left\langle\bm{R}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},\qquad\bm{\mathcal{P}}(t)=\left\langle\bm{P}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}.

(17)

\bm{\alpha}(t)
,
\bm{\beta}(t)
and
\bm{\gamma}(t)
are
3N\times 3N
real tensors (
\bm{\alpha}(t)
and
\bm{\beta}(t)
are symmetric) and represent the instantaneous position-momentum correlators:

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt=

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}(t)-\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}^{-1}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}(t)\right)^{-1},

(18a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt=

\displaystyle-\left(\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}(t)-\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{-1}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}(t)\right)^{-1},

(18b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt=

\displaystyle\left(\widetilde{\bm{\beta}}(t)-\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{T}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}^{-1}(t)\cdot\widetilde{\bm{\gamma}}(t)\right)^{-1},

(18c)

where
\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)=\widetilde{\bm{R}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(t)
,
\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)=\widetilde{\bm{P}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(t)
and the
\widetilde{\circ}
indicates a mass-rescaled variable like

\widetilde{R}_{a}=\sqrt{m_{a}}R_{a},\qquad\widetilde{P}_{a}=\frac{P_{a}}{\sqrt{m_{a}}}.

(19)

The detailed derivation of Eq. (
18
) is reported in Appendix
A
.

The dynamics of the Wigner distribution can be obtained by transforming the time-dependent equation of the TDSCHA in the Wigner basis. We prove in Appendix
B
that this is equivalent to evolve Eq. (
15
)
with a self-consistent Wigner-Liouville equation

\vskip-7.11317pt\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)+i\mathcal{L}^{\text{sc}}\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},\bm{P},t)=0,

(20)

where

i\mathcal{L}^{\text{sc}}\circ=-\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{\rho})\overset{\leftrightarrow}{\Lambda}\circ,

(21)

and
\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{\rho})
is a quadratic time-dependent Hamiltonian that depends self-consistently on
\widetilde{\rho}(t)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\widetilde{\rho})=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\frac{P^{2}_{a}}{2m_{a}}+\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\delta R(t)_{a}\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{(\text{tot})}(\bm{R},t)}{\partial R_{a}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

(22)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}\delta R(t)_{a}\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V^{(\text{tot})}(\bm{R},t)}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt\delta R(t)_{b}.

Inserting the Wigner-TDSCHA distribution, Eq. (
15
), in Eq. (
20

), we get the equations of motion

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},

(23a)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\frac{\partial V^{(\text{tot})}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},

(23b)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},

(23c)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}=

\displaystyle-\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V^{(\text{tot})}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt\cdot\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

(23d)

\displaystyle-\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt\cdot\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V^{(\text{tot})}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

(23e)

\displaystyle-\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\hskip-8.5359pt\cdot\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V^{(\text{tot})}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)},

where to compact the notation we drop the explicit time and position dependence.

The TDSCHA dynamics of Eq. (
23
) are simpler than the original derivation using the standard formalism of operators in quantum mechanics. As anticipated in Sec.
II.2
, Eqs (
23
) do not contain
\hbar
, and the dynamic is the same for quantum and classical distribution. Nevertheless, quantum features are included in the initial condition and preserved during the dynamics.
The classical limit of the TDSCHA dynamics was not evident in
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
, as the off-diagonal parameters of the density matrix are ill-defined as
\hbar\rightarrow 0
.

This approach is exact in the case of a time-dependent harmonic oscillator since the Wigner distribution is a Gaussian. Thanks to self-consistency, this theory goes beyond harmonic/perturbative methods. No approximations are made on the total potential itself so anharmonic effects are included in a non-perturbative way. The dynamics of Eq. (

C
).

III.1
Equilibrium SCHA in the Wigner formalism

A particular solution of Eq. (
23
) is the steady state equilibrium in absence of a time-dependent perturbation. From Eqs (
23
) we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\partialderivative{V^{\text{BO}}}{\widetilde{\bm R}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=

\displaystyle\bm{0}

(24a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=

\displaystyle\bm{0}\qquad\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=\bm{0},

(24b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{(0)}\cdot\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}V^{(\text{BO})}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\right\rangle_{(0)},

(24c)

where
(0)

As the mixed position-momentum correlation vanishes at equilibrium, the Wigner distribution becomes

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}^{(0)}(\bm{R},\bm{P})=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{(0)}\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\bm{P}}\cdot\left\langle\widetilde{\bm{P}}\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(0)}^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{P}}

(25)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\cdot\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{(0)}^{-1}\cdot\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\biggl]

III.1.1
Diagrammatic representation of the SCHA

We present a diagrammatic expansion of the SCHA in a quartic potential to clarify the anharmonic processes involved in the theory, and to highlight the differences between SCHA, TDSCHA, and other approaches such as Self-Consistent Phonon (SCP)

;
Tadano and Tsuneyuki 2015

We define the SCHA propagator through Eq. (

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(0)}(\omega)^{-1}=\omega^{2}-\overset{(2)}{\bm{D}}\overset{\omega=0}{\longrightarrow}-\overset{(2)}{\bm{D}}.

(29)

Since the SCHA is a static theory and it is defined through
\omega=0

\small\bm{g}^{(0)}(\omega)^{-1}=\omega^{2}-\frac{\partial^{2}V^{\text{(BO)}}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\biggl|_{\bm{R}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{BO}}}\hskip-8.5359pt\overset{\omega=0}{\longrightarrow}-\frac{\partial^{2}V^{\text{(BO)}}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\biggl|_{\bm{R}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{BO}}},

(30)

where

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{BO}}
is the minimum of

\frac{\partial V^{\text{(BO)}}}{\partial\widetilde{\bm{R}}}\biggl|_{\bm{R}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{\text{BO}}}=\bm{0},

(31)

To connect the SCHA with perturbation theory, we expand the BO energy landscape

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{a_{1}..a_{n}=1}^{3N}\hskip-8.5359pt\overset{(k+n)}{d}\hskip-5.69046pt_{b_{1}..b_{k}a_{1}..a_{n}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\delta\widetilde{R}+\widetilde{\delta}\right)_{a_{1}}..\left(\delta\widetilde{R}+\widetilde{\delta}\right)_{a_{n}}\right\rangle_{(0)},

IV
Linear response

The static SCHA corrects the bare phonon propagator with a real self-energy, thus only renormalizing the phonon frequency without introducing a finite lifetime of phonons.
In this Section, we revise the fully-dynamical TDSCHA linear response within the Wigner formalism and show how new diagrams with a nonvanishing imaginary part emerge.

IV.1
Linearized equations of motion and general response function

When an external time-dependent potential is coupled to phonons without causing irreversible changes in the material, the system is in the linear response regime. This scenario is relevant, for example, when the ionic degrees of freedom are probed with electromagnetic fields (X-ray or Raman scattering and IR absorption) or with neutrons. In these cases the external perturbation
V^{(\text{ext})}(\bm{R},t)
is in the form

V^{(\text{ext})}(\bm{R},t)=\mathcal{B}(\bm{R})\mathcal{V}(t).

(45)

In this regime, the SCHA distribution
\widetilde{\rho}^{(0)}(\bm{R})
(see Appendix
D
) is perturbed by
\widetilde{\rho}^{(1)}(\bm{R},t)

\widetilde{\rho}(\bm{R},t)=\widetilde{\rho}^{(0)}(\bm{R})+\widetilde{\rho}^{(1)}(\bm{R},t).

(46)

The probability distribution change leads to the emergence of time-dependent correction (denoted by the
(1)
) to the static correlators, Eqs (
18
),

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(t)_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(0)}_{\mu}+\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}^{(1)}_{\mu},

(47a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{R}_{\mu}\delta\widetilde{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\delta\widetilde{R}_{\mu}\delta\widetilde{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{(0)}+\left\langle\delta\widetilde{R}_{\mu}\delta\widetilde{R}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{(1)},

(47b)

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta\widetilde{P}_{\mu}\delta\widetilde{P}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\widetilde{P}_{\mu}\widetilde{P}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{(0)}+\left\langle\delta\widetilde{P}_{\mu}\delta\widetilde{P}_{\nu}\right\rangle_{(1)},

(47c)

where we express the tensors in the polarization basis
\{\bm{e}_{\mu}\}
of Eq. (
27
). In Appendix
E
, we demonstrate that the dynamics of the average momentum
\small\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}^{(1)}
and of the mixed correlator
\small{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(1)}}
can be reabsorbed in those of the variables of Eqs (
47
), which define unambiguously the state of the system.

The linearized equations of motion are obtained by plugging the perturbed correlators, Eqs (
47
), into the equations of motion, Eq. (
23
), and using Eq. (
46
) when computing averages of the total potential. This (see Appendix
E
for details) leads to

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{(1)}\\
\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{(1)}\\
\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(1)}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{p}^{\prime}\mathcal{V}(\omega),

(48)

which defines the linearized TDSCHA equations in frequency domain.
In Eq. (
48
),
\omega^{2}
comes from the Fourier transform of second-order time derivatives, and
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
is the linearized Wigner-Liouville operator Eq. (
21
). In general, we can separate
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
in two terms

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\dagger}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{harm}}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{anh}}.

(49)

The harmonic part
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{harm}}
describes the free evolution of the SCHA phonons defined in Eq. (
27
).
The scattering, hence the interaction between these phonons arises from the anharmonic part
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{anh}}
. The perturbation vector
\bm{p}^{\prime}
depends, in general, on equilibrium averages of first and second derivatives of
\mathcal{B}(\bm{R})
.

The external potential modifies the non-interacting equilibrium state, defined by Eq. (
25
). The response function encodes how the observable
\mathcal{A}(\bm{R})
changes when the system is out of equilibrium. In the linear response regime, this modification depends only on equilibrium quantities.

The TDSCHA response function is obtained expanding
\small{\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}^{(0)}+\widetilde{\rho}^{(1)}}}
(see Eq. (
46
)) in the perturbed parameters of Eqs (
47
) around the equilibrium value
\small{\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{(0)}}
. In Appendix
E
, we show that the first-order correction in the frequency domain is a simple scalar product in the space of the correlators (Eq. (
47
))

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{(1)}(\omega)=\bm{r}^{\prime\dagger}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}^{(1)}\\
\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}\right\rangle_{(1)}\\
\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}\right\rangle_{(1)}\end{bmatrix},

(50)

where the response vector
\bm{r}^{\prime}
, similarly to
\bm{p}^{\prime}
, contains equilibrium averages of position-derivatives of
\mathcal{A}(\bm{R})
.

Finally, inverting the linearized equations of motion, Eq. (
48
), we get

\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{(1)}(\omega)=\bm{r}^{\prime\dagger}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}\mathcal{V}(\omega),

(51)

where
\circ^{-1}
denotes the inverse.
The general response function of an observable
\mathcal{A}(\bm{R})
to an external perturbation
\mathcal{B}(\bm{R})
is

\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}=\bm{r}^{\prime\dagger}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}.

(52)

This expression has the same form as the one presented in Ref.
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
where the standard description of quantum mechanics is adopted.

In Appendix
F
we show how to compute the general response function (Eq. (
52
)) with the Lanczos algorithm following the original work on TDSCHA
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
. The algorithm generates a basis in which
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}
is tridiagonal. As shown in Appendix
F
, from this form it is easy to get in one shot the response function for all values of
\omega
. The Lanczos basis is generated from
N_{\text{steps}}
sequential applications of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
to a starting vector. Each iteration corresponds to free propagations (
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{harm}}
) and scattering (
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{anh}}
). In this way, we build the full anharmonic propagators (see next Section
IV.2
). In addition, we leverage the properties of the Wigner formulation that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\dagger}
and that, if
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
,
\bm{p}^{\prime}=\bm{r}^{\prime}
. These features imply that a symmetric Lanczos algorithm is sufficient to compute Eq. (
52
), effectively speeding up the original code by a factor of two
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
.

Momentum Green function

In Wigner-TDSCHA, the ionic momentum is controlled directly, which was not possible in the original formulation. Here we discuss the TDSCHA momentum-momentum Green’s function
\small\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)

\mathcal{A}=\widetilde{P}_{\mu}\quad\mathcal{B}=\widetilde{P}_{\nu}.

i\omega\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(\omega)=\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\omega)\longrightarrow\mathcal{G}^{(0)}_{\text{p}}(\omega)_{\mu\nu}=\omega_{\mu}^{2}\mathcal{G}^{(0)}(\omega)_{\mu\nu}.

(84)

The free propagators are the building blocks for the interacting theory. Hence the interacting momentum Green’s function
\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)
satisfies a perturbative expansion that is proportional to the one of
\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\omega)
once the TDSCHA diagrams are selected, i.e. those from Eq. (

J
for details,

\bm{\mathcal{G}}_{\text{p}}(\omega)=-\bm{1}+\omega^{2}\bm{\mathcal{G}}(\omega).

(85)

Thus, a Lanczos calculation also provides access to the TDSCHA momentum Green’s function.

V
Nonlinear phonon-photon coupling: Infrared and Raman

In this section, we provide an overview of the infrared (IR) and Raman response in TDSCHA, with a particular emphasis on the two-phonon effect.

IR experiments involve the absorption of infrared light by normal modes, which are associated with a variation in dipole moment and, in crystals, these are optical phonons. The IR signal is proportional to the imaginary part of the dipole-dipole response function,
\imaginary[\chi(\omega+i0^{+})_{\mathrm{p}_{\alpha},\mathrm{p}_{\beta}}]
, along two Cartesian directions
\alpha
and
\beta
hence

\mathcal{A}(\bm{R})=\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}(\bm{R}),\qquad\mathcal{B}(\bm{R})=\mathrm{p}_{\beta}(\bm{R}),

(90)

where the dipole
\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}
is per unit volume.

To get the response function we need the response and perturbation vector
\bm{r}^{\prime}
and
\bm{p}^{\prime}
(see Section
IV.1
). The first component of these vectors contains equilibrium averages of the effective charges:

\small\left\langle\frac{\partial\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}(\bm{R})}{\partial R_{a}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=\left\langle Z^{*}(\bm{R})_{a,\alpha}\right\rangle_{(0)},

(91)

where
a
is a supercell index and
\bm{Z}^{*}(\bm{R})
is the effective charges tensor. This vertex is the coupling for one phonon process.

The second and third components of
\bm{r}^{\prime}/\bm{p}^{\prime}
contain the first derivatives of the effective charges, the second-order dipole moment:

\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathrm{p}_{\alpha}(\bm{R})}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=\left\langle\frac{\partial Z^{*}(\bm{R})_{a,\alpha}}{\partial R_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}.

(92)

A Raman process consists in the scattering of light (usually visible) by zone-center phonons that induce a change in polarizability. The Raman cross-section contains the imaginary part of polarizability-polarizability response,
\imaginary[\chi_{\alpha_{\mu\nu},\alpha_{\eta\lambda}}(\omega+i0^{+})]
, obtained with

\mathcal{A}(\bm{R})=\alpha(\bm{R})_{\mu\nu}\qquad\mathcal{B}(\bm{R})=\alpha(\bm{R})_{\eta\lambda},

(93)

where
\mu,\nu,\eta,\lambda
are Cartesian directions. In a non-resonant Stokes Raman process phonons and photons scatter so we take into account the quantization of the electromagnetic field by
multiplying
\imaginary[\chi_{\alpha_{\mu\nu},\alpha_{\eta\lambda}}(\omega+i0^{+})]
by
1+n(\omega)
where
n(\omega)
is the Bose-Einstein distribution for the photons. The first component of
\bm{r}
and
\bm{p}
gives one-phonon processes and contains:

\left\langle\frac{\partial\alpha(\bm{R})_{\mu\nu}}{\partial R_{a}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=\left\langle\Xi(\bm{R})_{a,\mu\nu}\right\rangle_{(0)},

(94)

where
\bm{\Xi}(\bm{R})
is the Raman tensor. As before, the other components of
\bm{r}^{\prime}
and
\bm{p}^{\prime}
depends on the second-order Raman polarizability:

\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha(\bm{R})_{\mu\nu}}{\partial R_{a}\partial R_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}=\left\langle\frac{\partial\Xi(\bm{R})_{a,\mu\nu}}{\partial R_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}.

(95)

Eq. (
92
) and Eq. (
95
) trigger second-order IR/Raman processes
Rasetti 1931
;
Fermi and Rasetti 1931
exiting two phonons in the system, see Fig.
5
.
In principle, higher-order processes are possible, such as three-phonon etc. However, TDSCHA can not account for them as we showed that the three-phonon propagator is a disconnected diagram (see Sec.
IV.4
).

A two-phonon process, observable in both IR and Raman spectra, involves the scattering of photons and phonons while conserving both energy and momentum. The long-wavelength electromagnetic field can either absorb or generate two phonons. Another possibility is that one phonon is absorbed and the other one is emitted interacting with photons. This involves pairs of phonons with opposite momentum in the Brillouin zone forming a continuum signal overlapped to the sharper peaks of one phonon process.

This phenomenon is found both in harmonic and anharmonic systems. In systems like Si and Ge, which lack IR-active phonons due to inversion symmetry, two-phonon processes are essential for explaining the IR spectra
Deinzer and Strauch 2004
. Additionally, anharmonic systems such as liquid water
Silvestrelli et al. 1997
exhibit features resulting from effective charge position modulations. Two-phonon effects also play a significant role in many Raman spectra, including those of diamond and SiC
Windl et al. 1994
;
Windl et al. 1993
, as well as BaTiO
3

Menahem et al. 2022
.

The most common approximation is

\displaystyle Z^{*}(\bm{R})_{a,\alpha}\simeq Z^{*}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(0)})_{a,\alpha},

(96a)

\displaystyle\Xi(\bm{R})_{a,\mu\nu}\simeq\Xi(\bm{\mathcal{R}}^{(0)})_{a,\mu\nu},

(96b)

which suppresses all two phonon processes.

We use integration by parts and a Monte Carlo sampling, as proposed in
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
, to compute all the components of
\bm{r}^{\prime}
and
\bm{p}^{\prime}
in an efficient and non-perturbative way using only effective charges and Raman tensor:

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathrm{p}(\bm{R})_{\alpha}}{\partial\widetilde{R}_{a}\partial\widetilde{R}_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}\hskip-11.38092pt=-\sum_{c=1}^{3N}\mathcal{G}^{(0)}(t=0^{-})_{ac}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{R}^{(0)}_{c}\frac{Z_{b,\alpha}(\bm{R})}{\sqrt{m_{b}}}\right\rangle_{(0)},

(97a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha(\bm{R})_{\mu\nu}}{\partial\widetilde{R}_{a}\partial\widetilde{R}_{b}}\right\rangle_{(0)}\hskip-11.38092pt=-\sum_{c=1}^{3N}\mathcal{G}^{(0)}(t=0^{-})_{ac}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{R}^{(0)}_{c}\frac{\Xi_{b,\mu\nu}(\bm{R})}{\sqrt{m_{b}}}\right\rangle_{(0)}.

(97b)

We remark that TDSCHA is the only method that computes second-order Raman tensors or effective charges with full position dependence without the need for higher-order DFT response. In Appendix
K
we report in detail how to prepare a IR/Raman calculation.

VII
Conclusions

The Wigner picture simplifies the TDSCHA equations improving the physical intuition of the method. This allows us to discuss the equivalence of quantum and classical dynamics and rewrite the equations of motion in terms of position and momentum correlators.

We have established a direct relationship between the response function and the diagrammatic expression of the interacting Green’s function, which has allowed us to build a bridge to many-body perturbation theory. In the context of linear response theory, we clarified which diagrams and scattering processes are included in the method.

The TDSCHA infrared spectra of high-pressure hydrogen phase III showed that only two phonon effects explain the overtone experimentally observed in Ref.
Goncharov et al. 2001
.

\derivative{\left\langle H(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}=\frac{d}{dt}\left\{\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\frac{1}{2}\left[\left\langle\delta\widetilde{P}(t)_{a}\delta\widetilde{P}(t)_{a}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(t)_{a}^{2}\right]+\left\langle V^{(\text{tot})}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\right\}.

(116)

The time derivative of the kinetic energy gives:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\frac{1}{2}\left[\left\langle\delta\widetilde{P}(t)_{a}\delta\widetilde{P}(t)_{a}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(t)_{a}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\derivative{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{P}(t)_a \delta\widetilde{P}(t)_a\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}+\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(t)_{a}\derivative{\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}(t)_a}{t}

(117)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\derivative{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm P}(t) \delta\widetilde{\bm P}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}\right]-\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(t)\cdot\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}.

The derivative of the total potential average is more involved since the position probability distribution depends on time through
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(t)
and
\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)\delta\widetilde{\bm{R}}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}
. The derivative is worked out using the formulas proved in Ref.
Bianco et al. 2017
:

\displaystyle\derivative{ \left\langle V^{(\text{tot})} \right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}=\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{ext})}}{ t}\right\rangle+\sum_{a=1}^{3N}\derivative{\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(t)_a}{t}\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(t)_a}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ab=1}^{3N}\derivative{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{ R}(t)_a \delta\widetilde{ R}(t)_b\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{R}_b}{ \widetilde{R}_a}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}

(118)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{ext})}}{ t}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}(t)\cdot\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\derivative{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm R}(t) \delta\widetilde{\bm R}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}\cdot\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\right].

Then using Eqs (
23c
)-(
23d
) and the permutation properties of the trace it is shown that:

\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)\delta\widetilde{\bm{P}}(t)\right\rangle\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\derivative{\left\langle\delta\widetilde{\bm R}(t) \delta\widetilde{\bm R}(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}\cdot\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{tot})}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}{ \widetilde{\bm R}}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}\right].

(119)

So in the end, we found:

\derivative{\left\langle H(t)\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}}{t}=\left\langle\partialderivative{ V^{(\text{ext})}}{ t}\right\rangle_{\widetilde{\rho}(t)}.

(120)

This derivation is more compact than the one presented in Ref.
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
.

\widetilde{\bm{b}}^{\prime(1)}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}=\bm{r}^{\prime}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}=\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}

(154)

where
\left\langle\mathcal{A}\right\rangle_{(1)}(\omega)
is expressed as a scalar product in the space of the perturbative parameters.
This is the expression of
\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}
implemented in the code.

Appendix F
Lanczos algorithm

In this Appendix, we discuss the Lanczos implementation
Rocca et al. 2008
;
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
of the general response function Eq. (
154
). Both for infrared and Raman calculations, we can always work with
\bm{p}^{\prime}=\bm{r}^{\prime}
setting
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}
(see Eqs (
140
) (
153
)) so Eq. (
154
) becomes

\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}}=(\bm{p}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime})\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}

(155)

where we normalize the vector
\bm{p}^{\prime}
(Eq. (
140
))

\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}=\frac{\bm{p}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{\bm{p}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}}}.

(156)

To get in one shot for all values of
\omega
the response formula, Eq. (
155
), we modified the Lanczos algorithm presented in
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
exploiting that
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\dagger}
. This algorithm allows to find a basis in which
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
is tridiagonal

\bm{P}^{\prime-1}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime}=\bm{T}^{\prime},

(157)

where
\bm{T}^{\prime}
has the following form

\bm{T}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}t_{1}&r_{1}&\dots&\dots&0\\
r_{1}&t_{2}&\ddots&&\vdots\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&r_{n-1}\\
0&&&r_{n-1}&t_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(158)

where
n
is the size of
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
.
The change of basis matrix
\bm{P}^{\prime}
is

\bm{P}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{p}^{\prime}_{1}\quad\bm{p}^{\prime}_{2}\quad...\quad\bm{p}^{\prime}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

(159)

and it is unitary

\bm{P}^{\prime-1}=\bm{P}^{\prime\dagger}.

(160)

The coefficients of
\bm{T}^{\prime}
can be found following this iterative procedure
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
;
Rocca et al. 2008

\displaystyle t_{k}=

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}\cdot\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}

(161a)

\displaystyle r_{k}\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}=(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}-t_{k})\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}-r_{k-1}\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k-1}

(161b)

\displaystyle r_{k}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\bm{v}_{k}\cdot\bm{v}_{k}}

(161c)

\displaystyle\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k+1}=

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{k}/r_{k}

(161d)

with the initial vector equal to the normalized perturbation vector,
\bm{p}^{\prime}_{1}=\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}
. This procedure ends when either
\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}
is a linear combination of the previous vectors or
\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime}_{k}=0
. Unless the system is perfectly harmonic, this
condition is usually never reached in practical runs, and
the algorithm is truncated after a maximum number of
steps
N_{\text{steps}}
.

After we build the change of variables matrix
\bm{P}^{\prime}
we can use it in Eq. (
155
)

\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}}=(\bm{p}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime})\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime}\cdot\left[\bm{P}^{\prime-1}\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime}\right]\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime-1}\cdot\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}=(\bm{p}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime})\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime}\cdot\left(\bm{T}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot\bm{P}^{\prime-1}\cdot\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}

(162)

then noting that

\bm{P}^{\prime-1}\cdot\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}=\bm{P}^{\prime\dagger}\cdot\overline{\bm{p}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}

(163)

we get that the response function is given by

\chi(\omega)_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{A}}=(\bm{p}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{p}^{\prime})\left(\bm{T}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}_{11}

(164)

where
\left(\bm{T}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}_{11}
can be written as a continuous fraction using the coefficients obtained up to
N_{\text{steps}}

\left(\bm{T}^{\prime}+\omega^{2}\right)^{-1}_{11}=\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+t_{1}-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+t_{2}-\frac{r_{2}^{2}}{\omega^{2}+...}}}.

(165)

At each Lanczos step, we have to apply
\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
to a given vector
\bm{w}
in the space of the perturbed free parameters.
As showed in Appendix
E

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
contains two terms

\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{w}=\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{harm}}\cdot\bm{w}+\bm{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}_{\text{anh}}\cdot\bm{w}.

(166)

The application of the harmonic part is done using Eq. (
142
), while the anharmonic part is done using Eq. (
149
) applying a reweighting procedure to compute the perturbed average as explained in
Monacelli and Mauri 2021
.

Appendix G
Symbolic inversion

In this Appendix we describe the symbolic inversion of a symmetric square super-tensor with this form

\bm{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}&\bm{C}\\
\bm{C}^{\dagger}&\bm{B}\end{bmatrix}

(167)

where
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
,
\bm{C}
,
\bm{C}^{\dagger}
are tensors.
Using Gaussian reduction we get the inverse

\bm{L}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{D}^{-1}&\quad-\bm{D}^{-1}\cdot\bm{C}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}\\
-\bm{B}^{-1}\cdot\bm{C}^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{D}^{-1}&\quad\bm{B}^{-1}+\bm{B}^{-1}\cdot\bm{C}^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{D}^{-1}\cdot\bm{C}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}.\end{bmatrix}

(168)

where
\bm{D}=\bm{A}-\bm{C}\cdot\bm{B}^{-1}\cdot\bm{C}^{\dagger}
. It is trivial to check that
\bm{L}\cdot\bm{L}^{-1}=\bm{L}^{-1}\cdot\bm{L}=\bm{1}
. For what follows we need
\bm{C}=\bm{1}
so Eq. (
168
) becomes

\bm{L}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}^{-1}-\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\left(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}\right)^{-1}&\left(1-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}\\
\left(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}\right)^{-1}&-\bm{A}\cdot\left(1-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}.\end{bmatrix}

(169)

Again, for our purposes (see next Appendix
H
), we need to find a formula for the sum of entries of Eq. (
169
). Summing the coefficients of Eq. (
169
) we get

\displaystyle\bm{L}^{-1}_{11}+\bm{L}^{-1}_{21}+\bm{L}^{-1}_{12}+\bm{L}^{-1}_{22}=

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{-1}-\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\left(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}\right)^{-1}+\left(\bm{1}-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}+\left(\bm{1}-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}\right)^{-1}-\bm{A}\cdot\left(\bm{1}-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}

(170)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\left[(\bm{1}-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B})-(\bm{1}-\bm{A})\right]\cdot(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B})^{-1}+(1-\bm{A})\cdot\left(\bm{1}-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{-1}\cdot\left[\bm{A}\cdot(\bm{1}-\bm{B})\right]\cdot(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B})^{-1}+(1-\bm{A})\cdot\left(\bm{1}-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\bm{B})\cdot\left(1-\bm{A}\cdot\bm{B}\right)^{-1}+(1-\bm{A})\cdot\left(\bm{1}-\bm{B}\cdot\bm{A}\right)^{-1}.

Now we set
\bm{A}=\bm{1}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}
and
\bm{B}=\bm{1}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}
so we have that Eq. (
170
) is

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{B}}\cdot\left(\widetilde{\bm{A}}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{B}}\right)^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}\cdot\left(\widetilde{\bm{A}}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1}

(171)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\widetilde{\bm{A}}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}\right)^{-1}+\left(1+\widetilde{\bm{B}}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{-1}+\bm{1}\right)^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{A}}+\left(\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{-1}+\bm{1}\right)^{-1}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\cdot(\widetilde{\bm{A}}^{-1}+\widetilde{\bm{B}}^{-1}).

We will use this formula in Appendix
H
.
