Quantum d-separation and quantum belief propagation
Robert R. Tucci

Abstract




Abstract

The goal of this paper is to generalize
classical d-separation
and
classical Belief Propagation (BP)
to the quantum realm.
Classical d-separation
is
an essential ingredient of most of Judea Pearl’s
work. It
is crucial
to all 3 rungs of
what Pearl calls the 3 rungs
of Causation.
So having a quantum version
of d-separation
and BP
probably
implies
that most of Pearl’s
Bayesian networks
work,
including
his theory of
causality,
can be translated
in a straightforward
manner to
the quantum realm.

Notational Conventions
and Preliminaries

This paper
will employ the same
notational
conventions as
Bayeuvius (Ref.
[
6
]
).
Hence, if
the reader encounters
any notation that
is not defined
in this paper,
he/she should consult Bayesuvius (especially
its chapter
entitled “Notational Conventions and Preliminaries”),
where it is very likely
to be defined.

Henceforth,
whenever
we write

[{\cal E}][h.c.]
,
where
{\cal E}

is some quantum operator,
we will mean
{\cal E}{\cal E}^{\dagger}
,
where
{\cal E}^{\dagger}

is the Hermitian conjugate
of
{\cal E}
.
Also, whenever
we write

{\cal N}(!x)
,
we will
mean a
normalization
constant that is independent
of
x
.

Consider
a DAG with
nx
nodes

{\underline{x}}^{nx}=({\underline{x}}_{i})_{i=0,1,\ldots,nx-1}
.
A qbnet
consists of such a DAG with
a TPM (transition probability matrix)
attached to each node,
but unlike the TPMs for a classical bnet,
these ones are complex valued
and normalized differently.
If we represent the TPM
of node
{\underline{x}}_{j}

by a
probability
amplitude

A(x_{j}|pa({\underline{x}}_{j}))
,
then
A(x_{j}|pa({\underline{x}}_{j}))
must satisfy
the normalization condition

\sum_{x_{j}}\left|A(x_{j}|pa({\underline{x}}_{j}))\right|^{2}=1\;.

(1)

The amplitude for the full
DAG is defined as in the classical
case, by multiplying
the TPMs of all the nodes:

A(x^{nx})=\prod_{j}A(x_{j}|pa({\underline{x}}_{j}))\;.

(2)

Note that

\sum_{x^{nx}}\left|A(x^{nx})\right|^{2}=1\;.

(3)

Suppose

{\underline{a}}.,{\underline{b}}.
are disjoint multinodes
in
{\underline{x}}^{nx}
. Denote
the complement
multinode of
{\underline{a}}.
by

\underline{a_{.}}^{c}={\underline{x}}^{nx}-{\underline{a}}.
Then
we define
the
vector amplitude

|A\rangle(a.)

and the probability
P(a.)
by

|A\rangle(a.)=|A(a.)\rangle=|a.\rangle\sum_{a^{c}_{.}}A(a.,a.^{c})|a.^{c}\rangle\;,

(4)

with boundary cases

|A\rangle(x^{nx})=A(x^{nx})
,

|A\rangle=\sum_{x^{nx}}A(x^{nx})|x^{nx}\rangle
.
and

P(a.)=\sum_{a.^{c}}\left|A(a.,a.^{c})\right|^{2}\;.

(5)

Note that
\sum_{a.}P(a.)=1

so
P(a.)
is a bona
fide probability
distribution as the notation implies. Note also that

\langle A(a.)|A(a.)\rangle=P(a.)\;,

(6)

so you
can think of
|A\rangle(a.)

as being a generalized square root of
P(a.)
.

We define the
conditional vector amplitude

|A\rangle(b.|a.)
by

|A\rangle(b.|a.)=|A(b.|a.)\rangle=\frac{|A\rangle(b.,a.)}{|A\rangle(a.)}\;.

(7)

This is analogous to the definition
of conditional
probability,

P(b.|a.)=\frac{P(b.,a.)}{P(a.)}
.
Note that
|A\rangle(b.|a.)

isn’t really a ket; it’s a tuple
of two kets, but it is convenient
to represent it as a ket.
We can also define the dual
\langle A|(b.|a.)

of
|A\rangle(b.|a.)
so that
the following equations are satisfied:

\langle A|(b.|a.)=\langle A(b.|a.)|=\frac{\langle A|(b.,a.)}{\langle A|(a.)}\;,

(8)

\langle A(b.|a.)|A(b.|a.)\rangle=\frac{\langle A(b.,a.)|A(b.,a.)\rangle}{\langle A(a.)|A(a.)\rangle}=\frac{P(b.,a.)}{P(a.)}=P(b.|a.)\;.

(9)

Hence, you can think of

|A\rangle(b.|a.)

as being a generalized square root
of
P(b.|a.)
.

Suppose
{\underline{a}}.,{\underline{b}}.,{\underline{c}}.

are disjoint multinodes such that

{\underline{a}}.\cup{\underline{b}}.\cup{\underline{c}}.={\underline{x}}^{nx}
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{b.}|A\rangle(a.,b.)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b.}\sum_{c.}|A\rangle(a.,b.,c.)

(10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|A\rangle(a.)

(11)

This is analogous to the probability marginalization
rule
\sum_{b.}P(a.,b.)=P(a.)
.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{b.}|A\rangle(a.|b.)|A\rangle(b.)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{b.}|A\rangle(a.,b.)

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|A\rangle(a.)\;.

(13)

This
is analogous to
the probability splitting rule
P(a.)=\sum_{b.}P(a.|b.)P(b.)
.

Suppose
{\underline{a}}.
and
{\underline{e}}.
are
disjoint multinodes,
with
{\underline{e}}.

representing evidence. Note that

\displaystyle|A\rangle(a.|e.)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|A\rangle(a.,e.)}{|A\rangle(e.)}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|A\rangle(e.|a.)|A\rangle(a.)}{|A\rangle(e.)}\;.

(15)

This is analogous
to the classical Bayes rule

P(a.|e.)={\cal N}(!a.)P(e.|a.)P(a.)
.

Let
{\cal P}_{\underline{\lambda}}

be the
set of all
probability

P_{\underline{\lambda}}(\lambda)

with
\lambda\in S_{\underline{\lambda}}
.

Let
{\cal H}_{\underline{x}}
represent
a Hilbert space spanned
by an orthonormal basis
|x\rangle
,
where
x\in S_{\underline{x}}
.
Let
{\cal H}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}={\cal H}_{\underline{x}}\otimes{\cal H}_{\underline{y}}
.

Let
{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}
be the set of all
density matrices acting on
{\cal H}_{\underline{x}}
.
Likewise, let
{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}

be the set of all density matrices
acting on
{\cal H}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}
.
If
\rho_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}\in{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}
,
then
\rho_{\underline{x}}

will denote the partial trace

{\rm tr}_{\underline{y}}\rho_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}
.

Let
{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}

be the set of all
density matrices in

{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}}

which are diagonal in
\lambda
.
In other words,
\rho_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}\in{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}

if it is of the form

\rho_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}=\sum_{\lambda}P_{\underline{\lambda}}(\lambda)|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}\;,

(16)

where

\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}\in{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}

for all
\lambda

and
P_{\underline{\lambda}}\in{\cal P}_{\underline{\lambda}}
.

classical

quantum

Entropy

H({\underline{x}})=-\sum_{x}P(x)\ln P(x)

for
\rho\in{\cal D}_{\underline{x}}

Quantum d-separation

The next box comes
from the chapter of Bayesuvius
entitled “D-Separation”.

Claim 1

(Classical d-separation Theorem)

Suppose

{\underline{A}}.,{\underline{B}}.,{\underline{Z}}.

are disjoint multinodes
of a DAG
G
.

{\underline{A}}.\perp_{G}{\underline{B}}.|{\underline{Z}}.
iff

H({\underline{A}}.:{\underline{B}}.|{\underline{Z}}.)=0

for all
P

compatible with
G
.

The proof
of this theorem
will
not be presented
here. It isn’t
presented in the
current version of Bayesuvius either.
To see it,
you will
have to look
in Ref.
[
2
]
,
and in the original
papers cited therein.

The definition
of classical d-separation
(i.e., of
{\underline{A}}.\perp_{G}{\underline{B}}.|{\underline{Z}}.
)
only depends on the topology
of the DAG. We will
define quantum d-separation
exactly as it
is
defined classically.
Whether the bnet has
probabilities
or amplitudes
for its TPMs
does not make a difference
at the
level
of the definition
of d-separation.
It only becomes
important
when trying
to
find a quantum
analogue of the
whole classical
d-separation
theorem

which is stated in the box above.
The remainder
of this section
will
be dedicated to finding
a quantum
analogue
to the whole box above.

We start by
using
the definitions
introduced
in the previous section
to conclude that:

S_{\rho}({\underline{x}}:{\underline{y}}|{\underline{\lambda}}^{d})=\sum_{\lambda}P(\lambda)\left[S(\rho^{\lambda}_{x})+S(\rho^{\lambda}_{y})-S(\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}})\right]

(17)

for
\rho\in{\cal D}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}
.

Claim 2

S_{\rho}({\underline{x}}:{\underline{y}}|{\underline{\lambda}}^{d})=0

iff
\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}=\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}}}\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{y}}}

for all
\lambda
.

proof:

S(\rho^{\lambda}_{x})+S(\rho^{\lambda}_{y})-S(\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}})=0

iff

\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}=\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}}}\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{y}}}

QED

Next, we express
each density matrix

\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}
as

\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}=UDU^{\dagger}\;,

(18)

where
U

is a unitary
matrix
and
D

is a diagonal matrix
with non-negative diagonal entries.
Now let

A(x,y|x_{0},y_{0},\lambda)=\langle x|\langle y|U|x_{0}\rangle|y_{0}\rangle

(19)

and

\langle x_{0}|\langle y_{0}|D|x_{0}\rangle|y_{0}\rangle=P(x_{0},y_{0}|\lambda)\;.

(20)

Hence,

\rho^{\lambda}_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}}}=\sum_{x_{0},y_{0}}\Big[\sum_{x,y}|x\rangle|y\rangle A(x,y|x_{0},y_{0},\lambda)\Big]P(x_{0},y_{0}|\lambda)\Big[h.c.\Big]

(21)

To make our
future
expressions
more concise, define
the two abbreviations

R_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})
,

R=(x,y)
. Then

\rho_{{\underline{x}},{\underline{y}},{\underline{\lambda}}^{d}}=\sum_{R_{0},\lambda}\Big[|\lambda\rangle\sum_{R}|R\rangle\underbrace{A(R|R_{0},\lambda)\sqrt{P(R_{0}|\lambda)}\sqrt{P(\lambda)}}_{A(R,R_{0},\lambda)}\Big]\Big[h.c.\Big]\;.

(22)

From the
definitions
of conditional
probabilities
and conditional amplitudes,
we get

A(R|R_{0},\lambda)=A(y|x,R_{0},\lambda)A(x|R_{0},\lambda)

(23)

and

P(R_{0}|\lambda)=P(y_{0}|x_{0},\lambda)P(x_{0}|\lambda)\;.

(24)

Therefore,

A(R,R_{0},\lambda)\;=\;A(y|x,R_{0},\lambda)A(x|R_{0},\lambda)\sqrt{P(y_{0}|x_{0},\lambda)P(x_{0}|\lambda)P(\lambda)}\;.

(25)

4
Quantum Belief Propagation

The rules
(and their proof)
of classical BP
can be found
in the Bayesuvius chapter
entitled “Message
Passing (Belief Propagation)”.
Just like
the proof
of the classical
d-separation
theorem,
the
proof
of the rules for classical BP
relies on 3
ingredients:

•

the topology
of a
DAG

•

the definition
of conditional
probabilities
as ratios of
joint
probabilities

•

the splitting rule
for probabilities

Since these
3 ingredients
are also
available
in the quantum
side
if we replace
probabilities
by vector amplitudes,
we can
conclude that
the rules
for quantum
BP
are formally
the same as those
for classical BP,
modulus the
replacement of
probabilities
by
vector amplitudes.
The
difference in
normalization
of probabilities
and
vector amplitudes
does not
make
the
rules
for classical
and quantum
BP
different
because
these
rules
are defined up
to a normalization
constant.

In Bayesuvius,
the BP chapter entitled “Message Passing (Belief Propagation)”
considers a general case of classical BP (viz., BP for
polytrees (BP-Gen)) and a special case of classical
BP (viz., BP for bipartite bnets (BP-BB)).
We end this section
with 2 subsections dedicated
to the quantum
analogues of the rules
for BP-Gen and BP-BB.
Those 2 subsections
are simply exact quotes
from the BP chapter in
Bayesuvius,
except that
all
P
’s have been
crossed out and replaced by
|A\rangle
’s.

4.1
Quantum BP for polytrees

Let
{\underline{a}}^{na}=({\underline{a}}_{i})_{i=0,1,\ldots,na-1}

denote the parents of
{\underline{x}}

and

{\underline{b}}^{nb}=({\underline{b}}_{i})_{i=0,1,\ldots,nb-1}

its children.

Define

\displaystyle\pi_{\underline{x}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{na}}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|a^{na})\prod_{i}\pi_{{\underline{x}}\Leftarrow{\underline{a}}_{i}}(a_{i})

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{{\underline{a}}^{na}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|a^{na})]

(33)

(boundary case: if
{\underline{x}}

is a root node, use
\pi_{\underline{x}}(x)={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x)
.)
and

\lambda_{\underline{x}}(x)=\prod_{i}\lambda_{{\underline{b}}_{i}\Rightarrow{\underline{x}}}(x)\;.

(34)

(boundary case: if
{\underline{x}}

is a leaf node, use
\lambda_{\underline{x}}(x)=1
.)

•

RULE 1: (red parent)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\lambda_{{\underline{x}}\Rightarrow{\underline{a}}_{i}}(a_{i})}_{OUT}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(!a_{i})\sum_{x}\left[\underbrace{\lambda_{\underline{x}}(x)}_{IN}\sum_{(a_{k})_{k\neq i}}\left({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|a^{na})\prod_{k\neq i}\underbrace{\pi_{{\underline{x}}\Leftarrow{\underline{a}}_{k}}(a_{k})}_{IN}\right)\right]

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(!a_{i})\sum_{x}\left[\lambda_{\underline{x}}(x)E_{({\underline{a}}_{k})_{k\neq i}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|a^{na})]\right]

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal N}(!a_{i})E_{({\underline{a}}_{k})_{k\neq i}}E_{{\underline{x}}|a^{na}}\lambda_{\underline{x}}(x)

(37)

(boundary case:
if
{\underline{x}}
is a root node, use

\lambda_{{\underline{x}}\Rightarrow{\underline{a}}_{i}}(a_{i})={\cal N}(!a_{i})
.)

•

RULE 2: (red child)

\underbrace{\pi_{{\underline{b}}_{i}\Leftarrow{\underline{x}}}(x)}_{OUT}={\cal N}(!x)\underbrace{\pi_{\underline{x}}(x)}_{IN}\prod_{k\neq i}\underbrace{\lambda_{{\underline{b}}_{k}\Rightarrow{\underline{x}}}(x)}_{IN}

(38)

(boundary case:
if
{\underline{x}}
is a leaf node, use

\pi_{{\underline{b}}_{i}\Leftarrow{\underline{x}}}(x)={\cal N}(!x)\pi_{\underline{x}}(x)

.)

In the above
equations, if the
range set of a product is empty, then
define the product as 1; i.e.,

\prod_{k\in\emptyset}F(k)=1
.

Claim:
Define

BEL^{(t)}(x)={\cal N}(!x)\lambda^{(t)}_{\underline{x}}(x)\pi^{(t)}_{\underline{x}}(x)\;.

(39)

Then

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}BEL^{(t)}(x)={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|\epsilon)\;.

(40)

This says that
the belief in
{\underline{x}}=x

converges to
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x|\epsilon)
and it
equals the product
of messages received from all
parents and children of
{\underline{x}}=x
.

4.2
Quantum BP for Bipartite Bnets

1.

Traversing an
x
(i.e., root) node.

For
i=0,1,\ldots,nx-1
, if

{\alpha}\in nb(i)
, then,

m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Leftarrow i}(x_{i})=\prod_{\beta\in nb(i)-\alpha}m^{(t-1)}_{\beta\Rightarrow i}(x_{i})\;,

(41)

whereas if
{\alpha}\notin nb(i)

m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Leftarrow i}(x_{i})=m^{(t-1)}_{{\alpha}\Leftarrow i}(x_{i})\;.

(42)

2.

Traversing an
f
(i.e., leaf) node.

For
{\alpha}=0,1,\ldots,nf-1
, if

i\in nb({\alpha})
, then

\displaystyle m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Rightarrow i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(x_{k})_{k\in nb(\alpha)-i}}f_{\alpha}(x_{nb(\alpha)})\prod_{k\in nb(\alpha)-i}m^{(t-1)}_{{\alpha}\Leftarrow k}(x_{k})

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E^{(t-1)}_{(x_{k})_{k\in nb(\alpha)-i}}[f_{\alpha}(x_{nb(\alpha)})]\;,

(44)

whereas if
i\notin nb({\alpha})

m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Rightarrow i}(x_{i})=m^{(t-1)}_{{\alpha}\Rightarrow i}(x_{i})\;.

(45)

In the above
equations, if the
range set of a product is empty, then
define the product as 1; i.e.,

\prod_{k\in\emptyset}F(k)=1
.

Claim:

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x_{i}|\epsilon)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\cal N}(!x_{i})\prod_{{\alpha}\in nb(i)}m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Rightarrow i}(x_{i})\;

(46)

and

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\cancelto{|A\rangle}{P}}(x_{nb({\alpha})}|\epsilon)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}{\cal N}(!x_{nb({\alpha})})f_{\alpha}(x_{nb({\alpha})})\prod_{k\in nb({\alpha})}m^{(t)}_{{\alpha}\Leftarrow k}(x_{k})\;.

(47)

In the classical
case,

f_{\alpha}(x_{nb({\alpha})})

stands for a real valued
function (e.g.,
P(f_{\alpha}=1|x_{nb({\alpha})})
),
whereas in the quantum case,
it stands for a vector amplitude
(e.g.,
|A\rangle(f_{\alpha}=1|x_{nb({\alpha})})
).
