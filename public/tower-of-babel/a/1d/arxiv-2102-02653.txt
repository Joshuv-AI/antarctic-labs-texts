Typicality and entropy of processes on infinite trees
Ágnes Backhausz 1,2, Charles Bordenave 3, Balázs Szegedy 2

Abstract
. Consider a uniformly sampled random d d -regular graph on n n vertices. If d d is fixed and n n goes to ∞ \infty then we can relate typical (large probability) properties of such random graph to a family of invariant random processes (called "typical" processes) on the infinite d d -regular tree T d T_{d} . This correspondence between ergodic theory on T d T_{d} and random regular graphs is already proven to be fruitful in both directions. This paper continues the investigation of typical processes with a special emphasis on entropy. We study a natural notion of micro-state entropy for invariant processes on T d T_{d} . It serves as a quantitative refinement of the notion of typicality and is tightly connected to the asymptotic free energy in statistical physics. Using entropy inequalities, we provide new sufficient conditions for typicality for edge Markov processes. We also extend these notions and results to processes on unimodular Galton-Watson random trees.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Typical processes and sofic entropy

Random
d
-regular graphs have been extensively studied over the past 50 years
[
10
,
21
,
28
,
32
]
. Sophisticated methods from probability theory, combinatorics and statistical physics have been successfully used to uncover many of their properties such as independence ratio, the density of a maximal cut or its spectral gap
[
18
,
31
,
21
]
.
The recently emerging theory of graph limits
[
8
,
24
,
22
,
26
]
gives a new, limiting point of views on the subject. It turns out that many of the crucial properties of random
d
-regular graphs for
d
fixed and
n
going to infinity can also be studied in the framework of ergodic theory on the infinite
d
-regular tree
T_{d}

[
2
]
. An illustration of the power of this method is the proof of the Gaussianity of the almost eigenvectors of random
d
-regular graphs
[
3
,
11
]
. The proof is a combination of ergodic theory on
T_{d}
with information theoretic methods.

In this paper we are interested in a question which is also related to the limit of random regular graphs, namely, to the family of typical processes on
T_{d}
introduced in
[
2
]
. These objects arise as the local limits of vertex-colored random
d
-regular graphs (see formal definition below). These typical processes contain useful information on the structure of
d
-regular graphs. For example, the classical fact from
[
9
]
that the independence ratio is separated from
1/2
is equivalent to the fact that the alternating coloring of the infinite
d
-regular tree is not typical. Several necessary conditions have been formulated for typical processes in the last years. Some of them are about the covariance structure, others are entropy inequalities
[
4
,
5
,
15
]
. However, general sufficient conditions for a process to be typical are less common.

In this paper we obtain sufficient conditions for the typicality of processes on
T_{d}
, by studying a new micro-state entropy. This entropy measures in some sense the number of finite approximations of a process on a random instance of a large
d
-regular graph. This entropy is tightly connected to Bowen’s sofic entropy in measured group theory, see
[
13
]
.

Our approach has another connection to the convergence of random graphs. While graph limit theory shows great promise in a variety of questions related to random
d
-regular graphs, it also revealed an intriguing open problem. It is believed that for fix
d
and
n\to\infty
we have that a random
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices is convergent (in probability) in the local-global sense of
[
24
]
and also right-convergent in the sense of
[
22
]
, this last notion of convergence relies on a deep statistical physics theory, see the monograph
[
27
]
. These general conjectures are a common strengthening of a large variety of conjectures many of which are already proven. For example the convergence of the independence ratio is proven in
[
6
,
17
]
and the convergence of asymptotic free energies of a large class of statistical physics models on random
d
-regular graphs in
[
29
,
14
]
.
In this paper, we introduce an upper and a lower version of the micro-state entropies. Equality of these entropies imply the convergence of random regular graphs. For a family of processes we can establish this equality. This will lead to the sufficient conditions mentioned above, and this shows that our results might lead to a deep understanding of the structure of random regular graphs.

1.2.
Definitions.

We now formalize the main definitions. Let
d\geq 3
be an integer and
T_{d}
the infinite
d
-regular tree (all vertices have
d
neighbors). Let
M
be a finite set. A process
X
on
T_{d}
is a random variable on
M^{T_{d}}
. This process (or its law) is
invariant
if the law of
X
is invariant by all automorphisms of the tree
T_{d}
. We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
the set of invariant probability measures on
M^{T_{d}}
.

We now define the Benjamini-Schramm topology. A pair
(G,f)
formed by a graph
G=(V,E)
and a map
f:V\to M
will be called a colored graph with color set
M
. A rooted colored graph is a triple
(G,f,o)
formed by a connected colored graph
(G,f)
and a distinguished vertex
o
of
G
, called the root. Two rooted colored graphs
(G,f,o)
and
(G^{\prime},f^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism of
G
and
G^{\prime}
which preserves the colors and the roots. An equivalence class of rooted colored graphs is called an unlabeled rooted colored graph in combinatorics.

Unlabeled rooted graphs give the proper setup for defining a meaningful notion of convergence. It is however more convenient to work with rooted labeled graphs instead of unlabeled rooted graphs. To this end, we now define a randomized canonical graph in each equivalence class. We define the set of finite integer sequences as

\mathbb{N}^{f}=\cup_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{N}^{k}

(1.1)

where
\mathbb{N}^{0}=\{o\}
and
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots,\}
by convention. The tree
T_{d}
can be classically built on a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{f}
as follows. The root of the tree is
o
, its
d
-neighbors of
o
are
V_{1}=\{1,\ldots,d\}\subset\mathbb{N}
, the neighbors of
i\in V_{1}
are
o
and
\{(i,1),\ldots,(i,d-1)\}\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
and so on. More generally, if
(G,o)
is a rooted graph, the breadth-first search tree started at the root
o
, where ties between vertices are broken uniformly at random defines a random graph
(G^{\prime},o)
on a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{f}
whose law depends only the equivalence class of
(G,o)
: a vertex at distance
k
from the root receives a label in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
, if
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1})
is the label of its parent in the search tree, it has the label
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k-1},j)
if it the
j
-th offspring of its parent in the random ordering. We call this random rooted graph, the
randomly labeled rooted graph
associated to
(G,o)
. Conversely, we will say that a random labeled colored graph
(G,f,o)
on a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{f}
is randomly labeled if its law is equal to the law of the randomly labeled rooted colored graph associated to its unlabeled rooted colored graph. By definition if
X\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
then
(T_{d},X,o)
is randomly labeled.

Recall that a graph is locally finite if all its vertices have a finite number of neighbors. We denote by
\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}
(respectively
\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M}
) the set of locally finite graphs (respectively locally finite colored graphs on the color set
M
) on the vertex set
\mathbb{N}^{f}
rooted at
o
which are admissible in the sense that they are realizable as a breadth-first search labeling of a locally finite graph. The sets
\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M}
are complete separable metric spaces when equipped with the distance
{\mathrm{d}}(g,g^{\prime})=\sum_{r\geq 0}2^{-r}{\mathbf{1}}_{(g)_{r}\neq(g^{\prime})_{r}}
where
{\mathbf{1}}
is the indicator function and
g_{r}
is the restriction of
g
to the vertices at distance at most
r
from the root. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
the set of probability measures on
\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M}
. We equip
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
with a distance, also denoted by
{\mathrm{d}}
, which generates the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
(for example, the Lévy-Prohorov distance).

If
(G,f)
is a locally finite colored graph and
v
is a vertex of
G
then we denote by
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G,v}(f)
the law in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
of the randomly labeled rooted graph
((G,f)(v),v)
where
(G,f)(v)
is the restriction of
(G,f)
to the connected component of
G
containing
v
. The law
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G,v}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet})
is defined similarly for a graph
G
and a vertex
v
. Finally, if
G
is finite with vertex set
V
, we may define the probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet})
and
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
:

{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}{\mathrm{distr}}_{G,v}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}{\mathrm{distr}}_{G,v}(f).

For integers
n\geq d+1
with
nd
even, the set
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
of simple
d
-regular graphs on the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is not empty. For each integer
n\geq d+1
with
nd
even, let
G_{n}
be a uniformly distributed random graph on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
. Almost-surely, the probability distribution
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}
converges as
n
goes to infinity to the Dirac mass at
T_{d}
rooted at
o
(it is a consequence of the fact that the number of cycles of length
k
in
G_{n}
is
O(1)
for any fixed
k
, see
[
10
]
). It can further be checked that, a.s. if
\mu
is an accumulation point of
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}(f_{n})
for some sequence of colorings
f_{n}\in M^{n}
, then
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
. This motivates the following definition introduced in
[
2
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Typical process)
.

A measure
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
is
weakly typical
if

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\exists f\in M^{n}:{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}(f),\mu)\leq\epsilon)=1.

It is
strongly typical
if

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\exists f\in M^{n}:{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}(f),\mu)\leq\epsilon)=1.

Note that it is apparent from the definition that being typical does not depend on the choice of the distance
{\mathrm{d}}
which generates the weak topology. We note also that the definition in
[
2
]
is slightly different but it turns out to be equivalent by using some measure concentration phenomena (see
[
3
, Section 5]
).

We may also define a notion of micro-state entropy of
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
as follows. For
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 0
, the probability measure
\mu_{r}
is defined as the restriction of
\mu
to the vertices at distance at most
r
from the root. Similarly, for
f\in M^{n}
, we define
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r}
as the restriction of the distribution
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)
to the set of vertices at distance at most
r
from the root. For any
\epsilon>0
, if
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
, we define

\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\{f\in M^{n}:{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\mu_{r})\leq\epsilon\}.

(1.2)

This is the set of coloring functions
f
on
G
which are
\epsilon
close to
\mu_{r}
in the Benjamini-Schramm sense. Let
G_{n}
be a uniformly distributed random graph on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
with
n\geq d-1
and
nd
even. Roughly speaking the sofic entropy of
\mu
is a limit of

H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|,

(1.3)

in
n\to\infty
and then in
\epsilon\to 0
and
r\to\infty
. Notice that random regular graphs could be replaced by other locally tree-like graph sequences, but as the expectation of
|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|
is easier to compute in our case, we use the definition based on random regular graphs. Since
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
is a random variable in
\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty)
some care is needed. More formally,
we fix
0<\alpha<1
and consider the following median value of
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
,

h_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon,\alpha)=\sup\left\{h\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty):\mathbb{P}\left(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h\right)\geq\alpha\right\}.

Since
h_{n}
is non-decreasing in
\epsilon
, we may define the upper and lower entropies of
\mu
as

\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}h_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon,\alpha)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}h_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon,\alpha).

These entropies are extended real numbers in
\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty)
. This entropy can be interpreted as a version of Bowen’s sofic entropy, see the survey
[
13
]
.

The sofic entropies
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
do not depend on
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Note that they do not depend neither on the choice of the distance
{\mathrm{d}}
(in the sense that if two distances are topologically equivalent, the corresponding quantities
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
are equal). We shall prove the following.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 0
. The function
\alpha\mapsto(\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha),\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha))
is constant on
(0,1)
.

By Lemma
1.2
, we may consider the common value of the entropies: for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

\bar{h}(\mu,r)=\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\underline{h}(\mu,r)=\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha).

By construction,
\bar{h}(\mu,r)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r)
are the growth rates of the number of coloring of a random
d
-regular graph whose
r
-neighborhood is close to
\mu_{r}
. Finally, since
\bar{h}(\mu,r)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r)
are non-increasing in
r
, we may define the
upper and lower sofic entropies
as

\bar{h}(\mu)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\bar{h}(\mu,r)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\underline{h}(\mu)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\underline{h}(\mu,r).

Taking the limit as
\alpha\to 1
, for
h\geq 0
, the inequality
\bar{h}(\mu)\geq h
is equivalent to the existence of a vanishing sequence
(\epsilon_{n})
and such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,1/\epsilon_{n},\epsilon_{n})\geq(h-\epsilon_{n})_{+})=1,

where
(x)_{+}=x\vee 0
,
and similarly for
\underline{h}(\mu)
. Since for
\nu,\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
,
{\mathrm{d}}(\nu_{r},\mu_{r})
converges to
{\mathrm{d}}(\nu,\mu)
as
r\to\infty
, the sofic entropy is thus closely related to the typicality:

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
. We have
\bar{h}(\mu)\geq 0
(resp.
\underline{h}(\mu)\geq 0
) if and only if
\mu
is weakly (resp. strongly) typical.

This work is notably motivated the following conjecture which is connected to the notion of right convergence, see
[
22
]
and Subsection
1.6
below.

Conjecture 1.4
.

For all
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
, we have
\underline{h}(\mu)=\bar{h}(\mu)
. In particular,
\mu
is weakly typical if and only if it is strongly typical.

In this work, we will compute the entropy
\underline{h}(\mu)=\bar{h}(\mu)
for a large class of invariant measures
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
. This class is a class of processes where a second moment method can be applied. In a subsequent work, we will use more advanced statistical physics methods to refine our criterion. This conjecture might also be related to the Guerra’s interpolation method
[
23
]
developed in the context of random graphs notably in
[
20
,
6
,
22
,
29
,
14
,
25
]
.

1.3.
Annealed entropy

If
r\geq 0
is an integer and
S
is a subset of
T_{d}
, we define
B_{r}(S)
the subset of vertices of
T_{d}
at distance at most
r
from a vertex in
S
. For ease of notation, for
r\geq 1
, we define
S_{r}=B_{r}(o)
as the ball of radius
r
around the root of
T_{d}
and
E_{r}=B_{r-1}(\{o,1\})
is the set of vertices at distance
r-1
from the edge
\{o,1\}
of
T_{d}
.

If
X
is an invariant process with law
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 1
integer, we set

\Sigma_{r}(X)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu)=H(X_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(X_{E_{r}}),

(1.4)

where
X_{S}
is the restriction of
X
to the subset
S\subset T_{d}
and
H
is the usual Shannon entropy: if
Y
is a random variable taking value a finite set
F
, then

H(Y)=-\sum_{x\in F}\mathbb{P}(Y=x)\ln\mathbb{P}(Y=x).

By abusing notation, we denote entropy in the same way for both random variables and their distributions. It follows from
[
3
,
12
]
(see Subsection
2.2
below for details) that
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
is non-increasing in
r
. We may thus define

\Sigma(\mu)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

Note that the law of
X_{S_{r}}
is
\mu_{r}
and that the law of
X_{E_{r}}
is a marginal of
\mu_{r}
. The quantities
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
and
\Sigma(\mu)
will be called the annealed entropy of
\mu_{r}
and
\mu
, the reason will be clear in the forthcoming Subsection
2.3
. The following first moment bound is essentially contained in
[
2
,
12
]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and integer
r\geq 1
, we have

\bar{h}(\mu,r)\leq\Sigma_{r}(\mu)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\bar{h}(\mu)\leq\Sigma(\mu).

As a corollary, we recover the "star-edge inequality" of
[
2
,
3
]
which is a necessary condition of typicality: if
\mu
is weakly typical then by Lemma
1.3
,
\bar{h}(\mu)\geq 0
and thus, by Theorem
1.5
,
\Sigma(\mu)\geq 0
.

Corollary 1.6

(
[
2
,
3
]
)
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
is a weakly typical process then
\Sigma(\mu)\geq 0
.

The main result of this paper is a matching lower bound for a large class of invariant processes. To this end, we first recall the notion of coupling restricted to our setting. Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be two finite sets,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be two random variables on
M_{1}^{T_{d}}
and
M_{2}^{T_{d}}
with respective laws
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. A coupling of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is a distribution
\nu
on
(M_{1}\times M_{2})^{T_{d}}
such that if
Y=(Y_{1},Y_{2})
has law
\nu
,
Y_{i}
has law
\mu_{i}
for
i=1,2
. If
X_{i}
is an invariant process for
i=1,2
, we say that
\nu
or
Y
is an invariant coupling if
\nu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M_{1}\times M_{2})
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
. For any integer
r\geq 1
, if all invariant couplings
\nu
of
\mu
and
\mu
satisfy
\Sigma_{r}(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
then
\underline{h}(\mu,r)=\bar{h}(\mu,r)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

In particular, if the above condition holds for an increasing sequence of integers
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
then

\underline{h}(\mu)=\bar{h}(\mu)=\Sigma(\mu).

We note that the bound
\Sigma_{r}(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
is attained for the independent coupling
Y=(X_{1},X_{2})
with
X_{i}
independent with law
\mu
.
Note also that if
\nu
is the trivial coupling of
\mu
and
\mu
, that is
Y=(X,X)
with
X
with law
\mu
, we find
\Sigma_{r}(\nu)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
. Hence, under the condition of Theorem
1.7
, we have
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)\leq 2\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
or equivalently
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)\geq 0
. As a corollary, by Lemma
1.3
, we thus obtain the following sufficient condition for typicality.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
an increasing sequence of integers be such that for all invariant couplings
\nu
of
\mu
and
\mu
and all
k\geq 1
we have
\Sigma_{r_{k}}(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma_{r_{k}}(\mu)
. Then
\mu
is strongly typical.

1.4.
Edge-Markov processes

There is a specific class of processes in
\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
for which it is possible to improve on Theorem
1.7
, the edge-Markov processes defined as follows. As above, for integer
r\geq 0
,
B_{r}(S)
is the
r
-neighborhood of a subset
S
in
T_{d}
. For
r\geq 1
, recall that
S_{r}=B_{r}(o)
and
E_{r}=B_{r-1}(\{o,1\})
.

Definition 1.9

(Edge-Markov process)
.

A probability measure in
M^{T_{d}}
is edge-Markov if conditioned on the value at an edge, the processes on the left and right subtrees of that edge are independent.

More generally, for integer
r\geq 1
, a probability measure on
M^{T_{d}}
is
r
-Markov if conditioned on the value at
B_{r-1}(e)
, the
(r-1)
-neighborhood of an edge
e
, the processes on the left and right subtrees of
e
are independent (for
r=1
we recover the edge-Markov process).

Let
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
denote the set of probability measures on
M^{S_{r}}
that are invariant by automorphisms of
S_{r}
and whose restriction to
E_{r}
is invariant by switching the two sides of the edge
\{o,1\}
. If
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
, then,
\mu_{r}
, its restriction to
S_{r}
, is in
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
. Conversely, the following lemma is easy to see.

Lemma 1.10
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
. Then there is a unique
r
-Markov process
\mu(p)\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
such that the marginal of
\mu(p)
on
S_{r}
is equal to
p
.

If
p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
, we define
\Sigma(p)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu(p))=H(X_{S_{r}})-(d/2)H(X_{E_{r}})
as in Equation (
1.4
), where
X
has law
p
. As above, if
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are probability measures on
M_{1}^{S_{r}}
and
M_{2}^{S_{r}}
, a coupling of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
is a probability measure on
M_{1}^{S_{r}}\times M_{2}^{S_{r}}
whose marginals are
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. The following theorem is a strengthening of Theorem
1.7
for egde-Markov processes.

Theorem 1.11
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
. If for all couplings
q\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M^{2})
of
p
and
p
, we have
\Sigma(q)\leq 2\Sigma(p)
then
\underline{h}(\mu(p))=\bar{h}(\mu(p))=\Sigma(p)
and
\mu(p)
is strongly typical.

Theorem
1.11
provides an easy to check criteria for typicality for edge Markov processes. In the course of the proof, we will need an important maximizing property satisfied by edge Markov processes (a closely related characterization can be found in
[
12
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
3
, Lemma 10.1]
).

Lemma 1.12
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 1
. We have

\Sigma_{r+1}(X)\leq\Sigma_{r}(X)

with equality if and only if
X_{S_{r+1}}
is a
r
-Markov process on
S_{r+1}
.

Notice that we also get that
\Sigma(X)=\Sigma_{r}(X)
holds for
r
-Markov processes.

1.5.
Vertex-Markov processes

There is a subclass of edge-Markov processes for which the annealed entropy takes a particularly simple form.

Definition 1.13

(Vertex-Markov process)
.

Let
T
be a tree, a probability measure in
M^{T}
is vertex-Markov if conditioned on the value at a vertex, the processes on the pending subtrees of that vertex are independent.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
denote the set of probability measures on
M^{E_{1}}
that are invariant by switching the two sides of the edge
\{o,1\}
. If
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(\mu)
then its restriction to
E_{1}
is in
\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
. Conversely, if
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
, there exists a unique vertex-Markov process whose restriction to
E_{1}
is
p
. We denote the law of this process by
\mu(p)
. If
X\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
, we define

\Sigma_{e}(X)=\frac{d}{2}H(X_{E_{1}})-(d-1)H(X_{o}).

If
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
, we set
\Sigma(p)=\Sigma_{e}(\mu(p))
. Vertex-Markov processes satisfy the following extremal property.

Lemma 1.14
.

If
X\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
then

\Sigma_{1}(X)\leq\Sigma_{e}(X),

with equality if and only if
X_{S_{1}}
is a vertex-Markov process on
S_{1}
.

Combined with Theorem
1.11
, the above lemma implies the following corollary.

Theorem 1.15
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
. If for all couplings
q\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M^{2})
of
p
and
p
we have
\Sigma(q)\leq 2\Sigma(p)
, then
\underline{h}(\mu(p))=\bar{h}(\mu(p))=\Sigma(p)
and
\mu(p)
is strongly typical.

Proof.

Let
p^{\prime}=\mu(p)_{1}\in\mathcal{I}_{d,1}(M)
be the law of
\mu(p)
restricted to
S_{1}
. Let
q^{\prime}
be an invariant coupling of
p^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
and let
q
be its restriction to
E_{1}
. By construction
q\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M^{2})
. Moreover, by Lemma
1.14
,
\Sigma(q^{\prime})\leq\Sigma_{1}(\mu(q^{\prime}))=\Sigma_{e}(\mu(q))=\Sigma(q)
. It follows that Theorem
1.15
is a consequence of Theorem
1.11
applied to
r=1
and
p^{\prime}=\mu(p)_{1}
.
∎

1.6.
Application to factor graphs and combinatorial optimization

In this paragraph, we discuss a basic connection between asymptotic free energy of factor graphs and sofic entropy. This may serve an extra motivation for studying the sofic entropy.

Let
M
be a finite set,
r\geq 1
be an integer and let
\varphi
be a function on the set of rooted unlabeled
M
-colored graphs of radius
r
taking value in
(0,\infty)
. If
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
,

Z_{G}=\sum_{f\in M^{n}}\prod_{v=1}^{n}\varphi((G,f,v)_{r}),

where
(G,f,v)_{r}
is the rooted colored graph associated to the ball of radius
r
around
v
in
G
. We set

\psi=\ln\varphi.

The asymptotic free energy is defined as the limit of

(1/n)\ln Z_{G_{n}}
where
G_{n}
is a uniformly sampled graph in
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
(provided that the limit exists). By standard concentration inequality (see argument in Theorem
2.1
), it is easy to check that if
G_{n}
is uniformly sampled in
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
, then, in probability, as
n
goes to infinity,

\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}-{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}\to 0.

(1.5)

It is straightforward to express the limits of the expected free energy in terms of the entropy. If
p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
, we set for ease of notation
\underline{h}(p)=\underline{h}(\mu(p),r)
and similarly for
\bar{h}(p)
(there are the upper and lower growth rates of the number of colorings of
G_{n}
whose
r
-neighborhood is close to
p
). In the statement below, we use the notation
\langle p,\psi\rangle={\mathbb{E}}\psi(X)
where
X
has law
p
.

Lemma 1.16
.

For integer
r\geq 1
and
\psi
as above, if
G_{n}
is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
(with
nd
even and
n\geq d+1
), we have

\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}{{\left(\underline{h}(p)+\langle p,\psi\rangle\right)}}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}{{\left(\bar{h}(p)+\langle p,\psi\rangle\right)}}.

In particular, if Conjecture
1.4
holds true, then it would automatically imply the convergence of the expected free energy for all functions
\psi
. Note also that Theorem
1.7
can be used to obtain a lower bound expected free energy while Theorem
1.5
can be used to get an upper bound. With proper technical conditions, it is possible to extend Lemma
1.16
to some hard-constrained models, that is to some functions
\varphi=e^{\psi}
which take value in
[0,\infty)
. For simplicity, we will however not discuss in details this possibility here.

In the same vein, in combinatorial optimization problems, we are often interested in the computation of a graph functional of the form:

L_{G}=\max_{f\in M^{n}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\psi((G,f,v)_{r}),

with
r\geq 1
and
\psi
as above. Again, it is easy to check that if
G_{n}
is uniformly sampled in
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
, we have, in probability,

\frac{L_{G_{n}}}{n}-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\to 0.

The following statement is a corollary of Lemma
1.16
. It shows that typical processes are intimately connected to the computation of limits of
{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}/n
.

Lemma 1.17
.

For integer
r\geq 1
and
\psi
as above, if
G_{n}
is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
(with
nd
even and
n\geq d+1
), we have

\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M):\underline{h}(p)\geq 0}\langle p,\psi\rangle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M):\bar{h}(p)\geq 0}\langle p,\psi\rangle.

Again, we observe that Theorem
1.7
can be used to obtain a lower bound on
{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}/n
and Theorem
1.5
an upper bound.

Remark 1.18
.

We conclude this paragraph by mentioning that, for
r=1
, there is a simplification of Lemma
1.16
and Lemma
1.17
for functions
\psi
of the form

\psi((G,f,v)_{1})=\psi_{0}(f(v))+\sum_{u:u\sim v}\psi_{1}(f(v),f(u))

where the supremum in Lemma
1.16
and Lemma
1.17
is taken over
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
instead of
p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,1}(M)
and the entropic term is given by
\underline{h}(p)=\sup\underline{h}(q)
where the supremum is over all
q\in\mathcal{I}_{d,1}(M)
whose restriction to
E_{1}
is
p
, and similarly for
\bar{h}(p)
. This can be useful because it reduces the dimension of the underlying optimization problem. In that case, Theorem
1.15
can be used to give lower bounds.

Organization of the paper

The remainder of this text is organized as follows. In Section
2

3
, we will prove the main results of this paper. In the final Section

2.1.
Concentration of entropy: proof of Lemma
1.2

Let
G_{n}
be a uniformly distributed random graph on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
with
n\geq d-1
and
nd
even. Recall the definition of
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
in (
1.3
). The aim of this subsection is to establish the following concentration result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 0
,
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
,
h\in\{-\infty\}\cup[0,\infty)
. For all functions
\delta:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty]
with
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)=0
, we have the following: if for all
\epsilon>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h)\geq-\delta(\epsilon),

(2.1)

then
\overline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)\geq h
for all
0<\alpha<1
. Conversely, for all functions
\delta
as above such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-2}\delta(\epsilon)=0
: if for all
\epsilon>0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,,r,\epsilon)\leq h)\geq-\delta(\epsilon)

(2.2)

then
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)\leq h
for all
0<\alpha<1
. Finally, the same claims hold with
\liminf
and
\underline{h}
replacing
\limsup
and
\bar{h}
.

It is immediate to check that Lemma
1.2
is a corollary of Theorem
2.1
. Beware of the asymmetry between the lower and upper bound. We believe that it is a caveat of our proof. It is ultimately due to the fact that
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
can be equal to
-\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
makes a detour through a relaxation of the entropy. Fix
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 0
. If
G
is in
\mathcal{G}_{d}(n)
, we define for
\beta>0
,

Z_{G}(\beta)=\sum_{f\in M^{n}}e^{-n\beta{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\mu_{r})}.

We start the proof of the proposition with a concentration inequality.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G_{n}
be uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
with
dn
even and
n\geq d-1
. There exists a constant
C
depending on
(d,r)
and a deterministic number
s_{n}(\beta)
depending on
(n,d,r,\beta)
such that for any
t>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}{{\left({{\left|\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta)-s_{n}(\beta)\right|}}\geq t\right)}}\leq C\exp(-nt^{2}/(C\beta)^{2}).

Proof.

The proof follows a standard path.
By classical contiguity results, it is enough to establish the claim for the configuration model (see Bollobás
[
10
, Section 2.4]
). Recall that the configuration model is the graph (with possible loops and multiple edges) obtained as follows. We attach to each vertex in
[n]
,
d
half-edges. We sample a matching
m
on the set
\vec{E}
of
nd
half-edges uniformly at random (recall that a matching is an involution without fixed point). Finally, we form a
d
-regular graph
G=G(m)
by creating an edge for each pair of matched half-edges.

Let us say that two matchings
m,m^{\prime}
differ by a switch if there exists
(a,b,c,d)
in
\vec{E}
such that
m(e)=m^{\prime}(e)
for all
e\in\vec{E}\backslash\{a,b,c,d\}
and
m(a)=b
,
m^{\prime}(a)=c
,
m(c)=d
,
m^{\prime}(b)=d
. If
m
and
m^{\prime}
differ by a switch, we claim that for any
f\in M^{n}
,

{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G(m)}(f)_{r},{\mathrm{distr}}_{G(m^{\prime})}(f)_{r})\leq C_{0}\frac{4d(d-1)^{r-1}}{n}=\frac{\theta}{n},

where
C_{0}
is the diameter of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G}^{\bullet}_{M})
for the distance
{\mathrm{d}}
.
Indeed, we have
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G,v}(f)_{r}={\mathrm{distr}}_{G^{\prime},v^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})_{r}
if the rooted subgraphs
(G,f,v)_{r}
and
(G^{\prime},f^{\prime},v^{\prime})_{r}
are isomorphic. Notably,
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G(m),v}(f)_{r}={\mathrm{distr}}_{G(m^{\prime}),v}(f)_{r}
unless
v
is at distance at most
r
from an edge in the symmetric difference of
G(m)
and
G(m^{\prime})
.

We deduce that

e^{-\beta\theta}\leq\frac{Z_{G(m^{\prime})}}{Z_{G(m)}}\leq e^{\beta\theta}

and

{{\left|\ln Z_{G(m)}-\ln Z_{G(m^{\prime})}\right|}}\leq\beta\theta.

From
[
32
, Theorem 2.19]
, if
m
is a uniformly sampled matching on
\vec{E}
, we get

\mathbb{P}{{\left({{\left|\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G(m)}-{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G(m)}\right|}}\geq t\right)}}\leq 2\exp(-nt^{2}/(2d\theta^{2}\beta^{2})).

The conclusion follows with
s_{n}(\beta)={\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G(m)}(\beta)
.
∎

We are ready for the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Recall the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
in (
1.2
). Since
\mu
and
r
are fixed, we write simply
\mathcal{F}_{G}(\epsilon)
and set
F_{G}(\epsilon)=|\mathcal{F}_{G}(\epsilon)|
. For any

, we have

\ln Z_{G}(\beta)\geq\ln F_{G}(\epsilon)-n\beta\epsilon,

(2.3)

where we have used that
{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\mu_{r})\leq\epsilon
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{G}(\epsilon)
. The other way around, if
f\notin\mathcal{F}_{G}(\epsilon)
, we have
{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\mu_{r})\geq\epsilon
. Hence,

\ln Z_{G}(\beta)\leq\ln{{\left(|M|^{n}e^{-n\beta\epsilon}+F_{G}(\epsilon)\right)}}\leq\ln 2+{{\left(\ln F_{G}(\epsilon)\right)}}\vee(n(\ln|M|-\beta\epsilon)).

(2.4)

We may now prove the first claim of the theorem. Assume that (
2.1
) holds for some
h\geq 0
(if
h=-\infty
, there is nothing to prove). Let
h_{1}<h
and
\mathcal{E}=\{H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h\}
. On the event
\mathcal{E}
, from (
2.3
), we have for all
\beta>0
,

\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta)\geq h-\beta\epsilon.

There exists
\beta_{\epsilon}
such that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,
\beta_{\epsilon}\epsilon\to 0
,
\beta_{\epsilon}\to\infty
and
\beta_{\epsilon}^{2}\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
. For this choice of
\beta
,
(t_{\epsilon}/\beta_{\epsilon})^{2}\gg\delta(\epsilon)
for some
t_{\epsilon}\to 0
. It follows from Lemma
2.2
and (
2.1
) that for any
h_{2},h_{3}
such that
h_{1}<h_{2}<h_{3}<h
,
s_{n}(\beta_{\epsilon})\geq h_{3}
for all
n
large enough (depending on
\epsilon
), because the event
\mathcal{E}
overlaps with the event from Lemma
2.2
when
\beta_{\epsilon}
satisfies this condition. Let
0<\alpha<1
. Applying again Lemma
2.2
, we deduce that the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta_{\epsilon})\geq h_{2}\}
has probability greater than
\alpha
for all
n
large enough.

Now, there exists
\eta_{\epsilon}
such that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,
\eta_{\epsilon}\to 0
and
\beta_{\epsilon}\eta_{\epsilon}\to\infty
(for example
\eta_{\epsilon}=1/\sqrt{\beta}_{\epsilon}
). We apply (
2.4
) with
\epsilon=\eta_{\epsilon}
. We get on the event
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, if
\epsilon
is small enough,

H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\eta_{\epsilon})\geq\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta_{\epsilon})-\frac{1}{n}\ln 2\geq h_{2}-\frac{1}{n}\ln 2.

The right-hand side is larger than

if

is large enough. We deduce that
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)\geq h_{1}
, since

can be arbitrarily close to

The second claim is proven similarly. Since

takes value in

, we have
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\leq-1
if and only if

. We may thus prove the second claim with

. Assume that (
2.2
) holds for some
\delta(\epsilon)
which will be defined later on. We now set
\mathcal{E}=\{H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\leq h\}
. On the event
\mathcal{E}
, from (

), we have for all
\beta>0
,

\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta)\leq\frac{1}{n}\ln 2+h\vee(\ln|M|-\beta\epsilon).

If
\beta\geq\beta_{\epsilon}=(\ln|M|+1)/\epsilon
then we get, for all

large enough

\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}(\beta)\leq\frac{1}{n}\ln 2+h.

For our choice of

, we have
\delta(\epsilon)\beta^{2}_{\epsilon}\to 0
as

. Then, if

and

The latter is less than

∎

2.2.
Maximizers of the annealed entropy: proofs of Lemma
1.12
and Lemma
1.14

In this subsection, we prove Lemma
1.12
and Lemma
1.14
. If
X,Y
are discrete random variables, we recall that the relative entropy of
X
given
Y
is

H(X|Y)=-\sum_{x,y}\mathbb{P}((X,Y)=(x,y))\ln\mathbb{P}(X=x|Y=y),

where
\mathbb{P}(A|B)=\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)/\mathbb{P}(B)
is the usual conditional probability (if
\mathbb{P}(B)=0
,
\mathbb{P}(A|B)
takes an arbitrary value). In other words,
H(X|Y)
is the average over
Y
of the entropy of the conditional law of
X
given
Y
. We will repeatedly use that

H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X|Y)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad H(X|(Y,Y^{\prime}))\leq H(X|Y),

(2.5)

with equality if and only if
X
conditioned on
Y
is independent of
Y^{\prime}
.

We start with the proof of Lemma
1.12
.

Proof of Lemma
1.12
.

The following fact is useful. For a given integer
r\geq 1
, we introduce the finite set
N=M^{S_{r-1}}
where as usual
S_{r-1}=B_{r-1}(o)
. We consider the map
\Psi
from
M^{T_{d}}
to
N^{T_{d}}
which maps
x
to
\Psi(x)
such that for
v\in T_{d}
,
\Psi(x)_{v}
is the restriction of
x
to
S_{r-1}(v)
(composed by a given isomorphism from
S_{r-1}(v)
to
S_{r-1}
). If
X
is a process on
T_{d}
then for all integers
t\geq 0
, we have
\Sigma_{t+r}(X)=\Sigma_{t+1}(\Psi(X))
. Moreover, if
X
is a
r
-Markov process, then
\Psi(X)
is an edge-Markov process.

As a byproduct, it is sufficient to prove Theorem
1.12
with
r=1
: we should check that

\Sigma_{2}(X)\leq\Sigma_{1}(X)

with equality if and only if
X_{S_{2}}
is an edge Markov process. Note that the above inequality can be equivalently written as

H(X_{S_{2}})-H(X_{S_{1}})-\frac{d}{2}H(X_{E_{2}})+\frac{d}{2}H(X_{E_{1}})\leq 0.

(2.6)

To check that (
2.6
) holds, we need some extra notation. We denote by
L=\{2,\ldots,d\}
the left side of
E_{2}
along
E_{1}=\{o,1\}
. We also set
L_{i}=\{(i,1),\ldots,(i,d-1)\}
with
i=1,\ldots,d
. We have
S_{1}=L\cup E_{1}
and thus, from (
2.5
)

H(S_{1})=H(E_{1})+H(L|E_{1}),

where for ease of notation, for sets
S,T
, we write
H(S)
and
H(S|T)
in place of
H(X_{S})
and
H(X_{S}|X_{T})
.
Similarly, since
E_{2}=S_{1}\cup L_{1}
,

H(E_{2})=H(S_{1})+H(L_{1}|S_{1})=H(E_{1})+H(L|E_{1})+H(L_{1}|S_{1}).

Finally, since
S_{2}
is the disjoint union of
E_{2}
and
\cup_{i=2}^{d}L_{i}
, we have,

H(S_{2})=H(E_{2})+H(\cup_{i=2}^{d}L_{i}|E_{2}).

The last three identities imply that Equation (
2.6
) is equivalent to

H(\cup_{i=2}^{d}L_{i}|E_{2})-{{\left(\frac{d}{2}-1\right)}}H(L_{1}|S_{1})-\frac{d}{2}H(L|E_{1})\leq 0.

(2.7)

Using the invariance, we deduce from (
2.5
) that

H(\cup_{i=2}^{d}L_{i}|E_{2})\leq(d-1)H(L_{2}|E_{2})

with equality if and only if there is conditional independence of the
X_{L_{i}}
’s given
X_{E_{2}}
. Now, since
E_{2}
contains
S_{1}
, we get

H(L_{2}|E_{2})\leq H(L_{2}|S_{1})=H(L_{1}|S_{1}),

with equality in case of conditional independence of
X_{L_{2}}
and
X_{E_{2}\backslash S_{1}}
given
X_{S_{1}}
. It follows that the left-hand side of (
2.7
) is upper bounded by

\frac{d}{2}H(L_{1}|S_{1})-\frac{d}{2}H(L|E_{1}).

(2.8)

From the invariance of
X
by switching the two sides of
e
, we get
H(L|E_{1})=H(L_{1}|E_{1})
and thus (
2.8
) is equal to

\frac{d}{2}{{\left(H(L_{1}|S_{1})-H(L_{1}|E_{1})\right)}}.

Using again (
2.5
), since
E_{1}\subset S_{1}
, this last expression is always non-positive with equality if and only if
X_{L_{1}}
is conditionally independent of
X_{S_{1}}
given
X_{e}
. This proves that (
2.6
) holds. By considering the case of equality, it is then easy to check that it implies that
X_{S_{2}}
is an edge Markov process. It concludes the proof of Lemma
1.12
.∎

We now prove Lemma
1.14
.

Proof of Lemma
1.14
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
. From (
2.5
), with the notation used in the proof of Lemma
1.12
, we have

H(S_{1})=H(o)+H(S_{1}|o)\leq H(o)+dH(E_{1}|o),

with equality if the variables
(X_{o},X_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
conditioned on
X_{o}
are independent. Using (
2.5
) again, we get

H(S_{1})\leq dH(E_{1})-(d-1)H(o).

So finally,
\Sigma_{1}(X)=H(S_{1})-\frac{d}{2}H(E_{1})\leq\frac{d}{2}H(E_{1})-(d-1)H(o)=\Sigma_{e}(X)
as requested.
∎

2.3.
Combinatorial characterization of the annealed entropy

In this subsection, we give a combinatorial interpretation of the annealed entropy
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
. Recall that
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
is the set of simple
d
-regular graphs on the vertex set
[n]
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
,
r\geq 0
integer and
\epsilon>0
, we define the set of colored graphs whose
r
-neighborhood is close to
\mu_{r}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(G,f):G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d),f\in M^{n},{\mathrm{d}}({\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\mu_{r})\leq\epsilon\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)}\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu,r,\epsilon),

where
\sqcup
is the disjoint union and
\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
was defined in (
1.2
). We then set

\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n}{{\left(\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|-\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)|\right)}}=\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|,

(2.9)

where the expectation is with respect to the random graph
G_{n}
uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
. In comparison with the definition of
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
in (
1.3
),
\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
appears as an annealed quantity in the sense that there is an average over the randomness of
G_{n}
inside the logarithm. The following theorem asserts that
\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
is close to
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
as
n
goes to infinity and
\epsilon
goes to
0
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
and
r\geq 1
integer. We have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

Proof.

One side of this identity can be found in
[
3
, Lemma 6.2]
. We will however give a proof which relies on
[
16
]
which is a generalization of
[
12
]
to colored graphs. This is interesting because it connects
[
12
,
16
]
to the entropic inequalities found in
[
2
,
3
]
. First, a classical result of Bender and Canfield
[
7
]
implies that

\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)|=\frac{d}{2}\log n-s(d)-\log(d!)+o(1),

(2.10)

where
s(d)=d/2-(d/2)\log d
. On the other hand, Proposition 5 and Proposition 6 in Delgosha and Anantharam
[
16
]
imply that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|-\frac{d}{2}\log n\right)}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|-\frac{d}{2}\log n\right)}}=J_{r}(\mu),

where
J_{r}(\mu)
has an explicit formula that we now describe (the same formula appears in
[
12
]
).

We define
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\bullet}_{r-1}
as the set of unlabeled colored rooted
(d-1)
-ary trees of depth
r-1
. An element
g=(t,t^{\prime})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\bullet}_{r-1}\times\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}^{\bullet}_{r-1}
can be seen as an unlabeled coloring of
E_{r}
rooted on the oriented edge
(o,1)
. For
g=(t,t^{\prime})
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
and
X
a coloring of
T_{d}
, we then define

N_{X}(g)
as the number of neighbors
v
of the root such that
X
restricted to
E_{r}(o,v)=B_{r-1}(\{o,v\})
is isomorphic to
g
: more precisely such that the restriction of
X
to
(d-1)
-ary tree rooted at
o
(respectively
v
) in
E_{r}(o,v)\backslash\{o,v\}
is isomorphic to
t
(respectively
t^{\prime}
). By construction

\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}N_{X}(g)=\deg(o)=d.

(2.11)

If
X
is a random coloring of
T_{d}
with
\mu
, we then define a probability measure on
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
by, for all
g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
:

\pi_{\mu}(g)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[N_{X}(g)]}{d},

where the expectation is with respect to the randomness of
X
. We have

J_{r}(\mu)=-s(d)+H(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\pi_{\mu})-\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)],

where
\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}}
is the rooted unlabeled coloring associated to
X_{S_{r}}
. As a sanity check, if
M
is a singleton, then
J_{r}(\mu)=-s(d)-\log(d!)
and we retrieve Equation (
2.10
). Moreover, in view of Equation (
2.10
), the theorem follows from the claim

J_{r}(\mu)=-s(d)-\log(d!)+H(X_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(X_{E_{r}}).

(2.12)

The expression (
2.12
) is obtained by putting random labeling on an unlabeled rooted coloring and following the effect on the Shannon entropy. We first observe that, since
X
is invariant, for any
g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(X_{E_{r}}\simeq g)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{v=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}(X_{E_{r}(o,v)}\simeq g)=\pi_{\mu}(g).

It follows that
\pi_{\mu}
is the law of
\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}}
defined as the unlabeled coloring associated to
X_{E_{r}}
rooted at the oriented edge
(o,1)
. Besides, since
X
is invariant,
X_{E_{r}}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the triple
(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}},\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
where, given
\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}}
,
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are independent and
\sigma
is a uniform random labeling of
\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}}
restricted to
E_{r}^{o}
, the
(d-1)
-ary tree rooted at
o
in
E_{r}\backslash\{o,1\}
, and similarly for
\sigma^{\prime}
. From the relative entropy identity (
2.5
), we find that

H(X_{E_{r}})=H(\pi_{\mu})+2K,

where
K
is the relative entropy of
\sigma
given
\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}^{o}}
.

Secondly, we observe that
X_{S_{r}}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the vector
Y=(X_{E^{1}_{r}},\ldots,X_{E^{d}_{r}})
where
E^{k}_{r}
is the
(d-1)
-ary tree rooted at
k
in
E_{r}\backslash\{o,k\}
. It follows that
H(Y)=H(X_{S_{r}})
. Also, if
\widetilde{Y}=(\widetilde{X}_{E^{1}_{r}},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{E^{d}_{r}})
, we find from what precedes and the invariance of
X
that

H(X_{S_{r}})=H(Y)=H(\widetilde{Y})+dK.

Finally, the difference between
\widetilde{Y}
and
\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}}
is that the neighbors of
o
are ordered (or labeled) in
\widetilde{Y}
. We deduce from Lemma
2.4
below that

H(\widetilde{Y})=H(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)]+\log(d!).

This concludes the proof of (
2.12
).
∎

In the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we have used the following elementary lemma. Recall that a vector is exchangeable if its law is invariant by any permutation of its coordinates.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
F
be a finite set and
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
a random exchangeable vector in
F^{n}
. The counting measure
N_{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
associated to
Z
satisfies

H(Z)=H(N_{Z})-\sum_{x\in F}{\mathbb{E}}[\log N_{Z}(x)!]+\log n!.

Proof.

We consider the equivalence class on
F^{n}
,
z\sim z^{\prime}
if
z
and
z^{\prime}
are equal up to a permutation of the coordinates of
z
. We have
z\sim z^{\prime}
if and only if
N_{z}=N_{z^{\prime}}
. Moreover, the number of vectors in the equivalence class of
z
is given by the multinomial formula:

\frac{n!}{\prod_{x\in F}N_{z}(x)!}.

Using the exchangeability of
Z
, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(Z=z)=\frac{\prod_{x\in F}N_{z}(x)!}{n!}\sum_{z^{\prime}\sim z}\mathbb{P}(Z=z^{\prime})=\frac{\prod_{x\in F}N_{z}(x)!}{n!}\mathbb{P}(N_{Z}=N_{z}).

It then remains to use the relative entropy formula (
2.5
).
∎

3.
Proofs of main results

3.1.
First moment method: proof of Theorem
1.5

Let
r\geq 1
,
\epsilon>0
and
G_{n}
be uniformly sampled on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
. From Markov inequality, for any real
h
,

\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h)=\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|\geq e^{nh})\leq e^{-nh}{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|.

In particular, we find

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h)\leq\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)-h.

From Theorem
2.3
, we deduce the large deviations bound

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h)\leq\Sigma_{r}(\mu)-h+\delta(\epsilon),

where
\delta(\epsilon)
goes to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. If
h>\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
, the right-hand side of the above expression is negative for all
\epsilon
small enough. We deduce in particular that for all
\epsilon
small enough,
\mathbb{P}(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq h)
converges to
0
. By Lemma
1.2
, this proves that
\overline{h}(\mu,r)<h
. It concludes the proof of Theorem
1.5
. ∎

3.2.
Second moment method: proof of Theorem
1.7

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{d}(M)
,
r\geq 1
and set
p=\mu_{r}\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
. In view of Theorem
1.5
, we should prove that

\underline{h}(\mu,r)\geq\Sigma(p).

(3.1)

For ease of notation, we write
\mathcal{F}_{G}(p,\epsilon)
in place of
\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
(since this depends of
\mu
only through
\mu_{r}=p
). The Paley-Zygmund inequality implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{{\left(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)-\frac{1}{n}\right)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}{{\left(|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|\geq e^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|\right)}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-e^{-1})^{2}\frac{{{\left({\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|\right)}}^{2}}{{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-e^{-1})^{2}\frac{\exp(2n\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon))}{{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}}.

Since
\mu_{r}=p
, we have
\Sigma(p)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
and, by Theorem
2.3
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq\Sigma(p)-\delta(\epsilon),

where
\delta(\epsilon)
goes to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
. We deduce that if we manage to prove that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}\leq 2\Sigma(p)+\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon),

(3.2)

where
\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon)
goes to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
, then we would get that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}{{\left(H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)\geq\Sigma(p)-2\delta(\epsilon)\right)}}\geq-2\delta(\epsilon)-\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon).

From Equation (
2.1
) in Theorem
2.1
, this would imply that

\underline{h}(\mu,r)\geq\Sigma(p)
as claimed in (
3.1
).

It thus remains to prove Equation (
3.2
). For concreteness, we may assume that the chosen distance
{\mathrm{d}}
generating the weak topology is the total variation distance. To that end, let
\epsilon>0
and
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-net on the set of invariant coupling
q\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M^{2})
of
p
and
p
. Given a graph
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
, consider two colorings of
G
with color set
M
whose
r
-neighborhood statistics are at most at total variation distance
\varepsilon
from
p
. The number of such pairs is
|\mathcal{F}_{G}(p,\varepsilon)|^{2}
. On the other hand, each pair is in fact a coloring of
G
on
M^{2}
. Then its
r
-neighborhood statistics is an element
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M^{2})
. Since both marginals of
q^{\prime}
are at most at total variation distance
\varepsilon
from
p
, there is a measure
q^{*}\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M^{2})
whose total variation distance is at most
2\varepsilon
from
q^{\prime}
and whose both marginals are exactly
p
(for each marginal of
q^{\prime}
, there is an invariant coupling of this marginal and
p
such that the two colorings are equal with probability
1-\epsilon
). Therefore there exists an element in the
\varepsilon
-net,
q\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}
such that the distance of
q
from the original pair of coloring is at most
3\varepsilon
. We conclude that

|\mathcal{F}_{G}(p,\varepsilon)|^{2}\leq\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{F}_{G}(q,3\varepsilon)|.

This implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)|}\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{F}_{G}(q,3\varepsilon)|=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}\exp(n\Sigma_{n}(\mu(q),r,3\epsilon)).

It follows that,

\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}\leq\max_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\Sigma_{n}(\mu(q),r,3\epsilon)+\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}|.

By Theorem
2.3
, we deduce that, for some function
\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon)
going to
0
with
\epsilon\to 0
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log{\mathbb{E}}|\mathcal{F}_{G_{n}}(p,\epsilon)|^{2}\leq\max_{q\in\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}}\Sigma(q)+\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon).

By assumption for any
q\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M^{2})
,
\Sigma(q)\leq 2\Sigma(p)
. We thus have proved that (
3.2
) holds. ∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.11

In view of Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.7
, it remains to prove that for any

,

\underline{h}(\mu(p),t)\geq\Sigma_{r}(\mu(p))=\Sigma(p).

Let

be an invariant coupling of

and
(\mu(p))_{t}
. Then

and

Application to factor graphs: proofs of Lemmas

and Lemma

We start with the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
1.16
.

By construction, we have

Z_{G}=\sum_{f\in M^{n}}\prod_{v=1}^{n}e^{\psi((G,f,v)_{r})}=\sum_{f\in M^{n}}e^{n\langle{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)_{r},\psi\rangle}.

Let
\epsilon>0
and
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
be an
\varepsilon
-net of
\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)
. The function
p\to\langle p,\psi\rangle
being uniformly continuous (since
M
is finite), there exists a function
\delta(\epsilon)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
such that for any probability measure, say
q
, on rooted colored graphs of radius
r
, if
{\mathrm{d}}(q,p)\leq\epsilon
then
|\langle q,\psi\rangle-\langle p,\psi\rangle|\leq\delta(\epsilon)
. If
N_{G}
is the number of colorings such that
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G}(f)
is at distance larger than
\epsilon
from
\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}
, it follows that

\displaystyle Z_{G}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu(p),r,\epsilon)|e^{n\langle p,\psi\rangle+n\delta(\epsilon)}+N_{G}e^{n\|\psi\|_{\infty}},

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}|\max_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}e^{n(H_{G}(\mu(p),r,\epsilon)+\langle p,\psi\rangle+\delta(\epsilon))}+N_{G}e^{n\|\psi\|_{\infty}}.

Now, if
G_{n}
is uniformly sampled on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d)
, then, for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,
\mathbb{P}(N_{G_{n}}=0)
converges to

(since
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}
converges in probability to a Dirac mass at
(T_{d},o)
). Using (

) and taking the limit in

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\frac{1}{n}\ln Z_{G_{n}}\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}{{\left(\bar{h}(p)+\langle p,\psi\rangle+\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon)\right)}}

with
\delta^{\prime}(\epsilon)\to 0
as

. This gives the upper bound in Lemma

.

For the lower bound, we write similarly,

\displaystyle Z_{G}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}|\mathcal{F}_{G}(\mu(p),r,\epsilon)|e^{n\langle p,\psi\rangle-n\delta(\epsilon)},

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{N}_{\varepsilon}}e^{n(H_{G}(\mu(p),r,\epsilon)+\langle p,\psi\rangle-\delta(\epsilon))}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}e^{n(H_{G}(\mu(p),r,\epsilon)+\langle p,\psi\rangle-2\delta(\epsilon))}.

The conclusion follows easily. ∎

Lemma

is a corollary of Lemma

.

Proof of Lemma
1.17
.

For
\beta>0
, let

Z_{G}(\beta)=\sum_{f\in M^{n}}\prod_{v=1}^{n}e^{n\beta\psi((G,f,v)_{r})}.

By construction, we have

|M|^{-n}Z_{G}(\beta)\leq e^{\beta L_{G}}\leq Z_{G}(\beta).

By Lemma

, we find

\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}{{\left(\frac{\underline{h}(p)}{\beta}+\langle p,\psi\rangle-\frac{\ln|M|}{\beta}\right)}}\leq\liminf_{n}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\limsup_{n}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M)}{{\left(\frac{\bar{h}(p)}{\beta}+\langle p,\psi\rangle\right)}}.

We recall that

. We get

\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M):\underline{h}(p)\geq 0}{{\left(\langle p,\psi\rangle-\frac{\ln|M|}{\beta}\right)}}\leq\liminf_{n}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\limsup_{n}\frac{{\mathbb{E}}L_{G_{n}}}{n}\leq\sup_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{d,r}(M):\underline{h}(p)\geq 0}{{\left(\frac{\ln|M|}{\beta}+\langle p,\psi\rangle\right)}}.

∎

4.
Extension to processes on unimodular Galton-Watson trees

4.1.
An extended setting

We now discuss an extension to processes on random trees. We will focus our attention on
unimodular Galton-Watson trees
. In this section, we fix a probability measure
\pi
on integers with positive and finite expectation:

d=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k\pi(k)>0.

We define
\hat{\nu}
, the size-biased version of
\nu
as the probability measure defined by: for all integers
k\geq 0

\hat{\pi}(k)=\frac{(k+1)\pi(k+1)}{d}.

Then, the unimodular Galton-Watson tree with degree distribution
\pi
, is the Galton-Watson tree whose vertex set is a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{f}
defined in (
1.1
) such that the root
o
has a number of offsprings
N_{o}
with distribution
\pi
indexed by
1,\ldots,N_{o}
and all other vertices
v
have an independent number of offsprings
N_{v}
with distribution
\hat{\pi}
indexed by
(v,1),\ldots,(v,N_{v})
. We will denote by
T
a realization of this random tree and
{\mathrm{UGW}}(\pi)
the law of the rooted tree
(T,o)
. We note that
(T,o)
is randomly labeled in the sense defined in Subsection
1.1
.

For example, if
\pi
is a Dirac mass at
d
then
T
is the
d
-regular tree. If
\pi
is a Poisson random variable with mean
d
, then
\hat{\pi}=\pi
and
T
is a standard Galton-Watson tree with Poisson offspring distribution.

As its name suggests, the random rooted tree
T
is unimodular. Recall that a random rooted graph
(G,o)
is unimodular if for all non-negative functions
f
on the set of doubly rooted graphs (a connected graph with two ordered distinguished vertices) which are invariant by isomorphisms, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{v\in V}f(G,o,v)={\mathbb{E}}\sum_{v\in V}f(G,v,o).

(4.1)

where
V
is the vertex set of
G
and the expectation is with respect to the randomness of
(G,o)
.

If
M
is a finite set, an invariant process
X
on
T
is defined as a random colored tree
(T,X)
such that
(T,X,o)
is unimodular (that is, it satisfies (
4.1
) with
G=(T,X)
and
f
defined on the set of doubly rooted colored graphs which are invariant by isomorphisms). We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
the set of laws of
(T,X)
with
X
invariant colorings of
T
on the color set
M
.

Now, in order to define a relevant notion of sofic entropy, we need to choose the ensemble of finite graphs
G_{n}
such that
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}
converges to
{\mathrm{UGW}}(\pi)
. A natural choice is the family of uniform random graphs with a given degree sequence. Let
d_{n}=(d_{n}(1),\ldots,d_{n}(n))
be a sequence of integers, indexed by a subset of
\mathbb{N}
, whose sum is even and such that

{\mathrm{distr}}_{d_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\delta_{d_{n}(v)}

converges weakly to
\pi
. For technical simplicity, we assume that the degree sequence is uniformly bounded:
for some real
\Delta
,

\sup_{n}\max_{1\leq v\leq n}d_{n}(v)\leq\Delta.

(4.2)

Note in particular that (
4.2
) implies that the support of
\pi
is contained in
\{0,\ldots,\Delta\}
. From Erdős-Gallai Theorem
[
19
]
, for all
n
large enough, the set
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d_{n})
of simple graphs with vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that for all
v\in[n]
,
v
has degree
d_{n}(v)
is not empty. Under these conditions, if
G_{n}
is uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d_{n})
then almost surely
{\mathrm{distr}}_{G_{n}}
converges to
{\mathrm{UGW}}(\pi)
, see for example
[
30
]
. In the statements below, we will not repeat the above assumptions on the sequence
(d_{n})
.

For a given probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
(that is,
\mu
is the law of an invariant coloring
(T,X)
), we can now reproduce the definition of weakly and typical processes and define the sofic entropy by taking limits of
H_{G_{n}}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
defined in (
1.3
). We do not repeat the definitions since there are identical except that
G_{n}
is now a random graph uniformly distributed on
\mathcal{G}_{n}(d_{n})
. For integer
r\geq 1
and
0<\alpha<1
, we define the quantity
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
exactly as done below (
1.3
). Lemma
1.2
continues to holds in this more general setting.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
and
r\geq 0
. The function
\alpha\mapsto(\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha),\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha))
is constant on
(0,1)
.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
2.1
works verbatim under the assumption (
4.2
).
∎

We define
\bar{h}(\mu,r)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r)
as the common value of
\bar{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r,\alpha)
. The upper and lower sofic entropies
\bar{h}(\mu)
and
\underline{h}(\mu)
are the limits in
r
of
\bar{h}(\mu,r)
and
\underline{h}(\mu,r)
. Exactly as in Lemma
1.3
, as a corollary of Lemma
4.1
, we obtain the following claim.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
. We have
\bar{h}(\mu)\geq 0
(resp.
\underline{h}(\mu)\geq 0
) if and only if
\mu
is weakly (resp. strongly) typical.

4.2.
Annealed entropy

In this broader setting, the annealed entropy is defined as follows. Let
(T,o)
be a randomly labeled rooted unimodular tree and
X
an invariant coloring of
T
with
(T,X)
having law
\mu
. The degree of a vertex
v
of
T
is denoted by
\deg(v)
. We assume that
d={\mathbb{E}}\deg(o)>0
. As above, if
r\geq 0
is an integer and
S
is a subset of the vertices of
T
,
B_{r}(S)
is the subset of vertices of
T
at distance at most
r
from
S
. For
r\geq 1
, we set
S_{r}=B_{r}(o)
and, if
\deg(o)\geq 1
, we set
E_{r}=B_{r-1}(\{o,1\})
(since
T
is randomly labeled, the neighbors of the root are indexed by
(1,\ldots,\deg(o))
).

We denote by
X_{S_{r}}
the colored tree
(T,X)
restricted to
S_{r}
: by construction,
X_{S_{r}}
has law
\mu_{r}
. We also need to define the law of
X
restricted to
E_{r}
but this requires a biasing of the tree
T
. This is done as follows. A (directed) edge-rooted graph is defined as a pair
(G,\rho)
formed by a connected graph
G
and a distinguished directed edge
\rho=(u,v)
(that is,
\{u,v\}
is an edge of the graph).
Now, we denote by
\vec{\mu}
the law on colored edge-rooted trees defined by:

\vec{\mu}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{d}{\mathbb{E}}{{\left[\deg(o){\mathbf{1}}((T,X,(o,1))\in\cdot\right]}},

(4.3)

where
(T,X)
has law
\mu
and
d={\mathbb{E}}\deg(o)
. Note that under the probability measure
\vec{\mu}
,
o
has at least degree
1
and thus
\{o,1\}
is an edge of the tree. We denote by
(\vec{T},\vec{X},\rho)
, with
\rho=(o,1)
a random variable with law
\vec{\mu}
. It is easy to check that Equation (
4.1
) implies that
\vec{\mu}
is invariant by switching the two sides of the oriented edge. Moreover, if
T
has law
{\mathrm{UGW}}(\pi)
then
\vec{T}
is given by two independent Galton-Watson trees with offspring distribution
\hat{\pi}
whose roots are connected by the root-edge, see
[
1
, Example 1.1]
. In particular, if
\pi
is a Dirac mass at
d
, then
T=\vec{T}
.

We denote by
\vec{X}_{E_{r}}
the colored tree
(\vec{T},\vec{X})
restricted to
E_{r}
. The law of
\vec{X}_{E_{r}}
is
\vec{\mu}_{r}
, the restriction of
\vec{\mu}
to
E_{r}
. We observe that
\vec{\mu}_{r}
depends on
\mu
only through its marginal
\mu_{r}
.

Now, if
X
is an invariant coloring of
T
with law
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
and
r\geq 1
is an integer, we set

\Sigma_{r}(X)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu)=H(X_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{r}})-H(\pi).

(4.4)

See Remark
4.5
for an alternative expression which is arguably more natural. Thanks to assumption (
4.2
) it is immediate that the above entropies are finite as soon as
M
is finite. Note also that
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
depends on
\mu
only through
\mu_{r}
. We will check in Lemma
4.10
below that
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
is non-increasing in
r
. We may thus define

\Sigma(\mu)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

The quantities
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
and
\Sigma(\mu)
are the annealed entropies of
\mu_{r}
and
\mu
. The following lemma generalizes Lemma
4.3
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For any
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
and integer
r\geq 1
, we have

\bar{h}(\mu,r)\leq\Sigma_{r}(\mu)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\bar{h}(\mu)\leq\Sigma(\mu).

There is also an analog of Theorem
1.7
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
. For any integer
r\geq 1
, if all invariant couplings
\nu
of
\mu
and
\mu
, we have
\Sigma_{r}(\nu)\leq 2\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
then
\underline{h}(\mu,r)=\bar{h}(\mu,r)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

In particular, if the above condition holds for an increasing sequence of integers
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
then

\underline{h}(\mu)=\bar{h}(\mu)=\Sigma(\mu).

Remark 4.5
.

The annealed entropy is also given by the formula:

\Sigma_{r}(X)=H(X_{S_{r}}|T_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{r}}|\vec{T}_{E_{r}}),

where
H(X|Y)=H(X,Y)-H(Y)
is the relative entropy. Indeed
\vec{T}
is the union of two independent copies of
T^{\prime}
, a Galton-Watson tree with offspring distribution
\hat{\pi}
, while
T
is the union of
N
independent copies of
T^{\prime}
and with
N
independent with distribution
\pi
. It follows that,
H(\vec{T}_{E_{r}})=2H(T^{\prime}_{S_{r-1}})
and
H(T_{S_{r}})=H(N)+dH(T^{\prime}_{S_{r-1}})
(from (
2.5
)). In particular,
H(T_{S_{r}})-(d/2)H(\vec{T}_{E_{r}})=H(N)=H(\pi)
.

4.3.
Markov processes

There is an extension of Theorem
1.11
and Theorem
1.15
in our extended setting. The previous definitions of Markov processes carry over when conditioned on the random tree. More precisely, we use the following definitions.

Definition 4.6

(Markov process)
.

Let
(T,X)
be a random coloring of a random tree
T
on a finite set
M
with law
\mu
. For integer
r\geq 1
,
X
or
\mu
is
r
-Markov if conditioned on
T
on
B_{r-1}(e)
and on the value at
B_{r-1}(e)
, the
(r-1)
-neighborhood of an edge
e
, the processes on the left and right subtrees of
e
are independent. Similarly,
X
or
\mu
is vertex-Markov if conditioned on
T
and on the value at a vertex, the processes on the pending subtrees of that vertex are independent.

For integer
r\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{I}_{\pi,r}(M)
denote the set of laws
\mu
of coloring
(T^{\prime},X^{\prime})
on
M
which are randomly labeled, such that
T^{\prime}
has law
{\mathrm{UGW}}(\pi)_{r}
(the law of the restriction of
T
to
S_{r}
) and such that
\vec{\mu}
as defined above is invariant by switching the two sides of the oriented edge. If
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
then
\mu_{r}\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
. Conversely, we have the following (see
[
12
, Proposition 1.1]
):

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
r\geq 1
integer and
p\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi,r}(M)
. Then there is a unique
r
-Markov process
\mu(p)\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
such that the marginal of
\mu(p)
on
S_{r}
is equal to
\mu
.

If
p\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi,r}(M)
, we define
\Sigma(p)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu(p))
as in Equation (
4.4
). The following theorem is an extension of Theorem
1.11
.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
r\geq 1
be an integer and
p\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi,r}(M)
. If for all couplings
q\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi,r}(M^{2})
of
p
and
p
, we have
\Sigma(q)\leq 2\Sigma(p)
then
\underline{h}(\mu(p))=\bar{h}(\mu(p))=\Sigma(p)
and
\mu(p)
is strongly typical.

There is a version of this theorem for vertex-Markov processes. As above, let
\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
denote the set of probability measures on
M^{E_{1}}
that are invariant by switching the two sides of the edge
\{o,1\}
. If
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\mu}(M)
then the restriction of
\vec{\mu}
to
E_{1}
is in
\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
. Conversely, if
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
, there exists a unique vertex-Markov process
\mu(p)
in
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
such that
\vec{\mu}
restricted to
E_{1}
is in
\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
. If
(T,X)\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
, we define

\Sigma_{e}(X)=\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{1}})-dH(\vec{X}_{o})+H(X_{o})-H(\pi).

If
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
, we set
\Sigma(p)=\Sigma_{e}(\mu(p))
. The following theorem is an extension of Theorem
1.15
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M)
. If for all couplings
q\in\mathcal{I}_{e}(M^{2})
of
p
and
p
, we have
\Sigma(q)\leq 2\Sigma(p)
then
\underline{h}(\mu(p))=\bar{h}(\mu(p))=\Sigma(p)
and
\mu(p)
is strongly typical.

In the remainder of the paper, we explain the proofs of Theorem
4.3
, Theorem
4.4
, Theorem
4.8
and Theorem
4.9
. The proofs are entirely similar to the proof of the corresponding results for invariant processes on
T_{d}
. We will only sketch the proof and explain the differences.

4.4.
Maximizers of the annealed entropy

The following lemma is the exact analog of Lemma
1.12
and Lemma
1.14
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
X\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
and
r\geq 1
. We have

\Sigma_{r+1}(X)\leq\Sigma_{r}(X),

with equality if and only if
X_{S_{r+1}}
is a
r
-Markov process on
S_{r+1}
. Moreover,

\Sigma_{1}(X)\leq\Sigma_{e}(X),

with equality if and only if
X_{S_{1}}
is a vertex-Markov process on
S_{1}
.

Proof.

We start by the first statement. Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
4.10
, it is enough to check the inequality for
r=1
. The inequality
\Sigma_{2}(X)\leq\Sigma_{1}(X)
is equivalent to

H(X_{S_{2}})-H(X_{S_{1}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{2}})+\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{1}})\leq 0.

(4.5)

Let
\deg_{T}(o)
and
\deg_{\vec{T}}(o)
be the degrees of the root in
T
and
\vec{T}
. For integer
k\geq 1
, from (
4.3
), we have, for any event
A
,

\mathbb{P}((\vec{X},\vec{T})\in A,\deg_{\vec{T}}(o)=k)=\frac{k}{d}\mathbb{P}((X,T)\in A,\deg_{T}(o)=k).

It follows that
\mathbb{P}(\deg_{\vec{T}}(o)=k)=k\pi(k)/d
and if
k\geq 1
is in the support of
\pi
,

\mathbb{P}((\vec{X},\vec{T})\in A|\deg_{\vec{T}}(o)=k)=\mathbb{P}((X,T)\in A|\deg_{T}(o)=k).

(4.6)

In other words,
(X,T)
and
(\vec{X},\vec{T})
have the same law when conditioned on the root degree is
k\geq 1
.
For a set
S
of vertices, let us denote by by
H_{k}(S)
the entropy of the variable
(T,X)
restricted to
S
and conditioned on the event
\deg_{T}(o)=k
. Similarly, we set
H_{k}(S|S^{\prime})=H_{k}(S,S^{\prime})-H_{k}(S^{\prime})
is the associated relative entropy.

From (
2.5
), we may write, for
t=1,2
,

H(X_{S_{t}})=H(\deg_{T}(o))+\pi(0)H_{0}(X_{o})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k)H_{k}(S_{t}).

and

H(\vec{X}_{E_{t}})=H(\deg_{\vec{T}}(o))+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k\pi(k)}{d}H_{k}(E_{t}).

Hence, (
4.5
) is equivalent to the claim:

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(S_{2})-H_{k}(S_{1})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(E_{2})+\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(E_{1})\right)}}\leq 0.

(4.7)

On the event
\deg_{T}(o)=k
, for
1\leq i\leq k
, let
L_{i}=\{(i,1),\ldots,(i,n_{i})\}
be the offspring of vertex
i
. Note that the random variables
X_{L_{i}}
conditioned on the event
\deg_{T}(o)=k
are exchangeable.
Then, the computation from (
2.6
) to (
2.8
) gives

H_{k}(S_{2})-H_{k}(S_{1})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(E_{2})+\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(E_{1})\leq\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(L_{1}|S_{1})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(L|E_{1})

The left-hand side of (
4.7
) is thus upper bounded by

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{2}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(L_{1}|S_{1})-H_{k}(L|e)\right)}}=\frac{d}{2}{{\left(H(\vec{X}_{L_{1}}|\vec{X}_{S_{1}})-H(\vec{X}_{L}|\vec{X}_{E_{1}})\right)}}.

We now use the invariance of
\vec{\mu}
by switching the two sides of the edge
E_{1}=\{o,1\}
. We get
H(\vec{X}_{L}|\vec{X}_{E_{1}})=H(\vec{X}_{L_{1}}|\vec{X}_{E_{1}})
. Finally, since
E_{1}\subset S_{1}
, we deduce that the inequalities (
4.5
)-(
4.7
) hold. As in the proof of Lemma
1.12
, the case of equality is a directed consequence of the case of equality in (
2.5
).

We now prove the second statement of Lemma
4.10
,
\Sigma_{1}(X)\leq\Sigma_{e}(X)
. It is equivalent to prove that

H(X_{S_{1}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{1}})\leq\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{1}})-dH(\vec{X}_{o})+H(X_{o}).

Arguing as above, we find that this is equivalent to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(S_{1})-kH_{k}(E_{1})-(k-1)H_{k}(o)\right)}}\leq 0.

As in the proof of Lemma
1.14
, it remains to use that
H_{k}(S_{1})\leq H(o)+kH_{k}(E_{1}|o)
and
H_{k}(E_{1}|o)=H_{k}(E_{1})-H_{k}(o)
. In the case of equality, this implies the conditional independence of
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
given
X_{o}
and root degree equal to
k
.
∎

4.5.
Combinatorial characterization of the annealed entropy

In our extended setting, the combinatorial interpretation of the annealed entropy
\Sigma_{r}(\mu)
explained in Subsection
2.3
continues to hold. The definition of
\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)
in (
2.9
) remains unchanged. The following theorem is an extension of Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(M)
and
r\geq 1
integer. We have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Sigma_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)=\Sigma_{r}(\mu).

Proof.

Let
s(d)=d/2-(d/2)\log d
. From
[
10
, Theorem 2.16]
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(d_{n})|-\frac{d}{2}\log n\right)}}=-s(d)+H(\deg(o))-{\mathbb{E}}[\log(\deg(o)!)],

where
\deg(o)
has law
\pi
. Also, from
[
12
,
16
]
, we have

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|-\frac{d}{2}\log n\right)}}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{n\to\infty}{{\left(\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{G}_{n}(\mu,r,\epsilon)|-\frac{d}{2}\log n\right)}}=J_{r}(\mu),

where
J_{r}(\mu)=-s(d)+H(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\pi_{\mu})-\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)]
is defined exactly as in Theorem
2.3
, the only difference being that (
2.11
) is replaced by

\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}N_{X}(g)=\deg(o).

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we have
H(\pi_{\mu})=H(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})
where
\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}}
is the unlabeled coloring associated to
\vec{X}_{E_{r}}
. Hence, the theorem follows by checking that

H(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})=H(X_{S_{r}})-\frac{d}{2}H(\vec{X}_{E_{r}})+\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)]-{\mathbb{E}}[\log(\deg(o)!)].

(4.8)

In order to prove that (
4.8
) holds, we decompose the left-hand side of the possible values of the root-degree. We write

H(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})=H(\pi)+H_{0}(X_{o})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k)H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}}),

where
H_{k}
is the entropy conditioned on
\deg_{T}(o)=k
. Similarly,

\frac{d}{2}H(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})=\frac{d}{2}H(\hat{\pi})+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k}{2}\pi(k)H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}}).

Let
{\mathbb{E}}_{k}[\cdot]
is the expectation conditioned on
\deg_{T}(o)=k
. From (
4.6
), it follows that the identity (
4.8
) is equivalent to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})\right)}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(X_{S_{r}})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(X_{E_{r}})+\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}_{k}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)]-\log(k!)\right)}}.

(4.9)

We denote by
E_{r}^{o}
, the tree rooted at
o
in
E_{r}\backslash\{o,1\}
and by
E_{r}^{1}
the tree rooted at
1
. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we find

H_{k}(X_{E_{r}})=H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})+K_{k}+K^{\prime}_{k},

where
K_{k}
is the relative entropy of a random labeling
\sigma
of
\widetilde{X}_{E^{o}_{r}}
conditioned on
\deg(o)=k
and
K^{\prime}_{k}
is the relative entropy of a random labeling
\sigma^{\prime}
of
\widetilde{X}_{E^{1}_{r}}
conditioned on
\deg(o)=k
. Similarly, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we get

H_{k}(X_{S_{r}})=H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})+kK^{\prime}_{k}-\sum_{g\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{r}}{\mathbb{E}}_{k}[\log(N_{X}(g)!)]+\log(k!).

We deduce that the right-hand side of (
4.9
) is equal to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{S_{r}})-\frac{k}{2}H_{k}(\widetilde{X}_{E_{r}})\right)}}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(\frac{k}{2}K_{k}^{\prime}-\frac{k}{2}K_{k}\right)}}.

Finally, we observe that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\pi(k){{\left(\frac{k}{2}K_{k}^{\prime}-\frac{k}{2}K_{k}\right)}}=\frac{d}{2}{{\left(H(\sigma)-H(\sigma^{\prime})\right)}}.

The above expression is equal to
0
because the law
\vec{\mu}
is invariant by switching the two sides of the edge
\{o,1\}
. This concludes the proof of Equation (
4.8
).
∎
