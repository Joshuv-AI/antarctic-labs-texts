Water shielding in the terrestrial planet-forming zone: Implication for inner disk organics Journal: ApJL Astropy ( Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013 ; Astropy Collaboration et al. 2018 ) , SciPy ( Virtanen et al. 2020 ) , NumPy ( Van Der Walt et al. 2011 ) , Matplotlib ( Hunter 2007 ) .
Sara E. Duval, Arthur D. Bosman, Edwin A. Bergin

Abstract
The chemical composition of the inner region of protoplanetary disks can trace the composition of planetary building material. The exact elemental composition of the inner disk has not yet been measured and tensions between models and observations still exist. Recent advancements have shown UV-shielding to be able to increase emission of organics. Here, we expand on these models and investigate how UV-shielding may impact chemical composition in the inner 5 au. In this work, we use the model from Bosman et al. 2022a and expand it with a larger chemical network. We focus on the chemical abundances in the upper disk atmosphere where the effects of water UV-shielding are most prominent and molecular lines originate. We find rich carbon and nitrogen chemistry with enhanced abundances of C 2 ​ H 2 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} , CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} , HCN, CH 3 ​ CN \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN} , and NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} by > > 3 orders of magnitude. This is caused by the self-shielding of H 2 ​ O \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O} , which locks oxygen in water. This subsequently results in a suppression of oxygen-containing species like CO and CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} . The increase in C 2 ​ H 2 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} seen in the model with the inclusion of water UV-shielding allows us to explain the observed C 2 ​ H 2 \text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} abundance without resorting to elevated C/O ratios as water UV-shielding induced an effectively oxygen-poor environment in oxygen-rich gas. Thus, water UV-shielding is important for reproducing the observed abundances of hydrocarbons and nitriles. From our model result, species like CH 4 \text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} , NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} , and NO are expected to be observable with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).



I
Introduction

Within protoplanetary disks, the inner 2 to 3 au is a critical location in which much of the process of planet formation is believed to occur, referred to as the planet-forming zone
(
Pierrehumbert & Gaidos 2011
;
Morbidelli et al. 2012
;
Raymond et al. 2014
;
Morbidelli & Raymond 2016
, e.g.)
. Most directly, this region corresponds to radii at which terrestrial planets are formed
(
Mulders et al. 2015
;
Madhusudhan et al. 2021
)
. Observations have shown that many stars are expected to have a planet within 1 au
(
Johnson et al. 2010
;
Mulders et al. 2018
)
. The inner 1 au is inside or contains the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
iceline and water plays a large role in the evolution of life
(
Brown et al. 2013
;
Cockell et al. 2016
;
Lingam & Loeb 2019
, e.g.)
. Furthermore, inside the water iceline the elemental C/O ratio is predicted to be
\sim
stellar, which becomes inherited by giant planets
(
Öberg et al. 2011
;
Ida et al. 2019
;
Öberg et al. 2021
)
. Thus, the location of formation impacts chemical composition
(
Lahuis et al. 2006
;
Öberg et al. 2011
;
Pontoppidan et al. 2011
;
Walsh et al. 2012
;
Madhusudhan 2019
, e.g.)
.

The inner disk is dust-rich leading to high optical depths. This makes it difficult to determine chemical content of the disk midplane. However, the radiation from the star, which is in close proximity, leads to a heated disk surface (i.e. T
gas

>
T
dust
), which produces a rich spectrum of emission from volatile molecules, particularly at infrared wavelengths
(
Carr & Najita 2008
;
Brown et al. 2013
, e.g.)
. Water vibrational lines at 3
\mu m
, HCN vibrational at 3.3
\mu m
, and CO vibrational at 4.7
\mu m
have been observed with both Keck Near Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSPEC) and VLT-CRIRES
(
Salyk et al. 2011a
;
Brown et al. 2013
;
Mandell et al. 2013
;
Carr et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. Further lines of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
, OH, HCN,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
have been detected by the Spitzer Space Telescope Infrared Spectrograph (IRS) which ranges from 10-37
\mu

(
Carr & Najita 2008
;
Salyk et al. 2008
;
Salyk et al. 2011b
;
Pontoppidan et al. 2010
, e.g.)
. All these lines are thought to originate in the inner 2 to 3 au, which allows us to trace the gas composition These observations show that a diverse chemistry is present in the inner planet-forming zone.

Najita et al. 2013
and
Najita et al. 2018
investigate the ratio of HCN/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
line flux and find it is related to disk dust mass. They argue that this relation is due to the formation of planetesimals which decouple from the dust and lock up water in distant regions. This effectively increases the C/O ratio in the inner disk directly impacting the HCN/
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
line flux.
Banzatti et al. 2020
explores the same dataset and find that the strongest relation is an anti-correlation between L
{}_{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}}
and R
dust
. Instead of an elevated C/O, they propose that the inner disk is fed by drifting pebbles, where large disks are a sign of little drift and small disks are a sign of substantial drift. Since these pebbles are water-ice rich, a high drift rate will enhance the inner disk oxygen content when the water ice sublimates. To distinguish these scenarios we need to understand the C/O ratio of inner disk gas.

One way to determine the inner disk chemical content and C/O ratio is to use detailed thermo-chemical models. Models such as Dust and LInes (DALI)
(
Bruderer et al. 2012
;
Bruderer 2013
)
, RAC2D
(
Du & Bergin 2014
)
, and Protoplanetary Disk Model (ProDiMo)
(
Woitke et al. 2009
)
, solve for both the gas thermal physics and the chemical equilibrium, given stellar parameters, the dust properties and mass distribution, and an overall gas-to-dust mass ratio.
Based on these models,
Woitke et al. 2018
and
Anderson et al. 2021
find that altering the C/O ratio changes the predicted emission of molecules arising from inner disk gas. In their models, they have matched emission from multiple organics, but underproduced
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
unless an elevated C/O ratio is invoked.

Bethell & Bergin 2009
showed that strong formation rates of water vapor in hot (
>
400 K) surface gas can compete with ultraviolet (UV) photodestruction, allowing water to self-shield. Since water has a broad UV photoabsorption cross-section
(
Yoshino et al. 1996
)
, this can also shield other molecules downstream, a process called water UV-shielding.
Bosman et al. 2022a
(hereafter, Paper I),
Calahan et al. 2022
(Paper II), and
Bosman et al. 2022b
(Paper III) have shown that water UV-shielding lowers the UV flux deeper into the disk and is important in understanding H
2
O, H
{}_{2}^{18}
O, and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission.

Additional species beyond
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
such as:
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, CO,
CI
,
HI
and
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are also abundant in the surface layers to potentially impact the UV field.
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, CO, atomic carbon, and
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
all absorb wavelengths less than 110 nm, while most other species dissociate at wavelengths
>
110 nm and most of the UV photons are also in this wavelength range
(
Herczeg et al. 2004
;
Heays et al. 2017
, e.g.)
. Thus, while the UV attenuation of these species greatly impacts each other, they do not greatly impact the dissociation of other species, in contrast to
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
. Shielding by atomic H, specifically scattering of Ly-
\alpha
does not seem to have a big impact of the chemistry of the inner disk (Paper II).

This naturally raises the question as to whether water UV-shielding affects the rest of the chemistry in the inner disk.
This is what we investigate in this paper: the impact of water UV-shielding on chemistry in the inner disk, with the goal of reconciling current models with observations and making predictions for observations with the James Webb Space Telescope.

II.1
Model setup

We use the DALI models from Paper I. These models include modification from standard DALI
(
Bruderer et al. 2012
;
Bruderer 2013
)
to better represent the inner disk regions. These include more efficient
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation at high temperature, more efficient heating following photo-dissociation
(
Glassgold & Najita 2015
, following)
and water UV-shielding
(
Bethell & Bergin 2009
, Paper I)
. The models have an AS 209 like input spectrum with most of the UV in Ly-
\alpha
taken from
Zhang et al. 2021
. Model setup details are in Paper I and model parameters are reiterated in Table
1
. For this Letter we will only focus on one of the four structures discussed in Paper I, the flat (
h_{c}
= 0.08) model with a gas-to-dust ratio of
10^{5}
as this model is best able to reproduce both the water and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission (Paper III). The elemental abundances assumed in the chemistry are found in Table
1
and are based on
Jonkheid et al. 2006
, with reduced Mg, Si, S and Fe. Finally, as the chemical time-scale in the region of interest are short, we solve for statistical equilibrium
(
Anderson et al. 2021
)
.

The DALI models use a simplified chemical network, which is sufficient for species such as
CO
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. However, it does not include the reactions for realistic abundances of the organics, including
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
HCN
, which have been commonly observed with
Spitzer
-IRS
(
Carr & Najita 2008
;
Pontoppidan et al. 2010
)
. To correct this, we use an expanded chemical network. This network is based on the network from
Walsh et al. 2015
and includes modifications as denoted in
Bosman et al. 2021
. Furthermore, we made sure that the adaptations made to the simplified network, such as the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation reactions, 3-body reaction, and the collisional dissociation reactions are correctly incorporated into the bigger chemical network.
This chemical network is then used to calculate the chemical composition with the gas-temperature and UV field from the standard DALI model. We adopt the
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
abundances from the full chemical calculation to calculate the shielding of UV photons for the chemistry, but we do not update the gas temperature. More details on the model setup can be seen in Appendix
A
. Figure
4
contains information about the disk structure for our UV-shielding model. In Fig.
5
, we show that the water abundances between the simplified and full network are very similar in the surface layers where water UV shielding is most important.

II.2
Determination of emitting layer

In Paper I, hot (
>
300–400 K) water vapor is found in high abundance in a thin surface layer that is radially confined within 1 au. In this region, the water vapor column exceeds 10
20
cm
-2
and with a gas-to-dust ratio of 10
5
, dust UV absorption is negligible compared to water UV shielding. Thus, including water shielding alters the UV transfer in the surface of the disk which should lead to large differences in our models.
In the disk surface, UV photons heat the gas, creating T
gas

>
T
dust
, a condition necessary for the emission of molecular lines. Inclusion of UV shielding thus is expected to lower the gas temperature and decrease emission deeper in the disk. This provides a boundary from below which we do not expect emission. In Papers I - III, we found that the emission comes from gas of T
gas

\gtrapprox
300-400 K and a hydrogen nuclei column of
\ \lessapprox
10
24
cm
-2
. This paper does not explore the radiation transfer, so we consider the top 10
24
cm
-2
as our “infrared (IR) emitting layer”, corresponding to a z/r
\gtrsim
0.15, and focus our analysis on the chemical composition of this layer.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Carbon

In Fig.
1
, the CO abundance is reduced within the
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
iceline when water UV-shielding is included. The self-shielding of water lowers the abundance of water photo-products, such as atomic oxygen and OH, in the gas. These species are critical in the formation of CO, so its formation is slowed. This effect combined with dissociation reactions with He
+
in the upper atmosphere as discussed in Section
III
reduces the abundance of CO. Thus, more carbon is available for the formation of other species.

Similarly to CO, we can also see that the abundance of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is reduced when water self-shielding is included due to the atomic oxygen and OH-poor environment
(
Bosman et al. 2022b
)
; both are crucial to its formation. In contrast, we see increases in abundance for HCN,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
.

Figure
3
shows that at 0.3 au, most of the carbon is incorporated into long carbon chains, such as
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
for the UV-shielding model with abundances of
3\times 10^{-6}
(relative to total H). This result is similar to
Wei et al. 2019
, which explores releases of carbon from refractory carbon-rich grains. They find the efficient creation of large carbon chain species if carbon grain destruction is included. Though there are differences in the origin of the carbon chains between
Wei et al. 2019
and our models, the similar end results suggests that inner disk chemistry drives large carbon chain production whenever there is little atomic oxygen available.

Past models by
Woitke et al. 2018
and
Anderson et al. 2021
have found low abundances and fluxes of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. In their models, matching observation and theory requires a high C/O ratio, thus creating an inability to reproduce
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
observations. Figure
1
shows that the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
abundance is elevated in an
effectively
oxygen-poor environment, as created by UV-shielding in gas that is oxygen-rich (i.e. stellar O/H in content). As T
{}_{gas}>
T
dust
in this region, this should lead to a strong increase in 13.7
\mu
m
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emission. At 0.3 au, our models produce a
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
column density of
8.4\times 10^{17}
cm
-2
in the region of the disk in the IR emitting layer. The models of
Anderson et al. 2021
have found the column density of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to be in the range of
10^{14}-10^{16}
cm
-2
, and
Woitke et al. 2018
found it to be equal to
10^{17}
cm
-2
for a C/O ratio of 0.46. It should be noted that most of this column is at a low gas temperature of 230K and thus only weakly contributes to any line emission. Our models thus produce a larger amount of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in higher regions of the disk without invoking a elevated C/O ratio.

Comparing to the observations of
Salyk et al. 2011b
, they find best fit columns on the order of
10^{14}-10^{15}
cm
-2
for
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. This is low compared to our value of
10^{18}
cm
-2
at 0.3 au. This discrepancy could be explained by the way the column in derived in
Salyk et al. 2011b
, in which the emitting area for water was determined and applied it to all species. The region in which
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
has a high abundance is smaller than that of water, so it is expected that the
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
emitting region is smaller as well. This smaller emitting region would also imply a
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
excitation temperature that is higher than that of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
as is seen in
Salyk et al. 2011b
. If the actual emitting region of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is smaller than assumed in the fitting by
Salyk et al. 2011b

IV.2
Nitrogen

Water UV-shielding has a strong effect on the nitrogen-bearing species, increasing abundances of
HCN
,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
while lowering the abundance of NO. The abundance of
HCN
is known to be sensitive to the gas phase C/O ratio
(
Cleeves et al. 2018
, e.g.)
. Our model effectively changes the C/O ratio and thus, HCN rises in abundance for the water UV-shielding model. This is in large part driven by the chemistry discussed in Sec.
IV.1
. However, the changes in
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
NO
imply that the active nitrogen chemistry is also changed.

There seems to be three driving factors for the increased abundance of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
,
HCN
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
. The first driving factor is due to an impediment placed within the formation pathway of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
is primarily formed from atomic N by the reactions:

\begin{split}{}\mathrm{N}{}+{}{}\mathrm{OH}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{NO}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}\\
{}\mathrm{NO}{}+{}{}\mathrm{N}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{N}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}+{}{}\mathrm{O}.\end{split}

(2)

With the inclusion of water self-shielding the OH abundance is lowered and this channel is suppressed. Further, in both the standard and full models, the
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation through
CN
+
N
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
+
C
is suppressed by the competition with the
CN
+
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}
HCN
+
H
reaction. The slow
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation leads to more nitrogen being available for species beyond
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, most notably HCN and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. This also impacts the abundance of
NO
which is formed less efficiently in the UV-shielding model and thus has a lower abundance (Fig. 1).

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
formation is initiated by the reaction of He
+
with
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
or HCN, forming N
+
. The addition reaction with
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
allows for the eventual formation of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
+
, the precursor to
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. The main destruction channel for
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
in these hot layers is atomic
H
. Suppressing the photo-dissociation of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
lowers the production, and thus abundance of atomic
H
. This increases the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
lifetime and thus abundance as more hydrogen is available in the form of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. We see that
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
has a maximum abundance occurring at the water iceline with the inclusion of UV-shielding of roughly
10^{-7}
, a value four orders of magnitude higher than when UV-shielding is excluded.

Lastly, the active carbon chemistry allows for atomic nitrogen to react with the abundant carbon chains (
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{x\/}}}\text{H}
,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{y\/}}}\text{N}
), which produces
CN
, which reacts with
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to form
HCN
.
HCN
can then react with the more abundant
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
to form
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CNH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}^{\text{+}}
the precursor for
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}

The only nitrogen species that has so far been observed in the inner disk is HCN. HCN has been observed with columns of
10^{14}-10^{15}
cm
-2
in
Salyk et al. 2011b
and predicted to have columns of
10^{14}-10^{16}
cm

\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
as the column is built up within deeper, cooler gas. Higher HCN fluxes are only seen by letting the C/O ratio approach unity in
Woitke et al. 2018

We produced a column of
10^{19}
cm

with the effects of UV-shielding included. This column has a strong contribution from warm surface layers (e.g. Fig.

and thus the flux from our model is likely stronger than with the C/O = 0.46 from

The species that that might be detectable are NO and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

V
Conclusions

In this work, we have studied the effects of water UV-shielding on the chemical compositions of the inner, planet forming region of protoplanetary disks. Specifically, we are looking at chemical abundances in the upper disk atmosphere where the IR line emission originates and the effects of water UV-shielding are most prominent. This is done in order to further our understanding of observed emission from organics which will be critical for the interpretation of observations by the James Webb Space Telescope.

We have concluded that water self-shielding has notable effects for hydrocarbons and nitriles. The lack of OH produced by
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
dissociation suppresses
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
CO
,
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
formation. As a result, there is more carbon and nitrogen available for a rich carbon and nitrogen chemistry (
>
3 orders of magnitude), boosting the abundance of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, HCN,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
, and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
.

The depletion seen in CO and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
cannot be explained alone by the formation of species such as HCN,
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
. A significant amount (up to 53 percent) of the total volatile carbon finds its way into larger carbon chains, such as
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{6}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{9}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
. The nitrogen released from
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
finds its way to HCN with traces in
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{CN}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
. We expect to observe
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, HCN, NO, and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
with column densities shown in Fig.
3
.

The inclusion of water UV-shielding provides a way to increase the production of
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and HCN which have been historically under-produced, without invoking a C/O ratio near unity. Model abundances for
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
are already in agreement with observation, noted in Paper I and Paper III, thus this is a step forward in matching all four species at the same time.

Water UV-shielding is important for the entire chemical inventory. Through its ability to both block UV rays from penetrating deep into the disk and to create an effectively oxygen-poor environment, formation conditions become more favorable for various hydrocarbons and nitriles. This work has shown that water UV-shielding impacts the inner disk chemical composition and better reproduces observation. Thus, further studies that vary the C/O ratio with UV-shielding are needed to advance our understanding of the inner disk chemistry and its evolution.
