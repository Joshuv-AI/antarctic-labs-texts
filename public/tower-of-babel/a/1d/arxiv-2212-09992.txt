Basmajian’s identity over non-Archimedean local fields
Yan Mary He, Chenxi Wu

Abstract
. Let Σ \Sigma be a connected compact oriented surface with boundary and negative Euler characteristic. Let k k be a non-Archimedean local field. In this paper, we prove Basmajian’s identity for projective Anosov representations ρ : π 1 ​ Σ → PSL ​ ( d , k ) , d ≥ 2 \rho\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k),d\geq 2 . 1.
Introduction

If
\Sigma
is a connected compact oriented hyperbolic surface with geodesic boundary
\partial\Sigma
, an orthogeodesic
\gamma
on
\Sigma
is a properly immersed geodesic arc that is perpendicular to
\partial\Sigma
at both ends. In
[
2
]
, Basmajian proved the following identity relating the sum of hyperbolic lengths of boundary geodesics to the lengths of orthogeodesics on
\Sigma
:

\ell(\partial\Sigma)=\sum_{\gamma}2\log\coth^{2}(\ell(\gamma)/2)

where the summation is taken over all orthogeodesics
\gamma
on
\Sigma
.

A hyperbolic structure on
\Sigma
can be identified with a discrete faithful representation
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
acting on the hyperbolic plane as Möbius transformations. For simplicity, we look for now surfaces with one boundary component. Up to
conjugation, we may assume that
\rho([\partial\Sigma])
stabilizes the positive imaginary axis
L
. Orthogeodesics are in one-to-one correspondence with double cosets of
\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)
in

\pi_{1}\Sigma

[
9
,
19
]
. For each nontrivial double coset
\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)w\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)
, the hyperbolic geodesic
w\cdot L
corresponds to another boundary component of
\widetilde{\Sigma}
,
and the contribution to Basmajian’s identity from this term is

\log[\infty,0,\rho(w)(\infty),\rho(w)(0)]
, where
[a,b,c,d]
is the cross ratio on
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Hence Basmajian’s identity can be equivalently written as

\ell({\partial\Sigma})=\sum_{w}\log[\infty,0,\rho(w)(\infty),\rho(w)(0)]

where the summation is taken over all the double cosets
\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)w\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)
.

In this paper, instead of considering the Archimedean field
\mathbb{R}
, we consider projective Anosov representations
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
where
d\geq 2
is an integer and
(k,|\cdot|)
is a
non-Archimedean
local field with a non-trivial absolute value
|\cdot|
. Following Pozzetti-Sambarino-Wienhard
[
16
, Definition 3.1]
, we say that a homomorphism
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
is
projective Anosov
if there exist positive constants
c,\mu
such that for all
g\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
we have

\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}(\rho(g))\leq ce^{-\mu|g|_{w}}

where
|g|_{w}
is the word length of
g
with respect to a fixed generating set. The numbers
\sigma_{i}
are the
ratios of the semi-homothecy
; see Section
2.2
for details. The main theorem of this paper is the following Basmajian-type identity for projective Anosov representations
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a connected compact oriented surface with boundary and negative Euler characteristic. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
represent the free homotopy classes of boundaries
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
. Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and let
k
be a non-Archimedean local field with a non-trivial absolute value
|\cdot|
. If
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
is a projective Anosov representation, then we have

(1.2)

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})=\sum_{j,q=1}^{m}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{j,q}}\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{+},w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{-})\right|

where
\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-}\in\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
are the attracting and repelling fixed points of
\alpha_{j}
, respectively.

Here
\ell_{\rho}
and
C_{\rho}
denote certain length and cross ratio associated to
\rho
respectively, and
\log_{v}
is the logarithm function with base
p>1
being the cardinality of the residue field of
k
. The set
\mathcal{L}_{j,q}
is a set of double coset representatives associated to boundary components
a_{j}
and
a_{q}
. We note that if we put an ordering on a symmetric generating set of
\pi_{1}\Sigma
, each double coset
\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)w\pi_{1}(\partial\Sigma)
has a
unique
representative
w
; namely, the shortest in word length and the largest with respect to the ordering.

It is worth pointing out that the series on the right hand side of (
1.2
) is in fact a
finite signed
sum. We will show in Section
5
that there are projective Anosov represenations for which the right hand side series of Theorem
1.1
consists of only positive terms, as well as representations for which the right hand side series consists of both positive and negative terms.
This is a major difference from the identities over Archimedean fields where the series involved is always an
infinite
sum of
positive
terms.

Before we outline the strategy of the proof of the theorem, we mention related work on identities in hyperbolic geometry, higher Teichmüller theory and super Teichmüller theory. We note that all these identities are for representations over Archimedean fields. For identities on hyperbolic manifolds, we refer the reader to the survey paper
[
4
]
and the references therein. The first author extended Basmajian’s identity to certain Schottky representations
[
9
]
. Vlamis-Yarmola established Basmajian’s identity for Hitchin representations
[
19
]
. The first author established Basmajian’s identity for hyperconvex Anosov representations
[
10
]
. Fanoni-Pozzetti obtained Basmajian-type inequalities for maximal representations
[
16
]
. Moreover, there is recent progress in generalizing McShane’s identity to higher Teichmüller theory
[
15
,
12
]
and super Teichmüller theory
[
11
]
.

1.1.
Strategy of the proof

We first prove Theorem
1.1
for
d=2
using Berkovich hyperbolic geometry and then give a proof for the general case.

If
k
is a non-Archimedian local field which is complete with respect to a non-trivial absolute value
|\cdot|
, then the topology on
k
induced by the given absolute value is Hausdorff but it is totally disconnected. Berkovich’s theory allows us to overcome this difficulty coming from the topology of
k
and study analytic objects on
k
. The Berkovich affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1,an}_{k}
is a locally compact, Hausdorff and path-connected topological space which contains
k
, with the topology induced by
|\cdot|
, as a dense subspace. The Berkovich projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,an}_{k}
is obtained by adding the point
\infty
to compactify
\mathbb{A}^{1,an}_{k}
. Therefore, the Berkovich projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,an}_{k}
is a compact, Hausdorff and path-connected topological space which contains the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
as a dense subspace. Moreover,
\mathbb{P}^{1,an}_{k}
admits a tree structure and it is in fact an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. Based on the tree structure, the boundary of
\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1,an}
is
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
and the set
\mathbb{H}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{P}_{k}^{1,an}\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is called the
Berkovic hyperbolic space
.

The group
\text{PGL}(2,k)
of Möbius transformations acts continuously on
\mathbb{P}^{1,an}_{k}
in a natural way which is compatible with its action on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. This action preserves the Berkovic hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{k}
, and moreover, there exists a metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\mathbb{H}_{k}
such that
\text{PGL}(2,k)
acts by isometries.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface as in Theorem
1.1
. A projective Anosov representation
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(2,k)
gives rise to an action of
\rho(\pi_{1}\Sigma)
on
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. Since
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(2,k)
is projective Anosov, it is discrete and faithful, and for every
g\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
, the matrix
\rho(g)
is hyperbolic as an element in
\text{PGL}(2,k)
. The
limit set
of
\rho
is the image of the Gromov boundary
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
under the limit map
\zeta^{1}_{\rho}:\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
.

The general idea of proving Theorem
1.1
when
d=2
is analogous to Basmajian’s original proof. Namely, for each boundary component
a_{j}
of the surface, the hyperbolic length of a fundamental domain on the axis
{\rm Axis}(\alpha_{j})
of
\alpha_{j}
is equal to the sum of the
projections
onto
{\rm Axis}(\alpha_{j})
of the translates
g\cdot{\rm Axis}(\alpha_{j})
, where
g
ranges over representatives of double cosets
\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle\backslash\pi_{1}\Sigma/\langle\alpha_{q}\rangle
. However, the key difference of the proof from that for Archimedean fields is that only finitely many projections are non-zero. This is due to the
non-Archimedean
feature of the absolute value of the field. Moreover, different from the case of identities over Archimedean fields, the length of the projections are
signed
, which may not always be positive as in the case of identities over Archimedean fields.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
in the general case does not use any geometric argument. Instead, for each boundary component
a_{j}
of the surface, we interpret the length
\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})
as the length of a fundamental domain on the real line
\mathbb{R}
which plays the role of the axis
{\rm Axis}(\alpha_{j})
of
\alpha_{j}
. Then we show that the length of this fundamental domain is equal to the sum of lengths, one for each double coset in
\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle\backslash\pi_{1}\Sigma/\langle\alpha_{q}\rangle
. Similar to the case
d=2
, this sum is finite and some terms in the summation may be negative.
We will give examples of Anosov representations in Section
5
to illustrate this phenomena.

Organization of the paper

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review and prove preliminary results about Anosov representations over non-Archimedean local fields. In Section 3, we review and prove preliminary results about the Berkovich projective line and the Berkovich hyperbolic space. In Section 4, we prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section 5, we give examples to demonstrate Theorem

Anosov representations over non-Archimedean local fields

In this section, we first give a quick introduction to non-Archimedean local fields in Section

, we give a brief overview of definitions and properties of Anosov representations
\rho:\Gamma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
from a Gromov hyperbolic group
\Gamma
into
\text{PGL}(d,k)
where
k
is a non-Archimedean local field. We then discuss a

2.1.
Non-Archimedean local fields

In this section, we collect the basics of non-Archimedean local fields. Standard references are
[
5
,
17
]
.

Let
k
be a field and let
k^{\times}=k\setminus\{0\}
. An
absolute value
on
k
is a map
|\cdot|:k\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

|x|=0\iff x=0;|xy|=|x|\cdot|y|,\forall x,y\in k;\text{ and }|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|.

An absolute value on
k
is called
non-Archimedean
if it also satisfies the ultrametric inequality

|x+y|\leq\max\{|x|,|y|\}.

Otherwise the absolute value is called
Archimedean
.

The absolute value
|\cdot|
on
k
induces a metric on
k
; namely
d(x,y)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=|x-y|
for any
x,y\in k
. A field is
complete
if every Cauchy sequence has a limit point in the field. It is a classical theorem that if
k
is complete with respect to an Archimedean absolute value, then
k
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and the absolute value is equivalent to the usual absolute value.

A
valuation
on a field
k
is a group homomorphism
v:k^{\times}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x,y\in k
, we have

v(x+y)\geq\min\{v(x),v(y)\}.

We may extend
v
to a map
k\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by setting
v(0)=\infty
. For any
c\in(0,1)
,
|\cdot|\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=c^{v(\cdot)}
gives a non-Archimedean absolute value. The image of
v
in
\mathbb{R}
is called the
value group
and is denoted by
|k^{\times}|
. We say that
v
is a
discrete valuation
if the value group is equal to
\mathbb{Z}
. The set
k^{\circ}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{x\in k:v(x)\geq 0\}
is the
valuation ring
of
k
(with respect to
v
). It is a
discrete
valuation ring if
v
is discrete.
If
k^{\circ}
is a discrete valuation ring, any element
\pi\in k^{\circ}
with
v(\pi)=1
is called a
uniformizer
. The ideal generated by
\pi
is the unique maximal ideal of
k^{\circ}
. The
residue field
of
k
is the field
k^{\circ}/\pi k^{\circ}
.

A
local field
is a field
k
which is complete with respect to a nontrivial absolute value induced by a discrete valuation and whose residue field is finite.
An Archimedean local field is isomorphic to either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
.
A non-Archimedean local field is isomorphic to either a finite extension of the
p
-adic numbers
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
if it has characteristic zero, or the field of formal Laurent series
\mathbb{F}_{q}((T))
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
where
q
is a power of a prime number
p
if it has characteristic
p
.

2.2.
Projective Anosov representations

Let
\Gamma
be a Gromov hyperbolic group. Let
(k,|\cdot|)
be a non-Archimedean local field. Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and let
p
be an integer with
1\leq p\leq d-1
. Following Pozzetti-Sambarino-Wienhard
[
16
, Definition 3.1]
which generalizes the work of Bochi-Potrie-Sambarino
[
3
]
, we say that a homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
is
p
-Anosov
if there exist positive constants
c,\mu
such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

\frac{\sigma_{p+1}}{\sigma_{p}}(\rho(\gamma))\leq ce^{-\mu|\gamma|_{w}}

where
|\gamma|_{w}
is the word length of
\gamma
with respect to a fixed generating set. The numbers
\sigma_{i}
are defined as follows. Let
V
be a
k
-vector space (e.g.
V=k^{d}
). Given a
k
-norm
||\cdot||_{V,k}
on
V
, we say that
g\in{\rm GL}(V,k)
is a
semi-homothecy
if there exists a
g
-invariant
k
-orthogonal decomposition
V=V_{1}\oplus...\oplus V_{m}
and
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{m}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that for every
i=1,...,m
and every
v_{i}\in V_{i}
one has

||gv_{i}||_{V,k}=\sigma_{i}||v_{i}||_{V,k}.

The numbers
\sigma_{i}
are called the
ratios of the semi-homothecy
. A
1
-Anosov representation is called a
projective Anosov
representation.

An important property of Anosov representations is that they admit equivariant transverse limit maps given by the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
16
, Proposition 3.5]
)
.

If
\rho:\Gamma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
is
p
-Anosov, then for any geodesic ray
\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
in (the Cayley graph of)
\Gamma
with endpoint
x
in the Gromov boundary
\partial\Gamma
, the limits

\zeta^{p}_{\rho}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{p}(\rho(\gamma_{n}))

\zeta^{k-p}_{\rho}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}U_{n-p}(\rho(\gamma_{n}))

exist and do not depend on the ray. Moreover, they define continuous
\rho
-equivariant transverse maps
\zeta^{p}:\partial\Gamma\to\mathcal{G}_{p}(k^{d})
and
\zeta^{d-p}:\partial\Gamma\to\mathcal{G}_{d-p}(k^{d})
, where
\mathcal{G}_{j}(k^{d})
denotes the Grassmannian
j
-planes in
k^{d}
for
j=p,n-p
. Here
U_{p}(\rho(\gamma))
denotes the Cartan attractor of
\rho(\gamma)
(see
[
16
, p. 9]
), i.e.

U_{p}(\rho(\gamma))=\beta\cdot(e_{1}\oplus...\oplus e_{p})

with
\rho(\gamma)=\beta a\beta^{\prime}
coming from the Cartan
KAK
decomposition of
GL(d,k)
and
\{e_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
being the standard basis for the vector space
k^{d}
.

If
\rho:\Gamma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
is projective Anosov, then the set
\zeta_{\rho}^{1}(\partial\Gamma)\subset\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
is called the
limit set
of
\rho
.

2.3.
Cross ratios

Let
\Sigma
be a connected compact oriented surface with boundary and negative Euler characteristic. In this subsection, we define a cross ratio for four points on the Gromov boundary
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
of the hyperbolic group
\pi_{1}\Sigma
which is a natural generalization of Labourie’s cross ratio; see
[
14
,
15
]
. This is achieved by using the transverse limit maps of a projective Anosov representation which take points of
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
into the projective spaces, and then taking the cross ratio considered by Falbel-Guilloux-Will
[
7
]
that we introduce now.

Let
k
be any field. Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and denote by
k^{d*}
the dual of the vector space
k^{d}
. Consider two non-empty sets
\Omega\subset\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
and
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{P}(k^{d*})
such that

\varphi(\omega)\neq 0

for any
\omega\in\Omega
and
\varphi\in\Lambda
. In other words,
\Omega
is disjoint from all hyperplanes defined by points in
\Lambda
.

Let
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Lambda^{2}\times\Omega^{2}
with
\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}
so that
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
span a projective line
(\omega\omega^{\prime})
. In
[
7
]
, Falbel-Guilloux-Will considered the following cross ratio

[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=[\varphi_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},\varphi^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]

where
\varphi_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
and
\varphi^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
are the points
\text{ker}(\varphi)\cap(\omega\omega^{\prime})
and
\text{ker}(\varphi^{\prime})\cap(\omega\omega^{\prime})
in
(\omega\omega^{\prime})
respectively, and
[w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]
is the usual cross ratio for four distinct points in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)=(\omega\omega^{\prime})
, namely
[w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]=(w_{1}-w_{3})(w_{2}-w_{4})/(w_{1}-w_{4})(w_{2}-w_{3})
.

The cross ratio satisfies the following properties.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given
(\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime})\in\Lambda^{2}\times\Omega^{2}
, the cross ratio
[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]
satisfies

(2.3)

[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]=\frac{\bar{\varphi}(\bar{\omega})\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega}^{\prime})}{\bar{\varphi}(\bar{\omega}^{\prime})\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega})}

where
\bar{\varphi},\bar{\varphi}^{\prime},\bar{\omega}
and
\bar{\omega}^{\prime}
are any lifts of
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
. Moreover, if
\omega,\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}
are three points in
\Omega
and
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime\prime}
are three points in
\Lambda
, we have

(1)

[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]=1
if and only if
\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}
or
\omega=\omega^{\prime}
,

(2)

[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]=[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime\prime}]]\cdot[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\omega^{\prime}]]
, and

(3)

[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]]=[[\varphi,\varphi^{\prime},\omega^{\prime},\omega]]^{-1}
.

Proof.

We give a sketch of the proof here and refer the interested reader to
[
7
, Lemma 2.3, Proposition 2.4]
. Equation (
2.3
) is obvious if
\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}
.
Suppose
\varphi\neq\varphi^{\prime}
, we choose a basis
\{e_{m}\}_{m=1}^{n}
of the vector space
k^{n}
such that the lifts
\bar{\varphi},\bar{\varphi}^{\prime},\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
and
\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
(in
k^{n*}
and
k^{n}
) are as follows:

\bar{\varphi}=e_{1}^{*},\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}=e_{2}^{*},\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}=e_{2}+\bar{u},\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}=e_{1}+\bar{u}^{\prime}

where
\bar{u}
and
\bar{u}^{\prime}
are vectors in Span
(e_{3},\cdots,e_{n})
. Note that
\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
and
\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
form a basis for the plane spanned by
\bar{\omega}
and
\bar{\omega}^{\prime}
. Therefore
\bar{\omega}
and
\bar{\omega}^{\prime}
can be written as

\bar{\omega}=\lambda^{\prime}\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}+\lambda\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\text{ and }\bar{\omega}^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}+\mu\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}.

With respect to the coordinate basis
\{\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},\bar{\varphi}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\}
, in the projective line
(\omega\omega^{\prime})
,
\varphi_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}
has projective coordinate
[1,\infty=1/0]
,
\varphi^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}=[1,0]
,
\omega=[\lambda^{\prime},\lambda]=[1;\lambda/\lambda^{\prime}]
and
\omega^{\prime}=[\mu^{\prime},\mu]=[1;\mu/\mu^{\prime}]
.

Therefore, we have

\frac{\bar{\varphi}(\bar{\omega})\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega}^{\prime})}{\bar{\varphi}(\bar{\omega}^{\prime})\bar{\varphi}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega})}=\frac{\mu\lambda^{\prime}}{\mu^{\prime}\lambda}=[\varphi_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},\varphi^{\prime}_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}},\omega,\omega^{\prime}]

proving equation (
2.3
).

Identities (1)-(3) can be verified by straightforward computations.
∎

Now we define a cross ratio for four points on the boundary
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
. Let
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
be a projective Anosov representation with limit maps
\zeta^{1}
and
\zeta^{d-1}
. We denote by
(\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma)^{4*}
the set

(\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma)^{4*}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{(x,y,u,v)\in(\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma)^{4}:x\neq v,y\neq u\}.

The
cross ratio
is a continuous function
C_{\rho}:(\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma)^{4*}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v)=[[\zeta^{d-1}(x),\zeta^{d-1}(y),\zeta^{1}(u),\zeta^{1}(v)]].

We note that if
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,\mathbb{R})
is Hitchin, then
C_{\rho}
is Labourie’s cross ratio.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
be a projective Anosov representation. The cross ratio
C_{\rho}
satisfies the following properties

(1)

C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v)=0\iff x=u
or
y=v
.

(2)

C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v)=1\iff x=y
or
u=v
.

(3)

C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v)=C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v^{\prime})C_{\rho}(x,y,v^{\prime},v)

(4)

C_{\rho}(x,y,u,v)=C_{\rho}(x,y,v,u)^{-1}

Proof.

(1)-(4) can be checked directly by definition and Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Given a projective Anosov representation
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PGL}(d,k)
, the
translation length
of any nontrivial element
\gamma\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
is defined as

\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\log_{v}\left|\frac{\lambda_{1}(\rho(\gamma))}{\lambda_{d}(\rho(\gamma))}\right|

where
\lambda_{1}(\rho(\gamma))
and
\lambda_{d}(\rho(\gamma))
are the eigenvalues of maximum and minimum absolute value of
\rho(\gamma)
, respectively, and
\log_{v}
is the logarithm function with base
q>1
being the cardinality of the residue field of
k
. The
period
of a nontrivial element
\gamma\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
is defined as

P_{\rho}(\gamma)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-},x,\gamma x)\right|

where
x
is any point in
\partial\pi_{1}\hat{\Sigma}\setminus\{\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-}\}
.

If

or
\mathbb{C}
, then translation lengths and periods become the usual notion of (complex) lengths and (complex) periods. Moreover, similar to the case
k=\mathbb{R}
proved by Labourie
[

, Proposition 5.8]
, the period of a nontrivial element equals its translation length.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any nontrivial element
\gamma\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
,

.

Proof.

The proof follows from the same calculation which is carried in details in
[

, Proposition 5.8]

3.
The Berkovich projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}

Let
(k,|\cdot|)
be a complete metrized non-Archimedean field. In this section, we give a brief overview of the Berkovic projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
, the Berkovic hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{k}
and the group
\text{PGL}(2,k)
that we will use in the next section. Our exposition follows closely from
[
6
]
and
[
1
]
.

3.1.
The Berkovich projective line and the Berkovich hyperbolic space

Let
(k,|\cdot|)
be a complete metrized non-Archimedean field. Recall that
k^{\times}=k\setminus\{0\}
and
|k^{\times}|
is the value group of
k
. Let
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
be the projective line over
k
, viewed as an algebraic variety with Zariski toplogy, and by
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
the set of
k
-points of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
which as a set is
k\cup\{\infty\}
. The Berkovich analytification
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
is defined as follows. Recall that a
multiplicative semi-norm
on a ring
A
is a function
[~]_{x}:A\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

[0]_{x}=0,[1]_{x}=1,[fg]_{x}=[f]_{x}\cdot[g]_{x}\text{ and }[f+g]_{x}\leq[f]_{x}+[g]_{x}

for all
f,g\in A
.
We first define the Berkovich analytification
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
of the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}_{k}
as the set of multiplicative semi-norms on
k[Z]
whose restriction to
k
coincides with
|\cdot|
, endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence. More specifically, given a point
x\in\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
and a polynomial
P\in k[Z]
, the value of the semi-norm defined by
x
on
P
is denoted by
|P(x)|\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.
The
Gauss norm

\sum_{i}a_{i}Z^{i}\mapsto{\rm max}|a_{i}|
defines a point
x_{g}
in
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
which is called the
Gauss point
. The Berkovich projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
can be defined topologically as the one-point compactification of
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
; namely, we have
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
correspond to balls in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. Recall that a closed (resp. open) ball in
k
of radius
R\geq 0
is defined as

\overline{B}(z_{0},R)=\{z\in k:|z-z_{0}|\leq R\}

(resp.
B(z_{0},R)=\{z\in k:|z-z_{0}|<R\}
). A ball in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is either a ball in
k
or the complement of a ball in
k
. Any closed (or open) ball
B\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
determines a point
x_{B}\in\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. Indeed, if
B
or its complement is a singleton
\{z\}
, then the point
x_{B}
is the rigid point associated to
z
. If
B
is a (closed or open) ball of finite radius in
k
, then
x_{B}
is the point in
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
corresponding to the semi-norm

|P(x_{B})|\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup_{B}|P|.

If the complement of
B
is a ball of finite radius, then we set

|P(x_{B})|\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\sup_{k\setminus B}|P|.

The Gauss point
x_{g}
corresponds to
x_{\bar{B}(0,1)}
.
Moreover, the Berkovich projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
is a (profinite)
\mathbb{R}
-tree in the sense that it is uniquely path connected; namely, for any two points
x,y\in\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
, there is a unique segment
[x,y]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
.

A point in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
is said to be
rigid
if the multiplicative semi-norm has a non-trivial kernel. There is a canonical bijection between the set of closed points of the
k
-scheme
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}
and the set of rigid points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. To see this, let
z
be a point in a finite extension of
k
. Then the semi-norm
|\cdot|_{z}
defined by
P\mapsto|P(z)|
is a rigid point in
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. Therefore,
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
naturally embeds in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
as a set of rigid points.

Now suppose
(k,|\cdot|)
is a complete metrized non-Archimedean field which is algebraically closed.
Points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
can be classified by their
types
. Points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
corresponding to balls of diameter zero in
k
are called
type I
points of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. These points are in bijection with rigid points in the Berkovich projective space
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. Points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
corresponding to balls of diameter
{\rm diam}(B)\in|k^{\times}|
are said to be of
type II
. Points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
corresponding to balls of diameter
{\rm diam}(B)\notin|k^{\times}|
are said to be of
type III
.

More generally, points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
are in bijection with (equivalence classes of) decreasing sequences of balls. If
k
is spherically complete (i.e. every decreasing interseciton of balls is non-empty), then
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
consists of type I, II or III points. A local field is spherically complete. In general,
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
could contain
type IV
points corresponding to decreasing sequences of balls whose intersection is empty. For each
i=1,...,4
, the set of type
i
points, if it is non-empty, is dense in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
.

Types of points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
are related to the
\mathbb{R}
-tree structure of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
as follows. Type I and IV points are vertices of the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
which have only one branch; namely, they are the end points of the tree. Type II points are vertices of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
which have at least three branches. Finally type III points are the vertices of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
which have exactly two branches; that is, the regular points.

As a set, the Berkovich hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}_{k}
is defined as

\mathbb{H}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}(k).

It is a proper subtree of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
which is neither open nor closed. Moreover, it contains no rigid points.

There is a partial order on
\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. If
x,y\in\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
, we say that
x\leq y
if

[f]_{x}\leq[f]_{y}\text{ for all }f\in k[Z].

In terms of balls, if
x,y
are of type I, II or III, we say that
x\leq y
if the ball corresponding to
x
is contained in the ball corresponding to
y
. For any pair of points
x,y\in\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
, there exists a unique least upper bound
x\cup y\in\mathbb{A}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
with respect to this partial order.

We now define a metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. If
x,y\in\mathbb{H}_{k}
with
x\leq y
, then we define

d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\log_{v}\frac{{\rm diam}(y)}{{\rm diam}(x)}.

More generally, if
x,y\in\mathbb{H}_{k}
, we define

d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y)=d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,x\cup y)+d_{\mathbb{H}}(y,x\cup y).

It is straightforward to check that
d_{\mathbb{H}}
defines a metric on
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. We can extend
d_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
by setting
d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y)=\infty
if
x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
and
y\in\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
with
x\neq y
, and
d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y)=0
if
x=y
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
1
, Proposition 2.29]
)
.

The space
(\mathbb{H}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
is an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. The group
\text{PGL}(2,k)
acts as isometries on
(\mathbb{H}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
.

Now suppose
k
is not algebraically closed. Let
\bar{k}^{a}
be the completion of an algebraic closure of
k
. Then
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
is homeomorphic to the quotient of
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
by the Galois group
{\rm Gal}(\bar{k}^{a}/k)
. Let
\pi_{\bar{k}^{a}/k}:\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{\bar{k}^{a}}\to\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
be the projection map. The action of the Galois group
{\rm Gal}(\bar{k}^{a}/k)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
preserves types of points. Therefore, we can define the type of a point
x\in\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
by the type of the point
\pi_{\bar{k}^{a}/k}^{-1}(x)\in\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
. We note that in general some type I point in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
may
not
be rigid.

The action of the Galois group
{\rm Gal}(\bar{k}^{a}/k)
preserves the diameter of balls in
\bar{k}^{a}
. Therefore
{\rm Gal}(\bar{k}^{a}/k)
acts by isometries on
(\mathbb{H}_{\bar{k}^{a}},d_{\mathbb{H}})
. The set
\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
of fixed points of this action turns out to be the convex hull
\overline{{\rm Conv}(\mathbb{P}^{1}(k))}
of
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
in
\mathbb{H}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
; see
[
6
, Lemma 1.6]
. Hence, in
\mathbb{P}_{\bar{k}^{a}}^{1,{\rm an}}
, we define

\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\overline{{\rm Conv}(\mathbb{P}^{1}(k))}\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}(k).

Then
(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
is a complete metric
\mathbb{R}
-tree as shown in the previous subsection. Define

\mathbb{H}_{k}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\pi_{\bar{k}^{a}/k}(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k})

and endow
\mathbb{H}_{k}
with the metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
so that the projection map
\pi_{\bar{k}^{a}/k}:(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})\to(\mathbb{H}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
is an isometry. We call
(\mathbb{H}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
the Berkovic hyperbolic space of
k
.

The action of
\text{PGL}(2,k)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{\bar{k}^{a}}
preserves types of points; see
[
1
, Proposition 2.15]
. Therefore, it also preserves types of points in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. Morevoer,
\text{PGL}(2,k)
acts on
(\mathbb{H}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
by isometries as it does on
(\widetilde{\mathbb{H}}_{k},d_{\mathbb{H}})
.

3.2.
Elements of
\text{PGL}(2,k)

The first proposition of this subsection gives a classification of elements in
\text{PGL}(2,k)
.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
6
, Proposition 2.2]
)
.

Let
A\in\text{PGL}(2,k)
and
A\neq{\rm id}
. Then exactly one of the following holds.

(1)

|{\rm tr}(\gamma)|>1
: then
A
is diagonalizable over
k
and has one attracting (resp. repelling) fixed point
x_{att}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
(resp.
x_{rep}\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
). Furthermore, for any
x\neq x_{rep}
in
\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
, the sequence
A^{n}\cdot x
converges to

as

. In this case,
A
is said to be hyperbolic.

(2)

|{\rm tr}(\gamma)|\leq 1
: then
A
admits a fixed point in
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. In this case,
A
is said to be elliptic. More precisely if

•

{\rm tr}^{2}(\gamma)=4
: then
A
is not diagonalizable, and is conjugate in
\text{PGL}(2,k)
to
z\mapsto z+1
; thus it fixes a segment
[x,y]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
where

(resp.

) is a type II (resp. type I) point belonging to the

A
is said to be parabolic.

•

{\rm tr}^{2}(\gamma)\neq 4
: then in a field extension
K/k
of degree at most 2, the matrix
A
is diagonalizable. In addition,
A
is conjugate to an element in
\text{PGL}(2,k^{\circ})
and fixes a type II point in
\mathbb{H}_{k}

If
\gamma\in{\rm SL}(2,k)

||\gamma||
of

Proposition 3.3

(Cartan decomposition,
[

Let
\gamma\in{\rm SL}(2,k)

m,n\in{\rm SL}(2,k^{\circ})
and

\lambda\in k

3.3.
Projections and cross ratios

In this subsection,
(k,|\cdot|)
is a non-Archimedean local field with a non-trivial absolute value
|\cdot|
.
Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
and consider the geodesic
[0,\infty]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
from
0\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
and
\infty\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. We say that
\beta\in[0,\infty]
is the
projection
of
\alpha
onto
[0,\infty]
if
\beta
is the unique point in
[0,\infty]
such that there exists a geodesic segment
[\beta,\alpha]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
. The following lemma gives a formula for
\beta
in terms of balls in
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
. Then the projection of
\alpha
onto the geodesic
[0,\infty]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
is the ball
B(0,|\alpha|)
.

Proof.

The point
\alpha\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
corresponds to the ball
B(\alpha,0)
in
k
, which is disjoint from the ball
B(0,0)
. Therefore the point of projection of
\alpha
onto
[0,\infty]
is
B(0,|\alpha-0|)
.
∎

The next lemma states that for
a,b\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
satisfying
0<|a|<|b|
, the quantity
\log_{v}\left|[\infty,0;|a|,|b|]\right|
is equal to the hyperbolic distance between the projection of
a
and
b
onto the geodesic
[0,\infty]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
a,b\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
satisfy
0<|a|<|b|
, then we have

d_{\mathbb{H}}(B(0,|a|),B(0,|b|))=\log_{v}\left|[\infty,0;|a|,|b|]\right|

where
[u,v;x,y]
is the cross ratio on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
.

Proof.

If
0<|a|<|b|
, the geodesic segment
[B(0,|a|),B(0,|b|)]\subset[0,\infty]\subset\mathbb{P}^{1,{\rm an}}_{k}
is isometric to the interval
[\log_{v}|a|,\log_{v}|b|]\subset\mathbb{R}
equipped with the Lebesgue measure. Therefore, we have

d_{\mathbb{H}}(B(0,|a|),B(0,|b|))=\log_{v}|b|-\log_{v}|a|=\log_{v}\left|[\infty,0;|a|,|b|]\right|.

∎

4.
Non-Archimedean Basmajian’s identity

In this section, we first give a geometric proof of Theorem
1.1
when

using Berkovic hyperbolic geometry in Section
4.1
.
We then prove Theorem
1.1
in Section

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
for
d=2
using Berkovic hyperbolic geometry

Let
\Sigma
be a connected compact oriented surface with boundary
\partial\Sigma
and negative Euler characteristic. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
represent the free homotopy classes of boundaries
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
. Each boundary
a_{j}
is oriented so that the surface is on the right. Fix a finite area hyperbolic structure on
\Sigma
so that
\partial\Sigma
is totally geodesic. Let
V\subset\mathbb{H}^{2}
be the universal cover of
\Sigma
so that
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma=\partial_{\infty}V=\overline{V}\cap\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Fix a boundary component
\alpha_{j}
of
\Sigma
and let
\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-}\in\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
be the attracting and repelling fixed points of
\alpha_{j}
, respectively. We consider the interval
(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})
as a subset of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the given orientation.

We first observe that
\mathbb{S}^{1}\setminus\partial_{\infty}V
is a disjoint union of intervals of the form

(4.1)

\tilde{I}_{\beta}=(\beta^{+},\beta^{-})

where
\beta=g\alpha_{k}g^{-1}
for some boundary element
\alpha_{k}
and
g\in\pi_{1}\Sigma
, since the endpoints
\beta^{-}
and
\beta^{+}
are the endpoints of lifts of boundary elements.

Lemma 4.2
.

Set
H_{j}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle,j=1,\ldots,m
. Each interval
\tilde{I}_{\beta}
correspond to a coset
gH_{q}
in
\bigsqcup_{1\leq q\leq m}\pi_{1}\Sigma/H_{q}.

Proof.

If
\tilde{I}_{g_{1}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}g_{1}^{-1}}=\tilde{I}_{g_{2}\alpha_{l}g_{2}^{-1}}
, then we know that

g_{1}\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{\pm}=g_{2}\alpha_{l}^{\pm}

and therefore
(g_{2}^{-1}g_{1})\alpha_{l^{\prime}}^{\pm}=\alpha_{l}^{\pm}
. Since
\alpha_{l^{\prime}}
and
\alpha_{l}
are boundary elements, it follows that
{l^{\prime}}=l
and therefore
g_{2}^{-1}g_{1}\in H_{l}
.
∎

Let
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(2,k)
be projective Anosov. Consider the action of
\rho(\pi_{1}\Sigma)
on the Berkovic space
\mathbb{P}^{1,an}_{k}
.
We conjugate
\rho
so that
\zeta^{1}(\alpha_{j}^{-})=0
and
\zeta^{1}(\alpha_{j}^{+})=\infty
. Therefore
[0,\infty]\subset\mathbb{H}_{k}
is the axis of
\alpha_{j}
. Let
P\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)\to[0,\infty]
be the projection onto the axis
[0,\infty]
given by
P(x)=B(0,|x|)
by Lemma
3.4
. Define the map
\Psi_{\rho}\colon(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})\to(0,\infty)
by

\Psi_{\rho}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=P\circ\zeta^{1}(x).

By definition, for any
x\in(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
,
\Psi_{\rho}(x)
is the projection of the point
\zeta^{1}(x)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
in the limit set onto the axis
(0,\infty)\subset\mathbb{H}_{k}
of
\alpha_{j}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x\in(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then we have
\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})=d_{\mathbb{H}}(\Psi_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}\cdot x),\Psi_{\rho}(x)).

Proof.

By definition of
\Psi_{\rho}
and Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}}(P\circ\zeta^{1}(\alpha_{j}\cdot x),P\circ\zeta^{1}(x))

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(\alpha_{j}\cdot x)|-\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(x)|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};x,\alpha_{j}\cdot x)\right|

\displaystyle=\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}).

The proof is completed.
∎

For each interval
\tilde{I}_{\beta}
, define
\hat{I}_{\beta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{+}),\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{-}))\subset[0,\infty]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

All but finitely many intervals
\tilde{I}_{\beta}
satisfy
\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})=\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})
.

Proof.

By definition of
\Psi_{\rho}
and Lemma
3.5
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}}(P\circ\zeta^{1}(\beta^{+}),P\circ\zeta^{1}(\beta^{-}))

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(\beta^{+})|-\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(\beta^{-})|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};\beta^{+},\beta^{-})\right|.

Since
\zeta^{1}\colon\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is continuous, if
\beta^{+}
and
\beta^{-}
are close, the cross ratio
C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};\beta^{+},\beta^{-})
is close to 1. Then the non-Archimedean absolute value gives
|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};\beta^{+},\beta^{-})|=1
. Therefore there are only finitely many
\widetilde{I}_{\beta}
with
\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})\neq\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})
.
The proof is completed.
∎

By Lemma
4.4
, there are only finitely many
\hat{I}_{\beta}
which are not a singleton. Moreover, we note that
\hat{I}_{\beta}
may or may not have the same orientation as
(0,\infty)
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
\mathbb{T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(0,\infty)/\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})\mathbb{Z}
is a fundamental domain of
\alpha_{j}
in
\mathbb{H}_{k}
. Let
\pi\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{T}
be the projection map. Define
I_{\beta}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\pi(\hat{I}_{\beta})
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have the following decomposition of
\mathbb{T}
:

\mathbb{T}=\pi(\Psi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V))\sqcup\left(\bigsqcup_{q=1}^{m}\bigcup_{\beta\in\mathcal{L}_{j,q}}I_{\beta}\right)

where
\pi(\Psi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V))
is a finite set and
\mathcal{L}_{j,q}
is the subset of the set of double cosets
\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle\backslash\pi_{1}\Sigma/\langle\alpha_{q}\rangle
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
, the projection
\Psi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V)
of the limit set onto
[0,\infty]
is a finite set. Therefore
\pi(\Psi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V))
is a finite set. Since the map
\pi\circ\Psi_{\rho}
is continuous,
\mathbb{T}
is equal to the image of the union of non-trivial
\hat{I}_{\beta}
’s under
\pi\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the sets
I_{\beta}
and the double cosets
\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle\backslash\pi_{1}\Sigma/\langle\alpha_{q}\rangle
. To see this, we observe that

\hat{I}_{\alpha_{j}\beta\alpha_{j}^{-1}}=(\Psi_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}\cdot\beta^{+}),\Psi_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}\cdot\beta^{-}))=\hat{I}_{\beta}-\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Let
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}
be the hyperbolic measure on
[0,\infty]
; namely,
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}((a,b))=d_{\mathbb{H}}(a,b)=\log_{v}(b/a)
for any interval
(a,b)
with
0<a<b<\infty
.

Lemma 4.6
.

We have
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{T})=\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})
and

\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(I_{\beta})=\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{+},w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{-})\right|

where
w
and
q
are as in Lemma
4.2
.

Proof.

By construction of
\mathbb{T}
, we have
\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{T})=\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})
.
We compute

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(I_{\beta})

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(\beta^{-})|-\log_{v}|\zeta^{1}(\beta^{+})|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|[\infty,0;\zeta^{1}(\beta^{+}),\zeta^{1}(\beta^{-})]|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{+},w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{-})\right|

where
w
is the unique representative of the double coset corresponding to
I_{\beta}
. Here
w
is unique if we put an ordering on a symmetric generating set of
\pi_{1}\Sigma
and let
w
be the double coset representative that is the shortest in word length and the largest with respect to the ordering.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
for
d=2
.

Fix a boundary component
\alpha_{j}
.
By Lemmas
4.5
and
4.6
, we have

\displaystyle\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(\mathbb{T})

\displaystyle=\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(\pi(\Psi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V)))+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{j,q}}\nu_{\mathbb{H}}(I_{\beta})

\displaystyle=0+\sum_{q=1}^{m}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{j,q}}\log_{v}\left|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{+},w\cdot\alpha_{q}^{-})\right|.

where the series on the right hand side is a finite sum. The identity is obtained by summing over
\alpha_{j}
’s.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The proof of Theorem
1.1
in general does not use any geometry. Instead, we consider the function
\Phi_{\rho}
defined in (
4.7
).

Let
\Sigma
be as in the previous section. Fix a boundary component
\alpha_{j}
of
\Sigma
and let
\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-}\in\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
be the attracting and repelling fixed points of
\alpha_{j}
, respectively.
For
d\geq 2
, let
\rho:\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
be projective Anosov. To ease notation, we denote by
\zeta^{1}=\zeta^{1}_{\rho}
. Let
z
be a reference point in
(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})
. Define
\Phi_{\rho}\colon(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})\to\mathbb{R}
by

(4.7)

\Phi_{\rho}(x)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},x,z)|.

Lemma 4.8
.

For any
x\in(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-})
, we have

\Phi_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}\cdot x)=\Phi_{\rho}(x)-\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}).

Proof.

By definition of
\Phi_{\rho}
, Lemma
2.4
(3) and Lemma
2.5
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}\cdot x)

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\alpha_{j}\cdot x,z)|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\frac{|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},x,z)|}{|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},x,\alpha_{j}\cdot x)|}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\rho}(x)-\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}).

The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

For each interval
\tilde{I}_{\beta}
, we have

\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})-\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},g\alpha_{q}^{+},g\alpha_{q}^{-})|
, where
g
and
q
are as in Lemma
4.2
.

Proof.

By definition of
\Phi_{\rho}
and Lemma
2.4
(4) and (3), we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})-\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\frac{|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\beta^{+},z)|}{|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\beta^{-},z)|}

\displaystyle=\log_{v}\left(|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\beta^{+},z)|\cdot|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},z,\beta^{-})|\right)

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\beta^{+},\beta^{-})|

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},g\alpha_{q}^{+},g\alpha_{q}^{-})|.

The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

All but finitely many intervals
\widetilde{I}_{\beta}
satisfy
|\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})-\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})|=0
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.9
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})-\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})

\displaystyle=\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-},\beta^{+},\beta^{-})|.

Since
\zeta^{1}\colon\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\mathbb{P}^{1}(k)
is continuous, if
\beta^{+}
and
\beta^{-}
are close, the cross ratio
C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};\beta^{+},\beta^{-})
is close to 1. Then the non-Archimedean absolute value gives
|C_{\rho}(\alpha_{j}^{+},\alpha_{j}^{-};\beta^{+},\beta^{-})|=1
. Therefore there are only finitely many
\widetilde{I}_{\beta}
with
\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{+})\neq\Psi_{\rho}(\beta^{-})
.
The proof is completed.
∎

Let
\mathbb{T}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\mathbb{R}/\ell_{\rho}(\alpha_{j})\mathbb{Z}
be a fundamental domain of
\alpha_{j}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Let
\pi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{T}
be the projection map. For each interval
\tilde{I}_{\beta}
, we denote by
\hat{I}_{\beta}\subset\mathbb{R}
the interval
(\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-}),\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+}))
or
(\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{+}),\Phi_{\rho}(\beta^{-}))
.

Lemma 4.11
.

We have the following decomposition of
\mathbb{T}
:

\mathbb{T}=\pi(\Phi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V))\sqcup\left(\bigsqcup_{q=1}^{m}\bigcup_{\beta\in\mathcal{L}_{j,q}}\pi(\hat{I}_{\beta})\right)

where
\pi(\Phi_{\rho}(\partial_{\infty}V))
is a finite set and
\mathcal{L}_{j,q}
is the collection of double cosets
\langle\alpha_{j}\rangle\backslash\pi_{1}\Sigma/\langle\alpha_{q}\rangle
.

Proof.

The proof follows the same argument as that of Lemma

.
∎

.

Proof of Theorem

.

Fix a boundary component
\alpha_{j}
.
Let
\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on

We first observe that

5.
Examples

In this section, we give examples of projective Anosov representations from the fundamental group of the one-holed torus
\Sigma_{1,1}
into
\text{PSL}(d,k),d\geq 2
to demonstrate that the right hand side series of Theorem
1.1
may consists of only positive terms, as well as both positive and negative terms.

5.1.
The case
d=2

Example 5.1
.

Let
k
be a non-Archimedean local field whose residue field has cardinality
3
and
\Sigma_{1,1}
be the one-holed torus. We give an example of projective Anosov representations from
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}
into
\text{PSL}(2,k)
where the right hand side series of Theorem
1.1
consists of only positive terms.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
be an oriented graph which is a wedge of two oriented circles. Embed
\Gamma_{1}
in
\Sigma_{1,1}
as in Figure
1
. Since
\Sigma_{1,1}
deformation retracts to the graph
\Gamma_{1}
, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}
acts geometrically on the universal cover of
\Gamma_{1}
, which is the 4-valent tree
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}
.

Figure 1.
\Gamma_{1}
on the one-holed torus
\Sigma_{1,1}

We now describe a projective Anosov representation
\rho_{1}\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}\to\text{PSL}(2,k)
for which the right hand side series of Theorem
1.1
consists of only positive terms. Let
a,b
be generators of
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}
.
Define

\rho_{1}(a)(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=3z
and
\rho_{1}(b)(z)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\frac{3\frac{z-2}{z-1}-2}{3\frac{z-2}{z-1}-1}
.

The identity in this case is
4=1+1+1+1
.

Example 5.2
.

Let

.
We now give an example of projective Anosov representations from
\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}
into
\text{PSL}(2,k)
where the right hand side series of Theorem

Let
\Gamma_{2}
be the oriented dumbbell graph as in Figure
2
. Note that
\Sigma_{1,1}
deformation retracts to

\Gamma_{2}\to\Sigma_{1,1}

deformation retracts to the graph
\Gamma_{2}

\pi_{1}\Sigma_{1,1}
acts geometrically on the universal cover of

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}
.

The oriented dumbell graph

Let
a
and
cbc^{-1}
be the generators of the fundamental group of the dumbbell graph. Then the boundary of the one-holed torus is represented by
acbc^{-1}a^{-1}cb^{-1}c^{-1}

8=(3-1)+(3-1)+(3-1)+(3-1)

5.2.
The case
d\geq 3

Let
d\geq 3
be an integer.
Recall the Veronese embedding
\iota\colon\text{PSL}(2,k)\to\text{PSL}(d,k)
. Let
x^{d-1},x^{d-1}y,\ldots,y^{d-1}
be a basis of the real
d
-dimensional vector space of homogeneous polynomials in 2 variables of degree
\leq d-1
. Let

g=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{bmatrix}\in{\rm SL}(2,k)
. Then
\iota(g)\in{\rm SL}(d,\mathbb{R})
is the matrix whose action on the basis elements is
\iota(g)(x^{d-i-1}y^{i})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=(ax+by)^{d-i-1}(bx+dy)^{i}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\rho\colon\pi_{1}\Sigma\to\text{PSL}(2,k)
be a projective Anosov representation. Let
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
be 4 distinct points on
\partial\pi_{1}\Sigma
. Then we have
(d-1)\log_{v}|C_{\rho}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})|=\log_{v}|C_{\iota\circ\rho}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})|
.

Proof.

We use notations from Lemma
2.2
. Let
\phi\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\zeta^{1}(x_{1})\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d})
and let
\omega\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\zeta^{d-1}(x_{3})\in\mathbb{P}(k^{d*})
.
Let
\bar{\phi}
and
\bar{\omega}
be lifts of
\phi
and
\omega
. Let
\bar{\phi}_{\iota}
be the lift of
\zeta^{1}_{\iota\circ\rho}(x_{1})
and
\bar{\omega}_{\iota}
be the lift of
\zeta^{d-1}_{\iota\circ\rho}(x_{3})
.
Then we have

\bar{\phi}_{\iota}(\bar{\omega}_{\iota})=(\bar{\phi}(\bar{\omega}))^{d-1}
. Therefore every term in the formula in Lemma
2.2
is raised to the power
(d-1)
after composing the representation
\rho
by
\iota
. Taking log of the cross ratio, the conclusion follows.
∎
