Coupling between tilts and charge carriers at polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces
Daniel Bennett, Pablo Aguado-Puente, Emilio Artacho, Nicholas Bristowe

Abstract
The phenomenological theory for the polar instability giving rise to a two-dimensional electron gas at perovskite interfaces is hereby extended to include the coupling to perovskite tilts. A Landau theory for homogeneous tilts is first explored, setting the scene for the further, more realistic Landau-Ginzburg theory describing varying tilt amplitudes across a thin film. The theory is also generalized to account for the response to an applied electric field normal to the interface, which allows a finer control on phase transitions. The conventionally described physics of a single metal-insulator transition is substantially enriched by the coupling, the model describing various scenarios with one or two transitions, possibly continuous or discontinuous. First-principles calculations permit the estimation of the parameters defining the model, which have been calculated for the interface between lanthanum aluminate and strontium titanate.

Introduction

The discovery of an insulator–metal transition at the
interface between two insulating perovskites sparked considerable interest in the
scientific community

When thin films of lanthanum aluminate
(

(
\mathrm{SrTiO_{3}}
, STO), a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) was
found to appear at the interface in order to screen the polar

This 2DEG has been found to be associated with interesting

, superconductivity
Reyren et al. 2007

and magnetism
Brinkman et al. 2007
, even at the same time

Li et al. 2011b
;
Bert et al. 2011
.
It also has potential for applications in field effect
transistors (FET)

;
Cen et al. 2008
;
Förg et al. 2012
, sensors
Xie et al. 2011
, photodetection

Irvin et al. 2010
, thermoelectrics

Pallecchi et al. 2010
;
Filippetti et al. 2012
and solar cells

Assmann et al. 2013
;
Liang et al. 2013
.

The physical origin and character of this insulator–metal
transition have been debated for many years, and discussed in
several reviews
Hwang 2006
;
Pauli and Willmott 2008
;
Huijben et al. 2009
;
Mannhart and Schlom 2010
;
Pentcheva and Pickett 2010
;
Chen et al. 2010
;
Warusawithana et al. 2013
;
Chambers 2011
;
Pentcheva et al. 2012
;
Gabay et al. 2013
;
Stemmer and James Allen 2014
;
Bristowe et al. 2014
.
In response to a polar discontinuity at the
interface, first proposed in the original papers on LAO/STO

Ohtomo et al. 2002
;
Ohtomo and Hwang 2004
, two of the most popular
theories for the appearance of the 2DEG
are indirectly supported by experimental evidence: the first
is electronic reconstruction, where the 2DEG forms via a
transfer of electrons from the valence band at the surface
of the thin film to the conduction band at the interface
in order to screen the polar discontinuity

Nakagawa et al. 2006
;
Popović et al. 2008
;
Pentcheva and Pickett 2009
.
The second achieves the same screening by means of redox
defects, where oxygen vacancies or
hydrogen adatoms, for example, form at the surface, creating
charge carriers that can move to the interface

Kalabukhov et al. 2007
;
Siemons et al. 2007
;
Herranz et al. 2007
;
Eckstein 2007
;
Shibuya et al. 2007
;
Chen et al. 2011
;
Bristowe et al. 2011
;
Lee et al. 2012
.
In Ref.
Liu et al. 2013
it was predicted that both
mechanisms are possible, depending on the experimental
conditions such as the oxygen pressure and the growth
temperature
Lee et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2015
.

The character of the transition with film thickness, i.e. whether the carriers appear continuously or discontinuously after a critical thickness, is still debated. It has been observed in experimental studies
Ohtomo and Hwang 2004
and suggested in theoretical studies
Bristowe et al. 2014
;
Yu and Zunger 2014
based on redox effects that a discontinuous transition occurs at a critical thickness
d_{\text{c}}
between 3 and 4 unit cells of LAO. However, film thickness is not a good parameter with which to make any conclusions about the order of the transition, since it is fixed for each sample and is changed discretely by a number of unit cells. Thus, it is impossible to conclude whether the transition is continuous or discontinuous, and the problem is only of theoretical interest. A more realistic approach to investigate this would be to apply an electric field, using top and back gates
Chen et al. 2016
, to a sample which is close to the critical thickness. An electric field can be used to switch the 2DEG on
Kim et al. 2015
and off
Hosoda et al. 2013
in a single sample, which is a desirable feature in practical applications. It can also enhance the properties of the 2DEG such as the superconductivity
Bert et al. 2012
;
Eerkes et al. 2013
;
Davis et al. 2018
magneto-transport
Liu et al. 2015
and optical behavior
Lei et al. 2014
. An electric field could be tuned with more precision and thus would be more suitable for studying the character of the insulator–metal transition experimentally.

A phenomenological theory at the mean-field level, which treats the carriers as a homogeneous charge distribution
\sigma
, predicts that the onset of carriers with thickness is continuous, with a critical exponent for
\sigma
of 1
Bristowe et al. 2014
. When thinking about the redox defects proposed in Ref.
Bristowe et al. 2014
, an assumption of non-interacting defects suggests a drastically discontinuous transition, switching on directly to
\sigma\sim P_{s}

Yu and Zunger 2014
,
P_{s}
being the polar discontinuity, half an electron per primitive unit cell surface area for LAO/STO; when it becomes favorable for one vacancy-carrier pair to form, it is favorable for all of them to form, giving the mentioned discontinuity at
d_{\text{c}}
. One can go beyond the mean-field level by considering the interactions between the traps
Bristowe et al. 2011
. The vacancy at the surface and carrier at the interface act like a dipole, and thus there would be dipole-dipole interactions between the vacancies. This predicts a transition which is still continuous, but with a critical exponent of
\frac{2}{3}
.

Something that has to our knowledge not yet been considered is the influence of other phase transitions, such as the antiferrodistortive (AFD) rotations of oxygen octahedra (tilts), which both LAO and STO can exhibit. Tilts compete with the polar mode, so it is reasonable to expect that they may indirectly interact with the carriers, which appear to screen the polar discontinuity. The competition between tilts and the polar mode in LAO/STO has been considered in a previous study
Gazquez et al. 2017
, although the influence of the tilts on the appearance of the carriers at the phenomenological level has not been considered.

In this paper we generalize the phenomenological model of carrier formation at polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces to account for coupling to tilts in the thin film. We show that, upon coupling to homogeneous tilts, four new distinct types of transitions are possible, depending only on the energetics of the tilts, polar discontinuity and the coupling between them. These include continuous and discontinuous transitions of the carriers, facilitated by the tilts, and both simultaneous and distinct transitions of tilts and carriers. Using first-principles calculations, we can make predictions about the type of transition which occurs at the LAO/STO interface. We then generalize the model to allow for inhomogeneous tilts in the polar thin film, using Ginzburg-Landau theory. The inhomogeneity of the tilts is determined by the correlation length in the film and the extrapolation lengths, which describe the relative energy differences between the tilts in the interior and at the surfaces. We show that two of the transitions predicted at the homogeneous level are unaffected by the inhomogeneity of the tilts, other than the values at which the transitions occur being renormalized. For the other two transitions, their character can be changed by decreasing the correlation length, leading to two entirely new types of transitions which are not possible for homogeneous tilts.

Phenomenological theory of carrier formation

Bristowe et al. 2014

Aguado-Puente et al. 2015

Bennett et al. 2020

Stengel et al. 2009

Förg et al. 2012

\sim 100

Paruch and Guyonnet 2013

2
termination, a 2DEG of carrier concentration
\sigma

The displacement fields in the film, substrate and air region between the film and tip are
D_{\text{film}}=\epsilon_{\text{film}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}+P_{s}
,
{D_{\text{sub}}=\epsilon_{\text{sub}}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}
, and
D_{\text{air}}=\epsilon_{\text{air}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{air}}
, respectively, where
P_{s}
is the polar discontinuity. We consider the displacement field in the substrate as a tunable parameter with which the field (or potential difference) applied to the system is measured. The displacement field in the film could also be used
Gazquez et al. 2017
. All vectors are normal to the interface, so vector notation is omitted. From Maxwell’s equations, the boundary condition at interface relates the displacement fields in the film and the substrate to the free charge,
\sigma
:

\begin{gathered}\left(D_{\text{sub}}-D_{\text{film}}\right)\cdot\hat{n}=-\sigma\\
\epsilon_{\text{sub}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}-\epsilon_{\text{film}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}-P_{s}=-\sigma\\
\implies\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}=\frac{\epsilon_{\text{sub}}}{\epsilon_{\text{film}}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\text{film}}}(P_{s}-\sigma)\end{gathered}\;,

(1)

where
\hat{n}
points outwards from the film. Assuming that the boundaries of the film have equal and opposite carrier concentration, the boundary condition at the surface between the film and the air region is also satisfied by enforcing finite-
D
boundary conditions:
D_{\text{sub}}=D_{\text{air}}
(not the electric fields).

The electrostatic energy of the film is
Jackson 1999

\mathcal{F}_{\text{elec}}=\int_{0}^{D_{\text{film}}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}\cdot\delta D_{\text{film}}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\text{film}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}^{2}\;.

(2)

Using Eq. (
1
) we can write the electrostatic energy in terms of
\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}
,
P_{s}
and
\sigma
:

\mathcal{F}_{\text{elec}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\epsilon_{\text{sub}}^{2}}{\epsilon_{\text{film}}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}^{2}-\frac{\epsilon_{\text{sub}}}{\epsilon_{\text{film}}}\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}\cdot(P_{s}-\sigma)+\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{\text{film}}}(P_{s}-\sigma)^{2}

(3)

The first term corresponds to the energy of the applied field in the substrate, which does not affect the carrier concentration and can be neglected (the energy of the substrate is also neglected for the same reason). The second term represents the coupling between the applied field and the polar mode and the third term is the electrostatic energy of the polar discontinuity. Writing Eq. (
3
) in terms of reduced variables
\sigma^{\prime}=\frac{\sigma}{P_{s}}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\text{sub}}}{(P_{s}/\epsilon_{\text{sub})}}
, which is the displacement field in the substrate, in units of the polar discontinuity, we get

\mathcal{F}_{\text{elec}}(\sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{2\epsilon}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\cdot(1-\sigma^{\prime})\;,

(4)

where we drop the subscript
\epsilon_{\text{film}}\to\epsilon
, since the permittivity of the substrate has been absorbed into the scale for the applied field and no longer appears in the free energy. For
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=0
, the energy is minimized when
\sigma^{\prime}=1
, i.e. the polar discontinuity is fully screened by the carriers. For LAO/STO, this corresponds to a carrier concentration of exactly half an electron per unit cell surface area. However, we have neglected the formation energy of the carriers which appear the interface. Including the cost of generating an electron-hole pair across a gap
\Delta
, the free energy is

\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}(\sigma^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\left[\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}\sigma^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\cdot(1-\sigma^{\prime})\right]\;,

(5)

where
d_{c}=\frac{{\epsilon}_{0}\Delta}{P_{s}}
is the critical thickness, above which it is favorable for carriers to appear. Minimizing with respect to
\sigma^{\prime}
gives:

\sigma^{\prime}(d,\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=1-\left(\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\;,

(6)

which implies that the carrier concentration increases with the thickness of the film, and can be tuned, and even switched on/off with an electric field. When an electric field is applied, the critical thickness for carriers to appear is

d_{\text{c}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\;,

(7)

which can be reduced or increased, depending on the sign of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
; the sign convention used for electric field is so that it is aligned with the polar discontinuity when positive. In Fig.
2
(a) we show the carrier concentration as a function of thickness at zero field, and at positive and negative values of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. When
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=0

Bristowe et al. 2014

\sigma^{\prime}
switches on at
d_{\text{c}}
and approaches 1 asymptotically from below. When a negative field is applied, the polar discontinuity enhanced, and the carrier transition occurs at a reduced thickness. In this case there is a second critical thickness where
\sigma^{\prime}
reaches 1 and saturates:

d_{\text{c},2}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=-\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\;.

(8)

II.2
Coupling to tilts

In this section we consider the effect of coupling to homogeneous tilts on the formation of carriers. The simplest way to consider this is to add the independent free energies of the tilts and carriers plus a coupling term:
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}+\mathcal{F}_{\phi}+\mathcal{F}_{\sigma\phi}
.

Bulk LAO undergoes a transition from cubic to rhombohedral at
T_{C}\sim 541^{\circ}$\mathrm{C}$

Lehnert et al. 2000
. The tilt pattern observed is, in Glazer notation
Glazer 1972
,
a^{-}a^{-}a^{-}
. For a thin film of LAO, the tilt pattern changes to
a^{-}a^{-}c^{0}
due to the tensile biaxial strain imposed by clamping to the STO substrate
Hatt and Spaldin 2010
;
Fister et al. 2014
. If a compressive biaxial strain were applied by using a substrate with a smaller lattice constant than LAO, the observed tilt pattern changes to
a^{0}a^{0}c^{-}

Hatt and Spaldin 2010
. For simplicity we assume that the thin film undergoes a simple displacive transition from untilted to tilted below some temperature
T_{C}
. Thus, the free energy of tilts can be described by a double well:

\mathcal{F}_{\phi}(\phi^{\prime},T)=\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime 4}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}\right)\phi^{\prime 2}\right]\;,

(12)

where
\phi^{\prime}=\frac{\phi}{\phi_{0}}
is the tilt angle in units of
\phi_{0}
, the bulk equilibrium tilt angle, and
X_{\phi}^{-1}
is the curvature about the minima of the double well, in units of energy per unit volume, playing the role of an inverse susceptibility.

The simplest coupling we can introduce is a biquadratic coupling between the tilts and the polar mode
Benedek and Fennie 2013
;
Salje 1992
;
Gazquez et al. 2017
:

\mathcal{F}_{\sigma\phi}=\frac{1}{2}A\phi^{\prime 2}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\text{film}}(\sigma^{\prime})}{P_{s}/\epsilon}\right)^{2}\;,

(13)

where
A
is the biquadratic coupling coefficient, in units of energy per unit volume. Combining Eqs. (
5
), (
12
) and (
13
), the total free energy is

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{F}_{\text{tot}}=\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\left[\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}\sigma^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{A}{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}\phi^{\prime 2}\right)\left(1-\sigma^{\prime}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]\\
+\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}\left[\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime 4}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}\right)\phi^{\prime 2}\right]\end{gathered}\;.

(14)

Note that there are three independent energy scales
X_{\phi}^{-1}
,
\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}
and
A
, corresponding to the tilts, the polar discontinuity, and the biquadratic coupling, respectively.

Setting
T=0
and minimizing Eq. (
14
) with respect to
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
, we get

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}&=\sqrt{1-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}}\\
\sigma^{\prime}&=1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}-\frac{d_{\text{c}}/d}{1+\frac{A}{(P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon)}\phi^{\prime 2}}\end{split}\;.

(15)

Note that when we set
A=0
, we recover the solutions of the uncoupled order parameters: Eq. (
6
) for
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}=1
. Eq. (
15
) is a pair of coupled equations which does not have analytic solutions. However, we can use physical constraints to understand the behavior of
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
upon coupling. Firstly, the square of the tilts cannot be negative:

\begin{gathered}\phi^{\prime 2}=1-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}\geq 0\\
\implies 1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}}}\leq\sigma^{\prime}\leq 1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}}}\end{gathered}\;.

(16)

Note that Eq. (
16
) provides bounds on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, since the upper bound must be greater than or equal to 0, and the lower bound must be less than or equal to 1:

-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}}}\leq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}}}\;,

(17)

although the upper bound is redundant because
{\sigma^{\prime}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}>1)<0}
, which is unphysical. The lower bound in Eq. (
16
) can also lead to situations where
\sigma^{\prime}<0
. This leads to a change in behaviour when the following condition relating the coefficients
X_{\phi}^{-1}
and
A
is held:

A=\frac{1}{2(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}}X_{\phi}^{-1}\;.

(18)

Above this line, for
\sigma^{\prime}=0
we would have
\phi^{2}<0
, so the tilts must be zero in the absence of carriers. Thus, the carriers will appear at
d_{\text{c}}
as in the uncoupled model, and the tilts will appear at some thickness, which we call
d_{\phi}
, which is greater than
d_{\text{c}}
. Below this line, there are finite tilts in the absence of carriers. In this case, carriers appear earlier at
d_{\phi}<d_{\text{c}}
, facilitated by a change in the tilts.

To summarize, above the straight line in Eq. (
18
), there are two possibilities: an uncoupled appearance of carriers at
d_{\text{c}}
and a kink in the carrier concentration facilitated by the appearance of tilts at
d_{\phi}>d_{\text{c}}
. Below the line, both order parameters appear simultaneously at
d_{\phi}<d_{\text{c}}
, i.e. there is one critical thickness which is reduced by tilting.

While the uncoupled carrier transition at
d_{\text{c}}
is always continuous, we can investigate whether the transition at
d_{\phi}
is continuous or discontinuous. If we insert the solution for
\phi^{\prime}
into
\sigma^{\prime}
in Eq. (
15
), we obtain a cubic equation,

\begin{split}f(\sigma^{\prime})=2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{3}&-\left(1+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)(1-\sigma^{\prime}-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\\
&+\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}=0\end{split}\;,

(19)

the roots of which determine the value of
\sigma^{\prime}
. We cannot obtain the roots analytically, but we can plot
f(\sigma^{\prime})
at different thicknesses and gain some insight about the transition, see Fig.
3
.

\sigma^{\prime}_{\text{min/max}}=1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{6AX_{\phi}}\left(1+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)}\;.

(20)

Again, Eq. (
20
) leads to bounds on the applied field:

-\sqrt{\frac{1}{6AX_{\phi}}\left(1+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)}\leq\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\leq 1+\sqrt{\frac{1}{6AX_{\phi}}\left(1+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)}\;,

(21)

and again the upper bound is redundant. Eq. (

) is shifted by changing

, and carriers appear when there is a root between 0 and 1. If

0<\sigma^{\prime}_{\text{min}}<1
they appear discontinuously. Thus,
\sigma^{\prime}_{\text{min}}=0
defines a boundary between first and second order transitions:

6(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}A^{2}-X_{\phi}^{-1}\left(A+\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)=0\;,

(22)

which is quadratic in
A
and has one positive solution:

A=\frac{1}{12(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}}X_{\phi}^{-1}\left(1+\sqrt{1+24(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}\frac{(P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon)}{X_{\phi}^{-1}}}\right)\;.

(23)

Above this curve, the transition at
d_{\phi}
is discontinuous and below it is continuous. Combining Eqs. (
18
) and (
23
) results in four different possible sequences of transitions, depending only on the energy densities
X_{\phi}^{-1}
,
\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}
,
A
and the field
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.

Transitions with thickness: zero field

II.4
Transitions with thickness: nonzero field

At a finite applied field, the different possible transitions with thickness are determined by the lines

\begin{gathered}A=\frac{1}{2(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}}X_{\phi}^{-1}\\
A=\frac{1}{12(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}}X_{\phi}^{-1}\left(1+\sqrt{1+24(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}\frac{(P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon)}{X_{\phi}^{-1}}}\right)\end{gathered}\;,

(26)

with a tetra-critical point

\begin{gathered}A^{*}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\\
{X_{\phi}^{-1}}^{*}=(1-\mathcal{E}^{\prime})^{2}\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\end{gathered}\;.

(27)

Interestingly, Eq. (
27
) reduces to Eq. (
25
) when
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=0,+2
, although
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=+2
is not physical. A positive field moves the tetra-critical point to the left, making region II the largest region as
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\to 1
. A negative field moves the tetra-critical point to the right. Thus, using a negative or positive field, the boundary lines can be moved, deforming the regions, and it may be possible to tune the order of the carrier and tilt transitions with thickness at a polar-nonpolar perovskite interface. The phase transition diagram is sketched in Fig.
6
for
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=-0.2
. We can see that the tetra-critical point moves to the right, changing the positions of the regions.

II.5
Transitions with temperature

In this section we investigate the possibility of inducing a carrier transition with temperature. When the order parameters are uncoupled, at a nonzero temperature we have

\begin{split}\sigma^{\prime}(d)&=1-\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}\\
\phi^{\prime}(T)&=\sqrt{1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}}\end{split}\;,

(28)

where we set
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=0
for simplicity. For nonzero
A
, minimizing Eq. (
14
) gives

\begin{split}\phi^{\prime}&=\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}\right)-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}}\\
\sigma^{\prime}&=1-\frac{1}{1+\frac{A}{(P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon)}\phi^{\prime 2}}\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}\end{split}\;.

(29)

If we require that
\phi^{2}\geq 0
as before, we get

1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}(T)}}\leq\sigma^{\prime}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}(T)}}\;,

(30)

where
X_{\phi}(T)=\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}\right)^{-1}X_{\phi}
. As in Eq. (
16
), the inequality on the left hand side can lead to situations where
\phi^{2}<0
. This implies that there is an additional critical temperature,
T_{C}^{\prime}
:

T_{C}^{\prime}\equiv T_{C}\left(1-2AX_{\phi}\right)\;.

(31)

This is the reduced critical temperature of the thin film, a phenomenon which is typically seen in phase transitions when going from bulk to thin films
Lubensky and Rubin 1975
;
Kretschmer and Binder 1979
;
De Gennes 2018
;
Chandra and Littlewood 2007
. Interestingly, we have
T_{C}^{\prime}=0
when

A>\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}\;,

(32)

i.e. there is no tilt transition at all. This is the same straight line obtained for transitions with thickness and applied field at zero temperature. However, it has a different meaning for transitions with temperature: below the line there is a tilt transition at a reduced critical temperature
T_{C}^{\prime}
, and above the line there is no tilt transition. There is a second transition temperature,
T_{C}^{\prime\prime}
, below which the carriers appear. Inserting
\phi^{\prime}
into
\sigma^{\prime}
in Eq. (
29
) and letting
\sigma^{\prime}(T_{C}^{\prime\prime})=0
, we get

T_{C}^{\prime\prime}\equiv T_{C}^{\prime}-T_{C}\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\left(\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}-1\right)

(33)

We can see that
T_{C}^{\prime\prime}<T_{C}^{\prime}
for sub-critical films, and
T_{C}^{\prime\prime}=T_{C}^{\prime}
when
d=d_{c}
. Thus, two transitions are possible by decreasing temperature in sub-critical films and only one transition is possible otherwise. Additionally, it is possible for the transition at
T_{C}^{\prime\prime}
to be first or second order. Inserting
\phi^{\prime}
into
\sigma^{\prime}
as before, we obtain another cubic equation in
\sigma^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}f_{T}(\sigma^{\prime})=\underbrace{2AX_{\phi}}_{\Lambda}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{3}&-\underbrace{\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)}_{\Gamma(T)}(1-\sigma^{\prime})\\
&+\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}=0\end{split}\;,

(34)

with extrema

\sigma^{\prime}_{\text{max/min}}(T)=1\pm\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(T)}{3\Lambda}}\;.

(35)

In the previous section, the positions of the extrema were not dependent on the quantity that induced the transitions. Assuming the transition occurs at some temperature
T^{*}
, we can determine the character of the transition by requiring that
f_{T}(\sigma_{\text{min}}(T^{*}))=0
,

\begin{split}-\Lambda\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(T^{*})}{3\Lambda}}\right)^{3}&+\Gamma(T^{*})\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma(T^{*})}{3\Lambda}}\right)\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}=0\\
\implies&\Gamma(T^{*})=\sqrt[3]{\frac{27}{4}\left(\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\frac{d_{c}}{d}\right)^{2}\Lambda}\end{split}\;,

(36)

and also requiring that
\sigma_{\text{min}}(T^{*})>0
,

\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}<\Lambda\;.

(37)

Thus, we get

A>\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}\left(\frac{P_{s}^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)X_{\phi}^{-1}}

(38)

The lines in Eqs. (
32
) and (
38
) form a diagram for transitions with temperature, shown in Fig.

\frac{d}{d_{\text{c}}}
, so four different types of carrier transitions are possible. Plots of the order parameters for the four different scenarios are shown in Fig.

II.6
Transitions with applied field

For transitions with applied field, note from the left-hand side of Eq. (
16
) that if
\sigma=0
, there is a critical field value at which the tilts switch on or off:

\mathcal{E}_{\phi}=1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2AX_{\phi}}}\geq 0

(39)

We get
\mathcal{E}_{\phi}=0
when
A=\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}
, which is the same condition for transitions with thickness at zero field. In order to determine whether transitions with applied field are continuous or discontinuous, we must solve Eq. (
19

f(\sigma^{\prime}_{\text{min}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime*}))=0
for
\mathcal{E}^{\prime*}
in a similar manner to the previous section. The boundary between continuous and discontinuous transitions of the tilts with applied field is:

X_{\phi}^{-1}=\frac{27}{2}\frac{\left(d_{\text{c}}/d\right)^{2}}{\left(1+\frac{P_{s}^{2}/\epsilon}{A}\right)^{3}}\frac{1}{A}\;,

(40)

which in general depends on the ratio
\frac{d}{d_{\text{c}}}
. For
\frac{d}{d_{\text{c}}}=1

III
Coupling to inhomogeneous tilts

Figure 11:
Sketch of inhomogeneous tilts in a thin film grown on a substrate, e.g. LAO/STO, and the effect of
(a)
:
\lambda
, the correlation length of the tilts in the LAO film
(b)
:
\delta_{\text{surface}}
, the relative difference in the energy of the tilts at the surface with respect to the bulk and
(c)
:
\delta_{\text{interface}}
, the relative difference in the energy of the tilts at the interface with respect to the bulk. The effect of the energetics of the tilts in the substrate may be absorbed into
\delta_{\text{interface}}
.

We can allow for inhomogeneous tilts by generalizing from a Landau theory to a Ginzburg-Landau theory, i.e. the free energy is expanded in powers of both the order parameter and its gradient. We do this following the methodology which has been used to describe semi-infinite ferromagnetic
Lubensky and Rubin 1975
, ferroelectric
Kretschmer and Binder 1979
;
Chandra and Littlewood 2007
;
Luk’yanchuk et al. 2009
and superconducting
De Gennes 2018
systems in which the order parameter is inhomogeneous due to the presence of a free surface. Considering only the tilts in the thin film, Eq. (
12
) becomes an integral over the volume of the film:

{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\phi}^{\text{GL}}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\nabla\phi^{\prime}(\bm{r})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{r})^{4}-\frac{1}{2}\phi^{\prime}(\bm{r})^{2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V}\;,

(41)

where
{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}\equiv\mathcal{F}/\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}
and
\lambda>0
is the correlation length of the tilts, which sets the length scale for the variations of the tilts, see Fig.
11
(a). The correlation length depends in general on the temperature of the system, but here we assume a fixed value for simplicity. Eq. (
41
) is written so that each term in the integrand is dimensionless, and the free energy is in units of
\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}
, the energy scale of the homogeneous tilts.

Next, we reintroduce the carriers. We take the order parameter to be the carrier concentration averaged over an in-plane unit cell:
\sigma^{\prime}\equiv\left<\sigma^{\prime}\right>=\frac{1}{A}\int\sigma^{\prime}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{A}
. If we assume that the in-plane variance in
\sigma^{\prime}
is small, so that
\left<(1-\sigma^{\prime})\right>^{2}\approx(1-\left<\sigma^{\prime}\right>)^{2}
, then
{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\sigma}^{\text{GL}}={\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\sigma}
. It should be noted that this is a significant assumption, especially in the low carrier regime.

For inhomogeneous tilts, the biquadratic coupling term becomes

{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\sigma\phi}^{\text{GL}}=\frac{1}{V}\int\frac{1}{2}(2AX_{\phi})\phi^{\prime}(\textbf{r})^{2}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V}\;,

(42)

which, since we assume the in-plane variance in
\sigma^{\prime}
is small, reduces to

{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}^{\text{GL}}_{\sigma\phi}=\frac{1}{2}(2AX_{\phi})(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\left<\phi^{\prime 2}\right>\;,

(43)

where

\left<\phi^{\prime 2}\right>=\frac{1}{V}\int\phi^{\prime}(\textbf{r})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V}

(44)

is the mean of the square of the tilt throughout the film.

Under these assumptions, the free energy can be minimized with respect to
\sigma^{\prime}
as in the homogeneous case, yielding

\sigma^{\prime}=1-\frac{1}{1+\frac{A}{P_{0}^{2}/\epsilon}\left<\phi^{\prime 2}\right>}\left(\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\;,

(45)

which is the same as the second line of Eq. (
15
), but with
{\phi^{\prime 2}\to\left<\phi^{\prime 2}\right>}
. We only consider transitions with thickness for illustrative purposes, although it would be simple enough to include an applied field as we did in the previous section.

For the tilts, we have

\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\phi}^{\text{GL}}+{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}_{\sigma\phi}^{\text{GL}}=&\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\nabla\phi^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime 4}\right.\\
&\left.\qquad\quad-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right)\phi^{\prime 2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V}\end{split}\;.

(46)

Following a similar treatment of ferroelectric thin films with inhomogeneous polarization
Chandra and Littlewood 2007
, we split the free energy into interior and surface contributions:
{\mathcal{F}^{\text{GL}}=\mathcal{F}^{\text{GL}}_{\text{interior}}+\mathcal{F}^{\text{GL}}_{\text{boundary}}}
, where
\mathcal{F}^{\text{GL}}_{\phi,\text{boundary}}
is given in terms of an expansion of a local order parameter at the boundaries. First, we use the reverse product rule to rewrite the gradient term:

(\nabla\phi^{\prime})^{2}=\nabla\cdot(\phi^{\prime}\nabla\phi^{\prime})-\phi^{\prime}\nabla^{2}\phi^{\prime}\;,

(47)

then we use Stokes’ theorem on the first term in Eq. (
47
):

\begin{split}\int(\nabla\phi^{\prime})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V&=\int\left[\nabla\cdot(\phi^{\prime}\nabla\phi^{\prime})-\phi^{\prime}\nabla^{2}\phi^{\prime}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V\\
&=\int\phi^{\prime}\nabla\phi^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}S-\int\phi^{\prime}\nabla^{2}\phi^{\prime}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}V\end{split}\;,

(48)

where for a thin film on a substrate the surface integral has two terms: one at the free surface and one at the interface with the substrate:

\begin{gathered}{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}^{\text{GL}}=\frac{1}{V}\int_{V}\bigg[\frac{1}{4}\phi^{\prime 4}-\frac{1}{2}\left(1-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right)\phi^{\prime 2}\\
-\lambda^{2}\phi^{\prime}\nabla^{2}\phi^{\prime}\bigg]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{V}\\
+\frac{1}{V}\int_{\text{s}}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{\prime}\right)\phi^{\prime}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{S}\\
+\frac{1}{V}\int_{\text{i}}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{\prime}\right)\phi^{\prime}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{S}\end{gathered}\;,

(49)

where s and i refer to the surface and interface, respectively. Note that the surface free energies are also in units of
\frac{1}{2}X_{\phi}^{-1}
, and the surface integrals are dimensionless.

Next we claim that there must be a difference in the energies at the boundaries compared to the interior of the film
Lubensky and Rubin 1975
;
De Gennes 2018
;
Chandra and Littlewood 2007
, which can be described using an expansion of the local order parameters at the boundaries:

\begin{split}{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}^{\text{GL}}_{\phi,\text{boundary}}&=\frac{1}{V}\int_{\text{s}}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{\prime}\right)\phi^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\text{s}}\phi^{\prime 2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{S}\\
&+\frac{1}{V}\int_{\text{i}}\left[\lambda^{2}\left(\hat{n}\cdot\nabla\phi^{\prime}\right)\phi^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\text{i}}\phi^{\prime 2}\right]\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{S}\end{split}\;.

(50)

The quadratic coefficient of the surface order parameter describes the relative difference between the energy of the tilts at the surface and the energy per unit volume of the tilts in the bulk, and therefore must have units of length. We call this the extrapolation length,
\delta
.
\delta_{\text{s}}
and
\delta_{\text{i}}
are the surface and interface extrapolation lengths, respectively, which describe the difference in energy at the surface and interface with respect to the bulk, see Figs.
11
(b) and (c). In general, higher order terms could be included in this expansion, but we assume that the leading term is the quadratic one
Chandra and Littlewood 2007
. Also, the boundaries may have a temperature dependence which differs from the bulk, which could lead to a change in sign of the extrapolation lengths.

Minimizing the total free energy in the bulk and at both boundaries, we get

\begin{gathered}\phi^{\prime 3}-\left(1-2AX_{\phi}(1-\sigma^{\prime})^{2}\right)\phi^{\prime}-\lambda^{2}\nabla^{2}\phi^{\prime}=0\\
(\hat{n}\cdot(\nabla\phi^{\prime}))+\frac{\delta_{\text{i}}}{\lambda^{2}}\phi^{\prime}=0,\qquad z=0\\
(\hat{n}\cdot(\nabla\phi^{\prime}))+\frac{\delta_{\text{s}}}{\lambda^{2}}\phi^{\prime}=0,\qquad z=d\\
\sigma^{\prime}=1-\frac{1}{1+\frac{A}{P_{0}^{2}/\epsilon}\left<\phi^{\prime 2}\right>}\left(\frac{d_{\text{c}}}{d}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\end{gathered}\;.

(51)

This is the generalization of Eq. (
15
) but with inhomogeneous tilts. The expression for
\sigma^{\prime}
is almost identical, but with the square of the tilt replaced with the mean of the square of the tilt throughout the film.
\phi^{\prime}
is described by a second order differential equation plus two boundary conditions, one for the free surface and one for the interface. These boundary conditions are referred to as Robin boundary conditions, i.e. a linear combination of the tilt and its gradient at each boundary. The equations for
\phi^{\prime}
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are again self-consistent, making them difficult to solve. In the previous section we used physical constraints to determine the character of the carrier and tilt transitions under various conditions, summarized in Fig.
4
, but this would be difficult or impossible for Eq. (
51
). Thus, our only option is obtaining numerical solutions, solving for
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\phi^{\prime}
iteratively until both are converged below a suitable tolerance. This approach was used in the previous section to obtain the order parameter plots shown in Figs.
5
,
8
and
10
.

Eq. (
51
) is solved as follows: the tilt profile inside the film is obtained by solving the first three lines using finite difference methods. The mean of the tilt squared in the film is then used to calculate the carrier concentration, which is re-inserted into the ODE. The two are solved self-consistently until their relative changes between successive iterations are below a suitable tolerance.

For a given set of system parameters, we can obtain order parameter plots as a function of thickness or applied field, similar to Figs.
5
,
8
and
10
. The main difference with the inhomogeneous tilts is that
\lambda
,
\delta_{\text{s}}
and
\delta_{\text{i}}
need to be specified, in addition to
X_{\phi}^{-1}
,
A
and
d_{\text{c}}
. Thus, for inhomogeneous tilts, Fig.
4
becomes five dimensional, and systemically navigating such a diagram would be impractical.

It is also difficult to estimate
\lambda
,
\delta_{\text{s}}
and
\delta_{\text{i}}
. Previous studies have fixed
\lambda
to a small number of unit cells
Kretschmer and Binder 1979
. In Ref.
Gazquez et al. 2017
,
\lambda
is estimated to be around 1-2 unit cells in bulk LAO from calculations of the dispersion of the phonon branch corresponding to the
a^{-}a^{-}c^{0}
tilt. To our knowledge, it is not possible to directly measure the extrapolation lengths. In principle, one could fit measurements of tilts in a thin film, either from experiment or first-principles calculations, to Eq. (
51
), but the tilts at the boundaries are strongly affected by both the correlation length and the extrapolation length, which would lead to overfitting.

It is helpful to note that, since we only consider indirect coupling, the carrier concentration depends only on the mean of the square of the tilts, and not on the specific shape of the tilts. Thus, we can understand the influence of the inhomogeneity of the tilts by fixing the extrapolation lengths and allowing the correlation length to change.

IV
Discussion and Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a phenomenological theory which considers the influence of homogeneous tilts on the appearance of carriers at polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces. We show that, upon coupling, four new types of transitions of tilts and carriers with film thickness or applied field are possible, depending only on the energetics of the tilts, the polar discontinuity, and the biquadratic coupling between the tilts and the polar mode. These include the simultaneous appearance of tilts and carriers at a reduced thickness compared to the uncoupled theory, which can be either continuous or discontinuous, and separate transitions, with carriers appearing first and then tilts, the second transition being either continuous or discontinuous.

First-principles calculations of bulk LAO were performed to predict the character of the transitions at the LAO/STO interface. For various exchange-correlation functionals, all of the calculations predicted that at zero temperature and for homogeneous tilts, LAO/STO lies in region II of Fig.
4
, i.e. a single continuous transition of both tilts and carriers. We also investigated the possibility of tuning the character of the transition via biaxial strain by changing the in-plane lattice constant by up to
\pm
1% and repeating the calculations. We found that the position of the point in Fig.
4
can be changed by adjusting the strain, but not by enough to change the character of the transitions. However, in general, if a system were closer to a boundary between regions, it may be possible to change the regions using biaxial strain. Additionally, if compressive strain is applied to
a_{\text{LAO}}
, the tilt pattern changes to
a^{0}a^{0}c^{-}

Hatt and Spaldin 2010
, which would change the competition with the polar mode. This could be achieved by using a nonpolar perovskite with a smaller lattice constant than LAO as the substrate material.

In addition to changing the strain and the tilt pattern as mentioned above, changing the materials which form the polar-nonpolar interface can also change the chemistry at the interface, and hence the polar discontinuity. Different interfaces have been investigated experimentally
Li et al. 2015
;
Kumar et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2016
;
Moetakef et al. 2011
, computationally
Fan et al. 2014
;
Maznichenko et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2018
, and high throughput searches for new interfaces have been done
Yang et al. 2016
. Carrier transitions have also been observed at spinel-perovskite interfaces
Chen et al. 2013
;
Niu et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2020
. Thus, it may be possible to observe a wide variety of behavior in the carrier transitions at different polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces for different combinations of materials. More specifically, by estimating where different systems would lie on Fig.
4
, it may be possible to establish an atlas of transitions across different possible polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces.

We also showed how the appearance of carriers can be tuned via temperature through the temperature dependence of the tilts. At a finite temperature, the curvature of the tilt double well is
{X_{\phi}^{-1}(T)=X_{\phi}^{-1}\left(1-\frac{T}{T_{C}}\right)}
, so increasing the temperature would move any point on the diagram in Fig.
4
to the left. We would expect the dependence of the biquadratic coupling term
A(T)
on temperature to be similar but weaker. Thus, it may be possible to move a system from one point in the diagram to another if the initial point is sufficiently close to a boundary. In Ref.
Fister et al. 2014
, when grown on a substrate of cubic STO, LAO was found to undergo a transition from untilted to tilted below
695^{\circ}$\mathrm{C}$
for a thickness of 24 unit cells and
540^{\circ}$\mathrm{C}$
for a thickness of 9 unit cells, where both samples were under the same strain conditions. Because both films were supercritical there was no carrier transition associated with the tilt transitions, but their observations are in agreement with our prediction of a second transition temperature,
T_{C}^{\prime\prime}
, which is sensitive to the ratio
\frac{d}{d_{\text{c}}}
. Assuming that the films were sufficiently thick so that the effect of gradients are negligible, this may provide evidence for the additional transition temperature arising from the coupling between tilts and carriers.

We also investigated the influence of the inhomogeneity of the tilts on the coupling between tilts and carriers. Upon coupling to inhomogeneous tilts, the diagram in Fig.
4
becomes five dimensional, with additional axes for the correlation length of the tilts in the polar film, and extrapolation lengths for the interface and the free surface of the film. Practically, it would be very demanding to explore this five dimensional space numerically, and to our knowledge, it can’t be done analytically. However, since the biqaudratic coupling between tilts and carriers only depends on the mean of the tilt squared throughout the film, we can examine the influence of inhomogeneous tilts by examining the effect of the correlation length on transitions in each region in Fig.
4
, with the rest of the parameters fixed. We found that, for the regions corresponding to the separate appearances of tilts and then carriers, the order of the transitions is unaffected by the correlation length, except for the value of the second transition shifting slightly. For the regions corresponding to simultaneous transitions, we found that a finite correlation length can actually change the character of the transition, and can lead to two new types of transitions which are not possible for homogeneous tilts, where the tilts appear before carriers, either continuously or discontinuously.

Due to the large number of system parameters required, many of which cannot be directly measured, it remains difficult to conclusively determine the order of the carrier transition at polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces. However, we have shown that, even using a phenomenological description, an extremely rich variety of behavior is possible for the appearance of carriers at polar-nonpolar perovskite interfaces. This includes first-order transitions, which have been observed experimentally
Ohtomo and Hwang 2004
, and have been claimed to occur in other theoretical studies
Yu and Zunger 2014
, but without a detailed explanation.
