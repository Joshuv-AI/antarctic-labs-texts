On 1 1 d quadratic Klein-Gordon equations with a potential and symmetries
Pierre Germain, Fabio Pusateri, Katherine Zhiyuan Zhang

Abstract
. This paper is a continuation of the previous work [ 5 ] by the first two authors. We focus on 1 1 dimensional quadratic Klein-Gordon equations with a potential, under some assumptions that are less general than [ 5 ] , but allow us to present some simplifications in the proof of global existence with decay for small solutions. In particular, we can propagate a stronger control on a basic L 2 L^{2} -weighted type norm while providing some shorter and less technical proofs for some of the arguments.

2.1.
Linear scattering theory

Define
f_{+}(x,\xi)
and
f_{-}(x,\xi)
by the requirements that

\displaystyle Hf_{\pm}:=(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V)f_{\pm}=\xi^{2}f_{\pm},\quad\mbox{for all $x\in{\mathbb{R}}$, \quad and}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f_{+}(x,\xi)\sim e^{ix\xi}&\mbox{as $x\to\infty$}\\
f_{-}(x,\xi)\sim e^{-ix\xi}&\mbox{as $x\to-\infty$}.\end{array}\right.

Define further

\displaystyle m_{+}(x,\xi)=e^{-i\xi x}f_{+}(x,\xi)\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{-}(x,\xi)=e^{i\xi x}f_{-}(x,\xi),

(2.3)

so that
m_{\pm}
is a solution of

\partial_{x}^{2}m_{\pm}\pm 2i\xi\partial_{x}m_{\pm}=Vm_{\pm},\qquad m_{\pm}(x,\xi)\to 1\;\mbox{as $x\to\pm\infty$}.

(2.4)

These satisfy the estimates

\begin{array}[]{ll}\left|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}(m_{\pm}(x,\xi)-1)\right|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-N}\langle\xi\rangle^{-1-\beta}&\mbox{if $\pm x>-1$},\\
\left|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}m_{\pm}(x,\xi)\right|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{1+\beta}\langle\xi\rangle^{-1-\beta}&\mbox{if $\pm x<1$}.\end{array}

(2.5)

Denote
T(\xi)
and
R_{\pm}(\xi)
respectively the
transmission

and
reflection
coefficients associated to the potential
V
.
These coefficients are such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&f_{+}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{T(\xi)}f_{-}(x,-\xi)+\frac{R_{-}(\xi)}{T(\xi)}f_{-}(x,\xi),\\
&f_{-}(x,\xi)=\frac{1}{T(\xi)}f_{+}(x,-\xi)+\frac{R_{+}(\xi)}{T(\xi)}f_{+}(x,\xi),\end{split}

(2.6)

and are explicitly given by the formulas

\displaystyle\begin{split}&T(\xi)=\frac{2i\xi}{2i\xi-\int V(x)m_{\pm}(x,\xi)\,dx},\\
&R_{\pm}(\xi)=\frac{\int e^{\mp 2i\xi x}V(x)m_{\mp}(x,\xi)\,dx}{2i\xi-\int V(x)m_{\pm}(x,\xi)\,dx}.\end{split}

(2.7)

These formulas are only valid for
\xi\neq 0
a priori,
but
T
and
R_{\pm}
can be extended to be smooth functions on the whole real line.
From (
2.7
) and (
2.5
) we can see that

\begin{split}&|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}(T(\xi)-1)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-1-\beta},\qquad|\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}R_{\pm}(\xi)|\lesssim\langle\xi\rangle^{-N}.\end{split}

(2.8)

Finally, the scattering matrix

S(\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}T(\xi)&R_{+}(\xi)\\
R_{-}(\xi)&T(\xi)\end{pmatrix}

is unitary.

\displaystyle T(0)=\frac{2a}{1+a^{2}},\qquad R_{+}(0)=\frac{1-a^{2}}{1+a^{2}},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad R_{-}(0)=\frac{a^{2}-1}{1+a^{2}}.

(2.10)

2.3.
Flat and distorted Fourier transform

The normalization we adopt for the flat Fourier transform is

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}\phi(\xi)=\widehat{\phi}(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-i\xi x}\phi(x)\,dx.

Its inverse is given by

\widehat{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{i\xi x}\phi(\xi)\,d\xi.

We now define the wave functions associated to

\displaystyle\psi(x,\xi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T(\xi)f_{+}(x,\xi)&\mbox{for $\xi\geq 0$}\\
\\
T(-\xi)f_{-}(x,-\xi)&\mbox{for $\xi<0$}.\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}\phi(\xi)=\widetilde{\phi}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{\psi(x,\xi)}\phi(x)\,dx.

(2.16)

It is self-adjoint and has the inverse

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\phi(x)=\int\psi(x,\xi)\phi(\xi)\,d\xi.

(2.17)

Let

Define

where

H=\mathbf{1}_{+}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}

\chi_{+}(x)=\chi_{-}(-x).

With

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mbox{for}\quad\xi<0\qquad\sqrt{2\pi}\psi(x,\xi)&=\chi_{-}(x)T(-\xi)m_{-}(x,-\xi)e^{ix\xi}\\

&\sqrt{2\pi}K_{o}(x,\xi):=T(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi}-m_{+}(x,-\xi)e^{-ix\xi}-R(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{i\xi x},\end{split}

(2.26)

We also have

\displaystyle K_{o}(x,0)=0,

(2.27)

and can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(x)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[\chi_{+}(x)K_{o}(x,\xi)-\chi_{-}(x)K_{o}(-x,\xi)\big]\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi.\end{split}

(2.28)

•

When
f
is even, and the zero energy resonance is odd, we have the formulas

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\widetilde{f}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\overline{\psi_{e}(x,\xi)}f(x)\,dx,\\
&f(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\psi_{e}(x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi,\end{split}

(2.29)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\psi_{e}(x,\xi):=\mathbf{1}_{+}(\xi)\chi_{+}(x)K_{e}(x,\xi)+\mathbf{1}_{-}(\xi)\chi_{-}(x)K_{e}(-x,-\xi),\\
&\sqrt{2\pi}K_{e}(x,\xi):=T(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi}+m_{+}(x,-\xi)e^{-ix\xi}+R(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{i\xi x}.\end{split}

(2.30)

We also have

\displaystyle\quad K_{e}(x,0)=0.

(2.31)

and can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}f(x)&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big[\chi_{+}(x)K_{e}(x,\xi)+\chi_{-}(x)K_{e}(-x,\xi)\big]\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi.\end{split}

(2.32)

Note that there is a slight asymmetry between the cutoffs in

in the

Proof.

The formulas (

)-(
2.26
) and (

)-(

) and using (

)-(

),
as well as

.
We show this in the case when

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big[\chi_{+}(x)T(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi}+\chi_{-}(x)(m_{-}(x,-\xi)e^{i\xi x}+R(\xi)m_{-}(x,\xi)e^{-i\xi x})\big]^{\ast}\,f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\chi_{+}(x)\big[T(\xi)m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi}-m_{-}(-x,-\xi)e^{-i\xi x}-R(\xi)m_{-}(-x,\xi)e^{i\xi x}\big]^{\ast}\,f(x)\,dx

3.
Linear decay estimates

Let

\displaystyle B:=\sqrt{-\partial_{xx}+V+1}.

(3.1)

We have the following standard decay estimate:

Proposition 3.1

(Pointwise decay)
.

Assume that either
V
is generic, or that
V
is exceptional with an
even, resp. odd, zero energy resonance and that
f
is odd, resp. even.
Then, we have

\displaystyle{\|e^{itB}P_{c}f\|}_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-1/2}{\|\langle\xi\rangle^{3/2}\widetilde{f}\|}_{L^{\infty}}+\langle t\rangle^{-11/20}{\|\langle\xi\rangle\partial_{\xi}\widetilde{f}\|}_{L^{2}}+\langle t\rangle^{-7/12}{\|f\|}_{H^{4}}.

(3.2)

This statement corresponds to Proposition 3.11 in
[
5
]
,
where the factor of
\langle t\rangle^{-11/20}
, which is not optimal,
is replacing the factor of
\langle t\rangle^{-3/4+\beta\gamma}

(where
\beta,\gamma
were chosen so that
\beta\gamma=1/4-
);
this latter was present in the cited work due to the “bad” frequencies
\pm\sqrt{3}

that required a special treatment in
[
5
]
, which is not the case in the present article.
We also refer the reader to Lemma 2.2 of
[
10
]
where a linear estimate
similar to (
3.2
) is proved (for the case
V=0
); the slightly different rate
of decay in front of the weighted
L^{2}
norm is due to the different handling
of the high frequencies.

Next, we provide an improved local decay estimate.
It will not be used in this work, but we include it for the sake of completeness and future reference.
Such improved decay is one of the keys to nonlinear stability,
but it will be more convenient for us to take another route to proving nonlinear bounds,
working in (distorted) Fourier space, rather than using physical space estimates.

Proposition 3.2

(Local decay)
.

Assume that either
V
is generic, or that
V
is exceptional with an
even, resp. odd, zero energy resonance and that
f
is odd, resp. even.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\big\|\langle x\rangle^{-2}e^{itB}P_{c}f\big\|}_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{-1}\Big({\big\|\partial_{\xi}\widetilde{f}\big\|}_{L^{1}_{\xi}}+{\big\|\widetilde{f}\big\|}_{L^{1}_{\xi}}+{\|f\|}_{H^{1}_{x}}\Big).\end{split}

(3.3)

Remark 3.3
.

One should notice that, in the more general case treated in
[
5
]
,
or in the exceptional case without the present symmetry assumptions,
decay rates as strong as (
3.3
) are not available.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

We are first going to give
the details of the proof in the case of an exceptional potential with even resonance
and with
f
odd, and we will then indicate how the other cases can be treated similarly.

Let us bound the first quantity on the left-hand side of (
3.3
).
In view of Sobolev’s embedding, we may assume
t\geq 1
.
We look at different cases depending on the parity.

The case of odd
f
.
By (
2.25
)-(
2.28
), we can write:

\displaystyle\big(e^{itB}f\big)(x)=\chi_{+}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}K_{o}(x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi-\chi_{-}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}K_{o}(-x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi.

We will only bound the first term above, since the other one can be treated identically.
Observe that we can write (
2.26
) as

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi}K_{o}(x,\xi)=m_{+}(x,-\xi)2i\operatorname{sin}(x\xi)+[m_{+}(x,\xi)-m_{+}(x,-\xi)]e^{ix\xi}

\displaystyle+(T(\xi)-1-R(\xi))m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi}.

This expression makes the cancellations even more apparent, leading,
with the help of (
2.5
) and (
2.11
), to the estimates

\displaystyle\begin{split}&|K_{o}(x,\xi)|\lesssim\mathrm{min}(|x\xi|,1)+\mathrm{min}(\xi,1),\qquad|\partial_{\xi}K_{o}(x,\xi)|\lesssim\langle x\rangle,\\
&\big|\partial_{\xi}\big(\xi^{-1}K_{o}(x,\xi)\big)\big|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{2},\qquad|\xi|\leq 1,\end{split}

(3.4)

valid in the support of
\chi_{+}(x)
.
Using that
K_{o}(x,0)=0=\widetilde{f}(0)
,
we can integrate by parts through the identity
e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}=\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{it\xi}\partial_{\xi}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\chi_{+}(x)}{\langle x\rangle^{2}}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}K_{o}(x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\frac{1}{t\langle x\rangle^{2}}\int\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{|\xi|}|K_{o}(x,\xi)||\partial_{\xi}\widetilde{f}(\xi)|\,d\xi+\frac{1}{t\langle x\rangle^{2}}\int|\widetilde{f}(\xi)|\left|\partial_{\xi}\left[\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{\xi}K_{o}(x,\xi)\right]\right|\,d\xi.

Using the bounds (
3.4
)
this is

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t\langle x\rangle^{2}}\int\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{|\xi|}(\mathrm{min}(|x\xi|,1)+\mathrm{min}(\xi,1))|\partial_{\xi}\widetilde{f}(\xi)|\,d\xi+\frac{1}{t}\int|\widetilde{f}(\xi)|\,d\xi

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t}\left[{\|\partial_{\xi}\widetilde{f}\|}_{L^{1}}+{\|\widetilde{f}\|}_{L^{1}}\right].

The case of even
f
.
When
f
is even we can use (
2.29
)-(
2.32
) to write

\displaystyle e^{itB}f=\chi_{+}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}K_{e}(x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi+\chi_{-}(x)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}K_{e}(-x,\xi)\widetilde{f}(\xi)\,d\xi.

Similarly to the odd case, also here we have cancellation at zero (see (
2.31
)).
It suffices again to just look at the first integral. Writing

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\pi}K_{e}(x,\xi)=-m_{+}(x,\xi)2i\sin(x\xi)+[m_{+}(x,-\xi)-m_{+}(x,\xi)]e^{-ix\xi}

\displaystyle+(T(\xi)+1+R(\xi))m_{+}(x,\xi)e^{ix\xi},

we see that estimates like (
3.4
) hold for
K_{e}
as well,
and we can apply the same argument above.

The case of generic
V
.
When
V
is generic we have
T(0)=0
and
R_{\pm}(0)=-1

and arguments similar to those above can be applied to the general formula (
2.16
)
with (
2.19
)-(
2.20
).
∎

The quadratic spectral distribution

The odd case

Proposition 4.1

(The odd case)
.

Let

•

(4.3)

\displaystyle\varphi^{\ast}(p,\eta,\sigma)=\varphi_{\leq-D}\big(pR(\eta,\sigma)\big),\quad R(\eta,\sigma)=\frac{\langle\eta\rangle\langle\sigma\rangle}{\langle\eta\rangle+\langle\sigma\rangle}.

•

(4.6)

Remark 4.2

(About the formula (

5.1.
The equation on the profile

Consider

a solution of the quadratic Klein-Gordon equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}^{2}u+(-\partial_{x}^{2}+V+1)u=a(x)u^{2},\qquad(u,u_{t})(t=0)=(u_{0},u_{1}).\end{split}

(5.1)

Note that we are disregarding the cubic terms from (
1.1
) since they are lower order;
moreover, cubic terms that are more complicated than
u^{3}
,
will appear after normal forms, and will be treated in detail in what follows.

In order to make the equation first order in time, we first define

\displaystyle v=\big(\partial_{t}-iB\big)u,\qquad B=\sqrt{-\partial_{x}^{2}+V+1},

(5.2)

which solves

\big(\partial_{t}+iB\big)v=a(x)u^{2}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\big(\partial_{t}+i\langle\xi\rangle\big)\widetilde{v}=\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(a(x)u^{2})

Next, we filter by the linear evolution to obtain the profile

g(t,\cdot)=e^{itB}v(t,\cdot)

which solves

\partial_{t}\widetilde{g}(t,\xi)=e^{it\langle\xi\rangle}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}(a(x)u^{2}).

Using the quadratic spectral distribution described in Proposition

we write this explicitly as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\widetilde{g}(t,\xi)&=-\sum_{\iota_{1},\iota_{2}}\iota_{1}\iota_{2}\iint e^{it\Phi_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)}\widetilde{g}_{\iota_{1}}(t,\eta)\widetilde{g}_{\iota_{2}}(t,\sigma)\,\frac{1}{4\langle\eta\rangle\langle\sigma\rangle}\mu_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}^{o}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)\,d\eta\,d\sigma.\\
&\Phi_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)=\langle\xi\rangle-\iota_{1}\langle\eta\rangle-\iota_{2}\langle\sigma\rangle.\end{split}

(5.4)

For convenience we are omitting the limits of integration
\eta,{\sigma}>0

and, from now on, we will also omit the apex

from
\mu_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}^{o}
and similar expression
such as
\mu^{o,S}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}
.

5.2.

Some simple calculations show that

does not vanish on the support of the distribution

defined in (
4.2

In other words, the corresponding interaction is not resonant,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}T_{\iota_{1},\iota_{2}}(g,g)(t)&:=\iint e^{it\Phi_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)}\widetilde{g}(t,\eta)\widetilde{g}(t,\sigma)\mathfrak{m}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)\,d\eta\,d\sigma\\
&\mathfrak{m}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,{\sigma})=-\frac{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}{\langle\eta\rangle\langle\sigma\rangle}\frac{\mu^{S}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)}{i\Phi_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}(\xi,\eta,\sigma)}.\end{split}

(5.5)

The full normal form transformation is given by
\sum_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}\in\{+,-\}}T_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}

\displaystyle f:=g-T(g,g),\qquad T=\sum_{\iota_{1},\iota_{2}\in\{+,-\}}T_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}.

(5.6)

It is not hard to check that, under our assumptions,

Let us write

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}\widetilde{f}=\mathcal{Q}^{R}(g,g)+\mathcal{C}^{S}(g,g,g)+\mathcal{C}^{R}(g,g,g)\end{split}

•

The regular quadratic term

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{Q}^{R}(a,b)=\sum_{\iota_{1},\iota_{2}\in\{+,-\}}\mathcal{Q}_{\iota_{1}\iota_{2}}^{R}(a,b),\\

Note that we can use the factors of

Decomposition of (

We consider here the singular cubic terms defined in (

\displaystyle{\Big\|\langle\xi\rangle\partial_{\xi}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{C}^{S}(f,f,f)(s)\,ds\Big\|}_{L^{2}}\lesssim{\varepsilon}_{1}^{3}\langle t\rangle^{\alpha}.

\|\langle\xi\rangle^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{R}_{Q}ds\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim{\varepsilon}_{1}^{2}

via a similar argument as in the estimate (
8.6
),
since
\mathcal{R}_{Q}
is a cubic version of the quadratic terms
\mathcal{Q}^{R}
,
and then conclude using interpolation as above.

Finally, for the term
\mathcal{R}_{C}
in (
5.23
) we can first substitute all the

f

noticing that the error terms generated in this process are higher order remainders.
Then, we can estimate very similarly to Section

),

) to obtain the following analogue of (
