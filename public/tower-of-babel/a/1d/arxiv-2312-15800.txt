Jacobi integral: A real-world application of the Lagrangian formulation
Jeremy A. Riousset, Manasvi Lingam

Abstract
The applicability of advanced classical mechanics (viz., the Lagrangian and/or Hamiltonian approaches) to real-world problems may not always seem straightforward, despite the mathematical rigor and elegance of this field. Here, we present a proof of the Jacobi integral using the Lagrangian formulation as a viable alternative to the usual demonstration using Newton’s second law. The result represents a useful example of how advanced classical mechanics can provide a significant advantage over standard methods (i.e., Newton’s laws). We conclude with an illustration of the Jacobi integral in our Solar system: the Circular Restricted 3-Body Problem (CR3BP) around Pluto and Charon.

II
Jacobi constant (integral) derivation

Hereafter, we will work with specific energy (i.e., energy per unit mass) and therefore will omit the term “specific” for the sake of brevity. First, let us define a non-inertial reference frame associated with the masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. The
x

m_{1}
to
m_{2}
, and the

-axis is perpendicular to the orbital plane of
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. The
y
-direction results from the cross-product
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}=\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{z}\crossproduct\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}
. It is worth recalling that in the system of uniformly rotating coordinates
(Oxyz)
, the masses
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are at rest.

The third body of mass

\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}

(x,y)

G
is at rest (
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{G}=\overrightarrow{{\bf\it 0}}

m_{1}
and

is in uniform angular rotation, so that
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it\Omega}}=\dfrac{2\pi}{P}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{z}
, where
P
denotes the orbital period that is given by Kepler’s third law:

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\dfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\mu}r_{12}^{3}}\quad\mbox{where $r_{12}=\norm{\va*R_2-\va*R_1}$}\quad.

(1)

Thus, the relative position, velocity, and acceleration can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{\rm rel}=\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}&=&x\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+y\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}\\
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{\rm rel}=\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}&=&\dot{x}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+\dot{y}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}\\
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it a}}_{\rm rel}=\overrightarrow{{\bf\it a}}&=&\ddot{x}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+\ddot{y}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}\\
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it\Omega}}&=&\Omega\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{z}\end{array}\right..

Let us denote
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{\rm abs}
the absolute velocity in the inertial reference frame. Under the aforementioned assumptions, the three-term velocity equation yields:

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{\rm abs}

\displaystyle=\cancelto{\overrightarrow{{\bf\it 0}}}{\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{G}}+\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{\rm rel}+\overrightarrow{{\bf\it\Omega}}\times\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{\rm rel}

\displaystyle=(\dot{x}-\Omega y)\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+(\dot{y}+\Omega x)\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}.

(6)

Consequently, the specific kinetic energy is:

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\overrightarrow{{\bf\it v}}_{\rm abs}^{2}}{2}=\dfrac{\left(\dot{x}-\Omega y\right)^{2}+\left(\dot{y}+\Omega x\right)^{2}}{2}.

(7)

Next, let us denote by
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{1}
and
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{2}
the relative positions of
m
with respect to
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
(Fig.
1
). We can straightforwardly write:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{1}&=&\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}-\overrightarrow{{\bf\it R}}_{1}=(x-x_{1})\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+(y-y_{1})\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}\\
\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}_{2}&=&\overrightarrow{{\bf\it r}}-\overrightarrow{{\bf\it R}}_{2}=(x-x_{2})\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}+(y-y_{2})\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{y}\end{array}.\right.

The gravitational potential
V
(or specific gravitational potential energy) is defined as:

\displaystyle V=\sum_{i\in\{1,2\}}\int_{r}^{\infty}\dfrac{\overrightarrow{{\bf\it F}}_{i}}{m}\cdot\differential{\va*r}_{i}=-\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}.

(10)

The Lagrangian is conventionally defined as
Goldstein:2002
:

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=T-V.

(11)

Substituting (
7
) and (
10
) into (
11
) lets us write:

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left(\dot{x}-\Omega y\right)^{2}+\left(\dot{y}+\Omega x\right)^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}.

(12)

Eq. (
12
) lets us apply Lagrange’s equations to obtain the equations of motion (Ref.
Goldstein:2002
, p. 21
):

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\partialderivative{L}{\dot{q}_i}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{L}{q_i}\quad\mbox{where $\left\{\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}q_{1}&=&x&;&\dot{q}_{1}&=&\dot{x}\\
q_{2}&=&y&;&\dot{q}_{2}&=&\dot{y}\end{array}\right.$}\quad,

which can be understood as a mathematical formulation of the stationary-action principle or Hamilton’s principle of least action
(
VIA89
;
Goldstein:2002
;
WG03
;
DS09
;
ED10
;
TB17
)
. Starting with
i=1
, the left-hand side (LHS) of (
II
) gives:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\partialderivative{L}{\dot{q}_1}

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\dot{x}}\left(\dfrac{\left(\dot{x}-\Omega y\right)^{2}+\left(\dot{y}+\Omega x\right)^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}\right)=\derivative{(\dot{x}-\Omega y)}{t}=\ddot{x}-\Omega\dot{y}

(15a)

and similarly,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\partialderivative{L}{\dot{q}_2}

\displaystyle=\ddot{y}+\Omega\dot{x}.

(15b)

On the other hand, the right-hand side (RHS) of (
II
) gives:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{L}{q_1}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{x}\left(\dfrac{\left(\dot{x}-\Omega y\right)^{2}+\left(\dot{y}+\Omega x\right)^{2}}{2}+\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}\right)=\Omega(\dot{y}+\Omega x)-\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}^{2}}\partialderivative{r_1}{x}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}^{2}}\partialderivative{r_2}{x}.

By employing the relations
\displaystyle\partialderivative{r_1}{x}=\dfrac{x-x_{1}}{r_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\partialderivative{r_2}{x}=\dfrac{x-x_{2}}{r_{2}}
from (
II
), we have:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{L}{q_1}

\displaystyle=\Omega\dot{y}+\Omega^{2}x-\dfrac{\mu_{1}(x-x_{1})}{r_{1}^{3}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}(x-x_{2})}{r_{2}^{3}}.

(16a)

Similarly, we can write
\displaystyle\partialderivative{r_1}{y}=\dfrac{y-y_{1}}{r_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\partialderivative{r_2}{y}=\dfrac{y-y_{2}}{r_{2}}
to obtain:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{L}{q_2}

\displaystyle=-\Omega\dot{x}+\Omega^{2}y-\dfrac{\mu_{1}(y-y_{1})}{r_{1}^{3}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}(y-y_{2})}{r_{2}^{3}}.

(16b)

Setting (
15a
) equal to (
16a
) and (
15b
) equal to (
16b
) leads to the result:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}

\displaystyle=2n\dot{y}+\Omega^{2}x-\dfrac{\mu_{1}(x-x_{1})}{r_{1}^{3}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}(x-x_{2})}{r_{2}^{3}},

(17a)

\displaystyle\ddot{y}

\displaystyle=-2n\dot{x}+\Omega^{2}y-\dfrac{\mu_{1}(y-y_{1})}{r_{1}^{3}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}(y-y_{2})}{r_{2}^{3}}.

(17b)

Finally, we calculate
\ddot{x}\dot{x}+\ddot{y}\dot{y}
and simplify to get:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}\dot{x}+\ddot{y}\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\Omega^{2}(x\dot{x}+y\dot{y})-\mu_{1}\dfrac{(x-x_{1})\dot{x}+(y-y_{1})\dot{y}}{r_{1}^{3}}-\mu_{2}\dfrac{(x-x_{2})\dot{x}+(y-y_{2})\dot{y}}{r_{2}^{3}}.

(18)

The application of the chain rule to
\displaystyle\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{1}}
yields:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{1}}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{x}\dfrac{1}{r_{1}}\cdot\partialderivative{x}{t}+\partialderivative{y}\dfrac{1}{r_{1}}\cdot\partialderivative{y}{t}=-\dfrac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\dfrac{x-x_{1}}{r_{1}}\dot{x}-\dfrac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\dfrac{y-y_{1}}{r_{1}}\dot{y}.

(19a)

The same approach for
\displaystyle\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{2}}
straightforwardly returns:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{2}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{(x-x_{2})\dot{x}+(y-y_{2})\dot{y}}{r_{2}^{3}}.

(19b)

Substituting (
19a
) and (
19b
) into (
18
) reduces the latter to:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}\dot{x}+\ddot{y}\dot{y}

\displaystyle=\Omega^{2}(x\dot{x}+y\dot{y})+\mu_{1}\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{1}}+\mu_{2}\derivative{t}\dfrac{1}{r_{2}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 0

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\left(\dfrac{\Omega^{2}\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)}{2}+\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}-\dfrac{\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}}{2}\right).

This last equation demonstrates that the quantity inside the parenthesis is constant. Using (
II
), we note that:
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
and
v^{2}=\dot{x}^{2}+\dot{y}^{2}
and thus we obtain:

\displaystyle\boxed{C_{\rm J}=\Omega^{2}r^{2}+\dfrac{2\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{2\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}-v^{2}},

(20)

where
C_{\rm J}
is the Jacobi constant, also known as the Jacobi integral. Various versions of this equation can be found in the literature with alternative definitions of the constant (e.g., Ref.
Curtis:2020
, p. 127
) or use of normalized units (e.g., Ref.
DePater:2015
, p. 27
and Ref.
Vallado:2013
, p. 970
). However, their proofs are usually lengthy and intricate, because they entail the application of Newton’s second law with appropriate geometric considerations. In contrast, the above derivation based on the Lagrangian formalism simplifies the proof, and constitutes an excellent example of the Lagrangian formulation evincing a substantial edge over Newtonian mechanics, for what turns out to be a classic textbook topic in orbital mechanics.

Another pathway toward establishing the nature of the Jacobi integral, which we do not explicitly work out here, is to first construct the Hamiltonian
H
for the CR3BP from the Lagrangian

, given by (
12
), via the Legendre transform
(
VIA89
;
Goldstein:2002
;
WG03
;

)
. Next, it can be demonstrated that
\derivative*{C_{\rm J}}{t}\equiv\poissonbracket{C_{\rm J}}{H}=0
for this system, where
\poissonbracket{\cdot}{\cdot}
is the canonical Poisson bracket, thereby confirming that the Jacobi integral is a constant of motion.

IV
Discussion

The rotating, non-inertial nature of the CR3BP reference frame does not enforce the conservation of energy and angular momentum. However, for the sake of clarity, we can rewrite (
21
) in the following fashion:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\left(\dfrac{1}{2}v^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}\Omega^{2}r^{2}\right)}_{\mbox{(I)}}+\underbrace{\left(-\dfrac{\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}-\dfrac{\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}\right)}_{\mbox{(II)}}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}C_{\rm J}=\dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{m},

(22)

which represents the preferred form in Ref.
Curtis:2020
, p. 127
. The term (I) in (
22
) represents the total specific kinetic energy, i.e., the relative specific kinetic energy supplemented with the specific kinetic energy of rotation of the non-inertial reference frame. The latter is equivalent to the specific potential energy of the centrifugal acceleration. Term (II) is the total gravitational potential energy arising from the masses of the two main bodies. Therefore,
\ifrac{\mathcal{E}}{m}
represents the total mechanical energy per unit mass of the third body, and the Jacobi integral is a quantity proportional to
\ifrac{\mathcal{E}}{m}
, which will remain constant for a third body in the CR3BP. Zero velocity curves, i.e., the location in space where the speed
v
would go to zero, enable us to demarcate the possible regions accessible to the third body with a given potential energy. When
v
=0, the Jacobi constant is equal to the pseudo-potential
U
in (
21
). Hence, Eq. (
22
) becomes
U(v=0)=C_{\rm J}=\Omega^{2}r^{2}+\dfrac{2\mu_{1}}{r_{1}}+\dfrac{2\mu_{2}}{r_{2}}
.

C_{\rm J}\in\{150,155,160,175,185\}\,\mathrm{kJ/kg}

The principal results and contributions from this work can be summarized as follows:

1.

The Circular Restricted 3-Body Problem (CR3BP) offers a practical illustration of the benefits of the Lagrangian formalism over the classical approach entailing Newton’s laws of motion.

2.

The Pluto–Charon system offers ideal conditions to demonstrate a real-world instantiation of the CR3BP in our Solar system.
