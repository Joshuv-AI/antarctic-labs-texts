The Hessian perspective into the Nature of Convolutional Neural Networks
Sidak Pal Singh, Thomas Hofmann, Bernhard Schölkopf

Abstract
While Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have long been investigated and applied, as well as theorized, we aim to provide a slightly different perspective into their nature — through the perspective of their Hessian maps. The reason is that the loss Hessian captures the pairwise interaction of parameters and therefore forms a natural ground to probe how the architectural aspects of CNN get manifested in its structure and properties. We develop a framework relying on Toeplitz representation of CNNs, and then utilize it to reveal the Hessian structure and, in particular, its rank. We prove tight upper bounds (with linear activations), which closely follow the empirical trend of the Hessian rank and hold in practice in more general settings. Overall, our work generalizes and establishes the key insight that, even in CNNs, the Hessian rank grows as the square root of the number of parameters.

Precise bounds on the Hessian rank.

Despite the much interest in Hessian over the decades, the upper bounds remained quite trivial (like a factor of the number of samples) and the empirically observed degeneracy
(
Sagun et al. 2016
)

(

(

)
thoroughly demonstrate that their bounds hold empirically for ReLU-based FCNs as well. Their empirical analysis is equally rigorous, for they compute exact Hessians (without any approximation) in Float64 precision.

Overall, the surprising finding of

Why Hessian Rank?

The finding about the growth of Hessian rank carries thought-provoking implications on the number of effective parameters within a particular architecture. Let us illustrate this by considering the Occam’s factor
(
MacKay 1992b
;
Gull 1989
)
which, said roughly, describes the extent to which the prior hypothesis space shrinks on observing the data. More formally, this is the ratio of posterior to prior volumes in the parameter space. This is then used to derive the following measure for the effective degrees of freedom, assuming a quadratic approximation to the posterior:

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{p}\dfrac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\epsilon}\,,

where,
\lambda_{i}
denotes the
i

p

\epsilon

i

\epsilon

(
Maddox et al. 2020
)
empirically noted that this measure also explains double descent
(
Belkin et al. 2019
)
in neural networks. Thus, it makes it all the more pertinent

4.1
Toeplitz representation of deep CNNs

Now, let us assume we have

Assume, say the ReLU nonlinearity

\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})={\mathcal{W}}^{(L+1)}\ast\sigma({\mathcal{W}}^{(L)}\ast\sigma(\,\cdots\ast\sigma({\mathcal{W}}^{(1)}\ast\mathbf{x})))\,.

As before, we would like to express the above function in terms of a sequence of appropriate Toeplitz matrix products. Unlike the warmup scenario, the convolutional kernels in this general case will be tensors. The key idea is to do a column-wise stacking of individual Toeplitz matrices across the input channels while maintaining the row-wise stacking, as before, across the output channels.

First, we need to introduce a notation about indexing the fibres of a tensor
{\mathcal{W}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}^{m_{l}\times m_{l-1}\times k_{l}}}

{{\bf T}}^{{\mathcal{W}^{(l)}_{(i,j)\,\bullet}}}:=\toep({\mathcal{W}^{(l)}_{(i,j)\,\bullet}},d_{l-1})\in{\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}}}

{{{\bf T}}^{(l)}:=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf T}}^{{\mathcal{W}^{(l)}_{(1,1)\,\bullet}}}&\cdots&{{\bf T}}^{{\mathcal{W}^{(l)}_{(1,m_{l-1})\,\bullet}}}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots&&\vdots\\[2.84526pt]

As evident, the fact that a convolution of a set of vectors can be expressed as a matrix-vector product with a suitable Toeplitz matrix is rather straightforward. Also, a Toeplitz representation for CNN is not new — works from approximation theory incorporate a similar formalism

5
Key results on the CNN Hessian Rank

Finally, we can now present our key results. A quick note about assumptions: for simplicity, assume that the (uncentered) input-covariance
{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}=\operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{x})
has full rank
\operatorname{rank}\left({\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}\right):=r=d
. We will analyze the ranks of the outer product and functional Hessian, later combining them to yield a bound on the rank of the loss Hessian.
This is without loss of generality for one can always pre-process the input to ensure that this is the case, and results of
(
Singh et al. 2021
)
hold for
r\neq d
with appropriate modifications.

Outer-Product Hessian
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}
.

From the structure of the
kl
-th block in Eqn. (
2
), it’s easy to see to arrive at the following proposition:

Proposition 4
.

For a deep linear convolutional network,
\,\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}={\bf Q}_{o}^{\top}{\bf A}_{o}{\bf B}_{o}{{\bf A}_{o}^{\top}}{\bf Q}_{o}\,
, where
\,{\bf B}_{o}={\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}\,\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd\times Kd}
,

\,\,\,{\bf A}_{o}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf T}}^{(L+1:2)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{d}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
{{\bf T}}^{(L+1:l+1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf T}}^{(1:l-1)}\\[2.84526pt]
\cdots\\[2.84526pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf T}}^{(1:L)}\end{pmatrix}\,\,\in\mathbb{R}^{Kd\times\widehat{p}}\,,
and
{\bf Q}_{o}=\diag\left(\widetilde{{\bf Q}}^{(1)},\cdots,\widetilde{{\bf Q}}^{(L+1)}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}^{\widehat{p}\times p}}
where
\diag(\cdot)
denotes a block-diagonal matrix.

Besides,
p=\sum_{i=1}^{L+1}m_{l}m_{l-1}{k_{l}}
and
\widehat{p}=\sum_{i=1}^{L+1}m_{l}m_{l-1}d_{l}d_{l-1}
. The former denotes the number of parameters in the CNN while the latter is the number of parameters in the ‘Toeplitzed’ fully-connected network.

Our first key result, the proof of which is located in Section
C.1
of the Appendix, can then be described as follows :

Theorem 5
.

The rank of the outer-product Hessian is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})

\displaystyle\leq\min\big(p,d_{0}\rank({{\bf T}}^{(2:L+1)})+K\rank({{\bf T}}^{(L:1)})\,-

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt\rank({{\bf T}}^{(2:L+1)})\rank({{\bf T}}^{(L:1)})\big)

\displaystyle=\min\left(p,q\,(d_{0}+K-q)\right)\,.

Here,
q:=\min(d_{0},m_{1}d_{1},\cdots,m_{L}d_{L},K)
.

Assuming no bottleneck layer, we will have that
q=\min(d,K)
, and resulting in
\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})\leq Kd_{0}
.

Functional Hessian
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}
.

Our approach here will be similar to that in the Theorem above. We will try to factor out all the
{{\bf Q}}^{(l)}
matrices and then analyze the rank of the resulting decomposition. But, this requires more care as the form of the
kl
-th block is different depending on
k\leq l
or not.

Theorem 6
.

For a deep linear convolutional network, the rank of
l
-th column-block,
{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet l}}
, of the matrix
{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}}
, can be upper bounded as

\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet l}})\leq\min(\widehat{q}\,m_{l-1}d_{l-1}+\widehat{q}\,m_{l}d_{l}-\widehat{q}^{\,2}\,,m_{l}m_{l-1}k_{l})\,,

for
l\in[2,\cdots,L]\,.
When
l=1
, we have

\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet 1}})\leq\min(\widehat{q}\,m_{1}d_{1}+\widehat{q}\,s-\widehat{q}^{\,2}\,,m_{1}m_{0}k_{1})\,.

And, when
l=L+1
, we have

\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet L+1}})\leq\min(\widehat{q}\,m_{L}d_{L}+\widehat{q}\,s-\widehat{q}^{\,2}\,,m_{L+1}m_{L}k_{L+1})\,.

Here,
\widehat{q}:=\min(d_{0},m_{1}d_{1},\cdots,m_{L}d_{L},K,s)=\min(q,s)
and
s:=\rank({\mathbf{\Omega}})=\rank(\mathbf{E}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}])
.

The proof is located in Section
C.2
of the Appendix. The upper bound on the rank of
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}
follows by summing the above result over all the columns,
\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}})\leq\sum\limits_{l=1}^{L+1}\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet l}})
by the sub-additivity of rank. A remarkable empirical observation, like in the case of FCNs, is that the block-columns are mutually orthogonal — hence, we don’t loose anything by simply summing the ranks of the block columns.

Loss Hessian
{\mathbf{H}_{{\mathrm{L}}}}
.

One can then bound the rank of the loss Hessian simply as,
\rank({\mathbf{H}_{{\mathrm{L}}}})\leq\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})+\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}})
. Also, we can infer that, in the likely case where
q=\min(K,d_{0})
, rank of the loss Hessian grows linearly with number of channels, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(m\cdot L\cdot d_{0})
, while the number of parameters grow quadratically in the number of channels, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}\cdot L\cdot d_{0})
. Thereby, we confirm that like FCNs, a similar linear trend also holds for CNNs (and hence the Figure
1
).
Besides, for very large networks,
m
will be the dominating factor and we can infer that rank will show a square root behaviour relative to the number of parameters. Hence, we generalize the key finding of
(
Singh et al. 2021
)
in the fully-connected case to the case of convolutional neural networks.

Exact results for one-hidden layer case

While the above bounds are much tighter than any existing bounds, we now try to understand how tight our bounds are by looking at the case of a 1-hidden layer:

Theorem 7
.

The rank of the outer product Hessian can be bounded as:

\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}\right)\leq\min\big(Kd_{0},q(k_{1}+K(d_{0}-k_{1}+1)-q)\big)\,

The rank of the functional Hessian is bounded as:

\operatorname{rank}\left({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)\leq 2\min\big(k_{1},K(d_{0}-k_{1}+1)\big)\,m_{1}\,.

The strategy behind the proofs (section
C.3

7
Locality and Weight-sharing

We would like to do a little deep dive and explore a simplified setting, in order to gather some intuition.
We study the two crucial components behind CNN, namely, local connectivity (i.e, the localized patches of the input are convolved with a possibly distinct filter) and weight sharing (where the same filter is applied to each of these patches).

Setting.

Concretely, let us begin by considering the case of a one-hidden layer neural network (with linear activations for simplicity). We now try to get rid of the layer indices for the sake of clarity. So the input dimension is
d
, output dimension is
K
, filter size is
k
, number of hidden layer filters is
m
. Further, we consider non-overlapping filters, like in MLPMixer
(
Tolstikhin et al. 2021
)
. In other words, the stride is set equal to the filter size
k
and so the number of patches under consideration will be
t=d/k
, and to avoid unnecessary complexity, we assume
d
is an integer multiple of
k
.

Locally Connected Networks (LCNs).

As the filters that get applied on the patches are distinct, let us denote them by
{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
and the output layer matrix as
{\bf V}:=\begin{pmatrix}{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}\end{pmatrix}
, with
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times m}
, where the superscript denotes the index of the local patch upon which they get applied. Mathematically we can represent the resulting neural network function as (where
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
denotes the
i
-th chunk of the input):

\displaystyle\small\begin{pmatrix}{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf W}}^{(11)}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
\bm{0}&\cdots&{{\bf W}}^{(tt)}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}

We indexed the local weight matrices of the first layer as
{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}
to reflect that it is
(i,i)
-th block on the diagonal. After carrying out the block matrix multiplication, the above formulation can also be expressed as:

\displaystyle\vskip-8.53581pt\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}\,{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\,.

(7)

We can then easily bring to our minds the case of fully-connected networks which will also contain the off-diagonal blocks and would be represented as:

\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{FCN-large}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}\,{{\bf W}}^{(ij)}\,\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\,.

Clearly, fully-connected networks form a generalization of locally-connected networks.
Another point of comparison for LCNs in Eq. (

s
distinct

8

This superposition point of view would hold even if we had a non-linearity, and so do our empirical results.

\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\,\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{FCN-small}}\big(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\,;\,\{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\}\big)\,.

In this scenario of LCNs, we get the following bounds on the rank of the outer-product and functional Hessian:

Theorem 9
.

For the locally connected network as described in Eqn. (

\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\text{LCN}})\leq t\cdot q(k+K-q)\,,

\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}^{\text{LCN}})\leq t\cdot 2m\min(k,K)
, where
q=\min(k,K,m)
and
t=d/k
.

The proof is located in Section
C.4

d\rightarrow k
and scale the bounds by a factor of
t=d/k
(the number of smaller fully-connected that are being superpositioned). In short, even though these weight matrices must ‘act’ together in the loss, their contributions to the Hessian came out individually (i.e,
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}

Now all the weight matrices in the first layer are shared, i.e.,

For the locally connected network with weight sharing defined in Eqn. (

8
Conclusion

All in all, we have illustrated how the key ingredients of CNNs, such as local connectivity and weight sharing, as well as architectural aspects like filter size, strides, and number of channels get manifested through the Hessian structure and rank. Moreover, we can utilize our Toeplitz representation framework to deliver tight upper bounds in the general case of deep convolutional networks and generalize the recent finding of
(
Singh et al. 2021
)
about square root growth of rank relative to parameter count for CNNs as well.
Looking ahead, our work raises some very interesting questions: (a) Is the growth of rank as a square root in terms of the number of parameters a universal characteristic of all deep architectures? Including Transformers? Or are there some exceptions to it? (b) Given the uncovered structure of the Hessian in CNNs, there are also questions about understanding which parts of the architecture affect the spectrum more — normalization or pooling layers?
(c) On the application side, it would be interesting to see if we can use our results to understand the approximation quality of existing pre-conditioners such as K-FAC or come up with better ones, given the rich properties of Toeplitz matrices.

A.2.3
Frequently used properties of Kronecker Products

Often in the analysis, we use several properties of Kronecker products, and we cannot afford to reiterate them at every step of the proof. Hence, if a reader feels a bit uneasy about a particular step, we recommend they consult some of the properties mentioned here. The proofs are readily available online, or check
(
Magnus & Neudecker 2019
)
as a good reference for matrix analysis.

For conformable matrices
{\bf A},{\bf B},{\bf C},{\bf D},{\bf X}
, we have that:

•

({\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B})({\bf C}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf D})={\bf A}{\bf B}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf C}{\bf D}

•

\rank({\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B})=\rank({\bf A})\cdot\rank({\bf B})

•

({\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B})\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf C}={\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}({\bf B}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf C})

•

({\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B})^{\top}={\bf A}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B}^{\top}

•

\vect_{r}({\bf A}{\bf X}{\bf B})=({\bf A}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B}^{\top})\vect_{r}({\bf X})

For column vectors
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, we have that:
\mathbf{a}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{b}=\vect_{r}(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{\top})

For

where in the second line, we used the result of the
\rank({\bf A}_{F}^{(l)})
from
(
Singh et al. 2021
)
, while the
\rank({\bf Q}^{(l)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{l}}))\leq\min(m_{l}m_{l-1}k_{l},m_{l},m_{l-1}d_{l}d_{l-1})=m_{l}m_{l-1}k_{l}
as
k_{l}\leq d_{l-1}
for valid convolution.

Likewise, we can carry out a similar operation for the first block-columnn
{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet 1}}

\displaystyle{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet 1}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
\left({\bf I}_{m_{2}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf Q}}^{(2)}{}^{\top}\right)\left({{\bf T}}^{(3:L)}{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\right)\left({{\bf Q}}^{(1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\right)\\[2.84526pt]

\vdots\\[2.84526pt]

\end{pmatrix}={\bf Q}_{F}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
{{\bf T}}^{(3:L)}{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
{{\bf T}}^{(k+1:L)}{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf T}}^{(k-1:2)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathbf{\Omega}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf T}}^{(L:2)}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf A}_{F}^{(1)}}\left({{\bf Q}}^{(1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\right)

The rank can be then bounded as:

\displaystyle\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet 1}})

\displaystyle\leq\min(\rank({\bf Q}_{F}),\rank({\bf A}_{F}^{(1)}),\rank({\bf Q}^{(1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}))

\displaystyle=\min(p,\widehat{p},\,\widehat{q}\,m_{1}d_{1}+\widehat{q}\,s-\widehat{q}^{\,2}\,,m_{1}m_{0}k_{1})

\displaystyle=\min(\widehat{q}\,m_{1}d_{1}+\widehat{q}\,s-\widehat{q}^{\,2}\,,m_{1}m_{0}k_{1})\,,

Finally, we discuss the case of last column-block:

\displaystyle{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet{L+1}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\left({\bf I}_{m_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf Q}}^{(1)}{}^{\top}\right)\left({{\bf T}}^{(2:L)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\top}\right)\left({{\bf Q}}^{(L+1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{L+1}}\right)\\[2.84526pt]

\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}={\bf Q}_{F}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf T}}^{(2:L)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\top}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]

\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf A}_{F}^{(L+1)}}\left({{\bf Q}}^{(L+1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{L+1}}\right)

The rank can be then bounded as:

\displaystyle\rank({\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{F}}^{\bullet L+1}})

C.3
Rank results for one-hidden layer CNN case

Theorem
7
.

For a one-hidden layer 1D CNN, the rank of the outer product Hessian can be bounded as:

\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}\right)\leq\min\big(Kd_{0},q(k_{1}+K(d_{0}-k_{1}+1)-q)\big)\,
, where
q=\min(k_{1},K(d_{0}-k_{1}+1),m_{1})
.

Proof.

Consider a one-hidden layer CNN as follows:

\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})={{\bf T}}^{(2)}{{\bf T}}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}\,,\quad\text{with}\quad{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{{\mathcal{W}}^{(2)},{\mathcal{W}}^{(1)}\}\,.

Now, at the output layer
k_{2}=d_{1}
so as to project the hidden features to an output of size
K=m_{2}
hence Thus, spatially we will have that
d_{2}=1
, and as a result
{{\bf T}}^{(2)}={{\bf W}}^{(2)}
. Also, the number of input channels is simply assumed to be
m_{0}=1
, else we can flatten the input to obtain this.

Remember that, in this scenario,
{{\bf T}}^{(1)}
amounts to:

{{\bf T}}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf T}}^{{\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{(1,1)\,\bullet}}}\\
\vdots\\
{{\bf T}}^{{\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{(m_{1},1)\,\bullet}}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

While, as
m_{0}=1
,
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}w_{1,1,1}&\cdots&w_{1,1,k_{1}}\\
w_{m_{1},1,1}&\cdots&w_{m_{1},1,k_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}
and we would like to ideally group the elements of
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}
inside
{{\bf T}}^{(1)}
. We will do this by suitable row permutations using the auxiliary permutation matrices, discussed in the section
A.2.2
.

Let’s rewrite our network function as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle={{\bf W}}^{(2)}{{\bf T}}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle={{\bf W}}^{(2)}\,{\bf P}^{(d_{1})}{\bf P}_{(d_{1})}\,{{\bf T}}^{(1)}\mathbf{x}

where
{\bf P}_{(d_{1})}{{\bf T}}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{{\bf W}}^{(1)}\pi_{C}^{0}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{{\bf W}}^{(1)}\pi_{C}^{d_{1}-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}
and
\widetilde{{\bf W}}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}_{m_{1}\times k_{1}}&\bm{0}_{m_{1}\times(d_{0}-k_{1})}\end{pmatrix}={{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}
.

Next, let’s denote the matrix
{{\bf W}}^{(2)}{\bf P}^{(d_{1})}\in{\mathbb{R}^{K\times m_{1}d_{1}}}
by
{\bf V}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf V}^{(1)}&\cdots&{\bf V}^{(d_{1})}\end{pmatrix}
. Using these we can represent the network function in the following superposition form:

\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}{\bf V}^{(i)}\,{{\bf W}}^{(1)}\,{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}\,.

(37)

Having done this reformulation, let’s get back to the business of computing the derivatives, which are listed below:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})={\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}

(38)

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(1)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}

(39)

Now let’s collect these individual parameter gradients to form the overall gradient,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{0}\mathbf{x}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{d_{1}-1}\mathbf{x}\\
\sum\limits_{i-1}^{d_{1}}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\cdots&{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\quad\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{0}\mathbf{x}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{d_{1}-1}\mathbf{x}\\
\end{pmatrix}\\[10.00002pt]
\begin{pmatrix}{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(d_{1})}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,\,\,\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{0}\mathbf{x}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{d_{1}-1}\mathbf{x}\\
\end{pmatrix}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{Kd_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}\\[2.84526pt]
\widetilde{{\bf V}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf A}_{o}}\,\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{0}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,\pi_{C}^{d_{1}-1}\\
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf C}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf B}}

where,
\widetilde{{\bf V}}=\begin{pmatrix}{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}^{\top}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(d_{1})}}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}^{m\times Kd_{1}}}

Finally, we can take the outer product and average them over the dataset to yield the outer-product Hessian:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{o}{\bf C}\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}\right){\bf C}^{\top}{\bf A}_{o}^{\top}

(40)

Hence, we have that:

\displaystyle\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})

\displaystyle\leq\min(\rank({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}),\rank({\bf A}_{o}),\rank({\bf C})))

\displaystyle=\min(Kd_{0},q(Kd_{1}+k-q))

where
q=\min(k,Kd_{1},m)
and using the fact that, for
{\bf C}\in{\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}k_{1}\times Kd}}
, it has
\rank({\bf C})\leq\min(Kd_{1}k_{1},Kd)=Kd
.
∎

Theorem
8
.

For a one-hidden layer 1D CNN, the rank of the functional Hessian obeys the following formula:

\operatorname{rank}\left({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)\leq 2\min\big(k_{1},K(d_{0}-k_{1}+1)\big)\,m_{1}\,.

Proof.

We will start atop the network function, eq. (
37
), and parameter gradients, eq. (
38
), (
39
), obtained after carrying out the manipulations with permutation matrices in the proof of Theorem
7
. Let’s then get the gradient with respect to the parameters at the
c
-th output index:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\vect_{r}\left({\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\pi_{R}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{}^{\top}\right)

(41)

And that with respect to
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(1)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\pi_{C}^{i-1}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d_{1}}\vect_{r}\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\pi_{R}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\right)

(42)

Now, we perform the second differentiation with respect to transposed matrices, i.e.,
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{}^{\top}
or
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}
.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{{\bf W}}^{(1)}{}^{\top}}={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\pi_{R}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\,.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(1)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}}={\bf I}_{m_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{\top}\pi_{C}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\times d_{0}}\,.

Let’s now take the sum of the above matrices over the input as well as, with appropriate scaling by the residual, over the output indices to get the blocks of the functional Hessian. This yields,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{{\bf W}}^{(1)})}}={\mathbf{\Omega}}\pi_{R}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\,.

(43)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{\bf W}}^{(1)},{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})}}={\bf I}_{m_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{\top}\pi_{C}^{i-1}{\bf I}_{k_{1}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}d_{0}}\,.

(44)

where, recall
{\mathbf{\Omega}}=\mathbf{E}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbf{x}^{\top}]\in{\mathbb{R}^{K\times d_{0}}}
denotes the (uncentered) covariance of the residual with the input.

Since the matrix is zero on the block diagonals (i.e., block hollow), we can analyze the rank of all the row blocks with respect to
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}
(same as the column block with respect to all the
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}
) or the row blocks with respect to all the
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}
(same as the column block with respect to the
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}
, i.e., either
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{\bf W}}^{(1)},{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})}}
or
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{{\bf W}}^{(1)})}}
. Focussing on the latter, the column blocks with respect to
{{\bf W}}^{(1)}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{(\bullet,{{\bf W}}^{(1)})}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(1)}},{{\bf W}}^{(1)})}}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(d_{1})}},{{\bf W}}^{(1)})}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{\Omega}}\pi_{R}^{0}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbf{\Omega}}\pi_{R}^{d_{1}-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\end{pmatrix}

The above is equivalent upto row permutations to,

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{\Omega}}\pi_{R}^{0}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\mathbf{\Omega}}\pi_{R}^{d_{1}-1}{\bf I}_{d_{0}\times k_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}

(45)

where,
\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}\in{\mathbb{R}^{Kd_{1}\times k_{1}}}

The rank of the functional Hessian thus amounts to:

\displaystyle\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}})

\displaystyle=2\rank\left(\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=2m_{1}\rank(\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\min(Kd_{1},k_{1})m_{1}\,.

In the first line, the rank is scaled by
2
to account for both the non-zero blocks present in the functional Hessian. The rest follows from the properties of Kronecker product and rank, thus completing the proof.

∎

C.4
Rank results for Locally connected network

Theorem
9
.

For the locally connected network as described in Eqn. (
7
), the rank of the outer-product and the functional Hessian can be upper bounded as follows:

\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\text{LCN}})\leq t\cdot q(k+K-q)\,,
and
\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}^{\text{LCN}})\leq t\cdot 2m\min(k,K)
, where
q=\min(k,K,m)
and
t=\dfrac{d}{k}
.

Proof.

We now proceed to showing the proofs of the rank of outer-product and functional Hessian for the LCN case. Let’s recall the Eqn. (
7
) for the LCN:

\displaystyle\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}\,{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\,.

Outer-product Hessian.

Let’s compute the gradient of the function with respect to the matrix
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})={\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(46)

And that with respect to
{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})={{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(47)

So the gradient of the function output with respect to all the parameters can be arranged as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})\\[5.69054pt]
\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(11)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})\\[5.69054pt]
\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(tt)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(11)}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(tt)}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[5.69054pt]
{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf A}_{o}^{(1)}\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\right)\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf A}_{o}^{(t)}\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\right)\end{pmatrix}\,,

where,
{\bf A}_{o}^{(i)}:=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\\[5.69054pt]
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{d}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}
. Next, we take the outer-product of the gradient above, and then average over the inputs, resulting in:

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}

\displaystyle={\bf A}_{o}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(11)}&\cdots&{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(1t)}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(t1)}&\cdots&{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(tt)}\\
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf B}_{o}}{\bf A}_{o}^{\top}

(48)

Here,
{\bf A}_{o}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf A}^{(1)}_{o}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\bm{0}&\cdots&{\bf A}^{(t)}_{o}\\
\end{pmatrix}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(ij)}:=\operatorname{cov}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)},\mathbf{x}^{(j)})
.
Then we have that,

\displaystyle\rank({\bf A}_{o})

\displaystyle=t\rank({\bf A}_{o}^{(i)})

(49)

\displaystyle\hskip-40.00006pt=K\rank({{\bf W}}^{(ii)})+k\rank({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})-\rank({{\bf W}}^{(ii)})\rank({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})

(50)

\displaystyle=t\,\cdot q(K+k-q)

(51)

where,
q:=\min(K,k,m)
.

Now, the matrix
{\bf B}_{o}
is equivalent, upto row and column permutations, to the matrix
\widetilde{{\bf B}}_{o}={\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}
, since

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(11)}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(1t)}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(t1)}&\cdots&\mathbf{\Sigma}^{(tt)}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}={\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}}=\left(\mathbf{E}\,[x_{i}x_{j}]\right)_{ij}

Therefore, rank of
{\bf B}_{o}
can be upper bounded as
\rank({\bf B}_{o})\leq K\rank({\bm{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}})\leq Kd
.
Finally, we can bound the rank of the outer-product Hessian, since the rank of a product of matrices is upper bounded by the minimum of the ranks of individual matrices. Hence,

\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})\leq\min\left(\rank({\bf A}_{o}),\rank({\bf B}_{o})\right)=t\cdot q(K+k-q)\,.

(52)

Functional Hessian.

We need to differentiate again the network output gradients in Eqns. (
46
), (
47
). First, let’s just obtain the gradient for the output at the
c
-th index:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\vect_{r}\left({\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}{}^{\top}\right)

(53)

And that with respect to
{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle={{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\vect_{r}\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\right)

(54)

Clearly, if we are to differentiate the above gradients with respect to matrix
{{{\bf V}}^{(j)}}
or
{{\bf W}}^{(jj)}
, for
j\neq i
, we will just get a
\bm{0}
matrix. And, even more starkly, differentiating a second time with respect to the same matrix will also lead to that outcome. So, let’s focus on the more relevant non-zero cases. A last reminder before we head off to the calculations, we will again consider this second differentiation with respect to transposed matrices, i.e.,
{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}{}^{\top}
or
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}
.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}{}^{\top}}={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\,.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN}}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}}={\bf I}_{m}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{\top}\,.

Let’s now take the sum of the above matrices over the input as well as, with appropriate scaling by the residual, over the output indices to get the blocks of the functional Hessian. This yields,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{{\bf W}}^{(ii)})}}={\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\,.

(55)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{\bf W}}^{(ii)},{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})}}={\bf I}_{m}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\,.

(56)

where,
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{E}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}]\in{\mathbb{R}^{K\times k}}
denotes the (uncentered) covariance of the residual with the
i
-th input chunk. The rank of the functional Hessian thus amounts to:

\displaystyle\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}})

\displaystyle=t\cdot\left(\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{{\bf W}}^{(ii)})}})+\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{\bf W}}^{(i)},{{{\bf V}}^{(ii)}})}})\right)

\displaystyle=t\cdot 2\rank({\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m})

\displaystyle=t\cdot 2m\rank({\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)})=t\cdot 2m\min(k,K)\,.

∎

C.5
Rank results for LCN with Weight Sharing

Theorem
10
.

For the locally connected network with weight sharing defined in Eqn. (
8
), the rank of the outer-product and the functional Hessian can be bounded as:

\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}^{\text{LCN+WS}})\leq q(k+Kt-q)\,,
and
\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}})\leq 2m\min(k,Kt)
, where
q=\min(k,Kt,m)
and
t=\dfrac{d}{k}
.

Proof.

The proof strategy is similar to the case of 1-hidden layer CNN as presented in the proofs of Theorems
7
,
8
. But over here since we are effectively dealing with a CNN that has stride equal to filter size, i.e.,
k_{1}
, we will now present simplified proofs by not involving the permutation matrices
\pi_{R}
or
\pi_{C}
.

Outer-product Hessian.

Let’s compute the gradient of the function with respect to the matrix
{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN + WS}}(\mathbf{x})={\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(57)

And that with respect to
{\bf W}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{\bf W}}^{(ii)}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

(58)

So the gradient of the function output with respect to all the parameters can be arranged as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})\\[5.69054pt]
\nabla_{{\bf W}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[5.69054pt]
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\bm{0}&\cdots&{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}\\[14.22636pt]
\begin{pmatrix}{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}&\cdots&\bm{0}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
\bm{0}&\cdots&{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\\[5.69054pt]
{{{\bf V}}^{(1)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}&\cdots&{{{\bf V}}^{(t)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[5.69054pt]
\vdots\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{Kt}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\\[5.69054pt]
{\bf V}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{k}\\[5.69054pt]
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf A}_{o}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(1)}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
{\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}}_{{\bf B}}

In the last line, we used the associativity of Kronecker product and the fact that
{\bf C}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf D}=\begin{pmatrix}{{\bf C}}_{\bullet\,1}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf D}\cdots{{\bf C}}_{\bullet\,n}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf D}\end{pmatrix}
.

The outer-product Hessian can the be calculated by taking the outer-product of the above gradient and averaging over the samples in the dataset. This leads to

\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}}={\bf A}_{o}\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf B}_{o}\right){\bf A}_{o}^{\top}\,,

where,
{\bf B}_{o}
comes out to be the same matrix as in Eqn. (
48
) before. Now, let’s analyze the rank of the resulting matrices:

\displaystyle\rank(\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{O}})

\displaystyle=\min(\rank({\bf A}_{o}),K\rank({\bf B}_{o}))\leq\min(\rank({\bf A}_{o}),Kd)

\displaystyle=\min(q\cdot(k+Kt-q),Kd)=q\cdot(k+Kt-q)\,,

with
q=\min(k,Kt,m)
.

Functional Hessian.

We need to differentiate again the network output gradients in Eqns. (
57
), (
58
). First, let’s just obtain the gradient for the output at the
c
-th index:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\left({\bf I}_{K}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf W}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\vect_{r}\left({\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}{\bf W}^{\top}\right)

(59)

And that with respect to
{\bf W}
is:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf W}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right){\mathbf{e}}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{t}\vect_{r}\left({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\right)

(60)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN+WS}}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{\bf W}^{\top}}={\mathbf{e}}_{c}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\,.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\,\partial\nabla_{{\bf W}}\mathrm{F}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{c,\text{LCN+FS}}(\mathbf{x})}{\partial{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}}^{\top}}={\bf I}_{m}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{\mathbf{e}}_{c}^{\top}\,.

Let’s now take the sum of the above matrices over the input as well as, with appropriate scaling by the residual, over the output indices to get the blocks of the functional Hessian. This yields,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({{{\bf V}}^{(i)}},{\bf W})}}={\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\,.

(61)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}^{({\bf W},{{{\bf V}}^{(i)}})}}={\bf I}_{m}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}{}^{\top}\,.

(62)

where,
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(i)}=\mathbf{E}\,[\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\,\mathbf{x}^{(i)}{}^{\top}]
denotes the (uncentered) covariance of the residual with the
i
-th input chunk.

Hence the rank of the functional Hessian, since it is block hollow, comes out to be:

\displaystyle\rank({\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{F}}})

\displaystyle=2\rank\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\\[2.84526pt]
\vdots\\[2.84526pt]
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(t)}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m}\\[2.84526pt]
\end{pmatrix}=2\rank(\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}\mathbin{\mathop{\otimes}}{\bf I}_{m})=2m\rank(\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}})\leq 2m\min(k,Kt)\,.

Here in the above line, we can instead consider the rank of
\widetilde{{\mathbf{\Omega}}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{\Omega}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}^{Kt\times k}}
kroneckered with the identity matrix (
{\bf I}_{m}
) as rank is unaffected by row or column permutations.
∎
