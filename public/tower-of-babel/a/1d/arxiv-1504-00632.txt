Energy and Vorticity Spectra in Turbulent Superfluid 4 He from T = 0 T=0 to T λ T_{\lambda} .
Laurent Boué ∗ , Victor S. L’vov ∗ , Yotam Nagar ∗ , Sergey V. Nazarenko † , Anna Pomyalov ∗ , and Itamar Procaccia ∗

Abstract
We discuss the energy and vorticity spectra of turbulent superfluid 4 He in the entire temperature range from T = 0 T=0 up to the phase transition “ λ \lambda point”, T λ ≃ 2.17 T_{\lambda}\simeq 2.17\, K. Contrary to classical developed turbulence in which there are only two typical scales, i.e. the energy injection L L and the dissipation scales η \eta , here the quantization of vorticity introduces two additional scales, the vortex core radius a 0 a_{0} and the mean vortex spacing ℓ \ell . We present these spectra for the super- and the normal-fluid components in the entire range of scales from L L to a 0 a_{0} including the cross-over scale ℓ \ell where the hydrodynamic eddy-cascade is replaced by the cascade of Kelvin waves on individual vortices. At this scale a bottleneck accumulation of the energy was found earlier at T = 0 T=0 . We show that even very small mutual friction dramatically suppresses the bottleneck effect due to the dissipation of the Kelvin waves. Using our results for the spectra we estimate the Vinen “effective viscosity” ν ′ \nu^{\prime} in the entire temperature range and show agreement with numerous experimental observation for ν ′ ​ ( T ) \nu^{\prime}(T) .

II.3
Temperature dependence of the vorticity spectra in turbulent
4
He

At this point we cannot compare our predictions for energy spectra with experimental observations,
especially in the cross-over and in the small scale regions. This stems from the lack of small probes, see
cf. the review
BLR
. On the other hand, the attenuation of second sound or ion scattering may be used to measure the mean
vortex line density
{\mathcal{L}}=1/\ell^{2}
in
4
He or even its time and space dependence
BLR
. In turn, the value
{\mathcal{L}}^{2}

can be expressed in terms of the mean-square superfluid vorticity
\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s}}|^{2}\right\rangle
via the quantum of circulation

\kappa

Vin-2001
:

\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s}}|^{2}\right\rangle\approx(\kappa{\mathcal{L}})^{2}\ .

(14)

Therefore, the information about the vorticity is very important from the viewpoint of comparison with available and future experiments.

By analogy with the energy spectra (
9
), let us define the power spectra of vorticity
\Omega^{2}(k)
so that the
mean-square vorticity
\left\langle|\omega|^{2}\right\rangle
is given by the integral:

\left\langle|\omega|^{2}\right\rangle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\Omega^{2}(k)\,dk=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}k\ \Omega^{2}(k)\,d\ln k\ .

(15)

In isotropic incompressible turbulence
\Omega^{2}(k)=2k^{2}{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
. Therefore we define

\displaystyle\Omega^{2}_{\text{s}}(k)=2k^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\,,\quad\Omega^{2}_{\text{n}}(k)=2k^{2}{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{n}}(k)\,.

(16)

For brevity, we omit the subscript “
HD
” for the normal component;
{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{n}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\Rightarrow{\mathcal{E}}^{\text{n}}(k)
.

Plots of
k\,\Omega^{2}_{\text{s,n}}(k)
for different temperatures are shown in Figure
2
. According to Eq. (
15
), the area under
these plots is proportional to the total mean square vorticity,
\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s,n}}|^{2}\right\rangle
. Fig.
2
a shows the results for the eddy-wave model (the corresponding energy spectra for the same temperatures are shown in Fig.
1
a). One sees
that the largest (and temperature independent) value of
\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s}}|^{2}\right\rangle
is reached for

T<0.49\,
K: plots for
T=0.32\,\ 0.44\,
and
0.49\,
K practically coincide. Accordingly, the temperature range
T<0.49\,
K
may be considered as zero-temperature limit with the maximal value of
\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s}}|^{2}\right\rangle
(and correspondingly, the smallest
value of
\nu_{\text{s}}(T)
, as we will discuss later). At temperatures above 0.5K the area under the plots decreases (and
correspondingly,
\nu_{\text{s}}(T)
increases).

In Fig.

II.4.1
Correlations of the normal and superfluid velocities

It is often assumed (see e.g. Ref.
VinenNiemela
) that the normal and superfluid velocities are “locked” in the sense that

{\bm{u}}_{\text{n}}({\bm{r}},t)={\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{r}},t)\,,

(17)

(at least in the inertial interval of scales).
For quantitative understanding to which extent this assumption is statistically valid we consider the simplest possible case of
stationary, isotropic and homogeneous turbulence. Here we introduce a
cross-correlation function (in 1D
k
-representation) of the normal and the superfluid velocities
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}(k)
. This
correlation function is defined using the simultaneous, one-point cross-velocity correlation
\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{\text{n}}({\bm{r}},t)\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{r}},t)\right\rangle
similarly to Eq. (
9
):

\int{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}(k)\,dk=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{\text{n}}({\bm{r}},t)\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{r}},t)\right\rangle\ .

(18)

If, for example, motions of the normal and the superfluid components at a given
k
are completely correlated, then
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}(k)={\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}(k)={\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k)
. If this is true for all scales, then Eq. (
17
) is valid.

It is natural to normalize
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}
by the normal and the superfluid energy densities,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}
. This can
be reasonably done in one of two ways:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)\equiv\frac{2\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}(k)}{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}(k)+{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ s}}(k)}\,,

(19a)

\displaystyle\hbox{or}\;\;\;{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(k)\equiv\frac{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ns}}(k)}{\sqrt{{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}(k)\cdot{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{ s}}(k)}}\ .

(19b)

Both coefficients are equal to unity for fully locked superfluid and normal velocities, Eq. (
17
), and both vanish if the
velocities are statistically independent. However, if
{\bm{u}}_{\text{n}}({\bm{r}},t)=C{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{r}},t)
, with
C\neq 1

then
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)=2C/(C^{2}+1)<1
, but still
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(k)=1
. In any case
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)\leqslant{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(k)
.

In shell models, the coefficients
{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)
and
{\mathcal{K}}_{2}(k)
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle K_{1}(k_{m}\ell)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{2\,\mbox{Re}\left\langle v_{m}^{\text{s*}}v_{m}^{\text{n}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle v_{m}^{\text{s*}}v_{m}^{\text{s}}\right\rangle+\left\langle v_{m}^{\text{n*}}v_{m}^{\text{n}}\right\rangle}\,,

(20a)

\displaystyle K_{2}(k_{m}\ell)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{Re}\left\langle v_{m}^{\text{s*}}v_{m}^{\text{n}}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{\left\langle(v_{m}^{\text{s*}}v_{m}^{\text{s}}\right\rangle\left\langle v_{m}^{\text{n*}}v_{m}^{\text{n}}\right\rangle}}\ .

(20b)

These objects are shown in Fig.
3
.
At first glance, it is surprising that the correlations
K_{2}(k_{m}\ell)
(dashed lines in Fig.
3
) for
T\lessgtr 1.8\,
K
persist for much larger wave vectors than
K_{1}(k_{m}\ell)
, approaching
k_{m}\ell\sim 10^{2}
. For example, for
T=1.07\,
K (blue lines)

K_{1}(k_{m}\ell)
vanishes at
k_{m}\ell\simeq 1
, while
K_{2}(k_{m}\ell)>0.95
all the way up to

k_{m}\ell\simeq 100
. In this range of scales (
1\lesssim k_{m}\ell\lesssim 100
)
v_{m}^{\text{n}}\ll v_{m}^{\text{s}}
, but
v_{m}^{\text{n}}(t)\propto v_{m}^{\text{s}}(t)
, meaning that strongly damped normal velocity does not have its own dynamics and should be considered as
“slaved” by the superfluid velocity. The damped velocity (normal or superfluid) at any temperature
T\lessgtr 1.8\,
K would
follow this ”slaved” dynamics.

A model expression of the cross-correlation
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{sn}}
in terms of the self-correlation functions
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}
and

{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}
was found in
. In current notations it reads:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{sn}}(k)=\frac{\alpha\,\bar{\omega}_{\text{s}}[\rho_{\text{n}}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}(k)+\rho_{\text{s}}\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k)]}{\alpha\,\bar{\omega}_{\text{s}}\rho+\rho_{\text{n}}[(\nu^{\prime}_{\text{s}}+\nu_{\text{n}})\,k^{2}+\gamma_{\text{n}}(k)+\gamma_{\text{s}}(k)]}\,,

(21)

where the characteristic interaction frequencies (or turnover frequencies) of eddies in the normal and superfluid components,
\gamma_{\text{n}}(k)
and
\gamma_{\text{s}}(k)
, are given by Eq. (
11
) and
\bar{\omega}_{\text{s}}
is defined as:

\bar{\omega}_{\text{s}}\equiv\sqrt{\left\langle|\omega_{\text{s}}|^{2}\right\rangle}\ .

(22)

The derivation of Eq. (
21
) in
involves diagrammatic perturbation approach and is rather cumbersome. However the
simplicity of the final result (
21
) motivated us to re-derive it in a simple and transparent way which is presented in the Appendix.

Let us analyze first Eq. (
21
) in the inertial interval of scales, where according to Fig.
1
b,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}={\mathcal{E}}_{\text{n}}
and the terms
with the viscosities in the denominator may be neglected. In this case

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Big[1+\frac{2\rho_{\text{n}}\gamma_{\text{s}}(k)}{\alpha\,\rho\,\bar{\omega}_{\text{s}}})\Big]^{-1}

(23)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\Big[1+\frac{2\,\rho_{\text{n}}}{\alpha\,\rho}\Big(\frac{k}{k_{\text{max,s}}}\Big)^{2/3}\Big]^{-1}\,,

where the viscous cutoff of the superfluid inertial interval
k_{\text{max,s}}
is given by estimate (
12
).
First of all we see that the correlation coefficient is governed by the dimensionless parameter
\alpha\rho/\rho_{\text{n}}
which involves
the mutual friction coefficient
\alpha
, as expected. What is less expected, is that this parameter, according to Tab.
1
,
depends on the temperature only weakly and is close to unity. Therefore, in the inertial interval
k\ll k_{\text{max,s}}
we have:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{1}(k)

III.1.1
Simple closure for the large-scale energy dissipation due to mutual friction

Originally in HVBK equations the mutual friction force has the form:

{\bm{F}}_{\text{ns}}=\alpha~\hat{{\bm{\omega}}}_{\text{s}}\times[{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}\times({\bm{u}}_{\text{n}}-{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}})]+\alpha^{\prime}{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}\times({{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}}-{\bm{u}}_{\text{n}})\ .

Here
{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}\equiv\nabla\times{\bm{u}}_{\text{s}}
is the superfluid
vorticity and the unit vector
\hat{{\bm{\omega}}}_{\text{s}}\equiv{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}/\omega_{\text{s}}
is pointing in the direction of the vorticity. The dimensionless phenomenological parameters

\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
describe the dissipative and the reactive mutual friction forces acting on a vortex line as it moves with respect to the normal component.

In our Eqs. (
1

1c

which accounts for the fact that the
vorticity in developed turbulence is usually dominated by the
smallest eddies in the system, with the Kolmogorov viscous scale
\eta

characteristic wave-vector
k_{\eta}\sim 1/\eta

the smallest turnover time
\tau_{\eta}
which is of the order of
their decorrelation time. On the contrary, the main contribution
to the velocity in the equation for the dissipation of the

k

k

k\ll k_{\eta}
,
is dominated by
the
k^{\prime}
-eddies with
k^{\prime}\sim k

these eddies
\tau_{k^{\prime}}\gg\tau_{\eta}

1c
)
by averaging the vorticiy in Eqs. (
30

\tau_{\eta}\ll\tau\ll\tau_{k^{\prime}}
). Thus the vorticity
{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{s}}
may be
considered as uncorrelated with the velocities

III.1.2
Vinen-Niemela model for the superfluid energy dissipation

The energy dissipation term involving
\nu^{\prime}_{\text{s}}
in Eq. (
1a
)
for the superfluid velocity attempts to take into account
the existence of quantized vortex lines in an essentially classical regime of motion. An early approach
to this dissipation term

normal component. With the definition (

\nu^{\prime}_{\text{rnd}}=\alpha\rho_{\text{s}}\big/\rho\,,

(31)

shown by a black thin dashed line in Fig.

the picture of a random vortex tangle moving in a quiescent normal fluids predicts a higher dissipation compared
to the realistic situation in which the normal and superfluid velocities are almost locked together as discussed in Sec.
II.4
. The missing physics that needs to be considered to resolve this contradiction is that of vortex reconnections.

During reconnections, sharp angles necessarily appear on the vortex lines, leading to their fast motion. This motion is uncorrelated with the motion of
other vortex lines (except the ones involved in the reconnection) as well as with the (relatively slow) motion of the normal-fluid
component. This leads to large local energy dissipation events due to the mutual friction, which smoothes the vortex lines and
removes the regions of high curvature appearing after the reconnection events. A detailed analysis of this and related
effects led Vinen and Niemela

\nu^{\prime}_{\text{s}}
which in

\nu^{\prime}_{\text{s}}=\beta(T)\,\alpha\kappa\,,\qquad\beta(T)\equiv s\,\Big(\frac{c_{2}\Lambda}{4\pi}\Big)^{2}\ .

(32)

Here, the parameter

relates the vortex line density and the mean-square curvature

KLPP-2014

accounts for the suppression of effective line density due to partial polarization of vortex lines and was roughly estimated in

estimated with the help of Tab. III of

\displaystyle\quad K_{\text{n}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m}|v_{m}^{\text{n}}|^{2}\,,

\displaystyle F_{m}=\alpha\,\omega_{\text{s}}(v^{\text{s}}_{m}-v^{\text{n}}_{m})\,,\hskip-28.45274pt

{}_{m}\{v_{m^{\prime}}\}

The NL

K_{\text{s}}
and

(

IV.1.1
Small scale motions of Kelvin waves

It is now recognized that the typical turbulent state of a
superfluid consists of a complex tangle of quantized vortex

26
swept by the velocity field produced by the entire tangle according to the Biot-Savart equation
1

of the superfluid component with characteristic scales

Kelvin waves. An important step in studying Kelvin-wave turbulence
was done by Sonin

a Hamiltonian form of the Biot-Savart Equation for a slightly perturbed straight
vortex line. The final form of this Hamiltonian, found in Ref.
10LLNR
served as a basis for consistent statistical description of
Kevin wave turbulence by Lvov and Nazarenko

in the framework of standard kinetic equations for weak wave
turbulence
92ZLF
;
11Nazar
. This approach
LN-09
;
KW-2
;
KW-T
led to the spectrum of Kelvin waves

E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)

Zero temperature limit.

The total “line-energy density” of Kelvin waves
E_{{}_{\rm KW}}
(per unit length of the vortex line and normalized by the superfluid density) is given by the
k
-integral of the energy spectrum
E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)

\displaystyle E_{{}_{\rm KW}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)\,dk\,,\ E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)=2\,\omega(k)\,n(k)\ .~~~

(36a)

Here

n(k)/\hbar\to N(k)

\omega(k)
is the frequency of Kelvin waves. For our purposes it is sufficient to use Local-Induction-Approximation (LIA)

\displaystyle\omega(k)=\frac{\Lambda\kappa}{4\pi}\,k^{2}\,,\ \Lambda\equiv\ln\Big(\frac{\ell}{a}_{0}\Big)\,,

(36b)

A previous model of gradual eddy-wave crossover

;
LNR-2
was based on the Kozik-Svistunov (KS) spectrum of Kelvin
wave turbulence

E_{{}_{\rm KW}}^{{}^{\text{KS}}}(k)=C_{{}_{\rm KS}}\frac{\Lambda\,\kappa^{7/5}\,\epsilon^{1/5}}{k^{7/5}}\,,\quad\mbox{KS-spectrum .}

(37)

Here
C_{{}_{\rm KS}}\sim 1
is a dimensionless constant and
\epsilon
is the flux of
E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)
in the one-dimensional

k
-space. The KS spectrum (
37

92ZLF
;
11Nazar
for weakly
interacting Kelvin waves under the crucial assumptions that the energy transfer in Kelvin wave turbulence is a
step-by-step cascade, in which only Kelvin waves of similar wave numbers effectively interact with each other. However in
Ref.
10LLNR
it was shown that this locality assumption is not satisfied. This means that KS-spectrum is NOT a solution of
the kinetic equations and thus physically irrelevant.

The Kelvin wave turbulence theory was corrected and a
new
local Kelvin wave spectrum was derived by L’vov and Nazarenko (LN) in Ref.
LN-09

E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)=\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}}{\Psi^{2/3}}\,\frac{\Lambda\,\kappa\,\epsilon^{1/3}}{k^{5/3}}\,,\quad\mbox{LN-spectrum}.

(38)

Here
C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}\approx 0.304

KW-2
and the dimensionless
constant
\Psi
is given by Eq. (
5b

This KS vs LN controversy triggered an intensive debate (see e.g.
Refs
LN-debate2
;
LN-debate3
;
KS-debate
;
Sonin
;
LN-Sonin
),
which is outside the scope of this article. The two predicted exponents,
\frac{7}{5}=1.4
and
\frac{5}{3}\approx 1.67
are very close to
each other;
indeed vortex-filament simulations
Carlo4
could not distinguish them
(probably because in this numerical experiment the regime of weak turbulence
on which the theory is based and which requires a small ratio of the amplitude of the waves compared to the wavelength,
was not the sufficiently realized).
Nevertheless, more recent simulations by Krstulovic

long time integration of the Gross-Pitaevskii equations and averaged over an ensemble of
initial conditions (slightly deviating from a straight line), support the LN spectrum. The
most recent vortex-filament simulations by Baggaley and Laurie

C^{\text{num}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}\approx 0.308
close to
C^{\text{anal}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}\approx 0.304
while
C^{\text{num}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KS}}\approx 0.009

Differential approximation for the Kelvin-wave energy flux.

In
39

\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)=-\frac{\Psi^{2}k^{6}}{5(C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}\Lambda\kappa)^{3}}\,\frac{\partial E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}^{3}(k)}{\partial k}\,,

It was constructed by analogy with Eq. (

To analyze the temperature suppression of the Kelvin waves energy spectrum, consider the energy balance equation

\displaystyle E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k,T)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{LN}}}{\Psi^{2/3}}\,\frac{\Lambda\,\kappa\,\epsilon_{0}^{1/3}}{k^{5/3}}\Big[1-\Big(\frac{k}{k_{\text{max}}}\Big)^{4/3}\Big]\,,

\displaystyle 1/\ell\leqslant k\leqslant k_{\text{max}}\ .

Here

IV.1.2

Large-scale hydrodynamic region

In the hydrodynamic range of scales, the Biot-Savart description of superfluid turbulence is too detailed for our purposes and we
can return to the continuous medium approximation, Eqs. (
1
), used in the high temperature regime. The difference with Sec.
III

is that now we will first perform the statistical averaging of the velocity field, and only then analyze the resulting equations for
the energy spectra in the one-fluid approximation. This different strategy is dictated by a natural requirement that Kelvin waves and
hydrodynamic eddies have to be treated in a similar formal scheme in order to describe the intermediate region of scales where one
type of motion continuously turns into the other. As a candidate for this scheme we choose a differential closure that allows us to
express the energy flux as a differential form of the energy spectra. For the Kelvin waves this approximation was given by
Eq. (
39a
) and for hydrodynamic eddies it is discussed below.

The simplest approximation for the hydrodynamic energy flux
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
, based on the Kolmogorov idea of the locality
of the energy transfer and dimensional reasoning goes back to Kovasznay 1947 paper
Kov47
:

\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\simeq C{\mathcal{[}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)]^{3/2}k^{5/2}\ .

(41)

Here
C\sim 1
is a dimensionless constant.
The basic idea of such models is that the nonlinear terms, being of the
simplest possible form, should preserve the original turbulence
scalings and, in particular, predict correctly the Kolmogorov
cascade. Indeed, in the stationary case and in the
absence of dissipation the energy flux becomes
k
-independent,
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\Rightarrow\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}
. Then
Eq. (
41
) turns into the
Kolmogorov-Obukhov
5/3
–law for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
, given by Eq. (
6
).

Unfortunately, the simple relation (
41
) does not describe the thermodynamic equilibrium (with equipartition of energy between
states), when the energy flux vanishes for
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}\propto k^{2}
. This disadvantage is corrected in the Leith-1967 differential
model
Leith67
, given by Eq. (
7a
).
This approximation coincides dimensionally with the Kovasznay model (
41
), but has a derivative
d[{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)/k^{2}]
that guarantees that
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)=0
, if
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\propto k^{2}
.
The numerical factor
\frac{1}{8}
, suggested in
Nazar-Leith
, gives the value of the Kolmogorov constant
C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{K41}}=(24/11)^{2/3}\approx 1.7
in Eq. (
6
) that is reasonably close to the experimentally observed value.

A generic hydrodynamic spectrum with a constant energy flux was found in
Nazar-Leith
as a solution to the equation
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}=\mathrm{const}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)=C_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{K41}}\frac{\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{2/3}}{k^{5/3}}\left[1+\Big(\frac{k}{k_{\text{eq}}}\Big)^{11/2}\right]^{2/3}\,,

(42)

in which
k_{\text{eq}}
is as yet a free parameter describing the crossover between the low-
k
KO41 spectrum (
6
) and the thermalized part of the spectrum,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)\propto k^{2}
with equipartition of energy at large
k
.

Notice, that Eq. (
42
) does not account for the energy dissipation due to the mutual friction and the viscosity. We will do this
later, introducing dissipation terms in the energy balance equation and numerically solving them.

IV.2

One-fluid differential model of gradual eddy-wave crossover

IV.2.1

“Line-” and “volume-” energy densities, spectra and fluxes

Our goal here is to formulate a model which will allow to describe in a unified form the hydrodynamic energy flux
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
at small
k
and the corresponding objects for the Kelvin waves. However we cannot do it straightforwardly using
the equations for
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)
and the spectrum
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)
. The reason is simple: the hydrodynamic and Kevin wave objects have different physical meaning and different dimensions. Indeed, the hydrodynamic motions fill the three-dimensional space
(volume), their energy density
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}
per unit mass has a dimension
[{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}]
=cm
2
/sec
2
. Accordingly, the
dimensions of energy spectrum
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
and the energy flux
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)
are as follows:

[{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)]=\mbox{cm}^{3}/\mbox{sec}^{2}\,,\quad[\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}(k)]=\mbox{cm}^{2}/\mbox{sec}^{3}\ .

(43a)

On the other hand, Kelvin waves propagate along one-dimensional lines – vortex filaments. Therefore the energy density of Kelvin
waves on individual vortex filament
E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}
is normalized by unit vortex length and (for the sake of convenience) by superfluid
density. Therefore its dimension is
[E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}]
=cm
4
/sec
2
. Then the dimensions of the corresponding energy spectrum and the energy flux are:

[E_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)]=\mbox{cm}^{5}/\mbox{sec}^{2}\,,\quad[\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)]=\mbox{cm}^{4}/\mbox{sec}^{3}\ .

(43b)

Different normalization of the same objects dictates the relation between them
in a statistically homogeneous and isotropic vortex tangle with the line density
L=1/\ell^{2}

{\cal E}_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)=\frac{E_{{}_{\rm KW}}(k)}{\ell^{2}}\,,\ \varepsilon_{{}_{\rm KW}}=\frac{\epsilon_{{}_{\rm KW}}}{\ell^{2}}\ .

(44)

IV.2.2

Energy balance equation

Consider a general form (
8
) of the continuity equation for the energy density
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)
of the isotropic space-homogeneous
turbulence of the superfluid component
which accounts for the energy dissipation with the help of the Vinen-Niemella viscosity.
The remaining physical problem here is how to describe the energy density
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)
, the energy flux over scales

\varepsilon_{\text{s}}(k,t)
, and the damping term
{\mathcal{D}}(k,t)
in the entire range of wave vectors
k
, including the intervortex
scales
k\sim 1/\ell
. A step toward this direction was suggested in Ref.
LNR-1
in the form of the “Eddy-wave model” in which the superfluid motions with scales
R\sim\ell
are considered as a superposition of two coexisting and interacting types of motion: random eddies and Kelvin waves. In some sense the problem here is similar to the description of the mechanics of the matter
at intermediate range of scales, where it behaves like particles and waves simultaneously. In quantum mechanics it was suggested
to formulate explicitly the basic equation of motion (the Schrödinger equation) and to compare its prediction with
observations. We are not so ambitious, our goal is to discuss below a set of uncontrolled approximations, based currently only on our
physical intuition, which leads to an explicit set of model equations having a predictive power. As we will see below, our model
predicts that in the range
R\sim\ell
, due to the bottleneck energy accumulation, the energy distribution between scales is
close to the energy equipartition, like in the thermodynamic equilibrium. It is well known that in thermodynamic equilibrium
the statistics is universal and independent of the details of interaction. Therefore we hope that many details of the vortex
dynamics (including the vortex reconnections), that are ignored in our model, do not affect the model results: we believe that
these results are closer to the reality than the model itself.

IV.2.3

Gradual model for the energy spectra of superfluid component

The basic physical idea is to approximate the total turbulent superfluid energy density
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)
as a sum of the
hydrodynamic energy spectrum
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}(k,t)

and the energy spectrum of the Kelvin waves
{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k,t)
, with
the energy distribution between the components depending only on the dimensionless blending function
g(k\ell
) of the dimensionless wave-number
k\ell
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}(k,t)+{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k,t)\,,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}(k,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(k\ell)\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)\,,

(45)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[1-g(k\ell)]\,{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)\ .

In order to find a qualitative form of the blending function
g(k\ell)
we follow Ref
LNR-1
. Consider a system of locally near-parallel vortex lines (in
the vicinity of some point
{\bm{r}}_{0}
), separated by the mean distance
\ell
. Denote the individual vortex lines by an index
j
.
Notice that in principle the same vortex line can go far away and
come close to
{\bm{r}}_{0}
several times. To avoid this problem one should assign the same vortex
line a different index
j
if it leaves (or enters) the ball of radius
\ell\sqrt{\Lambda}

centered at
{\bm{r}}_{0}
. Each vortex line produces a superfluid velocity field
{\bm{u}}_{j}^{\text{s}}({\bm{r}})
, which can be found by the
Biot-Savart Law.

The total superfluid kinetic energy density (per unit mass)
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\text{s}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{j}^{\text{s}}\right\rangle
may be divided into two parts,

E_{\text{s}}={\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}+{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}
, where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\left\langle(u_{j}^{\text{s}})^{2}\right\rangle\,,

(46)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\text{s}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{j}^{\text{s}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i<j}\left\langle{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\text{s}}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{j}^{\text{s}}\right\rangle\ .

The same subdivision can also be made for the energy spectrum in the (one-dimensional)
k
-space,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k)={\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}(k)+{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}(k)
, with two terms, that may be found via
{\bm{k}}
-Fourier components of the superfluid velocity fields
{\bm{v}}_{j}^{\text{s}}({\bm{k}})
similar to Eq. (
46
).
Now the idea is as follows: the energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}(k)
is defined by the form of the individual vortex lines that is determined
by the Kelvin waves, while the energy
{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}(k)
depends on correlations in the form of different vortices, that produce
collective, hydrodynamic type of motions. Therefore
{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}(k)
may be associated with the Kelvin waves energy,
{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}(k)\Rightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)
, while
{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}(k)
has to be associated with the superfluid
hydrodynamic energy,
{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}(k)\Rightarrow{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}(k)
. This allows us to conclude that

g(k\ell)=\big[1+{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rm s}(k)/{\mathcal{E}}_{2\rm s}(k)\big]^{-1}\ .

(47)

The rest are technical details presented in Ref.
LNR-1
, where it was concluded that in practical calculations it is
reasonable to use an analytical form
g(k\ell)
of the blending function

\displaystyle g(k\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{0}\big[c_{1}\,\ln(\Lambda+7.5)\ k\ell\big]\,,

(48)

\displaystyle g_{0}(k\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[1+\frac{(k\ell)^{2}\exp(k\ell)}{4\pi(1+k\ell)}\,\Big]^{-1}\,,

where
c_{1}\approx 0.32
is the fitting parameter, chosen in
LNR-1
.

IV.2.4

Gradual model for the superfluids energy flux
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}(k,t)

Modeling the total superfluid energy flux over scales,
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}(k,t)
, is less straightforward than the model (
45
)
for the energy itself,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\text{s}}(k,t)
. We first assume that
\varepsilon_{\text{s}}(k,t)
may be presented as the sum of the fluxes over
hydrodynamic and Kelvin wave components,

\varepsilon_{\text{s}}=\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{\text{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}+\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}\,,

(49a)

however the fluxes
\widetilde{\varepsilon}^{\text{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}
and
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}
are
not equal
to the fluxes
\varepsilon^{\text{s}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}
, Eq. (
7a
),
and
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}
in
isolated
hydrodynamic and Kelvin wave systems. Equation (
39a
) for
\epsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)

in the volume normalization (
44
) takes the form (
7b
).

The fluxes
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}
and
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}
contain additional cross-contributions
\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{{}^{\text{KW}}}(k)
and

\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}^{{}^{\text{HD}}}(k)
that originate from the interaction of two types of motion, hydrodynamic and Kelvin waves:

\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}+\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{{}^{\text{KW}}}\,,\quad\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}=\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}+\varepsilon^{{}^{\text{HD}}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}\ .

(49b)

We modeled the cross-terms in the linear approximation with respect to the energies (i.e. the Hydrodynamic energy affecting
the Kelvin waves flux and vice versa):

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{{}^{\text{KW}}}(k)={\mathcal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}\}\ d\,[{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}(k)/k_{\text{en}}^{2}]/dk^{2}\,,

(49c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}^{{}^{\text{HD}}}(k)={\mathcal{F}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{KW}}\}\ d\,[{\mathcal{E}}_{\scriptscriptstyle\rm{HD}}^{\text{s}}(k)/k^{2}]/dk^{2}\,,

where
k_{\text{en}}
is the wave number, at which
g(k_{\text{en}}\ell)=\frac{1}{2}

Our goal here is to suggest a relatively simple and physically transparent model of the cross-correlation function of the normal

To start, we recall some definitions and relationships, required for our derivation, which are well-known in statistical physics. The

{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}G_{\text{nn}}({\bm{k}})\,\delta({\bm{k}}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\,,~~~

(54a)

\displaystyle\left\langle{\bm{v}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{k}},t)\cdot{\bm{v}}^{*}_{\text{s}}({\bm{k}}^{\prime},t)\right\rangle
