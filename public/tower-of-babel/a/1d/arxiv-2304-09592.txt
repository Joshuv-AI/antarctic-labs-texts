Efficient High-Order Space-Angle-Energy Polytopic Discontinuous Galerkin Finite Element Methods for Linear Boltzmann Transport
Paul Houston and Matthew E. Hubbard and Thomas J. Radley and Oliver J. Sutton and Richard S.J. Widdowson

Abstract
. We introduce an h ​ p hp –version discontinuous Galerkin finite element method (DGFEM) for the linear Boltzmann transport problem. A key feature of this new method is that, while offering arbitrary order convergence rates, it may be implemented in an almost identical form to standard multigroup discrete ordinates methods, meaning that solutions can be computed efficiently with high accuracy and in parallel within existing software. This method provides a unified discretisation of the space, angle, and energy domains of the underlying integro-differential equation and naturally incorporates both local mesh and local polynomial degree variation within each of these computational domains. Moreover, general polytopic elements can be handled by the method, enabling efficient discretisations of problems posed on complicated spatial geometries. We study the stability and h ​ p hp –version a priori error analysis of the proposed method, by deriving suitable h ​ p hp –approximation estimates together with a novel inf-sup bound. Numerical experiments highlighting the performance of the method for both polyenergetic and monoenergetic problems are presented. Keywords : h ​ p hp -finite element methods; discontinuous Galerkin methods; linear Boltzmann transport problem; polytopic meshes; discrete ordinates methods. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 65N12, 65N15, 65N30.



1.
Introduction

The linear Boltzmann transport problem describes the flow of particles through a scattering and absorbing medium, and is a widely used model in areas as diverse as medical imaging, radiotherapy treatment planning, and the design of nuclear reactors, for example.
Here, we consider the numerical approximation of the stationary form of the problem, seeking a solution which is a function of up to six independent variables:
d
,
d=2,3
, spatial variables varying over a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(d-1)
angular variables on the surface of the
d
-dimensional unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
, and an energy variable on the non-negative real line
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
The high dimensionality of this problem means that it is imperative to develop efficient numerical approximation methods.
Over the years numerous methods have been proposed for this problem, which we shall briefly review below.

Given the structure of the underlying problem, the space, angle and energy components of the solution are typically discretised separately using a variety of techniques.
Historically, there has largely been a predominant standard approach to energy discretisation known as the
multigroup approximation
; see
[
34
, Chapter 2]
and the references cited therein.
Essentially, this approach approximates the energy by a piecewise constant function with respect to a finite number of non-overlapping
energy groups
.
A key appeal of this approach is that the numerical solution is computed by sequentially solving a single monoenergetic Boltzmann transport problem (i.e., only depending on the spatial and angular variables) for each energy group.
This is possible because the scattering process is typically structured in such a way that particles only lose energy in each collision with the medium, either by producing secondary particles or depositing energy locally, and hence the solution in a given energy group only depends on the solution in groups at higher energies, cf., also
[
21
]
.

On the other hand, discretisations of the angular component of the solution have a rich history and numerous numerical schemes have been proposed.
A few classes of such schemes have received particular attention within the literature due to their numerical properties.
Spherical harmonic approximations are a widely used form of spectral discretisation in angle, constructed utilising a basis of typically high-order smooth spherical harmonic functions defined globally on the sphere; see
[
14
,
19
,
34
]
.
The emphasis of such schemes is to simplify the implementation of the scattering operator, typically at the expense of a more expensive implementation of the streaming operator.
Such schemes offer a natural variational setting for their analysis, but the global nature of the basis functions makes local adaptivity a challenging task and Gibbs’-type oscillations may be expected around sharp variations in the solution.

An alternative strain of methods are collectively known as discrete ordinates methods, in which the angular component of the problem is discretised via collocation at a discrete set of angular quadrature points.
The advantage of this approach is that, when combined with an appropriate linear solver, the Boltzmann transport problem may be solved in parallel as a set of independent linear transport problems in the spatial domain with fixed wind directions.
There appear to be two predominant flavours of discrete ordinates-type methods in the literature, which may be coarsely classified as
global high-order methods
and
local low-order methods
.
Schemes in the former category typically fall within the family of spectral collocation methods, based on sets of interpolatory or quadrature points for high-order spherical harmonic functions on the sphere, designed according to the principles laid out by Sobolev and Vaskevich in
[
39
]
.
Such schemes include those based on the widely used
level symmetric quadrature formulae in
[
29
,
13
,
26
]
,
Lebedev quadrature schemes in
[
33
,
32
]
,
general double cyclic triangle quadratures in
[
27
,
26
]
,
or sets of points arranged on spherical
t
-designs in
[
3
]
, to name but a few.
The appeal of such methods is that they formally approximate the solution using high-order spherical harmonics,
although generating efficient point sets can become difficult for very high-orders, limiting the theoretical accuracy of such schemes.
Moreover, it is typically challenging to produce such point sets adaptively, i.e., to focus quadrature points in zones of the angular domain where higher resolution is required, for instance, around beams or other localised structures present in the underlying solution.

Complementing these are methods based on quadrature sets constructed locally using an angular mesh.
Typically, the quadrature schemes used are exact for constant functions on each element, such as so-called
T_{N}
schemes, cf.
[
42
]
, or sometimes linear or quadratic functions; see
[
23
,
24
,
30
,
31
,
43
]
.
In a similar category, we include methods based on interpolation using continuous finite element basis functions in angle, such as those of
[
20
]
, and schemes incorporating piecewise spherical harmonic approximations on an angular mesh in
[
28
]
and wavelet-based approaches in
[
8
,
2
]
.
Although such schemes formally approximate the solution using lower-order polynomials, the ability to generate a higher fidelity approximation by refining the mesh, either locally or globally, has contributed to their significant popularity.
Recent work has generalised these schemes to use higher order polynomials in angle in various different ways; see, for example,
[
28
,
21
,
43
]
.
While such schemes offer the possibility of high-order convergence and mesh adaptivity, underpinned by a variational framework, they can be more challenging to implement efficiently because the high-order nature of the basis functions on each angular element means that the problem does not immediately facilitate a discrete ordinates-like decoupling into independent spatial transport problems.

In this article, we propose a state of the art
hp
-version discontinuous Galerkin finite element method (DGFEM) for the discretisation of the linear Boltzmann transport problem, in which the space, angle, and energy components of the solution are approximated in a unified manner. In many applications, particularly those arising in medical physics, the spatial domain may be highly complicated; to deal with such strong complexity of the physical geometry, in an efficient manner, we admit the use of general polytopic meshes; see, for example,
[
12
,
10
,
11
]
and the references cited therein. The key properties and advantages of the proposed methodology include: the exploitation of a unified DGFEM discretisation of the linear Boltzmann problem over the entire computational domain ensures that the resulting scheme is naturally high-order; note that, in particular, the use of the aforementioned multigroup approximation limits the accuracy of the
resulting numerical method to first-order. Taking advantage of the intrinsic variational formulation of the scheme means that the convergence and stability analysis of the underlying DGFEM can be developed, which is the key objective of this article. Furthermore, the proposed framework naturally lends itself to the exploitation of
hp
–adaptivity techniques coupled with rigorous a posteriori error estimation to ensure that the spatial, angular, and energy meshes can be focused around solution features of interest. Moreover, as already highlighted above, complex geometries can be efficiently meshed and easily handled. Finally, and perhaps most importantly from a practical viewpoint, the proposed method enables arbitrary order mesh-based approximations to be built independently in each of the space, angle and energy domains, while still being implemented in the same way as conventional multigroup discrete ordinates schemes.
This highly efficient and naturally parallelisable implementation is made possible by exploiting a novel set of basis functions for the polynomial function spaces which satisfy a Lagrangian property at the nodes of a (tensor product) Gaussian quadrature scheme.
We point out that the mathematical convergence results presented in this article complement those of Johnson and Pitkäranta in
[
25
]
, who derived the first
a priori
error estimates for a discrete ordinates DGFEM approximation of the monoenergetic Boltzmann transport problem, albeit under very low regularity assumptions on the analytical solution.

We remark that a very popular alternative computational framework for simulating the linear Boltzmann transport problem are Monte Carlo methods, which are widely used in practice.
Such methods naturally incorporate the stochastic nature of the underlying physical processes and are highly efficient to implement, as the trajectories of individual incoming particles are simulated independently, yet the mean observed behaviour may only be expected to converge with the square root of the number of samples used.
For this reason, Börgers in
[
9
]
identified that finite element-based methods could expect to perform more efficiently than Monte Carlo-based methods if high-order finite element methods could be utilised in a suitably efficient way.
Our work therefore provides a stepping stone to answering the open question of how to achieve this objective in practice, as we are able to compute high-order numerical approximations with minimal additional computational overhead compared to conventional multigroup discrete ordinates methods.

The outline for this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the linear Boltzmann transport problem. Then in Section
3
, we formulate the unified
hp
–version DGFEM discretisation.
Section
4
introduces the necessary inverse and
hp
–approximation results; on the basis of these bounds, the stability and convergence analysis of the underlying DGFEM is undertaken in Section
5
. In Section
6
we outline how the proposed DGFEM may be implemented in a highly efficient and parallelisable manner based on employing a careful selection of the quadrature and local polynomial bases in the angular and energy domains.
The practical performance of the method is assessed in Section
7
through a series of numerical examples. Finally, in Section
8
we summarise the work presented in this paper and draw some conclusions.

\|\cdot\|_{H^{k}(\omega)}
, as detailed in

]
, for example. Furthermore, we let
L_{p}(\omega)
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, be the standard Lebesgue space on

, equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}(\omega)}
. Similarly, for a bounded
(d-1)

2.
Model problem

Given an open bounded polyhedral spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d=2
or
3
, let
\mathcal{D}=\Omega\times\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{E}
, where
\mathbb{S}=\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:{\lvert\bm{\mu}\rvert}_{2}=1\}
denotes the surface of the
d
-dimensional unit sphere and
\mathbb{E}=\{E\in\mathbb{R}:E\geq 0\}
is the real half line.

The linear Boltzmann transport problem reads:
find
u:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}u({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)+(\alpha({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)+\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E))u({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}[u]({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)

\displaystyle\qquad+f({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\text{ in }\mathcal{D},

\displaystyle u({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)

\displaystyle=g_{\rm D}({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\text{ on }\Gamma_{\operatorname{in}},

(1)

where
f,g_{\rm D},\alpha,\beta:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
are given data terms (discussed further below),
\nabla_{{\bf x}}
is the spatial gradient operator,
and
\Gamma_{\operatorname{in}}=\{({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\in\bar{\mathcal{D}}:{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega\text{ and }\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}<0\}
denotes the inflow boundary of
\mathcal{D}
, where
\bm{n}
denotes the unit outward normal vector on the boundary
\partial\Omega
of
\Omega
.
The action of the
scattering operator
applied to the solution
u
is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}[u]({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\to\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)u({\bf x},\bm{\eta},E^{\prime})\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime},

where
\theta
is a specified scattering kernel,
and

\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\mu}\to\bm{\eta},E\to E^{\prime})\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}
.

Physically, the model (
1
) describes the transport of particles through a scattering medium, and is linear due to the key physical assumption that particles are only scattered by interactions with the medium and do not interact with one another.
The solution
u({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)
represents the fluence of particles with a particular energy
E\in\mathbb{E}
, travelling in direction
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}
, passing through the point
{\bf x}\in\Omega
.
The scattering kernel
\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\to\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)
represents the proportion of particles at position
{\bf x}
with energy
E^{\prime}
travelling in direction
\bm{\eta}
which transition to direction
\bm{\mu}
and energy
E
as a result of an instantaneous collision with the medium.
Conversely, the reaction coefficient
\alpha({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)+\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)
, commonly referred to as the
total scattering cross section
, models loss of particles from the fluence in direction
\bm{\mu}
with energy
E
as they are absorbed by the medium (
\alpha
) or scattered into other directions and energies (
\beta
).

We simplify the model slightly by assuming that the medium is angularly isotropic in the sense that
\alpha({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\equiv\alpha({\bf x},E)
and the scattering kernel depends only on the cosine of the angle between the initial and final directions; i.e.,
\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\to\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)\equiv\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)
.
This has the implication that
\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\equiv\beta({\bf x},E)
by symmetry.
Furthermore, we make the (physically reasonable) assumption that
\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)=0
for
E^{\prime}<E
, which states that particles cannot gain energy by scattering off the medium.
Finally, we assume that
f
and
g
are compactly supported functions of energy, and that there exists a constant
c_{0}
such that

c({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E):=\alpha({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)+\frac{1}{2}(\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)-\gamma({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E))\geq c_{0}>0,

(2)

where
\gamma({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\eta}\to\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\to E)\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}
.
For notational simplicity, henceforth we will suppress the dependence of the data terms
\alpha,\beta,f
and
g_{\rm D}
on the independent variables.

Remark 1
.

In practice the absorption cross section
\alpha
may be equal to zero; hence, in this setting, condition (
2
) reduces to the requirement that
\beta-\gamma\geq c_{0}^{\prime}>0
,
c_{0}^{\prime}=2c_{0}
, or more precisely that the macroscopic scattering cross section related to outgoing directions and energies (
\beta
) is greater than the corresponding quantity related to the incoming directions and energies (
\gamma
). An important scattering model employed in practice for photons is the Klein-Nishina scattering model, discussed in Section
7
; one can show that this model does indeed satisfy (
2
) within a physical range of energies; see
[
36
]
for details.

3.
Discrete scheme

We discretise the Boltzmann transport problem (
1
) using a DGFEM approach, seeking an approximate solution which is a product of discontinuous piecewise polynomial functions with respect to meshes defined in the spatial, angular, and energy domains separately.
For this, we introduce the following notation.

3.1.
Spatial discretisation

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
be a subdivision of the spatial domain
\Omega
into non-overlapping open polytopic elements
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
with diameter
h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
such that
\overline{\Omega}=\cup\,\overline{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
. The set of faces in
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{\Omega}
, which are defined as the
(d-1)
-dimensional planar facets of the elements
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
present in the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
. For
d=3
, we assume that each planar face of an element
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
can be subdivided into a set of co-planar
(d-1)
-dimensional simplices and we refer to this set as the set of faces, as in
[
11
]
.
Given
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
, we denote by
p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\geq 0
the polynomial degree on
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
, and define the vector
{\bf p}:=(p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}:\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega})
.
The spatial finite element space is then defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\bf p}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\{v\in L_{2}(\Omega):v|_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\in\mathbb{P}_{p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})\text{ for all }\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{k}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})
denotes the space of polynomials of total degree
k
on
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
.
We denote by
\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
the union of
(d-1)
–dimensional open faces of
the element
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
. Then, for a given direction
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}
the inflow and outflow parts of
\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
are defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}

\displaystyle=\{{\bf x}\in\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}:~\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}({\bf x})<0\},

\displaystyle\partial_{+}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}

\displaystyle=\{{\bf x}\in\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}:~\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}({\bf x})\geq 0\},

respectively, where
\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}({\bf x})
denotes the unit outward normal vector to
\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}

at
{\bf x}\in\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
.

Given
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
, the trace of a (sufficiently smooth) function
v
on
\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
from
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}

is denoted by
v^{+}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
. Further, if
\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega
is nonempty, then for
{\bf x}\in\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega

there exists a unique
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
such that
{\bf x}\in\partial_{+}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}^{\prime}
; with this notation, we
denote by
v^{-}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
the trace of
v|_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}^{\prime}}
on
\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega
.
Hence the upwind jump of the function
v
across a face
F\subset\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega
is denoted by

\lfloor v\rfloor:=v^{+}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}-v^{-}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}.

In the remainder of the article we suppress the subscript
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
, since it will always be clear which element
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
the quantities
v_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\pm}
correspond to.

3.2.
Angular discretisation

A general framework developed for solving partial differential equations on surfaces has been developed in
[
4
,
17
,
18
]
and the references cited therein.
Given that
our particular setting is greatly simplified, we proceed in a slightly different manner.
Let
\mathbb{S}_{h}
to denote a polyhedral surface in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
composed of (closed)
planar faces
\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
which are assumed to be either simplices (intervals if
d=2
;
triangles if
d=3
)
or (affine) quadrilaterals (when
d=3
). We write
\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}=\{\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\}
to denote the
associated regular conforming triangulation of
\mathbb{S}_{h}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{S}_{h}=\cup_{\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}}\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
.
We now introduce a smooth invertible mapping
\phi_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
;
for example, assuming the surface is star-shaped with respect to the origin, we may simply define
\phi_{\mathbb{S}}(\tilde{\bm{\mu}})={\lvert\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\rvert}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}
, where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the
l_{2}
-norm.
With this notation, we define a mesh of curved surface elements defined on
\mathbb{S}
by

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}=\left\{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}:\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}=\phi_{\mathbb{S}}(\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})~\forall\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}\right\}.

Crucially, we assume that elements
\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}
are mapped to

\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}
, without any significant rescaling. More precisely, we assume that the
determinant of the inverse of the first fundamental form of the mapping

\phi_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
is uniformly bounded from above and below,
cf.
[
18
]
. Following
[
4
,
17
,
18
]
, in the
case when
\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}
is composed of simplices, then
\mathbb{S}_{h}
may, for example, be chosen to be a piecewise
linear approximation of
\mathbb{S}
, whereby the elements forming
\mathbb{S}_{h}
may be
constructed so that their vertices lie on
\mathbb{S}
.
In the case when quadrilateral elements are employed,
then an initial polyhedral domain
\mathbb{S}_{h}^{\prime}
may be constructed in a similar fashion, though in
general the resulting element domains will not be affine.
In this setting,
we assume there exists
\mathbb{S}_{h}
consisting of affine quadrilateral elements, in such a manner
that the corresponding quadrilateral facets of
\mathbb{S}_{h}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{S}_{h}
may be
mapped to one another without any significant rescaling.
We stress that, irrespective of the specific choice of
\mathbb{S}_{h}
, the assumption on scaling of the Jacobian of the mapping

\phi_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
is crucial to ensure that Lemma
13

holds, see Section
4
below.

Since the surface we are interested in discretising is simply the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, a practical choice for
\mathbb{S}_{h}
is the surface of the cube
[-1,1]^{d}
.
This leads to the widely used cube-sphere discretisation of the sphere, and enables a particularly simplified implementation of the method.

Let
\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
denote the reference element
(either a simplex or quadrilateral),
\phi_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}:\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\rightarrow\tilde{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
, which is assumed to be affine, and define

F_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}:\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\rightarrow\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
by

F_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}=\phi_{\mathbb{S}}\circ\phi_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}
.
For each
\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}
, let
q_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}\geq 0
denote the polynomial degree used on
\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
, and introduce
{\bf q}:=(q_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}:\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}})
.
The finite element space defined on the surface of the sphere
\mathbb{S}
is then given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\bf q}_{\mathbb{S}}=\{v\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}):v|_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}=\hat{v}\circ F_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}^{-1},~\hat{v}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{q_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})\text{ for all }\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}\},

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})=\mathbb{P}_{k}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})
if
\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
is a simplex and
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})=\mathbb{Q}_{k}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})
if
\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
is a square; here
\mathbb{Q}_{k}(\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}})
denotes the space of tensor product polynomials on
\hat{\kappa}_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}
of degree
k
in each coordinate direction.

3.3.
Energy discretisation

We first restrict the energy domain to be a finite interval by selecting minimum and maximum energy cutoffs
E_{\min}
and
E_{\max}
, respectively. Due to the assumption that the problem data is compactly supported in energy and the assumption on the structure of the scattering kernel, these limits may be chosen so that the analytical solution is compactly supported in energy; with a slight abuse of notation we refer to
\mathbb{E}
to be this restricted domain
(E_{\min},E_{\max})
.

Then, for
N_{\mathbb{E}}\geq 1
, let
E_{\max}=E_{0}>E_{1}>\ldots>E_{N_{\mathbb{E}}-1}>E_{N_{\mathbb{E}}}=E_{\min}
define a partition of the energy domain of the problem into
N_{\mathbb{E}}

energy groups
.
We will refer to the interval
\kappa_{g}=(E_{g},E_{g-1})
as energy group
g
,
g=1,\dots,N_{\mathbb{E}}
, and define
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}=\{\kappa_{g}\}_{g=1}^{N_{\mathbb{E}}}
. To each energy group
\kappa_{g}
,
g=1,\dots,N_{\mathbb{E}}
, we associate a polynomial degree
r_{g}\geq 0
. Defining
{\bf r}=(r_{\kappa_{g}})_{g=1}^{N_{\mathbb{E}}}
, we introduce the energy finite element space

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\bf r}_{\mathbb{E}}=\{v\in L_{2}(E_{\min},E_{\max}):v|_{\kappa_{g}}\in\mathbb{P}_{r_{\kappa_{g}}}(\kappa_{g})\text{ for all }\kappa_{g}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}\}.

3.4.
Discontinuous Galerkin scheme

Employing the definitions introduced in the previous sections, we define the full space-angle-energy mesh by

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}=\{\kappa:\kappa=\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\times\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g},~\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},~\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}},~\kappa_{g}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}\}.

Over the mesh
\mathcal{T}
, we combine the separate function spaces defined above to obtain the discretisation space

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}=\mathbb{V}^{\bf p}_{\Omega}\otimes\mathbb{V}^{\bf q}_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\mathbb{V}^{\bf r}_{\mathbb{E}},

and, for any
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}
, let
\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\mu},h}=\{v\in L_{2}(\Omega):\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v|_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\in L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})\text{ for all }\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\}
denote the broken spatial graph space.

We define the upwind transport bilinear form
a_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}:\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\mu},h}\times\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\mu},h}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle a_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(w,v)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}wv+(\alpha+\beta)wv)\,d{\bf x}

\displaystyle-\!\!\!\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}\!\!(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})\lfloor w\rfloor v^{+}\,ds

\displaystyle-\!\!\!\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})w^{+}v^{+}\,ds,

and further define the scattering bilinear form
s_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}:L_{2}(\mathcal{D})\times L_{2}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
and load linear form
\ell_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}:\mathcal{G}_{\bm{\mu},h}\to\mathbb{R}
, respectively, by

\displaystyle s_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(w,v)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}[w]({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)v\,d{\bf x},

and

\displaystyle\ell_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(v)=\int_{\Omega}fw\,d{\bf x}-\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})g_{\rm D}w\,ds.

Finally, we introduce the DGFEM: find
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
such that

\displaystyle b(u_{h},v_{h})\equiv a(u_{h},v_{h})-s(u_{h},v_{h})=\ell(v_{h})

(3)

for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
, where
a,s:\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\times\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\ell:\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
are given, respectively, by

\displaystyle a(w_{h},v_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}a_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(w_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE,\qquad s(w_{h},v_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}s_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(w_{h},v_{h})\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE,

\displaystyle\ell(v_{h})=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\ell_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(v_{h})\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE.

We note that this scheme is consistent in the sense that if the analytical solution
u
to (
1
) is sufficiently smooth then

\displaystyle b(u,v)=\ell(v)

for all
v\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
.

5.
Stability and convergence of the discrete scheme

In this section we study the stability and convergence of the DGFEM (
3
).
To this end, we introduce the DGFEM-
energy norm

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{\rm DG}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{c}\,v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\Big(\|v^{+}-v^{-}\|_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}^{2}+\|v^{+}\|_{\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\Big)\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE,

(12)

and

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}={\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{\rm DG}^{2}+\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}{\lVert\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE.

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{\omega}
,
\omega\subset\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}
, denotes the (semi)norm associated with the (semi)inner product
(v,w)_{\omega}=\int_{\omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}|vw\,ds
. Furthermore, for
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
, we define

\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}=\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}{p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}.\

Firstly, we state the following coercivity bound.

Theorem 17

(Coercivity)
.

The DGFEM (
3
) is coercive with respect to the DGFEM-energy norm
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{\cdot}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{\rm DG}
in the sense that the following bound holds:

\displaystyle b(v,v)\geq{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{\rm DG}^{2}

for all
v\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
.

Proof.

Integrating by parts and rearranging the face terms, the transport bilinear form satisfies

\displaystyle a_{\bm{\mu}}^{E}(v,v)={\lVert(\alpha+\beta)^{1/2}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\Big(\|v^{+}-v^{-}\|_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}^{2}+\|v^{+}\|_{\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\Big),

as shown in
[
22
]
.
Recalling that
\beta({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\eta},E\to E^{\prime})\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}
and
\gamma({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\eta},E^{\prime}\to E)\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}
, employing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that the scattering term may be bounded by

\displaystyle s(v,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\eta},E^{\prime}\to E)v({\bf x},\bm{\eta},E^{\prime})v({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\,d{\bf x}\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

\displaystyle\leq\|\beta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\|\gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\beta^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}v\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}.

The result then follows by combining these bounds with the definition of
c
in (
2
).
∎

We now derive an inf-sup stability result in the streamline norm

.

Theorem 18

(Inf-sup stability)
.

Given that Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
7
hold, then the DGFEM (
3
) is inf-sup stable in the streamline norm, i.e., there exists a constant
\Lambda>0
, independent of discretisation parameters, such that

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{w\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(v,w)}{{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}}\geq\Lambda.

Proof.

The proof follows a standard form for inf-sup results, and is similar to the argument presented in
[
10
]
for a scalar advection problem, adapted to the Boltzmann setting.
To this end, we construct a function
w\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
for each
v\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}
such that
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq\Lambda_{1}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
and

b(v,w)\geq\Lambda_{2}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}
.
The result then follows with
\Lambda=\nicefrac{{\Lambda_{2}}}{{\Lambda_{1}}}
.

Let
w({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)=v({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)+\delta v_{s}({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)
where
\delta>0
is a constant which will be determined, depending only on the problem data, and

on each spatial element
\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
.
To prove that there exists
C>0
such that
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq C{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
, we apply the triangle inequality to find

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}+\delta{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v_{s}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s},

and bound each term of
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v_{s}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
by
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
individually.
Observing that
{\lvert\bm{\mu}\rvert}=1
, upon application of the inverse inequality stated in Lemma
8
, recalling the definition of
\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
and noting that
h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}\leq h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{c}v_{s}\|_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}{\lVert\sqrt{c}\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}{\lVert c\rVert}_{L_{\infty}\!(\mathcal{D})}}{c_{0}}{\lVert\sqrt{c}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}{\lVert\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v_{s}\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

\displaystyle\qquad\leq C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}{\lVert\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE.

We now consider the face terms arising in the definition of the streamline norm
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{\cdot}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
. Noting
that
{\lvert\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\rvert}\leq 1
, applying the inverse inequality stated in Lemma
6
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\Big(\|v_{s}^{+}-v_{s}^{-}\|_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}^{2}+\|v_{s}^{+}\|_{\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\Big)\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\sum_{F\subset\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\|v_{s}^{+}\|_{L_{2}(F)}^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE\leq C_{\rm inv}^{F}C_{F}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}.

Since the terms resulting from these bounds are components of
{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{\cdot}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq\Lambda_{1}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\quad\text{ with }\quad\Lambda_{1}=1+\delta\Big(C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\Big(1+\frac{{\lVert c\rVert}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}}{c_{0}}\Big)+C_{\rm inv}^{F}C_{F}\Big)^{1/2}.

We now show that

.

17
, we deduce that

\displaystyle b(v,w)=b(v,v)+\delta b(v,v_{s})\geq{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{\rm DG}^{2}+\delta(a(v,v_{s})-s(v,v_{s})),

(13)

and expanding the second term on the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle a(v,v_{s})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\Big({\lVert\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}+\int_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}(\alpha+\beta)(\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v)v\,d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})\lfloor v\rfloor\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v^{+}\,ds

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{n}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})v^{+}\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v^{+}\,ds\Big)\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

Term
{\rm I}
is already in the required form; employing Lemma
8

{\rm II}
may be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle|{\rm II}|

\displaystyle\leq{\lVert\alpha+\beta\rVert}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\|\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v\|_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}\|v\|_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE

\displaystyle\leq\left(C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\frac{{\lVert\alpha+\beta\rVert}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}}{c_{0}}\|\sqrt{c}v\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}.

We now consider the face terms present in terms
{\rm III}
and
{\rm IV}
; employing the inverse inequality in Lemma

together with Young’s inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle|{\rm III}+{\rm IV}|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\!\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\!\!\!\big(C_{F}^{2}C_{\rm inv}^{F}\big(\|v^{+}\!\!-\!v^{-}\|_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}^{2}+\|v^{+}\|_{\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}}{4}\|\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{{\bf x}}v\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\big)\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE.

Finally, we bound the scattering term; recalling
the definition of
\beta
and
\gamma
, employing
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
8
gives

\displaystyle s(v,v_{s})=\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta({\bf x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\eta},E^{\prime}\to E)v({\bf x},\bm{\eta},E^{\prime})\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v({\bf x},\bm{\mu},E)\,d\bm{\eta}\,dE^{\prime}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE\,d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\beta v^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE\,d{\bf x}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}\int_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\gamma(\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v)^{2}\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE\,d{\bf x}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\frac{{\lVert\beta\rVert}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}{\lVert\gamma\rVert}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}}{c_{0}}{\lVert\sqrt{c}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}.

Combining the individual estimates above, we deduce that

\displaystyle b(v,w)\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\Big(C_{1}\|\sqrt{c}v\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}+C_{2}\Big(\|v^{+}-v^{-}\|_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\backslash\partial\Omega}^{2}+\|v^{+}\|_{\partial\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{3\delta}{4}\tau_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}{\lVert\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\bf x}v\rVert}_{L_{2}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega})}^{2}\Big)\,d\bm{\mu}\,dE.

where

and

.
Setting

\displaystyle 0<\delta<\min\left\{\frac{c_{0}}{\left(C_{\rm inv}^{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big({\lVert\alpha+\beta\rVert}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}+{\lVert\beta\rVert}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}{\lVert\gamma\rVert}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{L_{\infty}(\mathcal{D})}\big)},\frac{1}{2C_{F}^{2}C_{\rm inv}^{F}}\right\},

we conclude that

b(v,w)\geq\Lambda_{2}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{v}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}

and the result follows.

Theorem 19

(Convergence in the streamline norm)
.

Given the mesh
\mathcal{T}
defined over the space-angle-energy domain
\mathcal{D}
, we assume that the spatial polytopic mesh
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
satisfies Assumptions
2
,
3
, and
7
. Let

denote the solution of the problem (
1
) and suppose that

.
Then it follows that

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{u-u_{h}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle~C\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2s_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}}}{p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2l_{\kappa}}}\left({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+\frac{1}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}(1+p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2})+\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}{h^{2}_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}}\right){\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa)}^{2}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}^{2s_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}}}{q_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}^{2l_{\kappa}}}+\frac{h_{\kappa_{g}}^{2s_{\kappa_{g}}}}{r_{\kappa_{g}}^{2l_{\kappa}}}\right)\left(\left({\mathcal{L}}_{\kappa}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+\frac{1}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}\right){\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa)}^{2}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\quad+\left(\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}+\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}{p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}\right){\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{1}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega};H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g}))}^{2}\right)\right),

where

,

Proof.

The triangle inequality implies that

{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{u-u_{h}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{u-\Pi u}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}+{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{\Pi u-u_{h}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s},

(14)

where
\Pi

. Exploiting

14
, the first term on the right-hand side of (

) can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{u-\Pi u}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle~C\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{T}}M_{\kappa}{\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa)}^{2}+T_{\kappa}\left(\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}+\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}{p_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}\right){\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{1}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega};H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g}))}^{2},

where

{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{\Pi u-u_{h}}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}\leq\frac{1}{\Lambda}\sup_{w\in\mathbb{V}^{{\bf p},{\bf q},{\bf r}}_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(u-\Pi u,w)}{{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}}.

\displaystyle\left.+\left(\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}^{2s_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}}}{q_{\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}}^{2l_{\kappa}}}+\frac{h_{\kappa_{g}}^{2s_{\kappa_{g}}}}{r_{\kappa_{g}}^{2l_{\kappa}}}\right)\left(\left(\frac{{\lVert\alpha+\beta\rVert}_{L_{\infty}(\kappa)}^{2}}{c_{0}}+\frac{1}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}\right){\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa)}^{2}\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{2}}{h_{\kappa_{\tiny\Omega}}^{\bot}}{\lVert u\rVert}_{H^{1}(\kappa_{\tiny\Omega};H^{l_{\kappa}}(\kappa_{\tiny\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g}))}^{2}\right)\right)\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert\kern-0.85358pt\lvert{w}\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert\kern-0.85358pt\rvert}_{s}.

\displaystyle s(u_{\Pi},w)

6.
Efficient implementation as a multigroup discrete ordinates scheme

The numerical method (
3
) introduced above can be implemented in the framework of a multigroup discrete ordinates scheme.
Although at first sight it appears that the method fully couples the space, angle and energy unknowns, we show that, through a judicious choice of basis functions and element quadrature schemes, it is possible to evaluate the DGFEM solution by simply computing a sequence of linear transport problems in the
d
spatial variables. To this end, we first consider the multigroup approximation in energy before outlining the angular implementation.

6.1.
Multigroup implementation in energy

We first show how the energy dependence of the problem may be decoupled.
If we had perfect knowledge of the function

6.2.
Discrete ordinates implementation in angle

To investigate the structure of the matrix

8.
Conclusions

We have introduced a unified
hp
–version DGFEM for the numerical approximation of the linear Boltzmann transport problem.
We have proven stability and convergence results for the method, through an inf-sup condition in an appropriate norm, and shown how it may be efficiently implemented as a high-order version of the widely used multigroup discrete ordinates method.
The unified DGFEM formulation in the space, angle and energy domains therefore provides a simple and flexible way of computing arbitrary order approximations of solutions to the Boltzmann transport problem for the first time.
General classes of polytopic elements are admitted for the design of the spatial computational mesh, which facilitates the accurate and efficient representation of complex geometries.
Numerical experiments have been presented which confirm the theoretical results derived in this paper.
Further work will include using this scheme within an
hp
-refinement mesh adaptation algorithm, and investigating problems arising in medical physics applications.
