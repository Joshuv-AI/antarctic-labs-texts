Exploring the Enigmatic Chiral Phase Transition of QCD at Finite Temperature
Jingxu Wu 1 , Congyu Li 2 , Yan Wang 3 and Tuerdi Wumaier 1 1 Key Laboratory of New Energy Materials Research, Xinjiang Institute of Engineering 2 Cuiying Honors College, Lanzhou University 3 School of Physics Science and Technology, Lanzhou University

Abstract
This paper offers a comprehensive overview of recent technological advancements concerning the critical point of chiral phase transition, with a particular focus on Effective Field Theories in Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD). It delves into the intricacies of these pivotal points, utilizing theoretical tools to explore associated phenomena.

Introduction

In the forefront of contemporary scientific research, Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) plays a crucial role as the fundamental theory that reveals the internal structure and interactions of hadrons. QCD provides the framework for understanding the strong interactions between quarks and gluons, offering essential theoretical foundations for exploring high-energy physics, cosmology, and nuclear physics

Within the complexity of QCD, the phenomenon of chiral phase transition stands out as an intriguing aspect, providing a unique perspective for understanding the behavior of matter under extreme conditions. Chiral phase transition refers to a fundamental change in the properties of quark interactions under finite temperature conditions

. This phenomenon offers valuable insights into the early evolution of the universe and the formation of quark-gluon plasmas, believed to exist within a few microseconds after the Big Bang.

At zero temperature, the QCD Lagrangian respects chiral symmetry, meaning that the transformation properties of left-handed and right-handed quarks are identical. However, finite temperature environments can disrupt this symmetry, leading to the spontaneous breaking of chiral symmetry. This breaking manifests at a macroscopic scale as quark condensation, revealing the presence of a chiral condensate in the vacuum. As the system’s temperature increases, the significant influence of the thermal environment causes changes in the vacuum structure. At the critical temperature known as the chiral phase transition temperature (
T_{c}
), the QCD system undergoes a phase transition. Above this critical temperature, chiral symmetry is restored, and quark condensates diminish to zero. The occurrence of chiral phase transition is closely related to the transition from hadronic matter to quark-gluon plasma, where the distinct particle properties of quarks and gluons become less apparent, resembling the early conditions in the universe a few microseconds after the Big Bang
[
13
,
14
]
.

Experimental studies of chiral phase transition face significant challenges due to the necessity of conducting experiments under extreme conditions. However, heavy-ion collision experiments, such as those conducted at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) and Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC), provide valuable opportunities to investigate chiral phase transition. Researchers attempt to reveal the characteristics of chiral phase transition by analyzing particle spectra, collective flow, and other observable quantities. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of the fundamental properties of matter and offers a window into the evolution of the universe and the behavior of matter under extreme energy conditions.

In this context, this paper aims to delve into the chiral phase transition and its proximity to the critical point in QCD effective theories. We will focus on the theoretical framework, key concepts, and relevant experimental progress, considering the QCD effective theories near the critical point of the chiral phase transition.

In this paper, we consider QCD effective theories near the critical point of the chiral phase transition. For QCD with
N_{f}
flavors of massless quarks, its classical Lagrangian exhibits significant symmetries:

G=SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})\times U_{A}(1)\times U_{B}(1)\times SU_{c}(3)

(1)

Considering the dynamical breaking and restoration of symmetries, and we assume that the
U_{B}(1)\times SU_{c}(3)
sector remains unbroken, a hypothesis that might be violated in high-density color superconductors.

We introduce the order parameter for chiral symmetry, which is an
N_{f}\times N_{f}
matrix and transforms as a singlet under
U_{B}(1)\times SU_{c}(3)
:

\Phi_{ij}\sim\frac{1}{2}\bar{q}^{j}(1-\gamma_{5})q^{i}=\bar{q}_{R}^{j}q_{L}^{i}

(2)

Where
i
and
j
are flavor indices. The behavior of this order parameter under transformations of
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})\times U_{A}(1)
is given by:

\Phi_{ij}\rightarrow e^{i\alpha V_{L}}\Phi e^{i\alpha V_{R}^{\dagger}}

(3)

Here,
V_{L}(V_{R})
is an element of
SU_{L}(N_{f})
(
SU_{R}(N_{f})
), and
\alpha
is the rotation angle of
U_{A}(1)
. The left and right quarks transform under the same change as:

q_{L}\rightarrow e^{-i\alpha/2}V_{L}q_{L},\quad q_{R}\rightarrow e^{i\alpha/2}V_{R}q_{R}

(4)

If chiral symmetry is dynamically broken, the thermal average of
\Phi
is nonzero.

The decomposition of
\Phi
is represented as:

\Phi=\sum_{a=0}^{N_{f}^{2}-1}\Phi^{a}\frac{\lambda^{a}}{\sqrt{2}}

(5)

Where
\Phi^{a}=S^{a}+iP^{a}
. In this expression,
t^{a}=\frac{\lambda^{a}}{2}
(for
a=1,2,3,\ldots,N_{f}^{2}-1
), and
t^{0}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{1}{2N_{f}}}
is the basic representation generator of
U(N_{f})
. It is noted that
S^{a}(P^{a})
are Hermitian fields, and they have
N_{f}^{2}
even (odd) parity components. For
N_{f}^{2}=2
,
P^{1},2,3
correspond to the
\pi
meson fields
\pi^{1},2,3
.

2
Landau Functional of QCD

Inspired by discussions on universality, we employ the order parameter field
\Phi
to construct the Landau functional
S_{\text{eff}}=\int d^{d}x\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}
possesses the same symmetries as the QCD Lagrangian. In the vicinity of the critical point, we can expand
\Phi
, and its form is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi)+\frac{a}{2}\text{tr}(\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi)+\frac{b_{1}}{4!}(\text{tr}(\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi))^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle+\frac{b_{2}}{4!}(\text{tr}(\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi))^{2}-\frac{c}{2}(\det\Phi+\det\Phi^{\dagger})-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}(h(\Phi+\Phi^{\dagger}))\

Here,
tr
and
\det
operate on flavor indices. The first four terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (6) exhibit
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})\times U_{A}(1)
symmetry. The fifth term involves a determinant structure, acting as an operator that preserves
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})
symmetry but breaks
U_{A}(1)
symmetry; this term ensures the axial anomaly in QCD. The final term in Eq. (6) arises from quark masses, where
h\propto\text{diag}(m_{u},m_{d},m_{s},\dots)
, and it explicitly breaks
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})
and
U_{A}(1)
symmetries.

If the system exhibits a second-order phase transition, the
\Phi
field near the critical point will manifest soft modes with divergent correlation lengths. Modes with finite correlation lengths will be integrated out in the path integral, affecting the coefficients
a
,
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
,
c
, and
h
. The existence of soft modes, such as vector mesons, with Lorentz vector character is a complex issue.

3
Anomalous Massless QCD with Axial Anomaly

When quarks are considered to be massless, the study of anomalous massless QCD with axial anomaly involves investigating the impact of axial anomalies in QCD. This may lead to interesting phenomena related to the breaking of chiral symmetry, a crucial aspect of understanding the behavior of quarks in the QCD vacuum.

3.1
Massless QCD without Axial Anomaly

To systematically study the phase structure of
L_{\text{eff}}
, let’s first consider the case where
c=0
and
h=0
in Eq. (6). In this scenario,
L_{\text{eff}}
possesses the symmetry of
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})\times U_{A}(1)
.

For large values of
\Phi
, if
(b_{1}+b_{2})/(N_{f})>0
,
L_{\text{eff}}
has no lower bound. Under such conditions, a change in the sign of
a
in the mean-field theory leads to a second-order phase transition. Setting
c=h=0
in Eq. (6) and rewriting it with
S^{0}(=\Phi^{0})
, we can demonstrate the above conclusion by comparing it with Eq. (7).

\mathcal{L}_{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}a\sigma^{2}+\frac{1}{4}b\sigma^{2}-h\sigma^{2}.

(7)

However, considering thermal fluctuations of
\Phi
, multiple phase transitions may occur. To illustrate this, we use coupling constants
g_{1},g_{2}=b_{1},b_{2}\,S_{d}/(2\pi)^{2}
to express the next-to-leading-order
\beta
-functions:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta_{1}&=-\epsilon g_{1}+\frac{(N_{f}^{2}+4)}{3}g_{1}^{2}+\frac{4N_{f}}{3}g_{1}g_{2}+g_{2}^{2}\end{aligned}

(8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \beta_{2}&=-\epsilon g_{2}+2g_{1}g_{2}+\frac{2N_{f}}{3}g_{2}^{2}\end{aligned}

(9)

These are the results of a one-loop effective action, and the calculation is performed at the phase transition point where
a=0
, with the four-point vertices proportional to
b_{1},b_{2}
. For different numbers of flavors, Eq. (8) and (9) exhibit different renormalization group flows.

For
N_{f}=1
: In this case, Eq. (8) (with
c=h=0
) is equivalent to discussing the critical exponents of
O(N)
symmetric
\Phi^{4}
model with a single even coupling constant
b\equiv b_{1}+b_{2}
and
O(2)
symmetry. It is easy to see that the fixed point
g^{*}=3\epsilon/5
is infrared stable. This indicates that the phase transition is second-order, with critical exponents given for
N=2
in Table 1.

•

For
N_{f}\geq 2
: In this scenario,
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
are independent coupling constants, and Eq. (6) (with
c=h=0
) has
SU_{L}(N_{f})\times SU_{R}(N_{f})\times U_{A}(1)
symmetry. There are two solutions with
\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=0
:
g^{*}=(0,0)
and
g^{*}=(3\epsilon)/(N_{f}^{2}+4)
.

When there are multiple dimensionless coupling constants
b=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{n})
, we need to consider the renormalization group flow on the multi-dimensional critical hypersurface defined by
a=0
. This flow satisfies

\frac{d\bar{\mathbf{b}}(s)}{ds}=\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{b}(s),\epsilon)

(10)

Assuming we find a fixed point solution satisfying
\boldsymbol{\beta}=0
, let’s study its stability. Linearize
\beta
near the fixed point:

\frac{d\bar{\mathbf{b}}(s)}{ds}\sim\boldsymbol{\Omega}\cdot\bar{\mathbf{b}}(s),\quad\Omega_{ll^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{\partial\beta_{l}}{\partial\bar{b}_{l^{\prime}}}\right)\Big|_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}

(11)

Here,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
is an
n\times n
stability matrix, which may not be symmetric. Consider a special case with
n
independent eigenvectors
\mathbf{B}_{l}
and corresponding eigenvalues
\omega_{l}
. It can be diagonalized as
\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\text{diag}(\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{n})
, leading to

\mathbf{B}_{l}=(P\cdot\bar{\mathbf{b}}(s))_{l}=e^{s\omega_{l}}

(12)

Therefore, in the infrared limit as
s\to-\infty
, the fixed point
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}
is stable (unstable) along the direction where
\text{Re}(\omega_{l})>0
(
\text{Re}(\omega_{l})<0
). The eigenvalues
\omega_{l}
of the stability matrix
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{ll^{\prime}}=\left(\frac{\partial\beta_{l}}{\partial g_{l^{\prime}}}\right)
determine whether the fixed point is infrared stable. Through simple algebraic calculations, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\begin{cases}(-\epsilon,-\epsilon)&\text{for }\bar{g}^{*}=(0,0)\\
(\epsilon,-\frac{N_{f}^{2}-2}{N_{f}^{2}+4}\epsilon)&\text{for }\bar{g}^{*}=\left(\frac{3}{N_{f}^{2}+4}\epsilon,0\right)\end{cases}\end{aligned}

(13)

For the case of
N_{f}=2
, there always exists a negative eigenvalue, indicating no infrared-stable fixed point on the critical hypersurface. This implies that the phase transition is a first-order transition induced by fluctuations, as discussed in the stability matrix. Among them, the renormalization group flow on the two-dimensional critical surface is shown in Figure 1. Regardless of the starting point of the flow, the coupling constants will flow into unbounded regions (
b_{2}<0
or
b_{1}+b_{2}/N_{f}<0
).

Effects of Light Quark Mass

So far, our discussion has been based on the assumption of zero quark mass. To bring us closer to the real world, in this paper, we consider the case where the
u
,
d
, and

c\neq 0
and
h\neq 0
.

(m_{ud},m_{s})

m_{ud}\equiv m_{u}=m_{d}

(m_{ud},m_{s})=\begin{cases}(\infty,\infty),&\text{for }N_{f}=0\\
(\infty,0),&\text{for }N_{f}=1\\
(0,\infty),&\text{for }N_{f}=2\\
(0,0),&\text{for }N_{f}=3\end{cases}

5
Effects of Finite Chemical Potential

The phrase ”QCD at finite chemical potential” refers to QCD, the theory describing the strong force between quarks and gluons, under conditions where there is a non-zero baryon chemical potential. In the context of high-energy nuclear physics, this often occurs in studies of the properties of quark-gluon plasma and the phase diagram of nuclear matter.In QCD, the chemical potential is associated with the number of quarks in the system. At finite chemical potential, one investigates the behavior of QCD matter under the influence of varying baryon densities. This is particularly relevant for understanding the phases of nuclear matter at high temperatures and/or densities.

Introducing the quark chemical potential
\mu
enriches the QCD phase diagram. We consider the phase diagram in the three-dimensional
(T,\mu,m_{ud})
space, with a relatively large strange quark mass
m_{s}
. When
m_{ud}
is small, the system’s Landau functional has a similar form to the model with
O(4)
symmetry in Eq.(20).

L_{\text{eff}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial\boldsymbol{\phi})^{2}+\frac{a(\mu,T)}{2}\boldsymbol{\phi}^{2}+\frac{b(\mu,T)}{4!}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{2})^{2}+\frac{c}{6!}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{2})^{3}-h\phi_{0}

(22)

Here,
h\propto m_{ud}
, and
c
is assumed to be a positive value. Due to two parameters
\mu
and
T
controlling
a
and
b
, a tricritical point (TCP) may appear at the origin where
a=b=h=0
. In fact, the position of this tricritical point was first calculated in the NJL
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
model, and subsequent studies extended to more general models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, technological advancements have propelled our understanding of the chiral phase transition critical point in effective QCD theories. As we stand at the intersection of theoretical innovation and computational capabilities, this paper discusses potential future directions, emphasizing the role of emerging technologies in further unraveling the mysteries of strong dynamics.
