Probability-Based Estimation
Jobst Heitzig

Abstract
We develop a theory of estimation when in addition to a sample of n n observed outcomes the underlying probabilities of the observed outcomes are known, as is typically the case in the context of numerical simulation modeling, e.g. in epidemiology. For this enriched information framework, we design unbiased and consistent “probability-based” estimators whose variance vanish exponentially fast as n → ∞ n\to\infty , as compared to the power-law decline of classical estimators’ variance.

Problem statement

There is a discrete probability space with finite outcome set

\Omega
and probability weight function
p:\Omega\to[0,1],\omega\mapsto p(\omega)
,
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}p(\omega)=1
.
There is also an event
A\subseteq\Omega
the probability of which,

\pi=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)
, we want to estimate.
We don’t know
p
but we do know
\Omega
and
A
, in particular we know the number
m=|A|
of outcomes in
A
.

We have access to a sampler which draws iid samples
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
from
(\Omega,p)
and which in addition (!) gives us the corresponding probabilities
x_{1}=p(\omega_{1}),\dots,x_{n}=p(\omega_{n})
.

How to “best” make use of this additional information? E.g., what consistent (and maybe also unbiased) estimator of
\pi
has the smallest standard error given this information?

Use case: costly simulations

In an important class of use cases in which this occurs, each
\omega
is a possible trajectory of some stochastic dynamical system that we can simulate, and the simulator allows us to compute
p(\omega)
iteratively by multiplying up the probabilities of the changes performed in individual time steps.
A
encodes some macroscopic event that we are interested in, such as: the system tips, an epidemic gets detected, the system converges back to a certain attractor, etc.

Application: Epidemic spreading on a network

Assume a network (graph)
G=(V,E)
and an SI infection process where initially all nodes are susceptible, at discrete time
t\geqslant 0

v\in V
has a basic probability of getting infected of
p_{1}(v,t)
, and independently for each edge
e=\{v,v^{\prime}\}\in E
with infected
v
,
v^{\prime}
has a transmission probability of getting infected of
p_{2}(v,v^{\prime},t)
(e.g.,
[
1
]
.
Finally, there is a sequence
((s_{j},\tau_{j}))_{j}
with sentinel nodes
s_{j}\in V
and testing time points
\tau_{j}\leqslant T
.
The event
A
is the fact that an outbreak has been detected by one of the latter tests, i.e., for at least one

is infected at time

If the network is complex, there is no simple analytical solution for

As the simulator can easily track the probability

\pi

Benchmark: relative frequency

As is well-known, without knowledge of the probabilities
x_{i}
, the most straightforward estimator
\hat{\pi}_{0}
of
\pi
is the relative frequency

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{0}

\displaystyle=|\{i:\omega_{i}\in A\}|/n.

(8)

That estimator is unbiased, consistent, and has variance

\displaystyle v_{0}

\displaystyle=\pi(1-\pi)/n,

(9)

which can be estimated by the plug-in estimator

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{0}

\displaystyle=\hat{\pi}_{0}(1-\hat{\pi}_{0})/n.

(10)

Since the estimator is unbiased, its standard error is simply
\sqrt{v_{0}}=\sqrt{\pi(1-\pi)/n}
, a very well-known fact.

Any estimator using also the additional information given by the
x_{i}
must be compared against this benchmark.

An obvious improvement is to use

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{0,\max{}}

\displaystyle=\max(\hat{\pi}_{0},\sum_{\omega\in O\cap A}p(\omega)),

(11)

where
O=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\}
is the set of observed outcomes. This clearly has a smaller standard error (if only negligibly smaller), but it is
not unbiased
and surely not optimal in any sense yet.

3

Idea 1: use a weighted sum of the observed probabilities

Let

\displaystyle q(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-p(\omega),

(12)

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\},

(13)

the latter being the set of observed outcomes (counting each distinct outcome only once!), and note that we know
p(\omega)
for each
\omega\in O
(it equals one of the
x_{i}
).
Then the following is a consistent and unbiased estimator of
\pi
:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{1}=\sum_{\omega\in O\cap A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}.

(14)

It is consistent because for
n\to\infty
,
O\to\Omega
almost surely, and
[1-p(\omega)]^{n}\to 0
for all
\omega
with
p(\omega)>0
.
It is unbiased because

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}\hat{\pi}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}\mdmathbb{E}\mathbbm{1}_{O}(\omega)

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}(1-q(\omega)^{n})

(16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)=\pi,

(17)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{O}
is the indicator function of
O

and
1-q(\omega)^{n}
is the probability that
\omega\in O
.
What is its standard error?
We have

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}\hat{\pi}_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}\frac{p(\omega^{\prime})}{1-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}}\mdmathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{\omega\in O}\mathbbm{1}_{\omega^{\prime}\in O})

(18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A,~\omega\neq\omega^{\prime}}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}\frac{p(\omega^{\prime})}{1-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\times(1-q(\omega)^{n}-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p(\omega)-p(\omega^{\prime})]^{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{p(\omega)^{2}}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})^{2}}(1-q(\omega)^{n})

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}\frac{p(\omega^{\prime})}{1-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}}\times

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\times(1-q(\omega)^{n}-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p(\omega)-p(\omega^{\prime})]^{n})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{p(\omega)^{2}}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})^{2}}(q(\omega)^{n}-[1-2p(\omega)]^{n}).

(20)

(The final bracket in the second line equals
1-P(\omega\notin O)-P(\omega^{\prime}\notin O)+P(\omega,\omega^{\prime}\notin O)
).
The exact variance of
\hat{\pi}_{1}
is then

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}\hat{\pi}_{1}^{2}-\pi^{2}

(21)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A}p(\omega)p(\omega^{\prime})\times

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\times\left[\frac{1-q(\omega)^{n}-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p(\omega)-p(\omega^{\prime})]^{n}}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})(1-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n})}-1\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{2}\frac{q(\omega)^{n}-[1-2p(\omega)]^{n}}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})^{2}},

(22)

Toy example.

In our toy example from the introduction, a numerical estimation of
v_{0}
and
v_{1}
shows that for
L=10
,
T=20
, already at
n=10
we have
v_{1}<v_{0}
, improving fast as
n
grows.

Asymptotic variance.

For large
n
, we have

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A}p(\omega)p(\omega^{\prime})[1-p(\omega)-p(\omega^{\prime})]^{n}+\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{2}q(\omega)^{n}

(23)

\displaystyle\leqslant m^{2}\bar{p}^{2}(1-2\underline{p})^{n}+m\bar{p}^{2}(1-\underline{p})^{n}\sim m\bar{p}^{2}(1-\underline{p})^{n},

(24)

where
\underline{p}=\min_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)
and
\bar{p}=\max_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)
.
This bound declines exponentially fast with
n
rather than just as an
O(1/n)
like for the relative frequency!

In other words, asymptotically for
n\to\infty
,
\hat{\pi}_{1}
will vastly outperform
\hat{\pi}^{0}
, but we don’t know when that asymptotics kicks in.
For large
\Omega
, it seems likely that a very large
n
will be needed for
\hat{\pi}_{1}
to outperform
\hat{\pi}^{0}
.

Dependence of variance on distribution.

If the probability mass within
A
is distributed equally among
m
different
\omega
, then

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}\left[\frac{1-2[1-\pi/m]^{n}+[1-2\pi/m]^{n}}{(1-[1-\pi/m]^{n})^{2}}-1\right]+

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\pi^{2}\frac{[1-\pi/m]^{n}-[1-2\pi/m]^{n}}{m(1-[1-\pi/m]^{n})^{2}}

(25)

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}\frac{(m-1)[1-2\pi/m]^{n}-m[1-\pi/m]^{2n}+[1-\pi/m]^{n}}{m(1-[1-\pi/m]^{n})^{2}}.

(26)

Variance estimation.

The quantity

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{2}q(\omega)^{n}

(27)

occurring in the above approximation can be estimated without bias by

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in A\cap O}\frac{p(\omega)^{2}q(\omega)^{n}}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}.

(28)

Similarly,
v_{1}
can be estimated without bias by

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A\cap O}p(\omega)p(\omega^{\prime})\left[\frac{1}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})(1-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n})}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\left.~-\frac{1}{1-q(\omega)^{n}-q(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p(\omega)-p(\omega^{\prime})]^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A\cap O}p(\omega)^{2}\frac{q(\omega)^{n}-[1-2p(\omega)]^{n}}{(1-q(\omega)^{n})^{3}}.

(29)

Dual and combined estimators.

While
\hat{\pi}_{1}
estimates
\pi
based on
O\cap A
, one can of course also estimate
1-\pi
based on
O-A
in the same fashion. This gives another unbiased estimator of
\pi
:

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}^{\prime}_{1}=1-\sum_{\omega\in O-A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q(\omega)^{n}}.

(30)

Now it seems that a suitable (convex) combination of
\hat{\pi}_{0}
,
\hat{\pi}_{1}
and
\hat{\pi}^{\prime}_{1}
should still be unbiased and have even smaller variance.
But which combination is optimal?
If the three estimators were independent, the following convex combination would have minimal variance:

(\hat{\pi}_{0}/v_{0}+\hat{\pi}_{1}/v_{1}+\hat{\pi}^{\prime}_{1}/v^{\prime}_{1})/(1/v_{0}+1/v^{\prime}_{1}+1/v_{1})
.
Since we don’t know
v_{1},v^{\prime}_{1}
, we can only use their estimates, leading to the estimator

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{1}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{\pi}_{0}/\hat{v}_{0}+\hat{\pi}_{1}/\hat{v}_{1}+\hat{\pi}^{\prime}_{1}/\hat{v}^{\prime}_{1}}{1/\hat{v}_{0}+1/\hat{v}^{\prime}_{1}+1/\hat{v}_{1}}

(31)

(where
\hat{v}^{\prime}_{1}
is like
\hat{v}_{1}
with
O-A
in place of
A\cap O
).

\xi

The variance is

\displaystyle v^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle=\mdmathbb{E}\hat{\mu}_{1}^{2}-\mu^{2}

\displaystyle=\xi^{2}+2\xi(\mu-\xi)-\mu^{2}

Idea 2: estimate the mean outcome probability

We note that
\pi=m\xi
where
\xi=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)/m
is the average probability of the outcomes in
A
.
Also,
\xi
can be interpreted as the expected value of
p(\omega_{i})
when an
\omega\in A
is drawn uniformly (!) at random (rather than with relative probabilities
p(\omega)
).
Each
x_{i}
of an
i
with
\omega_{i}\in A
can be seen as an estimate of
\xi
.
W.l.o.g. let us order the sample so that
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k}\in A
and
\omega_{k+1},\dots,\omega_{n}\notin A
.
Then also each weighted average
\sum_{i=1}^{k}w_{i}x_{i}
of the
k
values
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, with
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, is an estimate of
\xi
.

To make such an estimate unbiased, we need to choose the averaging weights
w_{i}
taking account of the fact that the
\omega_{i}
were
not
sampled uniformly from
A
but using the distribution given by
p
.
The correct averaging weight
w_{i}
for
x_{i}
must thus be proportional to the ratio between the uniform probability
1/m
and the actually used probability
p(\omega_{i})/\pi
. In other words, we need

w_{i}\propto(1/m)/(p(\omega_{i})/\pi)\propto 1/x_{i}
.
This results in the estimators

\displaystyle\hat{\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}}}=\frac{k}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}}},

(38)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{2}

\displaystyle=m\hat{\xi}=\frac{mk}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{x_{i}}}.

(39)

In other words, rather than using the arithmetic mean of the
x_{i}
to estimate
\xi
, we use the harmonic mean.

Indeed, the expected value of
\hat{\xi}
is

\displaystyle\mdmathbb{E}\hat{\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega_{1}\dots\omega_{n}}\left(\prod_{i}p(\omega_{i})\right)\frac{\sum_{i}1_{A}(\omega_{i})}{\sum_{i}\frac{1_{A}(\omega_{i})}{p(\omega_{i})}}

(40)

Variance.

Because
\hat{\xi}
is the harmonic mean of the
x_{i}
, which are an iid sample from the distribution given by
p^{\prime}(\omega)=p(\omega)/\pi
on
A
, it is unbiased and its variance
u
is

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta^{4}\sigma^{2}}{k}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{m^{4}k}\left[\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(\omega)}-m^{2}\right],

(41)

where

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle=1/E_{p^{\prime}}\left[\frac{1}{X}\right]=1/\sum_{\omega\in A}p^{\prime}(\omega)\frac{1}{X(\omega)}=1/\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{1}{\pi}=\frac{\pi}{m},

(42)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=E_{p^{\prime}}\left[\frac{1}{X}-\frac{1}{\theta}\right]^{2}=\sum_{\omega\in A}p^{\prime}(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{p(\omega)}-\frac{m}{\pi}\right]^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)\left[\frac{1}{p(\omega)^{2}}-\frac{2m}{\pi p(\omega)}+\frac{m^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{1}{p(\omega)}-\frac{m^{2}}{\pi^{2}}.

(45)

From the sampled
x_{i}
, this variance can be estimated using standard methods, e.g., using the jackknife (leave-one-out) method:

\displaystyle\hat{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{k-1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\hat{\xi}-\frac{k-1}{\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{x_{j}}}\right)^{2}=\frac{k-1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{k-\frac{\hat{\xi}}{x_{i}}}\right)^{2}\hat{\xi}^{2}.

(46)

For large
k
, this is approximately

\displaystyle\hat{u}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{k-1}{k^{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{\hat{\xi}}{x_{i}}\right)^{2}\hat{\xi}^{2}.

(47)

The variance of
\hat{\pi}_{2}
is then

\displaystyle v_{2}

\displaystyle=m^{2}u=\frac{\pi^{2}}{m^{2}k}\left[\sum_{\omega\in A}\frac{1}{p^{\prime}(\omega)}-m^{2}\right]

(48)

which can be estimated as

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=m^{2}\hat{u}=\frac{k-1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{k-1}{k-\frac{\hat{\xi}}{x_{i}}}\right)^{2}\hat{\pi}_{2}^{2}.

(49)

Generalization to importance sampling

Assume now that the
\omega_{i}
are not from the “distribution of interest”
p

p^{\prime}
, that both
x_{i}=p(\omega_{i})
and
y_{i}=p^{\prime}(\omega_{i})
are known, and that still we want to estimate
\pi=\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)

Put
z_{i}=x_{i}/y_{i}

The relative frequency estimator of
\pi
is then replaced by the standard estimator from importance sampling
[
2
]
,

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}},

(50)

for which we do not need to know the
x_{i}
or the
y_{i}
but only the
z_{i}
.

Put
q^{\prime}(\omega)=1-p^{\prime}(\omega)
.
Our novel estimators
\hat{\pi}_{1}
and
\hat{\pi}_{2}
should then be defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in O\cap A}\frac{p(\omega)}{1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}},

(51)

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{2}

\displaystyle=m\hat{\xi},\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\xi}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{y_{i}}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{y_{i}}}=\frac{Z}{W},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}z_{i},

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{y_{i}},

(52)

and their variance can be calculated or estimated as

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A}p(\omega)p(\omega^{\prime})\times

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\times\left[\frac{1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}-q^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p^{\prime}(\omega)-p^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})]^{n}}{(1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n})(1-q^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{n})}-1\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{2}\frac{q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}-[1-2p^{\prime}(\omega)]^{n}}{(1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n})^{2}},

(53)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in A\cap O}p(\omega)p(\omega^{\prime})\left[\frac{1}{(1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n})(1-q^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{n})}\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\left.-\frac{1}{1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}-q^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{n}+[1-p^{\prime}(\omega)-p^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})]^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\sum_{\omega\in A\cap O}p(\omega)^{2}\frac{q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}-[1-2p^{\prime}(\omega)]^{n}}{(1-q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n})^{3}},

(54)

\displaystyle\hat{v}_{2}

\displaystyle=m^{2}\frac{k-1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\hat{\xi}-\frac{\sum_{j\neq i}z_{j}}{\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{y_{j}}}\right)^{2}

(55)

\displaystyle=m^{2}\frac{k-1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{Z}{W}-\frac{Z-z_{i}}{W-\frac{1}{y_{i}}}\right)^{2}.

(56)

As in the standard theory of importance sampling, one can now ask how the sampling distribution
p^{\prime}
should be chosen to minimize
v_{1}
or
v_{2}
, assuming that one has some influence on the choice of
p^{\prime}
.

For large
n
, we have roughly

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{2}q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n}.

(57)

Let’s see whether we can find the optimal
p^{\prime}
simply via first-order conditions.
Shifting an infinitesimal sampling probability mass
dp^{\prime}
from
p^{\prime}(\omega)
to
p^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})
changes this by

\displaystyle dv_{1}

\displaystyle\approx np(\omega)^{2}q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n-1}-np(\omega^{\prime})^{2}q^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{n-1}.

(58)

Setting this to zero for all
\omega\in A
would imply that
p(\omega)^{2}q^{\prime}(\omega)^{n-1}
is constant, hence

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-Cp(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)}

(59)

for some constant
C
, hence

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\omega\in A}p^{\prime}(\omega)=|A|-C\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)},

(60)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=(|A|-1)/\sum_{\omega\in A}p(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)},

(61)

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{|A|-1}{|A|}\frac{p(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)}}{\langle p(\omega^{\prime})^{-2/(n-1)}\rangle_{\omega^{\prime}\in A}},

(62)

which might be smaller than 0. So the optimal
p^{\prime}
will likely be a boundary solution with some
p^{\prime}(\omega)=0
in general rather than an interior solution given by the above equation.
Ansatz:
p^{\prime}(\omega)=0
whenever
p(\omega)<\alpha
for some
\alpha
, and

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-Cp(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)}

(63)

whenever
p(\omega)\geqslant\alpha
, hence

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|-1}{|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|}\frac{p(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)}}{\langle p(\omega^{\prime})^{-2/(n-1)}\rangle_{\omega^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}(\alpha)}},

(64)

where
A^{\prime}(\alpha)=\{\omega\in A:p(\omega)\geqslant\alpha\}
and
\alpha
is the smallest value for which all
p^{\prime}(\omega)
thus computed are non-negative. This is probably the smallest
\alpha
for which

\displaystyle\frac{|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|-1}{|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|}\frac{\alpha^{-2/(n-1)}}{\langle p(\omega^{\prime})^{-2/(n-1)}\rangle_{\omega^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}(\alpha)}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1.

(65)

Because always
\alpha^{-2/(n-1)}/\langle p(\omega^{\prime})^{-2/(n-1)}\rangle_{\omega^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}(\alpha)}>1
, the factor
(|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|-1)/|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|
needs to compensate for this to get the product
\leqslant 1
, hence the resulting set
A^{\prime}(\alpha)
is likely small, i.e., only a few
\omega
with the largest
p(\omega)
get a positive
p^{\prime}(\omega)
. Since for these largest
p(\omega)
, the values
p(\omega)^{-2/(n-1)}
are all close to 1, the resulting
p^{\prime}(\omega)
are all approx.
1/|A^{\prime}(\alpha)|
. In other words, selecting a suitable number of
\omega\in A
with the largest
p(\omega)
and then sampling uniformly from them is close to optimal.

6

Application: hypothesis testing in epidemic control

Assume now that we want to test the hypothesis
H_{0}
that an epidemic outbreak of type SI has occurred in a population into which the respective disease is introduced from the outside with a known probability
p_{1}
per time and individual and can be transmitted with a known probability
p_{2}
whenever two individuals meet, and that we know the contact network and have performed a number of tests for infection at certain nodes and timepoints, all of which turned out negative.

We can then simulate
n
potential outbreaks and corresponding sets of tests, giving trajectories
\omega_{i}
and corresponding probabilities
x_{i}
, and observe which simulations resulted in all tests being negative,
\omega_{i}\in A
, and which resulted in at least one test being positive
\omega_{i}\in\Omega\setminus A
.

Using the above designed methods, one can then estimate the probability
\pi
of all tests being negative under the hypothesis
H_{0}
of an outbreak having occurred. If this probability is below the set level of the test, say
0.01
, one would then reject the hypothesis and conclude that no outbreak has occurred.
