Spin-lattice relaxation with non-linear couplings: Comparison between Fermi’s golden rule and extended dissipaton equation of motion
Rui-Hao Bi, Yu Su, Yao Wang, Lei Sun, Wenjie Dou

Abstract
Fermi’s golden rule (FGR) offers an empirical framework for understanding the dynamics of spin-lattice relaxation in magnetic molecules, encompassing mechanisms like direct (one-phonon) and Raman (two-phonon) processes. These principles effectively model experimental longitudinal relaxation rates, denoted as T 1 − 1 T_{1}^{-1} . However, under scenarios of increased coupling strength and nonlinear spin-lattice interactions, FGR’s applicability may diminish. This paper numerically evaluates the exact spin-lattice relaxation rate kernels, employing the extended dissipaton equation of motion (DEOM) formalism. Our calculations reveal that when quadratic spin-lattice coupling is considered, the rate kernels exhibit a free induction decay-like feature, and the damping rates depend on the interaction strength. We observe that the temperature dependence predicted by FGR significantly deviates from the exact results since FGR ignores the higher order effects and the non-Markovian nature of spin-lattice relaxation. Our methods can be easily extended to study other systems with nonlinear spin-lattice interactions and provide valuable insights into the temperature dependence of T 1 T_{1} in molecular qubits when the coupling is strong.

Theory

II.1
Spin-boson model for spin-lattice relaxation

First, we consider a simplified model describing a spin-
\frac{1}{2}
system interacting with a vibrational environment. The total Hamiltonian reads
[
40
,
26
,
44
,
45
]
(We set
\hbar
as unit.)

\displaystyle H_{\text{T}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{z}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\omega_{k}(\hat{p}_{k}^{2}+\hat{q}_{k}^{2})+H_{1}

\displaystyle=H_{\text{S}}+H_{\text{B}}+H_{1}

(1)

with
\Delta
being the Zeeman energy induced by the static external field and
\{\sigma_{i}\}_{i=x,y,z}
the Pauli matrices. Here, the second term
H_{\text{B}}
represents the phonon environment of the spin, where
\omega_{k}
is the vibrational frequency of the
k
-th bath oscillator. The spin relaxation dynamics will be determined by how
these vibration modes couple to the spin system. Here, we consider the spin-lattice interaction Hamiltonian that can be represented in this specific form as

\displaystyle H_{1}=\hat{Q}_{\text{S}}(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}\hat{x}_{\text{B}}+\alpha_{2}\hat{x}_{\text{B}}^{2}+\cdots),

(2)

where
\hat{Q}_{\text{S}}
is some spin–subspace operator and
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}
denotes a collective lattice mode consisted of linear combination of the bath oscillators, given by
\sum_{k}c_{k}\hat{q}_{k}
. Here, it is assumed the interaction between the spin and the collective lattice mode can be written as a Taylor expansion, and
\{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\}
denote the polynomial coefficients. In a concrete simulation, the collective mode coefficients and the polynomial coefficients need to be determined. Ref.
44
demonstrated that these parameters could be obtained from
ab initio
calculations.

To proceed, we assume the collective mode consists of a continuum of oscillators, as in the cases of condensed phase, which can be characterized by a continuous spectral density function,

J(\omega)=\frac{\pi}{2}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k}),\quad\text{for $\omega>0$,}

(3)

and
J(-\omega)=-J(\omega)
. The spectral density relates to the bare–bath response function
\chi_{\rm B}(t)\equiv i\langle[\hat{x}_{\rm B}(t),\hat{x}_{\rm B}(0)]\rangle_{\rm B}
via

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{1}{2{\rm i}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!{\rm d}t\,e^{\textup{i}\omega t}\chi_{\rm B}(t).

(4)

Here,
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}(t)=e^{\textup{i}H_{\text{B}}t}\hat{x}_{\text{B}}e^{-\textup{i}H_{\text{B}}t}
and
\expectationvalue{(\cdot)}_{\text{B}}\equiv\frac{\tr_{\text{B}}[(\cdot)e^{-\beta H_{\text{B}}}]}{\tr_{\text{B}}[e^{-\beta H_{\text{B}}}]}
denotes the thermal averages over the bath with
\beta=1/(k_{\text{B}}T)
. We then have the fluctuation–dissipation theorem,
[
46
]

\expectationvalue{\hat{x}_\text{B}(t)\hat{x}_\text{B}(0)}_{\text{B}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}J(\omega)}{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}.

(5)

In this work, we use the two-level system (TLS) Hamiltonian in Eq.
1
to study a simple yet general relaxation process—an out-of-equilibrium spin one-half particle relaxes to equilibrium through the coupling to lattice vibrations. This thermal relaxation process depends on the system operator
\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}
, collective mode
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}
, and the bath polynomial. When
\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}\propto\sigma_{z}
, the TLS becomes the so-called pure decoherence model
[
47
]
, representing the decoherence contribution from the coupling to lattice vibrations; while
\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}\propto\sigma_{x}\text{ or }\sigma_{y}
denotes the spin relaxation contribution. Without loss of generality, we will restrict to the case of
\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}\propto\sigma_{x}
to describe spin-lattice relaxation.

II.2
Fermi’s golden rule rates

In the following, we calculate the thermally averaged FGR rates for the TLS. The FGR rates for the transition from spin state
i
to
f
(
i,f=0,1
) are given by
[
48
]

k_{f\leftarrow i}(\beta)=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{a}P_{a}(\beta)\sum_{b}\absolutevalue{W_{f,b \gets i,a}}^{2}\delta(E_{f,b}-E_{i,a}).

(6)

Here
i,a
and
f,b
denote coupled spin-vibrational states
\ket{i, a}
and
\ket{f, b}
;
P_{a}(\beta)=\exp(-\beta\omega_a)/Z
denotes the Boltzmann weight for the
a
-th vibrational state where
Z=\sum_{a}\exp(-\beta\omega_a)
is the bare–bath partition function; and
W_{f,b\leftarrow i,a}=\matrixelement{f, b}{H_1}{i, a}
denotes the transition matrix element. For the FGR calculation, we only consider
\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}=\sigma_{x}
, i.e., the relaxation contribution from the spin-lattice interaction. Thus, only two terms are non-vanishing:
W_{f,1\leftarrow i,0}=\matrixelement{1, b}{z(\hat{x}_\text{B})}{0, a}
and
W_{f,0\leftarrow i,1}=\matrixelement{0, b}{z(\hat{x}_\text{B})}{1, a}
.

The discrete Eq.
6
for
k_{f\leftarrow i}(T)
can be recast in terms of correlation function (see Eq.
28
):

k_{f\leftarrow i}(\beta)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{t}e^{\pm\textup{i}\epsilon t}\expectationvalue{z(\hat{x}_\text{B}(t))z(\hat{x}_\text{B}(0))}_{\text{B}},

(7)

where we use the upper sign (
+
in this equation) in the exponential denotes the
1\leftarrow 0
process, and the lower sign (
-
) denotes the
0\leftarrow 1
process, respectively. The upper and lower sign convention will be consistently applied henceforth. Here,
\epsilon
denotes the energy difference between states 0 and 1. In other words, the FGR rates depend on the time correlation functions of polynomial
z(\hat{x}_{\text{B}})
.
In moderate coupling strength, we are safe to truncate the polynomial up to the second order that
z(x)=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}x+\alpha_{2}x^{2}
. Additionally, we can also absorb the zero-th order interaction
\alpha_{0}
into the quantum system
H_{\text{s}}
. When
\alpha_{0}=0
, the spin states
\ket{0}
and
\ket{1}
are eigen states, hence
\epsilon=\Delta
. Otherwise we have to calculate
\epsilon
from diagonalize
H_{s}+\alpha_{0}\sigma_{x}
. With these considerations, the calculations of
T^{-1}
using FGR becomes straightforward, which are outlined in Appendix
A
. It is noteworthy that two terms,

\alpha_{1}^{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{x}_\text{B}(t) \hat{x}_\text{B}(0)}_{\text{B}},\text{ and }\alpha_{2}^{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{x}_\text{B}^2(t) \hat{x}_\text{B}^2(0)}_{\text{B}},

prove to be finite, while the cross linear-quadratic terms in the correlation function vanish. After some algebra, we obtain closed expressions for the FGR relaxation rates given by

\displaystyle k_{f\leftarrow i}=\alpha_{1}^{2}k_{f\leftarrow i}^{\text{(1)}}+\alpha_{2}^{2}k_{f\leftarrow i}^{\text{(2)}},

(8a)

\displaystyle k_{f\leftarrow i}^{\text{(1)}}=2\frac{J(\pm\epsilon)}{1-e^{\mp\beta\epsilon}},

(8b)

\displaystyle k_{f\leftarrow i}^{\text{(2)}}=\frac{4}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{\omega}\frac{J(\omega)}{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}\frac{J(\omega\pm\epsilon)}{e^{\beta(\omega\pm\epsilon)}-1},

(8c)

with superscript
(1)
and
(2)
denoting
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}
and
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}^{2}
coupling term
[
44
]
, respectively.

Notably, an oscillating term appears from the quadratic contribution to the rates

{\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\differential{\omega}}{\pi}J(\omega)\coth{\frac{\beta\omega}{2}}\right]^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential{t}e^{\pm\textup{i}\epsilon t},}

(9)

which is not incorporated to the rate equations (Eqs.
8
). This is because the second oscillating integral in this term does not converge to a finite value. Intriguingly, we do observe oscillating behaviors of the rate kernels in the extended DEOM simulations, as discussed in Sec.
III
.

II.3
The extended DEOM for both linear and quadratic environments

In the standard HEOM formalism, only the linear coupling to the bath mode
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}
is considered.
[
42
,
38
]
However, to accurately model the spin-lattice interaction dynamics requires the explicit considerations of non-linear coupling at least to the second order (
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}^{2}
), since
the relaxations with two phonon processes are very common in experiments. Fortunately, the extended dissipaton equation of motion (DEOM) formalism, by R.-X. Xu and colleagues, extends the HEOM methods for non-linear interactions up to the quadratic order.
[
43
,
42
,
49
]
This study employs the extended DEOM formalism to investigate the spin-lattice relaxation. Subsequently, we provide a concise introduction to this method.

Similar to the HEOM formalism, the extended DEOM requires to expand the bath correlation function in finite exponential series,

\expectationvalue{\hat{x}_\text{B}(t)\hat{x}_\text{B}(0)}_{\text{B}}=\sum_{k}^{K}\eta_{k}e^{-\gamma_{k}t},

(10)

where
K
sets of parameters
\eta_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
are generally obtained from fitting or spectral function decomposition such as the Padé decomposition.
[
50
,
51
]
In contrast to the auxiliary density matrix interpretation of HEOM, DEOM employs a quasi-particle interpretation of such decomposition that leads to the following algebra

\displaystyle\hat{x}_{\text{B}}=\sum_{k}^{K}\hat{f}_{k},

(11)

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\hat{f}_k(t) \hat{f}_{k'}(0)}_{\text{B}}=\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\eta_{k}e^{-\gamma_{k}t}.

(12)

Here,
\{\hat{f}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are the so-called dissipaton operators, where
the dissipatons are independent quasi-particles that represent the collective bath.

With the dissipaton operators, we can represent the total system dynamics as a set of equations of motion for the dissipaton density operators (DDOs). To begin, we know the composite system density operator
\rho_{\text{T}}
satisfies the von Neumann equation

\dot{\rho}_{\text{T}}(t)=-\textup{i}\commutator{H_\text{T}}{\rho_\text{T}(t)}.

(13)

The DDOs can be defined as

\rho_{\bm{n}}^{(n)}(t)=\rho_{n_{1}\dots n_{K}}^{(n)}(t)=\tr_{\text{B}}[(\hat{f}_{K}^{n_{K}}\dots\hat{f}_{1}^{n_{1}})^{\circ}\rho_{\text{T}}(t)].

(14)

Here, index
\bm{n}\equiv\{n_{1},\dots,n_{K}\}
denotes the configuration, and
n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{K}
denotes the total number of disspaton exitations.
The notation
(\cdots)^{\circ}
denotes the irreducible representation. For bosonic dissipaton, as is in the case of lattice vibration, we have
(\hat{f}_{k}\hat{f}_{k^{\prime}})^{\circ}=(\hat{f}_{k^{\prime}}\hat{f}_{k})^{\circ}
.
With these, we can know the reduced system density operator are given by
\rho_{\text{S}}\equiv\rho_{\bm{0}}^{(0)}
.
Refs.
[
43
,
42
]
show that the reduced system dynamics given by Eq.
2
is obtained by applying the dissipaton algebra, including the generalized generalized Wick’s theorems (GWT-I and GWT-II). The equations of motion of
\{\rho_{\bm{n}}^{(n)}\}
read

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\bm{n}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=-(\textup{i}\mathcal{L}_{\text{s}}+\sum_{k}n_{k}\gamma_{k})\rho_{\bm{n}}^{(n)}-\textup{i}(\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{2}\expectationvalue{\hat{x}_\text{B}^2}_{\text{B}})\mathcal{A}\rho_{\bm{n}}^{(n)}

(15)

\displaystyle-\textup{i}\alpha_{1}\sum_{k}(\mathcal{A}\rho_{\bm{n}_{k}^{+}}^{(n+1)}+n_{k}\mathcal{C}_{k}\rho_{\bm{n}_{k}^{-}}^{(n-1)})-2\textup{i}\alpha_{2}\sum_{kk^{\prime}}n_{k}\mathcal{C}_{k}\rho_{\bm{n}_{kk^{\prime}}^{+-}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle-\textup{i}\alpha_{2}\sum_{kk^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{A}\rho_{\bm{n}_{kk^{\prime}}^{++}}^{(n-2)}+n_{k}(n_{k^{\prime}}-\delta_{kk^{\prime}})\mathcal{B}_{kk^{\prime}}\rho_{\bm{n}_{kk^{\prime}}^{--}}^{(n-2)}\right],

with some of the convenient operators used above defined as the following,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{s}}\hat{O}\equiv\commutator{H_\text{s}}{\hat{O}},

(16a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\hat{O}\equiv\commutator{\hat{Q}_\text{s}}{\hat{O}},

(16b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{kk^{\prime}}\hat{O}\equiv\eta_{k}\eta_{k^{\prime}}\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}\hat{O}-\eta_{\bar{k}}^{*}\eta_{\bar{k}^{\prime}}^{*}\hat{O}\hat{Q}_{\text{s}},

(16c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}\hat{O}\equiv\eta_{k}\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}\hat{O}-\eta_{\bar{k}}^{*}\hat{O}\hat{Q}_{\text{s}}.

(16d)

For later use, we introduce a compact notation,

\dot{\bm{\rho}}(t)=-\textup{i}\bm{\mathcal{L}}(t)\bm{\rho}(t).

(17)

Here,
\bm{\rho}(t)=\{\rho_{\bm{n}}^{(n)};n=0,1,2,\dots\}
denotes the set of DDOs. The equations of motion encoded in Eq.
15
are represented by the DEOM-space dynamics generator
\bm{\mathcal{L}}(t)
.

II.4
Rate kernel from the extended DEOM formalism

As introduced in Sec.
II.3
, DDO equations of motions (Eq.
15
) provide a numerically exact recipe for the reduced system dynamics. We can go one step further to extract the rate kernels for the spin-lattice relaxation by applications of the projection operator techniques.
[
52
]

Following ref.
[
52
]
, we use the Nakajima-Zwanzig projection operator technique onto the DDOs.
[
53
,
54
]

For the reduced system density
\rho^{(0)}
, it’s intuitive to define
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}\equiv\mathrm{1}-\mathcal{P}
as the following

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{P}\rho^{(0)}=P_{0}(t)\outerproduct{0}{0}+P_{1}(t)\outerproduct{1}{1},\\
\mathcal{Q}\rho^{(0)}=\rho^{(0)}_{01}(t)\outerproduct{0}{1}+\rho^{(0)}_{10}(t)\outerproduct{1}{0},\end{gathered}

(18)

where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
represents the population and coherence, respectively. The projection definitions can then be generalized to the full DDO-space by

\begin{gathered}\bm{\mathcal{P}}\bm{\rho}(t)=\{\mathcal{P}\rho^{0}(t);0,0,\dots\}\equiv\bm{p}(t),\\
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\bm{\rho}(t)=\{\mathcal{Q}\rho^{0}(t);\rho^{(n>0)}_{\bm{n}}(t)\}\equiv\bm{\sigma}(t),\end{gathered}

(19)

such that Eq.
17
can be recasted into:

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\bm{p}}\\
\dot{\bm{\sigma}}\end{pmatrix}=-\textup{i}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathcal{PLP}}&\bm{\mathcal{PLQ}}\\
\bm{\mathcal{QLP}}&\bm{\mathcal{QLQ}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}\\
\bm{\sigma}\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

Formally, one can integrate the equation for the
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
-space density
\bm{\sigma}
and obtain solution:

\bm{\sigma}(t)=e^{-\textup{i}\bm{\mathcal{QL}}t}\bm{\sigma}(0)-\textup{i}\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}e^{-\textup{i}\bm{\mathcal{QL}}(t-\tau)}\bm{\mathcal{QL}}\bm{p}(\tau).

(21)

By inserting this formal solution of
\bm{\sigma}(t)
to the equations of motion for
\bm{\mathcal{P}}
-space density
\bm{p}
, one obtains:

\begin{gathered}\dot{\bm{p}}(t)=-\textup{i}\bm{\mathcal{PL}}e^{-\textup{i}\bm{\mathcal{QL}}t}\bm{\sigma}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t-\tau)\bm{p}(\tau),\\
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t)=-\bm{\mathcal{PL}}e^{-i\bm{\mathcal{QL}}t}\bm{\mathcal{QLP}}.\end{gathered}

(22)

In practice, the standard factorization
\bm{\rho}(0)=\rho_{\text{S}}(0)\otimes\rho_{\text{B}}^{\text{eq}}
of the initial condition is used, meaning
\rho^{(n>0)}_{\bm{n}}(t)=0
.
As a result, projected system density
\bm{p}(t)
satisfies intergo-differential equation

\dot{\bm{p}}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t-\tau)\bm{p}(\tau).

(23)

The rate kernel
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t)
encodes the full non-Markovian dynamics of the reduced system, and we can readily extract the
T_{1}^{-1}
rate from
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(t)
. In particular, the reduced population of spin state
a
(
a=0,1
) satisfies the following kinetic equation:

\displaystyle\dot{P}_{a}(t)=-

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\mathcal{K}_{b\leftarrow a}(t-\tau)P_{a}(\tau)+

(24)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\differential{\tau}\mathcal{K}_{a\leftarrow b}(t-\tau)P_{b}(\tau).

In the Markovian limit, the above kinetic equation becomes simpler:
\dot{P}_{1}(t)=k_{0\leftarrow 1}P_{1}(t)+k_{1\leftarrow 0}P_{0}(t)
, where

k_{a\leftarrow b}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential{\tau}\mathcal{K}_{a\leftarrow b}(\tau),\quad a,b\in\{0,1\}.

(25)

Here,
k_{0\leftarrow 1}
and
k_{1\leftarrow 0}
encode the long time behaviour of the rate kernel components
\mathcal{K}_{0\leftarrow 1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1\leftarrow 0}
.
In the context of spin-lattice relaxation,

T_{1}^{-1}=k_{0\leftarrow 1}+k_{1\leftarrow 0}.

(26)

Rate kernels of linear and quadratic coupling

Notably, the behaviors of the rate kernel as a function of time are drastically different for

9

Overall, we demonstrate a stronger coupling and a mixed linear quadratic interaction can enhance the relaxation rate of the FID-like rate kernel. As we soon demonstrate in Section

IV
Conclusion

In this work, we apply the extend DEOM method to examine the validity of FGR in modeling the dynamics of spin-lattice interaction. The numerical exact DEOM approach reveals the free induction decay (FID) feature of quadratic coupling dynamics. The decay rate of the kernel can be rather slow [FIG.
1
(b)], indicating strong non-Markovian nature for the two-phonon processes encoded in
\hat{x}_{\text{B}}^{2}
-coupling. We demonstrate this damping rate depends on 1) the coupling strength, and 2) the interactions between the one and two phonon processes. Indeed, damping of the rate kernel is completely neglected in perturbation treatments as they are encoded in higer order interactions. Consequently, methods such as FGR and Markovian master equations fail to correctly predict the relaxation dynamics and the temperatures dependencies when the coupling is strong.

Looking forward, these findings as well as the methods presented in this work should be very useful in understanding qubits relaxation dynamics in the field of quantum information. One particular applications of our methodology is the study of spin-lattice relaxation in the NV center,
[
58
]
is a three level system that can encode more complicated and interesting dynamics.
Furthermore, systems with large spin-orbit couplings exhibit strong spin-lattice interaction,
[
59
,
60
,
61
]
it will be interesting to see whether strong couplings play an important role for these materials. In addition, the method here can be coupling with data driven methods such as DMD,
[
62
]
provide the possibility of studying spin-relaxation dynamics for systems of large dimension.
