Estimation of prediction error with known covariate shift
Hui Xu , Robert Tibshirani

Abstract
In supervised learning, the estimation of prediction error on unlabeled test data is an important task. Existing methods are usually built on the assumption that the training and test data are sampled from the same distribution, which is often violated in practice. As a result, traditional estimators like cross-validation (CV) will be biased and this may result in poor model selection. In this paper, we assume that we have a test dataset in which the feature values are available but not the outcome labels, and focus on a particular form of distributional shift called “covariate shift”. We propose an alternative method based on parametric bootstrap of the target of conditional error Err 𝐗 \textup{Err}_{\bf X} [ BHT21 ] . Empirically our method outperforms CV for both simulation and real data example across different modeling tasks.



1
Introduction

In predictive modeling, it is essential to estimate the generalization error on future test datasets. Given a particular model, such generalization error is implicitly dependent on the distribution from which the test data is drawn. Existing methods such as cross-validation (CV) usually rely on stationary assumptions between training and test data, which are often violated in practice due to time shift, location change, sampling bias, batch effects, etc. We consider estimation of generalization error when the covariate shift between training and test data is observed, and seek to improve upon existing methods by leveraging the additional covariate information of test data.

More specifically, we focus on the scenario where covariates are observed for both training and test data, and the conditional distribution of outcome given covariates is the same for training and testing. This is known as “covariate shift”
[
SKM07
,
GSH+09
,
TBCR19
]
. In the case of time series data, the term “virtual concept drift”
[
GŽB+14
,
LLD+18
]
is also used interchangeably. Our method is based on a slightly modified version of the target of inference
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}

[
BHT21
]
, which is the average prediction error of models fit on other unseen training datasets, and is shown to be an approximate estimand for CV when the stationary assumption between training and testing datasets is satisfied. We propose two ways to estimate the target of inference
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}
using either direct estimation or decomposition formula, resulting in two alternative estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
.

Our method can be applied to a wide range of practical settings where covariate information of test data is readily available. For example, in genomics or proteomics settings, labeled data might be very expensive to obtain while unlabeled data is plentiful. Before committing to the cost of labeling more data, our method can be used to learn about the ability of the current model to generalize to unlabeled data. We will mainly discuss linear regression and classification, but our method can be applied to any predictive model.

1.2
Related work

There are two main categories for error prediction in a general framework without observing test data covariates, namely resampling techniques including cross-validation (
[
Efr83
]
) and related bootstrap-based techniques (
[
ET94
]
,
[
ET97
]
), as well as covariance penalty methods such as Mallow’s
C_{p}
(
[
Mal73
]
), AIC (
[
Aka74
]
), BIC (
[
Sch78
]
), and Stein’s unbiased risk estimate (
[
Ste81
]
) etc. In
[
Efr04
]
, the connection between the two theories was studied, and it was found that covariance penalties are a Rao-Blackwellized version of cross-validation. Cross-validation and related bootstrap techniques are nonparametric, while covariance penalties are model based, relying on the additive homoscedastic modeling assumption.

The properties of CV are subtle and its estimand is elusive to precise definition. But it is generally agreed that just like covariance penalty methods, CV can also be considered as an estimate of expected prediction error, where the expectation is taken over both training data on future test points. There is plenty of work (
[
Zha95
]
,
[
HTF09
]
,
[
You20
]
,
[
RT20
]
,
[
Wag20
]
,
[
BHT21
]
) that discusses the estimand of CV.

In particular, instead of the instance-specific error for a particular training set, the estimand
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}
was recently proposed by
[
BHT21
]
as a better estimand for cross-validation error.
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}
is the average prediction error of models fit on other
unseen training outcomes drawn from the original superpopulation. The difference between CV and Mallow’s
C_{p}
is that while Mallow’s
C_{p}
targets in-sample error in the sense that future test points have the same covariates as training data, there is no such restriction for cross-validation.

Widely used methods for error prediction mentioned above are tailored to the classical setting where there is no distribution shift. And covariate information of test data is usually not used for error prediction.
When the covariate values are available for the target test data, a direct approach to estimate prediction on target is to use importance weighting (IW), which struggles when the supports of training and test data have little overlap especially in high dimensional settings (
[
PBWL20
]
,
[
SGCZ19
]
). The authors in
[
CGS+21
]
proposed to mitigate the problem by guiding IW with prior information about directions in which distribution changes. When restricted to linear models,
[
SM05
]
derived an exact formula for prediction error on unlabeled test data, which can be estimated using parameter estimates from an importance weighted regression problem. Another method proposed recently
[
YYW+22
]
to predict out-of-distribution error shows that there is high empirical correlation between projection norm and true test error. This method makes no assumptions on whether the conditional distribution shifts, but leverages on the feasibility of manually creating pseudo shifted datasets for calibration, which restricts its application to image or text classification.

1.3
Organization of the paper

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we clarify notations and describe our proposed method. In Section 3, we elaborate on our method in a few statistical models with corresponding simulation results. In Section 4, we demonstrate our method further with real-data example in crime rate prediction. In Section 5, we include some discussions of the advantages and limitations of our proposed method.

2
Description of method

2.1
Setting and notation

We consider the supervised learning setting, where we have a training data set
{\bf X}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}),{\bf Y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
of
n
observations drawn i.i.d from some joint distribution
P
. That is,
(x_{i},y_{i})_{i\in[n]}\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
\delta_{\bf X}
the empirical distribution of covariates in
{\bf X}
,
P_{X}
as the marginal distribution of covariates, and
P_{Y|X}
as the conditional distribution of
Y
given
X
. Let
\hat{f}(x,\theta)
be a function that predicts outcome
y
from covariates
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
using a parametric model with parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\hat{\theta}
be a function that maps values from
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})^{n}
to parameter estimates in
\Theta
. Suppose that there is a new dataset consisting of i.i.d draws from
Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, where
Q
could be a different joint distribution but the conditional distribution of outcome given covariates remains the same. We are interested in predicting the test error, without access to the ground truth outcomes, as measured by a loss function,

\displaystyle\ell:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle(\hat{y},y)\mapsto\ell(\hat{y},y)

such that
\ell(y,y)=0
for all
y
. (For example,
\ell
could be square error loss, misclassification error, or deviance.)

The most intuitive target of inference for test error is the

out-of-sample error
,

\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}^{Q}:=\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},y_{0})\sim Q}\left[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))\mid{\bf X},{\bf Y}\right],

(1)

which is the expected loss when applying a model trained with
{\bf X},{\bf Y}
on a new data point
(x_{0},y_{0})\sim Q
.
Suppose that we are given a particular unlabeled test set with
n_{test}
samples and test covariates
{\bf X}_{test}
. Similarly, let
\delta_{{\bf X}_{test}}
be the empirical distribution of covariates in
{\bf X}_{test}
, and
Q_{Y|X}
be the conditional distribution of outcome given covariates in the test population. Since we make the assumption that the conditional distribution remains unchanged and only deals with observed covariate shift, we have that
Q_{Y|X}=P_{Y|X}
. Notice that for an abuse of notation, we write
y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}
to mean the sampling of a random vector,
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\sim P_{Y|X=x_{1}}\times\ldots\times P_{Y|X=x_{n}}
.

2.2
Target

We propose to estimate out-of-sample error in equation (
1
) by studying a similar averaged target first introduced in
[
BHT21
]
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}:=\mathbb{E}[\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}^{Q}|{\bf X}]&=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},y_{0})\sim Q}[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))\mid{\bf X}].\end{split}

(2)

The motivation for using this target of inference is that when
P=Q
, the averaged target of
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
is closer to the true estimand of linearly invariant estimators (including cross-validation, data splitting, and Mallow’s
C_{p}
) than the instance-specific
\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}^{Q}
. Since
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
works well for test error prediction without distributional shift, we hope to generalize it to situations with observed covariate shifts. For more details, we refer interested readers to Theorem 1, Corollary 1 and 2 of
[
BHT21
]
.

Notice that since
Q
is usually unknown, it is difficult to estimate
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
directly. But given test data covariates and the assumption of no conditional distribution shift, we can study a slightly modified version of the estimand,

\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf X}_{test}}^{Q}:=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}\sim P_{Y|X=x_{0}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{{\bf X}_{test}}}[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))\mid{\bf X}].

(3)

The only difference between (
2
) and (
3
) is that we replace the sampling from an unknown joint distribution
(x_{0},y_{0})\sim Q
with its empirical counterpart.

2.2.1
Decomposition and connection to Mallow’s
C_{p}

Since our new target
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
is a function of features in the training set, it has connections with
in-sample error
, which is the target of estimation for traditional covariance-penalty based methods. Recall that
in-sample error

\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})
is the error for a fresh sample with the same covariates as training data.

\displaystyle\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{y,y^{\prime}\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\left(y^{\prime}_{i},\hat{f}(x_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))\right)\mid{\bf X}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}\sim P_{Y|X=x_{0}}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{{\bf X}}}[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))\mid{\bf X}].

(4)

Notice that we can combine equations (
3
) and (
4
) to obtain the following decomposition similarly as in
[
BHT21
]
.

\begin{split}\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf X}_{test}}^{Q}=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}\sim P_{Y|X=x_{0}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{{\bf X}_{test}}}[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))]\right.\\
\left.-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{\bf X}}[\ell(y_{0},\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y))]\right].\end{split}

(5)

The value of this decomposition in (
5
) is that it offers an alternative way to estimate our target of
\textup{Err}_{{\bf X},{\bf X}_{test}}^{Q}
. For estimation of in-sample error
\textup{Err}_{\rm in}
, we can use standard Mallows
C_{p}
for linear models and bootstrap estimation for covariance penalty otherwise. For the remaining of the paper, we will denote estimators for the target in (
3
) as
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
(introduced later in Algorithm
1
) and that for the decomposition in (
5
) as
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
(introduced later in Algorithm
2
).

2.3
General methods of estimation

This section considers estimation of targets introduced in the previous section under general supervised learning settings before elaborating in specific model applications later. We will illustrate separate procedures for direct estimation of target (
3
) and decomposition target (
5
) respectively.

Our methods of estimation are based on the idea of parametric bootstrap. Let
P_{Y|X}^{\theta}
be a parametric model and
\hat{\theta}
be a parameter estimate. Then drawing parametric bootstrap samples
y\sim P_{Y|X}^{\hat{\theta}}
means generating new outcomes for given covariate information based on the model parameterized by
\hat{\theta}
. For example, if
P^{\theta}_{Y|X}
is a linear model parameterized by
\theta
with Gaussian noise of mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, then drawing parametric bootstrap sample
y\sim p^{\hat{\theta}}_{Y|X=x}
means generating
y=x^{T}\hat{\theta}+\epsilon
for
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and parameter estimate
\hat{\theta}
. Again with abuse of notation, we write
Y\sim P^{\hat{\theta}}_{Y|X={\bf X}}
to mean generating a random vector of independent parametric bootstrap samples
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\sim P^{\hat{\theta}}_{Y|X=x_{1}}\times\ldots\times P^{\hat{\theta}}_{Y|X=x_{n}}
.

For direct estimation of the target in (
3
), we illustrate the steps in Algorithm
1
. After obtaining the initial parameter estimate from training data, we draw parametric bootstrap samples of new outcomes for both training and test covariates. For each bootstrap sample, we obtain an instance of the target by computing the loss between new test outcome and predicted outcome on test covariates using new training outcomes. The final estimate
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
can be obtained as an average of bootstrap errors.

Similarly, for estimation of the decomposition target in (
5
), we illustrate the steps in Algorithm
2
. Given the same conditions and a suitable estimate for
\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})
, algorithm
2
estimates the difference term in (
5
) again by taking an average of bootstrap error differences. In addition to the steps in direct estimation, the only extra step is to compute the loss between new training outcomes and predicted outcomes for each bootstrap sample as bootstrap estimates of in-sample error.

3
Applications

In this section, We illustrate the application of the estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
to specific settings, including linear models (OLS and Lasso) and logistic regression for classification. We will discuss how the general methodology can guide us to estimate test error under various modeling assumptions and fitting algorithms with corresponding simulation results.

3.1
Linear model with Gaussian error

First consider the setting of linear model with homoscedastic Gaussian errors,

y_{i}=x_{i}^{T}\theta+\epsilon_{i},\text{ where }\epsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}).

Given a particular choice of loss function such as square error loss, the general method above can be directly applied. But there are subtleties involved depending on the initial fitting algorithm. For OLS there is a closed form solution for test error and our proposed estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
are also unbiased. On the other hand, for model fitting with regularization such as the Lasso, while our estimators show advantage over traditional estimators such as CV in the presence of covariate shift, we will introduce additional debiasing modification that can help with our prediction.

3.1.2
Estimation for Lasso

There is no explicit formula for the error target if we use fitting algorithms with regularization, such as the Lasso. However, our general method in algorithms
1
and
2
can be used. By
[
RTF16
]
, we know that a good estimate for

in Lasso regression is

\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-\hat{s}_{\hat{\lambda}}}\|{\bf Y}-{\bf X}\hat{\theta}_{\hat{\lambda}}\|_{2}^{2},

where
\hat{\theta}_{\hat{\lambda}}
is the Lasso estimate at regularization parameter
\hat{\lambda}

\hat{s}_{\hat{\lambda}}
is the number of nonzero elements in
\hat{\theta}_{\hat{\lambda}}
. For estimation of
\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})
, we can use covariance penalty identity in equation (

). Notice that in Lasso regression
[
TT12
]
,

df=\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mbox{Cov}\left(y_{i},\hat{f}(x_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))\right)=\hat{s}_{\hat{\lambda}}.

Therefore we can use the following unbiased estimator for in-sample error

\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}({\bf X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\hat{f}(x_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})))^{2}+\frac{2\hat{s}_{\hat{\lambda}}\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{n}.

(9)

An additional subtlety for Lasso fitting algorithm is that in addition to the general method listed in Algorithms
1
and
2
, we need bias correction steps in order to achieve better prediction accuracy. Since the Lasso estimator is biased, the parametric bootstrap step will carry on the bias, requiring corrections to the output estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
. Here we propose two existing methods of bias correction for predicting test error of Lasso as an example, i.e. (1) Multiplicative bootstrap correction and (2) Relaxed Lasso. The details are as follows.

1.

Multiplicative bootstrap bias correction:
Multiply estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
by a constant shrinking factor
c
. Let
\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})
and
\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}^{(b)})
denote the fitted parameters from initial model and bootstrap samples. Then we propose to choose,

c=\frac{\|\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})\|^{2}}{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\|\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}^{(b)})\|^{2}}.

The intuition is that we adjust for the scaling factor between the true parameter
\theta
and learned parameter
\hat{\theta}
by that between
\hat{\theta}
and refitted parameter after bootstrap.

2.

Relaxed Lasso correction:
Use relaxed Lasso fit on the initial training data to form parametric bootstrap samples. The idea is that we want to reduce the bias between true parameter
\theta
and that used in generating parametric bootstrap samples.

5
Discussion

We propose an alternative method to estimate test error with observed covariate shift based on the target of average test errors over different potential training outcomes. Under the assumption of no conditional distribution shift, we provide two ways of estimation
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
, based on parametric bootstrap. Unlike previous methods based on importance weighting, our method is not limited by the amount of overlap or shift between training and test covariates. Empirically, both
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
demonstrate consistent advantage over cross-validation under covariate shift across many modeling assumptions for both simulation data and real data example. While
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
performs slightly better than
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
in simulation settings, the two estimators have similar results in real data examples.

However, the performance of
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
is heavily dependent on obtaining a good parametric model. In the presence of regularization, debiasing corrections are needed for better prediction accuracy. Besides, computation cost may also be a concern, especially for models that are hard to train since refitting in parametric bootstrap is involved. The assumption of no conditional distribution shift is also sometimes difficult to guarantee in practice. In the real data example of crime rate prediction, the estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
do not fully recover the actual test errors, but consistently show improvements over cross-validation despite underlying model misspecification and potential conditional distribution shift. The advantage of
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
over CV grows when the amount of covariate shift is larger.

Proof of Proposition
1

Proposition 1
.
For linear models with the OLS fitting algorithm and squared error loss, assume in addition that the test data covariates are standardized so that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]=0
and
\mbox{Var}_{Q}[X]=\Sigma
of full rank. Then

\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}=\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mbox{tr}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma\right)=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\left(\mbox{tr}\left(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma\right)-p\right),

where
\hat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{i}^{T}
is empirical covariance for training data.

Proof.

The proof is almost identical to that of Proposition 2 in
[
BHT21
]
except that the target
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
depends on the test data distribution
Q
, which may be different from that of training data. Notice that by the decomposition formula,

\displaystyle\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}

\displaystyle=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\mathbb{E}_{y_{0}\sim P_{Y|X=x_{0}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{{\bf X}_{test}}}[(y_{0}-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)))^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{{\bf X}}}[(y_{0}-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)))^{2}]\right]

\displaystyle=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\left[\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)^{T}\Sigma\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)-\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)^{T}\hat{\Sigma}\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\sigma^{2}\mbox{tr}\left[(\Sigma-\hat{\Sigma})({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\left(\mbox{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma)-p\right).

Similarly, we get by direct expression of
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
that

\displaystyle\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{(x_{0},y_{0})\sim Q}[(y_{0}-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)))^{2}]\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{y\sim P_{Y|X={\bf X}}}\left[\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)^{T}\Sigma\left(\theta-\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\Sigma({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mbox{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma).

∎

A.2
Proof of Proposition
2

Proposition 2
.
For linear models with the OLS fitting algorithm and squared error loss, assume in addition that the test data covariates are standardized so that
\mathbb{E}_{Q}[X]=0
and
\mbox{Var}_{Q}[X]=\Sigma
of full rank. Then the estimators
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dir
and
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}.dec
in the algorithms
1
and
2
are unbiased for the target
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
.

Proof.

Since we are given an unbiased estimator of in-sample error
\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}({\bf X})
, it suffices to show that,
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\bf X}}^{(b)}
is an unbiased estimator for
\textup{Err}_{\bf X}^{Q}
, and that
\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}^{(b)}({\bf X})
is an unbiased estimator for
\textup{Err}_{\rm in}({\bf X})
, respectively. In other words, we want to show that for any
\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{n}\sigma^{2})
,

\mathbb{E}_{y^{*}={\bf X}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})+\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q_{X}}\left(x_{0}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y^{*}))\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mbox{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma),

and

\mathbb{E}_{y^{*}={\bf X}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})+\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{\bf X}}\left(x_{0}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y^{*}))\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}p.

We will begin by proving the first equality. Let
M={\bf X}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}
be the hat matrix. Since
x_{0}\sim Q_{X}
,
\mathbb{E}(x_{0})=0
and
\textup{Var}(x_{0})=\Sigma
, we have that,

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}_{y^{*}={\bf X}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})+\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q_{X}}\left(x_{0}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y^{*}))\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y^{*}={\bf X}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})+\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q_{X}}\left[\left(x_{0}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}({\bf Y}-y^{*})\right)^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim Q_{X}}\left[\left(x_{0}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}((I-M){\bf Y}-\eta)\right)^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[\mbox{Var}_{x_{0}\sim Q_{X}}\left[x_{0}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}((I-M){\bf Y}-\eta)\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[((I-M){\bf Y}-\eta)^{T}{\bf X}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\Sigma({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}((I-M){\bf Y}-\eta)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}\left[\mbox{tr}\left[{\bf X}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\Sigma({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}\eta\eta^{T}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\Sigma({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mbox{tr}(\hat{\Sigma}^{-1}\Sigma),

where the third last step is due to

\displaystyle((I-M){\bf Y})^{T}{\bf X}\left({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X}\right)^{-1}\Sigma\left({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X}\right)^{-1}{\bf X}^{T}((I-M){\bf Y})=0.

Similarly, the second equality as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y^{*}={\bf X}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})+\eta}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim\delta_{\bf X}}\left(x_{0}\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})-\hat{f}(x_{0},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},y^{*}))\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\mbox{tr}\left[\hat{\Sigma}({\bf X}^{T}{\bf X})^{-1}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}p.

∎

6:

Compute sample averages
\bar{Y}_{i}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}{\bf Y}^{(b)}_{i}
and
\bar{f}_{i}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{f}(x_{i},\hat{\theta}^{(b)})
.

7:

Compute

\widehat{\mbox{Cov}}_{i}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left({\bf Y}_{i}^{(b)}-\bar{Y}_{i}\right)\left(\hat{f}(x_{i},\hat{\theta}^{(b)})-\bar{f}_{i}\right)

8:

Compute

\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}({\bf X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left({\bf Y}_{i}-\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))\right)^{2}+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mbox{Cov}}_{i}

Output
:
\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}({\bf X})

The covariance penalty based method of estimating in-sample can be generalized to a wider class of loss functions beyond squared error
[
Efr04
]
.
In the case of logistic regression with counting error, error function
q(u)=\mbox{min}(u,1-u)
. We have the following identity,

\mathbb{E}[\textup{Err}_{\rm in}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l({\bf Y}_{i},\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})))\right]+2\mbox{Cov}({\bf Y}_{i},\lambda_{i}),

where
l
is counting error function,
\lambda_{i}=-\frac{\partial{q}}{\partial{u}}(\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y})))/2
. That is,
\lambda_{i}=-1/2
if
\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))=0
and
\lambda_{i}=1/2
otherwise. We shift all
\lambda_{i}
by
1/2
to get
\hat{\lambda}_{i}=\lambda_{i}+1/2=\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))
without changing the covariance penalty term. Therefore, to estimate in-sample error for logistic regression with
\l
being counting error loss, we only need to replace the last step in algorithm
3
with

\widehat{\textup{Err}_{\rm in}}({\bf X})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}l\left({\bf Y}_{i},\hat{f}({\bf X}_{i},\hat{\theta}({\bf X},{\bf Y}))\right)+\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{\mbox{Cov}}_{i}.
