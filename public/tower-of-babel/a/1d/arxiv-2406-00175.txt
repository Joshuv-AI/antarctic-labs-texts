Monodromies of Second Order q q -difference Equations from the WKB Approximation
Fabrizio Del Monte, Pietro Longhi

Abstract
This paper studies the space of monodromy data of second order q q -difference equations through the framework of WKB analysis. We compute the connection matrices associated to the Stokes phenomenon of WKB wavefunctions and develop a general framework to parameterize monodromies of q q -difference equations. Computations of monodromies are illustrated with explicit examples, including a q q -Mathieu equation and its degenerations. In all examples we show that the monodromy around the origin of ℂ ∗ \mathbb{C}^{*} admits an expansion in terms of Voros symbols, or exponentiated quantum periods, with integer coefficients. Physically these monodromies correspond to expectation values of Wilson line operators in five dimensional quantum field theories with minimal supersymmetry. In the case of the q q -Mathieu equation, we show that the trace of the monodromy can be identified with the Hamiltonian of a corresponding q q -Painlevé equation.

Introduction

The study of monodromy data of differential equations is ubiquitous both in Physics and in Mathematics, with wide ranging applications across Quantum Field Theory and String Theory.
Recent developments at the intersection of String Theory, Algebraic Geometry, Integrable Systems and Conformal Field Theory have motivated an interest in the related, but less-studied, subject of
q
-difference equations.

Foundational results on (generalised) character varieties of

Main results

In Proposition
1

q
-difference equation (

\psi(qx)+\psi(q^{-1}x)=2T(x,q)\psi(x),

(1)

Theorem

\lambda_{\pm,n}=\lim_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\log\mathcal{R}(x,\hbar)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}

{\rm Im\,}\left[\hbar^{-1}\,\int^{x}\left(\lambda_{s_{2},n_{2}}-\lambda_{s_{1},n_{1}}\right)\right]=0.

Some open questions.

There are several points that are raised throughout our work which deserve further attention.
One of these is the analysis of equations which feature singularities (punctures) at finite

Another important matter is the parametrization of the connection matrix expressing the transport from

Yet another natural question,

Although our results solely rely on WKB analysis of
q
-difference equations, the motivations for this work and our interest in the subject come from the role played by

For the interested reader, here we comment on implications of our results in relation to these subjects.

Let

(8)

Stokes diagrams, known as exponential networks in physics, arise in this context in the study of D3-branes wrapping special Lagrangian cycles on

[

,

Stokes data encoded by matrices such as (

[

In string theory the curve

-difference operator due to worldsheet instanton corrections weighted by

(with

[

The

and references therein.

Borel sums of Voros symbols, often called in this context

Exact WKB analysis of second order
q
-difference equations

Consider a generic second order
q
-difference equation

\phi(qx)+a_{1}(x,q)\phi(x)+a_{2}(x,q)\phi(q^{-1}x)=0,

(9)

where
a_{i}
are rational functions of
(x,q)\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
and possibly additional complex parameters.

Remark 1
.

Due to linearity, any
q
-periodic function
\theta(qx)=\theta(x)
gives rise to a new solution
\phi^{\prime}(x)=\theta(x)\phi(x)
to (
9
).
It is therefore natural to consider the space of solutions to linear
q
-difference equations modulo
q
-periodic functions.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{q}
denote the field of
q
-periodic functions in
x
.
We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the space of solutions to (
9
) as a vector space over
\mathcal{M}_{q}
.

Our first step will be to transform (
9
) into a more convenient form:

Proposition 1
.

Let
a_{1}(x),\,a_{2}(x)
be rational functions in
x
. Through an explicit change of variables
\phi\rightarrow\psi
, the general second order
q
DE (
9
) can always be transformed in its involutive form

\psi(qx)+\psi(q^{-1}x)=2T(x,q)\psi(x)

(10)

Proof.

The
q
-difference equation (
9
) can be written as a
2\times 2
linear system

\Phi(qx)=A(x)\Phi(x),\qquad\Phi(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\phi_{1}(x)&\phi_{2}(x)\\
\phi_{1}(qx)&\phi_{2}(qx)\end{array}\right)\qquad A(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-a_{2}(qx)&-a_{1}(qx)\end{array}\right)

(11)

where
\phi_{1},\,\phi_{2}
are linearly independent solutions of (
9
) over the field of
q
-periodic functions
\mathcal{M}_{q}
. Set

\Phi(x)=f(x)\Psi(x)

(12)

where
f(x)
is a scalar factor satisfying

f(qx)=\sqrt{\det A(x)}f(x)=\sqrt{a_{2}(qx)}f(x).

(13)

Since
a_{2}
is a rational function, this results in the
q
-difference equation for
f

f(qx)=a_{0}\prod_{i}(x-x_{i})^{k_{i}},\qquad k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},

(14)

which is easily seen to have solution

f(x)=x^{\frac{\log a_{0}}{\log q}}\prod_{i}f_{i}(x),\qquad f_{i}=\begin{cases}e^{\frac{k_{i}}{2}\log x\left(\frac{\log x}{\log q}-1\right)}&x_{i}=0,\\
e^{k_{i}\log x_{i}\frac{\log x}{\log q}}(x_{k}/x;q)_{\infty}^{k_{i}},&x_{i}\neq 0.\end{cases}

(15)

Here
(x;q)_{\infty}:=\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}(1-xq^{k})
is the infinite q-Pochhammer symbol, satisfying

(qx;q)_{\infty}=(1-x)^{-1}(x;q)_{\infty}.

(16)

∎

We will henceforth restrict to equations of this form.

Definition 1
.

We define the involutive form of a second order
q
-difference equation to be

\psi(qx)+\psi(q^{-1}x)=2T(x,q)\psi(x).

(17)

All information retained from (
9
) is encoded in theterm
T(x,q)
, that we will call the potential of the
q
DE, or its trace function.

2.1
The associated linear system

We will sometimes find it useful to write the second order
q
-difference equation (
17
) as a first order
q
-difference linear system

\Psi(qx)=A(x)\Psi(x),

(18)

where
A(x)
is a
2\times 2
matrix,

A(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&2T(qx)\end{array}\right),

(19)

\Psi
is the fundamental matrix of solutions

\Psi(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{1}(x)&\psi_{2}(x)\\
\psi_{1}(qx)&\psi_{2}(qx)\end{array}\right),

(20)

with
\psi_{1},\,\psi_{2}
linearly independent solutions to (
9
) over
\mathcal{M}_{q}
.

A
q
-gauge transformation acts on (
18
) from the left, by replacing
\Psi,A
with

\Psi^{\prime}(x)=g(x)\cdot\Psi(x)\,,\qquad A^{\prime}(x)=g(qx)\cdot A(x)\cdot g(x)^{-1}\,.

(21)

When the gauge transformation is
q
-periodic, i.e.
g(qx)=g(x)
is valued in
GL(2,\mathcal{M}_{q})
, it specializes to a global change of basis over the field of
q
-periodic functions (the analogue of constant gauge transformations in the setting of differential equations).

2.2
WKB ansatz

We will treat (
17
) as a singularly perturbed equation in the parameter
\hbar:=\log q\rightarrow 0
, and it will be
assumed that
T(x,q)
has a regular expansion around
\hbar=0

T(x,\hbar)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}T_{k}(x)\hbar^{k}.

(22)

To solve (
17
) we consider the WKB ansatz

\displaystyle\psi(x,\hbar)=\exp\left(\int_{x_{0}}^{x}S(x,\hbar)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}\right),

\displaystyle S(x,\hbar)=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}S_{k}(\log q)^{k}=\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}S_{k}\hbar^{k}\,,

(23)

where
x_{0}
encodes a choice of normalization.
The leading order term obeys

y+y^{-1}=2T_{0}(x),\qquad y:=e^{S_{-1}(x)}\,.

(24)

This equation defines the
WKB curve

\Sigma
as an algebraic curve in
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
In terms of the coefficients of (
9
), we have

T_{0}(x)=\lim_{q\to 1}-\frac{a_{1}(x,q)}{2\sqrt{a_{2}(x,q)}}.

(25)

Due to the logarithm in the definition of
S_{-1}=\log y
,
WKB solutions are labeled both by a discrete index
s=\pm
for solutions of the algebraic curve in
y

y_{\pm}:=T_{0}\pm\sqrt{T_{0}^{2}-1}

(26)

and by a logarithmic index
n
that defines
S_{-1}
.
In the limit
\hbar\to 0
the WKB solutions have the following asymptotics
[
30
]

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm,n}(x)\mathop{\sim}_{\hbar\rightarrow 0}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^{x}_{x_{0}}\lambda_{\pm,n}\right\}

(27)

where

\lambda_{\pm,n}:=S_{-1}(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}=\left(\log y_{\pm}+2\pi in\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}=\left(\pm\log y_{+}+2\pi in\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}\,.

(28)

Due to the presence of the logarithm function,
\lambda_{\pm,n}
are not differentials on the curve
\Sigma
, but rather on its infinite
\mathbb{Z}
-cover induced by
\log y\rightarrow y
.

As observed in remark
1
, while
\psi_{\pm,n}
provide infinitely many linearly independent solutions over
\mathbb{C}
, only two among these solutions are independent over the field
\mathcal{M}_{q}
of
q
-periodic functions. We will see later (see Section
2.5
) that any two choices of the logarithmic index are related by multiplication by simple
q
-periodic functions, and that
the dependence on the logarithmic index
n
enters only through the semiclassical behaviour in (
27
).

2.3
General structure of the involutive WKB curve

There are three types of points on the WKB curve that play a distinguished role in our analysis.

Square-root branch points.

For WKB curves in involutive form (
24
) these are defined by the condition
y=y^{-1}
. The location of these points is therefore determined by

T_{0}(x)=\pm 1.

(29)

We shall always assume that branch points are isolated, which means that locally
y\sim\pm(1+c_{0}\sqrt{x-x_{0}}+\dots)
and therefore

T_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(y+y^{-1})=\pm\left(1+\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{2}(x-x_{0})+\dots\right)

(30)

At square-root branch points the two sheets
y^{\pm 1}(x)
coincide, and get exchanged when going around a loop around the branch point.
Consequently, upon crossing a square-root branch cut, the two families of solutions are also exchanged:

\psi_{\pm,n}\to\psi_{\mp,n}\,.

(31)

Logarithmic branch points.

Another set of distinguished points are those where one of the sheets
y(x)
runs to infinity (correspondingly, at the same value of
x
the other sheet must go to zero).
These correspond to singularities
x=x_{*}
of the equations (they are points where the determinant of the q-connection
A(x)
vanishes). In this section we will consider
x_{*}\neq 0,\infty
. In terms of
T_{0}(x)
, they are characterized by the degree of divergence
k

T_{0}(x)\sim(x-x_{*})^{k}+\dots\qquad(k<0)

(32)

Near
x=x_{*}
we fix a labeling of sheets such that
y_{+}
is the one that becomes large, behaving as
y_{+}(x)\approx 2T_{0}(x)
while
y_{-}(x)\approx(2T_{0}(x))^{-1}
goes to zero.
If follows that on
\mathbb{C}^{\star}
, the multivalued functions
\log y_{\pm}(x)
undergo additive monodromy around a logarithmic singularity of
T_{0}

\log y_{\pm}(e^{2\pi i}w)=\log y_{\pm}(w)\pm 2\pi i\,k\qquad(w=x-x_{*}).

(33)

Figure 1
:

\mathbb{C}^{*}_{x}
. The singularity lifts to two logarithmic punctures on
\Sigma
with
y_{\pm}
running to infinity and to zero, respectively. The logarithmic cut lives on
\Sigma
and connects the two punctures on different sheets
\pm
(the cut therefore turns around a branch point elsewhere to switch between sheets).

In fact, since the monodromy preserves the sheet of the double cover
\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
, this type of branching occurs on
\Sigma
itself. It is natural to choose logarithmic branch cuts on
\Sigma
that connect the pole of
y_{\pm}
and the zero of
y_{\mp}
at the same value of
x
. Logarithmic cuts are defined on
\Sigma
, and to run from one sheet to another they must circle around one of the square-root ramification points.
Upon crossing a logarithmic branch cut in the counter-clockwise direction (as seen on the
x
-plane) the solutions are exchanged by a shift in the logarithmic index

\psi_{\pm,n}\to\psi_{\pm,n\pm k}\qquad\text{(counter-clockwise crossing)}\,.

(34)

Punctures of
\mathbb{C}^{*}_{x}
.

Finally, since we are working with
x\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
there are punctures at
x=0,\infty
, appearing due to the term
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}
in the differential (
28
). From the expression

\lambda_{\pm,n}=\left(\log y_{\pm}+2\pi in\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x}

(35)

we see that the origin and infinity are punctures of different types depending on the behaviour of
y
:

1.

If
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0}y_{\pm}=0,\,\infty
(respectively
\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}y_{\pm}=0,\,\infty
), then the origin (respectively
\infty
) is a logarithmic puncture, and the same considerations of the previous section apply.

2.

If
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0,\infty}=y_{0,\infty}\notin\{\pm 1,\,0,\,\infty\}
, then we have a regular puncture, signaled by a simple pole with residue

\mathrm{res}_{x=0}\lambda_{\pm,\,n}=\pm\log y_{0}+2\pi in,\quad\mathrm{res}_{x=\infty}\lambda_{\pm,\,n}=\pm\log y_{\infty}-2\pi in.

(36)

3.

If
\lim_{x\rightarrow 0,\,\infty}y_{+}=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0,\,\infty}y_{-}=a\in\{1,\,-1\}
, upon expanding
\log y
we can distinguish two subcases:

(a)

4.4
Quantum periods and monodromy representation of
q
-difference equations

By concatenating matrices of type (
64
), (
77
), (
109
), (
110
), it is possible to explicitly compute monodromies arising from analytic continuation of WKB solutions. Monodromy matrices of
q
-difference equations in general are not constant matrices: they can have nontrivial
x
-dependence, but must be q-periodic. In our present setting, by construction the monodromy matrix will be a Laurent polynomial in the variable
x^{\frac{2\pi i}{\hbar}}
:

M(x)=\sum_{k}x^{\frac{2\pi ik}{\hbar}}M_{k},\qquad M(qx)=M(x).

(120)

For certain applications, for example if one wants to define an
\mathcal{X}
-cluster variety structure on the space of monodromies, along the lines of what happens in differential equations
[
39
,
58
]
, it can become important to extract
x
-independent information out of the monodromy. This is necessary if one wants to introduce an analogue of the infinite-dimensional Riemann-Hilbert Problem of
[
43
,
19
]
for monodromies of the
q
DE.

We do this by working
\mathbb{Z}
-equivariantly with respect to the logarithmic cover
{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\rightarrow\Sigma
, which translates to keeping only the term of order zero in
x^{\frac{2\pi i}{\hbar}}
when taking traces of (
120
). This can be thought of as follows: one can think of the monodromy (
120
) in terms of the infinite-dimensional basis of WKB solutions over
\mathbb{C}
. Then it will act as an operator, that can be represented through the infinte matrix

\mathbb{M}_{m,n}:=\sum_{k}M_{k}\delta_{m,n+k}.

(121)

Taking the term of order zero in
x^{\frac{2\pi i}{\hbar}}
in the trace of (
120
) corresponds then to a regularised trace of the indinite-dimensional matrix (
121
). We will now consider several examples, keeping in mind both these perspectives. The distinction between the two notions of monodromies becomes relevant when studying analytic continuation around logarithmic singularities, see Section
5.4
.

5.1
The
q
-Airy equation

Consider the following

-difference equation

\psi(q^{2}x)-2x\psi(qx)+\psi(x)=0\,,

(122)

A q-series solution to (

\Sigma

The WKB curve is

(124)

The curve has turning points at

The two sheets are

At

The local monodromy around the origin can be easily computed as follows: in

We consider a path
{\mathcal{P}}
shown as a green dashed line, with basepoint denoted by a green dot.
The parallel transport along this path picks up contributions from detours at four Stokes lines, one from each of the branch points. Detour paths are highlighted by thick solid green segments of the network trajectories.
The path does not cross any branch cuts, and features diagonal matrices between detours, which we omit to lighten notation.
We shall work in conventions where a Stokes matrices are 2 by 2, with sheet
i

j

The parallel transport is therefore

F({\mathcal{P}},\vartheta)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&X_{a}\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
X_{b}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\

\star&1+X_{c}X_{d}+X_{b}X_{d}\end{array}\right)

Now observe that the soliton charges have the following types

a:\ (ij,0)\,,\qquad b:\ (ji,0)\,,\qquad c:\ (ji,1)\,,\qquad d:\ (ij,-1)\,.

In taking the trace over the infinite-dimensional vector space of vacua labeled both by polynomial and logarithmic indices, only paths that close up on the logarithmic branch should be retained.
The overall shift of logarithmic index is as follows for the six terms found in the polynomial trace

\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(i)}}}_{0}+\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(i)}ab}}_{0}+\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(i)}ac}}_{1}+\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(j)}}}_{0}+\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(j)}cd}}_{-1}+\underbrace{X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(j)}bd}}_{0}

(221)

Therefore the only terms that survive in the total trace are

X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(i)}}+X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(i)}ab}+X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(j)}}+X_{{\mathcal{P}}^{(j)}cd}
