Discordant edges for the voter model on regular random graphs
Luca Avena, Rangel Baldasso, Rajat Subhra Hazra, Frank den Hollander, Matteo Quattropani

Abstract
. We consider the two-opinion voter model on a regular random graph with n n vertices and degree d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . It is known that consensus is reached on time scale n n and that on this time scale the volume of the set of vertices with one opinion evolves as a Fisher-Wright diffusion. We are interested in the evolution of the number of discordant edges (i.e., edges linking vertices with different opinions), which can be thought as the perimeter of the set of vertices with one opinion. We show that if initially the two opinions are drawn independently from a Bernoulli distribution with parameter u ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) u\in(0,1) , then on time scale 1 1 the fraction of discordant edges decreases and stabilises to a value that depends on d d and u u , and is related to the meeting time of two random walks on an infinity tree of degree d d starting from two neighbouring vertices. Moreover, we show that on time scale n n the fraction of discordant edges moves away from the constant plateau and converges to zero in an exponential fashion. Our proofs exploit the classical dual system of coalescing random walks and use ideas from Cooper et al., 2010 built on the so-called First Visit Time Lemma. We further introduce a novel technique to derive concentration properties from weak-dependence of coalescing random walks on moderate time scales. Key words. Regular random graph, voter model, random walks, discordant edges, concentration. MSC2020: 05C81, 60K35. Acknowledgement. 1.
Model, literature and results

Random processes on random graphs constitute a research area that poses many challenges. In the past decade, considerable progress has been made in understanding how the geometry of the graph affects the evolution of the process. In terms of the choice of graph, the focus has been on the Erdös-Rényi random graph, the configuration model, the preferential attachment model, and the exponential random graph, either directed or undirected, and in regimes ranging from sparse to dense. In terms of the choice of process, the focus has been on percolation, random walk, the stochastic Ising model, the contact process, and the voter model. What makes the area particularly interesting is that there is a delicate interplay between the
size
of the graph and the
time scale
on which the process is observed. For short times, the process behaves as if it lives on an infinite graph. For instance, many sparse graphs are locally tree-like and therefore the process behaves as if it evolves on an infinite Galton-Watson tree. For long times, however, the process sees that the graph is finite and exhibits a crossover in its behaviour. For instance, the voter model, which will be the process of interest in the present paper, eventually reaches consensus on any finite connected graph, but the time at which it does depends on the size of the graph. Many such instances can be captured under the name of
finite-systems scheme
, i.e., the challenge to identify how a finite truncation of a stochastic system behaves as both the time and the truncation level tend to infinity, properly tuned together (
Cox and Greven, 1990
,
Cox and Greven, 1994
).

Random processes on random graphs are part of the larger research area of random processes in
random environment
, where the environment selects the random transition probabilities or transition rates. Another name is that of
interacting particle systems
in random environment. For random process on lattices a more or less complete theory has been developed over the past four decades. The challenge is to extend this patrimony to random graphs. In the present paper we focus on the voter model on the regular random graph in the sparse regime. We track how the fraction of
discordant edges
evolves over time in the limit as the size of the graph tends to infinity, and identify its scaling behaviour on
three time scales
: short, moderate, and long.

Voter models were introduced and studied in
Clifford and Sudbury, 1973
and
Holley and Liggett, 1975
. Voter models and their consensus times on finite graphs were analysed in
Donnelly and Welsh, 1983
and
Cox, 1989
. The behaviour of voter models on random networks depends on the realisation of the network:
Sood et al., 2008
made various predictions for the expected consensus time on heterogeneous random networks (including power-law random graphs). More recently,
Fernley and Ortgiese, 2019
study the asymptotics of the consensus time on inhomogeneous random graph models like the Chung-Lu model and the Norros-Reitu model. The expected consensus time for regular random graphs was studied in
Cooper et al., 2010
, in the discrete-time synchronous setting. As far as we know, there is no literature on the evolution of the number of discordant edges on random graphs.

1.1.
Model and background

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, the voter model is the Markov process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on state space
\{0,1\}^{V}
, with
\eta_{t}=\{\eta_{t}(x)\colon\,x\in V\}
, where
\eta_{t}(x)
represents the opinion at time
t
of vertex
x
. Each vertex is equipped with an independent exponential clock that rings at rate one, after which the vertex selects one of the neighbouring vertices uniformly at random and copies its opinion. (A formal description of this interacting particle system, its generator, and how its dynamics can be built up via the so-called graphical representation is postponed to Section
2
.)

As mentioned previously, while the voter dynamics has been widely studied on periodic lattices, periodic tori, and complete graphs, only recently its evolution on general connected graphs has been considered. In particular, as discussed below, if the underlying (random or non-random) graph has sufficiently nice properties, then it is possible to identify the time scale on which consensus takes place, and to determine how the process behaves on this time scale. In the present paper we specialise to the
d
-regular random graph ensemble with
d\geq 3
. In particular, we consider the sequence of random graphs
(G_{d,n}(\omega))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, with law denoted by
\mathbb{P}
, where each element
G_{d,n}(\omega)=(V,E(\omega))
is a regular random graph of degree
d\geq 3
, consisting of
|V|=n
vertices and
|E(\omega)|=m=dn/2
edges, uniformly sampled from the set of simple
d
-regular graphs with
n
vertices (to guarantee that
dn
is even, for
d
odd we restrict to
n
even). These graphs are locally tree-like and have good expansion properties (see Section
2.3
), lying within the realm of so-called
mean-field geometries
(see Section
1.2
). Therefore the voter model on the
d
-regular random graph ensemble can be investigated in some depth, and refined statements clarifying how consensus is reached can be derived. In the present paper we analyse the evolution of the density of
discordances
. In other words, denoting the set of discordant edges at time
t
by

D^{n}_{t}=\big\{e=(x,y)\in E\colon\,\eta_{t}(x)\neq\eta_{t}(y)\big\},

we will study the fraction of discordant edges at time
t\geq 0
given by

(1.1)

\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t}=\frac{|D^{n}_{t}|}{m}

in the limit as
n\to\infty
.

1.2.
Voter model on mean-field geometries

\bullet

Voter model.
A classical model of relevance in population genetics is the voter model on the
complete graph
, which is referred to as the
Moran model
or the
Fisher-Wright model
(depending on whether continuous-time asynchronous or discrete-time parallel updates are considered). The states
\{0,1\}
represent two different alleles, each coding for a specific genetic trait, and the
n
vertices of the complete graph
K_{n}
represent individuals in a population of size
n
. In the limit as
n\to\infty
, the consensus time (to be interpreted as the extinction time of one of the two traits) is known to scale linearly in
n
. Furthermore, on time scale
n
the fraction of the individuals of, say, type
1
converges as a process to the so-called
Fisher-Wright diffusion
. These results can be derived by analysing the backward genealogical progeny, which amounts to studying
n
coalescing random walks evolving on the same graph, which in turn is related to the so-called
Kingman coalescent
(we refer to
Durrett, 2008
for details). In Section
2
we give a precise description of this
duality
on arbitrary graphs.

These by now classical results have been recently extended to general
mean-field geometries
under two main conditions that can be roughly described as follows: (I) the stationary distribution of the random walk on the random graph must be
not too concentrated
; (II)
fast-mixing
in the sense of an asymptotically vanishing ratio between the mixing time of a single random walk and the expected meeting time of two independent random walks starting from stationarity.

\bullet

Dual process.

For what concerns the dual process of coalescing random walks,
Oliveira, 2013
computes the distributional limit of the coalescence time under the above mentioned assumptions. In particular, it follows from
(
Oliveira, 2013
, Theorem 1.2)
that

(1.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau_{\rm coal}]}{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\pi\otimes\pi}_{\rm meet}]}=2,

where
\tau_{\rm coal}
is the coalescence time of
n
random walks, each starting from a different vertex, and
\tau_{\rm meet}^{\pi\otimes\pi}
is the meeting time of two random walks independently starting from stationarity. This offers insight into how the dual voter model behaves in mean-field like geometries. In particular, (
1.2
) allows us to translate the question of how long it takes for the voter model to achieve consensus to the question of controlling the meeting time of two random walks starting from stationarity. Indeed, as a consequence of this duality, it is immediate that the
n
-coalescence time
\tau_{\rm coal}
stochastically dominates the above mentioned
consensus time
, defined as

(1.3)

\tau_{\rm cons}=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\,\eta_{t}(x)=\eta_{t}(y),\text{ for all }x,y\in V\}.

For what concerns the convergence to a Fisher-Wright diffusion after proper scaling, the recent work by
Chen et al., 2016
considers the
fraction of vertices in state
1
at time
t
in the voter model,

(1.4)

\mathcal{B}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{t}(x),\qquad t\geq 0,

under the above mentioned
mean-field conditions
(see
(
Chen et al., 2016
, Theorem 2.2)
for a precise formulation). The main result states that, when time is speeded up by a factor
\gamma_{n}\coloneqq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau_{\rm meet}^{\pi\otimes\pi}]
, the rescaled density of vertices in state
1
, i.e.,
(\mathcal{B}_{\gamma_{n}t}^{n})_{t\geq 0}
, converges as
n\to\infty
to a Fisher-Wright diffusion in the Skorokhod topology.

\bullet

Regular random graphs as mean-field geometries.

Let us next discuss the implications of the results mentioned above within the specific framework of
d
-regular random graphs. The latter is a geometric setting that satisfies the aforementioned
mean-field conditions
with high probability with respect to the law
\mathbb{P}
of the environment. Indeed, it is well known that a typical realisation of the graph is connected with high probability as soon as
d\geq 3
. Due to the undirectedness of the edges, the corresponding stationary distribution is uniform over the vertex set. Furthermore, with high probability under
\mathbb{P}
, the mixing time is of order
\log n
(see
Lubetzky and Sly, 2010
), while the expected meeting time (see
Chen, 2021
) satisfies

\frac{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau_{\rm meet}^{\pi\otimes\pi}]}{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2\theta_{d}}

with

(1.5)

\theta_{d}=\frac{d-2}{d-1}.

Hence, by applying (
1.2
) to this setting, we find that

\frac{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau_{\rm coal}]}{n}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{\theta_{d}}.

Moreover, for the voter model starting from independent Bernoulli opinions with parameter
u\in(0,1)
, by
Chen et al., 2016
we also have that the fraction of opinions of type
1
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}^{n}_{t}
converges, after time is speeded up by a factor
n
, to the Fisher-Wright diffusion
(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
given by the SDE

(1.6)

\mathrm{d}\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s}=\sqrt{2\theta_{d}\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s}(1-\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s})}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},\qquad\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{0}=u,

where
(W_{s})_{s\geq 0}
denotes the standard Brownian motion (with generator
\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta
).

In conclusion, on
d
-regular ensembles consensus is reached on average on time scale
n
and, as far as the law is concerned, on the time scale of the consensus the process can be well approximated by the Fisher-Wright diffusion in (
1.6
). Interestingly, as pointed out by
Chen et al., 2016
, the diffusion coefficient in (
1.6
) (which is referred to as the
genetic variability
in a population genetics context) can be related to the fraction of discordant edges. This will be the starting point of our investigation, which is devoted to a deeper understanding of the evolution of the voter model beyond the consensus time scale
n
, via a detailed analysis of the discordant edges .

1.3.
Main theorems: evolution of discordances and stabilisation before consensus

Before we present our main results, we introduce some notation. The symbols
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{E}
will be reserved for the probability space of the
d
-regular random graph. The abbreviation
whp
refers to events that occur with probability
\mathbb{P}
tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

Thanks to the duality between the
n
-vertex voter model and a system of
n
-coalescing random walks (to be described in more detail in Section
2
), it is sufficient to look at a collection of
2m
Poisson processes, each associated with an (oriented) edge of the graph, together with the initial assignment of the opinions. In this way, we can use the very same source of randomness to sample the evolution of the voter model, the dual system of coalescing random walks, and a system of
n
independent random walks, each starting at a different vertex of the graph. For this reason we adopt the symbols
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
and
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}
to refer to any of these three stochastic processes evolving on a quenched realisation of the graph. To distinguish between the random walks and the voter model, in the latter case we use a sub-index to refer to the initial distribution of the process. For instance,
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}
denotes the law of the voter model starting from the configuration
\eta_{0}=\xi\in\{0,1\}^{V}
. Similarly, when we write
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}
, we refer to the voter model initialised by a product of Bernoulli random variables of mean
u\in(0,1)
. On the other hand, when considering a system of two or more independent or coalescing random walks, we simply use the symbols
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
and
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}
, and the starting positions of the random walks will be clear from the context (with the exception of Section
5
, where an
ad-hoc
notation will be introduced). Finally, we write
{\rm Ber}(u)
for a Bernoulli distribution of parameter
u
.

We now present our main results. The first theorem identifies
whp
the first order asymptotics of the expected number of discordant edges on all time scales.

Theorem 1.1

(Expected density of discordances at all time scales)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
, and let
\theta_{d}
be as in (
1.5
). Fix
u\in(0,1)
, and consider the voter model on a
d
-regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
with initial distribution
[{\rm Ber}(u)]^{\otimes V}
. Then, for any non-negative time
t_{n}
, the density of discordant edges in (
1.1
) satisfies

(1.7)

\left|{\bf E}_{u}\left[\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t_{n}}\right]-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t_{n}))\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta_{d}\frac{t_{n}}{n}}\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0,

where

(1.8)

f_{d}(t)={\bf P}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm meet}\leq t)=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{(2t)^{\kappa}}{\kappa!}\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\kappa-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{2s}{s}\frac{1}{s+1}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\Big)^{s+1}\Big(\frac{d-1}{d}\Big)^{s},

with
{\bf P}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}
denoting the law of two independent random walks on the infinite
d
-regular tree
\mathcal{T}_{d}
starting from the endpoints of an edge
e=(x,y)
in
\mathcal{T}_{d}
.

Note that the limit is different depending on the time scale we are looking at:

•

(
Short time scale
) For
t_{n}=\Theta(1)
, the first order of the above expectation is given by
2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t_{n}))
, and the behaviour is governed by the non-decreasing function captured in (
1.8
), representing the meeting time of two random walks starting from adjacent vertices on an infinite
d
-regular tree. See Figure
1
.

•

(
Moderate time scale
) For
t_{n}=\omega(1)\cap o(n)
, the discordances stabilise at a limiting plateau
2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(\infty))
characterised by the probability that two adjacent random walks on an infinite tree never meet, i.e.,
1-f_{d}(\infty)=\theta_{d}
.

•

(
Long time scale
) For
t_{n}=sn
,
s\in(0,\infty)
, the dual system coalesces and the voter model reaches consensus. The evolution of the density of opinions is approximated by the Fisher-Wright diffusion in (
1.6
), and the expected density of discordances is characterised by the mean of the genetic variability
{\bf E}_{u}\left[\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s}(1-\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s})\right]=2u(1-u)\theta_{d}\mathrm{e}^{-2\theta_{d}s}
. See Figure
2
.

•

(
Consensus
) For
t_{n}=\omega(n)
, the system has reached consensus and the limiting formula in (
1.7
) degenerates to zero.

The second theorem characterises the process
\mathcal{D}^{n}
in (
1.1
) beyond its expectation. In particular, we show that on time scale
o(n)
the density of discordances concentrates around the expectation, while on time scale
\Theta(n)
it behaves as a deterministic function of the Fisher-Wright diffusion.

Theorem 1.2

(Beyond expectation)
.

Consider the voter model on a
d
-regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
with
d\geq 3
.

(i)

Concentration before coalescence.
Let
t_{n}
be such that
t_{n}/n\to 0
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{\xi\in\{0,1\}^{V}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t_{n}}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}[\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}^{n}]\right|>\varepsilon\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

(ii)

Discordance on consensus time scale.
Let
t_{n}
be such that
t_{n}/n\to s\in(0,\infty)
. Then, for every
u\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\left(\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t_{n}}\leq x\right)-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\left(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{2s\theta_{d}}(1-\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{2s\theta_{d}})\leq x\right)\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0,

where
(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is the solution of (
1.6
).

The third and last theorem is a strengthening of Theorem
1.2
(i)
. More precisely, we show that as soon as we focus on the process for times that are polynomially smaller than the consensus time, the concentration around the mean is actually uniform in time.

Theorem 1.3

(Uniform concentration on moderate time scale)
.

Consider the voter model on a
d
-regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
. Then, for every
u\in(0,1)
and
\delta,\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\Big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq n^{1-\delta}}\big|\mathcal{D}_{t}^{n}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[\mathcal{D}_{t}^{n}]\big|>\varepsilon\Big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

1.4.
Open problems

We point out two open problems.

•

Theorem
1.3
says that the concentration of the fraction of discordant edges is uniform up to times
n^{1-\delta}
, for any
\delta>0
. We expect that Theorem
1.3
can be strengthened to the statement that for every
t_{n}
such that
t_{n}/n\to 0
and every
C_{n}\to\infty
,

(1.9)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\left|\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t_{n}}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\mathcal{D}^{n}_{t_{n}}]\right|>C_{n}\sqrt{t_{n}/n}\,\big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Note that, thanks to Azuma inequality, the concentration in (
1.9
) holds for the quantity
\mathcal{B}_{t}^{n}
in (
1.4
), because this is a martingale. Note further that the concentration in Theorem
1.3
follows from (
1.9
) and a union bound. For now, (
1.9
) is beyond our reach.

•

We expect that Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
can be extended to
non-regular
sparse random graphs. We do not have a conjecture on how the function
f_{d}
and the diffusion constant
\theta_{d}
modify in this more general setting.

1.5.
Description of techniques

All our proofs are based on the classical notion of duality between the voter model and a collection of coalescent random walks. In particular, on time scales
o(\log n)
, i.e., below the typical distance between two vertices, the analysis can be carried out by coupling a system of two random walks, starting at adjacent vertices and evolving on the
d
-regular random graph, with two random walks on the
d
-regular tree. Because the tree is regular, the distance of the two random walks can be viewed as the distance to the origin of a single biased random walk on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Note that the same does not hold when the tree is not regular.

Clearly, in order to analyse the process on time scales
\Theta(\log n)
, i.e., on the typical distance between two vertices, the coupling argument must be combined with a finer control of the two random walks on the regular random graph. To this aim, we exploit the strategy developed in
Cooper et al., 2010
, which uses the so-called
First Visit Time Lemma
(see Theorem
2.2
). Such control, together with a first-moment argument, is enough to compute the evolution of the expected number of discordant on every time scale.

On the other hand, in order to obtain the concentration result in Theorem
1.3
, a much deeper analysis is required. Roughly, in order to have proper control on the correlations between the edges constituting
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{n}
, we must analyse a dual system of random walks whose number grows with
n
. Exploiting a classical result by
Aldous and Brown, 1992
, we derive upper bounds for the number of meetings of a poly-logarithmic number of independent random walks evolving on the random graph for a time
n^{1-o(1)}
. Such a bound will be exploited in the forthcoming Proposition
5.1
to deduce an upper bound for the deviation from the mean that is exponentially small in
n
and uniform in time. This upper bound can in turn be translated to the result in Theorem
1.3
by taking a union bound.

Notation and preparation

In this section we properly introduce the voter model on general graphs, and collect some results that will be needed along the way. In Section
2.1

coalescing random walks

2.2

Cooper and Frieze, 2005

2.1.
The voter model and coalescing random walks

Let
G(V,E)
be a connected (possibly infinite and locally-finite) undirected graph. For each
x\in V
, let
d_{x}
denote the degree of vertex
x
. The voter model on
G
is defined as the interacting particle system with state space
\{0,1\}^{V}
and generator
\mathcal{L}
acting on local functions
f\colon\,V\to\mathbb{R}
as

(\mathcal{L}f)(\eta)=\sum_{x\in V}\sum_{y\sim x}\frac{1}{d_{x}}\big(f(\eta^{x\leftarrow y})-f(\eta)\big),\qquad\eta\in\{0,1\}^{V},

where
\eta^{x\leftarrow y}
is obtained from
\eta
as

\eta^{x\leftarrow y}(z)=\begin{cases}\eta(y),&\quad\text{if }z=x,\\
\eta(z),&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In this system, agents (represented by the vertices of
G
) start with binary opinions and update these at rate one by copying the opinion of a uniformly chosen neighbour. For
t\geq 0
and
x\in V
, denote by
\eta_{t}(x)\in\{0,1\}
the opinion of vertex
x
at time
t
.

When
G
is finite and regular, i.e.,
d_{x}=d
for every
x\in V
, the number of vertices that have opinion
1
,

B_{t}=\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{t}(x),

is a martingale. Since
B_{t}
is bounded by
|V|
, it converges, which implies that the voter model eventually fixates on a constant configuration, composed entirely of
0
’s or
1
’s, denoted by
\bar{0}
and
\bar{1}
, respectively. Define the consensus time of
G
as the stopping time

\tau_{\rm cons}=\inf\big\{t\geq 0\colon\,\eta_{t}\in\{\bar{0},\bar{1}\}\big\}.

Let us next introduce the
graphical construction
of the voter model. Associate to every oriented edge
\vec{e}=(x,y)
a Poisson point process
\mathscr{P}_{\vec{e}}
on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity
1/d_{x}
. When a clock from
\vec{e}=(x,y)
rings, vertex
x
receives the opinion of vertex
y
. This constriction allows us to track the joint evolution of all the opinions via time duality. In order to determine
\eta_{t}(x)
, the state of a vertex
x
at time
t
, we start from the space time point
(x,t)
, and go backwards in time, crossing every clock ring from an edge that reaches the current position of the path (see Figure
3
).

t
-2
-1
0

The process that tracks the origin of the opinions can be viewed as a collection of
coalescing random walks
. To define this process we assume that
G
is finite and consider a family
\{(X^{v}_{t})_{t\geq 0}\}_{v\in V}
of independent rate-one continuous-time random walks such that
X^{v}_{0}=v
almost surely. In what follows we identify
V
with
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. When two walks meet at the same vertex they coalesce into a single walk (this can be seen immediately by the graphical representation in
3

Because
G
is finite, any two independent random walks meet in finite time. Consequently,
\tau_{\rm coal}
, the first time at which all the random walks sit at the same vertex called the
coalescence time
, satisfies
\tau_{\rm coal}<\infty
almost surely. In particular, the above mentioned duality implies that
\tau_{\rm cons}\leq\tau_{\rm coal}
, and therefore any upper bound on the coalescence time immediately yields an upper bound on the consensus time of the voter model.

Given
x,y\in[n]
, let

(2.1)

\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}=\inf\{t\geq 0\colon\,X_{t}^{x}=X_{t}^{y}\}

denote the
meeting time
of two independent continuous-time random walks starting from
x
and
y
, respectively. More generally, if the two random walks are initialised randomly and independently according to two distributions
\mu
and
\nu
on
[n]
, then we denote their meeting time by
\tau_{\rm meet}^{\mu\otimes\nu}
. We note that the extension to infinite graphs
G
follows from standard estimates on the probability that the process can be approximated by a finite dynamics in finite portions of space during a bounded amount of time.

2.2.
The First Visit Time Lemma

In this section we state the
First Visit Time Lemma
(FVTL), which was introduced by
Cooper and Frieze, 2005
and recently simplified by
Manzo et al., 2021
. This lemma contains estimates on the time a random walk takes to reach a distinguished site when starting from the stationary distribution.

Remark 2.1
.

Since the FVTL is defined in discrete time, in what follows it will be easier to derive the results in discrete time and then translate them to continuous time. For this reason, when considering two random walks in discrete time, where at each step the random walk that moves is selected uniformly at random, we use the notation
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}
to refer to their law.

Theorem 2.2

(First Visit Time Lemma;
Manzo et al., 2021
)
.

Consider a sequence of ergodic Markov chains labelled by
N\in\mathbb{N}
, living on a finite state space
\Omega_{N}
of size
N
, with transition matrix
Q_{N}
and stationary distribution
\mu_{N}
and a target state
x\in\Omega_{N}
. Define

(2.2)

T_{\rm mix}^{(N)}=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\,\max_{x,y\in\Omega_{N}}|Q_{N}^{t}(x,y)-\mu_{N}(y)|\leq\frac{1}{N^{3}}\right\},

and assume that

(1)

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2}\min_{x\in\Omega_{N}}\mu_{N}(x)=\infty
.

(2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}T_{\rm mix}^{(N)}\max_{x\in\Omega_{N}}\mu_{N}(x)=0
.

(3)

There exists a unique
quasi-stationary distribution
associated to
x
, i.e., for all
N
large enough the sub-matrix
[Q_{N}]_{x}
in which the row and column have been erased is
irreducible
. See also
(
Quattropani and Sau, 2022
, Remark 3.2 and Section 4.4)
.

Then there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{x}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left|\frac{\lambda^{(N)}_{x}}{\frac{\mu_{N}(x)}{R_{N}(x)}}-1\right|=0,\qquad R_{N}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{s=0}^{T^{(N)}_{\rm mix}}Q_{N}^{s}(x,x),

and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\frac{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\mu_{N}}(\tau_{x}>t)}{(1-\lambda^{(N)}_{x})^{t}}-1\right|=0,

with
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\mu_{N}}
the path measure of the Markov chain starting from
\mu_{N}
.

In words, the FVTL states that, under the assumptions, the hitting time of a state
x
is well approximated by a geometric (exponential, in continuous time) random variable with mean
\frac{R(x)}{\mu(x)}
.

2.3.
The geometry of regular random graphs

In this section we collect a number of definitions and asymptotic results about the
typical geometry
of regular random graphs. We start by introducing notation and conclude by stating some basic facts regarding typical
d
-regular random graphs.

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E)
and two vertices
x,y\in V
, we denote by
{\rm dist}(x,y)
the distance between
x
and
y
, given by the number of edges of the shortest path joining
x
and
y
. For a vertex
x\in V
and a radius
h\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{x}(h)
the ball of radius
h
centered at
x
, seen as a subgraph of
G
. The tree excess of a graph quantifies how far away from a tree a given connected graph is.

Definition 2.3

(Tree excess)
.

Given a connected graph
G=(V,E)
, the
tree excess
of
G
, denoted by
\tx(G)
, is the minimum number of edges that must be removed from
G
in order to obtain a tree. This can also be written as

\tx(G)=|V|-|E|-1.

Next, we introduce the notion of
locally tree-like vertices and edges
.

Definition 2.4

(Locally tree-like vertex)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, we say that a vertex
x\in V
is locally tree-like up to distance
\ell\geq 1
, denoted by
x\in{\rm LTL}(\ell)
, if the subgraph induced by the vertices at distance at most
\ell
from
x
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}_{x}(\ell)
, is a tree.

Definition 2.5

(Locally tree-like edge)
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and an edge
e\in E
, we say that
e=\{x,y\}
is locally tree-like up to distance
\ell\geq 1
, denoted by
e\in{\rm LTLE}(\ell)
, if the subgraph induced by the vertices at distance at most
\ell
from
x
or
y
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}_{x}(\ell)\cup\mathcal{B}_{y}(\ell)
, after removal of the edge
e
, is composed of two disjoint trees of depth at most
\ell
.

For
d\geq 3
and
n>d
such that
nd
is even, there exists at least one graph with
n
vertices and constant degree equal to
d
. In particular, for this range of parameters the
d
-regular random graph of size
n
, drawn from the uniform distribution on the set of all graphs with
n
vertices and constant degree equal to
d
, is well defined. Denote by
m=dn/2
the number of edges of this random graph. The following proposition collects properties of the
d
-regular random graph that will be needed in the rest of the paper.

Proposition 2.6

(Geometry of regular random graphs and random walks;
Lubetzky and Sly, 2010
)
.

Fix
d\geq 3
, and let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
denote the
d
-regular random graph of size
n
. Then,
whp
,

(i)

G
is connected.

(ii)

For every
x\in[n]
,
\tx(\mathcal{B}_{x}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n))\leq 1
.

(iii)

|{\rm LTLE}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n)|=m-o(n)
.

(iv)

There exists a constant
C=C_{d}>1
such that for
t=C\log n
and for every
\varepsilon>0
,

(2.3)

d_{\rm TV}(t):=\max_{x\in[n]}\|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\rm TV}\leq\varepsilon.

In particular, for
t_{n}
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\log n/t_{n}=0
, then
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\rm TV}(t_{n})=0
.

3.
Computation of the expectation

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
, in particular, we provide a
whp
first-order estimate of the expected density of discordant edges at time
t=t_{n}
, for every choice of the sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In Section
3.1
we collect some basic results for the voter model on the infinite
d
-regular tree
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, the
local approximation
of the
d
-regular random graph. We show that the density of discordant edges at time
t
on
\mathcal{T}_{d}
with
{\rm Ber}(u)
initialisation behaves as
2u(1-u)(1-f(t))
. This will be done by exploiting duality: we reduce the problem to the analysis of a pair of coalescing random walks starting at the two extremes of an edge of the tree. In Section
3.2
we show that a classical coupling argument suffices to show that the approximation holds for every sequence
t=(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
t/\log n\to 0
. When
t
starts to become comparable with the typical distance of the graph, i.e.,
t_{n}=\Theta(\log n)
, the coupling argument fails and we need a refined analysis of the process to compute the expected density. This scenario will be handled in Section
3.3
. The idea is to track a pair of random walks starting from the two extremes of a typical (hence,
locally tree-like
) edge up to their first meeting. Using the First Visit Time Lemma, we show that, under the event that the two random walks do not meet after a short time, the time of their first meeting is well approximated by exponential random variable with rate
\theta_{d}^{-1}n
.

3.1.
Discordant edges on the regular tree

Let
\mathcal{T}_{d}
denote the
infinite
d
-regular tree
with
d\geq 3
. Our first lemma concerns the probability that two independent random walks, starting at distance one from each other, do not meet within a time
t
. Recall that
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}
denotes the law of two independent random walks starting from then endvertices of an edge in
\mathcal{T}_{d}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Meeting on a regular tree)
.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{T}_{d}
with
{\rm dist}(x,y)=1
. Then

(3.1)

f_{d}(t)=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm meet}\leq t\big)=\sum_{\kappa=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-2t}\frac{(2t)^{\kappa}}{\kappa!}\sum_{s=0}^{\lfloor\frac{\kappa-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{2s}{s}\frac{1}{s+1}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\Big)^{s+1}\Big(\frac{d-1}{d}\Big)^{s}.

From this it follows that

(3.2)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm meet}=\infty)=\frac{d-2}{d-1}=\theta_{d}.

Proof.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be two independent continuous-time random walks starting from
x\sim y
, respectively. Put
Z_{t}={\rm dist}(X_{t},Y_{t})
and note that
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous-time biased random walk on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
that starts at
1
, jumps at rate
2
, and has jump distribution given by

(3.3)

p(z,z+1)=1-p(z,z-1)=\frac{d-1}{d},\qquad z\in\mathbb{N}.

The claim in (
3.1
) follows from the construction of the random walk
(Z_{t})_{t\geq 0}
via a Poisson process of rate
2
for the jump times, together with a skeleton chain given by the discrete-time biased random walk with jump distribution given by (
3.3
), and explicit expressions for hitting times of discrete-time random walks based on path-counting arguments.

To get (
3.2
) it is enough to note that, by the standard Gambler’s ruin argument,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm meet}<\infty\big)=\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(Z_{t}=0\text{ for some }t\geq 0\mid Z_{0}=1\big)=\frac{p(0,-1)}{p(0,1)}=\frac{1}{d-1},

from which the claim follows.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that (
3.2
) can be derived from (
3.1
) by direct computation. Indeed, clearly
f(0)=0
. The generating function
c(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}C_{k}x^{k}
for the Catalan numbers
C_{k}=\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{k+1}
solves the quadratic equation
c(x)=1+xc(x)^{2}
. Since
C_{0}=1
, we have

c(x)=\frac{[1-\sqrt{1-4x}\,]}{2x}.

It follows that

f(\infty)=\frac{1}{d}\,c\left(\frac{d-1}{d^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\,\frac{\left[1-\frac{d-2}{d}\right]}{2\>\frac{d-1}{d^{2}}}=\frac{1}{d-1}=1-\theta_{d}.

Lemma 3.3

(Density of discordant edges)
.

Consider the voter model on
\mathcal{T}_{d}
with initial density
u
. Then, for any given edge
e
and any time
t\geq 0
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t}\big)=2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t)),

where
f_{d}(t)
is given by (
3.1
).

Proof.

Put
e=\{x,y\}
. Note that in order to have
e\in D_{t}
, by duality, the (backward) random walks starting from
x
and
y
at time
t
do not coalesce before time
0
and end up at vertices with distinct initial opinions. The initial opinions are independent of the trajectories of these random walks. The claim follows by noting that
1-f_{d}(t)
is the probability that the two random walks do not meet in time
t
, and
2u(1-u)
is the probability that two distinct vertices have different initial opinions.
∎

3.2.
Expectation for
small times

We start this section with a general bound that does not require the graph
G
to be random nor the size of
G
to grow to infinity.

Lemma 3.4

(Discordant LTLEs)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be any
d
-regular graph and let
e=\{x,y\}\in E
be
{\rm LTLE}(\ell)
for some
\ell
. Consider the voter model on
G
with initial density
u
. Then, for any time
T>0
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}(e\in D_{t})-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t))\right|\leq\frac{4T}{\ell}.

Proof.

Note first that the term
2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t))
corresponds to the probability that a given edge in
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is discordant at time
t
. In particular, if we consider the dual system with coalescing random walks, then the probabilities we are interested in are the same, provided the random walks do not leave the tree-like neighbourhood of
e
. Therefore consider the event

\mathcal{E}_{T}=\big\{\text{the number of jumps the random walks perform before time }T\text{ is bounded by }\ell\big\},

and observe that, by the Markov inequality,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{E}_{T}^{c}\big)=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(\mathcal{E}_{T}^{c}\big)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(X\geq\ell\big)\leq\frac{2T}{\ell},

where
X\sim{\rm Poisson}(2T)
stochastically dominates the number of jumps that both random walks make up to time
T
. By construction, for
t\leq T
we can write

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t},\mathcal{E}_{T}\big)=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t},\mathcal{E}_{T}\big),

from which we can estimate

\begin{split}&\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}(e\in D_{t})-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t))\right|=\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t}\big)-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t}\big)\Big|\\
&\leq\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t},\mathcal{E}_{T}\big)-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(e\in D_{t},\mathcal{E}_{T}\big)\Big|+2\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\mathcal{T}_{d}}\big(\mathcal{E}_{T}^{c}\big)\leq\frac{4T}{\ell},\end{split}

which settles the claim.
∎

Proposition 3.5

(Short time average)
.

Consider the voter model on a regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
with initial density
u\in(0,1)
. Then, for any time
t_{n}
satisfying
t_{n}/\log n\to 0
,

(3.4)

\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}]-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t_{n}))\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Note that for every realisation of
G
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[|D_{t_{n}}|]=\sum_{e\in E}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}(e\in D^{n}_{t_{n}}).

Call
E_{\star}\subset E
the set of
{\rm LTLE}\big(\frac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)
edges. Then, by Lemma
3.4
and our assumption on
t_{n}
,

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}]-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t_{n}))\big|&\leq\Big|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{e\in E}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t}\big)-2u(1-u)(1-f_{d}(t_{n}))\Big|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{e\in E_{\star}}\frac{4t_{n}}{\frac{1}{5}\log_{d}n}+\frac{1}{m}|E_{\star}^{c}|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0,\end{split}

where the last convergence follows from Proposition
2.6
(iii).
∎

3.3.
Expectation for
long times

In this section we show how the behaviour in Proposition
3.5
can be extended up to the linear time scale. Clearly, we need a different argument, since we can no longer assume that the dual random walks starting at the extremes of a locally tree-like edge do not exit their neighbourhood of size
\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}(n)
. In order to proceed, we adapt the arguments in
(
Cooper et al., 2010
, Lemmas 17 and 20)
to our framework, which will constitute a crucial tool for our analysis. In what follows, we fix

(3.5)

\sigma_{n}=\lceil\left(\log\log n\right)^{2}\rceil.

The following lemma says that
whp
for every pair of starting vertices that are more than
\sigma_{n}
apart the probability that the two random walks meet before time
\log^{3}n
is exponentially small in
\sigma_{n}
.

Lemma 3.6

(Meeting time of distant random walks)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a regular random graph. Then there exists a constant
c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}>0
such that

\mathds{1}\left\{\max_{x,y\colon\,{\rm dist}(x,y)>\sigma_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq\log^{3}n\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}}\right\}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1.

Proof.

The proof comes in several steps.

1.

Consider two independent random walks
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
that start from
x
and
y
, respectively. The number of steps each random walk performs before time
\log^{3}n
has distribution
{\rm Poisson}(\log^{3}n)
. In particular, Markov’s inequality with exponential moments implies that, for
\lambda=\log\log n
,

\begin{split}&\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\bigg(\begin{array}[]{c}X_{t}\text{ or }Y_{t}\text{ performs more than}\log^{4}n\text{ steps before time }\log^{3}n\end{array}\bigg)\\
&\leq 2\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\log^{4}n}\exp\big\{(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}-1)\log^{3}n\big\}=2\exp\big\{(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda}-1)\log^{3}n-\lambda\log^{4}n\big\}\\
&=2\exp\big\{(\log n-1-\log\log n\log n)\log^{3}n\big\}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}},\end{split}

provided
n
is large enough. We can consider only the case where
X
and
Y
perform discrete-time random walks in which at every step one of the random walks is selected uniformly at random to move. We then just need to verify that
whp

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq 2\log^{4}n\big)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}},

uniformly over the pairs
x
and
y
that are at least
\sigma_{n}
apart.

2.

Let
Z_{t}
denote the graph distance of
X_{t}
and
Y_{t}
at time
t
. Note that
Z_{0}\geq\sigma_{n}
, and
Z_{t+1}-Z_{t}\in\{-1,0,1\}
for every
t\geq 0
(the case
Z_{t+1}-Z_{t}=0
corresponds to jumps like the one in Figure
4
). This implies that the random walks cannot meet before time
\sigma_{n}
. Recall from Proposition
2.6
that
whp

\tx(\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(v))\leq 1,\qquad\text{for all }v\in V.

X
Y

Figure 4.
In a general
d
-regular graph the potential existence of cycles of length
3
allows the distance between the two random walks to remain constant after
1
step. In the picture, if
Y
moves along the blue arrow, then it remains at distance
1
to
X
.

It is now enough to show that

q_{n}=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\Big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\in\big\{\sigma_{n},\dots,2\log^{4}n\big\}\Big)\leq 4\log^{8}n\mathrm{e}^{-2c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}}\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}},

for some positive
c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}
and all
n
large enough. Put
\rho=\max\{t\leq\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\colon Z_{t}\geq\sigma_{n}\}
. We rewrite the probability of interest as

q_{n}\leq 2\log^{4}n\max_{r\leq 2\log^{4}n}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq 2\log^{4}n,\rho=r\big).

We aim at providing bounds on the above probability that are uniform in
r\leq 2\log^{4}n
. To that end, consider the ball of radius
2\sigma_{n}
around
X_{\rho}
, which by assumption satisfies

\tx(\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho}))\leq 1.

We distinguish between two cases: (1) the quantity above is
0
(and thus
\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho})
is a tree); (2) the tree excess of
\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho})
is
1
.

3.
First, consider the case when
\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho})
is a tree. Call
\mathfrak{p}
the unique path joining
X_{\rho}
and
Y_{\rho}
within the tree, and recall that
|\mathfrak{p}|={\rm dist}(X_{\rho},Y_{\rho})=\sigma_{n}
. Let us argue that at least one of the random walks does
\sigma_{n}/2
steps along
\mathfrak{p}
. Indeed, suppose first that for all
t\in\{\rho,\dots,\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\}
neither
X_{t}
nor
Y_{t}
leaves the tree
\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho})
. In order for the two random walks to meet, they need to jointly traverse the path
\mathfrak{p}
. If, on the other hand, one of the two random walks leaves
\mathcal{B}_{2\sigma_{n}}(X_{\rho})
, then our claim is met by the definition of
\rho
. Here, note that when we say that the first/second random walk must have made
\sigma_{n}/2
steps along
\mathfrak{p}
, we do not require that the steps are made consecutively. Nonetheless, under
\{\rho=r\}
, requiring that at least one of the two random walks does
\sigma_{n}/2
steps along
\mathfrak{p}
within time
2\log^{4}n
is equivalent to requiring that

\hat{\tau}_{r}=\inf\{u\geq r\colon\,{\rm dist}(X_{u},Y_{r})\wedge{\rm dist}(X_{r},Y_{u})\leq\tfrac{\sigma_{n}}{2}\}

satisfies
\hat{\tau}_{r}\leq 2\log^{4}n
. We can now bound, uniformly in
r\leq 2\log^{4}n
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq 2\log^{4}n,\rho=r\big)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\hat{\tau}_{r}\leq 2\log^{4}n,\rho=r\big)\leq 2\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm abs}\leq 2\log^{4}n\big),

where
\tau_{\rm abs}
is the absorption time at the origin of a biased random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
, and
(W_{u})_{u\geq 0}
is such that
W_{0}=\sigma_{n}/2
and

Hence, by the classical Gambler’s ruin argument,

Proposition 3.7

(Exponential scaling of meeting time starting in stationarity)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a regular random graph. There exists a sequence of random variables
\Theta_{d}=(\Theta_{d}^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(3.7)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\frac{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{\pi\otimes\pi}>t)}{\exp\left(-\frac{2\>\Theta_{d}\>t}{n}\right)}-1\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0\>,

and

\Theta_{d}^{(n)}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\theta_{d}=\frac{d-2}{d-1}.

Proof.

We show that for the discrete-time version of the process, in which at each time step one randomly chosen walk performs a jump, we have

(3.8)

\sup_{t\geq 0}\left|\frac{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{\pi\otimes\pi}>t)}{\left(1-\frac{\>\Theta_{d}}{n}\right)^{t}}-1\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

The claim in (
3.7
) follows by Poissonisation. For the discrete-time process we can exploit the full power of the FVTL in Theorem
2.2
. The proof comes in several steps.

1.

Consider the multi-graph
\Gamma
with vertex set
V_{\Gamma}=\{(x,y)\colon\,x,y\in[n],\>x\neq y\}\cup\{\Delta\}
, where
\Delta
is the merge of the vertices on the diagonal that retains the edges. More precisely,

((x,y),(v,w))\in E_{\Gamma}\text{ if and only if }\begin{cases}x=v\text{ and }(y,w)\in E,\\
y=w\text{ and }(x,v)\in E,\end{cases}

and

(\Delta,(v,w))\in E_{\Gamma}\text{ if and only if }(v,w)\in E,

with each edge adjacent to
\Delta
having multiplicity
2
. Roughly,
\Gamma
is such that every vertex except
\Delta
has degree
2d
, while
\Delta
has degree
4m=2dn
. It follows that the sum of the degrees in
\Gamma
equals
2m_{\Gamma}=2dn(n-1)+2dn=2dn^{2}
, and that the number of vertices is given by
N=n^{2}-n+1
. Throughout this proof, let
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}
denote the distribution of the simple random walk on
\Gamma
,
P_{\Gamma}
the associated transition matrix, and
V_{\Gamma}
the set of vertices. The stationary distribution for this random walk
\pi_{\Gamma}
is given by

\pi_{\Gamma}(\Delta)=\frac{1}{n},

while, for all
(x,y)
with
x\neq y
,

\pi_{\Gamma}(x,y)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

2.
Concerning the mixing time in (
2.2
), it is not hard to show that we can choose
T_{\rm mix}=\log^{2}n
. For completeness, we provide a full proof by adapting the proof of
(
Cooper et al., 2010
, Lemma 12)
to our asynchronous setting. As shown in
Friedman, 2008
, the second largest eigenvalue of
P
, denoted by
\lambda_{\star}
, is bounded away from
1

whp
, from which it follows via
(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Corollary 12.13)
that the same holds for the matrix
P_{\otimes 2}=\frac{1}{2}(P\otimes I+I\otimes P)
. Write
\lambda_{\otimes 2}
(respectively,
\lambda_{\Gamma}
) to denote the spectral gap of the matrix
P_{\otimes 2}
(respectively,
P_{\Gamma}
). By
(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Theorem 13.10)
and with the abbreviation

(3.9)

\Phi_{\otimes 2}\coloneqq\min_{S\subset V^{2}\>:\>\pi_{\otimes 2}(S)\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in S}\pi(\boldsymbol{x})\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in S^{c}}P_{\otimes 2}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{\pi_{\otimes 2}(S)}

for the
conductance
of the product chain, we know that

(3.10)

\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{\otimes 2}^{2}\leq 1-\lambda_{\otimes 2}\leq 2\Phi_{\otimes 2}.

Therefore
\Phi_{\otimes 2}
is bounded away from
0

whp
. By the construction of
\Gamma
, we must have
\Phi_{\otimes 2}\leq\Phi_{\Gamma}
, where the latter is the conductance of the Markov chain
P_{\Gamma}
. Indeed, the graph
\Gamma
is constructed from
G\times G
by contracting some vertices and retaining the edges. Moreover, again by (
3.10
) to the Markov chain
P_{\Gamma}
,

(3.11)

{1-\lambda_{\Gamma}}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{\Gamma}^{2}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{\otimes 2}^{2}>0.

Hence, we deduce that
\lambda_{\Gamma}
must be bounded away from
1

whp
. Finally, by
(
Levin and Peres, 2017
, Theorem 12.4)
, we conclude that
O(\log n)
steps suffice to get (
2.2
).

3.

Thus, Assumptions (1) and (3) in Theorem
2.2
are satisfied
whp
with
N=n^{2}-n+1
,
Q_{N}=P_{\Gamma}
,
\mu_{N}=\pi_{\Gamma}
. Moreover, choosing
x=\Delta
we easily check the
whp
existence of a unique quasi-stationary distribution, thanks to the fact that
d\geq 3
,
G
is connected, and the two random walks move asynchronously. In order to use the FVTL, we are left to compute the expected number of returns to
\Delta
before time
T_{\rm mix}\leq\log^{2}n
, starting at
\Delta
. For
t\geq 1
, put

R_{t}(\Delta)=\sum_{s=0}^{t}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{s}=\Delta\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big).

We will show that

(3.12)

R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1+\frac{1-\theta_{d}}{\theta_{d}}=\frac{1}{\theta_{d}}=\frac{d-1}{d-2}.

The term
1
comes from the fact that the random walk starts at
\Delta
.

4.
We start by giving the heuristics of the proof. Call
(x,y)
the vertex of
\Gamma
visited at time
1
. By Proposition
2.6
(ii), the probability that the edge
e=(x,y)\in E
is
{\rm LTLE}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)
is
1-o(1)
. On this event, the probability that there will be a second visit to
\Delta
before
T_{\rm mix}
is upper bounded by
\frac{1}{d-1}
. A simple combination of these arguments yields an upper bound. As for the lower bound, the probability that the random walk on
\Gamma
starting at
(x,y)
reaches a vertex
(v,w)
with
{\rm dist}_{G}(v,w)\geq\sigma_{n}
before reaching
\Delta
is
\theta_{d}+o(1)
. Once at
(v,w)
, the probability that the random walk visits
\Delta
before
T_{\rm mix}
is
o(1)
. Hence, up to
o(1)
corrections,
R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)
can be estimated by the expectation of a geometric random variable with parameter
\theta_{d}+o(1)
, representing the first excursion of the random walk that hits
(v,w)
with
{\rm dist}_{G}(v,w)\geq\sigma_{n}
before returning to
\Delta
.

5.
With this heuristics in mind, we are ready to provide the proof of (
3.12
). We will do so by first assuming that

(3.13)

\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\leq\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq T_{\rm mix}\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0

and

(3.14)

\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1-\theta_{d}.

Write

(3.15)

\begin{split}R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)&=1+\sum_{s=1}^{T_{\rm mix}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{s}=\Delta\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\\
&=1+\sum_{r=1}^{T_{\rm mix}}\sum_{s=r}^{T_{\rm mix}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{s}=\Delta,\tau_{\Delta}^{+}=r\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\\
&=1+\sum_{r=1}^{T_{\rm mix}}\sum_{s=r}^{T_{\rm mix}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{s}=\Delta\mid X_{r}=\Delta\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}=r\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\\
&=1+\sum_{r=1}^{T_{\rm mix}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}=r\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\sum_{s=0}^{T_{\rm mix}-r}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{s}=\Delta\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\\
&=1+\sum_{r=1}^{T_{\rm mix}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}=r\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)R_{T_{\rm mix}-r}(\Delta).\end{split}

To establish the upper bound in (
3.12
), observe that (
3.15
) yields

R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)\leq 1+\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq T_{\rm mix}\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta),

which yields

R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)\leq\Big(1-\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq T_{\rm mix}\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\Big)^{-1},

and the claim follows by combining (
3.13
) and (
3.14
).

6.
To establish the lower bound in (
3.12
), observe that (
3.15
) yields

\begin{split}R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)&\geq 1+\sum_{r=1}^{\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}=r\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)R_{T_{\rm mix}-r}(\Delta)\\
&\geq 1+\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)R_{T_{\rm mix}-\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n}(\Delta).\end{split}

By iterating the estimate above, we obtain

(3.16)

R_{T_{\rm mix}}(\Delta)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{5T_{\rm mix}/\log_{d}n}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)^{i}=\frac{1}{\theta_{d}}-o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

where the last equality follows from (
3.14
). This concludes the verification of the hypotheses of Theorem
2.2
.

To conclude the proof, we still need to verify (
3.13
) and (
3.14
). We start with (
3.13
). By Proposition
2.6
(iii), we have

(3.17)

\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{1}\in\text{LTLE}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1.

Indeed, by construction, the random walk on
\Gamma
starting at
\Delta
after a single step visits a
(x,y)\in E
uniformly at random. Assume that
X_{1}=(x,y)\in{\rm LTLE}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)
, and let
\tilde{\tau}
denote the first time at which the process on
\Gamma
is found at some
(w,z)
with
{\rm dist}_{G}(w,z)=\sigma_{n}
. Then, up to time
\tau_{\Delta}\wedge\tilde{\tau}
, the random walk
(X_{t})_{t\geq 1}
can be coupled with a biased random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
(given by the distance between the two vertices) that starts from
1
, jumps to the right (respectively, left) with probability
\tfrac{d-1}{d}
(respectively,
\tfrac{1}{d}
). Call this random walk
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and let
\tau_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
, be its first hitting of
j
. Then a gambler’s ruin argument yields

\Exp_{1}\big(\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{\sigma_{n}}\big)\leq\frac{d\sigma_{n}}{d-2},

which implies

(3.18)

\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{1}\big(\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{\sigma_{n}}>\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n-1\big)\leq\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{1}\big(\tau_{0}\wedge\tau_{\sigma_{n}}>\sigma_{n}^{3}\big)\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}

for all
n
sufficiently large. Compute

\begin{split}&\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\wedge\tilde{\tau}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)\\
&\geq\sum_{(x,y)\in(V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\})\cap{\rm LTLE}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\Delta}\big(X_{1}=(x,y)\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{(x,y)}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\wedge\tilde{\tau}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n-1\big)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:bound_probability}}{\geq}\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}\Big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{1}\in\text{LTLE}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big).\end{split}

Note that the last line above converges to 1 in probability as
n\to\infty
(see (
3.17
)), which concludes the verification of (
3.13
).

We are left with proving (
3.14
). In this case, we can assume once again that
X_{1}\in\text{LTLE}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n)
, which in particular implies that, up to time
\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n
, the random walk can be perfectly coupled with a biased random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
that starts at
1
and jumps to the right (respectively, left) with probability
\tfrac{d-1}{d}
(respectively,
\tfrac{1}{d}
). The claim follows by noting that

\begin{split}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{+}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\mid X_{0}=\Delta\big)&=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)+\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{1}\big(\tau_{0}\leq\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n-1\big)\\
&=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)+\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{1}\big(\tau_{0}<\infty\big)=o(1)+\frac{1}{d-1},\end{split}

which concludes the verification of (
3.14
).
∎

Lemma 3.8

(Tail of meeting time of distant random walks)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a regular random graph and consider two independent random walks. Then

(3.19)

\max_{x,y\colon\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\geq\sigma_{n}}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm meet}>t\big)-\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{2t\theta_{d}}{n}}\Big|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

We need to deduce from Proposition
3.7
an (additive) analogous result when the two random walks start far away. For that we use Lemma
3.6
.

For simplicity we work again in discrete time, so that what we actually need to show is

(3.20)

\max_{x,y\colon\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\geq\sigma_{n}}\max_{t\geq 0}\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t)-\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{t}\right|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Fix
x
and
y
such that
{\rm dist}(x,y)\geq\sigma_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that the two random walks meet before the mixing time
T_{\rm mix}
. Since
T_{\rm mix}\leq\log^{3}n
, Lemma
3.6
states that
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\mathcal{E})\leq\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}}

whp
for every pair
(x,y)
as above. Hence, if
t\leq T_{\rm mix}
, then

(3.21)

\min_{x,y\colon\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\geq\sigma_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t\big)\geq 1-\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}}.

On the other hand, if
t>T_{\rm mix}
, then by definition of
T_{\rm mix}
,

(3.22)

\max_{(x,y)\in V_{\Gamma}}|\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X_{t}^{x}=v,Y^{y}_{t}=w\big)-\pi_{\Gamma}(v,w)|\leq\frac{1}{n^{6}}.

Therefore

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t\big)&=\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{(x,y)}>T_{\rm mix},\,X^{x}_{T_{\rm mix}}=v,\,Y^{y}_{T_{\rm mix}}=w\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)\\
&\leq\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X^{x}_{T_{\rm mix}}=v,\,Y^{y}_{T_{\rm mix}}=w\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)\\
&\leq\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\}}\pi_{\Gamma}(v,w)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-{T_{\rm mix}}\big)+\frac{1}{n^{6}}\\
&\leq\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{\pi_{\Gamma}}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)+\frac{1}{n^{4}}.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}&\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t\big)\\
&=\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\Delta}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\Delta}^{(x,y)}>T_{\rm mix},\,X^{x}_{T_{\rm mix}}=v,\,Y^{y}_{T_{\rm mix}}=w\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)\\
&\geq\left(\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(X^{x}_{T_{\rm mix}}=v,\,Y^{y}_{T_{\rm mix}}=w\big)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)\right)-\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq T_{\rm mix}\big)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:mixingtime}}{\geq}\sum_{(v,w)\in V_{\Gamma}\setminus\{\Delta\}}\pi_{\Gamma}(v,w)\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{(v,w)}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)-\frac{1}{n^{6}}-\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq T_{\rm mix}\big)\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:meeting_time_estimate}}{=}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau^{\pi_{\Gamma}}_{\Delta}>t-T_{\rm mix}\big)-\frac{1}{n^{4}}-\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\ref{c:bound_meeting_time}}\sigma_{n}}.\end{split}

This, when combined with Propostition
3.7
, implies the claim because

\Big|\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{t}-\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{t-T_{{\rm mix}}}\Big|=\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{t}\Big|1-\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{-T_{{\rm mix}}}\Big|\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t\theta_{d}}{n}}\Big|1-\Big(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\Big)^{-T_{{\rm mix}}}\Big|

converges to zero as
n\to\infty
, uniformly in
t\geq 0
.
∎

Proposition 3.9

(Tail of meeting time of adjacent random walks on a LTLE)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a regular random graph, let
e=(x,y)
be in
{\rm LTLE}\big(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n\big)
, and consider two independent random walks starting from the endvertices of
e
. Then, for all
t_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}/n=s\geq 0
,

\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t_{n})-\theta_{d}\expo^{-2s\theta_{d}}\Big|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Note that it is enough to assume that
t\geq\sigma_{n}^{3}
, since otherwise the claim follows from Lemma
3.4
. Let
\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}
be the first time at which the two random walks are at distance at least
\sigma_{n}
, and
\tau_{\rm exit}^{x,y}
the first time at which one of the two random walks exits the locally tree-like neighbourhood of radius
\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n
of
e
. Put
\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\wedge\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}\wedge\tau_{\rm exit}^{x,y}
. For simplicity, we start by analysing the discrete-time process. Up to time
\tau_{0}^{x,y}
, the process can be coupled to a biased random walk
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with
W_{0}=1
and

p=\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(W_{t+1}-W_{t}=1\big)=1-\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(W_{t+1}-W_{t}=-1\big)=\frac{d-1}{d}.

In particular,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}^{\rm dt}\big[\tau_{0}^{x,y}\big]\leq\frac{d\sigma_{n}}{d-2},

and therefore

(3.23)

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{0}=\tau^{x,y}_{\rm exit}\big)&\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm exit}\leq\sigma_{n}^{3}\big)+\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau^{x,y}_{0}>\sigma_{n}^{3}\big)\\
&\leq 0+\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\frac{d\sigma_{n}}{d-2}=o(1),\end{split}

where the last term follows from the Markov inequality. Similarly, by a gambler ruin argument,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\left(\tau^{x,y}_{\rm far}>\sigma_{n}^{3}\right)=o(1),

and therefore

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t_{n},\,\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}\leq\sigma_{n}^{3}\big)+o(1).

The probability in the right-hand side of (
3.24
) can be written, in the discrete-time setting, as

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t_{n},\,\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}\leq\sigma_{n}^{3}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=0}^{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\sum_{(v,w)\colon\,{\rm dist}(v,w)>\sigma_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}=u,\>(X_{\tau_{0}^{x,y}},Y_{\tau_{0}^{x,y}})=(v,w))\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{v,w}>t_{n}-u)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{u=0}^{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\sum_{(v,w)\colon\,{\rm dist}(v,w)>\sigma_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}=u,\>(X_{\tau_{0}^{x,y}},Y_{\tau_{0}^{x,y}})=(v,w))\left(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\right)^{t_{n}-u}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\right)^{t_{n}}\sum_{u=0}^{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}=u)\left(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\right)^{-u}

\displaystyle\sim\left(1-\frac{\theta_{d}}{n}\right)^{t_{n}}\sum_{u=0}^{\sigma_{n}^{3}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}=u)\sim\mathrm{e}^{-s\theta_{d}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}(\tau_{0}^{x,y}=\tau_{\rm far}^{x,y}\leq\sigma_{n}^{3})\sim\theta_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{-s\theta_{d}}\qquad\textit{whp}.

In order to pass to the continuous-time setting, it is enough to realise that, for all
\varepsilon\in(0,s)
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>t_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left({\rm Poisson}(2t_{n})=u\right)\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>u\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u=2(s-\varepsilon)n\vee 0}^{2(s+\varepsilon)n}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left({\rm Poisson}(2sn)=u\right)\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}^{\rm dt}\big(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}>u\big)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle=\theta_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2s\theta_{d}}+O_{\mathbb{P}}(\varepsilon),

after which the claim follows by taking the limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
∎

The next two statements turn the result in Proposition
3.9
into a statement about the expected density of discordant edges at time
O(n)
for the voter model.

Corollary 3.10

(Discordant edges at small distances)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a
d
-regular random graph, let
e=(x,y)
be in
{\rm LTLE}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n)
, and consider the voter model with initial density
u\in(0,1)
. Then, for all
t_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}/n=s\in[0,\infty)
,

\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\big(e\in D_{t_{n}}\big)-2u(1-u)\,\theta_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2s\theta_{d}}\Big|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

The claim follows from Proposition
3.9
via duality.
∎

Proposition 3.11

(Expected density of discordant edges)
.

Let
G_{d,n}(\omega)
be a
d
-regular random graph, and consider the voter model with initial density
u\in(0,1)
. Then, for all
t_{n}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}/n=s\in[0,\infty)
,

\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}]-2u(1-u)\,\theta_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2s\theta_{d}}\Big|\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

The claim follows from Corollary
3.10
and the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
3.5
.
∎

4.
Beyond the expectation

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. We start with a proposition that implies the first statement of the theorem.

Proposition 4.1

(Concentration for worst case initialization)
.

Consider times
t_{n}
such that
t_{n}\to\infty
and
t_{n}/n\to 0
. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sup_{\xi\in\{0,1\}^{V}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}\left[\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}\right]\right|>\varepsilon\right)\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Proof.

Putting
E_{\star}={\rm LTLE}(\tfrac{1}{5}\log_{d}n)\subset E
and recalling that
|E_{\star}|=m-o(n)

whp
by Proposition
2.6
(ii), we have

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[|D_{t}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e,e^{\prime}\in E}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)=\sum_{e,e^{\prime}\in E_{\star}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)+o(n^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E_{\star}}\left[\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{\star},\>{\rm dist(e,e^{\prime})>\sigma_{n}}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)+O(d^{\sigma_{n}})\right]+o(n^{2}),

with
\sigma_{n}
as in (
3.5
).
Fix
e=(x,y),e^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in E_{\star}
such that
{\rm dist}(e,e^{\prime})>\sigma_{n}
, and consider the event

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{e,e^{\prime}}\coloneqq\left\{\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,x^{\prime}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y^{\prime}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{y,x^{\prime}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{y,y^{\prime}}>t\right\}.

We observe that, on the event
\mathcal{E}
, the events
\{e\in D_{t}\}
and
\{e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\}
are negatively correlated. Indeed, denote by
\sigma_{e}
(respectively,
\sigma_{e^{\prime}}
) a realisation of length
t
of the two independent random walk trajectories starting at
x
and
y
(respectively,
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
). Let
\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{e^{\prime}})
denote the set of possible realisations of
\sigma_{e^{\prime}}
that never meet the trajectory
\sigma_{e}
. We then have

(4.2)

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t},\>\mathcal{E})&=\sum_{\sigma_{e}\in\{e\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\in\{e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{e})}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\mid\sigma_{e}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma_{e}\in\{e\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\in\{e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{e})}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{\sigma_{e}\in\{e\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\in\{e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(\sigma_{e})\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\sigma_{e^{\prime}}\right)=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(e\in D_{t})\>\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(e^{\prime}\in D_{t}),\end{split}

which gives the claimed negative dependence.

Furthermore, by Lemma
3.8

, we have
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\mathcal{E}^{c})=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, which together with (
4.2
) guarantees that

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\ \right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t},\mathcal{E}\ \right)+\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(\mathcal{E}^{c})\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(e\in D_{t})\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Inserting the latter inequality into (
4.1
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}[|D_{t}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E_{\star}}\left[\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{\star},\>{\rm dist}(e,e^{\prime})>\sigma_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(e,e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)+O(d^{\sigma_{n}})\right]+o(n^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E_{\star}}\left[\sum_{e^{\prime}\in E_{\star},\>{\rm dist}(e,e^{\prime})>\sigma_{n}}\left[\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}(e\in D_{t})\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(e^{\prime}\in D_{t}\right)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)\right]+O(d^{\sigma_{n}})\right]+o(n^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}[|D_{t}|]^{2}+o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{2})+o(n^{2})=(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}[|D_{t}|]^{2}.

Therefore, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\big||D_{t}|-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}|D_{t}|\big|>\varepsilon m\right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\xi}\left(\big||D_{t}|-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}|D_{t}|\big|>\varepsilon\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}|D_{t}|\right)\leq\frac{{\rm Var}_{\xi}(|D_{t}|)}{\varepsilon^{2}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\xi}[|D_{t}|]^{2}}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

∎

We next consider times
t_{n}
such that
t_{n}/n\to s\in(0,\infty)
and use results from
Chen et al., 2016
. Let

(4.3)

\gamma_{n}=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau_{\textnormal{meet}}^{\pi\otimes\pi}]

be the expected meeting time of two independent continuous-time simple random walks with uniformly chosen initial positions.
Chen et al., 2016
(see Theorems 2.1 and 2.2 therein) provide conditions on the underlying sequence of graphs under which the proportion of type-1 vertices
(\mathcal{B}^{n}_{\gamma_{n}t})_{t\geq 0}
converges as a process in the space of càdlàg paths to the Fisher-Wright diffusion
(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\gamma_{n}
as in
Eq.

4.3
. In the case of
d
-regular random graphs,

(4.4)

\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\theta_{d}^{-1}\,n+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

and the requirements in
Chen et al., 2016
are easily verified
whp
.

The following lemma is the central estimate that we need in order to establish convergence of the proportion of discordant edges.

Lemma 4.2

(Linking density of discordant edges to density of type-1)
.

Let
s_{n}
converge to a finite value. Then

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}})\Big|\Big]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Before proving this lemma, we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
(ii).

Recall that we are assuming that
t_{n}/n\to s
. Consider
s_{n}=t_{n}/\gamma_{n}
and note that

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=2s\,\theta_{d}
,
due to (
4.4
). Hence Lemma
4.2
yields

(4.5)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}})\Big|\Big]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

Moreover, since
x\mapsto x(1-x)
is continuous and so is the Fisher-Wright diffusion
(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
Proposition
A.2
implies that

2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}})\quad\text{ converges in distribution to }\quad 2\theta_{d}\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{2s\,\theta_{d}}(1-\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{2s\,\theta_{d}}).

In view of (
4.5
), the same holds
whp
for
\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

To prove Lemma
4.2
, we use the following concentration estimate derived in
Chen et al., 2016
.

Lemma 4.3

(Concentration on arbitrary connected graphs)
.

Fix a finite connected graph
G
on
n
vertices and
m
edges and let
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote the voter model evolving on
G
. For any
0\leq s\leq t
,

\sup_{\eta_{0}}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{t}]-2\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}[\tau^{e}>s]\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{x}\eta_{0}(x)\sum_{x}(1-\eta_{0}(x))\Big|\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\in(s,t]\big]+4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}>s\big]d_{\rm TV}(t-s),

where
\tau^{e}
denotes the meeting time of two random walks starting from the two vertices at the end of a uniformly chosen edge of
G
, and
d_{TV}
is defined as in (
2.3
).

The proof of Lemma
4.3
is provided in
(
Chen et al., 2016
, Lemma 6.1)
. Note that it applies to any deterministic connected graph on which the voter dynamics takes place.

We will also need the asymptotic estimate

(4.6)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\geq k\log^{2}n\big]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\theta_{d},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N},

which follows from Proposition
3.9
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Fix
\delta_{n}=2\log^{2}n
, and split

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}})\Big|\Big]\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}})\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle+2\theta_{d}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}})-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}})\Big|\Big].

We will show that each of the expectations in the right-hand side above converges to zero.

1.

Apply the Markov property at time
s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}
, to obtain

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}})\Big|\Big]\leq\sup_{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{0}(1-\mathcal{B}_{0})\Big|\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\eta_{0}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}\Big[\big|\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}]\big|\Big]+\sup_{\eta_{0}}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}]-2\theta_{d}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{x}\eta_{0}(x)\sum_{x}(1-\eta_{0}(x))\Big|.

The first expectation in the right-hand side of (
4.8
) converges to zero by Proposition
4.1
and the fact that
|\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}]|
is bounded by one. To control the second expectation in (
4.8
), apply Lemma
4.3
with
t=\delta_{n}=2\log^{2}n
and
s=\log^{2}n
, to obtain

\begin{split}&\sup_{\eta_{0}}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}]-2\theta_{d}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{x}\eta_{0}(x)\sum_{x}(1-\eta_{0}(x))\Big|\\
&\leq\sup_{\eta_{0}}\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{\eta_{0}}[\mathcal{D}_{\delta_{n}}]-2\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\geq\log^{2}n\big]\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{x}\eta_{0}(x)\sum_{x}(1-\eta_{0}(x))\Big|+2\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\geq\log^{2}n\big]-\theta_{d}\Big|\\
&\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\in(\log^{2}n,2\log^{2}n]\big]+4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}>\log^{2}n\big]\textnormal{d}_{TV}(\log^{2}n)+2\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\geq\log^{2}n\big]-\theta_{d}\Big|.\end{split}

Note that both

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\in(\log^{2}n,2\log^{2}n]\big]=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}>2\log^{2}n\big]-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}>\log^{2}n\big]

and

\Big|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big[\tau^{e}\geq\log^{2}n\big]-\theta_{d}\Big|

converge to zero
whp
by (
4.6
), while
d_{\rm TV}(\log^{2}n)
goes to zero thanks to Proposition
2.6
(iv). Hence also the second expectation in (
4.8
) vanishes, and so we have

(4.9)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{D}_{t_{n}\gamma_{n}}-2\theta_{d}\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{t_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}})\Big|\Big]\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}0.

2.
It remains to show that the second term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
4.7
) vanishes
whp
, for which we argue by continuity in the proper topology.
For an arbitrary
T>0
, let
[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]
denote the product of
[0,T]
with the càdlàg space
\mathcal{D}[0,T]
endowed with the
J_{1}
-Skorohod topology. For a given
s>0
and an arbitrary evaluation function
h\colon\,[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
defined via
h(t,\phi)=\phi(t)
, we consider the incremental function
\tilde{h}\colon\,[0,T]^{2}\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\tilde{h}(t,u,\phi)=|h(t,\phi)-h(u,\phi)|,

and note that, by continuity of the modulus function and Lemma
A.1
, all points in
[0,T]^{2}\times\mathcal{C}[0,T]
are continuity points of
\tilde{h}
. In particular, since
\frac{\delta_{n}}{\gamma_{n}}\to 0
and with
s=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}<T
, we have that

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}})-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{s_{n}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n}})\Big|\Big]=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\Big|h\big(s_{n},(\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}))_{u\in[0,T]}\big)

\displaystyle-h\big(s_{n}-\frac{\delta_{n}}{\gamma_{n}},(\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}))_{u\in[0,T]}\big)\Big|\Big]=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\tilde{h}\big(s_{n},s_{n}-\frac{\delta_{n}}{\gamma_{n}},(\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}(1-\mathcal{B}_{u\gamma_{n}}))_{u\in[0,T]}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}\Big[\tilde{h}\big(s,s,(\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{u}(1-\bar{}\mathcal{B}_{u}))_{u\in[0,T]}\big)\Big]=0,

where the limit is justified by (
1.6
) and Proposition
A.2
.

3.

Combine (
4.7
), (
4.9
), and (
4.10
), to conclude the proof.
∎

5.
Uniform concentration

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
, i.e., we sharpen the result in Theorem
1.2
(i)
to a uniform bound over sublinear times up to the scale
n^{1-o(1)}
. Note that, up to a union bound on the set of jump times for the process
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{n}
, the proof amounts to showing the following.

Proposition 5.1

(Strengthening of the pointwise concentration)
.

Consider the voter model with initial density
u\in(0,1)
on a regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
. For any fixed
\varepsilon,\delta,a>0
,

\mathds{1}\left\{\max_{t\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}_{u}[\mathcal{D}_{t}]\right|>\delta\right)\leq n^{-a}\right\}\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\longrightarrow}1.

Proof.

The proof comes in several steps. To ease the reading, we drop the subindex
u
from the notation of the voter measure
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{u}
and abbreviate
p_{t}=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\mathcal{D}_{t}]
.

1.

Fix

t\leq n^{1-\varepsilon},\qquad K_{n}=\log^{2}n,

and write
A
to denote a collection of
K
edges of the graph sampled uniformly at random, independently of the voter dynamics. Call

\mathcal{D}^{A}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{K_{n}}\sum_{e\in A}\mathds{1}_{\{e\in D_{t}\}}

the fraction of edges in the random set
A
that are discordant at time
t
. By the triangle inequality, we have

(5.1)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}-p_{t}\right|>\delta\right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{t}^{A}\right|>\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right)+\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}^{A}-p_{t}\right|>\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right).

Note that, given
\mathcal{D}_{t}=q
, the quantity
\mathcal{D}_{t}^{A}
is distributed as
Z/K_{n}
, where
Z\sim{\rm Bin}(K_{n},q)
. Therefore, conditioning on
\mathcal{D}_{t}=q\in[0,1]
, we can bound the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.1
) by means of the Chernoff bound

(5.2)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{t}^{A}\right|>\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\>\bigg\rvert\>\frac{|D_{t}|}{m}=q\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{12q}K_{n}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{12}K_{n}\right).

Since the bound in (
5.2
) is uniform over
q\in[0,1]
and
t\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
, we conclude that, for every
a>0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,

\max_{t\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\left|\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{t}^{A}\right|>\tfrac{1}{2}\delta\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{12}K_{n}\right)\leq n^{-a},

for all
n
sufficiently large.

2.

We next show that a similar bound holds for the second term on the right-hand side of (
5.1
). The latter will be proved by means of duality. Consider a system of
n
independent random walks starting from the different vertices of
G
and evolving independently. We consider a (multi)sub-set of
2K_{n}
random walks starting at the extremes of the edges in the random set
A
. Note that these are distributed at time zero as
\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu
, where
\nu
is the probability distribution on
[n]^{2}
defined as

\nu(x,y)=\pi(x)\frac{1}{d}\mathds{1}_{x\sim y},\qquad(x,y)\in[n]^{2}.

Moreover, since
\pi\equiv\frac{1}{n}
, the two marginal distributions of
\nu
coincide with the stationary distribution, i.e., for every
x\in[n]
,

\sum_{z\in[n]}\nu(x,z)=\sum_{z\in[n]}\nu(z,x)=\frac{1}{n}.

Observe that

(5.3)

p_{t}=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\mathcal{D}_{t}]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{e\in E}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(e\in D_{t})=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\nu}(e\in D_{t}).

In order to simplify the notation, when considering a system of
2K
independent random walks starting at
\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu
, for each of the
K_{n}
random edges
A=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{K_{n}}\}
, we label the extremes as
e_{j}^{-}
and
e_{j}^{+}
, for all
j\in\{1,\dots,K_{n}\}
. For
i,j\leq K_{n}
, define the quantities

(5.4)

\tau^{e_{i},e_{j}}\coloneqq\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{i}^{-},e_{j}^{-}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{i}^{+},e_{j}^{-}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{i}^{-},e_{j}^{+}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{i}^{+},e_{j}^{+}}.

When
\tau^{e_{i},e_{j}}\leq t
we say that
the edges
i
and
j
interact before
t
. We say that
\tau^{e_{i},e_{j}}=0
when
\{e_{i}^{-},e_{i}^{+}\}\cap\{e_{j}^{-},e_{j}^{+}\}\neq\emptyset
. Note that, for all
i,j\leq K_{n}
and
t\geq 0
,

(5.5)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}(\tau^{e_{i},e_{j}}\leq t)=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\nu\otimes\nu}(\tau^{e_{1},e_{2}}\leq t)\leq 4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\pi\otimes\pi}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq t).

Indeed, the inequality in (
5.5
) follows from the definition of
\nu
and a union bound.

We next argue that for all
t=o(n)
the probability on the right-hand side of
Eq.

5.5
can be bounded above
whp
by

(5.6)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\pi\otimes\pi}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq t)=O\left(\frac{t}{n}\right).

Let us start by pointing out that the FVTL is not enough to deduce (
5.6
). Indeed, the FVTL is particularly suited to have sharp estimates of the right tail of the hitting time of a set on time scales that are
at least
as big as the mean hitting time. For this reason, we will exploit a result in
Aldous and Brown, 1992
that in a sense complements the FVTL on short time scales (see also
(
Hermon et al., 2021
, Lemma 2.11)
).

Lemma 5.2
.

For an irreducible and reversible continuous-time Markov chain with stationary distribution
\mu
and any subset of states
A
,

(5.7)

\left|\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\tau^{\mu}_{A}\leq t)-\frac{t}{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}]}\right|\leq\left(\frac{t}{\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}]}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{\star}\>\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}]},\qquad 0\leq t\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}],

where
\tau_{A}^{\mu}
is the hitting time of the set
A
when starting with distribution
\mu
, and
\lambda_{\star}
denotes the minimal non-trivial eigenvalue of the infinitesimal generator of the process.

In our setting we can think of the Markov chain as the simple random walk on
G\otimes G
and of
A
as the diagonal, so that
\mu=\pi\otimes\pi
,
\lambda_{\star}\in(0,1)
and
\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}]=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\pi\otimes\pi}_{\Delta}]=\Theta(n)
, where the latter follows by Proposition
3.7
. Note that, as soon as
t/n\to 0
, we have that the term on the right-hand side of (
5.7
) is
O(t/n)
, as for
t/\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}[\tau^{\mu}_{A}]
, so that (
5.6
) immediately follows.

The following lemma states that it is unlikely to have within
A
a density of edges interacting before time
t
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Consider a system of
n
independent random walks, each starting from a different vertex of a regular random graph
G_{d,n}(\omega)
. Select a sub(multi)set
A
of
E
of size
2K_{n}
with joint distribution
\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu
. Fix
\varepsilon,\gamma>0
,
t\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
and
K_{n}=\log^{2}n
. Let

\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}:=\left\{\exists I\subset A\text{ s.t. }\max_{e\in I}\min_{f\in A\setminus\{e\}}\tau^{e,f}\leq t,\text{ and }|I|\geq\gamma K_{n}\right\},

where
\tau^{e,f}
is defined as in (
5.4
). Then, for all
a>0
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}\right)\leq n^{-a}\quad\textit{whp}.

Before proving Lemma
5.3
, we show how the latter can be exploited to conclude the proof of Proposition
5.1
.

3.
To conclude the proof of Proposition
5.1
, we argue as follows. Let

(5.8)

\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{|\mathcal{D}^{A}_{t}-p_{t}|>\delta\}

be the event of interest. Consider a system of
2K_{n}
independent random walks starting at the extremes of the vertices in
A
, and let these evolve up to time
t
. Let
I\subset K_{n}
denote the set of edges in
A
interacting within time
t
with other vertices of
A
. Consider the event
\mathcal{E}_{t}
in Lemma
5.3
for which we know that, for every constants
a,\gamma>0
,
whp

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma})\leq n^{-a}.

Therefore

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\mathcal{H}_{t}\right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma})+n^{-a}.

In order to conclude the lemma, it suffices to bound the probability on the right-hand side of the above display.

4.

In order to get the desired bound we next show negative correlation for the discordancy of a collection of edges, in the spirit of the proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Lemma 5.4

(Negative correlation under no-meeting events)
.

Consider a sub(multi)set of edges
A_{k}=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{k}\}
sampled according to
\otimes_{k}\nu
, and let
\mathcal{G}_{t,k}
be the event in which the
2k
independent random walks starting at the extremes of the
k
edges never meet before time
t
. Then, for all
j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,k\}
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{k}\nu}\left(\text{Exactly $j$ of the $k$ edges are discordant at time $t$},\>\mathcal{G}_{t,k}\right)\leq\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left({\rm Bin}(k,p_{t})=j\right).

Proof.

For
e=(x,y)\in A_{k}
, call
\sigma_{e}
a realisation of length
t
of the two independent trajectories starting at
x
and
y
. For
2\leq j\leq k
, call
\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j-1})
the set of the possible realisations of
\sigma_{j}
that never meet the
2(j-1)
trajectories
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{j-1}
. Observe that

\begin{split}&\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{k}\nu}(e_{i}\in D_{t},\forall i\leq j,\>\>e_{i}\not\in D_{t},\forall i>j,\>\mathcal{G}_{t,k})\\
&=\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\{e_{1}\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\{e_{2}\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1})}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{k}\in\{e_{k}\not\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k-1})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\sigma_{i}\mid\cap_{j<i}\sigma_{j}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\{e_{1}\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\{e_{2}\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1})}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{k}\in\{e_{k}\not\in D_{t}\}\cap\mathcal{H}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{k-1})}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{\sigma_{1}\in\{e_{1}\in D_{t}\}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}\in\{e_{2}\in D_{t}\}}\cdots\sum_{\sigma_{k}\in\{e_{k}\not\in D_{t}\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)\\
&=\prod_{i=1}^{j}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{k}\nu}(e_{i}\in D_{t})\prod_{i=j+1}^{k}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{k}\nu}(e_{i}\not\in D_{t})\\
&=\left(p_{t}\right)^{j}\left(1-p_{t}\right)^{k-j},\end{split}

where in the last equality we use the identity in (
5.3
). By the product form of
\otimes_{k}\nu
the result immediately follows.
∎

5.
Recall (
5.8
) and that
I
is the set of edges that are involved in some interaction before time
t
. Under
\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}
, we have
|I|\leq\gamma K_{n}
. Therefore

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}\big)\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{t};\>|I|=w\Big)=\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\sum_{B\subset A\colon\,|B|=w}\frac{1}{\binom{K_{n}}{w}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{t};\>I=B\Big).\end{split}

Setting
k(w)=K_{n}-w
, exchangeability implies

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}\big)&\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{t};A\setminus I={e_{1},\dots,e_{k(w)}}\Big).\end{split}

Observe that the event
\{A\setminus I={e_{1},\dots,e_{k(w)}}\}
implies that the first
k(w)
edges do not interact with any edge in
A

and
that each of the last
w
edges has at least one interaction. This is clearly contained in the event
\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}=\{\min_{i,j\leq k(w)}\tau^{e_{i},e_{j}}>t\}
introduced in Lemma
5.4
. Hence

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}\big)&\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}\Big).\end{split}

Observe that, under the event
\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}
,

\displaystyle\delta K_{n}<\Big|\sum_{e\in A}\mathds{1}_{\{e\in D_{t}\}}-p_{t}K_{n}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}-w}\mathds{1}_{\{e_{i}\in D_{t}\}}-p_{t}|K_{n}-w|\Big|+w+p_{t}w

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{K_{n}-w}\mathds{1}_{\{e_{i}\in D_{t}\}}-p_{t}|K_{n}-w|\Big|+2\gamma K_{n},

which implies, with

\mathcal{R}_{t,k(w)}:=\left\{\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{k(w)}\mathds{1}_{\{e_{i}\in D_{t}\}}-p_{t}k(w)\Big|>(\delta-2\gamma)k(w)\right\},

that

\mathcal{R}_{t,k(w)}\cap\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}\supset\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}.

It follows that

(5.9)

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}\big)&\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}\Big)\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\Big(\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}\cap\mathcal{R}_{t,k(w)}\Big),\end{split}

from which, thanks to Lemma
5.4
, for every
a>0

whp
,

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{H}_{t}\cap\mathcal{E}^{c}_{t,\gamma}\big)&\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\big(\mathcal{G}_{t,k(w)}\cap\mathcal{R}_{t,k(w)}\big)\\
&\leq\sum_{w=0}^{\gamma K_{n}}\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}(\big|{\rm Bin}(K_{n}-w,p_{t})-p_{t}|K_{n}-w|\big|>(\delta-2\gamma)(K_{n}-w))\leq n^{-a}.\end{split}

∎

Proof of Lemma
5.3
.

The proof comes in several steps. For simplicity, we suppress the dependence on
\gamma
from the notation.

1.
For a vertex
x\in[n]
, we call
\mathcal{P}_{x}
the subset of times
[0,t]
in which the random walk starting at
x
moves. Put

\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}=\left\{\forall x\in[n],\>\>|\mathcal{P}_{x}|\leq n^{\varepsilon/2}t\right\}

and note that

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left(\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)\geq 1-n\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}\left({\rm Poisson}(t)>n^{\varepsilon/2}t\right)\geq 1-n^{-a},\qquad\text{for all }a>0,

where the last inequality holds for all
n
large enough. Therefore it is enough to show that, for all
a>0
,

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)\leq n^{-a}\qquad whp.

Clearly,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{I\subset A\colon\,|I|\geq\gamma K_{n}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\max_{e\in I}\min_{f\in A\setminus\{e\}}\tau^{e,f}\leq t,\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C=\gamma K_{n}}^{K_{n}}\binom{K_{n}}{C}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\max_{e\in I}\min_{f\in I\setminus\{e\}}\tau^{e,f}\leq t,\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right),

where in the second line, due to the exchangeability of the edges in
A
and with a slight abuse of notation, we can take for
I
the set of the first
C
edges of
A
. Given
I
, associate to it one possible collection of first interactions between its vertices. Call this set

B_{I}=\{(1,j_{1}),\dots,(C,j_{C})\},

where
(\ell,j_{\ell})
stands for the event that the first edge with which the edge
e_{\ell}
interacts is
e_{j_{\ell}}
.

2.
For every choice of
B_{I}
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{t}(I,B_{I})
the event
\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}
in which the set of interacting edges is
I
and
B_{I}=\{(1,j_{1}),\dots,(C,j_{C})\}
is the set of first interactions. Then

(5.11)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}(I,B_{I})\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{1},e_{j_{1}}}\leq t,\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(1)\right)\prod_{\ell=2}^{C}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right),

where, for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,C\}
, we put

\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell):=\{|\mathcal{P}_{e_{\ell}^{-}}|\leq n^{\varepsilon/2}t\}\cap\{|\mathcal{P}_{e_{\ell}^{+}}|\leq n^{\varepsilon/2}t\}\cap\{|\mathcal{P}_{e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}|\leq n^{\varepsilon/2}t\}\cap\{|\mathcal{P}_{e_{j_{\ell}}^{+}}|\leq n^{\varepsilon/2}t\}

and

\mathcal{F}_{\ell}:=\cap_{\iota\leq\ell}\{\tau^{e_{\iota},e_{j_{\iota}}}\leq t\}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\iota),\qquad\ell=\{2,\dots,C\}.

By construction, regardless of the specific ordering of the elements in the set
B_{I}
, there is a collection of at least
C/2
indices
\ell\in\{1,\dots,C\}
such that

|\Xi_{\ell}|:=\big|\{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}\}\cap\{e_{1},e_{j_{1}},\dots,e_{{\ell-1}},e_{j_{\ell-1}}\}\big|\leq 1.

We claim that, for every
\ell\in\{1,\dots,C\}
for which
|\Xi_{\ell}|=0
,

(5.12)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\nu\otimes\nu}\left(\tau^{e,f}\leq t\right)+\frac{4C}{n}=O\left(\frac{C+t}{n}\right).

Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{\ell}^{+},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{+}\}\cap\cup_{\iota<\ell}\{e_{\iota}^{-},e_{\iota}^{+},e_{j_{\iota}}^{-},e_{j_{\iota}}^{+}\}=\emptyset\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)+\frac{4\ell}{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{i_{\ell}},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{\ell}^{+},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{+}\}\cap\cup_{\iota<\ell}\{e_{\iota}^{-},e_{\iota}^{+},e_{j_{\iota}}^{-},e_{j_{\iota}}^{+}\}=\emptyset\right)+\frac{4\ell}{n}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{i_{\ell}},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t\right)+\frac{4C}{n}.

Therefore, by (
5.5
), we conclude that, under
|\Xi_{\ell}|=0
,

(5.13)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)\leq 4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\pi\otimes\pi}(\tau_{\rm meet}^{x,y}\leq t)+\frac{4C}{n},

and (
5.12
) follows by inserting (
5.6
) into (
5.13
).

3.

Assume now that for some
\ell\in\{1,\dots,C\}
we have
|\Xi_{\ell}|=1
. More precisely, under
\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\cap\left\{|\Xi_{\ell}|=1\right\}
, one among
e_{\ell}
and
e_{j_{\ell}}
is still unsampled at step
\ell
. Without loss of generality, assume that
e_{\ell}
is such an unsampled random edge. By the same argument used to derive (
5.13
), and putting

\mathcal{J}_{\ell}:=\left\{\{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{\ell}^{+}\}\bigcap\cup_{\iota<\ell}\{e_{\iota}^{-},e_{\iota}^{+},e_{j_{\iota}}^{-},e_{j_{\iota}}^{+}\}=\emptyset\right\},

we bound

\begin{split}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)&\leq 2\>\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\mathcal{J}_{\ell}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)+\frac{8C}{n}.\end{split}

Arguing as in
(
Oliveira, 2013
, Proposition 4.5)
, we get that, under
|\Xi_{\ell}|=1
with
e_{\ell}
unsampled at step
\ell
,

(5.14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\mathcal{J}_{\ell}\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{\ell},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{+},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{+}}\wedge\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{+},e_{j_{\ell}}^{+}}\leq t\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\leq t\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\right)

\displaystyle=4\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}E}}\left[\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\leq t\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{\ell}^{-}},\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\right)\bigg\rvert\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell)\right],

where the second inequality follows from the equality of the marginals. Note that, conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\ell}
, for all
s\leq t
the position of the random walk starting at
e_{\ell}^{-}
is distributed according to
\pi
. On the other hand, the position of the random walk starting at
e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}
at time
s
is distributed in a non-trivial way. Moreover, the positions of the two random walks at time
s
are independent. Hence

(5.15)

\begin{split}&\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau_{\rm meet}^{e_{\ell}^{-},e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\leq t\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{\ell}^{-}},\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{j_{\ell}}}}\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(X^{(e_{\ell}^{-})}_{s}=X_{s}^{(e_{j_{\ell}}^{-})}\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>\mathcal{J}_{\ell},\>\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{\ell}^{-}},\>\mathcal{P}_{e_{j_{\ell}}^{-}}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{j_{\ell}}}}\max_{\lambda}\sum_{x\in[n]}\lambda(x)\pi(x)\leq\frac{|\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{e^{-}_{j_{\ell}}}|}{n}.\end{split}

Inserting (
5.15
) into the expectation in (
5.14
), we obtain

(5.16)

\begin{split}&\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\tau^{e_{i_{\ell}},e_{j_{\ell}}}\leq t,\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}(\ell),\mathcal{J}_{\ell}\>\big\rvert\>\mathcal{F}_{\ell-1},\>|\Xi_{\ell}|=1\right)\leq\frac{8n^{\varepsilon/2}t}{n}.\end{split}

Inserting (

)–(
5.16

), we obtain

(5.17)

\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{C}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}(I,B_{I})\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)\leq\left(\frac{8(n^{\varepsilon/2}t+C)}{n}\right)^{C/2}\leq\left(\sqrt{\frac{8(n^{\varepsilon/2}t+K)}{n}}\right)^{C}\eqqcolon\Gamma^{C}.

4.

By (

), (

(C-1)^{C}
different ways to select the set

, we are now ready to conclude that
whp

\displaystyle\mathbf{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}P}}_{\otimes_{K_{n}}\nu}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t,\gamma}\cap\mathcal{P}^{\rm tot}\right)

Appendix A
Auxilliary facts for càdlàg processes

In the next lemma, for an arbitrary
T>0
, we let
[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]
denote the product of
[0,T]
with the càdlàg space
\mathcal{D}[0,T]
endowed with the
J_{1}
-Skorohod topology.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider the function
h\colon\,[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
defined via
h(s,\phi)=\phi(s)
. Every point in
[0,T]\times\mathcal{C}[0,T]
is a continuity point of
h
.

Proof.

Assume that
(s_{n},\phi_{n})
is a sequence that converges to
(s,\phi)
, with
\phi\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
. We need to verify that
h(s_{n},\phi_{n})
converges to
h(s,\phi)
. Indeed, if
\phi_{n}
converges to
\phi
, then there exists a sequence of increasing functions
\lambda_{n}:[0,T]\to[0,T]
with
\lambda_{n}(0)=0
and
\lambda_{n}(T)=T
such that
\lambda_{n}(u)\to u
and
\phi_{n}(u)-\phi(\lambda_{n}(u))\to 0
uniformly over
u\in[0,T]
. From this we obtain

||\phi_{n}-\phi||_{\infty}\leq||\phi_{n}-\phi\circ\lambda_{n}||_{\infty}+||\phi\circ\lambda_{n}-\phi||_{\infty}\to 0,

since any function in
\mathcal{C}[0,T]
is uniformly continuous. In particular,
\phi_{n}
converges to
\phi
uniformly, which readily implies that
\phi_{n}(s_{n})\to\phi(s)
and concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Assume that
(X^{n}_{u})_{u\in[0,T]}
is a sequence of càdlàg processes that converge in distribution to a process
(X_{u})_{u\in[0,T]}
that has almost surely continuous trajectories. For any sequence
t_{n}\to t\in[0,T]
,
X^{n}_{t_{n}}
converges in distribution to
X_{t}
.

Proof.

From the assumptions of the proposition, it follows that
((X^{n}_{u})_{u\in[0,T]},t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in distribution to
((X_{u})_{u\in[0,T]},t)
. Let
h\colon\,[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
as in Lemma
A.1
. Note that, since
(X_{u})_{u\in[0,T]}
is almost surely continuous, it follows that
X^{n}_{t_{n}}=h(t_{n},(X^{n}_{u})_{u\in[0,T]})
converges in distribution to
X_{t}=h(t,(X_{u})_{u\in[0,T]})
.
∎
