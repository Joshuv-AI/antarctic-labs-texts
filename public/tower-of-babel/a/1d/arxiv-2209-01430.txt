A Variational Approach to the Quantum Separability Problem
Mirko Consiglio, Tony J. G. Apollaro, Marcin Wieśniak

Abstract
We present the VSV ( VSV ), which is a novel VQA ( VQA ) that determines the CSS ( CSS ) of an arbitrary quantum state with respect to the HSD ( HSD ). We first assess the performance of the VSV by investigating the convergence of the optimization procedure for GHZ ( GHZ ) states of up to seven qubits, using both statevector and shot-based simulations. We also numerically determine the CSS of X-MEMS ( X-MEMS ), and subsequently use the results of the algorithm to surmise the analytical form of the aforementioned CSS . Our results indicate that current NISQ ( NISQ ) devices may be useful in addressing the N ​ P NP -hard full separability problem using the VSV , due to the shallow quantum circuit imposed by employing the destructive SWAP test to evaluate the HSD . The VSV may also possibly lead to the characterization of multipartite quantum states, once the algorithm is adapted and improved to obtain the k-CSS ( k-CSS ) of a multipartite entangled state.

I
Introduction

Entanglement is the principal defining feature of quantum mechanics
Einstein et al. 1935
, and a quantitative description of the phenomenon started with Bell’s inequalities
Bell 1964
. Different measures of entanglement quantify distinct resources, although most require the fulfillment of specific conditions to be considered “good” entanglement measures. These conditions can be defined as a set of axioms, such as the nullification of the measure for separable states, invariance under local unitary operations, and monotonicity under
LOCC
(
LOCC
)
Vedral et al. 1997
. To this end, many entanglement measures have been defined, such as distillable entanglement, entanglement cost, and the entanglement of formation
Plenio and Virmani 2007
;
Eltschka and Siewert 2014
. The difficulty in calculating these measures for generic states lies in their non-closed form, given that they generally include the computation of a minimum over a large Hilbert space, which is typically intractable as the dimension of the system scales. On the other hand, the problem of measuring entanglement is more approachable for pure states, given that even for different entanglement classes, that is under
SLOCC
(
SLOCC
)
Eltschka and Siewert 2014
, many different measures of entanglement can be computed.

The simplest system that has been completely characterized is a two-qubit one. An interesting property for two qubits is that only two
SLOCC
classes exist, one which contains the only type of entanglement possible, i.e. bipartite entanglement, and the other consists of fully separable states, i.e. non-entangled states. The relevant literature for three- and four-qubit pure states, as well as the general multipartite case can be found in Refs.
Coffman et al. 2000
;
Cunha et al. 2019
;
Gühne and Seevinck 2010

We can also relax our specificity of requiring an exact quantifier of entanglement, and focus on whether a particular state is certified to be entangled. This is known as the quantum separability problem, and is in fact deemed to be

II
The Hilbert–Schmidt Distance as a Measure of Entanglement

The
HSD
between two quantum states
D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)
, is defined as

D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right)\equiv\Tr{\left( \rho- \sigma\right)^2},

(1)

which is a non-monotonic Riemannian metric
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
. Using this definition, an entanglement measure can be induced by the
HSD

Witte and Trucks 1999
;
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
, denoted as the
HSE
,

E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)\equiv\underset{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}{\min}~D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right),

(2)

with the
CSS
defined as

\rho_{\text{CSS}}\equiv\underset{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}{\arg\min}~D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma\right).

(3)

where
\mathcal{C}
is the convex set of states for some degree
k
of separability. To define it concretely, a state
\rho
is said to be
k
-separable if it can be written as a convex sum of
k
-separable states, that is

\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{\Psi_i^k}{\Psi_i^k},

(4)

where a
k
-separable pure state can be written as a tensor product of
k
-local states

\ket{\Psi_i^k}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}\ket{\psi_j},

(5)

such that
\ket{\psi_j}
are
k
-separable states on subsets of the
n
-qubit parties
Shang and Gühne 2018
. More specifically, states are called biseparable for
k=2
, triseparable for
k=3
, up to fully separable for
k=n
. Although the
k-CSS
(
3
) (and corresponding
HSE
(
2
)) can be distinctly defined for all convex sets of
k
-separable states, as stated in Sec.
I
, we designed the
VSV
to find the
CSS
(and corresponding
HSE
) of entangled states with respect to the set of
fully
separable states.

It was proven in Ref.
Ozawa 2000
that the
HSD
is not non-increasing under
CPTP
(
CPTP
) maps, posing a principal question of whether the
HSE
is a good entanglement measure. Nevertheless, the
HSD
, and the corresponding
HSE
, are still useful quantities to investigate, since they are utilized in generalized Bell inequalities
Bertlmann et al. 2002
;
Silva and Franco 2022
, while also providing insight into the geometry of entangled states
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
;
Streltsov et al. 2010
. The
HSD
is also relatively straightforward to evaluate on a quantum device, since it can be decomposed as

\Tr{\left( \rho- \sigma\right)^2}=\Tr{\rho^2}+\Tr{\sigma^2}-2\Tr{\rho\sigma},

(6)

coupled with using quantum primitives such as the destructive
SWAP
test to measure the overlap and purities of
\rho
and
\sigma
.

On the other hand, while it was shown in Ref.
Streltsov et al. 2010
that distance measures, such as the Bures measure of entanglement
Bengtsson and Zyczkowski 2006
, are directly related to the fidelity
Uhlmann 1976
of a
CSS
, the fidelity is a harder quantity to evaluate on both a classical and quantum device
Witte and Trucks 1999
;
Brun 2004
;
Garcia-Escartin and Chamorro-Posada 2013
;
Bartkiewicz et al. 2013
;
Cincio et al. 2018
;
Cerezo et al. 2020
when compared with the simplicity of the
HSD
.

It is also interesting to mention that the
HSD
is being used for tackling the quantum low-rank approximation problem
Ezzell et al. 2022a
, with applications in principal component analysis and preparing arbitrary mixed states on quantum computers
Ezzell et al. 2022b
.

\rho
as being the test state, and
\sigma
as our trial state(s), which are the states that iteratively approach the

\sigma(\bm{p},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}\outerproduct{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)},

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}p_{1}\\
p_{2}\\
\vdots\\
p_{s}\\
\end{array}\right),~\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}=1,~p_{i}\in[0,1],

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\theta}_{1}\\
\bm{\theta}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\theta}_{s}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\theta_{11}&\theta_{12}&\cdots&\theta_{1n}\\
\theta_{21}&\theta_{22}&\cdots&\theta_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{s1}&\theta_{s2}&\cdots&\theta_{sn}\\
\end{array}\right),~\theta_{ij}\in[0,2\pi),

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\bm{\phi}_{1}\\
\bm{\phi}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\bm{\phi}_{s}\\
\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\phi_{11}&\phi_{12}&\cdots&\phi_{1n}\\
\phi_{21}&\phi_{22}&\cdots&\phi_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_{s1}&\phi_{s2}&\cdots&\phi_{sn}\\
\end{array}\right),~\phi_{ij}\in[0,2\pi),

totaling
s(2n+1)
parameters, where
n
is the number of qubits and

is the number of separable pure states needed to generate the trial state. Due to Carathéodory’s theorem, we require

;
Vedral and Plenio 1998
;
Streltsov et al. 2010

is the dimension of the Hilbert space. However, in our simulations we found that
s=d
suffices to find the
CSS
of all our test states.
\left\{p_{i},\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}\right\}
represent the ensemble of separable pure states, which can be decomposed as

\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\left(\cos\left(\theta_{ij}\right)\ket{0_j}+e^{\imath\phi_{ij}}\sin\left(\theta_{ij}\right)\ket{1_j}\right).

(28)

\ket{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}U(\theta_{ij},\phi_{ij})\ket{0}_{j},

(29)

where

U(\theta_{ij},\phi_{ij})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\cos\left(\theta_{ij}\right)&-\sin\left(\theta_{ij}\right)\\[4.30554pt]
e^{\imath\phi_{ij}}\sin\left(\theta_{ij}\right)&e^{\imath\phi_{ij}}\cos\left(\theta_{ij}\right)\end{array}\right),

(30)

which is equivalent to applying an
R_{y}(\theta_{ij})

gate.

III.1
Measuring the Hilbert–Schmidt Distance

Preparing a mixed state on a quantum computer, requires, in general, twice as many qubits when compared to the size of the state
Benenti and Strini 2009
. This, coupled with the issue of preparing an arbitrary separable state, leads us to find an alternative method for computing the overlap of the trial states with the test state. Our solution is to individually supply separable (non-orthogonal) pure states of our separable mixed state on the quantum computer, evaluate the individual overlaps using the destructive
SWAP
test, and then classically mix them during the optimization procedure. The benefit of this method is that we do not require the direct preparation of the
CSS
on the quantum computer, as we are only interested in the computation of the
HSD
.

Suppose, we can decompose the trial state
\sigma
as in Eq. (
7
), then we can evaluate the overlap between
\rho
and
\sigma
as

\displaystyle\Tr{\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=\Tr{\rho\sum_{i=1}^s p_i \ketbra{\psi_i}{\psi_i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}\Tr{\rho\ketbra{\psi_i}{\psi_i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}\expectationvalue{\rho}{\psi_i}.

(31)

If we want to compute the purity of
\sigma
, then

\displaystyle\Tr{\sigma^2}

\displaystyle=\Tr{\sum_{i=1}^s p_i \ketbra{\psi_i}{\psi_i} \sum_{j=1}^s p_j
\ketbra{\psi_j}{\psi_j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}p_{i}p_{j}\Tr{\ket{\psi_i}\braket{\psi_i}{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{s}p_{i}p_{j}|\innerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i\neq j}^{s}p_{i}p_{j}|\innerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_j}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}^{2}+2\sum_{i<j}^{s}p_{i}p_{j}|\innerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_j}|^{2}.

(32)

The purity of
\rho
is trivially obtained assuming we can prepare it directly on a quantum computer; given that it is our test state.

III.2
Measuring Overlaps on a Quantum Computer

Given Eqs (
31
) and (
32
), we require subroutines in the
VSV
capable of measuring the purity and overlap of quantum states. Specifically, one can utilize the destructive
SWAP
test
Garcia-Escartin and Chamorro-Posada 2013
;
Cincio et al. 2018
to calculate these quantities. The concept of this procedure stems from the fact that measuring in the Bell basis determines the amount of correlations present between two systems, and it can be shown that it is equivalent to the non-destructive
SWAP
test
Buhrman et al. 2001
. Fig.
1
shows the quantum circuit for performing the destructive
SWAP
test.

Figure 1:
Destructive
SWAP
test for determining the overlap between two quantum states. The bundled
CNOT
gate here actually represents
CNOT
gates between each pair of qubits of
\rho
and
\sigma
, with the bundled Hadamard gate equating to performing a Hadamard gate on each qubit of
\rho
. This effectively reduces to a Bell basis transformation for each pair of qubits of
\rho
and
\sigma
.

By placing a
CNOT
gate followed by a Hadamard gate on the control qubit, each pair of qubits of
\rho
and
\sigma
are measured in the Bell basis. Following this, each pair of measurement results are post-processed using the vector
\vec{c}=(1,1,1,-1)
, which corresponds to summing the probabilities of getting 00, 01 and 10, and subtracting the probability of getting 11. This is equivalent to measuring the expectation value of a
CZ
operator, resulting in obtaining the overlap
\Tr{\rho\sigma}
, as long as the qubits are rearranged as
R_{1}S_{1}R_{2}S_{2}\dots R_{n}S_{n}
, where
R_{i}
and
S_{i}
denote the subsystems of
\rho
and
\sigma
, respectively. This results in a linear scaling in post-processing, given that we do not directly compute
\vec{c}\cdot\vec{p}
, where
\vec{p}
is the probability vector, but rather binning the paired measurement outcomes into a
+1
and
-1
bin, and then averaging the results
Cincio et al. 2018
. The purity of a state can be similarly obtained by supplying two copies of the state as inputs to the test. The one-qubit gates necessary to generate the separable pure states of Eq. (
29
), coupled with the two-depth circuit needed for the destructive
SWAP
test, results in a noticeably shallow circuit for the
VSV
.

III.3
Variational Optimization

Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
) give us the means to compute the
HSD
as in Eq. (
6
), and by combining these with Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), we can devise a
VQA
that is able to compute the
CSS
and the corresponding
HSE
.

The optimizer in this scenario is tasked with providing angles
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\phi}
to prepare the separable pure states, and probabilities
\bm{p}
to classically mix them during post-processing, to generate a separable state
\sigma(\bm{p},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})
. One can notice that the quantum computer is only tasked with computing
\Tr{\rho^2}
,
\expectationvalue{\rho}{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}
, and
|\innerproduct{\psi(\bm{\theta}_i, \bm{\phi}_i)}{\psi(\bm{\theta}_j, \bm{\phi}_j)}|^{2}

\forall~i,j\in[s]
. The probabilities
\bm{p}
are only incorporated classically when computing the final results for calculating the purity
\Tr{\sigma(\bm{p}, \bm{\theta}, \bm{\phi})^2}
, and the overlap
\Tr{\rho\sigma(\bm{p}, \bm{\theta}, \bm{\phi})}
. As a result, we split our optimization routine into a bilevel system, which is essentially a nested optimization routine
Dempe 2002
.

The structure of the bilevel system is as follows: at the beginning of every iteration, an upper level optimizer selects the parameters
\bm{\theta}
and
\bm{\phi}
, and proceeds to call the quantum computer to compute the overlaps in Eqs. (
31
) and (
32
). The upper level optimizer then launches a lower level optimizer to obtain the parameters
\bm{p}
to minimize Eq. (
6
). We thus obtain (near-)optimal parameters of the minimization of the cost function (
1
),

\{\bm{p^{*}},\bm{\theta^{*}},\bm{\phi^{*}}\}\approx\underset{\bm{p},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi}}{\arg\min}~D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma(\bm{p},\bm{\theta},\bm{\phi})\right),

(33)

such that the
CSS
of
\rho
would then be

\rho_{\text{CSS}}\approx\sigma(\bm{p^{*}},\bm{\theta^{*}},\bm{\phi^{*}}),

(34)

from which we can calculate the
HSE
of
\rho
as

E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)\approx D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma(\bm{p^{*}},\bm{\theta^{*}},\bm{\phi^{*}})\right).

(35)

VSV
algorithm is shown in Fig.

III.4
Complexity Analysis

The scalability of the
VSV
, similar to other hybrid quantum–classical algorithms, is characterized from both the classical and quantum point of view. The algorithm prepares the test state
\rho
(which may be unknown, but reproducible) and trial states
\sigma
on a quantum computer, which provides an exponential memory reduction when compared with purely classical algorithms. We also assume
\rho
can be efficiently prepared on a quantum device: either via direct input, for example, from another quantum system; or else using quantum state preparation methods
Araujo et al. 2021
. Aside from preparing
\rho
, the algorithm requires
n

CNOT
gates and
n
Hadamard gates for carrying out the destructive
SWAP
test, which scales linearly with the number of qubits, and uses a constant circuit depth of two, assuming the quantum computer has a ladder-like connectivity.

In the current implementation of the
VSV
, the number of parameters scales as
s(2n+1)
for determining the closest
fully
separable state of
\rho
, where
s
is the number of (non-orthogonal) components representing the trial state
\sigma
. Unless
\rho
is separable, we found that the
CSS
of
\rho
being full rank. Thus, we require at least
s\geq d
, although we found that in all of our simulations
s=d
suffices to find the
CSS
of an entangled state. This implies that we have an exponential scaling in the number of parameters needed to determine the
CSS
via this method. This may be alleviated if one finds a way to directly prepare the
CSS
as a separable mixed state on the quantum device, which will (generally) require doubling the number of qubits
Benenti and Strini 2009
. On the other hand, the number of parameters necessary to define the
CSS
may be reduced such that it scales sub-exponentially with the number of qubits. It should also be noted that finding the
k-CSS
would require more complex ansätze, which are more likely to require a larger set of parameters to represent
k
-separable states.

Similar to

(which are also
X
-states), the

-state itself is robust against local damping channels

;
Hashemi Rafsanjani et al. 2012
. This is due to the fact that decoherence effects that introduce additional non-
X

. As a consequence, the

In the case of two-qubit states, the
HSE
and the concurrence is known to be equal for a restricted set of states

;
Ganardi et al. 2022
. With our

, we have numerical evidence supporting the conjecture that the
HSE
of two distinct, arbitrary, two-qubit states,
\rho
and

\rho
and
\sigma
is also equal, that is

C(\rho)=C(\sigma)\iff E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)=E_{\text{HS}}\left(\sigma\right),

(37)

and similarly, that the
HSE
of
\rho
is greater than that of
\sigma
if, and only if, the concurrence of
\rho
is also greater than that of
\sigma
, that is

C(\rho)>C(\sigma)\iff E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)>E_{\text{HS}}\left(\sigma\right).

(38)

Following this, we tested whether similar statements hold when comparing
GME
concurrence and
HSE
for
(n\geq 3)
-qubit
X
-states. All of the simulations we carried out pointed towards the null hypothesis in both cases, meaning that equality does not hold:

C_{\text{GME}}(\rho)=C_{\text{GME}}(\sigma)\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)=E_{\text{HS}}\left(\sigma\right),

(39)

nor does ordered inequality:

C_{\text{GME}}(\rho)>C_{\text{GME}}(\sigma)\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\displaystyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 5.0pt\kern-5.27776pt$\textstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.98611pt\kern-4.45831pt$\scriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\kern 3.40282pt\kern-3.95834pt$\scriptscriptstyle\not$\hss}{\iff}}}E_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho\right)>E_{\text{HS}}\left(\sigma\right).

(40)

This is not surprising, since the
GME
concurrence characterizes
n
-qubit entanglement. On the other hand, the
HSE
seems to describe the departure of a state from a fully separable one, where in the case of three-qubits, may also consist of biseparable states which are not detected by
GME
concurrence.

V
Conclusion

The
VSV
is a novel
VQA
that finds the
CSS
of arbitrary quantum states, with respect to the
HSD
, and obtain the
HSE
. The
VSV
has been applied to
n
-qubit
GHZ
states, showing the convergence of the optimizer. It has also been applied to investigate
X-MEMS
, producing a relation between the
GME
concurrence and
HSE
, as well as helping deduce the analytical form of the
CSS
for
n
-qubit
X-MEMS
. In this respect, the
VSV
can be useful for shedding light on possible analytical forms of
CSS
for arbitrary states. Further simulations carried out on two-qubit states demonstrated that while equality (or ordering) of the concurrence of two states is present if, and only if, equality (or ordering) of the
HSE
is also present, this does not hold in general for the
GME
concurrence and
HSE
in the case of
X
-states with three or more qubits.

If the calculated
HSE
of an entangled state is equal to zero, then we trivially acquire
\rho_{\text{CSS}}=\rho
, which immediately implies that our test state
\rho
is fully separable, and hence, is not entangled. In either case, if one knows the
CSS
, then an entanglement witness
Horodecki et al. 1996
;
Doherty et al. 2004
;
Gühne and Tóth 2009
can be defined as
\mathcal{W}\equiv\rho-\rho_{\text{CSS}}
. In general however, one does not exactly acquire the
CSS
, but rather a close approximation to it, say,
\sigma
. In this case, the optimal entanglement witness would be of the form

\mathcal{W}\equiv\rho-\sigma-\max_{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{C}}\expectationvalue{(\rho- \sigma)}{\psi}

Pandya et al. 2020
.

The strength of the
VSV
as a potential
NISQ
algorithm stems from its simplicity. While the algorithm requires in general many calls to the quantum computer for evaluating overlaps, the destructive
SWAP
test, coupled with the ease of preparing separable states through one-qubit gates, results in a three-depth circuit which is remarkably tractable on a
NISQ
device. It should be noted that the entire trial state(s) can be directly prepared on the quantum computer by utilizing (up to
n
) ancillary qubits, which are then traced out after performing the relevant unitary gates
Benenti and Strini 2009
, leading to less overlap computations for the cost of more qubits, as discussed in Sec.
III
. It is also noteworthy to mention that the evaluation of state overlaps could be improved by looking towards alternative methods, such as in Refs.
Flammia and Liu 2011
;
Elben et al. 2019
;
Elben et al. 2020
;
Fanizza et al. 2020
;
Liu and Yin 2022
.

Future work would involve enhancing the
VSV
to find the
k-CSS
of an arbitrary quantum state, which is not straightforward, since even the closest biseparable state for three-qubit states is significantly challenging to implement. The issue lies in properly expressing a biseparable state in a variational form, since the number of pure state bipartitions scales as
2^{n-1}-1
for an
n
-qubit state. The set of all biseparable states is generated by the convex hull of all pure state bipartitions. This, coupled with the fact that an
n
-qubit state can have rank up to
2^{n}
makes the extension of the
VSV
to determine the
k-CSS
extremely non-trivial. The question of finding the
k-CSS
, along with improvements to the performance of the
VSV
, are left as open problems.

Apart from the
VSV
, there are alternative approaches that aim to find the
CSS
of arbitrary states using other methods, such as neural networks
Girardin et al. 2022
and an adaptive polytope approximation
Ohst et al. 2022
. However, work that inspired the variational approach to the problem at hand is the so-called
QGA

Brierley et al. 2016
;
Shang and Gühne 2018
;
Wieśniak et al. 2020
;
Pandya et al. 2020
. The implementation of the
QGA
on a quantum device and related discussion can be found in Appendix
B
.

The
Python
code for running the
VSV
simulations, using the package
Qulacs

Suzuki et al. 2021
, and the
Mathematica
code for determining the
CSS
of
X-MEMS
, can be found on
GitHub

Consiglio 2022
.

Appendix B
Comparison with the Quantum Gilbert Algorithm

The
QGA
is the quantum analogue of Gilbert’s algorithm
Gilbert 1966
, which is utilized to determine the distance between a given point and a convex set, according to the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. The
QGA
has insofar only been implemented on classical computers to test for membership of states in different
SLOCC
classes
Brierley et al. 2016
;
Shang and Gühne 2018
;
Wieśniak et al. 2020
;
Pandya et al. 2020
.

It is only natural to attempt to extend the
QGA
to operate on a quantum computer, owing to many reasons. The first of which is the storage of quantum mixed state data, which is known to increase exponentially as
4^{n}
, with respect to the number of qubits
n
. The second is the input of arbitrary mixed states into a quantum computer, created via open quantum systems as an example, where these reasons also apply to the
VQA
counterpart. The issue in simply extending the
QGA
to be utilized on a quantum computer, in order to improve the performance over its classical counterpart, is due to the nature of the algorithm itself, requiring possibly, an infinite number of state mixtures to the trial state. The
QGA
relies on computing overlaps of different trial states, similar to the
VSV
, however, the main difference is that the
VSV
keeps a constant number of states in memory. On the other hand, the
QGA
, at each successful iteration of the algorithm adds one state to memory, increasing the number of future overlap calculations. This means that more calls to the quantum computer are required for subsequent iterations of the algorithm. In general if we suppose we require one million trial states for a three-qubit state to acquire a reasonable
CSS
, which is exactly the amount of states taken by the
QGA
, as given in Ref.
Pandya et al. 2020
, and we assume that the number of successes,
c_{s}
, is proportional to the number of trial states,
c_{t}
, by
c_{s}\propto c_{t}^{0.44127}
, then the number of overlaps is equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{10^{6}}\sum_{j=1}^{c_{s}^{(i)}}j=\sum_{i=1}^{10^{6}}\frac{c_{s}^{(i)}\left(c_{s}^{(i)}+1\right)}{2},

(55)

where
c_{s}^{(i)}
is the number of successes at iteration
i
, which starts at
1
for
i=1
, and increases by
1
every
\frac{c_{t}}{c_{s}}\approx\frac{c_{t}}{c_{t}^{0.44127}}=c_{t}^{0.55873}
times, which for one million trial states is approximately every
2251
trial states. However, in the beginning, almost every trial state will correspond to a success, due to the initial state on average lying farther away from the actual
CSS
. As the number of successes increases, and the state moves closer to the border of the set of separable states, nearer to the
CSS
, it will be harder to find a closer separable state. But, even if we take it to be split evenly, then this results to be around
3.3\times 10^{10}
calls to the quantum computer. On the other hand, the
VSV
calls the quantum computer
5\times 10^{3}
times to obtain a
CSS
comparable to the
QGA
, which would equate to more than
6\times 10^{6}
calls for the
QGA
for each call of the
VSV
.

In either case, we shall describe the direct implementation of the
QGA
on a quantum device. Given an initial guess in the form of a pure product state
\sigma_{0}=\outerproduct{\psi_0}{\psi_0}
, after the
n^{\text{th}}
success, the new
CSS

\rho_{n}
to the test state
\rho
is given iteratively as

\rho_{n}=p_{n}\rho_{n-1}+(1-p_{n})\sigma_{n},

(56)

which equates to

\rho_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)\sigma_{i},

(57)

where
\sigma_{i}=\outerproduct{\psi_i}{\psi_i}
is a pure product state and
p_{i}\in(0,1)
, such that

\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)=1.

(58)

Note that this definition entails that
\rho_{0}\equiv\sigma_{0}
. The implementation of a
QGA
can be thus be described as follows
Pandya et al. 2020
:

1.

Input data: the state to be tested
\rho
and any pure product state
\sigma_{0}
.

2.

Output data: the closest state found after
n
successes
\rho_{n}
, and lists of values of
D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{i}\right)=\Tr{(\rho- \rho_i)^2}
,
p_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
.

0.

Calculate the value of
D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sigma_{0}\right)\equiv D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{0}\right)
and add it to the list.

1.

Increase the counter of trials
c_{t}
by 1. Draw a random pure product state
\sigma_{1}
, hereafter called the first trial state.

2.

Run a preselection for the trial state by checking a value of a linear functional. If it fails, go back to step 1.

3.

In the case of successful preselection, find the minimum of
\Tr{(\rho- \rho_n)^2}
with respect to
p_{n}
, which in the case of the first trial state is
\Tr{(\rho- p_1\rho_0 - (1 - p_1)\sigma_1)^2}
with respect to
p_{1}
.

4.

If the minimum occurs for
p_{1}\in[0,1]
, then
\rho_{1}=p_{1}\rho_{0}+(1-p_{1})\sigma_{1}
is saved to the list as the new
CSS
, along with the value of
D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{1}\right)
,
p_{1}
and
\sigma_{1}
, increase the success
c_{s}
counter value by 1.

5.

Go to step 1., now drawing a new random pure product state
\sigma_{2}
, such that after a successful preselection, one determines a new
p_{2}
, and consequently
\rho_{2}
, and then saves
D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{2}\right)
,
p_{2}
and
\sigma_{2}
. Continue in this fashion until a specified HALT criterion is met.

and

Pandya et al. 2020
, ensuring that a state between
\rho_{n-1}
and

exists such that it is closer to

. Eq. (
59
) can rewritten and computed as

\Tr{\rho_{n-1}^2}+\Tr{\rho\sigma_n}>\Tr{\rho\rho_{n-1}}+\Tr{\rho_{n-1}\sigma_n}.

(60)

The purity of each new
CSS
when calculated iteratively (starting at 1 due to the initial trial state
\sigma_{0}
being a pure state), is given by

\displaystyle\Tr{\rho_n^2}

\displaystyle=\Tr{(p_n\rho_{n-1} + (1 - p_n)\sigma_n)^2}

\displaystyle=p_{n}^{2}\Tr{\rho_{n-1}^2}+(1-p_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+2p(1-p_{n})\Tr{\rho_{n-1}\sigma_n},

(61)

while the overlap is calculated as

\Tr{\rho\rho_n}=p_{n}\Tr{\rho\rho_{n-1}}+(1-p_{n})\Tr{\rho\sigma_n}.

(62)

Thus, the cost function for the
n^{\text{th}}
iteration is defined as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr{(\rho- p_n\rho_{n-1} - (1 - p_n)\sigma_n)^2}

\displaystyle=\Tr{\rho^2}+p_{n}^{2}\Tr{\rho_{n-1}^2}+(1-p_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+2p_{n}(1-p_{n})\Tr{\rho_{n-1}\sigma_n}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-2p_{n}\Tr{\rho\rho_{n-1}}-2(1-p_{n})\Tr{\rho\sigma_n},

(63)

which is explicitly written and computed as

\displaystyle D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\rho_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\text{HS}}\left(\rho,\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)\sigma_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr{\left(\rho- \sum_{i=0}^{n} \left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n} p_j\right) \left(1-p_i\right)\sigma_i \right)^2}

\displaystyle=\Tr{\rho^2}+\Tr{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} \left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n} p_j\right) \left(1-p_i\right)\sigma_i \right)^2}-2\Tr{\rho\sum_{i=0}^{n} \left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n} p_j\right) \left(1-p_i\right)\sigma_i}

\displaystyle=\Tr{\rho^2}+2\sum_{i<k}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}\right)\left(\prod_{l=k+1}^{n}p_{l}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)\left(1-p_{k}\right)\Tr{\sigma_i\sigma_k}\ +

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}^{2}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)^{2}-2\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}p_{j}\right)\left(1-p_{i}\right)\Tr{\rho\sigma_i}.

(64)

This entails that the overlap between every new trial state
\sigma_{n}
and every previous trial state
\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{n-1}
, as well as with the test state
\rho
, must be calculated at every iteration. This implies that the parameters of each separable pure trial state must be saved at each success, resulting in an ever-increasing memory and quantum device utilization.
