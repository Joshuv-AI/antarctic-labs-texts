Intersection problems and a correlation inequality for integer sequences
Peter Frankl, Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
. Let us consider a collection 𝒢 {\mathcal{G}} of codewords of length n n over an alphabet of size s s . Let t 1 , … , t s t_{1},\ldots,t_{s} be nonnegative integers. What is the maximum of | 𝒢 | |{\mathcal{G}}| subject to the condition that any two codewords should have at least t i t_{i} positions where both have letter i i ( 1 ≤ i ≤ s 1\leq i\leq s ). In the case s = 2 s=2 it is a longstanding open question. Quite surprisingly we obtain an almost complete answer for s ≥ 3 s\geq 3 . The main tool is a correlation inequality.



For an integer
s\geq 2
let
[s]=\{1,2,\ldots,s\}
be the standard
s
-set. Let
[s]^{n}
denote the collection of all
s^{n}
integer sequences
\vec{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
with
a_{i}\in[s]
. Calling
[s]
the alphabet and each
\vec{a}
a codeword,
[s]^{n}
is the central object in
coding theory
(cf., e.g.,
[
15
]
).

Another way to look at these sequences is to consider
n
pairwise disjoint sets
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
and consider the complete
n
-partite
n
-graph
Y_{1}\times\ldots\times Y_{n}
consisting of all vectors
\vec{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
,
y_{i}\in Y_{i}
. If
|Y_{1}|=\ldots=|Y_{n}|=s
, it is essentially the same combinatorial object.

For two vectors
\vec{y}
and
\vec{z}
let us define their
intersection
(
meet
)
\vec{y}\wedge\vec{z}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, where

w_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i},\text{ if }y_{i}=z_{i}\\
0,\text{ if }y_{i}\neq z_{i}.\end{cases}

A family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset Y_{1}\times\ldots\times Y_{n}
is called
t
-intersecting
if for all
\vec{y},\vec{z}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
\vec{y}\wedge\vec{z}
has at least
t
non-zero coordinates. Note that in the
n
-partite setting it is the same as requiring
|\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\cap\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\}|\geq t
.

In the case of
t=1
we omit the
1
and say simply
intersecting
. Let us recall two, by now classical results, analogous to the famous Erdős–Ko–Rado Theorem
[
5
]
.

Theorem 1

(Deza and Frankl
[
4
]
)
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset Y_{1}\times\ldots\times Y_{n}
is intersecting and
|Y_{1}|\leq|Y_{i}|
,
i=2,\ldots,n
. Then

(1)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\prod_{2\leq i\leq n}|Y_{i}|.

Let us note that the case
|Y_{1}|=\ldots=|Y_{n}|
goes back to Meyer
[
16
]
.

Theorem 2

(Frankl and Füredi
[
9
]
)
.

Suppose that
s>t\geq 1
and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
is
t
-intersecting. then

(2)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq s^{n-t}.

Note that both (
1
) and (
2
) are easily seen to be best possible.
Even that the case
s=2,t=1
of Theorem
2
is trivial it is closely related to a classical result in extremal set theory.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{G}}\subset 2^{X}
satisfies
G\cap G^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
and
G\cup G^{\prime}\neq X
for all
G,G^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}
. Then

(3)

|{\mathcal{G}}|\leq 2^{|X|-2}.

This important result was proved nearly simultaneously by several sets of authors (
[
3
]
,
[
17
]
,…). The authors of the present note recently gave an alternative proof of Theorem
3
along with some generalizations.
Theorem
3
can be restated in the terminology of integer sequences as follows.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
and for all
\vec{y},\vec{z}\in{\mathcal{F}}
there exist
i,j\in[n]
so that
y_{i}=z_{i}=1
and
y_{j}=z_{j}=2
. Then
|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq 2^{n-2}
. Let us generalize this situation by introducing the notion of
(t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting
property.

Definition 4
.

Let
t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}
be non-negative integers and
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
. then
{\mathcal{F}}
is called
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting if for all
\vec{y},\vec{z}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
\vec{y}\wedge\vec{z}
has at least
t_{\ell}
coordinates equal to
\ell
for all
1\leq\ell\leq s
.

Note that
{\mathcal{F}}\neq\emptyset
implies
t_{1}+\ldots t_{s}\leq n
and also that any
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting family is
(t_{1}+\ldots t_{s})
-intersecting.

The simplest version of the main result of the present paper is the following

Theorem 5
.

Let
s\geq 2
and suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
is
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting,
n\geq t_{1}+\ldots+t_{s}
,
0\leq t_{i}<s
for each
1\leq i\leq s
. Then

(4)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq s^{n-\sum_{i\in[s]}t_{i}}.

1.
A correlation inequality for integer sequences

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
and
P
a proper subset of
[s]
. Let us introduce the relationship
<_{P}
by setting
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})<_{P}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
iff for each
1\leq i\leq n
either
x_{i}=y_{i}
or
x_{i}\notin P.
Note that for
y,z\notin P
, both
(y)<_{P}(x)
and
(x)<_{P}(y)
hold! In human language, to make a ‘larger’ vector in
<_{P}
we can change any coordinates with entries not in
P
to arbitrary entries.

Definition 6
.

The family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
is called
P
-complete if
\vec{x}<_{P}\vec{y}
and
\vec{x}\in{\mathcal{F}}
always imply
\vec{y}\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Theorem 7
.

Supposet that
P,Q\subset[s]
are non-empty and disjoint. Consider two families
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\subset[s]^{n}
such that
{\mathcal{F}}
is
P
-complete and
{\mathcal{G}}
is a
Q
-complete. Then

(5)

|{\mathcal{F}}||{\mathcal{G}}|\geq s^{n}|{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}|.

Note that (
5
) is equivalent to

\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}|}{s^{n}}\leq\frac{|{\mathcal{F}}|}{s^{n}}\cdot\frac{|{\mathcal{G}}|}{s^{n}}.

That is, (
5
) shows that the probabilistic events
\vec{x}\in{\mathcal{F}},\vec{x}\in{\mathcal{G}}
are negatively correlated w.r.t. the uniform measure on
[s]^{n}
.

The case
[s]=\{1,2\}
,
P=\{1\}
,
Q=\{2\}
of (
5
) is equivalent to the Kleitman–Harris correlation inequality (cf.
[
14
]
,
[
12
]
) for up-sets and down-sets.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. Consider the base case,
n=1
, first. Then
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}
are subsets of
[s]
. If
{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}=\emptyset
then (
5
) holds. Assume that
x\in{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}
. If
x\notin P\cup Q
then
{\mathcal{F}}={\mathcal{G}}=[s]
and therefore (
5
) follows. Say,
{\mathcal{F}}\neq[s]
. Then
{\mathcal{F}}\subset P
. Using
x\in{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}
and
P\cap Q=\emptyset
,
{\mathcal{G}}=[s]
follows. Thus
|{\mathcal{F}}|=|{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}|=|{\mathcal{F}}||{\mathcal{G}}|/s
, proving (
5
).

Next, assume that (
5
) holds for
n
and let us prove it for
n+1
. Take two families
{\mathcal{F}},{\mathcal{G}}\in[s]^{n+1}
and consider the families
{\mathcal{F}}_{i}
,
{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
,
i\in[s]
, where

{\mathcal{F}}_{i}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in[s]^{n}:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},i)\in{\mathcal{F}}\}

and
{\mathcal{G}}_{i}
is defined analogously. Set also
f_{i}=|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}|
(and similarly
g_{i}=|{\mathcal{G}}_{i}|
). Note the relations
f_{i}\leq f_{j}
if
i\neq P
(implying
f_{i}=f_{j}
if
i,j\notin P
) and
g_{i}\leq g_{j}
if
i\notin Q
.

Let
f
(
g
) be the common value of
f_{i}
(
g_{i}
) for
i\notin P
(
i\notin Q
), respectively. Note that

|{\mathcal{F}}|=f_{1}+\ldots+f_{s}=(s-|P|)f+\sum_{i\in P}(f_{i}-f),

|{\mathcal{G}}|=g_{1}+\ldots+g_{s}=(s-|Q|)g+\sum_{i\in Q}(g_{i}-g),

and also
(f_{i}-f)(g_{i}-g)=0
by
P\cap Q=\emptyset
for all
i
.
By the induction hypothesis, we have

s^{n+1}|{\mathcal{F}}\cap{\mathcal{G}}|=s^{n+1}\sum_{i\in[s]}|{\mathcal{F}}_{i}\cap{\mathcal{G}}_{i}|\leq s\sum_{i\in[s]}f_{i}g_{i}=s^{2}fg+sf\sum_{i\in Q}(g_{i}-g)+sg\sum_{i\in P}(f_{i}-f).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}||{\mathcal{G}}|=

\displaystyle(\sum_{i\in[s]}f_{i})(\sum_{i\in[s]}g_{i})=(sf+\sum_{i\in P}(f_{i}-f))(sg+\sum_{i\in Q}(g_{i}-g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{2}fg+sf\sum_{i\in Q}(g_{i}-g)+sg\sum_{i\in P}(f_{i}-f)+(\sum_{i\in P}(f_{i}-f))(\sum_{i\in Q}(g_{i}-g)).

Since the last term is non-negative, the proof of (
5
) is complete.
∎

2.
The proof of Theorem
5

For
\vec{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
let us define

p(n,s,\vec{t})=\max\big\{|{\mathcal{F}}|/s^{n}:{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}\text{ is }\vec{t}\text{-intersecting}\big\}.

Lemma 8
.

For
1\leq r<s
,

(6)

p(n,s,\vec{t})\leq p(n,s,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r},0,0,\ldots,0)p(n,s,0,\ldots,0,t_{r+1},\ldots,t_{s}).

Proof.

Suppose that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
is
\vec{t}
-intersecting,
|{\mathcal{F}}|
is maximal. For
P\subset[s]
let us define the
P
-complete family
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
by

{\mathcal{F}}(P)=\{\vec{z}\in[s]^{n}:\exists\vec{y}\in{\mathcal{F}},\vec{y}<_{P}\vec{z}.\}

In human words
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
is obtained from
{\mathcal{F}}
by adding all possible sequences obtained from some sequence in
{\mathcal{F}}
by changing entries
y\notin P
to arbitrary
y\in[s]
. The
P
-completeness of
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
should be clear.

Since no entry
Y_{\ell}
of
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying
y_{\ell}\in P
is ever changed for each
i\in P
,
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
is
(0,0,\ldots,t_{i},\ldots,0)
-intersecting.

Consequently,

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}([r])|/s^{n}

\displaystyle\leq p\big(n,s,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r},0,\ldots,0)\big)\ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle|{\mathcal{F}}([r+1,s])|/s^{n}

\displaystyle\leq p\big(n,s,(0,\ldots,0,t_{r+1},\ldots,t_{s})\big).

Since
{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}([r])\cap{\mathcal{F}}([r+1,s])
is obvious, (
6
) follows from (
5
).
∎

Now the proof of Theorem
5
is easy. In view of Theorem
3
,
p(n,s,(0,\ldots,0,t_{i},\ldots,0))=s^{-t_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq s
and
0\leq t_{i}<s
. Applying repeatedly (
6
), we obtain

p(n,s,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}))\leq s^{-t_{1}}p(n,s,(0,t_{2},\ldots,t_{s}))\leq s^{-t_{1}-t_{2}}p(n,s,(0,0,t_{3},\ldots,t_{s}))\leq\ldots\leq s^{-t_{1}-\ldots-t_{s}}.

3.
The case
s=2

The results for
s=2
that we mentioned in the introduction might appear meager. They concern the case
t=1
only. There is a good reason for it.

Claim 9
.

We have
\lim_{n\to\infty}p(n,2,(t_{1},t_{2}))=\frac{1}{4}
for any fixed positive integer vector
t_{1},t_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
p(n,2,(t_{1},t_{2})\leq 2^{-2}
follows from (
3
), we only need to show the opposite inequality by construction.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}\subset[n]
with
X_{1}\cap X_{2}\neq\emptyset
and
|X_{1}|=n_{1},|X_{2}|=n_{2}
. Define

\mathcal{K}(X_{1},X_{2},\vec{t})=\Big\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in 2^{[n]}:\big|\{j:j\in X_{i}\text{ and }y_{j}=i\}\big|\geq\frac{n_{i}+t_{i}}{2}\text{ for }i=1,2\Big\}.

For
t_{i}
fixed and
n_{i}\to\infty
,
|\mathcal{K}(X_{1},X_{2},\vec{t})|=(\frac{1}{2}+o(1))^{2}2^{n}
should be clear as well as the
(t_{1},t_{2})
-intersecting property.
∎

In the Ph.D. dissertation of the first author
[
8
]
the following conjecture was stated.

Conjecture 1
.

Suppose that
\vec{t}=(t_{1},t_{2})
is a positive integer vector with
t_{1}+t_{2}\leq n
. Assume that
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is
\vec{t}
-intersecting. Then

(7)

|{\mathcal{F}}|\leq\max_{X_{1},X_{2}}|\mathcal{K}(X_{1},X_{2},\vec{t})|.

Let us mention that the maximum of the RHS is achieved for
X_{1},X_{2}
satisfying
|X_{i}|\equiv t_{i}\mod 2
and
|X_{1}|+|X_{2}|=n
or
n-1
. The case
\min\{t_{1},t_{2}\}=1
was proved in
[
7
]
.

Set
q=n-t_{1}-t_{2}
. For the cases
q=0
or
1
,
\max|{\mathcal{F}}|=2^{q}
is easy to verify. Bang, Sharp and Winkler
[
2
]
proved (
7
) for
q=2
or
3
. Recently, Wang and the first author
[
11
]
settled the case
n>n_{0}(q)
by proving (
7
) for
n>(q+1)^{3}
and also for
q=4
.

Let us also mention that the cases
(t,0)
and
(0,t)
are equivalent to the classical Katona Theorem
[
13
]
.

4.
The general case

Suppose that
s\geq 3
. There is a striking difference to the case
s=2
. Let us explain it by considering the
(t,0,\ldots,0)
case.

Suppose that
X\subset[n]
,
|X|\equiv t\mod 2
. The construction

\mathcal{L}(X)=\Big\{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in[s]^{n}:|\{i\in X:y_{i}=1\}|\geq\frac{|X|+t}{2}\Big\}

is clearly
(t,0,\ldots,0)
-intersecting. However, the overwhelming majority of sequences
\vec{y}\in[s]^{n}
has around
n/s
coordinates equal to
1
. Hence for
|X|>(\frac{1}{s}+\epsilon)n
with constant
\epsilon>0
,
|\mathcal{L}(X)|/s^{n}
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. In fact, this ratio is at most
e^{-2\epsilon^{2}n/s^{2}}
using standard Chernoff-type concentration inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
1
, Theorem A.1.4]
).

Let us define the
p
-biased measure
\mu_{p}
on
2^{[n]}
by setting
\mu_{p}(F)=p^{|F|}(1-p)^{n-|F|}
for all
F\in[n]
. Filmus
[
6
]
proved a Complete
t
-intersection Theorem for families in
2^{[n]}
with a
p
-biased measure. In order to state it, we need some definitions. Put
r^{*}
to be the largest integer satisfying
r^{*}\leq(n-t)/2
. For each
r\leq r^{*}
define

{\mathcal{F}}_{t,r}=\{A\subset[n]:|A\cap[t+2r]|\geq t+r\}.

Also, define

(8)

w(n,t,p)=\begin{cases}\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}}_{t,r}),&\mbox{if }\frac{r}{t+2r-1}\leq p\leq\frac{r+1}{t+2r+1}\text{ for some }r<r^{*},\\
\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}}(t,r^{*})),&\mbox{if }\frac{r^{*}}{t+2r^{*}-1}\leq p.\end{cases}

Then the theorem of Filmus states

Theorem 10
.

If
{\mathcal{F}}\subset 2^{[n]}
is
t
-intersecting with
t\geq 2
then
\mu_{p}({\mathcal{F}})\leq w(n,p,t)
.

We are particularly interested in the case
p=1/s
. Doing simple algebraic manipulations, we see that for
p=1/s
the right inequality on
p
in the first line of (
8
) is equivalent to

(9)

t+2r\geq\frac{s}{s-2}t-\frac{2s-2}{s-2}=t+2\cdot\frac{t-s+1}{s-2}.

That is, if
t+2\big\lceil\frac{t-s+1}{s-2}\big\rceil\leq m
for some integers
m
and
n\geq m
, then
w(n,t,1/s)=\mu_{1/s}({\mathcal{F}}_{t,r})
for some
r
such that
t+2r\leq m
. Moreover, note that, for any integer
n^{\prime},n^{\prime}\geq m
, we have
w(n^{\prime},t,1/s)=w(n,t,1/s)
in these conditions.

The case of
p=\frac{1}{s}
is actually very relevant to our question for the following reason. Given a
(t,0,\ldots,0)
-intersecting family
{\mathcal{G}}\subset[s]^{n}
, we can form a family
\mathcal{P}_{1}({\mathcal{G}})\subset 2^{[n]}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{i}({\mathcal{G}})
is defined as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{i}(G)=\big\{G\subset[n]:G=\{i:y_{i}=i\}\text{ for some }(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in{\mathcal{G}}\big\}.

If we assume that
{\mathcal{G}}
is
\{1\}
-complete (which holds obviously for the largest
(t,0,\ldots,0)
-intersecting family in
[s]^{n}
), then it is straightforward to see that

|{\mathcal{G}}|/s^{n}=\mu_{1/s}(\mathcal{P}_{1}(G)).

The following theorem is the main result of this note.

Theorem 11
.

Suppose that
n\geq s\geq 3
and
t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}
are non-negative integers. Assume that
{\mathcal{G}}\subset[s]^{n}
is
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting of maximum size and
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\big(t_{i}+2\big\lceil\frac{t_{i}-s+1}{s-2}\big\rceil\big)\leq n
(which is implied by
\sum_{i=1}^{s}t_{i}\leq\frac{s-2}{s}n+s
). Then

\frac{|{\mathcal{G}}|}{s^{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}w(n,t_{i},1/s).

Proof.

The upper bound is akin to the proof of Theorem
5
. Using (
6
), we get that

\frac{|{\mathcal{G}}|}{s^{n}}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{s}p(n,s,(0,\ldots,0,t_{i},0\ldots,0)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}w(n,t_{i},1/s).

We go on to the proof of the lower bound. Define
r_{i}
so that
w(n,t_{i},1/s)=\mu_{1/s}({\mathcal{F}}_{t,r_{i}})
and take a partition of
[n]
into
s
pairwise disjoint sets
X_{1},\ldots,X_{s}
so that
|X_{i}|=t_{i}+2r_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,s-1
and
|X_{s}|=n-\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}(t_{i}+2r_{i})\geq t_{s}+2r_{s}
. Here, the last inequality holds by (
9
) and the subsequent analysis. For each
i\in[s]
, let
{\mathcal{F}}^{i}\subset[s]^{X_{i}}
be an
\{i\}
-complete family of words so that
\mathcal{P}_{i}({\mathcal{F}}^{i})\subset 2^{X_{i}}
is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{F}}_{t,r_{i}}
. Note that
{\mathcal{F}}_{t,r_{i}}
‘fits’ into
2^{X_{i}}
by the choice of
|X_{i}|
. For a word
\vec{y}
define
\vec{y}|_{X_{i}}
as the part of the vector
\vec{y}
induced on coordinates from
X_{i}
. Let the family
{\mathcal{F}}\subset[s]^{n}
be defined as follows:

{\mathcal{F}}=\big\{\vec{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in[s]^{n}:\text{ for all }i\in[s]\text{ and }i\in X_{i}\text{ we have }\vec{y}|_{X_{i}}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{i}\big\}.

It is easy to see that, first,
{\mathcal{F}}
is
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{s})
-intersecting and, second,

|{\mathcal{F}}|=\prod_{i\in[s]}|{\mathcal{F}}^{i}|=s^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{s}w(|X_{i}|,t,1/s)=s^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{s}w(n,t,1/s).

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎
