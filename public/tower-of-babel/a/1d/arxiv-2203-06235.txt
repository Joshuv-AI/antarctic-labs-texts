Boundary dynamics for holomorphic sequences, non-autonomous dynamical systems and wandering domains
Anna Miriam Benini, Vasiliki Evdoridou †, Núria Fagella †, Philip J. Rippon ‡, Gwyneth M. Stallard ‡

Abstract
There are many classical results, related to the Denjoy–Wolff Theorem, concerning the relationship between orbits of interior points and orbits of boundary points under iterates of holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc. Here, for the first time, we address such questions in the very general setting of sequences ( F n ) (F_{n}) of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains. We show that, while some classical results can be generalised, with an interesting dependence on the geometry of the domains, a much richer variety of behaviours is possible. Some of our results are new even in the classical setting. Our methods apply in particular to non-autonomous dynamical systems, when ( F n ) (F_{n}) are forward compositions of holomorphic maps, and to the study of wandering domains in holomorphic dynamics. The proofs use techniques from geometric function theory, measure theory and ergodic theory, and the construction of examples involves a ‘weak independence’ version of the second Borel–Cantelli lemma and the concept from ergodic theory of ‘shrinking targets’.

Introduction

We consider sequences of holomorphic maps
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
between proper, simply connected domains, and prove results concerning the relationship between sequences
(F_{n}(z))
, where
z
is an interior point of
U
and
(F_{n}(\zeta))
, where
\zeta
lies in the
boundary
of
U
. In general, the maps
F_{n}
are not defined on the boundary of
U
but we shall introduce a ‘radial extension’ of
F_{n}
which in many cases exists at almost all boundary points, with respect to harmonic measure – in this situation we say that
F_{n}
has a ‘full radial extension’ to
\partial U
; see Section
2
for the details. For simplicity, we use the notation
F_{n}
to refer also to the extension of our original map at these boundary points.

In this general setting, we describe the sequence of images
(F_{n}(z)
), where
z\in U
or
z\in\partial U
, as an
orbit
. There are several natural questions about such orbits: does the behaviour of interior orbits determine the behaviour of boundary orbits (and vice-versa)? If all interior orbits approach the boundary, then do they approach the orbit of a particular boundary point and, if so, how large is the set of boundary points with similar limiting behaviour?

An important case of our general setting is a non-autonomous dynamical system in which
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
is a
forward composition
of holomorphic maps
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
U_{0}=U
. Systems of forward compositions of holomorphic maps have been studied extensively in recent years in a variety of contexts; see, for example,
[
FS91
,
Com04
,
Ses99
,
RGU16
,
LZ22
]
.

A particularly well-studied special case of our general setting is when
U
is a simply connected
wandering domain
of an entire or meromorphic map
f
and
F_{n}=f^{n}
is the
n
-th iterate of
f
; see, for example,
[
BFJK20b
,
RS11
,
Bis15
,
MPS20
,
ERS23
,
BEF+22
,
MPRW22
,
EGPS23
]
for many results on such wandering domains. In fact, obtaining a better understanding of the orbits of boundary points of wandering domains was the original motivation for this work.

Another important special case is the following result, where
F_{n}
are the iterates
f^{n}
of a holomorphic self-map
f
of the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
; see
[
CG93
, page 79]
, and also
[
AC22
]
for many references to this classical result.

Theorem 1.1

(Denjoy–Wolff Theorem)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
be holomorphic. Provided
f
is not a rotation about a point in
{\mathbb{D}}
, there exists a unique point
p\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
(the Denjoy–Wolff point) such that
f^{n}(z)\to p
as
n\to\infty
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
.

In this unit disc setting, there is a remarkable dichotomy concerning orbits of boundary points, which originates in the work of Neuwirth
[
Neu78
]
, Aaronson
[
Aar78
]
, and Doering and Mañé
[
DM91
]
. It concerns the iterates of an
inner function

f
; that is, a holomorphic self-map of the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
whose radial extension maps
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
to
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, apart from a set of measure zero. Our statement is a modification of the original dichotomy given in
[
Aar78
]
and
[
DM91
]
, more suited to our present purposes.

Theorem 1.2

(ADM dichotomy)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
be an inner function with a Denjoy–Wolff point
p\in\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
.

(a)

If
\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-|f^{n}(z)|)<\infty
for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,
then
p\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(\zeta)=p
for almost every
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

(b)

If
\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-|f^{n}(z)|)=\infty
for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, then the iterates
f^{n}(\zeta),n\in{\mathbb{N}},
are dense in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
for almost every
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

Thus in this setting if interior orbits tend towards the boundary sufficiently quickly, then almost all boundary orbits tend to the Denjoy–Wolff point, whereas almost no boundary orbits do this if interior orbits tend towards the boundary more slowly. This dichotomy is our main inspiration for obtaining results in the general setting of holomorphic sequences and non-autonomous dynamical systems, with many applications, including to boundary orbits of simply connected wandering domains.

Theorem
1.2
, part (a) is in
[
Aar78
, Section 3]
and
[
DM91
, Theorem 4.1]
, and it was generalised to iterates of holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
in
[
BMS05
, Theorem 4.2]
. The proof of Theorem
1.2
, part (b) uses various results from ergodic theory; see Section
8.3
.

Before discussing generalisations of Theorem
1.2
, we point out that in our general setting orbits of interior points need not have common limiting behaviour; see Example
7.3
. However, if the orbit of one interior point converges to the boundary (in any manner), with respect to the Euclidean distance,
\operatorname{dist}(.,.)
, then the orbits of all interior points converge to the boundary and, moreover, they all have a common limiting behaviour.

Theorem A
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains. If there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that

\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then, for all
z\in U
,

|F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(z_{0})|\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

In Section
3
, we classify all the possible types of behaviour of interior orbits in relation to convergence to the boundary, and we prove Theorem A.

In view of Theorem A, we can make the following definition.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, for which interior orbits converge to the boundary. The
Denjoy–Wolff set
of
(F_{n})
consists of those
\zeta\in\partial U
such that, for all
z\in U
,

|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(z)|\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Remarks
.

1.  In our general setting the orbits of points
\zeta\in\partial U
need not lie on the boundaries of the sets
U_{n}
; they need only satisfy
F_{n}(\zeta)\in\overline{U_{n}}
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

2.  In the case of iterates of a holomorphic self-map
f
of the unit disc, if the Denjoy–Wolff point
p
of
f
is on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, then
f
is defined at
p
via its radial limit, and
p
is a fixed point of
f
which belongs to the Denjoy–Wolff set. Theorem
1.2
shows that in this situation the Denjoy–Wolff set has either full measure or measure zero.

All our results about the boundary orbits of a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains are stated in terms of the size of the Denjoy–Wolff set.

In Section
4
, we show that part (a) of Theorem
1.2
does indeed have an analogue in our general setting. Interestingly, the result that we obtain depends on the geometry of the boundaries of the domains
(U_{n})
. In the case that all the domains are the unit disc, we obtain a direct generalisation of the result for iterates of inner functions.

Theorem B
.

Let
F_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
and suppose that there exists
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1-|F_{n}(z_{0})|\right)<\infty.

(1.1)

Then almost all points in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
belong to the Denjoy–Wolff set of
(F_{n})
.

In the setting of general simply connected domains
U
and
U_{n}
instead of the unit disc, an analogue of the condition in Theorem B would be that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})<\infty.

(1.2)

However, the less smooth the boundaries of the domains
U_{n}
are, the stronger the hypothesis we need. Without any smoothness assumptions, we can prove the following.

Theorem C
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension to
\partial U
, and suppose that there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})^{1/2}<\infty.

(1.3)

Then almost all points in
\partial U
belong to the Denjoy–Wolff set of
(F_{n})
.

Remarks
.

1. Condition (
1.3
) in Theorem C is sharp. Indeed, an example in Section
4
shows that the power
1/2
in (
1.3
) cannot be increased.

2. A special case of Theorem C is when
(U_{n})
is an orbit of simply connected wandering domains of a transcendental entire or meromorphic function
f
. In this case
F_{n}=f^{n}
always has a continuous extension to the boundary, except perhaps at
\infty
.

Despite this successful generalisation of the first part of the ADM dichotomy (Theorem
1.2
, part (a)), the second part of the dichotomy fails in the general setting of sequences of functions, and even in the setting of non-autonomous dynamical systems; indeed, under the same assumption that interior orbits converge to the boundary slowly, very different types of behaviour can occur on the boundary itself; we illustrate this with examples in Section
8
.

In Section 5, we give an example which shows, perhaps surprisingly, that it is possible for the orbits of all interior points to converge to the boundary with the same limiting behaviour and yet the Denjoy–Wolff set is
empty
. (This cannot occur in the classical case since the Denjoy–Wolff point always belongs to the Denjoy–Wolff set.)

Theorem D

(Empty Denjoy–Wolff set)
.

Let
(a_{n})
be an increasing sequence in
[0,1)
such that
a_{0}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=1
, and let
(\lambda_{n})
be any sequence in
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Then, for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

F_{n}(z)=\frac{\lambda_{n}z+a_{n}}{1+\lambda_{n}a_{n}z}\to 1\;\;\text{as }n\to\infty.

If, in addition,
\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-F_{n}(0))=\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-a_{n})=\infty,
then there exists
(\lambda_{n})
in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
such that no point of
\partial\mathbb{D}
(even the point 1) has an orbit under
F_{n}
that converges to 1; in other words, the Denjoy–Wolff set of
(F_{n})
is empty.

Remarks
.

1. If
\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-a_{n})<\infty
in Theorem D, then it follows from Theorem B that almost all points in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
are in the Denjoy–Wolff set of
(F_{n})
.

2. Each of the functions
F_{n}
in Theorem D can in fact be written as a forward composition of Möbius maps
f_{n}
with
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ F_{n-1}
, so this is a non-autonomous example. It remains an open question as to whether this behaviour can occur for wandering domains.

So far, we have considered the effect of the behaviour of interior orbits on the behaviour of boundary orbits. In Section 6, we consider the converse problem. We show that if a sufficiently large set of boundary points have orbits with common long-term behaviour then this set must be in the Denjoy–Wolff set; that is, the orbits of all interior points must have the same limiting behaviour as the orbits of this set of boundary points. This is new even for iterates of inner functions.

Theorem E
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension, and suppose that there exists
\zeta_{0}\in\partial U
such that
\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(\zeta_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. If

L_{0}:=\{\zeta\in\partial U:|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})|\to 0\;\;\text{as }n\to\infty\}

has positive harmonic measure with respect to
U
, then for all
z\in U
we have

|F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})|\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In particular,
L_{0}
is in the Denjoy–Wolff set of
(F_{n})
.

It follows that at most one such set of positive harmonic measure may exist.

For the later sections of the paper we restrict ourselves to the setting of non-autonomous dynamical systems, where each map
F_{n}
is the forward composition of holomorphic maps
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
between simply connected domains. In this setting, we give a classification of possible internal dynamics in terms of the limiting behaviour of the hyperbolic distance between orbits of pairs of points, which extends our classification of wandering domains in
[
BEF+22
]
. This classification is in Section
7
, together with an example which shows that the classification does not hold in general for
sequences
of holomorphic maps. One of the three possibilities in our classification is that the maps are
contracting
, defined as follows. Here we use the notation
\operatorname{dist}_{U}
to denote the
hyperbolic distance
in a simply connected domain
U
.

Definition 1.4
.

A sequence of maps
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
is
contracting
if

\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,\quad\mbox{for all }z,z^{\prime}\in U.

For contracting forward compositions of holomorphic maps which preserve boundaries, we prove the strong result that the Denjoy–Wolff set has either full or zero measure.

Theorem F
.

Let
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension, and let
F_{n}:U_{0}\to U_{n}
be defined by

F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1},\quad\text{for }n\in{\mathbb{N}},

with the additional assumption that

f_{n}(\zeta)\in\partial U_{n},\;\text{ for almost all }\zeta\in\partial U_{n-1}.

(1.4)

If
(F_{n})
is contracting and interior orbits converge to the boundary, then the Denjoy–Wolff set has either full or zero harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
.

Remarks
.

1. In Section
2
, we show that the composition of functions with full radial extensions also has a full radial extension; this is the case, for example, when
U_{0}
is a wandering domain of an entire function
f
with
F_{n}=f^{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
or when all the functions
f_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, are inner functions.

2. Several results in this paper, including Theorem F, can be applied when the domains
(U_{n})
are multiply connected and we are concerned with orbits converging to the ‘outer boundary’; that is, the boundary of the topological hull of
U_{n}
. This approach is used in
[
Fer22
, Theorem 1.4]
, where a result analogous to Theorem A is given in the setting of dynamical behaviour near the outer boundary of multiply connected wandering domains of meromorphic functions.

In Section
7
, we prove a general result, Theorem
7.4
, that has Theorem F as a corollary. Our proof of Theorem
7.4
uses an ergodic theory result of Pommerenke and generalises one aspect of it. We also give an example to show that Theorem F does not hold in general if the forward compositions are not contracting.

In Section
8
, we give a number of examples of forward compositions of self-maps of the unit disc, related to part (b) of the ADM-dichotomy. Whether the interior and boundary dynamics demonstrated in these examples can be replicated by examples of simply connected wandering domains is an open question, and will be the subject of further investigation. Our final example, Example
8.3
, is a contracting sequence; for this example, we are able to show that the Denjoy–Wolff set has measure zero and, in fact, that orbits of almost all boundary points are dense in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. This raises the question of whether an analogue of part (b) of the ADM dichotomy may exist in the setting of contracting forward compositions. We discuss this in Section
8.3
and also in the final section of the paper on open questions.

In Section
9
, we discuss briefly the extent to which our results hold if we replace the Euclidean metric as a measure of proximity by the spherical metric; the latter metric has the advantage of including those points whose orbits tend to
\infty
but at the same time we potentially lose control of some of the more subtle behaviour amongst those orbits that do tend to
\infty
.

The proofs of Theorems A to F and of our examples use a variety of techniques from geometric function theory, harmonic analysis and measure theory including contraction of the hyperbolic metric, Löwner’s lemma, the Milloux–Schmidt inequality, the Severini–Egorov theorem, and a version of the second Borel–Cantelli lemma associated with the ergodic theory concept of ‘shrinking targets’.

2
Preliminary results

In this section we record a number of properties of certain boundary extensions of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains and the relationship of these boundary extensions to the harmonic measure of sets on the boundaries of these domains. In doing so, we use a number of classical results on the boundary behaviour of holomorphic maps, which can mostly be found in
[
Pom92
]
.

In this section only, we denote such boundary extensions of a holomorphic map
f
by
f^{*}
for clarity; elsewhere in the paper we drop the * notation for simplicity.

2.1
Boundary extensions

Recall that our general setting is that of sequences of holomorphic maps

F_{n}:U\longrightarrow U_{n},\quad n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
U
and
U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
are simply connected domains, always assumed to be proper subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
and not necessarily distinct, and that in some cases the functions
F_{n}
are defined by forward composition as
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
, where each
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
is holomorphic and
U_{0}=U
.

In the wandering domains context, where
F_{n}=f^{n}
with
f
entire, and
f:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
, the maps are naturally defined and continuous on the whole of
\partial U_{0}
(except maybe at the point at infinity). In the inner functions context, however, where
U_{n}={\mathbb{D}}
, for
n\geq 0
, each
F_{n}
extends in the sense of radial limits and even nontangential limits to a full measure subset of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
by theorems of Fatou and Lindelöf; see
[
CL66
, p.19]
, for example. We now consider whether such boundary extensions exist in the general setting of this paper.

We shall suppose quite generally that
U
and
V
are simply connected proper subdomains of
{\mathbb{C}}
and
f:U\to V
is holomorphic. We wish to define the concept of a ‘radial extension’ of
f
to boundary points of
U
, wherever this extension makes sense.

A simple case is obtained when
U={\mathbb{D}}
, that is,
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to V
is a holomorphic map (not necessarily conformal). If
V
is bounded, then the radial limit at
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
exists if and only if the non-tangential limit at
\zeta
exists and this occurs if and only the limit exists along any curve in
{\mathbb{D}}
ending at
\zeta
, by the theorem of Lindelöf mentioned above; moreover, all these limits coincide. In the general case, since the complement of
V
contains an unbounded continuum, the function

g(z)=\sqrt{f(z)-w_{0}}

where
w_{0}\notin V
and a suitable branch of the square root is taken, is holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and
g({\mathbb{D}})
omits a disc, with centre
w_{1}
say; thus the function
z\mapsto(g(z)-w_{1})^{-1}
is holomorphic and bounded in
{\mathbb{D}}
, so we see that the relationship between radial limits, nontangential limits and limits along curves remains true for such
f
. Therefore it makes sense to define the
radial extension
of such an
f
as the map

f^{*}(\zeta)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 1}f(r\zeta),\;\text{ for }\zeta\in\operatorname{Rad}(f),

where
\operatorname{Rad}(f)
denotes the subset of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
at which
f
has a finite radial limit. The function
f^{*}
assigns to each point of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
its radial limit, whenever it exists, and takes values in the closure of
f({\mathbb{D}})
. Note that even when
f
has a nontangential limit at
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, the function
f
may fail to converge to that limit along curves in
{\mathbb{D}}
ending at
\zeta
that are not nontangential.

Moving to the case where
f:U\to V
, with
U
and
V
being proper simply connected domains, we introduce a Riemann map
\varphi:{\mathbb{D}}\to U
. By the discussion above,
\varphi
has radial (and equal nontangential) limits almost everywhere on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and in fact
\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\operatorname{Rad}(\varphi)
has capacity zero by a theorem of Beurling
[
Pom92
, Theorem 9.19]
.

A point
p\in\partial U
is called
accessible
from
U
if there exists an arc
\gamma:[0,1)\to U
such that
\gamma(t)\to p
as
t\to 1
. We denote the set of accessible boundary points of
U
by
\operatorname{AP}(U)
(there seems to be no standard notation for this set). Every homotopy class of curves
\gamma\subset U
with their endpoints fixed and one endpoint at an accessible boundary point
p
is called an
access
to
p
from
U
. It follows from another theorem of Lindelöf
[
CG93
, page 8]
that there is a bijection between the set
\operatorname{Rad}(\varphi)
and the set of accesses to boundary points of
U
, induced by
\varphi
; see
[
BFJK17
, Correspondence Theorem]
, for example.

In defining the concept of a radial extension of
f:U\to V
, our aim is that the extension at almost all accessible boundary points of
U
is consistent with the limiting behaviour of
f
along curves to
\partial U
through all possible accesses to such points.

Definition 2.1

(Radial extensions)
.

Let
f:U\to V
be a holomorphic map between simply connected domains, let
p\in\operatorname{AP}(U)
, and let
\varphi:{\mathbb{D}}\to U
be a Riemann map. Then we say that

(a)

a curve in
U
approaches
p

radially
if it is the image of a curve in
{\mathbb{D}}
that approaches a point of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
nontangentially;

(b)

f
has a
radial extension
to
p
with value
f^{*}(p)
if for every arc
\gamma(t),t\in[0,1),
in
U
such that
\gamma(t)\to p
radially as
t\to 1
, the image curve
f(\gamma(t))\to f^{*}(p)
as
t\to 1
;

(c)

f
has a
full radial extension
(to
\partial U
) if the domain of
f^{*}
includes a subset of
\operatorname{AP}(U)
of full harmonic measure with respect to
U
.

We make two remarks about Definition
2.1
.

Remarks
.

1. We focus on the limiting behaviour of
f
only on curves in
U
that approach a boundary point
p

radially
in order to be consistent with the definition of radial extension of a holomorphic map
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to V
in terms of nontangential limits, given above.

2. The existence of a full radial extension of
f
implies that (up to a set of harmonic measure zero)
f
maps accesses to a point
p\in\partial U
to accesses to
f^{*}(p)\in\partial V
, unless
f^{*}(p)
belongs to
V
, which is a priori also possible.

In general, the radial extension of a conformal map
\varphi:{\mathbb{D}}\to U
is not an injective map, since boundary points of
U
can have several (possibly uncountably many) accesses from
U
. Nevertheless
(\varphi^{*})^{-1}(p)
is always a set of measure zero, by the F. and M. Riesz theorem
[
Dur70
]
. Boundary points with a single access from
U
are called
uniquely accessible
and domains for which all their accessible boundary points are uniquely accessible are said to satisfy the
unique accessibility property
. In this case the radial extension of
\varphi
is injective (and therefore bijective).

There are some situations in which full radial extensions exist in a trivial way; in particular, this is the case when
U
and
V
are Fatou components of a holomorphic map or when
f
is an inner function, since
U=V={\mathbb{D}}
in this case. More generally, we have the following.

Proposition 2.2

(Simple cases)
.

Let
f:U\to V
be a holomorphic map between simply connected domains. If

(a)

f
has a continuous extension to
\partial U
, or

(b)

U
has the unique accessibility property,

then
f
admits a full radial extension
f^{*}
. In case (a), we have
f^{*}:=f|_{\partial U}
.

It is interesting to ask whether if
f:U\to V
is a holomorphic map between simply connected domains and
f
has a full radial extension to
\partial U
, then this implies that at almost all
\zeta\in\partial U
, with respect to harmonic measure, either
f
has a continuous extension to
\zeta
or the point
\zeta
is uniquely accessible from within
U
?
It is straightforward to construct examples where both types of boundary behaviour occur in sets of positive harmonic measure.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

If
f
is continuous in
\overline{U}
, then the conclusion that
f
has a full radial extension is trivial.

For case (b) let
\varphi_{U},\varphi_{V}
be Riemann maps from
{\mathbb{D}}
to
U,V
respectively and let
g:{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
be defined as
g:=\varphi_{V}^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi_{U}
.

If
U
has the unique accessibility property, then
\varphi_{U}^{*}
is injective, so its inverse is well defined on
\varphi^{*}(\partial{\mathbb{D}})
, which has full harmonic measure in
U
because
\varphi_{U}
is the Riemann map. Hence we can define

f^{*}:=(\varphi_{V}\circ g)^{*}\circ(\varphi_{U}^{*})^{-1}

on the set
E=\operatorname{AP}(U)\cap\varphi_{U}^{*}(\operatorname{Rad}(\varphi_{V}\circ g))
, which is of full harmonic measure in
U
since it is the intersection of two sets of full harmonic measure. With this definition, if
\gamma(t)
in
U
converges radially to
p\in E
, then
\varphi_{U}^{-1}(\gamma(t))
converges nontangentially to a point
\zeta\in\operatorname{Rad}(\varphi_{U})\cap\operatorname{Rad}(\varphi_{V}\circ g)
, so
f
has a full radial extension.
∎

We also point out to what extent the radial extension property is preserved under forward composition.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
f_{1}:U_{0}\to U_{1}
and
f_{2}:U_{1}\to U_{2}
be holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, with radial extensions
f_{1}^{*}
to
E_{0}\subset\operatorname{AP}(U_{0})
and
f_{2}^{*}
to
E_{1}\subset\operatorname{AP}(U_{1})
, respectively.

Then
f_{2}\circ f_{1}:U_{0}\to U_{2}
has a radial extension to almost all points of
E_{0}\setminus(f_{1}^{*})^{-1}(\partial U_{1}\setminus E_{1})
.

Proof.

For
\zeta_{0}\in E_{0}\setminus(f_{1}^{*})^{-1}(\partial U_{1}\setminus E_{1})
,
let
\gamma_{0}(t),0\leq t<1,
be a path in
U_{0}
that converges radially to
\zeta_{0}
. Then, by our assumption about
\zeta_{0}
, we have
\gamma_{1}(t):=f_{1}(\gamma_{0}(t))\to\zeta_{1}:=f_{1}^{*}(\zeta_{0})
as
t\to 1
; if
\zeta_{1}\in U_{1}
we are done, otherwise
\zeta_{1}\in E_{1}
. Note that
\gamma_{1}(t)
may or may not converge radially to
\zeta_{1}
.

Now let
\hat{\gamma}_{1}(t),0\leq t<1,
be a path in
U_{1}
that does converge radially to
\zeta_{1}
and is homotopic in
U_{1}
to
\gamma_{1}(t)
. Then
f_{2}(\hat{\gamma}_{1}(t))\to\hat{\zeta}_{2}:=f_{2}^{*}(\zeta_{1})
as
t\to 1
. Suppose, if possible, that
f_{2}(\gamma_{1}(t))=f_{2}(f_{1}(\gamma_{0}(t)))\nrightarrow\hat{\zeta}_{2}
as
t\to 1
. Since
\gamma_{0}(t)
converges to
\zeta_{0}
radially, we know that apart from a subset of
E_{0}
of harmonic measure zero
f_{2}(f_{1}(\gamma_{0}(t)))
converges to a point,
\zeta_{2}\in\partial U_{2}
say, and we can only have
\zeta_{2}\neq\hat{\zeta}_{2}
for countably many
\zeta_{0}\in E_{0}
, by Bagemihl’s ambiguous point theorem
[
Pom92
]
, applied in
U_{1}
. Therefore, for all
\zeta_{0}\in E_{0}\cap(f_{1}^{*})^{-1}(E_{1})
apart from a set of harmonic measure zero, we have
f_{2}(\gamma_{1}(t))=f_{2}(f_{1}(\gamma_{0}(t)))\to\hat{\zeta}_{2}
, and so
f_{2}\circ f_{1}
has a radial extension to
\zeta_{0}
, as required.
∎

2.2
Properties of harmonic measure

In order to work with our definition of a radial extension, which involves the harmonic measure of boundary sets of simply connected domains, we first recall certain topological properties of boundary sets, and the behaviour of their images and pre-images under holomorphic mappings. Theorem
2.4
, part (a) is given in
[
Pom92
, Proposition 6.5]
and part (b) is due to Cantón, Granados and Pommerenke
[
CGP03
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 2.4

(Borel sets on the boundary)
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}
be continuous.

(a)

The set
\operatorname{Rad}(f)
is a Borel set and, for any Borel set
B\subset\hat{\mathbb{C}}
,

(f^{*})^{-1}(B)=\{\zeta\in\operatorname{Rad}(f):f^{*}(\zeta)\in B\}\;\text{ is a Borel set.}

(b)

Moreover, if
f
is a conformal map and
A\subset\operatorname{Rad}(f)
is a Borel set, then

f^{*}(A)=\{f^{*}(\zeta):\zeta\in A\}\;\text{ is a Borel set}.

In particular, it follows from Theorem
2.4
that the set of accessible points of
\partial U
is a Borel set, since
\operatorname{AP}(U)
is the image under a Riemann map of the Borel set
\operatorname{Rad}(f)\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
(of full measure).

Note that every Borel set is a Souslin set (see
[
Pom92
, page 132]
) and hence universally measurable, and in particular measurable with respect to Lebesgue measure on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and with respect to harmonic measure, which we discuss next.

We shall briefly recall the definition of harmonic measure and some properties of it that we need. Good references for harmonic measure are
[
Con95
, Chapter 2]
,
[
GM05
]
,
[
Pom92
]
and
[
BCDM20
]
.

A standard definition of harmonic measure in the unit disc is in terms of the Poisson integral

\omega(z,A,{\mathbb{D}})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{A}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|\zeta-z|^{2}}\,|d\zeta|,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},

where
A\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
is measurable. The real function
\omega
is harmonic in
{\mathbb{D}}
with
0\leq\omega(z,A,{\mathbb{D}})\leq 1
there, and has radial limit 1 (and indeed nontangential limit 1) at almost every point in
A
and radial limit 0 at almost every point in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus A
.

It follows that for any such set
A
we have
\omega(0,A,{\mathbb{D}})=\lambda(A)
, where
\lambda
is normalised Lebesgue measure on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, and more generally
\omega(z,A,{\mathbb{D}})=\lambda(M(A))
, where
M
is a Möbius map of
{\mathbb{D}}
onto
{\mathbb{D}}
taking
z
to 0; see
[
Pom92
, page 85]
.

We now define harmonic measure in a general simply connected domain
U
in terms of the pullback under a Riemann map of the normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle, following the approach in
[
BCDM20
, Chapter 7]
.

Definition 2.5

(Harmonic measure in a simply connected domain)
.

Let
U\neq{\mathbb{C}}
be a simply connected domain,
z\in U
, and let
\varphi:{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow U
be a conformal map such that
\varphi(0)=z
. The
harmonic measure
of a Borel set
A\subset\partial U
is defined as

\omega(z,A,U)=\lambda((\varphi^{*})^{-1}(A)).

(Note that
(\varphi^{*})^{-1}(A)
is a Borel set in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
by Theorem
2.4
, part (a).)

Since
\lambda
is invariant under rotation, this definition is independent of the choice of
\varphi
provided it satisfies
\varphi(0)=z
.

The harmonic measure
\omega(z,A,U)
can also be interpreted as the probability of hitting the boundary at a point in
A
while following a Brownian path in
U
starting at the point
z
; see
[
MP10
, Section 3.4]
and
[
GM05
, Chapter III]
, for example.

By the theorem of Beurling mentioned earlier, the set
\operatorname{AP}(U)
has full harmonic measure with respect to
U
. Also, for any Borel set
A\subset\partial U
, the function
\omega(z,A,U)
is harmonic in
U
and takes values in the interval
[0,1]
. Moreover, for almost all points
\zeta\in\operatorname{AP}(U)
there is a path
\gamma(t)\subset U,0\leq t<1
, the image of a radius in
{\mathbb{D}}
, such that
\gamma(t)\to\zeta
and
\omega(\gamma(t),A,U)\to 1_{A}(\zeta)
as
t\to 1-
. In this sense, the function
\omega(z,A,U)
is the solution of the Dirichlet problem in
U
with boundary values
\omega(\zeta,A,{\mathbb{D}})=1_{A}(\zeta)
.

2.3
Radial extensions expand harmonic measure

In this section we show that the radial extension of a holomorphic map as defined in Section
2.1
expands the harmonic measure of sets in the boundary. Our result depends on the following version of Löwner’s lemma, which can be found in
[
Pom92
, Proposition 4.15]
and
[
BCDM20
, Theorem 7.1.8 and Proposition 7.1.4 part (4)]
.

Theorem 2.6

(Löwner’s lemma)
.

Let
f
be a holomorphic self-map of
{\mathbb{D}}
,
f^{*}
be its radial extension, and let
S\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
be a Borel set. Then

\omega(z,(f^{*})^{-1}(S),{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(f(z),S,{\mathbb{D}}),\;\text{ for }z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

(2.1)

Remark
.

If
f
is a Möbius map of
{\mathbb{D}}
onto
{\mathbb{D}}
, or even an inner function, then equality holds in (
2.1
) throughout
{\mathbb{D}}
; see
[
DM91
, Corollary 1.5(b)]
.

In our proofs we use the following extension of Theorem
2.6
to simply connected domains.

Theorem 2.7

(Expansion on the boundary of simply connected domains)
.

Let
f:U\to V
be a holomorphic map between simply connected domains with full radial extension
f^{*}
and let
S\subset\partial V
be a Borel set. Then
(f^{*})^{-1}(S)
is a Borel set and, for
z\in U
,

\omega(z,(f^{*})^{-1}(S),U)\leq\omega(f(z),S,V).

(2.2)

In particular, if
(f^{*})^{-1}(S)
has positive (respectively, full) harmonic measure with respect to
U
, then
S
has positive (respectively, full) harmonic measure with respect to
V
.

Proof.

First, let
\varphi_{U}
and
\varphi_{V}
be Riemann maps from
{\mathbb{D}}
to
U
and
V
, respectively. Then
(f^{*})^{-1}(S)
is a Borel set because it is the image under
\varphi_{U}^{*}
of the preimage under
(f\circ\varphi_{U})^{*}
of
S
, apart from a possible set of harmonic measure 0. This follows from our definition of radial extensions and by applying both parts of Theorem
2.4
.

Now define

g(w)=\varphi_{V}^{-1}\circ f\circ\varphi_{U}(w),\;\text{ for }w\in{\mathbb{D}},

so
g
is a self-map of
{\mathbb{D}}
. Then
(\varphi_{V}^{*})^{-1}(S)
is a Borel set, by Theorem
2.4
(a). Also, by our definition of radial extension, and also the bijection between accesses and non-tangential limits of Riemann maps, mentioned earlier, it is straightforward to check that apart from a set of harmonic measure 0 on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

((f\circ\varphi_{U})^{*})^{-1}(S)\subset(g^{*})^{-1}((\varphi_{V}^{*})^{-1}(S)).

Therefore, by Theorem
2.6
, we know that, for
w\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

\omega(w,((f\circ\varphi_{U})^{*})^{-1}(S),{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(w,(g^{*})^{-1}((\varphi_{V}^{*})^{-1}(S)),{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(g(w),(\varphi_{V}^{*})^{-1}(S),{\mathbb{D}}).

(2.3)

Finally, we take
z\in U
and assume that the Riemann maps have been chosen so that

\varphi_{U}(0)=z\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi_{V}(0)=f(z).

Then
g(0)=0
and we have, by the definition of harmonic measure,

\omega(0,((f\circ\varphi_{U})^{*})^{-1}(S),{\mathbb{D}})=\omega(z,(f^{*})^{-1}(S),U)\;\;\text{and}\;\;\omega(0,(\varphi_{V}^{*})^{-1}(S),{\mathbb{D}})=\omega(f(z),S,V),

so the inequality (
2.2
) follows by applying (
2.3
) with
w=0
.
∎

Combining Theorem
2.7
with Proposition
2.3
, we obtain the following useful result about composing full radial extensions.

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
f_{1}:U_{0}\to U_{1}
and
f_{2}:U_{1}\to U_{2}
be holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, with full radial extensions
f_{1}^{*}
and
f_{2}^{*}
, respectively.

Then
f_{2}\circ f_{1}:U_{0}\to U_{2}
has a full radial extension given by
f_{2}^{*}\circ f_{1}^{*}
.

Proof.

By hypothesis the sets
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
specified in Proposition
2.3
have full harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
and
U_{1}
, respectively, and we can deduce that
(f_{1}^{*})^{-1}(\partial U_{1}\setminus E_{1})
must have zero harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
by Theorem
2.7
. It follows that
E_{0}\setminus(f_{1}^{*})^{-1}(\partial U_{1}\setminus E_{1})
has full harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
, as required.
∎

3
Boundary convergence and proof of Theorem A

Before proving Theorem A, we give a classification of sequences
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains in terms of whether orbits
F_{n}(z)
of interior points
z\in U
converge towards the boundary or not. We gave such a classification in the special case that
U
is a wandering domain of a holomorphic map
f
in
[
BEF+22
, Theorem C]
. The same proof holds in our general setting and the techniques used also lead to a proof of Theorem A.

Theorem 3.1

(Boundary convergence classification)
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains. Then exactly one of the following holds:

(a)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z),\partial U_{n})>0
for all
z\in U
,
that is, all orbits stay away from the boundary;

(b)

there exists a subsequence
n_{k}\to\infty
for which
\lim_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n_{k}}(z),\partial U_{n_{k}})=0
for all
z\in U
, while for a different subsequence
m_{k}\to\infty
we have that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{m_{k}}(z),\partial U_{m_{k}})>0,\quad\text{for }z\in U;

(c)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z),\partial U_{n})=0
for all
z\in U
, that is, all orbits converge to the boundary.

Our proof of this result in
[
BEF+22
]
in the special case that
U
is a wandering domain of a holomorphic function
f
with
F_{n}=f^{n}
depends on the following lemma
[
BEF+22
, Lemma 4.1]
, relating hyperbolic density
\rho_{U}
to hyperbolic distance in a simply connected domain
U
. Hence the original proof of Theorem
3.1
for simply connected wandering domains also works in our general setting.

Lemma 3.2

(Estimate of hyperbolic quantities)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{C}
be a simply connected domain. Then, for all
z,z^{\prime}\in U
,

\exp(-2\operatorname{dist}_{U}(z,z^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\rho_{U}(z^{\prime})}{\rho_{U}(z)}\leq\exp(2\operatorname{dist}_{U}(z,z^{\prime})).

Theorem A strengthens part (c) of the boundary convergence classification by showing that all orbits converge to the boundary with the same limiting behaviour, and its proof is also based on Lemma
3.2
. It is useful here to state a more precise result than Theorem A.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains and let
z,z_{0}\in U
with
d=\operatorname{dist}_{U}(z,z_{0})
. Then

|F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(z_{0})|\leq 2de^{2d}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n}),\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.1)

Therefore, if we also have

\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then, for all
z\in U
,

|F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(z_{0})|\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Let
z,z_{0}
and
d
be as in the statement and let

\delta_{n}:=\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n}),\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

By the standard estimates of the hyperbolic density on simply connected domains (see
[
CG93
, page 13]
or
[
BM07
, Theorem 8.6]
),

\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z_{0}))\geq\frac{1}{2\operatorname{dist}(F^{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})}=\frac{1}{2\delta_{n}},\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Hence, by Lemma
3.2
, for any hyperbolic disc
\Delta_{n}
of radius
d
centred at
F_{n}(z_{0})
we have

\rho_{U_{n}}(w)\geq\frac{e^{-2d}}{2\delta_{n}},\quad\text{for }w\in\Delta_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(3.2)

By the contracting property of the hyperbolic metric, we have
F_{n}(z)\in\overline{\Delta_{n}}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Now let
\gamma\subset\Delta_{n}
be a hyperbolic geodesic joining
F_{n}(z)
to
F_{n}(z_{0})
; then the Euclidean distance apart of these two points is bounded from above by the Euclidean length
{\ell_{\operatorname{Eucl}}}(\gamma)
. Since
\gamma
is a hyperbolic geodesic, we deduce from (
3.2
) that

d\geq\ell_{U_{n}}(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\rho_{U_{n}}(z)\,|dz|\geq\frac{e^{-2d}}{2\delta_{n}}{\ell_{\operatorname{Eucl}}}(\gamma),

so

|F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(z_{0})|\leq{\ell_{\operatorname{Eucl}}}(\gamma)\leq 2\delta_{n}de^{2d},\quad\text{for }n\in{\mathbb{N}},

which gives (
3.1
) and completes the proof.
∎

Remark
.

The matter of convergence to the boundary is somewhat delicate in that it is closely related to the shape of the domains
U_{n}
, and there may be situations where it is more appropriate to use an alternative definition. For example, if the domains shrink with Euclidean diameters tending to 0, then the Denjoy–Wolff set is automatically the whole of
\partial U
. This geometric problem was discussed briefly in
[
BEF+22
]
, where the following observation was made.

Recall that the Euclidean distance of a point
z
from the boundary of a hyperbolic domain
U
is closely related to the hyperbolic density at the point in the domain. Indeed, when
U_{n}
are simply connected domains, the standard estimates for hyperbolic density mentioned above imply that

\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z),\partial U_{n})\to 0\iff\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z))\to\infty.

The proof of the boundary convergence classification and of Theorem
3.3
use the hyperbolic densities
\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z))
and, as in
[
BEF+22
, Section 4]
, we point out that these results remain true if we replace
\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z))
by
a_{n}\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z))
, where the positive sequence
(a_{n})
is chosen in some way depending on the geometry of the domains
U_{n}
, and the Euclidean distances within
U_{n}
are also scaled appropriately. For example, if the domains
U_{n}
are shrinking, then it may make sense to say that
F_{n}(z)
converges to the boundary if
a_{n}\rho_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z))\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
where

a_{n}=\sup_{D}\{\operatorname{diam}D:D\;\text{is a disc contained in}\;U_{n}\}.

4
Fast convergence to the boundary

In this section we prove a general result, Theorem
4.1
, of which Theorem B
and Theorem C are special cases. This result shows that if orbits of points converge sufficiently quickly to the boundary, then the Denjoy–Wolff set has full measure, thus generalising the ADM dichotomy Theorem
1.2
, part (a).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension, and suppose that the domains
U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
all satisfy an
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition
for some
\frac{1}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1
and some
C(r),r>0,
independent of
n
.

Suppose that there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})^{\alpha}<\infty.

(4.1)

Then, for almost every
\zeta\in\partial U
,

|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(z_{0})|\to 0\;\text{ as $n\to\infty$},

(4.2)

and hence, in view of Theorem A, the Denjoy–Wolff set has full measure in
\partial U
.

Remarks
.

1. The estimate (
3.1
) in the statement of Theorem
3.3
shows that condition (
4.1
) is independent of the choice of
z_{0}\in U
.

2. If we replaced (
4.1
) by the hypothesis that
\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then we would obtain the same result for any subsequence
(F_{n_{k}})
for which the corresponding series in (
4.1
) is convergent.

We give a formal definition of the
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition at the beginning of Subsection
4.1
. This is a geometric condition on the boundaries of the domains which determines the rate of convergence required in (
4.1
).

We shall see that a domain with
C^{2}
boundary, such as the unit disc, satisfies the
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition with
\alpha=1
. On the other hand, a general simply connected domain
U
, under no assumptions on the regularity of its boundary, satisfies an
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition with
\alpha=1/2
. Hence

(a)

Theorem
1.2
, part (a) is a special case of Theorem
4.1
, with
U=U_{n}=\mathbb{D}
and
F_{n}=f^{n}
, where
f
is an inner function, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(b)

Theorem B is a special case of Theorem
4.1
, with
U_{n}=\mathbb{D}
and
F_{n}:{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
holomorpic self maps of the disc, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(c)

Theorem C follows from Theorem
4.1
if we make no assumptions on the geometric nature of the domains and take
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
in (
4.1
).

In Section
4.3
we give an example which shows that, in one sense, Theorem
4.1
is best possible. For this example, where the domains are all cardioids, the conclusion holds if we take
\alpha=1/2
in (
4.1
), but does not hold for any larger value of
\alpha
.

4.1
Domains with the
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition

We begin with a formal definition and discussion of our geometric condition.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\frac{1}{2}\leq\alpha\leq 1
. A simply connected domain
U
satisfies an
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition
if there exists a function
C(r)>0
,
r>0
, such that, for every
\zeta\in\partial U
and
r>0
, we have that

\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)\leq C(r)|z-\zeta|^{\alpha},\;\text{ for }z\in U\cap D(\zeta,r),

(4.3)

where
V
is the component of
U\cap D(\zeta,r)
that contains
z
.

Remark
.

Observe that the left-hand side of (
4.3
) can be interpreted as the probability of a Brownian path in
U
starting at the point
z
first exiting
V
through
\partial V\cap U
, which tends to be larger or smaller according to the relative size of
\partial V\cap U
in
\partial V
. As shown by our Example
4.5
, the largest case for
\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)
occurs when the boundary has an external cusp at
\zeta
.

The following geometric criteria for satisfying the
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition are well known and we give only a brief indication of where proofs can be found.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assuming the notation of Definition
4.2
:

(a)

for any simply connected domain
U
and
\zeta\in\partial U
, we have

\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)\leq C(r)|z-\zeta|^{\frac{1}{2}},\;\text{ for }z\in V,

so we can take
\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}
;

(b)

if every
\zeta\in\partial U
is the vertex of a sector of angle
\beta
,
0<\beta\leq\pi
, and side-length
s>0
, contained in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U
, then

\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)\leq C(r)|z-\zeta|^{\frac{\pi}{2\pi-\beta}},\;\text{ for }z\in V,

where
C(r)
also depends on
\beta
and
s
, so we can take
\alpha=\pi/(2\pi-\beta)
;

(c)

if every
\zeta\in\partial U
lies on the boundary of some disc of radius
s>0
contained in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U
, then

\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)\leq C(r)|z-\zeta|,\;\text{ for }z\in V,

where
C(r)
also depends on
s
, so we can take
\alpha=1
.

Property (a) can be proved by applying the Milloux–Schmidt inequality
[
Hay89
, page 289]
to the subharmonic function

u(z):=\begin{cases}\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V),\quad z\in V,\\
0,\quad z\in D(\zeta,r)\setminus V;\end{cases}

we state a version of the Milloux–Schmidt inequality here, since we will use it again in Section 6.

Lemma 4.4

(Milloux–Schmidt inequality)
.

Suppose that
u
is subharmonic and continuous in
\{z:|z|\leq r\}
and that

\min_{|z|=\rho}u(z)=0,\;\;\text{for }0<\rho<r.

(4.4)

Then

u(z)\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\max_{|z|=r}u(z)\tan^{-1}(|z|/r)^{1/2},\;\;\text{for }0<|z|<r.

Lemma
4.4
shows that in Lemma
4.3
part (a) we can take
C(r)=4/(\pi r^{1/2})
.

Parts (b) and (c) of Lemma
4.3
can be proved by applying standard techniques such as conformal mappings and the maximum principle or Ahlfors’ distortion theorem. In fact, using Ahlfors’ distortion theorem the values of
\alpha
stated in (b) and (c) can be obtained with slightly smaller exterior domains than sectors and discs, respectively, expressed in terms of a so-called Dini condition; more details can be found in
[
Pom92
, Section 3.4]
and
[
Ahl10
, Chapter 3-3, p. 41]
.

Remark
.

Using results from
[
BT21
]
, it is possible to construct individual wandering domains which satisfy (sharply) any of the geometries described in Lemma
4.3
.

4.2
Proof of Theorem
4.1

We now prove our main result. The first part of the argument has its origins in the proof of
[
RS11
, Theorem 1.1]
but the very general context here, with maps not being surjective, introduces significant new challenges; see also
[
OS16
, Lemma 4.1]
for a related generalisation of
[
RS11
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We claim that we need only prove the result in the case that
U={\mathbb{D}}
and
z_{0}=0
. Indeed, if we know that the result holds for a sequence of the form
\hat{F}_{n}=F_{n}\circ\varphi_{U}:{\mathbb{D}}\to U_{n}
, where
\varphi_{U}
is a Riemann map from
{\mathbb{D}}
onto
U
with
\varphi_{U}(0)=z_{0}
, then the result holds for
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
since almost every
\xi\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
has the properties that the radial limit
\zeta:=\varphi_{U}(\xi)
exists and the full radial extension
F_{n}(\zeta)
exists (by hypothesis and the invariance of harmonic measure under a conformal map), so

F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(z_{0})=\hat{F}_{n}(\xi)-\hat{F}_{n}(0)\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for almost every
\xi\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and hence for almost every
\zeta\in\partial U
, with respect to harmonic measure.

So, from now on we assume that
U={\mathbb{D}}
and
z_{0}=0
. We have to show that for almost all
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
we have
F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(0)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\zeta_{n}
be a point in
\partial U_{n}
that satisfies

|F_{n}(0)-\zeta_{n}|=\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(0),\partial U_{n}).

By hypothesis,
F_{n}(0)-\zeta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

For every
r>0
, we define the sequence of open sets
S_{n}(r)=U_{n}\cap D(\zeta_{n},r)
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We then define the set
E(r)
of points
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
such that
F_{n}(\zeta)
eventually lies in
\overline{S_{n}(r)}
, that is,

E(r)=\bigcup_{N\geq 0}\bigcap_{n\geq N}\left(F_{n}^{-1}\left(\overline{S_{n}(r)}\right)\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}}\right).

Notice that by the definition of
S_{n}(r)
we have

|F_{n}(\zeta)-\zeta_{n}|\rightarrow 0\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$,\quad for }\zeta\in\bigcap_{r>0}E(r),

(4.5)

and, since the sets
E(r)
decrease as
r
decreases, for any sequence of radii
r_{k}\to 0
as
k\to 0
we have
\bigcap_{r>0}E(r)=\bigcap_{k\geq 1}E(r_{k})
. To prove our result it is sufficient to show that the latter set has full harmonic measure in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

First note that
\overline{U_{n}}\setminus\overline{S_{n}(r)}\subset\overline{U_{n}\setminus S_{n}(r)}
, so

\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus E(r)\subset\bigcap_{N\geq 0}\bigcup_{n\geq N}(F_{n}^{-1}(\overline{U_{n}\setminus S_{n}(r)})\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}}).

(4.6)

We shall use (
4.6
) to show that

\omega(0,\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus E(r),{\mathbb{D}})=0,\quad\text{for }r>0.

(4.7)

To prove this, we take
N(r)\in{\mathbb{N}}
so large that
F_{n}(0)\in S_{n}(r)
for all
n\geq N(r)
, and then for all
n\geq N(r)
we define the following sets, shown in Figure
2
:

•

V_{n}(r)
is the component of
S_{n}(r)=U_{n}\cap D(\zeta_{n},r)
that contains
F_{n}(0)
,

•

W_{n}(r)
is the component of
F_{n}^{-1}(V_{n}(r))\cap{\mathbb{D}}
that contains
0
, and

•

\Gamma_{n}(r):=\partial V_{n}(r)\cap U_{n}
.

Figure 1:

Illustration of the proof of Theorem
4.1
. The set
V_{n}(r)
and the connected component
W_{n}(r)
of its preimage which contains
0
are shaded (seagreen online). The arcs
\Gamma_{n}(r)
and some of their preimages are drawn thicker (pink online). Notice that there may also be preimages of
\Gamma_{n}(r)
in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, since iterates of points in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
may fall in the interior of
U_{n}
. Points in
{\mathbb{D}}\setminus W_{n}(r)
, which are shown in white, may be mapped under
F_{n}
to either
U_{n}\setminus V_{n}(r)
or
V_{n}(r)
, since the preimage of
V_{n}(r)
under
F_{n}
may have more than one connected component. Finally, recall that the points
(\zeta_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
do not form an orbit.

Note that
V_{n}(r)
and
W_{n}(r)
are both simply connected domains, by the maximum principle, and
\Gamma_{n}(r)
consists of arcs of
\partial D(\zeta_{n},r)
. Moreover,
W_{n}(r)
is a Jordan domain since the preimage of
\Gamma_{n}(r)
in
{\mathbb{D}}
consists of at most countably many curves that accumulate at no point of
{\mathbb{D}}
, and the ends of each such preimage curve consist of single points of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

The latter property follows from a theorem of G. MacLane
[
Mac63
, Theorem 1]
, applied here to the function
z\mapsto F_{n}(z)-\zeta_{n}
, which has finite radial limits almost everywhere on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. MacLane’s theorem states that the level curves of any function holomorphic in
{\mathbb{D}}
accumulate at singleton points of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
(rather than at arcs of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
) if and only if the function has asymptotic values at all points of a dense subset of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, which is certainly the case for the function
z\mapsto F_{n}(z)-\zeta_{n}
.

In particular,
W_{n}(r)
has the unique accessibility property and so the restricted map
F_{n}:W_{n}(r)\to V_{n}(r)
has full radial extension; see Proposition
2.2
.

We now apply Theorem
2.7
to the map
F_{n}:W_{n}(r)\to V_{n}(r)
to deduce that

\omega(0,F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n}(r))\cap\partial W_{n}(r),W_{n}(r))\leq\omega(F_{n}(0),\Gamma_{n}(r),V_{n}(r)).

(4.8)

Next we deduce from the fact that
\overline{V_{n}(r)}\subset\overline{S_{n}(r)}
that, for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

\omega(z,F_{n}^{-1}(\overline{U_{n}\setminus S_{n}(r)})\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(z,F_{n}^{-1}(\overline{U_{n}\setminus V_{n}(r)})\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}}).

(4.9)

Using the interpretation of harmonic measure as the exit distribution of Brownian motion, it is clear that, for all
z\in W_{n}(r)
, we have

\omega(z,F_{n}^{-1}(\overline{U_{n}\setminus V_{n}(r)})\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(z,F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n}(r))\cap\partial W_{n}(r),W_{n}(r)),

(4.10)

since any Brownian path in
{\mathbb{D}}
originating at 0 can only exit
{\mathbb{D}}
at a point of
F_{n}^{-1}(\overline{U_{n}\setminus V_{n}(r)})\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}}
by first exiting
W_{n}(r)
at a point of
F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n}(r))\cap\partial W_{n}(r)
.

Alternatively, we can apply the maximum principle in the Jordan domain
W_{n}(r)
to justify (
4.10
), by arguing that this inequality holds everywhere on
\partial W_{n}(r)
, apart from at most countably many points. Indeed, apart from the endpoints of the preimage curves of
\Gamma_{n}(r)
, these two bounded positive harmonic functions in
W_{n}(r)
have the same radial boundary extensions everywhere on
\partial W_{n}(r)\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and the positive harmonic function on the right takes the value 1 everywhere on
F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n}(r))\cap\partial W_{n}(r)=\partial W_{n}(r)\cap{\mathbb{D}}
.

Using the inequalities (
4.6
), (
4.9
), (
4.10
) and (
4.8
), in this order, we deduce that

\displaystyle\omega(0,\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus E(r),{\mathbb{D}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{N\geq N(r)}\sum_{n\geq N}\omega(0,F_{n}^{-1}\left(\overline{U_{n}\setminus S_{n}(r)}\right)\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})

(4.11)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{N\geq N(r)}\sum_{n\geq N}\omega(0,F_{n}^{-1}\left(\overline{U_{n}\setminus V_{n}(r)}\right)\cap\partial{\mathbb{D}},{\mathbb{D}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{N\geq N(r)}\sum_{n\geq N}\omega(0,F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n}(r))\cap\partial W_{n}(r),W_{n}(r))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{N\geq N(r)}\sum_{n\geq N}\omega(F_{n}(0),\Gamma_{n}(r),V_{n}(r)).

Since the domains
U_{n}
are all assumed to satisfy the
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition, we deduce from (
4.3
) that

\omega(F_{n}(0),\Gamma_{n}(r),V_{n}(r))\leq C(r)|F_{n}(0)-\zeta_{n}|^{\alpha},

for some positive function
C(r),r>0
.
Hence, by (
4.1
), the right-hand side of (
4.11
) is equal to 0 for every
r>0
and (
4.7
) follows.

Since a countable union of sets of zero measure has zero measure, we deduce from (
4.7
) that for any sequence of radii
r_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty

\omega(0,\bigcup_{k\geq 1}(\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus E(r_{k})),{\mathbb{D}})=0.

Therefore, by (
4.5
), we have
|F_{n}(\zeta)-\zeta_{n}|\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
for
\zeta
in a subset of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
of full harmonic measure, namely,
\bigcap_{k\geq 1}E(r_{k})
.

Since
|\zeta_{n}-F_{n}(0)|\rightarrow 0
by assumption, this implies that

|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(0)|\leq|F_{n}(\zeta)-\zeta_{n}|+|\zeta_{n}-F_{n}(0)|\rightarrow 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for
\zeta
in a set of full harmonic measure, as claimed.
∎

It can be seen from the proof of Theorem

satisfy an

(F_{n}(0))

4.3
Example of self-maps of a cardioid domain

We give an example which has the property that in Theorem
4.1
the exponent in condition (
4.1
) cannot be taken to be greater than
\frac{1}{2}
. We consider holomorphic self-maps of the domain

U=\varphi({\mathbb{D}}),\quad\text{where }\varphi(z)=(z-1)^{2},

which is the conformal image of
{\mathbb{D}}
under
\varphi
, meeting the real axis in the interval
\varphi((-1,1))=(0,4)
, with
\partial U
a cardioid having its inward pointing cusp at
\varphi(1)=0
.

By part (a) of Lemma
4.3
the domain
U
satisfies an
\alpha
-harmonic measure condition for
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
. On the other hand,
U
does not satisfy such a condition for any
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
. Indeed, we can check that the harmonic measure
\omega(z,\partial V\cap U,V)
, where
V=U\cap D(0,r),r>0
, satisfies

\omega(x,\partial V\cap U,V)\sim C(r)x^{1/2}\;\ \text{ as }x\to 0+,

for some constant
C(r)>0
, by using
\varphi^{-1}
to map this harmonic measure to a boundary neighbourhood of
1
in
{\mathbb{D}}
within which the corresponding harmonic measure behaves near
1
like a multiple of the distance to
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

Example 4.5
.

There exists a sequence of holomorphic maps
F_{n}:U\to U
such that

(a)

F_{n}(1)\to 0\in\partial U
as
n\to\infty
(note that
1=\varphi(0)\in U
);

(b)

\sum\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(1),\partial U)^{\alpha}\begin{cases}<\infty,\quad\text{for }\alpha>1/2,\\
=\infty,\quad\text{for }\alpha=1/2;\end{cases}

(c)

\{\zeta\in\partial U:F_{n}(\zeta)\to 0\;\text{as}\;n\to\infty\}
has measure 0; that is, the Denjoy–Wolff set has measure zero.

Proof.

Let
B_{n}
be the sequence of Blaschke products defined as
B_{n}(z)=M_{n}(z^{2^{n}})
, with
M_{n}(z)=\frac{z+1-1/{n}}{1+(1-1/n)z}
. We then have that

B_{n}(0)=1-\frac{1}{n}\rightarrow 1\;\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

(4.12)

and

B_{n}(\zeta)\nrightarrow 1\;\;\text{as }n\to\infty,\quad\text{for almost every }\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D},

(4.13)

where (
4.13
) follows by applying Example
8.3
with
a_{n}=1-1/n,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, according to which the orbit of almost every point
\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}
is dense in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

Now define the self-maps
F_{n}:U\rightarrow U
as

F_{n}=\varphi\circ B_{n}\circ\varphi^{-1}.

Then, by (
4.12
) and the fact that
\varphi(0)=1
, we have

F_{n}(1)=\varphi(B_{n}(0))=\varphi(1-1/n)=1/n^{2}\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

(4.14)

which gives property (a) and also property (b), since
\operatorname{dist}(F_{n}(1),\partial U)=1/n^{2}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Finally, property (c) holds since
F_{n}(\zeta)\nrightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
for almost all
\zeta\in\partial U
, by (
4.13
).
∎

Note that we can write the maps
F_{n}:U\to U
as a forward composition
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
, where
f_{n}=\varphi\circ b_{n}\circ\varphi^{-1}
and
b_{n}
are the maps given in Example
8.3
such that
B_{n}=b_{n}\circ\cdots\circ b_{1}
.

5
Empty Denjoy–Wolff sets – the proof of Theorem D

In this section we prove Theorem D. In the proof we need the following basic estimate for the mapping properties of Möbius transformations, which is also used in Section
8
.

Lemma 5.1

(Preimages under Möbius maps)
.

Consider a Möbius map of the form

M_{a}(z)=\frac{z+a}{1+az},\ 0<a<1,\ a=1-\varepsilon,

and let
T\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
be an arc
(e^{i\theta},e^{i\varphi})
, with
0<\theta<\varphi<2\pi
.
Then there exists
c(\theta,\varphi)>0
such that

|M_{a}^{-1}(T)|\sim c(\theta,\varphi)\varepsilon\ \ \text{ as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0$}.

(5.1)

Proof.

The proof is a direct computation. For
z,w\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
we have that

M_{a}^{-1}(z)-M_{a}^{-1}(w)=\frac{z-a}{1-az}-\frac{w-a}{1-aw}=\frac{(1-a^{2})(z-w)}{(1-az)(1-aw)},

so

\displaystyle|M_{a}^{-1}(e^{i\varphi})-M_{a}^{-1}(e^{i\theta})|

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-a^{2})|e^{i\varphi}-e^{i\theta}|}{|(1-ae^{i\varphi})(1-ae^{i\theta})|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon(2-\varepsilon)|e^{i\varphi}-e^{i\theta}|}{(|1-e^{i\varphi}|+O(\varepsilon))(|1-e^{i\theta}|+O(\varepsilon))}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2|e^{i\varphi}-e^{i\theta}|}{|(1-e^{i\varphi})(1-e^{i\theta})|}\right)\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})\;\text{ as }\varepsilon\rightarrow 0.

Since
|M_{a}^{-1}(T)|\sim|M_{a}^{-1}(e^{i\varphi})-M_{a}^{-1}(e^{i\theta})|
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem D.

Recall that

F_{n}(z)=\frac{\lambda_{n}z+a_{n}}{1+\lambda_{n}a_{n}z},\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}},

where
(a_{n})
is an increasing sequence in
[0,1)
, with
a_{0}=0
and
a_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, and
(\lambda_{n})
is a sequence in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
to be chosen. It is clear that
F_{n}(0)=a_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, whatever choice is made of
\lambda_{n}
, so

F_{n}(z)\to 1\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,\text{ for all }z\in{\mathbb{D}},

by Theorem A.

We now put
\varepsilon_{n}=1-a_{n}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and assume that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-a_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon_{n}=\infty.

(5.2)

Then we write

F_{n}(z)=B_{n}(\lambda_{n}z),\quad\text{where}\quad B_{n}(z)=\frac{z+a_{n}}{1+a_{n}z},\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Let
S=(e^{-i\theta},e^{i\theta})
be any arc, where
0<\theta<\pi/4
. We show how to choose
(\lambda_{n})
so that no
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
has an orbit that eventually lies in
S
. We have that
B_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})
is an arc in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
with centre
-1
and it follows by Lemma
5.1
that

|B_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})|\sim c(S)\varepsilon_{n}\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(5.3)

Therefore, for any choice of
(\lambda_{n})
, the preimage
F_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})
is an arc with centre
-\lambda_{n}^{-1}
and with
|F_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})|\sim c(S)\varepsilon_{n}
as
n\to\infty
. We introduce intervals
I_{n}=[\theta_{n},\theta_{n+1}],n=0,1,\dots
, such that
\theta_{0}=0
and

\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}=|B_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})|,\quad\text{so}\quad\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{n}=[0,\infty),

by (
5.2
) and (
5.3
).

Now, we choose
\lambda_{n}\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that

-\lambda_{n}^{-1}=e^{i(\theta_{n}+\theta_{n+1})/2}.

With this choice we know that if
\theta_{n}\leq\theta\leq\theta_{n+1}
, then

e^{i\theta}\in F_{n}^{-1}(S^{c}),\quad\text{so}\quad\lambda_{n}e^{i\theta}\in B_{n}^{-1}(S^{c})

and hence
B_{n}(\lambda_{n}e^{i\theta})\in S^{c}
. But any
\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}
(including
\zeta=1
) is of the form
\zeta=e^{i\varphi_{n}}
, where
\varphi_{n}\in I_{n}
for infinitely many
n
, so

F_{n}(\zeta)=B_{n}(\lambda_{n}e^{i\varphi_{n}})\in S^{c},\quad\text{for such }n.

Hence with this choice of
\lambda_{n}
we have
F_{n}(\zeta)\nrightarrow 1
as
n\to\infty
, as required.
∎

.

Blaschke products, where

as

6
Proof of Theorem E

In this section we prove Theorem E. The first part of the proof involves similar techniques to that of Theorem
4.1
, so we omit some of the details here.

First, we can assume that
\zeta_{0}\in\partial U_{0}
is accessible since
L_{0}
has positive harmonic measure. We claim we can assume that
U_{0}={\mathbb{D}}
. To see this, let
L^{\prime}_{0}=\varphi_{0}^{-1}(L_{0})
, where
\varphi_{0}:{\mathbb{D}}\to U_{0}
is a Riemann map. Then
L^{\prime}_{0}\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
has positive measure and the sequence
\hat{F}_{n}=F_{n}\circ\varphi_{0}:{\mathbb{D}}\to U_{n}
,

, satisfies
\hat{F}_{n}(\xi)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
\xi\in L^{\prime}_{0}
. Thus if the result holds for
\hat{F}_{n}:{\mathbb{D}}\to U_{n}
, that is,
\hat{F}_{n}(z)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
or equivalently
F_{n}(\varphi_{0}(z))-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, then
F_{n}(z)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
z\in U_{0}
, as required.

So, from now on we assume that
U={\mathbb{D}}
and
L_{0}\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
has positive measure. Let
\zeta_{n}:=F_{n}(\zeta_{0})
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so by hypothesis
\operatorname{dist}(\zeta_{n},\partial U_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
(possibly,
\operatorname{dist}(\zeta_{n},\partial U_{n})=0
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
if
\zeta_{n}\in\partial U_{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
). Then take any
z_{0}\in{\mathbb{D}}
and put
z_{n}=F_{n}(z_{0})\in U_{n}
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. To obtain our result it is sufficient, by Theorem A, to prove that

|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(6.1)

By the Severini–Egorov theorem (see
[
Roy88
, Chapter 3]
or
[
Tao11
, Theorem 1.3.26 and Exercise 1.4.31]
, for example), there is a compact set
E_{0}\subset L_{0}\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
such that

\omega(z_{0},E_{0},{\mathbb{D}})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}\omega(z_{0},L_{0},{\mathbb{D}})\;\text{ and }\;|F_{n}(\zeta)-\zeta_{n}|\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,\;\text{ uniformly for }\zeta\in E_{0}.

(6.2)

Hence there exists a positive sequence
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
such that

F_{n}(E_{0})\subset D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})\quad\text{and}\quad D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})\cap\partial U_{n}\neq\emptyset,\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(6.3)

We prove (
6.1
) by showing that, for some positive absolute constant
C>1
we have

z_{n}\in D(\zeta_{n},Cr_{n}),\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(6.4)

It is sufficient to consider only those values of
n
for which
z_{n}\notin D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})
.

For such
n
, we let
V_{n}
denote the component of
U_{n}\setminus\overline{D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})}
that contains
z_{n}
,
W_{n}
denote the component of
F_{n}^{-1}(V_{n})\subset{\mathbb{D}}
that contains
z_{0}
, and put
\Gamma_{n}=\partial V_{n}\cap U_{n}
. Note that
V_{n}
and
W_{n}
are both simply connected domains, by the maximum principle. Moreover, as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
,
W_{n}
is a Jordan domain since the part of the preimage of
\Gamma_{n}
that lies in
{\mathbb{D}}
consists of at most countably many curves that accumulate at no point of
{\mathbb{D}}
, and the ends of each such preimage curve consist of single points of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
.

We can now apply Theorem
2.7
to
F_{n}
in
W_{n}
to deduce that

\omega(z_{0},F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n})\cap\partial W_{n},W_{n})\leq\omega(z_{n},\Gamma_{n},V_{n}).

(6.5)

Next observe that
E_{0}
lies in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\partial W_{n}
, by the definitions of
L_{0}
,
V_{n}
and
W_{n}
. Therefore, since any Brownian path in
W_{n}
originating at
z\in W_{n}
can only exit
{\mathbb{D}}
at a point of
E_{0}
by first exiting
W_{n}
at a point of
\partial W_{n}\cap{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

\omega(z,E_{0},{\mathbb{D}})\leq\omega(z,F_{n}^{-1}(\Gamma_{n})\cap\partial W_{n},W_{n}),\;\text{ for }z\in W_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(6.6)

Alternatively, we can prove (
6.6
) by using the maximum principle, because the harmonic measure on the left has radial boundary extension 0 almost everywhere on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}\cap\partial W_{n}
(since
E_{0}\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}\setminus\partial W_{n}
) and the harmonic measure on the right takes the value 1 almost everywhere on
{\mathbb{D}}\cap\partial W_{n}
.

Now we estimate the right-hand side of (
6.5
) from above. To do this we choose an unbounded closed connected set
K_{n}
which lies in
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U_{n}
and contains a point in
D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})\cap\partial U_{n}
. This set exists since
U_{n}
is a simply connected proper subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Then let
\Omega_{n}
denote the component of the complement of
K_{n}\cup\overline{D(\zeta_{n},r_{n})}
that contains
z_{n}
, which is a simply connected domain such that
V_{n}\subset\Omega_{n}
and
\Gamma_{n}\subset\partial\Omega_{n}
. Then, by the maximum principle again, this time applied first in
V_{n}
and second in
\Omega_{n}
, we have

\omega(z,\Gamma_{n},V_{n})\leq\omega(z,\Gamma_{n},\Omega_{n})\leq\omega(z,\partial\Omega_{n}\cap U_{n},\Omega_{n}),\;\text{ for }z\in V_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(6.7)

In particular, (
6.7
) holds for
z=z_{n}
.

Now the right-hand function in (
6.7
) can be estimated from above by first mapping
\Omega_{n}
to a bounded domain using
w=\psi_{n}(z):=1/(z-\zeta_{n})
and then applying the Milloux-Schmidt inequality to the harmonic function

u(w)=\omega(\psi_{n}^{-1}(w),\partial\Omega_{n}\cap U_{n},\Omega_{n}),\quad w\in\psi_{n}(\Omega_{n}),n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Note that the domain
\psi_{n}(\Omega_{n})
lies in
D(0,R_{n})
, where
R_{n}:=r_{n}^{-1}
, and
u
vanishes on the part of the boundary of
\psi_{n}(\Omega_{n})
that lies in
D(0,R_{n})
. After extending
u
to be 0 throughout
D(0,R_{n})\setminus\psi_{n}(\Omega_{n})
and hence subharmonic in
D(0,R_{n})
, we deduce from the Milloux–Schmidt inequality, Lemma
4.4
, that for
n\in{\mathbb{N}},
we have

u(w)\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{|w|}{R_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{|w|}{R_{n}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\;\text{ for }w\in\psi_{n}(\Omega_{n}),

and in particular, taking
w=\psi_{n}(z_{n})
, that

\omega(z_{n},\partial\Omega_{n}\cap U_{n},\Omega_{n})=u(\psi_{n}(z_{n}))\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{R_{n}|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{4}{\pi}\left(\frac{r_{n}}{|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Combining this estimate with (
6.2
), (
6.5
), (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), we deduce that, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\omega(z_{0},L_{0},{\mathbb{D}})\leq 2\omega(z_{0},E_{0},{\mathbb{D}})\leq\frac{8}{\pi}\left(\frac{r_{n}}{|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad\text{so}\quad|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|\leq\frac{64r_{n}}{(\pi\omega(z_{0},L_{0},{\mathbb{D}}))^{2}}.

Since
r_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and
\omega(z_{0},L_{0},{\mathbb{D}})>0
by hypothesis, this proves (
6.4
), as required.

7
Classifying forward compositions and proof of Theorem F

In this section we focus on forward compositions of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains. We first classify such sequences in terms of hyperbolic distances, extending the classification of wandering domains given in
[
BEF+22
]
. We then prove a general result concerning contracting sequences which implies Theorem F. At the end of the section we give an example which shows that the conclusion of Theorem F does not hold if the hypothesis of contracting is omitted.

7.1
Hyperbolic classification

In
[
BEF+22
, Theorem A]
, we classified simply connected wandering domains into three distinct types:
contracting
,
semi-contracting
or
eventually isometric
, according to whether hyperbolic distances between orbits of points, for almost all points, tend to 0, decrease but do not tend to 0, or are eventually constant, respectively. In Section
1
of this paper we extended the definition of ‘contracting’ to the more general context of sequences of holomorphic maps
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
between simply connected domains; see Definition
1.4
.

In this section, we extend the concepts of ‘semi-contracting’ and ‘eventually isometric’ from wandering domains to sequences of holomorphic maps that can be expressed as forward compositions of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains; that is, sequences of the form
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
, where
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
are holomorphic maps.

We also give a criterion to discriminate between the three types of forward compositions based on the concept of
hyperbolic distortion

[
BM07
, Sect. 5,11]
.

Definition 7.1

(Hyperbolic distortion)
.

If
f:U\rightarrow V
is a holomorphic map between two hyperbolic domains
U
and
V
, then the
hyperbolic distortion
of
f
at
z
is

\|Df(z)\|_{U}^{V}:=\lim_{z^{\prime}\rightarrow z}\frac{\operatorname{dist}_{V}(f(z^{\prime}),f(z))}{\operatorname{dist}_{U}(z^{\prime},z)}=\frac{\rho_{V}(f(z))|f^{\prime}(z)|}{\rho_{U}(z)},

where
\rho_{U}(z)
denotes the hyperbolic density at
z\in U
.

Note that in the following result the holomorphic maps need not be surjections.

Theorem 7.2

(Hyperbolic classification theorem)
.

Let
U_{n},n\geq 0
be a sequence of simply connected domains (not necessarily distinct or disjoint) and
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n},n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of holomorphic maps. Define the forward compositions
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1},n\in{\mathbb{N}},
and the set

E=\{(z,z^{\prime})\in U_{0}\times U_{0}:F_{n}(z)=F_{n}(z^{\prime})\text{\ for some $n\in{\mathbb{N}}$}\}.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\lambda_{n}(z)
denote the hyperbolic distortion
\|Df_{n}(F_{n-1}(z))\|_{U_{n-1}}^{U_{n}}
.

Then exactly one of the following holds:

(1)

\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}c(z,z^{\prime})=0
for all
z,z^{\prime}\in U_{0}
, that is, the sequence
(F_{n})
is contracting on
U_{0}
; this case occurs if and only if
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\lambda_{n}(z))=\infty
.

(2)

\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}c(z,z^{\prime})>0
and
\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))\neq c(z,z^{\prime})

for all
(z,z^{\prime})\in(U_{0}\times U_{0})\setminus E
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, in which case we say that the sequence
(F_{n})
is semi-contracting on
U_{0}
.

(3)

There exists
N>0
such that for all
n\geq N
,
\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))=c(z,z^{\prime})>0
for all
(z,z^{\prime})\in(U\times U)\setminus E
, in which case we say that the sequence
(F_{n})
is eventually isometric on
U_{0}
; this case occurs if and only if
\lambda_{n}(z)=1
, for
n
sufficiently large.

By the Schwarz-Pick Lemma, the eventually isometric case occurs if and only if
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
is univalent for all sufficiently large
n
.

The proof of Theorem
7.2
is essentially identical to that of the corresponding result for simply connected wandering domains,
[
BEF+22
, Theorem A]
, depending as it does on using Riemann maps to reduce the situation to classifying non-autonomous systems of forward compositions of self-maps of the unit disc that fix the point 0, as discussed in
[
BEF+22
, Section 2]
.

The hyperbolic classification theorem does not hold in general for sequences of holomorphic maps
between simply connected domains that are not defined by forward composition, as the following example shows.

Example 7.3
.

Consider the sequence
(F_{n})
of degree 2 Blaschke products defined as follows. For
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

F_{n}(z):=z\varphi_{2/n}(\varphi_{1/2}(z)),\quad\text{where}\quad\varphi_{a}(z):=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline{a}z},\;\;a\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Then,
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathbb{D}}}(F_{n}(1/2),F_{n}(0))\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,
but

\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathbb{D}}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z^{\prime}))\nrightarrow 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,\;\text{ for all }z,z^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{D}},\;(z,z^{\prime})\neq(0,1/2).

(7.1)

Proof.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
F_{n}(0)=0,F_{n}(1/2)=-1/n
, so
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathbb{D}}}(F_{n}(1/2),F_{n}(0))\to 0\text{ as }n\to\infty,
and

F_{n}(z)=z\frac{\varphi_{1/2}(z)-2/n}{1-(2/n)\varphi_{1/2}(z)}\to z\varphi_{1/2}(z)\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,\;\;\text{for all }z\in{\mathbb{D}},

from which the property (
7.1
) readily follows.
∎

The form of the functions in Example
7.3
was suggested by work of P. Mercer (
[
Mer97
]
).

7.2
Proof of Theorem F

Theorem F is a special case of part (b) of the following result, obtained by including the assumption that the interior orbits converge to the boundary.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
F_{n}:U_{0}\to U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each having a full radial extension, with the additional hypothesis that

F_{n}(\zeta)\in\partial U_{n},\;\text{ for almost all }\zeta\in\partial U_{0}.

(7.2)

Also, suppose that
(F_{n})
is contracting in
U_{0}
.

(a)

If there are measurable subsets
L\subset\partial U_{0}
and
L_{n}\subset\partial U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
L=F_{n}^{-1}(L_{n})
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, up to a set of harmonic measure 0, then
L
has either full or zero harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
.

(b)

If in addition
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1},
where
f_{n}:U_{n-1}\to U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, are holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension, then for any
\zeta_{0}\in\partial U_{0}
the set

\{\zeta\in\partial U_{0}:|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})|\rightarrow 0\}

(7.3)

either has full or zero harmonic measure with respect to
U_{0}
.

For the proof of Theorem
7.4
we need the following ‘strong mixing’ result due to Pommerenke
[
Pom81
, Theorem 1]
.

Lemma 7.5

(Pommerenke)
.

Let
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of inner functions such that

G_{n}(0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad G_{n}(z)\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,\;\text{ for all }z\in{\mathbb{D}},

and suppose there are measurable subsets
E
and
E_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, of
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
such that
E=G_{n}^{-1}(E_{n})
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, up to a set of measure 0. Then, for all arcs
A\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, we have

|A\cap E|=\frac{1}{2\pi}|A|.|E|.

(7.4)

It follows that the set
E
has either full or zero Lebesgue measure.

Remark
.

The statement of Lemma
7.5
is different from that of
[
Pom81
, Theorem 1]
in several respects. First, for simplicity, we have normalised the inner functions to fix 0. Second, the proof of the identity (
7.4
) is obtained by following the proof of
[
Pom81
, Theorem 1]
while exchanging the roles of the sets
E_{n}
and
E
in that proof. Third, the final statement of Lemma
7.5
follows by applying (
7.4
) to a nested sequence of arcs converging to a point of density of
E
, when such a point exists.

Proof of Theorem
7.4
.

Let
z_{0}
be an arbitrary point in
U_{0}
and let
h_{n}
be a univalent map from
U_{n}
onto
U_{0}
with
h_{n}(F_{n}(z_{0}))=z_{0}
. Then the map
h_{n}\circ F_{n}
is a self-map of
U_{0}
fixing
z_{0}
(see Figure
2
). Now let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to U_{0}
be a Riemann map such that
\varphi(0)=z_{0}
. Accessible boundary points of
U_{0}
correspond to points of
\partial\mathbb{D}
where
\varphi
has radial limits (see Section
2.1
).

Figure 2:

Illustration of the proof of Theorem
7.4
. The sets
E_{n}
and
L_{n}
are represented as arcs for simplicity but are in fact just measurable.

For
n\geq 1
we define the map
G_{n}:{\mathbb{D}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
as

G_{n}=\varphi^{-1}\circ h_{n}\circ F_{n}\circ\varphi,

and note that
G_{n}(0)=0
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Since the sequence
(F_{n})
is contracting we have that
\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z_{0}))\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
z\in U_{0}
. Also, since
\varphi^{-1}\circ h_{n}:U_{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{D}}
is a conformal map, we have, for every
w\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{D}}(G_{n}(w),G_{n}(0))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{\mathbb{D}}(\varphi^{-1}\circ h_{n}\circ F_{n}\circ\varphi(w),\varphi^{-1}\circ h_{n}\circ F_{n}(z_{0}))

\displaystyle=\operatorname{dist}_{U_{n}}(F_{n}(\varphi(w)),F_{n}(z_{0}))\rightarrow 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

which means that
(G_{n})
is also contracting.

Next define the sets
E_{n}\subset\partial{\mathbb{D}}
as

E=\varphi^{-1}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad E_{n}=\varphi^{-1}(h_{n}(L_{n})),\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(7.5)

By (
7.2
) and (
7.5
) we have, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, up to a set of measure zero (by an argument similar to the one used in the proof of Proposition
2.3
), that

\displaystyle G_{n}^{-1}(E_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}\circ F_{n}^{-1}\circ h_{n}^{-1}\circ\varphi(E_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}\circ F_{n}^{-1}\circ h_{n}^{-1}\circ h_{n}(L_{n})

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}(L)=E.

Suppose now that
L
has positive harmonic measure in
U_{0}
, so
E
has positive Lebesgue measure in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. Since
F_{n}
maps
\partial U_{0}
to
\partial U_{n}
,
G_{n}
is an inner function for every
n
.
We can now apply Lemma
7.5
to the sequence
(G_{n})
and deduce that
|E|=2\pi
, and so
L\subset\partial U_{0}
has full harmonic measure.

For part (b), let
L
be the set in (
7.3
), and assume that for some
\zeta_{0}\in\partial U_{0}
the set
L
has positive harmonic measure in
U_{0}
. Then define the set
L_{n}\subset\partial U_{n}
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, as

L_{n}:=\{\zeta\in\partial U_{n}:|f_{n+m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{n+1}(\zeta)-F_{m+n}(\zeta_{0})|\to 0\;\text{ as }m\to\infty\}.

(7.6)

By definition,

L=\{\zeta\in\partial U_{0}:|F_{n}(\zeta)-F_{n}(\zeta_{0})|\rightarrow 0\}=F_{n}^{-1}(L_{n}),\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}},

(7.7)

up to a set of harmonic measure 0, so we can apply part (a) of the theorem to deduce that
L
has full harmonic measure in
U_{0}
.
∎

7.3
An example related to Theorem F

The following example shows that Theorem F (and Theorem
7.4
) fail without the hypothesis which requires the sequence of maps to be contracting. The example consists of a sequence of Möbius self-maps
M_{n}
of
{\mathbb{D}}
, and hence it can also be viewed as a forward composition
M_{n}=m_{n}\circ\cdots\circ m_{1}
of Möbius self-maps by defining
m_{n}=M_{n}\circ M^{-1}_{n-1}
.

Example 7.6
.

There exists a sequence
(M_{n})
of Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
such that
M_{n}(1)=1
for all
n
, and

(a)

M_{n}(z)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, for all
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
;

(b)

M_{n}(\zeta)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, for all
\zeta=e^{i\theta}
,
|\theta|<\pi/2
; but

(c)

M_{n}(\zeta)\not\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, for all
\zeta=e^{i\theta}
,
\pi/2\leq|\theta|\leq\pi
.

Proof.

We define the maps first on the right half-plane. For points
\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{H}
and
a>1
we shall consider affine self-maps of
\mathbb{H}
of the form

\widehat{M}(z;\zeta,a)=a(z-\zeta)+\zeta.

Clearly
\widehat{M}(\infty)=\infty
,
\widehat{M}(\zeta)=\zeta
.

For
j=1,2,\ldots
we divide the segment
[-i,i]
in the imaginary axis into
j
segments
I_{j,k}
,
k=0,\ldots,j-1
, each of length
2/j
, that is

I_{j,k}=\left[-1+\frac{2k}{j},-1+\frac{2(k+1)}{j}\right]i,\quad k=0,\ldots,j-1,

with midpoint

\zeta_{j,k}=\left(-1+\frac{2k+1}{j}\right)i.

Hence, for all
k=0,\ldots,j-1
,

\left|I_{j,k}\right|=\frac{2}{j}\text{ \ \ and \ \ }\bigcup_{k=0}^{j-1}I_{j,k}=[-i,i].

Now, for all
k=0,\ldots,j-1
, we define the maps

\widehat{M}_{j,k}(z)=\widehat{M}(z;\zeta_{j,k},j)=j(z-\zeta_{j,k})+\zeta_{j,k},\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{H}}.

Then, on the one hand, for
\zeta\in I_{j,k}
we have

|\widehat{M}_{j,k}(\zeta)|\leq j|\zeta-\zeta_{j,k}|+1\leq j(1/j)+1=2,

(7.8)

and on the other hand, for all
|z|>1
and since
|\zeta_{j,k}|<1
,

|\widehat{M}_{j,k}(z)|\geq j|z-\zeta_{j,k}|-1\geq j(|z|-1)-1,\quad 0\leq k\leq j-1,j=1,2\ldots.

(7.9)

Now we arrange the maps
\widehat{M}_{j,k}
into a single sequence
\widehat{M}_{n}=\widehat{M}_{j,k}
, where
n=\frac{1}{2}j(j+1)+k
,
0\leq k\leq j-1
,
j=1,2\ldots
, and observe that for all
z\in\overline{{\mathbb{H}}}
with
|z|>1
we have that
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, by (
7.9
). On the other hand, every
z\in[-i,i]
belongs to an infinite number of segments
I_{j,k}
, so there exists an infinite number of distinct values of
n
for which
|\widehat{M}_{n}(z)|<2
by (
7.8
)and therefore
\widehat{M}_{n}(z)\not\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.

Finally, we define the corresponding maps on the closed unit disc by
M_{n}(z)=\beta^{-1}\circ\widehat{M}_{n}\circ\beta(z)
, where
\beta:{\mathbb{D}}\to\mathbb{H}
is the Möbius map such that
\beta(1)=\infty
,
\beta(-1)=0
and
\beta(\pm i)=\pm i
. By the discussion above, properties (b) and (c) are immediate. Property (a) follows immediately from Theorem A, for example.
∎

8
Examples related to the ADM dichotomy

In this section we give three examples related to Theorem
1.2
, part (b). Recall that this states that for an inner function
f:{\mathbb{D}}\to{\mathbb{D}}
with a Denjoy–Wolff point and such that
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-|f^{n}(z)|)=\infty
, for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
, almost all boundary orbits of
f
are dense in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
. Here we show that this result fails to hold in general if iterates of inner functions are replaced by forward compositions of inner functions.

In Section
8.1
, we give two examples which show this failure: the first consists of forward compositions of Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
and is therefore an isometric sequence in the sense of Theorem
7.2
, and the second consists of forward compositions of Blaschke products of degree 2, which turns out to be a semi-contracting sequence. Then, in Section
8.2
we give an example of a forward composition of distinct Blaschke products of degree 2 which is contracting and for which the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
, part (b) does hold – the techniques used in the proof of this example have some independent interest.

These examples raise the question of whether part (b) of the ADM dichotomy holds for
contracting
forward compositions of inner functions (or indeed for more general contracting sequences), which we discuss at the end of this section.

8.1
Forward compositions for which Theorem
1.2
, part (b) fails

Our first example is a sequence of Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
.

Example 8.1
.

Let
(a_{n})
be an increasing sequence on
[0,1)
such that
a_{0}=0
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=1
, let
m_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, be the Möbius map such that

and
m_{n}(a_{n-1})=a_{n}
, and define

, that is,

Then, every point

satisfies that
\lim_{n\to\infty}M_{n}(z)=1

converges or not.

Proof.

We consider the cross-ratio

\frac{(z_{1}-z_{3})(z_{4}-z_{2})}{(z_{3}-z_{2})(z_{1}-z_{4})},

which is invariant under Möbius transformations. Take
z_{1}=1,z_{2}=-1,z_{3}=0,z_{4}=\zeta\in\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\{\pm 1\}.
Then the invariance under
M_{n}
gives

0\neq\frac{(1-0)(\zeta+1)}{(0+1)(1-\zeta)}=\frac{(1-a_{n})(M_{n}(\zeta)+1)}{(a_{n}+1)(1-M_{n}(\zeta))}.

Since

we have
\frac{1-a_{n}}{a_{n}+1}\to 0
as

, so

\frac{1-M_{n}(\zeta)}{M_{n}(\zeta)+1}\to 0\quad\text{as}\;n\to\infty,

as required.
∎

As announced, a similar effect to that in Example

can be achieved with a forward composition of Blaschke products

of degree two. In this example,

as

in

and the summability condition of Theorem

Example 8.2
.

There exists a sequence of degree two Blaschke products
(b_{n})
of the form

(8.1)

where

is a decreasing sequence such that
\mu_{n}\to 1/3
as

, such that

(a)

satisfies
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-B_{n}(0))=\infty,

(b)

\lim_{n\to\infty}B_{n}(z)=1
, for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}\cup\{\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}:|\zeta-1|<\rho\},
for some
\rho>0.

Proof.

First note that the map
z\mapsto\left(\frac{z+\mu}{1+\mu z}\right)^{2}
has an attracting fixed point at 1 for
\mu\in(1/3,1)
and a parabolic fixed point at 1 for
\mu=1/3
. It is convenient to construct the sequence
(\mu_{n})
by making a change of variables to the right half-plane
\mathbb{H}=\{z:\operatorname{Re}z>0\}
via the map
\alpha(z)=\frac{1+z}{1-z}
.

Now
z\mapsto\frac{z+\mu_{n}}{1+\mu_{n}z}
is conjugate to
z\mapsto\lambda_{n}z
under
\alpha
, where
\lambda_{n}=\frac{1+\mu_{n}}{1-\mu_{n}}
and we require
(\lambda_{n})
to be a decreasing sequence tending to 2 as
n\to\infty
. Also
z\mapsto z^{2}
is conjugate to
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(z+1/z)
under
\alpha
. We deduce that
b_{n}
in
{\mathbb{D}}
is conjugate to the map
z\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{n}z+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}z})
in
{\mathbb{H}}
. Abusing notation, we shall denote this map also by
b_{n}
, and also write
B_{n}=b_{n}\circ\cdots\circ b_{1}
in
{\mathbb{H}}
. The sum in statement (a) now becomes

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|1-\alpha^{-1}(B_{n}(1))\right|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{B_{n}(1)+1},

which diverges if and only if
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/B_{n}(1)
diverges.

We choose
(\lambda_{n})
in such a way that
B_{n}(1)=n+1
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so that this divergence is assured. To achieve this we need

B_{n}(1)=b_{n}(n)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{n}n+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}n}\right)=n+1,

(8.2)

so we define

\lambda_{n}:=\frac{n+1+\sqrt{n^{2}+2n}}{n}=1+\frac{1}{n}+\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)^{1/2}=2\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)+O(1/n^{2})\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(8.3)

We have
B_{n}(z)\to\infty
for all
z
in
\mathbb{H}
, by Theorem A, since this property holds for
z=1
. So to prove (b) we need to show that, for all sufficiently large

:

B_{n}(iy)\to\infty\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

(8.4)

Now

b_{n}(iy)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{n}iy+\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}iy}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{n}iy-\frac{i}{\lambda_{n}y}\right)=:i\beta_{n}(y),

(8.5)

say. By symmetry we need only to consider
y>0
. It is easy to check that the maps
y\mapsto\beta_{n}(y)
have a unique positive fixed point
y^{*}_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{n}+o(1)
and, if
y>y_{n}^{*}
then
\beta_{n}(y)>y
. Hence we choose to check that if
y_{n}>n^{\frac{3}{4}}
, for some

, say, then
y_{n+1}=\beta_{n}(y_{n})>(n+1)^{\frac{3}{4}}
also.

We have

\displaystyle y_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}(y_{n})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{n}n^{3/4}-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}n^{3/4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((2(1+1/n)+O(1/n^{2}))n^{3/4}-\frac{1}{(2(1+1/n)+O(1/n^{2}))n^{3/4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{n^{1/4}}+O(1/n^{5/4})-\frac{n^{1/4}}{4(n+1+O(1/n))}\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

We claim that for
n
large enough,
y_{n+1}>(n+1)^{3/4}.
This holds since

\frac{n+1}{n^{1/4}}-(n+1)^{3/4}>\frac{n^{1/4}}{4(n+1+O(1/n))}+O(1/n^{5/4}),

that is,

\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{1/4}-1>\frac{n^{1/4}}{4(n+1)^{3/4}(n+1+O(1/n))}+O\left(1/n^{2}\right),

which follows from

\frac{1}{4n}>\frac{n^{1/4}}{4(n+1)^{7/4}+O(1/n^{1/4})}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right),

true for
n
large enough. Hence there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

implies that
y_{n}>n^{3/4}
, for
n\geq N
. We deduce from this and from (
8.5
) that, for all

,

and so (

) holds if we relabel the

starting with

.
∎

We need some preliminaries before we can prove Example

, part (b).

Lemma 8.4

(Borel–Cantelli)
.

If

are measurable subsets of
[0,1]
such that
\sum|E_{n}|=\infty
and

|E_{m}\cap E_{n}|\leq C|E_{m}|.|E_{n}|,\;\text{ for }n>m\geq L,

where
C\geq 1
and
L\in\mathbb{N}
, then

|\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}|=|\{x\in[0,1]:x\in E_{n}\text{ \rm{infinitely often}}\}|=\left|\bigcap_{N\geq 0}\bigcup_{n\geq N}E_{n}\right|>0.

Lemma
8.4
is due to Ciesielski and Taylor
[
CT62
]
and independently to Lamperti
[
Lam63
]
, whose proof is admirably short. Another version is due to Petrov
[
Pet02
]
, which has the same hypotheses but the stronger conclusion that
|\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{n}|\geq 1/C
. Closely related results of this type are due Kochen and Stone
[
KS64
]
, and Yan
[
Yan06
]
.

We use Lemma
8.4
to prove a ‘shrinking target’ result. This description of a result that concerns the size of the set of points where a dynamical system visits infinitely often a sequence of balls whose size shrinks to 0 with

seems to originate with Hill and Velani
[

]
in the context of metric Diophantine approximation.

Lemma 8.5

8.3
Part (b) of the ADM dichotomy

Earlier in this section we showed that part (b) of the ADM dichotomy does not hold in general in the setting of forward compositions, at least in the isometric and semi-contracting cases. This leaves open the possibility that it holds in the contracting case.

To provide some evidence that this may be the case (apart from our failure to produce a contracting counter-example!), we outline briefly how to prove Theorem
1.2
, part (b) by using results of Aaronson, Doering and Mañé, and Neuwirth, most of which appear in the text
[
Aar97
]
; see also
[
BFJK20a
, Section 2]
for a convenient summary of the concepts from ergodic theory mentioned here and the relationships amongst them.

All the steps in the proof concern the behaviour of the inner function
f
on the boundary of
{\mathbb{D}}
. (Recall that an inner function is defined at almost all boundary points.)

•

the condition that
\sum_{n\geq 0}(1-|f^{n}(z)|)=\infty
for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
implies that
f
is conservative and hence ergodic on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, by
[
Aar97
, Propositions 6.1.7 and 6.1.8]
;

•

since
f
is an inner function it is non-singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, by
[
Aar97
, Proposition 6.1.1]
;

•

hence
f
is non-singular, conservative and ergodic on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, which implies, by
[
Aar97
, Proposition 1.2.2]
, that for any set
E
of positive measure in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, the iterates
f^{n}(\zeta)
visit
E
infinitely often for almost every
\zeta\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
, a strong recurrence property which implies the density of orbits property stated in Theorem
1.2
, part (b).

The reader may wish to compare these arguments to those in the proof of
[
BFJK20a
, Theorem C]
which uses a slightly different path to arrive at the same conclusion.

The relevance of the proof outlined above to our question about contracting sequences is as follows. We see from the first step in the proof that, for an inner function
f
, the divergence condition given in Theorem
1.2
, part (b) implies the ergodic property, and we know from
[
DM91
, Theorem 3.1]
that the iterates of an inner function which is ergodic on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
form a contracting sequence in the disc.

So, it is reasonable to ask whether some version of part (b) of the ADM dichotomy holds for
any
contracting composition (or indeed sequence) of holomorphic functions between simply connected domains. However, concepts like invariance, ergodicity, recurrence and density of orbits only make sense when the domains
U_{n}
are all identical so that boundary points can be identified. In particular, these concepts do not make sense for wandering domains.

We can, however, ask whether a weaker version of part (b) of the dichotomy holds, with a different type of proof. More precisely, suppose that
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
is a contracting forward composition (or perhaps sequence) of holomorphic functions between simply connected domains for which interior orbits converge to the boundary sufficiently slowly (in some precise sense that takes the geometry of the domains into account). Then it is plausible that the Denjoy–Wolff set has measure zero.

To prove such a result, possible tools include versions of the
second
Borel–Cantelli lemma (such as the one used to prove Example
8.3
) and Löwner’s lemma (the case of equality, mentioned in the remark following Lemma
2.6
), together with Pommerenke’s strong boundary mixing result
[
Pom81
, Theorem 1]
in the case of forward compositions. Such an approach would complement our proof of the generalisation of part (a) of the ADM dichotomy, where we used the Löwner’s lemma inequality, together with an argument similar to that used to prove the
first
Borel–Cantelli lemma. The Borel–Cantelli lemmas have a dichotomy similar to Theorem
1.2
, in which convergent and divergent series lead to sets of zero measure or positive measure, respectively, and in that sense they are a natural tool to attack this problem.

9
Versions of results with the spherical metric

It is natural to ask to what extent our results hold when the Euclidean metric for measuring distances to the boundary of a domain is replaced by the spherical metric. Using the spherical metric has the advantage that sequences tending to
\infty
are included but the disadvantage that points may be close together in the spherical metric but far apart in the Euclidean metric. As an illustration, consider the sequence

F_{n}(z)=M_{n}(z)+n,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},\;n=1,2,\ldots,

where
(M_{n})
is the sequence of Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
in Example
7.6
. In this case, the spherical distances between all pairs of points of
\overline{F_{n}({\mathbb{D}})}
tends to 0 as
n\to\infty
, but the subtle behaviour of
F_{n}
on
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
is only seen when using the Euclidean metric.

Also, our hypotheses on the functions
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
are so general that Theorem A does not hold when expressed in terms of the spherical metric. For example, sequences of the form

F_{n}(z)=C_{n}B(z)+z,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},\;n=1,2,\ldots,

(9.1)

where
B
is a Blaschke product,
C_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
U={\mathbb{D}}
and
U_{n}=\{z:|z|<|C_{n}|+1\}
, and ones of the form

F_{n}(z)=(2z)^{p_{n}},\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}},\;n=1,2,\ldots,

(9.2)

where
p_{n}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
p_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
U={\mathbb{D}}
and
U_{n}=\{z:|z|<2^{p_{n}}\}
, show that we can have
F_{n}(z)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
for some
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
but not all. However, it follows easily from the Euclidean version of Theorem A that with the extra hypothesis of normality we have the following spherical version; here we denote spherical distance by
\chi
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains and suppose that
(F_{n})
is normal in
U
. If there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that

\chi(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then, for all
z\in U
,

\chi(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(z_{0}))\to 0\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

The role of the normality hypothesis in Theorem
9.1
is to ensure that if
\chi(F_{n_{k}}(z_{0}),\infty)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
for any subsequence
(F_{n_{k}})
, then
\chi(F_{n_{k}}(z),\infty)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
for all
z\in U
.

Next we state a spherical version of Theorem C in which the assumption of normality is not required.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension to
\partial U
, and suppose that there exists
z_{0}\in U
such that

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{dist_{\chi}}(F_{n}(z_{0}),\partial U_{n})^{1/2}<\infty.

(9.3)

Then for almost all points
\zeta\in\partial U
we have

\chi(F_{n}(\zeta),F_{n}(z_{0}))\to 0\;\;\text{as}\;n\to\infty.

Remarks
.

1.

Examples of the form (
9.1
), with
C_{n}=n^{3}
for instance, show that in the spherical setting the hypothesis (
9.3
) is not independent of
z_{0}\in U
, in contrast to the independence of condition (
1.3
) in Theorem C; see the first remark after Theorem
4.1
.

2.

A special case of Theorem
9.2
can be found in
[
RS11
, remark after Theorem 1.1]
, where it is observed that if
U
is a Baker domain of an entire function
f
such that for some
z_{0}\in U
and all sufficiently large
n
we have

|f^{n+1}(z_{0})|\geq k|f^{n}(z_{0})|,\;\text{where }k>1,

(9.4)

then, for almost all
\zeta\in\partial U
, we have

f^{n}(\zeta)\to\infty\;\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Indeed, since for a point
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have that
\operatorname{dist}_{\chi}(z,\infty)\sim 1/|z|
, the assumption (
9.4
) implies that the series in (
9.3
) is convergent by comparing it to a geometric series. Similarly, by observing for example that the
p
-series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{p}}<\infty
for
p>1
, we obtain that the claim of Theorem
9.2
holds whenever for some
z_{0}\in U
and all sufficiently large
n
we have
|f^{n}(z_{0})|\geq n^{2p}
for
p>1
, or analogously,

|f^{n+1}(z_{0})|\geq\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{2p}|f^{n}(z_{0})|,\;\text{where }p>1.

(9.5)

There is also a spherical version of Theorem E in which we again need an extra hypothesis, one that implies normality.

Theorem 9.3
.

Let
F_{n}:U\to U_{n}
be a sequence of holomorphic maps between simply connected domains, each with a full radial extension, and suppose that there exists
R>0
such that

(\hat{\mathbb{C}}\setminus U_{n})\cap\{z:|z|=R\}\neq\emptyset,\;\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

(9.6)

If there exists
\zeta_{0}\in\partial U
such that
\chi(F_{n}(\zeta_{0}),\partial U_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and

L_{0}:=\{\zeta\in\partial U:\chi(F_{n}(\zeta),F_{n}(\zeta_{0}))\to 0\;\;\text{as }n\to\infty\}

has positive harmonic measure with respect to
U
, then, for all
z\in U
, we have

\chi(F_{n}(z),F_{n}(\zeta_{0}))\to 0\;\;\text{as }n\to\infty.

The extra hypothesis (
9.6
) ensures that each point of
\partial U_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, lies in a continuum that is exterior to
U_{n}
and contains a point of
\{z:|z|\leq R\}
. This continuum is required because replacing the Euclidean metric by the spherical metric means that the spherical discs needed in the proof sometimes contain
\infty
, and in this circumstance the condition (
9.6
) enables us to replace the continuum to
\infty
by a continuum to
\{z:|z|=R\}
in the final stage of the proof where we apply the Milloux–Schmidt inequality; we omit the details.

Note that the condition (
9.6
) implies that
(F_{n})
forms a normal family by using a form of Montel’s theorem due to Carathéodory and Landau; see
[
Car54
, p. 202]
.

Finally, Theorem F holds with no modifications when the Euclidean metric is replaced by the spherical metric, since the deduction of part (b) of Theorem
7.4
from part (a) is essentially unchanged in the spherical setting.

Remark
.

As this paper was being completed we learnt of very interesting recent work by Martí-Pete, Rempe and Waterman in
[
MPRW22
]
. Of relevance to our current paper,
[
MPRW22
, Theorem 1.10]
concerns the dynamical behaviour of boundary points of a wandering domain
U
of an entire function. They prove that the set of points
z\in\partial U
such that
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\chi(f^{n}(z),f^{n}(z_{0}))>0
, where
z_{0}\in U
, which they call
maverick points
, forms a set of harmonic measure zero. In the setting of wandering domains and the spherical metric, their result is stronger than the results given in this section, though the results in this section hold in our more general setting. Our results in earlier sections capture the different possible behaviours of orbits of boundary points tending to infinity.

10
Open questions

In this final section we discuss several interesting questions, which arise in connection with our new results. The first relates to a possible generalisation of the ADM dichotomy, part (b), discussed in Section
8.3
. Recall that the Denjoy–Wolff set is only defined if interior orbits converge to the boundary, and that it follows from Theorem A that, in this case, all interior orbits have the same limiting behaviour. For simplicity, the question is stated for inner functions; in a more general version, the geometry of the domain’s boundary would play a role.

Question 10.1
.

(a) Must the Denjoy–Wolff set have zero measure for any contracting forward composition of inner functions
F_{n}=f_{n}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that interior orbits converge to the boundary sufficiently slowly that
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}(1-|F_{n}(0)|)
is divergent?

(b) More generally, we can ask this question for contracting sequences
(F_{n})
of inner functions such that interior orbits converge to the boundary sufficiently slowly that this series is divergent.

Including the contracting hypothesis in Question
10.1
is in some sense natural since for iteration of a single inner function the divergence condition implies the contracting property; see the discussion in Section
8.3
.

If the answer to Question
10.1
, part (b) does turn out to be ‘yes’, this would imply that a version of Theorem F holds for sequences of inner functions, not just forward compositions of them, since if the sum is convergent we can apply Theorem B, which gives full measure for the Denjoy–Wolff set.

Next we have a couple of questions relating specifically to our examples. In connection with Theorem D, concerning a sequence of Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
with empty Denjoy–Wolff set, the following question arises.

Question 10.2
.

Does there exist an example of a forward composition of non-Möbius self-maps of
{\mathbb{D}}
for which interior orbits converge to the boundary and the Denjoy–Wolff set is empty? Similarly, does there exist an orbit of wandering domains, univalent or not, for which the interior orbits converge to the boundary and the Denjoy–Wolff set is empty?

Another of our examples suggests a further question.

Question 10.3
.

In Example
8.2
, in which an entire boundary neighbourhood of 1 converges to 1, can we deduce that the set of boundary points which converge to 1 is dense in
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
or even has full measure there?

Finally, we learnt the following related and interesting question from Marco Abate: it is known that there is no straightforward generalisation of the Denjoy–Wolff theorem from
{\mathbb{D}}
to a general simply connected domain
U
; see
[
Mil06
, Problem 5-a]
for an example of a comb domain with non-locally connected boundary within which orbits of a univalent self-map accumulate at a continuum in the boundary.

Question 10.4
.

Let
f
be a holomorphic self-map of a simply connected domain
U
. What non-trivial conditions on
f
and
U
are sufficient to ensure that a Denjoy–Wolff point exists in
\overline{U}
for
f
?

First note that our Theorem A implies that if
f
is a holomorphic self-map of a simply connected domain
U
and one orbit converges to a point
p\in\partial U
, then all others must do the same and hence
p
is the unique Denjoy–Wolff point.

A trivial sufficient condition for Question
10.4
is that
U
is a Jordan domain, and one might conjecture that if
f
has a full radial extension to
\partial U
, possibly mapping
\partial U
to itself almost everywhere, then this property would also be sufficient. However, it is straightforward to modify the example in
[
Mil06
, Problem 5-a]
, replacing the teeth of the comb by thin triangles, to ensure that the univalent self-map has a full radial extension. Moreover, the univalent self-map of such a domain
U
can be replaced by a self-map of the domain that is conjugate via a Riemann map to a degree 2 hyperbolic Blaschke product with an attracting fixed point on the boundary, and within
U
orbits under this self-map also accumulate at a continuum.

Remark
.

There are versions of the Denjoy–Wolff theorem for self-maps of hyperbolic Riemann surfaces in which the boundary limit set is in general a single point or a continuum; see
[
Hei41
]
and
[
Hei88
]
. See also
[
CS21
]
for results concerning the stability of the Denjoy–Wolff theorem under small perturbations of a holomorphic self-map of
{\mathbb{D}}
and
[
AC22
]
for generalisations of such results to self-maps of hyperbolic Riemann surfaces.
