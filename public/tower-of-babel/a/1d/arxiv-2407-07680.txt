Residual finiteness and discrete subgroups of Lie groups Dedicated to the memory of Nicolas Bergeron
Matthew Stover

Abstract
Let G G be a real Lie group and Γ < G \Gamma<G be a discrete subgroup of G G . Is Γ \Gamma residually finite? This paper describes known positive and negative results then poses some questions whose answers will lead to a fairly complete answer for lattices.



1
Definitions and first examples

A group
\Gamma
is
residually finite
if for every nontrivial
\gamma\in\Gamma
there is a homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to F
onto a finite group
F
so that
\rho(\gamma)
is nontrivial. The applications of residual finiteness in algebra, number theory, geometry, and topology are abundant and significant. Just to give an example, there is
Scott’s criterion

[
37
, Lem. 1.3]
:

If
X
is a Hausdorff space with fundamental group
\Gamma
and universal cover
\widetilde{X}
, then
\Gamma
is residually finite if and only if for every compact subset
C\subseteq\widetilde{X}
there is a finite cover
Y\to X
so that the natural map
\widetilde{X}\to Y
restricts to an embedding on
C
.

Thus residual finiteness can be used to promote certain immersions to embeddings in finite covers. The purpose of this paper is to present an account of what is known and what is expected regarding the residual finiteness of arguably the most important groups in all four of the aforementioned areas, namely discrete subgroups of Lie groups.

Question 1.1
.

Let
G
be a connected real Lie group. Which discrete subgroups
\Gamma<G
are residually finite?

It ends up that
Question

1.1
is too broad. The remainder of this section provides some results and contrasting examples that motivate this survey’s restriction to
lattices
, i.e., discrete subgroups such that Haar measure on
G
descends to a finite measure on the quotient
\Gamma\backslash G
. First, if
G
admits a faithful linear representation, then Malcev famously showed that all finitely generated subgroups of
G
are residually finite.

Theorem 1.2

(Malcev
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated subgroup of
\GL_{N}(\mathbb{C})
for some
N\geq 1
. Then
\Gamma
is residually finite.

The hypothesis that
\Gamma
is finitely generated is essential, since for example the trivial group is the only finite quotient of
\mathbb{Q}
. One even finds counterexamples among discrete subgroups. For example, while one can show that every discrete subgroup of
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is residually finite, this does not even remain true for
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Example 1.3

(Folklore)
.

The linear Lie group
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
contains a discrete subgroup isomorphic to a countably infinite free product

\Gamma\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\ast\mathbb{Z}/4\ast\cdots\ast\mathbb{Z}/2^{j}\ast\cdots

of cyclic groups of order
2^{j}
. One constructs
\Gamma
using rotations
\sigma_{j}\in\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
of order
2^{j}
fixing appropriate points
z_{j}
in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
. The preimage of each
\sigma_{j}
in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is an element
\tau_{j}
of order
2^{j+1}
for which
\tau_{j}^{2^{j}}=-\Id
. Thus the preimage
\widehat{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
in
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
fits into a central exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\langle\pm\Id\rangle\longrightarrow\widehat{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

induced by the cover
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. If
\rho:\widehat{\Gamma}\to F
is a finite quotient of
\widehat{\Gamma}
, then there is some
n_{0}
so that
\rho(\tau_{j})
has order
2^{n_{j}}
for some
0\leq n_{j}\leq n_{0}
. As soon as
n_{0}<j
, one obtains

\rho(-\Id)=\rho\!\left(\tau_{j}^{2^{j}}\right)\!=\rho\!\left(\tau_{j}^{2^{n_{j}}}\right)^{2^{j-n_{j}}}\!=1

and therefore
-\Id
maps trivially to every finite quotient of
\widehat{\Gamma}
.

If
\Gamma<\SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is discrete and torsion-free, then it is isomorphic to its image in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
under the covering projection and thus is residually finite. One might hope based on this that torsion is essential for producing examples like
Example

1.3
for more general Lie groups, but one only needs to replace
\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{C}
to eliminate that possibility.

Example 1.4

(Cremaschi and Souto
[
6
]
)
.

There is a discrete, torsion-free subgroup
\Gamma<\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{C})
that is not residually finite.

As indicated by
Example

1.3
, residual finiteness can behave badly under finite coverings of Lie groups. This phenomenon is not restricted to infinitely generated monstrosities. The symplectic group
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})<\SL_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
is homotopy equivalent to the unitary group
\U(g)
, which has fundamental group
\mathbb{Z}
. Thus
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
has a connected
n
-fold cyclic cover for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
; these covers are all nonlinear real Lie groups. The following theorem of Deligne will be put in greater context in
Section

3
.

Theorem 1.5

(Deligne
[
8
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma=\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
and
G_{n}\to\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
be the unique finite, connected cover of degree
n
. If
g\geq 2
and
n\geq 3
, then the preimage
\Gamma_{n}
of
\Gamma
in
G_{n}
is a
\mathbb{Z}/n
central extension of
\Gamma
that is not residually finite.

Since
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
is finitely generated, the linearity assumption in

1.2
is just as essential as finite generation of
\Gamma
. In fact, Deligne gives a precise measure of the failure of residual finiteness. If

is the center, then the intersection of the kernels of all homomorphisms from

onto finite groups is
2Z
. Thus

1.1
is even subtle for lattices. Indeed,
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
is a lattice in
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
and its preimage
\Gamma_{n}
in the

-fold cyclic cover
G_{n}
of
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
is a lattice in
G_{n}
. Having now seen that residual finiteness is even delicate for lattices in Lie groups, the following questions are the primary focus of this article.

Question 1.6
.

For which connected real Lie groups
G
are all lattices in
G
residually finite?

Question 1.7
.

Which connected real Lie groups contain a lattice that is not residually finite?

Both questions are quickly answered in the linear and solvable cases.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
G
be a connected real Lie group that is either solvable or that admits a faithful linear representation. Then every lattice in
G
is residually finite.

Proof.

If
G
is linear, then every lattice in
G
is finitely generated (see
[
13
, Cor. 1.6]
for a unified proof), hence lattices are residually finite by
Theorem

1.2
. If
G
is solvable, then Mostow showed that every lattice in

is polycyclic
[
24
, Cor. 4.2]
and Hirsch proved that polycyclic groups are residually finite
[
17
, Thm. 3]
. This proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 1.9
.

Baumslag showed that not all finitely generated solvable groups are residually finite
[
2
]
. These groups evidently are not isomorphic to a lattice in a solvable Lie group.

2.1
Residual finiteness

Recall that a group
\Gamma
is
residually finite
if for all nontrivial
\gamma\in\Gamma
there is a homomorphism
\rho
onto a finite group
F
so that
\rho(\gamma)
remains nontrivial. The next collection of facts are standard, and the uninitiated reader should prove them as warm-up exercises for what follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a group and
\Lambda<\Gamma
is a finite index subgroup. Then
\Gamma
is residually finite if and only if
\Lambda
is residually finite.

Two subgroups
\Gamma,\Lambda
of a group
G
are (widely) commensurable if there is a
g\in G
so that
g\Lambda g^{-1}\cap\Gamma
is finite index in
g\Lambda g^{-1}
and
\Gamma
. Then one has:

Corollary 2.2
.

If
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
are commensurable subgroups of a group
G
, then
\Gamma
is residually finite if and only if
\Lambda
is residually finite.

Lemma 2.3

(Malcev
[
21
]
)
.

Suppose that
\Lambda=\Delta\rtimes\Gamma
is a semidirect product of groups, with
\Delta
finitely generated. Then
\Lambda
is residually finite if and only if
\Delta
and
\Gamma
are residually finite.

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a residually finite group. For any finite subset
X\subseteq\Gamma
there is a homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to F
onto a finite group
F
for which the restriction of
\rho
to
X
is injective.

A important consequence of Lemma
2.4
is the following corollary.

Corollary 2.5

(cf. Lem. 2.5
[
40
]
)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a group for which there is a central extension

1\longrightarrow Z\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow\Gamma_{0}\longrightarrow 1

with
Z
finite and
\Gamma_{0}
residually finite. Then
\Gamma
is residually finite if and only if there is a homomorphism
\rho:\Gamma\to F
onto a finite group
F
so that
\rho
is injective on
Z
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is residually finite, then the desired conclusion follows from Lemma
2.4
. Suppose the converse. If
\gamma\in\Gamma
has nontrivial image in
\Gamma_{0}
, then residual finiteness of
\Gamma_{0}
provides a finite quotient of
\Gamma_{0}
, which is thus a finite quotient of
\Gamma
, where the image of
\gamma
is nontrivial. Thus it suffices to consider the case where
\gamma\in Z
is nontrivial, where the desired finite quotient exists by hypothesis.
∎

This section concludes with some remarks on using nilpotent quotients to prove residual finiteness. If a central extension

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

is residually finite, then the reductions modulo
d\in\mathbb{N}

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/d\longrightarrow\Gamma_{d}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

are residually finite for
some
infinite collection of
d
. The use of nilpotent quotients to prove residual finiteness of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
allows for even stronger conclusions. Residual finiteness of finitely generated nilpotent groups is particularly robust, thus nilpotent quotients of arbitrary finitely generated groups can be used to prove stronger residual finiteness statements. For example, the following result will play an important role later in this paper.

Lemma 2.6

(Lem. 2.6
[
40
]
)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a finitely generated residually finite group and that

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

is a central exact sequence associated with
\phi\in H^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z})
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\phi_{d}\in H^{2}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Z}/d)
be the reduction of
\phi
modulo
d
and

1\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}/d\longrightarrow\Gamma_{d}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

be the associated central exact sequence given by reducing the kernel
\langle\sigma\rangle
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\Gamma
modulo
d
. Suppose that there is a nilpotent quotient
\mathcal{N}
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
that is injective on
\langle\sigma\rangle
. Then
\Gamma_{d}
is residually finite for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

The reader interested in the basic idea behind the proof should consider the case where
\mathcal{N}
is the Heisenberg group of
3\times 3
strictly upper-triangular integer matrices and where
\sigma
maps to the element

\begin{pmatrix}1&0&c\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix}

for some nonzero
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. In a certain sense, the proof for that case (virtually) generalizes to arbitrary nilpotent groups by a famous theorem of Malcev
[
20
]
, since all finitely generated nilpotent groups virtually embed in the subgroup of upper-triangular matrices in
\GL_{N}(\mathbb{R})
for some
N
.

2.2
Lie groups

This section covers some basics on structure theory of Lie groups, along with some general consequences for residual finiteness of finitely generated subgroups.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
G
be a connected real Lie group with universal cover
\widetilde{G}
. Then
\widetilde{G}
is a Lie group and there is a natural homomorphism

c:\pi_{1}(G)\to Z(\widetilde{G})

to the center of
\widetilde{G}
so that
\widetilde{G}\to\widetilde{G}/c(\pi_{1}(G))\cong G
is the universal covering.

In particular, notice that the universal covering map is a homomorphism and that
\pi_{1}(G)
is abelian, hence all connected covers of
G
are regular and are naturally central extensions of
G
. From this point forward, the embedding of
\pi_{1}(G)
into
Z(\widetilde{G})
will be fixed and the homomorphism
c
will be suppressed.
Theorem

2.7

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
G
be a connected real Lie group and
G^{\prime}\to G
be a connected cover with covering group

. If
\Gamma<G
is a group and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is the preimage of
\Gamma
in
G^{\prime}
under the covering, then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is a central extension

1\longrightarrow Z^{\prime}\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

of
\Gamma
by the abelian group
Z^{\prime}
.

3
Semisimple groups

This section describes what is known about and expected regarding residual finiteness of lattices in real semisimple Lie groups. As with much of the theory of lattices in semisimple groups, the discussion breaks naturally into two cases depending on the real rank of the ambient group. In the higher rank (i.e., real rank at least two) setting, assuming some widely-believed conjectures that are proved in many important cases, one obtains a very precise understanding of when irreducible lattices in arbitrary semisimple Lie groups are residually finite and when they are not. This is described in §
3.1
. The real rank one setting, where much is known but some concrete cases remain mysterious, is discussed in §
3.2
.

3.1
Higher rank

The following very basic example, first found by Millson
[

[

]

Example 3.1

(Millson
[

]
)

Fix

and let
\mathcal{O}
be the ring of integers in the real quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p})
, where
p

7

8

\Gamma=\SL_{n}(\mathcal{O})

\widetilde{\Gamma}

\widetilde{\Gamma}

Recall that
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

\Gamma

\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a central extension

that is not residually finite, and the only obstruction is the lone nontrivial element of the central

Turning to the general case, if
\Gamma

The topology induced by the

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{O}_{k})

are the finite index subgroups of
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{O}_{k})

.

{}^{2}\mathrm{A}_{r}
arising from a noncommutative division algebra over a quadratic extension of

[

, §2]
. This conjecture presently remains open.
by Prasad and Rapinchuk
[
28
, Main Thm.]
. For this paper, it suffices to say that
\M(\emptyset,\mathcal{G})
is isomorphic to the dual
\widehat{\mu}(k)
of the group
\mu(k)
of roots of unity of
k
. Since

is totally real,
\mu(k)=\{\pm 1\}
. This implies that there are many lattices in higher rank Lie groups that are not residually finite. For example, the following result can be used in conjunction with Tables

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that
G
is a connected semisimple Lie group with real rank at least two and
\Gamma<G
is an irreducible lattice with associated absolutely almost simple, absolutely simply connected algebraic group
\mathcal{G}
defined over the totally real number field
k
. Let
G_{0}
denote the connected, absolutely almost simple, absolutely simply connected real linear algebraic group locally isomorphic to
G
, and suppose that

is a covering of
G_{0}
with degree greater than
\#\mathrm{M}(\emptyset,\mathcal{G})\leq 2
. If

has the congruence subgroup property, then
\Gamma

Note that lattices in nonlinear groups
G
covered by

may be residually finite by Proposition

. Indeed,

is linear and has finite center. To point out one notable special case in the opposite direction, lattices in groups with infinite fundamental group should never be residually finite (cf.
[

).

Corollary 3.4
.

Let

be a simple Lie group of higher rank with infinite fundamental group, and assume the congruence subgroup property for lattices in the absolutely almost simple, absolutely simply connected real linear algebraic group whose real points are locally isomorphic to

. Then no lattice in
G
is residually finite.

Thus the story in higher rank is fairly well-understood, modulo the congruence subgroup problem. In fact, the following remark indicates that this understanding is more general.

Remark 3.5
.

While this paper is focused on real Lie groups, the considerations in this section should also give a complete understanding for irreducible
S
-arithmetic lattices in higher rank semisimple groups that are products of groups over local fields of characteristic zero. Note that even allowing the field

to have complex places makes

and hence
\mathrm{M}(\emptyset,\mathcal{G})
potentially more complicated. Interestingly, fixing a product of simple groups but changing the field

could possibly influence the degree of the covering to which one must pass to find a lattice that is not residually finite. A motivated reader should be able to assemble precise statements and expectations from the references in this section.

3.2
Rank one

The real Lie groups of real rank one are locally isomorphic to
\SO^{\circ}(n,1)
,
\SU(n,1)
,
\Sp(n,1)
, or the adjoint group
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{(-20)}

3.2.1
Full understanding:
\SO^{\circ}(n,1)
and
\Sp(n,1)

For
n\geq 3
the universal cover of
\SO^{\circ}(n,1)
is
\mathrm{Spin}(n,1)
, which is a linear finite cover, and
\Sp(n,1)
is simply connected and linear for all
n\geq 2
. It follows from Proposition
2.9
that these examples are discarded with relative ease.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
G
be a connected Lie group locally isomorphic to
\SO^{\circ}(n,1)
for
n\geq 3
or
\Sp(n,1)
for
n\geq 2
. If
\Gamma<G
is a lattice, then
\Gamma
is residually finite.

The case
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cong\SO^{\circ}(2,1)
is very special, first because it is arguably the most important simple Lie group, but also in the context of this paper since its fundamental group is
\mathbb{Z}
. This case is quite classical and there are arguments employing any of the many points of view that intersect in and around Riemann surfaces (see
[
7
, §IV.48]
for an account). One argument is sketched here, since it gives some preparation for what comes later in this section where an analogous argument will be given for certain other lattices.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
G
be
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
G^{\prime}\to G
be any connected covering. If
\Gamma^{\prime}<G^{\prime}
is a lattice, then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is residually finite.

Sketch of proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the image of
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. By Corollary
2.2
it suffices to consider the case where
\Gamma
is the fundamental group of a hyperbolic surface of finite area. Consider the case where
G^{\prime}=\widetilde{G}
is the universal cover. Then the standard identification of
\widetilde{G}
with the unit tangent bundle to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
identifies the preimage
\widetilde{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
in
\widetilde{G}
with the fundamental group of the unit tangent bundle to
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

If
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is noncompact, then
\widetilde{\Gamma}\cong\mathbb{Z}\times\Gamma
, which is certainly residually finite, as is the preimage
\mathbb{Z}/d\times\Gamma
of
\Gamma
in any
d
-fold cover of
G
. It remains to consider the case where
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is closed with genus
g\geq 2
. The unit tangent bundle is the circle bundle with Euler number
2(1-g)
, which implies that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
has presentation

\widetilde{\Gamma}=\!\left\langle a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{g},b_{g},z\ \Big|\ \prod_{j=1}^{g}[a_{j},b_{j}]=z^{2(1-g)},\,z\textrm{ central }\right\rangle,

which has an associated exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\langle z\rangle\longrightarrow\widetilde{\Gamma}\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow 1

compatible with
\widetilde{G}\to G
. Taking the quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
by the normal subgroup generated by all generators but
a_{1},b_{1},z
gives a surjective homomorphism onto the group

\mathcal{N}=\!\left\langle\alpha,\beta,\xi\ \Big|\ [\alpha,\beta]=\xi^{2(1-g)}\right\rangle,

which is a two-step nilpotent group. In fact,
\mathcal{N}
is isomorphic to a finite index subgroup of the integer Heisenberg group of strictly upper-triangular
3\times 3
integer matrices.

Note that the surjection
\widetilde{\Gamma}\to\mathcal{N}
is injective on the center
\langle z\rangle
of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
. Since
\Gamma<\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is residually finite, Lemma
2.6
implies that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
and its reductions modulo
d\in\mathbb{N}
are residually finite for all
d
. The reduction modulo
d
of the center is the preimage of
\Gamma
in the
d
-fold cyclic cover of
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, so this proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

To complement Remark
3.8
and complete the picture for lattices in products of semisimple groups over local fields of characteristic zero, lattices in adjoint rank one groups over nonarchimedean fields of characteristic zero are virtually free, since the associated Bruhat–Tits building is a tree
[
41
, §2.7]
. Any extension of a free group by a finitely generated residually finite group is itself residually finite
[
35
, Cor. 7.11]
. Therefore lattices in rank one groups over
p
-adic fields are always residually finite.

3.2.2
Positive results for
\PU(n,1)

Note that
\PSL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is also locally isomorphic to
\PU(1,1)
. For the purposes of this paper it ends up that

is more closely related to

than

Question 3.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a lattice in the unique cover
G_{d}
of
\PU(n,1)
of degree
1\leq d\leq\infty
. Is
\Gamma
residually finite?

The cases

and

are certainly yes:

and its

are linear. Proposition

implies that the same holds for the cover of

of any degree dividing

, since it is intermediate to the covering by
\SU(n,1)
. Much less trivially, Question

Theorem 3.10

(
[
39
,
16
]
)

Suppose that

and
\Gamma<\PU(n,1)
is a cocompact lattice that is arithmetic of simplest kind. Then the preimage of
\Gamma
in any connected cover of

is residually finite.

Lattices of simplest kind are those for which the associated algebraic group is the special unitary group of a hermitian form over a number field. The methods from joint work with Domingo Toledo
[
39
]
and those of Richard Hill
[
16
]
are similar in the broad approach but are ultimately different. In a very precise sense, the proof in
[
39
]
is a generalization of the argument given above for Theorem
3.7

3.10
is an immediate consequence of Lemma
2.6
and the following result.

Theorem 3.11

(
[
39
]
)
.

Suppose that

and
\Gamma<\PU(n,1)
is a cocompact arithmetic lattice of simplest kind. If
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is the preimage of
\Gamma
in the universal cover
\widetilde{G}
of
\PU(n,1)
, then there is a two-step nilpotent group
\mathcal{N}
and a finite index subgroup
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}
of

that admits a surjection
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}\to\mathcal{N}
that is injective on the center of
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{0}
.

The assumption that
\Gamma
is cocompact of the simplest kind is used to produce the nilpotent group

using the structure of its cohomology ring. The first piece of the puzzle is the following general result.

Theorem 3.12

(Thm. 1.2
[
40
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma<\PU(n,1)
be a torsion-free cocompact lattice and

\widetilde{\Gamma}
is the preimage of
\Gamma
in the universal cover of
\PU(n,1)

\widetilde{\Gamma}
that is injective on the center of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
if and only if the Kähler class on

is in the image of the cup product map

c_{\mathbb{Q}}:\bigwedge\nolimits^{2}H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q}).

The connection between the Kähler class and the preimage in the universal cover is that the first Chern class
c_{1}(X)
is proportional to the Kähler class and is also the class in
H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
associated with the preimage in the universal cover. This is because
\widehat{\Gamma}\backslash\SU(n,1)/\SU(n)
is homotopy equivalent to the canonical bundle minus its zero section, where
\widehat{\Gamma}
is the preimage in
\SU(n,1)
. See
[

, §3.4]
for details. This also completes the connection with the above proof of Theorem

, since the canonical bundle of a Riemann surface is the cotangent bundle. The connection between a cohomology class being in the image of the cup product and nilpotent quotients is through the following result from algebraic topology.

Theorem 3.13

(Thm. 5.1
[
40
]
)
.

Let
X
be a closed aspherical manifold with fundamental group
\Gamma
and

\U(1)\longrightarrow Y\longrightarrow X

be a principal
\U(1)
bundle with Euler class
\omega\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Z})
that has infinite additive order, equivalently, its image in

is not zero. If
\widetilde{\Gamma}=\pi_{1}(Y)
and

denotes a generator for
\pi_{1}(\U(1))<\widetilde{\Gamma}
, then the image of

in the maximal two-step nilpotent quotient of
\widetilde{\Gamma}
has infinite order if and only if

is in the image of the map

c_{\mathbb{Q}}:\bigwedge\nolimits^{2}H^{1}(X,\mathbb{Q})\longrightarrow H^{2}(X,\mathbb{Q})

given by evaluation of the cup product.

Sketch of the direction used in this paper.

Taking the
K(\Gamma,1)
map from the natural homomorphism
\Gamma\to H_{1}(\Gamma,\mathbb{Q})
gives a
\pi_{1}
-surjective map from

to a

-dimensional torus

. The hypothesis on the cup product precisely implies that

is the pull-back of a class in

has two-step nilpotent fundamental group, and the induced map

∎

Note that Theorems
3.12
and

are very general, and have nothing to do with arithmeticity. However, the assumption that the lattice is arithmetic is simplest kind is used in a fundamental way in showing that these results apply to prove Theorem

on congruence subgroups via the

Theorem 3.14

(Thm. 3.3
[
39
]
)
.

Let

be a smooth compact ball quotient,

, with

In short, Theorem

This is precisely the sort of result one obtains from the theta correspondence. Now following work of Bergeron, Millson, and Moeglin, joint work with Toledo proved the following result that, following the previous outline given in this section, proves Theorem

Theorem 3.15

(Thm. 3.4
[

]
)

Let

1.

.

Theorem 3.17

(
[

Let

Question 3.18
.

Let
X
be a fake projective plane with fundamental group
\Gamma<\PU(2,1)

3.2.4
The wide open case of
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{(-20)}

One final case remains in rank one, and it is quite mysterious. Since the universal cover of
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{(-20)}
is a nonlinear (double) cover, it is unclear whether or not lattices there are residually finite.

Question 3.20
.

Let
G
be the universal cover of the adjoint real Lie group
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{(-20)}
of type
\mathrm{F}_{4}
and
\Gamma<G
be a lattice. Is
\Gamma
residually finite?

Lattices as in Question
3.20
admit a central exact sequence

1\longrightarrow L\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow\Gamma_{0}\longrightarrow 1

with
L\leq\mathbb{Z}/2
and
\Gamma_{0}
a lattice in the linear group
\mathrm{F}_{4}^{(-20)}
. Therefore, by Corollary
2.5
, it suffices to prove that there is a finite quotient of
\Gamma
that is injective on its center. It is not clear how one would find such a quotient, and seems difficult at the moment to predict whether the answer to Question
3.20
should be yes or no.

3.2.5
Gromov hyperbolic groups

One of the major open problems in geometric group theory is whether or not every Gromov hyperbolic group is residually finite; see for example
[
26
, Prob. 3, p. 208]
. It is widely expected that there are hyperbolic groups that are not residually finite, but an example has yet to be discovered. The direct connection with this survey is that cocompact rank one lattices are Gromov hyperbolic groups. Indeed, they are virtually the fundamental group of compact manifolds admitting a Riemannian metric of negative curvature, which are the quintessential hyperbolic groups. However, something slightly stronger is true thanks to relatively hyperbolic Dehn filling. The following is well-known to experts.

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
\Gamma
be a lattice in a connected rank one real Lie group
G
with finite center. If all Gromov hyperbolic groups are residually finite, then
\Gamma
is residually finite. If
\Gamma
is not residually finite, then there is a Gromov hyperbolic group that is not residually finite.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is cocompact, then both conclusions of the proposition are immediate, since
\Gamma
is itself hyperbolic. Therefore, assume that
\Gamma
is nonuniform. Then there is a finite collection of peripheral subgroups
\mathcal{P}_{i}
in
\Gamma
so that
\Gamma
is hyperbolic relative to the collection
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}\}

[
12
, Thm. 5.1]
. Note that
\Gamma
fits into a central extension

1\longrightarrow Z\longrightarrow\Gamma\longrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}\longrightarrow 1,

where
\overline{\Gamma}
is a lattice in the adjoint form of
G
and
Z
is finite. Since
\overline{\Gamma}
is linear and thus residually finite, residual finiteness of
\Gamma
is equivalent to the existence of a finite quotient of
\Gamma
that is injective on
Z
by Corollary
2.5
.

The groups
\mathcal{P}_{i}
are virtually nilpotent, so there are finite index normal subgroups
\mathcal{Q}_{i}\leq\mathcal{P}_{i}
that intersect
Z
trivially. Choosing the
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
sufficiently deep inside
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, a theorem of Osin
[
27
, Thm. 1.1]
implies that the quotient

\Lambda=\Gamma/\ll\!\{\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}\!\gg

is hyperbolic relative to the finite groups
\{\mathcal{P}_{i}/\mathcal{Q}_{i}\}
and
\Gamma\to\Lambda
is injective on
Z
. Since finite groups are hyperbolic,
\Lambda
is a hyperbolic group
[
27
, Cor. 1.2]
.

If every hyperbolic group, in particular
\Lambda
, is residually finite, then there is a finite quotient of
\Lambda
that is injective on
Z
by Lemma
2.4
. As mentioned earlier in the proof, this implies that
\Gamma
is residually finite. Conversely, if
\Gamma
is not residually finite, then there is no finite quotient of
\Gamma
that is injective on
Z
. Since
\Gamma\to\Lambda
is injective on
Z
, then
\Lambda
also has no such quotient and therefore it is a hyperbolic group that is not residually finite. This proves the proposition.
∎

Remark 3.22
.

One can also extend Proposition
3.21
to include the last remaining case where the center is infinite, namely the universal cover
\widetilde{G}
of
\PU(n,1)
for
n\geq 2
. Note that even cocompact lattices in
\widetilde{G}
fail to be Gromov hyperbolic, since they have infinite center. The key observation is simply that if
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is a lattice in
\widetilde{G}
and it is not residually finite, then its projection to some finite cover of
\PU(n,1)
is also not residually finite.

4
The general case

With all the above information on lattices in solvable and semisimple groups in hand, this section is actually quite short. Suppose that
G
is a connected real Lie group and
\Lambda<G
is a lattice. If
G
is simply connected, then it has a
Levi decomposition

G=S\rtimes H

where
S
and
H
are simply connected,
S
is solvable, and
H
is semisimple
[
42
, §1.4]
. When
G
is not simply connected, one only obtains an exact sequence with connected solvable kernel and semisimple quotient.

As seen throughout this article, it generally suffices to assume that
G
is simply connected and study quotients only when necessary. For simplicity it is a therefore standing assumption throughout this section that
G
is simply connected. The first item of business is then to connect the Levi decomposition of
G
to
\Lambda
, which is subtle in general. The following theorem gives a sufficient condition for
\Lambda
to be the semidirect product of a lattice in
H
with a lattice in
S
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
G
is a simply connected Lie group with Levi decomposition
S\rtimes H
, and assume that
H
has no compact factor. Then any lattice
\Lambda<G
can be decomposed as a semidirect product
\Delta\rtimes\Gamma
, where
\Delta
is a lattice in
S
and
\Gamma
is a lattice in
H
.

See
[

]
for history and subtleties, including discussion of some flaws in

related only to the case where
H
has compact factors. Even for general
G
, where the semisimple part is technically a quotient and may have compact factors,
\Lambda
still intersects
S
in a lattice if and only if its projection to
H
is a lattice; see
[

for a reference. This leads to the following very general positive result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G
be a simply connected Lie group with Levi decomposition
S\rtimes H
such that the semisimple part
H
has no compact factor. If every lattice in
H
is residually finite, then every lattice in
G
is residually finite.

Proof.

If
\Lambda<G
is a lattice, then Theorem
4.1
implies that
\Lambda=\Delta\rtimes\Gamma
is a semidirect product of a lattice in
S
and a lattice in
H
. Since
\Delta
is residually finite by Theorem
1.8
and finitely generated, Lemma
2.3
implies that
\Lambda
is residually finite if and only if
\Gamma
is residually finite. This proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Note that residual finiteness of all lattices in
G
does not imply that every lattice in
H
is residually finite. This conclusion would require knowing that for every lattice
\Gamma_{0}<H
, there is a finite index subgroup
\Gamma\leq\Gamma_{0}
and a lattice
\Delta<S
so that there is a lattice in
G
isomorphic to
\Delta\rtimes\Gamma
. This statement often fails badly. For a well-known example, suppose
\Gamma
is a cocompact lattice in
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
for
n\geq 3
. Then there is no lattice in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}\rtimes\SL_{n}(\mathbb{R})}
associated with
\Gamma
in this fashion. Indeed, such a lattice would necessarily be isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\rtimes\Gamma
, and thus
\Gamma
is conjugate to a finite-index subgroup of
\SL_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, which is impossible.

For an easy example of a lattice that is not residually finite, consider the semidirect product

\mathbb{Z}^{2g}\rtimes\widetilde{\Gamma}<\mathbb{R}^{2g}\rtimes\widetilde{G},

where
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is the preimage of
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
in the universal cover
\widetilde{G}
of
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
and
\widetilde{G}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{2g}
through the standard action of
\Sp_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
. Similar examples lead to a wealth of lattices in general Lie groups that are not residually finite, but for fairly trivial reasons given the semisimple case. Indeed, perhaps the main takeaway of this section is that the semisimple part of the Levi decomposition is the primary deciding factor in whether a lattice in
G
is residually finite
4
4

4

This is equally the case when
H
has compact factors, where the projection of
\Lambda
to
H
may not be discrete and thus has a slim chance to not be residually finite even if every lattice in
H
is residually finite. It would be quite surprising if such an example exists.
. Hopefully, with the previous results of this paper in hand the reader is now well-prepared to enumerate the numerous other possibilities, theorems, examples, and pathologies regarding residual finiteness of lattices in general Lie groups.
