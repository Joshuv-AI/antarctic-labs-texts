Ordering and Convergence of Large Degrees in Random Hyperbolic Graphs
Loïc Gassmann

Abstract
We describe the asymptotic behaviour of large degrees in random hyperbolic graphs, for all values of the curvature parameter α \alpha . We prove that, with high probability, the node degrees satisfy the following ordering property: the ranking of the nodes by decreasing degree coincides with the ranking of the nodes by increasing distance to the centre, at least up to any constant rank. In the scale-free regime α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 , the rank at which these two rankings cease to coincide is n 1 / ( 1 + 8 ​ α ) + o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{1/(1+8\alpha)+o(1)} . We also provide a quantitative description of the large degrees by proving the convergence in distribution of the normalised degree process towards a Poisson point process. In particular, this establishes the convergence in distribution of the normalised maximum degree of the graph. A transition occurs at α = 1 / 2 \alpha=1/2 , which corresponds to the connectivity threshold of the model. For α < 1 / 2 \alpha<1/2 , the maximum degree is of order n − O ⁡ ( n α + 1 / 2 ) n-O(n^{\alpha+1/2}) , whereas for α ≥ 1 / 2 \alpha\geq 1/2 , the maximum degree is of order n 1 / ( 2 ​ α ) n^{1/(2\alpha)} . In the cases α < 1 / 2 \alpha<1/2 and α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 , the limit distribution of the maximum degree belongs to the class of extreme value distributions (Weibull for α < 1 / 2 \alpha<1/2 and Fréchet for α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 ). This refines previous estimates on the maximum degree for α > 1 / 2 \alpha>1/2 and extends the study of large degrees to the dense regime α ≤ 1 / 2 \alpha\leq 1/2 .

1
Introduction

The class of
complex networks
consists of large real-life networks that primarily arise from human interactions, such as social networks and the Internet, as well as from other fields like biology
[
2
]
. Networks in this class exhibit four essential features:
high clustering
, the
small-world
property,
sparseness
and a
scale-free degree distribution

[
11
]
. Krioukov, Papadopoulos, Kitsak, Vahdat and Boguñá empirically showed that these four properties naturally emerge in graphs constructed on hyperbolic spaces. This led them to introduce the
random hyperbolic graph
(also denoted RHG) as a model for
complex networks

[
17
]
. Boguñá, Papadopoulos and Krioukov further illustrated this point by providing an embedding of the Internet graph into a hyperbolic space
[
6
]
. For modelling purposes, the model can be tuned through a curvature parameter
\alpha
and a parameter
\nu
determining the average degree.

It has now been rigorously proven that, in the regime
\alpha>1/2
, the
random hyperbolic graph
exhibits all the properties of
complex networks
listed above.
Sparseness
is proven in
[
19
]
, the
small-world
property is shown in
[
1
]
, the
high clustering
is also fully tested
[
10
,
13
,
14
]
and the
scale-free degree distribution
of the RHG has been proven in
[
14
]
. In this regime, the
random hyperbolic graph
is a particular case of the
geometric inhomogeneous random graph
(also called GIRG)
[
8
]
. In the GIRG model, nodes are sampled on a torus and inhomogeneity is obtained via power law weights on the nodes, which determine the connection probabilities. It is established in
[
8
,
9
]
that the GIRG model possesses the main properties of
complex graphs
listed above, thereby re-proving, in a more conceptual way, that the
random hyperbolic graph
exhibits all the properties of
complex networks
.

A graph is said to have a
scale-free degree distribution
when its degree sequence follows a power law distribution, meaning that for large
k
, the number of nodes with degree
k
behaves like an inverse power of
k
. In this case, the graph has a large number of hubs (nodes with degrees much larger than the average degree). A
random hyperbolic graph
is typically structured as follows: high-degree nodes are well connected to each other and serve as hubs for nodes with slightly lower degrees. These intermediate-degree nodes, in turn, connect to nodes with even lower degrees, and so on, forming a hierarchical, tree-like structure. In addition to this tree-like organization, there are also connections between nodes with similar angular coordinates. This structure provides an intuitive explanation for the
small-world
phenomenon observed in these graphs, as short paths naturally emerge by travelling from hub to hub.

In the case
\alpha\leq 1/2
, the
random hyperbolic graph
is no longer
sparse
nor
scale-free
. However, the model remains highly inhomogeneous in the sense that the degree distribution of a node depends heavily on its position in the hyperbolic space, leading to the presence of a large number of hubs. This regime is referred to as the
dense regime
. The value
\alpha=1/2
is also the transition point between the connected and the non-connected regime: for
\alpha<1/2
, the graph has a high probability of being connected, with connectivity being entirely ensured by a few large hubs located near the centre. Conversely, for
\alpha>1/2
, the graph has a high probability of being disconnected. In the critical phase
\alpha=1/2
, the probability of connectivity tends to a constant that depends on the parameter
\nu
. This constant takes the value
1
if and only if
\nu\geq\pi
(see
[
5
]
).

Main results
. In this paper, we are interested in the nodes with the largest degrees, which are the most important hubs of the graph. Theorem
3.1
proves the ordering of these nodes, namely, that for
k
fixed, with high probability, the node with the
k
-th largest degree is the node with the
k
-th smallest distance to the centre of the underlying space. In the regime
\alpha>1/2
, Theorem
3.2
even shows that this ordering property holds up to rank
n^{1/(1+8\alpha)+o(1)}
and fails beyond. Finally, Theorem
3.3
states the convergence in distribution of the normalised point process of the degrees towards a Poisson point process. In particular, it establishes the convergence in distribution of the normalised maximum degree of the graph, for all
\alpha>0
.

Node degrees in RHGs are closely related to the measures of certain regions of the underlying hyperbolic space. The exact expressions of these quantities are seldom tractable, but since we are seeking asymptotic results, we only need to approximate them. The approximations we employ depend on the value of the curvature parameter
\alpha
, as the asymptotic position of the closest node to the centre is strongly influenced by this parameter. In the regime
\alpha>1/2
, we use the approximations from
[
14
, Lemma 3.2]
(see Lemma
5.3
of this paper and Lemma
8.1
for a refinement). In the two regimes
\alpha<1/2
and
\alpha=1/2
, we use new approximations (given by Lemmas
5.4
and
5.5
, respectively).

Structure of the paper
. Section
2
contains a presentation of the random hyperbolic graph model. Our main results are presented in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we prove the convergence of the node radii. Section
5
is dedicated to estimations of the measure of the balls involved in the connection rule. The results of these two last sections are used to prove Theorem
3.1
(in Section
6
), Theorem
3.3
(in Section
7
) and Theorem
3.2
(in Section
8
).

2
Definition of the model

2.1
Hyperbolic geometry

Before introducing the random hyperbolic graph model, let us review some definitions and notations concerning hyperbolic geometry. We refer to the book of Stillwell
[
21
]
for a broader introduction to hyperbolic geometry. The Poincaré disc, denoted by
\mathbb{H}
, is the open unit disc of
\mathbb{C}
equipped with the Riemannian metric
\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{H}}
, defined at
w\in\mathbb{H}
by

\displaystyle\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{H}}\coloneqq\frac{4\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{C}}}{(1-|w|^{2})^{2}},\quad\mbox{where\penalty\ $\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{C}}$ is the Euclidean metric on\penalty\ $\mathbb{C}$.}

We denote by
d_{\mathbb{H}}
the distance induced by
\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{H}}
on
\mathbb{H}
. Throughout this paper, we make extensive use of the polar coordinates to describe points in the Poincaré disc. The polar coordinates of a points
w
in
\mathbb{H}
are given by the pair
(r(w),\theta(w))
, where
r(w)
denotes its hyperbolic distance to the origin and
\theta(w)
denotes its angle in the complex plane. The quantity
r(w)
is also referred to as the radius of
w
. If
x
and
y
are two points of
\mathbb{H}
with respective polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
and
(s,\beta)
, the hyperbolic distance between
x
and
y
is given by the celebrated hyperbolic law of cosines:

\displaystyle\cosh(d_{\mathbb{H}}(x,y))=\cosh(r)\cosh(s)-\sinh(r)\sinh(s)\cos(\theta-\beta).

(1)

For visualization purposes, we will use the native representation to draw pictures of random hyperbolic graphs, as done in
[
17
]
. This means that instead of representing random hyperbolic graphs directly in
\mathbb{H}
, we will represent their images under the mapping
\omega\mapsto r(w)e^{i\theta(w)}
, defined from
\mathbb{H}
to
\mathbb{C}
. This transformation dilates all distances to the origin, ensuring that every point
(r,\theta)
is represented with a Euclidean distance to the origin equal to its radial coordinate
r
.

For a point
x
with polar coordinates
(r,\theta)
in
\mathbb{H}
and a radius
s>0
, we denote by
\mathcal{B}_{x}(s)
or
\mathcal{B}_{(r,\theta)}(s)
the open hyperbolic ball of radius
s
centred at
x
. For
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
, we define
\mathcal{C}(r_{1},r_{2})
as the annulus with inner radius
r_{1}
and outer radius
r_{2}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}(r_{1},r_{2})\coloneqq\mathcal{B}_{0}(r_{2})\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(r_{1})
.

2.2
The Random Hyperbolic Graph

We now proceed with the formal definition of the random hyperbolic graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
, as defined in
[
17
]
. Fix two parameters
\alpha>0
and
\nu>0
and for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, set

\displaystyle R_{n}\coloneqq 2\log(n/\nu).

(2)

Define a probability measure
\mu_{n}
on
\mathbb{H}
such that if a point
(r,\theta)
(in polar coordinates) is chosen according to
\mu_{n}
, then
r
and
\theta
are independent,
\theta
is uniformly distributed in
(-\pi,\pi]
and the probability distribution of
r
has a density function on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(r)\coloneqq\frac{\alpha\sinh(\alpha r)}{(\cosh(\alpha R_{n})-1)}\textbf{1}_{\left\{r<R_{n}\right\}}.

(3)

Let
X_{1}^{n},X_{2}^{n},\dots,X_{n}^{n}
be a sequence of
n
independent points sampled from the Poincaré disc according to the distribution
\mu_{n}
(for brevity, the superscript
n
in
X_{i}^{n}
will often be omitted). We denote by
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
the random hyperbolic graph with
n
nodes and parameters
\alpha
and
\nu
. It is defined as the undirected graph with nodes at the points
X_{1}^{n},X_{2}^{n},\dots,X_{n}^{n}
, where an edge exists between two nodes if and only if their hyperbolic distance is at most
R_{n}
.

The degree of a node
X_{i}
in the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
is defined as the number of its direct neighbours in
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and is denoted by
\deg(X_{i})
. Since we focus on the behaviour of large graphs, the value of
n
will always be considered large, while the parameters
\alpha
and
\nu
are fixed. We say that an event is realised with high probability if its probability tends to
1
as
n
goes to infinity.

Observe that the
n
nodes of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
are located within
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
. Moreover, due to the choice of the measure, the points tend to concentrate near the boundary of
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
. Also note that the measure
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{(r,\theta)}(s))
of a ball centred at
(r,\theta)
is independent of
\theta
. Therefore, we omit the angle
\theta
to shorten notation and instead write
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(s))
. Likewise, we write
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(s)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(s^{\prime}))
instead of
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{(r,\theta)}(s)\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(s^{\prime}))
.

In the special case
\alpha=1
,
\mu_{n}
is the uniform measure on
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
associated with the Riemannian metric
\textbf{g}_{\mathbb{H}}
. In the general case
\alpha>0
,
\mu_{n}
corresponds to a uniform measure on the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
of curvature
-\alpha^{2}
. More precisely, for fixed
\alpha>0
, multiplying the differential form in the Poincaré disc model by a factor
1/\alpha^{2}
we obtain a hyperbolic plane of curvature
-\alpha^{2}
. Choosing a point according to the measure
\mu_{n}
amounts to choosing a point uniformly in the ball of radius
R_{n}
of
\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
and projecting it on
\mathbb{H}
, by keeping the same polar coordinates. The measure of a ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{H}_{\alpha}
is

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\alpha^{2}}(\cosh(\alpha r)-1),

thus, the larger
\alpha
is, the faster it increases with
r
. Therefore, the larger
\alpha
is, the more the points of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
concentrate near the boundary of
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
. So, the maximum degree is expected to decrease with
\alpha
(see Figure
1
). The degree distribution is also influenced by the parameter
\nu
. Increasing
\nu
makes the domain smaller, which limits the concentration of the nodes near the boundary of the domain, resulting in a higher expected degree. In the scale-free regime
\alpha>1/2
, the expected degree evolves linearly with
\nu

[
14
]
.

The Poissonised random hyperbolic graph
\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Poi}}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
is obtained by choosing the nodes according to a Poisson point process with intensity measure
n\mu_{n}
, instead of choosing
n
points according to the measure
\mu_{n}
. All the results proved in this paper also hold for the Poissonised model. In the proof of the non-ordering result (
5
), the use of a classical Poissonisation/de-Poissonisation procedure allows to avoid some technicalities by using the properties of Poisson processes.

3
Results

3.1
Ordering of Large Degrees

We denote by
X_{(1)}^{n},X_{(2)}^{n},\dots,X_{(n)}^{n}
a reordering of the nodes of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
by increasing radius, i.e.,
r(X_{(1)}^{n})\leq r(X_{(2)}^{n})\leq\dots\leq r(X_{(n)}^{n})
. To shorten notation, we will often omit the superscript
n
. Our first result shows that this ranking of the nodes coincide with the ranking of the nodes by decreasing degree, at least up to any constant rank.

Theorem 3.1
.

For fixed
\alpha>0
,
\nu>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, with high probability,

\displaystyle\forall i>k,\;\deg(X_{(1)}^{n})>\deg(X_{(2)}^{n})>\dots>\deg(X_{(k)}^{n})>\deg(X_{(i)}^{n})

In the regime
\alpha>1/2
, we will even prove the following result, which provides an estimate of the rank at which the ranking of the nodes by increasing radius and the ranking of the nodes by decreasing degree cease to coincide. We believe that similar polynomial rank orderings hold in the other regimes, but we choose to present this refined result only for the case
\alpha>1/2
to avoid additional computations.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let us fix
\alpha>1/2
,
\nu>0
and a sequence
v_{n}\to\infty
. Define

\displaystyle\beta\coloneqq\frac{1}{1+8\alpha}\quad\mbox{and}\quad k_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq n^{\beta}\log(n)^{-2\alpha}.

We have, with high probability,

\displaystyle\forall i>k_{n},\;\deg(X_{(1)}^{n})>\deg(X_{(2)}^{n})

\displaystyle>\dots>\deg(X_{(k_{n})}^{n})>\deg(X_{(i)}^{n})

(4)

and there exists
i\in[n^{\beta},n^{\beta}v_{n}]
such that

\displaystyle\deg(X_{(i)}^{n})<\deg(X_{(i+1)}^{n}).

(5)

If we choose, for example,
v_{n}=\log(n)
, then the result shows that, with high probability, the ordering of the degree holds up to rank
n^{\beta-o(1)}
and fails before rank
n^{\beta+o(1)}
. This proves the optimality of the exponent
\beta
as the ordering exponent.

The ordering results above show that the position of a node in the underlying hyperbolic space is a precise estimate of its position in the hierarchy of hubs. This is natural, as the degree of the
k
closest nodes to the centre stochastically dominates the degree of the
n-k
following nodes. However, this observation alone is insufficient to conclude, since having more connections than these
n-k
nodes requires competing with a polynomial number of nodes. For example, in the random recursive tree model, similar ordering results do not hold, as the root has the highest expected degree but does not necessarily have the highest degree (see
[
12
]
). In RHGs, we use the fast decay of degree with distance to the centre to show that the
k
(or
k_{n}
) closest nodes to the centre can compete with the remaining
n-k
(or
n-k_{n}
) nodes.

3.2
Convergence of Large Degrees

For every subspace
E
of the compactified real line
[-\infty,\infty]
we denote by
M_{p}(E)
the space of locally finite point measures on
E
. A point process on
E
is a random element of
M_{p}(E)
(see
[
20
,
7
,
15
]
for details on point processes). We denote by
\xrightarrow{d}
the convergence in distribution (or weak convergence) of point processes in
M_{p}(E)
. We will also use the notation
\xrightarrow{d}
for the convergence in distribution of random variables or random vectors. For
x\in[-\infty,\infty]
, we write
\delta_{x}
for the Dirac measure at
x
.

We want to describe the asymptotic behaviour of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\deg(X_{i}^{n})}
, which is the point process of node degrees . We consider this point process as an element of
M_{p}((0,\infty])
. Since the interval includes
+\infty
, this point process captures information about the largest degrees of the graph. Given that the expected value of the maximum degree goes to infinity with
n
, we normalise the degree process by a quantity depending on the expected value of the maximum degree. This quantity heavily depends on the curvature parameter
\alpha
. As with connectivity, a transition occurs at
\alpha=1/2
: for
\alpha<1/2
, the maximum degree is of order
n-O(n^{\alpha+1/2})
, whereas for
\alpha\geq 1/2
, the maximum degree is of order
n^{1/(2\alpha)}
. This compels us to treat the three regimes
\alpha<1/2
,
\alpha=1/2
and
\alpha>1/2
separately. The result below states the convergence in distribution of the normalised point process. We recall that for
\beta>0
, a random variable
Z
follows the distribution
\mbox{Weibull}\left(\beta\right)
if for all
z\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}[Z\leq z]=1-e^{-z^{\beta}}
and it follows the distribution
\mbox{Fr\'{e}chet}\left(\beta\right)
if for all
z\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}[Z\leq z]=e^{-z^{-\beta}}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Fix
\alpha,\nu>0
and denote by
D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}
the maximum degree of the graph
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. We have the following convergences:

\bullet
For
\alpha<1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}(\deg(X_{i}^{n})-n)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{g_{1}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((-\infty,0]),

where
\eta_{g_{1}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
g_{1}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(-\infty,0],\,g_{1}(y)=2\pi^{2}\nu^{2\alpha}|y|.

In particular,

\displaystyle\pi\nu^{\alpha}\frac{n-D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}}{n^{\alpha+1/2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mbox{Weibull}\left(2\right).

\bullet
For
\alpha=1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-1}\deg(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{g_{2}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((0,1]),

where
\eta_{g_{2}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
g_{2}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(0,1],\,g_{2}(y)=\nu|(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1})^{\prime}(y)|\sinh(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1}(y)/2),

where
V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}
is defined by

\displaystyle\forall r>0,\;V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\arccos\left(\max\left(-1,\frac{\cosh(r)-x^{-2}}{\sinh(r)}\right)\right)dx.

In particular,

\displaystyle n^{-1}D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(2\arcosh(\mbox{Exponential}\left(2\nu\right)+1)).

\bullet
For
\alpha>1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-1/(2\alpha)}\deg(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{g_{3}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((0,\infty]),

where
\eta_{g_{3}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
g_{3}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(0,\infty],\,g_{3}(y)=2\alpha(C_{\alpha}\nu)^{2\alpha}y^{-2\alpha-1},

where
C_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{2\alpha}{\pi(\alpha-1/2)}
. In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}}{C_{\alpha}\nu n^{1/(2\alpha)}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mbox{Fr\'{e}chet}\left(2\alpha\right).

Remark 3.4
.

Convergence in distribution of a sequence of processes
\mathcal{P}_{n}
towards a process
\mathcal{P}
in
M_{p}((a,b])
(with
a,b\in[-\infty,\infty]
) is equivalent to the convergence in distribution of the vectors
(Y_{1}^{n},Y_{2}^{n},\dots,Y_{k}^{n})
towards
(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{k})
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
for every positive integer
k
, where
Y_{1}^{n}\geq Y_{2}^{n}\geq\dots\geq Y_{k}^{n}
are the
k
largest points of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
Y_{1}\geq Y_{2}\geq\dots\geq Y_{k}
are the
k
largest points of
\mathcal{P}
(assuming that
\mathcal{P}
almost surely has infinitely many points). Thus, the convergence of the maximum degree follows directly from the convergence of the point process of the degrees. One get a similar characterisation of convergence in distribution in
M_{p}([a,b))
by inverting the order. Convergence in distribution in
M_{p}(E)
can be characterised in many other ways, such as via the convergence of evaluation functions or the convergence of Laplace transforms (see
[
20
,
7
,
15
]
).

In the regime
\alpha>1/2
, Theorem
3.3
refines the estimate of
n^{1/(2\alpha)+o(1)}
for the maximum degree given in
[
14
]
. It also extends the description of the scale-free degree sequence presented in the same paper by describing precisely the sequence of the
k
largest degrees for
k
fixed. Also note that in the regime
\alpha>1/2
, a more general result can be found in
[
3
]
, where the convergence towards a Poisson point process with a power law intensity is proven for a general class of scale-free inhomogeneous random graphs. By refining the proof of the representation of the RHG as a GIRG from
[
8
]
, one can show that the random hyperbolic graph in the regime
\alpha>1/2
is indeed part of the more general model considered in
[
3
]
. This provides an alternative proof of Theorem
3.3
for
\alpha>1/2
. Note that this result would not hold directly for GIRGs, even for unspecified limit distributions, because the
O
-notations appearing in the definition of the edge probabilities may allow large degrees to oscillate instead of converging. In the present paper, we propose proof strategies that work for both the scale-free regime
\alpha>1/2
and the dense regime
\alpha\leq 1/2
.

In the cases
\alpha<1/2
and
\alpha>1/2
, we obtain extreme value distribution limits for the maximum degree
D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}
. This is not surprising, as
D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}
is the maximum of a weakly correlated sequence of variables. However, a difficulty in proving a formal result using a standard extreme value theorem (see for example
[
18
,
20
]
) arises from the fact that the distribution of the typical degree depends on
n
. Nevertheless, we can still use extreme value theory to give an intuitive explanation of the limit distributions of
D^{\mbox{\tiny{max}}}_{n}
as follows: In the regime
\alpha>1/2
, the limit distribution is easy to interpret since the degree of a typical node converges to a power law with exponent
2\alpha+1
, which belongs to the domain of attraction of
\mbox{Fr\'{e}chet}\left(2\alpha\right)
. In the regime
\alpha<1/2
, the degree distribution does not converge, but since the largest degrees are attained by nodes that are close to the centre, we can focus on a variable
D_{n}
distributed as the degree of a typical node in the annulus
\mathcal{C}(0,n^{\alpha-1/2+\varepsilon})
, with
\varepsilon>0
small. A direct computation using Lemma
5.4
shows that
(n-D_{n})n^{-(1/2+\alpha+\varepsilon)}
converges in distribution towards the inverse of a power law with exponent
1
, which belongs to the domain of attraction of
\mbox{Weibull}\left(2\right)
. Similar arguments using polynomial normalisations cannot hold in the regime
\alpha=1/2
as, in this regime, the highest degrees are too much influenced by the geometry of the model. The influence of the geometry can be seen in the function
V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(2\arcosh(\cdot+1))
appearing in the limit and explains why we do not obtain an extreme value distribution in this case.

Landau’s notations:
In this paper, we use the Landau notations
o
and
O
to describe the asymptotic behaviour of certain quantities as the number of nodes
n
tends to infinity. Specifically, we use a version of these notations that allows us to express uniformity in some other variables
y_{1},y_{2},...,y_{d}
. More precisely, for
(J_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and two functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we write

\displaystyle\quad g(n,y)=o_{y\in J_{n}}(f(n,y))\quad\mbox{if}\quad\forall\varepsilon>0,\exists n_{0},\forall n\geq n_{0},\forall y\in J_{n},\;|g(n,y)|\leq\varepsilon|f(n,y)|,

and we write

\displaystyle\quad g(n,y)=O_{y\in J_{n}}(f(n,y))\quad\mbox{if}\quad\exists c>0,\exists n_{0},\forall n\geq n_{0},\forall y\in J_{n},\;|g(n,y)|\leq c|f(n,y)|.

Note that the subscript "
y\in J_{n}
" indicates that the comparison holds uniformly for
y
in the set
J_{n}
as
n
tends to infinity. To simplify notation, this subscript is specified beforehand and omitted in most cases. When
f
and
g
are functions of the variable
n
only, we keep the same definitions.

4
Convergence of the Radii

The particularity of random hyperbolic graphs, compared to graphs constructed in Euclidean spaces, is that the degree of a node strongly depends on its position in the underlying space, specifically on its radius. In this section, we study the asymptotic behaviour of the radii of the closest points to the centre by proving the convergence of the point process of the radii
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{r(X_{i}^{n})}
in
M_{p}([0,\infty))
. Since the interval includes
0
, this point process captures information about the smallest degrees of the graph (see Remark
3.4
). As in Theorem
3.1
, normalisation by the expected value of the smallest radius is required.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have the following convergences:

\bullet
For
\alpha<1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{1/2-\alpha}r(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{\gamma_{1}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}([0,\infty)),

where
\eta_{\gamma_{1}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
\gamma_{1}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall u\in[0,\infty),

\displaystyle\;\gamma_{1}(u)=2\alpha^{2}\nu^{2\alpha}u.

\bullet
For
\alpha=1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{r(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{\gamma_{2}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}([0,\infty)),

where
\eta_{\gamma_{2}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
\gamma_{2}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall u\in[0,\infty),

\displaystyle\;\gamma_{2}(u)=\nu\sinh(u/2).

\bullet
For
\alpha>1/2
,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{r(X_{i}^{n})-(1-1/(2\alpha))R_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{\gamma_{3}},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}([-\infty,\infty)),

where
\eta_{\gamma_{3}}
is the Poisson process whose intensity measure has density
\gamma_{3}
, given by

\displaystyle\forall u\in[-\infty,\infty),

\displaystyle\;\gamma_{3}(u)=\alpha\nu e^{\alpha u}.

Proof.

We introduce the function
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
and
\gamma_{3}
exactly like in the statement of the Proposition. For
E
a subspace of
[-\infty,\infty]
, let us write
C_{K}^{+}(E)
for the set of continuous, real-valued and non-negative functions on
E
with compact support. We begin with the case
\alpha<1/2
. Fix
\varphi\in C_{K}^{+}([0,\infty))
. By a simple change of variables, we get

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(n^{1/2-\alpha}r(X_{1}^{n}))\right]

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(n^{1/2-\alpha}r)\rho_{n}(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(u)\frac{\alpha\sinh(\alpha un^{\alpha-1/2})}{\cosh(\alpha R_{n})-1}n^{1/2+\alpha}\textbf{1}_{\left\{u\leq n^{1/2-\alpha}R_{n}\right\}}du.

Using that
\sinh(x)\sim x
for
x
close to
0
and approximating
\cosh(R_{n}/2)
by
n/(2\nu)
, we get by the dominated convergence theorem,

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(n^{1/2-\alpha}r(X_{1}^{n}))\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(u)\gamma_{1}(u)du.

(6)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we denote by
\Psi_{n}
the Laplace functional associated with the point process
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{1/2-\alpha}r(X_{i}^{n})}
and we denote by
\Psi
the Laplace functional associated with
\eta_{\gamma_{1}}
. Since the variables
r(X_{1}^{n}),\dots,r(X_{n}^{n})
are independent, it follows from (
6
) and a classical computation that for all
f\in C_{K}^{+}([0,\infty))
,
\Psi_{n}(f)\to\Psi(f)
(see
[
20
, Proposition 3.21]
for a detailed computation). This is sufficient to establish the desired convergence in distribution for
\alpha<1/2
.

In the case
\alpha=1/2
, for
\varphi\in C_{K}^{+}([0,\infty))
, we have

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(r(X_{1}^{n}))\right]

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r)\frac{\sinh(r/2)}{2(\cosh(R_{n}/2)-1)}\textbf{1}_{\left\{r\leq R_{n}\right\}}dr

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r)\gamma_{2}(r)dr,

where the convergence follows from approximating
\cosh(R_{n}/2)
by
n/(2\nu)
and applying the dominated convergence theorem. Thus, we can conclude exactly like in the case
\alpha<1/2
.

In the case
\alpha>1/2
, a simple change of variables gives, for
\varphi\in C_{K}^{+}([-\infty,\infty))
,

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(r(X_{1}^{n})-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi(r-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n})\rho_{n}(r)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}}^{R_{n}/(2\alpha)}\varphi(u)\frac{\alpha n\sinh(\alpha(u+(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}))}{\cosh(\alpha R_{n})-1}du.

(7)

Moreover, there exists
K>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and all

,

\displaystyle\varphi(u)\frac{\alpha n\sinh(\alpha(u+(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}))}{\cosh(\alpha R_{n})-1}\leq K\varphi(u)\exp(\alpha u).

The bound above is integrable on
\mathbb{R}
. Applying the dominated convergence theorem to (
4
) and using that
\cosh(x)\sim e^{x}/2
and
\sinh(x)\sim e^{x}/2
for

large, we get

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(r(X_{1}^{n})-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n})\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(u)\gamma_{3}(u)du.

We conclude exactly like in the case
\alpha<1/2
.
∎

V_{\alpha}
is a decreasing diffeomorphism from
[0,+\infty)
to
(0,1]
follows directly from its expression. Combining this with the fact that the functions
r\mapsto\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))
are decreasing and take values in
(0,1]
, we conclude that the convergence is necessarily uniform on
[0,\infty)
.
∎

We conclude this section by providing estimates for the volume of balls centred at the origin. These volumes are much easier to obtain than those of the balls studied earlier, as integrating the density
\rho_{n}
directly gives, for all
r\in(0,R_{n})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0}(r))=\frac{\cosh(\alpha r)-1}{\cosh(\alpha R_{n})-1}.

Approximating the hyperbolic terms, we get the following estimates.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let us fix
\alpha>0
and a sequence
u_{n}\to 0
. We have for
r\in[0,u_{n}]
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0}(r))

\displaystyle=(\alpha r)^{2}e^{-\alpha R_{n}}(1+o(1)).

(21)

Let us fix
\alpha>0
and a sequence
v_{n}\to\infty
. We have for
r\in[v_{n},R_{n})
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0}(r))

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(r-R_{n})}(1+o(1)).

(22)

6
Constant rank Ordering (Proof of Theorem
3.1
)

The results of the two previous sections give enough information on the degrees of the closest nodes to the centre to proceed with the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Indeed, the convergence of the (normalised) point process of the radii, as stated in Proposition
4.1
, also gives estimates for the radius gap between consecutive nodes,
r(X_{(i+1)})-r(X_{(i)})
. This, combined with the volume estimates of the previous part allows us to get a lower bound on the difference of the expected degrees of the nodes
X_{(i)}
and
X_{(j)}
with
i<j
. The following lemma provides a way to translate this information into a bound on the probability that
X_{(i)}
has smaller degree than
X_{(j)}
. Note that the two variables
A
and
B
are not required to be independent. Thus
A
and
B
may be legitimately replaced by two node degrees that we want to compare. In the following, we denote by
\mbox{Bin}\left(m,p\right)
the binomial distribution with parameters
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
p\in[0,1]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A\sim\mbox{Bin}\left(n,a\right)
and
B\sim\mbox{Bin}\left(n,b\right)
. If
0<b\leq a
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\leq B\right]\leq 2\exp\Big(-\tfrac{(a-b)^{2}n}{8a}\Big).

Proof.

By splitting at
n\frac{a+b}{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\leq B\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[B\geq n\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[A\leq n\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[B\geq\left(1+\tfrac{a-b}{2b}\right)nb\right]+\mathbb{P}\left[A\leq\left(1-\tfrac{a-b}{2a}\right)na\right].

For
Z\sim\mbox{Bin}\left(n,p\right)
and
\mu=np
, we recall the multiplicative Chernoff bounds:

\displaystyle\forall\delta\geq 0

\displaystyle,\;\mathbb{P}\left[Z\geq(1+\delta)\mu\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2+\delta}\mu}

\displaystyle\forall\delta\in[0,1)

\displaystyle,\;\mathbb{P}\left[Z\leq(1-\delta)\mu\right]\leq e^{-\mu\delta^{2}/2}.

Since
\frac{a-b}{2b}\geq 0
and
0\leq\frac{a-b}{2a}<1
, we can use these bounds to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\leq B\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(-\tfrac{(a-b)^{2}n}{2(a+3b)}\Big)+\exp\Big(-\frac{(a-b)^{2}n}{8a}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\Big(-\tfrac{(a-b)^{2}n}{8a}\Big),

which proves our claim.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let us fix
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and define the ordering event
O_{n}
as:

\displaystyle\forall i>k,\;\deg(X_{(1)})>\deg(X_{(2)})>\dots>\deg(X_{(k)})>\deg(X_{(i)}).

We want to show that the complementary
O_{n}^{c}
of this event has probability converging to
0
. We start with the case
\alpha<1/2
. The case
\alpha>1/2
can be handled in a similar fashion but we do not present it since the result in this case follows from the stronger result of Theorem
3.2
. We deal with the case
\alpha=1/2
at the end of the proof.

Define the localisation event
L_{n}
and the gap event
G_{n}
by

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{t_{n}\leq r(X_{(1)})\;\mbox{and}\;r(X_{(k)})\leq r_{n}\},

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\forall i\leq k,\,r(X_{(i+1)})-r(X_{(i)})\geq\lambda_{n}\},

where the sequences
t_{n}
,
r_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}
are defined for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
by

\displaystyle t_{n}\coloneqq n^{\alpha-1/2}\log(n)^{-1},\quad r_{n}\coloneqq n^{\alpha-1/2}\log(n),\quad\lambda_{n}\coloneqq n^{\alpha-1/2}\log(n)^{-1}.

By the convergence result stated in Proposition
4.1
and the characterisation of convergence in distribution in
M_{p}([a,b))
given in Remark
3.4
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

So, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(23)

If the event
O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}
occurs, then there must exist
i\leq k
and
i<j
such that
\deg(X_{(i)})\leq\deg(X_{(j)})
. Since
L_{n}
occurs, we also know that
t_{n}\leq r(X_{(i)})\leq r_{n}
and since
G_{n}
occurs, we know that
r(X_{(j)})-r(X_{(i)})\geq\lambda_{n}
. So, the probability to control can be bounded like this (note that the bound does not refer to the reordering of the points)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\exists i\neq j,\;t_{n}\leq r(X_{i})\leq r_{n},\,r(X_{j})-r(X_{i})\geq\lambda_{n},\,\deg(X_{i})\leq\deg(X_{j})\right].

Using a union bound and noting
\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(X_{1},X_{2})}\left[\cdot\right]
for the conditional probability with respect to
(X_{1},X_{2})
, we finally get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\leq n^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle(X_{1},X_{2})}\left[\deg(X_{1})\leq\deg(X_{2})\right]\textbf{1}_{\left\{t_{n}\leq r(X_{1})\leq r_{n},\;r(X_{2})-r(X_{1})\geq\lambda_{n}\right\}}\right].

Conditionally on the couple
(X_{1},X_{2})
, the variables
\deg(X_{1})
and
\deg(X_{2})
have binomial distributions with
n-2
trials and probabilities
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r(X_{1})}(R_{n}))
and
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r(X_{2})}(R_{n}))
, respectively (we neglect the potential connection between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, since it does not contribute to the inequality between their degrees). Given that
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r(X_{1})}(R_{n}))
is larger than
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r(X_{2})}(R_{n}))
(see Lemma
5.1
), we can apply Lemma
6.1
to bound the conditional probability of the previous display. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\leq\max\limits_{(s_{1},s_{2})}2n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-2)(\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n})))^{2}}{8\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))}\right),

(24)

where the maximum is taken over the couples
(s_{1},s_{2})
belonging to the set

\displaystyle E_{n}\coloneqq\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in[0,R_{n})^{2}\,|\,t_{n}\leq s_{1}\leq r_{n},\,s_{2}-s_{1}\geq\lambda_{n}\}.

Using Lemma
5.4
to approximate the volume of the balls
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{j}}(R_{n}))
for
j=1,2
, we get
K>0
such that, for large
n
and
(s_{1},s_{2})\in E_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n})))^{2}}{8\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))}\geq Kn\lambda_{n}^{2}=Kn^{2\alpha}\log(n)^{-2}.

Reporting this in (
24
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{2}e^{-Kn^{2\alpha}\log(n)^{-2}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

(25)

which proves (
23
) and concludes the proof of this case.

Now, let us proceed with the case
\alpha=1/2
. We take
\varepsilon>0
and for all
\delta,\lambda>0
, we introduce the following set:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\delta)\coloneqq\{r\geq 0:V_{\alpha}(r)-V_{\alpha}(r+\lambda)>\delta\},

where
V_{\alpha}
is the function defined in Lemma
5.5
.
In this case, we define the localisation event
L_{n}
and the gap event
G_{n}
by

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\forall i\leq k,\,r(X_{(i)})\in\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\delta)\}

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{\forall i\leq k,\,r(X_{(i+1)})-r(X_{(i)})\geq\lambda\},

where the parameters
\lambda
and
\delta
will be fixed later. Since the function
V_{\alpha}
is strictly decreasing, we find that, for every
\lambda>0
, the set
\bigcup_{\delta>0}\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\delta)
covers the entire interval
[0,+\infty)
.
Moreover, the convergence result stated in Proposition
4.1
(without normalisation in the case
\alpha=1/2
) and the characterisation of convergence in distribution in
M_{p}([a,b))
given in Remark
3.4
, imply that the vector
(r(X_{(i)}),\,i\leq k+1)
converges in distribution. So, we can fix
\lambda>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Therefore, it remains to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(26)

Proceeding exactly as we did for proving (
24
) in the previous case, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{(s_{1},s_{2})}2n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-2)(\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n})))^{2}}{8\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))}\right),

(27)

where the maximum is taken over the couples
(s_{1},s_{2})
belonging to the set

\displaystyle E_{n}\coloneqq\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in[0,R_{n})^{2}\,|\,s_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\delta),\,s_{2}-s_{1}\geq\lambda\}.

By the definition of
\mathcal{L}(\lambda,\delta)
and the uniform convergence of
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))
towards
V_{\alpha}(r)
(see Lemma
5.5
), it follows that, for
n
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\forall(s_{1},s_{2})\in E_{n},\;\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n}))\geq\delta/2.

Reporting this in (
27
) and bounding
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))
by
1
proves (
26
) and concludes the proof of this case.

∎

7
Convergence of the Degrees (Proof of Theorem
3.3
)

The key to proving the convergence of the largest degrees is to show that the degrees of the closest nodes to the centre concentrate around their conditional expected values, given the positions of the corresponding nodes. This implies that the point process of the degrees is comparable to the image of the point process of the radii under the mapping
r\mapsto\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))
. The conclusion then follows from the convergence of the point process of the radii and the continuous mapping theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let us begin with the case
\alpha>1/2
. The convergence of the normalised degree process towards a point process
\mathcal{P}
will be stated if we prove that for all
k
, the normalised vector of the
k
highest degrees converges in distribution to the vector of the
k
largest points of
\mathcal{P}
(see Remark
3.4
). By Theorem
3.1
, with high probability, the vector of the
k
highest degrees is the vector of the degrees of the
k
closest points to the centre (in the same order). So, to prove the convergence of the normalised degree process towards a point process
\mathcal{P}
, it suffices to prove that, for all
k
,

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}(\deg(X_{(1)}),\deg(X_{(2)}),\dots\deg(X_{(k)}))\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}(Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{k}),

(28)

where the variables
Y_{1}\geq\dots\geq Y_{k}
are the
k
largest points of the point process
\mathcal{P}
. For all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

\displaystyle p_{n}(s)\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s}(R_{n})\setminus\mathcal{B}_{0}(s))}{1-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0}(s))}\textbf{1}_{\left\{s\in[0,R_{n})\right\}}

and we define the vectors
\Delta_{k}^{n}
,
\widetilde{\Delta_{k}^{n}}
and
W_{k}^{n}
by

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\deg(X_{(1)}),\deg(X_{(2)})\dots,\deg(X_{(k)}))

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Delta_{k}^{n}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(1)}),\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(2)})\dots,\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(k)}))

\displaystyle W_{k}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq((n-i)p_{n}(r(X_{(i)})),\,1\leq i\leq k),

where
\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(k)})
denotes the number of neighbours of the point
X_{(k)}
that are in the annulus
\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(k)}),R_{n})
. We first show that
n^{-1/(2\alpha)}\Delta_{k}^{n}
can be approximated by
n^{-1/(2\alpha)}W_{k}^{n}
and in a second step we will prove the convergence of
n^{-1/(2\alpha)}W_{k}^{n}
.

Since the difference
\Delta_{k}^{n}-\widetilde{\Delta_{k}^{n}}
is bounded by
k
it is clear that

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}(\Delta_{k}^{n}-\widetilde{\Delta_{k}^{n}})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

(29)

For all
i
, conditionally on
X_{(i)}
, the
n-i
nodes with radius larger than
r(X_{(i)})
are independently and identically distributed according to the restriction of
\mu_{n}
to the annulus
\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(i)}),R_{n})
. So, conditionally on
X_{(i)}
, the variable
\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})
is distributed according to the binomial distribution:

\displaystyle\mbox{Bin}\left(n-i,p_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\right).

(30)

Let us fix
K>0
and denote by
L_{n}
the event that all the variables
r(X_{(i)}),\,i\leq k
belong to the interval
[(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}-K,(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}+K]
. Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
. By the convergence of the radii stated in Proposition
4.1
, we can choose
K>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
, the event
L_{n}
occurs with probability greater than
1-\varepsilon
. By the volume estimates (
12
) and (
22
), we know that there exists
K^{\prime}>1
such that, on the event
L_{n}
, for large
n
and
i\leq k
, we have

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{K^{\prime}}n^{1/(2\alpha)}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})\,\big|\,X_{(i)}\right]\leq K^{\prime}n^{1/(2\alpha)}.

(31)

Let us fix
\delta>0
. Denoting by
\mathbb{P}_{L_{n}}
the probability measure conditioned on the event
L_{n}
, it follows from (
30
) and (
31
) that, for large
n
and
i\leq k
,

\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle L_{n}}\left[\big|\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})-(n-i)p_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\big|\geq n^{1/(2\alpha)}\delta\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle L_{n}}\left[\Big|\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})-\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})\,\big|\,X_{(i)}\right]\Big|\geq\tfrac{\delta\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})\,|\,X_{(i)}\right]}{K^{\prime}}\right].

Combining this with a Chernoff bound and using (
31
) again yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle L_{n}}\left[\big|\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})-(n-i)p_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\big|\geq n^{1/(2\alpha)}\delta\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

From this and (
29
) we conclude that

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}(\Delta_{k}^{n}-W_{k}^{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

(32)

Now, it remains to prove the convergence in distribution of the vector
n^{-1/(2\alpha)}W_{k}^{n}
. For this purpose, we remark that

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}W_{k}^{n}=\varphi_{n}\big(r(X_{(i)})-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n},\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

(33)

where for all
n
, the application
\varphi_{n}
is defined from
\mathbb{R}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\big(z_{i},1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(n^{-1/(2\alpha)}(n-i)p_{n}(z_{i}+(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big).

By the volume estimates (
12
) and (
22
), the applications
\varphi_{n}
converges uniformly on every compact of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
towards the function
\varphi
defined by

\displaystyle\varphi\big(z_{i},1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(T(z_{i}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

where the application
T
is a diffeomorphism from
[-\infty,\infty)
to
(0,\infty]
given by

\displaystyle T(z)\coloneqq C_{\alpha}\nu^{1-1/(2\alpha)}e^{-z/2}.

Let us denote by
Z_{1}\leq\dots\leq Z_{k}
the
k
smallest points of the point process
\eta_{\gamma_{3}}
(see Proposition
4.1
for the definition of
\gamma_{3}
). By the convergence of the radii stated in Proposition
4.1
, the vector
\big(r(X_{(i)})-(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n},1\leq i\leq k\big)
converges in distribution to
(Z_{i},1\leq i\leq k)
. Consequently, from (
33
) and the continuous mapping theorem (see for example
[
16
, Theorem 5.27]
for a version of the continuous mapping theorem with mappings depending on
n
), we obtain that

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}W_{k}^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\varphi\big(Z_{i},\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

(34)

Combining (
32
) and (
34
) with Slutsky theorem finally gives

\displaystyle n^{-1/(2\alpha)}\Delta_{k}^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\varphi\big(Z_{i},\,1\leq i\leq k\big).

This proves that (
28
) holds for all
k
with the limit measure
\eta_{\gamma_{3}}\circ T^{-1}
, so

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-1/(2\alpha)}\deg(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta_{\gamma_{3}}\circ T^{-1},\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((0,\infty]),

By classical results on transformations of Poisson processes, the point process
\eta_{\gamma_{3}}\circ T^{-1}
is a Poisson process with an intensity measure whose density
g_{3}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure is given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(0,\infty],\,g_{3}(y)=\frac{\gamma_{3}(T^{-1}(y))}{|T^{\prime}(T^{-1}(y))|}=2\alpha(C_{\alpha}\nu)^{2\alpha}y^{-2\alpha-1}.

This concludes the proof for this case. The proofs for the two other regimes follow the same method. We will omit the parts that are too much similar to the proof of the previous case.

In the case
\alpha=1/2
, we want to prove convergence in distribution of the vector

\displaystyle n^{-1}(\deg(X_{(1)}),\deg(X_{(2)})\dots\deg(X_{(k)})).

We define the functions
p_{n}
and the vectors
\Delta_{k}^{n}
and
W_{k}^{n}
like in the previous case. Using the convergence of the radii stated in Proposition
4.1
, along with the volume estimate given by (
22
) and Lemma
5.5
, one get that, conditionally on the position of
X_{(i)}
, with high probability, the expected value of
\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})
is comparable to
n
. So, by copying the proof of (
32
), we get in this case

\displaystyle n^{-1}(\Delta_{k}^{n}-W_{k}^{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

It remains to prove the convergence in distribution of the vector
n^{-1}W_{k}^{n}
. For this purpose, we remark that

\displaystyle n^{-1}W_{k}^{n}=\varphi_{n}\big(r(X_{(i)}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

(35)

where the application
\varphi_{n}
is defined from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\big(z_{i},\,1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(p_{n}(z_{i}),1\leq i\leq k\big).

By the volume estimates given by (
22
) and Lemma
5.5
, the applications
\varphi_{n}
converges uniformly on every compact of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
towards the function
\varphi
defined by

\displaystyle\varphi\big(z_{i},1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}(z_{i}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

So, combining (
35
) with the convergence of the radii to
\eta_{\gamma_{2}}
as we did in the case
\alpha>1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-1}\deg(X_{i}^{n})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta,\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((0,1]),

where
\eta
is a Poisson process with an intensity measure whose density
g_{2}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure is given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(0,1],\,g_{2}(y)=\frac{\gamma_{2}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1}(y))}{|V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{\prime}(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1}(y))|}=\nu|(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1})^{\prime}(y)|\sinh(V_{\scriptscriptstyle 1/2}^{-1}(y)/2).

This concludes the proof of this case.

In the case
\alpha<1/2
, we are interested in proving convergence in distribution of the following vector

\displaystyle n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}(\deg(X_{(1)})-n,\deg(X_{(2)})-n,\dots\deg(X_{(k)})-n).

Because of the additional normalisation by
n
, we shall define the vectors
\Delta_{k}^{n}
and
W_{k}^{n}
in a different manner in this case, by letting

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(\deg(X_{(1)})-n,\deg(X_{(2)})-n\dots\deg(X_{(k)})-n)

\displaystyle W_{k}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq((n-i)(p_{n}\big(r(X_{(i)})\big)-1),\,1\leq i\leq k).

Using the convergence of the radii stated in Proposition
4.1
, together with the volume estimates given by (
21
) and Lemma
5.4
, we get that, conditionally on the position of
X_{(i)}
, with high probability, the expected value of
\widetilde{\deg}(X_{(i)})-n
is comparable to
-n^{\alpha+1/2}
. So, by copying the proof of (
32
), we get in this case

\displaystyle n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}(\Delta_{k}^{n}-W_{k}^{n})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0.

It remains to prove the convergence in distribution of the vector
n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}W_{k}^{n}
. For this purpose, we remark that

\displaystyle n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}W_{k}^{n}=\varphi_{n}\big(n^{1/2-\alpha}r(X_{(i)}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

(36)

where, the application
\varphi_{n}
is defined from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}_{-}^{k}
by

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\big(z_{i},1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}(n-i)(p_{n}(n^{\alpha-1/2}z_{i})-1),\,1\leq i\leq k\big).

By the volume estimates given by (
21
) and Lemma
5.4
, the applications
\varphi_{n}
converges uniformly on every compact of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k}
towards the function
\varphi
defined by

\displaystyle\varphi\big(z_{i},1\leq i\leq k\big)\coloneqq\big(T(z_{i}),\,1\leq i\leq k\big),

where
T
is a diffeomorphism from
[0,+\infty)
to
(-\infty,0]
given by

\displaystyle T(z)\coloneqq-\frac{\alpha z}{\pi}.

So, proceeding exactly like in the case
\alpha>1/2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{-(\alpha+1/2)}(\deg(X_{i}^{n})-n)}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\eta,\quad\mbox{in }M_{p}((-\infty,0]),

where
\eta
is a Poisson process with an intensity measure whose density
g_{1}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure is given by

\displaystyle\forall y\in(-\infty,0],\,g_{1}(y)=\frac{\gamma_{1}(T^{-1}(y))}{|T^{\prime}(T^{-1}(y))|}=2\pi^{2}\nu^{2\alpha}|y|.

This concludes the proof of this case.

∎

8
Ordering/non-Ordering transition (Theorem
3.2
)

From the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we observe that the key quantities determining the ordering properties of the node degrees are the differences between the volumes of successive balls,
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{X_{(i)}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{X_{(i+1)}}(R_{n}))
. We expect the ordering to break around the first
i
for which this difference is sufficiently small. The following lemma is a refinement of Lemma
5.3
that allows to obtain fine estimates on these differences.

Lemma 8.1
.

For
\alpha>1/2
, there exists
r_{0}>0
such that, for
r\in(r_{0},R_{n})
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))=-\tfrac{C_{\alpha}}{2}e^{-r/2}(1+O(e^{-(\alpha-1/2)r}+re^{-r})),

where
C_{\alpha}\coloneqq\frac{2\alpha}{\pi(\alpha-1/2)}
(as in Lemma
5.3
).

We explain in Remark
8.2
why this estimate is necessary for proving Theorem
3.2
when
\alpha
is close to
1/2
. We defer the proof of this lemma to the end of the section and proceed directly with the proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

In this proof
K
stands for a positive constant (depending only on
\alpha
and
\nu
) whose value may change throughout the proof. We define the sequence
k_{n}
as in the statement of the Theorem. The proof of the two assertions (
4
) and (
5
) are done separately.

Ordering up to rank
k_{n}
(Proof of (
4
))

\bullet

First step: Localisation of the
k_{n}
first nodes

Let
w_{n}\coloneqq\log(\log(n))
and define the localisation event
L_{n}
by

\displaystyle L_{n}\coloneqq\{t_{n}\leq r(X_{(1)})\;\mbox{and}\;r(X_{(k_{n})})\leq r_{n}\},

where

\displaystyle t_{n}\coloneqq(1-\tfrac{1}{2\alpha})R_{n}-w_{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad r_{n}\coloneqq t_{n}+\tfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\log(n).

Let us prove that, with this choice of
t_{n}
and
r_{n}
, the event
L_{n}
is realised with high probability. We already know from Proposition
4.1
that
t_{n}\leq r(X_{(1)})
holds with high probability. On the other hand, we note that
r(X_{(k_{n})})\leq r_{n}
holds if and only if the number of nodes in the ball
\mathcal{B}_{0}(r_{n})
is larger than
k_{n}
. The number of points falling in this ball has a binomial distribution with expected value

\displaystyle n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{0}(r_{n}))\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}\nu n^{\beta}\log(n)^{-\alpha}\gg k_{n}.

Using a Chernoff bound it follows that
r(X_{(k_{n})})\leq r_{n}
holds with high probability, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[L_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

\bullet

Second step: Proving existence of large gaps between the
k_{n}
first nodes

We define the gap event
G_{n}
by

\displaystyle G_{n}\coloneqq\left\{\forall i\leq k_{n},\;r(X_{(i+1)})-r(X_{(i)})\geq\lambda_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\right\},

where, for all
n
, the function
\lambda_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\forall s\geq 0,\;\lambda_{n}(s)\coloneqq e^{\alpha(R_{n}-s)}n^{-(\beta+1)}.

Let us prove that with this choice of
\lambda_{n}
, the event
G_{n}
is realised with high probability, which will prove that the radius gaps between the
n^{\beta}
first nodes are relatively large.

The first
k_{n}+1
nodes (i.e.
X_{(1)},X_{(2)},\dots,X_{(k_{n}+1)}
) can be sampled in the following way. First, sample
n
nodes in
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
according to the distribution
\mu_{n}
, select the closest to the centre as
X_{(1)}
and erase the
n-1
other points. Next, sample
n-1
points according to the restriction of
\mu_{n}
to the annulus
\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(1)}),R_{n})
, choose the closest to the centre as
X_{(2)}
and erase the other ones. Repeat this process until the
k_{n}+1
first nodes have been sampled (at step
i+1
sample
n-i
points in the annulus
\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(i)}),R_{n})
, choose the closest to the centre as
X_{(i+1)}
and erase the other ones). Considering this process, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[G_{n}^{c},\,L_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{P}\left[Z_{i}^{n}\neq 0,\,L_{n}\right],

where
Z_{i}^{n}
counts the number of points that fall in the annulus
\mathcal{C}\big(r(X_{(i)}),r(X_{(i)})+\lambda_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\big)
at step
i+1
. Conditionally on the position of
X_{(i)}
, the variable
Z_{i}^{n}
follows a binomial distribution with
n-i
trials and probability
p_{i}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle p_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{n}\big(\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(i)}),r(X_{(i)})+\lambda_{n}(r(X_{(i)})))\big)}{\mu_{n}\big(\mathcal{C}(r(X_{(i)}),R_{n})\big)}.

From this expression, we conclude that, for
n
large enough and
t_{n}\leq r(X_{(i)})\leq r_{n}
,

\displaystyle p_{i}^{n}

\displaystyle\leq K\lambda_{n}(r(X_{(i)}))\exp(\alpha(r(X_{(i)})-R_{n}))=Kn^{-(\beta+1)}.

Writing
\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle X_{(i)}}\left[\cdot\right]
for conditional probability with respect to the variable
X_{(i)}
, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[G_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle X_{(i)}}\left[Z_{i}^{n}\neq 0\right]\textbf{1}_{\left\{t_{n}\leq r(X_{(i)})\leq r_{n}\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq k_{n}(1-(1-Kn^{-(\beta+1)})^{n})

\displaystyle\underset{n\to\infty}{\sim}K\log(n)^{-2\alpha}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

Since
L_{n}
occurs with high probability, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[G_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

\bullet

Third step: Comparing the successive expected values of the degrees

Let us denote by
O_{n}
the ordering event described in (
4
). By the previous two steps, it remains to prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

(37)

Proceeding as we did for proving (
24
) in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we get the following upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{(s_{1},s_{2})}2n^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{(n-2)(\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n})))^{2}}{8\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))}\right),

(38)

where the maximum is taken over the couples
(s_{1},s_{2})
belonging to the set

\displaystyle E_{n}\coloneqq\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in[0,R_{n})^{2}\,|\,t_{n}\leq s_{1}\leq r_{n},\,s_{2}-s_{1}\geq\lambda_{n}(s_{1})\}.

We are now interested in bounding the fraction appearing in the exponential term. Using Lemma
8.1
to estimate the numerator and (
12
) to estimate the denominator, we get
K>0
such that, for
(s_{1},s_{2})\in E_{n}
and
n
large,

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)(\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))-\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{2}}(R_{n})))^{2}}{8\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{s_{1}}(R_{n}))}

\displaystyle\geq K\frac{n(\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}e^{-y/2}dy)^{2}}{e^{-s_{1}/2}}

\displaystyle\geq Kne^{-s_{1}/2}(1-e^{-(s_{2}-s_{1})/2})^{2}

\displaystyle\geq Kne^{-r_{n}/2}\lambda_{n}(r_{n})^{2},

(39)

Moreover, straightforward computations give

\displaystyle ne^{-r_{n}/2}=Kn^{\tfrac{1-\beta}{2\alpha}}\log(n)^{1/2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\lambda_{n}(r_{n})=Kn^{-2\beta}\log(n)^{\alpha}

By choice of
\beta
, we have
\frac{1-\beta}{2\alpha}=4\beta
. So, reporting the above in (
39
) and combining with (
38
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[O_{n}^{c}\cap L_{n}\cap G_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{2}\exp(-K\log(n)^{1/2+2\alpha})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

which proves (
37
) and concludes the proof of (
4
).

Remark 8.2
.

One could obtain a bound similar to (
39
) by using the volume estimate from
[

instead of Lemma

to bound the numerator. However for

which is not sufficiently precise here. The estimate of the differential of

in

is more suitable for measuring volume differences of balls with close radii.

n
large enough,

\displaystyle n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V}(R_{n}))\leq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V}(R_{n})\cap\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n}^{\varepsilon}))\leq n^{-5\varepsilon^{\prime}}ne^{-t_{n}^{\prime}/2}\leq n^{-4\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{n}^{2}

(the sequence
\delta_{n}
is defined below (
40
)). Since
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V}(R_{n})
does not intersect the annulus
\mathcal{C}(t_{n}^{\prime},r_{n}^{\prime})
, we have that, conditionally on
C_{n}
and on the position of the node
V
, the variable
\deg^{\prime}(V)
follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V}(R_{n}))
. Thus, by Chebyshev’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle C_{n}}\left[\deg^{\prime}(V)\leq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V}(R_{n}))+n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

(46)

With a similar argument, we also prove that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle C_{n}}\left[\deg^{\prime}(V^{\prime})\geq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V^{\prime}}(R_{n}))-n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{n}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

(47)

Now, concerning
\deg^{\prime\prime}
, a direct computation gives, for
r\in[0,R_{n})
,

\displaystyle n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{C}(t_{n}^{\prime},r_{n}^{\prime}))=o(n^{\beta}\log(n)^{3\alpha}).

(48)

Since
\deg^{\prime\prime}(V)
is smaller than the number of nodes that fall in the annulus
\mathcal{C}(t_{n}^{\prime},r_{n}^{\prime})
, it follows from Chebyshev’s inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\scriptscriptstyle C_{n}}\left[\deg^{\prime\prime}(V)\leq n^{\beta}\log(n)^{3\alpha}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}1.

(49)

Combining the definition of
V
with (
46
) and (
49
), we get, with high probability,

\displaystyle\deg(V)

\displaystyle=\deg_{\varepsilon}(V)+\deg^{\prime}(V)+\deg^{\prime\prime}(V)

\displaystyle\leq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\varepsilon}_{V}(R_{n}))+n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V}(R_{n}))+n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{n}+n^{\beta}\log(n)^{3\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V}(R_{n}))+o(\delta_{n}).

(50)

Likewise, combining the definition of
V^{\prime}
with (
47
) and (
48
), we get, with high probability,

\displaystyle\deg(V^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq\deg_{\varepsilon}(V^{\prime})+\deg^{\prime}(V^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\varepsilon}_{V^{\prime}}(R_{n}))+2\delta_{n}+n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{V^{\prime}}(R_{n}))-n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{n}

\displaystyle\geq n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V^{\prime}}(R_{n}))+2\delta_{n}+o(\delta_{n}).

(51)

On the other hand, using that
r(V^{\prime})-r(V)\leq\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
, Lemma
8.1
yields,

\displaystyle n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V}(R_{n}))-n\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V^{\prime}}(R_{n}))\leq Kne^{-t_{n}^{\prime}/2}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}=o(\delta_{n}).

Combining this with (
8
) and (
8
) proves that, with high probability,

\displaystyle\deg(V)+\delta_{n}\leq\deg(V^{\prime}).

(52)

Since
t_{n}^{\prime}\leq r(V)\leq r(V^{\prime})\leq r_{n}^{\prime}
, this concludes the proof of (
40
).

\bullet

Fourth step: de-Poissonisation

Let us now explain how to transfer the result from the Poissonised model
\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Poi}}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
to the original model
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
. We use the standard procedure that consists in coupling the original model with the Poissonised model by sampling the points of
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
in the following manner: sample the graph
\mathcal{G}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Poi}}}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and call
N_{n}
the number of nodes in this graph. If
N_{n}>n
, randomly remove
N_{n}-n
nodes from the graph. If
N_{n}<n
, add
n-N_{n}
nodes, independently sampled from
\mathcal{B}_{0}(R_{n})
according to
\mu_{n}
and connect all pairs of nodes that are within distance
R_{n}
. The resulting graph follows the same distribution as
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha,\nu}(n)
and is referred to as the de-Poissonised graph.

For a node
x
that appears in both the Poissonised and de-Poissonised graph, we denote its degree in each graph by
\deg(x)
and
\widetilde{\deg}(x)
, respectively. Fix
\varepsilon>0
small enough. The random variable
N_{n}
follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
n
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[|N_{n}-n|\geq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right]\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0.

From this, it follows that, with high probability, the nodes
V
and
V^{\prime}
are not removed during the de-Poissonisation procedure. For the remainder of the proof, we work on the event that
V
and
V^{\prime}
are not removed. In the case where
N_{n}<n
, conditionally on the position of
V
, the variable
\widetilde{\deg}(V)-\deg(V)
follows a binomial distribution with
n-N_{n}
trials and probability parameter
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{V}(R_{n}))
. Thus, with high probability,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\deg}(V)-\deg(V)\leq n^{1/2+2\varepsilon}\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r_{n}^{\prime}}(R_{n}))\leq Kn^{2\beta+2\varepsilon-1/2}e^{-w_{n}^{\prime}/2}\delta_{n}=o(\delta_{n}),

where the last equality holds if
\varepsilon
is chosen sufficiently small (because
\beta<1/5
). In the case
N_{n}>n
, we can also prove with similar arguments that the de-Poissonisation reduces the degree of
V^{\prime}
by at most
o(\delta_{n})
. Therefore, we finally conclude from (
52
) that
\widetilde{\deg}(V)<\widetilde{\deg}(V^{\prime})
holds with high probability. In addition, straightforward estimates, using the fact that
t_{n}^{\prime}\leq r(V)\leq r(V^{\prime})\leq r_{n}^{\prime}
, show that in the de-Poissonised graph, the ranks of the nodes
V
and
V^{\prime}
in the ranking of the nodes by increasing radii are in the interval
[n^{\beta},n^{\beta}v_{n}^{3\alpha}]
, with high probability. It follows that (
5
) holds with high probability (the power
3\alpha
is not problematic, as the sequence
v_{n}
is an arbitrary sequence diverging to
+\infty
).
∎

Let us conclude by proving Lemma
8.1
.

Proof of Lemma
8.1
.

We recall the integral expression (
8
) of
\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))
:

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{R_{n}}\theta_{r}(y)\rho_{n}(y)dy.

Using the expression of
\theta_{r}(y)
given by (
11
), we get

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{R_{n}-r}^{R_{n}}f_{n,r}(y)\rho_{n}(y)dy,

where
f_{n,r}(y)\coloneqq\tfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\arccos(c_{n,r}(y))
, with
c_{n,r}(y)\coloneqq\frac{\cosh(r)\cosh(y)-\cosh(R_{n})}{\sinh(r)\sinh(y)}
. Since the angle
\theta_{r}(y)
is decreasing in
r
(see Lemma
5.1
), the integral above is always well-defined in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. The contribution of the integral over
(R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0})
requires special treatment, because the quantity
f_{n,r}(y)
diverges as
y
approaches
R_{n}-r
. We show at the end of the proof that this contribution is
O(e^{-\alpha r})
(which can be incorporated into the first error term of the formula given in the statement). More precisely, we will prove at the end of the proof that, for
r\in(r_{0},R_{n})
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\mu_{n}(\mathcal{B}_{r}(R_{n}))=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{R_{n}-r+r_{0}}^{R_{n}}f_{n,r}(y)\rho_{n}(y)dy+O(e^{-\alpha r}).

(53)

For now, let us estimate the integrand for
r\in(r_{0},R_{n})
and
y\in(R_{n}-r+r_{0},R_{n})
. A direct computation gives

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\cosh(R_{n})\cosh(r)-\cosh(y)}{\sinh(r)^{2}\sinh(y)\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}.

Using
\cosh(x)=e^{x}(1/2+O(e^{-2x}))
and
\sin(x)=e^{x}(1/2+O(e^{-2x}))
, it follows that

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2e^{R_{n}-r-y}(1+O(e^{-2r})+O(e^{y-R_{n}-r}))}{(1+O(e^{-2r}+e^{-2y}))\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}.

(54)

If
r_{0}
is chosen large enough, using
1/(1+x)=1+O(x)
for
|x|<1/2
, we get

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2e^{R_{n}-r-y}(1+O(e^{y-R_{n}-r})+O(e^{-2y}))}{\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}.

(55)

Note that we used that
y-R_{n}-r\geq-2r
, to get rid of the
O(e^{-2r})
term.
A similar computation gives, for
r\in(r_{0},R_{n})
and
y\in(R_{n}-r+r_{0},R_{n})
,

\displaystyle c_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=1-2e^{R_{n}-r-y}+O(e^{-2r}+e^{-2y}).

So, if
r_{0}
is chosen large enough, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(R_{n}-r-y)/2}}{2}(1+O(e^{R_{n}-r-y})).

Reporting this in (
55
) yields, for
r\in(r_{0},R_{n})
and
y\in(R_{n}-r+r_{0},R_{n})
,

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=-e^{(R_{n}-r-y)/2}(1+O(e^{R_{n}-r-y})).

Estimating the hyperbolic terms in
\rho_{n}(y)
, it follows that

\displaystyle f_{n,r}(y)\rho_{n}(y)

\displaystyle=-\alpha e^{(1/2-\alpha)R_{n}-r/2}e^{(\alpha-1/2)y}(1+O(e^{R_{n}-r-y})+O(e^{-\alpha R_{n}})).

(56)

Solving the integral in (
53
) using (
56
), without taking into account the error terms, gives

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{R_{n}-r+r_{0}}^{R_{n}}e^{(1/2-\alpha)R_{n}-r/2}e^{(\alpha-1/2)y}dy

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{C_{\alpha}}{2}e^{-r/2}(1+O(e^{-(\alpha-1/2)r})).

If
\alpha\neq 3/2
, then the integral over the first error term gives

\displaystyle O\Big(\int_{R_{n}-r+r_{0}}^{R_{n}}e^{(1/2-\alpha)R_{n}-r/2}e^{(\alpha-1/2)y}e^{R_{n}-r-y}dy\Big)

\displaystyle=O(e^{-3r/2})+O(e^{-\alpha r}).

In the case
\alpha=3/2
, the addition of the error term
re^{-r}
in the statement makes it correct. The integral over the second error term (corresponding to the
O(e^{-\alpha R_{n}})
term of (
56
)) is of order
O(e^{-r/2-\alpha R_{n}})
, which can be incorporated into the first error term of the result. Thus, the computation of the integral appearing in (
53
) yields the correct estimate.

It remains to prove (
53
). This requires a bound on the speed of divergence of the function
f_{n,r}(y)
, as
y
approaches
R_{n}-r
. To obtain this bound, we first need to bound
c_{n,r}(y)
, for
y
in the neighbourhood of
R_{n}-r
. In the following,
K
stands for a positive constant whose value depends only on
\alpha,\nu
and
r_{0}
and may change throughout the proof. For two functions
f
and
g
we denote by
f\wedge g
(resp.
f\vee g
) the minimum (resp. maximum) of
f
and
g
. Using the formula
\cosh(a+b)=\cosh(a)\cosh(b)+\sinh(a)\sinh(b)
and the mean value theorem to bound the difference
\cosh(r+y)-\cosh(R_{n})
, we obtain, for
y\in(R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0})
,

\displaystyle c_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\cosh(r+y)-\sinh(r)\sinh(y)-\cosh(R_{n})}{\sinh(r)\sinh(y)}

\displaystyle\geq K(y-(R_{n}-r))e^{(R_{n}-r)-y}-1.

Since
(R_{n}-r)-y\geq-r_{0}
, we finally get

\displaystyle c_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle\geq(K(y-(R_{n}-r))-1)\wedge 0.

On the other hand, for
r_{0}
and
n
large enough, approximating the hyperbolic terms by exponentials gives, for
y\in(R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0})
,

\displaystyle c_{n,r}(y)

\displaystyle\leq 1-K.

Thus, for
y\in(R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0})
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(1-K)^{2}}}\vee\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-((K(y-(R_{n}-r))-1)\wedge 0)^{2}}}.

It follows that the function
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-c_{n,r}(y)^{2}}}
is integrable over the interval
(R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0})
and the integral is bounded by a constant that does not depend on
n
. From this and the approximation of
f_{n,r}(y)
given in (
54
) (which also holds for
y\in[R_{n}-r,R_{n}-r+r_{0}]
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{R_{n}-r}^{R_{n}-r+r_{0}}f_{n,r}(y)\rho_{n}(y)dy=O(\rho_{n}(R_{n}-r+r_{0}))=O(e^{-\alpha r}).

Which concludes the proof.
∎
