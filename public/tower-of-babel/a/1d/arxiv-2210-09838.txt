Heavy tailed and compactly supported distributions of quadratic Weyl sums with rational parameters
Francesco Cellarosi, Tariq Osman

Abstract
We consider quadratic Weyl sums S N ​ ( x , α , β ) = ∑ n = 1 N exp ⁡ [ 2 ​ π ​ i ​ ( ( 1 2 ​ n 2 + β ​ n ) ​ x + α ​ n ) ] S_{N}(x;\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\exp\!\left[2\pi i\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}n^{2}+\beta n\right)\!x+\alpha n\right)\right] for ( α , β ) ∈ ℚ 2 (\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2} , where x ∈ ℝ x\in\mathbb{R} is randomly distributed according to a probability measure absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We prove that the limiting distribution in the complex plane of 1 N ​ S N ​ ( x , α , β ) \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}S_{N}(x;\alpha,\beta) as N → ∞ N\to\infty is either heavy tailed or compactly supported, depending solely on α , β \alpha,\beta . In the heavy tailed case, the probability (according to the limiting distribution) of landing outside a ball of radius R R is shown to be asymptotic to 𝒯 ⁡ ( α , β ) ​ R − 4 \mathcal{T}(\alpha,\beta)R^{-4} , where the constant 𝒯 ⁡ ( α , β ) > 0 \mathcal{T}(\alpha,\beta)>0 is explicit. The result follows from an analogous statement for products of generalized quadratic Weyl sums of the form S N f ​ ( x , α , β ) = ∑ n ∈ ℤ f ⁡ ( n N ) ​ exp ⁡ [ 2 ​ π ​ i ​ ( ( 1 2 ​ n 2 + β ​ n ) ​ x + α ​ n ) ] S_{N}^{f}(x;\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f\left(\frac{n}{N}\right)\exp\!\left[2\pi i\left(\left(\tfrac{1}{2}n^{2}+\beta n\right)\!x+\alpha n\right)\right] where f f is regular. The precise tails of the limiting distribution of 1 N ​ S N f 1 ​ S N f 2 ¯ ​ ( x , α , β ) \frac{1}{N}S_{N}^{f_{1}}\overline{S_{N}^{f_{2}}}(x;\alpha,\beta) as N → ∞ N\to\infty can be described in terms of a measure –which depends on ( α , β ) (\alpha,\beta) – of a super level set of a product of two Jacobi theta functions on a noncompact homogenous space. Such measures are obtained by means of an equidistribution theorem for rational horocycle lifts to a torus bundle over the unit tangent bundle to a cover of the classical modular surface. The cardinality and the geometry of orbits of rational points of the torus under the affine action of the theta group play a crucial role in the computation of 𝒯 ⁡ ( α , β ) \mathcal{T}(\alpha,\beta) . This work complements and extends [ 6 ] and [ 32 ] , in which the cases ( α , β ) ∉ ℚ 2 (\alpha,\beta)\notin\mathbb{Q}^{2} and α = β = 0 \alpha=\beta=0 are considered.

Definition 1.0.1

Denote by

Moreover, we shall refer to rationals pairs whose denominator is congruent to 2 modulo 4 and whose numerators are both odd as

to indicate that

has denominator

Let us also introduce the Dedekind

\displaystyle\psi(n)=n\prod_{p|n}\left(1+\frac{1}{p}\right).

(1.4)

We prove the following

Theorem 1.0.2
.

Let

absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Let

(i)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}=0.

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}\sim\mathcal{T}(q;r)R^{-4},

\displaystyle C(q)=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{\psi(m)}&if $\ell=0$ or ($\ell=1$ and either $a$ or $b$ is even);\\
\frac{1}{2^{\ell-1}\psi(m)}&if $\ell>1$,\end{cases}

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{rat}}(r)=\begin{cases}2\log 2&\mbox{if $r=1$;}\\
2r\,\mathrm{coth}^{-1}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\log(r^{2}-1)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\log(1-\frac{1}{r^{2}})&\mbox{if $r>1$.}\end{cases}

(1.9)

Theorem 1.0.7
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}

(i)

If

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}^{f_{1}}\overline{S_{N}^{f_{2}}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}=0.

(1.13)

(ii)

If
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathscr{H}(q)
, there exists a constant
\mathcal{T}(q;f_{1},f_{2})>0

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}^{f_{1}}\overline{S_{N}^{f_{2}}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}\sim\mathcal{T}(q;f_{1},f_{2})R^{-4}.

2.1
The Heisenberg Group and the Schrödinger Representation

Let
\omega:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be the standard symplectic form, i.e.

\omega\!\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\xi_{1}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}}\!\\
\!\xi_{2}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}.}}\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\xi^{\prime}_{1}\!\\
\!\xi^{\prime}_{2}\!\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)=\xi_{1}\xi^{\prime}_{2}-\xi^{\prime}_{1}\xi_{2}
.
We define the Heisenberg group as
\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
with multiplication law

\displaystyle({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},\zeta)({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}}^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime})=({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}}+{\text{\boldmath$\xi$}}^{\prime},\zeta+\zeta^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}\omega({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},{\text{\boldmath$\xi$}}^{\prime})).

(2.1)

Note that
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is isomorphic to the quotient of
\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{R})
by its centre.
The
Schrödinger representation

W
is a homomorphism from
\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{R})
to the group of unitary operators on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Specifically, using the fact that each element in
\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{R})
can be written as
\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\xi_{1}\!\\
\!\xi_{2}\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),\zeta\right)=\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\xi_{1}\!\\
\!0\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),0\right)\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!0\!\\
\!\xi_{2}\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),0\right)\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!0\!\\
\!0\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),\zeta-\frac{\xi_{1}\xi_{2}}{2}\right)
, we can define
W
via

\displaystyle\left[W\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\xi_{1}\!\\
\!0\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),0\right)f\right](w)=e(\xi_{1}w)f(w),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left[W\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!0\!\\
\!\xi_{2}\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),0\right)f\right](w)=f(w-\xi_{2}),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left[W\left(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!0\!\\
\!0\!\end{smallmatrix}\right),\zeta\right)f\right](w)=e(\zeta)f(w),

(2.4)

where
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. For further details on this construction, see e.g.
[
19
]
, Chapter 1, Section 3.

2.2
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
and the Shale-Weil Representation

For any
M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
we may define a new representation of
\mathbb{H}(\mathbb{R})
as
W_{M}({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},\zeta)=W(M{\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},\zeta)
where
W
is the Schrödinger representation defined above. Any such representation is irreducible and hence
all the
W_{M}
must be unitarily equivalent (see
[
19
]
, Theorem 1.50 therein). Therefore
there exists a unitary operator
R(M)
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\displaystyle R(M)W_{M}({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},\zeta)R(M)^{-1}=W({\text{\boldmath$\xi$}},\zeta).

(2.5)

By Schur’s Lemma the map
M\mapsto R(M)
is unique up to a phase. Theorem 1.6.11 in
[
30
]
shows that in fact if
M_{1}=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
c_{1}&d_{1}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
,
M_{2}=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
c_{2}&d_{2}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
and
M_{1}M_{2}=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}a_{3}&b_{3}\\
c_{3}&d_{3}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
then

\displaystyle R(M_{1}M_{2})=\mathrm{e}\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{8}\mathrm{sgn}(c_{1}c_{2}c_{3})\right)R(M_{1})R(M_{2}).

(2.6)

This map is therefore a projective unitary representation of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, known as the
projective Shale-Weil representation
.
By Iwasawa decomposition, any
M\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
may be written as

\displaystyle M=n_{x}a_{y}k_{\phi}

(2.7)

where
x+iy\in\mathfrak{H}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)>0\}
,
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
, and

\displaystyle n_{x}:=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}1&x\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr),\>\>\>\>a_{y}:=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}y^{1/2}&0\\
0&y^{-1/2}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr),\>\>\>\>k_{\phi}:=\bigr(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{smallmatrix}\bigr).

(2.8)

Note that if
M=n_{x}a_{y}k_{\phi}
, then
-M=n_{x}a_{y}k_{\phi^{\prime}}
, where
\phi^{\prime}=\phi+\pi\bmod 2\pi
. Therefore
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})=\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})/\{\pm I\}
may be identified with
\mathfrak{H}\times[0,\pi)
.
The projective representation on
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is then defined via (
2.7
) as

\displaystyle[R(n_{x})f](w)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}w^{2}x\right)f(w),

(2.9)

\displaystyle[R(a_{y})f](w)

\displaystyle=y^{\frac{1}{4}}f\left(y^{\frac{1}{2}}w\right),

(2.10)

\displaystyle[R(k_{\phi})f](w)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}f(w)&if $\phi=0\pmod{2\pi}$\\
f(-w)&if $\phi=\pi\pmod{2\pi}$\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\sin\phi|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}\!\left(\frac{\frac{1}{2}(w^{2}+v^{2})\cos\phi-wv}{\sin\phi}\right)f(v)\>\mathrm{d}v&if $\phi\neq 0\pmod{2\pi}$.\end{cases}

(2.11)

Recall that
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
acts on
\mathfrak{H}
via Möbius transformations (see also (
2.30
) below). We consider
the universal cover of
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, namely

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R}):=

\displaystyle\left\{[M,\beta_{M}]:\>M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\mbox{ and }\right.

(2.12)

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\left.\beta_{M}\text{ any continuous function on $\mathfrak{H}$ such that }e^{i\beta_{M}(z)}=\tfrac{cz+d}{|cz+d|}\right\},

(2.13)

which has group law and inverse

\displaystyle[M,\beta_{M}][M^{\prime},\beta_{M^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle=[MM^{\prime},\beta_{MM^{\prime}}],~~~~\beta_{MM^{\prime}}(z)=\beta_{M}(M^{\prime}z)+\beta_{M^{\prime}}(z),

(2.14)

\displaystyle[M,\beta_{M}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=[M^{-1},\beta_{M^{-1}}],~~~~~\beta_{M^{-1}(z)}=-\beta_{M}(M^{-1}z).

(2.15)

The Iwasawa decomposition (
2.7
) extends to
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
as

\displaystyle[M,\beta_{M}]=\tilde{n}_{x}\tilde{a}_{y}\tilde{k}_{\phi}=[n_{x},0][a_{y},0][k_{\phi},\beta_{k_{\phi}}],

(2.16)

and we can lift the projective representation
R
to a genuine representation of
\widetilde{\mathrm{SL}}(2,\mathbb{R})
. Namely, we define the
Shale-Weil representation
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{R}(\tilde{n}_{x})f=R(n_{x}),\>\>\widetilde{R}(\tilde{a}_{y})f:=R(a_{y}),\>\>\widetilde{R}(\tilde{k}_{\phi})=\mathrm{e}\!\left(\sigma_{-\phi}/8\right)R(k_{\phi}),

(2.17)

where the function
\phi\mapsto\sigma_{\phi}
is given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\phi}=\begin{cases}2\nu&if $\sigma=\nu\pi,\>\nu\in\mathbb{Z}$,\\
2\nu+1&if $\nu\pi<\phi<(\nu+1)\pi,\>\nu\in\mathbb{Z}.$\end{cases}

(2.18)

The definition of
\sigma_{\phi}
is motivated by the fact that

\displaystyle\lim_{\phi\to 0^{\pm}}[R_{\phi}f]=\mathrm{e}\!\left(\pm\tfrac{1}{8}\right)f,

(2.19)

and similarly for
\phi\to\nu\pi^{\pm}
with
\nu\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Indeed, first note that (
2.6
) implies

R(k_{\phi})=\mathrm{e}\!\left(-\mathrm{sgn}((\sin\phi)\sin(\phi-\tfrac{\pi}{2}))/8\right)R(k_{\phi-\pi/2})R(k_{\pi/2})
.
Since
R(k_{\pi/2})f
is simply the unitary Fourier transform,
\hat{f}
, we obtain

\displaystyle[R(k_{\phi-\pi/2})R(k_{\pi/2})f](w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\cos\phi|}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{e}\!\left(\frac{-\frac{1}{2}(w^{2}+v^{2})\sin\phi+wv}{\cos\phi}\right)\!\hat{f}(v)\>\mathrm{d}v.

(2.20)

Moreover, since Fourier inversion gives

as

2.19
).

Each
\sum_{s_{i}=1}^{\ell_{k_{i}}}p_{k_{i}}^{2s_{i}}\left(1-\tfrac{1}{p_{k_{i}}^{2}}\right)=\sum_{s_{i}=1}^{\ell_{k_{i}}}(p_{k_{i}}^{2s_{i}}-p_{k_{i}}^{2(s_{i}-1)})
telescopes, and so (

) simplifies to

\begin{split}&1+\sum_{k_{1}}^{n}(p_{k_{1}}^{2\ell_{k_{1}}}-1)+\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq n}\prod_{i=1}^{2}(p_{k_{i}}^{2\ell_{k_{i}}}-1)\>+\cdots+\sum_{1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}<\cdots<k_{n-1}\leq n}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(p_{k_{i}}^{2\ell_{k_{i}}}-1)\>+\\
&+\prod_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}^{2\ell_{i}}-1).\end{split}

(3.26)

Each summand in (
3.26
) either has a factor of
p_{n}^{2\ell_{n}}-1
or it does not. By the inductive hypothesis, the sum of terms without a factor of
p_{n}^{\ell_{n}}-1
is
p_{1}^{2\ell_{1}}\cdots p_{n-1}^{2\ell_{n-1}}
. For those with factor of
p_{n}^{\ell_{n}}-1
, we first factor this out, and then apply the inductive hypothesis to see that their sum is
(p_{n}^{2\ell_{n}}-1)p_{1}^{2\ell_{1}}\cdots p_{n-1}^{2\ell_{n-1}}
. Altogether, (
3.26
) simplifies to

\displaystyle p_{1}^{2\ell_{1}}\cdots p_{n-1}^{2\ell_{n-1}}+(p_{n}^{2\ell_{n}}-1)p_{1}^{2\ell_{1}}\cdots p_{n-1}^{2\ell_{n-1}}=q^{2}.

(3.27)

Now suppose
q
is even and write it as

with
\ell>0
and

odd. We first note that for any

,
{\text{\boldmath$r$}}_{1}\neq{\text{\boldmath$r$}}_{2}
the intersection

is empty by Proposition
3.2.2
. Since

is odd, by the above argument, we see that

(3.28)

For fixed

, we obtain from (
(i)
) and (

(3.29)

(3.30)

Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)=n^{2}\prod_{\underset{p\>\text{odd}}{p|n}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)
.
We can show directly that if
n=2^{\ell}p_{1}^{\ell_{1}}

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)=n^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{1}^{2}}\right).

Set
P
to be the set of odd primes dividing
n

|P|=k
then

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)=n^{2}\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right).

If
P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k+1}\}

with
\gcd(n^{\prime},p_{k+1})=1

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)=p_{k+1}^{2\ell_{k+1}}\sum_{d|n^{\prime}}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}}{d}\right)+p_{k+1}^{2(\ell_{k+1}-1)}\sum_{d|n^{\prime}}\mu(dp_{k+1})F\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}}{d}\right).

As
\gcd(n^{\prime},p_{k+1})=1

\gcd(d,p_{k+1})=1
, and so
\mu(dp_{k+1})=-\mu(d)
. Therefore, by the inductive hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)=\left(p_{k+1}^{2\ell_{k+1}}-p_{k+1}^{2(\ell_{k+1}-1)}\right)\sum_{d|n^{\prime}}\mu(d)F\!\left(\frac{n^{\prime}}{d}\right)=n^{2}\prod_{p\in P}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)

and the proposition is proven.
∎

This concludes the proof of Proposition

∎

Let

Theorem 4.2.1

(Limit theorem for indicators)
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
. Suppose
\lambda
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Then for every

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}:\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}\left(\Gamma\left(I;\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\!\alpha+\beta u\!\\
\!0\!\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right)\Psi^{u}\Phi^{t}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\right\}=\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}((\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}})^{-1}(\mathcal{A})).

(4.33)

whenever

is a measurable set whose boundary has measure zero with respect to the push-forward measure

(\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}})_{*}\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}

Proof.

The special case
\alpha=\beta=0
is stated as Theorem 3.4 in
[
5
]
. Its proof hinges on various approximations (specifically Lemmata 3.6–3.9 in
[
5
]
, which in turn closely follow Lemmata 4.6–4.9 in
[
6
]
) and can be replicated, almost verbatim, in the general rational case at hand. Simply replace

by
\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and invoke Theorem
4.1.3
instead of Theorem 3.2 from
[
5
]
. ∎

Remark 4.2.2
.

Observe that, by Corollary 2.4 in
[
6
]
, if
f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\Theta_{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{4}(\widetilde{\Delta}\backslash\widetilde{G},\mathrm{Haar}_{\widetilde{\Delta}\backslash\widetilde{G}})
and hence, recalling Sections
2.3
–
2.4
, we have that
\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G,\mathrm{Haar}_{\Gamma\backslash G})
. Furthermore, arguing as in Section 5.2 of
[
5
]
, one can show that there is a set of full
\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
-measure in
\Gamma\backslash G
on which

is well-defined as a product of absolutely convergent series and, in fact,
\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Gamma\backslash G,\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)})
. In particular, the limiting probability measure
(\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}})_{*}\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
in Theorem
4.2.1
has finite variance.

Corollary 4.2.3
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, and
r\geq 1
. Let
\lambda
be a probability measure, absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Then for every
r\geq 1
and
R>0
we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}=\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\!\left\{|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|>R^{2}\right\}.

(4.34)

Proof.

Let
s\in\{1,r\}
. Observe that
S_{\lfloor sN\rfloor}=S_{N}^{\mathds{1}_{(0,s]}}
and
S_{N}^{\chi_{s}}
are both finite sums and differ in absolute value by at most
1
. Therefore

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|S_{\lfloor sN\rfloor}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|S_{N}^{\chi_{s}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|+O\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

(4.35)

and

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|=

(4.36)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}^{\chi}\overline{S_{N}^{\chi_{s}}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|+O\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\right)+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|\tfrac{S_{N}^{\chi}(x;\alpha,\beta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right|\right)+O\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left|\tfrac{S_{N}^{\chi_{r}}(x;\alpha,\beta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right|\right).

(4.37)

Using (
2.65
),
we obtain from Theorem
4.2.1
(with
r=1
) that
\left|\frac{S_{N}^{\chi}(x;\alpha,\beta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right|
converges in law. A simple rescaling argument yields that the same is true for
\left|\frac{S_{N}^{\chi_{r}}(x;\alpha,\beta)}{\sqrt{N}}\right|
. The Cramér–Slutsky Theorem (see, e.g.
[
21
]
, Theorem 11.4 therein) implies that the sum of the three

-terms in (

) converge to zero in law as

. Therefore, again from (
2.65
) and Theorem
4.2.1
, we have that both
\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|
and
\frac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}^{\chi}\overline{S_{N}^{\chi_{s}}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|
converge in law to the same limit as
N\to\infty
. In other words,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|\in\mathcal{A}\right\}=\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left\{|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|\in\mathcal{A}\right\},

(4.38)

for any measurable
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}
whose boundary has measure zero with respect to the limiting measure,
|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|_{*}\mu_{\Gamma\setminus G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
. Taking
\mathcal{A}=(R,\infty)
gives the result.
∎

For
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
, Corollaries
4.1.4
and
4.2.3
show that in order to study the tail of the limiting distributions of

are regular, we find the asymptotic behaviour, as

Proof.

Remark 7.1.2

7.2
The tails of the limiting distribution for sharp indicators

Consider (
5.10
) when
f_{1}=\chi
and
f_{2}=\chi_{r}
with

. A close formula for

is provided by the following

Theorem 7.2.1

(
[
5
]
, Theorem 8.1 therein)
.

Let

. Then

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{rat}}(\chi,\chi_{r})=\begin{cases}2\log 2&\mbox{if $r=1$;}\\
2r\,\mathrm{coth}^{-1}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\log(r^{2}-1)+\frac{r^{2}}{2}\log(1-\frac{1}{r^{2}})&\mbox{if $r>1$.}\end{cases}

(7.5)

Theorem 7.2.2

(The tails of the limiting distribution of
\frac{1}{N}S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}
)
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}
. Suppose
\lambda
is a Borel probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. Let
r\geq 1
.

(i)

If
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathscr{C}(2m)
, then there exists
R_{0}=R_{0}(m,r)>0
such that if
R>R_{0}
then we have

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}=0.

(7.6)

In other words, the limiting distribution of
\tfrac{1}{N}S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)
is compactly supported.

(ii)

If
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathscr{H}(q)
, there exists a constant
\mathcal{T}(q;r)>0
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda\!\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\>\tfrac{1}{N}\left|S_{N}\overline{S_{\lfloor rN\rfloor}}(x;\alpha,\beta)\right|>R^{2}\right\}=\mathcal{T}(q;r)R^{-4}\left(1+O_{\varepsilon}(R^{-2+\varepsilon})\right),

(7.7)

as
R\to\infty
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(q;r)=\frac{C(q)D_{\mathrm{rat}}(r)}{\pi^{2}},

(7.8)

where

C(q)
is as in (
7.4
) and
D_{\mathrm{rat}}(r)=D_{\mathrm{rat}}(\chi,\chi_{r})
is as in (
7.5
).
The constants implied by the
O_{\varepsilon}
-notation in (
7.7
) depend on
\varepsilon
,
q
, and
r
.

The special case of Theorem
7.2.2
for
\alpha=\beta=0
, which fall under case (ii) since
q=1
, is Theorem 8.4 in
[
5
]
. The proof of that theorem uses a dynamical approximation —first introduced in
[
6
]
in the case of
(\alpha,\beta)\notin\mathbb{Q}^{2}
— of the sharp indicators by regular ones. For all rational pairs in case (ii), the proof is mostly the same. The only additional effort arises if we want to keep track of the explicit dependence upon
q
of the implied constants in (
7.7
). In this work we focus on the leading term
\mathcal{T}(q;r)R^{-4}
in the asymptotic, which arises in Proposition
5.3.1
when we consider regular indicators
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
. The constant
D_{\mathrm{rat}}(r)=D_{\mathrm{rat}}(\chi,\chi_{r})
then appears when we approximate
\chi
and
\chi_{r}
by some regular “dynamically defined”
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Instead of replicating the long technical arguments of Sections 5–8 of
[
5
]
for all rational pairs, we simply present here an outline of the proof.

(a)

For
a\leq b
and
\epsilon,\delta>
, define a
C^{1}
“trapezoidal” function
T_{a,b}^{\epsilon,\delta}
supported on
[a-\epsilon,b+\delta]
and identically 1 on
[a+\epsilon,b-\delta]
. We have
T_{a,b}^{\epsilon,\delta}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and for all
\eta\in(1,2]
we can estimate
\kappa_{\eta}(T_{a,b}^{\epsilon,\delta})
.
Consider the
C^{1}
“triangle” function
\Delta=T_{1/3,1/3}^{1/6,1/3}
with support
[\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{2}{3}]
. This function is used to obtain the partition of unity
\chi(w)=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Delta(2^{j}w)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Delta(2^{j}(1-w))
. This partition can be rewritten dynamically in terms of the geodesic flow (
2.56
) using the Shale-Weil representation (recall (
2.10
), (
2.17
), and (
2.24
)) as
\chi=\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\widetilde{R}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{(-2\log 2)j})\Delta+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-\frac{j}{2}}\widetilde{R}(\tilde{\rho}_{4}\widetilde{\Phi}^{(-2\log 2)j})\Delta_{-}
, where
\tilde{\rho}_{4}
is given in Section
2.4
, and
\Delta_{-}(w)=\Delta(-w)
. Each sum is renormalized by the flow, which maps each term to the next and provides the exponential weights. The flow can also be used to rescale the indicators, i.e.
\chi_{r}=\sqrt{r}\widetilde{R}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{2\log r})\chi
.

(b)

For
s\in\{1,r\}
and
J\geq 1
, truncate each series from (a) to the range
0\leq j\leq J
to define a regular trapezoidal approximation
\chi^{(J)}_{s}=\chi_{s,L}^{(J)}+\chi_{s,R}^{(J)}=T_{\tfrac{s}{3\cdot 2^{J-1}},\tfrac{s}{3}}^{\tfrac{s}{6\cdot 2^{J-1}},\tfrac{s}{3}}+T_{\tfrac{2s}{3},s-\tfrac{s}{3\cdot 2^{J-1}}}^{\tfrac{s}{3},\tfrac{s}{6\cdot 2^{J-1}}}
of
\chi_{s}
.
Defining
\chi^{(J,m)}_{s,L}=\widetilde{R}(\widetilde{\Phi}^{-(2\log 2)mJ})\chi^{(J)}_{s,L}
and
\chi^{(J,m)}_{s,R}=\widetilde{R}(\tilde{\rho}_{4}\widetilde{\Phi}^{-(2\log 2)mJ})\chi^{(J)}_{s,L}
for
s\in\{1,r\}
, we expand the product

\displaystyle\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}(g)=\>

\displaystyle\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}^{(J)}}}(g)+\sum_{k+\ell>0}2^{-\frac{(k+\ell)J}{2}}\mathcal{L}\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)+\sum_{k+\ell>0}2^{-\frac{(k+\ell)J}{2}}\mathcal{L}\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)+

(7.9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{k+\ell>0}2^{-\frac{(k+\ell)J}{2}}\mathcal{R}\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)+\sum_{k+\ell>0}2^{-\frac{(k+\ell)J}{2}}\mathcal{R}\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g),

(7.10)

with
\mathcal{L}\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)=\Theta_{\chi^{(J,k)}_{1,L}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J,\ell)}_{r,L}}}(g)
,
\mathcal{L}\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)=\Theta_{\chi^{(J,k)}_{1,L}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J,\ell)}_{r,R}}}(g)
,
\mathcal{R}\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)=\Theta_{\chi^{(J,k)}_{1,R}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J,\ell)}_{r,L}}}(g)
, and
\mathcal{R}\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{(J)}_{k,\ell}(g)=\Theta_{\chi^{(J,k)}_{1,R}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J,\ell)}_{r,R}}}(g)
.

(c)

Combining (
7.9
)-(
7.10
) with a union bound and (the leading term of) Proposition
5.3.1
, we obtain the following

Lemma 7.2.3
.

Fix
1<\eta\leq 2
,
r\geq 1
, and let
q
be the denominator of
(\alpha,\beta)
. There is a constant
K=K(r)>0
such that if

and

we have

\bullet

If

then

\displaystyle\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|>R^{2}\right)=\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}_{r}}}|>R^{2}(1\pm\delta)^{2}\right).

\bullet

\displaystyle\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|>R^{2}\right)-\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}_{r}}}|>R^{2}(1-\delta)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{1}{R^{4}\delta^{2}2^{J}}\right)

(7.12)

and

\displaystyle\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}_{r}}}|>R^{2}(1+\delta)^{2}\right)-\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left(|\Theta_{\chi}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}}}|>R^{2}\right)

(7.13)

.

(d)

If

,

\displaystyle\mu_{\Gamma\backslash G}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\left\{|\Theta_{\chi^{(J)}}\overline{\Theta_{\chi_{r}^{(J)}}}|>R^{2}(1\pm\delta)^{2}\right\}=

\displaystyle=\frac{C(q)D_{\mathrm{rat}}(\chi,\chi_{r})}{\pi^{2}R^{4}}\left(1+O(\delta)\right)\left(1+O_{\eta}(2^{2\eta(\eta-1)J}R^{-2\eta})\right)\left(1+O(2^{-J})\right).

(7.14)

.
