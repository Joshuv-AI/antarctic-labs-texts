On Schur Rings Over Infinite Groups
Nicholas Bastian, Jaden Brewer, Andrew Misseldine

Abstract
Schur rings are a type of subring of the group ring that is spanned by a partition of the group that meets certain conditions. Past literature has exclusively focused on the finite group case. This paper extends many classic results about Schur rings to the infinite groups, including Leung-Man’s classification of Schur rings over finite cyclic groups. Additionally, this classification will be extended to all torsion-free locally cyclic groups.

Section

Leung and Man provided a classification of Schur rings over finite cyclic groups by showing that all Schur rings fall into one of four families: partitions induced by orbits of automorphism subgroups (orbit Schur rings), partitions induced by a direct product factorization (dot product), partitions induced by cosets (wedge products), and the trivial Schur ring. \alpha\in\mathfrak{S}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have that
\alpha^{(m)}\in\mathfrak{S}
, that is, Schur rings over
G
are closed under freshman exponents.

The above theorem was proven by Wielandt
[
17
]
for finite groups. The proof of Theorem
2.15
is essentially the same as Wielandt’s in the case that the exponent
m
is relatively prime to the order of the group. In reality what is needed for Wieldant’s argument to hold is that
m
is relatively prime to all the orders of elements in
\supp{\alpha}
which is the case for torsion-free groups.

3
Classifying Schur Rings Over the Integers

Now that we see that Schur rings over
F[\mathcal{Z}]
are closed under freshman exponentiation, we can move onto proving that there are only two Schur rings over
F[\mathcal{Z}]
, the group ring and the symmetric Schur ring.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}=\langle z\rangle
. Let
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. If
z\in C
then
C=\{z\}
or
C=\{z,z^{-1}\}
.

Proof.

Let
w\in C
. Then
w=z^{a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
w\in C\cap C^{(a)}
. As
\overline{C}\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\overline{C}^{(a)}\in\mathfrak{S}
by Theorem
2.15
. Now,
\overline{C\cap C^{(a)}}=\overline{C}\circ\overline{C^{(a)}}=\overline{C}\circ\overline{C}^{(a)}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Since
\overline{C}
is a primitive quantity,
\overline{C}\circ\overline{C}^{(a)}=r\overline{C}
for some
r\in F
, which necessarily must be
r=1
or
r=0
as
\overline{C^{(a)}}
is a simple quantity. As
C\cap C^{(a)}\neq\emptyset
, it must be the former, that is,
\overline{C}\circ\overline{C}^{(a)}=\overline{C}
. This implies that
C\subseteq C^{(a)}
. But
|C|=|C^{(a)}|<\infty
. We conclude that
C=C^{(a)}
. This means
z\in C^{(a)}
, that is,
z=z^{ax}
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
1=ax
, which implies that
a=1
and
x=1
, or
a=-1
and
x=-1
. Hence,
w=z
, or
w=z^{-1}
, which proves the result.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

The only Schur rings over the infinite cyclic group are the group ring and the symmetric ring.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}
. Let
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
be the primitive set containing
z
. Then by, Lemma
3.1
, we consider two cases:
C=\{z\}
or
C=\{z,z^{-1}\}
. If
C=\{z\}
, then
z^{m}\in\mathfrak{S}
for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus,
F[\mathcal{Z}]\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
, forcing
\mathfrak{S}=F[\mathcal{Z}]
, the group ring.

If
C=\{z,z^{-1}\}
, then
z+z^{-1}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Then
=z^{a}+z^{-a}=(z+z^{-1})^{(a)}\in\mathfrak{S},\ \forall a\in\mathbb{Z}
by Theorem
2.15
. That is,
\{z^{a},z^{-a}\}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-set. Now, by way of contradiction, assume that
\exists m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\{z^{m},z^{-m}\}
is imprimitive for
m\neq 0
. Then
\{z^{m}\}
and
\{z^{-m}\}
are primitive sets of
\mathfrak{S}
, that is,
z^{m},z^{-m}\in\mathfrak{S}
. Then
z^{m}(z+z^{-1})=z^{m+1}+z^{m-1}\in\mathfrak{S}
. We also know that
z^{m+1}+z^{-(m+1)}\in\mathfrak{S}
, from freshman exponentiation. Then using the Hadamard product,
(z^{m+1}+z^{m-1})\circ(z^{m+1}+z^{-m+1)})=z^{m+1}\in{\mathfrak{S}}
. Hence,
z^{m+1}z^{-m}=z\in\mathfrak{S}
, a contradiction. Hence,
\{z^{a},z^{-a}\}
is a primitive set of
\mathfrak{S}
, for all
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. In this case
\mathfrak{S}
is the symmetric ring
F[\mathcal{Z}]^{\pm}
.
∎

Note that because
\mathcal{Z}
is infinite, the trivial Schur ring is not possible over
\mathcal{Z}
. Likewise, no dot product is constructible over
\mathcal{Z}
since it has no nontrivial direct product factorization. Finally, as
\mathcal{Z}
no nontrivial finite subgroups, no wedge product is constructible over
\mathcal{Z}
. Thus, of the four types of Schur rings available to finite cyclic groups by the Leung-Man Classification, orbit Schur rings are the only possible type available for
\mathcal{Z}
. Furthermore, as
\Aut(\mathcal{Z})=\{\pm 1\}
,
F[G]
and
F[G]^{\pm}
are the two orbit Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}
. Thus, we see that Theorem
3.2
is an extension of the Leung-Man Classification of Schur rings to all cyclic groups, both finite and infinite.

4
Classifying Schur Rings Over the Torsion-Free Locally Cyclic Groups

Now that we have a classification of Schur rings over the infinite cyclic group, we are ready to move onto classifying Schur rings over all torsion-free locally cyclic groups. It is well known that a group is torsion-free locally cyclic if and only if it is isomorphic to a subgroup of the additive group of rational numbers. To do this we will first look at some results concerning
\mathfrak{S}
-sets and
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroups.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a Schur ring over
G
, where
G
is a torsion-free locally cyclic group. Then
\mathfrak{S}
is either the group ring
F[G]
or the symmetric Schur ring
F[G]^{\pm}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be an S-ring over
G
, where
G
is a torsion-free locally cyclic group. Let
C\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
excluding
\{1\}
. As
C
is necessarily finite, the subgroup
H=\langle C\rangle\cong\mathcal{Z}
and is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup by Proposition
2.12
. Then
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]
or
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]^{\pm}
by Theorem
3.2
. As
\overline{C}\in\mathfrak{S}_{H}
, we see that
C=\{z^{a}\}
or
C=\{z^{a},z^{-a}\}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Let
C,D\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
excluding
\{1\}
. Consider
H=\langle C\rangle,\ K=\langle D\rangle
, which are necessarily cyclic. Like above,
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]
or
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]^{\pm}
, and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]
or
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]^{\pm}
. This presents us with three cases:

(i)

\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]
and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]
;

(ii)

\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]
and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]^{\pm}
;
4
4

4

The case
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]^{\pm}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]
is similar to (
ii
) and is omitted.

(iii)

\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]^{\pm}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]^{\pm}
.

Note that
H\cap K\cong\mathcal{Z}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}=\mathfrak{S}_{H}\cap\mathfrak{S}_{K}
, by Proposition
2.14
. As
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}
is an
\mathfrak{S}
-subgroup,
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}=F[H\cap K]
or
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}=F[H\cap K]^{\pm}
.

In case (
i
),
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}=F[H]\cap F[K]=F[H\cap K]
. Likewise, in case (
iii
),
\mathfrak{S}_{H\cap K}=F[H]^{\pm}\cap F[K]^{\pm}=F[H\cap K]^{\pm}
, as both sets consist of all elements of
F[H\cap K]
fixed by
\mathcal{H}=\{\pm 1\}
.

We next will show that case (
ii
) is impossible. Let
x\in H\cap K
. As
\mathfrak{S}_{H}=F[H]
and
x\in H
, it must be that
\{x\}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
. As
\mathfrak{S}_{K}=F[K]^{\pm}
and
x\in K
, it must be that
\{x,x^{-1}\}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
, which is a contradiction. This means that
\mathfrak{S}_{H}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{K}
cannot be different types of Schur rings, that is, they are both group rings or both symmetric rings over their respective subgroups. In summary, every class in
\mathcal{D}(\mathfrak{S})
is either a singleton or a symmetric pair and either all classes are singletons or all classes are pairs. Hence
\mathfrak{S}
is either the group ring over
G
or the symmetric ring over
G
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the group of rational numbers. Then the only Schur rings over
\mathcal{Q}
are the group ring
F[\mathcal{Q}]
and the symmetric Schur ring
F[\mathcal{Q}]^{\pm}
.
