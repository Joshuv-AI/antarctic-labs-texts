Incorporating Memory into Propagation of 1-Electron Reduced Density Matrices Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Harish S. Bhat, Hardeep Bassi, Karnamohit Ranka, Christine M. Isborn

Abstract
For any linear system with unreduced dynamics governed by invertible propagators, we derive a closed, time-delayed, linear system for a reduced-dimensional quantity of interest. This method does not target dimensionality reduction: rather, this method helps shed light on the memory-dependence of 1 1 -electron reduced density matrices in time-dependent configuration interaction (TDCI), a scheme to solve for the correlated dynamics of electrons in molecules. Though time-dependent density functional theory has established that the 1 1 -electron reduced density possesses memory-dependence, the precise nature of this memory-dependence has not been understood. We derive a symmetry/constraint-preserving method to propagate reduced TDCI electron density matrices. In numerical tests on two model systems ( H 2 \text{H}_{2} and HeH + \text{HeH}^{+} ), we show that with sufficiently large time-delay (or memory-dependence), our method propagates reduced TDCI density matrices with high quantitative accuracy. We study the dependence of our results on time step and basis set. To implement our method, we derive the 4 4 -index tensor that relates reduced and full TDCI density matrices. Our derivation applies to any TDCI system, regardless of basis set, number of electrons, or choice of Slater determinants in the wave function.



I
Introduction

For an
N
-electron system, the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) is a partial differential equation for the system’s electronic wave function
\Psi
. Ignoring spin and assuming fixed nuclear coordinates within the Born-Oppenheimer approximation,
\Psi
depends on
3N
spatial variables and time. This electronic wave function governs the electron dynamics of the system, important in ultrafast charge transfer in the initial steps of photosynthesis and laser-induced processes, among others
Jortner, Bixon, and Michel-Beyerle 1990
;
Kling and Vrakking 2008
;
Provorse and Isborn 2016
;
Li et al. 2020
. Unless
N
is very small, we cannot expect to solve for
\Psi
. Within a finite-dimensional basis set, a numerically exact solution of the time-independent problem is possible with configuration interaction (CI)

Shavitt 1977
;
McWeeny 1989
;
Szabo and Ostlund 2012
;
Shavitt 1998
. CI expands
\Psi
in a basis of Slater determinants; TDCI uses the same expansion but allows the coefficients to depend on time, enabling the solution of genuinely time-dependent problems—see Sections
III
and
IV
for details. When all possible determinants are used, as in the present paper, we refer to the resulting
\Psi
as a
full CI
wave function.

The full CI wave function was recently calculated for the C
3
H
8
molecule (
N=26
), which used distributed computing to determine the energy of the system with 1.31 trillion determinants, the largest known full CI calculation to date
Gao et al. 2024
. The full CI wave function can be approximated with various levels of truncation of the expansion of determinants, as is commonly done for molecular systems. In the limit where we use only a single Slater determinant to approximate
\Psi
, we have Hartree-Fock theory, which neglects electron correlation
Löwdin 1995
.

Note that
\Psi=\Psi(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{N},t)
where each
\mathbf{r}_{j}
is three-dimensional. Let us now define the
1
-electron reduced density
\rho(\mathbf{r},t)
as
N
times the integral of
|\Psi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{N},t)|^{2}
with respect to
\mathbf{r}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{r}_{N}
. Clearly,
\rho
is a much lower-dimensional object than
\Psi
. Many observables of practical interest can be computed easily and explicitly from
\rho
; additionally, given any physical observable, there exists
Ullrich 2012
a mapping expressing it as a function of
\rho
.

Time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT), which can be derived
Ullrich 2012
from the TDSE, gives us an alternative route to compute
1
-electron reduced densities
\rho

without
computing the full wave function
\Psi
. For the same
N
-electron system,
TDDFT yields computationally tractable evolution equations in three spatial variables plus time; solving these equations enables us to compute the
1
-electron reduced density
\rho(\mathbf{r},t)
. The Runge-Gross theorem establishes the existence of exchange-correlation potentials
V_{\text{xc}}
such that the
1
-electron densities computed via TDDFT exactly match those computed from solutions of the TDSE
Runge and Gross 1984
. Thus, in principle, TDDFT provides an accurate, scalable path to computing electron dynamics. Note that
V_{\text{xc}}
is shorthand for
V_{\text{xc}}[\rho](\mathbf{r},t)
—this potential depends
functionally
on the
\rho
trajectory
\{\rho(\mathbf{w},s)\mid\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},s\leq t\}
. Because exact, time-dependent exchange-correlation functionals are unavailable, TDDFT practitioners use approximations
Gross and Maitra 2012
in which
V_{\text{xc}}[\rho](\mathbf{r},t)
depends only on
\{\rho(\mathbf{w},t)\mid\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\}
, ignoring memory-dependence on
\rho(\cdot,s)
for
s<t
. This ubiquitous approximation, known as the adiabatic approximation within the TDDFT community, leads to errors that have been quantified in prior work
Elliott et al. 2012
;
Rappoport and Hutter 2012
;
Habenicht et al. 2014
;
Provorse, Habenicht, and Isborn 2015
;
Lacombe and Maitra 2020
;
Ranka and Isborn 2023
.

One way to devise more accurate
V_{\text{xc}}
models that incorporate memory-dependence is to use machine learning. For a spatially one-dimensional, two-electron model problem, the approach of learning memory-dependent
V_{\text{xc}}
models from data has been pursued recently
Suzuki, Nagai, and Haruyama 2020
;
Bhat et al. 2022
. To scale these machine learning methods to larger, three-dimensional molecular systems, it will be essential to incorporate prior knowledge regarding the memory-dependence of
V_{\text{xc}}
. This will enable targeted searches over neural network architectures that match physically relevant dynamics. In TDDFT, memory-dependence of
V_{\text{xc}}
results in time-delayed equations of motion for
1
-electron Kohn-Sham orbitals
Maitra, Burke, and Woodward 2002
. The sum of the squared magnitudes of all such
1
-electron Kohn-Sham orbitals equals the
1
-electron reduced density
\rho
. Memory-dependence of
V_{\text{xc}}
gives rise to memory-dependence of
\rho
.

Let
P(t)
and
Q(t)
denote, respectively, the full density matrix and
1
-electron reduced density matrix (1RDM) that can be computed from the TDCI solution
\Psi
.
In this paper, we use all possible Slater determinants in our CI expansion for
\Psi
. Then the resulting
\Psi
,
P
, and
Q
all incorporate the effects of electron correlation and exchange. Our TDCI approach circumvents the approximations of methods such as time-dependent Hartree-Fock and TDDFT. Therefore, we can logically aim to
improve
a theory such as TDDFT by studying the dynamics of TDCI
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
. The missing piece has been an equation of motion for
Q(t)
.

In this paper, we derive and study a closed, time-delay system that enables propagation of TDCI
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
.
As this system requires the full many-body propagator, it does not achieve computational savings. Instead, we use the system to generate insight into the explicit memory-dependence of
Q(t)
on past values
Q(s)
for
s<t
. As we explain in Section
IV
, the TDCI
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
are closely related to to the
1
-electron reduced densities
\rho
in TDDFT. Therefore, insight into the memory-dependence of the TDCI reduced electron density matrices
Q(t)
, enabled by the methods of this paper, will assist us in improving future models of
V_{\text{xc}}
for TDDFT.

I.1
Related Work

The idea of trading high-dimensionality of one dynamical system (
2
) for time-delayed dynamics in a reduced-dimensional space (
4
) is connected to notions from Mori-Zwanzig theory
Zwanzig 1961
;
Mori 1965
;
Chorin, Hald, and Kupferman 2000
;
Darve, Solomon, and Kia 2009
;
Chorin and Hald 2014
, Takens embedding theory
Takens 1981
;
Stark 99 6
;
Broer and Takens 2010
, and Koopman operator theory
Mauroy, Mezić, and Susuki 2020
;
Otto and Rowley 2021
;
Brunton et al. 2022
. We now review these connections.

Given a dynamical system in a high-dimensional space, Mori-Zwanzig theory produces closed systems for the time-evolution of low-dimensional observables
Zwanzig 1961
;
Mori 1965
. The resulting equations trade (i) non-time-delayed dynamics in the original, high-dimensional space for (ii) time-delayed dynamics in a low-dimensional space
Chorin and Hald 2014
. The time delays are expressed in terms of a memory kernel. Recent work has connected Mori-Zwanzig theory to Koopman operator methods, showing how to learn the memory kernel from data
Lin et al. 2021
.

Our method shares with Mori-Zwanzig theory the trade-off between (i) and (ii). A key difference is that Mori-Zwanzig methods often assume that the unobserved or irrelevant variables can be treated as noise, leading to generalized Langevin equations
Chorin, Hald, and Kupferman 2000
;
Darve, Solomon, and Kia 2009
. If we view the transformation from full electron density matrices
P(t)
to
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
as projection onto a subspace
S
, then the unobserved/irrelevant variables are the projection of
P(t)
onto the orthogonal complement
S^{\perp}
. We make no assumptions regarding the dynamics of these variables; they do not appear at all in our self-contained scheme for propagating
Q(t)
. This is a key difference between our work and Mori-Zwanzig approaches. In Section
II.2
, we explain this difference further through a concrete example.

Takens justified trading (i) for (ii) in the sense of establishing a sufficient criterion for how much memory is required (i.e., the number of time-delayed scalar observations) to accurately represent dynamics in the original phase space
Takens 1981
. Takens’ theory establishes the existence of time-delayed embeddings, but does not give methods to find them.
Time-delayed embeddings are linked to Koopman operator methods
Mezić and Banaszuk 2004
, which are themselves closely connected to the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)
Kutz et al. 2016
;
Kamb et al. 2020
;
Brunton et al. 2022
. In Section
II.1
, we show concretely that our method results in the same type of model as time-delayed DMD, but without any need to learn from data.

Our method shares structural features with self-energy methods where one solves for a many-body system’s Green’s function
G(t,t^{\prime})
via Dyson’s equation
Stefanucci and Van Leeuwen 2013
;
Kadanoff 2018

(i\partial_{t}\ -H_{0})G(t,t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t}\Sigma(t-t^{\prime\prime})G(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})dt^{\prime\prime}.

As in Mori-Zwanzig theory, forward evolution of
G
depends on past states via a memory integral, here represented by convolution with the self-energy
\Sigma
, which itself depends on
G
. Here
H_{0}
is the non-interacting Hamiltonian;
\Sigma
incorporates interactions that go beyond mean-field theories
Negele 1982
. There are close analogies between the terms that comprise
\Sigma
and the Coulomb and exchange-correlation (
V_{\text{xc}}
) potentials that one finds in TDDFT
Onida, Reining, and Rubio 2002
. As with
V_{\text{xc}}
, in general, the analytical form of
\Sigma
is unknown, necessitating approximations such as GW
Aryasetiawan and Gunnarsson 1998
;
Reining 2018
;
Hedin 1999
. Recent work has sought to use machine learning to improve these approximations
Bassi et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2024
. The present paper’s methods may yield insight into how to model
\Sigma
, especially its temporal decay within the memory integral.

A key feature of our system is that the propagator is unitary, implying the reversibility of the dynamics. The Liouville-von Neumann equation (
1
) that governs the dynamics of
P(t)
is not dissipative. Hence we cannot directly apply balanced truncation methods that have been developed for open quantum systems
Schäfer-Bung et al. 2011
;
Benner et al. 2020
. Variants of these methods, when applied to the TDCI system, may yield substantial computational savings. This is outside the scope of the present work.

I.2
Liouville-von Neumann

In several contexts, electron density matrices
P(t)
satisfy an equation of motion known as the Liouville-von Neumann equation:

i\frac{dP}{dt}=[H,P].

(1)

Here
[H,P]=HP-PH
denotes the commutator. In fact, we show in Section
III
that the TDCI
full
electron density matrices
P(t)
satisfy (
1
). The 1RDM
\widetilde{P}(t)
from time-dependent Hartree-Fock theory satisfies (
1
) with a density-dependent Hamiltonian
H(\widetilde{P})

McLachlan and Ball 1964
;
McWeeny 1989
. Thus it is natural to ask whether the TDCI
1
-electron
reduced
density matrices
Q(t)
evolve via (
1
). If they do, one can show that they must satisfy
Q(t)=U(t)^{\dagger}Q(0)U(t)
, where
U(t)
is unitary and
\dagger
denotes conjugate transpose; consequently, the eigenvalues of
Q(t)
must equal those of
Q(0)
for all
t\geq 0
.

Figure 1
:
For the molecule
\text{HeH}^{+}
in the STO-3G atomic orbital basis set, we apply the methods described in Section
V.1
to compute
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
. From the left plot, we conclude that the eigenvalues of
Q(t)
do not stay constant in time, implying that
Q(t)
cannot satisfy the Liouville-von Neumann equation for
any
choice of Hamiltonian
H(t)
. The right plot shows that
\trace(Q(t))=2
. Since
\text{HeH}^{+}
has
N=2
electrons, this agrees with
\trace(Q(t))=N
, which we prove in Appendix
B
.

This gives us a way to test if it is possible that TDCI
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
satisfy the Liouville-von Neumann equation. For four two-electron molecular systems (
\text{HeH}^{+}
and
\text{H}_{2}
in both STO-3G and 6-31G atomic orbital basis sets, for which we can compute the full CI solution with all possible determinants), we have applied TDCI methods—see Sections
III
and
V.1
—to compute full density matrices
P(t)
, from which we have computed
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
. Let us focus attention on
\text{HeH}^{+}
in the STO-3G atomic orbital basis set; here
Q(t)
is
2\times 2
and thus has two eigenvalues,
\lambda_{1}(t)
and
\lambda_{2}(t)
. In Figure
1
, we plot the absolute deviation
|\lambda_{j}(t)-\lambda_{j}(0)|
, and we see that the eigenvalues of the
Q(t)
matrices do not stay constant in time. We omit plots for the other three molecular systems, all of which would show substantial fluctuations in the eigenvalues of
Q(t)
.

Because the eigenvalues of the TDCI
1
-electron reduced density matrices
Q(t)
do not stay constant in time, there does not exist a Hamiltonian
H
such that these
Q(t)
matrices satisfy the Liouville-von Neumann equation.
Thus we cannot apply methods from our prior work
Bhat, Ranka, and Isborn 2020
;
Gupta et al. 2022
to learn a Hamiltonian
H
such that these
Q(t)
satisfy (
1
).

I.3
Reduced Density Matrices (RDMs)

A subset of the many-body quantum physics and chemistry literature is concerned with deriving governing equations for RDMs, for both time-independent
Coleman 1963
;
Mazziotti 1998
;
Mentel 2015
and time-dependent
Pernal, Gritsenko, and Baerends 2007
;
Giesbertz 2010
;
Giesbertz, Gritsenko, and Baerends 2010
;
Akbari et al. 2012
problems. Consider the operator obtained by integrating
|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|
with respect to all spins and all but
k
of the electrons’ positions; the matrix representation of this operator is the
k
-electron reduced density matrix or
k
RDM. Here we have used bra-ket notation—see Definition
4
.
Starting from the Schrödinger equation, one can derive a governing equation for the 1RDM
Q(t)
, but it will necessarily involve the 2RDM
Ferré, Filatov, and Huix-Rotllant 2016
. While there have been multiple attempts to close the 1RDM equation via approximate reconstruction of the 2RDM from the 1RDM
Mazziotti 1998
;
Herbert and Harriman 2002
;
Giesbertz, Baerends, and Gritsenko 2008
;
Giesbertz 2010
;
Jeffcoat and DePrince 2014
, we have not seen any prior attempts to use
memory
(present and past 1RDMs) to derive a closed system. More generally, if one seeks an equation of motion for the 2RDM, it will either involve reconstruction of higher-order matrices such as the 3RDM or 4RDM
Mazziotti 1998
;
Lackner et al. 2015
;
Lackner et al. 2017
, or a reexpression of constraints on higher-order RDMs (the
N
-representability conditions)
Fosso-Tande et al. 2016
;
Mazziotti 2023
. Even within this literature on higher-order RDM systems, the use of memory appears to be unexplored.

I.4
Summary of Contributions

In Section
II
, we give a general method to derive a self-contained linear delay equation for the propagation of a reduced quantity of interest. We provide this section to highlight the key steps of the derivation, which already constitutes one novel contribution of this paper, prior to introducing any notions from quantum mechanics. The general setting also allows us to contrast our method with both time-delayed DMD and a Mori-Zwanzig approach, which we do in Section
II

Section

Here we define a subset of concepts from quantum/computational chemistry such as orbitals, Slater determinants, and basis functions. We review TDCI and its relation to the TDSE. We show that the full density matrix

satisfies the Liouville-von Neumann equation. Theorem

shows that

-electron reduced density matrices

have constant trace equal to

, as exemplified by the right plot in

.

In Section

, we specialize the derivation from Section

to the TDCI setting. We begin by deriving several useful properties of
1
-electron reduced density matrices that stem from TDCI. The derivations here are general with respect to the number of electrons (
N
), the number of atomic/molecular orbitals (
K
), and the number of configurations (or Slater determinants) included in the wave function (
N_{C}
). Proposition
1
enables concrete evaluation of the
4
-index tensor
B
that relates full configuration interaction (CI) electron density matrices to
1
-electron reduced density matrices. Theorem

establishes a self-contained delay equation for

that preserves Hermitian symmetry, constant trace, and identically zero elements.

We present numerical results in Section

, applying the time-delayed scheme from Theorem

II
Core Ideas

Here we present derivations that illustrate the core ideas of our approach without the complexity of the quantum system that we present and analyze in Sections
III
and
IV
. The derivation in Section
II.1
clarifies the key steps of our time-delay scheme to propagate

. In Section
IV.3
, we revisit and refine this derivation for the quantum chemistry context. The derivation in Section

frames our problem as a partially observed linear system, enabling a comparison with Mori-Zwanzig theory.

We begin with the discrete-time linear system

\mathbf{z}(t+1)=A(t)\mathbf{z}(t)

(2)

with initial condition
\mathbf{z}(0)
. Here
t
is a nonnegative integer,
A(t)
is an
n\times n
complex, unitary matrix, and
\mathbf{z}(t)
is a complex
n\times 1
vector. We take
A(t)
to be unitary as this corresponds to quantum systems of interest. More generally, the derivations in this section hold when
A(t)
is invertible, a condition always satisfied when (
2
) arises by discretizing a linear ordinary differential equation with a sufficiently small time step. When
A
is unitary,
A^{\dagger}=A^{-1}
; in case
A(t)
is invertible but not unitary, one can replace
A(t)^{\dagger}
with
A(t)^{-1}
.

Suppose we are given a complex
m\times n
matrix
R
with
m\ll n
that enables one to compute, at each time
t
, a
reduced quantity of interest
:

\mathbf{y}(t)=R\mathbf{z}(t).

(3)

Here
\mathbf{y}(t)
is a complex
m\times 1
vector. Starting with
\mathbf{y}(t+1)
and using (
2
-
3
), we have

\mathbf{y}(t+1)=R\mathbf{z}(t+1)=RA(t)\mathbf{z}(t).

(4)

To close this equation, and thereby enable propagation of
\mathbf{y(t)}
, we must express
\mathbf{z}(t)
in terms of
\{\mathbf{y}(t-\ell),\ldots,\mathbf{y}(t-1),\mathbf{y}(t)\}
(for some
\ell\leq t
).

II.1
Self-Contained Propagation of Reduced Quantities

From (
2
), we can derive

\mathbf{z}(t-\ell)=\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}A(t-\ell+j)^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}(t).

(5)

Multiplying both sides by
R
and using (
3
) at time
t-\ell
yields

\mathbf{y}(t-\ell)=R\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}A(t-\ell+j)^{\dagger}\mathbf{z}(t).

(6)

Stacking column vectors vertically and applying both (
3
) and (
6
), we obtain

\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-1)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-2)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}(t-\ell)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R\mathbf{z}(t)\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-1)\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-2)\\
\vdots\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-\ell)\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}R\\
RA(t-1)^{\dagger}\\
RA(t-2)^{\dagger}A(t-1)^{\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
R\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}A(t-\ell+j)^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}}_{M(t)}\mathbf{z}(t).

(7)

Here
M(t)
has size
(\ell+1)m\times n
, i.e.,
\ell+1
block rows each of size
m\times n
. Assume that
R
, the first block of
M(t)
, has rank
m
(full rank because
m<n
). Each subsequent block of
M(t)
has rank
m
, since each consists of
R

M(t)
will have rank
n
as long as

\ell\geq\lfloor n/m\rfloor-1
.

Under this condition, the matrix
M(t)
will have a pseudoinverse

that is a one-sided left inverse.
Applying the pseudoinverse, we obtain

\mathbf{y}(t+1)=R\mathbf{z}(t+1)=RA(t)\mathbf{z}(t)=\underbrace{RA(t)M(t)^{+}}_{S(t)}\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(t)=S(t)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-1)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-2)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}(t-\ell)\end{bmatrix}

(8)

This self-contained, linear, time-delay equation for the reduced variable
\mathbf{y}(t)
underpins the present study.
Note that

has size
m\times(\ell+1)m
. In practice, we compute an approximate pseudoinverse

by ignoring sufficiently small singular values of

Time-delayed DMD aims to learn

that minimizes the squared error between the left- and right-hand sides of

\mathbf{y}(t+1)=\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}S_{j}\mathbf{y}(t-j)=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}S_{0}&S_{1}&\cdots&S_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}}_{S}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-1)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}(t-\ell)\end{bmatrix}

(9)

In time-delayed DMD, one learns
S
from observations of
\mathbf{y}(t)
. We see that (
8
) is structurally identical to (
9
) except that

is replaced by

.
The key difference between our method and time-delayed DMD is that rather than learn

from data, we derive

from first principles, leveraging the structure of (
2
-
3
).
In Section
IV
, we will see that no observations of
\mathbf{y}(t)
are needed or used to derive the

that appears in (

Reexamining the derivation above, we find that there is no reason why we must step back by precisely one time step at a time. That is, we can formulate our method using a

. With a general stride of

\mathbf{Y}_{\ell}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-k)\\
\mathbf{y}(t-2k)\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{y}(t-\ell k)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}R\mathbf{z}(t)\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-k)\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-2k)\\
\vdots\\
R\mathbf{z}(t-\ell k)\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}R\\
R\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}A(t-k+j)^{\dagger}\\
R\prod_{j=0}^{2k-1}A(t-2k+j)^{\dagger}\\
\vdots\\
R\prod_{j=0}^{\ell k-1}A(t-\ell k+j)^{\dagger}\end{bmatrix}}_{M(t)}\mathbf{z}(t).

7
), the block
RA(t-1)^{\dagger}
will be close to the
R
block. This may make it difficult for
M(t)
to achieve full rank. By using a sufficiently large stride
k
in (
10
), we can correct for this. Our motivating principle is that with
k
sufficiently large, each successive
\prod
term in the
M(t)
matrix in (
10
) will
differ enough from the identity
so that
M(t)
will have high (if not full) rank.

In this work, we focus on the discrete-time scheme (
10
). Our results in Section
V.3
—see Section
V.3.2
in particular—hint that the dynamics may converge as we refine our time step
\Delta t
while keeping the physical memory length
k\ell\Delta t
constant. In Appendix

, we give a continuous-time generalization of the above derivation, but we reserve further discussion of this for future work.

II.2
Connection with Partially Observed Linear Systems

To provide further mathematical motivation for the present paper, we examine the consequences of viewing (
2
-
3
) through the lens of partially observed linear systems
(
Pan and Duraisamy 2020
, Section 1)
. In this subsection only, we assume
A(t)=A
is a fixed (not time-dependent) unitary matrix. As mentioned above, we assume that the matrix
R
from (
3
) has rank
m
. Starting with
R
and completing the basis, one can find
\widetilde{R}
of size
(n-m)\times n
such that the square
n\times n
matrix
\mathbf{R}=\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}R\\
\widetilde{R}\end{bmatrix}
has rank
n
. Then

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}(t)\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbf{z}(t)=\mathbf{R}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)\end{bmatrix}.

With this, we have

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t+1)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t+1)\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{z}(t+1)=\mathbf{R}A\mathbf{z}(t)=\underbrace{\mathbf{R}A\mathbf{R}^{-1}}_{B}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)\end{bmatrix}

Now we partition the
n\times n
matrix
B
into blocks that match the sizes of
\mathbf{y}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
:
B_{11}
is
m\times m
,
B_{12}
is
m\times(n-m)
,
B_{21}
is
(n-m)\times m
, and
B_{22}
is
m\times m
. Then the previous equation becomes

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t+1)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t+1)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}B_{11}&B_{12}\\
B_{21}&B_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}(t)\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)\end{bmatrix}.

From this, one can derive the following time-delay evolution equation
Pan and Duraisamy 2020
:

\mathbf{y}(t+1)=B_{11}\mathbf{y}(t)+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}B_{12}B_{22}^{s}B_{21}\mathbf{y}((t-1)-s)+B_{12}B_{22}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(0).

(11)

This is essentially a Mori-Zwanzig equation: the three terms on the right-hand side can be viewed as (i) a non-time-delayed or Markovian term
B_{11}\mathbf{y}(t)
, (ii) a time-delayed or non-Markovian term that is a linear combination of
\{\mathbf{y}(0),\ldots,\mathbf{y}(t-1)\}
, and (iii) a closure term that involves
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}
. To handle this closure term, one assumes
Pan and Duraisamy 2020
that
B_{12}B_{22}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(0)\to 0
. More generally,
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(t)
is often viewed as noise
Chorin and Hald 2014
.

Here we note that as
A
is unitary and
B=\mathbf{R}A\mathbf{R}^{-1}
, the eigenvalues of
B
all have modulus
1
. Hence we cannot expect that
B_{12}B_{22}^{t}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(0)\to 0
for generic unitary choices of
A
and
\mathbf{R}
. More generally, we cannot expect to use arguments based on retaining (or projecting onto) eigenvectors of
B
associated with the top
k
(in modulus) eigenvalues of
B
. It is likely that we will not be able to truncate the summation in the memory term.

In preliminary work, we implemented (
11
) for a system that corresponds to (
2
-
3
) with unitary
A
. Given
R
, it is up to us to form
\widetilde{R}
such that
\mathbf{R}
is full rank. We found that when
A
is dense and unitary, for many choices of
\widetilde{R}
, the resulting system (
11
) is numerically unstable for long-time propagation. When
A
is diagonal and unitary, long-time propagation succeeds. In this regime, we found that we cannot ignore the
\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}(0)
term; it does not decay as
t\to\infty
. Similarly, regarding the second term in (
11
), unless we sum over
all
past states
\mathbf{y}(s)
for
s<t
, the resulting
\mathbf{y}(t)
propagation is highly inaccurate.

The above provides additional mathematical/computational motivation to pursue our propagation scheme (
8
). In (
8
), there is no requirement to solve for
\widetilde{R}
, the time delay is fixed, and we do not encounter any closure problems.

III
Background

We focus our attention on the dynamics of electrons in molecular systems under the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, in which we treat the nuclei as fixed. This yields the problem of solving for the dynamics of
N
electrons in the field of
N^{\prime}
nuclei. Then the equation of motion is the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE)

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)=\hat{H}(t)\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t).

(12)

Here each
\mathbf{x}_{k}=(\mathbf{r}_{k},\sigma_{k})
with spatial coordinate
\mathbf{r}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and spin coordinate
\sigma_{k}
that does not appear in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
. We describe our treatment of the spin coordinates below in Definition
1
. The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
is a self-adjoint operator that can be decomposed as
\hat{H}(t)=\hat{H}_{e}+\hat{V}_{\text{ext}}(t)
, the sum of the electronic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{e}
with an external potential operator
\hat{V}_{\text{ext}}(t)
that models, for instance, an applied electric field. In atomic units, the electronic Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}_{e}=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{k}^{2}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{A=1}^{N^{\prime}}\frac{Z_{A}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{A}\|}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{j<k}\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{k}\|}.

(13)

Here
\nabla_{k}^{2}
is the Laplacian over coordinates
\mathbf{r}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The first term encodes the kinetic energy operators of all
N
electrons; the remaining terms are potential energy operators. The second term encodes all electron-nuclear Coulomb attractions, with nucleus
A
having charge
Z_{A}
and fixed position
\mathbf{R}_{A}
. The third term encodes all electron-electron Coulomb repulsions. For now, we have ignored spin coordinates of
\Psi
, as they do not appear in the Hamiltonian. Further details can be found in standard references
Szabo and Ostlund 2012
;
McWeeny 1989
.

As
\Psi
is a function of
3N+1
variables, it should not be surprising that exact solutions are unknown except when
N=1
. For
N\geq 2
, solving (
12
-
13
) numerically presents serious challenges. Classical numerical methods such as finite differences and finite elements do not address the key issue, which is how to represent/store
\Psi
. Computational chemistry provides several methods to do this, at varying levels of approximation. One such method is configuration interaction, which we now describe.

III.1
Time-Dependent Configuration Interaction

We begin with a choice of
basis set
. As they are designed to approximate eigenfunctions of an atomic Hamiltonian, the functions in the basis set are often called
atomic orbitals
. Two atom-centered basis sets commonly used for theoretical studies, which we refer to later in this paper, are STO-3G
(
Szabo and Ostlund 2012
, §3.6.2)
and 6-31G
Hehre, Ditchfield, and Pople 1972
. STO-3G is a minimal basis set that uses a linear combination of three Gaussians to describe a Slater type orbital. For hydrogen and helium, STO-3G provides one s-orbital for each atom. 6-31G is a double valence basis set that uses a larger number of Gaussian functions to better describe the nuclear cusp, and for hydrogen and helium provides s-orbitals of different size on each atom to deliver more flexibility for the electronic distribution. Let
K
denote the number of functions (atomic orbitals) in the basis set. As
K
increases, we approach the complete basis set limit, yielding more accurate calculations at added computational cost.

Definition 1

(Coordinates, Spin, and Orbitals)
.

Let
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{r},\sigma)
where
\mathbf{r}
is a spatial coordinate and
\sigma
is a spin coordinate. Let
\{f_{k}(\mathbf{r})\}_{k=1}^{K}
denote a set of atomic orbitals.
Let
\alpha(\sigma)
and
\beta(\sigma)
denote two functions of
\sigma
that correspond to spin up and spin down, the two possible spin states of an electron. We take
\alpha
and
\beta
to be orthonormal, i.e.,
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle=\langle\beta,\beta\rangle=1
while
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=0
. Let
\{\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\}_{j=1}^{K}
be an orthonormal set of spatial molecular orbitals; each
\phi_{j}
is a linear combination of the
K
atomic orbitals, i.e.,

\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}Y_{jk}f_{k}(\mathbf{r}).

(14)

Define the orthonormal set of spin-orbitals
\{\chi_{k}(\mathbf{x})\}_{k=1}^{2K}
via

\chi_{2j-1}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\alpha(\sigma)\ \text{ and }\ \chi_{2j}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\beta(\sigma),\quad\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,K.

(15)

Using the
1
-electron molecular spin-orbitals, we form
Slater determinants

(
Szabo and Ostlund 2012
, §2.2.3)
. Their use is motivated by the Pauli exclusion principle, which requires that electronic wave functions be antisymmetric. Exchanging
\mathbf{x}_{j}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
on the left-hand side of (
16
) causes an exchange of rows
j
and
k
in the determinant, yielding a factor of
-1
.

Definition 2

(Slater Determinants)
.

With a combination
\mathbf{i}=\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{N}\}
of
N
distinct elements from
\{1,2,\ldots,2K\}
, we form an
N
-electron Slater determinant

\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\begin{vmatrix}\chi_{i_{1}}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})&\chi_{i_{2}}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})&\cdots&\chi_{i_{N}}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\\
\chi_{i_{1}}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})&\chi_{i_{2}}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})&\cdots&\chi_{i_{N}}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\chi_{i_{1}}(\mathbf{r}_{N},\sigma_{N})&\chi_{i_{2}}(\mathbf{r}_{N},\sigma_{N})&\cdots&\chi_{i_{N}}(\mathbf{r}_{N},\sigma_{N})\end{vmatrix}.

(16)

There are
\binom{2K}{N}
possible
N
-electron Slater determinants; we use a subset of these to form CI (Configuration Interaction) basis functions. Let
N_{C}
denote the size of this subset.

Definition 3

(CI Basis)
.

For
1\leq q\leq N_{C}\leq\binom{2K}{N}
, let
\mathbf{i}(q)
enumerate the combinations in a fixed subset of distinct Slater determinants. Then the CI basis functions are

\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{a}=\sum_{q=1}^{N_{C}}C_{aq}\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)}\qquad\text{ for }a=1,\ldots,N_{C}.

(17)

Given an atomic orbital basis set
\{f_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, in order to proceed with concrete CI basis functions (
17
), we must solve numerically for the matrices
Y
and
C
in (
14
) and (
17
). That is, we must pin down the coefficients that express the molecular orbitals in terms of the atomic orbitals, and the coefficients that express the CI basis functions in terms of the
N
-electron Slater determinants. In CI approaches, one first solves the time-independent Hartree-Fock equations, a nonlinear eigenvalue problem, to determine
Y
. The resulting
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are used to construct Slater determinants (
16
). One then solves for
C
such that the CI basis
\{\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{a}\}_{a=1}^{N_{C}}
is orthonormal and diagonalizes
\hat{H}_{e}
. In contrast, there exist other approaches, such as CASSCF (complete active space self-consistent field)
Roos, Taylor, and Sigbahn 1980
, where one solves jointly for
Y
and
C
. Either way, the CI basis functions (
17
) are orthonormal and diagonalize the
\hat{H}_{e}
operator.

In TDCI and TDCASSCF
Sato and Ishikawa 2013
;
Olsen et al. 1988
;
Peng, Fales, and Levine 2018
, the time-dependent versions of CI and CASSCF, the wave function takes the following form:

\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N},t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N_{C}}a_{n}(t)\Psi_{n}^{\mathrm{CI}}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N}).

(18)

Even with an incomplete (i.e., finite) set of atomic orbitals, if we use all possible Slater determinants in (
18
), and if we then solve for the coefficients
a_{n}(t)
such that
\Psi
satsifies (
12
), what we obtain is the exact solution of the TDSE within the finite atomic orbital basis set we chose. In particular, this solution accounts for electron correlation, an effect that we must model if we are to use our results to improve models of the exchange-correlation potential
V_{\text{xc}}
in TDDFT. Repeating this procedure for increasing values of basis set size (
K
),
\Psi
will converge to the solution of the TDSE (
12
).

Substituting (
18
) into the TDSE (
12
) and expressing the Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}(t)
in the CI basis
\{\Psi_{n}^{\mathrm{CI}}\}_{n=1}^{N_{C}}
yields an ordinary differential equation for the time-evolution of the coefficients
\mathbf{a}(t)=[a_{1}(t),\ldots,a_{N_{C}}(t)]
:

i\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{a}(t)=(H_{0}+V_{\text{ext}}(t))\mathbf{a}(t)=H(t)\mathbf{a}(t).

(19)

Here
H_{0}
and
V_{\text{ext}}(t)
are the matrix expressions of the corresponding operators
\hat{H}_{e}
and
\hat{V}_{\text{ext}}(t)
in the CI basis. In this paper, we typically apply to the molecule an electric field in the
z
-direction; the corresponding potential can be written within the dipole approximation as
V_{\text{ext}}(t)=f(t)M_{\text{dip}}
, where
f(t)
is a time-dependent field strength and
M_{\text{dip}}
is the dipole moment matrix (in the
z
direction). Both
H_{0}
and
M_{\text{dip}}
will depend on the particular molecular system that we are studying. Hence

H(t)=H_{0}+V_{\text{ext}}(t)=H_{0}+f(t)M_{\text{dip}}.

(20)

III.2
Full Density Matrix

The full density matrix associated with (
18
) is

P(t)=\mathbf{a}(t)\mathbf{a}(t)^{\dagger}.

(21)

Taking the time-derivative of both sides and using (
19
), we obtain (
1
) with
H=H(t)
, the time-dependent Hamiltonian (
20
). This shows that the
N_{C}\times N_{C}
full TDCI density matrix
P(t)
satisfies the Liouville-von Neumann equation with the same Hamiltonian matrix
H(t)
used in (
19
). By (
21
),
P(t)
is Hermitian for all
t
. As
H(t)
does not depend on
P
, the Liouville-von Neumann equation is linear in the
entries
of
P
. For a matrix
A
of size
M\times M
, let
\vvec(A)
denote its vectorized or flattened representation as an
M^{2}\times 1
vector. Then (
1
) can be written as

\frac{d}{dt}\vvec(P(t))=-i\mathcal{H}(t)\vvec(P(t)),

(22)

where, using
\otimes
to denote Kronecker product,
\mathcal{H}(t)=I\otimes H(t)-H(t)^{T}\otimes I
. This is the superoperator algebra formulation of the Liouville-von Neumann equation
Zwanzig 1964
;
Ohtsuki and Fujimura 1989
;
May and Kühn 2023
. Suppose we fix
\Delta t>0
and discretize (
22
) in time using the first-order scheme

\vvec(P(t+\Delta t))=\exp(-i\mathcal{H}(t)\Delta t)\vvec(P(t)).

(23)

With
\oplus
denoting Kronecker sum, we have

\exp(-i\mathcal{H}(t)\Delta t)=\exp((iH(t)^{T}\Delta t)\oplus(-iH(t)\Delta t))=\exp(iH(t)^{T}\Delta t)\otimes\exp(-iH(t)\Delta t),

(24)

a unitary matrix of size
N_{C}^{2}\times N_{C}^{2}
. Hence we can identify
\exp(-i\mathcal{H}(t))
with
A(t)
in (
2
), and further identify
\vvec(P(t+\Delta t))
and
\vvec(P(t))
with
\mathbf{z}(t+1)
and
\mathbf{z}(t)
, respectively.

Normalization of
\Psi
implies
\mathbf{a}(t)^{\dagger}\mathbf{a}(t)=1
for all
t
, which then implies
\trace(P(t))=1
for all
t
.

IV
Methodological Results

IV.1
Reduced Density

To define the
1
-electron reduced density matrix, we must first define density operators. Let
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N})
. We use
\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{X})
as equivalent notation for the wave function
\Psi(\mathbf{X},t)
defined by (
18
). Then, for each
t
,
\Psi_{t}
is an element of a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{S}
with inner product
\langle F,G\rangle=\int\overline{F(\mathbf{X})}G(\mathbf{X})\,d\mathbf{X}
. For the purposes of the present study, precise identification of
\mathcal{S}
is unimportant—all we need is the inner product.

Definition 4

(Full Density Operator)
.

For
F\in\mathcal{S}
, let
F^{\ast}
denote the linear functional in the dual space
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
that acts on a function
G\in\mathcal{S}
via
F^{\ast}(G)=\langle F,G\rangle
. This is equivalent to bra-ket notation; we identify
F\in\mathcal{S}
with
|F\rangle
, and we identify
F^{\ast}\in\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
with
\langle F|
. Then the full density operator
P_{t}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
associated with the wave function (
18
) is

\mathrm{P}_{t}=|\Psi_{t}\rangle\langle\Psi_{t}|=\Psi_{t}\Psi_{t}^{\ast}=\sum_{k,\ell}a_{k}(t)\overline{a_{\ell}(t)}\Psi_{k}^{\mathrm{CI}}(\Psi_{\ell}^{\mathrm{CI}})^{\ast}.

(25)

If we express this operator in the orthonormal CI basis
\{\Psi_{n}^{\mathrm{CI}}\}_{n=1}^{N_{C}}
, we obtain the full density matrix
P(t)
defined in (
21
).

To obtain the
1
-electron reduced density operator, we take the sum of
N
integrals: for the
j
-th such integral, we integrate the full density operator over all spins and all spatial coordinates other than
\mathbf{r}_{j}
. All of the integrals are identical due to the indistinguishability of electrons. Thus we carry out this procedure for
j=1
and scale the result by
N
:

Definition 5

(Marginalization and Reduced Density Operator)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a complex Hilbert space of functions that depend only on
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For the full density operator
\mathrm{P}_{t}
, marginalization yields a reduced density operator
\left(\mathrm{P}_{t}\right)_{1}:\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}
defined by its action on a test function
f\in\mathcal{R}
:

\left(\mathrm{P}_{t}\right)_{1}(f)=N\int\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{r},\sigma_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\int_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\overline{\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\sigma_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})}f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,d\sigma_{1}d\mathbf{x}_{2}\cdots d\mathbf{x}_{N}.

(26)

Equivalently, we can first define the
1
-electron reduced pair density function

\rho(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)=N\int\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{r},\sigma_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})\overline{\Psi_{t}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},\sigma_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n})}\,d\sigma_{1}d\mathbf{x}_{2}\cdots d\mathbf{x}_{N},

(27)

in which case we can more easily express the
1
-electron reduced density operator as

\mathrm{Q}_{t}=\left(\mathrm{P}_{t}\right)_{1}(f)=\int_{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\rho(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime},t)f(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\,d\mathbf{r}^{\prime}.

(28)

Note that the
1
-electron reduced density
\rho(\mathbf{r},t)
defined in Section
I
is obtained by evaluating the pair density (
27
) at
\mathbf{r}^{\prime}=\mathbf{r}
. The matrix representation of the operator (
28
) induced by the pair density (
27
) is the 1RDM (
1
-electron reduced density matrix)
Q(t)
. The details are collected here:

Definition 6

(Reduced Quantities)
.

Consider the reduced density operator:

\mathrm{Q}_{t}=\left(P_{t}\right)_{1}=N\sum_{k,\ell}a_{k}(t)\overline{a_{\ell}(t)}\left(\Psi_{k}^{\mathrm{CI}}(\Psi_{\ell}^{\mathrm{CI}})^{\ast}\right)_{1}.

(29)

The 1RDM
Q(t)
is the matrix expression of the operator
\mathrm{Q}_{t}
in the orthonormal basis of molecular orbitals
\{\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\}_{j=1}^{K}
. Using (
21
), we obtain both the
K\times K
matrix
Q(t)
and the
N_{c}\times N_{c}\times K\times K
tensor
B
:

\displaystyle Q_{b,c}(t)=\langle\phi_{b},\mathrm{Q}_{t}\phi_{c}\rangle=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{N_{C}}P_{k,\ell}(t)B_{k,\ell,b,c},

(30)

\displaystyle B_{k,\ell,b,c}=N\int\overline{\phi_{b}(\mathbf{r})}\left(\Psi_{k}^{\mathrm{CI}}(\Psi_{\ell}^{\mathrm{CI}})^{\ast}\right)_{1}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{c}(\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{r},

(31)

We reshape the tensor
B
into a matrix of size
N_{C}^{2}\times K^{2}
, which we denote as
\widetilde{B}^{T}
.

The full density operator
\mathrm{P}_{t}
yields the joint probability density of finding each electron with a particular spin at a particular spatial location at time
t
. In contrast, the reduced density operator
\mathrm{Q}_{t}
yields the probability density of finding
an
electron (with either spin) at a particular spatial location at time
t
.
Knowledge of the 1RDM
Q(t)
is often sufficient to compute quantities that can be compared against experiments.

IV.2
Computing the
B
Tensor

To compute
B
, we must carry out the integral in (
31
); in full generality, this boils down to marginalizing outer products of Slater determinants.

Proposition 1
.

For any fixed choice of distinct Slater determinants,
suppose the CI basis functions are defined by (
17
). Then for
1\leq k,\ell\leq N_{C}
, we can compute the core of
B
defined by (

).

\left(\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{k}(\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{\ell})^{\ast}\right)_{1}=\sum_{q=1}^{N_{C}}\sum_{q^{\prime}=1}^{N_{C}}C_{kq}\overline{C_{\ell q^{\prime}}}\bigl(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)}(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q^{\prime})})^{\ast}\bigr)_{1}.

(32)

To compute the right-hand side, there are two cases. When
q=q^{\prime}
,

\bigl(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)}(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)})^{\ast}\bigr)_{1}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\phi_{\lceil i_{k}(q)/2\rceil}\phi_{\lceil i_{k}(q)/2\rceil}^{\ast}

(33)

When
q\neq q^{\prime}
, there exist integers
a
,
a^{\prime}
, and

such that

and

\bigl(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)}(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q^{\prime})})^{\ast}\bigr)_{1}=\frac{(-1)^{Z}}{N}\phi_{\lceil a/2\rceil}\phi_{\lceil a^{\prime}/2\rceil}^{\ast}.

(34)

The results (
33
-
34
), together with (
32
), enable a complete evaluation of
B
.

For the proof, see Appendix
A.1
. Using Proposition
1
, we have developed Python code that computes the

tensor. This code takes as input the results of a static (time-independent) CI calculation: the coefficients
Y
in (
14
), the coefficients
C
in (
17
), and the enumeration of Slater determinants
\mathbf{i}(q)
in (
17
).

IV.3
Propagating 1RDMs

Equipped with the
B
tensor, we can propagate
1
-electron reduced densities forward in time, as summarized in the next result. As in (

), we formulate this for the case of a general stride
k\geq 1
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
k\geq 1
be the stride. With the Hamiltonian
H(t)
from (

) and
\widetilde{B}^{T}
from the end of Definition
6
, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{j}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{B}\left(C_{jk}(t)^{T}\otimes A_{jk}(t)\right)

\displaystyle C_{m}(t)

Suppose that

For the proof, see Appendix

to reconstruct/approximate the full density
\vvec{P(t)}

In practice, we use an approximate pseudoinverse computed via the SVD

\Sigma^{+}_{jj}=\begin{cases}1/\Sigma_{jj}&\Sigma_{jj}>r_{\text{tol}}\Sigma_{11}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here

All off-diagonal entries of

We expect that memory (
\ell>0

13

Returning to the setting where we retain all terms in the Hamiltonian (

IV.4
Incorporating Symmetries and Constraints

The crux of the method presented above is the solution of the linear system (
52
) which aims to reconstruct
P(t)
from present and past 1RDMs. Though we have mentioned that
P(t)
is always Hermitian and satisfies
\trace(P(t))=1
, we have not yet made use of these symmetries/constraints.

In Figure
2
, the second time-dependent coefficient (labeled
a_{1}(t)
in the plot) is identically zero for
\text{HeH}^{+}
in STO-3G. By (
21
), this implies that the second row and second column of
P(t)
will be identically zero. We can view this as an additional set of constraints on
P(t)
.

In Propositions
3
,
4
, and
5
, we make use of, respectively, the Hermitian symmetry of

, constant trace of
P(t)
, and identically zero elements in

. Each allows us to reduce the dimensionality of the linear system (
52
) that must be solved at each time step of (
36
). Combining these results, we obtain the reduced-dimensional system (
41
), which automatically preserves symmetries/constraints.

Recall (
21
), which implies that
P(t)
is Hermitian for all

. Thus for

, it is redundant to solve separately for

and

). This motivates replacing
\vvec(P(t))
in (

) with a representation involving only the upper-triangular (including diagonal) entries.

Definition 7

(Basis for Space of Hermitian Matrices)
.

Let

be the vector space of

(1,2),\ldots,(1,N_{C}),(2,3),\ldots,(2,N_{C}),\ldots,(N_{C}-1,N_{C})

For

For example, when

Lemma 1

Accounting for

Proposition 5

Proposition 6

(Constraint-Preserving Propagation of 1RDMs)
.

Suppose the hypotheses of Propositions
2
,
4

At time

For the above reasons, (

IV.5
Characterizing the Rank of
M(t)

Let us reexamine
M(t)
from (
52
). The purpose of including additional blocks
(\mathcal{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{D}_{\ell})
in
M(t)
is to increase the rank of the resulting matrix. Having found numerically that for sufficiently large
\ell
,
M(t)
achieves rank equal to its number of columns, we briefly examine the question of whether (and under what hypotheses) we can prove this.

As
H(t)
is always Hermitian,
C_{m}(t)^{T}
and
A_{m}(t)
defined by (
35
) will always be unitary. By (
35a
),
\mathcal{D}_{j}
consists of
\widetilde{B}
multiplied by a unitary matrix. Recall that
\widetilde{B}
is of size
K^{2}\times N_{C}^{2}
with
K<N_{C}
.
If
\rank(\widetilde{B})=K^{2}
, then
\rank(\mathcal{D}_{j})=K^{2}
as well.

A simple observation is that if
(C_{m}(t)^{T}\otimes A_{m}(t))
happens to be the identity matrix, then
M(t)
will consist of
\widetilde{B}
repeated
(\ell+1)
times; in this scenario,
\rank(M(t))=K^{2}
for any
\ell
. Thus the assumption that adding blocks
increases
the rank of
M(t)
stems from our intuition that as
t
increases, the system’s dynamics will move
C_{m}(t)^{T}\otimes A_{m}(t)

away
from the identity.

A quantum system’s dynamics are determined by its Hamiltonian
H(t)
. If we examine (
20
), we see three objects about which we have made no assumptions thus far: the static Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, the external field strength
f(t)
, and the dipole moment matrix
M_{\text{dip}}
. We have yet to find hypotheses on
H_{0}
,
f(t)
and
M_{\text{dip}}
that guarantee that, for sufficiently large
\ell
, the matrix
M(t)
has rank equal to
N_{C}^{2}
for all
t\geq 1
.

To motivate future work, we examine the
\ell=1
case in more detail. For a matrix
Z
and a set of positive integers
G
, let
Z^{G}
(respectively,
Z^{-G}
) denote the matrix formed by selecting only those columns of
Z
with indices in (respectively, not in)
G
. Then we can give a condition under which
M(t)
has full rank.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\ell=1
and consider
M(t)
from (
52
). Suppose
2K^{2}\leq N_{C}^{2}
and
\rank(\widetilde{B})=K^{2}
. Equipped with column indices
G\subset\{1,2,\ldots,N_{C}^{2}\}
, let us form the block matrix
\displaystyle\widehat{M}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{B}^{G}&\widetilde{B}^{-G}\\
\mathcal{D}_{1}^{G}&\mathcal{D}_{1}^{-G}\end{bmatrix}
. If there exists
G
of size
|G|=K^{2}
such that (i)
\widetilde{B}^{G}
has rank
K^{2}
and (ii) the Schur complement of
\widetilde{B}^{G}
of
\widehat{M}
has rank
K^{2}
, then
M(t)
has rank
2K^{2}
.

For the proof, see Appendix
A.4
. More generally, all we need is for
\rank(M(t))
to increase each time we increase
\ell
by
1
; an increase of precisely
K^{2}
is unnecessary. If we can reorder the columns of
M(t)
to form the block matrix
\widehat{M}
with the desired properties, then we can apply the Guttman rank additivity formula
Zhang 2005
to compute the rank of
M(t)
. Both the above lemma and the Guttman formula use the Schur complement.

V.1
Procedure

Because computing full CI solutions is expensive, and as this is the first time we are studying (
41

\text{HeH}^{+}
and
\text{H}_{2}

Computing ground truth 1RDMs

17

For

V.3.7
Further work is required to distill the relationship between memory-dependence and basis set

For each molecular system, as we increase the size of the basis set, we expect to compute more accurate electron dynamics. In the course of studying propagation method (
41

as computed by (

\text{H}_{2}

has a zero diagonal. This has non-trivial implications for the numerics in (
41

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented and derived a linear, closed time-delay system to propagate
1
-electron reduced density matrices (1RDMs). To construct this system, we needed to understand the details of how to compute 1RDMs from full density matrices. Building on concepts and methods from quantum chemistry, we developed a mathematical formalism that enables full evaluation of the
B
tensor that relates full configuration interaction densities to 1RDMs—see Proposition
1
. These results are general and do not depend on the basis set, number of electrons, or subset of Slater determinants that comprise the CI basis functions.

With
B
in hand, we derived (
36
), our first propagation scheme for 1RDMs. The main insight in this derivation was to add enough memory-based constraints—relating the full density at the present time to 1RDMs at past times—to change a linear system from underdetermined to overdetermined. Next, we accounted for properties of full density matrices: their Hermitian symmetry, constant trace, and zero elements that can be eliminated
a priori
. This resulted in the improved propagation scheme (
41
), which we implemented numerically and studied.

In our numerical tests, we focused on two
2
-electron molecules (
\text{H}_{2}
and
\text{HeH}^{+}
), each in two basis sets (STO-3G and 6-31G). For the resulting four molecular systems, we showed that the propagation scheme (
41
) produced highly accurate 1RDMs given a sufficient time delay. For each molecular system, the accuracy of our propagation method improved as we increased total memory-dependence (
k\ell\Delta t
) measured not in time steps but in physical units of time. For fixed
\Delta t
, when we held the number of delay steps
\ell
fixed but increased the stride
k
, we saw the error approach machine precision. We also saw that the error of our propagation scheme is approximately invariant under refinement of
\Delta t
. This was particularly important given that the true dynamics take place in continuous time.

The present study motivates three areas for future work. First, having developed mathematical methods to propagate 1RDMs that arise from CI wave functions, we are now well-positioned to address scientific questions. We aim to establish basis-independent physical time scales of memory-dependence. This will require disentangling the interdependence between memory-dependence, applied field parameters (including frequency, intensity, number of cycles), and the molecule as represented by its core Hamiltonian
H_{0}
and dipole moment matrix
M_{\text{dip}}
. We also hope to understand the memory-dependence of 1RDMs for larger systems. As
N
increases and the number of basis functions increases, we expect that the required memory will also increase due to the larger number of possible electronic states involved in the dynamics. For such systems, the method used here will not be feasible due to the need to construct the pseudoinverse. Also, the TDCASSCF method with an all electron, full orbital active space will become intractable. In this case, other, more approximate electronic structure methods such as TDCIS (time-dependent configuration interaction singles) can be used to compute ground truth 1RDMs
Hirata, Head-Gordon, and Bartlett 1999
;
Foresman et al. 1992
. Compared to TDCASSCF, TDCIS has fewer excited states for electrons to enter. It will be of interest to study if and how approximations such as TDCIS impact the memory-dependence of 1RDMs.

Second, we can improve the propagation scheme (
41
). At present, the linear system (
40
) involves roughly half of the entries of
P(t)
—the upper-triangular real and imaginary parts, including all but one element on the diagonal. Suppose we eliminate more columns from
M^{\prime\prime}(t)
and thereby learn fewer entries of the rank-
1
matrix
P(t)=\mathbf{a}(t)\mathbf{a}(t)^{\dagger}
. We can then formulate a quadratic programming problem to learn
\mathbf{a}(t)
from our incompletely determined
P(t)
. Solving this at each time step, we could propagate
\mathbf{a}(t)
forward in time, giving another route to propagate 1RDMs. The resulting method would be nonlinear, unlike the linear method we have derived in the present work. If enough columns could be eliminated from
M^{\prime\prime}(t)
, condition numbers would decrease, yielding greater accuracy.

Third, our work proves that there exists a time-dependent affine map that propagates 1RDMs forward in time; concretely, this affine map is the right-hand side of (
41
) not including
\mathbf{b}_{\ell}(t)
. Imagine if we could learn this time-dependent map without having to compute the Hamiltonian
H(t)
. Such a method would enable propagation of 1RDMs without the computational expense of running a static CI calculation to determine the core Hamiltonian
H_{0}
. This would achieve large-scale computational cost savings. It is an open question whether, for instance, machine learning methods could be used to learn the time-dependent affine map from data.

In Section
I
, we described recent efforts to machine learn exchange-correlation potentials
V_{\text{xc}}
with memory-dependence on
1
-electron reduced densities. In future work, we plan to use the methods developed in this paper (or improved versions thereof) to study memory-dependence of 1RDMs for a wide array of molecular systems in a broad set of field conditions, with the goal of achieving a comprehensive understanding of memory-dependence. This will enable a more robust and chemically insightful search of neural network architectures to accurately model and eventually learn
V_{\text{xc}}
in higher dimensions using, e.g., a three-dimensional version of the adjoint method derived previously
Bhat et al. 2022
. Learning memory-dependent
V_{\text{xc}}
functionals would would bring TDDFT beyond the realm of the adiabatic approximation, fixing the egregious errors of commonly employed memory-independent potentials and enabling more accurate time-dependent electron dynamics simulations of molecules and materials.

Appendix B
Constant Trace for 1RDMs

Suppose one computes 1RDMs
Q(t)
directly from full density matrices
P(t)
using the
B
tensor as in (
30
). The following theorem shows that the resulting 1RDMs must have constant trace.

Theorem 1

(Constant Trace)
.

Regardless of which Slater determinants are used to form the CI basis functions, the 1RDM
Q(t)
has constant trace equal to
N
, the number of electrons in the system.

Proof.

From (
30
), we obtain

\trace(Q(t))=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{N_{C}}P_{k,\ell}(t)\sum_{b=1}^{K}B_{k,\ell,b,b}.

(57)

From (
31
) and (
32
),

\sum_{b=1}^{K}B_{k,\ell,b,b}=N\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\int\overline{\phi_{b}(\mathbf{r})}C_{kq}\overline{C_{\ell q^{\prime}}}\left(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)}(\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q^{\prime})})^{\ast}\right)_{1}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{b}(\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{r}.

Examining (
34
), we see that this integral will vanish in Case II, whenever
q\neq q^{\prime}
. Thus we need only consider
q=q^{\prime}
(Case I), upon which (
33
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{K}B_{k,\ell,b,b}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{N^{C}}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\int\overline{\phi_{b}(\mathbf{r})}C_{kq}\overline{C_{\ell q}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\phi_{\lceil i_{m}(q)/2\rceil}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{\lceil i_{m}(q)/2\rceil}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{b}(\mathbf{r})\,d\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=1}^{N^{C}}\sum_{b=1}^{K}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\delta_{\lceil i_{m}(q)/2\rceil,b}C_{kq}\overline{C_{\ell q}}=\sum_{q=1}^{N^{C}}NC_{kq}\overline{C_{\ell q}}

(58)

To obtain the final equality, note that for each fixed
m
, there is precisely one
b
such that
\lceil i_{m}(q)/2\rceil=b
. Summing over
m
, we obtain
N
.

Next, we claim that Slater determinants themselves are orthonormal: to see this, return to (
45
) and integrate both sides with respect to
\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{N})
. We will obtain zero unless
\gamma^{n,q}\equiv\gamma^{m,q^{\prime}}
, which is impossible unless
q=q^{\prime}
. In that case, the integral will be
1
, i.e.,
\langle\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)},\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q^{\prime})}\rangle=\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}
. Using this, orthonormality of the CI basis functions (
18
) yields

\delta_{ab}=\langle\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{a},\Psi^{\mathrm{CI}}_{b}\rangle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}=1}^{N_{C}}\overline{C_{aq}}C_{bq^{\prime}}\langle\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q)},\psi^{\text{SL}}_{\mathbf{i}(q^{\prime})}\rangle=\sum_{q=1}^{N_{C}}\overline{C_{aq}}C_{bq},

i.e.,
C
is unitary. Using this, (
58
), (
57
), and
\trace(P(t))\equiv 1
, we find

\trace(Q(t))=\sum_{k,\ell=1}^{N_{C}}P_{k,\ell}(t)N\delta_{k,\ell}=N.

(59)

∎

Appendix C

1
-electron terms in the Hamiltonian

If we retain only
1
-electron terms in our Hamiltonian, then we neglect (entirely) all electron-electron interactions, including Coulomb, exchange, and correlation terms. In this case, it should be possible to propagate the 1RDM exactly with no memory. While this makes sense physically, we would like to establish that for such a Hamiltonian, the 1RDM propagation scheme from the manuscript works correctly with no memory. Specifically, consider the
\ell=0
(memoryless) case of (

\operatorname{vec}(Q(t+1))=\widetilde{B}\bigl(\exp(iH(t)^{T}\Delta t)\otimes\exp(-iH(t)\Delta t)\bigr)\widetilde{B}^{+}\operatorname{vec}(Q(t)).

Here
H(t)
stands for the
full
TDCI Hamiltonian. In this appendix, we analyze (
60
) fully in the case where

only contains
1
-electron terms, and show that it reduces to an exact propagation equation that involves only the
1-electron Hamiltonian
.

C.1
Setup

We will first work in the specific setting where we have
N=2
electrons,
K=2
atomic orbitals, and
N_{C}=4
total CI determinants. These parameters correspond to
\text{HeH}^{+}
and
\text{H}_{2}
in STO-3G. Later, in Subsection

, we will explain how the calculation generalizes to
\text{HeH}^{+}
and
\text{H}_{2}
in 6-31G.

In what follows, full CI density matrices

are of size

, while 1RDMs

are of size

. We will use capital

to denote
4\times 4
Hamiltonian matrices, and lowercase

to denote
2\times 2
Hamiltonian matrices.

Before analyzing our scheme (
36
), we set up the problem. We carry this out in detail to illustrate our methods concretely. For a particular index map
\mathbf{i}(q)

\Psi^{\text{CI}}_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{4}C_{aq}\left[\chi_{i_{1}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\chi_{i_{2}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})-\chi_{i_{1}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})\chi_{i_{2}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\right]

, from which we form

spin-orbitals (

\chi_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r})\alpha(\sigma),\ \chi_{2}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{1}(\mathbf{r})\beta(\sigma),\ \chi_{3}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{2}(\mathbf{r})\alpha(\sigma),\ \text{ and }\chi_{4}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{2}(\mathbf{r})\beta(\sigma);

and Slater determinants given by the index mapping

\mathbf{i}(1)=(1,2),\quad\mathbf{i}(3)=(3,2),\quad\mathbf{i}(2)=(1,4),\quad\mathbf{i}(4)=(3,4).

For future reference, we reconcile this with more standard notation:

•

The
\mathbf{i}(1)=(1,2)
determinant is the Hartree-Fock determinant
|\psi^{\text{RHF}}\rangle
, where both the
\alpha
(spin up) and
\beta
(spin down) electrons are in MO (molecular orbital) 1.

•

The
\mathbf{i}(2)=(3,2)
determinant corresponds to promoting the
\alpha
electron from MO 1 to MO 2. This is the
|\psi_{1\alpha}^{2\alpha}\rangle
determinant.

•

The
\mathbf{i}(3)=(1,4)
determinant corresponds to promoting the
\beta
electron from MO 1 to MO 2. This is the
|\psi_{1\beta}^{2\beta}\rangle
determinant.

•

The
\mathbf{i}(4)=(3,4)
determinant corresponds to promoting both electrons from MO 1 to MO 2. This is the
|\psi_{11}^{22}\rangle
determinant.

Suppose we write the one-electron parts of the Hamiltonian in the form
\widehat{H}=\widehat{h}^{1}+\widehat{h}^{2}
, where
\widehat{h}^{j}
encompasses the kinetic and electron-nuclear operators that affect electron
j
. Borrowing the notation from (

), we have

\widehat{h}^{j}=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{j}^{2}-\sum_{A=1}^{N^{\prime}}\frac{Z_{A}}{\|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{R}_{A}\|}.

We introduce the notation

h_{k,\ell}=\int\overline{\chi_{\ell}(\mathbf{r}_{j},\sigma_{j})}\widehat{h}^{j}\chi_{k}(\mathbf{r}_{j},\sigma_{j})\,d\mathbf{r}_{j}.

These integrals do not actually depend on
j
, which is why we purposely do not include a superscript

on the left-hand side. If we apply
\widehat{h}^{1}
to a CI wave function, we obtain

\widehat{h}^{1}\Psi^{\text{CI}}_{a}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{4}C_{aq}\left[\chi_{i_{2}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})\widehat{h}^{1}\chi_{i_{1}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})-\chi_{i_{1}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{2},\sigma_{2})\widehat{h}^{1}\chi_{i_{2}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\right].

Hence

\langle\Psi^{\text{CI}}_{b},\widehat{h}^{1}\Psi^{\text{CI}}_{a}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}C_{aq}\overline{C_{bq^{\prime}}}\biggl[\delta_{i_{2}(q),i_{2}(q^{\prime})}\int\overline{\chi_{i_{1}(q^{\prime})}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})}\widehat{h}^{1}\chi_{i_{1}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\,d\mathbf{r}_{1}\\

+\delta_{i_{1}(q),i_{1}(q^{\prime})}\int\overline{\chi_{i_{2}(q^{\prime})}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})}\widehat{h}^{1}\chi_{i_{2}(q)}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\sigma_{1})\,d\mathbf{r}_{1}\biggr].

-th entry of a

This is consistent with the Slater-Condon rules. Using the Kronecker product

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\mathscr{B}_{q,q^{\prime},\lceil j/2\rceil,(j-1)\%2+1}\mathscr{B}^{+}_{q,q^{\prime},\lceil m/2\rceil,(m-1)\%2+1}=\sum_{r}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{jr}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}^{+}_{rm}=\delta_{jm}.

This itself implies three of the four properties one must check for
\widetilde{B}^{+}
to be the pseudoinverse of
\widetilde{B}
:
\widetilde{B}\widetilde{B}^{+}\widetilde{B}=\widetilde{B}
,
\widetilde{B}^{+}\widetilde{B}\widetilde{B}^{+}=\widetilde{B}^{+}
, and
\widetilde{B}\widetilde{B}^{+}
is Hermitian. For the remaining property, we must show that the
16\times 16
matrix
\widetilde{B}^{+}\widetilde{B}
is Hermitian. Here we simply reverse the order of the multiplication above to find that

\displaystyle\sum_{r}\widetilde{B}^{+}_{jr}\widetilde{B}_{rm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}B^{+}_{k,\ell,\lceil j/2\rceil,(j-1)\%2+1}B_{k,\ell,\lceil m/2\rceil,(m-1)\%2+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell,q,q^{\prime},t,t^{\prime}}C^{\dagger}_{qk}\mathscr{B}^{+}_{q,q^{\prime},\lceil j/2\rceil,(j-1)\%2+1}C_{\ell q^{\prime}}C_{kt}\mathscr{B}_{t,t^{\prime},\lceil m/2\rceil,(m-1)\%2+1}C^{\dagger}_{t^{\prime}\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}}\mathscr{B}^{+}_{q,q^{\prime},\lceil j/2\rceil,(j-1)\%2+1}\mathscr{B}_{q,q^{\prime},\lceil m/2\rceil,(m-1)\%2+1}=\sum_{r}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}^{+}_{jr}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}_{rm}=(\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}^{+}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}})_{jm}.

The matrix
\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}^{+}\widetilde{\mathscr{B}}
is Hermitian; hence
\widetilde{B}^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\widetilde{B}
.
