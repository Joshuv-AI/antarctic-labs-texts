A Hill-Pick matrix criteria for the Lyapunov order Thanks: This work is based on the research supported in part by the National Research Foundation of South Africa (Grant Number 90670 and 118513).
S. ter Horst, A. van der Merwe

Abstract
. The Lyapunov order appeared in the study of Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation for positive real odd functions with general (real) matrix points. For real or complex matrices A A and B B it is said that B B Lyapunov dominates A A if H = H ∗ , H A + A ∗ H ≥ 0 ⟹ H B + B ∗ H ≥ 0 . H=H^{*},\quad HA+A^{*}H\geq 0\quad\implies\quad HB+B^{*}H\geq 0. (In case A A and B B are real we usually restrict to real Hermitian matrices H H , i.e., symmetric H H .) Hence B B Lyapunov dominates A A if all Lyapunov solutions of A A are also Lyapunov solutions of B B . In this paper we restrict to the case that appears in the study of Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation, namely where B B is in the bicommutant of A A and where A A is Lyapunov regular, meaning the eigenvalues λ j \lambda_{j} of A A satisfy λ i + λ ¯ j ≠ 0 , i , j = 1 , … , n . \lambda_{i}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{j}\neq 0,\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n. In this case we provide a matrix criteria for Lyapunov dominance of A A by B B . The result relies on a class of ∗ * -linear maps for which positivity and complete positivity coincide and a representation of ∗ * -linear matrix maps going back to work of R.D. Hill. The matrix criteria asks that a certain matrix, which we call the Hill-Pick matrix, be positive semidefinite.

Introduction

Throughout this paper
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
or
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
. To avoid confusion about transposes or adjoints, symmetric and Hermitian matrices, etc., we shall use notation and terminology as if
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
. We write
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
for the set of
n\times n
Hermitian matrices,
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
for the set of all positive definite matrices in
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
and
{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}
for the set of all positive semidefinite matrices in
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
. Given
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, we call
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
a solution to the Lyapunov inequality of
A
(Lyapunov solution of
A
for short) if

HA+A^{*}H\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\ \ (\text{strict case})\quad\text{or}\quad HA+A^{*}H\in{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}\ \ (\text{non-strict case}).

The corresponding solution sets are denoted

{\mathcal{H}}(A)=\{H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}:HA+A^{*}H\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\}\quad\text{and}\quad{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)=\{H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}:HA+A^{*}H\in{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}\}.

Note that these are
convex invertible cones
, that is, convex cones that are closed under inversion. Lyapunov inequalities play an important role in linear systems and control theory, cf.,
[
30
,
10
,
9
]
. The matrix
A
is said to have
regular inertia
if
A
has no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis
i{\mathbb R}
. Moreover, we say that
A
is
Lyapunov regular
if the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
of
A
satisfy

\lambda_{i}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{j}\neq 0,\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,n.

In
[
6
]
Cohen and Lewkowicz introduced the
Lyapunov order
(for
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
), namely, for matrices
A,B\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, it is said that
B

Lyapunov dominates

A
, denoted
A\leq_{{\mathcal{L}}}B
, if

(1.1)

H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n},\quad HA+A^{*}H\in{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}\implies HB+B^{*}H\in{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n},

or equivalently

(1.2)

{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)\subseteq{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(B).

Hence the Lyapunov order
A\leq_{\mathcal{L}}B
means that all non-strict Lyapunov solutions of
A
are also Lyapunov solutions of
B
.

More precisely, in
[
6
]
the Lyapunov order was studied under some natural constraints on
A
and
B
that appear in the context of Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation, see also
[
7
]
. For now restrict to
A,B\in{\mathbb R}^{n\times n}
. Write
{\mathcal{C}}(A)
for the convex invertible cone generated by
A
,
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{O}}
for the class of
positive real odd rational functions
, that is, rational functions with real coefficients that map the right half-plane into the right half-plane, and define
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{O}}(A)=\{f(A)\colon f\in{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{O}}\}
. Then
{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{O}}(A)={\mathcal{C}}(A)
, and
B\in{\mathcal{P}}{\mathcal{R}}{\mathcal{O}}(A)
implies that
B
is in the bicommutant
\{A\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}
of
A
and
A\leq_{{\mathcal{L}}}B
; cf.,
[
7
,
6
]
for further details. It is conjectured in
[
6
]
that the converse implication also holds when
A
is Lyapunov regular, but to the best of our knowledge there is no prove of this claim yet. In Subsection 1.4 of
[
6
]
a Pick test to verify whether
A\leq_{{\mathcal{L}}}B
is discussed. In the general setting, this Pick test corresponds to verifying whether a representative of each extreme ray of
{\mathcal{H}}(A)
is also in
{\mathcal{H}}(B)
, while for
B\in\{A\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}
it suffices to test this for a representative of a single extreme ray.

The main contribution of the present paper is that, by different methods, for both
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
and
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
, given
B\in\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
with
A
Lyapunov regular, we determine a single matrix
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
of size at most
n\times n
so that
A\leq_{{\mathcal{L}}}B
corresponds to
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
being positive semidefinite. The matrix
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
is obtained from what we called Hill representations for
*
-linear maps in
[
17
]
, going back to work of R.D. Hill in
[
14
]
, and coincides with the classical Pick matrix in the case that
A
and
B
are diagonal matrices
[
29
]
.

Determining when two matrices
A
and
B
have a common Lyapunov solution, that is,
{\mathcal{H}}(A)\bigcap{\mathcal{H}}(B)\neq\emptyset
or
{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)\bigcap{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(B)\neq\emptyset
, is a notoriously difficult problem, which has only been resolved in special cases, cf.,
[
23
,
5
,
2
,
12
,
22
,
21
,
8
]
and references given there. While the inclusion condition
{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)\subseteq{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(B)
of the Lyapunov order is a different problem, we expect that similar issues may arise when no further constraints are added; as mentioned above, the Pick test of
[
6
]
for the general problem, with
A
Lyapunov regular, involves determining the extreme rays of the Lyapunov solution set
{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)
. The essential condition in the present paper is that
B
is in the bicommutant of
A
, which implies
B
has a structure compatible with the Jordan structure of
A
; see Section
2
below.

We further point out here that there is an analogous matrix order based on the Stein inequality
H-A^{*}HA\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
, called the Stein order, which appears in the context of Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation in the unit disk, rather than the half-plane, which has also been extended to noncommutative several variable interpolation; see
[
1
,
3
,
25
,
4
]
for further details.

Given a square matrix
Y\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, we define the
Lyapunov operator
associated with
Y
by

(1.3)

{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}:{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}\to{\mathbb F}^{n\times n},\quad{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}(X)=XY+Y^{*}X,\quad X\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}.

The Lyapunov operator associated with
Y
is a linear matrix map which is bijective precisely when
Y
is Lyapunov regular, cf.,
[
16
, Corollary 4.4.7]
. It is clear that
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}(X^{*})={\mathcal{L}}_{Y}(X)^{*}
for each
X\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, so that
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}
is
*
-linear (i.e., linear and preserves adjoints), so that, in particular,
{\mathcal{L}}_{Y}
maps
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
into
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
; cf.,
[
17
]
.

Using the Lyapunov operator we can write the Lyapunov solution sets as

{\mathcal{H}}(A)={\mathcal{L}}_{A}^{-1}\left({\mathcal{P}}_{n}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad{\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}}(A)={\mathcal{L}}_{A}^{-1}\left({\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}\right).

If
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
is Lyapunov regular,
B\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
Lyapunov dominates
A
whenever the
*
-linear map

(1.4)

{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}={\mathcal{L}}_{B}{\mathcal{L}}_{A}^{-1}:{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}\to{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}

maps
{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}
into
{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}
, that is, when
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
is a positive linear matrix map. Determining whether a linear matrix map is positive is in general not easy. In contrast, it is easy to verify whether a linear matrix map is completely positive, by verifying that the associated Choi matrix is positive semidefinite. Complete positivity implies positivity, so that the Choi matrix criteria provides a sufficient condition, in general. However, one of the main results of this paper shows that in the specific case studied in this paper, the Choi matrix criteria is also necessary.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A,B\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
with
A
Lyapunov regular and
B\in\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
. Then

{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}\text{ is positive if and only if }{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}\text{ is completely positive.}

Hence
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
is positive if and only if the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}_{A,B}\in{\mathbb F}^{n^{2}\times n^{2}}
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
is positive semidefinite.

This result will be proved in Section
5
using Theorem
1.2
below, which provides a more general class of
*
-linear matrix maps for which positivity and complete positivity coincide, continuing a line of research initiated in
[
18
]
.

We now describe our main result in the setting of general
*
-linear matrix maps

(1.5)

{\mathcal{L}}:{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}\to{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}.

With such a map
{\mathcal{L}}
we associate two matrices, the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
given by

(1.6)

{\mathbb L}=\left[{\mathbb L}_{ij}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{nq\times nq},\ \ {\mathbb L}_{ij}={\mathcal{L}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{ij}^{(q)}\right)\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}\ \ \mbox{for $i,j=1,\ldots,q,$}

where
\mathcal{E}_{ij}^{(q)}
is the standard basis element in
{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}
with a 1 in position
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere, and what we call the matricization of
{\mathcal{L}}
, which is the matrix
L\in{\mathbb F}^{n^{2}\times q^{2}}
determined by the linear map

(1.7)

L:{\mathbb F}^{q^{2}}\to{\mathbb F}^{n^{2}},\quad L\,\left(\operatorname{vec}_{q}(V)\right)=\operatorname{vec}_{n}\left({\mathcal{L}}(V)\right),\quad V\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q},

where
\operatorname{vec}_{r\times s}:{\mathbb F}^{r\times s}\to{\mathbb F}^{rs}
is the vectorization operator, abbreviated to
\operatorname{vec}_{r}
in case
r=s
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a
*
-linear matrix map as in (
1.5
) with matricization
L
as in (
1.7
). If there exists a matrix
C\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
such that
L
is in
{\overline{\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}}}\otimes\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
, then positivity and complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
coincide.

Here
{\overline{\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}}}
is the algebra obtained by taking the (entrywise) complex conjugates of the elements of
\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
. We prove Theorem
1.2
in Subsection
4.2
below.

The condition that the matricization
L
be contained in the algebra
{\overline{\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}}}\otimes\{C\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
may seem restrictive, however, we point out that, by Theorem 5.1 in
[
17
]
, the fact that
{\mathcal{L}}
is
*
-linear implies that there always exists a subspace
{\mathcal{W}}\subset{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
such that
L\in{\overline{{\mathcal{W}}}}\otimes{\mathcal{W}}
, for instance by taking
{\mathcal{W}}
equal to the span of the
n\times q
blocks of
L
; see Corollary
3.6
below.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is based on a type of representation of linear matrix maps studied by R.D. Hill in
[
13
,
14
]
, which we will refer to as Hill representations. These representations have the form

(1.8)

{\mathcal{L}}(V)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}{\mathbb H}_{kl}\,A_{k}VA_{l}^{*},\quad V\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q},

for matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
. The matrix
{\mathbb H}=\left[{\mathbb H}_{kl}\right]_{k,l=1}^{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times m}
is called the Hill matrix associated with the representation (
1.8
). Moreover, we call the Hill representation (
1.8
) of
{\mathcal{L}}
minimal if
m
is the smallest number of matrices
A_{k}
that occurs in Hill representations for
{\mathcal{L}}
, and it turns out that this smallest number equals the rank of the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
. A detailed analysis of minimal Hill representations was conducted in
[
17
]
and a review of the relevant results from
[
17
]
will be given in Section
3
, along with some results on non-minimal Hill representations of
{\mathcal{L}}
, which were not covered in
[
17
]
.

If
{\mathcal{L}}
is given by the minimal Hill representation (
1.8
), then the Choi matrix factors as

(1.9)

{\mathbb L}=\widehat{A}^{*}{\mathbb H}^{T}\widehat{A}\quad\mbox{with }\widehat{A}^{*}:=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{1}\right)&\ldots&\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{m}\right)\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{nq\times m}

and
\widehat{A}
is a matrix with full row rank. As proved by Poluikis and Hill in
[
27
]
, it follows that complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
corresponds to positive definiteness of
{\mathbb H}
. Positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
corresponds to

(1.10)

\left(z\otimes x\right)^{*}{\mathbb L}\left(z\otimes x\right)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}\text{ and }z\in{\mathbb F}^{q},

see
[
19
]
. In particular, by the above factorisation of
{\mathbb L}
, it follows that a positive map
{\mathcal{L}}
is completely positive when the bilinear map determined by the matrix
\widehat{A}
:

(1.11)

(z,x)\mapsto\widehat{A}(z\otimes x),\quad x\in{\mathbb F}^{n},\,z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}

is surjective. Little appears to be known about the ranges of bilinear maps. In
[
18
]
we considered special cases for which (
1.11
) is surjective, although we also encountered a case where the bilinear map (
1.11
) was not surjective, while positivity and complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
still coincided. An instinctive approach we use in this paper is to find a vector
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
such that
\textup{rank\,}\widehat{A}\left(I_{q}\otimes x\right)=m,
or a vector
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that
\textup{rank\,}\widehat{A}\left(z\otimes I_{n}\right)=m
, since for both cases it follows that (
1.11
) is surjective; with
I_{p}
the identity matrix of size
p\times p
. We provide a criteria for when this happens, which includes the case of Theorem
1.2
.

The Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
is of size
nq\times nq
, but its rank
m
may be much smaller. Hence to test for complete positivity, it may be more convenient to test whether the Hill matrix
{\mathbb H}
is positive definite than to test positive semidefiniteness of
{\mathbb L}
. Moreover, as shown in examples in
[
17
]
and exploited further in
[
18
]
, by making an appropriate selection of the Hill representation, in the factorization of the Choi matrix (
1.9
), typically ‘structural properties’ of
{\mathcal{L}}
(more correctly, of
L
) are contained in the matrix
\widehat{A}
while the ‘data’ of
{\mathcal{L}}
is stored in
{\mathbb H}
. To some extend, this phenomenon also occurs in non-minimal Hill representations, provided the choice is made in the right way; see Subsection
3.3
.

In the context of the
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
, with
A
and
B
as in Theorem
1.1
, it turns out that the rank
m
of the Choi matrix is at most
n
, so that the Hill matrix
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
, called the Hill-Pick matrix for this specific case, has a size of at most
n\times n
. We compute this matrix explicitly, as well as the matricization
L_{A,B}
determined by
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
, in Section
5
. For the case where
A
and
B
are diagonal,
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
coincides with the Pick matrix from
[
29
]
, hence our choice of the name Hill-Pick matrix.

{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}

. In Section

we recall some results on
*
-linear maps and minimal Hill representations from

that will be used throughout the paper. We also prove some results on non-minimal Hill representations in this section. Then we present some new results on classes of

-linear matrix maps for which positivity and complete positivity coincide in Section

. In particular, in Section

we prove Theorem
1.2
. The proof of our main result on the Lyapunov order, Theorem

, will be given in Section
5
along with explicit formulas for the Hill-Pick matrix
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
for the case where

.

,

. For some more specialized results we do provide precise references or a proof in case we did not find an appropriate source. There is some overlap with notation introduced in the introduction, but we feel it may be helpful to the reader to have everything in one place.

2.1.
Notation and basic linear algebra results

Throughout this paper
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
or
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
. We write
{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
for the vector space of
n\times m
matrices over
{\mathbb F}
and
{\mathbb F}^{n}
for the space of all (column) vectors over
{\mathbb F}
of length
n
. Occasionally we will identify
{\mathbb F}^{n}
with
{\mathbb F}^{n\times 1}
, so that matrix operations can be applied to vectors in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
. Furthermore we write
\textup{GL}(n,{\mathbb F})
for the set of invertible matrices in
{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
.

The standard
j
-th basis element in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
is denoted by
e_{j}^{(n)}
or simply
e_{j}
when the length is clear from the context. We write
{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}^{(n,m)}
for the standard basis element of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
with
1
in position
(i,j)
and zeros elsewhere, i.e.,
{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}^{(n,m)}=e_{i}^{(n)}e_{j}^{(m)T}
, abbreviated to
{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}^{(n)}
when
m=n
. With
\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}
we indicate the all-one vector of length
n
and with
\mathbb{1}_{n\times m}
the all-one matrix of size
n\times m
, so that
\mathbb{1}_{n\times m}=\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{m}^{T}
. Also here, we write
\mathbb{1}_{n}
for
\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}
. Furthermore,
I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix.

For
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
we write
A^{T}
for its transpose,
A^{*}
for its adjoint and
\overline{A}
for its complex conjugate. Of course, if
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
, then
A^{T}=A^{*}
and
{\overline{A}}=A
. The null space of
A
is denoted
\textup{Ker\,}A
. Whenever
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
is a subset of matrices, then by
{\overline{\Lambda}}
,
\Lambda^{T}
,
\Lambda^{*}
, etc. we indicate the sets of matrices obtained by applying the appropriate operation to the matrices in
\Lambda
.

We write
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
for the real subspace of Hermitian matrices in
{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, which coincides with the
n\times n
symmetric matrices
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
in case
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
, but not if
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
. With
A\geq 0
(resp.
A>0
) we indicate that
A
is positive semidefinite (resp. positive definite), and
{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}
(resp.
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
) denotes the set of positive semidefinite (resp. positive definite) matrices in
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
.

The
Kronecker product
of matrices
A=\left[a_{ij}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
and
B\in{\mathbb F}^{k\times l}
is defined as

A\otimes B=\left[a_{ij}B\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{(nk)\times(ml)}.

Note that for matrices
A
,
B
,
C
and
D
of appropriate size we have

(2.1)

\left(A\otimes B\right)\left(C\otimes D\right)=(AC)\otimes(BD).

The
vectorization
of a matrix
T\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
is the vector
\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}{(T)}\in{\mathbb F}^{nm}
defined as

\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}(T)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(e_{j}^{(m)}\otimes I_{n}\right)Te_{j}^{(m)}.

Note that the vectorization operator
\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}
defines an invertible linear map from
{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
onto
{\mathbb F}^{nm}
whose inverse is indicated by
\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}^{-1}
. If
m=n
we just write
\operatorname{vec}_{n}
and
\operatorname{vec}_{n}^{-1}
and if the sizes are clear from the context, the indices are often left out. Furthermore, we have the identity

\operatorname{vec}\left(AXB^{T}\right)=(B\otimes A)\operatorname{vec}(X)

which, when
A
and
B
are taken to be (transposes of) vectors yields

(2.2)

(z\otimes x)^{T}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}(W)=x^{T}Wz,\quad W\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m},x\in{\mathbb F}^{n},z\in{\mathbb F}^{m}.

Moreover note that

\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}\left(vw^{T}\right)=w\otimes v,\quad v\in{\mathbb F}^{n},w\in{\mathbb F}^{m},

from which we obtain that

(2.3)

\operatorname{vec}_{m\times n}\left(\mathcal{E}_{lk}^{(m,n)}\right)=\operatorname{vec}_{m\times n}\left(e_{l}^{(m)}e_{k}^{(n)^{T}}\right)=e_{k}^{(n)}\otimes e_{l}^{(m)}=e_{(k-1)m+l}^{(nm)}.

Next, recall that the
Hadamard product
of matrices
A=\left[a_{ij}\right],B=\left[b_{ij}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}
is defined as

A\circ B=\left[a_{ij}b_{ij}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times m}.

The
canonical shuffle
is the matrix defined by

{\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{E}_{ij}^{(m,n)}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{ji}^{(n,m)}\in{\mathbb F}^{mn\times mn},

which occasionally will be identified with a linear map on
{\mathbb F}^{mn}={\mathbb F}^{nm}
. Note that
{\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}
is an invertible map with inverse
{\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}^{-1}={\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}^{*}={\mathfrak{S}}_{nm}
and that for
u\in{\mathbb F}^{m}
,
v\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
and
A\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times n}
we have

(2.4)

{\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times m}\left(A^{T}\right)=\operatorname{vec}_{m\times n}\left(A\right),\quad{\mathfrak{S}}_{mn}\left(u\otimes v\right)=v\otimes u.

We conclude this subsection with a lemma of a general linear algebra nature, that will be of use in the sequel.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K_{1},\ldots,K_{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
be linearly independent. Define

(2.5)

\widetilde{K}=\begin{bmatrix}{\overline{K}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\overline{K}}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{mn\times q},\,\,\widecheck{K}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{1}^{*}\\
\vdots\\
K_{m}^{*}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{mq\times n}\text{ and }\widehat{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{K}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{K}_{m}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times nq}

where
\widehat{K}_{l}:=\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left({\overline{K}}_{l}\right)^{T}
for
l=1,\ldots,m
. Then
\textup{rank\,}\widehat{K}=m
, hence
\widehat{K}
has full row rank, and for
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n},z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
we have

(2.6)

\widehat{K}\left(z\otimes I_{n}\right)=\left(I_{m}\otimes z\right)^{T}\widecheck{K}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\widehat{K}\left(I_{q}\otimes x\right)=\left(I_{m}\otimes x\right)^{T}\widetilde{K}.

Proof.

Using (
2.2
), we obtain the first identity from

\displaystyle\widehat{K}\left(z\otimes I_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(K_{1}\right)^{*}\left(z\otimes I_{n}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(K_{m}\right)^{*}\left(z\otimes I_{n}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{T}K_{1}^{*}\\
\vdots\\
z^{T}K_{m}^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\left(I_{m}\otimes z\right)^{T}\widecheck{K}.

The second identity as well as the remaining claims follow from Lemma 5.5 in
[
17
]
(where in the definition of
\widetilde{K}
the bars are not included), noting that the context in which they were proved there is not relevant to the argument.
∎

2.2.
Real and complex Jordan decomposition

The (complex) Jordan form of a matrix
A\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
with (distinct) eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
is the decomposition of
A
of the form

(2.7)

A=P\,J_{A}\,P^{-1},\quad\mbox{with }J_{A}=\textup{diag}\left(J_{{\underline{n}}_{1}}\left(\lambda_{1}\right),\ldots,J_{{\underline{n}}_{r}}\left(\lambda_{r}\right)\right)

where
P\in\textup{GL}(n,{\mathbb C})
and
{\underline{n}}_{j}=\left(n_{j,1},\ldots,n_{j,k_{j}}\right)\in{\mathbb N}^{k_{j}}
, ordered decreasingly, for some
k_{j}\in{\mathbb N}
and for
j=1,\ldots,r
, and where we set

J_{{\underline{n}}_{j}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)=\textup{diag}\left(J_{n_{j,1}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right),\ldots,J_{n_{j,k_{j}}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)\right),

with
J_{n}(\lambda)
denoting the
n\times n
Jordan matrix with eigenvalue
\lambda
:

(2.8)

J_{n}(\lambda)=\lambda I_{n}+S_{n},\quad\mbox{where}\quad S_{n}=[\delta_{i+1,j}]_{i,j=1}^{n},

with
\delta_{i,j}
the Kronecker delta symbol. Note that
S_{n}
is the upper shift matrix. In this form the matrix
J_{A}
is uniquely determined by
A
. In case
A
is a real matrix, non-real eigenvalues come in complex conjugate pairs with corresponding Jordan structure, i.e., when
J_{{\underline{n}}}(\lambda)
occurs in
J_{A}
with
\lambda\in{\mathbb C}
non-real, then also
J_{{\underline{n}}}\left({\overline{\lambda}}\right)
occurs in
J_{A}
.

In order to introduce the real Jordan form of a real matrix
A
, let
{\mathfrak{C}}\subset{\mathbb R}^{2\times 2}
denote the
2\times 2
matrix representation of the complex numbers, that is,
{\mathfrak{C}}
is the commutative subalgebra of
{\mathbb R}^{2\times 2}
given by

{\mathfrak{C}}=\left\{C_{\lambda}:=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
-b&a\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in{\mathbb R}^{2\times 2}\colon\lambda=a+ib\in{\mathbb C}\right\}.

With
C\in{\mathfrak{C}}
and
n\in{\mathbb N}
we associate the real Jordan matrix

(2.9)

J_{n}(C)=I_{n}\otimes C+S_{n}\otimes I_{2},

and for a tuple
{\underline{n}}=\left(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\right)\in{\mathbb N}^{k}
we set

J_{{\underline{n}}}(C)=\textup{diag}\left(J_{n_{1}}(C),\ldots,J_{n_{k}}(C)\right).

Given a real matrix
A\in{\mathbb R}^{n\times n}
with (distinct) complex eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r_{1}}
and (distinct) real eigenvalues
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r_{2}}
, in addition to its complex Jordan form described above,
A
also has a real Jordan form which is the decomposition of
A
of the form
A=P\,J_{A}\,P^{-1},
where

(2.10)

J_{A}=\textup{diag}\left(J_{{\underline{m}}_{1}}\left(C_{\lambda_{1}}\right),\ldots,J_{{\underline{m}}_{r_{1}}}\left(C_{\lambda_{r_{1}}}\right),J_{{\underline{n}}_{1}}\left(t_{1}\right),\ldots,J_{{\underline{n}}_{r_{2}}}\left(t_{r_{2}}\right)\right)

with
P\in\textup{GL}(n,{\mathbb R})
and
{\underline{n}}_{j}=\left(n_{j,1},\ldots,n_{j,k_{j}}\right)\in{\mathbb N}^{k_{j}}
,
{\underline{m}}_{s}=\left(m_{s,1},\ldots,m_{s,l_{s}}\right)\in{\mathbb N}^{l_{s}}
, both ordered decreasingly, for some
k_{j},l_{s}\in{\mathbb N}
and for
s=1,\ldots,r_{1}
and
j=1,\ldots,r_{2}
.

2.3.
Block Toeplitz matrices

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}\subset{\mathbb F}^{m\times m}
be a matrix algebra. We write
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}
for the class of
n\times n
block Toeplitz matrices with blocks from
{\mathfrak{A}}
, that is, the matrices of the form

T=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}S_{n}^{*j}\otimes A_{-j}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}S_{n}^{j}\otimes A_{j}\quad\mbox{with}\quad A_{-n+1},\ldots,A_{n}\in{\mathfrak{A}},

with
S_{n}
the upper shift matrix defined in (
2.8
). Furthermore,
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}^{+}
denotes the matrix algebra of upper triangular block Toeplitz matrices in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}
, i.e., with
A_{-n+1}=\cdots=A_{-1}=0
, and
{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}
the matrix algebra of lower triangular block Toeplitz matrices in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}
, i.e.,
A_{1}=\cdots=A_{n}=0
. We will encounter block Toeplitz matrices with various choices of
{\mathfrak{A}}
; apart from
{\mathbb C}
and
{\mathbb R}
there will also be instances where
{\mathfrak{A}}
is equal to
{\mathfrak{C}}
or
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb C}}^{+}
,
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb R}}^{+}
or
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+}
. Note that when
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a commutative algebra, then so are
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}^{+}
and
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{A}}}^{-}
.

Note that the Jordan matrix
J_{n}(\lambda)
in (
2.8
) is in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb C}}^{+}
, while the real Jordan matrix
J_{n}(C)
in (
2.9
) is in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+}
. To accommodate the matrices
J_{A}
appearing in the real and complex Jordan decompositions, we also introduce a class of repeated, compressed block Toeplitz matrices. For
{\underline{n}}=\left(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\right)\in{\mathbb N}^{k}
we write
{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}}
for the classes of block diagonal matrices of the form
T=\textup{diag}\left(T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\right)
with
T_{j}\in{\mathfrak{T}}_{n_{j},{\mathfrak{A}}}
and for
1\leq j,s\leq k
with
n_{j}\geq n_{s}
we have that
T_{s}
is the compression of
T_{j}
to the first
n_{s}
(block) columns and first
n_{s}
(block) rows. Similarly we define
{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}}^{+}
by restricting the block diagonal entries to be upper triangular block Toeplitz matrices and
{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}}
is defined by restricting the block diagonal entries to be lower triangular block Toeplitz matrices. Note that
{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}}^{+}
and
{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}}
are also algebras, which are commutative whenever
{\mathfrak{A}}
is commutative.

The following lemma will be of use in the next subsection.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
{\underline{n}}\in{\mathbb N}^{k}
and
{\mathfrak{A}}_{1},{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}
matrix algebras over
{\mathbb F}
, we have

{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}={\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}}.

Proof.

Note that
{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}}={\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathbb F}}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}
. By the associativity property of tensors we find that

{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}=\left({\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathbb F}}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}\right)\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}={\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathbb F}}\otimes\left({\mathfrak{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}\right)={\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}},{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}\otimes{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}}.\qed

2.4.
The real and complex bicommutant of a matrix

For any subset
{\mathcal{D}}\subseteq{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, the commutant of
{\mathcal{D}}
in
{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
is the matrix algebra, which is closed under inversion, given by

\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{{\mathbb F}}=\{C\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}:CD=DC\quad\text{for all}\quad D\in\mathcal{D}\}.

The subscript
{\mathbb F}
is added since if
{\mathcal{D}}
consists of real matrices only, one can consider the commutant both in
{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
and
{\mathbb R}^{n\times n}
. The bicommutant of
{\mathcal{D}}
is the commutant of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{{\mathbb F}}
in
{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
and denotes as
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb F}^{\prime\prime}
.

If
{\mathcal{D}}=\{A\}
for a matrix
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, then
\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}\subset\{A\}_{\mathbb F}^{\prime}
which implies
\{A\}_{\mathbb F}^{\prime\prime}
is a commutative algebra which is closed under inversion. The bicommutant
\{A\}_{\mathbb F}^{\prime\prime}
is obtained by applying polynomials to
A
:

\{A\}_{\mathbb F}^{\prime\prime}=\left\{p(A)\colon p\in{\mathbb F}[x]\right\}.

See Chapters 5 and 6 in
[
11
]
for more details.

For the purpose of this paper we are interested in a representation of the elements of
\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}
and
\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}
in terms of the Jordan forms of
A
. Note that

\displaystyle\{J_{n}(\lambda)\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb C}}^{+}\text{ for all }\lambda\in{\mathbb C},\quad\{J_{n}(t)\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}={\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb R}}^{+}\text{ for all }t\in{\mathbb R}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\{J_{n}(C)\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+}\text{ for all }C\in{\mathfrak{C}}.

Furthermore, when a polynomial is applied to a direct sum of Jordan blocks with the same eigenvalue one obtains a direct sum of repeated compressions of the same Toeplitz matrix while for different eigenvalues, polynomials can be selected in such a way that the Toeplitz matrices can be chosen independently. Put together, we find for
A\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
given in Jordan form (
2.7
) that
B\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
is in
\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}
if and only if

B=P\,\textup{diag}\left(T_{1},\dots,T_{r}\right)\,P^{-1},\quad\mbox{with}\quad T_{j}\in{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{j},{\mathbb C}}^{+},\quad j=1,\ldots,r.

Hence, we have

(2.11)

\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}=P\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb C}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},{\mathbb C}}^{+}\right)\,P^{-1}=P\,\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{-1}.

Note that

\{A^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}=\left(P^{-1}\right)^{T}\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb C}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},{\mathbb C}}^{-}\right)\,P^{T}=\left(P^{-1}\right)^{T}\,\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{T}

and

\{J_{A}^{*}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}=\{{\overline{J_{A}}}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}={\overline{\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}}=\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}.

The latter identities do not necessarily hold if
J_{A}
is replaced by
A
.

Likewise, for
A\in{\mathbb R}^{n\times n}
given in real Jordan form (
2.10
), a matrix
B\in{\mathbb R}^{n\times n}
is in
\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}
if and only if

B=P\,\textup{diag}\left(R_{1},\ldots,R_{r_{1}},T_{1},\dots,T_{r_{2}}\right)\,P^{-1},\mbox{ with }R_{s}\in{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{s},{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+},\quad T_{j}\in{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{j},{\mathbb R}}^{+},

for all
\ s=1,\dots,r_{1}
and
j=1,\ldots,r_{2}.

Hence, we have

(2.12)

\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}=P\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{1},{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{r_{1}},{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+},{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb R}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r_{2}},{\mathbb R}}^{+}\right)\,P^{-1}=P\,\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{-1}

and

\displaystyle\{A^{T}\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{-1}\right)^{T}\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{1},{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{r_{1}},{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-},{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb R}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r_{2}},{\mathbb R}}^{-}\right)\,P^{T}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{-1}\right)^{T}\,\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{T}.

Lastly, for a given matrix
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
we describe the structure of
\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}
and in case
A
is a real matrix also the structure of
\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}
. In both cases the description is based on the Jordan structures of
A
. We start with the complex case.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
be given in Jordan form (
2.7
). Then

\{A\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}=(P\otimes P)\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},\{J_{A}\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},\{J_{A}\}_{\mathbb C}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\,(P\otimes P)^{-1}.

Proof.

Making use of the formula for
\{A\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}
in (
2.11
) with (
2.1
) it follows that

\displaystyle\{A\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(P\,\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{-1}\right)\otimes\left(P\,\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\,P^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=(P\otimes P)\,\left(\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,(P\otimes P)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(P\otimes P\right)\,\left(\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb C}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},{\mathbb C}}^{+}\right)\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,\left(P\otimes P\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(P\otimes P\right)\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb C}}^{+}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime},\dots\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},{\mathbb C}}^{+}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,\left(P\otimes P\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(P\otimes P\right)\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+}\right)\,\left(P\otimes P\right)^{-1},

where in the last identity we applied Lemma
2.2
with
{\mathfrak{A}}_{1}={\mathbb C}
and
{\mathfrak{A}}_{2}=\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Applying the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
2.3
one obtains

(2.13)

\displaystyle\{A^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)^{-1}\,\left(\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)^{-1}\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-}\right)\,\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)

as well as

\displaystyle\{A\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(P\otimes P\right)\,\left(\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,\left(P\otimes P\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(P\otimes P)\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{1},{\mathfrak{C}}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{r_{1}},{\mathfrak{C}}\otimes\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+},\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r_{2}},\{J_{A}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{+}\right)\,(P\otimes P)^{-1}

and

(2.14)

\displaystyle\{A^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{A^{T}\}_{\mathbb R}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)^{-1}\,\left(\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}\right)\,\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right)^{-1}\,\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{1},{\mathfrak{C}}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{m}}_{r_{1}},{\mathfrak{C}}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-},\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r_{2}},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb R}}^{\prime\prime}}^{-}\right)\,\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{T}\right).

3.
*
-Linear matrix maps and Hill representations

In this section we provide a brief review of
*
-linear matrix maps
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
1.5
) and Hill representations for such maps as studied by R.D. Hill in
[
14
]
, see also
[
27
]
. For proofs and further details we refer to
[
17
]
. Only in the last subsection some new results are presented, on non-minimal Hill representations.

3.1.
*
-Linear matrix maps

Consider a linear matrix map
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
1.5
), that is,

(3.1)

{\mathcal{L}}:{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}\to{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}.

Following
[
19
]
, we say that
{\mathcal{L}}
is
*
-linear if
{\mathcal{L}}(V^{*})={\mathcal{L}}(V)^{*}
for all
V\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}
. Define the matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
associated with
{\mathcal{L}}
via (
1.7
) and (
1.6
), respectively. Then
{\mathcal{L}}
can be written as

{\mathcal{L}}(V)=\operatorname{vec}_{n}^{-1}\left(L\operatorname{vec}_{q}(V)\right)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}v_{ij}{\mathbb L}_{ij},\quad V=[v_{ij}]\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}.

*
-Linearity of
{\mathcal{L}}
can be characterized in the following way.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a linear map as in (
3.1
) with matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. Decompose
L
as a block matrix
L=\left[L_{ij}\right]
where
L_{ij}=\left[\ell^{ij}_{kl}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathcal{L}}
is
*
-linear;

(ii)

{\mathbb L}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{nq}
;

(iii)

\ell_{kl}^{ij}={\overline{\ell}}_{ij}^{kl},\quad\text{for all}\quad i,k=1,\ldots,n
and
j,l=1,\ldots,q.

It is clear what
{\mathbb L}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{nq}
means. Property (iii) can be interpreted as saying that “structural properties of
L
as a block matrix reoccur at the level of the blocks,” as illustrated by various examples in Subsection 6.1 in
[
17
]
. The two observations that are relevant for the present paper are (using the notation of Theorem
3.1
):

(i)

L_{ij}=0
if and only if
\ell_{ij}^{kl}=0
for all
k
and
l
.

(ii)

L_{ij}=L_{rs}
for
(i,j)\neq(r,s)
if and only if
\ell^{kl}_{ij}=\ell^{kl}_{rs}
for all
k
and
l
.

This implies in particular that a (upper or lower) block Toeplitz structure in
L
corresponds to a (upper or lower) Toeplitz structure of the blocks
L_{ij}
.

3.2.
Minimal Hill representations

R.D. Hill studied
*
-linear matrix maps
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
3.1
) in
[
13
,
14
]
via representations of the form

(3.2)

{\mathcal{L}}(V)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}{\mathbb H}_{kl}\,A_{k}VA_{l}^{*},\quad V\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q},

for
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
and
{\mathbb H}=[{\mathbb H}_{kl}]_{k,l=1}^{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times m}
. We call (
3.2
) a
Hill representation
of
{\mathcal{L}}
and refer to
{\mathbb H}
as the associated
Hill matrix
. The Hill representation (
3.2
) of
{\mathcal{L}}
is called minimal if the number
m
is the smallest among all Hill representations of
{\mathcal{L}}
. This number
m
is equal to the rank of the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. In the case of a minimal Hill representation, the
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
must be linearly independent.

The results contained in the subsection come from
[
17
]
, but most have their origin in
[
13
,
14
]
; for proper references please consult
[
17
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
3.1
) is
*
-linear if and only if
{\mathcal{L}}
admits a (minimal) Hill representation with
{\mathbb H}
Hermitian. Moreover,
{\mathcal{L}}
is completely positive if and only if
{\mathcal{L}}
admits a (minimal) Hill representation with
{\mathbb H}
positive definite.

It is possible to express the matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
directly in terms of a minimal Hill representation, as explained in the next result.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a
*
-linear map as in (
3.1
) with a minimal Hill representation (
3.2
). Then the matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
associated with
{\mathcal{L}}
are given by

(3.3)

L=\sum_{k,l=1}^{m}{\mathbb H}_{kl}\,{\overline{A}}_{k}\otimes A_{l}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb L}=\widehat{A}^{*}{\mathbb H}^{T}\widehat{A},

with
\widehat{A}^{*}:=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{1}\right)&\ldots&\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{m}\right)\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{nq\times m}
. Moreover,
\widehat{A}
has full row rank and
\textup{Ker\,}\widehat{A}=\textup{Ker\,}{\mathbb L}
.

One of the main new features of
[
17
]
is a description of the minimal Hill representations of
{\mathcal{L}}
. This works as follows. Write the matricization
L
of
{\mathcal{L}}
as a block matrix

(3.4)

L=\left[L_{ij}\right]\quad\mbox{with}\quad L_{ij}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}\quad\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,n,\,j=1,\ldots,q$}.

It then follows that
m=\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}
satisfies

(3.5)

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

The criteria (

) for

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume

) is

Formula (

Corollary 3.6
.

3.3.
Non-minimal Hill representations

For the purpose of this paper it will also be convenient to consider non-minimal Hill representations. In this subsection we look at what parts of the construction of minimal Hill representations at end of the previous subsection still works in the non-minimal case.

Choose
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
among the blocks
L_{ij}
of L:

(3.13)

\displaystyle L_{k}=L_{i_{k}j_{k}},\quad k=1,\ldots,r,

\displaystyle i_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,n\},\quad j_{k}\in\{1,\ldots,q\},

where
r\geq m=\textup{rank\,}\,{\mathbb L}
, so that

(3.14)

\textup{span}\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}\}=\textup{span}\{L_{ij}:i=1,\ldots,n,\,j=1,\ldots,q\}.

Following the construction as given in Subsection
3.2
, there exist scalars
\alpha^{ij}_{k},\beta_{ij}^{k}\in{\mathbb F}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
j=1,\ldots,q
and
k=1,\ldots,r
, so that

(3.15)

L_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_{ij}^{k}L_{ij},\quad L_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\alpha^{ij}_{k}L_{k}.

In this case, both the
\beta_{ij}^{k}
and the
\alpha^{ij}_{k}
need not be unique. It is, in particular, possible to choose these numbers so that

(3.16)

\displaystyle\beta_{i_{k}j_{k}}^{k}

\displaystyle=1\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{ij}^{k}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad(i,j)\neq(i_{k},j_{k});

(3.17)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{i_{k}j_{k}}

\displaystyle=1\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{l}^{i_{k}j_{k}}=0\quad\text{for all}\quad l\neq k,

and in the remainder of this section we shall assume (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) to hold. Set

(3.18)

A_{k}=\left[{\overline{\alpha}}_{k}^{ij}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}\quad\text{and}\quad B_{k}=\left[\beta_{ij}^{k}\right]\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\ldots,r.

From our choice (
3.17
) we see that the matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
are still linearly independent since the
(i_{k},j_{k})
-th entry of
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\eta_{k}A_{k}
is equal to
\eta_{k}
. Hence
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\eta_{k}A_{k}=0
holds only in the case where
\eta_{k}=0
for all
k
. Also note that (
3.16
) gives
B_{k}=\mathcal{E}^{(n,q)}_{i_{k}j_{k}}
for all
k.
It still follows that

(3.19)

L=\sum_{k=1}^{r}{\overline{A}}_{k}\otimes L_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{k}=\left(\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}\otimes I_{n}\right)^{*}\left(\left(B_{k}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{n\times q}\right)\circ L\right)\left(\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{q}\otimes I_{q}\right).

Define the Hill matrix associated with the selection
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}
as:

(3.20)

\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}=\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}\left({\mathcal{L}};L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}\right):=\left[\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}^{*}\left(B_{k}\circ{\overline{L}}_{l}\right)\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{q}\right]_{k,l=1}^{r}\in{\mathbb F}^{r\times r}.

Then the
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
admits a non-minimal Hill representation with
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
and
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
as constructed above, as follows from the next theorem, which is an analogy to our main result in
[
17
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Assume
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
3.1
) is
*
-linear with matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. Choose
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}
so that (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) hold and define
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
and
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
as above. Then

(3.21)

{\mathcal{L}}(V)=\sum_{k,l=1}^{r}\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}_{kl}A_{l}VA_{k}^{*},\quad L=\sum_{k,l=1}^{r}\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}_{kl}\,{\overline{A}}_{k}\otimes A_{l}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb L}=\widehat{A}^{*}\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}^{T}\widehat{A},

for each
V\in{\mathbb F}^{q\times q}
, where
\widehat{A}^{*}:=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{1}\right)&\ldots&\operatorname{vec}_{n\times q}\left(A_{r}\right)\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{nq\times r}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
is in
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
with
\textup{rank\,}\,\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}=\textup{rank\,}\,{\mathbb L}
and
\widehat{A}
has full row rank.

Outline of proof.

From the choice we made for
A_{k}
in (
3.17
) and
B_{k}
in (
3.16
), for
k=1,\ldots,r
, the identities in Lemma 5.2 in
[
17
]
, given by

\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}^{*}\left(B_{k}\circ{\overline{A}}_{k}\right)\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{q}=\alpha_{k}^{i_{k}j_{k}}=1\quad\text{and}\quad\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{n}^{*}\left(B_{k}\circ{\overline{A}}_{l}\right)\vec{\mathbf{1}}_{q}=\alpha_{l}^{i_{k}j_{k}}=0\quad\text{for}\quad l\neq k,

still hold. Since only these identities are needed to prove Proposition 5.3 in
[
17
]
, we get the representation of
L
in (
3.21
) and the fact that
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
belong to
{\mathcal{H}}_{r}
. The representation of
{\mathbb L}
and the non-minimal Hill representation of
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
3.21
) follows by the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem 5.1 in
[
17
]
. The full row rank of
\widehat{A}
follows by Lemma
2.1
. Now, since
{\mathbb L}=\widehat{A}^{*}\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}^{T}\widehat{A}
, with
\widehat{A}
having full row rank, it follows at once from (
3.21
) that
\textup{rank\,}\,{\mathbb L}=\textup{rank\,}\,\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}.

∎

Hence,
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
need not be invertible, as in case of a minimal Hill representation. Moreover, we no longer have that
\textup{Ker\,}\,{\mathbb L}=\textup{Ker\,}\widehat{A}
. This is because
\textup{rank\,}\widehat{A}=r
and
\textup{rank\,}\,{\mathbb L}=m
, and therefore
\dim\textup{Ker\,}\,{\mathbb L}=nq-m
and
\dim\textup{Ker\,}\widehat{A}=nq-r.
When
r>m
, we only have the one inclusion
\textup{Ker\,}\widehat{A}\subset\textup{Ker\,}{\mathbb L}
, since the dimensions do not add up. Furthermore, from the fact that

\dim\textup{span}\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}\}=r\quad\mbox{and}\quad\dim\textup{span}\{L_{ij}:\,i=1,\ldots,n,\,j=1,\ldots,q\}=m

it follows that the equality in (
3.6
), with
m
replaced by
r
, no longer hold. Instead, from (iii) in Proposition 5.3 in
[
17
]
we get

\textup{span}\{L_{ij}:\,i=1,\ldots,n,\,j=1,\ldots,q\}=\textup{span}\{L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}\}\subset\textup{span}\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}\}.

We conclude with a result on the complete positivity of a
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
as constructed above.

Theorem 3.10
.

Assume
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
3.1
) is
*
-linear. Define
L
as in (
1.7
) and
{\mathbb L}
as in (
1.6
) and let
m=\textup{rank\,}\,{\mathbb L}.
Choose
L_{1},\ldots,L_{r}
so that (
3.13
) and (
3.14
) hold and define
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
as in (
3.18
) and
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
as in (
3.20
). Then
{\mathcal{L}}
is completely positive if and only if
\widetilde{{\mathbb H}}
is positive semidefinite.

Proof.

Since complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
coincides with the positive semidefiniteness of the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
, from the representation of
{\mathbb L}
in (
3.21
) along with the fact that
\widehat{A}
has full row rank, the result follows.
∎

Hence, to verify complete positivity with a non-minimal Hill representation as constructed above, in particular, with (
3.17
) and (
3.16
), checking whether the Hill matrix is positive semidefinite is still both a necessary and sufficient condition. With other choices of non-minimal Hill representations, this need not be the case.

4.
Positive maps that are also completely positive

We start this section with an observation made in
[
18
]
which enabled us to determine various classes of
*
-linear matrix maps
{\mathcal{L}}
for which positivity and complete positivity coincide. Recall that
{\mathcal{L}}
is completely positive if and only if the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
of
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive semidefinite. Whether
{\mathcal{L}}
is positive is not so easy to determine. The following proposition provides a necessary and sufficient criteria in terms of the Choi matrix. The result is essentially contained in Propositions 3.1 and 3.6 of
[
19
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

A
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
as in (
3.1
) is positive if and only if the Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
in (
1.6
) satisfies

(4.1)

\left(z\otimes x\right)^{*}{\mathbb L}\left(z\otimes x\right)\geq 0\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}.

Assume a
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
3.1
) is given by a minimal Hill representation (
3.2
) with Hill matrix
{\mathbb H}
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. Define
\widehat{A}
as in Proposition
3.3
. From

\left(z\otimes x\right)^{*}{\mathbb L}\left(z\otimes x\right)=\left(z\otimes x\right)^{*}\widehat{A}^{*}{\mathbb H}^{T}\widehat{A}\left(z\otimes x\right)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad z\in{\mathbb F}^{q},

we see that positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
is equivalent to
y^{*}{\mathbb H}^{T}y\geq 0
for all
y
from the set

(4.2)

{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}:=\{\widehat{A}(z\otimes x)\colon x\in{\mathbb F}^{n},\,z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}\}.

Complete positivity in turn, by Theorem
3.2
, is equivalent to
{\mathbb H}\geq 0
, which is the same as
{\mathbb H}^{T}\geq 0
. In particular, it follows that positivity and complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
coincide when
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
.

Note that

\widehat{A}(z\otimes x)=\left(\widehat{A}(I_{q}\otimes x)\right)z=\left(\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})\right)x.

From this it is clear that
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
holds whenever we can find a vector
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
such that the matrix
\widehat{A}(I_{q}\otimes x)\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times q}
has full row rank or a vector
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that the matrix
\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})\in{\mathbb F}^{m\times n}
has full row rank. Clearly there exists no such vector
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
if
m>q
and no such vector
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
is
m>n
. Hence proving that
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
in this way can only be done if
\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}
is at most
\max\{n,q\}
. We further point out that whether
\widehat{A}(I_{q}\otimes x)
or
\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})
has full row rank is independent of the choice of the minimal Hill representation, since the matrix
\widehat{A}
is unique up to multiplication on the left by a
T\in\textup{GL}(m,{\mathbb F})
; see Theorem 5.9 in
[
17
]
. Hence, these are properties of the
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}
and not of a specific minimal Hill representation.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
3.1
) be a
*
-linear map with matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. Set
m=\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}
, decompose
L
as in (
3.4
) and define
{\mathcal{W}}
as in (
3.6
). Then for any minimal Hill representation (
3.2
), for
\widehat{A}
defined as in Proposition
3.3
there exists a vector
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that
\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})
has full row-rank if and only if the subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
has the following property:

(C1)

\displaystyle\text{For any linearly independent }X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\text{ in }{\mathcal{W}},\text{ there exists a }v\in{\mathbb F}^{q}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ such that }X_{1}v,\ldots,X_{k}v\text{ is linearly independent in }{\mathbb F}^{n}.

Hence, if
(C1)
holds, then
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
and positivity and complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
coincide. Moreover, in that case
\dim{\mathcal{W}}\leq n
.

Proof.

We already observed above that the choice of the minimal Hill representation is irrelevant, by
[
17
, Theorem 5.9]
, so we may select a minimal Hill representation (
3.2
) of
{\mathcal{L}}
arbitrarily. Then it remains to show that there exists a vector
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that
\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})
has full row-rank if and only if (C1) holds.

First assume that (C1) holds. We know from Theorem
3.4
that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
are linearly independent matrices contained in
{\mathcal{W}}
. Hence there exists a vector
v\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
so that
A_{1}v,\ldots,A_{m}v
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
. Equivalently, by taking adjoints, the vectors
{\overline{v}}^{T}A_{1}^{*},\ldots,{\overline{v}}^{T}A_{m}^{*}
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb F}^{1\times n}
which is the same as saying that
(I_{m}\otimes{\overline{v}})^{T}\widecheck{A}
has full row-rank, with
\widecheck{A}
defined in the same way as
\widecheck{K}
in (
2.5
). By the first identity in (
2.6
) in Lemma
2.1
,
(I_{m}\otimes{\overline{v}})^{T}\widecheck{A}
having full row rank is the same as
\widehat{A}({\overline{v}}\otimes I_{n})
having full row rank, and thus our claim follows.

Conversely, assume there exists a
z\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that
\widehat{A}(z\otimes I_{n})
has full row-rank. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
be linearly independent matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}
. Then
k\leq m=\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}=\dim{\mathcal{W}}
. Without loss of generality we can assume
k=m
, extending
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
to a basis of
{\mathcal{W}}
if needed. Again using the fact that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
can be chosen arbitrarily, as long as (
3.6
) holds, we may assume that
A_{j}=X_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Then, since
\widehat{A}({\overline{v}}\otimes I_{n})
for
v={\overline{z}}
has full row-rank, by the analysis given above, which goes two ways, it follows that
A_{1}v,\ldots,A_{m}v
are linearly independent, and hence
X_{1}v,\ldots,X_{k}v
are linearly independent.
∎

The following result characterizes when there exists a vector
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
so that
\widehat{A}(I_{q}\otimes x)
has full row-rank. The proof is analogous to the proof of Theorem
4.2
, now relying on the second identity in (
2.6
) rather than the first, so it will be omitted.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
in (
3.1
) be a
*
-linear map with matricization
L
and Choi matrix
{\mathbb L}
. Set
m=\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}
, decompose
L
as in (
3.4
) and define
{\mathcal{W}}
as in (
3.6
). Then for any minimal Hill representation (
3.2
), for
\widehat{A}
defined as in Proposition
3.3
there exists a vector
x\in{\mathbb F}^{n}
such that
\widehat{A}(I_{q}\otimes x)
has full row-rank if and only if the subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
has the following property:

(C2)

\displaystyle\text{For any linearly independent }X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\text{ in }{\mathcal{W}},\text{ there exists a }v\in{\mathbb F}^{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{ such that }X_{1}^{*}v,\ldots,X_{k}^{*}v\text{ is linearly independent in }{\mathbb F}^{q}.

Hence, if
(C2)
holds, then
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
and positivity and complete positivity of
{\mathcal{L}}
coincide. Moreover, in that case
\dim{\mathcal{W}}\leq q
.

Although
{\mathcal{W}}
in (
3.6
) is the minimal subspace one can work with, sometimes it is convenient to identify a larger subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
so that
L\in{\overline{{\mathcal{V}}}}\otimes{\mathcal{V}}
, since
{\mathcal{V}}
may better capture the structural properties of
L
. In this case it is still sufficient that
{\mathcal{V}}
satisfies (C1) or (C2) to conclude that
{\mathfrak{Y}}_{\widehat{A}}={\mathbb F}^{m}
, but possibly not necessary. This claim will be proved in Theorem
4.11
below.

4.1.
Subspaces of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
satisfying condition (C1) or (C2)

Since subspaces
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
satisfying (C1) or (C2) are of particular importance to us, we further investigate such subspaces in this subsection. We start with a duality result between (C1) and (C2).

Lemma 4.4
.

A subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
satisfies condition
(C1)
if and only if
{\mathcal{W}}^{*}
satisfies
(C2)
.

Proof.

Assume
{\mathcal{W}}
satisfies property (C1). Let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\in{\mathcal{W}}^{*}
be linearly independent. Then
Y_{1}^{*},\ldots,Y_{k}^{*}
are in
{\mathcal{W}}
and are also linearly independent. Hence there exists a vector
v\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
so that
Y_{1}^{*}v,\ldots,Y_{k}^{*}v
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
. This shows that
{\mathcal{W}}^{*}
satisfies (C2). The converse implication follows by a similar argument.
∎

Because of this duality relation between properties (C1) and (C2) we shall prove our results only for property (C1) and list the analogous results for property (C2), in Lemma
4.9
below.

Lemma 4.5
.

A subspace
{\mathcal{W}}
of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
satisfies condition
(C1)
if and only if
{\overline{{\mathcal{W}}}}
satisfies
(C1)
.

Proof.

Since linearly independence of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
is equivalent to
{\overline{X}}_{1},\ldots,{\overline{X}}_{k}
being linearly independent, and the same is true for
X_{1}v,\ldots,X_{k}v
and
{\overline{X}}_{1}{\overline{v}},\ldots,{\overline{X}}_{k}{\overline{v}}
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{W}}
is a subspace of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
that satisfies the condition
(C1)
. Then
P{\mathcal{W}}Q
also satisfies
(C1)
for any
P\in\textup{GL}(n,{\mathbb F})
and
Q\in\textup{GL}(q,{\mathbb F})
.

Proof.

Take any linearly independent
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
in
P{\mathcal{W}}Q
. Then
X_{i}=PY_{i}Q
, where
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}
are linearly independent matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}
. Hence there exists a vector
v\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that
Y_{1}v,\ldots,Y_{k}v
are linearly independent vectors in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
. Define
u:=Q^{-1}v\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
and note that for all
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\in{\mathbb F}
we have

P\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}Y_{i}v\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}PY_{i}v=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}Q^{-1}v=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}u.

The invertibility of
P
together with the linear independence of
Y_{1}v,\ldots,Y_{k}v
implies that
X_{1}u,\ldots,X_{k}u
are linearly independent. Hence
P{\mathcal{W}}Q
satisfies (C1).
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{W}}
is subspace of
{\mathbb F}^{n\times q}
that satisfies condition
(C1)
. Then any subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
of
{\mathcal{W}}
also satisfies
(C1)
.

Proof.

The claim follows because any linearly independent
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
in
{\mathcal{V}}
are also linearly independent matrices in
{\mathcal{W}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
is a subspace of
{\mathbb F}^{n_{1}\times q_{1}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
is a subspace of
{\mathbb F}^{n_{2}\times q_{2}}
, that both satisfy the condition
(C1)
. Then
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
in
{\mathbb F}^{n_{1}+n_{2}\times q_{1}+q_{2}}
also satisfies the condition
(C1)
.

Proof.

Take any linearly independent
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
in
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
, where
X_{i}=Y_{i}\oplus Z_{i}
with
Y_{i}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and
Z_{i}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. Write
k_{1}+k_{2}=k
with
k_{1}=\textup{dim span}\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k}\}
. Reorder the set
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
so that
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k_{1}}
are linearly independent in
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and hence we can write
Y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha_{j}^{i}Y_{j}
with
\alpha_{j}^{i}\in\mathbb{F}
for all
i
. Let
n:=n_{1}+n_{2}
,
q:=q_{1}+q_{2}
and

X:=\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times kq}.

Decompose
X
as

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y&{\widetilde{Y}}\\
Z&{\widetilde{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{where}\quad Y:=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&Y_{k_{1}}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{n_{1}\times k_{1}q},

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Y}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{k_{1}+1}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&Y_{k}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{n_{1}\times k_{2}q},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&Z_{1}&\ldots&0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&Z_{k_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{n_{2}\times k_{1}q}\quad\mbox{and}\quad

\displaystyle{\widetilde{Z}}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&Z_{k_{1}+1}&\ldots&0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&Z_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{n_{2}\times k_{2}q}.

Define

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle:=-\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{k_{1}+1}&\ldots&\alpha_{1}^{k}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k_{1}+1}&\ldots&\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{k_{1}\times k_{2}},\quad\widehat{U}:=\begin{bmatrix}I_{k_{1}}&T\\
0_{k_{2}\times k_{1}}&I_{k_{2}}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb F}^{k\times k}

and

U:=\left(\widehat{U}\otimes I_{q}\right)\in{\mathbb F}^{kq\times kq}.

Then

\displaystyle XU

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y&{\widetilde{Y}}\\
Z&{\widetilde{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{k_{1}q}&T\otimes I_{q}\\
0_{k_{2}q\times k_{1}q}&I_{k_{2}q}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Y&Y\left(T\otimes I_{q}\right)+{\widetilde{Y}}\\
Z&Z\left(T\otimes I_{q}\right)+{\widetilde{Z}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Y&0_{n_{1}\times k_{2}q}\\
Z&{\widehat{Z}}\end{bmatrix},

where

\widehat{Z}:=Z\left(T\otimes I_{q}\right)+\widetilde{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&\widehat{Z}_{1}&\ldots&0_{n_{2}\times q_{1}}&\widehat{Z}_{k_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

with
\widehat{Z}_{l}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
for
l=1,\ldots,k_{2}.
Indeed, the
0
entry in the right upper corner follows since

\displaystyle-Y\left(T\otimes I_{q}\right)=Y\left(-T\otimes I_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&Y_{k_{1}}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{k_{1}+1}I_{q_{1}}&0_{q_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&\alpha_{1}^{k}I_{q_{1}}&0_{q_{1}\times q_{2}}\\
0_{q_{2}\times q_{1}}&\alpha_{1}^{k_{1}+1}I_{q_{2}}&\ldots&0_{q_{2}\times q_{1}}&\alpha_{1}^{k}I_{q_{2}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k_{1}+1}I_{q_{1}}&0_{q_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k}I_{q_{1}}&0_{q_{1}\times q_{2}}\\
0_{q_{2}\times q_{1}}&\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k_{1}+1}I_{q_{2}}&\ldots&0_{q_{2}\times q_{1}}&\alpha_{k_{1}}^{k}I_{q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha_{j}^{k_{1}+1}Y_{j}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&\sum_{j=1}^{k_{1}}\alpha_{j}^{k}Y_{j}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{k_{1}+1}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}&\ldots&Y_{k}&0_{n_{1}\times q_{2}}\end{bmatrix}={\widetilde{Y}}.

Let
\widetilde{X}:=XU,
with
\widetilde{X}
decomposed as
\widetilde{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{X}_{1}&\ldots&\widetilde{X}_{k}\end{bmatrix}
. Since
U
is invertible and
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
are linearly independent, it follows that
\widetilde{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{X}_{k}
are linearly independent. This can only be the case if
\widehat{Z}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{Z}_{k_{2}}
are linearly independent in
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. Now we make use of the fact that there exists a vector
v\in{\mathbb F}^{q_{1}}
and
w\in{\mathbb F}^{q_{2}}
such that
Y_{1}v,\ldots,Y_{k_{1}}v
form a linearly independent set in
{\mathbb F}^{n_{1}}
and
\widehat{Z}_{1}w,\ldots,\widehat{Z}_{k_{2}}w
form a linearly independent set in
{\mathbb F}^{n_{2}}.
Let
\widetilde{u}:=\begin{bmatrix}v^{T}&w^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
. Then

\displaystyle\widetilde{X}\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y&0_{n_{1}\times k_{2}q}\\
Z&{\widehat{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}Y&0_{n_{1}\times k_{2}q}\\
Z&{\widehat{Z}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I_{k_{1}}\otimes\widetilde{u}&0_{k_{1}q\times k_{2}}\\
0_{k_{2}q\times k_{1}}&I_{k_{2}}\otimes\widetilde{u}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Y\left(I_{k_{1}}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)&0_{n_{1}\times k_{2}}\\
Z\left(I_{k_{1}}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)&{\widehat{Z}}\left(I_{k_{2}}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}v&\ldots&Y_{k_{1}}v\end{bmatrix}&0_{n_{1}\times k_{2}}\\
\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}w&\ldots&Z_{k_{1}}w\end{bmatrix}&\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{Z}_{1}w&\ldots&\widehat{Z}_{k_{2}}w\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

Hence there exists a vector
\widetilde{u}\in{\mathbb F}^{q}
such that

\textup{rank\,}\widetilde{X}\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=k,

since

\displaystyle\textup{rank\,}\widetilde{X}\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\textup{rank\,}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}v&\ldots&Y_{k_{1}}v\end{bmatrix}+\textup{rank\,}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{Z}_{1}w&\ldots&\widehat{Z}_{k_{2}}w\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=k_{1}+k_{2}=k.

Now from

\widetilde{X}\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=XU\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=X\left(\widehat{U}\otimes I_{q}\right)\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=X\left(\widehat{U}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=X\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)\widehat{U},

and the fact that
\widehat{U}
is invertible it follows that

\textup{rank\,}X\left(I_{k}\otimes\widetilde{u}\right)=\textup{rank\,}\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}\widetilde{u}&\ldots&X_{k}\widetilde{u}\end{bmatrix}=k.

This proves that there exists a vector
\widetilde{u}
such that
X_{1}\widetilde{u},\ldots,X_{k}\widetilde{u}
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb F}^{n}
. Hence
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\oplus{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
satisfy
(C1)
whenever both
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
satisfy
(C1).

∎

Via the duality relation between properties (C1) and (C2) obtained in Lemma

, the following result follows directly from Lemmas
4.6
–
4.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}
in (

) be a

-linear map with matricization

satisfies
(C1)
(resp.
(C2)
). Then for any minimal Hill representation (

), for

defined as in Proposition

4.2.
The bicommutant of a matrix satisfies (C1) and (C2)

Our main result in this subsection is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.13
.

For any matrix
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
the algebra
\left\{A\right\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
satisfies both conditions
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

To prove this result we first prove the claim for some special case.

Lemma 4.14
.

The matrix algebras
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}
and
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{+}
both satisfy conditions
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Proof.

We prove the
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}
satisfies (C1) by showing we can always take
v=e_{1}
. Note that
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}=\{S_{n}^{T}\}^{\prime\prime}_{{\mathbb F}}
is commutative. Take any linearly independent
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}
. Suppose

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{1}=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb F}\quad\text{with}\quad i=1,\ldots,k.

Then for
j=0,\ldots,n-1
we have

0=\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}X_{i}e_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}e_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{j+1}

Hence all columns of the matrix
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}
are zero, so that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}=0
. The linear independence of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
then implies that
\alpha_{1}=\cdots=\alpha_{k}=0
. Hence
X_{1}e_{1},\ldots,X_{k}e_{1}
are linearly independent, and thus
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}
satisfies (C1).

The proof of (C2) goes analogously, with the required vector being
e_{n}\in\mathbb{F}
. The claims for
{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{n,{\mathbb F}}
follow by the duality result of Lemma
4.4
since
({\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{n,{\mathbb F}})^{*}={\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{n,{\mathbb F}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.15
.

The matrix algebras
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-}
and
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{+}
both satisfy conditions
(C1)
and
(C2)
.

Proof.

For the proof of (C1) we make use of the fact that
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-}
is a commutative algebra. By Lemma
2.2
we know
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-}={\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathbb F}}^{-}\otimes{\mathfrak{C}}
and hence
\left(\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{l}\otimes I_{2}\right)\in{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-}
for all
l=0,\ldots,n-1
. Take any linearly independent
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
in
{\mathfrak{T}}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}^{-}
.
Suppose

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{1}^{(2n)}=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha_{i}\in{\mathbb F}\quad\text{with}\quad i=1,\ldots,k.

Then also

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}\otimes I_{2}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{1}^{(2n)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\left(\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}\otimes I_{2}\right)X_{i}\left(e_{1}^{(n)}\otimes e_{1}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}\left(\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{j}\otimes I_{2}\right)\left(e_{1}^{(n)}\otimes e_{1}^{(2)}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}\left(e_{j+1}^{(n)}\otimes e_{1}^{(2)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}e_{2j+1}^{(2n)}\quad\text{for}\quad j=0,\ldots,n-1.

Hence all columns of
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}
with indices
2j+1
are zero. Now because of the relation that exists between the entries of column
2j+1
and
2j+2
, for all
j=0,\ldots,n-1
, it follows that all columns with indices
2j+2
are also zero.
Therefore
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}X_{i}=0
, which can only be if
\alpha_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
, since
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
are linearly independent. This proves that for the basis vector
e_{1}^{(2n)}\in{\mathbb F}^{2n}
, the vectors
X_{1}e_{1}^{(2n)},\ldots,X_{k}e_{1}^{(2n)}
are linearly independent in
{\mathbb F}^{2n}
, therefore
{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}
satisfies (C1).

The proof for (C2) goes analogously, with the required vector being the basis vector
e_{2n-1}^{(2n)}\in{\mathbb F}^{2n}.
The claims for
{\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}
again follow by the duality result of Lemma
4.4
and the fact that
({\mathfrak{T}}^{+}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}})^{*}={\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{n,{\mathfrak{C}}}

∎

Now we prove Theorem
4.13
, first for
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}
and
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb R}
separately.

A Hill-Pick matrix criterium for the Lyapunov order

In this section we return to the Lyapunov order. We prove Theorem
1.1
and use this result together with Hill representations to determine an explicit matrix criterium for Lyapunov domination.

Throughout this section let
A,B\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
with

Lyapunov regular and

Lemma 5.1

The matricization

and Choi matrix

for

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbb L}_{A,B}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{-1}\right)^{*}{\mathbb L}_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{-1}\right).

Here

and

defined as in (

Proof.

L_{A}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{J_{A}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right),\ \ L_{B}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right).

This gives

\displaystyle L_{A,B}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)\left(\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{J_{A}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{\widetilde{B}}L_{J_{A}}^{-1}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right).

Next we compute

Proposition 5.2
.

For
A\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}
Lyapunov regular, with Jordan decomposition as in (

) and

as in (
5.3

L_{A,B}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}\textup{diag}\left(R_{1},...,R_{r}\right)\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right),

where

R_{j}=\textup{diag}\left(R_{j,1},...,R_{j,k_{j}}\right)\in{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{{\underline{n}}_{j},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}},

with

(5.6)

\displaystyle R_{j,q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes F_{j,i}\in{\mathbb C}^{nn_{j,q}\times nn_{j,q}}

and

(5.7)

\displaystyle F_{j,i}=\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}D_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}\in\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime};

here
D_{j,0}=t_{j,0}I_{n}+\widetilde{B}^{*}
and
D_{j,l}=t_{j,l}I_{n}
for

Moreover, the matrix
F_{j,i}
can be computed explicitly as

(5.8)

F_{j,i}=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}f_{j,i}^{a,c}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}

where

(5.9)

f_{j,i}^{a,c}=\sum_{d=0}^{c}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d+i}{\overline{t}}_{a,c-d}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}+\sum_{l=0}^{i}\binom{c+i-l}{c}\frac{(-1)^{i-l+c}t_{j,l}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{c+i-l+1}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.1
we have

L_{A,B}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right),\quad\mbox{with}\quad L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}=L_{\widetilde{B}}L_{J_{A}}^{-1},

where

L_{J_{A}}=J_{A}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{\widetilde{B}}=\widetilde{B}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}.

Note that the matrix
L_{J_{A}}
can be written as

\displaystyle L_{J_{A}}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}\left(J_{{\underline{n}}_{1}}\left(\lambda_{1}\right)^{T},\ldots,J_{{\underline{n}}_{r}}\left(\lambda_{r}\right)^{T}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes J_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\left(J_{{\underline{n}}_{j}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\right).

Similarly the matrix
L_{\widetilde{B}}
can be written as

\displaystyle L_{\widetilde{B}}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}\left(T_{1}^{T},...,T_{r}^{T}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\left(T_{j}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}\right).

This gives

L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}=L_{\widetilde{B}}L_{J_{A}}^{-1}=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\left(T_{j}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}\right)\left(J_{{\underline{n}}_{j}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-1}.

For
j=1,\ldots,r
it follows that

\displaystyle J_{{\underline{n}}_{j}}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}\left(J_{n_{j,1}}^{T}(\lambda_{j}),...,J_{n_{j,k_{j}}}^{T}(\lambda_{j})\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{q=1}^{k_{j}}\left(J_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\right),

and

\displaystyle T_{j}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}\left(T_{j,1}^{T},...,T_{j,k_{j}}^{T}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{q=1}^{k_{j}}\left(T_{j,q}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}\right).

For
j=1,\ldots,r
and
q=1,\ldots,k_{j}
it follows that

\displaystyle T_{j,q}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}t_{j,i}\left(S^{T}_{n_{j,q}}\right)^{i}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\widetilde{B}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes t_{j,i}I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\left(t_{j,0}I_{n}+\widetilde{B}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes D_{j,i},

with
D_{j,0}
and
D_{j,i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n_{j,q}-1
, as defined in the proposition.
Furthermore, for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
q=1,\ldots,k_{j}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left(J_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}(-1)^{l}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{l}\otimes\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-l-1}.

Thus for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
q=1,\ldots,k_{j}
we have

\displaystyle\left(T_{j,q}^{T}\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes\tilde{B}^{*}\right)\left(J_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)\otimes I_{n}+I_{n_{j,q}}\otimes J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes D_{j,i}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}(-1)^{l}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{l}\otimes\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-l-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}(-1)^{l}\left(\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes D_{j,i}\right)\left(\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{l}\otimes\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-l-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}(-1)^{l}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{l}\otimes D_{j,i}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-l-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}(-1)^{l}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i+l}\otimes D_{j,i}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-l-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}D_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}=R_{j,q}.

Hence

L_{A,B}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{q=1}^{k_{j}}R_{j,q}\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right).

Since
\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}=\textup{diag}\left({\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{1},{\mathbb C}}^{-},\dots,{\mathfrak{T}}_{{\underline{n}}_{r},{\mathbb C}}^{-}\right)
is an algebra which is closed under inversion and conjugation, i.e.,
\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}=\{J_{A}^{*}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}
, it follows that for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
i=0,\ldots,n_{j,q}-1
:

F_{j,i}=\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}D_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}\in\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}

From the fact that
F_{j,i}
does not depend on
q,

we see that

R_{j,q}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,q}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,q}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes F_{j,i}

is the
nn_{j,q}\times nn_{j,q}
left upper corner of

R_{j,1}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{j,1}-1}\left(S_{n_{j,1}}^{T}\right)^{i}\otimes F_{j,i},

for
j=1,\ldots,r
and
q=1,\ldots,k_{j}
. Taking all of the above into consideration gives

L_{A,B}=\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right)^{-1}\textup{diag}\left(R_{1},...,R_{r}\right)\left(P^{T}\otimes P^{*}\right),

with
R_{j}
as defined in the proposition.

It remains to determine the explicit formula for
F_{j,i}
. Note first that

\displaystyle F_{j,i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}D_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}D_{j,0}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-i-1}+\sum_{l=1}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}D_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\left(t_{j,0}I_{n}+\widetilde{B}^{*}\right)\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-i-1}+\sum_{l=1}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}t_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}

(5.10)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\widetilde{B}^{*}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-i-1}+\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}t_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1},

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}^{*}=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}T^{*}_{a,b}=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}{\overline{t}}_{a,c}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}.

Furthermore, by making use of the formula for powers of the inverse of a Jordan block, cf.,
[
24
, Page 70]
, it follows for
i=0,\ldots,n_{j,q}-1
that

\displaystyle\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-i-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}J_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)\right)^{-i-1}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\left(J_{n_{a,b}}\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{-i-1}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\left(\sum_{d=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}S_{n_{a,b}}^{d}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{d=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{d}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{i}\widetilde{B}^{*}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{-i-1}=

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\left(\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}{\overline{t}}_{a,c}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}\right)\left(\sum_{d=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d+i}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{d}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d+i}{\overline{t}}_{a,c}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c+d}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\sum_{d=0}^{c}\binom{d+i}{d}\frac{(-1)^{d+i}{\overline{t}}_{a,c-d}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{d+i+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-l}t_{j,l}\left(\lambda_{j}I_{n}+J_{A}^{*}\right)^{l-i-1}=

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{l=0}^{i}t_{j,l}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\binom{c+i-l}{c}\frac{(-1)^{i-l+c}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{c+i-l+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigoplus_{a=1}^{r}\bigoplus_{b=1}^{k_{a}}\sum_{c=0}^{n_{a,b}-1}\sum_{l=0}^{i}\binom{c+i-l}{c}\frac{(-1)^{i-l+c}t_{j,l}}{\left(\lambda_{j}+{\overline{\lambda}}_{a}\right)^{c+i-l+1}}\left(S_{n_{a,b}}^{T}\right)^{c}.

Inserting the end results of the last two computations into (

5.2.
Computing
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
for
{\mathbb F}={\mathbb C}

The Hill matrix of the
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
depends on a choice of matrices
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
so that their span corresponds to the span of the block entries of the matricization
L_{A,B}
. Via the relation between
L_{A,B}
and
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
obtained in Lemma
5.1
, where
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
is the matricization of
{\mathcal{L}}_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
, it follows that we just as well make a selection of the block entries of
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
, with the
L_{1},\ldots,L_{m}
being determined by (
5.5
). Using the Jordan structure of
A
given by (
5.2
), for
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
there is a natural choice of block entries to select, based on the formula in (
2.13
), and it is the Hill matrix with respect to this choice that we will call the Hill-Pick matrix, denoted
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
, associated with
A
and
B
. This selection of the block entries of
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
consists of
w=n_{1,1}+\cdots+n_{r,1}
entries and corresponds to the selection of indices given by

\displaystyle\Upsilon=

\displaystyle\left\{(1,1),\ldots,(n_{1,1},1),(n_{1}+1,n_{1}+1),\ldots,(n_{1}+n_{2,1},n_{1}+1),\ldots\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\ldots,(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r-1}+1,n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r-1}+1),\ldots\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.\ldots(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r-1}+n_{r,1},n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r-1}+1)\right\},

where for
j=1,\ldots,r
we define
n_{j}=n_{j,1}+\cdots+n_{j,k_{j}}
. In the “Toeplitz structure with possible repetition” of
\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}
as described in (
2.13
), in which
L_{J_{A},\widetilde{B}}
is included, the
j
-th set of indices, running from
(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j-1}+1,n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j-1}+1)
to
(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j-1}+n_{j,1},n_{1}+\cdots+n_{j-1}+1)
, corresponds to taking the matrices in the first column of each of the lower triangular block Toeplitz matrices with possible repetition coming from
{\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{{\underline{n}}_{j},{\mathbb C}}\otimes\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}={\mathfrak{T}}^{-}_{{\underline{n}}_{j},\{J_{A}^{T}\}_{{\mathbb C}}^{\prime\prime}}
.

We note here that the number
w
may be larger than the rank of the Choi matrix,
m=\textup{rank\,}{\mathbb L}_{A,B}
, so that
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
may be singular. Our results on non-minimal Hill representations in Subsection
3.3
, in particular Theorem
3.10
, show that even in this case positive semidefiniteness of
{\mathbb H}_{A,B}
remains a necessary and sufficient criterium to check whether
{\mathcal{L}}_{A,B}
is (completely) positive, and hence whether
B
Lyapunov dominates
A
.

For the matrix representation of

and

Now note that the

Let

Example 5.4
.

Let

For
L_{A,B}
it follows that

\displaystyle L_{A,B}

\displaystyle=\textup{diag}\left(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad L_{i}=\textup{diag}\left(\ell_{i,1},\ldots,\ell_{i,n}\right)\in{\mathbb C}^{n\times n}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\ell_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\frac{{\overline{t}}_{j}+t_{i}}{{\overline{\lambda}}_{j}+\lambda_{i}}\quad\text{ for all }i,j=1,\ldots,n.

In case

with

Next we consider the case of a single Jordan block.

\displaystyle L_{A,B}

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{l=0}^{i}\binom{c+i-l}{c}\frac{(-1)^{i-l+c}t_{l}}{\left(\lambda+{\overline{\lambda}}\right)^{c+i-l+1}}\right)\left(S_{n}^{T}\right)^{c}.

The case

When

Let the Lyapunov regular

5.4.
The Stein order

As mentioned in the introduction, there is an analogous matrix order associated with Nevanlinna-Pick interpolation in the unit disk, based on the Stein inequality; cf., Remark 2.6 in
[
3
]
. For
A\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
, the Stein inequality asks for a
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
so that
H-AHA^{*}\in{\overline{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{n}
. Associated with this is the
*
-linear map
{\mathcal{L}}_{A}(X)=X-AXA^{*}
, which has a matricization
L_{A}=I_{n^{2}}-{\overline{A}}\otimes A
and is invertible whenever the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
of
A
satisfy
\lambda_{i}{\overline{\lambda}}_{j}\neq 0
,
i,j=1\ldots,n
. For
A,B\in{\mathbb F}^{n\times n}
so that
{\mathcal{L}}_{A}
is invertible, one then says that
B
Stein dominates
A
if each Stein solution
H\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}
of
A
is also a Stein solution of
B
, or equivalently,
{\mathcal{L}}_{B}{\mathcal{L}}_{A}^{-1}
is a positive map. If one further restricts to
B\in\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
, reasoning as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
it follows that
L_{A}
,
L_{B}
,
L_{A}^{-1}
and consequently
L_{B}L_{A}^{-1}
are all in
{\overline{\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}}}\otimes\{A\}_{{\mathbb F}}^{\prime\prime}
, leading to the observation that
{\mathcal{L}}_{B}{\mathcal{L}}_{A}^{-1}
is a positive map precisely when it is completely positive. Hence, checking positive semidefiniteness of the corresponding Choi matrix provides as necessary and sufficient criteria for Stein domination and one can further employ Hill representations to determine a more efficient matrix criteria, which will correspond to the classical Pick matrix criteria in the case of the diagonal matrices.
