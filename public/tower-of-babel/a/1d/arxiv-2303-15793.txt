Black hole as spherically-symmetric horizon-bound objects
Pravin K. Dahal, Fil Simovic, Ioannis Soranidis, Daniel R. Terno

Abstract
Working in a semi-classical setting, we consider solutions of the Einstein equations that exhibit light trapping in finite time according to distant observers. In spherical symmetry, we construct near-horizon quantities from the assumption of regularity of the renormalized expectation value of the energy-momentum tensor, and derive explicit coordinate transformations in the near-horizon region. We examine the boundary conditions appropriate for embedding the model into a cosmological background, describe their evaporation in the linear regime and highlight the observational consequences, while also discussing the implications for the laws of black hole mechanics.

II.3
Linear mass loss in Vaidya metric: coordinate transformations

Different aspects of the black hole geometry are best captured by different coordinate systems. However, transformations between them are difficult

, and where exact coordinate transformations do exist, multiple coordinate patches are required to cover the entire spacetime

A linear dependence of the MS mass on the null coordinate
v
or

;

;
DST:22
. Here we complement these works by providing an explicit analytic expression for the coordinate transformation from
(v,r)
to
(t,r)
coordinates.

For a slowly contracting horizon we have that
r_{+}^{\prime}\ll 1
, which holds for the emission of Hawking radiation by a macroscopic black hole and is a good approximation for a sufficiently long interval of
v
. In what follows we assume a linear evaporation law, such that

\displaystyle r_{+}(v)=r_{0}-\alpha v\ ,\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha>0\ ,

(47)

where
r_{0}
is the initial areal radius and
\alpha
is the evaporation rate. The timelike nature of the Schwarzschild sphere allows for arbitrary values of
\alpha
, but the previous considerations restrict it to
\alpha\ll 1
. While the extension of this metric to large distances

is not justified, it provides a setting in which the exact transformations to
(t,r)
and
(u,r)
coordinates are possible. Moreover, its counterpart with decreasing

provides a good description of an evaporating black hole at distances

and its transformation to
(t,r)
coordinates can be performed analogously.

The first step in the transformation to Schwarzschild coordinates is to bring the metric into a form that is conformally equivalent to the Schwarzschild metric in
(u,r)
coordinates. This is effected by defining

\displaystyle v=\vcentcolon\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\left(1-e^{-\alpha{\mathcal{V}}/r_{0}}\right),\qquad r=\vcentcolon Re^{-\alpha{\mathcal{V}}/r_{0}}\ ,

(48)

with the explicit form of the metric in
(\mathcal{V},R)
coordinates given in Appendix
A.3

Then, defining a time coordinate
\tilde{t}
by

\displaystyle d\tilde{t}=d\mathcal{V}-{b(R)}\left(1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\frac{2\alpha R}{r_{0}}\right)^{-1}dR\ ,

(49)

allows the metric to take the form

ds^{2}=\textsl{g}_{\tilde{t}\tilde{t}}d\tilde{t}^{2}+2\textsl{g}_{\tilde{t}r}d\tilde{t}dr+\textsl{g}_{rr}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega_{2}\ ,

(50)

where expressions for the metric components

are again given in Appendix
A.3

\cal{V}
and

\tilde{t}
and

is then chosen such that the off-diagonal metric component

b(R)=\left(1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\frac{2\alpha R}{r_{0}}\right)\left(1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\frac{\alpha R}{r_{0}}\right)^{-1}

(51)

As a result, the metric becomes

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-e^{-2\alpha\mathcal{V}/r_{0}}\frac{\left(1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\frac{\alpha R}{r_{0}}\right)^{2}}{1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}}d\tilde{t}^{2}+\frac{dr^{2}}{1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}}+r^{2}d\Omega_{2}\ .

(52)

Comparing (

2

\displaystyle f(\tilde{t},r)=1-\frac{C(\tilde{t},r)}{r}=1-\frac{r_{+}(v)}{r}=1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}\ ,

(53)

with

e^{\tilde{h}(\tilde{t},r)}=e^{-a{\mathcal{V}}/r_{0}}\left(\frac{1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}+\frac{\alpha R}{r_{0}}}{1-\frac{r_{0}}{R}}\right)\ .

(54)

From Eqs. (
48

49

\tilde{t}\big(r=r_{0},v=0\big)=0

\displaystyle\tilde{t}(v,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}}{2\alpha}\ln\left(\frac{\alpha r_{0}^{2}}{\alpha r^{2}-r_{+}^{2}+r_{+}r}\right)

(55)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha\sqrt{1+4\alpha}}\,\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+4\alpha}(r-r_{+})}{(1+2\alpha)r-r_{+}}\right)\ .

Note that it is still possible to apply an arbitrary coordinate transformation
\tilde{t}\to t=T(\tilde{t})

Using Eq. (

\displaystyle\to\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\left[-\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_{0}}\right)+\gamma\right]\ ,

where we have defined

\displaystyle\gamma:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4\alpha}}\,\mathrm{arctanh}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+4\alpha}}{1+2\alpha}\right)\ .

(57)

Similarly we find that

\tilde{h}\to\ln\left(\frac{\alpha r}{r_{0}}\right)\ .

(58)

(61)

Noting that

(64)

III
Physical black holes in cosmology

Models of compact objects with cosmological boundary conditions have been investigated since the introduction of
the McVittie metric
Mv:933
, which generalizes the Schwarzschild solution to
arbitrary FRLW spacetimes. In isotropic coordinates
(t,\bar{r})
the McVitte metric has the form

\displaystyle ds^{2}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\left(1-m(t)/2\bar{r}\right)^{2}}{\left(1+m(t)/2\bar{r}\right)^{2}}dt^{2}

\displaystyle+a^{2}(t)\left(1+\frac{m(t)}{2\bar{r}}\right)^{4}\left(d\bar{r}^{2}+\bar{r}^{2}d\Omega_{2}\right),

(65)

where the time dependence of the mass function is governed by the scale factor
a(t)
such that

m(t)\equiv m_{0}/a(t)\ .

(66)

Due to the non-linearity of the Einstein equations, it is impossible to split a metric into a homogenous and isotropic cosmological background and a part describing even a spherical inhomogeneity. It is possible, however, to loosely describe the “embedding” of black holes into a cosmological “background” if the metric reduces to a FRLW metric when the parameter that describes the inhomogeneity vanishes
vF:15
. Despite the existence of numerous models with quite remarkable properties
vF:15
;
FGF:21
, there currently exist no MBH solutions that satisfy observational constraints at the horizon scale while approaching an FRLW metric on the largest scales
FCZT:23
.

We begin the procedure of embedding a PBH into a spatially flat FRLW background by writing its metric in the form of Eq. (
2
)
vF:15
. In comoving coordinates
(\bar{t},\chi)
one has

ds^{2}=-d\bar{t}^{2}+a^{2}(\bar{t})\left(d\chi^{2}+\chi^{2}d\Omega_{2}\right)\ ,

(67)

while using the areal radius

as the radial coordinate brings the metric into Painlevé-Gullstrand form

ds^{2}=-\left(1-H^{2}r^{2}\right)d\bar{t}^{2}-2Hrd\bar{t}dr+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega_{2}\ ,

(68)

where
H=\dot{a}/a
is the Hubble parameter.
The cross term can be eliminated and the metric can be written in Schwarzschild form by introducing a new time coordinate
t
for which

dt=\frac{1}{F}\left(d\bar{t}+\beta dr\right)\ ,

(69)

where
F(t,r)
is an integration factor satisfying

\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{1}{F}\right)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{t}}\left(\frac{\beta}{F}\right),

(70)

and the function
\beta
is chosen so that
\textsl{g}_{tr}=0
. This is accomplished by having

\beta=\frac{Hr}{1-H^{2}r^{2}}\ ,

(71)

which results in the line element

ds^{2}=-\left(1-H^{2}r^{2}\right)F^{2}dt^{2}+\frac{1}{1-H^{2}r^{2}}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega_{2}\ .

(72)

This is the spatially flat FLRW metric in Schwarzschild coordinates. De Sitter space is the special case where
H\equiv\text{const.}
and
F\equiv 1
.

The above result easily follows from Eqs. (
8
)–(
10
), where we set

T_{\mu\nu}=T_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{mat}}-\Lambda\textsl{g}_{\mu\nu}/8\pi\ ,

(73)

separating the EMT into the matter and the cosmological vacuum parts, respectively. Then Eqs. (
9
) and (
10

\tau_{a}\to\tau_{a}^{\mathrm{mat}}
, while Eq. (
8
) takes the form

{\partial}_{r}C=8\pi\tau^{\mathrm{mat}}_{t}/f+\Lambda r^{2}\ .

(74)

Setting
\tau_{a}=0
then results in Eq. (
72
) with
F=1
and
H=\sqrt{\Lambda/3}
. The cosmological constant does not affect the structure of the function
C(t,r)
, but the Schwarzschild radius is modified as

r_{\textsl{g}}\to r_{\textsl{g}}(1+H^{2}r_{\textsl{g}}^{2}/3)+\mathcal{O}(H^{4})\ ,

(75)

while the expansions of the metric functions
C
and
h

In the case of a spatially flat de Sitter space, the advanced and retarded null coordinates become generalizations of the Eddington–Finkelsein coordinates. They can be defined analogously to the Schwarzschild spacetime. For example, using the advanced null coordinate

It is natural to consider Vaidya black holes, both using retarded
M:85

or

\displaystyle f(v,r)=1-\frac{2m(v)}{r}-H^{2}r^{2}\ ,

In a cosmological setting,

IV
Discussion

Our results show that for an uncharged physical black hole, compliance with the first law of black hole mechanics results in the coincidence of the Hayward–Kodama surface gravity with its Schwarzschild black hole value
\kappa_{\mathrm{K}}=1/(2r_{\textsl{g}})=1/4M
, and implies that the metric near the outer apparent horizon is approximately Vaidya (that
w_{1}=0
). For a charged black hole it is possible to match the surface gravity with that of the Reissner–Nordström black hole,

\kappa=\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{2r_{+}^{2}}\ ,

(83)

where
r_{+}
and
r_{-}
are the areal radii of the outer and the inner horizons, respectively, by having
w_{1}\neq 0

MS:23
.
While it is obvious that Page’s law can be maintained in both
(t,r)
and
(v,r)
coordinate systems in identical form, it is unclear if this is compatible with the redefinition of the null coordinate
v
required to have
\zeta_{0}=0
.

We also have seen that a simple and pathology-free PBH model in asymptotically de Sitter space does not adhere to the ordinary form of the first law. This can be seen as a natural consequence of the inclusion of back-reaction in our model. In asymptotically de Sitter black hole spacetimes, it is known that a first law can be formulated separately for the event and cosmological horizons
hawking77
. However, if back-reaction from the Hawking flux of each horizon is not ignored, the heat flux between the two horizons places the system out of equilibrium and the first law no longer suffices to capture variations between nearby equilibrium states for the entire spacetime. This back-reaction issue (along with ambiguities in the definition of mass in de Sitter spacetimes
dolan13
) makes formulating the laws of black hole mechanics in de Sitter technically and conceptually challenging, though a number of solutions have been extensively pursued
urano09
;
sim16
;
sim19
. While it may be superfluous, we stress that thermodynamic considerations for non-equilibrium systems without backing from microscopic calculations or a non-equilibrium framework should be treated with care.

Modelling black holes as PBHs results in a number of important peculiarities. The NEC violation (and existence of a macroscopic domain with negative energy density) is a necessary consequence of the formation of a trapped region in finite time according to a distant observer. This property is shared with many ECO models. However, spherically-symmetric PBHs that purport to model zero angular momentum ABHs do not allow growth; only a solution with decreasing
r_{\textsl{g}}(t)
is possible. Thus we conclude with the following chain of conditional statements: spherically-symmetric ABHs do not grow, or if they do then either they are horizonless objects or semiclassical gravity breaks down at the horizon scale.

It remains to be understood how the near-horizon EMT is compatible with current cosmological observations, such as those reported in Ref.
FCZT:23
, and if/how this EMT can be generated by relatively weak (at macroscopic scales) quantum effects
FN:98
;
M:15
. Moreover, it is unclear how the presence of regions with negative energy density and/or pressure is compatible with absorption of the cosmic microwave background radiation.

While dealing with axially-symmetric PBHs is much more difficult
DT:20
, the investigation of their embedding in a cosmological background is very important. In forthcoming work, we will detail the embedding of Kerr–Vaidya metrics in asymptotically de Sitter spacetimes. These models will serve as a basis for developing even more sophisticated descriptions of dynamical physical black holes, and provide a framework for extracting observational features of their astrophysical manifestations. These issues will be addressed in part II and III of this series.
