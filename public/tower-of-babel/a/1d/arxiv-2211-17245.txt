Abstract
. In decision making under uncertainty and risk, worst-case risk assessments are often conducted using maxitive monetary risk measures. In this article, we study maxitive monetary risk measures on the space L 0 L^{0} of all random variables identified modulo almost sure equality. We prove that a monetary risk measure is maxitive and continuous from below if and only if it is a penalized maximum loss. Furthermore, we characterize the maximum loss as the unique maxitive and law-invariant monetary risk measure. We apply the results to large deviation theory by providing a general criterion to establish a sharp large deviation estimate for sequences of probability measures. We use these findings to provide a formula for the asymptotics of the distortion-exponential insurance premium principle under risk pooling. 1.
Introduction

In decision making under uncertainty and risk, a monetary risk measure is a monotone and translation invariant function
\phi
defined on a vector space
E
of functions, containing the constant functions, mapping to the extended real numbers. Here, each
f\in E
represents the losses of a financial position (negative losses are interpreted as gains), and
\phi(f)
indicates the additional capital required to make the position
f
acceptable. For an introduction to risk measures, we refer to Föllmer and Schied
[
9
]
.

Often, a monetary risk measure
\phi
takes the form of an expectation, where
\phi(f)
represents the weighted average of the losses associated with the financial position
f
. However, risk management decisions frequently rely on assessments of worst-case scenarios rather than averages. These assessments are performed using maxitive monetary risk measures. A monetary risk measure
\phi
is said to be maxitive if
\phi(f\vee g)\leq\phi(f)\vee\phi(g)
for any
f,g\in E
, where
f\vee g
denotes the pointwise supremum of
f
and
g
. The use of maxitive monetary risk measures in risk analysis is explained in more detail in Section
3
below. A discussion on maxitive decision functions under uncertainty and risk can be also found in
[
5
]
.
A basic example of maxitive monetary risk measure is the
maximum loss

{\rm ML}(f):={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f)
, which gives the worst possible outcome.
Another example of maxitive monetary risk measure is the
penalized maximum loss
, which is defined as
{\rm ML}_{I}(f):={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f-I)
, where
I\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is a measurable penalty function that penalizes certain outcomes, reflecting additional incomes, capital reserves, or other loss mitigants across different scenarios.

The class of maxitive monetary risk measures on the space
C_{b}(X)
has been studied in
[
13
]
. The aim of this paper is to drop the topological and continuity assumptions in
[
13
]
and establish different representation results for maxitive risk measures on the space
L^{0}
of all real-valued measurable functions on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{X})
, identified modulo
\mu
-almost sure equality for a given probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
. We establish that a monetary risk measure
\phi\colon L^{0}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is maxitive and continuous from below on
L^{\infty}
if and only if there exists a penalty
I
such that
\phi(f)={\rm ML}_{I}(f)
for all
f\in L^{\infty}
.
Furthermore, under some regularity conditions on the underlying probability space
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
, we show that a monetary risk measure
\phi
is maxitive and law-invariant if, and only if,
\phi(f)={\rm ML}(f)
.
Moreover, if
\phi
is maxitive on the entire space
L^{0}
, the aforementioned representations extend to functions
f\in L^{0}
that are not necessarily bounded, provided that
\phi(tf)<\infty
for some
t>1
.
Additionally, we complement these findings by establishing local conditions on
\phi
that ensure the representation as a penalized maximum loss.

Furthermore, by giving a different interpretation of our main results, we explore other applications to large deviation theory and the limit theory of risk measures:

Sharp large deviation estimates

Originating from ruin theory, large deviation theory covers the asymptotic tail behaviour of sequences of probability distributions, see
[
8
]
and references therein.
Consider a sequence

(1.1)

\phi(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n},

shares the properties of a maxitive monetary risk measure

verifies the Laplace principle

\phi(f)=\sup_{x\in X}(f(x)-I(x))\quad\text{ for all }f\in C_{b}(X)

whenever the sequence

In the general case, where

\phi(f)={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f-I)\quad\text{ for all }f\in L^{\infty},

As pointed out by Barbe and Broniatowski

We use our representation result to provide a criterion for the sharp LDP (

We show that this criterion in fact covers the sharp LDP provided in

A limit result for distorted expectations

Our theoretical framework covers other monetary risk measures beyond the Varadhan functional. Specifically, for a concave distortion

\phi_{g}(f)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(e^{nf})\quad\mbox{ for all }f\in L^{\infty},

where
\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(f)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g\circ\nu_{n}(f>x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the distorted expectation associated with
g
. This functional retains the properties of a maxitive monetary risk measure.
Moreover, we establish that if the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the sharp LDP with rate
I
, then

(1.5)

\phi_{g}(f)={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f-\tfrac{1}{p}I),

where
p
is the vanishing order of the distortion

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\tfrac{g(x)}{x^{1/p}}
is finite and non-zero.

Tsanakas

17

In the exponential premium principle the premium to be paid for a claim

In the distortion-exponential premium principle the premium to be paid for the claim

\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi)=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\log\mathcal{E}_{\mu,g}(e^{\gamma\xi})

Using (

\pi_{n}
per contract

Since the portfolio is homogeneous, all insured individuals pay the same premium, which is calculated as
\pi_{n}=\tfrac{1}{n}\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\cdots+\xi_{n})
.
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(x-\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}I\right),

3.
Maxitive Risk Measures and Worst-Case Risk Assessment

Let
E
be a vector sublattice of
L^{0}
that contains
L^{\infty}
. The concept of a
maxitive monetary risk measure
, originally studied in
[
13
]
, can be adapted to our setting as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

A monetary risk measure
\phi\colon E\to[-\infty,\infty]
is called
maxitive
if it satisfies the inequality
\phi(f\vee g)\leq\phi(f)\vee\phi(g)
for all
f,g\in E
. We say that
\phi
is
\sigma
-maxitive
if, for every sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
E
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}\in E
, it holds that
\phi(\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n})\leq\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\phi(f_{n})
.

Remark 3.2
.

By monotonicity, any maxitive monetary risk measure automatically satisfies
\phi(f\vee g)=\phi(f)\vee\phi(g)
for all
f,g\in E
.
Every
\sigma
-maxitive risk measure is in particular maxitive. Moreover, every maxitive monetary risk measure is convex; see
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
.

A canonical example of a
\sigma
-maxitive monetary risk measure is the
maximum loss

\mathrm{ML}(f):={\rm ess.sup}_{X}f.

A penalized version is given by

\mathrm{ML}_{I}(f):={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f-I)
,
where
I\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is a measurable function with
{\rm ess.inf}_{X}I=0
.

A maxitive monetary risk measure captures a
worst-case
evaluation of risk. This can be seen as follows. In Proposition
3.3
below, we show that a monetary risk measure
\phi
is maxitive if and only if, for every financial position
f\in E
and every event
A\in\mathcal{X}
, one has

(3.1)

\phi(f)=\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(f),

where
\phi_{A}(f)
denotes the
localized risk evaluation
of
f
on
A
, formally defined in (
3.2
) below.

The decomposition (
3.1
) implies that, for any set
A
, the risk evaluation
\phi(f)
corresponds to the worst of its localized risk evaluations on
A
and
A^{c}
. This shows that worst-case risk assessments are characterized by maxitive monetary risk measures.

Another feature of maxitive monetary risk measures is that, in contrast to expectations, which average losses across different states, a maxitive monetary risk measure does not allow large profits in some states to compensate for losses in others. To see this, suppose that
\phi_{A}(f)\geq\phi_{A^{c}}(f)
in (
3.1
). Then, for any
g\in E
that agrees with
f
a.s. on
A
, we still have

\phi(f)=\phi_{A}(f)=\phi_{A}(g),

regardless of the values that
g
takes on
A^{c}
. In particular, this implies that gains on
A^{c}
cannot offset losses on
A
.

As a result, maxitivity ensures that risk is not underestimated by averaging outcomes across different scenarios. From a financial perspective, maxitive monetary risk measures align well with a conservative approach to risk assessment, particularly in contexts where robustness and prudence are critical. This is especially relevant in settings such as stress testing or regulatory capital requirements, where the goal is to ensure resilience under extreme adverse events.
However, such an approach may be too pessimistic in portfolio management, as it neglects potential benefits of average performance.

We now formalize this characterization.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\phi\colon E\to[-\infty,\infty]
be a monetary risk measure. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\phi
is maxitive.

(2)

For all
f\in E
and all
A\in\mathcal{X}
,

\phi(f)=\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(f),

where

(3.2)

\phi_{A}(f):=\inf\left\{\phi(g)\colon g\in E,\,f=g\text{ a.s. on }A\right\}.

Remark 3.4
.

In the definition of
\phi_{A}(f)
, the infimum is taken over all positions
g\in E
that agree with
f
on
A
, no matter what they do on
A^{c}
. By allowing arbitrarily large gains on
A^{c}
, we ignore the risk in that region. As a result,
\phi_{A}(f)
captures the localized risk evaluation of
f
on
A
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2):
Let
g,h\in E
be such that
g=f
a.s. on
A
and
h=f
a.s. on
A^{c}
. Since
\phi
is maxitive and
f=(f\wedge g)\vee(f\wedge h)\in E
,
7
7

7

Recall that
E
is a lattice.

\phi(f)=\phi(f\wedge g)\vee\phi(f\wedge h).

Taking first the infimum over all
g\in E
such that
g=f
a.s. on
A
, and then the infimum over all
h\in E
such that
h=f
a.s. on
A
, we obtain
\phi(f)=\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(f)
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1):
Let

, and define

. Then, using (2) twice,

\displaystyle\phi(f\vee g)

\displaystyle=\phi_{A}(f\vee g)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(f\vee g)

\displaystyle=\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(g)

\displaystyle\leq(\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A}(g))\vee(\phi_{A^{c}}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(g))

\displaystyle=(\phi_{A}(f)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(f))\vee(\phi_{A}(g)\vee\phi_{A^{c}}(g))

\displaystyle=\phi(f)\vee\phi(g).

The two most popular monetary risk measures in financial practice are Value at Risk (VaR) and Average Value at Risk (AVaR).

We can use Theorem

Suppose that the sequences
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{M}_{1}

Then, we claim that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\rm AVaR}_{\nu_{n},\alpha_{n}}(f)=\operatorname{ess\,sup}_{X}f\quad\text{for all }f\in L^{\infty}.

Remark 4.12
.

Theorem
4.4
shows that the maximum loss
{\rm ML}
is the unique example of a maxitive monetary risk measure that is law-invariant (provided that
\mu
is atomless and
L^{2}
is separable). In addition, as discussed in
[
12
]
, the assumption that the probability space is atomless is common in financial modeling, where asset prices are typically modeled as continuous variables. In fact, the probability space
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
is atomless if and only if there exists a random variable
\xi
on
(X,\mathcal{X},\mu)
with a continuous distribution; see
[
7
]
. Moreover, the condition that
L^{2}
is separable is not restrictive in practice, as it holds whenever we are dealing with countably many random variables, which is typical in financial applications; see
[
12
]
for further discussion.

These considerations suggest that, in practical matters, the maximum loss is the only law-invariant monetary risk measure that is also maxitive. This rules out other popular law-invariant measures, such as
Value at Risk
(
{\rm VaR}
). While this might seem limiting, it is conceptually quite natural, as worst-case risk evaluations are inherently sensitive to specific scenarios
x\in X
, making them incompatible with law invariance, which depends solely on the distribution of outcomes rather than their realizations.

However, one can still construct examples of law-invariant monetary risk measures that are maxitive in a weaker sense. This can be achieved by identifying a random variable
f
with its quantile function
q_{\alpha}(f)
, and ordering them by
usual stochastic dominance
, defined as
f\leq_{\rm st}g
whenever
q_{\alpha}(f)\leq q_{\alpha}(g)
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
(in other words, replacing states by quantile levels). Then, a monetary risk measure
\phi
is said to be maxitive (with respect to
\leq_{\rm st}
) if

\phi(f\vee_{\rm st}g)\leq\phi(f)\vee\phi(g),

where the
f\vee_{\rm st}g
represents a random variable whose quantile function is given by
q_{\alpha}(f\vee_{\rm st}g)=q_{\alpha}(f)\vee q_{\alpha}(g)
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. A typical example of such a maxitive monetary risk measure is
{\rm VaR}
. This form of maxitivity has been studied recently in
[
6
,
14
]
.

On the other hand, maxitive monetary risk measures (in the strong sense) offer reasonable alternatives to traditional VaR. For example, consider the following maxitive analogue of VaR. Let
X=A\cup B
be a partition of the sample space, where
B
represents a set of stressed scenarios with
\nu(B)=1-\alpha
, for some confidence level
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The
maxitive VaR
at level
\alpha
is defined as

{\rm maxVaR}_{\alpha}(f)={\rm ess.sup}_{A}f.

This risk measure corresponds to the penalized maximum loss
{\rm ML}_{I}
, where the penalty function
I
is equal to
0
on
A
and
\infty
on
A^{c}
.

Like traditional VaR, the maxitive VaR estimates the maximum loss within a specified confidence region. However, whereas standard VaR relies on the underlying probability distribution to determine this region, maxitive VaR allows it to be explicitly constructed from selected scenarios. This enables the design of confidence regions that can better reflect the structure and interdependence of specific risk factors. Also, since maxVaR is maxitive, and thus convex, and positively homogeneous, it is a coherent risk measure. This contrasts with VaR, which does not satisfy coherence. Such risk measures have been studied in detail in
[
16
]
.

5.
Applications

In this section, by giving a different interpretation to the main results, we explore other applications to large deviations and the limit theory of risk measures.

5.1.
Sharp large deviation estimates

In this subsection, let
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of probability measures on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{X})
such that
\nu_{n}\ll\mu
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
for a given reference probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
. Given a measurable function
I\colon\mathcal{X}\to[0,\infty]
, we say that the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the
sharp large deviation principle
(sharp LDP) with rate
I
if

(5.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}I\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{X}.

In the special case where
\nu_{n}
are the laws of the sample means of a sequence of i.i.d.
d
-dimensional random variables, Barbe and Broniatowski
[
3
]
proved that if the law
\nu_{1}
satisfies certain regularity conditions, then the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a sharp LDP as stated in (
5.1
). In the following, we apply the results in the previous section to analyze the non-i.i.d. case and establish sufficient conditions for the sharp LDP in terms of the Radon–Nikodym derivatives
\frac{{\rm d}\nu_{n}}{{\rm d}\mu}
.

Remark 5.1
.

In the particular situation where
X
is a topological space,
\mathcal{X}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(X)
, and
I\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is a lower semicontinuous function, the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to satisfy the
large deviation principle
(LDP) with rate
I
if

(5.2)

-\inf_{x\in\operatorname{int}(A)}I(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)\leq-\inf_{x\in\operatorname{cl}(A)}I(x)

for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(X)
,
where
\operatorname{int}(A)
and
\operatorname{cl}(A)
denote the interior and closure of
A
, respectively.
As explained in the introduction, the bounds in (
5.2
) may not be sharp and can sometimes lead to trivial estimates. In contrast, the sharp version of the LDP (
5.1
) provides the exact asymptotics of
\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)
.

Additionally, we have some relations between the sharp LDP (
5.1
) and the rough LDP (
5.2
).
Suppose that the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the sharp LDP with rate
I
. Since
\inf_{x\in F}I(x)\leq{\rm ess.inf}_{F}I
for all closed sets
F\subset X
, the upper bound in the rough LDP (
5.2
) holds for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(X)
. On the other hand, the lower bound in the rough LDP (
5.2
) may not hold for some open sets
G
if
\inf_{x\in G}I(x)<{\rm ess.inf}_{G}I
.
However, if
I\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is continuous and every
x\in X
has a neighborhood base
\mathcal{U}_{x}
such that
\mu(U)>0
for all
U\in\mathcal{U}_{x}
, then
\inf_{x\in G}I(x)={\rm ess.inf}_{G}I
for all open sets
G\subset X
. Consequently, the lower bound in the rough LDP (
5.2
) holds.

In the following, we consider the (upper) Varadhan functional
\phi\colon L^{0}\to[-\infty,\infty]
defined by

\phi(f):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{n}.

The function
\phi
shares the properties of a monetary risk measure, and is maxitive as a consequence of the principle of the largest term (see (1) in Examples
4.11
).
We also consider the associated concentration
J
defined as in (
4.1
), the minimal penalty
I_{\min}
defined as in (
4.2
), and the set
L^{\phi}
defined as in (
4.3
). For every
A\in\mathcal{X}
,

J_{A}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A).

As a consequence of Theorem
4.1
, we have the following.

Proposition 5.2
.

The sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies the sharp LDP (
5.1
) with rate
I
if, and only if, it satisfies the Laplace principle (LP)

(5.3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}{\rm d}\nu_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{X}\{f-I\}\quad\mbox{ for all }f\in L^{\phi}.

In that case,
I=I_{\min}
a.s.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the sharp LDP with rate
I
. In that case,
{J}_{A}=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}I
for all
A\in\mathcal{X}
, and it follows from
(4)\Rightarrow(6)
in Theorem
4.1
that

{\phi}(f)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{X}\{f-I\}\quad\text{for all }f\in L^{\phi},

and
I=I_{\min}
a.s. To establish (
5.3
), it remains to show that the limit superior in
\phi(f)
is in fact a limit. Fixed
f\in L^{\phi}
, take a subsequence
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n_{k}}\log\int_{X}e^{n_{k}f}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n_{k}}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n}.

The monetary risk measure
\psi(h):=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n_{k}}\log\int_{X}e^{n_{k}h}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n_{k}}
is maxitive by the principle of the largest term, and the associated concentration
J^{\psi}
satisfies

J^{\psi}_{A}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n_{k}}\log\nu_{n_{k}}(A)=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}I\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{X}.

Again, by Theorem
4.1
, we have

\psi(h)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nh}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{X}\{h-I\}\quad\text{for all }h\in L^{\psi}.

In particular, since
f\in L^{\phi}\subset L^{\psi}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm ess.sup}_{X}\{f-I\}

\displaystyle=\phi(f)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{X}e^{nf}\,{\rm d}\nu_{n}=\psi(f)={\rm ess.sup}_{X}\{f-I\}.

This shows that the limit superior in
\phi(f)
is indeed a limit, as required.

The converse direction, where the LP (
5.3
) implies the sharp LDP, follows from the implication
(6)\Rightarrow(4)
in Theorem
4.1
. The existence of the limit in this case can be established by a subsequence argument similar to the one used above.

Remark 5.3

The following result is standard. For a proof, refer to

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
f\in L^{\infty}_{+}
, it holds that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\int_{X}f^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/n}={\rm ess.sup}_{X}f.

We establish the following criterion for the existence of a sharp LDP, which seems to be
new.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
I\colon X\to[0,\infty]
be a measurable function, and suppose that
(A_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{X}
satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

\{I<\infty\}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{k}
a.s.

(2)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A_{k}^{c})\to-\infty
as
k\to\infty
.

(3)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{1}{n}\log\tfrac{{\rm d}\nu_{n}}{{\rm d}\mu}\to-I\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\text{ a.s.\penalty\ uniformly on }A_{k}.

Then,
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies both the sharp LDP (
5.1
) and the LP (
5.3
) with rate
I
.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define
h_{n}:=\frac{{\rm d}\nu_{n}}{{\rm d}\mu}
. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. By the condition (3), for sufficiently large
n
, it holds that

e^{-\delta n}\leq h_{n}e^{nI}\leq e^{\delta n}\quad\text{a.s. on }A_{k}.

For any
B\subset A_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(B)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}h_{n}\,{\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}h_{n}e^{nI}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}e^{\delta n}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu

\displaystyle=\delta+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu.

Similarly, we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(B)\geq-\delta+\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu.

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
, we conclude that the limit superior in
J_{B}
is indeed a limit, and

(5.4)

J_{B}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(B)=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{B}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu.

Applying Lemma
5.4
, we obtain

J_{B}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\int_{B}e^{-nI}\,{\rm d}\mu\right)^{1/n}=\log\left({\rm ess.sup}_{B}e^{-I}\right)=-{\rm ess.inf}_{B}I.

Since
B\subset A_{k}
was arbitrary, we conclude that the condition (3) in Theorem
4.10
is satisfied.
Also, the conditions (1) and (2) are exactly those in Theorem
4.10
. Therefore, we obtain from that result that

(5.5)

J_{A}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}I\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{X},

and, furthermore,
I_{\min}=I
a.s.

To complete the proof, it suffices to show that the limit superior in (
5.5
) is indeed a limit; in that case, both the sharp LDP and the LP hold by Proposition
5.2
. Suppose by contradiction that
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)<\beta<-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}I
for some
A\in\mathcal{X}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. In that case,
\mu(A\cap\{I<-\beta\})>0
. By the condition (1), there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B:=A_{k}\cap A\cap\{I<-\beta\}
satisfies
\mu(B)>0
.

Since
B\subset A_{k}
, the limit superior in
J_{B}
is a limit by (
5.4
), and

\beta>\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(B)=J_{B}=-{\rm ess.inf}_{B}I\geq\beta,

which is a contradiction. The proof is complete.
∎

The previous result gives us a technique to establish a sharp large deviations principle if it exists.
Namely, by computing the
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\frac{{\rm d}\nu_{n}}{{\rm d}\mu}=-I
, we obtain a candidate for the rate
I
.
The next step is to exhaust
\{I<\infty\}
by sets
A_{k}
where the convergence is uniform and concentrating low probability mass in the complements
A_{k}^{c}
.

Example 5.6
.

For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
X=[1,\infty)^{d}
endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}([1,\infty)^{d})
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\nu_{n}
be the law of a multivariate Pareto distribution of the first kind on
[1,\infty)^{d}
with shape parameter
n
, i.e.,
\nu_{n}
has the density function

f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\frac{n(n+1)\cdots(n+d-1)}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-d+1\right)^{n+d}}\quad\text{for all }x_{i}\geq 1.

In this case, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=-\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-d+1\right).

This suggests the candidate rate function
I(x)=\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-d+1\right)
, assuming a sharp LDP holds.
The convergence is uniform on the sets
A_{k}:=[1,k]^{d}
.

Using the fact that the cumulative distribution function of
\nu_{n}
is

F_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=1-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-d+1\right)^{-n},

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle J_{A_{k}^{c}}

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A_{k}^{c})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\left(1-F_{n}(k,\ldots,k)\right)

\displaystyle=-\log\left(d(k-1)+1\right)\to-\infty\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

We have verified the conditions (1)–(3) in Theorem
5.5
.
Thus, we obtain the sharp LDP, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\nu_{n}(A)=-{\rm ess.inf}_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in A}\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}-d+1\right)\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{B}([1,\infty)^{d}).

Theorem
5.5
provides sufficient conditions for the sharp LDP for general sequences of probability measures. In the following, we revisit the i.i.d. case under this new criterion. For the remainder of this subsection, we assume that
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(the Borel

\mu
has the same null sets as the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d.
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\nu_{n}

The

Remark 5.7
.

If
\Lambda
is finite, then Cramér’s theorem asserts that

satisfies the rough LDP (

) with the rate

, see

In this case, the rate function

Although, in this situation, the rough LDP (

To give an example, consider the distribution

In that case, the set

verifies

for odd

Therefore, the sequence

Next, we show that Theorem

also covers the i.i.d. case studied by Barbe and Browniatoski
[

]
. Specifically, Andriani and Baldi

Corollary 5.8
.

Suppose that:

(1)

\Lambda
is finite.

(2)

\nu_{n}
has a bounded density

for

large enough.

Then,
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies the sharp LDP and the LP with rate

Proof.

Set

.
If the conditions (1) and (2) above are satisfied,

asserts that the asymptotic equivalence

(5.6)

f_{n}(x)\sim\left(\frac{n}{2\pi}\right)^{d/2}\left|I^{\prime\prime}(x)\right|^{1/2}e^{-nI(x)}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

holds uniformly on compact subsets of the effective domain

denotes the Hessian of

9
9

9

[

considers instead the

As pointed out in

for every

5.2.
A limit result for distorted expectations

In this subsection, let
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of probability measures on a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{X})
such that
\nu_{n}\ll\mu
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mu
is a given reference probability measure on
(X,\mathcal{X})
. Given a concave distortion
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, we define the monetary risk measure
\phi:L^{0}\to[-\infty,\infty]
by

(5.8)

\phi(f):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(e^{nf}),

where
\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(f):=\int_{0}^{\infty}g\circ\nu_{n}(f>x)\,\mathrm{d}x
is the distorted expectation associated with
g
(see (3) in Example
4.11
). Since
g
is concave,
\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}
is subadditive for all
n
, and
\phi
is maxitive by the principle of the largest term (see (1) in Example
4.11
).
The concentration function
J
associated with
\phi
is given by

J_{A}:=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log(g\circ\nu_{n}(A))\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{X}.

Lemma 5.10
.

If
h\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]
is a concave distortion, then the right derivative
h^{\prime}_{+}(0)
exists as an extended real number in
[1,\infty]
.
Moreover, if
h^{\prime}_{+}(0)<\infty
, then
h(x)\leq x\cdot h^{\prime}_{+}(0)
for all
x\in(0,1]
.

Proof.

All the assertions follow from the fact that the function
x\mapsto\tfrac{h(x)}{x}
is non-decreasing on
(0,1]
. To prove that, assume that
0<x_{1}<x_{2}\leq 1
. Then, by concavity,

h(x_{1})=h\left(\tfrac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}x_{2}+(1-\tfrac{x_{1}}{x_{2}})0\right)\geq\tfrac{x_{1}}{x_{2}}h(x_{2}).

Dividing by
x_{1}
, we get the
\tfrac{h(x_{1})}{x_{1}}\geq\tfrac{h(x_{2})}{x_{2}}
, as desired.
∎

Definition 5.11
.

The distortion
g
is said to have
vanishing order

p
, with
p\geq 1
, if the distortion
h:=g^{p}
is concave and verifies
h^{\prime}_{+}(0)<\infty
.

The vanishing order of the distortion
g
measures how fast the function
g(x)
falls to
0
as as
x\downarrow 0
. More specifically, if
g
has vanishing order
p
, then

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{g(x)}{x^{1/p}}=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{h(x)^{1/p}}{x^{1/p}}=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\left(\frac{h(x)}{x}\right)^{1/p}=(h^{\prime}_{+}(0))^{1/p}\in[1,\infty).

Since the limit
(h^{\prime}_{+}(0))^{1/p}
is finite and not null, then
g
behaves asymptotically like
x^{1/p}
near
0
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Suppose that
g
has vanishing order
p
.
If the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the sharp LDP (
5.1
) with rate
I
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(e^{nf})={\rm ess.sup}_{X}(f-\tfrac{1}{p}I)\quad\mbox{ for all }f\in L^{\phi}.

Proof.

Set
h:=g^{p}
. Given
A\in\mathcal{X}
, by Lemma
5.10
, we have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log(g\circ\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log(h\circ\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log\left(\nu_{n}(A)\cdot h^{\prime}_{+}(0)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log(\nu_{n}(A))+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log(h^{\prime}_{+}(0))

\displaystyle=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}\tfrac{1}{p}I,

where we have used that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the sharp LDP with rate
I
. On the other hand, by concavity,
h(x)\geq x
for all
x\in[0,1]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log(g\circ\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log(h\circ\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{np}\log(\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{p}\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log(\nu_{n}(A))

\displaystyle=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}\tfrac{1}{p}I.

It follows that

J_{A}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log(g\circ\nu_{n}(A))=-{\rm ess.inf}_{A}\tfrac{1}{p}I\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal{X}.

Then, by Theorem
4.1
, we obtain

\phi(f)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}(e^{nf})={\rm ess.sup}_{X}\left(f-\tfrac{1}{p}I\right)\quad\text{for all }f\in L^{\phi}.

Finally, a subsequence argument similar to that in the proof of Proposition
5.2
shows that the limit superior above is indeed a limit.
∎

Tsanakas
[
17
]
introduced the
distortion-exponential
insurance premium principle. For an insurance claim whose random outcome is given by a real-valued random variable
\xi
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,P)
, the premium to be paid for the claim is

(5.9)

\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi):=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\log\mathcal{E}_{P,g}\left(e^{\gamma\xi}\right),

where
\gamma>0
is the risk aversion parameter, and
g
is a concave distortion reflecting the insurer’s attitude towards risk.
In the special case where
g(x)=x
is the identity function, we recover the usual exponential premium principle

(5.10)

\Pi_{\gamma}(\xi):=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\log\mathbb{E}_{P}\left(e^{\gamma\xi}\right).

We now use Theorem
5.12
to study the asymptotic behavior of the distortion-exponential principle (
5.9
) under the pooling of risks. Consider a homogeneous portfolio of
n
insurance claims, whose uncertain outcomes are represented by the real-valued random variables
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}
. According to (
5.9
), the premium to be paid for the entire portfolio is
\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{n})
. Hence, the premium for each individual contract in this portfolio is given by

\pi_{n}=\tfrac{1}{n}\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{n}).

The following result gives the asymptotics of the individual premium as the number of claims tend to infinity.

Corollary 5.13
.

Suppose that
g
is a concave distortion with vanishing order
p
. Consider a sequence of insurance contracts
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
such that the laws
\nu_{n}
of the averages
\bar{\xi}_{n}

satisfy the sharp LDP with rate
I
. Assume that there exists
t>1
such that

(5.11)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\Pi_{g,\gamma}\left(t(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\cdots+\xi_{n})\right)<\infty.

Then,

(5.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(x-\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}I(x)\right).

Moreover, if the sequence
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
is i.i.d. and satisfies the conditions (1) and (2) in Corollary
5.8
, then

(5.13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}=\Pi_{p\gamma}(\xi).

Proof.

To apply Theorem
5.12
, suppose
X=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
, and that
\mu
has the same null sets as the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Consider the monetary risk measure
\phi(f):=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\mathcal{E}_{\nu_{n},g}\left(e^{nf}\right)
, defined as in (
5.8
).
By (
5.11
), the function
f_{\gamma}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
f_{\gamma}(x)=\gamma x
, satisfies
f_{\gamma}\in L^{\phi}
. Then, it follows from Theorem
5.12
that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\Pi_{g,\gamma}(\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}+\cdots+\xi_{n})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}g\circ\mathbb{P}\left(e^{nf_{\gamma}(\bar{\xi}_{n})}>x\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\gamma}\phi(f_{\gamma})={\rm ess.sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(x-\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}I(x)\right),

obtaining (
5.12
).
Now, if the sequence
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
is i.i.d. and satisfies the conditions (1) and (2) of Corollary
5.8
, then the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies the sharp LDP with rate
\Lambda^{\ast}
. Hence, in that case

\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}={\rm ess.sup}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(x-\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}\Lambda^{\ast}(x)\right).

As pointing out in the proof of Corollary
5.8
,
\Lambda^{\ast}
is continuous on its effective domain. Thus, the essential supremum above is actually a supremum and,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(x-\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}\Lambda^{\ast}(x)\right)=\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left(p\gamma x-\Lambda^{\ast}(p\gamma x)\right)=\tfrac{1}{p\gamma}\Lambda^{\ast\ast}(p\gamma)=\tfrac{1}{\gamma p}\Lambda(p\gamma),

where in the last equality we have applied the Fenchel-Moreau theorem (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Proposition A.6]
). The conclusion follows by noting that
\Pi_{p\gamma}(\xi)=\frac{1}{p\gamma}\Lambda(p\gamma)
.
∎

Equality (
5.13
) reveals that as the number of independent claims in a homogeneous portfolio grows, the individual distortion-exponential premium converges to the usual exponential premium for a single claim, where the risk aversion parameter
\gamma p
is determined by the vanishing order
p
of the distortion
g
.

Proposition A.2
.

Suppose that

is a measurable function that is bounded from above. Then

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mu)}\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\nu={\rm ess.sup}_{X}f.

Proof.

Clearly,
\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mu)}\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\nu\leq{\rm ess.sup}_{X}f

(A.1)

\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mu)}\int_{X}f\,{\rm d}\nu<{\rm ess.sup}_{X}f.
