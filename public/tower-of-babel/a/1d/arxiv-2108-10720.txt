Imaging swiFTly: streaming widefield Fourier Transforms for large-scale interferometry
P. Wortmann, J. Kent, B. Nikolic

Abstract
Aims. We describe a scalable distributed imaging algorithm framework for next-generation radio telescopes, managing the Fourier transform from apertures to sky (or vice versa) with a focus on minimising memory load, data transfers, and computation. Methods. Our algorithm uses smooth window functions to isolate the influence between specific regions of spatial-frequency and image space. This allows the distribution of image data between nodes and the construction of segments of frequency space exactly when and where needed. Results. The developed prototype distributes terabytes of image data across many nodes, while generating visibilities at throughput and accuracy competitive with existing software. Scaling is demonstrated to be better than cubic in problem complexity (for baseline length and field of view), reducing the risk involved in growing radio astronomy processing to large telescopes like the Square Kilometre Array.



1
Introduction

Upcoming large-scale radio telescopes like the Square Kilometre
Array (SKA)
(
Dewdney et al. 2009
)
are designed for deep observations of large
areas of the sky. These observations require many long baselines, wide
bandwidths, and high frequency resolution. This translates to demanding
computing requirements; for SKA imaging observations, more than a petabyte of
visibility data might be generated per hour. Pipelines will need to perform approximately 40 exa-operations to produce images that are terabytes in
size
(
Bolton et al. 2016
)
. This means that efficient use of
high-performance computing facilities will be essential, which will require
algorithms that distribute computation, visibility data, as well as image data
evenly across many compute nodes.

This distribution is not trivial because radio astronomy imaging requires a
Fourier transform where every measured visibility impacts every image
pixel. Conventional strategies for distributing radio-interferometric imaging
split visibilities by frequency or by observation time, but replicate full copies of the image to every node. A well-known
alternative approach is to instead split the image into ‘facets’, previously used for
non-coplanarity
(
Cornwell & Perley 1992
)
, direction-dependent calibration,
and deconvolution
(
Van Weeren et al. 2016
)
. This requires visibilities to be
gridded to a separate low-resolution grid for every facet, which combined with
averaging can be a viable scaling
strategy
(
Bolton et al. 2016
;
Tasse et al. 2018
)
. On the other hand,
repeated gridding is inefficient, phase rotation can become a bottleneck, and
averaging introduces inherent inaccuracies into the result.

Figure 1
:
Distribution concept sketch of our algorithm. The visibilities are gridded to subgrids (solid lines),
and the contributions are accumulated to facets. The dashed lines indicate
the reverse direction: the contributions are extracted from the facets,
and the visibilities are degridded from the subgrids.

Therefore, this paper focuses on the Fourier transform specifically,
describing a scalable algorithm that addresses the core challenges of
large-scale interferometric processing. Our method fully distributes the image
data as well as the computation, never needing to assemble the entire image or

uv(w)
-grid at any stage. As with previous work, this is achieved using a distributed
facet approach, so different parts of the image are held and processed in
memory separately. However, our method solves the conceptually easier problem of
transforming them from and to cut-outs of the full-resolution spatial-frequency
grid, which we call ‘subgrids’. Hence, in our approach both the image-plane and
the aperture-plane are divided into smaller parts that can be processed in
parallel. This can be used to distribute conventional visibility (de)gridding algorithms
as a separate step, as shown in

Fig.
1
. This would process every
visibility only once on a single node, with a roughly predictable processing
order.

All we need is an efficient way to transform between such subgrids and
facets, which would make it a form of distributed Fourier transform.
Distributed fast Fourier transform (FFT)
algorithms
(
Frigo & Johnson 1998
, e.g.)
might seem the obvious choice;
however, they are not well suited for our purposes. For non-coplanarity
corrections we are often only interested in local regions of the
uvw

spatial-frequency space, and therefore we need to minimise not only the computational
work spent on unneeded
uvw
regions, but also the time the full grid or
image data is held in memory. Additionally, we
would like to apply extra image-space factors per subgrid, for example for
widefield corrections, which further pushes us towards a different class of
algorithm.

Our solution is therefore to stream portions of spatial-frequency space to
worker nodes (or, during de-gridding, from them) exactly as and when required
to cover the telescope baselines: an incremental semi-sparse Fourier
transform algorithm for contiguous partitions of image and frequency
space. In
Sect. 2
we define the core algorithm, which we
then extend in
Sect. 3
to cover the effects of Fresnel
diffraction when observing widefield. In
Sect. 4
we
demonstrate how to assemble and parametrise the algorithm, which allows us to
perform scaling and performance tests using SKA-scale parameters in

Sect. 5
. We finally wrap things up with a discussion in

Sect. 6
.

2
Core algorithm

Interferometric imaging measures complex visibilities
V
,
and relates them to the planar projection of the sky intensity distribution
I
as

\displaystyle V(u,v,w)=\iint I(l,m)\,e^{-2\pi iw(\sqrt{1-l^{2}-m^{2}}-1)}e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}\,\text{d}l\,\text{d}m,

(1)

where
l
and
m
are sky direction cosines, and
u
,
v
,
and
w
are coordinate components of telescope baselines
(
Cornwell & Perley 1992
, compare e.g.)
.
The right-hand side involves a Fourier transform,
which is why an efficient approximation of
I
from visibility samples

V
(or vice versa) requires discrete Fourier
transforms.

2.1
Notation

For simplicity we limit ourselves to one-dimensional functions for the moment.
By convention, all named functions are in
visibility space (i.e. using spatial-frequency
u
coordinate).
The image or gridded visibilities to be transformed are represented
by a function
\Upsilon
, no matter the transformation direction considered.
Furthermore, all functions are discrete using a given step rate of

2l_{\Upsilon}
and repeating with a period of
2u_{\Upsilon}
satisfying
4u_{\Upsilon}l_{\Upsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This allows us to define a ‘scaled’
discrete Fourier transform as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\mathrm{f}](l)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2u_{\Upsilon}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-u_{\Upsilon}\leq u<u_{\Upsilon}\\
2ul_{\Upsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{f}(u)\,e^{-2\pi iul}

\displaystyle\text{ for }2lu_{\Upsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2)

This makes
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}
an
image-space function (
l

coordinate) that is sampled with a step rate of
2u_{\Upsilon}
and repeats
with a period of
2l_{\Upsilon}
.
We use the following notations and properties:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{f}\!+\!\mathrm{g})(u)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{f}(u)+\mathrm{g}(u)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}+\mathrm{g}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}+\mathcal{F}\!\mathrm{g}],

(3)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{f}\mathrm{g})(u)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{f}(u)\mathrm{g}(u)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}\mathrm{g}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2l_{\Upsilon}}\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}\!\ast\!\mathcal{F}\!\mathrm{g}],

(4)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{f}\!\ast\!\mathrm{g})(u)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}-u_{\Upsilon}\leq\tau<u_{\Upsilon}\\
2\tau l_{\Upsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\mathrm{f}(\tau)\mathrm{g}(u\!-\!\tau)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}\!\ast\!\mathrm{g}

\displaystyle=2u_{\Upsilon}\mathcal{F}^{-1}[\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}\ \mathcal{F}\!\mathrm{g}].

(5)

For this paper we often need to deal with functions
that are sampled more coarsely than the base
u_{\Upsilon}
/
l_{\Upsilon}
discretisation.
We use Kronecker comb functions to represent this:

\displaystyle\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}(u)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(u-k2u_{\mathrm{f}})\;\Rightarrow\;\mathcal{F}[\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}]=\tfrac{1}{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}\Sh_{(2u_{\mathrm{f}})^{\shortminus 1}}.

(6)

Here
2u_{\mathrm{f}}l_{\Upsilon},2u_{\mathrm{f}}u_{\Upsilon}^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Multiplication
with
\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}
samples frequency space at a rate of
(2u_{\mathrm{f}})^{-1}
,
while convolution with
\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}
samples image space at a rate of
2u_{\mathrm{f}}
.
With
2u_{\Upsilon}l_{\mathrm{f}},2l_{\mathrm{f}}l_{\Upsilon}^{-1},4u_{\mathrm{f}}l_{\mathrm{f}}\in\mathbb{Z}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{f}=\tfrac{u_{\Upsilon}}{u_{\mathrm{f}}}\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{f}}}\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{f}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{f}\;\;\Leftrightarrow\;\;\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}=\tfrac{l_{\mathrm{f}}u_{\Upsilon}}{u_{\mathrm{f}}l_{\Upsilon}}(\Sh_{2l_{\mathrm{f}}}\ast\Sh_{(2u_{\mathrm{f}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f});

(7)

in other words, where a function
\mathrm{f}
repeats with a period of
2u_{\mathrm{f}}
and
is non-zero at a rate of
2l_{\mathrm{f}}
, its Fourier transform repeats with a
period of
2l_{\mathrm{f}}
and has a rate of
2u_{\mathrm{f}}
. For
numerical purposes,
Eq. 7
furthermore
shows that we can represent
\mathrm{f}
or
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{f}

entirely using exactly
|4u_{\mathrm{f}}l_{\mathrm{f}}|
samples.

2.2
Problem statement

The distributed Fourier transform problem is now given by mask
functions
\mathrm{A}_{i}
and
\mathrm{B}_{j}
, which encode how
we wish to partition
\Upsilon
in spatial-frequency and image space:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon

\displaystyle=\Upsilon

and

\displaystyle\sum_{j}(\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon)

\displaystyle=\Upsilon

(8)

with
u_{\mathrm{A}}<u_{\Upsilon}
and
l_{\mathrm{B}}<l_{\Upsilon}
such that we can represent subgrids

\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
using
4u_{\mathrm{A}}l_{\Upsilon}
samples (subgrid size) and facets
\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon
using
4u_{\Upsilon}l_{\mathrm{B}}
samples (facet size). We expect

\mathrm{A}_{i}
to be boxcar filters in frequency space and
\mathrm{B}_{j}
boxcar
filters in image space (blue graphs in

Fig. 2
), and therefore with
u_{\Upsilon}u_{\mathrm{A}}^{-1},l_{\Upsilon}l_{\mathrm{B}}^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon=\mathrm{A}_{i}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{A}}}\!\ast\!\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon),\,\,\mathrm{B}_{j}\!\ast\!\Upsilon=\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{2u_{\Upsilon}l_{\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{B}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\mathrm{B}_{j}\!\ast\!\Upsilon)

(9)

by Whittaker–Shannon interpolation.
As illustrated in
Fig.
1
, now the goal
is to reconstruct
\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon
given all

\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
. On paper this is straightforward due to
linearity:

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon=\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\sum_{i}\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon=\sum_{i}\left(\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\right).

(10)

So the contribution of subgrid
i
to facet
j
is simply

\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
.
For an efficient distributed algorithm, this contribution
would have to be computed and exchanged between nodes.
Unfortunately, naïve attempts to represent

\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
using
4u_{\mathrm{A}}l_{\mathrm{B}}
samples would fail, as we cannot apply
Whittaker-Shannon simultaneously in image and spatial-frequency space:

\displaystyle\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{2u_{\Upsilon}l_{\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{B}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{A}}}\ast\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)\neq\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon.

(11)

The reason is that
\mathrm{B}_{j}
never approaches zero in frequency space (see

Fig. 2
, bottom left plot), and therefore neither does

\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
. Hence, tiling it with a period of
2u_{\mathrm{A}}

effectively loses information.

2.3
Approximation

Figure 3
:
Image space error (light colours: worst case
\Upsilon
)

The key idea is to use a different representation. We suppose a function

\mathrm{n}_{j}
that falls close to zero in both frequency
and image space (with associated limits
u_{\mathrm{n}}
and
l_{\mathrm{n}}

respectively). Then the same should be true for
\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
,
so we should be able to say

\displaystyle\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)\approx\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon,

(12)

where
\mathrm{m}_{i}
is a boxcar filter with
u_{\mathrm{m}}>u_{\mathrm{A}}+u_{\mathrm{n}}
(
Fig. 2
, left plots). If we
furthermore find an ‘inverse’ function
\mathrm{b}_{j}
that gets us back to

\mathrm{B}_{j}=\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j}
(which will require
l_{\mathrm{n}}\geq l_{\mathrm{B}}
), it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon=\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\sum_{i}(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)\approx\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\sum_{i}\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon).

(13)

This approach mirrors visibility gridding. If
\mathrm{A}_{i}
were delta
functions identifying positions of visibilities (oversampled at a
rate of
2l_{\Upsilon}
), then
\mathrm{n}_{j}
would be the gridding kernel,
\mathrm{m}_{i}
the
grid environment around each visibility, and
\mathrm{b}_{j}
the gridding
correction. What we are doing is simply ‘bulk re-gridding’ to
coarser facet grids.

This suggests that window functions like prolate spheroidal wave
functions (PSWFs; see
Fig. 2
) are a good choice
for
\mathrm{n}_{j}
.
Unfortunately, there is no window function that is perfectly limited
in both image and frequency space (uncertainty principle), so there will
always be errors. We can quantify the effects by subtracting both
sides from above for a subgrid
i
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast(1-\mathrm{m}_{i})(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)\approx 0.

(14)

Fig. 3
shows what these errors look like in image space
for a centred facet. Before convolution with
\mathrm{b}_{j}
(dark blue
line) the absolute error is mostly constant with small peaks at
\pm l_{\mathrm{n}}
. As the light blue line shows, this only becomes slightly worse
for a worst-case
\Upsilon
(single source at
-l_{\mathrm{n}}
).
On the other hand, convolution with
\mathrm{b}_{j}
introduces a distinct
U-shaped error pattern. The reason is that for
\mathrm{n}_{j}
to be
approximately limited in spatial-frequency space, it needs to be
smooth in image space, and as we require
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}
to fall to
zero, the inverse
|\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}|^{-1}
tends to infinity at

\pm l_{\mathrm{n}}
(see also
Fig. 2
). This is why we need to
somewhat over-dimension
\mathrm{n}_{j}
in image-space (
l_{\mathrm{n}}>l_{\mathrm{B}}
) to keep
error magnification in check.

2.4
Method

At this point we know that
\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
is approximately limited to an

u_{\mathrm{m}}
region in frequency space, so assuming
u_{\Upsilon}u_{\mathrm{m}}^{-1},4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}\in\mathbb{Z}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{l_{\mathrm{n}}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon

(15)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}_{i}\,\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{l_{\mathrm{n}}}\framebox{$\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)$}

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon

(16)

by associativity of multiplication. Therefore
\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
can
be represented approximately using
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}
samples (boxed
expression), solving the core challenge from
Sect. 2.2
.

If we define
\mathrm{n}_{j}^{0}
as a mask representing the
image-space extent of
\mathrm{n}_{j}
(
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}^{0}(l)=1
exactly
where
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}(l)\neq 0
), we can also show how to compute this
contribution efficiently,

\displaystyle\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\left(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\Upsilon}}{2u_{\Upsilon}^{2}l_{\mathrm{n}}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j})\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\mathrm{n}_{j}^{0}\ast\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon),

(17)

as
\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{m}}}{u_{\Upsilon}}\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}
,

\mathrm{n}_{j}=\frac{1}{2u_{\Upsilon}}\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j}^{0}
, and
\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\mathrm{f}\ast\mathrm{g})=\frac{l_{\Upsilon}}{l_{\mathrm{n}}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{f}\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{g}
, where

\mathrm{f}
and
\mathrm{g}
are zero in image space outside a
2l_{\mathrm{n}}

region. This means that if we have padded subgrid data
\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon
(
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\Upsilon}
samples;
u_{\mathrm{m}}>u_{\mathrm{A}}
) in image space, then we just need to
select
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}
samples where we know
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}(l)\neq 0
, multiply by

\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}_{j}
(sampled at the same points), and we have calculated our
subgrid contribution representation.

Applying the contribution is equally straightforward:

\displaystyle\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\sum_{i}(\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon){}

\displaystyle\approx\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\sum_{i}\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}_{i}\,\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{l_{\mathrm{n}}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\ast\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)

(18)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{l_{\Upsilon}}{l_{\mathrm{n}}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{b}_{j}\!\ast\!\sum_{i}(\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{m}_{i})\;\framebox{$\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\Sh_{2u_{\mathrm{m}}}\!\ast\!\mathrm{n}_{j}\!\ast\!\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon)$}

(19)

substituting in the term from
Eq. 16
, then using

\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}=\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}
and

\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}(\mathrm{f}\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{g})=\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{f}\ast\Sh_{(2l_{\mathrm{n}})^{\shortminus 1}}\mathrm{g}
, where
\mathrm{f}
is zero in image space outside a
2l_{\mathrm{n}}

region. This means that we
need to pad in frequency space from
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}
to
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\Upsilon}
samples, then
multiply the sum in image space by an equivalently sampled
\mathrm{b}_{j}
.

2.6
Two dimensions

We can easily generalise our reasoning to images simply by
re-defining
\Upsilon,\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B},\mathrm{m},\mathrm{b}
, and
\mathrm{n}
to be two-dimensional
functions. We additionally
assume
\mathrm{m}_{i}
and
\mathrm{b}_{j}
to be separable,

\displaystyle\mathrm{m}_{i}=\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i},\quad\mathrm{b}_{j}=\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}_{j}\ast\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j},

(25)

such that
\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}
and
\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}
are constant along the v- and u-axes, respectively, and
\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}(u,v)=0
for
v\neq 0
and

\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}(u,v)=0
for
u\neq 0
. Then we can re-order as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\approx\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}_{j}\ast\sum_{i}\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}\left(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i}\big(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\big)\right).

(26)

Convolution with
\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}
straight after multiplication with
\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i}

is a small optimisation. An implementation can discard
4u_{\Upsilon}(l_{\mathrm{n}}-l_{\mathrm{B}})
rows where

\mathcal{F}\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}
is zero by doing the Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}^{(v)}
along the
v
-axis right away. The sum can be pulled further inwards if
we assume that some subgrids are in the same column (i.e. share a
certain value of
\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}
). Then with
M^{\text{(u)}}
the set of all
\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\approx\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}_{j}\ast\sum_{\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}\in M^{\text{(u)}}}\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}\Big(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}\ast\sum_{i,\,\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}=\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{\phantom{i}}}\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i}\big(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\big)\Big).

(27)

As illustrated in
Fig.
4
a
, this means we can accumulate contributions from
entire columns of subgrids, reducing the number of Fourier transforms
needed.

2.7
Dual variant

As degridding relates to gridding, there is a similar dual
algorithm variant for going from facets to subgrids. Analogously to the steps taken in

Sect. 2.1
, we start with the observation that

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon=\mathrm{A}_{i}\,\Big(\sum_{j}\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\Big)=\sum_{j}\mathrm{A}_{i}\left(\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\right),

(28)

and we then decompose
\mathrm{B}_{j}=\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{b}_{j}
and

\mathrm{A}_{i}=\mathrm{A}_{i}\mathrm{m}_{i}
as done in

to approximate the sum term

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\left(\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\right)=\mathrm{A}_{i}\mathrm{m}_{i}\left(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\right)\approx\mathrm{A}_{i}\left(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\Upsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\approx\mathrm{A}_{i}\sum_{j}\left(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i}\left(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}\left(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\right)\right)\right).

The optimisation from the previous section also has an equivalent here:

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\Upsilon\approx\mathrm{A}_{i}\sum_{j}\left(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{T}_{j,\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}_{i}}\right),\quad\mathrm{T}_{j,\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}}=\mathrm{m}^{\text{(v)}}_{i}\left(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(v)}}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}^{\text{(u)}}\left(\mathrm{b}^{\text{(u)}}_{j}\ast\Upsilon\right)\right).

So we only need to calculate

3
Handling widefield effects

A general-purpose discrete Fourier transform
algorithm cannot quite cover the full complexity of the measurement equation from

Sect. 2
as wide fields of view require correction for telescope non-coplanarity (
w\neq 0
) / sky curvature (
\mathrm{n}\neq 1
).
As noted by
Cornwell et al. 2008
, we can represent this
using a convolution with a
w
-specific Fresnel diffraction pattern:

\displaystyle\mathrm{g}_{w}=\mathcal{F}_{l,m}^{-1}[e^{2\pi w(\sqrt{1-l^{2}-m^{2}}-1)}].

(32)

This convolution effectively introduces a third dimension into the problem, but
only on the spatial frequency side. To represent this, we modify our subgrid
definition such that each subgrid
i
has an arbitrary
w
-offset
\hat{w}_{i}

associated with it:

\displaystyle A_{i}(\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}\ast\Upsilon).

(33)

In this section we consider the problem variant of generating such
w
-shifted
subgrids from facets, or vice versa. We note that even with
w
-offsets,
satisfying
\sum_{i}\mathrm{A}_{i}(\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}\ast\Upsilon)=\Upsilon
(compare

Sect. 2.2
) is still possible by weighting
\mathrm{A}_{i}
. This is well
understood in radio astronomy, but is a distraction for the purpose of this
paper, so we stick to the dual algorithm (facet to subgrid; see

Sect. 2.7
) for this section.

3.1
Implementation options

If we simply replace
\Upsilon
by
(\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}\!\ast\!\Upsilon)
in our subgrid formula
from
Sect. 2.7
we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}(\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}\ast\Upsilon)\approx\mathrm{A}_{i}\sum_{j}\left(\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}\ast\Upsilon)\right).

(34)

So the obvious way to obtain
w
-shifted subgrids with our algorithm is to
multiply facets by
\mathcal{F}\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}
, which is known as
w
-stacking

(
Offringa et al. 2014
, e.g.)
. This is simple and precise no matter the
field of view or value of
|\hat{w}_{i}|
.

On the other hand, this forces us to repeat the entire data re-distribution for
every needed value of
\hat{w}_{i}
. Sharing intermediate subgrid column buffers, as
shown in
Fig.
4
, would now
especially require
\hat{u}_{i}
and
\hat{w}_{i}
to match. We can still minimise the
amount of subgrid data to reproduce for any given
\hat{w}_{i}
level by skipping
generation of unneeded subgrids, yet this is still not particularly efficient.

Fortunately we have additional options.
After all, we can treat
\mathrm{B}^{\prime}_{j,\hat{w}_{i}}=\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}
like a modified facet mask, which means that we
can decompose, as in
Sect. 2.3
, into

\mathrm{B}^{\prime}_{j,\hat{w}_{i}}=\mathrm{n}^{\prime}_{j,\hat{w}_{i}}\ast\mathrm{b}_{j}
using
a modified window function
\mathrm{n}^{\prime}_{j,\hat{w}_{i}}=\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}}
.
This yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\sum_{j}\left((\mathrm{B}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}})\ast\Upsilon)\right)\approx\mathrm{A}_{i}\sum_{j}\left((\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}})\ast\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\Upsilon)\right).

(35)

This is attractive because
\mathrm{m}_{i}(\mathrm{b}_{j}\ast\Upsilon)
is the
distributed contribution term, so this introduces

w
-shifts after data re-distribution and all image-size FFTs. However, this
can only work as long as

\mathrm{n}^{\prime}_{j,\hat{w}_{i}}
still acts
like a window function for our purposes:

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{i}\left((\mathrm{n}_{j}\ast\mathrm{g}_{\hat{w}_{i}})\ast(1-\mathrm{m}_{i})\right)\approx 0.

(36)

So for this approach to hold up, we must now identify masks
\mathrm{m}_{i}

compatible not only with
\mathrm{A}_{i}
and all
\mathrm{n}_{j}
, but also given

\hat{w}_{i}
.

Fortunately, it is not unreasonable to expect

Now we can use the fact that

and

3.4
Optimisation of
w
-distribution

The number of subgrids or
w
-tower storeys to generate is still a major cost
factor, so optimising the visibility
uvw
distribution for a minimum
w
-range
is highly desirable. For instance, we can use a shear transformation as
proposed in
Cornwell et al. 2012
to transform visibility
(u,v,w)

coordinates to
(u,v,w^{\prime})
as follows:

\displaystyle w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=w-h_{u}u-h_{v}v,

(39)

\displaystyle l^{\prime}

\displaystyle=l+h_{u}n=l+h_{u}(\sqrt{1-l^{2}-m^{2}}-1),

(40)

\displaystyle m^{\prime}

\displaystyle=m+h_{v}n=m+h_{v}(\sqrt{1-l^{2}-m^{2}}-1).

(41)

This allows us to choose
h_{u}
and
h_{v}
to minimise
|w^{\prime}|
, while
solving the same problem due to
ul+vm+wn=ul^{\prime}+vm^{\prime}+w^{\prime}n
.
We still have
n=\sqrt{1-l^{2}-m^{2}}-1
, and therefore the definition
of
\mathrm{g}_{w}
from
Sect. 3
remains unchanged, except that we now
sample in
l^{\prime}
and
m^{\prime}
for the purpose of
w
-stacking or
w
-towers.
Using computer algebra we can solve for the original
l
and
m
:

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=\frac{l^{\prime}(1+h_{\text{v}}^{2})+h_{\text{u}}(1-m^{\prime}h_{\text{v}}-\sqrt{c})}{h_{\text{u}}^{2}+h_{\text{v}}^{2}+1},

(42)

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\frac{m^{\prime}(1+h_{\text{u}}^{2})+h_{\text{v}}(1-l^{\prime}h_{\text{u}}-\sqrt{c})}{h_{\text{u}}^{2}+h_{\text{v}}^{2}+1},

(43)

\displaystyle\text{where }c

\displaystyle=1-2l^{\prime}h_{\text{u}}-2m^{\prime}h_{\text{v}}+2l^{\prime}h_{\text{u}}m^{\prime}h_{\text{v}}

\displaystyle\qquad-l^{\prime 2}\big(h_{\text{v}}^{2}+1\big)-m^{\prime 2}\big(h_{\text{u}}^{2}+1\big).

To first order
l\approx l^{\prime}
and
m\approx m^{\prime}
, which means that this
transformation will change neither
l_{\text{max}}
nor the required
subgrid margin
u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}\mathrm{n}}
from
Sect. 3.2
significantly until
quite close to the horizon, so there is little efficiency loss
associated with this optimisation. This in contrast to the variant described
in
Offringa et al. 2014
, where flat offsets in
l
and
m

directly increase both
l_{\text{max}}
and
u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}\mathrm{n}}
, reducing the maximum height of
w
-towers
and therefore efficiency much more rapidly.

Gridding typically uses spatial-frequency space
convolutions, which means that just as when we split

in
Sect. 2.3

\mathrm{n}_{j}

In practice, gridding efficiency depends greatly on how finely

allows us to adjust both parameters easily. Due to linearity

increase the image size and therefore spatial-frequency sampling rate

,
while only covering a given
l_{\text{FoV}}
area with facets
(see
8(b)
).
Extra
w
-tower storeys are also
easily added. On the other hand, either measure will increase computation,
leading to a trade-off against
gridding complexity.

In addition to data sizes, the other fundamental parameter is what level of approximation
we are willing to accept. As established in
Sect. 2.3
, this
is mostly determined by two factors.
The first is the base error level, which effectively derives from the PSWF parameter

W=4u_{\mathrm{n}}l_{\mathrm{n}}
. From this we can especially derive
4u_{\mathrm{n}}l_{\Upsilon}
, the
minimum subgrid margin size.
The second factor is the base error magnification due to
\mathrm{b}
, which depends
directly on
l_{\mathrm{B}}l_{\mathrm{n}}^{-1}
(i.e. how much facet space we are willing to
sacrifice).

We know that the worst-case error magnification happens at the facet
borders, and therefore we just need to determine how large we can make

l_{\mathrm{B}}
until the base error multiplied by
|\mathcal{F}\mathrm{n}(l_{\mathrm{B}})|^{-1}

becomes larger than the acceptable error level (compare

Fig. 2
). This means that for any target error, we
have a range of PSWF parameters
W
to choose from, as illustrated in

Fig. 9
.

Facet–subgrid contributions have size
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}
, yet effectively
sample only
4u_{\mathrm{A}}l_{\mathrm{B}}
points of image–frequency space. Therefore, we
can calculate an upper bound on communication efficiency:

\displaystyle\text{efficiency}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{A}}l_{\mathrm{B}}}{u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}}\quad\leq\frac{(u_{\mathrm{m}}-u_{\mathrm{n}})l_{\mathrm{B}}}{u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}}

(45)

This already shows a fundamental trade-off between facet and
subgrid margins, as shown in
Fig. 10
(top,
dotted). For larger
W
we have to give up subgrid area, whereas for
small
W
we lose facet area to compensate for the worse base error.

For a measure of
w
-tower efficiency, we observe that in

Sect. 3.2
there is to first order a linear dependency
between maximum tower size
|w|
and the effective margin reserved for
the
w
-term
u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}}^{\prime}=u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}\mathrm{n}}-u_{\mathrm{n}}
. Therefore, we can use a

l^{\prime}_{\text{max}}
-normalised
uvw
volume as an efficiency measure
subject to
u_{\mathrm{A}}\leq u_{\mathrm{m}}-u_{\mathrm{n}}-u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\text{$w$-tower efficiency}=\frac{u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}}^{\prime}}{u_{\mathrm{m}}}\left(\frac{u_{\mathrm{A}}l_{\mathrm{B}}}{u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\mathrm{n}}}\right)^{2}\quad\leq\frac{4(u_{\mathrm{m}}-u_{\mathrm{n}})^{3}l_{\mathrm{B}}^{2}}{27u_{\mathrm{m}}^{3}l_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}}.

(46)

Optimally
u_{\mathrm{g}_{w}}^{\prime}\approx\frac{1}{3}(u_{\mathrm{m}}-u_{\mathrm{n}})
, which again yields an upper bound, as shown in

Fig. 10
(bottom, dotted). As should be
expected, larger
W
are somewhat less efficient here as the
PSWF and
\mathrm{g}_{w}
compete for limited subgrid space.

The reasoning about computational complexity is more complex in
this case. To determine the number of
w
-stacking planes, we first note that
scaling
l_{\Upsilon}
means scaling frequency-space resolution; therefore, a given
subgrid size
4u_{\mathrm{m}}l_{\Upsilon}
and its margins
4(u_{\mathrm{m}}-u_{\mathrm{A}})l_{\Upsilon}
now cover less
frequency space. This means that for the purpose of the discussion in

Sect. 3.2

field of view and therefore
l_{\text{max}}
increases. It follows that the
number of required
w
-stacking planes increases as
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{2})
to first order. As each plane is also
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{2})
larger,
the total facet Fourier transform complexity growth is about
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{4})
.
The cost for subgrid Fourier transforms will simply
increase with the number of subgrids we need to produce. As the
subgrids cover less spatial-frequency space each, we need to increase their number by
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{2})

to cover the telescope layout in
uv
. However, to implement (de-)gridding (e.g.
Ye et al. 2022
) we also need to decrease the
w
distance between

w
-tower planes as the image curvature
n\sim l_{\text{fov}}^{2}

increases. The result is an overall scaling of
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{4})

again. Interestingly enough, this means that the number
of storeys in a
w
-tower is constant. The tower layout shrinks linearly
along the
u
- and
v
-axes, and about quadratically along the
w
-axis.

As
Fig. 11
shows, the experiments again demonstrate slightly
better than expected scaling behaviour; facet work grows super-cubically
once we get to larger image sizes, but the subgrid Fourier transform work and
data transfer volume stay at or slightly below
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{3})
. This is because a
larger number of fine-grained subgrids and
w
-towers allow us to more closely
match the baseline distribution. In a way we are observing the fractal
dimension of the telescope layout. We note that existing widefield imaging
algorithms would show the same
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{4})
scaling (e.g. using
constant-size facets would see a
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{2})
growth both in the number of
visibilities and facet count for a combined scaling of
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{4})
of
phase rotation cost growth).

5.3
Communication

In the largest configuration we are dealing with an image of size 196 608
2
,
which at double precision translates to about 618.5 GB of data. To ensure
the accuracy of the gridder we leave 18.75 % margins, which means that the
effective field of view size corresponds to about 408.3 GB of data. The results in

Table 2
show that 997.0 GB of communication were exchanged,
so a bit more than twice the size of the information-containing part of the
image.

This is quite efficient, as the configuration has 33
w
-stacking planes, with

w
-towers decomposing them into 154 subplanes each, so we are effectively
sampling a
uvw
grid
33\cdot 154\cdot 618.5\,\text{GB}=2.6\,\text{EB}
in
size. We can also contrast against the visibility volume of 42.3 TB, which
puts the network overhead at about 1.8 % relative to the throughput required
to load visibilities from distributed storage.

Fig. 12
shows the run-time performance of tests in

runs of the

(
Arras et al. 2021
)
. The
ducc

class of degridding algorithm
(
Ye et al. 2022
)
and is in
regular scientific use as a (de)gridding kernel for the

(
Offringa et al. 2014
)
. For this

Our implementation significantly outperforms
ducc
in raw

approach. There are a number of possible explanations. Our kernel was
hand-optimised for AVX2, uses a pre-tabulated kernel, and also
convolves across all

-tower storeys in one go. Meanwhile,

ducc
has a more generic implementation that generates the
convolution kernel on the fly. This calculation even has to be
repeated multiple times per visibility, due to the high memory pressure
of

-stacking. Our implementation likely further benefits from
executing degridding and
w
-towers FFTs in parallel as these
workloads have very different operational intensity, and therefore work
well with hyper-threading.

For the traditional
w
-stacking method used by
ducc
, FFT work dominates
past images sizes of 32 768
2
due to unmitigated
O(l_{\Upsilon}^{4})
complexity
growth (see
Sect. 5.2
, green dashed line). In comparison, the

w
-towers approach together with the shear transformation from

Sect. 3.4
substantially blunt the impact of larger fields of
view in terms of number and size of required FFTs (green dotted line). Time
spent on FFTs still eventually overtakes degridding despite the lower operation
count (compare
Fig. 11
) due to lower operational intensity.

Finally, we consider the cost of distribution, represented through the
facet FFT costs (orange lines) and scheduling inefficiencies (violet
lines). The former are basically insignificant, while the latter
eventually start dominating. This is so because while there is an
abundance of parallel tasks, they vary greatly in terms of
computational and communication complexity. This makes
saturating 4864 threads quite challenging in practice.

Our simple scheduling algorithm approaches this by alternating ‘expensive’
(many visibilities) and ‘cheap’ (few visibilities)
w
-stacking planes to
balance FFT and degridding work as evenly as possible throughout. It then
dynamically schedules subgrid tasks to subgrid workers from a pre-populated work
queue. For the most densely populated subgrids it splits the degridding work
further to allow multiple subgrid workers to work on them in parallel.
Queue sizes were optimised for the large-scale case, and kept constant for all
test runs. This also explains why small configurations sometimes have worse
scheduling efficiency, as due to the small number of subgrids work is scheduled
before imbalances can become apparent. As a result, some workers end up running idle
towards the end of the run. It is clear that effective work scheduling
is one of the main remaining challenges with this algorithm.

6
Conclusion

We have presented a scalable way to distribute interferometric
imaging calculations by using spatial distribution and window
functions. This results in a streaming widefield Fourier transform
algorithm that enables interferometry imaging to be parallelised to
thousands of distributed threads, while taking full advantage of both
image and
uvw
sparseness to reduce transfer and compute costs.

6.1
Future improvements

There are still a number of improvements and generalisations that can be made to
the algorithm. Centring contribution terms as in
Sect. 2.5
is
not actually necessary, and removing the
\hat{u}_{i}\hat{l}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
side
condition allows more flexible subgrid and facet placement. Additional
efficiency could be gained by combining different parameters within the same
imaging task (e.g. adjust subgrid size per
w
-level), or even introducing
additional layers between
w
-stacking and
w
-towers on either side of the
distribution pattern (for dealing with very large images). There is good reason
to believe that such techniques could improve performance further.

6.2
Implementation

Despite our efforts, the MPI implementation
(
Wortmann 2023
)
is still severely limited by
its primitive work scheduling. A more sophisticated work-stealing mechanism
will likely be required as data flow predictability will only get worse from
here. For instance,
(de-)gridding
from and to subgrids could be tackled by
accelerators, and storage might struggle to keep pace with visibility data
flows: processing
\sim
40 TB in under 4 min means 10 GB/s/node,
with non-obvious access patterns.

Additionally, to integrate calibration as well as corrections for various
instrumental and environmental effects, our algorithm will have to work in
concert with other state-of-the-art radio astronomy algorithms. These methods
come with significant complexity of their own, which makes integration even
trickier. We are planning to investigate use of a
specialised execution framework to take over the data transfer and task
scheduling functions of the prototype code.

6.3
Imaging and calibration

Transformations between the sky and aperture planes are common and well-defined
operations in radio-astronomy pipelines, which means that the presented algorithm can help distribute
a variety of functions relevant to radio astronomy. In
Sect. 3.5
we
discuss how it can work with a variety of gridding approaches; extensions
to cover polarisation, Taylor terms, or point-spread function generation are
straightforward. Furthermore, as every subgrid gets represented in image space along the way, we
can introduce slow-changing image-space multipliers (e.g. corrections for primary beam effects) very cheaply and at good
resolution. This might complement approaches that deal with fast-changing direction-dependent effects such as
IDG
(
van der Tol et al. 2018
)
.

On the other hand, pipelines that require traversing visibilities in a specific ordering
may need substantial rework to make use of the presented algorithm
because much of its efficiency derives from traversing the grid in a
very particular order: iterating over
w
-stacking planes, then over

u
-columns, and finally visiting individual subgrids. This is not an issue
for a CLEAN major loop iteration that predicts
visibilities to be subtracted for an imaging step; the forward and
backward directions of the algorithm can be combined so that
processing proceeds from a model image to the current residual dirty
image, while touching every observed visibility exactly once.
Combined with a distributed by-facet image-based ‘minor loop’
deconvolution step, this should yield a scalable CLEAN pipeline.

The presented algorithm is however a worse fit for standard antenna-based
calibration approaches as they require combining data from all baselines to
reliably solve for antenna-based gain terms. The algorithm is designed
such that visibilities from different
w
-stacking planes would basically never
meet in the same memory space. A possible solution would be to reduce the size
of the calibration problem representation enough so that we could make progress even
with an incomplete
view
(
Yatawatta 2015
, e.g.)
. Alternatively, one might
attempt to devise a distributed algorithm that re-aggregates optimisation
problems, similarly to how this distributed Fourier transform algorithm
re-assembles a complete image.

6.4
Outlook

The presented algorithm significantly improves on existing
radio-astronomy imaging algorithms by enabling the distribution of both
the computation and the working memory load. This enables imaging in
less wall-clock time by distribution over relatively small
general-purpose (and therefore cost effective) nodes. Reducing
wall-clock time to process data sets is a key requirement for
telescopes like SKA where storage of visibilities during processing is
a major cost driver.
