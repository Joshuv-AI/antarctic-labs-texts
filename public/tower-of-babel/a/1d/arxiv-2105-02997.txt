A Note on the Convolution of Circle Impulses
Brad Osgood

Abstract
We present a formula for the convolution δ C 1 ∗ δ C 2 \delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}} of two circle impulses. The derivation uses a classical addition theorem for Bessel functions.



I
INTRODUCTION and MAIN RESULT

The purpose of this short note is to derive a formula for the convolution of two circle impulses. To my knowledge such a result has not appeared. Appealing aspects of the derivation are the use of an old addition formula for Bessel functions, and at one point the algebra is rather remarkable. We proceed with the formula.

For
i=1,2
let
C_{i}
be the circle
\|x-b_{i}\|=R_{i}
centered at
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with radius
R_{i}
. Let
\delta_{C_{i}}
be the impulse supported on
C_{i}
(definition below) and let
\rho=\|x-(b_{1}+b_{2})\|
. The main result is that convolution results in the function

\small{(\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}})(\rho)={\frac{4R_{1}R_{2}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{2}-(R_{1}-R_{2})^{2})((R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}-\rho^{2}))}}}},

(1)

if
|R_{1}-R_{2}|<\rho<R_{1}+R_{2}
, and
(\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}})(\rho)=0
if
0<\rho<|R_{1}-R_{2}|
or
\rho>R_{1}+R_{2}
.
In particular, the formula expresses the fact that
\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}}
is radial with respect to the point
b_{1}+b_{2}
. It is also symmetric in
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
as it should be.

II
DEFINITIONS and BACKGROUND

One can define a circle impulse
\delta_{C}
in several equivalent ways: via a limiting process, as for example in
[
1
]
, or directly as a distribution paired with a smooth test function
\varphi
that is of compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The pairing is

\langle\delta_{C},\varphi\rangle=\int_{C}\varphi\,ds,

(2)

where
ds
is arclength along
C
.

II-A

Use of Pullbacks

It is useful for definitions and formulas to employ pullbacks of functions and distributions, see
[
2
]
for the general setup and
[
3
]
for special cases. For the circle
\|x-b\|=R
, the formula (
2
) in fact comes from the definition
of
\delta_{C}
as the pullback,
\delta_{C}=g^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}\delta
, of the usual one-dimensional point
\delta
by
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
g(x)=\|x-b\|-R
. Keeping to the custom of evaluating
\delta
’s at points, this is usually expressed as
\delta_{C}(x_{1},x_{2})=\delta(g(x_{1},x_{2}))
. Note that
\delta_{C}
and subsequent formulas depend on the choice of the function
g
describing
C
.
Observe that for pullback we write
g^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}
instead of the more common
g^{*}
so as not to clash, inevitably, with
*
used for convolution.

Let
\tau_{b}(x)=x-b
be the shift operator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is easy to show that if
C
is the circle
\|x\|=R
then
\tau_{b}^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}\delta_{C}
is the circle impulse for
\|x-b\|=R
. Moreover,
as a shift of the convolution is the convolution of the shifts, if
C_{i}
,
i=1,2
, are the circles
\|x\|=R_{i}
we have

(\tau_{b_{1}+b_{2}})^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}(\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}})=\tau_{b_{1}}^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}\delta_{C_{1}}*\tau_{b_{2}}^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}\delta_{C_{2}},

(3)

so it suffices to prove (
1
) for circles concentric to the origin.

II-B

Radial Functions

To express a function (distribution)
f
in polar coordinates is to form
\tilde{f}(r,\theta)=(P^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}\!f)(r,\theta)
using the polar coordinate mapping
P(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
. A function (distribution) is radial if
A^{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.65}{$\displaystyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\textstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptstyle<$}}{\scalebox{0.65}{$\scriptscriptstyle<$}}}f=f
for any orthogonal transformation
A
, in which case we write the polar coordinate version simply as
\tilde{f}(r)
. The convolution of two radial functions (distributions) is radial and
\delta_{C}
is radial for a circle centered at the origin. The Fourier transform of a radial function is also radial and

(\mathcal{F}f)^{\sim}(r)=\mathcal{H}\tilde{f}(r)=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\tilde{f}(\rho)J_{0}(2\pi r\rho)\,\rho d\rho.

Here,
J_{0}
is the zeroth order Bessel function of the first kind and
\mathcal{H}
is the Hankel transform.

For the circle
C
,
\|x\|=R
, the Fourier transform of
\delta_{C}
is the radial function

(\mathcal{F}\delta_{C})^{\sim}(r)=2\pi RJ_{0}(2\pi rR)
.
This is a standard result. We can write it as

\mathcal{H}\delta_{C}^{\sim}(r)=2\pi RJ_{0}(2\pi rR)

See
[
4
]
for a general discussion of radial distributions.

III
CONVOLUTION and the NEUMANN ADDITION FORMULA

We consider the convolution
\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}}
for the concentric circles
\|x\|=R_{i}
. Invoking the convolution theorem and then expressing the result in polar coordinates, we have
(\mathcal{F}(\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}}))^{\sim}=(\mathcal{F}\delta_{C_{1}})^{\sim}(\mathcal{F}\delta_{C_{2}})^{\sim}
, or

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\delta_{C_{1}}

\displaystyle*\delta_{C_{2}})^{\sim}(r)=\mathcal{H}\delta^{\sim}_{C_{1}}(r)\mathcal{H}\delta^{\sim}_{C_{2}}(r)

(4)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}R_{1}R_{2}J_{0}(2\pi R_{1}r)J_{0}(2\pi R_{2}r).

The Hankel transform is its own inverse, so we want to find
\mathcal{H}
applied to the right-hand side.

An old result on the product of two Bessel functions, a consequence of the Neumann addition formula, comes to our aid. In the present setting it reads

\displaystyle J_{0}(2\pi R_{1}r)J_{0}(2\pi R_{2}r)

\displaystyle=

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{0}((R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-2R_{1}R_{2}

\displaystyle\cos\theta)^{1/2}2\pi r)\,d\theta.

See
[
5
]
, Section 11.2.
Temporarily drop the factor
(2\pi)^{2}R_{1}R_{2}
in (
4
), and to further save space let

\Psi(\theta)=(R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-2R_{1}R_{2}\cos\theta)^{1/2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{0}(\Psi(\theta)2\pi r)\,d\theta\right)(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{0}(2\pi r\rho)

\displaystyle J_{0}(\Psi(\theta)2\pi r)\,rd\theta dr

Interchange the order of integration and for the inner integral appeal to the “closure equation:”

\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{0}(kr)J_{0}(k^{\prime}r)\,rdr=\frac{1}{k}\delta(k-k^{\prime}),\quad k,k^{\prime}>0.

(The classical version of this, not in terms of
\delta
, goes back to
Hankel, see
[
5
]
, Section 14.4.) Then,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}J_{0}(2\pi\rho r)J_{0}(2\pi\Psi(\theta)r)\,rdr

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\rho}\delta(2\pi\rho-2\pi\Psi(\theta))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}\rho}\delta(\rho-\Psi(\theta)).

Finally, put the factor
(2\pi)^{2}R_{1}R_{2}
back to obtain

(\delta_{C_{1}}*\delta_{C_{2}})^{\sim}(\rho)=\frac{R_{1}R_{2}}{\rho}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\delta(\rho-\Psi(\theta))\,d\theta.

(6)

III-A

\delta
and Zeros

We can further analyze the right-hand side of (
6
) using the well known formula

\delta(\Phi(\theta))=\sum_{n}\frac{\delta(\theta-\theta_{n})}{|\Phi^{\prime}(\theta_{n})|},

(7)

where

\Phi(\theta)=\rho-\Psi(\theta)

and the
\theta_{n}
are the zeros of
\Phi(\theta)
. This may be derived via pullbacks, or by other means, see, e.g.,
[
3
]
. The formula holds so long as
\Phi^{\prime}(\theta_{n})\neq 0
. The derivative is

\Phi^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{R_{1}R_{2}\sin\theta}{(R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-2R_{1}R_{2}\cos\theta)^{1/2}}.

(8)

This is zero at
\theta=0,\pi
and
2\pi
, and only there, and we will exclude these points by restricting
\rho
.

The minimum of
\Psi(\theta)=(R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-2R_{1}R_{2}\cos\theta)^{1/2}
occurs for
\theta=0
and
\theta=2\pi
and has the value
|R_{1}-R_{2}|
. The maximum occurs at
\theta=\pi
and has the value
R_{1}+R_{2}
. Thus if we restrict
\rho
to

|R_{1}-R_{2}|<\rho<R_{1}+R_{2},

(9)

then within this range
\Phi(\theta)
has two zeros with nonzero derivatives, say at
0<\theta_{1}<\pi
and,
\pi<\theta_{2}<2\pi
. Evidently
\theta_{2}=2\pi-\theta_{1}
and the derivatives have equal absolute value. Strictly outside this range of
\rho
, either
0<\rho<|R_{1}-R_{2}|
or
\rho>R_{1}+R_{2}
, there are no zeros and
\delta(\Phi(\theta))=0
.

Now,
\Phi(\theta)=0
when

\cos\theta=\frac{R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}}{2R_{1}R_{2}}.

(10)

At such a value,

\Phi^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}=\left(\frac{R_{1}R_{2}}{\rho}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\theta=\left(\frac{R_{1}R_{2}}{\rho}\right)^{2}(1-\cos^{2}\theta),

and we can use (
10
) to write

\Phi^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}=\left(\frac{R_{1}R_{2}}{\rho}\right)^{2}\left(1-\left(\frac{R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-\rho^{2}}{2R_{1}R_{2}}\right)^{2}\right).

This simplifies (!) to

\Phi^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}=\frac{1}{4\rho^{2}}(\rho^{2}-(R_{1}-R_{2})^{2})((R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}-\rho^{2}).

Note how directly the condition (
9
) comes into play.

Going back to (
7
), again denoting the two zeros by
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}=2\pi-\theta_{1}
we have

|\Phi^{\prime}(\theta_{1})|=|\Phi^{\prime}(\theta_{2})|
and

\displaystyle\delta(\Phi(

\displaystyle\theta))=\frac{2\rho(\delta(\theta-\theta_{1})+\delta(\theta-\theta_{2}))}{\sqrt{(\rho^{2}-(R_{1}-R_{2})^{2})((R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}-\rho^{2}))}},

from which

\displaystyle\frac{R_{1}R_{2}}{\rho}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\delta(\Phi(\theta))\,d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4R_{1}R_{2}}{\sqrt{(\rho^{2}-(R_{1}-R_{2})^{2})((R_{1}+R_{2})^{2}-\rho^{2}))}}.

This is (
1
) for circles concentric at the origin. The general result follows from this as in (
3
)

IV
Additional Formulas

Finally, it is natural to inquire about two other operations with impulses, namely multiplication and convolution with a function. Let
C
be the circle
\|x\|=R
. For a function
f
and for
x\neq 0
let
f_{C}(x)=f(Rx/\|x\|)
. Then

f\delta_{C}=f_{C}\delta_{C}.

(11)

Note that if
f
is radial then
f\delta_{C}=\tilde{f}(R)\delta_{C}
.

For convolution the result is

(f*\delta_{C})(x_{1},x_{2})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(x_{1}+R\cos\theta,x_{2}+R\sin\theta)\,Rd\theta.

(12)

In words, forming
(f*\delta_{C})(x)
replaces
f(x)
with an average of
f
on the circle centered at
x
of radius
R
. Thus
(f*\delta_{C})(x_{0})
depends only on the values of
f
on the circle
\|x-x_{0}\|=R
, a kind of “locally radializing” the function at each point.

The derivations of (
11
) and (
12
) are straightforward and we omit the details.
