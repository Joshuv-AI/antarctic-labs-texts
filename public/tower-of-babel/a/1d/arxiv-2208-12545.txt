Abstract
Multi-view representation learning is essential for many multi-view tasks, such as clustering and classification. However, there are two challenging problems plaguing the community: i)how to learn robust multi-view representations from mass unlabeled data and ii) how to balance the view consistency and specificity. To this end, in this paper, we proposed a novel hybrid contrastive fusion method to extract robust view-common representations from unlabeled data. Specifically, we found that introducing an additional representation space and aligning representations on this space enables the model to learn robust view-common representations. At the same time, we designed an asymmetric contrastive strategy to ensure that the model does not obtain trivial solutions. Experimental results demonstrated that the proposed method outperforms 12 competitive multi-view methods on four real-world datasets in terms of clustering and classification. Our source code will be available soon at https://github.com/guanzhou-ke/mori-ran .

1.

We proposed a novel hybrid contrastive fusion method, called MORI-RAN, which can balance the consistency and specificity of views by an additional alignment representation space. Moreover, the ablation study and visualization results demonstrate that the proposed method can preserve the diversity among views without destroying the original representation space.

2.

We design an asymmetric representation contrastive strategy to prevent model collapse during the fusion process. The ablation experiment shows that our proposed fusion strategy could learn a more robust representation than the cross-view implicit fusion.

3.

We conduct experiments on four real-world datasets to measure the clustering and classification performance of MORI-RAN. Extensive experiments verify the effectiveness of the proposed fusion module.

II
Related Work

II-A

Multi-view Representation Learning

Multi-view representation learning aims to learn representations from multi-view data that facilitate extracting rich information for downstream tasks, such as clustering or classification. MRL methods can be roughly divided into two categories: alignment and fusion. The alignment approach focuses on capturing the relationships among multiple resource data, and the other aims to fuse the view-specific representation into a single compact (view-common) representation. The CCA-based methods
[
3
,
4
,
22
]
are representative of the alignment. These approaches explore the statistical properties between two views, searching for two projections to map the two views onto a low-dimensional view-common representation. Furthermore, some methods based on distance and similarity have been proposed
[
23
,
24
]
. These methods address the cross-view similarity learning problem by embedding multi-view data into a common metric space. Alignment-based approaches have achieved awe-inspiring success for tasks such as information extraction and recommender systems. This paper falls into multi-view fusion, and we will describe related works on fusion in the next part.

II-B

[

]
, the subspaces fusion
[
26

17

, or the weight-sum fusion
[

, we leverage the neural network as the fusion mapper. The benefit of neural networks is the ability to map representations to feature spaces of arbitrary shape. The literature on
[
15
,
12
]
employed the attention mechanism fusion technique. These methods require a long training time and a large amount of training data to converge. Some other methods
[

,
13
]
using contrastive fusion can alleviate these drawbacks, but literature
[
13
]
aligns directly on view-specific representations, which could destroy the diversity between views. The literature
[
11

drives network training based on clustering, which is less compatible for various downstream tasks.

III
Method

Our goal is to learn a set of robust view-common representation
z
from
n
data points consisting of
V
views
\mathcal{D}=\{\textbf{X}^{1},\textbf{X}^{2},...,\textbf{X}^{V}\}
, where
\textbf{X}^{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{v}}
denotes the samples of the dimension
d_{v}
from the
v
-th views. In this work, we suggest a non-linear multi-view fusion method, i.e., hybrid contrastive fusion, to obtain view-common representations
\mathbb{z}
. In this section, we first describe the network architecture of MORI-RAN. Next, we introduce the hybrid contrastive fusion method and the asymmetrical contrastive strategy. Finally, we describe the objective function of MORI-RAN.

III-A

Networks Architecture

The proposed approach, MORI-RAN, consist of
V
view-specific encoder networks
e_{v}(\cdot)
, a fusion block
f(\cdot)
, and a hybrid contrastive module (HCM), as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

•

View-specific encoder networks
e_{v}(\cdot)
that extracts view-specific representations(vectors)
h
from each view data point, i.e.,
\mathbb{h^{v}}=e_{v}(\mathbb{X_{v}})
. MORI-RAN is model-agnostic, meaning that various forms of the network architecture can be treated as encoders, e.g., Fully-Connected Networks (FCNs) or Convolution Neural Networks (CNNs). For simplicity, we employ FCNs to build view-specific encoders.

•

A fusion block
f(\cdot)
that fusions concatenating vectors
\mathbb{\bar{h}}=[\mathbb{h^{1}},...,\mathbb{h^{v}}]
to obtain view-common representations
\mathbb{z}=f(\mathbb{\bar{h}})
, where
\mathbb{\bar{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(V\times d_{h})},\mathbb{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{h}}
. We build it with fully-connected layers with ReLU and employ the skip connection to connect the fusion block’s input and output. The major advantage of our fusion block is that it can map any complex data into a compatible representation space
[
27
]
.

•

The hybrid contrastive module (HCM) treats view-common representations
\mathbb{\bar{z}}
and view-specific representations
\mathbb{\bar{h}}
as inputs. It leverage instance-level contrastive method to maximize the mutual information between
\mathbb{\bar{z}}
and
\mathbb{\bar{h}}
with the asymmetrical contrastive strategy, reducing the view-redundant representations with class-level contrastive method. We describe the detail of HCM in the next part.

\mathbb{z}
and each
\mathbb{\bar{h}}
, reducing their category-agnostic information.

III-B

The Hybrid Contrastive Module and The Asymmetrical Strategy

We introduce an additional alignment space
\mathbb{z}
in the fusion block. Our motivation is that learns robust view-common representations
\mathbb{z}
. In other words, we desire to balance the view consistency and specificity. To achieve this, we design the hybrid contrastive module (HCM), in which we leverage the instance-level contrastive method to preserve view-specific information, i.e., we aim to maximize the mutual information between views, as shown in (
1
). Moreover, to maximize the view consistency, we optimize the consistency of the assignment of soft labels for view-specific representations
\mathbb{h}
and view-common representations
\mathbb{z}
using the class-level contrastive method
[
28
]
. We integrate and trade-off the instance-level and the class-level contrastive method into HCM, elaborating their objective functions in the next part.

\max I(\mathbb{z};\mathbb{h}^{v});\quad\text{where}\quad v=1,2,\cdots,V

(1)

However, in a multi-view setting, it is prone to model collapse if directly performed contrastive method between view-common representations
\mathbb{z}
and view-specific representations
\mathbb{h}
. It is because
\mathbb{z}=f([\mathbb{h}^{1},\cdots,\mathbb{h}^{v}])
, so a trivial solution is that f equals the constant mapping function, meaning that
\mathbb{z}
has not learned effective representations for downstream tasks.

To this end, we designed an asymmetrical contrastive strategy to prevent the model from obtaining trivial solutions. Concretely, unlike previous methods
[
5
,
6
,
13
]
, we disable the alignment between each view-specific representation h and allow only view-specific representations and the introduced view-common representation z to be aligned. The experimental result demonstrates that the model can learn robust representations when employing the asymmetric strategy only on the instance-level contrastive method.

III-C

The Objective Function

With the above definitions, we design the following objective function for MORI-RAN:

\mathcal{L}=\lambda_{1}\mathcal{L}_{ins}+\lambda_{2}\mathcal{L}_{cls}

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}_{ins}
and
\mathcal{L}_{cls}
are instance-level contrastive loss and class-level contrastive loss, respectively. The parameters
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are the trade-off factors for instance-level and class-level contrastive loss, respectively. According to our experimental results, we simply fix these two parameters to 1 and 0.5, respectively. We describe the detail of the objective function in the following.

III-C
1
The instance-level contrastive loss

In previous works
[
5
,
6
,
13
,
11
]
, various forms of contrastive loss have been proposed, such as InfoNCE-based
[
21
]
, momentum-based
[
20
]
, etc. However, all these works require a larger batch size or extra memory during training
[
29
]
. These limitations become bottlenecks for multi-view learning. Therefore, inspired by
[
30
]
, we extend the redundancy-reduction principle to the multi-view. We define the multi-view cross-correlation matrix as following:

\mathcal{C}_{ij}\triangleq\frac{\sum_{b}z_{b,i}h^{v}_{b,j}}{\sqrt{\sum_{b}(z_{b,i})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{b}(h^{v}_{b,j})^{2}}}\ \text{where}\ v=1,2,\cdots,V

(3)

where
b
is batch samples and
i,j
denotes the representation dimension of the networks’ outputs.
\mathcal{C}
is a square matrix of the same size as the dimension of the networks’ output, with values range
[-1,1]
. According to the asymmetric contrastive strategy, we fix the view-common representations
\mathbb{z}
and then compute the cross-correlation between
\mathbb{z}
and each view-specific representation
\mathbb{h}
. Finally, we compute the instance contrastive loss as the following:

\mathcal{L}_{ins}=\underbrace{\sum_{i}(1-\mathcal{C}_{ii})^{2}}_{\text{invariance term}}+\lambda_{ins}\underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{2}}_{\text{redundancy reduction term}}

(4)

where
\lambda_{ins}
indexes a positive constant trade-off factor. According to the original setting
[
30
]
, we set
\lambda_{ins}=5\times 10^{-3}
in our experiments.

III-C
2
The class-level contrastive loss

We require that the view-common representation
\mathbb{z}
and view-specific representations
\mathbb{h}^{v}
be consistent in terms of the soft label assignment. Therefore, we define the class-level contrastive loss as following:

\mathcal{L}_{cls}(\mathbb{z},\mathbb{h})=-\sum_{k}Q_{(k)}(\mathbb{z})\log P_{(k)}(\mathbb{z},\mathbb{h})

(5)

where
k
denotes the number of soft label, and
p(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined as the following:

P_{(k)}(\mathbb{z},\mathbb{h})=\frac{\exp(\mathbb{z}^{T}g_{(k)}(\mathbb{h})/\tau)}{\sum_{i\neq k}\exp(\mathbb{z}^{T}g_{(i)}(\mathbb{h})/\tau)}

(6)

where
g(\cdot)
denotes the soft label assignment network, with output
k
dimension vector. For simplicity, we employ a MLP to build
g(\cdot)
.
\tau
is a temperature parameter
[
31
]
, we set
\tau=0.07
in our experiments.

Furthermore, we employ the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
32
]
to compute the optimal soft label assignment of
\mathbb{z}
, the transportation plan as following:

Q_{(k)}(\mathbb{z}):=\{\mathbb{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times b}|\mathbb{T1}_{b}=\frac{1}{k}\mathbb{1}_{k},\mathbb{T}^{T}\mathbb{1}_{k}=\frac{1}{b}\mathbb{1}_{b}\}

(7)

where
\mathbb{T}
is the transportation matrix, and
\mathbb{1}
denotes the identity matrix.
b
is the size of mini-batch. According to the previous work’s conclusion
[
33
]
, we can obtain a discrete optimal solution
\mathbb{T}^{*}
by using a rounding procedure. Concretely, the algorithm

Implementation Details

We implement MORI-RAN and other non-linear comparison methods on the PyTorch 1.10

]

IV-C
1
Networks setting

For simplicity, we set the dimensionality of view-specific encoders to
I-1024-1024-1024-O
for all experiments, where

indicate the dimension of the data’s input and the encoder’s output, respectively. Concretely, we set

IV-C
2
Training

We use the Adam optimization technique
[
46
]
with default parameters and an initial learning rate of
0.0001
for both clustering and classification. For clustering, we treat the whole dataset as the training set and validation set, shuffling the training set during training. We train the model for 100 epochs and set the size of the mini-batch to 256 for all experiments. Specifically, we train the model 5 times with the different random seeds and report the result from the lowest value of Eq. (
2
). For classification, we split the dataset into a training set, a validation set, and a test set in the ratio

. We first train the network for 90 epochs without labels. Then, we leverage the method provied by

6

to generate the predicted label. Finally, we use the test set to evaluate the classification performance of MORI-RAN.

V
Discussion

Although our method achieves superior performance on four datasets, it does not make a significant margin with the second-best method on the image-text dataset. Therefore, we argue that MORI-RAN has some limitations in the multimodal downstream tasks. In this study, we have used some smaller or medium-scale datasets. However, more large-scale datasets have been released
[
49
,
50
]
, and we will try to extend MORI-RAN to fit super-large datasets in the future. It is worth noting that MORI-RAN is not a reconstruction-based approach to learning multi-view representations, and we claim that reconstruction is a challenging task. However, we have noticed some promising works
[
51
,
52
]
emerging recently, and we will try to balance the contrastive approach and the reconstruction approach in future work.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed a novel hybrid contrastive fusion method and an asymmetrical contrastive strategy for learning robust multi-view representation. This study introduced an additional alignment space to preserve the multi-view diversity and extract the multi-view shared information. The ablation studies demonstrated that the instance-level contrastive module with an asymmetrical strategy could preserve the multi-view diversity. The class-level contrastive module could make the view-common structure more separable and compact. Experiments on four datasets show that the proposed method performs the clustering and classification tasks well compared to the other 12 competitive methods.
