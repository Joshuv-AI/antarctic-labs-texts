A full approximation scheme multilevel method for nonlinear variational inequalities Thanks: Draft August 8, 2026.
Ed Bueler, Patrick E. Farrell

Abstract
We present the full approximation scheme constraint decomposition (FASCD) multilevel method for solving variational inequalities (VIs). FASCD is a common extension of both the full approximation scheme (FAS) multigrid technique for nonlinear partial differential equations, due to A. Brandt, and the constraint decomposition (CD) method introduced by X.-C. Tai for VIs arising in optimization. We extend the CD idea by exploiting the telescoping nature of certain function space subset decompositions arising from multilevel mesh hierarchies. When a reduced-space (active set) Newton method is applied as a smoother, with work proportional to the number of unknowns on a given mesh level, FASCD V-cycles exhibit nearly mesh-independent convergence rates, and full multigrid cycles are optimal solvers. The example problems include differential operators which are symmetric linear, nonsymmetric linear, and nonlinear, in unilateral and bilateral VI problems.



keywords

multigrid, variational inequalities, full approximation scheme, constraint decomposition

Introduction

The constraint decomposition (CD) methods of Tai
[
32
]
are designed to solve variational inequality (VI) problems arising as the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker optimality conditions for the minimization of a convex functional over a convex set. In a finite element (FE) context, these methods converge for multilevel and domain decompositions. The multilevel CD method has almost grid-independent error bounds, and near-optimal complexity, for elliptic, linear obstacle problems
[
32
, Subsection 5.4]
,
[
22
, Theorem 4.6]
.

Here we extend Tai’s multilevel CD method by replacing the coarser-level corrections with a full approximation scheme (FAS;
[
5
,
8
]
) approach. Specifically, our
full approximation scheme constraint decomposition
(FASCD) method enjoys the following advantages:

(i)

The FAS coarse corrections are adapted for general VI problems. The method requires neither an objective function nor linearity of the residual.

(ii)

Box constraints, i.e. both upper and lower obstacles, are allowed.

(iii)

The method is strictly admissible, and so can be applied even if the residual operator is defined only on those functions which satisfy the constraints.

(iv)

V-cycles (Algorithm
6.1
) and full multigrid (FMG;
[
34
]
) cycles (Algorithm
7.1
) are proposed.

(v)

We identify a “telescoping” subset property which can be exploited on the up-smoothing side of a V-cycle for greater performance.

(vi)

The method is (nonlinear) smoother-agnostic. When a (mesh-dependent) reduced-space Newton method
[
3
]
is used as relaxation, we demonstrate that application of FMG is an optimal solver for the examples in Section
8
.

Prior multilevel algorithms for VIs have limitations compared to the FASCD framework. The projected full approximation scheme (PFAS) algorithm of Brandt & Cryer
[
6
]
applies to linear complementarity problems and it uses projected (truncated) Gauss–Seidel smoothers. While FASCD reduces to general, nonlinear FAS for PDEs when the inequality constraints are removed, PFAS reduces to linear multigrid. Another work which combines FAS and VIs is
[
24
]
, but the problems solved, and the approach, are completely different. For an optimal control problem with an elliptic VI constraint,
[
24
]
applies FAS to a system of PDEs derived from penalizing the VI and the control constraints; their V- and W-cycles are not altered in any manner to maintain admissibility. On the other hand, the multilevel CD iteration of Tai
[
32
]
is formulated in terms of an objective function, so as stated it only applies to optimization-type problems, and furthermore each of its coarser-level problems refers to the finest-level discretization. The latter limitation is removed by Gräser & Kornhuber’s
[
22
, Algorithm 4.2]
reformulation using defect constraints and monotone injection operators; see Section
5
below. The monotone multigrid methods of Kornhuber and co-authors
[
22
,
29
]
appear to be formulated only with Gauss–Seidel type smoothers, and may have been applied only to optimization-type VI problems
[
26
, for example]
. Furthermore, unlike the fastest variants of monotone multigrid, no zeroing of the basis functions associated with the active set (truncation) is employed in FASCD, and this simplifies the implementation. Nonetheless FASCD coarse grid corrections can update the estimate of the active set on the fine grid, and indeed the numerical evidence indicates that FASCD coarse corrections are effective even when the coarse meshes are far too coarse to resolve the active set. On a problem with a complicated coincidence set, for which the truncated Newton/monotone multigrid methods were apparently designed
[
22
, problem 7.1.1]
, the performance of FASCD is at least as good (Example
12
). A limitation of FASCD is that, as formulated, it only solves bound-constrained VI problems.

In our experiments, FASCD V-cycles exhibit essentially the same mesh-independent convergence rates for VI problems that FAS multigrid V-cycles exhibit for the corresponding unconstrained PDE problems. The FMG results in Section
8
show near-textbook multigrid efficiency, yielding solutions within discretization error in work comparable to a few smoother sweeps on the finest-level mesh
[
7
]
. Specifically, we see this performance for classical (unilateral, Laplacian) obstacle problems,
p
-Laplacian obstacle problems, and box-constrained advection-diffusion equations. Furthermore, V-cycle results are largely insensitive to the geometric complexity of the coincidence set and free boundary.

All iterates in FASCD, on all mesh levels, are admissible, and thus the nonlinear operator in the VI needs only be defined for admissible states. For example, we solve an ice sheet geometry problem (Examples
6
and
15
) in which the ice flow formulas are only meaningful for surface elevation iterates that do not penetrate the bedrock. (Non-admissible methods, including semi-smooth methods
[
3
]
, would require unnatural modifications of the operator formula.) FASCD solves this low-regularity ice sheet problem, wherein the solution has unbounded gradient at the free boundary, by a small, mesh-independent number of FMG iterations (Example
15
). The same Example investigates the parallel weak scaling
[
11
]
of our implementation.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we recall the theory of coercive and monotone VIs, and describe several motivating examples. Section
3
reviews CDs and the iterations they induce. Section
4
sets up multilevel FE hierarchies, and then Section
5
extends the CD method via defect constraints generated by monotone injection operators
[
22
]
. Here we propose an apparently new understanding of telescoping sets for up-smoothing, based on an “incomplete” CD and an associated iteration. Section
6
then states the V-cycle, with Section
7
addressing convergence criteria, FMG cycles, and our smoother choice. Section
8
gives numerical results from our open source Python/Firedrake
[
31
]
implementation.

2
Variational inequalities

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a real reflexive Banach space with norm
\|\cdot\|
and topological dual space
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Denote the dual pairing of
\phi\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
by
\left<\phi,v\right>=\phi(v)
, and define the (Banach space) norm on
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
by
\|\phi\|^{\prime}=\sup_{\|v\|=1}|\!\left<\phi,v\right>\!|
. Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a nonempty, closed, and convex subset, the
constraint set
; elements of
\mathcal{K}
are said to be
admissible
. For a continuous, but generally nonlinear, operator
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and a linear
source functional

\ell\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
we consider the following
variational inequality
(VI) problem: find
u^{\star}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\left<f(u^{\star}),v-u^{\star}\right>\geq\left<\ell,v-u^{\star}\right>\quad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{K}.

(1)

Because
f
is (generally) nonlinear, the source term
\ell
is not strictly needed in stating this class of problems—by redefining
f
we may take
\ell=0
—but introducing
\ell
clarifies the algorithms which follow.

VI (
1
) generalizes nonlinear systems of equations
f(u^{\star})=\ell
to problems where
u^{\star}
is also constrained to be in
\mathcal{K}
. Pretending the dual pairing is an inner product, by (
1
) the angle between
f(u^{\star})-\ell
and any arbitrary vector
v-u^{\star}
pointing from
u^{\star}
into
\mathcal{K}
is at most
90^{\circ}
; the dual vector
f(u^{\star})-\ell
must point into
\mathcal{K}
. If
u^{\star}
is in the interior of the constraint set then (
1
) implies
f(u^{\star})=\ell
.
Variational inequalities may generally permit multiple solutions
[
19
]
.

The following definitions are standard
[
28
]
.

Definition 1
.

An operator
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is
monotone
if

\left<f(u)-f(v),u-v\right>\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathcal{K},

(2)

strictly monotone
if equality in (
2
) implies
u=v
, and
coercive
if there exists
w\in\mathcal{K}
so that

\frac{\left<f(u)-f(w),u-w\right>}{\|u-w\|}\to+\infty\qquad\text{as }\|u\|\to+\infty.

(3)

It is well-known that if
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is continuous, monotone, and coercive then VI (
1
) has a solution
[
28
, Corollary III.1.8]
, and that the solution is unique when
f
is strictly monotone. As is standard in the calculus of variations
[
17
]
, coercivity permits a compactness argument for unbounded sets
\mathcal{K}
; recall that the closed and bounded subsets of a reflexive Banach space are weakly compact. Some VIs solved in this paper satisfy a stronger inequality than (
3
).

Definition 2
.

Let
p>1
. The map
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is
p
-coercive
if there exists
\kappa>0
such that

\left<f(u)-f(v),u-v\right>\geq\kappa\|u-v\|^{p}\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in\mathcal{K}.

(4)

It is easy to see that if
f
is
p
-coercive then it is monotone, strictly monotone, and coercive. Thus if
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is continuous and
p
-coercive then there exists a unique
u^{\star}\in\mathcal{K}
solving VI (
1
).

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a bounded, open set with piecewise-smooth boundary. Sobolev spaces
[
17
]
will be denoted by
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
, for integer
k
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, with
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
denoting the subspace with zero trace. The following example includes the classical and
p
-Laplacian obstacle problems
[
14
]
.

Example 3
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. For
u,v\in\mathcal{V}=W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, define
f:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
by

\left<f(u),v\right>=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x,

(5)

a continuous map
[
30
, Theorem A.0.6]
. It follows from inequalities for
|\cdot|^{p}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the Poincaré inequality, that
f
is
p
-coercive
[
14
]
;
[
10
, Appendix A]
.

For monotone operators
f
, VI (
1
) generalizes the problem of minimizing a convex function over
\mathcal{K}
. Suppose
F:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
is lower semi-continuous and (Gâteaux) differentiable with continuous derivative
F^{\prime}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Then
F
is convex if and only if
F^{\prime}
is monotone
[
15
, Proposition I.5.5]
. Furthermore, Proposition II.2.1 in
[
15
]
shows that if
F
is convex then (
1
) holds for
f=F^{\prime}
if and only if

u^{\star}=\argmin_{v\in\mathcal{K}}F(v)-\left<\ell,v\right>.

(6)

The CD methods of Tai
[
32
]
address problem (
6
); the analysis in
[
32
]
supposes that an objective
F
exists with
F^{\prime}
2-coercive. For Example
3
we may define

F(v)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

(7)

Then
F
is a convex functional and
F^{\prime}(v)=f(v)
for
f
given in (
5
). For any closed and convex
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{V}
, VI problem (
1
) is then equivalent to optimization problem (
6
).

Next we give two examples which are
not
of optimization type (
6
). The first is an advection-diffusion problem; it requires the following Lemma.

Lemma 4
.

[
16
]
Suppose
\mathbf{X}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded and boundedly-differentiable vector field on
\Omega
with
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{X}=0
. For
u,v\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
let
b(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla u)v\,\mathrm{d}x
. Then
b(u,u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}u^{2}\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}x
where
\mathbf{n}
is the outward normal on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

Integration by parts gives
b(u,v)=-b(v,u)+\int_{\partial\Omega}uv\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\mathrm{d}x
.

Example 5
.

Suppose
\partial\Omega
is partitioned into Dirichlet and Neumann portions,
\partial\Omega=\partial_{D}\Omega\cup\partial_{N}\Omega
, with
\partial_{D}\Omega
of positive Hausdorff measure. Let
\mathcal{V}\subset W^{1,2}(\Omega)
be the space of functions which have zero trace on
\partial_{D}\Omega
. Consider a velocity field
\mathbf{X}
on
\Omega
satisfying the conditions of Lemma
4
, and assume that the flow is outward on the Neumann boundary:
\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}\geq 0
on
\partial_{N}\Omega
. For
u,v\in\mathcal{V}
,
\epsilon>0
, and
\phi\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, define

\left<f(u),v\right>=\epsilon\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+b(u,v)-\left<\phi,v\right>.

(8)

With this operator, consider VI (
1
) with
\ell=0
for any closed and convex
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{V}
. The interior condition in strong form is the following linear advection-diffusion equation

-\epsilon\nabla^{2}u+\mathbf{X}\cdot\nabla u=\phi.

(9)

On the other hand, it is easy to see that
|\!\left<f(u),v\right>\!|\leq\left((\epsilon+\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\infty})\|u\|+\|\phi\|^{\prime}\right)\|v\|
, so that
f:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
is continuous. Lemma
4
says that the bilinear form
b(u,v)
is skew-symmetric up to a term which is nonnegative by the outward flow assumption. From the Poincaré inequality,

\displaystyle\left<f(u)-f(v),u-v\right>

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u-\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+b(u-v,u-v)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u-\nabla v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial_{N}\Omega}(u-v)^{2}\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}\geq\epsilon C\|u-v\|^{2}.

Thus
f
is 2-coercive, and so the associated VI problem is well-posed. If
\mathbf{X}\neq 0
then
f\neq F^{\prime}
for any objective
F
, because
\left<f(u),v\right>
does not possess the symmetry of a gradient: for
u,v
which are zero on
\partial\Omega
, note that
\left<f(u),v\right>-\left<f(v),u\right>=-2b(u,v)
. References
[
10
,
13
]
consider similar advection-diffusion VI problems over
\mathcal{K}=\{v\geq 0\}
.

The next VI example is a model for steady ice sheet geometry in a given climate. It is fully nonlinear and not of optimization type. Related VI problems arise for any fluid layer subject to processes which can remove fluid mass
[
10
]
.

Example 6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a fixed region of land, and suppose
b\in C^{1}(\Omega)
is a given bedrock elevation. Let
a\in L^{2}(\Omega)
denote a given “surface mass balance” function, the annually-averaged rate of ice accumulation (snow) minus melt and runoff. Then the surface elevation
s\in C(\Omega)
of a steady-state, isothermal, and non-sliding ice sheet (glacier) under the
shallow ice approximation
(SIA)
[
23
]
satisfies the following VI problem: find
s\in\mathcal{K}=\{s\geq b\}
such that

\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(s-b)^{n+2}|\nabla s|^{n-1}\nabla s\cdot\nabla(v-s)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{\Omega}a(v-s)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\text{ for all }v\in\mathcal{K},

(10)

where
n>1
and
\Gamma>0
are physical constants for ice;
n=3
is a typical value
[
23
]
. The doubly-nonlinear and doubly-degenerate operator here is

\left<f(s),v\right>=\int_{\Omega}\Gamma(s-b)^{n+2}|\nabla s|^{n-1}\nabla s\cdot\nabla v\,\mathrm{d}x.

(11)

If the bed is flat (
b=0
) then a power transformation converts (
11
) into form (
5
) with
p=n+1
. Less is understood for general beds
b
, but existence holds for the VI
[
25
]
, and a power of the solution lives in a Sobolev space:
(s-b)^{2p/(p-1)}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
. This theory, and also observations of ice sheets, shows that
|\nabla s|
is usually unbounded as one approaches the free boundary from the icy (
s>b
) side.

8
will apply FASCD Algorithms
6.1
and

to the above Examples.

3
Constraint decomposition (CD)

Suppose that there are
m<\infty
closed linear subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{i}\subset\mathcal{V}
, so that the sum

\mathcal{V}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{V}_{i}

(12)

holds in the sense that if
w\in\mathcal{V}
then there exist
w_{i}\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
so that
w=\sum_{i}w_{i}
. We say (
12
) is a
space decomposition
of
\mathcal{V}

[
36
]
. For a closed, convex subset
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{V}
, suppose further that
\mathcal{K}_{i}\subset\mathcal{V}_{i}
are nonempty, closed, and convex subsets such that

\mathcal{K}=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{K}_{i}.

(13)

The sum in (
13
) must hold in two senses
[
33
]
:
(i)
if
w\in\mathcal{K}
then there exist
w_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
so that
w=\sum_{i}w_{i}
, and
(ii)
if
z_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
for each
i
then
\sum_{i}z_{i}\in\mathcal{K}
. Note that neither decomposition (
12
) or (
13
) is required to be unique, and also notice that sense
(ii)
is automatic for (
12
) because the
\mathcal{V}_{i}
are subspaces. Finally, suppose there are bounded, generally nonlinear,
decomposition operators

\Pi_{i}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{K}_{i}
such that if
v\in\mathcal{K}
then

v=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Pi_{i}v,

(14)

with the decomposition operators satisfying the stability property

\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\|\Pi_{i}u-\Pi_{i}v\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\lesssim\|u-v\|.

(15)

Clearly (
14
) implies sense
(i)
for (
13
). A
constraint decomposition
(CD) of
\mathcal{K}
is a choice of
\mathcal{V}_{i},\mathcal{K}_{i},\Pi_{i}
satisfying (
12
)–(
15
)
[
32
]
. Figure
1
suggests how a low-dimensional example might look; note
\mathcal{K}_{i}\not\subset\mathcal{K}
in this and many other cases.

Figure 1:
A constraint decomposition (CD) for a unilateral obstacle problem on a two-point space
\Omega=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
, with
\mathcal{V}=\left\{v\,:\,\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\right\}
and
\mathcal{K}=\{v\geq\psi\}
.

We will construct a multilevel FE CD discretization in Sections
4
–
7
. However, the CD concept can be applied at the level of the continuum problem. For example, in a unilateral obstacle problem (
\mathcal{K}=\{v\geq\psi\}
) one can construct an overlapping domain decomposition of
\mathcal{V}
by using a smooth, finite partition of unity
\{\varphi_{i}\}
, subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{i}=\{w\in\mathcal{V}:w=0\text{ if }\varphi_{i}=0\}
, constraint sets
\mathcal{K}_{i}=\{v\in\mathcal{V}_{i}:v\geq\varphi_{i}\psi\}
, and decomposition operators
\Pi_{i}(v)=\varphi_{i}v
. One may also construct a CD which is a disjoint frequency decomposition of a Hilbert space of periodic functions on a cube
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
. The multilevel CD, over a finite-dimensional FE discretization, will, in a vague sense, approximate such a frequency decomposition.

Associated to any CD are certain iterative methods
[
32
,
36
]
. The following multiplicative/successive
cd-mult
algorithm, which generalizes the Gauss–Seidel iteration, replaces
u\in\mathcal{K}
with a new iterate
w\in\mathcal{K}
which should be closer to
u^{\star}\in\mathcal{K}
solving (
1
). The idea is to solve smaller VI problems over each subset
\mathcal{K}_{i}
. Each subset solution corrects the global iterate immediately, so the ordering of the
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is important.

cd-mult
(u)
:

for
i=0,\dots,m-1
:

find
w_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\Big<f\Big(\sum_{j<i}w_{j}+w_{i}+\sum_{j>i}\Pi_{j}u\Big),\,v_{i}-w_{i}\Big>\geq\left<\ell,v_{i}-w_{i}\right>\textnormal{ for all }v_{i}\in\mathcal{K}_{i}

return
w=\sum_{i}w_{i}\in\mathcal{K}

The sum “
\sum_{j>i}\Pi_{j}u
” should be read as “those parts of
u
which need improvement”. Tai
[
32
]
also proposes an additive/parallel version of the algorithm, but it is not used here.

The VI problems in
cd-mult
reduce to constrained line searches when the
\mathcal{V}_{i}
are one-dimensional. If
e_{i}=w_{i}-\Pi_{i}u\in\mathcal{V}_{i}
denotes the correction from
\mathcal{K}_{i}
, then the new iterate
w=u+\sum_{i}e_{i}
is computed by adding a correction from each sub
space

\mathcal{V}_{i}
, but so that
w\in\mathcal{K}
; the corrections preserve admissibility. Furthermore, if VI problem (
1
) corresponds to convex optimization (
6
) then
cd-mult
generates monotonically-decreasing objective values.

Lemma 7
.

[
32
]
Suppose
f=F^{\prime}
for
F:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
convex and differentiable, and let
u\in\mathcal{K}
. The output
w\in\mathcal{K}
from
cd-mult
satisfies

F(w)-\left<\ell,w\right>\leq F(u)-\left<\ell,u\right>.

(16)

cd-mult
is meaningful whether or not we are in the optimization case
f=F^{\prime}
, and even if
f
is nonlinear, non-local, or defined only on
\mathcal{K}
, but its practical and efficient FE implementation for such general operators
f
seems not to have been addressed in the literature. In particular, references
[
22
,
32
]
only apply the multilevel CD method to the classical obstacle problem, wherein
f=F^{\prime}
is linear, local, and defined on all of
\mathcal{V}
. In Section
6
we will extend multilevel CD iterations to general nonlinear operators by applying the full approximation scheme (FAS) idea of Brandt
[
5
]
, and then illustrate its application to general operators in Examples
13
–
15
.

From now on we restrict to the case of
box constraints

[

,
21
]
, equivalently
bilateral obstacle problems
, over
\mathcal{V}=W^{1,p}(\Omega)

lower obstacle

\underline{\gamma}
and

\overline{\gamma}

\overline{\Omega}
, with values in the extended real line
\tilde{\mathbb{R}}=[-\infty,+\infty]
, satisfying the following properties:
\underline{\gamma}<+\infty
,

, and

\partial\Omega
is split into disjoint sets

\partial_{N}\Omega

\underline{\gamma}\leq g_{D}\leq\overline{\gamma}

\mathcal{K}=\left\{w\,:\,\underline{\gamma}\leq w\leq\overline{\gamma}\,\text{ and }\,w\big|_{\partial_{D}\Omega}=g_{D}\right\}\subset\mathcal{V}=W^{1,p}(\Omega),

(17)

the closed and convex

17

, and

is assumed to be as regular as needed. Any Neumann conditions over
\partial_{N}\Omega
are imposed weakly by the operator

, via an integral over
\partial_{N}\Omega

7
]
; it reduces to FAS for PDEs when inequality constraints are removed. Each V-cycle generates a correction to a finest-level iterate

, via information from each coarser FE subspace
\mathcal{V}^{j}
, by approximately solving VI problems in constraint sets
\mathcal{D}^{j}
and
\mathcal{U}^{j}
during the downward and upward stages, respectively. The corrections are inductively constructed so that iterates remain admissible at every level. A key idea is that up-smoothing solves less-restricted VI problems than down-smoothing. Going downward one does not know what coarse corrections are coming, thus one must choose smaller constraint sets (
\mathcal{D}^{j}
) such that the correction sum is admissible once we return to a given level. Going upward, however, no further coarse corrections can violate admissibility, so we relax in the larger sets
\mathcal{U}^{j}
.

Suppose

w^{J}+y^{J}+\dots+y^{j+1}\in\mathcal{K}^{J}
. Relaxation then occurs at the next-coarser level, yielding the next correction
y^{j}\in\mathcal{D}^{j}
. Solving the coarsest-level VI results in the full downward correction
y^{J}+\dots+y^{1}+z^{0}\in\mathcal{U}^{J}
. Starting upward,

is an initial iterate for relaxation on level

, which then yields

and an admissible correction

, using inclusion (

\mathcal{U}^{j}
, starting from initial iterate
y^{j}+z^{j-1}

as the V-cycle correction. The new finest-level iterate is

Implementation

In this section we propose a convergence criterion, construct the full multigrid (FMG) extension of Algorithm
6.1
, and summarize the reduce-space Newton method used as the smoother and coarse solver.

Repeated application of Algorithm
6.1
to solve finite-dimensional VI problem (
21
) will not generally reduce the residual
f^{J}(w^{J})-\ell^{J}
to zero everywhere. However, the VI is equivalent to a
mixed complementarity problem
(MCP)
[
18
]
, which says, by definition, that certain conditions hold for
u^{J}
at nodes
x_{p}\in\mathcal{T}^{J}
. Let
r(u^{J})_{p}=\left<f^{J}(u^{J})-\ell^{J},\psi_{p}\right>
be the ordinary (pointwise) residual. If
x_{p}\notin\partial_{D}\Omega
then exactly one of these four conditions must hold:

•

\underline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})<u^{J}(x_{p})<\overline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})
and
r(u^{J})_{p}=0
,

•

\underline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})=u^{J}(x_{p})<\overline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})
and
r(u^{J})_{p}\geq 0
,

•

\underline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})<u^{J}(x_{p})=\overline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})
and
r(u^{J})_{p}\leq 0
, or

•

\underline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})=u^{J}(x_{p})=\overline{\gamma}^{J}(x_{p})
.

Otherwise, if
x_{p}\in\partial_{D}\Omega
then

•

u^{J}(x_{p})=g_{D}^{J}(x_{p})
.

These 5 cases might be labeled
inactive
,
lower active
,
upper active
,
pinched
(
both active
), and
boundary
, respectively. Where
pinched
or
boundary
apply,

can have any value.

This MCP interpretation allows us to construct expressions whose norms should approach zero. Define the Fischer-Burmeister function
[
3
,
35
]

\phi(a,b)=a+b-\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}.

(45)

Observe that

is semi-smooth, thus continuous, and that

if and only if

are complementary, i.e.

,

, and

. For any

denote

and

(46)

Discussion and outlook

As a multilevel solver for VI problems, the FASCD method is a strategy for generating coarser-level problems which, once solved, make helpful admissible additions to a V-cycle update. The method is smoother-agnostic, and our implementation within the extensible Firedrake
[
31
]
and PETSc
[
2
]
library framework will allow easy experimentation with other smoothers (projected Gauss–Seidel, semi-smooth Newton, interior-point, etc.), beyond the active-set Newton method chosen for demonstration here. For smoothers of Newton-Krylov type one could also add linear geometric or algebraic multigrid preconditioning
[
34
]
for the linear step problems, instead of our simpler choice of incomplete factorization preconditioners. In a different direction, a restructured multilevel CD approach which applies the additive/parallel CD iteration
[
32
]
might be considered, in pursuit of a VI algorithm analogous to the BPX multigrid method for PDEs
[
4
]
. In any case, the performance observed in Section
8
is already excellent on the tested examples.
