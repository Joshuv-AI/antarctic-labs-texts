Dynamical Casimir effect enhanced by decreasing the mirror reflectivity
Andreson L. C. Rego, Alessandra N. Braga, Jeferson Danilo L. Silva, Danilo T. Alves

Abstract
In the present paper, we show that a partially reflecting static mirror with time-dependent properties can produce, via dynamical Casimir effect in the context of a massless scalar field in 1 + 1 1+1 dimensions, a larger number of particles than a perfectly reflecting one. As particular limits, our results recover those found in the literature for a perfect static mirror imposing a generalized or an usual time-dependent Robin boundary condition.

One of the goals of the present paper is to show that transparency can enhance the number of particles created via DCE, when compared to the limiting case of a perfect mirror.

II
The Model

We consider a massless scalar field in
1+1
dimensions in the presence
of partially reflecting static mirror with time-dependent material
properties. The mirror is simulated by a
\delta-\delta^{\prime}

potential at
x=0
coupled to the field, and the material properties
of the mirror are represented by the coupling parameters. The
\delta

term is coupled to the field by a time-dependent parameter,
\mu(t)
,
and the
\delta^{\prime}
one by a time-independent parameter,
\lambda_{0}
.
Moreover, it is included a modification in the kinetic term of the
Lagrangian density at
x=0
(where the mirror is located), namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[1+2\chi_{0}\delta(x)\right](\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}

(1)

\displaystyle-[\mu(t)\delta(x)+\lambda_{0}\delta^{\prime}(x)]\phi^{2}(t,x),

where
\chi_{0}
is a constant parameter. The modified kinetic term
of Eq. (
1
) originates a second-order time-derivative
that appears in the generalized Robin boundary condition (BC)
Fosco et al. 2013
;
Rego et al. 2014
.
The model described by Eq. (
1
) generalizes that
of a perfectly reflecting time-dependent Robin boundary condition, found in Ref.
Silva and Farina 2011
,
and that of a perfectly reflecting mirror imposing a generalized time-dependent Robin BC to the field, found in Ref.
Rego et al. 2014
.
It also generalizes the semi-transparent time-dependent model
considered Ref.
Silva et al. 2020
.
At the end of this section, we clarify our motivations
for choosing
\chi_{0}
and
\lambda_{0}
constant in time, whereas

\mu
is made time-dependent, and also connect this model with some physical situations.

The field equation for this model is given by

\displaystyle[1+2\chi_{0}\delta(x)]\partial_{t}^{2}\phi(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}\phi(t,x)

\displaystyle+2[\mu(t)\delta(x)+\lambda_{0}\delta^{\prime}(x)]\phi(t,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(2)

which becomes the massless Klein-Gordon equation

\partial_{x}^{2}\phi(t,x)-\partial_{t}^{2}\phi(t,x)=0,\text{ for }x\neq 0.

(3)

A particular case of this model, with
\lambda_{0}=0
, was considered
in Ref.
Fosco et al. 2013
.

It is convenient to rewrite the field as

\phi(t,x)=\Theta(x)\phi_{+}(t,x)+\Theta(-x)\phi_{-}(t,x),

(4)

where
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function,
\phi_{+}
and

\phi_{-}
are

\phi_{+}(t,x)=\varphi_{\text{out}}(t-x)+\psi_{\text{in}}(t+x),

(5)

\phi_{-}(t,x)=\varphi_{\text{in}}(t-x)+\psi_{\text{out}}(t+x),

(6)

and the labels “out” and “in” indicate, respectively, the outgoing and incoming
fields with respect to the mirror. Taking the Fourier
transform, we obtain

\phi_{+}(t,x)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\left[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\text{e}^{i\omega x}+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)\text{e}^{-i\omega x}\right]\text{e}^{-i\omega t},

(7)

\phi_{-}(t,x)=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}\left[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)\text{e}^{i\omega x}+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\text{e}^{-i\omega x}\right]\text{e}^{-i\omega t}.

(8)

After two successive integrations of Eq. (
2
)
across
x=0
, we obtain the following matching conditions

\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)=+\frac{1+\lambda_{0}}{1-\lambda_{0}}[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)],

(9)

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)-\tilde{\psi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\lambda_{0}}{1+\lambda_{0}}\left[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)-\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\right]

(10)

\displaystyle+\frac{2i\chi_{0}\omega}{1-\lambda_{0}^{2}}\left[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{2i}{\omega(1-\lambda_{0}^{2})}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\tilde{\mu}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\times\left[\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega^{\prime})+\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega^{\prime})\right],

where
\tilde{\mu}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of
\mu(t)
.

We shall consider

\mu(t)=\mu_{0}[1+\epsilon f(t)],

(11)

where
\mu_{0}
is a constant parameter,
f(t)
is an arbitrary
limited function with
|f(t)|\leq 1
and
\epsilon\ll 1
. Moreover,
the outgoing and incoming fields are grouped in column matrices:

\Phi_{\text{out}}(\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\\
\tilde{\psi}_{\text{out}}(\omega)\end{array}\right),\quad\Phi_{\text{in}}(\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\varphi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)\\
\tilde{\psi}_{\text{in}}(\omega)\end{array}\right).

(12)

Manipulating Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
), considering
Eq. (
11
) and neglecting the terms
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
,
the outgoing fields can be rewriten in term of the incoming ones,
namely

\Phi_{\text{out}}(\omega)=S(\omega)\Phi_{\text{in}}(\omega)+\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\Phi_{\text{in}}(\omega^{\prime}),

(13)

where
S(\omega)
is the scattering matrix for the case
\mu(t)\rightarrow\mu_{0}
,
and
\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is the correction to the
scattering matrix due to the time-dependence of
\mu(t)
. Explicitly,

S(\omega)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{+}(\omega)&r_{+}(\omega)\\
r_{-}(\omega)&s_{-}(\omega)\end{array}\right),

(14)

where

s_{\pm}(\omega)=\frac{\omega(1-\lambda_{0}^{2})}{i\mu_{0}-i\chi_{0}\omega^{2}+\omega(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})},

(15)

r_{\pm}(\omega)=-\frac{i\mu_{0}-i\chi_{0}\omega^{2}\mp 2\omega\lambda_{0}}{i\mu_{0}-i\chi_{0}\omega^{2}+\omega(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})},

(16)

are the transmission and reflection coefficients, respectively. The
term
\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})
is given by

\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})=-\frac{i\epsilon\mu_{0}\tilde{f}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\left[J_{2}+S(\omega^{\prime})\right]}{i\mu_{0}-i\chi_{0}\omega^{2}+\omega(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})},

(17)

where
\tilde{f}(\omega)
is the Fourier transform of
f(t)
and

J_{2}
is the
2\times 2
backward identity matrix. The scattering
matrix (
14
) must be real in the temporal
domain, unitary and analytic for
\mathrm{Im}\,\omega>0

Jaekel and Reynaud 1992
;
Lambrecht et al. 1996
,
which is guaranteed if
\mu_{0}
and
\chi_{0}
are non-negative.
Particularly, the limits
\mu_{0}\rightarrow\infty
or
\chi_{0}\rightarrow\infty

lead to the simpler case of a perfect mirror [
s_{\pm}(\omega)\rightarrow 0
]
imposing the Dirichlet BC to the field in both sides of the mirror,
namely

\phi(t,0^{+})=\phi(t,0^{-})=0,

(18)

or, equivalently,
r_{\pm}(\omega)\rightarrow-1
, which leads to

\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\rightarrow 0
. On the other
hand, the limit
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow-1
(or
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow 1
)
also leads to a perfect mirror, but in this case
\mathcal{S}(\omega,\omega^{\prime})\neq 0
,
and it leads to the BCs

\phi(t,0^{+})=0,

(19)

\mu(t)\phi(t,0^{-})+2\partial_{x}\phi(t,0^{-})+\chi_{0}\partial_{t}^{2}\phi(t,0^{-})=0.

(20)

identified respectively as the Dirichlet BC (
19
) and the
generalized Robin BC with a time-dependent Robin parameter (
20
).
Therefore, as we shall see, the results for the spectra of created
particles recover, in the appropriate limits, those
found in the literature
Silva and Farina 2011
;
Rego et al. 2014
.

Before continuing, let us make a brief comment about the nomenclature
we are using for the BC given in Eq. (
20
).
Considering the particular case where
\mu(t)=0
and
\chi_{0}=0
in Eq. (
20
), one has the Neumann BC,
\partial_{x}\phi(t,0^{-})=0
.
When we consider
\mu(t)=\mu_{0}>0
and
\chi_{0}=0
, one has

\mu_{0}\phi(t,0^{-})+2\partial_{x}\phi(t,0^{-})=0,

(21)

which is a particular case of that usually called Robin’s BC,
although G. Robin seems to have never used this BC (a very interesting
discussion on this subject is found in Ref.
Gustafson and Abe 1998
).
For
\mu(t)>0
and
\chi_{0}=0
, Eq. (
20
) gives

\mu(t)\phi(t,0^{-})+2\partial_{x}\phi(t,0^{-})=0,

(22)

which is a particular case of that called in Ref.
Silva and Farina 2011
a
time-dependent
Robin BC.
Following the nomenclatures adopted in the literature, the full BC given in Eq. (
20
) was called in Ref.
Fosco et al. 2013
as a
generalized
Robin BC.

Concluding this section, we discuss our motivations for choosing
\chi_{0}
and
\lambda_{0}
constant in time, whereas
\mu
is time dependent, and also
connect the model investigated here with physical situations.
The BC in Eq. (
20
) is
related to the first observation of photon creation from vacuum,
which involved a superconducting coplanar waveguide, terminated at a
SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device)
Wilson et al. 2011
.
In this case, the time-dependent parameter
\mu
is
related to a time-varying effective Josephson energy, whereas

\chi_{0}
is related to the constant capacitance and inductance per unit length
of the superconducting coplanar waveguide
Johansson et al. 2009
;
Johansson et al. 2010
.
This motivated our choice to investigate a model where
\chi_{0}
is a constant,
and
\mu
is a time-dependent function.
In addition, we chose the parameter
\lambda_{0}
as a constant
whose value controls the reflectivity of the object in such a way that
when
\lambda_{0}=\pm 1
one recovers a perfectly reflecting object, independently
of the values assumed by
\mu(t)
and
\chi_{0}
[as shown in Eq. (
16
)].
Lastly, particular cases of the BC Eq. (
20
) are given in Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
), and are also connected
with another physical situation related to the DCE.
The Robin BC in Eq. (
21
) can be used to describe
a plasma model for a real metal,
with the parameter
\mu_{0}
related with the plasma frequency

Mostepanenko and Trunov 1985
;
Silva and Farina 2011
.
Moreover, when considering a time-dependent parameter
\mu(t)
, as given in Eq.
(
22
), one has the simulation of a
perfectly reflecting metal with a time-dependent plasma frequency
Silva and Farina 2011
.
Since Eq. (
22
) is a
particular case of our model given in Eq. (
1
),
the Lagrangian proposed here can simulate a partially reflecting metal with a time-dependent plasma frequency.

III
Particle Creation

Considering vacuum as the initial state of the field, the spectrum
of created particles can be computed by
Lambrecht et al. 1996

N(\omega)=2\omega\,\mathrm{Tr}\left\langle 0_{\text{in}}\right|\Phi_{\text{out}}(-\omega)\Phi_{\text{in}}^{\mathrm{T}}(\omega)\left|0_{\text{in}}\right\rangle.

(23)

Substituting Eq. (
13
) into (
23
), we obtain

N(\omega)=N_{+}(\omega)+N_{-}(\omega),

(24)

where

N_{\pm}(\omega)=\frac{\epsilon^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{\prime}}\frac{\mathrm{Re}\left[1+r_{\pm}(-\omega^{\prime})\right]|\tilde{f}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})|^{2}}{\left(\mu_{0}-\chi_{0}\omega^{2}\right)^{2}+\omega^{2}(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})^{2}},

(25)

with
N_{+}(\omega)
and
N_{-}(\omega)
being the spectra for the
right and left sides of the mirror, respectively.
Manipulating this formula, we obtain

\displaystyle N_{\pm}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\pi}(1\pm\lambda_{0})^{2}(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})

(26)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\Upsilon(\omega)\Upsilon(\omega^{\prime})|\tilde{f}(\omega+\omega^{\prime})|^{2},

where

\Upsilon(\omega)=\frac{\mu_{0}\omega}{(\mu_{0}-\chi_{0}\omega^{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})^{2}}.

(27)

Considering
\mu_{0}\rightarrow\infty
or
\chi_{0}\rightarrow\infty
,
we have
N_{\pm}(\omega)\rightarrow 0
, a Dirichlet
BC on both sides of the mirror, as mentioned in the previous section
[Eq. (
18
)].
We also remark the symmetry
\lambda_{0}\leftrightarrow-\lambda_{0}
for

N(\omega)
,

N(\omega)\big|_{\lambda_{0}}=N(\omega)\big|_{-\lambda_{0}}.

(28)

From Eq. (
26
), we conclude that

N_{-}(\omega)=\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{0}}{1+\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}N_{+}(\omega).

(29)

Therefore, the spectra, for each side of the mirror, differ from each
other only by a frequency-independent global factor.
For
\lambda_{0}>0
,

N_{-}(\omega)
is smaller than
N_{+}(\omega)
for all frequencies,
and the opposite occurs for
\lambda_{0}<0
. The spectra are symmetric
i.e.,
N_{-}(\omega)=N_{+}(\omega)
only if
\lambda_{0}=0
,
or in the limit
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow\infty
. Furthermore, from
Eq. (
29
), we can conclude that there will not be particle
creation for one of the sides of the mirror if
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow\pm 1
,
specifically
N_{-}(\omega)=0
if
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow 1
,
or
N_{+}(\omega)=0

if
\lambda_{0}\rightarrow-1
, which is a consequence of the fact
that, in these limits, the field obeys the (time-independent) Dirichlet
BC for one of the sides and the time-dependent generalized Robin BC
on the other side [see Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
)].

The total number of created particles is obtained by integrating
the spectrum for all frequencies,

\mathcal{N}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega N(\omega).

(30)

From Eq. (
24
), the last equation can be written as
\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{N}_{+}+\mathcal{N}_{-}
,
where
\mathcal{N}_{+}
and
\mathcal{N}_{-}
are the total number
of particles for the right and left sides of the mirror, respectively.
From Eq. (
29
), it follows that

\frac{\mathcal{N}_{-}}{\mathcal{N}_{+}}=\left(\frac{1-\lambda_{0}}{1+\lambda_{0}}\right)^{2}.

(31)

From Eq. (
28
), we have

\mathcal{N}\big|_{\lambda_{0}}=\mathcal{N}\big|_{-\lambda_{0}}.

(32)

The total energy
\mathcal{E}
, dissipated from the mirror and converted in real particles, is given by

\mathcal{E}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega N(\omega)\omega=\mathcal{E}_{+}+\mathcal{E}_{-},

(33)

where

\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega N_{\pm}(\omega)\omega.

(34)

The integral in Eq. (
30
) is suitable for numerical
integration and we shall discuss these results in the following.

IV
Application

From now on, we consider, in Eq. (
11
),
the time-varying behavior given by

f(t)=\cos(\omega_{0}t)\exp(-|t|/\tau),

(35)

where
\tau
is the time interval for which the oscillations occur
effectively and
\omega_{0}
is the oscillation frequency.
We also
consider
\omega_{0}\tau\gg 1
, called monochromatic
limit
Silva et al. 2015
.
This is a typical oscillatory behavior considered in investigating
the DCE
Johansson et al. 2009
;
Johansson et al. 2010
;
Silva and Farina 2011
;
Silva et al. 2015
.
The Fourier transform of

, considering the monochromatic limit,
is given by
Silva et al. 2015

|\tilde{f}(\omega)|/\tau=(\pi/2)[\delta(\omega+\omega_{0})+\delta(\omega-\omega_{0})].

(36)

Therefore, substituting Eq. (
36
) into (
26
)
we obtain the following expression for the spectra

\frac{N_{\pm}(\omega)}{\tau}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}(1\pm\lambda_{0})^{2}(1+\lambda_{0}^{2})}{4\pi}\Upsilon(\omega)\Upsilon(\omega_{0}-\omega)\Theta(\omega_{0}-\omega).

(37)

The spectrum in Eq. (
37
) presents the symmetry

N_{\pm}(\omega_{0}/2+\zeta)=N_{\pm}(\omega_{0}/2-\zeta),

(38)

where
|\zeta|<\omega_{0}/2
.
Taking this symmetry into account in (
34
), we have
a proportionality between energy and number of particles, given by

\mathcal{E}_{\pm}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\mathcal{N}_{\pm},

(39)

which leads to

\mathcal{E}=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\mathcal{N}.

(40)

.

, let us highlight that

;

.

In order to interpret these results, we remark that
N_{\pm}(\omega)
, in Eq. (
25
),
depends on the reflectivity
|r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})|
and also on
the phase
\text{arg}[r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})]
[see Eq. (
16
)].
Mirrors with
|r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})|=1

(ideal mirrors, correspondent to the dashed lines in Figs.
1
and
2
) have
a maximum reflectivity, but not necessarily the
combination of
|r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})|=1
and
\text{arg}[r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})]

that creates a maximum of particles.
On the other hand, we show that there are points not belonging to
the dashed lines for which the combination of

|r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})|\neq 1
and
\text{arg}[r_{\pm}(\omega^{\prime})]

can produce the same or even a greater number of particles than perfectly reflecting mirrors.

V
Final Remarks

The creation of real particles by excitation of the quantum vacuum can be caused,
for instance, by the movement of a mirror
Moore 1970
, or by the time-dependent properties of a static material medium
Yablonovitch 1989
;
Lozovik et al. 1995
.
In the context of moving mirrors,
it had already been shown that transparency can enhance the number of created particles from vacuum, when compared to a perfect mirror
Silva et al. 2016
.
In the present paper, we showed that, even in the context of static mirrors
with time-dependent properties, a partially reflecting mirror can produce
a greater number of particles than a perfectly reflecting one.
