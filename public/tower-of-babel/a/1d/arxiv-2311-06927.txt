Topologically modified Einstein equation: a solution with singularities on 𝕊 3 \mathbb{S}^{3}
Quentin Vigneron, Áron Szabó, Pierre Mourier

Abstract
Vigneron [ Foundations of Physics , 54 , 15, ( 2024 )] recently proposed a modification of general relativity in which a non-dynamical term related to the spatial topology is introduced in the Einstein equation. The original motivation for this theory is to allow for the non-relativistic limit to exist in any physical topology. In the present paper, we derive a first inhomogeneous exact vacuum solution of this theory for a spherical topology, assuming staticity and spherical symmetry. The metric represents a black hole and a repulsive singularity at opposite poles of a 3-sphere. The solution is similar to the Schwarzschild metric, but the spacelike infinity is cut, and replaced by a repulsive singularity at finite distance, implying that the spacelike hypersurfaces have finite volume, and the total mass is zero. We discuss how this solution paves the way to massive, non-static solutions of this theory, more directly relevant for cosmology.

1
Introduction and motivations

In a recent paper
[
31
]
, we showed why general relativity (GR) is not compatible with Newtonian gravitation (understood as Galilean invariant gravitation, see
[
19
,
29
]
) on a 3-sphere, and more generally on any closed non-Euclidean topology, i.e. for which the spatial Riemannian covering space is not
\mathbb{E}^{3}
(e.g.
\mathbb{S}^{3}
,
\mathbb{H}^{3}
,
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, …
[
20
]
). In that same paper, we gave arguments to interpret this result as a “needle in the foot” of general relativity when non-Euclidean topologies are considered, and we minimally modified the Einstein equation such that it becomes compatible with Newtonian gravitation in any spatial topology. In that modification, a non-dynamical term related to the topology of the Universe is added in the Einstein equation. This term depends on a reference non-dynamical connection, additional to the physical metric connection.
As will be shown in Section
2
, this modification is conceptually close to GR, e.g. the equivalence principle is still present, but differs at larges scales where the effects of topology become important. The two theories are equivalent for Euclidean topologies, and differ for any other type of topology.
Different names could be attributed to this modification: e.g. “Topological Symmetric Metric Affine Gravity” as it can be seen as a subcase of metric affine gravity (e.g.
[
16
]
); “Topologically Corrected General Relativity” as it aims at correcting GR for its lack of non-relativistic limit depending on topology. In the present paper, we choose the second proposition and abbreviate it as “topo-GR”, which highlights the fact that the theory remains close to GR.

In
[
33
]
, we derived the homogeneous and isotropic solution of this theory, along with its first order perturbations with the aim of testing the theory with cosmological data. The goal of the present paper is to study an exact inhomogeneous solution in a non-Euclidean topology: a vacuum spherically symmetric metric on a
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
spacetime topology. Such a solution does not exist in general relativity due to Birkhoff’s theorem
[
28
]
.
In this paper, we will focus on a static solution. As will be shown, it represents an attractive singularity shielded by a horizon (black hole) and a repulsive naked singularity placed at opposite poles of a 3-sphere. The presence of the repulsive naked singularity restricts the physical relevance of this exact solution. However, as argued in Section
4.4
, removing this singularity, i.e. having only one black hole on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, requires dropping the staticity hypothesis. This is beyond the scope of this paper, but constitutes one of the main prospect for a generalization of the solution.
Still, this solution allows us to further understand the differences brought by topo-GR compared to GR when non-trivial spatial topologies are considered, as discussed in Section
4
.

The outline of the paper is the following: in Section
2
, we describe the topo-GR theory introduced in
[
31
]
; in Section
3
, we provide the static vacuum spherically symmetric solution of the theory and analyse its main properties (Penrose–Carter diagram, stable orbits, mass, topology); in Section
4
, we put in perspective this solution with the description of singularities on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in GR, and we discuss the main prospects of the paper for the study of topo-GR. We conclude in Section
5
.

2
Topologically Corrected General Relativity

We summarise in this section the theory developed in
[
31
]
, which we name “Topogically Corrected General Relativity” (topo-GR) in the present paper. We present here only what is needed for this paper. The reader
may find a more exhaustive description of the theory in
[
31
]
. The first section
2.1
presents the mathematical context related to topology, while Sections
2.2
and
2.3
present the fields involved in the theory and the physical equations.

2.1
Classification of 3-dimensional closed topologies

The classification of closed 2-dimensional surfaces states that any closed connected surface is a connected sum of prime surfaces whose Riemannian universal covering space (or surface) is either the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{E}^{2}
, the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
or the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. In three dimensions, a similar classification exists for closed connected spaces, called the Thurston classification. But instead of featuring three classes, this classification has eight distinct classes with Riemannian universal covering spaces being
\tilde{\Sigma}\in\{\mathbb{E}^{3},\ \mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2},\ Nil,\ Sol,\ \tilde{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
(see
[
20
]
for an overview of topology in the context of cosmology). As for the 2-dimensional case, a 3-manifold
\Sigma
whose Riemannian covering space
\tilde{\Sigma}
is
\mathbb{E}^{3}
,
\mathbb{S}^{3}
or
\mathbb{H}^{3}
is said to have, respectively, a Euclidean, spherical or hyperbolic topology.

Note that the right mathematical vocabulary that should be used is “geometry” instead of “topology”. The reason is that the Thurston classification is related to the classification of (geometric) G-structures on compact 3-manifolds, which implies a classification of 3-dimensional closed topological spaces. However, “geometry” as usually considered in general relativity or cosmology has a different meaning. Especially, “Euclidean geometry” usually refers to having a flat physical metric. This is not the case here. So, we prefer to use “topology” instead of “geometry”, highlighting the fact that “Euclidean”, “Spherical” or “Hyperbolic” refer first to topological properties rather than curvature properties.

Finally, in the context of the theory of
[
31
]
, globally hyperbolic spacetimes 4-manifolds
\mathcal{M}
with closed and connected spatial leaves are always considered, i.e.
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma
with
\Sigma
a closed 3-manifold. Therefore, the choice of spacetime covering space
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
directly corresponds to a choice of spatial covering space
\tilde{\Sigma}
because
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbb{R}\times\tilde{\Sigma}
(see Theorem A (ii) in
[
15
]
). Consequently, the spatial hypersurfaces are described by the Thurston classification presented above.

2.2
The reference curvature

The theory constructed in
[
31
]
is a bi-connection theory: it is defined on a 4-manifold
\mathcal{M}
equipped with a (physical) Lorentzian metric
g_{\alpha\beta}
with its Levi-Civita connection
\boldsymbol{\nabla}
and defining the physical spacetime Riemann curvature
{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}
; and a non-dynamical, symmetric (reference) connection
\boldsymbol{\bar{\nabla}}
, defining a reference spacetime Riemann curvature
\bar{R}^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}
. This second connection is non-dynamical in the sense that it is the same for any metric and energy-momentum tensor, for a given topology. The reference connection
\boldsymbol{\bar{\nabla}}
is not necessarily related to a metric.

The key feature of topo-GR is that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}
is chosen to depend on the spacetime topology. More precisely, it depends on the spacetime covering space, and therefore on the spatial covering space due to the closed connected global hyperbolicity hypothesis made in the previous section. The reference curvature is chosen to be the curvature of the (external Whitney) sum of any connection on
\mathbb{R}
and a connection on
\Sigma
, which comes from the universal cover
\tilde{\Sigma}
. In practice, this means that there exists a coordinate system adapted to a
\Sigma
-foliation for which the reference Riemann tensor can be written as
1
1

1

Throughout this paper, we denote indices running from 0 to 3 by Greek letters and indices running from 1 to 3 by Roman letters.

\bar{R}^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\nu\beta}=\delta^{\mu}_{a}\delta^{i}_{\alpha}\delta^{b}_{\nu}\delta^{j}_{\beta}\,\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{a}{}_{ibj}(x^{k}),

(1)

where
\delta^{\mu}_{a}
is the Kronecker symbol and
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{a}{}_{ibj}
is independent of
x^{0}
and corresponds to the Riemann tensor of the Riemannian covering space
\tilde{\Sigma}
.
For instance, in the case
\Sigma
is a prime manifold (i.e.
\tilde{\Sigma}\in\{\mathbb{E}^{3},\ \mathbb{S}^{3},\ \mathbb{H}^{3},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2},\ \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2},\ Nil,\ Sol,\ \tilde{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})\}
), then
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{a}{}_{ibj}
is related to the standard homogeneous metric of these Riemannian manifolds (they are listed in e.g.
[
20
, Section 5.1]
). In particular, in the case of interest for the present paper, for
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{S}^{3}
(i.e. the spherical topological class) we have
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{ij}=2\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}
, where
\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}
is a homogeneous and isotropic metric on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, implying

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}=2\,\delta^{i}_{\alpha}\delta^{j}_{\beta}\;\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}(x^{k})\quad{\rm for}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}.

(2)

The only case where
\bar{\mathcal{R}}^{a}{}_{ibj}=0
, and therefore
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}=0
, is for
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}^{3}
(the Euclidean topological class).

2.3
The field equations

The action of the theory is
[
31
]

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{M}}\sqrt{-g}\,\left[\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(R_{\mu\nu}-\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\right)g^{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}\right]\mathrm{d}x^{4},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\rm m}
is the Lagrangian of matter,
\kappa\coloneqq 8\pi G
. The field equation resulting from this action is

G_{\alpha\beta}=\kappa\,T_{\alpha\beta}-\Lambda g_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta},

(4)

where we define

\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}\coloneqq\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu}}{2}g_{\alpha\beta}.

(5)

Equation (
4
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}-\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}=\kappa\,\left(T_{\alpha\beta}-\frac{T}{2}g_{\alpha\beta}\right)+\Lambda g_{\alpha\beta},

(6)

which highlights more explicitly the difference with general relativity: the metric Ricci tensor is replaced by the difference between this tensor and the reference Ricci tensor. The action (
3
) also implies that the additional term
\mathcal{T}_{\alpha\beta}
in equation (
4
), compared to the Einstein equation, is conserved (assuming the matter Lagrangian is independent of
\bar{\nabla}
)
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle g^{\mu\nu}\left(\nabla_{\mu}\bar{R}_{\nu\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\alpha}\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\right)=0.

(7)

Equation (
7
), called the
bi-connection condition
, constraints the additional (diffeomorphism) degrees of freedom, compared to general relativity, coming from the introduction of the reference connection. It does not constrain this connection, but only how it is related to the physical connection.

As presented in the previous section, if a Euclidean spatial topology is chosen, then
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}=0
, implying that equation (
6
) becomes the standard Einstein equation and that equation (
7
) becomes trivial.
Therefore, GR and topo-GR coincide for this class of topology, and differ for any other type of topology.

Equations (
6
)–(
7
) are equivalent to the ones of the bi-connection theory proposed by
Rosen 1980
. The only difference (but a fundamental one) is the choice and motivation for the reference connection: Rosen chose a reference connection related to a de Sitter metric in order to remove singularities from general relativity, while in our case, the reference connection is topology dependent as it is related to the universal cover of the spacetime manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Currently, the main reason for considering topo-GR with the above choice of reference connection is to have a theory admitting a non-relativistic limit in any topology, something not possible with general relativity. We direct the reader to Section 4.3 in
[
31
]
for a detailed argumentation. Additionally, the well-posedness of inflation for any background curvature recently came as a second motivation for this theory
[
32
]
.

An interesting analogy we can make to qualitatively understand topo-GR is to consider the surface of the Earth. The presence of “matter”, here which we consider to be the mountains, water waves, trees, etc, intrinsically curves the surface of the Earth locally. Still, without this matter, because this surface is topologically a 2-sphere, its intrinsic curvature can never be identically zero: it is a topological (thus global) constraint. Therefore, the effect of matter is not to curve the Earth surface, but to induce local deviations from the simplest curvature imposed by topology. This is how we can understand the role of the reference curvature in equation (
6
). The interpretation of that equation is that the presence of matter, and/or cosmological constant, induces a departure of the physical spacetime curvature from the “topological curvature”
\boldsymbol{\bar{R}}
. This must be compared with general relativity, in which matter directly curves spacetime.

3
The solution

3.1
Derivation

For general relativity, the first exact inhomogeneous solution found was the static vacuum spherically symmetric solution. We are searching for the equivalent solution for topo-GR, in the case
\tilde{\Sigma}\not=\mathbb{E}^{3}
. As said previously, in the case
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}^{3}
, topo-GR is equivalent to GR because the reference curvature is zero. Since we are dealing with closed spatial manifolds, spherical symmetry is only possible if the spatial covering space is
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.
2
2

2

Only two closed 3-manifolds can have spherical symmetry: the 3-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and the real projective space
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Both of them are spherical topologies (i.e.
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{S}^{3}
), therefore
\bar{R}_{\alpha\beta}=2\delta^{i}_{\alpha}\delta^{j}_{\beta}\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}
for both, by definition of the theory.
Any other choice of closed spatial topology (e.g. the 3-torus for
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{E}^{3}
or the Seifert–Weber topology for
\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathbb{H}^{3}
) breaks this symmetry. Therefore, by definition (
2
), we can choose a coordinate system
\{\tilde{t},\tilde{\chi},\theta,\varphi\}
where the reference Ricci tensor takes the form

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}=2\mathrm{d}\tilde{\chi}^{2}+2\sin^{2}\left(\tilde{\chi}\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2},

(8)

where
\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}:=\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin(\theta)^{2}\mathrm{d}\varphi^{2}
. Since we assume spherical symmetry, in the same coordinate system, the line element of the metric can be written, in full generality, as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=\left[-A_{1}(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})+\beta(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})^{2}/A_{2}(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})\right]\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}^{\,2}+2\beta(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\chi}

\displaystyle\qquad+A_{2}(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\chi}^{2}+A_{3}(\tilde{\chi},\tilde{t})\sin^{2}(\tilde{\chi})\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(9)

From here, we make a first restrictive hypothesis by assuming staticity of the metric in the same coordinate system where (
8
) holds. In general relativity, the Birkhoff theorem shows that the assumption of staticity for vacuum spherically symmetric solutions is not a restriction, and corresponds to the general solution. This is not the case in topo-GR, where staticity is expected to be a restrictive assumption. In particular, as will be discussed later, the main consequence of this hypothesis is that the total mass represented by the solution will be zero. In Section
4.4
, we discuss what we expect to obtain without this hypothesis, and why it will be important as a future prospect to search for a non-static solution.

With staticity, the shift
\beta
in equation (
9
) can be removed by a change of coordinates
\tilde{t}\mapsto t+f(\tilde{\chi})
without changing the expression of the reference Ricci tensor (
8
), such that we obtain the line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}=-B_{1}(\tilde{\chi})\mathrm{d}t^{2}+B_{2}(\tilde{\chi})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\chi}^{2}+B_{3}(\tilde{\chi})\sin^{2}(\tilde{\chi})\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(10)

At this point, we make a second hypothesis which is, a priori, a loss of generality, by assuming

\displaystyle B_{1}(\tilde{\chi})={\rm const}/B_{2}(\tilde{\chi}).

(11)

This ansatz, inspired by its Schwarzschild counterpart, allows us to obtain an analytical solution. When solving the system (
6
)–(
7
), the following solution is obtained (details can be found in Appendix
A
):

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(\tilde{\chi}-\chi_{\rm h}/2)}{\sin(\tilde{\chi}+\chi_{\rm h}/2)}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\sin(\tilde{\chi}+\chi_{\rm h}/2)}{\sin(\tilde{\chi}-\chi_{\rm h}/2)}R_{\rm c}^{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\chi}^{2}+R_{\rm c}^{2}\sin^{2}(\tilde{\chi}+\chi_{\rm h}/2)\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2},

(12)

where
\chi_{\rm h}\in[0,\pi]
and
R_{\rm c}>0
are free parameters. As will be shown in Section
3.5
, the former parameter corresponds to the horizon coordinate of the black hole, and the latter to the curvature radius of the 3-sphere in the case
\chi_{\rm h}=0
. Because the assumption (
11
) is a loss of generality, the above solution might not be the general static vacuum spherically symmetric solution of topo-GR. However, as will be shown in Section
4.1
, the non-relativistic limit of this solution corresponds to the general static vacuum spherically symmetric solution of Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. For this reason, we expect (
11
) not to be a loss of generality and the solution (
13
) to be the general solution.

With the change of coordinates
\tilde{\chi}\mapsto\chi-\chi_{\rm h}/2
, we obtain the standard line element
R_{\rm c}^{2}\sin^{2}(\chi)\mathrm{d}\Omega
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
for the coordinate variables
\theta
and
\varphi
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sin(\chi-\chi_{\rm h})}{\sin\chi}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+\frac{\sin\chi}{\sin(\chi-\chi_{\rm h})}R_{\rm c}^{2}\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}+R_{\rm c}^{2}\sin^{2}\chi\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(13)

In these coordinates, the reference Ricci curvature takes the form

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}x^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x^{\nu}=2\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}+2\sin^{2}\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}/2\right)\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}.

(14)

In the coordinates (
12
), the solution is similar to the Schwarzschild metric in harmonic coordinates, while in the coordinates (
13
), the solution is similar to the Schwarzschild metric in standard coordinates, with
\frac{\sin(\chi-\chi_{\rm h})}{\sin\chi}
playing the role of
1-\frac{R_{\rm S}}{r}
. As we will see in the following sections, there are a lot of similarities between our solution and the Schwarzschild metric, with key differences related to topology.

3.2
Singularities

For
\chi_{\rm h}\not=0
, there are two infinite singularities at
\chi=0
and
\chi=\pi
, which can be identified through the analysis of the Ricci scalar:
3
3

3

Contrary to the Schwarzschild metric for which
R_{\mu\nu}=0
, in our solution, where the vacuum condition rather reads
R_{\mu\nu}=\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}\not=0
for
\mathbb{S}^{3}

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu}=\frac{2+\cos\chi_{\rm h}-3\cos\left(2\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)}{{R_{\rm c}}^{2}\sin^{2}\chi}.

As will be made clear from the Penrose–Carter diagrams of that solution (Figure

The Ricci scalar (

-extension of the metric at the coordinates
\chi=0
and
\chi=\pi

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu}=R_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu}=\frac{2+\cos\chi_{\rm h}-3\cos\left(2\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)}{{R_{\rm c}}^{2}\sin^{2}\chi}.

Because

involves no derivatives of the metric, the singularities of equation (

-extension of the metric at
\chi=0
and

The fact that we are easily able to determine the

, is because of the presence of an extra structure: the reference curvature. As shown above, it allows us to construct a singular scalar field involving no derivative of the metric.

3.3
Stable orbits

When restricted to the plane
\theta=\pi/2
, the geodesic equations are

\displaystyle\dot{t}=k\frac{\sin\chi}{\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)}\quad;\quad\dot{\varphi}=\frac{l}{R_{\rm c}\sin^{2}\chi}\quad;

(17)

\displaystyle\ddot{\chi}+\frac{\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)\sin\chi_{\rm h}}{2R_{\rm c}^{2}\sin^{3}\chi}\,{\dot{t}}^{2}-\frac{\sin\chi_{\rm h}}{2\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)\sin\chi}\,{\dot{\chi}}^{2}-\frac{\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)}{\cos\chi}\,{\dot{\varphi}}^{2}=0,

where
l
quantifies the angular momentum of the geodesics, and
k
its energy. The first integral
\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}g_{\mu\nu}=-\epsilon
, where
\epsilon=1
for timelike geodesics, and
\epsilon=0
for null geodesics, gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{\rm c}\dot{\chi}\right)^{2}+V_{{\rm eff},\epsilon}\left(\chi,\chi_{\rm h},l\right)=\frac{k^{2}-\epsilon}{2},

(18)

where

\displaystyle V_{{\rm eff},\epsilon}\left(\chi,\chi_{\rm h},l\right):=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\left(\frac{\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)}{\sin\chi}-1\right)+\frac{\sin\left(\chi-\chi_{\rm h}\right)l^{2}}{2\sin^{3}\chi}.

(19)

The characterisation of the stable orbits is made by studying the properties of this effective potential. For
\chi_{\rm h}<\pi/6
, as for the Schwarzschild metric, there is an innermost stable timelike orbit
\chi_{\rm ims}
defined as the radius of the closest minima of
V_{{\rm eff},\epsilon}
for
\chi_{\rm h}
fixed. It is obtained for
{l=\frac{\sqrt{3}\sin\chi_{\rm h}}{\sqrt{2\cos\left(2\chi_{\rm h}\right)-1}}}
and is

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm ims}=\arctan\left[3\tan\chi_{\rm h}\right]\quad{\rm for}\quad 0<\chi_{\rm h}<\frac{\pi}{6}.

(20)

This is consistent with the Schwarzschild result (
r_{\rm ims}=3R_{\rm S}
) when the horizon radius is small compared to the size of the sphere:
\chi_{\rm ims}=3\chi_{\rm h}+\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{\rm h}^{2}\right)
. However, unlike for the Schwarzschild metric, this innermost stable timelike orbit exists only if
\chi_{\rm h}<\pi/6
.
There is also an unstable photon orbit at

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm Null,u}={\rm arccot}\left[\frac{\cos\chi_{\rm h}-\sqrt{2\cos\left(2\chi_{\rm h}\right)-1}}{3\sin\chi_{\rm h}}\right]\quad{\rm for}\quad 0<\chi_{\rm h}<\frac{\pi}{6},

(21)

which is consistent with the Schwarzschild result (
r_{\rm Null,u}=\frac{3}{2}R_{\rm S}
) since
\chi_{\rm Null,u}=\frac{3}{2}\chi_{\rm h}+\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{\rm h}^{2}\right)
. However, unlike for the Schwarzschild metric, there is also a stable photon orbit at

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm Null,s}={\rm arccot}\left[\frac{\cos\chi_{\rm h}+\sqrt{2\cos\left(2\chi_{\rm h}\right)-1}}{3\sin\chi_{\rm h}}\right]\quad{\rm for}\quad 0<\chi_{\rm h}<\frac{\pi}{6},

(22)

which is close to the 3-sphere equator for a small horizon radius, i.e.
\chi_{\rm Null,s}=\pi/2+\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{\rm h}\right)
. Both the stable and unstable photon orbits exist only for
\chi_{\rm h}<\frac{\pi}{6}
, as for the innermost stable timelike orbit. The radii
\chi_{\rm ims}
,
\chi_{\rm Null,u}
and
\chi_{\rm Null,s}
are represented as functions of
\chi_{\rm h}
in Figure

.

3.6
Mass

The notions of mass or energy in general relativity often require the hypothesis of asymptotic flatness (or at least asymptotical conicity, see e.g. Definition 1.11 in
[
18
]
), and/or the introduction of a reference flat metric (e.g. the ADM energy
[
1
]
or the Brown–York energy
[
4
]
). Such definitions cannot be used to determine the mass in our solution, where the volume of the spacelike hypersurface between the BH horizon at
\chi=\chi_{\rm h}
and the RNS at
\chi=\pi
is finite. One notion of mass not directly related to asymptotic flatness is the Komar mass. It is defined for spacetimes having a timelike Killing vector
k^{\mu}
. Inside a spacelike surface
\mathcal{S}_{t}\sim\mathbb{S}^{2}
on a hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
, the mass is given by
[
14
]

\displaystyle M_{\rm K}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{t}}\left(s_{\mu}n_{\nu}-n_{\mu}s_{\nu}\right)\nabla^{\mu}k^{\nu}\sqrt{q}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}y,

(32)

where
n^{\mu}
is the normal unit vector to
\Sigma_{t}
,
s^{\mu}
is the normal unit vector to
\mathcal{S}_{t}
within
\Sigma_{t}
and oriented outwards from
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, and
q={\rm det}(q_{\mu\nu})
where
q_{\mu\nu}
is the metric on
\mathcal{S}_{t}
induced by
g_{\mu\nu}
.

The timelike Killing vector
k^{\mu}
is defined up to a constant factor. For asymptotically flat spacetimes, this factor is chosen such that
k^{\mu}=(1,0,0,0)
at infinity. So, the Komar mass is actually not uniquely defined and, as for the ADM mass, it requires a reference observer at infinity to make the definition unique. Nevertheless, the definition (
32
) does not explicitly require an infinite volume (and flatness), and therefore can be considered for a metric inducing a finite volume, for which the mass will necessarily (unless there is a natural choice of lapse) be defined up to a constant factor.

We consider our solution written in the form (
13
). The vector
(1,0,0,0)
is a timelike Killing vector for
\chi>\chi_{\rm h}
. We choose
\mathcal{S}_{t}
such that it encompasses the BH horizon. The definition (
32
) leads to

\displaystyle M_{\rm K}^{\rm BH}=\frac{R_{\rm c}}{2}\sin\chi_{\rm h}.

(33)

As expected,
M_{\rm K}
does not depend on the radius
\chi
of the surface
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, since
\mathcal{S}_{t}
is located in vacuum for any value of
\chi>\chi_{\rm h}
. This is a known property of the Komar mass in GR, which also holds in topo-GR as long as either
k^{\mu}
or the normal vector
n^{\mu}
to
\Sigma_{t}
lie in the kernel of
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}
(see Appendix
C
).

The mass (
33
) is the mass of the BH. By reversing the orientation of the surface
\mathcal{S}_{t}
, we directly obtain the mass
M_{\rm K}^{\rm RNS}
of the repulsive naked singularity as the opposite of the one of the BH:
{M_{\rm K}^{\rm RNS}=-M_{\rm K}^{\rm BH}}
. Therefore the total mass in the static region I of the Penrose–Carter diagram (equivalently, in the static region I’) is zero. This does not depend on the normalisation of the timelike Killing vector in the definition of the Komar mass. This result is discussed in Section
4.2
.

3.7
Which topology?

:

The metric is singular and not continuously extensible at
\chi=0
and
\chi=\pi
. Considering either the full extension of the metric [equation (
29
)], i.e. describing regions I, II, I’ and II’, or only part of this extension, e.g. describing only regions I and II [line element (
13
)], or only regions I and I’ [line element (
B2
)], the manifold on which is defined the metric, as a smooth continuous tensor field, is always
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
. On the other hand, the reference Ricci curvature
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}
is defined, as a smooth continuous tensor field, on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

This situation is similar to the solution of Newtonian gravitation for a point mass in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In this case, we start with a (flat) spatial metric defined everywhere on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and solve the Poisson equation in vacuum with spherical symmetry. The solution for the gravitational potential turns out to be defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
. However, we do not consider the spatial topology to be
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
: we still consider it to be
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Can we do the same thing with respect to the BH-RNS metric? In other words: while mathematically
g_{\mu\nu}
is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, does it make sense, physically, to interpret the topology it describes to be rather
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
? In the following, we shall argue in favour of this interpretation.

First, having
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}
defined on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
g_{\mu\nu}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
used together in the same equation [the topo-GR equations (
6
)–(
7
)] is not in contradiction. Indeed,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
can be (densely) embedded in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
(i.e.
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
corresponds to
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
from which two lines have been removed) which implies that
g_{\mu\nu}
can be seen as being defined on a subset of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
, i.e. everywhere away from the singularities.

Second, to our opinion, the key property for interpreting the topology described by the BH-RNS metric to be
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
rather than
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, is to have finiteness of the spacelike hypersurface volume as induced by the metric: i.e. on a spacelike foliation
\{\Sigma_{t}\sim\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the volume of the hypersurfaces of this foliation should be finite:

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}\sqrt{{\rm det}h_{ij}}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}x<\infty,

(34)

where
h_{ij}
is the metric induced by
g_{\mu\nu}
on
\Sigma_{t}
.

Property (
34
) can never be fulfilled by the Schwarzschild metric regardless of the foliation we consider, due to the spatial infinities. Therefore, while this metric is also defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
which can be embedded in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
, it makes no sense, with respect to condition (
34
), to interpret the Schwarzschild metric as describing a spherical universe. The same applies for the Minkowski metric defined on
\mathbb{R}^{4}
which can also be embedded in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
, i.e. a point is added at spatial infinity.

The situation is different with the BH-RNS metric for which the spacelike volume around each repulsive naked singularity is finite, and property (
34
) is fulfilled. This can be seen with the Kruskal–Szekeres line element (
29
), or more easily with the isotropic line element (
B2
) describing regions I and I’ (derived in Appendix
B
). The finiteness of the spacelike volume is schematized in Figure
3
.

To summarise, the finiteness of the spacelike hypersurface volume and the fact that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a (dense) subset of
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
are the reasons why we can
interpret
our solution as describing a
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe. That interpretation is further supported by the fact that we can remove the horizon and fill the BH with a finite volume
static
distribution of matter, letting only the repulsive naked singularity, around which the volume is spacelike and finite. This is represented in Figure
5
. Overall, the present discussion highlights the way solutions should be searched for in topo-GR:

(i)

a spacetime topology must be chosen. This sets the reference curvature
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}
, as described in Section
2.2
.

(ii)

a Lorentzian (physical) metric solution of (
6
) is considered. That solution,
a posteriori
, might be only defined on a (dense) subset of the initial topology, as is the case with the BH-RNS metric.

Note that this would have been contradictory to find an infinite volume as induced by the metric solution of the topo-GR equations (
6
)–(
7
), while by hypothesis and definition of the theory, we initially considered
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
and defined everywhere on this manifold the Ricci tensor
\boldsymbol{\bar{R}}
. Therefore, we suspect that any solution of (
6
)–(
7
) will necessarily have a finite spacelike hypersurface volume (with or without singularities) once we consider spherical topologies, i.e. once we take
\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}=2\,\delta_{\mu}^{i}\delta_{\nu}^{j}\,\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}

4.1
Compatibility with Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}

As shown in
[
31
]
, general relativity is not compatible with Newtonian gravitation in non-Euclidean spatial topologies, i.e. for which the covering space is not
\mathbb{E}^{3}
. Currently, the main motivation for the construction of topo-GR is to have a relativistic theory admitting such a compatibility, i.e. having a non-relativistic limit in any topology. In this section, we check the compatibility of our solution with the static vacuum spherically symmetric solution obtained in the Newtonian theory on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, which was defined in
[
30
,
34
]
.

Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is described by similar equations as Newtonian gravitation on a Euclidean topology, i.e. classical Newtonian theory
[see
34
, for a summary of the full system of equations]
. In particular, the gravitational potential
\Phi
is given by the cosmological Poisson equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\Phi=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\rho-M_{\rm tot}/V_{\Sigma}\right),

(35)

where
V_{\Sigma}\propto a(t)^{3}
is the volume of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
and
M_{\rm tot}
is the total (Newtonian) mass in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. The only difference with the Euclidean Newtonian theory is the spatial metric which is curved and has the form
h_{ij}(t,x^{k})=a^{2}(t)\bar{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}(x^{k})
.
4
4

4

Actually, the most general form of the theory features a metric with anisotropic expansion. However, this does not change the discussion of this section since we consider a static spacetime.
The scale factor
a(t)
is solution to the Friedmann equation
{3\ddot{a}/a=-4\pi GM_{\rm tot}/V_{\Sigma}}
(we assumed
\Lambda=0
). For this reason, a static solution, for which
\dot{a}=0
, of Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
necessarily requires
M_{\rm tot}=0
.
5
5

5

This results actually holds for any closed topology, and therefore holds in the classical Newtonian theory, since the same Friedmann equation is valid.

The spherically symmetric static vacuum (i.e. with
M_{\rm tot}=0
) solution of (
35
) is obtained by taking
\rho=M_{\rm N}\left[\delta^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}(\chi)-\delta^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}(\pi-\chi)\right]
, where
\delta^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}
is the Dirac distribution on
\left(\mathbb{S}^{3},\boldsymbol{\bar{h}}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}\right)
and
M_{\rm N}
is the Newtonian mass of the point masses. The solution is

\displaystyle\Phi=\frac{M_{\rm N}}{R_{\rm c}}\cot\chi,

(36)

where
R_{\rm c}
is the curvature radius of the 3-sphere. As for the relativistic solution (
13
), it represents an attractive and a repulsive point masses of opposite Newtonian mass
\pm M_{\rm N}
at opposite poles of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, taken to be of radius
R_{\rm c}
.

The dictionary between the metric solution of topo-GR and Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
was derived in
[
31
, Section 6.3]
and is similar to the standard dictionary in general relativity. In particular in a gauge without shift, as is the case with the line element (
13

g_{00}=-c^{2}+2\Phi+\mathcal{O}\left(1/c^{2}\right)
. By reintroducing
c^{2}
in (
13

t\rightarrow c\,t
and
\chi_{\rm h}\rightarrow\arcsin\left[2M^{\rm BH}_{\rm K}/(c^{2}\,R_{\rm c})\right]
, we obtain
g_{00}=-c^{2}+2\frac{M^{\rm BH}_{\rm K}}{R_{\rm c}}\cot\chi+\mathcal{O}\left(1/c^{2}\right)
, consistent with the Newtonian potential derived above, associating
M^{\rm BH}_{\rm K}
to
M_{\rm N}
. In conclusion, the BH-RNS solution is the relativistic equivalent of the Newtonian solution featuring a positive and negative equal point masses at opposite poles of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

4.2
Total mass and staticity

Just like it could have been expected from the Newtonian limit presented in the previous section, having
M_{\rm K}^{\rm tot}=0
was also expected from the homogeneous and isotropic solution of topo-GR. As shown in
[
33
]
, the expansion laws for such a solution do not depend anymore on the spatial curvature:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}3H^{2}&=\kappa\bar{\rho}+\Lambda,\quad\forall\Omega_{K}\\
3\frac{\ddot{a}}{a}&=-\frac{\kappa}{2}(\bar{\rho}+3\bar{p})+\Lambda,\end{cases}

(37)

where
\bar{\rho}
and
\bar{p}
are homogeneous density and pressure, and
\Omega_{K}
is the curvature parameter. In the case
\bar{\rho}>0
and
\bar{p}=0
, because
\Omega_{K}
is not present anymore in the first expansion law, no static solution on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is possible, for any value of
\Lambda
. This differs from general relativity for which such a solution exists in the form of the Einstein static model. The only possibility for a static homogeneous solution on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in topo-GR is

\displaystyle\Lambda=-\kappa\bar{\rho}\quad;\quad\bar{\rho}=-\bar{p},

(38)

which implies
R_{\mu\nu}=\bar{R}_{\mu\nu}
. In the case
\Lambda=0
, a zero homogeneous density is required. If we extrapolate this result to inhomogeneous solutions and replace
\bar{\rho}
and
\bar{p}
by the spatial average density
\left\langle\rho\right\rangle_{\Sigma_{t}}=M_{\rm tot}/V_{\Sigma}
and pressure
\left\langle p\right\rangle_{\Sigma_{t}}
on
\Sigma_{t}
, this implies that any static solution on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
must have a zero total mass; and reversely, any inhomogeneous solution with
\left\langle\rho\right\rangle_{\Sigma_{t}}>0
and
\Lambda=0
will necessarily be non-static. This gives a qualitative justification of the zero mass result obtained from the solution (
13
).

Of course the conclusion drawn from the extrapolation from a homogeneous solution to an inhomogeneous one is far from being fully justified, especially since additional kinematic terms are expected to be present in the average expansion laws, like they are in GR
[
5
]
. A full justification requires a careful analysis of the averaging problem in differential geometry, in the case of topo-GR. Nevertheless, as for the non-relativistic limit of GR (in Euclidean topologies)
[
6
]
, this extrapolation holds in the non-relativistic limit of topo-GR, for which the laws (
37
), with
\left\langle\rho\right\rangle_{\Sigma}
and
\left\langle p\right\rangle_{\Sigma}
in place of
\bar{\rho}
and
\bar{p}
, hold regardless of the inhomogeneities present in
\Sigma
, for
\rho
being smooth as well as for
\rho
being, e.g., a Dirac comb
[
33
,
34
]
. This gives us confidence in our prediction that a solution of topo-GR with positive mass cannot be static.

Note that, since topo-GR is equivalent to GR in Euclidean (closed) topologies, this also suggests that closed static solutions of GR should necessarily have a zero total mass. Using a quasi-local definition for the mass, i.e. involving a surface integral as for the Komar mass (
32
), this property for GR is exact in closed topologies. Indeed, evaluating the mass on both (compact) sides of a given 2-surface amounts to flipping the sign of the surface integral [
s_{\mu}\mapsto-s_{\mu}
in equation (
32
)], and therefore necessarily leads to a zero total mass. However, using other definitions of mass, that property might not hold anymore. Of course, this argument is also valid in topo-GR.

4.3
Vacuum
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in General Relativity vs. in topo-GR

In this section, we compare general relativity with topo-GR in describing an empty
\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe while allowing for singularities, taking the example of the solution studied in Section
3
.

4.3.1
With spherical symmetry

Let us consider the following setup: a 3-sphere filled with a compact spherically symmetric patch of matter, and vacuum for the rest of the volume. We would expect this setup to be described by general relativity. Due to the Birkhoff theorem, if that setup is solution of the Einstein equation with
\Lambda=0
, then the vacuum region must be locally equivalent to the Schwarzschild metric, and more precisely, that whole region, by continuity, is described by a single Schwarzschild metric.
6
6

6

A similar situation, with two patches of matter instead of one, was studied by Sussman
[
26
,
27
]
. In this study, the two patches of matter are homogeneous and correspond to FLRW spacetimes replacing the spatial infinities of the Schwarzschild metric.

Placing the matter at the north pole, the south pole is by hypothesis a regular vacuum center of symmetry, around which the volume is finite. Such a center of symmetry is only possible with the Schwarzschild metric if a zero mass parameter is assumed. In other words, the metric in the vacuum region of a 3-sphere with one compact spherically symmetric distribution of matter is the Minkowski metric due to Birkhoff’s theorem. Still within GR, this result holds regardless of the size of the patch of matter. Therefore, for a patch of matter small compared to the size of the 3-sphere, that sphere would be exactly spatially flat nearly everywhere except from this small patch. This is not a behaviour we would deem ‘‘natural’’. What would be expected for such a setup is to have a vacuum region having an isotropic positive spatial curvature, constant far from the patch of matter.
7
7

7

In reality, what probably happens for this setup is that the total volume of the flat region (i.e. the vacuum) should be small compared to the volume of the patch of matter, since all the curvature needed to retrieve the topology of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is localised in the matter.

The situation is even “worse” if, instead of a patch of matter, we consider vacuum everywhere on the sphere except for eventual singularities (still assuming spherical symmetry). The Birkhoff theorem still applies, meaning that everywhere on the sphere but on one or two poles, the metric is locally equivalent to a single Minkowski metric (for one singularity), or to a single Schwarzschild metric (for two singularities). This is not possible if we require finite spatial volume induced by the metric
[
28
]
.

The above discussion is a quick explanation for why a spherically symmetric vacuum (but allowing for singularities) solution on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
of general relativity is not possible
[see
28
, for a detailed explanation]
. In other words, general relativity cannot describe one BH (or two if placed at opposite poles) or repulsive naked singularities (the result does not depend on the sign of the Schwarzschild mass parameter) in an empty
\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe with
\Lambda=0
. As is well known, this also holds for a homogeneous vacuum in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with or without
\Lambda
.

To our opinion, this result suggests the same interpretation we made from the result derived in
[
33
]
: i.e. that general relativity might be incomplete for non-Euclidean topologies. In
[
33
]
, that interpretation came from the result of the non-existence of a non-relativistic limit of general relativity in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{3}
. In the present discussion, if we consider that one or two black holes in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
should be something authorised by our relativistic gravitation theory (regardless of the value of
\Lambda
), this means that we should modify the Einstein equation such that this physical system is authorised. It turns out that the modification proposed in
[
33
]
to solve the “problem” related to the non existence of the non-relativistic limit, also solves the “problem” related to the non-existence of a vacuum, or a single/double singularity in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in GR (for the regular vacuum case, it is given by our solution with
\chi_{\rm h}=0
). Of course the static singular solution discussed in the present paper features the unphysical repulsive singularity, but as a proof of concept, it shows that we can describe systems in non-trivial topologies that are not authorised by general relativity. The definite proof that a physical system with only attractive singularities in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
can be described by topo-GR still remains to be given with an exact metric. This is discussed in Section
4.4
.

4.3.2
Without spherical symmetry

If we drop the hypothesis of one or two BHs, i.e. we drop spherical symmetry, it is possible to describe BHs in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
in GR using the ‘‘lattice cosmology’’ approach
8
8

8

It is possible to keep spherical symmetry and have a solution on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
if
\Lambda\not=0
is assumed
[
28
, see]
, but two causally disconnected BHs are needed.
. This approach aims at describing BHs in an
\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe by solving exactly the Gauss–Codazzi constraints for the initial conditions
[
9
,
2
,
8
,
3
, e.g.]
, and then numerically evolving the solution. This method is of importance for cosmology as it describes a cosmological model with a highly inhomogeneous distribution of matter, allowing for the study of the non-linear regime of structure formation
[
3
]
. For this reason, a comparison between the implementation of this method in general relativity and in topo-GR is of interest. In the following discussion, we aim at presenting what difference we expect between lattice cosmology in GR and in topo-GR, taking into account the solution studied in this paper.

The principle of the method of lattice cosmology is to assume vacuum; initial staticity of the metric, i.e.
K_{ij}=0
for the initial extrinsic curvature
9
9

9

It is also possible to have initial non-staticity by assuming, e.g.,
K_{ij}=\sqrt{\Lambda/3}\,h_{ij}

[
11
]
.
; and perform a conformal decomposition of the initial spatial metric assuming the conformal metric to be
\tilde{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}
. In that case, the initial conformal factor
\phi
is solution of a linear equation: the Lichnerowicz–York equation

\displaystyle\left(\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{3}}-\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{R}}}{8}\right)\phi=0,

(39)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{R}}
is the scalar curvature of the conformal spatial metric. The BH setup on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, or “punctures” in the vocabulary used by
[
2
,
17
]
, is then obtained by summing the solution of that equation with a single Dirac delta as a source, and placed at different positions on the sphere. The initial condition is then evolved numerically.

The initial hypersurface described by the metric
h_{ij}=\phi^{4}\tilde{h}^{\mathbb{S}^{3}}_{ij}
is fully spacelike and is similar to the fully spacelike hypersurfaces we obtain with the Schwarzschild metric and the BH-RNS solution in isotropic coordinates, as described in Appendix
B
. Similarly to Schwarzschild, that hypersurface in the case of lattice cosmology has infinite volume, and features horizons which delimit different causally disconnected regions. While the full volume is infinite, it can be shown that for
N\geqslant 3
black holes, one of the regions has a finite volume and is surrounded by
N
horizons (see for instance Figure 2 in
[
8
]
). This region is interpreted as describing
N
black holes in an expanding 3-sphere. The other regions are asymptotically flat (called “infinite flat ends”), as is the case with the Schwarzschild metric. These regions represent an asymptotically flat space filled with one non spherically symmetric BH.

The BH-RNS solution of topo-GR has a finite spatial volume (see Figure
3
) as the infinities of the Schwarzschild metric have been replaced by repulsive naked singularities at finite distance. This suggests that the same behaviour will be present in a lattice cosmology approach of topo-GR: with any number of BHs, all regions should have a finite volume and could each be interpreted as an
\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe. In other words, we expect that the infinite flat ends present in lattice cosmology in GR will be replaced by repulsive naked singularities at finite distance in topo-GR.
It remains to be shown that the lattice cosmology approach can be performed in topo-GR. In particular, a linear equation similar to (
39
) must be obtained. Such a study is an interesting follow-up to the work of the present paper.

4.4
Towards a non-static physical solution

In this section, we discuss what is the next step in the search for exact inhomogeneous solutions of topo-GR. We also put in perspective this search with the study of BHs in an expanding closed universe.

4.4.1
Removing the staticity hypothesis

As presented in Section
4.2
, the initial hypothesis of staticity is probably what led to the presence of (non-physical) repulsive singularities. Therefore, a more physical approach would be to solve the system (
6
)–(
7
) with the line element (
9
), i.e. with time dependence. We expect the solution to represent one, or two, BHs (with positive mass) at opposite poles of an
expanding
3-sphere. As a first step towards finding this metric, Newtonian gravitation on
\mathbb{S}^{3}
can help us guessing its first order. The Newtonian gravitational potential of a point mass in an expanding vacuum
\mathbb{S}^{3}
universe was derived in
[
34
]
to be

\displaystyle\Phi(\chi,t)=-\frac{M_{\rm N}}{a(t)}\left(\cot\chi\right)\left(1-\chi/\pi\right),

(40)

where
a(t)=\left(\frac{3\kappa M_{\rm N}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)^{1/3}\,t^{2/3}
. Topo-GR is built such that it includes Newtonian gravitation in any spatial topology in a limit
c\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, using the dictionary derived in
[
33
, Section 6.3]
, we can predict a possible solution of equations (
6
)–(
7
) with the non static spherical line element (
9
) to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}

\displaystyle=-\left[1+2\frac{M_{\rm N}}{a(t)\,c^{2}}\left(\cot\chi\right)\left(1-\chi/\pi\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle\qquad+a^{2}(t)\left\{\left[1-2\frac{M_{\rm N}}{a(t)\,c^{2}}\left(\cot\chi\right)\left(1-\chi/\pi\right)\right]\mathrm{d}\chi^{2}+\sin^{2}\chi\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^{2}\right\}+\mathcal{O}\left(1/c^{3}\right),

at leading orders. The full exact metric is expected to be only a function of the Newtonian potential (
40
) and the scale factor, as is often the case in solutions of general relativity built from Newtonian potentials.

4.4.2
A black hole in an expanding, finite volume, universe

The aforementioned metric would describe a black hole in an expanding Universe. The study of such a system is of high interest with respect to a recent debate on a possible coupling between black holes and the expansion of the Universe. This coupling was hypothesised by
Croker &
Weiner 2019
, claimed to be observed by
Farrah
et al. 2023
, and questioned by (e.g.)
Cadoni et al. 2023
. Therefore, the interest of searching for such a metric goes beyond the study of topo-GR, as it will also enter the debate about the role of black holes in cosmology.

Most of the existing approaches for studying a black hole in an expanding universe assume: i) non-vacuum; ii) asymptotically infinite FLRW spacetime. To our opinion, a key complementary approach would be to rather consider the following set of hypothesis:

(i)

vacuum,

(ii)

finite volume, non-static spacelike hypersurfaces.

The vacuum hypothesis may be more representative of our Universe which can be seen as being filled with small patches of matter surrounded by vacuum. Because of the vacuum hypothesis, the evolution of the matter density at infinity cannot be used to track the expansion anymore. In other words, without matter, there is no reference allowing for the definition of expansion. In a sense, this is in line with the interpretation of Mach’s principle made by Einstein. Therefore, the role of the volume finiteness hypothesis is to reintroduce such a reference and to allow for the study of the expansion via the evolution of the global compact spacelike volume surrounding the BH. The non-static metric we would look for, as described in Section
4.4.1
, would fulfill these two hypotheses. In this sense, this metric would offer a complementary approach in studying the relation between black holes and expansion.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we derived a first exact inhomogeneous solution in a non-Euclidean topology (i.e. for which the spatial covering space in not
\mathbb{E}^{3}
) of the Topologically Corrected General Relativity (topo-GR) developed in
[
31
]
. The solution is static with spherical symmetry and represents an attractive singularity shielded by a horizon (black hole) and a repulsive naked singularity placed at opposite poles of a 3-sphere. We analysed the properties of this solution. In particular, we showed that its Penrose–Carter diagram is similar to the Schwarzschild diagram, but from which the two spatial infinities are cut and replaced by repulsive naked singularities at finite distance, and that the total Komar mass of the solution is zero: positive mass for the black hole and opposite negative mass for the repulsive naked singularity. This zero total mass is consistent with an important result of topo-GR derived in
[
33
]
for cosmology: a closed universe filled with homogeneous matter cannot be static unless the total mass is zero. While that result was derived for a homogeneous solution, the metric obtained in the present paper confirms this result for a specific inhomogeneous solution. This suggests that any physical solutions (i.e. with positive mass) of topo-GR in closed topologies require non-staticity.

A direct follow-up we expect for this paper is to search for a non-static solution that would describe a single black hole in an expanding universe with the topology of a 3-sphere, something which is not possible in general relativity. To further identify the differences between topo-GR and general relativity in closed topologies, and to obtain a highly inhomogeneous model universe for cosmology in this context, we also argued that an interesting study would be to develop the method of lattice cosmology for topo-GR.
