Online Submodular Maximization via Online Convex Optimization
Tareq Si Salem, Gözde Özcan, Iasonas Nikolaou, Evimaria Terzi, Stratis Ioannidis

Abstract
We study monotone submodular maximization under general matroid constraints in the online setting. We prove that online optimization of a large class of submodular functions, namely, weighted threshold potential functions, reduces to online convex optimization (OCO). This is precisely because functions in this class admit a concave relaxation; as a result, OCO policies, coupled with an appropriate rounding scheme, can be used to achieve sublinear regret in the combinatorial setting. We show that our reduction extends to many different versions of the online learning problem, including the dynamic regret, bandit, and optimistic-learning settings.

Introduction

In online submodular optimization (OSM)
[
1
]
, submodular reward functions
chosen by an adversary are revealed over several rounds. In each round, a decision maker first commits to a set satisfying, e.g., matroid constraints. Subsequently, the reward function is revealed and evaluated over this set. The objective is to minimize
\alpha

\alpha

,

3

5

Online convex optimization (OCO) studies a similar online setting in which reward functions are concave, and decisions are selected from a compact convex set. First proposed by
Zinkevich 2003
, who showed that projected gradient ascent attains sublinear regret, OCO generalizes previous online problems like prediction with expert advice
[
7
]
, and has become widely influential in the learning community
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Its success is evident from the multitude of OCO variants in literature: in

[

,
11

12

16

Bandit
OCO algorithms
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
study the aforementioned bandit setting, where again only rewards (i.e., function evaluations) are observed.

We make the following contributions:

•

We provide a methodology for reducing OSM to OCO, when submodular functions selected by the adversary are bounded from above and below by concave relaxations and coupled with an opportune rounding. We prove that algorithms and regret guarantees in the OCO setting transfer to
\alpha
-regret guarantees in the OSM setting, via a transformation that we introduce. Ratio
\alpha
is determined by how well concave relaxations approximate the original submodular functions.

•

We show that the above condition is satisfied by a wide class of submodular functions, namely,
weighted threshold potential
(WTP) functions. This class strictly generalizes weighted coverage functions
[
20
,
21
]
, and includes many important applications, including influence maximization

[

We show our reduction also extends to the dynamic regret and optimistic settings, reducing such full-information OSM settings to the respective OCO variants. To the best of our knowledge, our resulting algorithms are the first to come with guarantees for the dynamic regret and optimistic settings in the context of OSM with general matroid constraints.
Finally, we also provide a different reduction for the bandit setting, again for all three (static, dynamic regret, and optimistic) variants; this reduction applies to general submodular functions, but is restricted to partition matroids.

Related Work

Offline Submodular Maximization and Relaxations of Submodular Functions.
Continuous relaxations of submodular functions play a prominent role in submodular maximization. The so-called continuous greedy algorithm
[
34
]
maximizes the multilinear relaxation of a submodular objective over the convex hull of a matroid, using a variant of the Frank-Wolfe algorithm
[
35
]
. The fractional solution is then rounded via pipage
[
36
]
or swap rounding
[
37
]
, which we also use. The multilinear relaxation is not convex but is continuous DR-submodular
[
38
,
39
]
, and continuous greedy comes with a
1-\frac{1}{e}
approximation guarantee. However, the multilinear relaxation is generally not tractable and is usually estimated via sampling.
Ever since the seminal paper by Ageev and Sviridenko
[
36
]
, several works have exploited the existence of concave relaxations of weighted coverage functions (e.g.,
[
20
,
25
]
), a strict subset of the threshold potential functions we consider here. For coverage functions, a version of our “sandwich” property (Asm.
2
) follows directly by the Goemans & Williamson inequality
[
40
]
, which we also use. Both in the standard
[
36
]
and stochastic offline
[
20
,
25
]
submodular maximization setting, in which the objective is randomized, exploiting concave relaxations of coverage functions yields significant computational dividends, as it eschews any sampling required for estimating the multilinear relaxation. We depart from both by considering a much broader class than coverage functions and studying the online/no-regret setting.

OSM via Regret Minimization.
Several online algorithms have been proposed for maximizing general submodular functions
[
4
,
2
,
3
,
1
]
under different matroid constraints. There has also been recent work
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
on the online maximization of continuous DR-submodular functions
[
38
]
. Proposed algorithms are applicable to our setting, because the multi-linear relaxation is DR-submodular, and guarantees can be extended to matroid constraint sets again through rounding
[
37
]
, akin to the approach we follow here. Also pertinent is the work by
Kakade et al. 2007
: their proposed online algorithm operates over reward functions that can be decomposed as the weighted sum of finitely many (non-parametric) reference functions—termed Linearly Weighted Decomposable (LWD); the adversary selects only the weights. Applied to OSM, this is a more restrictive function class than the ones we study.

and remaining algorithms that either operate on restricted constraint sets

. Most importantly, our work generalizes to the dynamic and optimistic settings. With the exception of

, who study dynamic regret restricted to uniform matroids, our work provides the first guarantees for OSM in the dynamic and optimistic settings under general matroid constraints.

Our reduction to OCO in the bandit setting extends the analysis by

Technical Preliminary

Submodularity and Matroids.

Given a ground set
V=[n]\triangleq\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}
,
a set function
f:2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}
is
submodular
if
f(S\cup\left\{i,j\right\})-f(S\cup\left\{j\right\})\leq f(S\cup\left\{i\right\})-f(S)
for all
S\subseteq V
and
i,j\in V\setminus S
and
monotone
if
f(A)\leq f(B)
for all
A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{V}
. A
matroid
is a pair
\mathcal{M}=(V,\mathcal{I})
, where
\mathcal{I}\subseteq 2^{V}
, for which the following holds: (1) if
B\in\mathcal{I}
and
A\subseteq B
, then
A\in\mathcal{I}
, (2) if
A,B\in\mathcal{I}
and
\left|A\right|<\left|B\right|
, then there exists an
x\in B\setminus A
s.t.
A\cup\left\{x\right\}\in\mathcal{I}
. The
rank

r\in\mathbb{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
is the cardinality of the largest set in
\mathcal{I}
.
With slight abuse of notation, we represent set functions
f:2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}
as functions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
: given a set function
f
and an
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, we denote by
f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
as the value
f(\mathrm{supp}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right))
, where
\mathrm{supp}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)\triangleq\left\{i\in V:x_{i}\neq 0\right\}\subseteq V
is the support of
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
. Similarly, we treat matroids as subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Online Learning.

In the general protocol of
online learning

[
41
]
, a decision-maker makes sequential decisions and incurs rewards as follows: at timeslot
t\in[T]
, where
T\in\mathbb{N}
is the time horizon, the decision-maker first commits to a decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
from some set
\mathcal{X}
. Then, a reward function
f_{t}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is selected by an adversary from a set of functions
\mathcal{F}
and revealed to the decision-maker, who accrues a reward
f_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
. The decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is determined according to a (potentially random) mapping
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X},t}:\mathcal{X}^{t-1}\times\mathcal{F}^{t-1}\to\mathcal{Y}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X},t}\left(\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{s}\right)^{t-1}_{s=1},\left(f_{s}\right)^{t-1}_{s=1}\right).

(1)

Let
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}=({\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{X},t})_{t\in[T]}
be the
online policy
of the decision-maker.
We define the
regret
of
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
at horizon
T
as:

\begin{split}\mathrm{regret}_{T}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})\triangleq\sup_{({f}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{T}}\bigg\{\max_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})-\underset{{{\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right]\bigg\}.\end{split}

(2)

We seek policies that attain a sublinear (i.e.,
o(T)
) regret; intuitively, such policies perform on average as
well as the static optimum in hindsight.
Note that the regret is defined w.r.t. the optimal
fixed
decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
, i.e., the time-invariant decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
that would be optimal in hindsight, after the sequence
(f_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is revealed. When selecting
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
, the decision-maker has no information about the upcoming reward
f_{t}
. Finally, this is the

setting: at each timeslot, the decision maker observes the entire reward function
f_{t}(\,\cdot\,)
, rather than just the reward
f_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Deviating from these assumptions is of both practical and theoretical interest. In the
dynamic regret
setting
[
6
]
, the regret is measured w.r.t. a time-variant optimum, appropriately constrained so that changes from one timeslot to the next do not vary significantly.
In
learning with optimism

[
14
]
, additional information is assumed to be available w.r.t.
f_{t}
, in the form of so-called predictions. In the
bandit
setting
[
42
]
, the online policy
{\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
of the decision maker only has access to rewards
f_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, as opposed to the entire reward function.

Problem Formulation

We consider a combinatorial version of the online learning protocol defined in Eq. (
1
). In particular, we focus on the case where (a) the decision set is
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
, i.e., the vectorized representation of subsets of
V=[n]
, and (b) the set
\mathcal{F}
of reward functions comprises set functions over
\mathcal{X}
. Though some of our results (e.g., Thm.
2
) pertain to this general combinatorial setting, we are particularly interested in the case where (a)
\mathcal{X}
is a matroid, and (b)
\mathcal{F}
is the WTP class, i.e., the set of functions whose form is given by Eq. (
4
).

Both in the general combinatorial setting and for WTP functions, evaluating the best fixed decision may be computationally intractable even in hindsight, i.e., when all reward functions were revealed. As is customary (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
), instead of the regret in Eq. (
2
), we consider the so-called
\alpha
-regret:

\begin{split}\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})\triangleq\sup_{\left(f_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{T}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{T}}\bigg\{\max_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})-\underset{{{\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\right]\bigg\}.\end{split}

(5)

Intuitively, we compare the performance of the policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
w.r.t. the best polytime
(n)

\alpha
-approximation of the static offline optimum in hindsight. For example, the approximation ratio would be
\alpha=1-1/e
in the case of submodular set functions maximized over matroids.

\Xi

Armed with this assumption, we can convert any OCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
operating over
\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
to a
Randomized-rounding Augmented OCO
(RAOCO) policy, denoted by
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
, operating over
\mathcal{X}
. This transformation (see Alg.
1
) uses both the randomized-rounding
\Xi
, as well as the concave relaxations
(\tilde{f}_{s})_{s=1}^{t-1}
of the functions
(f_{s})_{s=1}^{t-1}
observed so far. At
t\in[T]
, the RAOCO policy amounts to:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{Y},t}\left((\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{s})^{t-1}_{s=1},(\tilde{f}_{s})^{t-1}_{s=1}\right),

(8a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\Xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t})\in\mathcal{X}.

(8b)

In short, the OCO policy
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is first used to generate a new fractional state
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
by applying
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{Y},t}
to the history of concave relaxations.
Then, this fractional decision
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
is randomly mapped to an integral decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
according to the rounding scheme
\Xi
. Then, the reward
f(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})
is received and
f_{t}
is revealed, at which point a concave function
\tilde{f}_{t}
is constructed from
f_{t}
and added to the history. Our first main result is the following:

Theorem 2
.

Under Asm.
2
, given an OCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
, the RAOCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
described by Alg.
1
satisfies

\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)\leq\alpha\cdot\mathrm{regret}_{T}\left({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}\right).

The proof is in Appendix
D
. As a result,
any regret guarantee obtained by an OCO algorithm over
\mathcal{Y}
, immediately transfers to an
\alpha
-regret for RAOCO, where
\alpha
is determined by Asm.
2
.

In particular, the decision set
\mathcal{Y}
is closed, bounded, and convex by construction. Combined with the fact that concave relaxations
\tilde{f}
are
L
-Lipschitz (by Asm.
2
), Thms.
1
and
2
yield the following corollary:

Corollary 1
.

Under Asm.
2
, RAOCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
in Alg.
1
equipped with OGA, OMA, or FTRL as OCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
has sublinear
\alpha
-regret. That is,
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{T}\right).

To use this result, Asm.
2
should hold, and both the randomized rounding and the concave relaxations used in RAOCO should be poly-time: all are true for WTP functions optimized over matroid constraints, which we turn to next.

5.2
The Case of Weighted Threshold Potentials

We now consider the case where the decision set is a matroid, and reward functions belong to the class of WTP functions, defined by Eq. (
4
). We will show that, under appropriate definitions of a randomized rounding and concave relaxations, the class
\mathcal{F}
satisfies Asm.
2
and, thus, online learning via RAOCO comes with the regret guarantees of Corollary
1
.
For
\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
, consider the map
f\mapsto\tilde{f}
of WTP functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
to concave relaxations
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form:

\begin{split}\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\triangleq f(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\textstyle\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\min\left\{b_{\ell},\sum_{j\in S_{\ell}}y_{j}w_{\ell,j}\right\},\end{split}

(9)

for
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
. In other words,
the relaxation of
f
is itself
: it has the same functional form, allowing integral variables to become fractional.
2
2

2

In Appendix
G.IV
, we provide an example where the functional form of concave relaxations differs.
This is clearly concave, as the minimum of affine functions is concave, and the positively weighted sum of concave functions is concave. Finally, all such functions are Lipschitz, with a parameter that depends on
c_{\ell}
,
b_{\ell}
,
\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in C
.
Let
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
be the image of
\mathcal{F}
under the map (

).
We make the following assumption, which is readily satisfied if, e.g., all constituent parameters (
c_{\ell}
,
b_{\ell}
,
\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in C
) are uniformly bounded, or the set
\mathcal{F}
is finite, etc.:

Assumption 3
.

There exists an
L>0
such that all functions in
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}
are
L
-Lipschitz.

Next, we turn our attention to the randomized rounding
\Xi
. We can in fact characterize the property that
\Xi
must satisfy for Asm.
2
to hold for relaxations given by Eq. (
9
):

Definition 1
.

A randomized rounding
\Xi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
is
negatively correlated
if, for
\bm{\mathbf{x}}=\Xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\in\mathcal{X}
(a) the coordinates of
\bm{\mathbf{x}}
are negatively correlated
3
3

3

A set of random variables
x_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\},i\in[n]
, are negatively correlated if
\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right]\leq\prod_{i\in S}\mathbb{E}\left[x_{i}\right]
for all

and (b)
\mathbb{E}_{\Xi}\left[\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right]=\bm{\mathbf{y}}
.

Our next result immediately implies that
any negatively correlated rounding can be used in RAOCO

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Xi:{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathcal{X}
be a negatively correlated randomized rounding, and consider the concave relaxations
\tilde{f}
constructed from
f\in\mathcal{F}
via Eq. (

3

satisfies Asm.
2

\alpha=\left(1-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}

The proof is in Appendix
E
. As
\Delta\to\infty

\alpha

\Delta

B
.
Finally, and most importantly,
a negatively-correlated randomized rounding can always be constructed if
\mathcal{X}
is a matroid

Chekuri et al. 2010
provide two polynomial-time randomized rounding algorithms that satisfy this property:

Lemma 2
.

(
Chekuri et al. 2010
)
Given a matroid
\mathcal{X}\subset\{0,1\}^{n}
, let
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
and
\Xi
be either

or

\Xi
is negatively correlated.

We review swap rounding in Appendix
F

\mathcal{X}
is a matroid

1

Putting everything together, Lemmas
1
–
2
and Corollary
1
yield the following:

Theorem 3
.

Let

be a matroid, and
\mathcal{F}
be a subset of the WTP class for which Asm.

holds. Then, the RAOCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
defined by Alg.

, and concave relaxations in Eq. (

)
has sublinear
\alpha

\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{T}\right).

Note that, though all algorithms yield

regret, the dependence of constants on problem parameters (such as

Concave Relaxations and Randomized Rounding.

Concave relaxations are linear in the representation of the function
f
, but in practice come “for free”, once parameters in Eq. (
4
) are provided. Swap rounding over a general matroid is
\mathcal{O}\left(nr^{2}\right)
, where
r
is the rank of the matroid
[
37
]
. This dominates the remaining operations (including OCO), and thus determines the overall complexity of our algorithm.
This
\mathcal{O}\left(nr^{2}\right)
term assumes access to the decomposition of a fractional point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
to bases of the matroid. Carathéodory’s theorem implies the existence of such decomposition of at most
n+1
points in
\mathcal{X}
; moreover, there exists a decomposition algorithm
[
49
]
for general matroids with running time
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{6}\right)
.
However, in all practical cases we consider (including uniform and partition matroids) the complexity is significantly lower. More specifically,
for partition matroids, swap rounding reduces to an algorithm with linear time complexity, namely,
\mathcal{O}\left(mn\right)
for
m
partitions
[
50
]
.

Extensions

Dynamic Setting.

In the dynamic setting, the decision maker compares its performance to the best sequence of decisions
(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t})_{t\in[T]}
with a path length regularity condition
[
6
,
51
]
. I.e., let

\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}(T,P_{T})\triangleq\left\{\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\right)^{T}_{t=1}\in\mathcal{X}^{T}:\sum^{T}_{t=1}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}-\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rVert\leq P_{T}\right\}\subset\mathcal{X}^{T}

be the set of sequences of decision in a set
\mathcal{X}
with path length less than
P_{T}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
over a time horizon
T
. We extend the definition of the regret (Eq. (
5
)) to that of
dynamic
\alpha
-regret
:

\displaystyle\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})\triangleq\sup_{\left({f}_{t}\right)^{T}_{t=1}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{T}}\bigg\{\max_{\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\star}_{t}\right)^{T}_{t=1}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{X}}(T,P_{T})}\alpha\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\star}_{t})-\sum^{T}_{t=1}{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t})\bigg\}.

When
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})
is sublinear in
T
, the policy attains average rewards that asymptotically compete with the optimal decisions
of bounded path length
, in hindsight.

Through our reduction to OCO, we can leverage OGA
[
6
]
or meta-learning algorithms over OGA
[
52
]
to obtain dynamic regret guarantees in OSM. As an additional technical contribution, we provide the first sufficient and necessary conditions for OMA to admit a dynamic regret guarantee (see Appendix
A
). This allows to extend a specific instance of OMA operating on the simplex, the so-called fixed-share algorithm
[
51
,
53
]
, to matroid polytopes. This yields a tighter regret guarantee than OGA
[
6
,
52
]
(see Theorem
7
in Appendix
A
).
Putting everything together, we get:

Theorem 4
.

Under Asm.
2
, RAOCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
described by Alg.
1
equipped with an OMA policy in Appendix
A
as OCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}

has sublinear dynamic
\alpha
-regret, i.e.,

\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{P_{T}T}\right).

This follows from Thm.
2
and the dynamic regret guarantee for OMA in Appendix
A
.

Optimistic Setting.

In the optimistic setting, the decision maker has access to additional information available in the form of predictions: a function
\pi_{t+1}:\left[0,1\right]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, serving as a prediction of the reward function
f_{t+1}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
is made available before committing to a decision
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}
at timeslot
t\in[T]
. The prediction
\pi_{t}
encodes prior information available to the decision maker at timeslot
t
.
4
4

4

Function
\pi_{t}
can extend over fractional values, e.g., be the multi-linear relaxation of a set function.
Let
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}
and
\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}
be supergradients of
\tilde{f}_{t}
and
\pi_{t}
at point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
, respectively. We can define an optimistic OMA policy
(see Alg.
3
in Appendix
A
) that leverages both the prediction and the observed rewards. Applying again our reduction of OSM to this setting we get:

Theorem 5
.

Under Asm.
2
, RAOCO policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}
in Alg.
1
equipped with OOMA in Appendix
A
as policy
\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
yields

\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}({\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}}_{\mathcal{X}})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{P_{T}\sum^{T}_{t=1}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\infty}}\right).

This theorem follows from Theorem
2
and the optimistic regret guarantee established for OMA policies in Theorem
7
in Appendix
A
.
The optimistic regret guarantee in Theorem

shows that the regret of a policy can be reduced to

when the predictions are perfect, while providing
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{T}\right)
guarantee in Thm.
3
when the predictions are arbitrarily bad (with bounded gradients).
To the best of our knowledge, ours is the first work to provide guarantees for optimistic OSM.

Appendix A
Online Convex Optimization

In this section, we describe two powerful families of OCO policies: online mirror ascent (OMA, in Appendix
A.I
) and follow the regularized leader (FTRL, in Appendix
4
). Online gradient ascent (OGA) can be described as a special case of OMA, under a Euclidean mirror map. Our exposition below also simultaneously covers the guarantees that come with OMA in the dynamic and optimistic settings; both follow from Theorem
7
(see Appendix
G
for the derivation). Results for the static setting (e.g., Theorem
1
) also follow from Theorem
7
by appropriately restricting the corresponding parameters (see Appendix
A.III
).

The setting, algorithms, and analysis we present here are a combination of the ones provided by
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2013a
,
Zinkevich 2003
, and
Zhao et al. 2020b
. In particular,
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2013a
present OMA in the optimistic setting, while
Zinkevich 2003
and
Zhao et al. 2020b
studied OGA and OMA, respectively, in the dynamic setting. In the “master theorem” (Theorem
7
) that we present below, we combine the proof techniques by all three, to give an algorithm that comes with simultaneous guarantees in all settings (static or dynamic, optimistic or non-optimistic, and combinations thereof).

A.I
Online Mirror Ascent

Online Mirror Ascent
[
43
, Sec. 5.3]
is the online version of the mirror ascent (MA) algorithm
[
59
]
for convex optimization of a fixed, known function; OMA is a class of policies that generalize OGA. The main premise behind mirror ascent is that variables and supergradients live in two distinct spaces: the primal space, for variables, and the dual space, for supergradients. The two are linked via a function known as a mirror map. Contrary to standard supergradient ascent, updates using the gradient occur on the dual space; the mirror map is used to invert this update to a change on the primal variables. For several constrained optimization problems of
interest, mirror ascent leads to faster convergence compared to gradient ascent
[
43
, Sec. 4.3]
.

The primal and dual spaces are
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To disambiguate between the two, we denote primal points by
\bm{\mathbf{y}},\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and dual points by
\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}},\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. Formally, OMD is parameterized by (1) a learning rate
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and (2) a differentiable map
\Phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{D}
is its domain.

Assumption 4
.

The map
\Phi:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

The domain
\mathcal{D}
of
\Phi
is a convex and open set such that the decision set
\mathcal{Y}
is included in its closure, i.e.,
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathrm{closure}(\mathcal{D})
, and their intersection is nonempty
\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{D}\neq\emptyset
.

2.

The map
\Phi
is
\rho
strongly-convex over
\mathcal{D}
w.r.t. a norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert
and differentiable over
\mathcal{D}
.

3.

The map
\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{x}}):\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is surjective.

4.

The gradient of
\Phi
diverges on the boundary of
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.,
\lim_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\to\partial\mathcal{D}}\lVert\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\rVert=+\infty
, where
\partial\mathcal{D}=\mathrm{closure}(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{D}
.

When the map
\Phi
satisfies the above properties is said to be a
mirror map
.

OMA takes the form described in Alg.
2
. Given
\eta
and
\Phi
, an OMA iteration proceeds as follows: after receiving the reward
\tilde{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t})
and the reward function
\tilde{f}_{t}(\,\cdot\,)
is revealed, the current state
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
is first mapped from the primal to the dual space via:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}=\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}).

(10)

Then, a regular supergradient ascent step is performed in the
dual space
to obtain an updated dual point:

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}_{t+1}=\hat{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}_{t}+\eta\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t},

(11)

where
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}\in\partial\tilde{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t})
is the supergradient of
\tilde{f}_{t}
at point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}
. This updated dual point is then mapped back to the primal space using the inverse of mapping
\nabla\Phi
, i.e.:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}=\left(\nabla\Phi\right)^{-1}\!(\hat{\bm{\mathbf{z}}}_{t+1}).

(12)

The resulting primal point
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}
may lie outside the decision set
\mathcal{Y}
. To obtain the final feasible point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}\in\mathcal{Y}
, a projection is made using the Bregman divergence associated with the mirror map
\Phi
. The final result becomes:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{D}}^{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{t+1}),

(13)

where
\Pi_{\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{D}}^{\Phi}(\,\cdot\,)
is the Bregman projection (
15
) associated to
\Phi
onto the set
\mathcal{Y}\cap\mathcal{D}
. The steps described Eqs. (
10
)–(
13
) define OMA. The illustration in Fig.
2
summarizes these steps. Note that
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}
is a function of
\{(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t},\tilde{f}_{t})\}\subseteq\{(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{s},\tilde{f}_{s})\}^{t}_{s=1}
, hence OMA is indeed an OCO policy.

We conclude our description of OMA by providing a formal definition of supergradients and the Bregman projection.

Definition 2
.

The superdifferential of a concave function
\tilde{f}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined as the set
\partial\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of supergradients s.t. every
\bm{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\in\partial\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})
satisfies the following inequality

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})-\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})\geq\bm{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\cdot(\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}),\quad\text{for every $\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.}

(14)

When the function
\tilde{f}
is differentiable, then by definition
\partial\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\{\nabla\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\}
, where
\nabla\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})
is the gradient of
\tilde{f}
at point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 3
.

The Bregman projection
[
60
]
associated to a map
\Phi
onto a convex set
\mathcal{S}
is denoted by
\Pi^{\Phi}_{\mathcal{S}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{S}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\Pi^{\Phi}_{\mathcal{S}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathcal{S}}}{\arg\min}\,D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}),

where

\displaystyle D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})=\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})-\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})-\nabla{\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})}\cdot(\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}).

(15)

OMA considers that the decision set is bounded w.r.t. the Bregman divergence associated to
\Phi
. Formally, we assume the following.

Assumption 5
.

Consider a decision set
\mathcal{Y}
and a mirror map
\Phi
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1}\in\mathcal{Y}
. The Bregman divergence
D_{\Phi}
associated to
\Phi
is bounded over
\mathcal{Y}
, i.e., there exits
D<\infty
s.t.

\displaystyle{D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1})}

\displaystyle\leq D^{2},

for any
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

(16)

Assumption 6
.

Consider a decision set
\mathcal{Y}
and a mirror map
\Phi
. The dual norm
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\star}
of the gradient of the mirror map
\Phi
is bounded by
L_{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\rVert_{\star}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\Phi},

for every
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
.

(17)

Note that Asm.
6
is typically not required to establish static regret guarantees (
2
) (see, e.g.,
Hazan 2016
). However, as we remark in Appendix
C
, policies with sublinear static regret do not necessarily perform well in dynamic settings, e.g., this the case for OMA configured with the standard negative entropy mirror map
[
43
]
. In Theorem
7
, we show that Asm.
6
is sufficient to establish sublinear dynamic regret against a comparator sequence (see Sec.
6
) with path length
P_{T}>0
.

Here, we provide a general regret bound for

OMA in Alg.
3
configured with a general mirror map
\Phi
; note that the same algorithm applies to both the optimistic and dynamic settings, and so does the formal guarantee that we provide below. The non-optimistic regret bounds follow from the optimistic regret bounds by setting the predictions to

(

Theorem 7
.

Consider an OCO setting with a convex decision space
\mathcal{Y}
, concave rewards
\tilde{f}\in\hat{\mathcal{F}}
satisfying Asm.
1
from a set
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
selected by an adversary, and predictions
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
for
t\in[T]
. The regret of OOMA in Alg.
3
configured with
\Phi
satisfying Asms.
4
–
6
against a sequence of decision
\left(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}^{\star}\right)_{t\in[T]}\in\Lambda_{\mathcal{Y}}(T,P_{T})
is upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle\leq{\eta{\sum^{T}_{t=1}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}}}/{2\rho}+\left(D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}P_{T}\right)/{\eta}.

(18)

When the learning rate
\eta_{\star}=\sqrt{{2\rho\left(D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}P_{T}\right)}/{\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}}}
is selected, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathrm{regret}_{T,P_{T}}(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{Y}})\leq\sqrt{{2/\rho\left(D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}P_{T}\right)\sum^{T}_{t=1}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}}}

(19)

Proof.

We adapt the proof of
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2013b
to support dynamic regret.

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert_{\star}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert_{\star}+\frac{1}{\eta}\left(D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t-1})-D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})-\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\rVert^{2}\right)

(21)

In order to upper bound the r.h.s. term in the above inequality. We first bound the term
\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert_{\star}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert
, and then, secondly, we bound the term
\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t-1})-D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})\right)
.

First, note that it holds that
ab=\inf_{\eta^{\prime}}\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}a^{2}+\frac{1}{2\eta^{\prime}}b^{2}
, so we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert_{\star}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert

\displaystyle=\inf_{\eta^{\prime}}\left\{\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}+\frac{1}{2\eta^{\prime}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}\right\}

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta}{2\rho}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}+\frac{\rho}{2\eta}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\text{(for some $\eta/\rho$)}.

(23)

Second, we employ the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the bounds on
D_{\Phi}
and
\nabla\Phi
from the assumptions, so we have the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t-1})-D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})\right)

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{0})}_{\text{$\leq D^{2}$}}+\sum_{t\in[T]}\left(D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})-D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\leq D^{2}+\sum_{t\in[T]}\underbrace{\left(\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1})-\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}\right)}_{\text{Cauchy-Schwarz's Ineq.}}-\underbrace{D_{\Phi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1})}_{\text{$\geq 0$}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq D^{2}+\sum_{t\in[T]}\underbrace{\lVert\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1})-\nabla\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\pi}_{t})\rVert_{\star}}_{\text{$\leq L_{\Phi}$\penalty\ \eqref{eq:bounded_grad_map}}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}\rVert

(27)

\displaystyle\leq D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}\rVert.

(28)

Combine Eqs. (
21
)–(
28
) to obtain the final upper bound.

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[T]}\tilde{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t})-\tilde{f}_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t\in[T]}{\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}}\cdot{\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t}}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\Big({D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}\underbrace{\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t+1}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\star}_{t}\rVert}_{\text{$P_{T}$}}}\Big)+\frac{\eta}{2\rho}\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}

(30)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}\Big({D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}P_{T}}\Big)+\frac{\eta}{2\rho}\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}.

(31)

The learning rate
\eta_{\star}=\sqrt{{2\rho\left(D^{2}+2L_{\Phi}P_{T}\right)}/{\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}}}
yields the tightest upper bound given by the theorem. ∎

Note that, to obtain the tightest upper bound on the regret, we assume that when setting the learning rate we have access to quantities
\rho
,
D
,
L_{\Phi}
,
P_{T}
, as well as quantity
l_{T}\triangleq\sqrt{\sum_{t\in[T]}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}\rVert^{2}_{\star}}
. The first three are problem-specific bounds and are usually easy to determine given the problem instance (see also Appendix
C
). Parameter
P_{T}
is a usual problem input, as it bounds the power of the (dynamic) adversary; assuming that it is known is standard (see, e.g.,
Zinkevich 2003
,
Besbes et al. 2015
). Guarantees however can be still provided even if it is not a priori known using the same meta-learning approach as the one discussed next (see, e.g.,
Zhao et al. 2020a
).

Quantity
l_{T}
can readily be bounded by
L\sqrt{T}
in the non-optimistic setting, by Assumption
1
.
However, to obtain a tighter bound, but also when operating in the optimistic setting, this parameter can be learned through a meta-algorithm (see also
Hazan 2016
,
Shalev-Shwartz 2012
,
McMahan 2017
). In short, one can execute in parallel multiple instances of OMA algorithms configured for different
l_{T}
with an appropriately chosen range and resolution,
and frame an expert problem to learn the best expert (policy). The meta problem is a standard prediction with expert advice problem, and can be tackled with well understood parameter-free and computationally efficient learning algorithms
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. Alternatively, one could consider adaptive online algorithms
[
9
]
that adapt to the observed gradients with a slight degradation in performance.

Note that in our implementation and experiments, we perform a grid search for
\eta
values (see Sec.
7
). We also implemented the meta-learning algorithm for the optimistic setting (see Sec.
H
), also with a grid search for
\eta
.

Algorithm 4

Follow the Regularized Leader:
\mathrm{FTRL}_{\eta,R,\mathcal{Y}}(({\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{s}})^{t}_{s=1},({\tilde{f}_{s}})^{t}_{s=1})

1:

Learning rate
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, regularization function
R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, decision set
\mathcal{Y}
, decisions
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{s}
, reward functions
\tilde{f}_{s}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
for
s\in[t]

2:

\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{s}\leftarrow\partial_{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}\tilde{f}_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{s})

3:

\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}\leftarrow\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\,\,\eta\,\sum^{t}_{s=1}\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{s}\cdot\bm{\mathbf{y}}+R(\bm{\mathbf{y}})

\mathcal{X}

\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{t+1}

It is well known that
follow the leader
(FTL) policy, also known as
fictitious play
in economics, fails to provide sublinear regret guarantee
[
8
]
. The FTL policy selects naïvely the decision that would minimize the past costs, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t+1}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum^{t}_{s=1}f_{s}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})
. This policy fails against an adversary; an adversary can drive the policy to change severely its decision from one timeslot to another. The policy can be modified to exhibit more

B.I
Submodularity and Monotonicity

Consider a threshold potential
\Psi_{{b,\bm{\mathbf{w}},S}}(A)
defined in Eq. (
3
) for
A\subseteq V=[n]
. We omit the parameters
{b,\bm{\mathbf{w}},S}
when they are clear from the context. We split our proof into two parts, by first proving submodularity and then monotonicity.

Submodularity.
Given a finite set
V
, the function is said to be submodular if and only if for every
A\subseteq V
and
i,j\in V\setminus A
it holds

\displaystyle f(A\cup\left\{i,j\right\})-f(A\cup\left\{j\right\})

\displaystyle\leq f(A\cup\left\{i\right\})-f(A).

(37)

We have the following

\displaystyle\Psi(A\cup\left\{i\right\})-\Psi(A)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{for $\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k}\geq b$},\\
\min\left\{b-\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k},w_{i}\right\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(38)

It also holds

\displaystyle\Psi(A\cup\left\{i,j\right\})-\Psi(A\cup\left\{j\right\})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\text{for $\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k}\geq b-w_{j}$},\\
\min\left\{b-\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k}-w_{j},w_{i}\right\}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(39)

Combine Eqs. (
38
) and (
39
) to obtain

\displaystyle\Psi(A\cup\left\{i,j\right\})-\Psi(A\cup\left\{j\right\})\leq\Psi(A\cup\left\{i\right\})-\Psi(A).

(40)

Thus, we proved that the set function
g
is submodular. The function of interest in Eq. (
4
) can be expressed for every
S\subseteq V
as

\displaystyle f(A)=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\Psi_{b_{\ell},\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell},S_{\ell}}(A).

(41)

The function
f
is a weighted sum with positive weights of submodular function; therefore, from the definition
f
is also submodular.

Monotonicity.
Consider the sets
A\subseteq B\subseteq V
. We have the following

\displaystyle\Psi(B)=\min\left\{b,\sum_{k\in S\cap B}w_{k}\right\}=\min\left\{b,\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k}+\sum_{k\in S\cap\left(B\setminus A\right)}w_{k}\right\}.

(42)

Note that
\sum_{k\in S\cap\left(B\setminus A\right)}w_{k}\geq 0
. Thus, it holds

\displaystyle\Psi(B)\geq\min\left\{b,\sum_{k\in S\cap A}w_{k}\right\}=\Psi(A).

(43)

We conclude the proof by noting that
f
is a weighted sum with positive weights of monotone functions (
43
) as expressed in Eq. (
41

B.II.1
Weighted Coverage Functions

For
V=[n]

(44)

Observation 1
.

The weighted coverage function

4

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\Psi_{1,\bm{\mathbf{1}},S_{\ell}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

for every
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
.

(45)

This follows from the simple fact that

\displaystyle 1-\prod_{i\in S}(1-x_{i})=\min\left\{1,\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\right\},\quad\text{for all}\penalty\ \bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{n},S\subseteq V.

(46)

Classic problems such as

[
22
]
and facility location
[

can all be expressed via the maximization of weighted coverage functions subject to matroid constraints (see also
[
20
]
). Another such example is cache networks
[
25
]
, which have also been studied in the online setting
[

]
.
Our online setting naturally captures different epidemics over the same graph in influence maximization and dynamic tasks in facility location
[

Influence Maximization
[
22
,
20
]
.

In the classic paper by Kempe et al.
[
22

, a network is represented as a graph

and a probabilistic model like Independent Cascades
[

or Linear Threshold
[

V

from the underlying probabilistic epidemic model, and estimating the objective in expectation. This estimation is indeed a WC function: in particular,
C=V
, and there is one set
S_{i}
for every node

if they are placed in the seed set, and

The influence maximization setting maps very well to the online submodular maximization problem we consider, particularly in the bandit setting. Different reachability graphs/coverage functions correspond to different instances the epidemic propagation model at each timeslot or, more broadly, different epidemics, potentially with time-varying statistics. The decision maker gets to pick the seeds subject to, e.g., a cardinality/uniform matroid constraint, and observes either the entire reachability graph (in the full information setting), or just the final number of nodes infected (in the bandit setting).

Facility Location

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{v\in S}w_{v,v^{\prime}}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(w_{\pi_{j},v^{\prime}}-w_{\pi_{i+1},v^{\prime}}\right)\left(1-\prod_{j=1}^{i}(1-x_{\pi_{j}})\right)+w_{\pi_{n},v^{\prime}}\left(1-\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1-x_{\pi_{n}})\right)\end{split}

This also maps well to our online setting. In the context of facility location, we wish to choose facilities per timeslot in the presence of time-varying, online arrivals of customers/tasks, and in the context of exemplar clustering we wish to choose summaries/exemplars that are good across a potentially time-varying sequence of sets of points. Both fit very well in the full-information setting, as all distances/similarities are immediately observable/computable whenever the tasks/points are revealed.

This also matches the OSM setting well. For example, different team performance functions can correspond to the score/quality attained by the team at a sequence of progressively revealed tasks. C.II
A Negative Result

OMA configured with the negative entropy mirror map
\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{y\in[n]}y_{i}\log(y_{i})
which enjoys sublinear static regret over the simplex
\mathcal{Y}=\Delta_{n}\subset[0,1]^{n}
does not maintain such a guarantee for dynamic regret. We prove this negative result in Proposition
2
through a simple construction; since
L_{\Phi}=\infty
in this setting, then Asm.
6
is also necessary for OMA to yield a sublinear dynamic regret policy. Formally:

Proposition 2
.

OMA in Alg.
2
configured with negative entropy mirror map
\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{y\in[n]}y_{i}\log(y_{i})
has linear dynamic regret (
P_{T}>0
) under simplex constraint set
\mathcal{Y}=\Delta_{n}\subset[0,1]^{n}
for any
\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Proof.

Consider
n=2
, and the following rewards
f_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=x_{1}
for
t\leq T/2
, and
f_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=x_{2}
for
T/2\leq t\leq T
. The dynamic optimum selects
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=(1,0)
for
t\leq T/2
, and
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=(0,1)
for
T/2\leq t\leq T
incurring a total reward of
T
. It is easy to verify that the policy’s state is given by
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\left(e^{(t-1)\eta}/(e^{(t-1)\eta}+1),1/(e^{(t-1)\eta}+1)\right)
for
t\leq T/2
and
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=\big(e^{(T/2-1)\eta}/(e^{(t-1-T/2)\eta}+e^{(T/2-1)\eta})
,
e^{(t-1-T/2)\eta}/(e^{(t-1-T/2)\eta}+e^{(T/2-1)\eta})\big)
for
T/2+1\leq t\leq T
. Thus, the regret is given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathrm{regret}_{T,2}(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\Delta_{2}})=T-\sum^{\frac{T}{2}}_{t=1}\underbrace{\frac{e^{(t-1)\eta}}{e^{(t-1)\eta}+1}}_{\text{$\leq 1$}}+\underbrace{\frac{e^{(t-1)\eta}}{e^{\left({\frac{T}{2}}-1\right)\eta}+e^{(t-1)\eta}}}_{\text{$\leq 1/2$}}\geq T/4=\Omega(T).

(58)

This concludes the proof.
∎

We overcome this negative result next, by extending the so-called fixed share update rule, originally proposed for optimization over the simplex
[

,
53
]

C.VI
Regret Bounds Derivation and Comparison with Related Work

In this section, we first discuss the derivation of the regret bounds attained by this work in Table

. Then, we discuss algorithm-specific parameters of related work.

Regret Bounds.

We discuss the regret bound appearing in Table
1
. We obtain the tightest regret bounds by employing the negative entropy setup of OMA in Sec.
C
, allowing us to optimize the regret constants. In particular, the regret of OMA when configured with the negative-entropy mirror map grows logarithmically w.r.t. the problem dimension (i.e.,
\mathcal{O}\left(r\sqrt{\log(n/r)T}\right)
in Corollary

in Corollary

. We make the same assumption to better compare our bounds in Table

. However, note that our bounds are further scaled by

which can further improve our bounds under the static and dynamic full-information settings.

Swap Rounding

In swap rounding
[
37
]
, we are given a fractional solution
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}=\mathrm{conv}\left(\mathcal{X}\right)
. We assume that
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
is decomposed as a convex combination of bases (i.e., maximal elements) of
\mathcal{X}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k},

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle\in[0,1],\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}=1,\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X},k\in[K],\penalty\ \text{are bases of}\penalty\ \mathcal{X}.

(138)

We note again that Carathéodory’s theorem implies the existence of such decomposition of at most
n+1
points in
\mathcal{X}
; moreover, there exists a decomposition algorithm
[
49
]
for general matroids with a running time
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{6}\right)
.
However, in all practical cases we consider (including uniform and partition matroids) the complexity is significantly lower. More specifically,
on partition matroids, swap rounding reduces to an algorithm with linear time complexity, namely,
\mathcal{O}\left(mn\right)
for
m
partitions
[
50
]
.

In short, swap rounding randomly picks between pairs of basis vectors over several iterations, producing a random final integral solution. Let
\beta_{k}\triangleq\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\gamma_{k}
,
k\in[K]
. Then:

•

Set
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{1}=\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1}
.

•

Then, at iteration, a random selection is made between vector
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
and basis
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{X}
to produce a new integral
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{X}
as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{k},&\text{with probability}\penalty\ \frac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{k+1}},\\
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1},&\text{with probability}\frac{\gamma_{k+1}}{\beta_{k+1}}.\end{cases}

(139)

The authors refer to this randomized selection as a “merge” between
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
and
\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k+1}
.

The final vector
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{K}
is the output of the algorithm and satisfies, by construction:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{K}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{k}=\bm{\mathbf{y}}.

(140)

Proposition 6

Given the convex set

, where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{i,j}+\delta\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{i,j}=(1-2|V_{i}|\delta)\underbrace{\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}_{i,j}}_{\text{$\in\Delta_{V_{i}}$}}+2|V_{i}|\delta\underbrace{(\bm{\mathbf{1}}_{|V_{i}|}+\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{i,j})/2|V_{i}|}_{\text{$\in\Delta_{V_{i}}$}}.

Definition 5

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}+t\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\geq\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+t\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}),

(154)

,
\bm{\mathbf{y}}+t\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i},\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+t\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\in\mathcal{Y}

for
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\leq\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
. When

is twice differentiable, the DR-submodularity is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

\displaystyle\text{ for all $i,j\in[n]$ and $\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}$}.

(155)

Moreover,

is monotone if
\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})\leq\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})
when
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\leq\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 7
.

[
5
, Lemma 5.1]

Given a submodular monotone function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
s.t.

\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
and
f(\bm{\mathbf{0}})=0
. The randomized rounding scheme
\hat{\Xi}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
in Def.
4
defines a multi-linear, monotone-increasing, DR-submodular, and
L
-lipschitz (w.r.t.
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}
) function
\hat{f}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\sim\hat{\Xi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right],\qquad\text{for $\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}$},

(158)

Proof.

The proof follows the same lines as the proof of
[
5
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Multilinearity.
Given a point
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}\sim\hat{\Xi}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}\sim\xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(T(\omega))\right]=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})f(T(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})).

(159)

Note that
\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})=\prod_{i\in[m]}\prod_{j\in[r_{i}]}\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{i,j})
; moreover,
\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{i,j})=y_{i,j,k}
when
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
, otherwise
\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{i,j})=1-\sum_{k\in V_{i}}y_{i,j,k}
when
\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
, for
i\in[m]
and
j\in[r_{i}]
. The factor
f(T(\bm{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))
is a constant independent from
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
, so we conclude
\hat{f}
is a multilinear function.

Monotonicity and lipschitzness.
Let
\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}=(\mathds{1}\left((i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime})=(i,j,k)\right))_{i^{\prime}\in[m],j^{\prime}\in[r_{i}],k^{\prime}\in V_{i}}
and
\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i,j,k}=\bm{\mathbf{1}}-\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}
. Since the function
\hat{f}
is multilinear, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i,j,k}}=\frac{\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i,j,k})}{\lambda}.

(160)

where
\lambda=1-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in V_{i}:k^{\prime}\neq k}y_{i,j,k^{\prime}}>0
, and the binary operators
\oplus
and
\ominus
are component-wise maximum and minimum operations, respectively, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\left(\max\left\{y_{i,j,k},y^{\prime}_{i,j,k}\right\}\right)_{i\in[m],j\in[r_{i}],k\in V_{i}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}=\left(\min\left\{y_{i,j,k},y^{\prime}_{i,j,k}\right\}\right)_{i\in[m],j\in[r_{i}],k\in V_{i}}
. Consider the two mapping
L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
and
L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\left(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right)_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\mathds{1}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}\right)&\text{if $(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})=(i,j)$},\\
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases},

(161)

\displaystyle\left(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\right)_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\mathds{1}\left(\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\neq\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{k}\right)&\text{if $(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})=(i,j)$},\\
\bm{\mathbf{x}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(162)

for
i^{\prime}\in[m],j^{\prime}\in[r_{i}]
and
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
. One could check that

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}\sim\xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})))\right],\quad\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}\sim\xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(T(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})))\right].

(163)

Thus, it holds

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i,j,k}}=\frac{\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i,j,k})}{\lambda}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}\sim\xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\left[f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})))-f(T(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})))\right]}{\lambda}\geq 0.

(164)

Because
\lambda\geq 0
,
L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})\leq L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})
(element-wise) by construction, and
f
is monotone increasing. Note that
L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})\neq L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}})
only when
\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{k}
or
\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
. So, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i,j,k}}\leq L\frac{\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}_{i,j}\in\left\{\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{k},\bm{\mathbf{0}}\right\})}{1-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in V_{i}:k^{\prime}\neq k}y_{i,j,k^{\prime}}}=L\frac{1-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in V_{i}:k^{\prime}\neq k}y_{i,j,k^{\prime}}}{1-\sum_{k^{\prime}\in V_{i}:k^{\prime}\neq k}y_{i,j,k^{\prime}}}=L.

(165)

The function
\hat{f}
is
M
-lipschitz w.r.t.
\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert_{\infty}
.

DR-submodularity.
The partial derivative of a multilinear function is also multilinear. So, we have the following

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i,j,k}\partial y_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}\left(\frac{\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i,j,k})}{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}\oplus\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})-f(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})+f(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})}{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}.

(167)

As established in Eq. (
163
), it holds

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}\oplus\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})-\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bar{\bm{\mathbf{e}}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\oplus\lambda\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})-f(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\oplus\lambda^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k})+f(\bm{\mathbf{y}}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i,j,k}\ominus\bm{\mathbf{e}}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}})=

(168)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}\sim\xi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}\bigg[f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\oplus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))-f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\ominus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))-f(T(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\oplus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))

(169)

\displaystyle+f(T(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\ominus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))\bigg].

(170)

Considering that
f
is submodular and the second-order differences definition of submodularity
[
71
]
, we obtain
f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\oplus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))-f(T(L^{\oplus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\ominus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))-f(T(L^{\ominus}_{i,j,k}(L^{\ominus}_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}(\bm{\mathbf{\omega}}))))\geq 0
for any

. It follows that the second-partial derivative is non-positive

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})}{\partial y_{i,j,k}\partial y_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}}\leq 0.

(171)

Thus,
\hat{f}
is DR-submodular. This concludes the proof.

Lemma 8

[

]

Part I.
Consider the case when

Using Calculus Stoke’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\delta}}l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{v}})d\bm{\mathbf{v}}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{u}})\frac{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{u}}\rVert}d\bm{\mathbf{u}}.

(173)

We have the following

\displaystyle l^{\delta\bm{\mathbf{I}}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\sim\mathbb{B}}\left[l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\delta\bm{\mathbf{v}})\right]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{B}_{\delta})}\int_{\mathbb{B}_{\delta}}l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{v}})d\bm{\mathbf{v}},

{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{B}_{\delta})}
is the volume of the
n

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}\sim\mathbb{S}_{n-1}}\left[l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\delta\bm{\mathbf{u}})\bm{\mathbf{u}}\right]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}(\mathbb{S}_{n-1})}\int_{\mathbb{S}_{\delta}}l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{u}})\frac{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{u}}\rVert}d\bm{\mathbf{u}}.

The ratio of the volume of a ball in

of radii

\displaystyle\nabla l^{\delta\bm{\mathbf{I}}}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{n}{\delta}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\sim\mathbb{S}_{n-1}}\left[l(x+\delta\bm{\mathbf{v}})\bm{\mathbf{v}}\right].

Part II.
Let

\displaystyle n\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\sim\mathbb{S}_{n-1}}\left[l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\mathbf{v}})\bm{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\right]

\displaystyle=n\bm{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\left[l(\bm{\mathbf{x}}+\bm{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\mathbf{v}})\bm{\mathbf{v}}\right]

(177)

\displaystyle\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)\leq\frac{W}{\eta}\Big(4r^{2}+2L_{\Phi}{P_{T}}\Big)+\frac{W\eta}{2\rho}\left(\sum_{l\in T/W}\mathbb{E}\left[\lVert\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{l}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{\star}\right]\right)+\frac{LT}{W}+2\alpha\delta r^{2}nLT.

(222)

This bound is obtained considering the regret bound in Theorem
5
and OOMA policy regret bound in Theorem

. Consider an Euclidean mirror map
\Phi(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
with the corresponding bounds in Table

.

Static non-optimistic setting.
In the static non-optimistic setting (
P_{T}=0,\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{l}^{\pi}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
), we have:

\displaystyle\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4r^{2}W}{\eta}+\eta\left(8\frac{\alpha^{2}r^{4}n^{4}L^{2}}{\delta^{2}}T\right)+\frac{LT}{W}+2\alpha\delta r^{2}nLT

(223)

\displaystyle\leq(12\alpha r^{5/2}n^{3/2}+1)LT^{4/5}

(224)

For
\eta=\frac{32^{1/4}}{2^{1/2}\alpha r^{1/2}n^{3/2}L}T^{-3/5}
, where
\delta=(32)^{1/4}(r^{1/2}n^{1/2})T^{-1/5}
,
W=T^{1/5}
.

Dynamic non-optimistic setting.
In the static non-optimistic setting (
P_{T}>0
and
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{l}^{\pi}=\bm{\mathbf{0}}
). We have the following:

\displaystyle\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{P_{T}}T^{4/5}\right).

(225)

For
\eta=\Theta\left(\sqrt{P_{T}}T^{-3/5}\right)
,
\delta=\Theta\left(T^{-1/5}\right)
,
W=\Theta\left(T^{1/5}\right)
.

Dynamic optimistic setting.
In the dynamic optimistic setting (
P_{T}>0
and
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{l}^{\pi}\neq\bm{\mathbf{0}}
). We have the following:

\displaystyle\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{\alpha}\sqrt{P_{T}\sum^{T/W}_{l=1}\lVert\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}_{l}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{2}}+T^{1-\beta}+T^{1-\alpha}\right).

(226)

For
\eta=\Theta\left(\sqrt{P_{T}/\sum^{T/W}_{l=1}\lVert\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}_{l}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{2}}\right)
,
\delta=\Theta\left(T^{-\beta}\right)
,
W=\Theta\left(T^{\alpha}\right)
. To interpret the above bound, consider for simplicity that
P_{T}=\Theta\left(1\right)
and that the optimistic gradients
\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{t}
are inaccurate (but bounded by some constant). Then, when we take
\eta=\Theta\left(T^{-3/5}\right)
,
\delta=\Theta\left(T^{-1/5}\right)
,
W=\Theta\left(T^{1/5}\right)
and recover the worst-case regret
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{4/5}\right)
. However, when the optimistic gradients are accurate the above bound can be much tighter. For example, in the extreme scenario when
\sqrt{\sum^{T/W}_{l=1}\lVert\tilde{\bm{\mathbf{g}}}_{l}-\bm{\mathbf{g}}^{\pi}_{l}\rVert^{2}_{2}}=\epsilon
for

, selecting

, and
\eta=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)

.

Specialized Algorithms for Static Regret.

The regret attained through the reduction to an arbitrary OCO algorithm (
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{4/5}\right)
) is higher than with the specific instance of FTRL used by
Wan et al. 2023
(
\mathcal{O}\left(T^{2/3}\right)
), which however does not extend to the dynamic and optimistic settings.

In particular, in the static regret setting, we can recover the bound by
Wan et al. 2023
by instantiating the generic reduction in the manner they do, by employing a specialized OCO algorithm with appropriate exploration matrices
\bm{\mathbf{H}}_{l}
.
Wan et al. 2023

showed that when the OCO policy
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{Y}}
is selected to be FTRL in Alg.
4
with a
self-concordant regularizer

[
8
]

R(\bm{\mathbf{y}}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the exploration matrix can be configured as
\bm{\mathbf{H}}_{l}=\left(\nabla^{2}R(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l})\right)^{1/2}
. Then, the sampled action
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l}+\bm{\mathbf{H}}_{l}\bm{\mathbf{v}}_{l}
is located on the surface of the
Dikin ellipsoid
centered at
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l}
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l})\triangleq\left\{\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l}\rVert_{l}\leq 1\right\},

(227)

where
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{l}
is a local norm given by
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{l}=\sqrt{\bm{\mathbf{y}}^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}R(\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{l})\bm{\mathbf{y}}}
. The Dikin ellipsoid has the nice property that it is fully contained in
\mathcal{Y}
. This permits selecting
\delta=0
, and this reduces the variance of the gradient estimates due to
1/\delta
scaling.
Wan et al. 2023
demonstrate that this configuration attains a regret
\alpha\text{-}\mathrm{regret}\left(\bm{\mathbf{{\mathcal{P}}}}_{\mathcal{X}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{2/3}\right)
.

G.IV
Alternate Concave Relaxation to Reduce Computational Complexity

In our setup so far, the concave relaxation
\tilde{f}
was the WTP function “itself”. As an additional extension, we present a setting in which a concave relaxation is different from
f
, while still satisfying Asm.
2
. This example demonstrates that our framework and, in particular, Asm.
2
provide additional flexibility to design algorithms with low time complexity.

Consider the following set function

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\left(b_{\ell}-b_{\ell}\prod_{j\in S_{\ell}}(1-(w_{\ell,j}/b_{\ell})x_{j})\right),

\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
,

(228)

where
b_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
S_{\ell}\subseteq V
is a subset of
V=[n]
,
\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell}=(w_{\ell,j})_{j\in S}\in[0,b]^{|S|}
bounded by
b_{\ell}
, and
c_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for
\ell\in C
for some index set
C
.

This objective appears in many applications such as product ranking
[
72
,
4
]
and influence maximization
[
22
,
20
]
. Note for
\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell}=\bm{\mathbf{1}},\ell\in C
, this set function reduces to the weighted coverage (
44
).

Lemma
5
implies that the function
f
belongs to the class of WTP functions; in particular,
it can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\left(b_{\ell}-b_{\ell}\sum_{S\subseteq S_{\ell}}\prod_{i\in S}(1-x_{i})\prod_{i\in S}w_{\ell,i}/b_{\ell}\prod_{j\in S_{\ell}\setminus S}(1-w_{\ell,j}/b_{\ell})\right).

(229)

We obtain this equality by considering that
1-\prod_{i\in S}(1-x_{i})=\Psi_{1,\bm{\mathbf{1}},S}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
for all
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{|S|}
and, hence:

\displaystyle f(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\left(b_{\ell}-b_{\ell}\sum_{S\subset S_{\ell}}\prod_{i\in S}w_{\ell,i}/b_{\ell}\prod_{j\in S_{\ell}\setminus S}(1-w_{\ell,j}/b_{\ell})\left(\Psi_{1,\bm{\mathbf{1}},S}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})-1\right)\right)

(230)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}b_{\ell}\sum_{S\subset S_{\ell}}\left(\prod_{i\in S}w_{\ell,i}/b_{\ell}\prod_{j\in S_{\ell}\setminus S}(1-w_{\ell,j}/b_{\ell})\right)\Psi_{1,\bm{\mathbf{1}},S}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})\triangleq h(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

for
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{n}
.

(231)

The second inequality follows from the fact that
1=\sum_{S\subset S_{\ell}}\prod_{i\in S}w_{\ell,i}/b_{\ell}\prod_{j\in S_{\ell}\setminus S}(1-w_{\ell,j}/b_{\ell})
for
\ell\in C
(see Lemma
5
).

A straightforward approach to construct a concave relaxation of
f
is to extend
h(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
to fractional values
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in[0,1]^{n}
, i.e.,
\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=h(\bm{\mathbf{y}}),\forall\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in[0,1]^{n}
as in Sec.
5.2
. However,
this extension is intractable, since it involves a summation of potentially
2^{n}
terms
. Alternatively, we can consider the concave relaxation given by

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{\ell\in C}c_{\ell}\Psi_{b_{\ell},\bm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell},S_{\ell}}(\bm{\mathbf{y}})

for
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in[0,1]^{n}.

(232)

It is easy to check that Lemmas
3
–
4
emply that this is a valid concave function satisfying Asm.
2
. This relaxation is of interest for two reasons: (1) it provides an example of the “sandwich” property under which the two functions do not necessarily exactly match over integral values, and (2) it illustrates that carefully designing the concave relaxation can greatly change the computational complexity of the proposed method in Sec.
5.1
.

Optimistic Learning.

We now consider the third variant of
SynthWC

P_{T}=\Omega\left(T\right)
(see our theoretical result in Sec.

\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{P_{T}T}\right)
is order-wise optimal
[

]
). However, if we incorporate predictions, the large

can be dampened when the predictions are accurate. We provide as predictions the future supergradient
\bm{\mathbf{g}}_{t}
with added Gaussian noise with mean
\bm{\mathbf{0}}
and standard deviation
n_{\sigma}\in\left\{10,200\right\}
.

The results are provided in Fig.
4
. We observe that when the predictions are accurate
Optimistic OGA
is able to exploit these predictions to match the performance of the dynamic optimum. We note that for larger learning rate, the performance improves as the policy can follow aggressively the predictions instead of the past rewards, whereas classical
OGA
