Compositional Solution of Mean Payoff Games by String Diagrams
Kazuki Watanabe, Clovis Eberhart, Kazuyuki Asada, Ichiro Hasuo

Abstract
Following our recent development of a compositional model checking algorithm for Markov decision processes, we present a compositional framework for solving mean payoff games (MPGs). The framework is derived from category theory, specifically that of monoidal categories: MPGs (extended with open ends) get composed in so-called string diagrams and thus organized in a monoidal category; their solution is then expressed as a functor, whose preservation properties embody compositionality. As usual, the key question to compositionality is how to enrich the semantic domain; the categorical framework gives an informed guidance in solving the question by singling out the algebraic structure required in the extended semantic domain. We implemented our compositional solution in Haskell; depending on benchmarks, it can outperform an existing algorithm by an order of magnitude.

In this paper, we present a novel compositional algorithm for solving MPGs; it is a mapping

To obtain a suitable semantic category
\mathbb{S}
and a winning-position functor
\mathcal{W}\colon\mathbf{oMPG}\to\mathbb{S}
, we follow the categorical workflow introduced in
[
29
]
, in which
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathcal{W}
are obtained in a two-step process. See
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors.
In the two steps we utilize general categorical constructions, namely the
Int construction

[
14
]
and the
change of base
construction
[
11
,
9
]
.
The Int construction turns a framework of “unidirectional” open MPGs into that of “bidirectional” open MPGs,
and for this we need a
trace operator
for the category
\mathbf{roMPG}
of “unidirectional” open MPGs.
Intuitively, the trace operator is an algebraic operation that creates a loop in an MPG (
\Cref
fig:trace_operator).
In the other step, the change of base construction builds the category
\mathbf{roMPG}
of “unidirectional” open MPGs from a category
\mathbf{roPG}
of “plays,” by adding two nondeterministic structures for the two players
\exists
and
\forall
.
Change of base has been applied in computer science to
game semantics

[
20
]
, too.

The two-step process described above (
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors) on the syntax side (i.e.
\mathbf{roPG}\mapsto\mathbf{roMPG}\mapsto\mathbf{oMPG}
) also takes place in parallel on the semantics side, i.e., on semantic categories and winning-position functors.
The change of base construction (does not generally, but does in our specific case) lift a trace operator, so we need a trace operator for the semantic category
\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
for “plays.” For its construction, we use a
priority-based
technique inspired by the construction for parity games in
[
28
]
(which is further inspired by
[
12
]
).

We implemented the winning-position functor
\mathcal{W}\colon\mathbf{oMPG}\to\mathbb{S}
;
it receives a string diagram of oMPGs and outputs the solution of its composition.
Experiments show that our implementation (we call it
\mathrm{CompMPG}
)
outperforms the known algorithm QDPM
[
1
]
on both 1) simple but realistic benchmarks and 2) randomized benchmarks.
\mathrm{CompMPG}
solved benchmarks as big as
10^{7}
positions within three seconds, demonstrating its efficiency.

Our contributions are summerized as follows:

1.

A compositional algorithm for solving MPGs composed by string diagrams.

2.

Its structural and disciplined derivation by category theory.

3.

Its implementation
\mathrm{CompMPG}
and experimental evaluation that shows its efficiency.

Related Work

We have already mentioned related work on pseudo-polynomial algorithms for MPGs
[
4
,
1
]
, and compositional model checking
[
6
,
19
,
28
,
29
,
25
,
27
]
. Here, we give a detailed comparison between our work and closely related work
[
25
,
28
,
27
,
29
]
.

The work
[
25
,
24
]
uses string diagrams to compose Petri nets and computes
reachability probabilities in a compositional way. A major difference from our work is that they do not allow loops in composition; consequently, they use symmetric monoidal categories without traces or compact closed structures. We find loops to be essential in accommodating real-world examples (see e.g.
\Cref
fig:exbenchmark). Treatment of loops is a major theoretical challenge, too, which we successfully address by a priority-based construction of traces in
\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
.

The formalism of string diagrams
originates from the theory of
monoidal categories
(see e.g.
[
21
, Chap. XI]
). Capturing the mathematical essence of the algebraic structure of arrow composition
\circ
and tensor product
\otimes
—they correspond to
;
and
\oplus
in this work, respectively—monoidal categories and string diagrams have found their application in a vast variety of scientific disciplines, such as quantum field theory
[
18
]
, quantum mechanics and computation
[
13
]
, linguistics
[
23
]
, signal flow diagrams
[
3
]
, and so on.

A compositional framework for parity games with string diagrams is introduced in
[
28
]
, whose semantic category is designed following the work
[
12
]
on denotational semantics of higher-order model checking. Unlike the present paper, the winning-position functor in
[
28
]
does not use the change of base construction; as a result, the functor collects too many strategies and is thus not suited for efficient implementation. We note that parity games can be reduced to MPGs
[
15
]
and solved by our current algorithm; besides, we expect it is possible to adapt our current algorithm from MPGs to parity games.

A compositional algorithm for computing expected rewards of MDPs with
string diagrams is introduced in
[
29
]
.
In the current paper, we follow the categorical workflow in
[
29
]
—in fact,
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors is very similar to one in
[
29
]
. However, there is a technical difference in the most challenging part of the workflow, namely the construction of a trace operator in
\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
(corresponding to
\mathbb{S}^{\mathrm{MC}}_{\mathbf{r}}
in
[
29
]
). The construction in
[
29
]
is least fixed point-based: it captures an arbitrary number of iterations and the reward collected in its course. In constrast, our current construction for MPGs is priority-based: we think of MPGs as an extension of parity games from finitely many priorities to infinitely many; therefore we adapt the construction of traces in
[
28
,
12
]
.

Definition 5

\displaystyle\textstyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(s_{j})\geq 0.

(1)

The following lemma is fundamental for MPGs. It says that an infinite play—we have to restrict to memoryless ones (
\Cref

(note that the cycle is unique since the play is memoryless).
The proof is by elementary calculation.

Lemma 6

(
[
10
,
4
]
)
.

Let
\pi^{\tau_{\exists},\tau_{\forall}}=(s_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite memoryless play, induced by (memoryless) strategies
\tau_{\exists},\tau_{\forall}
, on oMPG
\mathcal{A}=(\overline{m},\overline{n},Q,E,\rho,w)

There is the least
(j,k)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
(w.r.t. the product order) such that:
for all
i>j

s_{i}=s_{((i-j)\%k)+j}
.
Moreover,
the following conditions are equivalent:

•

\textstyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w(s_{j})\geq 0

•

\sum_{j<i\leq j+k}w(s_{i})\geq 0
.
∎

The last lemma is fundamental in this paper, too.
It suggests us the notion of the denotation of a play (

We now define the

3
Decomposition Equalities for Memoryless Plays

Here we introduce
decomposition equalities
, a cornerstone of our compositional MPG solution.

Looking at our workflow (
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors),
the bottom level
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}\colon\mathbf{roPG}\to\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
is about “plays”:
the category
\mathbf{roPG}
has as its arrows
rightward open play graphs (roPGs)
—they are roughly
(indexed families of) memoryless plays.
Once we get
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
compositional (i.e. preserving traced monoidal structures), this compositionality
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
carries over to the middle and top levels of
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors via categorical constructions, eventually realizing a compositional MPG solution
\mathcal{W}
. Decomposition equalities are a key to the compositionality of the bottom level
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
.

We first define an roPG. It is intuitively a collection of plays, one for each entrance
i
, indexed by
i
.

Definition 16

(roPG)
.

A
rightward open play graph (roPG)
from
m
to
n
is an roMPG from
m
to
n
(
\Cref
def:roMPG) whose set of edges

is a partial function (i.e. at most one successor).

Rightward open play graphs forms a TSMC
\mathbf{roPG}
just in the same way as
\mathbf{roMPG}
—
\mathbf{roPG}
is a subcategory of
\mathbf{roMPG}
. Due to its determinancy,
an roPG
\mathcal{C}:m\rightarrow n
has a unique play from each entrance
i\in[m]
; this play is denoted by
\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
. Note that
\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is also the play induced by the unique
\exists
- and
\forall
-strategies of
\mathcal{C}
, and thus is memoryless by definition.

We start with the decomposition equality for
\mathbin{;}
.

Proposition 17

(decomposition equality for
\mathbin{;}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}:m\rightarrow l
,
\mathcal{D}:l\rightarrow n
be roPGs, and
i\in[m]
.
The following equality holds regarding the denotation
\llbracket\_\,\rrbracket
of plays (
\Cref
def:denoPlays):

\displaystyle\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}\mathbin{;}\mathcal{D}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}\mathbin{;}\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}&\text{ if }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}\in\{\star_{\exists},\star_{\forall}\},\\
\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{D}}&\text{ if }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}=j\in[l],\\
\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{D}}&\text{ if }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}=(r,j)\in\mathbb{R}\times[l]\text{ and }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{D}}\in\{\star_{\exists},\star_{\forall}\},\\
(r,k)&\text{ if }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}=(r,j)\in\mathbb{R}\times[l]\text{ and }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{D}}=k\in[n],\\
(r+r^{\prime},k)&\text{ if }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{C}}_{i}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{C}}=(r,j)\in\mathbb{R}\times[l]\text{ and }\llbracket\pi^{\mathcal{D}}_{j}\rrbracket_{\mathcal{D}}=(r^{\prime},k)\in\mathbb{R}\times[n].\qed\end{cases}

Here is some intuition.
In the first case, the winner of the composed play
\pi^{\mathcal{C}\mathbin{;}\mathcal{D}}_{i}
is already decided within
\mathcal{C}
.
In the second case, if the play
\pi^{\mathcal{C}\mathbin{;}\mathcal{D}}_{i}
goes immediately to exit

\pi^{\mathcal{C}\mathbin{;}\mathcal{D}}_{i}

\mathcal{D}

of MPGs if an ultimate winner is decided in
\mathcal{D}
, then the “prefix” in
\mathcal{C}
does not matter. The fourth case is easy; finally, in the fifth case, we accumulate weights

from
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
, respectively.

The decomposition equality for the sum

Remark 32

(Kleisli construction or change of base)
.

In the compositional approach for
parity games

[
28
, cf. Rem. 4.9]
, the non-deterministic structures of the semantic category and functor are constructed not by the change of base construction but by the Kleisli construction used e.g. in
[
12
]
.
In this Kleisli approach for parity games, an algorithmic result comes from the finitary models
[
12
]
.
It seems difficult to obtain a finitary model for MPGs based on the Kleisli approach that induces an algorithm since there are infinitely many priorities (while for parity games, there are finitely many).

Even if one obtains some finitary model, another question is whether the
trace operator
is computable. A computationally tractable trace operator will probably only consider memoryless strategies—exploiting memoryless determinacy—which is easy to enforce in the change of base approach but not easy in the Kleisli approach.

Implementation and Experiment

We describe our implementation
\mathrm{CompMPG}
of the compositional algorithm for oMPGs, and show experimental results. The experiment results 1)
show that our compositional framework has advantages over the state-of-the-art solver QDPM
[
1
]
, and 2)
identify two major factors that affect the performance of
\mathrm{CompMPG}
.
QDPM is a pseudopolynomial algorithm based on

small progress measure

[
16
]
and
quasi
dominion

[
2
]
.

Meager Semantics

In our implementation of
\mathrm{CompMPG}
, to enhance performance, we use
meager semantics
,
a refinement of the semantics given in
\Cref
sec:semanticCategories (we call it
fat semantics
).
An arrow
F:\overline{m}\rightarrow\overline{n}
in the fat semantic category
\mathbb{S}
collects all possible strategies and some can be redundant; that is, for deciding whether the entrance is winning, losing, or pending, some strategies are dominated by others, and thus can be forgotten.
Since oMPGs have several exits in general, the situation is much like that of multi-objective optimization.
Such dominance relationship between strategies can be described
by a certain order
\leq
.

Specifically, we define the order
\leq
between arrows in
\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
(the bottom level in
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors of plays) as follows:
for arrows
f,g:m\rightarrow n
in
\mathbb{S}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbf{r}}
(which are functions
[m]\to[n]+\mathbb{R}\times[n]+\{\star_{\exists},\star_{\forall}\}
), we define
f\leq g
if for each
i\in[m]
, one of the following conditions is satisfied:
(i)
f(i)=g(i)\in[n]
,
(ii)
f(i)=(r_{f},j_{f})
,
g(i)=(r_{g},j_{g})
,
r_{f}\geq r_{g}
and
j_{f}=j_{g}
,
(iii)
f(i)=\star_{\exists}
,
or
(iv)
g(i)=\star_{\forall}
.
We can also formulate this
meager semantics
as a compact closed category/functor similarly to
\Cref
sec:semanticCategories, drawing the same picture as
\Cref
fig:catsFunctors. See
\Cref
sec:meagerSemantics.

Implementation
Our implementation
\mathrm{CompMPG}
of our compositional algorithm is based on the meager semantics in Haskell (it is available at
https://github.com/Kazuuuuuki/compMPG
).
We evaluate
\mathrm{CompMPG}
comparing with QDPM.
\mathrm{CompMPG}
takes an oMPG
\mathcal{A}
as input, which is expressed by (a textual format for) a
string diagram
with sequential composition
\mathbin{;}
, sum
\oplus
, and constants such as
\mathrm{id}
(
\Cref
fig:id-swap-co-unit). See
\Cref
fig:algebraicOperations.

Formally, those inputs are represented in a
free prop
for oMPGs, which is a slight variant of the free prop for open parity games introduced in
[
28
]
.
The input also expresses which component is duplicated (such as
\verb|let t = ... in (t; t; t)|
), by which
\mathrm{CompMPG}
can solve the entire input without solving the repeated component
\verb|t|
more than once.
Given such an oMPG
\mathcal{A}
as input,
\mathrm{CompMPG}
returns the arrow
\mathcal{W}(\mathcal{A})
as output.
If the entire input is an oMPG from
(1,0)
to
(0,0)
(recall arities from e.g.
\Cref
fig:openMPG), then the input can be interpreted as an MPG whose initial position is the entrance.
We note that
\mathrm{CompMPG}
only decides the winner at each entrance, while QDPM decides that at all positions.

is a finite set. Then,
g\circ f(x^{\prime})=g\circ f(x)
because
g
is monotone and
g\circ f(x)
is maximal in

. By the same argument, we can conclude that

. Thus,
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{max}}(g\circ f)(S)\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{max}}(g)\circ\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{max}}(f)(S)
.

∎

Proposition 55
.

The functor
