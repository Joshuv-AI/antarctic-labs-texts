Abstract
. This paper concerns the enumeration of z z -classes of Weyl groups of the types B n B_{n} , C n C_{n} , D n D_{n} , and the exceptional types F 4 F_{4} , G 2 G_{2} , E 6 E_{6} , E 7 E_{7} and E 8 E_{8} . Through this work, we answer the question about z z -classes in Weyl Groups, which Anupam and Sushil have asked in their survey tiled “ z z -classes in groups: a survey.”



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a group. We recall the definition of a
z
-class. We say that two elements
x,y\in G
, are said to be
z
-equivalent, if and only if their respective centralizers
Z_{G}(x)
and
Z_{G}(y)
are conjugate to each other by an element of
G
. This relation is an equivalence relation, and the equivalence classes of this relation are called
z
-classes
,
conjugacy class types
, or simply
types.

z
-classes were introduced by Ravi Kulkarni in
[
Ku
]
. These have since been studied widely.

For an arbitrary group
G
, there is the question of the finiteness of the number of
z
-classes in it. The answer is true for a finite group. For an infinite group, the answer to this question is not necessarily true. The group
GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q})
of
2\times 2
invertible matrices over the field of rationals has an infinite number of
z
-classes. It was due to Steinberg, that reductive algebraic groups over an algebraically closed field were proven to have a finite number of
z
-classes. Let
k
be a perfect field, where for any integer
n
,
k
has only finitely many extensions of degree
n
. Such a field
k
is called a field of type
(F)
. Garge and Singh
[
GS
]
proved that reductive groups over a field of type
(F)
have a finite number of
z
-classes. However, when a group is not reductive, the number of
z
-classes need not be finite. Bhunia
[
Bh
]
proved that for
n\geq 6
, and an infinite field
F
, the group of upper triangular
n\times n
matrices over
F
has an infinite number of
z
-classes.

z
-classes have been studied for classical groups over finite fields
[
Sh
,
SS
]
, upper triangular and upper unitriangular matrices over finite fields
[
KSS
]
. In these, they were a useful tool in enumerating the number of simultaneous conjugacy classes of commuting tuples of elements these groups. In general determining simultaneous conjugacy classes for a finite group
G
are usfeul in calculating the probability
cp_{k}(G)
that a
k
-tuple of elements of
G
commutes.

z
-classes have been studied for various groups of Lie type. They have been studied for isometry groups of the real hyperbolic space by Gongopadhyay and Kulkarni (
[
GK
]
).
z
-classes of Lie Groups of types
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{n}

F_{4}
and
G_{2}
were calculated explicitly by Bose
[
Bo
]
.For more literature in this area, we refer the reader to a survey article by Bhunia and Singh
[
BS
]
.

W We attempt to determine the
Z
-classes of Weyl groups of the types
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
,
D_{n}
, etc. The Weyl group of type
A_{n}
is the symmetric group
S_{n+1}
. The
z
-classes of
S_{n+1}
have already been determined by Bhunia, Kaur and Singh in
[
BKS
]
. Thus, we need to determine the
z
-classes of
B_{n}
,
C_{n}
and
D_{n}
. It is a well known fact that the Weyl groups of
B_{n}
and
C_{n}
are isomorphic, and the group is the Wreath product of
C_{2}
with
S_{n}
,
C_{2}\wr S_{n}
. The Weyl group of
D_{n}
,
W(D_{n})
is an index 2 subgroup of
C_{2}\wr S_{n}
.

In Section
2
, we state the preliminary results, with some background about wreath products. In Section
3
we deal with the centralizers in
W(C_{n})
. In Section
4
we explore the relation between

-classes in Weyl Groups and those in Symmetric groups. In Section

, we prove the results for

. In Section
6
, we tackle

, and in Section
7
we put out our results for Weyl groups of the exceptional types.

Prelimnary Results

he Weyl groups of
B_{n}
and
C_{n}
are isomorphic, and the group is the Wreath product of
C_{2}
with
S_{n}
,
C_{2}\wr S_{n}
. We first recall the definition of the wreath product
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Let
C_{2}^{n}
denotes the direct product of
n
-copies of cyclic groups of order
2.
The symmetric group
S_{n}
acts on
C_{2}^{n}
in the following way: For
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in C_{2}
, and
\sigma\in S_{n}
:

\sigma.(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})=(a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}).

Then the semidirect product of
C_{2}^{n}
with
S_{n}
, with respect to the above action, is called
w
reath product of
C_{2}
and
S_{n}
.

We write an element of
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
as
[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma]
, where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in C_{2}
, and
\sigma\in S_{n}
. Here, we take
C_{2}=\{-1,1\}
.

Let
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma],\mathbf{b}_{\pi}=[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n};\pi]\in C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
, then the product
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}\mathbf{b}_{\pi}
is

[a_{1}b_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,a_{n}b_{\sigma^{-1}(n)};\sigma.\pi].

And the inverse of
\mathbf{a}
is
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}^{-1}=[a^{-1}_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,a^{-1}_{\sigma(n)};\sigma^{-1}]
.

A partition of a positive integer
n
is a sequence of positive integers
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{l}>0
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{l}\lambda_{i}=n.
The
\lambda_{i}
’s are called the parts of the partition. We write this partition of
n
by
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})

A partition is said to be “signed partition” if each part can occur as
2
colours. For example, there are five signed partitions of
2:

1+1,\bar{1}+1,\bar{1}+\bar{1},2,\bar{2}.

If
\lambda_{i}
is an even number then it is called even part and if
\lambda_{i}
is an odd number then it is called odd part. The part of signed partition
\lambda_{i}
without bar is called part with positive sign and the part
\bar{\lambda_{i}}
with bar is called part with negative sign.

The conjugacy classes of
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
are determined by Carter
[
Ca
]
. The conjugacy classes of
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
are in one to one correspondance with signed partitions of
n
. To understand this correspondance, we define the cycle products.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
be a partition of
n
, with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{l}
. Let
\sigma\in S_{n}
be a permutation of cycle-type
\lambda
, say
\sigma=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda_{1}})\cdots(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\lambda_{l}})
, and let
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma]
. Then the
c
ycle products of
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}
are the products
\Pi_{i=1}^{\lambda_{1}}a_{i_{j}}
,
\ldots
,
\Pi_{i=1}^{\lambda_{l}}a_{k_{i}}
.

For any element
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}\in C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
, the cycle products in
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}
are either
-1
or
1.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
MS
]
Let
\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma]
, and
\mathbf{b}=[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n};\pi]
. Then
\mathbf{a}
is conjugate in
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
to
\mathbf{b}
if and only if
\sigma
is conjugate to
\pi
, and if
\tau\in S_{n}
so that
\tau\sigma\tau^{-1}=\pi
, then for each cycle
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\lambda_{j}})
in
\sigma
, where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
is the cycle type of
\sigma
, the cycle product
a_{i_{1}}\cdots a_{i_{\lambda_{j}}}
in
\mathbf{a}
is conjugate to the cycle product
b_{\tau(i_{1})}\cdots b_{\tau(i_{\lambda_{j}})}
in
\mathbf{b}
.

The following proposition gives the correspondance between conjugacy classes and signed partitions of
n.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
be a partition of
n
. Let
\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},\sigma]

\lambda_{j}

of size

is
1.
We write
\overline{\lambda_{j}}
, if the associated cycle product with
\sigma
is
-1.
This associates signed permutation with elements of group

W(D_{n})
of

Now we discuss the conjugacy classes of Weyl group

An element
\mathbf{a}_{\sigma}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma]

The following proposition will be useful in determining the relation between centralizers in

Let
G
be a group and
g\in G.
We denote
centralizer of
g
in
G
by
Z_{G}(g)
and
Z(Z_{G}(g))
denotes the center of this centralizer.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}\times\dots\times G_{n}
be a finite group and
H
be a subgroup of
G
of index
2.
Let
h=(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{n})\in H
and
h_{i}\in G_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n.
Let
\mathbf{G_{i}}
denote the subgroup of
G
isomorphic to
G
and
\mathbf{h_{i}}=(1,1,\dots,h_{i},\dots 1,1)\in H
with
h_{i}
at the
i^{th}
position. Let
|Z_{H}(\mathbf{h_{i}})\cap\mathbf{G_{i}}|<|Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i})|
for atleast two distinct
i,j.
Then
Z(Z_{H}(h))=Z(Z_{G}(h))\cap H.

Proof.

Let
g=(g_{1},g_{2},\dots,g_{n})\in G.
We can write
g=\pi_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g_{i}},
where
\mathbf{g_{i}}=(1,1,\dots,g_{i},\dots 1,1)
with
g_{i}
at the
i^{th}
position. Since
H
is an index
2
subgroup of
G,
we have
G=H\cup Hx
for some
x\in G.
This implies
g=\pi_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{g_{i}}\in H
if and only if
\mathbf{g_{i}}\in Hx
for even number of
i.

It is easy to see that
Z_{H}(h)=Z_{G}(h)\cap H.
This implies
Z(Z_{H}(h))\supseteq Z(Z_{G}(h))\cap H.
To prove the converse let
k\in Z(Z_{H}(h)).
Since
Z(Z_{H}(h))\subseteq H\subseteq G,
let
k=(k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n})\in H
and
k_{i}\in G_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n.
We are done if we show that
k_{i}\in Z(Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i}))
for all
1\leq i\leq n
as
Z_{G}(h)=\pi_{i=1}^{n}Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i}).

We prove this by contradiction. Let
k_{i}\notin Z(Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i}))
for some
i.
This implies there exist
g_{i}\in Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i})
such that
k_{i}g_{i}\neq g_{i}k_{i}.
We divide the proof in to two cases.

(1)

Let
\mathbf{g_{i}}\in H.
This implies
\mathbf{g_{i}}\in Z_{H}(h)
as
g_{i}\in Z_{G_{i}}(h_{i}).
Therefore
k\mathbf{g_{i}}=\mathbf{g_{i}}k
as
k\in Z(Z_{H}(h)).
Using this we conclude that
k_{i}g_{i}=g_{i}k_{i},
which contradicts our assumption.

(2)

Let
\mathbf{g_{i}}\notin H.
By given there exist
j\neq i
such that
|Z_{H}(\mathbf{h_{j}})\cap\mathbf{G_{j}}|<|Z_{G_{j}}(h_{j})|.
Let
g_{j}\in Z_{G_{j}}(h_{j})
and
\mathbf{g_{j}}\notin Z_{H}(\mathbf{h_{j}})\cap\mathbf{G_{j}}.
This implies
\mathbf{g_{j}}\notin H.
As
H
is index
2
subgroup of
G,
the product
\mathbf{g_{i}}\mathbf{g_{j}}\in H
and hence
\mathbf{g_{i}}\mathbf{g_{j}}\in Z_{H}(h).
Therefore
k\mathbf{g_{i}}\mathbf{g_{j}}=\mathbf{g_{i}}\mathbf{g_{j}}k,
and in particular we get
k_{i}g_{i}=g_{i}k_{i},
which contradicts our assumption.

∎

Proposition 3.1
.

When
n
is odd, the respective centralizers of the conjugacy classes
n^{1}
and
\overline{n}^{1}
in
C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}
are the same, and both are conjugate to:

\{[\mathbf{1}_{n};\sigma_{n}^{k}],[\mathbf{-1}_{n};\sigma_{n}^{k}]\mid k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}.

Moreover, these centralizers are isomorphic to
(C_{2}\times C_{k})
.

Proof.

The element
\mathbf{a}=[\mathbf{1}_{n};\sigma_{n}]
, where
\sigma_{n}=(1,2,\ldots,n)
represents the conjugacy class
n^{1}
. We see that
\mathbf{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{n};\tau]
commutes with
\mathbf{a}
if and only if
\tau
commutes with
\sigma_{n}
, and the following hold.

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}h_{1}h_{n}^{-1}a_{\tau^{-1}(1)}&=&a_{1}&=&1\\
h_{2}h_{1}^{-1}a_{\tau^{-1}(2)}&=&a_{2}&=&1\\
\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
h_{n}h_{n-1}^{-1}a_{\tau^{-1}(n)}&=&a_{n}&=&1\end{array}.

But, as all of the
a_{i}=1
, then for any
\tau
,
a_{\tau^{-1}(i)}=1
. Thus
h_{1}=h_{2}=\cdots=h_{n}
. And, as
\tau
commutes with
\sigma_{n}
,
\tau=\sigma_{n}^{k}
, for some
k
. Thus,

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})=\{[\mathbf{1}_{n};\sigma_{n}^{k}],[\mathbf{-1}_{n};\sigma_{n}^{k}]\mid k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}.

For the conjugacy class
\overline{1}^{n}
, we take
\mathbf{b}=[\mathbf{-1}_{n};\sigma_{n}]
, as a reprsentative, as its cycle product is
(-1)^{n}=-1
, since
n
is odd. Thus by the same arguments, as for
\mathbf{a}
, we obtain
Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{b})=Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a}).

We define the following map
C_{2}\times C_{n}\rightarrow Z_{C_{2}\wr{C_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})
: Let
C_{n}
be the cyclic group
\{\sigma_{n}^{i}:0\leq i\leq n-2\}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:C_{2}\times C_{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{C_{2}\wr{C_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle(h,\sigma_{n}^{i})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle[h,h,\ldots,h;\sigma_{n}^{i}]

By a routine check, it is easy to see that

is an isomorphism.

\langle\overline{[}{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{2}

\displaystyle={[-\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]}^{2}=[-1.(1),1.(-1),1.(1)\ldots,1.(1);\sigma_{n}^{2}]

\displaystyle=[-1,-1,1,\ldots,1;\sigma_{n}^{2}]=[\mathbf{-1}_{2},\mathbf{1}_{n-2};\sigma_{n}^{2}]

Assume induction upto
k
. Then
\mathbf{a}^{k+1}
is equal to:

\displaystyle{[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]}^{k+1}

\displaystyle=[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}].[-\mathbf{1}_{k},\mathbf{1}_{n-k};\sigma_{n}^{k}]

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{-1\cdot 1,\ldots,-1\cdot 1}_{k-copies},1\cdot(-1),1\cdot 1,\ldots,1\cdot 1;\sigma_{n}^{k+1}]

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{k+1-copies},1\ldots,1;\sigma_{n}^{k+1}]=[-\mathbf{1}_{k+1},\mathbf{1}_{n-k-1};\sigma_{n}^{k+1}]

Thus,
[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]^{n}=[-\mathbf{1}_{n};e]
.

Now, for any
1\leq l\leq n
,

\displaystyle{[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]}^{n+l}

\displaystyle=[-\mathbf{1}_{n};e].[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]^{l}

\displaystyle=[-1,\ldots,-1;e].[\underbrace{-1,\ldots,-1}_{l-copies},1\ldots,1;\sigma_{n}^{l}]

\displaystyle=[\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{l-copies},-1\ldots,-1;\sigma_{n}^{l}]=[\mathbf{1}_{l},\mathbf{-1}_{n-l};\sigma_{n}^{l}]

Thus,
Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\overline{a})=\langle[\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{n};\sigma_{n}]\rangle
, which is a cyclic group of order
2n
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
1<k<n
be an odd number that divides
n
. Let
n=k.l
, where
l\leq 2
. Then the centralizers of the conjugacy classes
k^{l}
and
\overline{k}^{l}
are conjugate to

\{[\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-copies},\underbrace{h_{2},\ldots,h_{2}}_{k-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-copies};\tau]\mid h_{i}=\pm 1,\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})\},

where
\sigma_{0}
is the permutation:

\sigma_{0}=(1,2,\ldots,k)(k+1,k+2,\ldots,2k)\cdots(n-k+1,n-k+2,\ldots,n).

Moreover, this centralizer is isomorphic to the group
((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{l}).

Proof.

For the conjugacy class
k^{l}
, we take the representative,
\mathbf{a}=[\mathbf{1}_{n};\sigma_{0}]
, where
\sigma_{0}=(1\cdots k)(k+1\cdots 2k)\cdots((l-1)k+1\cdots n)
. Now, for any element
\overline{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{n};\tau]
to commute with
\mathbf{a}
, we need
\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})=\cong C_{k}\wr S_{l}
(see
[
JK
, Equation 4.1.19]
). So
\tau
can be written as a wreath product
[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\pi]
, where
\pi\in S_{l}
, and
f_{i}\in C_{k}=\langle(12\cdots k)\rangle
, such that, for
1\leq j\leq l
, and
1\leq i\leq k
:

\tau((j-1)k+i)=(\pi(j)-1)k+f_{\pi(j)}(i).

So,
\overline{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{n};[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\pi]]
commutes with
\mathbf{a}
if the following hold true for
1\leq u\leq l
:

\begin{smallmatrix}h_{(u-1)k+1}h_{uk}a_{(\pi^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(1)}&=&a_{(u-1)k+1}=1\\
h_{(u-1)k+2}h_{(u-1)k+1}a_{(\pi^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(2)}&=&a_{(u-1)k+2}=1\\
\vdots&~~&\vdots\\
h_{uk}h_{uk-1}a_{(\pi^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(k)}&=&a_{uk}=1\end{smallmatrix}

Now, as
a_{(u-1)k+i}=1
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, we get that
h_{(u-1)k+1}=h_{(u-1)k+2}=\cdots=h_{uk}
. Thus, the centralizer of
\mathbf{a}
is of the form:

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})=\{[\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-copies},\underbrace{h_{2},\ldots,h_{2}}_{k-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-copies};\tau]\mid h_{i}=\pm 1,\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})\}.

Now, for the signed cycle type
\overline{k}^{l}
, we take the representative
\mathbf{b}=[-1,-1,\ldots,-1;\sigma_{0}]
. Thus
\mathbf{b}=[\mathbf{-1}_{n};e].\mathbf{a}
. As
[-1,\ldots,-1;e]
is a central element, we get that
Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{b})=Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})
.
This centralizer is of order
(2k)^{l}l!

Now, we define the map:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{l}:(C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{l}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle{[(h_{1},f_{1}),\ldots(h_{l},f_{l});\pi]}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle[\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-copies},\underbrace{h_{2},\ldots,h_{2}}_{k-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-copies};\tau],

where
\tau=[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\pi]\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})
. By a routine check, one can prove that this map is an isomorphism.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, which is even, and let
k<n
, be an even integer that divides
n
. Suppose
n=l.k
, for some positive integer
l
. Then the conjugacy class
\overline{k}^{l}
is conjugate to:

\left\{\begin{matrix}[\underbrace{-h_{1},\ldots,-h_{1}}_{m_{1}-copies},\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-m_{1}-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{-h_{l},\ldots,-h_{l}}_{m_{l}-copies},\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-m_{l}-copies};\tau]\mid\\
h_{i}\in C_{2},\tau=[\pi_{k}^{m_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{k}^{m_{l}};\rho]\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})\end{matrix}\right\},

where
\sigma_{0}
is the permutation

\sigma_{0}=(1,2,\ldots,k)(k+1,k+2,\ldots,2k)\cdots(n-k+1,n-k+2,\ldots,n),

and
\pi_{k}
denotes the
k
-cycle
(1,2,\ldots,k)
. Moreover, this centralizer is isomorphic to the wreath product
C_{2k}\wr S_{l}
.

Proof.

We take
\mathbf{a}=[\underbrace{\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{k},\ldots,\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{k}}_{l~-~copies};\sigma_{0}]
, as a representative of the conjugacy class
\overline{k}^{l}
.

For
\mathbf{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{n};\tau]
to commute with
\mathbf{a}
, we first need
\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})=C_{k}\wr S_{l}
(see
[
JK
, Equation 4.1.19]
). Now, let
\tau_{0}=[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\rho]
, where
f_{i}\in C_{k}=\langle(1\cdots k)\rangle
, and
\pi\in S_{l}
. With this, we get that, for
\mathbf{h}
to commute with
\mathbf{b}
, the following should be satisfied for all
1\leq u\leq l
:

\begin{smallmatrix}h_{(u-1)k+1}h_{uk}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(1)}&=&a_{(u-1)k+1}&=&-1,\\
h_{(u-1)k+2}h_{(u-1)k+1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(2)}&=&a_{(w-1)k+2}&=&1\\
\vdots&~&\vdots&~&\vdots\\
h_{uk}h_{uk-1}a_{(\pi^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(k)}&=&a_{uk}&=&1\end{smallmatrix}

Now, let
f_{u}=\pi_{k}^{m_{u}}
. Then, like in the arguments from the proof of Proposition
3.3
, we get that
-h_{(u-1)k+1}=\cdots=-h_{(u-1)k+m_{u}}

=h_{(u-1)k+m_{u}+1}=\cdots=h_{uk}
. Hence, the centralizer of
\mathbf{a}
should be

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})=\left\{\begin{matrix}[\underbrace{-h_{1},\ldots,-h_{1}}_{m_{1}-copies},\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-m_{1}-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{-h_{l},\ldots,-h_{l}}_{m_{l}-copies},\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-m_{l}-copies};\tau]\mid\\
h_{i}\in C_{2},\tau=[\pi_{k}^{m_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{k}^{m_{l}};\rho]\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})\end{matrix}\right\},

where
\pi_{k}
denotes the
k
-cycle
(1\cdots k)
. We see here too that the size of the centralizer is
2^{l}k^{l}l!
.

Now, taking the
2k
cyclic group to be defined as
\langle\mathbf{c}\rangle
, where
\mathbf{c}
is the element in
C_{2}\wr{S_{k}}
, of the form
[-1,1,\ldots,1;\pi_{k}]
, we define the following map:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{kl}:C_{2k}\wr S_{l}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})

\displaystyle{[\mathbf{c}^{m_{1}},\mathbf{c}^{m_{2}},\ldots,\mathbf{c}^{m_{l}};\rho]}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle[\underbrace{-h_{1},\ldots,-h_{1}}_{m_{1}-copies},\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k-m_{1}-copies},\ldots,\underbrace{-h_{l},\ldots,-h_{l}}_{m_{l}-copies},\underbrace{h_{l},\ldots,h_{l}}_{k-m_{l}-copies};\tau],

where
\tau=[\pi_{k}^{m_{1}},\ldots,\pi_{k}^{m_{l}};\rho]
. By a routine check, it can be shown that
\mathcal{F}_{kl}
is an isomorphism. Thus,
Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})
is isomorphic to
C_{2k}\wr S_{l}
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
n
be a positive integer, and let
1<k<n
be an odd number that divides
n
. Let
n=k.l
. Let
s,t\geq 0
such that
s+t=l
. Then the centralizer of the conjugacy class
k^{s}\overline{k}^{t}
is conjugate to

\{[\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k\text{ copies}},\underbrace{h_{2},\ldots,h_{2}}_{k\text{ copies}},\ldots,\underbrace{h_{s+t},\ldots,h_{s+t}}_{k\text{ copies}};\tau_{s}\tau_{t}]\mid\tau_{s}\in Z_{S_{sk}}(\sigma_{s}),\tau_{t}\in Z_{S_{tk}}(\sigma_{t})\},

where
\sigma_{s}
denotes the permutation
(1,2,\ldots,k)\cdots((s-1)k+1,\ldots,sk)
, and
\sigma_{t}
denotes the permutation
(sk+1,\ldots,(s+1)k)\cdots(n-k+1,\ldots,n)
.

Moreover, this centralizer is isomorphic to
((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{s})\times((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{t}).

Proof.

For the conjugacy class
k^{s}\overline{k}^{t}
, we take the representative element
\mathbf{a}=[\mathbf{1}_{sk},\mathbf{-1}_{tk};\sigma_{0}]
, where
\sigma_{0}
is the permutation

(1,\ldots,k)(k+1,\ldots,2k)\cdots(n-k+1,\ldots,n).

For
\overline{h}=[h_{1},\ldots,h_{n};\tau]
to commute with
\mathbf{a}
we firstly need
\tau
to commute with
\sigma_{0}
.

As
\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})
, and, as
Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})=Z_{S_{n}}(k^{l})\cong C_{k}\wr S_{l}
it can be written as
[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\rho]
, where
\rho\in S_{l}
, and
f_{i}\in C_{k}=\langle(12\cdots k)\rangle
, such that:
for
1\leq j\leq l
, and
1\leq i\leq k
:

\tau((j-1)k+i)=(\rho(j)-1)+f_{\rho(j)}(i).

Now, let
\tau=[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\rho]\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})
, then we have, for
1\leq u\leq s
, and
s+1\leq w\leq s+t=l

\begin{smallmatrix}h_{(u-1)k+1}h_{uk}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(1)}=a_{(u-1)k+1}=1,&~~&h_{(w-1)k+1}h_{wk}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(1)}=a_{(w-1)k+1}=-1,\\
h_{(u-1)k+2}h_{(u-1)k+1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(2)}=a_{(u-1)k+2}=1,&~~&h_{(w-1)k+2}h_{(w-1)k+1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(2)}=a_{(w-1)k+2}=-1\\
\vdots&~~&\vdots\\
h_{uk}h_{uk-1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(k)}=a_{uk}=1,&~~&h_{wk}h_{wk-1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(k)}=a_{wk}=-1.\end{smallmatrix}

Suppose
\tau=[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l};\rho]
is such that for
1\leq u\leq s
,
s+1\leq\rho(u)\leq s+t=l
. Let
w=\rho(u)
. Then
a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+i}=a_{(u-1)k+i}=1
, for
1\leq i\leq k
. Hence we have

\begin{smallmatrix}h_{(w-1)k+1}h_{wk}=-1\\
h_{(w-1)k+2}h_{(w-1)k+1}=-1\\
\vdots\\
h_{wk}h_{wk-1}=-1&\end{smallmatrix}

Hence
h_{(w-1)k+1}=-h_{wk}
,
h_{(w-1)k+2}=-h_{(w-1)k+1}
. Thus, we get

h_{(w-1)k+1}=h_{(w-1)k+3}=\cdots=h_{wk},

but
h_{wk}=-h_{(w-1)k+1}
. Thus, we have a contradiction. So,
\tau\in Z_{S_{n}}(\sigma_{0})
is such that it commutes with
\sigma_{s}=(1\cdots k)\cdots((s-1)k+1\cdots sk)
, and also with
\sigma_{t}=(sk+1\cdots(s+1)k)\cdots(n-k\cdots n)
. For such a
\tau
, we see that for
1\leq m\leq l
:

Thus

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})=\{[\underbrace{h_{1},\ldots,h_{1}}_{k\text{ copies}},\underbrace{h_{2},\ldots,h_{2}}_{k\text{ copies}},\ldots,\underbrace{h_{s+t},\ldots,h_{s+t}}_{k\text{ copies}};\tau_{s}\tau_{t}]\mid\tau_{s}\in Z_{S_{sk}}(\sigma_{s}),\tau_{t}\in Z_{S_{tk}}(\sigma_{t})\},

which is equal to

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{sk}}}(k^{s})\times Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{tk}}}(\overline{k}^{t})
. By the reasoning used in the proof of Proposition
3.4
, it can be seen that
Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{n}}}(\mathbf{a})
is isomorphic to
((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{s})\times((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{t}).

∎

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n};\sigma_{0}]=[\mathbf{1}_{sk},\underbrace{\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{k},\ldots,\overline{\mathbf{1}}_{k}}_{l~-~copies};\sigma_{0}]

We already know that

Now, for

\mathbf{a}

( so let

\rho\in S_{l}
) and the following equations hold:

For
1\leq u\leq s
,
s+1\leq w\leq s+t=l
, and
1\leq i\leq k
, the following hold:

\begin{smallmatrix}h_{(u-1)k+1}h_{uk}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(1)}=a_{(u-1)k+1}=1,&~~&h_{(w-1)k+1}h_{wk}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(1)}=a_{(w-1)k+1}=-1,\\
h_{(u-1)k+2}h_{(u-1)k+1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(2)}=a_{(u-1)k+2}=1,&~~&h_{(w-1)k+2}h_{(w-1)k+1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(2)}=a_{(w-1)k+2}=1\\
\vdots&~~&\vdots\\
h_{uk}h_{uk-1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(u)-1)k+f^{-1}_{u}(k)}=a_{uk}=1,&~~&h_{wk}h_{wk-1}a_{(\rho^{-1}(w)-1)k+f^{-1}_{w}(k)}=a_{wk}=1.\end{smallmatrix}

h_{wk}=-h_{(w-1)k+1}
, and
h_{wk}=h_{wk-1}=\cdots=h_{(w-1)k+2}=h_{(w-1)k+1}

\tau=\tau_{s}\tau_{t}

Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{sk}}}(k^{s})\times Z_{C_{2}\wr{S_{tk}}}(\overline{k}^{t})
, which is isomorphic to
((C_{2}\times C_{k})\wr S_{s})\times(C_{2k}\wr S_{t})
(from Propositons
3.5
and
3.6
.)
∎

The code to compute

#This function returns complete list of z-classes of group g.

CentralizerClasses:=function(g)
local cl,cens,result,c,cen,pos,iso,p;
if IsPermGroup(g) or IsPcGroup(g) then
iso:=IdentityMapping(g);
p:=g;
else
iso:=IsomorphismPermGroup(g);
p:=Range(iso);
fi;
cl:=ConjugacyClasses(g);
cens:=[]; # the iso-images of centralizers
result:=[];
for c in cl do
cen:=Image(iso,Centralizer(c));
pos:=First([1..Length(cens)],x->Size(cens[x])=Size(cen) and
RepresentativeAction(p,cens[x],cen)<>fail);
if pos=fail then
# centralizer is new, make new class
Add(cens,cen);
Add(result,[c]);
else
# conjugate centralizer, add to result class
Add(result[pos],c);
fi;
od;
return result;
end;
