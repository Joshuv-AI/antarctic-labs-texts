𝝉 \boldsymbol{\tau} -Functions, Birkhoff Factorizations and Difference Equations
Darlayne ADDABBO † and Maarten BERGVELT ‡

Abstract
Q Q -systems and T T -systems are systems of integrable difference equations that have recently attracted much attention, and have wide applications in representation theory and statistical mechanics. We show that certain τ \tau -functions, given as matrix elements of the action of the loop group of GL 2 {\rm GL}_{2} on two-component fermionic Fock space, give solutions of a Q Q -system. An obvious generalization using the loop group of GL 3 {\rm GL}_{3} acting on three-component fermionic Fock space leads to a new system of 4 difference equations.

Introduction

Many integrable differential equations can be transformed to simpler, bilinear form by introducing new dependent variables called
\tau
-functions. In practice, these
\tau
-functions are given as matrix elements of infinite-dimensional groups or Lie algebras, etc.

For instance, the famous Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation

\displaystyle u_{t}+u_{xxx}+6uu_{x}=0

(1.1)

is transformed by the substitution

\displaystyle u=2\ln(\tau)_{xx}

into Hirota bilinear form

\displaystyle\big(D_{x}D_{t}+D_{x}^{4}\big)\tau\cdot\tau=0,

where
D_{u}
is the Hirota operator so that
D_{u}\sigma\cdot\tau=\sigma_{u}\tau-\sigma\tau_{u}
. See
[
16
]
for details and many more examples. In the case of the KdV equation, the
\tau
-function is a matrix element for the action of the loop group of
{\rm GL}_{2}
on one-component fermionic Fock space, see for instance
[
10
,
20
,
26
]
.

To produce the integrable equations from
\tau
-functions, one introduces an intermediate object, the Baker function. It satisfies linear equations, and the compatibility of these equations gives the integrable hierarchy.

For instance, for the KdV case, the
\tau
-function is a scalar function
\tau(t_{1},t_{3},t_{5},\dots)
of odd times (
t_{1}=x,t_{3}=t
), and the Baker function is also a scalar function
\Psi
of the times
t_{2k+1}
and of an extra variable
z
, the spectral parameter. It is defined by

\displaystyle\Psi(z;t_{1},t_{3},\dots)=(\Gamma(z,t)\circ\tau(t))/\tau(t),

where
\Gamma(z,t)={\rm e}^{\sum z^{k}t_{k}}{\rm e}^{-\sum\frac{1}{kz^{k}}\partial_{t_{k}}}
; this is essentially the vertex operator for the free fermion vertex algebra, see, e.g.,
[
18
]
. The Baker function satisfies linear equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{k}}\Psi(z;t)=B_{k}(\partial_{x})\Psi(z;t),

(1.2)

where
B_{k}(\partial_{x})
is a degree
k
differential operator in
\partial_{x}
. The compatibility of (
1.2
) for
k=1
and
k=3
turns out to give precisely the Korteweg–de Vries equation (
1.1
).

In this paper we are interested in integrable
difference
(as opposed to differential) equations. Still, we follow very much the setup sketched above for the KdV hierarchy.

In the first part of this paper, we introduce a collection of
\tau
-functions as matrix elements of the action of loop group elements for
{\rm GL}_{2}
, depending on discrete variables

We then define Baker functions. In this case, they are

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}(z)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\tau_{k}^{(\alpha)}}\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}&0\\

Next, we introduce linear equations for the

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k](\alpha+1)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}V^{(\alpha)}_{k},\qquad\Psi^{[k-1](\alpha+1)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}W^{(\alpha)}_{k}.

Here, the

\displaystyle V_{k}^{(\alpha)}\big(W_{k+1}^{(\alpha)}\big)^{-1}=\big(W_{k+1}^{(\alpha-1)}\big)^{-1}V_{k+1}^{(\alpha-1)}.

(1.3)

In another direction, the

Define an element of the central extension of the loop group of
{\rm GL}_{2}
:

\displaystyle g^{[k](\alpha)}=T^{-k}g^{(\alpha)}_{C},

and assume that it has a Birkhoff factorization

,

(see also Appendix

\displaystyle g^{[k](\alpha)}=g^{[k](\alpha)}_{-}g^{[k](\alpha)}_{0+},

where
\pi\big(g^{[k](\alpha)}_{-}\big)=1+{O}\big(z^{-1}\big)

is not zero, see for instance,
[

]

Remark 2.3
.

Theorem
2.2
implies that for

we have a Birkhoff factorization for

is not zero. Here we are dropping the

\displaystyle T^{k}g_{C}=(-1)^{k}\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}&0\\
z^{-k}C(z)&z^{-k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}&0\\
\big(z^{-k}C(z)\big)_{-}&z^{-k}\end{bmatrix}\left((-1)^{k}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\big(z^{-k}C(z)\big)_{0+}z^{k}&1\end{bmatrix}\right).

Here the subscripts

The existence a Birkhoff factorization for

\displaystyle\Gamma=\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}&0\\
(z^{-k}C(z))_{-}&z^{-k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}&0\\
\gamma_{0}z^{-1}+\gamma_{1}z^{-2}+\cdots&z^{-k}\end{bmatrix},

since the right hand side factor of

If we could write this factor as
\Gamma_{-}\Gamma_{0+}
, then we would have
\Gamma(\Gamma_{0+})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big)

\Gamma_{0+}
and its inverse would contain only non-negative powers of

so that

\displaystyle f(z)\begin{bmatrix}z^{k}\\
\gamma_{0}z^{-1}+\gamma_{1}z^{-2}+\cdots\end{bmatrix}+g(z)\begin{bmatrix}0\\
z^{-k}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big).

It is clear that for
k<0
such series

,
g(z)
do not exist (we would need
f(z)
to be zero, but there is no
g(z)
in
\mathbb{C}[[z]]
such that
g(z)z^{-k}=1+\mathcal{O}\big(z^{-1}\big)
).

The argument for
\alpha\neq 0

2.3
Matrix Baker functions and connection matrices

Next, we define the Baker functions. These are elements of the loop group defined by

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}=T^{k}Q_{0}^{-\alpha}g_{-}^{[k](\alpha)}.

(2.8)

Since the Baker functions are all invertible, they are related by
connection matrices
belonging to the loop group. Define
\Gamma_{[k](\alpha)}^{[\ell](\beta)}\in\widetilde{\rm GL}_{2}
by

\displaystyle\Psi^{[\ell](\beta)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}\Gamma_{[k](\alpha)}^{[\ell](\beta)}.

We are interested in connection matrices that implement nearest neighbor steps on the lattice of Baker functions. So define
elementary connection matrices

\displaystyle U_{k}^{(\alpha)}=\Gamma_{[k](\alpha)}^{[k+1](\alpha)},\qquad V_{k}^{(\alpha)}=\Gamma_{[k](\alpha)}^{[k](\alpha+1)},\qquad W_{k}^{(\alpha)}=\Gamma_{[k](\alpha)}^{[k-1](\alpha+1)},

so that we have

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k+1](\alpha)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}U_{k}^{(\alpha)},\qquad\Psi^{[k](\alpha+1)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}V_{k}^{(\alpha)},\qquad\Psi^{[k-1](\alpha+1)}=\Psi^{[k](\alpha)}W_{k}^{(\alpha)}.

(2.9)

Pictorially:

3.4
Matrix Baker functions and connection matrices,
\boldsymbol{3\times 3}
case

Next, we define the Baker functions. These are now elements of the loop group of
{\rm GL}_{3},
defined by

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=T_{1}^{k}T_{2}^{\ell}Q_{0}^{-\alpha}Q_{1}^{-\beta}g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}.

Since the Baker functions are all invertible, they are related by (right) multiplication by connection matrices belonging to
\widetilde{\rm GL}_{3}
. In particular, define

\displaystyle\Psi^{[k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}](\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}=\Psi^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\Gamma^{[k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}](\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)},

so that

\displaystyle\Gamma^{[k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}](\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=\big(g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{-1}Q_{0}^{x_{0}}Q_{1}^{x_{1}}Q_{2}^{x_{2}}g_{-}^{[k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}](\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})},

where

\displaystyle x_{0}=k-k^{\prime}+\alpha-\alpha^{\prime},\qquad x_{1}=k^{\prime}-k+\ell-\ell^{\prime}+\beta-\beta^{\prime},\qquad x_{2}=\ell^{\prime}-\ell.

The simplest connection matrices are those where
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
has two zero components and the other absolute value 1. We therefore define the elementary connection matrices

\displaystyle V_{k,\ell}^{(\alpha_{+},\beta)}=\Gamma^{[k,\ell](\alpha+1,\beta)}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=\big(g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{-1}Q_{0}^{-1}g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha+1,\beta)},

\displaystyle V_{k,\ell}^{(\alpha,\beta_{+})}=\Gamma^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta+1)}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=\big(g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{-1}Q_{1}^{-1}g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta+1)},

\displaystyle W_{k,\ell}^{(\alpha_{+},\beta)}=\Gamma^{[k-1,\ell](\alpha+1,\beta)}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=\big(g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{-1}Q_{1}^{-1}g_{-}^{[k-1,\ell](\alpha+1,\beta)},

\displaystyle W_{k,\ell}^{(\alpha,\beta_{+})}=\Gamma^{[k,\ell-1](\alpha,\beta+1)}_{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}=\big(g_{-}^{[k,\ell](\alpha,\beta)}\big)^{-1}Q_{2}^{-1}g_{-}^{[k,\ell-1](\alpha,\beta+1)}.

Lemma B.1

\displaystyle\det\big(V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big)^{2}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}\det\big(z_{\sigma(i)}^{i+j-2}\big)_{i,j=1}^{k}.

(B.2)

Proof.

The right hand side of (

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}{\sigma}\det\begin{bmatrix}1&z_{1}&z_{1}^{2}&\dots&z_{1}^{k-1}\\
z_{2}&z_{2}^{2}&z_{2}^{3}&\dots&z_{2}^{k}\\
z_{3}^{2}&z_{3}^{3}&z_{3}^{4}&\dots&z_{3}^{k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
z_{k}^{k-1}&z_{k}^{k}&z_{k}^{k+1}&\dots&z_{k}^{2k-2}\end{bmatrix}.

From this, we see that the

Now for every permutation

\displaystyle\sigma(A)=(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}\prod_{s=1}^{k}z_{\sigma(s)}^{s-1}\det\big(V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big).

Summing over all permutations, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}\sigma(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}}(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}\prod_{s=1}^{k}z_{\sigma(s)}^{s-1}\det\big(V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big)=\det\big(V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big)^{2}.

Lemma B.2

If

C.1

\boldsymbol{n}
-component fermions

Recall that most elements

\displaystyle\gamma=\gamma_{-}\gamma_{0+},

with

strictly lower triangular,
\gamma_{0+}
upper triangular (and invertible). Only the

A similar story works for the loop group
\widetilde{\rm GL}_{n}

{\rm GL}_{n}

\widetilde{\rm GL}_{n}
of elements

\displaystyle g=g_{-}g_{0+},\qquad g\in\widetilde{\rm GL}_{n}.

The existence of such a factorization is controlled by the non-vanishing of a fermion matrix element in the semi-infinite wedge space

Recall that, just as in the case of the loop algebra

\widehat{\rm GL}_{n0{+}}

Using Lemma

Appendix D
Factorization and reduction to one-component fermions

Often we want to calculate a matrix element in
F^{(n)}
of fermion fields of the form

\displaystyle\big\langle Q_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}\cdots Q_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}Q_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}v_{0},P\big(\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{a})\big)v_{0}\big\rangle,

where
P
is some polynomial in the fermion fields
\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{a})
,
a=0,1,\dots,n-1
. By linearity, we can reduce to the case where
P=M
is a monomial, and then we can rearrange the factors in the monomial as in Definition
A.1
:
M=M_{n-1}\cdots M_{1}M_{0}
,
M_{a}=M_{a}^{+}M_{a}^{-}
, where
M_{a}^{\pm}
is a monomial in a single type of fermions, ordered according to the subscript of the arguments of the fields:

\displaystyle M_{a}^{\pm}=\mathop{\overleftarrow{\prod}}\limits_{i=1}^{t}\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{i})=\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{t})\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{t-1})\cdots\psi^{\pm}_{a}(z_{2})\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z_{1}).

This defines the ordered product of fermion fields.

We calculate such matrix elements using the following factorization lemma.

Let us first introduce some notation. Let
F=F^{(1)}
be 1-component fermionic Fock space, with vacuum
v_{0}^{(1)}
, and with bilinear form
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{F}
. The fermionic translation operators
Q
,
Q^{-1}
, defined as in Section
A.3
, act on
F
. Let
N_{a}=N_{a}\big(\psi_{a}^{\pm}(z^{a}_{i})\big)
,
a=0,\dots,n-1
, be monomials in fermion fields
\psi_{a}^{+}(z^{a}_{i})
,
\psi_{a}^{-}(z^{a}_{j})
of just type
a
(acting on
F^{(n)}
), and let
\overline{N}_{a}
be the corresponding monomial in 1-component fermion fields
\psi^{\pm}(z^{a}_{i})
(acting on
F
). So for example if

\displaystyle N_{a}=\psi_{a}^{+}(z^{a}_{1})\psi_{a}^{+}(z^{a}_{2})\psi^{-}_{a}(z^{a}_{3}),

then

\displaystyle\overline{N}_{a}=\psi^{+}(z^{a}_{1})\psi^{+}(z^{a}_{2})\psi^{-}(z^{a}_{3}).

Lemma D.1
.

Then

\displaystyle\big\langle Q_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}\cdots Q_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}Q_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}v_{0},N_{n-1}\cdots N_{1}N_{0}v_{{}_{0}}\big\rangle_{F^{(n)}}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big\langle Q^{\alpha_{n-1}}v_{0}^{(1)},\overline{N}_{n-1}v_{0}^{(1)}\big\rangle_{F}\cdots\big\langle Q^{\alpha_{1}}v_{0}^{(1)},\overline{N}_{1}v_{0}^{(1)}\big\rangle_{F}\big\langle Q^{\alpha_{0}}v_{0}^{(1)},\overline{N}_{0}v_{0}^{(1)}\big\rangle_{F}.

Proof.

A basis for
F
is given by elementary wedges (see Definition
A.1
)
\omega_{\underline{k}}
, labeled by pairs of sequences
\underline{k}=(\underline{k}^{+},\underline{k}^{-})
, where each sequence
\underline{k}^{\pm}
is of length
l^{\pm}
(in general
l^{+}\neq l^{-}
) and is of the form
\underline{k}^{\pm}=\big(k_{1}^{\pm}<k_{2}^{\pm}<\cdots<k_{l^{\pm}}^{\pm}\leq-1\big)
. Roughly speaking
\omega_{\underline{k}}
is obtained from the vacuum
v_{0}^{(1)}
by deleting factors
z^{-k_{i}^{-}-1}
from the vacuum (using the contraction operators
i\big(z^{-k_{i}^{-}-1}\big)
) and then adding factors
z^{k_{j}^{+}}
(using the wedging operators
\big(z^{k_{j}^{+}}\wedge\big)
). Since the order in which we apply these operations matters, we must be more precise. So define

\displaystyle\omega_{\underline{k}}=M(\underline{k})v_{0}^{(1)}=M^{+}(\underline{k}^{+})M^{-}(\underline{k}^{-})v_{0}^{(1)},

where

\displaystyle M^{\pm}(\underline{k}^{\pm})={\prescript{}{}{\psi}_{(k^{\pm}_{1})}^{\pm}}{\prescript{}{}{\psi}_{(k_{2}^{\pm})}^{\pm}}\cdots{\prescript{}{}{\psi}_{(k^{\pm}_{s})}^{\pm}}.

Here, we still have
k^{\pm}_{1}<k^{\pm}_{2}<\dots<k^{\pm}_{s}\leq-1
.

Similarly, elementary wedges in
F^{(n)}
are labelled by
n
-tuples
(\underline{k}_{n-1},\underline{k}_{n-2},\dots,\underline{k}_{0})
, and are defined by

\displaystyle\omega_{(\underline{k}_{n-1},\underline{k}_{n-2},\dots,\underline{k}_{0})}=M_{n-1}(\underline{k}_{n-1})M_{n-2}(\underline{k}_{n-2})\cdots M_{0}(\underline{k}_{0})v_{0}^{(n)}.

For the duration of the proof, we will write
v_{0}^{(n)}
for the vacuum in
F^{(n)}
.

Now define a multilinear map from the
n
-fold product of
F
with itself to
n
-component fermionic Fock space
F^{(n)}
:

\displaystyle\phi\colon\ F\times F\times\cdots\times F\to F^{(n)},\qquad(\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-1}},\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-2}},\dots,\omega_{\underline{k}_{0}})\mapsto\omega_{(\underline{k}_{n-1},\underline{k}_{n-2},\dots,\underline{k}_{0})}.

By the universal property of the tensor product, this induces a unique map

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}\colon\ F\otimes F\otimes\cdots\otimes F\to F^{(n)},\qquad\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-1}}\otimes\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\omega_{\underline{k}_{0}}\mapsto\omega_{(\underline{k}_{n-1},\underline{k}_{n-2},\dots,\underline{k}_{0})}.

This map is an isomorphism of vector spaces, and is in fact an isometry, if we define a bilinear form on
F\otimes F\otimes\dots\otimes F
by declaring the basis
\{\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-1}}\otimes\omega_{\underline{k}_{n-2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\omega_{\underline{k}_{0}}\}
to be orthonormal. For this bilinear form on
F^{\otimes n}
we have (given
\omega_{a},\tilde{\omega}_{a}\in F
)

\displaystyle\langle\omega_{n-1}\otimes\omega_{n-2}\otimes\dots\otimes\omega_{0},\tilde{\omega}_{n-1}\otimes\tilde{\omega}_{n-2}\otimes\dots\otimes\tilde{\omega}_{0}\rangle_{F^{\otimes n}}=\prod\langle\omega_{a},\tilde{\omega}_{a}\rangle_{F}.

(D.1)

Now one checks that

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}\big(Q^{\alpha_{n-1}}v_{0}^{(1)}\otimes Q^{\alpha_{n-2}}v_{0}^{(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes Q^{\alpha_{0}}v_{0}^{(1)}\big)=Q_{n-1}^{\alpha_{n-1}}Q_{n-2}^{\alpha_{n-2}}\cdots Q_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}v_{0}^{(n)},

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}\big(\overline{N}_{n-1}v_{0}^{(1)}\otimes\overline{N}_{n-2}v_{0}^{(1)}\otimes\overline{N}_{0}v_{0}^{(1)}\big)=N_{n-1}N_{n-2}\cdots N_{0}v_{0}^{(n)}.

The lemma then follows from (
D.1
) and the fact that
\tilde{\phi}
is an isometry.
∎

So the correlation functions we want to calculate reduce to products of correlation functions on one-component semi-infinite wedge space
F
. In Appendix
E
we review some formulas for one component fermions.

Appendix E
One-component fermion correlation functions

In this appendix we collect some results on one-component fermions. In other words, we are dealing with the fermionic Fock space
F=F^{(1)}
, based on
H=H^{(1)}
. The results in this Appendix should be known, for instance Lemma
E.2
can be found (without proof) in
[
4
]
, but we could not find references with complete proofs of the facts we need.

The whole discussion of Appendix
A
transfers to the present one-component context. For typographical convenience we will write
\psi^{\pm}(z)
for
\psi_{0}^{\pm}(z)
and similarly we write
Q^{\pm 1}
for
Q_{0}^{\pm 1}
.

The correlation functions are matrix elements in
F=F^{(1)}
of the form
\langle Q^{k}v_{0},M(z_{i},w_{j})v_{0}\rangle
, where
M
is some monomial in
\psi(z_{i})
and
\psi(w_{j})
. For reasons of orthogonality of distinct charges we need to insert a power of the fermionic translation operator
Q
.

The simplest case of a correlation function is

\displaystyle\big\langle Q^{\pm 1}v_{0},\psi^{\pm}(z)v_{0}\big\rangle=\left\langle\psi^{\pm}_{(-1)}v_{0},\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}z^{k}\psi^{\pm}_{(-k-1)}v_{0}\right\rangle=1,

since only the
k=0
term contributes.

Lemma E.1
.

For all
k\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle Q^{\pm k}v_{0},\mathop{\overleftarrow{\prod}}\limits_{i=1}^{k}\psi^{\pm}(z_{i})v_{0}\right\rangle=\det\big(V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big).

Here the Vandermonde matrix in
\{z_{i}\}=\{z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}\}
is given by

\displaystyle V^{(k)}_{\{z_{i}\}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
z_{1}&z_{2}&\dots&z_{k}\\
z_{1}^{2}&z_{2}^{2}&\dots&z_{k}^{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
z_{1}^{k-1}&z_{2}^{k-1}&\dots&z_{k}^{k-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(E.1)

Then we have

\displaystyle\det\big(V^{k}_{\{z_{i}\}}\big)=\prod_{k\geq\alpha>\beta\geq 1}(z_{\alpha}-z_{\beta}).

We need

Lemma E.2
.

\displaystyle\big\langle Q^{m-n}v_{0},\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}\big\rangle=\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(w_{i}-w_{j})\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(y_{i}-y_{j})}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(w_{i}-y_{j})}.

Proof.

We first consider the case that
m<n
. So

\displaystyle\big\langle Q^{m-n}v_{0},\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big\langle\psi_{(m-n)}^{-}\psi_{(m-n+1)}^{-}\cdots\psi_{(-1)}^{-}v_{0},\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}\big\rangle,

which is equal to the sum of the coefficients corresponding to all ways of pulling out

\displaystyle\psi_{(m-n)}^{-}\psi_{(m-n+1)}^{-}\cdots\psi_{(-1)}^{-}v_{0},

from the product of fermionic fields acting on the vacuum,

\displaystyle\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}.

Given a
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we reorder the fermionic fields and record the sign obtained from doing so

\displaystyle(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(n)})v_{0}.

We then pull out terms in such a way that no additional sign changes occur from permuting the operators: Pull out
\psi_{(-1)}^{-}
from
\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(n)})
,
\psi_{(-2)}^{-}
from
\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(n-1)})
,
\dots
, and
\psi_{(m-n)}^{-}
from
\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(m-n)})
. The product of the coefficients corresponding to these choices is

\displaystyle y_{\sigma(n)}^{0}y_{\sigma(n-1)}^{1}\cdots y_{\sigma(m+1)}^{n-m-1}.

The remaining contributions for this choice of
\sigma
come from pulling out coefficients of products of wedging and contracting operators from

\displaystyle\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(1)})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(m)})

whose actions cancel with each other. We again pull out these terms such a way that no additional sign changes occur: We count only contributions coming from terms in
\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(1)})
that cancel with each other, terms in
\psi^{+}(w_{m-1})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(2)})
that cancel with each other,
\dots
and terms in
\psi^{+}(w_{1})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(m)})
that cancel with each other.

We claim that we can count each pair,
\psi^{+}(w_{m-i})\psi^{-}(y_{\sigma(i+1)})
,
0\leq i\leq m-1
, as contributing
\frac{1}{w_{m-i}-y_{\sigma(i+1)}}=\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{\sigma(i+1)}^{\ell}}{w_{m-i}^{\ell+1}}
. We know we are not omitting any nontrivial terms in doing this, since any
\frac{y_{\sigma(i+1)}^{\ell}}{w_{m-i}^{\ell+1}}
with
\ell<0
corresponds to
\psi^{+}_{(\ell)}\psi^{-}_{(-\ell-1)}
and
\psi^{-}_{(-\ell-1)}
kills the vacuum or any vector obtained by acting by contracting operators on the vacuum. We must therefore only prove that we are not including any extra nontrivial terms. Towards this end, consider some monomial,

\displaystyle(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}y_{\sigma(n)}^{0}y_{\sigma(n-1)}\cdots y_{\sigma(m+1)}^{n-m-1}\frac{y_{\sigma(1)}^{\ell_{1}}}{w_{m}^{\ell_{1}+1}}\frac{y_{\sigma(2)}^{\ell_{2}}}{w_{m-1}^{\ell_{2}+1}}\cdots\frac{y_{\sigma(m)}^{\ell_{m}}}{w_{1}^{\ell_{m}+1}},

corresponding to a product of wedging operators acting on the vacuum vector which give 0. Since all of the wedging operators,
\psi^{-}_{(\ell)}
, are such that
\ell<0
, the only way this is possible is if two of the wedging operators are the same. But this means that two of the
y_{i}
s in the above expression are being raised to the same power. Define a new element,
\gamma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
by composing
\sigma
with the transposition that interchanges these two
y_{i}
s. The sign of this new element is
-(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}
. So there is a monomial in the expansion of

\displaystyle(-1)^{\lvert\gamma\rvert}\frac{y_{\gamma(n)}^{0}y_{\gamma(n-1)}\cdots y_{\gamma(m+1)}^{n-m-1}}{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{m-1}(w_{m-i}-y_{\gamma(i+1)})},

which cancels with the above monomial.

Summing over all
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, we have that

\displaystyle\big\langle Q^{m-n}v_{0},\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}\big\rangle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}\frac{y_{\sigma(n)}^{0}y_{\sigma(n-1)}\cdots y_{\sigma(m+1)}^{n-m-1}}{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{m-1}(w_{m-i}-y_{\sigma(i+1)})}.

Using Leibniz’s formula to expand this as a determinant and then computing the determinant, we find that this is exactly equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(w_{i}-w_{j})\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(y_{i}-y_{j})}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(w_{i}-y_{j})}.

The proof in the case that
m\geq n
is similar. Here, we need to argue that

\displaystyle\big\langle Q^{m-n}v_{0},\psi^{+}(w_{1})\cdots\psi^{+}(w_{m})\psi^{-}(y_{1})\cdots\psi^{-}(y_{n})v_{0}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{m}}(-1)^{\lvert\sigma\rvert}\frac{w_{\sigma(1)}^{m-n-1}w_{\sigma(2)}^{m-n-2}\cdots w_{\sigma(m-n)}^{0}}{\prod\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}(w_{\sigma({m-i})}-y_{i+1})}.

∎

In Lemma
E.2
we see that this particular matrix element of fermion fields is the expansion of a rational function in the variables appearing in the fermion fields. This is not an accident, but is a basic property of vertex algebras, see
[
13
,
14
,
18
]
, referred as rationality of vertex operators. Indeed, the one-component fermionic Fock space
F^{(1)}
is an example of a vertex algebra, and the fermionic fields are vertex operators for this vertex algebra structure. Another basic property of vertex algebras is called commutativity; roughly speaking it says that if we permute the vertex operators in a matrix element of a product of vertex operators the answer is again an expansion of the same rational function, but in a different region, up to a sign. See also
[
8
]
.

For instance we will also need the following matrix elements.

Lemma E.3
.

\displaystyle\left\langle Q^{m-n-1}v_{0},\psi^{-}(z)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\psi^{+}(w_{i})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi^{-}(y_{i})v_{0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}(z-y_{i})\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(w_{i}-w_{j})\prod\limits_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(y_{i}-y_{j})}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{n}(z-w_{i})\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(w_{i}-y_{j})}.

One could derive this lemma from Lemma
E.2
by commutativity of vertex operators, using the general theory of vertex algebras. For the convenience of the reader we give an elementary proof of this lemma, just using the commutation relations of fermion fields.

Proof.

Let
z,w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{m}
be variables and define

\displaystyle W=\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq m}(w_{j}-w_{k}).

Consider the following rational function

\displaystyle R=\frac{W}{\prod\limits_{1\leq j\leq m}(w_{j}-z)}.

The partial fraction expansion
3

3

Recall that if
f(z)

at

\displaystyle R=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{W_{i}}{w_{i}-z},

\displaystyle W_{i}=\frac{W}{\prod\limits_{1\leq j\leq m\atop j\neq i}(w_{j}-w_{i})}=(-1)^{i+m}\prod\limits_{1\leq j<k\leq m\atop j,k\neq i}(w_{j}-w_{k}).

Instead of

\displaystyle R^{\prime}=\frac{W}{\prod\limits_{1\leq j\leq m}(z-w_{j})}=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{W_{i}}{z-w_{i}}.

Recall the formal series

\displaystyle\delta(z,w)=\frac{1}{z-w}+\frac{1}{w-z}.

Here (and from now on) we use the convention that we expand in the second variable, so that for example

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta(z,w_{i})(-1)^{m+1}W_{i}+(-1)^{m}\frac{W}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}(w_{j}-z)}=\frac{W}{\prod\limits_{i=1}^{m}(z-w_{i})}.

(E.2)

which is what we wanted to show.
