Abstract
For a bivariate P ⁡ ( x , y ) ∈ ℝ ⁡ [ x , y ] ∖ ( ℝ ⁡ [ x ] ∪ ℝ ⁡ [ y ] ) P(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]\setminus(\mathbb{R}[x]\cup\mathbb{R}[y]) , our first result shows that for all finite A ⊆ ℝ A\subseteq\mathbb{R} , | P ⁡ ( A , A ) | ≥ α ​ | A | 5 / 4 |P(A,A)|\geq\alpha|A|^{5/4} with α = α ⁡ ( deg ⁡ P ) ∈ ℝ > 0 \alpha=\alpha(\deg P)\in\mathbb{R}^{>0} unless P ⁡ ( x , y ) = f ⁡ ( γ ​ u ​ ( x ) + δ ​ u ​ ( y ) ) ​ or ​ P ​ ( x , y ) = f ⁡ ( u m ​ ( x ) ​ u n ​ ( y ) ) P(x,y)=f(\gamma u(x)+\delta u(y))\text{ or }P(x,y)=f(u^{m}(x)u^{n}(y)) for some univariate f , u ∈ ℝ ⁡ [ t ] ∖ ℝ f,u\in\mathbb{R}[t]\setminus\mathbb{R} , constants γ , δ ∈ ℝ ≠ 0 \gamma,\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{\neq 0} , and m , n ∈ ℕ ≥ 1 m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1} . This resolves the symmetric nonexpanders classification problem proposed by de Zeeuw. Our second and third results are sum-product type theorems for two polynomials, generalizing the classical result by Erdős and Szemerédi as well as a theorem by Shen. We also obtain similar results for ℂ \mathbb{C} , and from this deduce results for fields of characteristic 0 0 and fields of large prime characteristic. Introduction

Suppose
K
is a field,
P(x,y)
is a bivariate polynomial over
K
, and
P(x,y)
has nontrivial dependence on
x
and
y
, in other words,
P(x,y)
is in
K[x,y]\setminus(K[x]\cup K[y]
). For
A,B\subseteq K
, we set

P(A,B):=\{P(a,b):a\in A,b\in B\}.

We say that
P(x,y)
is
nonexpanding
over
K
if for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there is arbitrarily large
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
and
A,B\subseteq K
with
|A|=|B|=n
such that
|P(A,B)|<n^{1+\varepsilon}
. We say that
P(x,y)
is
symmetrically nonexpanding
over
K
if for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there is arbitrarily large
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
and
A\subseteq K
with
|A|=n
such that
|P(A,A)|<n^{1+\varepsilon}
.
A symmetric nonexpanding polynomial over
K
(e.g.,
P(x,y)=x+y
, where
A
can be chosen to be a long arithmetic progression) is clearly nonexpanding over
K
. The converse is not true.
A well-known result by Elekes and Rónyai
[
12
]
provides us with a classification of nonexpanding polynomials when
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
: The polynomial
P(x,y)
is nonexpanding over
K
if and only if there are nonconstant univariate polynomials
f,u,v
over
K
such that

\text{either}\quad P(x,y)=f(u(x)+v(y))\quad\text{or}\quad P(x,y)=f(u(x)v(y)).

With
P(x,y)=x+y^{2}
, a result by Elekes, Nathanson,
and Ruzsa
[
11
]
implies that there is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
such that for all
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we have
|P(A,A)|\geq\alpha|A|^{5/4}
. In particular,
x+y^{2}
is nonexpanding but not symmetrically nonexpanding. This was pointed out by de Zeeuw in
[
9
]
, who then asked for the general form of
P(x,y)
for similar lower bound on
|P(A,A)|
to hold.
Our first theorem answers de Zeeuw’s question and gives us a classification of symmetrically nonexpanding polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, and
P(x,y)\in K[x,y]\setminus(K[x]\cup K[y])
. Then there is
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P)
such that exactly one of the following possibilities holds:

(i)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
A\subseteq K
with
|A|=n
, we have
|P(A,A)|\geq\alpha n^{5/4}.

(ii)

P(x,y)=f(\gamma u(x)+\delta u(y))
where
f
and
u
are nonconstant univariate polynomials over
K
, and
\gamma
and
\delta
are in
K^{\neq 0}
.

(iii)

P(x,y)=f(u^{m}(x)u^{n}(y))
where
f
and
u
are nonconstant univariate polynomials over
K
, and
m
and
n
are in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
.

Moreover,
P(x,y)
is symmetrically nonexpanding over
K
if an only if it satisfies either (ii) or (iii).

Erdős and Szemerédi observed in
[
13
]
the following sum-product phenomenon: There is
r\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}
such that for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, for all finite
A\subseteq\mathbb{Z}

with
|A|=n
, we have

\max\{|A+A|,|AA|\}\geq\alpha n^{r}.

In the same paper, they also conjectured that the exponent
r
can be taken arbitrarily close to
2
.
Partial results toward this conjecture with explicit
r
were obtained by Nathanson
[
22
]
with
r=32/31
, by Ford
[
14
]
with
r=16/15
, by Chen
[
6
]
with
r=6/5
, by Elekes
[
10
]
with
r=5/4
, by Solymosi
[
27
,
28
]
with
r=14/11-o(1)
and
r=4/3-o(1)
, and by Konyagin–Shkredov
[
18
,
19
]
, Rudnev–Shkredov–Stevens
[
24
]
, and Shakan
[
25
]
, with
r=4/3+c
for some explicit small
c\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
. Many of the above results are in fact proven for more general contexts, where
\mathbb{Z}
is replaced by either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. As polynomials involve both addition and multiplication, there is a general expectation that “most”
P(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
already exhibit on their own the sum-product phenomenon
|P(A,A)|\geq\alpha|A|^{r}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}
. Theorem
1.1
can therefore be seen as a sum-product phenomenon for a single polynomial with
r=5/4
, in which we clarify the meaning of “most”
P(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]
.

The proof of some of the above sum-product bounds also yields asymmetric versions for
\max\{|A+B|,|AB|\}
. The asymmetric counterpart of Theorem
1.1
for
\mathbb{R}
, with a better exponent
r=4/3
, is the following strengthening of the Elekes–Rónyai theorem by Raz, Sharir, and Solymosi: For
P(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}[x,y]\setminus(\mathbb{R}[x]\cup\mathbb{R}[y])
, there is
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P)\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
such that if there are finite
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
|A|=|B|=n
and
P(A,B)<\alpha n^{4/3}
, then either

P(x,y)=f(u(x)+v(y))
or
P(x,y)=f(u(x)v(y))
for some univariate polynomials
f,u,v
over
\mathbb{R}
.

Somewhat surprisingly, proving Theorem
1.1
requires a result for two polynomials along the line of the Raz–Sharir–Solymosi theorem. That is our second theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
K
is
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, and
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are bivariate polynomials in
K[x,y]\setminus(K[x]\cup K[y])
. Then there is
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)
such that exactly one of the following holds:

(i)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and subsets
A
and
B
of
K
with
|A|=|B|=n

\max\{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|\}\geq\alpha n^{5/4}.

(ii)

P(x,y)=f(\gamma_{1}u(x)+\delta_{1}v(y))
and
Q(x,y)=g(\gamma_{2}u(x)+\delta_{2}v(y))
where
f
,
g
,
u
, and
v
are nonconstant univariate polynomials over
K
and
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\delta_{1}
, and
\delta_{2}
are in
K^{\neq 0}
.

(iii)

P(x,y)=f(u^{m_{1}}(x)v^{n_{1}}(y))
and
Q(x,y)=g(u^{m_{2}}(x)v^{n_{2}}(y))
where
f
,
g
,
u
, and
v
are nonconstant univariate polynomials over
K
and
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
n_{1}
, and
n_{2}
are in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
.

With
P(x,y)=x+y
and
Q(x,y)=xy
, it is easy to see that we are not in scenario (ii) or (iii), so we must be in scenario (i). Hence, Theorem
1.2
implies the Erdős–Szemerédi theorem.
An early known special case of Theorem
1.2
with
P(x,y)=x+y
is the main result in the paper “Algebraic methods in sum-product phenomena” by Shen
[
26
]
. A variant for finite field of Shen’s result was obtained by Bukh and Tsimerman
[
4
]
.

We also obtain a generalization of Theorems
1.1
(except for the last assertion) for two polynomials. This is also the counterpart of Theorem
1.2
for
A=B
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. With the same
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)
as in Theorem
1.2
, exactly one of the following holds:

(i)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and all
A\subseteq K
with
|A|=n

\max\{|P(A,A)|,|Q(A,A)|\}\geq\alpha n^{5/4}.

(ii)

P(x,y)=f(\gamma_{1}u(x)+\delta_{1}u(y))
and
Q(x,y)=g(\gamma_{2}u(x)+\delta_{2}u(y))
where
f
,
g
, and
u
are univariate polynomials over
K
and
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\delta_{1}
, and
\delta_{2}
are in
K^{\neq 0}
.

(iii)

P(x,y)=f(u^{m_{1}}(x)u^{n_{1}}(y))
and
Q(x,y)=g(u^{m_{2}}(x)u^{n_{2}}(y))
where
f
,
g
, and
u
are univariate polynomials over
K
and
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
n_{1}
, and
n_{2}
are in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
.

By standard model-theoretic transfer, the analogues of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
, and
1.3
also hold for all algebraically closed fields and real closed fields. With some basic Galois theory, modified versions where “exactly one of the cases holds” is replaced by “at least one of the cases holds” in the statement of these three theorems can be obtained for all fields of characteristic
0
. Proposition
5.4
covers both these results. Our results also have some ramifications for fields with large positive characteristic. Results in the same direction for fixed finite fields were obtained by Tao
[
33
]
using an algebraic regularity lemma. See Remark
5.5
for the related discussion.

Erdős–Szemerédi sum-product type theorems, the Elekes–Rónyai type theorem, as well as our results can be seen as instances of a more general phenomenon: finite subsets of a one-dimensional space have expansion behavior under a “sufficiently nice” binary function unless the situation is “controlled” by a single abelian group. One reasonable interpretation of “sufficiently nice” is to be definable in a tame structure in the sense of model theory.
The current paper and recent qualitative generalizations of the Elekes–Rónyai theorem by Bays, Breuillard, Chernikov, Starchenko, and Peterzil
[
2
,
7
,
8
]
can be seen as two pieces of evidence for this view. (These other results are stated in the framework of points on a grid satisfying a “sufficiently nice” relation; see
[
9
, Section 2]
for a translation between the frameworks. A version of Theorem
1.2
without an explicit exponent in the bound and in a less precise form can in fact be deduced from the result by Bays and Breuillard
[
2
]
; see Remark
4.9
.)
Also using tools from model theory are the works of Hrushovski
[
16
]
and then of Breuillard, Green, and Tao
[
3
]
on the structure of approximate groups; the classification in the latter is not an instance of the above phenomenon but it is very close in spirit; see
[
17
]
for a related survey.

We now describe in more detail the strategies of the proofs and the structure of the paper. After the preliminary Section 2 on logic and o-minimality and Section 3 on standard nonexpanding examples, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is given in Section 4. This has three steps. First, we apply the strengthening
[
23
]
by Raz–Sharir–Solymosi of Elekes–Rónyai’s result in
[
12
]
and reduce to the case where
P(x,y)
is of the form
f_{1}(u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(y))
or
f_{1}(u_{1}(x)v_{1}(y))
, and
Q(x,y)
is of the form
f_{2}(u_{2}(x)+v_{2}(y))
or
f_{2}(u_{2}(x)v_{2}(y))
,
where
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
v_{1}
, and
v_{2}
are univariate polynomials over
K
.
In the second step, we use a suitable generalization of the Szemerédi–Trotter Theorem
[
32
]
(the result for
\mathbb{R}
comes from
[
15
]
by Fox, Pach, Sheffer, Suk, and Zahl, and that for
\mathbb{C}
can be deduced from
[
29
]
by Solymosi and de Zeeuw)
in the same fashion as in Elekes’ classical proof of sum-product phenomena
[
10
]
. This allows us to prove that (i) happens except for a special situation. In the final third step, we analyze the above special situation to show that either (ii) or (iii) must happen. This last step employs ideas from semialgebraic geometry/o-minimal geometry, in particular, a definable Ramsey-type result. It is quite close in principle to the proof in
[
26
]
and the proof in
[
23
]
, but we use o-minimality instead of algebraic geometry.

The third step is readily generalizable to many other settings related to o-minimality. We expect that Theorem
1.2
admits suitable generalizations as well, once the corresponding ingredients for the first and second steps are developed; in some cases, they already are. See Remark
4.8
for a more detailed discussion.

Finally, the proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
are given in Section 5. The key idea is to apply Theorem
1.2
for the two polynomials
P(x,y)
and
\hat{P}(x,y)
where we set
\hat{P}(x,y):=P(y,x)
.
We also discuss here generalizations of the main theorems to fields other than
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
in this section.

2
Preliminaries on Logic and o-minimality

To make the paper more accessible to readers without a background in model theory, we include here a brief introduction to the topics of logic, model theory, and semialgebraic geometry (through the lens of o-minimality).
We will keep the discussion brief, focusing on motivations and the general picture. For more systematic accounts of model theory in general and o-minimality in particular see
[
20
,
34
]
and
[
35
]
respectively.

The logician’s notion of a
structure
is a generalization of the notion of a field. In this paper, the main structures are
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, and
(\mathbb{R},<)
. The method of this paper, modulo some missing ingredients, looks ready for generalization to some
expansions
of
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
(i.e., structures enriching
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
); examples include the exponential field
(\mathbb{R},\text{exp})
of real numbers and the expansion
\mathbb{C}_{\text{an}}
of
\mathbb{C}
obtained by adding restrictions of analytic functions to bounded closed disks. So the reader may want to keep such structures in mind as well; see Remark
4.8
for further details.

The notion of
definable set
(always assumed to be with parameters) is the corresponding generalization of the notion of
algebraic set
(i.e., solution set of a system of polynomial equations in a field).
In a field
K
, definable sets include algebraic sets and also sets that can be obtained from these through finitely many applications of taking intersections, unions, complements, and projections to lower dimensions. For instance, the set

X=\{(a,b)\in K^{2}:\text{ there are }c,d\in K\text{ with }c^{2}+d^{2}=a\text{ and }c^{3}+d^{3}=b\}

is definable in
K
as it is the projection of the algebraic subset of
K^{4}
defined by the system
x=z^{2}+t^{2},y=z^{3}+t^{3}
onto the first two coordinates. By the above description,
K^{2}\setminus X
and the projection of
K^{2}\setminus X
onto the first coordinate are also definable. Definable sets are “solution sets” of first-order formulas (which, in the case of fields, involve logical symbols like
\wedge,\vee,\neg,\exists,\forall
on top of field-theoretic operations). For example,
X
is the “solution set” of the first-order formula

\exists z\exists t(x=z^{2}+t^{2}\wedge y=z^{3}+t^{3}).

Definable sets in an ordered field
(K,<)
can described similarly, but with the role of algebraic sets replaced by that of
semialgebraic sets
(i.e., solution sets of systems of polynomial inequalities). In fact, definability in
(\mathbb{R},<)
and in
\mathbb{R}
are equivalent because for
a
and
b
in
\mathbb{R}
, we have
a<b
if and only if there is
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
a+c^{2}=b
. For later use, a first-order formula is a first-order
statement
if all of its variables are attached to a quantifier
\exists
or
\forall
.

The idea of definability extends to more complicated objects like functions, families of sets, groups, etc. In a structure with underlying set
K
, a function
f:X\to K^{n}
with
X\subseteq K^{m}
is
definable
if the graph of
f
is definable; in particular, this implies that
X
and
\text{Image}(f)
are definable. Still in the same structure, a family
(X_{b})_{b\in Y}
of subsets of
K^{m}
is
definable
if
Y
is a definable subset of
K^{n}
for some
n
, and the set
X=\{(a,b)\in K^{m+n}:a\in X_{b},b\in Y\}
is definable.

Compared to more restricted notions like algebraic sets, definable sets are very versatile. For example, if
f:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is definable in
(\mathbb{R},<)
, then the set

\{a\in X:f\text{ is differentiable with continuous derivatives at }a\}

is definable simply because all the relevant concepts admit epsilon-delta definitions. Unfortunately, this flexibility often comes with the burden that definable sets are often overly complicated and resist geometric understanding. Model theory is, to a certain extent, the study of structures where we do not have these shortcomings. Examples of such structures are
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, and
(\mathbb{R},<)
; this is reflected by Fact
2.1
below. Fact
2.1
(i) implies that definable sets in
\mathbb{R}
are essentially well-behaved manifolds, which conceptually explain Fact
2.1
(ii) below.

Fact 2.1
.

Let
X
be definable in
\mathbb{R}
, equivalently, definable in
(\mathbb{R},<)
, and let
X^{\prime}
be definable in
\mathbb{C}
. Then we have the following:

(i)

(Tarski–Seidenberg Theorem)
X
is semialgebraic
[
20
, Theorem 3.3.15]
.

(ii)

(Abstract cell decomposition)
X
is a disjoint finite union
\sqcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
, where
X_{i}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-definably homeomorphic image of
(0,1)^{k_{i}}
with
k_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
; hence, by the Invariance of Domain Theorem,
X_{i}
is a connected open set when
k_{i}=m
and an open interval when
k_{i}=m=1
for each
i\in I
; moreover, if
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is a definable function, we can choose
(X_{i})_{i\in I}
such that
f
is continuous on
X_{i}
for each
i\in I

[
35
, Theorem 3.2.11]
.

(iii)

(Chevalley–Tarski Theorem)
X^{\prime}
is constructible (i.e., a boolean combination of algebraic sets in
\mathbb{C}
, or equivalently, a finite union of quasi-affine varieties over
\mathbb{C}
)
[
20
, Theorem 3.2.2]
. ∎

Another advantage in dealing with definable sets is a built-in mechanism for induction, as the image of a definable set under projection to fewer coordinates remains definable. This is particularly powerful when we have good notions of dimension.
By Fact
2.1
(i) and Fact
2.1
(iii), definable sets in
\mathbb{R}
are essentially real manifolds, and definable sets in
\mathbb{C}
are essentially algebraic varieties. Hence, it is not too surprising that there are good notions of dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}
in these cases. Fact
2.2
is about the properties of
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}
. The properties of
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}
are generally consequences of the fact that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}
agrees with the usual of notion of dimension of a real manifold
[
35
, page 5]
, and real closed fields are o-minimal
[
35
, Corollary 2.3.6]
. The properties of
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}
are generally consequences of the fact that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}
agrees with dimension in the sense of algebraic geometry
[
20
, Corollary 6.2.23]
, and algebraically closed fields are strongly minimal
[
20
, Corollary 3.2.9]
.

Fact 2.2
.

Suppose
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, and
X
and
Y
are definable in
K
. Then we have the following:

(i)

\dim_{K}(\emptyset)=-\infty
;
\dim_{K}(X)=0
if and only if
X
is finite;
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(I)=1
with
I=(a,b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty,+\infty\}
and
a<b
;
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C})=1
.
[
35
, page 5]
[
20
, Corollary 6.2.23]

(ii)

\dim_{K}(X\cup Y)=\max\{\dim_{K}(X),\dim_{K}(Y)\}

[
35
, Proposition 4.1.3]

[
20
, Lemma 6.2.7]
.

(iii)

If
(X_{b})_{b\in Y}
is a definable family, and

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
be in
\mathbb{C}
. For positive

, we considered the generalized arithmetic progression

G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}):=\left\{\sum_{0\leq i,j<l}a_{ij}\tau_{1}^{i}\tau_{2}^{j}:a_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z},0\leq a_{ij}<k\right\}.

Then we have the following:

(i)

If
\mathrm{trdeg}(\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})/\mathbb{Q})=0
, then
there is
h=h(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
such that for sufficiently large

, we have
|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|\asymp_{l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}k^{h}
.

(ii)

If
\mathrm{trdeg}(\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})/\mathbb{Q})=1
,
then there is
h=h(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
such that for sufficiently large
l
,
|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|\asymp_{l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}k^{lh}
.

(iii)

If
\mathrm{trdeg}(\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})/\mathbb{Q})=2
, then

|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|=k^{l^{2}}
.

Proof.

We will only prove statement (i) as statement (ii) can be proven in a similar fashion, and statement (iii) is immediate. Let
d_{1}
be the degree of the minimal polynomial of
\tau_{1}
over
\mathbb{Q}
and
d_{2}
be the degree of the minimal polynomial of
\tau_{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{1})
. It suffices to show

|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|\asymp_{l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}k^{h}\text{ with }h=d_{1}d_{2}\text{ and }l\geq\max\{d_{1}-1,d_{2}-1\}.

Note that
(\tau_{1}^{i}\tau_{2}^{j})_{0\leq i<d_{1},0\leq j<d_{2}}
are linearly independent. Hence,

|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|\geq k^{h}
. On the other hand, by using the minimal polynomial of
\tau_{1}
over
\mathbb{Q}
and the minimal polynomial of
\tau_{2}
over
\mathbb{Q}(\tau_{1})
, each element of

is equal to a sum

\sum_{0\leq i<d_{1},0\leq j<d_{2}}\frac{b_{ij}}{N}\tau_{1}^{i}\tau_{2}^{j}

where

is a positive integer, and

are integers with

for some positive integers

. Hence,
|G(k,l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})|=O_{l,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}}(k^{h})
and the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. Then we have the following:

(i)

If

is either additive or multiplicative, then for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there are

with arbitrarily large

In other words,

is nonexpanding.

(ii)

If

is either symmetrically additive or symmetrically multiplicative, then for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there is
A\subseteq K
with arbitrarily large
n=|A|
such that

|P(A,A)|<n^{1+\varepsilon}.

In other words,
P(x,y)
is symmetrically nonexpanding.

(iii)

If
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
forms an additive pair or a multiplicative pair, then for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there are
A,B\subseteq K
with arbitrarily large

such that

\max\{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|\}<n^{1+\varepsilon}.

(iv)

If

and

forms a symmetric additive pair or a symmetric multiplicative pair, then for each
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
, there is

with arbitrarily large

such that

\max\{|P(A,A)|,|Q(A,A)|\}<n^{1+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We note that (i) follows from (iii), and (ii) follows from (iv), so we only need to prove (iii) and (iv). We will only discuss the situation where

and

form an additive pairs in (iii) as the remaining parts of (iii) and (iv) can be handled in a similar fashion. Consider first the special case where

. For

By Lemma

such that for sufficiently large

Hence, choosing

with
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the standard way. Then every
\mathbb{C}
-definable set can be viewed as an
\mathbb{R}
-definable set. Note that if
X
is
\mathbb{C}
-definable and
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(X)=n
, then
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(X)=2n
. Indeed, using Fact
2.1
(iii), it suffices to consider the case where
X
is an affine variety. By Noether normalization lemma and Fact
2.2
(iii), we can further reduce it to the case where
X
is
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and the desired conclusion follows from Fact
2.2
(i-ii).

Assume
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and
S
is a subset of
K
. A
decomposition
of
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
consists of a finite set
I
, and an
\mathbb{R}
-definable family
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)=(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
for each
i\in I
such that for all
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}
, we have
C_{b_{1},b_{2}}^{i}\subseteq C_{b_{1},b_{2}}
, and

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\Big)<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

We say that
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
for

as above is

-scattered
if for all
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\big\}<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

Note that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
forms a decomposition of itself. It is easy to see that this notion of being
K
-scattered coincides with the previous one because of the relationship between
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
and
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(X)
for a
\mathbb{C}
-definable set
X
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
,
S
is a subset of
K
, and
I
together with
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
for
i\in I
form a decomposition of
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
.
If
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, then for some
i\in I
,
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
is not
K
-scattered.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)=(C_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
, and
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)=(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
for
i\in I
.
For
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}
, let
Y_{b_{1},b_{2}}=\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\}
and let

Y^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}=\big\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\big\}.

Hence,
Y_{b_{1},b_{2}}
and
Y^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}
are
\mathbb{R}
-definable for all
i\in I
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in K^{2}
by Fact
2.2
(iii).
Note that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
if and only if
\dim_{K}(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=1
for all
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})
in
S^{2}
, so
Y_{b_{1},b_{2}}
is also
K
-definable by Fact
2.2
(iii).
From the assumption that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, we obtain
(c_{1},c_{2})\in K^{2}
with
\dim_{K}(Y_{c_{1},c_{2}})\geq 1
, or equivalently,

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(Y_{c_{1},c_{2}})\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

For any
(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}
, we have
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big(C_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\big)<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}K
, so Fact
2.2
(ii) implies there is
i\in I
such that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big(C_{c_{1},c_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\big)\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

It follows that
Y_{c_{1},c_{2}}=\bigcup_{i\in I}Y^{i}_{c_{1},c_{2}}
.
Using Fact
2.2
(ii), we get
i\in I
such that
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(Y^{i}_{c_{1},c_{2}})\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
. Fix such
i
. Note that
C_{c_{1},c_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\subseteq C_{c_{1},c_{2}}
and

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big(C_{c_{1},c_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\big)=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C_{c_{1},c_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\quad\text{ for any }(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in Y^{i}_{c_{1},c_{2}}.

So we can apply Fact
4.5
to get
X^{\prime}\subseteq K^{2}
with
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(X^{\prime})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq K^{2}
with
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(Y^{\prime})\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
such that

X^{\prime}\subseteq C_{c_{1},c_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}\quad\text{for all }(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in Y^{\prime}

Hence, we have
\dim(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
for all
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(b_{1}^{\prime},b_{2}^{\prime})
in

. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

If
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
,
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are individually either additive or multiplicative, and for all cofinite
S\subseteq K
, the family
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, then exactly one of the following possibilities holds:

(i)

P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
form an additive pair over
K
;

(ii)

P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
form a multiplicative pair over
K
.

Moreover, with
N=\deg P+\deg Q+1
, the same conclusion holds with the weaker assumption that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered for all cofinite
S\subseteq K
with
|K\setminus S|<N
.

Proof.

Suppose
K
is either
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. We first prove the following special case of the proposition.

Claim 1
.

Suppose
P(x,y)=u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(y)
and
Q(x,y)=u_{2}(x)v_{2}(y)
where
u_{1}
,
v_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
v_{2}
are nonconstant univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K
, and

S=\{b\in K:v_{1}(b)\neq 0,v_{2}(b)\neq 0\}.

Then
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is scattered.

Proof of Claim
1
.
Towards a contradiction, we assume that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered. As
u_{1}(x)
and
u_{2}(x)
are nonconstant,
u_{1}^{\prime}(x)
and
u_{2}^{\prime}(x)
are nonzero. The numerator has higher degree than the denominator in the fraction
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}(x)
, and so
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}(x)
gives us a nonconstant rational function. Hence, we can find a finite
\mathbb{R}
-definable family
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
of Euclidean open subsets of
K
with

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i})<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)

and such that for each
i\in I
,
u^{\prime}_{2}(x)
is nonzero on
U_{i}
, the functions
u_{1}(x)
,
u_{2}(x)
, and
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}(x)
induce
\mathbb{R}
-definable differentiable homeomorphisms from the
U_{i}
to the respective images. For
i\in I
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in K^{2}
, let
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
be the
\mathbb{R}
-definable family
(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
with

C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}=\{(u_{1}(a)+v_{1}(b_{1}),u_{2}(a)v_{2}(b_{2})):a\in U_{i}\}.

By Fact
2.2
(iii), the set
I
together with
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
form a decomposition of
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
.
As
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, by Lemma
4.6
, there is
i\in I
such that
\mathscr{C}^{i}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered. We obtain, in particular,
i\in I
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}
such that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\big\}=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

Fix
(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}
such that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
, and set

\rho:U_{i}\to C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}},a\mapsto(u_{1}(a)+v_{1}(b_{1}),u_{2}(a)v_{2}(b_{2}))

and

\rho^{\prime}:U_{i}\to C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}},a\mapsto(u_{1}(a)+v_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1}),u_{2}(a)v_{2}(b^{\prime}_{2})).

By our choice of
U_{i}
, the maps
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
are
\mathbb{R}
-definable homeomorphisms from
U_{i}
onto their respective images. Using Fact
2.2
(vi), choose
C^{\prime}\subseteq C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}}
such that
C^{\prime}
is an
\mathbb{R}
-definable open subset of
C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}
with
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{\prime})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
. Set

U^{\prime}=\rho^{-1}(C^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda:U^{\prime}\to U_{i},a\mapsto(\rho^{\prime})^{-1}(\rho(a)).

Then
U^{\prime}
is a definable open subset of
U_{i}
, the function
\lambda:U^{\prime}\to U_{i}
is
\mathbb{R}
-definable, analytic, and a homeomorphism onto its image, and

(u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(b_{1}),u_{2}(x)v_{2}(b_{2}))=(u_{1}(\lambda(x))+v_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1}),u_{2}(\lambda(x))v_{2}(b^{\prime}_{2}))\text{ on }U^{\prime}.

Differentiating
u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(b_{1})=u_{1}(\lambda(x))+v_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1})
, we get
u^{\prime}_{1}(x)=u_{1}^{\prime}(\lambda(x))\lambda^{\prime}(x)\text{ on }U^{\prime}.

Taking logarithmic derivative of both sides of
u_{2}(x)v_{2}(b_{2})=u_{2}(\lambda(x))v_{2}(b^{\prime}_{2})
, we get

\frac{u^{\prime}_{2}(x)}{u_{2}(x)}=\frac{u^{\prime}_{2}(\lambda(x))}{u_{2}(\lambda(x))}\lambda^{\prime}(x)\text{ on }U^{\prime}.

Dividing the two equations involving
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
, we get

\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}(\lambda(x))\text{ on }U^{\prime}.

As we have arranged that
\frac{u_{1}^{\prime}u_{2}}{u_{2}^{\prime}}
is a homeomorphism on
U_{i}
,
\lambda(x)=x
on
U^{\prime}
. It follows that
v_{1}(b_{1})=v_{1}(b^{\prime}_{1})
and
v_{2}(b_{2})=v_{2}(b^{\prime}_{2})
. This is a contradiction, as it implies that the set
\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\}
is finite, which contradicts our earlier choice of
i\in I
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}
.
∎

The next claim is a strengthening of Claim
1
.

Claim 2
.

Suppose
P(x,y)=f(u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(y))
and
Q(x,y)=g(u_{2}(x)v_{2}(y))
where
f,g,u_{1},v_{1},u_{2},v_{2}
are univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K
,
and

S=\{b\in K:v_{1}(b)\neq 0,v_{2}(b)\neq 0\}.

Then
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is scattered.

Proof of Claim
2
.

Towards a contradiction, we assume that
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered.
Note that
f,g,u_{1},v_{1},u_{2},v_{2}
are nonconstant as
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
have nontrivial dependence on
x
and
y
.
In particular, this allows us to obtain a finite
\mathbb{R}
-definable family
(U_{i})_{i\in I}
of Euclidean open subsets of
K
where
K\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
is finite such that
f
and
g
are injective on
U_{i}
for each
i\in I
. Let
\mathscr{D}_{P,Q}(S)=(D_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
be the
K
-definable family with

D_{b_{1},b_{2}}=\{(u_{1}(a)+v_{1}(b_{1}),u_{2}(a)v_{2}(b_{2})):a\in K\}.

The family
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)=(C_{b_{1},b_{2}})_{(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}}
satisfies
C_{b_{1},b_{2}}=(f\times g)D_{b_{1},b_{2}}
.
For
(i_{1},i_{2})\in I^{2}
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in K^{2}
, set

D^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}=D_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap(U_{i_{1}}\times U_{i_{2}})\quad\text{and}\quad C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}=(f\times g)D^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}.

As
K\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
is finite and
u_{1}(x)+v_{1}(b_{1})
and
u_{2}(x)v_{2}(b_{2})
are nonconstant polynomials for all
(b_{1},b_{2})
is in
S^{2}
, we get
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(D_{b_{1},b_{2}}\setminus\bigcup_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in I^{2}}D^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}})<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
for all
(b_{1},b_{2})
in
S^{2}
. It follows that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(C_{b_{1},b_{2}}\setminus\bigcup_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in I^{2}}C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\Big)<\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\quad\text{for all }(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}.

As
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, we can apply Lemma
4.6
to get
(i_{1},i_{2})\in I^{2}
and
(b_{1},b_{2})\in S^{2}
such that

\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\big\{(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})\in S^{2}:\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)\big\}\geq\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K).

It is easy to see that
f\times g
is an injective and definable map on
U_{i_{1}}\times U_{i_{2}}
. By Fact
2.2
(iii),
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(D^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap D^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
whenever
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b_{1},b_{2}}\cap C^{i_{1},i_{2}}_{b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2}})=\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(K)
for
(b_{1},b_{2})
and
(b^{\prime}_{1},b^{\prime}_{2})
in
S^{2}
. Hence,
\mathscr{D}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered, contradicting Claim 1.
∎

Note that Claim
2
in particular implies that the two possibilities in the proposition are mutually exclusive. It remains to show under the assumption of the theorem that
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
must form an additive pair when they are individually additive, and
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
must form a multiplicative pair when they are individually multiplicative.

Suppose
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are individually multiplicative. Assume that

P(x,y)=f(u_{1}(x)v_{1}(y))\quad\text{and}\quad Q(x,y)=g(u_{2}(x)v_{2}(y)).

where
f,g,u_{1},v_{1},u_{2},v_{2}
are nonconstant univariate polynomials. If
u^{m}_{1}(x)=eu^{n}_{2}(x)
and
v^{m^{\prime}}_{1}(y)=e^{\prime}v^{n^{\prime}}_{2}(y)
for
m,n,m^{\prime},n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
and
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
, then
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
forms a multiplicative pair. So we are left with the case where

u^{m}_{1}(x)\neq eu^{n}_{2}(x)\quad\text{for all }m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}\text{ and }e\in\mathbb{C}.

We will in fact show that this cannot happen.
If
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u^{\prime}_{2}u_{1}}=c
with
c\in\mathbb{C}
, then by considering behavior when
|x|
is large, we see that
c
is a rational number
m/\penalty n
and
u_{1}^{m}(x)=eu_{2}^{n}(x)
and
e\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence,
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u^{\prime}_{2}u_{1}}
is not a constant function. We then deduce the contradiction
using the same argument in the proof of Claim
1
and Claim 2 substituting the role of
\frac{u_{1}^{\prime}u_{2}}{u^{\prime}_{2}}(x)
with that of
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}u_{2}}{u^{\prime}_{2}u_{1}}
.

Similarly, we can rule out the case where
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are each additive but do not form an additive pair using
\frac{u^{\prime}_{1}}{u^{\prime}_{2}}
in the place of
\frac{u_{1}^{\prime}u_{2}}{u^{\prime}_{2}}(x)
. We are left with the desired possibilities that
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
either form an additive pair or a multiplicative pair. Finally, note that with
S
chosen in Claim 1 and Claim 2, we have
|K\setminus S|<\deg P+\deg Q+1
, so the same conclusion can be reached under the weaker assumption of the second statement.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
N=\deg P+\deg Q+1
as in Proposition
4.7
, and set
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q,N)
as in Corollary
4.4
. So
\alpha
depends only on
\deg P
and
\deg Q
, and we can write
\alpha=\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)
.
Suppose
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
do not form a
(5/4,\alpha)
-expanding pair.
Using Corollary
4.2
, we can assume that
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are each either additive or multiplicative over
K
.
By Corollary
4.4
, the family
\mathscr{C}_{P,Q}(S)
is not scattered for all cofinite
S\subseteq K
with
|K\setminus S|<N
. Applying Proposition
4.7
yields that at least one of the three cases in the statement of the theorem holds, and further the additive and multiplicative cases are mutually exclusive. The conclusion then follows from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

The same strategy with obvious modifications will also allow us to prove analogues of Proposition
4.7
in more general settings where
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are replaced by definable binary functions in o-minimal expansions of
\mathbb{R}
or binary analytic functions restricted to bounded open sets of
\mathbb{C}
. Functions of the latter type can be interpreted in an o-minimal expansion of
\mathbb{R}
, and we expect them to be useful when we consider generalized sum-product phenomena for complex analytic functions, but the corresponding
A
and
B
are finite subsets of a fixed bounded open subset of
\mathbb{C}
.

As mentioned in the introduction, the analogue of the main theorem should hold in much more general settings. The missing ingredients include appropriate analogue of Szemerédi–Trotter Theorem (and Corollary
4.4
) and of the Elekes–Rónyai Theorem in these settings. For o-minimal expansion of
\mathbb{R}
, the analogue of the Szemerédi–Trotter Theorem is known
[
1
]
. There are not yet strict counterparts of Elekes–Rónyai results for many o-minimal expansion of
\mathbb{R}
, but the results in
[
7
]
are along this direction.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

As mentioned earlier in the introduction, a qualitative version of Theorem
1.2
without exponent in (i) and without precise form of
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
in (ii) and (iii) can be deduced from a result by Bays and Breuillard
[
2
, Theorem 1.4]
. They show the following: For a given algebraic variety
V\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
of dimension
d
, if for every
\varepsilon
, there is arbitrarily large
n
and a grid
A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{k}
with
|A_{1}|=\ldots=|A_{k}|=n
and

|V\cap(A_{1}\times\ldots\times A_{k})|>n^{d-\varepsilon},

then
(\mathbb{C}^{k},V)
is in a “coordinate-wise correspondence” to a pair
(G,H)
where the
k
-dimensional group
G
is a product
G_{1}^{k_{1}}\times\ldots\times G_{l}^{k_{l}}
of power of
1
-dimensional algebraic group over the complex numbers, and the
d
-dimensional group
H
is
H_{1}\times\ldots\times H_{l}
with
H_{i}
a connected algebraic subgroup of
G_{i}^{k_{i}}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,l\}
.

For given
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
, set

V=\{(a,b,c,d)\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{4}:c=P(a,b),d=Q(a,b)\}.

Then
V
has dimension
2
.
Suppose
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
do not form a
(1+\varepsilon,\alpha)
expanding pair for any
\varepsilon,\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{>0}
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose arbitrarily large
n
,
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{C}
with
|A|=|B|=n
and
\max{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|}<n^{1+\varepsilon}
. By the Pigeonhole principle, we can choose
C\subseteq P(A,B)
, and
D\subseteq Q(A,B)
with
|A|=|B|=|C|=|D|=n
such that

|V\cap(A\times B\times C\times D)|>n^{2-2\varepsilon}.

Hence, applying the above result and unpacking the notion of “coordinate-wise correspondence”, we can show that
(\mathbb{C}^{4},V)
is in a coordinatewise correspondence with
(G^{4},H)
where
G
is a 1-dimensional abelian algebraic group over the complex numbers, and
H
a connected subgroup of
G^{4}
. By standard arguments, one can show that there is open
U\subseteq\mathbb{C}
such that on
U

P(x,y)=f(u_{1}(x)+_{G}v_{1}(y))\text{ and }Q(x,y)=g(u_{2}(x)+_{G}v_{2}(y))

with
u_{1},v_{1},u_{2},v_{2}:U\to G
,
f:(u_{1}(U)+_{G}v_{1}(U))\to\mathbb{C}
, and
g:(u_{2}(U)+_{G}v_{2}(U))\to\mathbb{C}
are analytic maps. One can imagine using the assumption that
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
are polynomials to show that
G
must either be the additive or multiplicative groups, and
u_{1}
,
v_{1}
,
u_{2}
,
v_{2}
,
f
,
g
must be polynomials of the form in Theorem
1.1
. We do not pursue this further in the current paper.
∎

5
Proofs of Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
, and other results

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose
K
has
\fchar K=0
,
f
,
\hat{f}
,
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
,
\hat{v}
are nonconstant univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K
such that
f
and
\hat{f}
are monic,
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
,
\hat{v}
each have constant coefficient equal to
0
, and

f(u(x)+v(y))=\hat{f}(\hat{u}(x)+\hat{v}(y))\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in K.

Then we must have
f=\hat{f}
,
u=\hat{u}
and
v=\hat{v}
.

Proof.

Note that as
\fchar K=0
, the polynomials
u^{\prime}
,
v^{\prime}
,
\hat{u}^{\prime}
, and
\hat{v}^{\prime}
are nonzero.
Taking partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
and manipulating the equations, we get

\frac{u^{\prime}(x)}{\hat{u}^{\prime}(x)}=\frac{v^{\prime}(y)}{\hat{v}^{\prime}(y)}.

Hence, both sides of the equation must be equal to a constant
c\in K
. Hence,
u(x)=c\hat{u}(x)+d
and
v(y)=c\hat{v}(y)+e
. By the assumptions on
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
, and
\hat{v}
we see that
d=e=0
. Further,
c=1
since
f
and
\hat{f}
are monic. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

The following fact consists of instances of the so-called model-theoretic transfer principle. It is a consequence of the compactness theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.1.4]
, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem
[
20
, Theorem 2.3.7]
, and the familiar algebraic fact that every countable field of characteristic
0
is isomorphic to a subfield of
\mathbb{C}
.

Fact 5.2
.

Suppose
\sigma
is a first-order statement in the language of fields. If
\sigma
holds for
\mathbb{C}
, then
\sigma
holds for all algebraically closed fields with characteristic
0
. If
\sigma
holds for all subfields of
\mathbb{C}
, then
\sigma
holds for all fields with characteristic
0
. If
\sigma
holds for all fields with characteristic
0
, then there is
N=N(\sigma)
such that
\sigma
holds for all fields with characteristic at least
N
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
K
has
\fchar K=0
,
f
,
\hat{f}
,
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
,
\hat{v}
are nonconstant univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K
such that
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
,
\hat{v}
are monic, neither
u(x)v(y)
nor
\hat{u}(x)\hat{v}(y)
can be written as
\tilde{u}^{n}(x)\tilde{v}^{n}(y)
where
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
are univariate polynomials over
K
, and
n\geq 2
. If

f(u(x)v(y))=\hat{f}(\hat{u}(x)\hat{v}(y)),

then we must have
f=\hat{f}
,
u=\hat{u}
and
v=\hat{v}
.

Proof.

By Fact
5.2
, we can reduce to the case where
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}
. Taking partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
, and manipulating the equations we get

\frac{u^{\prime}(x)\hat{u}(x)}{\hat{u}^{\prime}(x)u(x)}=\frac{v^{\prime}(y)\hat{v}(y)}{\hat{v}^{\prime}(y)v(y)}.

Hence, both sides of the equation must be equal to a constant
c\in\mathbb{C}
. Letting
|x|
go to infinity, we get
c=\deg{u}/\penalty\deg\hat{u}=m/\penalty n
with
m/\penalty n
a rational number in lowest terms. So
nu^{\prime}(x)/\penalty u(x)=m\hat{u}^{\prime}(x)/\hat{u}(x)
. Integrating and taking exponential, we get
u^{n}=d\hat{u}^{m}
with
d\in\mathbb{C}
. By the assumption that
u
and
\hat{u}
are monic,
d=1
and
u^{n}=\hat{u}^{m}
. As
K[x]
is a unique factorization domain, we obtain a univariate polynomial
\tilde{u}
with coefficients in
K
such that
u(x)=\tilde{u}^{m}(x)
and
\hat{u}(x)=\tilde{u}^{n}(x)
. Likewise, we get a univariate polynomial
\tilde{v}
with coefficients in
K
such that
v(y)=\tilde{v}^{m}(y)
and
\hat{v}(y)=\tilde{v}^{n}(y)
. Then
u(x)v(y)=\tilde{u}^{m}(x)\tilde{v}^{m}(y)
and
\hat{u}(x)\hat{v}(y)=\tilde{u}^{n}(x)\tilde{v}^{n}(y)
.
By the assumption on
u
,
\hat{u}
,
v
, and
\hat{v}
, we must have
m=n=1
. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

We are now ready to deduce Theorems
1.3
and
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Suppose we are not in case (i) of Theorem
1.3
. Then, applying Theorem
1.2
on the pair of polynomials
(P(x,y),Q(x,y))
, we find ourselves in case (ii) or (iii) of Theorem
1.2
. Suppose we are in case (ii) of Theorem
1.2
. Then we get

P(x,y)=f(\gamma_{1}u(x)+\delta_{1}v(y))\quad\text{and}\quad Q(x,y)=g(\gamma_{2}u(x)+\delta_{2}v(y)),

where we can further assume that
f
and
g
are monic and
u
and
v
have constant coefficient 0.
Applying Theorem
1.2
again, now on the pair
(\hat{P}(x,y),Q(x,y))
with
\hat{P}(x,y)=P(y,x)
, we end up in case (ii) of Theorem
1.2
again due to mutual exclusivity of the cases of Theorem
1.2
. Combined with an application of Lemma
5.1
on
Q(x,y)
, we get

\hat{P}(x,y)=\hat{f}(\hat{\gamma}_{1}u(x)+\hat{\delta}_{1}v(y))\quad\text{and}\quad Q(x,y)=g(\gamma_{2}u(x)+\delta_{2}v(y)),

where
\hat{f}
is also monic. Hence
{P}(x,y)=\hat{f}(\hat{\delta}_{1}v(x)+\hat{\gamma}_{1}u(y))
.
Now we apply Lemma
5.1
to
P(x,y)
to conclude the proof for this special case.
We treat the situation where we are in case (iii) of Theorem
1.2
similarly, replacing the role of Lemma
5.1
by that of Lemma
5.3
. The exactness part of Theorem
1.3
follows easily from exactness part of Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

It suffices to prove the last assertion as the earlier part is a special case of Theorem
1.3
. The forward direction is immediate, and the backward direction follows from Proposition
3.2
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Suppose
K
is a field with
\fchar K=0
. We obtain weakened analogues of Theorem
1.2
, Theorem
1.3
, and Theorem
1.1
, with “exactly” replaced by “at least” in the respective statements. Moreover, when
K
is algebraically closed or real closed, then the full analogues of these results hold.

Proof.

We will only prove the proposition for the analogue of Theorem
1.2
; the deduction of the analogues of the other two statement from this is similar to what we have done earlier in this section. For a given pair
(d_{1},d_{2})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
, Theorem
1.2
implies the following when
K
is
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
: For all tuples
(c_{1,k,l})_{0\leq k+l\leq d_{1}}
and
(c_{2,k,l})_{0\leq k+l\leq d_{2}}
of elements in
K
, and all subsets
A
and
B
of
K
with
|A|=|B|=n
, with
P(x,y)=\sum_{0\leq k+l\leq d_{1}}c_{1,k,l}x^{k}y^{l}
and
Q(x,y)=\sum_{0\leq k+l\leq d_{2}}c_{2,k,l}x^{k}y^{l}

the inequality

\max\{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|\}\leq\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)n^{5/4}

implies that
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
form either an additive pair or a multiplicative pair. For each such
(d_{1},d_{2},n)
, the preceding statement admits a first-order expression in the language of fields. Hence, it also holds in all algebraically closed fields of char
0
and real closed fields, as the respective theories are complete. Therefore, in all algebraically closed fields of char
0
and real closed fields, the negation of (i) implies (ii) or (iii) in the corresponding analogues of Theorem
1.2
. The “exactness” part for algebraically closed and real closed fields are simpler and can be achieved similarly.

Now fix a field
K
with
\fchar K=0
, and suppose there is
n\in\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
, and finite subsets
A
and
B
of
K
with
|A|=|B|=n
such that

\max\{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|\}\geq\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)n^{5/4}.

Let
K^{\text{a}}
be the algebraic closure of
K
. Viewing
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
as elements of
K^{\text{a}}[x,y]
and applying the analogue of
Theorem
1.2
, we get that either

P=f(\gamma_{1}u(x)+\delta_{1}v(y))\quad\text{ and }\quad Q=g(\gamma_{2}u(x)+\delta_{2}v(y))

or

P=f(u^{m_{1}}(x)v^{n_{1}}(y))\quad\text{ and }\quad Q=g(u^{m_{2}}(x)v^{n_{2}}(y)),

where
f
,
g
,
u
, and
v
are univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K^{\text{a}}
,
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\delta_{1}
, and
\delta_{2}
are in
K^{\text{a}}
, and
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
,
n_{1}
, and
n_{2}
are in
\mathbb{N}^{\geq 1}
. In the additive case, we can moreover arrange that
f
and
g
are monic, and
u
and
v
are monic and have zero constant coefficients; in the multiplicative case, we can arrange that
u
and
v
are monic,
\gcd(m_{1},n_{1})=\gcd(m_{2},n_{2})=1
and
u(x)v(y)
can not be written
\tilde{u}^{n}(x)\tilde{v}^{n}(y)
where
\tilde{u}
and
\tilde{v}
are univariate polynomials over
K
, and
n\geq 2
. We will show that then
f
,
g
,
u
, and
v
have coefficients in
K
, and
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\delta_{1}
,
\delta_{2}
are in
K
.

Let
G=\mathrm{Aut}(K^{\text{a}}/K)
be the absolute Galois group of
K
. It suffices to show that the natural actions of
G
on
K^{\text{a}}
and the ring of univariate polynomials with coefficients in
K^{\text{a}}
fix
f
,
g
,
u
,
v
,
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\delta_{1}
, and
\delta_{2}
. We treat
P(x,y)
in the additive case. Let
\sigma
be in
G
. As
P(x,y)
is in
K[x,y]
, it is fixed by
\sigma
. Hence

f(\gamma_{1}u(x)+\delta_{1}v(y))=f^{\sigma}(\gamma_{1}^{\sigma}u^{\sigma}(x)+\delta_{1}^{\sigma}v^{\sigma}(y))

where we use exponential notation for group actions. Applying Lemma
5.1
in
K^{\text{a}}
, we get
f=f^{\sigma}
,
u=u^{\sigma}
,
v=v^{\sigma}
,
\gamma_{1}=\gamma^{\sigma}_{1}
, and
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}^{\sigma}
. We can deal with
Q(x,y)
identically. The multiplicative case is similar, but using Lemma
5.3
instead of Lemma
5.1
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

There is evidence that the full analogues of Theorem
1.2
, Theorem
1.3
, and Theorem
1.1
do not hold in all fields of characteristic
0
. For example,
x^{2}+y^{2}
is not 1-expanding over
\mathbb{C}
, but
1
-expanding over
\mathbb{Q}
due to a result by Chang
[
5
]
.

By the model-theoretic transfer principle (Fact
5.2
), for each
n
there is
N
such that if
A,B\subseteq K
with
|A|=|B|=n
,
K
has positive characteristic at least
N
, and

\max\{|P(A,B)|,|Q(A,B)|\}\leq\alpha(\deg P,\deg Q)n^{5/4},

then
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
must form either an additive or a multiplicative pair. In more details, for the given
n
, we can obtain a first order statement
\sigma
indicating that one the three possibilities in Proposition
5.4
must hold for
n
. Then the statement
\sigma
holds in all fields of characteristic
0
, so
N
can be chosen to be
N(\sigma)
as in Fact
5.2
.

In the above discussion for fields with positive characteristic,
n
is generally very small compared to size of the field. There are more involved results due to Tao along similar lines when
|A|=|B|=n
is close to the size of the field
[
33
]
. That suggests that similar results about arbitrary pairs of polynomials should also be true for intermediate values of
n
in finite fields, but no proof is currently known.
∎
