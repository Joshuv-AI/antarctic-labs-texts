A note on the maximum number of k k -powers in a finite word
Shuo Li, Jakub Pachocki ,, Jakub Radoszewski ,

Abstract
A power is a word of the form u ​ u ​ … ​ u ⏟ k ​ times \underbrace{uu...u}_{k\;\text{times}} , where u u is a word and k k is a positive integer; the power is also called a k k -power and k k is its exponent . We prove that for any k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , the maximum number of different non-empty k k -power factors in a word of length n n is between n k − 1 − Θ ⁡ ( n ) \frac{n}{k-1}-\Theta(\sqrt{n}) and n − 1 k − 1 \frac{n-1}{k-1} . We also show that the maximum number of different non-empty power factors of exponent at least 2 in a length- n n word is at most n − 1 n-1 . Both upper bounds generalize the recent upper bound of n − 1 n-1 on the maximum number of different square factors in a length- n n word by Brlek and Li (2022).

Introduction

Let
k
be an integer greater than
1
, the
k
-power
(or simply the
power
) of a word
u
is a word of the form
\underbrace{uu...u}_{k\;\text{times}}
. Here
k
is called the
exponent
of the power. We consider only powers of non-empty words. A factor (subword) of a word is its fragment consisting of a number of consecutive letters. In this paper, we investigate the bounds for the maximum number of different
k
-power factors in a word of length
n
. This subject is one of the
fundamental topics in combinatorics on words
[
10
]
. For any pair of positive integers
(n,k)
with
k>1
, let
N(n,k)
denote the maximum number of different non-empty
k
-powers that can appear as factors of a word of length
n
. For 2-powers (squares), the bounds for
N(n,2)
were studied by many authors; see
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
2
,
11
,
1
]
. The best known lower bound from
[
4
]
and a very recent upper bound from
[
1
]
match up to sublinear terms:

n-o(n)\leq N(n,2)\leq n-1.

Actually, one can check that the lower bound from
[
4
]
is of the form
n-\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. For
k=3
, it was proved in
[
6
]
that

\frac{1}{2}n-2\sqrt{n}\leq N(n,3)\leq\frac{4}{5}n.

More generally, for
k\geq 3
, it was studied in
[
8
]
and proved that

N(n,k)\leq\frac{n-1}{k-2},

with the same notation as above.
Further in
[
6
]
it was shown that the maximum number of different factors of a word of length
n
being powers of exponent
at least
3 is
n-2
.

In this article, we generalize the methods provided in
[
1
]
and
[
6
]
to give an upper and a lower bound for the number of different
k
-powers in a finite word. The main result is announced as follows:

Theorem 1

Let
k
be an integer greater than 1. For any integer
n>1
, let
N(n,k)
denote the maximum number of different
k
-powers being factors of a word of length
n
. Then we have

\frac{n}{k-1}-\Theta(\sqrt{n})\leq N(n,k)\leq\frac{n-1}{k-1}.

We also show the following result. It implies, in particular, that a word that contains powers of exponent greater than 2 has fewer squares than
n-1
.

Theorem 2

The maximum number of different factors in a word of length
n
being powers of exponent at least 2 is
n-1
.

Preliminaries

Let us first recall the basic terminology related to words. By a
word
we mean a finite concatenation of symbols
w=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}
, with
n
being a non-negative integer. The
length
of
w
, denoted
|w|
, is
n
and we say that the symbol
w_{i}
is at the
position

i
. The set
\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)=\left\{w_{i}|1\leq i\leq n\right\}
is called the
alphabet
of
w
and its elements are called
letters
. Let
|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|
denote the cardinality of
\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)
. A word of length
0
is called the
empty word
and it is denoted by
\varepsilon
.
Concatenation
of two words
u
,
v
is denoted as
uv
.

A word
u
is called a
factor
of a word
w
if
w=pus
for some words
p
,
s
;
u
is called a
prefix
(
suffix
) of
w
if
p=\varepsilon
(
s=\varepsilon
, respectively). The set of all factors of a word
w
is denoted by
\mathrm{Fac}(w)
.

Two words
u
and
v

conjugate
when there exist words
x,y
such that
u=xy
and
v=yx
.
The conjugacy class of a word
v
is denoted by
[v]
. If
v=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{m}
is word, then for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, we define
v_{p}(i)=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{i}
and
v_{s}(i)=v_{i+1}v_{i+2}\cdots v_{m}
.
Thus,
[v]=\left\{v_{s}(i)v_{p}(i),i=1,2,\dots,m\right\}
.

For any positive integer
k
, we define the
k
-power
(or simply a
power
) of a word
u
to be the concatenation of
k
copies of
u
, denoted by
u^{k}
. Here
k
is the
exponent
of the power. A word
w
is said to be
primitive
if it is not a power of another word, that is, if
w=u^{k}
implies
k=1
. For any word
w
, there is exactly one primitive word
u
such that
w=u^{k}
for integer
k\geq 1
; the word
u
is called the
primitive root
of word
w
, see
[
9
]
. Furthermore, two words that conjugate are either both primitive or none of them is (
[
9
]
). For a given word
w
, let
N_{k}(w)
denote the number of different non-empty
k
-power factors of
w
and
\mathrm{Prim}(w)
denote all primitive factors of
w
.

For any word
u
and any rational number
\alpha
, the
\alpha
-power
of
u
is defined to be
u^{a}u_{0}
where
u_{0}
is a prefix of
u
,
a
is the integer part of
\alpha
, and
|u^{a}u_{0}|=\alpha|u|
. The
\alpha
-power of
u
is denoted by
u^{\alpha}
. If
\alpha
is a rational number greater than 1 and there exists a word
u
such that
w=u^{\alpha}
, then the word
w
is said to have a
period

|u|
.

Lemma 3
(Fine and Wilf
[
3
]
)

Let
w
be a word having
p
and
q
for periods. If
|w|\geq p+q-\gcd(p,q)
, then
\gcd(p,q)
is also a period of
w
.

3
Rauzy graphs of a finite word

In this section, we recall the notion of Rauzy graph and some results obtained in
[
1
]
. Let
w
be a word of length
n
. For any integer
l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
L_{l}(w)
be the set of all length-
l
factors of
w
. For any integer
l\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let the Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{l}(w)
be an oriented graph whose set of vertices is
L_{l}(w)
and the set of edges is
L_{l+1}(w)
(here
L_{n+1}(w)=\emptyset
);
an edge
e\in L_{l+1}(w)
starts at the vertex
u
and ends at the vertex
v
, if
u
is a
prefix and
v
is a suffix of
e
. Let us define
\Gamma(w)=\cup_{l=1}^{n}\Gamma_{l}(w)
.

Let
\Gamma_{l}(w)
be a Rauzy graph of
w
. A sub-graph in
\Gamma_{l}(w)
is called an
elementary circuit
if there are
j
distinct vertices
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{j}
and
j
distinct edges
e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{j}
for some integer
j
, such that for each
t
with
1\leq t\leq j-1
, the edge
e_{t}
starts at
v_{t}
and ends at
v_{t+1}
, and for the edge
e_{j}
, it starts at
v_{j}
and ends at
v_{1}
; further,
j
is called the
size
of the circuit. The
small circuits
in the graph
\Gamma_{l}(w)
are those elementary circuits whose sizes are no larger than
l
.

Lemma and Notation 4
(Brlek and Li
[
1
]
)

Let
w
be a word and let
\Gamma_{l}(w)
be a Rauzy graph of
w
for some
l\in\{1,\ldots,|w|\}
. Then for any small circuit
C
on
\Gamma_{l}(w)
, there exists a unique primitive word
q
, up to conjugacy, such that
|q|\leq l
and the vertex set of
C
is
\left\{p^{\frac{l}{|p|}}|p\in[q]\right\}
and its edge set is
\left\{p^{\frac{l+1}{|p|}}|p\in[q]\right\}
.

Further, each small circuit can be identified by an associated primitive word
q
and an integer
l
such that
\Gamma_{l}(w)
is the Rauzy graph in which the circuit is located.
Let each small circuit be denoted by
C(q,r)
with the parameters defined as above.

Lemma 5
(Brlek and Li
[
1
]
)

Let
w
be a word. Then there are at most
|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|
small circuits in
\Gamma(w)
.

4
Upper bound for
N(n,k)

Let
w
be a word and let
v\in\mathrm{Prim}(w)
. A factor
u\in\mathrm{Fac}(w)
is said to be
in the class of factor
v
if there is a (primitive) word
y\in[v]
and an integer
p\geq 2
such that
u=y^{p}
. Let
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
denote the set of all factors in the class of
v
. By
|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|
we denote the cardinality of
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
.

For a factor
v
of
w
, let us define
m_{w}(v)=\max\left\{n|v^{n}\in\mathrm{Fac}(w),n\in\mathbb{N^{+}}\right\}
. Now given
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
, let us define its
index
to be an integer
\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)
such that
\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)=\max\left\{m_{w}(u)|u\in[v]\right\}
.
From the definition, the elements in
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
are all of the form
v_{s}(i)v^{j-1}v_{p}(i)
with
1\leq i\leq|v|
and
1\leq j\leq\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)
.
By
\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)
we denote the set of primitive words
v
such that
v^{n}
is in the class of
v
, where
n
is the index of this class. In other words,

\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)=\{v\in\mathrm{Prim}(w)|v^{\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)}\in\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)\}.

For
v\in\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)
, let
\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)=\left\{u^{\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)}|u\in[v]\right\}\cap\mathrm{Fac}(w)
and
\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)
denote the cardinality of
\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)
.

Let
v=\mathtt{00001}
and let us consider the following class of size 8 for some unspecified word
w
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)=\{

\displaystyle(\mathtt{00001})^{2},(\mathtt{00010})^{2},(\mathtt{00100})^{2},(\mathtt{01000})^{2},(\mathtt{10000})^{2},

\displaystyle(\mathtt{00001})^{3},(\mathtt{00100})^{3},(\mathtt{01000})^{3}\}.

In this case,
\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)=3
,
\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)=\{(\mathtt{00001})^{3},(\mathtt{00100})^{3},(\mathtt{01000})^{3}\}
, and
\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)=3
.
Moreover, the only words that conjugate with
v
in
\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)
are
\mathtt{00001},\mathtt{00100},\mathtt{01000}
.

Lemma 6

Let
u
and
v
be primitive words. If words
u
and
v
conjugate, then
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(u)=\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
. Otherwise, classes
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(u)
and
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
are disjoint.

Proof

The first part of the statement is obvious. Assume to the contrary that
y\in\mathrm{Class}_{w}(u)\cap\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
for primitive words
u
and
v
. This means that there exist words
u^{\prime}\in[u]
and
v^{\prime}\in[v]
and integers
k,t>1
such that
y=(u^{\prime})^{k}=(v^{\prime})^{t}
. Word
y
has periods
|u^{\prime}|
and
|v^{\prime}|
, so by Lemma
3
it has period
p=\gcd(|u^{\prime}|,|v^{\prime}|)
. If
p<|u^{\prime}|
(
p<|v^{\prime}|
, respectively), then
p
would divide
|u^{\prime}|
(
|v^{\prime}|
, respectively); consequently,
u^{\prime}
(respectively
v^{\prime}
) would not be primitive. Hence,
p=|u^{\prime}|=|v^{\prime}|
, so
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
and
u
and
v
conjugate.
∎

In this section we give an upper bound for
N_{k}(w)
. The strategy is as follows: first, we compute the exact number of powers in each class
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
of
w
; second, we prove that there exists an injection from
\cup_{v\in\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)}\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
to the set of small circuits in
\Gamma(w)
; third, we conclude by using the proprieties of Rauzy graphs introduced in the previous section.

Lemma 7

Let
w
be a word and
v\in\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)
. If
\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)\geq 2
, then we have
|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|=|v|(\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)-2)+\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)
. Further, we have
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)=\left\{u^{k}|u\in[v],2\leq k\leq\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)-1\right\}\cup\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)
.

Proof

We only need to prove that for any
u\in[v]
and any integer
k
satisfying
2\leq k\leq\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)-1
,
u^{k}\in\mathrm{Fac}(w)
. We can easily check that
u^{k}\in\mathrm{Fac}(v^{k+1})
. However, from the hypothesis,
k+1\leq\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)
and
v^{\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)}\in\mathrm{Fac}(w)
, thus,
v^{k+1}\in\mathrm{Fac}(w)
. Consequently,
u^{k}\in\mathrm{Fac}(w)
. ∎

2
below shows the main ideas from the proof of the following lemma for a concrete class.

Lemma 8

Let
w
be a word and
v\in\mathrm{Prim}^{\prime}(w)
such that
|v|=l
and
\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)\geq 2
. For any integer
t
satisfying
1\leq t\leq|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|
, there exists a small circuit
C(v,t+l-1)
in the Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{t+l-1}(w)
. Hence, there exists a bijective function
f_{v}
which associates each word in
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
to a small circuit in the set
\left\{C(v,t+l-1)|1\leq t\leq|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|\right\}
.

Proof

To prove the existence of the circuit
C(v,t+l-1)
for any integer
t\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|\}
, it is enough to prove that

S_{t}:=\left\{u^{\frac{t+l}{l}}|u\in[v]\right\}\subset\mathrm{Fac}(w).

If this is the case, then there exists a circuit in
\Gamma_{t+l-1}(w)
such that its edge set is
S_{t}
; further, it can be identified by
C(v,t+l-1)
.

If integers
t^{\prime},t
satisfy
1\leq t^{\prime}<t\leq|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|
, then each word in
S_{t^{\prime}}
is a prefix of a word in
S_{t}
. Hence, it is enough to prove that
S_{t}\subset\mathrm{Fac}(w)
for
t=|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|
.
From Lemma
7
, we have
t=l(\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)-2)+\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)
, so
\frac{t+l}{l}=\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)-1+\frac{\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)}{l}
.

Let
i=\mathrm{Index}_{w}(v)
and
j=\mathrm{mp}_{w}(v)
.
For any
u^{i-1+\frac{j}{l}}\in S_{t}
, the word
u^{i-1+\frac{j}{l}}=u^{i-1}u_{p}(j)
is a factor of the word
u_{s}(m)u^{i-1}u_{p}(m)=(u_{s}(m)u_{p}(m))^{i}
for all
m\in\{j,\ldots,l\}
.
Hence, there are at most
j-1
distinct words
y
that conjugate with
u
such that
u^{i-1+\frac{j}{l}}\not\in\mathrm{Fac}(y^{i})
.
In particular, there are at most
j-1
distinct words
y^{i}\in\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)
which do not contain
u^{i-1+\frac{j}{l}}
as a factor.
However, there are exactly
j
elements in
\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)
, so there exists at least one word in
\mathrm{MaxPow}_{w}(v)
containing
u^{i-1+\frac{j}{l}}
.
Thus,
S_{t}\subset\mathrm{Fac}(w)
.

The existence of the bijective function
f_{v}
is from the fact that the cardinalities of
\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)
and
\left\{C(v,t+|v|-1)|1\leq t\leq|\mathrm{Class}_{w}(v)|\right\}
are the same.∎

Each power factor of

Theorem 10
(Upper Bound)

Let
k
be an integer greater than 1. For any word
w
, we have

N_{k}(w)\leq\frac{|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|}{k-1}.

Consequently, for any integer
n\geq 1
, we have

N(n,k)\leq\frac{n-1}{k-1}.

Proof

To each
k
-power factor in
\mathrm{Powers}(w)
we can assign at least
k-2
powers in the set
\mathrm{Powers}(w)
that are not
k
-powers. More precisely, if the
k
-power factor is
v^{kp}
, for positive integer
p
and primitive word
v
, then the words
v^{kp-1},\dots,v^{kp-k+2}
are elements of
\mathrm{Powers}(w)
and are not
k
-powers by uniqueness of primitive roots. (If
p>1
, we could also assign
v^{kp-k+1}
to
v^{kp}
; however, for
p=1
this would be a 1-power.) Moreover, this way the sets of powers assigned to different
k
-powers are disjoint. By Theorem
2
,

N_{k}(w)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Powers}(w)}{k-1}\leq\frac{|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|}{k-1}.\quad\squareforqed

5
Lower bound for
N(n,k)

We show a family of binary words which yields a lower bound of
\frac{n}{k-1}-\Theta(\sqrt{n})

for the number of different factors which are
k
-powers, for an integer
k\geq 2
.

For integers
i\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
we denote

q^{(k)}_{i}=(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i})^{k-1}.

Let
r^{(k)}_{m}
be the concatenation

r^{(k)}_{m}=q^{(k)}_{1}q^{(k)}_{2}\cdots q^{(k)}_{m}\mathtt{10}^{m}.

E.g., for
k=2
, we obtain the family of words:

\mathtt{1010},\ \mathtt{10100100},\ \mathtt{1010010001000},\ \mathtt{1010010001000010000},\ldots

and for
k=3
, the family:

\mathtt{101010},\ \mathtt{1010100100100},\ \mathtt{1010100100100010001000},\ldots

Lemma 11

The length of
r^{(k)}_{m}
is
(k-1)\left(\frac{m^{2}}{2}+\frac{3m}{2}\right)+m+1
.

Proof

The length of
q^{(k)}_{i}
is
(k-1)(i+1)
, so

|r^{(k)}_{m}|=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(k-1)(i+1)\right)+m+1=(k-1)\left(\frac{m^{2}}{2}+\frac{3m}{2}\right)+m+1.\ \ \squareforqed

Lemma 12

N_{k}(r^{(k)}_{m})\geq\frac{m^{2}}{2}+\frac{m}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor
.

Proof

Let us note that for a positive integer
i
, the concatenation
\mathtt{0}^{i-1}q^{(k)}_{i}\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i}=\mathtt{0}^{i-1}(\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i})^{k}

contains as factors all the
k
-powers that conjugate with the
k
-power
(\mathtt{0}^{i}\mathtt{1})^{k}

that are different from this
k
-power.
Let us note that this concatenation is a factor of
r^{(k)}_{m}
for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
.
Indeed, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, the factor
q^{(k)}_{i}
in
r^{(k)}_{m}
is preceded by
\mathtt{0}^{i-1}
(for
i=1
this is the empty string, and otherwise it is a suffix of
q^{(k)}_{i-1}
)
and followed by
\mathtt{1}\mathtt{0}^{i}
(for
i<m
it is a prefix of
q^{(k)}_{i+1}
, and for
i=m
it is a suffix of
r^{(k)}_{m}
).

Additionally, in
r^{(k)}_{m}
there are
\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor
unary
k
-powers
\mathtt{0}^{k},\mathtt{0}^{2k},\ldots

In total we obtain

Theorem 13
(Lower Bound)

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer.
For infinitely many positive integers
n
there exists a word
w
of length
n

for which
N_{k}(w)>\frac{n}{k-1}-2.2\sqrt{n}
.

Proof

Due to Lemmas
11
and
12
, for any word
r^{(k)}_{m}
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}{k-1}-N_{k}(r^{(k)}_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{2}}{2}+\frac{3m}{2}+\frac{m+1}{k-1}-\frac{m^{2}}{2}-\frac{m}{2}-\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=m+\frac{m+1}{k-1}-\left\lfloor\frac{m}{k}\right\rfloor\leq m+\frac{m+1}{k-1}-\frac{m-k+1}{k}

\displaystyle=m+\frac{m+1}{k(k-1)}+1\leq m+\frac{m+1}{2}+1=\frac{3}{2}m+\frac{3}{2}.

This value is smaller than
c\sqrt{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}
for
c^{2}\geq\frac{9}{2}
; indeed, in this case, we have:

\left(\frac{3}{2}m+\frac{3}{2}\right)^{2}=\frac{9}{4}m^{2}+\frac{9}{2}m+\frac{9}{4}<c^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}m^{2}+\frac{5}{2}m+1\right)\leq c^{2}|r^{(k)}_{m}|.

Hence, for
c\geq 2.2
we conclude that:

\frac{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}{k-1}-N_{k}(r^{(k)}_{m})<c\sqrt{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}\quad\Rightarrow\quad N_{k}(r^{(k)}_{m})>\frac{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}{k-1}-c\sqrt{|r^{(k)}_{m}|}.\ \ \squareforqed
