Null Infinity and Horizons: A New Approach to Fluxes and Charges
Abhay Ashtekar, Simone Speziale

Abstract
We introduce a Hamiltonian framework tailored to degrees of freedom (DOF) of field theories that reside in suitable 3-dimensional open regions, and then apply it to the gravitational DOF of general relativity. Specifically, these DOF now refer to open regions ℛ ^ \hat{\mathcal{R}} of null infinity ℐ + \mathscr{I}^{+} , and R R of black hole (and cosmological) horizons Δ \Delta representing equilibrium situations. At ℐ + \mathscr{I}^{+} the new Hamiltonian framework yields the well-known BMS fluxes and charges [ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] . By contrast, all fluxes vanish identically at Δ \Delta just as one would physically expect [ 5 ] . In the companion paper [ 6 ] we showed that, somewhat surprisingly, the geometry and symmetries of ℐ + \mathscr{I}^{+} and Δ \Delta descend from a common framework. This paper reinforces that theme: Very different physics emerges in the two cases from a common Hamiltonian framework because of the difference in the nature of degrees of freedom on ℐ + \mathscr{I}^{+} and Δ \Delta , discussed in [ 6 ] . Finally, we compare and contrast this Hamiltonian approach with those available in the literature.



I
Introduction

In a companion paper
[
6
]

The common underlying WIH description also allows one to understand the symmetries of

6

[
7

This procedure gives conceptual precedence to fluxes; charges are obtained by a systematic ‘integration’ of these fluxes. We will find that very different physics arises at null infinity
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and black hole horizons
\Delta

[
6

\Delta

II
Phase spaces
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal R}}
associated with finite regions
\mathcal{R}

Our goal is to arrive at fluxes and charges associated with symmetries of
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and
\Delta
using a Hamiltonian framework that is tailored to degrees of freedom that reside in their finite sub-regions
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathcal{R}
. This procedure involves several new elements. Therefore, in this section we will make a detour and illustrate the strategy and main constructions using a simple and familiar example: a scalar field in Minkowski space-time. We will then apply this procedure to
\mathscr{I}^{+}
in section
III
and to
\Delta
in section
IV
.

Recall that there is a well-known covariant phase space framework for classical field theories in globally hyperbolic space-times
[
17
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
7
]
(whose foundations can be traced back all the way to Lagrange!). It provides a natural platform to define Hamiltonians generating canonical transformations associated with symmetries. The covariant phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
consists of suitably regular solutions of field equations. The idea now is to extract from
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
, a phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal R}}
associated with certain 3-dimensional regions
\mathcal{R}
in the space-time under consideration. Intuitively,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal R}}
can be thought of as capturing the degrees of freedom of the field that are registered on
\mathcal{R}
. We will show that the Hamiltonians on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal R}}
have the physical interpretation of fluxes associated with symmetries: they agree with the standard expressions of fluxes obtained from the stress-energy tensor of the field and symmetry vector fields.

This section is divided into two parts. In the first we explain the main ideas using finite regions with Cauchy surfaces in Minkowski space and in the second we extend them to obtain fluxes of BMS momenta across finite regions of
\mathscr{I}^{+}
. This approach uses some basic properties of infinite dimensional linear topological spaces. We will spell out the details since these structures are not commonly used in the physics literature on systems with boundaries. In the second part we discuss phase spaces
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
associated with finite regions
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
of
\mathscr{I}^{+}
.

5. It is clear from the main discussion of this subsection that the norm (

associated with finite regions

to illustrate the strategy we will use at WIHs, the idea of using phase spaces associated with finite 3-d regions has other applications as well since –e.g. at the future space-like infinity of asymptotically de Sitter space-times– since they encode the degrees of freedom just in that region.

II.2
Scalar field at null infinity

To extend these considerations to null infinity, let us now consider a massless scalar field satisfying the wave equation
\Box\,\phi=0
on Minkowski space
(M,g_{ab})
. Let
(\hat{M},\hat{g}_{ab})
be any conformal completion in which
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is divergence-free, i.e., its null normal
\hat{n}^{a}
satisfies
\hat{\nabla}_{a}\hat{n}^{a}\,\hat{=}\,0

\hat{\phi}:=\Omega^{-1}\hat{\phi}
is well-defined at
\mathscr{I}^{+}

\mathscr{I}^{+}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}\!\mid_{\phi}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathscr{I}^{+}}\big((\delta_{1}\hat{\phi})\hat{\nabla}_{a}(\delta_{2}\hat{\phi})\,-\,(\delta_{2}\hat{\phi})\hat{\nabla}_{a}(\delta_{1}\phi)\big)\,\,\hat{\epsilon}^{a}{}_{bcd}\,\,\mathrm{d}\hat{S}^{bcd}\,

(9)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\,\int_{\mathscr{I}^{+}}\hat{\mathfrak{J}}_{bcd}\,\,\mathrm{d}\hat{S}^{bcd}\,.

Let us now consider an open region
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
of
\mathscr{I}^{+}

||\hat{\phi}||^{2}_{{}_{\mathcal{R}}}\,=\,\int_{\mathcal{R}}\big(|\hat{n}^{a}D_{a}\hat{\phi}|^{2}+\hat{q}^{ab}\,\hat{D}_{a}\hat{\phi}\,\hat{D}_{b}\hat{\phi}+\frac{1}{l^{2}}|\hat{\phi}|^{2}\big)\,\mathrm{d}^{3}\!\mathscr{I}^{+}

(10)

where

\hat{q}^{ab}

\hat{q}_{ab}

the natural volume element on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
in the conformal frame

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}\!\!\mid_{{}_{\gamma}}(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})\,=\,\int_{\hat{\mathcal{R}}}\hat{\mathfrak{J}}_{abc}\,\,\mathrm{d}\hat{S}^{bcd}

\hat{\xi}^{a}

(For the supertranslation vector fields

of

on

on a dense subspace of

III.1
Radiative phase spaces at
\mathscr{I}^{+}

To discuss charges and fluxes associated with symmetries, we have to work with the phase space consisting of all space-times that admit
\mathscr{I}^{+}
as a boundary in their conformal completion. Let us then begin with the phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
of all vacuum solutions
(M,g_{ab})
of Einstein’s equations that are asymptotically flat at null infinity (in the sense of Definition 2 of
[
6
]
), and work with their conformal completions
(\hat{M},\,\hat{g}_{ab})
in which
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is divergence-free. Then
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is equipped with a kinematical structure that it is shared by all space-times in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
: Pairs
(\hat{q}_{ab},\,\hat{n}^{a})
where any two are conformally related via
(\hat{q}^{\prime}_{ab}=\mu^{2}\hat{q}_{ab},\,\hat{n}^{\prime\,a}=\mu^{-2}\hat{n}^{a})
with
\mu
satisfying
\mathcal{L}_{\hat{n}}\mu=0
. The role of this kinematic structure is analogous to that played by the Minkowski metric in section
II
.

As discussed in
[
6
]
, the dynamical information in the gravitational field at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is encoded in the intrinsic connection
\hat{D}
, induced by the space-time connection
\hat{\nabla}
.
\hat{D}
is compatible with the kinematic structure –i.e., satisfies
\hat{D}_{a}\hat{q}_{bc}\,\hat{=}\,0
and
\hat{D}_{a}\hat{n}^{b}\,\hat{=}\,0
– but varies from one space-time to another. It captures the radiative information in the physical metric
g_{ab}
that is locally registered at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and is thus analogous to the radiation field
\hat{\phi}
of section
II
. The phase space
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\rm rad}
adapted to
\mathscr{I}^{+}
consists of these degrees of freedom.

More precisely, we have the following structure
[
18
,
19
]
. The curvature of
\hat{D}
on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
encodes the Bondi news
\hat{N}_{ab}
and the part
{}^{\star}\!\hat{K}^{ab}:=\lim_{{\mathscr{I}^{+}}}\,(\Omega^{-1}\,{}^{\star}\hat{C}^{acbd}\,\hat{n}_{c}\hat{n}_{d})
of the asymptotic Weyl curvature of
\hat{g}_{ab}
. The five components of
{}^{\star}\!\hat{K}^{ab}
encode the radiative modes corresponding to the Newman-Penrose components
\Psi_{4}^{\circ},\,\Psi_{3}^{\circ},\,{\rm Im}\Psi_{2}^{\circ}
, all of which vanish in any stationary space-time. Because every
\hat{D}
annihilates both
\hat{q}_{ab}
and
\hat{n}^{a}
, any 2 of our derivative operators
\hat{D}
and
{\hat{D}}{}^{\prime}
on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
are related by

(\hat{D}_{a}-{\hat{D}}{}^{\prime}_{a})\hat{f}_{b}=n^{c}\hat{f}_{c}\,\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}\qquad\hbox{\rm for arbitrary 1-forms $\hat{f}_{b}$ on $\mathscr{I}^{+}$}\,,

(14)

and some symmetric tensor field
\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}
that is transverse to
\hat{n}^{a}
,  i.e., satisfies
\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}\hat{n}^{a}\,\hat{=}\,0
. This property brings out an important fact: the action of all derivative operators is the same on ‘horizontal’ 1-forms
\hat{h}_{a}
satisfying
\hat{h}_{a}\hat{n}^{a}\,\hat{=}\,0
. Therefore,  (as discussed in
[
6
]
)
\hat{D}
is characterized by its action on any one 1-form
\hat{\ell}_{a}
on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
satisfying
\hat{n}^{a}\,\hat{\ell}_{a}=-1
. This fact plays an important role in phase space considerations.

But there is gauge freedom in
\hat{D}
: If we change the conformal factor by
\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}=\mu\,\Omega\equiv(1+\Omega f)\Omega
in a neighborhood of
\mathscr{I}^{+}
, then clearly
\hat{q}_{ab}
and
\hat{n}^{a}
do not change, but
\hat{D}\to\hat{D}^{\prime}
with
\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}\,\hat{=}\,f\hat{q}_{ab}
. Thus the ‘pure-trace part’ of
\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}
is gauge and we are led to consider two connection as equivalent if they differ just by this gauge freedom:

\hat{D}\,,{\hat{D}}{}^{\prime}\,\in\{\hat{D}\}\qquad{\rm iff}\quad(\hat{D}_{a}-{\hat{D}}{}^{\prime}_{a})\hat{f}_{b}\,\propto\,(n^{c}\hat{f}_{c})\hat{q}_{ab}\,.

(15)

Therefore, the difference between any two equivalence classes is characterized by the trace-free part
\hat{\mathfrak{f}}_{ab}:=\hat{\Sigma}_{ab}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{q}^{cd}\hat{\Sigma}_{cd}\,\hat{q}_{ab}
, where
\hat{q}^{cd}
is any inverse of
\hat{q}_{ab}
. Note that this transverse traceless
\hat{\mathfrak{f}}_{ab}
has precisely two components; they represent the two radiative modes of the gravitational field. It is remarkable that they can be isolated locally in a simple way in spite of the non-linearities of full general relativity; this is why the
\mathscr{I}
-framework is so well-adapted to the study of radiation fields. Note however that for this very reason the connections
\{\hat{D}\}
have no knowledge of the Coulombic aspects of the gravitational field that are encoded, e.g., in
{\rm Re}\Psi_{2}^{\circ}
that enters the expression of the Bondi-Sachs 4-momentum and
\Psi_{1}^{\circ}
that enters the expression of the angular momentum charge. In terms of the discussion of section
II
of
[
6
]
, the degrees of freedom captured by
\{\hat{D}\}
are ‘freely specifiable’ fields on 3-dimensional
\mathscr{I}^{+}
. The Coulombic information can be specified on a 2-sphere (say in the distant past), and is then determined everywhere on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
by the radiative modes. It does
not
have ‘dynamical content’ that freely specifiable fields have; it constitutes the ‘corner data’.

The phase space that captures the degrees of freedom in
g_{ab}
that are relevant to
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is thus the space
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\rm rad}
of connections
\{\hat{D}\}
at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
(subject to appropriate regularity conditions specified in
[
2
]
). Therefore it has the structure of an affine space. For intermediate calculations, it is convenient to endow it a vector space-structure by choosing an origin. Fortunately, there is a natural class of connections that can serve as origins:
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}
that have trivial curvature, i.e., for which
{}^{\star}\!\hat{K}^{ab}=0
(which implies that
\hat{N}_{ab}
also vanishes
[
20
]

\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}\in{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\rm rad}
are referred to as

. Each classical vacuum
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}
is left invariant by the translation subgroup
\mathcal{T}
of the BMS group
\mathfrak{B}
, and the quotient
\mathcal{S}/\mathcal{T}
of the supertranslation group by its translation subgroup acts freely and transitively on the space of classical vacua; thus there as ‘as many’ classical vacua
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
as there are supertranslations modulo translations. Let us fix a
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}
and choose it as the origin in
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}_{\rm rad}

\{\hat{D}\}
and
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}
is completely characterized by a transverse, traceless, symmetric tensor field
\hat{\gamma}_{ab}

[
18
]

\hat{\gamma}_{ab}=\big(\{\hat{D}\}_{a}-\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}_{a}\big)\hat{\ell}_{b}={\rm TF}(\hat{D}_{a}-\hat{\mathfrak{D}}_{a})\hat{\ell}_{b}\,.

(16)

Here TF stands for “trace-free part of”,
\hat{D}
is any element of
\{\hat{D}\}
and
\hat{\mathfrak{D}}
of
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}

\hat{\ell}_{b}

\mathscr{I}^{+}
satisfying
\hat{n}^{b}\hat{\ell}_{b}=-1

\hat{\gamma}_{ab}

\hat{\gamma}_{ab}
as

\{\hat{D}\}
and the vacuum
\{\hat{\mathfrak{D}}\}

\hat{\ell}_{a}

\hat{\ell}_{a}

BMS Charges

We presented a self-contained summary of these results that is conceptually more complete in that it brings out the distinction between fluxes and charges, as well as the subtle difference between the nature of charge 2-forms for supermomenta versus angular momenta. Fluxes require only radiative degrees of freedom that are encoded

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}

V
Discussion

The fact that null infinity is a WIH seems very surprising at first because WIHs are commonly associated with black hole (and cosmological) horizons
\Delta
in equilibrium. Indeed,
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and
\Delta
have almost the opposite connotations.
\mathscr{I}^{+}
lies in the asymptotic, weak field region, while
\Delta
lies in a strong curvature region.
\mathscr{I}^{+}
is the arena for discussing gravitational waves, while
\Delta
is generally used to discuss the Coulombic properties of black holes in equilibrium. The companion paper
[
6
]
showed that, in spite of these striking contrasts, they share a number of geometrical properties. In particular, one can systematically arrive at the BMS group
\mathfrak{B}
at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
starting from the symmetry group
\mathfrak{G}
of WIHs. In this paper we continued our exploration of unity that underlies apparent diversity. We showed that, while fluxes associated with the BMS group
\mathfrak{B}
of
\mathscr{I}^{+}
have a rich structure and those associated with
\mathfrak{G}
at
\Delta
simply vanish, these diverse conclusions arise from the same conceptual setting.

To this end, we introduced a new Hamiltonian framework that could be useful also in other contexts. (For example, as pointed out in the Introduction, it is well-suited to future space-like infinity
\mathscr{I}^{+}
in asymptotically de Sitter space-times.) It has three novel features:

(1) It introduces Hamiltonian methods for degrees of freedom that reside in
finite sub-regions
of suitably chosen 3-dimensional surfaces.

(2) Since
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and
\Delta
are null surfaces, it is possible to extract the
unconstrained
degrees of freedom of general relativity that lie in their sub-regions
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathcal{R}
. Consequently, while the covariant phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
is highly non-linear, the local phase spaces
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal R}}
are essentially
linear
.
(3) Fluxes
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
associated with the BMS symmetries
\xi^{a}
emerge as Hamiltonians generating canonical transformations on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
, induced by the action of
\xi^{a}
on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
.

Most discussions of charges and fluxes associated with the BMS symmetries of general relativity is based on the covariant phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
. However one typically works only formally with this infinite dimensional nonlinear space; its underlying manifold structure is rarely made precise. In the case of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
on the other hand it is relatively straightforward to spell out their manifold structure and topology. It is instructive to compare and contrast
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
with the phase space of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
of local degrees of freedom.
While the covariant phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
of general relativity carries only a (formally defined) pre-symplectic structure,
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
is endowed with a continuous, weakly non-degenerate symplectic structure
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
. Non-degeneracy is a consequence of the fact that, in contrast to the covariant phase space
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
, there are no gauge degrees of freedom in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
. Topology on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
and continuity of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
provide a degree of mathematical control in the following sense.
The action of any BMS vector field
\xi^{a}
naturally leads to a Hamiltonian vector field
\delta_{\xi}
on a dense subspace
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}_{\!\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
: it satisfies
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}(\delta_{\xi},\delta)=\delta H_{\xi}
on
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}_{\!\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
.
9
9

9

If the symmetry vector field
\xi^{a}
is tangential to the boundary of
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
, the action can be integrated to finite symplectic transformations; if not, we only have a densely defined Hamiltonian vector field. There is an analogue situation in quantum theory: generators of space-time symmetries are represented by
densely defined
operators on the Hilbert space of quantum states. If the operator is self-adjoint, the action can be integrated to unitary transformations. Sometimes –as in the case of the infinitesimal action of translations on the Hilbert space of a particle on the half line– the operator is only symmetric and not self-adjoint, and we only have infinitesimal motions.

(As usual, there is freedom to add a constant to
H_{\xi}
which is eliminated by requiring that
H_{\xi}(\{\hat{D}\})
should vanish at points
\{\hat{D}\}
of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
for which
N_{ab}=0
.) Since the symplectic structure on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
has infinitely many degenerate directions, there is a corresponding gauge ambiguity in the infinitesimal canonical transformation generated by any given Hamiltonian on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
. By contrast, thanks to the non-degeneracy of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}

this ambiguity disappears and
\delta_{\xi}
is the unique Hamiltonian vector field generated by
H_{\xi}
.
Finally,
H_{\xi}
is continuous on
\mathcal{D^{\prime}}_{\!\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
. Therefore, it can be uniquely extended function all of
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
.

Since continuity is determined by the choice of topology, it is natural to ask whether our choice is natural. As explained in section
II
, the choice is initially motivated by examining zero rest-mass scalar fields in asymptotically flat space-times. In that case, the choice of topology is such that a sequence
\hat{\phi}_{n}
of radiation fields in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
converges to a field
\hat{\phi}
if and only if
\hat{\phi}_{n}
and their first derivatives converge to
\hat{\phi}
and its first derivative in an
L^{2}
sense on
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
. (This topology is insensitive to the additional structures needed to define the ‘
L^{2}
-sense’; it depends only those structures that are naturally available at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
.)
This choice of topology leads one to a unique Hamiltonian
H_{\xi}
on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
that agrees with the
physically correct flux

F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
that is
defined by the stress-energy tensor
:
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]=\int_{\hat{\mathcal{R}}}T_{ab}\,\xi^{a}\mathrm{d}S^{b}
for all BMS vector fields
\xi^{a}
. Thus, the strategy leads to the physically correct fluxes using Hamiltonian methods, without having to assume the existence of the stress-energy tensor. It is thus extremely well suited to general relativity where there is no stress-energy tensor for the gravitational field. But topological considerations go through and provide a strategy to define fluxes: since
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
again consists of radiative modes that reside in
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
, we can equip
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
with the same topology and calculate Hamiltonians
H_{\xi}
and interpret them as fluxes
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
of BMS momenta across
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
. The fact that these fluxes agree with those in the literature reenforces the motivation for choosing this topology. Note that this procedure to arrive at fluxes
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
uses (fields and) symmetry vector fields
\xi^{a}
that are intrinsically defined on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
; one does not need to extend them into the space-time interior.

The BMS fluxes
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
can be obtained as Hamiltonians generating canonical transformations induced by BMS vector fields on
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
because the expressions of
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
refer only to radiative modes that are captured in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
. Charges, on the other hand, are 2-sphere integrals that refer also to the Coulombic information in
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
that is filtered out in the passage to
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
. Therefore, to recover charges from fluxes, one needs to return to
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\rm cov}
and use field equations and Bianchi identities at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
. One then obtains the charge 2-forms
\mathcal{Q}^{(\zeta)}_{{}_{np}}
by ‘integrating’ the flux 3-forms
\hat{\mathcal{F}}^{(\xi)}_{mnp}
. As discussed in section
III.3
, this was in fact the procedure used to arrive at the Dray-Streubel charges
[
2
,
4
]
. Thus, in the present approach one first obtains fluxes
F_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
using radiative phase spaces
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
and Hamiltonian considerations, and then arrives at charges
Q_{\xi}[S]
in a second step. However, this step is also carried out at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
, without having to extend fields or symmetry generators to the space-time interior. This concludes our summary of the underlying strategy and main results.

Next, let us briefly compare and contrast this framework with other frameworks that also discuss null infinity (for details, see Appendix
A
).

(i) Expressions of fluxes and charges satisfy a variety of physical requirements both on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
and
\Delta
. In particular, all fluxes across
\Delta
vanish, just as one would physically expect. At
\mathscr{I}^{+}
, the fluxes vanish if the News
\hat{N}_{ab}
vanishes, and, even when
\hat{N}_{ab}
is non-zero, if the symmetry vector field arises from a Killing field in space-time. All charges vanish in Minkowski space-time, and, the angular momentum charges agree with the Dray-Streubel charges on general space-times. These requirements are not always met in other approaches (see, e.g.,
[
13
,
42
,
41
,
39
,
40
]
).

(ii) In the discussion of null infinity, the procedure does not need auxiliary structures such ‘preferred symplectic potentials’ that have to be selected in the Wald-Zoupas (WZ) procedure and its extensions
[
7
,
38
,
26
]
. One works with just the symplectic 2-form. The physical fields that feature in the discussion are all intrinsically defined on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
, using only the region
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
for fluxes
{F}_{\xi}[\hat{\mathcal{R}}]
and 2-spheres
S
for charges
Q_{\xi}[S]
. Additional structures that are often used –such as tetrads or coordinates on
\mathscr{I}^{+}
(and sometimes also in its neighborhood)– are not needed. Similarly, one only uses symmetry vector fields
\xi^{a}
at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
alone. In contrast to other approaches, one does not need prescriptions to extend them to a neighborhood in the space-time interior.

(iii) Reciprocally, our approach requires an ingredient that is not needed in other approaches: local phase spaces
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\scriptscriptstyle{\cal{\hat{R}}}}
with appropriate topology. Thanks to the characteristic initial value problem in general relativity
[
46
]
, one can extract the degrees of freedom that reside in open regions of null hypersurface and construct local phase spaces with the required topology. The resulting fluxes agree with those obtained using the preferred symplectic 1-form required in the WZ procedure. Thus, there is considerably synergy. However, there are also differences. As remarked above, our procedure can also be used at future space-like infinity
\mathscr{I}^{+}
for asymptotically de Sitter space-times, where the preferred symplectic potential satisfying all requirements does not exist
[
15
]
. Reciprocally, while the procedure discussed in this paper is well-developed only for general relativity, the WZ procedure offers an avenue to incorporate higher derivative gravity theories as well. The open issue for our procedure is whether one can introduce the required local phase spaces. This could be difficult because, while in general relativity we relied on results from the characteristic initial value problem, typically this problem has not been studied in higher derivative theories. In the WZ framework the open issue is whether there is a unique symplectic potential with desired properties.

Finally, this unified treatment of black hole (and cosmological) horizons
\Delta
and null infinity
\mathscr{I}^{+}
opens new directions for further research both in classical general relativity and quantum gravity. We will conclude with a few illustrations. For binary black hole mergers, our framework provides an avenue to correlate horizon dynamics in the strong field regime with waveforms at infinity, paving the way to gravitational tomography
[
47
,
48
]
. For example, in the late time, quasi-normal mode regime, the time evolution of mass multipole moments of the horizon appears to be strongly correlated with the flux of supermomentum across
\mathscr{I}^{+}
. This intertwining of observables associated with horizons with those associated with
\mathscr{I}^{+}
has the potential for providing fresh insights. In the analysis of isolated gravitating systems in presence of a positive cosmological constant one can use certain cosmological horizons as ‘local
\mathscr{I}^{\pm}
’
[
49
,
50
]
. The present unified treatment suggests concrete avenues to develop a framework to analyze gravitational waves emitted by these systems and registered at these ‘local
\mathscr{I}^{\pm}
’. In quantum gravity, this framework is likely to be useful to sharpen the analysis of the black hole evaporation process. It takes some
10^{67}
years for a solar mass black hole to shrink to lunar mass. During this very long phase of the evaporation process, the time-evolution of the dynamical horizon should be well-approximated by a perturbed WIH
[
51
]
. For this regime, the unified framework developed in this paper opens avenues to correlate dynamics of quantum observables defined at the perturbed horizon with those associated with the quantum radiation at
\mathscr{I}^{+}
. Availability of these two algebras of time-dependent Heisenberg observables will likely deepen our understanding of entanglement (at least) in this semi-classical phase
[
52
]
. In particular, it would help clarify whether anything dramatic happens to entanglement in the physical space-time at the Page time.
