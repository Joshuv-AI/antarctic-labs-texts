Logical properties of random graphs from small addable classes
Anuj Dawar \rsuper a, Eryk Kopczyński \rsuper b

Abstract
. We establish zero-one laws and convergence laws for monadic second-order logic (MSO) (and, a fortiori, first-order logic) on a number of interesting graph classes. In particular, we show that MSO obeys a zero-one law on the class of connected planar graphs, the class of connected graphs of tree-width at most k k and the class of connected graphs excluding the k k -clique as a minor. In each of these cases, dropping the connectivity requirement leads to a class where the zero-one law fails but a convergence law for MSO still holds.

1.
Introduction

The zero-one law for first-order logic
[
GKLT69
,
Fag76
]
established
that every first-order sentence
\phi
, when evaluated over a random

n
-element finite structure, has a probability of being true that
converges to either
0
or
1
as
n
goes to infinity. This prompted
much further investigation into the asymptotic behaviour of classes of
structures definable in logic. Zero-one laws have been established
for fragments of second-order logic
[
KV90
]
; extensions of
first-order logic such as the infinitary logic
L^{\omega}_{\infty\,\omega}

[
KV92
]

which subsumes various fixed-point logics; and logics with generalized
quantifiers
[
DG10
]
, among many others.

Another widely studied extension of first-order logic is
monadic
second-order logic
(MSO). This does not have a zero-one law but
its asymptotic behaviour has been studied on restricted classes of
structures. For many interesting classes it does admit a zero-one
law, such as on free labelled trees
[
McC02
]
. On rooted
labelled trees, MSO does not have a zero-one law, but still admits a
convergence law
[
McC02
]
. This means that the probability
of any given sentence
\phi
being true in an
n
-element structure
does converge to a limit, though that limit is not necessarily
0
or
1
.
Zero-one and convergence laws for MSO on a number of other classes
are shown in
[
Com89
]
.

In this paper, we are concerned with the asymptotic behaviour of
first-order logic (FO) and MSO on restricted classes of finite
structures, and more specifically, restricted classes of graphs. We
look particularly at
tame
classes of graphs in the sense
of
[
Daw07
]
. These include planar graphs, graphs of bounded
treewidth and some other proper minor-closed classes. A flavour of
our results is given by the following examples. There is a constant

c\leq 0.036747
such that the asymptotic probability of any MSO
sentence
\phi
on the class of planar graphs converges to a real number
in the range
[0,c)\cup(1-c,1]
. On the class of
connected
,
planar graphs, MSO admits a zero-one law. These results can be
strengthened (modulo changing the constant
c
) from planar graphs to
other minor-closed classes of graphs that are
smooth
and

addable
, which we define formally later. Here we note that
examples of smooth, addable, minor-closed graph classes include not
just the planar graphs but also the class of graphs of treewidth at
most
k
, for any
k
.

Technically, we rely on the combinatorial results on random planar
graphs proved in
[
MSW05
]
and the extensions to minor-closed
classes established in
[
McD09
]
. We combine these with
logical techniques from
[
Com89
]
and
[
McC02
]
.

Since the first submission, it has come to our attention that some of
the results reported here have also been obtained independently
in
[
HMNT18
]
.

2.
Graph Classes

Let
R_{n}
denote a random graph drawn from the uniform distribution on
graphs on the vertex set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Equivalently,
R_{n}

is obtained by putting, for each
i,j
with
1\leq i<j\leq n
an edge between

i
and
j
with probability
1/2
. If
\mathcal{C}
is a class of graphs
closed under isomorphisms, we write
\mathcal{C}_{n}
for the graphs in

\mathcal{C}
on the vertex set
[n]
and
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
to
denote the random graph drawn from the uniform distribution on

\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Unlike with
R_{n}
, it is not immediately clear how to
effectively sample from this distribution. When
\mathcal{C}
is the class
of planar graphs, a first partially successful attempt was
in
[
DVW96
]
, using a Markov chain whose only stable
distribution is the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{n}
. However, this
is not quite practical as the mixing rate of this Markov chain is
unknown. Nevertheless, this formulation did enable experimental
validation of some conjectures about random planar graphs. Finally,
in
[
BGK07
]
, it is shown that there is a polynomial-time
algorithm that can generate a random planar graph on
[n]
.

An excellent analysis of random planar graphs, resolving some
conjectures of
[
DVW96
]
was given in
[
MSW05
]
. Some
of this analysis was predicated on a conjecture, termed the “isolated
vertices conjecture” to the effect that the number of isolated
vertices in a random planar graph on
[n]
tends to a limit as
n

increases. This conjecture was proved in
[
GN05
]
and
later shown
[
McD09
]
to be an instance of the

smoothness
of
addable
minor-closed classes. Much of the
analysis of random planar graphs in
[
MSW05
]
can be extended to
other graph classes that are smooth, addable and

small

[
MSW06
]
. We begin by defining these central
notions.

{defi}

Say that a graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
decomposable
if
G\in\mathcal{C}
if, and only if, every connected component of
G
is in
\mathcal{C}
.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{C}
is decomposable if, and only if,
\mathcal{C}
is both
closed under disjoint unions and closed under taking connected
components.

{defi}

Say that graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
bridge-addable
if, for every

G\in\mathcal{C}
, if
u
and
v
are vertices in distinct connected
components of
G
, then the graph obtained by adding the edge

\{u,v\}
to
G
is also in
\mathcal{C}
.

{defi}

A graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
addable
if it is both decomposable
and bridge-addable.

Note that the class of planar graphs is clearly addable. As noted
in
[
MSW06
]
, the following classes of graphs are all
addable: forests; the class of graphs of treewidth at most
k
; the
class of graphs with no cycle of length greater than
k
; the class of
graphs that exclude
K_{k}
as a minor. On the other hand, the class of
graphs embeddable in a torus is not addable. It is bridge-addable but
not decomposable since it contains
K_{5}
but not the graph that is the
disjoint union of two copies of
K_{5}
. In general, the class of
graphs of genus at most
k
for positive
k
is not addable.

We write
{Q_{n}}(\mathcal{C})
to denote the number of graphs in

\mathcal{C}_{n}
(i.e. graphs in
\mathcal{C}
on the vertex set
[n]
).

{defi}

A graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
small
if
{Q_{n}}(\mathcal{C})\leq d^{n}n!
for some
d\in\mathbb{R}
.

With any class
\mathcal{C}
, we can associate a
growth constant

\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
defined by

\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{({Q_{n}}(\mathcal{C})/n!)}^{1/n}.

Then,
\mathcal{C}
is small just
in case
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
is finite.

{defiC}

[
[
MSW06
]
]
A graph class
\mathcal{C}
is
smooth
if
\frac{{Q_{n}}}{n{Q_{n-1}}}
tends to a finite limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

It is known (see
[
MSW06
]
) that if
\mathcal{C}
is smooth, then
the limit of
\frac{{Q_{n}}}{n{Q_{n-1}}}
is the growth constant
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}
. Thus, every smooth class is small. However,
there are small graph classes that are not smooth. When
\mathcal{C}
is
the class of forests,
\gamma_{\mathcal{C}}=e
. We can now state the
two results on smoothness that we need.

{thmC}

[
[
GN05
]
]
The class of planar graphs is smooth, and the growth constant is
\gamma\approx 27.22679
and is given by an explicit analytic expression.

{thmC}

[
[
McD09
]
]
Each addable proper minor-closed class of graphs is smooth.

3.
Logics

We assume the reader is familiar with the syntax and semantics of
first-order logic (FO) and monadic second-order logic (MSO) interpreted
on finite structures, as defined, for instance in
[
Lib04
]
. We
give a brief review of definitions, especially where our notation
deviates from the standard.

3.1.
Basics

We assume we have two disjoint countable sets of symbols
{\rm{Var}}^{1}
(the first-order
variables) and
{\rm{Var}}^{2}
(the second-order variables). Given a graph

G=(V,E)
, a
valuation

\sigma
on
G
is a pair

(\sigma^{1},\sigma^{2})
of
partial
functions
\sigma^{1}:{\rm{Var}}^{1}\rightharpoonup V
and

\sigma^{2}:{\rm{Var}}^{2}\rightharpoonup\mathscr{P}(V)
. For
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}\cup{\rm{Var}}^{2}
, we use

\sigma(x)
to denote
\sigma^{1}(x)
or
\sigma^{2}(x)
as appropriate.

A
structure
is a pair
\mathfrak{G}=(G,\sigma)
where
G
is a graph
and
\sigma
is a valuation on
G
. If
\mathfrak{G}=(G=(V,E),\sigma)
is a
structure, we sometimes write
G(\mathfrak{G})
,
V(\mathfrak{G})
,
E(\mathfrak{G})
,

\sigma(\mathfrak{G})
,
x(\mathfrak{G})
to denote
G,V,E,\sigma,\sigma(x)
, respectively. Moreover, we write
{\rm{Var}}^{1}(\mathfrak{G})

and
{\rm{Var}}^{2}(\mathfrak{G})
to denote the subsets of
{\rm{Var}}^{1}
and
{\rm{Var}}^{2}

respectively on which
\sigma^{1}(\mathfrak{G})
and
\sigma^{2}(\mathfrak{G})
are defined.
For even
greater brevity, when referring to a structure
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
, we may write

G_{1},V_{1}
, etc. for
G(\mathfrak{G}_{1})
,
V(\mathfrak{G}_{1})
, etc. when no
ambiguity arises. We write
\sigma[v/x]
to denote the valuation that
agrees with
\sigma
at all values other than
x
and that maps
x
to

v
. We also write
\mathfrak{G}[v/x]
to denote
(G(\mathfrak{G}),\sigma(\mathfrak{G})[v/x])
and
\mathfrak{G}[/x]
to denote the structure
(G(\mathfrak{G}),\sigma^{\prime}(\mathfrak{G}))
where the valuation
\sigma^{\prime}
is the same as
\sigma

except that it is undefined at
x
.

Note that, by including the interpretation of variables in our
definition of structure, we can uniformly talk of graphs, coloured
graphs and graphs with distinguished constants as structures, all over a fixed vocabulary.

The formulas of MSO are built-up as usual according to the following
grammar, where
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are formulas,
x,y\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}
and

X,Y\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}
.

\phi\ ::=\ x\in X\mid E(x,y)\mid\phi_{1}\wedge\phi_{2}\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\neg\phi_{1}\mid\forall x\phi\mid\exists x\phi\mid\forall_{2}X\phi\mid\exists_{2}X\phi

The formulas of first-order logic are those that involve no occurrence
of a variable from
{\rm{Var}}^{2}
.

The definition of satisfaction
\mathfrak{G}\models\phi
is standard. We
define the
quantifier rank
of a formula
\phi
, denoted
{\rm qr}(\phi)
, to be the
maximum depth of nesting of quantifiers in
\phi
counting both first-
and second-order quantifiers (as in
[
Lib04
, Definition 7.4]
).

3.2.
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games

We write
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
to denote that the two structures

\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
cannot be distinguished by any formula with
quantifier rank at most
m
. Formally, this is defined by induction
on
m
as follows. We say that
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{0}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
if, for any
quantifier-free formula
\phi
such that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are defined on all
the free variables of
\phi
, we have that
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\models\phi

if, and only if,
\mathfrak{G}_{2}\models\phi
. Inductively, we say that

\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m+1}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
if all the following conditions are
satisfied.

•

For
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}
,
\forall v_{1}\in V_{1}\exists v_{2}\in V_{2}\mathfrak{G}_{1}[v_{1}/x]\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}[v_{2}/x]
.

•

For
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}
,
\forall v_{2}\in V_{2}\exists v_{1}\in V_{1}\mathfrak{G}_{1}[v_{1}/x]\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}[v_{2}/x]
.

•

For
X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}
,
\forall U_{1}\subseteq V_{1}\exists U_{2}\subseteq V_{2}\mathfrak{G}_{1}[U_{1}/X]\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}[U_{2}/X]
.

•

For
X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}
,
\forall U_{2}\subseteq V_{2}\exists U_{1}\subseteq V_{1}\mathfrak{G}_{1}[U_{1}/X]\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}[U_{2}/X]
.

As usual, this definition
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
can be understood as
a game between two players conventionally called Spoiler and
Duplicator (known as the
m
-round Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game
). The game is played on a “board”
consisting of two structures
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. For
m=0
,
Duplicator wins iff
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{0}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. Otherwise, Spoiler
chooses either a variable
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}
and a vertex
v_{i}
in one of
the graphs
G_{i}
or a variable
X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}
and a set of vertices

U_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
in the graph
G_{i}
. In the first case,
Duplicator responds by choosing an element
v_{3-i}\in G_{3-i}
and
the play then proceeds for
m-1
rounds starting with the board

\mathfrak{G}_{1}[v_{1}/x]
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}[v_{2}/x]
. In the second case, Duplicator
responds by choosing a set
U_{3-i}\subseteq V_{3-i}
and again the play then proceeds for
m-1
rounds starting with the board

\mathfrak{G}_{1}[U_{1}/X]
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}[U_{2}/X]
. Then, it is clear that
Duplicator has a strategy for winning the
m
round game on the board

\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
just in case
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. The
connection with MSO comes from the following standard theorem
(see
[
Lib04
, Theorem 7.7]
)

Theorem 1
.

The following conditions are equivalent:

•

\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}

•

\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
satisfy exactly the same MSO formulas of quantifier rank at most
m
.

Finally, we note that, if we have only a fixed finite set of
variables, then the equivalence relation
\equiv_{m}
is of finite index
for each
m
. That is, fix a finite set
\Xi\subseteq{\rm{Var}}
and let

\mathcal{S}[\Xi]
denote the class of structures
\mathfrak{G}
such that

{\rm{Var}}^{1}(\mathfrak{G}),{\rm{Var}}^{2}(\mathfrak{G})\subseteq\Xi
.

Proposition 2
.

The relation
\equiv_{m}
restricted to
\mathcal{S}[\Xi]
has finite
index.

The proof is a proof by induction that there are, up to equivalence,
only finitely many formulas of MSO of quantifier rank
m
with free
variables among
\Xi
(see
[
Lib04
, Prop. 7.5]
). We write

t_{m}(\Xi)
to denote the index of
\equiv_{m}
restricted to

\mathcal{S}[\Xi]
. Note that the value of
t_{m}(\Xi)
is completely
determined by the number of elements in
\Xi\cap{\rm{Var}}^{1}
and
\Xi\cap{\rm{Var}}^{2}
.

4.
Adding graphs

For structures
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
, the disjoint union
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is defined if
{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{1}\cap{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{2}=\emptyset
.
In this case, the set of vertices of
\mathfrak{G}_{3}=\mathfrak{G}_{1}\oplus\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is the
disjoint union of
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
,
E_{3}=E_{1}\cup E_{2}
,

\sigma^{1}_{3}(x)=\sigma_{i}^{1}(x)
for
i\in\{1,2\}
whenever
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{i}

and
\sigma^{2}_{3}(X)=\sigma_{1}^{2}(X)\cup\sigma_{2}^{2}(X)
.

It is well-known that the equivalence relation
\equiv_{m}
is a
congruence with respect to the disjoint union of structures.
Moreover, for every
m
and finite set
\Xi\subseteq{\rm{Var}}^{1}\cup{\rm{Var}}^{2}
there is a threshold
r_{m}(\Xi)
such that for any

p,q>r_{m}(\Xi)
, the disjoint union of
p
copies of a structure is

\equiv_{m}
-equivalent to the disjoint union of
q
copies. We
formally state this for later use.

Lemma 3
.

Fix a positive integer
m
and a finite set
\Xi\subseteq{\rm{Var}}^{1}\cup{\rm{Var}}^{2}
. There is a positive integer
r_{m}(\Xi)
such that
if
\{\mathfrak{G}_{i}\mid i\in I\}
is a collection of structures such
that
\mathfrak{G}_{i}\equiv\mathfrak{G}_{j}
for all
i,j\in I
and
P,Q\subseteq I
are sets such that either
|P|=|Q|
or
|P|,|Q|\geq r_{m}(\Xi)
then

\bigoplus_{i\in P}\mathfrak{G}_{i}\equiv_{m}\bigoplus_{j\in Q}\mathfrak{G}_{j}.

Indeed,
similar facts can be established for many operations other than
disjoint union (see, for example
[
Mak04
]
).
Here, we are also
interested in a particular operation of taking the disjoint union of
two structures while adding an edge between distinguished vertices (as
in the definition of bridge addable graphs:
Definition
2
). We give the formal definition
below and prove the properties we need.

We say that a structure
\mathfrak{G}
is
rooted
if
{\rm{Root}}\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}(\mathfrak{G})
.
Let
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
be two rooted structures such that
{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{1}\cap{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{2}=\{{\rm{Root}}\}
and
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=\emptyset
. We write

\mathfrak{G}_{1}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}
to denote the structure
\mathfrak{G}
such that:

•

V=V_{1}\cup V_{2}
,
E=E_{1}\cup E_{2}\cup\{\sigma_{1}({\rm{Root}}),\sigma_{2}({\rm{Root}})\}

•

\sigma(X)=\sigma_{1}(X)\cup\sigma_{2}(X)
for
X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}_{1}\cap{\rm{Var}}^{2}_{2}

•

\sigma(X)=\sigma_{i}(X)
for
X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}_{i}-{\rm{Var}}^{2}_{3-i}

•

\sigma({\rm{Root}})=\sigma_{1}({\rm{Root}})

•

\sigma(x)=\sigma_{i}(x)
for
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{i}
and
x\neq{\rm{Root}}

That is to say that
\mathfrak{G}_{1}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is obtained by taking the
disjoint union of the structures
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
seen as
coloured graphs, putting an edge between the two roots, and making the
root of
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
the root of the combined structure. This asymmetry
in the choice of root means that, in general
\mathfrak{G}_{1}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}\neq\mathfrak{G}_{2}+\mathfrak{G}_{1}
. However, it is still the case that
(\mathfrak{G}_{1}+\mathfrak{G}_{2})+\mathfrak{G}_{3}=(\mathfrak{G}_{1}+\mathfrak{G}_{3})+\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

A simple argument using Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games establishes
the following proposition.

Proposition 4
.

If
\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{2}
, then
\mathfrak{G}_{0}+\mathfrak{G}_{1}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{0}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

Proof 4.1
.

Suppose Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
m
-move
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game played on
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. We
show that she also has a winning strategy in the
m
-move game
on
\mathfrak{G}_{0}+\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{0}+\mathfrak{G}_{2}
. The strategy is described
as follows.

•

if Spoiler chooses
w\in V_{0}
, then Duplicator responds with the same
w
;

•

if Spoiler chooses
w\in V_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then
Duplicator responds with
w^{\prime}\in V_{3-i}
given by her winning
strategy in the game on
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
; and

•

if Spoiler chooses
W\subseteq V_{0}\cup V_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then Duplicator responds with
(W\cap V_{0})\cup W_{2}
,
where
W_{2}
is her response to
W\cap V_{i}
in the game on
\mathfrak{G}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
.

It is easy to show that this does indeed describe a winning strategy.

If
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
is a structure with
{\rm{Var}}^{1}_{2}=\{{\rm{Root}}\}
and
c
is a
positive integer, we write
\mathfrak{G}_{1}+c\mathfrak{G}_{2}
to denote the structure
obtained by adding
\mathfrak{G}_{2}
to
\mathfrak{G}_{1}

c
times. More formally,
this is defined by induction on
c
:

•

\mathfrak{G}_{1}+0\mathfrak{G}_{2}=\mathfrak{G}_{1}
,

•

\mathfrak{G}_{1}+(c+1)\mathfrak{G}_{2}=(\mathfrak{G}_{1}+c\mathfrak{G}_{2})+\mathfrak{G}_{2}

Analogously to Lemma
3
, we have the following
proposition.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\Xi\subseteq{\rm{Var}}
be a finite set. For each
m
, there is
number
q_{m}(\Xi)
such that for any
\mathfrak{G}\in\mathcal{S}[\Xi]
and
any structure
\mathfrak{G}_{0}
we have

\mathfrak{G}_{0}+p\mathfrak{G}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{0}+q\mathfrak{G}

whenever
p,q\geq q_{m}(\Xi)
.

Proof 4.2
.

We define the value of
q_{m}(\Xi)
by induction on
m
,
simultaneously for all finite sets
\Xi\subseteq{\rm{Var}}
.

•

q_{0}(\Xi)=0
for all
\Xi
; and

•

q_{m+1}(\Xi)=\max(q_{m}(\Xi\cup\{x\})+1,\ t_{m}(\Xi\cup\{X\})\cdot q_{m}(\Xi\cup\{X\})+m)
,

where
x\in{\rm{Var}}^{1},\ X\in{\rm{Var}}^{2}
are any variables not in
\Xi
. Recall that
t_{m}(\Xi)

denotes the index of the relation
\equiv_{m}
in
\mathcal{S}[\Xi]
.

Now, we argue by induction on
m
that if
p,q\geq q_{m}(\Xi)
then

\mathfrak{G}_{0}+p\mathfrak{G}\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{0}+q\mathfrak{G}
. In the following, we use the
sets
[p]
and
[q]
to index the copies of
\mathfrak{G}
. Thus, we may
refer to the graph
G_{i}
for
i\in[p]
.
The base case,
m=0
is immediate. Suppose now that the claim is true for some
m
and all finite

\Xi
, and let
\mathfrak{G}_{A}=\mathfrak{G}_{0}+p\mathfrak{G}
and
\mathfrak{G}_{B}=\mathfrak{G}_{0}+q\mathfrak{G}

for some
p,q\geq q_{m+1}(\Xi)
. We consider a number of cases
corresponding to different moves that Spoiler might make in the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé game.

•

Suppose

4.1.
Universal Connected Structure

There is a sentence of MSO (with quantifier rank 5) which is true in a

\mathfrak{G}
is connected. It follows

\equiv_{m}

For the rest

\mathfrak{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{G}_{t}

5.
MSO zero-one law for random connected graphs

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a class of graphs. Recall that
\mathcal{C}_{n}
is the class
of graphs in
\mathcal{C}
on the vertex set
[n]
and

R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
denotes the random graph drawn from the uniform distribution on

\mathcal{C}_{n}
. The following is a direct consequence of Theorem 1.1
in
[
CP16
]
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small addable class of graphs. Then
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
is
connected with probability at least
1/\sqrt{e}-o(1)
.

Indeed, Theorem 1.1 of
[
CP16
]
establishes this more
generally for
bridge addable
classes. The bound
1/\sqrt{e}

is tight in that this is the limiting probability for connectedness
among forests.

We also rely on the following result
which is obtained as a consequence of Theorem 5.1
in
[
MSW06
]
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a small addable class of graphs, and
\mathfrak{G}
be a rooted
graph in
\mathcal{C}
. Then the probability that
\mathfrak{G}
appears in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
tends
to 1 as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

In fact, Theorem 5.1 of
[
MSW06
]
establishes the stronger
result that the number of appearances of
\mathfrak{G}
in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
grows
linearly with
n
(more precisely, there is an
\alpha>0
such that the
probability that
\mathfrak{G}
appears
\alpha n
times tends to 1).

Together, these enable us to establish the zero-one law for the class
of connected graphs in any small addable class
\mathcal{C}
. In the
following we write Conn for the class of
connected
graphs.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\phi
be a sentence of MSO, and
\mathcal{C}
a small addable class of
graphs. Let
p_{n}
denote the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\rm{Conn}})

satisfies
\phi
. Then
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}p_{n}
is defined
and equal to either
0
or
1
.

Proof 5.1
.

Let
m
be the quantifier rank of
\phi
. By Theorem
8

we know that the probability that
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}

appears in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
tends to 1. Moreover, since the probability
that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
is connected is non-zero (by
Theorem
7
) it follows that the probability that

\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}
, the
m
-universal connected rooted graph in
\mathcal{C}
, appears in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\rm{Conn}})
also tends to
1
.
Thus, by Proposition
6
, with probability tending to

1
, we have
R_{n}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\rm{Conn}})\equiv_{m}\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}
. Hence, if

\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\models\phi
, then
p_{n}
tends to
1
, otherwise
p_{n}

tends to
0
.

As an immediate consequence, we have a zero-one law for MSO for a
number of interesting classes of graphs.

Corollary 10
.

MSO admits a zero-one law on each of the following classes of
graphs.

•

The class of connected planar graphs.

•

For each
k
, the class of connected graphs of tree-width at
most
k
.

•

For each
k>2
, the class of connected graphs excluding
K_{k}
as
a minor.

6.
MSO limit law for random graphs

We are now ready to establish our general result on the existence of
a limit law (also known as a convergence law) for MSO on smooth addable
classes of graphs. Note that, while Theorem
9
was
stated for small addable classes, from now on we will restrict
ourselves further to
smooth
classes. Recall that every smooth
class is also small.

To establish the limit law we need two specific results
from
[
MSW06
]
. The first is a direct consequence of
Theorem 6.4 in that paper.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth addable class of graphs.
For any
\epsilon>0
there exist a constant
g(\epsilon)

such that, for sufficiently large values of
n
, with probability at
least
1-\epsilon
,
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
has a
connected component which contains at least
n-g(\epsilon)
vertices.

In general, we refer to the
giant component
of
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
.

The second result we need is a consequence of Theorem 9.2
of
[
MSW06
]
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth addable class of graphs,
H
be a
connected graph, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there is a number
p_{k}(H)

such that the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
has exactly
k
components
isomorphic to
H
tends to
p_{k}(H)
. Moreover, these events are
asymptotically independent for non-isomorphic graphs
H
.

Actually, Theorem 9.2 of
[
MSW06
]
does not state that the
events are asymptotically independent, but it is easily seen to be the case. Indeed,
what is stated there is that the distribution of the number of
components isomorphic to
H
tends to Poisson distribution as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
with parameter
\lambda=1/({\gamma(\mathcal{C})}^{|H|}|{\rm{Aut}}(H)|)
, where

|{\rm{Aut}}(H)|
is the number of automorphisms of
H
.

As an example, for large values of

n
, the random planar graph on
n
vertices has on average
1/\gamma(\mathcal{C})\approx 0.03673

isolated vertices,
1/2{\gamma(\mathcal{C})}^{2}<0.0007
pairs of vertices
with an edge between them (and no other edges incident on them), less than
0.00004
isolated connected subgraphs with
3 vertices, and so on. Summing over all of these, we can show that the random planar
graph has
\lambda\approx 0.037439
connected components other than the
giant component on average. From the fact that the distribution is
Poisson we get that the probability that the graph
is not connected is
1-e^{-\lambda}\approx 0.036746
.

We are interested in the frequency of occurrence of connected
components, not just up to isomorphism, but up to
\equiv_{m}
for suitable
values of
m
. Specifically, we are interested in whether the number
of components from a fixed
\equiv_{m}
equivalence class is greater than
the threshold
r_{m}(\emptyset)
from Lemma
3
and
the exact number if it is not. In the following result, we use
Theorems
11
and
12
to show that the relevant
probabilities converge.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth addable class of graphs and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\mathcal{A}
is an
\equiv_{m}
-equivalence class of connected graphs
then for each
k\leq r_{m}(\emptyset)

there is a
p_{k}(\mathcal{A})\in\mathbb{R}
such that the probability that

R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
has exactly
k
components from
\mathcal{A}
tends to

p_{k}(\mathcal{A})
as
n
goes to infinity.

Proof 6.1
.

Let
p_{n}
denote the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
contains exactly

k
components in
\mathcal{A}
. We
show that for any
\epsilon>0
there is a
p
such that
|p_{n}-p|<\epsilon
for large enough
n
. Thus, the sequence
{(p_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy
sequence and so converges to a limit.

Let
g=g(\epsilon/3)
be the value given by Theorem
11

such that, for sufficiently large
n
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon/3
,
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
has a
connected component which contains at least
n-g
vertices.
Moreover, let

be such that for all

, this

1-\epsilon/3
.

By exactly the same argument as in the proof of
Theorem
9
, we can show that the conditional
probability, given that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
has a
connected component which contains at least
n-g
vertices, that
this giant component is
\equiv_{m}
-equivalent to
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}

m
-universal connected rooted graph in
\mathcal{C}

1
as
n
goes to infinity. Thus, we can fix a value

such that this probability is at least
1-\epsilon/3
for all

.

Let
H_{1},\ldots,H_{M}
enumerate (up to isomorphism) all graphs in

\mathcal{A}
with at most
g
vertices. Let
K
denote the collection of
all functions
f:[M]\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that
\sum_{i\in[M]}f(i)=k

and note that this is a finite set. Let
p=\sum_{f\in K}\prod_{i\in[M]}p_{f(i)}(H_{i})
, where

is the limiting
probability, given by Theorem

, that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
contains
exactly
f(i)
components isomorphic to
H_{i}
. If
p^{\prime}_{n}
denotes the
probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
contains exactly
k

components that are isomorphic to one of
H_{1},\ldots,H_{M}
, then
clearly the sequence
{(p^{\prime}_{n})}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
tends to the limit
p
.
Let

be such that
|p^{\prime}_{n}-p|<\epsilon/3
for all

.

First, consider the case that
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\not\in\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
\mathcal{A}
is an
\equiv_{m}
-equivalence class distinct from that of

\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}
. In this case, our aim is to show that for all sufficiently large

, in particular
for all

p-\epsilon<p_{n}<p+\epsilon
,
establishing the result. We return to the case where
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\in\mathcal{A}
later.

Fix
n
with

and let

denote the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
contains
no

\mathcal{A}
except those that are isomorphic to one of

H_{1},\ldots,H_{M}
. Then clearly

since the

k

\mathcal{A}
and the right-hand side gives the probability

k

\mathcal{A}
in a particular way,

. Moreover

since if there is a giant component with

\equiv_{m}
-equivalent to
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}
then

\mathcal{A}
.
We then have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}p_{n}&>&p^{\prime}_{n}p_{0}\\
&>&{p^{\prime}_{n}(1-\epsilon/3)}^{2}\\
&>&{p(1-\epsilon/3)}^{3}\\
&>&p-\epsilon,\end{array}

where the second line follows by substituting the lower bound for

and the third line follows from the fact that
|p^{\prime}_{n}-p|<\epsilon/3
.

For the other direction, note that

p_{n}<p_{n}^{\prime\prime}p_{0}+(1-p_{n}^{\prime})(1-p_{0}),

where the first term on the right is as above and the second term is
an upper bound on the probability that
k
components from
\mathcal{A}
are
obtained in some way other than by having
k
components with
g

vertices or fewer and
0

g

We then have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}p_{n}&<&p^{\prime}_{n}p_{0}+(1-p^{\prime}_{n})(1-p_{0})\\
&<&p^{\prime}_{n}+(1-p_{0})\\

&<&p+\epsilon/3+(1-{(1-\epsilon/3)}^{2})\\
&<&p+\epsilon,\end{array}

where the second line is obtained by substituting the upper bound of

for
p_{0}
and
1-p^{\prime}_{n}
, the third line by substituting the lower
bound of
{(1-\epsilon/3)}^{2}
for

and the fourth by the fact that

.

For the case that
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\in\mathcal{A}
, an entirely analogous
argument can be used to show that
|p_{n}-p|<\epsilon
where

is
the limit of the sequence

of probabilities that

contains exactly

components that are isomorphic to one of

.

Fix a small addable class of graphs
\mathcal{C}
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Let

and

. Suppose

\mathcal{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{A}_{t}
enumerates all the

classes of graphs
in

. We call an

-profile

. We say
that a graph

matches

-profile

if the following conditions

for each

, if

then

has exactly

connected components which are in
\mathcal{A}_{i}
; and

(2)

for each

has at least

distinct connected components which are in

.

The following lemmas are now immediate from our previous

Lemma 14
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth addable class of graphs then for any

and
any

that

converges to a value

.

Define

to be

p_{f}=\left(\prod_{i:f(i)<r}p_{f(i)}(\mathcal{A}_{i})\right)\left(\prod_{i:f(i)=r}\sum_{k\geq r}p_{k}(\mathcal{A}_{i})\right).

The result than follows by the asymptotic independence asserted in
Theorem

Note that every graph matches some profile

and these events are
mutually exclusive for distinct

over all profiles

must be

. The reason for considering
profiles is, of course, that they completely determine the
\equiv_{m}

class of a graph.

Lemma 15
.

If
G_{1}
and

are graphs that both match the same

-profile
f
,
then
G_{1}\equiv_{m}G_{2}
.

Proof 6.3
.

This is immediate from Lemma

.

We can now establish the main convergence law for MSO.

Theorem 16
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a smooth, addable class of graphs and
\phi
is a
sentence of MSO, then the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies

\phi
tends to a limit as
n
goes to infinity.

Proof 6.4
.

Let
m
be the quantifier rank of
\phi
. By

, if
f
is an
m

either all graphs matching

\phi
or none do. Let us
say that

implies
\phi
if the former case holds. Then taking

p
to be the sum of
p_{f}
(as in Lemma

)
over all

that imply
\phi
,
we see that the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies

\phi
tends to
p
.

We can say somewhat more about the possible values
p
to which the
probability that
\phi
holds in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
may converge. Note that
the property of being connected is definable by a sentence of MSO and
thus the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
is connected converges to a
limit. By Theorem
7
, this value is at least

1/\sqrt{e}
and therefore greater than
1/2
.
This, together with the result below tells us that the
limiting probabilities of MSO sentences on smooth, addable classes
cluster near

and

.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a smooth, addable class and let
c
be the
limiting probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
is not connected. Then, for any
MSO sentence
\phi
, the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies

\phi
converges to a value
p
such that either
p\leq c
or
p\geq 1-c
.

Proof 6.5
.

Let
m
be the quantifier rank of
\phi
and let

\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}
be the universal connected structure defined in
Sec.
4.1
. If
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\models\phi
then
\phi

is true in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\rm{Conn}})
with probability tending to

1
. Thus the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies
\phi
tends
to at least
1-c
. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{G}_{U(m)}\not\models\phi
, then
\phi

is false in
R_{n}(\mathcal{C}\cap{\rm{Conn}})
with probability tending to

1
, and so the probability that
R_{n}(\mathcal{C})
satisfies
\phi
tends
to at most
c
.

For many interesting classes of graphs, the value of
c
is quite
small. As noted above, for planar graphs the value of
c
is about

0.036746
, giving us the result mentioned in the introduction.

7.
Future work

We have shown that the zero-one law holds for random connected graphs from
smooth addable classes of graphs, for formulas of first order and
monadic second-order logic. Moreover, a limit law holds for random graphs
of such classes which do not have to be connected. This includes many
of the tame classes of graphs that have been studied in finite model
theory in recent years. Still, there are other classes one could
explore. Most interesting would be proper minor-closed classes which
are not addable, such as the graphs embeddable in a torus or, more
generally, the class of graphs of genus at most
k
for a fixed value
of
k
.

Another general direction would be to explore logics beyond
first-order logic (other than MSO) such as fixed-point logics. These
are known to admit a zero-one law over the class of all graphs.
However, their study is based on equivalence relations
\equiv^{k}

stratified by the number of variables rather than the quantifier
rank. These equivalence relations do not have finite index and that
makes many of the methods we have used here infeasible to use.
