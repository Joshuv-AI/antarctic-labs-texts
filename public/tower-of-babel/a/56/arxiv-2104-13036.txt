Junhyeok Byeon, Seung-Yeal Ha, Gyuyoung Hwang, Hansol Park

Abstract
. We study a global well-posedness of measure-valued solutions to the kinetic Lohe Hermitian sphere(LHS) model derived from the Lohe tensor(LT) model on the set of rank-1 complex tensors(i.e. complex vectors) with the same size and investigate emergent behaviors. The kinetic LHS model corresponds to a complex analogue of the kinetic LS model which has been extensively studied in the literature on the aggregation modeling of Lohe particles on the unit sphere in Euclidean space. In this paper, we provide several frameworks in terms of system parameters and initial data leading to the local and global well-posedness of measure-valued solutions. In particular, we show emergent behaviors of the kinetic LHS model with the same free flows by analyzing the temporal evolution of the order parameter.

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &z=(z^{1},\cdots,z^{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\quad w=(w^{1},\cdots,w^{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d},\quad\langle w,z\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\overline{w^{i}}z^{i},\\

where

Consider the LHS model
[

]

(1.2)

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{j}=\Omega_{j}z_{j}+\displaystyle\frac{\kappa_{0}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\langle z_{j},z_{j}\rangle z_{k}-\langle z_{k},z_{j}\rangle z_{j}\big)+\frac{\kappa_{1}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\langle z_{j},z_{k}\rangle-\langle z_{k},z_{j}\rangle\big)z_{j},

where
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
are constant coupling gains coined as “
Lohe sphere coupling gain
” and “
rotational coupling gain
”, respectively. Here,
\Omega_{j}
is a
d\times d
skew-Hermitian matrix:

\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}=-\Omega_{j},\quad j\in\mathcal{N},

where
\Omega_{j}^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian conjugate of
\Omega_{j}
.

In this paper, we are interested in the situation in which the number of particles is sufficiently large so that system (
1.2
) can be effectively approximated by the corresponding mean-field model. To be more precise, we set a phase space
\Xi
:

\Xi:=\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}\times\mathrm{Skew}_{d}\mathbb{C},\quad\mathrm{Skew}_{d}\mathbb{C}:=\{A\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}:A^{\dagger}=-A\},

and let
f=f(t,z,\Omega)
be the one-particle distribution function of the Lohe infinite ensemble at the phase space point
(z,\Omega)
at time
t
. Then, by the standard BBGKY hierarchy (see Appendix
C
), the spatial-temporal dynamics of the kinetic density function
f
is governed by the kinetic LHS model:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{z}\cdot(L[f]f)=0,\quad t>0,\quad(z,\Omega)\in\Xi,\\
\displaystyle L[f](t,z,\Omega)=\Omega z+\int_{\Xi}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]f(t,z_{*},\Omega_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}d\Omega_{*}.\end{cases}

For this kinetic model, we are interested in the following two questions:

•

(Q1) (Global well-posedness): under what conditions on system parameters and initial data, can we guarantee a global well-posedness of measure-valued solutions?

•

(Q2) (Eemergent dynamics): under what framework, can we show collective behaviors?

The main purpose of this paper is to answer the above two questions (Q1) and (Q2). In fact, our main results can be summarized as follows.

First, we provide an improved exponential aggregation estimate for the LHS model (
1.2
) under the following system parameters and initial data (see Theorem
2.2
):

(1.4)

|\kappa_{1}|<\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\max_{1\leq k,l\leq N}|1-\langle z_{k}^{0},z_{l}^{0}\rangle|<1-\frac{2|\kappa_{1}|}{\kappa_{0}}-\delta,

for a constant
\delta\in\left(0,1-\frac{2|\kappa_{1}|}{\kappa_{0}}\right)
, and let
Z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{N})
be a solution to (
2.5
). Then, one has

\max_{1\leq k,l\leq N}\|z_{k}(t)-z_{l}(t)\|\leq C(Z^{0})\exp(-\kappa_{0}\delta t),\quad t\geq 0.

Note that the condition (
1.4
) allows
\kappa_{1}
to be negative which is different from earlier results in
[
22
]
.

Second, we provide a global well-posedness of measure-valued solutions to (
1.3
). More precisely, if system parameters and initial measure
\mu_{0}
satisfy

|\kappa_{1}|<\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2},\quad 0\leq\sup_{z,w\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{0})}|1-\langle z,w\rangle|<1-\frac{2|\kappa_{1}|}{\kappa_{0}}-\delta,\quad\Omega_{j}\equiv\Omega,

for some positive constant
\delta
, then there exists a unique measure-valued solution to (
1.3
) in the whole time interval
[0,\infty)
satisfying

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{0\leq t<\infty}W_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu^{N}_{t})=0.

where
W_{2}
is the Wasserstein-2 distance (see Theorem
3.1
), and
\mu_{t}^{N}
is an empirical measure. For measure-valued solutions
\mu
and
\nu
to (
1.3
) with the initial data
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
, respectively, we derive a finite-time stability estimate (Corollary
3.1
):

W_{p}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})\leq C(T)\cdot W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),\quad t\in[0,T).

Third, we consider the case in which natural frequency matrices are the same, say
\Omega_{j}=\Omega
. In this case, thanks to the solution splitting property (see Proposition
4.1
), we can set

\rho(t,z)=\rho_{t}(z):=\pi\#f_{t}(z,\Omega),

where
\pi
is the projection map
\pi:\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}\times\mathrm{Skew}_{d}\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}
. Then,
\rho
satisfies the following continuity equation with a nonlocal flux on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{H}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{z}\cdot(L[\rho]\rho)=0,\quad t>0,\quad z\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1},\\
\displaystyle L[\rho](t,z)=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\rho(t,z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}\\
\rho(0,z)=\rho_{0}(z).\end{cases}

Fourth, we deal with the emergent dynamics of kinetic system (
1.5
). More precisely, let
\rho
be a smooth solution of system (
1.5
) and coupling strengths satisfy

\kappa_{0}>0,\quad\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1}\geq 0.

We introduce an order parameter
R
:

R(t)=\left|\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}\right|.

Note that
R
denotes the modulus of the averaged points(centroid). By direct estimates in Proposition
4.1
, one can derive monotonicity of
R^{2}
and uniform bound for the second derivatives of
R^{2}
.

\frac{dR^{2}}{dt}\geq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{0\leq t<\infty}\Big|\frac{d^{2}R^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Big|<\infty.

Then, thanks to Barbalat’s lemma
[
3
]
, one obtains the following emergent estimate (see Theorem
4.1
):

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}(\|J_{\rho}\|^{2}-|z\cdot J_{\rho}|^{2})\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}=0,\quad J_{\rho}:=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}.

Note that for a fixed
t>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}(\|J_{\rho}\|^{2}

\displaystyle-|z\cdot J_{\rho}|^{2})\rho(z)d\sigma_{z}=0

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad(\|J_{\rho}\|^{2}-|z\cdot J_{\rho}|^{2})\equiv 0\quad\text{ a.e. in supp}(\rho),

and meanwhile unit modulus of
z
and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply

|z\cdot J_{\rho}|^{2}\leq\|z\|^{2}\|J_{\rho}\|^{2}=\|J_{\rho}\|^{2},

where the equality holds if and only if
z
and
J_{\rho}
are parallel. Therefore, an emergent estimate indicates that every
z\in\text{supp}(\rho)
tends to be parallel to
J_{\rho}
, which means the emergence of either bi-polar state or complete aggregation (see Definition (
4.1
)).

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we present the Lohe Hermitian sphere(LHS) model and review the kinetic Lohe sphere(LS) model and their basic properties in relationship with well-posedness and emergent dynamics.

2.1.
The LHS model

First, we begin with the Lohe tensor(LT) model
[
22
]
which is the first-order aggregation model on the space of rank-
m
complex tensors with the same size following the presentations in
[
22
,
23
]
. This incorporates aforementioned low-rank aggregation models such as the Kuramoto model, the Lohe sphere model, and the Lohe matrix model.

For a rank-
m
complex tensor
T
with size
d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\cdots\times d_{m}
, we denote
[T]_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{m}}
by the
(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{m})
-th component of
T
. We also set
\bar{T}
to be the rank-
m
with tensor whose components are the complex conjugate of the componentess in
T
:

[\bar{T}]_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{m}}=\overline{[T]_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{m}}}.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{m}(\mathbb{C};d_{1}\cdots d_{m})
be the space of all rank-
m
complex tensors with size
d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
, For simplicity of presentation, we introduce several handy notation as follows: for
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{m}(\mathbb{C};d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m})
and
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{2m}(\mathbb{C};d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m}\times d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m})
, we set

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &[T]_{\alpha_{*}}:=[T]_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{m}},\quad[T]_{\alpha_{*0}}:=[T]_{\alpha_{10}\alpha_{20}\cdots\alpha_{m0}},\quad[T]_{\alpha_{*1}}:=[T]_{\alpha_{11}\alpha_{21}\cdots\alpha_{m1}},\\
&[T]_{\alpha_{*i_{*}}}:=[T]_{\alpha_{1i_{1}}\alpha_{2i_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{mi_{m}}},\quad[T]_{\alpha_{*(1-i_{*})}}:=[T]_{\alpha_{1(1-i_{1})}\alpha_{2(1-i_{2})}\cdots\alpha_{m(1-i_{m})}},\\
&[A]_{\alpha_{*}\beta_{*}}:=[A]_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{m}\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{m}},\quad T_{c}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}T_{k}.\end{aligned}

Now, we are ready to provide the LT model. Consider collections
\{T_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
and
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
consisting of
N
-collection of rank-
m
complex tensors with size
d_{1}\cdots d_{m}
and skew-Hermitian rank-
2m
complex tensors with size
(d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m})\times(d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m})
satisfying

[\bar{A}_{j}]_{\alpha_{*0}\alpha_{*1}}=-[A_{j}]_{\alpha_{*1}\alpha_{*0}},

for all
\alpha_{*0},\alpha_{*1}
.

Then, the component-wise form of LT model can be expressed as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dot{[T_{j}]}_{\alpha_{*0}}=[A_{j}]_{\alpha_{*0}\alpha_{*1}}[T_{j}]_{\alpha_{*1}}+\displaystyle\sum_{i_{*}\in\{0,1\}^{m}}\kappa_{i_{*}}\left([T_{c}]_{\alpha_{*i_{*}}}\bar{[T_{j}]}_{\alpha_{*1}}[T_{j}]_{\alpha_{*(1-i_{*})}}-[T_{j}]_{\alpha_{*i_{*}}}\bar{[T_{c}]}_{\alpha_{*1}}[T_{j}]_{\alpha_{*(1-i_{*})}}\right),\\
T_{j}(0)=T_{j}^{0},\quad\|T_{j}^{0}\|_{\mathrm{F}}=1,\quad j\in\mathcal{N},\quad t>0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\{\kappa_{i_{*}}\}
is a set of nonnegative coupling strengths, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Frobenius norm defined as follows:

\|T\|_{\mathrm{F}}:=\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{m}}\Big|[T]_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{m}}\Big|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Here,
A_{j}
is a rank-
2m
complex tensor with size
(d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\cdots\times d_{m})\times(d_{1}\times d_{2}\times\cdots\times d_{m})
satisfying the relation:

[\bar{A}_{j}]_{\alpha_{*0}\alpha_{*1}}=-[A_{j}]_{\alpha_{*1}\alpha_{*0}},

for all
\alpha_{*0},\alpha_{*1}
.

Note that the first and second terms in the R.H.S. of
\eqref{M-1}_{1}
represent a free flow and cubic aggregation couplings, respectively. Despite its structural complexity of (
2.1
), system (
2.1
) can exhibit emergent collective behaviors under suitable frameworks (see
[
22
]
). Among others, one of our main purpose of this paper is to derive a sufficient framework for complete aggregation of the LT model:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{i,j}\|T_{i}(t)-T_{j}(t)\|_{\mathrm{F}}=0,

under the special situation:

T_{j}=z_{j}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{1}(\mathbb{C};d)=\mathbb{C}^{d},\quad\mbox{and}\quad A_{j}=\Omega_{j}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{2}(\mathbb{C};d\times d)=\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}.

In this setting, system (
2.1
) reduces to the
Lohe Hermitian sphere
(LHS) model:

(2.3)

\begin{cases}\dot{z}_{j}=\Omega_{j}z_{j}+\kappa_{0}\Big(\langle{z_{j},z_{j}}\rangle z_{c}-\langle{z_{c},z_{j}}\rangle z_{j}\Big)+\kappa_{1}\Big(\langle{z_{j},z_{c}}\rangle-\langle{z_{c},z_{j}}\rangle\Big)z_{j},\quad t>0,\vskip 5.69046pt\\
z_{j}(0)=z_{j}^{0},\quad\|z^{0}_{j}\|=1,\quad j\in{\mathcal{N}},\end{cases}

where
z_{c}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}z_{k}
, and
\Omega_{j}
is a skew-Hermitian
d\times d
matrix. It is shown in
[
23
]
that system (
2.3
) preserves the modulus of
z_{j}
:

\|z_{j}(t)\|=1,\quad t>0,\quad j\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Next, we present results from
[
23
]
on complete aggregation for the LHS model without proofs for the comparison with result to be presented in later sections.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Complete aggregation)
[
23
]

Suppose system parameters and initial data satisfy

0<\kappa_{1}<\frac{1}{4}\kappa_{0},\quad\|z_{c}(0)\|>\frac{N-2}{N},\quad\Omega_{j}=\Omega,\quad j\in{\mathcal{N}},

and let
\{z_{j}\}
be a global solution to (
2.3
). Then complete aggregation emerges asymptotically:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{i,j}\|z_{i}(t)-z_{j}(t)\|=0.

Proof.

Detailed proof can be found in Theorem 4.1 of
[
23
]
.
∎

Next, we present an exponential aggregation estimate for the LHS model with the same free flow
\Omega_{j}=\Omega
with a relaxed sign condition on
\kappa_{1}
. For this, we define functionals
\mathcal{F}
and
{\mathcal{G}}
as follows:

\mathcal{F}:=\max_{k,l}\left|1-\langle z_{k},z_{l}\rangle\right|,\quad\mathcal{G}:=\max_{k,l}\|z_{k}-z_{l}\|.

It follows from the following inequality:

\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}=2\mathrm{Re}(1-\langle z_{i},z_{j}\rangle)\leq 2|1-\langle z_{i},z_{j}\rangle|

that

(2.4)

\mathcal{G}\leq 2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}}.

For complete aggregation, it suffices to show the exponential decay of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note that for a homogeneous ensemble, without loss of generality, we may assume
\Omega=0
(thanks to the solution splitting property) so that
z_{i}
satisfies

(2.5)

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{i}=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(z_{k}-\langle z_{k},z_{i}\rangle z_{i})+\frac{\kappa_{1}}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}(\langle z_{i},z_{k}\rangle-\langle z_{k},z_{i}\rangle)z_{i}.

Theorem 2.2

(Exponential aggregation)
.

Suppose system parameters and initial data satisfy

|\kappa_{1}|<\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2},\quad 0\leq\max_{k,l}|1-\langle z_{k}^{0},z_{l}^{0}\rangle|<1-\frac{2|\kappa_{1}|}{\kappa_{0}}-\delta,

for some positive constant
\delta
, and let
Z=(z_{1}(t),\cdots,z_{N}(t))
be a solution to (
2.5
). Then, one has

(2.6)

\mathcal{F}(t)\leq\mathcal{F}^{0}\exp\left(-2\kappa_{0}\delta t\right),\quad\mathcal{G}(t)\leq 2\sqrt{\mathcal{F}^{0}}\exp(-\kappa_{0}\delta t).

Proof.

Since the proof is rather lengthy, we leave it to Appendix
A
, whereas the second estimate in (
2.6
) follows from the first estimate and (
2.4
) directly.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

Note that the framework in this theorem allows the coupling strength
\kappa_{1}
to be negative (i.e. repulsive coupling).

2.2.
The LS model

Consider the LS model on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for rank-1 real tensors which corresponds to the real counterpart of the LHS model (
2.3
) for
z_{j}=x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.
In this case, one has

\langle{z_{j},z_{c}}\rangle-\langle{z_{c},z_{j}}\rangle=0.

Thus, system (
2.3
) can be reduced to

(2.7)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dot{x}_{j}=\Omega_{j}x_{j}+\kappa_{0}\big(\langle{x_{j},x_{j}}\rangle x_{c}-\langle{x_{c},x_{j}}\rangle x_{j}\big),\quad t>0,\vskip 5.69046pt\\
\displaystyle x_{i}(0)=x_{i}^{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\quad i\in\mathcal{N}.\end{cases}

Note that once the initial data
x^{0}_{i}
lie in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, one can see that system (
2.7
) conserves the unit modulus. Thus, system (
2.7
) can be regarded as an aggregation model on
the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

For a homogeneous ensemble with
\Omega_{k}=\Omega
for all
k\in{\mathcal{N}}
, system (
2.7
) enjoys the solution splitting property(see Lemma 2.2 and Proposition 2.3 of

[
22
]
, respectively). Without loss of generality, we can assume
\Omega=0
and system (
2.7
) can be rewritten as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{j}=\kappa_{0}\big(x_{c}-\langle{x_{c},x_{j}}\rangle x_{j}\big)=\kappa_{0}\mathbb{P}_{x_{j}^{\perp}}x_{c},

where
\mathbb{P}_{x^{\perp}}
is a projection on the orthogonal complement of a unit vector
x
given as follows:

\mathbb{P}_{x^{\perp}}u=u-\langle u,x\rangle x,\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since
\kappa_{0}
is linear in (
2.8
), we may set
\kappa_{0}=1
by time scaling if necessary. Thus,
x_{j}
satisfies

(2.9)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{j}=\mathbb{P}_{x_{j}^{\perp}}x_{c}.

Since the R.H.S. of (
2.9
) belongs to
T_{x_{j}}\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, system (
2.9
) can be regarded as a
particle
model on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, as its governing equation describes the interaction between particles on the unit sphere. Let
\rho(t,\cdot)
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then, by the BBGKY hierarchy for the particle model (
2.9
), one can derive the
continuum aggregation model
:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho\mathbb{P}_{x^{\perp}}J_{\rho})=0,\quad\text{with}\quad J_{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x\rho\mathrm{d}\sigma_{x},

where
\nabla_{x}
and
\mathrm{d}\sigma_{x}
are divergence operator and standard surface measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, respectively. Here,
\rho(t,\cdot)
can be understood as a
measure-valued
extension of
\mathcal{X}(t)=\{x_{i}(t)\}
in the sense that

(1)

For a dirac mass
\delta_{x}
concentrated at
x
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{X}(t)=\{x_{i}(t)\}\text{ is a solution of }\eqref{M-5}\\
&\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mbox{the empirical mesure}~\rho(t):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}\text{ is a measure-valued solution of }\eqref{M-6}.\end{aligned}

(2)

If
\rho
is a probability density function of
x
, then
J_{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}x\rho\mathrm{d}\sigma_{x}
can be regarded as the expected value
\mathbb{E}[x;\mathbb{S}^{d-1}]
. In this sense,
J_{\rho}
is a generalized concept of a centroid
x_{c}
for the particle model (
2.9
).

Now we present an analogue of (
2.2
). We first provide definitions of Wasserstein spaces and distances.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(\Xi,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space, and
p\in[1,\infty)
.

(1)

The Wasserstein space of order
p
on
\Xi
is defined as a collection of probability measures with a finite
p
-th moment:

\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Xi):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Xi):\langle{\mu,\|y\|^{p}}\rangle=\int_{\Xi}\|y\|^{p}\mu(dy)<\infty\right\}.

(2)

Let
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Xi)
be two measures. Then, the Wasserstein metric
W_{p}
of order
p
between
\mu
and
\nu
is given by

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}\|y-\tilde{y}\|^{p}\gamma(dyd\tilde{y})\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the collection of probability measures on
\Xi\times\Xi
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Such
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)

can be approximated by a sequence of empirical measures
\mu^{N}_{0}
of (
2.3
) in Wasserstein metric:

\lim_{N\to\infty}W_{p}(\mu_{0},\mu^{N}_{0})=0.

(2)

There exists a unique measure-valued solution
\mu
of (
3.1
) with the initial data
\mu_{0}
, and for each
t\in[0,T)
,
\mu_{t}
can be approximated by a sequence of empirical measures
\{\mu^{N}_{t}\}
of (

) in the time interval

:

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T)}W_{p}(\mu_{t},\mu^{N}_{t})=0.

Next, we are ready to state our second main result on the unique solvability of (

).

Theorem 3.1
.

The following two assertions hold.

(1)

(Finite-in-time mean field limit): For
T\in(0,\infty)
, the kinetic LHS model with identical natural frequency matrices
\Omega_{j}\equiv\Omega
is derivable from the LHS model in a finite time interval
[0,T)
in the sense of Definition
3.4
.

(2)

(Uniform-in-time mean filed limit): Suppose system parameters and initial measure
\mu_{0}
satisfy

|\kappa_{1}|<\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2},\quad 0\leq\sup_{z,w\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{0})}|1-\langle z,w\rangle|<1-\frac{2|\kappa_{1}|}{\kappa_{0}}-\delta,\quad\Omega_{j}\equiv\Omega,

for some positive constant
\delta
. Then the kinetic LHS model is derivable from the LHS model in the whole time interval
[0,\infty)
with respect to
W_{2}
-metric.

Proof.

(1) (Proof of the first statement): We modify the proof of Theorem 3 of
[
19
]
. First, we construct a sequence of empirical measures converging to a measure-valued solution for (
3.1
) in Wasserstein metric, and then,
we verify the validity and uniqueness of the measure-valued solution. Since a proof is rather lengthy, we divide its proof into two parts.

\bullet

Part A
(Construction of approximate solutions): First, we recall the result of Theorem 6.18 in
[
39
]
. For a Polish space
(X,d)
, let
\mathcal{P}(X)
be a space of probability measures on
X
, which can be equipped with Wasserstein metric from Remark
2.2
. For any given
p\in[1,\infty)
and a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, the set

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\delta_{z_{i}}~:~\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\quad 0\leq a_{n}\in\mathbb{Q},\quad\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}=1,\quad I\textrm{ is a finite subset of }\mathbb{N}\right\}

is a dense subset of
\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)
.
Therefore, we can approximate the initial measure
\mu_{0}
by a sequence of empirical measures
\{\mu_{0}^{N}\}
:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}W_{p}(\mu_{0}^{N},\mu_{0})=0,

where
\mu_{0}^{N}
is the sum of
N
suitable Dirac measures uniformly weighted by
\frac{1}{N}
. To approximate the quantity
W^{p}_{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})
, we denote

\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{z_{i_{0}}},\quad\mu_{0}^{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta_{\bar{z}_{j_{0}}}.

Since above measures are concentrated on the finite number of particles, infimum in the definition of
W_{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})
is achieved and therefore, one has

\displaystyle W^{p}_{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{ij}\|z_{i_{0}}-\bar{z}_{j_{0}}\|_{p}^{p},

for some optimal plan
(a_{ij})
satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{kj}=n,\quad\sum_{l=1}^{m}a_{il}=m,\quad 0\leq a_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\quad i,j\in\mathcal{N}.

By perturbing
a_{ij}
, we can also approximate optimal strategy by rational strategy:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{kj}=n,\quad\sum_{l=1}^{m}r_{il}=m,\quad 0\leq r_{ij}\in\mathbb{Q},\quad i,j\in\mathcal{N},

in the sense that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{ij}\|z_{i_{0}}-\bar{z}_{j_{0}}\|_{p}^{p}-W^{p}_{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})<\varepsilon,

for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
. Next, we introduce a common denominator
D

\displaystyle r_{ij}:=\frac{N_{ij}}{D},\quad N_{ij},D\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

to rewrite rational strategy:

(3.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}r_{ij}\|z_{i_{0}}-\bar{z}_{j_{0}}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{nmD}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}N_{ij}\|z_{i_{0}}-\bar{z}_{j_{0}}\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{1}{nmD}\sum_{k=1}^{mnD}\|z_{k_{0}}-\bar{z}_{k_{0}}\|_{p}^{p},\end{aligned}

where
\|z_{i_{0}}-\bar{z}_{j_{0}}\|_{p}^{p}
is counted
N_{ij}
times when
k
runs through
1
to
mnD
in the last term.

On the other hand, we can associate rational numbers
(r_{ij})
satisfying (
3.11
) to a transport plan with marginals
\mu_{t}^{n}
and
\mu_{t}^{m}
. Therefore, by the same procedure as in (
3.12
), we have

\displaystyle W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t}^{m})\leq\frac{1}{nmD}\sum_{k=1}^{mnD}\|z_{k}(t)-\bar{z}_{k}(t)\|_{p}^{p}.

Let
T\in(0,\infty)
be given. Then we use Proposition
3.2
and obtain a constant
C=\max{(G_{T},1)}
to see

(3.13)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t}^{m})&\leq\frac{1}{nmD}\sum_{k=1}^{mnD}\|z_{k}(t)-\bar{z}_{k}(t)\|_{p}^{p}\\
&\leq\frac{C^{p}}{nmD}\sum_{k=1}^{mnD}\|z_{k_{0}}-\bar{z}_{k_{0}}\|_{p}^{p}\leq C^{p}\bigg(W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})+\varepsilon\bigg),\end{aligned}

for any
t\in(0,T]
, where
\varepsilon\to 0
as
(r_{ij})\to(a_{ij})
.

Since
W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{0}^{n},\mu_{0}^{m})
can be taken arbitrarily small by enlarging
n
and
m
, we can conclude that
W_{p}(\mu_{t}^{n},\mu_{t}^{m})
is a Cauchy sequence. From the completeness of
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Xi)
(see Theorem 6.18 of
[
39
]
), we can define a measure-valued solution
\mu
with the initial data
\mu_{0}
, as a weak limit of
\mu_{t}^{N}
in the Wasserstein metric.

\diamond

Part B
(weak limit
\mu
is a unique measure-valued solution): First, we show that the defining relation
3.7
of Definition
3.3
is satisfied (see
[
21
]
for the detailed arguments). To check (
3.8
) in Definition
3.3
, we need to show that
\mu
satisfies the following equation for all the test functions
\phi\in C_{0}^{1}([0,T)\times\Xi)
:

\langle{\mu_{t},\phi(t,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{0},\phi(0,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mu_{s},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle ds,

where
L[\mu]
is defined by

L[\mu](t,z,\Omega)=\Omega z+\int_{\Xi}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]d\mu(z_{*},\Omega_{*}).

Since
\mu^{N}
is a measure-valued solution (see Remark
3.1
), one has

(3.14)

\langle{\mu^{N}_{t},\phi(t,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle-\langle{\mu^{N}_{0},\phi(0,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mu^{N}_{s},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu^{N}_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle ds.

Due to Remark
2.2
, L.H.S. of (
3.14
) becomes

\langle{\mu_{t}^{N},\phi(t,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{0}^{N},\phi(0,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle\to\langle{\mu_{t},\phi(t,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{0},\phi(0,\cdot,\cdot)}\rangle\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

For R.H.S. of (
3.14
), we claim:

(3.15)

\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu_{s}^{N}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\to\langle{\mu_{s},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\quad\text{uniformly with respect to }s.

Proof of (
3.15
)
: Again from Remark
2.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},\partial_{s}\phi}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{s},\partial_{s}\phi}\rangle\right|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

Therefore, it is enough to make the following term arbitrary small:

\displaystyle\left|\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},L[\mu_{s}^{N}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{s},L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\right|.

This can be obtained from the following estimate:

\displaystyle\left|\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},L[\mu_{s}^{N}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle-\langle{\mu_{s},L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\left|\langle{\mu_{s}^{N}-\mu_{s},L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle+\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},\left(L[\mu_{s}^{N}]-L[\mu_{s}]\right)\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\leq\left|\langle{\mu_{s}^{N}-\mu_{s},L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle\right|+\sup\left(\left(L[\mu_{s}^{N}]-L[\mu_{s}]\right)\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi\right).

Recall that
\mu_{s}^{N}
converges weakly to
\mu_{s}
and this convergence is uniform from the fact that the estimate (
3.13
) is time-invariant. Therefore, the estimate (
3.15
) is achieved and this implies

\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mu_{s}^{N},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu_{s}^{N}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle ds\to\int_{0}^{t}\langle{\mu_{s},\partial_{s}\phi+L[\mu_{s}]\cdot\nabla_{z}\phi}\rangle ds,

for any test function
\phi\in C_{0}^{1}([0,T)\times\Xi)
. Therefore we have an existence of measure-valued solution. The uniqueness can be followed by the same argument as in
[
16
]
.

(2) (Proof of the second statement): Let
\mu^{N}_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{z_{i}^{0}}
be the initial empirical measure and
\mu_{0}
be the given initial measure. Since
\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{0})
is compact, we may restrict the domain
\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}
to
\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{0})
, so that support
\mu^{N}_{0}
is a subset of
\mu_{0}
(See
[
39
]
for the detailed argument). Therefore, by a priori condition on
\mathrm{supp}(\mu_{0})
and Remark
3.2
, the estimation (
3.13
) is valid uniformly for any
t\in(0,\infty]
.
∎

As a corollary of Theorem
3.1
, one obtains a finite-time stability estimate for measure-valued solutions with respect to initial measures.

Corollary 3.1
.

(Finite-in-time stability)
For finite

, let
\mu,\nu\in C_{w}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}(\Xi))
be the measure-valued solutions to the kinetic LHS model with the initial measures
\mu_{0},\nu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\Xi)
, respectively. Then, there exists a positive constant
C=C(T)
such that

W_{p}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})\leq C(T)\cdot W_{p}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),\quad t\in[0,T).

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of (
3.13
) and the triangle inequality.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

For
p=2

N
-particle system in Proposition

. It follows that under the same assumption with Corollary

.

4.
Emergent behaviors of the kinetic LHS model

In this section, we study emergent behaviors of the kinetic LHS model. In
[
17
]
, emergent dynamics of the mean-field kinetic model for the LM model has been investigated. Although the LM model and LS model are different, we basically follow the same strategy in
[
17
]
to analyze emergent dynamics of the kinetic LHS model for a homogeneous ensemble. Consider the LHS model (
2.3
) with the same natural frequency matrix:

\Omega_{j}\equiv\Omega\quad\mbox{for all $j\in\mathcal{N}$}.

First, we define the notion of complete aggregation in a kinetic setting. Let
\pi
be the projection map
\pi:\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}\times\mathrm{Skew}_{d}\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}
. Then we define a distribution function
\rho
by the push-forward of
f
with respect to
\pi
:

\rho(t,z)=\rho_{t}(z):=\pi\#f_{t}(z,\Omega),

where
f_{t}(z,\Omega)=f(t,z,\Omega)
. For any measurable set
A\subseteq\Xi
, we have

\rho(t,A)=f(\pi^{-1}(t,A)).

Now we can write the aggregation force in terms of
\rho
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} L[f](z,\Omega)&=\Omega z+\int_{\Xi}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]f(t,z_{*},\Omega_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}d\Omega_{*}\\
&=\Omega z+\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\rho(t,z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}.\end{aligned}

Then, equation (
3.1
) can be rewritten in terms of
\rho
as follows:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{z}\cdot(L[\rho]\rho)=0,\quad t>0,\quad z\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1},\\
\displaystyle L[\rho](z)=\Omega z+\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\rho(t,z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}\\
\rho(0,z)=\rho_{0}(z),\end{cases}

where we abused the notation
L[\rho](z):=L[f](z,\Omega)
. Note that for the particle model (
2.3
), we have the following equivalent formulation of complete aggregation (
2.2
):

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{i,j}\|z_{i}(t)-z_{j}(t)\|=0\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{k}\|z_{k}(t)-z_{c}(t)\|=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\max_{k}W_{p}(\delta_{z_{k}(t)},\delta_{z_{c}(t)})=0,\end{aligned}

where the second arrow follows from
W_{p}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=\|x-y\|
.

The last formulation in (
4.2
) can be interpreted in terms of measures. This motivates the following concept of complete aggregation for the kinetic LHS model (
4.1
).

Definition 4.1
.

(Complete aggregation)
The kinetic LHS model (
4.1
) exhibits complete aggregation, if for any measure-valued solution
\rho=\rho(t,z)
of (
4.1
), there exists a time-dependent Dirac measure
\delta_{w(t)}
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}W_{2}(\rho(t,\cdot),\delta_{w(t)})=0.

Our third main result is the following result on emergent dynamics.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose coupling strengths satisfy

\kappa_{0}>0,\quad\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1}\geq 0,

and let
\rho
be a solution to (
4.10
).Then, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\Big(\|J_{\rho}\|^{2}-|z\cdot J_{\rho}|^{2}\Big)d\sigma_{z}=0,

where
J_{\rho}
is the expectation of
z
:

(4.3)

J_{\rho}=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}.

Proof.

We leave its proof in Section
4.3
.
∎

4.1.
Solution splitting property

In this subsection, we study a solution splitting property for (
4.1
). Consider the continuity equation (
4.1
):

(4.4)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}+\nabla_{z}\cdot(\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}]\tilde{\rho})=0,\quad t>0,\quad z\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1},\\
\displaystyle\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}](z)=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\tilde{\rho}(t,z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}},\\
\tilde{\rho}(0,z)=\tilde{\rho}_{0}(z).\end{cases}

In the following proposition, we clarify the solution splitting property of (
4.1
) more precisely.

Proposition 4.1
.

(Solution splitting property)

Let
\rho
be a smooth solution to (
4.1
). If we set

(4.5)

\tilde{\rho}(t,z)=\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}z),

then
\tilde{\rho}
satisfies (
4.4
).

Proof.

It follows from (
4.5
) that

\rho(t,z)=\tilde{\rho}(t,e^{-\Omega t}z).

Recall that
\rho
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{z}\cdot(L[\rho]\rho)=0,\quad t>0,\quad z\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1},\\
\displaystyle L[\rho](z)=\Omega z+\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\rho(t,z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}.\end{cases}

Then, we use (
4.5
) to see

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(t,w)&=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)\right)=\left(\Omega e^{\Omega t}w\right)\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)+\partial_{t}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w),\\
\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}](w)&=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(w_{*}-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{w,w_{*}}\rangle w-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)]\tilde{\rho}(t,w_{*})d\sigma_{w_{*}}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(w_{*}-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{w,w_{*}}\rangle w-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)]\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w_{*})d\sigma_{w_{*}}\\
&=e^{-\Omega t}\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\Big[\kappa_{0}(e^{\Omega t}w_{*}-\langle{e^{\Omega t}w_{*},e^{\Omega t}w}\rangle e^{\Omega t}w)\\
&\hskip 5.69046pt+\kappa_{1}(\langle{e^{\Omega t}w,e^{\Omega t}w_{*}}\rangle e^{\Omega t}w-\langle{e^{\Omega t}w_{*},e^{\Omega t}w}\rangle e^{\Omega t}w)\Big]\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w_{*})d\sigma_{w_{*}}.\end{aligned}

Now, we use (
4.6
) and the fact that
e^{\Omega t}
is a rotational operator which preserves the surface area (i.e. roughly
d\sigma_{w_{*}}=dS_{e^{\Omega t}w_{*}}
) to obtain

\displaystyle e^{\Omega t}\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}](e^{-\Omega t}w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(w_{*}-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{w,w_{*}}\rangle w-\langle{w_{*},w}\rangle w)]\rho(t,w_{*})d\sigma_{w_{*}},

\displaystyle=L[\rho](t,w)-\Omega w

to see

\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}](w)=e^{-\Omega t}L[\rho](t,e^{\Omega t}w)-\Omega w.

This implies

\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}]\tilde{\rho}(t,w)=e^{-\Omega t}L[\rho]\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)-\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)\Omega w.

Now we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla_{z}\cdot(\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}]\tilde{\rho})(t,w)&=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(L[\rho]\rho\right)(t,e^{\Omega t}w)\\
&-(\Omega e^{\Omega t}w)\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)-\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)\left(\nabla_{w}\cdot\Omega w\right).\end{aligned}

From
w=x+\mathrm{i}y
and simple calculations, it follows that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla_{w}\cdot\Omega w&=\nabla_{x}\cdot\mathrm{Re}(\Omega w)+\nabla_{y}\cdot\mathrm{Im}(\Omega w)\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}w_{\beta}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Im}\left(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}w_{\beta}\right)\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}x_{\beta}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Im}\left(\mathrm{i}\Omega_{\alpha\beta}y_{\beta}\right)\\
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}x_{\beta}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{\alpha}}\mathrm{Re}\left(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}y_{\beta}\right)\\
&=\mathrm{Re}(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\alpha\beta})+\mathrm{Re}(\Omega_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\alpha\beta})=2\mathrm{Re}(\Omega_{\alpha\alpha})=2\mathrm{tr}(\Omega)=0.\end{aligned}

Here we used the fact that
\Omega
is skew-Hermitian.

If we combine (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}\cdot(\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}]\tilde{\rho})(t,w)=\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(L[\rho]\rho\right)(t,e^{\Omega t}w)-(\Omega e^{\Omega t}w)\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w).

Finally, we have the following relation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(t,w)+\nabla_{z}\cdot(\tilde{L}[\tilde{\rho}]\tilde{\rho})(t,w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\left(\Omega e^{\Omega t}w\right)\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)+\partial_{t}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(L[\rho]\rho\right)(t,e^{\Omega t}w)-(\Omega e^{\Omega t}w)\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\partial_{t}\rho(t,e^{\Omega t}w)+\nabla_{z}\cdot\left(L[\rho]\rho\right)(t,e^{\Omega t}w)=0.

In the last equality, we used the fact that
\rho
is a solution of (
4.1
).
∎

4.2.
Order parameter

Recall the definition of the order parameter
R
:

(4.9)

R(t):=|J_{\rho}|=\left|\int_{\Xi}zf(t,z,\Omega)d\sigma_{z}d\Omega\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}\right|.

In the rest of this subsection, for notational simplicity, we suppress
t
dependence in
R,f
and
\rho
:

R:=R(t),\quad f(z):=f(t,z),\quad\rho(z):=\rho(t,z).

Then, we see that

\displaystyle R^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z\rho(z)d\sigma_{z}\right|^{2}=\left\langle\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z_{1}\rho(z_{1})d\sigma_{z_{1}},\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}z_{2}\rho(z_{2})d\sigma_{z_{2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint_{(\mathbb{HS}^{d-1})^{2}}\langle z_{1},z_{2}\rangle\rho(z_{1})\rho(z_{2})d\sigma_{z_{1}}d\sigma_{z_{2}}.

In what follows, we will show

\frac{dR^{2}}{dt}\geq 0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sup_{0\leq t<\infty}\Big|\frac{d^{2}R^{2}}{dt^{2}}\Big|<\infty.

Then, these estimates yield the desired estimate in Theorem
4.1
. Thanks to the solution splitting property in Proposition
4.1
, we may assume
\Omega=0
without loss of generality. Thus,
\rho
satisfies

(4.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{z}\cdot(\rho L[\rho])=0,\quad z\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1},\quad t>0,\\
L[\rho](z)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}[\kappa_{0}(z_{*}-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z,z_{*}}\rangle z-\langle{z_{*},z}\rangle z)]\rho(z_{*})d\sigma_{z_{*}}.\end{cases}

In what follows, we present three elementary lemmas for the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
z
and
w
be complex vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then, the functional introduced in (
1.6
) satisfies

\langle z,w\rangle=z\cdot w-\mathrm{i}(z\cdot(\mathrm{i}w)),

where
\mathrm{i}z:=(\mathrm{i}z^{1},\cdots,\mathrm{i}z^{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Proof.

By definitions of (

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have studied the well-posedness and emergent behaviors of the kinetic mean-field model for the LHS model which corresponds to the complex analogue of the LS model for aggregation. The LS model is the first-order aggregation model on the unit sphere in Euclidean space. The LHS model also reduces to the LS model on the unit sphere in complex Euclidean space. The kinetic LHS model can be formally obtained via the BBGKY hierarchy from the LHS model. In this work, we have discussed three issues. First, we presented exponential aggregation estimates to the LHS model with relaxed coupling strengths for some restricted class of initial data. Second, we provided a global-in-time well-posedness of measure-valued solutions to the kinetic LHS model using the particle-in-cell method and uniform-stability estimate. Third, we presented the emergent dynamics of the kinetic LHS model. In general, the particle-in-cell method provides a measure-valued solution in any finite-time interval for generic initial data. However, this type of mean-field limit cannot be extended to the whole time interval due to the lack of a suitable uniform stability estimate. As long as the initial data and system parameters satisfy some admissible conditions, we can see that the uniform stability estimate follows, and the kinetic LHS model can be derivable uniformly in time from the LHS model. Of course, there are several issues to be discussed in future work. For example, in our work, the uniform stability estimate was obtained for some admissible class of initial data and system parameters. Thus, the extension of uniform stability to a relaxed setting will remain an interesting problem. In addition, our emergent dynamics in Section
4
has been studied only for the homogeneous ensemble with the same natural frequency matrices. Clearly, extension to a heterogeneous ensemble will be interesting to pursue in future work.

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|\tilde{Z}-Z\|\leq Ae^{-Bt}\left(\kappa_{0}+2\left|\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1}|\right)\right\|\tilde{Z}-Z\|.

Finally, we apply Gronwall’s lemma to get

\|\tilde{Z}(t)-Z(t)\|\leq\exp{\left(\int_{0}^{t}Ae^{-Bs}\left(\kappa_{0}+2|\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1}|\right)ds\right)}\|\tilde{Z}(0)-Z(0)\|,\quad t\geq 0.

This completes the proof.

Appendix C
A formal derivation of the kinetic LHS model

In this appendix, we use the standard BBGKY hierarchy to derive the mean-field kinetic model of the LHS model formally (see
[
17
,
21
,
33
]
for related results). Here, we briefly summarize the main steps of the derivation for the convenience of readers.

Consider the LHS model:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle\dot{z}_{j}=\Omega_{j}z_{j}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{j}}\rangle z_{j})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{j},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{j}}\rangle)z_{j}\big),\quad t>0,\\
\dot{\Omega}_{j}=0,\quad j\in\mathcal{N}.\end{cases}

Let
f^{N}=f^{N}(t,z_{1},\Omega_{1},z_{2},\Omega_{2},\cdots,z_{N},\Omega_{N})
be the
N
-particle distribution function which is symmetric, i.e., for any permutation
s
in a symmetric group
S_{N}
, we have

f^{N}(t,z_{s(1)},\Omega_{s(1)},z_{s(2)},\Omega_{s(2)},\cdots,z_{s(N)},\Omega_{s(N)})=f^{N}(t,z_{1},\Omega_{1},z_{2},\Omega_{2},\cdots,z_{N},\Omega_{N}).

Then, it satisfies

(C.1)

\partial_{t}f^{N}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{z_{i}}\cdot\left[\left(\Omega_{i}z_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle z_{i})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{i},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle)z_{i}\right)f^{N}\right]=0.

For notational convenience, we write

Z_{i}:=(z_{i},\Omega_{i}),\quad{\mathcal{Z}}=(Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{N}),

and set the measure
dZ_{i}
on
\Xi
as follows:

dZ_{i}:=d\sigma_{z_{i}}\otimes d\Omega_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad dZ^{i}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}^{N}dZ_{k}.

Then we can simply write

Now we integrate (
C.1
) with respect to variables
Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{N}
to get

(C.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&=\partial_{t}\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}f^{N}(t,\mathcal{Z})dZ^{1}\\
&+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}\nabla_{z_{i}}\cdot\bigg(\bigg(\Omega_{i}z_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle z_{i})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{i},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle)z_{i}\bigg)f^{N}(t,\mathcal{Z})\bigg)dZ^{1}.\end{aligned}

Then, by divergence theorem on
\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}
, we have

(C.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\nabla_{z_{i}}\cdot\bigg(\Omega_{i}z_{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle z_{i})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{i},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{i}}\rangle)z_{i}f^{N}(t,z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{N})\bigg)dz_{i}\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt=0,\qquad 2\leq i\leq N.\end{aligned}

If we integrate above relation (
C.3
) over
\mathrm{Skew}_{d}\mathbb{C}\times\Xi^{N-2}
with respect to the product measure
\displaystyle d\Omega_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\ell\neq i\end{subarray}}dZ_{l}

Then, this relation simplifies the R.H.S. of (

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\text{R.H.S. of \eqref{BB1}}\\
&=\partial_{t}\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}f^{N}(t,\mathcal{Z})dZ^{1}\\

, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}\nabla_{z_{1}}\cdot\bigg(\big(\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{1}}\rangle z_{1})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{1},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{1}}\rangle)z_{1}\big)f^{N}(t,Z_{1},Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{k},\cdots,Z_{N})\bigg)dZ^{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}\nabla_{z_{1}}\cdot\bigg(\big(\kappa_{0}(z_{2}-\langle{z_{2},z_{1}}\rangle z_{1})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{1},z_{2}}\rangle-\langle{z_{2},z_{1}}\rangle)z_{1}\big)f^{N}(t,Z_{1},Z_{k},\cdots,Z_{2},\cdots,Z_{n})\bigg)dZ^{1}

(C.5)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}\nabla_{z_{1}}\cdot\bigg(\big(\kappa_{0}(z_{k}-\langle{z_{k},z_{1}}\rangle z_{1})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{1},z_{k}}\rangle-\langle{z_{k},z_{1}}\rangle)z_{1}\big)f^{N}(t,\mathcal{Z})\bigg)dZ^{1}\\

From this definition, we can obtain the following:

(C.6)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Xi^{N-1}}\nabla_{z_{1}}\cdot\bigg(\big(\kappa_{0}(z_{2}-\langle{z_{2},z_{1}}\rangle z_{1})+\kappa_{1}(\langle{z_{1},z_{2}}\rangle-\langle{z_{2},z_{1}}\rangle)z_{1}\big)f^{N}(t,{\mathcal{Z}})\bigg)dZ^{1}\\

Below, we estimate
\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}

Appendix D
Proof of the second statement of Lemma
4.4

Recall that the order parameter

is given by

This yields

\frac{d^{2}R^{2}}{dt^{2}}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(2J_{\rho}\cdot\frac{dJ_{\rho}}{dt}\right)=2\left\|\frac{dJ_{\rho}}{dt}\right\|^{2}+2J_{\rho}\cdot\frac{d^{2}J_{\rho}}{dt^{2}}.

Next, we estimate the first and second derivatives of
J_{\rho}
. Let

be an arbitrary unit vector.

\bullet
Case A (Boundedness of
\Big|\frac{dJ_{\rho}}{dt}\Big|)
: It follows from (
4.11
) that

Then, we use
\|J_{\rho}\|\leq 1
to obtain the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &|\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(e)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(J_{\rho})|\leq\|\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(e)\|\cdot\|\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(J_{\rho})\|\leq\|e\|\cdot\|J_{\rho}\|\leq 1,\\
&|\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(e)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{i}z}(J_{\rho})|=\|\mathbb{P}_{z^{\perp}}(e)\|\cdot\|\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{i}z}(J_{\rho})\|\leq\|e\|\cdot\|J_{\rho}\|\leq 1.\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\left|e\cdot\frac{dJ_{\rho}}{dt}\right|

\mathcal{L}_{2}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\|L[\rho]\|\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\|\kappa_{0}(J_{\rho}-(J_{\rho}\cdot z)z)+(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})((\mathrm{i}z)\cdot J_{\rho})(\mathrm{i}z)\|\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}}\big(\kappa_{0}\|J_{\rho}-(J_{\rho}\cdot z)z\|+(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})\|((\mathrm{i}z)\cdot J_{\rho})(\mathrm{i}z)\|\big)\rho(t,z)d\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa_{0}+2(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})=4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}).

\diamond
Case B.3 (Estimate of
\mathcal{L}_{4}
): Similar to the previous cases,
|\mathcal{L}_{4}|
is bounded as follows:

|\mathcal{L}_{4}|\leq 4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}).

If we sum-up all the estimates of
\mathcal{L}_{j}
(
j=1,2,3,4
), we have

\displaystyle\left|e\cdot\frac{d^{2}J_{\rho}}{dt^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{0}|\mathcal{K}_{1}|+(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})|\mathcal{K}_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{0}(|\mathcal{L}_{1}|+|\mathcal{L}_{2}|)+(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})(|\mathcal{L}_{3}|+|\mathcal{L}_{4}|)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{0}(2M(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})+4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}))+(\kappa_{0}+2\kappa_{1})(2M(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})+4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}))

\displaystyle=4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})^{2}(M+2).

Since above inequality holds for any unit vector
e\in\mathbb{HS}^{d-1}
, we have

\left\|\frac{d^{2}J_{\rho}}{dt^{2}}\right\|\leq 4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})^{2}(M+2).

Finally, we have a uniform boundedness for the second derivative of
R
:

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}R^{2}}{dt^{2}}

\displaystyle=2\left\|\frac{dJ_{\rho}}{dt}\right\|^{2}+2J_{\rho}\cdot\frac{d^{2}J_{\rho}}{dt^{2}}\leq\big(2(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})\big)^{2}+4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})^{2}(M+2)

\displaystyle=4(\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1})^{2}(M+3).

This completes the proof.
