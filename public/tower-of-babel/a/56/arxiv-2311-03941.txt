Dimension-independent weak value estimation via controlled SWAP operations
Giulio Chiribella, Kyrylo Simonov, Xuanqiang Zhao

Abstract
Weak values of quantum observables are a powerful tool for investigating quantum phenomena. Some methods for measuring weak values in the laboratory require weak interactions and postselection, while others are deterministic, but require statistics over a number of experiments that grows linearly with the dimension of the measured system in the worst case over all possible observables. Here we propose a deterministic dimension-independent scheme for estimating weak values of arbitrary observables. The scheme is based on controlled SWAP operations, and associates states and observables in the mathematical expression of the weak value to preparations devices and measurements devices in the experimental setup, respectively. Thanks to this feature, it provides insights into the relation between states of two identical quantum systems at a single moment of time and states of a single quantum system at two moments of time, also known as two-time states. Specifically, our scheme provides an alternative expression for two-time states, and establishes a link between two-time states accessible through the controlled- SWAP scheme and bipartite quantum states with positive partial transpose.



I
Introduction

In a seminal 1988 paper
Aharonov et al. 1988
, Aharonov, Albert, and Vaidman introduced the notion of weak values and showed that they can be experimentally accessed by letting the measured system interact weakly with a pointer in the time interval between a pre-selection and a post-selection. Since then, weak values have proven a powerful tool for analyzing a broad spectrum of quantum phenomena
Aharonov et al. 2010
;
Shikano 2012
;
Kofman et al. 2012
;
Dressel et al. 2014
. On the foundational side, they provide a lens for experimentally investigating quantum paradoxes
Aharonov et al. 2002
;
Lundeen and Steinberg 2009
;
Kocsis et al. 2011
and the behaviour of quantum systems in time
Williams and Jordan 2008
;
Dressel et al. 2011
;
Goggin et al. 2011
;
Groen et al. 2013
, and they also serve as a quantitative indicator of non-classicality
Pusey 2014
;
Dressel et al. 2014
;
Dressel 2015
;
Kunjwal et al. 2019
. On the applied side, they provide probabilistic amplification techniques for quantum metrology
Hosten and Kwiat 2008
;
Dixon et al. 2009
;
Brunner and Simon 2010
;
Starling et al. 2010a
;
Starling et al. 2010b
;
Hofmann 2011
;
Hofmann et al. 2012
;
Strübi and Bruder 2013
and for the direct measurement of quantum states
Lundeen et al. 2011
;
Lundeen and Bamber 2012
;
Salvail et al. 2013
;
Malik et al. 2014
.

Several experimental schemes for measuring weak values have been proposed and demonstrated in the laboratory. The first schemes, based on the original definition of weak values, involve weak measurement interactions and post-selection (see
Dressel et al. 2014
for a review). More recently, there has been a growing interest in methods for estimating weak values without post-selection and weak couplings
Johansen 2007
;
Hiley 2012
;
Vallone and Dequal 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Denkmayr et al. 2017
;
Cohen and Pollak 2018
;
Denkmayr et al. 2018
;
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Wagner et al. 2024
. These schemes are conceptually interesting because they disentangle the notion of weak value from the notion of weak measurements, and expand the scope of application of the notion of weak value by linking it to fundamental protocols in quantum information and computation. For example, an ingenious method was developed by Hoffman
Hofmann 2012
, who showed that weak values can be estimated by performing standard measurements on the outputs of the universal quantum cloning machine
Bužek and Hillery 1996
;
Gisin and Massar 1997
;
Werner 1998
. Another method was proposed by Wagner
et al

Wagner et al. 2024
, based on a quantum algorithm called the cyclic shift test
Oszmaniec et al. 2024
.

A limitation of the existing deterministic estimation protocols, however, is that the number of samples needed to accurately estimate the weak value generally grows linearly in the system dimension
d
. For example, the cloning method
Hofmann 2012
provides an estimate of the weak value multiplied by a term of order
1/d
. Hence, obtaining a reliable estimate requires a number of repetitions of the experiment growing as
d
. For a quantum system composed of
n
particles, this scaling results into an exponential increase of the sample complexity, that is, the number of samples needed to achieve a desired level of accuracy of estimation. A similar issue arises in the cyclic test approach
Wagner et al. 2024
, whose sample complexity grows with
d
in the worst case over all possible observables.

In this paper, we provide a deterministic method for estimating weak values with dimension-independent sample complexity. In our method, two identical quantum systems are prepared in the initial and final states appearing in the definition of weak value. Then, the two systems undergo a controlled-
SWAP
operation, which exchanges them or leaves them unchanged depending on the quantum state of a control system, as illustrated in Figure
1
. After the controlled-
SWAP
operation, the two systems undergo a possibly noisy measurement, whose outcomes are used to estimate the weak value.

The controlled-
SWAP
scheme studied in this paper also provides insights into the theory of two-time states, a generalized type of states that describe quantum systems subject to both pre- and post-selections
Aharonov et al. 1964
;
Aharonov and Vaidman 1991
;
Silva et al. 2014
. We show that the expectation values associated to two-time states can be obtained from the expectation values associated to bipartite density matrices, by applying a linear fractional transformation that involves a
SWAP
operation and a partial transpose. Using this result, we show that the controlled-
SWAP
method can be used to estimate the expectation values of all two-time states associated to density matrices with positive partial transpose (PPT)
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
. Based on this fact, we discuss several adaptations of the controlled-
SWAP
method that allow to access the expectation values for two-time states associated to non-PPT states.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, we briefly review the notion of weak value and put forward a dimension-independent estimation scheme based on controlled-
SWAP
operations. In Section
III
, we develop several generalizations of our scheme and introduce a new quantity dubbed the “double weak value”. In Section
IV
, we provide the reader with an overview of the theory of two-time states. The matrix representation of two-time states is discussed in Section
V
. In Section
VI
, we highlight a fundamental connection between two-time states and the developed estimation scheme. Finally, we provide conclusions and an outlook in Section
VII
.

II
Weak value estimation

In this section we provide a brief introduction to weak values, and present a deterministic scheme for estimating weak values with dimension-independent sample complexity.

II.1
Introduction to weak values

Weak values were introduced by Aharonov, Albert and Vaidman, as part of a framework describing pre- and post-selected ensembles
Aharonov et al. 1988
. Their original definition referred to the scenario where a quantum system
S
is pre-selected and post-selected in two pure states, described by rays in the system’s Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Given two (generally unnormalized) vectors
|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
and
|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying the condition
\langle\psi_{\rm in}|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\not=0
, the weak value of an observable
A
is defined as

\displaystyle W(A\,|\,\psi_{\rm in},\psi_{\rm fin}):=\frac{\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|A|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle}\,.

(1)

Here the vector
|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle
(
|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle
) represents the initial (final) state of the system. The observable
A
is typically taken to be a self-adjoint operator, but more generally could be any linear operator acting on
\mathcal{H}
. Hereafter, the algebra of all linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
will be denoted by
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
.

More recently, the notion of weak value was generalized to mixed states
Silva et al. 2014
;
Vaidman et al. 2017
: for a pair of density matrices
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
satisfying the condition
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]\not=0
, the weak value is defined as

\displaystyle W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,A\,\rho_{\rm in}]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,\rho_{\rm in}]}\,.

(2)

Eq. (
1
) can be obtained as a special case of Eq. (
2
) by setting
\rho_{\rm in}=|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm in}|
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|
. Physically, the mixed-state weak value (
2
) can be reduced to the pure-state weak value (
1
) through a purification procedure presented in Ref.
Vaidman et al. 2017
.

An important question is how to measure weak values. In their seminal paper
Aharonov et al. 1988
, Aharonov, Albert, and Vaidman provided an experimental scheme using weak interactions and postselection. A number of other schemes was subsequently devised by other authors
Ritchie et al. 1991
;
Pryde et al. 2005
;
Hosten and Kwiat 2008
;
Dixon et al. 2009
;
Brunner and Simon 2010
;
Goggin et al. 2011
;
Lundeen et al. 2011
;
Li et al. 2011
;
Dressel and Jordan 2012
;
Xu et al. 2013
;
Magaña-Loaiza et al. 2014
;
Piacentini et al. 2016
;
Vaidman 2017
;
Cohen and Pollak 2018
;
Rebufello et al. 2021
;
Adam and Diósi 2023
;
Wiseman et al. 2023
.

The most recent scheme was presented in Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024
. In its simplest version, the scheme is defined for pure states
\rho_{\rm in}=|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm in}|
,
\rho_{\rm fin}=|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|
, and rank-one observables
A=|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
. Here, all the vectors
|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle,|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle,
and
|\alpha\rangle
are taken to have unit length. The protocol consists of two subprotocols: a cyclic test
Oszmaniec et al. 2024
for estimating the trace
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,\rho_{\rm in}]
, and a
SWAP
test
Barenco et al. 1997
;
Buhrman et al. 2001
for estimating the trace
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\rho_{\rm in}]
. The extension to mixed states is straightforward: since both the cyclic and
SWAP
tests are linear in the density matrix, they can be used to estimate the traces
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,\rho_{\rm in}]
and
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,\rho_{\rm in}]
for arbitrary
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
. The extension to general self-adjoint observables
A
is less direct, because, in the cyclic test, the rank-one observable
|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
is treated as a quantum state, and, therefore, it cannot be directly replaced by a general observable. Instead, one can use any diagonalization of the operator
A
, of the form
A=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\,a_{i}\,|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|
where
r
is the rank of
A
,
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
are (possibily degenerate) non-zero eigenvalues, and
\{|\alpha_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{r}
is an orthonormal basis for the support of
A
. Using this decomposition, one can then compute the weak value of
A
as a linear combination of the weak values of the rank-one observables
A_{i}:=|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|
. A limitation of this approach, however, is that in general the rank of the observable
A
can be as large as
d
, and therefore the number of experimental settings needed to estimate the weak value of
A
grows with the system’s dimension in the worst case. For a system of
n
particles, the number of settings for the estimation of a generic weak value grows exponentially with
n
.

II.2
The controlled-
SWAP
protocol

We now provide a way to estimate weak values of arbitrary observables in a dimension-independent way. Our scheme applies also to infinite-dimensional quantum systems, and does not require ideal projective measurements. A related scheme
Huang et al. 2023
was recently proposed in a different context, focusing on the study of non-Hermitian quantum mechanics and without making a connection to weak values.

To estimate the weak value
W(A|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
in Eq. (
2
) we employ two copies of the system, initialized in the states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
, respectively. To measure the observable
A
, we allow for a generally noisy measurement device, described by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), that is, a collection of operators
(P_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
satisfying the conditions
P_{j}\geq 0\,\forall j
and
\sum_{j}P_{j}=I
. The outcomes of the measurement are associated to the possible values
(x_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
of the observable
A
. We take the measurement to be unbiased, meaning that the expectation value of
A
on an arbitrary quantum state
\rho
is equal to the average of the values
(x_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
with respect to the probability distribution
p(j|\rho):=\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho]
,
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. In formula,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[A\rho]=\sum_{j}\,x_{j}\,\,\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho]\qquad\forall\rho\,,

(3)

or, more compactly,

\displaystyle A=\sum_{j}\,x_{j}\,P_{j}\,.

(4)

An example of POVM that satisfies the unbiasedness condition (
4
) is a noisy measurement of
A
. Given the canonical spectral decomposition
A=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\,Q_{j}
where
(a_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
are
N
distinct eigenvalues, and
(Q_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
are the projectors on the corresponding eigenspaces, a noisy measurement of the observable
A
can be defined as a POVM with operators
P_{j}=(1-p)\,Q_{j}+p\,\lambda_{j}\,I
, where
p\in[0,1)
is a probability quantifying the amount of noise,
(\lambda_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
is a probability distribution, and
j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. In this case, Eq. (
4
) is satisfied by the assignment
x_{j}:=(a_{j}-p\,\overline{a})/(1-p)
, with
\overline{a}:=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,\lambda_{j}\,a_{j}
.

The operators
P_{j}
can be used to define a function from measurement outcomes to complex numbers, given by

\displaystyle q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin}):=\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\,\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]\,.

(5)

Mathematically, this function is a complex measure
Axler 2020
, normalized as
\sum_{j}q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]
. We call this measure the
weak value (WV) measure
.

When condition Eq. (
4
) is satisfied, the weak value (
2
) can be written as the ratio of two expectation values with respect to the WV measure: the expectation value
\mathbb{E}_{q}(X):=\sum_{j}\,x_{j}\,q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
of the random variable
X
and the expectation value
\mathbb{E}_{q}(Y):=\sum_{j}\,q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
of the constant random variable
Y
with values
y_{j}=1\,\forall j
; in formula,

\displaystyle W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q}(X)}{\mathbb{E}_{q}(Y)}\,.

(6)

In general, the WV measure
q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
is not a probability measure, and therefore
\mathbb{E}_{q}(X)
and
\mathbb{E}_{q}(Y)
are not proper expectation values. Nevertheless, we now show that every expectation value with respect to the WV measure can be evaluated by computing expectations of suitable random variables with respect to a proper probability distribution. To this purpose, we use the following protocol.

Protocol 1
Sampling from the WV measure

Inputs:
Two copies
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
of the system, prepared in the states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
, respectively, an auxiliary qubit
C
prepared in the state
|+\rangle\langle+|
with
|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, and a measurement device described by the POVM
(P_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
.

Protocol:

1.

Apply the controlled
SWAP
gate

U=I_{S_{1}}\otimes I_{S_{2}}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{C}+{\tt SWAP}_{S_{1}S_{2}}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{C}\,,

(7)

where
{\tt SWAP}_{S_{1}S_{2}}
is the
SWAP
gate, uniquely defined by the relation
{\tt SWAP}_{S_{1}S_{2}}(|\alpha\rangle_{S_{1}}\otimes|\beta\rangle_{S_{2}})=|\beta\rangle_{S_{1}}\otimes|\alpha\rangle_{S_{2}}
, for arbitrary vectors
|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta\rangle
.

2.

Measure
S_{1}
with the POVM
(P_{j})_{j}
.

3.

Measure
C
with the four-outcome POVM
(R_{c})_{c=0}^{3}
with

\displaystyle R_{0}=\frac{1}{2}|+\rangle\langle+|\,,

\displaystyle R_{1}=\frac{1}{2}|-\rangle\langle-|\,

\displaystyle R_{2}=\frac{1}{2}|+i\rangle\langle+i|\,,

\displaystyle R_{3}=\frac{1}{2}|-i\rangle\langle-i|\,,

(8)

where
|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, and
|\pm i\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm i|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. Physically, the POVM
(R_{c})_{c}
can be realized as a random measurement, by measuring either on the basis
\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}
or on the basis
\{|+i\rangle,|-i\rangle\}
.

Output:
The protocol produces a pair of outcomes
(j,c)
, distributed with probability

\displaystyle p(j,c|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})

\displaystyle\qquad:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[(P_{j}\otimes I\otimes R_{c})\,U(\rho_{\rm in}\otimes\rho_{\rm fin}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|)U^{\dagger}\right]

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{8}\Big\{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\,\rho_{\rm in}]+\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\;2\theta(1-c)\,(-1)^{c}\,{\rm Re}\left(\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\;2\theta(c-2)\,(-1)^{c}\,{\rm Im}\left(\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]\right)\Big\}\,,

(10)

where
\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function, defined as
\theta(t)=1
for
t\geq 0
and
\theta(t)=0
for
t<0
. The derivation of Eq. (
10
) is provided in Appendix
A
.

Expectation values with respect to the weak value measure (
5
) can then be obtained from expectation values with respect to the probability distribution (
10
) generated by the above protocol. The explicit recipe is provided by the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

For every random variable
Z:j\mapsto z_{j}
, the expectation value of
Z
with respect to the WV measure
q(j|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
in Eq. (
5
) coincides with the expectation value of the random variable
\widetilde{Z}:(j,c)\mapsto\widetilde{z}_{j,c}
defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{z}_{j,c}:=2z_{j}\,(-1)^{c}\,\Big[\,\theta(1-c)-i\theta(c-2)\Big]

(11)

with respect to the probability distribution
p(j,c|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
in Eq. (
10
).

The proof of the lemma is provided in Appendix
B
. This lemma, combined with Eq. (
6
), implies that the weak value
W(A|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
can be estimated by computing the empirical average of the random variables
X
and
Y
with respect to the experimental frequencies generated by Protocol
II.2
. The sample complexity of the protocol is provided by the following:

Theorem 1
.

The weak value
W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
of an observable
A=\sum_{j}x_{j}P_{j}
with a POVM
(P_{j})_{j}
and real coefficients
\{x_{j}\}_{j}
can be estimated up to a small additive error
\epsilon
with a probability no less than
1-\delta
by using
K
copies of the states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
, with
K
given by

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\ln\Bigl(\frac{6}{\delta}\Bigr)}{\epsilon^{2}}\Biggl(\frac{x_{\rm max}+|W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})|}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]}\Biggr)^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+\;O\left(\frac{\ln\Bigl(\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr)}{\epsilon}\frac{x_{\rm max}+|W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})|}{(\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}])^{2}}\right)

where
x_{\rm max}\coloneqq\max_{j}|x_{j}|
.

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.

\blacksquare

Note that, when the POVM
(P_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
is the spectral decomposition of the observable
A
,
x_{\max}
is just the eigenvalue of
A
with maximum modulus, equal to the norm
\|A\|=\sup_{\||\psi\rangle\|=1}\,\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle
.

Theorem
1
provides the asymptotic scaling of the sample complexity for small
\epsilon
: it identifies the leading order in
1/\epsilon
for every fixed value of
\delta
and of the overlap
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]
. With respect to the overlap, the scaling
1/(\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}])^{2}
is the same as the scaling in the protocol of Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024
. An important difference, however, is that the sample complexity in Theorem
1
does not depend on the dimension of the system. As a consequence, Protocol
II.2
provides a reduction of the sample complexity with respect to the protocol proposed of Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024
whenever the observable
A
has rank larger than one.

From the experimental point of view, Protocol
II.2
provides a simplification with respect to the protocol of Ref.
Wagner et al. 2024
, in that it only requires a controlled-
SWAP
operation, instead of controlled shift operations, whose basic implementation consists of a sequence of controlled
SWAP
s
Wagner et al. 2024
. Since the
\tt SWAP
gate can be implemented as a sequence of three
\tt CNOT
gates
Lee et al. 2006
, the controlled
\tt SWAP
gate can be similarly realized as a sequence of three controlled-
CNOT
gates, also known as Toffoli gates. In the circuit model of quantum computing, the controlled
SWAP
gate is also known as the Fredkin gate and recently has been experimentally implemented with photonic qubits
Patel et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2021
.

It is also interesting to compare the sample complexity of our protocol with the sample complexity of the traditional measurement scheme involving weak interactions and postselection. When the initial and final states are pure, the sample complexity was evaluated in Refs.
Kofman et al. 2012
;
Wagner et al. 2024
, and was shown to scale inverse linearly with the overlap
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]=|\langle\psi_{\rm in}|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle|^{2}
. In this case, the weak measurement scheme appears to have lower sample complexity whenever
\rho_{\rm in}\not=\rho_{\rm fin}
. In the mixed state case, however, the situation is generally different. A weak measurement scheme for measuring mixed-state weak values was provided by Vaidman
et al

Vaidman et al. 2017
, who showed that every mixed-state weak value can be reduced to a pure-state weak value by introducing three auxiliary systems, of the same dimension of the original system
S
and hereafter denoted by
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, and
A_{3}
. Specifically, the purification recipe by Vaidman
et al
can be summarized by the following formula:

\displaystyle W(A|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=W(A\otimes I_{A_{1}}\otimes I_{A_{2}}\otimes I_{A_{3}}|\Psi_{\rm in},\Psi_{\rm fin}),

(13)

where
I_{A_{1}},I_{A_{2}},I_{A_{3}}
denotes the identity operator on the auxiliary systems
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}
, respectively, and
\Psi_{\rm in}
and
\Psi_{\rm fin}
are the pure states associated to the vectors

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\rm in}\rangle_{SA_{1}A_{2}A_{3}}

\displaystyle=|\Psi\rangle_{SA_{1}}\otimes|\Xi\rangle_{A_{2}A_{3}}

\displaystyle|\Psi_{\rm fin}\rangle_{SA_{1}A_{2}A_{3}}

\displaystyle=|\Phi\rangle_{SA_{2}}\otimes|\Gamma\rangle_{A_{1}A_{3}}\,,

(14)

where, in turn, the vectors

\displaystyle|\Psi\rangle_{SA_{1}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{m}\sqrt{r_{m}}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle_{S}\otimes|m\rangle_{A_{1}}

\displaystyle|\Phi\rangle_{SA_{2}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}^{\prime}}\,|\psi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle_{S}\otimes|n\rangle_{A_{2}}\,,

(15)

are purifications of the states
\rho_{\rm in}=\sum_{m}\,r_{m}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\psi_{m}|
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=\sum_{n}\,r_{n}^{\prime}\,|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|
, respectively, and the vectors
|\Xi\rangle_{A_{2}A_{3}}
and
|\Gamma\rangle_{A_{1}A_{3}}
are defined as

\displaystyle|\Xi\rangle_{A_{2}A_{3}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\,|j\rangle_{A_{2}}\otimes|j\rangle_{A_{3}}

(16)

\displaystyle|\Gamma\rangle_{A_{1}A_{3}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]}}\sum_{m,n}\,\sqrt{r_{m}\,r_{n}^{\prime}}\,\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|\psi_{m}\rangle\,|m\rangle_{A_{1}}\otimes|n\rangle_{A_{3}}\,.

(17)

The validity of Eq. (
13
) can be checked straightforwardly from the definitions.
With the above choice of purification, the overlap between the initial and final states is
\operatorname{Tr}[\Psi_{\rm in}\Psi_{\rm fin}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]/d
. Hence, the calculation of Refs.
Kofman et al. 2012
;
Wagner et al. 2024
implies that the sample complexity scales as
d/\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]
. Comparing this scaling with the
1/(\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}])^{2}
scaling of our protocol, we obtain that the weak measurement approach by Vaidman
et al
has lower sample complexity when
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]
is small compared to
1/d
, but higher sample complexity when
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}]
is large compared to
1/d
. Besides the different scalings of the sample complexity, it is also important to observe that our scheme and the scheme by Vaidman
et al
use different ingredients: our scheme uses access to the states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
, while the scheme by Vaidman
et al
uses access to the pure state
|\Psi_{\rm in}\rangle
and to a measurement that projects onto the pure state
|\Psi_{\rm fin}\rangle
. Both state and measurement include three auxiliary systems in addition to the system on which the weak value is defined, and preparing the state
|\Psi_{\rm in}\rangle
requires access to a purification of the state
\rho_{\rm in}
, which a strictly stronger resource compared to access to a device that prepares
\rho_{\rm in}
.

Finally, another interesting aspect of Protocol
II.2
is that its operational setup mirrors the mathematical expression of the weak value: the two states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
in the mathematical expression
W(A|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,A\,\rho_{\rm in}]/\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\rho_{\rm in}]
correspond to two state preparations in the setup, and the observable
A
corresponds to the measurement of an observable in the setup. In this aspect, Protocol
II.2
differs from other protocols, where the observable
A
sometimes corresponds to the generator of a unitary dynamics (as in the protocols based on weak interactions) or to the preparation of a quantum state (as in the cyclic-shift protocol of
Wagner et al. 2024
). Later in the paper, we will see that the correspondence between states and observables in the mathematical expression of weak value on one hand, and state preparations and measurement devices in the laboratory on the other hand enables a connection between bipartite quantum states and two-time states.

III
Variants of the controlled-
SWAP
protocol

The controlled-
SWAP
protocol presented in the previous section lends itself to several generalizations, discussed in this section. These generalizations allow one to estimate another type of quantities, which we call “double weak values.”

III.1
Two local measurements

A first generalization of Protocol
II.2
is to measure both systems
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1(b)
. Measuring two observables
A
and
B
on systems
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively, gives rise to the probability distribution

\displaystyle p(j,k,c|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})

\displaystyle\quad:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[(P_{j}\otimes Q_{k}\otimes R_{c})\,U(\rho_{\rm in}\otimes\rho_{\rm fin}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|)U^{\dagger}\right]\,,

(18)

where
(P_{j})_{j}
and
(Q_{k})_{k}
are the POVMs associated to observables
A
and
B
, respectively.

By sampling over this probability distribution, one can estimate the averages of arbitrary random variables with respect to the complex measure

\displaystyle q(j,k\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin}):=\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho_{\rm in}\,Q_{k}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]\,,

(19)

thereby estimating all quantities of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[A\,\rho_{\rm in}\,B\,\rho_{\rm fin}]\,,

(20)

for arbitrary observables
A
and
B
in the linear span of
\{P_{i}\}_{i}
and
\{Q_{j}\}_{j}
, respectively.

These quantities give rise to a generalization of the notion of weak value:

Definition 1
.

The double weak value of a pair of observables
A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
and
B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
with respect to the initial state
\rho_{\rm in}
and the final state
\rho_{\rm fin}
is the quantity

\displaystyle W_{2}(A,B\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin}):=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[A\,\rho_{\rm in}\,B\,\rho_{\rm fin}]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]}\,.

(21)

The double weak value generalizes the standard weak value (
2
), whose expression can be retrieved from Eq. (
21
) by setting
B=I
; in short, one has

\displaystyle W(A\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=W_{2}(A,I\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})\qquad\forall A,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,.

(22)

The double weak value has an interesting physical interpretation. For pure states
\rho_{\rm in}=|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm in}|:=\psi_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|:=\psi_{\rm fin}
, one has

\displaystyle W_{2}(A,B\,|\,\psi_{\rm in},\psi_{\rm fin})=W(A|\psi_{\rm in},\psi_{\rm fin})~W(B|\psi_{\rm fin},\psi_{\rm in})\,,

(23)

meaning that the double weak value is the product of the weak value of
A
in the forward time direction, with initial state
\psi_{\rm in}
and final state
\psi_{\rm fin}
, and the weak value of
B
in the backward time direction, with initial state
\psi_{\rm fin}
and final state
\psi_{\rm in}
.

For general mixed states
\rho_{\rm in}=\sum_{m}\,r_{m}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\psi_{m}|
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=\sum_{n}\,r^{\prime}_{n}\,|\psi^{\prime}_{n}\rangle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{n}|
, the double weak value quantifies the correlations between the weak values of
A
in the forward time direction and the weak values of
B
in the backward time direction; in formula:

\displaystyle W_{2}(A,B\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=\sum_{m,n}\,p(m,n)\,W(A|\psi_{m},\psi_{n}^{\prime})~W(B|\psi_{n}^{\prime},\psi_{m})\,,

(24)

where
p(m,n)
is the probability distribution

\displaystyle p(m,n):=\frac{r_{m}r^{\prime}_{n}\,|\langle\psi_{m}|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}}{\sum_{i,j}r_{i}r^{\prime}_{j}\,|\langle\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle|^{2}}\,.

(25)

The above expression provides an alternative way to estimate the double weak value, by sampling over pairs consisting of a pure initial state
\psi_{m}
and a pure final state
\psi_{n}^{\prime}
, according to the probability distribution
p(m,n)
.

Recalling that the weak values were originally defined in terms of weak measurement processes, the double weak value in Eq. (
24
) can be interpreted as the correlation between the quantities observed in two weak measurement processes where the roles of the pre- and post-selections are exchanged. These correlations would appear in an exotic scenario where two different agents operate in two opposite time directions
Chiribella and Liu 2022
, with one agent preparing inputs in the past and selecting outputs in the future, and the other agent preparing inputs in the future and selecting outputs in the past. In this setting, the double weak value is the correlation between the values observed by the two agents. It is quite remarkable that such exotic correlations can be experimentally observed through the controlled-
SWAP
protocol.

Yet another way to measure the double weak value for mixed states is to use a purification approach, in a similar vein as it was proposed by Vaidman
et al
for the mixed-state weak values
Vaidman et al. 2017
. Here, we show that the double weak value for mixed states can be reduced to the (standard) weak value for pure states. To this purpose, we consider two copies of the system of interest, denoted by
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, respectively, and two auxiliary systems,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, of the same dimension of the system. Explicitly, the reduction is provided by the following relation:

\displaystyle W_{2}(A,B\,|\,\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})=W(A\otimes B\otimes I_{A_{1}}\otimes I_{A_{2}}\,|\,\Gamma_{\rm in},\Gamma_{\rm fin})\,,

(26)

where
\Gamma_{\rm in}
and
\Gamma_{\rm fin}
are projectors onto the unit vectors

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{\rm in}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Psi\rangle_{S_{1}A_{1}}\otimes|\Phi\rangle_{S_{2}A_{2}}

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{\rm fin}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Phi\rangle_{S_{1}A_{2}}\otimes|\Psi\rangle_{S_{2}A_{1}}\,,

(27)

|\Psi\rangle:=\sum_{m}\sqrt{r_{m}}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle\otimes|m\rangle

and
|\Phi\rangle:=\sum_{n}\sqrt{r_{n}^{\prime}}\,|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\otimes|n\rangle
being purifications of the states
\rho_{\rm in}=\sum_{m}\,r_{m}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\psi_{m}|
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=\sum_{n}\,r_{n}^{\prime}\,|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|
, respectively. The validity of Eq. (
26
) can be checked straightforwardly from the above definitions.

It is worth noting that setting
B=I
in Eq. (
26
) provides a new way to reduce the mixed-state weak value
W(A|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
to a pure-state weak value. This purification scheme is different from the scheme by Vaidman
et al
, as it involves different pre- and post-selections. Our scheme also implies a way to measure the mixed-state weak value
W(A|\rho_{\rm in},\rho_{\rm fin})
through weak interactions, using pre- and post-selection on the states
\Gamma_{\rm in}
and
\Gamma_{\rm fin}
, respectively. Interestingly, the overlap between these states is
\operatorname{Tr}[\Gamma_{\rm in}\Gamma_{\rm fin}]=(\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\rho_{\rm fin}])^{2}
, and therefore the sample complexity of the above weak measurement scheme has the same scaling with respect to the overlap as the sample complexity of our controlled-
SWAP
protocol.

In summary, there are at least three different types of protocols to measure the double-weak value for mixed states: (1) our controlled-
\tt SWAP
protocol, (2) protocols that measure the standard weak values for the observables
A
and
B
and sample over different initial and final states, and (3) protocols that measure the weak value for the observable
A\otimes B
for pure entangled states on an extended system.

It is also interesting to observe that, when the two observables
A
and
B
coincide (
A=B
) and the final state is pure (
\rho_{\rm fin}=|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|
), the double weak value coincides with the associated weak value defined in Ref.
Kofman et al. 2012
(cf. Eq. (14.19) therein). The associated weak value was shown to arise in a non-linear theory of weak measurements
Kofman et al. 2012
, and the variant of Protocol
II.2
discussed in this section provides an alternative way to measure them in the laboratory, by sampling from the probability distribution (
18
), in which both POVMs
(P_{j})_{j}
and
(Q_{k})_{k}
correspond to two (possibly different) noisy measurements of the observable
A
.

III.2
Local measurements and general bipartite states

A further generalization of Protocol
II.2
is to replace the two uncorrelated states
\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho_{\rm fin}
with a single bipartite state
\rho_{\rm in,fin}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1(c)
.
If systems
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are measured with POVMs
(P_{j})_{j}
and
(Q_{k})_{k}
, respectively, the controlled-
SWAP
protocol produces a triple of outcomes
(j,k,c)
distributed with probability

\displaystyle p(j,k,c|\rho_{\rm in,fin}):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[(P_{j}\otimes Q_{k}\otimes R_{c})\,U(\rho_{\rm in,fin}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|)U^{\dagger}\right]\,.

(28)

By sampling over this probability distribution, one can simulate the averages of arbitrary random variables with respect to the complex measure

\displaystyle q(j,k\,|\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}):=\operatorname{Tr}[(P_{j}\otimes Q_{k})\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]\,,

(29)

thereby estimating quantities of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[(A\otimes B)\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]

(30)

for arbitrary observables
A
and
B
for which the POVMs provide unbiased measurements
(P_{j})_{j}
and
(Q_{k})_{k}
.

As a biproduct, one can also estimate the ratios

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[(A\otimes B)\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]}\,.

(31)

Note that we do not use the term “(double) weak values” for the ratios in Eq. (
31
). This omission is intentional: as we will see in the next section, the proper notion of (double) weak value does not, in general, coincide with the ratios in Eq. (
31
), but rather with a variant of Eq. (
31
) where the bipartite state
\rho_{\rm in,fin}
is subject to a partial transposition.

III.3
General bipartite states and joint measurements

A third generalization of the controlled-
SWAP
protocol consists in performing a joint measurement on the two copies of the system, as illustrated in Fig.
1(d)
. If the two copies are initially in the bipartite state
\rho_{\rm in,fin}
and are measured with the joint POVM
(\Pi_{j})_{j}
after the controlled-
SWAP
operation, the protocol produces a pair of outcomes
(j,c)
distributed with probability

\displaystyle p(j,c|\rho_{\rm in,fin}):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[(\Pi_{j}\otimes R_{c})\,U(\rho_{\rm in,fin}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|)U^{\dagger}\right]\,.

(32)

By sampling from this probability distribution, an experimenter can estimate the expectation value of arbitrary random variables with respect to the complex measure

\displaystyle q(j|\rho_{\rm in,fin})=\operatorname{Tr}[\Pi_{j}\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,{\tt SWAP}]\,,

(33)

and therefore the value of every quantity of the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[O\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,{\tt SWAP}]\,,

(34)

where
O\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
is an arbitrary observable for which the POVM
(\Pi_{j})_{j}
provides an unbiased measurement. Again, this setup allows one to estimate the ratios

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[O\,\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in,fin}\,\tt SWAP]}.

(35)

In the next section, we will compare the experimentally accessible quantities (
34
) and (
35
) with the weak values associated to general two-time states.

IV
Two-time states

Weak values can be interpreted as expectation values with respect to a generalized type of quantum states, known as
two-time states

Aharonov et al. 1988
;
Aharonov and Vaidman 1991
;
Silva et al. 2014
. In the following, we provide a brief review of the notion of two-time state.

IV.1
Two-time vectors

The prototype of a two-time state is the
two-time vector
introduced in the seminal work of Aharonov, Bergmann, and Lebowitz
Aharonov et al. 1964
. A two-time vector is a linear functional
\lambda:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
defined by the relation

\displaystyle\lambda(A):=\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|A|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(36)

where
|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
and
|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
are two arbitrary vectors. Here, the functional
\lambda
plays the role of an unnormalized state vector in textbook quantum mechanics. The complex number
\lambda(A)
is sometimes called the
expectation value of the observable
A
on the two-time vector
\lambda
.

The notion of two-time vector was later generalized by Aharonov and Vaidman
Aharonov and Vaidman 1991
, who considered general linear combinations of the form

\displaystyle\lambda(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\psi^{\prime}_{i}|\,A\,|\psi_{i}\rangle\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(37)

where
N
is a positive integer and
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{H}
and
\{|\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{H}
are arbitrary sets of vectors.

Mathematically, the set of two-time vectors (
37
) is the set of all linear functionals on the observables of the system.

Proposition 1
.

The set of all two-time vectors (
37
) is the vector space consisting of all linear functionals from
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
to
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.
For every linear functional
\lambda:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
, there exists one and only one matrix
L\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
such that
\lambda(A)=\operatorname{Tr}[L\,A]
. By the singular value decomposition
Datta 1995
, the matrix
L
can be rewritten as
L=\sum_{i=}^{d}\,|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|
, where
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
and
\{|\psi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\}
are two sets of orthogonal states. Hence, the functional
\lambda
has the Aharonov-Vaidman form (
37
). This proves that every linear functional is a valid two-time vector. The converse is trivial, since every two-time vector is, by definition, a linear functional.
\blacksquare

IV.2
Two-time matrices

The notion of two-time state was later been extended from pure to mixed states by Silva
et al

Silva et al. 2014
, who developed a framework for describing pre- and post-selected ensembles of quantum states, and by Vaidman
et al

Vaidman et al. 2017
, who developed the connection to weak interaction schemes. In this subsection we now review Silva
et al
’s framework, using a slightly different notation that facilitates the connection with the notion of double weak value introduced in this paper.

The transition from pure to mixed two-time states is similar to the transition from state vectors to density matrices in textbook quantum mechanics. First, one defines the matrices corresponding to pure states:

Definition 2
.

The two-time density matrix corresponding to a two-time vector
\lambda:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
is the bilinear functional
E_{\lambda}:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
defined by the relation

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}(A,B):=\lambda(A)\,\lambda^{\dagger}(B)\,,\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(38)

where
\lambda^{\dagger}
is the functional defined by the relation
\lambda^{\dagger}(A):=\overline{\lambda(A^{\dagger})}\,,\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
. We call a matrix
E_{\lambda}
of the form (
38
) a pure two-time density matrix.

Here, the term “matrix” refers to the fact that the observables
A
and
B
in Eq. (
38
) can be regarded as the row and column indices of a matrix. Explicitly,
E_{\lambda}
can be represented by a matrix by fixing two bases
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d^{2}}
and
\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d^{2}}
for the space
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
, and defining the matrix elements
[E_{\lambda}]_{ij}:=E_{\lambda}(A_{i},B_{j})
.

The correspondence between the two-time state
\lambda
and the two-time matrix
E_{\lambda}
is analogous to the correspondence between a unit vector
|\psi\rangle
and the corresponding density matrix
|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
.
It is instructive to see this correspondence in a few examples. First, a two-time vector of the basic form
\lambda(A)=\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|A|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle
gives rise to the two-time matrix

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}(A,B)=\langle\psi_{\rm fin}|A|\psi_{\rm in}\rangle\langle\psi_{\rm in}|B|\psi_{\rm fin}\rangle\,.

(39)

More generally, a two-time vector of the Aharonov-Vaidman form
\lambda(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|A|\psi_{i}\rangle
gives rise to the two-time matrix

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}(A,B)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|A|\psi_{i}\rangle\right)\,\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\psi_{j}|B|\psi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\right)\,.

(40)

Note that every pure two-time matrix
E_{\lambda}
satisfies the positivity condition

\displaystyle E_{\lambda}(A,A^{\dagger})\geq 0\qquad\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,.

(41)

Definition 3
.

A (generally unnormalized) two-time density matrix is a bilinear functional
\omega:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
of the form

\displaystyle\omega=\sum_{n}\,E_{\lambda_{n}}\,,

(42)

where
(\lambda_{n})_{n}
are arbitrary linear functionals and
(E_{\lambda_{n}})_{n}
are two-time matrices defined as in Eq. (
38
). The two-time density matrix
\omega
is normalized if
\omega(I,I)=1
, where
I
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

Note that every two-time density matrix
\omega
obeys the positivity condition

\displaystyle\omega(A,A^{\dagger})\geq 0\qquad\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(43)

which follows from Eqs. (
41
) and (
42
). In the next section, we will show that this condition is both necessary and sufficient for a bilinary functional
\omega
to be a two-time density matrix.

Hereafter, we will refer to
\omega(A,B)
as the
expectation value
of the pair of observables
(A,B)
with respect to the two-time matrix
\omega
. One way to estimate the expectation value
\omega(A,B)
from experimental data is to decompose
\omega
as in Eq. (
42
) and to measure the expectation values with respect to the pure two-time matrices
E_{\lambda_{n}}
, which are proportional to products of weak values. Later in the paper, we will see another way to estimate the expectation values based on the
SWAP
test.

Before concluding this section, we provide a concrete example of a mixed two-time density matrix. Consider two-time vectors of the form
\lambda_{mn}(A)=\sqrt{p_{m}p_{n}^{\prime}}\,\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|A|\psi_{m}\rangle
, where
(p_{m})_{m}
and
(p_{n}^{\prime})_{n}
are two probability distributions, and
\{|\psi_{m}\rangle\}_{m}
and
\{|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\}
are two orthonormal bases. This choice gives rise to the two-time matrix
\omega
defined by

\displaystyle\omega(A,B)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}E_{\lambda_{mn}}(A,B)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}p_{m}p_{n}^{\prime}\,\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|A|\psi_{m}\rangle\,\langle\psi_{m}|B|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[A\,\rho_{\rm in}\,B\,\rho_{\rm fin}]\,,

(44)

where
\rho_{\rm in}=\sum_{m}\,p_{m}\,|\psi_{m}\rangle\langle\psi_{m}|
and
\rho_{\rm fin}=\sum_{n}\,p_{n}^{\prime}\,|\psi_{n}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}^{\prime}|
are ordinary, single-time density matrices. Note that
\omega(I,I)=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]
: if the overlap
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm in}\,\rho_{\rm fin}]
is nonzero, then the two-time matrix
\omega
can be normalized, thus obtaining the normalized two-time density matrix
\omega_{*}:=\omega/\omega(I,I)
. Note that the expectation value
\omega_{*}(A,B)
coincides with the double weak value defined in Eq. (
21
).

In the following, the set of two-time density matrices of a system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
will be denoted by
{\sf TwoTimeD}(\mathcal{H})
. Mathematically, the set of all unnormalized two-time states is a convex cone,
i.e.
it contains all convex combinations and all positive multiples of its elements.

The set of normalized two-time density matrices will be denoted by

\displaystyle{\sf TwoTimeD}_{*}(\mathcal{H}):=\Big\{\omega\in{\sf TwoTimeD}(\mathcal{H})~|~\omega(I,I)=1\Big\}\,\,.

(45)

Mathematically, the set of normalized two-time density matrices is a convex set, contained in the convex cone
{\sf TwoTimeD}(\mathcal{H})
.

V
Characterization of the two-time states

Silva
et al

Silva et al. 2014
showed that (generally unnormalized) two-time states are in one-to-one correspondence with (generally unnormalized) bipartite density matrices. We now make this correspondence explicit, providing an expression that connects two-time states to the controlled-
SWAP
protocol introduced earlier in the paper.

Our main result is the following characterization:

Theorem 2
.

For every bilinear functional
\omega:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

\omega
is a two-time density matrix.

2.

\omega(A,A^{\dagger})\geq 0
for every
A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
.

3.

There exists a positive operator
P_{\omega}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
such that

\displaystyle\omega(A,B)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)\right]\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(46)

where
P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}
is the partial transpose of
P_{\omega}
over the second Hilbert space.

Proof.
See Appendix
D
.
\blacksquare

Note that the operator
P_{\omega}
in Theorem
2
is uniquely defined:

Proposition 2
.

For every bilinear functional
\omega:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
, if two operators
P_{\omega}
and
P_{\omega}^{\prime}
satisfy Eq. (
46
), then they are necessarily equal, namely
P_{\omega}=P_{\omega}^{\prime}
.

Proof.
Since the product operators
A\otimes B
are a spanning set for
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
, the condition
\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{\prime\,T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)\right]\,\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
implies
P_{\omega}^{\prime\,T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}=P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}
. Multiplying by
\tt SWAP
and taking the partial transpose on both sides of the equality, we then obtain
P_{\omega}^{\prime}=P_{\omega}
.
\blacksquare

Theorem
2
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the convex cone of two-time states and the convex cone of positive bipartite matrices. We now analyze the correspondence further, by characterizing the structure of the
normalized
two-time states and showing that they are in one-to-one correspondence with a convex subset of bipartite density matrices.

Theorem 3
.

A bilinear functional
\omega:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}

is a normalized two-time state if and only if it is of the form

\displaystyle\omega(A,B)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\right]}\,,\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(47)

where
\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
is a normalized density matrix such that
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}]\not=0
.

Proof.
See Appendix
E
.
\blacksquare

Note that, if the state
\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}
in Theorem
3
is of the product form
\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}=\rho_{\rm in}\otimes\rho_{\rm fin}^{T}
, then the expectation value
\omega(A,B)
in Eq. (
47
) coincides with the double weak value defined in Eq. (
21
), and therefore can be estimated using our controlled-
SWAP
protocol. More discussion on the estimation of expectation value of general two-time states using (variants of) the controlled-
SWAP
protocol will be provided in the next section.

It is worth noting the relation

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[\rho^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\right]=\langle\!\langle I|\,\rho\,|I\rangle\!\rangle\,,\qquad\forall\rho\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}),

(48)

where
|I\rangle\!\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle
is the canonical unnormalized maximally entangled state. Using this relation, we can see that Theorem
3
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the set of normalized two-time density matrices and the subset of bipartite density matrices defined by

\displaystyle{\sf D}_{*}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}):=

\displaystyle\Big\{\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})~|~\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}\geq 0\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}]=1\,,

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\langle\!\langle I|\rho_{{\rm in,fin}}|I\rangle\!\rangle>0\Big\}\,.

(49)

Note also that the correspondence between two-time states and bipartite matrices is a homeomorphism (that is, it is invertible and continuous), as it is given by the linear fractional transformation
\rho\mapsto\omega_{\rho}
, where
\omega_{\rho}
is the bilinear functional defined by

\displaystyle\omega_{\rho}(A,B):=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{T_{2}}\,{\tt SWAP}]}\,.

(50)

Summarizing, we have the following

Corollary 1
.

The sets of normalized two-time states and bipartite density matrices with nonzero overlap with the maximally entangled state are homeomorphic; in formula,
{\sf TwoTimeD}_{*}(\mathcal{H})\simeq{\sf D}_{*}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
.

Note that the correspondence between two-time density matrices and bipartite density matrices mapping is non-linear and it does not preserve convex combinations. Nevertheless, it is linear fractional, and therefore it maps convex combinations into convex combinations, although with generally different weights. Explicitly, the mapping
\rho\mapsto\omega_{\rho}
in Eq. (
50
) maps a convex combination of density matrices, say
\rho=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\,\rho_{i}
, into a convex combination of two-time states
\omega_{\rho}=\sum_{i}q_{i}\,\omega_{\rho_{i}}
, with
q_{i}=p_{i}\,\langle\Phi|\rho_{i}\,|\Phi\rangle/(\sum_{j}p_{j}\,\langle\Phi|\rho_{j}\,|\Phi\rangle)
. This condition implies that the mapping
\rho\mapsto\omega_{\rho}
maps pure bipartite states into extreme points of the set of two-time states, and
vice-versa
.

VI
Two-time states and the controlled-
SWAP
protocol

Theorem
3
reveals a fundamental connection between two-time states and the controlled-
SWAP
protocol presented earlier in the paper. As we saw in Eq. (
31
), the controlled-
SWAP
protocol allows an experimenter to estimate any quantity of the form

\displaystyle\widetilde{\omega}_{\rho}(A,B):=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,{\tt SWAP}\,(A\otimes B)]}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,{\tt SWAP}]}\,,

(51)

for every pair of observables
(A,B)
and every bipartite quantum state
\rho
.

The difference between the quantities (
51
) and the expectation values on two-time states (
47
) is the presence of the partial transpose on the second Hilbert space. Physically, the partial transpose can be interpreted as the signature of the difference between the spatial correlations accessible with the controlled-
SWAP
protocol and the time correlations associated to two-time states.

By comparing Eqs. (
51
) and (
47
) we can also have a clear view of the strengths and limitations of the controlled-
SWAP
protocol. First, if the density matrix
\rho
is invariant under partial transpose, an experimentalist who has access to two systems in the joint state
\rho
can directly estimate the expectation values on the two-time state
\omega_{\rho}
, by using the controlled-
SWAP
protocol.

More generally, if the density matrix is positive under partial transpose (PPT)
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
, the controlled-
SWAP
protocol can provide an estimate of the expectation values on the two time-state
\omega_{\rho}
, if the experimenter is given access to the quantum state
\rho^{T_{2}}
. This is the case of the protocols shown earlier in the paper, where we saw how to estimate the weak values (
1
) and (
2
), which correspond to the expectation values of the observables
(A,I)
on the two-time states

\omega_{\psi_{\rm in}\otimes\psi_{\rm fin}^{T}}
and
\omega_{\rho_{\rm in}\otimes\rho_{\rm fin}^{T}}
, respectively. Similarly, the double weak value
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,A\,\rho_{\rm in}\,B]/\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{\rm fin}\,\rho_{\rm in}]
is the expectation value of the observables
(A,B)
with respect to the two-time state
\omega_{\rho_{\rm in}\otimes\rho_{\rm fin}^{T}}
.

In contrast, the controlled-
SWAP
protocol does not provide, in general, an estimate of the expectation values on a two-time state when the density matrix
\rho
is not PPT. One way to circumvent this problem would be to approximate the partial transpose operation with a physical process, such as the optimal universal transpose map

Horodecki 2001
;
Buscemi et al. 2003
.
However, the estimation protocol resulting from this approach would be dimension-dependent: indeed, optimal universal transpose is the completely positive trace-preserving map given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}(\rho)=\frac{\rho^{\rm T}+I}{d+1}\,,

(52)

and the
1/(d+1)
factor implies that the sample complexity for the estimation of the expectation values
\omega_{\rho}(A,B)
grows linearly with
d
.

Another approach is to introduce an auxiliary system, and to reduce two-time states associated to non-PPT density matrices to extended two-time states associated to density matrices that are PPT with respect to the bipartition between pre- and post-selected systems. This approach can be easily illustrated in the pure state case. In this case, Aharonov and Vaidman
Aharonov and Vaidman 1991
showed that every two-time vector
\lambda(A):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle\psi^{\prime}_{i}|\,A\,|\psi_{i}\rangle
can be obtained by introducing an auxiliary system of dimension
N
, and a joint two-time vector
\Lambda(\cdot):=\langle\Psi_{\rm fin}|\,\cdot\,|\Psi_{\rm in}\rangle
corresponding to the initial vector
|\Psi_{\rm in}\rangle=\sum_{i}\,|\psi_{i}\rangle\otimes|i\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux}
and final vector
|\Psi_{\rm fin}\rangle=\sum_{i}\,|\psi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\otimes|i\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux}
is the Hilbert space of the auxiliary system and
\{|i\rangle\}_{i=1}^{N}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux}
. With this definition, one has the relation

\displaystyle\lambda(A)=\Lambda(A\otimes I_{\rm aux})\,,\qquad\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(53)

meaning that the expectation value of the observable
A
on the two-time vector
\lambda
coincides with the expectation value of the observable
A\otimes I_{\rm aux}
on the the joint two-time vector
\Lambda
. In turn, the two-time vector
\Lambda
consists just in a pre-selection and a post-selection to pure states, and therefore it can be reproduced by a product (and therefore PPT) state. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Lambda(A\otimes I_{\rm aux})}{\Lambda(I\otimes I_{\rm aux})}=\omega_{\Psi_{\rm in}\otimes\Psi_{\rm fin}^{T}}(A\otimes I_{\rm aux}\otimes I\otimes I_{\rm aux})\,,

(54)

where
\omega_{\Psi_{\rm in}\otimes\Psi_{\rm fin}^{T}}
, defined as in Eq. (
47
), is a normalized two-time state in
{\sf T}_{*}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux}\otimes\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\rm aux})
. Combining Eqs. (
53
) and (
54
), we can see that the normalized expectation value
\lambda(A)/\lambda(I)
can be estimated by the controlled-
SWAP
protocol, if the experimenter has access to the pure quantum states
\Psi_{\rm in}
and
\Psi_{\rm fin}^{T}
.

The above argument can be extended from two-time vectors to two-time matrices. For example, the expectation values with respect to the two-time matrix
E_{\lambda}
associated to the two-time vector
\lambda
in the previous paragraph can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\lambda}(A,B)}{E_{\lambda}(I,I)}=\omega_{\Psi_{\rm in}\otimes\Psi_{\rm fin}^{T}}(A\otimes I_{\rm aux},B\otimes I_{\rm aux})\,.

(55)

Eq. (
55
) means that the expectation value on the l.h.s. can be estimated by the controlled-
SWAP
protocol, if the experimenter has access to the pure quantum states
\Psi_{\rm in}
and
\Psi_{\rm fin}^{T}
.

Overall, the extension approach has the benefit of being dimension-independent, but it requires either the two-time state
\omega
to be known, or the appropriate PPT state of the system and the auxiliary system to be provided to the experimenter.

VII
Conclusions and outlook

This paper provided a dimension-independent scheme for estimating weak values of arbitrary observables. The scheme is based on the controlled-
SWAP
gate, and generates a probability distribution that can be used to sample from a complex measure underlying the weak values of interest.
Crucially, the scheme is modular: changing the initial and final states of the system in the mathematical expression of the weak value corresponds to changing two preparation devices in the experimental setup, while changing the observable in the mathematical expression corresponds to changing a measurement device in the experimental setup.

The structure of the controlled-
SWAP
scheme provides several insights into theory of two-time states. In particular, we have derived an alternative expression for two-time states, which provides an explicit characterization of the correspondence between two-time states and (a subset of) bipartite density matrices. Using this expression, we showed that the controlled-
SWAP
protocol can be used to estimate the expectation values of all two-time states corresponding to PPT density matrices (including, of course, the product density matrices corresponding to the usual definition of weak values). For two-time states corresponding to non-PPT density matrices, the controlled-
SWAP
protocol can still be used if the experimenter is given access to an extendend quantum states involving a pair of auxiliary systems.

Due to the ubiquitous presence of weak values in quantum mechanics, our estimation scheme can be useful in several applications
Dressel et al. 2014
, including
e.g.
estimating quantum sensitivity
Aharonov et al. 1988
;
Wiseman 2002
, coherence
Budiyono and Dipojono 2023
, and asymmetry
Budiyono 2023
. An interesting open question for future research is whether dimension-independent sampling schemes like ours could be constructed for the estimation of Kirkwood-Dirac (KD) quasiprobability distributions, an important type of complex measures that often arise in quantum information and foundations
Dressel 2015
;
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
;
Lostaglio et al. 2023
;
Budiyono et al. 2024
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2024
. KD distributions have a close connection with weak values, and their negativity provides a signature of quantum contextuality in Spekkens’ formulation
Spekkens 2005
. In turn, KD distributions have found numerous applications beyond quantum foundations, including quantum metrology
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2020
;
Jenne and Arvidsson-Shukur 2021
;
Lupu-Gladstein et al. 2022
, condensed matter physics
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
;
González Alonso et al. 2019
;
González Alonso et al. 2022
, and thermodynamics
Lostaglio et al. 2023
. The approach of Wagner
et al

Wagner et al. 2024
provides a way to estimate the value of the KD distribution at every fixed point. The open question is whether there exist ways to simulate sampling from the KD distribution, in analogy to the scheme for sampling from the weak distribution proposed in this paper.

Another interesting direction concerns the theory of two-time states and its relation of the study of causality in quantum theory
Chiribella et al. 2009
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
;
Oreshkov et al. 2012
;
Č. Brukner 2014
;
Barrett et al. 2019
;
Barrett et al. 2021
. Recent findings have suggested that anomalous weak values of observables shared between several parties can assist in witnessing the causal relationships between the parties’ laboratories
Abbott et al. 2019
. Moreover, two-time states (and multiple-time states in general) themselves can carry information about the underlying causal structure
Silva et al. 2017
and can be used to witness indefinite causal order of operations
Liu et al. 2024
. Finally, protocols exploiting controlled causal order of operations in a protocol known as the quantum SWITCH
Chiribella et al. 2009
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
have been shown to be useful for efficient estimation of quantities that can be connected with weak values and KD distributions, such as out-of-time-correlators
Swingle et al. 2016
and incompatibility of quantum observables
Gao et al. 2023
. Therefore, an interplay between weak values, KD distributions, and indefinite causal order in quantum SWITCH appears as a promising direction for future investigations.

Appendix A
Derivation of Eq. (
10
)

In the following, we will use the short-hand notation
\rho:=\rho_{\rm in}
and
\rho^{\prime}:=\rho_{\rm fin}
.

At the beginning of the protocol, the two copies of the system (
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
) and the control qubit are in the product state

\omega=\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|.

(56)

Application of the controlled-
SWAP
gate
U
produces the new state

\displaystyle U\omega U^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\{\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+\rho^{\prime}\otimes\rho\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|

(57)

\displaystyle+\;(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}){\tt SWAP}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 1|

\displaystyle+\;{\tt SWAP}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})\otimes|1\rangle\langle 0|\Bigr\}\,.

A measurement of the first system and auxiliary qubit with the POVM
(P_{j})_{j}
and
(R_{c})_{c}
, respectively, produces a pair of outcomes
(j,c)
distributed with probability

\displaystyle p(j,c|\rho,\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\Bigl[P_{j}\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}\otimes R_{c}|0\rangle\langle 0|

\displaystyle+\;P_{j}\rho^{\prime}\otimes\rho\otimes R_{c}|1\rangle\langle 1|

\displaystyle+\;(P_{j}\otimes I)(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}){\tt SWAP}\otimes R_{c}|0\rangle\langle 1|

\displaystyle+\;(P_{j}\otimes I){\tt SWAP}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})\otimes R_{c}|1\rangle\langle 0|\Bigr]\,.

Using the relations

\displaystyle\langle 0|R_{c}|0\rangle=\langle 1|R_{c}|1\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\qquad\forall c\in\{0,1,2,3\}

(58)

and

\displaystyle\langle 0|R_{c}|1\rangle=\frac{(-1)^{c}}{4}\,\Bigl(\theta(1-c)+i\theta(c-2)\Bigr)\qquad\forall c\in\{0,1,2,3\}

(59)

we then obtain

\displaystyle p(j,c|\rho,\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\Bigl\{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho]+\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]

(60)

\displaystyle+\;(-1)^{c}\Bigl(\theta(1-c)+i\theta(c-2)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\cdot\;\operatorname{Tr}\Bigl[(P_{j}\otimes I)(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime}){\tt SWAP}\Bigr]

\displaystyle+\;(-1)^{c}\Bigl(\theta(1-c)-i\theta(c-2)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\cdot\;\operatorname{Tr}\Bigl[(P_{j}\otimes I){\tt SWAP}(\rho\otimes\rho^{\prime})\Bigr]\Bigr\}.

Finally, using the relation
\operatorname{Tr}[(A\otimes B)\,{\tt SWAP}]=\operatorname{Tr}[AB]\,,\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
, we obtain

\displaystyle p(j,c|\rho,\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\Bigl\{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho]+\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]

(61)

\displaystyle+\;(-1)^{c}\theta(1-c)\Bigl(\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime}]+\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\;i(-1)^{c}\theta(c-2)\Bigl(\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime}]-\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]\Bigr)\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8}\Bigl\{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho]+\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]

\displaystyle+\;2(-1)^{c}\theta(1-c)\operatorname{Re}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime}]

\displaystyle-\;2(-1)^{c}\theta(c-2)\operatorname{Im}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime}]\Bigr\},

where the second equality follows from the identity
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{j}\rho^{\prime}]=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}\rho P_{j}]=\operatorname{Tr}[(P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime})^{\dagger}]=\overline{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{j}\rho\rho^{\prime}]}
. This concludes the proof of Eq. (
10
).

-2x_{\rm max}/K\leq X_{k}\leq 2x_{\rm max}/K
, where
x_{\rm max}=\operatorname{max}_{j}|x_{j}|
, we obtain

\sum_{k}(\max(X_{k})-\min(X_{k}))^{2}=\frac{8x_{\rm max}^{2}}{K}.

(67)

Therefore, the probability (
66
) is upper-bounded by

\Pr(|X_{\rm Re}-\nu_{\rm Re}|\geq\epsilon_{\nu})\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon_{\nu}^{2}K}{4x_{\max}^{2}}\right).

(68)

Requiring that it does not exceed
\delta
is equivalent to the upper bound

2\exp\left(-\epsilon_{\nu}^{2}K/4x_{\max}^{2}\right)\leq\delta,

(69)

bounding hence the estimation error as

\epsilon_{\nu}\geq 2x_{\rm max}\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}.

(70)

This means that, for
K/2
runs of Protocol
II.2
, we can make sure that the estimation of
\nu_{\rm Re}
is within an error
\epsilon_{\nu}
with a probability no less than
1-\delta

Lemma 3
.

Given an observable
A=\sum_{j}x_{j}P_{j}
, where
(P_{j})_{j}
is a set of POVM elements, and a pair of states
\rho
,
\rho^{\prime}
, for
K
runs of Protocol
II.2
, the estimate of its weak value
W(A\,|\,\rho,\rho^{\prime})
can be guaranteed to have an error at most:

\epsilon=\frac{\sqrt{2}(x_{\rm max}+|W(A\,|\,\rho,\rho^{\prime})|)}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1},

(73)

where
x_{\rm max}:=\operatorname{max}_{j}|x_{j}|
,
\mu=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{\prime}\rho]
, and
\epsilon_{\mu}=2\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\ln\frac{2}{\delta}}
, with probability at least
1-3\delta
.

Proof.

First, we we recall that
W(A\,|\,\rho,\rho^{\prime})=W_{\rm Re}+iW_{\rm Im}
, where
W_{\rm Re}=\nu_{\rm Re}/\mu
and
W_{\rm Im}=\nu_{\rm Im}/\mu
. Therefore, the corresponding estimation error is given by

\epsilon=\sqrt{\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}^{2}+\epsilon_{W_{\rm Im}}^{2}},

(74)

where
\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}
and
\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}
are errors in estimation of the real and imaginary parts of the weak value, respectively. The error in estimation of the real part
W_{\rm Re}
can be upper-bounded as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{X_{\rm Re}}{Y}-\frac{\nu_{\rm Re}}{\mu}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{X_{\rm Re}\mu-\nu_{\rm Re}\mu+\mu\nu_{\rm Re}-Y\nu_{\rm Re}}{Y\mu}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|X_{\rm Re}-\nu_{\rm Re}|\mu+|\mu-Y||\nu_{\rm Re}|}{|Y|\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\epsilon_{\nu}+\epsilon_{\mu}|W_{\rm Re}|}{|Y|},

(75)

where the first inequality follows from the triangle inequality
|a+b|\leq|a|+|b|
, while the second inequality, in accordance with Lemma
2
and Corollary
2
, follows from the upper bounds (
63
) and (
71
), respectively. As the latter can be given as
\mu-\epsilon_{\mu}\leq Y\leq\mu+\epsilon_{\mu}
, we can assume that the corresponding estimation error is small enough to fulfill the condition
\epsilon_{\mu}<\mu
, so that
|Y|\geq\mu-\epsilon_{\mu}\geq 0
and, thus,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{\nu}+\epsilon_{\mu}|W_{\rm Re}|}{\mu-\epsilon_{\mu}}.

(76)

Comparing (
63
) and (
71
), it is easy to note that
\epsilon_{\nu}=x_{\rm max}\epsilon_{\mu}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{W_{\rm Re}}\leq\frac{x_{\rm max}+|W_{\rm Re}|}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1}.

(77)

Similarly, the error of estimating
{\rm Im}(W(A\,|\,\rho,\rho^{\prime}))
is bounded from above as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{W_{\rm Im}}\leq\frac{x_{\rm max}+|W_{\rm Im}|}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1}.

(78)

Therefore, an upper bound on the total error in estimation of the weak value
W(A|\rho,\rho^{\prime})

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{(x_{\rm max}+|W_{\rm Re}|)^{2}+(x_{\rm max}+|W_{\rm Im}|)^{2}}}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2x_{\rm max}^{2}+2x_{\rm max}\left(|W_{\rm Re}|+|W_{\rm Im}|\right)+|W(A|\rho,\rho^{\prime})|^{2}}}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2x_{\rm max}^{2}+4x_{\rm max}|W(A|\rho,\rho^{\prime})|+|W(A|\rho,\rho^{\prime})|^{2}}}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}(x_{\rm max}+|W(A|\rho,\rho^{\prime})|)}{\mu/\epsilon_{\mu}-1},

(79)

is valid if the bounds
|X_{\rm Re}-\nu_{\rm Re}|\leq\epsilon_{\nu}
,
|X_{\rm Im}-\nu_{\rm Im}|\leq\epsilon_{\nu}
, and
|Y-\mu|\leq\epsilon_{\mu}
are satisfied. Since each bound is violated with a probability not higher than
\delta
, the violation probability of at least one of them is upper-bounded by
3\delta
. Hence, the estimation error of
W(A\,|\,\rho,\rho^{\prime})
does not exceed the upper bound (
79
) with probability at least
1-3\delta
.

\blacksquare

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
2

1\Longrightarrow 2
. This implication was already proven by Eq. (
43
).

2\Longrightarrow 3
. A bilinear functional
\omega:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying the positivity condition
\omega(A,A^{\dagger})\geq 0,\,\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
defines a scalar product on
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
through the relation

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{\omega}:=\omega(A^{\dagger},B)\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,.

(83)

With this notation, the positivity condition is equivalent to the positivity of the scalar product
\langle\cdot~,\cdot\rangle_{\omega}
.

We now use the correspondence between operators in
\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
and bipartite vectors in
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
given by the double-ket notation
|A\rangle\!\rangle:=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\,A|j\rangle\otimes|j\rangle,\,\forall A\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})
. Using this correspondence, the scalar product
\langle\cdot~,\cdot\rangle_{\omega}
can be equivalently written

\displaystyle\langle A,B\rangle_{\omega}=\langle\!\langle A|\,P_{\omega}\,|B\rangle\!\rangle\,,\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})

(84)

for some positive semidefinite operator
P_{\omega}\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\omega(A,B)

\displaystyle=\langle A^{\dagger},B\rangle_{\omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle A^{\dagger}|\,P_{\omega}\,|B\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}\,|B\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle A^{\dagger}|\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}\left(|B\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle A^{\dagger}|\right)^{T_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}(B\otimes I)\left(|I\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle I|\right)^{T_{2}}(A\otimes I)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}(B\otimes I){\tt SWAP}(A\otimes I)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}{\tt SWAP}\,{\tt SWAP}(B\otimes I){\tt SWAP}(A\otimes I)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}{\tt SWAP}(A\otimes B)\right]\,,

(85)

where
|I\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\,|j\rangle\otimes|j\rangle
.

3\Longrightarrow 1
. Let us consider first the case where the operator
P_{\omega}
is rank-one, that is
P_{\omega}=|\Gamma\rangle\langle\Gamma|
for some bipartite vector
|\Gamma\rangle\in\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}
. Let us write
|\Gamma\rangle=\sum_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle\otimes|\overline{\psi}^{\prime}_{i}\rangle

for some suitable vectors
|\psi_{i}\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}_{i}\rangle
in
\mathcal{H}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\omega(A,B)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left[P_{\omega}^{T_{2}}{\tt SWAP}(A\otimes B)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\otimes|\overline{\psi}_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\overline{\psi}_{j}^{\prime}|\right)^{T_{2}}{\tt SWAP}(A\otimes B)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\otimes|\psi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|\right){\tt SWAP}(A\otimes B)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|\otimes|\psi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|\right)(A\otimes B)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\langle\psi_{i}^{\prime}|A|\psi_{i}\rangle\,\langle\psi_{j}|B|\psi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda(A)\,\lambda^{\dagger}(B)\,,\qquad\forall A,B\in\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\,,

(86)

where
\lambda:\mathsf{Lin}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
is the linear functional defined by
\lambda(A):=\sum_{i}\langle\psi_{i}|A|\psi_{i}^{\prime}\rangle
. By definition,
E_{\lambda}(A,B):=\lambda(A)\,\lambda^{\dagger}(B)=
is the two-time matrix associated to the two-time vector
\lambda
. Hence, Eq. (
86
) implies that the functional
\omega
is equal to the two-time matrix
E_{\lambda}
.

To conclude, consider the case where the rank of
P_{\omega}
is larger than one. Since
P_{\omega}
is positive semidefinite, it can be written as a sum of rank-one terms, say
P_{\omega}=\sum_{n}\,|\Gamma_{n}\rangle\langle\Gamma_{n}|
. By the result of the previous paragraph, we then obtain the decomposition
\omega=\sum_{n}\,E_{\lambda_{n}}
, where
\lambda_{n}
is the two-time vector associated to
|\Gamma_{n}\rangle
. Hence,
\omega
is a two-time matrix.
\blacksquare
