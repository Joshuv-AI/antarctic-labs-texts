Laplace eigenvalues and non-planar minimal spheres into 3-dimensional ellipsoids
Romain Petrides

Abstract
. We give a sufficient condition for branched minimal immersions of spheres into ellipsoids to be embedded : we show that if the coordinate functions of the branched minimal immersion are first or second eigenfunctions with respect to a natural metric on the sphere involved in this problem, called critical metric , then it is an embedding into a 2-dimensional ellipsoid or an immersion into a 3-dimensional ellipsoid. If in addition this critical metric is rotationally symmetric with respect to an axis and symmetric with respect to the orthogonal plane then the immersion is an embedding. We also give a construction of non-planar minimal spheres into 3-dimensional ellipsoids such that the coordinate functions are first and second eigenfunctions with respect to the critical metric obtained by maximization of linear combinations of first and second (area renormalized) Laplace eigenvalues among metrics on the sphere having the aforementionned symmetry constraints or not.



A problem raised by Yau in 1987 (
[
Yau87
]
, section 4) asked for existence of non-planar embedded minimal spheres into 3-ellipsoids. By definition, a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
is planar if it lies into a hyperplane. This result is a priori surprising since for the analogous problem of geodesics, a classical result by Morse
[
Mor31
]
stated that some 2-ellipsoids contain at most three embedded closed geodesics, the equatorial ones. However, in higher dimension, fruitful works initiated by Marques and Neves
[
MN17
]
lead to the general resolution of the following famous Yau’s conjecture by min-max methods (see Song
[
Son23
]
): any manifold of dimension 3 admits infinitely many smoothly embedded minimal surfaces. This spectacular result shows that the problem of geodesics is not a good analogy to understand the embeddedness of minimal surfaces in higher dimension, but we do not get much information about the topology of these embedded minimal surfaces yet.

Therefore, we can ask to build many numbers of embedded minimal
spheres
into ellipsoids. Of course, ellipsoids contain at least
4
planar embedded spheres (equatorial spheres) but one might expect more ones, which are non-planar. This question, left open for years was recently solved by Haslhofer and Ketover
[
HK19
]
: sufficiently elongated ellipsoids contain an embedded non-planar minimal sphere. They combined Mean Curvature Flow and Min-Max methods to prove this result. Later, using Bifurcation Theory and the symmetries that arise if at least two semi-axes coincide, Bettiol and Piccione
[
BP22
]
showed the existence of arbitrarily many distinct embedded non-planar minimal 2-spheres in sufficiently elongated ellipsoids of revolution.

In the current paper, we give a new method to build non-planar embedded minimal spheres and a new way to describe them. Indeed, we noticed in
[
Pet21
]
(see also section
2.1
in the current paper) that if
\Sigma
is a minimal surface into some ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of parameters
p=\left(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\right)
, with
p_{i}>0
, defined by

\mathcal{E}=\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n};p_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+p_{n}x_{n}^{2}=1\}\hskip 2.84544pt,

endowed with the induced metric of the Euclidean metric
\xi
, then the coordinate functions
(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are eigenfunctions with eigenvalues
(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n})
, with respect to the Laplacian of
(\Sigma,g)
endowed with the metric

(0.1)

g=\frac{\left|H_{\Sigma}(x)\right|}{\left(p_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+p_{n}^{2}x_{n}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\xi

where
H_{\Sigma}
is the mean curvature vector of
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In fact, this simple remark gives a way to describe all the minimal immersions into any ellipsoids with the point of view of spectral geometry. This is a generalization of Takahashi’s result
[
Tak66
]
in the case of the sphere
p_{1}=\cdots=p_{n}
, where coordinate functions are Laplace eigenfunctions with respect to the induced metric on
\Sigma
of the Euclidean metric.

One natural question is: given a minimal embedding into the ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
, are the coordinate functions associated to the
n
first eigenvalues counted with multiplicity of the Laplacian on
\Sigma
? In general, such a question is very hard to answer. If we just assume that
\mathcal{E}
is a 3-sphere (
n=3
and
p_{1}=p_{2}=p_{3}
) and that
\Sigma
is an embedded surface, Yau conjectured that the coordinate functions are first eigenfunctions. If
\Sigma
is topologically a sphere, or a torus, this conjecture holds true because there are uniqueness results: the equatorial spheres are the unique embedded minimal spheres by
[
Alm66
]
, and the Clifford torus is the unique embedded minimal torus
[
Bre13
]

[
HKS16
]
. The uniqueness result for the torus was known as the Lawson conjecture, left open for years. Notice that this conjecture by Yau is much more general since it implies the Lawson conjecture. Indeed, the unique immersed tori into some sphere by first eigenfunctions are the Clifford torus (in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
) and the flat equilateral torus (in
\mathbb{S}^{5}
)
[
ESI00
]
. In that spirit, it would be interesting to know the spectral indices of the parameters which appear in the previous constructions of non-planar embedded ellipsoids. Counting the nodal domains of coordinates of the arbitrarily many embedded non-planar minimal 2-spheres into ellipsoids obtained in
[
BP22
]
(of parameters (
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{3})
and we assume in addition
p_{2}=p_{3}
), we remark that only one can be associated to the 4 first eigenvalues counted with multiplicity of the Laplacian associated to the metric defined in (
0.1
).

For a first study of this spectral point of view on minimal surfaces into ellipsoids, it is already interesting to assume
\Sigma=\mathbb{S}^{2}
and to look for non-planar minimal spheres into rotationally symmetric ellipsoids having parameters which are first and second eigenvalues. Indeed, the aforementioned spectral characterization of minimal immersions into ellipsoids is more precise: the metric

defined on
\Sigma
by (

]
(see also

]
). It was a generalization of a result by Nadirashvili
[
Nad96
]
for one eigenvalue.

Nad96

Theorem 0.1
.

There is a one parameter family
(p_{t},q_{t})_{t>t_{\star}}
for some
0<t_{\star}<1
such that there is an embedded non planar minimal topological sphere
S_{t}
into the rotationally symmetric ellipsoid

\mathcal{E}_{t}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{4};p_{t}x_{0}^{2}+q_{t}x_{1}^{2}+q_{t}x_{2}^{2}+q_{t}x_{3}^{2}=1\}

and such that the coordinates

g_{t}=\frac{H_{S_{t}}(x)}{\left(p_{t}^{2}x_{0}^{2}+q_{t}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+q_{t}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+q_{t}^{2}x_{3}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\xi

on the sphere

is decreasing and

is increasing, and we have that

and

with multiplicity

We refer to section
1
for a detailed presentation of the construction of this family. We would like to emphasize that the surfaces built by this method in Theorem

Theorem 0.2
.

If
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{2}\mapsto\mathcal{E}
is a minimal branched conformal immersion into some ellipsoid of parameter
p=\left(p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\right)
such that for any

is a first or second eigenvalue of
(\mathbb{S}^{2},g)
(and we have
\{p,q\}=\{p_{1},\cdots,p_{n}\}

g=\frac{\left|H_{S}\circ\Phi\right|}{\left(p_{1}^{2}\Phi_{1}^{2}+\cdots+p_{n}^{2}\Phi_{n}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\Phi_{\star}\xi.=e^{2v}dA_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}

Then up to rearrangements of coordinates,
S:=\Phi\left(\mathbb{S}^{2}\right)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\{0\}
and
\Phi
is embedded or
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}\times\{0\}
and
\Phi
does not have any branched point.
If in addition

is rotationally symmetric with respect to the axis

and symmetric with respect to
\{x_{3}=0\}

is an embedding.

Theorem
0.2
is similar to the result of embeddedness of (possibly branched) minimal free boundary immersions into balls
by first Steklov eigenfunctions
for surfaces with boundary of

0

]
but the proof of Theorem

needs more refinement (see section

and get the same conclusion, but we also give a general picture and slightly weaker natural assumptions on the road to prove the embeddedness. In particular, as a first step, we use that the coordinates are independent first or second eigenfunctions, and that their multiplicity is bounded by
3

) to deduce the maximal dimension of the target manifold. Notice also that Theorem

was left open in
[

The main idea to prove Theorem
0.1
is to look for Riemannian metrics
g_{t}
that maximize linear combinations of first and second eigenvalues
g\mapsto\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)+t\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)
among all the metrics on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Here we define for any metric
g
,

\bar{\lambda}_{k}(g)=\lambda_{k}(g)A_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{2}),

the scale-invariant
k
-th eigenvalue where
A_{g}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is the area of the sphere with respect to
g
and

\lambda_{0}(g)=0<\lambda_{1}(g)\leq\lambda_{2}(g)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{k}(g)\to+\infty\text{ as }k\to+\infty

are the eigenvalues of the Laplacian
\Delta_{g}
with respect to
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
counted with multiplicity. The parameter
t>0
is exactly the one that appears in Theorem
0.1
.

Let’s recall some results about maximization/minimization problems of Laplace eigenvalues on a surface
\Sigma
. It is well known from works by Yang-Yau
[
YY80
]
and Li-Yau
[
LY82
]
(first eigenvalues) and Korevaar
[
Kor93
]
(higher eigenvalues) that
g\mapsto\overline{\lambda}_{k}(g)
is bounded by a constant depending only on
k
and the topology of
\Sigma
. We know that
\inf_{g}\overline{\lambda}_{k}(g)=0
and that it is never realized on connected surfaces. Indeed, minimizing sequences are the classical Cheeger dumbells. The simplest way to hope for critical metrics is then a maximization. Therefore, we ask the following very natural questions: can we compute the optimal constant

\Lambda_{k}(\Sigma)=\sup_{g}\overline{\lambda}_{k}(g)

with respect to the topology and
k
? Is there a metric which realizes this supremum ?

Notice that Yang-Yau’s and Li-Yau’s inequalites were a refinement of a celebrated result by Hersch on the sphere to any topology. Since our paper is devoted to the spherical case for the bottom of the spectrum, let’s highlight the Hersch inequality:

Theorem 0.3

(Hersch
[
Her70
]
)
.

Let
g
be a smooth metric on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then

(0.2)

\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)}+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)}+\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{3}(g)}\geq\frac{3}{8\pi}

with equality if and only if
g
is a round metric.

From this theorem, he deduced that for metrics
g
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
again,

(0.3)

\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)}+\frac{2}{\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)}\geq\frac{3}{8\pi}

with equality if and only if
g
is a round metric and that

(0.4)

\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)\leq 8\pi

with equality if and only if
g
is a round metric. The latter inequality gives a complete answer to the two previous questions on the sphere. However, the refinements by Yang and Yau of the inequality (
0.4
) are not optimal anymore for surfaces with higher genuses, except in genus
2
(see
[
Kar19a
]
), and they left open the question of existence of maximizers for years. In the case of the tori, Berger
[
Ber73
]
conjectured that the flat equilateral torus is the maximizer of the first eigenvalue. This result was proved by Nadirashvili
[
Nad96
]
, opening knew methods to build maximizers of one eigenvalue for any topology, notably using that the maximizers have to be induced metrics of a minimal immersion into a sphere.

After several works for (not necessarily oriented) surfaces with low genuses and for
k\leq 2
initiated by
[
Nad96
]
, a global variational procedure for maximization of one eigenvalue was then given in
[
Pet14a
]
for
k=1
and in
[
Pet18
]
for
k>1
. We obtain new maximizers of higher genuses (see
[
Pet14a
]
) and the combination of
[
Pet18
]
and
[
Eji98
]
leeds to an explicit computation of
\Lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
(see
[
KNPP19
]
) and this method was extended for
\Lambda_{k}(\mathbb{RP}^{2})

[
Kar19b
]
. Since then, other different variational methods also appeared (see e.g
[
KS20
]
in the special cases
k=1
and
k=2
).

We highlight that while the round sphere is the unique maximizer of
g\mapsto\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)
, the functional
g\mapsto\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)
does not have any maximizer at all. Indeed, it is known from
[
Nad02
]
,
[
Pet14a
]
and
[
KNPP19
]
that

(0.5)

\Lambda_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=16\pi\text{ and that }\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)<16\pi

for any smooth metric
g
on the sphere.
16\pi
corresponds to the renormalized eigenvalue of two spheres of same area. In fact, this property is general for higher eigenvalues (see
[
KNPP19
]
),
\Lambda_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=8\pi k
and there is not any maximizer but we can build maximizing sequences converging to a disjoint union of
k
spheres with same area.

In the current paper, we use the very general result coming from
[
Pet21
]
(see also
[
Pet22
]
) of the case of one eigenvalue, for variational problems on
combination
of eigenvalues. For instance, the functional involved in the left-hand side of the Hersch inequality (
0.2
) is admissible in this setting: we ask for minimizers. Since the motivation of the current paper is to build embedded minimal spheres into ellipsoids, we focus on combinations of two eigenvalues because of Theorem (
0.2
), so that we look at functionals like the one involved in (
0.3
). Of course, the inequality (
0.3
) shows that we do not build non-planar minimal spheres by minimization of this functional. However, the admissible functionals given in
[
Pet21
]
(see Theorem
1.2
below) are numerous and we can choose for instance functionals like

(0.6)

g\mapsto\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)}+\frac{s}{\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)}

for some parameter
s>0
. For
s\leq 2
, the round sphere is again the unique minimizer but we can hope that there are some
s>2
such that minimizers give existence of non-planar minimal spheres into ellipsoids. As explained in Section
1
, there is such a parameter
s
. In order to prove Theorem
0.1
, we figured out that minimizing the functional

(0.7)

g\mapsto\frac{1}{\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)+t\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)}

for some parameter
t>0
is also interesting to build a one parameter family of non planar minimal surfaces into ellipsoids (see Section
1
). However, for deep reasons (see Theorem
1.2
below), we need to check some ”gap properties” on eigenvalues for this one.

More precisely, we need the sequence of eigenvalues
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g_{k})
associated to minimizing sequence of metrics
g_{k}
built in
[
Pet21
]
for the functional (
0.7
) not to converge to
0
. Indeed, if
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g_{k})\to 0
, we cannot prevent from topological degeneration of the sequence of the surfaces
(\mathbb{S}^{2},g_{k})
as
k\to+\infty
: one can loose connectedness of the limiting surface. This degeneration occurs if we look at the functionnal
g\mapsto\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)
. Indeed, as mentioned in (
0.5
),
g\mapsto\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)
cannot have maximizers but
\Lambda_{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})=16\pi
corresponds to the second eigenvalue of the disjoint union of two round spheres with same area. Then natural maximizing sequences are sequences of metrics on the sphere which ”bubble converge” to two round spheres with same area. However, using a very precise asymptotic analysis on the first and second eigenvalues of such a sequence of metrics, we prove that

Theorem 0.4
.

There is a one parameter family of metrics
g_{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that

\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g_{\varepsilon})=\frac{4\pi}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\left(\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}}\right)\text{ and }\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g_{\varepsilon})=16\pi-48\pi\varepsilon^{2}+o\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

If for one minimizing sequence
g_{k}
for (
0.7
), we have
\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g_{k})\to 0
, then we must have
sup_{g}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)+t\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)\right)=16\pi t
by (
0.5
). Testing this functional for the one parameter family
g_{\varepsilon}
given by Theorem
0.4
, we have

\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g_{\varepsilon})+t\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g_{\varepsilon})=16\pi t+\frac{4\pi}{\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\left(\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}}\right)\text{ as }\varepsilon\to 0

so that
sup_{g}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{1}(g)+t\bar{\lambda}_{2}(g)\right)>16\pi t
. Therefore, we obtain the ”gap assumption” that garanties convergence of some minimizing sequences to a smooth metric on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

.

If

2.1.

In this part, we explain the link between minimal immersions into ellipsoids and the Laplace eigenvalue of the induced metric times some function of the coordinates of the minimal immersion.
Let

Another well-known characterisation of an immersion

Conformality is characterized by the vanishing of

Let

are eigenfunctions on the sphere endowed with the (smooth, possibly with conical singularities) metric

(3.1)

g_{\Phi}=\frac{\left\langle\Lambda\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi\right\rangle_{g_{0}}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}g_{0}

where

\Lambda=diag\left(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{m}\right)

\mathcal{E}=\left\{(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m};\left\langle\Lambda x,x\right\rangle:=\lambda_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}x_{m}^{2}=1\right\}.

Notice that there is no reason for

to be the pull-back of the Euclidean metric by

\Lambda=\lambda I_{m}
. We assume that the coordinates of

3.1.
Nodal sets of eigenfunctions

In this short section, we give the main ingredients of the proof. They come from the properties of nodal sets and nodal domains. We recall that the nodal set of an eigenfunction is the set of vanishing points of the function and that the nodal domains are the connected components of the complementary of the nodal set.

As noticed for instance in
[
HON99
]
, we know the topology of the nodal sets of first and second eigenfunctions on the sphere. By the celebrated Courant nodal theorem, first eigenfunctions have exactly two nodal domains, and second eigenfunctions have two or three nodal domains. Moreover, the nodal set of eigenvalues is locally diffeomorphic to the zero set of a polynomial of two variables. Therefore the zero set of first eigenfunctions has to be an embedded circle. The zero set of second eigenfunctions has to be

•

either an embedded smooth circle,

•

or a disjoint union of two embedded smooth circles,

•

or an
\infty
-shaped curve (a union of two embedded circles having a unique intersection at a singularity point and smooth outside this singularity point).

In particular, one simple but useful remark is that any continuous map
\alpha:[0,1]\to E_{2}
, where
E_{2}
the vector space of second eigenfunctions, such that
\alpha(0)=-\alpha(1)
must satisfy that there is
t\in[0,1]
such that the nodal set of
\alpha(t)
is an embedded smooth circle. Indeed, if not, this means that every eigenfunction in
\alpha([0,1])
has three nodal domains. On the sphere, two of them are simply connected while the third one is not simply connected. By connectedness reasons, for any
t\in[0,1]
, the two simply connected nodal sets of
\alpha(t)
have the same constant sign. By assumption this is not the case for
\alpha(0)
and
\alpha(1)=-\alpha(0)
and we obtain a contradiction.

By
[
HON99
]
, we also deduce that the multiplicity of the first eigenvalue is bounded by
3
and the multiplicity of the second one is also bounded by
3
. Therefore,
\Phi
has at most
4
coordinates. Since
\Phi
is a (possibly branched) conformal immersion, it also has at least
3
coordinates.

Moreover, for any eigenfunction
\psi
, we have by the Hopf lemma that for any
x\in\psi^{-1}(0)

(3.2)

x\text{ is a smooth point of }\psi^{-1}(0)\Leftrightarrow\nabla\psi(x)\neq 0.

3.2.
Case of 2 dimensional target ellipsoids

We assume that
\Phi
has
3
coordinates. Since
\Phi
is a (possibly branched) conformal harmonic immersion then
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{E}_{2}
is a branched cover. If one of the coordinates is a first eigenfunction then the nodal set is a smooth embedded circle. If all the coordinates are second eigenfunctions, then up to a rotation of the (rotationally symmetric) ellipsoid, one of the coordinates must be an eigenfunction such that the nodal set is a smooth embedded circle (see Section (
3.1
)). This means that the degree of the branched cover is one. Then
\Phi
is an embedding.

3.3.
Case of 3 dimensional target ellipsoids

Notice that the current subsection can be seen as an independent section, except that we prove in Step 1 that any (possibly branched) minimal immersion by first and second eigenfunctions do not have branched points. We give, natural assumptions (see Step 9) in order to prove that they are embedded. These assumptions are implied by the symmetric assumptions of Theorem
0.2
. This section gives a general picture and justifies why we add these symmetries in the next subsection.

We assume that
\Phi
has
4
coordinates. We denote
\Phi=(\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3})
the coordinates of
\Phi
and we assume that
\phi_{0}
is a first eigenfunction with respect to

. Then
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3}
are second eigenfunctions. We have by definition of

that

(3.3)

\lambda_{1}\phi_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\left|\eta\right|^{2}=1

The strategy is to prove that the restriction of

is an injective map on the nodal set of
\phi_{0}
and on the two nodal domains of
\phi_{0}
. For that, we focus on the structure of nodal set

of second eigenfunctions

for

. We define

A_{1}=\{v\in\mathbb{S}^{2};N_{v}\text{ is an embedded circle }\}

A_{2}=\{v\in\mathbb{S}^{2};N_{v}\text{ is the disjoint union of two embedded circles }\}

B=\{v\in\mathbb{S}^{2};N_{v}\text{ is an $\infty$-shaped curve }\}

From section
3.1
, we know that
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is the disjoint union of

,
A_{2}
and

, that

and

are open sets while

is a closed set and that

. More generally, two antipodal points of

cannot be in the same connected component of

.

We also define

C=\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{2};\text{ there is }v\in B,z\text{ is the unique singular point of }N_{v}\}.

In fact, we can characterize
B
and
C
thanks to (
3.2
): for
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we have in any conformal chart that

(3.4)

z\in C\Leftrightarrow\det\left(\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)=0.

Indeed
z\in C
iff there is
v\in B
such that
z
is the unique singular point of
N_{v}
iff there is
v\in B
such that
\left\langle v,\eta(z)\right\rangle=0
and
\left\langle v,\nabla\eta(z)\right\rangle=0
iff
\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
. We also deduce that

(3.5)

B=\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\bigcup_{z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}^{\perp}=\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\bigcup_{z\in C}\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}^{\perp}.

Finally, we deduce from Section
3.1
that for any
z\in C
, then the rank of the familly
\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}
is equal to
2
. Indeed, if not then
\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}^{\perp}
contains at least one great circle of the sphere. It would give existence of two antipodal points which are in the same connected component of
B
. This is a contradiction.

Step 1
:
\nabla\eta
never vanishes and then
\Phi
does not have any branched point. This is the immediate consequence of the previous remark.
\nabla\eta(z)=0
for some

would imply that the rank of the family
\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}
is less than
1
.

Thanks to Step 1,
\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathcal{E}
is an immersion and we can define

a unit normal to
\Phi
. This means that in a conformal chart, for any
z\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the family
(\Phi_{x}(z),\Phi_{y}(z),n(z),\Lambda\Phi(z))
is orthogonal, where
\Lambda=diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2})
. We denote
n=(n_{0},\tilde{n})
, where
n_{0}
is the first coordinate of
n
. Notice that

(3.6)

\det\left(\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)=0\Leftrightarrow n_{0}(z)=0.

giving from (
3.4
) a new characterization of
C=n_{0}^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
.
Up to take
-n
we choose

such that
\det\left(\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)
and

have the same sign for any

.

We set

C^{\prime}=\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{2},\eta(z)\subset Span\left(\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)\}.

Of course

.

Step 2
:
C
is the disjoint union of
\left(C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})\right)
and

.

Proof of Step 2
: Indeed, we would like to prove that if
z\in C
, then

\phi_{0}(z)=0\Leftrightarrow Span\left(\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)\text{ is a line}.

If

is a line, then we have that
(\star,0,0,0)\in Span\left(\Phi_{x}(z),\Phi_{y}(z)\right)
. We know that
(\lambda_{1}\phi_{0}(z),\lambda_{2}\eta(z))\in Span\left(\Phi_{x}(z),\Phi_{y}(z)\right)^{\perp}
. This gives that
\phi_{0}(z)=0
.

Now, if
Span\left(\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)
is a plane, then since
z\in C
,
\eta(z)\in Span\left(\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)
. We can write
\eta(z)=\alpha\eta_{x}(z)+\beta\eta_{x}(z)
for some constants
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
(\Phi_{x},\Phi_{y},\Lambda\Phi)
is an orthogonal family, we obtain

1-\sigma_{1}\phi_{0}^{2}(z)=\sigma_{2}\left|\eta(z)\right|^{2}=\sigma_{2}\left\langle\eta(z),\alpha\eta_{x}(z)+\beta\eta_{x}(z)\right\rangle=-\sigma_{2}\phi_{0}(z)\left(\alpha\phi_{0,x}(z)+\beta\phi_{0,y}(z)\right),

so that
\phi_{0}(z)\neq 0
.

Notice that
\left(C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})\right)
and

must be disjoint sets because if not,

would satisfy that the rank of the family
\{\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\}
is less than
1
. This is not possible.

Step 3
: For any
z\in C
,
n_{0}
is sign changing at the neighborhood of
z
. In particular, we have that
\left(C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})\right)=\phi_{0}^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
or
\left(C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})\right)=\emptyset
. Moreover, the set
\eta^{-1}(\{0\})
is finite and cannot be isolated in
C^{\prime}
.

Proof of Step 3
:

We notice that
n_{0}
satisfies an elliptic equation and we aim at applying the maximum principle. We know that
\Phi
is a minimal immersion. Then
n
is harmonic so that

\Delta n\in Span\{n,\Lambda\Phi\}.

We write it as

\Delta n=\left|\nabla n\right|^{2}n+\beta\Lambda\Phi

where
\beta
is computed thanks to the property
\Delta\left(\left\langle n,\Lambda\Phi\right\rangle\right)=0
as

\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}\beta=2\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\Lambda\Phi\right\rangle-\frac{\left\langle\Lambda n,\Lambda\Phi\right\rangle}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\left\langle\Lambda\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi\right\rangle.

We notice that

\left\langle\Lambda\Phi,\Lambda n\right\rangle=\lambda_{1}^{2}\phi_{0}n_{0}+\lambda_{2}^{2}\left\langle\tilde{n},\eta\right\rangle=-\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})n_{0}\phi_{0}

noticing that
\left\langle n,\Lambda\Phi\right\rangle=0
and that

\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\Lambda\Phi\right\rangle=\lambda_{1}\nabla n_{0}\nabla\phi_{0}+\lambda_{2}\left\langle\nabla\tilde{n},\nabla\eta\right\rangle=-\left(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}\right)\nabla n_{0}\nabla\phi_{0}

noticing for the last inequality that since
\Phi
is minimal, we have that the mean curvauture is equal to
0
, which implies that
\left\langle\nabla n,\nabla\phi\right\rangle=0
. We obtain

\Delta n_{0}=\left(\left|\nabla n\right|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\phi_{0}^{2}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{4}}\left\langle\Lambda\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\right)n_{0}-\frac{2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\phi_{0}\nabla n_{0}\nabla\phi_{0}

Notice that

\frac{2\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\phi_{0}\nabla\phi_{0}=\frac{\nabla\left(\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\phi_{0}^{2}\right)}{\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\phi_{0}^{2}}=-\nabla\left(\ln\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}\right)

where we used
\lambda_{1}\phi_{0}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\left|\eta\right|^{2}=1
to compute
\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}=\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\phi_{0}^{2}
. We obtain the equation

(3.7)

-div\left(\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}\nabla n_{0}\right)=\left(\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}\left|\nabla n\right|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{1}^{2}(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\phi_{0}^{2}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\left\langle\Lambda\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\right)n_{0}

and
z\in C
is never a local maximum or minimum point of
n_{0}
.

To continue the proof of step 3, since
\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})
is a smooth embedded closed curve (
\phi_{0}
is a first eigenfunction) and by Step 2, we deduce that
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})
is connected if and only if
C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})=\emptyset
and that
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})
is disconnected if and only if
C\cap\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})=\phi^{-1}\left(0\right)
.

To complete the proof of step 2, we have that if
\eta^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
is isolated in
C
, then it contains a closed curve. However, we must have that
\eta^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
is finite. Indeed, if
\eta^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
is infinite, by compactness of
\eta^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
, we can find an accumulation point
z_{\infty}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that
z_{k}\to z_{\infty}
as

and
z_{k}\in\eta^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
. Up to a subsequence, there is
\tau\in T_{z_{\infty}}\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that
\tau=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{z_{\infty}-z_{k}}{\left|z_{\infty}-z_{k}\right|}
and we get that
D\eta(z_{\infty})(\tau)=0
. We obtain a contradiction because the set
\mathbb{S}^{2}\cap\{\eta_{x}(z_{\infty}),\eta_{y}(z_{\infty}),\eta(z_{\infty})\}\subset B
is a circle, giving existence of two antipodal points in the same connected component of
B
: a contradiction.

Step 4
: If
C=\emptyset
, then
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a diffeomorphism and
\Phi
is embedded.

Proof of Step 4
: Notice that

w:=\left(\eta_{x}-\left\langle\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|},\eta_{x}\right\rangle\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}\right)\wedge\left(\eta_{y}-\left\langle\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|},\eta_{y}\right\rangle\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}\right)=\left|\eta\right|^{2}\left(\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}\right)_{x}\wedge\left(\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}\right)_{y}

satisfies

w=\eta_{x}\wedge\eta_{y}+\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|^{2}}\wedge\left(\left\langle\eta,\eta_{y}\right\rangle\eta_{x}-\left\langle\eta,\eta_{x}\right\rangle\eta_{y}\right)

so that

\left\langle\eta,w\right\rangle=\left\langle\eta,\eta_{x}\wedge\eta_{y}\right\rangle

never vanishes if
C=\emptyset
. Therefore,
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is an immersion and it is a covering map since
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is simply connected. Therefore it is a diffeomorphism. In particular, it is injective. This proves that
\Phi
is injective and that
\Phi
is an embedding. Notice that in this case, all the eigenfunctions have two nodal domains.

Step 5
: If
\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)\subset C
, then
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is an embedded curve, moreover, it is a convex curve in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof of Step 5
:

Step 5.1
:
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is an immersed curve.

Proof of Step 5.1
: Let
z(t)
with
t\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be an arc length parametrization of
\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})
. Notice that
\left|\eta(z(t))\right|=\lambda_{2}^{-1}
for any

. We set
p(t)=\eta(z(t))
and we compute in a conformal chart

p^{\prime}(t)=z_{1}^{\prime}(t)\eta_{x}(z(t))+z_{2}^{\prime}(t)\eta_{y}(z(t)).

We know that
\phi_{0}(z(t))=0
so that

z_{1}^{\prime}(t)\phi_{0,x}(z(t))+z_{2}^{\prime}(t)\phi_{0,y}(z(t))=0.

and we deduce that up to take the parametrization
-z(t)
,

(3.8)

z_{1}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{\phi_{0,y}(z(t))}{\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t))}\text{ and }z_{2}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\phi_{0,x}(z(t))}{\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t))}

where we know that
\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t))\neq 0
since

and
\phi_{0}
is a first eigenfunction.

Now, knowing that we have a conformal parametrization:

\left|\eta_{y}\right|^{2}-\left|\eta_{x}\right|^{2}=\phi_{0,x}^{2}-\phi_{0,y}^{2}\text{ and }\left\langle\eta_{x},\eta_{y}\right\rangle=-\phi_{0,x}\phi_{0,y}

and that

\eta_{x}=-\phi_{0,y}.\tau\text{ and }\eta_{y}=\phi_{0,x}.\tau

. We obtain that

p^{\prime}(t)=\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t)).\tau(t)\neq 0

is an immersion.

Step 5.2
:
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a locally convex curve.

Proof of Step 5.2
: Again, let
z(t)
with
t\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be an arc length parametrization of
\phi_{0}^{-1}(\{0\})
and
p(t)=\eta(z(t))
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
\left|p(t)\right|=1
. We set

v(t)=p(t)\wedge\frac{p^{\prime}(t)}{\left|p^{\prime}(t)\right|}.

We aim at proving that the geodesic curvature
\left\langle\left(\frac{p^{\prime}(t)}{\left|p^{\prime}(t)\right|}\right)^{\prime},v(t)\right\rangle
never vanishes. This is equivalent to prove that
\left\langle p^{\prime\prime}(t),v(t)\right\rangle
never vanishes. Since
\Phi
is a minimal surface, we have the equations

(3.9)

\begin{cases}\Phi_{x,x}=u_{x}\Phi_{x}-u_{y}\Phi_{y}+en+\frac{\left|\Phi_{x}\right|^{2}_{\Lambda}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\Lambda\Phi\\
\Phi_{y,y}=-u_{x}\Phi_{x}+u_{y}\Phi_{y}+gn+\frac{\left|\Phi_{y}\right|^{2}_{\Lambda}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\Lambda\Phi\\
\Phi_{x,y}=u_{y}\Phi_{x}+u_{x}\Phi_{y}+fn+\frac{\left\langle\Phi_{x},\Phi_{y}\right\rangle_{\Lambda}}{\left|\Lambda\Phi\right|^{2}}\Lambda\Phi\\
\end{cases}

where
e^{2u}=\left|\Phi_{x}\right|^{2}=\left|\Phi_{y}\right|^{2}
is the conformal factor and

are the coefficients of the second fundamental form of the immersion into the ellipsoid. Since the immersion is conformal and minimal, we have

In particular,

\eta_{x,x}-e\tilde{n}\in Span(\eta,\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\text{ and }\eta_{y,y}+e\tilde{n}\in Span(\eta,\eta_{x},\eta_{y})\text{ and }\eta_{x,y}-f\tilde{n}\in Span(\eta,\eta_{x},\eta_{y})

Now, notice that

\begin{split}p^{\prime\prime}(t)-&\left((z_{1}^{\prime}(t))^{2}\eta_{x,x}(z(t))+2z_{1}^{\prime}(t)z_{2}^{\prime}(t)\eta_{x,y}(z(t))+(z_{2}^{\prime}(t))^{2}\eta_{y,y}(z(t))\right)\\
&\in Span\left(\eta(z(t)),\eta_{x}(z(t)),\eta_{y}(z(t))\right)=\{v(t)\}^{\perp}=Span\left(p(t),p^{\prime}(t)\right)\end{split}

and that
\tilde{n}(t)=\pm v(t)
and (
3.8
) to deduce that

\left\langle p^{\prime\prime}(t),v(t)\right\rangle=\pm\frac{1}{\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|^{2}}\left(e\left(\phi_{0,y}^{2}-\phi_{0,x}^{2}\right)-2f\phi_{0,x}\phi_{0,y}\right).

Let’s prove that this quantity never vanishes. Up to a rotation of the conformal parametrization, we assume that for a given
t
,
z^{\prime}(t)=(1,0)
. This means that
\phi_{0,x}(z(t))=\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t))
and that
\phi_{0,y}(z(t))=0
(the vertical axis is tangent to
\phi_{0}^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)

) so that

\left\langle p^{\prime\prime}(t),v(t)\right\rangle=\mp e(t).

Since we know by assumption that
C=n_{0}^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)=\phi_{0}^{-1}\left(\{0\}\right)
it is clear that
n_{0,y}(z(t))=0
and that
n_{0,x}(z(t))
is the normal derivative of
n_{0}
along the curve
C
at
t
. Since
n_{0}
satisfies the elliptic equation (
3.7
), we know that
n_{0,x}(z(t))\neq 0
. Now, we have that

n_{x}=-e^{2u}e\Phi_{x}-e^{2u}f\Phi_{y}+\left\langle n_{x},\Phi\right\rangle\Lambda\Phi

and we deduce
n_{0,x}(z(t))=-e^{2u(z(t))}e(z(t))\phi_{0,x}(z(t))
so that

\left\langle p^{\prime\prime}(t),v(t)\right\rangle=\pm\frac{n_{0,x}(z(t))}{\phi_{0,x}(z(t))}e^{2u(z(t))}=\pm\frac{\left|\nabla n_{0}\right|(z(t))}{\left|\nabla\phi_{0}\right|(z(t))}\frac{\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}(z(t))}{2}\neq 0.

Step 5.3
:
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a globally convex curve. In particular, it is a simple curve.

Proof of Step 5.3
: We recall that
\{-v(t),v(t)\}\in B
is the unique pair such that the nodal set of the function
z\mapsto\left\langle\eta(z),\pm v(t)\right\rangle
is an
\infty
-shaped curve. Moreover, the unique double point of this curve is
z(t)
. In particular, the curve
t\mapsto v(t)
is simple.

Now, we deduce from Step 5.2 that
v
is an immersion and that
v
is locally convex. Indeed, we have that
(p,\frac{p^{\prime}}{\left|p^{\prime}\right|},v)
is a Frenet frame of
p
, that is

\left|p\right|^{2}=\left|v\right|^{2}=1\text{ and }\left\langle p,v\right\rangle=\left\langle p^{\prime},v\right\rangle=0

We deduce that
\left\langle p,v^{\prime}\right\rangle=0
so that
v^{\prime}
and
p^{\prime}
are colinear. We also have that

\left\langle p^{\prime},v^{\prime}\right\rangle=-\left\langle v^{\prime\prime},p\right\rangle=-\left\langle p,v^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle.

We deduce from the first equation that
v^{\prime}
never vanishes so that
v
is an immersion and then that
\left\langle v^{\prime\prime},v\wedge v^{\prime}\right\rangle
never vanishes so that
v
is locally convex.

By a result by Little
[
Lit70
]
, we know that a simple locally convex curve on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
has to be a globally convex curve. Then
v
is a globally convex curve. This means that
v
satisfies (up to take
-p
) for any

,
\left\langle v(s),p(t)\right\rangle>0
(and of course, for any

,
\left\langle v(t),p(t)\right\rangle=0
). Now we also deduce that
p
is a globally convex curve and that in particular, it is a simple curve.

Step 6
: If
C=\phi_{0}^{-1}\left({\{0\}}\right)
, then
\Phi
is embedded.

Proof of Step 6
: The maps
\frac{\eta}{\left|\eta\right|}:\phi_{0}^{-1}\left(\mathbb{R}^{*}_{+}\right)\to\mathbb{S}^{2}

are immersions (on

, by definition of

, we deduce that
\Phi
is injective. Therefore,
\Phi
is an embedding.

Step 7
: If
\eta^{-1}(\{0\})=\emptyset
, then

is a disjoint union of closed curves.

Proof of Step 7
:

Step 7.1
We set the map defined in a conformal chart by

\psi(z)=\det\left(\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z),\eta_{y}(z)\right)=\left\langle\eta(z),\eta_{x}(z)\wedge\eta_{y}(z)\right\rangle.

For any

Now, by the Green representation formula,

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,1}(0)

, it suffices to prove that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)
is uniformly bounded. Integrating again (
4.31
), we have that

\begin{split}\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(r)-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)=&\int_{r}^{1}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{s}^{1}\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}e^{2\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}\left(-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(t)\right)tdt\right)ds\\
\leq&-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{r}^{1}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{s}^{1}e^{2\widetilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}tdt\right)ds\\
\leq&-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\end{split}

so that choosing
r=\rho\varepsilon
for any fixed positive constant
\rho>0
, we have by (
4.14
) that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(\rho\varepsilon)=\varphi^{N}_{\star,2}(R)+o(1)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and

-\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)\left(1-\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\left(\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\right)\leq-\varphi^{N}_{\star,2}(\rho)+o(1)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since we have (
4.2
), choosing
\rho
such that
2\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain that
\widetilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(1)
is uniformly bounded.

Then, since
\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}
is uniformly bounded, we have that for any
\rho>0
,

\int_{\rho\varepsilon}^{1}\left(\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}\right)^{2}e^{2\tilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}dx\leq\frac{C}{\rho^{2}}

for a constant
C
independent from
\rho
and
\varepsilon
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
and then
\rho\to+\infty
on the
L^{2}
formula for
\varphi_{\varepsilon,2}
, we get that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\varphi_{\star,2}^{N}\right)^{2}e^{2u}dx=\frac{4\pi}{3}\hbox{ and }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi_{\star,2}^{N}e^{2u}dx=0\hskip 2.84544pt.

Then, by orthogonality with the constant functions, we must have that
\lambda_{\star,2}=2
, and the inverse stereographic projection of
\left(\varphi_{\star,2}\right)^{N}
has to be a first eigenfunction on the sphere. We have the following linear combination:

\varphi_{\star,2}^{N}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}a_{\star,i}Y_{i}\hskip 2.84544pt,

where
Y_{i}(x)=\pi_{N}^{-1}\left(x\right).e_{i}
with
\sum_{i=0}^{3}\left(a_{\star,i}\right)^{2}=1
. Since
\varphi_{\star,2}^{N}
is radial and
\varphi_{\star,2}^{N}(0)>0
, we obtain that
a_{\star,0}=1
and that
a_{\star,1}=a_{\star,2}=0
.

Now, we aim at estimating
\delta_{\varepsilon}=\left|\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right|
.

We set
R_{\varepsilon}(x)=\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(x)-\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}(0)Y^{0}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)
so that
R_{\varepsilon}
is a radial function satisfying
R_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
satisfying the following equation

(4.32)

\Delta R_{\varepsilon}-2e^{2u_{\varepsilon}}R_{\varepsilon}=\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right)e^{2u_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}e^{2v_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}

in
\mathbb{D}
. Integrating this equation against
R_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain

(4.33)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|\nabla R_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}=&\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}R_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu}R_{\varepsilon}+\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right)\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{2u_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}R_{\varepsilon}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{D}}e^{2v_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}R_{\varepsilon}\\
\leq&\left\|\varphi_{\varepsilon,2}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\left|\left(\partial_{\nu}Y^{0}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\right|+\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+2\pi\left|\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right|+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)\right)\\
\leq&C\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\left(\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+\delta_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\end{split}

for a positive constant
C
independent from
\varepsilon
. Letting

(4.34)

\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+\delta_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\hskip 2.84544pt,

we get that

(4.35)

\int_{\mathbb{D}}\left|\nabla R_{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\leq O\left(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Letting
R_{\varepsilon}^{N}(x)=R_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon x)
, we get from (
4.32
) that

(4.36)

\Delta R_{\varepsilon}^{N}-2e^{2u}R_{\varepsilon}^{N}=\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right)e^{2u}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}^{N}+\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}\frac{4\varepsilon^{4}}{\left(1+\varepsilon^{4}\left|x\right|^{2}\right)^{2}}\tilde{\varphi}^{N}_{\varepsilon,2}\hskip 2.84544pt,

So that dividing this equation by
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
, by standard elliptic theory, we obtain up to the extraction of a subsequence that

(4.37)

\frac{R_{\varepsilon}^{N}}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\to R_{\star}^{N}\hbox{ in }\mathcal{C}^{2}\left(\mathbb{D}_{R}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
for any
\rho>0
. We also have that
\frac{\delta_{\varepsilon}}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\to\delta_{\star}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and
\frac{\varepsilon^{4}}{\alpha_{\varepsilon}}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. From (
4.36
),
R_{\star}^{N}
satisfies

(4.38)

\Delta R_{\star}^{N}-2e^{2u}R_{\star}^{N}=\delta_{\star}e^{2u}Y^{0}\hskip 2.84544pt.

We set
R_{\star}:=R_{\star}^{N}\circ\pi_{N}
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{N\}
. We obtain

(4.39)

\Delta_{h}R_{\star}-2R_{\star}=\delta_{\star}y_{0}

in
\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{N\}
. By (
4.35
), we have that
R_{\star}\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
and we can extend
R_{\star}
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
so that the equation holds in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Integrating (
4.39
) against
y_{0}
gives
\delta_{\star}=0
. By definition of
\delta_{\star}
, we obtain that
\delta_{\varepsilon}=o\left(\alpha_{\varepsilon}\right)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and we obtain by (
4.34
)

(4.40)

\delta_{\varepsilon}=o\left(\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Moreover, we obtain that
\Delta_{h}R_{\star}=2R_{\star}
. This means that
R_{\star}
is a first eigenfunction in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Since
R_{\star}
only depends on
y_{0}
and
R_{\star}(S)=0
, we obtain that
R_{\star}=0
. We obtain that for any
\rho>0

(4.41)

R_{\varepsilon}(\rho\varepsilon)=o\left(\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Let’s prove now that

(4.42)

\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}=O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Let
0\leq r_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
be such that
\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}=\left|R_{\varepsilon}(r_{\varepsilon})\right|
. By (
4.40
), if
r_{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
, we easily deduce (
4.42
). We then assume that
\varepsilon=o(r_{\varepsilon})
. By integration on the equation (
4.32
) satisfied by the radial function
R_{\varepsilon}
, we have that for
r\leq 1
,

(4.43)

\begin{split}R_{\varepsilon}(r)-R_{\varepsilon}(1)=&-\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}\Delta R_{\varepsilon}tdt\right)ds\\
=&-2\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}R_{\varepsilon}e^{2u_{\varepsilon}}tdt\right)ds-\left(\lambda_{\varepsilon}^{2}-2\right)\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}e^{2\tilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}tdt\right)ds\\
&-2\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}e^{2v_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}tdt\right)ds+(1-r)\left(R_{\varepsilon}\right)^{\prime}(1)\end{split}

so that since
R_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(1)=O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, that

\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}e^{2u_{\varepsilon}}tdt\right)ds=\ln\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)

and that

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(r)-\varphi_{\varepsilon}(1)=-\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{1}^{s}e^{2\tilde{\omega}_{\varepsilon}}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}tdt\right)ds\hskip 2.84544pt,

we have since
\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon,2}
is uniformly bounded and by definition of
\delta_{\varepsilon}
that

(4.44)

\left|R_{\varepsilon}(r)-R_{\varepsilon}(1)\right|\leq 2\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\ln\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)+O\left(\delta_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt.

In particular for
r=r_{\varepsilon}
, since we have (
4.40
), that
R_{\varepsilon}(r_{\varepsilon})=\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}
and that
\varepsilon=o(r_{\varepsilon})
, we obtain that

(4.45)

\left\|R_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left|R_{\varepsilon}(1)\right|+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt.

Applying again (
4.44
) for
r=\rho\varepsilon
for a fixed
\rho>0
such that
2\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\rho^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}
, we obtain that

(4.46)

\left|R_{\varepsilon}(1)\right|\leq\left|R_{\varepsilon}(\rho\varepsilon)\right|+O\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)\hskip 2.84544pt.

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. By (
4.41
), we easily obtain the expected result (
4.42
).

Thanks to (
4.42
) and (
4.40
), we then obtain that
\delta_{\varepsilon}=o(\varepsilon^{2})
in this case. This concludes subsection (
4.4.2
).
