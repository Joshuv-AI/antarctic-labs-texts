Pavel Gubkin

Abstract
. We prove that the Weyl function of the one-dimensional Dirac operator on the half-line ℝ + \mathbb{R}_{+} with exponentially decaying entropy extends meromorphically into the horizontal strip { 0 ⩾ Im z > − δ } \{0\geqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>-\delta\} for some δ > 0 \delta>0 depending on the rate of decay. If the entropy decreases very rapidly then the corresponding Weyl function turns out to be meromorphic in the whole complex plane. In this situation we show that poles of the Weyl function (scattering resonances) uniquely determine the operator.

The main object of the present paper is the one-dimensional Dirac operator
D_{Q}
on the positive half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)

(1)

\displaystyle D_{Q}=J\frac{d}{dr}+Q,

where

is a symmetric
2\times 2
zero-trace real potential and
J=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is the square root of the minus identity matrix. We will use the entropy approach developed by R. Bessonov and S. Denisov in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
to prove a version of the theorem by P. Nevai and V. Totik from
[

). We start with the formulation of the original Nevai-Totik theorem to explain the motivation of the work and then introduce all necessary objects from the spectral theory of Dirac operators and state the results of the present paper.

1.1.
Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. Nevai-Totik theorem

The basics of the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle can be found in the books
[
31
]
by G. Szegő and
[
27
]
by B. Simon. In this section we give some definitions and formulate the result by P. Nevai and V. Totik from
[
21
]
, where they proved that the rate of exponential decay of the recurrence coefficients is the same as the radius of analyticity of the inverse Szegő function.

Let
\mu=w\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits+\mu_{s}
be a probability measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\{\zeta\colon|\zeta|=1\}
; here
\mathop{\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
is a normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle,
w
and
\mu_{s}
are the density and the singular part of
\mu
with respect to
\mathop{\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
. Assume that
\mu
is nontrivial, i.e., that the support of
\mu
is not a finite set. Then the functions
1,z,z^{2}\ldots
are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\mu)
and therefore there exists a sequence
\{\Phi_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
of monic polynomials orthogonal in
L^{2}(\mu)
. They satisfy

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(z)=z\Phi_{n}(z)-\overline{\alpha_{n}}\Phi_{n}^{*}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\overline{z})}
and
\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{D}=\{\omega\colon|\omega|<1\}
– the recurrence coefficients. The Szegő theorem states that
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class, i.e.,
\displaystyle\log w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
if and only if
\sum_{n\geqslant 0}|\alpha_{n}|^{2}<\infty
. Moreover, in this case for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
the sequence
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)/\left\|\Phi_{n}^{*}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
converges to
\Pi(z)
, where
\Pi
is an outer function in the unit disc satisfying
{|\Pi(\zeta)|^{-2}=w(\zeta)}
for almost every
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
, see Theorem 2.3.5 in
[
27
]
. If
\mu
is an a. c. measure from the Szegő class on the unit circle let

\displaystyle r_{\Pi}=\inf\left\{r\colon\Pi\text{ is analytic in }\{|w|<r^{-1}\}\right\}

be the inverse radius of analyticity of
\Pi
. Otherwise put
r_{\Pi}=1
. Also let

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\alpha_{n}|^{1/n}

be the rate of exponential decay of the recurrence coefficients.

Theorem A

(Nevai-Totik theorem; Theorem 1,
[
21
]
; Chapter 7,
[
27
]
)
.

For any nontrivial probability measure
\mu
on the unit circle we have
r_{\alpha}=r_{\Pi}
.

Methods of the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle can be applied to the spectral theory of Dirac operators by means of Krein systems, see Section
2.1
. In the original proof of Theorem
A
the relation
\alpha_{n}=-\overline{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}
plays a significant role. It does not have an analogue for Krein systems. Let us give a prove of the inequality
r_{\alpha}\leqslant r_{\Pi}
in a way that will later work in the setting of spectral theory. The idea of the proof is similar to the idea in Chapter 12.3 of the Szegő ’s book
[
31
]
where the asymptotic behaviour of orthogonal polynomials is studied via the Christoffel minimizing functions

(2)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(z)=\lambda_{n}(\mu,z)=\min\left\{\left\|P\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}\colon\deg P\leqslant n,\;P(z)=1\right\},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

If
r_{\Pi}=1
then the claim
r_{\alpha}\leqslant r_{\Pi}
follows immediately.
Assume that
r_{\Pi}<1
, then
\mu
belongs to the Szegő class,
\Pi
is well-defined and
d\mu(\zeta)=|\Pi(\zeta)|^{-2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits
.
We have, see Chapter 2.2 in
[
27
]
,

(3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)=\prod\limits_{n=0}^{n-1}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2}),\qquad\lambda_{\infty}(0)=\inf_{n}\lambda_{n}(0)=|\Pi(0)|^{-2}.

Fix an arbitrary number
r_{0}\in[1,r_{\Pi}^{-1})
. The Taylor series of
\Pi
converges absolutely in
\{|z|\leqslant r_{0}\}
and
\Pi
does not have zeroes on
\mathbb{T}
, because the converse contradicts the assumption
\mu(\mathbb{T})<\infty
. Hence for
\zeta\in\mathbb{T}
we the uniform bound

(4)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\Pi(\zeta)-h_{n}(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|=O\left(r_{0}^{-n}\right),\quad n\to\infty

holds, where
h_{n}
is the
n
-th Taylor polynomial of
\Pi
. Substituting
h_{n}/h_{n}(0)
into (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|\frac{h_{n}}{h_{n}(0)}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\frac{1}{|h_{n}(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|h_{n}(\zeta)\right|^{2}d\mu(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|h_{n}(\zeta)\Pi^{-1}(\zeta)\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left|1+\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}1+2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\left(\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right)+\left|\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta).

Since
\displaystyle\frac{h_{n}-\Pi}{\Pi}
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, the second term vanishes after the integration. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{lambda n formula}}}{{=}}\lambda_{n}(0)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}1+\left|\frac{h_{n}(\zeta)-\Pi(\zeta)}{\Pi(\zeta)}\right|^{2}\mathop{d\mathrm{m}}\nolimits(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{taylor polynomial convergence}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{|\Pi(0)|^{2}}+O\left(r_{0}^{2n}\right),\quad n\to\infty.

We see that
\lambda_{n}(0)=\prod_{n=0}^{n-1}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2})
is exponentially close to
\lambda_{\infty}(0)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2})
. Hence

|\alpha_{n}|=O\left(r_{0}^{-n}\right)
as
n\to\infty
. Consequently

r_{\alpha}\leqslant r_{0}^{-1}
and the inequality
r_{\alpha}\leqslant r_{\Pi}
follows because
r_{0}
can be taken arbitrary close to
r_{\Pi}^{-1}
.

The sequence
\lambda_{n}(0)
is connected to the entropy function
\mathcal{K}(n)=-\log\prod_{k\geqslant n}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2})
introduced by R. Bessonov and S. Denisov in
[
3
]
. They also transferred
\mathcal{K}(n)
to the theory of Krein systems; this object will be the key tool of the present paper.

D. Damanik and B. Simon in the paper
[
6
]
proved a version of the Nevai-Totik theorem for orthogonal polynomials on the real line. They showed the equality between the degree of exponential decay of Jacobi parameters
a_{n},b_{n}
, i.e.,
\limsup\left(|a_{n}-1|+b_{n}\right)^{1/n}
, and the inverse radius of analyticity of the corresponding Jost solution
u
, for details see
[
6
]
or Section 13.7 in
[
28
]
.

1.2.
Main results

Recall that we are studying one-dimensional Dirac operator (
1
). Throughout this paper we assume that the entries
p,q
of
Q
are real-valued and
p,q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
(sometimes we will simply write
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
). The latter means that
p,q\in L^{1}([0,r])
for every
r>0
. Let us introduce the key objects of the spectral theory of Dirac operators; for a reference we use the book
[
19
]
by B. Levitan and I. Sargsjan. Consider the corresponding boundary value problem:

(5)

\displaystyle JN^{\prime}(t,\lambda)+Q(t)N(t,\lambda)=\lambda N(t,\lambda),\quad N(0,\lambda)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad t\geqslant 0.

Let
\theta_{\pm}
and
\varphi_{\pm}
be the entries of
N
,
N(t,\lambda)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\theta_{+}(t,\lambda)&\varphi_{+}(t,\lambda)\\
\theta_{-}(t,\lambda)&\varphi_{-}(t,\lambda)\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
For any potential
Q
there exists a unique Borel measure
\sigma_{Q}
on the real line such that
(1+x^{2})^{-1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},d\sigma_{Q})

and the mapping

(6)

\displaystyle(f_{1},f_{2})\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f_{1}(x)\theta_{+}(x,\lambda)+f_{2}(x)\theta_{-}(x,\lambda)\,dx,\quad f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

is an isometric operator between
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\oplus L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{Q})
.
The measure
\sigma_{Q}
is called the spectral measure of
D_{Q}
. The Weyl function of
D_{Q}
is an analytic function in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>0\}
defined by the relation

(7)

\displaystyle m(z)=-\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\varphi_{+}(t,z)}{\theta_{+}(t,z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

The spectral measure and the Weyl function are connected by the relation

(8)

\displaystyle m(z)-m(z_{0})=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda-z}-\frac{1}{\lambda-z_{0}}\right)\,d\sigma_{Q}(\lambda),\quad z,z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

A Borel measure
\sigma=w\,dx+\sigma_{s}
on the real line
\mathbb{R}
belongs to the Szegő class if

(9)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\sigma(x)}{1+x^{2}}<\infty,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log w(x)}{1+x^{2}}dx>-\infty.

Because of the inequality
\log w<w
, the second integral can’t diverge to
+\infty
and therefore for any measure in the Szegő class we have
\frac{\log w}{1+x^{2}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Furthermore, in this case there exists an outer function
\Pi
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
such that
\Pi(i)>0
and
|\Pi(x)|^{-2}=w(x)

for Lebesgue almost every point
x
on the real line (see Section 4 in
[
11
]
).
We will call
\Pi
the inverse Szegő function of
\sigma
.

Denote the solution of (
5
) for
\lambda=0
by
N_{Q}(t)=N_{Q}(t,0)
. Let also

(10)

\displaystyle H_{Q}(t)=N_{Q}^{*}(t)N_{Q}(t),\quad E_{Q}(r)=\det\int_{r}^{r+2}H_{Q}(t)\,dt-4.

As we will see in Section
2.2.2
, the matrix
H_{Q}
is the Hamiltonian of the canonical system corresponding to
D_{Q}
and
E_{Q}
is the leading term of the entropy function
\mathbf{K}_{H}
of this Hamiltonian. If
Q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then
E_{Q}
can be bounded via the Sobolev norm of the function
p+iq
, for details see Theorem
4
below.
For
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
we prove that the exponential decay of
E_{Q}
implies the existence of analytic continuation of
m
into a strip in the lower half-plane; we denote the half-plane
\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>-\Delta\}
by
\Omega_{\Delta}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p,q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be real-valued functions and
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Assume that there exists
\delta>0
such that
E_{Q}(r)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right)
as
r\to\infty
. Then

(1)

the spectral measure of
D_{Q}
is absolutely continuous and belongs to the Szegő class (
9
);

(2)

the inverse Szegő function of
D_{Q}
continues analytically into
\Omega_{\delta/4}
;

(3)

the Weyl function of
D_{Q}
continues meromorphically into
\Omega_{\delta/4}
.

In particular, if
E_{Q}(r)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right)
for every
\delta>0
, then the Szegő function and the Weyl function of
D_{Q}
extend into the whole complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.

We prove Theorem
1
and give details on the continuations of
\Pi
and
m
in Section
3
. The constant
1/4
in the theorem is sharp for some potentials, see Section
5.2
.

The simplest class of potentials with exponentially decaying entropy is a class of compactly supported potentials. Furthermore, the entropy function of exponentially decreasing or super-exponentially decreasing potentials, i.e., potentials that satisfy

\displaystyle|p(r)|+|q(r)|=O\left(e^{-ar}\right),\quad r\to\infty,

for some
a>0
or for all
a>0
is also exponentially decaying, see Theorem
5
below. Theorem
1
is widely known in these situations, see
[
14
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
26
]
,
[
10
]
. When
m_{Q}
extends meromorphicaly below the real line one can speak about the scattering resonances. Namely,
z
is a resonance of
D_{Q}
of multiplicity
n
if
m
has a pole of order
n
in
z
, see the book
[
8
]
by S. Dyatlov and M. Zworski for the general background on resonances and the papers
[
12
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
16
]
by E. Korotyaev for the case of Dirac operators. If
E_{Q}
decays super-exponentially then, by Theorem
1
,
m_{Q}
is meromorphic in the whole complex plane and the resonances are also well-defined. Let us introduce the class

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{\alpha>1}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha},\;\;\;\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}=\left\{Q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\colon E_{Q}=O\left(\exp(-{r^{\alpha}})\right),\;r\to\infty\right\}.

In Section
5.3
we show that the resonances are exactly the zeroes of the corresponding Szegő function
\Pi
if
E_{Q}
decreases super-exponentially and prove the following result.

Theorem 2
.

Resonances of the Dirac operator uniquely determine its potential in the class
\mathcal{E}
.

Description of the sets which can be resonances sets is an open problem. We plan to continue working in this direction.

1.3.
Square summable potentials

Denote by
\Delta_{E}
the rate of exponential decay of the entropy function, i.e., let

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}=\sup\left\{\delta\colon E_{Q}(r)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty\right\}.

Furthermore, recall that in our notation
\Omega_{\delta}=\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>-\delta\}
and define

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Pi}=\sup\left\{\delta\colon\Pi\mbox{ extends analyticaly into }\Omega_{\delta}\mbox{ such that }\frac{\Pi(x-i\delta)}{x+i}\in H^{2}(\mathbb{C}_{+})\right\}

if
\sigma
is an absolutely continuous measure from the Szegő class (
9
) and let
\Delta_{\Pi}=0
otherwise. The numbers
\Delta_{E}
and
\Delta_{\Pi}
play the roles of
r_{\alpha}
and
r_{\Pi}
from Nevai-Totik theorem
A
. Recall that
r_{\alpha}=r_{\Pi}
; in the following theorem we state that
\Delta_{E}
and
\Delta_{\Pi}
satisfy the two-sided inequality. In this sense Theorem
3
can be regarded as a version of Theorem
A
for Dirac operators.

Theorem 3
.

Let
p,q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be real-valued functions.
Then
4\Delta_{\Pi}\leqslant\Delta_{E}\leqslant 8\Delta_{\Pi}
.

If
Q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, then
\Delta_{E}
can be computed. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the isometric Fourier transform on the real line and define Sobolev space of tempered distributions as

\displaystyle W^{-1}_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\left\{f\colon\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{W^{-1}_{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(\mathcal{F}f)^{2}(\eta)}{1+\eta^{2}}d\eta<\infty\right\}.

Theorem 4.1 in
[
4
]
states that the Sobolev norm of the function
p+iq
is comparable to
\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}E_{Q}(r+n)
. More precisely, denote by
\mathbf{1}_{[r,\infty)}
the indicator function of the set
[r,\infty)
and let
f_{r}=(p+iq)\mathbf{1}_{[r,\infty)}
. Then the inequality
C^{-1}\left\|f_{r}\right\|_{W^{-1}_{2}}^{2}\leqslant\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)\leqslant C\left\|f_{r}\right\|_{W^{-1}_{2}}^{2}

holds with some constant
C
depending only on
\left\|p+iq\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\Delta_{E}=\sup\left\{\delta\colon\left\|f_{r}\right\|_{W^{-1}_{2}}^{2}=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty\right\}=2\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{r}\ln\left\|f_{r}\right\|_{W^{-1}_{2}}\right).

Therefore Theorem
3
can be rewritten in the following form.

Theorem 4
.

Let
p,q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be real-valued functions and let
f_{r}=(p+iq)\mathbf{1}_{[r,\infty)}
.
Then

\displaystyle 2\Delta_{\Pi}\leqslant\liminf_{r\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{r}\ln\left\|f_{r}\right\|_{W^{-1}_{2}}\right)\leqslant 4\Delta_{\Pi}.

Proof of Theorem
3
can be found in Section
4
. In Section
5.2
we show that the first inequality in Theorem
3
is sharp for some potentials just as the constant
1/4
in Theorem
1
.

1.4.
Examples

For a given
Q\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
(especially, for
Q\notin L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
) it can be hard to answer whether or not
E_{Q}
decreases fast enough. The following theorem provides some examples of potentials with a rapidly decaying entropy.

Theorem 5
.

In the two following situations, the assertion
E_{Q}(r)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right)
as
r\to\infty
holds.

•

Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p\\
p&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
p
is real-valued and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right|=O\left(e^{-\delta r/2}\right),\quad r\to\infty;

•

Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}-q&p\\
p&q\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, where
p
and
q
are real-valued,
\displaystyle{\sup_{r\geqslant 0}\int_{r}^{r+1}\left(|p|+|q|\right)\,ds<\infty}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right|=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}q(s)\,ds\right|=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty.

The fact that the oscillation may compensate the growth of the potential and lead to the properties typical to the properties of decreasing potentials is not new, see
[
20
]
,
[
30
]
,
[
25
]
and Appendix
2
to XI.
8
in
[
22
]
.

If we consider the function
p(x)=e^{x}\sin\left(e^{2x}\right)
then

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}p(x)\,dx\right|=\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}e^{x}\sin\left(e^{2x}\right)\,dx\right|=\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{e^{2r}}^{e^{2t}}\frac{\sin\left(s\right)}{2\sqrt{s}}\,ds\right|=O\left(e^{-r}\right),\quad r\to\infty.

Hence, by Theorems
1
and
5
the Weyl function of
D_{Q}
with
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p\\
p&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
is meromorphic in the half-plane
\{z\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>-1/2\}
. A similar argument for
p(x)=xe^{x^{2}}\sin\left(e^{2x^{2}}\right)
gives an example of a ‘‘large’’ potential corresponding to a meromorphic Weyl function in the whole complex plane.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Krein systems

2.1.1.
General definitions

Let
a\in L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a complex-valued function on the positive half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The Krein system with the coefficient
a
is the following system of differential equations:

(12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P(r,\lambda)=i\lambda P(r,\lambda)-\overline{a(r)}P_{*}(r,\lambda),&\quad\,\,P(0,\lambda)=1,\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\lambda)=-a(r)P(r,\lambda),&\quad P_{*}(0,\lambda)=1.\end{cases}

After seminal work
[
17
]
of M. Krein, the solutions of Krein system (
12
) are called the continuous analogs of polynomials orthogonal on the unit circle. Using Krein systems, one can transfer methods from the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle to the spectral problems for self-adjoint differential operators.
Detailed account of this approach can be found in the paper
[
7
]
by S. Denisov.

For any Krein system (
12
) there exists a unique Borel measure
\sigma_{a}
on the real line such that

(1+x^{2})^{-1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{a})

and the mapping

(13)

\displaystyle f\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(r)P(r,\lambda)\,dr,

initially defined on simple measurable functions with compact support, can be continuously extended to an isometry from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\sigma_{a})
. This measure is called the spectral measure of the Krein system. The following theorem is called the Krein theorem and can be regarded as an analog of the Szegő theorem in the setting of the Krein systems.

Theorem B

(Krein theorem; Section 8 in
[
7
]
;
[
32
]
)
.

Let
\sigma_{a}
be the spectral measure of Krein system (
12
) and let
P,P_{*}
be its solutions. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

\sigma_{a}
belongs to Szegő class (
9
) on the real line;

(b)

for some point
\lambda_{0}
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\colon\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z>0\}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}|P(r,\lambda_{0})|^{2}\,dr<\infty;

(c)

there exist a sequence
r_{n}\to\infty
and a number
\gamma\in[0,2\pi)
such that for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}
the limit

\displaystyle\Pi(\lambda)=e^{-i\gamma}\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{*}(r_{n},\lambda)

If the equivalent assertions of the Krein theorem hold then (see Lemma 8.6 in

2.1.2.
Reproducing kernels and the minimization problem.

Let
PW_{[0,r]}
denote the Paley-Wiener space of entire functions
f
that can be represented in the form

\displaystyle f(z)=\int_{0}^{r}\varphi(s)e^{izs}\,ds,\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\quad\varphi\in L^{2}[0,r].

The function

(15)

\displaystyle k_{r}(z^{\prime},z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{r}P(s,z)\overline{P(s,z^{\prime})}\,ds

is the reproducing kernel in
PW_{[0,r]}
at the point
z^{\prime}
, see Lemma 8.1 in
[
7
]
. In other words, for every
f\in PW_{[0,r]}
we have

\displaystyle f(z^{\prime})=\langle f,k_{r}(z^{\prime},\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}(\sigma_{a})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\overline{k_{r}(z^{\prime},x)}\,d\sigma_{a}(x).

For
r>0
, define

(16)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{r}(z)=\mathbf{m}_{r}(\sigma_{a},z)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\left\|f\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\sigma_{a})}\colon f\in PW_{[0,r]},\;f(z)=1\right\},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

The function
\mathbf{m}_{r}
is the analog of the Christoffel function (
2
). Lemma 8.2 in
[
7
]
says that

(17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{r}(z)=\left(2\pi k_{r}(z,z)\right)^{-1}=\left(\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,z)|^{2}\,ds\right)^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

For every
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
, the functions
P
,
P_{*}
satisfy the following Christoffel-Darboux formula:

(18)

\displaystyle P(r,\lambda)\overline{P(r,\mu)}-P_{*}(r,\lambda)\overline{P_{*}(r,\mu)}

\displaystyle=i(\lambda-\overline{\mu})\int_{0}^{r}P(s,\lambda)\overline{P(s,\mu)}\,ds,

(19)

\displaystyle|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|^{2}-|P(r,\lambda)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\lambda\int_{0}^{r}|P(s,\lambda)|^{2}\,ds,

see Lemma 3.6 in
[
7
]
.
Furthermore, a simple calculation shows that

(20)

\displaystyle P(r,z)=e^{izr}\overline{P_{*}(r,\overline{z})},\qquad P_{*}(r,z)=e^{izr}\overline{P(r,\overline{z})},\qquad r\geqslant 0,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

Together with Theorem
B
, relation (
19
) gives

(21)

\displaystyle|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2}=2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}|P(s,\lambda)|^{2}\,ds,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

Define
\mathbf{m}_{\infty}(z)=\inf_{r}\mathbf{m}_{r}(z)
. It follows that
\mathbf{m}_{\infty}
can be represented by

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbf{m}_{\infty}(\lambda)=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}|P(r,\lambda)|^{2}\,dr\right)^{-1}=\frac{2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\lambda}{|\Pi(\lambda)|^{2}},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

2.1.3.
Connections with the Dirac operator.

Consider a dual Krein system, i.e., Krein system (

but with the initial value
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle X^{\prime}(r,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}i\lambda&-\overline{a(r)}\\
-a(r)&0\end{pmatrix}X(r,\lambda),\quad X(0,\lambda)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where

\displaystyle X(r,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}P(r,\lambda)&\hat{P}(r,\lambda)\\
P_{*}(r,\lambda)&-\hat{P}_{*}(r,\lambda)\end{pmatrix}.

Define

(24)

\displaystyle p_{a}(r)=-2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a(2r),\qquad q_{a}(r)=2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits a(2r),\qquad Q_{a}=\begin{pmatrix}-q_{a}(r)&p_{a}(r)\\
p_{a}(r)&q_{a}(r)\end{pmatrix}.

A calculation shows (see Chapter 13 in

\displaystyle Y(r,\lambda)=\frac{e^{-i\lambda r}}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
-i&i\end{smallmatrix}\right)X(2r,\lambda)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)Y^{\prime}(r,\lambda)+Q_{a}Y(r,\lambda)=\lambda Y(t,\lambda),\quad Y(0,\lambda)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-i\end{smallmatrix}\right).

This differential system differs from Dirac system (
5
) only in the definition of the square root of the minus identity matrix. It can be easily seen that
f=(f_{1},f_{2})^{T}
solves
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)f+Q_{a}f=\lambda f
if and only if

solves
Jf^{\#}-Q_{a}f^{\#}=\lambda f^{\#}
. Therefore, the fundamental solution of Dirac system (

-Q_{a}
can be expressed in terms of

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{+}(t,\lambda)&\varphi_{+}(t,\lambda)\\
\theta_{-}(t,\lambda)&\varphi_{-}(t,\lambda)\end{pmatrix}=\frac{e^{-i\lambda r}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}P(2r,\lambda)+P_{*}(2r,\lambda)&i\hat{P}(2r,\lambda)-i\hat{P}_{*}(2r,\lambda)\\
iP(2r,\lambda)-iP_{*}(2r,\lambda)&-\hat{P}(2r,\lambda)-\hat{P}_{*}(2r,\lambda)\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 13.1 in
[
7
]
provides a relation between the spectral measures of
D_{-Q_{a}}
and the Krein system with the coefficient
a
.
Our normalization in definitions (
6
) and (
13
) of spectral measures differs from the one in
[
7
]
. Because of that Theorem 13.1 actually implies the coincidence of the measures
\sigma_{-Q_{a}}
and
\sigma_{a}
.

Using relation (
24

D_{-Q_{a}}
and the Krein system with the coefficient

correspond to each other.

By (
7
) and (

), for every Dirac system we have

\displaystyle m(z)=-\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\varphi_{+}(r,z)}{\theta_{+}(r,z)}=-\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{i\hat{P}(2r,z)-i\hat{P}_{*}(2r,z)}{P(2r,z)+P_{*}(2r,z)}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{i\hat{P}_{*}(r,z)-i\hat{P}(r,z)}{P_{*}(r,z)+P(r,z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

In the Szegő case, both
P(\cdot,z)
and
\hat{P}(\cdot,z)
are in

such that

and

\displaystyle m(z)=i\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\hat{P}_{*}(r_{n},z)}{P_{*}(r_{n},z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+}.

By Lemma

, we can choose a subsequence

such that both numerator and denominator converge. Therefore
there exists a constant

such that

(26)

\displaystyle m(z)=e^{i\gamma}\frac{\hat{\Pi}(z)}{\Pi(z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},

where
\Pi
and
\hat{\Pi}
are the inverse Szegő functions of

and

The latter equation will be crucial in the proof of part (

.

2.2.
Canonical systems

The entropy approach is based on the reduction of Dirac and Krein systems to a more general form of differential equations – the canonical Hamiltonian system. Below we introduce key objects and definitions of the theory, for details see
[
23
]
and
[
24
]
.

A Hamiltonian is a matrix-valued mapping of the form

(27)

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}h_{1}&h\\
h&h_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where
h,h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are real-valued functions from
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Moreover, it is assumed that
H
satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits H(t)>0
and
\det H(t)\geqslant 0
for every
t\geqslant 0
.
A Hamiltonian
H
is called singular if

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits H(s)\,ds=+\infty
. We will call a Hamiltonian
H
trivial if it coincides with
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
or
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and nontrivial otherwise.
The canonical system with Hamiltonian
H
is the differential equation

(28)

\displaystyle J\frac{\partial}{\partial t}M(t,z)=zH(t)M(t,z),\quad M(0,z)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\quad t\geqslant 0.

The solution
M
of (
28
) is often represented as

(29)

\displaystyle M(t,z)=(\Theta(t,z),\Phi(t,z))=\begin{pmatrix}\Theta_{+}(t,z)&\Phi_{+}(t,z))\\
\Theta_{-}(t,z)&\Phi_{-}(t,z))\end{pmatrix}.

The Weyl function of the canonical system is defined by

\displaystyle m(z)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{w\Phi_{+}(t,z)+\Phi_{-}(t,z)}{w\Theta_{+}(t,z)+\Theta_{-}(t,z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}_{+},\quad w\in\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}.

If the Hamiltonian is singular, this limit is correctly defined and does not depend on
w
.
Furthermore,
m
has a strictly positive imaginary part in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and therefore admits the following Herglotz representation:

(30)

\displaystyle m(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{x-z}-\frac{x}{x^{2}+1}\right)d\sigma(x)+az+b,

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\geqslant 0
are constants and
\sigma
is a Borel measure satisfying
(1+x^{2})^{-1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},\sigma)
. The measure
\sigma
is called the spectral measure of
H
and of canonical system (
28
).

2.2.1.
Reduction of the Dirac system to the canonical system.

As we mentioned it earlier, (
28
) is a more general form of a differential system than (
5
) or (
12
). In this subsection we outline the reduction of Dirac system (
5
) to the Hamiltonian canonical system. We omit the calculations, for a more detailed explanation see
[
24
]
or Section 2.4 in
[
1
]
.

Consider Dirac system (
5
) with the potential
Q
and define
N_{Q}(t)=N(t,0)
. Then
M(t,z)=N_{Q}^{-1}(t)N(t,z)
is the fundamental solution of canonical system (
28
) with the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{Q}(t)=N_{Q}^{*}(t)N_{Q}(t).

Moreover, the spectral measure of the canonical system with Hamiltonian
H_{Q}
coincides with the spectral measure of the Dirac operator

(see Section 2.4 in
[
1
]
).

2.2.2.
Entropy of a canonical system.

In the papers
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
R. Bessonov and S. Denisov described the class of Hamiltonians for which the corresponding spectral measure belongs to the Szegő class on the real line. They introduced the criterion in terms of the entropy function of the Hamiltonian. Let us define it.

Consider an arbitrary singular nontrivial Hamiltonian
H
and let
H_{r}
be its
r
-shift, i.e., let
H_{r}
be such that
H_{r}(x)=H(r+x)
,
r,x\geqslant 0
. Denote by
m_{r},\sigma_{r}
and
w_{r}
the Weyl function, the spectral measure and the density of the spectral measure corresponding tho the canonical system with the Hamiltonian
H_{r}
. Next, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{H}(r)=\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits m_{r}(i),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{H}(r)=\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits m_{r}(i),\qquad\mathcal{J}_{H}(r)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\log(w_{r}(x))}{1+x^{2}}\,dx.

The entropy function
\mathcal{K}_{H}
of
\sigma
is defined as

(31)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{H}(r)=\log\mathcal{I}_{H}(r)-\mathcal{J}_{H}(r),\quad r\geqslant 0.

If
\sqrt{\det H}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
then we can define

(32)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\left(\det\int_{\eta_{n}(r)}^{\eta_{n+2}(r)}H(t)\,dt-4\right),\quad\eta_{n}(r)=\min\left\{x\colon\int_{r}^{x}\sqrt{\det H(t)}dt=n\right\}.

The function
\mathbf{K}_{H}
is called the entropy function of the Hamiltonian
H
. The main result of
[
2
]
is the following theorem.

Theorem C

(Theorem 1.2,
[
2
]
)
.

The spectral measure of a singular nontrivial Hamiltonian
H
belongs to Szegő class (
9
) if and only if
\sqrt{\det H}\notin L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and
\mathbf{K}_{H}(0)<\infty
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle c_{1}\mathcal{K}_{H}(r)\leqslant\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)\leqslant c_{2}\mathcal{K}_{H}(r)\cdot e^{c_{2}\mathcal{K}_{H}(r)},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are some absolute constants.

In the present paper we are interested in the case when
\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)
decreases exponentially fast in
r
. It is equivalent to the exponential decay of
\mathcal{K}_{H}(r)
and, formally, can be written as

(33)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\left(\det\int_{\eta_{n}(r)}^{\eta_{n+2}(r)}H(t)\,dt-4\right)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty

for some
\delta>0
. We have
\eta_{n+1}(r)=\eta_{n}(\eta_{1}(r))
hence the latter in its turn is equivalent to the exponential decay of the first term of the sum, i.e.,

\displaystyle\det\int_{\eta_{0}(r)}^{\eta_{2}(r)}H(t)\,dt-4=\mathbf{K}_{H}(r)-\mathbf{K}_{H}(\eta_{1}(r))=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty.

Notice that if
H=H_{Q}
is the Hamiltonian constructed for the Dirac operator
D_{Q}
in Section
2.2.1
, then
\det H(t)=1
for every
t\geqslant 0
and consequently
\eta_{n}(r)=n+r
for every
n,r\geqslant 0
. In this situation assertion (
33
) becomes

\displaystyle\det\int_{r}^{r+2}H_{Q}(t)\,dt-4=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right),\quad r\to\infty,

which is exactly the assertion
E_{Q}(r)=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right)
from Theorem
1
.

2.3.
Regularized Krein systems

Fix a singular nontrivial Hamiltonian
H
. Assume that the spectral measure
\sigma
corresponding to
H
belongs to the Szegő class and let
\mathcal{K}_{H},\mathcal{I}_{H}
and
\mathcal{R}_{H}
be as in the previous section. In order to simplify the exposition we will omit the index

later on. The regularized Krein system corresponding to

(34)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)

\displaystyle=(z-i)f_{1}(r)\tilde{P}_{r}(z)+izf_{2}(r)\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z),

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{0}^{*}(z)=I_{H}(0)^{-1},

[
1

First of all, let us show that the regularized Krein system defined in
[
1
]
coincides with the one defined by (

\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}^{*}
the regularized Krein system from
[
1
]

\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}=\tilde{P}_{r}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}^{*}=\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}
. Indeed, the initial values of
\tilde{P}_{r}
and
\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}
is chosen so that it coincides with the initial values of

and

hence it is suffices to show that

and
\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}^{*}

(37)
in

\displaystyle=iz\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}(z)+e^{irz}\overline{\left((\overline{z}-i)f_{1}(r)\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}(\overline{z})+i\overline{z}f_{2}(r)\tilde{\mathbf{P}}_{r}^{*}(\overline{z})\right)}

Lemma 2

\displaystyle=e^{irz}\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}(\overline{z})},\quad\tilde{P}_{r}(z)=e^{irz}\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(\overline{z})}.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}|\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\left(\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}(z)}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\tilde{P}_{r}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\left(\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}(z)}\left(iz\tilde{P}_{r}(z)+(z+i)\overline{f_{1}(r)}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)-iz\overline{f_{2}(r)}\tilde{P}_{r}(z)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z|\tilde{P}_{r}(z)|^{2}+2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits\left((z+i)\overline{f_{1}(r)}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}(z)}\right)+2\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits f_{2}(r)|\tilde{P}_{r}(z)|^{2}.

Lemma 4

(Lemma 8,
[

]

The coefficient

\displaystyle|f_{1}(r)|\lesssim\sqrt{|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(r)|}+|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(r)|

Proof.

Due to the definition of

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(r)}{\mathcal{I}(r)}\right|+\left|\frac{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}(r)}{\mathcal{I}(r)}\right|\lesssim\sqrt{|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(r)|}+|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(r)|.

to denote the average of
f
over
I
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle f\rangle_{I}=\frac{1}{|I|}\int_{I}f(x)\,dx.

We fix some segment
I=[a,b]
and define

\displaystyle I_{1}=[a,(a+b)/2],\quad I_{2}=[(3a+b)/4,(a+3b)/4],\quad I_{3}=[(a+b)/2,b]

– the left, the middle and the right half of
I
respectively. This notation is used throughout the whole subsection.

Lemma 9
.

Let
g
be a positive measurable function on
I
. Then

\displaystyle\langle g\rangle_{I}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I}-1\lesssim\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}\left[\langle g\rangle_{I_{n}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{n}}-1\right],

provided that the latter maximum does not exceed
1
.

Proof.

Denote the maximum from the statement of the lemma by
\varepsilon
. We have

\displaystyle\langle g\rangle_{I}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}+\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)\left(\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}+\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left(\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)\left(\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}+\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle\langle g\rangle_{I}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I}-1

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}-1}{2}+\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}-1}{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\left|\left(\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)\left(\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon+\frac{1}{4}\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}\left|\left(1-\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}}\right)\right|

(82)

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon+\frac{1+\varepsilon}{4}\left|\left(1-\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}}\right)\left(1-\frac{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}}\right)\right|.

Let
f=\log g
. By Jensen’s inequality, for
n\in\{1,2,3\}
we have

(83)

\displaystyle 1\leqslant\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{n}}e^{-\langle f\rangle_{I_{n}}}\leqslant\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{n}}\langle e^{-f}\rangle_{I_{n}}=\langle g\rangle_{I_{n}}\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{n}}\leqslant 1+\varepsilon.

It follows that (see formula (4.12) in
[
5
]
or (3.7) in
[
15
]
)

\displaystyle\langle|f-\langle f\rangle_{I_{n}}|\rangle_{I_{n}}\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

As a corollary, we get

\displaystyle\left|\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\langle\left|\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}-\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}\right|\rangle_{I_{1}\cap I_{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\langle\left|\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}-f\right|\rangle_{I_{1}\cap I_{2}}+\langle\left|f-\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}\right|\rangle_{I_{1}\cap I_{2}}

(84)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\langle\left|\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}-f\right|\rangle_{I_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\left|f-\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}\right|\rangle_{I_{2}}\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Inequalities (
83
) and (
84
) yield

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}}{\langle g\rangle_{I_{2}}}-1\right|=\left|\frac{\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{1}}}{\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{2}}}-1\right|=\left|\frac{\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{1}}}{e^{\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}}}\cdot\frac{e^{\langle f\rangle_{I_{1}}}}{e^{\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}}}\cdot\frac{e^{\langle f\rangle_{I_{2}}}}{\langle e^{f}\rangle_{I_{2}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Similarly we can obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}}{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{2}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad\left|\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{2}}}{\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad\left|\frac{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{2}}}{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

Therefore, we have

(85)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\langle g\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g\rangle_{I_{1}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon},\quad\left|\frac{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{3}}}{\langle g^{-1}\rangle_{I_{1}}}-1\right|\lesssim\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

The substitution of (
85
) into (
82
) concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
G
be a matrix-valued function on
I
such that
G(t)\geqslant 0
and
\det G(t)=1
for almost every
t\in I
. Additionally assume that for every unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
t\in I
we have

(86)

\displaystyle\langle G(t)v,v\rangle\approx 1.

Then the inequality

(87)

\displaystyle\det\langle G\rangle_{I}-1\lesssim\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}\left[\det\langle G\rangle_{I_{n}}-1\right]

holds, provided that the latter maximum does not exceed
1
.

Proof.

Denote the maximum from the statement of the lemma by
\varepsilon
. For every
2\times 2
matrix
M
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det M}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\langle Mx,x\rangle}dx.

Rewriting this in polar coordinates, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det M}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-r^{2}\langle M\zeta,\zeta\rangle}r\,dr\right)d\zeta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\zeta}{\langle M\zeta,\zeta\rangle}.

Let
J
be some subinterval of
I
and define
A_{J}=\langle G\rangle_{J}
. By Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det A_{J}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\zeta}{\langle A_{J}\zeta,\zeta\rangle}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left\langle\frac{1}{\langle G\zeta,\zeta\rangle}\right\rangle_{J}d\zeta

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{d\zeta}{\langle G\zeta,\zeta\rangle}\right\rangle_{J}=\left\langle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det G}}\right\rangle_{J}=1.

Therefore

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\left(\left\langle\frac{1}{\langle G\zeta,\zeta\rangle}\right\rangle_{J}-\frac{1}{\langle A_{J}\zeta,\zeta\rangle}\right)d\zeta=2\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det A_{J}}}\right).

Denote
\langle G(t)\zeta,\zeta\rangle
by
g_{\zeta}(t)
then the latter equality can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\langle g_{\zeta}^{-1}\rangle_{J}-\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle^{-1}_{J}\,d\zeta=2\pi\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det A_{J}}}\right).

Next, we see that

\displaystyle\langle g_{\zeta}^{-1}\rangle_{J}-\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle^{-1}_{J}=\frac{\langle g_{\zeta}^{-1}\rangle_{J}\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle_{J}-1}{\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle_{J}}\approx\langle g_{\zeta}^{-1}\rangle_{J}\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle_{J}-1

because of assumption (
86
). Therefore

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det A_{J}}}\approx\int_{\mathbb{T}}F_{J}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,

where
F_{J}(\zeta)=\langle g_{\zeta}^{-1}\rangle_{J}\langle g_{\zeta}\rangle_{J}-1
. Moreover, (
87
) is equivalent to

(88)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}F_{I}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\lesssim\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}}F_{I_{n}}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right].

Define the set
S\subset\mathbb{T}
by

\displaystyle S=\{\zeta\colon F_{n}(\zeta)<1,\;\;n=1,2,3\}.

By Lemma
9
, for
\zeta\in S
we have

(89)

\displaystyle F(\zeta)\lesssim\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}F_{n}(\zeta).

On the other hand, we have
\displaystyle\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}F_{n}(\zeta)\geqslant 1
on
\mathbb{T}\setminus S
and
F_{I}\lesssim 1
on
\mathbb{T}\setminus S
because of assumption (
86
). Hence (
89
) holds on the whole circle with some other constant in
\lesssim
. Inequality (
88
) follows and the proof is concluded.
∎

4.2.2.
Ordered exponent

Let
A
be a matrix-valued function with real entries in
L^{1}_{\mathop{\mathrm{loc}}\nolimits}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. The solution of the differential equation

(90)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X_{A}(r,t)=A(t)X_{A}(r,t),\quad X_{A}(r,r)=I,\quad t\geqslant r

is called the ordered exponent of
A
. It follows from (
90
) that the relation

(91)

\displaystyle X_{A}(r,t)X_{A}(t_{0},r)=X_{A}(t_{0},t)

holds for every
t_{0}\leqslant r\leqslant t
. Moreover,
X_{A}
admits the following explicit representation:

(92)

\displaystyle X_{A}(r,t)=\sum_{m\geqslant 0}\int_{r}^{t}A(t_{1})\int_{r}^{t_{1}}A(t_{2})\int_{r}^{t_{2}}\ldots\int_{r}^{t_{m-1}}A(t_{m})\,dt_{m}\,\ldots dt_{3}\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1},

for details see the book
[
9
]
. We remark that the series converges in the operator norm because the norm of the
m
-th term does nor exceed
\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}\left(\int_{r}^{t}\left\|A(s)\right\|\,ds\right)^{m}
.

Fix an arbitrary unit vector
v
and consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|^{2}=2\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X_{A}(r,t)v,X_{A}(r,t)v\right\rangle=2\langle AX_{A}(r,t)v,X_{A}(r,t)v\rangle.

Hence

\displaystyle 2\left\|A(t)\right\|\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|^{2}\geqslant\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|^{2}\geqslant-2\left\|A(t)\right\|\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle 2\left\|A(t)\right\|\geqslant\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|^{2}\geqslant-2\left\|A(t)\right\|.

Integrating on
[r,t]
and taking the exponent, we get

(93)

\displaystyle\exp\left[\int_{r}^{t}\left\|A(s)\right\|\,ds\right]\geqslant\left\|X_{A}(r,t)v\right\|\geqslant\exp\left[-\int_{r}^{t}\left\|A(s)\right\|\,ds\right].

4.2.3.
Entropy and rescaling.

Recall definition (
10
) of the entropy function. In this subsection we show what happens if we replace the integration segment
[r,r+2]
with
[r,r+2l]
in (
10
) for different parameters
l>0
. Namely, define the scaled entropy function by

(94)

\displaystyle E_{Q}^{(l)}(r)=\frac{1}{l^{2}}\det\int_{r}^{r+2l}H_{Q}(t)\,dt-4,

so that

.
Equation (

\lambda=0

\displaystyle N_{Q}(t)=X_{JQ}(r,t)X_{JQ}(0,r),

\displaystyle H_{Q}(t)=(X_{JQ}(0,r))^{*}(X_{JQ}(r,t))^{*}X_{JQ}(r,t)X_{JQ}(0,r).

For

we have
\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits(JQ(r))=0
hence

(95)

\displaystyle E_{Q}^{(l)}(r)=\frac{1}{l^{2}}\det\int_{r}^{r+2l}(X_{JQ}(r,t))^{*}X_{JQ}(r,t)\,dt-4.

Theorem 7
.

Assume that
p,q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and let the scaled entropy
E_{Q}^{(l)}
be defined by (

f
be an arbitrary nonincreasing function with

as

\displaystyle E_{Q}^{(l)}(r)\lesssim f(r),\quad r\geqslant 0

holds for some

.

Proof.

Assume that (

) holds for

hold for
G_{r}(t)=(X_{JQ}(r,t))^{*}X_{JQ}(r,t)
on the segment

and
\det G_{r}(t)=1
for all

. Furthermore, if

is a unit vector, then

\displaystyle\langle G_{r}(t)v,v\rangle=\left\|X_{JQ}(r,t)v\right\|^{2}_{2}\approx 1,

because of inequality (

) and the assertion

. An application of Lemma

gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}E_{Q}^{(2l_{0})}(r)

\displaystyle=\det\langle G_{r}\rangle_{I}-1\leqslant\max_{n\in\{1,2,3\}}\left[\det\langle G_{r}\rangle_{I_{n}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\max\left[E_{Q}^{(l_{0})}(r),E_{Q}^{(l_{0})}(r+l_{0}),E_{Q}^{(l_{0})}(r+2l_{0})\right]\lesssim f(r).

Therefore (

Proof of Theorem

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
5

5.1.1.
Off-diagonal potentials.

Recall that we have the potential
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&p\\
p&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
where
p
is a real-valued function
p
such that
\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|\int_{r}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right|=O\left(e^{-\delta r}\right)
as
r\to\infty
. In this case both
N_{Q}
and
H_{Q}
defined by (
10
) are diagonal and can be calculated explicitly. Namely,

\displaystyle N_{Q}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\exp\left(-g_{0}(t)\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(g_{0}(t)\right)\end{pmatrix},\qquad H_{Q}(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\exp\left(-2g_{0}(t)\right)&0\\
0&\exp\left(2g_{0}(t)\right)\end{pmatrix},

where
g_{r}(t)=\int_{r}^{t}p(s)\,ds
. Moreover,

\displaystyle E_{Q}(r)=\det\int_{r}^{r+2}H_{Q}(t)\,dt-4

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{r+2}e^{-2g_{0}(t)}\,dt\cdot\int_{r}^{r+2}e^{2g_{0}(t)}\,dt-4

(97)

\displaystyle=\int_{r}^{r+2}e^{-2g_{r}(t)}\,dt\cdot\int_{r}^{r+2}e^{2g_{r}(t)}\,dt-4.

The assumption on
p
of Theorem
5
can be rewritten in the form
\sup_{t\geqslant r}|g_{r}(t)|\lesssim e^{-\delta r/2}
. Hence the inequalities
\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|e^{2g_{r}(t)}-2g_{r}(t)-1\right|\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
and
\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant r}\left|e^{-2g_{r}(t)}+2g_{r}(t)-1\right|\lesssim e^{-\delta r}

hold and (
97
) gives
E_{Q}(r)\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
. This completes the proof in the off-diagonal case.

5.1.2.
General situation.

Recall (
95
) that the the entropy function can be calculated as

(98)

\displaystyle E_{Q}(r)=\det\int_{r}^{r+2}(X_{JQ}(r,t))^{*}X_{JQ}(r,t)\,dt-4,

where
X_{JQ}
is the ordered exponent of
JQ
. We claim that under the assumptions of Theorem
5

X_{JQ}
is close to the identity operator. Indeed, the operator
\int_{r}^{t}Q(s)\,ds
is symmetric and has zero trace, hence
\left\|\int_{r}^{t}Q(s)\,ds\right\|=\sqrt{\left(\int_{r}^{t}p(s)\,ds\right)^{2}+\left(\int_{r}^{t}q(s)\,ds\right)^{2}}\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
. Apply this norm inequality to explicit formula (
92
) for the ordered exponent. We have

\displaystyle\left\|X_{JQ}(r,t)-I\right\|=\left\|\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\int_{r}^{t}JQ(t_{1})\int_{r}^{t_{1}}JQ(t_{2})\int_{r}^{t_{2}}\ldots\int_{r}^{t_{m-1}}JQ(t_{m})\,dt_{m}\,\ldots dt_{3}\,dt_{2}\,dt_{1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\int_{r}^{t}\int_{r}^{t_{1}}\ldots\int_{r}^{t_{m-2}}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\left\|Q(t_{k})\right\|\cdot\left\|\int_{r}^{t_{m-1}}Q(t_{m})\,dt_{m}\right\|dt_{m-1}\ldots dt_{2}\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{s\geqslant r}\left\|\int_{r}^{s}Q(s)\,ds\right\|\cdot\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\int_{r}^{t}\int_{r}^{t_{1}}\ldots\int_{r}^{t_{m-2}}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}\left\|Q(t_{k})\right\|dt_{m-1}\ldots dt_{2}\,dt_{1}

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\geqslant r}\left\|\int_{r}^{s}Q(s)\,ds\right\|\cdot\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\frac{\left(\int_{r}^{t}\left\|Q(t_{1})\right\|\,dt_{1}\right)^{m-1}}{(m-1)!}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-\delta r}\cdot\exp\left[\int_{r}^{t}\left\|JQ(t_{1})\right\|\,dt_{1}\right]\leqslant e^{-\delta r}\cdot\exp\left[\int_{r}^{t}|p(t_{1})|+|q(t_{1})|\,dt_{1}\right].

This for
t\in[r,r+2]
implies
\left\|X_{JQ}(r,t)-I\right\|\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
hence
(X_{JQ}(r,t))^{*}X_{JQ}(r,t)=I+M_{r}(t)
, where
M_{r}
is a matrix-valued function such that
\left\|M_{r}(t)\right\|\lesssim e^{-\delta r}
. To conclude the proof, substitute the latter into (
98
):

\displaystyle E_{Q}(r)=\det\int_{r}^{r+2}\left(I+M_{r}(t)\right)\,dt-4=\det\left(2I+\int_{r}^{r+2}M_{r}(t)\right)\,dt-4\lesssim e^{-\delta r}.

5.2.
On the sharpness of results

It Theorems
3
and
1
we established inequalities between the rate of exponential decay of the entropy function
E_{Q}
and the width of the strip, where the inverse Szegő function and the Weyl function have proper extensions. In this section we give an example of a potentials for which some of the estimates in these two theorems are precise. Consider Krein system (
12
) with the coefficient
a(r)=e^{-r}
. Then the potential of the corresponding Dirac operator
D_{Q}
is given by
Q=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&2e^{-2r}\\
2e^{-2r}&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Let us show that for this
Q
the corresponding inverse Szegő function does not extend into
\Omega_{\delta}
for any
\delta>{\Delta}_{E}/4
and, moreover, the equality
4{\Delta}_{\Pi}={\Delta}_{E}
holds.

The potential Q is off-diagonal hence the entropy can be calculated by formula (
97
). In our situation we have
g_{r}(t)=\int_{r}^{t}2e^{-2t_{1}}\,dt_{1}=e^{-2r}(1-e^{2(t-r)})
.
Then (
97
) gives
E_{Q}(r)\approx e^{-4r}
,
\Delta_{E}=4
and, by Theorem
1
,
\Pi
extends analytically into
\Omega_{1}
. Let us show that
\Pi
does not extend into
\Omega_{\delta}
for any
\delta>1
.
The inequality
|P(r,\lambda)|\leqslant|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|
in
\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
and the Gronwall-Bellman inequality for differential equation (
12
) for
P_{*}
give
|P_{*}(r,\lambda)|\approx 1
uniformly in
\lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
and
r\geqslant 0
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\overline{\lambda})\right|=\left|a(r)P(r,\overline{\lambda})\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Krein system reflection formula}}}{{=}}\left|a(r)e^{i\overline{\lambda}r}\overline{P_{*}(r,\lambda)}\right|=e^{-(1-\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\lambda)r}\left|P_{*}(r,\lambda)\right|.

Therefore
\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,\overline{\lambda})\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
is uniformly bounded in
\{\lambda\colon 1-\varepsilon\geqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\lambda\geqslant 0\}
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Consequently
\Pi
is uniformly bounded in
\Omega_{\delta}
for every
\delta<1
and
{\Delta}_{\Pi}\geqslant 1={\Delta}_{E}/4
. Theorem
3
then implies
4{\Delta}_{\Pi}={\Delta}_{E}
.

On the other hand, for
\lambda=ih
, the Krein system has only real-valued coefficients hence
P_{*}(r,ih)
is real-valued;
P_{*}(r,ih)
is positive for every
r\geqslant 0
because
P_{*}(r,ih)\neq 0
for every
r\geqslant 0
. Therefore the relation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}P_{*}(r,-ih)=e^{-(1-h)r}P_{*}(r,ih)\approx e^{-(1-h)r}

holds uniformly for
h\geqslant 0
. It follows that

\displaystyle|\Pi(-ih)|=\lim_{r\to\infty}|P_{*}(r,-ih)|\approx 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(1-h)\rho}d\rho=\frac{h}{1-h}.

Therefore
|\Pi(-ih)|\to\infty
as
h\to 1^{-}
and
\Pi
cannot be analytically extended to the point
\lambda=-i
and especially into any horizontal half-plane containing
-i
.

5.3.
Resonances. Proof of Theorem
2

Recall definition (

iff

) implies that if
z
is a pole of
m_{Q}
of multiplicity
n
, then
z
is a zero of
\Pi
of multiplicity not greater than
n
. To establish that the multiplicities are the same we need to show that
\Pi
and
\hat{\Pi}
have no common zeroes, which in its turn follows from

(99)

\displaystyle\overline{\Pi(\overline{z})}\hat{\Pi}(z)+\Pi(z)\overline{\hat{\Pi}(\overline{z})}=2,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

When
Q
has compact support the latter relation simply follows from (75) in
[
7
]
; in our situation we need to consider the dual regularized Krein system more carefully.
The coefficients of the dual regularized Krein system are not much different from the original system of (
34
) and (
35
). Namely (see the remark after Lemma 7.3 in
[
2
]
), we need to change the sign of
f_{1}
and leave
f_{2}
the same. In a matrix form two regularized systems become

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}izf_{2}&(z-i)f_{1}\\
(z+i)\overline{f_{1}}&iz(1-f_{2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r,d}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}izf_{2}&-(z-i)f_{1}\\
-(z+i)\overline{f_{1}}&iz(1-f_{2})\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r,d}\end{pmatrix},

where the functions with the subindex
d
form the regularized dual system. Denote the matrix in the first equality by
V
. A simple transformation gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r}\end{pmatrix}=V\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r}\end{pmatrix},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}\\
-\tilde{P}_{r,d}\end{pmatrix}=V\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}\\
-\tilde{P}_{r,d}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence the solution
X
of the
X^{\prime}=VX
with
X(0,z)=I
is given by

\displaystyle X=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}&\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}\\
\tilde{P}_{r}&-\tilde{P}_{r,d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)\tilde{P}_{r,d}(z)+\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}(z)\tilde{P}_{r}(z)\right)=\det X(r,z)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{r}\mathop{\mathrm{trace}}\nolimits V(t,z)\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{r}iz\,dt\right)=e^{irz}.

By (
37
) the latter is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)\overline{\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}(\overline{z})}+\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}(z)\overline{\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(\overline{z})}=2,\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

If
Q\in\mathcal{E}
then from the proof of Theorem
1
it follows that
\displaystyle\Pi(z)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)
and
\displaystyle\hat{\Pi}(z)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\tilde{P}_{r,d}^{*}(z)
for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Hence (
99
) follows and
\Pi(z)=\hat{\Pi}(z)=0
is not possible.

Thus the resonances are the zeroes of the entire function
\Pi
. The counting function of the zeroes is tightly connected to the order of
\Pi
.

Lemma 11
.

If
Q\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>1
then
\Pi
is of order not greater than
\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}
.

Proof.

We need to show that for
z\in\mathbb{C}
, the inequality

\displaystyle|\Pi(z)|\lesssim\exp\left(C|z|^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)

holds for some constant
C
. From Lemma
6
we know the stronger bound
\Pi(z)\lesssim\exp\left(C|z|\right)
in
\mathbb{C}_{+}
. The limit relation
\displaystyle\Pi(z)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\tilde{P}_{r}^{*}(z)
holds for every
z\in\mathbb{C}
hence (
50
) gives

\displaystyle|\Pi(z)|\lesssim(|z|+1)e^{c(|z|+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(\rho/2)|}+|\mathcal{K}^{\prime}(\rho/2)|\right)e^{\rho|\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z|}\,d\rho,\quad\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z\leqslant 0.

We have
E_{Q}(r)\lesssim\exp\left(-r^{\alpha}\right)
hence
\mathcal{K}(r)\lesssim\exp\left(-r^{\alpha}\right)
. It follows that

\displaystyle|\Pi(z)|

\displaystyle\lesssim(|z|+1)e^{c(|z|+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(\rho|\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z|-\rho^{\alpha}/2^{\alpha+1}\right)\,d\rho

\displaystyle\lesssim(|z|+1)e^{c(|z|+1)}\exp\left(c_{1}|\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits z|^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right)\lesssim\exp\left(c_{2}|z|^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha-1}}\right).

∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
be two potentials from
\mathcal{E}
with the same set of resonances with multiplicities
\{z_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
. Let
\sigma_{i},\Pi_{i},m_{i}
and
H_{i}
, for
i=1,2
be the spectral measures, the inverse Szegő functions, the Weyl functions and the Hamiltonians corresponding to
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
. Denote by
E_{n}
the elementary Weierstrass factor,

\displaystyle E_{n}(z)=\begin{cases}(1-z),&n=0,\\
(1-z)\exp\left(\frac{z}{1}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{z^{n}}{n}\right),&n>0.\end{cases}

By Lemma
11
, both
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
are of finite exponential order; let
n
be an integer such that the order of
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}
does not exceed
n
. Hadamard factorization theorem, see Section 4.2 in
[
18
]
, gives that

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}(z)=e^{g_{1}(z)}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{n}\left(\frac{z}{z_{k}}\right)\;\text{ and }\;\Pi_{2}(z)=e^{g_{2}(z)}\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}E_{n}\left(\frac{z}{z_{k}}\right),

where
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are polynomials of degree not greater than
n
.
Hence
\Pi_{1}(z)\Pi_{2}^{-1}(z)=e^{g(z)}
with
g=g_{1}-g_{2}
.
By Lemma
6
there exists
c>0
such that
|\Pi_{1}(z)|\lesssim e^{c|z|}
and
e^{-c|z|}\lesssim|\Pi_{2}(z)|
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
.
Consequently,
\displaystyle\left|e^{g(z)}\right|\lesssim e^{2c|z|}
or
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits g(z)\lesssim|z|+1
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}_{+}}
, which is possible only if
\deg g\leqslant 1
. Let
g(z)=a_{1}z+a_{2}
, where
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are some complex constants. Because of the Szegő class assumption (
9
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits g}{x^{2}+1}=\frac{\log|\Pi_{1}|-\log|\Pi_{2}|}{x^{2}+1}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}).

It follows that
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a_{1}=0
and
|\Pi_{1}(x)|=|\Pi_{2}(x)|e^{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a_{2}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Next, by Lemma
6
, both
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are a. c. hence
d\sigma_{1}=e^{-2\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\nolimits a_{2}}d\sigma_{2}
; relation (
8
) then gives
m_{1}=am_{2}+b
for some constants
a>0
and
b
. Proof of Lemma 2.4 in
[
2
]
implies
H_{2}(t)=A^{*}H_{1}(t)A
with
A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1/\sqrt{a}&b/\sqrt{a}\\
0&\sqrt{a}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
For
t=0
it becomes
I=A^{*}A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1/a&b/a\\
b/a&b^{2}/a+a\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Hence
a=1
,
b=0
and the Weyl functions
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
coincide. Therefore
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
and the proof is finished.
∎
