Weak-inertial effects on destabilized receding contact lines
Akhil Varma

Abstract
It is known that beyond a critical speed, the straight contact line of a partially-wetting liquid destabilizes into a corner. In one of the earliest theoretical works exploring this phenomenon, [ Limat and Stone , Europhys. Lett. 65 (3), 2004] elicited a self-similar conical structure of the interface in the viscous regime. However, noting that inertia is not expected to be negligible at contact line speeds close to, and beyond the critical value for many common liquids, we provide the leading-order inertial correction to their solution. In particular, we find the self-similar corrections to the interface shape as well as the flow-field, and also determine their scaling with the capillary number. We find that inertia invariably modifies the interface into a cusp-like shape with an increased film thickness. Furthermore, when incorporating contact line dynamics into the model, resulting in a narrowing of the corner as the contact line speed increases, we still observe an overall increase in the inertial contribution with speed despite the increased confinement.



I
Introduction

There is something intriguing about raindrops sliding down a window pane that fascinates us all on a rainy day. A keen observer might notice the drops speeding up as they slide down under gravity, forming long tails or rivulets that eventually break into smaller drops. This is a classic example of contact line destabilization. The capillary number, which is the dimensionless velocity of the contact line formed by the liquid interface and the solid surface, is the key player in this phenomenon; It is defined as
\mathrm{Ca}=\eta V/\gamma
, where
\eta
is the viscosity of the liquid,
\gamma
is its surface tension and
V
is the velocity of the moving contact line. It is beyond a critical value
\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}
, that a straight contact line destabilizes and starts to entrain air (at the advancing front) or form cornered tails (at the receding end). For many common liquids, the critical value at the receding contact line is
\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}\sim\mathcal{O}(10^{-3})
.

The instability of receding contact lines of partially wetting liquids were addressed as early as the works of
Blake and Ruschak [2]
,
Petrov and Sedev [3]
. They observed that beyond a critical capillary number, a straight receding contact line transforms into a wedge or corner having some semi-opening angle
\phi
. The slanted orientation of the contact line keeps its normal component of velocity below the threshold for wetting transition. Furthermore, they observed that exceeding the critical capillary number did not alter the normal velocity of the (destabilized) contact line. Instead, it consistently maintained the critical value by adjusting its opening angle accordingly
[
3
]
. This is given by the simple phenomenological relation,
\sin\phi\propto 1/\mathrm{Ca}
.

Since this work, many experimental and theoretical studies followed which aimed at understanding the instability
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
as well as the detailed shape of the destabilized contact line
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. However, the mechanism of this instability is not yet fully understood, and is still an active area of research
[
10
,
11
]
. A comprehensive understanding of the contact line dynamics is indispensable for many engineering applications ranging from precision thin film coatings, immersion lithography, nano-fabrication and ink-jet printing
[
12
,
13
]
to the design of liquid-repellent and anti-fogging surfaces
[
14
]
. They are also crucial for microfluidics and lab-on-chip technologies
[
15
]
.

For a drop of viscous liquid moving down an incline, the instability is observed to occur first at the receding (rear) end, giving rise to the classical ”tear drop” appearance. At velocities much higher than this critical value, the sharp corner transforms into a cusp, eventually breaking into drops at even higher velocities through a pearling instability
[
4
,
7
,
16
]
. The flow close to the corner is viscous-dominated and hence can be approximated as a Stokes flow. This realization led
Limat and Stone [1]
, and later
Snoeijer et al. [17]
to determine the interface profile and the flow field near the receding contact line. A thin-film approximation, assuming a slowly varying interface profile near the corner was assumed for their analysis. The theory matches well with the flow field observed in experiments with silicone drops
[
17
]
.
However, for fast-moving contact lines, inertial effects can be significant
[
18
,
8
]
. For example, consider water and silicone oil
(100\;\mathrm{cP})
drops having a critical capillary number
\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}\approx 4\times 10^{-3}
and liquid mercury, having
\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}\approx 1.5\times 10^{-3}
. The drop velocities in experiments of sliding drops are typically
10-100\;\rm{cm.s^{-1}}
for water and mercury, and
0.1-1\;\rm{cm.s^{-1}}
for silicone oil
[
7
,
8
]
. For these velocities the Reynolds number, which compares the inertial with the viscous effects, at distances of
l=10-100\rm{\mu m}
from the corner turn out to be
\mathrm{Re}_{l}\sim 0.01-0.1
for silicone oil,
\sim 1-10
for water and
\sim 10-100
for mercury. The low Reynolds number for silicone oil probably explains why the model with Stokes flow approximation agrees well with experiments. However, the large Reynolds number in the case of water and mercury drops indicate that inertial effects are dominant, even amounting to orders of
10^{3}
at the scale of the drops
[
8
]
. Thus, it seems that inertia cannot be excluded in the models of moving contact lines of these liquids. Prior theoretical works in this area have largely focused on the role of inertia near straight contact lines
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
or fully wetted films
[
22
,
23
]
, but studies on cornered contact lines are scarce. For dewetting corners,
Kim et al. [24]
suggested using an effective dynamic pressure in the classical lubrication theory to mimic inertia. However, this results in a solution that is qualitatively indifferent from the non-inertial case. Here, we will use a regular perturbation approach to systematically determine the inertial contribution and establish the correct scaling associated with it.

In this theoretical work, we shall focus on determining the inertial effects solely on
receding
destabilized contact lines. The mechanism of the instability is in itself quite complex, and is beyond the scope of this work. Thus, for our analysis, we shall presuppose that the contact line is destabilized, and forms a corner on the solid surface, as observed in experiments. Our objective then is to determine the shape of the interface and flow-field within. To this end, we closely follow the analyses of
Limat and Stone [1]
, and
Snoeijer et al. [17]
and improve on their solution by introducing weak-inertial corrections in §
II
. In §
III
we solve the boundary value problem for the interface profile, which gives us the leading-order effect of inertia near the contact line. The self-similar form of the inertial correction is obtained here. Taking water and mercury as example liquids, the inertial corrections to their interface shape and the flow field are discussed in §
IV
. Finally, a summary of our key findings and relevant discussions are offered in §
V
.

II
Problem formulation

Consider the steady motion of the contact line of a partially-wetting liquid over a solid surface (at
z=0
plane) with a velocity
V
. As an illustrative example, we take the case of a drop sliding along the

-axis as shown in Fig.

.

Let the liquid have a density
\rho
and viscosity
\eta
. The shape of the liquid interface is described by its height from the solid surface,
h(x,y)
. Close to the receding contact line, we assume a gradual variation of the interface height. We also assume the characteristic height of the interface,
a
, to be much smaller than the other dimensions which have a characteristic length
l
. This allows for a separation of scales between the planar (
x,y
) and the normal (
z
) directions, which is done by introducing the small parameter,
a/l=\delta\ll 1
.
We first rescale all the physical quantities with their respective characteristic values to obtain the corresponding dimensionless quantities (denoted by
\bar{\bullet}
). For example, the dimensionless coordinates
(\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z})=(x/l,y/l,z/a)
, velocity field
(\bar{u}_{x},\bar{u}_{y},\bar{u}_{z})=(u_{x}/V,u_{y}/V,u_{z}/(\delta V))
and pressure field
\bar{p}=p\delta^{2}l/(\eta V)
. Note that we have rescaled pressure using the lubrication pressure, rather than inertial, as we are interested in predominantly viscous flows.

II.1
Momentum conservation

The steady-state momentum conservation within the liquid is given by the Navier-Stokes equation, written in planar
(x,y)
and normal (
z
) directions respectively as,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\bar{\Delta}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}+\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\mathrm{Re}_{l}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}+\bar{u}_{z}\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\right),

(1)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{u}_{z}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}-\frac{\partial{\bar{p}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}

\displaystyle=\delta^{4}\mathrm{Re}_{l}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{u}_{z}+\bar{u}_{z}\frac{\partial{\bar{u}_{z}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\right).

(2)

Here,
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}=(\bar{u}_{x},\bar{u}_{y})
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}=(\partial/\partial\bar{x},\partial/\partial\bar{y})
is the planar gradient, and
\bar{\Delta}=\partial^{2}/\partial\bar{x}^{2}+\partial^{2}/\partial\bar{y}^{2}
is the planar Laplacian.
\mathrm{Re}_{l}=lV\rho/\eta
is the Reynolds number based on the larger length scale,
l
. Classical lubrication theory ignores the inertial term by assuming small values of
\mathrm{Re}_{l}\lesssim 1
. In contrast, here we consider the regime where
\mathrm{Re}_{l}\gg 1
, so as to retain the contribution of inertia. However, when the Reynolds number is sufficiently large, typically
\mathrm{Re}_{l}\gtrsim\delta^{-2}
, boundary layer separation starts coming into the picture
[
25
]
. To avoid this complication, we limit our analysis to the moderate inertial regime defined by
1\ll\mathrm{Re}_{l}\ll\delta^{-2}
, i.e.
\mathrm{Re}_{l}\sim\mathcal{O}(\delta^{-1})
. So now, by neglecting terms of
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
and smaller, (
1
) and (
2
) reduce to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\;(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}})\bar{\boldsymbol{u}},\qquad\mathrm{and}

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bar{p}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}

\displaystyle=0.

(4)

where
\epsilon=\delta^{2}\;\mathrm{Re}_{l}\sim\mathcal{O}(\delta)
is a small parameter. Comparison with full numerical simulations of channel flow have shown the “inertial thin-film equations” in (
3
) and (
4
) to be accurate even for much higher values of
\epsilon(\sim 1-10)\;

[
26
]
. To obtain the velocity field
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}
, one has to integrate the expression (
3
) between
\bar{z}=0
and
\bar{z}=\bar{h}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, with the following boundary conditions in the laboratory frame-of-reference: (i) no-slip at the solid surface,
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}|_{\bar{z}=0}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and (ii) free-shear at the liquid interface,
\partial\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}/\partial z|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Furthermore, it follows from these boundary conditions that
\bar{u}_{z}=0
.

To obtain an analytical solution in the limit of of small, but non-negligible values of
\epsilon
, we resort to a regular perturbation expansion of the form,

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}

\displaystyle=\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),\quad\bar{h}=\bar{h}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{h}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})\quad\mathrm{and},

\displaystyle\bar{p}

\displaystyle=\bar{p}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{p}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(5)

We truncate the expansion at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, the leading-order inertial term; The subscript
0
denotes
\mathcal{O}(1)
terms (Stokes flow) and
1
denotes
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
terms (inertial correction). The expansion in (
5
) is the weak-inertial correction that is used in a variety of problems to determine the influence of inertia in moderate
\mathrm{Re}_{l}
flows.
Substituting (
5
) in the Navier-Stokes equation (
3
) gives the governing equation for the zeroth and first order problems,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1):

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0},

(6a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\epsilon):

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{1}+(\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}})\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}.

(6b)

Next, we use the expansion (
5
) in the boundary conditions. It is straightforward to obtain the no-slip conditions at the respective order,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1):

\displaystyle\qquad\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}|_{\bar{z}=0}=\mathbf{0},

(7a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\epsilon):

\displaystyle\qquad\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}|_{\bar{z}=0}=\mathbf{0}.

(7b)

To write the free-shear boundary condition at the liquid interface
\bar{z}=\bar{h}=\bar{h}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{h}_{1}
, we use the
method of domain perturbations

[
27
]
. This method involves expanding a given function in Taylor series near the perturbed boundary. Thus, at
z=\bar{h}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{h}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}+\epsilon\bar{h}_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}+\epsilon\biggr(\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}+\bar{h}_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}\biggr)\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(8)

The final expression was obtained using the expansion (
5
). The free-shear condition at the respective order is then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1):

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}=\mathbf{0},

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\epsilon):

\displaystyle\qquad\frac{\partial{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}=-\bar{h}_{1}\;\frac{\partial^{2}{\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{z}}^{2}}\biggr|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}=-\bar{h}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}|_{\bar{z}=\bar{h}_{0}}.

(9b)

We are finally at a stage where we can solve the governing equations and boundary conditions at their respective orders. At the zeroth order, we have the classical lubrication theory, which includes the Stokes equation (
6a
) and the boundary conditions (
7a
) and (
9a
). Solving for
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}
, we get

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}=\frac{\bar{z}}{2}(\bar{z}-2\bar{h}_{0})\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}.

(10)

Next, we substitute (
10
) in the first-order governing equation (
6b
) and the boundary conditions (
7b
) and (
9b
), and subsequently solve for
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}
:

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}=

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{z}}{2}(\bar{z}-2\bar{h}_{0})\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{1}-\bar{z}\bar{h}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}+\frac{\bar{z}}{120}(\bar{z}^{5}-6\bar{h}_{0}\bar{z}^{4}+10\bar{h}_{0}^{2}\bar{z}^{3}-16\bar{h}_{0}^{5})\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}.

(11)

Equation (
11
) introduces the inertial correction to the well-known Poiseuille flow profile of the Stokes solution (
10
). One can factor out the
z
-dependence in these equations by computing the depth-averaged flow field,
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}(x,y)=\bar{h}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\bar{h}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}\;\mathrm{d}\bar{z}
. Substituting (
5
) gives the depth-averaged flow-field of the form
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, where

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{h}_{0}}\int_{0}^{\bar{h}_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\bar{z},

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{h}_{0}}\biggl(\int_{0}^{\bar{h}_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}\;\mathrm{d}\bar{z}+\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\bar{h}_{0}}^{\bar{h}_{0}+\epsilon\bar{h}_{1}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\bar{z}-\frac{\bar{h}_{1}}{\bar{h}_{0}}\int_{0}^{\bar{h}_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{0}\;\mathrm{d}\bar{z}\biggr).

(13)

Applying (
10
) and (
11
) in the above integrals and evaluating, we obtain,

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}=\frac{{-\bar{h}_{0}}^{2}}{3}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0},\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}=\frac{-\bar{h}_{0}^{2}}{3}\biggl(\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{1}+\frac{2\bar{h}_{1}}{\bar{h}_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\bar{p}_{0}+\frac{54}{35}(\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}})\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\biggr).

(14)

We can simplify the above expressions by determining the pressure field. Noting that the pressure is independent of
\bar{z}
(from (
4
)), and is simply the dimensionless Laplace pressure,

\bar{p}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=-\mathrm{Ca}^{-1}\delta^{3}\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}(\bar{x},\bar{y}),

(15)

where
\mathrm{Ca}=\eta V/\gamma
is the Capillary number. Since the pressure gradient has to balance the viscous stress at the leading order (see (
6a
)), we have
\delta\sim\mathrm{Ca}^{1/3}
. Disregarding the proportionality constant as done in the classical Stokes flow problem
[
1
,
17
]
, we get

\delta=\mathrm{Ca}^{1/3}\implies\epsilon=\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3}\;\mathrm{Re}_{l}.

(16)

Thus, by substituting (
5
) in (
15
) and collecting terms of the same asymptotic order, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1):

\displaystyle\quad\bar{p}_{0}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=-\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{0},

(17a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\epsilon):

\displaystyle\quad\bar{p}_{1}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=-\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{1}.

(17b)

The above expressions can be used in (
14
) to write
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}
explicitly in terms of
\bar{h}_{0}
and
\bar{h}_{1}
alone,

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bar{h}_{0}}^{2}}{3}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{0}),

(18a)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{h}_{0}^{2}}{3}\biggl(\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{1})+\frac{2\bar{h}_{1}}{\bar{h}_{0}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{0})-\frac{54}{35}(\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}})\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\biggr).

(18b)

II.2
Mass conservation

In addition to the momentum conservation described in §
II.1
, it is necessary for the flow-field to satisfy mass conservation given by,

\frac{\partial{\bar{h}}}{\partial{\bar{t}}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot(\bar{h}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}})=0.

(19)

In the laboratory frame-of-reference, a drop moving in the positive
x
-axis with a (dimensionless) unit velocity has
\partial\bar{h}/\partial\bar{t}=-\partial\bar{h}/\partial\bar{x}
. Using this relation and the expansion (
5
), we obtain the kinematic equation of the moving interface at each order,

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1):

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\partial{\bar{h}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{x}}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot(\bar{h}_{0}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0})=0

(20a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\epsilon):

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\partial{\bar{h}_{1}}}{\partial{\bar{x}}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot(\bar{h}_{0}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}+\bar{h}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0})=0

(20b)

Note that
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}
,
\bar{h}_{0}
and
\bar{h}_{1}
are yet unknown. However, since these fields are related through (
18a
) and (
18b
), we need only solve for the latter two.

III
Determining the interface profile

III.1
Zeroth order solution: Stokes flow

The shape of the interface in the Stokes regime was determined in
Limat and Stone [1]
. We briefly re-derive their results here for completeness. The kinematic equation for interface at
\mathcal{O}(1)
is given by (
20a
). Using (
18a
) in (
20a
),

\displaystyle-3\;\frac{\partial{\bar{h}_{0}}}{\partial{\bar{x}}}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\bar{h}_{0}^{3}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{0}))=0.

(21)

Using a self-similarity ansatz, the solution is of the form

\bar{h}_{0}(\bar{x},\zeta)=\bar{x}\;H(\zeta),

(22)

where
\zeta=y/x\;

[
1
]
. Substituting (
22
) in (
21
), one arrives at the ordinary differential equation,

\displaystyle\zeta H^{\prime}-H+H^{3}\;(f_{x}-\zeta{f_{x}}^{\prime}+{f_{y}}^{\prime})+3H^{2}H^{\prime}(-\zeta f_{x}+f_{y})=0

(23)

where the prime (
\bullet^{\prime}
) denotes derivative with respect to
\zeta
. The components of the vector
\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)=(f_{x}(\zeta),f_{y}(\zeta))
are given by

\displaystyle f_{x}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{3}\biggl((1+3\zeta^{2})H^{\prime\prime}+\zeta(1+\zeta^{2})H^{\prime\prime\prime}\biggr),

(24a)

\displaystyle f_{y}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\biggl(2\zeta H^{\prime\prime}+(1+\zeta^{2})H^{\prime\prime\prime}\biggr).

(24b)

Eq.(
23
) is a boundary value problem (BVP) that requires four boundary conditions to obtain a unique solution for
H(\zeta)
. By recognizing that the drop is symmetrical about its center line (
\zeta=0
), we get the first two conditions viz.
H^{\prime}(0)=H^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0
. Next, we have at the contact lines
\zeta=\zeta_{c}
(given), the height of the drop is zero i.e.
H(\zeta_{c})=0
. The final condition requires no flux penetrating the contact line i.e., the net flux through any given cross section of the drop should be zero.
H(0)
can be determined iteratively such that the no-flux condition is satisfied. However, the solution is extremely stiff near
\zeta=\zeta_{c}
and convergence of the BVP would entail using special routines.

So instead, we use an iterative procedure, following
Limat and Stone [1]
and solve for an initial value problem (IVP). The value of
H(0)
is first arbitrarily assigned. Note however that since
H(\zeta)
relates directly to the height of the liquid interface, negative values are physically irrelevant and hence, excluded from the choice of
H(0)
. Next, we have
H^{\prime}(0)=H^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)=0
as before due to symmetry. Finally, based on a particular choice of
H^{\prime\prime}(0)
, one can solve the IVP to obtain multiple solutions for
H(\zeta)
, out of which some do not have a root (see Fig
8
a); In other words, the drop interface does not meet the solid and form a contact line. These are not physically relevant solutions and may be discarded. However, there also exist multiple solutions of
H(\zeta)
which have roots. The correct choice of
H^{\prime\prime}(0)
is then the one that also obeys the no-flux condition at the contact line. Some of these valid solutions of
H(\zeta)
for the initial conditions
H(0)
and
H^{\prime\prime}(0)
tabulated in Table
1
are shown in Fig
2
(a); they yield unique values of
\zeta_{c}
. For example, when
H(0)=1.215
, the choice
H^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2.623
satisfies the zero net flux condition as shown in Fig
8
(b,c); This solution places the contact line along
\zeta=\zeta_{c}=1
(up to 4 digits of precision) which corresponds to an opening angle
\phi=\tan^{-1}(\zeta_{c})=45^{\circ}
.

III.2
First order solution: Inertial correction

To determine the leading-order inertial correction, we use the mass conservation equation at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. Substituting (
18a
) and (
18b
) in (
20b
), we obtain the differential equation for the correction to the interface profile,
\bar{h}_{1}
,

\displaystyle-3\;\frac{\partial{\bar{h}_{1}}}{\partial{\bar{x}}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\cdot\left(\bar{h}_{0}^{3}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{1})+3\bar{h}_{0}^{2}\bar{h}_{1}\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}(\bar{\Delta}\bar{h}_{0})-\frac{54}{35}\;\bar{h}_{0}^{3}(\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{\nabla}})\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}\right)=0.

(25)

with
\boldsymbol{U}_{0}
given in (
18a
). Keeping in mind (
22
), a quick examination of this equation reveals that it admits a self-similar solution of the form

\bar{h}_{1}(\bar{x},\zeta)=\bar{x}^{2}\;G(\zeta)

(26)

with
\zeta=y/x

Results

We can now use the solutions for
H(\zeta)
and
G(\zeta)
obtained in §
III
for various corner opening angles to determine the weakly-inertial interface profile and flow-fields. Inspired by the experiments of
Puthenveettil et al. [8]
, we shall use the properties of water and mercury as examples to quantify our findings because the drops of these liquids move in the inertial regime when their receding contact line destabilizes. At room temperature, the (density, surface tension, dynamic viscosity) of water and mercury are (
\rho_{w}=10^{3}\;\mathrm{kg\;m^{-3}}
;
\gamma_{w}=7\times 10^{-2}\;\mathrm{N\;m^{-1}}
;
\eta_{w}=10^{-3}\;\rm{Pa\;s}
) and (
\rho_{m}=10^{4}\;\mathrm{kg\;m^{-3}}
;
\gamma_{m}=5\times 10^{-1}\;\mathrm{N\;m^{-1}}
;
\eta_{m}=1.5\times 10^{-3}\;\rm{Pa\;s}
) respectively. Water and mercury have critical Capillary numbers of
\mathrm{Ca}_{cr}\approx 4\times 10^{-3}
and
\mathrm{Ca}_{cr}\approx 1.5\times 10^{-3}

[
8
]
.

Before we proceed, it is useful to define a local Reynolds number based on the dimensional distance
x
from the corner, given by
\mathrm{Re}_{x}=xV\rho/\eta=\bar{x}\;\mathrm{Re}_{l}
. Also note that one can relate
\mathrm{Re}_{x}
to the capillary number via
\mathrm{Re}_{x}=\mathrm{Ca}\;x/l_{vc}
, where
l_{vc}=\eta^{2}/(\gamma\rho)
is the visco-capillary length of the liquid; For mercury and water,
l_{vc}\sim 10^{-4}\mu\mathrm{m}
and
l_{vc}\sim 10^{-2}\mu\mathrm{m}
respectively.

IV.1
Interface profile

The liquid interface is obtained by substituting the expressions for

and
h_{1}
from (

) and (
26
),

\displaystyle\bar{h}(\bar{x},\zeta)

\displaystyle=\bar{x}\;H(\zeta)+\epsilon\bar{x}^{2}\;G(\zeta)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}).

(29)

Truncating at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and writing in dimensional form by using (
16

\displaystyle h(x,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ca}^{1/3}\;x\;H(\zeta)+\mathrm{Ca}\;\mathrm{Re}_{x}\;x\;G(\zeta).

(30)

Note that
\mathrm{Ca}\;\mathrm{Re}_{x}
together is the local Weber number. The first term in (
30

\mathrm{Ca}^{1/3}
scaling for partially-wetting liquids
[
28
,
1
]

\mathrm{Re}_{x}=\mathrm{Ca}\;x/l_{vc}
, we see that the leading-order inertial term obeys a
\mathrm{Ca}^{2}
scaling.

We have seen in §
III.2
that the solutions which satisfy the no-flux condition have

for all opening angles. Thus, the inertial correction in (

[

. In the Stokes limit, the interface height scales linearly with

, giving it a conical structure, as shown by the dashed lines in Figure

varies linearly with

IV.2
Depth-averaged flow field

Using the interface profile determined in the previous section, one can compute the pressure fields using (
17a
). At the zeroth and first orders, these are respectively (in dimensionless form),

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(1+\zeta^{2})}{\bar{x}}H^{\prime\prime}(\zeta),\qquad\mathrm{and,}

(34)

\displaystyle\bar{p}_{1}

\displaystyle=-(1+\zeta^{2})G^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)+2\zeta G^{\prime}(\zeta)-2G(\zeta).

(35)

Note that when approaching the corner,
\bar{x}\to 0
, the Stokes flow pressure
\bar{p}_{0}
becomes singular, whereas the inertial correction
\bar{p}_{1}
does not. This is unlike the case of a straight contact line, where the leading-order stress from inertia exhibits a logarithmic singularity
[
21
]
.

The depth-averaged velocity field at the zeroth order (Stokes flow) is obtained by substituting (
22
) in (
18a
),

\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}(\zeta)=H(\zeta)^{2}\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta),

(36)

with
\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)
given in (
24a
)-(
24b
). Likewise, at the first order, we get by substituting (
22
), (
26
) and (
36
) in (

),

\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}(\bar{x},\zeta)=\bar{x}\;H(\zeta)\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta),

(37)

with
\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta)
given in (
28a
)-(
28b
). Thus, the total flow velocity is then
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}(\bar{x},\zeta)=\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{0}(\zeta)+\epsilon\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{1}(\bar{x},\zeta)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. In dimensional form, this is

\displaystyle\frac{\boldsymbol{U}(x,\zeta)}{V}

\displaystyle={H(\zeta)}^{2}\boldsymbol{f}(\zeta)+\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3}\mathrm{Re}_{x}\;H(\zeta)\boldsymbol{g}(\zeta).

(38)

We remind that
\boldsymbol{U}=(U_{x},U_{y})
is the local depth-averaged velocity field within the liquid, and is different from the global velocity of the drop/contact line,
\boldsymbol{V}=(V,0)
. The Stokes velocity field depends only on the semi-opening angle,
\phi
, while the inertial correction depends on
\phi
,
\mathrm{Ca}
and distance from the corner,

\mathrm{Re}_{x}=\mathrm{Ca}\;x/l_{vc}

\mathrm{Ca}^{5/3}

In Figure

One can make the following qualitative observation, true for all opening angles: The fluid approaching the corner is forced further towards it due to the inertia. After turning the corner, the fluid accelerates away from it, and at the same time, is drawn further towards the centerline of the drop (
\zeta=0
). The flow velocity is maximum along this centerline.

V
Summary and discussions

We studied the effect of inertia in receding contact lines of partially-wetting liquids beyond their critical capillary number when they destabilize and form a corner. To this end, we modeled the flow-field near the contact line using thin-film equations, as is classically done, but retained the convective (inertial) term. The leading-order inertial contribution was then obtained by linearizing the equations using a regular perturbation expansion. The inertial effects were found to be significant when
\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3}\mathrm{Re}_{x}\gtrsim 1
, where
\mathrm{Re}_{x}
is the local Reynolds number based on the distance from the corner,
x
. For destabilized contact lines of many common liquids, including mercury and water considered here,
\mathrm{Ca}\sim 10^{-3}-10^{-2}
. This implies that inertia becomes significant when
\mathrm{Re}_{x}\gtrsim 10-10^{2}
, which corresponds to experimentally relevant length scales of
x\sim 10-100\;\mu\mathrm{m}
for mercury and
x\sim 100-1000\;\mu\mathrm{m}
for water. Gravitational effects can be ignored as these distances fall well within their respective capillary lengths.

In the Stokes regime, the thin-film equations predict the receding contact line to be of conical structure with a self-similar flow within
[
1
,
17
]
. Here, we have provided a self-similar leading-order inertial correction to this interface profile and the flow-field. We have also shown that for all corner opening angles, inertia enhances the interface height. A similar enhancement in the liquid film thickness by inertia was reported in the fully-wetting case (Landau-Levich problem)
[
23
,
22
]
. Furthermore, the leading-order inertial correction was found to scale linearly with the local Weber number (
\mathrm{We}_{x}=\mathrm{Ca}\;\mathrm{Re}_{x}
), giving rise to a cusp-like shape of the interface for all corner opening angles. However, note that in computing the correction, we assumed the contact line to remain a straight corner (
\phi
independent of
x
), which is not true in general, as experiments report a corner-to-cusp transition of contact lines at high speeds
[
7
,
8
]
.

To understand the inertial effects on the flow-field, we computed the depth-averaged velocity near the contact line. The inertial correction was found to scale as
\mathrm{Ca}^{5/3}
at given distance from the corner. We also found that inertia increases the fluid influx from the bulk and forces the flow further towards the corner. Consequently, this also enhances the outflux through the centerline of the drop for all corner opening angles.

We then introduced contact line dynamics in our model using a simple phenomenological relation for the corner opening angle, viz.
\sin\phi=\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}/\mathrm{Ca}\;

[
2
]
, which is to say that increasing
\mathrm{Ca}
reduces the opening angle. In doing so, we introduced a competition between the increased drop speed and the corner confinement on the flow; the former enhances the fluid inertia while the latter suppresses it. Interestingly, we found that despite this competition, there is an overall increase in the inertial contribution with increase in
\mathrm{Ca}
. We have shown this numerically by taking contact lines of water and mercury as examples. While these are theoretical estimates, note that in practice, the cornered end undergoes either a pearling instability or forms a rivulet when
\phi\ll 1
; In experiments, it happens around
\phi=\pi/6
or
30^{\circ}\;

[
4
,
8
]
. In future works, it would be interesting to see how inertia itself affects this instability
[
30
]
.

While the assumption of
\epsilon\;(=\mathrm{Ca}^{2/3}\mathrm{Re}_{l})\ll 1
was used to obtain a perturbative correction to the Stokes solution, the asymptotic expansion is valid numerically even for
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
. For fast motion of contact lines where
\epsilon\gg 1
, the flow develops a viscous boundary layer close to the surface. In such cases, the use of boundary layer theory instead has been shown to agree well with experiments
[
8
]
.

The Stokes flow stresses diverge as
1/x
when approaching the corner,
x\to 0
(see (
34
)). It has been observed in experiments that the corner rounds off around the visco-capillary length,
l_{vc}
, to regularize this corner singularity
[
31
]
. Additional contact line physics can be implemented in the model to capture this fine tip structure
[
31
]
, although no such regularization was used in the present work. This makes our analysis less suitable for comparison with experiments when
x\lesssim l_{vc}
. Furthermore, far away from the corner, our analysis is not valid close to and beyond the capillary length,
l_{c}
, where gravitational effects become important. Thus, it is best applicable in
l_{vc}\ll x\ll l_{c}
.

The inertial correction provided in the present work cannot be applied to advancing contact lines because of the large capillary numbers at which destabilization occurs (
\mathrm{Ca}_{\mathrm{cr}}\sim\mathcal{O}(10)
), accompanied by a large interface deformation
[
6
]
. However, it remains relevant for addressing problems concerning destabilized receding contact lines, both from a fundamental standpoint as well as in many industrial processes operating at moderately large Reynolds numbers
[
12
,
13
,
6
]
.
