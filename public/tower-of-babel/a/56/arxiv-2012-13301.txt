Abstract
Using graphs to model irregular information domains is an effective approach to deal with some of the intricacies of contemporary (network) data. A key aspect is how the data, represented as graph signals, depend on the topology of the graph. Widely-used approaches assume that the observed signals can be viewed as outputs of graph filters (i.e., polynomials of a matrix representation of the graph) whose inputs have a particular structure. Diffused graph signals, which correspond to an originally sparse (node-localized) signal percolated through the graph via filtering, fall into this class. In that context, this paper deals with the problem of jointly identifying graph filters and separating their (sparse) input signals from a mixture of diffused graph signals, thus generalizing to the graph signal processing framework the classical blind demixing (blind source separation) of temporal and spatial signals. We first consider the scenario where the supporting graphs are different across the signals, providing a theorem for demixing feasibility along with probabilistic bounds on successful recovery. Additionally, an analysis of the degenerate problem of demixing with a single graph is also presented. Numerical experiments with synthetic and real-world graphs empirically illustrating the main theoretical findings close the paper.

Introduction

Modern interconnected systems and networks are growing more pervasive and complex by the day. While this is rendering data more abundant, its structure is more intricate than the one exhibited by classical datasets. Relying on graphs to model irregular information domains has emerged as a successful and tractable approach to deal with some of the challenges present in contemporary datasets. This approach, which has a long history in statistics
[
2

In the latter case, graph signal processing (GSP) puts forth a systematic approach where variables are modeled as nodes, the information domain is modeled as a graph (capturing heterogeneous relations from explicit pairwise relations), and the data itself is modeled as graph signals.
Graph signals are abundant in engineering and nature, including, for example, the congestion in traffic networks, the electrical neurological activity in a brain network, and the folding rate on protein residue networks.

The key in GSP is to postulate meaningful and tractable: i) models relating the properties of the data with the properties of the graph and ii) graph-signal operators that depend on the topology of the graph

Mathematically, graph filters are graph-signal operators that can be represented by a matrix that is a polynomial of either the adjacency or Laplacian matrix of the graph
[

,

,

When the input graph signal is sparse (i.e., there are only a few nodes -sources- with a non-zero value), the associated output is referred to as a diffused graph signal, with sampling, reconstruction, blind deconvolution and filter identification of diffused graph signals having been investigated in detail

In this paper, we focus on blind demixing of graph signals.
In particular, the observed graph signal

This extends classical blind source separation (BSS) or demixing of signals in the time and spatial domains. II
Graph Filtering Fundamentals

Let
\mathcal{G}
denote a directed graph with a set of nodes
\mathcal{N}
(with cardinality
\mathrm{N}
) and a set of links
\mathcal{E}
such that if
i
is connected to
j
then
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. Local connectivity is captured by the set
\mathcal{N}_{i}:=\{j\;|(j,i)\in\mathcal{E}\}
, which stands for the (incoming) neighborhood of
i
. For any given
\mathcal{G}
, we define the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}}
as a sparse matrix with non-zero elements
A_{ji}
if and only if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. The value of
A_{ji}
captures the strength of the connection from
i
to
j
.
The focus of the paper is on analyzing and modeling (graph) signals defined on
\mathcal{N}
. These signals can be represented as vectors
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},...,x_{\mathrm{N}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{N}}
, where
x_{i}
represents the value of the signal at node
i
.

II-A

Graph-shift operator

Since the vector representation
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},...,x_{\mathrm{N}}]^{\top}
does not account explicitly for the network structure, GSP endows
\mathcal{G}
with the
graph-shift operator
(GSO)
\mathbf{S}

[
18
,
5
]
. The shift
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}}
is a matrix whose entry
S_{ji}
can be non-zero only if
i=j
or if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. The sparsity pattern of the matrix
\mathbf{S}
captures the local structure of
\mathcal{G}
, with no specific assumptions on the values of its non-zero entries.
The intuition behind
\mathbf{S}
is to represent a linear transformation that can be computed locally at the nodes of the graph. More rigorously, if
\mathbf{y}
is defined as
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}
, then node
i
can compute
y_{i}
as linear combination of the signal values
x_{j}
where
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
.
Typical choices for
\mathbf{S}
are the adjacency matrix
\mathbf{A}

[
18
]
and the graph Laplacian
[
6
]
. We assume henceforth that
\mathbf{S}
is diagonalizable, so that
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{-1}
with
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}}
being diagonal.
In particular,
\mathbf{S}
is guaranteed to be diagonalizable when it is normal, i.e., when it satisfies
\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\text{H}}=\mathbf{S}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}
(this is one of the core assumptions in
Section

IV
).
In that case, we have that
\mathbf{V}
is unitary, which implies
\mathbf{V}^{-1}=\mathbf{V}^{\text{H}}
, and leads to the decomposition
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{V}^{\text{H}}
.

II-B

Graph filters and diffusion processes

The shift
\mathbf{S}
can be used to define
linear graph-signal operators
of the form

\mathbf{H}:=\sum_{\mathrm{l}=0}^{\mathrm{L}-1}h_{\mathrm{l}}\mathbf{S}^{\mathrm{l}},

(1)

which are called
graph filters

[
18
,
20
]
. For a given input
\mathbf{x}
, the output of the filter is simply
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}
. The coefficients of the filter are collected into
\mathbf{h}:=[h_{0},\ldots,h_{\mathrm{L}-1}]^{\top}
, with
\mathrm{L}-1
denoting the filter degree. Graph filters are of particular interest because they model network diffusion processes and can be implemented in a distributed fashion
[
20
,
21
]
.
Note also that the Cayley-Hamilton theorem guarantees that even infinite-horizon processes (i.e., those depending on arbitrarily large powers of
\mathbf{S}
) can be rewritten as a filter of at most degree
\mathrm{N}-1
.

II-C

Spectral representation

Leveraging the spectral decomposition of
\mathbf{S}
, graph filters and signals can be represented in the frequency domain.
To be precise, let us use the eigenvectors of
\mathbf{S}
to define the
\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}
matrix
\mathbf{U}:=\mathbf{V}^{-1}
, and the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}
to define the
\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{L}
Vandermonde matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}
, where
\Psi_{ij}:=(\Lambda_{ii})^{j-1}
.
Using these conventions, the frequency representations of a
signal

\mathbf{x}
and of a
filter

\mathbf{h}
are defined as
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}:=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{h}}:=\mathbf{\Psi}\mathbf{h}
, respectively
[
5
,
20
]
.
Exploiting such representations, the output
\mathbf{y}\!=\!\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}
of a graph filter in the frequency domain is given by

\widehat{\mathbf{y}}=\diag\big(\mathbf{\Psi}\mathbf{h}\big)\mathbf{U}\mathbf{x}=\diag\big(\widehat{\mathbf{h}}\big)\widehat{\mathbf{x}}=\widehat{\mathbf{h}}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{x}},

(2)

with
\circ
being the Hadamart product. Identity (
2
) is the counterpart of the celebrated convolution theorem for temporal signals, and follows from
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{V}\big(\sum_{\mathrm{l}=0}^{\mathrm{L}-1}h_{\mathrm{l}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{l}}\big)\mathbf{U}
[cf. (
1
)] and
\sum_{\mathrm{l}=0}^{\mathrm{L}-1}h_{\mathrm{l}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{l}=\diag{(\mathbf{\Psi}\mathbf{h})}
. Having
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}=\widehat{\mathbf{h}}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{x}}
implies that the dependence of
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}
on the frequency representations of
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{x}
is bilinear, with no mixing among frequencies (i.e,
[\widehat{\mathbf{y}}]_{k}
depends only on
[\widehat{\mathbf{h}}]_{k}[\widehat{\mathbf{x}}]_{k}
). In contrast, if one wants to write
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}
as a function of the entries of
\mathbf{h}
and
\mathbf{x}
, it follows from (
2
) that the expression is

\widehat{\mathbf{y}}=(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\odot\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top})^{\top}\vect(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{h}^{\top}),

(3)

with
\odot
being the Khatri-Rao product; see, e.g,
[
24
]
. That is, the dependency is still bilinear, but any entry of
\widehat{\mathbf{y}}
depends on all the bilinear terms
[\mathbf{h}]_{\mathrm{l}}[\mathbf{x}]_{n}
. Interestingly, while in the time domain
\boldsymbol{\Psi}=\mathbf{U}
, in GSP the matrices representing the graph Fourier transform (GFT) for filters and signals are in general not the same.

III
Blind Demixing with Multiple Diffusing Graphs

Given an observation
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
, we aim to identify the
\mathrm{R}
individual signals under the assumption that each
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
can be modeled as a diffused graph signal. That is,
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
is the output obtained by processing a
sparse
input signal
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
of
unknown
support
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}
(of cardinality
|\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}|=\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
) with a graph filter of unknown coefficients
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}
and known GSO
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
. Formally, by modeling each
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
as

\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}=\sum_{\mathrm{l}=0}^{\mathrm{L}-1}[\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}]_{\mathrm{l}}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\mathrm{l}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}},\;\;\text{where}\;\;[\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}]_{n}=0\;\;\mathrm{n}\notin\Omega_{\mathrm{i}},

(4)

we look at the problem of recovering
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
from the observed
\mathbf{y}
.

With
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}
,
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}
and
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}
denoting the (inverse) GFT matrices associated with the shift
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
(cf.
Section

II-A
), and leveraging the spectral representation of graph signals and filters (cf.
Section

II-C
), the joint demixing and blind deconvolution problem can be written as

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{find}}

\displaystyle\quad\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}

(5a)

s. to

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}\left(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\odot\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}\vect(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top})\;\;\;\forall\mathrm{i}

(5b)

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{y}={\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}},\quad\quad\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{0}\leq\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\;\;\;\forall\mathrm{i},

(5c)

where s. to stands for subject to.
When solving the above optimization, we assume that we know: i) the GSOs
\{\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
, so that
\{\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}},\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
are available; and ii) the full vector of observations
\mathbf{y}
(see
Remark

1
for partially observable setups).

To handle the demixing problem in (
5
), note first that the constraint (
5b
) implies that signals
\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}
are fully determined by
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}
. Hence, (
5b
) can be combined with the pertinent constraint in (
5c
), so that
\{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
are no longer explicit optimization variables, but obtained using (
4
) after the optimization over
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}
has been solved. Although this reduces considerably the dimension of the feasible set, the optimization is still challenging for a number of reasons. Firstly, even if the support of the inputs were known, for the problem to be well-posed, the number of non-trivial unknowns
\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}+\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}})
needs to be less than the number of observations
\mathrm{N}
. Secondly, each of the terms
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}
is bilinear, which introduces a source of non-convexity and gives rise to an inherent scaling ambiguity. Thirdly, the presence of the
\ell_{0}
norm renders the optimization NP-hard.

The first step to design a tractable relaxation of (
5
), is to
lift
the problem by defining the
\mathrm{N}\!\times\!\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}}
rank-one matrices
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\!=\!\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}
, together with the
\mathrm{N}\!\times\!\mathrm{N}\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}}
transfer matrices
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}\left(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\odot\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}
. With these notational conventions, and combining the
linear
constraints in (
5b
)-(
5c
) into a single one, the demixing problem can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=\text{find}\quad\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s. to}\,\,\,\,\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\!=\!{\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}}\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}\text{vec}\!\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\right),\;\,\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}})\!=\!1,\;\,\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{2,0}\leq\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}.

Note that
\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{2,0}
, defined as the number of non-zero rows of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
, mimics the role of
\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{0}
in (
5
). The solution vectors
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
and
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
are given by the scaled versions of the right and left principal
singular vectors
of the rank-one matrix
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
. Although the equivalent formulation (
6
) is still difficult to solve, it leads to a convex relaxation which is described in the ensuing section, after the following remark.

Remark 1

(Robust demixing)

The demixing problem – in particular constraint (
5b
) – can be rewritten to account for noisy and incomplete observations. If the values of
\mathbf{y}
are observed only at a subset of
\mathrm{M}
nodes, both sides of (
5b
) must be left multiplied by the
sampling matrix

\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\!\in\!\{0,1\}^{\mathrm{M}\times\mathrm{N}}
whose rows correspond to canonical basis vectors identifying the indexes
\mathcal{M}=\{n_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\mathrm{M}}
of the observed entries. Presence of noise in the observations would require replacing the equality in (
5b
) with a term penalizing the difference between the two sides of (
5b
), with the penalty depending on the specific type of noise.

III-A

Convex relaxation and algorithmic approach

The
feasibility
problem in (
6
) is reformulated as a rank-
minimization
problem where, first, the objective is augmented with
\ell_{2,0}
constraints and, then, tractable surrogates of the (non-convex) rank and the
\ell_{2,0}
norm are used. Specifically, consider replacing the rank with the nuclear norm, and the
\ell_{2,0}
norm with the
\ell_{2,1}
norm
[
35
,
36
]
. The resultant optimization is

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}}\quad\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{*}+\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\beta_{\mathrm{i}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{2,1}

(7)

\displaystyle\text{s. to}\quad\mathbf{y}={\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}}\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\right),

where
\{\eta_{\mathrm{i}},\beta_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
are tuning constants that, if no prior information is available, can be set to
\eta_{\mathrm{i}}=1
and
\beta_{\mathrm{i}}=\beta
for all
\mathrm{i}
. The optimization in (
7
) is convex, so that it can be handled with generic off-the-shelf solvers. When computational complexity is a concern, one can alternatively develop efficient algorithms tailored to the structure of (
7
), see, e.g.,
[
37
]
for a related problem.

Recoverability conditions can be found by ignoring the low-rank nature of all the
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
and focusing on the fact that the concatenation of the vectorized forms of all these matrices
\mathbf{z}=\mathrm{vec}([\mathbf{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{R}}])
is effectively a sparse vector. In this way, one can think of recovering a sparse vector

satisfying
\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{R}}]\,\mathbf{z}
and derive recoverability conditions in this basic setting
[
38
]
. A more sophisticated analysis accounting for the structure of the linear operators
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}
and the low-rank nature of
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
is our main theoretical contribution, which is presented in
Section

IV
.

IV
Exact Blind Demixing Via Nuclear Norm Minimization

This section presents our main theoretical result, namely, under a few technical conditions (to be outlined next) all the filters

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}
and sparse input signals
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
in (
5
) can be exactly recovered
with high probability by solving a simplified version of the convex problem (

) where we only keep the nuclear norm in the objective, i.e.,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\text{min}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{*}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\text{s. to}}\quad\mathbf{y}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\right).

(8)

The objective under minimization is simplified from (
7
) to (
8
) to enable the analysis in the Appendix, which results in Theorem
1
. Nonetheless, while not promoted explicitly in (
8
), the colum sparsity of

To prove the recovery result stated formally in Theorem

to operate on the GFT matrices for filters and signals

, given an arbitrary matrix

\rho_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k)}:=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq M}\max_{\Omega\in\Omega_{k}^{N}}\|\mathbf{a}_{\ell_{\Omega}}\|_{2}^{2},

(9)

where

\mathbf{A}

\Omega

Building on this idea, let us now consider a second matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times N}
and, with positive integers
k_{1}
and

, define the function

\kappa_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}:=\max_{1\leq\ell\leq M}\max_{(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})\in\Omega_{k_{1}}^{N}\times\Omega_{k_{2}}^{N}}\|\mathbf{a}_{{\ell}_{\Omega_{1}}}\ \mathbf{b}_{{\ell}_{\Omega_{2}}}^{\text{H}}\|.

(10)

In order to relate
\rho

\kappa

\|\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=|\mathbf{b}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{x}|\ \|\mathbf{a}\|_{2}

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{b}

\rho

\kappa_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\leq\sqrt{\rho_{\mathbf{A}}^{(k_{1})}}\sqrt{\rho_{\mathbf{B}}^{(k_{2})}}.

(11)

IV-A

Assumptions and scope of the analysis

We start by introducing two assumptions that render the analysis more tractable.

Assumption 1 (AS1)
The GSOs
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}},\ \mathrm{i}\!\in\!\{1,2,\dots,\mathrm{R}\}
, are normal, i.e., they satisfy
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\text{H}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\text{H}}\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
, and for each
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
its eigenvalues are distinct.

Assumption 2 (AS2)

The sparse signals’ supports
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}},\mathrm{i}\!\in\!\{1,2,\dots,\mathrm{R}\}
are known. In other words, with
\mathbf{C}_{\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}}^{\top}
being a tall matrix whose rows are either all zeros, or rows from the identity matrix, the mapping
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{C}_{\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}}^{\top}\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{i}}
between the sparse signals
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
and the “seeding” signals
\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{i}}
is known.

Assumption 3 (AS3)
The observation is a mix of filtered graph signals and its frequency representation adheres to the model

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\diag(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}})\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\diag(\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}})\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{s}_{\mathrm{i}},}

(12)

where
\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{i}}:=\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{C}_{\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathrm{i}}
is a random
\mathrm{N}\times\mathrm{N}
matrix obtained by concatenating
\mathrm{N}
rows sampled independently and uniformly with replacement from
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}
.

Under (AS1) the matrices
\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}
are unitary, which implies
\mathbf{U}_{\mathrm{i}}:=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}^{-1}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\text{H}}
and leads to the decomposition
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{V}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\text{H}}
. Normality is for instance satisfied when
\mathcal{G}
is undirected and the GSO is chosen to be the adjacency matrix or the graph Laplacian. Furthermore, that each
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
has distinct eigenvalues ensures that the Vandermonde matrices
\mathbf{\Psi}_{\mathrm{i}}
are full-rank independently of
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}}
, which is required for uniqueness.

Assuming the supports
\Omega_{\mathrm{i}}
are known via (AS2) enables reducing the degrees of freedom of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
. Signals
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
belonging to a vector subspace of dimension
\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
smaller than
\mathrm{N}
will allow us to obtain appropriate bounds.

Concerning the probabilistic model for the observations in (AS3), this class of models is customary when establishing recovery guarantees of sparse signal reconstruction and low-rank matrix completion in, e.g., compressive sensing
[
38
]
and demixing of classical signals
[
31
,
39
]
.
In particular, the application of Bernstein inequalities for random matrices is instrumental to the proof of Theorem
1
(see the Appendix).

V
Blind Demixing With A Single Diffusing Graph

So far, we have considered the case where each input
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
is diffused across its own graph characterized by its GSO
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
.
The setting where all the signals are diffused in the same graph and then combined leads to a more challenging blind demixing problem.
Notice that if
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{S}
for all
\mathrm{i}
, then the transfer matrix
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{M}
is equivalent for all the inputs. As a result, the observation equation
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}\text{vec}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}})
simplifies to
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{M}\text{vec}(\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\!\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}})
, which introduces a new source of ambiguity as explained in detail next.

V-A

Single graph demixing ambiguity

First, consider the minimization form of the feasibility problem in (
6
), i.e., a non-relaxed predecessor of (
7
),

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}}\quad\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\eta_{\mathrm{i}}\,\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}})+\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\beta_{\mathrm{i}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{2,0}

s. to

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{M}\,\text{vec}\!\left({\textstyle\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\!\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}}\right).

(13)

To illustrate the ambiguity emerging in the single graph demixing problem, consider the case where
\mathrm{R}\!=\!2
and, accordingly,
\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{(*)}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{(*)}
denote the true rank-one matrices.
Next, use these matrices to define
\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{(*)}+\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{(*)}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
. It readily follows that: 1) both
\{\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{(*)},\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{(*)}\}
and
\{\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\prime}\}
are in the feasible set of (
V-A
); and 2) employing the triangle inequality:
\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime})+\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\prime})=\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime})\leq\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{(*)})+\text{rank}(\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{(*)})
. In words, the true
\{\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{(*)},\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{(*)}\}
involve a (rank) cost larger than or equal to that of
\{\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\prime}\}
. Furthermore, if
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}
then a solution where either
\mathbf{Z}_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
or
\mathbf{Z}_{2}^{\prime}=\mathbf{0}
always achieves a smaller rank cost. A similar argument holds for the
\ell_{2,0}
norm that constitutes the objective in (
V-A
) together with the rank just analyzed.

This demonstrates that the demixing problem with a single diffusing graph presents additional challenges. With respect to the multiple-graph problem, the individual
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
matrices are in general non-identifiable separately, albeit the combination
\mathbf{Z}\!=\!\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\!\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
can be recovered. Thus, we propose a two-step approach in which we first efficiently obtain the true
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}\!=\!\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\!\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}
and then outline conditions for which the constituent
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}
matrices can be uniquely recovered.

V-B

Convex relaxation and algorithmic approach

Following the strategy in
Section

III-A
to design an efficient demixing algorithm, the combinatorial norms in (
V-A
) are replaced with their convex surrogates
\|\cdot\|_{*}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2,1}
. This results in an instance of (
7
) with all
\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{M}
whose solutions
\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
also suffer from the linear ambiguity present in single graph demixing. In fact, given that the nuclear as well as the
\ell_{2,1}
norms are absolutely homogeneous (whereas the rank and the
\ell_{2,0}
penalties are not), the ambiguity is more severe in the convex formulation. To see this, note that any solution of the form
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\!=\!\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}
with
\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\!\geq\!0
and
\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\theta_{\mathrm{i}}\!=\!1
is feasible and yields the same value of
\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\|\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}\|_{*}
. Hence, the postulated optimization will have multiple solutions with the same cost than that of the actual
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
we aim to identify.

Another option consists in reformulating (
7

\mathbf{Z}=\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
as follows

\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}=\argmin_{\mathbf{Z}}\,\,\textstyle\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{*}+\beta\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{2,1}\quad\,\,\text{s. to}\,\,\,\mathbf{y}=\textstyle\mathbf{M}\text{vec}\left(\mathbf{Z}\right).

(14)

In terms of computational complexity, this reformulation presents a lower cost because the number of optimization variables are effectively reduced by a factor of
\mathrm{R}
. Moreover, the formulation in (

[
24
]
(that is, a single source without demixing) with the exception that the true
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}
to be recovered, albeit being low-rank, is not necessarily rank-one. Still, the results in
[
24
, Theorem 1]
can provide probabilistic guarantees for the recovery of the true
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}
in terms of the matrices
\mathbf{\Psi}
and
\mathbf{U}
associated with the common diffusing graph.

V-C

Resolving the ambiguity

It is in general not feasible to recover
\{\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
from
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}
due to the single graph ambiguity already explained.
Consequently, we propose one way to ensure recoverability by means of imposing orthogonality conditions on the filter coefficients and the input signals.
Specifically, we consider the following two separate assumptions:

(a1)

\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{j}}=0
or
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{j}}=0
for all
\mathrm{i}\neq\mathrm{j}
.

(a2)

\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{j}}=0
or
\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{j}}=0
for all
\mathrm{i}\neq\mathrm{j}
.

These orthogonality conditions are satisfied, e.g., when the vectors are sparse and their support does not overlap.
In this way, two sparse inputs that are non-zero in non-overlapping nodes satisfy the first requirement in (a1).
Similarly, two bandpass filters defined in non-overlapping bands satisfy the second requirement in (a2).

Before formally stating recoverability in the single graph case, we define auxiliary matrices to capture the orthogonality choices in (a1) and (a2).
Consider the matrix
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{I}
if the first requirement in (a1) holds and
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{U}
if the second requirement is true.
Similarly, consider
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{I}
if the first requirement in (a2) is true and
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}=\mathbf{\Psi}
if the second one holds.
These matrices allow us to state recoverability conditions and a final algorithmic step for single graph demixing succinctly.
That is,
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}
(
\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}
) captures whether the input signals (filter taps) are orthogonal in the node or spectral domains, rendering the remaining analysis independent of the domain.

Proposition 1

If the input signals
\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
satisfy (a1), the diffusing graph filters
\{\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
satisfy (a2) and
\|\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\|\|\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\|\neq\|\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(*)}\|\|\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{j}}^{(*)}\|
for all
\mathrm{i}\neq\mathrm{j}
; then
\{\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
can be recovered from
\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}
.

Proof :
First, form the matrix
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{Z}^{(*)}\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}^{\top}
and factorize it using the singular value decomposition (SVD) as
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{L}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathbf{R}^{\top}

Then, the mixing components
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
are estimated individually by

\breve{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}=\sigma_{\mathrm{i}}(\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger})\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{i}}\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}(\mathbf{T}_{\mathbf{h}}^{\dagger})^{\top},

(15)

\sigma_{\mathrm{i}}

\mathrm{i}

\mathbf{T}

\mathbf{l}_{\mathrm{i}}

\mathbf{r}_{\mathrm{i}}

The remaining part is to show that
\breve{\mathbf{Z}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}=\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}

\mathrm{i}

To this end, notice that we can write
\mathbf{T}

(16)

for all
\mathrm{i}

Proposition
1
reveals that even for the highly challenging scenario of multiple signals being diffused and aggregated in a single graph, demixing is still possible under more stringent constraints.
The analysis in this section also hints at the fact that the similarity between graphs might be an indicator of how hard the demixing problem is, with identical graphs being an extreme setting.
We revisit this point from an empirical viewpoint in the next section.

Remark 2 (A priori information)
Blind demixing benefits when prior knowledge on
\{\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)},\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
is available. Even though this applies for both demixing cases with multiple diffusing graphs or a single diffusing graph, a priori information is particularly helpful in the single-graph scenario, where the inherent ambiguity is larger. Two types of a priori information can be employed,
probabilistic
and
deterministic
. Probabilistic information can be incorporated by augmenting the objective with tractable (log) prior distributions. Alternatively, deterministic knowledge about entries of the signals
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
or the filter taps
\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
can be incorporated in the form of linear equality constraints. In particular, if
\{\mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}\}_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}
is known then the structure of the optimization can be simplified. That is, the bilinearity disappears and the resultant problem can be handled using classical sparse signal reconstruction tools.
Another case occurs when
\text{K}_{\mathrm{i}}
values of the signal
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
are known
. Since we optimize over
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i}}
instead of directly over
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}
, exploiting this information requires considering the constraints

\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i},\ell}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i},\ell+1}^{(*)}=\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{i},\ell+1}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i},\ell}^{(*)}\quad\forall\ {\ell\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{i}}},

(18)

where
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{i}}=\{n_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{\text{K}_{\mathrm{i}}}
collects the indexes of the entries of
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}^{(*)}
that are known. Note that the introduction of this type of constraints still renders a convex program.

VI
Numerical Results

Five experiments are performed to illustrate and validate our proposed blind demixing approach. In the first two, we assess and compare the effectiveness of single-graph and multiple-graph demixing using synthetic random graphs. In the third experiment, we analyze multiple-graph demixing using real-world brain graphs (where nodes represent regions of the human brain and edges anatomical connections) associated with different individuals. In the fourth experiment, we analyze the recovery performance of single-graph demixing using a real-world social network and, in the last experiment, we rely on some of the graph-dependent parameters identified in Theorem
1
to predict demixing performance in real-world graphs.

Throughout this section, we discuss the results from a collection of blind demixing problems, where we change the type of graphs, as well as the numbers of sources
\mathrm{R}
, filter coefficients
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}}
, and seeding nodes
\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}
.

The signal and filter estimates will be denoted by
\{\breve{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{i}},\breve{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}\}
.
Unless otherwise stated, the GSO
\mathbf{S}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathbf{A}_{\mathrm{i}}
(the adjacency matrix),
\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathrm{L}
;
\mathrm{S}_{\mathrm{i}}=\mathrm{S}
for all the sources;
\mathbf{x}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}
and
\mathbf{h}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}

In the single-graph experiments,

\text{DE}:=\frac{1}{\mathrm{R}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{R}}\left\|\breve{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{i}}\breve{\mathbf{h}}_{\mathrm{i}}^{\top}-\mathbf{x}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}{\mathbf{h}^{(*)}_{\mathrm{i}}}^{\top}\right\|_{\text{F}},

VII
Conclusions

We formulated and analyzed the problem of blind demixing of filtered diffused graph signals, an extension of blind demixing of time (or spatial) domain signals to graphs. Envisioned application domains encompass a particular class of bilinear inverse problems, where the observed graph signal is modeled as a linear combination of diffusion processes driven by a number of sources.
While the graph signal observations are bilinear functions of the filter coefficients and the sparse input signal, we rely on the frequency interpretation of graph signals and filters to show that they are also linearly related to the entries of a lifted rank-one, row-sparse matrix. Accordingly, the blind demixing problem of graph signals was tackled via rank and sparsity minimization subject to linear constraints, an inverse problem amenable to convex relaxations offering provable recovery guarantees under simplifying assumptions.
The theoretical results reveal key features driving the demixing performance including the number of sources and graph nodes, the sparsity levels, the filter orders, as well as spectral properties of the underlying graphs and their pairwise coherences. These spectral properties are captured via conveniently defined concentration parameters.
Numerical tests validated the theoretical claims and demonstrated that the proposed approach offers satisfactory recovery performance, even for settings well beyond the scope of the analysis.
Building on the insights gained here, it is of interest to develop spectral graph theoretical tools to facilitate identifying the key relationships between the (e.g., topological) properties of graphs and the resulting recovery performance. Future research includes also estimating the shift operator employing network topology inference
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
.
