A new Riemannian Multiplicative Updates for Chordal Nonnegative Matrix Factorization
Flavia Esposito, Andersen Ang

Abstract
Nonnegative Matrix Factorization (NMF) is the problem of approximating a given nonnegative matrix 𝑴 \bm{M} through the product of two nonnegative low-rank matrices 𝑾 \bm{W} and 𝑯 \bm{H} . Traditionally NMF is tackled by optimizing a specific objective function evaluating the quality of the approximation. This assessment is often done based on the Frobenius norm (F-norm). In this work, we argue that the F-norm, as the “point-to-point” distance, may not always be appropriate. Viewing from the perspective of cone, NMF may not naturally align with F-norm. So, a ray-to-ray chordal distance is proposed as an alternative way of measuring the quality of the approximation. As this measure corresponds to the Euclidean distance on the sphere, it motivates the use of manifold optimization techniques. We apply Riemannian optimization technique to solve chordal-NMF by casting it on a manifold. Unlike works on Riemannian optimization that require the manifold to be smooth, the nonnegativity in chordal-NMF defines a non-differentiable manifold. We propose a Riemannian Multiplicative Update (RMU), and showcase the effectiveness of the chordal-NMF on synthetic and real-world datasets.

1.1
Deriving and motivating chordal distance

From the conic view of NMF (see introduction), the geometry of chordal distance can be seen as the Euclidean distance between unit vectors on the sphere.
Writing
\|\bm{M}-\bm{W}\bm{H}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{j}\|\bm{m}_{:j}-\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}\|_{2}^{2}
,
if both
\bm{m}_{:j}
and
\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}
have unit
\ell_{2}
-norm, then by the Euclidean inner product
\langle\bm{\xi},\bm{\zeta}\rangle=\|\bm{\xi}\|_{2}\|\bm{\zeta}\|_{2}\cos\theta(\bm{\xi},\bm{\zeta})
with
\theta(\bm{\xi},\bm{\zeta})
the angle between the vectors
\bm{\xi}
,
\bm{\zeta}
, we have

\dfrac{1}{2}\|\bm{M}-\bm{W}\bm{H}\|_{F}^{2}~=~\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Big(1-\big\langle\bm{m}_{:j},\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}\big\rangle\Big)~=~\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Big(1-\cos\theta(\bm{m}_{:j},\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j})\Big).

(2)

While the normalization of
\bm{m}_{:j}
is assumed, the normalization of
\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}
is important here.
Note that enforcing
\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}
to have unit
\ell_{2}-
norm forms a nonconvex constraint.

The expression (
2
) tells that the normalized Euclidean distance is the cosine distance.
Cosine distance is insensitive to the length of vector, see Fig.
1
for an example.

•

Quotient space interpretation.

From (
2
), we define a new objective in (
Chordal-NMF
) for measuring the distance between
\bm{m}_{:j}
and
\bm{W}\bm{h}_{:j}
by angle.
The division of the norm

that are proportional to

In this way, the chordal distance becomes purely angle-based, since the length information is ignored.

Navy navigation

makes use of the haversine
\textrm{hav}(\theta)\coloneqq(1-\cos\theta)/2
, where

Sphere interpretation.

In
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with Euclidean inner product
\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle
and norm
\|\bm{u}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle\bm{u},\bm{u}\rangle}
, the map

in (

sends nonzero vector

Considering

\bar{f}_{\text{chord}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow[0,2]\cup\{\infty\}\cup\{\pm\sqrt{-1}\infty\}\quad\text{as}\quad\bm{u},\bm{v}\mapsto\sqrt{2-2\dfrac{\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle}{\|\bm{u}\|_{2}\|\bm{v}\|_{2}}},

can be seen as the Euclidean distance between two unit vectors

,

For this function it should be noted that:

–

\bar{f}_{\text{chord}}
is undefined at

and/or
\bm{v}=\bm{0}

–

is nondifferentiable with respect to (wrt.) parallel unit vectors as

.

–

In the Euclidean case,
\bar{f}_{\text{chord}}
is non-convex wrt.
\bm{u}
,
\bm{v}
and the pair

.

These undesirable properties lead to choice of chordal distance below.

Definition 1
.

On

f_{\text{sq-chord}}:\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{m-1}\rightarrow[0,4]:\bm{u},\bm{v}\mapsto 2-2\langle\bm{u},\bm{v}\rangle.

Background of optimization on manifold

In this section, we discuss about nonnegative-constrained optimization, then we review manifold optimization.
Lastly, we present the Riemannian Multiplicative Update with some analysis.

Let
E
be a linear space (e.g.,
\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
) with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E}
and an induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{E}
.
Given a differentiable function
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
and the nonnegative orthant
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in E\,|\,\bm{x}\geq\bm{0}\}
, consider the minimization problems

\mathcal{P}_{0}:\argmin_{\bm{x}}f(\bm{x})~\,\textrm{s.t.}\,~\bm{x}\in\mathcal{M},\,\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}~~\overset{\text{restriction}}{\iff}~~\mathcal{P}_{1}:\argmin_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{M}}f(\bm{x})~\,\textrm{s.t.}\,~\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},

with the constraint set
\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in E\,|\,h(\bm{x})=0\}
under the defining function
h(\bm{x})
.
We focus on convex compact set
\mathcal{M}
being a smooth embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
h(\bm{x})
is many-times continuously differentiable.

In Euclidean optimization,
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{M}
is a constraint in
(\mathcal{P}_{0})
.
We “remove” such constraint using a restricted function
f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Here
f
is the
extension
of
f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}
that extends
\textrm{dom}f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}
from
\mathcal{M}
to
E
.
In this way,
(\mathcal{P}_{0})
can be written as a constrained manifold optimization
(\mathcal{P}_{1})
, which can further be converted into a unconstrained problem using indicator
\iota(\bm{x}):E\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
that
\iota(\bm{x})=0
if
\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\iota(\bm{x})=+\infty
if
\bm{x}\notin\mathcal{X}
.
The problems are related, solving one of them will help solving the others.
Below we review approaches in manifold optimization on solving these problems.

Nonnegative-constrained manifold optimization

Solving

Dual methods: not strictly feasible.

\mathcal{M}
after a gradient operation, so we need to pull it back onto
\mathcal{M}
.
This can be achieved by a particular smooth map, known as the retraction
\mathcal{R}:T_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
with
\bm{v}\mapsto\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{v})
.
A retraction maps a point on the tangent space
T_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{M}
onto
\mathcal{M}
such that each curve
c(t)=\mathcal{R}_{x}(tv)
satisfies
c(0)=\bm{x}
and
c^{\prime}(0)=\bm{v}

(
Boumal, 2023
, Ch3, Def3.47)
.
There are different ways to perform retractions, such as exponential map and metric retractions
(
Boumal, 2023
, §3.6)
.
However, in general, to obtain the exponential map of a manifold, one requires to solve a differential equation.
Thus, for computational efficiency, we consider the metric retraction
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{v})=\argmin_{\bm{y}\in E}\|\bm{x}+\bm{v}-\bm{y}\|_{E}^{2}~\,\textrm{s.t.}\,~h(\bm{y})=0

(
Boumal, 2023
, §7.7)
.
Note that
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{v})
is possibly non-unique and hard to compute.
Among all the retractions we focus on

\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}}(\bm{v})\coloneqq\dfrac{\bm{x}+\bm{v}}{\|\bm{x}+\bm{v}\|_{E}}.

(Metric retraction)

Riemannian and Euclidean gradient.

Given a (restricted) function
f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
with its smooth extension
f:E\to\mathbb{R}
,
let
\nabla f(\bm{x})
be the Euclidean gradient of
f
in the standard Euclidean basis at a point
\bm{x}\in E
, the Riemannian gradient of
f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}
defined on
\mathcal{M}
, denoted as
\textrm{grad}f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}
, at a point
\bm{z}
, wrt. the reference point
\bm{x}
, is the Euclidean gradient
\nabla f(\bm{z})
projected onto
T_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{M}
.
That is
\textrm{grad}f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{z})=\textrm{proj}_{T_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{M}}\nabla f(\bm{z})
.
For the sake of cheap computational cost, we endow the manifold with the standard Euclidean metric
2
2

2

The complete Riemannian gradient is computed using metric tensor.
Let
g
be the metric tensor of
\mathcal{M}
and let
G_{\bm{x}}
denotes the matrix representation of
g
,
then
\textrm{grad}f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}(\bm{z})=G^{-1}_{\bm{x}}\textrm{proj}_{T_{\bm{x}}\mathcal{M}}\nabla f(\bm{z})
.
.
To ease the notation, we write
f\big|_{\mathcal{M}}
as
f
taking the natural extension.

2.3
Riemannian Multiplicative Update (RMU)

In this work, we propose a RMU for solving Chordal-NMF in the form of
(\mathcal{P}_{1})
.
RMU is a modified Riemannian gradient descent (RGD) update:
\bm{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}_{k}}(\alpha\bm{v}_{k})
, where
\bm{v}_{k}=-\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})
is the (negative) Riemannian gradient of
f
at
\bm{x}_{k}
.
RMU is projection-free, and it guarantees the nonnegativity of
\bm{x}
by selecting a special element-wise stepsize such that
\bm{x}_{k+1}
stays within
\mathcal{X}
.
Below we review Euclidean Multiplicative Update (EMU) and then we generalize it to the Riemannian case.

Euclidean MU (EMU).

EMU was first proposed in
Richardson (1972)
;
Lucy (1974)
;
Daube-Witherspoon and
Muehllehner (1986)
, and gained popularity in NMF
Gillis (2020)
.
EMU can be done via a “element-wise sign decomposition” of the Euclidean gradient
\nabla f(\bm{x})=\nabla^{+}f(\bm{x})-\nabla^{-}f(\bm{x})
, where
\nabla^{+}f(\bm{x})\geq\bm{0}
and
\nabla^{-}f(\bm{x})\geq\bm{0}
.
Let denote
\odot
the element-wise product and
\oslash
the element-wise division, for solving a nonnegative-constrained Euclidean optimization problem,
the EMU step has the form

\bm{x}_{k+1}~=~\bm{x}_{k}\odot\nabla^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})\oslash\nabla^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k}),

(EMU)

which is obtained by choosing an element-wise stepsize
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{x}_{k}\oslash\nabla^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})
in the Euclidean gradient descent step
\bm{x}_{k+1}=\bm{x}_{k}-\alpha\nabla f(\bm{x}_{k})
, see
Yoo and Choi (2008)
for the derivation
3
3

3

In the literature (
EMU
) is written as
\bm{x}_{k+1}=\bm{x}_{k}\dfrac{\nabla^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})}{\nabla^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})}
.
We do not use this convention because it confuses with metric retraction for our purpose.
.

RMU.

Now we generalize EMU.
First, a Riemannian gradient
\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x})
always admits element-wise sign decomposition
4
4

4

For any object
\bm{b}
, it can be written as
\bm{b}=\bm{b}^{+}-\bm{b}^{-}
with
\bm{b}^{+}=\max(\bm{0},\bm{b})
and
\bm{b}^{-}=\max(\bm{0},-\bm{b})
.

\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x})=\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x})-\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x})
.

We generalize (
EMU
) to the Riemannian case in Proposition
2
, where we prove that the nonnegativity of the update is preserved by RMU without projection.
The key idea of the proof is the metric retraction and the choice of step-size
\bm{\alpha}
that acts component-wise on the update direction
\bm{v}_{k}
.

Proposition 2

(RMU)
.

Denote
\bm{v}_{k}=-\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})
the anti-parallel direction of the Riemannian gradient of a manifold
\mathcal{M}
at
\bm{x}_{k}
, and let
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}_{k}}
be the metric retraction onto
\mathcal{M}
.
If a nonnegative
\bm{x}_{k}
is updated by
\bm{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}_{k}}(\bm{\alpha}\odot\bm{v}_{k})

with an element-wise stepsize
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{x}_{k}\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})\in E
, then
\bm{x}_{k+1}\geq\bm{0}
and is on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

The term
\bm{\alpha}\odot(-\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k}))
with
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{x}_{k}\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})
can be computed as

\begin{array}[]{lll}-\bm{\alpha}\odot\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})&=\big(\bm{x}_{k}\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})\big)\odot\big(\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})-\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})\big)\\
&=\bm{x}_{k}\odot\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})-\bm{x}_{k}.\end{array}

The element-wise operations as linear transformations in general do not preserve the tangent condition of a manifold into a point.
However here we can still apply (
Metric retraction
) on
-\bm{\alpha}\odot\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})
, the
\bm{x}_{k}
terms got canceled and gives

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bm{x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{\bm{x}_{k}}\big(-\bm{\alpha}\odot\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})\big)&=\dfrac{\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{\alpha}\odot\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})}{\|\bm{x}_{k}-\bm{\alpha}\odot\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})\|_{E}}\\
&=\dfrac{\bm{x}_{k}\odot\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})}{\|\bm{x}_{k}\odot\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})\oslash\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})\|_{E}}.\end{array}

The ratio between
\textrm{grad}^{+}f
and
\textrm{grad}^{-}f
is nonnegative as its parts are nonnegative by definition, the denominator is nonnegative, therefore
\bm{x}_{k+1}
is nonnegative if
\bm{x}_{k}
is nonnegative.
∎

Proposition
2
also gives a convergence condition to a critical point:

•

If the solution lies strictly inside the nonnegative orthant, this corresponds to
\textrm{grad}f(\bm{x}_{k})=\bm{0}
, which is equivalent to
\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})=\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})
.
That is, the convergence of the sequence
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
can be easily checked by comparing
\textrm{grad}^{-}f(\bm{x}_{k})
and
\textrm{grad}^{+}f(\bm{x}_{k})
.

•

Otherwise, in the constrained setting, convergence is characterized by the KKT conditions on
\mathcal{M}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

As RMU is projection-free, it has a lower per-iteration computational cost, making it suitable for Chordal-NMF.

We are now ready to move on to the explicit update of
\bm{H}
and
\bm{W}
.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a NMF model called Chordal-NMF, which is different from the classical NMF using a point-to-point Euclidean distance, Chordal-NMF uses a ray-to-ray distance.
Based on the geometric interpretation that NMF describes a cone, we argued that chordal distance, which measures the angle between two vector in the nonnegative orthant, is more suitable than the Euclidean distance for NMF.
Under a BCD framework, we developed a new projection-free algorithm, called Riemannian Multiplicative Update to solve the Chordal-NMF, where Riemannian optimization technique is used.
We showcase the effectiveness of the Chordal-NMF on the synthetic dataset and real-world multispectral images.

\mathcal{M}^{[2]}
is the set

T_{\bm{Z}}\mathcal{M}^{[2]}\coloneqq\Big\{\bm{W}\,\big|\,\langle\bm{W}\bm{A}_{1},\bm{Z}\rangle_{\textrm{F}}=\langle\bm{W}\bm{A}_{2},\bm{Z}\rangle_{\textrm{F}}=0\Big\},

its adjoint is

\textrm{D}h(\bm{W})^{*}[{\bm{\alpha}}]~=~2\alpha_{1}\bm{W}\bm{A}_{1}+2\alpha_{2}\bm{W}\bm{A}_{2}~=~2\bm{W}(\alpha_{1}\bm{A}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\bm{A}_{2}).

Let vec be vectorization and
let
\otimes_{\text{K}}
be the Kronecker product,
now the
\bm{\alpha}
that minimizes

\big\|\bm{W}-\textrm{D}h(\bm{Z})^{*}[{\bm{\alpha}}]\big\|_{\textrm{F}}^{2}
also minimizes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\Big\|\textrm{vec}\Big(\bm{W}-2\bm{Z}(\alpha_{1}\bm{A}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\bm{A}_{2})\Big)\Big\|_{2}^{2}&=&\displaystyle\Big\|\textrm{vec}(\bm{W})-2(\bm{I}\otimes_{\text{K}}\bm{Z})\textrm{vec}\Big(\alpha_{1}\bm{A}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\bm{A}_{2}\Big)\Big\|_{2}^{2}\\
&=&\bigg\|\textrm{vec}(\bm{W})-2(\bm{I}\otimes_{\text{K}}\bm{Z})\begin{bmatrix}\textrm{vec}\bm{A}_{1}~\textrm{vec}\bm{A}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{bmatrix}\bigg\|_{2}^{2}.\end{array}

To simplify the notation, let
\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}=(\bm{I}\otimes_{\text{K}}\bm{Z})[\textrm{vec}\bm{A}_{1}~\textrm{vec}\bm{A}_{2}]
, where the subscript
\bm{Z}
indicates the dependence of
\bm{Z}
.
Now
\bm{\alpha}
is the root of the following normal equation

\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{*}\\
\alpha_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}~=~\dfrac{1}{2}\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\textrm{vec}\bm{W}~~\implies~~\bm{\alpha}^{*}~=~\dfrac{1}{2}(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}})^{-1}\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\top}\textrm{vec}\bm{W}~=~\dfrac{1}{2}\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\bm{W}.

Now the orthogonal projector
\textrm{proj}_{T_{\bm{Z}}\mathcal{M}^{[2]}}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\mapsto\mathcal{M}^{[2]}
is

\textrm{proj}_{T_{\bm{Z}}\mathcal{M}^{[2]}}(\bm{W})~=~\bm{W}-2\bm{Z}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\dfrac{1}{2}\Big(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\bm{W}\Big)_{j}\bm{A}_{j}~=~\bm{W}-2\bm{Z}(\alpha_{1}^{*}\bm{A}_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{*}\bm{A}_{2}).

(8)

The Riemannian gradient is then

\textrm{grad}F(\bm{W})~=~2\textrm{proj}_{T_{\bm{Z}}\mathcal{M}^{[2]}}\Big(\nabla F(\bm{W})\Big)~\overset{\eqref{M2:proj}}{=}~\nabla F(\bm{W})-2\bm{Z}(\alpha_{1}^{*}\bm{A}_{1}+\alpha_{2}^{*}\bm{A}_{2}).

(9)

Note that the value of
\alpha_{1}^{*}
and
\alpha_{2}^{*}
in
(
9
)
is an implicit function of
\bm{Z}
and
\nabla F(\bm{W})
as we have that

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{*}\\
\alpha_{2}^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\Big(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\nabla F(\bm{W})\Big)_{1}\\
\Big(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\nabla F(\bm{W})\Big)_{2}\end{bmatrix}

.
So the explicit expression of the Riemannian gradient is

\textrm{grad}F(\bm{W})~=~\nabla F(\bm{W})-\bm{Z}\Big(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\nabla F(\bm{W})\Big)_{1}\bm{A}_{1}-\bm{Z}\Big(\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\textrm{vec}\nabla F(\bm{W})\Big)_{2}\bm{A}_{2}.

Then we compute
\textrm{grad}^{+}F=\max\{\textrm{grad}F,\bm{0}\}
and
\textrm{grad}^{-}F=\max\{-\textrm{grad}F,\bm{0}\}
to proceed with RMU.

In conclusion, we can see that to run RMU on
\mathcal{M}^{[2]}
, there are challenges:

•

the computation of
\alpha^{*}
, which includes the computation of
\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m^{2}\times 2}
,

\bm{S}_{\bm{Z}}^{\dagger}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, in which all these terms have to be re-computed in each iteration.

•

the computation of the metric projection onto
\mathcal{M}^{[2]}
itself is a difficult problem.
