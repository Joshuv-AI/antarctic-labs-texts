Subword Complexes and Kalai’s Conjecture on Reconstruction of Spheres Thanks: Both authors were supported by the Austrian Science Fund FWF, grant P 33278.
Cesar Ceballos, Joseph Doolittle

Abstract
. A famous theorem in polytope theory states that the combinatorial type of a simplicial polytope is completely determined by its facet-ridge graph. This celebrated result was proven by Blind and Mani in 1987, via a non-constructive proof using topological tools from homology theory. An elegant constructive proof was given by Kalai shortly after. In their original paper, Blind and Mani asked whether their result can be extended to simplicial spheres, and a positive answer to their question was conjectured by Kalai in 2009. In this paper, we show that Kalai’s conjecture holds in the particular case of Knutson and Miller’s spherical subword complexes. This family of simplicial spheres arises in the context of Coxeter groups, and is conjectured to be polytopal. In contrast, not all manifolds are reconstructible. We show two explicit examples, namely the torus and the projective plane.



1.
Introduction

The combinatorial structure of a simple polytope is known to be completely determined by its graph.
In other words, one can determine all the faces of a simple polytope knowing only information about its vertices and the edges connecting them.
This astonishing and beautiful result was originally raised as a conjecture by Perles during conversations at Oberwolfach meetings on convex bodies in Germany in 1984 and 1986, see
[
8
]
.
The result was then proved by Blind and Mani in their 1987 seminal paper
[
8
]
, where they give a non-constructive proof using topological tools from homology theory in the dual context of simplicial polytopes.

Theorem 1.1

(Blind and Mani
[
8
]
)
.

Let
P,Q
be
d
-dimensional simple polytopes. Every isomorphism between their graphs
f:G(P)\to G(Q)
has a unique extension to a face isomorphism
g:P\to Q
.

Shortly after, a simple and elegant constructive proof was given by Kalai in his work “A simple way to tell a simple polytope from its graph”
[
30
]
.
Kalai’s proof uses an exponential time algorithm. The complexity of algorithms for reconstructing a simple polytope from its graph (or equivalently, a simplicial polytope from its facet-ridge graph) is studied in
[
28
]
and extended to a polynomial time algorithm for determining the
(d-2)
-faces in
[
20
]
.
One natural question is whether Blind and Mani’s result also holds for simplicial spheres.
This was posed as a question by Blind and Mani in
[
8
, Question 1]
.
A positive answer to this question was conjectured by Kalai in his more recent online blog
[
31
]
.

Conjecture 1.2

(Blind and Mani
[
8
]
, Kalai
[
31
]
)
.

Let
P,Q
be
d
-dimensional simplicial spheres. Every isomorphism between their facet-ridge graphs
f:FR(P)\to FR(Q)
has a unique extension to a simplicial isomorphism
g:P\to Q
.

The classification and comparison of simplicial spheres and simplicial polytopes is an interesting and challenging topic on its own. It is well known, by Steinitz’s theorem, that every 2-dimensional sphere can be realized as the boundary complex of a 3-dimensional polytope
[
23
, Chapter 13]
.
The smallest examples of non-polytopal spheres area the Barnette sphere
[
3
]
and the Brückner–Grünbaum–Sreedharan sphere
[
24
]
.
Both of these are 3-dimensional simplicial spheres with 8 vertices which are not the boundary of a 4-dimensional polytope (but both are reconstructible from their facet-ridge graphs using Kalai’s method from
[
30
]
).

In
[
37
]
, Mani showed that every simplicial
d
-sphere with
d+4
vertices can be realized as the boundary of a
(d+1)
-polytope.
The enumeration of simplicial 3-spheres (some of which are not polytopal) with up to 10 vertices can be found in
[
36
]
; see also
[
18
]
for an overview of more recent results.
Examples on non-polytopal spheres which are non-constructible, and in particular non-shellable, using knot constrains appear in
[
25
,
35
]
.
Interestingly, starting in dimension
d=3
, most
d
-spheres are not polytopal.
For
d>3
, this follows from results of Goodman and Pollack from 1986
[
21
,
22
]
and Kalai from 1988
[
29
]
, who showed upper and lower bounds for the number of simplicial polytopes and simplicial spheres, respectively. For
d=3
, the result follows from the lower bound for the number of simplicial 3-spheres shown by Pfeifle and Ziegler in 2004
[
41
]
.
Given the substantial differences between simplicial spheres and simplicial polytopes, one could guess that Kalai’s conjecture on the reconstruction of spheres from their facet ridge-graph might be false.
One possibility to look for counterexamples to Kalai’s conjecture is to look at explicit families of non-polytopal spheres. One special class of simplicial spheres which are conjectured to be polytopal and which have received a lot of attention in recent years are Knutson and Miller’s spherical subword complexes.

Knutson and Miller introduced the concept of subword complexes first in type
A
, in connection to their study of Gröbner geometry of Schubert varieties
[
33
]
, and then generalized them in the general context of Coxeter groups in
[
32
]
.
Subword complexes appear in many different contexts and have connections to toric and Bott-Samelson varieties
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
,
cluster algebras
[
12
]
,
Hopf algebras
[
4
]
,
combinatorics and discrete geometry
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
17
,
26
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
46
,
47
]
.
They also contain diverse families of simplicial complexes of interest, including
boundaries of polytopes such as
cyclic polytopes
[
10
]
,
duals of associahedra and generalized associahedra
[
10
,
13
,
19
,
43
]
,
duals of pointed-pseudotriangulation polytopes
[
42
,
45
]
,
and
simplicial multiassociahedra
[
27

42
]
(the last only conjectured to be polytopal).
A fundamental result in the theory of subword complexes is that they are topological balls or spheres
[
32
]
. Knutson and Miller asked whether spherical subword complexes can be realized as boundary complexes of polytopes
[
32
]
, and this was conjectured to be the case in
[
10
]
.
An outstanding and difficult open problem is to find polytopal constructions of spherical subword complexes.
This has been done in some cases, but remains open in general, see e.g.
[

]
and the references therein for more information in this regard.
We refer to
[

We began this project hoping to disprove the two conjectures at the same time.
If there was a pair of non-isomorphic spherical subword complexes with the same facet-ridge graph, then Kalai’s conjecture and the polytopality conjecture of spherical subword complexes would both be false.
Indeed, on one hand, we would have a direct counterexample to Kalai’s conjecture.
On the other hand, since Kalai’s conjecture does hold for the special case of polytopes, at most one of the two subword complexes could be polytopal, indirectly disproving the polytopality conjecture.
We did not find two such subword complexes. Instead,
we proved that spherical subword complexes of finite type satisfy Kalai’s conjecture.

Theorem 1.3

(c.f. Theorem
5.1
)
.

Spherical subword complexes of finite type are completely determined by their facet-ridge graph.

Our proof is not constructive and relies on the topological tools developed by Blind and Mani in
[
8
]
with some appropriate modifications. A key ingredient in our proof is Theorem
4.5
, which states that spherical subword complexes of finite type are strongly vertex decomposable, and therefore strongly shellable.
We suspect that the finite type condition is not necessary, see Conjecture
4.14
and Conjecture
5.2
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we cover the required background material. In Section

4
, we show the required properties of spherical subword complexes. In Section
5
, we combine the previous sections to state and prove our main result.
In Section
6
, we show that the analogue of Kalai’s conjecture does not hold for simplicial manifolds in general.
More precisely, we present two explicit examples of non-isomorphic triangulations of the torus and of the projective plane with the same facet-ridge graph.
In Section
7
, we discuss further directions of potential research.

2.
Preliminaries

For this paper, we had to pull together ideas from several areas. In this section we reiterate the required background. We split this in two. The first subsection deals with the fundamental properties of simplicial complexes. The second covers the basics of subword complexes.

2.1.
Simplicial complexes

The central mathematical object in this paper is the simplicial complex. A
simplicial complex

\Delta
on a set
V
is a subset of the power set
2^{V}
, such that if
J\subset I
and
I\in\Delta
, then
J\in\Delta
.

The elements of a simplicial complex are the
faces
of the complex. The
dimension
of a face
I
is
\dim{I}=|I|-1
. The
vertices
of a simplicial complex are the
0
-dimensional faces. The
edges
are the
1
-dimensional faces. The
facets
are those faces which are maximal within
\Delta
, meaning that there are no faces which properly contain them. We denote the facets of
\Delta
as
F(\Delta)
. The
ridges
of
\Delta
are the faces which contain all but one vertex of a facet.

From the definition, all faces of a simplicial complex can be determined by its facets. We give this idea notation,
\Delta=\langle F(\Delta)\rangle
, where
\Delta
is exactly the complex
generated
by the facets of
\Delta
. A simplicial complex is said to be
pure
if all of its facets are the same dimension. In such case, this is called the dimension of the complex.

Simplicial complexes provide a common structure for surprisingly broad collection of other mathematical objects. A simplicial complex can be given geometrical information. The standard geometric representation of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is denoted
|\Delta|
and is defined by

|\Delta|:=\bigcup_{I\in\Delta}e_{I}

where
e_{I}
is the convex hull of the standard basis vectors
e_{i}
with
i\in I
. This notation of
|\Delta|
should not be confused with the cardinality notation, which we also use throughout this paper.
Geometric realizations will be restricted to complexes, whereas cardinality will only be applied to faces.
Simplicial complexes can have many geometric realizations, where the standard basis vectors are replaced by another collection of points in space. We say such a realization is a geometric realization as long as the intersection of the convex hull associated to a pair of faces is the convex hull of the intersection of those faces.

The geometric realization gives a topology to simplicial complexes, inherited from the standard topology on euclidean space.
We define a simplicial complex to be a
spherical
simplicial complex if its geometric realization is homeomorphic to a sphere.
We also define a simplicial complex to be a
simplicial manifold
if its geometric realization is homeomorphic to a topological manifold.

There are several operations which can be done to a simplicial complex which result in another simplicial complex.
We list several of these operations, which we will use later in this paper.

Given a simplicial complex
\Delta
and a subset of its ground set
W\subset V
, we define the
induced subcomplex

\Delta|_{W}
. In set notation,

\Delta|_{W}=\{J\in\Delta\st J\subset W\}
.

The
deletion
of a face
I
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is the simplicial complex where all faces containing
I
are removed. In set notation,

del_{\Delta}(I)=\{J\in\Delta\st I\not\subseteq J\}
. When
I
is a single vertex, this is equivalent to removing that vertex from the vertex set,
del_{\Delta}(I)=\Delta|_{V\setminus I}

The
star
of a face
I
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
is the complex generated by the facets containing
I
. In set notation,

star_{\Delta}(I)=\{J\in\Delta\st I\cup J\in\Delta\}
.

The
link
of a face
I
is the set of faces of the star of
I
that do not intersect
I
non-trivially. In set notation,

link_{\Delta}(I)=\{J\in\Delta\st I\cup J\in\Delta,I\cap J=\emptyset\}
.

Our final operation is less standard than those that appear before. We define the
costar
of a face
I
to be the complex generated by the facets not containing
I
. In set notation,

costar_{\Delta}(I)=\{J\in\Delta\st\exists\Gamma\in F(\Delta),J\subseteq\Gamma,I\not\subseteq\Gamma\}
.

From a simplicial complex, we can obtain the facet-ridge graph. There are multiple possible definitions of this graph, useful in different circumstances, for our purposes, the most common definition is sufficient. The
facet-ridge
graph of
\Delta
is the graph
FR(\Delta)
whose vertices are the facets of
\Delta
, and whose edges are pairs of facets that contain a ridge of both facets.

2.1.1.
Shellability and vertex decomposability

A very important concept in the study of simplicial complexes is that of shellability. A
shellable
simplicial complex
\Delta
is either a simplex, or a pure complex with a facet
F_{i}
such that
costar_{\Delta}(F_{i})
is shellable and
\langle\{F_{i}\}\rangle\cap costar_{\Delta}(F_{i})
is pure.
This definition is one of many equivalent definitions.

For its utility in proofs, we define a variant of shellability used by Blind and Mani in
[
8
]
, called strong shellability. A simplicial complex
\Delta
is
strongly shellable
if for any face
I
, both
costar_{I}(\Delta)
and
star_{I}(\Delta)
are shellable.

Vertex decomposability is a similar condition to shellability, but quite similar.
A
vertex decomposable
simplicial complex
\Delta
is either a simplex, or a pure complex with a vertex
v_{i}
such that
del_{\Delta}(v_{i})
is vertex decomposable and
link_{\Delta}(v_{i})
is vertex decomposable.

Similarly to shellability, we can define a stronger condition than vertex decomposable.
A simplicial complex
\Delta
is
strongly vertex decomposable
if for any face
I
, both
costar_{I}(\Delta)
and
star_{I}(\Delta)
are vertex decomposable.

These strong definitions imply their base counterparts. In particular, choosing
I=\emptyset
gives
star_{\Delta}(\emptyset)=\Delta
, and
costar_{I}(\emptyset)=\emptyset
, a simplex.
Since the simplex is trivially vertex decomposable and shellable,
\Delta
having the strong version of these properties implies that
\Delta
has the base property as well.

Since vertex decomposability implies shellability
[
6
]
, we have the following straightforward implication.

Lemma 2.1
.

Strongly vertex decomposable simplicial complexes are strongly shellable.

2.2.
Subword complexes

Subword complexes are simplicial complexes which naturally arise in the context of Coxeter groups.
Before defining them we recall some basic definitions. We refer to
[
7
]
for a through exposition on Coxeter groups.

A
Coxeter group

W
is a group generated by a collection
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
which satisfies the relations
(s_{i}s_{j})^{m_{ij}}=1
, where
m_{ii}=1
and
m_{ij}\geq 2
for
i\neq j
.
The pair
(W,S)
is called a
Coxeter system
.
The
length
of an element
w\in W
is the smallest
r
such that it can be written of the form
w=s_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\dots s_{i_{r}}
. A
reduced expression
of
w
is an expression of minimal length.

Coxeter groups generalize the notion of reflection groups, groups generated by a set of reflections of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space.
A notable example of a Coxeter group is the symmetric group of permutations of
[n+1]
.
This group is generated by the collection
S=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
, where
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
is the simple transposition that swaps the numbers
i
and
i+1
.
These simple transpositions satisfy the relations
s_{i}^{2}=1
,
(s_{i}s_{i+1})^{3}=1
(also known as the braid relations) and
(s_{i}s_{j})^{2}=1
whenever
|i-j|>1
.
Unlike the case of the symmetric group, a Coxeter group associated to some general choice of values
m_{ij}
is usually infinite.
In 1935, Coxeter gave a classification of finite Coxeter groups in terms of Dynkin diagrams.
In this language, the symmetric group corresponds to the type
A_{n}
Coxeter group.

Given a Coxeter system
(W,S)
, we recall certain simplicial complexes introduced by Knutson and Miller in
[
32
]
.
For this, we consider a word
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{r})
in the generators
S
and an element
\pi\in W
of the group.
For
J\subseteq[r]
, we denote by
Q_{J}
the subword of
Q
consisting of the letters with positions in
J
.
The
subword complex

\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
is the simplicial complex whose facets are subsets
I\subseteq[r]
such that
Q_{[r]\setminus I}
is a reduced expression for
\pi
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
W=S_{4}
be the symmetric group generated by simple transpositions
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
with
i=1,2,3
.
Let
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4},q_{5})=(s_{1},s_{2},s_{1},s_{2},s_{1})
and
\pi=s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}=s_{2}s_{1}s_{2}
.
The facets of the subword complex
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
are

\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{3,4\},\{4,5\},\{1,5\}.

This complex is depicted on the left of the figure below.
It is the boundary complex of a pentagon, a one dimensional sphere.

, no reduced expression of

uses the letter
q_{6}=s_{3}
, which implies that
6
belongs to every facet. The facets of
\mathcal{S}(\widetilde{Q},\pi)
are

\{1,2,6\},\{2,3,6\},\{3,4,6\},\{4,5,6\},\{1,5,6\}.

Two fundamental theorems in the theory of subword complexes are:

Theorem 2.2

(
[
32
, Theorem 2.5]
)
.

Subword complexes
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
are vertex decomposable, hence shellable.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
32
, Theorem 3.7]
)
.

A non-empty subword complex
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
is either a ball or a sphere. Moreover, it is a sphere if and only if the Demazure product
\operatorname{Dem}(Q)=\pi
.

Here, the
Demazure product

\operatorname{Dem}(Q)\in W
is an element of the group which is defined recursively as follows. If
Q^{\prime}
is the word obtained from

by adding
s\in S
at the end, then

\operatorname{Dem}(Q^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\mu s,&\text{if }\ell(\mu s)>\ell(\mu),\\

\mu=\operatorname{Dem}(Q)

.

[
32

The Demazure product
\operatorname{Dem}(Q)\in W
is the unique maximal element in Bruhat order among all expressions obtained from subwords of

.

3.
Manifold Reconstruction

In this section, we revisit Blind and Mani’s foundational work on the topic of reconstruction from the facet-ridge graph. We have updated some of their terminology, and generalized some of their lemmas. We use the same numbering of lemmas, for easier cross-reference. In every case, we bold the portion of the lemma that is mathematically different from their work. We make no explicit mention of notation and style changes. Further generalizations of their lemmas may be possible, but are not required for our purposes in this work.

In this section,
A
and
B
will always refer to some simplicial complexes, with further conditions specified within the lemmas. The function
f
will always be an isomorphism from the facet-ridge graph of
A
to the facet-ridge graph of
B
. There is an induced map on faces,
g:A\to B
, defined as follows:

g(I)=\bigcap_{F\supset I}f(F)

It is important to note that
g
restricted to facets and ridges is identical to
f
. In general,
g
may not be an isomorphism for any other faces.

Lemma 2
.

[
8
, Lemma 2]

Let
A,B
be simplicial
manifolds without boundary
.
If
I
is a
(d-2)
-face of
A
, then either
g(I)
is a
(d-2)
-face, or every
(d-2)
-face of
\langle f(F(star_{A}(I)))\rangle
is contained in only one facet.

Proof.

The proof of this lemma remains identical to the original proof.
∎

Lemma 4
.

[
8
, Lemma 4]

Let
A,B
be
simplicial spheres
, and let
I
be a
(d-2)
-face of
A
.
Then either
g(I)
is a
(d-2)
-face, or
\widetilde{H}_{d-2}[B-I]
is nontrivial, where

B-I=\langle F(B)\setminus f(F(star_{A}(I)))\rangle
.

Proof.

In the original proof, the authors suggest that their work could be extended in this way.
Using the generalized Schoenflies theorem
[
40
,
9
]
fills the gap left when extending the original lemma to non-polytopal spheres.
∎

Lemma 8
.

[
8
, Lemma 8]

Let
A
and
B
be
strongly shellable simplicial spheres
.
Then
g
restricted to the
(d-2)
-faces of
A
is a bijection to the
(d-2)
-faces of
B
.

Proof.

This follows from the previously updated Lemma, and from the assumption that without loss of generality,

has at least as many
(d-3)
-faces as

.
Of course, following the result of the lemma,

and

have the same number of

-faces, but this is not assumed a priori.
∎

We conclude this section with a more general version of Blind and Mani’s Theorem 1.

Theorem 3.1

(
[

)
.

If

and

The proof is identical to Blind and Mani’s proof, with the new Lemma

4.
Spherical Subword Complexes

The main purpose of this section is to show that spherical subword complexes of finite type are strongly vertex decomposable (Theorem
4.5
).
This will be used to prove our main result in Section
5
(Theorem
5.1
).
We also present a necessary and sufficient condition for the same statement to hold for infinite types (Proposition
4.15
). We start by recalling some basic properties about subword complexes of finite type.

4.1.
Basic properties for finite types

Let
(W,S)
be a Coxeter system of finite type.
The first lemma allows us to restrict the study of spherical subword complexes to those for which
\pi=\omega_{\circ}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
be a spherical subword complex of finite type. Then
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)\cong\mathcal{S}(Q^{\prime},\omega_{\circ})
for some word
Q^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{r})
. By the spherical condition we know that the Demazure product
\operatorname{Dem}(Q)=\pi
. Consider a word
P=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
which gives a reduced expression of
\pi^{-1}\omega_{\circ}
, and let
Q^{\prime}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{r},p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
be the word obtained by appending
P
to
Q
.
Note that every reduced expression of
\pi
in
Q
determines a reduced expression of
\omega_{\circ}
in
Q^{\prime}
by adding all the letters of
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
to it.
In the other direction, every reduced expression of
\omega_{\circ}
in
Q^{\prime}
contains all the letters of
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k})
, and its restriction to
Q
is a reduced expression of
\pi
; otherwise, its restriction to
Q
would give a reduced expression whose length is bigger than
\ell(\pi)
, which contradicts the fact that
\operatorname{Dem}(Q)=\pi
.
As a consequence, the facets of
\mathcal{S}(Q^{\prime},\omega_{\circ})
are exactly the facets of
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
.
∎

The subword complexes of the form
\mathcal{S}(Q,\omega_{\circ})
are especially interesting and have received a lot of attention in the literature.
The involution
\psi:S\rightarrow S
given by
\psi(s)=\omega_{\circ}^{-1}s\omega_{\circ}
allows us to define the
rotated word

Q_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circlearrowleft}}{{s}}}
or the
rotation
of
Q=(s,q_{2},\ldots,q_{r})
along the letter
s
as
(q_{2},\dots,q_{r},\psi(s))
.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
10
, Proposition 3.9]
)
.

Let
Q=(s,q_{2},\ldots,q_{r})
. Then
\mathcal{S}(Q,\omega_{\circ})\cong\mathcal{S}(Q_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circlearrowleft}}{{s}}},\omega_{\circ})
.

The isomorphism between
\mathcal{S}(Q,\omega_{\circ},
) and
\mathcal{S}(Q_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circlearrowleft}}{{s}}},\omega_{\circ})
in the previous lemma is induced by sending
q_{i}
to
q_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq r
and the initial

to the final
\psi(s)
(in terms of positions,

is mapped to

).

4.2.
Strong vertex decomposability

Our main goal is to prove the following result.

Theorem 4.5
.

Spherical subword complexes of finite type are strongly vertex decomposable, hence strongly shellable.

By definition, this is equivalent to showing that the star and costar of any non-empty face are vertex decomposable.
These properties are proven in Theorem
4.6
and Theorem
4.7
, respectively. The first is a fairly simple argument, but the second is significantly more involved.

4.2.1.
The star of a face

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
be a spherical subword complex and
I\in\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
be a non-empty face.
The complex

is vertex decomposable.

Proof.

The star of a face
I
is equal to the join of
I
and its link. So, it suffices to show that the link is vertex decomposable.
The link

can be naturally identified with the subword complex
\mathcal{S}(Q^{\prime},\pi)
, where
Q^{\prime}
is obtained from
Q
by removing the letters
q_{i}
for
i\in I
. Since subword complexes are vertex decomposable, the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)
be a spherical subword complex and

be a non-empty face.
We want to show that the complex

|I|

Assume
|I|\geq 2

is pure by definition. We will show that it has a vertex whose link and deletion are vertex decomposable.

By Lemma
4.10
, any element

By Lemma

link_{costar_{\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)}(I)}(i)=costar_{\mathcal{S}(Q\setminus q_{i},\pi)}(I\setminus i).

\mathcal{S}(Q\setminus q_{i},\pi)

For every spherical subword complex

Spherical subword complexes of infinite type are strongly vertex decomposable.

and

Let
\mathcal{S}(Q,\pi)

4.14
holds, and consider a vertex

Since

5.
Subword complex reconstruction

As a consequence of Theorem
4.5
and Blind and Mani’s original techniques summarized in Theorem
3.1
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A,B
be spherical subword complexes of finite type. Every isomorphism between their facet-ridge graphs
f:FR(A)\to FR(B)
has a unique extension to a simplicial isomorphism
g:A\to B
.

In other words, spherical subword complexes satisfy Kalai’s Conjecture
1.2
, and are completely determined by their facet-ridge graph.
However, our proof is not constructive and it is an open problem to describe the faces of the complex in terms of their facet-ridge graph.

Open Problem 5.1
.

Given the facet-ridge graph of a spherical subword complex, find a direct description of its faces.

We remark that Theorem
5.1
does not hold for subword complexes that are topological balls.
For instance, the subword complexes in Example
4.1
and Example
4.4
have the same facet-ridge graph but are not isomorphic.

We conjecture that Theorem
5.1
holds in general, not only for finite types.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Spherical subword complexes of infinite type are completely determined by their facet-ridge graph.

This conjecture follows from either of the two equivalent Conjecture
4.13
or Conjecture
4.14
.

6.
Non reconstructible manifolds

Not all manifolds are reconstructible. We fully illustrate two examples. The first is the projective plane, the second is a torus.
In each case, we provide a pair of facet lists with the same facet-ridge graph, but different combinatorics. We further illustrate where each component of the Blind and Mani result fails to hold.

Strategy 6.1
.

The strategy we use to construct two non-isomorphic simplicial complexes with the same facet-ridge graph is as follows.
We start with two identical copies
A
and
A^{\prime}
of a triangulation whose simplicial automorphism group has order smaller than the order of the automorphism group of its facet-ridge graph.
We choose a graph automorphism
f
which does not come from a simplicial automorphism.
Then, we use
f
to perform a sequence of stellar subdivisions on
A
and
A^{\prime}
simultaneously, such that at each step the corresponding facet-ridge graphs are isomorphic.
After performing several of these transformations the combinatorics of the two triangulations will be changed, but there will still be an isomorphism of their facet-ridge graphs.

This is how we proceed.
If we perform a stellar subdivision of a facet
F
in
A
, then we simultaneously perform a stellar subdivision of the facet
f(F)
in
A^{\prime}
.
In the graph, this operation replaces a vertex (associated to the facets
F
and
f(F)
) by the graph of a
d
-simplex, and the adjacent
d+1
edges to these vertices become adjacent to the
d+1
vertices of the added simplex.
We call this operation the
truncation of a vertex
, see Figure
2
.

The resulting two graphs are still isomorphic, and the automorphism relating them is just a small modification of
f
.
Performing these operations along a well-chosen sequence of facets will eventually cause the combinatorics of the two triangulations to differ from each other.

Figure 2.
Stellar subdivision of a 2-dimensional simplex and the truncation of the corresponding vertex on the facet-ridge graph.

This idea of using graphs with large automorphism group is not new.
Blind and Mani already mention at the end of their introduction in
[
8
]
, “also, there exist simple cellular decompositions
\mathfrak{C}
of the torus, whose 1-skeleton has a larger automorphism group than
\mathfrak{C}
itself.”
Adiprisito described a decoration of the minimal triangulation of both the torus and the projective plane as counterexamples to Kalai’s conjecture for surfaces
[
1
]
.
Mohar and Vodopivec found multiple embedings of a certain class of graph in non-orientable surfaces of high genus.
The dual simplical complexes to these embeddings forms a large class of complexes with the same facet-ridge graph but different combinatorics
[
39
, Section 5]
.

We offer the rest of this section as a concrete and fully detailed expansion of these ideas.
With facet lists explicitly written and isomorphisms clearly defined, we hope these examples will provide in-context insights into the obstruction to extending Kalai’s conjecture to more general surfaces.

7.
Further Directions

We re-iterate Knutson and Miller’s question in
[
32
]
and the conjecture in
[
10
]
, and ask,

Question 7.1
.

Are spherical subword complexes polytopal?

In Section
6
, we very carefully described two different 2-manifolds that are not reconstructable from their facet-ridge graph.
Using these as a starting point, most tame 2-manifolds can be shown to not be reconstructable, specifically those with a torus or projective plane in their connected sum decomposition.
However the 2-sphere is reconstructable
[
23
, Chapter 13]
.
This leads us to the thought that the 2-sphere is the only 2-manifold reconstructable from its facet-ridge graph.
The natural question extending this thought and Kalai’s conjecture is

Question 7.2
.

Is the
d
-sphere the only
d
-manifold reconstructable from its facet-ridge graph?

In light of the falsehood of the triangulation conjecture, this question is of course specialized to triangulable manifolds.
We are in fact unaware of any pair of
k
-manifolds that share their facet-ridge graph for
k>2
.
This is likely due to a lack of searching, rather than the crazy alternative, that
d
-manifolds are reconstructable from their facet-ridge graphs when
d>2
.

A very slight narrowing of Kalai’s conjecture could focus more on the special role of polytopes.

Question 7.3
.

Is there a non-polytopal simplicial sphere that shares its facet-ridge graph with a simplicial polytope?

It may be that Kalai’s conjecture is false in general, but polytopes have distinguished graphs among all spheres.

Reconstruction from the facet-ridge graph, and reconstruction in general, must be done within a particular class.
Our main result is reconstruction within the class of subword complexes. This leads to the following natural question.

Question 7.4
.

Is there a simplicial sphere, which is not isomorphic to any subword complex, but shares its facet-ridge graph with a spherical subword complex?

Kalai’s conjecture is within the class of simplicial spheres.
The previous two questions are within triangulations of a particular manifold (a sphere).
We can also take a larger class, and ask

Question 7.5
.

Do there exist two simplicial complexes with the same facet-ridge graph, but whose geometric realizations are different manifolds without boundary?

Continuing this line of thought, it could be that the graphs of spheres are distinguished among manifolds.

Question 7.6
.

Is there a non-spherical simplical manifold which shares its facet-ridge graph with a sphere?

We could continue asking questions we don’t know the answer to in this vein for a long time, but it may be best to leave it here.
