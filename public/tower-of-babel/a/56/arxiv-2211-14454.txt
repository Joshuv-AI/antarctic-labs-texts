Dual gradient method for ill-posed problems using multiple repeated measurement data
Qinian Jin, Wei Wang

Abstract
. We consider determining ℛ {\mathcal{R}} -minimizing solutions of linear ill-posed problems A ​ x = y Ax=y , where A : 𝒳 → 𝒴 A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}} is a bounded linear operator from a Banach space 𝒳 {\mathscr{X}} to a Hilbert space 𝒴 {\mathscr{Y}} and ℛ : 𝒳 → [ 0 , ∞ ] {\mathcal{R}}:{\mathscr{X}}\to[0,\infty] is a proper strongly convex penalty function. Assuming that multiple repeated independent identically distributed unbiased data of y y are available, we consider a dual gradient method to reconstruct the ℛ {\mathcal{R}} -minimizing solution using the average of these data. By terminating the method by either an a priori stopping rule or a statistical variant of the discrepancy principle, we provide the convergence analysis and derive convergence rates when the sought solution satisfies certain variational source conditions. Various numerical results are reported to test the performance of the method.



1.
Introduction

Inverse problems arise from many practical applications in science and engineering when one aims at determining unobservable causes from observed effects. Due to their significance in wide range of applications, inverse problems have received tremendous attention in the literature.

In this paper we will consider linear inverse problems of the form

\displaystyle Ax=y,

(1.1)

where
A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
is a bounded linear operator from a Banach space
{\mathscr{X}}
to a Hilbert space
{\mathscr{Y}}
. Throughout the paper we will assume that
y\in\mbox{Ran}(A)
, the range of
A
, which guarantees (
1.1
) has a solution. However, (
1.1
) may have many solutions. In order to find a solution with desired feature, by incorporating
a priori
available information we take a proper, lower semi-continuous, convex function
{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathscr{X}}\to[0,\infty]
and search for a solution
x^{\dagger}
of (
1.1
) satisfying

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})=\min\{{\mathcal{R}}(x):x\in{\mathscr{X}}\mbox{ and }Ax=y\}

(1.2)

which is called a
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
).

In practical applications, the exact data
y
is generally unknown. Very often, we only have corrupted measurement data at hand. Due to the inherent ill-posedness property of inverse problems, the
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
) may not depend continuously on the data. How to use the noisy data to reconstruct the sought solution therefore becomes a central topic in computational inverse problems.

To conquer ill-posedness, many iterative regularization methods have been developed to solve inverse problems using noisy data;
see
[
4
,
8
,
10
,
23
,
24
,
32
]
for instance. The performance of these methods depends crucially
on a proper termination of the iterations. In case an accurate upper bound of noise level is available,
various
a posteriori
rules, including the prominent discrepancy principle, have been proposed to choose the
stopping index of iteration. In case the information on noise level is difficult to infer, one has to resort to
the so-called heuristic rules (
[
12
,
20
,
21
,
25
,
26
]
) which use only the noisy data to select
a termination index. Although Bakushiski’s veto (
[
1
]
) states that heuristic rules can not lead to
convergence in the sense of worst case scenario for any regularisation method, such rules often work as well as,
or even better than, the rules using information on noise level.
The drawback of a heuristic rule is that it chooses a stopping index of iteration based on minimizing a function
over
{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,\cdots\}
for which the existence of a minimizer can not be guaranteed unless the noisy data satisfies
certain restrictive conditions.

In this paper we assume that the data can be measured multiple times repeatedly and the sought solution does not change during the measurement. This is a common practice in applications where the experiments are set up to allow acquiring multiple
observation data by repeating the experiments. Therefore, instead of the unknown exact data
y
, we are given multiple unbiased measurements
y_{1},y_{2},\cdots
of
y
which are independent identically distributed
{\mathscr{Y}}
-valued random variables. Based on these multiple measurements, for each
n\geq 1
we use

\displaystyle\bar{y}^{(n)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}

(1.3)

as an estimator of
y
. We then use
\bar{y}^{(n)}
to construct regularized solutions and consider their behavior as
n\to\infty
.

Using the average of multiple measurements to decrease the data error is a standard noise reduction technique in engineering which is called the signal averaging method (
[
14
,
29
,
35
]
). Assume
(y_{i})
are
{\mathscr{Y}}
-valued random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
with
{\mathbb{E}}
denoting the expectation. If
(y_{i})
are unbiased, independent, identically distributed estimators of
y
with a finite variance
\sigma^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}[\|y_{1}-y\|^{2}]
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle y_{i}-y,y_{j}-y\rangle\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|y_{i}-y\|^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}

which demonstrates that using
\bar{y}^{(n)}
as an estimator of
y
can reduce the variance by a factor of
n
. Therefore, it is reasonable to expect more accurate approximate solution can be constructed using the averaged data
\bar{y}^{(n)}
than using an individual data
y_{i}
.

In
[
13
]
a general class of linear spectral regularization methods have been considered for solving linear ill-posed problems in Hilbert spaces using the average of multiple measurement data. Without using any knowledge of the noise distribution, the convergence and rates of convergence of the regularized solutions, as the number of measurements increases, have been provided under either an
a priori
parameter choice rule or the discrepancy principle. The results obtained in
[
13
]
can be applied mainly for (
1.2
) with
{\mathscr{X}}
being a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
. In many applications, however, the sought solution may sit in a Banach space instead of a Hilbert space, and the sought solution may have
a priori
known special features, such as nonnegativity, sparsity and piecewise constancy.
Therefore, it is necessary to develop regularization methods using multiple repeated measurement data for finding a
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution of (
1.1
) in a general Banach space
{\mathscr{X}}
with a general convex penalty function
{\mathcal{R}}
.

In this paper we will consider a dual gradient method for solving (
1.2
) using multiple repeated measurement data.
When the exact data
y
is available, this method can be derived by applying the gradient method to the dual problem of
(
1.2
) and it takes the form (see
[
22
]
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&x_{t}=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\lambda_{t},Ax\rangle\right\},\\
&\lambda_{t+1}=\lambda_{t}-\gamma(Ax_{t}-y)\end{split}

(1.4)

with the initial guess
\lambda_{0}=0
, where
A^{*}:{\mathscr{Y}}\to{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
A
and
\gamma>0
is a step-size.
This method actually belongs to the class of Uzawa methods, see
[
2
,
33
,
34
]
.
This method also has connection to the mirror descent method,
see
[
4
,
22
,
24
,
30
]
.
In case the exact data
y
is unavailable and we are given multiple repeated random
measurement data
y_{1},y_{2},\cdots
, we may
define
\bar{y}^{(n)}
by (
1.3
) for each
n\geq 1
and replace
y
in (
1.4
) by
\bar{y}^{(n)}
to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{t}^{(n)}&=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\langle\lambda_{t}^{(n)},Ax\rangle\right\},\\
\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}&=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)})\end{split}

(1.5)

with the initial guess
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
and a step size
\gamma>0
. This is the dual
gradient method that we will consider for solving (
1.2
) using the average of
multiple random measurement data. Since
y_{1},y_{2},\cdots
are random variables, we need to
consider the convergence of (
1.5
) based on a stochastic analysis.

When
X
is a Hilbert space and
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\|x\|^{2}/2
, the method (
1.5
) becomes the Landweber iteration in Hilbert spaces using the average of multiple repeated measurement data which has been analyzed in
[
13
]
as a special case of a class of linear spectral regularization methods. The analysis in
[
13
]
is based on the spectral theory and singular value decomposition of bounded linear self-adjoint operators in Hilbert spaces. These tools however are no longer applicable to the method (
1.5
) due to the possible non-Hilbertian structure of
X
and non-quadraticity of the regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}
. In this paper we use tools of convex analysis in Banach spaces to analyze the method (
1.5
) with general Banach space
X
and general strongly convex function
{\mathcal{R}}
. Without assuming any knowledge of the noise distribution, we obtain the convergence and rates of convergence for the method (
1.5
) as
n\to\infty
when the method is terminated by either an
a priori
stopping rule or a modification of the discrepancy principle.

The paper is organized as follows, In Section
2
we give a brief review of some
basic facts from convex analysis in Banach spaces. In Section
3
we provide the convergence analysis of the dual gradient method (
1.5
) when the method is terminated by either an
a priori
stopping rule or a modification of the discrepancy principle; in particular we derive the convergence rates when the sought solution satisfies certain variational source conditions.
Finally in Section
4
, we provide various numerical results to test the performance of the proposed method.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some basic facts on convex analysis in Banach
spaces which will be used in the analysis of the dual gradient method (
1.5
); for
more details please refer to
[
36
]
.

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
be a Banach space, we use
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
to denote its dual space; in case
{\mathscr{X}}
is a Hilbert space,
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
is identified with
{\mathscr{X}}
. Given
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
and
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
we write
\langle\xi,x\rangle=\xi(x)
for the duality pairing. For a convex function
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, its effective domain is

\mbox{dom}(f):=\{x\in{\mathscr{X}}:f(x)<\infty\}.

If
\mbox{dom}(f)\neq\emptyset
,
f
is called proper.
Given
x\in\mbox{dom}(f)
, an element
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
is called a subgradient of
f
at
x
if

f(\bar{x})\geq f(x)+\langle\xi,\bar{x}-x\rangle,\quad\forall\bar{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}.

The collection of all subgradients of
f
at
x
is denoted as
\partial f(x)
and is called the
subdifferential of
f
at
x
. If
\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset
, then
f
is called subdifferentiable at
x
. By definition we have

\displaystyle x^{*}\in\arg\min_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}f(x)\Longleftrightarrow 0\in\partial f(x^{*}).

(2.1)

Note that
x\to\partial f(x)
defines a set-valued mapping
\partial f
. Let

\mbox{dom}(\partial f):=\{x\in\mbox{dom}(f):\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\}.

which is the domain of definition of
\partial f
. Given
x\in\mbox{dom}(\partial f)
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
, the Bregman distance induced by
f
at
x
in
the direction
\xi
is defined by

D_{f}^{\xi}(\bar{x},x):=f(\bar{x})-f(x)-\langle\xi,\bar{x}-x\rangle,\quad\forall\bar{x}\in{\mathscr{X}}

which is always nonnegative.

For a proper convex function
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, its convex conjugate is
defined by

f^{*}(\xi):=\sup_{x\in X}\{\langle\xi,x\rangle-f(x)\},\quad\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}

which is a convex function taking values in
(-\infty,\infty]
. By definition there holds the Fenchel-Young inequality

\displaystyle f^{*}(\xi)+f(x)\geq\langle\xi,x\rangle,\quad\forall x\in{\mathscr{X}},\ \xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}.

(2.2)

If
f:X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is proper, lower semi-continuous
and convex,
f^{*}
is also proper and

\displaystyle\xi\in\partial f(x)\Longleftrightarrow x\in\partial f^{*}(\xi)\Longleftrightarrow f(x)+f^{*}(\xi)=\langle\xi,x\rangle.

(2.3)

In applications, optimization problems of the form

\inf_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{f(x)+g(Ax)\right\}

are frequently encountered, where
A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
is a bounded linear operator between two Banach spaces
{\mathscr{X}}
and
{\mathscr{Y}}
, and
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
and
g:{\mathscr{Y}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
are proper convex functions. Let
A^{*}:{\mathscr{Y}}^{*}\to{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
denote the adjoint of
A
. By the Fenchel-Young inequality we have

f(x)\geq\langle\xi,x\rangle-f^{*}(\xi),\qquad g(Ax)\geq\langle-\eta,Ax\rangle-g^{*}(-\eta)

and therefore

f(x)+g(Ax)\geq\langle\xi-A^{*}\eta,x\rangle-f^{*}(\xi)-g^{*}(-\eta)

for all
x\in{\mathscr{X}}
,
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
and
\eta\in{\mathscr{Y}}^{*}
. Thus, taking
\xi=A^{*}\eta
gives

\inf_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{f(x)+g(Ax)\right\}\geq\sup_{\eta\in{\mathscr{Y}}^{*}}\left\{-f^{*}(A^{*}\eta)-g^{*}(-\eta)\right\}.

It is natural to ask under what conditions on
f
and
g
we have equality in the above equation. This is answered by the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality formula given below (see
[
36
, Corollary 2.8.5]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
and
{\mathscr{Y}}
be Banach spaces, let
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
and
g:{\mathscr{Y}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be proper, convex functions, and let
A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
be a bounded linear operator. If there is

x_{0}\in\emph{dom}(f)
such that
Ax_{0}\in\emph{dom}(g)
and
g
is continuous at
Ax_{0}
, then

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\{f(x)+g(Ax)\}=\sup_{\eta\in{\mathscr{Y}}^{*}}\{-f^{*}(A^{*}\eta)-g^{*}(-\eta)\}.

(2.4)

A proper function
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is called
c_{0}
-strongly convex for some constant
c_{0}>0
if

\displaystyle f(s\bar{x}+(1-s)x)+c_{0}s(1-s)\|\bar{x}-x\|^{2}\leq sf(\bar{x})+(1-s)f(x)

(2.5)

for all
\bar{x},x\in\mbox{dom}(f)
and
s\in[0,1]
. It is easy to show that for
a proper
c_{0}
-strongly convex function
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
there holds

\displaystyle D_{f}^{\xi}(\bar{x},x)\geq c_{0}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}

(2.6)

for all
\bar{x}\in\mbox{dom}(f)
,
x\in\mbox{dom}(\partial f)
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
.
Furthermore,
[
36
, Corollary 3.5.11]
contains the following important result
concerning the differentiability of
f^{*}
for strongly convex
f
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
{\mathscr{X}}
be a Banach space and let
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
be a proper,
lower semi-continuous,
c_{0}
-strongly convex function for some constant
c_{0}>0
.
Then
\emph{dom}(f^{*})={\mathscr{X}}^{*}
,
f^{*}
is Fréchet differentiable and its gradient

\nabla f^{*}
maps
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
into
{\mathscr{X}}
with

\|\nabla f^{*}(\xi)-\nabla f^{*}(\eta)\|\leq\frac{\|\xi-\eta\|}{2c_{0}}

for all
\xi,\eta\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
.

It should be emphasized that
{\mathscr{X}}
in Proposition 2.2 can be an arbitrary Banach
space; it can even be a general normed vector spaces. The gradient
\nabla f^{*}
of
f^{*}
is in general a mapping from
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}\to{\mathscr{X}}^{**}
, the second dual space of
{\mathscr{X}}
. Proposition 2.2 actually concludes that, for each
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
,

\nabla f^{*}(\xi)
is an element in
{\mathscr{X}}
, and thus
\nabla f^{*}
is a mapping from
{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
to
{\mathscr{X}}
.

Given a proper strongly convex function
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
, we may consider for each
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
the convex minimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{f(x)-\langle\xi,x\rangle\right\}

(2.7)

which is involved in the dual gradient method (
1.5
). According to
[
36
, Theorem 3.5.8]
, (
2.1
), (
2.3
) and Proposition
2.2
we have

Proposition 2.3
.

If
f:{\mathscr{X}}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a proper, lower semi-continuous, strongly convex function, then for any
\xi\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
the minimization problem (
2.7
) has a unique minimizer given by
\nabla f^{*}(\xi)
.

3.
Convergence analysis

In this section we will provide convergence analysis of the method (
1.5
) when it is terminated by either an
a priori
stopping rule or a statistical variant of the discrepancy principle. We will make use of the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

(i)

{\mathscr{X}}
is a Banach space,
{\mathscr{Y}}
is a Hilbert space, and
A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
is a bounded linear operator.

(ii)

{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathscr{X}}\to[0,\infty]
is proper, lower semi-continuous and
c_{0}
-strongly convex for some
constant
c_{0}>0
.

(iii)

The equation
Ax=y
has a solution in
\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})
.

(iv)

y_{1},y_{2},\cdots
is a sequence of independent identically distributed
{\mathscr{Y}}
-valued random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}})
with
{\mathbb{E}}[y_{1}]=y
and
0<\sigma^{2}:={\mathbb{E}}[\|y_{1}-y\|^{2}]<\infty
.

For a sequence of noisy data
\{y_{i}\}
satisfying (iv) in Assumption
3.1
, we define
\bar{y}^{(n)}
by (
1.3
) for each
n\geq 1
. There holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

(3.1)

According to (i)-(iii) in Assumption
3.1
and Proposition
2.3
, the equation (
1.1
) has a unique
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution, denoted as
x^{\dagger}
, and the method (
1.5
) is well-defined with

\displaystyle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad x_{t}^{(n)}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})

(3.2)

for each integer
t\geq 0
.

Our convergence analysis of the method (
1.5
), in particular the derivation of convergence rates, is based on the following result which has been established in
[
22
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and let
d_{\bar{y}^{(n)}}(\lambda):={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-\langle\lambda,\bar{y}^{(n)}\rangle
. Let
L:=\|A\|^{2}/(2c_{0})
. Consider the dual gradient method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. If
0<\gamma<1/L
then for any
\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}
there holds

\displaystyle d_{\bar{y}^{(n)}}(\lambda)-d_{\bar{y}^{(n)}}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\gamma(t+1)}\left(\|\lambda-\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}-\|\lambda\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\left\{\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{L\gamma}{4}\right)t+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{L\gamma}{2}\right)\right\}\gamma\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}

for all integers
t\geq 0
.

Based on Proposition
3.1
, we can obtain the following estimates which will be used frequently in the forthcoming analysis.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Let
L:=\|A\|^{2}/(2c_{0})
and assume
0<\gamma<1/L
. Then

c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq\eta_{t}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

for all
t\geq 0
, where
c:=1/2-L\gamma/2>0
and

\eta_{t}:=\sup_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\frac{1}{3}\gamma(t+1)\|Ax-y\|^{2}\right\}.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.1
it follows for all
\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}
that

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{\bar{y}^{(n)}}(\lambda)-d_{\bar{y}^{(n)}}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})-\frac{1}{2\gamma(t+1)}\left(\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|^{2}-\|\lambda\|^{2}\right).

Let
d_{y}(\lambda):={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-\langle\lambda,y\rangle
. We then have

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})+\langle\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda,\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\,-\frac{1}{2\gamma(t+1)}\left(\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|^{2}-\|\lambda\|^{2}\right).

(3.3)

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\langle\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda,\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\leq\frac{1}{4\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|^{2}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}.

Combining this with (
3
) gives

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})+\frac{1}{2\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda\|^{2}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

which together with the inequality
\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq 2(\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda\|^{2}+\|\lambda\|^{2})
then shows that

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma(t+1)}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}.

(3.4)

By using the fact
y=Ax^{\dagger}
and the Fenchel-Young inequality (
2.2
), we have

d_{y}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})-\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}\rangle\geq-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger}).

Therefore

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-\langle\lambda,y\rangle+{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma(t+1)}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

for all
\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}
and hence

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-\langle\lambda,y\rangle+{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma(t+1)}\right\}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}}\left\{-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)+\langle\lambda,y\rangle-\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma(t+1)}\right\}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

By the Fenchel-Rockafellar duality formula given in Proposition
2.1
we finally obtain

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1)\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\inf_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)+\frac{1}{3}\gamma(t+1)\|Ax-y\|^{2}\right\}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}+\gamma(t+1)\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}.

The proof is therefore complete.
∎

3.1.
Convergence analysis under a priori stopping rule

In this subsection we consider the dual gradient method (
1.5
) and show that
x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}
converges to the unique
{\mathcal{R}}
-minimizing solution
x^{\dagger}
of (
1.1
) if
t_{n}
is chosen such that
t_{n}\to\infty
and
t_{n}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Furthermore we derive the convergence rates under suitable
a priori
choice of
t_{n}
when
x^{\dagger}
satisfies certain variational source conditions.

By using
[
24
, Lemma 3.7]
we immediately obtain the following convergence result for the sequence
\{x_{t},\lambda_{t}\}
defined by the method (
1.4
) with exact data.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let (i)-(iii) in Assumption
3.1
hold. Consider the method (
1.4
) with exact data. If
\lambda_{0}=0
and
0<\gamma<4c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
, then
D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t})\to 0
as
t\to\infty
.

Although
\{x_{t},\lambda_{t}\}
are deterministic,
\{x_{t}^{(n)},\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\}
are random variables. Therefore, we need
to consider
\{x_{t}^{(m)},\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\}
by a stochastic analysis.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold. Let
\{x_{t},\lambda_{t}\}
and
\{x_{t}^{(n)},\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\}
be defined by (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) respectively with the initial guess
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Then for all integers
t\geq 0
there hold

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t}^{(n)}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]\to 0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t}\|^{2}\right]\to 0

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

We will use an induction argument. Since
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=\lambda_{0}=0
, we have

x_{0}^{(n)}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{0}^{(n)})=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{0})=x_{0}
. Thus the result is true for
t=0
. Now we assume that the result is true for some
t\geq 0
.
Then, by the definition of
\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}
and
\lambda_{t+1}
, we have

\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t+1}\|^{2}=\|(\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t})-\gamma A(x_{t}^{(n)}-x_{t})+\gamma(\bar{y}^{(n)}-y)\|^{2}.

By using the inequality
(a+b+c)^{2}\leq 3(a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2})
, taking the expectation, and using (
3.1
), we can obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t+1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t}\|^{2}\right]+3\gamma^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|A(x_{t}^{(n)}-x_{t})\|^{2}\right]+3\gamma^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t}\|^{2}\right]+3\gamma^{2}\|A\|^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t}^{(n)}-x_{t}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{3\gamma^{2}\sigma^{2}}{n}.

Thus, by the induction hypothesis we can conclude that
{\mathbb{E}}[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t+1}\|^{2}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Note that

x_{t+1}^{(n)}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad x_{t+1}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}).

By Proposition
2.2
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t+1}^{(n)}-x_{t+1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})-\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4c_{0}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|A^{*}(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t+1})\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|A\|^{2}}{4c_{0}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t+1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold. Consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
and assume that
0<\gamma<4c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
. Let
\Delta_{t}^{(n)}:=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t}^{(n)})
. Then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]\leq C_{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}

for all integers
t\geq 0
, where
C_{1}:=c_{0}\gamma/(4c_{0}-\gamma\|A\|^{2})
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}-\Delta_{t}^{(n)}=\left(\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)},x_{t+1}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle-{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t+1}^{(n)})\right)+\left({\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})+\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right).

By using the fact
A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})
we have from (
2.3
) that

{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})+{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})=\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle

for all
t\geq 0
. Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}-\Delta_{t}^{(n)}=\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})-\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}\rangle\right)-\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})-\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}\rangle\right).

Since
x_{t}^{(n)}=\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})
, by using Proposition
2.2
we can obtain

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}-\Delta_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\left({\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)})-{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})-\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},\nabla{\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x_{t}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4c_{0}}\|A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x_{t}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle.

According to the definition of
\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}
it is easy to see that

A^{*}\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}=-\gamma A^{*}(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}-\Delta_{t}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4c_{0}}\|A^{*}(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)})\|^{2}-\gamma\langle Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)},Ax_{t}^{(n)}-y\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\left(1-\frac{\gamma\|A\|^{2}}{4c_{0}}\right)\gamma\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\gamma\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|.

By using the Young’s inequality we have

\displaystyle\gamma\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|\leq\left(1-\frac{\gamma\|A\|^{2}}{4c_{0}}\right)\gamma\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+C_{1}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}.

with
C_{1}
defined above. Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}-\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\leq C_{1}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}.

By taking the expectation and using (
3.1
) we then obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t+1}^{(n)}\right]-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]\leq C_{1}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=C_{1}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}

which shows the desired inequality.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold. Consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
and assume that
0<\gamma<4c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
. If
t_{n}
is chosen such that
t_{n}\to\infty
and
t_{n}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]\to 0

and hence
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\right]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

By the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
it suffices to show
{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta_{t_{n}}^{(n)}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Let
t\geq 0
be any fixed integer. Since
t_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, we have
t_{n}>t
for large
n
. Thus, we may repeatedly use Lemma
3.5
to obtain

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]+C_{1}(t_{n}-t)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

Since
t_{n}/n\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we thus have

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\right]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]

(3.5)

for any
t\geq 0
. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]-D_{{\mathcal{R}}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}\rangle-{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad\,+\left({\mathcal{R}}(x_{t})-{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})\right]\right).

(3.6)

With the help of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right]-\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle A^{*}(\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t}),x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right]\right|+\left|{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t},x_{t}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|A\|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\lambda_{t}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+\|A^{*}\lambda_{t}\|\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t}-x_{t}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}.

Thus we may use Lemma
3.4
to conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}^{(n)}\rangle\right]=\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t},x^{\dagger}-x_{t}\rangle.

(3.7)

Next we will show that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})\right].

(3.8)

To see this, we take a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}
with
n_{k}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty

such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n_{k})})\right]=\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})\right].

According to Lemma
3.4
we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\|x_{t}^{(n_{k})}-x_{t}\|^{2}]\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. By taking a subsequence of
\{n_{k}\}
if necessary, we can guarantee
\|x_{t}^{(n_{k})}-x_{t}\|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
almost surely. Thus, from the lower semi-continuity of
{\mathcal{R}}
and Fatou’s lemma it follows

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t})]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\liminf_{k\to\infty}{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n_{k})})\right]\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n_{k})})\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n_{k})})\right]=\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t}^{(n)})\right]

which shows (
3.8
). Consequently it follows from (
3.1
), (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t}^{(n)}\right]\leq D_{{\mathcal{R}}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t}).

Therefore we may use (
3.5
) to obtain

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Delta_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\right]\leq D_{{\mathcal{R}}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t})

for all
t\geq 0
. Letting
t\to\infty
and using Lemma
3.3
we thus obtain
{\mathbb{E}}[\Delta_{t_{n}}^{(n)}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

We next consider deriving convergence rates of the method (
1.5
) under an
a priori
stopping rule when the sought solution
x^{\dagger}
satisfies the variational source conditions specified in the following assumption.

Assumption 3.2
.

For the unique solution
x^{\dagger}
of (
1.1
) there is an error measure function
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}:\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})\to[0,\infty)
with
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x^{\dagger})=0
such that

{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x)\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+M\|Ax-y\|^{q},\quad\forall x\in\emph{dom}({\mathcal{R}})

for some
0<q\leq 1
and some constant
M>0
.

Remark 3.1
.

Variational source conditions were first introduced in
[
15
]
, as a generalization
of the spectral source conditions in Hilbert spaces, to derive convergence rates of Tikhonov regularization in Banach spaces. This kind of source conditions was further generalized and refined subsequently, see
[
9
,
16
,
17
,
19
]
for instance. The error
measure function
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}
in Assumption
3.2
is used to measure the speed of convergence;
the usual choice of
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}
is the Bregman distance induced by
{\mathcal{R}}
.

Remark 3.2
.

When
x^{\dagger}
satisfies the benchmark source condition
A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})
for some
\lambda^{\dagger}\in{\mathscr{Y}}
, it is straightforward to see that

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\|\lambda^{\dagger}\|\|Ax-y\|,\quad\forall x\in\mbox{dom}({\mathcal{R}})

which shows the variational source condition is satisfied with
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x)=D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})
,
M=\|\lambda^{\dagger}\|
and
q=1
; this is a well-known fact, see
[
15
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

When both
X
and
Y
are Hilbert spaces,
{\mathcal{R}}
is
c_{0}
-strongly convex for some constant
c_{0}>0
, and
x^{\dagger}
satisfies the source condition

\displaystyle\xi^{\dagger}:=(A^{*}A)^{\nu/2}\omega\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})

(3.9)

for some
0<\nu\leq 1
and
\omega\in X
, then the variational source condition in Assumption
3.2
holds with

{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x)=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger}),\quad M=C_{\nu}\|\omega\|^{\frac{2}{1+\nu}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad q=\frac{2\nu}{1+\nu},

where
C_{\nu}:=\frac{1+\nu}{2}\left(\frac{1-\nu}{c_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1-\nu}{1+\nu}}
.
Indeed, by the given condition (
3.9
), we can obtain

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})-\langle\omega,(A^{*}A)^{\nu/2}(x-x^{\dagger})\rangle

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\|\omega\|\|(A^{*}A)^{\nu/2}(x-x^{\dagger})\|.

By the interpolation inequality (
[
8
]
) we then have

D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\|\omega\|\|x-x^{\dagger}\|^{1-\nu}\|A(x-x^{\dagger})\|^{\nu}.

Thus, an application of the Young’s inequality gives

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\|x-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+C_{\nu}\|\omega\|^{\frac{2}{1+\nu}}\|Ax-y\|^{\frac{2\nu}{1+\nu}},

Finally, by invoking the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
and (
2.6
) we can obtain

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x,x^{\dagger})+C_{\nu}\|\omega\|^{\frac{2}{1+\nu}}\|Ax-y\|^{\frac{2\nu}{1+\nu}}

which implies the assertion.

It should be pointed out that the source condition (
3.9
) has been used in
[
11
]
to derive convergence rates of Tikhonov regularization in Hilbert spaces with non-quadratic penalty terms. We would also like to mention that for the special case
{\mathcal{R}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathscr{C}}(x)
, where
\iota_{\mathscr{C}}
denotes the indicator function of a closed convex set
{\mathscr{C}}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
, i.e.
\iota_{\mathscr{C}}(x)=0
if
x\in{\mathscr{C}}
and
\infty
otherwise, the source condition (
3.9
) becomes the projected spectral source condition

x^{\dagger}=P_{\mathscr{C}}((A^{*}A)^{\nu/2}\omega)

for which it has been shown in
[
22
]
that the projected source condition implies the variational source condition. Here
P_{\mathscr{C}}
denotes the metric projection of
{\mathscr{X}}
onto
{\mathscr{C}}
.

Remark 3.4
.

The variational source conditions have been verified for various concrete inverse problems, see
[
6
,
7
,
17
,
18
]
for instance.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Assume that
0<\gamma<2c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
and that
x^{\dagger}
satisfies Assumption
3.2
. If
t_{n}
is chosen such that
t_{n}\sim(n/\sigma^{2})^{\frac{2-q}{2}}
, then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]=O\left(\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)^{q/2}\right).

Proof.

From Lemma
3.2
and (
3.1
) it follows that

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1){\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{t}+\gamma(t+1){\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=\eta_{t}+\gamma(t+1)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

By using the variational source condition and the nonnegativity of
{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}
, we have

0\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x)-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+M\|Ax-y\|^{q},\quad\forall x\in{\mathscr{X}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle\eta_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in{\mathscr{X}}}\left\{M\|Ax-y\|^{q}-\frac{1}{3}\gamma(t+1)\|Ax-y\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{s\geq 0}\left\{Ms^{q}-\frac{1}{3}\gamma(t+1)s^{2}\right\}=c_{2}(t+1)^{-\frac{q}{2-q}},

(3.10)

where
c_{2}:=(1-\frac{q}{2})\left(\frac{3qM}{2\gamma}\right)^{\frac{q}{2-q}}M
. Consequently

\displaystyle c\gamma(t+1){\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{8\gamma(t+1)}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}(t+1)^{-\frac{q}{2-q}}+\gamma(t+1)\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

which shows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\left((t+1)^{-\frac{2}{2-q}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\left((t+1)^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}+(t+1)^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right),

where here and below we use
C
to denote a generic constant independent of
t
,
n
and
\sigma
.
Since
t_{n}
is chosen such that
t_{n}\sim(n/\sigma^{2})^{\frac{2-q}{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\leq C\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{1-q}.

The first estimate and (
3.1
) in particular imply

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]\leq 2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]+2{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]\leq C\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}.

Now we are ready to complete the proof. By using the variational source condition on
x^{\dagger}
, the convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, and the fact
A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})+M\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle+M\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}

\displaystyle=\langle\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)},Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\rangle+M\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|+M\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}.

(3.11)

Therefore

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|\right]+M{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad\,+M\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]\right)^{q/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)^{q/2}.

The proof is thus complete.
∎

3.2.
Convergence analysis under a posteriori stopping rule

We next consider the dual gradient method (
1.5
) terminated by an
a posteriori
stopping rule. The discrepancy principle is one of the most prominent rule that has been studied extensively. In this stopping rule, one needs the information on the noise level
\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|
. Since
y
is unknown, we can not use this quantity directly. Recall that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=\sigma^{2}/n.

To get an estimate on
\sigma^{2}
, we consider the square root of the sample variance

s_{n}:=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|y_{i}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}}

for which it is known that
{\mathbb{E}}[s_{n}^{2}]=\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, we may use
s_{n}/\sqrt{n}
as an estimator of
\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|
. This leads us to propose the following stopping rule which is
a statistical variant of the discrepancy principle.

Rule 3.8
.

Let
\beta_{0}>0
be a given number. We define
t_{n}
to be the first integer such that
t_{n}\leq\beta_{0}n
and

\displaystyle\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|\leq\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}},

(3.12)

where
\tau_{n}>1
is a number that may depend on
n
; if there is no such an integer
t_{n}
satisfying (
3.12
), we take
t_{n}=\beta_{0}n
.

Recall that we have used
s_{n}/\sqrt{n}
as an estimator of
\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|
. In case
s_{n}/\sqrt{n}
is an underestimated estimator, (
3.12
) may not be satisfied at a right number of iterations; continuing the iterations until (
3.12
) holds may result in a bad reconstruction result. Therefore, the requirement
t_{n}\leq\beta_{0}n
serves as an emergency stop.
It should be emphasized that
t_{n}
is a random variable instead of a deterministic quantity;
this is a key difference between Rule
3.8
and the classical discrepancy principle.

According to the spirit of the discrepancy principle, it is suggestive to take
\tau_{n}>1
in Rule
3.8
to be a number close to
1
.
Due to the technical reasons, however, in the forthcoming convergence analysis, the number
\tau_{n}
in Rule
3.8
is required to tend to
\infty
as
n\to\infty
but does not go to
\infty
too fast; specifically we require

\displaystyle\tau_{n}\to\infty\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\tau_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

(3.13)

For instance, we may take

\displaystyle\tau_{n}:=\max\{\tau_{0},\log|\log|\log n||\},

(3.14)

where
\tau_{0}>1
is a fixed number.
This requirement on
\tau_{n}
is imposed to guarantee the existence of an event
\Omega_{n}
with
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
on which we can perform the convergence analysis. Note that

s_{n}^{2}=\frac{n}{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|y_{i}\|^{2}-\|\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\right).

Since
y_{1},y_{2},\cdots
is a sequence of independent identically distributed random variables, by the strong law of large numbers (
[
28
, Corollary 7.10]
) we have

\|\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\to\|y\|^{2}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|y_{i}\|^{2}\to{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|y_{1}\|^{2}\right]

as
n\to\infty
almost surely. Therefore

s_{n}^{2}\to{\mathbb{E}}[\|y_{1}\|^{2}]-\|y\|^{2}=\sigma^{2}

and hence
s_{n}\to\sigma
as
n\to\infty
almost surely. Since almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|s_{n}-\sigma|\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)\to 1\quad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

(3.15)

Now we define the event

\Omega_{n}:=\left\{|s_{n}-\sigma|\leq\frac{\sigma}{2},\,\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\leq\tau_{n}\sqrt{\frac{cs_{n}^{2}}{2n}}\right\},

where
c>0
is the constant appearing in Lemma
3.2
. We claim that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n})\to 1\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

(3.16)

Indeed, by the Markov’s inequality, (
3.1
), and the assumption
\tau_{n}\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(|s_{n}-\sigma|\leq\frac{\sigma}{2},\,\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|>\tau_{n}\sqrt{\frac{cs_{n}^{2}}{2n}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|>\sigma\tau_{n}\sqrt{\frac{c}{8n}}\right)\leq\frac{8n}{c\sigma^{2}\tau_{n}^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]=\frac{8}{c\tau_{n}^{2}}\to 0

as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, by using (
3.15
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n})={\mathbb{P}}\left(|s_{n}-\sigma|\leq\frac{\sigma}{2}\right)-{\mathbb{P}}\left(|s_{n}-\sigma|\leq\frac{\sigma}{2},\,\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|>\tau_{n}\sqrt{\frac{cs_{n}^{2}}{2n}}\right)\to 1

as
n\to\infty
.

The following result gives an upper bound estimate of
t_{n}
on
\Omega_{n}
defined by Rule
3.8
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Assume that
0<\gamma<2c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
. Let
t_{n}
be defined by Rule
3.8
with
\tau_{n}
satisfying (
3.13
) and set
\phi(n):=\frac{\sigma\tau_{n}}{2\sqrt{n}}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is an integer
n_{\varepsilon}
such that

t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}\leq\varepsilon\quad\mbox{ on }\Omega_{n}

for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Note that on
\Omega_{n}
we always have
\sigma/2\leq s_{n}\leq 3\sigma/2
. If
t_{n}\leq 1/\phi(n)
, then
t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}\leq\phi(n)
. In the following we will assume
t_{n}>1/\phi(n)
. By the definition of
t_{n}
we have

\|Ax_{t_{n}-1}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|>\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Therefore, by using (
3
) we have on
\Omega_{n}
that

\displaystyle c\gamma t_{n}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{n}+\frac{1}{8\gamma t_{n}}\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\gamma t_{n}\|Ax_{t_{n}-1}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma t_{n}}\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma t_{n}}+\gamma t_{n}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma t_{n}}+c\gamma t_{n}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{2n}

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{c}{2}\gamma t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma t_{n}}\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c\gamma t_{n}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{2n}+\frac{1}{8\gamma t_{n}}\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq d_{y}(\lambda)-d_{y}(\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+\frac{3\|\lambda\|^{2}}{4\gamma t_{n}}

(3.17)

for all
\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}
. Note that

d_{y}(\lambda)={\mathcal{R}}^{*}(A^{*}\lambda)-\langle A^{*}\lambda,x^{\dagger}\rangle\geq-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})

which shows that

\inf_{\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}}d_{y}(\lambda)\geq-{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})>-\infty.

Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{Y}}
such that

d_{y}(\lambda_{\varepsilon})\leq\inf_{\lambda\in{\mathscr{Y}}}d_{y}(\lambda)+\frac{c\gamma}{4}\varepsilon\leq d_{y}(\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+\frac{c\gamma}{4}\varepsilon.

This and (
3.2
) with
\lambda=\lambda_{\varepsilon}
show that

\displaystyle\frac{c}{2}\gamma t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}+\frac{1}{8\gamma t_{n}}\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq\frac{c\gamma}{4}\varepsilon+\frac{3\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{4\gamma t_{n}}

(3.18)

which together with
t_{n}>1/\phi(n)
in particular implies

\displaystyle t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{3\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{2c\gamma^{2}t_{n}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{3\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{2c\gamma^{2}}\phi(n).

Therefore, we always have

t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}\leq\max\left\{\phi(n),\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{3\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{2c\gamma^{2}}\phi(n)\right\}\quad\mbox{ on }\Omega_{n}.

Since
\phi(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we can find
n_{\varepsilon}
such that
\phi(n)\leq\varepsilon
and
\frac{3\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{2c\gamma^{2}}\phi(n)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
. Thus
t_{n}\phi(n)^{2}\leq\varepsilon
on
\Omega_{n}
for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Based on Lemma
3.9
, we are now ready to show a convergence result for
x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}
with
t_{n}
determined by Rule
3.8
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Assume that
0<\gamma<2c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
. Let
t_{n}
be determined by Rule
3.8
with
\tau_{n}
satisfying (
3.13
). Assume that there exists
\xi^{\dagger}\in\partial{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})
such that
\xi^{\dagger}\in\overline{A^{*}({\mathscr{Y}})}
. Then for any
0<\alpha<1
there holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{\alpha}\right]\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty

(3.19)

and consequently for any
0<p<2
there holds

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|^{p}\right]\to 0\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

(3.20)

Proof.

First we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+D_{\mathcal{R}}^{\xi^{\dagger}}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)},x^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\xi^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle.

(3.21)

Since
\xi^{\dagger}\in\overline{A^{*}({\mathscr{Y}})}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find
\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{Y}}
and
v_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{X}}^{*}
such that

\xi^{\dagger}=A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}+v_{\varepsilon}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\|v_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}c_{0}\varepsilon.

Therefore

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq\langle A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-A^{*}\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}-v_{\varepsilon},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\langle\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon},Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\rangle+\|v_{\varepsilon}\|\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|.

By using the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
and the estimate on
\|v_{\varepsilon}\|
we have

\displaystyle\|v_{\varepsilon}\|\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|\leq\frac{1}{2}c_{0}\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{\|v_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}{2c_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}.

Consequently

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq 2\langle\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon},Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\rangle+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|+\|\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}\|\right)\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(3.22)

We now show that there exists
n_{\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq\varepsilon\quad\mbox{ on }\Omega_{n}

(3.23)

for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
.
According to Lemma
3.9
and (
3.13
) we have
t_{n}<1/\phi(n)^{2}\leq\beta_{0}n
on
\Omega_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. Therefore

\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|\leq\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}

and consequently

\displaystyle\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|+\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\leq\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}+\tau_{n}\sqrt{\frac{cs_{n}^{2}}{2n}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\sigma\tau_{n}}{2\sqrt{n}}+\frac{3\sigma\tau_{n}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c}{2n}}=3\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{c}{2}}\right)\phi(n)

\displaystyle<\frac{9}{2}\phi(n).

(3.24)

By using (
3.18
) we have

\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq 2c\gamma^{2}\varepsilon t_{n}+6\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2},

where
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{Y}}
is an element chosen in the proof of Lemma
3.9
. Therefore, it follows from (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq 9\left(\sqrt{2c\gamma^{2}\varepsilon t_{n}+6\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}+\|\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}\|\right)\phi(n)+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

(3.25)

on
\Omega_{n}
. By using Lemma
3.9
and the property
\phi(n)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we may find a sufficiently large
n_{\varepsilon}
such that

\left(\sqrt{2c\gamma^{2}\varepsilon t_{n}+6\|\lambda_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}}+\|\lambda^{\dagger}_{\varepsilon}\|\right)\phi(n)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{12}

for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
. Combining this with (
3.25
) we thus obtain (
3.23
).

Next we will show (
3.19
). Let
\chi_{\Omega_{n}}
denote the characteristic function of
\Omega_{n}
, i.e.
\chi_{\Omega_{n}}(\omega)=1
if
\omega\in\Omega_{n}

and
\chi_{\Omega_{n}}(\omega)=0
otherwise. By using (
3.23
) and the Hölder inequality we have for sufficiently large
n
that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{\alpha}\chi_{\Omega_{n}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{\alpha}\chi_{\Omega_{n}^{c}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}+\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]\right)^{\alpha}{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n}^{c})^{1-\alpha}.

(3.26)

If we are able to show

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]\leq C

(3.27)

for some constant
C
independent of
n
, then, by using the fact that
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n}^{c})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, we can conclude

\limsup_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{\alpha}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{\alpha}

and thus obtain (
3.19
), due to the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
.

It remains only to show (
3.27
). From (
3.2
), the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the strong convexity of
{\mathcal{R}}
it follows that

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq\langle\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)},Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\rangle+\|\xi^{\dagger}\|\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|+\frac{1}{2c_{0}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

which implies

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|+\frac{1}{c_{0}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}

We next use Lemma
3.2
. By the definition of
\eta_{t}
and the nonnegativity of
{\mathcal{R}}
, we have
0\leq\eta_{t}\leq{\mathcal{R}}(x^{\dagger})<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
. Thus, it follows from Lemma
3.2
that

\displaystyle\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{1}{t_{n}}+\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right),\quad\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq C\left(t_{n}+t_{n}^{2}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right).

Therefore, by using
t_{n}\leq\beta_{0}n
, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sqrt{t_{n}}+t_{n}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t_{n}}}+\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|\right)+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\sqrt{t_{n}}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|+t_{n}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\sqrt{n}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|+n\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}.

(3.28)

By taking the expectation and using (
3.1
), we can obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\sqrt{n}({\mathbb{E}}[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}])^{1/2}+n{\mathbb{E}}[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}]\right)+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\sigma+\sigma^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}

which shows (
3.27
). The proof is therefore complete.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In Theorem
3.10
we have obtained the convergence result (
3.19
) with
0<\alpha<1
. If in addition

\sigma_{4}:={\mathbb{E}}[\|y_{1}-y\|^{4}]<\infty,

we can improve the convergence result (
3.19
) to include
\alpha=1
and hence
{\mathbb{E}}[\|x_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. To see this, we may use the similar argument for deriving (
3.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right]\leq\varepsilon+\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]\right)^{1/2}{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n}^{c})^{1/2}.

Therefore, it suffices to show

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]\leq C

(3.29)

for some constant
C
independent of
n
. By virtue of (
3.2
) it is easy to derive

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(D_{\mathcal{R}}^{A^{*}\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}}(x^{\dagger},x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+n{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\right]+n^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{4}\right]\right)+\frac{2}{c_{1}}\|\xi^{\dagger}\|^{2}.

(3.30)

With the similar argument in the proof of
[
13
, Corollary 3]
, we can derive

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{4}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{3}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|y_{1}-y\|^{4}\right]+\frac{3(n-1)}{n^{3}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\left[\|y_{1}-y\|^{2}\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{4}}{n^{3}}+\frac{3(n-1)}{n^{3}}\sigma^{4}.

By invoking this estimate and (
3.1
), we can obtain (
3.29
) from (
3.5
) immediately.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let Assumption
3.1
hold and consider the method (
1.5
) with
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. Assume that
0<\gamma<2c_{0}/\|A\|^{2}
. Let
t_{n}
be determined by Rule
3.8
with
\tau_{n}
satisfying (
3.13
). If
x^{\dagger}
satisfies the variational source condition given in Assumption
3.2
, then there is a constant
C
such that

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq C\phi(n)^{q}\right)\to 1

as
n\to\infty
, where
\phi(n):=\frac{\sigma\tau_{n}}{2\sqrt{n}}
is defined in Lemma
3.9
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
, it suffices to establish

{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq C\phi(n)^{q}\quad\mbox{ on }\Omega_{n}

for some constant
C
independent of
n
. Under the given variational source condition on
x^{\dagger}
, we have the estimate (
3.1
) on
\eta_{t}
. Combining this with Lemma
3.2
shows that

\displaystyle c\gamma\|Ax_{t_{n}-1}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq c_{2}t_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2-q}}+\gamma\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\leq c_{2}t_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2-q}}+c\gamma\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{2n}

(3.31)

and

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq 8\gamma t_{n}^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}+8\gamma^{2}t_{n}^{2}\|\bar{y}^{(n)}-y\|^{2}\leq 8\gamma t_{n}^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}+4\gamma^{2}t_{n}^{2}\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{n}

(3.32)

on
\Omega_{n}
. Since
\|Ax_{t_{n}-1}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|>\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}
, we have from (
3.31
) that

c\gamma\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{n}\leq c_{2}t_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2-q}}+c\gamma\frac{\tau_{n}^{2}s_{n}^{2}}{2n}

which together with
s_{n}\geq\sigma/2
on
\Omega_{n}
implies

\frac{1}{2}c\gamma\phi(n)^{2}\leq c_{2}t_{n}^{-\frac{2}{2-q}}.

Therefore

\displaystyle t_{n}\leq c_{3}\phi(n)^{q-2}

(3.33)

where
c_{3}>0
is a constant independent of
n
. This in particular shows that
t_{n}<\beta_{0}n
and hence
\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}\|\leq\tau_{n}\frac{s_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}
on
\Omega_{n}
for sufficiently large
n
. Consequently

\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|\leq\frac{9}{2}\phi(n)

as argued in (
3.2
). By using (
3.32
), (
3.33
) and
s_{n}\leq 3\sigma/2
we also have

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|^{2}\leq 8\gamma t_{n}^{\frac{2(1-q)}{2-q}}+4c\gamma^{2}t_{n}^{2}\phi(n)^{2}\leq c_{4}\phi(n)^{2(q-1)}

for some constant
c_{4}
independent of
n
. Finally we may use (
3.1
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}^{\dagger}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})

\displaystyle\leq\|\lambda_{t_{n}}^{(n)}\|\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|+C\|Ax_{t_{n}}^{(n)}-y\|^{q}\leq c_{5}\phi(n)^{q}

for some constant
c_{5}
independent of
n
. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

According to the definition of
\phi(n)
, Theorem
3.11
gives the convergence rate

{\mathbb{P}}\left({\mathcal{E}}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq C\tau_{n}^{q}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\right)^{q/2}\right)\to 1\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty.

Because
\tau_{n}\to\infty
, this rate is worse than the one derived in Theorem
3.7
under an
a priori
stopping rule. The requirement
\tau_{n}\to\infty
is used to construct
\Omega_{n}
with
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega_{n})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. If such an even
\Omega_{n}
could be constructed without using the requirement on
\tau_{n}
, the convergence rate in Theorem
3.11
could be upgraded to
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{E}}(x_{t_{n}}^{(n)})\leq C(\sigma^{2}/n)^{q/2})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. This question however remains open.

4.
Numerical results

In this section we will report various numerical results to test the performance of the method (
1.5
).

Example 4.1
.

We first consider the application of the method (
1.5
) to solve linear ill-posed
problems in Hilbert spaces with convex constraint. Let
A:{\mathscr{X}}\to{\mathscr{Y}}
be a bounded linear operator between two Hilbert spaces
{\mathscr{X}}
and
{\mathscr{Y}}
and let
{\mathscr{C}}\subset{\mathscr{X}}
be a closed convex set. Given
y\in A({\mathscr{C}})
, we consider finding the unique solution
x^{\dagger}
of
Ax=y
in
{\mathscr{C}}
with minimal norm which can be stated as (
1.2
) with

{\mathcal{R}}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}+\iota_{\mathscr{C}}(x),

where
\iota_{\mathscr{C}}
denotes the indicator function of
{\mathscr{C}}
. Clearly
{\mathcal{R}}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
(ii) with
c_{0}=1/2
. It is easy to see that the method (
1.5
) takes the form

\displaystyle x_{t}^{(n)}=P_{\mathscr{C}}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}),\qquad\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)}),

(4.1)

where
P_{\mathscr{C}}
denotes the metric projection of
{\mathscr{X}}
onto
{\mathscr{C}}
. In case
{\mathscr{X}}=L^{2}(\mathscr{D})
for some domain
\mathscr{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathscr{C}}=\{x\in{\mathscr{X}}:x\geq 0\mbox{ a.e. on }\mathscr{D}\}
, the iteration scheme (
4.1
) becomes

\displaystyle x_{t}^{(n)}=\max\{A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)},0\},\qquad\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)})

(4.2)

with initial guess
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
. This method can be viewed as an application of the Landweber iteration to the dual variable with nonnegative constraint on the primal variable.

3.8

Example 4.2
.

Consider the equation
Ax=y
, where
A:L^{1}(\mathscr{D})\to{\mathscr{Y}}
is a bounded linear operator,
{\mathscr{Y}}
is a Hilbert space, and
\mathscr{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded domain. Assuming the sought solution is a probability density function, we may find such a solution by considering the convex minimization problem (
1.2
) with

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}(x):=f(x)+\iota_{\Delta}(x),

(4.7)

where
\iota_{\Delta}
denotes the indicator function of the closed convex set

\displaystyle\Delta:=\left\{x\in L^{1}(\mathscr{D}):x\geq 0\mbox{ a.e. on }\mathscr{D}\mbox{ and }\int_{\mathscr{D}}x=1\right\}

in
L^{1}(\mathscr{D})
and
f
denotes the negative of the Boltzmann-Shannon entropy, i.e.

f(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\int_{\mathscr{D}}x\log x&\mbox{ if }x\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathscr{D})\mbox{ and }x\log x\in L^{1}(\mathscr{D}),\\[5.16663pt]
\infty&\mbox{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

where
L_{+}^{1}(\mathscr{D}):=\{x\in L^{1}(\mathscr{D}):x\geq 0\mbox{ a.e. on }\mathscr{D}\}
. According to
[
3
]
,
{\mathcal{R}}
satisfies Assumption
3.1
(ii) with
c_{0}=1/2
. By the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker theory, for any
\ell\in L^{\infty}(\mathscr{D})
the unique minimizer of

\displaystyle\min_{x\in L^{1}(\mathscr{D})}\left\{{\mathcal{R}}(x)-\int_{\mathscr{D}}\ell x\right\}

is given by
\hat{x}:=e^{\ell}/\int_{\mathscr{D}}e^{\ell}
. Therefore the dual gradient method (
1.5
) using multiple repeated measurement data takes the form

\displaystyle x_{t}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{\int_{\Omega}e^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}}}e^{A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}},\qquad\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma(Ax_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{y}^{(n)})

(4.8)

with initial guess
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}=0
, which is an entropic dual gradient method (
[
22
]
). This method is
actually equivalent to the exponentiated gradent method (
[
5
,
27
]
)

x_{t+1}^{(n)}=\frac{x_{t}^{(n)}e^{\gamma A^{*}(\bar{y}^{(n)}-Ax_{t}^{(n)})}}{\int_{\Omega}x_{t}^{(n)}e^{\gamma A^{*}(\bar{y}^{(n)}-Ax_{t}^{(n)})}}.

Example 4.3
.

In this example we consider using the method (
1.5
) to reconstruct sparse solutions for ill-posed problems
Af=u
, where
A:L^{2}(\mathscr{D})\to L^{2}(\mathscr{D})
is a bounded linear operator and
\mathscr{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a bounded domain. For this purpose, we take
{\mathcal{R}}(f)
to be a strongly convex perturbation of
\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{D})}
, i.e.

{\mathcal{R}}(f)=\|f\|_{L^{1}(\mathscr{D})}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathscr{D})}^{2},

where
\beta>0
is a large number. The method (
1.5
) then becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{t}^{(n)}&=\beta\mbox{sign}(A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)})\max\{|A^{*}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}|-1\},\\
\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}&=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma(Af_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{u}^{(n)}),\end{split}

(4.9)

where
\bar{u}^{(n)}
is the average of a sequence of unbiased independent identically distributed noisy data
\{u_{i}\}
,
i=1,\cdots,n
, of the exact data
u
.

For numerical simulations we consider determining the initial data
f
in the time fractional diffusion equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)-\triangle u(x,t)=0,&x\in\mathscr{D},\,t>0,\\
u(x,t)=0,&x\in\partial\mathscr{D},\,t>0,\\
u(x,0)=f(x),&x\in\mathscr{D}\end{array}\right.

from the measurement of
u(x,T)
at a fixed later time
T>0
, where
\mathscr{D}=[0,1]\times[0,1]
,
0<\alpha<1
, and
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u
denotes the Caputo fractional derivative

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}(x,s)dx

with
\Gamma
denoting the Gamma function.
The mapping from
f
to
u(x,T)
is a compact linear operator
A:L^{2}(\mathscr{D})\to L^{2}(\mathscr{D})
. Time fractional diffusion equations occur naturally in anomalous diffusion in which the variance of the process behaves like a non-integer power of time (
[
31
]
), sharply contrast to the classical normal diffusion which is governed by the heat equation.

The exact solution, the exact data and the noisy data (random sample) for Example

.

To solve this inverse problem numerically, we discretize
\mathscr{D}
by taking
(N+1)\times(N+1)
grid points

(x_{i},y_{j}):=(i/N,j/N)
,
i,j=0,1,\cdots,N

and write
u_{i,j}(t)
for
u(x_{i},y_{j},t)
and
f_{i,j}
for
f(x_{i},y_{j})
. Let
h=1/N
. Then,
by the finite difference approximation of
-\triangle u
, the diffusion equation becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u_{i,j}+\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(4u_{i,j}-u_{i+1,j}-u_{i-1,j}-u_{i,j+1}-u_{i,j-1}\right)=0,\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad i,j=1,\cdots,N-1,\\
&u_{0,j}=u_{N,j}=u_{i,0}=u_{i,N}=0,\,\,\,\,\qquad i,j=0,\cdots,N,\\
&u_{i,j}(0)=f_{i,j},\qquad i,j=1,\cdots,N-1.\end{split}

(4.10)

It turns out that the solution of (
4.10
) has the form

u_{i,j}(t)=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N-1}c_{p,q}(t)\sin(iph\pi)\sin(jqh\pi),

where each
c_{p,q}(t)
satisfies the fractional ordinary differential equation

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}c_{p,q}+\mu_{p,q}c_{p,q}=0,\quad\mbox{with }\mu_{p,q}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left(4-2\cos(ph\pi)-2\cos(qh\pi)\right).

Let
S
and
S^{-1}
denote the discrete sine transform and the inverse disrete sine transform defined respectively by

\displaystyle(Sv)_{p,q}

\displaystyle:=4h^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N-1}v_{i,j}\sin(iph\pi)\sin(jqh\pi),

\displaystyle(S^{-1}v)_{i,j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N-1}v_{p,q}\sin(iph\pi)\sin(jqh\pi)

for any
(N-1)\times(N-1)
matrix
(v_{i,j})
. Then we have
c_{p,q}(0)=(Sf)_{p,q}
and thus

c_{p,q}(t)=(Sf)_{p,q}E_{\alpha}(-\mu_{p,q}t^{\alpha})
, where
E_{\alpha}
denotes the Mittag-Leffler function

E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}.

Consequently

u_{i,j}(t)=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N-1}(Sf)_{p,q}E_{\alpha}(-\mu_{p,q}t^{\alpha})\sin(iph\pi)\sin(jqh\pi)

for
i,j=1,\cdots,N-1
. If we define the transform
W_{t}
by
(W_{t}v)_{p,q}=E_{\alpha}(-\mu_{p,q}t^{\alpha})v_{p,q}
, then

u(t)=S^{-1}W_{t}Sf
. Let
A=S^{-1}W_{T}S
. Then
f
can be determined by solving
Af=u
, where
u:=(u_{i,j}(T))
.

Example 4.4
.

In this final example we consider using the method (
1.5
) to reconstruct piecewise constant solutions.
We consider again the equation (
4.4
) with the kernel given by (
4.1
) and assume that the sought solution
x^{\dagger}
is piecewise constant. By dividing
[0,1]
into
m=400
subintervals of equal length and approximating integrals by the trapezoidal rule, we have a discrete ill-posed problem
{\bf A}{\bf x}={\bf y}
, where
{\bf A}
is a matrix. We use the model

\displaystyle\min\left\{{\mathcal{R}}({\bf x}):=\|{\bf D}{\bf x}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\bf D}{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}:{\bf A}{\bf x}={\bf y}\right\},

(4.11)

where
{\bf D}
denotes the discrete gradient operator and
\beta
is a large positive number. Thus,
{\mathcal{R}}
is a strongly convex perturbation of the total variation
\|{\bf D}{\bf x}\|_{1}
. If we apply the method (
1.5
) to (
4.11
) directly, we need to solve a minimization problem related to
{\mathcal{R}}
to obtain
{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}
at each iteration. This can make the algorithm time-consuming since those minimization problems can not be solved explicitly.

Figure 8.
The noisy data (10 samples, green circle), the exact data (blue one) and the reconstruction results with different sample sizes for Example
4.4
.

To circumvent this difficulty, by introducing
{\bf z}={\bf D}{\bf x}
we reformulate (
4.11
) as

\displaystyle\min\left\{f({\bf z})+\phi({\bf x}):{\bf A}{\bf x}={\bf y}\mbox{ and }{\bf D}{\bf x}-{\bf z}=0\right\},

(4.12)

where

f({\bf z})=\|{\bf z}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\bf z}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad\phi({\bf x})=\frac{1}{2\beta}\|{\bf x}\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
f({\bf z})+\phi({\bf x})
is strongly convex, we may apply the method (
1.5
) to (
4.12
) to obtain the iteration scheme

\displaystyle({\bf x}_{t}^{(n)},{\bf z}_{t}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{{\bf x},{\bf z}}\left\{f({\bf z})+\phi({\bf x})-\langle\lambda_{t}^{(n)},{\bf A}{\bf x}\rangle-\langle\mu_{t}^{(n)},{\bf D}{\bf x}-{\bf z}\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle(\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)},\mu_{t+1}^{(n)})

\displaystyle=(\lambda_{t}^{(n)},\mu_{t}^{(n)})-\gamma({\bf A}{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{\bf y}^{(n)},{\bf D}{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}-{\bf z}_{t}^{(n)}).

Since
{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}
and
{\bf z}_{t}^{(n)}
can be given explicitly, this leads to the following algorithm

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}&=\beta({\bf A}^{T}\lambda_{t}^{(n)}+{\bf D}^{T}\mu_{t}^{(n)}),\\
{\bf z}_{t}^{(n)}&=-\beta\mbox{sign}(\mu_{t}^{(n)})\max\{|\mu_{t}^{(n)}|-1,0\},\\
\lambda_{t+1}^{(n)}&=\lambda_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma({\bf A}{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}-\bar{\bf y}^{(n)}),\\
\mu_{t+1}^{(n)}&=\mu_{t}^{(n)}-\gamma({\bf D}{\bf x}_{t}^{(n)}-{\bf z}_{t}^{(n)}).\end{split}

(4.13)
