SketchDeco: Decorating B&W Sketches with Colour
Chaitat Utintu, Pinaki Nath Chowdhury, Aneeshan Sain, Subhadeep Koley, Ayan Kumar Bhunia, Yi-Zhe Song

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel approach to sketch colourisation, inspired by the universal childhood activity of colouring and its professional applications in design and story-boarding. Striking a balance between precision and convenience, our method utilises region masks and colour palettes to allow intuitive user control, steering clear of the meticulousness of manual colour assignments or the limitations of textual prompts. By strategically combining ControlNet and staged generation, incorporating Stable Diffusion v 1.5 1.5 , and leveraging BLIP-2 text prompts, our methodology facilitates faithful image generation and user-directed colourisation. Addressing challenges of local and global consistency, we employ inventive solutions such as an inversion scheme, guided sampling, and a self-attention mechanism with a scaling factor. The resulting tool is not only fast and training-free but also compatible with consumer-grade Nvidia RTX 4090 Super GPUs, making it a valuable asset for both creative professionals and enthusiasts in various fields.

Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DPMs)

Global Sketch Colourisation

Predicting Class Semantics:
We adopt the robust language-vision pre-training (VLP) technique, BLIP-2
[
34
]

[
34
]
, comprising two transformers: the image transformer and the text transformer, which share the same self-attention layers. Throughout the training process, BLIP-2 incorporates a set of learnable query embeddings as input to the image transformer. These queries dynamically interact with each other and textual features via self-attention layers, as well as with image features via cross-attention layers. This mechanism enables the queries to extract visual information that is most relevant to the text. Moreover, by employing distinct self-attention masks, the interaction between queries and text can be manipulated to address diverse tasks (
e.g
.
, image captioning, image-text retrieval, and visual question answering, etc.)
[
34

that expands the range of available colour choices for each region. However, despite their advantages, prior works necessitate retraining or fine-tuning the pre-trained text-to-image diffusion model.

To capitalise on the rich priors of large-scale diffusion models, we present a training-free framework for controlling the colourisation of multiple regions within a sketch in parallel. Given a user-defined sketch
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 512\times 3}
, local colour palettes
\left\{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{2},...,{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{n}\right\}
, where,
{\mathcal{P}_{H}}
is a list of colour hexcodes
\left\{h_{1},h_{2},\dots\right\}
,
and their binary mask for regions-of-interest
\left\{\mathcal{M}^{(1)},\mathcal{M}^{(2)},...,\mathcal{M}^{(n)}\right\}
;
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 512\times 1}
, we employ a generative prior derived from the pre-trained Text-to-Image (T2I) stable diffusion v2.1 model to enrich the semantic information and seamlessly blend colours. The colourised result
\mathcal{I}^{L}
is expected to fulfil three objectives:
(a)
maintain the identity of the input sketch (
i.e
.
,
id(\mathcal{I}^{L})\approx id(\mathcal{S})
),
(b)
preserve local colour consistency – the colour palette within mask region
\mathcal{M}
should align with the corresponding colour hexcodes
\mathcal{P_{H}}
(
i.e
.
,
\text{colour}(\mathcal{I}^{L}\odot\mathcal{M})\approx\text{colour}(\mathcal{P_{H}})
),
(c)
homogeneous transitions between local coloured regions.

Parallel Local Colour Composition:
We reframe the local sketch colourisation problem as an image composition task, drawing upon the rationale that the colourised outcomes derived from the global colourisation pipeline
\mathcal{I}^{G}
already addresses our first objective: maintaining sketch identity (
i.e
.
,
id(\mathcal{I}^{G})\approx id(\mathcal{S})
) and aligning with the specified colour palettes (
i.e
.
,
\text{colour}(\mathcal{I}^{G})\approx\text{colour}(\mathcal{P_{H}})
). To achieve this, we first concurrently produce the globally-colourised results
\left\{{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{1}},{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{2}},...,{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{n}}\right\}
based on given colour palettes
\left\{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{1},{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{2},...,{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{n}\right\}
. Moreover, we generate an additional image
{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{\mathcal{P}\phi}
without colour description, which serves as the main image for the composition process. Thereafter, we apply the corresponding mask to each globally-colourised result for background removal to isolate the region-of-interest for each colour hexcodes as
{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{(i)}}={\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{{\mathcal{P}_{H}}_{i}}\odot\mathcal{M}^{(i)}
, for
i=1,2,...,n
and
\odot
denotes hadamard product. Next, we integrate all segmented local colour images with the main image to get composited result
{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}={\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{\mathcal{P}\phi}\odot(1-{\mathcal{M}}_{tot})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{I}}^{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{(i)}}
, where,
{\mathcal{M}}_{tot}=\max(\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathcal{M}}^{(i)},1)
. This injects the colour-blending ability of our global colourisation pipeline into local colourisation process and ensures local colour consistency in the final output.

ODE Inversion on Composited Image:
The composited image
\mathcal{I}^{\ast}
satisfies our second objective – local colour consistency – but lacks smooth transition between local coloured regions (third objective). In order to achieve smooth transitions between local colourised regions, a crucial step involves transforming the composited image
{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}
into a latent representation
z^{\ast}
.
The sampling process within the diffusion probabilistic models (DPM) can be executed by solving diffusion ordinary differential equations (ODE) from
T
to
0
[
60
]
:

\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}=f(t)x_{t}+\frac{g^{2}(t)}{2\sigma_{t}}\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\;\;\text{ where, }\;\;x_{T}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tilde{\sigma}^{2}I),

(5)

Hence, the inversion process can be achieved by solving the diffusion ODE from
0
to
T
. We utilise DPM-Solver++
[
40
]
for inversion due to its fast-guided sampling ability and generate higher-quality samples in 15 to 20 steps, compared to commonly-used DDIM
[
59
]
that achieves similar results in 100 to 250 steps. Additionally, DPM-Solver++ demonstrates superior alignment between forward and backward trajectories, which makes it suitable for real image inversion and maintains local colour consistency. However, in the context of T2I diffusion models, numerous studies
[
21
,
45
,
63

67
]
have faced notable challenges associated with significant reconstruction errors, particularly when attempting to perform inversion in the text-conditioned setting
\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t,t_{p})
. This issue primarily arises from the inherent instability of classifier-free guidance (CFG) mechanisms:

(6)

where,
t_{p}=\mathrm{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\textbf{T}(p))
and
\varnothing=\mathrm{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(``\ ")
denotes text encodings of normal and null prompt (empty string) embeddings, and

is the guidance scale. In this study, we replace null prompt with exceptional prompt embedding
\textbf{T}_{except}
[

and removes positional embeddings and special tokens like
[startoftext]

[endoftext]

[pad]

\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t},t,\mathrm{\mathcal{E}}_{t}(\textbf{T}_{except}))

Initial Gaussian Noise Incorporation:
After inversion process
\mathcal{I}^{\ast}\rightarrow z^{\ast}
, we incorporate the additional Gaussian noise
z^{+}
to enhance transition smoothness by leveraging the rich generative priors
[
50
]
to effectively in-paint the transition area between regions inside (
i.e
.
,
\mathcal{M}_{tot}
) and outside (
i.e
.
,
1-\mathcal{M}_{tot}
) the user masks. Initially, we determine the spatial extent of each regional mask
\mathcal{M}
by computing its coverage rectangular area
\mathcal{M}_{rect}
. Subsequently, we identify the transitional area
\mathcal{M}_{tran}
by employing the summation of XOR operation
\oplus
results between each rectangular area and its corresponding user-defined mask as
{\mathcal{M}}_{tran}=\max(\sum_{i=1}^{n}({{\mathcal{M}}_{rect}^{i}}\oplus{\mathcal{M}}^{i}),1)
. Therefore, the initial noise incorporation for final diffusion ODE solving becomes:
z^{\ast}_{f}=z^{\ast}\odot(1-\mathcal{M}_{tran})+z^{+}\odot\mathcal{M}_{tran}

Self-attention Injection for Global Consistency:
In the final backward diffusion process, the incorporated noise
{z}^{\ast}_{f}
derived from the composite image
{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}
serves as the initial point for solving the diffusion ODE from
T
to
0
. Throughout this process, the additional prompt
p
(
e.g

hyper-realistic

[class]

in photography style.
”) plays a pivotal role in blending colours in transitional regions seamlessly. Nevertheless, relying solely on initial noise may not adequately retain the colour context (
i.e
.
,
\textit{colour}(\mathcal{I}^{L}\odot\mathcal{M})\not\approx\textit{colour}(\mathcal{P_{H}})
) or the fidelity of sketch (
i.e
.
,
id(\mathcal{I}^{L})\not\approx id(\mathcal{S})

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{l,t}
of the composited image
{\mathcal{I}}^{\ast}
to guide the denoising process. This self-attention map is derived using self-attention modules from the pre-trained Stable Diffusion model, incorporating exceptional prompt embedding
\textbf{T}_{except}
for enhanced contextual understanding. Particularly, the self-attention module at layer
l
composes of three projection matrices as
W_{Q_{l}},W_{K_{l}},W_{V_{l}}
and the encoded features of composited image at timestep
t
and layer
l
is
{z}^{\ast}_{l,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times w\times d}

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{l,t}

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{l,t}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_{l,t}K^{T}_{l,t}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\cdot V_{l,t}

(7)

In the sampling process to produce the colourised output
\mathcal{I}^{L}
guided by prompt
p

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{l,t}
by simultaneously solving the diffusion ODE using an exceptional prompt embedding
\textbf{T}_{except}

\tau

{\mathcal{A}}^{\ast}_{l,t}
to maintain global fidelity; whereas, in the subsequent phase
t\in[T\cdot(1-\tau),0]

t_{p}

p

5
Conclusion

In conclusion, our work introduced a novel approach to sketch colourisation, one that achieves a striking balance between precision and convenience by utilising region masks (easily generated from Photoshop) and colour palettes to allow intuitive user control. Our solution combines ControNet and staged generation, BLIP-2 for class semantics in text prompts, DPM-Solver++, guided sampling, and a self-attention mechanism with a scaling factor. The resulting model enforces a “local colour consistency” property,
i.e
.
, the localised colouring is constrained to only within that region. Finally, our model is fast (takes less than
20
steps), training-free, and works on consumer-grade Nvidia RTX 4090 GPU.

G
Limitation and Future Study

In our work, we acknowledge several limitations and suggest avenues for future research. Firstly, when processing small selected regions for local colourisation, the initial blending ability derived from global colourisation pipeline may lead to some losses in colour fidelity, necessitating exploration of novel techniques to address this challenge. This could involve the development of adaptive blending strategies or localised colour correction mechanisms to better preserve colour accuracy in intricate or fine-textured areas. Additionally, while our approach relies on matching colour hexcodes with name entries in a standard CSS3 colour database
[
1
]
, there is potential to enhance the database to accommodate a wider variety of colours. However, caution must be exercised to ensure compatibility with the pretrained Stable Diffusion
[
50
]
model, as ambiguous or non-standard colour names may introduce uncertainty and inconsistency in the colourisation process. Furthermore, with BLIP2’s
[
34
]
ability to predict class categories, future research could extend the Visual Question Answering (VQA) task to include fine-grained classification, providing more detailed and informative class semantics for colourisation pipelines.
