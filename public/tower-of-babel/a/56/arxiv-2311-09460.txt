Near-Optimal Streaming Ellipsoidal Rounding for General Convex Polytopes
Yury Makarychev, Naren Sarayu Manoj, Max Ovsiankin

Abstract
We give near-optimal algorithms for computing an ellipsoidal rounding of a convex polytope whose vertices are given in a stream. The approximation factor is linear in the dimension (as in John’s theorem) and only loses an excess logarithmic factor in the aspect ratio of the polytope. Our algorithms are nearly optimal in two senses: first, their runtimes nearly match those of the most efficient known algorithms for the offline version of the problem. Second, their approximation factors nearly match a lower bound we show against a natural class of geometric streaming algorithms. In contrast to existing works in the streaming setting that compute ellipsoidal roundings only for centrally symmetric convex polytopes, our algorithms apply to general convex polytopes. We also show how to use our algorithms to construct coresets from a stream of points that approximately preserve both the ellipsoidal rounding and the convex hull of the original set of points.



1
Introduction

We consider the problem of approximating convex polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with “simpler” convex bodies.
Consider a convex polytope
Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Our goal is to find a convex body
\widehat{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a given family of convex bodies, a translation vector
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a scaling factor
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\widehat{Z}\subseteq Z\subseteq\bm{c}+\widehat{Z}.

(1.1)

We say that
\widehat{Z}
is a
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
-approximation to
Z
; an algorithm that computes
\widehat{Z}
is a
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
-approximation algorithm. In this paper, we will be interested in approximating
Z
with (a) ellipsoids and (b) polytopes defined by small number of vertices.

This problem has many applications in computational geometry, graphics, robotics, data analysis, and other fields (see
[
AHV05
]
for an overview of some applications).
It is particularly relevant when we are in the big-data regime and storing polytope
Z
requires too much memory. In this case, instead of storing
Z
, we find a reasonable approximation
\widehat{Z}
with a succinct representation and then use it as a proxy for
Z
. In this setting, it is crucial that we use a
low-memory
approximation algorithm to find
\widehat{Z}
.

In this paper, we study the problem of approximating convex polytopes in the streaming model. The streaming model is a canonical big-data setting that conveniently lends itself to the study of low-memory algorithms.
We assume that
Z
is the convex hull of points
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}
:
Z=\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\}\right)
; the stream of points
\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\}
contains all the vertices of
Z
and additionally may contain other points from polytope
Z
.
In our streaming model, points
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}
arrive one at a time. At every timestep
t
, we must maintain an approximating body
\widehat{Z}_{t}
and translate
\bm{c}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right\}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+\widehat{Z}_{t}.

(1.2)

Once a new point
\bm{z}_{t+1}
arrives, the algorithm must compute a new approximating body
\widehat{Z}_{t+1}
and translation
\bm{c}_{t+1}
such that the guarantee (
1.2
) holds for timestep
t+1
. Finally, after the algorithm has seen all
n
points, we must have

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{n}+\alpha\cdot\widehat{Z}_{n}\subseteq\underbrace{\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\right)}_{Z}\subseteq\bm{c}_{n}+\widehat{Z}_{n}.

(1.3)

for some
0<\alpha\leq 1
(where
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
is the approximation factor).
Note that the algorithm may not know the value of
n
beforehand. We consider two types of approximation.

Ellipsoidal roundings.

In one thrust, we aim to calculate an
ellipsoidal rounding
of
Z
– we are looking for ellipsoidal approximation
\widehat{Z}=\mathcal{E}
. Formally, we would like to output an origin-centered ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
, a center/translate
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a scaling parameter
0<\alpha\leq 1
such that

\displaystyle\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}\subseteq Z\subseteq\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}.

Ellipsoidal roundings are convenient representations of convex sets. They have applications to preconditioning convex sets for efficient sampling and volume estimation
[
JLLV21
]
, algorithms for convex programming
[
Nes08
]
, robotics
[
RB97
]
, and other areas. They also require the storage of at most
\sim d^{2}
floating point numbers, as every ellipsoid can be represented with a center
\bm{c}
and semiaxes
\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}}
for
d^{\prime}\leq d
.

We note that by John’s theorem
[
Joh48
]
, the minimum-volume outer ellipsoid for
Z
achieves approximation
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}\leq d
. Moreover, the upper bound of
d
is tight, which is witnessed when
Z
is a
d
-dimensional simplex (that is, the convex hull of
d+1
points in general position).

We now formally state the streaming ellipsoidal rounding problem.

Problem 1

(Streaming ellipsoidal rounding)
.

Let
Z=\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
receives points
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
one at a time and produces a sequence of ellipsoids
\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
and scalings
\alpha_{t}
.
The algorithm must satisfy the following guarantee at the end of the stream:

\bm{c}_{n}+\alpha_{n}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{n}\subseteq Z\subseteq c_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}

We say that
\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}
is an ellipsoidal rounding of
Z
with approximation factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}
.

We note that in the special case where
Z
is centrally symmetric (i.e.,
Z=-Z
), there are algorithms with nearly optimal approximation factors
O(\sqrt{d\log\left(n\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}\right)})
and
O(\sqrt{d\log\kappa})
due to
[
WY22
]
and
[
MMO22
]
, respectively (here,
\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}
is the online condition number and
\kappa
is the aspect ratio of the dataset). The running times of these algorithms nearly match those of the best-known offline solutions. However, these algorithms do not work with non-symmetric polytopes and we are not aware of any way to adapt them so that they do. We defer a more detailed discussion of the algorithms for the symmetric case to Section
1.2
.

Convex hull approximation.

In another thrust, we want to find a translate
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, subset
S\subseteq[n]
, and scale
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}:i\in S\right\}\right)\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}-\bm{c}:i\in S\right\}\right).

Note that
\bm{c}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}\cdot\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}-\bm{c}:i\in S\right\}\right)
is a
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
-scaled copy of
\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}:i\in S\right\}\right)
. In other words, we desire to find a
coreset

\left\{\bm{z}_{i}:i\in S\right\}
that approximates
Z
. This approach has the advantage of yielding an interpretable solution – one can think of a coreset as consisting of the most “important” datapoints of the input dataset.

We formally state the streaming convex hull approximation problem we study in Problem
2
.

Problem 2

(Streaming convex hull approximation)
.

Let
Z=\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A streaming algorithm
\mathcal{A}
receives points
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
one at a time and produces a sequence of scalings
\alpha_{t}
, centers
\bm{c}_{t}
, subsets
S_{t}\subseteq[n]
such that
S_{t}\subseteq S_{t+1}
.
The algorithm must satisfy the following guarantee at the end of the stream.

\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}:i\in S_{n}\right\}\right)\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}_{n}+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{i}-\bm{c}_{n}:i\in S_{n}\right\}\right)

We say that
\left\{\bm{z}_{i}:i\in S_{n}\right\}
is a coreset of
Z
with approximation factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}
. We will also call
S_{n}
a coreset.

Note that the model considered in Problem
2
is essentially the same as the
online coreset model
studied by
[
WY22
]
.
Similar to Problem
1
, Problem
2
has been studied in the case where
Z
is centrally symmetric. In particular,
[
WY22
]
obtain approximation factor
O(\sqrt{d\log\left(n\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}\right)})
(where
\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}
is the same online condition number mentioned earlier).
However, whether analogous results for asymmetric polytopes hold was an important unresolved question.

1.1.1
Algorithmic results

We start with defining several quantities that we need to state the results and describe their proofs.

Notation.

We will denote the linear span of a set of points
A
by
\mathsf{span}\left(A\right)
. That is,
\mathsf{span}\left(A\right)
is the minimal linear subspace that contains
A
. We denote the affine span of

by
\mathsf{aff}\left(A\right)
. That is,
\mathsf{aff}\left(A\right)
is the minimal affine subspace that contains
A
. Note that
\mathsf{aff}\left(A\right)=\bm{a}+\mathsf{span}\left(A-\bm{a}\right)

.

Definition 1

(Inradius)
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex body. The inradius

of

is the largest

such that there exists a point
\bm{c}_{I}
(called the incenter) for which
\bm{c}_{I}+r\cdot\left(B_{2}^{d}\cap\mathsf{span}\left(K-\bm{c}_{I}\right)\right)\subseteq K

Definition 2

(Circumradius)
.

Let

be a convex body. The circumradius

Definition 3

(Aspect Ratio)
.

Let

be a convex body. We say that

1.1.2
Approximability lower bound

Observe that the approximation factors obtained in Theorems
1
,
2
, and
4
all incur a mild dependence on (variants of) the aspect ratio of the dataset. A natural question is whether this dependence is necessary. In Theorem
5
, we conclude that the approximation factor from Theorem
1
is in fact nearly optimal for a wide class of
monotone
algorithms. We defer the discussion of the notion of a monotone algorithm to Section
2.1
. Loosely speaking, a monotone algorithm commits to the choices it makes; namely, the outer ellipsoid may only increase over time
\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}\supseteq\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
and the inner ellipsoid
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}
satisfies a related but more technical condition
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\cup\{\bm{z}_{t}\}\right)
.

Theorem 5
.

Consider the setting of Problem
1
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any monotone algorithm (see Definition
4
in Section
2.1
) that solves
1
with approximation factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}
.
For every
d\geq 2
, there exists a sequence of points
\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

such that algorithm
\mathcal{A}
gets an approximation factor of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}\geq\Omega\left(\frac{d\log\left(\kappa(Z)\right)}{\log d}\right)
on
Z=\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\right\}\right)
.

1.2
Related work and open questions

Streaming asymmetric ellipsoidal roundings.

To our knowledge, the first paper to study ellipsoidal roundings in the streaming model is that of
[
MGSS
]
. The authors consider the case where
d=2
and prove that the approximation factor of the greedy algorithm (that which updates the ellipsoid to be the minimum volume ellipsoid containing the new point and the previous iterate) can be unbounded. Subsequent work by
[
MSS10
]
generalizes this result to all
d\geq 2
.

Nearly-optimal streaming symmetric ellipsoidal roundings.

Recently,
[
MMO22
]
, and
[
WY22
]
gave the first positive results for streaming ellipsoidal roundings. Both
[
MMO22
]
and
[
WY22
]
considered the problem only in the
symmetric setting
– when the goal is to approximate the polytope
\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\pm\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\pm\bm{z}_{n}\right\}\right)
.
[
MMO22
]
and
[
WY22
]
obtained
O(\sqrt{d\log\kappa(Z)})
and
O(\sqrt{d\log n\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}})
-approximations, respectively (here,
\kappa^{\mathsf{OL}}
is the online condition number; see
[
WY22
]
for details). Their algorithms use only
\widetilde{O}(\mathsf{poly}(d))
space, where the
\widetilde{O}
suppresses
\log d
,
\log n
, and aspect ratio-like terms.
Note that by John’s theorem, the
\Omega(\sqrt{d})
dependence is required in the symmetric setting even for offline algorithms.

A natural question is whether the techniques of
[
MMO22
]
or
[
WY22
]
extend to Problems
1
and
2
. The update rule used in
[
MMO22
]
essentially updates
\mathcal{E}_{t+1}
to be the minimum volume ellipsoid covering both
\mathcal{E}_{t}
and points
\pm\bm{z}_{t+1}
. In the non-symmetric case, it would be natural to consider the minimum volume ellipsoid covering
\mathcal{E}_{t}
and point
\bm{z}_{t+1}
. However, this approach does not give an
\tilde{O}(d)
approximation.
The algorithm in
[
WY22
]
maintains a quadratic form that consists of sums of outer products of “important points” (technically speaking, those with a constant online leverage score). Unfortunately, this approach does not suggest how to move the previous center
\bm{c}_{t-1}
to a new center
\bm{c}_{t}
in a way that allows the algorithm to maintain a good approximation factor. It is not hard to see that there exist example streams for which the center
\bm{c}_{t-1}
must be shifted in each iteration to maintain even a bounded approximation factor. This means that any nontrivial solution to Problems
1
and
2
must overcome this difficulty.

Offline ellipsoidal roundings for general convex polytopes.

[
Nes08
]
gives an efficient
offline

O(d)
-approximation algorithm for the ellipsoidal rounding problem, with a runtime of
\widetilde{O}(nd^{2})
. Observe that this is essentially the same runtime as those achieved by the algorithms we give (see Theorems
1
and
2
).

Streaming convex hull approximations.

[
AS10
]
studied related problems of computing
extent measures
of a convex hull in the streaming model, in particular finding coresets for the minimum enclosing ball, and obtained both positive and negative results.

[
BBKLY18
]
showed that one cannot maintain an
\varepsilon
-hull
in space proportional to the number of vertices belonging to the offline optimal solution (where a body
\widehat{Z}
is an
\varepsilon
-hull for
Z
if every point in
\widehat{Z}
is distance at most
\varepsilon
away from
Z
).

Coresets for the minimum volume enclosing ellipsoid problem (MVEE).

Let
\mathsf{MVEE}(K)
denote the minimum volume enclosing ellipsoid for a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that a subset
S\subseteq[n]
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset for the MVEE problem if we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}\left(\mathsf{MVEE}(Z)\right)\leq\left(1+\varepsilon\right)^{d}\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}\left(\mathsf{MVEE}(Z|_{S})\right).

(1.4)

There is extensive literature on coresets for the MVEE problem, and we refer the reader to papers by
[
KY05
]
,
[
TY07
]
,
[
Cla10
]
,
[
BMV23
]
, and the book by
[
Tod16
]
.

Importantly,
\mathsf{MVEE}(Z|_{S})
may not be a good approximation for
\mathsf{MVEE}(Z)
(for that reason, some authors refer to coresets satisfying (
1.4
) as weak coresets for MVEE). Therefore, even though
\mathsf{MVEE}(Z)
provides a good ellipsoidal rounding for
Z
,
\mathsf{MVEE}(Z|_{S})
generally speaking does not. Please see
[
TY07
, page 2]
and
[
BMV23
, Section 2.1]
for an extended discussion.

2
Summary of Techniques

In this section, we give an overview of the technical methods behind our results.

2.1
Monotone algorithms

The algorithm we give in
1
is of a certain class of
monotone
algorithms, which we now define.

Definition 4

(Monotone algorithm)
.

Consider the setting of Problem
1
. Note the following invariants for every timestep
t
.

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle\supseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\cup\{\bm{z}_{t}\}\right)

(2.1)

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\cup\{\bm{z}_{t}\}\right)

(2.2)

We say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
monotone
if for any initial
(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0},\alpha_{0})
and sequence of data points
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
, the resulting sequence
\{(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0},\alpha_{0}),(\bm{c}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{1},\alpha_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n},\alpha_{n})\}
arising from applying
\mathcal{A}
to the stream satisfies the two invariants (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). Refer to Figure
1
.

We will sometimes consider how a monotone algorithm
\mathcal{A}
makes a single update upon seeing a new point
\bm{x}
. In this setting, we will call
\mathcal{A}
a monotone update rule.

It ensures that each successive outer ellipsoid contains the previous outer ellipsoid. Thus once the algorithm decides that some

The second condition (

\bm{c}_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\mathcal{E}_{t-1}\subseteq Z

2.2
Streaming ellipsoidal rounding (Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
)

Now we describe the algorithm from Theorem
1
in more detail.
Our algorithm keeps track of the current ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, center
\bm{c}_{t}
, and scaling parameter
\alpha_{t}
. Initially,
\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0}
is the ball of radius
r_{0}
around
\bm{c}_{0}
(
r_{0}
and
\bm{c}_{0}
are given to the algorithm), and
\alpha_{0}=1
. Each time the algorithm gets a new point
\bm{z}_{t}
, it updates
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
,
\bm{c}_{t-1}
,
\alpha_{t-1}
using a

) and obtains
\mathcal{E}_{t}
,
\bm{c}_{t}
,
\alpha_{t}
.
The monotonicity condition is sufficient to guarantee that the algorithm gets a
1/\alpha_{n}
approximation to
Z
.
Indeed, first using condition (
2.1
), we get

\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}\supseteq(\bm{c}_{n-1}+\mathcal{E}_{n-1})\cup\{\bm{z}_{n}\}\supseteq(\bm{c}_{n-2}+\mathcal{E}_{n-2})\cup\{\bm{z}_{n-1},\bm{z}_{n}\}\supseteq\dots\supseteq\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\}.

Thus,
\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}\supseteq Z
.
Then, using condition (
2.2
), we get

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{n}+\alpha_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n}

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{n-1}+\alpha_{n-1}\mathcal{E}_{n-1}\right)\cup\{\bm{z}_{n}\})\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{n-2}+\alpha_{n-2}\mathcal{E}_{n-2}\right)\cup\{\bm{z}_{n-1},\bm{z}_{n}\})

\displaystyle\subseteq\dots\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{0}+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{E}_{0})\cup\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\}\right).

The initial ellipsoid
\bm{c}_{0}+\alpha_{0}\mathcal{E}_{0}=\bm{c}_{0}+r_{0}B_{2}^{d}
is in
Z
and therefore
\bm{c}_{n}+\alpha_{n}\mathcal{E}_{n}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n}\right)=Z
.
We verified that the algorithm finds a
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}
approximation for
Z
.

Now, the main challenge is to design an update rule that ensures that
1/\alpha_{n}
is small (as in the statement Theorem
1
) and prove that the rule satisfies the monotonicity conditions/invariants from Definition
4
.
We proceed as follows.

First, we design a monotone update rule that satisfies a particular evolution condition. This condition upper bounds the increase of the approximation factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}
. Second, we prove that any monotone update rule satisfying the evolution condition yields the approximation we desire. These two parts imply Theorem
1
. Finally, we remove the initialization requirement from Theorem
1
and obtain Theorem
2
.

Designing a monotone update rule.

Suppose that at the end of timestep
t-1
our solution consists of a center
\bm{c}_{t-1}
, ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
, and scaling parameter
\alpha_{t-1}
for which the invariants in Definition
4
hold. We give a procedure that, given the next point
\bm{z}_{t}
, computes
\bm{c}_{t},\mathcal{E}_{t},\alpha_{t}
that still satisfy the invariants of Definition
4
. Further, we prove that the resulting update satisfies an evolution condition (
2.3
):

\displaystyle\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}}{\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t})-\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})}\leq C

(2.3)

where
C
is an absolute constant;
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}{\mathcal{E}}
denotes the volume of ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
.
While it is possible to find the optimal update using convex optimization (the update that satisfies the invariants and minimizes the ratio on the left of (
2.3
)), we instead provide an explicit formula for an update that readily satisfies (
2.3
) and as we show is monotone.

Now we describe how we get the formula for the update rule.
By applying an affine transformation, we may assume that
{\mathcal{E}}_{t-1}
is a unit ball and
\bm{c}_{t-1}=0
. Further, we may assume that
\bm{z}_{t}
is colinear with
\bm{e}_{1}
(the first basis vector):
\bm{z}_{t}=\|\bm{z}_{t}\|\bm{e}_{1}
. Importantly, affine transformations preserve (a) the invariants in Definition
4
(if they hold for the original ellipsoids and points, then they also do for the transformed ones and vice versa) and (b) the value of the ratio in (
2.3
), since they preserve the value of
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}({\mathcal{E}_{t}})/\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}({\mathcal{E}_{t-1}})
.

Now consider the group
G=\mathbb{O}\left(d\right)_{\bm{e}_{1}}\cong\mathbb{O}\left(d-1\right)
of orthogonal transformations that map
\bm{e}_{1}
to itself: all of them map the unit ball
{\mathcal{E}}_{t-1}
to itself and
\bm{z}_{t}
to itself. Thus, it is natural to search for an update
(\bm{c}_{t},{\mathcal{E}}_{t})
that is symmetric with respect to all these transformations.
It is easy to see that in this case
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}
is defined by equation

(x_{1}/a)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}(x_{i}/b)^{2}=1
where
a
and
b
are some parameters (equal to the semiaxes of
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}
) and
\bm{c}_{t}=c\bm{e}_{1}
for some
c
.
Since all ellipsoids and points appearing in the invariant conditions are symmetric w.r.t.
G
, it is sufficient now to restrict our attention to their sections by
2
d-plane
\mathsf{span}\left(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{2}\right)
and prove that the invariants hold in this plane. Hence, the problem reduces to a statement in two-dimensional Euclidean geometry (however, when we analyze (
2.3
), we still use that the volume of
{\mathcal{E}}_{t}
is proportional to
ab^{d-1}
and not
ab
).

Let us denote the coordinates corresponding to basis vectors
\bm{e}_{1}
and
\bm{e}_{2}
by
x
and
y
. For brevity, let
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}_{t-1}
,
\bm{z}=\bm{z}_{t}
,
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{E}}_{t}
,
\bm{c}=\bm{c}_{t}=c\bm{e}_{1}
,
\alpha=\alpha_{t-1}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha_{t}
.
We now need to choose parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
so that invariants from Definition
4
and equation (
2.3
) hold. See
Figure

1
.
As shown in that figure, the new outer ellipse
\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
must contain the previous outer ellipse
\mathcal{E}
and the newly received point
\bm{z}
. The new inner ellipse
\bm{c}+\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
must be contained within the convex hull of the previous inner ellipse
\alpha\mathcal{E}
and
\bm{z}
.

It is instructive to consider what happens when point
\bm{z}
is at infinitesimal distance
\Delta
from
\mathcal{E}
:
\|\bm{z}\|=1+\Delta
. We consider a minimal axis-parallel outer ellipse
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
that contains
\mathcal{E}
and
\bm{z}
. It must go through
\bm{z}=(1+\Delta,0)
and touch
\mathcal{E}
at two points symmetric w.r.t. the
x
-axis, say,
(-\sin\varphi,\pm\cos\varphi)
. Angle
\varphi
uniquely determines
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
. Now we want to find the largest value of the scaling parameter
\alpha^{\prime}
so that
\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
fits inside the convex hull of
\mathcal{E}
and
\bm{z}
. When
\Delta
is infinitesimal, this condition splits into two lower bounds on
\alpha^{\prime}
– loosely speaking, they say that
\mathcal{E}
does not extend out beyond the convex hull in the horizontal (one bound) and vertical directions (the other). The former bound becomes stronger (gives a smaller upper bound on
\alpha^{\prime}
) when
\varphi
increases, and the latter becomes stronger when
\varphi
decreases. When
\varphi=\alpha/2\pm O(\alpha^{2})
, then all terms linear in
\alpha
vanish in both bounds and then
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-\Theta(\alpha^{2}\Delta)
satisfies both of them; for other choices of
\varphi
, we have
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha-\Omega(\alpha\Delta)
.
So we let
\varphi=\alpha/2
and from the formula for
\alpha^{\prime}
get
1/\alpha^{\prime}=1/\alpha+O(\Delta)
. On the other hand,
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})\geq(1+\Delta/2)\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E})
, since
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
covers
\bm{z}=(1+\Delta,0)
. It is easy to see now that the evolution condition (
2.3
) holds: the numerator is
O(\Delta)
and the denominator is
\Omega(\Delta)
in (
2.3
).

We remark that letting
\bm{c}+{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
be the minimum volume ellipsoid that contains
\mathcal{E}
and
\bm{z}
is a highly suboptimal choice (it corresponds to setting
\varphi=\Theta(1/d)
). To derive our specific update formulas for arbitrary
\bm{z}
, we, loosely speaking, represent an arbitrary update as a series of infinitesimal updates, get a differential equation on
a

b
,
c
, and
\alpha^{\prime}
, solve it, and then simplify the solution (remove non-essential terms etc). We get the following.

Our updates come from a family parameterized by
\gamma\geq 0
. Define
\alpha^{\prime}
by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha^{\prime}}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}+2\gamma
. With this choice of
\alpha^{\prime}
, define the new ellipses to be

\underbrace{\frac{1}{a^{2}}(x-c)^{2}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}y^{2}=1}_{\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}},\qquad\underbrace{\frac{1}{a^{2}}(x-c)^{2}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}y^{2}=\alpha^{\prime 2}}_{\bm{c}+\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}

where we use parameters

\left.\begin{aligned} a&=\mathsf{exp}\left(\gamma\right)\\
b&=1+\frac{\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\\
c&=-\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a\end{aligned}\qquad\right\}.

Choose
\gamma\approx\ln\|\bm{z}\|
so that
\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
covers point
\bm{z}
.
We use two-dimensional geometry to prove that
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
,
\bm{c}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}
satisfy the invariants (see Figure
1
).
Now to prove the evolution condition, we observe two key properties: (1) the increase in the approximation factor is given by
\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\alpha}=2\gamma
and (2) the length of the horizontal semiaxis of the new outer ellipse is
\exp(\gamma)
. The length of the vertical semiaxis is at least
1
, so by the second property we have
\log\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime})-\log\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E})\geq\gamma
. We combine this with the first property to prove that this update satisfies the evolution condition (
2.3
).

Finally, we obtain an upper bound on
1/\alpha_{n}
from the evolution equation. We have

/

1

α

n

=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{0}}}+\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tiny(\text{by }\ref{eq:overview_evolution})}}{{\leq}}1+C\sum_{t=1}^{n}(\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t})-\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1}))=1+C\log\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}}}.

It remains to get an upper bound on
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{n})
. We know that
\mathcal{E}_{n}
approximates
Z
, and
Z
, in turn, is contained in the ball of radius
R(Z)
. Loosely speaking, we get
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{n})\approx\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(Z)\leq R(Z)^{d}\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})
. Since
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is the ball of radius
r

\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}{\mathcal{E}_{0}}=r^{d}\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})
. We conclude that
the approximation factor is at most
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{n}}}\lessapprox 1+C\log\frac{R(Z)^{d}}{r^{d}}=1+O(d\log\frac{R(Z)}{r})
, as desired.

Removing the initialization assumption.

Once we have a monotone update rule and guarantee on its approximation factor, we have to convert this to a guarantee where the algorithm does not have access to the initialization.

One natural approach is as follows. Let
d^{\prime}\leq d
be the largest timestep for which points
\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{d^{\prime}+1}
are in general position. We can compute the John ellipsoid for
\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right\}\right)
and after that apply the monotone update rule guaranteed by Theorem
1
to obtain the rounding for every
t\geq d^{\prime}+2
, so long as for every such timestep we have
\bm{z}_{t}\in\mathsf{aff}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t-1}\right)
.

The principal difficulty in this approach is designing an
irregular update step
that will handle points
\bm{z}_{t}
outside of
\mathsf{aff}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t-1}\right)
; when we add these points the dimensionality of the affine hull increases by 1. We consider the special case where the new point
\bm{z}_{t}
is conveniently located with respect to our previous ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
(see
Figure

2
for a 2d-picture):
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
is the unit ball in
\mathsf{span}\left(\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{d^{\prime}}\right)
; point
z_{t}=(0,\dots,0,\sqrt{1+2\alpha}),0,\dots)
, here only coordinate
d^{\prime}+1
is non-zero. We show that we can design an irregular update step for this special case that makes the new approximation factor
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}
satisfy
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}+1
.

\mathcal{E}_{t-1}

It turns out that it is sufficient to consider only this special case. To see this, note that we can choose an affine transformation that maps any new point
\bm{z}_{t}
and previous ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
to the setting shown in
Figure

.
Observe that there are at most
d-1
irregular update steps. This means that the irregular update steps contribute at most an additive

to the final approximation factor.

Finally, observe that the inradius of
\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right\}\right)
is not monotone in
t

\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right)
contains for all

1
to this setting, and
2
follows.

Improved bounds on lattices.

Finally, we briefly discuss how to remove the aspect ratio dependence in the setting where the input points
\bm{z}_{t}
have coordinates in
[-N,N]
. At a high level, this improvement follows from carefully tracking how the approximation factors of our solutions change after an irregular update step. Following (
2.3
), recall that our goal is to analyze (where we write
\alpha_{0}=1
)

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t-1}}.

By (
2.3
), we see that for all “regular” updates, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t-1}}\lesssim\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}_{d_{t}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t}\right)}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}_{d_{t}}\left(\mathcal{E}_{t-1}\right)}\right),

where
d_{t}=\mathsf{dim}\left(\mathsf{aff}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right)\right)
. Furthermore, as previously mentioned, in our irregular update step, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t-1}}=1.

In order to control the sum of the
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}
, it remains to bound
\nicefrac{{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}_{d_{t}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})}}{{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}_{d_{t-1}}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})}}
for an irregular update step
t
. We will then get a telescoping upper bound whose last term is the ratio of the volume of the final ellipsoid to the Euclidean ball in the same affine span.

Similarly to the improvements of
[
WY22
]
in the integer-valued case, it will turn out that we will be interested in the total product of these volume changes. By carefully tracking these, we will get that this product can be expressed as the determinant of a particular integer-valued matrix. Then, since this matrix has integer entries, the magnitude of its determinant must be at least
1
. We then observe that the volume of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
after normalizing by the volume of
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d_{n}})
must be at most
(N\sqrt{d})^{d_{n}}
, since the length of any vector in this lattice is at most
N\sqrt{d}
. The desired result then follows.

2.3
Coresets for convex hull (
4
)

We now outline our proof strategy for
4
.
Our main task is to design an appropriate selection criterion for every new point – in other words, we must check whether a new point
\bm{z}_{t}
is “important enough” to be added to our previous set of points
S_{t-1}
. We then have to show that this selection criterion yields the approximation guarantee promised by Theorem
4
.

To design the selection criterion, we run an instance of the algorithm in Theorem
2
on the stream. For every new point
\bm{z}_{t}
, we ask two questions – “Does
\bm{z}_{t}
result in an irregular update step? Does it cause
\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t})
to be much larger than
\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})
?” If the answer to any of these questions is affirmative, we add
\bm{z}_{t}
to the coreset. The first question is necessary to obtain even a bounded approximation factor (for example, imagine that the final point
\bm{z}_{n}
results in an irregular update step, then we must add it). The second question is quite natural, as it ensures that the algorithm adds “important points” – those that necessitate a significant update.

We now observe that at every irregular update step
t_{d^{\prime}}
for
d^{\prime}\leq d
and subsequent timestep
t\geq t_{d^{\prime}}
for which there are no irregular update steps in between
t_{d^{\prime}}
and
t
, there exists a translation
\bm{c}_{d^{\prime}}
(which is the center for
\mathcal{E}_{d^{\prime}}
that the algorithm maintains) and a value
r_{d^{\prime}}
for which we know

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{d^{\prime}}+r_{d^{\prime}}\cdot\left(B_{2}^{d}\cap\mathsf{span}\left(\bm{z}_{1}-\bm{c}_{d^{\prime}},\dots,\bm{z}_{d^{\prime}}-\bm{c}_{d^{\prime}}\right)\right)\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}_{C}+R_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d},

where
\bm{c}_{C}
is the circumcenter of
\mathsf{conv}\left(\left\{\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{t}\right\}\right)
. The resulting bound on
\left\lvert S_{t}\right\rvert
follows easily from the above observation and a simple volume argument.

Finally, we obtain the approximation guarantee from noting that for all
t
, the output of the algorithm from Theorem
2
given the first
t
points is the same as running it only on the points selected by
S_{t}
.

2.4
Lower bound (Theorem
5
)

Whereas in the upper bound we demonstrated a particular algorithm that satisfies the evolution condition (
2.3
),
for the lower bound it suffices to show that for any monotone algorithm, there exists an instance of the problem (a sequence of
\bm{z}_{1}
,…,
\bm{z}_{n}
) where the algorithm must satisfy the “reverse evolution condition”, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha_{t-1}}}}{\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t})-\log\mathsf{vol}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})}\geq C

(2.4)

for some
C>0
.
In analogy to the argument of the upper bound, showing this reverse evolution condition yields a lower bound of the form
\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}}\geq\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d\log(\kappa)\right)
.
Given any monotone algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, the instance we use is produced by an adversary that repeatedly feeds
\mathcal{A}
a point that is a constant factor away from the previous ellipsoid.

In order to simplify showing this reverse evolution condition, we use a symmetrization argument.
Specifically, by a particular sequence of Steiner symmetrizations, we see that the optimal response of
\mathcal{A}
can be completely described in two dimensions. Thus, it is sufficient to only show this reverse evolution condition in the two-dimensional case where the previous outer ellipsoid is the unit ball.

This transformed two-dimensional setting is significantly simpler to analyze. Specifically, we can assume that the point given by the adversary is always
2\bm{e}_{1}
. The rest of the argument proceeds by cases, again using two-dimensional Euclidean geometry. On a high level, the constraints placed on the new outer and inner ellipsoid by the monotonicity condition force the update of
\mathcal{A}
to satisfy the reverse evolution condition.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Notation

We denote the standard Euclidean norm of a vector
\bm{v}
by
\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert
and the Frobenius norm of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
by
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert_{F}
. We denote the singular values of a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by
\sigma_{1}(\mathbf{A}),\dots,\sigma_{d}(\mathbf{A})
. Let
\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{A})
and
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{A})
be the largest and smallest singular values of
\mathbf{A}
, respectively.
We write
\mathsf{diag}\left(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}\right)
to mean the
d\times d
diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
.
We use
\mathbf{S}_{++}^{d}
to denote the set of
d\times d
positive definite matrices.
We use
\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{d}
for the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Denote the
\ell_{2}
-unit ball by
B_{2}^{d}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}{\;\;:\;\;}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1\right\}
,
and
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\|\bm{x}\|_{2}=1\right\}
the unit sphere. We use
\partial S
for the boundary of an arbitrary set
S
. We use natural logarithms unless otherwise specified.

In this paper, we will work extensively with ellipsoids. We will always assume that all ellipsoids and balls we consider are centered at the origin. We use the following representation of ellipsoids. For a non-singular matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{x}{\;\;:\;\;}\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\bm{x}\right\rVert\leq 1\right\}
. In other words, the matrix
\mathbf{A}
defines an bijective linear map satisfying
\mathbf{A}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}=B_{2}^{d}
. Every full-dimensional ellipsoid (centered at the origin) has such a representation. We note that this representation is not unique as matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}
define the same ellipsoid if matrix
\mathbf{M}
is orthogonal (since
\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\bm{v}\right\rVert=\left\lVert\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}\bm{v}\right\rVert
for every vector
\bm{v}
). Sometimes, we will have to consider lower-dimensional ellipsoids within an ambient space of higher dimension; in this case, we will use the notation
\mathcal{E}\cap H
where
H
is some linear or affine subspace – note that
\mathcal{E}\cap H
is also an ellipsoid.

Now consider the singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{A}
:
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{T}
(it will be convenient for us to write
\Sigma^{-1}
instead of standard
\Sigma
in the decomposition). The diagonal entries of
\Sigma
are exactly the semi-axes of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}
. As mentioned above, matrices
\mathbf{U}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{T}
and
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{T}
define the same ellipsoid for any orthogonal
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
; in particular, every ellipsoid can be represented by a matrix of the form
\mathbf{A}=\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{V}^{T}
.

3.2
Geometry

We restate the well-known result that five points determine an ellipse.
This is usually phrased for conics, but for nondegenerate ellipses the usual condition that no three of the five points are collinear is vacuously true.

Claim 3.1

(Five points determine an ellipse)
.

Let
\bm{c}_{1}+\partial\mathcal{E}_{1},\bm{c}_{2}+\partial\mathcal{E}_{2}
be two ellipses in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
If they intersect at five distinct points, then
\bm{c}_{1}+\partial\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\bm{c}_{2}+\partial\mathcal{E}_{2}
are the same.

The following claim, that every full-rank ellipsoid (i.e. an ellipsoid whose span has full dimension) can be represented by a positive definite matrix, follows from looking at the SVD.

Claim 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a full-rank ellipsoid.
Then there exists
\mathbf{A}\succ 0
such that
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}
.

We also have the standard result relating volume and determinants, which follows from observing
\mathbf{A}\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}}=B_{2}^{d}
.

Claim 3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\succ 0
. Then

\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{A}})=\det(\mathbf{A}^{-1})\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})

In order to give the reduction in the lower bound from the general case to the two-dimensional case, we use the technique of Steiner symmetrization (see e.g.
[
AGM15
, Section 1.1.7]
).
Given some unit vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
S_{\bm{u}}(K)
for the
Steiner symmetrization
in the direction of
\bm{u}
.
Recall that the Steiner symmetrization is defined so that for any
\bm{x}\perp\bm{u}
:

\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}((\bm{x}+\mathbb{R}\bm{u})\cap K)=\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}((\bm{x}+\mathbb{R}\bm{u})\cap S_{\bm{u}}(K))

and so that
(\bm{x}+\mathbb{R}\bm{u})\cap S_{\bm{u}}(K)
is an interval centered at
\bm{x}
.
Note that we will overload notation slightly as we will allow you
\bm{u}
to be a vector of any non-zero length while Steiner symmetrization is usually defined with
\bm{u}
being a unit vector, but we will simply take
S_{\bm{u}}=S_{\frac{\bm{u}}{\|\bm{u}\|_{2}}}
.

Importantly, Steiner symmetrization will preserve important properties of the update.
We have the key facts that
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(S_{\bm{u}}(K))=\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(K)
,
S_{\bm{u}}(K^{\prime})\subseteq S_{\bm{u}}(K)
if
K\subseteq K^{\prime}
, and further the Steiner symmetrization preserves
K
being an ellipsoid:

Claim 3.4

(
[
BLM06
, Lemma 2]
)
.

If
c+\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an ellipsoid,
S_{\bm{u}}(c+\mathcal{E})
is still an ellipsoid.

Further, if we apply Steiner symmetrization to a body that is a body of revolution about an axis,
it does not change the body if
\bm{u}
is perpendicular to the axis of revolution.

Claim 3.5
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a body of revolution about the
\bm{e}_{1}
-axis.
Then if
\bm{u}\perp\bm{e}_{1}
,
S_{\bm{u}}(K)=K
.

4
Streaming Ellipsoidal Rounding

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorems
1
and
2
.

4.1
Monotone algorithms solve
1

To design algorithms to solve the streaming ellipsoidal rounding problem,
we first show that any monotone algorithm gives a valid solution.
We let
\bm{c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r_{0}\geq 0
be given so that

\bm{c}_{0}+r_{0}\cdot B_{2}^{d}\subseteq Z
,
and denote the initial ellipsoid as
\mathcal{E}_{0}=r_{0}\cdot B_{2}^{d}
.
Note that
r_{0}
need not be the inradius, although it is upper bounded by the inradius.

If we had for each intermediate step
t

that
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots\bm{z}_{t}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
,
then clearly any algorithm that satisfies this would give a valid final solution as well.
However, in intermediate steps it is not clear that

\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots\bm{z}_{t}\right)
,
due to the initialization of
\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0}
in our monotone algorithm framework.
Instead, we relax this invariant to
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots\bm{z}_{t}\}\cup(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0})\right)
,
which still suffices to produce a valid final solution.

Claim 4.1
.

To solve
1
, it suffices for the sequence of ellipsoids
\bm{c}_{i}+\mathcal{E}_{i}
and scalings
\alpha_{i}
to satisfy the invariants of
4
.

Proof.

First, we argue that
\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right)\subseteq\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
As
\mathcal{E}_{n}
is an ellipsoid and therefore a convex set, it suffices to show
\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\}\subseteq\bm{c}_{n}+\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
We actually argue by induction that
\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t}\}\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
for all
0\leq t\leq n
.
This is vacuously true for
t=0
. At each step
t>0
the inductive hypothesis gives
\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t-1}\}\subseteq\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
, and thus by (
2.1
) we have
\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t}\}\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
.

Now, we argue that
\bm{c}_{n}+\alpha_{n}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{n}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right)
.
We show by induction that
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t}\}\cup(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0})\right)

for all
0\leq t\leq n
.
This is sufficient as
\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\}\cup(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0})\right)=Z
.
The case for
t=0
is trivial.
For
t>0
, the inductive hypothesis gives
\bm{c}_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t-1}\}\cup(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0})\right)
,
and by (
2.2
) we have

c_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((c_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\cup\{\bm{z}_{i}\}\right)\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left(\{\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{t}\}\cup(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0})\right)

as desired.
∎

4.2
Special case

In light of
4.1
, our strategy is to design an algorithm that preserves the invariants given in
4
. This algorithm can be thought of as an
update rule
that,
given the previous outer and inner ellipsoids
\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1},\bm{c}_{t-1}+\alpha_{t-1}\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
and next point
\bm{z}_{t}
, produces the next outer and inner ellipsoids
\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t},\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}
.

It is in fact sufficient to consider the simplified case where the previous outer ellipsoid is the unit ball, and the previous inner ellipsoid is some scaling of the unit ball;
we will show this in
Section

4.3
.
We can further specialize by considering only the two-dimensional case
d=2
.
We will later show that the high-dimensional case is not much different, as all the relevant sets
\bm{c}_{t_{1}}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1},\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
and
\mathsf{conv}\left(\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1}\cup\{\bm{z}_{t}\}\right)
form bodies of revolution about the axis through
\bm{c}_{t-1}
and
\bm{z}_{t}
.

We now describe our two-dimensional update rule.
In order to simplify notation, we will let
\alpha
be the previous scaling
\alpha_{t-1}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}

be the next scaling
\alpha_{t}
.
We will assume that
\alpha\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
to simplify the analysis
of our update rule; this will not affect the quality of our final approximation as this update rule
will only be used in ‘large approximation factor’ regime.
We will also overload notation; writing
c+\mathcal{E}
even when
c

is a scalar to mean
(c,0)+\mathcal{E}
.
We can describe the previous outer ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
with the equation
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1
, and the previous inner ellipsoid
\alpha\mathcal{E}
with
x^{2}+y^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}
.
We define the next outer and inner ellipsoids
c+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
,
c+\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
as

\underbrace{\frac{1}{a^{2}}(x-c)^{2}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}y^{2}\leq 1}_{c+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}},\qquad\underbrace{\frac{1}{a^{2}}(x-c)^{2}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}y^{2}\leq\alpha^{\prime 2}}_{c+\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}

where we use parameters

\left.\begin{aligned} a&=\mathsf{exp}\left(\gamma\right)\\
b&=1+\frac{\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}}{2}\\
c&=-\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a\\
\alpha^{\prime}&=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\alpha}+2\gamma}\\
\end{aligned}\qquad\right\}

(4.1)

We will let
\bm{z}
be the rightmost point of
c+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, so that
\bm{z}=(c+a,0)
. Eventually, we will choose
\gamma
so that
\bm{z}
coincides with
\bm{z}_{t}
, the point received in the next iteration.
In
Section

4.4
, these parameters
a(\gamma),b(\gamma),c(\gamma),\alpha^{\prime}(\gamma)

will be used as functions of the parameter
\gamma\geq 0
. However, we will not yet explicitly specify
\gamma
, so in this section these parameters can be thought of as constants for some fixed
\gamma
.
This update rule is pictured in
Figure

1

We first collect a few straightforward properties of this update rule.

Claim 4.2
.

The parameters in the setup (
4.1
) satisfy

1.

\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}+2\gamma

2.

b\geq 1

3.

c\geq 0

4.

c+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a\geq\alpha

Before proving these properties, we provide geometric interpretations.
Intuitively, (
1
) means that
\gamma
is proportional to the increase in the approximation factor at this step, a fact that we will use when analyzing the general-case algorithm.
(
2
) means that the outer ellipsoid grows on every axis;
and (
3
) means that the centers of the next ellipsoids are to the right of the
y
-axis, i.e. the centers of the next ellipsoids are further towards
\bm{v}
than those of the previous ellipsoids.
The rightmost point of
c+\alpha^{\prime}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is
c+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a
, so (
4
) shows that this point is to the right of the rightmost point of
\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

(
1
) is clear from rearranging the definition of
\alpha^{\prime}
.
From (
1
) we also have
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
, so that (
2
) follows immediately.

For (
3
), observe that
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}=1+2\gamma\alpha
.
When
\alpha\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, this means

\frac{\alpha}{\alpha^{\prime}}\leq 1+\gamma\leq\mathsf{exp}\left(\gamma\right)=a

(4.2)

using
1+x\leq e^{x}
,
7.1
-(
1
).
By definition of
c
,
\alpha/\alpha^{\prime}\leq a
is equivalent to
c\geq 0
.

To show (
4
), by definition we have that
c+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a=-\alpha+2\alpha^{\prime}a
.
Thus showing
c+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot a\geq\alpha
is equivalent to showing that
\alpha^{\prime}a\geq\alpha
, which is equivalent to the inequality in (
4.2
).
∎

\displaystyle\bm{z}\in Z:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left\lVert\bm{z}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}\cdot\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+2\alpha}}\leq\frac{1+\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left\lVert\bm{z}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}\cdot\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{1+2\alpha}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}=\frac{1+\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}

We now analyze (

\displaystyle\left\{\bm{z}\in\mathsf{aff}\left(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right){\;\;:\;\;}\left\lVert\bm{z}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}\cdot\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}}\right\}

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\mathsf{conv}\left(\alpha\cdot\left\{\bm{z}\in\mathsf{aff}\left(\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{d}\right){\;\;:\;\;}\left\lVert\bm{z}\right\rVert_{2}\leq 1\right\}\cup\left\{\sqrt{1+2\alpha}\cdot\bm{v}_{d^{\prime}+1}\right\}\right)

Proof of

.

Using

It remains to verify the approximation factor

Consider a timestep

,
r_{t^{\prime}}=r(Z_{t^{\prime}})

, and
R_{t^{\prime}}=R(Z_{t^{\prime}})
be the circumradius of

Let
{\hat{r}}=\min_{t^{\prime}\leq t}r_{t^{\prime}}
. Consider the

\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d_{t^{\prime}}}(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}})}{\mathsf{vol}_{d_{t}}(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1})}=\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}.

Now applying the evolution condition (

\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}-1}}\leq C\log\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d_{t^{\prime}}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})}{\mathsf{vol}_{d_{t^{\prime}}}(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1})}=C\log\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}.

We have obtained the following upper bound on the approximation-factor increase:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}-1}}\leq\begin{cases}1&\text{if $t^{\prime}$ is an irregular update step}\\
C\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}\right)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(4.18)

\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}=\alpha_{0}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t^{\prime}-1}}\right)\leq d_{t}+C\sum_{t^{\prime}\in T_{\mathsf{reg}}}\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}\right).

Using this inequality and plugging in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}

\displaystyle=1+d_{t}+C\sum_{t^{\prime}\in T_{\mathsf{reg}}}\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}\right)\leq 1+d_{t}+C\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}))}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim d_{t}+\log\left(\frac{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{t}))}{\mathsf{vol}_{d}(T(\mathcal{E}_{0}))}\right)\lesssim d_{t}+\log\left(\frac{(R_{t}/\alpha_{t})^{d_{t}}{\hat{r}}^{d-d_{t}}}{{\hat{r}}^{d}}\right)\lesssim d_{t}+d_{t}\log\left(\frac{R_{t}}{\alpha_{t}\hat{r}}\right).

We conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\lesssim d_{t}+d_{t}\log\left(\frac{R_{t}}{\hat{r}}\right)+d_{t}\log d_{t}.

This concludes the proof of

Claim 4.21

Let

Our approach is reminiscent of that used in the proof of Theorem 1.5 in

.

By applying a translation to all points, we may assume without loss of generality that

. We will prove the guarantee for the last timestamp

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 1}\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t-1}}\lesssim d_{t}\log\left(dN\right).

It therefore follows that
\left\lvert S\right\rvert\lesssim d_{t}\log\left(dN\right)
, as desired.

Bounding the distortion of the chosen points.

Consider some iteration
t^{\prime}\leq t
. Without loss of generality, let
\bm{c}_{t^{\prime}-1}=0
. Suppose
\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}
does not result in an update to
S_{t^{\prime}-1}
. This implies that
\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}\in 2e\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}
. Next, observe that
0\in\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}
. Putting these together, we have
\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}\in\left(\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}\right)+2e\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is monotone, we must have
2e\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t^{\prime}-1}\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+e\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}
; hence, we may write
\bm{z}_{t^{\prime}}\in\bm{c}_{t}+\left(2e+1\right)\mathcal{E}_{t}
.

The inner ellipsoid
\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}
will still be an inner ellipsoid for the points determined by
S_{t}
. Stitching together all our inclusions, we have

\displaystyle\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq Z|_{S_{t}}\subseteq Z\subseteq\bm{c}_{t}+(2e+1)\mathcal{E}_{t}\subseteq\frac{2e+1}{\alpha_{t}}\cdot Z|_{S_{t}}.

(5.1)

which means that

\displaystyle Z|_{S_{t}}\subseteq Z\subseteq O\left(d_{t}\cdot\log\left(d_{t}\cdot\max_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\frac{R(Z|_{([t]\cap S_{t})})}{r(Z|_{([t^{\prime}]\cap S_{t})})}\right)\right)\cdot Z|_{S_{t}}.

Notice that this is nearly what we want, except that the aspect ratio term is in terms of the subset body
Z|_{S_{t}}
. To obtain the final guarantee in terms of the aspect ratio of
Z|_{[t]}
, observe that the above guarantee readily implies that

\displaystyle O\left(d_{t}\cdot\log\left(d_{t}\cdot\max_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\frac{R(Z|_{([t]\cap S_{t})})}{r(Z|_{([t^{\prime}]\cap S_{t})})}\right)\right)\leq O\left(d_{t}\cdot\log\left(d_{t}\cdot\max_{t^{\prime}\leq t}\frac{R(Z|_{[t]})}{r(Z|_{[t^{\prime}]})}\right)\right).

We now give the corresponding improvement when the
\bm{z}_{t}
are integer-valued. As before, (
5.1
) holds. From this, we get

\displaystyle Z|_{S_{t}}\subseteq Z\subseteq O\left(d_{t}\cdot\log\left(dN\right)\right)\cdot Z|_{S_{t}},

as desired. This concludes the proof of Theorem
4
.
∎

6
Lower Bound

In this section, we show
5
.

6.1
Lower bound adversary

Our proof of
5
constructs an adversary, which given a monotone algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and
\kappa\geq 1
,
constructs a sequence of points
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}
satisfying
\kappa(\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{n}\right))\leq\kappa
to witness that the algorithm does not produce an approximation better than
\widetilde{\Omega}(d\log\kappa)
.
While by definition
\kappa=\frac{R}{r}
, our construction keeps
r=1
(notice that any lower bound construction must be scale-invariant),
and for simplicity we use
R=\kappa
.

Let
\bm{z}^{\Delta}_{1},\bm{z}^{\Delta}_{2},\ldots,\bm{z}^{\Delta}_{d+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the
d+1
vertices of a regular simplex
\Delta_{d}
that circumscribes
B_{2}^{d}
.
Our adversary is described in
Algorithm

6.1
.
It uses a first phase that feeds
\mathcal{A}
the vertices of
\Delta_{d}
,
then a second phase that repeatedly feeds
\mathcal{A}
points at a constant distance from the previous ellipsoid.
Specifically, every new point
\bm{z}_{t}
in the second phase is in
\bm{c}_{t-1}+2\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
, i.e. its distance is 2 from
\bm{c}_{t-1}
in the norm that is the gauge of
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
.

Algorithm 6.1

Lower bound adversary

Input
: Monotone algorithm
\mathcal{A}
,
R\geq 1

1:

(\bm{c}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{0},\alpha_{0})=(0+B_{2}^{d},1)

\triangleright
Initialize to the unit ball

2:

for

t\in\{1,2,\ldots,d+1\}

do

\triangleright
Phase I: feed
\mathcal{A}
the vertices of a simplex

3:

(\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t},\alpha_{t})=\mathcal{A}(\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1},\alpha_{t-1},\bm{z}^{\Delta}_{t})

4:

t\leftarrow d+2

5:

while

\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\leq\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}\left(\frac{R}{2}\cdot B_{2}^{d}\right)

do

\triangleright
Phase II: feed
\mathcal{A}
points outside the previous ellipsoid

6:

Let
F_{t-1}=\partial(\bm{c}_{t-1}+2\mathcal{E}_{t-1})\cap(R\cdot B_{2}^{d})

7:

if

F_{t-1}=\varnothing

then

8:

stop

9:

Let arbitrary
\bm{z}_{t}\in F_{t-1}

(\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t},\alpha_{t})=\mathcal{A}(\bm{c}_{t-1}+\mathcal{E}_{t-1},\alpha_{t-1},\bm{z}_{t})

t\leftarrow t+1

Remark 6.1
.

This particular construction we give of the hard case is adaptive, meaning that the adversary’s choice of points depend on the previous ellipsoids the algorithm outputs.
However, this adversary can be made non-adaptive by taking an
\varepsilon
-net
S
of
B_{2}^{d}
for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
,
then feeding
\mathcal{A}
the sequence of points in sets
S,2\cdot S,4\cdot S,\ldots,2^{\log_{2}R-1},2^{\log_{2}R}\cdot S
.
In consequence, this means that randomization on the part of the monotone algorithm does not help, unlike some other online settings.

Let
T
be the largest value of
t-1
before the adversary halts.
We first show that the adversary only gives finitely many points before halting.

Claim 6.2
.

T\leq O(d\log R)

Proof.

We argue that the volume of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
increases by at least a constant factor on each iteration.
This is sufficient to bound the number of iterations by
O(d\log R)
,
as
\mathcal{E}_{0}=B_{2}^{d}
,
and Line
5
is no longer true when the volume of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
exceeds
\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{d}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})
.

We claim that for all
t\geq d+2
,
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})\geq\frac{3}{2}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t-1})
.
By applying a nonsingular affine transformation, we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}=B_{2}^{d}
.
With a further rotation, we can assume the newly received point is
\bm{z}_{t}=2\bm{e}_{1}
.
From monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
we must have that
\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}\supseteq B_{2}^{d}\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}
.
Clearly every semi-axis of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
must have length
at least 1 in order to contain
\mathcal{E}_{t-1}
.
Observe that
\mathcal{E}_{t}
must also contain the segment connecting

-1\bm{e}_{1}
and
2\bm{e}_{1}
, and so at least one semi-axis must have length at least
\frac{3}{2}

(if not, the diameter of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
would be strictly less than
3
).
Hence as
\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}
equals the product of the length of the semi-axes of
\mathcal{E}_{t}
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})\geq\frac{3}{2}\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})
.
∎

For the analysis we define quantities
A_{t},P_{t}
associated with the sequence of ellipsoids for
1\leq t\leq T
:

A_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}},\quad P_{t}\coloneqq\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{t})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)

By the monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
, we have that
A_{t}
and
P_{t}
are both nondecreasing in
t
.
We first observe that the adversary guarantees that the final volume of the ellipsoid output by
\mathcal{A}
is large:

Claim 6.3
.

At the conclusion of
Algorithm

6.1
’s execution, we have

P_{T}\geq d\log\frac{R}{2}

Proof.

There are two ways that the adversary stops:
if the condition in Line
5
is no longer true,
or if Line
8
is reached.
If the former occurs, then we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{T})>\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\frac{R}{2}\cdot B_{2}^{d})
,
and clearly then
P_{T}\geq d\log\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)
.

In the latter stopping condition, the algorithm halts at time
T
when
\partial(c_{T}+2\mathcal{E}_{T})\cap R\cdot B_{2}^{d}=\varnothing
.
The sets
\partial(c_{T}+2\mathcal{E}_{T})
and
R\cdot B_{2}^{d}
can be disjoint in two cases:

c_{T}+2\mathcal{E}_{T}
and
R\cdot B_{2}^{d}
are disjoint;
or
R\cdot B_{2}^{d}\subseteq c_{T}+2\mathcal{E}_{t}
with the boundaries of both ellipsoids disjoint.
By the monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
, we have
1\cdot B_{2}^{d}\subseteq c_{T}+2\mathcal{E}_{T}
,
and so eliminate the former case.
But then
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(2\cdot\mathcal{E}_{T})\geq\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(R\cdot B_{2}^{d})=R^{d}\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})
, and taking logarithms on both sides yields the claim.
∎

Now in contrast to the upper bound where we essentially gave an algorithm for which
\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta P}
was upper bounded by a constant,
here we will show a constant lower bound on the same quantity for any monotone algorithm.

Claim 6.4
.

There exists a constant
C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}>0
such that if
A_{t}\geq d
, we have

A_{t+1}-A_{t}\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}(P_{t+1}-P_{t})

(6.1)

Observe that this lower bound requires
A_{t}\geq d
,
hence necessitating a first phase using the simplex,
whose optimal roundings show tightness for John’s theorem for general convex bodies.
In order to prove the lower bound we also need a second property, that
A_{t}
is large compared to
P_{t}
.

Claim 6.5
.

Let
0\leq\alpha\leq 1,\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{E}
be an ellipsoid such that

\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}\subseteq\Delta_{d}\subseteq\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}

then we have:

1.

\alpha\leq\frac{1}{d}

2.

\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)\leq O\left(\log(d)\cdot\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)

With the statements of these claims in hand, we are ready to prove the lower bound.

Proof of
5
.

It is clear that
\kappa(\mathsf{conv}\left(\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{T})\right)\leq R
, as for every
1\leq t\leq T
the adversary guarantees
1\leq\|\bm{z}_{t}\|\leq R
. So we focus on showing a lower bound on the quality of the approximation produced by
\mathcal{A}
.

As
\mathcal{A}
is monotone, after the end of Phase I
we must have that

\bm{c}_{d+1}+\alpha_{d+1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{d+1}\subseteq\Delta_{d}\subseteq\bm{c}_{d+1}+\mathcal{E}_{d+1}

(6.2)

Now because
\mathcal{E}_{d+1}
satisfies the conditions of
6.5
,
we get using the definition
A_{d+1}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{d+1}}
that
A_{t}\geq A_{d+1}\geq d
for any
t\geq d+1
.
Then we can apply
6.4
for every
t\geq d+1
until termination of the algorithm:

\displaystyle A_{d+2}-A_{d+1}

\displaystyle\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\left(P_{d+2}-P_{d+1}\right)

\displaystyle A_{d+3}-A_{d+2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\left(P_{d+3}-P_{d+2}\right)

\displaystyle~\vdots

\displaystyle A_{T-1}-A_{T-2}

\displaystyle\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\left(P_{T-1}-P_{T-2}\right)

\displaystyle A_{T}-A_{T-1}

\displaystyle\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\left(P_{T}-P_{T-1}\right)

Summing these inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=d+1}^{T-1}A_{t+1}-A_{t}\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\left(\sum_{t=d+1}^{T-1}P_{t+1}-P_{t}\right)

Both sides of this inequality are telescoping sums, so simplifying we get

A_{T}\geq A_{d+1}+C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}(P_{T}-P_{d+1})

(6.3)

Again because we can apply
6.5
for
\mathcal{E}_{d+1}
, we have
P_{d+1}\leq O(\log(d)\cdot A_{d+1})
, which along with (
6.3
) yields

A_{T}\geq A_{d+1}+\Omega(P_{T}-\log(d)\cdot A_{d+1})\geq\Omega(P_{T}-\log(d)\cdot A_{d+1})

Thus we have

A_{T}\geq\Omega(\max(A_{d+1},P_{T}-\log(d)\cdot A_{d+1}))\geq\Omega\left(\frac{P_{T}}{\log(d)}\right)

and we get the desired bound using
6.3
.
∎

Our proof of
6.4

relies on a symmetrization argument to a reduced case (essentially two-dimensional, like for our algorithms). We now define this reduced case, and related quantities.

Definition 6.6
.

In the reduced case, the previous outer and inner ellipsoids are given by
B_{2}^{d},\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{d}
, and the received point is
\bm{z}=2\bm{e}_{1}
.
The next outer and inner ellipsoids are given by
c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}},c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}}

for
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathbf{M}=\mathsf{diag}\left(a,b,b,\ldots,b,b\right)
for
a,b\geq 1
.
We let
\Delta A=\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
\Delta P=\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)
.

Note that the update in this reduced case is monotone if

B_{2}^{d}\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\subseteq c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}}

and
c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right)
.

Now we state the lower bound on
\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta P}
in this setting, which is established in
Section

6.2
.
It is exactly the bound of
6.4
in this special case.

Claim 6.7
.

In the reduced case, for any monotone update
c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}},c\cdot\bm{e}_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{M}}
when
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\frac{1}{d}
we have

\frac{\Delta A}{\Delta P}\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}

(6.4)

We now give the symmmetrization argument that shows that the above bound in the special case
implies the bound in the general case.

Proof of
6.4
.

By the monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
, we have
(\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t})\cup\{\bm{z}_{t+1}\}\subseteq\bm{c}_{t+1}+\mathcal{E}_{t+1}

and
\bm{c}_{t+1}+\alpha_{t+1}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t+1}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\bm{c}_{t}+\alpha_{t}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{t})\cup\{\bm{z}_{t+1}\}\right)
.
Without loss of generality we assume that
\bm{c}_{t}+\mathcal{E}_{t}=B_{2}^{d}
and
\bm{z}_{t+1}=2\cdot\bm{e}_{1}
;
we do this by applying a nonsingular affine transformation that maps
\bm{c}_{t}
to the origin and
\mathcal{E}_{t}
to
B_{2}^{d}
, then apply a rotation
that maps
\bm{z}_{t+1}
to
2\cdot e_{1}
.
Let
\bm{c}=\bm{c}_{t+1}
,
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{t+1}
, and
\alpha=\alpha_{t+1}
.
Summarizing the conditions guaranteed by the monotonicity of
\mathcal{A}
, we have that
B_{2}^{d}\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\subseteq\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}

and
\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right)
.

To perform the reduction to the two-dimensional case, we apply a sequence of volume-preserving symmetrizations to the new inner and outer ellipsoids; these symmetrizations will also ensure that the update remains monotone.
We will first apply two Steiner symmetrizations. The first of these Steiner symmetrizations transforms the ellipsoids so that their center lies on the
\bm{e}_{1}
-axis. The second ensures that the ellipsoids have a semi-axis that is parallel to
\bm{e}_{1}
.
Then, by a final symmetrization step we can transform the ellipsoids into bodies of revolution about
\bm{e}_{1}
.
At that point it will suffice to consider the two-dimensional reduced case.

Let
\bm{c}^{\prime}
be the projection of
\bm{c}
onto the
\bm{e}_{1}
-axis.
The goal of the first symmetrization step is to transform
\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}
to
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
so that
\bm{c}^{\prime}
lies on the
\bm{e}_{1}
axis. If
\bm{c}=\bm{c}^{\prime}
then we do not need to do anything, otherwise we apply Steiner symmmetrization and consider
S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}+\mathcal{E})
.
By
3.4
this is still an ellipsoid,
and we also have that the center of
S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}+\mathcal{E})
is actually
\bm{c}^{\prime}
;
thus we may write
S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}+\mathcal{E})=\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
for some
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
.
Further, we have that
S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E})=\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, as the Steiner symmetrization acts similarly on the scaled version of
\mathcal{E}
.
To show that the update is still monotone, we observe that
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}+\mathcal{E})\subseteq S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right))
.
But by
3.5
and that
\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}\perp\bm{e}_{1}
,
\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right)
is invariant under the symmetrization
S_{\bm{c}-\bm{c}^{\prime}}
and so we still have the inclusion
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right)
.
The ‘outer’ inclusion

B_{2}^{d}\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\subseteq\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
follows in the same way.

We now apply the second and final Steiner symmetrization. Let
\bm{r}
be the rightmost point of
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
along
\bm{e}_{1}
; i.e.
\bm{r}=\arg\max_{\bm{r}\in\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}\left\langle\bm{r},\bm{e}_{1}\right\rangle
.
Also let
\bm{r}^{\prime}
be its projection along the
\bm{e}_{1}
-axis;
if
\bm{r}=\bm{r}^{\prime}
we again do not need to perform this symmetrization step, otherwise
the Steiner symmetrization we apply is
S_{\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime})
.
Since
\bm{c}^{\prime}
is at the midpoint of
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathbb{R}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})
the center of the new ellipsoid is still
\bm{c}^{\prime}
,
so we may write
S_{\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
and similarly

S_{\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime})=\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
.
Like for the previous symmetrization, the fact that
\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}\perp\bm{e}_{1}

means that both inclusions of the monotone update are preserved.
Note finally that
\bm{r}^{\prime}
is the rightmost point of
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}

and that the tangent plane of
\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
at
\bm{r}^{\prime}
is orthogonal to the line segment
\overline{\bm{c}^{\prime}\bm{r}^{\prime}}
,
so
\bm{e}_{1}
is a semi-axis of
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
.

Our last transformation is a symmetrization of a different form, to turn
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
into a body of revolution.
Let
\sigma_{1}
be the length of the semi-axis
\bm{e}_{1}
of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
, and
\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
be the lengths of the other semi-axes of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
.
We let
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
be the ellipsoid that has a
\bm{e}_{1}
as a semi-axis of length
\sigma_{1}
,
and where every other semi-axis of
\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
has length
\sigma^{\prime}\coloneqq\left(\prod_{i=2}^{d}\sigma_{i}\right)^{1/(d-1)}
.
Clearly
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
is now a body of revolution about
\bm{e}_{1}
whose volume is the same as that of
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime}
(and hence also of
\bm{c}+\mathcal{E}
).
Note that
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
is also now a body of revolution.
Since
\sigma^{\prime}\geq\min_{2\leq i\leq d}\sigma_{i}
we have
B_{2}^{d}\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\subseteq\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
, and correspondingly since
\sigma^{\prime}\leq\max_{2\leq i\leq d}\sigma_{i}
we have that
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}\subseteq\mathsf{conv}\left((\alpha_{t}\cdot B_{2}^{d})\cup\{2\bm{e}_{1}\}\right)
.

Clearly
\bm{c}^{\prime}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime},\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime}
now adhere to the reduced case of
Definition

6.6
.
Since the update is monotone as well (and still
\alpha\leq 1/d
) we can apply
6.7
.
As
\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime\prime\prime})=\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E})
, this means we have

A_{t+1}-A_{t}\geq C_{\ref{eqn:lb_step_main}}\cdot(P_{t+1}-P_{t})

as desired.
∎

Proof of
6.5
.

For the first property, this is exactly the well-known fact that the best ellipsoidal rounding for the simplex
\Delta_{d}
(see e.g.
[
How97
, Remark 1.1]
) has approximation factor
d
.

Now we show the second property.
Again because the ball rounds the simplex
\Delta_{d}
with approximation factor
d
, we have

\frac{1}{d}\cdot\Delta_{d}\subseteq B_{2}^{d}\subseteq\Delta_{d}

As a result of this, we have

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\Delta)}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(d\cdot B_{2}^{d})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq O(d\log d)

And so

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\mathcal{E})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(\bm{c}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{E})}{\operatorname{\mathsf{vol}}(B_{2}^{d})}\right)+d\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\leq O\left(d\log\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\text{as }\alpha\leq\frac{1}{d}

To establish the second property, it remains to show

.
Observe that

is increasing for
x\geq e
, so we have

\frac{d}{\log d}\leq O\left(\frac{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}}{\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}})}\right)

for all
d\geq 2
as
d\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{\alpha}}
.
Rearranging gives the desired inequality and thus the second property.
∎
