Symmetric KL-divergence by Stein’s Method
Liu-Quan Yao, Song-Hao Liu

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the symmetric KL-divergence between the sum of independent variables and a Gaussian distribution, and obtain a convergence rate of order O ⁡ ( ln ⁡ n n ) O\left(\frac{\ln n}{\sqrt{n}}\right) . The proof is based on Stein’s method. The convergence rate of order O ⁡ ( 1 n ) O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right) and O ⁡ ( 1 n ) O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) are also obtained under higher moment condition.



1
Introduction

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT), as one of the fundamental principles in probability theory, elucidates a pivotal truth. Specifically, it asserts that for a sequence of independent random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots
, under the Lindeberg condition, the standardized partial sum

W_{n}=\frac{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}(X_{k}-\mathbb{E}X_{k})}{\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{k})}}

(1.1)

tends towards a standard Gaussian random variable in distribution. A classical proof of this theorem relies on the utilization of characteristic functions, while alternative methodologies are also capable of deriving the CLT, potentially leading to even stronger convergence. In this paper, we focus on the entropic central limit theorem.

1.1
Entropic Central Limit Theorem

An interesting method to prove CLT is by using the tools of information theory, and it is called an entropic CLT (
[
13
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
11
]

et al.
), and the KL-divergence is the ’core metric’
which is defined as follows.

Definition 1.1

Consider two random variables
X,Y
that
X\ll Y
with distribution
P_{X},P_{Y}
respectively, the Kullback-Leibler divergence(KL-divergence) is defined as

\text{KL}(X\|Y):=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left(\ln\frac{dP_{X}(X)}{dP_{Y}(X)}\right),

(1.2)

particularly, when
X,Y
are random variables on
\mathbb{R}
with density functions
p_{X},p_{Y}
, then the KL-divergence is defined as

\text{KL}(X\|Y):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{X}(x)\ln\frac{p_{X}(x)}{p_{Y}(x)}dx.

(1.3)

Let
X
be any absolutely continuous random variable
(w.r.t.
Lebesgue measure) and set
Y=G_{X}
, which is a Gaussian random variable with
\mathbb{E}G_{X}=\mathbb{E}X
and
\text{Var}(G_{X})=\text{Var}(X)
, then the well-known equality
\text{KL}(X\|G_{X})=h(G_{X})-h(X)
holds, where

h(V):=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{V}(x)\ln p_{V}(x)dx

(1.4)

is defined as the differential entropy of the absolutely continuous random variable
V
with density
p_{V}
. Furthermore, the Pinsker’s inequality from
[
5
]
shows that the the KL-divergence is an upper bound of total variation distance,
i.e.

\text{KL}(X\|Y)\geq\frac{1}{2}\|P_{X}-P_{Y}\|^{2}_{TV},

(1.5)

for any random variables
X
and
Y
such that
X\ll Y
, where
X\ll Y
means the distribution of
X
is absolutely continuous
w.r.t.
that of
Y
. After taking
Y=G_{X}
in (
1.5
), we concluded that the KL-divergence is able to measure the asymptotic Gaussianity, which implies a stronger convergence compared with classical CLT in weak convergence sense.

We first consider the traditional independent and identically distributed (
i.i.d.
) case,
i.e.

X_{1},X_{2},\cdots
in (
1.1
) are
i.i.d.
with means 0 and variances
\sigma^{2}
, then
W_{n}=(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n})/\sqrt{n\sigma^{2}}
.
Started by Linnik (1959)
[
13
]
, the entropy method catches the following two facts.

1.

h(X)\leq h(G_{X})
.

2.

The entropy of the sum of
i.i.d.
random variables
h(W_{2n})
is increasing about
n
, because we have the well-known entropy jump inequality:

h\left(\frac{X+Y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\geq\frac{h(X)+h(Y)}{2},

(1.6)

where the equality holds iff
X
and
Y
are Gaussian with same variance.

Thus,
\text{KL}(W_{n}\|G_{W_{n}})
converges. After more precise estimation for the difference
h\left((X+Y)/\sqrt{2}\right)-(h(X)+h(Y))/2
called
entropy jump
, Carlen (1991)
[
6
]
obtained an entropic CLT by proving

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\text{KL}(W_{n}\|G)=0,

(1.7)

where
W_{n}
is an
i.i.d.
sum with
\mathbb{E}W_{n}=0
and
\text{Var}(W_{n})=1
and
G
is standard Gaussian. Johnson
et al.
(2004)
[
11
]
used the theory of projection in
L^{2}
space to obtain an estimate on the decrease of Fisher information, which is closely related with entropy, defined for any random variable
X
with absolutely continuous density
f
:

J(X):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}{f(x)}dx.

(1.8)

They proved that

J\left(\frac{X+X^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\leq\frac{2R^{*}_{X}}{\sigma^{2}+2R^{*}_{X}}J(X)

(1.9)

holds for any random variable
X
and its independent copy
X^{\prime}
, with absolutely continuous density, variance
\sigma^{2}
and restricted Poincaré constant
R_{X}^{*}
, which is defined as

R_{X}^{*}:=\sup_{g\in\{f|\text{Var}(f(X))>0,\mathbb{E}f(X)=0,\mathbb{E}f^{2}(X)<\infty,\mathbb{E}f^{\prime}(X)=0\}}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}g^{2}(X)}{\mathbb{E}g^{\prime}(X)^{2}}.

(1.10)

Notice that (
1.9
) immediately leads to a lower bound on the entropy jump using de Bruijn identity. Furthermore, the convergence rate of
h(W_{n})
was also estimated in
[
11
]
by using (
1.9
).
Bobkov
et al.
(2014)
[
4
]
proved a Berry-Esseen type bound for entropic CLT:

Lemma 1.1

Denote
D(X)=\text{KL}(X\|G_{X}).
Consider independent random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
and

W_{n}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{i})}}.

If
\sup_{i}D(X_{i})\leq D<\infty
, then

D(W_{n})\leq ce^{62D}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|X_{i}-\mathbb{E}X_{i}|^{4}/(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{i}))^{2},

where
c
is a constant.

It may be thought as a "theoretical better convergence rate" since it implies classical Berry-Esseen bound due to Pinsker’s inequality.

1.2
Symmetric KL-divergence

Compared to KL-divergence, the symmetric KL-divergence, defined as follows, possesses the inherent property of symmetry, rendering it applicable in a wide range of scenarios as a measure of similarity for distributions.

Definition 1.2

Consider two random variables,
X
and
Y
, which are absolutely continuous with each other, i.e.
X\ll Y
and
Y\ll X
. The symmetric KL-divergence is defined as:

d(X,Y):=\text{KL}(X\|Y)+\text{KL}(Y\|X).

(1.11)

Remark 1.1

Since the KL-divergence can be defined by distribution functions (or random vectors), so does
d
. Specifically, given two distribution
P,Q
,
d(P,Q):=\text{KL}(P\|Q)+\text{KL}(Q\|P)
. Clearly, if
X,Y
has distribution
P_{X},P_{Y}
, then
d(X,Y)=d(P_{X},P_{Y})
.

To the best of our knowledge,
[
23
]
showed the first application of the symmetric KL-divergence as a spatiogram similarity measure in 2011, whose idea was motivated by Vasconcelos (2004)
[
21
]
, and then the symmetric KL-divergence has been used in plenty of fields until now.
As discussed by Domke (2021)
[
9
]
, when it comes to interpreting diagnostic inference problems, certain methods like Laplace’s method prove impractical without the inclusion of sample distribution information, in addition to a mere set of samples. Moreover, the KL-divergence measure provides limited insight into the absolute error present in approximate algorithms. However, the symmetric KL-divergence overcomes these shortcomings, thus serving as a valuable error measure in simulation-based interpret diagnostics. Andriamanalimanana (2019)
[
2
]
concluded that the inherent symmetry advantages of the symmetric KL-divergence, when compared to the KL-divergence, further extends its application as a numerical score within anomaly detection systems. In the field of text classification, Chen
et al.
(2018)
[
7
]
discovered that centroid estimation based on the symmetric KL-divergence outperforms the conventional naive Bayes method, which employs average estimated centroids or relies solely on KL-divergence.

In the realm of information theory, symmetric KL-divergence has a strong connection with channel theory. Aminian
et al
.(2014)
[
1
]
demonstrated that for any channel
W=P(y|x)
, where
x
represents the input signal with alphabet
\mathcal{X}
,and
y
represents the output with alphabet
\mathcal{Y}
, the capacity
C
of
W
is bounded above by the expression:

C\leq\max_{P_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}d(P_{xy},P_{x}\times P_{y}),

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is the set of all distribution on
\mathcal{X}
,
P_{x}
means a distribution of the input signal and
P_{y}
means a distribution of the output signal,
P_{xy}
means the joint distribution of the signals (Since channel
W
is fixed,
P_{y}
is uniquely determined by
P_{x}
). Specifically, in the case of an add white Gaussian noise channel with a fixed signal to noise ratio (SNR), we have

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mbox{SNR})\leq\max_{P_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}d(P_{xy},P_{x}\times P_{y})

(1.12)

\displaystyle=d(P_{xy},P_{x}\times P_{y})=\text{Var}(X_{in})/\sigma^{2}=\mbox{SNR},\;\;\forall P_{x}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),

(1.13)

where
X_{in}
is the random variable with distribution
P_{x}
,

\sigma^{2}
is the variance of the channel noise. These observations are utilized in diffusion-based molecular communication for capacity estimation, as discussed in
[
1
]
and
[
14
]
. Furthermore, the symmetric KL-divergence is also called as the bidirectional KL-divergence in neural networks and deep learning fields, considered as a loss function in the corresponding optimization model recently (
[
12
]
,
[
17
]
).

The combination of Gaussian distribution and symmetric KL-divergence has also been explored in various contexts, such as
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
. For instance, Welandawe
et al
.(2022)
[
22
]
employed symmetric KL-divergence to detect convergence in Black-box variational inference in Machine Learning. They demonstrated that the Wasserstein distance, often used in MCMC diagnostic for detecting stationarity, can be controlled by the symmetric KL-divergence under the exponentially controlled assumption. Given that this assumption holds when discussing the symmetric KL-divergence
d(X,\hat{G})
, where
\hat{G}
is Gaussian, Welandawe
et al
. focused on the symmetric KL-divergence for two different Gaussian random variables and deduced the convergence order as the two distributions gradually approach each other.

In summary, symmetric KL-divergence is a common measure that indicates the similarity between two distributions. Therefore, it is natural to consider symmetric KL-divergence as a standard for measuring the distance of a random variable with Gaussian distribution. According to the definition and Pinsker’s inequality (
1.5
), we have:

d(X,G)\geq\text{KL}(X\|G),\;\;d(X,G)\geq\|P_{X}-\Phi\|^{2}_{TV},

(1.14)

where
G
denotes the standard Gaussian random variable,
\Phi
is the distribution of
G
and the notations will be used throughout the paper. Consequently, the CLT under symmetric KL-divergence implies a much stronger convergence compared to KL-divergence, total variation distance, or Wasserstein distance, as stated in
[
22
]
.

By discussing the CLT under symmetric KL-divergence, we not only establish a uniform upper bound for the aforementioned convergence measures, but also cater to situations that specifically require symmetric KL-divergence as a similarity metric, since its wide range of applications. Despite some research being conducted on the convergence properties of symmetric KL-divergence, such as
[
23
]
and
[
22
]
, little attention has been given to the CLT or other probability convergence theorems. This could be attributed to the challenging analysis of the term
KL(G\|X)
. Specifically, this term cannot be easily expressed in terms of entropy like
KL(X\|G_{X})=h(G_{X})-h(X)
, and requires X to have a strictly positive density
p(x)>0
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

In this paper, we use Stein’s method (as introduced in Section
2
) to overcome the untractable problem of symmetric KL-divergence. Since we aim to deduce the CLT under symmetric KL-divergence, the paper only focus on

d(W_{n},G)=\text{KL}(W_{n}\|G)+\text{KL}(G\|W_{n}),

(1.15)

where
W_{n}
is a normalized sum of independent random variables with
\mathbb{E}W_{n}=0
and
\text{Var}(W_{n})=1
. Given that following regular assumption is valid:

Assumption 1.1

Given random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
with density functions
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,\rho_{n}
. Assume that

\exists\delta_{1}\in(\frac{1}{4},1)
and set
A_{n}\subset\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
s.t
\delta_{1}\leq\frac{|A_{n}|}{n}
and

\rho_{i}(x)\geq l_{1}e^{-l_{2}\frac{x^{2}}{2}},\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\forall i\in A_{n},

(1.16)

for some
l_{1}>0,l_{2}>0
.

Some remarks on Assumption
1.1
are discussed in Section
2
. Our main result can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 1.1

Given a random variable sequence
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n},\cdots
s.t. for any
i=1,2,\cdots,

\mathbb{E}X_{i}=0,\sup_{i}J(X_{i})<J<\infty,\sup_{i}\mathbb{E}|X_{i}|^{4+\delta_{0}}<M<\infty
for some
\delta_{0}>0
.
Let

W_{n}
be a normalized sum of independent random variables, i.e.

W_{n}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{i})}}.

If Assumption
1.1
holds for
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{n}
with
(\delta_{1},l_{1},l_{2})
when
n
is large enough,
then there exists a
constant
C=C(J,M,\delta_{0},\delta_{1},l_{1},l_{2})
such that the symmetric KL-divergence is bounded
by

d(W_{n},G)\leq C\frac{\ln n}{\sqrt{n}},

(1.17)

where
G
is the standard Gaussian random variable.

Additionally, the well-known local central limit theorem says that the total variation distance between
W_{n}
and standard Gaussian random variable
G
meets the order
O(1/\sqrt{n})
(
e.g.
Lemma
2.3
). Thanks to (
1.14
), we conclude that the convergence rate of
d(W_{n},G)
is at least
O(1/n)
.
However, Theorem
1.1
still remains a gap of the rate estimation between
O(\ln n/\sqrt{n})
and
O(1/n)
. In order to overcome this gap, we add the finite exponential moment condition and combine the result in
[
4
]
, and then we obtain the ’best rate estimation’
O(1/n)
for symmetrical KL-divergence

(see Section
4
for details).

2
Basic Ideas

2.1
Stein’s Method

Stein (1972)
[
19
]
discovered that a random variable
\hat{G}
follows a Gaussian distribution
\hat{G}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
if and only if

\mathbb{E}(\hat{G}f(\hat{G}))=\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(f^{\prime}(\hat{G}))

(2.1)

for any absolutely continuous function
f
for which the above expectation exists. Hence, (
2.1
) can be regarded as a criterion for the "Gaussianity" of a random variable. Specifically, when
|\mathbb{E}(Xf(X))-\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}(f^{\prime}(X))|
is small for any absolutely continuous function
f
, we may infer that
X
is close to the Gaussian random variable
\hat{G}
.

Without loss of generality, standard Gaussian random variable
G
is considered after now. Building upon (
2.1
) and Taylor expansion, Goldstein and Reinert (1997)
[
10
]
presented the ensuing inequality for any absolutely continuous function
g
with
\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\infty
and a random variable
W
with
\mathbb{E}W=0
and
\text{Var}(W)=1
:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}g(W)-\mathbb{E}g(G)|

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}(f_{g}^{\prime}(W)-Wf_{g}(W))|

(2.2)

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{E}(f_{g}^{\prime}(W)-f^{\prime}_{g}(W^{*}))|

(2.3)

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{g}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}|\Delta|,

(2.4)

where
W^{*}
represents the zero bias
1
1

1

Given a random variable
V
with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
, we say
V^{*}
is the zero bias of
V
if
\sigma^{2}\mathbb{E}f^{\prime}(V^{*})=\mathbb{E}[Vf(V)]
for all absolutely continuous function
f
for which these expectations exist.
of
W
,
\Delta:=W^{*}-W
, and
f_{g}
is the solution of Stein’s equation with a given function
g
as follows:

f_{g}^{\prime}(w)-wf_{g}(w)=g(w)-\mathbb{E}g(G).

(2.5)

It should be noted, as proved by Stein (1986)
[
18
, Lemma 3]
(see also Rai
\check{\mbox{c}}
(2004)
[
16
, Lemma 1]
), that upper bounds exist for the solution
f_{g}
of Stein’s equation generated by
g
, as follows.

Lemma 2.1

If
g
is an absolutely continuous function, then

\|f_{g}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty},\;\;\|f_{g}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2/\pi}\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty},\;\;\|f_{g}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 2\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

(2.6)

where
f_{g}
is the solution of Stein’s equation with respect to the function
g
, as (
2.5
).

Therefore, we can promptly obtain a CLT using Stein’s method, which can be summarized as follows.

Lemma 2.2

For any absolutely continuous function
g
with
\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}<\infty
and random variable
W
with
\mathbb{E}W=0
and
\text{Var}(W)=1
:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}g(W)-\mathbb{E}g(G)|\leq 2\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{E}|\Delta|,

(2.7)

where
G
denotes the standard Gaussian random variable,
W^{*}
represents the zero bias of
W
, and
\Delta:=W^{*}-W
.
In particular, when
W=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}
represents the sum of independent random variables
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots\xi_{n}
with
\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}=0
for all
i
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(\xi_{i})=1
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}g(W)-\mathbb{E}g(G)|\leq 3\|g^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}|\xi_{i}|^{3}.

(2.8)

The proof can be found in
[
10
, Section 3]
(see also
[
18
, Corollary 1]
and
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
).

Thanks to the feature abstraction idea, as (
2.1
) for Gaussian random variable, Stein’s method can not only address asymptotic Gaussianity for independent sum cases, but it can also be applied to more general cases and even to cases with other limiting distributions, such as the Poisson distribution, as (
2.1
) becomes other appropriate expressions. Despite being a powerful tool, Stein’s method still encounters challenges when the function
g
lacks a bounded first-order derivative, and
g
may depend on the step parameter
n
, which tends to infinity.

In summary, Stein’s method extracts the feature of a Gaussian random variable that (
2.1
) holds, while the entropy method may note that a Gaussian random variable has maximum entropy when its variance is fixed. These two ideas capture the characteristics of the Gaussian distribution and then deduce a CLT, thus it is natural to consider them together.

2.2
Symmetrical KL-divergence in Stein Form

In order to ensure
\text{KL}(G\|X)
in symmetric KL-divergence be well-defined, we always consider a random variable with strictly positive and absolutely continuous density unless otherwise stated.

Given a random variable
X
with mean
0
, variance
1
and density
p
, denote
g=\ln p
, then we directly obtain

\displaystyle d(X,G)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}[p(x)\ln p(x)-p(x)\ln\phi(x)+\phi(x)\ln\phi(x)-\phi(x)\ln p(x)]dx

(2.9)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}g(X)-\mathbb{E}g(G),

where
\phi
is the density function of standard Gaussian random variable
G
. Replace
X
by
W_{n}
defined in Theorem
1.1
, we have

g^{\prime}(x)=\rho_{W_{n}}(x)=\frac{p^{\prime}_{n}(x)}{p_{n}(x)},

(2.10)

where
p_{n}
is the density of
W_{n}
, and
\rho_{W_{n}}
is the score function of
W_{n}
. Undoubtedly, it is impossible to impose a uniform bound on the score functions
\rho_{W_{n}}(x)
, hence we cannot apply Lemma
2.1
directly. However, the local limit theorem presented below can address the issue of unbounded derivatives in our Stein function model (
2.9
). Note that for the rest of this paper, we will abbreviate
d(W_{n},G)
as
d(W_{n})
.

2.3
A Core Property of the Density of
W_{n}

Clearly, to deal with the convergence of
d(W_{n})
, we need to focus on the density function
p_{n}
of the partial sum
W_{n}
. For the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we weaken Assumption
1.1
by the following property of the density of partial sum.

Property 2.1

Given the density
p_{n}
of
W_{n}
, where

W_{n}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{n}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{i})}},

there exists
a>1,k_{1}>0,k_{2}>0,s>0,
and
v\geq 0
, s.t.

\frac{a}{a-1}\Big(\frac{1}{2}+s\Big)<2,

(2.11)

and

p_{n}(x)\geq k_{1}e^{-n^{s}k_{2}e^{\frac{x^{2}}{2a}}},\;\;\forall|x|>v\sqrt{\ln n}.

(2.12)

Remark 2.1

Obviously,
Property
2.1
can be written as limit form: for the density
p_{n}
of
W_{n}
, we assume that
\exists a>1,s>0
, s.t. for any large
n
,

\liminf_{|x|\to\infty}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2a}}\ln p_{n}(x)}{n^{s}}>-\infty.

(2.13)

We have proven that under the Assumption
1.1
, Property
2.1
holds for
W_{n}
immediately (see
A
),
henceforth, we only focus on the Property
2.1
rather than Assumption
1.1
. A remark for Property
2.1
is discussed as follow.

Remark 2.2

In order to make

d(W_{n})<\infty,
we need the term

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\ln p_{n}(x)dx
to be finite, i.e.

\mathbb{E}|\ln p_{n}(G)|<\infty.

(2.14)

Since
|\phi\ln p_{n}|\leq p_{n}\phi\leq p_{n}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
when
\ln p_{n}>0
, we only need a lower bound for
p_{n}
. Note that if on an infinite Lebesgue measure set
S
,

p_{n}(x)\leq e^{-e^{x^{2}/2}},\;\;\forall x\in S,

then
\mathbb{E}|\ln p_{n}(G)|\geq\int_{S}e^{x^{2}/2}\phi(x)dx=\infty.

Thus to a certain extent, Property
2.1
is considered as a quiet necessary condition for (
2.14
).

2.4
Local Limit Theorem

The local limit theorem elucidates the distance between the density function
p_{n}
of
W_{n}
and the density function
\phi
of a standard Gaussian random variable
G
. By virtue of its meticulous expansion,
p_{n}
can be approximated by
\phi
and thereby attain a more manageable scenario. In this section, we present the local limit theorem that is utilized in our proof for Theorem
1.1
.

Using the classical results in
[
20
]
: Theorem 14 in Chapter VII, (1.10) in Chapter VI, and the statement in Page 173, and (6.13) in
[
3
]
, we conclude that,

Lemma 2.3

Consider a sequence of independent random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,
with densities
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\cdots,
and
\mathbb{E}X_{i}=0,\forall i=1,2,\cdots
. Denote

B_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{Var}(X_{i}).

(2.15)

If the following conditions hold:

1.

4
Some Remarks on Rate Improvement

In this section, we discuss some methods to improve the convergence rate

in Theorem

4.1
Assumptions for
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
Convergence Rate

If we have
\sup_{n}\mathbb{E}W_{n}^{6}<\infty

) can be bounded by a constant not depending on

\displaystyle+\|f_{h_{1}}^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\xi_{i}^{2}\mathbb{E}|(\xi_{i}-\xi_{i}^{*})\xi_{i}|+\gamma\|f_{h_{1}}^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\frac{1}{n},

is a random variable between

and

and

.

to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbb{E}|\xi_{i}|^{3})(\mathbb{E}|h^{\prime}_{1}(W_{n}-\xi_{i})|+\mathbb{E}|h_{1}(G)|\mathbb{E}|W_{n}-\xi_{i}|+\mathbb{E}|(W_{n}-\xi_{i})h_{1}^{\prime}(W_{n}-\xi_{i})|+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}W_{n}^{4}})

Without Property

4.3
Discussion for

If we consider function

In fact, under the conditions in Theorem
1.1
, the convergence rate of KL-divergence

\displaystyle\text{KL}(W_{n}\|G)

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\int_{|x|>u\sqrt{\ln n}}p_{n}(x)\ln p_{n}(x)dx-\int_{|x|>u\sqrt{\ln n}}p_{n}(x)\ln\phi(x)dx

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;+\int_{|x|<u\sqrt{\ln n}}p_{n}(x)\ln(p_{n}(x)/\phi(x))dx

(4.9)

\displaystyle:=\hat{R}_{1}-\hat{R}_{2}+\hat{R}_{3}

is
\frac{1}{n}
since the results in
[
4
]
and the following well known inequality in
[
8
]
.

Lemma 4.1

Given
\xi
,
\mathbb{E}\xi=0,\text{Var}(\xi)<\infty
, then

(4.11)

Additionally,
\hat{R}_{1}
can be estimate by Property
2.1
and
\hat{R}_{3}
may be almost canceled by the term

\int_{|x|\leq u\sqrt{\ln n}}\phi(x)\ln(p_{n}(x)/\phi(x))dx

. Note that the remaining term

has the same order of

On the other hand, we can add stronger moment condition to overcome the unknown speed of
