Introduction

Frames, introduced by Duffin and Shaeffer in
[
7
]
, have recently received great attention due to their wide range of applications in both mathematics and engineering science. The classical definition of a frame is that of a sequence of vectors in a Hilbert space
H
that satisfies a double inequality involving the scalar product and the norm maps. Specifically, and generalizing slightly from sequences to families indexed by general sets (possibly uncountably infinite), we have

Definition 1
.

We say that a family of vectors
u=(u_{i})_{i\in I}
with
u_{i}\in H
for all
i\in I
is a classical (or discrete) frame if

\exists A,B>0,\forall v\in H,A\|v\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I}|\langle v,u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|v\|^{2}.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{I}^{H}
the set of
I
-indexed frames with values in
H
.

Remark 1
.

Classical (or discrete) Bessel families are the families for which only the second inequality holds.

Frames have been generalized in different directions. For example, we have frames associated to measure spaces (also called continuous frames)

2
The «test space and pairing» idea: application to the
\ell^{2}
and
L^{2}
spaces

Let
I
be some set and
\mathcal{H}=(H_{i})_{i\in I}
be a family of Hilbert spaces over the same field
\mathbb{F}
:=
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. We define
\ell^{2}(I;\mathcal{H}):=\{u=(u_{i})_{i\in I};u_{i}\in H_{i},\sum_{i\in I}||u_{i}||^{2}_{H_{i}}<\infty\}
. We endow
\ell^{2}(I;\mathcal{H})
with the pointwise scalar product:
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i\in I}\langle u_{i},v_{i}\rangle_{H_{i}}
. It is well-known that
\ell^{2}(I;\mathcal{H})
is a Hilbert space. It is also a (vector) subspace of the product
\bigtimes_{i\in I}H_{i}
. We now fix the family
\{H_{i}\}_{i\in I}
such that each
H_{i}
is equal to the same Hilbert space
H
. We define
\ell^{2}(I;H):=\ell^{2}(I;\mathcal{H})
. Similarly, for every measure space
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
, we have the Hilbert space
L^{2}(X,\mu;H)
of Bochner square-integrable functions from
X
to
H
.

In the frame inequality given in the introduction, the variable
x
runs through the Hilbert space
H
. Therefore,
H
may be seen as a «test space» over which we are testing the frame inequality. Now, we could raise the following question: what happens of the notion of a frame if we replace
H
with some other test space and change the map by which we pair an element of this test space to the frame? In the classical case, this «pairing» is

P:(v;(u_{i})_{i\in I})\in H\times\mathcal{F}_{I}^{H}\mapsto(\langle v,u_{i}\rangle)_{i\in I}\in\ell^{2}(I;\mathbb{F}),

which corresponds closely to the expression of the analysis operator
T_{u}
of
u
:

T_{u}:v\in H\mapsto(\langle v,u_{i}\rangle)_{i\in I}\in\ell^{2}(I;\mathbb{F}),

In this section, we first consider the case of the test space
\ell^{2}(J;H)
with the pairing

P^{J}:((v_{j})_{j\in J};(u_{i})_{i\in I})\in\ell^{2}(J;H)\times\mathcal{F}_{I}^{H}\mapsto(\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle)_{(i,j)\in I\times J}\in\ell^{2}(I\times J;\mathbb{F}),

which now agrees with the expression of an analysis operator
T_{u}^{J}
of
u
in
\ell^{2}(J;H)
:

T_{u}^{J}:(v_{j})_{j\in J}\in\ell^{2}(J;H)\mapsto(\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle)_{(i,j)\in I\times J}\in\ell^{2}(I\times J;\mathbb{F}),

and even more generally, if
u
is a frame
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
in
H
associated to the measure space
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
, we consider the test space
L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)
with the pairing

\displaystyle P^{(Y,\nu)}\text{ : }

\displaystyle((v_{y})_{y\in Y};(u_{x})_{x\in X})\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)\times\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{H}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle)_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}\in L^{2}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu;\mathbb{F}),

which matches the expression of an analysis operator
T_{u}^{Y,\nu}
of
u
in
L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)
:

T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}:(v_{y})_{y\in Y}\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)\mapsto(\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle)_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}\in L^{2}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu;\mathbb{F}).

The two following propositions assert that, in the
\ell^{2}
and
L^{2}
cases, the resulting notions of extended frames are the same as the usual ones. We start with the
\ell^{2}
case ; however, this case can obviously be seen as a particular case of
L^{2}
case.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
u=(u_{i})_{i\in I}
with
u_{i}\in H
for all
i\in I
. Then the following are equivalent.

1.

\exists A,B>0:\forall v\in H,A\|v\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I}|\langle v,u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|v\|^{2}

(i.e.
(u_{i})_{i\in I}
is a classical
I
-frame)

2.

There exists
A,B>0
such that for one or every non-empty set
J
,

\forall(v_{j})_{j\in J}\in\ell^{2}(J;H):A\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}|\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}

(i.e.
(u_{i})_{i\in I}
is a
J
-extended classical
I
-frame)

Proof.

(1
\Rightarrow
2) Immediate through summation.

(2
\Rightarrow
1) Suppose there exists constants
A,B>0
such that :

\forall(v_{j})_{j\in J}\in\ell^{2}(J;H):A\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}|\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}

Take
v\in H
and choose
(v_{j})_{j\in J}
such that
v_{j_{\bullet}}=v
for only one fixed index
j_{\bullet}\in J
and
v_{j}=0
for other indices
j\in J\setminus\{j_{\bullet}\}
. We obtain

A\|v\|^{2}\leq\sum_{i\in I}|\langle v,u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|v\|^{2}.

∎

Proposition 2
.

Let
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
be a
\sigma
-finite measure space with positive measure
\mu
and
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
a family of elements in
H
such that
\forall h\in H
,
v\mapsto\langle v,u_{x}\rangle
is a measurable function on
X
.
The following are equivalent :

1.

\exists A,B>0:\forall v\in H:A\|v\|^{2}\leq\int_{X}|\langle v,u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)\leq B\|v\|^{2}

(i.e.
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
is a frame associated with the measure space (
X,\Sigma,\mu
) (or a
(X,\mu)
-continuous frame))

2.

There exist constants
A,B>0
such that for one or every measure space
(Y,\mathfrak{S},\nu)
such that
\nu
is a non-zero positive
\sigma
-finite measure, we have

\displaystyle\forall(v_{y})_{y\in Y}\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;H):A\int_{Y}\|v_{y}\|^{2}d\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X\times Y}|\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq B\int_{Y}\|v_{y}\|^{2}d\nu(y)

(i.e.
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
is a
(Y,\nu)
-extended frame associated with the measure space (
X,\Sigma,\mu
) (or a
(Y,\nu)
-extended
(X,\mu)
-continuous frame))

Proof.

(1
\Rightarrow
2) This part follows immediately by integration and Tonelli’s theorem.

(2
\Rightarrow
1) Take
v\in H
and
D\subset Y
a measurable subset of
Y
with
0<\nu(D)<+\infty
. Then
f=\frac{1_{D}}{\sqrt{\nu(D)}}\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;\mathbb{R})
satisfies
\|f\|_{2}=1
. Applying the hypothesis to
(v_{y})_{y\in Y}:=(f(y)v)_{y\in Y}
yields the result.
∎

Besides, based on the idea of changing the test spaces to
\ell^{2}(J;H)
and
L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)
, we can define extended versions of the analysis, synthesis and frame operators.

For a classical
I
-indexed frame
(u_{i})_{i\in I}
with values in
H
and bounds
A,B>0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{u}^{J}:\ell^{2}(J;H)

\displaystyle\to\ell^{2}(I\times J;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle(v_{j})_{j\in J}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle)_{(i,j)\in I\times J;\mathbb{F}}

\displaystyle(T_{u}^{J})^{*}:\ell^{2}(I\times J;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle\to\ell^{2}(J;H)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in I\times J}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i,j}u_{i})_{j\in J}

\displaystyle S_{u}^{J}=(T_{u}^{J})^{*}T_{u}^{J}:\ell^{2}(J;H)

\displaystyle\to\ell^{2}(J;H)

\displaystyle(v_{j})_{j\in I}

\displaystyle\mapsto\{\sum_{i\in I}\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle u_{i}\}_{j\in J}

called respectively the
J
-extended analysis, synthesis and frame operators.

We have :

A\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}\leq\langle S_{u}^{J}v,v\rangle=||T_{u}^{J}v||^{2}=\sum_{(i,j)\in I\times J}|\langle v_{j},u_{i}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\sum_{j\in J}\|v_{j}\|^{2}

which shows that
S_{u}^{J}
is an invertible, positive definite operator on
\ell^{2}(J;H)
, that
T_{u}^{J}
is a continuous and bounded below operator and thus a continuous and injective operator with closed range, and that
(T_{u}^{J})^{*}
is a continuous and surjective operator as the adjoint of a continuous and injective operator with closed range.

For a
(Y,\mathfrak{S},\nu)
-continuous frame
(u_{y})_{y\in Y}
with values in
H
and bounds
A,B>0
, we have

\displaystyle T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}:L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle(v_{y})_{y\in Y}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle)_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}

\displaystyle(T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)})^{*}:L^{2}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu;\mathbb{F})

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{x,y})_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\int_{x\in X}\lambda_{x,y}u_{x}d\mu(x))_{y\in Y}

\displaystyle S_{u}^{J}=(T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)})^{*}T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}:L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)

\displaystyle\to L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)

\displaystyle(v_{y})_{y\in Y}

\displaystyle\mapsto(\int_{x\in X}\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle u_{x}d\mu(x))_{y\in Y}

called respectively the
(Y,\nu)
-extended analysis, synthesis and frame operators.

We have :

\displaystyle A\int_{y\in Y}\|v_{y}\|^{2}d\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\langle S_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}v,v\rangle=||T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}v||^{2}=\int_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}|\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq B\int_{y\in Y}\|v_{y}\|^{2}d\nu(y)

which shows that
S_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}
is an invertible, positive definite operator on
L^{2}(Y,\nu;H)
, that
T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)}
is a continuous and bounded below operator and thus a continuous and injective operator with closed range, and that
(T_{u}^{(Y,\nu)})^{*}
is a continuous and surjective operator as the adjoint of a continuous and injective operator with closed range.

In fact all of these extended analysis, synthesis, and frame operators are natural block-diagonal operators, so their importance compared to the classical analysis, synthesis, and frame operators is not yet clear.

3
Some characterizations of Euclidean continuous frames

3.1
Useful rewritings of the quotients
N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})
and
N((v_{y})_{y\in Y};(u_{x})_{x\in X})
in the Euclidean case

Let
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
be a
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-continuous frame with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Note that this implies that each component
u^{k}:X\to\mathbb{F}
(where
k\in[\![1,n]\!]
) is actually in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
. The objective of this subsection is to propose some useful rewritings of the quotients
N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{\int_{x\in X}|\langle v,u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)}{||v||^{2}}
(where
v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
) and
N((v_{y})_{y\in Y};(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{\int_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}|\langle v_{y},u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)}{\int_{y\in Y}||v_{y}||^{2}d\nu(y)}
(where
(v_{y})_{y\in Y}\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\setminus\{0\}
) respectively.

Definition 2
.

In a Hilbert space
H
over
\mathbb{F}
, let
(w^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
be a sequence of
n
vectors in
H
. The Gramian matrix of
(w^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is defined as the matrix
W\in M_{n}(\mathbb{F})
whose
k,l
-th component is
W_{k,l}=\langle w^{k},w^{l}\rangle
.

This concept will be used in the following proposition where different Hilbert spaces are in play.

Proposition 3
.

For
v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
(v_{y}^{Y})_{y\in Y}\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\setminus\{0\}
, we have

N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v^{k}\overline{v^{l}}\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v^{k}|^{2}}=\frac{\Tr(VU)}{\Tr(V)}=\frac{||\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{v^{k}}u^{k}||^{2}}{||v||^{2}}

(1)

N((v_{y}^{Y})_{y\in Y};(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\langle v^{Y,k},v^{Y,l}\rangle\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}||v^{Y,k}||^{2}}=\frac{\Tr(V^{Y}U)}{\Tr(V^{Y})}

(2)

where
U
,
V
, and
V^{Y}
denote respectively the Gramian matrix of
(u^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
, the Gramian matrix of
(v^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
, and the Gramian matrix of
(v^{Y,k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
.

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle\int_{x\in X}|\langle v,u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x\in X}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}(v^{k}\overline{u_{x}^{k}})\overline{(v^{l}\overline{u_{x}^{l}})}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v^{k}\overline{v^{l}}\left(\int_{x\in X}u_{x}^{l}\overline{u_{x}^{k}}d\mu(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v^{k}\overline{v^{l}}\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle

On the one hand, we have
\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v^{k}\overline{v^{l}}\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\langle\overline{v^{l}}u^{l},\overline{v^{k}}u^{k}\rangle=||\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{v^{k}}u^{k}||^{2}
. On the other hand, we have obviously
\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}v^{k}\overline{v^{l}}\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle=\Tr(VU)
. Moreover,

||v||^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v^{k}|^{2}=\Tr(V).

In the same way, we find that

\displaystyle\int_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}|\langle v_{y}^{Y},u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{(x,y)\in X\times Y}\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}(v_{y}^{Y,k}\overline{u_{x}^{k}})\overline{(v_{y}^{Y,l}\overline{u_{x}^{l}})}d\mu(x)d\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\left(\int_{y\in Y}v_{y}^{Y,k}\overline{v_{y}^{Y,l}}d\nu(y)\right)\left(\int_{x\in X}u_{x}^{l}\overline{u_{x}^{k}}d\mu(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\langle v^{Y,k},v^{Y,l}\rangle\langle u^{l},u^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Tr(V^{Y}U)

and

\displaystyle\int_{y\in Y}||v_{y}^{Y}||^{2}d\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{y\in Y}\sum_{k=1}^{n}|v_{y}^{Y,k}|^{2}d\nu(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{y\in Y}|v_{y}^{Y,k}|^{2}d\nu(y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}||v^{Y,k}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\Tr(V^{Y}).

∎

3.2
Generalization of the well-known characterizations of Euclidean finitely indexed frames and normalized tight frames (Parseval frames) to the Euclidean continuous case

Let
(u_{x})_{x\in X}
be a
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-Bessel family with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Note again that this implies that each component
u^{k}:X\to\mathbb{F}
(where
k\in[\![1,n]\!]
) is actually in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
. Therefore
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
. Conversely, if
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
, then it is a continuous Bessel family by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Hence:

Remark 2
.

Let
u=(u_{x})_{x\in X}
be a continuous family of vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then

u\text{ is a continuous Bessel family}\Leftrightarrow u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n}),

and

\displaystyle u\text{ is a continuous frame}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\text{ and }

\displaystyle[\exists A>0:\forall v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\},A\leq N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})].

We have the following characterization

Theorem 1
.

Let
u=(u_{x})_{x\in X}
be a continuous family of vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then

\displaystyle u\text{ is a continuous frame}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\quad u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\text{ and}

\displaystyle\quad(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}\text{ is free}

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) First let’s assume that
u
is a continuous frame. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is dependant. So there exist
\lambda_{1}\cdots,\lambda_{n}
not all zero such that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}u^{k}=0
. We then have
N(\overline{\lambda};(u_{x})_{x\in X})=0
using formula
1
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is free.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose that
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is free. In this case, the nonnegative continuous function
v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\mapsto N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})\in[0,+\infty[
restricted to the unit sphere of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
has a global minimum, the unit sphere being compact. This minimum is nonnegative and different from 0 because
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is free (see formula
1
). Let’s denote it by
A>0
. We then have for
v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})=N(\frac{v}{||v||};(u_{x})_{x\in X})\geq A
by homogeneity of
N
in the first variable.
∎

Remark 3
.

This theorem has been proved in
[
6
]
in the case of a finite sequence of vectors
u=(u_{i})_{i\in[\![1,m]\!]}
with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
(see proposition 1.4.3 p. 19).

Another short proof of the direction (
\Leftarrow
) of the theorem is now given. Suppose that
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is free. So the Gramian matrix
U
of
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is invertible, i.e.
\det(U)\neq 0
. The matrix U is a positive semidefinite matrix, so the condition
\det(U)\neq 0
is equivalent to the fact that all the eingenvalues of U are (strictly) positive. Now, using formula
2
, we have

\forall(v_{y}^{Y})_{y\in Y}\setminus\{0\}:N((v_{y}^{Y})_{y\in Y},(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{\Tr(V^{Y}U)}{\Tr(V^{Y})}\geq\lambda_{k}(U)>0

where
V^{Y}
denotes the Gramian matrix of
(v^{Y,k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
and
\lambda_{k}(U)
is the smallest eigenvalue of U (we have used a trace inequality that can be easily derived from the trace inequalities in
[
12
]
).

Definition 3
.

We say that a continuous family
u=(u_{x})_{x\in X}
of vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is a normalised tight frame (or a Parseval frame) if

\forall v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:\frac{\int_{x\in X}|\langle v,u_{x}\rangle|^{2}d\mu(x)}{||v||^{2}}=1

We have the following characterization

Theorem 2
.

Let
u=(u_{x})_{x\in X}
be a continuous family of vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. Then

u\text{ is a continuous Parseval frame}\Leftrightarrow u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\text{ and }U=I_{n}.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
u
is a normalised tight frame. Then :
\forall v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})=1
. By choosing
v^{k}=1
for one index
k
and 0 otherwise, we have :
\forall k\in[\![1,n]\!]:||u^{k}||=1
. Next, by picking two indices
k
and
l
and choosing
v^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and
v^{l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and 0 otherwise, we have
\Re(\langle u^{k},u^{l}\rangle)=0
. Choosing this time
v^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and
v^{l}=\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}
and 0 otherwise, we have
\Im(\langle u^{k},u^{l}\rangle)=0
and so
\langle u^{k},u^{l}\rangle=0
. This means that
U=I_{n}
.

(
\Leftarrow
) Suppose
U=I_{n}
. Then by Pythagoras’ theorem,

\forall v\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\setminus\{0\}:N(v;(u_{x})_{x\in X})=\frac{||\sum_{k=1}^{n}\overline{v^{k}}u^{k}||^{2}}{||v||^{2}}=\frac{||v||^{2}}{||v||^{2}}=1

which means that
u
is a normalised tight frame
∎

Remark 4
.

This theorem has been proved in
[
6
]
in the case of a finite sequence of vectors
u=(u_{i})_{i\in[\![1,m]\!]}
with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
(see proposition 1.4.7 p. 21).

If
n=2
, a necessary and sufficient condition for
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{2})
to be a continuous frame is that the determinant of the Gramian matrix is (strictly) positive (see theorem
1
), that is
||u^{1}||||u^{2}||>|\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle|
. In the next section, we will provide a sufficient but not necessary condition for
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{2})
to be a frame.

3.3
A sufficient condition for a continuous family in
\mathbb{F}^{2}
to be a continuous frame

Proposition 4
.

Let
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{2})
. A sufficient condition for
u
to be a continuous frame is

\min(||u^{1}||^{2},||u^{2}||^{2})>|\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle|

Proof.

We have for all
v\in L^{2}(Y,\nu;\mathbb{F}^{2})\setminus\{0\}
:

\displaystyle N((v_{y})_{y\in Y};(u_{x})_{x\in X})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\langle u^{i},u^{j}\rangle\langle v^{j},v^{i}\rangle}{\sum_{j=1}^{2}||v^{j}||^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{||u^{1}||^{2}||v^{1}||^{2}+||u^{2}||^{2}||v^{2}||^{2}+2Re(\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle\langle v^{2},v^{1}\rangle)}{||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2}}

If
||u^{1}||^{2}\leq||u^{2}||^{2}
, we have :

\displaystyle\frac{||u^{1}||^{2}||v^{1}||^{2}+||u^{2}||^{2}||v^{2}||^{2}}{||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{||u^{1}||^{2}(||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2})}{||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2}}+\frac{||v^{2}||^{2}(||u^{2}||^{2}-||u^{1}||^{2})}{||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq||u^{1}||^{2}

\displaystyle=\min(||u^{1}||^{2},||u^{2}||^{2})

By symmetry, the same lower bound is obtained if
||u^{1}||^{2}\geq||u^{2}||^{2}
.

By using the inequality

2|Re(a\langle v^{2},v^{1}\rangle)|\leq 2|a\langle v^{2},v^{1}\rangle|\leq 2|a|||v^{1}||||v^{2}||\leq|a|(||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2})

valid for all
a\in\mathbb{F}
, we then obtain

\displaystyle\frac{||u^{1}||^{2}||v^{1}||^{2}+||u^{2}||^{2}||v^{2}||^{2}+2Re(\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle\langle v^{2},v^{1}\rangle)}{||v^{1}||^{2}+||v^{2}||^{2}}\geq\min(||u^{1}||^{2},||u^{2}||^{2})-|\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle|

The quantity
\min(||u^{1}||^{2},||u^{2}||^{2})-|\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle|
being strictly positive, remark
2
shows that
u
is a continuous frame.
∎

Example 1
.

Let’s give an example of a classical frame in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
using the previous criterion. We’ll define two sequences of complex numbers
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{*}
that satisfy the previous criterion and that are square summable. We take
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}:u_{n}^{1}=\frac{1}{n}e^{2\pi ian}
and
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}:u_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}e^{2\pi ibn}
with
a
and
b
two real numbers such that
a-b
is not an integer.
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
are square summable with sum
\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}
. Moreover,

|\langle u^{1},u^{2}\rangle|=|\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}e^{2\pi i(a-b)n}|<\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}

by the strict triangle inequality, the equality case being excluded because
\{e^{2\pi i(a-b)n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
are not positively proportional due to the conditions imposed on
a
and
b
. From this, we deduce that
u=(\frac{1}{n}e^{2\pi ian},\frac{1}{n}e^{2\pi ibn})_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is a classical frame in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.

4
Some topological properties of Euclidean continuous frames

Theorem 3
.

Let’s denote by
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
the set of
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-continuous frames with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

Then
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is open in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
, non-empty and dense in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
if
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq n
, and polygonally-connected if
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
.

Proof.

\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is open in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
because theorem
1
implies that
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}=(\det\circ G)^{-1}(]0,+\infty[)
, where the continuous function
G
sends
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
to the Gramian matrix of
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
.

Suppose that
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq n
. Let
(a^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
be a finite independant set of functions in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
. Then
a=(a_{x})_{x\in X}=(a_{x}^{1},\cdots,a_{x}^{n})_{x\in X}
is a
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-continuous frame by theorem
1
, which shows that
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is non-empty. To prove the density of
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
, let’s consider
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
. Let
(a^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
be a finite independant set of functions in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
(this is again possible since
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq n
). For each real number
t
, we define
v_{t}\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
by :
\forall k\in[\![1,n]\!]:v_{t}^{k}=u^{k}+t(a^{k}-u^{k})
. Let
V(t)
be the determinant of the Gramian of
(v_{t}^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
. Clearly,
V(t)
is a polynomial in
t
which satisfies
V(1)\neq 0
(theorem
1
). Therefore
V(t)\neq 0
except for a finite number of
t
’s. Moreover,

||v_{t}-u||^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}||v_{t}^{k}-u^{k}||^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}|t|^{2}||a^{k}-u^{k}||^{2}\to 0\text{ when t }\to 0.

Hence, there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
v_{t}
is near enough to
u
and
V(t)\neq 0
, hence
v_{t}
is a continuous frame.

Suppose that
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
. Let’s prove the path-connectedness of
\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
. Let
u,v\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
be two continuous frames. We take
(a^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
to be a finite independant set of functions in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
satisfying
\Span(\{a^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})\cap\Span(\{u^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}\cup\{v^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})=\{0\}
(this is possible because
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
). We define the continuous paths

\left\{\begin{aligned} \gamma_{1}&:[0,1]\to L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\\
\gamma_{2}&:[0,1]\to L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\end{aligned}\right.

by
\gamma_{1}(t)=tu+(1-t)a
and
\gamma_{2}(t)=tv+(1-t)a
respectively. We have
\gamma_{1}(1)=u
and
\gamma_{2}(1)=v
. We are going to show that
\forall t\in[0,1]:\gamma_{1}(t)\text{ and }\gamma_{2}(t)
are continuous frames, which will prove the polygonal-connectedness after composing
\gamma_{1}
with the inverse path of
\gamma_{2}
. By symmetry, we’ll show it only for
\gamma_{1}
. Consider
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{F}
such that :
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\gamma_{1}^{k}(t)=0
. It follows that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}tu^{k}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}(1-t)a^{k}\in\Span(\{a^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})\cap\Span(\{u^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})=\{0\}
. Now, since
\{u^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
is free, we have
\forall k\in[\![1,n]\!]:\lambda_{k}=0
.
∎

Theorem 4
.

Let’s denote by
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
the set of
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-continuous Parseval frames with values in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

Then
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is closed in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
, non-empty if
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq n
, and path-connected if
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
.

Proof.

\mathcal{P}\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is closed in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
because theorem
2
implies that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}=G^{-1}(Id)
, where the continuous function
G
sends
u\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
to the Gramian matrix of
(u^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
.

Suppose that
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq n
. Let
(a^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
be a finite orthonormal set of functions in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
. Then
a=(a_{x})_{x\in X}=(a_{x}^{1},\cdots,a_{x}^{n})_{x\in X}
is a
(X,\Sigma,\mu)
-continuous Parseval frame by theorem
2
, which shows that
\mathcal{P}\mathcal{F}_{(X,\mu)}^{\mathbb{F}^{n}}
is non-empty.

Suppose that
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
. Let’s prove the path-connectedness of
\mathcal{F}^{*}
. Let
u,v\in L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})
be two continuous Parseval frames. We take
(a^{k})_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}
to be a finite orthonormal set of functions in
L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})
satisfying
\Span(\{a^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})\cap\Span(\{u^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]}\cup\{v^{k}\}_{k\in[\![1,n]\!]})=\{0\}
(this is possible because
\dim(L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F})\geq 3n
). We define the continuous paths

\left\{\begin{aligned} \gamma_{1}&:[0,1]\to L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\\
\gamma_{2}&:[0,1]\to L^{2}(X,\mu;\mathbb{F}^{n})\end{aligned}\right.

by
\gamma_{1}(t)=\frac{tu+(1-t)a}{\sqrt{2t^{2}-2t+1}}
and
\gamma_{2}(t)=\frac{tv+(1-t)a}{\sqrt{2t^{2}-2t+1}}
respectively. We have
\gamma_{1}(1)=u
and
\gamma_{2}(1)=v
. We are going to show that
\forall t\in[0,1]:\gamma_{1}(t)\text{ and }\gamma_{2}(t)
are continuous Parseval frames, which will prove the path-connectedness after composing
\gamma_{1}
with the inverse path of
\gamma_{2}
. By symmetry, we’ll show it only for
\gamma_{1}
. But we have obviously that
||\gamma_{1}(t)^{k}||=1
and
\langle\gamma_{1}(t)^{k},\gamma_{1}(t)^{l}\rangle=0
for all
k,l\in[\![1,n]\!]
, which shows that the Gramian matrix of
\gamma_{1}(t)
is
I_{n}
. So we can conclude using theorem
2
.
∎
