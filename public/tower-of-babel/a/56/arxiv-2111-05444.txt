Sharp, strong and unique minimizers for low complexity robust recovery
Jalal Fadili, Tran T. A. Nghia, Trinh T. T. Tran

Abstract
In this paper, we show the important roles of sharp minima and strong minima for robust recovery. We also obtain several characterizations of sharp minima for convex regularized optimization problems. Our characterizations are quantitative and verifiable especially for the case of decomposable norm regularized problems including sparsity, group-sparsity, and low-rank convex problems. For group-sparsity optimization problems, we show that a unique solution is a strong solution and obtain quantitative characterizations for solution uniqueness.

Introduction

1.1
Problem statement

Inverse problems and regularization theory is a central theme in various areas of engineering and science. A typical case is where one observes a vector
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of linear measurements according to

y_{0}=\Phi x_{0}

(1.1)

where
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is known, and is typically an idealization of the sensing mechanism in signal/imaging science applications, or the design matrix in a parametric statistical regression problem. The vector
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the unknown quantity of interest.

Solving the inverse problem associated to the linear forward model (
1.1
) amounts to recovering
x_{0}
, either exactly or to a good approximation, knowing
y_{0}
and
\Phi
. This is however a quite challenging task especially when the linear system (
1.1
) is underdetermined with
m\ll n
. In fact, even when
m=n
,
\Phi
is in general ill-conditioned or even singular. This entails that the linear inverse problem is in general ill-posed.

In order to bring back the problem to the land of well-posedness, it is necessary to restrict the inversion process to a well-chosen subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the plausible solutions including
x_{0}
. This can be achieved by adopting a variational framework and solving the following optimization problem

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad J(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad\Phi x=y_{0},

(1.2)

where
J
is function which is bounded from below (wlog non-negative). The function
J
, known as a regularizer, is designed in such way that it is the smallest on the sought-after solutions. Popular example in signal/image processing and machine learning is the
\ell_{1}
norm to promote sparsity, the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm to promote group sparsity, analysis sparsity seminorm (i.e.,
J=J_{0}\circ D^{*}
,
D^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is linear and
J_{0}
is the
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm); see
[
6
,
9
,
12

,
29
,

to cite a few. Another popular example is the nuclear norm, i.e., the

[

]
. These prototypical cases are included in the class of decomposable norms
[

]
that covers a wide range of optimization-based recovery problems in different areas of data science.

When the observation is subject to errors, the system (
1.1
) is modified to

y=\Phi x_{0}+\omega

(1.3)

where
\omega
accounts either for noise and/or modeling errors. The errors can be either deterministic (in this case, one typically assumes to know some bound on
\|\omega\|
) or random (in which case its distribution is assumed to be known). Throughout,

will be assumed deterministic with
\|\omega\|\leq\delta

To recover

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x)\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\|y-\Phi x\|\leq\delta

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|\Phi x-y\|^{2}+\mu J(x).

We say that

1.2
Contributions and relation to prior work

The chief goal of this paper is to show the intricate and important roles of sharp minima and strong minima in robust recovery. Our main contributions are as follows.

\bullet

As discussed above, solution uniqueness and sharp minima play a pivotal role for robust recovery with linear rate. However, except the case where the regularizer
J
is merely polyhedral, a unique solution may not be sharp. For example, as we will show in our Section
5
, a unique solution to the group-sparsity problem (
1.2
) is always a strong minimum but not a sharp one. The natural question is therefore whether strong minima could also lead to some meaningful results for robust recovery. Section
3
answers this question positively without even needing convexity of
J
, unlike the previous work of
[
39
,
42
]
which showed that a sharp minimizer implicitly guarantees robust recovery with linear rate, but convexity was essential there. We show that if
x_{0}
is a strong solution to (
1.2
), then we have robust recovery by solving (
1.4
) or (
1.5
) with a convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
(Theorem
3.12
). This rate can be improved to the linear rate
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
when
J=\|\cdot\|
. Whether this linear rate for robust recovery can be achieved for other regularizers under the sole strong minimality assumption is an open question. Besides that main result, we also revisit in Theorem
3.10
robust recovery results when
x_{0}
is assumed to be a sharp minimizer by proving linear convergence and providing more explicit bounds in comparison to the results in
[
10
,
14
,
19
,
44
]
.

\bullet

Our second main contribution, at the heart of Section
4
, is to study necessary and sufficient conditions for the properties of sharp and strong minima. As mentioned above, solution uniqueness is characterized via the descent cone
[
10
]
. But it is hard to compute the descent cone for general functions
J
. On the other hand, sharp and strong minima can be characterized explicitly via first and second-order analysis
[
5
,
28
,
32
]
. We leverage these tools (see Theorem
4.6
and Proposition
4.12
) to get equivalent charaterizations of unique/sharp/strong solutions, and to obtain a quantitative condition for convex regularized problems that allows us to check numerically that a solution is sharp. This condition bears similarities with the Nondegenerate Source Condition of
[
19
,
39
,
42
]
. Our approach does not rely on any polyhedral structure. This distinguishes our results from several ones in the literature as in
[
17
,
24
,
44
]
, and allows us to work on more general optimization problems. We also believe that non-smooth second-order analysis of the type we develop here, which is a powerful tool in variational analysis and nonsmooth optimization, is not well-known in the robust recovery literature, and are thus of particular importance in this context.

\bullet

Finally, Section
5
is devoted to a comprehensive treatment of unique/strong solutions for group-sparsity minimization problems
[
29
,
33
,
43
]
, an important class of (
1.2
) where
J
is the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm. Sufficient conditions for solution uniqueness for group-sparsity minimization problems were achieved in
[
7
,
18
,
21
,
29
,
33
,
39
,
42
]
, but none of them are necessary. To find a characterization for solution uniqueness, we first obtain a closed form for the descent cone (Theorem
5.1
). To the best of our knowledge, this was an open question in the literature, though the
statistical dimension
of this cone is more computable
[
1
]
. By relying again on second-order analysis
[
5
,
32
]
, we show that a unique solution to group-sparsity minimization problems (
1.2
) and (
1.5
) is indeed a strong solution (Theorem
5.3
). This result is interesting in two ways: first, the function in (
1.2
) is not piecewise linear-quadratic, which rules out the approach developed in
[
4
]
to unify solution uniqueness and strong minima; second, strong solutions are possibly more natural than sharp solutions for non-polyhedral minimization problems whenever solution uniqueness occurs. Moreover, we establish a quantitative condition for checking unique/strong solutions to group-sparsity problems that is equivalent to solving a smooth convex optimization problem. This convex problem can be solved via available packages such as
cvxopt
as we will illustrate in the numerical results of Section
6
. Consequently, we show that solution uniqueness to group-sparsity problem is equivalent to the robust recovery with rate
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta})
for both problems (
1.4
) and (
1.5
).

Remark 1.1
.

Although we do not have a rigorous study for the case where
J
is the nuclear norm in this paper, we provide several examples showing that a unique solution to (
1.2
) with the nuclear norm is neither sharp nor strong. This raises the challenge of understanding the interplay between unique, sharp, and strong solutions in this case. Some open questions will be discussed in Section
7
for further research in this direction.

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{R}^{n}

. In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote the inner product by

, the Euclidean norm by

2.1
Some linear algebra

Given an
m\times n
matrix
A
,
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,A
(resp.
\mbox{\rm Im}\,A
) is the kernel/null (resp. image) space of
A
. The Moore-Penrose generalized inverse
[
22
, Page 423]
of
A
is denoted by
A^{\dagger}
, which satisfies the property

AA^{\dagger}A=A.

(2.1)

Moreover, we have

\begin{gathered}Ax=b\text{ is consistent if and only if }AA^{\dagger}b=b,\enskip\text{and}\enskip\\
\text{the set of solutions to $Ax=b$ is }A^{\dagger}b+\mbox{\rm Ker}\,A.\end{gathered}

(2.2)

If
A
is injective then
A^{\dagger}=(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}
, where
A^{\top}
is the transpose of
A
. If
A
is surjective then
A^{\dagger}=A^{\top}(AA\top)^{-1}
.
A^{*}
will also denote the adjoint of
A
as a linear operator.

Suppose that
\bar{x}
is a solution to the consistent system
Ax=b
. The projection of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto the affine set
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}A^{-1}b
is

\mbox{\rm P}_{C}(x)=(\mathbb{I}-A^{\dagger}A)(x-\bar{x})+\bar{x}=x-A^{\dagger}A(x-\bar{x});

(2.3)

see, e.g.,
[
22
, page 435–437]
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
be two arbitrary norms in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. The matrix operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}:\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is defined by

\|A\|_{\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\|Ax\|_{\mathcal{A}}}{\|x\|_{\mathcal{B}}}.

We write
\|A\|_{2,2}
for
\|A\|
. The Frobenius norm of
A
is known as

\|A\|_{F}=\sqrt{\mbox{\rm Trace}\,(A^{*}A)}.

Recall that the nuclear norm of
A
is

\|A\|_{*}=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(m,n)}\sigma_{k},

where
\sigma_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq\min(m,n)
are all the singular values of
A
.

2.2
Some convex analysis

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a proper lower semi-continuous (lsc) convex function with domain
\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\varphi(x)<\infty\right\}
. The subdifferential of
\varphi
at

is defined by

\partial\varphi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\varphi(u)\geq\varphi(x)+\left\langle v,\,u-x\right\rangle,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

When
\varphi(\cdot)=\iota_{C}(\cdot)
, the indicator function of a nonempty closed convex set
C
, i.e.,
\iota_{C}(x)=0
if

and
+\infty
otherwise, the subdifferential of
\iota_{C}
at
x\in C
is the
normal cone
to
C
at

N_{C}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\left\langle v,\,u-x\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall u\in C\right\}.

(2.4)

For a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mbox{\rm cone}\,\Omega
is its
conical hull
,
\mbox{\rm int}\,\Omega
is the interior of
\Omega
, and
\mbox{\rm ri}\,\Omega
is the relative interior of the convex set
\Omega
. Given a convex cone
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
polar
of
K
is defined to be the cone

K^{\circ}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\left\langle v,\,u\right\rangle\leq 0,\quad\forall u\in\Omega\right\}.

(2.5)

It is well known, see
[
32
, Corollary 6.21]
that
K^{\circ}
is closed and convex, and that

K^{\circ}=\mbox{\rm cl}\,(K)^{\circ}\enskip\text{and}\enskip K^{\circ\circ}=(K^{\circ})^{\circ}=\mbox{\rm cl}\,(K),

(2.6)

where
cl
denotes the topological
closure
. We will also use
cl
for the closure of a function, i.e., the topological closure of its epigraph.

The
gauge function
of
\Omega
is defined by

\gamma_{\Omega}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\inf\left\{r>0|\;u\in rC\right\},\quad\text{for}\quad u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.7)

The
support function
to
\Omega
is denoted by

\sigma_{\Omega}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left\langle v,\,x\right\rangle,\quad\text{for}\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.8)

It is a proper lsc convex function as soon as
\Omega
is non-empty.
The
polar set
of
\Omega
is given by

\Omega^{\circ}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\sigma_{C}(v)\leq 1\right\}.

(2.9)

When
\Omega
is a non-empty convex set,
\Omega^{\circ}
is a non-empty closed convex set containing the origin. When
\Omega
is also closed and contains the origin, it is well-known that

\gamma_{\Omega}=\sigma_{\Omega^{\circ}},

(2.10)

see, e.g.,
[
30
, Corollary 15.1.2]
. In this case,
\gamma_{C}
is a non-negative lsc convex and positively homogenous function.

2.3
Sharp and strong minima

Let us first recall the definition of sharp and strong solutions in
[
5
,
11
,
28
,
32
]
that are playing central roles throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1

(Sharp and strong minima)
.

We say
\bar{x}
to be a sharp solution/minimizer to the (non necessarily convex) function
\varphi
with a constant
c>0
if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(\bar{x})+c\|x-\bar{x}\|,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bar{x}).

(2.11)

Moreover,
\bar{x}
is said to be a strong solution/minimizer with a constant
\kappa>0
if there exists
\delta>0
such that

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(\bar{x})+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{B}_{\delta}(\bar{x}).

(2.12)

Properties (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) are also known as respectively, first order and second order (or quadratic) growth properties.

Strong minima is certainly weaker than sharp minima. Moreover, if
\bar{x}
is a sharp or strong solution to
\varphi
, it is a unique local minimizer to
\varphi
(and the unique minimizer if
\varphi
is convex). When the function
\varphi
is a convex
piecewise linear convex
, i.e., its epigraph is a polyhedral,
\bar{x}
is a unique solution to
\varphi
if and only if it is a sharp solution. Furthermore, if
\varphi
is a convex
piecewise linear-quadratic
function in the sense that its domain is a union of finitely many polyhedral sets, relative to each of which
\varphi
is a quadratic function,
\bar{x}
is a strong solution if and only if it is a unique solution, see, e.g.,
[
4
]
.

To characterize sharp and strong solutions, it is typical to use directional derivative and second subderivative; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
32
]
.

Definition 2.2

(directional derivative and second subderivative)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc convex function. The directional derivative of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
is the function
d\varphi(\bar{x}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{\varphi(\bar{x}+tw)-\varphi(\bar{x})}{t}\quad\text{for}\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.13)

The second subderivative of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
is the function
d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|\bar{v}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
defined by

d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|\bar{v})(w)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\liminf_{t\downarrow 0,w^{\prime}\to w}\dfrac{\varphi(\bar{x}+tw^{\prime})-\varphi(\bar{x})-t\left\langle\bar{v},\,w^{\prime}\right\rangle}{\frac{1}{2}t^{2}}\quad\text{for}\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.14)

It is well-known that
d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)=\max\left\{\left\langle v,\,w\right\rangle|\;v\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})\right\}
if
\varphi
is continuous at
\bar{x}
. The calculation of

is quite involved in general; see, e.g.,
[
5
,
32
,
23
]
. Note that from
[

, the function

is non-negative, lsc, positively homogeneous of degree 2, and

\mbox{\rm dom}\,d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\subset\mathcal{T}(\bar{x}|\bar{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)=\left\langle\bar{v},\,w\right\rangle\right\}.

(2.15)

If, moreover,
\varphi
is twice-differentiable at
\bar{x}
, we have (see
[

, Example 13.8]
)

d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|\nabla\varphi(\bar{x}))(w)=\left\langle w,\,\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bar{x})w\right\rangle.

(2.16)

Lemma 2.4

(Characterization of sharp and strong minima)
.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lsc convex function with
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
. We have:

(i)

\bar{x}
is a sharp minimizer to
\varphi
if and only if there exists
c>0
such that
d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)\geq c\|w\|
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)>0
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

(ii)

\bar{x}
is a strong minimizer to
\varphi
if and only if
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
and there exists
\kappa>0
such that
d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)(w)\geq\kappa\|w\|^{2}
for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which means

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;d^{2}\varphi(\bar{x}|0)(w)=0\right\}=\{0\}.

It is important to observe that although the sharpness of a minimizer as defined in (

Lemma 2.5

(Global sharp minima)
.

Suppose that
\bar{x}
is a sharp solution to the proper lsc convex function
\varphi
. Then there exists
c>0
such that

\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})\geq c\|x-\bar{x}\|\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proof.

Since
\bar{x}
is a sharp solution to
\varphi
, we have from Lemma
2.4
(i)
that
\exists c>0
such that
d\varphi(\bar{x})(w)\geq c\|w\|
,
\forall w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, by the characterization of the directional derivative for convex functions, we have for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\varphi(x)-\varphi(\bar{x})=\varphi(\bar{x}+(x-\bar{x}))-\varphi(\bar{x})\geq\inf_{t>0}\dfrac{\varphi(\bar{x}+t(x-\bar{x}))-\varphi(\bar{x})}{t}=d\varphi(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})\geq c\|x-\bar{x}\|.

The proof is complete.
∎

(Sharp minima for robust recovery)

(3.17)

(ii)

(3.18)

Proof.

3.3
Strong minima and robust recovery

A natural question is to whether robust recovery is still possible if the sharp minima property is replaced by the weaker strong minima property. We here show that the answer is affirmative, but at the price of a slower rate of convergence.

Theorem 3.12

(Strong minima for robust recovery)
.

Suppose that
J
is a non-negative lsc function which satisfies (
3.2

x_{0}
with modulus
L

\displaystyle\|x_{\delta}-x_{0}\|\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{\kappa}L\|\Phi^{\dagger}\|\delta+\|\Phi^{\dagger}\|^{2}\delta^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

and

(3.25)

Proof.

(i)

Since
x_{0}
is a strong solution to (
1.2
) with constant
\kappa>0
, there exists
\nu>0
such that

J(x)-J(x_{0})\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\Phi^{-1}y_{0}\cap\mathbb{B}_{\nu}(x_{0}).

Let
\bar{x}_{\delta}
be the projection of
x_{\delta}
onto
\Phi^{-1}y_{0}
. Since
x_{0}
is the unique solution to problem (
1.2
), we argue as in the proof of Theorem

(i)
to show that for all
\delta
small enough, one has

with

in (

) and (

).
We then have

\displaystyle L\|\bar{x}_{\delta}-x_{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\geq J(\bar{x}_{\delta})-J(x_{\delta})

\displaystyle\text{by \eqref{Lip}},

\displaystyle\geq J(\bar{x}_{\delta})-J(x_{0})

\displaystyle\text{by optimality of $x_{0}$},

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\left(\|x_{\delta}-x_{0}\|^{2}-\|\bar{x}_{\delta}-x_{\delta}\|^{2}\right).

(ii)

Let
\bar{x}_{\mu}
be the projection of
x_{\mu}
to
\Phi^{-1}y_{0}

\delta
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle J(x_{\mu})-J(x_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\kappa}{2}\|\bar{x}_{\mu}-x_{0}\|^{2}-L\|\bar{x}_{\mu}-x_{\mu}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x_{\mu}-x_{0}\|^{2}-\|\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi(x_{\mu}-x_{0})\|^{2}-L\|\Phi^{\dagger}\Phi(x_{\mu}-x_{0})\|

Remark 4.5

One important class of regularizers is

such that

Hence, problem (

Theorem 4.6

(ii)

(4.13)

Moreover, the sharpness constant

(4.14)

and
s>0

Proof.

[
(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii)
]. Suppose that

(i)

\displaystyle d\Psi(x_{0})(w)

\displaystyle=dJ(x_{0})(w)+\iota_{\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi}(w)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\partial J_{0}(D^{*}x_{0})}(D^{*}w)+\iota_{\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi}(w)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left\langle v,\,D^{*}w\right\rangle|\;v_{T}=e,v_{S}\in\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0}+C\right\}+\iota_{\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi}(w)

\displaystyle=\left\langle e,\,D_{T}^{*}w\right\rangle+\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D_{S}^{*}w\right\rangle+\sup_{z\in C}\left\langle z,\,D_{S}^{*}w\right\rangle+\iota_{\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi}(w)

\displaystyle=\left\langle e,\,D_{T}^{*}w\right\rangle+\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D_{S}^{*}w\right\rangle+\sigma_{C}(D_{S}^{*}w)+\iota_{\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi}(w).

For any

\displaystyle\sigma_{C}(D^{*}_{S}w)-c\|w\|

\displaystyle\geq-\left\langle e,\,D^{*}_{T}N^{*}u\right\rangle-\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D^{*}_{S}N^{*}u\right\rangle

(4.16)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle ND_{T}e,\,u\right\rangle-\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D^{*}_{S}N^{*}u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle ND_{S}(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e,\,u\right\rangle-\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D^{*}_{S}N^{*}u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\left\langle(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e+\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D^{*}_{S}w\right\rangle.

If

, we deduce from (

) that

, which means

. Thus the Restricted Injectivity (
4.12
) is satisfied.

Moreover, as

is a continuous convex function,
\partial J_{0}(D^{*}x_{0})
is compact, and so is

, whence it follows that

\sigma_{C}(D_{S}^{*}w)\leq r\|D^{*}_{S}w\|\leq r\|D^{*}_{S}\|\|w\|\quad\text{with}\quad r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\max\left\{\|z\||\;z\in C\right\}.

Combining the latter with (

-\left\langle(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e+\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,v\right\rangle\leq\left(1-\frac{c}{r\|D^{*}_{S}\|}\right)\sigma_{C}(v).

(4.17)

as defined in (

.

such that and
\mathbb{B}_{s}(0)\cap S\subset C
. For any

. It follows that

is a compact set.

Let us bound from below the right hand side of (

[

\displaystyle\max_{v\in K\cap\mbox{\rm Im}\,D^{*}_{S}N^{*}}\left\langle-(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e-\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\max_{v\in K}\left(\left\langle-(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e-\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,v\right\rangle-\iota_{\mbox{\rm Im}\,D^{*}_{S}N^{*}}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,ND_{S}}\max_{v\in K}\left\langle-(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e-\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0}+z,\,v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,ND_{S}}\sigma_{K}(-(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e-\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0}+z).

On the other hand, for any

\displaystyle 0\leq\min_{z\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,ND_{S}}\sigma_{K}(-(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e-\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0}+z)

\displaystyle=-\left\langle(ND_{S})^{\dagger}ND_{T}e+\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,v^{\star}\right\rangle

[

\displaystyle\sigma_{K}=\left(\iota_{C^{\circ}}+\iota_{S}\right)^{*}=\mbox{\rm cl}\,\left(\inf_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\sigma_{C^{\circ}}(\cdot+\eta)+\sigma_{S}(\eta)\right)=\mbox{\rm cl}\,\left(\inf_{\eta\in T}\sigma_{C^{\circ}}(\cdot+\eta)\right)=\mbox{\rm cl}\,\left(\inf_{\eta\in T}\gamma_{C}(\cdot+\eta)\right).

Since

Thus, the closure operation can be omitted above to get for any

Inserting this into (
4.19
), we get
(ii)
, after observing that

and that the minimum in

is achieved on

(see Proposition
4.4
).

[
(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
]. Let

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}d\Psi(x_{0})(w)&=\left\langle e,\,D^{*}w\right\rangle+\left\langle\mbox{\rm P}_{S}v_{0},\,D^{*}_{S}w\right\rangle+\sigma_{C}(D^{*}_{S}w)\\

4.4
Connections between unique/sharp/strong solutions in the noiseless case

When there is noise in observation (
1.3
), problem (
1.5
) is usually used to recover the original signal
x_{0}
. Solution uniqueness to (
1.5
) is especially important for exact recovery
[
16
,
41
]
. We show next that Restricted Injectivity and Nondegenerate Source Condition are sufficient for strong minima to problem (
1.5
), and they become necessary for the
\ell_{1}
problem. This result is true for a larger class of regularized problems taking the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad\Theta(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}f(\Phi x)+\mu J(x),

(4.33)

where
\mu
is a positive parameter and the loss function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an extended real-valued convex function satisfying the following two conditions:

(A)

f
is twice continuously differentiable in
\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
.

(B)

\nabla f^{2}(x)
is positive definite for all
x\in\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, i.e.,
f
is strictly convex in the interior of its domain.

In (
1.5
), the function
f
is
\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot-y\|^{2}
which certainly satisfies the above two conditions. Moreover, the standing assumptions
(A)
and
(B)
for
f
cover the important case of the Kullback-Leiber divergence:

\displaystyle f_{\rm KL}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}y_{i}\log\dfrac{y_{i}}{z_{i}}+z_{i}-y_{i}&\mbox{if}\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{++}\\
+\infty&\mbox{if}\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{R}^{m}_{++},\end{array}\right.

where
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
and
0\log 0=0
. This offers a natural way to measure of similarity of two nonnegative vectors (e.g., two discrete distributions) and is broadly used in statistical/machine learning and signal processing.

In the following result, we provide the connections between unique/sharp/strong solutions for the two problems (
1.2
) and (
4.33
). Part
(i)
of this result could be obtained from
[
24
, Proposition 3.2]
, but we still give a short proof as our assumptions on
f
are slightly different, e.g.,
f
may not have full domain.

Proposition 4.12

(Unique/sharp/strong solutions for problems (
1.2
) and (
4.33
))
.

Suppose that
\bar{x}
is an optimal solution to problem (
4.33
) and
\Phi\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
. Then
\bar{x}
is a solution to problem (
1.2
) with
x_{0}=\bar{x}
. Moreover, the following statements hold:

(i)

\bar{x}
is the unique solution to (
4.33
) if and only if it is the unique solution to (
1.2
).

(ii)

If
\bar{x}
is a sharp solution to (
1.2
) then it is a strong solution to problem (
4.33
).

(iii)

If
\bar{x}
is a strong solution to (
4.33
) then it is also a strong solution to (
1.2
).

Proof.

For any
x\in\Phi^{-1}(\Phi\bar{x})
, we have

f(\Phi\bar{x})+\mu J(x)=f(\Phi x)+\mu J(x)\geq f(\Phi\bar{x})+\mu J(\bar{x}).

(4.37)

It follows that
\bar{x}
is a solution to problem (
1.2
) with
y_{0}=\Phi\bar{x}
.

(i)

If
\bar{x}
is the unique solution to problem (
4.33
), we have the strict inequality in (
4.37
) provided that
x\neq\bar{x}
, which shows that
\bar{x}
is also the unique solution to problem (
1.2
).
Assume now that
\bar{x}
is the unique solution to (
1.2
). By contradiction, suppose that
\bar{x}
is not the unique solution to (
4.33
). Since
\mbox{\rm Argmin}\,(\Theta)
is convex and
\Phi^{-1}(\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
is an open set containing
\bar{x}
, there exists
r>0
such that
\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bar{x})\subset\Phi^{-1}(\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f))
and
\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bar{x})\cap\mbox{\rm Argmin}\,(\Theta)\neq\emptyset
, while the later is not the singleton
\{\bar{x}\}
. Choose
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{B}_{r}(\bar{x})
with
\widehat{x}\neq\bar{x}
. We have
-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\widehat{x})\in\partial J(\widehat{x})
and
-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})\in\partial J(\bar{x})
. The monotonicity of the subdifferential tells us that

-\left\langle\nabla f(\Phi\widehat{x})-\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\,\Phi\widehat{x}-\Phi\bar{x}\right\rangle=-\left\langle\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\widehat{x})-\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\,\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\right\rangle\geq 0.

Convexity of
f
entails the opposite inequality which shows that

\left\langle\nabla f(\Phi\widehat{x})-\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\,\Phi\widehat{x}-\Phi\bar{x}\right\rangle=0.

Since
f
is twice continuously differentiable in
\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, we obtain from the mean-value theorem that

\int_{0}^{1}\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t(\widehat{x}-\bar{x})))\Phi(\widehat{x}-\bar{x}),\,\Phi(\widehat{x}-\bar{x})\right\rangle dt=0.

(4.38)

Since the Hessian is positive definite and continuous on
\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mbox{\rm dom}\,f)
, there exists some
\tau>0
such that

\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi(\bar{x}+t(\widehat{x}-\bar{x})))\Phi(\widehat{x}-\bar{x}),\,\Phi(\widehat{x}-\bar{x})\right\rangle\geq\tau\|\Phi(\widehat{x}-\bar{x})\|^{2}

for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Combining this with (
4.38
) implies that
\Phi\widehat{x}=\Phi\bar{x}
. Using this together with the fact that both
\widehat{x}
and
\bar{x}
are minimizers to (
4.33
), entails that
J(\widehat{x})=J(\bar{x})
. This contradicts uniqueness of
\bar{x}
for (
1.2
).

(ii)

Assume that
\bar{x}
is a sharp solution to (
1.2
). By Proposition
3.8
, we have

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})=\{0\}

(4.39)

where we recall
\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})
from (
3.10
). From the sum rule (
2.17
) and convexity of
J
, we have

d^{2}\Theta(\bar{x}|0)(w)=\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi\bar{x})\Phi w,\,\Phi w\right\rangle+\mu d^{2}J(\bar{x}|-\mu^{-1}\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x})(w))\geq\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi\bar{x})\Phi w,\,\Phi w\right\rangle

(4.40)

and from (
2.15
),
\mbox{\rm dom}\,d^{2}\Theta(\bar{x}|0)\subset\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;d\Theta(\bar{x})(w)=0\right\}
. We also have

d\Theta(\bar{x})(w)=\left\langle\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\,\Phi w\right\rangle+\mu dJ(x_{0})(w).

It then follows that

\mbox{\rm dom}\,d^{2}\Theta(\bar{x}|0)\subset\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;\left\langle\nabla f(\Phi\bar{x}),\,\Phi w\right\rangle+\mu dJ(x_{0})(w)=0\right\}.

(4.41)

To verify that
\bar{x}
is a strong solution to problem (
4.33
) by using Lemma
2.4
, we claim that

\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi\bar{x})\Phi w,\,\Phi w\right\rangle>0\quad\text{for all}\quad w\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,d^{2}\Theta(\bar{x}|0)\setminus\{0\}.

(4.42)

Suppose that
w\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,d^{2}\Theta(\bar{x}|0)
satisfying
\left\langle\nabla^{2}f(\Phi\bar{x})\Phi w,\,\Phi w\right\rangle\leq 0
. Since
\nabla^{2}f(\Phi\bar{x})\succ 0
, we have
\Phi w=0
. This together with (
4.41
) tells us that
w\in\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})=\{0\}
by (
4.39
). Thus inequality (
4.42
) holds and
\bar{x}
is a strong solution to problem (
4.33
).

(iii)

Suppose that
\bar{x}
is a strong solution to problem (
4.33
). There exist constants
\kappa,\gamma>0
such that

f(\Phi x)+\mu J(x)\geq f(\Phi\bar{x})+\mu J(\bar{x})+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad x\in\mathbb{B}_{\gamma}(\bar{x}).

For any
x\in\mathbb{B}_{\gamma}(\bar{x})\cap\Phi^{-1}(\Phi\bar{x})
, we obtain that

J(x)\geq J(\bar{x})+\frac{\kappa}{2\mu}\|x-\bar{x}\|^{2},

which means that
\bar{x}
is also a strong solution to (
1.2
) as claimed.

∎

For the special case (
1.5
) of (
4.33
),

4.12
advances

[

).

Example 4.14

(Strong solutions of (
1.2

4.33
))

Let us consider the following nuclear norm minimization problem

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\Theta(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\dfrac{1}{2}\|Y-\Phi(X)\|^{2}+\|X\|_{*},

(4.43)

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
stands for the nuclear norm of
X
, and
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the diagonal operator, i.e.,
\Phi(X)=(X_{11},X_{22})^{\top}
and
Y=(2,1)^{\top}
. This is a special case of (
4.33
) with
f(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|Y-\cdot\|^{2}
. Let

\overline{X}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}
.
We have

\Phi^{*}\nabla f(\Phi(\overline{X}))=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\enskip\text{and}\enskip\partial\|\overline{X}\|_{*}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\alpha\end{pmatrix}|\;\alpha\in[-1,1]\right\}.

Thus
0\in\partial\Theta(\overline{X})
, i.e.,
\overline{X}
is a solution to (
4.43
). By Proposition
4.12
, it is also a solution to

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}}\|X\|_{*}\enskip\text{and}\enskip\Phi(X)=(1,0)^{\top}.

(4.44)

For any
2\times 2
matrix
X
, let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
be its singular values. We have

\|X\|_{*}=\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}=\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{2}+2\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}=\sqrt{\|X\|_{F}^{2}+2|{\rm det}\,(X)|}.

(4.45)

The feasible set of (
4.44
) consists of matrices of the form

X=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\
b&0\end{pmatrix}
, and we obtain from (
4.45
) that

\|X\|_{*}=\sqrt{1+(|a|+|b|)^{2}}.

Thus
\overline{X}
is the unique solution to (
4.44
) as well as
(
4.43
)
due to Proposition
4.12
again. Next we claim that
\overline{X}
is the strong solution to (
4.44
), which means there exist
\kappa,\delta>0
such that

\|X\|_{*}-\|\overline{X}\|_{*}\geq\dfrac{\kappa}{2}\|X-\overline{X}\|^{2}_{F}\quad\text{for all}\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\
b&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{with}\quad\|X-\overline{X}\|_{F}=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}\leq\delta.

(4.46)

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\|X\|_{*}-\|\overline{X}\|_{*}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1+(|a|+|b|)^{2}}-1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{a^{2}+b^{2}+2|ab|}{\sqrt{1+a^{2}+b^{2}+2|ab|}+1}

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{\sqrt{1+2\delta^{2}}+1}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+2\delta^{2}}+1}\|X-\bar{X}\|_{F}^{2}.

This certainly verifies (
4.46
). Next we claim that
\overline{X}
is not a strong solution to (
4.43
). Pick
X_{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon^{2}&\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon&\varepsilon^{2}\end{pmatrix}
with
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, observe that
\|X-\overline{X}\|^{2}_{F}=2(\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{4})
. It follows from (
4.45
) that

\displaystyle\Theta(X_{\varepsilon})-\Theta(\overline{X})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{2}-1\\
\varepsilon^{2}-1\end{pmatrix}\right\|^{2}+\left\|\begin{pmatrix}1+\varepsilon^{2}&\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon&\varepsilon^{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{*}-2

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon^{2}-1)^{2}+\sqrt{((1+\varepsilon^{2})^{2}+2\varepsilon^{2}+\varepsilon^{4})+2\varepsilon^{4}}-2

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon^{2}-1)^{2}+(1+2\varepsilon^{2})-2

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{4}.

Therefore,
\overline{X}
cannot be a strong solution to (
4.43
), which also implies that
\overline{X}
is neither a sharp solution to (
4.43
) nor to (
4.44
) due to Proposition
4.12
.

In this section, we study the following particular case of (

), where
J_{0}
is the
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm that promotes group sparsity
[

,
33
,

]
.

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad\|D^{*}x\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}\quad\mbox{subject to}\quad\Phi x=\Phi x_{0}.

(5.1)

Following the notation in
[
7
]
, we suppose that
\mathbb{R}^{p}
is decomposed into
q
groups by

\mathbb{R}^{p}=\bigoplus_{g=1}^{q}V_{g},

(5.2)

where each
V_{g}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with the same dimension
G
. For any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we write
u=\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{q}u_{g}
with

being the vector in group

. The
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
is defined by

\|u\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}=\sum_{g=1}^{q}\|u_{g}\|.

(5.3)

Its dual is the
\ell_{\infty}/\ell_{2}

\|u\|_{\ell_{\infty}/\ell_{2}}=\max_{1\leq g\leq q}\|u_{g}\|.

(5.4)

With
\bar{u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}D^{*}x_{0}

\partial\|\bar{u}\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}|\;v_{g}=\frac{\bar{u}_{g}}{\|\bar{u}_{g}\|_{2}}\mbox{ for }g\in I\mbox{ and }\|v_{g}\|\leq 1\mbox{ for }g\in K\right\}.

(5.5)

Thus
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
norm is decomposable at
\bar{u}
as in Remark
4.5
, where
T=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{g\in I}V_{g}
,

, and

e=\sum_{g\in I}\frac{\bar{u}_{g}}{\|\bar{u}_{g}\|}.

(5.6)

5.1
Descent cone of group sparsity

Sharp minima at
x_{0}
for problem (

) is studied in our previous section. However, unlike the case of
\ell_{1}
problem, a unique solution to problem (

. To characterize the solution uniqueness to group sparsity problem (

in (

Theorem 5.1

(Descent cone to
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}
problem and geometric characterization for solution uniqueness)
.

The descent cone at
x_{0}
to problem (
5.1
) is given by

\mathcal{D}_{J}(x_{0})=(\mathcal{E}\cap\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0}))\cup(\mbox{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})),

(5.7)

where

\mathcal{E}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;D_{T}^{*}w\in\mbox{\rm Im}\,\{(D^{*}_{T}x_{0})_{g}|\;g\in I\}\right\},

(5.8)

and
\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})
stands for the boundary of the critical cone
\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})
defined in (

), i.e.,

\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})=\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{p}||\;\left\langle D_{T}e,\,w\right\rangle+\|D^{*}_{S}w\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}=0\right\}.

(5.9)

Consequently,

is a unique solution to (

\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\Phi\cap\Big((\mathcal{E}\cap\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0}))\cup(\mbox{\rm int}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0}))\Big)=\{0\}.

(5.10)

Proof.

Let us start by verifying the inclusion “
\supset
” in (
5.7
). Recall that
J(x)=\|D^{*}x\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}
. For any
w\in\mbox{\rm int}\,(\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0}))
, we get from (

) that

dJ(x_{0})(w)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\dfrac{J(x_{0}+tw)-J(x_{0})}{t}<0.

Hence there is some

such that
J(x_{0}+t_{0}w)<J(x_{0})
. This ensures
w\in\mathcal{D}_{J}(x_{0})
. For any
w\in\mathcal{E}\cap\mbox{\rm bd}\,\mathcal{C}_{J}(x_{0})
, we represent
D_{T}^{*}w=\sum_{g\in I}\lambda_{g}(D^{*}x_{0})_{g}
with
\lambda_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
,
g\in I
and

\displaystyle J(x_{0}+t_{1}w)-J(x_{0})

\displaystyle=\|D^{*}x_{0}+t_{1}D^{*}w\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}-\|D^{*}x_{0}\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in I}|1+t_{1}\lambda_{g}|\|(D^{*}x_{0})_{g}\|+t_{1}\|D_{S}^{*}w\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}-\|D^{*}x_{0}\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}

where the third equality is from the choice of

To justify the reverse inclusion “

It follows from the latter and (

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\|D^{*}x_{0}+\alpha tD^{*}w\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}-\|D^{*}x_{0}\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}

Lemma 5.2

Proof.

x_{0}

Remark 5.4

(Checking the Strong Restricted Injectivity and the Analysis Nondegenerate Source Condition)
.

Set the matrix
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm\tiny def}}}{{=}}((D^{*}_{T}x_{0})_{g})_{g\in I}
to be an
p\times|I|
matrix, where
|I|
is the cardinality of
I
. Observe from (
5.15
) that

if and only if the following system

Since
S
is injective, we have
\lambda=S^{\dagger}D^{*}_{T}w=(S^{*}S)^{-1}S^{*}D^{*}_{T}w
due to (
2.2
). It follows that
w\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if

S(S^{*}S)^{-1}S^{*}D^{*}_{T}w-D^{*}_{T}w=0

(5.34)

Note that
S^{*}S={\rm diag}\,(\|(D^{*}_{T}x_{0})_{g}\|^{2})_{b\in I}
is an
|I|\times|I|
diagonal matrix. Representing
S
in terms of groups, we have

S(S^{*}S)^{-1}S^{*}={\rm diag\,}\left(\sum_{g\in I}\|(D_{T}^{*}x_{0})_{g}\|^{-2}\delta_{g}\right)SS^{*},

where
\delta_{g}=(0,\ldots,0,\underbrace{1,1,\ldots,1}_{\text{in $V_{g}$}},0,\ldots,0)
is the unit vector in
V_{g}
. This together with (
5.34
) tells us that

\mathcal{E}
is the kernel of the following matrix

Q=\mbox{\rm diag}\,\left(\sum_{g\in I}\|(D_{T}^{*}x_{0})_{g}\|^{2}\delta_{g}\right)D_{T}^{*}-SS^{*}D_{T}^{*}\quad\mbox{with}\quad S=((D^{*}_{T}x_{0})_{g})_{g\in I}.

(5.35)

The Strong Restricted Injectivity (
5.33
) is equivalent to
\mbox{\rm Ker}\,\begin{pmatrix}\Phi\\
Q\end{pmatrix}\cap\mbox{\rm Ker}\,D^{*}_{S}=\{0\}.

Furthermore,
M^{*}
forms a basis matrix of

\begin{pmatrix}\Phi\\

. Similarly to (

is the optimal solution to

So

is the optimal value to the following convex optimization problem

with

For any

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\langle De,w\rangle+\|D^{*}_{S}w\|&=\langle Dv,w\rangle-\langle DP_{S}v,w\rangle+\|D^{*}_{S}w\|\\
&=\displaystyle-\langle P_{S}v,D^{*}_{S}w\rangle+\|D^{*}_{S}w\|_{\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}}\\
&\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\left(-\langle v_{g},(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\rangle+\|(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\|\right)+\sum_{g\in H}\left(\langle v_{g},(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\rangle+\|(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\|\right)\\
&\displaystyle=\sum_{g\in L}\left(-\langle v_{g},(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\rangle+\|(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\|\right)+\sum_{g\in H}\|(D^{*}_{T}w)_{g}\|\geq 0.\end{array}

It follows that

5.43

Example 5.11

Conclusion

In this paper we show that sharp minima and strong minima play important roles for robust recovery with different rates. We also provide some quantitative characterizations for sharp solutions to convex regularized problems. Unique solutions to
\ell_{1}
problems are actually sharp solutions. For group sparsity problems, unique solutions are strong solutions. We also obtain several conditions guaranteeing solution uniqueness to group sparsity problems. As solution uniqueness to
\ell_{1}
problem plays a central role in the area of exact recovery with high probability, we plan to use our results to find a better bound for exact recovery for group-sparsity problems in comparison with the one obtained in
[
7
,
29
]
, at which they only use sufficient conditions for solution uniqueness.

Example
4.14
and Example
5.11
raise important questions about the solution uniqueness and strong minima for the nuclear norm minimization problems. Unique solutions to (
1.2
) with the nulear norm are neither sharp nor strong solutions. But a strong solution in this case is certainly a unique solution. It means that second-order analysis can provide a sufficient condition for solution uniqueness, and such a condition should be weaker than the one in Theorem
4.6
for sharp solutions. However, unlike the analysis in Lemma
5.2
, the second subderivative of the nuclear norm is far more intricate to compute. Understanding solution uniqueness and strong minima for the case of the nuclear norm, or more generally for spectral functions, is a project that we plan to pursue in the future.
