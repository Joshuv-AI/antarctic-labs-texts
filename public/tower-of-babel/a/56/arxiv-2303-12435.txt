Discussing semigroup bounds with resolvent estimates
B. Helffer, J. Sjöstrand, J. Viola

Abstract
The purpose of this paper is to revisit the proof of the Gearhart-Prüss-Huang-Greiner theorem for a semigroup S ⁡ ( t ) S(t) , following the general idea of the proofs that we have seen in the literature and to get an explicit estimate on the operator norm of S ⁡ ( t ) S(t) in terms of bounds on the resolvent of the generator. In [ 13 ] by the first two authors, this was done and some applications in semiclassical analysis were given. Some of these results have been subsequently published in two books written by the two first authors [ 11 , 21 ] . A second work [ 14 ] by the first two authors presents new improvements partially motivated by a paper of D. Wei [ 25 ] . In this third paper, we continue the discussion on whether the aforementioned results are optimal, and whether one can improve these results through iteration. Numerical computations will illustrate some of the abstract results.

1.3.2
Riccati equation and definition of

Define

F(x,y):=-(x^{2}+2xy+1)\,.

For
\mu:[0,+\infty[\to\mathbb{R}

\Psi:]0,T[\to\mathbb{R}
the maximal solution to

\begin{cases}\Psi^{\prime}(b)=F(\Psi(b),\mu(b))\\
\lim_{b\to 0^{+}}\Psi(b)=+\infty.\end{cases}

(1.14)

We then define

b^{*}(\mu):=\inf\{b>0\>:\>\Psi(b)=1\},

(1.15)

with the usual understanding that if
\Psi(b)
is never equal to
1
then
b^{*}(\mu)=+\infty
.
If needed we write
\Psi(b;\mu)
when we want to mention the dependence on
\mu
.

Equivalently, if

\begin{cases}\Phi^{\prime}(b)=-F(\Phi(b),\mu(b))\\
\Phi(0)=0,\end{cases}

(1.16)

then it can be proven that
\Phi(b)=1/\Psi(b)
for all
b\in]0,b^{*}(\mu)]
and

b^{*}(\mu)=\inf\{b>0\>:\>\Phi(b)=1\}.

(1.17)

The proofs of these results and others are given in
[
14
]
(Section 3).

In Theorem
1.7
which follows, one uses a rescaled version of

. When

\mu(t)=\mu(t;m,\omega,r)=\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{m^{\prime}(t)}{m(t)}-\omega\right),

(1.18)

and

\begin{cases}\phi^{\prime}(t)=r(\phi(t)^{2}+2\mu(t)\phi(t)+1),\\
\phi(0)=0,\end{cases}

(1.19)

let

a^{*}=a^{*}(m,\omega,r)=\inf\{t\geq 0\>:\>\phi(t)=1\}.

(1.20)

One may check that

a^{*}=\frac{1}{r}b^{*}(\mu).

(1.21)

1.4
Goal and organization of the paper

The goal of the present work is to explore the optimality of this result and possible improvements which could be obtained by iteration of the theorem or its application with different pairs

with

.

In Section 2, we discuss how the result of Theorem
1.7
depends on the parameters

,
\omega
, and

. In Section 3, we discuss the example of a shift on a bounded interval, for which the bound of Theorem

3
Analysis of the differentiation
operator on an interval.

The starting point is a paragraph in
[
11
, Chapter 14]
presenting a toy model described by Embree-Trefethen
[
23
, Chapter 15]
. The goal is to prove that in this case the Wei constant
e^{\pi/2}
in (
1.13
) is optimal in the sense that

\sup\{e^{r(0)t}\|\exp(tA)\|\>:\>-A\textnormal{ is }m\textnormal{-accretive},t\geq 0\}=e^{\pi/2}.

We consider the operator
A
defined on
L^{2}(]0,1[)
by

D(A)=\{u\in H^{1}(]0,1[)\,,\,u(1)=0\}\,,

(3.1a)

and

Au=u^{\prime}\,,\,\forall u\in D(A)\,.

(3.1b)

This is clearly a closed operator with dense domain.

The adjoint of
A
is defined on
L^{2}(]0,1[)

by

D(A^{*})=\{u\in H^{1}(]0,1[)\,,\,u(0)=0\}\,,

and

A^{*}u=-u^{\prime}\,,\,\forall u\in D(A^{*})\,.

Lemma 3.1
.

With
A
as above,

\sigma(A)=\emptyset
and
A
has compact resolvent.

Proof.

First we can observe that
(A-z)
is injective on
D(A)
for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. This is simply the observation that
u\in{\rm Ker}(A-z)
should satisfy

-u^{\prime}(t)=z\,u(t)\mbox{ and }u(1)=0\,.

One also easily verifies that, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
,
the
inverse is given by

[(z-A)^{-1}f](x)=-\int_{x}^{1}\exp z(x-s)\;f(s)\,ds\,.

(3.2)

It is also clear that this operator is compact (for example because its distribution kernel is in
L^{2}(]0,1[\times]0,1[)
).
∎

We recall that

\psi(z):=||(A-z)^{-1}||

(3.3)

is subharmonic. Observing that, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the map

u\mapsto U_{\alpha}u:=\exp(i\alpha x)\,u

is a unitary
transform on
L^{2}(]0,1[)
, which maps
D(A)
onto
D(A)
and such that
U_{\alpha}^{-1}AU_{\alpha}=A+i\alpha
, we deduce that
\psi
depends only on
\Re z
.

For
u\in D(A)
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

-\Re\langle(A-z)u\,,\,u\rangle=\Re z\|u\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|u(0)|^{2}\geq\Re z\|u\|^{2}\,.

In particular
-A
is accretive and satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1.6
of D. Wei.

In order to apply Theorem
1.6
, we have to compute
r(0)=1/\psi(0)
. Hence we have to compute
||A^{-1}||
. In our case, we get that
r(0)
is the square root of the smallest eigenvalue of
A^{*}A
. It is easy to show that the domain of
A^{*}A
is given by

D(A^{*}A)=\{u\in H^{2}([0,1]),u^{\prime}(0)=u(1)=0\}\,,

(3.4a)

and that for
u\in D(A^{*}A)

A^{*}Au=-u^{\prime\prime}\,.

(3.4b)

The lowest eigenvalue is
\pi^{2}/4
with corresponding eigenspace generated by
u_{0}(x)=\cos(\pi x/2)\,
. So finally we have

r(0)=\pi/2\,,

and Wei’s theorem gives

||S(t)||\leq\exp\left(\pi/2(1-t)\right)\,.

(3.5)

On the other hand, one can directly compute the norm of
S(t)
. We have indeed for
u\in L^{2}(]0,1[)
:

(S(t)u)(x)=\tilde{u}(x+t)\,,

where
\tilde{u}
is the extension of
u
by
0
on
]1,+\infty[
. For
t>1
, one immediately sees that

S(t)=0\,.

For
t<1
, one gets

||S(t)||=1\,.

We have indeed

1\geq||S(t)||\geq||S(t)\phi||_{L^{2}}\,,

for any normalized
L^{2}
function on
]0,1[
. If we choose
\phi_{t}
such that
||\phi_{t}||=1
and
{\rm supp}(\phi_{t})\subset(t,1)
, we immediately get

||S(t)\phi||_{L^{2}}=||\tilde{\phi}_{t}(\cdot+t)||=1\,.

We now show that Wei’s constant is optimal. Suppose that there exists
C<1
such that

||S(t)||\leq C\exp\big(\pi/2(1-t)\big)\,.

(3.6)

For
t<1
, this implies

1\leq C\exp\big(\pi/2(1-t)\big)\,.

(3.7)

We get a contradiction as
t\rightarrow 1
(with
t<1
).

Remark 3.2
.

As observed in
[
23
]
, one can discretize the preceding problem by considering, for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the matrix

A_{n}=nA_{1}
with
A_{1}=I+J

where
J
is the
n\times n
matrix such that

. One can observe that the spectrum of

is
\{n\}
.

\triangle

We begin by introducing the function

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a unique continuous function
\nu(z)
on
\mathbb{R}
with values in
i]0,+\infty[
for

[0,\pi[

The functions
\nu(z)^{2}
and

z^{2}+\nu(z)^{2}=4z^{2}e^{-2|z|}(1+\mathcal{O}(e^{-2|z|}).

Proof.

The function

f(\nu)=-\nu\cot\nu,\quad\nu\in]0,\pi[

is increasing, since its derivative is
f^{\prime}(\nu)=(2\sin^{2}\nu)^{-1}(2\nu-\sin(2\nu))
, with limits

\lim_{\nu\to 0^{+}}f(\nu)=-1,\quad\lim_{\nu\to\pi^{-}}f(\nu)=+\infty.

Similarly,

g(\eta)=f(i\eta)=-\eta\coth\eta,\quad\eta\in]0,+\infty[

is decreasing with

\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}g(\eta)=-1,\quad\lim_{\eta\to+\infty}g(\eta)=-\infty.

These functions together allow us to define an implicit function
\nu:\mathbb{R}\to i]0,+\infty[\cup[0,\pi[
such that
\nu(-1)=0
and

f(\nu(z))=z,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{-1\}.

If

there is a unique
\nu(z)\in]0,\pi[

. If
z<-1
, there is a unique
\eta(z)\in]0,+\infty[
satisfying

, and we let
\nu(z)=i\eta(z).
Hence, we have

. The value of
\nu(-1)
ensures that
\nu(z)
is continuous on all of
\mathbb{R}
.

Because

\nu(z)
is an increasing function from
]-1,+\infty[
to
]0,\pi[
, and
\nu(z)^{2}
is likewise increasing on
]-1,\infty[
. For
z\in]-\infty,-1[

\eta(z)=-i\nu(z)
is decreasing from
]-\infty,-1[
to
]0,+\infty[
, so
\nu(z)^{2}=-\eta(z)^{2}
is increasing. Since
\nu
is continuous,
\nu(z)^{2}
is increasing on
\mathbb{R}
.

As for

, if

then

z^{2}+\nu^{2}=(-\nu\cot\nu)^{2}+\nu^{2}=\left(\frac{\nu}{\sin\nu}\right)^{2}.

The function
\nu/\sin\nu
is increasing and positive on
]0,\pi[
, as can be seen from its derivative

\left(\frac{\nu}{\sin\nu}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sin\nu^{2}}\,(\sin\nu-\nu\cos\nu)>0.

Therefore

is increasing on

, as an increasing function of an increasing function. If
z<-1
, then

with

with
\eta/\sinh\eta

As for the asymptotic behavior of

\eta=-z\left(1-2e^{-2|z|}+\mathcal{O}(e^{-4|z|})\right).

4
Combining iteration and the semigroup property

4.1
Semigroup property for
m

In this subsection we study whether an upper bound
m(t)
can be improved through the elementary observation that, when
S(t)
is a one-parameter semigroup,

\|S(t_{1}+t_{2})\|\leq\|S(t_{1})\|\|S(t_{2})\|\,.

Suppose that

m:\,[0,+\infty[\to]0,+\infty[\textnormal{ is continuous with }m(0)=1.

(4.1)

We assume also that

\|S(t)\|\leq m(t),\ \ t\geq 0\,,

(4.2)

where as usual
S(t)
is a strongly continuous semigroup of operators with
S(0)=I
.

4.1.1
Semigroupization

If
t=t_{1}+t_{2}
,
t_{j}\geq 0
,
we have

\|S(t)\|\leq\|S(t_{1})\|\|S(t_{2})\|\leq m(t_{1})m(t_{2}),

so

\|S(t)\|\leq\widetilde{m}_{2}(t),

where

\widetilde{m}_{2}(t)=\inf_{t_{1}+t_{2}=t}m(t_{1})m(t_{2}),

where it is understood that the
t_{j}
are restricted to
[0,+\infty[
. Since
m(0)=1
, we have
\widetilde{m}_{2}(t)\leq m(t).
Put

\widetilde{m}_{1}=m
and define for
N\geq 2
,

\widetilde{m}_{N}(t)=\inf_{t_{1}+\dots+t_{N}=t}m(t_{1})m(t_{2})\cdots m(t_{N}).

(4.3)

The continuity of
m
implies that
\widetilde{m}_{N}
is continuous.
Clearly,
\widetilde{m}_{N+1}\leq\widetilde{m}_{N}
, and

\|S(t)\|\leq\widetilde{m}_{N}(t).

If
N_{1},\dots,N_{M}\in\{1,2,\dots\}
,
N=N_{1}+N_{2}+\dots+N_{M}
, then

\widetilde{m}_{N}(t)=\inf_{s_{1}+...+s_{M}=t}\widetilde{m}_{N_{1}}(s_{1})\widetilde{m}_{N_{2}}(s_{2})...\widetilde{m}_{N_{M}}(s_{M}).

(4.4)

From this we get

Proposition 4.1
.

If for some
N\in\{1,2,\dots\}
we have
\widetilde{m}_{N+1}=\widetilde{m}_{N}
, then

\widetilde{m}_{M}=\widetilde{m}_{N}
for all
M>N
.

Recall that
N\mapsto\widetilde{m}_{N}
is decreasing and define

\widetilde{m}_{\infty}:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{m}_{N}.

(4.5)

Since the
\widetilde{m}_{N}
’s are continuous,
\widetilde{m}_{\infty}
is upper semi-continuous.
By construction,
\|S(t)\|\leq\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t)
if
(
4.1
) and (
4.2
) hold. Moreover,

\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t)=\inf_{s_{1}+...+s_{M}=t}\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(s_{1})...\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(s_{M}),

(4.6)

From now on we denote by
\mathfrak{S}
the map associating with
m
the function
\widetilde{m}_{\infty}
and note that

\mathfrak{S}\circ\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}\,.

(4.7)

We say that
m
is the
\mathfrak{S}
-invariant if

\mathfrak{S}(m)=m
.

Remark 4.2
.

We can modify the definitions above by restricting
s_{j}
to
certain finite
t
-dependent sets. For
N=1,2,2^{2},2^{3},...
, let

\Gamma_{N}(t)=\mathbb{N}\frac{t}{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,...\}
. For
t\geq 0
, define
\widehat{m}_{1}(t)=m(t)
,

\widehat{m}_{N}(t)=\inf_{t_{1},...,t_{N}\in\Gamma_{N}(t),\atop t_{1}+...+t_{N}=t}m(t_{1})...m(t_{N}).

Notice that

\widehat{m}_{2}(t)=\min\left(m(t/2)^{2},m(t)\right).

Again,
N\mapsto\widehat{m}_{N}
is decreasing, and if (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) hold, then
\|S(t)\|\leq\widehat{m}_{N}(t)
.

Clearly,
\widehat{m}_{N}(t)\geq\widetilde{m}_{N}(t)
, so

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{m}_{N}(t)\geq\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{m}_{N}(t)\,.

On the
other hand, due to the continuity of
m
, we see that for every

N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{m}_{N}(t)\leq\widetilde{m}_{N_{0}}(t)\,,

hence

\lim_{N\to\infty}\widehat{m}_{N}(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\widetilde{m}_{N}(t).

\triangle

4.1.2
Semi-groupization in an interval

We say that
m
is
\mathfrak{S}
-invariant on
[0,T]
, if
m(t_{1}+t_{2})\leq m(t_{1})m(t_{2})
for
all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,T]
with
t_{1}+t_{2}\in[0,T]

We have

\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t)=\inf_{N\geq 1}\inf_{t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{N}\geq 0\atop t_{1}+...+t_{N}=t}\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t_{1})\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t_{2})...\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t_{N}).

(4.8)

In fact, it suffices to use the definition (
4.3
)-(

) for each
factor
\widetilde{m}_{\infty}(t_{j})
in the right-hand side of (

) .

More generally, for

(4.9)

As above we check that

\mathfrak{S}(\widetilde{m}_{\infty},s,t)=\mathfrak{S}(m,s,t).

(4.10)

4.1.3
Semi-groupization in a discrete setting

We now discuss the question of approximating

[[m,n]]=[m,n]\cap\mathbb{Z}

Whether

A direct approach to evaluating this formula would involve a number of terms equal to the partition function of

), but this is unnecessary. If

are increasing and that therefore either

or

. In the latter case,

and the former case

formally corresponds to
t_{1}=0
and
t_{2}=t
. We therefore see that for any

,

\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(s)=\min\{m(s),\min\{\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(s_{1})\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(s-s_{1})\>:s_{1}\in h\mathbb{N},h\leq s_{1}\leq s/2\}\}.

For example, using that

, we compute

\begin{array}[]{ll}\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(h)&=m(h),\\
\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(2h)&=\min\{m(2h),\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(h)^{2}\},\\
\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(3h)&=\min\{m(3h),\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(h)\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(2h)\},\\
\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(4h)&=\min\{m(4h),\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(h)\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(3h),\widetilde{m}_{\infty,h}(2h)^{2}\},\end{array}

and so on.

When

.

4.3
Preservation of log-concave upper bounds

It is well-known that a concave function

satisfying
f(0)\geq 0
is subadditive, since, by writing

and similarly for

f(s)+f(t)=f(\frac{s}{s+t}(s+t))+f(\frac{t}{s+t}(s+t))\geq f(s+t).

Therefore whenever
\log m
is concave,
m
is
\mathfrak{S}
-invariant. We now show that this property is preserved by
U
, and one does not need to apply
\mathfrak{S}
if one begins the iterations with a

-concave function.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
m:[0,+\infty[\to[0,+\infty[
is log-concave, then for any

and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
U(m,\omega,r)(t)
in (
1.24
) is also log-concave.

Proof.

If
a^{*}=+\infty
then
U(m,\omega,r)=m
; the claim is trivially true in this case. We therefore suppose that
a^{*}<+\infty
.

Let

\ell(t)=2\log m(a^{*})+(\omega-r)(t-2a^{*}),

so that
\log U(m,\omega,r)(t)=\min\{\log m(t),\ell(t)\}
for all
t\geq 2a^{*}
. By the semigroup property,
\log m(2a^{*})\leq\ell(2a^{*})
. By concavity of

there exists some

, for instance the derivative from the right of

at

, such that

If

, then

for all

, so

r>r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{4},\omega)
, then
a^{*}(m_{0},\omega,r)<\frac{\pi}{4}
and
\omega-r<-1
. Therefore
a^{*}(m_{1},\omega,r)=a^{*}(m_{0},\omega,r)
and
U(m_{1},\omega,r)=U(m_{0},\omega,r)
is less than
m_{1}
on

. Consequently,

U(m_{0},\omega,r)=U(m_{1},\omega,r)=\min\{m_{1},U(m_{0},\omega,r)\}.

It is therefore
U(m_{0},\omega,r)
which is everywhere stronger as an upper bound than
m_{1}
. For similar reasons, if
r<\omega+1
and if
r>r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{4},\omega)
, then

m_{1}=U(U(m_{0},\omega,r),0,1)=\min\{m_{1},U(m_{0},\omega,r)\},

and it is
m_{1}
which provides the smaller upper bound.

We next consider the case of those
(\omega_{2},r_{2})
which satisfy

\max\{r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{2},\omega_{2}),1+\omega_{2}\}\leq r_{2}\leq r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{4},\omega_{2}).

Note that this implies that
\omega\leq 0
. In this case
m_{1}=U(m_{0},0,1)
and
U(m_{1},0,1)
are both constant and equal to
1
on
[0,\frac{\pi}{4}]
. Consequently, the values of
a^{*}
are unchanged (that is,
a^{*}(m_{0},1,0)=a^{*}(U(m_{0},\omega_{2},r_{2}),1,0)
and
a^{*}(m_{0},\omega_{2},r_{2})=a^{*}(m_{1},\omega_{2},r_{2})
). Therefore

U(m_{1},\omega_{2},r_{2})=U(U(m_{0},\omega_{2},r_{2}),0,1)=\min\{m_{1},U(m_{0},\omega_{2},r_{2})\}.

(5.8)

In other words, taking the minimum of the estimates obtained from
(\omega_{1},r_{1})=(0,1)
and
(\omega_{2},r_{2})
gives a different and superior upper bound to the estimates taken separately, but iterating the procedure coming from Theorem
1.7
gives no new information.

For the same reasons, if

r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{4},\omega_{2})\leq r_{2}\leq\min\{\omega_{2}+1,r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{8},\omega_{2})\},

(which implies
\omega\geq 1
) then (
5.8
) holds as well.

The only cases in which iterating Theorem
1.7
could provide a better estimate are when

\max\{0,\omega_{2}+1\}<r_{2}<r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{2},\omega_{2})

or when

r^{*}(\frac{\pi}{8},\omega_{2})<r_{2}<\omega_{2}+1.

This is a very restrictive set of
(\omega_{2},r_{2})

\omega<-0.8891
and the latter requires that
\omega>4.7391

1.7

. We illustrate this phenomenon with an example.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

be as in Proposition

a^{*}(m_{0},\omega,r)=\frac{1}{2},\quad\forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}.

Proof.

We compute
a_{0}^{*}

\phi(t_{0})=0

\nu=\nu(\omega)\in]0,\pi[

We omit the computation when

which is essentially the same.
∎

We note that, with

6
Concluding remarks

We have seen that Theorem
1.6
of D. Wei and Theorem
1.7
of the first two authors of the current work are optimal when applied to the differentiation operator on an interval in (
3.1
). The authors are not aware, however, of an example showing that the upper bound in Theorem
1.6
is optimal for
t>\frac{\pi}{2r(0)}
.

There are also simple examples (such as semigroups generated by Jordan blocks) such that the upper bounds of Theorem
1.7
are not optimal. In certain situations, using the semigroup property for norms (Section
4
) and iteration (Sections
4
and
5
) can offer some modest improvements, but a significant gap remains. The authors would naturally be interested in results which narrow this gap, either by improving Theorem
1.7
or finding examples with large semigroup norms (and appropriate bounds on the resolvents of generators).
