A Theoretical Justification for Image Inpainting using Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models
Litu Rout, Advait Parulekar, Constantine Caramanis, Sanjay Shakkottai

Abstract
We provide a theoretical justification for sample recovery using diffusion based image inpainting in a linear model setting. While most inpainting algorithms require retraining with each new mask, we prove that diffusion based inpainting generalizes well to unseen masks without retraining. We analyze a recently proposed popular diffusion based inpainting algorithm called RePaint [ LDRYTV22 ] , and show that it has a bias due to misalignment that hampers sample recovery even in a two-state diffusion process. Motivated by our analysis, we propose a modified RePaint algorithm we call RePaint + that provably recovers the underlying true sample and enjoys a linear rate of convergence. It achieves this by rectifying the misalignment error present in drift and dispersion of the reverse process. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first linear convergence result for a diffusion based image inpainting algorithm.

Introduction

We study the mathematical principles that underlie the empirical success of image inpainting using Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)

,
HJA20
,

,

[

]
, and Stable Diffusion

A major challenge in this task is to generate parts that are consistent with the available parts of the image; generative modeling is the key tool for this inpainting goal.

Variational-Auto Encoders (VAEs)

DDPM represents a class of generative models that learn to diffuse a clean image into tractable noise and then follow a reverse Markov process to produce a clean image by progressively denoising pure noise

(see

[

. For restoration tasks such as image inpainting, DDPMs are interesting because they easily adapt to unknown tasks, such as new masks without having to go through the entire retraining process. This gives DDPM an edge over prior restoration techniques based upon GANs
[

,
YLYSLH18
]
and VAEs
[

.

However, one challenging aspect limiting their pervasive usage stems from the choice of hyper-parameters in the reverse Markov process. Although the forward diffusion process can be explicitly computed beforehand, the reverse process is computationally very expensive as it requires sampling at every intermediate state. Furthermore, popular diffusion based inpainting algorithms, such as RePaint
[
LDRYTV22
]
require additional resampling at each of these states to harmonize inpainted parts with the rest of the image. These resampling steps increase the computational burden at the cost of semantically meaningful reconstruction. Also, Figure

shows that RePaint generates
biased
samples that hamper perfect recovery. Therefore, it is becoming increasingly important to address these bottlenecks to facilitate their successful real-world deployment. A persistent challenge in this respect stems from the fact that despite the remarkable progress of diffusion based inpainting, our theoretical understanding remains in its early stages.

To address these issues,
one emerging line of theoretical research aims to provide convergence guarantees for Score-based Generative Models (SGMs) that constitute DDPMs
[
CCLLSZ22
,
LLT22
,
LLT22a
,
CLL22
]
. A crucial assumption of these approaches is that the target distribution
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
assumes a density with respect to Lebesgue measure. Another line of work
[
Bor22
,
Pid22
]
studies convergence of SGMs under the famous manifold hypothesis
[
TSL00
,
FMN16
]
. Starting from a prior
p(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}})
, these lines of work analyze convergence of the reverse process
p_{{\bm{\theta}}}(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
to the target distribution
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
. Different from these lines of research, our paper focuses on the convergence of resampling step used to harmonize the inpainted image (
\wasyparagraph
3
).

The assumption that the target distribution admits a density according Lebesgue measure indicates that
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})>0
for all
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words, every
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
is a possible sample drawn from

FMN16
]

[

]

[

Bor22
]
prove that SGMs learn distributions supported on a low dimension substructure, satisfying the manifold hypothesis.

For inpainting, however, we need an additional structure on the data distribution similar to prior works in related disciplines
[

,
DGE18
,
JLDC20
,
JADPDT21
]
. This is because our goal is not just to show minimum discrepancy between

but also to prove sample wise convergence, i.e.,
\left\|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}-\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right\|\leq\epsilon
. It is worth mentioning that the problem of sample recovery is well studied in optimization literature

,
FNW07
,
BHSN10
,
FN03
,
BJPD17
]
. The focus of our paper, instead, is to use this well understood setting to provide a
theoretical justification for sample recovery using a DDPM-based non-optimization technique
[
LDRYTV22
]
. In this technique, we leverage the expressive power of diffusion based generative models
[
HJA20
]
without attempting to directly solve a constrained optimization problem. Following conventional wisdom
[
BJPD17
,
JLDC20
,
JADPDT21
]
, we implant a specific model on the data generating distribution that satisfies the manifold hypothesis, and show that the learning algorithm recovers this model by detecting the underlying substructure: see Figure

1.1
Contributions

Our main contribution is to provide a theoretical justification for diffusion based image inpainting in a linear model setting. By analyzing diffusion over two states,
our analysis explains previously not understood phenomena. Importantly, we derive algorithmic insights which, as we demonstrate later,
deliver improvement beyond the two-state processes.

First, unlike prior inpainting methods, we prove that diffusion based image inpainting easily adapts to each new mask without retraining (
Theorem
4
in
\wasyparagraph
3
).

Next, inspired by the empirical success of a recently proposed diffusion based image inpainting method
[
LDRYTV22
]
, we analyze its theoretical properties in a system of two-state diffusion processes. We observe that there exists a bias due to misalignment that hampers perfect recovery: see Figure
1
. Our analysis motivates us to rectify this misalignment that helps eliminate the bias. We refer to this method as RePaint
+
. We provide a simplified version of RePaint
+
in
Algorithm
2
and a general version in
Algorithm
3
. We conduct toy experiments in Appendix
B
.

In the linear model setting, we derive a closed-form solution of the generative model using the transition kernels of DDPM (
Theorem
3
). Using this solution, we prove that RePaint
+
enjoys a linear rate of convergence in the resampling phase (
Theorem
4
). This allows us to appropriately choose the number of resampling rounds in practice. We generalize this notion of convergence to a setting where the generative model is approximate (
Theorem
6
). Further, we justify the benefits of resampling over slowing down the diffusion process in the context of inpainting (
\wasyparagraph
3.2.4
).

Background on Diffusion Models

Like other generative models, such as GANs
[
GPMXWOCB14
]
, VAEs
[
KW13
]
, and Flow
[
DSB17
]
, Score-based generative models (SGM)
[
SE19
]
learn to sample from an unknown distribution given a set of samples drawn from this distribution. In this section, we first briefly introduce key ingredients of SGM. Then, we detail the DDPM interpretation of SGM in
\wasyparagraph
2.2
, which holds the foundation of RePaint
+
.

2.1
Score-based Generative Model

The central part of SGM consists of two Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs). The
forward SDE
(
1
), an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process in its simplest form, transforms the data distribution
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
to a reference distribution, which is
{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
in most cases. Here, a sample
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}
at time
t
follows:

\displaystyle d\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}=-\frac{1}{2}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}dt+dW_{t};~\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}).

(1)

With time rescaling parameters
\alpha_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
, where
\alpha_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}ds
, and normalized time
t=\{0,\cdots,1\}
, the transition kernel of (
1
) becomes:

\displaystyle q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\mid\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)={\mathcal{N}}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}};\exp{(-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{2})\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}},\left(1-\exp{(-\alpha_{t})}\right){\bm{I}}}\right),

which leads to the well known form of forward SDE:

\displaystyle d\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}=-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{t}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}d\mathbf{B}_{t};~\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}).

Here,
\left\{d\mathbf{B}_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
represents the standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
reverse SDE
has a form similar to the forward SDE with time reversal:

\displaystyle d\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}=\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1-t}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}+\beta_{1-t}\nabla\log q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})+\sqrt{\beta_{1-t}}d\mathbf{B}_{t},

where
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\sim p\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\right)\coloneqq{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
. Usually, the Euler-Maryuama discretization scheme is employed while implementing these SDEs in practice. Since we do not have access to
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
, a neural network
s_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)
is used to approximate the score function
\nabla\log q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
. Training is performed by minimizing a score-matching objective
[
HD05
,
Vin11
,
SE19
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\\
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\sim q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\end{subarray}}\left[\left\|\nabla\log q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})-s_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)\right\|^{2}\right].

Next, we discuss DDPM and its connection with SGM.

2.2
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model

Diffusion model
[
SWMG15
,
HJA20
]
is an emerging class of generative models that share strikingly similar properties with score-based generative models
[
SE19
]
. It consists of two stochastic processes. First, the
forward process (diffusion process)
gradually adds Gaussian noise to an image according to a variance schedule. This is a Markov chain with stationary distribution typically set to a tractable distribution that is easy to sample from, e.g., a standard Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
. Second, the
reverse process (denoising process)
learns to gradually denoise a sample drawn from the tractable distribution. The denoising process continues until it produces a high-quality image from the original data distribution.

2.2.1
Forward Process

Let
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
denote a sample in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
drawn from the data distribution
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})
. For discrete time steps
1\leq t\leq T
,
q({\bm{x}}_{t})
represents the distribution at time
t
. In the
forward process
, the diffusion takes place according to a
fixed
Gaussian transition kernel, i.e.,

\displaystyle q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1:T}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\coloneqq\prod_{t=1}^{T}q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}});~q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}})\coloneqq\mathcal{N}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}},\beta_{t}{\bm{I}}_{d}\right),

(2)

where
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{T}
denote a deterministic variance schedule. Thus, a sample from
q(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}})
is given by
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}=\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\mathbf{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
, where
\mathbf{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
. The first term is called drift and the second, dispersion. An important property of the Gaussian diffusion process is that it has a simple form when sampling from any intermediate time steps. For
\alpha_{t}\coloneqq 1-\beta_{t}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{t}\coloneqq\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_{s}
, the conditional probability at time
t
becomes:

\displaystyle q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}\right)={\mathcal{N}}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}};\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}},\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right){\bm{I}}_{d}\right).

2.2.2
Reverse Process

The
reverse process
is a Markov chain initialized at the stationary distribution of the
forward process
, i.e.,
p(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}})={\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
. Unlike the forward process, the reverse process
p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0:T}}\right)
is generated from a
learned
Gaussian kernel, i.e.,

\displaystyle p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0:T}}\right)=p(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}})\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right);~p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right)\coloneqq{\mathcal{N}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}};\mu_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right),\Sigma_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)\right),

(3)

where
\mu_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)
and
\Sigma_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)
are neural networks parameterized by
\theta
to predict the mean and the variance of
p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right)
, respectively.

2.2.3
Training and Inference

DDPM aims to maximize the likelihood of a sample generated by the reverse process. As per equation (
3
), the probability assigned to such a sample is obtained by marginalizing over the remaining random variables, i.e.,
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}},\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{2}},\cdots,\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}
as denoted by the following expression:

\displaystyle p(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}})=\int p(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0:T}})d\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1:T}},

which is not easy to compute. It becomes tractable by considering relative probability between the forward and the reverse processes
[
Jar97
,
SWMG15
]
, leading to the variational lower bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[-\log p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\log\frac{p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0:T}}\right)}{q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1:T}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[-\log p\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\right)-\sum_{t\geq 1}\log\frac{p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right)}{q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}\right)}\right].

(4)

This objective is further simplified to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[{\mathcal{D}}_{KL}\left(q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\parallel p\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\right)\right)+\sum_{t>1}{\mathcal{D}}_{KL}\left(q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\parallel p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right)\right)-\log p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}}\right)\Big],

(5)

where
q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}};\tilde{\mu}_{t}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right),\tilde{\beta}_{t}{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
,
\tilde{\mu}_{t}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)\coloneqq\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{a}_{t}}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{t}{\bm{I}}_{d}\coloneqq\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\beta_{t}
. Equation (
5
) contains
three
crucial terms. The
first
term measures the divergence between the stationary distribution of the forward process conditioned on a clean sample
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)
and the initial distribution of the reverse process. The divergence is negligible in practice, thanks to the exponential convergence of OU processes. This is ignored as there are no trainable parameters,
{\bm{\theta}}
.

The
second
term measures the divergence between the forward and the reverse process at intermediate time steps,
1<t\leq T
. Since the conditional probabilities are Gaussians, this
can be explicitly computed as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\frac{1}{2\beta_{t}^{2}}\left\|\tilde{\mu}_{t}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)-\mu_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)\right\|^{2}\right]+Constant,

(6)

where
p_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}\right)={\mathcal{N}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}};\mu_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right),\beta_{t}{\bm{I}}\right)
. In the training phase,
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}
is replaced with
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}
since the
first
term in (
5
) is negligible. Further, reparameterization of the posterior mean as
\mu_{\theta}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}~\mathbf{\epsilon}_{\theta}\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)\right)
yields better results and a simplified loss
[
HJA20
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}_{0},\mathbf{\epsilon}}\left[\frac{1}{2\alpha_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\left\|\mathbf{\epsilon}-\mathbf{\epsilon}_{\theta}\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}~\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}~\mathbf{\epsilon},t\right)\right\|^{2}\right].

(7)

During inference, for
\mathbf{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(0,{\bm{I}}\right)
, a sample is generated by
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t-1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}}~\mathbf{\epsilon}_{\theta}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)\right)+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}~\mathbf{\epsilon}
.

The
third
term is the standard maximum likelihood estimator.
With this reparameterization, the problem of learning probability is converted to a practically implementable minimum mean squared error (MMSE) problem. The goal of training DDPMs is to obtain an optimal
\theta^{*}
that solves the MMSE problem with posterior mean (
6
) or noise (
7
).

During inference, the reverse process is initialized at the stationary distribution of the forward process, usually
{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
. Then, the reverse Gaussian transition kernel is followed using
\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{t}},t\right)
. In the final step,
\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}},1\right)
is displayed without noise, which is a sample from the original data distribution. Ignoring some constant factors, the forward and reverse
SDEs
of SGM and the forward and reverse
processes
of DDPM are idential if we use Gaussian transition kernel. Throughout this paper, we interchangeably use these terms.

3.2.2
Image Inpainting using RePaint
+

Continuing with the two-state model, there are essentially three key ingredients to diffusion based image inpainting. First, we initialize the reverse process at
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}_{d}\right)
. Using the reverse Gaussian transition kernel obtained in
Theorem
3
, we generate
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}=\left(1/\sqrt{1-\beta}\right)\mu_{{\bm{\theta}}^{*}}\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}}\right)
. As per
Theorem
3
,
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
is
a sample
that lies on the manifold. However, we note that
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
is
not the sample
we are looking for as it could potentially lie far away from
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
.

Second, we replace certain parts of
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
as per the given mask
{\bm{m}}
with known information from
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
using the following rule:
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}={\bm{m}}\odot\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}+(\bm{1}-{\bm{m}})\odot\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
. This produces an arbitrary sample
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since the reverse SDE pushes an arbitrary sample in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto the manifold, we feed
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}
as an
input
to the reverse SDE in the first resampling round. Alternatively, one may pass
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}
through the forward SDE without adding any noise. In this case, the generated sample
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{1}^{1}}
serves as the input to the reverse SDE. This provides just an additional scaling that is easy to handle in our analysis.

Finally, the reverse SDE generates
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}
, which upon realignment is expected to be
\mathbf{\epsilon}
-close to the original image, i.e.,
\left\|\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}^{1}}-\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right\|\leq\epsilon
. This completes one resampling step.

Usually, one resampling step is not sufficient to obtain an
\mathbf{\epsilon}
-close solution. We ask how many resampling steps
Algorithm
2
needs to generate a satisfactory inpainted image. Furthermore, what is the rate at which it converges to the
\mathbf{\epsilon}
-neighborhood of the original image. We answer these questions favorably in
Theorem
4
.

Among other controlling parameters,

Finally, the distance from the original sample, i.e.,

We note that

is not necessarily an image residing on the manifold of the data generative distribution

Another cause of concern is the lack of harmonization between generated portions and rest of the image. Notably, the bias is persistent even if we consider diffusion over infinitely many states, justifying the importance of resampling over slowing down the diffusion process. This phenomenon is also observed in practice (

On the contrary, diffusion over two states with many resampling steps recovers the original sample as discussed in

4
Conclusion

We studied mathematical underpinnings of sample recovery in image inpainting using denoising diffusion probabilistic models. We presented a universal mask principle to prove that diffusion based inpainting easily adapts to unseen masks without retraining. Our theoretical analysis of a popular inpainting algorithm in a linear setting
revealed a previously not understood bias that hampered perfect recovery. With realignment of drift and dispersion in the reverse process, we proposed an algorithm called Repaint
+
that eliminated this bias, leading to perfect recovery in the limit. Further, We proved that RePaint
+
converged linearly to the
\epsilon
-neighborhood of the true underlying sample.
In the future, it would be interesting to study the convergence properties in a more general non-linear manifold setting.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim{\mathcal{U}}\left(1,\dots,T\right)\end{subarray}}\begin{bmatrix}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)^{-1}\left[{\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right]&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{3T}{T^{3}-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\times\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)\\
t\sim{\mathcal{U}}\left(1,\dots,T\right)\end{subarray}}\begin{bmatrix}\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}+\mathbf{\epsilon}_{i}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right)\mathbf{\epsilon}\right)\\

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim{\mathcal{U}}\left(1,\dots,T\right)\end{subarray}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\left[{\bm{I}}-{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right]&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{3T}{T^{3}-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\times\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\sim{\mathcal{U}}\left(1,\dots,T\right)\end{subarray}}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}+{\bm{e}}_{i}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right)\\
0\end{bmatrix},

where
{\bm{e}}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
is a one-hot encoded vector with only
i^{th}
coordinate taking the value
1
. Bringing the expectation inside and using matrix products, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{i}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right){\bm{I}}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{3T}{T^{3}-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{e}}_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right){\bm{I}}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{e}}_{i}\right)\\
0\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}\\
+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{e}}_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right)\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right){\bm{e}}_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)\par\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{a}}_{i}\\
+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{e}}_{i}-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right)\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right){\bm{e}}_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Stacking all the rows together, we obtain the closed-form solution for generative modeling:

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\\

-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\\
+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{I}}\\
-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\sqrt{\alpha_{t}}\right)-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\right)\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\\
+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}\right)}\right)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\end{subarray}}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}_{t}\left(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}\right)\right){\bm{I}}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\nu{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}+\gamma{\bm{I}}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
\square

A.7
Proof of
Theorem
11

To prove DDPM learns the distribution
q\left(\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)
, it is sufficient to show that
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\sim p\left(\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}\right)
has the structure
{\bm{A}}\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}
, where
\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
. Using the closed-form solution from
Corollary
10
, we run one step of the reverse process starting from
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
. Then, we rectify the misalignment in drift and dispersion of the reverse process. After recursively applying the reverse step for all the states, we obtain

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\xi_{T}\sqrt{\beta_{T}}\overleftarrow{\epsilon_{T}}+\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-2}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\xi_{T}\sqrt{\beta_{T-1}}\overleftarrow{\epsilon_{T-1}}

\displaystyle+\cdots+\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\left(\omega_{2}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{3}\sqrt{\beta_{3}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{3}}+\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{2}\sqrt{\beta_{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{2}}.

Since
\{\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{t}}\}_{t>1}
are IID Gaussian random variables, we rewrite the above epxression as:

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\bar{\Sigma}^{1/2}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}},

where
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}\left(\mathbf{0},{\bm{I}}\right)
and
\bar{\Sigma}
captures the sum of variances of the terms containing
\{\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{t}}\}_{t>1}
. Recall that
{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}={\bm{I}}
, the coefficients of drift
\omega_{t}=\omega=\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}\sqrt{2}^{1/T}}
and dispersion
\xi_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{2^{(t-1)/T}}{2\beta_{t}(T-1)}}
. Now, let us compute the variances of each term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{2}\sqrt{\beta_{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{2}}\right)\left(\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{2}\sqrt{\beta_{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{2}}\right)^{T}\right]=\omega_{1}^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\xi_{2}^{2}\beta_{2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{2}}^{T}\right]{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}=\omega_{1}^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\xi_{2}^{2}\beta_{2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\xi_{2}^{2}\beta_{2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}=\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}\sqrt{2}^{1/T}}\right)^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\left(\frac{2^{1/T}}{2\beta_{2}(T-1)}\right)\beta_{2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}=\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{2(T-1)};

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\left(\omega_{2}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{3}\sqrt{\beta_{3}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{3}}\right)\left(\left(\omega_{1}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\left(\omega_{2}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\xi_{3}\sqrt{\beta_{3}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{3}}\right)^{T}\right]=\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{2(T-1)};\cdots;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\xi_{T}\sqrt{\beta_{T}}\overleftarrow{\epsilon_{T}}\right)\left(\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\xi_{T}\sqrt{\beta_{T}}\overleftarrow{\epsilon_{T}}\right)^{T}\right]=\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{2(T-1)}.

Note that
\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)^{2}\coloneqq{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}={\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}
. Since there are
(T-1)
terms, the total covariance
\bar{\Sigma}=\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{2}
, which is equivalent to the following sample generated at the end of the reverse process:

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\omega_{t}\bar{\nu}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\right)\right)\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\left(\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}\sqrt{2}^{1/T}}\right)\bar{\nu}\right)\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}}{\sqrt{2}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}={\bm{A}}\left(\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)={\bm{A}}\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}.

Now, let us verify if
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}
lies on the data manifold. To see this, it is sufficient to show
\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}
has zero mean and unit covariance. Therefore, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]=\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\right]}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\right]}{\sqrt{2}}=\mathbf{0}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}\overleftarrow{{\bm{z}}_{0}}^{T}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{T}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(i)}}{{=}}\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}^{T}\right]{\bm{A}}}{2}+\frac{{\bm{A}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}^{T}\right]{\bm{A}}}{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(ii)}}{{=}}{\bm{I}},

where (i) uses the fact that
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}
and
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
are independent zero-mean random variables, and (ii) relies on unit covariance of
\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}
,
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
, and the property that
{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{A}}={\bm{I}}
. Thus, we finish the proof.
\square

. Starting from the Gaussian prior

\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}

\omega_{t}=\omega=\frac{1}{\bar{\nu}\sqrt{2}^{1/T}}

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\prod_{T-1}^{1}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{D}}({\bm{m}})\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}
. Similarly, by collecting the terms involving
\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}

\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{t/T}\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{D}}({\bm{m}})\right)^{t-1}\right)\times{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{D}}(\bm{1}-{\bm{m}})\overrightarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}

\{\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{t}}\}_{t=2}^{T}

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{t}}

\overrightarrow{\bar{\Sigma}}
and terms containing
\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}_{t}}
in the reverse process as
\overleftarrow{\bar{\Sigma}}
. Therefore, the final sample generated at the end of the reverse process with inpainting at each intermediate state becomes:

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{0}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\prod_{T-1}^{1}{\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{D}}({\bm{m}})\right){\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}\overleftarrow{{\bm{x}}_{T}}+\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{t/T}\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{A}}^{T}{\bm{D}}({\bm{m}})\right)^{t-1}\right)

\overleftarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}

\overrightarrow{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
to represent as a single Gaussian random variable. We keep them separate to explicitly highlight their contributions. This finishes the proof.
