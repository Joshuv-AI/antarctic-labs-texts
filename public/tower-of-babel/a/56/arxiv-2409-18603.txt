Which depth to use to construct functional boxplots?
Stanislav Nagy, Tomáš Mrkvička, Antonio Elías

Abstract
This paper answers the question of which functional depth to use to construct a boxplot for functional data. It shows that integrated depths, e.g., the popular modified band depth, do not result in well-defined boxplots. Instead, we argue that infimal depths are the only functional depths that provide a valid construction of a functional boxplot. We also show that the properties of the boxplot are completely determined by properties of the one-dimensional depth function used in defining the infimal depth for functional data. Our claims are supported by (i) a motivating example, (ii) theoretical results concerning the properties of the boxplot, and (iii) a simulation study.

This work aims to point out that the boxplot based on an infimal depth has much better properties than a boxplot based on an integrated depth (e.g., MBD). The reason is that the boxplot uses local information to decide which function is an outlier — namely, a function is flagged as outlying if it lies at any point outside the whiskers band. Thus, if the depth that defines the central region follows global principles, as all integrated depths do, then it does not match the locality paradigm used to flag outliers. The following motivating example demonstrates this.

Assume a simple Gaussian model on the interval
[0,1]
and two kinds of deviating functions. First is a
local
outlier, which significantly departs from the base model on bounded sub-domains of
[0,1]
. Second is a
global
outlier, which deviates from the base model in a less pronounced way, but on the whole domain
[0,1]
. The functions that we used for this illustrative example are shown in Figure
2
. Figure
3

erl
index (right) for our set of 120 functions, where 100 are from the base model, 10 are local outliers, and 10 are global outliers. The
erl

[
19
,
16
]
.

One expects that the functional boxplot based on an integrated depth will be sensitive particularly to the global outliers, whereas the functional boxplot based on an infimal depth will be sensitive to local outliers. Figure
4
allows us to assess this. On the
x
-axis, we see the depth attached to the data. On the
y
-axis, we display the outlyingness index of the curves, defined as the maximum inflation factor of the
50\%
central region that makes the datum fall outside the inflated central region. Outlyingness index
4
corresponds to the usual constant
1.5\times\mathrm{IQR}
from the one-dimensional boxplot. As we see in Figure
4
, MBD is indeed able to detect the global outliers (the curves in magenta get very low MBD on the
x
-axis), but its boxplot (
y
-axis) does not flag these functions as outlying. The figure also shows that the
erl
index is sensitive to local outliers (green points with low depth values on the
x
-axis), and the same is true for its boxplot (
y
-axis). Thus, the functional boxplot with an infimal depth is capable of detecting the local outliers, but the functional boxplot with an integrated depth fails to detect even the global outliers that are identified as extreme by the integrated depth. It is unrealistic to expect a functional boxplot to detect outliers that are not identified as extreme by the used depth; thus, we do not anticipate this. We, however, see that the boxplot based on an integrated depth fails to flag even the outliers detected by its own depth, while the boxplot based on an infimal depth does not suffer from such problems. Our example illustrates two fundamental problems of MBD-based boxplots:

Problem 1:

Lack of robustness.
Local outliers can seriously bloat the central region computed by an integrated depth. This happens because local outliers can obtain high integrated depth, and thus contribute to the shape of the central region. The subsequent expansion of the
50\%
central region to obtain the whiskers band then makes the functional boxplot insensitive to any deviations in the data.

Problem 2:

Outlier masking.
Even though the global outliers have the lowest MBD of all sample functions (see the left-hand panel of Figure
4
), they fail to be detected as outliers by the MBD boxplot. Even worse, they are completely contained in the
50\%
MBD central region.

The gist of our example shows that the integrated depth-based central regions do not satisfy a natural property one would expect from a boxplot based on bands:

(
BC
)
Band convexity:
A function with depth lower than all functions used to construct the central region
C
must leave
C
at least at one argument of the function.

We call this crucial property
band convexity
; in other works, it is also called the
intrinsic graphical interpretation
property
[
16
]
.

Fig 5:
Real data example: Household voltage circuit monitoring using an integrated depth-based boxplot (left) and an infimal depth-based boxplot (right). For a detailed description of the data see the Supplementary Material.

Our motivating example is not unrealistic — a real dataset exhibiting the same phenomenon can be found in Figure
5
. Both these examples illustrate our main misgivings concerning using integrated depths together with a functional boxplot. Several additional, more elaborate examples in this spirit can be found in the Supplementary Material. The rest of this paper contains theoretical proofs supporting and refining our arguments. First, after introducing the necessary background on bands and functional depths in Sections
2.1
–
2.4
, in Section
2.5
we prove that the central region constructed from an integrated depth and a finite sample of functions is inconsistent as the number of functions increases; in fact, it converges to the whole function space. Second, in Section
2.6
we prove that the band convexity of a depth is satisfied exclusively for depths of infimal type. Third, we prove that infimal depth satisfies the rearrangement invariance, another natural desideratum for the construction of a functional boxplot. Finally, in Section
2.7
we specify the properties of one-dimensional depths that are used to define the infimal depths, in relation to the properties of the resulting boxplot. A brief simulation study can be found in Section
3
. There, we further explore the statistical properties of various boxplots. In particular, we demonstrate
that the population MBD central region depends heavily on the number of functions from which it is constructed as well as on the amount of smoothness of the functions. The same problem does not appear for boxplots based on infimal depths. Some extensions of our theory and concluding remarks are in Section
4
. Technical details and additional supporting results are postponed to the Supplementary Material.

Functional boxplots and functional depth

Let
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathsf{P}\right)
be the probability space on which all random variables are defined. For a topological space
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{S}}\right)
stands for all Borel probability measures on
\mathcal{S}
, and
X\sim P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{S}}\right)
denotes a random variable
X
with distribution
P
. The one-dimensional Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
is denoted by
\lambda
.

We consider data living in
\mathcal{F}
, a Banach space of functions
\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}
for
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}
a compact interval; without loss of generality, we take
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]
. Only very mild properties are imposed on the function space
\mathcal{F}
. We assume that constant functions belong to
\mathcal{F}
, and that for any
x,y\in\mathcal{F}
we also have
x\,y
defined by
t\mapsto x(t)\,y(t)
as an element of
\mathcal{F}
. We further assume that the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
in
\mathcal{F}
is
compatible with
the absolute value
\left|\cdot\right|
in
\mathbb{R}
. This means that whenever
\left|u_{n}\right|\to\infty
in
\mathbb{R}
, then for every sequence of functions
\left\{x_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{F}
such that
x_{n}(t)=u_{n}
for some
t\in[0,1]
we can write
\left\|x_{n}\right\|\to\infty
. Finally, we require a weak
approximation property

(A)

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
t_{0}\in[0,1]
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\varepsilon>0
there exists a function
g=g(f,t_{0},y,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{F}
such that
g(t_{0})=y
, and
\lambda\left(\left\{t\in[0,1]\colon g(t)\neq f(t)\right\}\right)<\varepsilon
.

This property is satisfied for all interesting function spaces. For instance, for
\mathcal{F}
the space of continuous functions,
(A)
holds true thanks to the classical Luzin theorem
[
5
, Theorem 7.5.2]
and the Tietze-Urysohn extension theorem
[
5
, Theorem 2.6.4]
. The compatibility of the supremum norm
\left\|x\right\|=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\left|x(t)\right|
used in the space of continuous functions is trivially guaranteed as
\left|x(t)\right|\leq\left\|x\right\|
for each
t\in[0,1]
.

For a functional distribution
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
with
X\sim P
we denote by
P_{t}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
the distribution of the random variable
X(t)\in\mathbb{R}
, for
t\in[0,1]
.

2.1
Bands of functions

Band depths for functional data are inherently tied to the concept of bands in function spaces. A
band
of a collection of functions
B
is a set in
\mathcal{F}
of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)

(1)

\displaystyle=\Big\{x\in\mathcal{F}\colon\inf_{y\in B}y(t)\leq x(t)\leq\sup_{y\in B}y(t)\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,1]\Big\}.

The delimiting functions
y\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{*}
from
B
do not necessarily come from
\mathcal{F}
; they are allowed to take infinite values in
\mathbb{R}^{*}=\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{-\infty,\infty\right\}
. A trivial example of a band is the whole space
\mathcal{F}
, if
B
is composed of
y_{1}(t)=-\infty
and
y_{2}(t)=\infty
for all
t\in[0,1]
. In the common situation when
B
is finite with elements
y_{1},\dots,y_{m}
we write also
\mathcal{B}\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\right)
instead of
\mathcal{B}\left(\left\{y_{1},\dots,y_{m}\right\}\right)
. The set

\displaystyle\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}\colon\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)}x(t)\leq u\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)}x(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x(t)\colon x\in\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}

is called the
slice
of the band
\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)
at
t\in[0,1]
. Bands of functions are closely related to the envelopes of functional data
[
16
]
.

2.2
One-dimensional depths

The theoretical background of depths defined on Euclidean spaces
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is well studied
[
12
,
30
]
. According to
Zuo and Serfling [30]
, a depth function
D

should satisfy the following minimal criteria:

(1)

Affine invariance;

(2)

Maximality at the center;

(3)

Monotonicity w.r.t. the deepest point; and

(4)

Vanishing at infinity.

Restrict now the argumentation to the one-dimensional case
d=1
. For a one-dimensional distribution
Q\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
we will understand the median of
Q
in the broad sense as any
u\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\lim_{v\to u-}F(v)\leq 1/2\leq F(u)
, where
F
is the distribution function of
Q
; we write
u\in\mathrm{med}\left(Q\right)
. A one-dimensional depth
D_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)\to[0,1]
is expected to verify for each
Q\equiv Q_{Y}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
and
Y\sim Q

(D
1
)

Affine invariance:
For any
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
a\neq 0
and any
b\in\mathbb{R}
we have

D_{1}(u;Q_{Y})=D_{1}(a\,u+b;Q_{a\,Y+b})\quad\mbox{for all }u\in\mathbb{R},

where
Q_{a\,Y+b}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
stands for the distribution of
a\,Y+b
.

(D
2
)

Maximality at the median:
If for
m\in\mathbb{R}
we have

D_{1}(m;Q)=\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}}D_{1}(u;Q)>0,

(2)

then
m\in\mathrm{med}\left(Q\right)\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
m
is a median of
Q
.

(D
3
)

Monotonicity w.r.t. the median:
For any
m\in\mathbb{R}
that satisfies (
2
), the function
u\mapsto D_{1}(u;Q)
is non-decreasing on
(-\infty,m]
and non-increasing on
[m,\infty)
.

(D
4
)

Vanishing at infinity:

\lim_{u\to\infty}D_{1}(u;Q)=\lim_{u\to-\infty}D_{1}(u;Q)=0.

Classical examples of one-dimensional depths are the
halfspace
(or
Tukey
) depth in
\mathbb{R}

[
28
,
4
]
given by

D_{1,H}(u;Q)=\min\left\{Q((-\infty,u]),Q([u,\infty))\right\}

(3)

for
u\in\mathbb{R}
, or the one-dimensional

[
12
]
defined as

D_{1,S}(u;Q)=\mathsf{P}\left(u\in\left[\min\left\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\right\},\max\left\{Y_{1},Y_{2}\right\}\right]\right)

for
u\in\mathbb{R}
, where
Y_{1},Y_{2}\sim Q
are independent. The simplicial depth can be equivalently written in terms of the distribution function
F
of
Q
as

\displaystyle D_{1,S}(u;Q)

\displaystyle=1-Q((-\infty,u))^{2}-Q((u,\infty))^{2}

(4)

\displaystyle=2\,F(u)(1-F(u))+Q(\left\{u\right\})^{2}

for
u\in\mathbb{R}
. The halfspace depth (
3
) satisfies all conditions
(D1)
–
(D4)
for any
Q\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
. The simplicial depth (
4
) verifies
(D1)
and
(D4)
for any
Q\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
, but
(D2)
and
(D3)
only if
Q\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)
does not contain atoms; see the Supplementary Material.

2.3
Functional depths

A general
functional depth
is a mapping

D\colon\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)\to[0,1]

that satisfies (some of) the desirable properties listed in
[
10
]
. These properties directly translate
(D1)
–
(D4)
required from one-dimensional, or more generally,
d
-variate depth from
Zuo and Serfling [30]
. The following versions of these conditions will be important in the sequel. For every
P\equiv P_{X}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
and
X\sim P
we want
D
to satisfy:

(FD
1
)

Function-affine invariance:
For any
a,b\in\mathcal{F}
,
a(t)\neq 0
for all
t\in[0,1]
we have

D(x;P_{X})=D(a\,x+b;P_{a\,X+b})\quad\mbox{for all }x\in\mathcal{F},

where
P_{a\,X+b}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
stands for the distribution of
a\,X+b\colon t\mapsto a(t)\,X(t)+b(t)
.

(FD
2
)

Maximality at the coordinate-wise median:
If for some
m\in\mathcal{F}

D(m;P)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{F}}D(x;P)>0

(5)

then
m(t)\in\mathrm{med}\left(P_{t}\right)
for all
t\in[0,1]
, i.e.
m
must be a coordinate-wise median of
P
.

(FD
3
)

Band monotonicity w.r.t. the median:
For any
m\in\mathcal{F}
that satisfies (
5
), any
x\in\mathcal{F}
and any
y\in\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{B}\left(m,x\right)
we have
D(x;P)\leq D(y;P)
.

(FD
4
)

Vanishing at infinity:

\lim_{\left\|x\right\|\to\infty}D(x;P)=0
.

The
band depth

[
13
]
of a function
x\in\mathcal{F}
w.r.t.
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
is given by

BD(x;P)=\mathsf{P}\left(x\in\mathcal{B}\left(X_{1},X_{2}\right)\right),

(6)

where
X_{1},X_{2}\sim P
are independent random functions.
5
5

5

This definition corresponds to the common choice of
J=2
random functions

in
López-Pintado and
Romo [13]
. Analogous band depths for

can also be considered, with only minor changes in what follows.
The band depth presents a functional analog of the simplicial depth from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, see (
4
). A depth even more popular in the construction of functional boxplots is the
modified band depth

[
13
, MBD,]
, defined for
x\in\mathcal{F}
w.r.t.
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
as

\displaystyle MBD(x;P)=\mathsf{E}

\displaystyle\left[\lambda\left(\left\{t\in[0,1]\colon\min\{X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t)\}\right.\right.\right.

(7)

\displaystyle\left.\left.\left.\leq x(t)\leq\max\{X_{1}(t),X_{2}(t)\}\right\}\right)\right].

Again,
X_{1},X_{2}\sim P
are independent. The integrand in (

) is interpreted as the proportion of time points

such that
x
is contained inside the (random) band
\mathcal{B}\left(X_{1},X_{2}\right)
. MBD is a particular case of a wider collection of functional depths of

(or

)
[
7
,
1
,

]
. An integrated depth is always defined with a one-dimensional depth as its building block. First, a one-dimensional depth
D_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{P}\left({\mathbb{R}}\right)\to[0,1]
is given. Then a

based on
D_{1}
is defined for
x\in\mathcal{F}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
as

D(x;P)=\int_{0}^{1}D_{1}(x(t);P_{t})\,\mathrm{d}\,t.

(8)

It is simple to observe
[
18

that MBD from (
7
) can be written as the integrated depth (
8
) based on the one-dimensional simplicial depth (
4
).

Another important family of functional depths is that of
infimal depths
given by

D(x;P)=\inf_{t\in[0,1]}D_{1}(x(t);P_{t}).

(9)

Infimal depths were first considered in
Mosler [14]
and
Mosler and
Polyakova [15]
. Under different names, they have been used and amended in
Narisetty and Nair [19]
,
Myllymäki et al. [17]
and
Myllymäki and
Mrkvička [16]
.

A major advantage of integrated and infimal depths is their computational simplicity — as the common one-dimensional depths
D_{1}
are computable in linear time
O(n)
for

the sample size, both functional depths (
8
) and (
9
) are of complexity
O(n)
too. This should be compared with the band depth (
6
), whose naive computation requires
O(n^{2})
operations.

2.4
Band convexity of functional depths

Given a dataset
\mathcal{X}
of independent and identically distributed functions
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
sampled from
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
, a functional depth
D
is used to rank the observations in
\mathcal{X}
from the most central toward the peripheral ones.
\mathcal{X}
are used to construct the empirical distribution
P_{n}\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
that gives weight
1/n
to each
X_{i}
, and to assess the (functional) depth of each observation

D_{i}=D(X_{i};P_{n}),\quad\mbox{for }i=1,\dots,n.

The higher
D_{i}
is, the more centrally positioned is
X_{i}
inside the dataset. We obtain an ordering
X_{1:n},\dots,X_{n:n}
of
\mathcal{X}
according to the decreasing depth value. The function
X_{1:n}
is defined to be
X_{i}
such that
D_{i}
is maximal among
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
; this function is termed the
median function
of
\mathcal{X}
.
X_{2:n}
is the
X_{i}
whose depth
D_{i}
is the second largest, etc. Cases of ties are broken as in
\mathbb{R}
— if a common value
D_{i}
is shared by
m
functions from
\mathcal{X}
, then all these functions are assigned the average rank.

The key step in constructing the functional boxplot in
Sun and Genton [26]
is defining the central region. This is done using a functional depth. In general, the
\tau
-sample central region
of a dataset
\mathcal{X}
with
\tau\in(0,1)
is given as

\mathcal{B}\left(X_{1:n},\dots,X_{\lceil\tau\,n\rceil:n}\right),

(10)

that is the band of the
\lceil\tau\,n\rceil
deepest sample functions. Even though any functional depth can be used in a functional boxplot, the central region in (
10
) is always a band. For the specific construction of a functional boxplot, the
\tau
-central region is used with
\tau=1/2
. That is, one half of the deepest sample functions are taken in the construction of the central region.

When considering the
\tau

not smaller than

as any of the functions

. Not all functional depths, however, possess this property. Recall our motivating example from Section

MBD central region.

In analogy with the common convexity requirement for depths in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
24
]
, we require the

from functional depths:

(BC)

For any
P\in\mathcal{P}\left({\mathcal{F}}\right)
and any
\alpha\in[0,1]
, the central region

D_{\alpha}(P)=\mathcal{B}\left(D_{\alpha}(P)\right)
.

This condition is a formal translation of
(BC)
imposed in Section
1
.

Observe that the indexing in the sample case (
10
) and the population case (
11
) is different — the constant
\alpha
in (

(1-\tau)
-quantile of the depth distribution

is random. We denote the latter value by

;
\alpha\colon(0,1)\to[0,1]
is then a non-increasing function of

. The quantity

is designed so that approximately with probability

Our motivating example showed that integrated depths do not satisfy
(BC)
. An example showing that also the standard band depth (

is presented in the Supplementary Material.

2.5
The central region of an integrated depth

A fundamental problem with the functional boxplot based on an integrated depth concerns its population version. We have a dataset

2.6
A necessary condition for band convexity

Our main result states that the only functional depths that satisfy the crucial band convexity criterion
(BC)

(D4)

Formula (

\displaystyle D(x;

\displaystyle P_{X})=\inf_{t\in[0,1]}D_{1}(x(t);P_{t})

2.7
One-dimensional depth and its properties

In the common situation when all marginal depths

In the following result, we characterize all one-dimensional depth functions that verify all conditions
(D1)
–
(D4)
. For that reason, it will be instrumental to partition the space of all probability distributions

into what we call
affine classes
. A collection

is called an

if for each

there exist

,

, and

such that

has the same distribution as the random variable

with

. We write

Theorem 4
.

–
(D4)
if and only if for each affine class

and

as above there exists a function

•

of

for each

, and

•

for each

4
Conclusions and discussion

We have shown that the functional boxplots founded on local principles must be based on depths that follow the same paradigm. Since the functional boxplot decides about the outlyingness of functions locally (by evaluating whether a function falls outside the whiskers band at some argument
t\in[0,1]
), the central region that defines this threshold must also be based on local principles. We demonstrated that the only functional depths satisfying the ensuing desirable band convexity property are depths of infimal type.

From a practical perspective, we observed that depths of infimal type tend to produce ties in rankings. We recommend refining the depth using a tie-breaking procedure before constructing functional boxplots. Both refinements investigated in this paper,
erl
and
area
, have demonstrated to be viable by giving near-desired coverage of the sample central region in our simulation study. Our simulation study also shows that their sample central region width is quite stable; therefore, such refinements can be used to approximate the population central region of the functional distribution. The situation is far less favorable for integrated depths, as demonstrated theoretically and in simulation studies.

Reaching beyond classical functional boxplots, it is natural to consider functional data with multi-dimensional domains and/or co-domains
[
9
,
29
,
20
]
. The situation with a compact domain
\mathcal{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}
-valued responses is simple and follows the theory derived in this paper without any alteration. The case of vector-valued functions
x\colon\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is less trivial. First, we can again assume that
\mathcal{T}=[0,1]
, without loss of generality. There are two natural ways to extend the functional band to vector-valued functions. The first possibility is to require convexity of all the slices of bands in
\mathcal{F}
. Let
B\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a subset of the space of functions
\left\{x\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\right\}
. The
band
of
B
is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathcal{F}\colon x(t)\in\right.

(16)

\displaystyle\left.\mathrm{conv}\left(\left\{y(t)\colon y\in B\right\}\right)\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,1]\right\},

where
\mathrm{conv}\left(A\right)
is the convex hull of
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Another option is to consider (finite-dimensional) bands themselves as a generalization of the intervals used in (
1
). This leads to the alternative notion of a band

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)

\displaystyle=\Big\{x\in\mathcal{F}\colon\inf_{y\in B}y_{j}(t)\leq x_{j}(t)\leq\sup_{y\in B}y_{j}(t)

(17)

\displaystyle\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,1]\mbox{ and }j=1,\dots,m\Big\}.

Here we use the notation
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})^{\mathsf{T}}
for any
y\in\mathcal{F}
, where
y_{j}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
are the component functions of
y
. The advantage of convex bands (
16
) is their geometric interpretation; they are also conveniently visualized if
m=2
. The appeal of rectangular bands (
17
) comes from their simplicity. Each slice of
\mathcal{B}\left(B\right)
from (
17
) at
t\in[0,1]
takes the form of an
m
-dimensional rectangle
[a_{1}(t),b_{1}(t)]\times\dots\times[a_{m}(t),b_{m}(t)]\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
a_{j}(t)\leq b_{j}(t)
for each
j=1,\dots,m
and
t\in[0,1]
. This makes it easy to visualize the band for
m>2
, as each its component is determined only by two functions
a_{j},b_{j}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{*}
. The coordinate-wise approach (
17
) necessarily loses information about the dependence structure between the component functions. Each of the two methods (
16
) and (
17
) has its particular pros and cons. While some of our findings can also be adapted to multivariate functional data (e.g., depths of infimal type are preferable to those of integrated type), the complexity of visualization of multivariate curves invariably calls for further study of boxplots for multivariate functional data.

Concluding from a wider perspective, this paper demonstrated that infimal depths are the best choice for constructing the central region and functional boxplot. This fact, however, certainly does not diminish the value of other functional depths, which can be useful for ranking the functional data, and nonparametric inference.

If our task is to detect functional outliers, the functional boxplot with the preferred infimal depth is useful for detecting locally outlying functions. To detect global outliers or functions outlying in shape, the infimal depth-based boxplot may be used in conjunction with appropriate functional transformations revealing the outliers
[
3
]
.
