Solutions of Poisson’s Equation for Stochastically Monotone Markov Chains
Peter W. Glynn, Alex Infanger

Abstract
Stochastically monotone Markov chains arise in many applied domains, especially in the setting of queues and storage systems. Poisson’s equation is a key tool for analyzing additive functionals of such models, such as cumulative sums of waiting times or sums of rewards. In this paper, we show that when the reward function for such a Markov chain is monotone, the solution of Poisson’s equation is monotone. This implies that the value function associated with infinite horizon average reward is monotone in the state when the reward is monotone.



1
Introduction

Many stochastic models arising in queueing and storage applications can be formulated as Markov chains that are stochastically monotone, in the sense that if the process is initialized with more “work” in the system, then the system remains more congested. To be more precise, suppose that
X=(X_{n}:n\geq 0)
takes values in a state space
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. For
x\in S
, let
P_{x}(\cdot|X_{0}=x)
be the probability on the path-space of
X
conditional on
X_{0}=x
and let
E_{x}(\cdot)=E(\cdot|X_{0}=x)
be its associated expectation. We say that
X
is
stochastically monotone
if for each
y\in S
,
P_{x}(X_{1}>y)
is non-decreasing as a function of
x\in S
. Many birth-death chains on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
are stochastically monotone, as is the waiting time sequence
W=(W_{n}:n\geq 0)
satisfying the recursion

\displaystyle W_{n+1}=[W_{n}+Z_{n+1}]^{+}

for
n\geq 0
, where
Z_{1},Z_{2},...,
are independent and identically distributed (iid) random variables (rv’s) and
W_{0}\in S=\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Stochastically monotone Markov chains are ubiquitous within single station queueing environments; see
Stoyan 1983
,
Lund and
Tweedie 1996
, and
Lund
et al. 1996
for additional discussion and examples.

Consider such a Markov chain
X
, having a stationary distribution
\pi=(\pi(dx):x\in S)
. Assume
r:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a “reward” function which is non-decreasing, so that
r(x)\leq r(y)
for
x,y\in S
with
x\leq y
. This covers the great majority of performance measures used within the queueing setting. Let
P=(P(x,dy):x,y\in S)
be the one-step transition kernel (or matrix, when
S
is discrete), and let

\displaystyle\pi r=\int_{S}r(y)\pi(dy)

be the steady-state expectation of
r(X_{n})
. A fundamental object of interest in the analysis of Markov chains is
Poisson’s equation
, in which we wish to find the function
g
for which

\displaystyle(P-I)g=-r_{c},

(1.1)

where
r_{c}
is the centered reward function given by
r_{c}(x)=r(x)-\pi r
.

The solution
g
to (
1.1
) plays a key role in the analysis of the cumulative reward functional
S_{n}(r)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r(X_{j})
. In particular, the sequence of rv’s defined by

\displaystyle g(X_{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r(X_{j})-n\cdot\pi r

(1.2)

is then a martingale (in the presence of appropriate integrability). The martingale structure then implies that

\displaystyle E_{x}S_{n}(r)=n\pi r+g(x)-E_{x}g(X_{n}).

When
X
is aperiodic and
g
is
\pi
-integrable, this leads to the approximation, valid for large time horizons
n
, given by

\displaystyle E_{x}S_{n}(r)\approx n\pi r+g(x)-\pi g.

The martingale representation (
1.2
) also allows one to apply martingale theory to establish central limit theorems (CLT’s) and laws of the iterated logarithm (LIL’s) for
S_{n}(r)
; see, for example,
Maigret 1978
and
Glynn and
Infanger 2022
.

We note also that the value function
v=(v(x):x\in S)
for a Markov decision process (MDP) associated with maximizing the long-run average reward
n^{-1}S_{n}(r)
satisfies (
1.1
), where
P
is the transition kernel (or matrix) associated with the dynamics of
X
under the optimal policy.

In view of the importance of Poisson’s equation, this paper establishes the following fundamental result. In particular, when
X
is stochastically monotone and
r
is non-decreasing, the solution
g
to Poisson’s equation must necessarily also be non-decreasing. This monotonicity property implies, for example, that when constructing approximating numerical schemes for computing MDP value functions or value functions for uncontrolled Markov chains, the approximation should ideally be monotone when
X
is suitably monotone. It turns out that the monotonicity of
g
also plays a key role in studying truncation schemes for numerically computing stationary distributions for stochastically monotone chains; see
Infanger
et al. 2022
.

Our proof uses coupling arguments that may be of separate interest. Section
2
contains statements of our main results, as well as our proofs.

2
The Monotonicity Results

We discuss here our monotonicity results, starting with a simple proof that covers most examples, and then developing theory that covers richer classes of models. Our theory hinges on the following coupling of the Markov chain starting from
x\in S
with the chain starting from
y\in S
. In particular, let

\displaystyle F(x,y)=P_{x}(X_{1}\leq y)

for
x,y\in S
, and set

\displaystyle F^{-1}(x,y)=\inf\{z:F(x,z)\geq y\}.

For
x\in S
, let
X_{0}(x)=x
and put

\displaystyle X_{n+1}(x)=F^{-1}(X_{n}(x),U_{n+1})

for
n\geq 0
, where
U_{1},U_{2},...
is an iid sequence of rv’s uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
under the probability
P
(having associated expectation
E
). The key observation is that for each
x\in S
,

\displaystyle P((X_{j}(x):j\geq 0)\in\cdot)=P_{x}((X_{j}:j\geq 0)\in\cdot),

so that
X(x)=(X_{j}(x):j\geq 0)
has the same distribution as
X
under
P_{x}
. This coupling goes back to
Kamae
et al. 1977
.

Because
X
is stochastically monotone,
F^{-1}(\cdot,y)
is non-decreasing for each
y\in S
. Consequently,
F^{-1}(\cdot,U_{n+1})
is non-decreasing, and it follows by induction that for each
n\geq 0
,
X_{n}(x)
is a non-decreasing function of
x\in S
. Hence, if
r
is a non-decreasing function
r_{c}(X_{n}(x))
is non-decreasing in
x\in S
. Thus, if
r_{c}(X_{n}(x))
is integrable, it is evident that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}E_{x}r_{c}(X_{j})

is non-decreasing in
x
. As a result, if

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|E_{x}r_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

(2.1)

for
x\in S
, then

\displaystyle g(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}E_{x}r_{c}(X_{j})

(2.2)

must be non-decreasing in
x\in S
. But if (
2.1
) holds, then it is easy to see that
g
as defined by (
2.2
) is a solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.1
). We summarize our discussion thus far with our first result.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
X
is stochastically monotone and that
r
is non-decreasing. If (
2.1
) holds for each
x\in S
, then
g
as defined by (
2.2
) is a non-decreasing solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.1
).

As noted in
Glynn and
Infanger 2022
, there exist Markov chains
X
for which (
1.1
) is solvable, and yet the representation (
2.2
) for the solution fails to be valid. Consequently, we wish to develop alternative proofs that are more general.
Glynn and
Infanger 2022
show that when
r
is
\pi
-integrable,
X
is irreducible, and
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, then

\displaystyle g_{z}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

(2.3)

always solves (
1.1
), where
\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}=z\}
is the first return time to any (fixed) state
z\in S
. Furthermore, it is shown there that (
1.2
) is
P_{x}
-integrable and that

\displaystyle M_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}g_{z}(X_{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

is a
P_{x}
-martingale adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{n}:n\geq 0)
, where
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{j}:j\leq n)
.

We now use our coupling to prove the following result.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
X
is an irreducible positive recurrent stochastically monotone Markov chain taking values in
S=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
having stationary distribution
\pi
. If
r:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\pi
-integrable non-decreasing function, then

\displaystyle g_{0}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

(
2.3
a)

(with
\tau=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}=0\}
) is a non-decreasing solution of Poisson’s equation (
1.1
) and
(M_{n}:n\geq 0)
is a
P_{x}
-martingale for each
x\in S
.

Proof.

Fix
z=0\leq x<y
. Expressed in terms of our coupling, the martingale structure of
(M_{n}:n\geq 0)
implies that

\displaystyle M_{n}(x)\overset{\Delta}{=}g_{0}(X_{n}(x))+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(x))

and

\displaystyle M_{n}(y)\overset{\Delta}{=}g_{0}(X_{n}(y))+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(y))

are both martingales adapted to
(\mathcal{G}_{n}:n\geq 0)
, where
\mathcal{G}_{n}=\sigma(X_{j}(x),X_{j}(y):0\leq j\leq n)
. For
w\in S
, let
\tau(w)=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}(w)=0\}
. We now wish to apply optional sampling at time
\tau(y)
to
(M_{n}(x):n\geq 0)
and
(M_{n}(y):n\geq 0)
. Assuming temporarily that optional sampling can be applied, we find that

\displaystyle g_{0}(x)=Eg_{0}(X_{\tau(y)}(x))+E\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(x))

(2.4)

and

\displaystyle g_{0}(y)=Eg_{0}(X_{\tau(y)}(y))+E\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(y)).

(2.5)

But under our coupling,
X_{\tau(y)}(x)\leq X_{\tau(y)}(y)=0
. Since
S=\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
X_{\tau(y)}(x)\geq 0
, so it follows that
X_{\tau(y)}(x)=0
. So,

\displaystyle g_{0}(x)-g_{0}(0)=E\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(x))

and

\displaystyle g_{0}(y)-g_{0}(0)=E\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(y)).

The monotonicity of
r_{c}
implies that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(x))\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(y)),

(2.6)

thereby implying that
g_{0}(x)\leq g_{0}(y)
.

We now need to establish the validity of optional sampling. Note that
\tau(y)\land n\overset{\Delta}{=}\min(n,\tau(y))
is a bounded stopping time, so it follows from the optional sampling theorem (see, for example, Theorem 6.2.2 in
Ross 1996
) that

\displaystyle EM_{\tau(y)\land n}(x)=EM_{0}(x)=g_{0}(x).

(2.7)

The validity of (
2.4
) follows from (
2.7
), once we prove that
(M_{\tau(y)\land n}:n\geq 0)
is a uniformly integrable sequence. Note that

\displaystyle|M_{\tau(y)\land n}(x)|\leq|g_{0}(X_{\tau(y)\land n}(x))|+\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(x))|.

(2.8)

Because
r_{c}
is non-decreasing,
r_{c}
can be expressed as
r_{c}(x)=\tilde{r}_{c}(x)+r_{c}(0)
, where
\tilde{r}_{c}
is non-decreasing and non-negative. Hence,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(x))|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\tilde{r}_{c}(X_{j}(x))+|r_{c}(0)|\tau(y)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}\tilde{r}_{c}(X_{j}(y))+|r_{c}(0)|\tau(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(y))|+2|r_{c}(0)|\tau(y).

Recall that

\displaystyle E\sum_{j=0}^{\tau(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(y))|+2|r_{c}(0)|\tau(y)

\displaystyle=E_{y}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|+2|r_{c}(0)|E_{y}\tau.

The latter expectations are finite because
r_{c}
is
\pi
-integrable and
X
is positive recurrent; see Section 2 of
Glynn and
Infanger 2022
for details. Hence, the latter term on the right-hand side of (
2.8
) is integrable (and therefore trivially uniformly integrable).

We now argue that the other term appearing on the right-hand side of (
2.8
), namely
(g_{0}(X_{\tau(y)\land n}):n\geq 0)
, is also uniformly integrable. Note that
|g_{0}(X_{\tau(y)\land n}(x))|\rightarrow|g_{0}(X_{\tau(y)}(x))|
a.s. as
n\rightarrow\infty
. As argued earlier,
X_{\tau(y)}(x)=0
, so uniform integrability follows (see Theorem 4.6.3 of
Durrett 2019
) if

\displaystyle E|g_{0}(X_{\tau(y)\land n}(x))|\rightarrow|g_{0}(0)|=0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which is a consequence of establishing that

\displaystyle E|g_{0}(X_{n}(x))|I(\tau(y)\geq n)\rightarrow 0

(2.9)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Let
\beta_{n}(x)=\inf\{j>n:X_{j}(x)=0\}
, and observe that

\displaystyle g_{0}(X_{n}(x))=E[\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(x)-1}r_{c}(X_{j}(x))|X_{n}(x)].

In view of the fact that
\beta_{n}(x)\leq\beta_{n}(y)
, the left-hand side of (
2.9
) can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle E\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(x)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(x))|I(\tau(y)>n)

\displaystyle\leq E\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(x)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(y))|I(\tau(y)>n)

\displaystyle\leq E\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(y))|I(\tau(y)>n)

\displaystyle=E\sum_{j=n}^{\tau(y)-1}|r_{c}(X_{j}(y))|I(\tau(y)>n)

\displaystyle=E_{y}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau>n)\rightarrow 0,

proving (
2.9
). A similar (but easier) argument proves (
2.5
), thereby proving the theorem.
∎

We finish this section by developing the corresponding theory when the state space
S
is a continuous state space. In particular, we assume that
S=\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Our first result concerns the case where
X
is a positive recurrent Harris chain. Specifically, we will invoke the following assumption

A1
.

There exist
b,\lambda>0
, a probability
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and non-negative functions
v_{i}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}

(i=1,2)
such that

a)

\sup\{E_{x}v_{i}(X_{i}):0\leq x\leq b\}<\infty,\ i=1,2
;

b)

\sup\{|r(x)|:0\leq x\leq b\}<\infty
;

c)

E_{x}v_{1}(X_{1})\leq v_{1}(x)-1,\ x>b
;

d)

E_{x}v_{2}(X_{1})\leq v_{2}(x)-|r(x)|,\ x>b
;

e)

P_{x}(X_{1}\in\cdot)\geq\lambda\phi(\cdot),\ x\in[0,b]
.

Athreya and
Ney 1978
and
Nummelin 1978
noted that condition e) allows one to “split” the transition kernel
P
over
[0,b]
, so that one can then write

\displaystyle P_{x}(X_{1}\in\cdot)=\lambda\phi(\cdot)+(1-\lambda)Q(x,\cdot)

(2.10)

for
x\in[0,b]
, where
Q(x,\cdot)
is a probability on
S
for each
x\in[0,b]
. The mixture (
2.10
) then allows one to interpret transitions from
x\in[0,b]
in terms of a “randomization”. In particular, if
X_{n}\in[0,b]
, then
X_{n+1}
is distributed according to
\phi
with probability
\lambda
and distributed according to
Q(X_{n},\cdot)
with probability
1-\lambda
. Every time that
X
distributes itself according to
\phi
, the Markov chain “regenerates.”

Let
\tau
be the first time that
X
distributes itself according to
\phi
. We are now ready to state a theorem that provides a continuous state space analog to the discrete state space representation (
2.3
) for the solution of Poisson’s equation.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X=(X_{n}:n\geq 0)
be a Markov chain satisfying
A1
. Then,
X
possesses a unique stationary distribution
\pi
and
r
is
\pi
-integrable. If
r_{c}(x)=r(x)-\pi r
for
x\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

for each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x)=E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

is a finite-valued solution of Poisson’s equation for which

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(X_{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

is a
P_{x}
-martingale for each
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Condition e) implies that
[0,b]
is a small set, in the terminology of
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
. In view of conditions a) and c), we may apply Theorem 11.3.4 of
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
, thereby ensuring that
X
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
. Furthermore, conditions a), b), and d), together with Theorem 14.3.7 of
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
imply that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\pi(dx)|r(x)|\leq\sup\{E_{x}v_{2}(X_{1})-v_{2}(x)+|r(x)|:0\leq x\leq b\}<\infty.

Since
\pi r
is finite-valued, we may put
r_{c}(x)=r(x)-\pi r
.

In view of conditions a)-d), we may apply the Comparison Theorem (p. 344,
Meyn and
Tweedie 2012
) to conclude that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|\leq v_{2}(x)+|\pi r|v_{1}(x)+\beta E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}I(X_{j}\leq b)

(2.11)

where

\displaystyle\beta=\sup\{|\pi r|v_{1}(x)+v_{2}(x):0\leq x\leq b\}.

With the aid of the splitting idea discussed earlier, we see that

\displaystyle P_{x}(\tau>T_{k+1}|X_{0},...,X_{T_{k}})=(1-\lambda),

where
T_{k}
is the time step at which
X
visits
[0,b]
for the
k
’th time, and hence

\displaystyle P(\tau>T_{k})=(1-\lambda)^{k}.

It follows that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}I(X_{j}\leq b)=E_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}I(\tau>T_{j})=\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Consequently, (
2.11
) implies that

\displaystyle E_{x}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

(2.12)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

With the knowledge that
\tilde{g}
is finite-valued, then by conditioning on
X_{1}
, we find that

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x)=r_{c}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\tilde{g}(y)P_{x}(X_{1}\in dy)

(2.13)

for
x>b
, whereas

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x)=r_{c}(x)+(1-\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\tilde{g}(y)Q(x,dy)

(2.14)

for
x\leq b
.

Since
X
regenerates at time
\tau
and has distribution
\phi
at that time, it is well known (see, for example,
Smith 1955
) that

\displaystyle 0=\pi r_{c}=\frac{E_{\phi}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}r_{c}(X_{j})}{E_{\phi}\tau}=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R_{+}}}\phi(dx)\tilde{g}(x)}{E_{\phi}\tau},

(2.15)

(where
E_{\phi}(\cdot)
is the expectation under which
X_{0}
has distribution
\phi
) so that (
2.14
) becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(x)

\displaystyle=r_{c}(x)+(1-\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\tilde{g}(y)Q(x,dy)+\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\tilde{g}(y)\phi(dy)

\displaystyle=r_{c}(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\tilde{g}(y)P_{x}(X_{1}\in dy),

proving that Poisson’s equation is solved by
\tilde{g}
.

To prove the martingale property, we note that

\displaystyle E_{x}|r_{c}(X_{n})|=E_{x}|r_{c}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{x}(\tau=j)E_{\phi}|r_{c}(X_{n-j})|

(2.16)

and

\displaystyle E_{\phi}|r_{c}(X_{n})|=E_{\phi}|r_{c}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{\phi}(\tau=j)E_{\phi}|r_{c}(X_{n-j})|

(2.17)

(where
P_{\phi}(\cdot)
is the probability under which
X_{0}
has distribution
\phi
). But the
\pi
-integrability of
r
ensures that

\displaystyle E_{\phi}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|<\infty

(2.18)

(using the same regenerative identity as in (
2.15
)), so that
E_{\phi}|r_{c}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)<\infty
for
n\geq 0
. Also, (
2.12
) guarantees that
E_{x}|r_{c}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)<\infty
for
n\geq 0
. It follows from an induction based on the recursions (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) that
r_{c}(X_{n})
is
P_{x}
-integrable for
n\geq 0
.

We can similarly analyze the
P_{x}
-integrability of
\tilde{g}(X_{n})
. The same inductive argument, based on equations analogous to (
2.16
) and (
2.17
), establishes the integrability provided that we show

\displaystyle E_{x}|\tilde{g}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)<\infty

and

\displaystyle E_{\phi}|\tilde{g}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)<\infty

for
n\geq 0
. Set
\beta_{n}=\inf\{j>n:X\text{ regenerates at time }j\}
. Then,

\displaystyle E_{x}|\tilde{g}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)

\displaystyle=E_{x}|\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}-1}r_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau>n)

\displaystyle\leq E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\beta_{n}-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau>n)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau>n)<\infty,

due to (
2.12
). Similarly,

\displaystyle E_{\phi}|\tilde{g}(X_{n})|I(\tau>n)\leq E_{\phi}\sum_{j=n}^{\tau-1}|r_{c}(X_{j})|I(\tau>n)<\infty,

due to (
2.18
). Hence,

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(X_{n})+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}r_{c}(X_{j})

is
P_{x}
-integrable for
n\geq 0
. The martingale property then follows easily from the fact that
\tilde{g}
solves Poisson’s equation.
∎

We now proceed to prove that
\tilde{g}
is monotone when
X
and
r
are suitably monotone.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose that
X
is a stochastically monotone Markov chain satisfying
A1
. If the function
r
appearing in
A1
is non-decreasing, then
\tilde{g}
is non-decreasing.

Proof.

To prove this result, we modify the coupling discussed earlier. In particular, for
0\leq x\leq y
, we modify the dynamics of
((X_{n}(x),X_{n}(y)):n\geq 0)
when
X_{n}(y)\leq b
.

For
w\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v\in[0,b]
, put

\displaystyle G(v,w)=\frac{F(v,w)-\lambda\phi([0,w])}{1-\lambda},

and note that
1-G(\cdot,w)
is non-decreasing for each
w\geq 0
(since this is also true of
1-F(\cdot,w)
).

When
X_{n}(x)\leq X_{n}(y)\leq b
, we distribute
X_{n+1}(y)
according to
\phi
with probability
\lambda
and put
X_{n+1}(x)=X_{n+1}(y)
. Otherwise, with probability
1-\lambda
, put
X_{n+1}(x)=G^{-1}(X_{n}(x),U_{n+1})
and
