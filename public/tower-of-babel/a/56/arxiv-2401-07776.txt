Computing the clique number of tournaments
Guillaume Aubian

Abstract
The clique number of a tournament is the maximum clique number of a graph formed by keeping backwards arcs in an ordering of its vertices. We study the time complexity of computing the clique number of a tournament and prove that, for any integer k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , deciding whether a tournament has clique number at most k k is NP-complete. This answers an interrogation of Nguyen, Scott and Seymour. To do so, we make use of a construction which we then modify to provide a counterexample to a conjecture of Aboulker, Aubian, Charbit and Lopes.

Introduction

Given an undirected graph
G
, its clique number, denoted
\omega(G)
, is the maximum number of pairwise adjacent vertices of
G
. Its chromatic number, denoted
\chi(G)
, is the minimum size of a partition of
V(G)
in stable sets. A tournament is an orientation of a complete graph.

The clique number is a central notion in graph theory, finding applications in domains as diverse as chemistry
[
12
]
, automatic test pattern generation
[
7
]
, bioinformatics
[
4
]
etc. Computing the clique number of a graph has been proven to be NP-complete
[
8
]
. However, for a fixed integer
k
, whether a graph on
n
vertices has clique number at most
k
can be decided in time complexity
O(n^{k})
, by checking all subsets of at most
k
vertices.

Given a tournament
T
and an ordering
\prec
of
V(T)
, we define the backedge graph of
T
relative to
\prec
as
T^{\prec}=(V(T),\{uv\in A(T)\mid v\prec u\})
.
In
[
1
]
, Aboulker, Aubian, Charbit and Lopes formally study the clique number of tournaments defining it as the minimum over all orderings
\prec
of
V(T)
of the clique number of
T^{\prec}
, and denoting it as
\diomega(T)
. Note that while they formally introduced it, the same notion was already studied in
[
11
]
by Nguyen, Scott and Seymour. In the beginning of Section 9, they wonder what is the time complexity of computing the clique number of tournaments.

In this paper, we prove that, akin to the undirected case, this problem is NP-complete:

1.1
.

Computing the clique number of tournaments is NP-complete.

We prove a stronger theorem. Let
k\geq 1
, the problem
k
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is defined as follows:

k
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING

Input:
A tournament
T

Output:
Does
\diomega(T)\leq k
?

Unlike the undirected case, we prove the following:

1.2
.

Let
k\geq 3
.
k
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is NP-complete.

Note that, since
1
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is equivalent to testing whether a given tournament is acyclic, and thus is polynomial, only the case

remains open:

1.3
.

2
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is NP-complete.

Every graph
G
satisfies
\chi(G)\geq\omega(G)
. However, there exist graphs with
\chi(G)>\omega(G)
– for example a cycle on
5
vertices. Another question is whether there exists a function
f
such that for any graph
G
,
\chi\leq f(\omega(G))
. This question was answered in the negative by Zykov in
[
14
]
. However, various graph classes
\mathcal{C}
actually satisfy that for any graph
G\in\mathcal{C}
,
\chi(G)\leq f(\omega(G))
for some function
f
. Such graph classes are said to be
\chi
-bounded.

\chi
-bounded classes of graphs have been widely studied, see for example
[
13
]
for a nice survey on
\chi
-boundedness. Since most graph classes
\mathcal{C}
that appear in the literature are preserved under taking induced subgraphs, and thus can be defined from a set
S
as the class of graphs not containing a graph in
S
as an induced subgraph, the following question arises naturally: at which condition on
S
is
\mathcal{C}

\chi
-bounded? In
[
6
]
, Erdős proved that, if
S
is finite and
\mathcal{C}
is
\chi
-bounded,
S
must contain at least one forest. Gyárfás and Sumner thus asked whether the converse holds:

1.4
Gyárfás, 1975 & Sumner, 1981.

For any forest
F
, the class of graphs not containing
F
as an induced subgraph is
\chi
-bounded.

This conjecture remains largely open. See
[
13
]
.

In
1982
, Victor Neumann-Lara introduced in
[
10
]
a directed analogue of the chromatic number: the dichromatic number of a directed graph
D
, denoted
\dic(D)
, is the minimum size of a partition of its vertices into acyclic subsets
[
10
]
. This notion has been reintroduced by Mohar in
[
9
]
, and since then this notion has known a growing interest, mainly in trying to translate undirected results to a directed setting.

Similarly to the undirected setting, Aboulker, Aubian, Charbit and Lopes have then defined in
[
1
]
a notion of
\chi
-boundedness for tournaments: a tournament class
\mathcal{T}
is
\dic
-bounded if there exists a function
f
such that for any tournament
T\in\mathcal{T}
,
\dic(T)\leq f(\diomega(T))
. A question that arises naturally is the following: which tournaments
T
are such that the class of tournaments not containing
T
are
\dic
-bounded? In a similar flavour to the directed case, they proved that such a
T
must contain a backedge graph that is a forest. This directly implies an analogue of Gyárfás-Sumner conjecture for tournaments:

1.5
.

Let
T
be a tournament with a backedge graph that is a forest. The class of tournaments not containing
T
as a subgraph is
\dic
-bounded.

We answer this question in the negative.

3
Computing the clique number of tournaments is NP-complete

In this section, we will be interested in the algorithmic aspects of computing the clique number of tournaments. Our main result is the following:

3.1
.

Let
k\geq 3
.
k
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is NP-complete.

which directly implies:

3.2
.

Computing the clique number of tournaments is NP-complete.

Note that while Theorem
3.2
is expected, as an analogue of the undirected case, Theorem
3.1
is surprising, in that it contrasts with the undirected case where the corresponding decision problem can be answered in polynomial time. To prove this, we will need intermediate results: this is the purpose of Subsection
3.1
.

3.1
Useful tools to increase the clique number

Note that a variation of the construction in the following proof will be used in Definition

.

3.3
.

Let

\diomega
-ordering of

to

T^{\prime}

by reversing arc

–

i<j
,

–

\psi_{i}(u)=\psi_{j}(w)
,

.

Let us first prove that
\diomega(T^{\prime})=\diomega(T)
. Let

. Let

inducing a clique in

.

Suppose that for every

.

|K\cap\bigcup_{j\in B_{i}}V(T_{j})|\geq 2

. Let

. Let

. Hence

\psi_{j}(u)\neq\psi_{j}(w)

5
Conclusion and future works

The main problem that remains open is the following conjecture:

5.1
.

2
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
is NP-complete.

A way of attacking this problem could be to see it as a problem on strings. For example, let us consider the following problem:

PERMUTATION-AVOIDING-SMALL-SUBWORDS

Input:
A finite set
\Sigma
, and
S\subseteq\Sigma^{\leq 3}
.

Output:
Does there exist a permutation
P
of
\Sigma
such that no
w\in S
is a subsequence of
P
?

Given a tournament
T
, one can consider the instance with
\Sigma=V(T)
and
S=\{wvu\mid uv,vw,uw\in A(T)\}
. Then, a permutations
P
of
\Sigma
such that no
w\in S
is a subsequence of
P
are exactly orderings
\prec
of
V(T)
with
\omega(T^{\prec})=2
. Thus if
PERMUTATION-AVOIDING-SMALL-SUBWORDS
was solvable in polynomial time, so would be
2
-DIOMEGA-ORDERING
.

Unfortunately, one can prove that
PERMUTATION-AVOIDING-SMALL-SUBWORDS
. However, one could study slight variations of this problem: for example, we can assume that if
abc,dbe\in S
then
abe,dbc\in S
.

Another problem that is mentioned by Nguyen, Scott and Seymour in
[
11
]
is the question of whether
k

While Conjecture
