Experimental Masking of Real Quantum States
Rui-Qi Zhang, Zhibo Hou, Zihao Li, Huangjun Zhu, Guo-Yong Xiang, Chuan-Feng Li, Guang-Can Guo

Abstract
Masking of quantum information is a way of hiding information in correlations such that no information is accessible to any local observer. Although the set of all quantum states as a whole cannot be masked into bipartite correlations according to the no-masking theorem, the set of real states is maskable and is a maximal maskable set. In this work, we experimentally realize a masking protocol of the real ququart by virtue of a photonic quantum walk. Our experiment clearly demonstrates that quantum information of the real ququart can be completely hidden in bipartite correlations of two-qubit hybrid entangled states, which are encoded in two different degrees of freedom of a single photon. The hidden information is not accessible from each qubit alone, but can be faithfully retrieved with a fidelity of about 99 % 99\% from correlation measurements. By contrast, any superset of the set of real density matrices cannot be masked.



Introduction.
—Hiding information in correlations is a useful idea that
plays crucial roles in a wide spectrum of subjects. Although classical
information can be completely hidden in quantum correlations of a bipartite
system, quantum information of an arbitrary state cannot be
completely hidden in a similar way according to the no-hiding theorem

Braunstein and Pati 2007
and no-masking theorem
Modi et al. 2018
. These no-go theorems
offer valuable insights on the power and limitation of quantum information
processing, which are complementary to the famous no-cloning theorem

Wootters and Zurek 1982
;
Dieks 1982
;
Lamas-Linares et al. 2002
and no-broadcasting
theorem
Barnum et al. 1996
. Meanwhile, they are of intrinsic interest to many
active research areas, including quantum communication, quantum secret
sharing
Hillery et al. 1999
;
Cleve et al. 1999
;
DiVincenzo et al. 2002
,
information scrambling, and the black-hole information paradox

Page 1993
;
Hayden and Preskill 2007
;
Sekino and Susskind 2008
;
Liu et al. 2018
.

Notwithstanding the limitations set by the no-hiding and no-masking theorems

Modi et al. 2018
, it is possible to hide and mask quantum information in certain
restricted sets of quantum states

Li et al. 2019a
;
Liang et al. 2019
;
Ding and Hu 2020
;
Liang et al. 2020
;
Du et al. 2020
;
Zhu 2020
(see also Ref.
Li and Wang 2018
for information masking in the multipartite scenario).
Notably, very recently Ref.
Zhu 2020
showed that
the no-hiding and no-masking theorems break down in real quantum mechanics,
in sharp contrast with complex quantum mechanics. Information
about real density matrices (with respect to the computational basis) can be
completely hidden in bipartite correlations. Moreover, the set of real states
is a maximal maskable set in the sense that it is not contained in any other
maskable set. These results give a twist to the problem of information masking, which is of intrinsic interest to the resource
theory of imaginarity
Hickey and Gour 2018
;
Wu et al. 2021
and foundational
studies on quantum mechanics
Wootters 1986
;
Chiribella et al. 2011
;
Hardy 2001
in addition to the research areas mentioned above.
Despite these theoretical progresses, experimental works on
quantum information masking are quite rare

Ghosh et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021

(see Refs.
Samal et al. 2011
;
Kalra et al. 2019
on the test of the no-hiding
theorem). Moreover, all masking protocols implemented so far are restricted
to qubit systems, which is a severe limitation.

In this work, we experimentally realize for the first time the masking protocol
of the real ququart proposed in Ref.
Zhu 2020
using a photonic system.
To achieve this goal, we devise a four-step photonic quantum walk to realize
the desired masking isometry, which turns any pure state of the real ququart
into a two-qubit hybrid entangled state.
Our experimental results clearly demonstrate
that quantum information of the real ququart can be completely hidden in
bipartite correlations, and no information can be retrieved from each subsystem
alone. In addition, the encoded quantum information can be faithfully retrieved
from correlation measurements. By contrast, the output state associated with any
input state that is not real cannot be maximally entangled, so partial
information has to leak to each subsystem. Moreover, the concurrence of the output state is determined by the robustness of imaginarity of
the input state, which is of key interest to the resource
theory of imaginarity
Hickey and Gour 2018
;
Wu et al. 2021
.
Here, we encode two-qubit hybrid entangled states in
two different degrees of freedom (DoFs) of a single photon

Englert et al. 2001
;
Fiorentino and Wong 2004
,
but it can be generalized to two-photon two-qubit states by simply
cascading the protocol of quantum state fission

Vitelli et al. 2013
after our photonic quantum walk.

Masking of quantum information.
—A set
\mathscr{S}
of quantum states
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

of dimension
d
is maskable if there exists an
isometry
M
from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, such
that both
\tau_{A}={\rm tr}_{B}(M\rho M^{\dagger})

and
\tau_{B}={\rm tr}_{A}(M\rho M^{\dagger})
are independent of

\rho
for all
\rho\in\mathscr{S}

Modi et al. 2018
. In this case, no information
about the original state
\rho
can be retrieved from subsystem
A
or
B
alone,
and all information spreads over
the bipartite correlations between
A
and
B
. Let
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})

and
\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{H})

be the sets of all density matrices and all pure states (rank-1 projectors)
on
\mathcal{H}
; let
\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
and

\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
be the sets of all real density matrices and
all real pure states with respect to the computational basis

\{|j\rangle\}_{j=0}^{d-1}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
The no-masking theorem states that
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})

and
\mathscr{P}(\mathcal{H})
are not maskable
Modi et al. 2018
. However,
this theorem does not apply to restricted sets of quantum states in general.
Notably, the set
\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
is maskable and is actually
a maximal maskable subset of
\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})

Zhu 2020
.
Here a maskable set is maximal if it is not a proper subset of any other
maskable set. Such maximal maskable sets are particularly appealing because
they reflect the potential and limitation of quantum information masking as well
as the distinction between quantum information processing and classical
information processing.

Since any mixed state is a convex mixture of pure states, masking of

\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
is equivalent to the masking of

\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
. According to Ref.
Zhu 2020
,
the set
\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
can be masked by virtue of a
set
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d-1}
of
d-1
unitary Hurwitz-Radon (HR) matrices
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{A}

Hurwitz 1922
;
HR 2006
, which is characterized
by the equation

U_{j}U_{k}+U_{k}U_{j}=-2\delta_{jk}\mathbbm{1}_{A}.

(1)

Let
|\Phi\rangle=\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}|rr\rangle
be the canonical maximally entangled
state in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, assuming that both
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
have
dimension
m
. Let
U_{0}:=\openone_{A}
and define

|\Phi_{j}\rangle:=(U_{j}\otimes\openone_{B})|\Phi\rangle,\quad j=0,1,\ldots,d-1.

(2)

Then the isometry
M
from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
defined
by the map
j\mapsto|\Phi_{j}\rangle
is a masker for

\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
and
\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
. Note that,
for any normalized vector
\bm{c}:=(c_{0},c_{1},...,c_{d-1})
, the ket

|\psi(\bm{c})\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}{c_{j}|j\rangle}
is mapped to

[U(\bm{c})\otimes\openone_{B}]|\Phi\rangle
under the masker
M
, where
U({\bm{c}}):=\sum_{j}{c_{j}U_{j}}
.
Moreover,

U(\bm{c})
is a unitary operator for any normalized real vector
\bm{c}

thanks to the characteristic of the HR matrices presented in Eq. (
1
).

Masking of the real ququart.
—In the case of
d=4
, to mask

\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
, we need a set of three HR matrices. A
simple example can be constructed from Pauli matrices:

\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}:=\{\mathrm{i}\sigma_{z},\mathrm{i}\sigma_{x},\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\}
, which can also be written as
\{\mathrm{i}Z,\mathrm{i}X,\mathrm{i}Y\}
.
With this
choice, we can define an isometry

M:\mathcal{H}\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
as follows:

M|j\rangle=-\mathrm{i}|\Phi_{j}\rangle:=-\mathrm{i}(U_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})|\Phi\rangle,\quad j=0,1,2,3,

(3)

where
|\Phi\rangle:=(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}

and
U_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
. Here the choice of the phase factor
-\mathrm{i}
is not essential but
is convenient for the following discussion.
Under this isometry, the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}
is mapped to the
two-qubit magic basis
Hill and Wootters 1997
; accordingly, all real pure states are
mapped to maximally entangled states. So the isometry
M
is a masker for

\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})

and
\mathscr{D}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})

Zhu 2020
. By contrast, the
image of any state in

\mathscr{D}(\mathcal{H})\setminus\mathscr{P}^{\mathrm{R}}(\mathcal{H})
is not
maximally entangled. Moreover, when
\rho
is pure, the concurrence of the output state

M\rho M^{\dagger}
is determined by the robustness of imaginarity of

\rho

Zhu 2020
,

C(M\rho M^{\dagger})=\sqrt{1-\mathscr{I}_{\mathrm{R}}^{2}(\rho)},

(4)

where
\mathscr{I}_{R}(\rho)=\|\rho-\rho^{\mathrm{T}}\|_{1}/2=\sqrt{1-\operatorname{tr}(\rho\rho^{\mathrm{T}})}

Wu et al. 2021
.

To realize the masker
M
of the real ququart, here we design a simple scheme
based on a quantum walk as illustrated in Fig.
1
, which
can be implemented in a photonic system.
In a quantum walk
Kurzyński and Wójcik 2013
;
Hou et al. 2018
;
Tang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2019b
,
the state
|x,c\rangle
of
the walker-coin joint system is characterized by two indices
x
and
c
, where

x=\dots,-1,0,1,\dots
denotes the position of the walker on a one-dimensional chain, and
c=0,1
labels the
state of the coin qubit, which
determines the moving direction of the walker in the next step.
Each step of the quantum walk can be described by a unitary operator of the
form
U(t)=\mathcal{T}C(t)
, where

C(t)=\sum_{x}|x\rangle\langle x|\otimes C(x,t)
, with
C(x,t)
being
position-dependent coin operators, and
\mathcal{T}
is the conditional translation operator,

\mathcal{T}=\sum_{x}{(|x-1\rangle\langle x|\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+|x+1\rangle\langle x|\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|)}.

(5)

The ququart can be encoded into the initial state (with
t
= 0) of the
walker-coin system using the path degree of freedom (DoF). To be concrete,
a general pure state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j=0}^{3}{a_{j}|j\rangle}
of the
ququart with
\sum_{j=0}^{3}{|a_{j}|^{2}}=1
is encoded as follows:

|\Psi\rangle=(a_{0}|-3\rangle+a_{1}|-1\rangle+a_{2}|1\rangle+a_{3}|3\rangle)\otimes|1\rangle.

(6)

After the first step of the quantum walk, the joint state evolves into

|\Psi_{1}\rangle=a_{0}|-2,1\rangle+a_{1}|-2,0\rangle+a_{2}|2,1\rangle+a_{3}|2,0\rangle.

(7)

After the second step, the joint state evolves into

\displaystyle|\Psi_{2}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}a_{0}+a_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}|-3,0\rangle+\frac{\mathrm{i}a_{0}-a_{1}}{\sqrt{2}}|-1,1\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{a_{2}+\mathrm{i}a_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}|1,0\rangle+\frac{a_{2}-\mathrm{i}a_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}|3,1\rangle.

(8)

Following a similar procedure, the final state after the quantum walk reads

\displaystyle|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{a_{1}-\mathrm{i}a_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}|1,0\rangle-\frac{a_{1}+\mathrm{i}a_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}|-1,1\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{a_{2}-\mathrm{i}a_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}|1,1\rangle+\frac{a_{2}+\mathrm{i}a_{3}}{\sqrt{2}}|-1,0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\bigl(a_{0}|\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\rangle+a_{1}|\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\rangle+a_{2}|\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\rangle+a_{3}|\Phi_{3}^{\prime}\rangle\bigr),

(9)

where

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|+1,0\rangle+|-1,1\rangle),

(10)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}(|+1,0\rangle-|-1,1\rangle),

\displaystyle|\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}(|+1,1\rangle+|-1,0\rangle),

\displaystyle|\Phi_{3}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|+1,1\rangle-|-1,0\rangle).

Now
|\Psi^{\prime}\rangle
can be regarded as a two-qubit state on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where
A
denotes the effective walker
qubit composed of positions
+1
and
-1
, and
B
denotes the coin qubit. Here

\{|\Phi_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\}_{j=0}^{3}
is exactly the two-qubit magic basis in

\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, so
the quantum walk illustrated in Fig.
1
indeed realizes
the masker
M
defined in Eq. (
3
).

Experimental setup.
—The setup for masking the real ququart is shown
in Fig.
2
. It contains
four modules: a heralded single-photon source, a state-preparation module, a
masking module, and a measurement
module. Here we use the path DoF to encode the real ququart
and the polarization DoF to encode the coin state employed in the quantum walk
within the masking module.

In the module of single-photon source, an ultraviolet laser with central
wavelength of 404nm is used to pump a type-I phase-matched
beta-barium borate (BBO) crystal to generate a photon pair in a product
(polarization) state via spontaneous parametric
down-conversion

herald the generation of its twin photon, which is then transmitted to the
state-preparation module.

In the state-preparation module, a polarization beam
splitter (PBS) first prepares the photon
in the horizontal-polarization state
|H\rangle
(in contrast with the
vertical-polarization state
|V\rangle
). Then two beam displacers (BDs)
and three half-wave plates (HWPs) transform the photon state into

(a_{0}|-3\rangle+a_{2}|1\rangle)\otimes|H\rangle+(a_{1}|-1\rangle+a_{3}|3\rangle)\otimes|V\rangle,

(11)

where the four real parameters
a_{i}
for
i=0,...,3
are controlled
by three HWPs (see Appendix A). Finally,
we prepare the ququart state

(a_{0}|-3\rangle+a_{1}|-1\rangle+a_{2}|1\rangle+a_{3}|3\rangle)\otimes|V\rangle

(12)

of the form in Eq. (
6
) by inserting a HWP at 45
∘

(which realizes the
X
or NOT gate) on paths
-3
and
1
to turn
|H\rangle

into
|V\rangle
. Note that the first two
X
gates on paths
-1
and
3
in the
masking module in Fig.
1
can also be implemented by a HWP
at 45
∘
; moreover, the four paths can share a same HWP
placed at the beginning of the masking module in Fig.
2
.
The removable quarter-wave plate (QWP) in the preparation module is used only to
prepare pure states with complex coefficients (see Appendix B).

After state preparation, the ququart is sent into the masking module, which
realizes the quantum walk illustrated in Fig.
1
. The two
QWP-HWP-QWP combinations realize the coin operators
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
respectively, while the

0^{\circ}
HWPs realize the
Z

hybrid entangled state between the path qubit and
the polarization qubit of the heralded photon.

The measurement module consists
of two QWP-HWP combinations designed to control the measurement settings employed for measuring the path qubit and polarization qubit (see Appendix C). In addition, a K9 plate is used to compensate for different path lengths among
the interference arms. Finally, the heralded photon is collected by four
single-photon counting modules (SPCMs).

Experimental results.
—To demonstrate the performance of the masking
protocol realized using the photonic quantum walk as described above, we select the
following four probe states:

\begin{gathered}|0\rangle,\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle),\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle),\quad\frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle+|3\rangle),\end{gathered}

(13)

Finally, we verify the relation between the concurrence of the output state
and the robustness of imaginarity of the input state as presented
in Eq. (
4

(14)

The robustness of imaginarity of the input state and the concurrence of the output state are respectively given by

\!\!\mathscr{I}_{R}(|\psi(\phi)\rangle)=\sin\phi,\quad C(M|\psi(\phi)\rangle)=\cos\phi.

(15)

where

is the reduced state of the path qubit of the output state as determined by

The experimental results shown in Fig.

agree very well with the theoretical prediction, which implies
that partial information of the
input state is accessible to each subsystem once the robustness of

Summary.
—We experimentally realized a masking protocol of the real
ququart using a photonic quantum walk. This is the first experiment on quantum
information masking beyond a qubit system. Our experiment clearly demonstrates
that quantum information of the real ququart can be completely hidden in
bipartite correlations of hybrid entangled states, which is not accessible
to each subsystem alone, but can be faithfully retrieved from correlation
measurements. By contrast, any superset of the set of real density matrices
cannot be masked. Furthermore, the entanglement of the output state is tied to
the robustness of imaginarity of the input state. These results manifest a
sharp distinction between real quantum mechanics and complex quantum mechanics
in hiding and masking quantum information. Moreover, they
offer valuable insights on the potential and limitation of quantum information
masking, which are of intrinsic interest to many active research areas.

It should be pointed out that
the two DoFs sharing
the masked information in our experiment reside in the same location (photon), and the meaning of
“masking”
is slightly different from the common literature. Here the information is masked into the correlations between two DoFs instead of two space-separated particles. Nevertheless, the underlying mathematical structures in the two scenarios are identical. So the main conclusions obtained in our proof-of-principle experiment should apply to both scenarios. Also, in principle our experiment can be generalized to realize quantum information masking in two photons by
cascading the protocol of quantum state fission

Vitelli et al. 2013
after the photonic quantum walk.

APPENDIX D: Fidelity estimation based on quantum state verification

To estimate the fidelity between a two-qubit state
\varrho
and the Bell state

|\Phi\rangle=(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
, we can apply the idea of quantum
state verification according to Ref.
Pallister et al. 2018
. To be specific, we can randomly perform
one of the three local projective tests associated with the three test projectors

P_{XX}^{+}
,
P_{YY}^{-}
,
P_{ZZ}^{+}
, respectively, where

\displaystyle P_{XX}^{+}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}+X\otimes X}{2},

(A17)

\displaystyle P_{YY}^{-}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}-Y\otimes Y}{2},

\displaystyle P_{ZZ}^{+}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}+Z\otimes Z}{2},

and
\mathbbm{1}
stands for the identity operator acting on the two-qubit system. To
optimize the performance, each test is performed with probability
1/3
. The resulting
verification operator reads

\Omega=\frac{1}{3}(P_{XX}^{+}+P_{YY}^{-}+P_{ZZ}^{+})=|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|+\frac{1}{3}(\mathbbm{1}-|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|).

(A18)

Now suppose that the fidelity between
\varrho
and
|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|

is
F(\varrho,|\Phi\rangle)=\langle\Phi|\varrho|\Phi\rangle=1-\epsilon
,
where
\epsilon
is the infidelity. Then the probability that

\varrho
can pass each test on average is given by

p_{\mathrm{succ}}={\rm tr}(\varrho\Omega)=1-\frac{2}{3}\epsilon,

(A19)

which implies that

\epsilon=\frac{3}{2}(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}}).

(A20)

If
\varrho
passes
S
tests after
N
tests in total in a given experiment, then

\hat{p}_{\mathrm{succ}}:=S/N
is an unbiased estimator for
p_{\mathrm{succ}}
,
from which we can construct an unbiased estimator for the infidelity,

\hat{\epsilon}=\frac{3}{2}(1-\hat{p}_{\mathrm{succ}}).

(A21)

To calculate the 95% confidence interval of
\hat{\epsilon}
, here we
assume that all the states
\varrho
in the
N
runs are independent and identically
distributed. A common choice for the 95% confidence interval of

\hat{p}_{\mathrm{succ}}
is
the Agresti-Coull interval
Brown et al. 2001
, which has the form

\tilde{p}-\kappa_{\alpha}(\tilde{p}\tilde{q})^{1/2}\tilde{N}^{-1/2}\leq p_{\mathrm{succ}}\leq\tilde{p}+\kappa_{\alpha}(\tilde{p}\tilde{q})^{1/2}\tilde{N}^{-1/2},

(A22)

where

\tilde{p}=\frac{\tilde{S}}{\tilde{N}},\quad\tilde{q}=1-\tilde{p},\quad\tilde{S}=S+\frac{\kappa_{\alpha}^{2}}{2},\quad\tilde{N}=N+\kappa_{\alpha}^{2},

(A23)

and
\kappa_{\alpha}:=z_{\alpha/2}
is the (
1-\alpha/2
)th quantile of the standard normal distribution. Accordingly, the 95% confidence interval of
\hat{\epsilon}
reads

\epsilon_{\rm low}\leq\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{\rm high},

(A24)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm low}=\frac{3}{2}\bigl[1-\tilde{p}-\kappa_{0.05}(\tilde{p}\tilde{q})^{1/2}\tilde{N}^{-1/2}\bigr],

(A25)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\rm high}=\frac{3}{2}\bigl[1-\tilde{p}+\kappa_{0.05}(\tilde{p}\tilde{q})^{1/2}\tilde{N}^{-1/2}\bigr].

When the target state changes from
|\Phi\rangle
to, say,

|\Phi^{\prime}\rangle=(U\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})|\Phi\rangle
, the above approach still
applies as long as the set of test projectors is replaced by

\{P_{X^{\prime}X}^{+},P_{-Y^{\prime}Y}^{+},P_{Z^{\prime}Z}^{+}\}
accordingly, where
X^{\prime}=UXU^{\dagger}
,
Y^{\prime}=UYU^{\dagger}
,

Z^{\prime}=UZU^{\dagger}
.

In our experiment, the total number of tests is chosen to be
N=5000
to reduce the
statistical fluctuation. The values of
S
associated with the four probe states
indicated in Fig. 3 in the main text are
4986
,
4969
,
4946
, and
4963
, respectively.
The estimation errors of the fidelities shown in Fig. 3 are determined
by virtue of these numbers and the formula

\displaystyle\max\{\hat{\epsilon}-\epsilon_{\rm low},\epsilon_{\rm high}-\hat{\epsilon}\}.

(A26)

Note that the estimation error of each fidelity is the same as the estimation error of each infidelity.

APPENDIX F: Decoding input ququart from correlation measurements

In the main text, we introduced a simple masker
M:\mathcal{H}\mapsto\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
for the real
ququart associated with the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, where both
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
are qubit Hilbert spaces. To be specific,

M
is the isometry defined by its action on the computational basis:

M|j\rangle=-\mathrm{i}(U_{j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B})|\Phi\rangle,\qquad j=0,1,2,3,

(A27)

where
U_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}_{A}
,
\{U_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}:=\{\mathrm{i}\sigma_{z},\mathrm{i}\sigma_{x},\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\}
forms a
set of Hurwitz-Radon matrices on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
, and
|\Phi\rangle=(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
is the canonical maximally entangled state in
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
Here we shall show that the information of the real input state
can be faithfully decoded from the bipartite correlations of the output state of the masker
M
.

Recall that any two-qubit state
\varrho
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
can be expressed as

\varrho=\frac{1}{4}\bigg(\mathbbm{1}+\bm{a}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B}+\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\bm{b}\cdot{\bm{\sigma}}+\sum_{j,k}{T_{jk}}\,\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k}\bigg),

(A28)

where
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
,
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
are the Bloch vectors of the two reduced states, respectively, and
T
is the correlation matrix.
The entries of the correlation matrix can be determined by suitable
Pauli measurements according to the following equation

T_{jk}=\langle\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k}\rangle={\rm tr}[\varrho(\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k})],\quad j,k\in\{x,y,z\}.

(A29)

To decode the input state, we first consider the case in which the input real state is pure.
A general real pure state in
\mathcal{H}
can be written as
|\psi(\bm{c})\rangle=\sum_{j}c_{j}|j\rangle
,
where
\bm{c}=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
is a normalized real vector.
The corresponding output state of the masker
M
has the form

|\Psi(\bm{c})\rangle:=M|\psi(\bm{c})\rangle=-\mathrm{i}[U(\bm{c})\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{B}]|\Phi\rangle,

(A30)

where
U(\bm{c}):=\sum_{j=0}^{3}{c_{j}U_{j}}
. Note that

|\Phi\rangle\langle\Phi|=\frac{1}{4}(\mathbbm{1}+\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}-\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}+\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{z}),

(A31)

and

\displaystyle U(\bm{c})\sigma_{x}U(\bm{c})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(c_{0}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}-c_{3}^{2})\sigma_{x}+2(c_{2}c_{3}-c_{0}c_{1})\sigma_{y}+2(c_{0}c_{3}+c_{1}c_{2})\sigma_{z},

(A32)

\displaystyle U(\bm{c})\sigma_{y}U(\bm{c})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2(c_{0}c_{1}+c_{2}c_{3})\sigma_{x}+(c_{0}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}+c_{3}^{2})\sigma_{y}+2(c_{1}c_{3}-c_{0}c_{2})\sigma_{z},

\displaystyle U(\bm{c})\sigma_{z}U(\bm{c})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2(c_{1}c_{2}-c_{0}c_{3})\sigma_{x}+2(c_{1}c_{3}+c_{0}c_{2})\sigma_{y}+(c_{0}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}-c_{3}^{2})\sigma_{z}.

So the density operator of
|\Psi(\bm{c})\rangle
reads

|\Psi(\bm{c})\rangle\langle\Psi(\bm{c})|=\frac{1}{4}\bigg[\mathbbm{1}+\sum_{j,k}{T(\bm{c})_{jk}}\,\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k}\bigg],

(A33)

where the correlation matrix
T(\bm{c})
has the form

T(\bm{c})=\left(\begin{matrix}c_{0}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}-c_{3}^{2}&-2(c_{0}c_{1}+c_{2}c_{3})&2(c_{1}c_{2}-c_{0}c_{3})\\
2(c_{2}c_{3}-c_{0}c_{1})&-(c_{0}^{2}-c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}+c_{3}^{2})&2(c_{1}c_{3}+c_{0}c_{2})\\
2(c_{0}c_{3}+c_{1}c_{2})&-2(c_{1}c_{3}-c_{0}c_{2})&c_{0}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}-c_{2}^{2}-c_{3}^{2}\end{matrix}\right).

(A34)

In conjunction with the normalization condition
c_{0}^{2}+c_{1}^{2}+c_{2}^{2}+c_{3}^{2}=1
,
we can deduce that

\displaystyle c_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(1+T_{11}-T_{22}+T_{33}),\qquad

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(1-T_{11}+T_{22}+T_{33}),

(A35)

\displaystyle c_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(1+T_{11}+T_{22}-T_{33}),\qquad

\displaystyle c_{3}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(1-T_{11}-T_{22}-T_{33}),

and

\displaystyle c_{2}c_{3}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{21}-T_{12}),\qquad

\displaystyle c_{1}c_{3}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{23}-T_{32}),

\displaystyle c_{1}c_{2}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{13}+T_{31}),

(A36)

\displaystyle c_{0}c_{1}=-\frac{1}{4}(T_{21}+T_{12}),\qquad

\displaystyle c_{0}c_{2}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{23}+T_{32}),

\displaystyle c_{0}c_{3}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{31}-T_{13}).

By linearity the above result can be generalized to any mixed
input state
\rho
that has a real density matrix.
In this case, in analogy to Eq. (
A34
), the correlation matrix of the output
state
M\rho M^{\dagger}
reads

T=\left(\begin{matrix}\rho_{00}-\rho_{11}+\rho_{22}-\rho_{33}&-2(\rho_{01}+\rho_{23})&2(\rho_{12}-\rho_{03})\\
2(\rho_{23}-\rho_{01})&-(\rho_{00}-\rho_{11}-\rho_{22}+\rho_{33})&2(\rho_{13}+\rho_{02})\\
2(\rho_{03}+\rho_{12})&-2(\rho_{13}-\rho_{02})&\rho_{00}+\rho_{11}-\rho_{22}-\rho_{33}\end{matrix}\right).

(A37)

Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{00}=\frac{1}{4}(1+T_{11}-T_{22}+T_{33}),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho_{11}=\frac{1}{4}(1-T_{11}+T_{22}+T_{33}),

(A38)

\displaystyle\rho_{22}=\frac{1}{4}(1+T_{11}+T_{22}-T_{33}),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho_{33}=\frac{1}{4}(1-T_{11}-T_{22}-T_{33}),

and

\displaystyle\rho_{23}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{21}-T_{12}),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho_{13}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{23}-T_{32}),

\displaystyle\rho_{12}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{13}+T_{31}),

(A39)

\displaystyle\rho_{01}=-\frac{1}{4}(T_{21}+T_{12}),\qquad

\displaystyle\rho_{02}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{23}+T_{32}),

\displaystyle\rho_{03}=\frac{1}{4}(T_{31}-T_{13}),

which generalize Eqs. (
A35
) and Eq. (
A36
).
Therefore, the density matrix of any real input state of the ququart can be faithfully
decoded from the correlation matrix of the output state,
which can easily be determined by virtue of product Pauli measurements
\sigma_{j}\otimes\sigma_{k}
for

j,k\in\{x,y,z\}
.
