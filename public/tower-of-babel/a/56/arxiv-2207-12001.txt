Graphene Dirac fermions in symmetric electric and magnetic fields: the case of an electric square well
İsmail Burak Ateş, Şengül Kuru, Javier Negro

Abstract
In this paper, a simple method is proposed to get analytical solutions (or with the help of a finite numerical calculations) of the Dirac-Weyl equation for low energy electrons in graphene in the presence of certain electric and magnetic fields. In order to decouple the Dirac-Weyl equation we have assumed a displacement symmetry of the system along a direction and some conditions on the magnetic and electric fields. The resulting equations have the natural form to apply the technique of supersymmetric quantum mechanics. The example of an electric well with square profile is worked out in detail to illustrate some of the most interesting features of this procedure.

2
Interactions with symmetry in the y-axis direction

We will restrict here to the stationary Dirac-Weyl equation in the plane for quasi-particles of zero mass and
1/2
-spin but with the charge of an electron. This is the continuous approximation to describe the electronic interaction in graphene for a range of energies near the Dirac points (hereafter we will restrict to one of them,
K
). The equation for the interaction in external static fields, applying the minimal coupling rule, is

v_{F}\big({\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot}({\bf p}+e{\bf A})\big)\Psi({\bf x})=\big(E-V({\bf x})\big)\Psi({\bf x})

(2.1)

where, as usual
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y})
are sigma Pauli matrices,

\bf p
the two-component momentum operator in the
xy
-plane,
v_{F}
the Fermi velocity of graphene,
e
the electric charge and
{\bf A}({\bf x})
,
V({\bf x})
magnetic and electric potentials defined on the plane; the wavefunction

\Psi({\bf x})=\Psi(x,y)
is a two-component pseudospinor
[
2
]
.

We will assume the symmetry of the fields along displacements in one direction (in the
y
-direction) and both fields to be perpendicular (the electric on the
xy
-plane, the magnetic in the
z
-direction perpendicular to the graphene plane). In fact, we will restrict to the potentials having the form

{\bf A}({\bf x})=(0,A_{y}(x)),\qquad V({\bf x})=V(x)

(2.2)

Therefore, the magnetic field may depend only on
x
in this case and it is given by
{\bf B}(x)=A^{\prime}_{y}(x)\hat{z}=\frac{dA_{y}(x)}{dx}\hat{z}
, where
\hat{z}
is the unit vector of
z
-axis;
while the electric field
{\bf E}(x)=-V^{\prime}(x)\hat{x}=-\frac{dV(x)}{dx}\hat{x}
will have the
x
-direction and it may depend also on
x
. Hereafter, the “prime”, will be used for derivatives with respect to the argument.
Then, we can choose the wavefunction of (
2.1
) to be an eigenfunction of the translation operator
p_{y}=-i\hbar\partial_{y}
, with eigenvalue
\hbar k
,

\Psi(x,y)=e^{ik\,y}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\
i\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right),\qquad p_{y}\Psi(x,y)=\hbar k\Psi(x,y)

(2.3)

where the imaginary unit
i
has been included in the second component by convenience.
Replacing these fields and operators together in the initial equation (
2.1
), we have a matrix differential eigenvalue problem

\hbar v_{F}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\partial_{x}+k+e/\hbar\,A_{y}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
-\partial_{x}+k+e/\hbar A_{y}(x)&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)=(E-V(x))\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)

(2.4)

Next, we will manipulate this equation to see if it can be diagonalized (or decoupled). Thus, we rewrite the equation in the following form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{x}&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&\partial_{x}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(k+\frac{e}{\hbar}\,A_{y}(x))&-(E-V(x))/\hbar v_{F}\\[4.30554pt]
(E-V(x))/\hbar v_{F}&-(k+\frac{e}{\hbar}\,A_{y}(x))\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)

(2.5)

We can simplify the notation as follows:

W(x):=k+e/\hbar\,A_{y}(x):=k+{\cal A}_{y}(x),\qquad\Delta(x):=\frac{E}{\hbar v_{F}}-\frac{V(x)}{\hbar v_{F}}:=\varepsilon-{\cal V}(x),

(2.6)

so that
W(x)
is related to the magnetic potential and
\Delta(x)
with the
electric one.
This is the Dirac-Weyl equation in the normal form of a system of two differential equations (in other words, with only the derivative of the components to the left):

\partial_{x}\Psi_{R}(x)=M\Psi_{R}(x)

(2.7)

where the
M=M(x)
matrix and the column vector
\Psi_{R}(x)
are

M=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}W(x)&-\Delta(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\Delta(x)&-W(x)\end{array}\right)\,,\qquad\Psi_{R}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\psi_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)

(2.8)

Notice that this system of two linear equations is real and we are looking for real square integrable solutions

\Psi_{R}(x)=\Psi_{R}(x)^{*}
,

\psi_{1}(x)=\psi_{1}(x)^{*},\qquad\psi_{2}(x)=\psi_{2}(x)^{*}

Differentiating equation (
2.7
) gives

\partial_{xx}\Psi_{R}(x)=(M^{\prime}+M^{2})\Psi_{R}(x)

(2.9)

where
M^{2}
is diagonal, while
M^{\prime}=\frac{dM(x)}{dx}
carries the nondiagonal part:

M^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}W^{\prime}(x)&-\Delta^{\prime}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\Delta^{\prime}(x)&-W^{\prime}(x)\end{array}\right),\qquad M^{2}=\Big(W(x)^{2}-\Delta(x)^{2}\Big)\,I

(2.10)

In the following we will discuss the simplest options to diagonalize
M^{\prime}
which will allow us to decouple eq. (
2.9
).

3
Solvable cases

The formulation of the problem is as follows. We must diagonalize
M^{\prime}\to T^{-1}M^{\prime}T
by means
of a constant matrix,
T
, in order that it should commute with the differential operator
\partial_{xx}
in
equation (
2.9
). In this way we will arrive to two differential equations, one for each component.
There are three situations where this is possible, as it will be seen below.

3.1
Pure magnetic field
{\cal V}=0

In this first case,
\Delta^{\prime}(x)={\cal V^{\prime}}(x)=0
, that is, we have a pure magnetic field and
M^{\prime}
,
M^{2}
take the form

M^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}W^{\prime}(x)&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&-W^{\prime}(x)\end{array}\right),\qquad M^{2}=\Big(W(x)^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\Big)\,I

This is the simplest case, where we have just a magnetic field perpendicular to the plane
XY
, determined by the component
{\cal A}_{y}
. Besides, the matrix
M^{\prime}(x)
is already diagonal, and
replacing this in eq. (
2.9
) we get the explicit form of the SUSY-QM formal identification of these two equations:

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\partial_{xx}+W^{\prime}(x)+W(x)^{2}&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&-\partial_{xx}-W^{\prime}(x)+W(x)^{2}\end{array}\right)\Psi_{R}(x)=\varepsilon^{2}\Psi_{R}(x)

(3.11)

where
W(x)
plays the role of superpotential.
This case was exhaustively studied in previous references,
including also a generalization to surfaces with curvature

[
3
,
11
,
12
,
28
,
30
]
.

3.2
Pure electric field
{\cal A}(x)=0

In this case equation (
2.9
) takes the form

\partial_{xx}\Psi_{R}(x)=\Delta^{\prime}(x)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\[4.30554pt]
1&0\end{array}\right)\Psi_{R}(x)+(k^{2}-\Delta(x)^{2})\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&1\end{array}\right)\Psi_{R}(x)

(3.12)

where the non-diagonal matrix is

M^{\prime}=\Delta^{\prime}(x)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-1\\[4.30554pt]
1&0\end{array}\right)={\cal V}^{\prime}(x)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\[4.30554pt]
-1&0\end{array}\right)

The eigenvalues of the matrix
M^{\prime}
are complex so that it can be transformed in a diagonal form
\tilde{M}^{\prime}
by means of a complex matrix
T
:

\tilde{M}^{\prime}=T^{-1}M^{\prime}T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-i\,{\cal V}^{\prime}(x)&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&i\,{\cal V}^{\prime}(x)\end{array}\right),\qquad T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}1&1\\[4.30554pt]
i&-i\end{array}\right),\quad T^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}1&-i\\[4.30554pt]
1&i\end{array}\right)

Then, defining

\tilde{\Psi}=T^{-1}\Psi_{R}\ \implies\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{\psi}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{1}-i\psi_{2})\\[4.30554pt]
\tilde{\psi}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\psi_{1}+i\psi_{2})\end{array}\right.

(3.13)

the system of equations (
3.12
) becomes

\partial_{xx}\tilde{\Psi}(x)=(\tilde{M}^{\prime}+\tilde{M}^{2})\tilde{\Psi}(x)

(3.14)

which consists in two complex conjugate equations, one for each component:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Big(-\partial_{xx}+V_{1}(x)\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{1}:=\Big(-\partial_{xx}-\big(i\,{\Delta}\big)^{\prime}+\big(i\,{\Delta}\big)^{2}\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{1}=-k^{2}\,\tilde{\psi}_{1}\\[8.61108pt]
\Big(-\partial_{xx}+V_{2}(x)\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{2}:=\Big(-\partial_{xx}+\big(i\,{\Delta}\big)^{\prime}+\big(i\,{\Delta}\big)^{2}\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{2}=-k^{2}\,\tilde{\psi}_{2}\end{array}\right.

(3.15)

Let us mention some aspects of the above equations.

1.

If
\tilde{\psi}_{1}
is a solution of the first equation, then its complex
conjugate
\tilde{\psi}_{1}^{*}:=\tilde{\psi}_{2}
will be a solution of the second.

2.

Each of the two equations may constitute a PT symmetric Hamiltonian

[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
. For example, if the potential
\cal V
is real and even, so that
V_{i}
,
i=1,2
, will also be even. The point is whether in that case, the solutions are also PT symmetric. We can check that this is true for the example of Section 4.

3.

The equations have potentials depending on the energy of the initial Dirac-Weyl problem.
In this situation, the role of
k
and
\varepsilon
has been
interchanged with respect to the pure magnetic case in (
3.11
).
However, we assume that
k
is a continuous parameter and for each value
of
k
we should have a discrete number of energies
\varepsilon_{n}(k)
.

4.

In general, we will
have complex solutions for each equation (
3.15
), but the spectrum given by the eigenvalues
\varepsilon
must be real.
The Dirac-Weyl equation (
2.7
) in the new components is obtained by applying
T^{-1}
to (
3.13
);
the resulting pair of equations take the form

(\partial_{x}\sigma_{1}+\Delta(x)\sigma_{2})\tilde{\Psi}(x)=k\tilde{\Psi}(x),\quad\quad\Delta(x)=\varepsilon-{\cal V}(x)\,,\quad\tilde{\Psi}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\psi}_{1}(x)\\[4.30554pt]
\tilde{\psi}_{2}(x)\end{array}\right)

(3.16)

Therefore, the solutions must satisfy the separated equations (
3.15
) and also the version (
3.16
) of the Dirac-Weyl equation. As we will see later, in an example, the solutions of (
3.15
) can be chosen conjugate, but one must find the right complex phase coefficient so that the coupled equations (
3.16
) be also satisfied.
Assume, for instance, that eq. (
3.15
) is PT symmetric, according to point 2. Then,
eq. (
3.16
) is invariant under the operator
PT\sigma_{3}
. If both symmetries are not broken, each solution
\tilde{\Psi}(x)
of both equations will also be eigenfunction of both symmetries:

PT\sigma_{3}\tilde{\Psi}(x)=\lambda\tilde{\Psi}(x),\quad PT\tilde{\psi}_{1}(x)=\mu\tilde{\psi}_{1}(x),\quad\tilde{\psi}_{2}(x)=\tilde{\psi}_{1}(x)^{*}

(3.17)

where
\lambda
,
\mu
are the eigenvalues.
The compatibility of these conditions leads to
\mu=\pm i
.
We will check this result later on in the example of Section 4.

5.

Remark that eq. (
3.16
) together with (
3.15
) have the characteristic form of SUSY-QM
[
28
]
, where the intertwining operators
A^{\pm}
have complex superpotentials,

A^{\pm}=\partial_{x}\pm i\Delta(x)
.

If we compare the Schrödinger equations for pure electric fiedls (
3.15
) and the corresponding for pure magnetic field (
3.11
) we see that, by means of our diagonalization, we have transformed an electric problem into the form of a magnetic one including some imaginary terms. This process is the same as the “complex Lorentz boost” of
[
39
]
. The interpretation
of this kind of manipulation in terms of Lorentz transformations goes back
to
[
26
]
. A general version of this calculation for position dependent Fermi velocity, paying attention to SUSY-QM, was also considered in
[
40
]
.

We can apply this formalism to different electric potentials. We have to take into account the asymptotic conditions of the potential
{\cal V}(x)
in order the Schrödinger like equations
(
3.15
)
have square-integrable eigenfunctions corresponding to a discrete spectrum. Take, for instance the first equation of (
3.15
) and rewrite it as follows:

\Big(-\partial_{xx}+(i\,{\cal V}^{\prime}+2\varepsilon{\cal V}-{\cal V}^{2})\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{1}=-(k^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})\,\tilde{\psi}_{1}

(3.18)

We consider an effective complex potential (depending on
\varepsilon
)

V_{\rm eff}(x)=i\,{\cal V}^{\prime}+2\varepsilon{\cal V}-{\cal V}^{2}

(3.19)

and an effective energy

E_{\rm eff}=-(k^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})

(3.20)

Let us assume, for instance, that
V_{\rm eff}(x)^{\pm}\ \to\ 0
when
x\ \to\ \pm\infty
. Then, a necessary condition to have an square integrable eigenfuntion is,
according to (
3.20
), that
|\varepsilon|<|k|
; in other words,
in the context of null mass graphene quasiparticle, the parallel momentum
k
plays a similar role of the mass in a Dirac particle. We will see in the following example a simple case to confirm this behaviour.

In general, the conditions to have an effective well potential are not easy to satisfy if we are interested in potentials allowing for analytic solutions. Some examples
considered in previous references are the following.
a)
{\cal V}(x)=\frac{e}{|x|}
, similar to the Coulomb potential but in one dimension
[
24
]
;
b)
{\cal V}(x)=\frac{e}{1+x^{2}}
, a Lorentzian type potential
[
24
]
;
c)
{\cal V}(x)=\tanh x
, a Pöschl-Teller like potential
[
25
]
.

A way to obtain partly analytic formulas of eigenfunctions is to make use of piecewise regular functions for the potentials and then apply matching conditions. This is the case of the following example that we will work out
in detail.
It will give us the main features of the discrete spectrum and a number of properties of the electric interaction: Boundary lines of the spectrum; atomic collapses produced by deepening the potential; the role of the parallel momentum
k
, or the shape and orthogonality of the complex eigenfunctions.

3.3
Proportional electromagnetic fields:
{\cal V}\propto{\cal A}

In this case we will assume that both potential intensities are proportional,

{\cal V}(x)=\alpha{\cal A}(x)\ \implies\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W(x)=k+{\cal A}(x)\\[6.45831pt]
\Delta(x)=\varepsilon-\alpha{\cal A}(x)\end{array}\right.

Then, we can write

\Delta^{\prime}(x)=-\alpha{\cal A^{\prime}}(x),\qquad W^{\prime}(x)={\cal A^{\prime}}(x)

Thus, the matrices
M^{\prime}
and
M^{2}
will take the form

M^{\prime}={\cal A^{\prime}}(x)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha\\[4.30554pt]
-\alpha&-1\end{array}\right):={\cal A^{\prime}}(x)M^{\prime}_{\alpha},\qquad M^{2}=\Big((k+{\cal A}(x))^{2}-(\varepsilon-\alpha{\cal A}(x))^{2}\Big)\,I

The characteristic polynomial,
\det(M_{\alpha}^{\prime}-\lambda I)=0
, for the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
M_{\alpha}^{\prime}
gives

\lambda^{2}=1-\alpha^{2}
.
Then, we have the following cases depending on
\alpha
:

a)

|\alpha|<1
both eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
are real (trigonometric or
{\cal A}
-dominant)

b)

|\alpha|>1
both eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
are pure imaginary (hyperbolic or
{\cal V}
-dominant)

c)

\alpha=\pm 1
, both eigenvalues are zero
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
(parabolic or equal
{\cal A}
-
{\cal V}
)

Henceforth we will assume
0<|\alpha|<1
. The second order equation (
2.9
) can be written in the form

-\Psi_{R}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left(M^{\prime}+\Big((k+{\cal A}(x))^{2}-(\varepsilon-\alpha{\cal A}(x))^{2}\Big)\,I\right)\Psi_{R}(x)=0

(3.21)

Next, we diagonalize the matrix
M^{\prime}(x)
by means of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the constant matrix
M^{\prime}_{\alpha}
:

\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}},\qquad{\bf v}_{\pm}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\\[6.45831pt]
-1\pm\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\end{array}\right)

Sometimes we use the notation
\alpha=\sin\gamma
; in this case we have

\lambda_{\pm}=\pm\cos\gamma,\qquad{\bf v}_{\pm}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin\gamma\\[6.45831pt]
-1\pm\cos\gamma\end{array}\right)

The diagonalization is realized by means of the matrix
T
given by

T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\alpha\\[6.45831pt]
-1+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}&-1-\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\end{array}\right)

as follows,

\Psi_{R}(x)=T\tilde{\Psi}(x),\quad T^{-1}M^{\prime}T={\cal A}^{\prime}(x)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}&0\\[6.45831pt]
0&-\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\end{array}\right):={\cal A}^{\prime}(x)D_{\alpha},

Once replaced these expressions in (
3.21
) we get

-\tilde{\Psi}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left({\cal A^{\prime}}(x)D_{\alpha}+\Big(\frac{\varepsilon\alpha+k}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}{\cal A}(x))\Big)^{2}-\frac{(\varepsilon+\alpha k)^{2}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\,I\right)\tilde{\Psi}(x)=0

(3.22)

Let us call the upper and lower components of
\tilde{\Psi}(x)
in the form
\tilde{\psi}^{+}(x)
and
\tilde{\psi}^{-}(x)
,
respectively, then each one satisfies the equation:

-(\tilde{\psi}^{\pm})^{\prime\prime}(x)+\left(W^{2}(x)\pm W^{\prime}(x)-\mu\right)\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}(x)=0

(3.23)

where

W(x)=\frac{\varepsilon\alpha+k}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,{\cal A}(x),\quad\mu=\frac{(\varepsilon+\alpha k)^{2}}{1-\alpha^{2}},

(3.24)

or

W(x)=\frac{\varepsilon\sin\gamma+k}{\cos\gamma}+\cos\gamma\,{\cal A}(x),\quad\mu=\frac{(\varepsilon+k\sin\gamma)^{2}}{\cos^{2}\gamma}

(3.25)

All this means that the components
\tilde{\psi}^{\pm}
are supersymmetric components of
two partner Hamiltonians
[
12
]
.

These formulas are valid for the magnetic case where
0<|\alpha|<1
; however they can be
extended to the electric case with
|\alpha|>1
, just by including some pure
imaginary terms in (
3.24
):

\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}=i\sqrt{\alpha^{2}-1}=i\,\sinh\gamma\,,\qquad\alpha=\cosh\gamma

For the trigonometric case,
\alpha=\cos\gamma
, if we choose
{\cal A}(x)=\beta x
then we obtain the effective potential
of the harmonic oscillator with a frequency depending on
\alpha
.
This kind of examples were dealt with in
[
26
,
27
,
41
]
and references therein; also for applications of this case to coherent states see
[
42
]
.

4
Square well potential

Let us consider in this section the case of a pure electric square well potential with profile defined by

{\cal V}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-v_{0},&|x|<1\,,\\[6.45831pt]
0,&|x|>1\end{array}\right.

(4.26)

The corresponding Schrödinger equation with effective potential (
3.19
) is:

-\tilde{\psi}_{1}^{\prime\prime}(x)+V_{\rm eff}(x)=-(k^{2}-\varepsilon^{2})\tilde{\psi}_{1}(x)

(4.27)

Here, the complex effective potential has the following form:

V_{\rm eff}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-iv_{0}\delta(x+1)+iv_{0}\delta(x-1)-2v_{0}\varepsilon-v_{0}^{2},\quad|x|\leq 1,\\[6.45831pt]
0,\quad|x|>1\end{array}\right.

(4.28)

For this potential we have three different regions:
x<-1
,
-1<x<1
, and
x>1
. In the first and third region the potential vanishes while, in the second one is a constant
(-2v_{0}\varepsilon-v_{0}^{2})
. Each region is separated from its neighbour by a Dirac’s delta with imaginary intensity. Next, we write the solutions corresponding to bound states within each of these regions:

•

x<-1
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}-\tilde{\psi}_{1,I}^{\prime\prime}(x)=-p^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{1,I}(x),&p^{2}=k^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}>0\\[6.45831pt]
\tilde{\psi}_{1,I}(x)=Ae^{px},&p=\sqrt{k^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}}>0\end{array}

(4.29)

•

-1<x<1
:

\begin{array}[]{ll}-\tilde{\psi}_{1,II}^{\prime\prime}(x)+(-2v_{0}\varepsilon-v_{0}^{2})\tilde{\psi}_{1,II}(x)=-p^{2}\tilde{\psi}_{1,II}(x),&q^{2}=2v_{0}\varepsilon+v_{0}^{2}-p^{2}>0\\[6.45831pt]
\tilde{\psi}_{1,II}(x)=Ce^{iqx}+De^{-iqx},&q=\sqrt{(\varepsilon+v_{0})^{2}-k^{2}}>0\end{array}

(4.30)

•

x>1
:

5
Conclusions

In this paper we have investigated the conditions to obtain analytic solutions of
Dirac-Weyl equation in graphene under electric and magnetic fields. We have obtained
a classification in three cases, which was implicit in many references, but we
hope that our approach includes many of them in a simple and general way.

An example,
for a pure electric potential, has been examined in detail, where we found some
interesting properties such as: i) the relation of this problem with complex effective Schrödinger
potentials; ii) the components of the spinor satisfy the formalism of supersymmetric quantum mechanics but with complex superpotentials; iii) the spectrum and bound states have been computed;
the effective Hamiltonian is PT symmetric and we show that the eigenfunctions implement this
symmetry. Therefore, the PT symmetry is not broken which leads to a real spectrum. The eigenfunctions constitute an orthogonal system.

All these properties are quite appealing, specially the connection of electric potentials with complex effective Schrödinger equations. We have also shown different plots of energy-momentum
k
of the particle in the
y
direction, as well as energy-depth of the potential well. These two kinds of energy plots are complementary, but they are seldom present in the literature.

We also plotted the form of some eigenfunctions together with the probability and current densities which we hope will enlighten some points of the presentation, in particular the symmetries (spacial and PT).
