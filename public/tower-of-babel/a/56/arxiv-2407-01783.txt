Preconditioning of the generalized Stokes problem arising from the approximation of the time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations. Thanks: Draft version, August 8, 2026
Melvin Creff, Jean-Luc Guermond

Abstract
The paper compares standard iterative methods for solving the generalized Stokes problem arising from the time and space approximation of the time-dependent incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. Various preconditioning techniques are considered (Schur complement, fully coupled system, with and without augmented Lagrangian). One investigates whether these methods can compete with traditional pressure-correction and velocity-correction methods in terms of throughput (number of degrees of freedom per time step per core per second). Numerical tests on fine unstructured meshes (68 millions degrees of freedoms) demonstrate GMRES/CG convergence rates that are independent of the mesh size and improve with the Reynolds number for most methods. Three conclusions are drawn: (1) Whether solving the pressure Schur complement or the fully coupled system does not make any significant difference in terms of throughput. (2) Although very good parallel scalability is observed for the augmented Lagrangian method, the best throughput is achieved without using the augmented Lagrangian formulation. (3) The throughput of all the methods tested in the paper are on average 25 times slower than that of traditional pressure-correction and velocity-correction methods. Hence, although all these methods are very efficient for solving steady state problems, none of them is unfortunately competitive for solving time-dependent problems.

Augmented Lagrangian, Stokes problem, preconditioning, Saddle point problem

Introduction

By far, the most popular time-stepping techniques for solving the
time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations are the so-called projection
methods initially proposed by Chorin
[
9
]
and
Temam
[
33
]
. Although significant progresses have been made
since the original work of Chorin and Temam (see e.g.,
[
23
]
for a brief overview of the
literature on the topic), all these methods have however limited
accuracy in time. Moreover, when combined with space discretization,
they do not allow for a good control of the weak divergence of the
velocity field. A better control on the accuracy and on the weak
divergence of the velocity can be obtained by using more traditional
time-stepping techniques combining implicit and explicit Runge-Kutta
techniques. The downside of these techniques though is that one has to
solve at each time step a generalized Stokes problem with a saddle
point structure. Many iterative techniques to solve this problem have
been proposed in the literature (see e.g.,
Elman et al. 2005
for a review of the
literature). They all more or less involve the pressure Schur
complement at some point. But the
condition number of the Schur complement grows unboundedly as the time
step and the viscosity go to zero. This in turn implies that the
performance of these methods strongly depends on how they are
preconditioned. The objective of the paper is to revisit
preconditioning methods for solving the generalized
Stokes problem arising from the time approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations, and
determine whether they can match projection methods in terms of
throughput.

The solution of the generalized Stokes problem has a long history that
started with the landmark papers of
Cahouet and Chabard 1988
and

Fortin and Glowinski 1983
. In
[
7
]
,
the authors propose a preconditioner of the pressure Schur complement
that behaves well in the two limits
\mu{\tau}h^{-2}\to 0
and

\mu{\tau}h^{-2}\to\infty
where
\mu
is the viscosity,
{\tau}
is the
time step, and
h
is the mesh size. In

[
18
]
the authors introduce the augmented
Lagrangian method and show that the conditioning of the pressure Schur
complement improves as the scalar multiplier of the augmented
Lagrangian term increases (cf. e.g.,
Golub and Greif 2003

Benzi and Olshanskii 2006
and
Proposition
2.2
below). An
alternative approach to the Uzawa iterations on the pressure Schur
complement suggested in

Rusten and Winther 1992
,

Elman and Silvester 1996
, consists of iteratively solving
the coupled velocity-pressure system. This method also involves the
pressure Schur complement, but its main advantage is that it does
not require to solve the velocity problem with very high precision
when estimating the action of the Schur complement. This method is
used with the augmented Lagrangian in

Benzi and Olshanskii 2006
,

Benzi et al. 2011
, and

Benzi and Wang 2011
. In these papers the authors show that
combining the Cahouet&Chabard preconditioner and the augmented
Lagrangian technique gives a good preconditioning method of the
generalized Stokes problem. This method has been numerically
evaluated in many papers like

Farrell et al. 2019
,

Moulin et al. 2019
, and

Shih et al. 2023
, where it is indeed shown that
the number of MINRES or GMRES iterations used to solve the pressure
Schur complement problem behaves well with respect to the mesh size
and the viscosity.

We revisit the preconditioning methods discussed above (for the
pressure Schur complement and the fully coupled system) assuming
that the generalized Stokes problem is generated from the time and
space approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations with the
nonlinear term made explicit. The time step is set to satisfy the
standard CFL restriction, see (
2.8
). The approximation
in space is done with mixed finite elements.
Thorough numerical tests on large two-dimensional problems are done
on a HPC cluster using PETSc for the parallel linear algebra and
mixed Hood–Taylor finite elements
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}/{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
and

{\mathbb{P}}_{3}/{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
. The tests are also done with the generic Newtonian
form of the viscous tensor inducing nontrivial couplings between the
Cartesian components of the velocity. This allow us to test the
robustness of the methods and their capability to handle nonlinear
expressions for the stress tensor.

Three key conclusions are drawn from the paper. The first one is that
whether solving the pressure Schur complement problem or the fully
coupled system does not make any significant difference in term of
throughput (see Figure
7
). The second
conclusion is that although very good parallel scalability is observed
for the augmented Lagrangian formulation,
the best throughput is achieved without the augmented Lagrangian
term. The two main reasons are that it takes too many inner
iterations to solve the velocity problem associated with the
augmented Lagrangian term when using the pressure Schur complement
method, and it takes too many outer GMRES iterations when
using the fully coupled method.
Note that this conclusion only holds true in the context the
generalized Stokes problem arising from the time approximation of
the Navier-Stokes equation. The augmented Lagrangian formulation is
very efficient for solving steady state problems.
The third conclusion, summarized in Table
7
,
is that the throughput of all the methods tested in the
paper is nowhere near that of projection methods. They are on average
25 times slower than traditional pressure-correction and
velocity-correction methods.
Hence, although all these methods are
very efficient and close to optimality for solving steady state
problems, our conclusion, at the time of this writing, is that they
cannot reasonably compete with projection methods or variations
thereof for solving the time-dependent Navier–Stokes equations.
This paper demonstrate that to achieve a throughput comparable to
projection methods while being more robust and accurate, new
solution methods accounting for both time and space must be
invented. This paper is an invitation to start new
lines of investigations in this direction.

. We describe the discrete setting and review
preconditioners combining ideas from
Cahouet and Chabard 1988
and

Fortin and Glowinski 1983

Benzi and Olshanskii 2006
,
[

]
,

,

,

. We discuss in

Discarding

{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3}
defined
in (

)). In

2
Formulation of the problem

We formulate the problem and introduce the notation in this section.

2.1
Semi-discrete problem

We consider the generalized Stokes problem arising from the
approximation in time of the time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations in
a domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Here
{\mathcal{D}}
is an open bounded
Lipschitz polyhedron. We denote by
\ell_{\mathcal{D}}
the diameter of
{\mathcal{D}}
.
The exact form of the time discretization that is adopted to
approximate the problem in time is not the concern of this paper and could be
any implicit method like (BDF, IRK, etc.). Let

{\bm{V}}
be a closed subspace of
{\bm{H}}^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
and
Q
be a closed
subspace of
L^{2}({\mathcal{D}})
. All these spaces are equipped with their natural
norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\bm{H}}^{1}({\mathcal{D}})}
,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{D}})}
. We assume
that we are given at each time step a linear form

{\bm{f}}\in{\mathcal{L}}({\bm{V}};{\mathbb{R}})
, and the problem is reduced to seeking a
pair
({\bm{u}},p)\in{\bm{V}}{\times}Q
so that

(2.1)

a({\bm{u}},p)-b({\bm{v}},p)+b({\bm{u}},q)={\bm{f}}({\bm{v}}),\qquad\forall({\bm{v}},q)\in{\bm{V}}{\times}Q,

where the bilinear form
a
and
b
are defined by

(2.2)

a({\bm{v}},{\bm{w}}):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\frac{1}{{\tau}}{\bm{v}}{\cdot}{\bm{w}}+2\mu{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{v}}){:}{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{w}})\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\quad b({\bm{v}},q):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}q\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{v}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x.

Here
{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{v}}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla{\bm{v}}+(\nabla{\bm{v}})^{\mathsf{T}})
is the strain
rate tensor,
\mu
is the shear viscosity, and
{\tau}
is the time
step normalized to includes other factors from the time-steeping method.
The boundary conditions of the problem are encoded in the
definition of the space
{\bm{V}}
and the linear form
{\bm{f}}
. This
problem has a unique solution (see e.g.,
Girault and Raviart 1986
,

Boffi et al. 2013
). Our objective is to
construct an iterative method for the solution of a discrete version
of (
2.1
) that performs well with respect to the product

{\tau}\mu
and the mesh size.

2.2
Discrete problem

In the rest of this paper we solely focus on a discrete version of
(
2.1
). We consider two sequences of finite-dimensional
vector spaces
\{{\bm{V}}_{h}\}_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}
and
\{Q_{h}\}_{h\in{\mathcal{H}}}
where
the index set
{\mathcal{H}}
is countable and has
0
as unique accumulation
point. The velocity is approximated in
{\bm{V}}_{h}
and the pressure is
approximated in
Q_{h}
. To simplify some arguments, we assume that the
velocity approximation is conforming, i.e.,
{\bm{V}}_{h}\subset{\bm{V}}
, and the
pair
({\bm{V}}_{h},Q_{h})
is inf-sup stable, i.e.,

(2.3)

\beta_{h}:=\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}{\setminus}\{0\}}\sup_{{\bm{v}}_{h}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}{\setminus}\{{\bm{0}}\}}\frac{b({\bm{v}}_{h},q_{h})}{\|{\bm{v}}_{h}\|_{{\bm{H}}^{1}({\mathcal{D}})}\|q_{h}\|_{L^{2}({\mathcal{D}})}}>0.

The discrete version of (
2.1
) consists of seeking

{\bm{u}}_{h}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
and
p_{h}\in Q_{h}
so that the following holds true
for all
{\bm{v}}_{h}\in{\bm{V}}_{h}
and all
q_{h}\in Q_{h}
:

(2.4)

a({\bm{u}}_{h},{\bm{v}}_{h})-b({\bm{v}}_{h},p_{h})={\bm{f}}({\bm{v}}_{h}),\qquad b({\bm{u}}_{h},q_{h})=0.

Let
\{{\bm{\phi}}_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}
and
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in{\mathcal{Q}}}
be bases of

{\bm{V}}_{h}
and
Q_{h}
(say, finite-element-based global shape
functions). We define the velocity mass matrix
M_{\bm{V}}
, the velocity
stiffness matrix
E_{\bm{V}}
, and the pressure mass matrix
M_{Q}
by setting

(2.5a)

\displaystyle M_{{\bm{V}}\!,ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\bm{\phi}}_{i}{\cdot}{\bm{\phi}}_{j}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}},

(2.5b)

\displaystyle M_{Q,ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\bm{\psi}}_{i}{\cdot}{\bm{\psi}}_{j}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}},

(2.5c)

\displaystyle E_{{\bm{V}}\!,kl}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}2{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{\phi}}_{k}){:}{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{\phi}}_{l})\,{\mathrm{d}}x\qquad\forall k,l\in{\mathcal{Q}}.

To formulate the algebraic version of (
2.4
), we
introduce the matrices associated with the bilinear forms
a
and
b
:

(2.6)

\displaystyle A_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{{\tau}}M_{{\bm{V}}\!,ij}+\mu E_{{\bm{V}}\!,ij}=a({\bm{\phi}}_{j},{\bm{\phi}}_{i}),\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}},

(2.7)

\displaystyle B_{kj}

\displaystyle:=b({\bm{\phi}}_{j},\psi_{k}),\qquad\forall k\in{\mathcal{Q}},\ \forall j\in{\mathcal{V}}.

To be representative of situations corresponding to the approximation
of the time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations, where the time step
decreases like the mesh size due to the nonlinearities being made
explicit in time, in the rest of this paper we set

(2.8)

\tau=N^{-\frac{1}{d}},

where
N
is the total number of degrees of freedom for one scalar component
of the velocity and
d

is the space dimension.
N^{-\frac{1}{d}}
scales like the mesh size. The
tests reported in the paper are done in two dimensions.

Denoting
{\bm{u}}_{h}:=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathsf{U}}_{i}{\bm{\phi}}_{i}
and

p_{h}:=\sum_{k\in{\mathcal{Q}}}{\mathsf{P}}_{k}\psi_{k}
the approximations of the
velocity and the pressure, the discrete
problem (
2.4
) is then equivalent to solving the
following linear system:

(2.9)

A{\mathsf{U}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{F}},\qquad B{\mathsf{U}}=0.

Two different strategies are advocated in the literature to solve the
saddle point problem (
2.9
). The first one,
which is the oldest, consists of constructing the pressure Schur
complement of the problem and solving it. This method solves first for
the pressure then solves for the velocity. We henceforth refer
to this approach and variations thereof as Method 1. The second
approach consists of solving for both the velocity and the pressure at
the same time; see e.g.,
Rusten and Winther 1992
,

Elman and Silvester 1996
,

Furuichi et al. 2011
,

Shih et al. 2023
. We henceforth refer to
this approach and variations thereof as Method 2. We now detail these
two methods.

2.3
Method 1: The pressure Schur complement

As the coercivity of
a
implies that the matrix
A
is invertible, a
standard way of solving (
2.9
) consists of
constructing the Schur complement with respect to the
pressure. Letting
S:=BA^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
, the problem (
2.9
)
is equivalent to finding

{\mathsf{P}}
and
{\mathsf{U}}
so that

(2.10)

S{\mathsf{P}}=-BA^{-1}{\mathsf{F}},\qquad A{\mathsf{U}}={\mathsf{F}}+B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{P}}.

Note that here the pressure and the velocity problems are somewhat
uncoupled. One first solves for the pressure, then one computes the
velocity. The pressure problem
S{\mathsf{P}}=-BA^{-1}{\mathsf{F}}
is in
general solved iteratively. As the convergence rate of iterative
methods is controlled by the condition number, we now recall what is
known on the
\ell^{2}
-condition number of
S
. The following result
can be found in
Elman et al. 2005
(see also

[
14
, Prop. 50.14]
for other details on
the topic).

Proposition 2.1
(Spectrum of
S
).

Let
\sigma(S)
be the spectrum
of
S
. Let
\mu_{\min}
,
\mu_{\max}
be the smallest and largest
eigenvalues of the pressure mass matrix
M_{Q}
. Let
\|a\|
and

\|b\|

be the norms of the bilinear forms
a
and
b
. Let
\alpha_{h}
be
the coercivity constant of
a
. Then

\sigma(S)\subset[\mu_{\min}\frac{\beta_{h}^{2}}{\|a\|},\mu_{\max}\frac{\|b\|^{2}}{\alpha_{h}}]
.

\square

As
\|a\|\sim\frac{\mu}{\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}+\frac{1}{{\tau}}}
and

\alpha_{h}\sim\frac{\mu}{\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}
, the
\ell^{2}
-condition number
of
S
scales like

(1+\frac{\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}{{\tau}\mu})\frac{\|b\|}{\beta_{h}}
. This number is
larger than
(1+\frac{\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}{{\tau}\mu})
and grows unboundedly as

\frac{\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}}{{\tau}\mu}\to\infty
. Hence, if not properly
preconditioned, iterative methods for solving

S{\mathsf{P}}=-BA^{-1}{\mathsf{F}}
have convergence rates that degrade as the
viscosity decreases and the time step goes to zero.

The loss of convergence rate mentioned above can
be mitigated by using the augmented Lagrangian technique introduced by

Fortin and Glowinski 1983
. This method replaces the original
system (
2.9
) by the following equivalent system:

(2.11a)

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{U}}-B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{F}},\qquad B{\mathsf{U}}=0,

(2.11b)

\displaystyle A_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=A+\lambda\mu B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B,

where
\lambda
is a non-dimensional positive parameter. Here again, one can solve the
problem by constructing the pressure Schur complement,

(2.12a)

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}=-B(A+\lambda\mu B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B)^{-1}{\mathsf{F}},\qquad A{\mathsf{U}}={\mathsf{F}}+B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{P}},

(2.12b)

\displaystyle S_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:=B(A+\lambda\mu B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}.

The main properties of this method are summarized in the following
standard result (cf. e.g.,
Golub and Greif 2003

Benzi and Olshanskii 2006
)

Proposition 2.2
(Augmented Lagrangian).

Let
s_{\flat}
and
s_{\sharp}
be the smallest and largest eigenvalues
of
S
. Then the following holds true:

(2.13a)

\displaystyle S_{\rho}^{-1}=\rho M_{Q}^{-1}+S^{-1},

(2.13b)

\displaystyle\sigma(M_{Q}^{-1}S_{\rho})\subset[(\rho+s_{\flat}^{-1})^{-1},(\rho+s_{\sharp}^{-1})^{-1}].

\square

The above estimate of the spectrum of
S_{\lambda}
shows that the

\ell^{2}
-condition number of
S_{\lambda}
converges to unity as

\lambda\mu{\tau}/\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\to\infty
. But, as the condition number
of
S_{\lambda}
converges to unity, the condition number of

A_{\lambda}
becomes unrealistically large when

\lambda\mu{\tau}/\ell_{\mathcal{D}}^{2}\to\infty
. Hence, one important
difficulty with the augmented Lagrangian method is to find a value of

\lambda
that is balanced.

In the rest of the paper we discuss left preconditioning techniques for

S_{\lambda}
and the pros and cons for the augmented Lagrangian
method.

2.4
Method 2: coupled problem

As observed in
Rusten and Winther 1992
(second
to last paragraph of the introduction therein), for
Krylov methods based on the pressure Schur complement to
work properly, it is necessary to
compute
S{\mathsf{R}}
exactly for all pressure vector
{\mathsf{R}}
.
This entails that it is imperative to solve the velocity problem

A{\bm{W}}={\bm{K}}
exactly for all velocity vector
{\mathsf{K}}
. One way to avoid
this constraint consists of using Krylov methods based on the
full velocity-pressure problem as advocated in

[
31
]
; (see also Eq. (2.5)
and (2.12) in
Elman and Silvester 1996
). The idea is to
exploit the following exact factorization

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\lambda}&-B^{\mathsf{T}}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\bm{V}}&A_{\lambda}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}\\
0&I_{Q}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{\lambda}^{-1}&0\\
0&S_{\lambda}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{\bm{V}}&0\\
-BA_{\lambda}^{-1}&I_{Q}\end{pmatrix},

where
I_{\bm{V}}
and
I_{Q}
are the identity matrices. This identity
shows that is suffices to replace
A^{-1}
and
S^{-1}
by
preconditioners to construct preconditioners of
{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}
.
Denoting by
{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}
,
{\widetilde{S}}_{\lambda}^{-1}

preconditioners of
{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}
,
{\widetilde{S}}_{\lambda}
, the second
method we consider (henceforth called Method 2) consists of
using GMRES with the following preconditioner:

(2.15)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}\sim\begin{pmatrix}I_{\bm{V}}&{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}\\
0&I_{Q}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}&0\\
0&{\widetilde{S}}_{\lambda}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{\bm{V}}&0\\
-B{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}&I_{Q}\end{pmatrix}.

No exact inverse has to be computed. It should also be noted that,
contrary to appearances, this method only requires two inversions of

A_{\lambda}
at each Krylov iteration.

In the rest of the paper we are going to investigate left
preconditioning techniques for
{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}
using variations of the preconditioners
{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}^{-1}
and
{\widetilde{S}}_{\lambda}^{-1}

in (
2.15
). We are also going to investigate
whether the augmented Lagrangian version of the method is indeed
superior to the non augmented one (
A_{\lambda}
vs.
A_{0}:=A
) in
terms of throughput. Finally we are going to compare Method 1 and
Method 2 in terms of throughput.

Solving the velocity problem

associated with the solution of the velocity
problem which consists of solving
A_{\lambda}{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{F}}
for

\lambda{\geq}0
.

3.2
Preconditioners for the velocity
problem

When
\lambda>0
,
it is very difficult to assemble the matrix coefficients of

A_{\lambda}=A+\lambda\mu B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B
because we use
continuous finite elements to approximate the pressure (i.e.,
{\mathbb{P}}_{k+1}/{\mathbb{P}}_{k}
Hood–Taylor elements,
k\geq 1
). The first
reason is that the inverse of the pressure matrix
M_{Q}^{-1}
cannot be
easily computed. It can be approximated by using lumping techniques
though. The second reason is that even if the mass is lumped,
the sparsity pattern of the matrix is greatly increased because the
stencil of each dof is based on two concentric layers of cells
instead of only one. This drastically increases the memory footprint
as well as the communications needed for parallel computing.
Therefore, we iteratively solve
A_{\lambda}{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{F}}
by using
the preconditioned CG algorithm in PETSc only requiring the matrix
action of
A_{\lambda}
.

As we use Dirichlet boundary conditions, the bilinear form

\int_{\mathcal{D}}{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{u}}){:}{\mathbbm{e}}({\bm{v}})\,{\mathrm{d}}x
is also equal to

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\bm{u}}{:}\nabla{\bm{v}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{u}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{v}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x

because integrating by parts twice shows that
\int_{\mathcal{D}}(\nabla{\bm{u}})^{\mathsf{T}}{:}\nabla({\bm{v}})\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{u}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{v}}\,{\mathrm{d}}x
for all
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in{\bm{H}}_{0}^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
. Hence defining
L_{\bm{V}}
to be the stiffness matrix
associated with the bilinear form

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\bm{v}}_{h}{:}\nabla{\bm{w}}_{h}\,{\mathrm{d}}x
and
D
to be
the stiffness matrix associated with the grad-div bilinear form

\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{v}}_{h}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{w}}_{h}\,{\mathrm{d}}x
; i.e.,

(3.1)

\displaystyle L_{{\bm{V}},ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\bm{\phi}}_{i}{:}\nabla{\bm{\phi}}_{j}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}},

(3.2)

\displaystyle D_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{\phi}}_{i}\nabla{\cdot}{\bm{\phi}}_{j}\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}},

we obtain the following
equivalent representation of the matrix
A
:

(3.3)

A={\tau}^{-1}M_{\bm{V}}+\mu(L_{\bm{V}}+D),

where
M_{\bm{V}}
is the velocity mass matrix. This leads use to
consider the following three candidates to precondition the matrix

A_{\lambda}
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,1}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}M_{\bm{V}}+\mu L_{\bm{V}}+\mu(1+\lambda)D=A+\lambda\mu D,

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,2}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}M_{\bm{V}}+\mu L_{\bm{V}}+\mu D=A,

(3.6)

\displaystyle{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}M_{\bm{V}}+\mu L_{\bm{V}}.

In the first preconditioner, we replace
B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B
by
D
, (see e.g.,
Heister and Rapin 2013
). In
the second preconditioner, we remove
B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B
. In the third
preconditioner, we remove both
D
and
B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B
. The stencil
for these three preconditioners is standard when using Lagrange finite
elements. Once the coefficients of the matrices

{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,1},{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,2},{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3}
are assembled, the
linear systems involving these matrices can be easily solved using
BoomerAMG.

Performances of the velocity preconditioners

We now test the three preconditioners
(

)–(
3.6
)
with continuous Lagrange finite elements in 2D.

The setting

We use the parameters

3.5
Conclusions for §
3

We draw two important conclusions from the
series of tests done in section §
3
. The first
one is that although the grad-div operator has very interesting
properties emphasized in the literature
(
Benzi and Olshanskii 2006
,
Olshanskii et al. 2009
,

Guermond and Minev 2015
,
de Frutos et al. 2016
),
efficiently solving problems requiring the solution of linear systems
involving the matrix
D
or variations thereof is difficult (see e.g.,
Heister and Rapin 2013
,
Jenkins et al. 2014
,
Fiordilino et al. 2018
).
The best preconditioner for this problem is
{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3}
, which
consists of dropping the coupling block
D
.
The second conclusion is
that a speed-up factor between 2 and 3 can be gained by requesting that BoomerAMG
only performs 2 V-cycles at each iteration of the velocity problem, as done in
({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
,
instead of reaching a fixed relative threshold.

4
Solving the pressure Schur complement

In this section we consider various preconditioners of the pressure
Schur complement
S_{\lambda}
defined in (
2.12b
).
These preconditioners are tested in
§
5
and
§
6
.

4.1
Preconditioning
S_{\lambda}

Ideas to precondition
S_{\lambda}
can be found by investigating how

S_{\lambda}
degenerates in the limit
\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to\infty

and in the limit
\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to 0
; see e.g.,
Cahouet and Chabard 1988
and

Bramble and Pasciak 1997
.

In the limit
\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to 0
, we have
S_{\lambda}\to{\tau}B(M_{\bm{V}})^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
. The inverse of
S_{\lambda}
in this limit is then
{\tau}^{-1}(B(M_{\bm{V}})^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}
.

In the limit
\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to\infty
, we have

S_{\lambda}\to\mu^{-1}B(E_{\bm{V}}+\lambda B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
, where we recall that the matrix
E_{\bm{V}}
is defined in
(
2.5c
). As
E_{\bm{V}}
and
B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B
have similar
spectra, it seems natural to approximate
S_{\lambda}
by

\mu^{-1}(1+\lambda)^{-1}B(B^{\mathsf{T}}M_{Q}^{-1}B)^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
in the limit

\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to\infty
. Hence we
posit that the inverse of
S_{\lambda}
approximately behaves like

\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1}
when
\mu{\tau}/h^{2}\to\infty
.

In conclusion, the first preconditioner for
S_{\lambda}
that we consider in the paper
is

(4.1)

S_{\lambda}^{-1}\sim C_{\lambda}:={\tau}^{-1}(B(M_{\bm{V}})^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}+\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1}.

In light of the identity (
2.13a
),
the above expression is consistent with the approximation of

S_{0}^{-1}
by
{\tau}^{-1}(B(M_{\bm{V}})^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}+\mu M_{Q}^{-1}
,
which is the preconditioner of the Schur complement
S_{0}
considered
in
Cahouet and Chabard 1988
.

As the matrix
BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}

is only used as part of the preconditioner for
S_{\lambda}
,
replacing the mass matrix
M_{\bm{V}}
by a lumped one could save some
computation time. To explore this idea, we propose to replace
M_{\bm{V}}
by a diagonal
matrix
\Lambda_{\bm{V}}
with entries defined by

(4.2)

\Lambda_{{\bm{V}},ij}:=\delta_{ij}\int_{\mathcal{D}}|\varphi_{i}({\bm{x}})|\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\forall i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}.

Taking the absolute value of the shape function is important here as

{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
and
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}
velocity shape functions are not uniformly positive.

In
[
7
]
, the authors also propose using the
discrete pressure Laplacian instead of
B(M_{\bm{V}})^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
, i.e., they propose the preconditioner

S_{\lambda}^{-1}\sim{\tau}^{-1}(L_{Q})^{-1}+\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1}
where

L_{Q}
is the matrix of the pressure Laplacian with entries

(4.3)

L_{Q,kl}:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla\psi_{k}{\cdot}\nabla\psi_{l}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\qquad\forall k,l,\in{\mathcal{Q}}.

In conclusion, in the rest of this paper we are going to compare the
following three preconditioners for
S_{\lambda}
:

(4.4a)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}(B\ M_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}+\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1},

(4.4b)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}(B\ \Lambda_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}+\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1},

(4.4c)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle:={\tau}^{-1}(L_{Q})^{-1}+\mu(1+\lambda)M_{Q}^{-1}.

In the next two sections we discuss practical details regarding the
implementation of the above preconditioners using PETSc and BoomerAMG.

4.2
Solving
BM_{V}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{Y}}

The coefficients of the matrix
BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
cannot be easily
computed when using continuous elements for the pressure. Even if the
velocity mass matrix is lumped, the stencil of the resulting
simplified matrix is based on two concentric layers of cells
around each dof. Hence, the only practical way to solve the linear
system
BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{Y}}
is to use a preconditioned
iterative method. We use the PETSc version of GMRES only requiring
the matrix action.

We use BoomerAMG with strong threshold equal to 0.1 to estimate

M_{\bm{V}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
(i.e., to solve
M_{\bm{V}}{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
). Henceforth, when
we write
(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
, we mean that the linear system

M_{\bm{V}}{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
is solved with BoomerAMG with relative threshold

10^{-10}
. As
BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
is just a preconditioner, we can
also replace the solution of the linear system
M_{\bm{V}}{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
by
just using 2 V-cycles in BoomerAMG. We are going to use the notation

B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
to refer to this other method.

A natural preconditioner for the matrices
B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
and

B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
is the pressure Laplacian matrix

L_{Q}
defined in (
4.3
). However, this matrix is
singular when Dirichlet boundary conditions are enforced on the
velocity over the entire boundary of the domain
{\mathcal{D}}
. As we only
want to construct a preconditioner, one can consider instead the
matrix with entries
\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q}
with
\epsilon>0
. We henceforth set

\epsilon=1
.

In the rest of this section we
evaluate the performance of the preconditioner
\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q}
using BoomerAMG (with
strong threshold equal to 0.1).
We do two series of tests. In the first series, we
precondition the matrix
B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}

with
\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q}
, and
we solve the linear system
(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q}){\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
with
BoomerAMG by iterating until the relative threshold
10^{-10}
is reached. We use the
notation
(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}
for this preconditioner.
In the second series of tests, we precondition the matrix

B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
with 2 V-cycles of BoomerAMG using
the coefficients of the matrix
\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q}
. We use the notation

(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
for this preconditioner.

The tests are done with continuous Lagrange finite elements in two
space dimensions. We consider mixed
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}/{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
and

{\mathbb{P}}_{3}/{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
elements.
For each mesh we build the
right-hand
{\mathsf{Y}}:=B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{X}}
(or

{\mathsf{Y}}:=B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{X}}

matrix
B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}

5
Numerical illustration of the performance of Method 1

In this section we focus on the solution Method 1 defined
in (
2.12a
) and compare the performance of the
preconditioners
C_{\lambda}
,
C_{\lambda}^{\Lambda}
, and

C_{\lambda}^{\Delta}
defined (
4.4
).

5.1
Notation

To be precise and make sure that the tests
reported in the paper are reproducible, we precisely define the
preconditioners we are going to use for the pressure Schur
complement. Using the symbols “
\mathsf{th}
” and “
\mathsf{2Vc}
”
introduced in the previous sections, for all

{\mathsf{a}}\in\{\mathsf{th},\mathsf{2Vc}\}
, we set

(5.1a)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}}}

\displaystyle:=\mu(1+\lambda)(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}+{\tau}^{-1}(B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1},

(5.1b)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}}}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle:=\mu(1+\lambda)(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}+{\tau}^{-1}(B\Lambda_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1},

(5.1c)

\displaystyle C_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}}}^{\Delta}

\displaystyle:=\mu(1+\lambda)(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}+{\tau}^{-1}(L_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}.

We recall that the notation
{\mathsf{W}}=(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
means that we
solve the problem
M_{Q}{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
with BoomerAMG by iterating until
the relative residual reaches the threshold
10^{-10}
if

{\mathsf{a}}=\mathsf{th}
or by using 2 V-cycles if
{\mathsf{a}}=\mathsf{2Vc}
. The
same convention holds for
{\mathsf{W}}=(L_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
and

{\mathsf{W}}=(M_{\mathsf{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
.

The notation
{\mathsf{W}}=(B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}

means that the problem

B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{Z}}
is solved with GMRES using the matrix
action and preconditioned by
(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{Q})_{{\mathsf{{\mathsf{a}}}}}^{-1}
. We use the same definition for

{\mathsf{W}}=(B\Lambda_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
.
We refer the reader to §
4.2
and §
4.3
where we
explain how the problems
(B(M_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})W={\mathsf{Z}}
and

(B\Lambda_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}W={\mathsf{Z}}
are solved.

5.2
Numerical details

We recall that to be representative of situations corresponding to the
approximation of the time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations, where the
time step decreases like the mesh size due to the nonlinearities being
made explicit in time, we set
\tau=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, where
N
is the
total number of grid points for the velocity.

Method 1 consists of iterating on the pressure Schur complement,

S_{\lambda}
, using GMRES until the relative threshold on the residual
is less than or equal to
10^{-10}
. The
following operations are done at each GMRES iterations:

•

One matrix-vector multiplication with the Schur complement matrix

S_{\lambda}
. The only nontrivial step in this operation consists of solving one
velocity problem
A_{\lambda}{\mathsf{X}}={\mathsf{Y}}
where
A_{\lambda}
is defined in
(
2.11b
). This is done with CG only using the matrix action. The
relative threshold is
10^{-10}
. The preconditioner is

({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
. This requires the following
non-trivial operations:

–

If
\lambda>0
, the matrix-vector multiplications by
A_{\lambda}
require
solving a pressure mass problem. This is done using
(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}
.

–

The application of the preconditioner

({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
.

•

One application of the preconditioner, i.e.,
C_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}},{\mathsf{b}}}
,
C^{\Lambda}_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}}}
, or

C^{\Delta}_{\lambda,{\mathsf{a}},{\mathsf{b}}}
. This requires:

–

Solving one pressure mass problem
(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
.

–

Solving one problem involving

(B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{T})^{-1}_{{\mathsf{a}}}
or

(B\Lambda_{V}^{-1}B^{T})^{-1}_{{\mathsf{a}}}
. This is done using GMRES with
rel. thr.
10^{-10}
and preconditioning the system with

(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{V})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}
. This requires the
following non-trivial operations:

*

Solving a velocity mass problem
(L_{\bm{V}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}{\mathsf{Z}}
if one uses
B(M_{{\bm{V}}})_{{\mathsf{a}}}^{-1}B^{T}
.

*

The application of the preconditioner

(\epsilon M_{Q}+L_{V})_{\textup{th}}^{-1}
.

All the simulations are done with mixed

{\mathbb{P}}_{2}/{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
continuous finite elements.
We test five meshes with
the following velocity/pressure grid point counts: Mesh 1
(118,785/29,857); Mesh 2 (473,857/118,785); Mesh 3 (1,892,865/473,857);
Mesh 4 (7,566,337/1,892,865); Mesh 5 (30,255,105/7,566,337).
As the velocity field is two dimensional,
the total number of degrees of freedom is for each mesh: Mesh 1
(267,427); Mesh 2 (1,066,499); Mesh 3 (4,259,587); Mesh 4
(17,025,539); Mesh 5 (68,076,547).
Computation done on
{\mathbb{P}}_{3}/{\mathbb{P}}_{2}
finite elements gave similar results as the ones shown here
and are therefore not reported for brevity.

In order to properly compare the various methods,
we estimate the throughput per second that is achieved with the relative threshold
10^{-10}
.
This is the ratio consisting of dividing the total number of degrees of freedom by
the wall-clock time (in second) multiplied by the number of processors:

(5.2)

\text{TPS}:=\frac{\text{nb. vel.+press. degrees of freedom}}{\text{wall-clock time(s)}{\times}\text{nb. processor}}.

The inverse of
TPS
is the computational time (s) spent per
degrees of freedom to achieve the desired relative threshold on the
residual (
10^{-10}
in our case
).

To avoid making a compilation of dozens of tables, we have chosen to
only focus our attention on the throughput per second. We thus do not
discuss the convergence of the errors on the velocity and pressure but
we have verified that they scales properly with the mesh size.

reported numerous time in the literature). As a result, the time spent
solving the augmented Lagrangian velocity problem is not even compensated
by the reduction in GMRES iterations.

5.5
Conclusions of §
5

We conclude that the preconditioner
(C_{0}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}

is excellent. We also conclude that
(C_{0}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}

outperforms in throughput
its augmented Lagrangian counterparts. Although the method
is robust in terms of GMRES iteration, a slight loss of weak
scalability is observed due to communications between nodes.

6
Numerical illustration of the performance of Method 2

In this section we illustrate the performance of the solution method
described in §
2.4
.

6.1
Numerical details

We recall that Method 2 consists of iteratively solving the coupled problem

(6.1)

{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathsf{U}}\\
{\mathsf{P}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}{\mathsf{F}}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{with}\quad{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}:=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\lambda}&-B^{\mathsf{T}}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix}.

We use GMRES to solve this problem. For all the tests reported in the
paper, we keep iterating until the threshold on the
relative residual reaches
10^{-10}
. The following operations are
done at each GMRES iterations:

•

One matrix-vector multiplication with the matrix
{\mathbb{A}}_{\lambda}
.

–

If
\lambda=0
, all the operations are trivial.

–

If
\lambda>0
, the augmented Lagrangian part of

A_{\lambda}
requires solving one pressure mass problem.
This is done using
(M_{Q})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}
.

•

One application of the preconditioner. This entails the following operations:

–

Two inversion of
{\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda}
. This is done with CG with
rel. thr.
10^{-10}
using one of the preconditioners

({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,2})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}
,
({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,2})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
,

({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3})_{{\mathsf{th}}}^{-1}
, or
({\widetilde{A}}_{\lambda,3})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}^{-1}
.

–

Inversion of the Schur complement using either

(C_{\lambda}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{th}}}
or

(C_{\lambda}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}
(see §
5.1
and
§
5.2
for details).

The method is tested using
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}/{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
continuous Lagrange
elements on the same meshes as above and the viscosities

\mu\in\{1,10^{-2},10^{4}\}
.

7
Conclusions

We summarize the findings of the paper in this section. All the conclusions
recorded here hold assuming that all the subproblems are solved
iteratively (which is a necessity for very large
problems). No direct solver is used at any stage. We also recall
that, to be representative of situations corresponding to the approximation
of the time-dependent Navier-Stokes equations,
all the tests done in the paper assume that the time step scales like
the mesh size, i.e.,
{\tau}=N^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in dimension 2, where
N
is
the total number of velocity grid points (see (
2.8
)).

7.1
Cahouet&Chabard

We have shown that
(C_{0}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{2Vc}}}
and

(C_{0}^{\Lambda})_{{\mathsf{th}}}
are excellent preconditioners of the pressure
Schur complement matrix (see Figure
1
).

We have showed/confirmed that using the discrete pressure Laplacian
instead of the full matrix
BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
in the Cahouet&Chabard
preconditioning of the pressure Schur complement is not a
robust strategy. This gives a methods that converges slowly on fine
meshes and when the thresholds is stringent (see
Figure
2
). Hence, we recommend using

BM_{\bm{V}}^{-1}B^{\mathsf{T}}
in the preconditioner even if this means solving
another linear problem using another iterative method.

7.2
Augmented Lagrangian versus Cahouet&Chabard

We have confirmed that the preconditioned augmented
Lagrangian method requires less GMRES iterations than the traditional
Cahouet&Chabard method for solving the pressure Schur complement
problem. However, too much time is spent solving the velocity problem
in the augmented Lagrangian method. The main difficulty consists of
inverting the discrete version of the grad-div operator. Although the
augmented Lagrangian method requires less outer iterations to
reach convergence, the overall throughput of the augmented
Lagrangian method is significantly lower than that of the Cahouet&Chabard method and
deteriorates as
\lambda
grows (see
Figure
3
).

7.3
Schur complement versus the full system

We have shown that, when the time step
{\tau}

scales like the mesh size, there is no significant throughput gain
by solving the full system instead of just solving the
pressure Schur complement.
