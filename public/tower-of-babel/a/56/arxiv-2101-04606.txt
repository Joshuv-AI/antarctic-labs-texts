The effect of disorder on quenched and averaged large deviations for random walks in random environments: boundary behavior
Rodrigo Bazaes, Chiranjib Mukherjee, Alejandro F. Ramírez and Santiago Saglietti

Abstract
. For a random walk in a uniformly elliptic and i.i.d. environment on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} with d ≥ 4 d\geq 4 , we show that the quenched and annealed large deviations rate functions agree on any compact set contained in the boundary ∂ 𝔻 := { x ∈ ℝ d : | x | 1 = 1 } \partial\mathbb{D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|_{1}=1\} of their domain which does not intersect any of the ( d − 2 ) (d-2) -dimensional facets of ∂ 𝔻 \partial\mathbb{D} , provided that the disorder of the environment is low enough. As a consequence, we obtain a simple explicit formula for both rate functions on ∂ 𝔻 \partial\mathbb{D} at low disorder. In contrast to previous works, our results do not assume any ballistic behavior of the random walk and are not restricted to neighborhoods of any given point (on the boundary ∂ 𝔻 \partial\mathbb{D} ). In addition, our results complement those in [ BMRS19 ] , where, using different methods, we investigate the equality of the rate functions in the interior of their domain. Finally, for a general parametrized family of environments, we show that the strength of disorder determines a phase transition in the equality of both rate functions, in the sense that for each x ∈ ∂ 𝔻 x\in\partial\mathbb{D} there exists ε x \varepsilon_{x} such that the two rate functions agree at x x when the disorder is smaller than ε x \varepsilon_{x} and disagree when its larger. This further reconfirms the idea, introduced in [ BMRS19 ] , that the disorder of the environment is in general intimately related with the equality of the rate functions.



1.
Introduction and background

The model of a random walk in a random environment (RWRE) can be described as follows. Let
|x|_{1}
denote the
\ell^{1}
-norm of any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define
\mathbb{V}:=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|x|_{1}=1\}=\{\pm e_{1},\dots,\pm e_{d}\}
, the set of all unit vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, along with
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{V}):=\big\{\vec{p}=(p(e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\in[0,1]^{\mathbb{V}}\colon\,\,\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}p(e)=1\big\}
, the space of all probability vectors therein and the product space
\Omega:=(\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{V}))^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with the usual product topology.
Any element
\omega\in\Omega
will be called an
environment
, i.e. each
\omega=(\omega(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is a sequence of probability vectors
\omega(x)=(\omega(x,e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}
on
\mathbb{V}
indexed by the sites in the lattice. Given any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, the
random walk in the environment
\omega
starting at
x
is defined as the Markov chain
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
whose law
P_{x,\omega}
is given by

\displaystyle P_{x,\omega}(X_{0}=x)=1\quad\text{ and }\quad

\displaystyle P_{x,\omega}(X_{n+1}=y+e\,|\,X_{n}=y)=\omega(y,e)\quad\forall\,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,,\,e\in\mathbb{V}.

We call
P_{x,\omega}
the quenched law of the RWRE. Then, if the environment
\omega
is now chosen at random according to some Borel probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Omega
, we now obtain the measure
P_{x}
on
\Omega\times(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
defined as

P_{x}(A\times B):=\int_{A}P_{x,\omega}(B)\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\qquad\forall\,A\in\mathcal{B}(\Omega)\,,\,B\in\mathcal{B}((\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}).

We call
P_{x}
the annealed law of the RWRE and, in general, we will call the sequence
X=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
under
P_{x}
a RWRE with environmental law
\mathbb{P}
.

In the sequel, we shall work with environmental laws satisfying the following assumption:

Assumption
\mathrm{A}
: Under
\mathbb{P}
, the environment is
i.i.d.
(the random vectors
(\omega(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
are independent and identically distributed) and
uniformly elliptic
, i.e., there is a constant
\kappa>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(\omega(x,e)\geq\kappa\text{ for all $x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$ and $e\in\mathbb{V}$})=1.

(1.1)

In
[
V03
]
, Varadhan proved that, for any
d\geq 1
and under Assumption
\mathrm{A}
, both the quenched law
P_{0,\omega}\big(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in\cdot\big)

and its annealed version
P_{0}\big(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in\cdot\big)
satisfy a
large deviations principle
(LDP), i.e. there exist lower-semicontinuous functions
I_{a},I_{q}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
such that for any
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with interior
G^{\circ}
and closure
\overline{G}
,

\displaystyle-\inf_{x\in G^{\circ}}I_{q}(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}\left(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in G\right)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}\left(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in G\right)\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{G}}I_{q}(x)

(1.2)

\displaystyle-\inf_{x\in G^{\circ}}I_{a}(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}\left(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in G\right)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}\left(\frac{X_{n}}{n}\in G\right)\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{G}}I_{a}(x)

(1.3)

with the first assertion being true for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega\in\Omega
. It can be shown that the rate functions
I_{q}
and
I_{a}
are both convex and are finite if and only if
x\in\mathbb{D}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|_{1}\leq 1\}
. Being also lower semicontinuous, the former implies that
I_{q}
and
I_{a}
are continuous on
\mathbb{D}
, see
[
R97
, Theorem 10.2]
. Moreover, by Jensen’s inequality and Fatou’s lemma, we always have the dominance
I_{a}(\cdot)\leq I_{q}(\cdot)
.
In
[
V03
]
it was also shown that, for
d\geq 2
,
I_{a}(0)=I_{q}(0)
and both rate functions have the same zero-sets, leaving open the question of whether both rate functions are in fact equal in other parts of their domain. In this regard, Yilmaz showed later in
[
Y11
]
that, for RWRE with
d\geq 4
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
, both rate functions agree on
some
neighborhood of the non-zero velocity, whenever the random walk satisfies Sznitman’s
condition
-(T) for ballisticity, see
[
S01
]
for a precise definition.
1
1

1

In contrast, this has been shown to be false in
[
YZ10
]
for dimensions
d\in\{2,3\}
: there exists a class of
non-nestling
random walks in i.i.d. and uniformly elliptic environments verifying that there is no neighborhood of the velocity on which the two rate functions are identical.
Recently in
[
BMRS19
]
, we have shown that for
d\geq 4
the two rate functions agree on any compact set in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
which does not contain zero, provided that the disorder of the environment is low enough and regardless of whether the RWRE is ballistic. In the current work, we show that, despite the behavior of the RWRE on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
of
\mathbb{D}
being quite different than in its interior, the above low-disorder phenomenon extends also to
\partial\mathbb{D}
. Indeed, we show that
I_{q}=I_{a}
holds on any compact set contained in
\partial\mathbb{D}
(avoiding its
(d-2)
-dimensional facets), provided that the disorder of the environment is sufficiently low. As a consequence, we obtain a simple explicit formula for the quenched rate function on
\partial\mathbb{D}
at low disorder. Finally, for a general parametrized family of environments, we show that the strength of disorder determines a phase transition in the equality of both rate functions, in the sense that for each
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
there exists
\varepsilon_{x}
such that the two rate functions agree at
x
when the disorder is smaller than
\varepsilon_{x}
and disagree when its larger. We turn to the precise statements of these results.

2.
Main result: Quenched and Annealed rate functions on the boundary

Given any such environmental law
\mathbb{P}
, we define its
disorder
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P}):=\inf\big\{\varepsilon>0:\xi(x,e)\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon],\,\,\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s. for all }e\in\mathbb{V}\text{ and }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\big\},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\qquad\mbox{with }\,\,\xi(x,e):=\frac{\omega(x,e)}{\alpha(e)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha(e):=\mathbb{E}[\omega(x,e)]\qquad\forall\,e\in\mathbb{V}.,

(2.2)

where
\mathbb{E}
denotes expectation w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}
and the definition of
\alpha(e)
does not depend on
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
by Assumption
\mathrm{A}
. Moreover, both

\xi(x,e)
and
\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P})
are well-defined since
\mathbb{P}
satisfies Assumption
\mathrm{A}
, whereas

\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P})
is the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{P})
-norm of the random vector
(\xi(x,e)-1)_{e\in\mathbb{V}}
for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

We set
\partial{\mathbb{D}}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\colon|x|_{1}=1\}
for the boundary of the unit ball and write

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{D}(s):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x|=1\text{ and }x_{j}s_{j}\geq 0\ {\rm for}\ {\rm all}\ 1\leq j\leq d\}\quad\mbox{and also},}

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}:=\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:x_{j}=0\text{ for some }1\leq j\leq d\}.}

Notice that the subsets
\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
for
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
correspond to the different
faces
of the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
.

2.1.
Equality of
I_{a}
and
I_{q}
for small disorder.

Here is our first main result.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
d\geq 4
,
\kappa>0
and compact set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}

there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(d,\kappa,\mathcal{K})>0
such that, for any RWRE satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
, if

\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P})<\varepsilon

(2.4)

then we have the equality
I_{q}(x)=I_{a}(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{K}
.

Remark 2.2
.

One can think of Theorem
2.1
above as saying that part of the region of equality in the boundary
\{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}:I_{q}(x)=I_{a}(x)\}
covers the whole of
\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}
in the limit as
\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P})\rightarrow 0
uniformly over all environmental laws
\mathbb{P}
with a uniform ellipticity constant bounded from below by some
\kappa>0
. However, we remark that, for a
fixed
environmental law
\mathbb{P}
,
I_{a}
and
I_{q}
can never be equal everywhere in
\partial\mathbb{D}
unless
\mathbb{P}
is degenerate (i.e.
\omega
is non-random under
\mathbb{P}
), see
[
Y11
, Proposition 4]
.
∎

Our next result states that there exists
at least one
open neighborhood on which there is equality, whenever the environment satisfies the weaker condition of small enough
imbalance
. More precisely, given
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
we define the
imbalance of
\mathbb{P}
on the face
\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P}):=\inf\bigg\{\varepsilon>0:\zeta_{s}(x)\in[1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon],\,\,\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s. for all }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\bigg\}

\displaystyle\mbox{with }\zeta_{s}(x):=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\omega(x,s_{i}e_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})},

or, equivalently,
\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})
is the
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{P})
-norm of the random variable
\zeta_{s}(x)-1
, for any given
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Here is the statement of our next main result.

Theorem 2.3
.

For any
d\geq 4
,
\kappa>0
and
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
, there exists
\varepsilon^{\star}=\varepsilon^{\star}(d,\kappa)>0
such that, for any RWRE satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
, if

\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})<\varepsilon^{\star},

(2.5)

then the following statements hold:

\bullet

I_{a}
and
I_{q}
have the same minimum over
\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
,

\min_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)}I_{q}(x)=\min_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)}I_{a}(x)=-\log\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i}).

(2.6)

\bullet

I_{a}
and
I_{q}
have the same unique minimizer,

{\arg\min}_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)}I_{q}(x)={\arg\min}_{x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)}I_{a}(x)\\
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})s_{i}e_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})}=:\overline{x}_{s}.

\bullet

There exists a neighborhood
\mathcal{O}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
of
\overline{x}_{s}
such that
I_{a}
and
I_{q}
agree on
\mathcal{O}
,

I_{q}(x)=I_{a}(x)\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{O}.

(2.7)

Moreover, the set
\mathcal{O}
can be taken to be uniform over all environmental laws
\mathbb{P}
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
in the following sense: there exists
r=r(d,\kappa)>0
such that, for any
\mathbb{P}
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
, if
\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})<\varepsilon^{\star}
(with
\varepsilon^{\star}
as above) then

I_{q}(x)=I_{a}(x)\qquad\text{ for all }x\in B_{r}(\overline{x}_{s})\cap\partial\mathbb{D}(s).

(The point being that
r
is independent of
\overline{x}_{s}
and uniform over
\mathbb{P}
.)

Remark 2.4
.

Note that in the current general setup, we do not require the RWRE to possess any limiting velocity, nor do we impose any ballisticity condition on the RWRE.
However, one can show that, whenever (
2.5
) holds, the unique minimizer
\overline{x}_{s}
in Theorem
2.3
is the velocity of
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
under the annealed conditional measure
P_{0}(\tfrac{X_{n}}{n}\in\cdot\,|\,\tfrac{X_{n}}{n}\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s))
and the quenched and annealed rate functions of the walk under this conditioning can be seen to equal
I_{q}-I_{q}(\overline{x}_{s})
and
I_{a}-I_{a}(\overline{x}_{s})
, respectively. Also, under this conditioning, the set-up bears some resemblance
to a random walk in a
space time i.i.d. environment
(
[
Y09
]
) which corresponds to the case when
\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})=0
. The latter choice is
included as a particular case of Theorem
2.3
(certainly Theorem
2.3
also covers the case when
\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})
is sufficiently small, not necessarily zero).
Also, from this viewpoint, our Theorem
2.1
then indicates that previously known equality results for dynamic random environments (available for neighborhoods of the velocity) can be extended to neighborhoods of
arbitrary points
in the domain, provided that the disorder of the environment is sufficiently low (a fact which can be proved rigorously by an adaptation of our method).
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Notice that if
\mathbb{P}
is the law of a
balanced random environment
, i.e.
\mathbb{P}
is such that
\mathbb{P}(\omega(x,e)=\omega(x,-e)\text{ for all }x,e)=1
, then
\mathrm{imb}_{s}(\mathbb{P})=0
for any
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
. In particular, such environments, as well as small perturbations of them, readily satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
2.3
. Observe also that balanced random environments never satisfy condition-
(\mathrm{T})
and, as such, had not been considered before in the study of equality of the rate functions for standard RWRE.
∎

2.2.
Formulas for
I_{q}
and
I_{a}
on the boundary.

Using the observation that
the rate functions on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
can be studied as
that of a random process in a space-time i.i.d. environment, Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
2.1

now provide a simple formula for the quenched rate function
I_{q}
.
Define the moment generating function
\lambda:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\lambda(\theta):=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(e)\mathrm{{\mathrm{e}}}^{\langle\theta,e\rangle}.

(2.8)

Here is our next main result.

Theorem 2.6
.

Fix
d\geq 4
and
\kappa>0
. Then:

(i)

Given any compact set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}

there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(d,\kappa,\mathcal{K})>0
such that, for any RWRE satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
, whenever (
2.4
) holds we have

I_{a}(x)=I_{q}(x)=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\langle\theta,x\rangle-\log\lambda(\theta)\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\log\frac{|x_{i}|}{\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})}\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{K}.

(2.9)

(ii)

Given any
s\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
there exists
\varepsilon^{\star}=\varepsilon^{\star}(d,\kappa)>0
such that, for any RWRE satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa
, whenever (
2.5
) holds there exists a non-empty open subset
\mathcal{O}\subset\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}
such that the representation in (
2.9
) holds for all
x\in\mathcal{O}
. This open subset is the same from Theorem
2.3
and hence can be taken to be uniform over all
\mathbb{P}
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant

.

Remark 2.7
.

As a matter of fact, the formula

I_{a}(x)=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\langle\theta,x\rangle-\log\lambda(\theta))=\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\log\frac{|x_{i}|}{\alpha(\frac{x_{i}}{|x_{i}|}e_{i})}

, used whenever

, not just for

or

(it is the equality with

Remark 2.8
.

The annealed rate function

2.3.
Monotonicity in the disorder and phase transition in the equality of rate functions.

We now turn to the statement that provides a phase transition in the behavior of the difference
I_{a}(x,\cdot)-I_{q}(x,\cdot)
as a function of the underlying disorder. We first need some further notation. Given a probability vector
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{V})
with strictly positive entries, let

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}:=\bigg\{(r(e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\in[-1,1]^{\mathbb{V}}:\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(e)r(e)=0\ {\rm and}\ \sup_{e\in\mathbb{V}}|r(e)|=1\bigg\}.

We denote probability measures on the space
\Gamma_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
by
\mathbb{Q}
.
We also write
\eta=(\eta(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\in\Gamma_{\alpha}
, with

\eta(x)=(\eta(x,e))_{e\in\mathbb{V}}
being a typical element of the space
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
. Since
\alpha
will remain fixed in the remainder of this subsection, we will omit the dependence on
\alpha
of
\mathbb{Q}
and
\eta
from the notation.

Now, given a probability vector
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{V})
with strictly positive entries and a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
\Gamma_{\alpha}
, let us consider the parametrized family of random environments
\{\omega_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in[0,1)}
given by

\omega_{\varepsilon}(x,e):=\alpha(e)(1+\varepsilon\eta(x,e)).

We will make the following assumptions on
\mathbb{Q}
:

Assumption
\mathrm{B}
.
The probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
satisfies the following three properties:

\bullet

The support of
\mathbb{Q}
is not a singleton.
2
2

2

As matter of fact, this condition is already implied by the third one since
\sup_{e\in\mathbb{V}}|\xi(x,e)|=1
by definition of
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha}
. Nevertheless, we still include it for clarity purposes.

\bullet

The family
(\eta(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
is i.i.d. under
\mathbb{Q}
.

\bullet

\mathbb{E}\eta(x,e)=0
for all
e\in\mathbb{V}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

The assumption that the support of
\mathbb{Q}
is not a singleton is made to ensure that there exists some true randomness in the environments
\omega_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
. On the other hand, the other two assumptions guarantee that for each
\varepsilon\in[0,1)
the law
\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}
of the environment
\omega_{\varepsilon}
satisfies Assumption
\mathrm{A}
with ellipticity constant
\kappa:=(1-\varepsilon)(\min_{e\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(e))>0
and
\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon})=\varepsilon
, with
\mathbb{E}(\omega_{\epsilon}(x,e))=\alpha(e)
for all
e\in\mathbb{V}
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In this context, we will denote by
I_{a}(\cdot,\varepsilon)
and
I_{q}(\cdot,\varepsilon)
to be the annealed and quenched rate functions, respectively. Recall that
I_{a}(x,\varepsilon)\leq I_{q}(x,\varepsilon)
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
by Jensen’s inequality. Our next main result establishes the monotonicity property for the difference of these two rate functions
I_{a}(x,\cdot)-I_{q}(x,\cdot)
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Fix
d\geq 4
. Then, for any probability vector
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{V})
with strictly positive entries and probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
\Gamma_{\alpha}
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{B}
, the following assertions hold:

•

For each
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,
the map

[0,1)\ni\varepsilon\mapsto I_{a}(x,\varepsilon)-I_{q}(x,\varepsilon)

is non-increasing and continuous.
In particular, there is
\varepsilon_{c}(x)\geq 0
such that
for

Remark 2.11

We emphasize that the family of random environments considered presently is quite general and contains several widely studied models for RWRE (see

(1)

(2)

Clearly the affirmation of (2) implies the same for (1). However, (2) does not make sense in general. Indeed,

and

need not be functions of the underlying disorder, only perhaps when dealing with

is that for us the “source of randomness” is fixed beforehand, so that when we make its influence smaller and smaller by taking the limit

then it is natural to expect equality to hold. However, we do not know whether there exists some

2.4.
Outline of the proofs

For the sake of conceptual transparency and also to provide guidance to the reader, we find it convenient to present a brief description of the method of proof developed in the present article. This will then also underline the technical novelty of our contribution.

To treat the boundary behavior of

and
I_{a}

[
BMRS19
]
to deal with the behavior in the interior of
\mathbb{D}
. The method in the interior used there relied on the construction of an auxiliary random walk in a deterministic environment possessing a regeneration structure and showing that its large deviation properties are intimately related to those of the true RWRE. Since the RWRE behaves differently on the boundary,

developed originally by Bolthausen

(3.20)

Taking
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{C_{0}}
then yields the result. We now owe the reader only the proof of the claim (
3.18
). But this is an immediate consequence of Lemma
3.3
below, which is a well-known application of the Fourier inversion formula.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a random walk in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with law
\mathrm{P}_{0}
starting at the origin and assume that
\chi_{\mu}
, the characteristic function of
Z_{1}
, takes only real non-negative values. Then for any
r>0
and
\delta=\sqrt{d}/r
,

\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{P}_{0}\big[Z_{n}\in B_{\delta}(0)\big]\leq\frac{C_{d}}{r^{d}}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{1-\chi_{\mu}(\xi)}.

Proof.

Since we are interested in the event
\{Z_{n}\leq\delta\}
we need to consider the function
\prod_{j=1}^{d}f(x_{j}/\delta)
where
f(x_{j})=\max(1-|x_{j}|,0)
. Then we have the Fourier transform of
the product

\widehat{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f(x_{j})}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\widehat{f}(\xi_{j})\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widehat{f}(\xi_{j})=\frac{2}{\xi_{j}^{2}}(1-\cos\xi_{j}).

If
\mu
denotes the law of
Z_{1}
and
\mu^{\star n}=\mu\star\dots\star\mu
its
n
-fold convolution, then for any
\delta>0
,
4
4

4

Recall that if
\mu
and
\nu
are two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with charactersitic functions
\chi_{\mu}
and
\chi_{\nu}
respectively, then
\int\chi_{\nu}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\chi_{\mu}(\xi)\nu(\mathrm{d}\xi)
.

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f\big(\frac{x_{j}}{\delta}\big)}\mu^{\star n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\delta^{d}\int\prod_{j=1}^{d}f(\delta\xi_{j})(\chi_{\mu}(\xi))^{n}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Therefore, for any
a\in(0,1)
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f\big(\frac{x_{j}}{\delta}\big)}\sum_{n\geq 0}a^{n}\mu^{\star n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\delta^{d}\int\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f(\delta\xi_{j})}{1-a\chi_{\mu}(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi,

(3.21)

which implies that, for
\delta=\sqrt{d}/r
and a suitable constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{P}_{0}\big[Z_{n}\in B_{\delta}(0)\big]=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mu^{\star n}(B_{\delta}(0))\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f\big(\frac{x_{j}}{\delta}\big)}\sum_{n\geq 0}\mu^{\star n}(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle=C\delta^{d}\sup_{a\in(0,1)}\int\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{d}f(\delta\xi_{j})}{1-a\chi_{\mu}(\xi)}\mathrm{d}\xi.

\displaystyle\leq C_{d}r^{-d}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\xi}{1-\chi_{\mu}(\xi)}.

∎

3.3.
Strict positivity of the limit
\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}

The next step in the proof is to show the martingale limit
\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}
is strictly positive.

Proposition 3.4
.

Given
d\geq 4
,
\kappa>0
and a compact set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
we have that, for any RWRE in dimension
d
with
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}
, if
\mathrm{dis}(\mathbb{P})<\varepsilon^{\prime}
(with
\varepsilon^{\prime}
as in Lemma
3.1
) then for each
\theta\in\Theta
,

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(x)>0\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\big\}=1.

Proof.

By (
3.4
) we have

z=(0,z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}\Longleftrightarrow\Delta_{1}(z)\in\mathbb{V}(s)\text{ and }(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\partial R_{n-1}(z_{1})

so that, by conditioning on the first step of the walk
X_{1}
, a straightforward computation yields that

\mathscr{Z}_{n,\theta}(\omega,0)=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}(s)}\omega(0,e)\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,\pi(e)\rangle-\log\psi(\theta)}\mathscr{Z}_{n-1,\theta}(\omega,e).

(3.22)

On the other hand, if for
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we define
T_{y}:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega
to be the translation

T_{y}(\omega)(x):=\omega(x+y),

(3.23)

then it follows that for any
e\in\mathbb{V}

\mathscr{Z}_{n-1,\theta}(\omega,e)=\mathscr{Z}_{n-1,\theta}(T_{e}(\omega),0),

so that (
3.22
) becomes

\mathscr{Z}_{n,\theta}(\omega,0)=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}(s)}\omega(0,e)\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,\pi(e)\rangle-\log\psi(\theta)}\mathscr{Z}_{n-1,\theta}(T_{e}(\omega),0).

(3.24)

By translation invariance of
\mathbb{P}
we know that
\mathscr{Z}_{n,\theta}(T_{e}(\omega),0)\rightarrow\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(T_{e}(\omega),0)
for
\mathbb{P}
-almost every
\omega
, so that we may take the
\mathbb{P}
-almost sure limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
on (
3.24
) to obtain

\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(\omega,0)=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{V}(s)}\omega(0,e)\mathrm{e}^{\langle\theta,\pi(e)\rangle-\log\psi(\theta)}\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(T_{e}(\omega),0).

(3.25)

Moreover, it follows from (
3.25
) (and again translation invariance of
\mathbb{P}
) that the event
\{\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(0)=0\}
is almost
T_{e}
-invariant for any
e\in\mathbb{V}(s)
so that, by ergodicity of
\mathbb{P}
, its probability must be either
0
or
1
. Since Lemma
3.1
dictates that the mean-one martingale
(\mathscr{Z}_{n,\theta}(0))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(0)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
, we have
\mathbb{E}(\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(0))=1
and thus it must be
\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(0)=0)=0
. By translation invariance of
\mathbb{P}
we conclude the validity of the last sentence for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
so that

\mathbb{P}\big\{\mathscr{Z}_{\infty,\theta}(x)=0\text{ for some }x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\big\}=0,

implying the desired result.
∎

3.4.1.
Existence of the LDP limits and properties of moment generating functions.

In order to conclude the proof of Theorem

Lemma 3.5
.

Under Assumption
\mathrm{A}
, for any

there exists an admissible sequence

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}(X_{n}=x_{n})=-I_{a}(x).

The proof of Lemma
3.5
is deferred until the end of Section

.

Next, recall from (
3.7
) that
S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the projected walk of the RWRE

. Now, for each
n\geq 1
, let us set

to be the empirical mean and, for each

and

, define the quenched log-moment generating function of

A^{\omega}_{n}(\theta):=\log E_{0,\omega}\Big(e^{\langle\theta,\overline{S}_{n}\rangle}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{B}_{n}}\Big),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

where the event

is defined in (

quenched log-moment generating function is

\Lambda^{\omega}(\theta)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}A^{\omega}_{n}(n\theta).

(3.26)

We recall some qualitative properties of
\Lambda^{\omega}
stated in the following result.

Lemma 3.6
.

For each
\mathbb{P}
satisfying Assumption
\mathrm{A}
there exists a full
\mathbb{P}
-probability event
\overline{\Omega}=\overline{\Omega}(\mathbb{P})
such that, for any
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}
, the following holds:

i.

The limit in (
3.26
) exists and is finite for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, i.e. for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

\Lambda^{\omega}(\theta)=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}A^{\omega}_{n}(n\theta)\in(-\infty,+\infty).

ii.

\Lambda^{\omega}
is convex and continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
.

iii.

If
y=\nabla\Lambda^{\omega}(\eta)
for some
\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}

\langle\eta,y\rangle-\Lambda^{\omega}(\eta)=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}[\langle\theta,y\rangle-\Lambda^{\omega}(\theta)]=:\overline{\Lambda}^{\omega}(y).

Moreover,

is an exposed point of
\overline{\Lambda}^{\omega}
and
\eta
is its exposing hyperplane, i.e. for all

iv.

\overline{\Lambda}^{\omega}
is lower semicontinuous.

v.

For any closed set

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}(\{\overline{S}_{n}\in F\}\cap\mathbb{B}_{n})\leq-\inf_{x\in F}\overline{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x).

vi.

For any open set

\liminf_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}(\{\overline{S}_{n}\in G\}\cap\mathbb{B}_{n})\geq-\inf_{x\in G\cap\mathcal{F}^{\omega}}\overline{\Lambda}^{\omega}(x),

where
\mathcal{F}^{\omega}
denotes the set of exposed points of
\Lambda^{\omega}
.

Proof.

All the assertions are found in the standard literature (see
[
DZ98
, Section 2.3]
) which follows from the existence of a full
\mathbb{P}
-probability event
\overline{\Omega}
such that, for any
\omega\in\overline{\Omega}
and all

(3.27)

Alternatively, once we have (

), one can introduce the conditional probabilities

\mu_{n}:=P_{0,\omega}(\overline{S}_{n}\in\cdot\,|\,\mathbb{B}_{n})

and deduce the remaining parts of the lemma by applying the standard Gärtner-Ellis theorem for the sequence

.
The existence of the limit (

[

for all

\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}
, we have

\pi(B_{r_{x}}(x)\cap(\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}))\in G_{R_{x}}=\{\nabla\log\psi(\theta):|\theta|<R_{x}\},

(3.34)

where as usual we write
B_{r_{x}}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|y-x|<r_{x}\}
. Indeed, if this is the case then, given any compact set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}
, there exists some finite
n_{\mathcal{K}}=n_{\mathcal{K}}(d,\kappa,\mathcal{K})\geq 1
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{n_{\mathcal{K}}}\in\mathcal{K}
such that

\mathcal{K}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{n_{\mathcal{K}}}\big(B_{r_{x_{j}}}(x_{j})\cap(\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2})\big),

so that by (
3.34
), if we set
R_{\mathcal{K}}:=\max_{j=1,\dots,n_{\mathcal{K}}}r_{x_{j}}<\infty
then,for any
\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\kappa}
, we obtain that

\pi(\mathcal{K})\subseteq G_{R_{\mathcal{K}}}.

Hence, we only need to show (
3.34
).

To this end, notice that any
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2}
can be written as

x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}s_{i}e_{i}

where
\delta_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
and
\sum_{i=1}\delta_{i}=1
. Since
\pi
is affine, it follows that

\pi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{i}\pi(s_{i}e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}(\delta_{i}-\delta_{d})e_{i}.

On the other hand, a simple computation shows that for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,

\nabla\log\psi(\theta)=\frac{1}{\psi(\theta)}\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}[\alpha(s_{i}e_{i}){\mathrm{e}}^{\theta_{i}}-\alpha(s_{d}e_{d}){\mathrm{e}}^{-(\theta_{1}+\dots+\theta_{d-1})}]e_{i}.

Therefore, in order to check that
\pi(x)\in G_{R}
for some
R>0
, we only need to show that there exists some
\theta(x)=(\theta_{1}(x),\dots,\theta_{d-1}(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
such that

\delta_{i}-\delta_{d}=\frac{1}{\psi(\theta)}\left[\alpha(s_{i}e_{i}){\mathrm{e}}^{\theta_{i}(x)}-\alpha(s_{d}e_{d}){\mathrm{e}}^{-(\theta_{1}(x)+\dots+\theta_{d-1}(x))}\right]

(3.35)

for all
i=1,\dots,d-1
. But it is straightforward to check that, for
\theta(x)
given by

\theta_{i}(x):=\log\left(\frac{\delta_{i}C}{\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})}\right)\hskip 28.45274pt\text{ with }\hskip 28.45274ptC:=\sqrt[d]{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})}{\delta_{i}}}

for each
i=1,\dots,d-1
, (
3.35
) is satisfied and so
\pi(x)=\nabla\log\psi(\theta(x))
. Finally, since the mapping

(\alpha,x)\mapsto\theta(x)

is continuous on
\mathcal{M}^{(\kappa)}_{1}(\mathbb{V})\times(\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2})
, (
3.34
) follows upon taking
r_{x}:=\tfrac{1}{2}d(x,\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2})>0
and
R_{x}:=1+\sup\{|\theta(y)|:y\in B_{r_{x}}(x)\cap(\partial\mathbb{D}(s)\setminus\partial\mathbb{D}_{d-2})\}<\infty
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

3.4.3.
Proof of Lemma
3.5
:

We consider the quenched and annealed limits separately.

Case 1: the quenched limit
. This is consequence of several results found in
[
RS14
]
. Indeed, in
[
RS14
, Theorem 2.2]
it is proved that
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely for all
x\in\mathbb{D}
, the following limit exists

\widehat{I}_{q}(x):=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0,\omega}(X_{n}=x_{n})\in[0,\kappa]

for a suitable admissible sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\tfrac{x_{n}}{n}\to x
. Moreover, by
[
RS14
, Theorem 2.4]
this limit
\widehat{I}_{q}(x)
is deterministic and, by
[
RS14
, Theorem 3.2-(b)]
, the map
x\to\widehat{I}_{q}(x)
is continuous on
\mathbb{D}
. Finally,
[
RS14
, Theorem 4.3]
shows that
I_{q}\equiv\widehat{I}_{q}
on
\mathbb{D}^{\circ}
. The continuity of both
I_{q}
and
\widehat{I}_{q}
now allow us to extend the equality to the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}
, thus proving the quenched case.

Case 2: the annealed limit
. First, given
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
, let us write it as
x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}|x_{i}|e_{i}
. Now, consider any admissible sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that:

\bullet

\tfrac{x_{n}}{n}\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
for each
n
, i.e.
x_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}s_{i}n_{i}e_{i}
for some
n_{i}\geq 0
with
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}=n
.

\bullet

If
x_{i}=0
then
n_{i}=0
.

\bullet

\tfrac{x_{n}}{n}\to x
as
n\to\infty
.

It is straightforward to check that such a sequence always exists, see
[
RS14
]
for details.

Observe that, for any such sequence, by (
3.14
) the quantity
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\alpha(\Delta_{j}(z))
is independent of the path
z
of length
n
going from
0
to
x_{n}
, so that

P_{0}(X_{n}=x_{n})=\#\{z\in\mathcal{R}_{n}:z_{n}=x_{n}\}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})^{n_{i}}=\frac{n!}{n_{1}!\cdots n_{d}!}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})^{n_{i}}

Taking logarithm and dividing by
n
, we get

\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}(X_{n}=x_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\left[\log n!-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log n_{i}!+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\log\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})\right]

Now, since
\tfrac{x_{n}}{n}\to x
, we obtain that
\tfrac{n_{i}}{n}\to|x_{i}|
for all
i
and thus that as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\log\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})\to\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\log\alpha(s_{i}e_{i}).

On the other hand, since
\tfrac{n_{i}}{n}\to|x_{i}|
, by Stirling’s approximation we have
\log n!=n\log n-n+o(n)
and
\log n_{i}!=n_{i}\log n_{i}-n_{i}+o(n)
(if
x_{i}=0
for some
i
, the equality still holds since
n_{i}=0
), so that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[\log(n!)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log(n_{i}!)\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\left[n\log n-n-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\log n_{i}-n_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{n_{i}}{n}\log\frac{n_{i}}{n}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\log|x_{i}|.

Therefore, we conclude that

-\widehat{I}_{a}(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}P_{0}(X_{n}=x_{n})=-\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|\log\frac{|x_{i}|}{\alpha(s_{i}e_{i})}.

To conclude the proof, we must now check that
\widehat{I}_{a}(x)=I_{a}(x)
. To this end, define

\widetilde{I}_{a}(x):=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\langle\theta,x\rangle-\log\lambda(\theta))

(3.36)

for
\lambda
as in (
2.8
). It is straightforward to check that
\widetilde{I}_{a}
is the annealed rate function corresponding to a random walk
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in a space-time random environment
\bar{\omega}=(\bar{\omega}(n,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where the
\bar{\omega}(n,\cdot)
are i.i.d. having common law
\mathbb{P}
. Furthermore, by standard considerations of Fenchel-Legendre transforms (see Lemma
3.6
, for instance), it is straightforward to check that for all
x\in\partial\mathbb{D}(s)
the supremum in (
3.36
) coincides with the expression derived for
\widehat{I}_{a}(x)
, so that
\widehat{I}_{a}(x)=\widetilde{I}_{a}(x)
. Thus, in order to conclude the proof, it will suffice to show that

\widetilde{I}_{a}(x)\leq I_{a}(x)\leq\widehat{I}_{a}(x).

(3.37)

To check the right inequality in (
3.37
) we observe that, by the annealed LDP for the random walk and the fact that
\tfrac{x_{n}}{x}\to x
, for any
\delta>0
we have

-\widehat{I}_{a}(x)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log P_{0}(\tfrac{1}{n}X_{n}\in\overline{B_{\delta}(x)})\leq-\inf_{y\in\overline{B_{\delta}(x)}}I_{a}(y),

(3.38)

where
B_{\delta}(x):=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|y-x|<\delta\}
. By the lower semicontinuity of
I_{a}
, taking
\delta\to 0
in (
3.38
) then yields the right inequality in (
3.37
).

On the other hand, if
Q_{0}
denotes the law of the random walk
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in a space-time environment introduced previously starting from
0
, then for any
\delta>0
we have

P_{0}(\tfrac{1}{n}X_{n}\in B_{\delta}(x))\leq\kappa^{-n\delta}Q_{0}(\tfrac{1}{n}Y_{n}\in B_{\delta}(x)).

(3.39)

Indeed, notice that

\displaystyle P_{0}(\tfrac{1}{n}X_{n}\in B_{\delta}(x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{n}\,:\,\frac{z_{n}}{n}\in B_{\delta}(x)}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\omega(z_{j-1},\Delta_{j}(z))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{-n\delta}\sum_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{n}\,:\,\frac{z_{n}}{n}\in B_{\delta}(x)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\alpha(\Delta_{j}(z))=\kappa^{-n\delta}Q_{0}(\tfrac{1}{n}Y_{n}\in B_{\delta}(x)),

where the middle equality follows from the fact that the factors in the product
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\omega(z_{j-1},\Delta_{j}(z))
are all independent except for at most
n\delta
of them, but we can majorize these by independent versions at the expense of an additional
\kappa^{-1}
factor. It follows from (
3.39
) that

\inf_{y\in B_{\delta}(x)}I_{a}(y)\geq\inf_{y\in\overline{B_{\delta}(x)}}\widetilde{I}_{a}(y)-\delta\log k^{-1}.

By the lower semicontinuity of both
I_{a}
and
\widetilde{I}_{a}
, letting
\delta\to 0
in the last display above reveals that
\widetilde{I}_{a}(x)\leq I_{a}(x)
and thus (
3.37
) is proved.
∎
