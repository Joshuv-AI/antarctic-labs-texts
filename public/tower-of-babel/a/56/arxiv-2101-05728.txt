New bounds for k k -means and information k k -means
Gautier Appert, Olivier Catoni

Abstract
In this paper, we derive a new dimension-free non-asymptotic upper bound for the quadratic k k -means excess risk related to the quantization of an i.i.d sample in a separable Hilbert space. We improve the bound of order 𝓞 ⁡ ( k / n ) \bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(k/\sqrt{n}\bigr) of Biau, Devroye and Lugosi, recovering the rate k / n \sqrt{k/n} that has already been proved by Fefferman, Mitter, and Narayanan and by Klochkov, Kroshnin and Zhivotovskiy but with worse log factors and constants. More precisely, we bound the mean excess risk of an empirical minimizer by the explicit upper bound 16 ​ B 2 ​ log ⁡ ( n / k ) ​ k ​ log ⁡ ( k ) / n 16B^{2}\log(n/k)\sqrt{k\log(k)/n} , in the bounded case when ℙ ⁡ ( ∥ X ∥ ≤ B ) = 1 \mathds{P}(\lVert X\rVert\leq B)=1 . This is essentially optimal up to logarithmic factors since a lower bound of order 𝓞 ⁡ ( k 1 − 4 / d / n ) \bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{k^{1-4/d}/n}\bigr) is known in dimension d d . Our technique of proof is based on the linearization of the k k -means criterion through a kernel trick and on PAC-Bayesian inequalities. To get a 1 / n 1/\sqrt{n} speed, we introduce a new PAC-Bayesian chaining method replacing the concept of δ \delta -net with the perturbation of the parameter by an infinite dimensional Gaussian process. In the meantime, we embed the usual k k -means criterion into a broader family built upon the Kullback divergence and its underlying properties. This results in a new algorithm that we named information k k -means , well suited to the clustering of bags of words. Based on considerations from information theory, we also introduce a new bounded k k -means criterion that uses a scale parameter but satisfies a generalization bound that does not require any boundedness or even integrability conditions on the sample. We describe the counterpart of Lloyd’s algorithm and prove generalization bounds for these new k k -means criteria.

General notation

We will use the following notation throughout this document.

On some measurable probability space
\Omega
, we will consider various
random variables
X:\Omega\rightarrow\bm{\mathcal{X}}
,

Y:\Omega\rightarrow\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
, etc. that are nothing but measurable functions.
We will also consider several probability measures on
\Omega
, and typically
two measures
\mathds{P}
and
Q\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)
, where
\mathds{P}

describes the usually unknown data distribution and
Q
describes
an estimation of
\mathds{P}
. Then we will use the short notation
\mathds{P}_{X}

for the push forward measure
\mathds{P}\circ X^{-1}
, that is the
law of
X
. Similarly we will
let
Q_{X}=Q\circ X^{-1}
. In the same way
\mathds{P}_{X,Y}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times\bm{\mathcal{Y}})
will
be the joint distribution of the couple
(X,Y)
under
\mathds{P}

and
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
the corresponding regular conditional
probability measure of
Y
knowing
X
when it exists.
We will always work under sufficient hypotheses to ensure that
the decomposition

\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}=\mathds{P}_{X}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}

(1)

is valid, meaning that for any bounded measurable function

f(X,Y)

\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}=\int\biggl(\int f\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}.

Moreover, we will use the short notation

\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}=\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}(f),

so that the previous formula becomes

\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}(f)=\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}(f)\bigr].

We will often use the Kullback Leibler divergence

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q,\mathds{P}\bigr)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle Q\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}}\biggr)\biggr]&\text{ when }Q\ll\mathds{P},\\
+\infty&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We will always be in this article in a situation where the decomposition

Lemma 1
.

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,Y}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X},\mathds{P}_{X}\bigr)+Q_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y\,|\,X},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y}\bigr)+Q_{Y}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X\,|\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)\bigr]

is valid.

It follows from the decomposition (
1
).
A precise statement and a rigorous proof dealing with measurability issues
can be found in

[
9
, Appendix section 1.7 page 50]
.

1
Introduction

This paper is about the most widely used
loss function for vector quantization,
the
k
-means criterion.
We will be interested in the statistical setting
where the problem is to minimize the criterion
for a random vector whose distribution is unknown
but can be estimated through an i.i.d. random
sample. Our main contribution will be
to prove a new dimension free non-asymptotic
generalization bound with a better
k/n

dependence, where
k
is the number of centers
used for vector quantization and
n
is
the size of the statistical sample.
We will also give an interpretation
of the
k
-means criterion in terms of
the Kullback divergence and use it
to embed it in
a broader family of criteria with interesting
properties. This will provide a specific
algorithm for the quantization of conditional
probability distributions ranging in an
exponential family that can be used
in particular to analyse bag of words models.
This generalization will also provide a
new robust criterion for the quantization
of unbounded random vectors.

Our general setting is the following. Given a random
variable
X\in H
ranging in a separable Hilbert
space
H
, we are interested in minimizing the risk
function

\bm{\mathcal{R}}\bigl(c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\bigr)=\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}\Bigr),\quad(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})\in H^{k}.

We will assume that the statistician does not know the
distribution
\mathds{P}_{X}
, but has access instead to
a sample
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
made of
n
independent
copies of
X
. If
\displaystyle\overline{\mathds{P}}_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}

is the empirical measure of the sample,
the empirical risk, or empirical
k
-means criterion, is defined as

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(c)=\overline{\mathds{P}}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert X_{i}-c_{j}\rVert^{2}\Bigr),\quad c\in H^{k}.

We will first consider the bounded case.
Given a ball
\bm{\mathcal{B}}=\bigl\{x\in H\,:\,\lVert x\rVert\leq B\bigr\}
,
we will assume that
\mathds{P}\bigl(X\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}\bigr)=1
. We will
study the upper deviations of the random variable

\sup_{c\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)-\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(c)\Bigr].

This will provide an observable upper bound for the risk that is
uniform with respect to the choice of centers
c\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
.
Being uniform with respect to
c
covers
the case where the centers have been computed from
the observed sample through some algorithm.
In order to study the excess risk of estimators,
we will also study the upper deviations of

\sup_{c\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)-\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c_{*})-\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(c)+\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(c_{*})\Bigr].

This random variable compares uniformly with respect
to
c
the excess risk of
c
with respect to a
non random reference
c_{*}
and the corresponding
empirical excess risk.
In particular, the non random reference
c_{*}

can be chosen to be a minimizer, or more generally
an
\varepsilon
-minimizer, of the risk
\bm{\mathcal{R}}
.

Indeed, we will consider
\widehat{c}\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}
,
depending on the sample, such that

\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\widehat{c}\,)\leq\inf_{c\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}}\overline{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(c)+\varepsilon,

and provide a bound for the excess risk
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(\widehat{c}\,)-\inf_{c\in\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{k}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)
.
To complement deviation bounds, we will also provide
corresponding bounds in expectation.

Regarding the sample size
n
,
we obtain a speed of order
\bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(1/\sqrt{n}\bigr)
as in

[
6
]
,
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
.
However, we get a better dependence in
k
,
with a rate of convergence of
\bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{k/n}\bigr)
up to log factors.
This is essentially optimal up to log
factors, at least in infinite dimension,
since minimax lower bounds for the
excess
k
-means risk are of order
\bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{k^{1-4/d}/n}\bigr)
in dimension
d
, see
[
4
]
and
[
1
]
.
We should mention that
the speed
\sqrt{k/n}
has already been established
in
[
15
]
(see Lemma 6),

[
18
]
and
[
21
]
, but with worse
log factors and less explicit constants.

These bounds will be obtained using PAC-Bayesian inequalities
combined with a kernel trick
and a new kind of PAC-Bayesian chaining method that we developed.
In particular, borrowing ideas
from the construction of the isonormal Gaussian process

[
26
, section 3.5]
,
we will use the distribution of
an infinite sequence of shifted Gaussian random variables
both for the prior and the posterior parameter distribution.
We will also use some arguments from the proofs of

[
11
]
and
[
12
]
,
concerning the estimation of the mean of a random vector.
Furthermore,
we take inspiration from the classical chaining procedure for bounding the expected suprema of
sub-Gaussian processes (see section 13.1 in
[
7
]
). We create a PAC-Bayesian version of chaining in which
the concept of
\delta
-net and
\delta
-covering is replaced by the use of a sequence of
Gaussian perturbations parametrized by a variance ranging on a
logarithmic grid.
We combine this PAC-Bayesian chaining with the use of the influence function
\psi
described in

[
10
]
to decompose the excess risk into
a sub-Gaussian part and an other part representing extreme values.
It is worth mentioning that
we will work with weak hypotheses and will in particular
not consider the kind of margin assumptions that are
necessary to get bounds decreasing faster than
\sqrt{1/n}

for a given value of
k
, as in
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
,
[
24
]
, and
[
25
]
.

2
Extensions of the
k
-means criterion

Before proving generalization bounds, let us
embed the
k
-means criterion in a broader family of
risk functions.

We will do this while considering the square of the Euclidean
distance, or more generally in possibly infinite dimension
the square of the Hilbert norm,
as the Kullback divergence between two Gaussian
measures. In this interpretation, vector quantization
according to the
k
-means criterion will appear
as a special case of conditional probability measure
quantization according to an entropy criterion.

To describe things at a more technical level, we need
first to define the classification function underlying
vector quantization.

To a set of centers
c\in H^{k}
indeed corresponds a classification
function
\ell:H\rightarrow\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
into Voronoï cells
defined as

\ell(x)=\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert x-c_{j}\rVert.

This definition may not be unique if the minimum is reached more
than once, in which case, we make an arbitrary choice,
as for instance

\ell(x)=\min\Bigl\{\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert x-c_{j}\rVert\Bigr\}.

The corresponding vector quantization function is

f(x)=c_{\ell(x)},\quad x\in H.

To study the quality of the quantization of a random
variable
X\in H
in terms of conditional probability distributions,
we introduce another random
variable
Y\in\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}}
and consider on
some probability space
\Omega
a realization
(X,Y):\Omega\rightarrow H\times\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}}
of the couple of random variables
(X,Y)
.
We can for instance take
\Omega=H\times\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}}

and let
(X,Y)
be the identity. Introduce now a probability measure

\mathds{P}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)
such that
\mathds{P}_{X}
is the
law of
X
and such that
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
is the law
of the independent sequence
\langle X,e_{i}\rangle+\sigma\varepsilon_{i}
,
i\in\mathds{N}
,
where
\bigl(\varepsilon_{i},i\in\mathds{N}\bigr)

is an i.i.d. sequence of standard normal
random variables, where
\bigl(e_{i},i\in\mathds{N}\bigr)

is a basis of
H
and where
\sigma>0
is a standard deviation
parameter. In other words, let

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathds{N}}\bm{\mathcal{N}}\bigl(\langle X,e_{i}\rangle,\sigma^{2}\bigr).

Let us also introduce a probability measure
Q^{(c)}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)

such that
Q^{(c)}_{X}=\mathds{P}_{X}
and

Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X}=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathds{N}}\bm{\mathcal{N}}\bigl(\langle c_{\ell(X)},e_{i}\rangle,\sigma^{2}\bigr),

where
\ell
is defined from
c
as explained above.
In other words,
Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X}

is the distribution of the random sequence

\langle c_{\ell(X)},e_{i}\rangle+\sigma\varepsilon_{i},i\in\mathds{N}
.
We see that
Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X}
is a quantization of
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}

that takes
k
values, in the same way as
f(X)=c_{\ell(X)}

is a quantization of
X
itself.

Proposition 2
.

The
k
-means criterion

\bm{\mathcal{R}}
can be expressed as

\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=2\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{X,\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr).

(2)

The first equality comes from the fact that

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)=\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\langle c_{\ell(X)},e_{i}\rangle,\sigma^{2}),\bm{\mathcal{N}}(\langle X,e_{i}\rangle,\sigma^{2})\bigr)\\
=\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigl(\langle c_{\ell(X)},e_{i}\rangle-\langle X,e_{i}\rangle\bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\lVert X-c_{\ell(X)}\rVert^{2}=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}.

The second equality is a consequence of the decomposition stated
in Lemma
1
, that says that

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X,\,Y}^{(c)},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr)=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X}\bigr)+Q^{(c)}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{Y\,|\,X},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]

and of the fact that
Q^{(c)}_{X}=\mathds{P}_{X}
by definition of
Q^{(c)}
.
∎

The two equalities of Proposition
2

will be interesting to extend the
k
-means
criterion. Let us draw the consequences of the first one first.
Introduce

\mu^{(c)}_{j}=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathds{N}}\bm{\mathcal{N}}\bigl(\langle c_{j},e_{i}\rangle,\sigma^{2}\bigr)\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}}),\qquad j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket.

The first part of equation (
2
) can be written as

\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)}^{(c)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=2\sigma^{2}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr],

since

2\sigma^{2}\,\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)=\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}.

Note that we could also have used

2\sigma^{2}\,\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X},\mu^{(c)}_{j}\bigr)=\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}
, since the Kullback divergence between
Gaussian measures is symmetric. This would have lead to another
interpretation of the
k
-means criterion in a space of conditional
probability measures. The choice we made is quite unusual, but is
justified by the following property.

Proposition 3
.

The minimization of
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)
seen as a function of
\mu^{(c)}

can be extended to the larger set
\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}\bigl(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}}\bigr)^{k}
.
In other words, if we put

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu)=2\sigma^{2}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr],\qquad\mu\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}})^{k},

we can see that
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu^{(c)})

and the minimum of
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}
coincides with
the minimum of
\bm{\mathcal{R}}
in the sense that

\inf_{c\in H^{k}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=\inf_{c\in H^{k}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu^{(c)})=\inf_{\mu\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}})^{k}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu).

Given
\mu\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}})^{k}
,
we have to find
c\in H^{k}
such that

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu)\geq\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)
.
This will prove that

\inf_{c\in H^{k}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=\inf_{c\in H^{k}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu^{(c)})\leq\inf_{\mu\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\mathds{R}^{\mathds{N}})^{k}}\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu),

and since the reverse inequality is obvious
from the fact that we take the infimum on
a larger set, this will prove the
proposition.

Consider then

\ell(X)=\min\Bigl\{\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\Bigr\}

and

c_{j}=\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}(X),\qquad j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket.

It is easy to check that the centers
c_{j}
are such that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu^{(c)}_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\mu^{(0)}_{j}}=Z_{j}^{-1}\exp\biggl\{\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0)}}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\},

where
Z_{j}
is a normalizing constant and
\mu^{(0)}
is
\mu^{(c)}

with
c=0\in H^{k}
, the centered Gaussian measure. Indeed, Gaussian measures
with the same covariance form an exponential family indexed by
their means. Taking the arithmetic mean of the parameter in
an exponential family results in taking the geometric mean
of the probability measures.
Thus,
\mu_{j}^{(c)}
is the geometric mean of

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
with weights
\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}
.
As a consequence, for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,

\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\biggl\{\mu_{j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\}\\
=\mu_{j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0)}}\biggr)\biggr]-\mu_{j}\biggl\{\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mu^{(0)}_{j}}\biggr)\biggr]\biggr\}\\
=\mu_{j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0)}}\biggl)\biggr]-\mu_{j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(c)}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0)}}\biggr)\biggr]-\log(Z_{j})=\mu_{j}\biggl[\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}}{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(c)}}\biggr)\biggr]-\log(Z_{j}).

Moreover, considering the case when
\mu=\mu^{(c)}
,
we see that

\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)}^{(c)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=-\log(Z_{j}).

Therefore

\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=\mathds{P}_{\ell(X)}\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell(X)},\mu^{(c)}_{\ell(X)}\bigr)\bigr]+\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{\ell(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\\
\geq\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{\ell(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\geq\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{j},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr],

showing that
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu)\geq\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}\bigr)
.
∎

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
.
This is an invitation to study more generally

\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}(\mu)
.

Let us now come back to the second equality of equation (
2
) on page
2
.
It relates the minimization of the
k
-means criterion
with the estimation of the joint probability measure
\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}
.
Instead of considering the single distribution
Q^{(c)}_{X,\,Y}

we can optimize the value of
Q^{(c)}_{X}
, considering the model

\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(c)=\bigl\{Q\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)\,:\,Q_{Y\,|\,X}=\mu^{(c)}_{\ell(X)}\bigr\}\ni Q^{(c)}_{X,\,Y}.

In order to get a better approximation of
\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}
,
it is natural to consider instead of
\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)

the criterion

\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{2}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\inf_{Q\in\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(c)}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X,\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr)\leq\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{(c)}_{X,\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr).

Proposition 4
.

Consider the classification function

\ell_{c}(x)=\min\Bigl\{\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,\,k\rrbracket}\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert\Bigr\}.

(3)

The above criterion is equal to

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{2}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\inf_{Q\in\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(c)}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X,\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-2\sigma^{2}\log\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl\{\exp\bigl[-\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu_{\ell_{c}(X)}^{(c)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=-2\sigma^{2}\log\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\bigl\lVert X-c_{\ell_{c}(X)}\bigr\rVert^{2}\Bigr]\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=-2\sigma^{2}\log\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bigl\lVert X-c_{j}\bigr\rVert^{2}\Bigr]\Bigr\}.

For any
Q\in\bm{\mathcal{Q}}(c)
, use the decomposition stated
in Lemma
1
, to obtain that

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X,\,Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr)=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X},\mathds{P}_{X}\bigr)+Q_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y\,|\,X},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{X},\mathds{P}_{X}\bigr)+Q_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mu^{(c)}_{\ell_{c}(X)},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr].

(4)

Minimizing this last expression with respect to
Q_{X}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(H)

according to forthcoming Lemma

gives the first
equality of the proposition, the others being obvious.
∎

The criterion
\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{2}(c)
is not a risk function in the sense
that it is not the expectation of a loss function, but it
is closely related to one. Indeed we can introduce

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\Bigl[1-\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{2}(c)\Bigr)\Bigr]\leq\bm{\mathcal{C}}_{2}(c)\leq\bm{\mathcal{R}}(c)

(5)

that is equal to

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(c)=2\sigma^{2}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl[1-\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\lVert X-c_{j}\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\Bigr]

according to the previous proposition.
We see that the risk
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
is a natural
modification of the risk
\bm{\mathcal{R}}
when we relate

\bm{\mathcal{R}}
to the estimation of
\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}
.
This new risk
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
is smaller, meaning that
it should be easier to minimize and indeed, as
it is the expectation of a bounded loss function,
we will get a generalization bound under weaker
hypotheses than what we will ask for
\bm{\mathcal{R}}
.
More specifically, we will assume no more
that the sample is bounded.

4
Study of the information
k
-means criterion

In this section, we will study the information
k
-means criterion

\widetilde{R}(\mu)
of Proposition
3
for more general
models of regular conditional probability measures
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
.

Consider a couple of random variables
(X,Y)\in\bm{\mathcal{X}}\times\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
,
where
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
are complete separable metric spaces,
so that we can define regular conditional probability measures.
Suppose there exists a
reference measure
\nu\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}\bigl(\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\bigr)

such that
\mathds{P}\Bigl(\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\ll\nu\Bigr)=1
.
Define
\displaystyle p_{X}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\nu}
.
We are interested in the case where
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}

is known therefore providing
a bag of words model. This means that each random sample
X
is described
by a random probability measure
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
. In the original
bag of words model,
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}
is a set of words, and
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}

is the distribution of words in a text
X
drawn at random from some
corpus of texts. Here we include the case where
\bm{\mathcal{X}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{Y}}

can be more general measurable spaces.

We introduce the following generalization
of the criterion
\widetilde{\bm{\mathcal{R}}}
of Proposition
3
,
that we will name the
information
k
-means criterion:

\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X})\Bigr),

where
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket=\{1,\dots,k\}
,
\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)=\Bigl\{q\in\mathds{L}^{1}(\nu)\,:\,q\geq 0,\int\!q\,\mathrm{d}\nu=1\Bigr\}

and

\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X})=\begin{cases}\int q_{j}\log\bigl(q_{j}/p_{X}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu,&\int q_{j}\mathds{1}\bigl(p_{X}=0\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0,\\
+\infty,&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

is the Kullback divergence between densities.
The purpose of this section is to discuss the general properties of the information
k
-means problem and to build a mathematical framework and algorithms to perform the minimization.
As we have seen in the previous section, we chose to study this algorithm
rather than the better known
k
-means divergence algorithm

\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}_{+,1}^{1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(p_{X},q_{j}\bigr)\Bigr)

because of Proposition
3
, showing that our proposal contains
the classical Euclidean
k
-means as a special case. More generally, using
the divergence in the way we do when the conditional probability measures

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
belong to an exponential family ensures that
the optimal centers for a given classification function
\ell

belong to that same exponential family.

We should point out that clustering histograms or more generally probability distributions based on the Kullback divergence or other information criteria is not a new subject. It has been extensively used in text categorization and image indexing, especially in word clustering to extract features or reduce the original space dimension, see
[
31
]
,
[
33
]
,
[
32
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
35
]
,
and
[
20
]
. The clustering is essentially performed using the aforementioned
k
-means divergence algorithm. However, in the information
k
-means framework we follow a different route since the grouping step is done by minimizing the Kullback divergence with respect to its first argument instead of its second one. This leads to very different centroids, computed as geometric means of distributions instead of arithmetic means, see
[
5
]
and
[
34
]
.
This follows from the fact that the Kullback divergence is asymmetric.
Nevertheless, symmetric extensions of the Kullback divergence built
upon averaged symmetrizations have been studied.
Especially, centroids and
k
-means type
algorithms derived from symmetrized divergence functions
are analyzed in
[
34
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
30
]
and
[
28
]
.

Besides,
following the set-up provided by the typical
k
-means divergence,
[
3
]
presents a general

k
-means framework based on the Bregman divergence. The authors show that such criteria can be minimized iteratively using a
k
-means centroid-based algorithm. The Bregman distance encompasses many traditional similarity
measures such as the Euclidean distance, the Kullback divergence,
the logistic loss and many others.
However, in the Kullback case, the minimization is
performed with respect to the second
argument, and not the first as in our proposal.
Nevertheless, the study of a symmetrized version of the Bregman
divergence, and especially the resulting centroids coming from it,
is undertaken in
[
29
]
.

Our contribution in this paper is to provide
a mathematical framework
for the information
k
-means criterion.
In particular, we will prove generalization bounds
and deal with the infinite dimension case.

Let us state some version of the Bayes rule that will
be useful in the following discussion.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathds{P}_{X,Y}
be a joint distribution
defined on the product of two Polish spaces.
The following statements are equivalent:

1.

There exists a measure
\mu
such that

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\ll\mu
,
\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely;

2.

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\ll\mathds{P}_{Y}
,
\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely;

3.

\mathds{P}_{X,Y}\ll\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathds{P}_{Y}
;

4.

\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\ll\mathds{P}_{X}
,
\mathds{P}_{Y}
almost surely.

Moreover, they imply the following identities between
Radon–Nikodym derivatives:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\big(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathds{P}_{Y}\big)}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}}.

To prove that
1.
implies
2.
, it is sufficient to show that

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\Bigr)=0
,

\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely.
But when
1.
is true

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\Biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\Biggr)=\int\mathds{1}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\biggr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu.

Thus by the Tonelli-Fubini theorem

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{X}\Biggl(\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\Biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\Biggr)\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{X}\Biggl(\int\mathds{1}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\biggr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\Biggr)

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{1}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\biggr)\,\mathds{P}_{X}\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\right]\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\int\mathds{1}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\biggr)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

Therefore
\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}=0\Bigr)=0
,
\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely.
Obviously
2.
implies
1.
with
\mu=\mathds{P}_{Y}
.
Now let us show that
2.

implies
3.

Let
f
be a bounded measurable function, we have by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}

\displaystyle=\int\biggl(\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}=\int\biggl(\int f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}

\displaystyle=\int f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}\,\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{Y}\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigr),

implying
3.
and that
\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\big(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathds{P}_{Y}\big)}.

We will show now that
3.
implies
2.

Let
f
be a bounded measurable function, we have by Fubini’s theorem

\displaystyle\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}

\displaystyle=\int f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathds{P}_{Y}\bigr)}\,\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int\biggl(\int f\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes\mathds{P}_{Y}\bigr)}\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X}

\displaystyle=\int\biggl(\int f\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{X},

showing that
\mathds{P}_{X}
almost surely

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\ll\mathds{P}_{Y}
and

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{Y}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{X,Y}}{\mathrm{d}\big(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y}\big)}.

The equivalence between
3.
and
4.
is immediate by
interchanging the roles of
X
and
Y
.
∎

The following lemma will be useful to optimize the
information
k
-means criterion and is related to
the Donsker Varadhan representation.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\pi\in\mathcal{M}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)
be a
probability measure on the measurable space
\Omega
.
Let
h:\Omega\rightarrow\mathds{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a measurable
function such that

Z=\int\exp(-h)\,\mathrm{d}\pi<\infty.

Let
\pi_{\exp(-h)}

be the probability measure whose density
with respect to
\pi
is proportional to
\exp(-h)

so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi_{\exp(-h)}}{\mathrm{d}\pi}=\frac{\exp(-h)}{Z}.

The identity

\displaystyle\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\biggl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)+\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\biggr)

\displaystyle=-\log\Biggl(\int\exp(-h)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\Biggr)+\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-h)})\in\mathds{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

is satisfied for any
\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)
and implies that

\inf_{\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\Omega)}\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\Biggl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)+\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\Biggr)=-\log\Biggl(\int\exp(-h)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\Biggr),

the minimum being reached when
\rho=\pi_{\exp(-h)}
.

Note that the lemma could also be written as

\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)+\int h\,\mathrm{d}\rho=-\log\biggl(\int\exp(-h)\,\mathrm{d}\pi\biggr)+\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-h)}\bigr)

if we are willing to follow the convention that

\int h\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho

and that
+\infty-\infty=+\infty
.

See
[
9
, page 159]
.
Note that the role of
\eta\in\mathds{Z}
in this lemma is only
to make sure that the integrals are always well defined
in
\mathds{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
in the
sense that the negative part of the integrand is integrable.
When
\rho
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi
,
it is also not absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\exp(-h)}

since
\pi(A)=0
if and only if
\pi_{\exp(-h)}(A)=0
.
In this case
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)=\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-h)})=+\infty

and the identity is true, both sides being equal to
+\infty
.
When
\rho\ll\pi
, then
\rho\ll\pi_{\exp(-\max\{h,\eta\})}
and

\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{\exp(-\max\{h,\eta\})}}=Z_{\eta}\exp(\max\{h,\eta\})\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi},

where

Z_{\eta}=\int\exp(-\max\{h,\eta\})\,\mathrm{d}\pi<+\infty.

Therefore

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-\max\{h,\eta\})}\bigr)=\log\bigl(Z_{\eta}\bigr)+\int\Bigl[\max\{h,\eta\}+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

By the monotone convergence theorem

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow-\infty}Z_{\eta}=Z\text{ and }\lim_{\eta\rightarrow-\infty}\int\Bigl[\max\{h,\eta\}+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int\Bigl[h+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

since we know that

\int\Bigl[\log(Z)+h+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]_{-}\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\int\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{\exp(-h)}}\Bigr)_{-}\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi_{\exp(-h)}}\,\mathrm{d}\pi_{\exp(-h)}\leq\exp(-1)<+\infty

and therefore that

\int\Bigl[h+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]_{-}\,\mathrm{d}\rho<+\infty.

This proves that

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow-\infty}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-\max\{h,\eta\})}\bigr)=\log(Z)+\int\Bigl[h+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-h)}\bigr)\\
=\log(Z)+\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\int\Bigl[\max\{h,\eta\}+\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]\,\mathrm{d}\rho\\
=\log(Z)+\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\biggl(\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho+\int\log\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\rho\biggr)\\
=\log(Z)+\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\biggl(\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho+\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)\biggr),

and therefore that

\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\rho,\pi_{\exp(-h)}\bigr)-\log(Z)=\inf_{\eta\in\mathds{Z}}\biggl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)+\int\max\{h,\eta\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho\biggr)

as stated in the lemma. The second statement of the lemma
is a consequence of the fact that the Kullback divergence
is non negative.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathds{P}_{X,Y}

Z\in[0,1]

\inf_{Q_{Y}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})}\,\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X})\bigr]=+\infty.

Otherwise,
Z>0
and for any
Q_{Y}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})

\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},Q^{\star}_{Y}\bigr)+\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{\star}_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},Q^{\star}_{Y}\bigr)+\log\bigl(Z^{-1}\bigr),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_{Y}^{\star}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\,\exp\bigl[-\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\,\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\,\exp\Biggl\{\mathds{P}_{X}\Biggl[\log\Biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}}{\mathrm{d}\mathds{P}_{Y}}\Biggr)\Biggr]\Biggr\}.

(6)

Consequently

\inf_{Q_{Y}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{Y}})}\,\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X})\bigr]=\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}(Q_{Y}^{\star},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X})\bigr]=\log\bigl(Z^{-1}\bigr)<\infty,

The probability measure
Q_{Y}^{\star}
represents the geometric

\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}
with respect to

\mathds{P}_{X}
.

By Lemma
1
,

\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X}\otimes Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y}\bigr)\bigr]=\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y}\bigr)+Q_{Y}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)\bigr].

(7)

Thus, when (

Q_{Y}\ll\mathds{P}_{Y}

and

Q_{Y}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)<+\infty\bigr)\Bigr]=1,

so that

\mathds{P}_{Y}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)<+\infty\bigr)\Bigr]>0,

implying that
Z>0
.
Assuming from now on that (

\bm{\mathcal{A}}=\Bigl\{y\,:\,\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y=y}\bigr)<+\infty\Bigr\}.

From Lemma
7
and (

Q_{Y}\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{A}})

\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]\\
=\underbrace{-\log\bigl[\mathds{P}_{Y}(\bm{\mathcal{A}})\bigr]-\log\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,Y\in\bm{\mathcal{A}}}\Bigl\{\exp\Bigl[-\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(\mathds{P}_{X},\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,Y}\bigr)\Bigr]\Bigr\}}_{=\log\bigl(Z^{-1}\bigr)}+\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},Q^{\star}_{Y}\bigr)\\
=\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q^{\star}_{Y},\mathds{P}_{Y\,|\,X}\bigr)\bigr]+\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(Q_{Y},Q^{\star}_{Y}\bigr).

Moreover, when
Q_{Y}(\bm{\mathcal{A}})<1
,
Q_{Y}\not\ll Q^{\star}_{Y}
,
so that both members are equal to
+\infty

The identity (

We are now ready to express the minimum of the information

Proposition 9
.

The information
k
-means problem can be expressed as

\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X})\Bigr)=\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\;\inf_{(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{\ell(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)\\
=\inf_{(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\;\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\;\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{\ell(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)\\
=\inf_{(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{\ell^{\star}_{q}(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)\\
=\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\,\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q^{\star,\ell}_{\ell(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)\\
=\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\log\bigl(Z^{-1}_{\ell(X)}\bigr)\Bigr),

where the infimum in
\ell
is taken on measurable classification
functions
\ell
, where
\ell^{\star}_{q}:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
is the best classification function
for a fixed
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
defined as

\ell^{\star}_{q}(x)=\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{x}),\qquad x\in\bm{\mathcal{X}},

whereas
q^{\star,\ell}_{1},\dots,q^{\star,\ell}_{k}
are
the best information
k
-means centers
with respect to
\ell(X)

defined as

q^{\star,\ell}_{j}=Z_{j}^{-1}\;\exp\Bigl\{\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\bigl[\log(p_{X})\bigr]\Bigr\},\qquad j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket,

where

Z_{j}=\int\exp\Bigl\{\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\bigl[\log(p_{X})\bigr]\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}\nu,

with the convention that
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}
can
be given any arbitrary value in the case when
Z_{j}=0
,
the corresponding criterion being in this case infinite.
Besides, we have the following Pythagorean identity

\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{\ell(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)=\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q^{\star,\ell}_{\ell(X)},p_{X})\Bigr)+\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{\ell(X)},q^{\star,\ell}_{\ell(X)}\bigr)\Bigr).

This proposition is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
8

applied to
\mathds{P}_{X,\,Y\,|\,\ell(X)=j}
.
∎

It may be of some help to state the empirical counterpart of
the previous proposition, where formulas are somehow more
explicit.

Corollary 10
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be an i.i.d sample drawn from
\mathds{P}_{X}
.
Then, the empirical version of the information
k
-means problem
tries to partition the observations
p_{X_{1}},\dots,p_{X_{n}}
into

k
-clusters, what is expressed here by

\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{j},p_{X_{i}}\bigr)=\inf_{\ell:\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\rightarrow\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\,\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{\ell(i)},p_{X_{i}}\bigr)\\
=\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\inf_{\ell:\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\rightarrow\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\,\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{\ell(i)},p_{X_{i}}\bigr)=\inf_{q\in\bigl(\mathds{L}^{1}_{+,1}(\nu)\bigr)^{k}}\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{\ell_{q}^{\star}(i)},p_{X_{i}}\bigr)\\
=\inf_{\ell:\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\rightarrow\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q^{\star,\ell}_{\ell(i)},p_{X_{i}}\bigr)=\inf_{\ell:\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\rightarrow\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\;\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\bigl\lvert\ell^{-1}(j)\bigr\rvert}{n}\;\log\bigl(Z^{-1}_{j}\bigr),

where
\ell^{\star}_{q}:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
is the best classification function
for a fixed
q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})
defined as

\ell^{\star}_{q}(i)=\arg\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X_{i}})

whereas
q^{\star,\ell}_{j},j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
are
the information
k
-means centers defined as

q^{\star,\ell}_{j}=Z^{-1}_{j}\,\Biggl(\prod_{i\in\ell^{-1}(j)}p_{X_{i}}\Biggr)^{1/|\ell^{-1}(j)|},

where

Z_{j}=\int\Biggl(\prod_{i\in\ell^{-1}(j)}p_{X_{i}}\Biggr)^{1/|\ell^{-1}(j)|}\,\mathrm{d}\nu.

Apply the previous proposition to the empirical measure

\displaystyle\overline{\mathds{P}}_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}
of the sample

X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.
∎

We will now see that when the sample is in
\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)

and has a finite second moment, the optimal centers for
a given classification function are also in
\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)
,
so that the optimization of the centers can be reduced to
this space.

Lemma 11
.

Let us assume that
\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\int p_{X}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\Bigr)<\infty
.
Then, the optimal centers
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}
defined in the previous lemma
verify
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}\in\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)
. Furthermore,
in this case

\inf\;\biggl\{\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X})\Bigr)\,:\,q\in\Bigl(\mathds{L}_{+,1}^{1}(\nu)\Bigr)^{k}\;\biggr\}\\
=\inf\;\biggl\{\;\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}(q_{j},p_{X})\Bigr)\,:\,q\in\Bigl(\mathds{L}_{+,1}^{1}(\nu)\cap\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)\Bigr)^{k}\;\biggr\}.

Apply Jensen’s inequality and the Fubini-Tonelli theorem to
obtain that
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}\in\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)
.
Indeed, for any
j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, if
Z_{j}=0
, we can pick up
any value for
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}
, and in paticular a value
in
\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)
, in the same way if
\mathds{P}_{X}(\ell(X)=j)=0
, we can make
an arbitrary choice for
q^{\star,\ell}_{j}
, otherwise,
Z_{j}>0
,
and

\int(q^{\star,\ell}_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu=Z_{j}^{-2}\int\exp\biggl\{2\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\bigl[\log(p_{X})\bigr]\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\\
\leq Z_{j}^{-2}\;\mathds{P}_{X\,|\,\ell(X)=j}\biggl(\int p_{X}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\biggr)\leq Z_{j}^{-2}\mathds{P}_{X}\bigl(\ell(X)=j\bigr)^{-1}\mathds{P}_{X}\biggl(\int p_{X}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\nu\biggr)<\infty

Then according to Proposition
9

\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{j},p_{X}\bigr)\Bigr]=\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q_{\ell(X)},p_{X}\bigr)\Bigr]\\
\geq\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl[\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q^{\star,\ell}_{\ell(X)},p_{X}\bigr)\Bigr]\geq\inf_{\ell:\bm{\mathcal{X}}\mapsto\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\mathds{P}_{X}\Bigl[\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\bm{\mathcal{K}}\bigl(q^{\star,\ell}_{j},p_{X}\bigr)\Bigr],

showing that we can restrict the optimization to
q_{j}\in\mathds{L}^{2}(\nu)
.
∎

5
PAC-Bayesian generalization bounds for the
linear
k
-means criterion

In this section, we derive non asymptotic generalization
bounds for the linear
k
-means criterion defined hereafter.

Definition 12
.

Given a random vector
W
in a separable Hilbert space
H
and
a bounded measurable set of parameters
\Theta\subset H^{k}
,
the
k
-means linear criterion is defined as

\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr),\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

If
W_{1},\dots,W_{n}
are
n
independent copies of
W
,
the empirical linear
k
-means criterion is defined by taking
the expectation with respect to the empirical measure

\overline{\mathds{P}}_{W}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{W_{i}}
instead of
integrating with respect to
\mathds{P}_{W}
.

Using a change of representation based on the kernel trick,
we will show that all the criteria we defined so far
can be rewritten as linear
k
-means criteria in suitable
spaces of coordinates.

Consequently, our approach will be to prove a generalization
bound for the linear
k
-means criterion and to study its
consequences for the other criteria.

To reach a
\bm{\mathcal{O}}\bigl(\sqrt{k/n}\bigr)
speed up to
logarighmic factors, we will
borrow ideas from the classical chaining method used to upper bound the expected supremum of Gaussian processes (see
[
7
]
).
However, we will
transpose the idea of chaining into the setting of
PAC-Bayesian deviation inequalities.
To obtain dimension free bounds, we will use a sequence
of perturbations of the parameter by isonormal processes
with a variance parameter ranging in a geometric grid.
This multiscale perturbation scheme will play the same
role as the
\delta
-nets in classical chaining.

Let us begin with an existence result.

Proposition 13
.

In the setting of Definition
12
, let us assume
that
\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}=\ess\sup_{\mathds{P}_{W}}\lVert W\rVert<+\infty
. There is
\theta^{*}\in\overline{\Theta}
, the weak closure of

\Theta
, such that

\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta^{*}_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)\Bigr].

Moreover
\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert\leq\lVert\Theta\rVert\overset{\text{\rm def}}{=}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\lVert\theta\rVert
.

This is inspired by the proof of Theorem 3.2 in
[
16
]
.
Let us begin with the second statement. Since

\theta\longmapsto\lVert\theta\rVert=\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in H^{k},\,\lVert\theta^{\prime}\rVert=1}\langle\theta^{\prime},\theta\rangle

is weakly lower semicontiuous,

\lVert\overline{\Theta}\rVert\leq\lVert\Theta\rVert
, so that in
particular
\lVert\theta^{*}\rVert\leq\lVert\Theta\rVert
.
Moreover, for any
w\in H
,

\displaystyle H^{k}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathds{R}

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\longmapsto\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},w\rangle

is weakly continuous, since, by definition of the weak topology of
H^{k}
,

\theta\mapsto\langle\theta_{j},w\rangle
are weakly continuous, and taking a finite
minimum is a continuous operation.

Let
(\theta_{n})_{n\in\mathds{N}}
be a bounded
sequence in
H^{k}
, converging weakly to
\theta
. By the dominated convergence theorem

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\,\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{n,\,j},W\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{n,j},W\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\,\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr),

since
\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{n,j},W\rangle\bigr\rvert\leq\rVert\theta_{n}\rVert\,\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}
.
Thus

\bm{\mathcal{R}}:\theta\longmapsto\mathds{P}_{W}\bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\bigr)

is weakly continuous on
\overline{\Theta}
. But the unit ball, and therefore
any ball of
H^{k}
, is weakly compact, so that
\overline{\Theta}
being weakly
closed and bounded is also weakly compact. Consequently,
\bm{\mathcal{R}}

reaches its minimum on
\overline{\Theta}
at some (non necessarily unique)
point
\theta^{*}\in\overline{\Theta}
. Therefore

\bm{\mathcal{R}}(\theta^{*})=\inf_{\theta\in\overline{\Theta}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}(\theta)=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\bm{\mathcal{R}}(\theta),

the last equality being due to the fact that
\bm{\mathcal{R}}
is weakly
continuous.

Note that we used the weak topology, since the
unit ball of
H^{k}
is not strongly compact when the dimension
of
H
is infinite.
∎

We will prove generalization bounds based on the following PAC-Bayesian lemma.
We will use it as a workhorse to produce all the deviation
inequalities necessary to achieve
our goals. Combined with Jensen’s inequality, it will also
produce bounds in expectation.

Lemma 14
.

Consider two measurable spaces
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
and
\bm{\mathcal{W}}
,
a prior probability measure

\pi\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})
defined on
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
,
and a measurable function
h:\bm{\mathcal{T}}\times\bm{\mathcal{W}}\rightarrow\mathds{R}
. Let
W\in\bm{\mathcal{W}}
be a random
variable and let
(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})
be a sample
made of
n
independent copies of
W
.
Let
\lambda
be a positive real parameter.

\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\,\dots,\,W_{n}}\Biggl\{\exp\Biggl[\sup_{\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})}\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\biggl\{\;\int\min\Bigl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\\
-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})-\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)\biggr\}\Biggr]\Biggr\}\leq 1.

(8)

Consequently, for any
\delta\in]0,1[
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\sup_{\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})}\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\biggl\{\;\int\min\Bigl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\\
-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})-\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)\biggr\}\leq\log(\delta^{-1}).

(9)

Note that the role of
\eta
in this formula is to give a meaning to
the integration with respect to
\rho
in all circumstances.

We follow here the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition 1.7 in
[
19
]
.
Remark that
the supremum in
\rho
can be restricted to the case when

\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)<\infty
,
and recall that in this case
\rho\ll\pi
and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)=\int\log\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})
. Note also that

\int\mathds{1}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})>0\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})=\int\mathds{1}\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})>0\Bigr)\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})=1.

Applying Jensen’s inequality, we get

\exp\Biggl\{\sup_{\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})}\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\int\min\biggl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\\
-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})-\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)\Biggr\}\\
\leq\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})\\
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)<\infty\end{subarray}}\int\exp\Biggl\{\min\biggl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\\
-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\biggr\}\Biggr\}\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})\\
=\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})\\
\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)<\infty\end{subarray}}\int\exp\Biggl\{\min\biggl\{\eta,\\
-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\biggr\}\Biggr\}\mathds{1}\biggl(\frac{\mathrm{d}\rho}{\mathrm{d}\pi}(\theta^{\prime})>0\biggr)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})\\
\leq\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\int\exp\Biggl\{\min\biggl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})\\
\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{monotone}\\
\text{convergence}\end{subarray}}{=}\int\exp\Biggl\{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime}).

Let us put

Y^{\prime}=\sup_{\rho\in\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{+}^{1}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})}\sup_{\eta\in\mathds{N}}\biggl\{\;\int\min\Bigl\{\eta,\;-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\\
-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Bigr\}\,\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta^{\prime})-\bm{\mathcal{K}}(\rho,\pi)\biggr\}\text{ and }\\
Y=\log\int\exp\Biggl\{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime}).\hfill

We just proved that
Y^{\prime}\leq Y
. Moreover,
Y
is measurable,
according to Fubini’s theorem for non-negative functions.
Therefore
Y
is a random variable. Note that we did not prove
that
Y^{\prime}
itself is measurable.
Remark now that

\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\,\dots\,,W_{n}}\bigl[\exp(Y)\bigr]\\
=\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\,\dots\,,W_{n}}\int\exp\Biggl\{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime}),\\
\underset{\text{Fubini}}{=}\int\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\,\dots\,,W_{n}}\exp\Biggl\{-\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})-n\log\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\exp\bigl[-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr]\Bigr]\Biggr\}\,\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})\\
=\scalebox{1.2}[1.5]{$\displaystyle\int$}\left(\mathds{1}\biggl(\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\Bigr)\Bigr]<+\infty\biggr)\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mathds{P}_{W_{i}}\bigl[\exp\bigl(-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W_{i})\bigr)\bigr]}{\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl[\exp\Bigl(-\lambda h(\theta^{\prime},W)\bigr)\bigr]}\right)\mathrm{d}\pi(\theta^{\prime})\leq 1,

proving the first part of the lemma.
From Markov’s inequality,

\mathds{P}\bigl(Y\geq\log(\delta^{-1})\bigr)\leq\delta\,\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\,\dots\,,W_{n}}\bigl[\exp(Y)\bigr]\leq\delta.

Consequently
\mathds{P}\bigl(Y\leq\log(\delta^{-1})\bigr)\geq 1-\delta
.
We have proved that the non necessarily measurable event
Y^{\prime}\leq\log(\delta^{-1})

contains the measurable event
Y\leq\log(\delta^{-1})
whose
probability is at least
1-\delta
.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove our generalization bounds for the linear

k
-means criterion.

Lemma 15
.

Let
W
be a random vector in a separable Hilbert space
H
.
Let
(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})
be a sample made of
n
independent
copies of
W
. Let
\Theta\subset H^{k}
be a bounded
measurable set of parameters. Define

\lVert\Theta\rVert=\sup\biggl\{\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lVert\theta_{j}\rVert^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}\,:\,\theta\in\Theta\biggr\}<\infty

and assume that, for some real valued parameters
a
and
b
,

\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\in[a,b]\text{ for any }\theta\in\Theta\Bigr)=1.

Assume also that
\displaystyle\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}\overset{\text{\rm def}}{=}\ess\sup_{\mathds{P}_{W}}\,\lVert W\rVert<\infty
.

Our first result gives an observable upper bound for the

k
-means criterion, provided that the above parameters are
known or upper bounded by known quantities.

For any
k\geq 2
, any
n\geq 2k
and any
\delta\in]0,1[
, with
probability at least
1-\delta
, for any
\theta\in\Theta
,

\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)\leq\overline{\mathds{P}}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)\\
+\Biggl(\frac{\log(n/k)}{\log(2)}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(k)}{n}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\log(k)}{n}}\;\Biggr)\lVert\Theta\rVert\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}\\
+\sqrt{\frac{(\sqrt{2}+1)\Bigl(k(b-a)^{2}+2\log(ek)\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert\Theta\rVert^{2}\Bigr)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(\delta^{-1})}{2n}}(b-a),

where
\displaystyle\overline{\mathds{P}}_{W}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{W_{i}}

is the empirical measure.

Our second result deals with the excess risk with respect
to a non random reference parameter
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
.

If
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
is a non random value of the parameter,
with probability at least
1-\delta
,
for any
\theta\in\Theta
,

\Bigl(\mathds{P}_{W}-\overline{\mathds{P}}_{W}\Bigr)\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle-\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j}^{*},W\rangle\Bigr)\\
\leq\Biggl(\frac{\log(n/k)}{\log(2)}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(k)}{n}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\log(k)}{n}}\;\Biggr)\lVert\Theta\rVert\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}\\
+\sqrt{\frac{(\sqrt{2}+1)\Bigl(k(b-a)^{2}+2\log(ek)\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert\Theta\rVert^{2}\Bigr)}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(\delta^{-1})}{n}}(b-a).

Our third result draws the consequences of this excess
risk bound for an
\varepsilon
-minimizer

\widehat{\theta}
.

In the case when the estimator
\widehat{\theta}(W_{1},\dots,W_{n})\in\Theta

is such that
\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\dots,\,W_{n}}
almost surely

\overline{\mathds{P}}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\widehat{\theta}_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)\leq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\overline{\mathds{P}}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)+\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\widehat{\theta}_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)-\varepsilon
satisfies the same bound with at least the same
probability.

Moreover, the expected excess risk satisfies

\mathds{P}_{W_{1},\dots,W_{n}}\Bigl[\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\widehat{\theta}_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)-\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathds{P}_{W}\Bigl(\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\Bigr)\Bigr]\\
\leq\Biggl(\frac{\log(n/k)}{\log(2)}\sqrt{\frac{8\log(k)}{n}}+2\sqrt{\frac{\log(k)}{n}}\;\Biggr)\lVert\Theta\rVert\,\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}\\

The lemma follows from the definition of

\mu=BC^{-1}\log(B^{2})^{-1}
,
we get

\lVert\Theta_{B}^{k}\rVert^{2}\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\leq k\bigl(B^{2}+\mu\log(B^{2})^{2}\bigr)\bigl(C^{2}+\mu^{-1}\bigr)=k\bigl(BC+2\log(B)\bigr)^{2}.

Remark also that
for any
\theta\in\Theta_{B}^{k}
, with probability one,

\min_{j\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket}\langle\theta_{j},W\rangle\in\bigl[0,\lVert\Theta_{B}\rVert\lVert W\rVert_{\infty}\bigr]\subset\bigl[0,BC+2\log(B)\bigr].

Use these bounds in Lemma

to conclude the proof.
