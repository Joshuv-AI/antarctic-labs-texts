Difference Operators and Duality for Trigonometric Gaudin and Dynamical Hamiltonians
Filipp UVAROV

Abstract
We study the difference analog of the quotient differential operator from [Tarasov V., Uvarov F., Lett. Math. Phys. 110 (2020), 3375–3400, arXiv:1907.02117]. Starting with a space of quasi-exponentials W = ⟨ α i x ​ p i ​ j ​ ( x ) , i = 1 , … , n , j = 1 , … , n i ⟩ W=\langle\alpha_{i}^{x}p_{ij}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,\,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n_{i}\rangle , where α i ∈ ℂ ∗ \alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{*} and p i ​ j ​ ( x ) p_{ij}(x) are polynomials, we consider the formal conjugate S ˇ W † \check{S}^{\dagger}_{W} of the quotient difference operator S ˇ W \check{S}_{W} satisfying S ^ = S ˇ W ​ S W \widehat{S}=\check{S}_{W}S_{W} . Here, S W S_{W} is a linear difference operator of order dim W \dim W annihilating W W , and S ^ \widehat{S} is a linear difference operator with constant coefficients depending on α i \alpha_{i} and deg ⁡ p i ​ j ​ ( x ) \deg p_{ij}(x) only. We construct a space of quasi-exponentials of dimension ord ⁡ S ˇ W † \operatorname{ord}\check{S}^{\dagger}_{W} , which is annihilated by S ˇ W † \check{S}^{\dagger}_{W} and describe its basis and discrete exponents. We also consider a similar construction for differential operators associated with spaces of quasi-polynomials, which are linear combinations of functions of the form x z ​ q ​ ( x ) x^{z}q(x) , where z ∈ ℂ z\in\mathbb{C} and q ⁡ ( x ) q(x) is a polynomial. Combining our results with the results on the bispectral duality obtained in [Mukhin E., Tarasov V., Varchenko A., Adv. Introduction

1.1.
Consider an operator
T
acting on functions of a variable
x
by the rule
(Tf)(x)=f(x+1)
. An operator
S
of the form

S=\sum_{i=0}^{N}a_{i}(x)T^{N-i}
, where
a_{0}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{N}(x)
are complex valued functions
of
x
and
a_{0}(x)\neq 0
, is called a linear difference operator of
order
N
. Say that the operator
S
is monic if
a_{0}(x)=1
. Let us write
\operatorname{ord}(S)
for the order of
S
.

Let us fix a branch of
\ln x
and write
\alpha^{x}
for
{\rm e}^{x\ln\alpha}
for any non-zero complex number
\alpha
. A quasi-exponential is a function of the form
\alpha^{x}p(x)
for some non-zero
\alpha
and polynomial
p(x)
. We will say that a complex vector space
W
is a space of quasi-exponentials if
W
has a basis consisting of quasi-exponentials.
Let
W
be a space of quasi-exponentials with a basis

\{\alpha_{i}^{x}p_{ij}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,\,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n_{i}\}
,
where the numbers
\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}
are distinct, and
p_{ij}
are some polynomials. Set
d_{i}=\max_{j}\bigl(\deg p_{ij}(x)\bigr)
.
It can be shown that there exists a unique monic linear difference operator
S_{W}
of order
\dim{W}
annihilating
W
and a monic linear difference operator
\check{S}_{W}
such that

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i})^{d_{i}+1}=\check{S}_{W}S_{W},

see Sections
2.1
–
2.4
for details. We will call
\check{S}_{W}
the quotient difference operator.

Write
\check{S}_{W}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\check{a}_{i}(x)T^{m-i}
and denote
T_{-}=T^{-1}
. The formal conjugate
\check{S}^{\dagger}_{W}
of
\check{S}_{W}
is a linear difference operator acting on a function
f(x)
as follows:

\big(\check{S}^{\dagger}_{W}f\big)(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{-}^{m-i}(\check{a}_{i}(x)f(x)).

In Section
2.4
, we construct a vector space of functions
Q(W)
of dimension
\operatorname{ord}\big(\check{S}^{\dagger}_{W}\big)=m
such that
\check{S}^{\dagger}_{W}
annihilates
Q(W)
. We prove that
Q(W)
has a basis of the form

\{\alpha_{i}^{-x}q_{ij}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,\,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{i}\},\qquad q_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}[x],

and describe the degrees of the polynomials
q_{ij}(x)
.

For a space of quasi-exponentials
W
and a point
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we define
the discrete exponents of
W
at
z
associated with the operator
T
and
the
T_{-}
-discrete exponents of
W
at
z
associated with the operator

T_{-}
. In Sections
2.5
and
2.6
, we compute the
T_{-}
-discrete
exponents of the space
Q(W)
at the point
z-1
using
the discrete exponents of
W
at the point
z
.

1.5.
Our study of the map
\mathfrak{T}_{1}
is motivated by the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality between the trigonometric Gaudin Hamiltonians
H_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak H_{n}\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
and the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians
G_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G_{n}\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}

,
14
]
, and Section

. Both
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
and
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
act on the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
of polynomials in
k
times
n
anticommuting variables
\xi_{ai}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,

. Let
\rho(H_{1}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\rho(H_{n})
be the images of the trigonometric Gaudin Hamiltonians in

\rho(H_{i})=-\rho(G_{i}),\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,

and Proposition

. In particular, any common eigenvector of

in

is a common eigenvector of
G_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G_{n}
, and vice versa.

Common eigenvectors of the Hamiltonians can be found using the Bethe ansatz
method. For an “admissible” space of quasi-polynomials

V\in\mathcal{P}\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
,
the Bethe ansatz associates an eigenvector

of

acting in
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
, see
[
8
]
and Sections

,
6.3
for details. Denote the corresponding eigenvalues as
h^{V}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h^{V}_{n}
. Similarly, for an “admissible” space of
quasi-exponentials

W\in\mathcal{E}\big(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}^{\prime};1-\bar{z}-\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}_{1},\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}\big)
,
the Bethe ansatz associates an eigenvector
v_{W}
of
G_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G_{n}

acting in
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
, see
[
8
]
and

6.5

. We will show that if

, then

see Theorems
6.12
and
6.17
.
This “matches” the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality (
1.1
), so, using that for generic

,

\bar{z}
,

is proportional to

, see Sections

,

.
Here and below, when we say “for generic

,

The exchange of the trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians under
the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality is expected to be a part of the duality between
the Bethe algebras of the trigonometric Gaudin model and the XXX-type
spin chain model. The Bethe algebra of the trigonometric Gaudin model is
a commutative subalgebra of the universal enveloping algebra

U\big(\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}_{k}}\big)
of the loop algebra
\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}_{k}}

see
[

a commutative subalgebra of the Yangian

, see
[
4

. Both

and

act on the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
. The images of the trigonometric Gaudin Hamiltonians in
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{P}_{kn})
belong to the image of the Bethe algebra of the trigonometric Gaudin model, and the images of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians in
\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{P}_{kn})
belong to the image of the Bethe algebra of the XXX-type spin chain model.
It is expected that the equality of the images of the Hamiltonians extends to the equality of the images of the Bethe algebras. The corresponding result for
the rational Gaudin model was established in
[
11
]
, where we developed and used the differential analogs of the results for the quotient difference operator studied here. Therefore, the results of this paper can be considered as the first steps in establishing the duality between the Bethe algebras of the trigonometric Gaudin model and the XXX-type spin chain model.

For the eigenvalues

2.1
Factorization of a difference operator

For any functions
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n}
, let

\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n})=\det\big(\big(T^{j-1}g_{i}\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}\big)

be their discrete Wronskian.
Let
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{i}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n})
be the determinant of the
n\times n
matrix whose
j
-th row is
g_{j},Tg_{j},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak T^{n-i-1}g_{j},

T^{n-i+1}g_{j},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak T^{n}g_{j}
.

Fix functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
such that
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{i_{1}},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{i_{m}})\neq 0
for any
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\leq n
. In particular, the functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
are linearly independent.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exists a unique monic linear difference operator
S=T^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}T^{n-i}
of order
n
such that
Sf_{i}=0
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.
Moreover, the coefficients
a_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{n}
of the difference operator
S
are given by the formula

a_{i}=(-1)^{i}\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{i}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,

(2.1)

and for any function
g
, we have

Sg=\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},g)}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})}.

(2.2)

Proof.

Solving

\begin{pmatrix}f_{1}&Tf_{1}&\dots&T^{n-1}f_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
f_{n}&Tf_{n}&\dots&T^{n-1}f_{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{n}\\
\vdots\\
a_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}T^{n}f_{1}\\
\vdots\\
T^{n}f_{n}\end{pmatrix}

for
a_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{n}
by Cramer’s rule yields formula (
2.1
), and this solution is unique. Formula (
2.2
) follows
from the last row expansion of the determinant in the numerator.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

The difference operator
S
can be written in the following form:

S=\left(T-\frac{g_{1}(x+1)}{g_{1}(x)}\right)\left(T-\frac{g_{2}(x+1)}{g_{2}(x)}\right)\cdots\left(T-\frac{g_{n}(x+1)}{g_{n}(x)}\right),

(2.3)

where
g_{n}=f_{n}
, and

g_{i}=\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{n},f_{n-1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{i})}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{n},f_{n-1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{i+1})},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1.

(2.4)

Proof.

Denote by
S_{1}
the difference operator in the right-hand side of (
2.3
). By uniqueness of the operator
S
stated in Lemma
2.1
, it is sufficient to prove that
S_{1}f_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. We will prove it by induction on
n
.

If
n=1
, then
g_{1}=f_{1}
, and
S_{1}f_{1}=(T-f_{1}(x+1)/f_{1}(x))f_{1}(x)=0
.
Let
S_{2}
be the monic linear difference operator of order
n-1
such that
S_{2}f_{i}=0
,
i=2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. By induction assumption,

S_{2}=\left(T-\frac{g_{2}(x+1)}{g_{2}(x)}\right)\left(T-\frac{g_{3}(x+1)}{g_{3}(x)}\right)\cdots\left(T-\frac{g_{n}(x+1)}{g_{n}(x)}\right).

Since
S_{1}=(T-g_{1}(x+1)/g_{1}(x))S_{2}
, we have
S_{1}f_{i}=0
,
i=2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. Formulas (
2.2
) and (
2.4
) yeild
S_{2}f_{1}=g_{1}
, thus
S_{1}f_{1}=0
as well.
∎

2.2
Formal conjugate difference operator

Denote
T_{-}=T^{-1}
. Then
(T_{-}f)(x)=f(x-1)
. Let
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
, and
S
be like in the previous section.
Define the
formal conjugate
of
S
by the formula:

S^{\dagger}h(x)=(T_{-})^{n}h(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{-})^{n-i}\bigl(a_{i}(x)h(x)\bigr).

By Proposition
2.2
, we have

S^{\dagger}=\left(T_{-}-\frac{g_{n}(x+1)}{g_{n}(x)}\right)\left(T_{-}-\frac{g_{n-1}(x+1)}{g_{n-1}(x)}\right)\cdots\left(T_{-}-\frac{g_{1}(x+1)}{g_{1}(x)}\right).

(2.5)

Define

h_{i}=T\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{i-1},f_{i+1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})}\right).

Proposition 2.3
.

We have
S^{\dagger}h_{i}=0
for all
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

Proof.

Since
h_{1}=(-1)^{n-1}/g_{1}(x+1)
, formula (
2.5
) immediately gives
S^{\dagger}h_{1}=0
. To prove that
S^{\dagger}
annihilates
h_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{n}
, one can consider factorization (
2.3
) of
S
, where functions
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n}
are defined using a different order of functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
, see the proof of Proposition 6.3 in
[
11
]
for a differential analog of this argument.
∎

2.3
Quotient difference operator

Consider functions
f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k}
such that
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{m})\neq 0
for any subset
\{g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{m}\}
of
\{f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k}\}
. Let
S
and
\widehat{S}
be the monic linear difference operators of order
n
and
n+k
annihilating
f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
and
f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k}
, respectively. Then there is a unique difference operator
\check{S}
such that
\widehat{S}=\check{S}S
. Indeed, the existence of
\check{S}
can be seen from the factorization formula (
2.3
), and the uniqueness follows from the long division algorithm. We will call
\check{S}
the
quotient difference operator
.

Define functions
\phi_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\phi_{k}
by the formula

\phi_{a}=T\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{a-1},h_{a+1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k})}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k})}\right).

(2.6)

Proposition 2.4
.

We have
\check{S}^{\dagger}\phi_{a}=0
for all
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
.

Proof.

Set

\tilde{h}_{a}=Sh_{a}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. Formula (
2.2
) yields
\tilde{h}_{i}=\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{i})/\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})
. Using this and the Wronskian identities (
A.1
) and (
A.4
), it is easy to check that

\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{h}_{i_{1}},,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{i_{m}}\big)=\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{i_{1}},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{i_{m}})}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})}

(2.7)

for any
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\!\leq k
. In particular,
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{h}_{i_{1}},,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{i_{m}}\big)\!\neq 0
for any
{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m}\!\leq k}
.

By Proposition
2.3
, the functions

\tilde{\phi}_{a}=T\left(\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{h}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{a-1},\tilde{h}_{a+1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{k}\big)}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{h}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{k}\big)}\right),\qquad a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k,

vanish under the action of
\check{S}^{\dagger}
.

Taking
\{i_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak i_{m}\}=\{1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a-1,a+1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k\}
and
\{i_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak i_{m}\}=\{1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k\}
in formula (
2.7
), it is easy to see that
\phi_{a}=\tilde{\phi}_{a}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. The proposition is proved.
∎

Let
W
and
\widehat{W}
be the vector spaces with the bases
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
and
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{k}
, respectively. We will call the span of
\phi_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\phi_{k}
the quotient conjugate space for the pair
\big(W,\widehat{W}\big)
.

2.4
Quotient difference operator and spaces of quasi-exponentials

Recall that a quasi-exponential is a function of the form
\alpha^{x}p(x)
for some non-zero
\alpha
and a polynomial
p(x)
, and a space of quasi-exponentials is a vector space with a basis consisting of quasi-exponentials. It is straightforward to check that if
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{m}
are quasi-exponentials, then
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{m})=0
if and only if
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{m}
are linearly dependent. Therefore, by Lemma
2.1
, for any space of quasi-exponentials
W
, there exists a unique monic linear difference operator
S_{W}
of order
\dim{W}
annihilating
W
. We will call
S_{W}
the
fundamental difference operator
of
W
. The following lemma will be useful for us later.

Lemma 2.5
.

If for two spaces of quasi-exponentials
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, we have
S_{W_{1}}=S_{W_{2}}
, then
W_{1}=W_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
and
h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{n}
be the quasi-exponential bases of
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, respectively. Using formula (
2.2
), for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, we have
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{i})=\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})S_{W_{1}}h_{i}=0
. Therefore,
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{i}
are linearly dependent for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, and
W_{2}\subset W_{1}
. Similarly, one proves that
W_{1}\subset W_{2}
.
∎

In this paper, a partition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots{})
is an infinite nonincreasing sequence of nonnegative integers stabilizing at zero. Let
\mu^{\prime}=(\mu^{\prime}_{1},\mu^{\prime}_{2},\dots{})
denote the conjugated partition,
that is,
\mu^{\prime}_{i}=\#\{j\,|\,\mu_{j}\geq i\}
.
In particular,

\mu_{1}^{\prime}
equals the number of nonzero entries in
\mu
.

Fix nonzero complex numbers
\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}
and nonzero partitions

\mu^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu^{(n)}
. Assume that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for
i\neq j
.
For each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
denote
n_{i}=\big(\mu^{(i)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}
.
Let
W
be a space of quasi-exponentials with a basis

\{\alpha_{i}^{x}q_{ij}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,\,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n_{i}\},

where
q_{ij}(x)
are polynomials such that

\deg q_{ij}=n_{i}+\mu^{(i)}_{j}-j
.

Denote
p_{i}=\mu_{1}^{(i)}+n_{i}=\max_{j}\deg q_{ij}+1
, and take
\widehat{W}
to be the span the functions
\alpha_{i}^{x}x^{p}
,

i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
p=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak p_{i}-1
. Let
Q(W)
denote the quotient conjugate space for the pair
\big(W,\widehat{W}\big)
.

Let
S_{W}
be the monic linear difference operator of order
\dim W
annihilating
W
. We will say that
S_{W}
is the fundamental difference operator of
W
. On the other hand, the difference operator
\widehat{S}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i})^{p_{i}}
annihilates
\widehat{W}
. Then there exists a difference operator
\check{S}_{W}
such that
\widehat{S}=\check{S}_{W}S_{W}
,
see Section
2.3
. By Proposition
2.4
, the difference operator
\check{S}_{W}^{\dagger}
annihilates

.

Proposition 2.6
.

The space

\big\{\alpha_{i}^{-x}\check{q}_{ij}(x)\,|\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n,\,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu_{1}^{(i)}\big\},

i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu_{1}^{(i)}

Proof.

Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big({\widehat{W}}\big)=\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\alpha_{1}^{x},\alpha_{1}^{x}x,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{1}^{x}x^{p_{1}-1},\dots,\alpha_{n}^{x},\alpha_{n}^{x}x,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}^{x}x^{p_{n}-1}\big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{ij}\big(\widehat{W}\big)=\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big({}\dots,\widehat{\alpha_{i}^{x}x^{j}},\dots{}\big).

The functions in the second line are the same except
the function
\alpha_{i}^{x}x^{j}
is omitted.

For each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, set

Notice that the functions
\alpha_{i}^{x}x^{l}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
l\in\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{c}
, complement
the basis
{\{\alpha_{i}^{x}q_{ij}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n}
,
j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n_{i}\}
of
W
to a basis of
\widehat{W}
. Therefore, from the construction of the space
Q(W)
, in particular, from formula (
2.6
), it follows that
Q(W)
is spanned by functions
f_{ij}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,

of the form

f_{ij}=T\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{ij}\big(\widehat{W}\big)}{\mathcal{W}\big(\widehat{W}\big)}+T\sum_{s=j+1}^{p_{i}-1}C_{ils}\frac{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{is}\big(\widehat{W}\big)}{\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\widehat{W}\big)},

are complex numbers.

Using an induction similar to what we used in the proof of Lemma 6.5 in
[

, we obtain the following formulas:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\widehat{W}\big)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\alpha_{i}^{p_{i}x}\prod_{s=1}^{p_{i}-1}\alpha_{i}^{s}s!\right)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})^{p_{i}p_{j}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}_{ij}\big(\widehat{W}\big)=r_{ij}(x)\prod_{l=1}^{n}\Bigg(\alpha_{l}^{(p_{l}-\delta_{il})x}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}s=1\\

where
r_{ij}(x)
is a monic polynomial in

and
\deg r_{ij}=p_{i}-j-1
. Then for the functions

.

Notice that

. This can be illustrated by enumerating, starting from

2.5
Transform of discrete exponents

Denote
M^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\mu^{(i)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}=\dim W
and
M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu^{(i)}_{1}=\dim Q(W)
. For
z\in\mathbb{C}
, define
the sequence of discrete exponents of
W
at
z
as
a unique sequence of integers
(e_{1}>\dots>e_{M^{\prime}})

with the property:
there exists a basis
\psi_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\psi_{M^{\prime}}
of
W
such that for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak M^{\prime}
,
\big(T^{j}\psi_{i}\big)(z)=0
for
j=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e_{i}-1
and
\big(T^{e_{i}}\psi_{i}\big)(z)\neq 0
.

The sequence of discrete exponents of
W
at

(M^{\prime}-1,M^{\prime}-2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 0)
if and only if
z
is a root of
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{M^{\prime}})
, where
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{M^{\prime}}
is any basis of
W
. If
z
is such a root, we will call it a
discrete singular point
of
W
.

Define
the sequence of
T_{-}
-discrete exponents of
W
at
z
by replacing the operator
T
in the definition of the sequence of discrete exponents by the operator
T_{-}=T^{-1}
.

.

(e_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e_{M^{\prime}})

at

. Define a partition

\{\psi_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\psi_{M^{\prime}}\}

2.8
), the Wronskian

\widehat{W}

and for
each

,

and
f_{i+1}(z+i)\neq 0
.

Consider a matrix-valued function

F_{a}(x)=\big(T^{j}f_{i}\big)_{\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr&i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak M+M^{\prime},\hskip 4.90001pti\neq a\\

\{e_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e_{M^{\prime}}\}\subset\{0,1,2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak M+M^{\prime}-1\}
, in particular,
\lambda_{1}\leq M
. Denote
\boldsymbol{e}^{c}=\{0,1,2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak M+M^{\prime}-1\}\setminus\{e_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e_{M^{\prime}}\}

span

. Let us prove that

,

,

-duality of the trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians.

3
Quotient differential operator

3.1
Spaces of quasi-polynomials

By quasi-polynomial we mean a function of the form
x^{z}p(x)
, where
z\in\mathbb{C}
and
p(x)
is a polynomial.

Fix complex numbers
z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k}
and nonzero partitions

\lambda^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\lambda^{(k)}
. Assume that
z_{a}-z_{b}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
a\neq b
.
Let
V
be a vector space of functions in one variable with a basis

\{x^{z_{a}}q_{ab}(x)\,|\,a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k,\,b=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}\}
,
where
q_{ab}(x)
are polynomials and
\deg q_{ab}=\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}+\lambda^{(a)}_{b}-b
.
Assume that the space
V
satisfies the following property, which we will call the non-degeneracy at
0
: for each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
and any
b=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}
,
there exists a linear combination of polynomials
q_{a1},q_{a2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak q_{a(\lambda^{(a)})^{\prime}_{1}}
which has a root at
x=0
of multiplicity
b-1
.

Denote
L^{\prime}=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}=\dim V
. For
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, define
the sequence of exponents of
V
at
\alpha
as
a unique sequence of integers
(e_{1}>\dots>e_{L^{\prime}})
,
with the property:
there exists a basis
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{L^{\prime}}
of
V
such that for each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak L^{\prime}
, we have
f_{a}(x)=(x-\alpha)^{e_{a}}(1+o(1))
as
x\to\alpha
.

For any sufficiently differentiable functions
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{s}
, let

\operatorname{Wr}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{s})=\det\big(\big(({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{j-1}g_{i}(x)\big)_{i,j=1}^{s}\big)

be their Wronskian. The sequence of exponents of
V
at
\alpha
differs from the sequence
(L^{\prime}-1,L^{\prime}-2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 0)
if and only if
\alpha
is a root of
\operatorname{Wr}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}})
, where
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}}
is any basis of
V
. If
\alpha
is such a root, we will call it a
singular point
of
V
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}
be the singular points of
V
and for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, let
\big(e^{(i)}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e^{(i)}_{L^{\prime}}\big)
be the sequence of exponents of
V
at
\alpha_{i}
. For each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, define a partition
\mu^{(i)}=\big(\mu^{(i)}_{1},\mu^{(i)}_{2},\dots{}\big)
as follows:
e^{(i)}_{a}=L^{\prime}+\mu^{(i)}_{a}-a
for
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak L^{\prime}
, and
\mu^{(i)}_{a}=0
for
a>L^{\prime}
.
Clearly, all partitions
\mu^{(1)}\!,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu^{(n)}
are nonzero.

Denote the sequences
(z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k})
,
\big(\lambda^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\lambda^{(k)}\big)
,
\big(\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}\big)
, and
\big(\mu^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu^{(n)}\big)
as
\bar{z}
,
\bar{\lambda}
,
\bar{\alpha}
, and
\bar{\mu}
, respectively. We will say that
V
is a
space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
V
be a space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
. Then

\sum_{a=1}^{k}\big|\lambda^{(a)}\big|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big|\mu^{(i)}\big|.

(3.1)

Here
|\lambda|
denotes the number of boxes in the Young diagram corresponding to the partition
\lambda
.

Proof.

Let
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}}
be some basis of the space
V
. Denote
N_{a}=\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}
. Then

\operatorname{Wr}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}})=x^{\sum_{a=1}^{k}N_{a}z_{a}-\sum_{a,b=1}^{k}N_{a}N_{b}}p(x),

where
p(x)
is a polynomial of degree
\sum_{a=1}^{k}\big|\lambda^{(a)}\big|
. On the other hand, the numbers
\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}
are zeros of
p(x)
with multiplicities
|\mu^{(1)}\big|,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak|\mu^{(n)}\big|
, respectively, and
p(x)
has no other zeros.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that in the case of spaces of quasi-exponentials with the difference data
\big(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu};\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}\big)
, we had to include the condition (
3.1
) into the definition. As Lemma
3.1
shows, in case of quasi-polynomials, this condition holds automatically. This can be explained by the fact that for the space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
, we additionally require the non-degeneracy at
0
.

Remark 3.3
.

Notice that if
V
is a space of quasi-polynomials with some data, then this data
is defined uniquely. This is not the case for spaces of quasi-exponentials with a difference
data, see Example
2.15
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
V
be the span of the functions
f_{1}=x-1
,
f_{2}=(x-1)^{2}
, and
f_{3}=\sqrt{x}(x-1)
. Then
\operatorname{Wr}(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})=-1/4\,x^{-3/2}(x-1)^{3}
. The sequence of exponents of
V
at
1
is
(3,2,1)
. Therefore,
V
is a space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
, where
\bar{z}=(0,1/2)
,
\bar{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
with
\lambda_{1}=(1,1,0,\dots)
,
\lambda_{2}=(1,0,\dots)
,
\bar{\alpha}=(1)
, and
\bar{\mu}=(\mu_{1})
with
\mu_{1}=(1,1,1,0,\dots)
.

3.2
Spaces of quasi-polynomials and quotient differential operator

We will use the following two facts about linear differential operators. For proofs, see for example,
[
11
]
.

1.

Let
f_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{s}(x)
be sufficiently differentiable functions such that
\operatorname{Wr}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{s})\neq 0
. Then there is a unique monic linear differential operator
D=({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\!a_{i}(x)({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s-i}\!
of order
s
such that
Df_{i}=0
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak s
. The coefficients of the operator
D
are given by the formulas

a_{i}(x)=(-1)^{i}\frac{\operatorname{Wr}_{i}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{s})}{\operatorname{Wr}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{s})},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak s,

(3.2)

where
\operatorname{Wr}_{i}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{s})
is the determinant of the
s\times s
matrix whose
j
-th row is
f_{j},({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)f_{j},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s-i-1}f_{j},

({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s-i+1}f_{j},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s}f_{j}
.

2.

Let
V
and
\widehat{V}
be two spaces of functions such that
V\subset\widehat{V}
, and for any
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{m}\in\widehat{V}
,
\operatorname{Wr}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{m})\neq 0
if and only if
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{m}
are linearly independent. Let
D
and
\widehat{D}
be linear differential operators of order
\dim V
and
\dim\widehat{V}
annihilating
V
and
\widehat{V}
, respectively. Then there exists a differential operator
\check{D}
such that
\widehat{D}=\check{D}D
.

Consider a space
V
like in the previous section. By item (1) above, there exists a unique monic differential operator
D_{V}
of order
L^{\prime}
annihilating
V
. We will say that
D_{V}
is

the fundamental differential operator of

V
.

Denote
l_{a}=\lambda^{(a)}_{1}+\big(\lambda^{(a)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}-1
. Introduce a differential operator

\widehat{D}=\prod_{a=1}^{k}\prod_{b=0}^{l_{a}}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}-z_{a}-b\right).

Then the span
\widehat{V}
of the functions
x^{z_{a}+b}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,
b=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{a}
is annihilated by
\widehat{D}
.

Since
V\subset\widehat{V}
, there exists a differential operator
\check{D}_{V}
such that
\widehat{D}=\check{D}_{V}x^{k}D_{V}
, see item (2) in the beginning of the section.

For a differential operator
D=\sum_{i=0}^{s}b_{i}(x)({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)^{s-i}
, define its
formal conjugate

D^{\dagger}
by the formula:

D^{\dagger}f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{s}\left(-\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{s-i}(b_{i}(x)f(x)),

where
f(x)
is any sufficiently differentiable function.

Let
\check{D}_{V}^{\dagger}
be the formal conjugate of
\check{D}_{V}
. Denote
1-\bar{z}-\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}_{1}-\bar{\lambda}_{1}=\big(1-z_{1}-\big(\lambda^{(1)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}-\lambda^{(1)}_{1},1-z_{2}-\big(\lambda^{(2)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}-\lambda^{(2)}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1-z_{k}-\big(\lambda^{(k)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}-\lambda^{(k)}_{1}\big)
. We have the following theorem

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
V
be a space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
. Then there exists a unique space
\mathfrak{T}_{2}(V)
of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(1-\bar{z}-\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}_{1}-\bar{\lambda}_{1},\bar{\lambda}^{\prime};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}^{\prime}\big)
, which is annihilated by
\check{D}_{V}^{\dagger}
.

We will prove Theorem
3.5
in Section
5.1
.

Let us write
\mathcal{P}\big(\!\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
for the set of all spaces of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\!\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
. By Theorem
3.5
, we have a map

\begin{split}\mathfrak{T}_{2}\colon\ \mathcal{P}\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)&\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\big(1-\bar{z}-\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}_{1}-\bar{\lambda}_{1},\bar{\lambda}^{\prime};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}^{\prime}\big),\\
V&\mapsto\mathfrak{T}_{2}(V).\end{split}

In this section, we recall a transformation introduced in

Fix sequences
\bar{z}
,
\bar{\alpha}
,
\bar{\lambda}
, and

p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)

\displaystyle p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})^{(\mu^{(i)})^{\prime}_{1}},

(4.1)

\displaystyle q_{\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}}(x)=\prod_{a=1}^{k}\prod_{b=1}^{(\lambda^{(a)})^{\prime}_{1}}(x-z_{a}-n_{ab}).

(4.2)

Let
V
be a space of quasi-polynomials with the data

.
Let

be the fundamental differential operator of

Lemma 4.1
.

The following holds

1.

The functions
\beta_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\beta_{L^{\prime}}(x)
are rational functions regular at infinity.
Denote
\beta_{a}(\infty)=\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}\beta_{a}(x)

(4.3)

2.

For each

,
p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)\beta_{a}(x)
is a polynomial in
x
.

Proof.

The fact that

3.2
). Notice that
\ker\prod_{b=1}^{(\lambda^{(a)})^{\prime}_{1}}(x({\rm d}/{\rm d}x)-z_{a}-n_{ab})

Part (2) of the lemma follows from formula (
3.2
) and the following observations:

•

Let
g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}}
be a basis of
V
. Denote

. For each

by

(4.4)

where

is a polynomial, and for each

,
\alpha_{i}
is a zero of

of multiplicity not less than
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\big(\mu^{(j)}\big)^{\prime}_{1}
.

•

As noted in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we have

\operatorname{Wr}(g_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{L^{\prime}})=x^{\sum_{a=1}^{k}N_{a}z_{a}-\sum_{a,b=1}^{k}N_{a}N_{b}}p(x),

(4.5)

where
p(x)
is a polynomial, the numbers
\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}
are zeros of
p(x)
with multiplicities
\big|\mu^{(1)}\big|,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\big|\mu^{(n)}\big|
, respectively, and
p(x)
has no other zeros. ∎

We will call the differential operator
\bar{D}_{V}=x^{L^{\prime}}p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)D_{V}

the regularized fundamental differential operator
of
V
.

Let
W
be a space of quasi-exponentials with the difference data

Let

of

.

The following holds.

1.

The coefficients

of

5
Algebra of pseudo-difference operators

A pseudo-difference operator is a formal series of the form

\sum_{m=-\infty}^{M}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{L}C_{lm}x^{l}T^{m},

(5.1)

where
C_{lm}
are some complex numbers. Using the operator relations
T^{m}x^{l}=(x+m)^{l}T^{m}
,
l,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and identifying
(x+m)^{l}
with its Laurent series at infinity, one can multiply series (
5.1
). This multiplication is associative. Denote the algebra of pseudo-difference operators as
\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mathcal{S}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{M}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{L}C_{lm}x^{l}T^{m}
with
C_{LM}\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{S}
is invertible in
\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
.

Proof.

Define
\mathcal{\acute{S}}
by the rule
1+\mathcal{\acute{S}}=C_{LM}^{-1}x^{-L}\mathcal{S}T^{-M}
.
Then
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\mathcal{\acute{S}}^{j}
is a well-defined element of
\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
and the inverse of
\mathcal{S}
is given by the formula:

\mathcal{S}^{-1}=C_{LM}^{-1}T^{-M}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\mathcal{\acute{S}}^{j}\right)x^{-L}.

We consider a difference operator
S=\sum_{i=0}^{M}a_{i}(x)T^{M-i}
with rational coefficients
a_{0}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{M}(x)
as an element of
\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
replacing each
a_{i}(x)
by its Laurent series at infinity. By Lemma
5.1
, if
a_{0}(x)=1
, and
a_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{M}(x)
are regular at infinity, then
S
is invertible in
\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
.

Denote by
\bar{\mathfrak{D}}
the algebra of differential operators with rational coefficients. One can check that the assignment

\tau\colon\ x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\mapsto-x,\qquad x\mapsto T

(5.2)

defines a monomorphism of algebras
\tau\colon\bar{\mathfrak{D}}\rightarrow\Psi\mathfrak{D}_{q}
.

As before, fix sequences
\bar{z}
,
\bar{\alpha}
,
\bar{\lambda}
, and
\bar{\mu}
, where
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k})
is a sequence of complex numbers such that
z_{a}-z_{b}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
a\neq b
,
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})
is a sequence of nonzero complex numbers such that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and
\bar{\lambda}=\big(\lambda^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\lambda^{(k)}\big)
,
\bar{\mu}=\big(\mu^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu^{(n)}\big)
are sequences of non-zero partitions.

Let
V
be a space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)

\bar{D}_{V}\in\bar{\mathfrak{D}}

6
Duality for trigonometric Gaudin

and dynamical Hamiltonians

6.1
\boldsymbol{(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})}
-duality for trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians

Let
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
be the vector
space of
all polynomials in anticommuting variables
\xi_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{n}
.
Since
\xi_{i}\xi_{j}=-\xi_{j}\xi_{i}
for any
i
,
j
, in particular,

\xi_{i}^{2}=0
for any
i
,
the monomials
\xi_{i_{1}}\dots\xi_{i_{l}}
,

1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{l}\leq n
, form
a basis of
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
. Notice that the space
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
coincides with the exterior algebra of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

The left derivations
\partial_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\partial_{n}
on
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
are
linear maps such that

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(\xi_{j_{1}}\dots\xi_{j_{l}})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{s-1}\xi_{j_{1}}\dots\xi_{j_{s-1}}\xi_{j_{s+1}}\dots\xi_{j_{l}},&\text{if $i=j_{s}$ for some }s,\\
=0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6.1)

It is easy to check that

\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=-\partial_{j}\partial_{i}
for any
i
,
j
,
in particular,
\partial_{i}^{2}=0
for any
i
, and

\partial_{i}\xi_{j}+\xi_{j}\partial_{i}=\delta_{ij}
for any
i
,
j
.

Let
e_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, be the standard basis of the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, in particular, we have

[e_{ij},e_{kl}]=\delta_{jk}e_{il}-\delta_{il}e_{kj}
.
Define a
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-action on
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
by
the rule

e_{ij}\mapsto\xi_{i}\partial_{j}
. As a
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module,
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{l=0}^{n}L_{\omega_{l}}
,
where
L_{\omega_{l}}
is the irreducible finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module of highest weight

\omega_{l}=(\underset{l}{\underbrace{1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1}},0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 0).

The component
L_{\omega_{l}}
in
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
is spanned by the monomials of degree
l
.

Remark 6.1
.

As we mentioned in Introduction, the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{n},\mathfrak{gl}_{k})
-duality for integrable systems was first studied in works of Mukhin, Tarasov, and Varchenko for the case, when instead of the space
\mathfrak{X}_{n}
, one considers the space
P_{n}
of polynomials in commuting variables. The latter is also a
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module, and it decomposes into irreducibles as
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\infty}L_{s_{i}}
, where
L_{s_{i}}
is the irreducible finite-dimensional
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
-module of highest weight
s_{i}=(i,0,0,\dots)
.

From now on, we will consider the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{gl}_{k}
together.
We will write superscripts
\langle n\rangle
and
\langle k\rangle
to distinguish objects associated with algebras
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
and
\mathfrak{gl}_{k}
, respectively. For example,
e_{ij}^{\langle n\rangle}
,
i,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
is the basis of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
, and
e_{ab}^{\langle k\rangle}
,
a,b=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,
is the basis of
\mathfrak{gl}_{k}
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
be the vector space of polynomials in
kn
pairwise anticommuting variables
\xi_{ai}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.
We have two vector space isomorphisms
\psi_{1}\colon(\mathfrak{X}_{k})^{\otimes n}\to\mathfrak{P}_{kn},
and
\psi_{2}\colon(\mathfrak{X}_{n})^{\otimes k}\to\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
given by

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\colon\ (p_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes p_{n})\mapsto p_{1}(\xi_{11},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{k1})p_{2}(\xi_{12},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{k2})\cdots p_{n}(\xi_{1n},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{kn}),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}\colon\ (p_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes p_{k})\mapsto p_{1}(\xi_{11},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{1n})p_{2}(\xi_{21},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{2n})\cdots p_{k}(\xi_{k1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\xi_{kn}).

Let
\partial_{ai}
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, be the left derivations on
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
defined similarly to the left derivations
on
\mathfrak{X_{n}}
, see (
6.1
). For any
g\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})
, denote
g_{(i)}=1^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes g\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-i)}\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
. We will identify the algebra
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})
and its image under the
diagonal embedding

g\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{(i)}\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
. We will use similar conventions for
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
.
Define actions of
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
and
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
on
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
by the formulas

\displaystyle\rho^{\langle k,n\rangle}\colon\ \big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{ab}\big)_{(i)}\mapsto\xi_{ai}\partial_{bi},

(6.2)

\displaystyle\rho^{\langle n,k\rangle}\colon\ \big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ij}\big)_{(a)}\mapsto\xi_{ai}\partial_{aj}.

(6.3)

Then
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are isomorphisms of
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
- and
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
-modules, respectively.

For any
i,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
,
i\neq j
, define the following elements of
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}

\displaystyle\Omega^{+}_{(ij)}=\frac{1}{2}\underset{a=1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}\big)_{(i)}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}\big)_{(j)}+\underset{1\leq a<b\leq k}{\sum}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{ab}\big)_{(i)}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{ba}\big)_{(j)},

\displaystyle\Omega^{-}_{(ij)}=\frac{1}{2}\underset{a=1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}\big)_{(i)}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}\big)_{(j)}+\underset{1\leq a<b\leq k}{\sum}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{ba}\big)_{(i)}\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{ab}\big)_{(j)}.

Fix sequences of pairwise distinct complex numbers
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k})
and
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})
. For each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, define
the trigonometric Gaudin Hamiltonians

H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{i}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z})\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
by the following formula:

\displaystyle H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{i}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z})=\sum_{a=1}^{k}\left(z_{a}-\frac{e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}}{2}\right)\big(e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}\big)_{(i)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{i}\Omega^{+}_{(ij)}+\alpha_{j}\Omega^{-}_{(ij)}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}}.

For each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, define
the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians

G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{i}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})\in U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
by the following formula:

\displaystyle G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{i}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})=-\frac{(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii})^{2}}{2}+\sum_{a=1}^{k}z_{a}\big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii}\big)_{(a)}

\displaystyle\hphantom{G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{i}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})= }{}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}}\big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ij}e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ji}-e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii}\big)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq a<b\leq k}\big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ij}\big)_{(a)}(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ji})_{(b)}.

Denote
-\bar{z}+1=(-z_{1}+1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak-z_{k}+1)
. Let
\rho^{\langle k,n\rangle}
and
\rho^{\langle n,k\rangle}
be the
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{k})^{\otimes n}
and
U(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})^{\otimes k}
-actions on
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
defined in formulas (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), respectively. The following can be checked by a straightforward computation.

Proposition 6.2
.

For any
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, we have

\rho^{\langle k,n\rangle}\bigl(H_{i}^{\langle k,n\rangle}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z})\bigr)=-\rho^{\langle n,k\rangle}\bigl(G_{i}^{\langle n,k\rangle}(-\bar{z}+1,\bar{\alpha})\bigr).

is a part of Theorem 4.4 in

, we have the space

of polynomials in

commutative variables, can be found in

6.2
Bethe ansatz method for trigonometric Gaudin model

Fix sequences
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
\sum_{a=1}^{k}l_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
.
Let
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]\subset\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
be the span of all monomials
\xi_{11}^{d_{11}}\cdots\xi_{k1}^{d_{k1}}\cdots\xi_{1n}^{d_{1n}}\cdots\xi_{kn}^{d_{kn}}
such that
\sum_{a=1}^{k}d_{ai}=m_{i}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{ai}=l_{a}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]\neq\{0\}
. We have

\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]=\big\{p\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}\,|\,e^{\langle k\rangle}_{aa}p=l_{a}p,\,e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii}p=m_{i}p,\,a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k,\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n\big\}.

Under the map
\psi_{1}
, the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
correspond to the weight subspace of weight
(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})
of the subrepresentation
L^{\langle k\rangle}_{\omega_{m_{1}}}\otimes\dots\otimes L^{\langle k\rangle}_{\omega_{m_{n}}}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{k}^{\otimes n}=\big({\bigoplus}_{l=0}^{k}L^{\langle k\rangle}_{\omega_{l}}\big)^{\otimes n}
. Similarly, under the map
\psi_{2}
, the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
correspond to the weight subspace of weight
(m_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n})
of the subrepresentation
L^{\langle n\rangle}_{\omega_{l_{1}}}\otimes\dots\otimes L^{\langle n\rangle}_{\omega_{l_{k}}}
of
\mathfrak{X}_{n}^{\otimes k}=\big({\bigoplus}_{l=0}^{n}L^{\langle n\rangle}_{\omega_{l}}\big)^{\otimes k}
.

It is easy to check that all trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians commute with elements
e^{\langle k\rangle}_{11},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e^{\langle k\rangle}_{kk},e^{\langle n\rangle}_{11},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e^{\langle n\rangle}_{nn}
. Therefore,
H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{1}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{n}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z})
,
G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{1}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})
act on the subspace
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
. We will be interested in the common eigenvectors of the Hamiltonians in the subspace
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
.

For each
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
\omega_{m}
be a partition given by
\omega_{m}=(1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1,0,0,\dots)
with
m
ones. Define the sequence
\boldsymbol{l}_{0}=\big(l^{0}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l^{0}_{k}\big)
by
l^{0}_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\omega_{m_{i}})_{a}
.

For any sequence of integers
(c_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak c_{k})
and for each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k-1
, define a transformation

r_{a}\colon\ (c_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak c_{k})\mapsto(c_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak c_{a}-1,c_{a+1}+1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak c_{k}).

Since
\sum_{a=1}^{k}l_{a}=\sum_{a=1}^{k}l^{0}_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
, there exist integers
\bar{l}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{l}_{k-1}
such that
\boldsymbol{l}=r_{1}^{\bar{l}_{1}}\cdots r_{k-1}^{\bar{l}_{k-1}}\boldsymbol{l}_{0}
. It is easy to check that if
\bar{l}_{a}<0
for some
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k-1
, then
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]=0
. Therefore, we can assume that
\bar{l}_{a}\geq 0
for all
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k-1
.

Put

6.4
Bethe ansatz method for XXX-type spin chain model

Fix sequences
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
\sum_{a=1}^{k}l_{a}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}
. Assume that
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]\neq\{0\}
. Unlike in the previous section, we do not assume that
l_{a}\neq 0
and
m_{i}\neq 0
for all
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.
For each
i=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
, define

\bar{m}_{i}=\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\left(m_{j}-\sum_{a=1}^{k}(\omega_{l_{a}})_{j}\right).

(6.18)

The numbers
\bar{m}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{m}_{n-1}
are the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-dual analogs of the numbers
\bar{l}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{l}_{k-1}
, see formula (
6.4
). Recall that
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]\neq\{0\}
implies
\bar{l}_{a}\geq 0
,
a=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k-1
. Similarly,
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]\neq\{0\}
implies
\bar{m}_{i}\geq 0
,
i=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{t}
be a set of
\bar{m}_{1}+\dots+\bar{m}_{n-1}
variables:

\boldsymbol{t}=\big(t^{(1)}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak t^{(1)}_{\bar{m}_{1}},t^{(2)}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak t^{(2)}_{\bar{m}_{2}},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak t^{(n-1)}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak t^{(n-1)}_{\bar{m}_{n-1}}\big).

Fix sequences of pairwise distinct complex numbers
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k})
and
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})
. We have
\bar{m}_{0}=0
. Also, put
\bar{m}_{n}=0
. The XXX Bethe ansatz equations is the following system of
\bar{m}_{1}+\dots+\bar{m}_{n-1}
equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{i+1}}{\alpha_{i}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\

a\neq b\end{subarray}}^{\bar{m}_{i}}\frac{t_{b}^{(i)}-t_{a}^{(i)}-1}{t_{b}^{(i)}-t_{a}^{(i)}+1},\!\!\!

(6.19)

where
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
,
b=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{m}_{i}
.

A solution
\boldsymbol{t}
of the XXX Bethe ansatz equations (
6.19
) is called XXX-admissible if
t^{(i)}_{a}\neq t^{(i)}_{b}
,
t^{(j)}_{a^{\prime}}\neq t^{(j+1)}_{b^{\prime}}
for any
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
,
a,b=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{m}_{i}
,
a\neq b
,
j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-2
,
a^{\prime}=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{m}_{j}
,

.

For each
i,j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i}(x,\boldsymbol{t},\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})=\alpha_{i}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\

(6.20)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{j}(x,\boldsymbol{t},\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{j}\leq n}\mathcal{X}_{i_{1}}(x)\mathcal{X}_{i_{2}}(x-1)\dots\mathcal{X}_{i_{j}}(x-j+1).

In the last formula
\mathcal{X}_{i}(x)=\mathcal{X}_{i}(x,\boldsymbol{t},\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})

i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n

E(u,x,\boldsymbol{t},\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})=u^{n}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{E}_{j}(x,\boldsymbol{t},\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})u^{n-i},

4

\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]

\rho^{Y}_{\bar{z}}\colon Y(\mathfrak{gl}_{n})\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{P}_{kn})

The images of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians under the action

\boldsymbol{t}
is an XXX-admissible solution of the XXX Bethe ansatz equations (

is a common eigenvector of the dynamical Hamiltonians, and the corresponding eigenvalue can be computed using
[
4

j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}m_{i}m_{j}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}}-\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2},

(6.23)

6.6
Quotient difference operator and duality for trigonometric Gaudin

and dynamical Hamiltonians

Fix a pair
(\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m})
like in the previous section. Let the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
be like in Section
6.3
. Let
V
be a Gaudin admissible space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
.

Recall the maps
\mathfrak{T}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{3}
, see formulas (
2.15
) and (
4.6
), respectively. Set
W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V))
. Then
W
is a space of quasi-exponentials with the difference data
\big(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}^{\prime};-\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}\big)
. In this section, we will relate the map
V\mapsto W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V))
with the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality of the trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians.

We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 6.11
.

For generic
\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}
, the common eigenspaces
of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians
G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{1}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})

in
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
are one-dimensional.

Proof.

For every monomial
p\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
, we have

\big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii}\big)_{(a)}p=m^{a}_{i}(p)p
for some
m^{a}_{i}(p)\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Moreover, if
p\neq p^{\prime}
, there exist
i
,
a
such that
m^{a}_{i}(p)\neq m^{a}_{i}(p^{\prime})
. Thus, if
z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k}
are linearly independent
over
\mathbb{Z}
, the common eigenspaces of the operators
K_{i}=\sum_{a=1}^{k}z_{a}\big(e^{\langle n\rangle}_{ii}\big)_{(a)}
,

i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, in
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
are one-dimensional.
Therefore, the common eigenspaces of the
operators
G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{1}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}(\bar{z},\bar{\alpha})

in
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}
are one-dimensional
provided that
z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k}
are sufficiently large positive numbers linearly independent
over
\mathbb{Z}
.
Hence, the common eigenspaces for generic
\bar{\alpha}
,
\bar{z}
are one-dimensional.
∎

Let
v_{V}\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
be the Bethe vector corresponding to
V
, see Section
6.3
. Assume that
v_{V}\neq 0
. Then the vector
v_{V}
is an eigenvector of the trigonometric Gaudin Hamiltonians
H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{1}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}+\boldsymbol{l}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{n}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}+\boldsymbol{l})
. Denote the associated eigenvalues as
h^{V}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h^{V}_{k}
, respectively.

Assume that the space
W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V))
is XXX-admissible. Let
v_{W}\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
be the Bethe vector corresponding to
W
, see Section
6.5
. Assume that
v_{W}\neq 0
. Then the vector
v_{W}
is an eigenvector of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians
G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{1}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big)
. Denote the associated eigenvalues as
g^{W}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g^{W}_{n}
, respectively.

Theorem 6.12
.

The following holds:

h^{V}_{i}=-g^{W}_{i},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n.

(6.32)

Before proving the theorem, let us discuss how it explains the relation between the map
V\mapsto W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V))
and the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality. By Proposition
6.2
, for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, we have

G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big)v_{V}=-H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{i}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}+\boldsymbol{l})v_{V}=-h^{V}_{i}v_{V}.

(6.33)

Therefore, starting with the space
V
and the corresponding vector
v_{V}
, we have two different ways to obtain a common eigenvector of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians. First, by the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality,
v_{V}
is itself a common eigenvector of the dynamical Hamiltonians, see formula (
6.33
). Second, the map
V\mapsto W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V))
gives the vector
v_{W}
. Theorem
6.12
and Lemma
6.11
assure that for generic
\bar{z},\bar{\alpha}
, these two eigenvectors are the same up to a constant multiple.

Indeed, comparing formulas (
6.32
) and (
6.33
), we have

G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big)v_{V}=g^{W}_{i}v_{V},

which means that the vectors
v_{V}
and
v_{W}
belong to the same eigenspace. Then Lemma
6.11
implies that
v_{W}
is proportional to
v_{V}
.

Proof of Theorem
6.12
.

Denote
U=\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V)\in\mathcal{E}\big(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu};\bar{z}+\bar{\lambda^{\prime}}_{1},\bar{\lambda}\big)
.
By Lemma
4.2
, the fundamental difference operator
S_{U}=T^{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}b_{i}(x)T^{M-i}
of
U
has rational coefficients
b_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak b_{M}(x)
, which are regular at infinity. Therefore, there exist polynomials
B_{0}(u),B_{1}(u),B_{2}(u),\dots
such that

S_{U}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}B_{a}(T).

(6.34)

Moreover, Lemma
4.2
gives an explicit formula for the polynomial
B_{0}(x)

B_{0}(u)=p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}}.

(6.35)

Consider the regularized fundamental difference operator
\bar{S}_{U}=q_{\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}}(x)S_{U}
of
U
, where
q_{\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}}(x)\allowbreak=\prod_{a=1}^{k}(x-z_{a}-l_{a})
, see Section
4

\deg q_{\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}}(x)=k
, the coefficients
\bar{b}_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\bar{b}_{M}(x)
in the expansion
\bar{S}_{U}=T^{M}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\bar{b}_{i}(x)T^{M-i}
are polynomials in
x
of degree at most
k
.

Define numbers
A_{ia}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak M
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
by
\bar{S}_{U}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{k}A_{ia}x^{a}T^{i}
. Then we have

S_{U}=\frac{1}{\prod_{a=1}^{k}(x-z_{a}-l_{a})}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{k}A_{ia}x^{a}T^{i}.

(6.36)

Denote
\sum_{a=1}^{k}(z_{a}+l_{a})=Z
. Comparing formulas (
6.34
) and (
6.36
), we get

\displaystyle B_{0}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_{i,k}u^{i},\qquad B_{1}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}(A_{i,k-1}+ZA_{i,k})u^{i},

\displaystyle B_{2}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\big(A_{i,k-2}+ZA_{i,k-1}+Z^{2}A_{i,k}\big)u^{i}.

(6.37)

Let
\bar{D}_{V}
be the regularized fundamental differential operator of
V
. Since
U=\mathfrak{T}_{3}(V)
, by Theorem
4.3
, we have

\bar{D}_{V}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{a=1}^{k}A_{ia}x^{i}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{a}.

(6.38)

Let
D_{V}
be the fundamental differential operator of
V
. We have
\bar{D}_{V}=p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)\big(x^{k}D_{V}\big)
, where
p_{\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}}
, see Section
4
. Write

x^{k}D_{V}=\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k}+\sum_{a=1}^{k}\beta_{a}(x)\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k-a}.

Then formula (
6.38
) gives

\beta_{a}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}A_{i,k-a}x^{i}}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(x-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}}},\qquad a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k.

(6.39)

By Proposition
6.5
, we have

h^{V}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}(x)-\beta_{2}(x)\right)+\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}-m_{i}.

(6.40)

Using formulas (
6.37
), (
6.35
), and (
6.39

\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}(x)-\beta_{2}(x)\right)=\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{B^{2}_{1}(u)}{B^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{B_{2}(u)}{B_{0}(u)}\right).

Therefore, formula (
6.40

h^{V}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{B^{2}_{1}(u)}{B^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{B_{2}(u)}{B_{0}(u)}\right)+\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}-m_{i}.

(6.41)

Consider the space
W=\mathfrak{T}_{1}(U)\in\mathcal{E}\big(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}^{\prime};-\bar{z},\bar{\lambda}^{\prime}\big)
.
We have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}+1}=S_{W}^{\ddagger}S_{U},

(6.42)

where the involutive automorphism
(\cdot)^{\ddagger}
is defined in formula (
5.3
).

The fundamental difference operator
S_{W}
of

can be written in the form

S_{W}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}E_{a}(T).

Substituting this into formula (
6.42
), we have

\prod_{i=1}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}+1}=\left(\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}E_{a}(T)(-x)^{-a}\right)\left(\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}B_{a}(T)\right).

Writing the right hand side of the last formula in the form
\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}P_{a}(T)
with some polynomials

and comparing it to the left hand side, we see that

for all

E_{0}(u)B_{0}(u)=P_{0}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}+1}.

(6.43)

From

E_{0}(u)B_{1}(u)-E_{1}(u)B_{0}(u)=0.

(6.44)

From

E_{2}(u)B_{0}(u)+E_{0}(u)B_{2}(u)+uE^{\prime}_{1}(u)B_{0}(u)-uE^{\prime}_{0}(u)B_{1}(u)-E_{1}(u)B_{1}(u)=0.

(6.45)

In the last formula we used that for every polynomial
P(u)
, we have

P(T)x^{-1}=x^{-1}P(T)-x^{-2}TP^{\prime}(T)+\sum_{a\geq 3}x^{-a}\tilde{P}_{a}(T)

for some polynomials
\tilde{P}_{3}(u),\tilde{P}_{4}(u),\dots
.

Using relations (
6.44
) and (
6.45
), one can check

\frac{1}{2}\frac{B^{2}_{1}(u)}{B^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{B_{2}(u)}{B_{0}(u)}=-\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{E^{2}_{1}(u)}{E^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{E_{2}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)+u\left(\frac{E_{1}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)^{\prime}.

Therefore, formula (
6.41
) gives

\displaystyle h^{V}_{i}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{E^{2}_{1}(u)}{E^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{E_{2}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)+\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(u\left(\frac{E_{1}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)^{\prime}\right)+\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}-m_{i}.

(6.46)

Let
g^{W}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g^{W}_{n}
be the eigenvalues of the trigonometric dynamical Hamiltonians
G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{1}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{n}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big)
, respectively, associated with the Bethe vector
v_{W}
. By Proposition
6.10
, we have

g^{W}_{i}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\frac{E_{2}(u)}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}m_{i}m_{j}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}}-\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}.

(6.47)

We will use again
[
4
, Proposition B.1]
, which gives the following explicit formula for the quotient
E_{1}(u)/\prod_{i=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i})
:

\frac{E_{1}(u)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i})}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\alpha_{j}m_{j}}{\alpha_{j}-u}.

(6.48)

From formulas (
6.35
) and (
6.43
), we get

E_{0}(u)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i}).

(6.49)

Using (
6.48
) and (
6.49
), we can rewrite (
6.47
) in the following way:

g^{W}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{E^{2}_{1}(u)}{E^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{E_{2}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)-\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}.

(6.50)

Using (
6.48
) and (
6.49
) again, we compute

\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(u\left(\frac{E_{1}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)^{\prime}\right)=m_{i}.

(6.51)

Comparing formulas (
6.46
), (
6.50
), and (
6.51
), we get
(
6.32
).
Theorem
6.12
is proved.

6.7
Non-reduced data

In the previous section, we related the quotient difference operator and the
(\mathfrak{gl}_{k},\mathfrak{gl}_{n})
-duality of the trigonometric Gaudin and dynamical Hamiltonians acting on the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
, where
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n})
are such that
l_{a}\neq 0
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
and
m_{i}\neq 0
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. In this section, we are going to extend this result to all nontrivial subspaces
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
, that is, we are going to include the cases when some
l_{a}
,
m_{i}
are zero.

Fix
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}_{\geq 0}
. For each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, let
q_{a}(x)
be a polynomial of degree
l_{a}
such that
q_{a}(0)\neq 0
. Fix complex numbers
z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{k}
such that
z_{a}-z_{b}\notin\mathbb{Z}
if
a\neq b
. Denote by
V
the space spanned by the functions
x^{z_{a}}q_{a}(x)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
.

Define

V^{\mathrm{red}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
l_{a}=0\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}-z_{a}\right)V.

Denote
k^{\prime}=\dim V^{\mathrm{red}}
. Fix
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
. Let
(e_{1}>\dots>e_{k})
be the sequence of exponents of
V
at
\alpha
, and let
\big(e^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}>\dots>e^{\mathrm{red}}_{k^{\prime}}\big)
be the sequence of exponents of
V^{\mathrm{red}}
at
\alpha
.

Lemma 6.13
.

Define a partition
\mu=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots)
by
e^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}=k^{\prime}+\mu_{a}-a
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k^{\prime}
,
\mu_{k^{\prime}+1}=0
. Then
e_{a}=k+\mu_{a}-a
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
.

Conversely, if a partition
\mu
is such that
e_{a}=k+\mu_{a}-a
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, then
\mu_{k^{\prime}+1}=0
and
e^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}=k^{\prime}+\mu_{a}-a
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the lemma for the case when
l_{1}=0
, and
l_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k}
are not zero.
Let
D_{V}
and
D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}
be the monic linear differential operators of order
k
and
k-1
, respectively, annihilating
V
and
V^{\mathrm{red}}
, respectively. Then

x^{k}D_{V}=x^{k-1}D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}-z_{1}\right).

(6.52)

Define functions
b_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak b_{k}(x)
,
b_{1}^{\mathrm{red}}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak b_{k-1}^{\mathrm{red}}(x)
by

\displaystyle x^{k}D_{V}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\frac{b_{a}(x)}{(x-\alpha)^{a}}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k-a},

\displaystyle x^{k-1}D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}=\sum_{a=0}^{k-1}\frac{b^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}(x)}{(x-\alpha)^{a}}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k-1-a}.

Using formulas (
3.2
), (
4.4
), and (
4.5
), one can check that
b_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak b_{k}(x)
,
b_{1}^{\mathrm{red}}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak b_{k-1}^{\mathrm{red}}(x)
are regular at
\alpha
. Define polynomials
I(r)
and
I^{\mathrm{red}}(r)
by

\displaystyle I(r)=\sum_{a=1}^{k}b_{a}(\alpha)\alpha^{k-a}r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-k+a+1),

\displaystyle I^{\mathrm{red}}(r)=\sum_{a=1}^{k-1}b^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}(\alpha)\alpha^{k-1-a}r(r-1)(r-2)\cdots(r-k+a+2).

Notice that
\{e_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e_{k}\}
is the set of roots of the polynomial
I(r)
. Indeed, substituting a series
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}A_{i}(x-\alpha)^{i+r}
into the differential equation
D_{V}f=0
, and looking at the coefficient for the lowest power of
(x-\alpha)
, we get
I(r)=0
. Similarly,
\big\{e^{\mathrm{red}}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak e^{\mathrm{red}}_{k^{\prime}}\big\}
is the set of roots of the polynomial
I^{\mathrm{red}}(r)
. The polynomials
I(r)
and
I^{\mathrm{red}}(r)
are called the indicial polynomials of the differential equations
D_{V}f=0
and
D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}f=0
, respectively.

Using formula (
6.52
), we obtain the following relations:

b_{a}(x)=b_{a}^{\mathrm{red}}(x)-z_{1}(x-\alpha)b_{a-1}^{\mathrm{red}}(x),\qquad a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k,

(6.53)

where we assume that
b^{\mathrm{red}}_{k}(x)=0
. Relations (
6.53
) imply
b_{a}(\alpha)=b_{a}^{\mathrm{red}}(\alpha)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. Since
D_{V}
and
D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}
are monic, we also have
b_{0}(x)=b^{\mathrm{red}}_{0}(x)=1
. Therefore,
I(r)=rI^{\mathrm{red}}(r-1)
, which implies the lemma.
∎

Let
\{\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n}\}
be a set including all non-zero singular points of
V
. Assume that
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
if
i\neq j
, and
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. Suppose that for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, the sequence of exponents of
V
at
\alpha_{i}
is given by

(k,k-1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k-m_{i}+1,k-m_{i}-1,k-m_{i}-2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1,0)

for some
m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
0\leq m_{i}\leq k
.

Define a sequence of partitions
\bar{\lambda}=\big(\lambda^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\lambda^{(k)}\big)
by
\lambda^{(a)}=(l_{a},0,0,\dots)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. Define a sequence of partitions
\bar{\mu}=\big(\mu^{(1)},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mu^{(n)}\big)
by
\mu^{(i)}=(1,1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1,0,0,\dots)
with
m_{i}
ones,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
. Define sequences
\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}}
,
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}
,
\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}}
, and
\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}}
by removing all zero partitions from the sequences
\bar{\lambda}
,
\bar{\mu}
, and removing corresponding numbers from the sequences
\bar{z}=(z_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak z_{n})
,
\bar{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})
. We will call the data
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)
reduced if
\big(\bar{z},\bar{\lambda};\bar{\alpha},\bar{\mu}\big)=\big(\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}},\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}};\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}},\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)
, and non-reduced otherwise.

Proposition 6.14
.

V^{\mathrm{red}}
is a space of quasi-polynomials with the data
\big(\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}},\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}};\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}},\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)
.

Proof.

Recall that
V
is spanned by the functions
x^{z_{a}}q_{a}(x)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, where
q_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak q_{k}(x)
are polynomials such that
\deg q_{a}=l_{a}
, and
q_{a}(0)\neq 0
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. Then the space
V^{\mathrm{red}}
is spanned by the functions
x^{z_{a}}\tilde{q}_{a}(x)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, where

\tilde{q}_{b}(x)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a=1\\
l_{a}=0\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}+z_{b}-z_{a}\right)q_{b}(x).

(6.54)

If
l_{b}\neq 0
, then for each
a
in the product on the left hand side of formula (
6.54
), we have
z_{b}-z_{a}\notin\mathbb{Z}
, which yields
\deg\tilde{q}_{a}(x)=\deg q_{a}(x)
,
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
. If
l_{b}=0
, then formula (
6.54
) implies
\tilde{q}_{b}(x)=0
. This shows that the space
V^{\mathrm{red}}
has a basis

\big\{x^{z_{a}}\tilde{q}_{a}(x)\,|\,z_{a}\text{ is present in }\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}}\big\},

and the degrees of the polynomials
\tilde{q}_{a}(x)
appearing in this basis correspond to the sequence
\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}}
.

Notice that
\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}}
is the set of all singular points of
V
, and the sequences of exponents of
V
at these points correspond to the sequence
\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}
. Therefore, the proposition follows from Lemma
6.13
.
∎

Recall the maps
\mathfrak{T}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{T}_{3}
, see (
2.15
) and (
4.6
), respectively. Set
W^{\mathrm{red}}=\mathfrak{T}_{1}\big(\mathfrak{T}_{3}\big(V^{\mathrm{red}}\big)\big)
. Then
W^{\mathrm{red}}
is a space of quasi-exponentials with the difference data
\big(\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}},\big(\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)^{\prime};\allowbreak-\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}},\big(\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)^{\prime}\big)
. We are going to construct a space
W
such that

W^{\mathrm{red}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i})W.

For this we will need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.15
.

Fix
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, and a polynomial
p(x)
. Assume that
\alpha\neq\beta
. Then there exists a unique polynomial
\tilde{p}(x)
such that
\deg\tilde{p}(x)=\deg p(x)
, and

(T-\beta)\alpha^{x}\tilde{p}(x)=\alpha^{x}p(x).

(6.55)

Proof.

Relation (
6.55
) is the same as relation

\alpha\tilde{p}(x+1)-\beta\tilde{p}(x)=p(x).

(6.56)

Let
a_{0},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak a_{m}
be the coefficients of
p(x)
:
p(x)=a_{m}x^{m}+a_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\dots+a_{1}x+a_{0}
.
Substituting a polynomial
\tilde{p}(x)=\tilde{a}_{m}x^{m}+\tilde{a}_{m-1}x^{m-1}+\dots+\tilde{a}_{1}x+\tilde{a}_{0}
into equation (
6.56
) and comparing coefficients for powers of
x
, we get

\tilde{a}_{m-i}(\alpha-\beta)=a_{m-i}-\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{m-j}{m-i}\tilde{a}_{m-j},\qquad i=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m,

which is a recursion that allows to find the numbers
\tilde{a}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{a}_{n}
uniquely.
∎

For any
\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, define a linear operator
(T-\beta)^{-1}
on the space spanned by all functions of the form
\alpha^{x}p(x)
, where
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
,
\alpha\neq\beta
, and
p(x)
is a polynomial, by the formula

(T-\beta)^{-1}\alpha^{x}p(x)=\alpha^{x}\tilde{p}(x),

where
\tilde{p}(x)
is the polynomial from Lemma
6.15
.

Let
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{l}\leq n
be such that
m_{i}=0
if
i=i_{s}
for some
s=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l
, and
m_{i}\neq 0
otherwise. Denote by
W
the space spanned by the functions

(T-\alpha_{i_{1}})^{-1}(T-\alpha_{i_{2}})^{-1}\dots(T-\alpha_{i_{l}})^{-1}f,\qquad f\in W^{\mathrm{red}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{i_{1}}^{x},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{i_{l}}^{x}.

Let
S_{W}
be the fundamental difference operator of
W
. Let
S_{W^{\mathrm{red}}}
be the fundamental difference operator of
W^{\mathrm{red}}
. Then we have

S_{W}=S_{W^{\mathrm{red}}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}^{n}(T-\alpha_{i}).

(6.57)

Together with Lemma
2.5
, this shows that the order of
\alpha_{i_{1}},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\alpha_{i_{l}}
in the definition of
W
does not matter.

Recall that
W^{\mathrm{red}}
is a space of quasi-exponentials with the difference data
\big(\bar{\alpha}^{\mathrm{red}},\big(\bar{\mu}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)^{\prime};\allowbreak-\bar{z}^{\mathrm{red}},\big(\bar{\lambda}^{\mathrm{red}}\big)^{\prime}\big)
. Then the equality
\deg\tilde{p}(x)=\deg p(x)
in Lemma
6.15
implies that the space
W
has a basis of the form

\big\{\alpha_{i}^{x}r_{i}(x),\,i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n\big\},

where
r_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak r_{n}(x)
are polynomials such that
\deg r_{i}(x)=m_{i}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

Fix
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
(\tilde{e}_{1}>\dots>\tilde{e}_{n})
be the sequence of discrete exponents of
W
at
z
. Denote
n^{\prime}=n-l=\dim W^{\mathrm{red}}
. Let
\big(\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}>\dots>\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{n^{\prime}}\big)
be the sequence of discrete exponents of
W^{\mathrm{red}}
at
z
.

Lemma 6.16
.

Define a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots)
by
\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}=n^{\prime}+\lambda_{i}-i
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n^{\prime}
,
\lambda_{n^{\prime}+1}=0
. Then
\tilde{e}_{i}=n+\lambda_{i}-i
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

Conversely, if a partition
\lambda
is such that
\tilde{e}_{i}=n+\lambda_{i}-i
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
, then
\lambda_{n^{\prime}+1}=0
and
\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}=n^{\prime}+\lambda_{i}-i
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the Lemma for the case
m_{1}=0
, and
m_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n}
are not zero.

Let
f_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n-1}(x)
be a basis of
W^{\mathrm{red}}
such that for each
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
,
T^{j}f_{i}(z)=0
,
j=0,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}-1
, and
T^{\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}}f_{i}(z)\neq 0
. Set

\tilde{f}_{i}(x)=(T-\alpha_{1})^{-1}f_{i}(x)-\alpha_{1}^{x-z}(T-\alpha_{i})^{-1}f_{i}(z),\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n.

Then
\tilde{f}_{i}(x)\in W
,
(T-\alpha_{1})\tilde{f}_{i}(x)=f_{i}(x)
, and
\tilde{f}_{i}(z)=0
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-1
.

Since
T^{j}-\alpha_{1}^{j}=\big(\sum_{s=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{1}^{j-1-s}T^{s}\big)(T-\alpha_{1})
, we have

T^{j}\tilde{f}_{i}(x)=\alpha_{1}^{j}\tilde{f}_{i}(x)+\sum_{s=0}^{j-1}\alpha_{1}^{j-1-s}T^{s}f_{i}(x).

The last relation implies
T^{j}\tilde{f}_{i}(z)=0
,

, and
T^{\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}+1}\tilde{f}_{i}(z)=T^{\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{i}}f_{i}(z)\neq 0
.

Since
\{\alpha_{1}^{x},\tilde{f}_{1}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{f}_{n-1}(x)\}
is a basis of
W
, the sequence of discrete exponents of
W
at
z
is given by

\big(\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}+1>\dots>\tilde{e}^{\mathrm{red}}_{n-1}+1>0\big),

which implies the lemma.
∎

Notice that for each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, the sequence of discrete exponents of
W^{\mathrm{red}}
at
-z_{a}
is given by

(n^{\prime},n^{\prime}-1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n^{\prime}-l_{a}+1,n^{\prime}-l_{a}-1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1,0).

Therefore, by Lemma
6.16
, for each
a=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak k
, the sequence of discrete exponents of
W
at
-z_{a}
is given by

(n,n-1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n-l_{a}+1,n-l_{a}-1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak 1,0).

Consider the space
\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
, where
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{m}=(m_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m_{n})
. One can repeat all constructions in Section
6.3
for the space
V
. Assume that
V
satisfies conditions similar to those for a Gaudin admissible space in Section
6.3
. Then we obtain a vector
v_{V}\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
such that

H^{\langle k,n\rangle}_{i}(\bar{\alpha},\bar{z}+\boldsymbol{l})v_{V}=h_{i}^{V}v_{V},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n

for some numbers
h^{V}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{n}^{V}
. We will assume that

.

Similarly, one can repeat all constructions in Section
6.5
for the space
W
. Assume that
W
satisfies conditions similar to those for an XXX-admissible space in Section
6.5
. Then we obtain a vector
v_{W}\in\mathfrak{P}_{kn}[\boldsymbol{l},\boldsymbol{m}]
such that

G^{\langle n,k\rangle}_{i}\big({-}\bar{z}-\boldsymbol{l}+\bar{1},\bar{\alpha}\big)v_{W}=g_{i}^{W}v_{W},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n

for some numbers
g_{1}^{W},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n}^{W}
. We will assume that
v_{W}\neq 0
.

Theorem 6.17
.

The following holds:

h_{i}^{V}=-g_{i}^{W},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n.

Proof.

Define functions
\beta_{0}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\beta_{k}(x)
,
\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{0}(x),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{k^{\prime}}(x)
by

x^{k}D_{V}=\sum_{a=0}^{k}\beta_{a}(x)\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k-a},\qquad x^{k^{\prime}}D_{V^{\mathrm{red}}}=\sum_{a=0}^{k^{\prime}}\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}(x)\left(x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\right)^{k^{\prime}-a}.

The eigenvalues
h_{1}^{V},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{n}^{W}
can be expressed through
\beta_{1}(x)
,
\beta_{2}(x)
using the same formula as in the case of reduced data, see (
6.9
). For convenience, we repeat this formula here:

h_{i}^{V}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}(x)-\beta_{2}(x)\right)+\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}-m_{i}.

Define also the following numbers:

h^{V,\mathrm{red}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\big(\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}\big)^{2}(x)-\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{2}(x)\right)+\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}-m_{i}.

Suppose that
l_{1}=0
, and
l_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k}
are not zero. Relation (
6.52
) implies

\beta_{1}=\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{red}}-z_{1},\qquad\beta_{2}=\beta_{2}^{\mathrm{red}}-z_{1}\beta_{1}^{\mathrm{red}}.

Using the last two formulas, it is easy to check that

\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta_{1}^{2}(x)-\beta_{2}(x)\right)=\operatorname{Res}_{x=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\big(\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}\big)^{2}(x)-\beta^{\mathrm{red}}_{2}(x)\right).

(6.58)

By induction, formula (
6.58
) holds for any
l_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak l_{k}
. Therefore, we have
h_{i}^{V}=h^{V,\mathrm{red}}_{i}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

Define polynomials
E_{0}(u),E_{1}(u),E_{2}(u),\dots
,
E^{\mathrm{red}}_{0}(u),E^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}(u),E^{\mathrm{red}}_{2}(u),\dots
by

S_{W}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}E_{a}(T),\qquad S_{W^{\mathrm{red}}}=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{-a}E^{\mathrm{red}}_{a}(T).

The eigenvalues
g_{1}^{W},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak g_{n}^{W}
can be expressed through
E_{1}(u)
,
E_{2}(u)
using the same formula as in the case of reduced data, see (
6.50
). For convenience, we repeat this formula here:

g_{i}^{W}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{E^{2}_{1}(u)}{E^{2}_{0}(u)}-\frac{E_{2}(u)}{E_{0}(u)}\right)-\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}.

Define also the following numbers

g^{W,\mathrm{red}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}\operatorname{Res}_{u=\alpha_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\frac{\big(E^{\mathrm{red}}_{1}(u)\big)^{2}}{\big(E^{\mathrm{red}}_{0}(u)\big)^{2}}-\frac{E^{\mathrm{red}}_{2}(u)}{E^{\mathrm{red}}_{0}(u)}\right)-\frac{m_{i}^{2}}{2}.

Using relation (
6.57
), we have

E_{a}(u)=E_{a}^{\mathrm{red}}(u)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
m_{i}=0\end{subarray}}^{n}(u-\alpha_{i}),

which implies
g_{i}^{W}=g^{W,\mathrm{red}}_{i}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

In the proof of Theorem
6.12
, we already checked that
h^{V,\mathrm{red}}_{i}=-g^{W,\mathrm{red}}_{i}
for all
i
such that
m_{i}\neq 0
. If
m_{i}=0
, then
h^{V,\mathrm{red}}_{i}=g^{W,\mathrm{red}}_{i}=0
. Therefore, we have
h_{i}^{V}=-g_{i}^{W}
,
i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n
.

Theorem
6.17
is proved.
∎

Appendix A
Discrete Wronskian identities

In this section, we collect discrete Wronskian identities that were used in the paper. Identities (
A.1
)–(
A.4
) with proofs can also be found in
[
8
, Appendix B]
.

Recall that
T
is the shift operator defined by
Tf(x)=f(x+1)
. Recall that for any functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
, the discrete Wronskian
\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})
is the determinant of the matrix
\big(T^{j-1}f_{i}\big)_{i,j=1}^{n}
. Denote
T^{(n)}f=f(Tf)\big(T^{2}f\big)\cdots\big(T^{n-1}f\big)
.
We have the following obvious relations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(hf_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak hf_{n})=\big(T^{(n)}h\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})\qquad\text{for any }h,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(1,f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})=\mathcal{W}{\rm r}((T-1)f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak(T-1)f_{n}).

(A.2)

Assume that
f_{1}\neq 0
. Combining formulas (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), we get

\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},f_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n})=\big(T^{(n)}f_{1}\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\left((T-1)\frac{f_{2}}{f_{1}},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak(T-1)\frac{f_{n}}{f_{1}}\right).

(A.3)

Proposition A.1
.

For any functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n}
,
h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{m}
, where
f_{1}\neq 0
, the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{m}))

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(T^{(m-1)}\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(Tf_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak Tf_{n})\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{m}).

(A.4)

Proof.

We will prove the proposition by induction on
n
.
Let
n=1
. Denote
f_{1}=f
. Using formula (
A.3
), we compute

\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f,h_{i})=\big(T^{(2)}f\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\left((T-1)\frac{h_{i}}{f}\right)=\big(T^{(2)}f\big)(T-1)\frac{h_{i}}{f},\qquad i=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f,h_{1}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f,h_{m}))

\displaystyle=\big(T^{(m)}T^{(2)}f\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\left((T-1)\frac{h_{1}}{f},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak(T-1)\frac{h_{m}}{f}\right)

\displaystyle=\big(T^{(m-1)}Tf\big)\big(T^{(m+1)}f\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\left((T-1)\frac{h_{1}}{f},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak(T-1)\frac{h_{m}}{f}\right)

\displaystyle=\big(T^{(m-1)}Tf\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{m}).

Assume that formula (
A.4
) is true for some
n\geq 1
. For functions
f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n+1}
,
h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{m}
, define
\tilde{f}_{i}=(T-1)(f_{i}/f_{1})
,
\tilde{h}_{j}=(T-1)(h_{j}/f_{1})
,
i=2,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak n+1
,
j=1,\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak m
. Then we compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n+1},h_{1}),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n+1},h_{m}))

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(T^{(m)}T^{(n+2)}f_{1}\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{f}_{n+1},\tilde{h}_{1}\big),\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{f}_{n+1},\tilde{h}_{m}\big)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(T^{(m)}T^{(n+2)}f_{1}\big)\big(T^{(m-1)}\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(T\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak T\tilde{f}_{n+1}\big)\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{f}_{n+1},\tilde{h}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{m}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(T^{(m-1)}\big[\big(T^{(n+1)}Tf_{1}\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(T\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak T\tilde{f}_{n+1}\big)\big]\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}\times\big(T^{(n+m+1)}f_{1}\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}\big(\tilde{f}_{2},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{f}_{n+1},\tilde{h}_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak\tilde{h}_{m}\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\big(T^{(m-1)}\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(Tf_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak Tf_{n+1})\big)\mathcal{W}{\rm r}(f_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak f_{n+1},h_{1},\allowbreak\dots,\allowbreak h_{m}).

(A.5)

Here, on the first step, we used formulas (
A.1
) and (
A.3
), on the second step, we used the assumption hypothesis, on the third step, we used

T^{(m)}T^{(n+2)}f_{1}=\big(T^{(m-1)}T^{(n+1)}Tf_{1}\big)\big(T^{(n+m+1)}f_{1}\big),

and on the fourth step, we used formula (
A.3
) again.

Computation (
A.5
) proves the induction step finishing the proof of the proposition.∎
