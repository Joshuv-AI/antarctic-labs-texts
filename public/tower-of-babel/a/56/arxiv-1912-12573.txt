Introduction

Base 2 plays a key role in combinatorial game theory.
Specifically, the Sprague-Grundy function of the disjunctive sum of two impartial games is equal to the Nim-sum of their Sprague-Grundy functions.
Here a Nim-sum is the result of addition without carrying in base 2.
In particular, the Sprague-Grundy value of a position in Nim equals the Nim-sum of the heap sizes.
It is rare that the Sprague-Grundy function of an impartial game can be written explicitly like Nim.
Another well-known example is Welter’s game, a subtraction game like Nim;
Welter
[
20
]
gave an explicit formula for its Sprague-Grundy function by using the binary numeral system (see Theorem
2.7
).

A few games related to base
p
have also been found,
where
p
is an integer greater than 1, not necessarily prime.
For example, Flanigan found a game, called
\text{Rim}_{p}
;
the Sprague-Grundy value of a position in
\text{Rim}_{p}
equals the
p
-Nim-sum of the heap sizes, where a
p
-Nim-sum is the result of addition without carrying in base
p
.

\text{Rim}_{{p}}
was devised by James A. Flanigan in an unpublished paper entitled “NIM, TRIM and RIM.”

We use

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\Gamma

We can consider
\Gamma

Before the game, we put a token on a starting position

The first player moves the token from
{A}
to its option
{B}
.
Similarly, the second player moves the token from
{B}
to its option
{C}
.
In this way, the two players alternately move the token.
The winner is the player who moves the token last.
For example, let
\Gamma
be the impartial game with position set
\set{1,2,3,4}

and edge set
\set{(1,2),(2,3),(1,4)}

The first player can move the token to either position 2 or 4.

Thus she should move the token to 4.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Gamma^{1}
be a subtraction game
\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}})
with
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq{\mathscr{C}}_{{m}}^{({p})}
,
and let
\Gamma=\Gamma^{1}+{\mathcal{N}}_{1}
and
\tilde{\Gamma}
be a
p
-saturation of
\Gamma
.
Suppose that

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}}({A},{a})=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}({A})\oplus_{p}a

(7)

for every position
({A},{a})
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
.
If
{B}
is a proper descendant of
{A}
in
\Gamma^{1}
, then

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}({A})-\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}({B})\equiv\sum_{i}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}},

(8)

where
{N}=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{p}({A}-{B})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}
is a
p
-saturation of
\Gamma^{1}
.

proof.

We may assume that
\tilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma({\mathcal{P}}\times{\mathbb{N}},{\mathscr{C}}_{{m}+1}^{({p})})
and

\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}=\Gamma({\mathcal{P}},{\mathscr{C}}_{{m}}^{({p})})
since
{\mathscr{C}}\subseteq{\mathscr{C}}_{{m}}^{({p})}
.
Suppose that there are a position
{A}
and its proper descendant
{B}
not satisfying (
8
), that is,

\alpha-\beta\not\equiv\sum_{{i}}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}},

(9)

where
\alpha=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}(A)
and
\beta=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}(B)
.
Note that
\alpha\neq\beta
because if
\alpha=\beta
, then, by Lemma
3.1
,

[0,\ldots,0,\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{{N}}\ominus{b}^{i}_{{N}}]^{({p})}\equiv\sum_{{i}}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\not\equiv\alpha-\beta\equiv 0\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}},

so
{B}
is an option of
{A}
, which is impossible.

We prove that (
7
) does not hold.
Consider the two positions
(A,\beta\ominus_{p}\alpha)
and
(B,0)
.
Note that

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}(A)\oplus_{p}(\beta\ominus_{p}\alpha)=\beta\quad\text{\ \ {and} \ }\quad\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}(B)\oplus_{p}0=\beta.

We show that
(B,0)
is an option of
(A,\beta\ominus_{p}\alpha)
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
, which will imply that (
7
) does not hold.
Let
{M}=\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{p}(\beta\ominus_{{p}}\alpha)
.

Case 1

(
{M}<{N}
)
.

We see that

\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{p}}(({A},\beta\ominus_{{p}}\alpha)-({B},0))=\min\set{\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{p}({A}-{B}),\mathop{\operator@font ord}\nolimits_{{p}}(\beta\ominus_{{p}}\alpha)}=\min\set{{N},{M}}={M}.

Since

, it follows that

\biggl(\Bigl(\sum_{{i}}a^{i}-b^{i}\Bigr)+(\beta\ominus_{{p}}\alpha-0)\biggr)_{{M}}=\beta_{M}\ominus\alpha_{M}\neq 0.

Therefore
(B,0)
is an option of
(A,\beta\ominus_{p}\alpha)
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Case 2

(
{M}\geq{N}
)
.

Note that

\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{{p}}(({A},\beta\ominus_{{p}}\alpha)-({B},0))=\min\set{{N},{M}}={N}.

By Lemma
3.1
,

\sum_{i}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\equiv[0,\ldots,0,\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{N}\ominus{b}^{i}_{N}]^{({p})}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}

(10)

and

\alpha-\beta\equiv[0,\ldots,0,\alpha_{N}\ominus\beta_{N}]^{({p})}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}.

(11)

By combining (
9

\alpha_{N}\ominus\beta_{{N}}\neq\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{N}\ominus{b}^{i}_{N}.

Hence

\Bigl(\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{N}\ominus{b}^{i}_{N}\Bigr)\oplus(\beta_{N}\ominus\alpha_{N})\neq 0.

This implies that
({B},0)
is an option of
({A},\beta\ominus_{p}\alpha)
in
\tilde{\Gamma}
. Therefore (
7
) does not hold.

∎

3.4
A base-
p
Sprague-Grundy type theorem

The next theorem says that
p
-calm subtraction games satisfy (PN) and are closed under disjunctive sum.

Theorem 3.9
.

For
i\in\set{1,\ldots,{k}}
, let
\Gamma^{i}
be a
p
-calm subtraction game.
Then the disjunctive sum
\Gamma^{1}+\cdots+\Gamma^{k}
is
p
-calm.
Moreover, if
\tilde{\Gamma}
is a p-saturation of
\Gamma^{1}+\cdots+\Gamma^{k}
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a position in
\tilde{\Gamma}
, then

\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}}({\boldsymbol{A}})=\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{1}}({A}^{1})\oplus_{p}\cdots\oplus_{p}\mathop{\operator@font sg}\nolimits_{\tilde{\Gamma}^{k}}({A}^{k}),

where
{\boldsymbol{A}}=({A}^{1},\ldots,{A}^{k})
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{i}
is a
p
-saturation of
\Gamma^{i}
.

To prove Theorem
3.9
, we use the following simple lemma.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
p
-calm subtraction game and
\phi
be the Sprague-Grundy function of its
p
-saturation.
If
{A}
is a position in
\Gamma
and
{B}
is its proper descendant, then

\phi({A})\ominus_{p}\phi({B})\equiv\phi({A})-\phi({B})\equiv[0,\ldots,0,\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{N}\ominus{b}^{i}_{N}]^{({p})}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}},

(12)

where
{N}=\mathop{\operator@font mord}\nolimits_{p}({A}-{B})
.

proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,

\sum_{i}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\equiv\bigoplus_{{i}}\hskip-3.50006pt\ {}_{{p}}\hskip 3.50006pt{a}^{i}\ominus_{p}{b}^{i}\equiv[0,\ldots,0,\bigoplus_{i}{a}^{i}_{N}\ominus{b}^{i}_{N}]^{({p})}\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}.

Since
\Gamma
is
p
-calm, it follows that

\sum_{i}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\equiv\phi({A})-\phi({B})\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}.

We show that
\phi({A})-\phi({B})\equiv\phi({A})\ominus_{p}\phi({B})\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}
.
Since

\phi({A})-\phi({B})\equiv\sum_{i}{a}^{i}-{b}^{i}\equiv 0\pmod{{p}^{N}},

we see that
\phi({A})\equiv\phi({B})\pmod{{p}^{N}}
.
It follows from Lemma
3.1
that

\phi({A})-\phi({B})\equiv\phi({A})\ominus_{p}\phi({B})\pmod{{p}^{{N}+1}}.

Therefore (
12
) holds.
∎

4.3
A generalization of the
p^{\prime}
-component theorem

Let
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
be a
{k}
-tuple
({Y}^{1},\ldots,{Y}^{k})
of Young diagrams.
A
Young tableau

{\boldsymbol{T}}
of shape
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is a bijection from the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{{l}}{Y}^{l}
to
\set{1,2,\ldots\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{Y}}\right\rvert}
,
where
\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{Y}}\right\rvert=\sum_{l}\left\lvert{Y}^{l}\right\rvert
.
A Young tableau
{\boldsymbol{T}}
of shape
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is called a
standard tableau
if
{\boldsymbol{T}}({i},{j})\leq{\boldsymbol{T}}({i}^{\prime},{j}^{\prime})
for

({i},{j}),({i}^{\prime},{j}^{\prime})\in{Y}^{l}
and
{l}\in\set{1,\ldots,{k}}
with
{i}\leq{i}^{\prime}
and
{j}\leq{j}^{\prime}
.
Figure
8
shows an example of a standard tableau.

Figure 8:
A standard tableau of shape
((4,4,2),(2,1))
.

Let
f^{\boldsymbol{Y}}
denote the number of standard tableaux of shape
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
.
It follows from Theorem
4.5
that

f^{\boldsymbol{Y}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{n}}!}{n^{1}!\cdots n^{k}!}\frac{n^{1}!}{\prod_{{h}\in{\mathscr{H}}({Y}^{1})}{h}}\cdots\frac{n^{k}!}{\prod_{{h}\in{\mathscr{H}}({Y}^{k})}{h}}=\frac{{\boldsymbol{n}}!}{\prod_{{h}\in{\mathscr{H}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}{h}},

where
{\boldsymbol{n}}=\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{Y}}\right\rvert
,
{n}^{l}=\left\lvert{Y}^{l}\right\rvert
, and
{\mathscr{H}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})
is the multiset addition
{\mathscr{H}}({Y}^{1})+\ldots+{\mathscr{H}}({Y}^{k})
.
For example,
\set{1,2}+\set{1,3}=\set{1,1,2,3}
.
Moreover, by using Olsson’s generalization
[
11
]
of Macdonald’s characterization, we can show that

f^{\boldsymbol{Y}}
is prime to
{p}
if and only if
{\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}=\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{Y}}\right\rvert
, where

\displaystyle{\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}

\displaystyle={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({Y}^{1})}\oplus_{p}\cdots\oplus_{p}{\psi{{}^{({p})}}({Y}^{k})}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{{h}\in{\mathscr{H}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}\hskip-11.00008pt\ {}_{{p}}\hskip 3.00003pt{{\mathfrak{N}}{{}^{({p})}}({h})}.

Let
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
be
{k}
-tuples
({Y}^{1},\ldots,{Y}^{k})
and
({Z}^{1},\ldots,{Z}^{k})
of Young diagrams, respectively.
We write
{\boldsymbol{Z}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{Y}}
if
{Z}^{l}\subseteq{Y}^{l}
for
{l}\in\set{1,\ldots,{k}}
.
The following theorem follows from Proposition
3.20
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Every
{k}
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
of Young diagrams includes a
{k}
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
of Young diagrams
with
{\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}
cells in total such that
{f}^{\boldsymbol{Z}}
is prime to
{p}
.

proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{Y}}=({Y}^{1},\ldots,{Y}^{k})
,
{Y}^{l}=(\lambda^{{l},1},\ldots,\lambda^{{l},{m}^{l}})
,
{A}^{l}=(\lambda^{l,1}+{m}^{l}-1,

\lambda^{l,2}+{m}^{l}-2,

\ldots,

\lambda^{l,{m}^{l}})
,
and
{\boldsymbol{A}}=({A}^{1},\ldots,{A}^{k})
.
Then
{\boldsymbol{Y}}=({Y}({A}^{1}),\ldots,{Y}({A}^{k}))
.
We consider
{\boldsymbol{A}}
as a position in
\tilde{\Gamma}
,
where
\tilde{\Gamma}
is a
p
-saturation of
{\mathcal{W}}_{{m}^{1}}+\cdots+{\mathcal{W}}_{{m}^{k}}
.
By Proposition
3.20
,
the position
{\boldsymbol{A}}
has a full descendant
{\boldsymbol{B}}
with the same Sprague-Grundy value as
{\boldsymbol{A}}
, that is,
\lg_{\tilde{\Gamma}}({\boldsymbol{B}})={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{B}})}={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{A}})}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{Z}}=({Y}({B}^{1}),\ldots,{Y}({B}^{k}))
.
We see that
{\boldsymbol{Z}}\subseteq{\boldsymbol{Y}}
. Moreover,

\left\lvert{\boldsymbol{Z}}\right\rvert=\lg_{\tilde{\Gamma}}({\boldsymbol{B}})={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{B}})}={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{A}})}={\psi{{}^{({p})}}({\boldsymbol{Y}})}.

Therefore
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
is the desired
{k}
-tuple of Young diagrams.
∎
