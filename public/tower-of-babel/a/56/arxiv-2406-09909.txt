On Bourgain’s approach to stochastic homogenization
Mitia Duerinckx, Marius Lemm, François Pagano

Abstract
. In 2018, Bourgain pioneered a novel perturbative harmonic-analytic approach to the stochastic homogenization theory of discrete elliptic equations with weakly random i.i.d. coefficients. The approach was subsequently refined to show that homogenized approximations of ensemble averages can be derived to a precision four times better than almost sure homogenized approximations, which was unexpected by the state-of-the-art homogenization theory. In this paper, we grow this budding theory in various directions: First, we prove that the approach is robust by extending it to the continuum setting with exponentially mixing random coefficients. Second, we give a new proof via Malliavin calculus in the case of Gaussian coefficients, which avoids the main technicality of Bourgain’s original approach. This new proof also applies to strong Gaussian correlations with power-law decay. Third, we extend Bourgain’s approach to the study of fluctuations by constructing weak correctors up to order 2 ​ d 2d , which also clarifies the link between Bourgain’s approach and the standard corrector approach to homogenization. Finally, we draw several consequences from those different results, both for quantitative homogenization of ensemble averages and for asymptotic expansions of the annealed Green’s function.



1.
Introduction

In spatial dimension
d\geq 1
, given an underlying probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, consider a stationary measurable random coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
satisfying the following uniform ellipticity and boundedness assumptions,
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely,

(1.1)

e\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(x,\omega)e\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2},\qquad|\boldsymbol{a}(x,\omega)e|\,\leq\,C_{0}|e|,\qquad\text{for all $x,e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

Here, stationarity means that the (finite-dimensional) law of
\boldsymbol{a}
is shift-invariant; we refer to Section
3.1
for the detailed assumptions used throughout this work.
Given a deterministic field
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
, we consider the following heterogeneous elliptic problem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.2)

-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon},\omega)\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}(\cdot,\omega)=\nabla\cdot f,\qquad u_{\varepsilon,f}(\cdot,\omega)\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

where
\varepsilon>0
stands for the length scale of heterogeneities. For simplicity, we shall drop the
\omega
-dependence in the notation, simply writing for instance
\boldsymbol{a}(x)=\boldsymbol{a}(x,\omega)
and
u_{\varepsilon,f}=u_{\varepsilon,f}(\cdot,\omega)
, and we shall further abbreviate
\boldsymbol{a}_{\varepsilon}(x):=\boldsymbol{a}(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
.
To have a physical picture in mind, we may think of the solution field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
as an electric field generated by a given charge distribution
\nabla\cdot f
in an heterogeneous material with conductivity
\boldsymbol{a}_{\varepsilon}
.

In this setting, the goal of
homogenization theory
is to describe as accurately as possible the solution field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}
in the “macroscopic” limit
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
The present contribution builds on a new approach to stochastic homogenization pioneered by Bourgain
[
7
]
in 2018.
As described in Section
2
below,
the starting point of the approach is inspired by the Fourier method developed in the early works of Conlon and Naddaf
[
10
,
9
]
, and also studied by Sigal
[
31
]
: it starts by dividing the description of the solution field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
between its ensemble average
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
and its fluctuation
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
. Then, it proceeds by viewing the homogenization problem as a regularity question for suitable Fourier symbols. In the weakly random regime, this regularity question can be efficiently tackled by perturbative methods. More precisely, in
[
7
]
, Bourgain focused on the ensemble-averaged solution field and investigated the regularity of the corresponding Fourier symbol in the simplified setting of discrete elliptic equations with weakly random i.i.d. coefficients on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In an improved version obtained in
[
26
]
, in the same discrete setting, Bourgain’s regularity result has led to a refined homogenized description of ensemble averages, which took the homogenization community by surprise
[
16
,
15
]
: in a nutshell, it was shown that the ensemble-averaged field
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
allows for an homogenized approximation with an accuracy
four times better
than the field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
itself. This goes far beyond what could be obtained from the standard corrector approach to homogenization
[
22
,
18
]
.
In the present work, we extend this new theory in three main directions.

—

First, while only the case of
discrete
elliptic equations with i.i.d. coefficients was originally considered in
[
7
,
26
]
, we show the robustness of the approach by extending it to the continuum setting and to coefficient fields with stretched exponential
\alpha
-mixing rate; see Theorem
3.1
. This is achieved by a suitable coarse-graining argument.

—

Second, we investigate the possible extension to strongly-correlated coefficient fields:
as an illustrative model, we focus on
the Gaussian setting with power-law decaying correlations
and we show that a transition occurs at the power-law exponent
2d
for the accuracy of the homogenized description of ensemble averages; see Theorem
3.2
.
To this aim, we appeal to Malliavin calculus and discover how, when available, stochastic calculus leads to a completely new route to harness both oscillatory and probabilistic cancellations in the perturbation series: this reduces the main difficulty in Bourgain’s original approach, avoiding any use of the so-called disjointification lemma.

2.
Alternative perspective on homogenization

In this section, we recall the standard corrector approach to homogenization and compare it to an alternative approach first initiated by Conlon and Naddaf
[
10
,
9
]
and rediscovered by Sigal
[
31
]
.
We explain how the latter leads us to viewing the existence of two-scale expansions in homogenization theory as a regularity question for suitable Fourier symbols.
This new perspective on homogenization provides the starting point of Bourgain’s analysis
[
7
]
, which we shall further develop in the present work.

2.1.
Standard corrector approach to homogenization

Building on the expected separation of scales, a standard approach to describe the solution
u_{\varepsilon,f}
of the heterogeneous elliptic problem (
1.2
) is based on postulating a formal two-scale expansion, see e.g.
[
6
]
,

(2.1)

u_{\varepsilon,f}\penalty\ \sim\penalty\ \bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}+\sum_{{n\geq 1}}\varepsilon^{n}\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\,\nabla^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f},\qquad\text{in $\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega;\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))$},

where we use Einstein’s summation convention on repeated indices
1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\leq d
.
This amounts to approaching
u_{\varepsilon,f}
as a sum of small modulations at scale
O(\varepsilon)
around a deterministic profile
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}
. Modulations are given by so-called
correctors

\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, which we would expect to construct as stationary random fields just like the underlying coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
itself.
If such an expansion is possible, then we find that it must necessarily be characterized as follows:

—

The (higher-order) correctors
\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
must be defined iteratively by letting
\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}
be the stationary random field that has vanishing expectation, finite second moments, and satisfies almost surely the following equation in the weak sense on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(2.2)

\quad-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\,=\,\nabla\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{a}\varphi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)+e_{j_{n}}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}\big(\nabla\varphi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}+\varphi^{n-2}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}\big),

with the conventions
\varphi^{0}\equiv 1
and
\varphi^{-1}\equiv 0
, and with the notation
P^{\bot}=\operatorname{Id}-\mathbb{E}
.

—

The (higher-order) homogenized solution
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}
satisfies

(2.3)

\qquad-\nabla\cdot\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}(\varepsilon\nabla)^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\Big)\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}\,=\,\nabla\cdot f,

which is viewed as a suitable dispersive correction of the standard (first-order) homogenized equation
-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla\bar{u}_{f}=\nabla\cdot f
, in terms of the so-called (higher-order) homogenized tensors

(2.4)

\quad\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\,=\,\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{a}\big(\nabla\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\varphi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)\big],\qquad n\geq 1.

Note that, due to the dispersive corrections, equation (
2.3
) requires a suitable regularization to ensure its well-posedness; see Remark
2.3
below. If
f
has compactly supported Fourier transform, though, well-posedness holds for
\varepsilon
small enough.

The expansion (
2.1
) would give two pieces of information:

(A)

The ensemble average
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]\sim\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}
would satisfy an homogenized equation of the form (
2.3
).

(B)

The fluctuation
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
has spatial oscillations on the scale
O(\varepsilon)
, just as the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}_{\varepsilon}
itself, and these would be captured by the two-scale expansion (
2.1
) in form of

(2.5)

\qquad\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]\,\sim\,\nabla\sum_{n\geq 1}\varepsilon^{n}\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{j_{n}}u_{\varepsilon,f}\right],

or equivalently, expanding the gradient in the right-hand side,

\qquad\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\,\sim\,\sum_{n\geq 1}\varepsilon^{n-1}\psi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{j_{n}}u_{\varepsilon,f}\right],

in terms of
\psi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}:=\nabla\varphi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\varphi^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}
, which are stationary random fields satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{1}_{j}]=e_{j}
and
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{n}]=0
for
n>1
.
This is viewed as a series of stationary mean-zero oscillatory modulations around the ensemble average
\mathbb{E}[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}]
.

In case of a periodic coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
, all correctors
\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
can indeed be constructed as periodic solutions of the corrector equations (
2.2
), and the different series above are all convergent for
\varepsilon\ll 1
small enough provided that
f
is smooth enough (say, provided that
f
has compactly supported Fourier transform); see e.g.
[
16
, Proposition 3.3]
.
In contrast, in the case of a stationary random coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
, it is well-known that higher-order correctors cannot all be constructed as well-behaved stationary objects. More precisely, under suitable mixing assumptions, only the correctors
\varphi^{n}
’s with
n<\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
can be defined in general as stationary random fields with bounded second moments.
In this random setting, the above asymptotic expansions must then be truncated to order
\lceil\frac{d}{2}\rceil
and in turn only yield an accurate description of the ensemble average
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\right]
and of the fluctuation
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\right]
to order
O(\varepsilon^{d/2-})
; see e.g.
[
22
,
18
]
.

2.2.
Conlon–Naddaf–Sigal alternative approach

We now turn to another way to approach the homogenization question for (
1.2
), which was initiated in the early works of Conlon and Naddaf
[
10
,
9
]
and was rediscovered in slightly different terms by Sigal a few years ago in an unpublished note
[
31
]
.
Taking Sigal’s point of view, letting
P=\mathbb{E}
and
P^{\bot}=\operatorname{Id}-\mathbb{E}
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
, we consider the block decomposition

(2.6)

\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla\,\equiv\,\ \begin{pmatrix}\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla&\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P\nabla\\
\nabla\cdot P\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla&\nabla\cdot P\boldsymbol{a}P\nabla\end{pmatrix},

and a direct application of the Schur complement formula then yields the following non-asymptotic version of items (A)–(B) above.
The first instance of this result can be found in the work of Conlon and Naddaf
[
10
,
9
]
in form of a related representation formula for the Green’s function (see
[
10
, (2.4)]
, as well as
[
11
, (8.1)]
or
[
8
, (6.5)]
). Sigal’s note
[
31
]
only contains a formulation of item (A
′
) below for ensemble averages.
A short proof is included in Appendix
A
for completeness.

Lemma 2.1

(Conlon, Naddaf, Sigal)
.

Let
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
be the bounded pseudo-differential operator
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)^{d}
given by

(2.7)

\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)\,:=\,P^{\bot}\nabla(-\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla)^{-1}\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P,

and let
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
be the bounded convolution operator
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
given by

(2.8)

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{a}(\operatorname{Id}+\Psi(\cdot,\nabla))\right].

By the stationarity, ellipticity, and boundedness assumptions (
1.1
) for the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
, those operators are well-defined and satisfy the following properties:

—

The pseudo-differential operator
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
has a symbol
i\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))^{d\times d}
,

(2.9)

\qquad\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)\,=\,P^{\bot}(\nabla+i\xi)\Big(-(\nabla+i\xi)\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}(\nabla+i\xi)\Big)^{-1}(\nabla+i\xi)\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a},

where for all
\xi
the (matrix-valued) random field
\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)
is stationary and has vanishing expectation and finite second moments,

\qquad\mathbb{E}[\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)]\,=\,0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[|\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\,\leq\,C_{0}^{4}|e|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

—

The convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
has a symbol
i\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,

(2.10)

\qquad\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)\,=\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}(\operatorname{Id}+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi))],

where for all
\xi
the matrix
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
is uniformly elliptic and bounded in the sense of

\qquad e\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2},\qquad|\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e|\leq C_{0}^{3}|e|,\qquad\text{for all $e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

In these terms, the following exact representation result holds:

(A
′
)

The ensemble average
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]=\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}
satisfies the following well-posed pseudo-differential equation

(2.11)

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\varepsilon\nabla)\,\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}\,=\,\nabla\cdot f.

(B
′
)

The fluctuation
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
has spatial oscillations on the scale
O(\varepsilon)
and can be described as follows as a pseudo-differential operator with stationary symbol applied to the ensemble average,

(2.12)

\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]\,=\,\Psi(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\nabla)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right].

We view (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) as non-asymptotic versions of the formal higher-order homogenized equation (
2.3
) and of the two-scale expansion (
2.5
), respectively. Although this description is exact, it is complicated and of no immediate practical use since it involves general convolution and pseudo-differential operators.
It is therefore natural to investigate to what accuracy the convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\varepsilon\nabla)
in (
2.11
) and the pseudo-differential operator
\Psi(\frac{\cdot}{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\nabla)
in (
2.12
) can be approximated by partial differential operators as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
In fact, we showed in
[
16
]
that the regularity of the Fourier symbol
i\xi\mapsto\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
at the origin
\xi=0

is equivalent to the existence of a homogenized approximation for the ensemble average. We recall the following result from
[
16
]
, where this equivalence is made explicit: homogenized coefficients are equal to derivatives of the symbol
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
at
\xi=0
.

Proposition 2.2

(see Prop. 2.1 in
[
16
]
)
.

Given regularity exponents
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\eta<1
, the following two properties are equivalent:

(i)

The symbol
i\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:i\xi\mapsto\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)

is of Hölder class
C^{\ell-\eta}
at the origin.

(ii)

There exist constant tensors
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
,
where for all
n
and
1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{n-1}\leq d
the value
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}
is a matrix,
such that the following property holds. For all
\varepsilon>0
and
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, letting
u_{\varepsilon,f}\in\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be the unique Lax–Milgram solution of the heterogeneous elliptic equation (
1.2
),
and defining
\bar{u}^{\ell}_{\varepsilon,f}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as a suitable notion of solution (in the sense of Remark
2.3
below) for the
\ell
th-order homogenized equation

(2.13)

\qquad-\nabla\cdot\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}(\varepsilon\nabla)^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\Big)\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}^{\ell}\,=\,\nabla\cdot f+O(\varepsilon^{\ell}),

we have the following error bound for ensemble averages,

(2.14)

\qquad\|\nabla(\mathbb{E}[u_{\varepsilon,f}]-\bar{u}^{\ell}_{\varepsilon,f})\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\varepsilon^{\ell-\eta}C_{\ell}\|\langle\nabla\rangle^{2\ell-1}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

for some constant
C_{\ell}
depending only on
d,C_{0},\ell
.

Moreover, if those properties hold, then the so-called homogenized coefficients
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
are related to derivatives of the symbol of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
: for all
1\leq n\leq\ell
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(2.15)

\frac{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}+(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}})^{T}}{2}z_{j_{1}}\ldots z_{j_{n-1}}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \sum_{|\alpha|=n-1}\tfrac{z^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}(\nabla_{i\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{A}}|_{\xi=0}),

an identity between symmetric matrices.

Remark 2.3

(Higher-order homogenized solutions)
.

We recall that the higher-order homogenized equation (
2.13
) might not be well-posed as the symbol of the operator might not be positive due to the dispersive corrections. In the above statement, as in
[
18
,
16
]
, we can use for instance the following well-defined proxy for the higher-order homogenized solution, which solves the desired equation up to
O(\varepsilon^{\ell})
: we define
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}^{\ell}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon^{n-1}\tilde{u}^{n}_{f}
, where
\tilde{u}_{f}^{1}
is the solution in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of the (first-order) homogenized equation

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla\tilde{u}^{1}_{f}\,=\,\nabla\cdot f,

and where the corrections
\{\tilde{u}^{n}_{f}\}_{2\leq n\leq\ell}
are iteratively defined as the solutions in
\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla\tilde{u}^{n}_{f}\,=\,\nabla\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{n}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{k}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k-1}}\nabla^{k-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k-1}}\nabla\tilde{u}^{n+1-k}_{f},\qquad 2\leq n\leq\ell.

The proof of the above result in
[
16
]
is easily adapted to a similar equivalence result for fluctuations: the regularity of the Fourier symbol
i\xi\mapsto\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)
at the origin
\xi=0
is equivalent to the accuracy of higher-order two-scale expansions. Note that by stationarity it suffices to consider the symbol
i\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times d}:i\xi\mapsto\Psi(0,i\xi)
at
x=0
. We omit the proof for conciseness.

Proposition 2.4
.

Given regularity exponents
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\eta<1
, the following two properties are equivalent:

(i)

The symbol
i\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times d}:i\xi\mapsto\Psi(0,i\xi)
is of Hölder class
C^{\ell-\eta}
at the origin. In particular, by definition (
2.10
), this implies that the symbol
i\xi\mapsto\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
has also (at least) the same regularity.

(ii)

There exist random fields
\{\psi^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
and constant tensors
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
, where each
\psi^{n}
is a tensor-valued stationary random field with bounded second moments,
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{1}_{j}]=e_{j}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\psi^{n}]=0
for
n>1
, such that the following property holds.
For all
\varepsilon>0
and
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, letting
u_{\varepsilon,f}\in\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be the unique Lax–Milgram solution of the heterogeneous elliptic equation (
1.2
), and defining the
\ell
th-order homogenized solution
\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}^{\ell}
as in Proposition
2.2
(ii),
we have the following error bound for two-scale expansions,

\qquad\bigg\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\varepsilon^{n-1}\psi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon})\nabla_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}^{\ell}\bigg\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)}\,\leq\,\varepsilon^{\ell-\eta}C_{\ell}\|\langle\nabla\rangle^{2\ell-1}f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

for some constant
C_{\ell}
depending only on
d,C_{0},\ell
.

Moreover, if those properties hold, then the collection
\{\psi^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
is related to derivatives of the symbol of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
: for all
1\leq n\leq\ell
and
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(2.16)

\psi^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(x)\,z_{j_{1}}\ldots z_{j_{n}}\penalty\ =\penalty\ z\mathds{1}_{n=1}+\sum_{|\alpha|=n-1}\tfrac{z^{\alpha}}{\alpha!}(\nabla_{i\xi}^{\alpha}\Psi)(x,0).

In short, the above two propositions state that all standard homogenization questions are equivalent to regularity questions for the symbols

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}i\mathbb{R}^{d}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}:&i\xi&\mapsto&\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi),\\[2.84526pt]
i\mathbb{R}^{d}&\to&\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)^{d\times d}:&i\xi&\mapsto&\Psi(0,i\xi),\end{array}

at the origin
\xi=0
.
In the periodic setting, as two-scale expansions are convergent, both symbols can be checked to be analytic in a neighborhood of the origin; see
[
16
]
. In contrast, in the random setting, as homogenization theory only gives in general the accuracy of two-scale expansions at best to order
O(\varepsilon^{d/2-})
, cf.
[
22
,
18
]
, we conclude that the symbol
i\xi\mapsto\Psi(0,i\xi)
is at best of class
C^{d/2-}
at the origin.
As we will see below, in the weakly random regime, Bourgain proposed a new approach that goes
four times
beyond this threshold for the regularity of the symbol
i\xi\mapsto\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
, hence also for the accuracy of homogenized approximations for ensemble averages by Proposition
2.2
.

2.3.
Bourgain’s surprising result

As the ensemble average
\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}\right]
is an averaged quantity, we may expect a more accurate intrinsic description to hold than for the pointwise solution field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
itself. Equivalently, as the convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
is given by the expected value (
2.8
), we may expect its symbol to have a better regularity than that of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
.
As such improved results for ensemble averages could not be obtained from standard corrector theory, this direction of research was long abandoned in the homogenization community.
A recent result by Bourgain
[
7
]
, in its improved form obtained in
[
26
]
by Kim and the second-named author, has shown that the above intuition is indeed correct: in the weakly random regime, the symbol of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
is actually four times more regular than that of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
.
More precisely, the result in
[
7
,
26
]
was obtained for an i.i.d. discrete analog of the elliptic equation (

Remark 2.6
.

The above result actually states the

3.
Main results

From now on, we focus on the case

: indeed, for

, explicitly solving equation (

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)=\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}
and

3.1.
Statistical assumptions

Given an underlying probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, we consider a uniformly elliptic stationary measurable random coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, in the following sense:

—

Measurability:
The map
\boldsymbol{a}(x,\cdot):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is measurable for all
x
, and
\boldsymbol{a}
is jointly measurable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
. This ensures in particular that realizations
\boldsymbol{a}(\cdot,\omega)
are almost surely measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

—

Stationarity:
The finite-dimensional laws of the field
\boldsymbol{a}
are shift-invariant. More precisely, for all
n\geq 1
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the law of
(\boldsymbol{a}(x_{1}+x),\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}(x_{1}+x))
does not depend on the shift
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

—

Uniform ellipticity:
Almost surely, realizations of
\boldsymbol{a}
are uniformly elliptic in the sense that

\quad e\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(x,\cdot)e\geq\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2},\qquad|\boldsymbol{a}(x,\cdot)e|\leq C_{0}|e|,\qquad\text{for all $x,e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$},

for some
C_{0}<\infty
.

On top of those general assumptions, as usual for quantitative stochastic homogenization theory, we shall need some strong mixing condition on the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
. More precisely, we shall consider the following two situations. On the one hand, we consider a general
\alpha
-mixing condition with stretched exponential mixing rate, in which case we will be able to recover the same regularity (
2.18
) as in Bourgain’s theorem. On the other hand, we also wish to study what the result becomes in a strongly correlated setting and for that purpose we consider a model Gaussian setting with algebraic correlation structure.

(H
1
)

Stretched exponential
\alpha
-mixing setting:
The random field
\boldsymbol{a}
is
\alpha
-mixing with some stretched exponential rate function in the sense that for all
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all events
A\in\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{U})
and
B\in\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{V})
we have

\qquad|\,\mathbb{P}\left[A\cap B\right]-\mathbb{P}\left[A\right]\mathbb{P}\left[B\right]|\,\leq\,C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}{\operatorname{dist}}(U,V)^{\gamma}),

for some exponent
\gamma>0
and some constant
C_{0}<\infty
.

(H
2
)

Correlated Gaussian setting:
The random field
\boldsymbol{a}
is of the form

(3.1)

\boldsymbol{a}(x,\omega)\,=\,A_{0}(G(x,\omega)),

where the function
A_{0}\in C_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{\kappa};\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
is such that the uniform ellipticity assumption for
\boldsymbol{a}
is satisfied pointwise,
and where
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
is an
\mathbb{R}^{\kappa}
-valued stationary centered Gaussian random field on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, characterized by its covariance function

c(x-y)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[G(x,\cdot)\otimes G(y,\cdot)\right],\qquad c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\kappa}.

Moreover, we assume that the covariance function has algebraic decay at infinity in the following sense: we assume that
c
can be decomposed as
c=c_{0}\ast c_{0}
for some even convolution kernel
c_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{\kappa\times\kappa}
satisfying

(3.2)

|c_{0}(x)|\,\leq\,C_{0}(1+|x|)^{-\beta},\qquad\beta:=\gamma\vee\tfrac{d+\gamma}{2},

for some exponent
\gamma>0
and some constant
C_{0}<\infty
.
This implies in particular that the covariance function satisfies precisely

|c(x)|\lesssim_{\gamma}C_{0}^{2}(1+|x|)^{-\gamma}.

Under those assumptions, whenever
\gamma>0
, we note that the Gaussian field
G
is necessarily strongly mixing, hence ergodic, but it is
\alpha
-mixing only provided
\gamma>d
; see e.g.
[
13
]
.

Remark 3.3

(
\delta
-dependence and transitions)
.

We briefly comment on the dependence on
\delta
in the above results: similarly as in the result (
2.18
) obtained in the i.i.d. discrete setting, we may expect the prefactor
\delta
in (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) to be somehow replaced by
\delta^{3}
for
|x-y|\gg 1
.
In fact, the kernel of
\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)
can be decomposed into two contributions:

—

the first contribution is of order
O(\delta)
and has decay given by the naïve bound
\langle x-y\rangle^{-d}
multiplied by another decay rate driven by mixing properties;

—

the second contribution is of order
O(\delta^{3})
and has the unusual decay
\langle x-y\rangle^{-3d}
due to random cancellations.

This decomposition is clear from the proof: in the perturbation series and the path analysis performed in the proof, the two contributions correspond respectively to so-called reducible and irreducible paths.
More precisely, the bound (
3.5
) in Theorem
3.1
can be improved to the following, for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\|\mathds{1}_{Q(x)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\,\leq\,K\tfrac{q^{2}}{q-1}\Big(\delta\,\exp\big(\!-|x-y|^{\frac{\varepsilon\gamma}{3d}}\big)+\delta^{3}\langle x-y\rangle^{\varepsilon+\delta K-3d}\Big),

and similarly the bound (
3.6
) in Theorem
3.2
can be improved to

\|\mathds{1}_{Q(x)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\,\leq\,K\tfrac{q^{2}}{q-1}\Big(\delta\,\langle x-y\rangle^{\delta K-d-\gamma}+\delta^{3}\langle x-y\rangle^{\delta K-3d}\Big).

In this last estimate, we find that the decay of the first contribution dominates that of the second one when the decay of correlations is too slow in the sense of
\gamma<2d
. This amounts to a transition where the leading term in the perturbation series switches from the irreducible path
(x,y,x,y)
to the reducible path
(x,y)
, and this comes with a corresponding transition in the
\delta
-dependence.

Remark 3.4

(
q
-dependence)
.

The bounds (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) contain two distinct pieces of information.
The main piece is the surprising decay at large distances
|x-y|\gg 1
, which has important consequences as discussed below.
At short distances
|x-y|\lesssim 1
, on the other hand, the bounds do not give precise information on the pointwise singularity of the kernel of
\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)
, but they still contain nontrivial information: they show that the operator norm of
\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)
on
\operatorname{L}^{q}
is bounded by
O(\tfrac{q^{2}}{q-1})
,
which matches the bound on the operator norm of Calderón–Zygmund operators by Marcinkiewicz interpolation, at least for
q
not too close to
1
or
\infty
in the sense of
1+\delta K<q<\frac{1}{\delta K}
.

Remark 3.5

(Sobolev regularity of the symbol)
.

In the above statements, we focus on the Hölder regularity of the symbol of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
, which is shown to belong to
C^{2d-\delta K-}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
or
C^{(2d)\wedge\gamma-\delta K-}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively.
Alternatively, we may also investigate its Sobolev regularity: following the lines of the proof of Hölder regularity in Section
5.5
, we can easily check that the symbol also belongs for instance to
H^{\frac{5d}{2}-\delta K-}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
or
H^{\frac{d}{2}+(2d)\wedge\gamma-\delta K-}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
in the setting of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively.
This weak differentiability can be useful, as in particular for our estimates on the averaged Green’s function in Remark
3.11
below.

3.3.
Application 1: homogenization of ensemble averages

We briefly describe several consequences of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
to homogenization theory, and we start with the homogenization of ensemble averages. The following result is an immediate consequence of Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
combined with the equivalence stated in Proposition
2.2
. As a comparison, we recall that standard quenched homogenized expansions only reach the order
\ell=\frac{d}{2}
in the stretched exponential mixing setting
(H
1
)
, and the order
\ell=\frac{1}{2}(d\wedge\gamma)
in the correlated Gaussian setting
(H
2
)
with exponent
\gamma
, cf.
[
18
]
.
In particular, while in the stretched exponential mixing setting the ensemble-averaged solution allows for an homogenized approximation with an accuracy
four times better
than the quenched solution itself, we find that the accuracy only gets
two times better
in the very correlated Gaussian setting with
\gamma<d
, thus matching in that case the non-perturbative result that would be obtained with the weak corrector method of
[
15
]
.

Corollary 3.6

(Homogenization for ensemble averages)
.

(i)

Stretched exponential
\alpha
-mixing setting:

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
, there exist constant tensors
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq 2d}
such that the following property holds for any integer
\ell\geq 1
and any
0\leq\eta<1
with

\ell-\eta\,<\,2d-\delta K.

For all
\varepsilon>0
and
f\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, letting
u_{\varepsilon,f}\in\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
be the unique Lax–Milgram solution of the heterogeneous elliptic equation (
1.2
),
and defining
\bar{u}^{\ell}_{\varepsilon,f}\in\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as a suitable notion of solution (in the sense of Remark
2.3

\qquad-\nabla\cdot\Big(\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}(\varepsilon\nabla)^{n-1}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}\Big)\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}^{\ell}\,=\,\nabla\cdot f+O(\varepsilon^{\ell}),

we have the following error bound for ensemble averages,

\qquad\|\nabla(\mathbb{E}[u_{\varepsilon,f}]-\bar{u}^{\ell}_{\varepsilon,f})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\varepsilon^{\ell-\eta}C_{\ell}\|\langle\nabla\rangle^{2\ell-1}f\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

(ii)

Correlated Gaussian setting with exponent
\gamma

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
, the same result holds as in (i) up to any order

\ell-\eta\,<\,(2d)\wedge\gamma-\delta K.

For the above homogenization result to be any useful to practitioners, it should be complemented with a practical way to actually compute numerically the constant tensors
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq\ell}
that define the homogenized equation. According to Proposition

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
at the origin, cf. (

To solve this issue, we show that those homogenized tensors can also be obtained as the limits of their massive approximations, which in turn are amenable to numerical computations: indeed, as e.g. in
[
19
]
, we recall that massive approximations can be evaluated numerically by periodization and Monte Carlo methods. The proof of the following massive approximation result is postponed to Section

Corollary 3.7

(Massive approximation)
.

For
\mu>0

by its massive version
\mu-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla
, all correctors and homogenized coefficients can be constructed as follows:

—

The massive correctors
\{\varphi_{\mu}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
can be uniquely defined iteratively by letting
\varphi_{\mu,i_{1}\ldots i_{n}}^{n}
be the stationary random field that has vanishing expectation, finite second moments, and satisfies almost surely the following massive version of the corrector equation (

), in the weak sense on

,

\qquad(\mu-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla)\varphi^{n}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\,=\,\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}\varphi^{n-1}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}})\\
+e_{j_{n}}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}(\nabla\varphi^{n-1}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}+\varphi^{n-2}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-2}}e_{j_{n-1}}),

with the conventions
\varphi_{\mu}^{0}\equiv 1
and
\varphi_{\mu}^{-1}\equiv 0
.

—

The homogenized tensors
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
are defined by

\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\,=\,\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{a}\big(\nabla\varphi^{n}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}+\varphi^{n-1}_{\mu,j_{1}\ldots j_{n-1}}e_{j_{n}}\big)\big].

In these terms, letting

denote the highest order obtained in Corollary

\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}{\operatorname{sym}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu}^{n})\,=\,{\operatorname{sym}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}),

where we use the notation
{\operatorname{sym}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n})_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}:=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in{\operatorname{sym}}(n)}\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{\sigma(1)}\ldots j_{\sigma(n)}}+(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}_{j_{\sigma(1)}\ldots j_{\sigma(n)}})^{T})
for tensor symmetrization.

here for shortness.

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[(-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla)^{-1}\big](x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\nabla\big)^{-1}(x)\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,\big(\xi\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)\xi\big)^{-1}\,\frac{d\xi}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}.

Previous work on Green’s functions in stochastic homogenization has been focused on
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-annealed estimates. Specifically, the main concern was to show that the quenched Green’s function
G(x,y):=(-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla)^{-1}(x,y)
behaves similarly as the Green’s function for the Laplacian up to
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-averaging over the random ensemble: for
|x-y|\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\|G(x,y)\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|x-y|^{2-d},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}G(x,y)\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|x-y|^{1-d},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}\partial_{y}G(x,y)\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle|x-y|^{-d}.

While the first of those bounds follows from the Aronson estimates, the latter two show that the classical De Giorgi–Nash–Moser regularity theory can be substantially improved upon
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-averaging. This was first established in the discrete i.i.d. setting by Delmotte and Deuschel
[
12
]
(see also
[
9
,
29
]
), and has been largely extended and refined since then (see e.g.
[
27
,
20
,
1
,
4
]
). It can be viewed as a precursor of the large-scale regularity theory later developed in
[
2
,
1
,
21
]
.
We also refer to
[
28
,
3
,
4
]
for related two-scale expansions of the Green’s function, which amount to quenched asymptotic expansions of
G
.

In the present work, instead of those
\operatorname{L}^{2}
-averaged estimates on the quenched Green’s function
G
, we focus on the averaged Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
defined in (
3.7
),

\mathcal{G}(x-y)\,=\,\mathbb{E}[G(x,y)],

and we show that better estimates and expansions can be proved for the latter.
From the perspective of statistical mechanics, we recall that correlation functions for several equilibrium statistical ensembles can be written as the averaged Green’s function for some elliptic operator in divergence form with random coefficients, see
[
29
]
, thus providing further motivation for the present study.

We start with the leading-order asymptotics of the annealed Green’s function: more precisely, due to the leading-order expansion

(3.8)

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\nabla\,=\,-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla+O(|\nabla|^{3}),

we can naturally compare
\mathcal{G}
with the Green’s function
\bar{G}
associated with the homogenized operator
-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla
. Note that
\bar{G}
satisfies
|\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}(x)|\simeq_{\alpha}|x|^{2-d-|\alpha|}
for all
\alpha\geq 0
, so that the result below indeed identifies the leading asymptotics of
\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}
as
|x|\uparrow\infty
. The proof is postponed to Section
9.1
.
The possibility of removing the frequency cut-off
\chi
is the object of Remark
3.11
below.
Note that a corresponding result holds, with the same proof, under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
, which we skip here for conciseness.

Corollary 3.8

(Leading asymptotics of annealed Green’s function)
.

Let
d>2
and let the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
hold. Given a frequency cut-off
\chi
with Fourier transform
\hat{\chi}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have for all multi-indices
\alpha\geq 0
with
|\alpha|\leq d
,

(3.9)

|\chi*(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G})(x)|\,\lesssim_{\chi}\,\langle x\rangle^{1-d-|\alpha|}.

In addition, for
|\alpha|=d
, we have for all
\eta>\delta K
,

(3.10)

[\chi\ast(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G})]_{C^{1-\eta}(B(x))}\,\lesssim_{\chi,\eta}\,\langle x\rangle^{\eta-2d},

where
[\cdot]_{C^{1-\eta}(B(x))}
stands for the Hölder semi-norm on
B(x)
. If the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
is symmetric in law (that is, if
\boldsymbol{a}
has the same law as the pointwise transpose
\boldsymbol{a}^{T}
), then the different decay rates can be improved by one order:
\langle x\rangle^{1-d-|\alpha|}
and
\langle x\rangle^{\eta-2d}
can be replaced by
\langle x\rangle^{-d-|\alpha|}
and
\langle x\rangle^{\eta-2d-1}
in (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), respectively.

Pursuing the expansion (
3.8
) to higher order,
we are led to derive the following higher-order expansion of the averaged Green’s function. The corrections to the homogenized Green’s function
\bar{G}
are expressed in a hierarchical form similarly as for higher-order homogenized equations in Remark
2.3
.
By definition, the
n
th correction
\tilde{G}^{n}
in (
3.11
) is an homogeneous function of degree
3-d-n
that can be computed explicitly in terms of the constants
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
, so that the present result amounts to an expansion of the annealed Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
in powers of
1/|x|
.
The proof is postponed to Section
9.2
.

Corollary 3.9

(Higher asymptotics of annealed Green’s function)
.

Let
d>2
and let the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
hold.
For
1\leq\ell\leq 2d
, we define

(3.11)

\bar{G}^{\ell}(x)\,:=\,\sum_{n=1}^{\ell}\tilde{G}^{n}(x),

where
\tilde{G}^{1}:=\bar{G}
is the Green’s function for the homogenized operator
-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla
,
and where the corrections
\{\tilde{G}^{n}\}_{2\leq n\leq 2d}
are tempered distributions defined iteratively as follows,

(3.12)

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{1}\nabla\tilde{G}^{n}=\nabla\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{n}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{k}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k-1}}\nabla_{j_{1}\ldots j_{k-1}}^{k-1}\nabla\tilde{G}^{n+1-k},\qquad 2\leq n\leq 2d.

Given a frequency cut-off
\chi
with Fourier transform
\hat{\chi}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
we have for all
\ell\geq 1
and all multi-indices
\alpha\geq 0
with
|\alpha|+\ell\leq d+1
,

(3.13)

|\chi\ast(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}^{\ell})(x)|\,\lesssim\,\langle x\rangle^{2-d-|\alpha|-\ell}.

In addition, for
|\alpha|+\ell=d+1
, we have for all
\eta>\delta K
,

(3.14)

[\chi\ast(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}^{\ell})]_{C^{1-\eta}(B(x))}\,\lesssim_{\chi,\eta}\,\langle x\rangle^{\eta-2d}.

Remark 3.10

(Multipole screening interpretation)
.

The annealed Green’s function is the kernel for the averaged solution: for
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, considering the Lax–Milgram solution
v_{f}\in\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\dot{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
of the equation
-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla v_{f}=f
, the averaged solution is
\mathbb{E}[v_{f}]=\mathcal{G}\ast f
.
We claim that Corollary
3.8
yields the following screening effect for multipoles: for all
n\leq d+1
, if
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
corresponds to a
2^{n}
-pole, meaning that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x^{m}f(x)dx=0
for all
0\leq m\leq n-1
, then we have the decay
|\mathbb{E}[v_{f}](x)|\lesssim_{f}\langle x\rangle^{2-d-n}
.
This is indeed a consequence of the proof of Corollary
3.8
since the condition that
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a
2^{n}
-pole allows to represent
f=\nabla^{\alpha}h
for some
h\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
|\alpha|=n
, hence
\mathbb{E}[v_{f}]=\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}\ast h
.

Remark 3.11

(Estimates without frequency cut-off)
.

We may wonder in which cases the frequency cut-off
\chi
can be removed in Corollaries
3.8
and
3.9
.
Note that in the corresponding discrete setting of
[
25
,
34
]
this cut-off does naturally not occur.
In the continuum setting, removing the cut-off requires information on the high-frequency behavior of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
, and this question is entirely foreign to homogenization.
We can show for instance the following results, the proofs of which are postponed to Section
9.3
:

(a)

For the homogenized Green’s function
\bar{G}
or its corrections
\bar{G}^{\ell}
, the cut-off
\chi
can always be removed: for all
1\leq\ell\leq 2d
and all multi-indices
\alpha\geq 0
, we indeed have for all
|x|\geq 1
and
p\geq 0
,

|\chi\ast\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}(x)-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}|+|\chi\ast\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}^{\ell}(x)-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G}^{\ell}|\,\lesssim_{\chi,\alpha,p}\,|x|^{-p}.

(b)

For the annealed Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
, the cut-off
\chi
can be removed when less than two derivatives are taken: for
|\alpha|\leq 1
, we have for all
|x|\geq 1
,

|\chi\ast\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}(x)-\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}(x)|\,\lesssim_{\chi}\,|x|^{-d-1},

and in addition, for
|\alpha|=1
, for all
|x|\geq 2
,

[\chi\ast\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}]_{C^{1-\eta}(B(x))}\,\lesssim_{\chi,\eta}\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}|x|^{-d-1}&:&\eta>\frac{5d}{6d+6}+C\delta K,\\
|x|^{-d}&:&\eta>0.\end{array}\right.

(c)

If
\boldsymbol{a}
is rotationally symmetric in law (that is, if
\boldsymbol{a}
has the same law as
O^{T}\boldsymbol{a}(O\cdot)O
for all
O\in O(d)
), then for the annealed Green’s function
\mathcal{G}
the cut-off
\chi
can be removed when less than
\frac{d+3}{2}
derivatives are taken: for
|\alpha|<\frac{d+3}{2}
, we have for all
|x|\geq 1
,

|\chi\ast\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}(x)-\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}(x)|\,\lesssim_{\chi}\,|x|^{2-d-\frac{d+3}{2}}.

3.5.
Weak correctors

We turn to our last main result, that is, the construction of weak correctors, or alternatively, the regularity of the symbol of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
in a suitable weak sense.
As recalled in Section
2.1
, correctors
\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n}
are defined to describe the fine spatial oscillations of the solution field
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
, and, if well-defined, they are necessarily given by the recurrence equations (
2.2
).
Heuristically, focusing on the first right-hand side term in those corrector equations, and replacing the heterogeneous operator
-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla
by the Laplacian, one may expect in a perturbative perspective that the
n
th corrector
\varphi^{n}
behaves similarly as

(3.15)

\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\,:=\,[(-\triangle)^{-1}\nabla]^{n}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}.

Due to the iteration of the Riesz potential
(-\triangle)^{-1}\nabla
, such quantities can only make sense for
n
not too large. More precisely, under strong enough mixing assumptions, such as
(H
1
)
, we have the following easy observations:

—

\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
can be uniquely constructed as a well-defined stationary random field in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
provided that
n<\frac{d}{2}
;

—

\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
can be uniquely constructed as a well-defined stationary random field in a Schwartz-like distributional sense on
\Omega
(in the sense of
[
15
]
) provided that
n<d
. More precisely, this means that
\tilde{\varphi}^{n}
can be defined through
its conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{\varphi}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{a}|_{B_{R}}\right]\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
for all
R>0
.

The construction of genuine correctors
\{\varphi^{n}\}_{n}
satisfying the corrector equations (
2.2
) instead of (
3.15
) is much more intricate, but it is now well-understood that the same result holds: under
(H
1
)
, the
n
th corrector
\varphi^{n}
can be constructed as a stationary random field in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
for all
n<\frac{d}{2}
(see e.g.
[
18
]
), and as a stationary random field in a distributional sense on
\Omega
for all
n<d
(see
[
15
]
).
The latter notion of so-called
weak
correctors is unconventional and was first introduced in
[
15
]
by the first-named author in link with a non-perturbative approach to Bourgain’s result.
More precisely, we recall that the existence of weak stationary correctors of orders
n<d
essentially implies the following intermediate result (actually in some slightly weaker form):

(3.16)

\text{the symbol of $\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)$ is of class $C^{d-}$ at the origin},

which is to be compare to the
C^{2d-\delta K-}
regularity
obtained in the
\delta
-perturbative setting in Theorem
3.1
.
A natural question is therefore whether the above number of weak stationary correctors is optimal or not: if weak stationary correctors could be constructed to all orders
n<2d
, then we might expect the weak corrector theory developed in
[
15
]
to improve on (
3.16
) and to solve the Bourgain–Spencer conjecture.

Intuitively, constructing a larger number of weak stationary correctors seems unlikely as it would mean that the analogy (
3.15
) actually fails — but this is indeed so: it turns out that this heuristic comparison (
3.15
) does not accurately take into
account the subtle cancellations and algebraic properties of the hierarchy of corrector equations (
2.2
).
To see this, we propose a different way to construct correctors:
if the symbol of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
could be differentiated
k
times in some sense, then it could be applied to a polynomial of order
k
, say
p_{e,x}^{k}(z):=(e\cdot(z-x))^{k}
, which would yield, in view of (
2.16
),

\big(\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)p_{e,x}^{n}\big)(z)\,=\,\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\,\big(\nabla\varphi^{k+1}_{e\ldots e}(z)+\mathds{1}_{k>0}\varphi^{k}_{e\ldots e}(z)e\big)\,(e\cdot(z-x))^{n-k},

hence, when evaluated at
z=x
,

\nabla\varphi^{n+1}_{e\ldots e}(x)+\mathds{1}_{n>0}\varphi^{n}_{e\ldots e}(x)e\,=\,\tfrac{1}{n!}\big(\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)p_{e,x}^{n}\big)(x).

Similarly, recalling that
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
is given by (
2.7
), the following formula can be checked to define the
n
th-order stationary corrector, provided that it makes sense,

(3.17)

\varphi^{n}_{e\ldots e}(x)\,=\,\tfrac{1}{n!}\Big((-\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla)^{-1}\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P\nabla p_{e,x}^{n}\Big)(x).

Surprisingly, in the weakly random regime, the following result shows that weak stationary correctors can be constructed in this way for all
n<2d
. The proof is postponed to Section
7
.
We focus here for shortness on the construction of weak correctors, but this result could be alternatively formulated in terms of the weak regularity of the symbol of
\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)
. Recalling (
2.8
), this constitutes a generalization of the regularity of the symbol of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
obtained in Theorem
3.1
.
Note that a corresponding result holds, with the same proof, under the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
, which we skip here for conciseness.

Theorem 3.12

(Main result 3)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
,
we can define weak stationary correctors
\varphi^{n}_{e\ldots e}
for all
n<2d
via the above formula (
3.17
) in the distributional sense on
\Omega
(in the sense of
[
15
]
).
More precisely, the following estimates hold
for all
n<2d
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
R>0
,

\big\|\mathbb{E}\big[\varphi^{n}_{e\ldots e}(x)\|\boldsymbol{a}|_{B_{R}}\big]\big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,\leq\,\delta KR^{n}\big(1+\langle\tfrac{x}{R}\rangle^{\delta K+n-d}\big).

The above estimates show that for
n\geq d
the weak stationary corrector
\varphi^{n}_{e\ldots e}
starts to display a polynomial growth with respect to the distance
{\operatorname{dist}}(x,B_{R})
between the evaluation point
x
and the conditioning set
B_{R}
.
This growth, which we believe to be optimal in this perturbative setting, makes correctors hard to exploit. It explains why the weak corrector theory developed in
[
15
]
could only make use of weak stationary correctors up to order
n<d
and therefore failed at implying the full decay of Bourgain’s perturbative theorem.
This tends to show that correctors could not be used to prove Bourgain’s theorem in the non-perturbative setting beyond the intermediate decay (
3.16
) obtained in
[
15
]
.

4.
Deterministic estimates

We consider the perturbative regime (
3.3
).
For notational simplicity, let us assume that
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}]
is symmetric.
Without loss of generality, up to changing variables, we may then assume that
\boldsymbol{a}
is close to the constant coefficient
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}=\operatorname{Id}
.
More precisely, we may assume that the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
takes the form

(4.1)

\boldsymbol{a}\,=\,\operatorname{Id}+\delta\boldsymbol{b},\qquad\delta\ll 1,

for some random field
\boldsymbol{b}
satisfying

|\boldsymbol{b}|\leq 1,\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{b}\right]=0.

This convenient reduction is indeed allowed without loss of generality up to replacing the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
by
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{a}\right]^{-1/2}\boldsymbol{a}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{a}\right]^{-1/2}
, which amounts to changing variables in equation (
1.2
).
In this setting (
4.1
), we shall analyze the decay of the kernel of the convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
defined in Lemma
2.1
, and we start in this section with some preliminary deterministic estimates and useful notation.

4.1.
Representation formula

In terms of (
4.1
), the representation formulae (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) for the convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
takes on the following guise,

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\,=\,\operatorname{Id}+\delta^{2}P\boldsymbol{b}P^{\bot}\nabla\big(-\triangle-\delta\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}P^{\bot}\nabla\big)^{-1}\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}P.

Using Neumann series, this yields

(4.2)

\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\,=\,\operatorname{Id}+\delta\,\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla),\qquad\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\,:=\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-\delta)^{n}P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P,

in terms of the singular convolution operator

(4.3)

\boldsymbol{K}\,:=\,\nabla\triangle^{-1}\nabla\cdot\penalty\ :\penalty\ \operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}.

As we have

\|\boldsymbol{K}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}=1,

the Neumann series (
4.2
) is obviously convergent on
\mathcal{B}(\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d})
provided that
\delta<1
. Note that this calculation for
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
holds in any space dimension.

4.2.
Singular convolution operator
\boldsymbol{K}

The following provides an explicit description of the singular convolution operator
\boldsymbol{K}
defined in (
4.3
) above.

Lemma 4.1
.

The kernel of the operator
\boldsymbol{K}
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
can be decomposed as

\boldsymbol{K}(x)\,=\,\tfrac{1}{d}\delta(x)\operatorname{Id}\,+\,\boldsymbol{L}(x),

where the second term is given by the Calderón–Zygmund kernel

\textstyle\boldsymbol{L}(x)\,:=\,-\operatorname{p.v.}\tfrac{1}{|B|}|x|^{-d}\big(\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}-\tfrac{1}{d}\operatorname{Id}\big).

In particular, for all
1<q<\infty
, by the Calderón–Zygmund theory, the operator
\boldsymbol{K}
is bounded on
\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
with operator norm

\|\boldsymbol{K}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\,\leq\,\tfrac{Cq^{2}}{q-1}.

Proof.

The kernel of
\triangle
is given by the Green’s function
G(x)=|\partial B|^{-1}(2-d)^{-1}|x|^{2-d}
(and
G(x)=|\partial B|^{-1}\log|x|
in case
d=2
), so that the kernel of
\boldsymbol{K}
is obtained by integrating by parts in the representation
\boldsymbol{K}g(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(y)\nabla^{2}g(x-y)dy
for
g\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Alternatively, we may also argue by decomposing the symbol of the operator
\boldsymbol{K}
as

\tfrac{\xi\otimes\xi}{|\xi|^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{d}\operatorname{Id}+\tfrac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\big(\xi\otimes\xi-\tfrac{1}{d}|\xi|^{2}\operatorname{Id}\big),

and by appealing to the general result of
[
32
, Theorem 3.5]
to deduce the kernel.
Next, the Calderón–Zygmund theory ensures that
\boldsymbol{L}
satisfies a weak-
\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
bound, on top of being bounded on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, hence the claimed bound on
\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
follows by Marcinkiewicz interpolation for
1<q\leq 2
,
and by duality for
2\leq q<\infty
.
∎

The Calderón–Zygmund theory further yields the following approximation result. We shall use it occasionally to express operators as limits of absolutely converging integrals, which can then be manipulated more freely. The proof is standard and is skipped for shortness.

Lemma 4.2
.

In terms of the truncated kernels

\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}(x)\,:=\,\tfrac{1}{d}\delta(x)\operatorname{Id}+\boldsymbol{L}(x)\mathds{1}_{|x|>\eta},

we have for all
1<q<\infty
and
g\in\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
,

\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\|\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}g-\boldsymbol{K}g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0,\qquad\limsup_{\eta\downarrow 0}\|\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\leq\tfrac{Cq^{2}}{q-1}.

4.3.
Mixed Lebesgue spaces

It is often convenient to distinguish between the behavior of singular kernels on small and large scales. For that purpose, for all
1\leq p,q<\infty
, we shall consider the mixed Lebesgue space
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for the norm

\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,:=\,\bigg(\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big(\int_{Q(z)}|g|^{q}\Big)^{\frac{p}{q}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Note that
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that the norm is equivalent to

\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\simeq\,\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Big(\int_{Q(x)}|g|^{q}\Big)^{\frac{p}{q}}dx\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

For all
1\leq p,q,r<\infty
, we further consider the mixed Lebesque space
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))
as the closure of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))
for the norm

\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}\,:=\,\bigg(\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big(\int_{Q(z)}\mathbb{E}\left[|g|^{r}\right]^{\frac{q}{r}}\Big)^{\frac{p}{q}}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Note that those definitions can be naturally adapted also to possibly infinite exponents
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
.
For
1<p,q,r<\infty
, the singular convolution operator
\boldsymbol{K}
on
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can be extended as an operator on the mixed space
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))
,
and we show that the same operator bounds hold as in Lemma
4.1
in this mixed setting.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
1<p\leq q\leq r\leq 2
, the singular convolution operator
\boldsymbol{K}
on
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
extends as a bounded operator on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
with

\|\boldsymbol{K}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}}\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{p-1}.

Note that the same result holds in particular on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into two steps.

Step
1. Proof that for all
1<p\leq r\leq 2
we have

\|\boldsymbol{K}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}}\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{p-1}.

Starting point is Lemma
4.1
, which yields by Fubini’s theorem, for all
1<r\leq 2
and
g\in\operatorname{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
,

\|\boldsymbol{K}g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{r-1}\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}.

As
\boldsymbol{K}
is given by a Calderón–Zygmund kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, cf. Lemma
4.1
, we may then appeal to
[
5
]
and deduce the following Banach-valued weak-
\operatorname{L}^{1}
estimate,

|\{x:\|\boldsymbol{K}g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)}>\lambda\}|\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{r-1}\lambda^{-1}\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}.

By Marcinkiewicz interpolation, this yields the claim.

Step
2. Conclusion.

Consider the trivial extension of the convolution operator
\boldsymbol{K}
to
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))^{d}
,
and further consider the operator
T
on that space given by

Th(x,\alpha,\omega)\,:=\,\int_{Q}h(x+\alpha-\alpha^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\omega)\,d\alpha^{\prime}.

For all
1<q\leq r\leq 2
, integrating over
Q
, the result of Step 1 yields

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{K}Th\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \|\boldsymbol{K}Th\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{C}{q-1}\|Th\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\tfrac{C}{q-1}\|h\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}.

As in Step 1, we may then appeal to
[
5
]
and deduce the following weak-
\operatorname{L}^{1}
estimate,

|\{x:\|\boldsymbol{K}Th\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}>\lambda\}|\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{q-1}\lambda^{-1}\|h\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))},

which implies, by Marcinkiewicz interpolation, for all
1<p\leq q\leq r\leq 2
,

\|\boldsymbol{K}Th\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{p-1}\|h\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))}.

Considering the linear map
U:\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{q}(Q;\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)))^{d}
given by

Ug(x,\alpha,\omega)\,:=\,g(x+\alpha,\omega),

noting that
\boldsymbol{K}TUg(x,\alpha,\omega)=(\boldsymbol{K}g)(x+\alpha,\omega)
, and applying the above with
h=Ug
, the conclusion follows.
∎

4.4.
Discrete truncations of
\boldsymbol{K}

In order to tackle the perturbative expansion (
4.2
), it will be convenient to discretize the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
at a given scale
R>0
. More precisely, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define
z_{R}(x)\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the lattice point satisfying

(4.4)

|x-z_{R}(x)|_{\infty}\,=\,\min_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|x-z|_{\infty},

where we take
z_{R}(x)
to be the smallest with respect to lexicographic order in case the minimum is attained by several lattice points. In these terms, for all
\ell\geq R
, we define the following truncated kernel,

(4.5)

\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}(x,y)\,:=\,\boldsymbol{K}(x-y)\mathds{1}_{|z_{R}(x)-z_{R}(y)|_{\infty}\leq\ell}.

Of course, we note that
\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}
is no longer a convolution kernel, but we show that it still satisfies the same operator estimates.

Lemma 4.4
.

For all
1<p\leq q\leq r\leq 2
, uniformly with respect to
\ell\geq R>0
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}}\,\leq\,\tfrac{C}{p-1}.

Proof.

In view of the proof of Lemma
4.3
above, it suffices to prove the result on
\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
.
First define the radially-truncated kernel

\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}(x)\,:=\,\boldsymbol{K}(x)\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq\ell}\,=\,\tfrac{1}{d}\delta(x)\operatorname{Id}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x),\qquad\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x)\,:=\,\boldsymbol{L}(x)\mathds{1}_{|x|\leq\ell}.

We check that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}
satisfies the following properties, uniformly with respect to
\ell>0
,

(a)

|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x)|\lesssim|x|^{-d}
for all
x
;

(b)

\int_{a<|x|<b}\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x)dx=0
for all
b>a>0
;

(c)

\int_{x:|x|>2|y|}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x-y)|\,dx\,\lesssim\,1
for all
y
.

While properties (a) and (b) are obvious from Lemma
4.1
, property (c) is obtained as follows: noting that the inequalities
|x|>2|y|
and
|x-y|>\ell
imply
|x|>\frac{2}{3}\ell
, and that the inequalities
|x|>2|y|
and
|x|>\ell
imply
|x-y|>\frac{1}{2}\ell
, we can estimate

\displaystyle\int_{x:|x|>2|y|}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x)-\tilde{\boldsymbol{L}}_{\ell}(x-y)|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{x:|x|>2|y|}|\boldsymbol{L}(x)-\boldsymbol{L}(x-y)|\,dx+2\int_{x:\frac{1}{2}\ell<|x|\leq\ell}|\boldsymbol{L}(x)|\,dx,

from which property (c) follows as
\boldsymbol{L}
is itself a Calderón–Zygmund kernel.
From (a)–(c), the Calderón-Zygmund theory can be applied as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
, which entails that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
satisfies the following
\operatorname{L}^{p}
estimates: uniformly with respect to
\ell>0
, we have for all
1<p<\infty
,

(4.6)

\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\,\leq\,\tfrac{Cp^{2}}{p-1}.

It remains to compare
\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}
to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
. Noting that for
\ell\geq R
we have

\overline{B_{s_{1}(\ell)}(x)}\,\subset\,\big\{y:|z_{R}(x)-z_{R}(y)|_{\infty}\leq\ell\big\}\,\subset\,\overline{B_{s_{2}(\ell)}(x)},

with
s_{1}(\ell),s_{2}(\ell)\simeq\ell
,
we find for all
g\in\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell})g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{-d}|g(y)|\,\mathds{1}_{s_{1}(\ell)\leq|x-y|\leq s_{2}(\ell)}\,dy\bigg)^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ell^{-dp}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(y)|\,\mathds{1}_{s_{1}(\ell)\leq|x-y|\leq s_{2}(\ell)}\,dy\bigg)^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|g\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{p}.

Combined with (
4.6
), this yields the conclusion.
∎

4.5.
A useful operator notation

The following notation is particularly convenient in the continuum setting as it
allows to stick very closely to Bourgain’s discrete notation in
[
7
]
while singling out local regularity issues: given
1\leq q\leq\infty
, we define the locally averaged kernel of a bounded operator
T
on
\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
as

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{q}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{q}(x,y)\,:=\,\|\mathds{1}_{Q(x)}T\mathds{1}_{Q(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}.

It will occasionally be useful to further include spatial averaging with a different power over some intermediate scale
R\geq 1
:
given
1\leq p,q\leq\infty
, we define the
R
-locally averaged kernel of a bounded operator
T
on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
as

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{p,q;R}:R\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times R\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{p,q;R}(x,y)\,:=\,\|\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(x)}T\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}.

Note the useful lower bound,

(4.7)

[\![T]\!]_{p,q;R}(x,y)\,\geq\,[\![T]\!]_{p,q;1}(x,y)\,\simeq\,[\![T]\!]_{q}(x,y).

(To see the latter equivalence, note that
[\![T]\!]_{p,q;1}(x,y)
depends only on local integrability properties and that for these all
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
norms are equivalent for different values of
p
.)
As we shall often consider operators acting on random fields, that is, on functions defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
, we further define corresponding notions of averaged kernels: given
1\leq q,r\leq\infty
,
for a bounded operator
T
on
\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{q,r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{q,r}(x,y)\,:=\,\|\mathds{1}_{Q(x)}T\mathds{1}_{Q(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}},

and given
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty

and
R\geq 1
, for a bounded operator
T
on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
, we define

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}:R\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times R\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+},

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\,:=\,\|\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(x)}T\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}}.

For
p,q,r<\infty
, as the dual space of
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
is clearly isomorphic to
\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega))^{d}
, the above definition can be equivalently written as

(4.8)

[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\,=\,\sup\Big\{|\langle\phi_{x},T\phi_{y}\rangle|\penalty\ :\penalty\ \phi_{x},\phi_{y}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))^{d},\\
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{x})\subset Q_{R}(x)\times\Omega,\penalty\ {\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{y})\subset Q_{R}(y)\times\Omega,\\[-2.84526pt]
\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r^{\prime}}(\Omega))}=\|\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))}=1\Big\},

where henceforth we use the short-hand notation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
for the pairing of vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega
, that is,

\langle\phi_{x},T\phi_{y}\rangle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{x}\cdot T\phi_{y}\right].

Given
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
,
for a bounded operator
T
on
\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)^{d}
, extended trivially as an operator on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}
, note that we have
[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\leq\mathds{1}_{x=y}\|T\|_{\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega)^{d}}
for all
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
Moreover, given two operators
T,T^{\prime}
on
\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))
, we have

\displaystyle[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|T\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}},

(4.9)

\displaystyle[\![TT^{\prime}]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}[\![T]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,z)\,[\![T^{\prime}]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(z,y).

Finally, we show that the following estimate holds for the truncated kernel
\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
1<p\leq q\leq r\leq 2
, uniformly with respect to
\ell\geq R\geq 1
, we have

[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\,\lesssim\,\tfrac{1}{p-1}\wedge(|x-y|-2R)_{+}^{-d}\,\lesssim\,\tfrac{1}{p-1}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-y)\rangle^{-d}.

Proof.

For
|x-y|_{\infty}>2R
, the pointwise decay of the kernel yields

[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\,\lesssim\,|x-y|^{-d}.

As Lemma
4.4
further yields

[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}]\!]_{p,q,r;R}(x,y)\,\leq\,\|\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{r}(\Omega))^{d}}\,\lesssim\,\tfrac{1}{p-1},

the conclusion follows.
∎

4.6.
Bourgain’s deterministic lemma

Using Lemma
4.5
and (
4.9
), say for
R=1
,
a direct estimate of the terms in the expansion (
4.2
) for
\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)
yields for all
1<p\leq r\leq 2
,

[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P]\!]_{p,r}(x,y)\,\leq\,(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n}\sum_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle x-z_{1}\rangle^{-d}\ldots\langle z_{n-1}-y\rangle^{-d}.

Therefore, evaluating the sum,

[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P]\!]_{p,r}(x,y)\,\leq\,(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n}\langle x-y\rangle^{-d}\log(2+|x-y|)^{n-1}.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, using
\log t\leq\varepsilon^{-1}t^{\varepsilon}
for
t\geq 1
, this bound translates into

(4.10)

[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P]\!]_{p,r}(x,y)\,\leq\,n^{n}\varepsilon^{1-n}(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n}\langle x-y\rangle^{\varepsilon-d}.

The combinatorial factor
n^{n}
destroys any possible use of this direct estimate in the perturbative expansion (
4.2
).
Instead, in
[
7
, Lemma 1]
, Bourgain made a more clever use of the global structure of the terms to show that this combinatorial factor can, in fact, be removed. In the present continuum setting, Bourgain’s argument can be adapted as follows. We include a general statement for later purposes.

Lemma 4.6

(Bourgain’s deterministic lemma)
.

Given
n\geq 1
, let
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n}\geq R\geq 1
and let
\boldsymbol{b}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}
be bounded self-adjoint operators on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)^{d}
such that
\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z)}\boldsymbol{b}_{j}=\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z)}
for all
j
and
z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Define

B_{p,q}\,:=\,\sup_{j}\|\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))^{d}}.

Then, for all
1<p\leq q\leq 2
, we have

[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}]\!]_{p,q,2;R}(x,y)\,\leq\,(CB_{p,q}\theta^{-1})^{n}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-y)\rangle^{\theta-d},

where we have set
\theta:=\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}>0
, provided that
2\theta<d
.

Proof.

For
|x-y|_{\infty}\leq 2nR
, the claimed bound is an immediate consequence of (
4.9
) and Lemma
4.4
in form of

\displaystyle[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}]\!]_{p,q,2;R}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\tfrac{C}{p-1}B_{p,q})^{n}.

Now let
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be fixed with
|x-y|_{\infty}>2nR
,
and consider
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))^{d}
with
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{x})\subset Q_{R}(x)\times\Omega
and
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{y})\subset Q_{R}(y)\times\Omega
.
Decomposing each operator
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
as

\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\,=\,\sum_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z)},

we may then write by Fubini’s theorem,

(4.11)

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\\
\,=\,\sum_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big\langle\phi_{x},(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n-1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n-1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle,

since this sum is absolutely convergent: indeed, we can bound

\Big|\Big\langle\phi_{x},(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n-1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n-1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\Big|\\[2.84526pt]
\,\leq\,B_{p,q}^{n}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\|\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}]\!]_{p,q,2;R}(x,z_{1})\ldots[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}]\!]_{p,q,2;R}(z_{n-1},y),

and the summability of the right-hand side over
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
immediately follows from Lemma
4.5
.
To estimate the sum (
4.11
), we shall distinguish with respect to the first interval
|z_{j}-z_{j+1}|_{\infty}
that reaches the largest dyadic value, and we denote by
j=j_{1}
the corresponding index. More precisely, setting for notational simplicity
z_{0}:=x
and
z_{n}:=y
, we define for all
0\leq j_{1}<n
and
m\geq 0
,

S_{j_{1};R}^{m}(x,y):=\Big\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}\penalty\ :\penalty\ \max_{0\leq j<n}|z_{j}-z_{j+1}|_{\infty}\leq 2^{m+1}R,\\
\penalty\ |z_{j_{1}}-z_{j_{1}+1}|_{\infty}>2^{m}R,\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \max_{0\leq j<j_{1}}|z_{j}-z_{j+1}|_{\infty}\leq 2^{m}R\Big\}.

Note that by the triangle inequality the condition
|x-y|_{\infty}>2nR
implies

\max_{0\leq j<n}|z_{j}-z_{j+1}|_{\infty}\,>\,2R.

In these terms, the sum (
4.11
) becomes

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\,=\,\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S^{m}_{j_{1};R}(x,y)}\\
\times\Big\langle\phi_{x}\,,\,(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n-1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n-1}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle.

For
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S^{m}_{j_{1};R}(x,y)
, the triangle inequality

|x-y|_{\infty}\,\leq\,\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|z_{j}-z_{j+1}|_{\infty}\,\leq\,n2^{m+1}R

entails that the sum over
m
is automatically restricted to
2^{m}R\geq\frac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}
. Similarly, the sums over
z_{j_{1}},z_{j_{1}+1}
are restricted to
|x-z_{j_{1}}|_{\infty}\leq n2^{m}R
and
|y-z_{j_{1}+1}|_{\infty}\leq n2^{m+1}R
, respectively.
Further noting that the truncated kernels are defined in (
4.5
) to satisfy for all
z,z^{\prime}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\geq R
,

\mathds{1}_{|z-z^{\prime}|_{\infty}\leq\ell^{\prime}}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z)}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell;R}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z^{\prime})}\,=\,\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z)}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell\wedge\ell^{\prime};R}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z^{\prime})},

the above can be rewritten as

(4.12)

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\,=\,\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{2^{m}R\geq\frac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}}\\
\times\sum_{z_{j_{1}},z_{j_{1}+1}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\mathds{1}_{2^{m}R<|z_{j_{1}}-z_{j_{1}+1}|_{\infty}\leq 2^{m+1}R}\,\mathds{1}_{|x-z_{j_{1}}|_{\infty},|y-z_{j_{1}+1}|_{\infty}\leq n2^{m+1}R}\\
\times\Big\langle\phi_{x}\,,\,(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1}\wedge(2^{m}R);R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}}\wedge(2^{m}R);R}\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}})\\
\times(\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{1}})}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}+1};R}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{1}+1})})\\
\times\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+1}(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}+2}\wedge(2^{m+1}R);R}\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+2})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n}\wedge(2^{m+1}R)}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle.

In other words, the restricted summation over
S_{j_{1};R}^{m}
has been replaced by kernel truncations.
Using the pointwise decay of the kernel
\boldsymbol{K}
along the long segment
[z_{j_{1}},z_{j_{1}+1}]
, cf. Lemma
4.1
,
we get

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\,\lesssim\,\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2^{m}R)^{-d}\mathds{1}_{2^{m}R\geq\frac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}}\\
\times\sum_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\atop|x-z|_{\infty}\leq n2^{m+1}R}\int_{Q_{R}(z)}\Big\|(\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}}\wedge(2^{m}R);R})\ldots(\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1}\wedge(2^{m}R);R})\phi_{x}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\\
\times\sum_{z^{\prime}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\atop|y-z^{\prime}|_{\infty}\leq n2^{m+1}R}\int_{Q_{R}(z^{\prime})}\Big\|\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+1}(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}+2}\wedge(2^{m+1}R);R}\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+2})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n}\wedge(2^{m+1}R)}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\Big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then using Hölder’s inequality to bound the sums over
z,z^{\prime}
, we find

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\,\lesssim\,\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{n-1}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(n2^{m+1}R)^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}}(2^{m}R)^{-d}\mathds{1}_{2^{m}R\geq\frac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}}\\
\times\|(\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}}\wedge(2^{m}R);R})\ldots(\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1}\wedge(2^{m}R);R})\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\times\|\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+1}(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{j_{1}+2}\wedge(2^{m+1}R);R}\boldsymbol{b}_{j_{1}+2})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n}\wedge(2^{m+1}R)}\boldsymbol{b}_{n})\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}.

By Lemma
4.4
together with the boundedness assumption for the
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
’s, this yields

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\\
\,\lesssim\,B_{p,q}^{n}\big(\tfrac{C}{p-1}\big)^{n-1}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\|\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}(2^{m}R)^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}-d}\,\mathds{1}_{2^{m}R\geq\frac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}},

and thus, evaluating the dyadic series, provided that
\frac{4d}{p^{\prime}}<d
,

\langle\phi_{x},\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\phi_{y}\rangle\,\lesssim\,B_{p,q}^{n}\big(\tfrac{C}{p-1}\big)^{n-1}|x-y|^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}-d}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\|\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}.

As
\phi_{x}
is supported in
Q_{R}(x)\times\Omega
, we find by Jensen’s inequality,

\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\,\lesssim\,R^{d\frac{2-p}{p}}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\,=\,R^{d-\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}.

Using this and taking the supremum over
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
, the above yields

[\![\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{1};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\ldots\boldsymbol{K}_{\ell_{n};R}\boldsymbol{b}_{n}]\!]_{p,q,2;R}\,\lesssim\,B_{p,q}^{n}\big(\tfrac{C}{p-1}\big)^{n-1}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-y)\rangle^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}-d},

and the conclusion follows.
∎

5.
Stretched exponential mixing setting

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.1
in the exponential mixing setting
(H
1
)
.
More precisely, the
\alpha
-mixing condition will be used in form of the following covariance inequality (see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 1.2.3]
): for all
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all bounded random variables
X,Y
, if
X
is
\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{U})
-measurable and if
Y
is
\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{V})
-measurable, we have for all
p,q,r\geq 1
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=1
,

(5.1)

|\!\operatorname{Cov}\left[{X};{Y}\right]\!|\,\leq\,8\big[C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}{\operatorname{dist}}(U,V)^{\gamma})\big]^{\frac{1}{r}}\|X\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}(\Omega)}\|Y\|_{\operatorname{L}^{q}(\Omega)}.

We start with several preliminary ingredients and conclude the proof of Theorem
3.1
in Section
5.4
. We follow the general strategy of Bourgain
[
7
]
, improved by Kim and the second-named author in
[
26
]
, but the present continuum setting requires several important modifications, starting with the coarse-grained notion of irreducibility in Definition
5.1
below. We emphasize that most of the present modifications are also needed in the discrete setting beyond the particular i.i.d. case treated in
[
7
,
26
]
.

5.1.
Path decomposition

Starting point is the perturbative expansion (
4.2
), and we shall proceed to a separate analysis of the kernel of the different terms
\{P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P\}_{n\geq 1}
.
Given
R\geq 1
and
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, arguing as in (
4.11
), we can decompose for all
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))^{d}
with
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{x})\subset Q_{R}(x)\times\Omega
and
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{y})\subset Q_{R}(y)\times\Omega
,

(5.2)

\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P\phi_{y}\rangle\\
\,=\,\sum_{z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big\rangle,

where the series is absolutely convergent.
At first sight, from the deterministic estimates of Section
4.6
, this expression might seem to have no reason to enjoy a better decay than the kernel
\boldsymbol{K}
itself, that is,
O(|x-y|^{-d})
. Yet, this decay happens to be drastically improved due to couplings that appear when computing the expectation of products of the stationary random field
\boldsymbol{b}
in this expression.
The resulting cancellations can be characterized in terms of the following notion of reducibility, which is a coarse-grained version of the one introduced by Bourgain in
[
7
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
R\geq 1
, and
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The sequence
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is said to be an
R
-reducible path
(of length
n
from
x_{0}
to
x_{n}
) if there exists
0\leq j<n
such that

|x_{a}-x_{b}|_{\infty}>R\qquad\text{for all $0\leq a\leq j$ and $j<b\leq n$}.

Otherwise, the sequence
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is said to be an
R
-irreducible path
.
We denote by
D_{R}^{n}(x,y)
the subset of elements
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}
such that
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
is an
R
-reducible path.

We emphasize that the condition
|x_{a}-x_{b}|_{\infty}>R
in this definition means that the cubes
Q_{R}(x_{a})
and
Q_{R}(x_{b})
are not neighbors, hence are at distance
\gtrsim R
of one another: this distance will allow us to use approximate independence in form of
\alpha
-mixing to neglect the contribution of those reducible paths, cf. Lemma
5.4
.
To start gently, let us illustrate this definition in the case when the coefficient field
\boldsymbol{a}
has a finite range of dependence bounded by
R
,
showing that the contribution of
R
-reducible paths vanishes in that case.
Although a priori counterintuitive, it will be crucial to use strict subsets
A(x,y)
of the set of irreducible paths: indeed, restricting the sum too much may destroy the special oscillatory structure of the composition of Calderón–Zygmund kernels and lead to worse estimates; see Remark
5.3
below.

Lemma 5.2
.

Assume momentarily that
\boldsymbol{a}
has finite range of dependence in the sense that for all
U,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
{\operatorname{dist}}(U,V)>R
the
\sigma
-algebras
\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{U})
and
\sigma(\boldsymbol{a}|_{V})
are independent.
Then, for all
n\geq 1
, the
n
th term in the perturbative expansion (
4.2
) can be written as follows: for all
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, for all
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
with
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{x})\subset Q_{R}(x)
and
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{y})\subset Q_{R}(y)
,

(5.3)

\big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})^{n}P\phi_{y}\big\rangle\\
\,=\,\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in A(x,y)}\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big\rangle,

where
A(x,y)
stands for any subset of
(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}
containing the set
(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}\setminus D_{R}^{n}(x,y)
of
R
-irreducible paths from
x
to
y
.

Proof.

Starting from (
5.2
), it suffices to show that the contribution of any
R
-reducible path vanishes in the case when
\boldsymbol{a}
has finite range of dependence bounded by
R
. For that purpose, we start by appealing to Lemma
4.2
to express operators as limits of absolutely converging integrals: for all
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we have

\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\\
\,=\,\lim_{\eta\downarrow 0}\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big\rangle,

where the limit exists. For fixed
\eta>0
, by Fubini’s theorem, the argument of the limit can now be written as follows in terms of truncated integral kernels,

\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\\
\,=\,\int\!\!\ldots\!\!\int_{Q_{R}(x)\times Q_{R}(z_{1})\times\ldots\times Q_{R}(z_{n-1})\times Q_{R}(y)}\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{b}(y_{0})P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}(y_{1})\ldots P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}(y_{n})\big]\\
\times\phi_{x}(y_{0})\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}(y_{0}-y_{1})\ldots\boldsymbol{K}^{(\eta)}(y_{n-1}-y_{n})\phi_{y}(y_{n})\,dy_{0}\ldots dy_{n},

where the integral is indeed trivially absolutely convergent due to the small-scale cut-off,
and where we omit to track matrix contractions for notational simplicity.
Now here is the key point: by definition, if
\boldsymbol{b}
has finite dependence length bounded by
R
as presently assumed, then the factor
\mathbb{E}\big[\boldsymbol{b}(y_{0})P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}(y_{1})\ldots P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}(y_{n})\big]
in the above expression vanishes for all
y_{0}\in Q_{R}(x),y_{1}\in Q_{R}(z_{1}),\ldots,y_{n-1}\in Q_{R}(z_{n-1}),y_{n}\in Q_{R}(y)
whenever
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
is an
R
-reducible path. The sum can therefore be restricted by removing any subset of
R
-reducible paths, and the conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 5.3

(Summing irreducible paths)
.

When restricting the summation (
5.3
) to irreducible paths, we can easily understand the best decay rate
O(\langle x-y\rangle^{-3d})
from straightforward graphical considerations.
Indeed, given an irreducible path
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
, consider the set
Z:=\{x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y\}
, and define the following equivalence relation on
Z
: two elements
a,b\in Z
are said to be equivalent if there is
k\geq 0
and a sequence
\{y_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq k}\subset Z
such that
y_{0}=a
,
y_{k}=b
, and such that
|y_{i}-y_{i+1}|_{\infty}=R
for all
0\leq i<k
.
Now consider the set
V
that is the quotient of
Z
with respect to this equivalence relation,
and consider the non-oriented graph
G
induced by the path
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
on the quotient set
V
.
Note that the points
x
and
y
are not equivalent provided that
|x-y|_{\infty}>2Rn
. Choosing representatives, we may then write
V\equiv\{x,y,v_{1},\ldots,v_{l}\}\subset Z
for some
0\leq l<n
.
As the path
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
is irreducible, we can deduce that the vertices
x
and
y
in the induced graph
G
have odd degrees
\geq 3
and that each other vertex has even degree
\geq 2
.
Due to this property, we can find three disjoint trails
L_{1},L_{2},L_{3}
from
x
to
y
in
G
. Separately evaluating the sums in (
5.3
) along each of these trails, the decay rate
O(\langle x-y\rangle^{-3d})
follows up to logarithmic corrections.
Yet, as this argument is based on a direct summation of iterated kernels, which is key to take full advantage of irreducibility, it would only provide an estimate with a prefactor
O(n^{n})
similarly as in (
4.10
); see also
[
26
, Section 1.3]
.
We skip the detail as such brutal estimates are anyway not summable over
n
and thus of no use.
In the sequel, as in
[
7
,
26
]
, the irreducibility will be used instead in a much weaker, minimal way, in order not to destroy the special oscillatory structure of compositions of Calderón–Zygmund kernels. More precisely, not all reducible paths will be removed from the summation, and the key technical ingredient is given by Lemma
5.5
below.

5.2.
Neglecting reducible paths

In the mixing setting, we cannot appeal to Lemma
5.2
above to remove the contribution of reducible paths, but we show that such paths still have only a negligible contribution for
R\gg 1
.
We proceed by a direct summation of reducible paths, which is why one loses a prefactor
O(n^{n})
similarly as in (
4.10
).
In the stretched exponential mixing setting, we shall see that this factor can be compensated by the excellent mixing rate. We do not know how to improve on the present estimate and leave it as an open question, in link with the treatment of
algebraic

\alpha
-mixing rates.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
n,R\geq 1
, and
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
the contribution of
R
-reducible paths in (
5.2
) is estimated as follows, for all
1<p\leq q\leq r\leq 2
and
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in D_{R}^{n}(x,y)}[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P]\!]_{p,q,r;R}\\
\,\leq\,(Cn)^{n}(\varepsilon(p-1))^{1-n}\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-y)\rangle^{\varepsilon-d},

where we recall that
D_{R}^{n}(x,y)
stands for the set of elements
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that the path
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
is
R
-reducible, cf. Definition
5.1
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be fixed, and consider
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
with
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{x})\subset Q_{R}(x)
and
{\operatorname{supp}}(\phi_{y})\subset Q_{R}(y)
.
For
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in D_{R}^{n}(x,y)
, as the path
(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y)
is
R
-reducible, there exists some
0\leq j_{0}<n
such that

{\operatorname{dist}}\big(\{x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{j_{0}}\},\{z_{j_{0}+1},\ldots,z_{n-1},y\}\big)\,>\,R.

Then writing

\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\\
\,=\,\int_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})}\operatorname{Cov}\Big[\Big((P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})}\boldsymbol{K})\ldots(P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})}\boldsymbol{K})P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}P\phi_{x}\Big)(v),\\
\Big((\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}+1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\Big)(v)\Big]\,dv,

we can appeal to the exponential
\alpha
-mixing condition in form of the corresponding covariance inequality (
5.1
), followed by Hölder’s inequality on
Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})
, to the effect of

\Big|\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\Big|\\
\,\leq\,8\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|(P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})}\boldsymbol{K})\ldots(P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})}\boldsymbol{K})P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}P\phi_{x}\big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{1}(Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}});\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\times\big\|(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}+1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})\times\Omega)}.

Using a pointwise bound on the kernel of
\boldsymbol{K}
along the long segment
|z_{j_{0}}-z_{j_{0}+1}|_{\infty}>R
in the last factor, and further using Hölder’s
inequality, we are led to

\Big|\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\Big|\\
\,\lesssim\,\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}-d}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(z_{j_{0}}-z_{j_{0}+1})\rangle^{-d}\\
\times\big\|\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}})}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}-1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{x}\big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\times\big\|\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}+1})}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j_{0}+2})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P\phi_{y}\big\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}.

Hence, by Lemma
4.5
,

\Big|\Big\langle\phi_{x},P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})\phi_{y}\Big\rangle\Big|\\
\,\lesssim\,\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}R^{\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}-d}(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n-1}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\|\phi_{y}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
\times\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-z_{1})\rangle^{-d}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(z_{1}-z_{2})\rangle^{-d}\ldots\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(z_{n-1}-y)\rangle^{-d}.

As
\phi_{x}
is supported in
Q_{R}(x)
, we find by Hölder’s inequality,

\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,R^{d\frac{2-p}{p}}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,=\,R^{d-\frac{2d}{p^{\prime}}}\|\phi_{x}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p^{\prime}}_{q^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Using this and taking the supremum over
\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
, the above yields

[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P]\!]_{p,q,2;R}\\
\,\lesssim\,\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n-1}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-z_{1})\rangle^{-d}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(z_{1}-z_{2})\rangle^{-d}\ldots\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(z_{n-1}-y)\rangle^{-d}.

Summing over
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
, we deduce

\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in D_{R}^{n}(x,y)}[\![P\boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{1})})\ldots(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b}\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{n-1})})(\boldsymbol{K}P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{b})P]\!]_{p,q,2;R}\\
\,\lesssim\,\big(C_{0}\exp(-\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}R^{\gamma})\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\tfrac{C}{p-1})^{n-1}\langle\tfrac{1}{R}(x-y)\rangle^{-d}\log(2+\tfrac{1}{R}|x-y|)^{n-1}.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, using
\log t\leq\varepsilon^{-1}t^{\varepsilon}
for
t\geq 1
, this yields the conclusion.
∎

5.3.
Bourgain’s disjointification lemma

We shall use the following generalized version of Bourgain’s disjointification lemma
[
7
, pp.319–320]
(see also
[
26
, Lemma 2.7]
). The present version is adapted to the new coarse-grained notion of reducibility, cf. Definition
5.1
.
In a nutshell, this result shows that sufficiently simple path restrictions do not destroy the behavior of compositions of kernels.

Lemma 5.5
.

Given
n\geq 1
, let
\boldsymbol{L}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{L}_{n}
be bounded operators and let
\boldsymbol{b}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_{n}
be bounded multiplication operators on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)^{d}
.
Given

,
x,y\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and

consider the function

Remark 5.6
.

Given

Proof of Lemma
5.5
.

First note that the restricted set
S^{\prime}
in the statement can be rewritten as follows, in terms of
N_{R}:=\{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|z|_{\infty}\leq R\}
,

S^{\prime}\,=\,S\cap\bigcap_{l=1}^{m}\bigcap_{\alpha\in N_{R}}\Big\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}:\{z_{j}+\alpha:j\in E_{l}\}\cap\{z_{j}:j\in F_{l}\}=\varnothing\Big\},

where we let
z_{0}:=x
and
z_{n}:=y
for notational simplicity.
Without loss of generality, we can assume
E_{l}\cap F_{l}=\varnothing
for all
l
, as otherwise
S^{\prime}=\varnothing
.
By a direct induction argument, successively adding restrictions to the set
S
, we observe that it suffices to prove the following reduced result:
given
E,F\subset\{0,\ldots,n\}
with
E\cap F=\varnothing
, and given
\alpha\in N_{R}
, considering the following restriction of the set
S
,

(5.7)

S^{\prime\prime}\,:=\,S\cap\Big\{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in(R\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{n-1}:\{z_{j}+\alpha:j\in E\}\cap\{z_{j}:j\in F\}=\varnothing\Big\},

we have

(5.8)

\bigg|\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S^{\prime\prime}}T_{x,y}^{n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\bigg|\,\leq\,2^{\sharp E+\sharp F-1}M(x,y).

We turn to the proof of this reduced result (
5.8
), for which we essentially follow Bourgain’s original argument in
[
7
, pp.319–320]
.
We start by introducing an additional set of variables
\bar{\theta}=(\theta_{z})_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with
\theta_{z}\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
, and
we define

r_{j}(x,\bar{\theta})\,:=\,\exp\Big(i\big(\mathds{1}_{j\in E}\theta_{z_{R}(x)+\alpha}-\mathds{1}_{j\in F}\theta_{z_{R}(x)}\big)\Big),

where we recall the definition of the map
z_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in (
4.4
).
By definition, this means for all
z_{j}\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\displaystyle r_{j}(\cdot,\bar{\theta})\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j})}\exp\Big(i\big(\mathds{1}_{j\in E}\theta_{z_{j}+\alpha}-\mathds{1}_{j\in F}\theta_{z_{j}}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{Q_{R}(z_{j})}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\exp(i\theta_{z_{j}+\alpha})&:&\text{if $j\in E$},\\
\exp(-i\theta_{z_{j}})&:&\text{if $j\in F$},\\
1&:&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

By assumption (
5.4
), using stability under the change (
5.5
) with the above choice of functions
r_{j}
’s,
and factoring out the phases,
we obtain

(5.10)

\bigg|\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S}T_{x,y}^{n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\exp\Big(i\sum_{j\in E}\theta_{z_{j}+\alpha}-i\sum_{j\in F}\theta_{z_{j}}\Big)\bigg|\,\leq\,M(x,y),

where the left-hand side is now viewed as a Steinhaus polynomial in the variables
\bar{\theta}
.
Next, we consider the Poisson kernel

P_{t}(\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}t^{|n|}e^{in\theta},\qquad t\in(-1,1),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z},

and we recall that
P_{t}(\theta)\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}}e^{im\theta}P_{t}(\theta)\tfrac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,=\,t^{|m|},\qquad\text{for all $m\in\mathbb{Z}$.}

Integrating the estimate (
5.10
) over
\bar{\theta}
with respect to the product measure

\prod_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}P_{t}(\theta_{z})\tfrac{d\theta_{z}}{2\pi},

we deduce for all
t\in(-1,1)
,

(5.11)

\bigg|\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S}T_{x,y}^{n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\penalty\ t^{w(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})}\bigg|\,\leq\,M(x,y),

where the left-hand side is now viewed as a polynomial in the variable
t
and where the powers are given by

w(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\,:=\,\sum_{z\in R\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big|\sharp\{j\in E:z_{j}+\alpha=z\}-\sharp\{j\in F:z_{j}=z\}\big|.

Note that we have

w(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})\,\leq\,\sharp E+\sharp F,

and that the equality
w(z_{0},\ldots,z_{n})=\sharp E+\sharp F
holds if and only if

\{z_{j}+\alpha:j\in E\}\cap\{z_{j}:j\in F\}\,=\,\varnothing.

By the Markov brothers’ inequality, we recall that the leading-order coefficient of a polynomial of degree
D
is bounded by
2^{D-1}
times the maximum of the polynomial on
(-1,1)
.
Applied to the above situation (
5.11
), this yields

\bigg|\sum_{(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\in S}T_{x,y}^{n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1})\,\mathds{1}_{\{z_{j}+\alpha:j\in E\}\cap\{z_{j}:j\in F\}=\varnothing}\bigg|\,\leq\,2^{\sharp E+\sharp F-1}M(x,y).

By definition of
S^{\prime\prime}
, cf. (
5.7
), this precisely proves the claim (
5.8
).
∎

5.5.
Proof of Hölder continuity

We claim in Theorem
3.1
that the decay estimate (
3.5
) for the kernel of
\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)
implies that its symbol is globally of Hölder class
C_{b}^{2d-\delta K-}
. Since the decay estimate (
3.5
) does not hold pointwise on the kernel, this is not completely standard and we include a short proof. Derivatives of the symbol can be expressed through the convolution kernel as

(5.24)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(-ix)^{\alpha}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)(x)\,\frac{dx}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-i)^{|\alpha|}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{Q(z)}\int_{Q(0)}e^{-i(x-y)\cdot\xi}(x-y)^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)(x-y)\,dxdy.

Using the binomial theorem to expand
(x-y)^{\alpha}
, this can be decomposed as follows,

\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)\,=\,\frac{(-i)^{|\alpha|}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}(-1)^{|\beta|}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\big\langle{\phi_{\xi}^{\alpha-\beta}},\mathds{1}_{Q(z)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(0)}\,\phi_{\xi}^{\beta}\big\rangle,

in terms of
\phi_{\xi}^{\beta}(x):=e^{ix\cdot\xi}x^{\beta}
,
and we are thus led to

|\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)|\,\lesssim_{\alpha}\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle z\rangle^{|\alpha|}\|\mathds{1}_{Q(z)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(0)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}.

Now appealing to the decay estimate (
3.5
), we obtain

|\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)|\,\lesssim\,\delta K\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\log(2+|z|)^{K}\langle z\rangle^{\delta K+|\alpha|-3d}.

This sum is convergent provided
|\alpha|\leq 2d-1<2d-\delta K
, which proves
\bar{\mathcal{B}}\in C_{b}^{2d-1}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
It remains to show that for
|\alpha|=2d-1
the derivative
\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}
is Hölder continuous with any exponent
<1-\delta K
.
Starting from (
5.24
), we can write for all
\xi,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)-\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i(\xi+w))\\
\,=\,\frac{(-i)^{|\alpha|}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\int_{Q(z)}\int_{Q(0)}e^{-i(x-y)\cdot\xi}(1-e^{-i(x-y)\cdot w})\,(x-y)^{\alpha}\,\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)(x-y)\,dxdy.

Decomposing
1-e^{-i(x-y)\cdot w}=(1-e^{iy\cdot w})+(1-e^{-ix\cdot w})e^{iy\cdot w}
, and using again the binomial theorem to expand
(x-y)^{\alpha}
, we get

\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)-\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i(\xi+w))\,=\,\frac{(-i)^{|\alpha|}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}(-1)^{|\beta|}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle\phi_{\xi}^{\alpha-\beta},\mathds{1}_{Q(z)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(0)}\phi^{\beta}_{\xi}\rho_{w}\rangle\\
+\frac{(-i)^{|\alpha|}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\sum_{\beta\leq\alpha}(-1)^{|\beta|}\binom{\alpha}{\beta}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle\phi^{\alpha-\beta}_{\xi}\rho_{w},\mathds{1}_{Q(z)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(0)}\phi^{\beta}_{\xi+w}\rangle,

where we have further set
\rho_{w}(x):=1-e^{ix\cdot w}
.
For
0<s<1
, noting that
|\rho_{w}(x)|\leq|x|^{s}|w|^{s}
, we deduce

\frac{|\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i\xi)-\nabla_{\xi}^{\alpha}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(i(\xi+w))|}{|w|^{s}}\,\lesssim_{\alpha}\,\sum_{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle z\rangle^{|\alpha|+s}\|\mathds{1}_{Q(z)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(0)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}.

Now appealing again to the decay estimate (
3.5
), we find that this sum is convergent provided
|\alpha|+s<2d-\delta K
, which concludes the proof of Hölder continuity.
∎

6.1.
Malliavin calculus

Since the covariance function
c
is uniformly bounded, cf. (
3.2
), the Gaussian random field
G
can be viewed as a random Schwartz distribution: for all
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\kappa}
, we define
G(\zeta_{1})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\cdot\zeta_{1}
and
G(\zeta_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G\cdot\zeta_{2}
as centered Gaussian random variables with covariance

\operatorname{Cov}\left[{G(\zeta_{1})};{G(\zeta_{2})}\right]\,=\,\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{1}(x)\cdot c(x-y)\zeta_{2}(y)\,dxdy.

We define
\mathfrak{H}
as the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\kappa}
for the seminorm

\|\zeta_{1}\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\,:=\,\langle\zeta_{1},\zeta_{1}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}},\qquad\langle\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\,:=\,\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\zeta_{1}(x)\cdot c(x-y)\zeta_{2}(y)\,dxdy.

Taking the quotient with respect to the kernel of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathfrak{H}}
, the space
\mathfrak{H}
is a separable Hilbert space.
We recall some basic definitions of the Malliavin calculus with respect to the Gaussian field
G
(see e.g.
[
30
]
for details). Without loss of generality, we work under the assumption that the probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
is endowed with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by
G
, which ensures that the linear subspace

\mathcal{R}\,:=\,\Big\{g\big(G(\zeta_{1}),\ldots,G(\zeta_{n})\big)\,:\,n\in\mathbb{N},\,g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{n}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\kappa}\Big\}\,\subset\,\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)

is dense in
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
For a random variable
X
in this model subspace, say of the form
X=g(G(\zeta_{1}),\ldots,G(\zeta_{n}))
, we define its Malliavin derivative
DX\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
as

(6.2)

\displaystyle DX\,=\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\,\partial_{i}g(G(\zeta_{1}),\ldots,G(\zeta_{n})).

It can be checked that this densely defined operator
D:\mathcal{R}\subset\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathfrak{H})
is closable. Setting

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{\mathbb{D}^{1,2}(\mathfrak{H}^{\otimes r})}\,:=\,\mathbb{E}\left[XY\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\langle DX,DY\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\right],

and defining the Malliavin-Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\subset\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
as the closure of
\mathcal{R}
for the corresponding norm, we may then extend the Malliavin derivative
D
by density to this space.
Next, we define a divergence operator
D^{*}
as the adjoint of the Malliavin derivative
D
,
and we construct the so-called Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

\mathcal{L}:=D^{*}D,

which is an essentially self-adjoint nonnegative operator.
We refer e.g. to
[
30
, p.34]
for a description of the explicit action of
D^{*}
and
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{R}
:
in particular, a direct computation leads to the commutator relation

(6.3)

\displaystyle D\mathcal{L}=(1+\mathcal{L})D.

Based on the above definitions, we can state the following useful result.
It is best known in the discrete Gaussian setting
[
23
]
,
and we refer e.g. to
[
18
, Appendix A]
for a short proof.

Lemma 6.1

(Helffer–Sjöstrand identity)
.

For all
X,Y\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, the covariance can be represented as

(6.4)

\operatorname{Cov}\left[{X};{Y}\right]\,=\,\mathbb{E}\left[\langle DX,(1+\mathcal{L})^{-1}DY\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}}\right],

where the inverse operator
(1+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
is well-defined on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and has operator norm bounded by
1
since
\mathcal{L}
is nonnegative.
In particular, this implies the following Malliavin–Poincaré inequality,

\mathrm{Var}\left[X\right]\,\leq\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|DX\|_{\mathfrak{H}}^{2}\right].

We shall proceed to a direct estimation of the different terms.
For shortness, we focus on the estimation of

and

, while the terms

and

(resp.

and

) are actually similar to

(resp.
T_{1,4}^{n}(x,y)
).
Note that in the estimation of

and
T_{1,5}^{n}(x,y)
we further need to use the following computation of the second Malliavin derivative of

, instead of (

\hat{D}_{w}\hat{D}_{z}\boldsymbol{b}(x)\,=\,c_{0}(z-x)c_{0}(w-x)B_{0}^{\prime\prime}(G(x)).

We start with the estimation of

. By the triangle inequality, we note that the kernel truncations entail that the summations in that term are further restricted to

\displaystyle|z_{j_{1}}-z_{i}|_{\infty},|z_{j_{1}+1}-z_{i^{\prime}}|_{\infty},|z_{j_{2}}-z_{l}|_{\infty},|z_{j_{2}+1}-z_{l^{\prime}}|_{\infty}\,\leq\,n2^{m+1},

\displaystyle 2^{m}\geq\tfrac{1}{2n}|x-y|_{\infty}.

Arguing similarly as for (

\phi_{x},\phi_{y}
, using (
4.9
), (
6.11
), the pointwise decay of
\boldsymbol{K}
, using Lemma
4.6
,
using (

and

8.
Massive approximation

This section is devoted to the proof of Corollary
3.7
.
For all
\mu>0
, let us consider the massive solution operator
\nabla(\mu-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla)^{-1}\nabla
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
. Repeating the proof of Lemma
2.1
, this leads to a corresponding convolution operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla(\mu-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla)^{-1}\nabla\big]\,=\,\nabla(\mu-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)\nabla)^{-1}\nabla,

which is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}(\operatorname{Id}+\Phi_{\mu}(\cdot,\nabla))],

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mu}(\cdot,\nabla)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle P^{\bot}\nabla(\mu-\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla)^{-1}\nabla\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P.

We note that the operator
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)
converges strongly to
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
, and it remains to show the convergence of derivatives of the symbols. For shortness, we focus on the stretched exponential
\alpha
-mixing setting of Theorem
3.1
, but the argument can be immediately adapted to the correlated Gaussian setting of Theorem
3.2
as well.
We split the proof into two steps.

Step
1. Uniform decay estimates: we can decompose

\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)\,=\,\boldsymbol{a}_{0}+\delta\,\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu}(\nabla),

and the following kernel estimate holds for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(8.1)

\|\mathds{1}_{Q(x)}\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu}(\nabla)\mathds{1}_{Q(y)}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}}\,\lesssim\,\delta K\log(2+|x-y|)^{K}\langle x-y\rangle^{\delta K-3d}e^{-\frac{\mu}{C}|x-y|}.

This follows by repeating the proof of Theorem
3.1
with a positive mass
\mu>0
, further noting that all kernels have an additional exponential decay
e^{-\frac{\mu}{C}|\cdot|}
due to the mass; see also
[
26
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Step
2. Conclusion.

The bound (
8.1
) ensures that for any
\mu>0
the symbol of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)
is locally analytic on
i\mathbb{R}^{d}
and that it is bounded in
C_{b}^{2d-\delta K-}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
uniformly with respect to
\mu>0
.
This smoothness allows to define all higher-order homogenized coefficients
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu}^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and to get for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0<\eta\leq 1
, and
0\leq\ell\leq 2d-\eta-\delta K
, uniformly with respect to
\mu>0
,

(8.2)

\Big|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(i\xi)-\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}(i\xi)^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu;j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\Big|\,\lesssim\,|\xi|^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}\eta}.

Comparing different values of
\mu
and extracting
\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu}^{\ell}
, we deduce for all
|e|=1
,
0<\kappa,\eta\leq 1
,
\mu,\mu^{\prime}>0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq 2d-\eta-\delta K
,

(8.3)

|e^{\otimes\ell}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu;j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}}^{\ell}-\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu^{\prime};j_{1}\ldots j_{\ell}}^{\ell})|\,\lesssim\,\kappa^{-\ell}|\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(i\kappa e)-\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu^{\prime}}(i\kappa e)|+\kappa^{\frac{1}{2}\eta}\\
+\sum_{n=0}^{\ell-1}\kappa^{n-\ell}|e^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu;j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}-\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu^{\prime};j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n})|.

Since
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(\nabla)
converges strongly to
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{d}
as
\mu\downarrow 0
and since symbols are continuous, we can deduce the pointwise convergence of symbols
\bar{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}(i\xi)\to\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Passing successively to the limit
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\downarrow 0
and
\kappa\downarrow 0
in (
8.3
), we can conclude by a direct induction over
\ell
that for all
|e|=1
and
0\leq n<2d-\delta K
the limit

(8.4)

e^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\,:=\,\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\,e^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}_{\mu;j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}

does actually exist in
\mathbb{R}
.
Then passing to the limit in (
8.2
), we deduce for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\eta>0
, and
0\leq\ell\leq 2d-\eta-\delta K
,

\Big|\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)-\sum_{n=0}^{\ell}(i\xi)^{\otimes n}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}_{j_{1}\ldots j_{n}}^{n}\Big|\,\lesssim\,|\xi|^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}\eta}.

As the coefficients
\{\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{n}
satisfying this estimate are necessarily unique, they must coincide with the coefficients
\{\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{n}\}_{n}
constructed in Corollary
3.6
(up to symmetrization): the convergence (
8.4
) then yields the conclusion.
∎

9.

This section is devoted to the proof of Corollaries
3.8

Let

Proof of Corollary

Recalling the definition (
3.7

(9.1)

Step
1. Proof that for all

and
|\alpha|<d+1-\delta K

9.2
) and proceed by dyadic decomposition to estimate the oscillatory integrals.
Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth radial cutoff function with
\varphi(\xi)=1
for
|\xi|\leq 1
and
\varphi(\xi)=0
for
|\xi|>2
, and set
\psi(\xi):=\varphi(\xi)-\varphi(2\xi)

\psi_{0}(\xi):=1-\varphi(\xi)
and
\psi_{l}(\xi):=\psi(2^{l-1}\xi)
for all

\sum_{l\geq 0}\psi_{l}=1
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In these terms, let us decompose

(9.4)

\chi*(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G})(x)\,=\,\sum_{l\geq 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\hat{\chi}(\xi)\,(i\xi)^{\alpha}\,\psi_{l}(\xi)\,R(\xi)\,\frac{d\xi}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}.

We separately analyze the cases
l=0
and
l\neq 0
, and we split the proof into three further substeps.

Substep
1.1. Case
l=0
: proof that for all
r<2d-\delta K
,

(9.5)

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\hat{\chi}(\xi)\,(i\xi)^{\alpha}\,\psi_{0}(\xi)\,R(\xi)\,d\xi\bigg|\,\lesssim_{\chi,r}\,\langle x\rangle^{-r}.

As by Theorem

\hat{\chi}
is compactly supported and as

is supported in

Substep
1.2. Case

and

(9.6)

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\hat{\chi}(\xi)\,(i\xi)^{\alpha}\,\psi_{l}(\xi)\,R(\xi)\,d\xi\bigg|\,\lesssim_{\chi,r}\,(2^{-l})^{d+|\alpha|-1}\langle 2^{-l}x\rangle^{-r}.

As

is supported in

for all

) follows.

Substep
1.3. Proof of (
9.3
).

Using (
9.5
) and (
9.6
) to estimate the dyadic sum (

), and distinguishing the cases

and
2^{l}\leq\langle x\rangle

we get for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

\displaystyle\big|\chi\ast(\nabla^{\alpha}\mathcal{G}-\nabla^{\alpha}\bar{G})(x)\big|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\chi,r}

Appendix A
Conlon–Naddaf–Sigal approach

This appendix is devoted to the proof of Lemma
2.1
,
which we split into three steps.

Step
1. Proof that
\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)
as defined in (
2.9
) is stationary and has vanishing expectation and finite second moments,

(A.1)

\mathbb{E}[\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)]\,=\,0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[|\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\,\leq\,C_{0}^{4}|e|^{2},\qquad\text{for all $e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

The well-definiteness of
\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)
as a stationary field with vanishing expectation and finite second moments is a consequence of the Lax–Milgram lemma as e.g. in
[
24
, Section 7.2]
. We turn to the proof of the actual bound (
A.1
) on second moments.
By uniform ellipticity (
1.1
) and by definition of
\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)

\mathbb{E}[|\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\leq C_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[(\overline{\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e\big]\,=\,-C_{0}\mathbb{E}[e\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e],

and thus, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}[|\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\leq C_{0}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{a}e|^{2}].

The claim (
A.1
) then follows from the boundedness of
\boldsymbol{a}
, cf. (
1.1
).

Step
2. Proof that the matrix
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
as defined in (
2.10
) is uniformly elliptic and bounded in the sense of

e\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2},\qquad|\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e|\leq C_{0}^{3}|e|,\qquad\text{for all $e\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

By definition of
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
and
\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)
, we have

e\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e\,=\,\mathbb{E}[e\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e)]\,=\,\mathbb{E}\big[(\overline{e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e)\big],

and thus, by uniform ellipticity (
1.1
) and by Jensen’s inequality,

e\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}\mathbb{E}[|e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\,\geq\,\tfrac{1}{C_{0}}|e|^{2}.

For the upper bound, we start by noting that the same argument as in Step 1 yields

\mathbb{E}[|e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\,\leq\,C_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[(\overline{e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e})\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e)\big]\,=\,C_{0}\mathbb{E}\big[e\cdot\boldsymbol{a}(e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e)\big],

and thus

\mathbb{E}[|e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|^{2}]\,\leq\,C_{0}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\boldsymbol{a}e|^{2}]\,\leq\,C_{0}^{4}|e|^{2},

which leads us to

|\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)e|\,=\,|\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}(e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e)]|\,\leq\,C_{0}\mathbb{E}[|e+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)e|]\,\leq\,C_{0}^{3}|e|,

Step
3. Proof that the ensemble average
\mathbb{E}[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}]=\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}

-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\varepsilon\nabla)\nabla\bar{u}_{\varepsilon,f}=\nabla\cdot f,

and that fluctuations of
\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}
can be described through

\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}-\mathbb{E}[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}]\,=\,\Psi(\tfrac{\cdot}{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\nabla)\mathbb{E}[\nabla u_{\varepsilon,f}].

In terms of the projections
P=\mathbb{E}
and
P^{\bot}=\operatorname{Id}-\mathbb{E}
on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)
, we consider the block decomposition (
2.6
) of the elliptic operator
L=-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{a}\nabla
. By the Schur complement formula, this entails the following block decomposition of the solution operator on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega)

\displaystyle P\nabla L^{-1}\nabla P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla\big(PLP-PLP^{\bot}(P^{\bot}LP^{\bot})^{-1}P^{\bot}LP\big)^{-1}\nabla,

\displaystyle P^{\bot}\nabla L^{-1}\nabla P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla(P^{\bot}LP^{\bot})^{-1}P^{\bot}LP\big(PLP-PLP^{\bot}(P^{\bot}LP^{\bot})^{-1}P^{\bot}LP\big)^{-1}\nabla,

provided that the inverses do make sense. In terms of
\Psi
and
\bar{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle P\nabla L^{-1}\nabla P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla(-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\nabla)^{-1}\nabla P,

\displaystyle P^{\bot}\nabla L^{-1}\nabla P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi(\cdot,\nabla)\nabla(-\nabla\cdot\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)\nabla)^{-1}\nabla P.

By Steps 1 and 2, these expressions both make sense on

Appendix B
Conlon–Naddaf lemma

We recall the following result due to Conlon and Naddaf
[
9
]
, which provides some frequency decay for derivatives of the symbol
\bar{\mathcal{A}}
. This high-frequency result is somehow orthogonal to the local regularity of the symbol that we are concerned with elsewhere in this work: it is unrelated to homogenization and it holds in the general stationary setting without any mixing assumption.
We refer to
[
9
, Lemmas 3.9 and 3.10]
for a proof in the discrete setting, which is easily generalized to the continuous setting as indicated in
[
9
, Lemma 6.4 and eqn (6.26)]
.
A sketch of the proof is included below for the readers’ convenience.

Lemma B.1

(Conlon & Naddaf
[
9
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{a}
be a stationary measurable random coefficient field satisfying the uniform ellipticity and boundedness assumptions (
1.1
), and let
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\nabla)
be the bounded convolution operator defined in Lemma
2.1
.
For all integers
n<d
we have
\nabla_{\xi}^{n}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\in\operatorname{L}^{d/n}_{w}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and in addition for all
0<\eta<1
,

\sup_{0<|y|\leq 1}\bigg\|\frac{\nabla_{\xi}^{n}\bar{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot+iy)-\nabla_{\xi}^{n}\bar{\mathcal{A}}}{|y|^{\eta}}\bigg\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\frac{d}{n+\eta}}_{w}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})}\,\lesssim_{\eta}\,1.

Sketch of the proof.

Let us focus on the proof that
\nabla_{\xi}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\in\operatorname{L}_{w}^{d}(i\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
We recall that

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)\,=\,\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}(\operatorname{Id}+\Psi(\cdot,i\xi))],\qquad\Psi(\cdot,i\xi)=K_{\xi}\boldsymbol{a},

\displaystyle K_{\xi}:=P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big(-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big)^{-1}\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot},\qquad\nabla_{\xi}:=\nabla+i\xi.

Taking the derivative of the above expression for
\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)
with respect to
\xi
, we find

\partial_{\xi_{l}}\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)\,=\,2\mathbb{E}\Big[\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}e_{l}\big(-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big)^{-1}\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}\Big]\\
+2\mathbb{E}\Big[\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big(-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big)^{-1}\big(e_{l}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big)\big(-\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}P^{\bot}\nabla_{\xi}\big)^{-1}\nabla_{\xi}\cdot P^{\bot}\boldsymbol{a}\Big],

which can be written as follows (without being too precise with matrix contractions),

(B.1)

\nabla_{\xi}\bar{\mathcal{A}}(i\xi)\,=\,2\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}U_{\xi}K_{\xi}\boldsymbol{a}]+2\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{a}K_{\xi}U_{\xi}\boldsymbol{a}K_{\xi}\boldsymbol{a}],

in terms of the Riesz potential

U_{\xi}:=\nabla_{\xi}\Delta_{\xi}^{-1}P^{\bot}.

Note that the operator
K_{\xi}
obviously satisfies for all
\xi
,

\|K_{\xi}\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\,\leq\,1,

while on the contrary
U_{\xi}
is not bounded on
\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
for any fixed
\xi
.
To grasp a better understanding of
U_{\xi}
, we first note that it can be written as

(B.2)

U_{\xi}\phi\,=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,\nabla\Delta^{-1}(x)\,P^{\bot}\phi(\tau_{x}\cdot)\,dx\,=\,h\ast P^{\bot}\hat{\phi}(\xi),\qquad h(\xi):=\xi|\xi|^{-2},

in terms of the Fourier transform
\hat{\phi}(\xi):=\int e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\phi(\tau_{x}\cdot)\,dx
, which is defined almost surely in the sense of tempered distributions, where
x\mapsto\phi(\tau_{x}\cdot)
stands for the stationary extension of
\phi
.
For fixed
\phi\in\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, this motivates to consider the map
U_{\phi}:f\mapsto f\ast P^{\bot}\hat{\phi}
defined on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
As by Bochner’s theorem the Fourier transform
\hat{C}_{\phi}
of the covariance function
C_{\phi}(x):=\operatorname{Cov}\left[{\phi(\tau_{x}\cdot)};{\phi}\right]
is a positive measure with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\hat{C}_{\phi}(dk)\leq\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}
, we can compute

\|(U_{\phi}f)(\xi)\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\,=\,\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(\xi+k)|^{2}\hat{C}_{\phi}(dk)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},

and we note that

\displaystyle\|U_{\phi}f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

\displaystyle\|U_{\phi}f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

By Hunt’s interpolation, this entails
for all
2<p<\infty
,

\|U_{\phi}f\|_{\operatorname{L}^{p}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\,\leq\,C_{p}\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|f\|_{\operatorname{L}_{w}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Applying this to (
B.2
) in form of
U_{\xi}\phi=(U_{\phi}h)(\xi)
with
h\in\operatorname{L}^{d}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
we deduce that the map
\phi\mapsto U\phi
given by
U\phi(\xi):=U_{\xi}\phi
satisfies

\|U\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{d}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega))}\,\lesssim\,\|\phi\|_{\operatorname{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Using this and the boundedness of
K
to estimate (
B.1
), we can deduce
\nabla_{\xi}\bar{\mathcal{A}}\in\operatorname{L}^{d}_{w}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
As shown in
[
9
]
, for higher derivatives, a careful (nontrivial) iteration of this argument is possible and yields the conclusion.
∎
