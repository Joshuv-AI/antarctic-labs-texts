Single-qubit gate teleportation provides a quantum advantage
Libor Caha, Xavier Coiteux-Roy and Robert König

Abstract
Gate-teleportation circuits are arguably among the most basic examples of computations believed to provide a quantum computational advantage: in seminal work [ 1 ] , Terhal and DiVincenzo have shown that these circuits elude simulation by efficient classical algorithms under plausible complexity-theoretic assumptions. Here we consider possibilistic simulation [ 2 ] , a particularly weak form of this task where the goal is to output any string appearing with non-zero probability in the output distribution of the circuit. We show that even for single-qubit Clifford-gate-teleportation circuits this simulation problem cannot be solved by constant-depth classical circuits with bounded fan-in gates. Our results are unconditional and are obtained by a reduction to the problem of computing parity, a well-studied problem in classical circuit complexity.

The behavior of a quantum circuit
\mathcal{Q}
is fully specified by
the conditional distribution
p^{\mathcal{Q}}(O|I)
of the output
O
given a fixed input
I
. Its computational power is directly tied to the difficulty of sampling from
p^{\mathcal{Q}}(\cdot|I)
for different inputs
I

\mathcal{Q}
give rise to various computational problems with more stringent notions of simulation associated with harder computational problems.
Arguably one of the weakest notions of simulating
\mathcal{Q}
is that of

[

[

such that

, i.e., such that

occurs with non-zero probability in the output distribution of
\mathcal{Q}
.

Our main result is that, for the gate-teleportation circuit
\mathsf{Telep}
, even the problem
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
is hard for constant-depth classical circuits. To state this, recall that an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit is a constant-depth polynomial-size classical circuit with bounded fan-in gates (where, following standard terminology, we often refer to an infinite circuit family simply as a circuit).

.

The

In contrast, any (even non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit fails to solve
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]

We note that average-case statements for suitable distributions over instances of
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
can be derived in a similar manner as e.g.,
[

The study of the difficulty of classically simulating quantum gate teleportation was initiated in seminal work by Terhal and DiVincenzo
[

The gate-teleportation circuit used in this argument acts in parallel on polynomially many qubits, and realizes any polynomial-size quantum circuit by a post-selected constant-depth quantum circuit. Furthermore, gate-teleportation circuits when combined with post-selection gives the complexity class
\mathsf{PostBQP}
of (uniform) polynomial-size post-selected quantum circuits.
This leads to the hardness of classically sampling from their output distribution (also called weak simulation in Ref.
[

This line of reasoning has also been used to show hardness of simulating “commuting quantum computation” as formalized by IQP circuits
[

[

and boson sampling circuits
[

Proof outline

We establish Theorem
1.1
by
connecting the gate-teleportation simulation problem
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]

to a basic (trivial) result from classical complexity theory, namely the fact that the parity function cannot be computed by an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit.
Our argument proceeds through a reduction to an intermediate computational problem which relates
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
and
\mathsf{PARITY}
.

Let us briefly describe the intermediate problem and give a high-level overview of our reasoning.

Quantum advantage against
\mathsf{NC}^{0}

The intermediate problem we use in our analysis is another simulation problem

for a two-qubit quantum circuit
\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}

\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}

of single-qubit Cliffords.
(These sets are representatives of different cosets of the Pauli respectively Clifford groups when taking the quotient with respect to global phases. We refer to e.g.,
[

It takes as input
L+1
single-qubit Clifford gates

\displaystyle(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L},D)\in\mathcal{C}^{L+1}

(1)

and outputs a Pauli

In Section
3
, we
show the following: assume that
\mathsf{C}
is an (even non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit
solving
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}_{n}\right]
(i.e., producing a valid output for all instances). Then, for
L=\Theta(n)
, there is a function

(that depends on
\mathsf{C}
) and a non-uniform
\mathsf{NC}^{0}

\mathsf{C^{\prime}}

\mathsf{C}

2

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\left(\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\exists Q\colon\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\,,

(2)

where
\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
is the class of problems solved by non-uniform families of
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuits.

Our second reduction heavily borrows from ideas developed in
[
13
]
in the context of interactive quantum advantage protocols. By adapting (specializing) these techniques, we show that the ability of possibilistically simulating
\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}
implies the ability to compute products of single-qubit Clifford gates by a randomized
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit with success probability at least
2/3
. In particular, given a (deterministic) oracle
\mathcal{O}
for
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]
(for any fixed function
Q
), this immediately yields an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit
with additional uniformly random input bits and oracle access to
\mathcal{O}
, i.e., an
(\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{rpoly})^{\mathcal{O}}
-circuit,
which solves
\mathsf{PARITY}
with success probability at least
2/3
. Succinctly, we summarize this as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\left(\mathcal{O}\textrm{ solves }\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]\right)\Rightarrow\left(\!(\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{rpoly})^{\mathcal{O}}\text{ solves }\mathsf{PARITY}\text{ with prob.}\geq\frac{2}{3}\right)\!.

(3)

Combining (
2
)
and (
3
) and
using a standard derandomization argument shows that

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\mathsf{PARITY}\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\ .

(4)

Thus the existence of such a circuit would contradict the folklore (trivial) result that
\mathsf{PARITY}
cannot be solved by a non-uniform
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit. Indeed, an output bit of an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit is influenced by at most a constant number of bits of an input instance, whereas
\mathsf{PARITY}
depends on all input bits. In conclusion, this shows that there is no
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit for the teleportation simulation problem
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
.

Related work

Let us briefly summarize related work. The first unconditional separation of con-stant-depth quantum circuits and constant-depth classical circuits with bounded fan-in gates, i.e.,
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
and
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
, was achieved by Bravyi, Gosset, and König
[
4
]
for the
2D
Hidden Linear Function problem
. This result was later extended to noisy quantum circuits by Bravyi, Gosset, König, and Tomamichel
[
5
]
. The latter construction uses a simpler underlying problem—the
1D
Magic Square Game
. Both of these constructions are physically motivated and based
on violations of Bell inequalities. In subsequent work Bene Watts, Kothari, Schaeffer, and Tal
[
14
]
achieved an even stronger separation, establishing a quantum advantage of constant-depth quantum circuits against
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
-circuits, i.e., the family of classical constant-depth polynomial-size circuits obtained by augmenting
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuits with unbounded fan-in AND and OR gates. The problem used in
[
14
]
is the
Relaxed Parity Halving problem
. Similarly to our result, this work establishes a quantum advantage based on classical circuit complexity lower bounds as opposed to the first two
[
4
,
5
]
Bell-inequality-based constructions.

In this work our aim is to give a quantum advantage proposal separating
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
from
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
. While the mentioned prior works have already established this complexity-theoretic separation, our focus is on the minimal quantum information-processing resources required to do so. We also use a novel and potentially more generally applicable proof technique.
To compare our circuits to these priors works, let us discuss what geometry and gates these constructions use. The quantum circuit of
[
4
]
for the
2D
Hidden Linear Function problem, and
the circuit of
[
14
]
for the
Relaxed Parity Halving problem are geometrically
2D
-local circuits

A circuit is said to be
geometrically
2D
-local
(resp.
1D
-local) if its underlying interaction graph is a
2D
lattice (resp.
1D

composed of classically controlled Clifford gates. The
1D
Magic Square Game problem considered in
[
5
]
is a classically controlled Clifford circuit on a chain of pairs of qubits.
(This geometric locality allows for a fault-tolerant implementation by a local quantum circuit in a
3D

quite similar to that of
[
5
]
, but is more directly associated with a ring of qubits rather than linear array of pairs of qubits. We note that the required resources are essentially the same despite this apparent difference. However, the use of periodic boundary conditions makes a novel argument possible, and leads to perhaps the most simple quantum circuit separating
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
from
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
.

Finally, we note that the work of Grier and Schaeffer
[
13
]
provides several quantum advantage protocols involving interaction.
Unlike for typical computational problems such as those considered here (where the task is to provide a certain output given an input), corresponding advantage proposals involve a task which requires several rounds of “challenges” and “responses”, respectively. These can be seen as messages passed between a prover (who aims to demonstrate quantum computational power) and a verifier.
In the work
[
13
]
, the quantum player (prover) can be realized by a constant-depth (classically controlled) Clifford circuit which is realized on a chain consisting of qubits, a pair of qubits, or a grid
depending on the complexity-theoretic result considered: for example, a chain of qubits is used to show hardness for
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
with additional
\textrm{MOD}_{6}
gates, and chain of pairs of qubits is used to show hardness for classical circuits with bounded fan-in gates and logarithmic depth, i.e.,
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
. We note that, while establishing several containments of classical and quantum complexity classes, this does not directly provide a separation between constant-depth quantum and constant-depth classical circuits (in the sense of separating
\mathsf{QNC}^{0}
from e.g.,
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
) because interactive protocols are studied (i.e., these results separate suitably defined complexity classes involving interaction.)
We emphasize that, although their focus is different from ours, some
of the key ideas developed in
[
13
]
are central to our derivation.

Let us briefly comment on the relation of our work to
[
13
]
, where an oracle
\tilde{R}
for the simulation of a certain interactive process is considered.
The proof technique in
[
13
]
assumes
that the oracle
\tilde{R}
provides
rewind
access. It uses a reduction that does not treat
\tilde{R}
as a black box. Instead, the classical algorithm is allowed to “rewind” the classical simulator (implementing the oracle) to an earlier state of the computation, change inputs or internal variables and continue the computation. Notice that this is a natural requirement for a classical machine (e.g. for
\mathsf{NC}^{0},\mathsf{AC}^{0}
or Turing machines it is implemented by the same class) but is generally impossible in a quantum computational model involving non-unitary dynamics such as measurements.

In more detail, the simulated interactive process considered in
[
13
]
is conceptually similar to a Bell experiment: in the first stage, given an
L
-tuple
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L})
of Clifford group elements, the stabilizer state
P(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L},m)(C_{L}\cdots C_{1})\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle
is prepared by a constant-depth quantum circuit using measurements, where the Pauli “correction”
P(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L},m)
is determined by the Cliffords
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L})
and the measurement results
m
. This first stage corresponds to preparing an initial entangled state for a Bell experiment. In the second stage of the process, an input is provided according to which a suitable Pauli measurement is applied to the prepared state. Without loss of generality, the input takes the form of a Clifford unitary
D
, and the executed measurement is the von Neumann (i.e., projective) measurement using the basis
\{D\left|z\right\rangle\}_{z}
. This second step corresponds to the execution of a Bell measurement on the prepared state. The work
[
13
]
considers simulation problems associated with different interactive protocols of this kind for
k
-qubit Clifford circuits, embedded into so-called graph state simulation problems.

Rewind access to an oracle
\tilde{R}
simulating such an interactive process means that oracle queries to
\tilde{R}
can be repeated in such a way that it yields an identical output
m
for the first step. In other words, the classical machine can be rewound to the beginning of the second stage. In contrast, reproducing this behavior using a quantum protocol would require post-selecting on fixed measurement outcomes occurring with exponentially small probability. The ability of a classical simulator to rewind is at the root of the hardness results of
[
13
]
concerning interactive quantum advantage.

In contrast to
[
13
]
, we do not need to assume rewind access to the classical oracle
\tilde{R}
. This is achieved by exploiting the internal structure of the classical simulator: we consider oracles realized by circuits and provide a non-black-box reduction. As we discuss below (see Section
3.2
), however, our arguments heavily rely on some of the techniques of
[
13
]
developed in the interactive scenario.

A more significant distinction between
our work and that of
[
13
]
is the fact that the computational problem we consider does not require interaction, i.e., two distinct stages. To illustrate this difference, consider the following simple example of a two-stage (interactive) quantum advantage in a problem involving two players Alice and Bob. The problem is commonly known as the CHSH game
[
15
]
. Let us assume that Alice and Bob start out with no classical or quantum correlations.
In stage
1
, Alice and Bob are allowed to communicate arbitrarily. In stage
2
, Alice and Bob are not allowed to communicate anymore, and they receive random bits
x
and
y
, respectively. They are asked to output bits
a
respectively
b
such that
a\cdot b=x+y\pmod{2}
. It is well-known that Alice and Bob can win with higher success probability than two classical players, if they can exchange quantum messages at stage
1
. It is easy to see that a similarly simple quantum advantage cannot be observed when there is only a single stage (i.e., where Alice and Bob obtain
x
and
y
at the very beginning). This example indicates that the challenge of establishing a quantum advantage heavily depends on
how many (interactive) rounds are considered. While our work removes the need for interaction, an even more extreme example is the problem of sampling: Here a quantum advantage has recently been established for problems “without input”
[
16
]
(see also
[
17
]
).

Outlook

Due to the simplicity of the circuit
\mathsf{Telep}
, our work provides a starting point for a fault-tolerant
3D
-local quantum advantage scheme based on the techniques of
[
5
]
.
A natural open question is whether the
\mathsf{Telep}
circuit also provides a quantum advantage over
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
, with a similar separation as the (up to the best of our knowledge) strongest known unconditional result
[
14
]
. More generally, it is unclear whether or not
quantum advantage schemes against
\mathsf{AC}^{0}
are
feasible based on geometrically
1D
-local quantum circuits only.

To motivate the construction, recall what the effect of

In this section, we give a protocol for

or

The protocol borrows heavily from the pioneering work
[

[

]

3.2.1
On the need for possibilistic state tomography

We emphasize that the problem of determining stabilizers of the state (
95
)
using an oracle
\mathcal{O}
for
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}\right]
cannot directly be solved using standard techniques for (stabilizer) quantum state tomography. This is because of the possibilistic nature of the simulation provided by
\mathcal{O}
. Accordingly, we refer to the problem under consideration as
possibilistic tomography
.

To clarify the special difficulties associated with possibilistic tomography, consider a setting where several copies of an unknown stabilizer state
\left|\Psi\right\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes 2}
can be generated/are available. This is the setting of standard tomography. Here a set
\{S_{1},S_{2}\}
of
generators of the stabilizer group (up to a phase) of
\left|\Psi\right\rangle

can efficiently be determined by a sequence of measurements with respect to different Pauli bases, see e.g.,
[
18
,
19
]
.

One of the key achievements of
[
13
]
is to extend this stabilizer state tomography to the possibilistic setting, where the process of measuring a state
\left|\Psi\right\rangle
in a Pauli basis is replaced by an oracle
\mathcal{O}
simulating the measurement.
Remarkably, possibilistic simulation is sufficient, i.e., the simulated measurement results produced by
\mathcal{O}
only need to lie in the support of the

\displaystyle P_{j,1}P_{j,2}P_{j,3}\

.

3.3
Quantum advantage against
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
from
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]

In this section, we finish the argument for our result, establishing a quantum advantage for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
problem against (non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
.

In Section
3.1
, we showed that a possibilistic (non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
simulator for
\mathsf{Telep}
circuits implies a possibilistic
\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
simulator for
\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)
for some fixed function
Q
:

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\ \quad\Rightarrow\ \quad\left(\exists Q\colon\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\,.

(110)

We will use the possibilistic simulation for the
\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)
problem together with possibilistic state tomography, Section
3.2
(specialized from
[
13
]
), to solve a certain word problem for a fixed group with success probability at least
2/3
. The word problem we consider is as hard as
\mathsf{PARITY}
.

This will lead to the following implication:

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{O}\textrm{ solves }\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]\right)\ \Rightarrow\ \left((\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{rpoly})^{\mathcal{O}}\text{ solves }\mathsf{PARITY}\text{ with prob. }\geq 2/3\right)\,.

(111)

Combining it with Eq. (
110
) and using a standard derandomization argument, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\left(\mathsf{PARITY}\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}\right)\ .

(112)

This contradicts that
\mathsf{PARITY}\not\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
, since the output of an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
circuit depends on a constant number of bits of an input instance. Therefore, we obtain
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\not\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
.

We relate
\mathsf{PARITY}
and the word problem for a fixed group in Section
3.3.1
and use it to complete the proof of quantum advantage from
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
in Section
3.3.2
.

3.3.1
\mathsf{PARITY}
as a word problem for the Clifford group

The word problem for groups is defined as follows. Let
G
be a group with identity element
\mathsf{e}\in G
. The word problem for the group
G
is to decide for an input sequence of group elements
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{L})\in G^{L}
whether the product
g_{L}\cdots g_{1}
equals the identity element
\mathsf{e}
. In our scenario we use the promise variant which promises that the product is either identity or some other chosen element
g
. We denote this problem
\mathsf{WP}(G,g)
. A sequence of group elements
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{L})
is a yes-instance if
g_{L}\cdots g_{1}=\mathsf{e}
and a no-instance if
g_{L}\cdots g_{1}=g
.

Consider the word problem
\mathsf{WP}(\mathcal{C},H)
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the group of one-qubit Clifford unitaries with the standard product operation and
H
the Hadamard unitary. We show that
\mathsf{PARITY}
reduces to this problem. Let
x\in\{0,1\}^{L}
be a bit string. We will show how to use
\mathsf{WP}(\mathcal{C},H)
with the input length
L
to compute
\mathsf{PARITY}
of the input
x
. Set

\displaystyle g_{i}:=H^{x_{i}}\qquad\text{ for all }\qquad i\in[n]\ .

(113)

Since
H^{2}=I
, the product

\displaystyle g_{L}\cdots g_{1}=H^{\mathsf{PARITY}(x)}=\begin{cases}I&\text{ if }x\text{ has even parity, and}\\
H&\text{ if }x\text{ has odd parity}\ .\\
\end{cases}

(114)

Hence we can use
\mathsf{WP}(\mathcal{C},H)
to solve
\mathsf{PARITY}
.

We note that this discussion may appear overly detailed to simply compute the parity. However, one may use other (Clifford) groups and corresponding word problems to address further computational tasks, along the lines of
[
13
]
, which we expand upon in Section
3.3.3
. In particular, using for example two qubits and corresponding gate-teleportation circuits, one can construct circuits solving word problems associated with
S_{6}
, related to Barrington’s theorem
[
21
]
.
However, because of the use of lightcone-type arguments, naive applications of our reasoning cannot provide quantum advantage beyond
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
.

3.3.2
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
cannot be in
\mathsf{NC}^{0}

In the following lemma we use a deterministic oracle for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]
problem together with the possibilistic state tomography from Section
3.2
to solve
\mathsf{PARITY}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be a deterministic oracle for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)\right]
problem. There is an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit with oracle access to
\mathcal{O}
which uses uniformly random bits, makes
O(1)
oracle queries and solves the
\mathsf{PARITY}
problem with success probability at least
2/3
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\{0,1\}^{L}
be an input instance of
\mathsf{PARITY}_{L}
. Set, as in Eq. (
113
),
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L})
where
C_{i}:=H^{x_{i}}
. Again
C_{L}\cdots C_{1}=H^{\mathsf{PARITY}(x)}
.

We will use the learning procedure with the oracle for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}\right]
problem established in
Theorem
3.4
to learn the stabilizer group (up to the sign factor) of the state

\displaystyle(I\otimes C_{L}\cdots C_{1})\left|\Phi\right\rangle=\left(I\otimes H^{\mathsf{PARITY}(x)}\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle\ .

(115)

This theorem tells us that there is a randomized
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
algorithm that makes
O(1)
queries to the oracle
\mathcal{O}
and either returns a pair
(S_{1},S_{2})\in(\mathcal{P}\otimes\mathcal{P})^{2}
of two distinct (non-identity) Pauli operators or reports failure. If the algorithm returns
(S_{1},S_{2})
,
which happens with probability at least
2/3
, each
S_{i}
has expectation value
\pm 1
in the state
\left(I\otimes H^{\mathsf{PARITY}(x)}\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle
. Let us list the possible outcomes for the two options, i.e., when
x
has even respectively odd parity:

(i)

If
x
has even parity the algorithm learns the stabilizer group of the state
\left(I\otimes H^{\mathsf{PARITY}(x)}\right)\left|\Phi\right\rangle=\left|\Phi\right\rangle
. It is straightforward to check that the valid outputs
(S_{1},S_{2})
of the learning algorithm are two distinct elements of the set
S_{\text{even}}:=\{X\otimes X,Z\otimes Z,Y\otimes Y\}
.

(ii)

If
x
has odd parity the algorithm learns the stabilizers of the state
(I\otimes H)\left|\Phi\right\rangle
. The output
(S_{1},S_{2})
are two distinct elements of the set
S_{\text{odd}}:=\{X\otimes Z,Z\otimes X,Y\otimes Y\}
.

Since
|S_{\text{even}}\cap S_{\text{odd}}|=1
, we can determine the parity of
x
from the output
(S_{1},S_{2})
. Therefore we can compute
\mathsf{PARITY}(x)
with probability at least
2/3
. This completes the proof.
∎

We use this result to complete our argument and show that there cannot be a (non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit for
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
. Since the
\mathsf{Telep}_{n}
quantum circuit trivially solves
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}_{n}\right]
this completes the proof of the main theorem, i.e., Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 3.8

(
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]\not\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
)
.

The possibilistic simulation of the
\mathsf{Telep}
circuit, i.e., the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
problem, is not in (non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
.

Proof.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that there is a (non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit
\mathsf{C}_{n}
for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}_{n}\right]
problem.

We will use
\mathsf{C}_{n}
in a non-black-box way.
From Lemma
3.3
we have that there is an
L=\Theta(n)
and a non-uniform
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that solves
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{CliffP}(Q)_{L}\right]
for some fixed function
Q\colon\mathcal{C}^{L}\to\mathcal{P}
. This algorithm can be constructed from the algorithm
\mathcal{B}^{\mathsf{C}_{n}}_{L,m,\mathcal{J}}
(Algorithm
2
) by fixing the parameters
m,\mathcal{J}
that are specific for the
\mathsf{C}_{n}
in the non-uniform way, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}
is in
\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
.

Lemma
3.7
tells us
that there is a randomized (uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit
\mathcal{D}
with oracle access to
\mathcal{A}
which solves
\mathsf{PARITY}
.
Since
\mathcal{A}\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
and
\mathcal{D}
makes only
O(1)
queries to
\mathcal{A}
, the algorithm
\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{A}}
can be realized by a non-uniform randomized
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit
\mathcal{E}
.

Using standard procedures, we derandomize the randomized circuit
\mathcal{E}
as follows. Because
\mathcal{E}
is a randomized
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit with a single output bit, its output is determined by
a constant number of bits of the input instance
x
, as well as a constant number of uniformly random bits. Enumerating all possible assignments of those random bits, computing the result of the circuit
\mathcal{E}
for each assignment, and taking the majority of the results gives the parity of
x
with certainty. This procedure is realized by an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
circuit. This shows that

\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
can solve
\mathsf{PARITY}
, contradicting the fact that
\mathsf{PARITY}\not\in\mathsf{NC}^{0}/\mathsf{poly}
. In conclusion, there is no (not even a non-uniform)
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
problem.
∎

Other word problems for Clifford modulo Pauli groups

Here we argue that the class of problems we could solve if we had an

.

The main idea relies on the observation that the technique used in Section
3.2
to distinguish the state
\left|\Phi\right\rangle
from
(I\otimes H)\left|\Phi\right\rangle
via classical simulation can be used to distinguish
\left|\Phi\right\rangle
from any state
(I\otimes C)\left|\Phi\right\rangle
, where
C\in\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{P}\setminus\{I\}
is a non-Pauli Clifford gate. This is because by definition of the Clifford group, any non-Pauli Clifford takes a non-stabilizer state to another, different non-stabilizer state. This allows us to solve the word problem
\mathsf{WP}(\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{P},C)
for any
C\in\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{P}\setminus\{I\}
, i.e., the problem of deciding whether a series of single-qubit Clifford modulo Pauli gates multiplies to
I
or
C
.

For example, the Clifford gate
SH
generates a cyclic group of order
3
. Thus replacing the Hadamard gate
H
by
SH
in our construction allows us to compute unbounded
\text{MOD}_{3}
gates, i.e., to compute whether
|x|\bmod{3}
of the Hamming weight of the input string
x
is
0
. Note that
\text{MOD}_{3}
cannot be computed by constant-depth circuits with unbounded
\mathsf{PARITY}
-gates
[
22
,
23
]
and vice versa, hence these problems are incomparable.

Combining the ability of computing
\text{MOD}_{3}
with that of computing
\mathsf{PARITY}
, we conclude that access to an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
simulator for the
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{Telep}\right]
problem elevates
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuits to
\mathsf{NC}^{0}[6]
, i.e.,
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
-circuit with
\text{MOD}_{6}
gates.

Alternatively, we can arrive at similar conclusions using Barrington’s result
[
24
]
. The latter states that the
promise word problem for the solvable group
S_{3}
characterizes width-3 permutation branching programs. The promise word problem for
S_{3}
can be treated in our setting because

S_{3}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{P}
. The isomorphism is given by

I\mapsto 1
,

H\mapsto(13)
,

S\mapsto(12)
,

HS\mapsto(123)
,

SHS\mapsto(23
)
,

(HS)^{2}\mapsto(132)
.

Two-qubit Clifford gate teleportation circuits

A similar construction can be obtained with two-qubit (instead of one-qubit) gate teleportation. Notice that this circuit can be implemented on a ring of pairs of qubits. Assuming an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
possibilistic simulator for this problem and following the lightcone argument, we can use it to solve a possibilistic simulation of the intermediate problem depicted in Figure
10
. We can ask what problems such a simulator allows us to solve.

Figure 10
:
The Pauli-corrected two-qubit Clifford circuit
\mathsf{2CliffP}(Q)_{L}
with correction function
Q:\mathcal{C}_{2}^{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}_{2}
. It takes an input
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{L},D)\in\mathcal{C}_{2}^{L+1}
and outputs two single-qubit Pauli operators
P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
obtained from the two Bell measurements.

Let us consider the word problem
\mathsf{WP}(\mathcal{C}_{2}/\mathcal{P}_{2},H\otimes H)
, i.e., deciding whether a series of two-qubit Clifford modulo Pauli gates multiplies to
I\otimes I
or
H\otimes H
, as used also in Ref.
[
13
]
. If we have an
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
simulator for
\mathsf{PSIM}\left[\mathsf{2CliffP}(Q)\right]
we can use it to do possibilistic tomography in a similar way to what we did in Section
3.2.2
on each of the two Bell states.

Notice that the two-qubit Clifford group modulo Pauli operators is isomorphic to
S_{6}
(see Ref.
[
13
]
).
Since
S_{6}
is a non-solvable group, the associated word problem is complete for the complexity class
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
, i.e., for the complexity class of bounded fan-in logarithmic-depth polynomial-size circuits, according to Barrington’s theorem
[
21
]
. A constant-depth classical simulator for the two-qubit gate-teleportation problem could therefore be used to solve any problem in
\mathsf{NC}^{1}
.

Note, however, that none of these more general computational problems can be used to obtain a quantum advantage beyond
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
when only our techniques are applied. The lightcone argument we rely on constitutes a bottleneck for such a separation. The existence of a quantum advantage beyond
\mathsf{NC}^{0}
for
1D
-local quantum circuits therefore remains an open problem.
