Minimum degrees of finite rectangular bands, null semigroups, and variants of full transformation semigroups


Abstract
For a positive integer n n , the full transformation semigroup 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} consists of all self maps of the set { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} under composition. Any finite semigroup S S embeds in some 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} , and the least such n n is called the (minimum transformation) degree of S S and denoted μ ⁡ ( S ) \mu(S) . We find degrees for various classes of finite semigroups, including rectangular bands, rectangular groups and null semigroups. The formulae we give involve natural parameters associated to integer compositions. Our results on rectangular bands answer a question of Easdown from 1992, and our approach utilises some results of independent interest concerning partitions/colourings of hypergraphs. As an application, we prove some results on the degree of a variant 𝒯 n a \mathcal{T}_{n}^{a} . (The variant S a = ( S , ⋆ ) S^{a}=(S,\star) of a semigroup S S , with respect to a fixed element a ∈ S a\in S , has underlying set S S and operation x ⋆ y = x ​ a ​ y x\star y=xay .) It has been previously shown that n ≤ μ ⁡ ( 𝒯 n a ) ≤ 2 ​ n − r n\leq\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-r if the sandwich element a a has rank r r , and the upper bound of 2 ​ n − r 2n-r is known to be sharp if r ≥ n − 1 r\geq n-1 . Here we show that μ ⁡ ( 𝒯 n a ) = 2 ​ n − r \mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})=2n-r for r ≥ n − 6 r\geq n-6 . In stark contrast to this, when r = 1 r=1 , and the above inequality says n ≤ μ ⁡ ( 𝒯 n a ) ≤ 2 ​ n − 1 n\leq\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-1 , we show that μ ⁡ ( 𝒯 n a ) / n → 1 \mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})/n\to 1 and μ ⁡ ( 𝒯 n a ) − n → ∞ \mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})-n\to\infty as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Among other results, we also classify the 3 3 -nilpotent subsemigroups of 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} , and calculate the maximum size of such a subsemigroup. Keywords : Transformation semigroup, transformation representation, semigroup variant, rectangular band, nilpotent semigroup, hypergraph. MSC: 20M20, 20M15, 20M30, 05E16, 05C65.

Introduction

Denote by
\mathcal{T}_{X}
the full transformation semigroup over the set
X
, which consists of all self-maps of
X
under composition. When
X=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for some integer
n
we denote
\mathcal{T}_{X}
by
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Cayley’s Theorem states that every semigroup
S
embeds in some
\mathcal{T}_{X}
with
|X|\leq|S|+1
; see
[
37
, Theorem 1.1.2]
. The
(minimum transformation) degree
of a semigroup
S
is defined to be the cardinal

\mu(S)={\min}\big\{{|X|}:{X\not=\varnothing,\ S\text{ embeds in }\mathcal{T}_{X}}\big\}.

(The requirement that
X\not=\varnothing
is exclusively to establish the convention that a semigroup of size
1
has degree
1
.)
Several authors have calculated
\mu(S)
for various classes of finite (semi)groups; for semigroups see especially the works of Easdown
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
and Schein
[
52
,
51
]
, but note that in some papers degrees are defined in terms of representations by
partial
transformations. (Writing
\mu^{\prime}(S)
for the degree with respect to partial transformations, the reader might like to verify that
{\mu(S)-1\leq\mu^{\prime}(S)\leq\mu(S)}
.) See also
[
4
,
21
,
35
]
for computational studies,
[
58
,
34
,
53
]
for connections to radical theory, and
[
59
,
38
,
23
,
18
,
36
,
3
,
39
,
49
,
50
]
for degrees of finite groups. The topic is also closely related to enumeration of (classes of) semigroups by size
[
11
,
41
,
10
,
9
,
25
]
.
We also mention the very recent study of Margolis and Steinberg
[
43
]
, which was written after the current article but published before it, and concerns the class of
Rhodes semisimple semigroups
. Apart from trivially small exceptions, none of the semigroups we study here belong to this class, so our techniques are necessarily different from theirs.

The initial source of motivation for the current paper came from semigroup variants. Recall that the
variant
of a semigroup
S
with respect to a fixed element
a\in S
is the semigroup
S^{a}=(S,\star)
, where the
sandwich operation

\star
is defined by
x\star y=xay
for
x,y\in S
. Variants were introduced by Hickey
[
33
,
32
]
, building on earlier ideas of Lyapin
[
40
]
and Brown
[
5
]
, and have been studied by many others since. The papers
[
2
,
12
,
56
,
57
,
19
,

]
study variants of full transformation semigroups; see also
[

14
]
for a categorical approach. Sandwich operations also play an important role in computational semigroup theory
[
22
]
.

Despite the simple definition, the structure of a variant
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
is vastly more complicated than that of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
itself. See for example Figure
1
, which gives egg-box diagrams for
\mathcal{T}_{4}
and a variant
\mathcal{T}_{4}^{a}
, both produced with GAP
[

. (Egg-box diagrams display the structure of a semigroup as determined by Green’s relations
[
29
]
, which are themselves defined below; see
[
37
,
8
]
for more details.) Nevertheless, it was shown in
[
19
]
that variants
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
can be embedded in (ordinary) transformation semigroups of relatively small degree. For the next statement, which is
[
19
, Theorem 1.4]
, the
rank
of a transformation is the size of its image.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
r=\operatorname{rank}(a)
, then
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
embeds in
\mathcal{T}_{2n-r}
.

In fact, it was shown in
[
19
]
that
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
is isomorphic to a
local subsemigroup
of
\mathcal{T}_{2n-r}
of the form
b\mathcal{T}_{2n-r}b
, where
b\in\mathcal{T}_{2n-r}
satisfies
\operatorname{rank}(b)=n
and
\operatorname{rank}(b^{2})=r
. Theorem
1.1
(and the fact that
|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}|=|\mathcal{T}_{n}|
) leads to bounds on the degree of a variant:

n\leq\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-r\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}$ with $r=\operatorname{rank}(a)$.}

(1.2)

It was shown in
[
19
]
that (for very simple reasons)
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
in fact attains the upper bound of
2n-r
for
r\geq n-1
. One of our main results, Theorem
5.15
below, improves this by showing that
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})=2n-r
for
r\geq n-6
.

The situation is very different, however, when the sandwich element
a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
has minimum possible rank
1
, where (
1.2
) becomes
n\leq\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-1
. For
\operatorname{rank}(a)=1
, we show that:

•

\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
achieves the upper bound of
2n-1
if and only if
n\leq 15
(Proposition
5.23
),

•

the ratio
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})/n
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
(Theorem
5.27
),

•

the difference
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})-n
tends to
\infty
as
n\to\infty
(Theorem
5.28
).

The above-mentioned results on variants of

2
Preliminaries

We begin with some preliminary material on integers and compositions, and (transformation) semigroups.

2.1
Basic numerical facts

We write
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
for the set of natural numbers, and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
{\bf n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We adopt the convention that
0^{0}=1
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For integers
n\geq k\geq 0
, we have
(n+1)^{k-1}\leq n^{k}
.

Proof.

This is clear for
k=0
, and for
k\geq 1
we use the Binomial Theorem:

(n+1)^{k-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\tbinom{k-1}{j}n^{k-1-j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(k-1)^{j}n^{k-1-j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}n^{j}n^{k-1-j}=k\cdot n^{k-1}\leq n^{k}.\qed

Corollary 2.2
.

For integers
n\geq k\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
, we have
(n+t)^{k-t}\leq n^{k}
.

Proof.

This is clear for
t=0
and for
t>k
. For
1\leq t\leq k
, we repeatedly apply Lemma
2.1
:

n^{k}\geq(n+1)^{k-1}\geq(n+2)^{k-2}\geq\cdots\geq(n+t)^{k-t}.\qed

Two closely-related functions
\xi,\alpha:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
will play an important role throughout, as well as an associated real function
L:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Here
\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the set of positive reals. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define (with the
{\bf n}
notation introduced at the start of the subsection):

\xi(n)=\max\{{t^{n-t}}:{t\in{\bf n}}\}\and\alpha(n)=\max\{{t\in{\bf n}}:{\xi(n)=t^{n-t}}\}.

(2.3)

The numbers
\xi(n)
and
\alpha(n)
appear as Sequences A003320 and A056155 on the OEIS
[
1
]
. Some calculated values are given in Table
1
. The
\xi
and
\alpha
functions played a role in
[
28
]
in relation to left zero subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
; they will appear in several other contexts in the current paper.

To define the function
L:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, first consider
f:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
f(x)=x+x\ln x
. Since
f^{\prime}(x)=2+\ln x
, we see that
f(x)
is strictly increasing for
x\geq e^{-2}
. Since
f(e^{-1})=0
, it follows that
f
restricts to a bijection
\{{x\in\mathbb{R}}:{x>e^{-1}}\}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We then define
L
to be the inverse of this bijection. Thus, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
L(t)
is the unique real
x>e^{-1}
satisfying
t=x+x\ln x
. Note that
L
is also an increasing function, and that
t\geq L(t)\ \Leftrightarrow\ t\geq 1\ \Leftrightarrow\ L(t)\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.4
.

(i)

We have
1=\xi(1)=\xi(2)<\xi(3)<\xi(4)<\cdots
.

(ii)

For any
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\xi(m)\xi(n)\leq\xi(m+n-1)
.

(iii)

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha(n)
is one of
\lfloor x\rfloor
or
\lceil x\rceil
, where
x=L(n)
, and
\xi(n)=\max\left(\lfloor x\rfloor^{n-\lfloor x\rfloor},\lceil x\rceil^{n-\lceil x\rceil}\right)
.

Proof.

(i)
. It is clear that
\xi(1)=\xi(2)=1
. Now suppose
2\leq m<n
, and write
s=\alpha(m)
. Since
m\geq 2
, we have
s\geq 2
. Since also
s\in{\bf m}\subseteq{\bf n}
, we have

\xi(m)=s^{m-s}<s^{n-s}\leq\max\{{t^{n-t}}:{t\in{\bf n}}\}=\xi(n).

(ii)
. Write
s=\alpha(m)
and
t=\alpha(n)
. Then since
1\leq s+t-1\leq m+n-1
we have

\displaystyle\xi(m+n-1)\geq(s+t-1)^{(m+n-1)-(s+t-1)}

\displaystyle=(s+t-1)^{m-s}(s+t-1)^{n-t}\geq s^{m-s}t^{n-t}=\xi(m)\xi(n).

(iii)
. As in
[
28
]
, define the real function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
by
g(x)=x^{n-x}
. Then differentiating, we obtain
g^{\prime}(x)=x^{n-x-1}(n-x-x\ln x)
, so
g^{\prime}(x)=0
when
x+x\ln x=n
, i.e. when
x=L(n)
. Moreover, if we write
u=L(n)
, then
g(x)
is increasing for
1\leq x<u
and decreasing for
x>u
. The result follows.
∎

2.2
Compositions

Recall that a
composition
of a natural number
n
is a tuple
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
of positive integers satisfying
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{r}=n
, in which case we write
\sigma\vDash n
and
|\sigma|=r
; we call
r
the
length
of
\sigma
. If also
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}
, then
\sigma
is a
partition
of
n
, and we write
\sigma\vdash n
. Certain parameters associated to compositions will play an important role in all that follows, as well as certain numerical functions/sequences defined in terms of them.

Perhaps the simplest parameter associated to a composition
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
is its product:

\textstyle{\prod\sigma}=s_{1}\cdots s_{r}.

For integers
1\leq r\leq n
, we define

\pi(n)={\max}\big\{{\textstyle{\prod\sigma}}:{\sigma\vDash n}\big\}\and\pi_{r}(n)={\max}\big\{{\textstyle{\prod\sigma}}:{\sigma\vDash n,\ |\sigma|=r}\big\}.

(2.6)

Clearly
\pi(n)=\max_{r}\pi_{r}(n)
. The numbers
\pi(n)
and
\pi_{r}(n)
are well understood, and a proof of the following can be found for example in
[
28
]
. The first part follows from the fact that the maximum value of
\prod\sigma
, for
\sigma\vDash n
with
|\sigma|=r
, occurs when the entries of
\sigma
are ‘almost equal’, in the sense that they are all within
1
of each other (as
s>t+1
implies
(s-1)(t+1)=st+s-(t+1)>st
). The second part then involves showing that the product of almost-equal numbers with a given sum occurs when almost all of the numbers are
3
.

Lemma 2.7
.

(i)

If
1\leq r\leq n
, then
\pi_{r}(n)=\lceil\tfrac{n}{r}\rceil^{t}\cdot\lfloor\tfrac{n}{r}\rfloor^{r-t}
, where
0\leq t\leq r-1
is such that
n\equiv t\ (\mathrm{mod}\ r)
.

(ii)

For any
n\geq 2
we have

\pi(n)=\begin{cases}3^{n/3}&\text{if $n\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$}\\
4\cdot 3^{(n-4)/3}&\text{if $n\equiv 1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$}\\
2\cdot 3^{(n-2)/3}&\text{if $n\equiv 2\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)$}.\end{cases}

The next family of parameters concerns certain
pairs
of compositions.
Given compositions
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
and
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
of the same length (but possibly with different sums), we write
\tau\preceq\sigma
if
t_{i}\leq s_{i}
for all
i
; for such compositions
\tau\preceq\sigma
, we define

\eta(\sigma,\tau)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}t_{i}^{s_{i}-t_{i}}.

Furthermore, for any composition
\sigma
we define

\xi(\sigma)=\max\{{\eta(\sigma,\tau)}:{\tau\preceq\sigma}\}.

Note that if
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
, then
\xi(\sigma)=\xi(s_{1})\cdots\xi(s_{r})
, which explains our re-use of the
\xi
symbol. For
1\leq r\leq n
, we define

\Xi(n)=\max\{{\xi(\sigma)}:{\sigma\vDash n}\}\and\Xi_{r}(n)=\max\{{\xi(\sigma)}:{\sigma\vDash n,\ |\sigma|=r}\},

so again
\Xi(n)=\max_{r}\Xi_{r}(n)
.

Lemma 2.8
.

For any
1\leq r\leq n
we have

(i)

\Xi(n)=\xi(n)
,

(ii)

\Xi_{r}(n)=\xi(n-r+1)
,

(iii)

\Xi_{1}(n)>\Xi_{2}(n)>\cdots>\Xi_{n-1}(n)=\Xi_{n}(n)=1
.

Proof.

Beginning with
(ii)
, consider a composition
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\vDash n
. If
r\geq 2
, then from Lemma
2.4
(ii)
and
\xi(1)=1
, we obtain

\xi(\sigma)=\xi(s_{1})\cdots\xi(s_{r-2})\xi(s_{r-1})\xi(s_{r})\leq\xi(s_{1})\cdots\xi(s_{r-2})\xi(s_{r-1}+s_{r}-1)\xi(1)=\xi(\sigma^{\prime}),

where
\sigma^{\prime}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r-2},s_{r-1}+s_{r}-1,1)
. Continuing, we obtain
\xi(\sigma)\leq\xi(\tau)
, for the composition
\tau=(n-r+1,1,\ldots,1)
. Since this is true for all
\sigma\vDash n
with
|\sigma|=r
, it follows that

\Xi_{r}(n)=\xi(\tau)=\xi(n-r+1)\xi(1)\cdots\xi(1)=\xi(n-r+1).

Now that we have proved
(ii)
, note that
(iii)
then follows from Lemma
2.4
(i)
. Item
(i)
quickly follows.
∎

2.3
Semigroups

For more background on semigroups, see
[
37
,
8
]
.

For a semigroup
S
, we denote by
S^{1}
the monoid obtained by adjoining an identity element to
S
if necessary; so
S=S^{1}
if
S
is a monoid. Green’s
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
,
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
relations are defined for
x,y\in S
by

x\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}y\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}x=S^{1}y,\hskip 20.00003ptx\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}y\ \Leftrightarrow\ xS^{1}=yS^{1},\hskip 20.00003ptx\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y\ \Leftrightarrow\ S^{1}xS^{1}=S^{1}yS^{1}.

Green’s
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
relations are defined by
{\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\cap{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
and
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\vee{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}
, where the latter is the join in the lattice of equivalence relations on
S
. We have
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}\circ{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}\circ{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}
, and moreover
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
if
S
is finite.

An element
x
of a semigroup
S
is
regular
if
x=xyx
for some
y\in S
(equivalently, if
x=xyx
and
y=yxy
for some
y\in S
), while
x
is an
idempotent
if
x=x^{2}
. We write
\operatorname{Reg}(S)
and
E(S)
for the sets of all regular elements and all idempotents of
S
, respectively, neither of which is a subsemigroup in general; both are non-empty when
S
is finite. Obviously
E(S)\subseteq\operatorname{Reg}(S)
.

An element
x
of a semigroup
S
is a
left zero
of
S
if
xa=x
for all
a\in S
. A
left zero semigroup
is a semigroup in which every element is a left zero: i.e.,
S
satisfies the law
xy=x
. Every left zero semigroup is
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-simple, meaning that all elements are
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-related. By a
left zero subsemigroup
of a semigroup
S
we mean a subsemigroup that happens to be a left zero semigroup (but note that its elements might not be left zeros of
S
itself).

Right zeros and right zero (sub)semigroups are defined analogously, and they have analogous properties.

If a semigroup
S
has a left zero and a right zero, then it has a unique left zero and a unique right zero, which are equal, and hence the unique
(two-sided) zero
of
S
. A
null semigroup
is a semigroup
S
for which
S^{2}=\{{xy}:{x,y\in S}\}=\{z\}
for some
z\in S
, which is then necessarily the zero of
S
.

A
rectangular band
is (isomorphic to) a semigroup of the form
P\times Q
, where
P
is a left zero semigroup and
Q
a right zero semigroup; multiplication obeys the rule
(p_{1},q_{1})(p_{2},q_{2})=(p_{1},q_{2})
. If
|P|=p
and
|Q|=q
we say the rectangular band is
p\times q
. Green’s relations on a rectangular band
P\times Q
are particularly easy to describe:

(p_{1},q_{1})\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}(p_{2},q_{2})\ \Leftrightarrow\ q_{1}=q_{2}\and(p_{1},q_{1})\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}(p_{2},q_{2})\ \Leftrightarrow\ p_{1}=p_{2}.

Further,
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
is the equality relation, and
{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
is the universal relation.
As extreme cases, a left zero semigroup of size
p
is a
p\times 1
rectangular band, with a similar statement for right zero semigroups.

2.4
Full transformation semigroups

The full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}
consists of all self-maps of the set
X
, under composition. When
X
is the set
{\bf n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
for a positive integer
n
, we write
\mathcal{T}_{X}=\mathcal{T}_{n}
. For more on transformation semigroups, see
[
8
,
37
,
26
]
.

For
f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
we write
\operatorname{im}(f)=\{{xf}:{x\in X}\}
for the
image
of
f
,
\operatorname{rank}(f)=|{\operatorname{im}(f)}|
for the
rank
of
f
, and
\ker(f)=\big\{{(x,y)\in X\times X}:{xf=yf}\big\}
for the
kernel
of
f
. Green’s relations on
\mathcal{T}_{X}
are given by

\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}g

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{im}(f)=\operatorname{im}(g),

\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}g

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \ker(f)=\ker(g),

\displaystyle f\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}g\ \Leftrightarrow\ f\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}f

\displaystyle\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}(f)=\operatorname{rank}(g).

Fix some subset
\Omega
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
. For any non-empty subset
A\subseteq X
, any equivalence relation
\varepsilon
on
X
(denoted
{\varepsilon\preceq X}
), and any cardinal
1\leq r\leq|X|
, we write

\displaystyle L_{A}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{{f\in\Omega}:{\operatorname{im}(f)=A}\},

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{{f\in\Omega}:{\ker(f)=\varepsilon}\},

\displaystyle D_{r}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\{{f\in\Omega}:{\operatorname{rank}(f)=r}\}.

When there is no chance of confusion, we generally write
L_{A}=L_{A}(\mathcal{T}_{X})
, and so on. So the sets
L_{A}
(
\varnothing\not=A\subseteq X
),
R_{\varepsilon}
(
\varepsilon\preceq X
) and
D_{r}
(
1\leq r\leq|X|
) are the
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-,
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
- and
{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}
-classes of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, respectively. For an equivalence
\varepsilon\preceq X
, we write
\|\varepsilon\|=|X/\varepsilon|
for the number of
\varepsilon
-classes.

At times it will be convenient to work with the
kernel partition
of a transformation
f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
, defined by
\operatorname{Ker}(f)=X/{\ker(f)}
. This is a set partition of
X
, and
|{\operatorname{Ker}(f)}|=\|{\ker(f)}\|=\operatorname{rank}(f)
.

A transformation
f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
will be represented in tabular form as
f=\binom{A_{i}}{a_{i}}_{i\in I}
to indicate that
\operatorname{im}(f)=\{{a_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
and
a_{i}f^{-1}=A_{i}
for all
i\in I
; we additionally assume that the indexing set
I
is
faithful
in the sense that
a_{i}\not=a_{j}
whenever
i\not=j
, so that
\operatorname{rank}(f)=|I|
. Note that
\operatorname{Ker}(f)=\{{A_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
. When
\operatorname{rank}(f)
is finite, we write
f=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{r}\\
a_{1}&\cdots&a_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. It is well known and easy to see that
f=\binom{A_{i}}{a_{i}}_{i\in I}
is idempotent if and only if
a_{i}\in A_{i}
for all
i
.

It will also be convenient to describe here an operation
\oplus
. Suppose we have a set of pairwise-disjoint non-empty sets
X_{i}
(
i\in I
), and a collection of transformations
f_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}}
(
i\in I
). Writing
X=\bigcup_{i\in I}X_{i}
, we define
\bigoplus_{i\in I}f_{i}
to be the unique transformation of
X
whose restriction to each
X_{i}
is
f_{i}
. For non-empty subsets
\Omega_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}}
(
i\in I
), we write
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\Omega_{i}
for the set of all
\bigoplus_{i\in I}f_{i}
with each
f_{i}\in\Omega_{i}
. In particular,
\bigoplus_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}}
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, and is isomorphic to the (external) direct product
\prod_{i\in I}\mathcal{T}_{X_{i}}
.

For a fixed non-empty subset
A
of the set
X
, we have a subsemigroup

\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}}:{\operatorname{im}(f)\subseteq A}\}.

These subsemigroups have been studied extensively in the literature
[
48
,
54
,
45
,
47
,
55
,
24
,
20
]
, and will play an important role in the current work. Note that for any
a\in\mathcal{T}_{X}
with
\operatorname{im}(a)=A
, the subsemigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)
is precisely the principal left ideal
\mathcal{T}_{X}a=\{{fa}:{f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}}\}
. When
X={\bf n}
and
A={\bf k}
for integers
1\leq k\leq n
, we write
\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)=\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)
.

While the semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}
is regular, the same is not true of
\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)
in general. It was shown in
[
48
]
that an element
f
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)
is regular (in the semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}(A)
) if and only if
Xf=Af
; an equivalent formulation
[
20
]
is that
A

saturates

\ker(f)
, meaning that every
\ker(f)
-class contains an element of
A
.

2.5
Left and right zero transformation semigroups

For a subset
\Omega
of a finite semigroup
S
, we write
\lambda(\Omega)
and
\rho(\Omega)
for the maximum size of a left or right zero subsemigroup of
S
contained in
\Omega
. In particular,
\lambda(S)
and
\rho(S)
are the maximum sizes of a left or right zero subsemigroup of
S
.

Now consider a finite semigroup
S
, and a left zero subsemigroup
T
of
S
. Since the elements of
T
are all
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-related in
T
, it follows that
T
is contained in a single
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-class of
S
, and hence in a single (regular)
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class; let these classes be
L
and
D
, respectively. Noting that all left zeros are idempotents, we have
T\subseteq E(L)
. It is easy to see that
E(L)
is a left zero semigroup for any regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-class
L
. It follows from all this, and the analogous considerations of right zero subsemigroups, that

\displaystyle\lambda(S)

\displaystyle=\max\{{\lambda(D)}:{D\in S/{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}}\}

and

\displaystyle\rho(S)

\displaystyle=\max\{{\rho(D)}:{D\in S/{\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}}}\},

and that for any
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class
D
of
S
,

\displaystyle\lambda(D)

\displaystyle={\max}\big\{{|E(L)|}:{L\in D/{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}}\big\}

and

\displaystyle\rho(D)

\displaystyle={\max}\big\{{|E(R)|}:{R\in D/{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}}\big\}.

The paper
[
28
]
classified the left and right zero subsemigroups of a finite full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and calculated the maximum size of such subsemigroups; see also
[
17
]
. It will be convenient to interpret these results in our current set-up, using the
\xi
and
\pi
parameters from Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
. For
1\leq r\leq n
, we will write

\lambda(n)=\lambda(\mathcal{T}_{n}),\hskip 20.00003pt\rho(n)=\rho(\mathcal{T}_{n}),\hskip 20.00003pt\lambda_{r}(n)=\lambda(D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})),\hskip 20.00003pt\rho_{r}(n)=\rho(D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})).

As discussed in Subsection
2.4
, the
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
- and
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-classes contained in the
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
are, respectively, the sets of the form

\displaystyle L_{A}=L_{A}(\mathcal{T}_{n})

\displaystyle=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{\operatorname{im}(f)=A}\}

for
A\subseteq{\bf n}
with
|A|=r

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}=R_{\varepsilon}(\mathcal{T}_{n})

\displaystyle=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{\ker(f)=\varepsilon}\}

for
\varepsilon\preceq{\bf n}
with
\|\varepsilon\|=r
.

The size of
E(L_{A})
is
r^{n-r}
, as any idempotent from
L_{A}
maps
A
identically, and maps
{\bf n}\setminus A
arbitrarily into
A
. The size of
E(R_{\varepsilon})
is equal to the product of the sizes of the
\varepsilon
-classes, as any idempotent from
R_{\varepsilon}
maps each
\varepsilon
-class onto a single element of that class. By maximising these values, one obtains the following, which is
[
28
, Propositions 3.1 and 3.2]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

For
1\leq r\leq n
, the maximum size of a left or right zero semigroup contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is equal to

\lambda_{r}(n)=r^{n-r}\and\rho_{r}(n)=\pi_{r}(n).

Remark 2.10
.

It is easy to check that for fixed
r
, the sequences
\lambda_{r}(n)
and
\rho_{r}(n)
are non-decreasing in
n\geq r
.

Remark 2.11
.

Keeping in mind the formula for
\rho_{r}(n)=\pi_{r}(n)
from Lemma
2.7
(i)
, we of course have
\rho_{n}(n)=1
for all
n
.
Less trivially, we have
\rho_{r}(n)=2^{n-r}
for
\frac{n}{2}\leq r\leq n
.

The values of
\lambda(n)
and
\rho(n)
are found by maximising
\lambda_{r}(n)
and
\rho_{r}(n)
over all
1\leq r\leq n
. The following is
[
28
, Theorems 3.3 and 3.4]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

For
n\geq 1
, the maximum size of a left or right zero subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is equal to

\lambda(n)=\xi(n)\and\rho(n)=\pi(n).

We will also need to extend Theorem
2.9
to the semigroups
\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{\operatorname{im}(f)\subseteq{\bf k}}\}
. For integers
{1\leq r\leq k\leq n}
, we write

\lambda_{r}(n,k)=\lambda(D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)))\and\rho_{r}(n,k)=\rho(D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)))

for the largest size of a left or right zero semigroup contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k))
, respectively. Note that
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k))
is generally not a
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)
; however, it contains a single regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class, as follows from results of
[
20
, Section 5]
.

Lemma 2.13
.

For any
1\leq r\leq k\leq n
, we have
\lambda_{r}(n,k)=\lambda_{r}(n)
and
\rho_{r}(n,k)=\rho_{r}(k)
.

Proof.

For any
A\subseteq{\bf k}
of size
r
, it is clear that
L_{A}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\subseteq D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k))
. Since the
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-classes contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
have a common number of idempotents, it quickly follows that
{\lambda_{r}(n,k)=\lambda_{r}(n)}
.

For the statement concerning
\rho_{r}(n,k)
, consider a regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-class
R
contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(k))
. Let
\varepsilon
be the common kernel of the elements of
R
, and let the
\varepsilon
-classes be
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
. Since
R
is regular,
{\bf k}
saturates
\varepsilon
, so it follows that
B_{i}=A_{i}\cap{\bf k}
is non-empty for each
i\in{\bf r}
; let
\eta\preceq{\bf k}
be the equivalence with classes
B_{1},\ldots,B_{r}
. Every idempotent from
R
is uniquely determined by (and uniquely determines) its restriction to
B_{1}\cup\cdots\cup B_{r}={\bf k}
, which is an idempotent from
R_{\eta}(\mathcal{T}_{k})
; thus,
|E(R)|=|E(R_{\eta}(\mathcal{T}_{k}))|
. Moreover, the above correspondence is reversible; given any
\eta\preceq{\bf k}
with
\|\eta\|=r
we can find a suitable
\varepsilon\preceq{\bf n}
by arbitrarily assigning the remaining elements of
{\bf n}\setminus{\bf k}
to
\eta
-classes. The result now follows.
∎

One could then obtain results concerning the largest left and right zero subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}(k)
by maximising the values of
\lambda_{r}(n,k)=\lambda_{r}(n)
and
\rho_{r}(n,k)=\rho_{r}(k)
over
r\in{\bf k}
. Note that the latter maximum will be
\rho(k)=\pi(k)
, as in Theorem
2.12
, but the former is
{\max\{{\lambda_{r}(n)}:{1\leq r\leq k}\}}
, and is not necessarily equal to
\lambda(n)=\xi(n)
.

3
Rectangular bands and hypergraphs

In this section we calculate the (minimum transformation) degree
\mu(B)
of an arbitrary finite rectangular band
B
, thereby answering a question of Easdown
[
17
]
. Since all
p\times q
rectangular bands are isomorphic, the degree of such a band depends only on the parameters
p
and
q
. Accordingly, for
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
we define

\beta(p,q)=\min\{{n}:{\mathcal{T}_{n}\text{ contains a $p\times q$ rectangular band}}\}.

Since rectangular bands are
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-simple, any such band contained in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is in fact contained in a single
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Accordingly, for
p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}
we define

\beta_{r}(p,q)=\min\{{n}:{D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\text{ contains a $p\times q$ rectangular band}}\},

and of course
\beta(p,q)=\min_{r}\beta_{r}(p,q)
. In the above definition of
\beta_{r}(p,q)
, we interpret
\min\varnothing=\infty
. In particular, we have
\beta_{1}(p,q)=\infty
for
p\geq 2
, since
D_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is an
n
-element right zero semigroup (i.e., a
1\times n
rectangular band) for any
n
.
It follows that
\beta(p,q)=\min_{r\geq 2}\beta_{r}(p,q)
when
p\geq 2
.

As we noted in Subsection
2.3
, the special cases of
q=1
and
p=1
correspond to left and right zero semigroups, respectively. In these cases, values of
\beta(p,q)
and
\beta_{r}(p,q)
can be quickly deduced from the results of
[
28
,
17
]
stated in Theorems
2.9
and
2.12
above. The following statement uses the
\xi
and
\pi
parameters from Subsections
2.1
and
2.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(i)

For any
p\geq 1
and any
r\geq 2
, we have

\beta_{r}(p,1)=\min\{{n}:{r^{n-r}\geq p}\}=\lceil\log_{r}p\rceil+r\and\beta(p,1)=\min\{{n}:{\xi(n)\geq p}\}.

(ii)

For any
q,r\geq 1
, we have

\beta_{r}(1,q)=\min\{{n}:{\pi_{r}(n)\geq q}\}\and\beta(1,q)=\min\{{n}:{\pi(n)\geq q}\}.

When
p,q\geq 2
, we cannot calculate the numbers
\beta(p,q)
and
\beta_{r}(p,q)
by finding the largest rectangular bands contained in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. The reason for this is that even if
\mathcal{T}_{n}
contained a rectangular band of size
pq
, it may not contain one with the correct dimentions,
p\times q
. In fact, it follows from results of
[
28
, Section 4]
that the largest rectangular band contained in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
(or in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
) is always a left or right zero semigroup, so such an approach is bound to fail. Also, since a rectangular band is the direct product of a left and right zero semigroup of appropriate sizes, and since
\mu(S\times T)\leq\mu(S)+\mu(T)
for any semigroups
S
and
T
, we have an upper bound of
\beta(p,q)\leq\beta(p,1)+\beta(1,q)
. This upper bound turns out to be an over-estimate, however, as can be seen by inspecting Table
2
.

Our approach for
p,q\geq 2
will be via
hypergraphs
. After proving some results on hypergraphs in Subsection
3.1
, which we believe are of independent interest (see especially Theorem
3.6
), we return to rectangular bands in Subsection
3.2
(see Theorem
3.9
). We give further applications of our results to rectangular groups in Subsection
3.3
(see Theorem
3.12
).

3.1
Hypergraphs

A
hypergraph
is a pair
H=(V,\mathbb{E})
, where
V
is a set of vertices, and
\mathbb{E}
is a set of non-empty subsets of
V
called
(hyper)edges
. If each edge has size
r
, we say that
H
is
r
-uniform
and call it an
r
-hypergraph
; obviously we must have
r\leq|V|
if
H
is non-empty (i.e., has at least one edge). By an
r
-partition
of an
r
-hypergraph
H
we mean a partition
{\bf V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}\}
of the vertex set such that each edge is a transversal of
{\bf V}
(i.e., contains a unique point from each block
V_{i}
of
{\bf V}
). We call
H

r
-partite
if such an
r
-partition exists.

Given a partition
{\bf V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}\}
of a set
V
, the
complete
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph

H_{\bf V}
has vertex set
V
, and its edges are all the transversals of
{\bf V}
. The number of edges of
H_{\bf V}
is equal to
|V_{1}|\cdots|V_{r}|
, which is the product
\prod\sigma_{\bf V}
of the composition
\sigma_{\bf V}=(|V_{1}|,\ldots,|V_{r}|)
. Any
r
-hypergraph over
V
for which
{\bf V}
is an
r
-partition is a subgraph of
H_{\bf V}
, and hence has at most
\prod\sigma_{\bf V}
edges.

From the above discussion we have the following well-known result, expressed in terms of the
\pi
parameters from Subsection
2.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n,r\geq 1
be integers. Then there exists a non-empty
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices if and only if
r\leq n
, in which case the maximum number of edges in such a hypergraph is
\pi_{r}(n)
. ∎

Our main goal in this subsection is to prove Theorem
3.6
below, which gives the corresponding result for hypergraphs with some specified number of partitions. The key technical step in the proof is Proposition
3.5
, which shows that
r
-hypergraphs with many
r
-partitions give rise to
r
-partite
r
-hypergraphs on smaller vertex sets. The proof of the proposition is inductive, and relies on the next two lemmas.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
H
is an
r
-hypergraph with at least one edge, and at least
rt+1
distinct
r
-partitions for some integer
t\geq 1
. Then there exists a pair of vertices that belong to the same block in at least
t+1
of the partitions, but not in all of them.

Proof.

Let
A=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
be an edge of
H
, and suppose
{\bf V}_{1},\ldots,{\bf V}_{rt+1}
are distinct
r
-partitions of
H
. For each
1\leq i\leq rt+1
, let
{\bf V}_{i}=\{V_{i;1},\ldots,V_{i;r}\}
, where
v_{j}\in V_{i;j}
for each
j\in{\bf r}
.
Since
{\bf V}_{1}=\{V_{1;1},\ldots,V_{1;r}\}
and
{\bf V}_{2}=\{V_{2;1},\ldots,V_{2;r}\}
are distinct, there exists
k\in{\bf r}
such that
V_{1;k}\not\subseteq V_{2;k}
; fix some
w\in V_{1;k}\setminus V_{2;k}
.
For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,rt+1\}
let
j_{i}\in{\bf r}
be such that
w\in V_{i;j_{i}}
. For each
j\in{\bf r}
let
I_{j}=\{{i\in\{1,\ldots,rt+1\}}:{j_{i}=j}\}
. Since
\{1,\ldots,rt+1\}=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{r}
, we have
|I_{m}|\geq t+1
for some
m\in{\bf r}
.
Then
w
and
v_{m}
belong to the same block in at least
t+1
of the partitions (all the
{\bf V}_{i}
for
i\in I_{m}
).

Aiming for a contradiction, suppose
w
and
v_{m}
belong to the same block in
all
of the partitions. Since
w\in V_{1;k}
and
v_{m}\in V_{1;m}
, it follows that
k=m
. But then
w,v_{m}\in V_{2;m}=V_{2;k}
, contradicting the definition of
w\in V_{1;k}\setminus V_{2;k}
.
∎

Consider a hypergraph
H
with vertex set
V
, and let
u,v\in V
be distinct vertices. Write
H/\{u,v\}
for the hypergraph obtained by identifying
u
and
v
. Formally, the vertex set of
H/\{u,v\}
is obtained from
V
by removing the two vertices
u,v
and replacing them by a single vertex denoted
uv
; all edges of
H
involving neither of
u,v
are still edges of
H/\{u,v\}
; all other edges of
H/\{u,v\}
are of the form
\{uv,w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\}
where at least one of
\{u,w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
,
\{v,w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
or
\{u,v,w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\}
is an edge of
H
. If
H
is
r
-uniform, then
H/\{u,v\}
might not be. Also note that
H/\{u,v\}
could have fewer edges than
H
, but it cannot have more.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
H
is an
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and
q
edges, with at least
t+1
distinct
r
-partitions, for some integer
t\geq 1
. Suppose vertices
u
and
v
belong to the same block in
t
of the
r
-partitions, but not in all of them. Then
H/\{u,v\}
is an
r
-hypergraph with
n-1
vertices and
q
edges, and has at least
t
distinct
r
-partitions.

Proof.

For simplicity, we write
H^{\prime}=H/\{u,v\}
throughout the proof. Let the vertex set of
H
be
W\cup\{u,v\}
, so the vertex set of
H^{\prime}
is
W\cup\{uv\}
. (This of course has size
n-1
.)

For each edge
A
of
H
, let
A^{\prime}
be the corresponding edge of
H/\{u,v\}
, obtained by replacing
u
and/or
v
by
uv
if necessary, as explained before the lemma. To show that
H^{\prime}
has
q
edges, we need to show that the map
A\mapsto A^{\prime}
is injective. So suppose
A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}
for edges
A,B
of
H
. If
A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}\subseteq W
, then
A=A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}=B
. Otherwise,
A^{\prime}=B^{\prime}=\{uv\}\cup C
for some
C\subseteq W
. Then
A
and
B
must be one of
\{u\}\cup C
,
\{v\}\cup C
or
\{u,v\}\cup C
. But
\{u,v\}\cup C
cannot be an edge of
H
, since
u,v
belong to the same block in some
r
-partition of
H
. On the other hand,
\{u\}\cup C
and
\{v\}\cup C
cannot both be edges of
H
, since
u,v
belong to distinct blocks in some
r
-partition of
H
. This all shows that
A=B
.

The previous paragraph also shows that
|A^{\prime}|=|A|=r
for every edge
A
of
H
, so
H^{\prime}
is
r
-uniform.

Finally, suppose
{\bf V}_{1},\ldots,{\bf V}_{t}
are distinct
r
-partitions of
H
in each of which
u,v
belong to the same block. For each
1\leq i\leq t
let
{\bf V}_{i}=\{V_{i;1},\ldots,V_{i;r}\}
, where
u,v\in V_{i;1}
. For each such
i
, let
{\bf V}_{i}^{\prime}=\{V_{i;1}^{\prime},V_{i;2},\ldots,V_{i;r}\}
, where
V_{i;1}^{\prime}
is obtained from
V_{i;1}
by replacing the pair of vertices
u,v
(from
H
) by the single vertex
uv
(from
H^{\prime}
). Then by the above characterisation of the edges of
H
, it is clear that each
{\bf V}_{i}^{\prime}
is an
r
-partition of
H^{\prime}
, and that these are still distinct.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
H
is an
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and
q\geq 1
edges, with at least
r^{l-1}+1
distinct
r
-partitions for some integer
l\geq 1
, then there is an
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph with
n-l
vertices and
q
edges.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
l
. Suppose first that
l=1
. So
H
has at least two distinct
r
-partitions, say
{\bf V}_{1}
and
{\bf V}_{2}
. Since these are distinct, there exist vertices
u,v
belonging to the same block in
{\bf V}_{1}
but not in
{\bf V}_{2}
. By Lemma
3.4
(with
t=1
),
H/\{u,v\}
is an
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph with
n-1=n-l
vertices and
q
edges.

Now suppose
l\geq 2
. Since
r^{l-1}+1=r\cdot r^{l-2}+1
, it follows from Lemma
3.3
(with
t=r^{l-2}
) that there are vertices
u,v
of
H
that belong to the same block in at least
r^{l-2}+1
of the partitions, but not in all of them. By Lemma
3.4
(with
t=r^{l-2}+1
),
H/\{u,v\}
is an
r
-hypergraph with
n-1
vertices and
q
edges, and with at least
r^{l-2}+1=r^{(l-1)-1}+1
distinct
r
-partitions. By induction there exists an
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph with
(n-1)-(l-1)=n-l
vertices and
q
edges.
∎

Here is the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
n,p\geq 1
and
r\geq 2
be integers, and let
l=\lceil\log_{r}p\rceil
. Then there exists a non-empty
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and at least
p
distinct
r
-partitions if and only if
r+l\leq n
, in which case the maximum number of edges in such a hypergraph is
\pi_{r}(n-l)
.

Proof.

If
p=1
then
l=0
, and the result reduces to Lemma
3.2
. For the rest of the proof we assume that
p\geq 2
. It follows from the definition of
l
that
l\geq 1
, and
r^{l-1}+1\leq p\leq r^{l}
.

Suppose there exists a non-empty
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and
q(\geq 1)
edges, and with
p
distinct
r
-partitions. Since
p\geq r^{l-1}+1
, Proposition
3.5
applies, and it tells us there is an
r
-partite
r
-hypergraph with
n-l
vertices and
q
edges. It then follows from Lemma
3.2
that
r\leq n-l
(i.e.,
r+l\leq n
) and that
q\leq\pi_{r}(n-l)
.

It remains to show that if
r+l\leq n
there exists an
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and
\pi_{r}(n-l)
edges, with (at least)
p
distinct
r
-partitions. To do so, suppose
r+l\leq n
. By Lemma
3.2
we may fix an
r
-hypergraph
H
on
n-l
vertices with
\pi_{r}(n-l)
edges. Let
{\bf V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}\}
be an
r
-partition of
H
. Now let
H^{\prime}
be the
r
-hypergraph obtained from
H
by adding
l
isolated vertices. Then
H^{\prime}
has
n
vertices and
\pi_{r}(n-l)
edges, and (at least)
r^{l}
distinct
r
-partitions (each of the new vertices, independently, can go in any of the blocks
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
). Since
r^{l}\geq p
, we are done.
∎

3.2
Rectangular bands

We now use the results of the previous subsection to calculate the degree of an arbitrary finite rectangular band, answering a question of Easdown
[
17
]
. The first result shows how to construct certain ‘large’ such bands. Its proof shares some ideas with that of Theorem
3.6
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
1\leq r\leq n
, and if
0\leq l\leq n-r
, then
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
contains an
r^{l}\times\pi_{r}(n-l)
rectangular band.

Proof.

Fix a partition
{\bf V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}\}
of
{\bf n}\setminus{\bf l}=\{l+1,\ldots,n\}
with
|V_{1}|\cdots|V_{r}|=\pi_{r}(n-l)
. For each function
f:{\bf l}\to{\bf r}
, and each transversal
A=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\}
of
{\bf V}
, with each
v_{i}\in V_{i}
, we define a transformation

e(f,A)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}V_{1}\cup 1f^{-1}&\cdots&V_{r}\cup rf^{-1}\\
v_{1}&\cdots&v_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n}).

It is easy to check that these compose according to the rule
e(f,A)\cdot e(g,B)=e(f,B)
. It follows that the set of all such
e(f,A)
is a rectangular band contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Since there are
r^{l}
functions
{\bf l}\to{\bf r}
, and
\pi_{r}(n-l)
transversals of
{\bf V}
, the band has the stated dimensions.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

If
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
contains a
p\times q
rectangular band, where
p,q\geq 2
, then with
l=\lceil\log_{r}p\rceil
we have
\pi_{r}(n-l)\geq q
.

Proof.

Suppose
B
is a
p\times q
rectangular band contained in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Since
D_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is a right zero semigroup, and since
p\geq 2
, we have
r\geq 2
. Let

\mathbb{I}=\{{\operatorname{im}(f)}:{f\in B}\}\and\mathbb{K}=\{{\operatorname{Ker}(f)}:{f\in B}\}

be the sets of all images and kernel partitions of the elements of
B
.

Since each element of
B
has rank
r
, each member of
\mathbb{I}
is a subset of
{\bf n}
of size
r
, which means that
H=({\bf n},\mathbb{I})
is an
r
-hypergraph.
Moreover, given
A\in\mathbb{I}
and
{\bf K}\in\mathbb{K}
we have
A=\operatorname{im}(f)
and
{\bf K}=\operatorname{Ker}(g)
for some
f,g\in B
, and then since
fg\in B\subseteq D_{r}
it follows that
A
is a transversal of
{\bf K}
. This means that each member of
\mathbb{K}
is an
r
-partition of
H
.

Since
B
has
p

\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-classes and
q

\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-classes, it follows that
|\mathbb{I}|=q
and
|\mathbb{K}|=p
. This all means that
H
is an
r
-hypergraph with
n
vertices and
q
edges, and with
p
distinct
r
-partitions. It then follows from Theorem
3.6
that
\pi_{r}(n-l)\geq q
.
∎

We can now give an expression for
\beta(p,q)
, which we recall denotes the degree of a
p\times q
rectangular band. The cases where one or both of
p,q
is
1
were treated in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
p,q\geq 2
, then the degree of a
p\times q
rectangular band is equal to

\beta(p,q)=\min_{r\geq 2}\beta_{r}(p,q),\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pt\beta_{r}(p,q)={\min}\big\{{n}:{\pi_{r}(n-\lceil\log_{r}p\rceil)\geq q}\big\}\text{ for $r\geq 2$.}

Proof.

At the beginning of Section
3
we explained that
\beta(p,q)=\min_{r\geq 2}\beta_{r}(p,q)
, where

\beta_{r}(p,q)=\min\{{n}:{D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\text{ contains a $p\times q$ rectangular band}}\}.

It therefore remains to show that for
n,r\geq 2
,
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
contains a
p\times q
rectangular band if and only if
\pi_{r}(n-l)\geq q
, where
l=\lceil\log_{r}p\rceil
. The forwards implication is Lemma
3.8
.

For the converse, suppose
\pi_{r}(n-l)\geq q
. Then of course we have
n-l\geq r
, and so
l\leq n-r
. Proposition
3.7
then tells us that
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
contains an
r^{l}\times\pi_{r}(n-l)
rectangular band, and hence also a
p\times q
rectangular band, since
p\leq r^{l}
and
q\leq\pi_{r}(n-l)
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

In principle, the expression for
\beta(p,q)
in Theorem
3.9
involves finding the minimum of an infinite sequence of numbers: i.e., the
\beta_{r}(p,q)
for all
r\geq 2
. In practice however, only a finite subsequence needs to be considered. For example, to find
\beta(p,q)
, one could first find (say)
m=\beta_{2}(p,q)
. We then know that
\beta(p,q)\leq m
, and since
\beta_{r}(p,q)\geq r
for all
r
it quickly follows that
\beta(p,q)=\min_{2\leq r\leq m}\beta_{r}(p,q)
.

Table
2
gives several values of
\beta(p,q)
, calculated in this way. The table also gives the ranks
r
such that
\beta(p,q)=\beta_{r}(p,q)
. For some values of
p,q
there are multiple such
r
, in which case the table gives the minimum
r
.

3.3
Rectangular groups

Recall that a
rectangular group
is (isomorphic to) a direct product
S=B\times G
, where
B
is a rectangular band and
G
is a group. If the band
B
is
p\times q
, we say that
S
is a
p\times q
rectangular group over
G
. An alternative characterisation of rectangular groups can be found in
[
37
, Exercise 10, page 139]
: they are precisely the
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-simple regular semigroups whose idempotents form a subsemigroup.

The results of previous subsections can be applied to calculate the degree of an arbitrary finite rectangular group:

Theorem 3.12
.

If
S
is a finite
p\times q
rectangular group over
G
, then

\mu(S)=\min_{r\geq\mu(G)}\beta_{r}(p,q).

The numbers
\beta_{r}(p,q)
are given in Theorems
3.1
and
3.9
.

Proof.

Consider a finite
p\times q
rectangular group
S=B\times G
, and for simplicity write

n=\mu(S)\and N=\min_{r\geq\mu(G)}\beta_{r}(p,q).

We must show that
n=N
.

By definition of
n
, there exists an embedding
\phi:S\to\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Since
S
is
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-simple,
\operatorname{im}(S)
is contained in a single
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, say
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Since
G
embeds in
S
, it also embeds into a group
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class in
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
; since such an
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class is isomorphic to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{r}
, it follows that
r\geq\mu(G)
. Similarly,
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
contains an isomorphic copy of
B
, and so
n\geq\beta_{r}(p,q)
by definition. This all shows that
n\geq N
.

To show that
n\leq N
, we must show that
S
embeds in
\mathcal{T}_{N}
. To do so, suppose
r\geq\mu(G)
is such that
N=\beta_{r}(p,q)
. By definition,
D_{r}(\mathcal{T}_{N})
contains a
p\times q
rectangular band
C
. Let
T=\bigcup_{c\in C}H_{c}
be the union of all the
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes of
\mathcal{T}_{N}
containing idempotents from
C
, so that
T
is a
p\times q
rectangular group over
\mathcal{S}_{r}
(this follows quickly from the above-mentioned characterisation of rectangular groups from
[
37
]
). Since
G
embeds in
\mathcal{S}_{r}
(as
r\geq\mu(G)
), it follows that
S=B\times G
embeds in
B\times\mathcal{S}_{r}\cong T
, and hence in
\mathcal{T}_{N}
.
∎

Remark 3.13
.

As in Remark
3.10
, the expression for
\mu(S)
in Theorem
3.12
involves finding the minimum of an infinite sequence of numbers. However, we again have
\mu(S)=\min_{\mu(G)\leq r\leq m}\beta_{r}(p,q)
for any
a priori
known upper bound
m
of
\mu(S)
. For example, we can take
m=\beta(p,q)+\mu(G)
, since
S
is the direct product of a
p\times q
rectangular band with
G
.

4
(Right) null and nilpotent semigroups

In this section we study null subsemigroups (Subsections
4.1
and
4.2
) of a full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, as well as some related kinds of subsemigroups: nilpotent subsemigroups (Subsections
4.3
and
4.4
), and so-called right null subsemigroups (Subsection
4.5
). As an application of our results on null semigroups, we give an example of a semigroup with a larger degree than one of its variants (Subsection
4.6
). Our results on right null semigroups will be used in Section
5
.

4.1
Null semigroups

Let
X
be an arbitrary set, possibly infinite. Obviously the zero of a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
is an idempotent. So we fix an idempotent
{\zeta\in E(\mathcal{T}_{X})}
, with the goal of classifying the null subsemigroups containing
\zeta
. We write
{\zeta=\binom{Z_{i}}{z_{i}}_{i\in I}}
, noting that
z_{i}\in Z_{i}
for all
i
, as
\zeta
is an idempotent.

Definition 4.1
.

Given an idempotent
\zeta=\binom{Z_{i}}{z_{i}}_{i\in I}
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, a
\zeta
-system
is a collection
{\bf W}
of sets

{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{such that}\hskip 20.00003ptz_{i}\in W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}\text{ for all $i\in I$.}

For such a
\zeta
-system
{\bf W}
, we define

N({\bf W},\zeta)=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}}:{Z_{i}f\subseteq W_{i}\text{ and }W_{i}f=\{z_{i}\}\text{ for all $i\in I$}}\}.

Lemma 4.2
.

Given an idempotent
\zeta
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, a subset
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
is a null semigroup with zero
\zeta
if and only if
\zeta\in S\subseteq N({\bf W},\zeta)
for some
\zeta
-system
{\bf W}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
). Suppose
S
is a null semigroup with zero
\zeta
. Obviously
\zeta\in S
. If
f\in S
, then from
\zeta=f\zeta
we obtain
Z_{i}f\subseteq Z_{i}
for all
i
. Since also
\{z_{i}\}=Z_{i}\zeta
for all
i
, it quickly follows that
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
is a
\zeta
-system, where

W_{i}=\bigcup_{f\in S}Z_{i}f\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $i\in I$}.

To see that
S\subseteq N({\bf W},\zeta)
, let
f\in S
and
i\in I
be arbitrary. We have
Z_{i}f\subseteq W_{i}
by definition. Moreover, for any
g\in S
we have
\{z_{i}\}=Z_{i}\zeta=(Z_{i}g)f
, from which it follows that
W_{i}f=\{z_{i}\}
.

(
\Leftarrow
). Now suppose
\zeta\in S\subseteq N({\bf W},\zeta)
for some
\zeta
-system
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
. Let
f,g\in S
be arbitrary. For any
i\in I
, and using
f,g\in N({\bf W},\zeta)
, we have
Z_{i}(fg)=(Z_{i}f)g\subseteq W_{i}g=\{z_{i}\}
; thus,
Z_{i}(fg)=\{z_{i}\}
, and so
fg=\zeta
.
∎

Now that we have characterised the null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we wish to find the maximum size of such a subsemigroup. We begin by calculating the size of the semigroups
N({\bf W},\zeta)
. It follows from the next lemma that this size depends only on the partitions
{{\bf Z}=\{{Z_{i}}:{i\in I}\}}
and
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
, indeed only on the cardinalities
|W_{i}|
and
|Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}|
for each
i\in I
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
is a
\zeta
-system, then

|N({\bf W},\zeta)|=\prod_{i\in I}|W_{i}|^{|Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}|}.

Proof.

An element
f
of
N({\bf W},\zeta)
is determined by the restrictions
f|_{Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}}
(
i\in I
), which are arbitrary functions
Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}\to W_{i}
.
∎

If
X
is infinite, then
\mathcal{T}_{X}
contains a null subsemigroup of maximum conceivable size
{2^{|X|}=|\mathcal{T}_{X}|}
; indeed it follows from Lemma
4.3
that
|N({\bf W},\zeta)|=2^{|X|}
if for example
|W_{i}|=|Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}|=|X|
for some
i
.

We now consider the finite case. For a positive integer
n
, we write
\nu(n)
for the maximum size of a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. We also write
\nu_{r}(n)
for the maximum size of a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
whose zero element has rank
r
.

Consider an idempotent
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{r}\\
z_{1}&\cdots&z_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
from
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and a
\zeta
-system
\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\}
with each
z_{i}\in W_{i}
. We then have two compositions
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
and
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
, where
s_{i}=|Z_{i}|
and
t_{i}=|W_{i}|
for each
i
, and we note that
\sigma\vDash n
and
\tau\preceq\sigma
, in the notation of Subsection
2.2
. Furthermore, Lemma
4.3
tells us that
|N({\bf W},\zeta)|=\eta(\sigma,\tau)
. It follows from the definitions, and using Lemma
2.8
, that

\displaystyle\nu(n)

\displaystyle=\max\{{\eta(\sigma,\tau)}:{\sigma\vDash n,\ \tau\preceq\sigma}\}

and

\displaystyle\nu_{r}(n)

\displaystyle=\max\{{\eta(\sigma,\tau)}:{\sigma\vDash n,\ |\sigma|=r,\ \tau\preceq\sigma}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{{\xi(\sigma)}:{\sigma\vDash n}\}

\displaystyle=\max\{{\xi(\sigma)}:{\sigma\vDash n,\ |\sigma|=r}\}

\displaystyle=\Xi(n)=\xi(n)

\displaystyle=\Xi_{r}(n)=\xi(n-r+1).

Combining this with Lemma
2.8
(iii)
, we have proved the following.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
n
be a positive integer.

(i)

For any
1\leq r\leq n
, the maximum size of a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
whose zero element has rank
r
is equal to
\nu_{r}(n)=\xi(n-r+1)
.

(ii)

We have
\nu_{1}(n)>\nu_{2}(n)>\cdots>\nu_{n-1}(n)=\nu_{n}(n)=1
.

(iii)

The maximum size of a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is equal to
\nu(n)=\nu_{1}(n)=\xi(n)
. Moreover, writing
t=\alpha(n)
, the set

N_{n}=\big\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{\operatorname{im}(f)\subseteq{\bf t}\text{ and }{\bf t}f=\{1\}}\big\}

is a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
of size
\nu(n)
. ∎

Remark 4.5
.

Although the numbers
\nu_{r}(n)
are defined in terms of the two parameters
n
and
r
, there really is only one independent parameter, namely the difference
n-r
.
(Thus, for example,
\nu_{5}(10)=\nu_{105}(110)=\xi(6)=27
.) This can be understood in the following way. Recall from the proof of Lemma
2.8
that the maximum value of
\xi(\sigma)
, for
\sigma\vDash n
with
{|\sigma|=r}
, occurs when
\sigma=(n-r+1,1,\ldots,1)
. This means that the biggest null subsemigroup whose zero
\zeta
has rank
r
occurs when there is a single
\ker(\zeta)
-class of size
n-r+1
,
say
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\{1,\ldots,n-r+1\}&n-r+2&\cdots&n\\
1&n-r+2&\cdots&n\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. A null subsemigroup
N
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
with zero
\zeta
, and of maximum size
|N|=\nu_{r}(n)=\xi(n-r+1)
, is then given by
N_{n-r+1}\oplus\mathcal{T}_{\{n-r+2\}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{T}_{\{n\}}
, where the subsemigroups
N_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{k}
are as defined in Theorem
4.4
(iii)
. And of course
N
is then isomorphic to
N_{n-r+1}
.

Remark 4.6
.

Null semigroups played an important role in the investigation of subsemigroup chains in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
undertaken in
[
6
]
. The null subsemigroups in
[
6
]
were contained in
principal factors
of
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-classes, so in a sense all their elements had the same rank (apart from the adjoined zero element). By contrast, the elements of the null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
considered here can have a range of ranks.

Turning Theorem
4.4
(iii)
around, we obtain the degree of a null semigroup:

Theorem 4.7
.

If
S
is a null semigroup of size
p
, then
\mu(S)=\min\{{n}:{\xi(n)\geq p}\}
. ∎

Remark 4.8
.

Comparing Theorems
4.7
and
3.1
(i)
, we see that null and left zero semigroups of the same size have the same degree.

4.2
Maximal null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}

It is also possible to calculate the number of maximal null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. (By a maximal null subsemigroup we mean a null subsemigroup that is not properly contained in another null subsemigroup.) To this end, we write
F(n)
for this number.

By Lemma
4.2
, any maximal null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is of the form
N({\bf W},\zeta)
for some idempotent
\zeta
, and some
\zeta
-system
{\bf W}
. So a first attempt to calculate
F(n)
would be to count the number of pairs
(\zeta,{\bf W})
where
\zeta
is an idempotent and
{\bf W}
a
\zeta
-system. However, due to a technicality explained below, this results in some over-counting. To get around this, it will be convenient to also write
F_{1}(n)
for the number of maximal null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
whose zero has rank
1
.

Consider an idempotent
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{r}\\
z_{1}&\cdots&z_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and a
\zeta
-system
{\bf W}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\}
, with each
z_{i}\in W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}
. For each
i
, let
\zeta_{i}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{i}\\
z_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
be the constant map with image
z_{i}
. Then each
{\bf W}_{i}=\{W_{i}\}
is trivially a
\zeta_{i}
-system, and it is clear that

N({\bf W},\zeta)=\bigoplus_{i\in{\bf r}}N({\bf W}_{i},\zeta_{i}),

with each factor
N({\bf W}_{i},\zeta_{i})
a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
whose zero has rank
1
. Thus, we can uniquely specify a maximal null subsemigroup
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
as follows:

(i)

Choose an integer partition
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\vdash n
(which, recall, means that
\sigma
is a composition of
n
and
s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{r}
).

(ii)

Choose a set partition
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\}
of
{\bf n}
with
|Z_{i}|=s_{i}
for each
i
.

(iii)

Choose maximal null subsemigroups
S_{i}
of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
,
i\in{\bf r}
, each of which has a zero of rank
1
.

The semigroup so-determined is then
S=S_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus S_{r}
.

Given a partition
\sigma
as in
(i)
, write
f(\sigma)
for the number of ways to choose the set partition in
(ii)
, and
g(\sigma)
for the number of ways to choose the semigroups
S_{i}
in
(iii)
. Note that the number of choices available in
(iii)
does not depend on the particular choice made in
(ii)
. It is therefore clear that

F(n)=\sum_{\sigma\vdash n}f(\sigma)g(\sigma),

(4.9)

and also that

g(\sigma)=F_{1}(s_{1})\cdots F_{1}(s_{r})\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\vdash n$.}

(4.10)

A formula for
f(\sigma)
is also well known, and easily calculated. For each
i\in{\bf n}
, let
\sigma_{i}
be the number of entries of
\sigma
equal to
i
. Then

f(\sigma)=\frac{n!}{\prod_{i\in{\bf n}}\sigma_{i}!(i!)^{\sigma_{i}}}.

(4.11)

Thus, given (
4.9
)–(
4.11
), to compute
F(n)
it remains to understand the numbers
F_{1}(n)
, and we now show that

F_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $n=1$}\\
2&\text{if $n=2$}\\
n(2^{n-1}-2)&\text{if $n\geq 3$.}\end{cases}

(4.12)

The
n\leq 2
cases are easily dealt with, so we now assume that
n\geq 3
.
Clearly
F_{1}(n)
is equal to
n
times the number of maximal null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
whose zero is
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf n}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, the constant map with image
\{1\}
, so it is enough to show that there are
2^{n-1}-2
such subsemigroups. As above, such a subsemigroup has the form
N({\bf W},\zeta)
, where
{\bf W}=\{W\}
for some subset
\{1\}\subseteq W\subseteq{\bf n}
. For simplicity we denote this subsemigroup by

N(W)=\big\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{{\bf n}f\subseteq W,\ Wf=\{1\}}\big\}.

As extreme cases, we observe that
N({\bf n})=N(\{1\})=\{\zeta\}
is not maximal, as it is obviously contained in every other
N(W)
; recall that we are assuming
n\geq 3
. On the other hand, given distinct subsets
{\{1\}\subset W_{1},W_{2}\subset{\bf n}}
, the subsemigroups
N(W_{1})
and
N(W_{2})
are incomparable in the inclusion order. Indeed, if
{x\in W_{1}\setminus W_{2}}
, then for any
y\in{\bf n}\setminus W_{1}
and
z\in W_{2}\setminus\{1\}
we have

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y\ &{\bf n}\setminus\{y\}\\
x\ &1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in N(W_{1})\setminus N(W_{2})\and\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\ &{\bf n}\setminus\{x\}\\
z\ &1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in N(W_{2})\setminus N(W_{1}).

(4.13)

It follows from all of this that the maximal null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
with zero
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf n}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
are precisely those of the form
N(W)
with
\{1\}\subset W\subset{\bf n}
. Since there are of course
2^{n-1}-2
of these, this completes the proof of (
4.12
).

Calculating the numbers
F(n)
using (
4.9
)–(
4.12
) requires the enumeration of all integer partitions of
n
, which is computationally challenging. However,
F(n)
may be computed more efficiently using a simple recurrence, as we now explain. Observe that we can also specify a maximal null subsemigroup
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
in the following way:

•

Choose the kernel-class containing
1
of the zero of
S
. Denote this class by
Z_{1}
.

•

Choose a maximal null subsemigroup
S_{1}
of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{1}}
whose zero has rank
1
.

•

Choose a maximal null subsemigroup
S_{2}
of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bf n}\setminus Z_{1}}
(with zero of any rank).

The semigroup so-determined is then
S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
. If
|Z_{1}|=k
, then we can perform the above steps in
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
,
F_{1}(k)
and
F(n-k)
ways, respectively. This then leads to the recurrence:

F(0)=1\and F(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}F_{1}(k)F(n-k)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for $n\geq 1$,}

(4.14)

with the numbers
F_{1}(k)
given in (
4.12
).

4.3
Nilpotent semigroups

The construction of the null semigroups
N({\bf W},\zeta)
in Subsection
4.1
can be generalised to nilpotent semigroups. Recall that a semigroup
S
is
nilpotent
if for some integer
k\geq 1
we have
S^{k}=\{z\}
for some fixed element
z\in S
, which is necessarily a zero element of
S
; if
k
is minimal with respect to this property, we say that
S
is
k
-nilpotent
. So
2
-nilpotent semigroups are null. Here we give the details for
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
; the construction for
k\geq 4
is easily adapted, though the enumeration becomes more complicated.

To describe
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
with zero
\zeta=\binom{Z_{i}}{z_{i}}_{i\in I}
, we require two systems
{{\bf V}=\{{V_{i}}:{i\in I}\}}
and
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
such that
z_{i}\in V_{i}\subseteq W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}
for all
i
. For such a pair of systems, it is easy to show that

N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)=\big\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{X}}:{Z_{i}f\subseteq W_{i},\ W_{i}f\subseteq V_{i},\ V_{i}f=\{z_{i}\}\text{ for all $i\in I$}}\big\}

is a
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
with zero
\zeta
. Strictly speaking, to ensure that
N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)
is indeed
3
-nilpotent, and not degenerately
2
-nilpotent (i.e., null), we must have
strict
inclusions
\{z_{i}\}\subset V_{i}\subset W_{i}\subset Z_{i}
for at least one
i\in I
. In particular, this forces
|X|\geq 4
.
Conversely, any
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup
S
with zero
\zeta
is contained in some such
N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)
. Indeed, given
S
we define

W_{i}=\bigcup_{f\in S}Z_{i}f\and V_{i}=\bigcup_{f\in S}W_{i}f\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for each $i\in I$.}

The size of
N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)
, as defined above, is given by

|N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)|=\prod_{i\in I}|W_{i}|^{|Z_{i}\setminus W_{i}|}|V_{i}|^{|W_{i}\setminus V_{i}|}.

In the finite case, say
|X|=n
, writing
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{r}\\
z_{1}&\cdots&z_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
|Z_{i}|=s_{i}
,
|W_{i}|=t_{i}
and
|V_{i}|=u_{i}
, we have three compositions
\upsilon=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
,
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
and
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
, satisfying
\upsilon\preceq\tau\preceq\sigma\vDash n
. We then calculate

|N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)|=t_{1}^{s_{1}-t_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{s_{r}-t_{r}}\cdot u_{1}^{t_{1}-u_{1}}\cdots u_{r}^{t_{r}-u_{r}}=\eta(\sigma,\tau)\cdot\eta(\tau,\upsilon).

Now write
u=u_{1}+\cdots+u_{r}
and
t=t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r}
, and note that
n=s_{1}+\cdots+s_{r}
. For integers
1\leq i\leq p
and
1\leq j\leq q
we have
i^{p-i}j^{q-j}\leq(i+j)^{p-i}(i+j)^{q-j}=(i+j)^{(p+q)-(i+j)}
. It quickly follows that

|N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)|\leq t^{n-t}\cdot u^{t-u}.

Note that when
r=1
we have size
t^{n-t}\cdot u^{t-u}
, so the maximum is achieved at rank 1: i.e., a
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
of maximum size has a zero of rank
1
. Thus, if we write
\kappa(n)
for the maximum size of a
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, then we have

\kappa(n)=\max\{{t^{n-t}\cdot u^{t-u}}:{1\leq u\leq t\leq n}\}.

For fixed
1\leq t\leq n
, note that
t^{n-t}\cdot u^{t-u}
is maximised (for
1\leq u\leq t
) when
u=\alpha(t)
, and then
u^{t-u}=\xi(t)
; the functions
\alpha
and
\xi
were defined in (
2.3
). We have therefore proved the following:

Theorem 4.15
.

For an integer
n\geq 4
, the maximum size of a
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is equal to

\kappa(n)=\max\{{t^{n-t}\cdot\xi(t)}:{t\in{\bf n}}\}.

4.4
Maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}

As with null semigroups in Subsection
4.2
, it is possible to calculate the number of maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, though the calculation is a little more delicate. Here, by a maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup we mean a
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup that is not properly contained in another
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup. To this end, we write

•

G(n)
for the number of maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and

•

G_{1}(n)
for the number of such subsemigroups whose zero has rank
1
.

For reasons that will become clear shortly, we also need to define some further sequences. Specifically, we also write

•

H(n)
for the number of null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
that are not properly contained in any null or
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup, and

•

H_{1}(n)
for the number of such subsemigroups whose zero has rank
1
.

Now consider some maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. So this must have the form
{S=N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)}
, as described in the previous subsection, and we write

\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{r}\\
z_{1}&\cdots&z_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt{{\bf W}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\}}\and{\bf V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}\},

where

\{z_{i}\}\subseteq V_{i}\subseteq W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for each $i$.}

(4.16)

Again, the inclusions in (
4.16
) do not all have to be strict, but to ensure that
S
is
3
-nilpotent (and not null) there must be at least one
i
such that
\{z_{i}\}\subset V_{i}\subset W_{i}\subset Z_{i}
. For each
i\in{\bf r}
, let
\zeta_{i}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{i}\\
z_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
,
{\bf W}_{i}=\{W_{i}\}
and
{\bf V}_{i}=\{V_{i}\}
, so that

S=\bigoplus_{i\in{\bf r}}S_{i}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where each}\hskip 20.00003ptS_{i}=N({\bf V}_{i},{\bf W}_{i},\zeta_{i}).

Moreover, since
S
is maximal
3
-nilpotent, each
S_{i}=N({\bf V}_{i},{\bf W}_{i},\zeta_{i})
is either:

•

a maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
, or else

•

a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{i}}
that is not properly contained in any null or
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup,

depending on the strictness of the inclusions in (
4.16
) for that particular
i\in{\bf r}
. In either case, the zero
\zeta_{i}
of each factor
S_{i}
has rank
1
. One can then follow either of the two methods in Subsection
4.2
to calculate the number
G(n)
of maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

We just give the details for the second approach, and obtain a recurrence for
G(n)
.
The basis of the recursion is the observation that a maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup
S
can be specified in the following way:

(i)

Choose the kernel-class containing
1
of the zero of
S
. Denote this class by
Z_{1}
.

(ii)

Choose a subsemigroup
S_{1}
of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{1}}
whose zero has rank
1
, and is either

•

maximal
3
-nilpotent, or else

•

null, but not properly contained in any null or
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{Z_{1}}
.

(iii)

Choose a subsemigroup
S_{2}
of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bf n}\setminus Z_{1}}
(with zero of any rank) that is either

•

maximal
3
-nilpotent, or else

•

null, but not properly contained in any null or
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{{\bf n}\setminus Z_{1}}
,

ensuring that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are not both null.

The semigroup so-determined is then
S=S_{1}\oplus S_{2}
. If
|Z_{1}|=k
, then we can perform step
(i)
in
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
ways. If
S_{1}
is null, then there are
H_{1}(k)
and
G(n-k)
ways to perform steps
(ii)
and
(iii)
, respectively. Otherwise,
S_{1}
is
3
-nilpotent, and there are
G_{1}(k)
and
G(n-k)+H(n-k)
ways. This then leads to the recurrence:

\displaystyle G(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(4.17)

\displaystyle G(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\Big(H_{1}(k)G(n-k)+G_{1}(k)\big(G(n-k)+H(n-k)\big)\Big)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $n\geq 1$.}

(4.18)

In order to implement this, it remains to understand the numbers
G_{1}(n)
,
H_{1}(n)
and
H(n)
. Formulae/recursions for these are given below in (
4.19
)–(
4.21
).

We begin with the
H
-numbers, and we first claim that

H_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}n!&\text{if $1\leq n\leq 3$}\\
0&\text{if $n\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

(4.19)

Indeed, for
n\leq 3
we have
H_{1}(n)=F_{1}(n)
, as there are no (properly)
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
for
n\leq 3
. We now assume that
n\geq 4
. Aiming for a contradiction, suppose
S
is a null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, with zero of rank
1
, that is not properly contained in any null or
3
-nilpotent subsemigroup. By the former,
S
is a maximal null subsemigroup. By symmetry, we may assume the zero of
S
is
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf n}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, so that
S=N({\bf W},\zeta)
, where
{\bf W}=\{W\}
for some
\{1\}\subset W\subset{\bf n}
. Since
n\geq 4
, we either have
|W|\geq 3
or else
|W|\leq n-2
. But in these cases, respectively, we have:

•

S=N({\bf W},\zeta)\subset N({\bf W}^{\prime},{\bf W},\zeta)
, where
{\bf W}^{\prime}=\big\{W\setminus\{x\}\big\}
for any
x\in W\setminus\{1\}
.

•

S=N({\bf W},\zeta)\subset N({\bf W},{\bf W}^{\prime\prime},\zeta)
, where
{\bf W}^{\prime\prime}=\big\{W\cup\{y\}\big\}
for any
y\in{\bf n}\setminus W
.

Since
N({\bf W}^{\prime},{\bf W},\zeta)
and
N({\bf W},{\bf W}^{\prime\prime},\zeta)
are both
3
-nilpotent, we have reached the desired contradiction, and this completes the proof of (
4.19
).

As in Subsection
4.2
, it is then easy to see that

H(0)=1\and H(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n-1}{k-1}H_{1}(k)H(n-k)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for $n\geq 1$.}

(4.20)

Keeping (
4.19
) in mind, we note that only the
k\leq 3
terms in the above sum are non-zero.

As for the
G
-numbers, we claim that

G_{1}(n)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $1\leq n\leq 3$}\\
3n(1+3^{n-2}-2^{n-1})&\text{if $n\geq 4$.}\end{cases}

(4.21)

As usual, this is clear for
n\leq 3
, so we assume that
n\geq 4
. And again,
G_{1}(n)
is equal to
n
times the number of maximal
3
-nilpotent subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
whose zero is
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf n}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. So consider some such subsemigroup
S
, which has the form

S=\big\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{{\bf n}f\subseteq W,\ Wf\subseteq V,\ Vf=\{1\}}\big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $\{1\}\subset V\subset W\subset{\bf n}$.}

(4.22)

Here,
S=N({\bf V},{\bf W},\zeta)
, for
{\bf W}=\{W\}
and
{\bf V}=\{V\}
, and we abbreviate this to
S=N(V,W)
. This time,
{S_{1}=N(V_{1},W_{1})}
and
{S_{2}=N(V_{2},W_{2})}
are incomparable if
(V_{1},W_{1})\not=(V_{2},W_{2})
. (If
V_{1}\not=V_{2}
or
W_{1}\not=W_{2}
, then as in (
4.13
), it is easy to construct transformations belonging to
S_{1}\setminus S_{2}
and
S_{2}\setminus S_{1}
.) Thus,
G_{1}(n)/n
is equal to the number of pairs
(V,W)
, as in (
4.22
). Specifying such a pair is equivalent to specifying the ordered partition
\big(V\setminus\{1\},W\setminus V,{\bf n}\setminus W)
of
{\bf n}\setminus\{1\}
. It follows that
G_{1}(n)/n=3!\cdot S(n-1,3)
, where
S(k,l)
is a Stirling number of the second kind. It is well known (and easy to verify using the standard Stirling recurrence) that
S(k,3)=(1+3^{k-1}-2^{k})/2
for
k\geq 3
, so (
4.21
) quickly follows.

4.5
Right null semigroups

When studying variants in Section
5
, an important role will be played by certain special kinds of semigroups we call
right null semigroups
.

Recall that a
band
is a semigroup consisting entirely of idempotents. Following Clifford
[
7
]
, a semigroup
S
is a
band of semigroups
if we have
S=\bigcup_{b\in B}S_{b}
, for some band
B
, where the
S_{b}
are pairwise-disjoint subsemigroups of
S
, and
S_{b}S_{c}\subseteq S_{bc}
for all
b,c\in B
.
Of particular interest for us is the case in which:

•

B
is a right zero semigroup (
bc=c
for all
b,c\in B
),

•

each
S_{b}
is a null semigroup, say with zero
z_{b}\in S_{b}
, and

•

S_{b}S_{c}=\{z_{c}\}
for all
b,c\in B
.

We will call such a semigroup
S=\bigcup_{b\in B}S_{b}
a
right null semigroup
. When
|B|=1
,
S
is a null semigroup; when each
|S_{b}|=1
,
S
is a right zero semigroup; so right null semigroups simultaneously generalise both classes.

For a right null semigroup
S
as above, the idempotents
E(S)={\{{z_{b}}:{b\in B}\}}
form a right zero subsemigroup of
S
isomorphic to
B
; so we will typically identify
E(S)
with
B
. We call
S

uniform
if the
S_{b}
have a common size. If
|B|=p
and
|S_{b}|=q
for all
b\in B
, we call
S
a
p\times q
(uniform) right null semigroup, and then of course
|S|=pq
. In fact, it is easy to show that a
p\times q
right null semigroup is simply a direct product of a
p
-element right zero semigroup and a
q
-element null semigroup.

In order to understand the right null subsemigroups of a full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we make use of the following natural extension of the
\zeta
-systems, and associated null semigroups, from Definition
4.1
.

Definition 4.23
.

Given a right zero subsemigroup
B
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, a
B
-system
is a collection
{\bf W}
of sets such that
{\bf W}
is simultaneously a
\zeta
-system for all
\zeta\in B
. For such a
B
-system
{\bf W}
, we define

N({\bf W},B)=\bigcup_{\zeta\in B}N({\bf W},\zeta).

Lemma 4.24
.

Given a right zero subsemigroup
B
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, a subset
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
is a right null semigroup with
E(S)=B
if and only if
{B\subseteq S\subseteq N({\bf W},B)}
for some
B
-system
{\bf W}
.

Proof.

The proof is easily adapted from that of Lemma
4.2
. For the forwards implication we still define
W_{i}=\bigcup_{f\in S}Z_{i}f
.
∎

Of course we have
|N({\bf W},B)|=|B|\cdot|N({\bf W},\zeta)|
for any
\zeta\in B
. Moreover, given a right zero subsemigroup
B\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{X}
, one could find the maximum size of a right null subsemigroup with idempotents
B
, by choosing
{\bf W}
so that each
N({\bf W},\zeta)
,
\zeta\in B
, is as large as possible.

On the other hand, given a partition
{\bf Z}=\{{Z_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
of
X
, and a system
{\bf W}=\{{W_{i}}:{i\in I}\}
with
\varnothing\not=W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}
for all
i\in I
, we can construct a maximum band
B=B({\bf Z},{\bf W})
such that
{\bf W}
is a
B
-system. Specifically, we take
B
to consist of all idempotents of the form
\zeta=\binom{Z_{i}}{w_{i}}_{i\in I}
for all choices of
w_{i}\in W_{i}
(
i\in I
). This
B
has size
\prod_{i\in I}|W_{i}|
.

When
X
is infinite, it is easy to describe
2^{|X|}\times 2^{|X|}
uniform right null subsemigroups of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
.

The next result gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{m}
to contain a right null semigroup of specified dimensions. The statement uses compositions and their associated parameters, as defined in Subsection
2.2
.

Lemma 4.25
.

For
m,p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
contains a
p\times q
uniform right null subsemigroup if and only if there exist compositions
\sigma
and
\tau
such that

\tau\preceq\sigma\vDash m,\hskip 20.00003pt\textstyle{\prod\tau\geq p},\hskip 20.00003pt\eta(\sigma,\tau)\geq q.

(4.26)

Proof.

By Lemma
4.24
,
\mathcal{T}_{m}
contains such a subsemigroup if and only it contains a subsemigroup of the form
N({\bf W},B)
for

•

some right zero subsemigroup
B\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{m}
with
|B|\geq p
, and

•

some
B
-system
{\bf W}
, such that
|N({\bf W},\zeta)|\geq q
for all
\zeta\in B
.

Consider some such
B
and
{\bf W}
. Suppose the common kernel-classes of the elements of
B
are
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}
, and write
{\bf W}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\}
, where
W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}
for each
i
. Also, write
s_{i}=|Z_{i}|
and
t_{i}=|W_{i}|
for all
i
. Clearly the compositions
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
and
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
satisfy
\tau\preceq\sigma\vDash m
. Moreover, since every
\zeta\in B
has the form
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}Z_{1}&\cdots&Z_{r}\\
w_{1}&\cdots&w_{r}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some
w_{i}\in W_{i}
, we have

p\leq|B|\leq t_{1}\cdots t_{r}=\textstyle{\prod\tau}\and q\leq|N({\bf W},\zeta)|=t_{1}^{s_{1}-t_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{s_{r}-t_{r}}=\eta(\sigma,\tau).

Conversely, suppose the compositions
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
and
\tau=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
satisfy conditions (
4.26
). We construct a (uniform)
\prod\tau\times\eta(\sigma,\tau)
right null subsemigroup
S
of
\mathcal{T}_{m}
as follows, and then
S
then of course contains a
p\times q
right null subsemigroup. Choose a partition
{{\bf Z}=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r}\}}
of
{\bf m}
, and a system
{\bf W}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\}
such that
W_{i}\subseteq Z_{i}
,
|Z_{i}|=s_{i}
and
|W_{i}|=t_{i}
for each
i
. We define
S=N({\bf W},B)
, as in Definition
4.23
, for the band
B=B({\bf Z},{\bf W})
described after the proof of Lemma
4.24
.
∎

It will transpire in Section
5
that we need to know the degree of a certain special kind of right null semigroup. Since all
p\times q
right null semigroups are isomorphic, we will write
\varrho(p,q)
for the degree of any such semigroup.

Theorem 4.27
.

(i)

For integers
p,q\geq 1
we have

\varrho(p,1)=\min\{{n}:{\pi(n)\geq p}\}\and\varrho(1,q)=\min\{{n}:{\xi(n)\geq q}\}.

(ii)

For integers
p,q\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\varrho(p,q)

\displaystyle=\min\{{2A+3B+C+D}:{0\leq A\leq 2,\ B,D\geq 0,\ C\geq 2,\ 2^{A}3^{B}C\geq p,\ C^{D}\geq q}\}

\displaystyle={\min}\big\{{2A+3\lceil\log_{3}(p/2^{A}C)\rceil+C+\lceil\log_{C}q\rceil}:{0\leq A\leq 2\leq C\leq\varrho(p,1)+\varrho(1,q)}\big\},

where the parameters
A,B,C,D
in the above sets are all integers.

Proof.

(i)
. This follows from Theorems
3.1
(ii)
and
4.7
, given that
p\times 1
and
1\times q
right null semigroups are right zero or null semigroups, respectively.

(ii)
. We now assume that
p,q\geq 2
. By Lemma
4.25
, the number
\varrho(p,q)
is the solution to the following optimisation problem:

Problem 1
.

Minimise
s_{1}+\cdots+s_{r}
, for all
r,s_{1},\ldots,s_{r},t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
, subject to:

t_{i}\leq s_{i}\text{ for all $i$},\hskip 20.00003ptt_{1}\cdots t_{r}\geq p,\hskip 20.00003ptt_{1}^{s_{1}-t_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{s_{r}-t_{r}}\geq q.

In the remainder of the proof, we reformulate Problem
1
until we arrive at the desired result.
Writing
d_{i}=s_{i}-t_{i}
in the above, Problem
1
is equivalent to the following:

Problem 2
.

Minimise
(t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r})+(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r})
, for all
r,t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\in\mathbb{N}
and
{d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
, subject to:

t_{1}\cdots t_{r}\geq p\and t_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{d_{r}}\geq q.

In the above, some of the
d_{i}
can be
0
, but not all (as
q\geq 2
). For a given sum
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}=D
, we have
t_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots t_{r}^{d_{r}}\leq\max(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})^{D}
, so Problem
2
is equivalent to:

Problem 3
.

Minimise
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r}+D
, for all
r,t_{1},\ldots,t_{r},D\in\mathbb{N}
, subject to:

t_{1}\cdots t_{r}\geq p\and\max(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})^{D}\geq q.

Adding an ordering constraint
t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{r}
of course does not change the solution to Problem
3
. Additionally writing
C=t_{r}=\max(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
, we can simplify the statement a little:

Problem 4
.

Minimise
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r-1}+C+D
, for all
r,t_{1},\ldots,t_{r-1},C,D\in\mathbb{N}
, subject to:

t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{r-1}\leq C,\hskip 20.00003ptt_{1}\cdots t_{r-1}C\geq p,\hskip 20.00003ptC^{D}\geq q.

Any solution to Problem
4
has
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r-1},C\geq 2
. Indeed,
C\geq 2
follows from
C^{D}\geq q\geq 2
, while if
r\geq 2
and
t_{1}=1
, then
t_{1}
could be removed from the sum
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r-1}+C+D
without decreasing the product
t_{1}\cdots t_{r-1}C
.

Also, for a given sum
t_{1}+\cdots+t_{r-1}
, the maximum value of
t_{1}\cdots t_{r-1}
occurs when at most two of the
t_{i}
are
2
, and the rest are
3
. Problem
4
is then equivalent to:

Problem 5
.

Minimise
2A+3B+C+D
, for integers

0\leq A\leq 2,\hskip 20.00003ptB,D\geq 0,\hskip 20.00003ptC\geq 2,

subject to
2^{A}3^{B}C\geq p
and
C^{D}\geq q
.

This gives the first claimed expression for
\varrho(p,q)
, and we now work towards establishing the second. To do so, suppose we have some solution
(A,B,C,D)
to Problem
5
, so that
{2A+3B+C+D=\mu(S)}
, where
S
is some
p\times q
right null semigroup. Since
{C^{D}\geq q}
we must have
D\geq\log_{C}q
, and so minimality forces
D=\lceil\log_{C}q\rceil
. We similarly deduce from
2^{A}3^{B}C\geq p
that
B=\lceil\log_{3}(p/2^{A}C)\rceil
.

It remains to show that
C
is bounded above by
{\varrho(p,1)+\varrho(1,q)}
. To see this, note first that
C\leq 2A+3B+C+D=\mu(S)
. But
S\cong R\times N
for some
p
-element right zero semigroup
R
and some
q
-element null semigroup
N
, so
C\leq\mu(S)\leq\mu(R)+\mu(N)=\varrho(p,1)+\varrho(1,q)
, as required.
∎

(ii)
reduces the calculation of
\varrho(p,q)
to three separate (finite) one-variable minimisations, one for each value of
A=0,1,2
. It is then easy to compute the numbers
\varrho(p,q)
; some values are given in Table

. We also observe (from the proof) that the upper bound on
C
of
\varrho(p,1)+\varrho(1,q)
could be replaced by any other
a priori

\varrho(p,q)
.

It is interesting to compare Table

\beta(p,q)
of a
p\times q
rectangular band. Indeed, consider a
p\times q
rectangular band
B
and a
q\times p
right null semigroup
S
(note the swapping of the parameters for
S
). Then we have

B\cong L\times R\and S\cong R\times N

for some left zero semigroup
L
, right zero semigroup
R
and null semigroup

, with
{|L|=|N|=p}
and
|R|=q

|L|=|N|

\mu(L)=\mu(N)

\mu(B)=\mu(S)

\beta(2,2)=4

\varrho(2,2)=3

\mu(T\times U)\not=\mu(T\times V)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{despite having}\hskip 20.00003pt\mu(U)=\mu(V).

Other examples of this phenomenon are not hard to find, and some can be constructed using other results from this paper. For example, let
B

2\times 2
rectangular band, and let

\mu(L)=\mu(G)=2
. Consulting Table

\mu(B)=4=\beta_{2}(2,2)
. It then follows from Theorem
3.12

\mu(B\times G)=4
as well. On the other hand,
B\times L
is a
4\times 2
rectangular band, and so
\mu(B\times L)=5
, again by Table
2
.

Remark 4.29
.

The above results also lead to a formula for the degree of an arbitrary finite right null semigroup. Specifically, if
S=\bigcup_{b\in B}S_{b}
is some such semigroup, then

\mu(S)=\varrho(p,q)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003ptp=|B|\text{ and }q={\max}\big\{{|S_{b}|}:{b\in B}\big\}.

Indeed, it follows from Lemma
4.24
that if
S
embeds in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
then
S
is contained in some uniform right null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, which of course contains a
p\times q
right null semigroup.

Remark 4.30
.

Of course one could also consider

4.6
Decreasing the degree by forming a variant

Before we move on, we pause to address another question asked in
[
19
]
.

Consider a finite semigroup
S
, and an arbitrary element
a\in S
. It is natural to wonder how the degrees of
S
and the variant
S^{a}
are related. Theorem
1.1
leads to the upper bound
{\mu(S^{a})<2\mu(S)}
. Indeed, if
n=\mu(S)
, then any embedding
\phi:S\to\mathcal{T}_{n}
yields an embedding
{S^{a}\to\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a\phi}}
, which we can follow with an embedding
{\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a\phi}\to\mathcal{T}_{2n-r}}
, where
r=\operatorname{rank}(a\phi)
.

Question 4.4 of
[
19
]
asks if there exists a finite semigroup
S
such that
\mu(S^{a})<\mu(S)
for some
a\in S
. This can be quickly answered in the affirmative, and we do so with two examples. The first involves cyclic groups, so we begin with the following basic result. It is most likely well known, but proofs are provided for convenience.

Lemma 4.31
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
\phi:G\to\mathcal{T}_{n}
be an embedding, where
n=\mu(G)
.

(i)

The image
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
is contained in the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

(ii)

For every
i\in{\bf n}
there exists
g\in G
such that
i(g\phi)\not=i
.

(iii)

If
S=G\cup\{0\}
is the semigroup obtained by adjoining a zero element to
G
, then
{\mu(S)=\mu(G)+1}
.

Proof.

(i)
. Clearly
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
is contained in some group
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class
H
of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and
H
is contained in some
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class
D_{r}
with
1\leq r\leq n
. But
H\cong\mathcal{S}_{r}
, so
G
embeds in
\mathcal{S}_{r}
, and hence in
\mathcal{T}_{r}
. Thus, by minimality of
n=\mu(G)
we have
n\leq r
, and so
r=n
. Thus,
H=\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

(ii)
. If not, then we may assume by symmetry that
n(g\phi)=n
for all
g\in G
. Since
\operatorname{im}(\phi)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n}
by
(i)
, it follows that
G
embeds in
\mathcal{S}_{n-1}
, contradicting
n=\mu(G)
.

(iii)
. Let
\zeta\in\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
be the constant map with image
\{n+1\}
. Then
\psi:S\to\mathcal{T}_{n+1}
, defined by
0\psi=\zeta
and
g\psi=g\phi\oplus\mathrm{id}_{\{n+1\}}
for
g\in G
, is an embedding, so that
\mu(S)\leq n+1
.

To show that
\mu(S)\geq n+1
, suppose to the contrary that there is an embedding
\theta:S\to\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Let
\zeta=0\theta
, and let
i\in\operatorname{im}(\zeta)
be arbitrary, so
i=i\zeta
. By
(ii)
there exists
g\in G
such that
i(g\theta)\not=i
, so it follows that
i=i\zeta=i(0\theta)=i(0\theta)(g\theta)=i(g\theta)\not=i
, a contradiction.
∎

Example 4.32
.

Let
G
be a cyclic group of prime order
p
, and let
S=G\cup\{0\}
be the semigroup obtained by adjoining a zero element to
G
. It is well known
[
38
]
that
\mu(G)=p
, so it follows from Lemma
4.31
that
\mu(S)=p+1
.
On the other hand, the variant
S^{0}
(with sandwich element
0
) is a null semigroup of size
p+1
. Thus, for
p\geq 5
we have
\mu(S^{0})<p+1=\mu(S)
, as follows quickly from Theorem
4.7
; cf. Table
1
.

In fact, the rapid growth of the
\xi
function means that the ratio
\mu(S)/\mu(S^{0})
can be made arbitrarily large by choosing
p
large enough. For example, again consulting Table
1
, if
p
has
20
digits, then so too does
\mu(S)=p+1
, while
\mu(S^{0})=30
. By contrast, we have
\mu(S^{a})/\mu(S)<2
for any semigroup
S
and any
a\in S
, as noted earlier.

We can also use null and nilpotent semigroups to provide an example of a semigroup whose degree is strictly greater than the degrees of
all
its variants.

Example 4.33
.

Consider the
3
-nilpotent semigroup
S=\{x,y,z,0\}
for which the only non-zero product is
x^{2}=y
:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccc}\cdot&0&x&y&z\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&0&0\\
x&0&y&0&0\\
y&0&0&0&0\\
z&0&0&0&0\\
\end{array}

We claim that:

(i)

\mu(S)=5
, but

(ii)

\mu(S^{a})=4
for all
a\in S
.

We first note that
(ii)
is clear, given the fact that any variant of a
3
-nilpotent semigroup is null, and that
\xi(3)=2
and
\xi(4)=4
; cf. Theorem
4.7
and Table
1
.

For
(i)
, we have
\mu(S)\leq 5
because we have an embedding
S\to\mathcal{T}_{5}
given by

x\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&1&1&2&4\end{smallmatrix}\right),\hskip 20.00003pty\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&1&1&1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right),\hskip 20.00003ptz\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&1&2&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\hskip 20.00003pt0\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4&5\\
1&1&1&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right).

(If we denote these transformations by
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
,
z^{\prime}
and
0^{\prime}
, respectively, then the only product not equal to
0^{\prime}
is
x^{\prime}\circ x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
.)

To show that
\mu(S)\geq 5
, we must show that
\mathcal{T}_{4}
contains no subsemigroup isomorphic to
S
. To do so, and aiming for a contradiction, suppose
\mathcal{T}_{4}
does contain such a subsemigroup
T\cong S
. Since
\{y,z,0\}
is a null subsemigroup of
S
, it follows that
T
contains a null subsemigroup of size
3
. For
2\leq r\leq 4
, the biggest null subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{4}
with a zero of rank
r
has size
\nu_{r}(4)\leq\nu_{2}(4)=\xi(3)=2
; cf. Theorem
4.4
. It follows that the zero
\zeta
of
T
has rank
1
. By symmetry, we may assume that
\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\bf 4}\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. As in Subsection
4.4
, it follows that
T
is contained in the subsemigroup

N=N(V,W)=\big\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{4}}:{{\bf 4}f\subseteq W,\ Wf\subseteq V,\ Vf=\{1\}}\big\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for some $\{1\}\subset V\subset W\subset{\bf 4}$.}

Again, by symmetry we can assume that
V={\bf 2}
and
W={\bf 3}
. Denoting the elements of
N
by

\zeta=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \ \ a=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \ \ b=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \ \ c=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&2&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \ \ d=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&2&2\end{smallmatrix}\right),\ \ \ e=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3&4\\
1&1&2&3\end{smallmatrix}\right),

the multiplication table for
N
is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c|cccccc}\cdot&\zeta&a&b&c&d&e\\
\hline\cr\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta\\
a&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta\\
b&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&a&a&a\\
c&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta\\
d&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta\\
e&\zeta&\zeta&\zeta&a&a&a\\
\end{array}

Since
S
contains an element with non-zero square, it follows that
e\in T
. Since all other products in
T
must equal
\zeta
, it follows from the last row of the table that
T=\{\zeta,a,b,e\}
. But this
T
has precisely two non-zero products,
b\circ e=e\circ e=a\not=\zeta
. This is the desired contradiction

5
The degree of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}

We now turn to variants of finite full transformation semigroups. Recall that for a fixed transformation
a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, the variant
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
is the semigroup with underlying set
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and operation
\star
defined by
g\star h=gah
for all
g,h\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Recall from (
1.2
) that when
\operatorname{rank}(a)=r
, we have

n\leq\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-r.

The main guiding theme of the current section is Question 4.2 of
[
19
]
, which asks whether the upper bound of
2n-r
is in fact the exact value of
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
.

We begin in Subsection
5.1
with some general results showing that any embedding of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
in
\mathcal{T}_{2n-r-1}
(which would ‘break’ the upper bound of
2n-r
) is rather restricted. We then apply these results in Subsection
5.2
to show that indeed
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})=2n-r
when
r
is suitably large (
r\geq n-6
); see Theorem
5.15
.

Subsection
5.3
then considers the other extreme case of
r=1
, where
a
is a constant map. Since
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
does not depend on the actual choice of the constant map
a
, we denote it by
\mu(n)
, and the above bounds become
n\leq\mu(n)\leq 2n-1
. By realising
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
as an
n\times n^{n-1}
right null semigroup (for
{\operatorname{rank}(a)=1}
), we apply Theorem
4.27
to give a formula for
\mu(n)
in Proposition
5.19
. We then show in Proposition
5.23
that it is indeed possible to break the upper bound of
2n-1
, and in Theorem
5.24
that the sequence
\mu(n)
is strictly increasing in
n
. Theorems
5.27
and
5.28
, concern the asymptotic behaviour of
\mu(n)
, showing in particular that the ratio
\mu(n)/n
tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
, while the difference
\mu(n)-n
grows without bound. We state some open problems in Subsection
5.4
.

5.1
General results

For the duration of this subsection, we fix an integer
n\geq 2
, and a transformation
a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
\operatorname{rank}(a)=r
. Since we wish to study the variant
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
, and since
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}\cong\mathcal{T}_{n}^{gah}
for any permutations
g,h\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
(see
[
26
, Proposition 13.1.3]
), we may assume without loss of generality that
a
is an idempotent with
\operatorname{im}(a)={\bf r}
.

As noted above, we have
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})\leq 2n-r
, and here we are interested in the question of whether we could in fact have strict inequality:
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})<2n-r
. If we do, then
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
can be embedded in
\mathcal{T}_{2n-r-1}
. For the duration of this subsection, we write
m=2n-r-1
for simplicity, and we hypothesise the existence of an embedding

\phi:\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}\to\mathcal{T}_{m}.

Since
\phi
obviously cannot exist for
r=n
(since then
m=n-1
), we assume that
r<n
in all that follows.
We will write
f^{\prime}=f\phi
for all
f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, noting that
(f\star g)^{\prime}=f^{\prime}g^{\prime}
for all
f,g\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. If
S\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{n}
, we write
S^{\prime}=S\phi=\{{f^{\prime}}:{f\in S}\}
.

We will occasionally need to refer to Green’s relations on the semigroups
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
and
\mathcal{T}_{m}
; those on the former will be denoted
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}^{a}
,
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}^{a}
, and so on, to distinguish them from those on the latter, which will be denoted in the usual way.

Consider the set

S=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{f|_{{\bf r}}=\mathrm{id}_{{\bf r}}}\}=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{af=a}\},

which is easily seen to be a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
of size
n^{n-r}
. Consequently,
S^{\prime}=S\phi
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{m}
of the same size. Also define

F=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\operatorname{im}(f^{\prime})\and G=\bigcup_{f\in S}\operatorname{im}(f^{\prime}),

noting that
G\subseteq F\subseteq{\bf m}
. The following is true by definition:

Lemma 5.1
.

The image
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
is contained in the subsemigroup

\mathcal{T}_{m}(F)=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{m}}:{\operatorname{im}(f)\subseteq F}\}.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
f\in S
and all
x\in F
we have
xf^{\prime}=xa^{\prime}
. Consequently,
n^{n-r}\leq|G|^{m-|F|}
.

Proof.

Fix some
f\in S
and
x\in F
. By definition, we have
x=yg^{\prime}
for some
y\in{\bf m}
and
g\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Since
f\in S
we have
a=af
, so
g\star f=gaf=ga=gaa=g\star a
, which gives
g^{\prime}f^{\prime}=g^{\prime}a^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{T}_{m}
. But then
xf^{\prime}=yg^{\prime}f^{\prime}=yg^{\prime}a^{\prime}=xa^{\prime}
.

Now that we have proved the first assertion, it follows that the elements of
S^{\prime}
are only distinguished by their restriction to
{\bf m}\setminus F
. Every such restriction is a map
{\bf m}\setminus F\to G
, of which there are
|G|^{m-|F|}
. Since
|S^{\prime}|=n^{n-r}
, the second assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

We have
|F|\leq n-2
.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
|F|=n-1+k
where
k\geq 0
. Then by Lemma
5.2
and Corollary
2.2
, and since
G\subseteq F
, we have

n^{n-r}\leq|G|^{m-|F|}\leq|F|^{m-|F|}=(n-1+k)^{(2n-r-1)-(n-1+k)}=(n-1+k)^{n-r-k}\leq(n-1)^{n-r},

a contradiction (as
n-r\geq 1
).
∎

In what follows, a crucial role will be played by the constant maps from
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and their images in
\mathcal{T}_{m}
under
\phi
.
For
i\in{\bf n}
, write
e_{i}=\binom{{\bf n}}{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
for the constant map with image
\{i\}
. We will frequently make use of the fact that
e_{i}f=e_{if}
for all
i\in{\bf n}
and
f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
.

Since the
e_{i}
are all
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}^{a}
-related in
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
, the
e_{i}^{\prime}=e_{i}\phi
are all
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-related in
\mathcal{T}_{m}
, so they have a common kernel, the classes of which we will denote by
B_{1},\ldots,B_{q}
. Thus, we may write

e_{i}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}B_{1}&\cdots&B_{q}\\
x_{i1}&\cdots&x_{iq}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for $i\in{\bf n}$.}

Since each
e_{i}^{\prime}
is an idempotent, we have
x_{ij}\in B_{j}
for all
i\in{\bf n}
and
j\in{\bf q}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

We have
q\geq 2
.

Proof.

If we had
q=1
then
e_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{n}^{\prime}
would be distinct transformations of rank
1
, and so
|F|\geq|\operatorname{im}(e_{1}^{\prime})\cup\cdots\cup\operatorname{im}(e_{n}^{\prime})|=n
, contradicting Lemma
5.3
.
∎

The next statement uses the
\oplus
operation defined in Subsection
2.4
.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and any
i\in{\bf q}
, we have
B_{i}f^{\prime}\subseteq B_{i}
. Consequently,
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
is contained in
\mathcal{T}_{B_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{T}_{B_{q}}
.

Proof.

In
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
we have
f\star e_{1}=e_{1}
, so it follows that
f^{\prime}e_{1}^{\prime}=e_{1}^{\prime}
. The claim follows.
∎

For each
i\in{\bf q}
let
F_{i}=F\cap B_{i}
. The next result follows immediately from Lemmas
5.1
and
5.5
.

Corollary 5.6
.

The image
\operatorname{im}(\phi)
is contained in
\mathcal{T}_{B_{1}}(F_{1})\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{T}_{B_{q}}(F_{q})
. ∎

Next we wish to find a lower bound on the rank of
a^{\prime}
. For this, we require the following result concerning Green’s
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
-preorder, defined on a semigroup
S
by
x\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\ \Leftrightarrow\ x\in S^{1}yS^{1}
. In a full transformation semigroup
\mathcal{T}_{X}
, we have
f\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}g\ \Leftrightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}(f)\leq\operatorname{rank}(g)
. The proof uses the natural partial order on the idempotents
E(S)
defined by
e\leq f\ \Leftrightarrow\ e=ef=fe
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose we have an embedding
\psi:S\to T
, where
S
and
T
are finite regular semigroups, and where all elements of
S
are comparable in the
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
-preorder. Then for all
{x,y\in S}
, we have
x\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
in
S
if and only if
x\psi\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\psi
in
T
.

Proof.

Clearly
x\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\ \Rightarrow\ x\psi\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\psi
. For the converse, suppose
x\psi\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\psi
. By assumption we have either
x\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
or
x\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
. In the former case we are done, so suppose instead that
x\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
. This implies that
x\psi\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y\psi
, so in fact
x\psi\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y\psi
. Since
x\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
in the regular semigroup
S
, it follows from
[
30
, Theorem 1]
that there exist idempotents
e,f\in E(S)
such that
x\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}e\geq f\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y
. This implies that the idempotents
e\psi,f\psi\in E(T)
satisfy
e\psi\geq f\psi
, and also
e\psi\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}x\psi\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y\psi\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}f\psi
. As
T
is finite, the natural order is trivial on
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-classes (see for example
[
31
, Result 6]
), so that
e\psi=f\psi
, and thus
e=f
as
\psi
is injective. We thus have
x\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}e=f\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}y
. In particular,
x\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}y
, as required.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

We have
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})\geq q+r-1
.

Proof.

Consider the set
R=\operatorname{Reg}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
of all regular elements of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
. As shown in
[
12
]
,
R
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
, and is a chain of
{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}^{a}}={\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}^{a}}
-classes:
D_{1}^{a}<\cdots<D_{r}^{a}
. Fix some
f_{i}\in D_{i}^{a}
for each
{i\in{\bf r}}
, and assume that
f_{1}=e_{1}
and
f_{r}=a
. Then using Lemma
5.7
and the above-mentioned characterisation of the
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
-ordering in
\mathcal{T}_{m}
,

\displaystyle f_{1}<_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}\cdots<_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}f_{r}

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ f_{1}^{\prime}<_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}\cdots<_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}f_{r}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ q=\operatorname{rank}(f_{1}^{\prime})<\operatorname{rank}(f_{2}^{\prime})<\cdots<\operatorname{rank}(f_{r}^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime}).

The result is then immediate.
∎

In the case
r=1
,
a
is a constant map, so that
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})
attains the lower bound of
q+r-1=q
just established. We show below that the lower bound is never attained when
r\geq 2
; see Lemma
5.12
.

First, however, we take a closer look at the transformation
a^{\prime}=a\phi
.
For each
i\in{\bf q}
, denote the restriction
a^{\prime}|_{B_{i}}
by
a_{i}^{\prime}
, and write
r_{i}=\operatorname{rank}(a_{i}^{\prime})
. By Lemma
5.5
we have
a^{\prime}=a_{1}^{\prime}\oplus\cdots\oplus a_{q}^{\prime}
, with each
a_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{B_{i}}(F_{i})
.

Lemma 5.9
.

We have
r_{i}\geq r
for some
i\in{\bf q}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\phi
is an embedding of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
into
\mathcal{T}_{B_{1}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathcal{T}_{B_{q}}
. For
i\in{\bf q}
, let
\phi_{i}:\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}\to\mathcal{T}_{B_{i}}
be the result of composing
\phi
with the projection onto the
i
th coordinate. As in
[
14
, Section 4.3]
, the set
T=a\mathcal{T}_{n}a=a\star\mathcal{T}_{n}\star a
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}_{r}
. Consider the restrictions
\psi=\phi|_{T}
and
\psi_{i}=\phi_{i}|_{T}
for each
i\in{\bf q}
, noting that
\psi
is injective. If all the
\psi_{i}
were non-injective, then each
\ker(\psi_{i})
would contain the minimum non-trivial congruence on
T
(the congruences on
T\cong\mathcal{T}_{r}
form a chain
[
42
]
), but then so too would
\ker(\psi)=\ker(\psi_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\ker(\psi_{q})
, meaning that
\psi
is non-injective, a contradiction. It follows that some
\psi_{i}:T\to\mathcal{T}_{B_{i}}
is injective. If we write
p
for the minimum rank of an element in
\operatorname{im}(\psi_{i})
, then as in the proof of Lemma
5.8
, we have
r_{i}=\operatorname{rank}(a_{i}^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}(a_{i}\psi)\geq p+r-1\geq r
.
∎

Thus, without loss of generality we may assume that
r_{1}\geq r
. It follows as well that
|F_{1}|\geq r
(as
a_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{B_{1}}(F_{1})
and
\operatorname{rank}(a_{1}^{\prime})\geq r
).

Lemma 5.10
.

If
r\geq 2
, then
r_{i}\geq 2
for some
2\leq i\leq q
.

Proof.

Seeking a contradiction, suppose instead that
r_{2}=\cdots=r_{q}=1
. Then
B_{2},\ldots,B_{q}
are all
\ker(a^{\prime})
-classes. Let the
\ker(a_{1}^{\prime})
-classes be
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r_{1}}
.
Thus, we may write

a^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A_{1}&\cdots&A_{r_{1}}&B_{2}&\cdots&B_{q}\\
y_{1}&\cdots&y_{r_{1}}&z_{2}&\cdots&z_{q}\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where each
y_{i}\in A_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq r_{1}
) and
z_{j}\in B_{j}
(
2\leq j\leq q
), as
a^{\prime}
is an idempotent. Linking back to the
e_{i}^{\prime}
(
1\leq i\leq n
), we claim that

x_{ij}=z_{j}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $1\leq i\leq n$ and $2\leq j\leq q$.}

(5.11)

To prove this, fix some
1\leq i\leq n
and
2\leq j\leq q
.

Suppose first that
1\leq i\leq r
.
Since
a
is an idempotent with image
{\bf r}
we have
i=ia
, and so
e_{i}=e_{i}a=e_{i}aa=e_{i}\star a
. Thus,
e_{i}^{\prime}=e_{i}^{\prime}a^{\prime}
, and so
x_{ij}\in\operatorname{im}(e_{i}^{\prime})=\operatorname{im}(e_{i}^{\prime}a^{\prime})\subseteq\operatorname{im}(a^{\prime})
.
But
x_{ij}\in B_{j}
, and since
\operatorname{im}(a^{\prime})\cap B_{j}=\{z_{j}\}
it follows that
x_{ij}=z_{j}
.

Now suppose
r<i\leq n
. Put
k=ia
, and let
l\in{\bf r}\setminus\{k\}
be arbitrary (recall that
r\geq 2
). Choose any
f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
such that
kf=i
and
lf=l
. Since
k,l\in\operatorname{im}(a)
, and since
a
is an idempotent, we have
k=ka
and
l=la
. It follows that
e_{k}\star f=e_{i}
and
e_{l}\star f=e_{l}
, so that
e_{k}^{\prime}f^{\prime}=e_{i}^{\prime}
and
e_{l}^{\prime}f^{\prime}=e_{l}^{\prime}
. It follows from these, respectively, that
x_{kj}f^{\prime}=x_{ij}
and
x_{lj}f^{\prime}=x_{lj}
. But
k,l\leq r
, so by the previous paragraph we have
x_{kj}=z_{j}=x_{lj}
, and it follows that
x_{ij}=x_{kj}f^{\prime}=x_{lj}f^{\prime}=x_{lj}=z_{j}
. This completes the proof of (
5.11
).

It now follows that

e_{i}^{\prime}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}B_{1}&B_{2}&\cdots&B_{q}\\
x_{i1}&z_{2}&\cdots&z_{q}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $i\in{\bf n}$.}

Since the
e_{i}^{\prime}
are pairwise distinct, it follows that the set
\{x_{11},\ldots,x_{n1}\}
has size
n
. But this set is contained in
F
, and this contradicts Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

If
r\geq 2
, then
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})\geq q+r
.

Proof.

We have
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})\geq q+r-1
by Lemma
5.8
. If in fact
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})=q+r-1
, then from

q+r-1=\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})=r_{1}+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{q}\geq r+r_{2}+\cdots+r_{q},

it follows that
r_{2}+\cdots+r_{q}\leq q-1
, so that
r_{2}=\cdots=r_{q}=1
, contradicting Lemma
5.10
.
∎

The following is a simple consequence of Lemmas
5.4
and
5.12
:

Corollary 5.13
.

If
r\geq 2
, then
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})\geq r+2
. ∎

Now that we have gathered all the general results we need, we proceed to consider separate cases for ‘large’ and ‘small’
r
in the next two subsections.

5.2
The case
r\geq n-6

We now consider the case in which the rank
r
of the sandwich element
a
is suitably large, meaning specifically that
r\geq n-6
. We will shortly prove that for such large
r
, the degree
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
reaches its upper bound of
2n-r
. For this we need the following simple lemma.

Lemma 5.14
.

For any composition
\sigma=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})\vDash n
we have
\prod\sigma\geq n-r+1
.

Proof.

First note that for any
s\geq t\geq 2
we have
st\geq 2s>2s-1\geq s+t-1=(s+t-1)\cdot 1
. It quickly follows that
\prod\sigma\geq\prod\sigma^{\prime}
for the composition
\sigma^{\prime}=(n-r+1,1,\ldots,1)
.
∎

Theorem 5.15
.

If
a\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
r=\operatorname{rank}(a)\geq n-6
, then
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})=2n-r
.

Proof.

Again we write
m=2n-r-1
, and aiming for a contradiction, we assume there is an embedding
\phi:\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}\to\mathcal{T}_{m}
. As usual, we may assume that
a
is an idempotent, and we keep the notation of Subsection
5.1
, writing
f^{\prime}=f\phi
, and so on. Keeping Lemmas
5.1
and
5.3
in mind, we may assume by symmetry that
\phi
maps
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
into
\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2)
.

Let
t=\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})
, so that
r+2\leq t\leq n-2
, by Corollary
5.13
. We immediately obtain a contradiction if
r+2>n-2
: i.e., if
r\geq n-3
. So for the rest of the proof we assume that
n-6\leq r\leq n-4
. Incidentally, this implies
n\geq 5
.

Let
D
be the
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}^{a}
-class of
a
in
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
. Since all elements of
D^{\prime}=D\phi
are
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-related (in
\mathcal{T}_{m}
) to
a^{\prime}
, and since
\operatorname{rank}(a^{\prime})=t
, it follows that

D^{\prime}\subseteq D_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2))=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2)}:{\operatorname{rank}(f)=t}\}.

(Note that
D_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2))
is not itself a
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class of
\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2)
.)
By
[
12
, Theorem 5.7(v)]
,
D
is an
r^{n-r}\times\Lambda
rectangular group over
\mathcal{S}_{r}
, where
\Lambda
is the product of the sizes of the
\ker(a)
-classes. By Lemma
5.14
we have
\Lambda\geq n-r+1
, so it follows that
D
contains an
r^{n-r}\times(n-r+1)
rectangular band. So too therefore does
D^{\prime}
, and hence also
D_{t}(\mathcal{T}_{m}(n-2))
. It follows, using the
\lambda
and
\rho
parameters from Subsection
2.5
, that

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}(m,n-2)\geq r^{n-r}

\displaystyle\and\rho_{t}(m,n-2)\geq n-r+1.

Using Lemma
2.13
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}(m)\geq r^{n-r}

\displaystyle\and\rho_{t}(n-2)\geq n-r+1.

For the rest of the proof we write
r=n-k
, noting that
k\in\{4,5,6\}
. The previous inequalities become

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}(n+k-1)\geq(n-k)^{k}

\displaystyle\and\rho_{t}(n-2)\geq k+1.

We will obtain the desired contradiction by showing that

\rho_{t}(n-2)<k+1\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $n-k+2=r+2\leq t\leq n-2$.}

(5.16)

To do so, we consider the allowable values of
k
separately. In the following we make use of Remark
2.11
, which tells us that
\rho_{n-2-l}(n-2)=2^{l}
if
0\leq l\leq\frac{n-2}{2}
. We only wish to apply this for
l\in\{0,1,2\}
, so since
n\geq 5
, this could only be invalid when
n=5
and
l=2
; however, in this case we have
\rho_{n-2-l}(n-2)=\rho_{1}(3)=3<4=2^{l}
, which is sufficient for our purposes.

•

When
k=4
, we only have
t=n-2
to consider, and (
5.16
) holds since
\rho_{n-2}(n-2)=1<5
.

•

When
k=5
, we only have
t=n-3
and
n-2
to consider, and (
5.16
) holds since

\rho_{n-3}(n-2)=2<6\and\rho_{n-2}(n-2)=1<6.

•

When
k=6
, (
5.16
) holds since

\rho_{n-4}(n-2)\leq 4<7,\hskip 20.00003pt\rho_{n-3}(n-2)=2<7,\hskip 20.00003pt\rho_{n-2}(n-2)=1<7.

(The
\leq
sign in the first of these is because of the
(n,l)=(5,2)
case mentioned above.) ∎

Remark 5.17
.

The argument in the above proof breaks down when
r=n-7
. Here
k=7
, and (
5.16
) does not hold for the minimum value of
t=n-5
, as we have
\rho_{n-5}(n-2)=8=k+1
. We do not currently know if Theorem
5.15
holds for
r=n-7
, or more generally.

5.3
The case

The previous subsection considered the case in which
r\geq n-6
was suitably large. We now consider the other extreme case in which
r=1
, meaning that the sandwich element
a
is a constant map. Since the value of
\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
does not depend on the particular choice of the constant map

, we denote it by
\mu(n)=\mu(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a})
. Here (
1.2
) gives the bounds

.

The variant
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}
has a rather simple structure when
\operatorname{rank}(a)=1
. To describe it, we may assume by symmetry that

. For each

S_{i}=\{{f\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}:{1f=i}\}.

Then
\mathcal{T}_{n}=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{n}

|S_{i}|=n^{n-1}
, and we note that only

is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. However, we have

S_{i}\star S_{j}=\{e_{j}\}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for all $i,j\in{\bf n}$,}

(5.18)

and this determines the entire multiplication table of

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{a}

4.5
. It therefore follows that

.

For

\displaystyle\mu(n)

\displaystyle=\min\{{2A+3B+C+D}:{0\leq A\leq 2,\ B,D\geq 0,\ C\geq 2,\ 2^{A}3^{B}C\geq n,\ C^{D}\geq n^{n-1}}\}

\displaystyle={\min}\big\{{2A+3\lceil\log_{3}(n/2^{A}C)\rceil+C+\lceil\log_{C}n^{n-1}\rceil}:{0\leq A\leq 2\leq C\leq 2n-1}\big\},

This follows immediately from Theorem

(ii)

Proposition 5.23
.

(i)

If

, then
\mu(n)=2n-1
.

(ii)

If

, then
\mu(n)\leq 2n-2
.

Proof.

(i)
. This follows by inspecting Table

.

(ii)
. By Table

. To do this, let

. We will show that the tuple

5.20

\displaystyle C^{D}=k^{2n-4-k}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{2n-4-\frac{n+1}{2}}

\tfrac{n}{2}\leq k\leq\tfrac{n+1}{2}

\displaystyle=n^{n-1}\cdot\frac{n^{(n-7)/2}}{2^{(3n-9)/2}}

\displaystyle\geq n^{n-1}\cdot\frac{2^{2n-14}}{2^{(3n-9)/2}}

as
n\geq 16=2^{4}

\displaystyle=n^{n-1}\cdot 2^{(n-19)/2}

\displaystyle\geq n^{n-1}

\displaystyle\text{as $n\geq 19$.}\qed

The upper bound of
2n-2
in Proposition
5.23
(ii)
is still not sharp in general, as clearly indicated in Table
7
. In fact, given any integer
k
, we have
\mu(n)\leq 2n-k
for suitably large
n
; indeed, in Theorem
5.27
below, we prove an even stronger statement.
First, however, we demonstrate another interesting property of the numbers
\mu(n)
, namely that they are strictly increasing:

Theorem 5.24
.

We have
\mu(1)<\mu(2)<\mu(3)<\cdots
.

Proof.

Let
n\geq 2
, and write
m=\mu(n)
. We must show that
\mu(n-1)\leq m-1
. Consulting Table
7
, it suffices to assume that
n\geq 16
, which we do for the rest of the proof. By Proposition
5.23
we have
m\leq 2n-2
.

Suppose
\mu(n)=m
is witnessed by the tuple
(A,B,C,D)
; as in Remark
5.22
, we can assume that
C\leq n
. We aim to show that the inequality
\mu(n-1)\leq m-1
is witnessed by
(A,B,C,D-1)
. Again, verification of the required conditions is mostly routine; the only exceptions this time are
D-1\geq 0
and
C^{D-1}\geq(n-1)^{n-2}
.

Keeping
C\leq n
in mind, we have
n^{n-1}\leq C^{D}\leq n^{D}
. It follows that
D\geq n-1
, so certainly
D-1\geq 0
. We also have

n\cdot n^{n-2}\leq C^{D}=C\cdot C^{D-1}\leq n\cdot C^{D-1},

which implies
C^{D-1}\geq n^{n-2}\geq(n-1)^{n-2}
. As noted above, this completes the proof.
∎

The next two theorems concern the asymptotic behaviour of the sequence
\mu(n)
. For their proofs, it will be convenient to use the following special case of Lemma
4.25
.

Lemma 5.25
.

For
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\mu(n)\leq m
if and only if there exist compositions
\sigma
and
\tau
such that

\displaystyle\tau\preceq\sigma\vDash m,\hskip 20.00003pt\textstyle{\prod\tau\geq n},\hskip 20.00003pt\eta(\sigma,\tau)\geq n^{n-1}.

(5.26)

Theorem 5.27
.

For any real
1<x<2
, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu(n)\leq xn
for all
n>N
. Thus, asympotically we have

\mu(n)\sim n\hskip 20.00003pt\text{as $n\to\infty$.}

Proof.

By Lemma
5.25
, it suffices to show that there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n>N
there exist compositions

\tau_{n}\preceq\sigma_{n}\vDash\lfloor xn\rfloor\hskip 20.00003pt\text{such that}\hskip 20.00003pt\textstyle{\prod\tau_{n}}\geq n\and\eta(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\geq n^{n-1}.

Let
0<y<x-1
be arbitrary, and set
z=x-y
, noting that
z>1
. Also let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
ky\geq 1
. Note that
y
,
z
and
k
depend only on
x
.

For any
n>k+2
, define

\sigma_{n}=(\lfloor xn\rfloor-k,k)\and\tau_{n}=(\lceil yn\rceil,k).

Clearly
\prod\tau_{n}\geq(ky)n\geq n
. Since

\lfloor xn\rfloor-k>(xn-1)-k=n+(x-1)n-1-k>(k+2)+yn-1-k=yn+1>\lceil yn\rceil,

it follows that
\sigma_{n}
is a composition of
\lfloor xn\rfloor
, and that
\tau_{n}\preceq\sigma_{n}
. Next, recalling that
z=x-y>1
, we have

\eta(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})=\lceil yn\rceil^{\lfloor xn\rfloor-k-\lceil yn\rceil}\geq(yn)^{(xn-1)-k-(yn+1)}=(yn)^{zn-k-2}.

Since
k,y>0
and
z>1
are constants (not depending on
n
), the function
(yn)^{zn-k-2}
dominates
n^{n-1}
, so there exists
N^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(yn)^{zn-k-2}>n^{n-1}
for all
n>N^{\prime}
. We then take
{N=\max(k+2,N^{\prime})}
.
∎

Theorem
5.27
tells us that the ratio
\mu(n)/n
tends to
1
as
n
increases. In contrast to this, the difference
\mu(n)-n
grows without bound:

Theorem 5.28
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
we have
\mu(n)\geq n+k
for all
n\geq\lceil k\ln k\rceil+1
.

For the proof we require the following lemma, concerning the
L
function defined in Subsection
2.1
:

Lemma 5.29
.

If
\mu(n)\leq m
where
n\geq 2
, then
n^{n-1}<\lceil L(m)\rceil^{m-\lfloor L(m)\rfloor}
.

Proof.

Fix compositions
\sigma
and
\tau
satisfying (
5.26
). Then using the definitions and Lemma
2.8
(i)
, we have

n^{n-1}\leq\eta(\sigma,\tau)\leq\xi(\sigma)\leq\Xi(m)=\xi(m).

Now write
x=L(m)
, so that
\xi(m)=\max\left(\lfloor x\rfloor^{m-\lfloor x\rfloor},\lceil x\rceil^{m-\lceil x\rceil}\right)
by Lemma
2.4
(iii)
. Since
m
is an integer,
x
is not an integer, so it follows that
\lfloor x\rfloor<\lceil x\rceil
. Since
m\geq\mu(n)>n\geq 2
, we also have
m\geq x
, so that
m\geq\lceil x\rceil>\lfloor x\rfloor
.
All of this implies that
\lfloor x\rfloor^{m-\lfloor x\rfloor}
and
\lceil x\rceil^{m-\lceil x\rceil}
are both strictly less than
\lceil x\rceil^{m-\lfloor x\rfloor}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.28
.

Fix some
n\geq\lceil k\ln k\rceil+1
where
k\geq 2
, and let
x=L(n+k-1)
, so that
n+k-1=x(1+\ln x)
by definition. Note that
n\geq k+1
since
k\geq 2
.

We first claim that
\lceil x\rceil\leq n
.
Indeed, this is clear if
x<e
since then
n\geq k+1\geq 3=\lceil e\rceil
. Now suppose
x\geq e
, so that
1+\ln x\geq 2
. Then
2x\leq x(1+\ln x)=n+k-1\leq 2n-1
, as
n\geq k
, and so
x<n
. The claim follows as
n
is an integer.

Next we claim that
\lfloor x\rfloor\geq k
. For this we have

x(1+\ln x)=n+k-1\geq(\lceil k\ln k\rceil+1)+k-1\geq k(\ln k+1).

Since
x,k\geq e^{-1}
, it follows that
x\geq k
, and again the claim follows as
k
is an integer.

Returning now to the main proof, suppose to the contrary that
\mu(n)\leq n+k-1
. Then the above two claims and Lemma
5.29
(with
m=n+k-1
) give

n^{n-1}<\lceil x\rceil^{n+k-1-\lfloor x\rfloor}\leq n^{(n+k-1)-k}=n^{n-1},

a contradiction.
∎

We conclude with a number of open problems. The most obvious is the following:
