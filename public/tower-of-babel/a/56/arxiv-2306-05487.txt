Boosting with Tempered Exponential Measures
Richard Nock, Ehsan Amid, Manfred K. Warmuth

Abstract
One of the most popular ML algorithms, AdaBoost , can be derived from the dual of a relative entropy minimization problem subject to the fact that the positive weights on the examples sum to one. Essentially, harder examples receive higher probabilities. We generalize this setup to the recently introduced tempered exponential measure s ( tem s) where normalization is enforced on a specific power of the measure and not the measure itself. tem s are indexed by a parameter t t and generalize exponential families ( t = 1 t=1 ). Our algorithm, t t - AdaBoost , recovers AdaBoost as a special case ( t = 1 t=1 ). We show that t t - AdaBoost retains AdaBoost ’s celebrated exponential convergence rate when t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) t\in[0,1) while allowing a slight improvement of the rate’s hidden constant compared to t = 1 t=1 . t t - AdaBoost partially computes on a generalization of classical arithmetic over the reals and brings notable properties like guaranteed bounded leveraging coefficients for t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) t\in[0,1) . From the loss that t t - AdaBoost minimizes (a generalization of the exponential loss), we show how to derive a new family of tempered losses for the induction of domain-partitioning classifiers like decision trees. Crucially, strict properness is ensured for all while their boosting rates span the full known spectrum. Experiments using t t - AdaBoost +trees display that significant leverage can be achieved by tuning t t .

Related work

Boosting refers to the ability of an algorithm to combine the outputs of moderately accurate, ”weak” hypotheses into a highly accurate, ”strong” ensemble. Originally, boosting was introduced in the context of Valiant’s PAC learning model as a way to circumvent the then-existing amount of related negative results
[
10
,
35
]
. After the first formal proof that boosting is indeed achievable
[
28
]
,
AdaBoost
became the first practical and proof-checked boosting algorithm
[
8
,
30
]
. Boosting was thus born in a machine learning context, but later on, it also emerged in statistics as a way to learn from class residuals computed using the gradient of the loss
[
9
,
20
]
, resulting this time in a flurry of computationally efficient algorithms, still called boosting algorithms, but for which the connection with the original weak / strong learning framework is in general not known.

Our paper draws its boosting connections with
AdaBoost
’s formal lineage.
AdaBoost
has spurred a long line of work alongside different directions, including statistical consistency
[
4
]
, noise handling
[
15
,
16
]
, low-resource optimization
[
21
]
,
etc
. The starting point of our work is a fascinating result in convex optimization establishing a duality between the algorithm and its memory of past iteration’s performances given by the probability distribution
over the examples
[
12
]
. From this standpoint,
AdaBoost
solves the dual of the optimization of a relative entropy between the new and current distribution subject to a linear constraint on the weak classifier’s performance. Whenever a relative entropy is minimized subject to linear constraints, then the solution is a member of an exponential family of distributions (see
e.g.

[
2
, Section 2.8.1]
for an axiomatization of exponential families). Indeed
AdaBoost
’s distribution on the examples is a member of a discrete exponential family where the training examples are the finite support of the distribution, sufficient statistics are defined from the weak learners, and the leveraging coefficients are the natural parameters. In summary, there is an intimate relationship between boosting à-la-
AdaBoost
, exponential families, and Bregman divergences
[
12
,
7
,
19
]
and our work
”elevates” these methods above exponential families.

\displaystyle\left\|\exp_{t}\left(\log_{t}\bm{q}_{j}-\mu\cdot\bm{u}_{j}\right)\right\|_{1/t^{*}}^{1/t^{*}}\quad\left(\mbox{with }u_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right).

(15)

In an equivalent form, one can easily show that

\displaystyle{Z}_{tj}^{2-t}(\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}q^{2-t}_{ji}\cdot\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(-\frac{\mu u_{ji}}{q^{1-t}_{ji}}\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{ji}\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(-\frac{\mu u_{ji}}{p^{1-t^{*}}_{ji}}\right),

where we remind that
\bm{p}_{j}
is the co-density of
\bm{q}_{j}
(Section
3
), which is in the probability simplex. Hence, (
16
) is a classical expectation, though the
\exp_{t}
also integrates the co-density weights. In boosting, examples that have received the right classification receive smaller weights. What (
16
) shows is that in this expectation, the contribution of examples with the right class (
\mu u_{ij}>0
) dimishes even further. We cannot easily unravel the normalization coefficient to make appear a complete classifier, because of the presence of
[.]_{+}
in
\exp_{t}
(
1
). However, if
\max_{i}|h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})|
is small enough for any
j\in[J]
, we easily obtain from (
15
) after dropping
j
and the argument
\mu
for readability, that we end up minimizing a loss being

\displaystyle F_{t}(H,\mathcal{S})\left(\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}{Z}^{2-t}_{tj}(\mu)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i}\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(-y_{i}H(\bm{x}_{i})\right),

(17)

where we have absorbed in
H
the factor
m^{1-t^{*}}
appearing in the
\exp_{t}
(scaling
H
by a positive value does not change its empirical risk). One retrieves
AdaBoost
’s exponential loss by setting
t\rightarrow 1^{-}
in (
17
), so we denote with a slight abuse of language (
17
), and (
15
) by extension, the
tempered exponential loss
. Notice that one can choose to minimize
F_{t}(H,\mathcal{S})
disregarding any constraint on
|H|
.

6
A broad family of boosting-compliant proper losses for decision trees

Losses for class probability estimation

When it comes to tabular data, it has long been known that some of the best models to linearly combine with boosting are decision trees (DT,
[
9
]
). Decision trees, like other domain-partitioning classifiers, are not trained by minimizing a
surrogate loss
defined over real-valued predictions, but defined over
class probability estimation
(CPE,
[
25
]
), those estimators being posterior estimation computed at the leaves. Let us introduce a few definitions for those. A CPE loss
\ell:\{-1,1\}\times[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle\ell(y,u)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\llbracket y=1\rrbracket\cdot\ell_{1}(u)+\llbracket y=-1\rrbracket\cdot\ell_{-1}(u).

(18)

Functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{-1}
are called
partial
losses. The pointwise conditional risk of local guess
u\in[0,1]
with respect to a ground truth
v\in[0,1]
is:

\displaystyle\mbox{{L}}(u,v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle v\cdot\ell_{1}(u)+(1-v)\cdot\ell_{-1}(u).

(19)

A loss is
proper
iff for any ground truth
v\in[0,1]
,
\mbox{{L}}(v,v)=\inf_{u}\mbox{{L}}(u,v)
, and strictly proper iff
u=v
is the sole minimizer
[
25
]
. The (pointwise)
Bayes
risk is
\underline{L}(v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\inf_{u}\mbox{{L}}(u,v)
. The log/cross-entropy-loss, square-loss, Matusita loss are examples of CPE losses. One then trains a DT minimizing the expectation of this loss over leaves’ posteriors,
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[\underline{L}(p_{\lambda})]
,
p_{\lambda}
being the local proportion of positive examples at leaf
\lambda
– or equivalently, the local posterior.

Deriving CPE losses from (ada)boosting

Recently, it was shown how to derive in a general way a CPE loss to train a DT from the minimization of a surrogate loss with a boosting algorithm
[
16
]
. In our case, the surrogate would be
{Z}_{tj}
(
15
) and the boosting algorithm,
t
-
AdaBoost
. The principle is simple and fits in four steps: (i) show that a DT can equivalently perform simple linear classifications, (ii) use a weak learner that designs splits and the boosting algorithm to fit the leveraging coefficient and compute those in closed form, (iii) simplify the expression of the loss using those, (iv) show that the expression simplified is, in fact, a CPE loss. To get (i), we remark that a DT contains a tree (graph). One can associate to each node a real value. To classify an observation, we sum all reals from the root to a leaf and decide on the class based on the sign of the prediction, just like for any real-valued predictor. Suppose we are at a leaf. What kind of weak hypotheses can create splits ”in disguise”? Those can be of the form

\displaystyle h_{j}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\llbracket x_{k}\geq a_{j}\rrbracket\cdot b_{j},\quad a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R},

where the observation variable
x_{k}
is assumed real valued for simplicity and the test
\llbracket x_{k}\geq a_{j}\rrbracket
splits the leaf’s domain in two non-empty subsets. This creates half of the split.
\overline{h}_{j}(\bm{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\llbracket x_{k}<a_{j}\rrbracket\cdot-b_{j}
creates the other half of the split. Interestingly,
h_{j}
satisfies the weak learning assumption iff
\overline{h}_{j}
does
[
16
]
. So we get the split design part of (ii). We compute the leveraging coefficients at the new leaves from the surrogate’s minimization / boosting algorithm, end up with new real predictions at the new leaves (instead of the original
b_{j},-b_{j}
), push those predictions in the surrogate loss for (iii), simplify it and, quite remarkably end up with a loss of the form
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[\mathrm{L}(p_{\lambda})]
, where
\mathrm{L}
turns out to be the pointwise Bayes risk
\underline{L}
of a proper loss
[
16
]
.

In the case of
[
16
]
, it is, in fact, granted that we end up with such a ”nice” CPE loss because of the choice of the surrogates at the start. In our case, however, nothing grants this
a priori
if we start from the tempered exponential loss
F_{t}
(
15
) so it is legitimate to wonder whether such a chain of derivations (summarized) can happen to reverse engineer an interesting CPE loss:

\displaystyle F_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\mapsto}}\mathrm{L}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\mapsto}}{\underline{L}^{(t)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{\mapsto}}\ell^{(t)}_{1};\ell^{(t)}_{-1}\quad(\mbox{proper ? strictly proper ? for which $t$s ?, ...})

(20)

When such a complete derivation happens until the partial losses
\ell_{1};\ell_{-1}
and their properties, we shall write that minimizing
F_{t}

elicits
the corresponding loss and partial losses.

Theorem 6.1
.

Minimizing
F_{t}
elicits the CPE loss we define as the
tempered loss
, with partial losses:

\displaystyle\ell^{(t)}_{1}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\left(\frac{1-u}{M_{1-t}(u,1-u)}\right)^{2-t}

,

\displaystyle\ell^{(t)}_{-1}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\ell^{(t)}_{1}(1-u),\quad(t\in[-\infty,2]).

(21)

The tempered loss is symmetric, differentiable, strictly proper for
t\in(-\infty,2)
and proper for
t=-\infty
.

Differentiability means the partial losses are differentiable, and symmetry follows from the relationship between partial losses
[
19
]
(the proof, in App, Section
II.3
, derives the infinite case,
\ell^{(-\infty)}_{1}(u)=2\cdot\llbracket u\leq 1/2\rrbracket
). Let us explicit the Bayes risk of the tempered loss and a key property.

Lemma 6.2
.

The Bayes risk of the tempered loss is (
M_{q}
defined in Theorem
5.1
):

\displaystyle{\underline{L}^{(t)}}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2u(1-u)}{M_{1-t}(u,1-u)},

(22)

and it satisfies
\forall u\in[0,1],\forall z\in[2\cdot\min\{u,1-u\},1]
,
\exists t\in[-\infty,2]
such that
{\underline{L}^{(t)}}(u)=z
.

Lemma
6.2
, whose proof is trivial, allows to show a key boosting result:
t=1
retrieves Matusita’s loss, for which a near optimal boosting rate is known
[
11
]
while
t=-\infty
retrieves the empirical risk, which yields the worst possible guarantee
[
11
]
. In between, we have, for example, CART’s Gini criterion for
t=0
, which yields an intermediate boosting guarantee. Continuity with respect to
t
of the Bayes risks in between the empirical risk and Matusita’s loss means the boosting ranges of the tempered loss cover
the full known spectrum of boosting rates
for
t\in[-\infty,1]
. We know of no (differentiable and) proper CPE loss with such a coverage. Note that (i) this is a non-constructive result as we do not associate a specific
t
for a specific rate, and (ii) the state of the art boosting rates for DT induction does not seem to cover the case
t\in(1,2)
, thus left as an open question.

Experiments

We have performed experiments on a testbed of 10 UCI domains, whose details are given in App (Section

8
Discussion: loss selection with
t
-
AdaBoost

Our theory yields a family of surrogate loss functions and corresponding training algorithms for the induction of linear combinations of classifiers or the induction of decision trees, with boosting-compliant rates on training. This formal picture looks quite uniform from the training standpoint but it is nuanced experimentally by the fact that the best values for
t
for good generalization depend on the domain at hand (and on additional experimental factors, such as the presence of noise, the size of models, etc.). While this could be expected because generalization entails prediction on unseen data, there would be a specific reason, in our case, not just to have
t
domain dependent, but in fact to tune
t
during training
[
19
]
. As we explain, this problem entails questions relative to training and generalisation and to make a parallel with model selection in ML
[
17
, Chapter 4]
, we call it loss selection.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is sampled i.i.d. according to some unknown
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a set of linear combinations of decision trees. We know that with probability
\geq 1-\delta
, every
H\in\mathcal{H}
has

\displaystyle F_{t}(H,\mathcal{D})\leq F_{t}(H,\mathcal{S})+\mathcal{O}(L_{t}R_{m}(\mathcal{H}))+Q(m,\delta),

where
L_{t}
is the Lipschitz constant of the tempered exponential loss,
R_{m}(\mathcal{H})
is the Rademacher complexity of
\mathcal{H}
(a capacity parameter) and
Q
does not depend on
t
nor
H
, see
[
5
]
and
[
17
]
. Since
F_{\nicefrac{{0}}{{1}}}(H,\mathcal{D})\leq F_{t}(H,\mathcal{D})
, (
8
) also brings guarantees on the true risk of every classifier in
\mathcal{H}
. Reasoning in terms of structural risk minimization through parameter
t
, we should pick
t
not just to get a convenient loss to minimize empirically (
F_{t}
)
but also to reduce the uncertainty on translating good empirical results in generalization as well, which would command to reduce
L_{t}
as well. We assume that the domain in which we are allowed to fix
t
is
[0,1]
.

Denote for short
u_{t}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\exp_{t}^{2-t}(-z)
. Without restriction on
z
,
u_{t}
is not Lipschitz, but (i) our experiments display that we tend to learn classifiers with bounded magnitude and (ii) scaling a classifier by a positive factor does not change its empirical risk, so assume its argument satisfies
|z|\leq Z
for some
Z>0
. In this case, the Lipschitz constant can be computed as
L_{t,Z}=(2-t)\exp_{t}(Z)
, which brings

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L_{t,Z}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}^{t}(Z)}{1-t^{*}}\cdot\left(\exp_{t}^{1-t}(Z)\cdot\left[\log\exp_{t}(Z)-(1-t^{*})\right]-Z\right)\>\left(\mbox{recall }t^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2-t}\right).

We observe
\frac{\partial L_{t,0}}{\partial t}=-1
but as
Z
increases, the function can get
>0
: for example, if
Z=1/(1-t)
, the function is positive for
t\in[1/2,1]
. From a ML standpoint, we thus have the following behaviour impacting the Lipschitz constant:

(
L
)

early during training or when we have a small number of trees, we can have
Z
very small, while later on, as the number of trees increases, the relevant
Z
increases as well.

Hence, to mitigate the impact of the capacity parameter in (
8
), (
L
) suggests to increase
t
early during training while eventually reducing it later on. What about the loss that we minimize during training, (
15
) ? It can be shown that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{Z}^{2-t}_{tj}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|1-t|}\sum_{i\in[m]}\left(\frac{1}{2-t}\cdot q^{2-t}_{(j+1)i}\cdot\log q^{2-t}_{(j+1)i}-q_{(j+1)i}q^{1-t}_{ji}\cdot\log q^{2-t}_{ji}\right),

(23)

which is perhaps not easily readable, but when
\bm{q}_{j}>\bm{0}
, has a nice equivalent expression. For
t\in[0,1)
, let
H_{t}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}(1/(2-t))\cdot\left(z^{2-t}\cdot\log z^{2-t}-z^{2-t}\right)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose
\bm{q}_{j}>\bm{0}
. Recalling
D_{.}(.\|.)
is the notation for a Bregman divergence, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{Z}^{2-t}_{tj}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|1-t|}\cdot D_{H_{t}}(\bm{q}_{j+1}\|\bm{q}_{j})+H_{t}(\bm{q}_{j}).

(24)

The arguments of
H_{t}
are
tem
s, so (
24
) is in disguise (and up to multiplicative factors) the subtraction of two non-negative quantities: the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two successive co-densities and Shannon’s entropy of the previous co-density. The total is thus negative especially when
\bm{q}_{j+1}
and
\bm{q}_{j}
come closer to each other (a Bregman divergence satisfies the identity of indiscernibles). From a ML standpoint, we thus have the following behaviour impacting the loss:

(
Z
)

early during training or as long as
t
-
AdaBoost
is far from the training optimum of
{Z}^{2-t}_{tj}
,
\bm{q}_{j+1}
and
\bm{q}_{j}
are substantially different from each other while as
t
-
AdaBoost
gets closer to the optimal classifier on training,
\bm{q}_{j+1}\rightarrow\bm{q}_{j}
.

Hence, increasing
t
early during training or reducing it later on, as suggested by (
L
) may lead from (
Z
) to a worse upperbound of the empirical risk in (
8
). Also true is the fact that reducing the loss parameter via (
Z
) may worsen the dependency on the Lipschitz constant from (
L
). There should thus be data- and classifier-dependent ways to tune
t
. We have made simple attempts at loss selection but none has proven impactful in terms of results.

9
Conclusion

AdaBoost
is one of the original and simplest Boosting algorithms.
In this paper, we generalized
AdaBoost
to maintaining a
tempered measure over the examples by minimizing a tempered relative entropy.
We kept the setup as simple as possible and therefore
focused on generalizing
AdaBoost
.
However, more advanced boosting algorithms have been designed based on
relative entropy minimization subject to linear constraints.
There are versions that constrain the edges of all past hypotheses to be zero
[
37
]
.
Also, when the maximum margin of the game is larger than zero, then
AdaBoost
cycles over
non-optimal solutions
[
26
]
. Later Boosting algorithms provably
optimize the margin of the solution by adjusting the
constraint value on the dual edge away from zero (see, e.g.,

[
23
]
).
Finally, the ELRP-Boost algorithm optimizes a trade-off
between relative entropy and the edge
[
36
]
.
We conjecture that all of these orthogonal
direction have generalizations to the tempered case as well
and are worth exploring.

These are theoretical directions that, if successful, would contribute
to bringing more tools to the design of rigorous boosting
algorithms. This is important because boosting suffers several
impediments, not all of which we have mentioned: for example, to get
statistical consistency for
AdaBoost
, it is known that early stopping
is mandatory
[
4
]
. More generally, non-Lipschitz losses like
the exponential loss seem to be harder to handle compared to Lipschitz
losses
[
34
]
(but they yield, in general, better convergence rates on training). The validity of the weak learning assumption of boosting can also be discussed, in particular, regarding the negative result of
[
15
]
which advocates, beyond just better (ada)boosting, for boosting for
more
classes of models / architectures
[
16
]
. Alongside this direction, we feel that our experiments on noise handling give a preliminary account of the fact that there is no ”one
t
fits all” case, but a much more in-depth analysis is required to elicit / tune a ”good”
t
. This is a crucial issue for noise handling
[
16
]
, but as we explain in Section
7

Appendix I
A short primer on Tempered Exponential Measures

We describe here the minimal amount of material necessary to understand how our approach to boosting connects to these measures. We refer to
[
3
]
for more details. With a slight abuse of notation, we define the perspective transforms
(\log_{t})^{*}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}t^{*}\cdot\log_{t^{*}}(z/t^{*})
and
(\exp_{t})^{*}(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}t^{*}\cdot\exp_{t^{*}}(z/t^{*})
. Recall that
t^{*}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}1/(2-t)
.

Definition A
.

[
3
]

A tempered exponential measure (
tem
) family is a set of unnormalized densities in which each element admits the following canonical expression:

\displaystyle q_{t|\bm{\theta}}(\bm{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\frac{\exp_{t}(\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x}))}{\exp_{t}(G_{t}(\bm{\theta}))}=\exp_{t}(\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x})\ominus_{t}G_{t}(\bm{\theta}))\quad\left(a\ominus_{t}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\frac{a-b}{1+(1-t)b}\right),

(25)

where
\bm{\theta}
is the element’s natural parameter,
\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x})
is the sufficient statistics and

\displaystyle G_{t}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\log_{t})^{*}\int(\exp_{t})^{*}(\bm{\theta}^{\top}\bm{\varphi}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{d}\xi

is the (convex) cumulant,
\xi
being a base measure (implicit).

Except for
t=1
(which reduces a
tem
family to a classical exponential family), the total mass of a
tem
is not 1 (but it has an elegant closed form expression
[
3
]
). However, the exponentiated
q_{t|\bm{\theta}}^{1/t^{*}}
does sum to 1. In the discrete case, this justifies extending the classical simplex to what we denote as the co-simplex.

Definition B
.

The co-simplex of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\tilde{\Delta}_{m}
is defined as
\tilde{\Delta}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\{\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\bm{q}\geq\bm{0}\wedge\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{q}^{1/t^{*}}=1\}
.

The connection between
t
-
AdaBoost
’s update and
tem
’s is immediate from the equation’s update ((
6
) in
mf
). We can show that
\tilde{\Delta}_{m}
can also be represented as
tem
s.

Lemma C
.

\tilde{\Delta}_{m}
is a (discrete) family of tempered exponential measures.

Proof.

We proceed as in
[
2
, Section 2.2.2]
for exponential families: let
{\bm{q}}\in\tilde{\Delta}_{m}
, which we write

\displaystyle{q}(n)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}{q}_{i}\cdot\llbracket i=n\rrbracket,n\in[m].

(26)

\llbracket\pi\rrbracket
, the Iverson bracket
[
13
]
, takes value 1 if Boolean predicate
\pi
is true (and 0 otherwise). We create
m-1
natural parameters and the cumulant,

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\log_{t}\frac{{q}_{i}}{{q}_{m}},i\in[m-1]

;

\displaystyle G_{t}(\bm{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\log_{t}\frac{1}{{q}_{m}},

and end up with (
26
) also matching the atom mass function

\displaystyle{q}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}\left(\sum_{i\in[m-1]}\theta_{i}\cdot\llbracket i=n\rrbracket\right)}{\exp_{t}G_{t}(\bm{\theta})},

which clearly defines a tempered exponential measure over
[m]
. This ends the proof of Lemma
C
.
∎

II.II.2.1
Clamped summations

For any
\delta\geq 0
, we define clamped summations of the sequence of ordered elements
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{J}
: if
J>1
,

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\min\left\{v_{J}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\}

,

\displaystyle\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\max\left\{v_{J}+\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j},-\delta\right\},

(41)

and the base case (
J=1
) is obtained by replacing the inner sum by 0. We also define the doubly clamped summation:

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\max\left\{\min\left\{v_{J}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\},-\delta\right\},

with the same convention for the base case. We prove a series of simple but useful properties of the clamped summation.

Lemma C
.

The following properties hold true for clamped summation:

1.

(doubly) clamped summations are non commutative;

2.

(doubly) clamped summations are ordinary summation in the limit: for any
J\geq 1
and any sequence
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{J}
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow+\infty}\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow+\infty}\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow+\infty}\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}=\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}

3.

clamped summations sandwich ordinary summation and the doubly clamped summation: for any
\delta\geq 0
, any
J\geq 1
and any sequence
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{J}
,

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\leq\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\leq\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}

;

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\leq\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}\leq\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}

Proof.

Non commutativity follows from simple counterexamples: for example, for
v\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}-1
and
w\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}2
, if we fix
v_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}v,v_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}w
, then
\sideset{{}^{(0)}}{}{\sum}^{2}_{j=1}v_{j}=1
while
\sideset{{}^{(0)}}{}{\sum}^{2}_{j=1}v_{3-j}=-1
. Property [2.] is trivial. The set of leftmost inequalities of property [3.] can be shown by induction, noting the base case is trivial and otherwise, using the induction hypothesis in the leftmost inequality,

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J+1}_{j=1}v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\min\left\{v_{J+1}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\}\leq\min\left\{v_{J+1}+\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\}\leq v_{J+1}+\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}=\sum^{J+1}_{j=1}v_{j},

and similarly

\displaystyle\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J+1}_{j=1}v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\max\left\{v_{J+1}+\sideset{{}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},-\delta\right\}\geq\max\left\{v_{J+1}+\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},-\delta\right\}\geq v_{J+1}+\sum^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}=\sum^{J+1}_{j=1}v_{j}.

A similar argument holds for the set of rightmost inequalities: for example, the induction’s general case holds

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J+1}_{j=1}v_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\min\left\{v_{J+1}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min\left\{v_{J+1}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\}\leq\max\left\{\min\left\{v_{J+1}+\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j},\delta\right\},-\delta\right\}=\sideset{{}^{(\delta)}_{(-\delta)}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}.

for the leftmost inequality. This ends the proof of Lemma
C
.
∎

Lemma D

Define

\displaystyle v_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right)&\mbox{ if }&t<1\\
\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-\sideset{{}_{(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right)&\mbox{ if }&t>1\end{array}\right..

Proof.

We start for the case
t<1
. We proceed by induction, noting first that the normalization constraint for the initial weights imposes
q_{1i}=1/m^{1/(2-t)}=1/m^{t^{*}}
and so (using
(1-t)t^{*}=1-t^{*}
)

\displaystyle q_{2i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}(\log_{t}q_{1i}-\mu_{1}u_{1i})}{Z_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\cdot\left[1+(1-t)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1-t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{m^{\frac{1-t}{2-t}}}-1\right)-\mu_{1}u_{1i}\right)\right]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{1}}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{m^{1-t^{*}}}-(1-t)\mu_{1}u_{1i}\right]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}Z_{1}}\cdot\left[1-(1-t)m^{1-t^{*}}\mu_{1}u_{1i}\right]_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}Z_{1}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right),

completing the base case. Using the induction hypothesis, we unravel at iteration
J+1
:

\displaystyle q_{(J+1)i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}(\log_{t}q_{Ji}-\mu_{J}u_{Ji})}{Z_{J}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}\left(\log_{t}\left(\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right)\right)-\mu_{J}u_{Ji}\right)}{Z_{J}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}\left(\log_{t}\exp_{t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right)\ominus_{t}\log_{t}\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)-\mu_{J}u_{Ji}\right)}{Z_{J}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{J}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(\frac{\max\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}-\log_{t}\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)}{1+(1-t)\log_{t}\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)}-\mu_{J}u_{Ji}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{J}}\cdot\left[1+\frac{(1-t)\cdot\max\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}-(1-t)\log_{t}\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)}{1+(1-t)\log_{t}\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)}-(1-t)\mu_{J}u_{Ji}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{J}}\cdot\left[1+\frac{(1-t)\cdot\max\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}-\left(\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)^{1-t}-1\right)}{\left(m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J-1}Z_{tj}\right)^{1-t}}-(1-t)\mu_{J}u_{Ji}\right]^{\frac{1}{1-t}}_{+},

which simplifies into (using
(1-t)t^{*}=1-t^{*}
)

\displaystyle q_{(J+1)i}

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\left[1+(1-t)\cdot\left(\max\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}-v_{J}u_{Ji}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-S_{J}\right),

with

\displaystyle S_{J}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\min\left\{-\max\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}+v_{J}u_{Ji},\frac{1}{1-t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{v_{J}u_{Ji}+\min\left\{\frac{1}{1-t},\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\},\frac{1}{1-t}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min\left\{v_{J}u_{Ji}+\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji},\frac{1}{1-t}\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}

(we used twice the definition of clamped summation), which completes the proof of Lemma
D
for
t<1
.

We now treat the case
t>1
. The base induction is equivalent, while unravelling gives, instead of (
44
):

\displaystyle q_{(J+1)i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\left[1+(1-t)\cdot\left(\min\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}_{-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{t-1}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}-v_{J}u_{Ji}\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}}\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-S_{J}\right),

and, this time,

\displaystyle S_{J}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\max\left\{-\min\left\{-\frac{1}{1-t},-\sideset{{}_{-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{t-1}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\}+v_{J}u_{Ji},-\frac{1}{t-1}\right\}

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{v_{J}u_{Ji}+\max\left\{-\frac{1}{t-1},\sideset{{}_{-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{t-1}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji}\right\},-\frac{1}{t-1}\right\}

(46)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{v_{J}u_{Ji}+\sideset{{}_{-(\nicefrac{{1}}{{t-1}})}}{}{\sum}^{J-1}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji},-\frac{1}{t-1}\right\}

(47)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sideset{{}_{(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{t-1}})}}{}{\sum}^{J}_{j=1}v_{j}u_{ji},

(48)

which completes the proof of Lemma
D
.
∎

II.II.2.3
Introducing classifiers

Ordinary linear separators

Suppose we have a classifier

\displaystyle H_{J}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\bm{x}),\quad\beta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}m^{t^{*}}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk},

where
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\forall j\in[J]
. We remark that
\llbracket z\neq r\rrbracket\leq\exp_{t}^{2-t}(-zr)
for any
t\leq 2
and
z,r\in\mathbb{R}
, and
z\mapsto\exp_{t}^{2-t}(-z)
is decreasing for any
t\leq 2
, so using [3.] in Lemma
C
, we get for our training sample
\mathcal{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i}),i\in[m]\}
and any
t<1
(from Lemma
D
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H_{J}(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

(49)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{J}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)}{m}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}v_{j}u_{ji}\right)}{m}

where

\displaystyle v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}

;

\displaystyle u_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i}).

(50)

Using Lemma
D
with those definitions, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H_{J}(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\left(q_{(J+1)i}m^{t^{*}}\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z_{tj}\right)^{2-t}}{m}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z^{2-t}_{tj}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}q^{2-t}_{(J+1)i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z^{2-t}_{tj},

because
q_{J}\in\tilde{\Delta}_{m}
. We thus have proven the following Lemma.

Lemma E
.

For any
t<1
and any linear separator

\displaystyle H_{J}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\bm{x}),\quad\left(\beta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}m^{t^{*}}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk},\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R},h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}},\forall j\in[J]\right),

where
Z_{tk}
is the normalization coefficient of
\bm{q}
in (
29
) with
u_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H_{J}(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z^{2-t}_{tj}.

(51)

t<1
)

\displaystyle H^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{J}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{J}(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

(52)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-y_{i}\cdot\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)}{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)}{m}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\frac{\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}m^{1-t^{*}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk}\right)^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)}{m}

, so it can be folded in the doubly clamped summation without changing its value, and the second inequality used [3.] in Lemma
C
. This directly leads us to the following Lemma.

Lemma F
.

For any
t<1
and any clamped linear separator

\displaystyle H^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{J}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sideset{{}^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}}{}{\sum}_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}^{1-t}\mu_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\bm{x}),\quad\left(\beta_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}m^{t^{*}}\prod_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{tk},\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R},h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}},\forall j\in[J]\right),

where
Z_{tk}
is the normalization coefficient of
\bm{q}
in (
29
) with
u_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}y_{i}h_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H^{(\nicefrac{{1}}{{1-t}})}_{J}(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{J}Z^{2-t}_{tj}.

(53)

II.II.2.4
Geometric convergence of the empirical risk

To get the right-hand side of (
51
) and (
53
) as small as possible, we can independently compute each
\mu_{j}
so as to minimize

\displaystyle Z^{2-t}_{tj}(\mu)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[m]}\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(\log_{t}q_{ji}-\mu u_{ji}\right).

(54)

We proceed in two steps, first computing a convenient upperbound for (
54
), and then finding the
\mu
that minimizes this upperbound.

Step 1
: We distinguish two cases depending on weight
q_{ji}
. Let
[m]_{j}^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\{i:q_{ji}>0\}
and
[m]_{j}^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\{i:q_{ji}=0\}
:

Case 1

i\in[m]_{j}^{+}
. Let
r_{ji}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}u_{ji}/q_{ji}^{1-t}
and suppose
R_{j}>0
is a real that satisfies

\displaystyle|r_{ji}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R_{j},\forall i\in[m]_{j}^{+}.

(55)

For any convex function
f
defined on
[-1,1]
, we have
f(z)\leq((1+z)/2)\cdot f(1)+((1-z)/2)\cdot f(-1),\forall z\in[-1,1]
(the straight line is the chord crossing
f
at
z=-1,1
). Because
z\mapsto[1-z]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}
is convex for
t\leq 2
, for any
i\in[m]_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(\log_{t}q_{ji}-\mu u_{ji}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[q_{ji}^{1-t}-(1-t)\mu u_{ji}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{ji}^{2-t}\cdot\left[1-(1-t)\mu R_{j}\cdot\frac{r_{ji}}{R_{j}}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle q_{ji}^{2-t}\cdot\frac{R_{j}+r_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1-(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}+q_{ji}^{2-t}\cdot\frac{R_{j}-r_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1+(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{ji}^{2-t}R_{j}+q_{ji}u_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1-(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}+\frac{q_{ji}^{2-t}R_{j}-q_{ji}u_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1+(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}.

Case 2

i\in[m]_{j}^{\dagger}
. Let
{q^{\dagger}_{j}}>0
be a real that satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{|u_{ji}|}{{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{1-t}}<R_{j},\forall i\in[m]_{j}^{\dagger}.

(56)

Using the same technique as in case 1, we find for any
i\in[m]_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(\log_{t}q_{ji}-\mu u_{ji}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp^{2-t}_{t}\left(-\frac{1}{1-t}-\mu u_{ji}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[-(1-t)\mu u_{ji}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{1-t}-(1-t)\mu u_{ji}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}R_{j}+{q^{\dagger}_{j}}u_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1-(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}+\frac{{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}R_{j}-{q^{\dagger}_{j}}u_{ji}}{2R_{j}}\left[1+(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}.

Folding both cases into one and letting

\displaystyle{q^{\prime}}_{ji}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}q_{ji}&\mbox{ if }&i\in[m]_{j}^{+}\\
{q^{\dagger}_{j}}&\mbox{ if }&i\in[m]_{j}^{\dagger}\end{array}\right.,

we get after summation, using
m_{j}^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Card}([m]_{j}^{\dagger})
and

\displaystyle\rho_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t})R_{j}}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}{q^{\prime}}_{ji}u_{ji}\quad(\in[-1,1]),

(60)

that

\displaystyle Z^{2-t}_{tj}(\mu)

(61)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t})R_{j}}{2R_{j}}\cdot\left((1+\rho_{j})\left[1-(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}+(1-\rho_{j})\left[1+(1-t)\mu R_{j}\right]^{\frac{2-t}{1-t}}_{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}}{2}\cdot\left((1+\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\mu R_{j}\right)+(1-\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(\mu R_{j}\right)\right).

Step 2
: we have our upperbound for (
54
). We now compute the minimizer
\mu^{*}
of (
61
). If this minimizer satisfies

\displaystyle|\mu^{*}|

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{j}|1-t|},

(62)

then it can be found by ordinary differentiation, as the solution to

\displaystyle(1-\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}\left(\mu^{*}R_{j}\right)-(1+\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}\left(-\mu^{*}R_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

which is equivalently

\displaystyle\frac{\exp_{t}\left(-\mu^{*}R_{j}\right)}{\exp_{t}\left(\mu^{*}R_{j}\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp_{t}\left(-\mu^{*}R_{j}\ominus_{t}\mu^{*}R_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1-\rho_{j}}{1+\rho_{j}},

where we recall
a\ominus_{t}b\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}(a-b)/(1+(1-t)b)
. Solving it yields

\displaystyle\mu^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{j}}\cdot-\frac{1}{1-t}\cdot\left(\frac{(1-\rho_{j})^{1-t}-(1+\rho_{j})^{1-t}}{(1-\rho_{j})^{1-t}+(1+\rho_{j})^{1-t}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R_{j}}\cdot-\frac{1}{1-t}\cdot\left(\frac{2(1-\rho_{j})^{1-t}}{(1-\rho_{j})^{1-t}+(1+\rho_{j})^{1-t}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{R_{j}}\cdot\log_{t}\left(\frac{1-\rho_{j}}{M_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}\right),

where
M_{q}(a,b)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}((a^{q}+b^{q})/2)^{1/q}
is the power mean with exponent
q
. We now check (
62
).

Lemma G
.

For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let

\displaystyle\mu_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{R_{j}}\cdot\log_{t}\left(\frac{1-\rho_{j}}{M_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}\right).

(63)

Then
|\mu_{j}|\leq 1/(R_{j}|1-t|)
.

Proof.

Equivalently, we must show

\displaystyle\left|\log_{t}\left(\frac{1-z}{M_{1-t}(1-z,1+z)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|1-t|},\forall z\in[-1,1],

which is equivalent to showing

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2(1-z)^{1-t}}{(1-z)^{1-t}+(1+z)^{1-t}}-1\right|\left(=\left|\frac{1-\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)^{1-t}}{1+\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)^{1-t}}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1,\forall z\in[-1,1].

Define function
f(z,t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{.}}}{{=}}(1-z^{1-t})/(1+z^{1-t})
over
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}
: it is easy to check that for
t\leq 1,f(z,t)\in[-1,1]
, and the symmetry
f(z,t)=-f(z,2-t)
also allows to conclude that for
t\geq 1,f(z,t)\in[-1,1]
. This ends the proof of Lemma
G
.
∎

For the expression of
\mu_{j}
in (
63
), we get from (
61
) the upperbound on
Z^{2-t}_{tj}(\mu_{j})
:

\displaystyle Z^{2-t}_{tj}(\mu_{j})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}}{2}\cdot\left((1+\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(-\mu_{j}R_{j}\right)+(1-\rho_{j})\cdot\exp_{t}^{2-t}\left(\mu_{j}R_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{(1+\rho_{j})(1-\rho_{j})^{2-t}}{M^{2-t}_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}+\frac{(1-\rho_{j})(1+\rho_{j})^{2-t}}{M^{2-t}_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}\right)\cdot\frac{(1-\rho^{2}_{j})M^{1-t}_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}{M^{2-t}_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1+m_{j}^{\dagger}{q^{\dagger}_{j}}^{2-t}\right)\cdot\frac{1-\rho^{2}_{j}}{M_{1-t}(1-\rho_{j},1+\rho_{j})}.

We conclude that for both sets of classifiers defined in Lemmata
E
and
F
, with the choice of
\mu_{j}
in (
63
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[m]}\llbracket\mathrm{sign}(H(\bm{x}_{i}))\neq y_{i}\rrbracket

Implementation details of

First, regarding file format, we only input a

. We do not specify a file with feature types as in ARFF files.
t

AdaBoost
recognizes the type of each feature from its column content and distinguishes two main types of features: numerical and categorical. The distinction is important to design the splits during decision tree induction: for numerical values, splits are midpoints between two successive observed values. For categorical, splits are partitions of the feature values in two non-empty subsets. Our implementation of
t
-
AdaBoost
(programmed in Java) makes it possible to choose

not just in the range of values for which we have shown that boosting-compliant convergence is possible (

(

because in theory this should never happen unless the weak classifiers achieve perfect (or perfectly wrong) classification), but also making sure leveraging coefficients for classifiers do not become infinite for
AdaBoost
, a situation that can happen because of numerical approximations in encoding. In our experiments, we have observed that none of these problematic cases did occur (notice that this could not be the case if we were to boost for a large number of iterations). We have implemented algorithm
t
-
AdaBoost
exactly as specified in
mf
. The weak learner is implemented to train a decision tree in which the stopping criterion is the size of the tree reaching a user-fixed number of nodes. There is thus no pruning. Also, the top-down induction algorithm proceeds by iteratively picking the heaviest leaf in the tree and then choosing the split that minimizes the expected Bayes risk of the tempered loss, computing using the same
t
values as for
t
-
AdaBoost
, and with the constraint to not get pure leaves (otherwise, the real prediction at the leaves, which relies on the link of the loss, would be infinite for
AdaBoost
