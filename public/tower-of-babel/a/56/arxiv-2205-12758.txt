Periodic perturbations of a class of scalar second order functional differential equations Thanks: The authors are members of the Gruppo Nazionale per l’Analisi Matematica, la Probabilità e le loro Applicazioni (GNAMPA) of the Istituto Nazionale di Alta Matematica (INdAM)
Alessandro Calamai, Maria Patrizia Pera, Marco Spadini

Abstract
. We study, by means of a topological approach, the forced oscillations of second order functional retarded differential equations subject to periodic perturbations. We consider a delay-type functional dependence involving a gamma probability distribution. By a linear chain trick we obtain a first order system of ODE’s whose T T -periodic solutions correspond to those of the functional equation.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
34K13, 34C25

1.
Introduction and setting of the problem

In this paper we investigate the forced oscillations of a class of second order functional retarded differential equations subject to periodic perturbations.
Namely, we study perturbations of
scalar, second order functional differential equations
of the following form:

(1)

\ddot{x}(t)=g\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{b}(t-s)\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right),

where
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous map and
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz.
The integral kernel
\gamma_{a}^{b}
, for
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
is the gamma probability distribution

(2)

\gamma_{a}^{b}(s)=\frac{a^{b}s^{b-1}\mathrm{e}^{-as}}{(b-1)!}\;\;\text{for $s\geq 0$},\qquad\gamma_{a}^{b}(s)=0\;\;\text{for $s<0$,}

with mean
b/a
and variance
b/a^{2}
.

The particular dependence on the past of the solution that is considered here is one that naturally arises in many contexts (not necessarily for second order equations) see, e.g.,
[
5
,
8
,
17
,
18
,
28
,
29
]
. In the equation we examine, the delay is spread along the whole history but more concentrated at a given point in the past. We can interpret this, from a probabilistic point of view, as a delay that follows a gamma-type distribution with a given mean (and variance). This approach seems to be reasonable in contexts when the delay cannot be measured with precision, but its mean value and variance are known, or it is genuinely spread in time as, e.g., in
[
14
,
15
]
.
Notice, in particular, that letting
a
and
b
tend to infinity in such a way that the quotient
r:=a/b
remains constant (for instance, put
a=nr
and
b=n
and let
n\to\infty
), one concentrates the memory effect close to the delay
r
. Indeed, at least in the sense of distributions, (
1
) approximates the second order delay differential equation

\ddot{x}(t)=g\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),\varphi\big(x(t-r),\dot{x}(t-r)\big)\right).

Observe that the function
\gamma_{a}^{b}
defined in (
2
) is continuous for
b=2,3,\ldots
but not for
b=1
. However, also in the latter case, assuming
x
in
C^{1}
, the function
t\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{1}(t-s)\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s
is continuous. Then the right hand side of (
1
) is continuous as well; consequently, any
C^{1}
solution of (
1
) is actually of class
C^{2}
.
It is also worth noticing (and we will use this fact later) that, since in the integral one has
t-s\geq 0
, then

\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{1}(t-s)\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-\infty}^{t}a\mathrm{e}^{-a(t-s)}\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s,

so that the function
t\mapsto\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{1}(t-s)\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s
is actually in
C^{1}
.

Given
T>0
, we consider the following
T
-periodic perturbation of (
1
):

(3)

\ddot{x}(t)=g\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{b}(t-s)\varphi(x(s),\dot{x}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+\lambda f\big(t,x(t),\dot{x}(t)\big),

in which we assume that the map
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and
T
-periodic in the first variable, and
\lambda
is a nonnegative real parameter. Our main purpose is to investigate the set of
T
-periodic solutions of (
3
).
Here, given
\lambda\geq 0
, by a
T
-periodic solution
(on
\mathbb{R}
) of (
3
) we mean a
C^{2}
function
x\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of period
T
that satisfies identically equality (
3
).

Roughly speaking, we explicitly construct a scalar function
\Phi
whose change of sign implies the existence of a connected set, called a
“branch”
, of nontrivial pairs
(\lambda,x)
– with
x
a
T
-periodic solution of (
3
) corresponding to
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
– that emanates out of the set of zeros of
\Phi
and whose closure is not compact.

We also give sufficient conditions yielding the multiplicity of
T
-periodic solutions of (
3
) for
\lambda>0
small. Such conditions are essentially based upon the notion of
ejecting set
(see, e.g.,
[
22
]
).

The methods that we employ are topological in nature and are based on the Brouwer degree. A familiarity with this notion, however, is not necessary to use our main results since its application is restricted only to the proofs. Nevertheless, we provide a brief summary.

Our results are so-to-speak dual to those of
[
10
]
,
where periodic perturbations containing delay terms are applied to
scalar, second order ODE’s.
The study, by means of topological methods, of the branching and multiplicity of periodic solutions of periodically perturbed equations, is now a well-investigated subject in the case of ODE’s both in Euclidean spaces and on manifolds
(see, e.g.,
[
21
,
24
]
).
The case of ODE’s perturbed with delayed forcing terms is also studied in the literature, although not so broadly (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
11
]
).
However, the presence of delay terms in the
unperturbed
equation (
1
) is peculiar of the problem addressed here. In spite of the apparent similarities, a different approach is called upon in order to manage the unperturbed equation.

In the undelayed case, that is for periodically forced second-order scalar autonomous ODE’s, both
the problems of existence and multiplicity of periodic solutions are quite classical.
However, this is still the subject of active research by the mathematical community.
There are many approaches that have been successfully pursued to get multiplicity results: among the others let us mention here, e.g., the recent contributions
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
19
]
,
the survey papers
[
25
,
26
]
as well as the monograph
[
20
]
and the references therein.

The strategy adopted in this paper is inspired to
[
30
]
and can be summarized as follows. First we construct a system of
b+2
,
T
-periodically perturbed first-order ordinary differential equations whose
T
-periodic solutions correspond to that of (
3
). We then use known results about these perturbed systems to ensure the existence of a branch as sought when the topological degree of the unperturbed field
G
has nonzero degree and finally, we show, by homotopy techniques, that the change of sign of
\Phi
imply that
G
has nonzero degree.

It should be noted that the result just described does not guarantee the existence of forced oscillations even for very small values of
\lambda
. For this reason, we also provide a nonlocal condition, based on an inequality of J. A. Yorke
[
31
]
, implying that the branch projects nontrivially onto
[0,\infty)
.

In order to develop a better understanding of the nature of the branch of
T
-periodic solutions and its relation with multiplicity results we discuss, following
[
2
]
and
[
30
]
, a method to visualize, in finite dimension, a homemorphic set that retains all the relevant properties. We illustrate the procedure with a numeric example.

2.
The linear chain trick

As pointed out in the introduction, a crucial remark is that the
T
-periodic solutions of (
3
) and those of the following system of
b+2
ordinary differential equations correspond in some sense:

(4)

\dot{\xi}=G(\xi)+\lambda F(t,\xi),

where
\lambda\geq 0
,
\xi=(u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})\in\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
, and the maps

G\colon\mathbb{R}^{b+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
and

F\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{b+2}\to\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
are respectively defined as

(5)

\begin{split}G(u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=\>&\\
\Big(v_{0},g(u,v_{0},v_{b})&,a\big(\varphi(u,v_{0})-v_{1}\big),a(v_{1}-v_{2})\ldots,a(v_{b-1}-v_{b})\Big)\end{split}

and

F(t,u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=\Big(0,f(t,u,v_{0}),\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{\text{$b$ times}}\Big),

with
g
,
\varphi
and
f
as in equation (
3
).
Clearly,
G
and
F
are continuous maps. By a
T
-periodic solution (on
\mathbb{R}
) of system (
4
) we mean a
C^{1}
function
\xi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
of period
T
that satisfies (
4
) identically.

In our main results we will be concerned with the Brouwer degree of the map
G

(see Section
4
for details).
Our first task will be to derive a formula for the computation of the degree of
G
in terms of the real-valued function
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

(6)

\Phi(u)=g(u,0,\varphi(u,0)).

Remark 2.1
.

Observe that if
(\bar{u},v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})\in G^{-1}(0)
then
\Phi(\bar{u})=0
,
v_{0}=0
and
v_{1}=v_{2}=\ldots=v_{b}=\varphi(\bar{u},0)
. Conversely, for any
\bar{u}\in\Phi^{-1}(0)
, then
G(\bar{u},0,\varphi(\bar{u},0)\ldots,\varphi(\bar{u},0))=0
.

Given a bounded open interval
(\alpha,\beta)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, define the open subset
W^{*}=(\alpha,\beta)\times\mathbb{R}^{b+1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
. We have:

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that
\Phi(\alpha)\cdot\Phi(\beta)<0
.
Then
G
is admissible for the degree in
W^{*}
and we have
\deg(G,W^{*})\neq 0
.

The proof of Theorem
2.2
is postponed to Section
4
.

Let us show now how a “linear chain trick”
(see, e.g.,
[
29
,
30
]
) can be used to prove the correspondence between
T
-periodic solutions of the second order equation (
3
) and of the first order system (
4
).

Let us introduce some notation. By
C_{T}^{n}(\mathbb{R}^{s})
,
n=0,1,2
, we will denote the Banach space of the
T
-periodic
C^{n}
maps
x\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
with the the standard norm

\|x\|_{C^{n}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\max_{t\in\mathbb{R}}|x^{(i)}(t)|.

Here,
x^{(i)}
denotes the
i
-th derivative of
x
, in particular
x^{(0)}
coincides with
x
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
x_{0}
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
3
), and let

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{0}(t):=\dot{x}_{0}(t),\\
y_{i}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad i=1,\ldots,b\end{array}\right.

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,
(x_{0},y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{b})
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
4
).

Proof.

First notice that, since
x_{0}\in C_{T}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous (we are actually assuming it to be locally Lipschitz), it is not difficult to prove that the functions
y_{i}
,
i=0,\ldots,b
, are in
C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

Moreover, we claim that, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}_{0}(t)=y_{0}(t),\\
\dot{y}_{0}(t)=g\big(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t),y_{b}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t)\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0,\\
\dot{y}_{1}(t)=a\big(\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))-y_{1}(t)),\\
\dot{y}_{i}(t)=a\big(y_{i-1}(t)-y_{i}(t)\big),\quad i=2,\ldots,b.\end{array}\right.

Indeed, the first equality follows by definition.
Since
x_{0}
is a solution of (
3
), for
\lambda\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\dot{y}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\ddot{x}_{0}(t)=g\left(x_{0}(t),\dot{x}_{0}(t),\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{b}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),\dot{x}_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+\lambda f\big(t,x_{0}(t),\dot{x}_{0}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=g\big(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t),y_{b}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x_{0}(t)y_{0}(t)\big),\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

Now observe that, when
i=1
, we have for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

y_{1}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{1}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-\infty}^{t}a\mathrm{e}^{a(t-s)}\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, taking the derivative under the integral sign,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\,y_{1}(t)=\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))-a\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{a(t-s)}\,\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

so that

\dot{y}_{1}(t)=a\big(\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))-y_{1}(t)\big).

Also, for
i=2,\ldots,b
, we get for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\,y_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{a}^{i}(0)\,\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))+\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\phantom{t}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\,\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\,\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}a\left(\gamma_{a}^{i-1}(t-s)-\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\right)\,\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=a\left(\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i-1}(t-s)\,\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s-\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\,\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=a\left(y_{i-1}(t)-y_{i}(t)\right)

proving our claim.

Finally, to see that
(x_{0},y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{b})
is
T
-periodic, observe that so are
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
, and with the change of variable
\sigma=s-T
we get

\displaystyle y_{i}(t+T)=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t+T}\gamma_{a}^{i}(T+t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-\sigma)\varphi(x_{0}(\sigma+T),y_{0}(\sigma+T))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-\sigma)\varphi(x_{0}(\sigma),y_{0}(\sigma))\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=y_{i}(t),

for
i=1,\ldots,b
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and this completes the proof.
∎

Conversely, we have the following:

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose that
(x_{0},y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{b})
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
4
), then
x_{0}
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
3
).

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is based on the following technical lemma on linear systems of ODEs, see
[
30
, Lemma 3.3]
,
cf. also
[
29
, Prop. 7.3]
.
The proof is omitted.

Lemma 2.5

(
[
30
]
, Lemma 3.3)
.

Given any continuous and bounded function

z_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

and any nonzero number
a
,
there exists a unique
C^{1}
solution
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{b}),\,z_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\,i=1,\ldots,b,

of the system in
\mathbb{R}^{b}

\dot{z}_{i}(t)=a\big(z_{i-1}(t)-z_{i}(t)\big),

which is bounded in the
C^{1}
norm. This solution is given by

z_{i}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)z_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad i=1,\ldots,b.

Remark 2.6
.

Observe in particular that, for
i=1
in the previous lemma, one has

z_{1}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{1}(t-s)z_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=a\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{a(t-s)}z_{0}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

so that
z_{1}
is actually a
C^{1}
function, and thus so are all the
z_{i}
’s for
i=2,\ldots,b
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Let
(x_{0},y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{b})
be a
T
-periodic solution of (
4
), and define
z_{0}(t)=\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
z_{0}
is bounded and (at least) continuous.
Thus, by Lemma
2.5
,

y_{i}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\quad i=1,\ldots,b,

is the unique solution of class
C^{1}
of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{y}_{1}(t)=a\big(\varphi(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t))-y_{1}(t)),\\
\dot{y}_{i}(t)=a\big(y_{i-1}(t)-y_{i}(t)\big),\quad i=2,\ldots,b.\end{array}\right.

In particular, we have

y_{b}(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{b}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s.

Thus, from (
4
),

\ddot{x}_{0}(t)=\dot{y}_{0}(t)=g\big(x_{0}(t),y_{0}(t),y_{b}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x_{0}(t)y_{0}(t)\big)=\\
=g\left(x_{0}(t),\dot{x}_{0}(t),\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{b}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),\dot{x}_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s\right)+\lambda f\big(t,x_{0}(t),\dot{x}_{0}(t)\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, whence the assertion.
∎

3.
Branches of
T
-pairs

This section investigates the structure of the set of
T
-periodic solutions of (
3
). We begin by recalling some notation and basic facts.

Consider the following first-order parameterized ODE on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
:

(7)

\dot{x}(t)=\mathfrak{g}\big(x(t)\big)+\lambda\mathfrak{f}\big(t,x(t)\big),

where
\lambda\geq 0
, the maps

\mathfrak{g}\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

\mathfrak{f}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
are continuous
and
\mathfrak{f}
is
T
-periodic in the first variable.

We say that a pair
(\lambda,x)\in[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
is a
T
-pair
(for (
7
)) if
x
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
7
) corresponding to
\lambda
. If
\lambda=0
and
x
is constant then the
T
-pair is said
trivial
. It is not hard to see that the trivial
T
-periodic pairs of (
7
) correspond to the zeros of
\mathfrak{g}
.
From now on, given any
p\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we will denote by
\overline{p}
the constant map
t\mapsto p
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Given an open subset
\mathcal{O}
of
[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, we will denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
the open set

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}:=\big\{p:(0,\overline{p})\in\mathcal{O}\big\}
.

We have the following fact concerning the
T
-pairs of (
7
):

Theorem 3.1

(
[
23
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be open in
[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, and assume that
\deg(\mathfrak{g},\widetilde{\mathcal{O}})
is well defined and nonzero. Then there exists a connected set
\Gamma
of nontrivial
T
-pairs in
[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
whose closure in
\mathcal{O}
is not compact and meets the set of trivial
T
-pairs contained in
\mathcal{O}
, namely the set:
\big\{(0,\overline{p})\in\mathcal{O}:\mathfrak{g}(p)=0\big\}
.

Let us now go back to the second-order
equation (
3
).
We need some further notation.
The pairs
(\lambda,x)\in[0,+\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})
,
with
x\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a
T
-periodic solution
of (
3
) corresponding to
\lambda
, will be called
T
-forced pairs
(of (
3
)), and we denote by
X
the set of these pairs.
Among the
T
-forced pairs we shall consider as
trivial
those of the type
(0,x)
with
x
constant.
Given an open subset
\Omega
of
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})
, we will denote by
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\big\{u\in\mathbb{R}:(0,\overline{u})\in\Omega\big\}
, where by
\overline{u}
we mean the constant map
t\mapsto u
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

As pointed out in Section
2
, there is a correspondence between
T
-periodic solutions of the second order equation (
3
) and of the first order system in (
4
). A similar correspondence holds between their “
T
-periodic pairs” in a sense that we are going to specify. We point out that, since equation (
4
) can be seen as a special case of (
7
), in accordance with the notation introduced above,
a pair
(\lambda,\xi)\in[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{b+2})
is called a
T
-pair
if
\xi
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
4
) corresponding to
\lambda
. We also recall that the set of
T
-forced pairs of (
3
) is regarded as a subset of
[0,+\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})
.

As a first remark we relate the corresponding “trivial
T
-periodic pairs”.

Remark 3.2
.

Observe that if
(0,\overline{q})
, with
q\in\mathbb{R}
, is a trivial
T
-forced pair of (
3
), then
(0,\overline{\xi_{0}})
is a
T
-pair for equation (
4
), where
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
is given by

\xi_{0}:=(q,0,\varphi(q,0)\ldots,\varphi(q,0))

that is, by Remark
2.1
,
\xi_{0}\in G^{-1}(0)
.
Conversely, any trivial
T
-pair of (
4
)
must be of the form
(0,\overline{\xi_{0}})
,
where
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
is such that
G(\xi_{0})=0
.
Thus, again by Remark
2.1
, we have
\xi_{0}=(q,0,\varphi(q,0)\ldots,\varphi(q,0))
for some
q\in\mathbb{R}
, and consequently
(0,\overline{q})
is a trivial
T
-forced pair for (
4
).

Let us now establish a general correspondence between the sets of
T
-forced pairs and of
T
-pairs, that preserves the notion of triviality.

Let
\mathcal{J}\colon[0,+\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{b+2})
be the map defined as follows:

(8)

\mathcal{J}:(\lambda,x_{0})\mapsto(\lambda,\xi)

where
\xi:=(x_{0},y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{b})
is given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}y_{0}(t):=\dot{x}_{0}(t),\\
y_{i}(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\gamma_{a}^{i}(t-s)\varphi(x_{0}(s),y_{0}(s))\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad i=1,\ldots,b.\end{array}\right.

4.
Computation of the degree

4.1.
Brouwer degree in Euclidean spaces

We will make use of the Brouwer degree in
\mathbb{R}^{k}

in a slightly extended version (see e.g.
[
1
,
16
,
27
]
)

Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
f
a continuous
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued map whose domain contains the closure
\overline{U}
of
U
, and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We say that the triple
(f,U,q)
is
admissible (for the Brouwer degree)
if
f^{-1}(q)\cap U
is compact.

The Brouwer degree is a function that to any admissible triple
(f,U,q)
assigns an integer, denoted by
\deg(f,U,q)
and called the
Brouwer degree of
f
in
U
with target
q
.
Roughly speaking,
\deg(f,U,q)
is an algebraic count of the solutions in
U
of the equation
f(p)=q
.
In fact, one of the properties of this integer-valued function is given by the following
computation formula.

Recall that, if
f\colon U\to\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
is a
C^{1}
map, an element
p\in U
is said to be a
regular point
(of
f
) if the differential of
f
at
p
,
df_{p}
, is surjective.
Non-regular points are called
critical (points)
.
The
critical values
of
f
are those points of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
which lie in the image
f(C)
of the set
C
of critical points.
Any
q\in\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
which is not in
f(C)
is a
regular value
.
Therefore, in particular, any element of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
which is not in the image of
f
is a regular value.

Computation formula.

If
(f,U,q)
is admissible,
f
is smooth, and
q

is a regular value for
f
in
U
, then

(10)

\deg(f,U,q)\;=\sum_{p\in f^{-1}(q)\cap U}\sign(df_{p}).

This formula is actually the basic definition of the Brouwer degree, and the integer associated to any admissible triple
(g,U,r)
is defined by

(11)

\deg(g,U,r):=\deg(f,U,q),

where
f
and
q
satisfy the assumptions of the Computation Formula and are, respectively, “sufficiently close” to
g
and
r
.
It is known that this is a well-posed definition.

The more classical and well-known definition of Brouwer degree is usually given in the subclass of triples
(f,U,q)
such that
f\colon\overline{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is continuous,
U
is bounded and
q\notin f(\partial U)
.
However, all the standard properties of the degree, such as homotopy invariance, excision, additivity, existence, are still valid in this more general context.

Since in this paper the target point
q
will always be the origin, for the sake of simplicity, we will simply write
\deg(f,U)
instead of
\deg(f,U,0)
.
In this context, we will say that an element
p\in f^{-1}(0)
is a
nondegenerate zero
(of
f
) if
\det(df_{p})\neq 0
; this means, equivalently, that
p
is a regular point. Observe that
\deg(f,U)
can be regarded also as the degree (or characteristic, or rotation) of the map
f
seen as a tangent vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

4.2.
The degree of the map
G

The last part of this section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
Roughly speaking we will relate the degree of the map
G
, defined in
(
5
) by

G(u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=\left(v_{0},g(u,v_{0},v_{b}),a(\varphi(u,v_{0})-v_{1}),a(v_{1}-v_{2})\ldots,a(v_{b-1}-v_{b})\right),

with that of the function
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
in (
6
), given by

\Phi(u)=g(u,0,\varphi(u,0)).

To simplify the computation of the degree, it is convenient to introduce the following map on
\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
:

\mathcal{G}(u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b}):=\left(v_{0},g(u,v_{0},v_{b}),a(\varphi(u,v_{0})-v_{b}),a(v_{1}-v_{2})\ldots,a(v_{b-1}-v_{b})\right)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
and
\mathcal{G}
be as above, and let
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{b+2}
be open. Suppose that one of the maps
G
or
\mathcal{G}
is admissible for the Brouwer degree in
V
; then, so is the other and they are admissibly homotopic in
V
.

Proof.

For
(\lambda,u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})\in[0,1]\times V
, consider the map

\mathcal{H}(\lambda,u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=\\
=\left(v_{0},g(u,v_{0},v_{b}),a(\varphi(u,v_{0})-[\lambda v_{b}+(1-\lambda)v_{1}]),a(v_{1}-v_{2})\ldots,a(v_{b-1}-v_{b})\right),

and observe that
\mathcal{H}(\lambda,u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=0
if and only if
(u,v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})\in V\cap G^{-1}(0)=V\cap\mathcal{G}^{-1}(0)
. Hence
\mathcal{H}
is an admissible homotopy.
∎

Now we prove that if the zeros of
\Phi
are
nondegenerate, so are those of
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

All zeros of
\Phi
are nondegenerate if and only if all zeros of
\mathcal{G}
are nondegenerate.

Proof.

First observe that, as in Remark
2.1
,
the zeros of
\mathcal{G}
and those of
\Phi
correspond.
In fact, if
\mathcal{G}(\bar{u},v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{b})=0
then
\Phi(\bar{u})=0
,
v_{0}=0
and
v_{1}=v_{2}=\ldots=v_{b}=\varphi(\bar{u},0)=:\bar{w}
.
Conversely, for any
\bar{u}\in\Phi^{-1}(0)
, then
\mathcal{G}(\bar{u},0,\bar{w}\ldots,\bar{w})=0
.

Assume now that
\bar{\xi}:=(\bar{u},0,\bar{w}\ldots,\bar{w})
is a nondegenerate zero of
\mathcal{G}
. We compute the Jacobian matrix of
\mathcal{G}
at
\bar{\xi}
as follows:

J\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\xi}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc|ccccc|c}0&1&0&0&\ldots&\ldots&0&0\\
\partial_{1}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})&\partial_{2}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})&0&0&\ldots&\ldots&0&\partial_{3}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})\\
a\,\partial_{1}\varphi(\bar{u},0)&a\,\partial_{2}\varphi(\bar{u},0)&0&0&\ldots&\ldots&0&-a\\
\hline\cr 0&0&a&-a&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&0&0&a&-a&\ddots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&\ldots&0&a&-a\end{array}\right)\;.

By direct computation based on Laplace expansion we obtain

\det J\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\xi}}=(-a)^{b-1}\,\det\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
\partial_{1}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})&\partial_{2}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})&\partial_{3}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})\\
a\partial_{1}\varphi(\bar{u},0)&a\partial_{2}\varphi(\bar{u},0)&-a\end{pmatrix}=

=-(-a)^{b-1}\,\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})&\partial_{3}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})\\
a\partial_{1}\varphi(\bar{u},0)&-a\end{pmatrix}=

=(-1)^{b-1}a^{b}\cdot\left[\partial_{1}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})+\partial_{3}g(\bar{u},0,\bar{w})\,\partial_{1}\varphi(\bar{u},0)\right]=(-1)^{b-1}a^{b}\cdot\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{u}).

Thus
\bar{u}
is a nondegenerate zero of the function
\Phi
.
The proof of the converse implication is analogous.
∎

Let us now compute the degree of the map
\mathcal{G}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume
\Phi
is admissible on an open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and all its zeros are nondegenerate, then
\mathcal{G}
is admissible in
U^{*}=U\times\mathbb{R}^{b+1}
, and

\deg(\mathcal{G},U^{*})=(-1)^{b-1}\deg(\Phi,U).

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
4.2
we have that all zeros of
\mathcal{G}
are of the form
\bar{\xi}:=(\bar{u},0,\bar{w}\ldots,\bar{w})
with
\Phi(\bar{u})=0
. Moreover, they are all nondegenerate.
In particular, if
\Phi
is admissible in
U
then
\Phi^{-1}(0)\cap U
is compact and so is
\mathcal{G}^{-1}(0)\cap U^{*}
, whence the admissibility of
\mathcal{G}
in
U^{*}
.
Let now
\bar{u}\in\Phi^{-1}(0)\cap U
be a nondegenerate zero of
\Phi
.
As in the proof of Lemma
4.2
we have,

\det J\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\xi}}=(-1)^{b-1}a^{b}\cdot\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{u})

and, consequently,

\sign\det\left(d\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\xi}}\right)=(-1)^{b-1}\sign\det\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{u})

Thus, by formula (
10
),

\deg(\mathcal{G},U^{*})=\sum_{\bar{\xi}\in\mathcal{G}^{-1}(0)\cap U^{*}}\sign\det\left(d\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\xi}}\right)=\\
=\sum_{\bar{u}\in\Phi^{-1}(0)\cap U}(-1)^{b-1}\sign\det\Phi^{\prime}(\bar{u})=(-1)^{b-1}\deg(\Phi,U),

whence the assertion.
∎

The above Lemmas imply now the assertion of Theorem
2.2
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be as in the assertion of Theorem
2.2
.
As in equality (
11
), we can assume that all zeros of
\Phi
in
(\alpha,\beta)
are nondegenerate. The assertion now follows from Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.1
.
∎

5.

As we have seen by the simple example at the end of Section

[

Let us first introduce the following notation: let
\Upsilon
be a subset of

\big\{x\in C_{T}^{1}(\mathbb{R}):(\lambda,x)\in\Upsilon\big\}

[

]

Definition 5.1
.

Let

T
-forced pairs of (

be the slice of

at

. In particular, when

is an

.

We now discuss a sufficient condition for an isolated point of

to be ejecting for the set

of
T
-forced pairs of (
3
). This condition is based on a result by J. Yorke (
[

]
, see also
[
6
]
) concerning the period of solutions of an autonomous ODE with Lipschitz continuous right-hand side.

We point out that an analysis of (
4
), for

, linearized at its zeros leads to a different, not entirely comparable, approach. A discussion of the latter technique, which is based on the notion of

-resonance and requires the knowledge of the spectrum of the linearized equation is outside the scope of the present paper (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Ch. 7]
and
[

[

]

]

Theorem 5.2

(

]
)

-forced pairs
of (

:

,

Assume by contradiction that there exists a nontrivial

.
