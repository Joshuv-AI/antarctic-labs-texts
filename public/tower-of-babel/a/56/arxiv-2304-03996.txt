A Unified Characterization of Private Learnability via Graph Theory
Noga Alon, Shay Moran, Hilla Schefler, Amir Yehudayoff

Abstract
We provide a unified framework for characterizing pure and approximate differentially private (DP) learnability. The framework uses the language of graph theory: for a concept class ℋ \mathcal{H} , we define the contradiction graph G G of ℋ \mathcal{H} . Its vertices are realizable datasets, and two datasets S , S ′ S,S^{\prime} are connected by an edge if they contradict each other (i.e., there is a point x x that is labeled differently in S S and S ′ S^{\prime} ). Our main finding is that the combinatorial structure of G G is deeply related to learning ℋ \mathcal{H} under DP. Learning ℋ \mathcal{H} under pure DP is captured by the fractional clique number of G G . Learning ℋ \mathcal{H} under approximate DP is captured by the clique number of G G . Consequently, we identify graph-theoretic dimensions that characterize DP learnability: the clique dimension and fractional clique dimension . Along the way, we reveal properties of the contradiction graph which may be of independent interest. We also suggest several open questions and directions for future research.

Introduction

Modern machine learning applications often involve handling sensitive data. Differential privacy (DP)
[
DMNS06
]
has emerged as a sound theoretical approach to reason about privacy in a precise and quantifiable fashion and has become the gold standard of statistical data privacy
[
DR14
]
. It has also been implemented in practice, notably by Google
[
EPK14
]
, Apple
[
app16b
,
app16a
]
, and in the 2020 US census
[
DLS+
]
.
These developments raise the question:

Which learning tasks can be performed subject to differential privacy?

Extensive research has been carried out on this question within the framework of the classical Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) model
[
VC68
,
Val84
]
,
leading to the development of various characterizations of private learnability.
Beimel, Nissim, and Stemmer introduced a quantity called the representation dimension that characterizes pure DP learnability
[
BNS13
,
BNS19
]
.
In a follow-up work, Feldman and Xiao found an interesting connection with communication complexity by associating every concept class
\mathcal{H}
with
a communication task whose complexity characterizes whether
\mathcal{H}
is pure DP learnable
[
FX15
]
.

Extensive research has also been devoted to studying the question of which learning tasks can be performed subject
to approximate differential privacy, which is comparatively less demanding than pure differential privacy.
Several characterizations of PAC learnability under this less restrictive version have been proven, including finite Littlestone dimension and online learnability
[
ALMM19
,
BLM20
,
ABL+22
]
,
replicability and reproducibility
[
ILPS22
,
BGH+23
]
,
low information complexity, PAC Bayes stability, and other variants of algorithmic stability
[
LM20
,
PNG22
]
.
For a more detailed discussion, please refer to
[
MM22
]
.

Our Contribution.

While the definitions of pure and approximate DP are closely related, the characterizations of learning under these distinct privacy constraints differ significantly.
In this work, we devise a unified approach for characterizing both pure and approximate DP learnability.
Our framework is based on graph theory; in particular, it demonstrates a tight link between private learnability and cliques in certain graphs, which we call
contradiction graphs
.

A clique in a graph
G
is a set
\delta
of vertices such that every pair of distinct vertices in
\delta

is connected by an edge. A fractional clique is a standard LP relaxation of a clique.
A function
\delta:V\to[0,1]
is a fractional clique if for every independent set
I\subseteq V
,

\sum_{v\in I}\delta(v)\leq 1.

The size of a fractional clique
\delta
is the sum
\sum_{v\in V}\delta(v)
. Notice that if
\delta(v)\in\{0,1\}
for all
v
then
\delta
is the indicator function of a clique,
and its size is the number of vertices in the clique.
The clique number of
G
, denoted
\omega(G)
, is the largest size of a clique in
G
. Similarly, the fractional clique number of
G
, denoted
\omega^{\star}(G)
, is the largest size of a fractional clique in
G
. Notice that
\omega^{\star}(G)\geq\omega(G)
.

Definition

(Contradiction Graph)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
be a concept class and let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. The
contradiction graph of order
m
of
\mathcal{H}
is an undirected graph
G_{m}=G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
whose vertices are datasets of size
m
that are consistent with
\mathcal{H}
. Two datasets are connected by an edge whenever they contradict each other.

In other words, the vertices of the contradiction graph
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
are
\mathcal{H}
-realizable sequences of length
m
, and
\{S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime}\}
is an edge if there is
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
(x,0)\in S^{\prime}
and
(x,1)\in S^{\prime\prime}
.
Let
\omega_{m}=\omega(G_{m})
and
\omega^{\star}_{m}=\omega^{\star}(G_{m})
denote the clique and fractional clique numbers of
G_{m}
.

1.

We prove that both
\omega_{m}
and
\omega^{\star}_{m}
satisfy a polynomial-exponential dichotomy:

(i)

For every
\mathcal{H}
, either
\omega_{m}^{\star}=2^{m}
for all
m
, or
\omega_{m}^{\star}\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
.

(ii)

For every
\mathcal{H}
, either
\omega_{m}=2^{m}
for all
m
, or
\omega_{m}\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
.

2.

These dichotomies characterize pure and approximate DP learnability:

(i)

\mathcal{H}
is pure DP learnable if and only if
\omega_{m}^{\star}\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
.

(ii)

\mathcal{H}
is approximately DP learnable if and only if
\omega_{m}\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
.

These characterizations yield graph-theoretic dimensions of
\mathcal{H}
that characterize private learning. Define the clique dimension of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted by
\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
, as the largest
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
for which
\omega_{m}=2^{m}
. Analogously, define the fractional clique dimension of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted by
\mathtt{CD}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
, as the largest
m
for which
\omega_{m}^{\star}=2^{m}
.
Thus,

(i)

\mathtt{CD}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
if and only if
\mathcal{H}
is pure DP learnable.

(ii)

\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
if and only if
\mathcal{H}
is approximately DP learnable.

Technically, our proofs rely on the fact that the fractional clique and chromatic numbers are equal.
For finite graphs, this fact follows from LP duality, however, in our setting the contradiction graph can be infinite.
Lastly, we prove that the contradiction graph exhibits strong duality: the fractional clique and chromatic numbers are equal for every (possibly infinite) contradiction graph.
This part is based on tools from functional analysis and topology.

Note that in this work we focus on providing a unified and concise framework to characterize and study private learning.
Important topics, such as regarding the informational or computational complexity are not addressed in this work.
Nevertheless, given that graph theory is a well-studied area with sophisticated tools and techniques,
we hope that the equivalence established here will facilitate a deeper integration between learning theory and graph theory.
Specifically, we anticipate that it will provide new tools and insights to tackle other fundamental questions in learning theory.

2.1
Dichotomies and Dimensions

In this section we present Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
which concern cliques and fractional cliques in the contradiction graph. These results are key in our characterizations of private learnability, but they might also be of independent interest as combinatorial results.

We begin with a basic lemma which shows that the contradiction graph does not contain cliques or fractional cliques of size larger than
2^{m}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class and
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\omega_{m}
and
\omega_{m}^{\star}
denote the clique and fractional clique numbers of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then,

\omega_{m}\leq\omega^{\star}_{m}\leq 2^{m}
.

A short proof of this lemma is provided in
Section

3.3
.

Theorem 2.2

(Clique Number)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class and let
\omega_{m}
denote the clique number of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, exactly one of the following statements holds:

1.

\omega_{m}=2^{m}
for all
m
.

2.

\omega_{m}\leq P(m)
for all
m
, where
P(m)
is a polynomial.

Theorem 2.3

(Fractional Clique Number)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class and let
\omega^{\star}_{m}
denote the fractional clique number of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
. Then, exactly one of the following statements holds:

1.

\omega^{\star}_{m}=2^{m}
for all
m
.

2.

\omega^{\star}_{m}\leq P(m)
for all
m
, where
P(m)
is a polynomial.

Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
motivate the following definitions.

Definition 2.4

(Clique Dimension)
.

The clique dimension of a concept class
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
, is defined as follows:

\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H}):=\sup\{m:\omega_{m}=2^{m}\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\},

where
\omega_{m}
is the clique number of the contradiction graph
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.

Definition 2.5

(Fractional Clique Dimension)
.

The fractional clique dimension of a concept class
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathtt{CD}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
, is defined as follows:

\mathtt{CD}^{\star}(\mathcal{H}):=\sup\{m:\omega_{m}^{\star}=2^{m}\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\},

where
\omega_{m}^{\star}
is the fractional clique number of the contradiction graph
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.

Thus, Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
demonstrate that the clique and fractional clique dimensions satisfy a dichotomy similar to the Sauer-Shelah-Perles (SSP) dichotomy of the VC dimension
[
Sau72
]
.
Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
are key in our characterizations of private PAC learnability.
This is analogous to the crucial role played by the SSP lemma in the characterization of PAC learnability.

It is worthwhile to note that the polynomial/exponential dichotomy in Theorem
2.3

is weaker than the one in Theorem
2.2
.
Specifically, in Theorem
2.2
the degree of the polynomial
P(m)
is the clique dimension
\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
(see Lemma
4.3
).
In contrast, our proof of Theorem
2.3
does not imply a bound on the degree of
P(m)
in terms of the fractional clique dimension. Rather, the implied bound depends on the difference
2^{m}-\omega^{\star}_{m}>0
,
where
m
is any integer for which this difference is positive (
m
exists by Lemma
2.1
).

We leave as an open question to determine whether the fractional clique number
\omega^{\star}_{m}
is upper bounded by a polynomial
P(m)
whose degree depends
only
on the fractional clique dimension.

2.1.1
Theorems
2.2
and
2.3
: Technical Overview

We begin with overviewing the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Assuming there exists a natural number
d
such that
\omega_{d}<2^{d}
, we need to show that
\lvert\delta\rvert\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
for every
m
and for every clique
\delta
in
G_{m}
.
The crux of the proof is to show that there exists a
balanced
instance
x_{1}
in the following sense:

\lvert\{S\in\delta:(x_{1},0)\in S\}\rvert\geq\Omega\Bigl(\frac{\lvert\delta\rvert}{m}\Bigr)\text{ and }\lvert\{S\in\delta:(x_{1},1)\in S\}\rvert\geq\Omega\Bigl(\frac{\lvert\delta\rvert}{m}\Bigr).

(1)

To see how
Equation

1
completes the proof, let
\mathcal{H}_{x\to y}=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(x)=y\}
.
Notice that at least one of
G_{d-1}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{1}\to 0})
,
G_{d-1}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{1}\to 1})
does not contain a clique of size
2^{d-1}
.
Indeed, if
\delta_{0},\delta_{1}
are cliques of size
2^{d-1}
in
G_{d-1}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{1}\to 0})
and
G_{d-1}(\mathcal{H}_{x_{1}\to 1})
, then they can be combined to form a clique of size
2^{d}
in
G_{d}(\mathcal{H})
by adding the example
(x_{1},0)
to every dataset in
\delta_{0}
and the example
(x_{1},1)
to every dataset in
\delta_{1}
, and taking their union.

Repeating this argument
d-1
times, we obtain a class
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}_{x_{1}\to y_{1},\ldots x_{d-1}\to y_{d-1}}
such that (i)
G_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
does not contain a clique of size
2
and (ii) there is a clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
whose size is at least
{\lvert\delta\rvert}/{(c\cdot m)^{d-1}}
for some constant
c
. On the other hand, the first item means that
\lvert\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\rvert=1
and hence every clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
has size
1
. Thus,
1\geq{\lvert\delta\rvert}/{(c\cdot m)^{d-1}}
, which implies that
\lvert\delta\rvert\leq(c\cdot m)^{d-1}=\mathtt{poly}(m)
as required.

We prove that there exists a balanced instance (i.e. that satisfies
Equation

1
)
constructively using a greedy procedure (see Lemma
4.2
for the short proof).

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is more involved and it integrates different ideas and techniques such as LP duality, probabilistic arguments, and regret analysis from online learning.

Our objective is to demonstrate that if there exists a natural number
d
such that
\omega^{\star}_{d}<2^{d}
, then
\lvert\delta\rvert\leq\mathtt{poly}(m)
for
every
fractional clique
\delta
in
G_{m}
.
The first step in the proof is to apply LP duality, which reduces the latter to showing that
there exists
a fractional coloring of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
that employs
\mathtt{poly}(m)
colors. It turns out that fractional colorings in the contradiction graph have a natural learning theoretic interpretation; they correspond to distributions over hypotheses.
This correspondence between fractional colorings and distributions over hypotheses implies that it suffices to prove the following statement: there exists a distribution
\mu
over hypotheses such that for every dataset
S
of size
m
that is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
:

\Pr_{h\sim\mu}[h\text{ is consistent with }S]\geq 1/\mathtt{poly}(m).

(2)

We obtain the distribution
\mu
as follows.
By the above correspondence, there is a distribution
\mu^{\prime}
such that

\Pr_{h\sim\mu^{\prime}}[h\text{ is consistent with }S]\geq 2^{-d}+\epsilon
,
for every realizable dataset
S
of size
d
.
The distribution
\mu
is obtained by independently sampling
\Theta(\log m/\epsilon^{2})
hypotheses from
\mu^{\prime}
and taking their majority vote.
Interestingly, the analysis showing that
\mu
satisfies
Equation

2
follows by a reduction to online prediction using expert advice.

2.2
Private Learnability: Characterizations

We next present the characterizations of pure and approximate private learnability.

Theorem 2.6

(Pure DP Learnability)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a concept class
\mathcal{H}
.

1.

\mathcal{H}
is pure differentially private PAC learnable.

2.

\mathcal{H}
has finite fractional clique dimension.

Theorem 2.7

(Approximate DP Learnability)
.

The following statements are equivalent for a concept class
\mathcal{H}
.

1.

\mathcal{H}
is approximately differentially private PAC learnable.

2.

\mathcal{H}
has finite clique dimension.

Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
provide a unified characterization of private PAC learnability
in terms of cliques and fractional cliques.
Roughly speaking, these theorems assert that large (fractional) cliques in the contradiction graph
correspond to tasks that are hard to learn privately.
However, our proofs of Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
do not explicitly illustrate this correspondence.
Instead, our proofs follow an indirect path by linking the clique and fractional clique dimensions
to the representation and Littlestone dimensions, respectively. This implies the stated equivalences since the representation and
Littlestone dimensions characterize pure and approximate DP learnability
[
BNS19
,
ABL+22
]
.

It would be interesting to find direct proofs that illustrate the correspondence between large cliques in the contradiction graphs and hard learning tasks.
A clique of size
k
in the contradiction graph is simply a set
\mathcal{S}
of
k
realizable datasets such that every two distinct datasets
S^{\prime},S^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
disagree on some example. Analogously, a fractional clique of size
k
is a distribution
\nu
over realizable data sets such that
\Pr_{S\sim\nu}[h\text{ is consistent with }S]\leq 1/k
for every hypothesis
h
.
Hence, it is quite natural to speculate that there exists a natural and direct conversion between cliques and fractional cliques, and realizable distributions on datasets that are hard for private learning.

Duality, Representation Dimension, and Communication Complexity.

The fractional clique number is defined via a linear program and as such it has a dual definition.
The latter is called the fractional chromatic number and is defined as follows.
Let
G
be an undirected graph and let
\mathcal{I}
denote the family of independent sets in
G
.
A fractional coloring is an assignment
c:\mathcal{I}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for every vertex
v
we have
\sum_{I:v\in I}c(I)\geq 1
.
The number of colors in
c
is defined by
\mathtt{col}(c)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}}c(I)
.
The fractional chromatic number, denoted by
\chi^{\star}(G)
, is the infimum number of colors in a fractional coloring.
Notice that
\chi^{\star}(G)\leq\chi(G)
, where
\chi(G)
is the chromatic number of
G
. This holds because any coloring defines a fractional coloring by assigning
1
to each color class.

Fractional colorings of the contradiction graph have a natural learning theoretic interpretation.
A fractional coloring
c
of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
corresponds to a distribution
\mu
over hypotheses such that for every realizable dataset
S
of size
m
:

\Pr_{h\sim\mu}[h\text{ is consistent with }S]\geq\frac{1}{\mathtt{col}(c)}.

(3)

The fractional chromatic number of the contradiction graph is tightly linked to the representation dimension (see
Definition

3.1
).
The latter is a dimension introduced by
[
BNS19
]
to characterize pure DP learnability.
Roughly, the representation dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is the minimal integer
d
for which there exists a distribution

\mathcal{P}
over hypothesis
classes
of size
\leq 2^{d}
such that for every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
,
a random class
H\sim\mathcal{P}
contains with probability at least
3/4
an hypothesis
h=h_{\mathcal{D}}
whose loss with respect to
\mathcal{D}
is at most
1/4
.

The representation dimension can be interpreted as a lossy variant of the fractional chromatic number.
Indeed, consider a uniformly sampled hypothesis
h
drawn from a random class
H\sim\mathcal{P}
,
and pick
\mathcal{D}
to be the uniform distribution over the examples in a realizable dataset
S
.
Thus, with probability at least
\frac{3}{4}2^{-d}
, the random hypothesis
h

classifies correctly
3/4
of the examples in
S
.

A similar link exists between the fractional clique number of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})

and the communication complexity theoretic characterization of pure private learnability by
[
FX15
]
.
The latter is based on a communication game between two players whom we call Alice and Bob.
In the game, Alice’s input is a hypothesis
h\in\mathcal{H}
, Bob’s input is a point
x
, and Alice sends a
single
message to Bob.
Their goal is that with probability at least
3/4
, Bob will be able to decode
h(x)
from Alice’s message.
Feldman and Xiao showed that the optimal number of bits required to perform this task is proportional to the representation dimension of
\mathcal{H}
,
and hence characterizes pure DP learnability.

Fractional cliques are linked to hard distributions for the above communication game.
A fractional clique
\delta
corresponds to a distribution
\nu
over realizable datasets
S
of size
m

such that for every hypothesis
h
:

\Pr_{S\sim\nu}\Bigl[h\text{ is consistent with $S$}\Bigr]\leq\frac{1}{\lvert\delta\rvert}.

(4)

Consider a variant of the above equation where the event “
h
is consistent with
S
”
is replaced by the event “
h
classifies correctly at least
3/4
of the examples in
S
”.
This variant induces a hard distribution for the communication game as follows.
Pick a random dataset
S\sim\nu
and a concept
h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
h
is consistent with
S
.
Let Alice’s input be
h
and Bob’s input be a random (unlabeled) example from
S
.
Standard arguments in communication complexity show that this distribution is indeed hard for the communication game.

To summarize the discussion, LP duality implies that the fractional clique and chromatic numbers are equal.
When considering this along with the aforementioned connections, it illuminates a dual relationship
between the representation dimension and the communication-complexity-based characterization of pure private learnability.

2.2.1
Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
: Technical Overview

Our proof of Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
rely on the exponential-polynomial dichotomies (Theorems
2.3
, and
2.2
).

We begin with overviewing the proof of Theorem
2.7
. By
[
ABL+22
]
, a class
\mathcal{H}
is approximately DP learnable if and only if it has a finite Littlestone dimension. Thus, it suffices to show that the clique dimension is finite if and only if the Littlestone dimension is finite.
Our proof yields explicit bounds of

\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})\leq\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})\leq O(\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})\log\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})),

where
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the Littlestone dimension of
\mathcal{H}
.
One direction is straightforward. If the Littlestone dimension is at least
m
, then there is a mistake tree of depth
m
that is shattered by
\mathcal{H}
. Each branch of the tree corresponds to a realizable dataset of length
m
, and the collection of
2^{m}
datasets correspond to the branches form a clique. Indeed, every two datasets disagree on the example corresponding to the least common ancestor of their branches.

The converse direction is more challenging. Assume that the clique dimension
\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})

is at least
m
. We prove the existence of a shattered mistake tree of depth
\tilde{\Omega}(m)
.
Like in Theorem
2.2
, we prove this using the existence of a balanced point (see
Equation

1
).
Indeed, given a clique
\delta
of size
2^{m}
in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
, we pick the root of the mistake tree to be a balanced point
x
in
\delta
,
and proceed to find a balanced point in each of
\{S\in\delta:(x,0)\in S\}
and
\{S\in\delta:(x,1)\in S\}
, corresponding to the left and right subtrees. We continue this way until one of the branches is consistent with exactly one dataset in
\delta
.
A standard calculation shows that this way one obtains a Littlestone tree of depth at least
\tilde{\Omega}(m)
.

We note that there is a simple example of a class
\mathcal{H}
that satisfies
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})<\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
(see
Section

6
).
We leave as an open question to determine whether
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})=\Theta(\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H}))
.

We now move to overview the proof of Theorem
2.6
. Our proof follows by relating the representaion dimension with the fractional clique dimension, showing that one is finite if and only if the other is finite. This finishes the proof because the representation dimension characterizes pure DP learnability
[
BNS19
]
.
The direction showing that finite representation dimension implies finite fractional clique dimension follows directly by results from
[
BNS19
]
. In particular,
[
BNS19
]
proved a boosting result which reduces the probability of error
of the random class from
1/4
to
\epsilon
. Applying their result with
\epsilon<1/m
yields a bound on the fractional clique number.

For the converse direction, we use Theorem
2.3
.
Assume that the fractional clique dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is finite;
therefore, for every
m
the fractional clique number is bounded by
\mathtt{poly}(m)
.
Thus, by LP duality there exists a fractional coloring
c
with
\mathop{\mathtt{poly}}(m)
many colors.
Now, since fractional colorings correspond to distributions
\mu
over hypotheses satisfying
Equation

3
,
we can define a distribution over hypothesis classes by sampling
\mathtt{poly}(m)
independent hypotheses from
c
.
By
Equation

3
it follows that for every realizable dataset
S
of size
m
,
one of the
\mathtt{poly}(m)
hypotheses is consistent with
S
with probability at least a constant (say
1/4
).
By a standard generalization argument, this yields the desired bound on the representation dimension.

2.3
Strong Duality in Infinite Contradiction Graphs

Our proofs heavily rely on the equality of the fractional clique and chromatic numbers.
For finite graphs, this equality is a consequence of LP duality.
However, in learning theory, we often study infinite hypothesis classes
\mathcal{H}

whose contradiction graphs are therefore also infinite.
In general, LP duality does not apply in infinite dimensional spaces.
Therefore, we prove the next theorem showing that any (possibly infinite) contradiction graph
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})

satisfies that its fractional clique and chromatic numbers are equal and are bounded from above by
2^{m}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be an arbitrary domain,
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
a concept class and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
\omega_{m}^{\star}
and
\chi_{m}^{\star}
denote the fractional clique and chromatic numbers of the contradiction graph
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then,

\omega_{m}^{\star}=\chi_{m}^{\star}\leq 2^{m}.

Moreover, there exists a fractional coloring realizing
\chi_{m}^{\star}
.
(I.e. the infimum is in fact a minimum.)

The proof of Theorem
2.8
uses tools from functional analysis, topology, and measure theory.
The equality
\omega_{m}^{\star}=\chi_{m}^{\star}
is derived using Sion’s Theorem
[
Sio58
]
,
and the upper bound of

hinges on Kolmogorov’s Extension Theorem (see, e.g. Theorem 2.4.3 in
[
Tao11
]
). This proof appears in
Appendix

A
,
which we attempted to present in a manner accessible for readers who may only have basic familiarity with topology and functional analysis.

Preliminaries

3.1
Learning

PAC Learning.

We use standard notations from statistical learning; for more details see e.g.
[
SSBD14
]
.
Let
\mathcal{X}
be a domain; for simplicity, in this work, we assume that
\mathcal{X}
is countable, although our arguments apply more generally. Given an hypothesis
h:\mathcal{X}\to\{0,1\}
, the
empirical loss
of
h
with respect to a dataset
S=\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},y_{m})\bigr)
is defined as

\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{S}}(h)\coloneqq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}[h(x_{i})\neq y_{i}]
.
We say that
h
is
consistent
with
S
if
\mathtt{L}_{\mathtt{S}}(h)=0
.
The
population loss
of
h
with respect to a distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
is defined as

\mathtt{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\coloneqq\Pr_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[h(x)\neq y]
.
A distribution
\mathcal{D}
over labeled examples is
realizable
with respect to
\mathcal{H}
if

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\mathtt{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)=0
.

For a set
Z
, let
Z^{\star}=\cup_{n=0}^{\infty}Z^{n}
. A
learning rule

\mathcal{A}
is a (possibly randomized) algorithm that takes as input a dataset
S\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{\star}
and outputs a hypothesis
{h=\mathcal{A}(S)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
.
In the PAC learning model, the input
S
is sampled i.i.d. from a realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and the learner’s goal is to output an hypothesis with small population loss with respect to
\mathcal{D}
.
More precisely, let
m,\alpha,\beta>0
.
We say that an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is an
(m,\alpha,\beta)
-learner for
\mathcal{H}
if for every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
,

\Pr_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\mathtt{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}(S))>\alpha\right]<\beta
.
Here,
\alpha
is called the
error
,
\beta
is the
confidence parameter
, and
m
is the
sample complexity
.
A class
\mathcal{H}
is
PAC learnable
if there exists vanishing
\alpha(m),\beta(m)\to 0

and an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
such that for all
m
, algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a
(m,\alpha(m),\beta(m))
-learner for
\mathcal{H}
.

Differential Privacy.

We use standard notations from differential privacy literature; for more details see e.g.
[
DR14
,
Vad17
]
.
A randomized learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is
differentially private
with parameters
(\epsilon,\delta)
, if for every input datasets
S,S^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\})^{m}
that differ on a single example, and every event
E\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
:

\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S)\in E]\leq e^{\epsilon}\Pr[\mathcal{A}(S^{\prime})\in E]+\delta,

where the probability is over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
.
The parameters
\epsilon
and
\delta
are usually treated as follows:

\epsilon
is a small constant (say
\leq 0.1
) and
\delta
is negligible,
\delta=m^{-\omega(1)}
where
m
is the input datasets size. When
\delta=0
we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
pure differentially private
, and when
\delta>0
we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
approximate differentially private
.

An hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is
pure privately learnable
(abbreviated
pure DP learnable
) if it is privately learnable by an algorithm which is
(\epsilon(m),0)
-differentially private, where
\epsilon(m)=O(1)
is a numerical constant.
An hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
is
approximately privately learnable
(abbreviated
DP learnable
) if it is PAC learnable by an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
which is
(\epsilon(m),\delta(m))
-differentially private, where
\epsilon(m)=O(1)
is a numerical constant and
\delta(m)=m^{-\omega(1)}
.

Representation Dimension.

The representation dimension is a combinatorial parameter introduced by Beimel et al.
[
BNS19
]
who used it to characterize pure DP learnability.

Definition 3.1

(Representation Dimension
[
BNS19
]
)
.

The
representation dimension
of a concept class
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathtt{RepDim}(\mathcal{H})
,
is defined to be
\ln(d)
, where
d
is the minimal integer for which there exists
a distribution
\mathcal{P}
over hypothesis classes of size
d
that satisfies the following.
For every distribution
\mathcal{D}
on labeled examples that is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
,

\Pr_{\mathcal{C}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\text{$\exists h\in\mathcal{C}$ s.t.\ $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\frac{1}{4}$}\right]\geq\frac{3}{4}.

As
[
BNS19
]
show, the constants
1/4,3/4
above can be replaced by any other pair of constants in
(0,1)
without changing the semantics of the definition. This follows from the next lemma:

Lemma 3.2

(Boosting Probabilistic Representation, Lemma 18
[
BNS19
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class with
\mathtt{RepDim}(\mathcal{H})=d<\infty
. Then for every
0<\alpha,\beta<1
there exists a probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
over hypothesis classes of size
O\left((\frac{1}{\alpha})^{d+\ln\ln\ln(\frac{1}{\alpha})+\ln\ln(\frac{1}{\beta})}\right)
which satisfies the following.
For every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
on labeled examples which is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
,

\Pr_{\mathcal{C}\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\text{$\exists h\in\mathcal{C}$ s.t.\ $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\alpha$}\right]\geq 1-\beta.

Littlestone Dimension.

The Littlestone dimension is a combinatorial parameter that captures mistake and regret bounds in online learning
[
Lit88
,
BDPSS09
]
. The definition of the Littlestone dimension uses the notion of
mistake trees
.
A
mistake tree
is a binary decision tree whose nodes are labeled with instances from
\mathcal{X}
and whose edges are labeled by
0
or
1
such that each internal node has one outgoing edge labeled
0
and one outgoing edge labeled
1
. A root-to-leaf path in a mistake tree is a sequence of labeled examples
(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{d},y_{d})
.
The point
x_{i}
is the label of the
i
’th internal node in the path, and
y_{i}
is the label of its outgoing edge to the next node in the path.
A class
\mathcal{H}

shatters
a mistake tree if every root-to-leaf path is realizable by
\mathcal{H}
.
The
Littlestone dimension
of
\mathcal{H}
, denoted
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
, is the largest number
d
such that there exists a complete binary mistake tree of depth
d
shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.
If
\mathcal{H}
shatters arbitrarily deep mistake trees then we write
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})=\infty
.

3.2
Graph Theory

Cliques, Colorings, and Distributions.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a (possibly countable) graph.
Denote by
\omega(G)
the
clique number
of
G
, which is the largest size of a clique in
G
.
Denote by
\chi(G)
the
chromatic number
of
G
, which is the smallest number of colors needed to color the vertices of
G
so that no two adjacent vertices share the same color.
The clique and chromatic numbers have natural LP relaxations.

A
fractional clique
is a function
\delta:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\sum_{v\in I}{\delta(v)\leq 1}
for every independent set
I
.
The
size
of
\delta
is
\lvert\delta\rvert\coloneqq\sum_{v\in V}{\delta(v)}
.
The
fractional clique number
of
G
, denoted
\omega^{\star}(G)
, is defined by

{\omega^{\star}(G)\coloneqq\sup_{\delta}\lvert\delta\rvert}
.
A
fractional coloring
is a finite measure
1
1

1

For a finite
G
, one typically defines a fractional coloring as a function
c:\mathcal{I}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that
\sum_{v\in I}c(I)\geq 1
for every
v\in V
. The latter amounts to
c
being a discrete measure on
\mathcal{I}
. In this work, we also consider infinite graphs and use this more general definition which allows for non-discrete measures on
\mathcal{I}
. We refer the reader to Section
A
for a more detailed discussion.

c
on
\mathcal{I}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the family of all independent sets in
G
, such that

c(\{I:v\in I\})\geq 1
for every
v\in V
.
The
fractional chromatic number
of
G
, denoted
\chi^{\star}(G)
, is defined by

{\chi^{\star}(G)\coloneqq\inf_{c}c(\mathcal{I})}
.
Note that from (weak) LP-duality, for any graph
G
,
\omega(G)\leq\omega^{\star}(G)\leq\chi^{\star}(G)\leq\chi(G)
. In finite graphs strong LP-duality holds and
\omega^{\star}(G)=\chi^{\star}(G)
. Theorem
2.8
extends this equality to any (possibly infinite) contradiction graph.

There is a natural correspondence between fractional colorings of a graph
G
and distributions over independent sets.
Given a fractional coloring
c
,
normalizing
c
by the number of colores in
c
, denoted by
\mathtt{col}(c)\coloneqq c(\mathcal{I})
, induces a distribution
\mu
over independent sets, such that for every vertex
v
,

\Pr_{I\sim\mu}[v\in I]=\frac{1}{\mathtt{col}(c)}\cdot c(\{I:v\in I\}).

Define the
value
of a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{I}
to be
{\mathtt{val}(\mu)\coloneqq\inf_{v\in V}\Pr_{I\sim\mu}[v\in I]}
.
By taking infimum,

\mathtt{val}(\mu)\coloneqq\inf_{v\in V}\Pr_{I\sim\mu}[v\in I]=\frac{1}{\mathtt{col}(c)}.

From the other direction, given a distribution
\mu
over
\mathcal{I}
, normalizing
\mu
by
\mathtt{val}(\mu)

induces a fractional coloring of
G
.
Minimizing the number of colors of a fractional coloring is equivalent to maximizing the value of the corresponding distribution, hence

\frac{1}{\chi^{\star}(G)}=\sup_{\mu}\mathtt{val}(\mu)=\sup_{\mu}\inf_{v\in V}{\Pr_{I\sim\mu}\left[v\in I\right]}.

Similarly, there is a correspondence between fractional cliques of a graph
G
and distributions over vertices.
Normalizing a fractional clique
{\delta:V\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}}
by
\lvert\delta\rvert
, induces a distribution
\nu
over vertices, and
normalizing a distribution
\nu
over
V
by

\mathtt{val}(\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{I\in\mathcal{I}}\Pr_{v\sim\nu}[v\in I]

induces a fractional clique.
Similarly,

\frac{1}{\omega^{\star}(G)}=\inf_{\nu}\mathtt{val}(\nu)=\inf_{\nu}\sup_{I\in\mathcal{I}}{\Pr_{v\sim\nu}\left[v\in I\right]}.

]
.

3.3
The Contradiction Graph: Basic Facts

In this section, we state basic lemmas about the structure of the contradiction graph.
We begin with a discussion about the relation between interpolating learning rules and colorings of the contradiction graph.
Then, we discuss the relation between fractional colorings and cliques, and distributions over hypotheses and realizable datasets.
Omitted proofs can be found at
Appendix

B
.

Interpolating Algorithms are Proper Colorings.

There is a correspondence between proper colorings of the contradiction graph and interpolating learning rules. A deterministic learning rule
\mathcal{A}
is said to be
interpolating
with respect to a class
\mathcal{H}
if for every realizable input dataset
S
, the output hypothesis
{h\coloneqq\mathcal{A}(S)}
satisfies
h(x)=y
for every labeled example
{(x,y)\in S}
.
A
proper coloring
of a graph is an assignment of a color to each vertex so that no two adjacent vertices share the same color. In other words, a coloring is a partition of the vertices such that every subset in the partition is an independent set.
The correspondence between colorings and interpolation algorithms is a direct result of the following lemma which identifies independent sets in the contradiction graph with hypotheses
2
2

2

The mapping described in

for maximal independent sets.

Lemma 3.3

(Independent sets and consistent hypotheses)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class and
m
be a natural number.

1.

For every independent set
I
in

, there exists an hypothesis
{h\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}}
such that
h
is consistent with every dataset
S\in I
; i.e. for every dataset
S\in I
and every example
(x,y)\in S
, we have
h(x)=y
.

2.

For every hypothesis
h
, the set of all datasets of size
m
that are consistent with
h
is an independent set in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
; i.e. the set

V_{h}\coloneqq\{S=((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},y_{m}))\in V_{m}(\mathcal{H})\mid\forall i,h(x_{i})=y_{i}\}

is independent in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.

Colorings
\to
Algorithms.

Let
S
be a realizable dataset of size

, and consider a coloring of

. By part 1 of

which is consistent with every dataset in

.
Now simply define

\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{H}
.

Algorithms
\to

Given an interpolating learning rule
\mathcal{A}
, by part 2 of

.

In the contradiction graph independent sets correspond to hypothesis and vertices are realizable datasets. Therefore, fractional colorings can be viewed as distributions over hypotheses, and fractional cliques as distributions over realizable datasets:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi^{\star}_{m}}

Let

denote the fractional clique number of

This identification between fractional colorings and distribution over hypotheses is useful, for example, for attaining bounds on

One of the basic properties that

Lemma

(

Let

Proof of

2.1
.

Let
\delta
be a fractional clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Draw a random

such that

is drawn uniformly and independently from

Note that

\displaystyle 1\geq\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]

4
Approximate Privacy and Cliques

In this Section, we prove
Theorems

2.7
and
2.2
.
Recall,
Theorem

2.2
states that the clique number of the contradiction graph obeys an exponential-polynomial dichotomy, and
Theorem

2.7
states that a class is approximately DP PAC learnable if and only if its clique dimension is finite.
The main idea is to study the relations between the clique dimension and the Littlestone dimension.
It is known that a class
\mathcal{H}
is approximately DP learnable if and only if the Littlestone dimension
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
is finite
[
ALMM19
,
BLM20
]
.
Therefore, to prove
Theorem

2.7
it suffices to show that the clique dimension
\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
is finite if and only if
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
is finite.
Recall that
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
is equal to the depth of the deepest mistake tree that is shattered by
\mathcal{H}
.
Observe that if a class
\mathcal{H}
shatters a mistake tree of depth
m
, then the
2^{m}
datasets corresponding to the leaves of the shattered tree form a clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
This observation proves the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an hypothesis class. Then
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})\leq\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})
.

In simple words, deep shattered trees imply large cliques. We will show that the opposite statement holds as well.
The following lemma is the crux of our proof.
It asserts that for any clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})

there exists an unlabeled data point
x
which
separates
a non-negligible fraction of the datasets in the clique.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an hypothesis class and let
C\subseteq V_{m}(\mathcal{H})
be a clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, there exists an unlabeled data point
x\in\mathcal{X}

which is
balanced
in the following sense:
at least
\frac{\lvert C\rvert-1}{2m}
datasets in
C
contain the labeled example
(x,1)

and at least
\frac{\lvert C\rvert-1}{2m}
datasets in
C
contain the labeled example
(x,0)
.

Before proving this lemma let us remark that quantitative improvements in the above lemma translate to improvements in
Theorem

2.2
and to tighter bounds relating the Littlestone and clique dimensions. We elaborate on this in
Section

6
.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
.

Denote
c\coloneqq|C|
. Given a labeled example
(x,b)
denote by
C_{(x,b)}
the set of all datasets in
C
that contains
(x,b)
:

C_{(x,b)}:=\{S\in C\mid(x,b)\in S\}.

We perform the following iterative process to find a balanced example
x
:

Eliminate Unbalanced Examples in Clique:

As long as there is
S\in C
and
(x,b)\in S
such that
\lvert C_{(x,1-b)}\rvert<\frac{c-1}{2m}
, do:

1.

Update
S\rightarrow S\smallsetminus\{(x,b)\}
.

2.

Update the edges of
C
accordingly: if a dataset
S^{\prime}\in C_{(x,1-b)}
does not contradict
S
anymore (i.e.
S
and
S^{\prime}
disagreed only
x
), then delete the edge between them.

Observe that:

1.

The number of iterations is at most
cm
:
Consider the sum
\sum_{S\in C}|S|
.
At the beginning of the process
\sum_{S\in C}|S\rvert=cm
,
and at each iteration, the size of one dataset is reduced by one
and hence
\sum_{S\in C}|S|
decreases by one.
The bound follows since
\sum_{S\in C}|S|
is always non-negative.

2.

At each iteration, the number of edges that are deleted is less than
\frac{c-1}{2m}
.

3.

Thus, the total number of edges that are deleted during the process is less than
cm\cdot\frac{c-1}{2m}={\binom{c}{2}}
.

Therefore, at the end of the process, there is at least one remaining edge.
That is, there are two datasets in
C
that contradict each other on an unlabeled example
x

which satisfies both
|C_{(x,1)}|
and
|C_{(x,0)}|
are at least
\frac{c-1}{2m}
, as required.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an hypothesis class and denote by
\omega_{m}
the clique number of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.
Then, for all
m
,

\omega_{m}\leq(2m+1)^{\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})}\leq(2m+1)^{\mathtt{CD}(\mathcal{H})}.

The idea of the proof is to apply
Lemma

4.2
on a maximal clique in
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
and construct inductively a shattered Littlestone tree whose depth is large (as a function of
\omega_{m}
).
Together with the fact that the depth is at most
\mathtt{LD}(\mathcal{H})
, the desired bound follows.

Note that proving this lemma completes the proof of
Theorem

5
Pure Privacy and Fractional Cliques

In this section we prove

and
2.3

Recall,

states that the fractional clique number of the contradiction graph obeys an exponential-polynomial dichotomy, and
Theorem

states that a class is pure DP PAC learnable if and only if its fractional clique dimension is finite.
Beimel et al. proved that a class

is pure DP learnable if and only if the representation dimension

is finite
[

.
Recall,
\mathtt{RepDim}(\mathcal{H})
is finite if there exists a distribution over finite hypothesis classes such that for every realizable distribution over labeled examples, with high probability, a random class contains an hypothesis that has small population error.
We will show that
\mathtt{RepDim}(\mathcal{H})
is finite if and only if the fractional clique dimension
\mathtt{CD}^{\star}(\mathcal{H})
is finite.
We begin with a technical lemma.
An optimal fractional coloring of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})
induces a distribution over hypotheses such that every realizable dataset of size
m
is consistent with a random hypothesis
with probability of at least
1/\omega^{\star}_{m}

(
Corollary

).
In other words, the probability that a random hypothesis has zero empirical loss (with respect to any realizable dataset of size
m
) is bounded from below by a positive constant.
Using measure concentration arguments gives similar results when considering the population loss of a random hypothesis instead of the empirical loss. That is, there exists a distribution over hypotheses such that for every realizable distribution
\mathcal{D}
, the probability that
a random hypothesis has small population loss (with respect to
\mathcal{D}
) is bounded from below.
This fact is essential for the proof of the equivalence between the representation dimension and the fractional clique dimension.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a class and

. Then there exists a distribution
\mu^{\star}
over hypotheses which satisfies the following. For every distribution
\mathcal{D}
over labeled examples which is realizable by

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\omega^{\star}_{m}}-(1-\theta)^{m},

\omega^{\star}_{m}
is the fractional clique number of
G_{m}(\mathcal{H})

Proof of

The proof idea is to consider an optimal fractional coloring of

Fractional colorings correspond to distributions over hypotheses, as demonstrated by

This optimal fractional coloring translates to an optimal distribution
\mu^{\star}
over hypotheses.
Then, we use a concentration argument to reason that this distribution achieves the desired bound.

Let

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{\star}_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{m}}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{h\sim\mu^{\star}}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\text{$h$ is consistent with $S$}\}}|S\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{h}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\text{$h$ is consistent with $S$}\}}|h\right]\right]

A.3
Tossing a fair coin infinitely many times

One of the basic facts we used in the paper is that the fractional chromatic number of the contradiction graph is bounded. To prove this fact we drew a random hypothesis by sampling a value independently for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
uniformly over
\{0,1\}
.

For a countable domain
\mathcal{X}
it is clear that this sampling process induces a well-defined probability distribution over
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. However, if
\mathcal{X}
is arbitrary, and possibly uncountable, it is not clear that this process is even well-defined.

In this section, we prove
Lemma

A.7
: we show that indeed the described sampling process induces a probability measure on the product space
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
. Furthermore, we show that this measure is a regular Borel measure, and hence a valid fractional coloring. (Recall that we identify fractional colorings as regular Borel probability measures over
\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}
, where the latter is equipped with the product topology).

A.3.1
Measure Theory Preliminaries

The Product
\sigma
-Algebra.

Let
(X_{\alpha},\Sigma_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be a family of measurable spaces. Denote by
{X_{\mathcal{I}}=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}X_{\alpha}}
the product space, and by
\pi_{\alpha}:X_{\mathcal{I}}\to X_{\alpha}
the projection map to
X_{\alpha}
.
Using the projection
\pi_{\alpha}
, we can pull back the
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{\alpha}
on
X_{\alpha}
, to a
\sigma
-algebra on
X_{\mathcal{I}}
:

\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\Sigma_{\alpha})\coloneqq\{\pi_{\alpha}^{-1}(E_{\alpha})\mid E_{\alpha}\in\Sigma_{\alpha}\}.

Define the
product
\sigma
-algebra
on
X_{\mathcal{I}}
, denoted
\Sigma_{\mathcal{I}}
, to be the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all
\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\Sigma_{\alpha})
:

\Sigma_{\mathcal{I}}\coloneqq\Bigl\langle\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\Sigma_{\alpha})\Bigr\rangle=\langle\pi^{-1}_{\alpha}(E_{\alpha})\mid\alpha\in\mathcal{I}\rangle.

Using similar notations, for every subset of indices
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, let
{X_{A}=\prod_{\alpha\in A}X_{\alpha}}
, let
\pi_{A}:X_{\mathcal{I}}\to X_{A}
denote the projection to
X_{A}
, and denote the product
\sigma
-algebra on
A
by

\Sigma_{A}=\Bigl\langle\bigcup_{\alpha\in A}\pi_{A}\circ\pi_{\alpha}^{*}(\Sigma_{\alpha})\Bigr\rangle.

Given
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, a measure
\mu_{\mathcal{I}}
on the product
\sigma
-algebra

\Sigma_{\mathcal{I}}
, induces a measure
\mu_{A}
on
\Sigma_{A}
(which is the pushforward of
\mu_{\mathcal{I}}
):

\mu_{A}(E)\coloneqq(\pi_{A})_{*}\mu_{\mathcal{I}}(E)=\mu_{\mathcal{I}}(\pi_{A}^{-1}(E)).

Note that those measures obey the compatibility relation:
For
B\subseteq A\subseteq I
, denote by
\pi_{A\to B}
the projection
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\pi_{B}\vphantom{|}\right|_{X_{A}}}:X_{A}\to X_{B}
.
Then

\mu_{B}=(\pi_{A\to B})_{*}\mu_{A},

(24)

i.e, for every
E\in\Sigma_{B}
,
\mu_{B}(E)=\mu_{A}((\pi_{A\to B})^{-1}(E))
.

Kolmogorov’s Extension Theorem.

A natural question is whether one can reconstruct
\mu_{\mathcal{I}}
solely from the projections
\mu_{A}
to
finite
subsets
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. It turns out that in the special case where the
\mu_{A}
are probability measures (and satisfy some additional regularity and compatibility conditions), it is indeed possible. This is the content of Kolmogorov’s Theorem (Theorem 2.4.3 in
[
Tao11
]
).

Theorem A.8

(Kolmogorov’s Extension Theorem)
.

Let
(X_{\alpha},\Sigma_{\alpha},\tau_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be a family of measurable spaces
(X_{\alpha},\Sigma_{\alpha})
, equipped with a topology
\tau_{\alpha}
.
For every finite subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, let
\mu_{A}
be a probability measure on
\Sigma_{A}
which is inner regular with respect to the product topology
\tau_{A}\coloneqq\prod_{\alpha\in A}\tau_{\alpha}
on
X_{A}
.
Furthermore, assume
\mu_{A},\mu_{B}
obey the compatibility condition in
Equation

24
for every
nested finite subsets
B\subseteq A
of
\mathcal{I}
.
Then, there exists a unique probability measure
\mu_{\mathcal{I}}
on the product
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{\mathcal{I}}
, with the property that
(\pi_{A})_{*}\mu_{\mathcal{I}}=\mu_{A}
for all finite subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. (I.e. the push-forward measure of
\mu_{\mathcal{I}}
when projected on

equals to
\mu_{A}
.)

Extending a product measure to a regular Borel measure.

Let us assume here and below that each
X_{\alpha}
is a compact Hausdorff space, and
\Sigma_{\alpha}
is the Borel

\sigma
-algebra on
X_{\alpha}
.
Kolmogorov’s theorem allows to construct a probability measure on the product

-algebra by specifying its behavior over finite projections.
The following theorem allows us to further extend this measure to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of the product space

. (Notice that the Borel

Theorem A.9

(Theorem 2,

)
.

Let

is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of

. Then, every probability measure on the product
\sigma
-algebra,

.
