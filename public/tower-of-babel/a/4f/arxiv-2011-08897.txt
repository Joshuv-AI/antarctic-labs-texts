The coframe of D D -sublocales of a locale and the T D T_{D} -duality
Igor Arrieta, Anna Laura Suarez

Abstract
The notion of D-sublocale is explored. This is the notion analogue to that of sublocale in the duality of T D T_{D} -spaces. A sublocale S S of a frame L L is a D D -sublocale if and only if the corresponding localic map preserves the property of being a covered prime. It is shown that for a frame L L the system of those sublocales which are also D D -sublocales form a dense sublocale 𝖲 D ​ ( L ) \mathsf{S}_{D}(L) of the coframe 𝖲 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{S}(L) of all its sublocales. It is also shown that the spatialization 𝗌𝗉 D ​ [ 𝖲 D ​ ( L ) ] \mathsf{sp}_{D}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)] of 𝖲 D ​ ( L ) \mathsf{S}_{D}(L) consists precisely of those D D -sublocales of L L which are T D T_{D} -spatial. Additionally, frames such that we have 𝖲 D ​ ( L ) ≅ 𝒫 ⁡ ( 𝗉𝗍 D ​ ( L ) ) \mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)) — that is, those such that D D -sublocales perfectly represent subspaces — are characterized as those T D T_{D} -spatial frames such that 𝖲 D ​ ( L ) \mathsf{S}_{D}(L) is the Booleanization of 𝖲 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{S}(L) . Introduction

For a point-free space (that is, a frame)
L

sublocales

\mathsf{S}(L)

\mathsf{S}(L)

\mathsf{Cl}(L)

\mathsf{S}(L)

\mathsf{Op}(L)

open

\mathsf{Op}(L)
is isomorphic to
L
. Since the system
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a frame, it has its own sublocales, and among these there are certain special ones that encode information about the frame
L
. Next to the sublocales of
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
that are particularly interesting are also certain subsets. Recently, some of these special sublocales and subsets have enjoyed special attention. We have the system of all joins of closed sublocales
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
(see
[
10
]
), which coincides with the smallest dense sublocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)
if and only if the frame
L
satisfies a weak separation axiom called
subfitness
. We then have
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, the smallest dense sublocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)
. The properties of this sublocale have been studied in
[
1
]
. We also have
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
(see
[
14
]
), the sublocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)
consisting of those sublocales which are spatial — that is, those sublocales which are the frame of opens of some topological space.

We explore the relation these important subsets of the assembly
\mathsf{S}(L)
of a frame
L
; and we identify another which also encodes some special properties of

, in particular in the context of the
T_{D}
-duality. We will look at the notion of

-sublocale
of

, a sublocale

such that the corresponding frame surjection is a

Preliminaries

We first recall some background on point-free topology. For more information on the categories of frames and locales, we refer the reader to Johnstone
[
6
]
or the more recent Picado-Pultr
[
8
]
. A
locale
(or
frame
) is a complete lattice
L
satisfying

a\wedge\bigvee B=\bigvee\{\,a\wedge b\mid b\in B\,\}

for all
a\in L
and
B\subseteq L
. A
frame homomorphism
is a function preserving arbitrary joins (including the bottom element
0
) and finite meets (including the top element
1
). Frames and their homomorphisms form a category
\mathsf{Frm}
. For each
a
the map
a\wedge(-)
preserves arbitrary joins, thus it has a right (Galois) adjoint
a\to(-)
, making
L
a complete Heyting algebra (i.e. a cartesian closed category, if one regards
L
as a thin category). This right adjoint is called the
Heyting operator
. In particular, the
pseudocomplement
of an
a\in L
is the element
a^{*}=a\to 0
and it can be characterized as the largest
b\in L
such that
a\wedge b=0
.

The categories
\mathsf{Loc}
and
\mathsf{Frm}

Given a topological space
X
, its lattice of open sets
\Omega(X)
is always a frame, and this construction can be upgraded to a functor
\Omega\colon{\mathsf{Top}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Frm}^{op}}
which sends a continuous map
f\colon X\to Y
to the preimage operator
f^{-1}[-]\colon\Omega(Y)\to\Omega(X)
. An element
p
of a frame
L
is said to be
prime
if whenever
p=x\wedge y
for some
x,y\in L
, then
p=x
or
p=y
. The collection of all primes of
L
will be denoted by
\mathsf{pt}(L)
and we shall refer to it as the
spectrum
(or the
classical spectrum
or
sober spectrum
) of
L
. For each
a\in L
, we set
\Sigma_{a}=\{\,p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)\mid a\not\leq p\,\}
. Then the family
\{\,\Sigma_{a}\mid a\in L\,\}
is a topology on
\mathsf{pt}(L)
. The assignment
\Sigma(L)=(\mathsf{pt}(L),\{\,\Sigma_{a}\mid a\in L\,\})
extends to an spectrum functor
\Sigma\colon{\mathsf{Frm}^{op}\longrightarrow\mathsf{Top}}
which yields an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Top}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Frm}^{op}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Sigma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ \bot}

The category
\mathsf{Loc}
of locales is by definition the opposite category of
\mathsf{Frm}
:

\mathsf{Loc}=\mathsf{Frm}^{op},

and
\Omega
restricts to a full embedding of a substantial part of
\mathsf{Top}
(namely the full subcategory of sober spaces) into
\mathsf{Loc}
. The latter can therefore be seen as a category of generalized spaces. We shall mostly speak of objects in
\mathsf{Loc}
as locales (instead of frames) when emphasizing the covariant approach. Morphisms in
\mathsf{Loc}
can be concretely represented by the right (Galois) adjoints
f_{*}\colon M\longrightarrow L
of the corresponding frame homomorphisms
f\colon L\longrightarrow M
; these will be referred to as
localic maps
.

A frame is said to be
spatial
if
L\cong\Omega(X)
for some space
X
, or equivalently if
L\cong\Omega(\Sigma(L))=\{\,\Sigma_{a}\mid a\in L\,\}
. For any frame
L
, there is a surjective frame homomorphism (the counit of the adjunction)
L\twoheadrightarrow\Omega(\Sigma(L))
sending
a
to
\Sigma_{a}
; this map is usually called the
spatialization
of
L
.

A topological space is
sober
if every prime of
\Omega(X)
is of the form
X\setminus\overline{\{x\}}
for a unique
x\in X
. It is well-known that the space
\mathsf{pt}(L)
defined above is sober for any frame
L
.

Sublocales

A regular subobject in
\mathsf{Loc}
(that is, an isomorphism class of regular monomorphisms) of a locale
L
is
a sublocale
of
L
. Sublocales of a locale
L
can be represented as the actual subsets
S\subseteq L
such that

1.

S
is closed under arbitrary meets in
L
, and

2.

a\to s\in S
for all
a\in L
and
s\in S
.

A different, but equivalent, representation of sublocales is by means of nuclei, i.e. inflationary and idempotent maps
\nu\colon L\longrightarrow L
which preserve binary meets. The sublocale associated to a nucleus
\nu
is the image
\nu[L]
, and conversely the nucleus associated to a sublocale
S\subseteq L
is given by
\iota_{S}\circ\nu_{S}
where
\iota_{S}
denotes the inclusion of
S
into
L
and
\nu_{S}
is its left adjoint frame homomorphism given by

\nu_{S}(a)=\bigwedge\{\,s\in S\mid s\geq a\,\}.

A locale
L
is said to be
totally spatial
if each of its sublocales is spatial.

A sublocale should not be confused with a
subframe
; the latter is a subobject of a frame in the category
\mathsf{Frm}
. Subframes can be represented as subsets which are closed under arbitrary joins and finite meets.

Closed and open sublocales

For each
a\in L
, one has an
open sublocale
and a
closed sublocale

\mathfrak{o}(a)=\{\,b\mid b=a\to b\,\}=\{\,a\to b\mid b\in L\,\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathfrak{c}(a)=\uparrow a

which in the spatial case
L=\Omega(X)
correspond to the open and closed subspaces.

If
S
is a sublocale of
L
, the
closure
of
S
in
L
, denoted by
\overline{S}
, is the smallest closed sublocale containing
S
, which can be computed as
\overline{S}=\mathfrak{c}(\bigwedge S)
.
A sublocale
S
is
dense
if
\overline{S}=L
, or equivalently if
0\in S
. A sublocale
S
is said to be codense if
\nu_{S}(a)=1
implies
a=1
.

Boolean sublocales

For each
a\in L
, there is a sublocale
\mathfrak{b}(a)=\{\,b\to a\mid b\in L\,\}
which turns out to be Boolean. Moreover, every Boolean sublocale of
L
is of the form
\mathfrak{b}(a)
for some
a\in L
. If
p\in L
is a prime, it is easy to check that then
\mathfrak{b}(p)=\{\,1,p\,\}
, and these sublocales are usually referred to as
one-point sublocales
.

In particular, the sublocale
\mathfrak{b}(0)=\{\,x^{*}\mid x\in L\,\}
is called the
Booleanization
of
L
and it can be characterized either as the least dense sublocale of
L
or the unique Boolean dense sublocale of
L
.

The coframe of sublocales

The family

\mathsf{S}(L)

of all sublocales of
L
partially ordered by inclusion is a coframe (i.e. its dual poset
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a frame) and lattice operations in
\mathsf{S}(L)
are given by

\bigwedge_{i\in I}S_{i}=\bigcap_{i\in I}S_{i},\quad\bigvee_{i\in I}S_{i}=\bigl\{\,\bigwedge A\mid A\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}S_{i}\,\bigr\}.

It is not generally the case that
\mathsf{S}(L)
is Boolean but it still has plenty of complemented sublocales: the frame
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is always zero-dimensional. More precisely, every sublocale
S
of
L
can be written
S=\bigcap_{i}\mathfrak{c}(a_{i})\vee\mathfrak{o}(b_{i})
for some
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,
\{b_{i}\}_{\in L}
, and closed sublocales
\mathfrak{c}(a)
and open sublocales
\mathfrak{o}(a)
are complements of each other. We also have

\mathfrak{c}\bigl(\bigvee_{i}a_{i}\bigr)=\bigcap_{i}\mathfrak{c}(a_{i}),\quad\mathfrak{o}\bigl(\bigvee_{i}a_{i}\bigr)=\bigvee_{i}\mathfrak{o}(a_{i}),

\mathfrak{c}(a\wedge b)=\mathfrak{c}(a)\vee\mathfrak{c}(b),\quad\mathfrak{o}(a\wedge b)=\mathfrak{o}(a)\cap\mathfrak{o}(b).

Further, a sublocale is said to be
locally closed
if it is of the form
\mathfrak{o}(a)\cap\mathfrak{c}(b)
for some
a,b\in L
.

Since
\mathsf{S}(L)
is a coframe, there is a
co-Heyting operator
giving the
difference

S\smallsetminus T
of two sublocales
S,T\in\mathsf{S}(L)
; this operator is characterized by the condition

S\smallsetminus T\subseteq R\iff S\subseteq T\vee R.

In particular, the
supplement
of
S\in\mathsf{S}(L)
is
S^{\#}=L\smallsetminus S
, i.e. the smallest sublocale of
L
whose join with
S
is
L
. We shall freely use some its properties, e.g. the ones listed below

1.

S\setminus T\subseteq S
;

2.

S\setminus T=0
iff
S\subseteq T
;

3.

S\setminus C=S\cap C^{\#}
;

4.

S\setminus\bigcap_{i}S_{i}=\bigvee_{i}(S\setminus S_{i})
;

5.

(S\setminus T)\setminus R=(S\setminus R)\setminus T
;

for each
S,T,R\in\mathsf{S}(L)
,
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq\mathsf{S}(L)
and complemented sublocale
C\subseteq L
. A comprehensive list of its properties may be found in
[
5
]
.

For several notions related to coframes, we shall use terminology from frame theory modified just by adding the prefix “co-”. For example, a subset
S
of a coframe
L
will be said to be a
subcolocale
of
L
if
S^{op}
is a sublocale of the frame
L^{op}
(i.e. iff it is a subset of
L
closed under arbitrary joins and the co-Heyting operator). Sometimes we will omit the prefix “co-” in order to avoid confusion: we will speak of a dense (resp. spatial,
T_{D}
-spatial) subcolocale
S
of a coframe
L
for meaning that
S^{op}
is a dense (resp. spatial,
T_{D}
-spatial) sublocale of
L^{op}
.

Images and preimages

Every localic map
f\colon L\longrightarrow M
gives, by pulling back in
\mathsf{Loc}
, an
inverse image
map
f_{-1}[-]\colon\mathsf{S}(M)\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}(L)
which turns out to be a coframe homomorphism (i.e. a function which preserves arbitrary meets and finite joins). Set-theoretic direct image yields a
direct image
map

f[-]\colon\mathsf{S}(L)\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}(M)
which is additionally a colocalic map (i.e. a left adjoint of a coframe homomorphism). In this context, the usual adjunction
f[-]\dashv f_{-1}[-]
is satisfied.

Joins of closed (resp. complemented) sublocales

Let
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
denote the subset of
\mathsf{S}(L)
consisting of joins of closed sublocales i.e.

\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)=\bigl\{\,\bigvee_{a\in A}\mathfrak{c}(a)\mid A\subseteq L\,\bigr\},

endowed with the inclusion order inherited from
\mathsf{S}(L)
. In the recent paper
[
10
]
, Picado, Pultr and Tozzi show (a.o.) that
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
is
always
a frame which is embedded as a join-sublattice in the coframe
\mathsf{S}(L)
. One of the main results from
[
10
]
is that

if
L
is subfit, and only in that case,
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
is a Boolean algebra and coincides precisely with the Booleanization of
\mathsf{S}(L)
.

More generally, if
L
is not necessarily subfit, it is also of interest to consider the Booleanization of
\mathsf{S}(L)
, denoted by
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
. The latter is precisely the system consisting
smooth
sublocales, that is, those which are joins of complemented sublocales (or equivalently joins of locally closed sublocales). A study of the properties of
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
may be found in
[
1
]
.

Subspaces and sublocales

In
[
14
]
, the relation between sublocales of a frame
L
and subspaces of its spectrum
\mathsf{pt}(L)
is explored. Spatial sublocales and sober subspaces are seen as fixpoints of the following adjunction of posets.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{S}(L)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{pt}}

Here,
\mathsf{pt}\colon\mathsf{S}(L)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))
maps each sublocale to the collection of primes contained in it, while
\mathfrak{M}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))\rightarrow\mathsf{S}(L)
takes a collection of primes of
L
and it closes it under arbitrary meets. For every frame
L
, we have that its spatialization is a surjection, and so it corresponds to a certain sublocale that we will denote by
\mathsf{sp}(L)
. For a sublocale
S\subseteq L
, its spatialization sublocale is
\mathfrak{M}(\mathsf{pt}(S))
. Hence, the composition
\mathfrak{M}\circ\mathsf{pt}
of the two adjoints above is spatialization. Similarly, the composition
\mathsf{pt}\circ\mathfrak{M}
is sobrification, up to homeomorphism. For every frame
L
we have the following commuting diagram in the category of coframes. The bottom arrow sends a sublocale
S
to its prime spectrum
\mathsf{pt}(S)
, which simply consists of the collection
\mathsf{pt}(L)\cap S
.

\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
denotes the ordered collection of spatial sublocales of
L
, while
\mathsf{sob}[\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))]
denotes the ordered collection of sober subspaces of
\mathsf{pt}(L)
. Finally, the vertical arrow maps each sublocale

.

The axiom
T_{D}

A space
X
is defined to be
T_{D}
if every point
x\in X
has some neighborhood
U
with
U{\setminus}\{x\}
open. This is an axiom stronger than
T_{0}
and weaker than
T_{1}
and it was introduced in
[
2
]
. It has been used (for instance in
[
11
]
) in order to answer the question of when a topological space can be completely recovered from its frame of opens. The
Skula space
of a space
X
, denoted as
Sk(X)
, is the space defined as follows. The set of points is the same set of points as
X
, while the topology is the
Skula topology
, that is the one generated by the opens of
X
together with their complements. The following characterizations of
T_{D}
-spaces can be found in
[
8
]
.

Proposition 0.1
.

The following are equivalent for a
T_{0}
space
X
.

1.

The space is
T_{D}
.

2.

For no
x\in X
do we have that the dualization of the inclusion
X{\setminus}\{x\}\subseteq X
is a frame isomorphism.

3.

If
Y\nsubseteq Z
then
\Omega^{\prime}(Y)\nsubseteq\Omega^{\prime}(Z)
, for all subspaces
Y,Z\subseteq X
.

4.

The Skula space
Sk(X)
is discrete.

One can exhibit the role of the axiom
T_{D}
in point-free topology by comparing it to sobriety. This has been done in detail in
[
3
]
. In particular, in the context of
T_{0}
spaces, the two axioms mirror each other in that sober spaces are maximal in the same sense in which
T_{D}
-spaces are minimal.

•

A space
X
is sober if and only if we can never have a nontrivial subspace inclusion
X\hookrightarrow Y
such that its dualization is an isomorphism.

•

A space
X
is
T_{D}
if and only if we can never have a nontrivial subspace inclusion
Y\hookrightarrow X
such that its dualization is an isomorphism.

Comparing the two axioms with respect to their implications for subspaces and sublocales yields the following. First, define
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(\Omega(X))]
as being the ordered collection of spatial sublocales of

. We always have a map

. The following is well-known:

•

A space

is sober if and only the map

•

A space

is

if and only if the map
\Omega^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(\Omega(X))]
is an injection.

A frame
L
is said to be
T_{D}
-

if
L\cong\Omega(X)
for some
T_{D}
-topological space

. A

of

is a

such that whenever

for some

, then there is an

with

. In general, it is not true that localic maps map covered primes into covered primes. Following
[
3
]
a frame homomorphism

will be said to be a

-homomorphism
if its associated localic map

sends covered primes into covered primes. The subset

will denote the set of covered primes of

, and we will refer to it as the

of

. For each

\Sigma^{\prime}_{a}=\{\,p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\mid a\not\leq p\,\}
. Then, the family

is a topology on
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
, and in fact
(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L),\{\,\Sigma^{\prime}_{a}\mid a\in L\,\})
is a
T_{D}
-space

]
.

Let
\mathsf{Top}_{D}
be the full subcategory of
\mathsf{Top}
consisting of

-spaces and let
\mathsf{Frm}_{D}
be the non-full subcategory of
\mathsf{Frm}
whose morphisms are the

-homomorphisms.
In
[

]
, the authors showed that the assignment
\Sigma^{\prime}(L)=(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L),\{\,\Sigma^{\prime}_{a}\mid a\in L\,\})
yields an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{Frm}_{D}^{op}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{\Omega}}

Analogously to the case of the classical spectrum, a frame

is
T_{D}
-spatial iff

, and there is always a surjective frame homomorphism (the counit of the adjunction)

. We will refer to
\Omega(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
as the

-spatialization
of

.

1
T_{D}
-spatiality and strong
T_{D}
-spatiality

Among the matters that we will tackle in this paper there is that of studying the system of all
T_{D}
-spatial sublocales of a frame. This is why we begin with an analysis of
T_{D}
-spatiality, and of a natural strengthening of this property which we will call “strong
T_{D}
-spatiality”. We recall that a frame
L
is
T_{D}
-spatial if it is isomorphic to the frame of opens of the subspace of its prime spectrum consisting of the covered primes. A natural strengthening of this condition is the following. We say that a frame
L
is
strongly
T_{D}
-spatial
if it is spatial and all its primes are covered. Thus, a strongly
T_{D}
-spatial is isomorphic to the frame of opens of its
T_{D}
spectrum, and furthermore its
T_{D}
spectrum coincides with the classical (sober) spectrum. In this section we will characterize both these conditions on a frame; in particular we will show that a frame is
T_{D}
-spatial if and only if the Booleanization of its assembly is spatial, and that a frame is strongly
T_{D}
-spatial if and only if the Booleanization of its assembly coincides with its spatialization.

Lemma 1.1

(
[
5
, Proposition 10.2]
)
.

For a frame
L
and a prime
p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)
we have that
\mathfrak{b}(p)
is complemented in
\mathsf{S}(L)
if and only if
p
is a covered prime.

From the fact that
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
is the collection of joins of sublocales complemented in
\mathsf{S}(L)
, we deduce the following corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

For a frame
L
and a prime
p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)
we have that
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
if and only if
\mathfrak{b}(p)
is a covered prime. Then, for a frame
L
and for
\{p_{i}\}_{i\in I}
a collection of covered primes, we have that all joins of the form
\bigvee_{i}\mathfrak{b}(p_{i})
are in
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
.

We recall that a frame
L
is spatial if and only if every element is a meet of primes. We have the following analogous characterization of
T_{D}
-spatiality.

Lemma 1.3
.

A frame is
T_{D}
-spatial if and only if all its elements are meets of covered primes.

Proof.

For the “only if” part, if
L=\Omega(X)
with
X
a
T_{D}
-space and
U
is an open, then
U=\bigwedge_{x\not\in U}X-\overline{\{x\}}
, where each
X-\overline{\{x\}}
is covered. Conversely, it is clear that if every element is a meet of covered primes, then the
T_{D}
-spatialization surjection
L\twoheadrightarrow\Omega(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
defined in the Preliminaries is also one-to-one and hence an isomorphism. Since
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
is a
T_{D}
-space, then
L
is
T_{D}
-spatial.
∎

We are now ready to prove our characterization theorem for
T_{D}
-spatiality.

Theorem 1.4
.

For a frame
L
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is
T_{D}
-spatial;

(2)

Every element of
L
is a meet of covered primes;

(3)

\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
is spatial;

(4)

\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
.

Proof.

The equivalence between (1) and (3) appears in
[
1
, Theorem 6.2]
and the equivalence between (1) and (2) follows by Lemma
1.3
.
Let us now show that (3) implies (4). If
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
is spatial, as this is a Boolean algebra it must be isomorphic to the powerset of its primes. By Corollary
1.2
, there is a bijection
\mathsf{pt}(\mathsf{S}_{b}(L))\cong\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
. Finally note that (4) implies (3) is trivial.∎

We now move on to looking at strong
T_{D}
-spatiality.

Lemma 1.5
.

A frame
L
is spatial if and only if the spatialization of
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a dense frame surjection.

Proof.

A frame
L
is spatial if and only if
L
is a fixpoint of the interior
\mathsf{sp}\colon\mathsf{S}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}(L)
, that is, if and only if the bottom element
L\in\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a fixpoint of the spatialization nucleus
\mathsf{sp}\colon\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}\rightarrow\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
.
∎

Since
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
is the least dense sublocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)
then one has the following:

Corollary 1.6
.

For a frame
L
the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is spatial;

(2)

\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
.

We may now prove the characterization theorem for strong
T_{D}
-spatiality.

Theorem 1.7
.

For a frame
L
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is strongly
T_{D}
-spatial;

(2)

Every element of
L
is a meet of covered primes and every prime of
L
is covered;

(3)

The frame
L
is spatial and
\mathsf{pt}(L)
is a
T_{D}
-space;

(4)

The frame
L
is isomorphic to
\Omega(X)
for some sober
T_{D}
-space
X
;

(5)

The frame
L
is spatial and we have an isomorphism
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))
;

(6)

\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)=\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2). This follows from Lemma
1.3
.

(2)
\implies
(3). If every element of
L
is a meet of primes, the frame
L
is spatial. Furthermore, if all primes of
L
are covered we have that
\mathsf{pt}(L)
is a
T_{D}
-space, as it coincides with the
T_{D}
spectrum of
L
.

(3)
\implies
(4). This is clear as
\mathsf{pt}(L)
is always sober.

(4)
\implies
(5). We recall that if a space
X
is
T_{D}
then the map
\Omega^{\prime}\colon\mathcal{P}(X)\rightarrow\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(\Omega(X))]
is injective, while if it is sober it is surjective (see the subsection on the
T_{D}
axiom in the preliminaries). Thus, if
L\cong\Omega(X)
for some
T_{D}
and sober space
X
we have an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))\cong\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{pt}(L))]
.

(5)
\implies
(6). If the frame

is spatial and we have an isomorphism
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\cong\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))
, then in particular the subcolocale
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}(L)
is Boolean. As
L
is spatial it is also dense (see Lemma
1.5
), and so it must be its Booleanization.

(6)
\implies
(1). By Corollary
1.6
,
L
is spatial. Let

be a prime of
L
. Then
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, and so by Corollary

it follows that
p
is covered.∎

2
D
-sublocales

We now investigate the notion equivalent to that of sublocale in the
T_{D}
-duality; the notion of
D
-sublocale
. For a sublocale to count as a
D
-sublocale, we will require that its corresponding localic inclusion is a morphism in the category
\mathsf{Loc}_{D}
. This constraint amounts to the condition that the localic map ought to send covered primes to covered primes. In this section we will analyze the collection
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}(L)
of
D
-sublocales of a frame
L
. In particular, we will show that
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}(L)
is always a dense subcolocale. We then will explore the question of how close the assignment
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)(-)\colon\mathsf{Obj}(\mathsf{Frm})\rightarrow\mathsf{Obj}(\mathsf{Frm})
is to being functorial.
It is known that localic maps do not generally send covered primes into covered primes; those frame homomorphisms such that their right adjoint localic maps send covered primes into covered primes were referred to as
D
-homorphisms in
[
8
]
.
Now we introduce one of the central notions in this paper: a sublocale
S
of
L
will be said to be a
D
-
sublocale
if the corresponding frame surjection
L\twoheadrightarrow S
is a
D
-homomorphism, i.e. iff the equality
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)=\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\cap S
(equivalently the inclusion
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
holds.

In general, not every sublocale is a
D
-sublocale, and neither is an intersection of two
D
-sublocales, as the following example shows:

Remark 2.1
.

One of the simplest examples of an intersections of two
D
-sublocales which is not a
D
-sublocale seems to be the following: let
L=[0,1]
(the unit interval with its usual total order). A subset of a totally ordered set is a sublocale iff it is closed under meets, so the following two subsets are indeed sublocales:

S=\{\,0,1\,\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2n},\frac{1}{2n-1}\bigg)\quad\textrm{and}\quad T=\{\,0,1\,\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2n+1},\frac{1}{2n}\bigg).

Obviously, for every
x\neq 1
in
S
, one has
x=\bigwedge_{x<t\in S}t
, and this shows that
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)=\varnothing
, so
S
is a
D
-sublocale of
L
. Similarly,
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(T)=\varnothing
and so it is also a
D
-sublocale of
L
. Now, observe that
S\cap T=\{\,0,1\,\}
and then one has
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S\cap T)=\mathsf{pt}(S\cap T)=\{0\}
. But
0
is not a covered prime in
L
as
0=\bigwedge_{t>0}t
. Hence
S\cap T
is not a
D
-sublocale.

Let
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
be the subset of
\mathsf{S}(L)
consisting of
D
-sublocales of
L
, equipped with the inherited order. We observe that we have the following.

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\{\,S\in\mathsf{S}(L)\mid\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\,\}.

In what follows, we investigate the structure of
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
:

Proposition 2.2
.

The system
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
is closed under arbitrary joins in
\mathsf{S}(L)
.

Proof.

Let
\{S_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
, i.e.
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{i})\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
for all
i\in I
. We have to check that
\bigvee_{i}S_{i}\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
, that is,
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\bigvee_{i}S_{i})\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
. Let then
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\bigvee_{i}S_{i})
. Since
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\bigvee_{i}S_{i})\subseteq\bigvee_{i}S_{i}
, there is a family
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subseteq L
such that
a_{i}\in S_{i}
for each
i\in I
and with
p=\bigwedge_{i}a_{i}
. Since for all
i\in I
one has
a_{i}\in S_{i}\subseteq\bigvee_{i}S_{i}
and
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\bigvee_{i}S_{i})
, then there is an
i_{0}\in I
such that
p=a_{i_{0}}\in S_{i_{0}}
. We claim that
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{i_{0}})
. Indeed, let
\{b_{j}\}_{j\in J}\subseteq S_{i_{0}}
a family such that
p=\bigwedge_{j}b_{j}
. Then
b_{j}\in S_{i_{0}}\subseteq\bigvee_{i}S_{i}
and since
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\bigvee_{i}S_{i})
, there is a
j_{0}\in J
with
p=b_{j_{0}}
. Then
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{i_{0}})\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

If
S\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
and
T\in\mathsf{S}(L)
, then
S\setminus T\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
.

Proof.

Let
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S\setminus T)
. Since
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is zero-dimensional, write
T=\bigcap_{i}C_{i}
with
C_{i}
complemented. Then
S\setminus T=S\setminus\bigcap_{i}C_{i}=\bigvee_{i}S\setminus C_{i}=\bigvee_{i}S\cap C_{i}^{c}
. Now,
p\in S\setminus T=\bigvee_{i}S\cap C_{i}^{c}
and so there is a family
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}
with
a_{i}\in S\cap C_{i}^{c}
and
p=\bigwedge_{i}a_{i}
. Since
a_{i}\in S\cap C_{i}^{c}\subseteq S\setminus T
and
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S\setminus T)
, then there is an
i_{0}\in I
with
p=a_{i_{0}}\in C_{i_{0}}^{c}
. Since
T=\bigcap_{i}C_{i}
, then we conclude
p\not\in T
. Now,
p\not\in T
is equivalent to
\mathfrak{b}(p)\not\subseteq T
, which is in turn equivalent to
\mathfrak{b}(p)\setminus T\neq 0
. But
\mathfrak{b}(p)\setminus T\subseteq\mathfrak{b}(p)=\{\,1,p\,\}
, and so
\mathfrak{b}(p)\setminus T=\mathfrak{b}(p)
.
I claim that
\mathfrak{b}(p)\not\subseteq S\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p)
. By way of contradiction assume otherwise. Then
(S\setminus T)\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p)=(S\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p))\setminus T\supseteq\mathfrak{b}(p)\setminus T=\mathfrak{b}(p)
. Then
0\neq\mathfrak{b}(p)=\mathfrak{b}(p)\cap[(S\setminus T)\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p)]
, which is a contradiction since
\mathfrak{b}(p)
is complemented in
S\setminus T
. Therefore, we must have
\mathfrak{b}(p)\not\subseteq S\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p)
, which is equivalent to
0=\mathfrak{b}(p)\cap(S\setminus\mathfrak{b}(p))
. Then
\mathfrak{b}(p)
is complemented in
S
, i.e.
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
, as we wanted.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
is a dense
D
-subcolocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)
. In particular it is a coframe.

Proof.

The fact that it is a subcolocale follows from the two previous propositions. Moreover, density follows from the obvious fact that
L
(i.e. the bottom element of
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
) belongs to
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. Finally, since
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a zero-dimensional frame, it is in particular regular; and it is well-known that primes in any regular frame are maximal (so in particular they are covered). Thus, every sublocale of
\mathsf{S}(L)^{op}
is a
D
-sublocale.
∎

Density of
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
has an important consequence: it implies that
\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. Hence, all open sublocales and closed sublocales are
D
-sublocales, and so are locally closed sublocales or more generally smooth sublocales.

The system
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
contains several different families of sublocales, for example:

1.

All smooth sublocales, as we have just pointed out;

2.

All pointless sublocales of
L
(as
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\subseteq\mathsf{pt}(S)=\varnothing
);

3.

Any join of pointless sublocales (joins of pointless sublocales may contain points, but their join will still be a
D
-sublocale because of Proposition
2.2
);

4.

All codense sublocales of
L
in which primes are maximal (these include codense naturally Hausdorff sublocales, codense fit sublocales,…). This only of interest if
L
not subfit, cf.
[
4
]
. In order to show this assertion, one easily checks that if
p
is a maximal element in a codense sublocale

, then it is maximal in

, and in particular it belongs to
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a frame

Proof.

By the universal property of the

-spatialization, this is the largest

-spatial. Suppose that

is a

-spatial

3.1
Local and global
T_{D}
-spatialization

It follows from Lemma
3.2
that the map
\mathfrak{M}\circ\mathsf{pt}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
assigns to each
D
-sublocale its
T_{D}
-spatialization. From now on, we will call this map
\mathsf{sp}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
.
Let us see what more we can learn about this operator.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
L
is a complete lattice and
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
is an interior operators, then
\iota[L]
is a complete lattice where the joins in are computed as in
L
, and the meets are computed as
\bigwedge^{\iota}\iota(x_{i})=\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
.

Proof.

Suppose that
L
is a complete lattice and that
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
is an interior operator. Suppose that
x_{i}\in L
. We claim that
\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i})
is the join of
\{\,\iota(x_{i})\mid i\in I\,\}
in
\iota[L]
. first, we show that
\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))=\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i})
. One inequality holds since
\iota
is deflationary. For the other, we notice that
\iota(x_{i})\leq\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i})
. By monotonicity and idempotence of
\iota
, it follows that
\iota(x_{i})\leq\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. The desired result follows. Let us show that
\bigvee_{i}^{\iota}\iota(x_{i})=\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. On the one hand we showed before that
\iota(x_{i})\leq\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. On the other hand, if we have
\iota(x_{i})\leq\iota(y)
for some
y\in L
we also have that
\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))\leq\iota(y)
, by monotonicity and idempotence of
\iota
. Then, indeed
\bigvee_{i}^{\iota}\iota(x_{i})=\iota(\bigvee_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. Now let us show that
\bigwedge_{i}^{\iota}\iota(x_{i})=\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. On the one hand we have
\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i})\leq\iota(x_{i})
and so
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))\leq\iota(x_{i})
by monotonicity and idempotence. On the other hand if
\iota(y)\leq\iota(x_{i})
then we also have
\iota(y)\leq\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i})
and so
\iota(y)\leq\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
by monotonicity and idempotence.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

If
L
is a complete lattice and
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
an interior operator on it, the surjection
\iota\colon L\twoheadrightarrow\iota[L]
preserves arbitrary meets. If
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
preserves finite joins, this surjection preserves finite joins, too.

Proof.

Suppose that
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
is an interior operator. Suppose that
x_{i}\in L
. We first claim that
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})=\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. We have that
\iota(x_{i})\leq x_{i}
since
\iota
is deflationary, and thus
\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i})\leq\bigwedge_{i}x_{i}
, from which we deduce
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))\leq\iota(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})
by monotonicity of
\iota
. For the reverse inequality, we start from the inequality
\bigwedge_{i}x_{i}\leq x_{i}
. By monotonicity of
\iota
we deduce
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})\leq\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i})
. Finally, by monotonicity and idempotence, we deduce
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})\leq\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))
. We have shown that
\iota(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})=\iota(\bigwedge_{i}\iota(x_{i}))=\bigwedge^{\iota}\iota(x_{i})
, where the last equality holds by Lemma
3.3
. Suppose now that
\iota\colon L\rightarrow L
preserves finite joins. For
x,y\in L
we have that
\iota(x\vee y)=\iota(x)\vee\iota(y)=\iota(x)\vee^{\iota}\iota(y)
, where the last equality holds by Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

For a frame
L
, the map
\mathsf{sp}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
is an interior operator which preserves finite joins.

Proof.

The map is monotone since it is the composition of the monotone maps
\mathsf{pt}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
and
\mathfrak{M}\colon\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. The map is clearly deflationary. Idempotence follows from the adjunction identity
\mathfrak{M}\circ\mathsf{pt}_{D}\circ\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{M}
. For preservation of finite joins, suppose that we have a finite collection
S_{m}\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. Since these are
D
-sublocales and by primality of the elements in
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
, we have that
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{1}\vee...\vee S_{n})=\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\cap(S_{1}\vee...\vee S_{n})=\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\cap(S_{1}\cup...\cup S_{n})=(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\cap S_{1})\cup...\cup(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)\cap S_{n})=\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{1})\cup...\cup\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S_{n})
. We have shown that
\mathsf{pt}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))
preserves finite joins. As
\mathfrak{M}
is a left adjoint, it preserves all joins, in particular finite ones. Thus the composition
\mathfrak{M}\circ\mathsf{pt}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
preserves finite joins.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Suppose that
L
is a frame.cThe map
\mathsf{sp}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{sp}_{D}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)]
is a coframe surjection whose codomain is the ordered collection of the

-spatial
D

.

Proof.

The fact that we have a coframe surjection follows from Lemma

-spatial

∎

Remark 3.7
.

The operator
\mathsf{sp}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\rightarrow\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
actually preserves arbitrary joins, too. In the proof of Lemma

, we have shown that for a finite collection

of sublocales we have that

for an arbitrary collection

the congruence associated with the sublocale

is a

[

).

We then have a local

-spatialization surjection
\mathsf{sp}_{D}\colon\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{sp}_{D}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)]
. As
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)^{op}
is a frame, it has its own

-spatialization sublocale; its global
T_{D}
-spatialization subcolocale, so to speak. We want to know how the local
T_{D}
-spatialization subcolocale
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
relates to the global one.

Lemma 3.8
.

For a frame
L
, a
D
-sublocale

is

-spatial if and only if
S=\bigvee\{\,\mathfrak{b}(p)\mid p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\,\}

Proof.

By Corollary

, a

-sublocale

is

-spatial if and only if
\mathfrak{M}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S))=S
. But
\mathfrak{M}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S))=\bigvee\{\,\mathfrak{b}(p)\mid p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)\,\}
and so we are done.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

The covered prime elements of
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)^{op}
are exactly the sublocales of the form
\mathfrak{b}(p)
with
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
. We claim that
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)^{op}
is a covered prime. Indeed, we observe that if we have
\mathfrak{b}(p)=\bigvee_{i}S_{i}
we clearly must have
S_{j}=\{\,1,p\,\}
for some

.

For the converse, suppose that

and that

for each

. Since for each prime

we have that
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
if and only if

is covered, we must have that
S
cannot be a two-element sublocale, and so it contains at least three elements. Suppose, then, that
\{\,x,y,1\,\}
are pairwise distinct and that
\{\,x,y\,\}\subseteq S
. Suppose, in particular, that
x\nleq y
. First, recall that all open and all closed sublocales of
L
are elements of
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. We claim that
S\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(x)\vee\mathfrak{c}(x)
is a nontrivial decomposition of
S
. We have that
x\rightarrow x=1\neq x
, and so
x\notin\mathfrak{o}(x)
, hence
S\nsubseteq\mathfrak{o}(x)
. On the other hand, we have that

and so

\mathfrak{c}(x)\vee\mathfrak{o}(x)=1
we have
S\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(x)\vee\mathfrak{c}(x)
.

Remark 3.14
.

It should be noted that
\mathsf{sp}_{D}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)]
is not only spatial, but it is also Boolean for any frame

and the fact that

is the identity that it is actually isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L))

determined by the element
\mathsf{sp}_{D}(L)
, i.e. we have
\mathsf{sp}_{D}[\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)]=\mathfrak{b}(\mathsf{sp}_{D}(L))

We wish to stress that the behaviour of the operator

exhibits a difference between the spatial-sober duality and the

) we have an adjunction

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{pt}(L))}

are precisely the spatial sublocales, and the fixpoints of
\mathsf{pt}\circ\mathfrak{M}
are the sober subspaces of
\mathsf{pt}(L)
. None of the two compositions is the identity. In particular, since not all subspaces of

are sober, this means that the map

do not arise as spectra of sublocales of

arise as spectra of

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\cong)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces=}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{pt}_{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{M}\circ\mathsf{pt}_{D}}

a
if
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{p\}\neq a
. It is clear that absolute essentiality is a condition stronger than essentiality. The terminology “absolutely” essential is justified by the following result.

Lemma 3.15
.

For a frame
L
and for
a\in L
with
a=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
and for
p\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

The prime
p
is an absolutely essential prime of
a
;

(2)

Whenever
a=\bigwedge P
for
P\subseteq\mathsf{pt}(L)
we must have
p\in P
;

(3)

The prime
p
is weakly covered and it is an essential prime of
a
.

Proof.

Let
L
be a frame, and let
a\in L
be an element such that it is the meet of the primes above it. Let
p\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
.

(1)
\implies
(2). Suppose that
p
is an absolutely essential prime of
a
, and that
P\subseteq\mathsf{pt}(L)
is such that
a=\bigwedge P
. We must have that
P\subseteq\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
and so
P{\setminus}\{p\}\subseteq\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{p\}
, from which we deduce that
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{p\}\leq\bigwedge P{\setminus}\{p\}
. From this fact, and by absolute essentiality of
p
, we deduce that
\bigwedge P{\setminus}\{p\}\nleq a
. As
\bigwedge P\leq a
, we must have
p\in P
.

(2)
\implies
(3). Suppose that
p\in P
whenever
\bigwedge P=a
for
P\subseteq\mathsf{pt}(L)
. In particular, we have that
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\nleq a
as
p\notin\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p
. Now suppose towards contradiction that
p
is not weakly covered, and so that
p=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}p){\setminus}\{p\}
. We then have that
\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p\neq q\,\}=\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p<q\,\}\wedge\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p\nleq q\,\}=p\wedge\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\leq a
. We have then contradicted (2).

(3)
\implies
(1). Suppose that
p
is an essential prime of
a
and that it is weakly covered. if we had
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\leq p
then we would have that
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\leq q
whenever
q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
, contradicting essentiality of
p
. Then, we must have
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\nleq p
. We also have
\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p<q\,\}\nleq p
by weak coveredness of
p
. By primality of
p
, this means that
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\wedge\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p<q\,\}=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{p\}\nleq p
, and so
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{p\}\nleq a
. Thus,
p
is an absolutely essential prime of
a
.
∎

Thus, we have called this condition “absolute” essentiality as opposed to relative essentiality: an essential prime of
a
is only essential relative to the particular meet of primes
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p<q\,\}
; whereas an absolutely essential prime is essential with respect to any meet of primes
\bigwedge P
with
\bigwedge P=a
. It is crucial that for covered primes these two conditions collapse together.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
p
is a covered prime and
p
is an essential prime of
a\in L
, then
p
is also an absolutely essential prime of
a
.

Proof.

If
p
is an essential prime of
a
and it is covered, in particular it is weakly covered and so it is absolutely essential, by Lemma
3.15
.
∎

Proposition 3.17

For a frame

Let us prove some general facts about essential and absolutely essential primes.

Lemma 3.18
.

For a frame
L
and an element
a
which is the meet of the primes above it, we have that
p\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)
is an essential prime of
a
if and only if it is a prime of
\mathfrak{b}(a)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
L
is a frame and that
a\in L
is the meet of the primes above it. Suppose first that
p
is an essential prime of
a
. We claim that
p\in\mathfrak{b}(a)
because we have

p=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\rightarrow a=\bigwedge\{\,\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\rightarrow q\mid q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\,\}.

The right hand side is a conjunction, in which every conjunct is either
1
or a prime above
a
. Then, this is equal to

\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){{\uparrow}}p\nleq q\,\}

As
p
is an essential prime, it is certainly in the set
\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\nleq q\,\}
. It must be the minimal element of this set, as for an element
q
to be in this set we need to have
p\leq q
. Then, indeed,
p=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\rightarrow a
and so
p\in\mathfrak{b}(a)
. For the converse, suppose that we have
p\in\mathfrak{b}(a)
. Then, we have that
(p\rightarrow a)\rightarrow a=p
. This means that

p=\bigwedge\{\,(p\rightarrow a)\rightarrow q\mid q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\,\}

The right hand side is again a conjunct of primes, and in particular it is

\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p\rightarrow a\nleq q\,\}

Now, let us observe that
p\rightarrow a
is exactly
\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid p\nleq q\,\}=\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p
. Then, the expression above equals

\bigwedge\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\nleq q\,\}.

Since this expression equals
p
, if we had
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\leq p
, we would also have
\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\leq q
for every element
q
of the set
\{\,q\in\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\mid\bigwedge\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a){\setminus}{\uparrow}p\nleq q\,\}
, as
p
is a lower bound for this set. This is a contradiction, by the membership condition of this set. The prime
p
must then be essential.
∎

Lemma 3.19
.

For a frame
L
and for
x,y\in L
we have that
\mathfrak{b}(x\rightarrow y)=\mathfrak{o}(x)\cap\mathfrak{b}(y)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
z\in\mathfrak{b}(x\rightarrow y)
. Then, we have that
z=z^{\prime}\rightarrow(x\rightarrow y)
for some
z^{\prime}\in L
. Then,
z=(z^{\prime}\wedge x)\rightarrow y
and so
z\in\mathfrak{b}(y)
. Additionally, we have
x\rightarrow z=x\rightarrow(z^{\prime}\rightarrow(x\rightarrow y))=(x\wedge z^{\prime})\rightarrow(x\rightarrow y)=(x\wedge z^{\prime})\rightarrow y=z^{\prime}\rightarrow(x\rightarrow y)=z
. Then,
z\in\mathfrak{o}(x)
. For the converse, suppose that
z\in\mathfrak{o}(x)\cap\mathfrak{b}(y)
. Then, we have
x\rightarrow z=z
and also that
z=z^{\prime}\rightarrow y
for some
z^{\prime}\in L
. Then,
z=x\rightarrow(z^{\prime}\rightarrow y)=x\wedge z^{\prime}\rightarrow y=z^{\prime}\rightarrow(x\rightarrow y)
, and so
z\in\mathfrak{b}(x\rightarrow y)
.
∎

Lemma 3.20
.

For a frame
L
, we have that it is spatial and every element other than
1
has an absolutely essential prime above it if and only if every element is the meet of the essential primes above it.

Proof.

If
L
is a frame in which every element is the meet of the essential primes above it, in particular every element

with

must have at least an essential prime above it. The frame is also spatial. For the other implication, suppose that
L
is spatial and that every element other than
1
has an absolutely essential prime above it. Let
a\in L
and let
\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)
be the collection of absolutely essential primes of
a
. Suppose towards contradiction that
\bigwedge\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)\nleq a
. Then,
\bigwedge\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)\rightarrow a\neq 1
and so there is an absolutely essential prime

of
\bigwedge\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)\rightarrow a
. This means that we have

(see Lemma
3.18
and
3.19
) and that

is weakly covered. Since

we have

\bigwedge\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)\nleq q
. Since
q\in\mathfrak{b}(a)
and so it is an absolutely essential prime of
a
. This contradicts that
\bigwedge\mathsf{AbsEss}(a)\nleq q
.
∎

For a frame

Proof.

(1) obviously implies (2).

(3)

(5)
\implies

(6)
\implies

(7)
\implies
(1). Clearly, the condition (7) is hereditary with respect to any sublocale. Hence, it suffices to show that

4
The relation between
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
and
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]

We begin this section with a non-spatial version of Theorem
1.7
, that is, we explore locales in which every prime is covered. Note that this is the
T_{D}
analogue of the point-free notion of
T_{1}

[

]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For a frame
L
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

All primes of
L
are covered;

(2)

\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
;

(3)

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\mathsf{S}(L)
;

(4)

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
is closed under arbitrary intersections in
\mathsf{S}(L)
;

(5)

\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
.

Proof.

Suppose that (1) holds. Then, (2) holds by Corollary
1.2
. Suppose that (2) holds and let
S
an arbitrary sublocale. If
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)
, then in particular
p\in\mathsf{pt}(S)
and so
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
. By Lemma
1.1
, one has that
p
is covered in
L
, and so
S\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. That (3) implies (4) is obvious and (4) implies (3) because every sublocale is an intersection of complemented sublocales (and hence of
D
-sublocales). Assume now that (3) holds and let
p
be a prime. Then
\mathfrak{b}(p)\in\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
and so
p\in\mathsf{pt}_{D}(\mathfrak{b}(p))\subseteq\mathsf{pt}_{D}(L)
. Hence (1) follows.
∎

We can use
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
for characterizing a number of well-known properties for a frame
L
. For example we have the following (but see also Theorem
3.21
or Theorem
4.1
).

Proposition 4.2
.

For a frame
L
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

L
is totally spatial
;

(2)

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2). This is trivial since if
L
is totally spatial then
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]=\mathsf{S}(L)
.

(2)
\implies
(1). By the observation preceding the proposition, every pointless sublocale of
L
is in
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
, and so by assumption, every pointless sublocale is spatial. Thus
L
has no nontrivial pointless sublocales, i.e. every nontrivial sublocale of
L
has a point. By
[
7
, p. 269]
, it follows that
L
is totally spatial. ∎

Next we explore the inclusion
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
(note that the inclusion is actually equivalent to the equality
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, since every frame has a unique Boolean and dense sublocale). We first give a necessary condition for it:

Proposition 4.3
.

If
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
then
\mathsf{sp}(L)
is totally spatial.

Proof.

Let
S
be a pointless sublocale contained in
\mathsf{sp}(L)
. Then
S\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
.
It is easy to show that one always has
\mathsf{S}_{b}(\mathsf{sp}(L))\supseteq\downarrow^{\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)}\mathsf{sp}(L)
. It follows that
S\in\mathsf{S}_{b}(\mathsf{sp}(L))
. But
\mathsf{sp}(L)
is spatial and so
S\in\mathsf{S}_{b}(\mathsf{sp}(L))\subseteq\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(\mathsf{sp}(L))]
, but a spatial pointless sublocale must be trivial. Hence
\mathsf{sp}(L)
does not contain any nontrivial pointless sublocales, which by
[
7
, p. 269]
implies that
\mathsf{sp}(L)
is totally spatial.
∎

We now characterize frames for which the inclusion
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
holds. Despite the fact that we have not been able to find a clear geometric condition as those occurring in other results of the paper (spatiality, total spatiality, scatteredness,
T_{D}
-spatiality,…), condition (2) below indeed tells us something: the assumption in (1) (i.e. that a given sublocale
S
is a
D
-sublocale) is relative to

, whereas the assumption in (2) is absolute, in the sense that it does not depend on the ambient frame

:

Proposition 4.4
.

For a frame
L
, the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
(i.e. every
D
-sublocale is smooth);

(2)

Sublocales of

without covered primes in themselves are smooth in

.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2). Let
S
be a sublocale such that
\mathsf{pt}_{D}(S)=\varnothing
. Then
S\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
so it is smooth.

(2)
\implies
(1). Let
S\in\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
, and consider the decomposition

. Observe that by Proposition

,

is a

and Lemma

. Hence

First, let us connect the frame of joins of closed sublocales with the spatialization sublocale of

Lemma 5.1
.

A frame

\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
.

Proof.

Suppose that
L

. By spatiality,

, but since all primes above

\bigwedge(\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a)\cap{\uparrow}a)=\bigwedge\{\,p\in{\uparrow}a\mid b\leq p\,\}

{\uparrow}a\subseteq\mathfrak{M}(\mathsf{pt}({\uparrow}a))

{\uparrow}a
is spatial. Conversely, if
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

A frame
L
is such that all its primes are maximal if and only if
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
.

Proof.

Suppose that for a frame
L
all its primes are maximal. This means that for every prime
p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)
we have that
\mathfrak{b}(p)
is closed, and since the elements of
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]
are all joins of elements of the form
\mathfrak{b}(p)
, we have
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
. Conversely, if we have a prime
p\in L
which is not maximal, say
p<x<1
, we have that
\mathfrak{b}(p)\neq\mathfrak{c}(p)
, and since
\mathfrak{b}(p)
does not equal its closure it is not closed. Then,
\mathsf{sp}[\mathsf{S}(L)]\nsubseteq\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
.
∎

Secondly, we look at the relation between
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
and
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
. First, we observe that we always have
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)
(we know that all closed sublocales are
D
-sublocales, and the collection of
D
-sublocales is closed under joins).

Proposition 5.3
.

For a frame
L
, we have that
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
if and only if
L
is subfit and
D
-scattered.

Proof.

Suppose that for a frame
L
we have
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
. We have that all closed sublocales are complemented, and so
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
. We then have that
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, which means that
L
is
D
-scattered. Furthermore, as this implies also that
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, we also have that
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
, which means that
L
is subfit (see
[
9
]
). Conversely, suppose that
L
is
D
-scattered and subfit. Since
L
is subfit, we have that
\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
(see
[
9
]
), and since
L
is
D
-scattered, we have that
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{b}(L)
. Hence,
\mathsf{S}_{D}(L)=\mathsf{S}_{c}(L)
.
∎
