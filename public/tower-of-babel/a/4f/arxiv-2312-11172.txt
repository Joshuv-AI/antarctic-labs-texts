First variation of functional Wulff shapes
Jacopo Ulivelli

Abstract
Using an approximation procedure we establish, for suitable perturbations, a weighted functional version of Aleksandrov’s variational lemma in the family of convex functions with compact domain. The resulting formula is then applied to evaluate the first variation of a class of functionals on log-concave functions. In particular, we extend a recent result by Huang, Liu, Xi, and Zhao [ 27 ] . † † MSC 2020 Classification: 52A20, 52A38, 52A41, 49J53. Keywords: Convex function, convex body, Fenchel-Legendre transform, Wulff shape.

Consider the family

Theorem 1.1

(Rotem)
.

Let

\begin{gathered}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{n}(u\square(t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}v))\right|_{t=0^{+}}\\

(4)

Equivalently, (

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{n}(u\square(t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}v))\right|_{t=0^{+}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v^{*}(y)\,d\mathcal{M}_{u}(y)+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,d\mathcal{S}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\nu}),

For

For

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathrm{Vol}(F_{t}K)\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f\,dS_{K}.

(6)

In

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, find sufficient and necessary conditions so that there exists

[

\zeta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with compact support and
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),t\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the perturbation

(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}.

(7)

A functional version of (

[

(later extended in
[

[

\zeta

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{n}((u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*})\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))e^{-u(x)}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(y)\,d\mathcal{M}_{u}(y),

(8)

where
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is coercive, that is,
\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u(x)=+\infty
.

A striking difference between the correspondences (
2
)-(
5
) and (
6
)-(
8
), is that (
8
) lacks the boundary term appearing in (
5
). This is where our work starts: We propose a method to compute the first variation of
\mu_{n}((u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}))
for a family of functions which allows the appearance of a boundary term in (
8
). In particular, this is achieved for
u
in

\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\{u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\mathrm{dom}(u)\text{ is compact}\}

and
\zeta
in

C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\{\zeta\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\rho_{\zeta}\text{ is continuous and }|\rho_{\zeta}-\zeta|\text{ is bounded}\}

where
\rho_{\zeta}
is the recession function of
\zeta
, defined as

\rho_{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\coloneqq\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\zeta(t\boldsymbol{\nu})}{t},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},

and then extended homogeneously on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Notice that if
v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then
v^{*}\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Therefore, by the properties of the Fenchel-Legendre transform, our approach includes perturbations by infimal convolution, covering the case (
3
) in
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We note that the choice of these families of functions is an inheritance vice of the method at hand. Indeed, our strategy will be to approximate the epi-graph of
(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}
via a sequence of compact Wulff shapes (see Section 3 for more details) with specific properties, allowing a limit process which might not be possible in general. Yet,
C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
extends substantially the family of suitable perturbations since
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
does not need to have compact support, nor to be bounded. See Remark
3.6
for further details on the choice of
C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Our main result, which will be later exposed in wider generality (that is, for a whole class of functionals including (
1
)) in Theorem
3.15
, reads as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{n}((u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*})\right|_{t=0}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))e^{-u(x)}\,dx+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))e^{-u(x)}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(y)\,d\mathcal{M}_{u}(y)+\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\rho_{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,d\mathcal{S}_{u}(\boldsymbol{\nu}).

In Theorem
3.15
we will prove that a variational formula in the spirit of (
9
) still holds considering different measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
other than
\mu_{n}
, under suitable summability assumptions. This is achieved through some recent results from the weighted Brunn-Minkowski theory. See Section 3 for more details and references. As pointed out later in Remark
3.9
, this kind of result can be interpreted as a Reynolds-type transport theorem under the flow induced by a Hamilton-Jacobi equation.

As a byproduct, we can extend (
9
) in the context of the singular integrals considered in
[
27
]
. In particular, we can remove some hypotheses in the setting of coercive convex functions, which were needed in
[
27
]
, and obtain further improvements in the restricted frame of
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
[
27
, Theorem 1.3]
. In particular, for
q>0
, we obtain a formula for the one-sided derivative of the functional

\mu_{q}(u)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx,\hskip 10.00002ptu\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
u,v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and choose
q>0
. If
0<\mu_{q}(u)<+\infty
,
v\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and
v(0),u(0)<+\infty
, then,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu_{q}(u\square(t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}v))\right|_{t=0^{+}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v^{*}(\nabla u(x))e^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx+

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}h_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))e^{-u(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

This work is structured as follows. In Section 2, we present some preliminaries on convex sets and convex functions. In Section 3, we justify the notion of functional Wulff shape and, after some technical results on weighted surface area measures, we prove our main result in Theorem

2
Preliminaries

The ambient space where we work is the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\in\mathbb{N}
. At times, we switch the point of view to
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and for the standard basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1}\}
we identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the hyperplane
H
orthogonal to
e_{n+1}
. The origin is denoted by
o
in both cases. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote their scalar product by
x\cdot y
and write
|x|=\sqrt{x\cdot x}
. The dimension of the ambient space will always be clear from the context, and we use the uppercase
X,Y
when working in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathrm{int}A
denotes its interior.

2.1
Convex sets

We start by summarizing some notions and results from the classical theory of convex sets.
Of particular interest is the family of non-empty compact convex sets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\mathcal{K}^{n}
, endowed with the Hausdorff metric. The family of convex bodies, i.e., the elements of
\mathcal{K}^{n}
with non-empty interior, is denoted
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{n}
. For an exhaustive exposition on the topic, see, for example, Schneider’s monograph
[
51
]
.

To every convex sets
K
we can associate a function to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, called support function, defined as

h_{K}(x)\coloneq\sup\{x\cdot y:y\in K\},\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Clearly,
h_{K}
is finite if and only if
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
. The behaviour of these functions with respect to Minkowski addition is encoded in the following property: for every convex set
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

h_{K}+h_{L}=h_{K+L}.

A further representation of a convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by its indicator function, defined as

\displaystyle I_{K}(x)\coloneq\begin{cases}0\qquad&\text{ if }x\in K,\\
+\infty\quad&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Another class of functions representing a family of convex sets is the one of radial functions. If
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
is such that
o\in\mathrm{int}K
, the radial function of
K
is defined as

r_{K}(x)\coloneqq\max\{s>0:sx\in K\},\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{o\}.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and consider its boundary
\partial K
. When
K
is a
C^{2}_{+}
body, the Gauss map

N_{K}:\partial K\to\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

gives for each
x\in\partial K
the unit normal vector to
\partial K
at
x
, and is well defined and bijective. In general, for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, the map
N_{K}
is defined
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost everywhere on
\partial K
. In the points
x
where this is not single-valued, we consider
N_{K}(x)
as the unit normal cone of
K
at
x
, or generalized Gauss map. This is the set of all the unit vectors
\boldsymbol{\nu}
such that
K
has a tangent hyperplane at
x
with outer unit normal
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. Whenever
N_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
is well defined at
r_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\partial K
, we write
\alpha_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
for
N_{K}(r_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})
. By
[
28
, Lemma 2.2]
, if a sequence of sets
K_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
converges to
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
with respect to the Hausdorff metric, then
\alpha_{K_{m}}
converges to
\alpha_{K}
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with respect to the spherical Lebesgue measure.

The surface area measure of
K
is defined as

S_{K}(B)\coloneqq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(N_{K}^{-1}(B))

(10)

for every Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. Surface area measures are finite Borel measures on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and they are weakly continuous (see
[
51
, Section 4.2]
). A sequence
(\mu_{m})
of Borel measures on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
converges weakly to a Borel measure
\mu
if, for every
f\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f\,d\mu_{m}\to\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f\,d\mu.

2.2
Convex functions

For a function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
, its epigraph is the set

\mathrm{epi}(u)\coloneqq\{(x,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:u(x)\leq z\}.

We say that
u
is a convex function if
\mathrm{epi}(u)
is a convex set. We work in the space

\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneq\{u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}\colon u\text{ is convex, lower semi-continuous, }u\not\equiv+\infty\}.

The domain of a convex function is the set

\mathrm{dom}(u)\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:u(x)<+\infty\}.

On the space
\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we consider the topology induced by epi-convergence, characterized as follows: A sequence of functions
u_{j}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
epi-converges to
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following conditions are satisfied.

•

For every sequence of points
x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
converging to
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
u(x)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}u_{j}(x_{j})
.

•

There exists a sequence
(x_{j})
converging to
x
, such that
u(x)=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{j}(x_{j})
.

Consider the subfamily

\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneq\{u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\lim_{|x|\to\infty}u(x)=+\infty\}

of convex coercive functions. Here, convergence of level sets gives a more intuitive characterization of epi-convergence. For
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),t\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
consider the sublevel set

\{u\leq t\}\coloneq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:u(x)\leq t\}.

For a sequence of functions
u_{j}\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we use the convention
\{u_{j}\leq t\}\to\emptyset
if there exists
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\{u_{j}\leq t\}=\emptyset
for every
j\geq j_{0}
.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
12
, Lemma 5]
)
.

A sequence of functions
(u_{j})\subset\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
epi-converges to
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
\{u_{k}\leq t\}\to\{u\leq t\}
in the Hausdorff metric for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
t\neq\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)
.

For
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, its Fenchel-Legendre transform is the function

u^{*}(x)\coloneq\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{x\cdot y-u(y)\}.

Note that since it is the supremum of affine (and thus convex) functions,
u^{*}
is again a convex function. Moreover, for a non-convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, it still makes sense to define
f^{*}
as above if we admit the trivial case
f^{*}\equiv+\infty
. In particular,
f^{*}
is convex. Moreover, when
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has the important fact

(u^{*})^{*}=u.

In
\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we consider the operations of pointwise addition and infimal convolution. For
u,v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the latter is defined as

u\square v(x)\coloneq\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{u(y)+v(x-y)\},

and
u\square v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if at least one of the two functions is coercive (which will always be our case). For
u\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),t\geq 0
, instead of the dilation, we have the two following notions of multiplication: The classical scalar multiplication and the
epi-multiplication

(t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}u)(x)\coloneq tu\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)

for
t>0
,
0\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}u=I_{\{0\}}
. Moreover, one has the fundamental relation

((t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}u)\square(s\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}v))^{*}=tu^{*}+sv^{*}

(11)

for
u,v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
s,t\geq 0
.
Notice that for
u,v\in\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
t>0
,
\mathrm{epi}(u\square v)
is the Minkowski sum
\mathrm{epi}(u)+\mathrm{epi}(v)
, while
\mathrm{epi}(t\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\rule{1.29167pt}{1.29167pt}}}}v)=t(\mathrm{epi}(v))
.

A further meaningful correspondence is the one between support functions and indicator functions of compact convex sets.
Indeed, if
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and
K
contains the origin, a quick calculation shows that
(I_{K})^{*}=h_{K}
.

2.3
The space
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

In our investigation, a fundamental role is played by the space

\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\{u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\mathrm{dom}(u)\text{ is compact}\},

which is a subset of
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Both these spaces are endowed with the topology induced by epi-convergence introduced earlier. The results and notions exposed in the remainder of this section are from the author and Knoerr
[
37
]
, where the family
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
was introduced as a tool to infer geometric properties of convex functions through local properties of compact convex sets.

These functions can be obtained from compact convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
using the following construction where we use the identification
H\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}=e^{\perp}_{n+1}
: To every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
we associate the function
\lfloor K\rfloor:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
defined by

\lfloor K\rfloor(x)\coloneqq\inf\{t\in\mathbb{R}:(x,t)\in K\}.

(12)

In addition,
\lfloor K\rfloor(x)=+\infty
if and only if
(x,t)\notin K
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Analogously, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
we can define the concave function

\lceil K\rceil(x)\coloneqq\sup\{t\in\mathbb{R}:(x,t)\in K\}.

In this case
\lceil K\rceil(x)=-\infty
if
(x,t)\notin K
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}

3
First variations for measures of epigraphs

Consider on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
a measure
\mu
such that

d\mu(x,z)=d\eta(x)d\omega(z),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\equiv H,z\in\mathbb{R}\equiv H^{\perp},

where
\eta
and

are positive Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}
respectively.
The space of interest is always some subset of the family of functions
\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and we want to evaluate the measure of the epigraph of
u
through
\mu
, that is (using the abuse of notation
\mu(u)\coloneqq\mu(\mathrm{epi}(u))
)

\mu(u)=\int_{\mathrm{epi}(u)}\,d\mu(z,x)=\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\int_{u(x)}^{+\infty}\,d\omega(z)d\eta(x).

Ignoring, for now, the various summability assumptions, if we define
\Phi(t)=\omega([t,+\infty))
we can write

\mu(u)=\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\Phi(u(x))\,d\eta(x).

We focus on the case where there exist
\phi\in C(\mathbb{R})
and
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
d\omega(z)=\phi(z)dz
and
d\eta(x)=\psi(x)dx
. We now introduce a suitable deformation process for functions in
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which we build upon the classical notion of Wulff shape.

3.1
Wulff shapes of convex functions

The concept of Wulff shape, introduced more than a century ago by Wulff
[
54
]
, is nowadays a well-established scientific tool, especially in the study of the shapes of crystals. Significant developments have been obtained throughout the 20th century from the mathematical perspective. See, for example, the work of Fonseca
[
20
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}
. Its Wulff shape is the set

[f]\coloneq\bigcap_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}H^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(f(\boldsymbol{\nu})),

where
H^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(t)\coloneq\{x\in\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\colon\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot x\leq t\}
is the negative closed half-space with outer normal
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and distance
t
from the origin. Equivalently,
[f]
is the unique maximal (with respect to inclusion) convex set satisfying the condition

h_{[f]}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\leq f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\text{ for every }\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

(19)

Remark 3.2
.

Notice that if
f>c>0
,
[f]
is clearly non-empty. In general, if
\ell_{y}(x)\coloneq y\cdot x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f-\ell_{y}>c>0
, then
[f]
is non-empty and
y
is in the interior of
[f]
. In particular, if
[f]
is non-empty,

[f+\ell_{y}]=[f]+y,

(20)

for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(notice that
\ell_{y}=h_{\{y\}}
). Indeed, by (
19
), for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

h_{[f]+y}=h_{[f]}+\ell_{y}\leq f+\ell_{y}.

If, by contradiction,
h_{[f]+y}
was not maximal for
f+\ell_{y}
, neither would be
h_{[f]}
for
f
, which would contradict (
19
), proving (
20
).

Recall that we consider the admissible family of perturbations

C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\coloneqq\{\zeta\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\rho_{\zeta}\text{ is continuous and }|\rho_{\zeta}-\zeta|\text{ is bounded}\},

where

\rho_{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\coloneqq\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\zeta(t\boldsymbol{\nu})}{t},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

As for support functions, we use
\rho_{\zeta}
to denote both a function on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and its
1
-homogeneous extension given by
\rho_{\zeta}(x)=|x|\rho_{\zeta}(x/|x|),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We now show how, for
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the perturbation

u_{t}\coloneq(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}

can be represented, for
t>0
sufficiently small, by means of a Wulff shape.

For
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we consider the function
\bar{\zeta}
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}
obtained as

\bar{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\coloneq\frac{\zeta\left(g(\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)}{\sqrt{1+\left|g(\boldsymbol{\nu})\right|^{2}}}.

(21)

Here
g:\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the gnomonic projection

\boldsymbol{\nu}\mapsto\frac{\boldsymbol{\nu}-(e_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu})e_{n+1}}{e_{n+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}}.

(22)

We will make use of the extension of
\bar{\zeta}
on the whole
\mathbb{S}^{n}
obtained by reflection on
H
, that is, if
\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n+1})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}
,
\bar{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\bar{\zeta}((\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n},-\nu_{n+1}))
. When
\nu_{n+1}=0
,
\bar{\zeta}
is extended by continuity. This extension exists and is finite since
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that with the identification
\mathbb{S}^{n}\cap H\equiv\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
we have that
\bar{\zeta}
restricted to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is equal to
\rho_{\zeta}
. To make the notation lighter, we refer to
\bar{\zeta}
both for the transform (
21
) and the extension.

We will use this construction to work with the Wulff shape (in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
) obtained as

[h_{K^{u}}+t\overline{\zeta}]

and its connection with
(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}
, which we investigate in this subsection. First, we show that the condition
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is sufficient to guarantee that
(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In fact, we prove more.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for every
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
for
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,

\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})=[h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta}].

Proof.

Since
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that
m\coloneqq\min_{x\mathrm{dom}(u)}u(x)
and
M\coloneqq\max_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(u)}u(x)
exist and are finite. Therefore,

I_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+m\leq u\leq I_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+M,

which, by the properties of the Fenchel-Legendre transform, is equivalent to

h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}-M\leq u^{*}\leq h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}-m.

(23)

Choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
u_{t}
is proper for every
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
. Then

h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}-M+t(\rho_{\zeta}-C)\leq u^{*}+t\zeta\leq h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}-m+t(\rho_{\zeta}+C).

By the properties of Wulff shapes (see, for example,
[
20
, Proposition 1.2]
)

(h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta})^{*}=I_{[h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta}]}.

Therefore, we infer

I_{[h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta}]}+m-tC\leq u_{t}\leq I_{[h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta}]}+M+tC,

concluding the proof.
∎

The following lemma will be crucial in the proof of our main result.

Lemma 3.4
.

In the hypotheses of Lemma
3.3
there exists
T>0
such that

[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T+\tau}}+t\overline{\zeta}]=[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T}}+t\overline{\zeta}]+\ell_{\tau}

for every
\tau>0
.

Proof.

First,notice that for every
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathrm{epi}(v^{*})=\bigcap_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}}H^{-}_{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\frac{v(g(\boldsymbol{\nu}))}{\sqrt{1+|g(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{2}}}\right).

Then, for
T\geq 0

[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T}}+t\overline{\zeta}]=\mathrm{epi}(u_{t})\cap\mathrm{epi}(-u_{t}+c+T)\cap[h_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}+t\rho_{\zeta}]\times\mathbb{R}

for a suitable constant

.

By Lemma
3.3

admits a global finite maximum on its domain (since the latter is compact). In particular, choosing

large enough (independently on

by compactness)

, and

[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T}}+t\overline{\zeta}]=\mathrm{epi}((u^{*}+t\zeta))\cap\mathrm{epi}(-u_{t}+c+T),

since they share the same domain. Noticing that
\ell_{T+\tau}=\ell_{T}+\ell_{\tau}
for
\tau>0
, standard computations (see, for example,
[
51

∎

Remark 3.9

3.2
Weighted Brunn-Minkowski theory

Formula (
9
) is an application of our results, which are built on a wider framework. Indeed, (
1
) can be interpreted as the measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of the epigraph of
u
, where
d\mu(x,z)=e^{-z}dxdz,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},z\in\mathbb{R}
. In recent years has drawn much interest the so-called weighted Brunn-Minkowski theory, where one considers instead of
\mathrm{Vol}
a generic measure
\mu
with continuous density
\Psi
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. See, for example, Livshyts
[
41
]
, Alonso-Gutierrez, Hernández Cifre, Roysdon, Yepes Nicolàs, and Zvavitch
[
2
]
, Kryvonos and Langharst
[
38
]
, and Fradelizi, Langharst, Madiman and Zvavitch
[
21
]
. We note that the mentioned papers concern compact convex sets, while recently Schneider
[
52
]
explored these ideas in the context of pseudocones, which, in our language, are epigraphs of functions in a suitable subfamily of
\mathrm{Conv}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We consider the following generalized notion of surface area measure in (
10
), that is, the weighted surface area measure, which is defined as

S_{\mu,K}(B)=\int_{N_{K}^{-1}(B)}\Psi(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(X)

for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
and Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{S}^{n}
. A core ingredient of our approach is Lemma 2.7 from
[
38
]
, which generalizes Aleksandrov’s variational lemma
[
51
, Lemma 7.5.3]
.

Lemma 3.10

(Kryvonos and Langharst)
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with continuous density
\Psi
with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Then for
f\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n})
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
, we have

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu([h_{K}+tf])\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\partial K}f(N_{K}(X))\Psi(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(X).

(25)

Remark 3.11
.

Lemma
3.10
was originally formulated with the additional assumption
o\in\mathrm{int}K
, which was unnecessary since the boundary structure of a compact convex set is invariant under translations. Thus, both the set and the measure can be suitably translated so that (
25
) still holds. For completeness, and since it will be a useful remark later, we provide a proof of this fact. Suppose that the origin is not contained in
K
. For every point
Y
in the interior of
K
,
h_{K}-\ell_{Y}>0
, where
\ell_{Y}(X)=Y\cdot X,X\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and
K-Y
has the origin in its interior. Then, by (
20
), we can consider the Wulff shape
[h_{K}-\ell_{Y}+tf]
to infer by Lemma
3.10

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu([h_{K}-\ell_{Y}+tf])\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\partial(K-Y)}f(N_{K-Y}(X))\Psi(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(X).

Consider
\tilde{\Psi}(\cdot)=\Psi(\cdot+Y)
and the measure
\tilde{\mu}
which has
\tilde{\Psi}
as density. By Lemma
3.10
, the translation invariance of the Hausdorff measure, and since
N_{K-Y}(X)=N_{K}(X+Y)
,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu([h_{K}+tf])\right|_{t=0}=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\mu}([h_{K}-\ell_{Y}+tf])\right|_{t=0^{+}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial(K-Y)}f(N_{K-Y}(X))\tilde{\Psi}(X)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(X)=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}f(N_{K}(Z))\tilde{\Psi}(Z-Y)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}f(N_{K}(Z))\Psi(Z)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}(Z),

proving that in Lemma
3.10
, we do not need the origin to belong to the interior of
K
.

We now show that the weighted volume is continuous for compact convex sets with non-empty interior, and the weighted surface area measure is weakly continuous. The latter fact follows implicitly, for continuous densities, from the stability result by Livshyts
[
41
, Proposition A.3]
. For our applications, we include the possibility of working with singular densities. Our ambient space is still
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the formalism introduced before, where we write
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as
X=(x,z),x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\equiv H,z\in\mathbb{R}\equiv\mathrm{span}\{e_{n+1}\}
.

Lemma 3.12
.

For
q>0
and
\Psi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
non-negative, consider the measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with density
d\mu(X)=\Psi(X)|x|^{q-n}dxdz
, and a sequence
(K_{m})\subset\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
converging to
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
in the Hausdorff metric. In case

, suppose moreover that

, where
\mathrm{pr}_{H}
is the orthogonal projection on
H\equiv\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

•

The volume is continuous (this conclusion is trivial for
q\geq n
), that is,

\mu(K_{m})\to\mu(K),

•

The sequence of measures
S_{\mu,K_{m}}
converges weakly to
S_{\mu,K}
.

Proof.

First, notice that without loss of generality, we can suppose
o\in\left(\mathrm{int}K\cap\bigcap_{m\geq M}\mathrm{int}K_{m}\right)
for
M
sufficiently large. Indeed, if
q\geq n
, we can follow Remark
3.11

we just need a vertical translation, which will affect only
\Psi

Under this assumption, there exists
\delta>1
such that

\frac{1}{\delta}B^{n}\subset K_{m}\subset\delta B^{n}

for every
m
sufficiently large. This chain of inclusions is equivalent to

\frac{1}{\delta}<r_{K_{m}}<\delta

(26)

for

in the Hausdorff metric the sequence of radial functions

converges uniformly to

. Moreover, for
f\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n})
we have that
\alpha_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\to\alpha_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
for almost every
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
with respect to the spherical Lebesgue measure.

By direct computations using polar coordinates,

\displaystyle\mu(K_{m})=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\int_{0}^{r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\psi(t\boldsymbol{\nu})|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(t\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}t^{n}\,dtd\boldsymbol{\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}\left(\int_{0}^{r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\psi(t\boldsymbol{\nu})t^{q}\,dt\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}.

The radial functions
r_{K_{m}}
are continuous and converge uniformly to
r_{K}
. Therefore

\int_{0}^{r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\psi(t\boldsymbol{\nu})t^{q}\,dt\to\int_{0}^{r_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\psi(t\boldsymbol{\nu})t^{q}\,dt

uniformly, and the continuity of
\mu
is proved once we show that

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}\,d\boldsymbol{\nu}<+\infty.

(27)

Consider indeed the representation

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}\,d\boldsymbol{\nu}=2\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}}|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}\,d\boldsymbol{\nu}.

By (
15
)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}_{-}}|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}\,d\boldsymbol{\nu}=\int_{B^{n}}\frac{|x|^{q-n+1}}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{t^{q}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}\,dtd\boldsymbol{\nu},

which is finite by direct computations. Thus, we have (
27
).

For the second statement, we adapt the argument in
[
29
, Theorem 3.4]
. By the definition of weighted surface area measure and
[
28
, Lemma 2.9]
, we infer

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})=\int_{\partial K_{m}}f(N_{K}(X))\Psi(X)|x|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}((x,z))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\frac{f(\alpha_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}))}{\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\alpha_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\Psi(r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}r_{K_{m}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,d\boldsymbol{\nu}.

By (
26
) and since
f
and
\Psi
are continuous, there exists
C>0
such that

\frac{f(\alpha_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}))}{\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\alpha_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\Psi(r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(r_{K_{m}}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}r_{K_{m}}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\leq\delta^{n+1+(q-n)\mathrm{sign}(q-n)}C|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}

for every
m
and for almost every
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, where
\mathrm{sign}(\tau)=\tau/|\tau|
if
0\neq\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathrm{sign}(0)=0
. Notice that the expression on the left converges almost everywhere to

\frac{f(\alpha_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu}))}{\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\alpha_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}\Psi(r_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|\mathrm{pr}_{H}(r_{K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\boldsymbol{\nu})|^{q-n}r_{K}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\nu}).

By (
27
) and a direct application of the dominated convergence theorem, the proof is concluded.
∎

Notice that the estimates provided in the proof imply that for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
such that
o\in\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\mathrm{int}K)
the values
\mu(K)
and
S_{\mu,K}(\mathbb{S}^{n})
are finite.

We now apply Lemma
3.12
to show that Lemma
3.10
holds for the measures we are considering. For the convenience of the reader, we recall the following classical result (see, for example,
[
50
, Theorem 7.17]
).

Lemma 3.13
.

Suppose
f_{m}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R},m\in\mathbb{N}
is a sequence of functions, differentiable on
[a,b]
and such that
f_{m}(x_{0})
converges for some
x_{0}\in[a,b]
. If the derivatives
f^{\prime}_{m}
converge uniformly on
[a,b]
, then
f_{m}
converges uniformly on
[a,b]
, to a function
f
, and

f^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{m\to\infty}f^{\prime}_{m}(x)

for every
x\in[a,b]
.

We treat only the case
0<q<n
, since for
q\geq n
the following is a direct consequence of Lemma
3.10
.

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with density
d\mu(X)=\Psi(X)|x|^{q-n}dxdz
,
0<q<n
. Then for
f\in C(\mathbb{S}^{n})
and
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
such that
o\in\mathrm{pr}_{H}(\mathrm{int}K)
, we have

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu([h_{K}+tf])\right|_{t=0}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K}(\boldsymbol{\nu}).

Proof.

Again, without loss of generality, we can suppose that
o\in\mathrm{int}K
. Consider the sequence of measures
\mu_{m}
defined by
d\mu_{m}(x,z)=\Psi(X)(|x|\wedge m)^{q-n}
, where
a\wedge b\coloneqq\min\{a,b\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, for every
m
, the density of
\mu_{m}(x,z)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure is a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Moreover, since
o\in\mathrm{int}K
, by Theorem
3.7
there exist
\varepsilon>0
such that
o\in\mathrm{int}[F_{t}K]
for every
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
, where
F_{t}K=[h_{K}+tf]
. In particular,
\mu_{m}(K_{t})
is continuous in
[0,\varepsilon]
for every
m
.

By Theorem
3.8
, for every
t_{0}\in[0,\varepsilon)
and
t>0
sufficiently small

\frac{\mu_{m}(F_{t_{0}+t}K)-\mu_{m}(F_{t}K)}{t}=\frac{\mu_{m}(F_{t}F_{t_{0}}K)-\mu_{m}(F_{t_{0}}K)}{t}.

Therefore, Lemma
3.10
shows that
\mu_{m}(F_{t}K)
has continuous right derivative on
[0,\varepsilon]
. Since
\mu_{m}(K_{t})
is continuous as well, it must be differentiable in
[0,\varepsilon]
for every
m
.

Consider the decomposition
f=f^{+}-f^{-}
, where
f^{+}=(f\vee 0)
and
f^{-}=-(f\wedge 0)
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f^{+}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu_{m},K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\to\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f^{+}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})

increasingly and pointwise in
t\in[0,\varepsilon]
. The same holds if we replace
f^{+}
with
f^{-}
.
By Lemma
3.12
the two limits are continuous and bounded, and therefore

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu_{m},K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\to\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K}(\boldsymbol{\nu})

uniformly in
t
(see, for example,
[
50
, Theorem 7.13]
).

The proof is concluded since we satisfy all the hypotheses of Lemma
3.13
and thus

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mu_{m}([h_{K}+tf])-\mu_{m}(K)}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{\mu_{m}([h_{K}+tf])-\mu_{m}(K)}{t}.

∎

3.3
Proof of the variational formula

We are finally ready to prove our core result. It reads as follows.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(u))\neq\emptyset
, and
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Consider, moreover, a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
d\mu(z,x)=\phi(z)\psi(x)dz\,dx
with non-negative functions
\phi\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{1}([a,+\infty))
and
\psi\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\phi(z)\to 0
as
z\to+\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\mu(u_{t})\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))\phi(u(x))\psi(x)\,dx+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}

\displaystyle\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))\Phi(u(y))\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y),

exists and is finite, where
\Phi(t)=\int_{t}^{+\infty}\phi(z)dz
.

Notice that, under these assumptions, the measure of the epigraph of
u
is finite for every
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remark 3.16
.

The assumption
d\mu(z,x)=\phi(z)\psi(x)dzdx
is a simplification we adopt to lighten the notation. The interested reader can easily repeat the calculations in the hypotheses
d\mu(z,x)=\Psi(z,x)dzdx
as in Remark
3.11
, adapting them to the proof of Theorem
3.15
.

As anticipated, our strategy is to work between convex sets and convex functions. To perform this passage formally, we introduce the family

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}\coloneq

\displaystyle\{K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:K\text{ is convex, closed, with nonempty interior, and }\text{pr}_{H}(K)\in\mathcal{K}^{n}_{n}\}.

Notice that this family includes precisely
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
and the epigraphs of the functions in
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
n
-dimensional domain, where
\mathbb{R}^{n}\equiv H=e_{n+1}^{\perp}
. Let
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}
and let
\mu
be a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The
\mu
-symmetric-difference between
K
and
L
is

d_{\mu}(K,L)\coloneq\mu(K\Delta L)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}\chi_{K\Delta L}(x)\,d\mu(x),

where for a measurable set
A
we denote by
\chi_{A}
is characteristic function. In particular, if we use the Gaussian measure
\gamma_{n+1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, defined by its density

d\gamma_{n+1}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}^{n+1}}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\,dx,

then
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}
defines a metric on
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}
. This follows from the convexity of the involved sets and the sets being of dimension
n+1
. Notice that
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}(K,L)\in[0,1]
for every
K,L\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}
. This kind of metric was studied in a wider generality by Li and Mussnig
[
40
]
. Here we provide a proof that
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}
induces an appropriate metric on
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}
, that is, we can approximate functions in
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by convex bodies in
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
.

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
\gamma_{n+1}
be the Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The function
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}:\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}\times\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{+}\to[0,1]
is a distance. Moreover, its restrictions to
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
and the family of epigraphs of functions in
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
induce the topology of the Hausdorff metric and epi-convergence, respectively.

Proof.

The equivalence of epi-convergence and convergence with respect to
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}
can be proved analogously to Theorem 1.2 in
[
40
]
.

To prove that
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}
induces the Hausdorff metric on
\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
, we use the first part of the proof as follows: by Lemma
2.1
, the Hausdorff convergence of a sequence
\mathcal{K}_{m}\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
to some
\mathcal{K}\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}_{n+1}
is equivalent to the epi-convergence of
I_{K_{m}}
to
I_{K}
as functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. However, as we just proved, this is equivalent to the convergence of
\mathrm{epi}(I_{K_{m}})
to
\mathrm{epi}(I_{K})
with respect to
d_{\gamma_{n+2}}
. Direct calculations show that

d_{\gamma_{n+2}}(\mathrm{epi}(I_{K_{m}}),\mathrm{epi}(I_{K}))=Cd_{\gamma_{n+1}}(K_{m},K)

for some absolute constant
C>0
, concluding the proof.
∎

With these instruments at hand, we can start the proof of Theorem
3.15
.

Proof of Theorem
3.15
.

Let us first sketch the outline of the proof. We start by appropriately re-writing the variational formula (
25
) with
K=K^{v}+\ell_{T},T>0
for some
v\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\Psi(x,z)=\phi(z)\psi(x)
, and
f=\bar{\zeta}
as defined in (
21
). The choice of

is given by Lemma
3.4
. For
\tilde{v}=\lceil K^{v}+\ell_{T}\rceil=\lceil K^{v}\rceil+T
, direct computations using (
15
) show

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mu([h_{K^{v}}+t\bar{\zeta}])-\mu(K^{v})}{t}=\int_{\partial(K^{v}+\ell_{T})}\bar{\zeta}(N_{K^{v}}((x,z)))\phi(z)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}((x,z))\\
&=\int_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}\zeta(\nabla v(x))\phi(v(x))\psi(x)\,dx\\
&+\int_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}\zeta(\nabla v(x))\phi(\tilde{v}(x))\psi(x)\,dx\\
&+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(v)}\bar{\zeta}((N_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(x),0))\left(\int_{v(x)}^{\tilde{v}(x)}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).\end{split}

(28)

In particular, notice that
\bar{\zeta}
restricted to the equator is by definition
\rho_{\zeta}
. We have used the following fact: Since
v
and
\tilde{v}
are one a suitable reflection of the other,
\nabla v(x)=-\nabla\tilde{v}(x)
for every

where the gradient exists. For

, the normal to the epigraph expressed through the gradient is

, while for

we have
(-\nabla\tilde{v},1)/\sqrt{1+|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{2}}
. Moreover, by construction
\bar{\zeta}\left(N_{K^{v}}(x,z)\right)=\bar{\zeta}\left(R_{H}(N_{K^{v}}(x,z))\right)
. Since

\bar{\zeta}\left(R_{H}(N_{K^{v}}(x,z))\right)=\bar{\zeta}\left(\frac{-\nabla\tilde{v}(x),1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla\tilde{v}(x)|^{2}}}\right)=\bar{\zeta}\left(\frac{\nabla v(x),1}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla v(x)|^{2}}}\right)=\zeta(\nabla v(x)),

we have the integral on the third line of (

Recall that for
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

sufficiently small we write
u_{t}\coloneqq(u^{*}+t\zeta)^{*}
. The idea is to approximate
\mathrm{epi}(u_{t})
with a suitable sequence of convex bodies in order to obtain our claim as a limit of the integrals of the form (
28
). Then, we conclude by checking the hypotheses required to apply Lemma
3.13
. The proof is structured in three steps after introducing the sequence approximating
\mathrm{epi}(u_{t})
.

Clearly, dim
(\mathrm{epi}(u))=n+1
. Consider now the segment
\ell_{m}=\{se_{n+1}\colon s\in[0,m]\}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For
T
chosen as in Lemma
3.4
for
u
and
\zeta
,
K^{u}+\ell_{T}+\ell_{m}\to\mathrm{epi}(u)
in the symmetric-difference distance
d_{\gamma_{n+1}}
as
m\to\infty
. Consider the sequence of Wulff shapes
K_{m,t}\coloneq F_{t}(K^{u}+\ell_{T}+\ell_{m})=[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T}+\ell_{m}}+t\bar{\zeta}]
. Notice that
K_{m,0}
is monotonic with respect to inclusion, and thus the sequence
\mu(K_{m,0})
is increasing. By the properties of the sequence
K_{m,0}
and the measure
\mu
it follows from the monotone convergence theorem that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mu(K_{m,0})=\mu(\mathrm{epi}(u_{0}))=\mu(\mathrm{epi}(u)).

Moreover, notice that the sequence of sets
K_{m,t}
converges to
\mathrm{epi}(u_{t})
in the symmetric-difference distance topology monotonically. In particular, Lemma
3.4
implies that
K_{m,t}=K^{u,t}+\ell_{m}
, where
K^{u,t}=F_{t}(K^{u}+\ell_{T})=[h_{K^{u}+\ell_{T}}+t\overline{\zeta}]
.

Step 1
(Limit as
t\to 0
). Define now
u_{m}\coloneq\lfloor K^{u}+\ell_{m}\rfloor
and
\tilde{u}_{m}\coloneq\lceil K^{u}+\ell_{m}\rceil+T
. Notice that
u_{m}=u
and
\tilde{u}_{m}=\tilde{u}+m
, where
\tilde{u}=\lceil K^{u}\rceil+T
. Then, replacing
K^{v}
with
K^{u}+\ell_{m}
in (
28
), we infer

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mu(K_{m,t})-\mu(K^{u}+\ell_{T}+\ell_{m})}{t}=\int_{\partial(K^{u}+\ell_{T+m})}f(N_{K^{u}+\ell_{T+m}}((x,z)))\phi(z)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}((x,z))\\
&=\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))\phi(u(x))\psi(x)\,dx\\
&+\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))\phi(\tilde{u}(x)+m)\psi(x)\,dx\\
&+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\rho_{\zeta}((N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(x))\left(\int_{u(x)}^{\tilde{u}(x)+m}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).\end{split}

(29)

Notice that for
m
fixed, the integrals in the last three lines of (
29
) are all finite since they are just a suitable decomposition of the right-hand side of the first line. We claim that the right-hand side of (
29
) converges to

\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))\phi(u(x))\psi(x)\,dx+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))\Phi(u(y))\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

as

. The first integral is determined by the graph of

and thus is fixed by the sequence we are considering. Concerning the second one, since
\tilde{u}_{m}=\tilde{u}+m
is the parametrization of the upper part of
\partial(K^{u}+\ell_{m})
, we infer
\lim_{m\to\infty}\tilde{u}_{m}(x)=+\infty
for every

. Therefore
\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi(\tilde{u}_{m}(x))=0
for every
x\in\mathrm{dom}(u)
since
\phi
converges to
0
by hypothesis. Then,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\zeta(\nabla u(x))\phi(\tilde{u}(x)+m)\psi(x)\,dx\right|\leq

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathrm{dom}(u)}\phi(\tilde{u}(x)+m)\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}\left|\zeta(\nabla u(x))\psi(x)\right|\,dx.

Since the integral on the right-hand side of the inequality is finite (it is part of the weighted surface area measure of

when in Lemma

, the integral on the third line of (

.

We conclude this step observing that

\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(x))\left(\int_{u(x)}^{\tilde{u}_{m}(x)}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

. Indeed

increasingly, and the limit is finite by hypothesis. Then since

\displaystyle\left|\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(x))\left(\int_{u(x)}^{\tilde{u}_{m}(x)}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\right|\leq\left|\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(x))\psi(x)\right|\Phi(u(x)),

the dominated convergence theorem grants the desired convergence.

Step 2
(The derivative exists). We now prove that
\mu(K_{m,t})
is differentiable with respect to

for each
t
in
[0,\varepsilon]
for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon
suitably small, and its derivative converges uniformly as

on

to

\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\zeta(\nabla u_{t}(x))\phi(u_{t}(x))\psi(x)\,dx+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(x))\Phi(u_{t}(x))\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

For

\frac{\mu(F_{t+t_{0}}(K^{u}+\ell_{T+m}))-\mu(F_{t_{0}}(K^{u}+\ell_{T+m}))}{t}=\frac{\mu(F_{t}F_{t_{0}}(K^{u}+\ell_{T+m}))-\mu(F_{t_{0}}(K^{u}+\ell_{T+m}))}{t},

and as long as

,

\frac{d\mu(K_{m,t})}{dt}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\bar{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\,dS_{\mu,K_{m,t_{0}}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}).

(30)

Notice that

. Thus, we can choose

. Since

is continuous in

and
\,dS_{\mu,K_{m,t}}

) , the right derivative of

is continuous in

. Since if the derivative of a continuous function is continuous, then the function itself is differentiable, the function

is differentiable in
(0,\varepsilon)
for every

, as desired.

Step 3
(Uniform convergence). To prove the uniform convergence of the derivatives (

) for

,
\varepsilon>0
as chosen in the previous step. Consider the decomposition in (

) applied to

for a general

. The first integral is independent of

. Indeed

for every

. Furthermore,
\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})=\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})
for every

. For the second integral, notice that since for

one has
\lceil K_{m,t}\rceil\eqcolon\tilde{u}_{m,t}\to\tilde{u}_{m}=\tilde{u}+m
, we can find a sequence of values
M_{m}\to+\infty
as

such that for every

we have
M_{m}\leq\min_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\tilde{u}_{m,t}
. Moreover, notice that for every

we have

since by Lemma
3.4

is the reflection of

with respect to

up to a constant. Then, for the second integral in (

), we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\zeta(\nabla u_{y}(x))\phi(\tilde{u}_{m,t}(x))\psi(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{t\in[M_{m},+\infty]}\phi(t)\right)\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}|\zeta(\nabla u_{t}(x))\psi(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{t\in[M_{m},+\infty]}\phi(t)\right)\int_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}|\zeta(\nabla u_{t}(x))\psi(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{t\in[M_{m},+\infty]}\phi(t)\right)\int_{{\partial K^{u,t}_{-}}}|\bar{\zeta}(g^{-1}(\nabla u_{t}(x)))|\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}((x,z))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sup_{t\in[M_{m},+\infty]}\phi(t)\right)\max_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}|\bar{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|\max_{t\in[0,\varepsilon]}\left(\int_{\partial K^{u,t}}\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n}((x,z))\right),

where

was defined in (

), and therefore the first line converges to

independently of

. In the last inequality we have used that

and

. Notice that the maximum in

is continuous in
t
. Finally, for the last integral, the convergence is granted again by the dominated convergence theorem and making use of the fact that

. Indeed, we get

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}(x))\Phi(u_{t}(x))\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)-\right.\\
&\left.\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}(x))\left(\int_{u_{t}(x)}^{\tilde{u}_{m,t}}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|\\
=&\left|\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}(x))\left(\int_{\tilde{u}_{m,t}(x)}^{+\infty}\phi(s)\,ds\right)\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|\\
\leq&\max_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}|\rho_{\zeta}(\boldsymbol{\nu})|\max_{t\in[0,\varepsilon]}\left|\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}\psi(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|\left(\int_{C+m/2}^{+\infty}\phi(s)\,ds\right),\end{split}

where

. Notice that the maximum on

is finite since the integral is continuous in

is the projection on

of

Conclusion
. We can now safely apply Lemma

, concluding the proof.

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}

The two integrals in (

Lemma 4.4
.

Consider
u\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{coe}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
o\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(u))
. If
\zeta\in C_{rec}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is Lipschitz, the integrals

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\zeta(\nabla u(x))\right|e^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\text{ and }

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\left|\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))\right|e^{-u(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

are finite.

Proof.

First notice that since
\zeta
is a Lipschitz function there exists
L\geq 0
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

|\zeta(x)|-|\zeta(0)|\leq|\zeta|-|\zeta(0)||\leq L|x|.

That is, there exists
a\geq 0
such that

|\zeta(x)|\leq L|x|+a

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\zeta(\nabla u(x))\right|e^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}L|\nabla u(x)|e^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}ae^{-u(x)}|x|^{q-n}\,dx.

The first integral on the right is finite thanks to
[
27
, Theorem 5.12]
, while the second is finite by
[
27
, Proposition 1.2]
since
u
is coercive.

Consider now the second integral in the statement. Since
u
is convex and coercive there exist
A,c>0
such that
e^{-u(x)}\leq Ae^{-c|x|}
(see
[
31
, Lemma 2.1]
). Moreover, notice that

e^{-c|x|}=c\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}\mathds{1}_{tB^{n}}(x)dt

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\mathds{1}_{tB^{n}}=e^{-I_{tB^{n}}}
is the characteristic function of the Euclidean unit ball. Then we infer

\begin{split}\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}e^{-u(y)}&|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\leq A\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}e^{-c|y|}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\\
=Ac&\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}\mathds{1}_{tB^{n}}(y)|y|^{q-n}\,dtd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\\
=Ac&\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)\cap tB^{n}}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)dt.\end{split}

(33)

If
0<q<n
we continue (
33
) by

\displaystyle\ldots\leq Ac\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-ct}\int_{\partial tB^{n}}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\,dt+\int_{1}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}\int_{\partial tB^{n}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=Ac\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-ct}t^{q-1}\,dt+\int_{1}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}t^{n-1}\,dt\right).

If, alternatively,
n\leq q
, analogously we obtain

\displaystyle\ldots\leq Ac\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-ct}\int_{\partial tB^{n}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\,dt+\int_{1}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}\int_{\partial tB^{n}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)|y|^{q-n}\,dt\right)

\displaystyle=Ac\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})\left(\int_{0}^{1}e^{-ct}t^{n-1}\,dt+\int_{1}^{+\infty}e^{-ct}t^{q-1}\,dt\right).

In any case, since the integrals in both the last lines are finite, all the preceding ones are. Observing that

\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}\left|\rho_{\zeta}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u)}(y))\right|e^{-u(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\leq L\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)}e^{-u(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y),

the proof is concluded.
∎

We now report two estimates from the proof of

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus mB^{n}}\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{n}}e^{-u(x)}\right||x|^{q-n}\,dx\leq\\

\displaystyle\left.\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}h_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}(y))e^{-u_{m,t}(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})\setminus\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}h_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}(y))e^{-u_{t}(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})\setminus\partial\mathrm{dom}(u_{t})}h_{\mathrm{dom}(v)}(N_{\mathrm{dom}(u_{m,t})}(y))e^{-u_{m,t}(y)}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

\displaystyle\leq 2LAc\int_{m}^{\infty}e^{-c\tau}\int_{\partial\mathrm{dom}(u)\cap\tau B^{n}}|y|^{q-n}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)d\tau.

If
0<q<n
we continue by

\ldots\leq 2LAc\int_{m}^{\infty}e^{-c\tau}\tau^{n-1}d\tau.

Otherwise, if
n\leq q
, we conclude by

\ldots\leq 2LAc\int_{m}^{\infty}e^{-c\tau}\tau^{q-1}d\tau.

In both cases, we have uniform convergence to
0
, concluding the proof.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We conclude this section noting that the same method in the proof of Theorem
1.3
can be specialized to the case
q\geq n
without requiring any assumption on the particular position of the epigraphs of
u
and
v
. In particular, it is possible to recover Theorem
1.1
when
v\in\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{cd}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
