Abstract
. We study the prescribed mean curvature equation for t t -graphs in a Riemannian Heisenberg group of arbitrary dimension. We characterize the existence of classical solutions in a bounded domain without imposing Dirichlet boundary data, and we provide conditions that guarantee uniqueness. Moreover, we extend previous results to solve the Dirichlet problem when the mean curvature is non-constant. Finally, by an approximation technique, we obtain solutions to the sub-Riemannian prescribed mean curvature equation.

1.
Introduction

The Plateau problem has been a fundamental issue in geometry since the pioneering works of Douglas (cf.
[
26
]
) and Radó (cf.
[
59
]
). The Euclidean prescribed mean curvature equation of the graph of a function
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
over a bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
H\in C(\Omega)
reads as

(PMC)

\divv\left(\frac{Du}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\right)=H,

where
D
stands for the Euclidean gradient. Notice that we are taking the mean curvature as the (not averaged) sum of the principal curvatures. When
H
is constant and
\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{2}
, Serrin (cf.
[
67
]
) characterized the existence of solutions to the Dirichlet problem for
any
boundary datum
\varphi\in C^{2}(\partial\Omega)
by the condition

(1.1)

|H|\leqslant H_{\partial\Omega}(z_{0})

for any
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, where
H_{\partial\Omega}
is the mean curvature of the boundary of
\Omega
. In the proof, Serrin obtained Schauder estimates for
C^{2}
solutions first by providing height estimates for
|u|
, and then, by means of a gradient maximum principle, showing that the maximum of the gradient is attained at the boundary of
\Omega
. In the final step, he estimated the gradient at the boundary exploiting the so-called
barriers
(cf.
[
37
]
), whose construction relies on (
1.1
).
When
H
is not constant, an approach based on the maximum principle typically fails. Therefore, in order to deal both with non-constant sources and to allow merely continuous boundary data, it is customary to rely on suitable interior and global gradient estimates.
Some references to these kind of estimates in the Euclidean space are the works of Korevaar and Simon (cf.
[
46
]
) and Wang (cf.
[
71
]
). Beyond the Euclidean framework, the Dirichlet problem for any sufficiently regular boundary datum and constant source
H
satisfying conditions analogous to (
1.1
) has been studied in warped products with a particular lower bound on the Ricci curvature (cf.
[
69
]
), in
3
-dimensional Heisenberg groups (cf.
[
3
]
) and in higher dimensional Heisenberg groups (cf.
[
38
]
). When
H
is not constant, the previous results were later extended in Riemannian manifolds with a Killing vector field and a lower bound on the Ricci curvature depending on
\Omega
(cf.
[
21
,
20
,
22
]
).
When instead (
1.1
) fails, meaning that

(1.2)

|H|>H_{\partial\Omega}(z_{0})

for some
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
,
we could lose control of the norm of the gradient of a solution near the boundary, and hence of the existence of solutions. More precisely, as shown in
[
37
]
, when (
1.2
) holds there always exists a boundary datum
\varphi
for which the Dirichlet problem has no solution.
Nevertheless, the validity of (
1.2
) does not preclude
a priori
the existence of a
suitable
boundary datum
\varphi
for which the Dirichlet problem is solvable. As an instance, taking as domain
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the ball of radius
1
centered at
0
, we can write the half sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
centered at
0
with radius
1
as a graph over
\Omega
. A simple computation reveals that it satisfies (
PMC
) with
H=\frac{n}{n-1}H_{\partial\Omega}
and with boundary datum
\varphi\equiv 0
. In particular, (
1.2
) is verified for any
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
In this regard, when
\Omega
has Lipschitz boundary, Giusti (cf.
[
40
]
) proved that the existence of solutions to (
PMC
) with a suitable boundary condition, not imposed
a priori
, is characterized by

(*)

\left|\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}H(x)\,dx\right|<P(\tilde{\Omega})

for any set
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
such that
\tilde{\Omega}\neq\emptyset
and
\tilde{\Omega}\neq\Omega
, where
P(\tilde{\Omega})
is the perimeter of
\tilde{\Omega}
. Moreover,
[
40
]
provides a characterization of those domains where (
PMC
) admits, up to vertical translations, a unique solution. Precisely, the previous statement is equivalent to each of the following conditions: there is no solution to (
PMC
) in any domain
\Omega\subsetneq\hat{\Omega}
; there is a solution on
\Omega
which is vertical at every point of
\partial\Omega
; (
*
‣
1
) holds and

(**)

\left|\int_{\Omega}H(x)\,dx\right|=P(\Omega).

In these cases,
\Omega
is called an
extremal domain
. Otherwise, i.e. when (
*
‣
1
) also holds for
\tilde{\Omega}=\Omega
, then
\Omega
is called a

. The proof of the existence of solutions under condition (
*
‣
1
) relies on previous results by Giaquinta (cf.
[
35
,
34
]
) and Miranda (cf.
[
51
]
). In the non-extremal case the proof consists in showing the existence of
BV
minimizers of the penalized functional

(1.3)

\mathcal{F}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}+\int_{\Omega}Hu\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}|u-\varphi|\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

for any
\varphi\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
, whose regularity is then gradually improved in several steps.
The more involved extremal case, i.e. when (
**
‣
1
) holds, follows by a compactness procedure. More precisely, in view of condition (
*
‣
1
), every domain
\tilde{\Omega}\subsetneq\Omega
is itself a non-extremal domain. Therefore, exploiting the existence result in the non-extremal case, together with a compactness argument based on a notion of

first introduced by Miranda (cf.
[
52
]
), existence in the extremal case follows. For similar results under weaker assumptions on the boundary of
\Omega
we refer the reader to
[
48
]
.

Although the Dirichlet problem, as previously discussed, has been widely studied beyond the Euclidean framework,
as far as the authors are aware no results in the spirit of
[
40
]
are available in the Riemannian setting. The aim of this work, consequently, is to lay the groundwork for the study of hypersurfaces with prescribed mean curvature outside the Euclidean setting and overcoming conditions inspired by (
1.1
).
As a relevant first instance, we choose as ambient manifold the
n
-th dimensional Riemannian Heisenberg group
. The Heisenberg group
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, for
n\geqslant 1
, is
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
endowed with
the non-Abelian group law

(x,y,t)\ast(x,y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{j}^{\prime}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}\right)\right),

where
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
x^{\prime}=(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{n}^{\prime})
,

and

(cf.
[
9
]
). The Lie algebra of
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is generated by the family of left-invariant vector fields

X_{i}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+y_{i}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad Y_{i}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-x_{i}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad T=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t},

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. A remarkable class of Riemannian structures can be defined on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
by choosing, for any
\varepsilon\neq 0
, the unique Riemannian metric
g_{\varepsilon}
which makes
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},\varepsilon T\}
an orthonormal frame. The importance of
({\mathbb{H}}^{n},g_{\varepsilon})
in the Riemannian framework is supported by several reasons. For instance, it appears in the classification of homogeneous
3
-spaces with isometry group of dimension
4
, usually denoted by
Nil_{3}(-1/\tau)
(cf.
[
1
]
). When

goes to

(\mathbb{H}^{n},g_{\varepsilon})
converges in Gromov-Hausdorff sense to the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group
({\mathbb{H}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the restriction of any of the metric
g_{\varepsilon}
to the
horizontal distribution

{\mathcal{H}}=\spann\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\}
. The sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is itself of fundamental importance in various settings, since it constitutes the prototypical model in the context of Carnot groups, sub-Riemannian manifolds (cf.
[
2
]
) and
CR manifolds
(cf.
[
14
]
). In the following, we deal with a relevant class of non-parametric hypersurfaces, namely that of
vertical or (
t
-) graphs
, i.e. Euclidean graphs over the horizontal hyperplane
\{t=0\}\equiv{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
. The equation of prescribed mean curvature of a
t
-graph over a bounded domain

for a given source

is formally given by

(
\varepsilon
-PMC)

\divv\left(\frac{Du+X}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+|Du+X|^{2}}}\right)=H,

where

(cf.
[

38
]
). Our main achievement, in the spirit of
[

, is an existence and regularity result for solutions to (

ε

-PMC

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
be a bounded domain with Lipschitz boundary, and let

for some

*
‣

which is a classical solution to (

with radius

is denoted by
B(p,r)
.

2.2.
The Heisenberg group
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

We follow the notation and background given in
[
62
]
. We define a non-Abelian group law
\cdot
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
by

p\cdot p^{\prime}=(x,y,t)\cdot(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{j}^{\prime}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}\right)\right),

where we denote points
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
by
p=(z,t)=(x,y,t)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},t)
.
The Lie group
({\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1},\cdot)
is referred to as the
n
-th Heisenberg group
and is denoted by
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. For any
p\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, the left-translation by
p
is the diffeomorphism
\ell_{p}(q)=p\cdot q
. A basis of left-invariant vector fields is given by

X_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}+y_{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad Y_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}-x_{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad T=\frac{\partial}{\partial t},

where
i=1\ldots,n
.
The horizontal distribution
\mathcal{H}
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is the smooth distribution generated by
X_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{n}
. A vector field
U
is said
horizontal
if
U(p)\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
for any
p\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Given a smooth function
f
, we define the
horizontal gradient
of
f
by

\nabla^{\mathbb{H}}f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}(f)X_{i}+Y_{i}(f)Y_{i}.

For any
\varepsilon\neq 0
, the matrix of change of basis from
X_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},\varepsilon T
to the canonical basis of
T{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
at
p=(x_{1}\ldots x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},t)
is given by

(2.1)

C_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\ldots&0&0&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&\ldots&0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&\ldots&0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
y_{1}&\ldots&y_{n}&-x_{1}&\ldots&-x_{n}&\varepsilon\\
\end{pmatrix}.

In the following, we consider for any
\varepsilon\neq 0
the left-invariant Riemannian metric
g_{\varepsilon}=\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{\varepsilon}
on
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
such that
\{X_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{n},\varepsilon T\}
is an orthonormal basis at every point. We shall drop the subindex in the metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
when considering horizontal vectors. We shall also use the compact notation
\{Z_{1},\ldots Z_{2n+1}\}
to denote the previous basis. Moreover, we denote by
\nabla^{\varepsilon}
its associated Levi-Civita connection. The norm of a vector field
U
with respect to
g_{\varepsilon}
will be denoted by
|U|_{\varepsilon}
.
By means of the
Koszul formula
(cf.
[
25
]
), the following relations hold.

\displaystyle\nabla_{X_{i}}^{\varepsilon}X_{j}=0,\qquad\qquad\ \,\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{Y_{i}}Y_{j}=0,\qquad\ \,\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{T}T=0,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{X_{i}}Y_{j}=-\delta_{i,j}T,\qquad\,\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{X_{i}}\varepsilon T=\frac{Y_{i}}{\varepsilon},\quad\ \ \nabla^{\varepsilon}_{Y_{i}}\varepsilon T=-\frac{X_{i}}{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{Y_{i}}X_{j}=\delta_{i,j}T,\qquad\ \ \,\,\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon T}X_{i}=\frac{Y_{i}}{\varepsilon},\ \ \quad\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{\varepsilon T}Y_{i}=-\frac{X_{i}}{\varepsilon}

for any
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n
, where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta.
Setting
J(U)=\nabla^{1}_{U}T
for any vector field
U
, we can easily deduce that
J(X_{i})=Y_{i}
,
J(Y_{i})=-X_{i}
and
J(T)=0
.
The Riemannian volume of a set
E
for
g_{\varepsilon}
is, up to a constant, the Lebesgue measure in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
.
Given a norm
|\cdot|
in
T{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, and
v\in T_{p}{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, we will drop the subindex
p
and write
|v|
.
We denote by
\ric
the quadratic form associated with the Ricci tensor induced by
g_{\varepsilon}
, that is

\begin{split}\ric(U)&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}g_{\varepsilon}\left(\nabla_{Z_{j}}\nabla_{U}U-\nabla_{U}\nabla_{Z_{j}}U+\nabla_{[U,Z_{j}]}U,Z_{j}\right)\end{split}

for any vector field
U
of class
C^{2}
.
As we will show in
Section

8
,

(2.3)

\ric(U)=-\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}g_{\varepsilon}\left(U,U\right)+(2n+2)\frac{u_{2n+1}^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}

for any vector field
U=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}u_{j}Z_{j}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{H}^{n};T\mathbb{H}^{n})
.

2.3.
Carnot-Carathéodory structure on
\mathbb{H}^{n}

If
\Gamma:[a,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}
is an absolutely continuous curve, we say that it is
horizontal
whenever
\dot{\Gamma}(t)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\Gamma(t)}

for almost every
t\in[a,b]
,
and we say that it is
sub-unit
whenever it is horizontal with
|\dot{\Gamma}(t)|=1
for almost every
t\in[a,b]
.
Moreover, we define

(2.4)

d^{\mathbb{H}}(p,p^{\prime}):=\inf\{T\,:\,\Gamma:[0,T]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}\text{ is sub-unit, $\Gamma(0)=p$ and $\Gamma(T)=p^{\prime}$}\}

which, by the Chow-Rashevskii theorem (cf.
[
19
]
), defines a distance on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, called
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
. The metric space
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d^{\mathbb{H}})
is then a prototype of
Carnot-Carathéodory space
(cf.
[
43
]
).

2.4.
Embedded hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

We consider oriented hypersurfaces of class
C^{1}
embedded in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, and we choose a unit normal to
S
. In case
S
is the boundary of a domain in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, we always choose the outer unit normal
\nu^{\varepsilon}
. The
characteristic set
of
S
, denoted by
S_{0}
, is the set of points
p\in S
where the tangent space
T_{p}S
coincides with the horizontal distribution
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
. The
horizontal unit normal

\nu^{\mathbb{H}}
is defined in
S\setminus S_{0}
by

\nu^{\mathbb{H}}=\frac{\nu^{\varepsilon}_{h}}{|\nu^{\varepsilon}_{h}|},

where we denote by
U_{h}
the orthogonal projection of a vector
U
onto
\mathcal{H}
.

2.5.
Perimeters in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

In this subsection,
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is a bounded open set and
\varepsilon\neq 0
. We denote the Euclidean norm, total variation, perimeter and divergence in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}
by
|\cdot|
,
Var
,
P
and
\divv_{eu}
respectively, and we denote the space of functions of bounded variation by
BV_{eu}
.
Given an open set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
and
f\in L^{1}(A)
, the
\varepsilon
-variation
of
f
in
A
is denoted by

Var_{\varepsilon}(f;A)=\sup\left\{\int_{A}f\divv_{\varepsilon}(U)\,dx:U\in C_{c}^{1}(A;T{\mathbb{H}}^{n}),\ |U|_{\varepsilon,\infty}\leqslant 1\right\},

where
C_{c}^{1}(A;T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
is the space of
C^{1}
compactly supported vector fields in
A
,
|U|_{\varepsilon,\infty}=\sup_{p\in A}|U(p)|_{\varepsilon}
and
\divv_{\varepsilon}
is the divergence associated to
g_{\varepsilon}
. The space of
L^{1}(A)
functions with bounded
\varepsilon
-variation is denoted by
BV_{\varepsilon}(A)
.
Given
E\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
a measurable set and
A\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
an open set, the
\varepsilon
-perimeter of
E
in
A
is given by

P_{\varepsilon}(E;A)=Var_{\varepsilon}(\chi_{E};A).

In case
A={\mathbb{H}}^{n}
we write
P_{\varepsilon}(E;{\mathbb{H}}^{n})=P_{\varepsilon}(E)
. Given a vector field
U\in C_{c}^{1}(A;T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
, it follows from (
2.1
) that

(2.5)

\divv_{eu}U=\divv_{\varepsilon}U,

so that from now on we shall simply write
\divv
.
There exist constants
C(\Omega,\varepsilon)>0
and
c=c(\Omega,\varepsilon)>0
such that

(2.6)

C|v|\leqslant|v|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant c|v|

for any
v\in T_{p}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}})
. Indeed, we assume for clarity that
n=1
and let
v=v_{1}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+v_{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}+v_{3}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
. Using (
2.1
) and recalling that
(a+b)^{2}\leqslant 2a^{2}+2b^{2}
for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we get

\begin{split}|v|_{\varepsilon}^{2}\leqslant v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}v_{3}^{2}+\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}}y^{2}v_{1}^{2}+\frac{4}{\varepsilon^{2}}x^{2}v_{2}^{2}\leqslant 4\left(1+\frac{1+\max_{z\in\bar{\Omega}}|z|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)|v|^{2}\color[rgb]{0,0,0},\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}|v|^{2}\leqslant v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+4x^{2}v_{2}^{2}+4y^{2}v_{1}^{2}+2(v_{3}-yv_{1}+xv_{2})^{2}\leqslant\max\left\{1+4\max_{z\in\overline{\Omega}}|z|^{2},2\varepsilon^{2}\right\}|v|_{\varepsilon}^{2}.\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\end{split}

Notice that, in (
2.6
),
c
can be chosen uniformly in
\varepsilon
for
|\varepsilon|
big enough, while
C
can be chosen uniformly in
\varepsilon
for
|\varepsilon|
small enough.
Given an open set
A\subseteq\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
and a vector field
U\in C_{c}^{1}(A;T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
with
|U|_{\varepsilon,\infty}\leqslant 1
, it follows from (
2.6
) that
|CU|\leqslant|U|_{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1

and

\int_{A}f\divv(U)dx=\frac{1}{C}\int_{A}f\divv(CU)dx\leqslant\frac{1}{C}Var(f,A)

for any
f\in L^{1}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}})
. Hence
Var_{\varepsilon}(f,A)\leqslant\frac{1}{C}Var(f,A)
. Similarly,
Var(f,A)\leqslant\frac{1}{c}Var_{\varepsilon}(f,A)
, so that

(2.7)

\frac{1}{c}Var(f,A)\leqslant Var_{\varepsilon}(f,A)\leqslant\frac{1}{C}Var(f,A).

Notice that (
2.7
) implies that
BV_{\varepsilon}(A)
and
BV_{eu}(A)
coincide for
A\subseteq\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}
, and shall be denoted by
BV(A)
. Moreover, the perimeters
P
and
P_{\varepsilon}
are absolutely continuous with respect to each other. Hence, the Euclidean reduced and essential boundaries of a Caccioppoli set coincide with the ones induced by
P_{\varepsilon}
. In the following, we will denote by
\partial^{*}E
the reduced boundary of
E
.
Given
E\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
measurable and
A\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
open, the
horizontal perimeter
of
E
in
A
is defined by

P_{\mathbb{H}}(E,A)=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\divv U\,dx,\,U\in C^{1}_{c}(A,\mathcal{H}),\,|U|_{1,\infty}\leqslant 1\right\},

where
C^{1}_{c}(A,\mathcal{H})
is the space of
C^{1}
compactly supported horizontal vector fields in
A
. We refer to
[
30
]
for the main properties of the horizontal perimeter.
We point out that both
P_{\varepsilon}
and
P_{\mathbb{H}}
behave like the Euclidean perimeter
P
for vertical sets. More precisely, given a Caccioppoli set
E\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and an open set
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
, arguing as in
[
13
, (3.2)]
and observing that the last component of the measure theoretic Euclidean unit normal to
E\times{\mathbb{R}}
is zero, then

(2.8)

P_{\varepsilon}(E\times{\mathbb{R}};A)=P_{\mathbb{H}}(E\times{\mathbb{R}};A)=P(E\times{\mathbb{R}},A).

By the above definitions, the following relations between horizontal and
\varepsilon
-perimeter hold.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be an open bounded set and
F\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be a Caccioppoli set. Then

P_{\mathbb{H}}(F,A)\leqslant P_{\varepsilon}(F,A)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}P_{\varepsilon}(F,A)=P_{\mathbb{H}}(F,A).

Moreover, if
(\varepsilon_{j})_{j}\subseteq(0,1)
satisfies
\varepsilon_{j}\searrow 0
as
j\to\infty
, and
E
and
(E_{j})_{j}
are measurable sets such that
\chi_{E_{j}}\to\chi_{E}
in
L^{1}_{loc}(A)
, then

(2.9)

P_{\mathbb{H}}(E;A)\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}P_{\varepsilon_{j}}(E_{j};A).

Moreover, the following compactness result holds.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
be an open and
(E_{k})_{k}
be a sequence of finite
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter sets in
A
. Assume that there exists
M>0
such that
\sup_{k}P_{\mathbb{H}}(E_{k},A)<M
.
Then there exists a finite
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter set
E
in
A
such that
\chi_{E_{k}}\to\chi_{E}
in
L^{1}_{loc}(A)
.

Proof.

Let
A^{\prime}\Subset A
open. Let
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}
be a covering of
A^{\prime}
of Carnot-Carathéodory balls, i.e. the metric balls with respect to the distance introduced in (
2.4
), such that

A^{\prime}\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{s}B_{j}\Subset A.

Notice that
\chi_{E_{k}}\in BV_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{j})
for any
j=1,\ldots,k
(cf.
[
29
]
for the definition of
BV_{\mathbb{H}}
). Let us consider first
B_{1}
. In view of
[
29
, Theorem 2.2.2]
, there exists
v_{k}\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})\cap BV_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1})
such that

\|v_{k}-\chi_{E_{k}}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}\leqslant\frac{1}{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|P_{\mathbb{H}}(E_{k},B_{1})-\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla^{\mathbb{H}}v_{k}|\,dx\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{k}

Therefore

\|v_{k}\|_{L^{1}(B_{1})}\leqslant|E_{k}\cap B_{1}|+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int_{B_{1}}|\nabla^{\mathbb{H}}v_{k}|\,dx\leqslant\sup_{k}P_{\mathbb{H}}(E_{k},A)+1,

so that
(v_{k})_{k}
is bounded in
BV_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1})
. Hence,
[
32
]
implies that there exists
v\in BV_{\mathbb{H}}(B_{1})
such that, up to a subsequence,
v_{k}\to v
in
L^{1}(B_{1})
. This fact trivially implies the existence of a set
E^{1}\subseteq B_{1}
such that, up to a subsequence,
\chi_{E_{k}}\to\chi_{E^{1}}
in
L^{1}(B_{1})
. The thesis then easily follows by a diagonal process and the lower semicontinuity of the
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter. ∎

2.6.
t
-graphs in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}

Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
and a measurable function
u:\Omega\longrightarrow[-\infty,+\infty]
, we write the subgraph of
u
as

(2.10)

E_{u}=\{(z,t)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}\,:\,t<u(z)\}.

A simple computation shows that, for
u\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega)
and
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
open, the perimeter of
E_{u}
in
\tilde{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}
can be computed as

(2.11)

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(u,\tilde{\Omega})=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+|Du+X|^{2}}\,dz,

where
X:{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is defined by
X(x,y)=(-y,x)
and
Du
is the gradient of
u
. The
L^{1}
-relaxation of
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
for
u\in BV(\Omega)
is

\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}(u,\tilde{\Omega})=\inf\left\{\liminf_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(u_{k},\tilde{\Omega}):\,(u_{k})_{k}\subseteq W^{1,1}(\Omega),\,u_{k}\to u\text{ in }L^{1}(\tilde{\Omega})\right\}.

We also define

S_{\varepsilon}(u,\tilde{\Omega})=\sup\left\{\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}(-u\divv\tilde{g}+\langle X,\tilde{g}\rangle+\varepsilon g_{2n+1})\,dz:\,g=(\tilde{g},g_{2n+1})\in C^{1}_{c}(\tilde{\Omega},{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1}),\,|g|\leqslant 1\right\}.

Lemma 2.3
.

Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
open and
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, then

P_{\varepsilon}(E_{u},\tilde{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}})=\overline{\mathcal{A}}_{\varepsilon}(u,\tilde{\Omega})=S_{\varepsilon}(u,\tilde{\Omega}).

Proof.

The proof follows exactly as the proof of
[
66
, Theorem 3.2]
.
∎

Recall that for any
u\in BV
, its distributional derivative
\tilde{D}u
can be decomposed as the sum of the two mutually singular measures
Du\mathcal{L}^{2n}+(Du)_{s}
, where
Du\in L^{1}

and
(Du)_{s}
is singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{2n}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Given a bounded open set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
\tilde{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega
open and
u\in BV(\Omega)
, it holds that

P_{\varepsilon}(E_{u},\tilde{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}})=(Du)_{s}(\tilde{\Omega})+\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+|Du+X|^{2}}\,dz.

Proof.

Let us define
L:C^{1}_{c}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

L(g)=\int_{A}(-u\divv\bar{g}+\langle X,\bar{g}\rangle+\varepsilon g_{2n+1})\,dz,

where
g=(\bar{g},g_{2n+1})
.
L
is clearly linear. Moreover, since by
Lemma

2.3

S_{\varepsilon}(u)<+\infty,
then
L
extends to a linear bounded functional on
C^{0}_{c}(A,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1})
. Therefore, by Riesz Theorem (cf. e.g.
[
5
, Theorem 1.54]
) there exists a unique
(2n+1)
-valued finite Radon measure
\mu
such that

L(g)=\int_{A}g\cdot d\mu\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{\varepsilon}(u,A)=|\mu|(A)

for any
g\in C^{1}_{c}(A,{\mathbb{R}}^{2n+1})
. By the uniqueness of such a measure it is easy to see that
\mu=(Du+Xd\mathcal{L}^{2n},\varepsilon d\mathcal{L}^{2n})
, and so

S_{\varepsilon}(u,A)=|(Du+Xd\mathcal{L}^{2n},\varepsilon d\mathcal{L}^{2n})|(A).

A trivial computation, together with
Lemma

2.3
, concludes the proof.
∎

3.
Geometry of hypersurfaces in
(\mathbb{H}^{n},g_{\varepsilon})

Along this section, we fix
\varepsilon\neq 0
and the metric
g_{\varepsilon}
, and write as
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{2n},Z_{2n+1}\}
the frame
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},\varepsilon T\}
to keep a compact notation. We let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be an embedded orientable hypersurface of class
C^{3}
with Riemannian unit normal
\nu^{\varepsilon}
.
We recall that

(3.1)

\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}(Z_{i}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{j})\nu^{\varepsilon}_{j}=0

for any
i=1,\ldots,2n+1
for any unitary extension of
\nu^{\varepsilon}
, being
(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1})
the coordinates of
\nu^{\varepsilon}
related to
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{2n+1}\}
.
Moreover, if we denote by
d
be the signed Riemannian distance from
S
,
then
d
is of class
C^{3}
near
S
, and satisfies the Eikonal equation
|\nabla d|=1

in a neighborhood of
S
, where we used the compact notation
\nabla=\nabla^{\varepsilon}
. Therefore,
\nu^{\varepsilon}
can be extended to a suitable neighborhood of
S
by letting
\nu^{\varepsilon}=\nabla d
.
With this extension, we have

(3.2)

Z_{i}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{j})=Z_{j}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i})

for any
i,j=1,\ldots,2n+1
such that either
i=2n+1
,
j=2n+1
or
|j-i|\neq n
.
Finally,

(3.3)

X_{i}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{n+i})=Y_{i}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i})-\frac{2\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_{i}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i})=X_{i}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{n+i})+\frac{2\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}

for any
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Hence

(3.4)

\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}(Z_{j}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i})\nu^{\varepsilon}_{j}=-2\frac{\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}J(\nu^{\varepsilon})_{i}

for any
i=1,\ldots,2n+1
, where we recall that
J(\nu^{\varepsilon})=(-\nu^{\varepsilon}_{n+1},\ldots,-\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n},\nu^{\varepsilon}_{1},\ldots,\nu^{\varepsilon}_{n},0)
.
We denote by
h^{\varepsilon}
and
H^{\varepsilon}
the associated
second fundamental form
and
mean curvature
of
S
respectively, i.e., for a given
p\in S
,
h^{\varepsilon}_{p}(v,w)=g_{\varepsilon}(\nabla_{v}\nu^{\varepsilon},w)

for any
v,w\in T_{p}S
, and

(3.5)

H^{\varepsilon}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}h^{\varepsilon}_{p}(e_{i},e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}g_{\varepsilon}(\nabla_{e_{i}}\nu^{\varepsilon},e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{2n+1}Z_{i}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i}(p)

for any orthonormal basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{2n}
of
T_{p}S
. It is possible to express the norm of
h^{\varepsilon}
by

(3.6)

|h^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\sum_{l,s=1}^{2n+1}Z_{s}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{l})Z_{l}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{s})+4\left\langle J(\nu^{\varepsilon}),\nabla\left(\frac{\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\rangle+(2n-2)\frac{(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}},

so that, combining (
2.3
) and (
3.6
), we infer that

(3.7)

\ric(\nu^{\varepsilon})+|h^{\varepsilon}|^{2}=\sum_{l,s=1}^{2n+1}Z_{s}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{l})Z_{l}(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{s})+4\left\langle J(\nu^{\varepsilon}),\nabla\left(\frac{\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\rangle+4n\frac{(\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1})^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}.

Let us denote by
\nabla_{S}
and
\Delta_{S}
the gradient and the Laplace-Beltrami operator in
(S,g_{\varepsilon}|_{S})
respectively.
A standard computation shows that

(3.8)

\begin{split}&|\nabla_{S}f|^{2}=|\nabla f|^{2}-g_{\varepsilon}(\nabla f,\nu^{\varepsilon})^{2}\\
&\Delta_{S}f=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n+1}g^{i,j}Z_{i}(Z_{j}f)-H^{\varepsilon}g_{\varepsilon}(\nabla f,\nu^{\varepsilon})+\frac{2\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}}{\varepsilon}\langle\nabla f,J(\nu^{\varepsilon})\rangle\end{split}

for any
f\in C^{2}(S)
, where

(3.9)

g^{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}-\nu^{\varepsilon}_{i}\nu^{\varepsilon}_{j}.

In particular, applying (
3.8
) to
\nu^{\varepsilon}_{2n+1}
and exploiting (
3.7
), we infer that

5.3.
Higher regularity of Lipschitz continuous t-graphs

In the rest of this section, inspired by
[

]
, we show classical regularity for minimizers of

.
The main difficulty consists in obtaining Lipschitz regularity since, as soon as a minimizer is Lipschitz, standard regularity results for uniformly elliptic equations apply. More precisely, the following regularity property for Lipschitz weak solutions to (

ε

-PMC
) holds.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
H\in\lip_{loc}(\Omega)
and
u\in\lip_{loc}(\Omega)
be a weak solution on
\Omega
to (

ε

-PMC
).
Then
u\in C^{2,\alpha}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
and is a classical solution to (

ε

-PMC
).
Moreover, if

H\in C^{k,\gamma}_{loc}(\Omega)
for some

and
\gamma\in(0,1)
, then
u\in C^{k+2,\gamma}_{loc}(\Omega)
. Finally, if
H\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, then

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Let
\tilde{\Omega}\Subset\Omega
be a bounded domain. It suffices to show that
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\tilde{\Omega})
. For
r>0,
set

\tilde{\Omega}_{r}=\left\{z\in\tilde{\Omega}\,:\,\min_{w\in\partial\Omega}|z-w|>r\right\}.

Given

\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\tilde{\Omega}_{r})
for
r>0
small enough. Let
v\in B(0,r)
.
Using
\psi
and
\psi(\cdot-v)
as test functions the weak formulation of (

ε

-PMC
), we get

(5.19)

\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\langle A(z+v,Du(z+v))-A(z,Du(z)),D\psi(z)\rangle\,dz=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}(H(z)-H(z+v))\psi(z)\,dz,

where

A(z,\xi)=\frac{\xi+X(z)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}+|\xi+X(z)|^{2}}}.

Fixed

, the fundamental theorem of calculus implies that

(5.20)

A(z_{0}+v,Du(z_{0}+v))-A(z_{0},Du(z_{0}))=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d(A(\alpha_{z_{0}}(s))}{ds}dt,

where
\alpha_{z_{0}}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}

\alpha_{z_{0}}(t)=(z_{0}+tv,Du(z_{0})+t(Du(z_{0}+v)-Du(z_{0}))).

Writing

, it follows from a direct computation that

(5.21)

\begin{split}\frac{d(A(\alpha_{z}(t))}{dt}&=A_{z}(\alpha_{z}(t))\cdot v+|v|A_{\xi}(\alpha_{z}(t))\cdot Du_{v}(z),\end{split}

where

(A_{\xi})_{ij}(z,\xi)=\frac{\delta_{ij}(\varepsilon^{2}+|\xi+X(z)|^{2})-(\xi_{i}+X(z)_{i})(\xi_{j}+X(z)_{j})}{(\varepsilon^{2}+|\xi+X(z)|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

We set

\begin{split}\tilde{A}(z)&=\int_{0}^{1}A_{\xi}(\alpha_{z}(t))\,dt,\\
\tilde{B}_{v}(z)&=\left(\int_{0}^{1}A_{z}(\alpha_{z}(t))\,dt\right)\cdot\frac{v}{|v|}\\
\tilde{H}_{v}(z)&=\frac{H(z)-H(z+v)}{|v|}.\end{split}

Inserting (
5.20
) and (
5.21
) in (
5.19
) and dividing by

, we get

\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\langle\tilde{A}(z)\cdot Du_{v}(z),D\psi(z)\rangle\,dz=\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\tilde{H}_{v}(z)\psi(z)\,dz-\int_{\tilde{\Omega}}\left\langle\tilde{B}_{v}(z),D\psi(z)\right\rangle\,dz.

In other words,
u_{v}
is a weak solution on
\tilde{\Omega}
to the linear equation

\divv\left(\tilde{A}\cdot Du_{v}\right)-\tilde{H}_{v}-\divv\tilde{B}_{v}.

Since

\tilde{B}_{v}
and

are uniformly bounded with respect to

. Moreover, since

, (

) implies that

is uniformly elliptic on

. Finally, since

, then

is bounded in

uniformly for

with

small enough. Therefore, using the celebrated De Giorgi-Nash-Moser method (cf.
[
15
, Chapter 4, Theorem 2.3]
and cf. the proof of the Corollary right after
[

), we conclude that, up to a smaller

,

is bounded in

uniformly for

, so that

and

. In particular,
\tilde{A}
and
\tilde{B}_{v}
are of class

. Therefore, in view of
[

, Theorem 8,32]
and up to a smaller

,

is bounded in

uniformly for

, so that

. In particular,

is a classical solution to (

ε

-PMC
).
Let

and assume

is a weak solution to (

-PMC
