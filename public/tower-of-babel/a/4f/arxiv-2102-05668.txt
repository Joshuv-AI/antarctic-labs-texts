Tidal Circularization of Binaries by Resonance Locking I: The Importance of the Pre-Main-Sequence
J. J. Zanazzi, Yanqin Wu

Abstract
Although tidal dissipation in binary stars has been studied for over a century, theoretical predictions have yet to match the observed properties of binary populations. This work quantitatively examines the recent proposal of tidal circularization by resonance locking, where tidal dissipation arises from resonances between the star’s natural oscillation frequencies and harmonics of the orbital frequency, and where resonances are ‘locked’ for an extended period of time due to concurrent stellar evolution. We focus on tidal resonances with axi-symmetric gravity-modes, and examine binaries with primary masses from one to two solar masses. We find that orbital evolution via resonance locking occurs primarily during the star’s pre-main-sequence phase, with the main-sequence phase contributing negligibly. Resonance locking, ignoring non-linearity, can circularize binaries with peri-centre distances out to ∼ 10 \sim 10 stellar radii, corresponding to circular periods of ∼ 4 − 6 \sim 4-6 days. However, we find resonantly excited gravity-modes will become nonlinear in stellar cores, which prevents them from reaching their full, linear amplitudes. We estimate that such a ‘saturated resonance lock’ reduces the circularization period by about a third, but resonance locking remains much more effective than the cumulative actions of equilibrium tides. In a companion paper, we examine recent binary data to compare against theory.



I
Introduction

How tidal dissipation drives orbital and rotational evolution in binary star systems has been studied for centuries
(
Darwin 1879
, e.g.)
, but its final resolution still eludes us
(
Mazeh 2008
;
Ogilvie 2014
, for reviews, see)
.

Tidal circularization is observed in binaries. Short period binaries tend to be more circular
(
Mayor & Mermilliod 1984
;
Duquennoy & Mayor 1991
;
Latham et al. 1992
;
Latham et al. 2002
;
Mathieu et al. 2004
, e.g.)
. The most systematic study to date is that by
Meibom & Mathieu 2005
, who found that as stars age from Pre-Main-Sequence (PMS) to late Main-Sequence (MS), the “characteristic circularization period” (
P_{\rm circ}
, below which orbits are mostly circular) generally increases with age. While binaries in young open clusters (less than a few hundred million years) are circularized out to
P_{\rm circ}\sim 8
days, older clusters appear to exhibit longer circularization periods, perhaps out to
P_{\rm circ}\sim 15
days. Unfortunately, the data used to establish such a trend are sparse. Each cluster contains only about a dozen or so spectroscopic binaries, making the measurement of circularization periods difficult and susceptible to sample variance. In a companion paper to the current one
(
Zanazzi & Wu 2020
)
, we will re-examine the issue of circularization periods using the much expanded binary samples from APOGEE and
Kepler
.

A large number of theoretical studies have been undertaken to explain the
Meibom & Mathieu 2005
results, and all have difficulties accounting for the long circularization periods in old binaries. In the following, we will review a few of these theoretical studies that are influential and are relevant for our study.

Zahn and collaboraters
(
Zahn 1966
;
Zahn 1989
;
Zahn & Bouchet 1989
;
Zahn 2008
)
developed the theory of equilibrium tides, where the star is assumed to react hydrostatically to the binary tidal forcing, and a lag time (or angle) is introduced to account for the internal dissipation of this response. The dissipation leads to energy and angular momentum transfer between the binary orbit and the star. This has been shown to be most effective in stars with surface convection zones where turbulent eddies can provide kinematic viscosity.

Zahn & Bouchet 1989
pointed out that, during the pre-main-sequence, this process is more efficient because stars are more distended and have more extensive convection zones. They found that binaries can be circularized out to
P_{\rm circ}\sim 8
days before they reach the main-sequence. While this appears to explain the circularization data for young clusters, later studies have called into question some of their assumptions
(see Sec.
VI.2
for a discussion). In addition, Zahn’s equilbrium tide theory has difficulties explaining the observed spin rates of some binary stars
(
Meibom et al. 2006
;
Marilli et al. 2007
;
Lurie et al. 2017
;
Mazeh 2008
)
, an area not considered in this work.
However, the equilibrium tide theory does enjoy an undisputed success in the case of evolved stars.
Observations show that post-MS binaries are circularized out to
\sim 100\ {\rm days}

(
Mayor & Mermilliod 1984
;
Bluhm et al. 2016
;
Price-Whelan et al. 2020
, e.g.)
, in accord with predictions from Zahn’s theory
(
Verbunt & Phinney 1995
;
Price-Whelan & Goodman 2018
)
.

Another parallel line of investigation is called the theory of dynamical tides.
While the equilibrium tide assumes the star reacts instantaneously to the forcing, this theory accounts for the tidal excitation of dynamical modes. In stars with radiative interiors, gravity-modes are the most relevant
(
Zahn 1975
;
Savonije & Papaloizou 1983
;
Terquem et al. 1998
;
Goodman & Dickson 1998
, e.g)
, while inertial-modes may also play a role in stars that are rotating sufficiently fast
(
Savonije & Papaloizou 1997
;
Ogilvie & Lin 2007
;
Ivanov & Papaloizou 2007
, e.g.)
.
In particular, when the frequency of tidal forcing lies close to a natural oscillation frequency of the host (a tidal resonance), the tidal response is strong and its associated dissipation brings about rapid orbital evolution. However, because the resonance width is normally very narrow, the binary orbit quickly shrinks through a resonance, and the latter has little lasting dynamical impact
(
Terquem et al. 1998
;
Goodman & Dickson 1998
)
.

To address this issue, a variant of the dynamical tide theory, ‘resonance locking,’ has been developed.
Savonije & Papaloizou 1983
;
Savonije & Papaloizou 1984
;
Witte & Savonije 1999b
;
Fuller et al. 2016
;
Fuller 2017
suggested that, when the mode in resonance increases in frequency (due to evolution in either stellar structure or stellar spin), in conjunction with the orbital frequency, the resonance may be maintained for an extended period, during which the binary continue to “surf” the resonance and experience significant orbital evolution. It is argued to be important for both early type

(
Witte & Savonije 1999b
;
Witte & Savonije 2001
;
Fuller & Lai 2012a
;
Burkart et al. 2012
)

and late type
(
Witte & Savonije 2002
;
Savonije & Witte 2002
)
main-sequence binaries.

A tidal resonance can be expressed as

k\Omega\approx\sigma=\omega+m\Omega_{\star}\,,

(1)

where
k\Omega
is the
k
-order harmonic of the binary orbital frequency, and
\sigma
the mode frequency in the inertial frame. The latter is related to the mode frequency in the star’s rotating frame (
\omega
), the mode’s azimuthal quantum number (
m
), and the star’s spin rate (
\Omega_{\star}
). Resonance locking requires that, as the orbit evolves,
k\dot{\Omega}\approx\dot{\sigma}
. The latter can be caused by stellar evolution (
\dot{\omega}
), or by changes in the host spin (
\dot{\Omega}_{\star}
), two possibilities addressed respectively by

Fuller et al. 2016
;
Fuller 2017
and
Witte & Savonije 1999b
;
Fuller & Lai 2012b
.

Despite the promise of resonance locking, it is surprising that no quantitative works have been performed to study its efficacy in circularizing solar-type binaries, and to compare the predictions against observations. In this work and an associated paper, we perform exactly these tasks. We study resonance locking by tracking how a star’s oscillation frequencies evolve over its lifetime
(
Fuller et al. 2016
;
Fuller 2017
, similar to)
. For the first time, we follow the evolution both during the pre-main-sequence and the main-sequence. We find that the pre-main-sequence phase plays a disproportionately large role, while the main-sequence phase contributes negligibly to tidal evolution. This is in stark contrast to the trend reported by
Meibom & Mathieu 2005
.

This result naturally stimulates us to re-examine the observed data. We are aided by the large influx of spectrocopic and transiting data
(
Milliman et al. 2014
;
Van Eylen et al. 2016
;
Triaud et al. 2017
;
Windemuth et al. 2019
;
Price-Whelan et al. 2020
, e.g.)
, and we present our results in Paper II
(
Zanazzi & Wu 2020
)
. With these two papers, we hope to establish that: 1) resonance locking works primarily during the PMS for solar-type binaries, and 2) resonance locking during the PMS may help explain the observed circularization periods.

While this paper is under review, the referee pointed out an important caveat in our work. The resonantly excited gravity-modes in our calculations may well reach such large amplitudes in the stellar radiative cores that the waves may overturn, a process that has been discussed by

Goodman & Dickson 1998
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2011
;
Su et al. 2020
. This is indeed born out by our estimates. As many of the gravity-modes that can resonantly lock onto the tidal perturbation are non-linearly damped and saturated at lower amplitudes compared to our linear results, the efficacy of resonance locking is called into question. We dedicate a new section to discuss this aspect. The overall picture remains, at this moment, not as clear as we have hoped.

The structure of this paper is as follows. Section
II
reviews the properties of gravity-modes (g-modes) in stars, the formalism describing their tidal excitation. We also describe how we calculate the g-mode properties over the host star’s lifetime. Section
III
discusses the conditions a binary must satisfy to maintain resonance lock, as well as the binary’s orbital evolution under resonance locking. Section
IV
presents our results on the tidal evolution of binary star populations under resonance locking. Section
VI
confirms the un-importance of equilibrium tides, discusses the theoretical uncertainties of our analysis, and observational predictions. Section
VII
summarizes our work.

II
Gravity Modes: Properties, Evolution, and Tidal Excitation

Before examining how the evolution of stellar binaries can drive rapid tidal circularization through resonance locking, we will review the properties of tidally excited gravity modes (g-modes).
Under a tidal forcing
-{\bm{\nabla}}U({\bm{r}},t)

produced by a binary companion of mass
M_{\star}^{\prime}
orbiting at a semi-major axis
a
and eccentricity
e
, the displacement in the host star (with mass
M_{\star}
, radius
R_{\star}
and rotation frequency
\Omega_{\star}
),
{\bm{\xi}}({\bm{r}},t)
evolves as

\frac{\partial^{2}{\bm{\xi}}}{\partial t^{2}}+\mathcal{R}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}={{\bm{f}}}-{\bm{\nabla}}U,

(2)

where
\mathcal{R}
is an operator acting on
{\bm{\xi}}
due to restoring force inside the star, and
{\bm{f}}
represents any (weak) frictional force. We restrict ourselves to gravity-modes, where the only contribution to
\mathcal{R}
is the buoyancy force. It is reasonable to ignore pressure-modes (restored by pressure) as their eigenfrequencies lie well above the tidal forcing frequency. We also assume that the tidal response is linear, that the host star’s spin axis is aligned with the binary’s orbital plane, and that tidal dissipation in the companion star is negligible. We further assume the stellar spin rate is much lower than the g-mode oscillation frequencies (
\Omega_{\star}\ll\omega_{\alpha}
) so we can ignore the Coriolis force. We also ignore another branch of oscillations, the so-called inertial modes. The validity of these approximations will be discussed in Section
VI
.

The above equation simplifies for adiabatic, free oscillations into

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}-}\omega_{\alpha}^{2}{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}+\mathcal{R}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}=0\,,

(3)

where
\omega_{\alpha}
is the frequency for eigen-mode
\alpha
. We can express the effect of the weak friction force
{\bm{f}}
by a damping rate, using the so-called quasi-adiabatic approximation
(
Friedman & Schutz 1978a
;
Friedman & Schutz 1978b
;
Christensen-Dalsgaard 2014
, see e.g.)

\gamma_{\alpha}=-\frac{{\rm i}}{2\omega_{\alpha}}\frac{\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{f}}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}t}\rangle}{\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}\rangle}\,.

(4)

where
{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}
is the adiabatic eigenfunction from equation (
3
). Here,
\gamma_{\alpha}>0
stands for the rate of energy (not amplitude) dissipation, and
\gamma_{\alpha}\ll\omega_{\alpha}
. The bracket stands for the volume integral,
\langle{\bm{u}}|{\bm{v}}\rangle\equiv\int{\bm{u}}^{*}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{v}}\rho\text{d}V
, and the factor of
2
arises when one converts the energy damping rate to amplitude damping rate.

Since the tidally forced perturbation is assumed to be linear, we can decompose the total displacement into a sum over adiabatic eigenfunctions
{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}
,

{{\bm{\xi}}({\bm{r}},t)}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t)\,{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}t}\,{{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}({\bm{r}})}\,,

(5)

where the mode amplitude
c_{\alpha}
varies over a timescale much longer than the mode frequency. As a result, we can approximately express

\dot{\bm{\xi}}({\bm{r}},t)\simeq\sum_{\alpha}-{\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}\,c_{\alpha}(t){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}t}{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}({\bm{r}})\,.

(6)

Substituting these above expressions into equation (
2
), we obtain the amplitude equation that describes the evolution of
c_{\alpha}(t)

(
Schenk et al. 2002
;
Burkart et al. 2014
;
Fuller 2017
, e.g.,)

\dot{c}_{\alpha}+({\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}+\gamma_{\alpha})c_{\alpha}=\frac{{\rm i}}{2\omega_{\alpha}}\frac{\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|-{\bm{\nabla}}U\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\omega_{\alpha}t}\rangle}{\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}\rangle}\,.

(7)

We review the derivation of equation (
7
) in Appendix
B
. In particular, when the Coriolis force is also present, orthogonality between eigenmodes is called into question. However, equations (
7
) and (
4
) remain valid.

The solution to this in-homogeneous equation describes the evolution of the tidal response, and through it, the tidal evolution of the binary orbit. In the following, we consider the quantities
\omega_{\alpha}
,
\gamma_{\alpha}
and the tidal overlap integral (right-hand-side of eq. [
7
]), separately. In particular, since we are interested in tidal evolution that proceeds in the same timescale as stellar evolution, we pay special attention to how these quantities change as the star ages.

In the following, we suppress the mode index
\alpha
for notational simplicity.

II.1
Gravity Mode Frequencies

This section calculates the g-mode eigen-frequencies for our stellar models (see eq. [
3
]). Since this exercise is standard, we move most of the relevant algebra
to Appendix
A
, and only list a few relevant results here. Again, we ignore the effects of rotation (Coriolis force and centrifugal force), except the effect of mode frequency shifting by the rotating frame.

Of relevance for later discussions is the structure of a gravity mode: it propagates (is oscillatory) in the star’s radiative region where its frequency lies below the local Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N
, and is evanescent in the convective region. For modes of high radial order (
n\gg 1
), the local dispersion relation (eq. [
A8
]) can be integrated across the star to yield a global dispersion relation

\omega\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{\pi n}\int_{0}^{R_{\star}}\frac{N(r)}{r}\text{d}r,

(8)

where
\omega
is mode frequency in a non-rotating frame,
n
is the number of zero nodes in its radial displacement, and
\ell
refers to the spherical degree when the angular dependence is described by the spherical harmonics function (
Y_{\ell m}
).

To normalize the mode eigenfunction, we adopt
(
Weinberg et al. 2012
, e.g.)

2\langle{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{\xi}}\rangle\equiv 2\int_{0}^{R_{\star}}\left[\xi_{r}^{2}+\ell(\ell+1)\xi_{\perp}^{2}\right]\rho r^{2}\text{d}r=1\,.

(9)

Here,
\xi_{r}
and
\xi_{\perp}
are the radial and horizontal displacements, respectively. Under this normalization, the displacements do not have the dimension of length, and the mode energy (in the star’s rotating frame) is
E=\epsilon=\omega^{2}
.

As a star ages and its stratification changes, its g-mode frequencies also evolve. Figure
1
exhibits the radial profiles for the Brunt-Väisälä frequency (
N^{2}
) for three different stellar masses, at different stages of the star’s Pre-Main Sequence (PMS) and Main Sequence (MS) evolution. During the PMS, which lasts from a few to a few tens of Myrs (see Fig.
7
), all stellar types have radiative cores and convective envelopes, with the magnitudes of
N^{2}

roughly independent of the star’s mass.
The magnitude of
N^{2}
in the radiative regions increases with time as the star contracts and its core becomes more stably stratified.
This causes the g-mode frequencies to increase significantly during the PMS (eq. [
8
]).

After stars reach the MS, their structures diverge, with the surface convection zone disappearing in the more massive models (
M_{\star}\gtrsim 1.4\ {\rm M}_{\odot}
). Over much of the MS, the magnitudes of
N^{2}
in the radiative regions rise only mildly (if at all), in contrast to the strong increase in the PMS phase. This leads to a slight increase in g-mode frequencies.

The radial structure of a representative g-mode is plotted in Figure
2
, where we see that the g-mode has an ‘evanescent tail’ in the surface convection zone (
r\gtrsim 0.6\,{\rm R}_{\odot}
), and its horizontal displacement rapidly oscillates in the interior. The former is important for coupling to the smooth tidal potential, while the latter is relevant for the dissipation of these modes.

II.2
Damping Rates

Here, we evaluate how fast the mechanical energy from g-modes can be converted into heat (mode damping rates).
The dissipation causes the tidal displacement to have a phase lag relative to the tidal forcing, exerting a back-reaction torque on the perturber, which in turn drives the tidal evolution of the binary system.

Dissipation of gravity-modes proceeds through three possible channels: turbulent damping inside the convection zone, radiative damping caused by photon diffusion, and last, the leakage of mode energy from the upper atmosphere. The detailed expressions for these rates are enumerated in Appendix
C
, where we also obtain order-of-magnitude estimates. Here, we present these estimates and compare them against numerical results (Fig.
3
).

Because a g-mode propagates almost adiabatically across the star, over (under) densities are hot (cold) spots relative to the stellar background. Radiative diffusion works to diffuse these temperature inhomogeneities, damping the mode at a rate

\gamma_{\rm diff}\sim\frac{L_{\star}}{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{2}\omega^{2}}\Lambda_{\rm core},

(10)

where

\Lambda_{\rm core}=\frac{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{2}}{\left({\rho Hr^{4}}\right)_{r=r_{\rm c}}}

(11)

parameterizes the characteristic mass concentration in the star’s radiative core (evaluated at the characteristic radius
r_{\rm c}
, see Appendix
C.1
), and
L_{\star}
is the stellar luminosity. The central concentration enters the estimate because the g-mode energy is mostly removed by radiative diffusion in the stellar centre, where its wavelength is short and its displacement is large. The parameter
\Lambda_{\rm core}
increases with age as the star becomes more centrally-concentrated. For a solar-mass star,
\Lambda_{\rm core}\sim 10^{2}-10^{3}
during the PMS, and
\sim 10^{3}-10^{4}
during the MS.
As a result, the damping rate increases as the star evolves from the PMS to the MS.
Examining the top panel of Figure
3
, we see estimate (
10
) does well at reproducing the scaling of
\gamma_{\rm diff}
with frequency, as well as its dependence on stellar age. Higher frequency modes have lower radial order and longer wavelengths. They suffer significantly weaker radiative dissipation.

Turbulent damping arises from convective eddies interacting viscously with the g-mode, causing a dissipation rate of order (see eq. [
C20
])

\gamma_{\rm turb}\sim\frac{\ell^{2}}{3n\omega}\left(\frac{z_{\rm cvz}^{2/3}L_{\star}^{2/3}}{R_{\star}^{2}M_{\rm cvz}^{2/3}}\right)\,,

(12)

where
z_{\rm cvz}
and
M_{\rm cvz}
are the depth and mass of the surface convection zone, respectively. The bottom panel of Figure
3
compares this estimate with our numerical results and shows reasonable agreement. Compared to the radiative damping rates, the turbulent damping rates decrease mildly as the star ages, and do not depend sensitively on mode frequency.

The g-mode perturbation may propagate in a thin region above the photosphere if the mode
frequency lies below the so-called “acoustic cut-off frequency.” Our calculation in Appendix
C.3
shows this to be negligible compared to other sources of damping, for g-modes with periods of order a few days.

Comparing the top and bottom panels of. Figure
3
, we see radiative diffusion dominates over turbulent viscosity (
\gamma_{\rm diff}\gtrsim\gamma_{\rm turb}
) over the PMS and MS evolution of the star, with the exception of modes of the lowest radial orders (
n\lesssim 10
). For all g-modes of concern, mode dissipation is weak, relative to the spacing between consecutive g-modes (
\gamma\ll\omega/n
). Therefore, our assumption of individual, discrete modes is appropriate
(
Goodman & Dickson 1998
)
.

II.3
Tidal Excitation

The tidal potential from the binary companion acting on the host star may be decomposed into spherical harmonics and a Fourier series as
(
Press & Teukolsky 1977
)

\displaystyle U({\bm{r}},t)=\

\displaystyle-\frac{GM_{\star}^{\prime}}{a}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{\ell+1}W_{\ell m}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X_{k\ell m}(e){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}{Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\varphi)}\,,

(13)

where

\displaystyle W_{\ell m}=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}Y^{*}_{\ell m}\left(\frac{\pi}{2},0\right)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle X_{k\ell m}(e)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\text{d}E}{(1-e\cos E)^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\times\cos\left[k(E-e\sin E)-2m\tan^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}\right)\right]\,,

(15)

\displaystyle\omega_{km}=k\Omega-m\Omega_{\star}\,,

(16)

and
\Omega=\sqrt{G(M_{\star}+M_{\star}^{\prime})/a^{3}}
is the orbital frequency/mean-motion. From now on, sums over
k
will implicitly be from
-\infty
to
\infty
,
\ell
from
2
to
\infty
, and
m
from
-\ell
to
\ell
, unless otherwise noted.
We focus on the dominant
\ell=2
component in our numerical results, but the following discussion applies to all
\ell
values.

Using this decomposition of the tidal potential, equation (
7
) is recast as

\dot{c}+({\rm i}\omega+\gamma)c={\rm i}\omega\sum_{k\ell m}U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}\,,

(17)

where

\displaystyle U_{k\ell m}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{\star}^{\prime}}{M_{\star}}\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{a}\right)^{\ell+1}\left(\frac{E_{\star}}{\epsilon}\right)W_{\ell m}I_{\ell m}X_{k\ell m}\,,

(18)

\displaystyle I_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{\nabla}}(r^{\ell}Y_{\ell m})\rangle}{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{\ell}}=\frac{1}{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{\ell}}\int\delta\rho\,r^{\ell+2}\text{d}r\,.

(19)

Here,
E_{\star}=GM_{\star}^{2}/R_{\star}
, and
\gamma
is the mode damping rate (Sec.
II.2
).

The in-homogeneous solution to equation (
17
) is

c(t)=\sum_{k\ell m}\frac{\omega}{\omega-\omega_{km}-{\rm i}\gamma}U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}\,.

(20)

This solution assumes that changes in the frequencies
(\omega,\omega_{km},\gamma)
are sufficiently slow that the mode amplitude
c(t)
can respond adiabatically. On the other hand, the dissipation needs to damp the mode amplitude quickly, compared with variations in
(\omega,\omega_{km},\gamma)
, so that no prior information is retained during the evolution of
c(t)
. In Appendix
D
, we further justify the assumption of adiabaticity for our problem, and argue for adiabadicity conditions contrary to that suggested in

Burkart et al. 2014
, who implicitly assumed negligible damping rates in their derivation.
Adiabaticity guarantees the success of resonance locking, a central topic in this work.

Near a resonance (
\omega\approx\omega_{km}
), one tidal component
(k,m)
dominates, and solution (
7
) simplifies to

c(t)\simeq C(t)\equiv\frac{\omega}{\omega-\omega_{km}-{\rm i}\gamma}U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}.

(21)

The maximum amplitude of
C
occurs at exact resonance (
\omega=\omega_{km}
):

C^{\rm max}={\rm i}\frac{\omega}{\gamma}U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}.

(22)

Each eigen-mode has energy
E_{\rm mode}
and angular momentum
J_{\rm mode}
in the inertial frame given by
(
Friedman & Schutz 1978a
;
Friedman & Schutz 1978b
;
Burkart et al. 2014
;
Fuller 2017
)

E_{\rm mode}=\frac{\sigma\epsilon}{\omega}|c|^{2},\hskip 14.22636ptJ_{\rm mode}=\frac{m\epsilon}{\omega}|c|^{2},

(23)

where

\sigma=\omega+m\Omega_{\star}

(24)

is the mode frequency in the inertial frame. The mode energy and angular momentum also attain their maximum values at exact resonance:

E_{\rm mode}^{\rm max}=\frac{\epsilon\sigma\omega}{\gamma^{2}}|U_{k\ell m}|^{2}\,,\hskip 14.22636ptJ_{\rm mode}^{\rm max}=\frac{m\epsilon\omega}{\gamma^{2}}|U_{k\ell m}|^{2}\,.

(25)

Numerically, the overlap integral

is delicate to calculate. To confirm numerical results, we estimate its magnitude here. Because the Eulerian density perturbation
\delta\rho
oscillates rapidly in a mode’s propagating region, most of the contribution to the overlap integral arises from the evanescent zone, or the surface convection zone:
1
1

1

Here, we consider low-frequency modes which propagate to the bottom of the convection zone.

I_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{\ell}}\int\delta\rho\,r^{\ell+2}\text{d}r\sim\frac{1}{M_{\star}}\int\left(\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho}\right)\rho r^{2}dr\,.

(26)

The Eulerian density perturbation is as expressed in equation (
A4
),

\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho}=\frac{r\omega^{2}}{c_{\rm s}^{2}}\xi_{\perp}+\frac{N^{2}}{g}\xi_{r}\simeq\frac{r\omega^{2}}{c_{\rm s}^{2}}\xi_{\perp}\,,

(27)

where
\xi_{\perp}(\xi_{r})
is the tangential (radial) displacement (see Appendix §
A
) and we have taken
N^{2}\approx 0
in the convection zone. Since
c_{s}^{2}\sim gz\propto z
near the surface, the integrand is the largest at the radiative-convective boundary, or

I_{\ell m}\sim\bar{I}\frac{M_{\rm cvz}}{M_{\star}}\left(\frac{r\omega^{2}\xi_{\perp}}{c_{\rm s}^{2}}\right)_{\rm cvz}\,,

(28)

where quantities within the brackets are to be evaluated at the bottom of the convection zone. Furthermore, we provide an estimate for
\xi_{\perp}
for our adopted normalization in equation (
A11

We have also inserted a numerical fudge-factor
\bar{I}
. As Figure

\bar{I}\sim 10^{-2}
(
\bar{I}=0.007
for this example).

This expression indicates that the overlap is reduced at higher mode order
n
, and for stars with shallower convection zones. In particular, for stars without a surface convection zone (
M_{\star}\gtrsim 1.4\,{\rm M}_{\odot}
on the MS), the overlap is entirely contributed by the thin region above the first radial node (not accounted for here) and is very small.
This makes resonant excitation of g-modes difficult for early type main-sequence stars.

\displaystyle k\dot{\Omega}

This section applies the formalism developed above to test the effectiveness of resonance locking with zonal g-modes (

Second, resonance locking is more feasible for modes that are strongly coupled to the tidal potential, and are weakly damped. Both of these are optimized during the PMS, where the stars are less heavily stratified and g-modes of the requisite periods have much lower radial orders.

V
The Role of non-linearity

The calculations presented above assume that the gravity-waves remain linear in its propagation and damping.
However, we are prompted by the referee to consider the role of non-linearity in resonance locking: if the tidal displacement becomes non-linear, the wave can overturn the stratification and dissipate energy rapidly. This physics complicates the resonant locking picture substantially.

In stars with radiative interiors,
as gravity-waves propagate towards the centre, their wavelengths shorten and their amplitudes rise. This is most extreme near the inner turning point (where
\omega\approx N
), where the background stratification can be overturned by the large amplitude wave, akin to the physics of Kelvin-Helmholtz instability
(
Goodman & Dickson 1998
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2011
;
Barker 2011
;
Weinberg et al. 2012
, e.g.)
.
For stars like the Sun,
Goodman & Dickson 1998
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
showed that gravity-waves excited by stellar or planetary companions can find themselves to be nonlinear on their first travel inward to the core (we call this non-resonant excitation). The non-linearity is stronger for longer period companions, because they excite lower frequency waves, which have shorter wavelengths, and can penetrate into more central regions. Given this, it is natural to ask whether similar physics operates for pre-main-sequence binaries, and if resonantly excited modes, with their large amplitudes, can in fact exist.

V.1
Magnitudes of non-linearity

Non-linearity for non-resonantly excited waves is insignificant for pre-main-sequence stars. Inserting values relevant for the solar model at 10 Myrs into equation (14) of
Goodman & Dickson 1998
(see also
Ogilvie & Lin 2007
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
),
we find that at the lower turning point (
r=r_{m}
), a
({k,\ell})=(1,2)
gravity-wave
reaches a dimensionless amplitude

|k_{r}\xi_{r}|_{r=r_{\rm m}}^{\rm non-res}\sim 6\times 10^{-4}\left(\frac{e}{{0.4}}\right)\,\left(\frac{P_{\rm orb}}{1\,{\rm day}}\right)^{1/6}\,,

(44)

or, non-resonant gravity-waves are linear for systems of interest (eq. [
43
]).
This non-linearity is about
10^{6}
weaker than that in a Solar model (eq. [34] in that work). This mostly results from the reduced stratification in PMS cores, which moves the inner turning point outward (eq. [
F2
]).

In contrast, resonantly excited gravity-modes can reach much higher amplitudes. In Appendix
F.1
, we estimate for the same gravity-wave but now resonantly excited to find (eq. [
F5
])

|k_{r}\xi_{r}|_{r=r_{\rm m}}^{\rm max}\sim 1.4\times 10^{4}\left(\frac{e}{0.4}\right)\left(\frac{1\,{\rm day}}{P_{\rm orb}}\right)^{7/2}.

(45)

For all systems of interest (eq. [
43
]), non-linearity is severe.

So in conclusion, during the PMS phase, tidally excited gravity-waves can still form discrete modes (not mere running waves), but they could not reach the amplitudes expected from (linear) resonant driving due to nonlinear breaking. The efficacy of resonant locking is severely compromised by the presence of non-linearity.

V.2
Saturated Resonant Lock?

What is the consequence of this compromise? While resonance lock still operates, the amplitude a mode can reach is now limited to a lower value than our linear estimates, leading to a reduced rate of tidal dissipation. This leads us to consider a new scenario, saturated resonant lock.

We discuss this scenario in detail in Appendix
F.2
. Here is a brief re-cap.
Consider a gravity-mode growing in amplitude due to resonant tidal forcing. It reaches
the threshold amplitude for breaking, in a time shorter than it takes to reach the linear estimate. At this point, its energy may quickly be converted into heat in the stellar core. Dividing this energy by the growth time,
we obtain an effective rate of energy dissipation. We then compare the rate of orbital evolution with the rate of stellar evolution to find a new condition for resonance lock. Inserting values appropriate for the Sun at 10 Myrs, we find that resonance locking is successful (satisfying eq. [
34
]) for binaries with

P_{\rm orb}\lesssim P_{\rm sat}\sim 3.6\,{\rm days}\,\left(\frac{e}{{0.4}}\right)^{6/41}\,.

(46)

This is
\sim 35\%
shorter than the estimate based on linear resonance lock (eq.
43
), but still much beyond what equilibrium tides can accomplish (see below).

Whether resonance lock in the presence of nonlinear saturation operates the way we speculate here is unclear. A more careful look, involving perhaps hydrodynamical simulations, is needed.

V.3
Nonlinear Wave Breaking during the main-sequence

Although the non-linearity of non-resonant dynamical tides is small during the PMS phase, this is not so during the main-sequence, and
Goodman & Dickson 1998
argued that non-resonant dynamical tides should circularize two solar-type binaries with ages
t
out to orbital periods
(
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
;
Barker 2020
, see also)

P_{\rm orb}\approx 6.4\left(\frac{t}{{10^{10}{\rm yrs}}}\right)^{1/7}\,{\rm days}\,.

(47)

Combining our picture of PMS resonant locking with the
Goodman & Dickson 1998
picture, we predict that solar-type binaries should be circularized out to
\sim 3
days by the end of the PMS, and exhibit a slow rise of the circularization period out to
\sim 6
days, as binary ages approach 10 Gyrs. Stars above the Kraft break (
M_{\star}\gtrsim 1.4\,{\rm M}_{\odot}

VI.3
Examining the Assumptions

In this work, we have adopted a simple approach to study resonance locking. We first calculate the properties of the gravity-modes in a given star (mass, age), then evaluate whether any component (quantum number
\{k,\ell,m=0\}
) of the tidal force can successfully lock on to a single g-mode
(eq. [
34
]). If so, the orbital frequency
\Omega(t)
is assumed to evolve in step with the frequency of this mode
\omega(t)
,
\Omega\simeq\omega/k
, as stellar evolution modifies the mode frequency. The orbit is assumed to circularize at a constant angular momentum (eq. [
41
]). We assume the orbital evolution stalls when there is no appropriate resonance locking. We have justified the last point by arguing that the equilibrium tide is ineffective. Dynamical tides without resonance locking has also previously been shown to be irrelevant for solar-type stars
(
Terquem et al. 1998
;
Goodman & Dickson 1998
)
.

In these calculations, we have only considered locking with zonal (
m=0
) g-modes, and have ignored the effects of Coriolis force and nonlinear wave interactions. We have adopted a form for the turbulent viscosity that is strongly reduced for slow tides. Here, we discuss these simplifications and assumptions in turn.

First, we are justified to consider locking with only one mode at any given time. G-modes are sparse, and the resonance width (
|\delta\omega|\sim|\omega-\omega_{km}|\sim|\gamma|
) is much smaller than the frequency difference between two consecutive g-modes (
|\Delta\omega|\sim\omega/n
; see Fig.
3
).

We consider only the zonal modes because of the mis-match in orbital and spin angular momenta (§
III.2
). The adoption of zonal modes means we can forgo the spin evolution.
7
7

7

In fact, our approach is fully self-consistent when the star is not spinning, since zonal modes then do not carry any angular momentum.
This simplifies the evolution significantly, and it ensures that the resonance locking is stable –

Burkart et al. 2014
showed that, if both energy and angular momentum are transferred between the binary orbit and the stellar spin (e.g., by
m\neq 0
modes or the equilibrium tide), it may be difficult to maintain resonance locking. We further justify our choice of zonal modes by investigating the inefficiency of sectoral (
m\neq 0
) modes in Appendix
E
.

However, our approach assumes that the frequencies of zonal modes are independent of the spin rate. This is only correct if
\Omega_{\star}\ll\omega
. Unfortunately, gravity-modes of interest have periods up to a few days (Fig.
7
), comparable in value to the spin periods of T Tauri stars
(
Bouvier 2013
, e.g.)
, or of stars that are spin synchronized with the orbit.
For modes in these stars, the Coriolis force provides a substantial restoring force, and their frequencies (and structure) become dependent on the spin rate
(
Bildsten et al. 1996
, for an example, see Fig. 2 of)
, in addition to their dependencies on stellar structure.
This could drive the system into or away from resonance locking
(
Lainey et al. 2020
, e.g.)
.

We have opted not to include the Coriolis effect in this work, for two reasons that are both to do with our inadequacies. First, including it will make g-modes much more analytically un-tractable – while one can use the ‘traditional approximation’ to calculate g-modes for fully stratified stars
(
Chapman & Lindzen 1970
;
Bildsten et al. 1996
, when
\Omega_{*}\ll N
,)
, our stars typically have convective outer shells. Buoyancy-dominated g-modes become inertial-gravity modes and there is currently no good technique to tackle them
(
Lai 1997
;
Witte & Savonije 1999a
;
Savonije & Witte 2002
;
Ogilvie & Lin 2004
;
Lai & Wu 2006
;
Ogilvie & Lin 2007
;
Wu 2005a
;
Wu 2005b
;
Ogilvie 2013
;
Lin & Ogilvie 2017
, but see attempts in e.g.)
. Second, as the mode frequency depends on the spin rate, we have to account for all physical processes that change the spin, including magnetic braking, stellar contraction, tidal torques from both the equilibrium tides and other dynamical tides. We also have to consider the re-distribution of angular momentum inside stars. This is beyond our current capability. The neglect of Coriolis force remains one of our most serious caveats.

Damping of g-modes directly affects resonance locking. If the modes are more strongly damped, the tidal torque will be smaller and resonance locks may break. We have experimented with enhancing the damping by a factor of
10
, and find that the circularization period moves inward by about a day.

We have briefly considered the role of nonlinear wave breaking on our results.
In stars with radiative interiors, mode interactions occur predominantly near the inner cores where the gravity-wave wavelengths are short and the amplitudes are large. We find that, even in the PMS phase, resonantly excited gravity-modes invariably reach nonlinear amplitudes, though they remain discrete modes.
The presence of non-linearity reduces the efficacy of resonance locking. Our crude estimate reduces the critical binary period by
\sim 35\%
(or separation by
\sim 23\%
), compared to when non-linearity is ignored. A more thorough investigation is needed.

Concurring with
Goodman & Oh 1997
, we find that the effects of equilibrium tide to be negligible in Sun-like stars, during both the PMS and MS phases. This conclusion depends on the turbulent viscosity form that we have adopted
(
Goldreich & Nicholson 1977
;
Goodman & Oh 1997
)
. If the form advocated by
Zahn 1966
is adopted instead, with a less severe reduction for the cases where convective turn-over is much slower than the tide,
Zahn & Bouchet 1989
found that binaries out to
P_{\rm orb}\approx 8
days can be circularized during the PMS phase. This would then make the equilibrium tide comparable in importance to resonance locking. If so, an intricate inter-play between spin and orbital evolution may result
(
Witte & Savonije 2002
;
Savonije & Witte 2002
;
Fuller & Lai 2012a
;
Burkart et al. 2012
, e.g.)
. As the equilibrium tide drives the star towards spin synchronization
(
Hut 1981
)
, resonance locking of g-modes may be modified or disrupted as the frequencies of these modes are altered by the spin. This is made more complicated by the fact that the equilibrium tide deposits its energy and therefore angular momentum primarily in the outer convective shell. In contrast, much of g-mode properties (frequency, structure and damping) are determined in the stellar interior. It is yet unclear how the two regions communicate.

There are a few situations where dynamical processes different what we study here may enter the play.
For more massive stars without surface convection zones, tidal excitation and dissipation of gravity-waves behave very differently. For instance, the absence of an evanescent tail weakens the tidal coupling of g-modes. Moreover, dissipation of gravity waves may be dominated by the so-called “critical layers”
(
Su et al. 2020
)
.
In another scenario, relevant for highly eccentric stellar binaries (
e\gtrsim 0.9
), f-modes may be stochastically excited at each periastron passage
(
Press & Teukolsky 1977
;
Mardling 1995a
;
Mardling 1995b
;
Vick & Lai 2018
;
Wu 2018
, e.g.)
. The diffusive growth of f-modes can efficiently extract orbital energy and circularizes the binary’s orbit rapidly.
Wu 2018
estimated an effective
Q\sim 1
during this phase.

VII
Conclusions

We have investigated the process of resonance locking to circularize binary systems. Tidal excitation of dynamical tides transfers orbital energy into mode energy, which is subsequently deposited in the stellar centre as heat. Where resonance between the tidal potential and an internal mode can be sustained, the orbit evolves over the same timescale as stellar evolution. We find this to be the case for pre-main-sequence binaries out to a few days. Main-sequence binaries, in contrast, rarely enjoy the benefit of resonance locking, and we find negligible tidal evolution during this phase of the stellar evolution.

Stars both above and below the Kraft break harbour surface convection zones during the PMS. Our calculations show that they can both be circularized to similar distances. Ignoring non-linearity, we find that as these binaries exit the PMS, their orbits should have evolved to fall under a curve of
a(1-e)\geq a_{\rm crit}
, where
a_{\rm crit}
corresponds to a period of
4-6
days (Fig.
9
). Expressed in units of MS stellar radii, we have

a_{\rm crit}/R_{\star}\approx 10\,.

(57)

In contrast, equilibrium tide can only circularize these stars out to
\sim 1.3
day periods, both during the PMS and the MS, or
a_{\rm crit}/R_{\star}\sim 5.5
.

Non-linearity in the stellar cores, however, is substantial for all models of interest. Our preliminary attempt to estimate its impact finds that resonance locking should still operate during the PMS, but with a lower efficiency. This process which we call ‘saturated resonance locking’ may circularize binaries out to
a_{\rm crit}/R_{\star}\sim 8
(equivalent to
\sim 35\%
reduction in period). More work is warranted.

These circularization limits seem to fall far short of what have been reported in the literature
(
Meibom & Mathieu 2005
;
Meibom et al. 2006
,
\sim 10-18
days for old stellar clusters and halo stars,)
. However, as we will argue in a companion paper, re-examining these observational claims lead to very different conclusions.

Despite our progress, there remains a number of theoretical uncertainties in the physics of resonance locking. We have shown that zonal gravity-modes (azimuthal number
m=0
) play the most important role in resonance locking. However, the frequencies and structure of these modes are affected by stellar spin, when the latter is sufficiently fast. So the spin evolution, ignored in this paper, may upset resonance locking in ways not accounted for here. Nonlinear mode interactions, which may enhance mode dissipation, could also affect our conclusions.

k_{r}
is

k_{r}^{2}=\frac{(L_{\ell}^{2}-\omega^{2})(N^{2}-\omega^{2})}{c_{\rm s}^{2}\omega^{2}}.

(A7)

Low-frequency g-modes propagate where
\omega^{2}<L_{\ell}^{2},N^{2}
, and are always evanescent in the star’s convective regions (
N^{2}\leq 0
). Since over most of the star’s radiative region (
N^{2}>0
), g-mode frequencies satisfy
\omega^{2}\ll L_{\ell}^{2},N^{2}
, we will frequently use the approximate expression

k_{r}\simeq{N\over{\omega}}k_{\perp}\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}N}{r\omega},

(A8)

where
k_{\perp}=\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}/r
is the azimuthal wavevector. This dispersion relation can in turn be used to solve for the g-mode’s eigenfrequency
\omega

(
Lai 1994
;
Fuller & Lai 2012b
, e.g.)
:

\omega\simeq\frac{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+1)}}{\pi n}\int_{0}^{R_{\star}}\frac{N(r)}{r}\text{d}r,

(A9)

where
n
is the number of radial displacement nodes (number of locations
\xi_{r}[r]=0
), while the integral is taken over the star’s radiative zone. We have implicitly assumed the radial node-number is large (
n\gg 1
). For the Solar model, a 10-day period
\ell=2
g-mode has
n\sim 1250
.

We assume a conventional normalization
(
Unno et al. 1979
;
Lai 1994
, e.g.)
, where the mode integral satisfies

2\langle{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{\xi}}\rangle=2\int_{0}^{R_{\star}}\left[\xi_{r}^{2}+\ell(\ell+1)\xi_{\perp}^{2}\right]\rho r^{2}\text{d}r=1.

(A10)

Here,
\langle{\bm{u}}|{\bm{v}}\rangle\equiv\int{\bm{u}}^{*}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{v}}\rho\text{d}V
, where
{\bm{u}}^{*}
is the complex conjugate of
{\bm{u}}
.
Although we will use normailization (
A10
) throughout this work, because different works frequently use different mode normailizations
(
Weinberg et al. 2012
;
Burkart et al. 2012
, e.g.)
, it is of use to write quantities in terms of
\epsilon=2\omega^{2}\langle{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{\xi}}\rangle
.
The quantity
\epsilon
is related to the mode’s energy, and with our normalization
\epsilon=\omega^{2}
.

For our order-of-magnitude calculations to follow, we estimate amplitude for the normalized eigen-function at the bottom of the star’s convection zone. For a Brunt-Väisälä that rises with depth
z
roughly as
N^{2}\sim g/z
, the gravity-modes propagate below a depth
z_{1}
, where
the upper turning point
z_{1}\sim\omega^{2}/(gk_{h}^{2})
. For high order modes (
n\geq{\sqrt{{R_{\star}}/z_{\rm cvz}}\sim{\rm few}}
for the sun), this turning point lies above the bottom of the convection zone
z_{\rm cvz}
, and
z_{1}\sim z_{\rm cvz}
. We consider this case here.

In the energy integral (eq. [
A10
]), every node contributes equally to the mode energy. So integration over the first node should be
1/n
. Combine this with
z_{1}\sim z_{\rm cvz}
, the horizontal displacement (the dominant component) has a normalized magnitude of

\left|{\tilde{\xi}_{\perp}}\right|^{2}_{z=z_{1}}\sim{1\over{n\,\ell^{2}\,M_{\rm cvz}}}\,

(A11)

where
M_{\rm cvz}
is the mass contained within the convection zone. The odd unit for the displacement is a result of our normalization convention. For modes with
z_{1}\gtrsim z_{\rm cvz}
(including cases where the star has no surface convection zone), one should substitute
M_{\rm cvz}
by
M_{z_{1}}
, the mass above the first turning point. We do not consider modes in stars without surface convection zones.

Inside the propagating cavity, according to the WKB analysis, the total energy flux carried by the mode is constant across all radii,

r^{2}\,\rho\xi_{\perp}^{2}\,v_{\rm group,r}\simeq{\rm constant}\,,

(A12)

where the radial group speed
v_{\rm group,r}={{\partial\omega}\over{\partial k_{r}}}\simeq\omega/k_{r}
. Using expression (
A8
), we obtain the following radial dependence for the envelope of the pulsation amplitude (the WKB envelope),

\left|{\bar{\xi}}_{\perp}(r)\right|^{2}\sim{{R_{\star}^{2}\rho(z_{1})\left|{\bar{\xi}_{\perp}}(z=z_{1})\right|^{2}\,z_{1}}\over{r^{2}\rho(r)/k_{r}(r)}}\sim{{k_{r}(r)}\over{n\ell^{2}\,r^{2}\rho(r)}}\simeq{1\over{\omega n\ell}}\,{N(r)\over{\rho r^{3}}}\,.

(A13)

This is also consistent with the statement that every radial node contributes the same amount of energy.
Moreover, the above expression gives
(k_{h}\xi_{\perp})^{2}\propto N/(\rho r^{5})
. This exhibits a steep rise toward the stellar centre.

Derivation of mode amplitude evolution and damping rate

In this appendix, we review the derivation of equation (

\ddot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}\dot{\bm{\xi}}+\mathcal{R}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}={\bm{f}}_{\rm ext},

(B1)

where
{\bm{f}}_{\rm ext}({\bm{r}},t)

\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}\dot{\bm{\xi}}=-2{\bm{\Omega}}_{\star}{\bm{\times}}\dot{\bm{\xi}}

is an operator for the Corioulus force, with
\mathcal{R}
defined as before. When

\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{\xi}}_{\beta}\rangle\neq 0

\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\rm i}\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}\rangle+(\lambda_{\alpha}+\lambda_{\beta})\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{\alpha}|{\bm{\xi}}_{\beta}\rangle=0

(B3)

when

. Because of this, it is non-trivial to determine
{\bm{\xi}}({\bm{r}},t)
in terms of the star’s natural oscillation modes

. To find a decomposition, we re-write equation (

) in a higher-dimensional vector space. Defining a new vector

{\bm{\zeta}}({\bm{r}},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\xi}}\\
\dot{\bm{\xi}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}\end{array}\right],

(B4)

\dot{\bm{\zeta}}+\mathcal{M}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}={\bm{F}}({\bm{r}},t),

(B5)

where

\mathcal{M}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}&1\\
-\mathcal{R}+\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}^{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}\end{array}\right],\hskip 14.22636pt{\bm{F}}({\bm{r}},t)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
{\bm{f}}_{\rm ext}({\bm{r}},t)\end{array}\right].

(B6)

We then decompose
{\bm{\zeta}}({\bm{r}},t)
as

\zeta({\bm{r}},t)=\sum_{A}c_{A}(t){\bm{\zeta}}_{A}({\bm{r}}){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\lambda_{A}t},

(B7)

where
{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}({\bm{r}})
satisfies

(\mathcal{M}+{\rm i}\lambda_{A}){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}=0.

(B8)

As in
Schenk et al. 2002
, we use the subscript
A
instead of
\alpha
, because the basis for
{\bm{\zeta}}
has a higher dimension than
{\bm{\xi}}
. Each left eigenvector
{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}
has a right eigenvector
{\bm{\chi}}_{A}
which satisfies

(\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}-{\rm i}\lambda_{A}^{*}){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\chi}}_{A}=0,

(B9)

where
\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}
is the Hermitian conjugate of
\mathcal{M}
. It may be shown
(
Schenk et al. 2002
)

{\bm{\chi}}_{A}
can be re-written as

{\bm{\chi}}_{A}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}{\rm i}\lambda_{A}^{*}{\bm{\tau}}_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\tau}}_{A}\\
{\bm{\tau}}_{A}\end{array}\right],

(B10)

where
{\bm{\tau}}_{A}
may be written in terms of
{\bm{\xi}}_{A}
using

{\bm{\tau}}_{A}=-\frac{1}{b_{A}^{*}}{\bm{\xi}}_{A},

(B11)

where the constant
b_{A}
has yet to be determined. Notice because

\langle{\bm{\chi}}_{A}|{\bm{\zeta}}_{B}\rangle=\frac{1}{b_{A}}\big[\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{A}|{\rm i}\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}_{B}\rangle-{\rm i}(\lambda_{A}+\lambda_{B})\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{A}|{\bm{\xi}}_{B}\rangle\big],

(B12)

{\bm{\chi}}_{A}
is orthogonal to
{\bm{\zeta}}_{B}
. We choose
b_{A}
so
{\bm{\chi}}_{A}
is orthonormal to
{\bm{\zeta}}_{B}
(
\langle{\bm{\chi}}_{A}|{\bm{\zeta}}_{B}\rangle=\delta_{AB}
):

b_{A}=\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{A}|{\rm i}\mathcal{C}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}_{A}\rangle+2\lambda_{A}\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{A}|{\bm{\xi}}_{A}\rangle.

(B13)

Inserting equation (
B7
) into (
B5
), and using the orthonormality of
{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}
with respect to
{\bm{\chi}}_{A}
, we obtain equation (
7
).

Our calculations include weak dissipative forces
\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
in
\mathcal{R}
, which we assume are much smaller in magnitude than
\mathcal{R}^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{R}^{(1)}
. To calculate the effects of the dissipative forces, we decompose
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}^{(0)}+\mathcal{M}^{(1)}
, where

\mathcal{M}^{(0)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}&1\\
-\mathcal{R}^{(0)}+\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{C}^{2}&-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{C}\end{array}\right],\hskip 14.22636pt\mathcal{M}^{(1)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
-\mathcal{R}^{(1)}&0\end{array}\right].

(B14)

Expanding
{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}={\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(0)}+{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(1)}+\dots
,
\lambda_{A}=\lambda_{A}^{(0)}+\lambda_{A}^{(1)}+\dots
, and matching terms of like order, we find at order (1)

(\mathcal{M}^{(0)}+{\rm i}\lambda_{A}^{(0)}){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(1)}=-(\mathcal{M}^{(1)}+{\rm i}\lambda_{A}^{(1)}){\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(0)}.

(B15)

Because both
{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(1)}
and

are undetermined in equation (
B15
), we may choose a solution

which satisfies

\langle{\bm{\chi}}_{A}^{(0)}|\mathcal{M}^{(0)}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}^{(1)}\rangle+{\rm i}\lambda^{(0)}\langle{\bm{\chi}}_{A}^{(0)}|{\bm{\zeta}}^{(1)}\rangle=0.

(B16)

Hence

is given by

\lambda_{A}^{(1)}=-{\rm i}\langle{\bm{\chi}}_{A}^{(0)}|\mathcal{M}^{(1)}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\zeta}}_{A}^{(0)}\rangle=\frac{1}{b_{A}}\langle{\bm{\xi}}_{A}^{(0)}|\mathcal{R}^{(1)}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}_{A}^{(0)}\rangle.

Dropping the zero superscript for

.

Appendix C
Damping rates for gravity Modes

In this appendix, we estimate the damping rates
\gamma
(eq. [
4
]) for g-mode oscillations due to three different drag forces
{{\bm{f}}}
: radiative diffusion in the star’s radiative regions, turbulent diffusivity in the star’s convective region, and wave leakage from the upper atmosphere.
The damping rates we are after are the time-averaged rates of energy dissipation

\gamma\equiv-{{\langle{\dot{E}}\rangle}\over E}=-{{\omega}\over{2\pi}}{1\over E}\oint\text{d}t{{\text{d}E}\over{\text{d}t}}\,,

(C1)

where our convention is that positive rate for damping.
In the following, we present both formal integrals for these rates, as well as their order-of-magnitude estimates.
This latter exercise is necessary as it acts both as a sanity check for our numerical results (presented in Fig.
3
), and it guides our understanding on how these rates depends on global properties (e.g., stellar luminosity, mass, mode frequency, etc). Moreover, we take the solar model as a reference when estimating the rates: this assumes a surface convection zone of depth
z_{\rm cvz}
and mass
M_{\rm cvz}
. We also discuss cases where a surface convection zone is absent.

C.1
Radiative Diffusion Damping

The radiative diffusion arises when heat is lost from the pulsational motion via photo diffusion. This could occur anywhere inside the star, even in the convective region, as long as radiative diffusion is carrying some flux. In the following, we focus on the radiative zone and follow
Goldreich & Wu 1999
for much of the discussions. We will frequently use the fact that radiative diffusion damps g-modes primarily in the radiative zone where
k_{r}r\gg 1
.

Radiative diffusion causes departure from adiabaticity and removes mode energy at a rate of
T\text{d}s/\text{d}t
, where
s
is entropy and is related to the flux variation by the equation of energy conservation

T{{\text{d}\Delta s}\over{\text{d}t}}=-{1\over\rho}{\text{d}\over{\text{d}r}}\Delta F_{\rm rad}\simeq-{{F_{\rm rad}}\over{\rho}}{\text{d}\over{\text{d}r}}\left({{\Delta F_{\rm rad}}\over{F_{\rm rad}}}\right)\,,

(C2)

where the flux variation can be related to other pulsational quantities by the equation of radiative diffusion and is
(
Goldreich & Wu 1999
, see)

{{\Delta F_{\rm rad}}\over{F_{\rm rad}}}=-(1+\kappa_{\rho}){{\Delta\rho}\over\rho}+(4-\kappa_{T}){{\Delta T}\over T}-{{\text{d}\xi_{r}}\over{\text{d}r}}+\left({{\text{d}\ln T}\over{\text{d}r}}\right)^{-1}{\text{d}\over{\text{d}r}}\left({{\Delta T}\over{T}}\right)\simeq\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln T}{\text{d}r}\right)^{-1}\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}r}\left(\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right)-\frac{\text{d}\xi_{r}}{\text{d}r}\,.

(C3)

Here
\kappa_{\rho}=\text{d}\ln\kappa/\text{d}\ln\rho
and
\kappa_{T}=\text{d}\ln\kappa/\text{d}\ln T
are the opacity dependence on density and temperature, neglected from here on out. Further using
\Delta\rho/\rho=-{\bm{\nabla}}{\bm{\cdot}}{\bm{\xi}}\simeq-\xi_{r}/H
everywhere for g-modes (where
H=c_{\rm s}^{2}/g
is the scaleheight, valid when
\omega^{2}\ll L_{\ell}^{2}
),

\frac{\text{d}\ln T}{\text{d}r}=-\frac{\text{d}\ln T}{\text{d}\ln\rho}\frac{g}{c_{\rm e}^{2}},

(C4)

where
c_{\rm e}^{2}=\text{d}p/\text{d}\rho
is the equilibrium sound speed, and the quasi-adiabatic approximation (
\Delta\rho/\rho=[\text{d}\ln\rho/\text{d}\ln T]_{S}[\Delta T/T]
), the flux perturbation becomes

\frac{\Delta F_{\rm rad}}{F_{\rm rad}}\simeq{\rm i}k_{r}\left[\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln\rho}{\text{d}\ln T}\right)_{S}\frac{c_{\rm s}^{2}}{g}-\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln\rho}{\text{d}\ln T}\right)\frac{c_{\rm e}^{2}}{g}\right]\frac{\Delta T}{T}.

(C5)

In addition, we make the crude approximation

\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln\rho}{\text{d}\ln T}\right)_{S}\frac{c_{\rm s}^{2}}{g}-\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln\rho}{\text{d}\ln T}\right)\frac{c_{\rm e}^{2}}{g}\approx\left(\frac{\text{d}\ln\rho}{\text{d}\ln T}\right)_{S}\frac{c_{\rm s}^{2}}{g}.

(C6)

Although this approximation will overestimate the magnitude of
\gamma_{\rm diff}
, it simplifies its numerical calculation, and still gives damping rates within a factor of
\sim 2-3
of more detailed calculations
(e.g.
Fuller 2017
, Fig. 2)
. Inserting equation (
C2
) into (
C1
), we obtain

\gamma_{\rm diff}=\frac{\omega}{2\pi\epsilon}\oint\text{d}t\int_{\rm rad}\left(\frac{\Delta T}{T}\right)\frac{\text{d}\Delta F_{\rm rad}}{\text{d}r}r^{2}\text{d}r\simeq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\rm rad}F_{\rm rad}(\Gamma_{3}-1)Hk_{r}^{2}\left|\frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\right|^{2}r^{2}\text{d}r,

(C7)

where the adiabatic exponent
\Gamma_{3}-1=(\text{d}\ln T/\text{d}\ln\rho)_{S}
. Equation (
C7
) is the expression we use to numerically calculate
\gamma_{\rm diff}
for our stellar models.

To estimate the value of
\gamma_{\rm diff}
analytically, we use
|\Delta\rho/\rho|\simeq|\xi_{r}/H|\simeq|(k_{\perp}/k_{r})(\xi_{\perp}/H)|
and
r^{2}F_{\rm rad}\sim L_{\star}
in the outer regions of the star’s radiative core (
r\gtrsim 0.2\,{\rm R}_{\odot}
for PMS and MS models). Then
\gamma_{\rm diff}
becomes

\gamma_{\rm diff}\sim\frac{L_{\star}}{\epsilon}\int_{\rm rad}\frac{k_{\perp}^{2}\xi_{\perp}^{2}}{H}\text{d}r.

(C8)

Because
r^{2}F_{\rm rad}\approx{\rm constant}
only applies to the outer regions of the radiative core, and
r^{2}F_{\rm rad}\to 0
as the core’s center is approached, the integrand remains finite even as
k_{\perp}^{2}\xi_{\perp}^{2}/H
diverges as
r\to 0
. To evaluate this integral, we use our estimate for the WKB envelope of
|\xi_{\perp}|^{2}
in the star’s radiative zone (eq. [
A13
]),
k_{r}\simeq(N/\omega)k_{\perp}\sim(N/\omega)(\ell/r)
, and the fact every radial node of
\xi_{\perp}
ha s a width
\Delta r\sim k_{r}^{-1}\sim\text{d}r
to obtain

\gamma_{\rm diff}\sim\frac{L_{\star}}{\epsilon}\left[n\left(\frac{r\omega}{\ell N}\right)\frac{1}{H}\left(\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\omega n\ell}\frac{N}{\rho r^{3}}\right)\right]_{\rm rad}\sim\frac{L_{\star}}{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{2}\omega^{2}}\Lambda_{\rm core},

(C9)

where the quantity

\Lambda_{\rm core}=\left(\frac{M_{\star}R_{\star}^{2}}{\rho Hr^{4}}\right)_{r=r_{\rm c}}

(C10)

measures the mass concentration in the star’s radiative core at the characteristic radius
r_{\rm c}
, and has values of order
\Lambda_{\rm core}\sim 10^{2}-10^{3}
(
10^{3}-10^{4}
) during the PMS (MS), and increases as the star evolves and becomes more centrally concentrated. The value of
\Lambda_{\rm core}
diverges near the center, but does not overwhelm the contribution to the integral for
\gamma_{\rm diff}
is because
r^{2}F_{\rm rad}
vanishes there.

Evaluating this for a 10 day period g-mode in the sun, we obtain
\gamma_{\rm rad}\sim 2\times 10^{-3}\ {\rm day}^{-1}\sim 2\times 10^{-8}\ {\rm s}^{-1}
. This agrees with the numerical calculation in Table 1 of
Terquem et al. 1998
and the illustrative estimate in equation (21) of
Goodman & Dickson 1998
.

We ignore radiative diffusion from inside the convection zone. This is justified because radiation carries only a tiny fraction of the total flux there. Our estimated damping rate (eq. [
C9
]) does not apply to stars without a surface convection zone, since most of the diffusion occurs in the deep interior.

C.2
Turbulent Damping

Our treatment of the force acting on g-modes from turbulent eddies in the star’s convective zone is more heuristic. We approximate the viscous force (which acts on the gradient of perturbed velocity) as

{\bm{f}}_{\rm turb}=-\frac{{\rm i}\omega}{r^{2}\rho}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\rho\nu_{\rm turb}\frac{\partial{\bm{\xi}}}{\partial r}\right)\,,

(C11)

where
\nu_{\rm turb}
is the effective turbulent viscosity. To account for the two extremes, when the turn-over time is much shorter or much longer than the mode period, we adopt the following prescription for
\nu_{\rm turb}

(
Goodman & Oh 1997
)
,

\nu_{\rm turb}=\frac{H_{\rm p}v_{\rm cv}}{1+(\omega t_{\rm cv}/2\pi)^{s}}.

(C12)

Here
H_{\rm p}=(\text{d}\ln p/\text{d}r)^{-1}\sim z
is the local pressure scale-height, and
z
the depth from the surface.
v_{\rm cv}
is the convective velocity, and
t_{\rm cv}=H_{\rm p}/v_{\rm cv}
is the eddy turnover time.
In the limit of slow convection,
Goldreich & Nicholson 1977
have
argued that a Kolmogorov turbulent spectrum gives rise to
s=2
. In particular, for eddies of a range of sizes, the most relevant ones for turbulent viscosity are those
that have turn-over time comparable to the tidal period,
t_{\lambda}=\lambda/v_{\lambda}
(
t_{\lambda}\omega\sim 2\pi
), where
\lambda
and
v_{\lambda}
are their length-scales and velocities, respectively. This, also advocated later by
Goodman & Oh 1997
, differs from the prescription by
Zahn 1966
, where he set
s=1
.
Direct numerical simulations have yet to conclusively settle this issue
(
Penev et al. 2007
;
Ogilvie & Lesur 2012
;
Duguid et al. 2020
;
Vidal & Barker 2020
, e.g.)
.

We insert this into equation (
4
), integrate by parts, and obtain an expression for the
turbulent damping rate,

\gamma_{{\rm turb}}=-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\epsilon}\int_{\rm cv}\nu_{\rm turb}\left|\frac{\partial{\bm{\xi}}}{\partial r}\right|^{2}\rho r^{2}\text{d}r\,,

(C13)

where
\epsilon
is the mode energy,
\epsilon=2\omega^{2}\langle{\bm{\xi}}|{\bm{\xi}}\rangle=\omega^{2}
,
for our adopted normalization.

To evaluate this integral at the order-of-magnitude level, we first estimate the magnitude of
\xi_{r}
and
\xi_{\perp}
in the convective zone. Since
N^{2}\approx 0
,
H\ll r
, and
\omega^{2}\ll L_{\ell}^{2}
, the ODEs for
\xi_{r}
and
\xi_{\perp}
are

\frac{\text{d}\xi_{r}}{\text{d}r}\simeq\frac{\xi_{r}}{H}+k_{\perp}^{2}r\xi_{\perp},\hskip 14.22636pt\frac{\text{d}\xi_{\perp}}{\text{d}r}\simeq\frac{\xi_{r}}{r}-\frac{\xi_{\perp}}{r}.

(C14)

Because
H\sim z=R_{\star}-r\ll r
in the convective zone, equation (
C14
) has the approximate solution

\xi_{r}\approx-\frac{1}{2}k_{\perp}^{2}rz\xi_{\perp}+\frac{A}{z^{2}},

(C15)

where the constant
A
is determined by regularity conditions. Regularity as
z\to 0
requires
A=0
, so an order-of-magnitude estimate for
\xi_{r}
in the convective zone is

\xi_{r}\sim-k_{\perp}^{2}rH\xi_{\perp}.

(C16)

Notice equations (
C14
) and (
C16
) imply
|\xi_{r}|\lesssim|\xi_{\perp}|
but
|\text{d}\xi_{r}/\text{d}r|\gtrsim|\text{d}\xi_{\perp}/\text{d}r|
. Hence in the convective zone,
|\partial{\bm{\xi}}/\partial r|\sim k_{\perp}^{2}r\xi_{\perp}
.

In the deeper part of the convection zone,
\omega t_{\rm cv}\gtrsim 2\pi
, and

\rho\nu_{\rm turb}\sim{{\rho v_{\rm cv}^{3}}\over{\omega^{2}z}}\sim{{F_{\rm cv}}\over{\omega^{2}z}}\,,

(C17)

where
F_{\rm cv}
is the convective flux and is of order the total flux,
F_{\rm cv}=L_{\star}/4\pi R^{2}
.
In contrast, near the surface,
\omega t_{\rm cv}\ll 1
, and

\rho\nu_{\rm turb}\sim\rho v_{\rm cv}z\sim\rho^{2/3}zF_{\rm cv}^{1/3}\,.

(C18)

As a result, the dominant contribution to viscous damping arises where
\omega t_{\rm cv}\sim 1
,
(
Wu & Lithwick 2019
, see also)
, at the depth

z_{\nu}\sim\frac{v_{\rm cv}}{\omega}\sim\frac{1}{\omega}\left(\frac{F_{\rm cv}}{\rho_{\rm cv}}\right)^{1/3},

(C19)

where
\rho_{\rm cv}\sim M_{\rm cvz}/(R_{\star}^{2}z_{\rm cvz})
. Hence, the turbulent damping rate is of order

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm turb}

\displaystyle\sim-\frac{\omega^{2}}{\epsilon}\int_{\rm cvz}\left(\frac{F_{\rm cv}}{\omega^{2}z_{\nu}}\right)(k_{\perp}^{2}r\xi_{\perp})^{2}r^{2}\text{d}r\sim\bar{\gamma}_{\rm turb}\frac{\ell^{2}\omega}{n}\left(\frac{z_{\rm cvz}^{2/3}L_{\star}^{2/3}}{\omega^{2}R_{\star}^{2}M_{\rm cvz}^{2/3}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\bar{\gamma}_{\rm turb}\ \frac{\omega}{n}\times\frac{1}{(\tau_{\rm th}\omega_{\rm dyn})^{2/3}}\times\left(\frac{z_{\rm cvz}}{z_{1}}\right)\times\left(\frac{z_{\rm cvz}}{R_{\star}}\right)^{1/3},

(C20)

where
\tau_{\rm th}\sim M_{\rm cvz}z_{\rm cvz}g/L_{\star}
is the thermal time at the base of the convection zone, and
\bar{\gamma}_{\rm turb}
is an order-unity coefficient used to reduce the magnitude of
\gamma_{\rm turb}
(since we evaluated
\rho\nu_{\rm turb}
at it’s maximum value). We find the reduction in the turbulent damping estimate (
C20
) of
\bar{\gamma}_{\rm turb}\sim 1/3
better matches the more detailed calculations using equation (
C13
).

For the Solar-model,
\gamma_{\rm turb}\sim 6\times 10^{-4}(\omega/n)\sim 4\times 10^{-7}\,{\rm day}^{-1}\sim 5\times 10^{-12}\,{\rm s}^{-1}
for a
\ell=2
mode at
10
day period, assuming
n\sim 1000
. Pre-main-sequence stars, with their deeper convection zones (and slower convection), experience stronger damping. In contrast, stars with shallow convection zones can’t remove mode energy as efficiently. Lastly, given the dispersion relation for g-modes (
\omega\propto l/n
), the damping rate is independent of mode radial order,
\gamma_{\rm turb}\propto\ell
.

Luan et al. 2018
got a different scaling with mode number (
\gamma_{\rm diff}\propto n
, see their eq. [A13]), because the turbulent dissipation rate
\rho\nu_{\rm turb}
was evaluated at the base of the convection zone (
z_{\rm cvz}
), rather than where turbulence peaked within the convection zone (
z_{\nu}
). The numerical calculations in this work support our estimate for
\gamma_{\rm turb}
.

C.3
Wave Leakage

Even for stars with a surface convection zone, the atmosphere above the photosphere is stratified. This creates a g-mode cavity through which the internal g-modes that are trapped below the surface convection zone can tunnel through and escape. We proceed to estimate the associated wave damping rate. Some of these calculations were also detailed in
Wu & Lithwick 2019
in the context of f-modes.

In this region,
the stratification
N\sim\sqrt{g/H}
, where

H
is the photospheric scale height, and the sound speed
c_{s}\sim\sqrt{gH}\sim NH
. The dispersion relation is

k_{z}={1\over 2}{{\Gamma_{1}}\over H}\pm{1\over 2}\sqrt{\left({{\Gamma_{1}}\over{H}}\right)^{2}-4\left[k_{\perp}^{2}\left({{N^{2}}\over{\omega^{2}}}-1\right)+{{\omega^{2}}\over{c_{s}^{2}}}\right]}\,.

(C21)

So propagative waves have either frequencies higher than the acoustic cut-off frequency
\omega_{\rm ac}={\Gamma_{1}c_{s}}/(2H)
, or frequencies lower than

\omega_{\rm ac,2}=\omega_{\rm ac}{H\over R_{\star}}=\Gamma_{1}{{c_{s}}\over{2R_{\star}}}\,,

(C22)

Low frequency gravity-waves with
\omega<\omega_{\rm ac,2}\sim 2\pi/(5\,{\rm days})
(evaluated with solar values,
H=20
km and
T=5800
K) can leak out, with a simplified dispersion relation of the form

k_{z}=k_{\perp}{N\over\omega}\approx{{\ell}\over{H}}\left({{\omega_{\rm ac,2}\over{\omega}}}\right)\,.

(C23)

The rate of energy leakage is determined by the flux of kinetic energy (dominated by the horizontal velocity) at the photosphere

{\dot{E}}_{\rm leak}=R_{\star}^{2}\times\left[\rho\omega^{2}\ell(\ell+1)\left|{\xi_{\perp}}\right|^{2}\times v_{\rm group,z}\right]_{\rm ph}\,,

(C24)

where
V_{\rm group,z}\approx\omega/k_{z}
is the wave’s vertical group velocity. This leads us to an estimate for the damping rate

\gamma_{\rm leak}\equiv{{{\dot{E}}_{\rm leak}}\over{E}}=R_{\star}^{2}\rho_{\rm ph}\left|{\tilde{\xi}}_{\perp}\right|^{2}{\omega\over{k_{z}}}\approx{\omega\over{n\ell}}\times\left({{M_{\rm ph}}\over{M_{\rm cvz}}}\right)\times\left({\omega\over{\omega_{\rm ac,2}}}\right)\,,

(C25)

where the photospheric mass
M_{\rm ph}\approx R_{\star}^{2}\rho_{\rm ph}H
, and we have used equation (
C23
) above, and equation (
A11
) for the normalized horizontal displacement, assuming that there is little gradient in the horizontal displacement across the convection zone.

For the Solar model,
M_{\rm ph}/M_{\rm cvz}\sim 10^{-9}
, and
\gamma_{\rm leak}\sim 10^{-9}(\omega/n\ell)
for a mode near
\omega_{\rm ac,2}
, and is much below the convective damping estimated above. However, leaking becomes increasingly more important as the convection zone thins.
In the limit of a vanishing convection zone, g-modes with
\omega<\omega_{ac,2}
are no longer trapped inside the star and are instead running waves with
\gamma_{\rm leak}\sim\omega/n
.

Appendix D
Adiabadic Evolution of The Amplitude Equation

This appendix derives the regime of validity for the mode amplitude
c(t)
to be given by equation (
21
). We start with the evolution equation for
c
excited by the tidal component
\{k,\ell,m\}
:

\dot{c}+({\rm i}\omega+\gamma)c={\rm i}\omega U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega_{km}t}.

(D1)

Equation (
D1
) has the general solution
(
Fuller & Lai 2012a
;
Vick et al. 2019
, e.g.)

c(t)=c(0){\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}({\rm i}\omega+\gamma)\text{d}t^{\prime}}+{\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{t}({\rm i}\omega+\gamma)\text{d}t^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{t}{\rm i}\omega U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\left[{\rm i}(\omega-\omega_{km})+\gamma\right]\text{d}t^{\prime\prime}}\text{d}t^{\prime}

(D2)

The first and second terms in equation (
D2
) are homogeneous and inhomogeneous solutions to equation (
D1
), respectively. To show how equation (
D2
) reduces to (
21
), we first notice for the systems of interest, their damping timescales are much shorter than their ages (
\gamma^{-1}\ll t
), which implies the homogeneous solution quickly damps by age
t
to give

c(t)\simeq\int_{0}^{t}{\rm i}\omega U_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}({\rm i}\omega+\gamma)\text{d}t^{\prime\prime}}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}\omega_{km}\text{d}t^{\prime\prime}}\text{d}t^{\prime}.

(D3)

We further note that binaries typically have mode damping times much shorter than mode evolution times (
\gamma^{-1}\ll t_{\rm evol}
), which implies all expressions outside the damped exponential

\exp\left(-\int_{t^{\prime}}^{t}\gamma\text{d}t^{\prime\prime}\right)\simeq\exp\left[-\gamma(t)(t-t^{\prime})\right]

(D4)

contribute negligibly to the integral from
0
to
t
in (
D3
) when
t^{\prime}\ll t
. Also assuming
\omega,\omega_{km}\gg t_{\rm evol}^{-1}
, we obtain equation (
21
). The key condition for solution (
21
) to hold is the mode damping rate be sufficiently high (
\gamma\gg t^{-1},t_{\rm evol}^{-1}
).

Burkart et al. 2014
derived a different condition for adiabadicity:

\gamma^{2}\gtrsim\omega/t_{\rm evol}.

(D5)

We briefly repeat the derivation of equation (
D5
). When
|c|\ll 1
,
\omega=\omega_{km}
,
\omega\gg t_{\rm evol}^{-1}
, and crucially
\gamma^{-1}\gg t
(where
t
is the age of the star), equation (
D2
) reduces to

c(t)\simeq{\rm i}\omega tU_{k\ell m}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}\omega t}.

(D6)

The time derivative of equation (
D6
) is assumed to be the maximum evolution rate a mode near resonance could evolve as:

\left.\frac{\text{d}|c|}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm max}=\omega U_{k\ell m}.

(D7)

When equation (
D7
) is less than

\left.\frac{\text{d}|c|}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm ev}\sim\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\omega-\omega_{km})^{2}-\gamma^{2}}\frac{U_{k\ell m}}{t_{\rm evol}},

(D8)

which is roughly the time derivative of the in-homogeneous mode amplitude (
21
), the locked mode can no longer keep up with the systems evolution, and equation (
21
) no longer holds. However, a crucial step in deriving solution (
D6
) for
c(t)
at resonance is the assumption
\gamma^{-1}\gg t
. Since most astrophysical systems satisfy
\gamma^{-1}\ll t
, equation (
D5
) is usually an invalid condition for adiabadicity.

Physically, the value of
\text{d}|c|/\text{d}t|_{\rm max}
given by equation (
D7
) is roughly the rate a mode out of equilibrium “catches up” to its equilibrium amplitude (
21
) when mode damping
\gamma
is negligible. However, if the mode damping rate is sufficiently high, any memory of a previous mode amplitude is erased, and remains approximately equal to its adiabatic amplitude (
21
).

Appendix E
resonance locking from sectoral Modes

This section looks at resonance locking from stellar evolution due to sectoral modes (
m\neq 0

\Omega_{\star}

III.2
, these

E.1
Stable resonance locks with evolving stellar spin

When a non-zero torque is exerted on the host star, both
\Omega

where

\Omega_{\star}/t_{\star}\simeq\text{d}\Omega_{\star}/\text{d}t|_{\rm tide}
, see eqs. [
E9

E11

39

\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm orb}}{E_{\rm orb}}=\frac{2}{3t_{\rm mode}}\left(1-\frac{m\Omega_{\star}}{k\Omega}\right)\Bigg/\left[1-\frac{m^{2}\mu_{\star}^{\prime}B}{3k^{2}\kappa_{\star}}\left(\frac{a}{R_{\star}}\right)^{2}\right].

(E2)

Because the orbital evolution at resonance is (using eq. [
29
] with
\sum c\simeq C^{\rm max}
)

\frac{\dot{E}_{{\rm orb};k\ell m}^{\rm max}}{E_{\rm orb}}=\frac{4k^{2}\epsilon{\rm Re}(U_{k\ell m})}{\mu_{\star}^{\prime}\gamma a^{2}M_{\star}}\left(1-\frac{m\Omega_{\star}}{k\Omega}\right),

(E3)

resonance locking is unstable when the denominator of equation (
E2

in the opposite direction as tidal angular momentum and energy dissipation (eq. [
E3

5
, case
t_{\rm evol}
[c]). Here

Stable resonance locks require the denominator of equation (

k_{\rm c}=\pm m\sqrt{\frac{B\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}{3\kappa_{\star}}}\left(\frac{a}{R_{\star}}\right).

E.2
Stable resonance locks with near-synchronous stellar spin

Instead of assuming
\Omega_{\star}
evolves with the locked mode as in the previous section, this section considers a situation where
\Omega_{\star}
evolves to an equilibrium state over a timescale much faster than the binary orbital evolution (since
I_{\star}\ll M_{\star}a^{2}
typically). The stellar spin
\Omega_{\star}(t)
for our model (eq. [
E5

\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}=\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm tide}+\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm wind}+\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm inert}=0.

(E5)

The first, second, and third terms in equation (
E5
) represent stellar spin up/down from the tidal torque from mode
\alpha
and potential component
\{k,\ell,m\}
, stellar winds, and stellar contraction/expansion, respectively. Equations (
39
) and (
E1
) may be shown to reduce to (when
t_{\star}\to\infty
)

\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm tide}=\frac{m\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}{3k\kappa_{\star}}\left(\frac{a}{R_{\star}}\right)^{2}\left(1-\frac{m\Omega}{k\Omega_{\star}}\right)\frac{\Omega}{t_{\rm mode}}

(E6)

We parameterize stellar wind spin-down with
(

)

\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm wind}=-\alpha_{\star}\Omega_{\star}^{3},

(E7)

where
\alpha=1.5\times 10^{-14}
for a solar-type star
(
Verbunt & Zwaan 1981
;
Barker & Ogilvie 2009
, e.g.)
. Spin up (down) from stellar contraction (expansion) is

\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm inert}=-\frac{\Omega_{\star}}{\tau_{\star}},

(E8)

where
\tau_{\star}=I_{\star}(\text{d}I_{\star}/\text{d}t)^{-1}
is the spin up/down timescale from changes in
I_{\star}
. The
\dot{\Omega}_{\star}

\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm wind}=\frac{m\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}{3k\kappa_{\star}}\left(\frac{a}{R_{\star}}\right)^{2}\frac{\Omega}{t_{\rm mode}}\Gamma_{km}^{\rm w}\bar{\Omega}_{\star}^{3},\hskip 14.22636pt\left.\frac{\text{d}\Omega_{\star}}{\text{d}t}\right|_{\rm inert}=\frac{m\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}{3k\kappa_{\star}}\left(\frac{a}{R_{\star}}\right)^{2}\frac{\Omega}{t_{\rm mode}}\Gamma_{km}^{\star}\bar{\Omega}_{\star},

(E9)

where
\bar{\Omega}_{\star}=\Omega_{\star}/\Omega

\displaystyle\Gamma_{km}^{\rm w}

\displaystyle=\frac{3k\kappa_{\star}}{m\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{a}\right)^{2}t_{\rm mode}\alpha_{\star}\Omega^{2}=0.0395\frac{k}{m}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\star}}{0.05}\right)\left(\frac{0.3}{\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{a}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{t_{\rm mode}}{10^{6}\,{\rm yrs}}\right)\left(\frac{\alpha_{\star}}{1.5\times 10^{-14}\,{\rm yrs}}\right)\left(\frac{1\,{\rm day}}{P_{\rm orb}}\right)^{2},

(E10)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{km}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{3k\kappa_{\star}}{m\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{a}\right)^{2}\frac{t_{\rm mode}}{\tau_{\star}}=0.5\frac{k}{m}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\star}}{0.05}\right)\left(\frac{0.3}{\mu_{\star}^{\prime}}\right)\left(\frac{R_{\star}}{a}\right)^{2}\frac{t_{\rm mode}}{\tau_{\star}}.

(E11)

Since

and

Equations (

1-\frac{m}{k}\bar{\Omega}_{\star}-\Gamma_{km}^{\rm w}\bar{\Omega}_{\star}^{3}-\Gamma_{km}^{\star}\bar{\Omega}_{\star}=0.

(E12)

The general analytic solution to the cubic equation (

\Delta\bar{\Omega}_{km}\equiv 1-\frac{m}{k}\bar{\Omega}_{\star},

(E13)

and assuming

\Delta\bar{\Omega}_{km}\simeq\frac{k}{m}\left(\Gamma_{km}^{\star}+\frac{k^{2}}{m^{2}}\Gamma_{km}^{\rm w}\right).

(E14)

Thus the semi-major axis evolution may be calculated using

\Omega(a)=\frac{\omega}{k\Delta\bar{\Omega}_{km}}\simeq\frac{m\omega}{k}\left(\Gamma_{km}^{\star}+\frac{k^{2}}{m^{2}}\Gamma_{km}^{\rm w}\right)^{-1}.

(E15)

When

F.2
Mode amplitude at nonlinear Breaking And Effective Dissipation

Here, we estimate the amplitude of a g-mode when wave-breaking becomes important (
C=C_{\rm sat}
). Scaling from equation (
F5
), an estimate for
C=C^{\rm max}
,
we find a threshold amplitude
(
|k_{r}\xi_{r}(r=r_{m})|\approx 10
) to be

\frac{|C^{\rm sat}|}{|C^{\rm max}|}\sim 4.2\times 10^{-5}\,X_{k20}^{-1}(e)k^{-3/2}\left(\frac{P_{\rm orb}}{1\,{\rm day}}\right)^{7/2}\,.

(F7)

We speculate that the following dynamical picture determines the effective dissipation. Without nonlinear breaking, the g-mode is pumped by tidal forcing to reach its maximum amplitude (
C^{\rm max}
) after a time
\Delta t\sim\gamma_{\rm diff}^{-1}
. With its damping dominated by linear dissipation, the rate of energy dissipation is
{\dot{E}}^{\rm max}_{\rm orb}=\epsilon|C^{\rm max}|^{2}/\Delta t
.

However, if non-linearity sets in earlier at a smaller amplitude
C^{\rm sat}
, the effective damping is reduced. The wave reaches this amplitude within a shorter time, of order
\Delta t^{\prime}\sim\gamma_{\rm diff}^{-1}|C^{\rm sat}|/|C^{\rm max}|
.
And within a few radial propagation times (
t_{\rm group}\sim R_{\star}[\partial\omega/\partial k_{r}]^{-1}\sim n/\omega\ll\Delta t^{\prime}
), most wave flux carried to the centre is converted to heat by overturning. This is consistent with results from
Barker & Ogilvie 2010
;
Su et al. 2020
where they found
that wave breaking reduces the amplitude of the outgoing wave to be a fraction of the ingoing wave (of order
20\%-50\%
).
The effective rate of energy loss is then
\dot{E}_{\rm orb}^{\rm sat}\sim\epsilon|C^{\rm sat}|^{2}/\Delta t^{\prime}
and is reduced from the linear form as

\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm orb}^{\rm sat}}{\dot{E}_{\rm orb}^{\rm max}}\approx\frac{|C^{\rm sat}|}{|C^{\rm max}|}\sim 4.2\times 10^{-5}\,X_{k20}^{-1}(e)k^{-3/2}\left(\frac{P_{\rm orb}}{1\,{\rm day}}\right)^{7/2}.

(F8)

Following the procedure that leads to equation (
43
),
we estimate a new binary period out to which (saturated) resonance locking can be effective in circularizing the binary. Setting again

, but now increasing

in equation (
32

P_{\rm orb}\leq P_{\rm sat}\sim 3.6\,{\rm days}\,\left({e\over{0.4}}\right)^{6/41}\,.

(F9)

Compared to the criterion for linear resonance locking to be effective (eq. [
43
