Analysis of Diagnostics (Part I): Prevalence, Uncertainty Quantification, & Machine Learning
Paul N. Patrone, Raquel A. Binder, Catherine S. Forconi, Ann M. Moormann, Anthony J. Kearsley

Abstract
Diagnostic testing provides a unique setting for studying and developing tools in classification theory. In such contexts, the concept of prevalence , i.e. the number of individuals with a given condition, is fundamental, both as an inherent quantity of interest and as a parameter that controls classification accuracy. This manuscript is the first in a two-part series that studies deeper connections between classification theory and prevalence, showing how the latter establishes a more complete theory of uncertainty quantification (UQ) for certain types of machine learning (ML). We motivate this analysis via a lemma demonstrating that general classifiers minimizing a prevalence-weighted error contain the same probabilistic information as Bayes-optimal classifiers, which depend on conditional probability densities. This leads us to study relative probability level-sets B ⋆ ​ ( q ) B^{\star}(q) , which are reinterpreted as both classification boundaries and useful tools for quantifying uncertainty in class labels. To realize this in practice, we also propose a numerical, homotopy algorithm that estimates the B ⋆ ​ ( q ) B^{\star}(q) by minimizing a prevalence-weighted empirical error. The successes and shortcomings of this method motivate us to revisit properties of the level sets, and we deduce the corresponding classifiers obey a useful monotonicity property that stabilizes the numerics and points to important extensions to UQ of ML. Throughout, we validate our methods in the context of synthetic data and a research-use-only SARS-CoV-2 enzyme-linked immunosorbent (ELISA) assay.

Appeal to the Reader

Data analysis for diagnostic testing is not a task that one necessarily associates with new applied mathematics. Perusing the literature, one could be forgiven for believing that most of the hard problems have been reduced to identifying a “cutoff value” for classifying test results and estimating rates of false-positives and false-negatives. Yet in the past few years, several of us have shown that many tasks in diagnostics are anchored in surprising ways at the intersection of measure theory, metrology, and optimization
(
43
;
42
)
. This perspective resolved several misconceptions and led to seemingly paradoxical (but true) results. For example, classifiers need not, and at times should not, be used to estimate the number of individuals in a given class, i.e. the
prevalence.

(
41
)
.

Motivated by such findings, this manuscript is the first in a series that further explores the interplay between probability and classification, using diagnostics to study their deeper relationship to uncertainty quantification (UQ). When we first started on this path, our goal was more simply to minimize the assumptions needed to solve practical classification problems in diagnostic settings. But this line of inquiry took us into unexpected territory, and we soon realized that the concept of prevalence, which is often ignored in classification and machine learning (ML), is in fact central to the whole enterprise. Thus, in some sense, this work is really about the many interpretations of prevalence and how they connect certain topics of applied mathematics. Given the range of issues that emerged, however, we felt that a single manuscript was not sufficient to cover them all. The present manuscript therefore focuses on a fundamental connection between prevalence, optimization, UQ, and supervised ML techniques. The second manuscript builds on this foundation to show how prevalence connects classification and unsupervised learning to aspects of linear algebra.

Because many of these concepts arise naturally in epidemiology and diagnostics, we believe that such settings are well-suited for our exposition. As a result, this manuscript is unabashedly interdisciplinary. Typical readers should have a broad interest in the aforementioned fields of applied mathematics, with a willingness to ground the underlying ideas in the basics of
serological assays (or blood-based diagnostic tests)
. The latter contain elements common to many classification and ML problems. We also feel that grounding the analysis in examples makes it easier to understand. In this vein, we employ a hybrid writing style, staying within the confines of mathematics but adopting diagnostics-inspired terminology and notation.

A key challenge in this work is to distinguish the interpretation of prevalence as a property of a population from its role as a parameter used in optimization. We only consider settings for which it is meaningful to assume that a test sample has some
a priori
probability
q

\boldsymbol{\rm r}
is optimally assigned a chosen class?” The latter perspective does not consider a specific population per se, but rather a family of them parameterized by
q

(
58

, and our main results reveal a third way of understanding prevalence in terms of classification accuracy.

The practical motivation for our line of inquiry arises from the way in which empirical distributions overcome issues of quantifying and controlling
model-form
errors; see Refs.
(
1
;
16
;
49
)
for a general overview of the latter. In typical assay design settings, one may only have access to
\mathcal{O}(100)
or fewer training samples with which to construct the conditional PDFs. This limits the usefulness of spectral methods, for example, which are unbiased but have a slow, mean-squared rate of convergence
(
29
;
44
;
49
;
55
;
56
)

(
15
;
34
;
48
;
49
)
, but in practice, their uncertainty can increase dramatically with dimension (e.g. number of antigens). The empirical distribution is thus a desirable object with which to work because its samples are generated from the underlying true (but unknown) distribution, which reduces the need for subjective modeling. Ultimately, our hope is that the resulting uncertainties are tied directly to the amount of sampling and therefore remain controllable.

This hope, however, also points to the key limitations of our numerical methods. One must make some choices to avoid trivially over-fitting the classification boundaries. In the examples herein, we assume that these boundaries are well described by low-order polynomials, since this facilitates optimization, works well with many biological datasets, and avoids introducing high-frequency structure. This choice is also more general and flexible than postulating parameterized forms of the conditional PDFs. However, our approach still assumes structure of the underlying data, and it is not clear when and to what extent the analysis is unbiased and/or converges. We numerically address these questions in the context of specific examples, although a deeper treatment remains an open problem.

Our analyis is also restricted to a binary setting. We make this choice for two reasons. First, the binary classification problem is sufficiently rich that we feel that it warrants a study of its own. Second, we anticipate that multiclass problems can be addressed by extension of the ideas developed herein. Thus, while we point to generalizations of our analysis to multiclass settings, we leave an in-depth study of such issues for future work.

2.4
A Motivating Lemma and Two Postulates

Equations (
3
) and (
6
) imply that the conditional PDFs are central to interpreting test data, which begs the question: how does one construct
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
? In practice, this requires

and

can be directly constructed via modeling, e.g. by fitting to parameterized distributions. While this task is often tractable when

for

or

is also convex. Moreover, in typical assays,

often has zero measure with respect to

for any

and

is an alternative to estimating

and

To motivate how we might accomplish this, recognize that the challenge of using Lemma
13
arises from needing to construct
two

(m+1)
-dimensional “manifolds,” i.e.
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. It would be convenient if we could use fewer and lower-dimensional objects. In fact, we often can. In practical diagnostic settings, is reasonable to assume that the boundary set
B^{\star}
is a
(m-1)
-dimesional manifold, and we only need one such boundary to partition
\Gamma
. Thus, we need to identify an isomorphism
U\simeq\{B,\mathcal{C}\}
, where
B
is a point-set boundary separating
D_{p}
and
D_{n}
, and
\mathcal{C}
is an auxiliary convention needed to specify which “side” of
B
corresponds to
D_{p}
(for example). As the next section clarifies, it is extremely convenient to represent
B
in terms of an analytical function
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
, since we may then define
\mathcal{C}
via inequalities of the form
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
. In other words, we propose to study the chain of isomorphisms

\displaystyle U=\{D_{p},D_{n}\}\simeq\{B,\mathcal{C}\}\simeq\{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}),>\},

(9)

where we could interpret
\mathcal{C}\simeq\,>
to mean that
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
defines
D_{p}
. In fact, given Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
), it is easy to establish that (
9
) exists.

Property 1
(Weak Level-Set Property).

For every
q\in(0,1)
, the set
B^{\star}(q)=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\}
is either the empty set or can be expressed as
B^{\star}(q)=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0\}
for some
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
. Moreover,
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(q)
can be defined so that
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)>0\iff\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\star}/B^{\star}(q)
and
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)<0\iff\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{n}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)
. We refer to
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
as a
boundary function
or
level-set function
.

The Weak Level-Set (WLS) Property is a trivial consequence of the structure of
B^{\star}(q)
. When
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
are known,
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
can always be constructed by inspection. This work, however, focuses on situations for which we wish to construct
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
without access to the conditional PDFs, in which case it is important to know
a priori
that the boundary function exists. From a modeling standpoint, it is also useful to introduce restrictions on the WLS property.

Property 2
(Strong Level-Set).

Let
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. If in addition to the WLS property, the locus of points solving
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0
defines an
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold, then we say that the optimal partition
U^{\star}
has the
Strong Level-Set (SLS) property
.

One possible complication in working directly with
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
is a loss of uncertainty in the class labels. Given
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, Lemma
13
guarantees an optimal way of classifying data, and for any partition
U
,
Z(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q,U)
quantifies point-wise accuracy. Yet how can we reconstruct
Z(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q,U)
given only a classification boundary, or more generally guarantee that we have not lost information about
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
? The following lemma speaks to this issue and is central to later sections.

Lemma 16
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be given by Eq. (
6
), and assume that up to a set of measure zero with respect to
Q(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
, there exists a partition
U^{\dagger}=\{D_{p}^{\dagger},D_{n}^{\dagger},B^{\dagger}\}
with a boundary set
B^{\dagger}
such that:

i.

D_{p}^{\dagger}\cap D_{n}^{\dagger}=D_{p}^{\dagger}\cap B^{\dagger}=D_{n}^{\dagger}\cap B^{\dagger}=\emptyset
;

ii.

D_{p}^{\dagger}\cup D_{n}^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}=\Gamma
; and

iii.

\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}_{p},D_{n}^{\dagger}\cup B^{\dagger}_{n}]
is minimized for an arbitrary partition
\{B^{\dagger}_{p},B^{\dagger}_{n}\}
of
B^{\dagger}
.

Then
U^{\dagger}
is in the equivalence class of partitions given by Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
).

Proof.

Consider first the case where
B^{\dagger}
is a set of measure zero, and assume the result is false. Then there exists a set of positive measure with respect to
Q(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
for which
D_{p}^{\dagger}
and/or
D_{n}^{\dagger}
differs from
D_{p}^{\star}
and
D_{n}^{\star}
. Consider the difference

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\dagger},D_{n}^{\dagger}]\!-\!\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\star},D_{n}^{\star}]\!=\!\int_{D_{p}^{\dagger}/D_{p}^{\star}}\hskip-22.76219pt(1\!-\!q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,{\rm d}r\!-\!\int_{D_{n}^{\star}/D_{n}^{\dagger}}\hskip-22.76219ptqP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,{\rm d}r\!+\!\int_{D_{n}^{\dagger}/D_{n}^{\star}}\hskip-22.76219ptqP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,{\rm d}r\!-\!\int_{D_{p}^{\star}/D_{p}^{\dagger}}\hskip-22.76219pt(1\!-\!q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,{\rm d}r.

Note also that
D_{p}^{\dagger}/D_{p}^{\star}=D_{n}^{\star}/D_{n}^{\dagger}
and
D_{n}^{\dagger}/D_{n}^{\star}=D_{p}^{\star}/D_{p}^{\dagger}
, since
U^{\dagger}
and
U^{\star}
are both partitions of
\Gamma
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\dagger},D_{n}^{\dagger}]-\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\star},D_{n}^{\star}]=\int_{D_{n}^{\star}/D_{n}^{\dagger}}\hskip-22.76219pt(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})-qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\,{\rm d}r+\int_{D_{p}^{\star}/D_{p}^{\dagger}}\hskip-22.76219ptqP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})-(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0,

where the last inequality is a consequence of the definitions of
D_{p}^{\star}
and
D_{n}^{\star}
. Thus, we find
\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\star},D_{n}^{\star}]<\mathcal{E}[D_{p}^{\dagger},D_{n}^{\dagger}]
, which is a contradiction.

The case for which
B^{\dagger}
has positive measure follows by analogous arguments, taking into account that this set can be arbitrarily partitioned between the positive and negative classification domains.

Lemma
16
is the converse of the Lemma
13
. Lemma
16
states that the equivalence class of partitions minimizing
\mathcal{E}(U;q)
is the same as the equivalence class
U^{\star}
defined explicitly in terms of
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. Perhaps unsurprisingly, this implies that any partition
U^{\dagger}
minimizing
\mathcal{E}
is as good as invoking Lemma
13
, no matter how we find
U^{\dagger}
. More importantly, however,
D_{p}^{\dagger}
and
D_{n}^{\dagger}
must preserve inequalities of the form
qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
etc., even though the conditional PDFs need not be used to construct these sets. In this context, the importance of Eq. (
9
) is difficult to overstate. In Sec.
5
, we show that the ability to construct
B^{\star}(q)
is central to extracting uncertainties in class labels.

However, it is necessary to first develop a practical method for constructing
B^{\star}(q)
. To this end, the following definition and postulates are useful.

Definition 17
(Boundary Classifier).

Let
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q))
depend on some parameters
\phi(q)
. We define
\hat{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q)))
to be a
boundary classifier
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the Heaviside step-function and
\mathcal{H}(0)
can arbitrarily be assigned the value
0
or
1
.

Clearly the locus of points
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q))=0
plays a special role in our analysis, and our aim is to understand in what sense
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q))=\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
. A key challenge, however, is that the locus of points satisfying
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q))=0
can differ from
B^{\star}(q)
on a set of measure zero, which is especially problematic if the latter is itself a set of measure zero!
The resolution to this problem is to postulate that such differences do not violate the structure of Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
).

Definition 18
.

We say that
U^{\dagger}(q)=\{D_{p}^{\dagger},D_{n}^{\dagger},B^{\dagger}\}
satisfies the
optimal partition convention
if
U^{\dagger}(q)
: (i) is in the equivalence class minimizing
\mathcal{E}(U;q)
; and (ii)
{\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\dagger}(q)\iff qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\geq(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}
and
{\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{n}^{\dagger}(q)\iff qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\leq(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}
.

Remark 19
.

Definition
18
implies that all
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
are classified correctly pointwise. This is needed to avoid situations in which, for example, a set of measure zero satisfying
qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is assigned to
D_{n}^{\star}(q)
. While such partitions do not affect the average classification accuracy, they clearly affect pointwise UQ. Note that
qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\nRightarrow\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\dagger}(q)
.

Postulate 20
(Weak Level Set).

A classifier minimizing
\mathcal{E}(U;q)
yields a partition that satisfies the optimal partition convention.

The WLS postulate does not guarantee that a boundary function identifies all
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q)
. From a modeling standpoint, this suggests a stronger postulate.

Postulate 21
(Strong Level-Set).

A boundary classifier minimizing
\mathcal{E}(U;q)
satisfies the WLS postulate and
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi(q))=\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
for all
q\in(0,1)
.

3
Classification Without PDFs

3.1
General Formulation of the Classification Method

Lemma
16
and the SLS postulate do not tell us how to find
B^{\star}
; one cannot avoid a modeling choice. Assume under the SLS postulate that an arbitrary boundary
B
is defined as the locus of points satisfying a general nonlinear equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi)=0

(10)

where
\phi
are parameters that determine the shape of the boundary. Consistent with Def.
17
, define the domains
D_{p}
and
D_{n}
via the inequalities
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi)>0
and
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi)<0
, respectively. Here we present the general framework and considerations for estimating
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi)
; specific models used in this work are discussed in Sec.
3.2
.

Remark 22
.

We assume that
\mathcal{B}
is twice differentiable in
\phi
, which permits the use of Newton’s Method and related approaches in subsequent calculations
(
39
;
51
)
.

To determine the
\phi
, we require a rigorous concept of
training data
. In particular:

Definition 23
(Pure Training Datapoint).

Let
\omega
be a sample point, and let
C(\omega)
be the true class of
\omega
. The pair
(C(\omega),\boldsymbol{\rm r}(\omega))
is a
pure training data point.

Definition 24
(Pure Training Population).

Assume that a population has two classes indexed by
k
, so that
k\in\{0,1\}
. Let
n_{k}
be positive integers, and let
\omega_{k,i}
be distinct sample points in
\Omega
. Then the collection of sets indexed by
k
and defined via

\displaystyle\Pi=\big\{\Pi_{k}\big\},

\displaystyle\Pi_{k}=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{i}\!:\!\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\rm r}(\omega_{k,i}),i\in\{1,...,n_{k}\},C(\omega_{k,i})=k\}_{k}

(11)

is a
training population
. We refer to the sets
\Pi_{k}
as training sets associated with population
k
. For brevity, we denote the
i
th element of the
k
th class by symbol
\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{k,i}
.

Training data and classifiers are ultimately used to deduce the properties of test population. We are usually only given a finite number of samples from such a population, which motivates the following definition:

Definition 25
(Empirical Test Population).

Let
\Omega
be a sample space and
C(\omega)
a binary random variable for
\omega\in\Omega
. Let
q_{s}\in[0,1]
, and the
\omega_{i}
are iid. We define

\displaystyle\Psi(q_{s},s)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}(\omega_{i}):i\in\{1,...,s\},\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{C(\omega_{i})}{s}=q_{s}\right\}

(12)

to be an
empirical test population
with
s
samples and prevalence
q_{s}
. When there is no risk of confusion, we also refer to
\Psi
more simply as a test population.

Remark 26
.

It is important to observe that the prevalence
q_{s}
of an empirical test population may not be identical to the
q
appearing in Eq. (
2
). The latter may take any value in
[0,1]
, whereas
q_{s}
is limited to rational numbers of the form
n_{1}/(n_{0}+n_{1})
. Only in the limit
n_{0}+n_{1}\to\infty
do we expect the two numbers to coincide.

Our goal is to use
\Pi
to find the boundary function that minimizes the empirical classification error. This can then be used to classify the samples in a test population
\Psi(q,s)
. However, in light of Sec.
2.3
, we need a method for estimating
q
, since
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
are no longer assumed to be known. This motivates the following.

Definition 27
(Empirical Prevalence Estimate).

Let
\Pi
be a pure training population, and let
D\subset\Gamma
be any subdomain chosen such that the empirical estimates

\displaystyle\tilde{N}_{D}=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{0}}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{0,j}\in D),

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{D}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{1,j}\in D)

(13)

are not equal, where
\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{i,j}\in\Pi_{i}
. Moreover, assume that
P_{D}\neq N_{D}
, and define
\tilde{Q}_{D}
according to Eq. (
3
) in terms of the empirical test population. Then we call

\displaystyle\hat{q}=\frac{\tilde{Q}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}}{\tilde{P}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}}

(14)

the
empirical prevalence estimate
of
q
.

Lemma 28
.

Assume there exists a positive-measure set
D
for which
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, and let
q\in(0,1)
. Let
\hat{q}
denote the empirical prevalence estimate, and assume that the number of training points
n_{k}\geq n
for all classes
k
and some
n>0
; see Def.
24
. Let
s\geq n
denote the number of test points. Then the following statements are true:

i.

\hat{q}
exists and is positive, both almost surely as
n\to\infty
;

ii.

\hat{q}
converges to Eq. (
3
) at a rate of
n^{-1/2}
as
n\to\infty
;

iii.

\hat{q}
is an asymptotically unbiased estimator of
q
.

Remark 29
.

In the proof of Lemma
28
, we temporarily assume the sample space corresponds to a set of countably infinite measurements on individuals
\omega\in\Omega
. Thus, the sample space referenced below is distinct from
\Omega
described in Sec.
2
.

Proof.

To prove i. note first that
P_{D}>N_{D}
. Let
\epsilon=(P_{D}-N_{D})/4
. Clearly if
\tilde{P}_{D}>P_{D}-\epsilon
and
\tilde{N}_{D}<N_{D}+\epsilon
, then
\hat{q}
exists. Thus, we seek to show that these inequalities hold almost surely.

Consider first the case for which
n_{0}=n_{1}=s=n
, and recognize that
n\tilde{P}_{D}
and
n\tilde{N}_{D}
are binomial random variables whose means are
nP_{D}
and
nN_{D}
, respectively. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the event for which
P_{D}-N_{D}\leq 0
,
\mathcal{A}_{P}
be the event for which
\tilde{P}_{D}\leq P_{D}-\epsilon
, and
\mathcal{A}_{N}
be the event for which
\tilde{N}_{D}\geq N_{D}+\epsilon
. Clearly
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{A}_{P}\cup\mathcal{A}_{N}
. This implies

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}]\leq\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{P}]+\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{N}].

(15)

Consider next
\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{P}]
, which is the probability of at most
k\leq nP_{D}-n\epsilon
successes given
n
trials. By Hoeffding’s inequality
(
32
)
,

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{P}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left[-2n\left(P_{D}-\frac{nP_{D}-n\epsilon}{n}\right)\right]=e^{-2n\epsilon}.

(16)

Similarly, one finds
\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{N}]\leq\exp(-2n\epsilon)
. Inequality (
15
) therefore implies

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}]\leq 2\exp(-2n\epsilon).

(17)

Consider next a sequence of events
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for which
\tilde{P}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}\leq 0
. One finds

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Pr[\mathcal{A}_{n}]\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2\exp(-2n\epsilon)<\infty.

(18)

Therefore, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma
(
19
;
12
)
, the event for which
\tilde{P}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}\leq 0
occurs infinitely often has measure zero. That is there exists almost surely a finite value of
n
for which
\tilde{P}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}
is guaranteed to be positive. As
P_{D}-N_{D}
is positive by assumption, the
\hat{q}
so constructed exists and is finite. A similar argument applied to the numerator of Eq. (
14
) proves that there exists an
n
for which
\tilde{Q}_{D}-\tilde{N}_{D}>0
.

To prove the result for the case in which the number of training and test points are not equal, let
n
be the minimum among
n_{0}
,
n_{1}
, and
s
. Then construct
\hat{q}
using
n
points from each training and test set. This reduces the problem to the previous case.

Assertion ii. is a consequence of the Central Limit Theorem. That is, as
n\to\infty
,
\tilde{P}_{D}
and
\tilde{N}_{D}
converge to normal random variables whose variances go as
P_{D}(1-P_{D})/n
and
N_{D}(1-N_{D})/n
. As
\hat{q}
is guaranteed to exist for some finite value of
n
, a Taylor expansion of the denominator in Eq. (
14
) yields the desired rate of convergence; see also the Delta method
(
17
)
. Note that assertion iii. follows directly from ii.

Remark 30
.

Lemma
28
only states that with sufficient data and a reasonable choice of
D
,
\hat{q}
exists and is positive definite. In such cases, it should be possible to estimate the
D
used in Lemma
28
by computing the
q=1/2
classification domain via the method presented at the end of this section. Based on the Central Limit Theorem, is also straightforward to show that
n\gg[q(P_{D}-N_{D})]^{-2}
characterizes the number of datapoints for which we can expect the estimator
\hat{q}
to be well-behaved with high probability. Thus, prevalence values close to
0
or
1
require a significant amount of data to resolve, as do measurements for which
P_{D}\approx N_{D}
.

Given a method for estimating the prevalence, we are now in a position to define the objective function whose minimum is our classification boundary of interest.

Definition 31
(Binary Empirical Error).

Let
\Pi
be a pure training population with
2
classes. Then the
prevalence-weighted empirical error

\mathcal{E}_{e}\left[U;\Pi,q\right]
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{e}\left[U;\Pi,q\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{p,i}\in D_{n})+\frac{1-q}{n_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{n}}\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{n,i}\in D_{p}).

(19)

Remark 32
.

The prevalence
q
appearing in Eq. (
19
) is either associated with a test population or left as a free parameter. We do not generally fix
{q=n_{p}/(n_{p}+n_{n})}
, which is the prevalence of the training population.

We justify Eq. (
19
) by observing that it is a Monte Carlo estimate of the expected error (
6
)
(
11
)
. By Def.
17
, it is also easy to see that the empirical error can be re-expressed in terms of the Heaviside step function
\mathcal{H}(x)
via

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{e}\left[\phi;\Pi,q\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n_{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{p}}\left[1-\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{p,i};\phi))\right]+\frac{1-q}{n_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{n}}\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{n,i};\phi)),

(20)

where, in a slight abuse of notation, we use the same symbol
\mathcal{E}_{e}
in Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
). Used as an objective function for determining
\phi
, however, Eq. (
20
) can yield an uncountably infinite number of partitions
U
with the same empirical error. Perhaps worse, for finite
n_{n}
and
n_{p}
, this objective function has a gradient (with respect to
\phi
) that is everywhere zero or infinite. This motivates the following modification.

Definition 33
(Regularized Empirical Error).

Let
\Pi
be a pure training population with two classes, and let
H(x)
be a strictly monotone increasing sigmoid function for which: the domain is
\mathbb{R}
; the range is
[0,1]
; and
H(0)=1/2
. Then the
regularized empirical error

\mathcal{L}\left[\phi;\Pi,q,\sigma^{2}\right]
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left[\phi;\Pi,q,\sigma^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{n_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{p}}\left[1-H\left(\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{p,j};\phi)}{\sigma^{2}}\right)\right]+\frac{1-q}{n_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{n}}H\left(\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{n,j};\phi)}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(21)

Remark 34
.

In this manuscript, we take
H(x)
to be
H(x)=0.5[1+{\rm tanh}(x)]
.

To understand the importance the regularization parameter
\sigma^{2}
in Def. (
21
), note that in the limit
\sigma\to 0
, Eq. (
21
) reverts to the empirical classification error of Eq. (
19
). Positive values of
\sigma^{2}
therefore play two related roles. First,
\sigma
renders the objective function continuous and differentiable in
\phi
, provided
\mathcal{B}
has these properties. Second, for vanishing values of
\sigma
, the objective function is clearly non-convex and may have many local minima. Informally speaking, large values of
\sigma
help to “convexify”
\mathcal{L}
, in essence expanding the radius of convergence of the optimization algorithm. However, this begs the question: how does one choose
\sigma
?

We address this conundrum by treating
\sigma
as a homotopy parameter; see Refs.
(
57
;
4
;
2
;
18
;
46
)
for background and related approaches. Our goal is to reach the limit
\sigma\to 0
while stabilizing the optimization, for which the latter requires
\sigma
to be large. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 35
.

Let
\{\sigma_{j}^{2}\}_{j=0}^{s}
be a monotone decreasing sequence. Let
\mathcal{M}
be an optimization algorithm that maps the pair
\mathcal{L}
and
\phi
back onto the space of
\phi
, where
\mathcal{M}
approximates the
\argmin
operator. Given an initial
\phi_{0}^{\star}
, we define the
\phi_{j+1}^{\star}
th
homotopy classifier
to be the solution to the optimization problem

\displaystyle\phi_{j+1}^{\star}=\mathcal{M}[\mathcal{L}(\phi;\Pi,q,\sigma_{j}^{2});\phi_{j}^{\star}],

(22)

where
\phi_{j}^{\star}
is used as initial point associated with the
(j+1)
th optimization problem.

Remark 36
.

Observe that we may estimate any level set
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi^{\star}(q))
by varying

in Eq. (
22
). However, the level-sets so constructed can potentially cross, which would lead to contradictions in the definitions of the underlying PDFs if interpreting
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi^{\star}(q))
as
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
. Section
5
addresses this issue.

The choices of
\{\sigma^{2}_{j}\}_{j=0}^{s}
and
\phi_{0}^{\star}
in Def.

are problem specific; in Sec.
4.2
we propose an algorithm for determining them. However, taking
\sigma^{2}
to be the minimum characteristic distance between datapoints should approximate the limit
\sigma\to 0
, provided
\mathcal{B}
remains order one for all values of
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
. Moreover, we are not guaranteed a unique minimizer in the limit
\sigma\to 0
. Herein, we never consider
\sigma^{2}<10^{-8}
.

We are now in a position to construct our classifier for
test data

Construction 37

3.2
Quadratic Approximation

To illustrate the concepts developed in the previous section, we consider an important model of
\mathcal{B}
.

Example 38
(Quadric Boundary Sets).

Assume that an arbitrary boundary set in
m
-dimensions can be represented as a generalized quadratic equation of the form

\displaystyle B=\left\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:(\boldsymbol{\rm r},1){\boldsymbol{\rm A}}\binom{\boldsymbol{\rm r}}{1}=0\right\}

(23)

where
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
is an
(m+1)\times(m+1)
matrix (for
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
) of free parameters that depend on
q
. That is, identify
\phi
as the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
. Letting
\chi^{\rm T}=(\boldsymbol{\rm r},1)
, this corresponds to the boundary function

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};{\boldsymbol{\rm A}})=\chi^{\rm T}{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}\chi.

(24)

Without loss of generality, supplement this with the symmetry constraint

\displaystyle A_{i,j}=A_{j,i}.

(25)

While the quadratic model is relatively simple, it supports efficient computation and generalization through vectorization. This example also illustrates auxiliary assumptions and regularization needed to stabilize the analysis. In particular, note that
\mathcal{L}
as defined by Eq. (
21
) has a plethora of connected sets when expressed in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
. Thus, the minimizer is not unique. Among these is a duplication of the off-diagonal parameters in
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
. To see this, recognize that a quadratic in
m
variables can be defined in terms of
(m+1)(m+2)/2
parameters, although
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
has
(m+1)^{2}
. The symmetry constraint given by Eq. (
25
) resolves this issue.

A more troublesome connected set arises from the interplay between
\sigma
and
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
. A general quadratic is more simply defined in terms of only
(m+1)(m+2)/2-1
parameters, one less than fixed by a symmetry constraint. The extra parameter amounts to a change of scale and is associated with the fact that we may divide all coefficients in a polynomial by any one of them. Thus, without further regularization, minimization of Eq. (
21
) yields a coefficient matrix
{\boldsymbol{\rm A}}
whose elements tend to diverge in a way that undoes the regularization associated with
\sigma^{2}
. This arises from the fact that large values of
\sigma
“blur-out” the individual data points, so that those near the boundary increase the objective function. A solution to this problem is to impose additional regularization that fixes the scale.

Definition 39
(Scale-Regularized Objective).

Let the quantity
\mathcal{L}_{\rm scale}
be defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm scale}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n_{p}}\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{p,j},\phi)^{2}}{n_{p}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{n}}\frac{\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{n,j},\phi)^{2}}{n_{n}}-1\right)^{2}.

(26)

Then we define the
scale-regularized objective function

\mathcal{L}_{sr}
to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{sr}=\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{L}_{\rm scale}.

(27)

Remark 40
.

The boundary set is the locus of points for which
\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0
. Thus, Eq. (
26
) requires that the sum of “variances” of the
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},\phi)
(relative to
0
) be
\mathcal{O}(1)
. This ensures that the argument of
H(x)
in Eq. (
21
) remains
\mathcal{O}(1/\sigma^{2})
, so that
\sigma
controls the regularization scale associated with the homotopy optimization.

\displaystyle\Xi_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{k}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{k,j}-\mu_{k})(\boldsymbol{\rm r}_{k,j}-\mu_{k})^{\rm T},

5
Uncertainty Level-Sets

Having developed a method for constructing classification boundaries, we now return to the task of uncertainty quantification foreshadowed in Sec.
2.4
. Our goals in this section are to: (i) show that despite not having access to the conditional PDFs, the function
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},\phi^{\star}(q))
contains all of the information needed to construct
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
; and (ii) develop a practical method for approximating
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
given an empirical training population
\Pi
. To this end, the various interpretations of
q
play a fundamental role; see Key Remark
14
.

5.1
Theoretical Issues

To better understand the relationship between
B^{\star}(q)
and
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
, recognize that only the ratio of conditional PDFs are needed for classification. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 46
.

The ratio
R:\Gamma\to[0,\infty]
defined as

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=\frac{N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}{P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}

(38)

is the
relative conditional probability
of a measurement outcome, where we interpret the situation
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=\infty
to correspond to
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
.

Remark 47
.

There is no ambiguity in the definition of
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
associated with the case
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
, which yields an indeterminate form for
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. Such points can always be excluded from
\Gamma
, as they have zero measure with respect to
Q(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
.

In light of the SLS property and SLS postulate, the relative conditional probability is isomorphic to the boundary function
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},\phi^{\star}(q))=0
in the sense that

\displaystyle B^{\star}(q)=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},q)=0\}=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q/(1-q)\}=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},\phi^{\star}(q))=0\}.

(39)

Since Eq. (
39
) does not alter the structure of the sets
B^{\star}(q)
,
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
must contain all the information necessary for both classification and UQ. This reduces both tasks to an
(m+1)
dimensional modeling problem when
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. To extract
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
from a
(m-1)
-dimensional boundary set, observe that we can “recover” an additional degree of freedom from Eq. (
39
), which yields the value of
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
on
B
. We recover the last degree of freedom by allowing
q
to vary from
0
to
1
.

To clarify this idea, recognize that the prevalence plays three distinct but related roles in our analysis. First,
q
defines a probability measure associated with the fraction of positive individuals in a test population. Second,
q
defines a convex combination of error terms in Eqs. (
6
) and (
21
). Thus, it could equally be viewed as a parameter of the objective function, not necessarily a property of any given population. Third, Eq. (
39
) shows that
q
has a one-to-one relationship with
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, which means that we can treat
q
as a function of
R
, or even
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
. It therefore stands to reason that
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is not even needed to estimate
Z(\boldsymbol{\rm r};U,q)
. We make this precise as follows.

Definition 48
.

We refer to
{{{q}}}:\Gamma\to[0,1]
defined via

\displaystyle{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})

\displaystyle=\frac{R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}{1+R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}=\frac{N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}{N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})+P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}

(40)

as the
prevalence function
.

Given these definitions, we now prove elementary but fundamental properties of the boundary sets
B^{\star}(q)
that establish them as level sets.

Proposition 49
(Level-Set Representation).

Assume the WLS property holds, and let
q\in(0,1)
. Then any partition
U^{\star}(q)
satisfying the optimal partition convention has the following properties:

I.

{B^{\star}(q)\subset D_{p}^{\star}(q^{\prime})/B^{\star}(q^{\prime})}
and
{B^{\star}(q^{\prime})\subset D_{n}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)}
for
q^{\prime}>q
, and in particular,
B^{\star}(q)\cap B^{\star}(q^{\prime})=\emptyset
for
q\neq q^{\prime}
;

II.

for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
, the optimally assigned class
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},U^{\star}(q))
is a monotone increasing function of
q
;

III.

for every
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in\Gamma
, there exists one and only one value of
q
for which
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q)
, and in particular,
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q)\iff{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q
and
{\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}))=0}
;

IV.

{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\in(0,1)
is the value of
q
for which
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
has a 50% probability of belonging to either class, so that
B^{\star}(q)
is the 50% probability level-set;

Proof.

Claim I is directly verified from the structure of
D_{p}^{\star}(q)
and
D_{n}^{\star}(q)
and the optimal partition convention, where the latter ensures that the inequality structure of Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
) always holds pointwise.

To prove claim II, fix
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
and
q\in(0,1)
. Because
U^{\star}(q)
is a partition, one of three possibilities must hold:
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)
,
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q)
, or
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{n}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)
. Observe also that
qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is a strictly monotone increasing function of
q
, and
(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is a strictly monotone decreasing function of
q
. This implies that
D_{p}^{\star}(q)\subset D_{p}^{\star}(q^{\prime})
if
q<q^{\prime}
. Thus in the case that
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)
, the class assignment of
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
remains unchanged as
q
increases. If
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q)
, then
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};U^{\star}(q))
depends on the partition, but by claim I,
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};U^{\star}(q^{\prime}))=1
for any
q^{\prime}>q
. Similar arguments imply that
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};U^{\star}(q^{\prime}))
cannot decrease if
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{n}^{\star}(q)/B^{\star}(q)
and
q^{\prime}>q
.

To prove III, note that
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})/P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is a mapping
R:\Gamma\to[0,\infty]
. Moreover, the mapping
{\mathcal{R}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty]}
defined as
\mathcal{R}({{{q}}})=\frac{{{{q}}}}{1-{{{q}}}}
is one-to-one, since
\mathcal{R}({{{q}}})
is a strictly monotone increasing function of
{{{q}}}
. The boundary set
B^{\star}(q)
is defined as the set of
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
for which
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=\mathcal{R}(q)
, and since the ranges of the two are identical and
\mathcal{R}(q)
is invertible, there must exist a
q
such that
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q/(1-q)
. In fact, this
q
is
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, which follows from Def.
48
. Moreover, since
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is a function, every
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
maps to only one value in the range of
R
, and hence one value of
q
. This proves the main claim of III, and
{\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}))=0}
follow from the WLS property.

To prove IV, observe that
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=\frac{q[1-{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})]\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\star})+(1-q){{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\mathbb{I}(\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{n}^{\star})}{q[1-{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})]+(1-q){{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})}.

(41)

The range of
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is
[0,1]
. Thus, when
q\in(0,1)
and
q={{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
for a fixed value of
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
, one finds
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0.5
, which is the probability correctly classifying the sample. Note that
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is indeterminate when
q={{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\in\{0,1\}
.

Interpretation:
Proposition
49
defines what we mean by a collection of level sets. Four properties must hold: level-sets must be disjoint (I), be well-ordered with respect to one another (I) and fixed
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
(II), cover
\Gamma
(III), and be physically meaningful (IV).

We are now in a position to determine the sense in which
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};\phi^{\star}(q))=\mathcal{B}^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
. However, it is possible to pose this as a consequence of a more general result by first taking into account the following definition.

Definition 50
(Partition Induced by a Classifier).

Let
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
be an arbitrary classifier, and let
D_{n}=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0\}
and
D_{p}=\{\boldsymbol{\rm r}:\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=1\}
. We say that
U=\{D_{n},D_{p}\}
is the
partition induced by the classifier.

Proposition 51
(Class-Switching Representation).

Let the WLS property and WLS postulate hold. Let
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
be an arbitrary classifier inducing a partition
U^{\dagger}
that minimizes
\mathcal{E}(U;q)
for
q\in(0,1)
, and assume that
U^{\dagger}
satisfies the optimal partition convention. Then for every
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
, either:
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\in\{0,1\}
; or
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, where

\displaystyle q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=\sup\{q:\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0\},

\displaystyle q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=\inf\{q:\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=1\}.

(42)

Proof.

Consider first the case for which
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
,
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
. Clearly
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in D_{p}^{\star}(q)
for any positive
q
. If
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0
, then by Lemma
16
,
qP(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\leq(1-q)N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
cannot fall on any boundary set, and we assign
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
. Similarly one can assign
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=1
when
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=0
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
.

If both
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})>0
, then by Proposition
49
and Def.
48
,
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\in(0,1)
. Arguing by contradiction, assume that
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q^{\prime}<q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. By Proposition
49
claim III,
\boldsymbol{\rm r}\in B^{\star}(q^{\prime})
. Let
q^{\prime\prime}
satisfy
q^{\prime}<q^{\prime\prime}<q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. By claim I of Proposition
49
and the definition of
B^{\star}(q^{\prime})
, the optimally assigned class
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};U^{\star}(q^{\prime\prime}))=1
, since
q^{\prime\prime}>q^{\prime}
. But by assumption and Lemma
16
,
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q^{\prime\prime})=0
, since
q^{\prime\prime}<q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. Thus,
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
is not optimal, which is a contradiction. Similarly, one finds that
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\ngtr q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. The equality
q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is proved in a similar manner. That is, assuming the result is false implies there exists a
q^{\prime\prime}
such that
q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=<q^{\prime\prime}<q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
. One finds that either
\hat{C}
is not optimal or one of the definitions
q_{l}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
,
q_{h}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
, is false, which is a contradiction.

Remark 52
.

Proposition

51
is general insofar as
\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
need not be a boundary classifier. Any ML algorithm whose classes induce a partition of some set in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfies the hypotheses, provided the optimal partition convention holds.

Corollary 53
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
,
Z^{\star}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=Z^{\star}_{l}=Z^{\star}_{h}
, where

\displaystyle Z^{\star}_{k}=\frac{q[1-q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})]\mathbb{I}(\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=1)+(1-q)q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})\mathbb{I}(\hat{C}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0)}{q[1-q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})]+(1-q)q_{k}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})},

\displaystyle k\in\{l,h\}.

(43)

Corollary 54
.

Assume that a boundary classifier
\hat{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
satisfies the SLS postulate. Then the locus of points solving
{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)=0
have the property that
{{{q}}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=q
.

5.2
Practical Implementation

In the context of Eq. (
22
), realizing the UQ estimates of Proposition
49
must address two challenges. First, it is generally difficult to estimate
\phi^{\star}(q)
except on a finite grid of
q
. This means that we may overestimate the set
[q_{l},q_{h}]
in Proposition
51
, especially when
q_{l}=q_{h}
. Second, the formulation of Sec.
3
does not prohibit crossing between any two estimated level sets with different values of
q
. While this may not occur in idealized cases, it is problematic given finite training populations. Figure
6
illustrates this problem in the context of the data from Example
43
. At points where the level sets cross, we cannot assign meaningful uncertainty estimates via Eq. (
43
), since
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
is no longer single valued.

In light of claim i. of Proposition
49
and Proposition
51
, we interpret crossing level-sets as a violation of the class monotonicity property. In Fig.
6
, for example, the class of point “W” can decrease with
q
. This suggests introducing monotonicity as a constraint of the optimization. However, doing so is challenging because
\hat{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
integer-valued. Instead, we apply the constraint directly to
\mathcal{B}(\boldsymbol{\rm r},\phi^{\star}(q))
as follows.

Definition 55
(Level-set Objective Function).

Let
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2,...,\tau\}
be an arbitrary, finite set of points in
\Gamma
. Let
\Pi
be a training population. Let
q_{j}
satisfy
0<q_{1}<...<q_{\theta}<1
for some
\theta
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{level}[\phi_{1},...,\phi_{\theta};\Pi,\sigma^{2}]=\sum_{j=1}^{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{sr}[\phi_{j};\Pi,q_{j};\sigma^{2}]

(44)

where the
\phi_{j}
are treated as unknown parameters. Then we call
\mathcal{L}_{level}
the
level-set objective function
and subject it to the constraint that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i},\phi(q_{j})\leq\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i},\phi(q_{j+1}))

(45)

for all pairs
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i},q_{j}
.

Remark 56
.

We refer to the
{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i}
as
shadow points
, since they need not be objects of classification per se. Their role is to ensure that the level-sets constructed by minimizing of
\mathcal{L}_{level}
do not intersect. Heuristically they do this by requiring
\mathcal{B}({\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{i},\phi^{\star}(q))
to be monotone increasing function
q
. In principle, however, this is not sufficient to guarantee that
\hat{C}_{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\rm r};q)
is also monotone increasing, which can happen if the values of
\phi_{j}
are poorly scaled. In practice we find that scale regularization [e.g. via Eq. (
26
)] combined with sufficiently many shadow points overcomes this problem. See Fig.
6
for an example of this analysis.

6
Discussion

6.1
Comparison with Past Works

Our approach is related to generative and discriminative ML techniques, although it does not neatly fall into either category. While there is some disagreement within the community as to how best to define these concepts (compare
(
33
;
38
)
), we adopt the perspective of Ref.
(
36
)
:

•

A generative model quantifies the probability of an outcome
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
conditioned on the class.

•

A discriminative model quantifies the probability of belonging to a class, conditioned on outcome
\boldsymbol{\rm r}
.

These definitions are clearly linked by the concept of conditional probability
(
14
)
, but in machine learning they have often been treated as irreconcilable.

To frame Eq. (
22
) in the context of generative modeling, consider methods such as linear discriminant and quadratic discriminant analyses (QDA)
(
22
;
30
;
47
;
54
)
. QDA, for example, assumes that the conditional PDFs are multivariate Gaussians. The classifier is constructed from the optimal Bayes rule
(
14
)
in the spirit of Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
). As a result, the classification boundary is necessarily quadratic, since the ratio of two such PDFs is still an exponential of a quadratic. However, the converse is not true: a quadratic classification boundary does not imply that
P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
and
N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
are Gaussian. Figure
2
is a notable counterexample, since the underlying distribution for the pair
x,y
arises from the sum of Gaussian and chi-squared random variables. Thus, the quadratic version of our analysis is more general than QDA.

To place Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) in the context of discriminative modeling, consider that they resemble support vector machines (SVM)
(
5
;
7
;
13
;
48
)
. However, SVMs optimize an empirical objective function that, loosely speaking, attempts to maximize the distance between the classification boundary and the nearest points in the training set (i.e. the hard-margin problem), with appropriate generalizations when the data is not linearly separable (the soft-margin problem)
(
5
)
. Importantly, the objective function is not the classification error
per se
. This appears to have limited the ability to connect SVMs to an underlying probabilistic framework, e.g. via Bayes optimal methods
(
24
;
45
;
50
)
. In contrast, the objective function given by Eq. (
27
) trivially reduces to the classification error when
\sigma\to 0
, and as a practical matter, we can take
\sigma
to be sufficiently small so that
\mathcal{L}
is an arbitrarily good (albeit empirical) estimate of Eq. (
6
). Moreover, Lemma
16
and Proposition
51
imply that the resulting classification boundaries quantify
all
relative probabilities of measurement outcomes. Thus, we interpret these results as directly connecting a discriminative classifier in the spirit of Eq. (
22
) to its generative counterpart.

These observations suggest that the distinction between discriminative and generative classifiers requires more nuance. We propose the following:

(1)

A generative classifier models the relative conditional probability
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})=N(\boldsymbol{\rm r})/P(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
directly, which induces the “discriminative” boundary given by Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
) via Lemma
13
.

(2)

A discriminative classifier invokes Lemma
16
and Proposition
51
to determine (or bound) level-sets of the generative model
R(\boldsymbol{\rm r})
.

In other words, these two tasks are converses of one another in a way that mirrors the relationship between Lemmas
13
and
16
.

These observations have implications for both discriminative and generative modeling. First, they suggest the need to revisit the objective functions that define the classifiers. To connect generative and discriminative classifiers, we needed to slightly modify the definition of both. Second, it may be useful to directly model relative probabilities instead of conditional probabilities, as this is both more general and relevant for classification. Third, structure of Eq. (
19
), which is a convex combination suggests connections to concepts such as linear independence. This points to possible extensions of our work to impure training data. Indeed, it would be surprising if SVMs could be extended to unsupervised learning tasks, given the current understanding of such techniques
(
7
)
. Such topics are the focus of the next manuscript in this series.

6.2
Limitations and Open Questions

A key limitation of this work is the need to specify a model associated with the classification boundary. While the low-order approximation made herein is likely reasonable for many assays, it is not
a priori
clear how to estimate the added uncertainties associated with this choice. We are aware that the choice of model can lead to rare but notable instances wherein data is obviously classified incorrectly, e.g. via the second branch of a hyperbola. While such issues can often be fixed manually after-the-fact, it may not always be clear how to do this in higher-dimensional settings.

We also note several important open directions. In particular, generalizing the analysis to more than two classes (e.g. SARS-CoV-2 infected, vaccinated, and naive) remains challenging, as well as determining the conditions under which impure data can be used for such problems. A more significant problem arises from waning of antibodies over time and associated variability, which depends on antigen, individual, and exposure level
(
28
)
.
‡
‡

‡

Cross-reactive antibodies that may yield false positives are already accounted for by our analysis, provided the negative population has samples exhibiting cross-reactivity.
Our analysis does not account for such effects and would require generalizations of Ref.
(
6
)
. Finally, rigorous convergence estimates with the size of the empirical training sets are important research directions for further grounding the analysis discussed herein.
