Distributional Signals for Node Classification in Graph Neural Networks
Feng Ji, See Hian Lee, Kai Zhao, Wee Peng Tay, Jielong Yang

Abstract
In graph neural networks (GNNs), both node features and labels are examples of graph signals, a key notion in graph signal processing (GSP). While it is common in GSP to impose signal smoothness constraints in learning and estimation tasks, it is unclear how this can be done for discrete node labels. We bridge this gap by introducing the concept of distributional graph signals. In our framework, we work with the distributions of node labels instead of their values and propose notions of smoothness and non-uniformity of such distributional graph signals. We then propose a general regularization method for GNNs that allows us to encode distributional smoothness and non-uniformity of the model output in semi-supervised node classification tasks. Numerical experiments demonstrate that our method can significantly improve the performance of most base GNN models in different problem settings.

Introduction

We consider the semi-supervised node classification problem
[
1
]
that determines class labels of nodes in graphs given sample observations and possibly node features. Numerous graph neural network (GNN) models have been proposed to tackle this problem. One of the first models is the graph convolutional network (GCN)
[
2
]
. Interpreted geometrically, a GCN aggregates information such as node features from the neighborhood of each node of the graph. Algebraically, this process is equivalent to applying a graph convolution filter to node feature vectors. Subsequently, many GNN models with different considerations are introduced. Popular models include the graph attention network (GAT)
[
3
]
that learns weights between pairs of nodes during aggregation, and the hyperbolic graph convolutional neural network (HGCN)
[
4
]
that considers embedding of nodes of a graph in a hyperbolic space instead of a Euclidean space. For inductive learning, GraphSAGE
[
5
]
is proposed to generate low-dimensional vector representations for nodes that are useful for graphs with rich node attribute information. While new models draw inspiration from GCN, GCN itself is built upon the foundation of graph signal processing (GSP).

GSP is a signal processing framework that handles graph-structured data
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. A graph signal is a vector with each component corresponding to a node of a graph. Examples include node features and node labels. Moreover, convolutions used in models such as GCN are special cases of convolution filters in GSP
[
6
]
. All these show the close connections between GSP theory and GNNs.

In GSP, signal smoothness (over the graph) is widely used to regularize inference tasks. Intuitively, a signal is smooth if its values are similar at each pair of nodes connected by an edge. One popular way to formally define signal smoothness is to use the Laplacian quadratic form. There are numerous GSP tools that leverage a smooth prior of the graph signals. For example, Laplacian (Tikhonov) regularization is proposed for noise removal in
[
6
]
and signal interpolation
[
9
]
. In
[
10
]
, it is used in graph signal in-painting and anomaly detection. In
[
11
]
, the same technique is used for graph topology inference.

However, for GNNs, it is remarked in
[
12
, Section 4.1.2]
that “graph Laplacian regularization can hardly provide extra information that existing GNNs cannot capture”. Therefore, a regularization scheme based on feature propagation is proposed. It is demonstrated to be effective by comparing with other methods such as
[
13
]
and
[
14
]
based on adversarial learning and
[
15
]
that co-trains GNN models with an additional agreement model, which gives the probability that two nodes have the same label. We partially agree with the above assertion regarding graph Laplacian regularization, while remaining reservative about its full correctness. In this paper, we propose a method that is inspired by Laplacian regularization. As our main contribution, we introduce the notion of distributional graph signals, instead of considering graph signals. Analogous to the graph signal smoothness defined using graph Laplacian, we define the smoothness of distributional graph signals. Together with another property known as non-uniformity, we devise a regularization scheme for GNNs in node classification tasks. This approach is easy to implement and can be used as a plug-in regularization term together with any given base GNN model. Its effectiveness is demonstrated with numerical results.

Distributional graph signals

II-A

GSP preliminaries and signal smoothness

In this subsection, we give a brief overview of GSP theory
[
6
]
. The focus is on the discussion of graph signal smoothness.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}})
be an undirected graph with
{\mathcal{V}}
the vertex set and
{\mathcal{E}}
the edge set. Suppose the size of the graph is
n=|{\mathcal{V}}|
. Fix an ordering of
{\mathcal{V}}
. Then, the
space of graph signals
can be identified with the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with a graph signal
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which assigns its
i
-th component to the
i
-th vertex of
{\mathcal{G}}
. By convention, signals are in column form, and
{\bm{x}}(i)
is the
i
-th component of
{\bm{x}}
.

In GSP, the key notion is
the graph shift operator
. Though there are several choices for the graph shift operator, in our paper, we consider a common choice:
{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
, the Laplacian of
{\mathcal{G}}
, defined by
{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}={\bm{D}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}-{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
, where
{\bm{D}}_{{\mathcal{G}}},{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
are the degree matrix and adjacency matrix of
{\mathcal{G}}
, respectively. The Laplacian
{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
is positive semi-definite and symmetric. By the spectral theorem, it has an eigendecomposition
{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}={\bm{U}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}\Lambda_{{\mathcal{G}}}{\bm{U}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}^{\top}
. In the decomposition,
\Lambda_{{\mathcal{G}}}
is a diagonal matrix, whose diagonal entries
\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\}
are eigenvalues of
{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
. They are non-negative and we assume
\lambda_{1}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{n}
. The associated eigenbasis
\{{\bm{u}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{n}\}
are the columns of
{\bm{U}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
. In GSP, an eigenvector with a small eigenvalue (and hence a small index) is considered to be smooth. The signal values of such a vector have small fluctuations across the edges of
{\mathcal{G}}
.

Given a graph signal
{\bm{x}}
, its
graph Fourier transform
is
\hat{{\bm{x}}}={\bm{U}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}
, or equivalently,
\hat{{\bm{x}}}(i)=\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}_{i}\rangle
, for
1\leq i\leq n
. The components
\hat{{\bm{x}}}(i)
of
\hat{{\bm{x}}}
are called the
frequency components
of
{\bm{x}}
. Same as above, the signal
{\bm{x}}
is smooth if
\hat{{\bm{x}}}(i)
has a small absolute value for large
i
. Quantitatively, we can define its
total variation
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}({\bm{x}}(i)-{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}{\bm{x}}.

(1)

It is straightforward to compute that
\mathcal{T}_{G}({\bm{u}}_{i})=\lambda_{i}
. This observation indicates that it is reasonable to use total variation as a measure of smoothness. Minimizing the total variation of graph signals has many applications in GSP as we have pointed out in
Section

I
.

Step graph signals

Let
{\mathbb{S}}
be a finite set of numbers. A

{\mathbb{S}}

{\bm{x}}
such that all its components take values in

{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}

Example 1
.

For the simplest example, consider the classical Heaviside function

\mathbb{R}

{\mathcal{G}}

2m+1

{\mathcal{G}}
with the integers in the interval

. Then

induces a step graph signal
{\bm{h}}
on
{\mathcal{G}}
. Same as the Heaviside function

, the signal
{\bm{h}}
should be considered to be a non-smooth graph signal.

Step graph signals occur naturally in semi-supervised node classification tasks. In particular, if
{\mathbb{S}}
is the set of all possible class labels, then the labels of the nodes of
{\mathcal{G}}
form a step graph signal
{\bm{c}}

w.r.t.

{\mathbb{S}}
on
{\mathcal{G}}
. We expect that analogous to
1
,
{\bm{c}}
can possibly be non-smooth. To demonstrate, we analyze
{\bm{c}}
using its Fourier transform
\hat{{\bm{c}}}
(cf.
Section

II-A
). More specifically, we take
{\mathcal{G}}_{0}
to be the main connected component of the Cora graph
[
16
]
and
{\bm{c}}
to be the ground truth labels. We also generate a random signal
{\bm{r}}
following the same empirical distribution (on
{\mathbb{S}}
) estimated using
{\bm{c}}
. We show plots of both Fourier transforms
\hat{{\bm{c}}}
and
\hat{{\bm{r}}}
in
Fig.

1
. We see that the high-frequency components of the ground truth labels
{\bm{c}}
can be large, and it is even possible that its spectrum, i.e., frequency components, resemble that of a random signal. The observations support our speculation about the non-smoothness of the step signals. Therefore, in order to leverage signal smoothness to enhance model performance, we need to find an alternative to the step (label) signals. This also supports the remark of
[
12
]
regarding Laplacian regularization from a different point of view (see also the experiments in
Section

).

II-C

Distributional graph signals and marginals

We want to use probability theory to introduce the notion of “smoothness” for step signals in the next section. For preparation, in this subsection, we formally introduce distributional graph signals and discuss how they arise naturally in GNNs. Our theory (e.g., in
1
and equation
Eq.

3
) is based on probability measures defined on metric spaces. To this end, we endow any discrete space
{\mathbb{S}}
with the discrete metric
d(s_{1},s_{2})=1
if
s_{1}\neq s_{2}\in{\mathbb{S}}
and
0
otherwise. For
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, one can use the usual Euclidean metric or any other norm.

Definition 1
.

For a metric space
{\mathbb{M}}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
be the space of probability measures on
{\mathbb{M}}
(
w.r.t.
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra) having finite second moments. An element
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
is called a
distributional graph signal
. The marginals of a distributional graph signal
\mu
are the marginal distributions
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}

w.r.t.
the
n
coordinates of either
\mathbb{S}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

To understand why a distribution
\mu
is related to GSP, consider a step graph signal (resp. ordinary graph signal)
{\bm{x}}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{n}
(resp.
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). It induces the delta distribution
\delta_{{\bm{x}}}
in
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}}^{n})
supported at
{\bm{x}}
. Its marginals are the delta distributions
\{\delta_{{\bm{x}}(i)},1\leq i\leq n\}
. Therefore,
1
subsumes ordinary graph signals as special cases.

:
We give an example of the marginals of a distributional graph signal. We notice that for
{\bm{X}}_{1,:}
, the probability weights are equal and it is hard to determine the
\argmax
of the
3
classes. Moreover, the weights of the
2
nd class have large differences along a few edges, e.g.,
(v_{2},v_{3}),(v_{3},v_{4})
. These features make prediction unreliable. We propose solutions to such issues in this paper.

Distributional graph signals also occur naturally in GNN models (illustrated in
Fig.

2
). As in the previous subsection, let
{\mathbb{S}}
be the set of all possible class labels of size
m
and
M_{n,m}(\mathbb{R})
be the space of

matrices. For a typical model
\mathcal{M}
such as the GCN, the last stage of the pipeline usually consists of the following steps
1

:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Logits: }{\bm{O}}\in M_{n,m}(\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\text{Softmax}:\phi}{\bm{X}}=\phi({\bm{O}})\in M_{n,m}(\mathbb{R})\\
&\xrightarrow{\argmax}\text{Output labels: }{\bm{c}}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n}.\end{split}

(2)

The

on

. They do not directly give a distributional graph signal in

. However, we shall interpret

In order to mimic the ways smoothness of graph signals is used in GSP, we introduce an appropriate notion of the total variation of distributional graph signals in the next section. III
Regularized graph neural networks

In this section, we study smoothness and non-uniformity properties of distributional graph signals in
Section

III-A
and
Section

III-B
respectively. Smoothness refers to the similarity of distributions for neighboring nodes, which is different from the classical point of view. Moreover, it only makes sense to talk about non-uniformity when dealing with distributional graph signals. Each of these subsections yields an expression that we want to minimize. They are combined in

III-A

Smoothness of distributional graph signals

The goal of this subsection is to introduce and compare different notions of total variation associated with distributional graph signals and their marginals that leverage signal smoothness. First of all, given
\mu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
for
{\mathbb{M}}={\mathbb{S}}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, its total variation can be modified directly from (
1
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\mu}\mathcal{T}_{G}({\bm{x}})=\int\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu({\bm{x}}).

(3)

where
d
is the metric on
{\mathbb{S}}
or
\mathbb{R}
.

However, in many cases of interest such as GNNs, only marginals of some
\mu
are observed (cf.
Section

II-C
). We want to define total variation in such a situation as well. We borrow ideas from the prototype of the Wasserstein metric
[
18
]
, which we recall now. The notations and assumptions follow those of
1
.

Definition 2
.

For
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
, the
Wasserstein metric

W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
between
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{2}=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\int d(x,y)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma(x,y),

where
\Gamma(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})
is the set of
couplings
of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
, i.e., the collection of probability measures on
{\mathbb{M}}\times{\mathbb{M}}
whose marginals are
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
, respectively.

It can be verified that
W(\cdot,\cdot)
indeed defines a metric on
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{M}})
(see
[
18
]
). As a special case, if
\delta_{x}
and
\delta_{y}
are delta distributions supported on
x,y\in{\mathbb{M}}
, then
W(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=d(x,y)
. The key insight is that we want to take infimum over all possible distributions given the prescribed marginals; and whatever we define, it should subsume (
1
) as a special case for a collection of delta distributions.

Definition 3
.

Given
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
(resp.
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
), for
1\leq i\leq n
, then the total variation of
\mathscr{N}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})=\inf_{\mu\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})}\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu),

(4)

where
\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
is the collection of all distributional graph signals in
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}}^{n})
(resp.
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
) whose marginals agree with
\mathscr{N}
.

Though an important theoretical tool, the Wasserstein metric is usually difficult to compute explicitly. On the other hand, if
{\mathcal{G}}
is the graph with
2
nodes connected by an edge and
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\}
, then
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})=W(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})^{2}
. As a consequence, finding the exact value of
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
can be challenging. Therefore, we next introduce approximations that can be more readily estimated.

In graph signal processing, a useful strategy to understand a general graph is to study its spanning trees
[
19
,
20
]
. A set
{\mathbb{T}}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\}
of
k
-spanning trees of
G

covers
if each edge of
G
is contained in
T_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq k
.

Definition 4
.

Define the
covering total variation

\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})=\inf_{{\mathbb{T}}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathcal{T}_{T_{i}}(\mathscr{N}),

where the infimum is over all covers
\mathbb{T}
of
G
by spanning trees.

\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
is the most principled notion of total variation. The proxy
\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
retains important features of
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
that encode edgewise differences of distributional signals while taking the entire graph
G
into consideration. Moreover,
\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
can be readily estimated using more familiar quantities.

For
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
, let
\bm{\mu}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the graph signal of probability weights of
s\in{\mathbb{S}}
, i.e.,
\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)
is the probability weight of
\mu_{i}
at
s
. We can also stack these signals as a matrix
{\bm{X}}_{\mathscr{N}}
whose columns are
\bm{\mu}_{s}
. Based on the GSP version of total variation (cf.
Eq.

1
), we introduce two more versions of total variation that are easy to compute.

Definition 5
.

Given
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
, we define the
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
versions of total variations as:

•

\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})=\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}|\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)-\bm{\mu}_{s}(j)|
, and

•

\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})=\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\big(\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)-\bm{\mu}_{s}(j)\big)^{2}=\Tr({\bm{X}}_{\mathscr{N}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}{\bm{X}}_{\mathscr{N}})
, where
\Tr
is the matrix trace.

The complexity of computing either
\mathcal{T}_{G,1}
or
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}
is at most
\mathcal{O}(|{\mathbb{S}}||{\mathcal{E}}|)
, and it involves only matrix multiplication for
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}
. In addition,
\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G},\mathcal{T}_{G,1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}
satisfy the following relation.

Theorem 1
.

Given
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})

\displaystyle\leq 2\min\big(\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N}),\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\big)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq c_{2}\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are constants independent of
\mathscr{N}
.

The discussion and proof of a more general result can be found in
Appendix

C
. The upshot is that if we expect
\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
to be small for some
\mathscr{N}
, then so are necessarily
\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})
and
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})
. In view of computation cost, we mainly use
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}
in the design of the regularization model in
Section

III-C
. It is discussed in many recent works regarding unfavorable side effects of signal smoothness (cf.
Section

III-D
). However, our point of view is that the smoothness of “distributional graph signals” might be a worthy alternative. This is conceptually different from classical Laplacian-based regularization.

III-B

Non-uniformity of distributional graph signals

As discussed in
Section

II-C
, a base GNN model may output a matrix
{\bm{X}}
(in (
2
)), with associated
\mathscr{N}_{{\bm{X}}}=\{\mu_{{\bm{X}},i},1\leq i\leq n\}
. For node classification problems, it is desirable that there is less ambiguity in the decision for each node so that one can pinpoint the correct class label. Mathematically, this requires that each
\mu_{{\bm{X}},i}
deviates from the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}({\mathbb{S}})
on the finite set of label classes
{\mathbb{S}}
, measured by the Wasserstein metric
W(\mu_{{\bm{X}},i},\mathcal{U}({\mathbb{S}}))
. The following result is proved in
Appendix

C
.

Lemma 1
.

For a fixed sequence of non-positive numbers
(a_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, there is a constant
C
independent of
{\bm{X}}
such that

\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})+C\geq 2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}W(\mu_{{\bm{X}},i},\mathcal{U}({\mathbb{S}}))^{2},

(5)

where
{\bm{D}}
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
(a_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
. Moreover,
\Tr({\bm{X}}_{o}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}_{o})\leq\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})\leq\Tr({\bm{X}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}}_{u})
, where
{\bm{X}}_{o}
is a matrix with each row a one-hot vector and
{\bm{X}}_{u}
is the matrix with each entry
1/|{\mathbb{S}}|
.

As we want each
\mu_{{\bm{X}},i}
to deviate from the uniform distribution, the right-hand side of
Eq.

5
should be made small (as negative as possible). This is ensured if the proxy
\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})
, which is easy to compute, is small. Moreover, we notice that
{\bm{X}}_{u}
(resp.
{\bm{X}}_{o}
) corresponds to uniform (resp.
\delta
) marginal distributions. The second half of the statement suggests that minimizing
\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})
may drive marginals, i.e., rows of
{\bm{X}}
, to comply with non-uniformity. Numerical experiments in
Section

IV-B
support that this proxy works well. We use this term in conjunction with
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}
introduced in
Section

III-A
in our proposed regularized model.

Regarding the numbers
(a_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, we propose to assign a larger number to a node with a larger degree. The reason is that we would like to promote the non-uniformity of large degree nodes, as each such node can influence more nodes during message passing.

III-C

The loss function with regularization

Suppose we are given a base GNN model, denoted by
\mathcal{M}
. Let
{\bm{F}}
be the matrix of input feature vectors and
\Theta
be the parameter space for
\mathcal{M}
. Assume
\mathcal{M}
has a loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}
, and the model is set to solve the optimization problem
\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{F}},\theta)
.

Consider the steps given in
Eq.

2
. We have
{\bm{X}}=\phi({\bm{O}})
viewed as a matrix of probability weights. For regularization, we introduce another loss
\mathcal{L}_{0}
to supplement
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}
.
Sections

III-A
and
III-B
suggest that
\mathcal{L}_{0}
should consist of two parts
\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2}
. The loss
\mathcal{L}_{1}
(cf. Definition
5
) is related to the smoothness of the distributional graph signal
{\bm{X}}
and takes the form
\mathcal{L}_{1}({\bm{X}})=\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{L}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}{\bm{X}})=\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}({\bm{D}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}-{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}){\bm{X}})
.
On the other hand,
\mathcal{L}_{2}
prevents the distributional signal from being uniform and can be explicitly expressed as
\mathcal{L}_{2}({\bm{X}})=\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})
for a suitably chosen negative semi-definite diagonal matrix
{\bm{D}}
(cf.
1
). Summing up
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{0}({\bm{X}})=\Tr\big({\bm{X}}^{\top}({\bm{D}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}-{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}+{\bm{D}}){\bm{X}}\big)
. In our experiments, we take
{\bm{D}}={\bm{I}}_{n}-{\bm{D}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
and obtain the easily computable loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}({\bm{X}})=\Tr\big({\bm{X}}^{\top}({\bm{I}}_{n}-{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}){\bm{X}}\big).

In summary, if we express the output of the second last layer of
\mathcal{M}
as
{\bm{O}}=\psi({\bm{F}},\theta)
of input
{\bm{F}}
and model parameters
\theta
, then the regularized model R-
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{M}
solves the optimization:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{\text{R-}\mathcal{M}}({\bm{F}},\theta)=\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{M}}({\bm{F}},\theta)+\eta\cdot\mathcal{L}_{0}\big(\phi\circ\psi({\bm{F}},\theta)\big),

(6)

where the coefficient
\eta
is a tunable hyperparameter and
\circ
denotes function composition. In the regularized model R-
\mathcal{M}
, we do not make any other changes to the based model
\mathcal{M}
apart from using the new loss function
\mathcal{L}_{\text{R-}\mathcal{M}}
during training. A schematic illustration is shown in
Fig.

3
(a).

, and in (b) an R-module that can be used at different places in GNN models.

III-D

Further discussions

Most regularization methods introduce a penalty to supplement the base model loss. For example, in the recent work, P-reg
[
12
]
proposes to apply a propagation matrix (e.g., the normalized adjacency matrix) to the output features. The model penalizes large discrepancies, measured by a metric such as squared error distance, between the original and transformed features. In LEreg
[
21
]
, intra-energy and inter-energy losses are introduced. Both are variants of the total variation
Eq.

1
. The novelty lies in introducing a “merged graph” with each node representing a whole label class. All these works are related to the smoothness prior used in GSP theory (
Section

II-A
). In this paper, we take a fundamentally different view of graph signals by
treating a distribution as a signal
. This allows a
principled
and
straightforward
adaption of existing GSP approaches, as long as we have suitable notions of the signal’s total variation. Moreover, non-uniformity does not have a counterpart for ordinary graph signals, which are essentially delta distributions.

Compared to GSP as well as other regularization methods such as P-reg and LEreg, a salient feature of our method is that the matrix
{\bm{I}}_{n}-{\bm{A}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}
in
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is in general not positive semi-definite. Therefore, the infimum of
\mathcal{L}_{0}({\bm{X}})
can be
-\infty
if the domain of the entries of
{\bm{X}}
is unbounded. This is another reason why restricting to distributional graph signals is essential.

The proposed regularization is primarily for node classification as distributional graph signals can be interpreted as the likelihoods of class labels. However, the method can be extended to other tasks through an
R-module
(
Fig.

3
(b)) that consists of the following steps:

•

Apply
\phi
(e.g.,
\mathrm{softmax}
) that turns a feature
{\bm{X}}
into a matrix of probability weights
\phi({\bm{X}})
.

•

Plug
\phi({\bm{X}})
in the loss
\eta_{{\bm{X}}}\mathcal{L}_{0}
with tunable coefficient
\eta_{{\bm{X}}}
.

Given a base model, multiple R-modules can be inserted at different places of the model pipeline, and all the losses are combined with the original loss of the model. The insight is that useful node features for graph learning tasks should be inherently associated with smooth and non-uniform distributional graph signals. We demonstrate this approach with link prediction and graph classification tasks in
Appendix

B
.

More on related works.

Though it is argued in
[
12
]
and supported by our evidence in
Section

II-B
that Laplacian regularization may have its drawbacks, it has achieved a certain degree of success in earlier works
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. These methods are based on the insight that neighboring nodes are likely to have the same labels. In the language of GSP, they intend to leverage the smoothness of the class label graph signal.

On the other hand, though graph signal smoothness has been fundamental in both GSP and GNNs, the negative effect of over-smoothing has also been examined
[
25
,
26
]
) and models are proposed to alleviate it. For example, apart from the more recent works such as
[
12
,
21
]
that have already been discussed in detail, PairNorm proposed in
[
27
]
encourages the similarity between connected nodes, and at the same time adds a negative term based on distances between disconnected pairs. MADReg in
[
28
]
proposes to use step size limits to make the graph nodes receive less interference noise. In
[
29
]
, randomly dropping nodes is proposed to reduce the convergence speed of over-smoothing. Adding skip connections is also introduced in
[
30
,
31
]
.

In our paper, we take a different point of view by not considering the smoothness of ordinary graph signals. Instead, we speculate that properties such as smoothness and non-uniformity of distributional graph signals may play important roles. Moreover, requiring a distributional graph signal to satisfy non-uniformity partially prevents the unfavorable situation that many connected nodes have similar marginal distributions that are approximately uniform. This can be viewed as a countermeasure to over-smoothing intrinsically contained in our approach.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce the notion of distributional graph signals and total variations that measure the smoothness of such signals. The method is analogous to the regularization method of a smooth signal prior in GSP.

Appendix C
Proofs of theoretical results

In this appendix, we discuss and prove a general result that implies
1
. In addition, we also prove
1
. We start with a computation of the Wasserstein distance.

Suppose
{\mathbb{S}}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}\}
is a finite discrete set and
d
is the discrete metric on
{\mathbb{S}}
. For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
, let
(\mu(s_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n}
and
(\nu(s_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n}
be their respective probability weights.

Lemma 2
.

\displaystyle W(\mu,\nu)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}|\mu(s_{i})-\nu(s_{i})|.

Proof.

The result is known
[
18
]
. For completeness, we provide a self-contained elementary proof. Let
\gamma=\big(\gamma(s_{i},s_{j})\big)_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
be in
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
. We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\gamma(s_{i},s_{j})d(s_{i},s_{j})^{2}\\
&=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq m}\gamma(s_{i},s_{j})\\
&=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left(\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\gamma(s_{i},s_{j})-\gamma(s_{i},s_{i})\right)\\
&=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left(\mu(s_{i})-\gamma(s_{i},s_{i})\right)\\
&\geq\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\left(\mu(s_{i})-\min(\mu(s_{i}),\nu(s_{i})\right).\end{split}

(7)

As
W(\mu,\nu)^{2}
is defined by taking the infimum of the left-hand side over all
\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
, we have
W(\mu,\nu)^{2}\geq\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\big(\mu(s_{i})-\min(\mu(s_{i}),\nu(s_{i})\big)
. By the same argument, we also have
W(\mu,\nu)^{2}\geq\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\big(\nu(s_{i})-\min(\mu(s_{i}),\nu(s_{i})\big)
. Summing up these two inequalities, we have

\displaystyle 2W(\mu,\nu)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}\big(\mu(s_{i})+\nu(s_{i})-2\min(\mu(s_{i}),\nu(s_{i})\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}|\mu(s_{i})-\nu(s_{i})|.

Therefore, to prove the lemma, it suffices to show that there is a
\gamma
such that
\gamma(s_{i},s_{i})=\min\big(\mu(s_{i}),\nu(s_{i})\big)
. For this, we prove a slightly more general claim: if non-negative numbers
(x_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
and
(y_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
satisfy
\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}x_{i}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}y_{i}=a
, then there are non-negative
(z_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m}
such that
\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}z_{i,j}=x_{i},1\leq i\leq m
,
\sum_{1\leq i\leq m}z_{i,j}=y_{j},1\leq j\leq m
, and
z_{i,i}=\min(x_{i},y_{i}),1\leq i\leq m
.

We prove this by induction on
m
. The case for
m=1
is trivially true by taking
z_{1,1}=x_{1}=y_{1}
. For
m\geq 2
, without loss of generality, we assume that
x_{1}\geq y_{1}
and
x_{2}\leq y_{2}
. Then we choose
z_{1,1}=y_{1}
,
z_{2,2}=x_{2}
,
z_{1,j}=0,1<j\leq m
and
z_{i,2}=0,1\leq i\neq 2\leq m
. As a result, we form another two sequences of non-negative numbers
x_{1}-y_{1},x_{3},\ldots,x_{m}
and
y_{2}-x_{2},y_{3},\ldots,y_{m}
with both summing to
a-x_{2}-y_{1}
. By the induction hypothesis, we are able to find non-negative
(z^{\prime}_{i,j})_{1\leq i,j\leq m-1}
for the two new sequences of length
m-1
each. It suffices to let
z_{i,j}=z^{\prime}_{i-1,j-1}
for
i>1
or
j>2
and
z_{i,1}=z^{\prime}_{i-1,1}
for
i>1
(illustrated in
Fig.

6
). This proves the claim and hence the lemma.

∎

To state and prove a general form of
1
, we need to introduce a few more notions. We fix marginal distributions
\mathscr{N}=\{\mu_{i}:1\leq i\leq n\}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}})
. For any pair of nodes
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
and

, define

\displaystyle\rho_{i,j}(s)=\mu_{j}(s)/\mu_{i}(s)\text{ if }\mu_{j}(s)\leq\mu_{i}(s)\text{ and }1\text{ otherwise.}

(8)

More generally, if
P=(v_{i_{0}},\ldots,v_{i_{l}})
is a directed path on
{\mathcal{G}}
from
v_{i_{0}}
to
v_{i_{l}}
, then

\displaystyle\rho_{P}(s)=\prod_{0\leq j<l}\rho_{i_{j},i_{j+1}}(s).

It is important to point out that
\rho_{P}
can be computed directly as long as
\mathscr{N}
is given.

In the graph
{\mathcal{G}}
, suppose
{\mathcal{H}}
is a spanning tree and
v_{0}
is a fixed (root) node. Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
be the edge set of
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{E}}\backslash{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}
. For each edge
e=(v_{i},v_{j})
, let
P_{i}
(resp.
P_{j}
) be the unique path on
{\mathcal{H}}
connecting
v_{0}
and
v_{i}
(resp.
v_{j}
). Moreover,
v_{0}
is an endpoint of
P_{i}\cap P_{j}
, and let
v_{k}
be the other endpoint of
P_{i}\cap P_{j}
. Denote by
Q_{i}
(resp.
Q_{j}
) be the direct path (on
{\mathcal{H}}
) from
v_{k}
to
v_{i}
(resp.
v_{j}
) (see
Fig.

7
). We introduce

\displaystyle\mathfrak{t}_{i,j}(s)=\mu_{i}(s)+\mu_{j}(s)-2\mu_{k}(s)\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s).

(9)

Definition 6
.

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})=\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\mathfrak{t}_{i,j}(s).

Q_{i}
and

We can compute the special case where
{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{H}
is a tree. Notice that for an edge
e=(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}
directed from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, then
v_{k}=v_{i}
and
Q_{i}=\{v_{i}\}
,
Q_{j}=e
and
\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)=1
. If
\mu_{i}(s)\geq\mu_{j}(s)
, then
2\mu_{k}(s)\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)=2\mu_{i}(s)\cdot\mu_{j}(s)/\mu_{i}(s)=2\mu_{j}(s)
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(s)

\displaystyle+\mu_{j}(s)-2\mu_{k}(s)\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}(s)-\mu_{j}(s)=|\mu_{i}(s)-\mu_{j}(s)|.

The case
\mu_{i}(s)<\mu_{j}(s)
is similar, and in summary

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:mmm}\begin{split}\mathfrak{t}_{i,j}(s)&=\mu_{i}(s)+\mu_{j}(s)-2\mu_{k}(s)\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)\\
&=|\mu_{i}(s)-\mu_{j}(s)|\end{split}

(10)

Therefore, for any
v_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})&=\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}|\mu_{i}(s)-\mu_{j}(s)|\\
&=\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}|\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)-\bm{\mu}_{s}(j)|=\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N}),\end{split}

(11)

where
\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)
is same as
\mu_{i}(s)
(cf.
Section

).

Following the notations in
Section

III-A
, we have the following generalization of
1
.

Theorem 2
.

For any spanning tree
{\mathcal{H}}
of
{\mathcal{G}}
and root node
v_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq 2\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N}).

Before proving the result, we remark that although
\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})
is defined in a convoluted way, it can however be computed directly given
\mathscr{N},\mathcal{H}
and
v_{0}
. Therefore, the result gives computable upper and lower bounds of
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
.

Proof.

As
|\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)-\bm{\mu}_{s}(j)|\leq 1
, it is trivially true that
\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})
.

To show
\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq 2\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
, we first claim that
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})=\inf_{\mu\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})}\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu)
is achieved for some
\mu_{0}\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
. The map
\alpha:\Gamma(\mathscr{N})\to\mathbb{R},\mu\mapsto\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu)
is continuous. On the other hand,
\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
is a compact subset of a Euclidean space. This is because
\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{S}}^{n})
is a bounded subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m^{n}}
with the components corresponding to weights of the joint distribution. Moreover,
\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
is closed because the condition to have the prescribed marginal distributions is a set of linear conditions. By the extreme value theorem,
\inf\alpha
is achieved for some
\mu_{0}\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
. For each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}
, let
\mu_{0,i,j}
be the marginal distribution of the pair
(v_{i},v_{j})
. The marginals of
\mu_{0,i,j}
are
\mu_{i}
and
\mu_{j}
at
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
respectively. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu_{0})=\int\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\int d({\bm{x}}(i),{\bm{x}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{0,i,j}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Def.}~\ref{defn:fmm}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})^{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem.}~\ref{lem:wmn}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}|\bm{\mu}_{s}(i)-\bm{\mu}_{s}(j)|=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N}).

We now prove
2\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})
. Given a spanning tree
\mathcal{H}
and node
v_{0}
, we construct
\mu_{\mathcal{H},v_{0}}\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
using ideas from the theory of Bayesian networks
[
45
]
as follows. We make
\mathcal{H}
directed by requiring that each edge is pointed away from the (root) node
v_{0}
. As a consequence, each node has at most
1
incoming edge. Let
{\bm{x}}=({\bm{x}}_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be the random vector with
{\bm{x}}_{i}
the (random) label at
v_{i}
. For each directed edge
(v_{i},v_{j})
in
\mathcal{H}
, let
\mu_{i,j}\in\Gamma(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})
be a distribution that realizes
W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})
, which give a conditional probability weights

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\{p_{i,j}(s,s^{\prime}):s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}\}

\displaystyle=\{p({\bm{x}}_{j}=s^{\prime}\mid{\bm{x}}_{i}=s):s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{S}}\}.

By
[
45
]
Section 8.1 (8.5), there is a distribution
\mu_{\mathcal{H},v_{0}}\in\Gamma(\mathscr{N})
such that its marginal for each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}
is
\mu_{i,j}
. For each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
, let
\mu_{i,j}^{\prime}
be the marginal of
\mu_{\mathcal{H},v_{0}}
to the pair
(v_{i},v_{j})
. As
\mu_{i,j}
realizes
W(\mu_{i},\mu_{j})
, by
2
,
\mu_{i,j}(s,s)=\min(\mu_{i}(s),\mu_{j}(s))
for each
s\in{\mathbb{S}}
. In particular,
p_{i,j}(s,s)=\rho_{i,j}(s)
(cf. (
8
)). More generally, if
P
is a directed path in
\mathcal{H}
from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, then the following inequality holds

\displaystyle p_{i,j}(s,s)\geq\rho_{P}(s).

(12)

According to the definition, we have
\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mu_{\mathcal{H},v_{0}})=\mathcal{S}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}}+\mathcal{S}_{{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}}
. The summand

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}}=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}}\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i,j}({\bm{y}})

is the summation over the edges of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}
, while

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}}=\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}}\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i,j}^{\prime}({\bm{y}})

is the summand over
{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
. For
\mathcal{S}_{{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}}
, we have seen that for each edge
(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{i,j}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem.}~\ref{lem:wmn}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}|\mu_{i}(s)-\mu_{j}(s)|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:mmm})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\big(\mu_{i}(s)+\mu_{j}(s)-2\mu_{k}(s)\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)\big),

where the right-hand-side is the term
\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{t}_{i,j}(s)
(cf. (
9
)) that corresponds to
(v_{i},v_{j})
in
\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})
.

Consider
(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}
and we first notice the following identity:

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\int d({\bm{y}}(i),{\bm{y}}(j))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}_{i,j}({\bm{y}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:sum})}}{{=}}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{S}}}\mu_{i}(s)+\mu_{j}(s)-2\mu^{\prime}_{i,j}(s,s).

To show the summand is bounded by
\mathfrak{t}_{i,j}(s)
, it suffices to show
\mu_{i,j}^{\prime}(s,s)
is bounded below by
\rho_{Q_{i}}(S)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)\mu_{k}(s)
, with
v_{k}
and paths
Q_{i},Q_{j}
be as in (
9
). We estimate using the construction of
\mu_{i,j}^{\prime}
based on the method of Bayesian network (on directed
\mathcal{H}
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}_{i,j}(s,s)

\displaystyle\geq p({\bm{x}}_{i}=s,{\bm{x}}_{j}=s,{\bm{x}}_{k}=s)

\displaystyle=p({\bm{x}}_{i}=s,{\bm{x}}_{j}=s\mid{\bm{x}}_{k}=s)\mu_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=p_{k,i}(s,s)p_{k,j}(s,s)\mu_{k}(s)

\displaystyle\geq\rho_{Q_{i}}(s)\rho_{Q_{j}}(s)\mu_{k}(s).

For the last equality, we use the fact that
Q_{i}\cap Q_{j}=\{v_{k}\}
; and hence
{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j}
are independent given
{\bm{x}}_{k}
by the Bayesian network construction. The last line follows from (
12
). Consequently, the inequality that
2\mathcal{T}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})
follows.
∎

If we examine the formula of
\mathcal{T}_{G,{\mathcal{H}},v_{0}}(\mathscr{N})
(by changing summation order), it can be decomposed into two parts
\sum_{(v_{i},v_{j})\in{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathcal{H}}}
and

. The former is essentially

of
\mathscr{N}
on the tree

. This is the reason that
2
implies
1
. Formally we have the following.

Proof of
1
.

For
G=(V,E)
of size
n
, recall
K_{n}
is the complete graph on
V
and the complement
\overline{G}
of
G
is obtained from
K_{n}
by removing edges in
E
. Let
\omega(G)
be the clique number of
\overline{G}
, i.e., the number of vertices in a largest clique of
\overline{G}
. Let
c_{1}=n-\omega(G)
,
c_{3}=\sqrt{|{\mathbb{S}}|n}
and
c_{2}=c_{1}c_{3}
. Notice that
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
are independent of
\mathscr{N}
.

It suffices to prove the following inequalities

•

\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq 2\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
;

•

2\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq c_{1}\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})
;

•

\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq c_{3}\mathcal{T}_{G,2}(\mathscr{N})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Let
{\mathbb{T}}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{k}\}
be any cover of
G
by spanning trees. We have
\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(T_{i})=\sum_{1\leq i\leq k}2\mathcal{T}_{T_{i}}(\mathscr{N})
by
2
. As the inequality is true for any
{\mathbb{T}}
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})\leq 2\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})
.

For the second inequality, by the theory of tree cover numbers of graphs
[
46
, Prop. 2]
, there is a
{\mathbb{T}}=\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{c_{1}}\}
that covers
G
. Therefore, by
2
,
2\mathcal{T}^{c}_{G}(\mathscr{N})\leq\sum_{1\leq i\leq c_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{T_{i}}(\mathscr{N})\leq c_{1}\mathcal{T}_{G,1}(\mathscr{N})
.

The last inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
∎

We end this section by proving
1
.

Proof of
1
.

Using
2
, we estimate

\displaystyle 2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}W(\mu_{{\bm{X}},i},\mathcal{U}({\mathbb{S}}))^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\left\lvert{\bm{X}}_{i,j}-\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}({\bm{X}}_{i,j}-\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}{\bm{X}}_{i,j}^{2}-2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\frac{{\bm{X}}_{i,j}}{|{\mathbb{S}}|}

\displaystyle+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})-2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|}+\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})-\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|}\Tr({\bm{D}}).

Therefore,
2\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}a_{i}W(\mu_{{\bm{X}},i},\mathcal{U}({\mathbb{S}}))^{2}\leq\Tr({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{D}}{\bm{X}})+C
with

, which is independent of
{\bm{X}}
.

The last statement of the lemma follows from the simple fact that for each

, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}\frac{1}{|{\mathbb{S}}|^{2}}\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq m}{\bm{X}}_{i,j}^{2}\leq 1.

∎
