A note on θ 2 \theta_{2}
David T. Nguyen

Abstract
. It is a classic result of Selberg in the 1950’s that θ 2 = 2 / 3 \theta_{2}=2/3 , where θ 2 \theta_{2} is the level of distribution of the divisor function in arithmetic progressions (defined more precisely below). Selberg applies this estimate, together with his Λ 2 \Lambda^{2} sieve, to prove weak forms of the binary Goldbach and twin prime conjectures. In this note, we give an unconditional proof of Selberg’s first result with smooth weights for prime moduli, and put forth a hypothesis via subconvexity to bootstrap to level 4 / 5 4/5 . Contingent outcomes of our proposal is an improvement of Selberg’s second result on approximations to twin primes and in lowering bounds on gaps between primes.

Introduction

Let
\tau_{2}(n)
denote the usual divisor function. For integers
m

denote the Kloosterman sums

(1.1)

S(m,n;q)=\sum_{h\overline{h}\equiv 1\pmod{q}}e^{2\pi i\frac{mh+n\overline{h}}{q}}

where

runs over a complete set of residue classes coprime to
q
. One knows that

(1.2)

|S(m,n;q)|\leq(m,n,q)^{1/2}q^{1/2}\tau_{2}(q),

where
(m,n,q)
denotes the G.C.D. of
m
,
n
, and
q
. As remarked by Selberg

1

see Footnote 64 on page 236 in
[
20
]
, the deepest part of (
1.2
) is when
q=p
is prime and is due to Weil
[
24
]
(1948) from his resolution of the Weil Conjectures for curves over finite fields, with the remaining cases follow from the earlier result of Salie
[
19
]
(1931) for prime powers
q=p^{\gamma},\gamma>1
, and multiplicative properties of
S(m,n;q)
. We will refer to (
1.2
) as Weil’s upper bound for complete Kloosterman sums, or Weil’s bound for Kloosterman sums for short, or, simply, Weil’s bound.

A classic unpublished result of Selberg from the 1950’s states that Weil’s upper bound for complete Kloosterman sums yields

Theorem A

(Selberg, 1950’s)
.

For
(a,q)=1
and
q<X^{2}
we have for any
\epsilon>0
, that

(1.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n)=X\frac{\varphi(q)}{q^{2}}\left(\log X+2\gamma+2\sum_{p\mid q}\frac{\log p}{p-1}\right)^{2}+O\left(q^{-1/4}X^{1/2+\epsilon}\right).

Hooley had obtained in
[
9

a related result but only for the leading order main term with a
\log X
saving in the error term. The error term above is
\ll q^{-1}X^{1-\delta}
when

(1.4)

q\ll X^{\frac{2}{3}-2\delta}.

The number
2/3
in the exponent of the above is called level of distribution of
\tau_{2}
, denoted by
\theta_{2}
. More precisely, we say that the divisor function has a level of distribution
\theta_{2}
if for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that

(1.5)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n)=\frac{X}{\varphi(q)}P_{2}(X,q)+O_{\delta}\left(\frac{X^{1-\delta}}{\varphi(q)}\right)

uniformly for all
(a,q)=1
and
q\leq X^{\theta_{2}-\epsilon}
, where
P_{2}(X,q)
is a linear polynomial in
\log X
with coefficients depending on
q
. We note the bound
1/\varphi(q)\ll(\log\log q)/q
. Thus, Selberg’s (
1.3
) gives
\theta_{2}=2/3
.

The bound (
1.3
) has applications towards a weak form of the twin prime and Goldbach conjectures (see Section
1.1
below), the classical correlation sum
\sum_{n\leq X}\tau_{2}(n)\tau_{2}(n+h)
, and, hence, to moments of
L
-functions. Thus, extending (
1.3
) beyond the two-thirds barrier is an important problem in number theory.

There are two published proofs of (
1.3
) with power-saving error terms, to our knowledge, that we now briefly review. The first proof that appeared in print seems to be from Heath-Brown in his 1979 paper
The Fourth Power Moment of the Riemann Zeta Function

[
7
]
. More precisely, Heath-Brown proved in
[

, and any

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n)=Xq^{-2}\left(A(q,a)(\log x/q^{2})+2\gamma-1+2B(q,a)\right)\ll X^{1/3+\epsilon},

uniformly for
1\leq q\leq X^{2/3}
, where

(1.7)

A(q,a)=\sum_{d\mid(q,a)}\sum_{\delta\mid q/d}d\delta\mu(q/d\delta)

(1.8)

The main terms in (

on the left half plane, and yields (c.f.
[
7

F(s;q,a)\ll(1+|t|)^{1-2\sigma}q^{\frac{1}{2}-2\sigma+\epsilon},\quad(\sigma<0).

Heath-Brown’s motivation for proving (
1.6
) was to obtain the finer full main term with a power-saving error term for the forth moment of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line.

The second published proof of (

n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n)\frac{dt}{t},(0<\delta<1),

and

(1.12)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{X}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq t\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a(q)\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n)\log\frac{X}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{-q/2<m,n\leq q/2}S(m,na;q)\int_{1}^{X}S_{t}(m/q)S_{X/t}(n/q)\frac{dt}{t},

1.1.
Why do people study
\theta_{2}
?

As touched upon earlier, one of Heath-Brown’s motivations for proving (
1.6
) was application to moments of the Riemann zeta functions on the critical line, which are approximations to the still unproven Lindelöf Hypothesis for
\zeta(s)
. As for Selberg, one of his motivations to proving Theorem
A
was applications of his
\Lambda^{2}
sieve towards the twin primes and Goldbach problem, which we now provide further details.

Selberg applied the bound (
1.3
) as a crucial technical ingredient to obtained in
[
20
, eq. (23.38), p. 244]
the following weak form of the twin prime problem.

Theorem B

(Selberg, 1990)
.

We have, for
X
sufficiently large,

(1.15)

\#\left\{n\leq X|\text{one of $n$ and $n+2$ has at most 2, the other at most 3 primes factors}\right\}\gg\frac{X}{\log^{2}X}.

The number of prime factors 2 and 3 from the above result depend on the level
\theta_{2}
in the following way. The
\Lambda^{2}
sieve applied to the twin prime problem gives an inequality of the form

(1.16)

\frac{4}{3}(2^{\nu(n)})+\frac{2}{3}(2^{\nu(n+2)})<\lambda_{2}+\epsilon

where

(1.17)

\lambda_{2}=\frac{8}{\theta_{2}}+2,

in which (
1.16
) holds for
\gg X/\log^{2}X
number of
n\leq X
, where
\nu(n)
denotes the number of prime factors of
n
counted with multiplicities. For Selberg’s
\theta_{2}=2/3
, (
1.17
) yields

(1.18)

\lambda_{2}=14,

which implies either

(1.19)

\{\nu(n)\leq 2\text{ and }\nu_{2}(n+2)\leq 3\}\quad\text{or}\quad\{\nu(n)\leq 3\text{ and }\nu_{2}(n+2)\leq 2\},

as both

(1.20)

\frac{4}{3}(2^{2})+\frac{2}{3}(2^{3})=\frac{32}{3}\approx 10.67\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{4}{3}(2^{3})+\frac{2}{3}(2^{2})=\frac{40}{3}\approx 13.33

are less than 14. To improve on this result of Selberg and eliminate the possibility
\nu(n)=3
and
\nu(n+2)=2
, the value of
\lambda_{2}
needs to be brought down to a number smaller than
40/3\approx 13.33
. Our method here could be used to sharpen this constant
\lambda_{2}
from
14
to
12
, thus eliminates this possibility; see the Applications section
2.1
following the statement of Theorem
2
below for more on this.

In
[
16
]
, the author showed numerically square-root cancellation in the error term of the classical shifted convolution

(1.21)

\sum_{n\leq X}\tau_{2}(n)\tau_{2}(n+1)

using the the full asymptotic for

(1.22)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq X\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{2}(n).

A modified form of the correlation (
1.21
) for the 3-fold divisor function is studied in
[
18
]
and found to adequately serve some applications. In this note, we derive an explicit error term for a smoothed version of (
1.22
) for prime moduli, which, in particular, gives another proof of the known level
\theta_{2}=2/3
(see Theorem
1
), and propose a conjectural way via a Lindelöf hypothesis to break through this barrier in Theorem
2
.

Recently, Khan in
[
13
]
(2016) succeeded in breaking through this two-thirds barrier when the moduli is a prime power
p^{\gamma}
for odd primes
p
and any fixed integer
\gamma\geq 7
. In contrast to this result of Khan, in which the condition
\gamma
is fixed and
p
sufficiently large is required, there is a reciprocal result of Liu, Shparlinski, Zhang,
[
14
]
(2018), brought to our attention by I. Shparlinski, that also broke through this barrier and gave a uniform bound for all moduli of the type

, with

\gamma

(3.4)

L(1-s,\overline{\chi_{1}})=i^{\mathfrak{a}}\pi^{\frac{1}{2}-s}d^{s-1}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(s+\mathfrak{a})\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-s+\mathfrak{a})\right)}\overline{\tau(\chi_{1})}L(s,\chi_{1}),

where

(3.5)

\mathfrak{a}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }\chi_{1}(-1)=1,\\
1,&\text{if }\chi_{1}(-1)=-1.\end{cases}

Proof.

By equation (9.14) on page 71 of Davenport, we have

(3.6)

\xi(1-s,\overline{\chi}_{1})=\frac{i^{\mathfrak{a}}d^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi_{1})}\xi(s,\chi_{1}),

where

(3.7)

\xi(s,\chi_{1})=(\pi/d)^{-\frac{1}{2}(s+\mathfrak{a})}\Gamma\left[\frac{1}{2}(s+\mathfrak{a})\right]L(s,\chi_{1}).

The equation (

\frac{d^{1/2}}{\tau(\chi_{1})}=\frac{\overline{\tau(\chi_{1})}}{d^{1/2}}.

In applying the functional equation, the arguments of the gamma factors take slightly different shape depending if the character is odd or even. We need the following lemma to sum over the different cases.

Lemma 3
.

We have

(3.9)

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\chi_{1}\pmod{p}}\chi_{1}(A)\overline{\chi_{1}}(B)=\begin{cases}p-2,&\text{ if }A\equiv B\pmod{p},\\
-1,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\flat}}{\sum}_{\chi_{1}\pmod{p}}\chi_{1}(A)\overline{\chi_{1}}(B)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}p-2,&\text{ if }A\equiv\pm B\pmod{p},\\
-2,&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{\sharp}}{\sum}_{\chi_{1}\pmod{p}}\chi_{1}(A)\overline{\chi_{1}}(B)=\frac{1}{2}\begin{cases}p-1,&\text{ if }A\equiv B\pmod{p},\\
1-p,&\text{ if }A\equiv-B\pmod{p},\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

where the
\flat
and
\sharp
denote summing over even and odd primitive characters, respectively. Here,
A\equiv\pm B\pmod{p}
means that either
A\equiv B\pmod{p}
or
A\equiv-B\pmod{p}
.

Proof.

The relation (
3.9
) follows from summing over all characters then subtracting off contribution from the principal character.

Next, we have

(3.12)

\frac{1+\chi(-1)}{2}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if $\chi(-1)=1$ is even,}\\
0,&\text{ if $\chi(-1)=-1$ is odd.}\end{cases}

With the above, the left side of (
3.10
) is equal to

(3.13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sideset{}{{}^{*}}{\sum}_{\chi_{1}\pmod{p}}\chi_{1}(A)\overline{\chi_{1}}(B)\left(\frac{1+\chi(-1)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{cases}\varphi(p),&\text{ if }A\equiv B\pmod{p}\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}-1\right)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{cases}\varphi(p),&\text{ if }A\equiv-B\pmod{p}\\
0,&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}-1.\right)

This leads to the right side of (
3.10
) after simplifications.

Similarly, by detecting odd primitive characters by

(3.15)

\frac{1-\chi(-1)}{2},

we obtain the relation (
3.11
).
∎

7.
Heuristics for Conjecture
1

In this last section, we give a heuristic argument based on a twisted second moment estimate which leads to Conjecture
1
. It is best read with a physics hat on.

By adding an extra variable, we may move the critical strip right to the region of absolute convergence so we can interchange order of summations and evaluate term by terms. This is the basic strategy. As such, the extra variable
w
in (
2.8
) does that, but for more flexibility, we separate variables in there, so that

) transforms into
