Abstract
Embodying the principle of simulation intelligence, digital twin (DT) systems construct and maintain a high-fidelity virtual model of a physical system. This paper focuses on ray tracing (RT), which is widely seen as an enabling technology for DTs of the radio access network (RAN) segment of next-generation disaggregated wireless systems. RT makes it possible to simulate channel conditions, enabling data augmentation and prediction-based transmission. However, the effectiveness of RT hinges on the adaptation of the electromagnetic properties assumed by the RT to actual channel conditions, a process known as calibration. The main challenge of RT calibration is the fact that small discrepancies in the geometric model fed to the RT software hinder the accuracy of the predicted phases of the simulated propagation paths. Existing solutions to this problem either rely on the channel power profile, hence disregarding phase information, or they operate on the channel responses by assuming the simulated phases to be sufficiently accurate for calibration. This paper proposes a novel channel response-based scheme that, unlike the state of the art, estimates and compensates for the phase errors in the RT-generated channel responses. The proposed approach builds on the variational expectation maximization algorithm with a flexible choice of the prior phase-error distribution that bridges between a deterministic model with no phase errors and a stochastic model with uniform phase errors. The algorithm is computationally efficient, and is demonstrated, by leveraging the open-source differentiable RT software available within the Sionna library, to outperform existing methods in terms of the accuracy of RT predictions.

I-C

Calibration of Ray Tracing

The geometric properties of the deployment scenario of interest can be generally determined up to a certain degree of precision based on in-situ measurements, e.g., using LiDAR
[
37
,
5
,
38
]
or neural surface reconstruction
[
39
]
, or based-on geo-spatial data, e.g., via the Open Street Maps database
[
28
]
.
However, in practical scenarios, it is not uncommon to observe small discrepancies, of the scale of a fraction of the carrier wavelength, between the ground-truth geometric properties and their virtual counterpart.
These discrepancies hinder the accuracy of the predicted phases of the simulated propagation paths, which are usually deemed to be unreliable
[
20
, Ch. 7.5]
.
Errors in the predicted phases can provide inaccurate interference patterns at the receiver when the temporal and/or spatial resolution of the system is not high enough to resolve each path individually, i.e., when the bandwidth and/or the antenna arrays are too small.

We distinguish two classes of existing calibration procedures, which make distinct assumptions on the
phase error
caused by imprecise geometric information.

•

Power profile-based
schemes aim to find the material parameters that yield the best match between the power-delay – or more generally power-angle-delay – profiles simulated by the RT and the corresponding power profiles measured from the real environment
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
Accordingly, power profile-based schemes disregard phase information, retaining solely the amplitudes of the measured and simulated channels.

•

Channel response-based
schemes find the material parameters that yield the best match between simulated and recorded channel frequency responses
[
13
]
.
The state of the art work
[
13
]
compares RT-generated and measured channel responses by relying on the phase provided by the RT to be sufficiently accurate to enable calibration.
We refer to this class of channel response-based methods as
phase error-oblivious
.
Such an approach adopts an opposite modelling assumption as compared to power profile-based methods, disregarding the presence of phase errors in the RT simulation.

This paper proposes a novel channel response-based scheme that, unlike the state of the art, compensates for the phase errors in the RT-generated channel responses prior to carrying out a comparison with measured channel responses during RT calibration.
The main idea is to use measured channel responses to estimate the phase error locally for each path and for each measurement.

Main Contributions

In this work, we present a novel channel response-based calibration scheme for RT that estimates and mitigates per-path phase errors.
Our specific contributions are as follows.

•

We introduce a novel
phase error-aware
calibration strategy for RT.
The proposed approach builds on the
variational expectation maximization
(VEM) algorithm
[
40
]
with a tailored choice of the variational distribution on the phase errors.
The algorithm is computationally efficient encompassing an expectation step in closed form and a maximization step via gradient descent.

•

Leveraging the open-source differentiable RT made available through the Sionna library
[
41
,
13
]
, we present a variety of experiments, evaluating calibration accuracy in terms of the estimated electromagnetic parameters, as well as of predicted power maps. The experiments confirm the significant advantages of the proposed phase error-aware calibration strategy as compared to existing power profile-based schemes and phase error-oblivious approaches.

II
Setting and Problem Definition

In this work, we study the problem of calibrating a DT-based RT using channel frequency response observations.
This section first describes the studied communication system and the RT output before introducing a stochastic phase-error channel model that accounts for the errors of the RT-predicted phases.
We end this section by specifying the available data and the calibration objective.

II-A

Setting

Consider a transmitter (Tx) located at coordinates
c^{\mathrm{Tx}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}
and a receiver (Rx) located at coordinates
c^{\mathrm{Rx}}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3}
in a three-dimensional Cartesian space.
The transmitter and receiver are equipped with antenna arrays of
N^{\mathrm{Tx}}
and
N^{\mathrm{Rx}}
antennas respectively.
Using the paths computed by the RT, the DT models the channel over a bandwidth
B=|f^{\mathrm{max}}-f^{\mathrm{min}}|
with end frequencies
f^{\mathrm{max}}>f^{\mathrm{min}}>0
and central carrier frequency
f^{c}=(f^{\mathrm{min}}+f^{\mathrm{max}})/2
[Hz].
Assuming multi-carrier transmission, the set of
S
subcarrier frequencies is
\{f_{s}\}_{s=1}^{S}
, where
f_{s}=f^{\mathrm{min}}+(s-1)\Delta f
is the frequency of the
s\mbox{-th}
subcarrier;
\Delta f
[Hz] is the subcarrier spacing; and
S=\left\lfloor(f^{\mathrm{max}}-f^{\mathrm{min}})/\Delta f\right\rfloor
is the total number of subcarriers.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
, the RT takes as input the geometric properties, specifying the shapes and positions of the objects in the scene, the coordinates
c=(c^{\mathrm{Rx}},c^{\mathrm{Tx}})
of the transmitter and receiver devices, and the materials properties, denoted as vector
\theta
, which includes the permittivity, conductivity, permeability, scattering coefficient and cross-polarization discrimination of the materials involved
[
30
,
22
,
23
]
.
Additional RT inputs not depicted in Fig.
1
are the antenna patterns of the devices
[
42
, Ch. 2.2]
, as well as the choice of a parameterized scattering model
[
22
]
.
Based on this information, the RT outputs the parameters of a number
P^{c}
of realizable propagation paths through specular and diffuse reflection, refraction, and diffraction
[
20
, Ch. 4]
,
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
.
Note that the RT is only able to consider a finite number of samples among the potentially infinite set diffusely scattered paths, whereas the finite set of specular components is treated exhaustively up to a pre-determined number of reflections or refractions.

In this work, we will only focus on the simulation of specular propagation paths, and the parameter vector
\theta
will encompass the permittivity, conductivity and permeability of the materials in the scene.
We leave the study of diffusely scattered components, along with the calibration of scattering coefficients and cross-polarization discrimination, for future work.
Accordingly, the RT output is assumed to be deterministic and given by the function

R(c|\theta)=\left\{\alpha^{c}_{p}(\theta),\tau_{p}^{c},\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{p},\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoA}}_{p}\right\}_{p=1}^{P^{c}},

(1)

which maps the input coordinates
c

into the parameters of
P^{c}
paths.
By (
1

p\in\{1,...,P^{c}\}
is described by a complex amplitude
\alpha^{c}_{p}(\theta)\in\mathbbm{C}
, a delay
\tau_{p}^{c}>0
[s], a pair of angles of departure
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{p}\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
, and a pair of angles of arrival
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoA}}_{p}\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
.
The angles of departure
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{p}=(\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{\mathrm{el},p},\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{\mathrm{az},p})\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
include the elevation
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{\mathrm{el},p}
and azimuth
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoD}}_{\mathrm{az},p}
angles from the transmitter perspective, and the angles of arrival
\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoA}}_{p}=(\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoA}}_{\mathrm{el},p},\Phi^{c,\mathrm{AoA}}_{\mathrm{az},p})\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
encompass the elevation-azimuth angles at the receiver, as illustrated in Fig.
3
.
In order to keep the notation concise, in what follows, we omit the position subscript
c
from the path parameters
R(c|\theta)
in (
1

P^{c}
.

For a given receiver and transmitter position
c=(c^{\mathrm{Rx}},c^{\mathrm{Tx}})
, signal propagation between each receive and transmit antenna pair is assumed to follow the same path parameters

, while differing only in terms of phase.
Accordingly, for each simulated path
p\in\{1,...,P\}
, phase offsets between neighbouring antenna elements are modeled as a

, where the functions

For instance, in the far-field regime, the steering vectors

a^{\mathrm{Rx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})=\begin{pmatrix}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{v}f^{c}(d^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{1})^{\top}u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})},\ldots,e^{j\frac{2\pi}{v}f^{c}(d^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{N^{\mathrm{Rx}}})^{\top}u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},

(2)

where

m/s denotes the speed of light, while
d^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 1}
is the position of the
n\mbox{-th}
receive array element and
u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 1}
is a unitary-norm vector pointing in the direction specified by
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p}
, both expressed in the local coordinates of the receiver (see Fig.
3

The transmit steering vector
a^{\mathrm{Tx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})

a^{\mathrm{Tx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})=\begin{pmatrix}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{v}f^{c}(d^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{1})^{\top}u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})},\ldots,e^{j\frac{2\pi}{v}f^{c}(d^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{N^{\mathrm{Tx}}})^{\top}u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})}\end{pmatrix}^{\top},

(3)

where
d^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 1}
is the position of the
n\mbox{-th}
transmit array element, and
u(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})\in\mathbbm{R}^{3\times 1}
denotes the direction specified by
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p}
, in the local transmitter coordinate system (see Fig.
3
).
Furthermore, for curved wavefronts in near-field regimes, the steering vectors can be approximated using a parabolic wavefront model

43
]
.
Alternatively, it is also possible to determine the vectors
a^{\mathrm{Rx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})

II-B

Deterministic Channel Model

Using the multipath parameters
R(c|\theta)
produced by the RT in (
1
), the DT can reconstruct the frequency response of each path component
p\in\{1,...,P\}
for each subcarrier frequency
f_{s}
as a
N^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1
vector of complex numbers, one for each of the transmit-receive antenna pairs.

In particular, the frequency response is given by

\alpha_{p}(\theta)e^{-j2\pi f_{s}\tau_{p}}a^{\mathrm{Rx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})\otimes{a^{\mathrm{Tx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})}^{*},

(4)

where
{\{\cdot\}}^{*}
denotes the element-wise conjugate operation, and
\otimes
represents the Kronecker product
[
44
]
.
Accordingly, each path is characterized by a magnitude
|\alpha_{p}(\theta)|
and by a phase
(\angle\alpha_{p}(\theta)-2\pi f_{s}\tau_{p})
dependent on the propagation delay
\tau_{p}
and on the phase
\angle\alpha_{p}(\theta)
of the complex amplitude
\alpha_{p}(\theta)
, in addition to the antenna-dependent contributions of the steering vectors.

Expanding from the per-subcarrier formulation in (
4
), we collect the contributions of the
p\mbox{-th}
path across all
S
subcarriers into a single
SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1
vector
\alpha_{p}(\theta)a(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})
, where

a(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})=w(\tau_{p})\otimes a^{\mathrm{Rx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})\otimes{a^{\mathrm{Tx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})}^{*},

(5)

represents the phase contributions emanating from all possible combinations of antenna pairs, represented by the
N^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1
vector
a^{\mathrm{Rx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})\otimes{a^{\mathrm{Tx}}(\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p})}^{*}
, and subcarriers, represented by the
S\times 1
vector

w(\tau_{p})=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-j2\pi f_{1}\tau_{p}},...,e^{-j2\pi f_{S}\tau_{p}}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}.

(6)

The deterministic frequency response model assumed by the DT is then obtained by summing the contributions of each path
p\in\{1,...,P\}
into the
SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1
vector

\hat{H}(c|\theta)=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\alpha_{p}(\theta)a(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p}),

(7)

where the “hat” notation is a reminder that this is an estimate produced by the DT on the basis of the RT output.

II-C

Phase Error Channel Model

The estimate
\hat{H}(c|\theta)
in (
7
) hinges on the assumption that the geometric properties provided by the DT are almost identical to the physical environment of interest.
In practice, as mentioned in Sec.
I
, even small discrepancies between the geometry assumed by the RT and the actual physical system, of a fraction of the studied wavelength, can significantly affect the accuracy of the predicted phase
(\angle\alpha_{p}(\theta)-2\pi f_{s}\tau_{p})
of each path
p\in\{1,...,P\}

[
20
, Ch. 7.5]
, resulting in random small-scale fading effects that are not captured by the deterministic model in (
7
).
Therefore, even for high-fidelity DT models, one cannot depend on the RT for a reliable estimate of the phases associated with each propagation path
[
20
, Ch. 7.5]
,
[
31
,
33
,
13
]
.

In order to account for the inherent phase error of the DT model in (
7
), we introduce a phase error term
z_{p}
for each path
p\in\{1,...,P\}
that is distributed according to a von Mises distribution
\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa_{0})
with mean
0
and concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}\geq 0
.
The von Mises distribution is the maximum entropy distribution for directional random variables with known mean direction
[
45
, Ch. 3.5.4]
, providing a worst-case model for randomly distributed phases where only the mean phase is assumed
[
46
,
47
]
.
The probability density of a von Mises random variable
X\sim\mathcal{VM}(\mu,\kappa)
is defined as
[
45
]

\mathcal{VM}\left(x|\mu,\kappa\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi I_{0}\left(\kappa\right)}e^{\kappa\cos\left(x-\mu\right)},

(8)

for
x\in[-\pi,\pi)
, with parameters
\mu\in[-\pi,\pi)
and
\kappa\geq 0
, and where
I_{0}(\cdot)
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
0
.
The concentration parameter
\kappa
determines the spread of the distribution as illustrated in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4
:
Von Mises probability density functions
\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa)
for different values of the concentration parameters
\kappa
.

Incorporating the phase errors
\{z_{p}\}_{p=1}^{P}
into the deterministic model (
7
) yields the stochastic model

\begin{split}&\hat{H}(c|\theta,\kappa_{0})=\sum_{p=1}^{P}e^{jz_{p}}\alpha_{p}(\theta)a(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p}),\end{split}

(9)

where the phase errors
\{z_{p}\}_{p=1}^{P}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa_{0})
are assumed to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.).
Note that all the subcarriers and antenna pairs share the same
phase error vector

Z=(z_{1},...,z_{P})^{T}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa_{0})

(10)

of i.i.d. phase error terms and, by extension, the same
P\times 1

phasor error vector

e^{jZ}=(e^{jz_{1}},\ldots,e^{jz_{P}})^{\top},

(11)

which corresponds to the element-wise application of the exponential function to the
P\times 1
phase-error vector
Z
.
Accordingly, the stochastic model (
9
) can be written as

\hat{H}(c|\theta,\kappa_{0})=\hat{G}(c,\theta)e^{jZ},

(12)

with the
SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times P
matrix

\begin{split}\hat{G}(c,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}a(\tau_{1},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{1},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{1}),...,a(\tau_{P},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{P},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{P})\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{Diag}\left(\alpha(\theta)\right),\end{split}

(13)

where
\alpha(\theta)=(\alpha_{1}(\theta),...,\alpha_{P}(\theta))^{\top}
is a
P\times 1
vector representing the complex amplitude of each of the
P
predicted paths, and
\mathrm{Diag}(\cdot)
denotes the diagonal matrix of the given argument.

In practice, it may be useful to assume that different paths have different concentration parameters
\kappa_{0}
. In particular, for line of sight (LoS) paths one can set the corresponding phase error
z_{p}
to zero, which corresponds to an infinite concentration value
\kappa_{0}
. For other paths, it is common to assume a uniform phase error, which is obtained by setting
\kappa_{0}=0
, i.e.,
\mathcal{VM}(z_{p}|0,0)=1/2\pi
for
z_{p}\in[-\pi,\pi)
. In this paper, we consider for simplicity of presentation the i.i.d. model (
10
), but it is straightforward to generalize the proposed technique to situations in which the phase errors are independent but not identically distributed.

By decreasing the value of the concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}
, the stochastic model (
12
) smoothly transitions from the deterministic-phase model (
7
) for
\kappa_{0}\to+\infty
, which neglects phase errors, to a worst-case phase-error RT model for
\kappa_{0}=0
, for which the distribution of the phase error is uniform.
Current applications of RT-simulated channels mostly assume the uniform phase error setting, i.e.,
\kappa_{0}=0
, for non-LoS paths.
Nevertheless, we keep the presentation general in order to evaluate the potential benefits of more accurate ray tracing models.

II-D

Data-Driven Calibration of the Ray Tracer

In addition to the geometric properties, the precision of the RT simulation also depends on the accuracy of the estimated electromagnetic properties of the materials.
These are typically more complex to acquire than their geometric counterpart
[
48
,
49
,
38
,
28
]
, and values estimated in technical reports for generic materials do not capture the variety of properties found in practical scenarios
[
30
]
.
As a result, previous works
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
13
]
have proposed to include the unknown electromagnetic parameters, namely the relative permittivity and conductivity, of all the material types in the scene in a vector
\theta
, which is optimized based on data obtained from the ground-truth scene at multiple locations in a process known as
calibration
.

To this end, we assume the availability of a dataset

\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left(c_{n},H_{n}\right)\right\}_{n=1}^{N}

(14)

of
N

channel frequency response
(CFR) observations
H_{n}\in\mathbbm{C}^{SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1}
for
n\in\{1,...,N\}
, with each observation
H_{n}
taken at receiver and transmitter coordinates
c_{n}=(c^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n},c^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{n})
for all
S
subcarriers.
Specifically, each observation
H_{n}
rearranges the observed CFRs across all antenna pairs and subcarrier frequencies into a single vector following the same order as the channel models in (
7
) and (
12
).

The goal of calibration is to enhance the accuracy of the RT output in (
1
) for locations
c
at which channel data are available, as well as possibly for new locations
c
not included in the dataset, thus extending the DT simulation capacity to new receiver and/or transmitter positions.
That is, the DT aims to provide a reliable simulation of the propagation and channel conditions between any existing or new pair of receiver and transmitter devices at coordinates
c
by leveraging the output
R(c|\theta)
of the RT.
Such a simulator can then be used by the DT to monitor the physical deployment of interest, or to design optimal control policies without the need to collect further data by training AI models in virtual space
[
27
,
17
,
11
,
19
,
25
,
26
]
.

III
Calibration Baselines

In this section, we review two existing classes of calibration methods.
The first approach, proposed in
[
13
]
, optimizes the electromagnetic parameters
\theta
given the channel observations
\mathcal{D}
in (
14
) by assuming the phase error-oblivious model
\hat{H}(c|\theta)
in (
7
).
We refer to this channel response-based method as
phase error-oblivious calibration
.
The second approach, representing the line of work
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, calibrates the parameters
\theta
by comparing the observed
channel power profiles
estimated from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
to the corresponding channel power profiles obtained from the RT under the uniform phase error model
\hat{H}(c|\theta,0)
in (
12
), i.e., with concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}=0
.
We refer to this power profile-based process as
uniform phase error calibration
.

III-A

Phase Error-Oblivious Calibration

As proposed in
[
13
]
, the material parameters
\theta
can be optimized by comparing each observed CFR
H_{n}
in the dataset
\mathcal{D}
to its respective simulated CFR in
\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta)
under the deterministic model (
7
) for all observations
n\in\{1,...,N\}
.
To this end, the resulting phase error-oblivious calibration strategy addresses the least-squares problem of finding the parameter

\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PEOC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})

(15)

that minimizes the difference between the simulated and observed CFRs

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PEOC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lVert H_{n}-\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta)\right\rVert^{2},

(16)

where
\lVert X\rVert^{2}={X}^{\dagger}X
denotes the squared 2-norm for a complex vector
X
.
Problem (
15
) is tackled in
[
13
]
through gradient descent.
This solution requires the differentiability of the predicted channel response
\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta)
with respect to the electromagnetic parameters
\theta
, which is ensured by Sionna RT
[
41
,
13
]
.

III-B

Uniform Phase Error Calibration

In earlier works
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, RT calibration was conducted by comparing the power profiles of the measured and simulated channels over time, i.e., the power delay profiles
[
31
,
33
]
, and/or over space, i.e., the power-angle-delay profiles
[
32
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, while assuming uniform phase errors for the RT-predicted path components.
In this subsection, we briefly elaborate on the resulting uniform phase error calibration approach.

Given an observation
H_{n}
of the channel frequency response from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, an estimation of its power-angle-delay profile can be obtained by projecting the CFR
H_{n}
onto a channel vector matched to a given triple
(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})
of delay
\tau\geq 0
, angle of departure
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
, and angle of arrival
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}\in[0,\pi)\times[0,2\pi)
.
Such vector can be defined by considering a normalized single-path component
a(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})
from (
5
) with the given path parameters
(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})
in lieu of
(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})
.
Accordingly, the
power-angle-delay profile
for measured CFR
H_{n}
is given as

\mathrm{P}_{n}(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}{a(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})}^{\dagger}H_{n}\right|^{2},

(17)

with
L=SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}
.

To enable calibration of the RT, the measured power-angle-delay profile (
17
) must be compared to the simulated power-angle-delay profile obtained from the RT under uniform phase errors. The latter can be obtained from the stochastic model in (
12
) with concentration
\kappa_{0}=0
, in a manner similar to (
17
), as

\hat{\mathrm{P}}(c,\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}|\theta)=\mathbbm{E}_{Z\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{VM}(0,0)}\left[\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}{a(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c,\theta)e^{jZ}\right|^{2}\right],

(18)

where the average is taken with respect to the uniformly distributed phases
Z=(z_{1},...,z_{P})^{\top}
.
The average in (
18
) can be evaluated explicitly as (see Appendix
B
)

\hat{\mathrm{P}}(c,\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}|\theta)=\left\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}{a(\tau,\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}})}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c,\theta)\right\rVert^{2}.

(19)

Optimization of the material parameters
\theta
is carried by finding the parameter

\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{UPEC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})

(20)

that minimizes the discrepancy between the measured and simulated power profiles for a set of pre-defined
M
angle-delay triples
\{(\tau_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
under the squared loss metric

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{UPEC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left|\mathrm{P}_{n}(\tau_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m})-\hat{\mathrm{P}}(c_{n},\tau_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m}|\theta)\right|^{2}.\end{split}

(21)

Problem (
20
) can be tackled through gradient descent, where differentiability of the loss
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{UPEC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})
with respect to the material parameters
\theta
is ensured by the differentiability of the RT output
[
13
]
. In this regard, we note that existing art has mostly relied on less efficient strategies based on simulated annealing
[
31
,
32
,
33
]
or exhaustive parameter search
[
34
,
36
]
.
We refer the reader to Appendix
A-B
for a more in-depth discussion on the selection of the
M
projection triples
\{(\tau_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
.

IV
Phase Error-Aware Calibration

In this section, we introduce a novel calibration scheme that accounts for the inherent phase uncertainty associated with the RT output
R(c|\theta)
in (
1
) via the stochastic channel model in (
12
).
The proposed
phase error-aware calibration
scheme aims at extracting coherent information about the multi-path phases for each channel realization based on the available data (
14
) with the goal of improving the quality of the calibrated RT parameters
\theta
.
Therefore, the approach contrasts with the phase error-oblivious strategy of
[
13
]
, reviewed in the previous section, which hinges on the deterministic model in (
7
), as well as with existing uniform phase error calibration schemes, also described in previous section, which account only for the received power.

IV-A

Calibration Problem

The phase error-calibration scheme models the channel observations
H_{n}
as noisy observations of the phase error-aware model in (
12
) expressed as

H_{n}=\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)e^{jZ_{n}}+W_{n},

(22)

where the additive noise vector
W_{n}\in\mathbbm{C}^{SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1}
is composed of random of i.i.d. circular Gaussian elements
\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
with zero mean and known power
\sigma^{2}
, and
e^{jZ_{n}}=(e^{jz_{n,1}},...,e^{jz_{n,P}})^{\top}
is the
P\times 1
random phasor vector accounting for phase errors.
The phase errors
Z_{n}=(z_{n,1},...,z_{n,P})^{\top}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa_{0})
are described by a prior concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}
.
We first assume parameter
\kappa_{0}
to be fixed, and then we extend the proposed algorithm to enable the estimation of the prior parameter
\kappa_{0}
from data.

By (
22
), the probability distribution of each observation conditioned on the phase-error vector
Z_{n}
is given by

P(H_{n}|Z_{n},c_{n},\theta)=\mathcal{CN}\left(H_{n}\Big|\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)e^{jZ_{n}},\sigma^{2}I\right),

(23)

where
I
denotes the
SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}
identity matrix.
The probability distribution
P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta)
of each measured CFR
H_{n}
is then obtained by marginalizing the joint distribution
P(H_{n},Z_{n}|c_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})=P(H_{n}|Z_{n},c_{n},\theta)P(Z_{n}|\kappa_{0})
over the distribution
P(Z_{n}|\kappa_{0})
of the phase-error vector
Z_{n}
, i.e.,

P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})=\int P(H_{n}|Z_{n},c_{n},\theta)P(Z_{n}|\kappa_{0})dZ_{n},

(24)

where integration is taken over the domain
[-\pi,\pi)^{\otimes P}
of the phase errors.
Given the i.i.d. assumption, the phase-error vector is distributed as
P(Z_{n}|\kappa_{0})=\prod_{p=1}^{P}\mathcal{VM}(z_{n,p}|0,\kappa_{0})
.

The cross-entropy loss with respect to the RT parameters
\theta
is then given as

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PEAC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\left(P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta)\right).

(25)

The resulting ML calibration problem is therefore defined as the minimization

\begin{split}\theta^{*}=\argmin_{\theta}\left\{-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\left(\int P(H_{n}|Z_{n},c_{n},\theta)P(Z_{n}|\kappa_{0})dZ_{n}\right)\right\}.\end{split}

(26)

Problem (
26
) reduces to (
15
) when choosing an infinitely large prior concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}
for the phase noise.

IV-B

Variational Expectation Maximization Algorithm

The direct solution of the problem (
26
) requires the expensive computation of the negative log-likelihood function (
25
) by marginalizing over the latent variables
Z_{n}
(
24
) for all observations
n\in\{1,...,N\}
.
To address this computational challenge, we propose a computationally efficient
variational expectation maximization
(VEM) algorithm
[
40
, Ch. 10]
that approximates the solution of the ML problem (
26
) by restricting the set of possible solutions to a parametric family of distributions.

The proposed VEM approach iterates between the problem of optimizing the parameter
\theta
and that of estimating the phase errors
z_{n,p}
for all paths
p\in\{1,...,P\}
and all observations
n\in\{1,...,N\}
.
The main underlying idea is that, while a priori all phase errors are assumed to have the same distribution
\mathcal{VM}(0,\kappa_{0})
, each channel observation
H_{n}
is characterized by specific per-path phase errors, which may be estimated and used to improve the calibration process.

The estimate of each phase error
z_{n,p}
is represented by a von Mises distribution
\mathcal{VM}\left(z_{n,p}|\mu_{n,p},\kappa_{n,p}\right)
with mean parameters
\mu_{n,p}
and concentration parameter
\kappa_{n,p}
.
The mean represents the nominal estimate of the phase error, and the concentration parameter quantifies the uncertainty of this estimate.
As detailed below, both parameters are estimated from the measured CFR
H_{n}
, as well as from the current iterate
\theta
.
Accordingly, the estimates of the phase errors for all paths
p\in\{1,...,P\}
associated with data point
n
are described by the
variational distribution

Q\left(Z_{n}|\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}\right)=\prod_{p=1}^{P}\mathcal{VM}\left(z_{n,p}|\mu_{n,p},\kappa_{n,p}\right),

(27)

which assumes the estimated phase errors to be independent (but not identically distributed).
The adjective “variational” indicates that distribution
Q\left(Z_{n}|\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}\right)
depends on free parameters that can be optimized, namely the mean parameter vector
\mu_{n}=(\mu_{n,1},...,\mu_{n,P})^{\top}\in[-\pi,\pi)^{\otimes P}
and the concentration parameter vector
\kappa_{n}=(\kappa_{n,1},...,\kappa_{n,P})^{\top}
with
\kappa_{n,p}\geq 0
for
p\in\{1,...,P\}
.
As discussed, distribution (
27
) captures the coherent information about the
p\mbox{-th}
path that can be extracted from the data.
Considering all data points
n\in\{1,...,N\}
, this information is encoded in the variational parameters
\{\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.

VEM approximates the solution of problem (
26
) by iteratively minimizing an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood (
26
) over the material parameters
\theta
and over parameters
\{\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
of the variational distribution (
27
).
This upper bound, known as the
variational free energy
is given by (see, e.g.,
[
50
,
40
]
)

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PEAC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}),

(28)

where

\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)=\mathbbm{E}_{Z_{n}\sim Q(Z_{n}|\mu_{n},\kappa_{n})}\left[\log\left(\frac{Q(Z_{n}|\mu_{n},\kappa_{n})}{P(H_{n},Z_{n}|c_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})}\right)\right],

(29)

and
P(H_{n},Z_{n}|c_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})
is the joint distribution defined in (
24
).
As detailed in Appendix
C
, using (
23
) and (
27
), the free energy (
29
) can be written more explicitly as

\begin{split}\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)=&\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left\{\log\left(\frac{I_{0}(\kappa_{0})}{I_{0}(\kappa_{n,p})}\right)+b(\kappa_{n,p})\left(\kappa_{n,p}-\kappa_{0}\cos(\mu_{n,p})\right)\right\}+L\log\left(\pi\sigma^{2}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\left\lVert\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)\mathrm{Diag}\left(B(\kappa_{n})\right)e^{j\mu_{n}}-H_{n}\right\rVert^{2}+L\left(\left|\alpha(\theta)\right|^{2}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbbm{1}-B^{2}(\kappa_{n})\right)\right],\end{split}

(30)

where
|\cdot|^{2}
denotes the element-wise squared amplitude of a vector;
\mathbbm{1}=(1,...,1)^{\top}
denotes the all-ones
P\times 1
vector;
I_{\nu}(\cdot)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind of order
\nu\geq 0
;
B(\kappa_{n})=(b(\kappa_{n,1}),...,b(\kappa_{n,P}))^{\top}
is the
P\times 1
vector of Bessel ratios

b(\kappa)=\frac{I_{1}(\kappa)}{I_{0}(\kappa)},

(31)

for
\kappa\geq 0
; and
B^{2}(\kappa_{n})=(b(\kappa_{n,1})^{2},...,b(\kappa_{n,P})^{2})^{\top}
is the
P\times 1
vector of squared Bessel ratios.
The Bessel ratio
b(\cdot)
in (
31
) is a strictly increasing and continuous function in
[0,+\infty)
, with
b(0)=0
and
\lim_{\kappa\to+\infty}b(\kappa)=1
.

As in the classical expectation maximization (EM) algorithm, each iteration
i\geq 0
of VEM comprises of two steps, an
expectation step
(E-step) and a
maximization step
(M-step), producing a sequence of iterates
\theta^{(i)}
and
\{\mu_{n}^{(i)},\kappa_{n}^{(i)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, with initializations
\theta^{(0)}
and
\{\mu_{n}^{(0)},\kappa_{n}^{(0)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.
In this regard, the electromagnetic parameters
\theta^{(0)}
may be initialized using prior knowledge from previous observations or using known properties of the materials in the scene
[
30
]
.
The E-step estimates the statistics of the phase errors
\{\mu_{n}^{(i)},\kappa_{n}^{(i)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
across all data points
(c_{n},H_{n})\in\mathcal{D}
by using the current iterate
\theta^{(i)}
; while the M-step updates the material parameters
\theta^{(i+1)}
for fixed updated phase error parameters
\{\mu_{n}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n}^{(i+1)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
.

IV-C

Expectation Step

As summarized by lines
5
-
13
of Algorithm
1
, during the E-step of the proposed phase error-aware calibration scheme, the material parameters
\theta^{(i)}
are fixed, and minimization of the variational free energy in (
30
) is carried out with respect to the local parameters
\mu_{n}
and
\kappa_{n}
. This is done by tackling the minimization problem

\left(\mu_{n}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n}^{(i+1)}\right)=\argmin_{\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}}\left\{\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta^{(i)},\kappa_{0}\right)\right\}.

(32)

This problem can be addressed, separately for each data point
n\in\{1,...,N\}
, using any optimization algorithms such as gradient descent.
In the following, we present an approximate analytical solution that will be tested experimentally in the next section.

As detailed in Appendix
D
, we propose to address a relaxed version of problem (
32
) in which optimization is carried out over the complex phasor
e^{j\mu_{n}}
while removing the constraint that the magnitude of all entries be equal to 1. For this relaxed problem, we obtain a stationary solution, and then we project the solution into the space of unitary magnitude vectors to recover an approximate estimate of the variational mean vector
\mu_{n}
. Note that we do not make any claim of optimality for this solution.

To elaborate on the obtained approximate solution, let us assume that the
L\times P
matrix
\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})
in (
13
) is of rank
P
.
This assumption requires that the number of channel coefficients measured for each data points,
L
, is no smaller than the number,
P
, of paths, i.e.,
L\geq P
.
In this common situation, full rank implies that the space-frequency signatures of each of the
P
paths, i.e., the columns of matrix
\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})
, are linearly independent and hence distinguishable using linear projections.
Accordingly, this condition implies that the system has sufficient resolution in the combined space-frequency domain to resolve the different paths from the predicted channel response in the given deployment scenario.

Under this assumption, by addressing the mentioned relaxed problem and projecting back the solution into the space of phasors, we obtain the next iterate

\mu_{n}^{(i+1)}=\angle\biggr\{\Bigr({\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})\Bigl)^{-1}\Bigr(\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{0}}{2}\mathbbm{1}+{\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})}^{\dagger}H_{n}\Bigl)\biggl\},

(33)

where
\angle(\cdot)
represents the element-wise phase of a complex vector.
Furthermore, the concentration parameter vector
\kappa_{n,p}
equals zero, i.e.,
\kappa^{(i+1)}_{n,p}=0
, if the inequality
L|\alpha_{p}(\theta^{(i)})|^{2}/\sigma^{2}\leq 1
holds, while it satisfies the fixed-point equation

\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}=\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}|\alpha_{p}(\theta^{(i)})|^{2}b(\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}).

(34)

otherwise. While equation (
34
) can be addressed by fixed-point iterations, an approximation can be obtained by bounding the Bessel ratio
[
51
]
, yielding the approximate closed-form solution

\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}=2\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}(\theta^{(i)})|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-1}\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}(\theta^{(i)})|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}},

(35)

for each path
p\in\{1,...,P\}
and observation
n\in\{1,...,N\}
.

The condition

\frac{L|\alpha_{p}(\theta^{(i)})|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}>1,

(36)

under which the fixed-point condition (
34
) applies, indicates that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) associated with the observation of path
p
is larger than one.
Accordingly, if the per-path SNR, i.e., the left-hand side of (
36
), is smaller than one, the estimated concentration parameter
\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}
corresponds to a uniform phase error, while, if it is larger than one, the concentration parameter
\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}
is larger than zero.
For any strictly positive concentration
\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)}>0
, the corresponding variational distribution
\mathcal{VM}(\mu_{n,p}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n,p}^{(i+1)})
exhibits a preferred direction along the mean phase
\mu_{n,p}^{(i+1)}
, and, therefore, contributes to lowering the uncertainty of the phase error of path
p
in observation
n
.

IV-D

Maximization Step

Let us consider first the case in which the concentration parameter
\kappa_{0}
is fixed.
As summarized in the line
16
of Algorithm
1
, during the M-step of the proposed scheme, the previously selected phase error parameters
\{\mu_{n}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n}^{(i+1)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, are given, and the optimal material parameter iterate
\theta^{(i+1)}
is obtained by tackling the problem

\theta^{(i+1)}=\argmin_{\theta}\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n}^{(i+1)},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)\right\}.

(37)

We suggest to use gradient descent for this purpose in a manner similar to the phase error-oblivious scheme in
[
13
]
.

When treating the prior concentration
\kappa_{0}
as a parameter to be jointly optimized with
\theta
(lines
17
-
21
of Algorithm
1
), the M-step tackles the problem

\left(\theta^{(i+1)},\kappa_{0}^{(i+1)}\right)=\argmin_{\theta,\kappa_{0}}\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n}^{(i+1)},\kappa_{n}^{(i+1)},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)\right\}

(38)

in lieu of (
37
).
To address the minimization (
38
), parameter
\theta^{(i+1)}
may still be updated through gradient descent; while, as derived in Appendix
E
, an optimal value for the prior concentration iterate
\kappa_{0}^{(i+1)}
is obtained as

\kappa_{0}^{(i+1)}=b^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{NP}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa^{(i+1)}_{n,p})\cos(\mu^{(i+1)}_{n,p})\right),

(39)

provided that the inequality
(NP)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa^{(i+1)}_{n,p})\cos(\mu^{(i+1)}_{n,p})\geq 0
holds, and as
\kappa_{0}^{(i+1)}=0
otherwise, where
b^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse function of the Bessel ratio
b(\cdot)

[
52
]
.

IV-E

Complexity Analysis

We now study the additional computational complexity incurred by the proposed method with respect to the calibration baselines introduced in Sec.
III
.
The main new added operation is the update of the variational parameters
\{\mu_{n}^{(i)},\kappa_{n}^{(i)}\}
in the E-step.
In this regard, we note that the matrix inversion during the computation of the mean parameter in (
33
) can be expressed as

\begin{split}\left({\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta^{(i)})\right)^{-1}=\mathrm{Diag}\left(\alpha(\theta)\right)^{-1}A(c_{n})^{-1}\mathrm{Diag}\left({\alpha(\theta)}^{*}\right)^{-1},\end{split}

(40)

where the
P\times P
matrix

A(c_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}{a(\tau_{i},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{i},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{i})}^{\dagger}a(\tau_{j},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{j},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{j})\end{pmatrix}_{(i,j)\in\{1,...,P\}^{2}}

(41)

does not depend on the material parameters
\theta^{(i)}
.
Accordingly, the inverse
A(c_{n})^{-1}
only needs to be computed once for each calibration position
c_{n}
at the beginning of training, while the successive E-step iterations can be computed efficiently in
O(N\max(LP,P^{2}))
operations.
All in all, our proposed method only incurs an additional complexity of
O(NP^{3})
when inverting the matrices
\{A(c_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N}
prior to calibration, while retaining the same computational efficiency as the baselines at each training step.

Experiments

V-A

Setting

Antenna arrays are modelled using evenly spaced antenna elements, with a half-wavelength spacing, and their steering vectors

The channel observations

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we have proposed a new phase error-aware method to calibrate the material properties of RT simulations based on multi-carrier channel response observations.
The proposed scheme estimates RT-predicted phase-errors from data, accounting for small-scale discrepancies between the assumed three-dimensional DT model and the ground-truth geometry.
The phase-error estimates are used to correct the predicted interference pattern among propagation paths during calibration of the material parameters.
This is demonstrated to yield more precise conductivity and permittivity estimates, as well as more accurate signal power predictions, especially in the low-resolution regime characterized by low-bandwidth conditions.
We conducted quantitative experiments for a single-material scene, showcasing the advantages of this approach against current state-of-the-art power profile-based and channel response-based schemes for practical conditions characterized by unreliable RT phase predictions.

Future work may investigate the simulation of diffracted and diffusely scattered propagation paths, along with the calibration of scattering coefficients and cross-polarization discrimination; the validation of the proposed approach on real-world measurements; the impact of antenna patterns on phase uncertainty; and the extension of the current study to a more diverse set of materials, such as position-dependent material properties through neural network parameterization
[
55
]
.

A-A

Phase Error-Oblivious Calibration as Observation Noise

The phase error-oblivious calibration scheme in Sec.
III-A
assumes that each channel observation
H_{n}
is a noisy realization of the deterministic channel model
\hat{H}(c|\theta)
in (
7
) given by

H_{n}=\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta)+W_{n},

(45)

where the entries of the stochastic vector
W_{n}\in\mathbbm{C}^{SN^{\mathrm{Rx}}N^{\mathrm{Tx}}\times 1}
are i.i.d. circular Gaussian
\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2})
variables with known noise power
\sigma^{2}
.
Accordingly, the distribution
P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta)
of each observation
H_{n}
is modelled as a circular Gaussian distribution

P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta)=\mathcal{CN}(H_{n}|\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta),\sigma^{2}I)

(46)

with mean
\hat{H}(c_{n}|\theta)
.
The phase error-oblivious calibration process addresses the maximum likelihood (ML) problem of finding the parameter
\theta^{*}
that minimizes the cross-entropy loss

\frac{\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{PEOC}}(\theta|\mathcal{D})}{\sigma^{2}}=-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\left(P(H_{n}|c_{n},\theta)\right)

(47)

for the available data
\mathcal{D}
, which amounts the least squares problem presented in (
15
).

A-B

Selection of Projections in Uniform Phase-Error Calibration

The definition of criterion (
21
) requires the choice of the
M
reference triples
\{(\tau_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
.
These may be selected from the predicted path triples
\{(\tau_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{p},\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{p})\}_{p=1}^{P}
, or as uniformly spaced values in a suitable space.
For instance, one may consider evenly spaced delays
\tau_{m}
between the minimal and maximal predicted times of arrival, while selecting the set of angles of departure
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoD}}_{m}
and arrival
\Phi^{\mathrm{AoA}}_{m}
as (approximately) evenly spaced points on a sphere via a spherical Fibonacci lattice
[
56
]
.
In practice, one may even restrict the points of the spherical lattice to the surface spanned by the estimated elevation and azimuth angles of departure or arrival, or to the relevant angles for the given antenna patterns.

These choices of angle-delay triples are in line with previous works, which have proposed to evenly spread delay points across a given delay-window
[
33
]
, or to select angle-delay points close to the angles and times of arrival of the predicted paths
[
31
,
32
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
.
That said, we note that the calibration approaches in
[
32
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
first carry out a pre-processing step of the measured CFRs, which assumes the time-angle resolution of the system to be sufficient to distinguish the different path contributions in the measured data.
Accordingly, such schemes first pair the predicted rays to estimated measured rays by matching the predicted time of arrival, angle of departure and angle of arrival of each simulated path to its nearest peak in the measured power-angle-delay profile.
The corresponding loss function is then computed by comparing the predicted and measured power of each pair.

57

\displaystyle\mathbbm{E}\left[\left\lVert\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)e^{jZ_{n}}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

(49b)

where we have indicated
\mathbbm{E}[\cdot]
for

(53)

13

\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)}{\partial\kappa_{n}}&=-\frac{\partial\sum_{p=1}^{P}\log{I_{0}(\kappa_{n,p})}}{\partial\kappa_{n}}+\frac{\partial\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa_{n,p})\left(\kappa_{n,p}-\kappa_{0}\cos(\mu_{n,p})\right)}{\partial\kappa_{n}}+\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\frac{\partial\lVert\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)\mathrm{Diag}\left(e^{j\mu_{n}}\right)B(\kappa_{n})-H_{n}\rVert^{2}}{\partial\kappa_{n}}+L\left(\left|\alpha(\theta)\right|^{2}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial\left(\mathbbm{1}-B^{2}(\kappa_{n})\right)}{\partial\kappa_{n}}\right]\\
&=\left(\kappa_{n}^{\top}-\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}B(\kappa_{n})^{\top}\mathrm{Diag}\left(\left|\alpha(\theta)\right|^{2}\right)-\Re\left\{C(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n})\right\}\right)\frac{\partial B(\kappa_{n})}{\partial\kappa_{n}},\end{split}

(63)

with row-vector
C(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n})
defined in (
62
).

D-B

Approximating the Stationary Points via Phasor Relaxation

In what follows, we address the subset of stationary points
(\mu_{n}^{*},\kappa_{n}^{*})
for the variational parameters that satisfy the equality
C(\mu_{n}^{*},\kappa_{n}^{*})=0
. Note that this may be a strict subset of the set of all stationary points, and that it may potentially be empty. To proceed, we relax the problem by optimizing over an arbitrary
P\times 1
complex vector
\rho_{n}
in lieu of the phasor
e^{j\mu_{n}}
in (
62
).
The stationarity condition of mean gradient (
61
) is verified for all solutions satisfying the equality
C(\rho_{n}^{*},\kappa_{n}^{*})=0
. Note that we have replaced
\mu_{n}
by
\rho_{n}
in order to highlight the adopted change of variables for optimization. Considering also the stationarity condition for the concentration gradient (
63
), we obtain the system of equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C(\rho_{n}^{*},\kappa_{n}^{*})=0\\
\left(\kappa_{n}^{*}-\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}\mathrm{Diag}\left(\left|\alpha(\theta)\right|^{2}\right)B(\kappa_{n}^{*})\right)\frac{\partial B(\kappa_{n})}{\partial\kappa_{n}}\biggr\rvert_{\kappa_{n}^{*}}=0.\end{array}\right.

(64)

Noting that the elements in the main diagonal of the matrix
\partial B(\kappa_{n})/\partial\kappa_{n}
are all non-null, the second equation in (
64
) simplifies into
P
independent scalar equations
f_{p}(\kappa_{n,p}^{*})=0
, with

f_{p}(\kappa_{n,p})=\kappa_{n,p}-\frac{2L}{\sigma^{2}}\left|\alpha_{p}(\theta)\right|^{2}b(\kappa_{n,p}),

(65)

for
p\in\{1,...,P\}
.
While
\kappa_{n,p}^{*}=0
is a trivial solution to
f_{p}(\kappa_{n,p}^{*})=0
, a strictly positive solution
\kappa_{n,p}^{*}>0
can be found if and only if we have

\frac{L|\alpha_{p}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}>1.

(66)

This statement can be proven by studying the sign of
f_{p}(\kappa_{n,p})
for
\kappa_{n,p}\in[0,+\infty)
.
When inequality (
66
) is verified, a solution
\kappa_{n,p}^{*}>0
to
f_{p}(\kappa_{n,p}^{*})=0
can be bounded using the Bessel ratio bounds in
[
51
]
as

2\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-1}\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}<\kappa_{n,p}^{*}<2\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L|\alpha_{p}|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}},

(67)

for
p\in\{1,...,P\}
.
In practice, we use the lower bound in (
67
) to approximate
\kappa_{n,p}^{*}
for
p\in\{1,...,P\}
.
Note that a tighter bound in (
67
) can be obtained using sharper bounds of the Bessel ratio in
[
51
]
.

Furthermore, assuming the
L\times P
matrix
\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)
is of rank
P
, the first equation in (
64
) yields

\mathrm{Diag}\left(B(\kappa_{n}^{*})\right)\rho^{*}_{n}=\left({\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{0}}{2}\mathbbm{1}+{\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)}^{\dagger}H_{n}\right),

(68)

where existence of the inverse
({\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta))^{-1}
stems from the full-column rank assumption on
\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)
.
By (
68
), if all entries in
B(\kappa_{n}^{*})
are strictly positive, the element-wise phase of vector
\rho^{*}_{n}
satisfies

\angle\left\{\rho^{*}_{n}\right\}=\angle\biggr\{\Bigr({\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)}^{\dagger}\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)\Bigl)^{-1}\Bigr(\frac{\sigma^{2}\kappa_{0}}{2}\mathbbm{1}+{\hat{G}(c_{n},\theta)}^{\dagger}H_{n}\Bigl)\biggl\}.

(69)

Finally, we project this solution back into the space of phasors, producing the proposed approximate solution (
33
).

Appendix E
Optimizing the Prior Concentration Parameter

From (
38
), given that the function
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})
is convex with respect to
\kappa_{0}
, an optimal solution
\kappa_{0}^{*}
can be computed by evaluating a stationary point
\kappa_{0}^{*}
as

\frac{\partial\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)}{\partial\kappa_{0}}\biggr\rvert_{\kappa_{0}^{*}}=0,

(70)

where

\frac{\partial\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}\left(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0}\right)}{\partial\kappa_{0}}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\left\{b(\kappa_{0})-b(\kappa_{n,p})\cos(\mu_{n,p})\right\}.

(71)

This yields

\kappa_{0}^{*}=b^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{NP}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa_{n,p})\cos(\mu_{n,p})\right),

(72)

where
b^{-1}(\cdot)
denotes the inverse function of the Bessel ratio
b(\cdot)

[
52
]
.
Given that
b^{-1}(\cdot)
is defined on
[0,1)
, the result in (
72
) holds only if
0\leq(NP)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa_{n,p})\cos(\mu_{n,p})<1
.
While the upper bound is verified for any set of parameters
\{\mu_{n},\kappa_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
, the lower bound can be exceeded if some of the variational mean estimates verify
|\mu_{n,p}|>\pi/2
for
\mu_{n,p}\in[-\pi,\pi)
.
For such cases, i.e., when
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa_{n,p})\cos(\mu_{n,p})<0
, the free energy derivative in (
71
) is always positive, and
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{F}_{n}(\mu_{n},\kappa_{n},\theta,\kappa_{0})
becomes a strictly increasing function with respect to
\kappa_{0}
.
Accordingly, we extend the result in (
72
) to
\kappa_{0}^{*}=0
when
\\
(NP)^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{P}b(\kappa_{n,p})\cos(\mu_{n,p})<0
.

Appendix F
FDTD Wave Propagation Simulation Implementation

FDTD simulations are carried using the gprMax library
[
53
]
for the two-dimensional (2D) scenario displayed in Fig.
11
.
The transmit and the receive antennas are set to the same polarization, which is perpendicular to the 2D-plane of the simulation.
We denote as
e^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}(t)\in\mathbbm{R}
the amplitude of the electric field along the given polarization for time
t\geq 0
at the
n
-th receiver with
n\in\{1,2,3\}
.
To avoid numerical dispersion errors, both axes in the scene are finely discretized into steps of
\delta=\lambda^{c}/20
m, where the wavelength
\lambda^{c}\approx 5
cm corresponds to the central frequency
f^{c}=6
GHz.
We set a current source at the transmitter equal to

i^{\mathrm{Tx}}(t)=\left(2(\pi f^{c})^{2}\left(t-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{f^{c}}\right)^{2}-1\right)e^{-(\pi f^{c})^{2}\left(t-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{f^{c}}\right)^{2}},

(73)

which is proportional to a Ricker impulse with spectral power centered at the carrier frequency
f^{c}
.
The resulting transmitter acts as a dipole of size
\delta
perpendicular to the plane of interest, which radiates energy uniformly across all 2D directions
[
59
]
.
By solving the wave equations via finite differences, the electric field is propagated across the scene, and we record the fields
\{e^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}(t)\}_{n=1}^{3}
at the receive locations every
1.18
ps in the interval
t\in[0,t_{\mathrm{max}}]
, where
t_{\mathrm{max}}=100
ns.

Using wave superposition, the electric field
e^{\mathrm{Tx}}(t,r)
generated in the 2D plane at a distance
r\gg\lambda^{c}
of the transmitter (i.e., at the far field) can be equivalently stated as a sum of Hertzian dipoles
e^{\mathrm{Tx}}(t,r)=\int_{f=-\infty}^{+\infty}E^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{0}(f)(e^{-jk_{f}r}/r)e^{j2\pi ft}df
with

E^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{0}(f)=j\frac{Z_{0}k_{f}\delta}{4\pi}I^{\mathrm{Tx}}(f),

(74)

where
k_{f}=2\pi f/v
is the wave number associated to frequency
f
,
Z_{0}\approx 377

\Omega
is the impedance of free space, and
I^{\mathrm{Tx}}(f)=\mathcal{F}\{i^{\mathrm{Tx}}\}(f)
is the Fourier transform of
i^{\mathrm{Tx}}(t)

[
42
, Ch.4.2.6]
.
We similarly decompose the received fields
e^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}(t)
into a sum of harmonic contributions weighted by their respective Fourier transform
E^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}(f)=\mathcal{F}\{e^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}\}(f)
.
In the following, we assume that the receive and transmit antennas share the same impedance and have a uniform pattern in the 2D plane, with the impedance of the transmission lines matched to their respective antennas.
Accordingly, the channel response at location
c_{n}=(c^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n},c^{\mathrm{Tx}})
for frequencies
f\in\mathbbm{R}
in the spectral support of the transmitted impulse (i.e., for
|I^{\mathrm{Tx}}(f)|\neq 0
) is obtained as
[
60
]

H\left(c_{n},f\right)=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\sqrt{G^{\mathrm{Tx}}G^{\mathrm{Rx}}}\frac{E^{\mathrm{Rx}}_{n}(f)}{E^{\mathrm{Tx}}_{0}(f)},

(75)

where
\lambda=v/f
is the wavelength and
G^{\mathrm{Tx}}
and
G^{\mathrm{Rx}}
denote the antenna gains at the receive and transmit devices, respectively.
The corresponding time-domain channel response
h(c_{n},\tau)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{H(c_{n},f)\}
is then obtained via the inverse Fourier transform; and the total received power is computed as the integral

\mathrm{P}_{\mathrm{sig}}(c_{n})=\int_{\tau=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\left|h(c_{n},\tau)\right|^{2}d\tau.

(76)
