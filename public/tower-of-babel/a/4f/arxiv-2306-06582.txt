Fast, Distribution-free Predictive Inference for Neural Networks with Coverage Guarantees
Yue Gao, Garvesh Raskutti, Rebecca Willet

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel, computationally-efficient algorithm for predictive inference (PI) that requires no distributional assumptions on the data and can be computed faster than existing bootstrap-type methods for neural networks. Specifically, if there are n n training samples, bootstrap methods require training a model on each of the n n subsamples of size n − 1 n-1 ; for large models like neural networks, this process can be computationally prohibitive. In contrast, our proposed method trains one neural network on the full dataset with ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -differential privacy (DP) and then approximates each leave-one-out model efficiently using a linear approximation around the differentially-private neural network estimate. With exchangeable data, we prove that our approach has a rigorous coverage guarantee that depends on the preset privacy parameters and the stability of the neural network, regardless of the data distribution. Simulations and experiments on real data demonstrate that our method satisfies the coverage guarantees with substantially reduced computation compared to bootstrap methods.



1
Introduction

To assess the accuracy of parameter estimates or predictions without specific distributional knowledge of the data, the idea of re-sampling or sub-sampling on the available data has been long-established to construct prediction intervals, and there is a rich history in the statistics literature on the jackknife and bootstrap methods, see
Stine 1985
,
Efron 1979
,
Quenouille 1949
,
Efron and Gong 1983
.
Among these re-sampling methods, leave-one-out methods (generally referred to as “cross-validation” or “jackknife”) are
widely used to assess or calibrate predictive accuracy, and can be found in a large line of literature
(
Stone 1974
,
Geisser 1975
)
.

While it has been demonstrated in a large body of past work with extensive evidence that jackknife-type methods have reliable empirical performance, the theoretical properties of these types of methods are studied relatively little until recently, see
Steinberger and Leeb 2018
,
Bousquet and Elisseeff 2002
.
One of the most important results among these theoretically guaranteed works is
Foygel Barber et al. 2019
, which introduces a crucial modification compared to the traditional jackknife method that permits rigorous coverage guarantees of at least
1-2\alpha
regardless of the distribution of the data points, for any algorithm that treats the training points symmetrically.
We will revisit this work and give more relative details in
Section

2.1
.

Although theoretically jackknife+ has been proven to have coverage guarantees without distributional assumptions, in practice, this method is computationally costly, since we need to train
n
(which is the training sample size) leave-one-out models from scratch to find the predictive interval. Especially for large and complicated models like neural networks, this computational cost is prohibitive. The goal of this paper is to provide a
fast
algorithm that provides similar theoretical coverage guarantees to those in jackknife+.

To achieve this goal, we develop a new procedure, called Differentially Private Lazy Predictive Inference (DP-Lazy PI), which combines two ideas: lazy training of neural networks and differentially private stochcastic gradient descent (DP-SGD). To accelerate the procedure, we introduce a lazy training scheme inspired by
Chizat et al. 2020
,
Gao et al. 2022
to train the leave-one-out models. The intuition is that with data exchangeability, the leave-one-out models should be quite close or similar to each other and there is no need to train each one from scratch (
i.e.
, random initialization). Instead, we first train a model on the full data and use this model as a good initialization. By using DP-SGD as the initializer for our full model, we are able to provide coverage guarantees since the privacy mechanism prevents information leakage across leave-one-out estimators. In particular, we prove that our DP-Lazy PI procedure has a coverage of at least
1-2\alpha-3\sqrt{2\eta+2\epsilon+\delta}
where
\eta
represents an out-of-sample stability parameter and
(\epsilon,\delta)
are the differential privacy parameters. Empirically, we show through simulations and real-data experiments that our method has significant advantage over the existing jackknife+ method in run-time while still maintaining good coverage.

2.1
Distribution-free Prediction Intervals

Suppose we have training data
(X_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and a new test point
(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
,
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},~Y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
drawn independently from the same distribution.
We fit a regression model to the training data,
i.e.
, a function
\hat{f}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
where
\hat{f}(x)
predicts
Y_{n+1}
given a new feature vector
X_{n+1}=x
, and then provide a prediction interval centered around
\hat{f}(X_{n+1})
for the test point.
Specifically, given some target coverage level
1-\alpha
, we aim to construct a prediction interval
\hat{C}_{n,\alpha}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{n+1}\in\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}(X_{n+1})\right]\geq 1-\alpha.

We call the probability
\mathbb{P}\left[Y_{n+1}\in\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}(X_{n+1})\right]
as the coverage, and
\text{len}[\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}(X_{n+1})]
as the interval width of
\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}(X_{n+1})
, which is the distance between the left and right endpoints.

Naïve Prediction Interval

A naïve way to construct a predictive interval at the new test point
(X_{n+1},Y_{n+1})
is to center the interval at
\hat{f}(X_{n+1})
and estimate the margin (
i.e.
, half of the interval width) from the training residuals
|Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})|,~i=1,\dots,n
.
Therefore, a naïve prediction interval can be constructed as:

\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}^{\text{na\"{i}ve}}(x)\coloneqq\left[\hat{f}(x)\pm Q_{\alpha}^{+}\left(\{|Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})|\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\right].

where
Q_{\alpha}^{+}\left(\{|Y_{i}-\hat{f}(X_{i})|\}_{i\in[n]}\right)
is defined as (
1
).
Due to the problem of overfitting when the training errors are typically smaller than the test errors, this naïve interval
\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}(x)
may undercover—
i.e.
, the probability that
Y_{n+1}
falls outside the interval can be larger than
\alpha
, as discussed in
Foygel Barber et al. 2019
.

Jackknife Prediction Interval

To avoid undercoverage due to model overfitting, the
jackknife
method estimates the margin of errors
is estimated by leave-one-out residuals instead of training residuals
(
Steinberger and Leeb 2016
,
Steinberger and Leeb 2018
)
.
The idea is straightforward: for each
j=1,\dots,n
, we fit a regression function
\hat{f}_{-j}
using all the training data except the
j
-th training sample. Based on these leave-one-out models
\hat{f}_{-1},\dots,\hat{f}_{-n}
, we can therefore compute the leave-one-out residuals:
|Y_{1}-\hat{f}_{-1}(X_{1})|,\dots,|Y_{n}-\hat{f}_{-n}(X_{n})|
.

With the leave-one-out residuals as well as the regression function
\hat{f}
fitted on the full training data, the jackknife prediction interval is constructed as:

\widehat{C}_{n,\alpha}^{\text{jackknife}}(x)\coloneqq\left[\hat{f}(x)\pm Q_{\alpha}^{+}\left(\{|Y_{i}-\hat{f}_{-i}(X_{i})|\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\right].

Jackknife+ Prediction Interval

The jackknife+ is a modification of jackknife, and both of these methods use the leave-one-out residuals when constructing the prediction intervals. The difference is that the jackknife interval is centered at the predicted value
\hat{f}(X_{n+1})
(where
\hat{f}
is fitted on the full training data), whereas jackknife+ uses the leave-one-out predictions
\hat{f}_{-i}(X_{n+1}),i=1,\dots,n
instead to the build the prediction interval:

\displaystyle\widehat{C}^{\text{jackknife+}}_{n,\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\coloneqq\left[Q_{\alpha}^{-}\left(\left\{\hat{f}_{-i}(x)-|Y_{i}-\hat{f}_{-i}(X_{i})|\right\}_{i\in[n]}\right),~~Q_{\alpha}^{+}\left(\left\{\hat{f}_{-i}(x)+|Y_{i}-\hat{f}_{-i}(X_{i})|\right\}_{i\in[n]}\right)\right].

Foygel Barber et al. 2019
provides the non-asymptotic coverage guarantee for jackknife+ without assumptions beyond the training and test data being exchangeable. Theoretically, a distribution-free coverage guarantee of at least
1-2\alpha
is provided, and it’s observed that the method can achieve
1-\alpha
coverage in empirical studies.

Method and Algorithm

Our method involves using the lazy estimation framework
(
Chizat et al. 2020
)
with an initialization using differential privacy stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD,
Abadi et al. 2016
).

3.1
Lazy Estimation

To calculate the predictive interval fast and reduce the computational cost, we consider using a lazy estimation scheme when estimating the leave-one-out (LOO) models for calculating the predictive interval. The key idea of our approach is given an initialization parameterized by
\theta_{0}
, for each
i
, we approximate
\hat{f}_{-i}
by linearizing around initial base model and training the linearized model using all but the
i^{\rm th}
training sample. Thus instead of training
n
neural networks explicitly, we train a single neural network followed by
n
linear models, leading to a substantial computational saving.

For any dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}
and a large model
f(x;~\theta)
, where
\theta
denotes the model parameters, we define a lazy estimation operator

\text{LAZY}_{\lambda}(\theta;\mathcal{D}):\Theta\times\mathcal{D}\mapsto\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
with the ridge regression parameter
\lambda>0

and initialization
\theta_{0}
, we estimate the LOO models by taking a linearization around
\theta_{0}
and minimize the penalized training loss on data
\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}={\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}}_{i\in[n]\backslash[j]}
,

\text{LAZY}_{\lambda}(\theta_{0};\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})=\theta_{0}+\underset{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits}\left\{\sum_{i\in[n]\backslash\{j\}}\mathcal{L}\left(Y_{i},f(X_{i},\theta_{0})+\Delta^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{i};\theta)|_{\theta=\theta_{0}}\right)+\lambda\|\Delta\|^{2}\right\}.

(3)

3.2
Differential Privacy (DP) initialization

One of the important questions with lazy estimation is how to choose the initialization
\theta_{0}
. For this we use the concept of
differential privacy
, which allows us to achieve coverage guarantees with a large reduction in computational costs.

Definition 1

(Differential Privacy
(
Dwork and Lei 2009
)
)
.

A randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{D}\mapsto\mathcal{R}
with domain
\mathcal{D}
and range
\mathcal{R}
satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if for any two adjacent datasets
d,d^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
and for any subset of outputs
S\subseteq\mathcal{R}
it holds that

P\left(\mathcal{M}(d)\in S\right)\leq e^{\epsilon}P\left(\mathcal{M}(d^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta.

(4)

In essence, this differential privacy (DP) condition ensures that the output distribution does not change much when the input data has some small change, which makes it hard to distinguish between the input databases on the basis of the output.

Abadi et al. 2016
proposes
Algorithm

1
, which performs stochastic gradient descent with noisy gradients and showed that it achieves
(\epsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy, where noise is added to the gradients in the scale of
O(\frac{\sqrt{\log{\delta}}}{n\epsilon})
. Algorithms to ensure DP are always designed based on the sensitivity of the original algorithm.
More precisely, given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and a norm function
\|\cdot\|
over the range of
\mathcal{A}
, the sensitivity of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

\displaystyle s(\mathcal{A})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(D,D^{\prime})=1,\text{card}(D)=n\end{subarray}}\|\mathcal{A}(D)-\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\|

(5)

The Laplacian mechanism to construct a differentially private algorithm is as follows (see
(
Koufogiannis et al. 2015
,
Holohan et al. 2018
)
): for an algorithm (or function)
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}\mapsto\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the random function
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(d)=\mathcal{A}(d)+\xi,~\forall d\in\mathcal{D}
satisfies
(\epsilon,0)
-differential privacy, where the elements
\xi_{i}
follows a Laplacian distribution
\text{Lap}(0,s(\mathcal{A})/\epsilon)
:

p(\xi)\propto e^{-\epsilon|\xi|/s(\mathcal{A})}
.
We can set
\epsilon
and
\delta
as small constants (
e.g.
,
\epsilon=0.01
and
\delta=10^{-3}
), and adjust the noise levels in different DP mechanisms.

Discussion and Limitations

In this paper, we describe a new method, Differential Privacy Lazy Predictive Inference or DP-Lazy PI, which provides a fast method for distribution-free inference for neural networks. Our method involves using differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) as an initial estimate and then computing leave-one-out approximations using lazy training and then re-combining the leave-one-out estimators to form the predictive interval. Importantly, we are able to provide coverage guarantees that closely match the coverage guarantees provided for the jackknife+ procedure with a fraction of the computational cost since only a single neural network model needs to be trained.

The two real data examples also suggest that when we have significant model
misspecification, the implicit regularization that lazy training provides means that our DP Lazy-PI method has narrower width prediction intervals than jackknife+.

An alternative method we evaluated in our experiments was lazy finetune, which removes the privacy mechanism prior to lazy training. The simulation results show very similar and at times slightly improved performance compared to our DP Lazy-PI approach; however, a limitation is that we lack theoretical guarantees for this approach. It remains an open question whether we can provide theoretical guarantees for lazy finetune, noting that in the zero training error regime where we can perfectly interpolate, lazy training does not apply since we are at a zero gradient initialization, making linearized models useless. However, in higher dimensions, the implicit regularization through early stopping of neural network training may be the reason lazy training works well. A further limitation of this work is that our method requires the base algorithm (
e.g.
, the neural network estimation) to be stable and satisfy the condition
(
9
).

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{z_{j}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}^{\prime}]\sum_{z_{j}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}]\left\{e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\in S_{z_{j}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}},Z_{j}=z_{j}^{\prime}]+\delta\right\}

(49)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{z_{j}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}]\sum_{z_{j}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}^{\prime}]\left\{e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\in S_{z_{j}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}},Z_{j}=z_{j}^{\prime}]+\delta\right\}

(50)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{z_{j}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}]\left\{e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\in S_{z_{j}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}]+\delta\right\}

(51)

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{\epsilon}\sum_{z_{j}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}[Z_{j}=z_{j}]\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\in S_{z_{j}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}]+\delta

(52)

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\otimes Z_{j}\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}]+\delta.

(53)

With a similar argument, we can prove its symmetric counterpart:

\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})\otimes Z_{j}\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}]\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D}),Z_{j})\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}]+\delta.

(54)

Since
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}=\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\mathcal{D})
, we finish the proof that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}\otimes Z_{j}
is (
\epsilon,\delta
)-indistinguishable with
(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}},Z_{j})
given the other data points
\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}
,
i.e.
,

(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}},Z_{j})\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\approx_{\epsilon,\delta}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}\otimes Z_{j})\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

The dependency of the leave-one-out estimator
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}
on the
j
-th data point
Z_{j}=(X_{j},Y_{j})
can be controlled by

(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j})\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\approx_{\epsilon,\delta}(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}\otimes Z_{j})\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}},

(55)

i.e., for an independent copy of
Z_{j}^{\prime}
for
Z_{j}
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}((\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j})\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}((\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j}^{\prime})\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})+\delta\qquad\text{and}\\
&\mathbb{P}((\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j})\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})\geq e^{-\epsilon}\mathbb{P}((\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j}^{\prime})\in S\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})-\delta.\end{split}

(56)

Proof.

Recall the definition of
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}
:

\begin{split}\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}=\widehat{\theta}^{\text{DP}}_{n}+\arg\min_{\Delta\theta}\left\{\sum_{i\in[n]\backslash\{j\}}\mathcal{L}\left(Y_{i},f(X_{i},\widehat{\theta}^{\text{DP}}_{n})+\Delta\theta^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{i};\theta)|_{\theta=\widehat{\theta}^{\text{DP}}_{n}}\right)+\lambda\|\Delta\theta\|^{2}\right\}.\end{split}

Denote
\widehat{G}(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}:=\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};~\theta)\cdot{\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};~\theta)}^{\top}
as the gram matrix (
aka.
the NTK matrix) on data
\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}},\mathbf{Y}_{\backslash{j}})
. Define
g(\cdot|\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}):\Theta\mapsto\Theta
:

\begin{split}g(\theta\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})\coloneqq\theta+{\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};~\theta)}^{\top}{\left(\widehat{G}(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};\theta)+\lambda I_{n-1}\right)}^{-1}\cdot\left[\mathbf{Y}_{\backslash{j}}-f(\mathbf{X}_{\backslash{j}};~\theta)\right];\end{split}

then the explicit form of
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}
can be represented as:

\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}\>[|\>\big]\mathbf{z}_{\backslash{j}}=g(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}|\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}=\mathbf{z}_{\backslash{j}})=g(\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{D})|\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}).

(57)

For any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Theta\times(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
,
\mathcal{S}_{g,\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}}\coloneqq\{(\theta,z):\forall z\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}),(g(\theta|\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}),z)\in\mathcal{S}\}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j})\in\mathcal{S}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(58a)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{z_{j}\in(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})}\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=z_{j})\mathbb{P}\left[(g(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}),Z_{j})\in\mathcal{S}|\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}},Z_{j}=z_{j}\right]

(58b)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\left[(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}},Z_{j})\in\mathcal{S}_{g,\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(58c)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}\otimes Z_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{g,\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta

(58d)

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}\otimes Z_{j}\in\mathcal{S}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta.

(58e)

where (
58d
) is derived from
Lemma

A.2
.
Similarly, we can show that

\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},Z_{j})\in\mathcal{S}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]\geq e^{-\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}\otimes Z_{j}\in\mathcal{S}\>[|\>\big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]-\delta,

finishing the proof of
Lemma

A.3
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

(Closeness of in-sample and out-of-sample stability)
For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg|\mathbb{P}\left[\left|f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|>x\right]

(59)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{P}\left[\left|f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|>x\right]\Bigg|\leq 2\epsilon+\delta.

(60)

Remark A.1
.

As we can see,
\left|f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|
illustrates the change of
out-of-sample
prediction when a training data
Z_{j}
is removed in the model training, while
\left|f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|
depicts the change of
in-sample
prediction when a training data
Z_{j}
is removed. Normally the in-sample and out-of-sample stability are different and have to be considered separately as in
Foygel Barber et al. 2019
, but due to the differential privacy in the training algorithm, we can show through the following lemma that these two types of stabilities can be bridged by differential privacy, therefore we only need out-of-sample stability in the algorithm.

Proof.

First, we know by the definition of
\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}
and the fact that
\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp\{(X_{j},Y_{j})\}
, we know that
\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\perp\!\!\!\!\perp(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{j})\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}
; therefore, for any
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\Theta
and
S_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{j})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(61)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\in S_{1}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]\cdot\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{j})\in S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(62)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\in S_{1}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]\left\{e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta\right\}

(63)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\in S_{1}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta

(64)

\displaystyle\qquad=e^{\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta.

(65)

Here (
62
) and (
65
) holds true due to the conditional independence; (
63
) is from
Lemma

A.3
.
By the same argument, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{j})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(66)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\geq e^{-\epsilon}\mathbb{P}\left[(\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3}\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]-\delta.

(67)

Hence for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\Bigg|\mathbb{P}\left[\left|f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{n+1};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|>x\right]

(68a)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{P}\left[\left|f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})-f(X_{j};~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})\right|>x\right]\Bigg|

(68b)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\Theta\\
S_{3}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}\Bigg|\mathbb{P}\left[((\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3})\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]

(68c)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{P}\left[((\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{j})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3})\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]\Bigg|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq\Theta\\
S_{3}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}\left|(e^{\epsilon}-1)\mathbb{P}\left[((\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}},\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}},X_{n+1})\in S_{1}\times S_{2}\times S_{3})\>[|\>\Big]\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}\right]+\delta\right|

(68d)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq e^{\epsilon}-1+\delta\leq 2\epsilon+\delta,

(68e)

for any
\epsilon<1/2
.
∎

Appendix B
Appendix. Discussion of the Stability Condition

B.1
The Laplacian Mechanism

Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and a norm function
\|\cdot\|
over the range of
\mathcal{A}
, the sensitivity of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as

s(\mathcal{A})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(D,D^{\prime})=1,\\
\text{card}(D)=n\end{subarray}}\|\mathcal{A}(D)-\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\|.

(69)

(Usually the norm function
\|\cdot\|
is either
\ell_{1}
or
\ell_{2}
norm.)

Based on the definition of sensitivity defined as
Equation

69
, the Laplacian mechanism to construct differential private algorithm is as follows
(
Koufogiannis et al. 2015
,
Holohan et al. 2018
)
: for an algorithm (or function)
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{D}\mapsto\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{M}
, the random function
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(d)=\mathcal{A}(d)+\xi,~\forall d\in\mathcal{D}
satisfies
(\epsilon,0)
-differential privacy, where
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
follows a Laplacian distribution
\text{Lap}(0,s(\mathcal{A})/\epsilon)
:

p(\xi)\propto e^{-\epsilon\|\xi\|/s(\mathcal{A})}.

Specifically, let
\mathcal{A}
be the algorithm to find the empirical risk minimization(ERM) on a certain data set
\mathcal{D}
:

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{(X_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{D}}[Y_{i}-f(X_{i};\theta)]^{2};

Let
s(\mathcal{A})
be it sensitivity defined in
Equation

69
.

Let
\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}\backslash\{Z_{j}\})
be the ERM estimations respectively on the full training data
\mathcal{D}
and leave-one-out data
\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}}
;

Consider the
(\epsilon,0)
-DP algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\text{DP}}(\cdot)=\mathcal{A}(\cdot)+\xi,~\xi\sim\text{Lap}(0,s(\mathcal{A})/\epsilon)
, and we have the
(\epsilon,0)
-DP estimators

\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}=\widehat{\theta}_{n}+\xi;~~\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}=\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}+\xi^{\prime}.

Assumption B.1
.

The parametrization of neural network
f(\cdot;\theta)
is differentiable with a locally Lipschitz differential
\nabla_{\theta}f
.

Assumption B.2
.

(Local strong convexity of neural networks)
For all
i\in[n]
,
f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta)
is locally Lipschitz continuous w.r.t
\theta
with Lipschitz constant
\text{Lip}(f)
, and
\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta)
is Lipschitz continuous with a Lipschitz constant
\text{Lip}(\nabla f)
, i.e., for any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{r}(\widehat{\theta}_{-j})
with
r=2(s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})})
:

\displaystyle\|f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{1})-f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{2})\|_{n}\leq\text{Lip}(f)\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|;

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{1})-\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{2})\|_{n}\leq\text{Lip}(\nabla f)\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|,

where
\|\cdot\|_{n}
is defined as the empirical norm.

According to
Lemma

B.1
, for one-hidden-layer neural networks with activation function being ReLU, sigmoid or tanh, can be satisfied. We’ll revisit this assumption in
Section

B.2
.

Theorem B.1
.

Consider the Laplacian mechanism with
(\epsilon,0)
-DP parameters. Suppose
B.1
and
B.2
and
s(\mathcal{A})=O(1/n)
, with probability at least
1-e^{-c\epsilon\sqrt{n}}
for some constant
c
,
we have

|f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})-f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})|=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}(1/n)}{n}\right).

(70)

Proof.

Step 1. Show that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}
and
\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}
are both in the neighborhood of
\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}
with high probability.

Recall the fact that
\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}=\widehat{\theta}_{n}+\xi
, by triangle inequality and the fact that
\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|=\|\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}_{\backslash{j}})\|\leq s(\mathcal{A})
, we have

\displaystyle\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|\leq\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|+\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}-\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}\|\leq s(\mathcal{A})+\|\xi\|;

Therefore, by the property of
\text{Lap}(0,\frac{s(\mathcal{A})}{\epsilon})
that
P(\|\xi\|>t)\leq\exp[-\frac{\epsilon t}{s(\mathcal{A})}]
, we have

\displaystyle P\left(\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|>s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle P\left(s(\mathcal{A})+\|\xi\|>s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}\right);

On the other hand,
\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}
is obtained by adding perturbations
\xi^{\prime}
around the
\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}
,
therefore we have

\displaystyle P\left(\|\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|>\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)\leq P\left(\|\xi^{\prime}\|>\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}\right).

Hence with probability at least
1-2e^{-\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}}
, we have
\|\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}+s(\mathcal{A})
and
\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}+s(\mathcal{A})
.

Step 2. Show that
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}
are close.

Recall that
\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}=g_{\backslash{j}}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}})
and

\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}=g_{\backslash{j}}(\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}})
,
denote
\nabla f(\theta_{0})=\nabla_{\theta}f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times M}
,
and the linearized function of
f(x;\theta)
around
\theta_{0}
as

\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta)=f(x;\theta_{0})+(\theta-\theta_{0})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f(x;\theta_{0})

then
g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})
is the ERM of the linearized function
\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta)
on the LOO data set:

\begin{split}&g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})=\theta_{0}+{\nabla f(\theta_{0})}^{\top}{\left(\nabla f(\theta_{0}){\nabla f(\theta_{0})}^{\top}+\lambda I_{n-1}\right)}^{-1}\left[\mathbf{Y}_{-j}-f(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\theta_{0})\right]\\
&=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\sum_{i\neq j}\left[Y_{i}-f(X_{i};\theta_{0})-(\theta-\theta_{0})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f(X_{i};\theta_{0})\right]^{2}+\lambda\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2}\right\}\\
&\coloneqq\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}{\mathcal{L}_{-j}}(\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta))+\lambda\|\theta-\theta_{0}\|^{2};\end{split}

(71)

Define the gradient flow of
{\mathcal{L}_{-j}}(\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta))
in the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{M}
as
\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})
, which satisfies
\bar{\theta}(0;\theta_{0})=\theta_{0}
and solves the ODE:

\bar{\theta}^{\prime}(t;\theta_{0})=-\nabla{\mathcal{L}_{-j}}\left(\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0}))\right)=\left[\mathbf{Y}_{-j}-\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(\mathbf{X}_{-j};\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0}))\right]^{\top}\nabla f(\theta_{0});

(72)

On the other hand, as
f(x;\theta)\approx f(x;\theta_{0})+(\theta-\theta_{0})^{\top}\nabla_{\theta}f(x;\theta_{0})=\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta)
, we define the gradient flow on the neural network
f(\cdot,\theta)
initialized at
\theta_{0}
(
i.e.
,
{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})=\theta_{0}
) and solves the ODE:

{\theta}^{\prime}(t;\theta_{0})=-\nabla{\mathcal{L}_{-j}}\left({f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;{\theta}(t;\theta_{0}))\right)=\left[\mathbf{Y}_{-j}-{f}(\mathbf{X}_{-j};{\theta}(t;\theta_{0}))\right]^{\top}\nabla f(\theta(t;\theta_{0}));

(73)

By Theorem 2.3 in
Chizat et al. 2020
, we can show that

\|{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})-\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})\|\leq t^{2}\text{Lip}(f)^{4}\text{Lip}(\nabla f)\left(\frac{2}{\text{Lip}(f)}+\frac{4t}{3}\text{Lip}(f)\right)\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|^{2};

(74)

By the strong convexity and continuity of
\mathcal{L}_{-j}(\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta))
, we can show that
\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})
converge to its minimizer
g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})
(take
\lambda=0
) fast:

\|\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})-g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})\|\leq(\mu_{0}/m_{0})^{2}\text{Lip}(f)\exp(-m_{0}\lambda_{0}t)\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|,

(75)

where
\mathcal{L}_{-j}(\bar{f}_{\theta_{0}}(x;\theta))
is
\mu_{0}
strong convex and
m_{0}
Lipschitz continuous, and
\lambda_{0}>0
is the smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla f(\theta_{0})\nabla f(\theta_{0})^{\top}
.

By Lemma B.1 in
Chizat et al. 2020
, if
\mathcal{L}_{-j}(f(x;\theta)):\mathbb{R}^{M}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is
m
strong convex with
\mu
-Lipschitz continuous gradient and the smallest eigenvalue of
\nabla f(\theta(t))\nabla f(\theta(t))^{\top}
lower bounded by
\lambda_{\min}>0
for
0\leq t\leq T
, then
\theta(t;\theta_{0})
can converge fast to the minimizer
\widehat{\theta}_{-j}
:

\|\theta(t;\theta_{0})-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|\leq(\mu/m)^{2}\text{Lip}(f)\exp(-m\lambda_{\min}t)\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|.

(76)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|

(77)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|g_{\backslash{j}}(\theta_{0})-\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})\|+\|\theta(t;\theta_{0})-\bar{\theta}(t;\theta_{0})\|+\|\theta(t;\theta_{0})-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|

(78)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left[\log\frac{1}{\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|}\right]^{2}\cdot\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|^{2},

(79)

here we take
t=O\left(\log\frac{1}{\|\theta_{0}-\widehat{\theta}_{-j}\|}\right)
.

By the result from the first step that
\|\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}+s(\mathcal{A})
and
\|\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}}-\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}\|\leq\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}+s(\mathcal{A})
w.h.p, as well as the fact that

\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}=g_{\backslash{j}}(\widehat{\theta}_{n}^{\text{DP}})
and

\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}=g_{\backslash{j}}(\widehat{\theta}_{\backslash{j}}^{\text{DP}})
, we have

\|\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\|\leq C\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}\right)\left(s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)^{2}.

(80)

Step 3. Complete the statement

With probability at least
1-2e^{-\epsilon/\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}
, we have

\displaystyle|f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})-f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})|\leq\text{Lip}(f)\|\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\text{Lip}(f)\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}}\right)\left(s(\mathcal{A})+\sqrt{s(\mathcal{A})}\right)^{2}.

Hence, suppose
s(\mathcal{A})=O(1/n)
, we have with probability at least
1-e^{-c\epsilon\sqrt{n}}
for some
c>0
,

|f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{n,\backslash{j}})-f(x;~\widetilde{\theta}_{\backslash{j},\backslash{j}})|=O\left(\frac{\log^{2}(n)}{n}\right).

∎
