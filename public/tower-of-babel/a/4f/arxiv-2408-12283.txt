On the convergence of higher order finite element methods for nonlinear magnetostatics
H. Egger 1,2 , F. Engertsberger 1 , and B. Radu 2

Abstract
. The modeling of electric machines and power transformers typically involves systems of nonlinear magnetostatics or -quasistatics, and their efficient and accurate simulation is required for the reliable design, control, and optimization of such devices. We study the numerical solution of the vector potential formulation of nonlinear magnetostatics by means of higher-order finite element methods. Numerical quadrature is used for the efficient handling of the nonlinearities and domain mappings are employed for the consideration of curved boundaries. The existence of a unique solution is proven on the continuous and discrete level and a full convergence analysis of the resulting finite element schemes is presented indicating order optimal convergence rates under appropriate smoothness assumptions. For the solution of the nonlinear discretized problems, we consider a Newton method with line search for which we establish global linear convergence with convergence rates that are independent of the discretization parameters. We further prove local quadratic convergence in a mesh-dependent neighborhood of the solution which becomes effective when high accuracy of the nonlinear solver is demanded. The assumptions required for our analysis cover inhomogeneous, nonlinear, and anisotropic materials, which may arise in typical applications, including the presence of permanent magnets. The theoretical results are illustrated by numerical tests for some typical benchmark problems.



Keywords:

Nonlinear magnetostatics, higher order finite element methods, error estimates, Newton method, global convergence, mesh independent convergence, anisotropic materials, electric machine simulation

1.
Introduction

Problems in nonlinear magnetostatics arise in the modeling of high-power low-frequency applications like electric machines or power transformers
[
29
,
37
]
. Their efficient simulation is required for optimization and control of such devices; see e.g.
[
24
,
30
]
.
In this paper, we consider systems of the form

(1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}h

\displaystyle=j_{s}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\qquad

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\partial_{b}w(b),

(2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}b

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\qquad

\displaystyle b\cdot n

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.

As usual,
h
and
b
denote the magnetic field and flux densities, and
j_{s}=\operatorname{curl}h_{s}
is the driving current which is represented here by a source field
h_{s}
; see
[
10
,
32
]
.
Following
[
40
]
, we describe the material behavior via a magnetic energy density
w(\cdot)
, which may additionally vary in space, so that
h=\partial_{b}w(b)
is to be understood as a short-hand notation for
h(x)=\partial_{b}w(x,b(x))
.
This form is general enough to account for inhomogeneous, nonlinear, anisotropic materials
[
40
]
, and also allows to incorporate permanent magnets, as illustrated in Section
7
.
In the case of isotropic materials, one simply has
w(b)=\widetilde{w}(|b|)
and hence
h=\nu(|b|)\,b
with chord reluctivity
\nu(s)=\frac{\widetilde{w}^{\prime}(s)}{s}
; see
[
27
,
32
]
for instance.

Energy-based approach.

Following standard computational practice, we use a vector potential
a
in the sequel to represent the magnetic field in the form

(3)

\displaystyle b=\operatorname{curl}a.

This allows to reduce the system (
1
)–(
2
) to the well-known vector potential formulation of magnetostatics; see e.g.
[
7
,
32
]
and
[
3
,
10
]
for extensions to magneto-quasistatics.
Instead of treating the resulting nonlinear differential equations directly, we here use the fact that the solution of (
1
)–(
2
) can be characterized equivalently as minimizer of a magnetic energy functional, i.e.

(4)

\displaystyle\min_{a\in V_{0}}\int_{\Omega}w(\operatorname{curl}a)-h_{s}\cdot\operatorname{curl}a\,dx.

The additional boundary and gauging conditions required for unique solvability are incorporated into the set
V_{0}
over which the minimization takes place; see Section
2
for details.
This
variational formulation
of nonlinear magnetostatics allows for a systematic analysis of the problem and further opens the way for the efficient numerical approximation by finite element methods and iterative solvers; see
[
7
,
32
]
and the references given there.
Similar arguments also apply to the scalar potential formulation of nonlinear magnetostatics
[
20
,
32
]
. A detailed analysis for the latter will be presented elsewhere.

Scope and main contributions.

We here consider the systematic numerical approximation of (
1
)–(
2
) by higher–order finite elements
[
15
,
38
]
. Numerical quadrature is used to efficiently handle the nonlinear terms and domain mappings are employed to represent geometries with curved boundaries. A Newton method with line search is considered for the iterative solution of the resulting finite dimensional minimization problems.
The variational form (
4
) of our model problem will play a key role in the numerical analysis of the approximation as well as the iterative solution process.
Our first contributions are

\bullet

to prove well-posedness of the Galerkin approximation for (
4
) under general assumptions on the problem data and the discretization;

\bullet

to establish order optimal error estimates in the presence of quadrature errors and under suitable smoothness assumptions for the true solution.

Finite element methods for Maxwell’s equations are widely used in computational practice
[
25
,
31
,
39
]
and they have also been studied intensively in the literature; see
[
7
,
15
,
33
]
for an introduction and further references.
An extensive amount of work exists concerning error estimates for linear problems; see e.g.
[
5
,
28
,
33
]
and the references given there.
Only a few results, however, seem available concerning a rigorous error analysis for nonlinear problems.
Let us explicitly mention
[
27
]
, where such an analysis is presented for problems in two space dimensions and for inhomogeneous but isotropic materials.
In this paper, we consider two- and three-dimensional problems, rather general material laws, and we also consider the effect of numerical quadrature and domain mappings, which are required to efficiently handle higher order approximations on curved domains.
Our further contributions are

\bullet

to establish
global linear convergence
of the damped Newton method with a convergence factor independent of the discretization parameters;

\bullet

to additionally prove
local quadratic convergence
with convergence radius depending on the mesh size and polynomial degree.

While the first result is of key relevance for computations with lower–order finite elements, the latter property becomes particularly important if high accuracy in the nonlinear solvers is required.
The iterative solution of nonlinear systems arising in magnetostatics has of course also been studied intensively in the literature; see the references given below.
We here consider more general material laws and prove global convergence of the proposed algorithms, whose design and analysis are strongly based on the variational formulation (
4
) of the problem and the corresponding discretization schemes.
We show that the convergence is at least linear with a convergence factor independent of the meshsize and the polynomial degree, while the onset of quadratic convergence depends on the discretization parameters.
As observed by Dular et al.
[
19
]
, the convergence behavior additionally depends on the nonlinearity of the material law.
A similar analysis can be performed for other iterative methods, including fixed-point iterations
[
17
]
, the Kacanov iteration
[
21
]
, and different variants of the Newton method
[
6
,
22
,
41
]
. A related analysis for the scalar potential formulation of magnetostatics was given in
[
20
]
and for abstract variational problems in
[
26
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

In the following four sections, we first present and then prove our main results for problems on polyhedral domains in three space dimensions. Domains with curved boundaries and in two-space dimensions will be discussed in Section
6
.

2.1.
Notation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be some open set. We write
L^{2}(\Omega)
for the space of square integrable functions and denote by
\langle f,g\rangle_{\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}f\cdot g\,dx
the scalar product of
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
, respectively.
The associated norms are designated by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
The spaces
H^{k}(\Omega)
consist of those functions with square integrable derivatives of order
\leq k
. By
H(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
and
H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
, we denote the space of functions in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
with
\operatorname{curl}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
and
\operatorname{div}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively; see e.g.
[
5
,
33
]
.
We write
H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
and
H_{0}(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
for the subspaces of
H(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
and
H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
with vanishing tangential resp. normal traces at the boundary.
To guarantee uniqueness and consistency of the representation (
3
), we require the domain
\Omega
to be of trivial topology and consider the stable decomposition

(5)

\displaystyle H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)=\nabla H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\oplus V_{0}.

The space
V_{0}
can be chosen, e.g., as the orthogonal complement of
\nabla H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, but all the following results will be independent of the particular choice of
V_{0}
.
Note that
\|v\|_{\operatorname{curl}}:=\|\operatorname{curl}v\|_{L^{2}}
defines a norm on
V_{0}
; see
[
4
,
33
]
.

2.2.
Main assumptions and well-posedness

In the main parts of our analysis, we use the following conditions for the problem data.

Assumption 1
.

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain of trivial topology, i.e., simply connected with connected boundary.
The energy density
w:\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
is piecewise continuous with respect to the first argument and satisfies

\bullet

w(x,\cdot)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

|w(x,0)|+|\partial_{b}w(x,0)|\leq C
,

\bullet

\gamma\,|\xi|^{2}\leq\langle\partial_{bb}w(x,\eta)\,\xi,\xi\rangle\leq L\,|\xi|^{2}\quad\forall\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

for all
x\in\overline{\Omega}
with uniform constants
L,\gamma>0
.
The source current density finally satisfies
j_{s}=\operatorname{curl}h_{s}
for some
h_{s}\in H(\operatorname{curl})
, and hence
\operatorname{div}j_{s}=0
.

Remark 2
.

For every point in space, the energy functional
w(x,\cdot)
is smooth, strongly coercive, and quadratically bounded. Furthermore

(6)

\displaystyle\gamma|a-b|^{2}\leq\langle\partial_{b}w(x,a)-\partial_{b}w(x,b),a-b\rangle\leq L|a-b|^{2}

holds uniformly for all
x\in\Omega
and all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, i.e.,
\partial_{b}w(x,\cdot)
is strongly monotone and Lipschitz continuous
[
42
]
.
In our analysis, we will usually drop the spatial dependence and simply write
w(b)
instead of
w(x,b)
and so on.

The above assumptions allow us to establish the well-posedness of problem (
1
)–(
2
). For completeness and later reference, we state the result in detail.

Theorem 3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold.
Then the nonlinear variational problem

(7)

\displaystyle\min_{a\in V_{0}}\int_{\Omega}w(\operatorname{curl}a)-h_{s}\cdot\operatorname{curl}a\,dx

has a unique solution
a\in V_{0}
which is characterized equivalently as the unique solution to the variational identity

(8)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a),\operatorname{curl}v\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall v\in V_{0}.

The functions
b=\operatorname{curl}a
and
h=\partial_{b}w(b)
in turn correspond to the unique weak solution of the boundary value problem (
1
)–(
2
).

Proof.

By our assumptions, the system (
7
) amounts to a convex minimization problem over the Hilbert spaces
V_{0}\subset H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
, with (
8
) denoting the necessary and sufficient optimality conditions.
Existence of a unique solution to (
8
), on the other hand, can be established by the Zarantonello lemma; see
[
42
]
.
For convenience of the reader, we recall the main arguments:
Let
\tau>0
and

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\tau}:V_{0}\to V_{0},\quad a\mapsto w:=\Phi_{\tau}(a)

be defined implicitly via the variational problem

(10)

\displaystyle\langle\operatorname{curl}w,\operatorname{curl}v\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\operatorname{curl}a,\operatorname{curl}v\rangle_{\Omega}-\tau\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a)-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v\rangle_{\Omega}

for all
v\in V_{0}
.
The Lax-Milgram lemma
[
42
, Theorem 18.E]
provides existence of a unique solution
w\in V_{0}
for any
a\in V_{0}
given. Hence
\Phi_{\tau}:V_{0}\to V_{0}
is well–defined. For sufficiently small step size
\tau>0
, it can further be shown to be contractive.
To see this, let
a_{1}
,
a_{2}\in V_{0}
be given and expand

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}\Phi_{\tau}(a_{1})-\operatorname{curl}\Phi_{\tau}(a_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\operatorname{curl}a_{1}-\operatorname{curl}a_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\tau^{2}\|\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{1})-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-2\tau\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{1})-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{2}),\operatorname{curl}a_{1}-\operatorname{curl}a_{2}\rangle_{\Omega}.

The conditions on the energy functional
w(\cdot)
in Assumption
1
guarantee global Lipschitz continuity
\|\partial_{b}w(u)-\partial_{b}w(v)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq L\|u-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and uniform monotonicity
\langle\partial_{b}w(u)-\partial_{b}w(u),u-v\rangle_{\Omega}\geq\gamma\|u-v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for all
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}
; see Remark
2
.
Together with the previous formula, this yields

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}(\Phi_{\tau}(a_{1})-\Phi_{\tau}(a_{2}))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq(1-2\tau\gamma+\tau^{2}L^{2})\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{1}-a_{2})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
\|\operatorname{curl}\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
is a norm on
V_{0}
, we see that the mapping
\Phi_{\tau}
is contractive on
V_{0}

for any
0<\tau<2\gamma/L^{2}
.
Hence the fixed-point problem
a=\Phi_{\tau}(a)
has a unique solution
a\in V_{0}
for such
\tau
.
From
b=\operatorname{curl}a
, we further obtain
\operatorname{div}b=0
, and using
h=\partial_{b}w(b)=\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a)
, we see that (
8
) amounts to the weak form of (
2
).
Hence any sufficiently regular solution of (
1
)–(
2
) also solves the variational principle (
8
), and vice versa.
∎

Remark 4
.

For later reference, let us also mention the following simple fact.
Since
H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)=\nabla H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\oplus V_{0}
and
\operatorname{curl}(\nabla\phi)=0
for all
\phi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, the variational identity (
8
) actually holds for all
v\in H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
.

3.
Finite element method

For the numerical solution of the magnetostatic problem (
1
)–(
2
), we consider finite element approximations of the variational problems (
7
) resp. (
8
). Numerical quadrature is used for handling the nonlinear terms.
For ease of presentation, we assume in this section that the domain
\Omega
is polyhedral.
The extension to domains with curved boundaries will be discussed in Section
6.1
.

3.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}
be a tetrahedral finite mesh of
\Omega
and
P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
denote the space of piecewise polynomials of degree
\leq k
on
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
Further, let

(11)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{h}=\sum\nolimits_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{\ell}u(x_{T,j})\cdot v(x_{T,j})\,\hat{w}_{j}|T|

denote an approximation for the
L^{2}
-scalar product
\langle u,v\rangle_{\Omega}
obtained by applying some quadrature rule to the integration on every element
T
.
We write
\|\cdot\|_{h}
for the corresponding norm.
Without further mentioning, we assume that the integration points
x_{T,j}=\phi_{T}(\hat{x}_{j})
are mapped from the reference element
\hat{T}
in the usual manner; see
[
11
]
for details.
Throughout our analysis, we make the following additional assumptions concerning the discretization.

Assumption 5
.

\mathcal{T}_{h}
is a geometrically conforming and uniformly shape-regular simplicial partition of
\Omega
; see
[
11
]
.
The local quadrature rule
(\hat{x}_{j},\hat{w}_{j})
has positive weights and is exact for piecewise polynomials of degree
\leq 2k
on
T
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{h}=\langle u,v\rangle_{\Omega}\qquad\forall u,v\in P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}.

Further let
W_{h}=\mathcal{N}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H_{0}(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)
be the Nedelec space of order
k
; see
[
5
,
34
]
.
This space is decomposed as
W_{h}=\nabla S_{h}\oplus V_{h}

with
S_{h}=P_{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
.
We assume that this splitting is stable, i.e.,
\|v_{h}\|_{H(\operatorname{curl})}\leq C_{*}\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{L^{2}}
for some
C_{*}>0
and for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
.
Furthermore, assume that
h_{s}\in H(\operatorname{curl};\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}
.

Remark 6
.

The latter condition allows us to evaluate the source field
h_{s}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega
, which is required to perform numerical quadrature.
An efficient implementation of a basis for the reduced Nedelec space
V_{h}
can be obtained by tree-cotree gauging and hierarchical basis constructions; see
[
2
,
18
]
and
[
38
,
36
]
for details.
Further note that

(12)

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}(V_{h})=\{z_{h}\in Z_{h}:\operatorname{div}z_{h}=0\},

where
Z_{h}=\mathcal{RT}_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\cap H_{0}(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
is the Raviart-Thomas space of order
k
; see again
[
5
]
for a definition of this space.
From the exactness properties of the quadrature rule, one can infer that
\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{h}=\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{L^{2}}
for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
, and hence
\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{h}
defines a norm on the approximation space
V_{h}
.

3.2.
Finite element method

For the actual discretization of our model problem (
1
)–(
2
), we consider the finite dimensional minimization problem

(13)

\displaystyle\min_{a_{h}\in V_{h}}\langle w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}),1\rangle_{h}-\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}a_{h}\rangle_{h},

which can be interpreted as an inexact Galerkin approximation of (
7
).
We note that this problem is suitable for an efficient implementation.
Let us start with establishing the well-posedness of the discretized problem.

Theorem 7
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
be valid. Then the finite–dimensional minimization problem (
13
) has a unique solution
a_{h}\in V_{h}
.

Proof.

By our assumptions, problem (
13
) is convex. The corresponding necessary and sufficient first-order optimality conditions for a minimum read

(14)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}),\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}=\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

The existence of a unique solution
a_{h}\in V_{h}
to this finite–dimensional nonlinear problem follows with the same arguments as used in the proof of Theorem
3
and using that
\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{h}
defines a norm on
V_{h}
; see Assumption
5
.
∎

3.3.
Error estimates

We start by collecting some auxiliary results about projection operators that will be required in our analysis below.

Lemma 8
.

Let Assumption
5
hold and let
V_{0}
,
V_{h}
, and
Z_{h}
be the function spaces introduced above.
Then there exist projection operators
\pi_{h}^{*}:V_{0}\cap H^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}\to V_{h}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}:H_{0}(\operatorname{div})\cap H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}\to Z_{h}
such that
\operatorname{curl}(\pi_{h}^{*}a)=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}\operatorname{curl}(a)
. Moreover

(15)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}(\pi_{h}^{*}a-a)\|_{L^{2}}+\|\operatorname{curl}(\pi_{h}^{*}a-a)\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{j}\|\operatorname{curl}a\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}

(16)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}b-b\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}b-b\|_{h}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{j}\|b\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}

for all
2\leq j\leq k+1
and all
a\in V_{0}\cap H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}
,
b\in H_{0}(\operatorname{div})\cap H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}
.

Proof.

We choose
\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}
as the standard Raviart-Thomas projection operator, which already implies the second error estimate; see
[
5
]
for details.
The projection operator
\pi_{h}^{*}
can then be constructed as follows: Let
\pi_{h}:H_{0}(\operatorname{curl})\cap H^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}\to W_{h}
denote the standard Nedelec projection operator and
a\in V_{0}\cap H^{2}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
.
Then the discrete function
\pi_{h}a\in W_{h}
can be split uniquely as
\pi_{h}a=a_{h}^{*}+\nabla\phi_{h}
with
\phi_{h}\in S_{h}=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
and
a_{h}^{*}\in V_{h}
.
We define
\pi_{h}^{*}a:=a_{h}^{*}
and obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}(\pi_{h}^{*}a)=\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{*})=\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{*}+\nabla\phi_{h})=\operatorname{curl}(\pi_{h}a)=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}\operatorname{curl}(a).

The last identity results from the commuting diagram property of the Nedelec and Raviart-Thomas projection operators; see
[
5
, Ch. 2]
.
The error estimate for the projection
\pi_{h}^{*}
claimed in the lemma finally follows from that for
\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}
.
∎

We can now already establish our first main result, which is concerned with quantitative error estimates for the proposed discretization scheme.

Theorem 9
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
hold and
a,a_{h}
denote the unique solutions of (
7
) and (
13
).
Further set
b=\operatorname{curl}a
,
h=\partial_{b}w(b)
and
b_{h}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}
,
h_{h}=\partial_{b}w(b_{h})
, and assume that
b,h,h_{s}\in H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})
for some
2\leq j\leq k+1
.
Then

\displaystyle\|b-b_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|h-h_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\,h^{j}\,(|b|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}+|h|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}),

and the constant
C
is independent of the mesh size and the particular solution.

Proof.

We start with the estimate for
b-b_{h}=\operatorname{curl}(a-a_{h})
, which will be the main step in the proof.
By the triangle inequality, we can decompose

(17)

\displaystyle\|b-b_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|b-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(i)+(ii),

with
b_{h}^{*}=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}b=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}\operatorname{curl}a=\operatorname{curl}\pi_{h}^{*}a
denoting the projection as defined in the previous lemma.
The first term can then be bounded by
(i)\leq Ch^{j}\|b\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}

using Lemma
8
.
For the second term, we get

\displaystyle\gamma\,(ii)^{2}

\displaystyle=\gamma\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\gamma\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{h}^{2}\leq\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{h})-\partial_{b}w(b_{h}^{*}),b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\rangle_{h},

where we used the exactness of the quadrature rule in the second and the monotonicity of the energy functional in the third step.
From the variational identities (
8
) and (
14
), using
b_{h}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}
,
b_{h}^{*}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{*}
, and the exactness of the quadrature rule once again, we may further deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma\,(ii)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{h}-\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a)-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{*}),\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle\qquad+\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a),\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a),\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=(iii)+(iv)+(v).

Now let
\hat{\pi}_{h}:L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}\to P_{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})^{3}
denote the elementwise
L^{2}
-projection. Then by the exactness of the quadrature rule, reverting to
b_{h}
,
b_{h}^{*}
, and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and standard projection error estimates
[
5
,
9
]
, we get

\displaystyle(iii)=\langle h_{s}-\hat{\pi}_{h}h_{s},\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{j}\|h_{s}\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{h}.

By the Lipschitz continuity of
\partial_{b}w(\cdot)
, we further see that

\displaystyle(iv)

\displaystyle\leq L\|\operatorname{curl}a-\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{*}\|_{h}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\|_{h}\leq Ch^{j}\|b\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{h}.

For the second step, we here used
b=\operatorname{curl}a
,
\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{*}=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}\operatorname{curl}a=\tilde{\pi}_{h}^{*}b
and the estimates of Lemma
8
.
For the last term, we again employ the
L^{2}
-projection operator
\hat{\pi}_{h}
and the exactness of the quadrature rule, to see that

\displaystyle(v)

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\pi}_{h}\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a)-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a),\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\|\hat{\pi}_{h}\partial_{b}w(b)-\partial_{b}w(b)\|_{h}\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{h}\leq Ch^{j}\|\partial_{b}w(b)\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{h}.

By assumption,
h=\partial_{b}w(b)
has the required regularity.
The exactness of the quadrature rule and the definition of
b_{h}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}
and
b_{h}^{*}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{*}
then further yield
\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{*})\|_{h}=\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
In summary, we thus see that

\displaystyle\gamma\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq Ch^{j}(\|h_{s}\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}+\|b\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}+\|h\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})})\|b_{h}-b_{h}^{*}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which implies
(ii)\leq C^{\prime}h^{j}(\|h_{s}\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}+\|b\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})}+\|h\|_{H^{j}(\mathcal{T}_{h})})
. Together with the estimate for the term
(i)
, this already yields the error bound for magnetic flux.
By the Lipschitz continuity of
\partial_{b}w(\cdot)
, we finally get

\displaystyle\|h-h_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\partial_{b}w(b)-\partial_{b}w(b_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq L\|b-b_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which yields the corresponding estimate for the error in the magnetic field.
∎

Remark 10
.

The assumptions on
b
and
h
implicitly encode certain regularity conditions on
\partial_{b}w(\cdot)
which, however, do not appear explicitly in our analysis.
This greatly simplifies our arguments compared to, e.g.
[
27
]
.
Further note that, in view of approximation properties, the regularity conditions for the problem data are also necessary to obtain the predicted convergence rates.

4.
Global convergence of Newton’s method

We now study the iterative solution of the discretized nonlinear variational problem (
13
).
Since this problem is smooth and convex, we employ the Newton method with line search
[
16
,
35
]
.
The iteration thus takes the form

(18)

\displaystyle a_{h}^{n+1}=a_{h}^{n}+\tau^{n}\delta a_{h}^{n},\qquad n\geq 0,

where
a_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}
is given and the increment
\delta a_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
is defined as the solution of

(19)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}=-\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{h}^{n})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}

for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
with
b_{h}^{n}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}
introduced for abbreviation.
The step size
\tau^{n}
will be chosen by Armijo back-tracking
[
35
, Ch. 3]
, i.e., by the rule

(20)

\displaystyle\tau^{n}

\displaystyle=\max\{\tau=\rho^{k}:k\geq 0\quad\text{such that}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad W(a_{h}^{n}+\tau\delta a_{h}^{n})\leq W(a_{h}^{n})+\sigma\tau\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{h}^{n})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}\}

with parameters
0<\rho\leq 1/2
and
0<\sigma<1/2
.
Here and below, we denote by

(21)

\displaystyle W(v_{h}):=\langle w(\operatorname{curl}v_{h}),1\rangle_{h}-\langle h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h},

the discrete magnetic energy, which will play an important role in the subsequent analysis.
The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 11

(Global linear convergence)
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
hold, and
a_{h}
denote the unique solution of the discrete variational problem (
13
).
Furthermore, let
a_{h}^{n}
,
n\geq 0
be the sequence of iterates generated by (
18
)–(
20
) with initial value
a_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}
.
Then, if
\delta a_{h}^{n}=0
for some
n=n^{*}<\infty
, one has
a_{h}^{n}=a_{h}
; otherwise
n^{*}=\infty
.
Moreover

(22)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}-\operatorname{curl}a_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq C\,q^{n}\|\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{0}-\operatorname{curl}a_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

for all
n<n^{*}+1
with
C=\frac{L}{\gamma}
and contraction factor
q=1-4\rho\sigma(1-\sigma)\frac{\gamma^{3}}{L^{3}}<1
.

Remark 12
.

The theorem shows that the iterates
a_{h}^{n}
converge at least
r
-linearly
to the discrete solution
a_{h}
.
Let us emphasize that the convergence is global and the convergence factor
q
is independent of the discretization parameters.
In the next section, we will also establish local quadratic convergence, but with a convergence radius depending on the mesh size.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the proof of the previous theorem. For the convenience of the reader, we split it into several steps.

Step 1.

We first ensure that the increment
\delta a_{h}^{n}
is well-defined. To see this, let us note that
\partial_{bb}w(x,\eta)
is symmetric, uniformly bounded, and positive definite. Hence (
19
) amounts to a linear elliptic variational problem. Existence of a unique solution
\delta a_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
can thus again be established by the Lax–Milgram theorem
[
42
]
.
Moreover, we see that

(23)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{h}^{n})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle\leq-\gamma\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|^{2}_{h},

and hence
\delta a_{h}^{n}
is a descent direction
[
35
]
for the minimization problem (
13
).
As a second step, we show that Armijo back-tracking yields a reasonable step size.

Lemma 13
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
hold, and
0\neq\delta a_{h}^{n}\in V_{h}
. Then the parameter rule (
20
) defines a unique step size
\tau^{n}
satisfying

(24)

\displaystyle 0<\tau_{*}\leq\tau^{n}\leq 1

with lower bound
\tau_{*}=2\rho(1-\sigma)\gamma/L
independent of the discretization space
V_{h}
.

Proof.

We abbreviate
\psi(\tau)=W(a_{h}^{n}+\tau\delta a_{h}^{n})
.
By Taylor expansion, we get

\displaystyle\psi(\tau)=\psi(0)+\sigma\tau\psi^{\prime}(0)+\delta(\tau)

with remainder term
\delta(\tau)=(1-\sigma)\tau\psi^{\prime}(0)+\tfrac{\tau^{2}}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\tau})
and
\xi_{\tau}\in(0,\tau)
.
Using elementary computations, one can verify that

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi_{\tau})

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n}(\xi_{\tau})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

with
b_{h}^{n}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}
and
b_{h}^{n}(\xi_{\tau})=\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}+\xi_{\tau}\delta a_{h}^{n})
used for abbreviation.
By Assumption
1
, we may thus conclude that

\displaystyle\delta(\tau)\leq\left(-\gamma(1-\sigma)\tau+L\tfrac{\tau^{2}}{2}\right)\,\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{h}^{2}.

For
\tau<2(1-\sigma)\gamma/L
, the remainder
\delta(\tau)
becomes negative, i.e., the step size
\tau^{n}
chosen by (
20
) is certainly larger then
\rho
times this bound.
∎

Step 2.

The next ingredient for our analysis is an equivalence between the squared norm distance to the solution and the difference in the energy.

Lemma 14
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
hold, and let
a_{h}\in V_{h}
denote the unique solution of (
13
).
Then for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
there holds

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\|\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h})\|_{h}^{2}\leq W(v_{h})-W(a_{h})\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h})\|_{h}^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\psi(t)=W(a_{h}+t(v_{h}-a_{h}))
. Then by Taylor expansion, one can verify that

W(v_{h})-W(a_{h})=\psi(1)-\psi(0)=\psi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)
for some
\xi\in(0,1)
.
From the definition of
\psi(\cdot)
and
W(\cdot)
, we see that

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(0)=\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h})\rangle_{h}=0,

since
a_{h}
is the solution of the discrete minimization problem (
13
).
Moreover

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}(\xi))\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h}),\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h})\rangle_{h},

with
b_{h}(\xi)=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}+\xi\operatorname{curl}(v_{h}-a_{h})
.
The estimates of the lemma then follow immediately from the bounds for
\partial_{bb}w(\cdot)
provided by Assumption
1
.
∎

Step 3.

We can now establish convergence of the energy
W(a_{h}^{n})
obtained by the damped Newton iteration (
18
)–(
20
) towards the minimal value
W(a_{h})
.

Lemma 15
.

Let Assumption
1
and
5
be valid, and
a_{h}
denote the unique solution of (
13
).
Further let
a_{h}^{n}
,
n\geq 0
be the iterates obtained by (
18
)–(
20
) with
a_{h}^{0}\in V_{h}
given, and assume that
\delta a_{h}^{n}\neq 0
for all
n<n^{*}
. Then

\displaystyle W(a_{h}^{n})-W(a_{h})\leq q^{n}\,[W(a_{h}^{0})-W(a_{h})]\qquad\text{for all }n<n^{*}+1

with a uniform contraction factor
q:=1-4\rho\sigma(1-\sigma)\gamma^{3}/L^{3}<1
.

Proof.

Let us abbreviate
\nu_{h}^{n}:=\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})=\partial_{bb}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})
, which by Assumption
1
is uniformly positive definite and bounded.
We may thus introduce the scaled norm
\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}:=\langle\nu_{h}^{n}\operatorname{curl}v_{h},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}
, which by Assumption
1
satisfies

(26)

\displaystyle\gamma\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|^{2}_{h}\leq\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}\leq L\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|^{2}_{h}.

Now let
0\leq n<n^{*}
. Then by assumption and the previous results, we know that
\delta a_{h}^{n}\neq 0
, and hence (
20
) selects a step size
0<\tau_{*}\leq\tau^{n}\leq 1
such that

\displaystyle W(a_{h}^{n+1})-W(a_{h})

\displaystyle\leq W(a_{h}^{n})-W(a_{h})+\sigma\tau^{n}\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{h}^{n})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

(27)

\displaystyle=W(a_{h}^{n})-W(a_{h})-\sigma\tau^{n}\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

In the last step, we used the definition (
19
) of the Newton step and the special construction of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}
.
From the definition of
\nu_{h}^{n}
, we further obtain

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\langle\nu_{h}^{n}\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}}{\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h})-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}),\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}}{\|\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{n})\|^{2}_{h}}{\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{n})\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}}\geq\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{L}}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}-a_{h}^{n})\|_{h}.

In the second step, we used the definition of the increment
\delta\psi_{h}^{n}
and the definitions
b_{h}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}
, resp.
b_{h}^{n}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}
; in the third step, we used
v_{h}=a_{h}-a_{h}^{n}
as a test function and the monotonicity of
\partial_{b}w(\cdot)
; in the last step, we employed the norm equivalence (
26
).
Together with the estimate of the previous lemma, we thus obtain
\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}\geq\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{L^{2}}\big(W(a_{h}^{n})-W(a_{h})\big)
.
Inserting this into (
27
) and invoking the lower bound
\tau^{n}\geq\tau_{*}
then yields

\displaystyle W(a_{h}^{n+1})-W(a_{h})\leq\left(1-\tfrac{2\sigma\tau_{*}\gamma^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\,\big(W(a_{h}^{n})-W(a_{h})\big).

The assertion of the lemma now follows by inserting the definition of
\tau_{*}
.
∎

Step 4.

We can finally conclude the proof of Theorem
11
.
By combining the estimates of Lemma
14
and
15
, and using the exactness of the quadrature rule, we immediately obtain the convergence estimates of the theorem.
Now assume that
\delta a_{h}^{n}=0
for some
n=n^{*}<\infty
. Then by (
19
) we can see that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})-h_{s},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}=-\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}v_{h}\rangle_{h}=0

for all
v_{h}\in V_{h}
. As shown in the proof Theorem
7
, this identity characterizes the unique minimizer of (
13
), and hence
a_{h}^{n}=a_{h}
.
∎

5.
Local quadratic convergence

For completeness of our analysis, we further establish local quadratic convergence of the Newton method (
18
)–(
20
).
In contrast to the global linear convergence, the onset of quadratic convergence will depend on the discretization parameters via an inverse inequality.
As a preliminary step, we show that a step size
\tau^{n}=1
is chosen by (
19
) provided that
a_{h}^{n}
is sufficiently close to
a_{h}
.

Lemma 16
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
5
hold, and
let
\partial_{bb}w(\cdot)
be uniformly Lipschitz continuous with respect to
the second argument with Lipschitz constant
L^{\prime\prime}
.
Furthermore, let
\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{(1-2\,\sigma)\,\gamma}{L^{\prime\prime}\,\tau_{*}}
, which is guaranteed to occur by Theorem
11
for all
n\gg 1
.
Then the step size rule (
19
) returns
\tau^{n}=1
.

Proof.

We proceed with similar reasoning as in the proof of Lemma
13
and abbreviate
\psi(\tau)=W(a_{h}^{n}+\tau\delta a_{h}^{n})
.
Then

\displaystyle\psi(1)-\psi(0)-\sigma\psi^{\prime}(0)=(1-\sigma)\psi^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime\prime}(\xi)=:\delta(1);

compare with the proof of Lemma
13
.
By the definition of
\psi(\cdot)
and using the definition (
19
) of the Newton direction
\delta a_{h}^{n}
, one can rewrite
\delta(1)
as

\displaystyle\delta(1)=(\sigma-1)\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\partial_{bb}w(b_{h}^{n}(\xi))\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

for
b_{h}^{n}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}
and
b_{h}^{n}(\xi)=\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}+\xi\,\delta a_{h}^{n})
. By splitting the first term, we get

\displaystyle\delta(1)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sigma-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}+\frac{L^{\prime\prime}}{2}\xi\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\langle\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sigma+\frac{L^{\prime\prime}}{2\gamma}\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{\nu_{h}^{n}}^{2}.

Hence we can see that
\delta(1)
certainly becomes negative, whenever

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{(1-2\,\sigma)\,\gamma}{L^{\prime\prime}}.

Application of the estimate

\tau_{*}\|\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
then
already yields the stated result.
∎

Remark 17
.

From Theorem
11
, we know that
\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}^{n+1})\|_{L^{2}\Omega)}\to 0
with
n\to\infty
.
By using an inverse inequality
[
9
,
11
]
, we may further deduce that

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}^{n+1})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(k)h_{\min}^{-3/2}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}^{n+1})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\to 0.

Hence the conditions of the previous lemma are certainly reached after a finite number of iterations which, however, depend on the minimal mesh size
h_{\min}
and the polynomial degree
k
of the approximation.

We can now establish the local quadratic convergence of the Newton method.

Theorem 18
.

Let Assumption
1
and
5
be valid, and let
a_{h}
denote the unique solution of problem (
13
).
Further, assume that
\partial_{bb}w(\cdot)
is uniformly Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L^{\prime\prime}
.
Then

(28)

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n+1}-a_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq M_{*}(h_{\min},k)\,\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

holds with
M_{*}(h_{\min},k)=C(k)\frac{L^{\prime\prime}}{\gamma}h_{\min}^{-3/2}
, whenever
\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
is sufficiently small.
In particular, the convergence of (
18
)–(
20
) is locally quadratic.

Proof.

By the strong convexity of
w(\cdot)
and the exactness of the quadrature rule required in our assumptions, we deduce that

\displaystyle\gamma\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n+1}-a_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\partial_{bb}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n+1}-a_{h}),\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\rangle_{h}=(*),

where we introduced
w_{h}=a_{h}^{n+1}-a_{h}
for abbreviation.
Using that
\tau^{n}=1
, see Lemma
16
, we know that
a_{h}^{n+1}=a_{h}^{n}+\delta a_{h}^{n}
, which yields

\displaystyle(*)

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{bb}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h}),\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\rangle_{h}+\langle\partial_{bb}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}\delta a_{h}^{n},\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\rangle_{h}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{bb}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})-\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n})+\partial_{b}w(\operatorname{curl}a_{h}),\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\rangle_{h}.

For the last step, we used the definition of
\delta a_{h}^{n}
and (
14
).
Note that for every smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
with Lipschitz continuous derivative, we have

\displaystyle|\partial_{b}f(b_{1})\cdot(b_{1}-b_{2})-f(b_{1})+f(b_{2})|\leq\frac{L^{\prime\prime}}{2}|b_{1}-b_{2}|^{2},

which follows from Taylor estimates. Applying this estimate to
f(b)=\partial_{b}w(x,b)
with
b_{1}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}^{n}(x)
and
b_{2}=\operatorname{curl}a_{h}(x)
for every
x\in\Omega
, we get

\displaystyle(*)\leq\frac{L^{\prime\prime}}{2}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})\|_{h}\|\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\|_{h}.

Using exactness of the quadrature rule to replace
\|\cdot\|_{h}
by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, an inverse inequality
[
11
]
to estimate
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
by
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, and canceling one term
\|\operatorname{curl}w_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
on both sides, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n+1}-a_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L^{\prime\prime}C(k)}{2\gamma}h_{min}^{-3/2}\|\operatorname{curl}(a_{h}^{n}-a_{h})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

from which we conclude the local quadratic convergence.
∎

Remark 19
.

Let us emphasize that the linear convergence guaranteed by Theorem
11
is global and with a convergence factor independent of the mesh size. The onset of the local quadratic convergence, on the other hand, is mesh dependent and of relevance only, if high accuracy of the iterative solver is required, which is the case, if a higher order approximations are employed; see Section
7
.

6.
Generalizations

In the following, we show how to generalize our main results to domains with curved boundaries as well as to problems in two space dimensions, which are of interest, e.g. in electric machine simulation
[
29
,
37
]
.

6.1.
Curved domains

Let Assumption
5
be satisfied and thus
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be polyhedral and
\mathcal{T}_{h}
a corresponding simplicial mesh.
Further let
\phi:\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}
be a diffeomorphism and piecewise smooth with repect to
\mathcal{T}_{h}
.
We call
\Omega
the reference domain and
\Omega^{\prime}=\phi(\Omega)
the physical domain. In this physical domain, we consider the magnetostatic problem

(29)

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}^{\prime}h^{\prime}

\displaystyle=j^{\prime}_{s}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega^{\prime},\qquad

\displaystyle h^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\partial_{b^{\prime}}w^{\prime}(b^{\prime}),

(30)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}^{\prime}b^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega^{\prime},\qquad

\displaystyle b^{\prime}\cdot n^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega^{\prime}.

We again assume that
j_{s}^{\prime}=\operatorname{curl}^{\prime}h_{s}^{\prime}
for some
h_{s}^{\prime}\in H(\operatorname{curl}^{\prime};\Omega^{\prime})
.
We then use the pull-back principle
[
5
,
11
]
to transform this system into an equivalent problem on the reference domain
\Omega
. To do so, we use the notation
x^{\prime}=\phi(x)
,
F(x)=D\phi(x)
,
J(x)=\operatorname{det}(F(x))
and define
b(x)
,
h(x)
,
h_{s}(x)
, and
w(b)
through

\displaystyle b^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{J(x)}F(x)b(x),\qquad

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=F(x)^{-\top}h(x),

\displaystyle h_{s}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=F(x)^{-\top}h_{s}(x),\qquad

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(x^{\prime},b^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{J(x)}w(x,b(x)).

With these definitions and the transformation rules for the differential operators and the normal vector
[
4
,
5
]
, one can see that the problem (
29
)–(
30
) posed on the physical domain
\Omega^{\prime}
is equivalent to the system (
1
)–(
2
) on the reference domain
\Omega
.
From the above formulas, one can further observe that

\displaystyle\partial_{b}w(x,b)=J(x)\partial_{b}w^{\prime}(x^{\prime},\tfrac{1}{J(x)}F(x)b)=F(x)^{\top}\partial_{b^{\prime}}w^{\prime}(x^{\prime},b^{\prime}),

which follows from the above relations between the functions and the chain rule of differentiation. This is in perfect agreement with
h(x)=F(x)^{\top}h^{\prime}(x^{\prime})
and the above formulae.
As a consequence, one can see that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{b}w(b_{1})-\partial_{b}w(b_{2}),b_{1}-b_{2}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\partial_{b^{\prime}}w^{\prime}(b_{1}^{\prime})-\partial_{b^{\prime}}w^{\prime}(b_{2}^{\prime}),b_{1}^{\prime}-b_{2}^{\prime}\rangle_{\Omega^{\prime}},

which implies the strong monotonicity of
\partial_{b}w(b)
; Lipschitz continuity follows in the same way.
Also, the regularity requirements on the fields
h_{s}
,
h
, and
b
made in Theorem
9
translate verbatim to corresponding piecewise regularity conditions on the fields
h_{s}^{\prime}
,
h^{\prime}
,
b^{\prime}
on the physical domain
\Omega^{\prime}
.
Hence all results of the previous sections transfer to problems on curved domains
\Omega^{\prime}=\phi(\Omega)
.

Remark 20
.

The mapping trick utilized in the arguments above was proposed and intensively used for the study of partial differential equations on surfaces; see e.g.
[
13
,
14
]
.
Further note that the material behavior on the reference domain
\Omega
will in general be anisotropic, even if the physical behavior on
\Omega^{\prime}
was isotropic. This can be seen as a good reason to consider anisotropic material laws right from the beginning.
By the same transformation formulas, the method considered in Sections
3
–
5
could be phrased equivalently on the curved domain
\Omega^{\prime}=\phi(\Omega)
, which is the usual computational practice. By equivalence, all assertions of the previous sections carry over verbatim.

6.2.
Two space dimensions

In the context of electric machine simulation, the following two-dimensional setting is of importance
[
27
,
32
]
.
We consider

(31)

\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}h

\displaystyle=j_{s}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\qquad

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\partial_{b}w(b),

(32)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}b

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,\qquad

\displaystyle b\cdot n

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where now
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a two-dimensional domain, e.g., the cross-section of an electric motor, and
h
,
b:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the in-plane components of the magnetic field and flux, and
j_{s}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is the out-of-plane component of the driving current.
The differential operators are defined by
\operatorname{curl}h=\partial_{x}h_{2}-\partial_{y}h_{1}
and
\operatorname{div}b=\partial_{x}b_{1}+\partial_{x}b_{2}
.
Under the assumption that
\Omega
is topologically trivial, we can represent
b=\operatorname{Curl}a
with
a:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
corresponding to the out-of-plane component of the magnetic vector potential and
\operatorname{Curl}a=(\partial_{y}a,-\partial_{x}a)
denoting the scalar–to–vector
\operatorname{curl}
–operator.
As solution space for the weak formulation of the above problem, we choose

\displaystyle V_{0}=H_{0}(\operatorname{Curl})=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega):\operatorname{Curl}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\ \text{and}\ u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},

which is equipped with the graph norm
\|u\|_{H(\operatorname{Curl})}=(\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\operatorname{Curl}u\|_{L^{2}}^{2})^{1/2}
.
We note that
\|\operatorname{Curl}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\simeq\|u\|_{H(\operatorname{Curl})}
defines a norm.
All results of the previous sections then translate verbatim to the two-dimensional setting.

Remark 21
.

We note that
\operatorname{Curl}a=(\partial_{y}a,-\partial_{x}a)=(\partial_{x}a,\partial_{y}a)^{\perp}=(\nabla a)^{\perp}
, and hence
H_{0}(\operatorname{Curl};\Omega)
is often identified with
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
; see e.g.
[
27
]
.
For isotropic materials, i.e., a scalar–valued reluctivity
\nu
, one can then write

(33)

\displaystyle\langle\nu\operatorname{Curl}a,\operatorname{Curl}a^{\prime}\rangle_{\Omega}=\langle\nu\nabla a,\nabla a^{\prime}\rangle_{\Omega},

which is frequently done in the literature. Such an identification, however, introduces additional complications in the case of anisotropic materials, and we advocate to stay with the natural function space
H_{0}(\operatorname{Curl};\Omega)
instead.

7.
Numerical tests

To illustrate our theoretical results and to demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed numerical schemes, let us briefly report on some computational tests.

7.1.
Details on the implementation

In our computations, we use Nedelec elements
\mathcal{N}_{k}
on tetrahedral meshes in three dimensions. For the two-dimensional setting, we use Courant elements
P_{k+1}
on triangular meshes.
The grids are adapted to the curved boundaries of the geometries where needed.
A quadrature rule of order
2k
is applied on each element for integration in accordance with the conditions of Assumption
5
and Theorem
9
.
The nonlinear systems arising after discretization are solved by the Newton method (
18
)–(
19
) with initial iterate
a^{0}=0
and step size
\tau^{n}
determined by Armijo backtracking (
20
).
Tree-cotree gauging
[
2
,
38
]
is used to ensure uniqueness of the vector potential in three space dimensions.
The symmetric and positive definite linear systems arising in every Newton step are solved by the conjugate gradient method.
The error tolerances in the linear and nonlinear solver are chosen sufficiently small so not to influence the convergence behavior.
The three-dimensional computations were realized in the finite element package
Netgen/NGSolve

[
39
]
and
ParaView

[
1
]
was used for visualization.
The two-dimensional results were obtained and visualized in
Matlab
.

8.
Summary and Discussion

In this paper, we studied the systematic approximation of nonlinear problems in magnetostatics by higher-order finite element methods.
Numerical quadrature was considered for treating the nonlinearities and global mesh-independent convergence of a damped Newton method was established.
The consideration of an energy-based anisotropic material law was a key in the error and convergence analysis, and further allowed a seamless generalization of the results to problems domains with curved boundaries.
The feasibility of the proposed methods and validity of the theoretical results were demonstrated with computational test for some typical benchmark problems.
While the contraction factors of the Newton method were not affected by singularities of the solution, the convergence rates of the errors were of course limited.
Adaptive and anisotropic mesh refinement would be required to reveal the full order of convergence also in these examples.
The consideration of adaptive mesh refinement and a-posteriori error estimation as well as the consideration of higher-order approximations for alternative formulations of nonlinear magnetostatics are left as topics for future research.
