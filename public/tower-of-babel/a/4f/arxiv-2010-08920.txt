Average-reward model-free reinforcement learning: a systematic review and literature mapping
Vektor Dewanto, George Dunn, Ali Eshragh, Marcus Gallagher, Fred Roosta

Abstract
Reinforcement learning is important part of artificial intelligence. In this paper, we review model-free reinforcement learning that utilizes the average reward optimality criterion in the infinite horizon setting. Motivated by the solo survey by Mahadevan, 1996a , we provide an updated review of work in this area and extend it to cover policy-iteration and function approximation methods (in addition to the value-iteration and tabular counterparts). We present a comprehensive literature mapping. We also identify and discuss opportunities for future work.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) is one promising approach to
the problem of making sequential decisions under uncertainty.
Such a problem is often formulated as a Markov decision process (MDP)
with a state set
\mathcal{S}
, an action set
\mathcal{A}
,
a reward set
\mathcal{R}
, and a decision-epoch set
\mathcal{T}
.
At each decision-epoch (i.e. timestep)
t\in\mathcal{T}
,
a decision maker (henceforth, an agent) is at a state
s_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
, and
chooses to then execute an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
.
Consequently, the agent arrives at the next state
s_{t+1}
and earns an (immediate) reward
r_{t+1}
.
For
t=0,1,\ldots,t_{\mathrm{max}}
with
t_{\mathrm{max}}\leq\infty
, the agent experiences a sequence of

S_{0},A_{0},R_{1},S_{1},A_{1},R_{2},\ldots,S_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
Here,
S_{0}
is drawn from an initial state distribution
p_{s_{0}}
, whereas

s_{t+1}
and
r_{t+1}
are governed by the environment dynamics,
which is fully specified by
the state-transition probability
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\coloneqq\mathrm{Pr}\{S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}|S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a\}
, and
the reward function
r(s,a,s^{\prime})=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathrm{Pr}\{r|s,a,s^{\prime}\}\cdot r
.

The solution to the decision making problem is a mapping from every state to
a probability distribution over the set of available actions in that state.
This mapping is called a policy, i.e.
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\mapsto[0,1]
,
where
\pi(a_{t}|s_{t})\coloneqq\mathrm{Pr}\{A=a_{t}|S_{t}=s_{t}\}
.
Thus, solving such a problem amounts to finding the optimal policy, denoted by
\pi^{*}
.
The basic optimality criterion asserts that a policy with the largest value is optimal.
That is,
v(\pi^{*})\geq v(\pi),\forall\pi\in\Pi
,
where the function
v
measures the value of any policy
\pi
in the policy set
\Pi
.
There are two major ways to value a policy based on the infinite reward sequence that it generates,
namely the average- and discounted-reward policy value formulations.
They induce the average- and discounted-reward optimality criteria, respectively.
For an examination of their relationship, pros, and cons in RL,
we refer the readers to
(
Dewanto and Gallagher, 2021
)
.

Furthermore, RL can be viewed as
simulation-based asynchronous approximate dynamic programming (DP)

(
Gosavi, 2015
, Ch 7)
.
Particularly in model-free RL, the simulation is deemed expensive because
it corresponds to direct interaction between an agent and its (real) environment.
Model-free RL mitigates not only the curse of dimensionality (inherently as approximate DP methods),
but also the curse of modeling (since no model learning is required).
This is in contrast to model-based RL, where an agent interacts with
the learned model of the environment
(
Dean et al., 2017
;
Jaksch et al., 2010
;
Tadepalli and Ok, 1998
)
.
Model-free RL is fundamental in that it encompasses the bare essentials for updating
sequential decision rules through natural agent-environment interaction.
The same update machinery can generally be applied to model-based RL.
In practice, we may always desire a system that runs both model-free and model-based mechanisms,
see e.g. the so-called Dyna architecture
(
Silver et al., 2008
;
Sutton, 1990
)
.

This work surveys the existing value- and policy-iteration based
average-reward model-free RL.
We begin by reviewing relevant DP (as the root of RL), before progressing to
the tabular then function approximation settings in RL.
For comparison, the solo survey on average-reward RL
(
Mahadevan, 1996a
)

embraced only the tabular value-iteration based methods.
We present our review in Secs
3
and
4
, which are accompanied
by concise Tables
and
(in Appendix
)
along with literature maps
(Figs
,
,
, and

in Appendix
).
We then discuss the insight and outlook in Sec
5
.
Additionally in Appendix
, we compile environments
that were used for evaluation by the existing works.

In order to limit the scope, this work focuses on model-free RL
with a single non-hierarchical agent interacting
online
with its environment.
We do not include works that
approximately
optimize the average reward by
introducing a discount factor (hence, the corresponding approximation error), e.g.
Schneckenreither, 2020
;
Karimi et al., 2019
;
Bartlett and Baxter, 2002
.
We also choose not to examine RL methods that are based on
linear programming
(
Wang, 2017
;
Neu et al., 2017
)
,
and decentralized learning automata
(
Chang, 2009
;
Wheeler and Narendra, 1986
)
.

2
Preliminaries

We assume that the problem we aim to tackle can be well modeled as
a Markov decision process (MDP) with the following properties.

•

The state set
\mathcal{S}
and action set
\mathcal{A}
are finite.
All states are fully observable, and all actions
a\in\mathcal{A}

are available in every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
The decision-epoch set
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
is discrete and infinite.
Thus, we have discrete-time infinite-horizon finite MDPs.

•

The state-transition probability
p(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})
and
the reward function
r(s_{t},a_{t})
are both stationary (time homogenous, fixed over time).
Here,
r(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(s_{t},a_{t},S_{t+1})}}\right]
, and it is uniformly bounded by
a finite constant
r_{\mathrm{max}}
, i.e.
|r(s,a)|\leq r_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty,\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.

•

The MDP is recurrent, and
its optimal policies belong to the stationary policy set
\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}
.

Furthermore, every stationary policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}
of an MDP induces
a Markov chain (MC), whose transition (stochastic) matrix is denoted as

\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}
.
The
[s,s^{\prime}]
-entry of
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}
indicates the probability of transitioning
from a current state
s
to the next state
s^{\prime}
under a policy
\pi
.
That is,
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}[s,s^{\prime}]=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
.
The
t
-th power of
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}
gives
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{t}
, whose
[s_{0},s]
-entry
indicates the probability of being in state
s
in
t
timesteps
when starting from
s_{0}
and following
\pi
.
That is,
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{t}[s_{0},s]=\mathrm{Pr}\{S_{t}=s|s_{0},\pi\}\eqqcolon p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})
.
The limiting distribution of
p_{\pi}^{t}
is given by

p_{\pi}^{\star}(s|s_{0})=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}p_{\pi}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}}(s|s_{0}),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(1)

where the first limit is proven to exist in finite MDPs, while
the second limit exists whenever the finite MDP is aperiodic

(
Puterman, 1994
, Appendix A.4)
.
This limiting state distribution
p_{\pi}^{\star}
is equivalent to
the unique stationary (time-invariant) state distribution that satisfies

(\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star})^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}=(\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star})^{\intercal}
,
which may be achieved in finite timesteps.
Here,
\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star}\in[0,1]^{|\mathcal{S}|}
is

p_{\pi}^{\star}(s|s_{0})
stacked together for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

The
expected
average reward (also termed the gain) value of a policy
\pi

is defined for all
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
as

\displaystyle v_{g}(\pi,s_{0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathbb{E}_{S_{t},A_{t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}r(S_{t},A_{t})\Big|S_{0}=s_{0},\pi}}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Big\{\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})\Big\}r_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{\pi}^{\star}(s|s_{0})r_{\pi}(s)

(3)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{t_{\mathrm{max}}},A_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(S_{t_{\mathrm{max}}},A_{t_{\mathrm{max}}})\Big|S_{0}=s_{0},\pi}}\right],

(4)

where
r_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\ r(s,a)
.
The limit in (
2
) exists when the policy
\pi
is stationary,
and the MDP is finite
(
Puterman, 1994
, Prop 8.1.1)
.
Whenever it exists, the equality in (
3
) follows due to
the existence of limit in (
1
) and
the validity of interchanging the limit and the expectation.
The equality in (
4
) holds if its limit exists, for instance
when
\pi
is stationary and the induced MC is aperiodic
(nonetheless, note that even if the induced MC is periodic,
the limit in (
4
) exists for certain reward structures,
see
Puterman, 1994
).
In matrix forms, the gain can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{v}_{g}(\pi)=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\boldsymbol{v}_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\pi)=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\frac{1}{t_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{t}\boldsymbol{r}_{\pi}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{\star}\boldsymbol{r}_{\pi},

where
\boldsymbol{r}_{\pi}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}
is
r_{\pi}(s)
stacked together for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Notice that the gain involves taking the limit of the average of
the
expected
total reward
\boldsymbol{v}_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}
from

t=0
to
t_{\mathrm{max}}-1
for
t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty
.

Since unichain MDPs have a single chain (i.e. a closed irreducible recurrent class),
the stationary distribution is invariant to initial states.
Therefore,
\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{\star}
has identical rows so that the gain is
constant across all initial states.
That is,
v_{g}(\pi)=\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{\pi}=v_{g}(\pi,s_{0}),\forall s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
,
hence
\boldsymbol{v}_{g}(\pi)=v_{g}(\pi)\cdot\boldsymbol{1}
.
The gain
v_{g}(\pi)
can be interpreted as the stationary reward because it
represents the average reward per timestep of a system in its steady-state under
\pi
.

A policy
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}
is gain-optimal (hereafter, simply called optimal) if

v_{g}(\pi^{*},\cancel{s_{0}})\geq v_{g}(\pi,\cancel{s_{0}}),\quad\forall\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}},\forall s_{0}\in\mathcal{S},\quad\text{hence}\ \pi^{*}\in\argmax_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}}v_{g}(\pi)

(5)

Such an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
is also a greedy (hence, deterministic) policy
in that it selects actions maximizing the RHS of
the average-reward Bellman
optimality
equation
(which is useful for deriving optimal control algorithms) as follows,

v_{b}(\pi^{*},s)+v_{g}(\pi^{*})=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\ v_{b}(\pi^{*},s^{\prime})\Big\},\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(6)

where
v_{b}
denotes the bias value.
It is defined for all
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}}
and all
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
as

\displaystyle v_{b}(\pi,s_{0})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{t},A_{t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\Big(r(S_{t},A_{t})-v_{g}(\pi)\Big)\Big|S_{0}=s_{0},\pi}}\right]

(7)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})-p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\Big)r_{\pi}(s)

(8)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})r_{\pi}(s)}_{\text{the expected total reward $v_{\tau}$}}-\ \tau v_{g}(\pi)+\underbrace{\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\sum_{t=\tau}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Big(p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})-p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\Big)r_{\pi}(s)}_{\text{approaches 0 as $\tau\to\infty$}}

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Big\{\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\Big(p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})-p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\Big)\Big\}r_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{\pi}(s|s_{0})r_{\pi}(s),

(10)

where all limits are assumed to exist, and
(
7
) is bounded because of the subtraction of
v_{g}(\pi)
.
Whenever exchanging the limit and the expectation is valid in (
10
),

d_{\pi}(s|s_{0})
represents the
[s_{0},s]
-entry of
the non-stochastic deviation matrix

\boldsymbol{D}_{\pi}\coloneqq(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}+\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{\star})^{-1}(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{\pi}^{\star})
.

The bias
v_{b}(\pi,s_{0})
can be interpreted in several ways.

Firstly
based on (
7
), the bias is the expected total difference
between the immediate reward
r(s_{t},a_{t})
and the stationary reward
v_{g}(\pi)

when a process starts at
s_{0}
and follows
\pi
.

Secondly
from (
8
), the bias indicates the difference between
the expected total rewards of two processes under
\pi
: one starts at
s_{0}
and
the other at an initial state drawn from
p_{\pi}^{\star}
.
Put in another way, it is the difference of the total reward of
\pi
and
the total reward that would be earned if the reward per timestep were
v_{g}(\pi)
.

Thirdly
, decomposing the timesteps as in (
9
) yields

v_{\tau}(\pi,s_{0})\approx v_{g}(\pi)\tau+v_{b}(\pi,s_{0})
.
This suggests that the bias serves as the intercept of a line around which
the expected total reward
v_{\tau}
oscillates, and eventually converges as
\tau
increases.
Such a line has a slope of the gain value.
For example in an MDP with zero reward absorbing terminal state (whose gain is 0),
the bias equals the expected total reward before the process is absorbed.

Lastly
, the deviation factor
(p_{\pi}^{t}(s|s_{0})-p_{\pi}^{\star}(s))
in (
10
)
is non-zero only before the process reaches its steady-state.
Therefore, the bias indicates the transient performance.
It may be regarded as the “transient” reward, whose
values are earned during the transient phase.

For any reference states
s_{\mathrm{ref}}\in\mathcal{S}
, we can define
the (bias) relative value
v_{brel}
as follows

v_{brel}(\pi,s)\coloneqq v_{b}(\pi,s)-v_{b}(\pi,s_{\mathrm{ref}})=\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\{v_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\pi,s)-v_{t_{\mathrm{max}}}(\pi,s_{\mathrm{ref}})\},\quad\forall\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{S}},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

The right-most equality follows from (
7
) or (
9
),
since the gain are the same from both
s
and
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
It indicates that
v_{brel}
represents the asymptotic different
in the expected total reward due to starting from
s
, instead of
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
More importantly, the relative value
v_{brel}
is equal to
v_{b}

up to some constant for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Moreover,
v_{brel}(\pi,s=s_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
.
After fixing
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
, therefore, we can substitute
v_{brel}
for
v_{b}
in
(
6
), then uniquely determine
v_{brel}
for all states.
Note that (
6
) with
v_{b}
is originally
an underdetermined nonlinear system with
|\mathcal{S}|
equations and

|\mathcal{S}|+1
unknowns (i.e. one extra of
v_{g}
).

In practice, we often resort to
v_{brel}
and abuse the notation
v_{b}
to
also denote the relative value whenever the context is clear.
In a similar fashion, we call the bias as the relative/differential value.
One may also refer to the bias as relative values due to (
8
),
average-adjusted values (due to (
7
)), or
potential values (similar to potential energy in physics
whose values differ by a constant
(
Cao, 2007
, p193)
).

For brevity, we often use the following notations,

v_{g}^{\pi}\coloneqq v_{g}(\pi)\eqqcolon g^{\pi}
, and
v_{g}^{*}\coloneqq v_{g}(\pi^{*})\eqqcolon g^{*}
, as well as

v_{b}^{\pi}(s)\coloneqq v_{b}(\pi,s)
, and
v_{b}^{*}(s)\coloneqq v_{b}(\pi^{*},s)
.
Here,
v_{b}^{\pi}(s)
may be read as the relative state value of
s
under a policy
\pi
.

3
Value-iteration schemes

Based on the Bellman optimality equation (
6
),
we can obtain the optimal policy once we know the optimal state value

\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{*}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}|}
, which includes knowing the optimal gain
v_{g}^{*}
.
Therefore, one approach to optimal control is to (approximately) compute
\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{*}
,
which leads to the value-iteration algorithm in DP.
The value-iteration scheme in RL uses the same principle as its DP counterpart.
However, we approximate the optimal (state-)action value

\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}\in\real{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}
, instead of
\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{*}
.

In this section, we begin by introducing the foundations of the value-iteration scheme,
showing the progression from DP into RL.
We then cover tabular methods by presenting how to estimate the optimal action values iteratively
along with numerous approaches to estimating the optimal gain.
Lastly, we examine function approximation methods, where
the action value function is parameterized by a weight vector, and
present different techniques of updating the weights.
Table
(in Appendix
) summarizes existing
average reward model-free RL based on the value-iteration scheme.

3.1
Foundations

In DP, the basic value iteration (VI) algorithm
iteratively
solves for
the optimal state value
\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{*}
and outputs an
\varepsilon
-optimal policy;
hence it is deemed exact up to the convergence tolerance
\varepsilon
.
The basic VI algorithm proceeds with the following steps.

Step 1:

Initialize the iteration index
k\leftarrow 0
and
\hat{v}_{b}^{k=0}(s)\leftarrow 0
,
where
\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)\approx v_{b}^{*}(s),\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Also select a small positive convergence tolerance
\varepsilon>0
.

Note that
\hat{v}_{b}^{k}
may not correspond to any stationary policy.

Step 2:

Perform updates on
all
iterates (termed as
synchronous
updates)
as follows,

\hat{v}_{b}^{k+1}(s)=\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)}}\right],\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(Basic VI: synchronous updates)

where the average-reward Bellman optimality operator
\mathbb{B}^{*}
is defined as

\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)}}\right]\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\ \hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s^{\prime})\Big],\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

Clearly,
\mathbb{B}^{*}
is non-linear (due to the
\max
operator), and
is derived from (
6
) with
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow 0
.

Step 3:

If the span seminorm
sp(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{b}^{k+1}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{b}^{k})>\varepsilon
,
then increment
k
and go to Step 2.

Otherwise, output a greedy policy with respect to the RHS of (
6
);
such a policy is
\varepsilon
-optimal.
Here,
sp(\boldsymbol{v})\coloneqq\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}v(s)-\min_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}v(s^{\prime})
,
which measure the range of all components of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
.
Therefore, although the vector changes, its span may be constant.

The mapping in Step 2 of the VI algorithm is not contractive (because there is no discount factor).
Moreover, the iterates
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{b}^{k}
can grow very large, leading to numerical instability.
Therefore,
White, 1963
proposed substracting out the value of
an arbitrary-but-fixed reference state
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
at every iteration.
That is,

\hat{v}_{b}^{k+1}(s)=\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)}}\right]-\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s_{\mathrm{ref}}),\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(Relative VI: synchronous updates)

which results in the so-called
relative
value iteration (RVI) algorithm.
This update yields the same span and the same sequence of maximizing actions as
that of the basic VI algorithm.
Importantly, as
k\to\infty
, the iterate
\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})
converges to
v_{g}^{*}
.

The
asynchronous
version of RVI may diverge

Abounadi et al., 2001
, p682
,
Gosavi, 2015
, p232
.
As a remedy,
Jalali and Ferguson, 1990
introduced the following update,

\hat{v}_{b}^{k+1}(s)=\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)}}\right]-\hat{v}_{g}^{k},\quad\forall(s\neq s_{\mathrm{ref}})\in\mathcal{S},

(Relative VI: asynchronous updates)

where
\hat{v}_{g}^{k}
is the estimate of
v_{g}^{*}
at iteration
k
, and

\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})=0
, for all iterations
k=0,1,\ldots
.
It is shown that this asynchronous method converges to produce a gain optimal policy
in a regenerative process, where there exists a
single
recurrent state
under
all
stationary and deterministic policies.

In RL, the reward function
r(s,a)
and the transition distribution
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
are unknown.
Therefore, it is convenient to define a relative (state-)action value
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}(\pi)

of a policy
\pi
as follows,

\displaystyle q_{b}(\pi,s,a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{t_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{t},A_{t}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{t=0}^{t_{\mathrm{max}}-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r(S_{t},A_{t})-v_{g}^{\pi}}}\right)\Big|S_{0}=s,A_{0}=a,\pi}}\right]

\displaystyle=r(s,a)-v_{g}^{\pi}+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v_{b}^{\pi}(S_{t+1})}}\right],\qquad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

(11)

where for brevity, we define
q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)\coloneqq q_{b}(\pi,s,a)
,
as well as
q_{b}^{*}(s,a)\coloneqq q_{b}(\pi^{*},s,a)
.
The corresponding average reward Bellman optimality equation is
as follows
(
Bertsekas, 2012
, p549)
,

q_{b}^{*}(s,a)+v_{g}^{*}=r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\underbrace{\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}q_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime}),}_{\text{$v_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime})$}}\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

(12)

whose RHS is in the same form as the quantity maximized over all actions
in (
6
).
Therefore, the gain-optimal policy
\pi^{*}
can be obtained simply by acting greedily over
the optimal action value; hence,
\pi^{*}
is deterministic.
That is,

\pi^{*}(s)=\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\Big[\underbrace{r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}q_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-v_{g}^{*}}_{q_{b}^{*}(s,a)}\Big],\qquad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

As can be observed,
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
combines the effect of
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{*}
,
without estimating them separately at the cost of an increased number of
estimated values since typically
|\mathcal{S}|\times|\mathcal{A}|\gg|\mathcal{S}|
.
The benefit is that action selection via
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
does not require
the knowledge of
r(s,a)
and
p(s^{\prime}|s,a)
.
Note that
v_{g}^{*}
is invariant to state
s
and action
a
;
hence its involvement in the above maximization has no effect.

Applying the idea of RVI on action values
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
yields the following iterate,

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{b}^{k+1}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{q}_{b}^{k}(s,a)}}\right]-v_{b}^{k}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})

(Relative VI on
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
: asynchronous updates)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{k}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})}_{\mathbb{B}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\hat{q}_{b}^{k}(s,a)}}\right]}-\underbrace{\max_{a^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{k}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a^{\prime\prime})}_{\text{can be interpreted as $\hat{v}_{g}^{*}$}},

(13)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{b}^{k}
denotes the estimate of
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
at iteration
k
,
and
\mathbb{B}^{*}
is based on (
12
) so it operates on action values.
The iterates of
\hat{v}_{b}^{k}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{k}(s,a)

are conjectured to converge to
v_{b}^{*}(s)
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}

by
Bertsekas, 2012
.

3.2
Tabular methods

In model-free RL, the iteration for estimating
\boldsymbol{q}_{b}^{*}
in (
13
)
is carried out asynchronously as follows,

\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)\leftarrow\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)+\beta\big\{r(s,a)+\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\hat{v}_{g}^{*}-\ \hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)\big\},

(14)

where
\beta
is a positive stepsize, whereas
s
,
a
, and
s^{\prime}
denote
the current state, current action, and next state, respectively.
Here, the sum over
s^{\prime}
in (
13
),
i.e. the expectation with respect to
S^{\prime}
, is approximated by a single sample
s^{\prime}
.
The stochastic approximation (SA) based update in (
14
) is
the essense of
Q_{b}
-learning
(
Schwartz, 1993
, Sec 5)
, and most of its variants.
One exception is that of
Prashanth and Bhatnagar, 2011
,
where there is no subtraction of
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)
.

In order to prevent the iterate
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}
from becoming very large (causing numerical instability),

Singh, 1994
advocated assigning
q_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a_{\mathrm{ref}})\leftarrow 0
,
for arbitrary-but-fixed reference state
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
and action
a_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
Alternatively,
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996
advised setting
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},\cdot)\leftarrow 0
.
Both suggestions seem to follow the heuristics of obtaining the unique solution of
the underdetermined Bellman optimality non-linear system of equations in (
6
).

There are several ways to approximate the optimal gain
v_{g}^{*}
in (
14
),
as summarized in Fig
(Appendix
).
In particular,
Abounadi et al., 2001
proposed three variants as follows.

i.

\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a_{\mathrm{ref}})

with reference state
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
and action
a_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

Yang et al., 2016
argued that properly choosing
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
can be difficult
in that the choice of
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
affects the learning performance,
especially when the state set is large.
They proposed setting
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow c
for a constant
c
from prior knowledge.
Moreover,
Wan et al., 2020
showed empirically that such a reference
retards learning and causes divergence.
This happens when
(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a_{\mathrm{ref}})
is infrequently visited,
e.g. being a trasient state in unichain MDPs.

ii.

\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a^{\prime})
,
used by
Prashanth and Bhatnagar, 2011
.
This inherits the same issue regarding
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
as before.
Whenever the action set is large, the maximization over
\mathcal{A}
should
be estimated, yielding another layer of approximation errors.

iii.

\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow\sum_{(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)/(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)
,
used by
Avrachenkov and Borkar, 2020
.
Averaging all entries of
\hat{\boldsymbol{q}}_{b}^{*}
removes the need for
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
a_{\mathrm{ref}}
.
However, because
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}
itself is an estimating function with diverse accuracy
across all state-action pairs, the estimate
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
involves the averaged approximation error.
The potential issue due to large state and action sets is also concerning.

Equation (
14
) with one of three proposals (i - iii) for
v_{g}^{*}
estimators
constitutes the RVI
Q_{b}
-learning.
Although it operates asynchronously, its convergence is assured by decreasing the stepsize
\beta
.

The optimal gain
v_{g}^{*}
in (
14
) can also be estimated
iteratively via SA as follows,

\hat{v}_{g}^{*}\leftarrow\hat{v}_{g}^{*}+\beta_{g}\Delta_{g},\quad\text{for some update $\Delta_{g}$ and a positive stepsize $\beta_{g}$}.

(15)

Thus, the corresponding
Q_{b}
-learning becomes 2-timescale SA,
involving both
\beta
and
\beta_{g}
.
There exist several variations on
\Delta_{g}
as listed below.

i.

In
Schwartz, 1993
,

\Delta_{g}\coloneqq r(s,a)+\underbrace{\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a^{\prime})}_{\text{to minimize the variance of updates}}-\ \hat{v}_{g}^{*},\quad\text{if}\ \underbrace{a=\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a^{\prime})}_{\text{a greedy action $a$}}.

(16)

By updating only when greedy actions are executed, the influence of exploratory actions
(which are mostly suboptimal) can be avoided.

ii.

In
Singh, 1994
, and
Wan et al., 2020
,

\Delta_{g}\coloneqq\underbrace{r(s,a)+\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a)}_{\text{$v_{g}^{*}$ in expectation of $S^{\prime}$ when using $q_{b}^{*}$
(see \eqref{equ:avgrew_bellmanopt_q})}}-\ \hat{v}_{g}^{*}.

(To update at every action)

Since the equation for
v_{g}^{*}
(
12
) applies to any state-action pairs,
it is reasonable to update
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
for both greedy and exploratory actions as above.
This also implies that information from non-greedy actions is not wasted.
Hence, it is more sample-efficient than that of (
16
).

iii.

In
Singh, 1994
, and
Das et al., 1999
,

\Delta_{g}\coloneqq r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{*},\quad\text{if $a$ is chosen greedily with $\beta_{g}$ set to $1/(n_{u}+1)$},

where
n_{u}
denotes the number of
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
updates so far (
15
).
This special value of
\beta_{g}
makes the estimation equivalent to
the sample average of the rewards received for greedy actions.

iv.

In
Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996
,
Abounadi et al., 2001
,
and
Bertsekas, 2012
,

\Delta_{g}\coloneqq\underbrace{\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a^{\prime})}_{\hat{v}_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})},\quad\text{for an arbitrary reference state $s_{\mathrm{ref}}$}.

(17)

This benefits from having
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
such that
v_{b}^{*}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})
can be interpreted as
v_{g}^{*}
,
while also satisfying the underdetermined system of average-reward Bellman optimality equations.

3.3
Methods with function approximation

We focus on
parametric
techniques for
Q_{b}
-learning with function approximation.
In such cases, the action value is parameterized by
a weight (parameter) vector
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}=\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}
,
where
\dim(\boldsymbol{w})
is the number of dimensions of
\boldsymbol{w}
.
That is,

\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})\approx q_{b}^{*}(s,a),\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Note that there also exist non-parametric techniques, for instance,
kernel-based methods
(
Ormoneit and Glynn, 2002
;
Ormoneit and Glynn, 2001
)

and those based on state aggregation
(
Ortner, 2007
)
.

Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis, 1996
proposed the following weight update,

\boldsymbol{w}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{w}+\beta\Big\{\Big(r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{*}+\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w})\Big)\nabla\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})-\boldsymbol{w}\Big\}.

(18)

Particularly, the optimal gain
v_{g}^{*}
is estimated using
(
15
,
17
).
They highlighted that even if
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(\boldsymbol{w})
is bounded,

\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
may diverge.

Das et al., 1999
updated the weight using temporal difference
(TD, or TD error) as follows,

\boldsymbol{w}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{w}+\beta\Big\{\Big(\underbrace{r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{*}+\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},\boldsymbol{w})-\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})}_{\text{relative TD in terms of $\hat{q}_{b}^{*}$}}\Big)\nabla\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})\Big\},

(19)

which can be interpreted as the parameterized form of (
14
),
and is a
semi-gradient
update, similar to its discounted-reward counterpart
in
Q_{\gamma}
-learning
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, Eqn 16.3)
.
This update is adopted by
Yang et al., 2016
.
The approximation for
v_{g}^{*}
can be performed in various ways,
as for tabular settings in Sec
3.2
.
Note that (
19
) differs from (
18
)
in the use of TD, affecting the update factor.
In contrast,
Prashanth and Bhatnagar, 2011
leveraged (
14
)
in order to suggest the following update,

\boldsymbol{w}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{w}+\beta\Big\{r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{*}+\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{b}^{*}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},\boldsymbol{w})\Big\},\quad\text{whose $\hat{v}_{g}^{*}$ is updated using (\ref{equ:vgstar_sa},~\ref{equ:optgain_sa_bertsekas})}.

4
Policy-iteration schemes

Instead of iterating towards the value of optimal policies, we can iterate
policies directly towards the optimal one.
At each iteration, the current policy iterate is evaluated, then
its value is used to obtain the next policy iterate by taking the greedy action
based on the RHS of the Bellman optimality equation (
6
).
The latter step differentiates this so-called policy iteration from
naive policy enumeration that evaluates and compares policies as prescribed
in (
5
).

Like in the previous Sec
3
, we begin with
the original policy iteration in DP, which is then generalized in RL.
Afterward, we review average-reward policy gradient methods,
including actor-critic variants, because of their prominence and proven empirical successes.
The last two sections are devoted to approximate policy evaluation, namely
gain and relative value estimations.
Table
(in Appendix
) summarizes
existing works on average-reward policy-iteration-based model-free RL.

4.1
Foundations

In DP,
Howard, 1960
proposed the first policy iteration algorithm to
obtain gain optimal policies for unichain MDPs.
It proceeds as follows.

Step 1:

Initialize the iteration index
k\leftarrow 0
and
set the initial policy arbitrarily,
\hat{\pi}^{k=0}\approx\pi^{*}
.

Step 2:

Perform (exact) policy evaluation:

Solve the following underdetermined linear system for
v_{g}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{k}
,

v_{b}^{k}(s)+v_{g}^{k}=r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\ v_{b}^{k}(s^{\prime}),\qquad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},\text{with}\ a=\hat{\pi}^{k}(s),

(20)

which is called the Bellman policy expectation equation, also
the Poisson equation
(
Feinberg and Shwartz, 2002
, Eqn 9.1)
.

Step 3:

Perform (exact) policy improvement:

Compute a policy
\hat{\pi}^{k+1}
by greedy action selection
(analogous to the RHS of (
6
)):

\hat{\pi}^{k+1}(s)\leftarrow\argmax_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\big[\underbrace{r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\ v_{b}^{k}(s^{\prime})}_{\text{$q_{b}^{k}(s,a)+v_{g}^{k}$}}\big],\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(Synchronous updates)

Step 4:

If stable, i.e.
\hat{\pi}^{k+1}(s)=\hat{\pi}^{k}(s),\forall s\in\mathcal{S}
,
then output
\hat{\pi}^{k+1}
(which is equivalent to
\pi^{*}
).

Otherwise, increment
k
, then go to Step 2.

The above
exact
policy iteration is generalized to
the generalized policy iteration (GPI) for RL
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, Sec 4.6)
.
Such generalization is in the sense of the details of policy evaluation and improvement,
such as approximation (
inexact
evaluation and
inexact
improvement) and
the update granularity, ranging from per timestep (incremental, step-wise) to
per number (batch) of timesteps.

GPI relies on the policy improvement theorem
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, p101)
.
It assures that any
\epsilon
-greedy policy with respect to
q_{b}(\pi)
is
an improvement over any
\epsilon
-soft policy
\pi
,
i.e. any policy whose

\pi(a|s)\geq\epsilon/|\mathcal{A}|,\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
.
Moreover,
\epsilon
-greediness is also beneficial for exploration.
There exist several variations on policy improvement that all share
a similar idea to
\epsilon
-greedy, i.e. updating the current policy
towards
a greedy policy.
They include

Chen-Yu Wei, 2019
,

Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2019a
,

Hao et al., 2020
,
and approximate gradient-ascent updates used in policy gradient methods
(Sec
4.2
).

4.2
Average-reward policy gradient methods

In this section, we outline policy gradient methods, which have proven empirical successes
in function approximation settings
(
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Duan et al., 2016
)
.
They requires explicit policy parameterization, which enables

not only
learning appropriate levels of exploration
(either control- or parameter-based

Vemula et al., 2019
,
Miyamae et al., 2010
, Fig 1
),

but also
injection of domain knowledge
(
Deisenroth et al., 2013
, Sec 1.3)
.
Note that from a sensitivity-based point of view, policy gradient methods belong to
perturbation analysis
(
Cao, 2007
, p18)
.

In policy gradient methods, a policy
\pi
is parameterized by a parameter vector

\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta=\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
,
where
\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})
indicates the number of dimensions of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
In order to obtain a smooth dependence on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
(hence, smooth gradients),
we restrict the policy class to a set of
randomized
stationary policies
\Pi_{\mathrm{SR}}
.
We further assume that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is twice differentiable with
bounded first and second derivatives.
For discrete actions, one may utilize a categorical distribution
(a special case of Gibbs/Boltzmann distributions) as follows,

\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s,a))}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\phi}(s,a^{\prime}))},\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

(soft-max in action preferences)

where
\boldsymbol{\phi}(s,a)
is the feature vector of a state-action pair
(s,a)

for this policy parameterization
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
,
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
).
Note that parametric policies may not contain the optimal policy because
there are typically fewer parameters than state-action pairs, yielding some approximation error.

The policy improvement is based on the following optimization with

v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq v_{g}(\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}))
,

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}\coloneqq\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\quad\text{with iterative update:}\ \boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}+\alpha\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(21)

where
\alpha
is a positive step length, and

\boldsymbol{C}\in\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})\times\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
denotes
some preconditioning positive definite matrix.
Based on (
3
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}r(s,a)\ \nabla\{p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\ \pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\}

(
\nabla\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\ \pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\ r(s,a)\ \{\nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})+\nabla\log p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\ \pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\ \underbrace{q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)}_{\text{in lieu of $r(s,a)$}}\nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})

(does not involve
\nabla\log p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)\ \pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\ p(s^{\prime}|s,a)\ v_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})\ \nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta}).

(22)

The penultimate equation above is due to the (randomized) policy gradient theorem
(
Sutton et al., 2000
,
Marbach and Tsitsiklis, 2001
,

Deisenroth et al., 2013
).
The last equation was proven to be equivalent by
Castro and Meir, 2010
.

There exist (at least) two variants of preconditioning matrices
\boldsymbol{C}
.

First
is through the second derivative

\boldsymbol{C}=-\nabla^{2}v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, as well as its approximation,
see
Furmston et al., 2016
,

(
Morimura et al., 2008
, Sec 3.3, 5.2)
,
Kakade, 2002
.

Second
, one can use a Riemannian-metric matrix for natural gradients.
It aims to make the update directions invariant to the policy parameterization.

Kakade, 2002
first proposed the Fisher information matrix (FIM) as such a matrix,
for which an incremental approximation was suggested by
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
.
A generalized variant of natural gradients was introduced by

Morimura et al., 2009
;
Morimura et al., 2008
.
In addition,
Thomas, 2014
derived another generalization that
allows for a positive semidefinite matrix.
Recall that FIM is only guaranteed to be positive semidefinite
(hence, describing a semi-Riemannian manifold);
whereas the natural gradient ascent assumes the function being optimized
has a Riemannian manifold domain.

In order to obtain more efficient learning with efficient computation,
several works propose using backward-view eligibility traces in policy parameter updates.
The key idea is to keep track of the eligibility of each component of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

for getting updated whenever a reinforcing event, i.e.
q_{b}^{\pi}
, occurs.
Given a state-action sample
(s,a)
, the update in (
21
)
becomes

\boldsymbol{\theta}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{\theta}+\alpha\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)\ \boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\quad\text{which is carried out after}\ \boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\underbrace{\nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{the eligibility}},

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
denotes
the accumulating eligibility vector for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
is initialized to
\boldsymbol{0}
, whereas

\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in(0,1)
the trace decay factor for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.
This is used by

Iwaki and Asada, 2019
,
Sutton and Barto, 2018
,
Furmston et al., 2016
,
Degris et al., 2012
,
Matsubara et al., 2010a
, and
Marbach and Tsitsiklis, 2001
.

As can be observed in (
22
), computing the gradient

\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
exactly
requires (exact) knowledge of
p_{\pi}^{\star}
and
q_{b}^{\pi}
,
which are both unknown in RL.
It also requires summation over all states and actions, which becomes an issue
when state and action sets are large.
As a result, we resort to the gradient estimate, which if
unbiased
,
leads to
stochastic
gradient ascent.

In order to reduce the variance of gradient estimates, there are two common techniques
based on control variates
(
Greensmith et al., 2004
, Sec 5, 6)
.

First
is the baseline control variate, for which the
optimal
baseline is
the one that minimizes the variance.
Choosing
v_{b}^{\pi}
as a baseline
(
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
, Lemma 2)

yields

\underbrace{q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)-v_{b}^{\pi}(s)}_{\text{relative action advantage, $\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)$}}=\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}\Big[\underbrace{\Big\{r(s,a)-v_{g}^{\pi}+v_{b}^{\pi}(S^{\prime})\Big\}-v_{b}^{\pi}(s)\big|s,a}_{\text{relative TD, $\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,S^{\prime})$}}\Big],\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},

(24)

where
S^{\prime}
denotes the next state given the current state
s
and action
a
.
It has been shown that

\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{S^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,S^{\prime})}}\right]
,
meaning that the TD, i.e.
\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,S^{\prime})
, can be used as
an
unbiased
estimate for the action advantage

Iwaki and Asada, 2019
, Prop 1
,
Castro and Meir, 2010
, Thm 5
,

Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
, Lemma 3
.
Hence, we have

\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\big[\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)\ \nabla\log\pi(A|S;\boldsymbol{\theta})\big]\approx\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime})\ \nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where the expectation of
S
,
A
, and
S^{\prime}
is approximated with a single sample
(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
This yields an unbiased gradient estimate with lower variance than that using

q_{b}^{\pi}
.
Note that in RL, the exact
\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}
and
v_{b}^{\pi}

(for calculating
\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}
) should also be approximated.

Second
, a policy value estimator is set up and often also parameterized by

\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}=\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}
.
This gives rise to actor-critic methods, where “actor” refers to the parameterized policy,
whereas “critic” the parameterized value estimator.
The critic can take either one of these three forms,

i.

relative state-value estimator
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{v})

for computing the relative TD approximate
\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}
,

ii.

both relative state- and action-value estimators for

\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)\leftarrow\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{q})-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{v})
,

iii.

relative action-advantage estimator
\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})
,

for all
s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
, and
with the corresponding critic parameter vectors
\boldsymbol{w}_{v}
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{q}
, and
\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}}
.
This parametric value approximation is reviewed in Sec
4.4
.

4.3
Gain Approximation

We classify gain approximations into incremental and batch categories.
Incremental methods update their current estimates using information
only from one timestep, hence step-wise updates.
In contrast, batch methods uses information from multiple timesteps in a batch.
Every update, therefore, has to wait until the batch is available.
Notationally, we write
g^{\pi}\coloneqq v_{g}(\pi)
.
The superscript
\pi
is often dropped whenever the context is clear.

4.3.1
Incremental methods

Based on (
4
), we can define an increasing function

f(g)\coloneqq g-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(S,A)}}\right]
.
Therefore, the problem of estimating
g
becomes finding the root of
f(g)
,
by which
f(g)=0
.
As can be observed,
f(g)
is unknown because
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(S,A)}}\right]
is unknown.
Moreover, we can only obtain noisy observations of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(S,A)}}\right]
, namely

r(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[r(S,A)}}\right]+\varepsilon
for some additive error
\varepsilon>0
.
Thus, the
noisy
observation of
f(\hat{g}_{t})
at iteration
t
is given by

\hat{f}(\hat{g}_{t})=\hat{g}_{t}-r(s_{t},a_{t})
.
Recall that an increasing function satisfies
df(x)/dx>0
for
x>x^{*}

where
x^{*}
indicates the root of the
f(x)
.

We can solve for the root of
f(g)
iteratively via
the Robbins-Monro (RM) algorithm as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\hat{g}_{t}-\beta_{t}\{\hat{f}(\hat{g}_{t})\}=\hat{g}_{t}-\beta_{t}\{\hat{g}_{t}-r(s_{t},a_{t})\}

(for an increasing function
f
)

\displaystyle=\hat{g}_{t}+\beta_{t}\{r(s_{t},a_{t})-\hat{g}_{t}\}=(1-\beta_{t})\hat{g}_{t}+\beta_{t}\{r(s_{t},a_{t})\},

(25)

where
\beta_{t}\in(0,1)
is a gain approximation step size;
note that setting
\beta_{t}\geq 1
violates the RM algorithm since
it makes the coefficient of
\hat{g}_{t}
non-positive.
This recursive procedure converges to the root of
f(g)
with probability 1
under several conditions, including
\varepsilon_{t}
is i.i.d with

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\varepsilon_{t}}}\right]=0
,
the step size satisfies the standard requirement in SA, and
some other technical conditions
(
Cao, 2007
, Sec 6.1)
.
The stochastic approximation technique in (
25
) is
the most commonly used.
For instance,
Wu et al., 2020
,

Heess et al., 2012
,
Powell, 2011
,

Castro and Meir, 2010
,
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
,

Konda and Tsitsiklis, 2003
,
Marbach and Tsitsiklis, 2001
, and

Tsitsiklis and Roy, 1999
.

Furthermore, a learning rate of
\beta_{t}=1/(t+1)
in (
25
)
yields

\hat{g}_{t+1}=\frac{t\times\hat{g}_{t}+r(s_{t},a_{t})}{t+1}=\frac{r(s_{t},a_{t})+r(s_{t-1},a_{t-1})+\ldots+r(s_{0},a_{0})}{t+1},

(26)

which is the average of a noisy gain observation sequence up to
t
.
The ergodic theorem asserts that for such a Markovian sequence, the time average
converges to (
4
) as
t
approaches infinity

(
Gray, 2009
, Ch 8)
.
This decaying learning rate is suggested by
Singh, 1994
.
Additionally, a variant of
\beta_{t}=1/(t+1)^{\kappa}
with
a positive constant
\kappa<1

is used by
Wu et al., 2020
for establishing
the finite-time error rate of this gain approximation under non-i.i.d Markovian samples.

Another iterative approach is based on the Bellman expectation equation (
20
)
for obtaining the noisy observation of
g
.
That is,

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{t+1}

\displaystyle=(1-\beta_{t})\hat{g}_{t}+\beta_{t}\big\{\underbrace{r(s_{t},a_{t})+\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t+1})-\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t})}_{\text{$g$ in expectation of $S_{t+1}$ when $\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}=v_{b}^{\pi}$}}\big\}

(The variance is reduced, cf. (
25
))

\displaystyle=\hat{g}_{t}+\beta_{t}\big\{\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\{}r(s_{t},a_{t})-\hat{g}_{t}+\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t+1}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\}}-\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t})}_{\text{$\hat{\delta}_{v_{b},t}^{\pi}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})$}}\big\}.

(27)

In comparison to (
25
), the preceding update is anticipated to
have lower variance due to the adjustment terms of

\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t+1})-\hat{v}_{b,t}^{\pi}(s_{t})
.
This update is used by
Singh, 1994
,
Degris et al., 2012
,
and
Sutton and Barto, 2018
.
An analogous update can be formulated by replacing
v_{b}^{\pi}
with
q_{b}^{\pi}
,
which is possible only when the next action
a_{t+1}
is already available,
as in the differential Sarsa algorithm
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, p251)
.

4.3.2
Batch methods

In batch settings, there are at least two ways to approximate the gain of a policy,
denoted as
g
.

First,

\hat{g}
is set to the total rewards
divided
by
the number of timesteps in a batch, as in (
26
), which
also shows its incremental equivalence.
This is used in

Powell, 2011
, p339
;
Yu and Bertsekas, 2009
, Sec 2B
;

Gosavi, 2004a
, Sec 4
,
Feinberg and Shwartz, 2002
, p319
.
The work of
Marbach and Tsitsiklis, 2001
also falls in this category,
but
\hat{g}
is computed as a weighted average of
all
past rewards from
all batches (regenerative cycles), mixing on- and off-policy samples.
Nonetheless, it is shown to yield lower estimation variance.

Second,
based on similiar justification as RVI, we can specify
a reference state and action pair.
For instance,

Cao, 2007
proposed
\hat{g}\leftarrow\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s_{\mathrm{ref}})
,
whereas

Lagoudakis, 2003
advocated
\hat{g}\leftarrow\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s_{\mathrm{ref}},a_{\mathrm{ref}})
.

4.4
Relative state- and action-value approximation

4.4.1
Tabular methods

Singh, 1994
proposed the following incremental TD-based update,

\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s)\leftarrow\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s)+\beta\{\underbrace{\big(r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}+\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})\big)-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s)}_{\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime})}\},\quad\text{for a sample $(s,a,s^{\prime})$}.

A similar update for
\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}
can be obtained by substituting

\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}
with
\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}
along with a sample for the next action,
as in Sarsa-like algorithms.

In a batch fashion, the relative action value can be approximated as follows,

q_{b}^{\pi}(s_{t},a_{t})\approx\underbrace{\sum_{\tau=t}^{t_{s_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{\pi}}r(s_{\tau},a_{\tau})-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi},}_{\text{an episode return}}\quad\text{using a sample set $\{(s_{\tau},a_{\tau})\}_{\tau=t}^{t_{s_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{\pi}}$}

(28)

where
t_{s_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{\pi}
denotes the timestep at which
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
is visited while
following a policy
\pi
, assuming that
s_{\mathrm{ref}}
is a recurrent state under all policies.
This is used by
Sutton et al., 2000
, and
Marbach and Tsitsiklis, 2001
.
Another batch approximation technique is based on the inverse-propensity scoring

(
Chen-Yu Wei, 2019
, Algo 3)
.

4.4.2
Methods with function approximation

Here, we review gradient-based approximate policy evaluation that relies on
the gradient of an error function to update its parameter.
Note that there exist approximation techniques based on least squares, e.g. LSPE(
\lambda
)
(
Yu and Bertsekas, 2009
)
,
gLSTD and LSTDc
(
Ueno et al., 2008
)
, and
LSTD-Q
(
Lagoudakis, 2003
, Appendix A)
.

Relative state-value approximation:

The value approximator is parameterized as

\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})\approx\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{\pi}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{W}=\real{\dim(\boldsymbol{w})}}
.
Then, the mean squared error (MSE) objective is minimized.
That is,

\|\boldsymbol{v}_{b}^{\pi}-\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star})}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{v_{b}^{\pi}(S)-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(S,\boldsymbol{w})\}^{2}}}\right],

where
\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star})
is a
|\mathcal{S}|
-by-
|\mathcal{S}|
diagonal matrix with

\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star}(s)
in its diagonal.
As a result, the gradient estimate for updating
\boldsymbol{w}
is given by

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{v_{b}^{\pi}(S)-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w})\}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{v_{b}^{\pi}(S)-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w})\}\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w})}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\{v_{b}^{\pi}(s)-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w})\}\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w})

(Via a single sample
s
)

\displaystyle\approx\big\{\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\{}r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}+\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\}}}_{\text{TD target that approximates $v_{b}^{\pi}(s)$}}-\ \hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w})\big\}\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle=\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w})\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}).

(29)

The key difference to supervised learning lies in the fact that
the true value
v_{b}^{\pi}(s)
is approximated by a bootstrapping TD target
involving
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w})
, which is biased due to its dependency on
\boldsymbol{w}
.
Such a dependency, however, is not captured (hence, ignored) in the gradient in (
29
)
because the TD target plays the role of true values.
As a result, this way of learning
\boldsymbol{w}
belongs to semi-gradient methods.

With the same motivation as in policy gradient updates (
23
),
one may use an (accumulating) eligibility trace vector
\boldsymbol{e}_{v}

with reinforcing event
\delta_{v_{b}}^{\pi}
.
This leads to a backward view of TD(
\lambda
), whose weight update is given by

\boldsymbol{w}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{w}_{t}+\beta_{t}\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})\boldsymbol{e}_{v,t},\qquad\text{with}\ \boldsymbol{e}_{v,t}=\lambda_{v}\boldsymbol{e}_{v,t-1}+\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s_{t};\boldsymbol{w}_{t}),

(30)

for a trace decay factor
\lambda_{v}\in(0,1)
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{-1}=\boldsymbol{0}

such that

\boldsymbol{e}_{v,t}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\lambda_{v}^{t-\tau}\nabla\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s_{\tau};\boldsymbol{w}_{\tau})
.
For linear function approximation,
Tsitsiklis and Roy, 1999
provide
an asymptotic convergence proof (with probability 1 with incremental gain estimation,
as well as with fixed gain estimates)
and a bound on the resulting approximation error.
This TD(
\lambda
) learning for
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}
is also used by

Sutton and Barto, 2018
, and

Castro and Meir, 2010
.

As can be observed, there are 2 dedicated step lengths,
i.e. the actor’s
\alpha_{t}
(
23
) and
the critic’s
\beta_{t}
(
30
).
Together, they form 2-timescale SA-type approximation,
where both actor and critic are updated at each iteration
but with different step lengths,
\alpha_{t}\neq\beta_{t}
.
Intuitively, the policy (actor) should be updated on a slower timescale so that
the critic has enough updates to catch up with the ever-changing policy.
This implies that
\alpha_{t}
should be smaller than
\beta_{t}
.
More precisely, we require that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\beta_{t}/\alpha_{t}=\infty

for assuring the asymptotic convergence
(
Wu et al., 2020
;
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
;
Konda and Tsitsiklis, 2003
)
.
Nevertheless,
Castro and Meir, 2010
argued that from biological standpoint,
2-timescale operation within the same anatomical structure is not well justified.
They analyzed the convergence of 1-timescale actor-critic methods to
the
neighborhood
of a local maximum of
v_{g}^{*}
.

Relative action-value and action-advantage approximation:

Estimation for
q_{b}^{\pi}
can be carried out similarly as that for
v_{b}^{\pi}

in (
30
), but uses the relative TD on action values, namely

\hat{\delta}_{q_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\coloneqq\underbrace{\{r(s,a)-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}+\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w})\}}_{\text{TD target that approximates $q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)$}}-\ \hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}),\quad\text{with $a^{\prime}\coloneqq a_{t+1}$},

as in Sarsa
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, p251)
, as well as
Konda and Tsitsiklis, 2003
.
It is also possible to follow this pattern but with different approaches to
approximating the true value of
q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)
, e.g. via episode returns in (
28
)
(
Sutton et al., 2000
, Thm 2)
.

For approximating the action advantage parametrically through
\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})
,
the technique used in (
29
) can also be applied.
However, the true value
\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}
is estimated by TD on state values.
That is,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star},A\sim\pi}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)-\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})\}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)-\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})\}\nabla\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\big\{\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)-\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})\big\}\nabla\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})

(Approximation via a single sample
(s,a)
)

\displaystyle\approx\big\{\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w}_{v})-\ \hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}})\big\}\nabla\hat{\mathrm{a}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{a}}).

(Approximation via
\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}\approx\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)
,
see (
24
))

This is used by
Iwaki and Asada, 2019
,
Heess et al., 2012
,
and
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
.
In particular,
Iwaki and Asada, 2019
proposed a preconditioning matrix
for the gradients, namely:

\boldsymbol{I}-\kappa(\boldsymbol{f}(s,a)\boldsymbol{f}^{\intercal}(s,a))/(1+\kappa\|\boldsymbol{f}(s,a)\|^{2})
,
where
\boldsymbol{f}(s,a)
denotes the feature vector of a state-action pair, while

\kappa\geq\beta_{\mathrm{a}}
some scaling constant.

Futhermore, the action-value or action-advantage estimators can be parameterized linearly
with the so-called
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-compatible state-action feature,
denoted by
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)
, as follows,

\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{w}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a),\quad\text{with}\ \underbrace{\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)\coloneqq\nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$-compatible state-action feature}},\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}.

(31)

This parameterization along with the minimization of MSE loss are beneficial
for two reasons.

First
, their use with (locally) optimal parameter
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}\in\mathcal{W}

satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\Big[\underbrace{\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*})}_{\text{in lieu of $q_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)$}}\nabla\pi(A|S;\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big]=\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

(Exact policy gradients when
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
)

as shown by
Sutton et al., 2000
.
Otherwise, the gradient estimate is likely to be biased
(
Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
, Lemma 4)
.

Second
, they make computing natural gradients equivalent to finding
the optimal weight
\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
for
\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})
.
That is,

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}(s)\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)\big(\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}(s,a)\boldsymbol{w}^{*}-q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)\big)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

(Since MSE is 0 when
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}^{*}
)

\displaystyle\Big\{\underbrace{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}(s)\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\intercal}(s,a)}_{\boldsymbol{F}_{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\Big\}\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\star}(s)\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)}_{\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}^{*}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\boldsymbol{F}_{a}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\ \nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}_{\text{The natural gradient}},

(32)

where
\boldsymbol{F}_{a}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
denotes the Fisher (information) matrix
based on the action distribution (hence, the subscript
a
),
as introduced by
Kakade, 2002
.
This implies that the estimation for natural gradients is reduced to
a regression problem of state-action value functions.
It can be either regressing

i.

action value
q_{b}^{\pi}

Sutton et al., 2000
, Thm 2
,
Kakade, 2002
, Thm 1
,

Konda and Tsitsiklis, 2003
, Eqn 3.1
,

ii.

action advantage
\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}

Bhatnagar et al., 2009a
, Eqn 30, Algo 3, 4
;

Heess et al., 2012
, Eqn 13
;
Iwaki and Asada, 2019
, Eqn 17
, or

iii.

the immediate reward
r(s,A\sim\pi)
as a known ground truth

(
Morimura et al., 2008
, Thm 1)
,
recall that the ground truth
q_{b}^{\pi}
(Item
i.
) and

\mathrm{a}_{b}^{\pi}
(Item
ii.
) above are unknown.
This reward regression is used along with
a
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-compatible state-action feature
that is defined differently compared to (
31
),
namely

\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s,a)\coloneqq\nabla\log p_{\pi}^{\star}(s,a)=\nabla\log p_{\pi}^{\star}(s)+\nabla\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})
,
where
\boldsymbol{f}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is based on
the stationary joint state-action distribution
p_{\pi}^{\star}(s,a)
.
In fact, this leads to a new Fisher matrix, yielding the so-called
natural state-action gradients
\boldsymbol{F}_{s,a}

(which subsumes
\boldsymbol{F}_{a}
in (
32
)
as a special case when
\nabla\log p_{\pi}^{\star}
is set to
\boldsymbol{0}
).

In addition,
Konda and Tsitsiklis, 2003
pointed out that
the dependency of
\nabla v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
to
q_{b}^{\pi}
is only
through its inner products with vectors in the subspace spanned by

\{\nabla_{i}\log\pi(a|s;\boldsymbol{\theta})\}_{i=1}^{\dim(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
.
This implies that learning the projection of
q_{b}^{\pi}
onto the aforementioned
(low-dimensional) subspace is sufficient, instead of learning
q_{b}^{\pi}
fully.
A
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-dependent state-action feature can be defined based on
either the action distribution (as in (
31
)), or
the stationary state-action distribution (as mentioned in Item
iii.

in the previous passage).

5
Discussion

In this section, we discuss several open questions as first steps towards
completing the literature in average-reward model-free RL.
We begin with those of value iteration schemes (Sec
5.1
),
then of policy iteration schemes focussing on policy evaluation
(Sec
5.2
).
Lastly, we also outline several issues that apply to both or beyond
the two aforementioned schemes.

5.1
Approximation for optimal gain and optimal action-values (of optimal policies)

Two main components of
Q_{b}
-learning are
\hat{q}_{b}^{*}
and
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
updates.
The former is typically carried out via either (
14
) for tabular,
or (
19
) for (parametric) function approximation settings.
What remains is determining how to estimate the optimal gain
v_{g}^{*}
,
which becomes the main bottleneck for
Q_{b}
-learning.

As can be seen in Fig
(Appendix
),
there are two classes of
v_{g}^{*}
approximators.
First, approximators that are not SA-based generally need

s_{\mathrm{ref}}
and
a_{\mathrm{ref}}
specification, which is shown to affect the performance
especially in large state and action sets
(
Wan et al., 2020
;
Yang et al., 2016
)
.
On the other hand, SA-based approximators require a dedicated stepsize
\beta_{g}
,
yielding more complicated 2-timescale
Q_{b}
-learning.
Furthermore, it is not yet clear whether the approximation for
v_{g}^{*}
should be on-policy,
i.e. updating only when a greedy action is executed, at the cost of reduced sample efficiency.
This begs the question of which approach to estimating
v_{g}^{*}
is “best” (in which cases).

In discounted reward settings,
Hasselt, 2010
(also,
Hasselt et al., 2016
)
pointed out that the approximation for
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}q_{\gamma}^{*}(S_{t+1},a)}}\right]

poses overestimation, which may be non-uniform and not concentrated at states
that are beneficial in terms of exploration.
He proposed instantiating two decoupled approximators such that

\mathbb{E}_{S_{t+1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}q_{\gamma}^{*}(S_{t+1},a^{\prime})}}\right]\approx\hat{q}_{\gamma}^{*}(s_{t+1},\argmax_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\hat{q}_{\gamma}^{*}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w}_{q}^{(2)});\boldsymbol{w}_{q}^{(1)}),

(Double
Q_{\gamma}
-learning)

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{q}^{(1)}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{q}^{(2)}
denote their corresponding weights.
This was shown to be successful in reducing the negative effect of overestimation.
In average reward cases, the overestimation of
q_{b}^{*}
becomes more convoluted
due to the involvement of
\hat{v}_{g}^{*}
, as shown in (
12
).
We believe that it is important to extend the idea of double action-value approximators to
Q_{b}
-learning.

To our knowledge, there is no finite-time convergence analysis for
Q_{b}
-learning thus far.
There are also very few works on
Q_{b}
-learning with function approximation.
This is in contrast with its discounted reward counterpart,
e.g. sample complexity of
Q_{\gamma}
-learning with UCB-exploration bonus
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
,
as well as deep
Q_{\gamma}
-learning neural-network (DQN) and its variants
with
non-linear
function approximation
(
Hessel et al., 2018
)
.

5.2
Approximation for gain, state-, and action-values of any policy

Gain approximation
\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}
:

In order to have more flexibility in terms of learning methods, we can parameterize
the gain estimator, for instance,

\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{f}^{\intercal}(s)\boldsymbol{w}_{g}
,
by which the gain
estimate
is state-dependent.
The learning uses the following gradients,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\{v_{g}^{\pi}-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\big\}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\big\{v_{g}^{\pi}-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\big\}\nabla\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(S;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})}}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\big\{v_{g}^{\pi}(s)-\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\big\}\nabla\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})

(By a single sample
s
)

\displaystyle\approx\big\{\underbrace{r(s,a)+\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(s)}_{\text{approximates the true $v_{g}^{\pi}$ based on \eqref{equ:poisson}}}-\ \hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\big\}\nabla\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g})

\displaystyle=\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}(s,a,s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w}_{g})\nabla\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{g}).

This requires further investigation whether the above parameterized approximator
for the gain
v_{g}^{\pi}
is beneficial in practice.

State-value approximation
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}
:

We observe that most, if not all, relative state-value approximators are based on TD, leading to
semi-gradient methods (Sec
4.4.2
).
In discounted reward settings, there are several non-TD approaches.
These include the true gradient methods
(
Sutton et al., 2009
)
,
as well as those using the Bellman residual (i.e. the expected value of TD),
such as
Zhang et al., 2019
;
Geist et al., 2017
;
see also surveys by
Dann et al., 2014
;
Geist and Pietquin, 2013
.

It is interesting to investigate whether TD or not TD for learning
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}
.
For example, we may formulate a (true) gradient TD method that optimizes
the mean-squared
projected
Bellman error (MSPBE), namely

\|\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})-\mathbb{P}[\mathbb{B}_{g}^{\pi}[\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w})]]\|_{\mathrm{diag}(\boldsymbol{p}_{\pi}^{\star})}^{2}

with a projection operator
\mathbb{P}
that projects any value approximation
onto the space of representable paramaterized approximators.

Action-value approximation
\hat{q}_{b}^{\pi}
:

Commonly, the estimation for
q_{b}^{\pi}
involves the gain estimate
\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}
as
dictated by the Bellman expectation equation (
20
).
It is natural then to ask:
is it possible to perform average-reward GPI
without estimating the gain of a policy at each iteration?

We speculate that the answer is affirmative as follows.

Let the gain
v_{g}^{\pi}
be the baseline for
q_{b}^{\pi}

in a similiar manner to
v_{b}^{\pi}
in (
24
);
cf. in discounted reward settings, see
Weaver and Tao, 2001
.
Then, based on the Bellman expectation equation in action values
(analogous to (
12
)), we have the following identity,

q_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)-(-v_{g}^{\pi})=\underbrace{r(s,a)+\mathbb{E}_{S_{t+1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v_{b}^{\pi}(S_{t+1})}}\right]}_{\text{The surrogate action-value $\mathfrak{q}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a)$}}.

(Note different symbols,
q_{b}^{\pi}
vs
\mathfrak{q}_{b}^{\pi}
)

This surrogate can be parameterized as
\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})
.
Its parameter
\boldsymbol{w}
is updated using the following gradient estimates,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{\mathfrak{q}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)-\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w})\}^{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S,A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{\mathfrak{q}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)-\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w})\}\nabla\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w})}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S,A,S^{\prime}}\Big[\Big\{\underbrace{r(S,A)+v_{b}^{\pi}(S^{\prime})}_{\text{The surrogate $\mathfrak{q}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A)$}}-\ \hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w})\Big\}\nabla\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(S,A;\boldsymbol{w})\Big]

\displaystyle\approx\Big\{r(s,a)+v_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})-\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})\Big\}\nabla\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})

(Using a single sample
(s,a,s^{\prime})
)

\displaystyle\propto\Big\{r(s,a)+\underbrace{v_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})+(v_{g}^{\pi}+\kappa)}_{\text{The surrogate state-value $\nu_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime})$}}-\ \hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w})\Big\}\nabla\hat{\mathfrak{q}}_{b}^{\pi}(s,a;\boldsymbol{w}),

(Note different symbols,
v_{b}^{\pi}
vs
\nu_{b}^{\pi}
)

for some arbitrary constant
\kappa\in\real{}
.
The key is to exploit the fact that we have one degree of freedom in
the underdetermined linear systems for policy evaluation in (
20
).
Here, the surrogate state-value
\nu_{b}^{\pi}
is equal to
v_{b}^{\pi}
up to
some constant, i.e.
\nu_{b}^{\pi}=v_{b}^{\pi}+(v_{g}^{\pi}+\kappa)
.
One can estimate
\nu_{b}^{\pi}
in a similar fashion as
\hat{v}_{b}^{\pi}
in
(
29
),
except that now the gain approximation
\hat{v}_{g}^{\pi}
is no longer needed.
The parameterized estimator
\hat{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(\boldsymbol{w}_{\nu})
can be
updated by following below gradient estimate,

-\frac{1}{2}\nabla\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p_{\pi}^{\star}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\{\nu_{b}^{\pi}(S)-\hat{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(S,\boldsymbol{w}_{\nu})\}^{2}}}\right]\approx\big\{r(s,a)+\nu_{b}^{\pi}(s^{\prime};\boldsymbol{w}_{\nu})-\hat{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{\nu})\big\}\nabla\hat{\nu}_{b}^{\pi}(s;\boldsymbol{w}_{\nu}),

(33)

which is based on a single sample
(s,a,s^{\prime})
.
In the RHS of (
33
) above, the TD of
\nu_{b}^{\pi}
looks similar to
that of discounted reward
v_{\gamma}
, but with abused
\gamma=1
.
Therefore, its stability (whether it will grow unboundedly) warrants experiments.

Extras:

We notice that existing TD-based value approximators simply re-use the TD estimate

\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}
obtained using the
old
gain estimate from the previous iteration,
e.g.
Sutton and Barto, 2018
.
They do not harness the newly updated gain estimate for cheap recomputation of
\hat{\delta}_{v_{b}}^{\pi}
.
We anticipate that doing so will yield more accurate evaluation of the current policy.

5.3
Further open research questions

In the following passages, relevant works in discounted reward settings are mentioned,
if any, inside the brackets at the end of each part.

On batch settings:

For on-policy online RL without experience replay buffer,
we pose the following questions.
How to determine a batch size that balances the trade-off between
collecting more samples with the current policy and
updating the policy more often with fewer numbers of samples?
How to apply the concept of backward-view eligibility traces in batch settings?
(cf.
Harb and Precup, 2017
).

On value- vs policy-iteration schemes:

As can be observed in Tables
and
(in Appendix
),
there is less work on value- than policy-iteration schemes;
even less when considering only function approximation settings.
Our preliminary experiments suggest that although following value iteration is straightforward
(cf. policy iteration with evaluation and improvement steps),
it is more difficult to make it “work”, especially with function approximation.
One challenge is to specify the proper offset, e.g.
v_{g}^{*}
or its estimate,
in RVI-like methods to bound the iterates.
Moreover,
Mahadevan, 1996a
highlighted that
the seminal value-iteration based RL, i.e. average reward
Q_{b}
-learning,
is sensitive to exploration.

We posit these questions.
Is it still worth it to adopt the value iteration scheme after all?
Which scheme is more advantageous in terms of exploration in RL?
How to reconcile both schemes?
(cf.
O’Donoghue et al., 2016
;
Schulman et al., 2017
;
Wang et al., 2020
).

On distributional (cf. expected-value) perspective:

There exist few works on distributional views.

Morimura et al., 2010
proposed estimating
\nabla\log p^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(s)
for obtaining
the gradient estimate
\hat{\nabla}v_{g}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
in (
22
),
removing the need to estimate the action value
q_{b}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
One important question is how to scale up the distribution (density) estimation for large RL problems.
(cf.
Bellemare et al., 2017
).

On MDP modeling:

The broadest class that can be handled by existing average-reward RL is the unichain MDP;
note that most works assume the more specific class, i.e. the recurrent (ergodic) MDP.
To our knowledge, there is still no average reward model-free RL for multichain MDPs
(which is the most general class).

We also desire to apply average-reward RL to continuous state problems,
for which we may benefit from the DP theory on general states, e.g.
Sennott, 1999
.
There are few attempts thus far, for instance,

(
Yang et al., 2019
)
, which is limited to linear quadratic regulator with ergodic cost.

On optimality criteria:

The average reward optimality is underselective for problems with transient states,
for which we need
(n=0)
-discount (bias) optimality, or even higher
n
-discount optimality.
This underselective-ness motivates the weighted optimality in DP
(
Krass et al., 1992
)
.
In RL,
Mahadevan, 1996b
developed bias-optimal Q-learning.

In the other extreme,
(n=\infty)
-discount optimality is the most selective criterion.
According to
Puterman, 1994
, it is proven to be equivalent to
the Blackwell optimality, which intuitively claims that upon considering sufficiently
far into the future via the Blackwell’s discount factor
\gamma_{\mathrm{Bw}}
,
there is no policy better than the Blackwell optimal policy.
Moreover, optimizing the discounted reward does not require any knowledge about
the MDP structure (i.e. recurrent, unichain, multichain classification).
Therefore, one of pressing questions is on estimating such
\gamma_{\mathrm{Bw}}
in RL.
