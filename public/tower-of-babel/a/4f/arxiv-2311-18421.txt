Dynamical Implications of the Kerr Multipole Moments for Spinning Black Holes arXiv: 2311.18421
T. Scheopner, and J. Vines

Abstract
Previously the linearized stress tensor of a stationary Kerr black hole has been used to determine some of the values of gravitational couplings for a spinning black hole to linear order in the Riemann tensor in the action (worldline or quantum field theory). In particular, the couplings on operators containing derivative structures of the form ( S ⋅ ∇ ) n (S\cdot\nabla)^{n} acting on the Riemann tensor were fixed, with S μ S^{\mu} the spin vector of the black hole. In this paper we find that the Kerr solution determines all of the multipole moments in the sense of Dixon of a stationary spinning black hole and that these multipole moments determine all linear in R R couplings. For example, additional couplings beyond the previously mentioned are fixed on operators containing derivative structures of the form S 2 ​ n ( p ⋅ ∇ ) 2 ​ n S^{2n}(p\cdot\nabla)^{2n} acting on the Riemann tensor with p μ p^{\mu} the momentum vector of the black hole. These additional operators do not contribute to the three-point amplitude, and so do not contribute to the linearized stress tensor for a stationary black hole. However, we find that they do contribute to the Compton amplitude. Additionally, we derive formal expressions for the electromagnetic and gravitational Compton amplitudes of generic spinning bodies to all orders in spin in the worldline formalism and evaluated expressions for these amplitudes to 𝒪 ⁡ ( S 3 ) \mathcal{O}(S^{3}) in electromagnetism and 𝒪 ⁡ ( S 5 ) \mathcal{O}(S^{5}) in gravity.

1.2
Summary of Method and Results

In Dixon’s landmark papers Ref.
dixon2
;
dixon3
on the worldline formalism, among other results, he proves that there is a unique way to define the multipole moments of the current density or stress tensor for an extended body in general relativity so that those multipole moments can be made into a generating function for the current density/stress tensor in the usual way and so that those multipole moments are fully reduced (there are no interdependencies between moments of different orders required by either the continuity of charge or local conservation of energy-momentum). The definitions of these moments are highly nontrivial and generally do not coincide with the “naive” moments (from integrating powers of displacement against the current density/stress tensor over a spatial slice).

The higher multipole moments of Dixon’s formalism (quadrupole and beyond) have time evolution completely unconstrained by the continuity of charge and local conservation of energy-momentum while the low order multipole moments (essential the total linear momentum and spin) have time evolution completely determined by the same two principles. Their equations of motion are the Mathisson-Papapetrou-Dixon (MPD) equations. In order to determine the time evolution of the higher multipole moments, each higher moment requires either a supplementary equation of motion or constitutive relation in terms of lower moments. It is possible to provide such constitutive relations in terms of the minimal Poincare degrees of freedom
(z,p,S)
by deriving them from a variational principle in terms of these variables. This minimal set of degrees of freedom is especially natural to consider from an effective theory perspective as at large distance from the body it is effectively point-like and these degrees of freedom form the generators Poincare group for which symmetry is broken by the existence of such a point-like source. The resulting constitutive relations will give spin-induced higher multipole moments.

From the Kerr solution, following Israel’s analysis
israel:1970
, we compute the stress tensor which acts as its source (in the maximally causally extended spacetime) and from that source we compute the multipole moments of a spinning black hole using Dixon’s definitions of the multipole moments. Those multipole moments can then be used to determine a stress tensor, which in turn can be used to determine an action for the spinning black hole, up to couplings to operators which are quadratic in the Riemann tensor. The action we find is put in dynamical mass function form in (
216
) and the specific dynamical mass function we find for spinning black holes is given in (
295
).

The dynamical mass function we find contains all of the equivalent black hole couplings identified in Ref.
Levi:2015msa
, which can be found by comparison to the three-point amplitude (the stationary stress tensor), as well as many new terms. Dixon’s formalism specifies the unique way to lift those naive moments to proper multipole moments, and that lifting fixes the additional couplings in (
295
) relative to Ref.
Levi:2015msa
. This lifting is only able to fix linear in Riemann couplings in the action because no information about higher order in Riemann operators is contained in the stationary moments. We find that the dynamical mass function in (
295
) can be made consistent with the spin-exponentiation proposed by Ref.
Guevara:2018wpp
and shift-symmetry proposed by Refs.
Aoude:2022trd
;
Bern:2022kto
;
Haddad:2023ylx
;
Aoude:2023vdk
through
\mathcal{O}(S^{4})
for the appropriate choice of quadratic in Riemann couplings. As well, it can be made consistent with any one of the three principles of spin-exponentiation, shift-symmetry, or the Teukolsky equation results found in Ref
Bautista:2022wjf
through
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
and made consistent with any pair of them except the combination of shift-symmetry and the Teukolsky equation, which are incompatible. To facilitate this analysis, we find a formal expression for the gravitational Compton amplitude for a generic spinning body to all orders in spin and explicitly compute that amplitude in terms of all possible Wilson coefficients in the action through
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
. We find that there is one linearly independent structure in the amplitude at
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
, with one more appearing at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
, seven more at
\mathcal{O}(S^{4})
, and eleven more at
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
.

In order to understand these gravitational results, it is instructive to first follow all of the same steps of analysis for a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle in electromagnetism. In section
2
we review the worldline formalism with a dynamical mass function for electromagnetism. In section
3
we review the basics of Dixon’s theory of multipole moments and specialize his results to the current-density in flat spacetime. In section
4
we use Dixon’s formalism to compute the multipole moments of a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle and from those moments we compute the necessary dynamical mass function for such a particle, up to corrections which are quadratic in the field strength. Our electromagnetic analysis culminates in section
5
in which we compute the electromagnetic Compton amplitude for a generic spinning body to all orders in spin in terms of its dynamical mass function. We then specialize that all orders result to cubic order in spin by enumerating all possible operators in the action and study the spin-exponentiation and shift-symmetry properties of the resultant amplitude. We find that for electromagnetism, it is possible to simultaneously demand spin-exponentiation, shift-symmetry, and consistency with the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
multipole moment based dynamical mass function through
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
.

In the second half of the paper, we perform the same analysis for gravity. We begin in
6
by reviewing the worldline formalism with a dynamical mass function in general relativity. In section
7
we use Dixon’s formalism to compute the multipole moments of a Kerr particle and from those moments we compute the necessary dynamical mass function for such a particle, up to corrections which are quadratic in the Riemann tensor. Then in
8
we derive a formal expression for the gravitational Compton amplitude for a generic spinning body to all orders in spin, which is unfortunately much more complex than the corresponding electromagnetic formula. We then specialize that all orders result to quintic order in spin by enumerating all possible operators in the action and study the requirements imposed on that amplitude by spin-exponentiation, shift-symmetry, and matches to the Teukolsky equation. We find that for gravity, the multipole moment based dynamical mass function is consistent with the combination of spin-exponentiation and the Teukolsky equation at
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
and that requiring these fixes all available coefficients in the dynamical mass function at this order.

{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mathcal{F}_{\rho\sigma}\implies\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}^{\rho\sigma}.

(28)

Simplifying finally gives the electromagnetic MPD equations of motion for the spinning body:

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{q}{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{}^{\star}{\mathcal{F}}^{\alpha\beta}u_{\alpha}S_{\beta}\right)\frac{\dot{z}^{\mu}}{\mathtt{e}}=

\displaystyle\ u^{\mu}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial u_{\mu}}+u^{\mu}\frac{u^{\nu}}{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial u^{\nu}}+S^{\mu}\frac{u^{\nu}}{\mathcal{M}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial S^{\nu}}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}^{2}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u_{\nu}S_{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial z^{\sigma}}

\displaystyle\ \phantom{u^{\mu}}+\frac{q}{\mathcal{M}^{2}}{}^{\star}{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu\nu}S_{\nu}+\frac{q}{\mathcal{M}^{3}}\left(S^{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial u^{\rho}}+S^{2}u^{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial S^{\rho}}\right){}^{\star}{\mathcal{F}}^{\mu\nu}u_{\nu}

(29)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\ q\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}\dot{z}^{\nu}-\mathtt{e}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial z^{\mu}}

(30)

\displaystyle\dot{S}^{\mu}=

\displaystyle\ u^{\mu}\dot{u}\cdot S+\mathtt{e}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u_{\nu}S_{\rho}\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial S^{\sigma}}.

(31)

For solving these equations of motion we will always choose
\lambda
so that
\mathtt{e}=1
.

To understand how the dynamical mass function relates to the multipole moments of the body, we will need to study the current produced by our action. Define the
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
moments:

\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\mu\nu}=\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{(\rho_{1}...\rho_{n})[\mu\nu]}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial\partial^{n}_{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}}

(32)

Then, our action produces a formal distributional expression for
J^{\mu}
:

J^{\mu}(X)=\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}}{\partial A_{\mu}}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(q\dot{z}^{\mu}\delta(X-Z)-2\mathtt{e}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\mu\nu}\partial^{n+1}_{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\nu}\delta(X-Z)\right)d\lambda

(33)

3
Dixon’s Multipole Moments

In this section we summarize some of the ingredients and results of Dixon’s definition of multipole moments
dixon2
. The multipole moments of the current density (in the case of electromagnetism) and of the energy-momentum tensor (in the case of gravity) directly enter the equations of motion of the body and can be computed from the stationary fields produced by the body when isolated. We find that these multipole moments determine all linear in
\mathcal{F}
(for electromagnetism) or linear in
R
(for gravity) operators in the dynamical mass function. The necessary matching is similar to the three-point amplitude matching performed in Ref.
Vines:2017hyw
in the worldline or in Ref.
Bern:2020buy
in the field theory. However, using Dixon’s multipole construction we are able to extract more physical information from the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
or Kerr solutions than is contained in the three-point amplitude, which allows the determination of an increased number of Wilson coefficients.

Following Dixon’s discussion, we first consider how multipole moments are defined for a generic scalar field, then a generic tensor field. Then, we see how the general multipole moments of a vector field are constrained in complicated ways if that vector field satisfies the continuity equation. This leads to Dixon’s definition of the reduced multipole moments of a conserved vector field which we apply to the current density. In this section, in all but the final subsection, we keep the spacetime generic in anticipation of applying our analysis to gravity. In the final subsection, we specialize to Minkowski space in preparation for electromagnetic calculations.

3.1
Moments of a scalar field

Using the exponential map we can take functions on
\mathbb{T}
and turn them into functions on
T_{z}(\mathbb{T})
. For a scalar field
\phi(X)
(
X\in\mathbb{T}
) we may define the function
\phi^{\prime}(Z,v)
(
v\in T_{Z}(\mathbb{T})
) by:

\phi^{\prime}(Z,v)=\phi(\exp_{Z}(v)).

(34)

Because
\phi^{\prime}(Z,v)
is a function on the flat tangent space
T_{Z}(\mathbb{T})
, it is simple to define the Fourier transform
\widetilde{\phi}(Z,k)
and inverse Fourier transform:

\widetilde{\phi}(Z,k)=\int e^{-ik\cdot v}\phi^{\prime}(Z,v)\frac{D^{4}v}{(2\pi)^{2}},\hskip 21.52771pt\phi^{\prime}(Z,v)=\int e^{ik\cdot v}\widetilde{\phi}(Z,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}

(35)

where the invariant tangent space measure and Fourier space measure are defined as:

D^{4}v=\sqrt{-\det g(Z)}d^{4}v,\hskip 21.52771ptD^{4}k=\frac{d^{4}k}{\sqrt{-\det g(Z)}}.

(36)

Using that
v^{\mu}=-\sigma^{\mu}(Z,X)
we can express the inverse Fourier transform for our original scalar function as:

\phi(X)=\int e^{-ik_{\mu}\sigma^{\mu}(Z,X)}\widetilde{\phi}(Z,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}

(37)

It immediately follows that for scalar functions
\phi(X)
and
\psi(X)
and their associated
\phi^{\prime}(Z,v),\psi^{\prime}(Z,v)
:

\int_{\Sigma(\lambda)}\psi^{*}(X)\phi(X)w^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}=\int\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{n}}{n!}k_{\mu_{1}}...k_{\mu_{n}}\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(Z,k)\int_{\Sigma}\sigma^{\mu_{1}}...\sigma^{\mu_{n}}\phi(X)w^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(38)

Define then the moments
F^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{n}}_{n}
and the moment generating function
F
associated to the scalar function
\phi
:

\displaystyle F_{n}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{n}}[\phi](\lambda)=\int_{\Sigma}(-\sigma^{\mu_{1}})...(-\sigma^{\mu_{n}})\phi(X)w^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}

(39)

\displaystyle F[\phi](\lambda,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}F_{n}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{n}}k_{\mu_{1}}...k_{\mu_{n}}.

(40)

Automatically the moments of
\phi
satisfy the conditions:

F^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{n}}_{n}=F^{(\mu_{1}...\mu_{n})}_{n},\hskip 21.52771ptu_{\mu_{1}}F_{n}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{n}}=0

(41)

where for this last condition it is useful to remember equation (
8
). The moment generating function determines how
\phi(X)
behaves against test functions and thus determines
\phi(X)
completely according to:

\int_{\Sigma(\lambda)}\psi^{*}(X)\phi(X)w^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}=\int\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(Z,k)F(\lambda,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(42)

3.2
Moments of a general tensor field

Suppose we now consider a tensor field
\phi^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m}}{}_{\beta_{1}...\beta_{n}}(X)
. Using Synge’s world function and the exponential map we can translate this to a tensor function on the tangent space at
z
by:

\phi^{\prime\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(Z,v)=(-\sigma^{\mu_{1}}{}_{\alpha_{1}})...(-\sigma^{\mu_{m}}{}_{\alpha_{m}})(-\sigma^{-\beta_{1}}{}_{\nu_{1}})...(-\sigma^{-\beta_{n}}{}_{\nu_{n}})\phi^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m}}{}_{\beta_{1}...\beta_{n}}(\exp_{Z}(v)).

(43)

The Fourier transform and its inverse may now be defined as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(Z,k)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{e^{-ik\cdot v}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\phi^{\prime\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(Z,v)D^{4}v

(44)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(Z,v)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{e^{ik\cdot v}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\widetilde{\phi}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(Z,k)D^{4}k

(45)

For a given
\phi
, suppose we define the lower rank tensor
\varphi
by contracting two indices:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m-1}}{}_{\beta_{1}...\beta_{n-1}}=\phi^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m-1}\alpha_{m}}{}_{\beta_{1}...\beta_{n-1}\alpha_{m}}

\displaystyle\implies\varphi^{\prime\mu_{1}...\mu_{m-1}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n-1}}=\phi^{\prime\mu_{1}...\mu_{m-1}\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n-1}\mu_{m}}.

(46)

This property only holds because the upper index bitensor propagator in (
43
)
(-\sigma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha})
is the matrix inverse of the lower index bitensor propagator
(-\sigma^{-\beta}{}_{\nu})
. Alternatively, suppose we consider the moments of a vector field
\phi_{\beta}
which is itself the gradient of a scalar field
\varphi
:

\phi_{\beta}=\nabla_{\beta}\varphi\implies\widetilde{\phi}_{\nu}=ik_{\nu}\widetilde{\varphi}.

(47)

This property only holds if the lower index bitensor propagator is
-\sigma^{-\beta}{}_{\nu}
. Therefore while one could have imagined other bitensor propagators to use for transporting the components of
\phi
, such as parallel transport, the choice in (
43
) is unique in satisfying both (
46
) and (
47
). Unfortunately, the expression given in (
43
) has the property that translation to a tensor function on tangent space does not commute with raising/lowering indices of the original tensor field. This causes no real inconvenience for our calculations but one should be aware that
\phi^{\prime\mu}\neq g^{\mu\nu}(Z)\phi^{\prime}_{\nu}
if
\phi^{\prime\mu}
is defined as above from
\phi^{\mu}
and
\phi^{\prime}_{\mu}
is defined as above from
\phi_{\mu}
.

For a generic
\phi
, the moments and moment generating function of
\phi
are defined by:

\displaystyle F_{N}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{N}\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}[\phi](\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}(-\sigma^{\mu_{1}})....(-\sigma^{\mu_{N}})\phi^{\prime\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}w^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}

(48)

\displaystyle F^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}[\phi](\lambda,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{N}}{N!}F_{N}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{N}\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}k_{\mu_{1}}...k_{\mu_{N}}.

(49)

Automatically the moments of
\phi
satisfy the conditions:

F_{N}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{N}\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}=F_{N}^{(\mu_{1}...\mu_{N})\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}},\hskip 21.52771ptu_{\mu_{1}}F_{N}^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{N}\rho_{1}...\rho_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}=0.

(50)

As well,
\phi
’s behavior against test functions is determined by:

\int_{\Sigma}\psi^{*\beta_{1}...\beta_{n}}{}_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m}}\phi^{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{m}}{}_{\beta_{1}...\beta_{n}}w^{\gamma}d\Sigma_{\gamma}=\int\widetilde{\psi}^{*\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}{}_{\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}F^{\mu_{1}...\mu_{m}}{}_{\nu_{1}...\nu_{n}}(\lambda,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(51)

Thus, just as in the scalar case the moment generating function determines the original tensor field.

3.3
Moments of a conserved vector field

If we consider a vector field
\phi^{\alpha}(X)
with moments as defined above then a brief calculation reveals:

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi^{*}\nabla_{\alpha}\phi^{\alpha}d\mathbb{T}=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(z,k)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{(n-1)!}k_{\mu_{1}}...k_{\mu_{n}}F^{(\mu_{1}...\mu_{n})}_{n-1}\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}D\lambda

(52)

If
\phi^{\alpha}
is a conserved vector field so that
\nabla_{\alpha}\phi^{\alpha}=0
, then the left hand side is 0 for all
\psi
. One may then wish based on this to conclude that
F^{(\mu_{1}...\mu_{n})}_{n-1}
is 0 for each
n\geq 1
. However, this is not a valid deduction.
\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(Z,k)
at each fixed
\lambda
determines the function
\psi(X)
through the inverse Fourier transform and so
\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(Z(\lambda_{1}),k)
and
\widetilde{\psi}^{*}(Z(\lambda_{2}),k)
for
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
are not independent of each other. Because of this, one cannot conclude that the integrand above at each
\lambda
must be individually 0 as they may conspire to cancel at different
\lambda
for arbitrary
\psi^{*}(X)
. Consequently, it is difficult to conclude anything explicit about the moments of
\phi^{\alpha}
from the condition
\nabla_{\alpha}\phi^{\alpha}=0
.

Because of the mentioned difficulty, Dixon helpfully introduced an alternate set of reduced moments for a conserved vector field. For us the vector field of interest will always be
J^{\alpha}
. The goal of this reduced set of moments is precisely to produce a set which does not have the entanglements of the naive moments for a conserved vector field. Due to the absence of entanglements between the moments, when using Dixon’s moments it is valid to equate integrands moment-by-moment. To arrive at Dixon’s reduced multipole moments, which for a conserved vector field are written
m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}
, we first need to introduce a few building blocks. Define:

\displaystyle\Theta^{\kappa\lambda}_{n}(Z,X)

\displaystyle=(n+1)\int_{0}^{1}{\sigma^{\kappa}}_{\alpha}(Z,\zeta(t))\sigma^{\alpha\lambda}(Z,\zeta(t))t^{n}dt

(53)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\int_{\Sigma}\sigma^{\lambda_{1}}...\sigma^{\lambda_{n}}\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{n-1}J^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}\hskip 21.52771pt(n\geq 1)

(54)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{j}_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\int_{\Sigma}\sigma^{\lambda_{1}}...\sigma^{\lambda_{n}}{\sigma^{\mu}}_{\alpha}J^{\alpha}w^{\beta}d\Sigma_{\beta}

(55)

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{j}_{n+1}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}[\mu\nu]}+\frac{1}{n+1}\mathfrak{q}_{n+1}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}[\mu\nu]\kappa}\frac{\dot{z}^{\kappa}}{\mathtt{e}}

(56)

\displaystyle m_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{2n}{n+1}Q^{(\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n})\mu}_{n-1}\hskip 21.52771pt(n\geq 1).

(57)

The
m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}
moments (called the reduced moments) will be the actual moments of interest. The other quantities defined are useful intermediate pieces for calculation. The reduced moments automatically satisfy:

\displaystyle m_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}=m_{n}^{(\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n})\mu},\hskip 21.52771ptm^{(\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu)}_{n}=0,

(58)

\displaystyle u_{\lambda_{1}}m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n-1}[\lambda_{n}\mu]}_{n}=0\hskip 21.52771pt(n\geq 2).

(59)

Dixon finds that beyond these conditions, the reduced moments are not restricted by the conservation of
J^{\alpha}
and that they are independent of each other for different values of
n
. It is useful to define the
0^{\text{th}}
moment:

m^{\mu}_{0}=q\frac{\dot{z}^{\mu}}{\mathtt{e}}

(60)

where
q
is the total charge of the body defined by:

q=\int_{\Sigma}J^{\alpha}d\Sigma_{\alpha}.

(61)

Due to the conservation of
J^{\alpha}
,
q
is independent of
\lambda
. Dixon finds also that the reduced moments are independent of this
0^{\text{th}}
moment. Then, define the reduced moment generating function:

M^{\mu}(\lambda,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}k_{\lambda_{1}}...k_{\lambda_{n}}.

(62)

Like the naive moments, the reduced moment generating functions determine the behavior of
J^{\alpha}
against test functions. In particular, for an arbitrary vector field
A_{\alpha}(X)
:

\int_{\Sigma}A^{*}_{\alpha}(X)J^{\alpha}(X)w^{\beta}d\Sigma_{\beta}=\int\widetilde{A}^{*}_{\mu}(Z,k)M^{\mu}(\lambda,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(63)

The moment generating function automatically satisfies:

M^{\mu}k_{\mu}=\frac{q}{\mathtt{e}}\dot{z}\cdot k.

(64)

This implies that for the gradient of a scalar function, using the definition of the reduced moment generating function:

\int_{\Sigma}J^{\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}fw^{\beta}d\Sigma_{\beta}=\frac{q}{\mathtt{e}}\frac{d}{d\lambda}f(Z).

(65)

For
f
which decay sufficiently quickly for no boundary term to be necessary under integration by parts we immediately have:

0=\int_{\mathbb{T}}f\nabla_{\alpha}J^{\alpha}d\mathbb{T}=-q\left.f(Z)\right|^{\lambda\to\infty}_{\lambda\to-\infty}

(66)

which is true without placing any restrictions on the reduced moments of
J^{\alpha}
beyond
m_{0}^{\mu}
. Dixon proved
dixon2
that these reduced moments are the unique set of moments which are independent of each other for different
n
, have only
m_{0}^{\mu}
restricted by the conservation law, and satisfy the index symmetry conditions in equations (
58
) and (
59
).

Through (
63
), the current density is determined in terms of the reduced multipole moments. Explicitly comparing that behavior against test functions to (
33
) and using crucially that the reduced multipole moments are unique and contain no interdependencies, we can identify:

m_{n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\mu}=-2\,n!\mathcal{Q}_{n-1}^{(\rho_{1}...\rho_{n})\mu}

(67)

which gives the reduced multipole moments from the couplings in the action. Alternatively, this can be nicely inverted using the index symmetry conditions of both quantities to find:

\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\mu\nu}=-\frac{1}{n!}\frac{1}{n+2}m_{n+1}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}[\mu\nu]}.

(68)

This allows the direct determination of the coupling of the body to the field strength in the action from its exact reduced multipole moments.

3.4
Moments of the current density in Minkowski space

We now specialize our calculations to Minkowski space. We can represent an arbitrary element
v^{\mu}
of
T_{z}(\mathbb{T})
by a vector
Y=y^{A}\vec{\iota}_{A}=y^{A}\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{A}\vec{e}_{\mu}
where
\vec{\iota}_{A}
are local Minkowskian basis vectors (
\vec{\iota}_{A}\cdot\vec{\iota}_{B}=\eta_{AB}
) and
\vec{e}_{\mu}
are coordinate basis vectors. Thus:

v^{\mu}={\Lambda^{\mu}}_{A}y^{A}.

(69)

Just as in section
2
we continue to always choose the tetrad so that
\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{0}=u^{\mu}
. In flat space:

\sigma(Z,X)=\frac{1}{2}(x-z)^{2},\hskip 21.52771pt\sigma^{\mu}=-(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}),\hskip 21.52771pt{\sigma^{\mu}}_{\alpha}=-\delta^{\mu}_{\alpha}\implies\Theta^{\mu\nu}_{n}=\eta^{\mu\nu}

(70)

We always use lowercase beginning Latin alphabet indices
a,b,c,...
for values
1,2,3
on the tangent space. Then, we have:

X\in\Sigma(\lambda)\implies\exists\ y^{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ :\ x^{\mu}=z^{\mu}+{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{a}y^{a}.

(71)

Using the
y^{a}
coordinates and the definition of
\tau(X)
we may identify explicit flat space expressions for
w^{\alpha}
and
d\Sigma_{\alpha}
:

\displaystyle\lambda=\tau(z+\lambda u)\implies 1=(\dot{z}^{\alpha}+{\dot{\Lambda}^{\alpha}}{}_{a}y^{a})\nabla_{\alpha}\tau

(72)

\displaystyle w^{\alpha}=\frac{\dot{z}^{\alpha}+{\dot{\Lambda}^{\alpha}}{}_{a}y^{a}}{\mathtt{e}},\hskip 21.52771pt\hskip 21.52771ptd\Sigma_{\alpha}=-u_{\alpha}d^{3}y.

(73)

With these the
\text{q}_{n}
and
\text{j}_{n}
moments become:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu\nu}_{n}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\mu\nu}{\Lambda^{\lambda_{1}}}_{A_{1}}...{\Lambda^{\lambda_{n}}}_{A_{n}}\int_{\Sigma}y^{A_{1}}...y^{A_{n}}(-J\cdot u)d^{3}y

(74)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{j}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}

\displaystyle={\Lambda^{\lambda_{1}}}_{A_{1}}...{\Lambda^{\lambda_{n}}}_{A_{n}}{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{B}\int_{\Sigma}y^{A_{1}}...y^{A_{n}}{\Lambda_{\nu}}^{B}J^{\nu}\frac{-u\cdot\dot{z}-u\cdot\dot{\Lambda}\cdot y}{\mathtt{e}}d^{3}y.

(75)

Using the explicit formula for the worldline velocity
\dot{z}^{\mu}
in (
29
),
u\cdot\dot{z}=-\mathtt{e}
. As well, due to
(
24
)
,
\dot{\Lambda}=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
. Therefore:

\mathfrak{j}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}={\Lambda^{\lambda_{1}}}_{A_{1}}...{\Lambda^{\lambda_{n}}}_{A_{n}}{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{B}\int_{\Sigma}y^{A_{1}}...y^{A_{n}}{\Lambda_{\nu}}^{B}J^{\nu}d^{3}y+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}).

(76)

It is useful to define one last class of moments:

\displaystyle K_{n}^{a_{1}...a_{n}B}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Sigma}y^{a_{1}}...y^{a_{n}}{\Lambda_{\nu}}^{B}J^{\nu}d^{3}y

(77)

\displaystyle\implies\mathfrak{q}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu\nu}_{n}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\mu\nu}{\Lambda^{\lambda_{1}}}_{a_{1}}...{\Lambda^{\lambda_{n}}}_{a_{n}}K^{a_{1}...a_{n}0}_{n}

(78)

\displaystyle\implies\mathfrak{j}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu}_{n}

\displaystyle={\Lambda^{\lambda_{1}}}_{a_{1}}...{\Lambda^{\lambda_{n}}}_{a_{n}}{\Lambda^{\mu}}_{B}K^{a_{1}...a_{n}B}_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}).

(79)

For a stationary body in its center of momentum frame, the
K_{n}
moments coincide with the naive moments of the stationary current density. By following the chain of definitions from (
53
) to (
57
) these
K_{n}
moments determine the full reduced moments
m_{n}
. Therefore, computing the naive moments of a stationary body allows the determination of the (coupling independent part of the) full set of reduced moments.

4
Root-Kerr Multipole Moments

In this section we determine the linear in
\mathcal{F}
couplings in the dynamical mass function to all orders in spin for a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle. We begin by reviewing how the vector potential which defines the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle arises from the Kerr-Newman solution and computing some basic mechanical properties of the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
fields. Then, we use the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
fields to determine the charge and current densities which are the source of the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
solution using analysis which parallels the calculation of the source of the Kerr metric performed in Ref.
israel:1970
. From these charge and current densities we are then able to determine the
K_{n}
moments for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
and consequently determine the
m_{n}
reduced multipole moments up to corrections of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})
. Comparing these reduced moments to those produced by the current density produced by (
33
) fully determines all linear in
\mathcal{F}
terms in the dynamical mass function for a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle.

4.1
From Kerr-Newman to Root-Kerr

The Kerr-Newman solution for a stationary charged spinning black hole is defined by the line element and vector potential:

\displaystyle-d\tau^{2}=

\displaystyle\ -\frac{r^{2}-2Gmr+a^{2}+\frac{q^{2}G}{4\pi}}{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}(dt-a\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi)^{2}+\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}((r^{2}+a^{2})d\varphi-adt)^{2}

\displaystyle\ +(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)\left(\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}-2Gmr+a^{2}+\frac{q^{2}G}{4\pi}}+d\theta^{2}\right)

(80)

\displaystyle A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}=

\displaystyle\ \ \frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{-rdt+ar\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi}{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}.

(81)

The
G\to 0
limit of the Kerr-Newman metric produces the line element:

-d\tau^{2}=-dt^{2}+\frac{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{r^{2}+a^{2}}dr^{2}+(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)d\theta^{2}+(r^{2}+a^{2})\sin^{2}\theta d\varphi^{2}.

(82)

This is just the Minkowski line element in a particular choice of coordinates. The associated spatial metric
g^{(3)}_{ab}
implied by this line element is the natural metric of oblate-spheroidal coordinates, related to Cartesian coordinates by:

\displaystyle x=\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}}\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\hskip 21.52771pty=\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}}\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\hskip 21.52771ptz=r\cos\theta

(83)

and with corresponding coordinate basis vectors:

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{r}=\frac{\partial\vec{x}}{\partial r},\hskip 21.52771pt\vec{e}_{\theta}=\frac{\partial\vec{x}}{\partial\theta},\hskip 21.52771pt\vec{e}_{\varphi}=\frac{\partial\vec{x}}{\partial\varphi},\hskip 21.52771ptg^{(3)}_{ab}=\vec{e}_{a}\cdot\vec{e}_{b}.

(84)

The vector potential of this system defines the vector potential of a stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle:

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle=-A_{0}=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{r}{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}

(85)

\displaystyle\vec{A}

\displaystyle=g^{(3)ab}A_{a}\vec{e}_{b}=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{ar}{(r^{2}+a^{2})(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)}\vec{e}_{\varphi}.

(86)

These coincide with the vector potential in equation 2.1 of
Chung:2019yfs
. From these potentials, we find the electric and magnetic fields:

\displaystyle\vec{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{(r^{2}+a^{2})(r^{2}-a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)}{(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}\vec{e}_{r}-\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{2ra^{2}\sin\theta\cos\theta}{(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}\vec{e}_{\theta}

(87)

\displaystyle\vec{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{2ar(r^{2}+a^{2})\cos\theta}{(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}\vec{e}_{r}+\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{a(r^{2}-a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)\sin\theta}{(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}\vec{e}_{\theta}.

(88)

Away from any singularities, this magnetic field satisfies
\nabla\times\vec{B}=0
and so can be expressed in terms of a magnetic scalar potential
\vec{B}=-\nabla\psi
. In general coordinates in Minkowski space, the condition that the magnetic field (away from any current sources) be given by both a magnetic scalar potential and vector potential is:

-\sqrt{\det g^{(3)}}g^{(3)ab}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x^{b}}=\epsilon^{abc}\frac{\partial A_{c}}{\partial x^{b}}.

(89)

Using the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
vector potential, one may use this to explicitly compute the associated magnetic scalar potential. Doing so gives:

\psi=\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{a\cos\theta}{r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}.

(90)

Defining
\vec{a}=a\vec{e}_{z}
, in oblate-spheroidal coordinates we have the identity:

|\vec{x}-i\vec{a}|=r-ia\cos\theta.

(91)

Consequently, the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle is equivalently defined
Lynden-Bell:2002dvr
by producing the electric and magnetic scalar potentials or electric and magnetic fields given by:

\phi+i\psi=\frac{q}{4\pi|\vec{x}-i\vec{a}|}\implies\vec{E}+i\vec{B}=-\nabla\left(\frac{q}{4\pi|\vec{x}-i\vec{a}|}\right).

(92)

4.2
Mechanical Properties of the Stationary Root-Kerr Solution

Consider a surface of constant
r=R
for integration. If we take
R\to\infty
this becomes the spatial boundary. The surface area element is:

d\vec{\mathbb{A}}=(r^{2}+a^{2})\sin\theta\vec{e}_{r}d\theta d\varphi.

(93)

Then, by Gauss’ law the total charge of the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle is given by:

\int_{\mathbb{S}}\rho d^{3}\vec{x}=\int_{\partial\mathbb{S}}\vec{E}\cdot d\vec{\mathbb{A}}=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{q}{4\pi}\frac{R^{2}-a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}{(R^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{2}}(R^{2}+a^{2})\sin\theta d\varphi d\theta=q

(94)

confirming our consistent use of the symbol
q
for the total charge of the distribution.

The magnetic dipole moment of a charge distribution is by definition:

\vec{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\vec{x}\times\vec{J}d^{3}\vec{x}.

(95)

Because the distribution is stationary, rewriting
\vec{J}=\nabla\times\vec{B}
and integrating by parts sufficiently many times this is equivalently:

\vec{\mu}=-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\partial\mathbb{S}}\vec{A}\times d\vec{\mathbb{A}}.

(96)

With the same surface of constant
r=R
:

\vec{\mu}=-\frac{3}{2}\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{qa}{4\pi}\frac{R}{R^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta^{2}}\vec{e}_{\varphi}\times\vec{e}_{r}\sin\theta d\varphi d\theta=q\vec{a}.

(97)

So,
\vec{a}
is the magnetic dipole moment per unit charge.

Like for a point charge, the total energy in the electromagnetic field for this system is divergent. To regulate this instead of integrating all the way down to
r=0
for the total energy, we integrate down to a cutoff
r=\varepsilon
. Then, the total energy is:

E

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\frac{|\vec{E}|^{2}+|\vec{B}|^{2}}{2}d^{3}\vec{x}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

(98)

The Poynting vector for the particle is:

\vec{\wp}=\vec{E}\times\vec{B}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi^{2}}\frac{a\vec{e}_{\varphi}}{(r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta)^{3}}.

(99)

This leads to the total linear momentum:

\vec{p}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\vec{\wp}d^{3}\vec{x}=0.

(100)

So, the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
fields given are in fact in the center of momentum frame. Consequently, the energy found before is the invariant mass of the distribution:

m=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{\varepsilon}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)\right).

(101)

The Poynting vector we found leads to the total angular momentum (which because we are in the center of momentum frame is the spin angular momentum):

\displaystyle\vec{S}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\vec{x}\times\vec{\wp}d^{3}\vec{x}=\frac{q^{2}}{16\pi\varepsilon}\left(1+\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)+\frac{2\varepsilon}{a}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{a^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{a^{3}}\arctan\left(\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\right)\right)\vec{a}.

(102)

While both the spin and the mass of the solution are divergent in
\varepsilon
, their ratio has a finite
\varepsilon
to 0 limit. Therefore, the physical value of the ratio of the spin to the mass is:

\frac{\vec{S}}{m}=\vec{a}.

(103)

Immediately it follows that for this distribution:

\vec{\mu}=\frac{q}{m}\vec{S}.

(104)

Thus, the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle has a gyromagnetic ratio of exactly
2
.

4.3
Charge and Current density

The electric and magnetic fields as well as scalar and vector potential given diverge whenever
r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta=0
and nowhere else. For
r>0
the potentials and their derivatives are continuous and so there are no surface charges or currents for
r>0
. Because the divergence of the right hand side in (
87
) is 0 away from any poles, there is no charge density for
r>0
. Because the curl of the right hand side in (
88
) is 0 away from any poles, there is no current density for
r>0
. Consequently, the source for the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle must only have support for
r=0
. For
r=0
the oblate spheroidal coordinates reduce to the disk in the
xy
plane with center at the origin and radius
a
. In order to approach this disk from above we must take
r\to 0
with
\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}
and in order to approach from below we must take
r\to 0
with
\theta>\frac{\pi}{2}
. It is useful to define the coordinate
\chi=\theta
for
z>0
and
\chi=\pi-\theta
for
z<0
. This way, two points approaching the disk, one from above and one from below, will limit to the same point in space when their limiting values of
\chi
and
\varphi
coincide. The disk is then parameterized by the coordinates
\chi
and
\varphi
by:

\displaystyle x=a\sin\chi\cos\varphi,\hskip 21.52771pty=a\sin\chi\sin\varphi,\hskip 21.52771ptz=0.

(105)

We can also use the cylindrical radial coordinate
\text{r}=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}=a\sin\chi
.

The surface charge density inside the disk is given by:

\sigma_{\text{disk}}=\vec{e}_{z}\cdot(\vec{E}(0,\chi,\varphi)-\vec{E}(0,\pi-\chi,\varphi))=-\frac{qa}{2\pi(a^{2}-\text{r}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

(106)

We can integrate this from
\chi=0
to
\chi=\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon
to get the total charge inside the disk:

Q_{\text{disk}}=\int_{\text{disk}}\sigma_{\text{disk}}d\mathbb{A}=q\left(1-\frac{1}{\sin\varepsilon}\right).

(107)

The surface charge density cannot be extended all the way to the ring at
\chi=\frac{\pi}{2}
as it has a nonintegrable divergence. We know from before that the total charge of the distribution is
q
and so there must be a charged ring at
\chi=\frac{\pi}{2}
so that the total charge between this ring and the disk is
q
. This ring is precisely the ring on which
r^{2}+a^{2}\cos^{2}\theta=0
and so where the potentials and fields are divergent. The charge density on the ring must be azimuthally symmetric because the fields are. So:

q=Q_{\text{disk}}+Q_{\text{ring}}=q-\frac{q}{\sin\varepsilon}+\int_{0}^{2\pi}\lambda_{\text{ring}}ad\varphi\implies\lambda_{\text{ring}}=\frac{q}{2\pi a\sin\varepsilon}.

(108)

Thus the total charge density of the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle is:

\rho=-\frac{qa}{2\pi(a^{2}-\text{r}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\delta(z)\vartheta(a\cos\varepsilon-\text{r})+\frac{q}{2\pi a\sin\varepsilon}\delta(z)\delta(\text{r}-a).

(109)

The surface current density inside the disk is:

\displaystyle\vec{K}_{\text{disk}}

\displaystyle=\vec{e}_{z}\times(\vec{B}(0,\chi,\varphi)-\vec{B}(0,\pi-\chi,\varphi))=\sigma_{\text{disk}}\frac{\text{r}}{a}\vec{\iota}_{\varphi}

(110)

where
\vec{\iota}_{\varphi}
is the unit vector in the direction of
\vec{e}_{\varphi}
. This is precisely the surface current density produced by rigidly rotating the surface charge density of the disk with angular velocity
\vec{\omega}
given by:

\vec{\omega}=\frac{1}{a}\vec{e}_{z}.

(111)

Rotating the ring with this same angular velocity produces a current in the ring of:

\vec{I}_{\text{ring}}=\lambda_{\text{ring}}\vec{\iota}_{\varphi}=\frac{q}{2\pi a\sin\varepsilon}\vec{\iota}_{\varphi}.

(112)

These currents produce a magnetic dipole moment:

\displaystyle\vec{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\text{disk}}\vec{x}\times\vec{K}_{\text{disk}}d\mathbb{A}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\text{ring}}\vec{x}\times\vec{I}_{\text{ring}}ds=q\vec{a}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\sin\varepsilon\right).

(113)

In the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit this produces exactly the magnetic dipole moment we found before. Therefore, the current density for the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
distribution is:

\vec{J}=\rho\frac{\vec{a}\times\vec{x}}{a^{2}}

(114)

and the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle is exactly a charged disk and ring with the charge distribution given by equation (
109
) and rotating with the angular velocity
\frac{1}{a}
about the central axis of the disk. These charge and current densities are precisely analogous to the mass density and energy-momentum tensor found in Ref.
israel:1970
for a stationary Kerr black hole.

4.4
Stationary Multipole Moments

We can now compute the
K_{n}
moments explicitly for a stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle:

K^{a_{1}...a_{n}0}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}x^{a_{1}}...x^{a_{n}}\rho(\vec{x})d^{3}\vec{x},\hskip 21.52771ptK^{a_{1}...a_{n}b}_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{S}}x^{a_{1}}...x^{a_{n}}J^{b}(\vec{x})d^{3}\vec{x}.

(115)

By using (
114
), we find immediately:

K^{a_{1}...a_{n}b}_{n}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}{\epsilon^{b}}_{cd}a^{c}K^{da_{1}...a_{n}0}_{n+1}

(116)

and so only the multipole moments of
\rho
need to be computed in order to determine the moments of the full distribution. Further, due to the full symmetrization of
K_{n}^{a_{1}...a_{n}0}
, for an arbitrary 3-vector
k_{a}
:

K^{a_{1}...a_{n}0}_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^{n}}{\partial k_{a_{1}}...\partial k_{a_{n}}}\left(K_{n}^{b_{1}...b_{n}0}k_{b_{1}}...k_{b_{n}}\right).

(117)

The charge density of the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle is localized to the
xy
plane and is rotationally symmetric about the
z
axis. So, it can be written as:

\rho(\vec{x})=\sigma(\text{r})\delta(z)

(118)

where in particular for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
:

\sigma(\text{r})=-\frac{qa}{2\pi(a^{2}-\text{r}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\vartheta(a\cos\varepsilon-\text{r})+\frac{q}{2\pi a\sin\varepsilon}\delta(\text{r}-a).

(119)

Then:

K_{n}^{a_{1}...a_{n}0}k_{a_{1}}...k_{a_{n}}=(k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{n}\varphi d\varphi\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(\text{r})\text{r}^{n+1}d\text{r}.

(120)

If
n
is odd the azimuthal integral gives 0, so we only need to consider even
n
. For even
n
the azimuthal integral is:

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{2n}\varphi d\varphi=2\pi\frac{(2n)!}{4^{n}n!^{2}}.

(121)

With the integration variable
x=\frac{1}{a}\sqrt{a^{2}-\text{r}^{2}}
the radial integral becomes:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(\text{r})\text{r}^{2n+1}d\text{r}=\frac{qa^{2n}}{2\pi}\left(1+\int_{\sin\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{1-(1-x^{2})^{n}}{x^{2}}dx\right).

(122)

Here it is safe to take the
\varepsilon\to 0
limit to give:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\sigma(\text{r})\text{r}^{2n+1}d\text{r}=\frac{qa^{2n}}{2\pi}\frac{4^{n}n!^{2}}{(2n)!}.

(123)

Thus the nonzero
K_{n}
moments become:

\displaystyle K_{2n}^{a_{1}...a_{2n}0}v_{a_{1}}...v_{a_{2n}}

\displaystyle=q|\vec{a}\times\vec{v}|^{2n}

(124)

\displaystyle K_{2n+1}^{a_{1}...a_{2n+1}b}v_{a_{1}}...v_{a_{2n+1}}

\displaystyle=q|\vec{a}\times\vec{v}|^{2n}(\vec{a}\times\vec{v})^{b}.

(125)

4.5
Dynamical Multipole Moments

Now we return the
K_{n}
moments to the definition of the reduced multipole moments. For a generic body allowed to respond to external fields, the multipole moments will in general depend on those external fields and so the general moments in external fields cannot be fully determined from the stationary moments. It is unclear at this time what is the appropriate response for a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
body to external fields. So, for an exact dynamical
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
body:

K_{n}^{a_{1}...a_{n}b}=K_{n\text{ stat}}^{a_{1}...a_{n}b}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})

(126)

where
K_{n\text{ stat}}^{a_{1}...a_{n}b}
are the
K_{n}
moments we found for a stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle. To express the reduced moments it is useful to define the projector
\perp^{\mu}{}_{\nu}
which projects 4-vectors into the plane of the disk:

\perp^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}+u^{\mu}u_{\nu}-\hat{a}^{\mu}\hat{a}_{\nu}.

(127)

We also write
k_{\perp}^{\mu}
as a shorthand for
k_{\perp}^{\mu}=\perp^{\mu}{}_{\nu}k^{\nu}
and
k_{\perp}
without an index as a shorthand for
\sqrt{|k_{\mu}\perp^{\mu}{}_{\nu}k^{\nu}|}
. Using the definition of the reduced multipole moments for arbitrary 4-vectors
k_{\mu}
and
v_{\mu}
we find:

\displaystyle k_{\lambda_{1}}...k_{\lambda_{2n}}v_{\mu}m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{2n}\mu}_{2n}

\displaystyle=qa^{2n}k_{\perp}^{2n}u\cdot v-qa^{2n}k_{\perp}^{2n-2}(u\cdot k)(k_{\perp}\cdot v)+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})

(128)

\displaystyle k_{\lambda_{1}}...k_{\lambda_{2n+1}}v_{\mu}m^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{2n+1}\mu}_{2n+1}

\displaystyle=qa^{2n}k^{2n}_{\perp}\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u^{\mu}a^{\nu}k^{\rho}v^{\sigma}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}).

(129)

Returning these to (
68
), we find:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{2n}\mu\nu}\partial^{2n}_{\rho_{1}...\rho_{2n}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\frac{q}{(2n+1)!}a^{2n}\partial_{\perp}^{2n}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2})

(130)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2n+1}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{2n+1}\mu\nu}\partial^{2n+1}_{\rho_{1}...\rho_{2n+1}}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{q}{(2n+2)!}a^{2n+2}\partial_{\perp}^{2n}\partial_{\perp}^{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2}).

(131)

With the definition of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, these produce the dynamical mass function:

\mathcal{M}=m+q\frac{\sinh(a\partial_{\perp})}{a\partial_{\perp}}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+q\frac{1-\cosh(a\partial_{\perp})}{\partial_{\perp}^{2}}\partial_{\perp}^{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2}).

(132)

There is no subtlety in defining the square root or inverse of the differential operator here because once the trigonometric functions are series expanded only positive even powers of
\partial_{\perp}
survive. We will see later that for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particles, at least at low orders in spin, the squared dynamical mass function
\mathcal{M}^{2}
may be a simpler object when
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F})^{2}
operators are considered. So, going forward we will express the dynamical mass function as:

\mathcal{M}^{2}=m^{2}+2mq\frac{\sinh(a\partial_{\perp})}{a\partial_{\perp}}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+2mq\frac{1-\cosh(a\partial_{\perp})}{\partial_{\perp}^{2}}\partial_{\perp}^{\mu}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2}).

(133)

This
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
dynamical mass function is our primary result for electromagnetism. When acting on a field strength which in the neighborhood of the body is a vacuum solution (
\partial_{\nu}\mathcal{F}^{\mu\nu}=0
) of Maxwell’s equations, this reduces to:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{2}=m^{2}+2mq\frac{\sin(a\Delta)}{a\Delta}{}^{\star}{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+2mq\frac{1-\cos(a\Delta)}{(a\Delta)^{2}}(a\cdot\partial)\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2})

(134)

where the
\Delta
differential operator is defined by:

a\Delta=\sqrt{(a\cdot\partial)^{2}-a^{2}(u\cdot\partial)^{2}}.

(135)

Again there is no subtlety in defining the square root or inverse of the differential operator. In general (
134
) and (
133
) are not equivalent, however we show that for computing Compton amplitudes they are interchangeable.

The dynamical mass function in (
134
) very nearly matches the couplings in Ref.
Levi:2015msa
(if the analysis done there for gravity is done for electromagnetism). In particular, phrased as a dynamical mass function, the couplings in Ref.
Levi:2015msa
determine:

\mathcal{M}^{2}=m^{2}+2mq\frac{\sin(a\cdot\partial)}{a\cdot\partial}{}^{\star}{\mathcal{F}}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+2mq\frac{1-\cos(a\cdot\partial)}{a\cdot\partial}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2})

(136)

Equation (
134
) becomes this result with the replacement:

a\Delta\rightarrow a\cdot\partial.

(137)

The
(u\cdot\partial)
terms present in
a\Delta
do not contribute to the three point amplitude and so
a\Delta
and
a\cdot\partial
are indistinguishable in a three point matching calculation. For this reason, the analyses of Refs.
Vines:2017hyw
;
Bern:2020buy
were insensitive to the couplings on terms of the form
S^{2n}(u\cdot\partial)^{2n}\mathcal{F}_{\cdot\cdot}
. The first of such terms is present in Ref.
Siemonsen:2019dsu
with an undetermined Wilson coefficient. Similarly, in Ref.
Levi:2015msa

(u\cdot\partial)
are not considered because if one uses the equations of motion in the action, they can be shuffled into order
\mathcal{F}^{2}
terms. One can see this quickly on the lowest order such term, for example:

\displaystyle\mathtt{e}a^{2}(u\cdot\partial)^{2}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}

\displaystyle=a^{2}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\dot{z}^{\sigma}\partial_{\sigma}\partial_{\rho}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2})

(138)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(a^{2}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}\right)-\frac{d}{d\lambda}(a^{2}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho})\partial_{\rho}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2})

(139)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(a^{2}a^{\mu}u^{\nu}u^{\rho}\partial_{\rho}{}^{\star}\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}^{2}).

(140)

For three-point amplitudes, (
133
), (
134
), and (
136
) are interchangeable. For Compton amplitudes, (
133
) and (
134
) are interchangeable but distinct from (
136
). The worldline evolution of all three are distinct. Importantly, by the definition of Dixon’s moments (
63
) holds for any test function vector field
A_{\alpha}(X)
, not only the physically relevant vector potential and that it holds without using the solution to the equations of motion. We have shown that for a
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle the only dynamical mass function for which (
63
) holds without using the electromagnetic MPD equations is (
133
). If one shuffles away
(u\cdot\partial)
operators in favor of
\mathcal{F}^{2}
so that the linear in
\mathcal{F}
dynamical mass function is given by (
136
), then (
63
) will not be true identically. In this sense, the advantage of (
133
) is that it uniquely provides at each
\lambda
the physically correct multipole moments for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
.

5
Electromagnetic Compton Amplitude

In this section we formally compute the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
Compton amplitude to all orders in spin up to contact terms. Those contact terms are determined by
\mathcal{F}^{2}
operators in the dynamical mass function which the multipole analysis is insensitive to. We begin by computing a formal expression for the all orders in spin Compton amplitude for a generic charged spinning body in terms of the dynamical mass function. Next we consider that generic Compton amplitude explicitly to order
S^{3}
. At
\mathcal{O}(S^{1})
there is a single Wilson coefficient in the action and it can be determined by matching the
\mathcal{O}(S^{1})
three-point amplitude or Compton amplitude. At
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
there are 5 new Wilson coefficients in the action. One of them can be determined by matching the
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
three-point amplitude while the other 4 can be determined uniquely by matching the
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
Compton amplitude. At
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
there are 8 new Wilson coefficients in the action. One of them can be determined by matching the
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
three-point amplitude while the other 7 appear only as contact terms in the Compton amplitude. Among those 7, there are only 6 linearly independent structures appearing in the
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
Compton amplitude, and so there is one linear combination of Wilson coefficients that the Compton amplitude is independent of at this order in spin.

Once we have the results for a generic body through
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
, we specialize our interest to
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
. Requiring the exponentiation of spin structure as found in Ref.
Guevara:2018wpp
through
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
for the helicity-preserving amplitude (which develops a spurious pole starting at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
) and through
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
for the helicity-reversing amplitude (which has no such spurious pole) fixes all Wilson coefficients through
\mathcal{O}(S^{2})
and 4 of the 8 new operators at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
. Alternatively, requiring the helicity-preserving amplitude to have the shift-symmetry described in Refs.
Aoude:2022trd
;
Bern:2022kto
;
Haddad:2023ylx
;
Aoude:2023vdk
through
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
fixes 3 of the 8 new operators at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
. The spin-exponentiation and shift-symmetry are consistent with each other, and together fix 6 of the 8 new operators at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
(as they share one redundant condition). The dynamical mass function (
134
) determines 2 Wilson coefficients at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
, one of which is fixed by the three-point amplitude and the other of which is independent of and consistent with both spin-exponentiation and shift-symmetry. Together then Dixon’s multipole moments, spin-exponentiation, and shift-symmetry fix 7 of the 8 Wilson coefficients at
\mathcal{O}(S^{3})
, which is the maximum amount possible by using the Compton amplitude (due to the presence of a linearly independent combination of Wilson coefficients that the amplitude is independent of).

5.1
Formal Classical Compton

We consider Compton scattering of an incoming photon with polarization
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and momentum
k_{1}
off of a massive spinning charged body with initial momentum
mv
(
v\cdot v=-1
) and initial spin
s
to an outgoing photon with polarization
\mathcal{E}_{2}
and momentum
k_{2}
and perturbed massive body. For a plane wave vector potential with strength
\epsilon
:

A_{\mu}(X)=\epsilon\mathcal{E}_{\mu}e^{ik\cdot x},\hskip 21.52771pt\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}(X)=\epsilon f_{\mu\nu}e^{ik\cdot x},\hskip 21.52771ptf_{\mu\nu}=ik_{\mu}\mathcal{E}_{\nu}-ik_{\nu}\mathcal{E}_{\mu}.

(141)

Because the tree level Compton amplitude is
\mathcal{O}(q^{2})
, it depends only on the

is the total linear momentum of the body defined by:

p^{\kappa}=\int_{\Sigma}(-\sigma^{-1}_{\alpha\lambda})\sigma^{\lambda\kappa}T^{\alpha\beta}d\Sigma_{\beta}

(263)

and

is the total spin tensor of the body defined by:

(264)

We will always choose our definition of the worldline and Cauchy slicing so that

is orthogonal to all tangent vectors to
\Sigma(\lambda)
. With this choice we can require:

p^{\mu}=|p|u^{\mu},\hskip 21.52771ptS^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}=0

(265)

and thus we can introduce the spin vector
S^{\mu}
defined so that:

S^{\mu}=-\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\epsilon}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u_{\nu}S_{\rho\sigma}\implies S_{\mu\nu}=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u^{\rho}S^{\sigma}.

(266)

The conservation of

determines the time evolution of

and
S^{\mu}
(through the MPD equations) but determines nothing about the time evolution of the higher moments. Dixon finds also that the reduced moments are independent of the
0^{\text{th}}
and

moments. Then, define the reduced moment generating function:

I^{\mu\nu}(\lambda,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!}I^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\mu\nu}_{n}k_{\lambda_{1}}...k_{\lambda_{n}}.

(267)

Like the naive moments, the reduced moment generating functions determine the behavior of

against test functions. In particular, for an arbitrary symmetric tensor field

:

\int_{\Sigma}h^{*}_{\alpha\beta}(X)T^{\alpha\beta}(X)w^{\gamma}d\Sigma_{\gamma}=\int\widetilde{h}^{*}_{\mu\nu}(Z,k)I^{\mu\nu}(\lambda,k)\frac{D^{4}k}{(2\pi)^{2}}.

(268)

The moment generating function automatically satisfies:

I^{\lambda\mu}k_{\lambda}=I^{\lambda\mu}_{0}k_{\lambda}-ik_{\kappa}k_{\lambda}I^{\kappa\lambda\mu}_{1}.

(269)

Dixon proved
dixon2
that these reduced moments are the unique set of moments which are independent of each other for different

and
I_{1}
restricted by the conservation law, and satisfy the index symmetry conditions in equations (

) and (
261
).

Through (

I_{n}^{\rho_{1}...\rho_{n}\mu\nu}=4n!\mathcal{Q}_{n-2}^{(\rho_{1}...\rho_{n-1}|\mu|\rho_{n})\nu}+\mathcal{O}(R)

(270)

which gives the reduced multipole moments from the couplings in the action. Alternatively, this can be nicely inverted using the index symmetry conditions of both quantities to find:

\mathcal{Q}_{n}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}\rho\mu\sigma\nu}=\frac{n+1}{(n+3)!}I_{n+2}^{\lambda_{1}...\lambda_{n}[\rho[^{\prime}\sigma\mu]\nu]^{\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(R)

(271)

7.2
Source of the Kerr Metric

Here we summarize the analysis of Ref.
israel:1970
to identify the energy-momentum tensor which produces the Kerr metric. The Kerr metric has no intrinsic singularities away from
r=0
and everywhere away from
r=0
it is a solution to the vacuum Einstein equations. Therefore, the source of the Kerr metric can only have support on the surface
r=0
. This surface is a disk and we introduce the same coordinate
\chi=\theta
above the disk and
\chi=\pi-\theta
below the disk as we did for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
so that
t,\chi,\varphi
provide an intrinsic coordinate system. Restricting the Kerr solution to the surface
r=0
produces the flat metric
\gamma_{ij}
on the disk:

\displaystyle\gamma_{tt}=-1,

\displaystyle\gamma_{t\chi}=0,

\displaystyle\gamma_{t\varphi}=0,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\chi\chi}=a^{2}\cos^{2}\chi,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\chi\varphi}=0,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\varphi\varphi}=a^{2}\sin^{2}\chi.

(272)

The extrinsic curvature
K_{ij}
of the disk, when approached from above toward
r=0
, is determined by the first
r
derivative of the Kerr solution and produces:

\displaystyle K_{tt}=\frac{Gm}{a^{2}\cos^{3}\chi},

\displaystyle K_{t\chi}=0,

\displaystyle K_{t\varphi}=-\frac{Gm}{a}\frac{\sin^{2}\chi}{\cos^{3}\chi},

\displaystyle K_{\chi\chi}=0,

\displaystyle K_{\chi\varphi}=0,

\displaystyle K_{\varphi\varphi}=Gm\frac{\sin^{4}\chi}{\cos^{3}\chi}.

(273)

The extrinsic curvature tensor of the disk when approached from below is simply the negative of the extrinsic curvature when approached from above. We may now use Israel’s junction conditions to determine the surface energy-momentum tensor on the disk
\text{S}^{\text{disk}}_{ij}
:

\text{S}^{\text{disk}}_{ij}=-\frac{1}{8\pi G}\left.\left(K_{ij}-K\gamma_{ij}\right)\right|^{\text{above}}_{\text{below}}.

(274)

The resulting surface energy-momentum tensor is:

\text{S}^{\text{disk}}_{ij}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{disk}}}{2}(\zeta_{i}\zeta_{j}+\xi_{i}\xi_{j})

(275)

where:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{disk}}=-\frac{m}{2\pi a^{2}\cos^{3}\chi}

(276)

\displaystyle\zeta_{t}=0,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\chi}=a\cos^{2}\chi,

\displaystyle\zeta_{\varphi}=0

(277)

\displaystyle\xi_{t}=-\sin\chi,

\displaystyle\xi_{\chi}=0,

\displaystyle\xi_{\varphi}=a\sin\chi.

(278)

As a distribution the disk energy-momentum tensor is:

T^{\text{disk}}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{disk}}}{2}(\zeta_{\mu}\zeta_{\nu}+\xi_{\mu}\xi_{\nu})\delta(r\cos\chi).

(279)

Taking the worldline which passes through the center of the disk as the worldline of the metric, we can use equations (
263
) and (
264
) to compute the total momentum and spin of the Kerr solution. With only the given surface energy-momentum tensor, the resultant linear momentum and spin are not
mu^{\mu}
and
m\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u^{\rho}a^{\sigma}
. Instead, the integrals diverge as
\chi\to\frac{\pi}{2}
in precisely the same way as occurred for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
. In order to produce the correct total momentum and spin it is necessary to have a linear energy-momentum tensor density on the ring singularity at
r=0,\chi=\frac{\pi}{2}
. In particular, the necessary effective mass density is:

\rho=-\frac{m}{2\pi a^{2}\cos^{4}\chi}\delta(r)\vartheta\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\varepsilon-\chi\right)+\frac{m}{2\pi a^{2}\sin\varepsilon\cos^{2}\chi}\delta(r)\delta\left(\chi-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

(280)

which is the same density as the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
solution. The resulting energy-momentum tensor is:

T_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\rho}{2}(\zeta_{\mu}\zeta_{\nu}+\xi_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}).

(281)

With this energy-momentum tensor using (
263
) and (
264

p^{\mu}=mu^{\mu},\hskip 21.52771ptS^{\mu}=ma^{\mu}.

(282)

8.1
Formal Classical Compton

To write Einstein’s equations explicitly for the metric perturbation, it is useful to introduce a shorthand for the volume form Jacobian:

V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}.

(301)

and to define the inverse metric tensor density
\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}
:

\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\nu}\sqrt{-\det g}.

(302)

Then, derivatives of the metric can be expressed as:

\partial_{\rho}g^{\mu\nu}=V\left(\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha\beta}\right),\hskip 21.52771pt\partial_{\rho}V=-\frac{V^{2}}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}.

(303)

With these definitions, we find that:

\displaystyle\frac{2}{V^{2}}\left(R^{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}Rg^{\mu\nu}\right)=

\displaystyle\ \mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}\partial^{2}_{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}+\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}\partial^{2}_{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}-\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\sigma}\partial^{2}_{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\nu}-\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\sigma}\partial^{2}_{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\mu}

\displaystyle\ -\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\rho}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\sigma}+\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}-g_{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\beta}

\displaystyle\ +g^{\mu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}+g^{\nu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\ -\frac{1}{8}(2g^{\mu\tau}g^{\nu\omega}-g^{\mu\nu}g^{\tau\omega})(2g_{\alpha\rho}g_{\beta\sigma}-g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\rho\sigma})\partial_{\tau}\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\omega}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}.

(304)

This expression is true in any coordinates. Going forward we will only use de Donder gauge, defined so that the coordinates are harmonic functions when viewed as scalars:

\nabla^{2}x^{\mu}=-g^{\alpha\beta}\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}=V\partial_{\nu}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}\overset{!}{=}0.

(305)

Using this gauge, Einstein’s equations can be written exactly in Landau-Lifshitz form as:

\displaystyle-\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}\partial^{2}_{\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}=

\displaystyle\ -16\pi GT^{\mu\nu}|\det\mathfrak{g}|-\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\rho}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\sigma}-g_{\alpha\beta}g^{\rho\sigma}\partial_{\rho}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\beta}

\displaystyle\ +g^{\mu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\nu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}+g^{\nu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\alpha\rho}\partial_{\sigma}\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle\ -\frac{1}{8}(2g^{\mu\tau}g^{\nu\omega}-g^{\mu\nu}g^{\tau\omega})(2g_{\alpha\rho}g_{\beta\sigma}-g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\rho\sigma})\partial_{\tau}\mathfrak{g}^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\omega}\mathfrak{g}^{\rho\sigma}

(306)

For studying gravitational waves we perturb about Minkowski space:

\mathfrak{g}^{\mu\nu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}+\kappa h^{\mu\nu},\hskip 21.52771pt\kappa=\sqrt{32\pi G}.

(307)

For this choice of coupling constant, the Einstein-Hilbert action is canonically normalized as a functional of the perturbation

\displaystyle\ \ \ \left.+g^{\mu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}h^{\nu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}h^{\rho\sigma}+g^{\nu\beta}g_{\rho\alpha}\partial_{\sigma}h^{\mu\alpha}\partial_{\beta}h^{\rho\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}g_{\alpha\rho}\partial_{\sigma}h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\beta}h^{\rho\sigma}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \left.\vphantom{\frac{T^{\mu\nu}}{2}}-\frac{1}{8}(2g^{\mu\tau}g^{\nu\omega}-g^{\mu\nu}g^{\tau\omega})(2g_{\alpha\rho}g_{\beta\sigma}-g_{\alpha\beta}g_{\rho\sigma})\partial_{\tau}h^{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\omega}h^{\rho\sigma}\right)

From Einstein’s equations, the
\kappa^{1}

\displaystyle-\partial^{2}h^{\mu\nu}_{(1)}=

9
Conclusion

Using the dynamical mass function worldline formalism, we derived formal expressions for the electromagnetic/gravitational Compton amplitudes of a generic spinning body to all orders in spin, with precise parameterized expressions in terms of Wilson coefficients for the amplitudes to order
S^{3}
in electromagnetism and
S^{5}
in gravity for bodies which match the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
/Kerr three-point amplitude. In electromagnetism we found 1 Wilson coefficient and 1 independent structure in the Compton at
S^{1}
, 5 new Wilson coefficients and 5 independent structures at
S^{2}
, and 8 new Wilson coefficients but only 7 independent structures at
S^{3}
. In gravity we found 4 Wilson coefficients and 1 independent structure in the Compton at
S^{2}
, 2 new Wilson coefficients and 1 independent structure at
S^{3}
, 18 new Wilson coefficients and 7 independent structures at
S^{4}
, and 16 new Wilson coefficients and 11 independent structures at
S^{5}
. As well, one of the Wilson coefficients on an
S^{4}
operator in gravity (
E_{4a}
) does not contribute to
S^{4}
piece of the Compton but does contribute to the
S^{5}
piece (whereas the other operator contributions which do not contribute at the order they are introduced actually do not contribute at all through
S^{5}
).

Dixon’s multipole moment formalism provides additional physical constraints on the dynamical mass function beyond those required by naive multipole moments which are determined by the three-point amplitude. Many of the additional terms induced by Dixon’s formalism (the operators with
E
coefficients), especially at low orders in spin, happen to not affect the Compton amplitude. However, at sufficiently high orders in spin (
S^{5}
and beyond in gravity) at least some of these additional terms do contribute to the Compton amplitude. Through
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
these combinations are linearly redundant in the Compton amplitude to contributions from Riemann squared operators. It would be interesting to see if these additional coefficients begin to contribute in linearly independent ways in the Compton at higher orders in spin or in higher-point processes, such as the five-point amplitude (with three graviton lines).

In electromagnetism, using Dixon’s multipole moments for
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
determines the dynamical mass function to be given by (
133
) with no room for additional operators which are linear in the field strength. Because the stationary
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
solution only determines its multipole moments up to corrections which are linear in the field strength, they only determine the couplings in the action up to corrections which are quadratic in the field strength. Without some additional physical principle which specifies how the multipole moments of the
\sqrt{\text{Kerr}}
particle deform in the presence of a background field (which would determine its electromagnetic susceptibility tensors), it is not possible to determine the quadratic in field strength couplings using the multipole moment formalism. The couplings in (
133
) contain all of the couplings of (
136
) (in that the coefficients of operators which are present in both agree). The story is very similar in gravity. Dixon’s multipole moment formalism applied to the Kerr solution determines the dynamical mass function to be given by (
295
) with no room for additional operators which are linear in the Riemann tensor. Because the stationary Kerr solution only determines its multipole moments up to corrections which are linear in the Riemann tensor, they only determine the couplings in the action up to corrections which are quaratic in the Riemann tensor. Without some further knowledge of the gravitational susceptibility tensors of a spinning black hole, it is not possible to determine the quadratic in Riemann couplings using the multipole moment formalism. The couplings in (
295
) contain all of the couplings of (
300
).

It is uncertain what precise physical principles determine the correction couplings in the action for a spinning black hole in general. Through
\mathcal{O}(S^{4})
, spin-exponentiation, shift-symmetry, the results of the Teukolsky equation, and the results from the multipole moment formalism can all be maintained simultaneously by appropriately choosing the values of Wilson coefficients for quadratic in Riemann tensor operators. However, beginning at
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
these different principles cannot all be maintained. Spin-exponentiation is only possible to maintain for one of the two independent helicity combinations and is consistent with shift-symmetry at
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
. However, shift-symmetry and the Teukolsky results are inconsistent with each other at
\mathcal{O}(S^{5})
. The couplings fixed by the multipole moment formalism are the unique values for the couplings so that the stress tensor they produce behaves correctly against test functions (meaning satisfies (
268
