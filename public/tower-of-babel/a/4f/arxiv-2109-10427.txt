1.
Introduction

The motivation for this paper comes from the striking work

[
7
]
of Candelas, de la Ossa, Green
and Parkes in the study of mirror symmetry of quintic threefolds from
1991. The story has been told many times, so we will give only a very brief description.
For more
details we like to refer to Duco van Straten’s excellent
[
17
]
and the many
references therein. Our short story starts with the differential operator

L=\theta^{4}-5t(5\theta+1)(5\theta+2)(5\theta+3)(5\theta+4),

where
\theta
denotes
t\frac{d}{dt}
.
The unique holomorphic solution to
L(y)=0

is of hypergeometric type and given by

F_{0}:=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(5n)!}{(n!)^{5}}t^{n}.

The equation
L(y)=0
has a unique basis of solutions of the form

y_{0}=F_{0},\ y_{1}=F_{0}\log t+F_{1},\ y_{2}=\frac{1}{2}F_{0}\log^{2}t+F_{1}\log t+F_{2},

y_{3}=\frac{1}{6}F_{0}\log^{3}t+\frac{1}{2}F_{1}\log^{2}t+F_{2}\log t+F_{3},

where
F_{0}
is given above and
F_{1},F_{2},F_{3}\in t{\mathbb{Q}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Straightforward computation shows
that the coefficients of
F_{1}
are certainly not integral. The surprise is that

q(t):=t\exp(F_{1}/F_{0})
, expanded as power series in
t
, does
have integer coefficients. The function
q(t)
is called the

canonical coordinate
. The inverse power series
t(q)

is called the
mirror map
.
Using this inverse series one can rewrite the solutions
y_{i}
as functions of
q
.
In particular,

y_{1}/y_{0}=\log q,\quad y_{2}/y_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\log^{2}q+V(q)

for some
V(q)\in q{\mathbb{Q}}\llbracket q\rrbracket
.
Let
\theta_{q}=q\frac{d}{dq}
. Then
K(q):=1+\theta_{q}^{2}V

is called the
Yukawa coupling
for reasons coming from theoretical physics.
In
[
7
]
the Yukawa coupling is expanded as a so-called Lambert series in the form

K(q)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{A_{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

Candelas et al conjectured that the numbers
a_{n}=5A_{n}/n^{3}
are integers which are
equal to the (virtual) number of degree
n
rational curves on a generic quintic threefold in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.
This counting relation is now a part of the mirror symmetry theory
from theoretical physics. The numbers
a_{n}
are called
instanton numbers
.
In this paper we shall concentrate on their integrality.

It turns out that there are many other examples of differential equations, similar to
the example above, which display integrality properties of the associated instanton numbers.
Motivated by this, Almkvist, van Enckevort, van Straten and Zudilin
[
1
]

compiled an extensive list of so-called Calabi-Yau operators. For each entry in the list
the authors give experimental evidence that the instanton numbers are
p
-adically integral
for almost all primes
p
, with some obvious exceptions. The quintic example that we started
with belongs to this list. Presumably, each equation in the list is a so-called Picard-Fuchs
equation corresponding to a one parameter family of Calabi-Yau threefolds.

Around 1995 Giventhal
[
9
]
showed that instanton numbers can be related to
the geometrical Gromov-Witten invariants of Calabi-Yau threefolds. However, the
integrality of these invariants is not a priori clear. Around 1998 Gopakumar and Vafa
introduced the so-called BPS-numbers for Calabi-Yau threefolds, which include the instanton numbers
as
g=0
case, see
[
6
, Def 1.1]
.
In 2018 Ionel and Parker
[
10
]
proved that these BPS-numbers
are integers by using methods from symplectic topology. So this opens up a path towards
proving integrality of instanton numbers.

Another approach to the integrality of instanton numbers was the use of Dwork’s
ideas in
p
-adic cohomology. This was started by Jan Stienstra
[
16
]
in 2003 with
partial success and later Kontsevich, Schwarz and Vologodsky
[
12
]
,

[
18
]
laid out the ideas for a completely general approach. However, since
[
18
]
is not
published yet, it is not clear to us what the status of the final results is.

One of the main goals of the present paper is to show by
elementary means that for the quintic example and a few
related examples the
a_{n}
are
p
-adic integers for almost all
p
. The method
showed up as a by-product of our prior work in the paper Dwork crystals III,

[
4
]
, which is a continuation of the preceding papers Dwork crystals I,II
(
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
).

We begin by sketching an outline of our approach. To study
p
-integrality
we let
p
be an odd prime.
Let
\sigma:{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
be a
p
-th power Frobenius lift, which
is a ring endomorphism such that
h(t)^{\sigma}:=\sigma(h(t))\equiv h(t^{p})({\rm mod}\ p)

for all
h\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.
In this paper we shall take it to be of the form
t^{\sigma}=t^{p}(1+ptu(t))
with

u(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.
We start with a linear differential equation
L(y)=0
where

L=\theta^{n}+a_{n-1}(t)\theta^{r-1}+\cdots+a_{1}(t)\theta+a_{0}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\theta],\quad\theta=t\frac{d}{dt}.

We also assume that it is of MUM-type (maximal unipotent monodromy) at the origin,
i.e
a_{i}(0)=0
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. The main examples of such equations are the Picard-Fuchs
equations which are associated to certain one parameter families of algebraic varieties.
We shall give a more precise definition later on.
For a MUM-type equation there is a unique basis of local solutions

y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-1}
of the form

(1)

y_{i}=F_{0}\frac{\log^{i}t}{i!}+F_{1}\frac{\log^{i-1}t}{(i-1)!}+\cdots+F_{i-1}\log t+F_{i},\ i=0,\ldots,n-1,

and
F_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
F_{0}(0)=1
and
F_{i}(0)=0
if
i>0
.
We extend the Frobenius lift
\sigma
to the solutions
y_{i}^{\sigma}
in the obvious
way, i.e. by taking
F_{i}^{\sigma}=F_{i}(t^{\sigma})
and

\log t^{\sigma}=p\log t-\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{m}(-p\,t\,u(t))^{m}.

A familiar property of Picard-Fuchs equations is the presence of a Frobenius
structure for almost all
p
. We give a closely related concrete definition in terms of
its solutions.

Definition 1.1

(Frobenius structure)
.

The equation
L(y)=0
is said to have a

p
-adic Frobenius structure if there exists an operator

\mathcal{A}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}A_{i}(t)\theta^{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\theta]

with
A_{0}(0)=1
such that for every solution
y(t)
of
L(y)=0
the
composition
\mathcal{A}(y(t^{\sigma}))
is again a solution of this differential equation.

In this paper we will call the numbers
\alpha_{i}=A_{i}(0)
the Frobenius constants.

Remark 1.2
.

In the standard basis of solutions (
1
) the operator
\mathcal{A}
acts by the formula

\mathcal{A}(y_{i}(t^{\sigma}))=p^{i}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\alpha_{j}\,y_{i-j}(t),\quad i=0,\ldots,n-1.

This will be shown in Section
2
.

Remark 1.3
.

Our definition of Frobenius structure is adapted to the integrality questions considered in this paper.
First of all, a
Frobenius structure is usually defined over a ring
R
, which is a (sub)ring of so-called
analytic elements. These are
p
-adic limits of rational functions. Granted that we take a Frobenius
structure over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
, this would give us
p^{i}A_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. This is weaker
than
A_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
, which is in our definition. The latter condition is crucial in our paper.
We believe that this condition holds in families of Calabi-Yau varieties which possess sufficiently many symmetries.

Recall the canonical coordinate
q(t)=t\exp(F_{1}/F_{0})
and the inverse
series
t(q)
which we called the mirror map.
It is expected that for Picard-Fuchs equations of
MUM-type these series are in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
for all but finitely
many primes
p
. So far this has been shown for a
large number of special cases, e.g
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
8
]
. In
Section
2
we prove the following.

Theorem 1.4

(
p
-integrality of the mirror map)
.

Suppose that
L(y)=0
has a
p
-adic Frobenius structure.
Then
\exp(F_{1}/F_{0})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Let us now suppose that
\exp(F_{1}/F_{0})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and
n\geq 3
.
Take
q=t\exp(F_{1}/F_{0})\in t+t^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.
We choose the special Frobenius lift given by
q^{\sigma}=q^{p}
, called the
excellent lift

in
[
4
]
.
Define
\theta_{q}=q\frac{d}{dq}
. Then the operator
\theta_{q}^{2}
annihilates the functions

y_{1}/y_{0}=\log q
and
y_{0}/y_{0}=1
.

Definition 1.5
.

The function
K(q)=\theta_{q}^{2}(y_{2}/y_{0})
is called the Yukawa coupling associated to
the operator
L
. It is equal to
1+\theta_{q}^{2}V
, where we consider

V=F_{2}/F_{0}-\frac{1}{2}(F_{1}/F_{0})^{2}
as function of
q
.

As in the quintic example we expand

K(q)=1+\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{A_{r}q^{r}}{1-q^{r}}.

In the following theorem we require that the Frobenius constant
\alpha_{1}
is
0
.
In general the Frobenius constants
\alpha_{i}
are notoriously difficult to compute, but
fortunately we need only
\alpha_{1}
. This is dealt with in the proof of Corollary

1.9
, which in its turn uses
[
4
, Proposition 7.7]
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Suppose that
L(y)=0
has a
p
-adic Frobenius structure with
\alpha_{1}=0
.
Then
A_{r}/r^{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for all
r\geq 1
.

The
p
-integrality of
A_{r}/r^{3}
follows if the differential equation
has order
4
and is self-dual. This is precisely the type of operators that is studied
in
[
1
]
and which are named Calabi-Yau operators.
There we find that an operator
L=\theta^{4}+a_{3}\theta^{3}+a_{2}\theta^{2}+a_{1}\theta+a_{0}
is called self-dual if

a_{1}=\frac{1}{2}a_{2}a_{3}-\frac{1}{8}a_{3}^{3}+\theta(a_{2})-\frac{3}{4}a_{3}\theta(a_{3})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{2}(a_{3}).

There are more intrinsic characterizations of self-duality but what matters for us is that
in the case of self-duality we have

\left|\begin{matrix}y_{0}&y_{3}\\
\theta y_{0}&\theta y_{3}\end{matrix}\right|=\left|\begin{matrix}y_{1}&y_{2}\\
\theta y_{1}&\theta y_{2}\end{matrix}\right|.

Theorem 1.7

(
p
-integrality of instanton numbers)
.

Suppose that the operator
L
of order
n=4
is self-dual and the differential equation
L(y)=0
has a

p
-adic Frobenius structure
with
\alpha_{1}=0
. Then
A_{r}/r^{3}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for all
n\geq 1
.

We remark that the theorem is sensitive to the normalization of
t
.
In other words, if we replace
t
by
2t
for example,
the theorem is not true anymore. The value
\alpha_{1}
has become non-zero. It is worth
mentioning that the instanton numbers and the Frobenius structure behave nicely
under the change of variables
t\to t^{w}
. One can easily check that the new Yukawa coupling is
given by
K(q^{w})
and the new constants
\alpha_{i}
of the Frobenius structure are given by

\alpha^{i}/w^{i}
,
i=0,1,\ldots

Theorems
1.4
,
1.6
and
1.7
are proven in Section

4
.

Let us now sketch the set up which we use to construct the Frobenius structure for differential operators. We start with a Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})
in
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
.
Let
\Delta
be the Newton polytope of
f
and
\Delta^{\circ}
its interior.
Let
R
be a ring containing
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
.
By
\Omega_{f}
we denote the
R
-module generated by the functions

(k-1)!A/f^{k}
, where
A
is a Laurent polynomial with coefficients in
R

and support in
k\Delta
and
k\geq 1
.
We define
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}
similarly, consisting of rational
functions as above, but with support of
A
in
k\Delta^{\circ}
.
In the notation of Dwork crystals I,II we use
\Omega_{f}:=\Omega_{f}(\Delta)

and
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}:=\Omega_{f}(\Delta^{\circ})
.
By
d\Omega_{f}
we define the module spanned by the partial derivatives of the
form
x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(\omega)
with
\omega\in\Omega_{f}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
.
We will denote the partial derivations
x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}
by
\theta_{i}
.

When
f
has suitable regularity properties, there exists a polynomial

D_{f}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
such that the quotient
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}/d\Omega_{f}

is a free module over
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}]
of finite rank.
It can roughly be identified with the
(n-1)
-st De Rham cohomology of
the zero set of
f
(although we shall not use this).

The derivation
\theta=t\frac{d}{dt}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
can be extended to

\Omega_{f}
in the obvious way. On easily checks that
\theta
sends
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}

to itself as well as
d\Omega_{f}
.
Hence
\theta
maps
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}/d\Omega_{f}
to itself.
This gives us the so-called Gauss-Manin connection on
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}/d\Omega_{f}
.

Our basic example is

f=1-t\left(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+\frac{1}{x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}}\right).

Then
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}/d\Omega_{f}
is a free
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/((5t)^{6}-5t)]
-module of
rank 4 with a basis given by
\theta^{i}(1/f)
with
i=0,1,2,3
.
Clearly
\theta^{4}(1/f)
depends on these and the
relation is given by
L(1/f)\in d\Omega_{f}
, where
L
is the linear differential
operator

L=\theta^{4}-(5t)^{5}(\theta+1)(\theta+2)(\theta+3)(\theta+4).

This operator is related to
the hypergeometric operator with which we began our introduction via the change of variable

t\to t^{5}
. We call it the
quintic example
.

Our second main example illustrates the use of symmetries of
f
.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite group of monomial substitutions under which
f
is invariant.
A monomial substitution has the form
x_{i}\to{\bf x}^{{\bf a}_{i}},i=1,\ldots,n

where
{\bf a}_{i}
are vectors of integers which form a basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
We denote the submodule of invariant rational functions by
(\Omega_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}
.
Consider

f=1-t\left(x_{1}+\frac{1}{x_{1}}+x_{2}+\frac{1}{x_{2}}+x_{3}+\frac{1}{x_{3}}+x_{4}+\frac{1}{x_{4}}\right).

It turns out that
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}/d\Omega_{f}
has rank
10
as module over

{\mathbb{Z}}[t,(2t(1-80t^{2}+1024t^{4}))^{-1}]
. However,
f
is invariant
under the group
\mathcal{G}
of monomial transformations generated by the permutations of
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}

and
x_{i}\to x_{i}^{\pm 1}
for all
i
. We denote the
\mathcal{G}
-invariant elements of
\Omega_{f}^{\circ}

by
(\Omega_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}
. The quotient module

(\Omega_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\Omega_{f}
turns out to have rank
4
and is generated by

\theta^{i}(1/f)
for
i=0,1,2,3
.
Then
L(1/f)\in d\Omega_{f}
with

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1024t^{4}-80t^{2}+1)\theta^{4}+64(128t^{4}-5t^{2})\theta^{3}

\displaystyle+16(1472t^{4}-33t^{2})\theta^{2}+32(896t^{4}-13t^{2})\theta+128(96t^{4}-t^{2}).

This is #16 in the database of Calabi-Yau equations
[
1
]
with
z=t^{2}
.
We call it the
diagonal example
.

Prompted by these examples we now restrict the Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})
to
a polynomial of the form
f=1-tg({\bf x})
, where
g
is a Laurent polynomial
in the variables
{\bf x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We also assume that its Newton polytope
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is
reflexive
.

We remind the reader that a lattice polytope
\Delta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

is reflexive if it is of maximal dimension, contains
{\bf 0}
and each of its
codimension 1 faces can be given by an equation
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=1

with coefficients
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. It follows from this definition that

{\bf 0}
is the unique lattice point in
\Delta^{\circ}
. Reflexivity is a
requirement needed for the
p
-adic considerations later on.

Let us remark that in Dwork crystals II
[
3
]
we prove congruences of
Dwork type for the power series
y_{0}(t)=\sum_{m\geq 0}g_{m}t^{m}
, with

g_{m}=\text{constant term of }g({\bf x})^{m}
,
which are associated to
\Omega_{f}
for these particular
f
. The above relation

L(1/f)\in d\Omega_{f}
implies that
y_{0}(t)
is a power series solution to the
differential equation
L(y)=0
. This follows from the properties of the period map
constructed in
[
3
, §2]
. See also the explicit families in

[
4
, §7]
.

Another property of reflexive polytopes which we will use is that to every
lattice point
{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
there is a unique integer
d\geq 0
such that
{\bf u}

lies on the boundary of
d\Delta
. We call this integer
d
the degree of both
{\bf u}

and its respective monomial
{\bf x}^{\bf u}
. For a Laurent polynomial

A\in R[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
its degree
\deg(A)
is the maximum degree
of the monomials it contains. Suppose that
R
has the form
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
as above with
D_{f}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]

and
D_{f}(0)\neq 0
. A Laurent polynomial

A(x)=\sum_{{\bf u}}a_{{\bf u}}{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}
with coefficients in

R
is called
admissible
if for every monomial
{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}

in
A
the
t
-adic order of its coefficient
a_{{\bf u}}
is greater or
equal to
\deg({\bf x}^{{\bf u}})
. For example,
f({\bf x})=1-tg({\bf x})
is admissible.

Throughout the paper we will work with the submodule

\mathcal{O}_{f}=\left\{(k-1)!\frac{A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{k}}\;\Big|\;k\geq 1,\,A\text{ is admissible},\,Supp(A)\subset k\Delta\right\}\subset\Omega_{f}.

We will call elements of
\mathcal{O}_{f}
admissible rational functions.
The submodule
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}\subset\mathcal{O}_{f}
is defined by the stronger condition

Supp(A)\subset k\Delta^{\circ}
. The submodule of derivatives

d\mathcal{O}_{f}\subset\mathcal{O}_{f}
is the
R
-module generated by

x_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}(\omega)
for
\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{f}
and
i=1,\ldots,n
.

The main motivation for choosing
\mathcal{O}_{f}
is that in our examples the quotient module

\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is of finite rank over the ring
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
for some
D_{f}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]

with
D_{f}(0)\neq 0
. So when
p
does not divide
D_{f}(0)
, this ring can be embedded in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
,
which is convenient for our
p
-adic considerations.

Letting
\mathcal{G}
be a group of monomial automorphisms of
f({\bf x})
,
we denote the submodule of
\mathcal{G}
-invariant admissible rational functions
by
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}
.
It is not hard to verify that
\gamma\circ\theta=\theta\circ\gamma
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{G}
.
In particular this implies that
\theta
maps
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
to itself.
We now assume that

M:=(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is free of finite rank
n
and is generated by the
elements
\theta^{i}(1/f)
for
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
. We actually need more refined
assumptions to be satisfied, in which case we call
M
a

cyclic
\theta
-module of MUM-type
. See Definition
4.1
for the precise formulation.
Just as in the above two examples we can associate to a cyclic
\theta
-module of
MUM-type an
n
-th order linear differential operator
L
of MUM-type such that
L(1/f)\in d\mathcal{O}_{f}
. We
call
L(y)=0
the
Picard-Fuchs differential equation
associated to
M
. These equations
are precisely the type of differential equations that we want to consider.

To introduce the Frobenius structure on the Picard-Fuchs equation we choose
an odd prime
p
and assume that
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
can be embedded in

{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Let
\sigma
be a Frobenius lift of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
satisfying the assumption

t^{\sigma}\in t^{p}(1+pt{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket)
which was introduced earlier. We recall the Cartier operator

\mathscr{C}_{p}
which was introduced
in Dwork crystals I,
[
2
]
. Using a vertex

{\bf b}
of
\Delta
we can expand any element of
\mathcal{O}_{f}
as a formal Laurent
series with support in the cone spanned by
\Delta-{\bf b}
. On such Laurent series
the Cartier operator acts as

(2)

\mathscr{C}_{p}:\sum_{{\bf k}\in C(\Delta-{\bf b})}a_{{\bf k}}{\bf x}^{{\bf k}}\mapsto\sum_{{\bf k}\in C(\Delta-{\bf b})}a_{p{\bf k}}{\bf x}^{{\bf k}}.

We extend
\sigma
to
\mathcal{O}_{f}
by applying it to the coefficients of rational functions.
For example,
f^{\sigma}({\bf x})=1-t^{\sigma}g({\bf x})
.
Let
\mathcal{O}_{f^{\sigma}}
be the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-module generated by the

\sigma
-images of the elements of
\mathcal{O}_{f}
.
The numerators of these images are admissible with respect to
t^{\sigma}
instead of
t
.
Then, in
[
4
, Proposition 2.7]

it is shown that
\mathscr{C}_{p}
maps
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}^{\circ}
,
where
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ}
denotes the
p
-adic completion of
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}
and similarly for

\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}^{\circ}
. Moreover, when

p\nmid\#\mathcal{G}

our Cartier operator maps
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric functions
(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}
into
(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}
. This can be seen by observing that the symmetrization
operator
\omega\mapsto\frac{1}{\#\mathcal{G}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}}\gamma(\omega)
commutes with
\mathscr{C}_{p}
.

One can easily deduce from (
2
) that
\mathscr{C}_{p}\circ\theta_{i}=p\,\theta_{i}\circ\mathscr{C}_{p}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, from which it follows that our Cartier operation maps derivatives
d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}
into
d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}
. Hence
\mathscr{C}_{p}
descends to a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-linear map

\mathscr{C}_{p}:(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}\to(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}.

When
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
are cyclic
\theta
-modules of rank
n
, this implies existence of power series
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
such that

\mathscr{C}_{p}(1/f)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(t)(\theta^{i}(1/f))^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}).

We shall explicitly construct a
p
-adic Frobenius structure
\mathcal{A}
for the Picard-Fuchs differential equation
L(y)=0
using the series
\lambda_{i}(t)
in this formula. It is crucial for our construction to know that the derivatives
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1
are linearly independent not just modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
but also modulo
d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}
. This would particularly imply that the coefficients
\lambda_{i}(t)
in the above formula are defined uniquely. Unfortunately, we were only able to show independence of derivatives modulo
d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}
under the so-called
n
-th Hasse–Witt condition, which is recalled in Section
3
.

Proposition 1.8
.

Suppose
f=1-tg({\bf x})
has a reflexive Newton polytope
\Delta
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a group of monomial
automorphisms of
g
. Suppose that
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is a cyclic
\theta
-module of MUM-type of
rank
n
.
Suppose
p\nmid\#\mathcal{G}\times D_{f}(0)
and
p>n
. Then, if the
n
-th Hasse–Witt condition holds,
the corresponding Picard-Fuchs equation
L(y)=0
has a
p
-adic Frobenius structure with
\alpha_{i}=p^{-i}\lambda_{i}(0)
,
0\leq i\leq n
.

We give a proof of this proposition in Section
4
.
In the literature one sees that existence of a
p
-adic Frobenius structure for Picard-Fuchs equations
is a well-known phenomenon. But as we said before, we need something a bit stronger in the form of
Definition
1.1
. We believe that families of Calabi-Yau
varieties with sufficient symmetry have a Frobenius structure that satisfies this definition,
but we are only able to give a few explicit examples.
Below we give two examples to which Proposition
1.8
applies.

Simplicial family.

Let

g({\bf x})=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}+\frac{1}{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}}.

We call the corresponding family of varieties
1-tg({\bf x})=0
the simplicial family, refering to the
shape of the Newton polytope. For
\mathcal{G}
we take the group generated by the permutations of

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
x_{1}\to(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})^{-1}
. It is isomorphic to
S_{n+1}
.
In Section
5
we show this family gives rise to a cyclic
\theta
-module
of rank
n
with
D_{f}(t)=(n+1)(1-((n+1)t)^{n+1})
.
The corresponding Picard-Fuchs operator reads

\theta^{n}-((n+1)t)^{n+1}(\theta+1)\ldots(\theta+n),

which is of hypergeometric origin and of MUM-type.
This family is related to the so-called Dwork families
X_{0}^{n+1}+\cdots+X_{n}^{n+1}=\frac{1}{t}X_{0}\cdots X_{n}
which one often finds in the literature. Simply replace
x_{i}
in the simplicial

for every

. The case

is of course the quintic family.

Hyperoctahedral family.

Let

g({\bf x})=x_{1}+\frac{1}{x_{1}}+x_{2}+\frac{1}{x_{2}}+\cdots+x_{n}+\frac{1}{x_{n}}.

The corresponding family of varieties
1-tg({\bf x})=0
is called the hyperoctahedral family,
also after the shape of its Newton polytope. For the group
\mathcal{G}
we take the group
generated by the permutations of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
x_{i}\to x_{i}^{\pm 1}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. In Section

6
we will show that there
exists a polynomial
D_{f}(t)
such that over the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
the module

is
generated by
\theta^{i}(1/f)
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. There we also construct a Picard-Fuchs operator

L
, which turns out to be of MUM-type. If

were irreducible in
{\mathbb{Q}}(t)[\theta]
then

would be a rank
n
cyclic
\theta
-module of MUM-type of rank
n
.
When

we recover the diagonal family introduced earlier.

In Section

we will show that the
n
-th Hasse–Witt condition holds for the simplicial and
hyperoctahedral families.
As a consequence of all this we find the following.

In the simplicial family the numbers

In the ’quintic example’ and the ’diagonal example’ above

We start with a classical lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(Dieudonné-Dwork lemma)
.

Let
g\in t{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Then
g(t)-\frac{1}{p}g(t^{\sigma})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
if and only if

\exp(g)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

For a proof see
[
4
, Lemma 7.10]
.
The following criterion allows us to decide
p
-integrality of the numbers
A_{r}/r^{2}
or
A_{r}/r^{3}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G(q)\in 1+q{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket q\rrbracket
. Consider its Lambert series expansion

G(q)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{h_{n}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}}.

Suppose there exists
s\geq 1
and
\phi\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket q\rrbracket

such that
G(q^{p})-G(q)=\theta_{q}^{s}\phi.

Then
h_{n}/n^{s}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Write
G(q)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}g_{n}q^{n}
. Then
G(q^{p})-G(q)=\theta_{q}^{s}\phi
implies
that
g_{n}\equiv g_{n/p}({\rm mod}\ n^{s}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
for all
n\geq 1
(use the convention
g_{n/p}=0

if
p
does not divide
n
). One has
g_{n}=\sum_{d|n}h_{d}
and its Möbius inversion reads

h_{n}=\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g_{n/d}
,
where
\mu(n)
is the Möbius function. Write

\displaystyle h_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g_{n/d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d|(n/p),d\not\equiv 0(p)}\mu(dp)g_{n/pd}+\mu(d)g_{n/d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{d|(n/p),d\not\equiv 0(p)}\mu(d)(g_{n/d}-g_{n/pd})

The latter sum is in
n^{s}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
because of the congruences for
g_{n}
.
∎

The following lemma gives a criterion to recognize elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
u(t)\in 1+t{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and suppose there exists a linear operator

\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\theta]
such that
\mathcal{A}(u^{\sigma})=u
.
Then
u\in 1+t{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{A}=\sum_{i\geq 0}A_{i}(t)\theta^{i}
.
Then, by setting
t=0
in
\mathcal{A}(u^{\sigma})=u
we see that
A_{0}(0)=1
.
Consider the recursion
u_{i+1}=\mathcal{A}(u_{i}^{\sigma}),i\geq 0
and
u_{0}=1
.
Since
A_{0}(0)=1
we get that
u_{i}\in 1+t{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
for all
i\geq 0
.

By induction on
i
we now prove that
u_{i}\equiv u({\rm mod}\ t^{p^{i}})
for all
i\geq 0
. For

i=0
this is trivial. For
i\geq 0
we see that
u_{i}\equiv u({\rm mod}\ t^{p^{i}})
implies

u_{i}^{\sigma}\equiv u^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ t^{p^{i+1}})
. Application of
\mathcal{A}
on both
sides gives
u_{i+1}\equiv u({\rm mod}\ t^{p^{i+1}})
. We conclude that
u\in 1+t{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.
∎

Proof of Remark
1.2
.

Choose
i
. Then
\mathcal{A}(y_{i}^{\sigma})
is a solution
of
L(y)=0
. So there exist constants
b_{0},\ldots,b_{n-1}
such that

(3)

\mathcal{A}(y_{i}^{\sigma})=b_{0}y_{0}+b_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+b_{n-1}y_{n-1}.

This is an equality in the ring
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\log t]
. When we say that we take
the constant term of an element of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\log t]
we simply mean that we evaluate
the
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-coefficients at
t=0

but keep de powers of
\log t
. This gives us a ring homomorphism from
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\log t]

to
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[\log t]
.
In particular the constant term of
y_{i}
is
\frac{1}{i!}\log^{i}t
and the
constant term of
y_{i}^{\sigma}
is
\frac{p^{i}}{i!}\log^{i}t
.
It is not hard to see that the operation of taking the constant terms commutes with
\theta
.
Hence if we take constant terms on both sides of (
3
)
we get

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}A_{j}(0)\theta^{j}\left(p^{i}\frac{\log^{i}t}{i!}\right)=\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}b_{l}\frac{\log^{l}t}{l!}.

Elaboration of the left hand side gives us

p^{i}\sum_{j=0}^{i}A_{j}(0)\frac{\log^{i-j}t}{(i-j)!}=\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}b_{l}\frac{\log^{l}t}{l!}.

We conclude that
b_{l}=p^{i}A_{i-l}(0)
if
l\leq i
and
b_{l}=0
if
l>i
. We put

\alpha_{i}=A_{i}(0)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Due to Remark
1.2

\mathcal{A}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\theta]

\mathcal{A}(y_{0}^{\sigma})=y_{0}

By Lemma
2.3

We also have that
\mathcal{A}(y_{1}^{\sigma})=p(y_{1}+\alpha_{1}y_{0})

Let us define

and rewrite

(4)

Divide on both sides by
y_{0}
and apply

Hence

Moreover,

This also implies

Using this in (

3.

We recall our setup which was introduced starting on page

.
We have a Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})=1-tg({\bf x})

with
g({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
and such that its Newton polytope

\mathscr{F}_{k}:=\{\omega\in\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}|\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}(\omega)\in p^{ks}\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma^{s}}}\ \text{for all $s\geq 1$}\}.

The main result in

and the Hasse–Witt determinants
hw^{(\ell)}

(7)

\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}(k)\oplus\mathscr{F}_{k},

where

is the

-module generated by the rational functions
t^{\deg({\bf u})}{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}/f({\bf x})^{k}

with
{\bf u}\in k\Delta
. Similarly we have
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f^{\sigma}}(k)\oplus\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\sigma}
,
where

\mathscr{F}_{k}^{\sigma}:=\{\omega\in\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}|\mathscr{C}_{p}^{s}(\omega)\in p^{ks}\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma^{s+1}}}\ \text{for all $s\geq 1$}\}.

For a set
S\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
we denote by
S_{\mathbb{Z}}
the set of integral points in
S
,
S_{\mathbb{Z}}=S\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
The Hasse–Witt determinants
hw^{(k)}
are defined as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
1\leq k<p
. The
k
th
Hasse–Witt matrix

HW^{(k)}
is the square matrix
indexed by
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}
, the
({\bf u},{\bf v})
-entry being the coefficient of

{\bf x}^{p{\bf v}-{\bf u}}
in the polynomial

(8)

F^{(k)}({\bf x})=f({\bf x})^{p-k}\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})-f({\bf x})^{p})^{r}f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})^{k-1-r}

divided by
t^{p\deg({\bf v})-\deg({\bf u})}
.

We note that the entries of
HW^{(k)}
are in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. This is because
F^{(k)}({\bf x})
is
admissible and
\deg(p{\bf v}-{\bf u})\geq p\deg({\bf v})-\deg({\bf u})
. We introduced these matrices
in
[
4
, §5]
. They arise naturally when one wants to decribe the Cartier action on
admissible rational functions modulo
p^{k}
. Namely, for any
{\bf u}\in(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}
one has

\mathscr{C}_{p}\frac{t^{\deg({\bf u})}{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{k}}=\sum_{{\bf v}\in(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}}HW^{(k)}_{{\bf u}{\bf v}}\;\frac{t^{p\deg({\bf v})}{\bf x}^{\bf v}}{f^{\sigma}({\bf x})^{k}}({\rm mod}\ p^{k}\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}),

see
[
4
, (11)]
. It follows from
[
4
, Proposition 5.7]
that
\det(HW^{(k)})

is divisble by
p^{L(k)}
where

L(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left(\#(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}-\#(\ell\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}\right)=\sum_{{\bf u}\in(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}}\left(\deg({\bf u})-1\right).

Definition 3.2
.

We say that the
k
-th Hasse–Witt condition holds when

(9)

hw^{(\ell)}:=p^{-L(\ell)}\det(HW^{(\ell}))\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\times}

for each
1\leq\ell\leq k
.

The present section is devoted to proving the following theorem. We say that a proper face
\tau

of
\Delta
is
a simplex of volume
1
if it has

\dim(\tau)+1
vertices and all lattice points in the
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
-cone generated by
\tau

are integer linear combinations of the vertex vectors.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
p>n
. If all proper faces of
\Delta
are simplices of volume 1 and
all coefficients of
g({\bf x})
are in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
then the
k
-th Hasse–Witt condition
holds for any
k<p
.

One easily verifies that the condition of the theorem is fullfilled by the simplicial
and octahedral Newton polytopes
\Delta
.
The proof will be given at the end of this section. We start with some preparations.
Note that for
{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
one has
\deg{\bf w}=\max_{\tau}\ell_{\tau}({\bf w})
, where
the maximum is taken over all codimension one faces
\tau\subset\Delta
and
\ell_{\tau}

is the linear functional which takes value
1
on
\tau
.
We will use the following properties of the degree function:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
{\bf w},{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For a face
\tau
of any dimension
we denote by
C(\tau)
the positive
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
-cone spanned by
\tau
. Then,

(a)

for any codimension 1 face
\tau
we have
\deg({\bf w})=l_{\tau}({\bf w})\Leftrightarrow{\bf w}\in C(\tau)

(b)

\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})\leq\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{1}

(c)

the condition
\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{1}
is equivalent to the fact that for all proper faces
\tau
one has:
{\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)\Rightarrow{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)
.

Proof.

(a) Let us assume
{\bf w}\neq 0
and set
\lambda=\deg{\bf w}
. It follows from the definition of
\deg
that
{\bf w}/\lambda\in\Delta
. Suppose that
\deg{\bf w}=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w})
. Then
1=\deg({\bf w}/\lambda)=\ell\tau({\bf w}/\lambda)
. Since
{\bf w}/\lambda\in\Delta
we see that it must lie in the face
\tau
.
Hence
{\bf w}\in\lambda\tau\subset C(\tau)
. Conversely, if
{\bf w}\in C(\tau)
, choose
\lambda^{\prime}
and
{\bf w}^{\prime}\in\tau
such that
{\bf w}=\lambda^{\prime}{\bf w}^{\prime}
. For all codimension one faces
\tau^{\prime}
we have
\ell_{\tau^{\prime}}({\bf w}^{\prime})\leq\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}^{\prime})=1
. After multiplication by
\lambda^{\prime}
we get
\ell_{\tau^{\prime}}({\bf w})\leq\ell_{\tau}({\bf w})=\lambda^{\prime}
. Hence
\deg{\bf w}=\lambda^{\prime}
.

(b) Choose
\tau
such that
\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})
. Then,

\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1})+\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{2})\leq\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{2}.

(c)“
\Rightarrow
” It suffices to prove the condition on the right side for all codimension one faces
\tau
. Choose
\tau
of codimension 1 such that
{\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)
. Then, by (a),
\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1})+\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{2})\leq\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{2}
. It is also given that
\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{1}
. Hence we must conclude that
\deg{\bf w}_{1}=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1})

and
\deg{\bf w}_{2}=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{2})
. Hence, again by (a),
{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)
.

“
\Leftarrow
” Take any codimension 1 face
\tau
such that
{\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)
. Then
{\bf w}_{1},{\bf w}_{2}\in C(\tau)
and it follows from (a) that
\deg({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1}+{\bf w}_{2})=\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{1})+\ell_{\tau}({\bf w}_{2})=\deg{\bf w}_{1}+\deg{\bf w}_{2}
.
∎

For a subset

the square submatrix of

indexed by

Proposition 3.5

With notations as above, one has

\tau
of

. By convention, when

Proof.

We show that the matrix

{\bf 0}
and the points in
kP(\tau^{\circ})
for all proper subfaces
\tau
of
\Delta
. We assume the indexes ordered according to increasing dimension of
\tau
, starting with

.

Choose
{\bf v},{\bf u}\in(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}

{\bf x}^{-{\bf u}}
in

In general, let

In the following lemma we use the assumption

Lemma 3.6

Let

From now on we will consider the situation when the proper subfaces of

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\tau
be a simplicial face of volume

Then the set
(kP(\tau^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}
is empty when

{\bf u},{\bf v}\in(kP(\tau^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}

{\bf x}^{p{\bf v}-{\bf u}}
in the polynomial

given by formula (

equals

\sum_{{\bf u}\in(kP(\tau^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}}\left(\deg({\bf u})-1\right).

Proof.

Let us introduce the coordinates
y_{i}={\bf x}^{{\bf v}_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,s
.
Since
{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{s}

are generators of a volume 1 simplex, every
{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}
with
{\bf w}\in C(\tau^{\circ})
can be written as a
a monomial in
y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}
(with exponents
>0
). Thus we see that
we can restrict ourselves to a polynomial
\tilde{h}({\bf y})=1-h_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-h_{s}y_{s}
. Then

F^{(k)}_{\tilde{h}({\bf y})}=F^{(k)}_{h({\bf x})}
. Furthermore, the degree
of the monomial
{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}
is now simply the usual total degree of the corresponding monomial in

y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}
. The monomials
{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}\in(kP(\tau^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}
simply become the monomials

{\bf y}^{{\bf w}}
with
{\bf w}\in Q_{k}
where
Q_{k}:=\{{\bf w}|w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}>0,w_{1}+\cdots+w_{s}\leq k\}
.
In particular
Q_{k}
is empty

, so we see that

H_{\tilde{h}({\bf y})}^{(k)}=1

Now assume that
k\geq s
. Observe that

F^{(k)}_{\tilde{h}({\bf y})}=\frac{(\tilde{h}({\bf y}^{p})-\tilde{h}({\bf y})^{p})^{k}-\tilde{h}({\bf y}^{p})^{k}}{-\tilde{h}({\bf y})^{k}}\equiv\frac{\tilde{h}({\bf y}^{p})^{k}}{\tilde{h}({\bf y})^{k}}\equiv\frac{(1-h_{1}y_{1}^{p}-\cdots-h_{s}y_{s}^{p})^{k}}{(1-h_{1}y_{1}-\cdots-h_{s}y_{s})^{k}}({\rm mod}\ p^{k}).

Expand the right hand side as power series in

.

(11)

\frac{\tilde{h}({\bf y}^{p})^{k}}{\tilde{h}({\bf y})^{k}}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}(h_{1}y_{1}^{p}+\cdots+h_{s}y_{s}^{p})^{j}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{i+k-1}{k-1}(h_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+h_{s}y_{s})^{i}.

Let
H^{\prime}\equiv H({\rm mod}\ p^{k})
be the matrix with entries

H^{\prime}_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}=\text{coefficient of ${\bf y}^{p{\bf u}-{\bf v}}$ in \eqref{HW-polynomial-cone-y}},

where
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in Q_{k}

In what follows we will compute the Smith normal form of

. To every

M
with coeffcients in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
there exist matrices

. The entries

are uniquely determined and known as elementary divisors of

Let us make an observation. Suppose that the elementary divisors of

satisfy

for all

and

. Then the

-adic order of

equals

. To prove this fact consider

for all

{\rm diag}(p^{k_{1}},\ldots,p^{k_{s}})^{-1}SHT=Id+O(p)
and take the determinant on both sides.

We will show that the elementary divisors of
H^{\prime}
are given by

(12)

p^{|{\bf u}|-1},\qquad{\bf u}\in Q_{k},

where
|{\bf u}|=u_{1}+\cdots+u_{s}
.
The statement of the proposition then follows from this fact due to the observation made above.

Consider
{\bf u},{\bf v}\in Q_{k}

is the coefficient of
{\bf y}^{p{\bf v}-{\bf u}}
in the
right-hand side of (
11

(\sum_{r}h_{r}y_{r}^{p})^{j}(\sum_{r}h_{r}y_{r})^{i}

with positive entries such that

{\bf w}={\bf v}-{\bf v}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{coeff of ${\bf y}^{p({\bf v}-{\bf v}^{\prime})}$ in}(-1)^{|{\bf v}-{\bf v}^{\prime}|}\binom{k}{|{\bf v}-{\bf v}^{\prime}|}\left(\sum_{r}h_{r}y_{r}^{p}\right)^{|{\bf v}-{\bf v}^{\prime}|}

and note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}P_{m,{\bf w}+{\bf e}_{i}}({\bf z})

\displaystyle=\left(\left(z_{1}+\ldots+z_{s}\right)_{m-1-\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}}\prod_{i=1}^{s}[z_{i}-1]_{w_{i}}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{s}(z_{i}-w_{i}-1)

\displaystyle=P_{m,{\bf w}}({\bf z})-\left(m+s-1\right)P_{m-1,{\bf w}}({\bf z}).

Since
0<m+s-1<p
we deduce from the above formula that polynomial
P_{m-1,{\bf w}}({\bf z})
belongs to
A
. Now it follows from our inductional assumption that
{\bf z}^{\bf w}\in A
for any
{\bf w}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}<m
. It remains to consider monomials
{\bf z}^{\bf w}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}=m
. For such
{\bf w}
we have

P_{m,{\bf w}}({\bf z})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}[z_{i}-1]_{w_{i}}={\bf z}^{{\bf w}}+\text{ terms of total degree }<m.

Since the terms of smaller degree belong to
A
, we have
{\bf z}^{\bf w}\in A
. This finishes the proof of the step of induction.

(b) When
{\bf w}
runs over the indexing set from part (a), polynomials

Q_{{\bf w}}({\bf z})=\prod_{i=1}^{s}[z_{i}-1]_{w_{i}}={\bf z}^{\bf w}+\text{ terms of smaller total degree }

form a basis in the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
-module of all polynomails of total degree
\leq m
. The transition matrix from the monomial basis to this basis has determinant 1, and therefore
\det({\bf v}^{\bf w})=\det(Q_{\bf w}({\bf v}))
. Note that
Q_{{\bf w}}({\bf v})=0
when
w_{i}\geq v_{i}
for some
i
. Let
{\bf e}=(1,\ldots,1)
. We then have
Q_{{\bf w}}({\bf v})=0
when
\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}>\sum_{i=1}^{s}(v_{i}-1)
or
\sum_{i=1}^{s}w_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}(v_{i}-1)
but
{\bf w}\neq{\bf v}-{\bf e}
. If we arrange both indexing sets so that the sum of coordinates is non-decreasing, then

\det(Q_{\bf w}({\bf v}))=\pm\prod_{{\bf v}}Q_{{\bf v}-{\bf e}}({\bf v})=\pm\prod_{{\bf v}}\prod_{i=1}^{s}(v_{i}-1)!.

In the set indexing columns the maximal value of
v_{i}-1
is
m
, hence the above determinant is not divisible by
p
when
p>m
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
k\leq n
. We use Proposition
3.5
to decompose

\det(HW^{(k)})|_{t=0}
into a product
the Hasse–Witt determinants corresponding to
P(\tau^{\circ})
for proper faces
\tau\subset\Delta

of all dimensions.
Let
\tau
be a proper face. By Lemma
3.6
the Hasse–Witt matrix

H=HW^{(k)}(P(F^{\circ})|_{t=0}
can be computed from the coefficients of the polynomial

F^{(k)}_{h}({\bf x})
with
h=1-\sum_{{\bf w}\in F_{\mathbb{Z}}}g_{\bf w}{\bf x}^{\bf w}
.
Since
{\rm vol}(F)=1
and all
g_{\bf w}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
we can apply
Proposition
3.7
and conclude that the
p
-adic order of
\det H

equals
\sum_{{\bf u}\in(kP(F^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}}(\deg{\bf u}-1)
. It remains to note that

\Delta\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
is the disjoint union of sets
P(F^{\circ})
for all proper faces
F
.
Multiplying by
k
and taking sets of integral points, we find that
(k\Delta)_{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}

is the disjoint union of sets
(kP(F^{\circ}))_{\mathbb{Z}}

It follows that

our claim follows.
∎

4.
Construction of the Frobenius structure

Now we turn to the proof of Proposition

introduced starting on page
1
. We have a Laurent polynomial
f({\bf x})=1-tg({\bf x})
with
g\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
such that its Newton polytope

is reflexive. We start with the ring
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
where
D_{f}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
is some polynomial with
D_{f}(0)\neq 0

-modules of admissible rational functions
\mathcal{O}_{f}
and
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}
consist of elements of the form
(k-1)!A({\bf x})/f({\bf x})^{k}
where

and

is an admissible polynomial with coefficients in

and support in
k\Delta
and
k\Delta^{\circ}
repectively. Recall that

is a submodule generated by
\theta_{i}\omega
for
\omega\in\mathcal{O}_{f}
and

. We also have a finite group
\mathcal{G}
of monomial substitutions which preserve

.

Definition 4.1
.

We shall say that
M=(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is a cyclic
\theta
-module
of rank

if

(i)

for every
m\geq 0
and for
every
\mathcal{G}
-invariant admissible Laurent polynomial

supported in

we have

m!\frac{A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}\equiv\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,n-1)}b_{j}(t)\theta^{j}(1/f)\quad({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f})

with
b_{j}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
for all
j
.

(ii)

The monic order
n
differential operator
L
such that
L(1/f)\in d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is irreducible in

{\mathbb{Q}}(t)[\theta]
.

Note that existence of
L
in (ii) follows from (i). We call

the Picard-Fuchs differential operator associated to
M
. Write
L=\theta^{n}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a_{j}(t)\theta^{j}
with
a_{j}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]

for all
j
.
We shall say that
M
is of MUM-type (maximally unipotent local monodromy) if
a_{j}(0)=0

for all
j<n
.

For any prime
p
not dividing
\#\mathcal{G}\times D_{f}(0)
and
p\geq n
we embed
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and fix a Frobenius lift
\sigma
on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. We assume it is given by
t^{\sigma}=t^{p}(1+p\,t\,u(t))
for some
u\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
M=(\mathcal{O}^{\circ}_{f})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
be a cyclic
\theta
-module of rank
n
. Suppose that
p\geq n
.
Then there exist
\lambda_{i}(t)\in p^{i}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
such that

(14)

\mathscr{C}_{p}(1/f)\equiv\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(t)(\theta^{i}(1/f))^{\sigma}\quad({\rm mod}\ d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}).

Proof.

From specialization of
[
4
, (3)]
to
m=1,A=1
we get the following explicit formula

(15)

\mathscr{C}_{p}(1/f)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{p^{k}}{k!}\times k!\frac{Q_{k}({\bf x})}{(f^{\sigma})^{k+1}},

with
Q_{k}({\bf x})=\mathscr{C}_{p}(G({\bf x})^{k}f({\bf x})^{p-1})
and

G({\bf x})=\frac{1}{p}(f^{\sigma}({\bf x}^{p})-f({\bf x})^{p}).

The discussion preceding
[
4
, (3)]
assures us that the Laurent polynomials
Q_{k}({\bf x})
are
admissible with respect to
t^{\sigma}
. So we can carry out Dwork-Griffiths reduction modulo

d\mathcal{O}_{f^{\sigma}}
using Definition
4.1
(i) with
f
replaced by
f^{\sigma}
.
The terms
k!Q_{k}({\bf x})/(f^{\sigma})^{k+1}

are equivalent modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f^{\sigma}}
to a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-linear combination of

(\theta^{i}(1/f))^{\sigma},i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
.
Thus the existence of the
\lambda_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
follows.

For any
0\leq i<n
the contributions to
\lambda_{i}(t)
come from terms in (
15

k\geq i
. Since
p\geq n
we have that
\mathrm{ord}_{p}(p^{k}/k!)\geq i
if
k\geq i
.
This implies that
\lambda_{i}(t)
is divisible by
p^{i}
for all
0\leq i<n
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Consider the
p
-adic completion
R={\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]\,\widehat{\;}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket

Remark 4.4
.

In the proof of Proposition
1.8

we need independence of
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1
modulo
d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.
It should follow from the irreducibility in

of the Picard-Fuchs operator.
Unfortunately we have seen no easy way do deduce this. Instead we need to invoke the

-th
Hasse–Witt assumption and the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{O}^{\circ}_{f})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
be a cyclic
\theta
-module of rank
n
.
Then the elements
\theta^{i}(1/f),i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
are linearly independent modulo

d\mathcal{O}_{f}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}(t)
.

Proof.

Rational functions
\omega=A({\bf x})/f({\bf x})^{m}\in\mathcal{O}_{f}
can be expanded as power series as follows:

A({\bf x})/(1-tg({\bf x}))^{m}=A({\bf x})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{k+m-1}{m-1}t^{m}g({\bf x})^{m}=\sum_{{\bf u}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{\bf u}{\bf x}^{\bf u}

with some coefficients

c_{\bf 0}=c_{\bf 0}(\omega)
vanishes when

. For this reason the map

in §2 of Dwork crystals II. Note that this map is

-linear and commutes with
\theta
. The constant term

shall be denoted by
F_{0}(t)
, this is precisely the power series solution to the Picard-Fuchs operator
L
normalized so that

. Suppose that there exist

not all zero such that

. Then

is also annihilated by the lower order operator

. Using the right Euclidean algorithm in

we then find a right common divisor
L^{\prime\prime}
of
L
and
L^{\prime}
. This operator of smaller order also annihilates

. Hence

, which contradicts irreducibility of

in
{\mathbb{Q}}(t)[\theta]
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(\mathcal{O}^{\circ}_{f})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
be a cyclic
\theta
-module of rank
n
.
Then the elements
\theta^{i}(1/f),i=0,1,\ldots,n-1

d\mathcal{O}_{f}

over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Proof.

Suppose we have

We can rewrite this equality as a system of linear equation in the

and the coefficients

5.
Simplicial family

Let

f({\bf x})=1-t\left(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}+\frac{1}{x_{1}\cdots x_{n}}\right).

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
D_{f}(t)=(n+1)(1-((n+1)t)^{n+1})
. Then the
{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
-module
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}

is a cyclic
\theta
-module of MUM-type. The Picard-Fuchs operator reads

L=\theta^{n}-((n+1)t)^{n+1}(\theta+1)\cdots(\theta+n).

Note that if we take the constant term (in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
) in each summand
of the series expansion
1/f({\bf x})=\sum_{k\geq 0}t^{k}g({\bf x})^{k}
we get in the simplicial case the power series

\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{((n+1)k)!}{(k!)^{n+1}},

which is the power series solution of our Picard-Fuchs equation
L(y)=0
.

Notice that in the following proof we do not have to invoke the symmetry group of
g({\bf x})
, which was denoted in the Introduction by
\mathcal{G}\cong S_{n+1}
. Proposition
5.1
tells us that the module
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
is generated by
\mathcal{G}
-symmetric functions, and therefore we can conclude that
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}
.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

In order to make the symmetric background of
g({\bf x})

more manifest we introduce
x_{0}=1/(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})
and write any monomial
{\bf x}^{{\bf u}}
in
the form
{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}=x_{0}^{w_{0}}x_{1}^{w_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{w_{n}}
with
w_{0},\ldots,w_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
.
Of course
the exponent vector
(w_{0},\ldots,w_{n})
is only determined up to a shift by integer multiples of
(1,\ldots,1)
.
Let us write
|{\bf w}|=w_{0}+\cdots+w_{n}
.
Then
\deg({\bf x}^{{\bf w}})\leq|{\bf w}|
and we have equality if and only if
\min_{i}w_{i}=0
.
We also rewrite
f({\bf x})
as
1-t(x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n})
. Observe that for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

(17)

\theta_{i}\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}=(w_{i}-w_{0})\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}+m(x_{i}-x_{0})\frac{{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}.

Take the sum over
i=1,\ldots,n
and multiply by
(m-1)!t^{|{\bf w}|}
.

0\equiv(|{\bf w}|-(n+1)w_{0})(m-1)!\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}+t(x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n}-(n+1)x_{0})m!\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

Now replace
t(x_{0}+\cdots+x_{n})
by
1-f({\bf x})
and we get, after rearranging terms,

(18)

m!\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|+1}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}x_{0}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}\equiv m!\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}+(m-1)!\left(\frac{|{\bf w}|-m}{n+1}-w_{0}\right)\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

Combining this with (
17
) we find

(19)

m!\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|+1}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}x_{i}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}\equiv m!\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}+(m-1)!\left(\frac{|{\bf w}|-m}{n+1}-w_{i}\right)\frac{t^{|{\bf w}|}{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f})

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
. Using these reduction formulae one easily sees by induction that any term

m!\frac{t^{\deg({\bf u})}{\bf x}^{\bf u}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}
with
\deg({\bf u})\leq m
is equivalent modulo

d\mathcal{O}_{f}
to a
{\mathbb{Z}}[(n+1)^{-1}]
-linear combination of
\frac{k!}{f({\bf x})^{k+1}}
with
k=0,\ldots,m
.

To finish the verification of part (i) of Definition
4.1
we must write
\theta^{n}(1/f)
as a

{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
-
linear combination of
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
. To that end we observe that
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n}=1
.
So

(20)

n!\frac{t^{n+1}x_{0}\cdots x_{n}}{f({\bf x})^{n+1}}=n!\frac{t^{n+1}}{f({\bf x})^{n+1}}=t^{n+1}(\theta+1)\cdots(\theta+n)\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right).

We now show that the left hand side equals
(n+1)^{-n-1}\theta^{n}(1/f({\bf x}))
modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
.
Set
i=n,m=n,{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}=x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{n-1}
in (
19
). We
find

(21)

n!\frac{t^{n+1}x_{0}\cdots x_{n}}{f({\bf x})^{n+1}}\equiv n!\frac{1}{n+1}\frac{t^{n}x_{0}\cdots x_{n-1}}{f({\bf x})^{n+1}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

Choose any
1\leq m\leq n
and set
i=m-1,{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}=x_{0}\cdots x_{m-2}
in (
19
). If
m=1

we take
{\bf x}^{{\bf w}}=1
. We get

\displaystyle m!\frac{t^{m}x_{0}\cdots x_{m-1}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\left(m!\frac{t^{m-1}x_{0}\cdots x_{m-2}}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}-(m-1)!\frac{t^{m-1}x_{0}\cdots x_{m-2}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\right)({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\theta\left((m-1)!\frac{t^{m-1}x_{0}\cdots x_{m-2}}{f({\bf x})^{m}}\right)({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

By recursive application for
m=n,n-1,\ldots,1
and finally (
21
),(
20
)
we find that

(n+1)^{-n-1}\theta^{n}\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)\equiv t^{n+1}(\theta+1)\cdots(\theta+n)\left(\frac{1}{f({\bf x})}\right)({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}),

which verifies part (i) of Definition
4.1
. It also establishes the MUM-property of
L
.

To verify part (ii) we remark that the hypergeometric equation with parameters
\frac{k}{n+1}
,

k=1,\ldots,n
and
1,1,\ldots,1
has the property that the parameter sets are disjoint modulo
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
This is the well-known irreducibility criterion for hypergeometric differential equations,
see
[
5
]
.
Our equation
L(y)=0
arises from this hypergemeometric equation after the substitution

t\to((n+1)t)^{n+1}
. The monodromy of the hypergeometric equation is generated by a maximally
unipotent matrix
U
coming from the monodromy at
0
and a pseudo reflection
S
from
the local monodromy at
1
. Then the monodromy group of
L(y)=0
contains
U^{n+1}
and
S
.
It is straightforward to see that if
U
and
S
generate a matrix group that acts irreducibly,
then the same holds for
U^{n+1}
and
S
. Hence the monodromy group of
L(y)=0
is irreducible,
which implies in particular that
L
is irreducible in
{\mathbb{Q}}(t)[\theta]
.
∎

6.
Hyperoctahedral family

Let

f({\bf x})=1-t\left(x_{1}+\frac{1}{x_{1}}+\cdots+x_{n}+\frac{1}{x_{n}}\right)

and let
\mathcal{G}\cong S_{n}\times({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{n}
be the symmetry group generated by the permutations of

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
x_{i}\to x_{i}^{\pm 1}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

There exists
D_{f}(t)\in n!{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
with
D_{f}(0)=n!
such that over the ring

{\mathbb{Z}}[t,1/D_{f}(t)]
the module
(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\mathcal{O}_{f}

satisfies property (i) of Definition
4.1
. There exists a monic operator
L
of order
n

with coefficients in this ring and of MUM-type such that
L(1/f)\in d\mathcal{O}_{f}
.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

For the beginning we will work over the ring
R={\mathbb{Z}}[1/n!][t]
.
Define
X_{i}=x_{i}+\frac{1}{x_{i}}
and
Y_{i}=x_{i}-\frac{1}{x_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Any element in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]^{\mathcal{G}}
is a symmetric function in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.
Notice also that

\theta_{i}X_{i}=Y_{i}
and
\theta_{i}Y_{i}=X_{i}
.

We first reduce
m!\frac{A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}
as a linear combination of
\theta^{i}(1/f)

for
i=0,1,\ldots,m
. We shall reduce this problem to the case when
A
is an
elementary symmetric function of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

For any
i=1,\ldots,n
, and polynomial
B
in the
X_{i}
consider

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}B}{f^{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{X_{i}B}{f^{m}}+\frac{Y_{i}\theta_{i}B}{f^{m}}+mt\frac{Y_{i}^{2}B}{f^{m+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{X_{i}B}{f^{m}}+\frac{Y_{i}\theta_{i}B}{f^{m}}+mt\frac{X_{i}^{2}B}{f^{m+1}}-mt\frac{4B}{f^{m+1}}

Multiply on both sides by
(m-1)!t^{\deg{B}+1}
. We get

m!t^{\deg{B}+2}\frac{X_{i}^{2}B}{f^{m+1}}\equiv m!t^{\deg{B}+2}\frac{4B}{f^{m+1}}-(m-1)!t^{\deg{B}+1}\frac{X_{i}B}{f^{m}}-(m-1)!t^{\deg{B}+1}\frac{Y_{i}\theta_{i}B}{f^{m}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

So we see that any admissible term of the form
m!\frac{t^{\deg(A)}A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}
, where
A
is divisible by the square of some
X_{i}
, is equivalent
modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
to a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination of an admissible term with denominator
f^{m+1}
and
a numerator of degree
\deg(A)-2
and admissible terms with denominator
f^{m}
.

Thus
m!\frac{t^{\deg(A)}A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}
is equivalent modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
to a
linear combination of terms with admissible numerators which have degree at most
1
in each
variable and denominator
f({\bf x})^{k+1}
with
0\leq k\leq m
.
So far we have not used the symmetry of
A
in the
X_{i}
, but for the next step this will be important.
Since
\#\mathcal{G}=n!2^{n}
is invertible in our ring, we can apply symmetrization with respect
to the action of
\mathcal{G}
and conclude that
m!\frac{t^{\deg(A)}A({\bf x})}{f({\bf x})^{m+1}}

is equivalent to a linear combination of terms
k!t^{r}s_{r}({\bf X})/f({\bf x})^{k+1}
where

0\leq r\leq k\leq m
and
s_{r}({\bf X})
is the elementary symmetric
polynomial in
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of degree
r
. When
r=0
we define
s_{0}=1
. In the following
computation we use the identities
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}=(n+1-r)s_{r-1}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}=rs_{r}

and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}=s_{1}s_{r}-(r+1)s_{r+1}
. These hold
for
r=0,1,\ldots,n
if we define
s_{-1}=s_{n+1}=0
.
We make the following computation.

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\theta_{i}\left(\frac{Y_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}}{f^{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{X_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}}{f^{m}}+mt\frac{(X_{i}^{2}-4)\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{i}}s_{r}}{f^{m+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{rs_{r}}{f^{m}}+mt\frac{s_{1}s_{r}-(r+1)s_{r+1}}{f^{m+1}}-4mt\frac{(n+1-r)s_{r-1}}{f^{m+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(r-m)\frac{s_{r}}{f^{m}}+m\frac{s_{r}}{f^{m+1}}-mt(r+1)\frac{s_{r+1}}{f^{m+1}}-4mt(n+1-r)\frac{s_{r-1}}{f^{m+1}}.

In the first equality we used that
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X_{i}^{2}}s_{r}=0
and
Y_{i}^{2}=X_{i}^{2}-4
.
In the third equality we used
ts_{1}=1-f
.

After multiplication by
r!(m-1)!t^{r}
we get

(22)

\displaystyle m!

\displaystyle\frac{(r+1)!t^{r+1}s_{r+1}}{f^{m+1}}

\displaystyle\equiv(r-m)(m-1)!\frac{r!t^{r}s_{r}}{f^{m}}+m!\frac{r!t^{r}s_{r}}{f^{m+1}}-4m!t^{2}(n+1-r)r\frac{(r-1)!t^{r-1}s_{r-1}}{f^{m+1}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

Thus we see that modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
every term
m!(r+1)!t^{r+1}s_{r+1}/f^{m+1}

with
0\leq r<n
equals a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination of similar
terms whose numerator or denominator have lower degree. By induction we can continue this reduction
procedure until we end with a linear combination of terms
k!/f^{k+1}
with
0\leq k\leq m
.

To finalize the proof we show that
\theta^{n}(1/f)
is a linear combination of
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,

i=0,\ldots,n-1
.

To that end we reduce

(n!)^{2}t^{n}s_{n}/f^{n+1}
in two ways as linear combination of
r!/f^{r+1}
with
r=0,\ldots,n
and show
that they are different.

First set
r=m=n
in (
22
).
Using our definition
s_{n+1}=0
we find that

n!\frac{n!t^{n}s_{n}}{f^{n+1}}\equiv 4n!nt^{2}\frac{(n-1)!t^{n-1}s_{n-1}}{f^{n}}({\rm mod}\ d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

Using (
22
)
we see that the right hand side reduces to
t^{2}
times a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination of
terms
r!/f^{r+1}
with
r=0,\ldots,n-1
modulo
d\mathcal{O}_{f}
. For the second reduction use the left hand
side and reduce again with the aid of (
22
).
This time we get a result that is not a multiple of
t^{2}

and therefore not the same as the first reduction. This shows the existence of a non-trivial relation
between
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,
i=0,1,\ldots,n
. We make this relation more explicit.

Note that the last term on the right hand side of (
22
) reduces to

t^{2}
times a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination of
k!/f^{k+1}
for
k=0,\ldots,m
. We now consider
(
22
) modulo
t^{2}
,

m!\frac{(r+1)!t^{r+1}s_{r+1}}{f^{m+1}}\equiv(r-m)(m-1)!\frac{r!t^{r}s_{r}}{f^{m}}+m!\frac{r!t^{r}s_{r}}{f^{m+1}}({\rm mod}\ t^{2},d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

We assert that

(m-1)!\frac{r!t^{r}s_{r}}{f^{m}}\equiv\theta^{r}(\theta+1)_{m-r-1}\left(\frac{1}{f}\right)({\rm mod}\ t^{2},d\mathcal{O}_{f})

for all
r<m
. An easy verification shows that this formula is a solution of our recursion
modulo
(t^{2},d\mathcal{O}_{f})
and it also satisfies the starting condition
(m-1)!/f^{m}=(\theta+1)_{m-1}(1/f)
.

We now conclude that

n!\frac{n!t^{n}s_{n}}{f^{n+1}}\equiv\theta^{n}\left(\frac{1}{f}\right)({\rm mod}\ t^{2},d\mathcal{O}_{f}).

This reduction is distinct from our first reduction because it is not zero modulo
t^{2}
.
Furthermore, we know that
n!\frac{n!t^{n}s_{n}}{f^{n+1}}
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination
of terms
r!/f^{r+1}
with
r=0,\ldots,n
. By the relation
r!/f^{r+1}=(\theta+1)\ldots(\theta+r)(1/f)

it is also a
{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
-linear combination of
\theta^{r}(1/f)
for
r=0,\ldots,n
. Using the above
congruence modulo
t^{2},d\mathcal{O}_{f}
we see that the coefficient of
\theta^{n}(1/f)
equals

1({\rm mod}\ t^{2})
. Thus we find that there exist
a_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
such that

\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}(t)\theta^{n-i}(1/f)\in d\mathcal{O}_{f},

with
a_{0}(0)=1
and
a_{i}(0)=0
for all
i>0
. We take
D_{f}(t)=n!a_{0}(t)
(to account for inversion of
n!
)
and our proposition is proven.
∎

7.
Vanishing of
\alpha_{1}

In Sections
3
-
6
we constructed the
p
-adic Frobenius structure for the Picard–Fuchs differential equations
L(y)=0
of order
n<p
corresponding to the simplicial and hyperoctahedral families in
n
dimensions. By Proposition
1.8
the respective Frobenius constants are given by
\alpha_{i}=p^{-i}\lambda_{i}(0)
, where
\lambda_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
are the coefficients in the formula

(23)

\mathscr{C}_{p}(1/f)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}(t)(\theta^{i}(1/f))^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f^{\sigma}}).

Let
n\geq 2
. The fact that
\alpha_{1}=0
is crucial for Corollary
1.9
. In the proof of this corollary in the introductory section we said that vanishing follows from
[
4
, Proposition 7.7]
. In this concluding Section we would like to explain how to apply this result to our situation. We work over the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
.

Lemma 7.1
.

For the simplicial and hyperoctahedral families in dimension
n\geq 2
we have

(i)

the quotient
(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/\mathscr{F}_{2}
is the free
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-module with basis
1/f
and
\theta(1/f)
;

(ii)

d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}\cap(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}\subset\mathscr{F}_{2}
.

Proof.

Recall that in the cases under consideration the
n
th Hasse–Witt condition holds due to Theorem
3.3
. By the results in Section
5
and
6
and Lemma
4.8
we have that
(\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ})^{\mathcal{G}}/d\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}
is a free
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
-module with the basis
\theta^{i}(1/f)
,
0\leq i\leq n-1
. Hence (ii) would follow from (i). We now prove (i). The
n
th Hasse-Witt condition implies in particular the second Hasse-Witt condition. Therefore we have the direct sum decomposition
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}_{f}^{\circ}\cong\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}(2)\oplus\mathscr{F}_{2}
. Note that
\mathcal{O}_{f}^{\circ}(2)^{\mathcal{G}}
is spanned by
1/f
and
\theta(1/f)
because
\mathcal{G}
acts transitively on the set of vertices of the Newton polytope
\Delta
and the only integral points in
2\Delta^{\circ}
are
{\bf 0}
and the vertices. Using the above direct sum decomposition and doing symmetrization with respect to
\mathcal{G}
we obtain (i).
∎

In
[
4
, §7]
we considered the series
A,B,\widetilde{\lambda}_{0},\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
such that

(24)

\displaystyle\theta^{2}(1/f)

\displaystyle=A(t)/f+B(t)\theta(1/f)({\rm mod}\ \mathscr{F}_{2}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{p}(1/f)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}(t)/f+\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(t)(\theta(1/f))^{\sigma}({\rm mod}\ \mathscr{F}_{2}^{\sigma}).

In loc.cit. we denote them by
\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda_{1}
, but here we need to change the notation to avoid collision with the coefficients in (
23
). Though we don’t use this fact, let us remark that
\theta^{2}-B\theta-A
is a right divisor of our Picard-Fuchs operator
L
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket[\theta]
as it follows from (ii) above. Here

A(0)=B(0)=0,\quad\widetilde{\lambda}_{0}(0)=1\text{ and }\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(0)=0

by Propositions 7.6 and 7.7 in loc.cit. respectively. Their proofs are based on congruences for expansion coefficients of the functions in the identities (
24
). The desired vanishing result follows from the next lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

In the above notations we have
\lambda_{1}(0)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}(0)
.

Proof.

Using induction For each
i\geq 2
we construct the series
A_{i},B_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}\llbracket t\rrbracket
such that
\theta^{i}(1/f)=A_{i}(t)/f+B_{i}(t)\theta(1/f)({\rm mod}\ \mathscr{F}_{2})
. Indeed, for
i=2
we take
A_{2}=A,B_{2}=B
and apply
\theta
consecutively to obtain the coefficients for higher
i
. It is not hard to show by induction that
A_{i}(0)=B_{i}(0)=0
for each
i
. Due to Lemma
7.1
(ii) identity (
23
) also holds modulo
\mathscr{F}_{2}^{\sigma}
. For every
i\geq 2
we substitute
(\theta^{i}(1/f))^{\sigma}
with
A_{i}(t^{\sigma})/f^{\sigma}+B_{i}(t^{\sigma})(\theta(1/f))^{\sigma}
and conclude that
\widetilde{\lambda}_{1}=\lambda_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}B_{i}(t^{\sigma})\lambda_{i}
. Our claim follows from this because
B_{i}(0)=0
for all
i
.
∎
