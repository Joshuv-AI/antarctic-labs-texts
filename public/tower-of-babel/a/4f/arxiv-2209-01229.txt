Cubic-Regularized Newton for Spectral Constrained Matrix Optimization and its Application to Fairness
Casey Garner Gilad Lerman Shuzhong Zhang

Abstract
Matrix functions are utilized to rewrite smooth spectral constrained matrix optimization problems as smooth unconstrained problems over the set of symmetric matrices which are then solved via the cubic-regularized Newton method. A second-order chain rule identity for matrix functions is proven to compute the higher-order derivatives to implement cubic-regularized Newton, and a new convergence analysis is provided for cubic-regularized Newton for matrix vector spaces. We demonstrate the applicability of our approach by conducting numerical experiments on both synthetic and real datasets. In our experiments, we formulate a new model for estimating fair and robust covariance matrices in the spirit of the Tyler’s M-estimator (TME) model and demonstrate its advantage. Keywords: matrix optimization; cubic-regularized Newton; matrix functions; Tyler’s M-estimator. MSC codes: 90C26, 15A16, 65K10, 68Q32

The focus of this section is defining matrix functions and demonstrating how they can be leveraged to rewrite the constrained optimization model (

Definition 1
.

Let

\bm{F}(\bm{X})=\bm{Q}\bm{F}(\bm{\Lambda})\bm{Q}^{\top}

(3)

As detailed in

Theorem 1
.

\displaystyle DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]

\displaystyle D^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]

2.1
Writing the Unconstrained Model

The role of matrix functions in this paper are to enforce the spectral constraints in (

Theorem 2
.

Let

\mathcal{I}_{k}

\mathcal{I}_{1}=(\alpha,\;+\infty)

•

\mathcal{I}_{2}=(-\infty,\alpha)

\bm{G}_{2}(\bm{X})=-e^{\bm{X}}+\alpha\bm{I}

\mathcal{I}_{3}=[\alpha,\;+\infty)

3
Matrix Calculus and a Second-Order Chain Rule Identity

Applying higher-order methods such as cubic-regularized Newton requires second-order derivative information. Thus, we must define gradient and Hessian operators as well as directional derivatives for real-valued matrix functions
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
where
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a matrix vector space.

Definition 2
.

The matrix gradient for
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function
\bm{\nabla}F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
whose elements are defined as,

\left(\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})\right)_{ij}:=\frac{\partial F}{\partial X_{ij}}(\bm{X}),\;\;\;\forall\;i,j\in\{1,2\ldots,n\},

provided all the partial derivatives exist.

We further define the directional derivative of a real-valued matrix function, and relate it to the matrix gradient in the expected manner.

Definition 3
.

The directional derivative of
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V}
in the direction of
\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}
, denoted
DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]
, is defined as,

DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]:=\frac{d}{dt}\left(F(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})\right)\big|_{t=0}.

(4)

As in the Euclidean setting, the directional derivatives of a real-valued matrix function relates closely to its gradient,

DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=\langle\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}),\bm{H}\rangle.

(5)

Note, Definition
3
could have been replaced with the more general Fréchet derivative of
F
where
{DF}(\bm{X}):\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a linear operator and,

\lim_{\bm{H}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\frac{|F(\bm{X}+\bm{H})-F(\bm{X})-DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]|}{\|\bm{H}\|_{F}}=0.

Moving to higher-order derivatives, we define the Hessian of a real-valued matrix function.

Definition 4
.

The Hessian of
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
at
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V}
is a function
\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\cdot]:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
where,

\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]:=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})\right)\big|_{t=0}.

(6)

The second-order directional derivative of
F
at
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V}
,
D^{2}F(\bm{X})[\cdot,\cdot]:\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, can then be defined through the Hessian of
F
at
\bm{X}
as,

D^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H}_{1},\bm{H}_{2}]:=\langle\bm{\nabla^{2}F}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}_{1}],\bm{H}_{2}\rangle.

(7)

It is important to note
\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})
is uniquely defined and
D^{2}F(\bm{X})
is a bi-linear, symmetric operator in
\bm{H}_{1}
and
\bm{H}_{2}
. Using this differential information one can present a matrix version of Taylor’s Theorem,

F(\bm{X}+\bm{H})=F(\bm{X})+DF(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]+\mathcal{O}(\|\bm{H}\|_{F}^{3}).

Additionally, we can define an operator norm on the second-order matrix derivative of
F
.

Definition 5
.

The operator norm of
D^{2}F(\bm{X})
over the matrix vector space
\mathcal{V}
is given as,

\|D^{2}F(\bm{X})\|:=\max\left\{|D^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H}_{1},\bm{H}_{2}]|\;\big|\;\|\bm{H}_{1}\|_{F}=\|\bm{H}_{2}\|_{F}=1,\;\bm{H}_{1},\bm{H}_{2}\in\mathcal{V}\right\}.

Using this norm, we introduce the notion of a Lipschitz Hessian which plays a crucial role in the convergence analysis of the cubic-regularized Newton algorithm.

Definition 6
.

The function
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
has Lipschitz Hessian with parameter
L>0
if,

\|D^{2}F(\bm{X})-D^{2}F(\bm{Y})\|\leq L\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F},\;\;\;\forall\;\bm{X},\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{V}.

A couple of key inequalities follow from assuming a Lipschitz Hessian. Lemma
3
, which is proved in the Supplemental Materials, is crucial in Appendix
10
.

Lemma 3
.

If the Hessian of
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{V}
with parameter
L>0
, then for all
\bm{X},\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\|\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})-\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{Y})-\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\|_{F}\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2},

(8)

|F(\bm{Y})-F(\bm{X})-\langle\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}),\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}],\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle|\leq\frac{L}{6}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{3}.

(9)

Since
\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]
is a linear function in
\bm{H}
, it follows there exists
\bm{A}(\bm{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times n^{2}}
such that,

\text{vec}\left(\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\right)=\bm{A}(\bm{X})\text{vec}(\bm{H}).

(10)

We will subsequently refer to
\bm{A}(\bm{X})
as the vectorization of the Hessian, and (
10
) will play a fundamental role in our new analysis for cubic-regularized Newton in Section
10
. For more details on matrix derivatives and directional derivatives, the reader is directed to
[
1
,
20
,
23
]
.

A final important result for implementing cubic-regularized Newton is the chain rule for the composition of functions which appears in (
2
).

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a vector space,
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{G}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
be any matrix function defined by an analytic scalar function. Let
\bm{X},\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}
and assume
F
is differentiable at
\bm{G}(\bm{X})
and
\bm{G}
is differentiable at
\bm{X}

F
and
\bm{G}

F\circ\bm{G}

\bm{X}

\bm{H}

D(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=DF(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg],

(11)

D^{2}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\\
D^{2}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}],D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg]+DF(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D^{2}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]\bigg].

(12)

It is worth stating (
11
) holds true for general vector spaces, and this identity is a standard result (see e.g.,
[
1
,
23
]
). On the other hand, we were unable to locate (
12
) in the literature with the general setting of matrix functions. We thus prove it in Section

4
Cubic-Regularized Newton for Matrix Optimization

We introduce the cubic regularized Newton method for the unconstrained matrix optimization problem,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;F(\bm{X})

(13)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V},

where we assume:
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a
d
-dimensional subspace,
F\in C^{2}(\mathcal{V})
has Lipschitz Hessian with parameter
L>0
, and there exists finite
F^{*}
such that
F^{*}\leq F(\bm{X})
for all
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{V}
.

In our discussion of convergence, we restrict ourselves to
\epsilon

13

\bm{X}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
is a second-order
\epsilon
-stationary point of (
13
) with
\epsilon>0
if
\|\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}^{*})\|_{F}\leq\epsilon
,
and
\langle\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X}^{*})[\bm{H}],\bm{H}\rangle\geq-\sqrt{\epsilon}\|\bm{H}\|_{F}^{2}

\forall\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}
.

Algorithm
1
sketches the cubic-regularized Newton method. This method has two key attributes, which are encapsulated in the theorems below: (1) global convergence to second-order
\epsilon
-stationary points and (2) local superlinear convergence near non-degenerate local minimum.

Theorem 5
.

Assume
F\in C^{2}(\mathcal{V})
has Lipschitz Hessian with parameter
L>0
and is bounded below by
F^{*}
on
\mathcal{V}
. Then the sequence
\{\bm{X}_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
1
converges to a second-order
\epsilon
-stationary point for (
13
) with rate
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-3/2})
.

Theorem 6
.

If Algorithm
1
is initialized sufficiently close to a non-degenerate local minimum
\bm{X}^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
, i.e.

\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}^{*})=0\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\exists\delta>0\;\text{ such that }\;\;\langle\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X}^{*})[\bm{H}],\bm{H}\rangle\geq\delta\|\bm{H}\|_{F}^{2}\;\;\text{for all }\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V},

then the sequence
\{\bm{X}_{k}\}
generated by Algorithm

\bm{X}^{*}

Implementation of Cubic-Regularized Newton for Matrix Optimization

We now discuss implementation of Algorithm
1
. The key hurdle to applying the method rests primarily on solving a matrix optimization subproblem of the form,

\min_{\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}}\;\;\langle\bm{G},\bm{H}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{L}(\bm{H}),\bm{H}\rangle+\frac{M}{6}\|\bm{H}\|_{F}^{3},

(14)

where
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
is a linear map,
\bm{G}\in\mathcal{V}
and
M>0
. In this section we describe one approach to obtain a global optimal solution to solve (
14
) by first rewriting it as an unconstrained cubic-regularized subproblem over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. First, select an orthonormal basis,
\{\bm{E}_{1},\ldots,\bm{E}_{d}\}
, for the d-dimensional vector subspace
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Then, any
\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}
can be rewritten in terms of the basis as,

\bm{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\bm{E}_{i},

where
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Using the decomposition of
\bm{H}
we then rewrite the terms in the objective function of the model:

\langle\bm{G},\bm{H}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\langle\bm{G},\bm{E}_{i}\rangle,

(15)

\langle\mathcal{L}(\bm{H}),\bm{H}\rangle=\langle\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\mathcal{L}(\bm{E}_{i}),\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}\bm{E}_{j}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{i}z_{j}\langle\mathcal{L}(\bm{E}_{i}),\bm{E}_{j}\rangle,

(16)

and because the
\bm{E}_{i}
’s are orthonormal it follows
\|\bm{H}\|_{F}^{3}=\|\bm{z}\|^{3}
; therefore, through the basis, we can compute the optimal solution to (
14
) by solving the unconstrained vector optimization model,

\min_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\;\;\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{z}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{z}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{z}+\frac{M}{6}\|\bm{z}\|^{3},

(17)

where
b_{i}=\langle\bm{G},\bm{E}_{i}\rangle
and
A_{ij}=\langle\mathcal{L}(\bm{E}_{i}),\bm{E}_{j}\rangle
for
i,j=1,\ldots,d
.

Many efficient solution methods exist to solve the vectorized subproblem
[
7
,
22
,
29
]
. Therefore, this approach for solving the matrix subproblem will be tractable provided the cost of constructing the vectorized subproblem is reasonable. From Algorithm
1
, we see
\bm{G}=\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}_{k})
and

\mathcal{L}(\bm{H}):=\bm{\nabla^{2}}F(\bm{X}_{k})[\bm{H}]
.
Thus, in-order to form the subproblem, it will cost one gradient computation and
d
-Hessian evaluations of the objective function where the Hessians will be computed at the basis matrices. This means the overall cost of forming the vectorized subproblem will depend on both the complexity of the objective function’s derivatives and the complexity of the basis for the matrix vector space.

Another approach to solving this model would be to apply a Lanczos-based approach as described by Agarwal et al. in Section 8 of
[
2
]
. This is a basis-free approach, but it would still require multiple calls to the Hessian and potentially
d
such calls if the global optimal solution is desired for (
14
). Though an inexact version of cubic-regularized Newton is not implemented in this paper such methods exist and would benefit computationally from a Lanczo-based approach. However, there is still much room for improvement on producing efficient methods for solving the matrix subproblem, and the recent works on (
17
) might enable improvements in the matrix setting.

6
Application to Fairness

We present a new model for fair and robust covariance matrix estimation which we solve by our proposed procedure. More precisely, we estimate the shape or scatter matrix, which is a scalar multiple of the covariance matrix when the covariance exists (for simplicity, we refer to the covariance).
Our fairness model is an adapted variant of the Tyler’s M-estimator (TME) model; we call it the Fair TME model. The Fair TME model seeks to simultaneously obtain both a robust and fair covariance matrix for multiple subgroups of a single dataset. Substantial effort has been done on robust covariance matrix estimation; however, it appears no one has considered fairness in their estimations. Our fairness model takes a single statistic for the subgroups of a dataset, which we derive from the TME model, and through a minimization problem seeks to make this statistic small and similar amongst the subgroups. This concept is exceptionally general and therefore extends to other data science problems.

Section
6.1
reviews the TME model and presents our proposed Fair TME model. Sections
6.2
and
6.3
provide the results of the numerical experiments we performed comparing the two models on simulated and real datasets respectively.

6.1
Review of the TME Model and Introduction to the Fair TME Model

The Tyler’s M-estimator (TME) model proposed by Tyler in
[
35
]
computes a robust estimator for the covariance matrix of a centered dataset. It is determined by solving the following equation:

\textbf{Find}\;\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}_{++}\;\;\textbf{s.t.}\;\;\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\bm{x}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}}{\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i}}-\bm{R}=0.

(18)

Since (
18
) is invariant to scaling in the matrix
\bm{R}
, which makes sense for shape matrix estimation, a trace constraint is often placed on
\bm{R}
, i.e.
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{R})=p
. It has been shown the solution to (
18
) can be found by solving the following minimization problem,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;\frac{p}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\bm{x}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{x}_{i})+\log\det\bm{R}

(19)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}_{++}.

The relationship between (
18
) and (
19
) is clear from the first-order optimality condition for (
19
). Additionally, efficient methods exist to solve for TME. Tyler
[
35
]
presented a fixed-point method which solves (
19
) under various data settings; this approach has been investigated extensively and has proven linear convergence under different data assumptions
[
15
,
16
]
.

We propose a Fair TME model which seeks a single robust covariance matrix that achieves a level of fairness between multiple subgroups of a dataset. Assume we have
r
independent centered subgroups
\left\{\{\bm{x}^{1}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,\{\bm{x}^{r}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{r}}\right\}
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
which could vary in size and be drawn from multiple distributions. Define the functions,

f_{j}(\bm{R}):=\frac{p}{n_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\log((\bm{x}_{i}^{j})^{\top}\bm{R}^{-1}(\bm{x}_{i}^{j}))+\log\det\bm{R},\;\;j=1,\ldots,r.

(20)

Letting
f_{j}^{*}:=\min\left\{f_{j}(\bm{R})\;|\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}_{++}\right\}
, we define the
TME error
for the
j
-th subgroup to be,

\mathcal{E}_{j}(\bm{R}):=f_{j}(\bm{R})-f_{j}^{*},\;\;j=1,\ldots,r.

(21)

We then define a perfectly fair covariance matrix for multiple subgroups of a dataset.

Definition 7
.

The matrix
\bm{R}_{f}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}_{++}
is a perfectly fair covariance matrix for the subgroups
\left\{\{\bm{x}^{1}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}},\ldots,\{\bm{x}^{r}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{r}}\right\}
contained in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
provided,

\mathcal{E}_{i}(\bm{R}_{f})=\mathcal{E}_{j}(\bm{R}_{f}),\;\;\forall\;i,j\in\{1,\ldots,r\}.

A perfectly fair covariance matrix provides equality in the TME errors. It is often unachievable, so we propose an optimization model which seeks a nearly perfectly fair covariance while minimizing the individual TME errors. Thus, our Fair TME model is stated as follows:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;\;\mu_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{E}_{j}(\bm{R})+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\;\sum_{j=i+1}^{r}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}(\bm{R})-\mathcal{E}_{j}(\bm{R})\right)^{2}

(22)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}_{++},

where
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\geq 0
are constants. These constants dictate the focus of the model. If
\mu_{2}>>\mu_{1}
, then fairness is given priority over the subgroups having small TME errors. This means a perfectly fair covariance might be obtained but at the cost of large
\mathcal{E}_{j}
values. The most desirable situation is obtaining a solution
\bm{R}^{*}
to (
22
) giving an objective value of zero. This would be finding a perfectly fair and universally robust covariance matrix. Since such a solution often does not exist, we seek a local minimizer of (
22
).

The non-convexity of the model and the fact it is a constrained problem over the set of positive definite matrices makes it a candidate for the methods outlined in this paper. One possible route to solve the model is to apply the change of variable
\bm{Z}=\bm{R}^{-1}
to (
22
) and then utilize the matrix function
\bm{G}(\bm{R})=\bm{R}^{2}
. These steps produce the unconstrained model,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;\;\mu_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}(\bm{R}^{2})+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\;\sum_{j=i+1}^{r}\left(\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{i}(\bm{R}^{2})-\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}(\bm{R}^{2})\right)^{2}

(23)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p},

where,

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{j}(\bm{R}):=\frac{p}{n_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{j}}\log((\bm{x}_{i}^{j})^{\top}\bm{R}(\bm{x}_{i}^{j}))-\log\det\bm{R}-f_{j}^{*},\;\;j=1,\ldots,r.

(24)

Thus, if
\bm{R}^{*}
solves (
23
), then
(\bm{R}^{*})^{-2}
solves (
22
). Theorem
2
guarantees and enables us to obtain a solution to (
22
) from (
23
) while Theorems
1
and
4
allow us to calculate the derivatives needed to apply Algorithm
1
to solve (
23
). While first-order methods could also be applied to (
23
), they will often lack the benefits of local superlinear convergence and guarantees to return second-order stationary points (see discussion of Figure
1
).

Other approaches to solving (
22
) can be implemented besides solving (
23
). We present one other method in Section
11
and compare it to the approach above.

7
Conclusions

We utilized matrix functions to rewrite a constrained matrix optimization model as an unconstrained model over the set of symmetric matrices. We then proposed to solve the non-convex unconstrained model with cubic-regularized Newton and proved a chain rule identity which enabled us to compute the necessary higher-order derivatives to implement the method. Finally, we tested the approach on a fair version of the Tyler’s M-estimator model, the Fair TME model, which sought to locate a fair and robust covariance matrix for multiple subgroups of a single dataset. Through experiments on real and simulated data we demonstrated the Fair TME model produced solutions with strong TME errors and fairness values which were superior to those obtained by the standard TME formulation.

Moving forward there are several ways to extend the outlined method. First, the key step in implementing Algorithm
1
is solving the matrix subproblem in Step 2. Recently efficient methods for solving the cubic-regularized subproblem in the vector setting have drawn research attention
[
7
,
21
,
22
]
; however, not as much work has been done on the matrix problem. Investigating how to extend these solution methods to the matrix setting without vectorizing the problem could yield efficient subroutines for large matrix problems.

Further, at this time we restricted our discussion to constrained matrix optimization problems over symmetric matrices and we only investigated one type of matrix function; however, another line of research would be to see what types of constrained problems could be handled with generalized matrix functions for non-square matrices
[
18
]
or even tensor functions
[
24
]
. This could enable higher-order methods to be generated solving non-square matrix and tensor optimization models which are becoming more prevalent in applications

Appendix A: Proof of Lemma

Lemma 7
.

If the Hessian of
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{V}
with parameter
L>0
, then for all
\bm{X},\bm{Y}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\|\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{Y})-\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})-\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\|_{F}\leq\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2},

(26)

|F(\bm{Y})-F(\bm{X})-\langle\nabla F(\bm{X}),\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}],\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle|\leq\frac{L}{6}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{3}.

(27)

Proof.

Define
g(t):=\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}+t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X}))
. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus,

\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{Y})-\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})=\int_{0}^{1}\;\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X}+t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X}))[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\;dt.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{Y})

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})-\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|\int_{0}^{1}\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X}+t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X}))[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]-\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\;dt\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}Lt\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}\;dt

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}.

By Taylor’s theorem and (
26
),

\displaystyle|F(\bm{Y})-

\displaystyle F(\bm{X})-\langle\nabla F(\bm{X}),\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}],\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\langle\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}+t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X}))-\nabla F(\bm{X})-t\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}],\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\rangle|\;dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\|\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X}+t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X}))-\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{X})-t\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{X})[\bm{Y}-\bm{X}]\|_{F}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\|_{F}\;dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\frac{L}{2}\|t(\bm{Y}-\bm{X})\|_{F}^{2}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\|_{F}\;dt

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{6}\|\bm{Y}-\bm{X}\|_{F}^{3}.

∎

Appendix B: Proof of Theorem

We prove Theorem
4
restated below.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a vector space,
F:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{G}:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
be any matrix function defined by an analytic scalar function. Let
\bm{X},\bm{H}\in\mathcal{V}
and assume
F
is differentiable at
\bm{G}(\bm{X})
and
\bm{G}
is differentiable at
\bm{X}
. Then, assuming
F
and
\bm{G}
are two-times continuously differentiable, the first and second-order directional derivatives of
F\circ\bm{G}
at
\bm{X}
in the direction of
\bm{H}
are:

D(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=DF(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg],

(28)

D^{2}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\\
D^{2}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}],D\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg]+DF(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[D^{2}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]\bigg].

(29)

The first order chain rule identity has been proven in multiple references for general vector spaces (e.g.
[
23
]
). Thus, we only verify the second identity, and this will be accomplished by proving (
29
) holds for arbitrary matrix polynomials,

\bm{G}(\bm{X})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\bm{X}^{i},

(30)

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{G}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. Proving the identity holds for arbitrary matrix polynomials will be sufficent because
\bm{G}
is generated by an analytic scalar function. Note, if
\bm{G}
is generated by the analytic scalar function
G
, then without loss of generality we can assume the scalar function
G
has an infinite Taylor series expansion about zero,

G(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{0}x^{i}.

Then, by Definition
1
, for
\bm{X}=\bm{Q}\operatorname{Diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\bm{Q}^{\top}
,

\bm{G}(\bm{X})=\bm{Q}\bm{G}(\bm{\Lambda})\bm{Q}^{\top}=\bm{Q}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\bm{\Lambda}^{i}\right)\bm{Q}^{\top}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\bm{X}^{i}.

Therefore, if we prove (
29
) holds for any matrix polynomial (
30
) of arbitrary degree, then it will hold true for any matrix function generated by an analytic scalar function. We now prove a few intermediate results which will allow us to prove Theorem
8
.

Lemma 9
.

If
\bm{G}(\bm{X})=\bm{X}^{n}
, then for
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}

\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=n-1}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}},

(31)

\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=n-2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=n-3}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}.

(32)

Proof.

We prove these identities by induction. Note,

\displaystyle(\bm{X}+\bm{H})\;

\displaystyle=\bm{X}+\bm{H},

\displaystyle(\bm{X}+\bm{H})^{2}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{2}+\bm{X}\bm{H}+\bm{H}\bm{X}+\bm{H}^{2},

\displaystyle(\bm{X}+\bm{H})^{3}

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{3}+\bm{X}^{2}\bm{H}+\bm{X}\bm{H}\bm{X}+\bm{X}\bm{H}^{2}+\bm{H}\bm{X}^{2}+\bm{H}\bm{X}\bm{H}+\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}+\bm{H}^{3},

from which we deduce:

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{1})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]

\displaystyle=\bm{H},\;\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}(\bm{X}^{1})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=0,

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{2})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]

\displaystyle=\bm{X}\bm{H}+\bm{H}\bm{X},\;\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}(\bm{X}^{2})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\bm{H}^{2},

\displaystyle\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{3})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{2}\bm{H}+\bm{X}\bm{H}\bm{X}+\bm{H}\bm{X}^{2},\;\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}(\bm{X}^{3})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}+\bm{H}\bm{X}\bm{H}+\bm{X}\bm{H}^{2}.

Thus, we have our base cases. Assume (
31
) and (
32
) hold for all
n
up to some
k\geq 3
. Then,

\displaystyle(\bm{X}+\bm{H})^{k+1}

\displaystyle=(\bm{X}+\bm{H})\left(\bm{X}^{k}+\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{k})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=(\bm{X}+\bm{H})\left(\bm{X}^{k}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{k+1}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\bm{H}\bm{X}^{k}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{2})

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{k+1}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{2}),

where the second equality follows from the inductive hypothesis. The final equality implies

\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{k+1})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}
proving (
31
) by induction. Similarly,

\displaystyle(\bm{X}+\bm{H})^{k+1}=(\bm{X}+\bm{H})\left(\bm{X}^{k}+\bm{D}(\bm{X}^{k})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]+\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}(\bm{X}^{k})(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=(\bm{X}+\bm{H})\hskip-3.61371pt\left(\bm{X}^{k}+\hskip-14.45377pt\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\hskip-10.84006pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\hskip-14.45377pt\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-2}\hskip-10.84006pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\hskip-18.06749pt\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=k-3}\hskip-18.06749pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{X}^{k+1}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 83.11005pt+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=k-3}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{3}).

Note,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-2}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}}=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-2}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}},

and

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=k-3}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,1}\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-2}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}}=\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=k-2}\hskip-14.45377pt\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}.

Therefore,

(\bm{X}+\bm{H})^{k+1}=\bm{X}^{k+1}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=k-1}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}\\
\hskip-43.36243pt+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=k-2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{H}^{3}),

(33)

and (
32
) follows by induction.
∎

Lemma

by the linearity of differential operators leads in a straightforward fashion to the following corollary which provides the first and second-order directional derivatives of all finite matrix polynomials.

Corollary 10
.

If
\bm{G}(\bm{X})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}\bm{X}^{n}
, then,

\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}},

\frac{1}{2}\bm{D}^{2}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H},\bm{H}]=\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}\left(\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}^{2}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}+\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=i-3}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}+1}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}}\right).

In the next two lemmas, we compute the gradient and Hessian of the composition
F\circ\bm{G}
which we will utilize in our proof of Theorem
8
.

Lemma 11
.

If
F
is as defined in Theorem
8
and
\bm{G}
is as defined in (
30
), then,

\bm{\nabla}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}.

Proof.

By the first chain rule identity in Theorem
4
and Corollary
10
,

\displaystyle D(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]

\displaystyle=D(F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg]

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X})),\;\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}\bigg\langle\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X})),a_{i}\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}\bigg\langle a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top},\bm{H}\bigg\rangle,

and the last equality follows since
\langle\bm{A},\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}}\bm{H}\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}}\rangle=\langle(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{A}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top},\bm{H}\rangle
for all
\bm{A},\bm{X},\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. ∎

Lemma 12
.

If
F
is as defined in Theorem
8
and
\bm{G}
is as defined in (
30

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}^{2}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=i-2}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{H}^{\top}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}})^{\top}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}+\ell_{3}=i-2}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}\bm{H}^{\top}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{3}})^{\top}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg](\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}.

(34)

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}^{2}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\bm{\nabla}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})\right)\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left\{(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})^{\ell_{1}}\right\}^{\top}\right]\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left\{(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})^{\ell_{2}}\right\}^{\top}\right]\bigg|_{t=0}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}(\bm{X}^{\ell_{1}})^{\top}\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg](\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}.

\frac{d}{dt}\left[\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}+t\bm{H}))\right]\bigg|_{t=0}=\bm{\nabla}^{2}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))\bigg[\bm{D}\bm{G}(\bm{X})[\bm{H}]\bigg].

Investigating the first term, using Lemma

to compute the scalar derivative, we see,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left\{(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})^{\ell_{1}}\right\}^{\top}\right]\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-1}a_{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left\{(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})^{\ell_{1}}\right\}^{\top}\right]\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-2}a_{i}\frac{d}{dt}\left[\left\{(\bm{X}+t\bm{H})^{\ell_{1}+1}\right\}^{\top}\right]\bigg|_{t=0}\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{n}\;\;\sum_{\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}=i-2}a_{i}\left(\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}=\ell_{1}}(\bm{X}^{k_{1}})^{\top}\bm{H}^{\top}(\bm{X}^{k_{2}})^{\top}\right)\bm{\nabla}F(\bm{G}(\bm{X}))(\bm{X}^{\ell_{2}})^{\top}

Rewriting the second term in a similar fashion we obtain (

Proof of Theorem

\langle\bm{\nabla}^{2}(F\circ\bm{G})(\bm{X})[\bm{H}],\bm{H}\rangle\\

Theorem 13

Let

\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
with dimension

be a basis for

\bm{B}
to be the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors of

\bm{B}:=\left[\bm{b}^{1}\;|\;\ldots\;|\;\bm{b}^{d}\right].

By the orthogonality of the basis, we see
\bm{B}^{\top}\bm{B}=\bm{I}_{d\times d}
and clearly
\text{Range}(\bm{B})=V
by the definition of a basis since,

\bm{B}\bm{z}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}\bm{b}^{i}.

∎

We present a simple example for such a matrix

Appendix D: Alternative Fair TME Formulations

Another approach to solving the Fair TME model (
22

and rewrite (

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\;\;\mu_{1}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{E}_{j}(e^{\bm{R}})+\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\;\sum_{j=i+1}^{r}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}(e^{\bm{R}})-\mathcal{E}_{j}(e^{\bm{R}})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\bm{R}\in\mathcal{S}^{p\times p}.

Both reformulations of (

), (

) and (

), solve the Fair TME model and each has its respective pros and cons. We note (
