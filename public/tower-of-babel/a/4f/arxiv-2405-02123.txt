2 × 2 2\times 2 hyperbolic systems of conservation laws in classes of functions of bounded p p -variation
Olivier Glass

Abstract
In this paper, we consider 2 × 2 2\times 2 hyperbolic systems of conservation laws in one space dimension with characteristic fields satisfying a condition that encompasses genuine nonlinearity and linear degeneracy as well as intermediate cases, namely, with standard notations, r i ⋅ ∇ λ i ≥ 0 r_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{i}\geq 0 . We prove the existence of entropy solutions in the fractional B ​ V BV spaces 𝒲 p ​ ( ℝ ) \mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}) of functions of bounded p p -variation, p ∈ [ 1 , 3 2 ] p\in\left[1,\frac{3}{2}\right] , for small initial data.



Keywords.
Hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, fractional
BV
spaces, entropy solutions, Glimm’s theorem.

1
Introduction

The existence of solutions to the initial value problem for hyperbolic systems of conservation laws
under standard conditions on the characteristic fields was established in the celebrated paper
by James Glimm
[
12
]
in 1965, when the initial data is a function of small total variation.
The space
BV(\mathbb{R})
is of central importance in Glimm’s result, and remains up to now
a standard space for many results on one-dimensional hyperbolic systems of conservation laws,
even if for particular genuinely nonlinear systems – typically
2\times 2
systems –
important results were obtained in the space
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

[
2
,
8
,
13
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the existence theory for entropy solutions of one-dimensional

2\times 2
hyperbolic systems of conservation laws in the Wiener class
{\mathcal{W}}_{p}(\mathbb{R})

of functions of bounded
p
-variation (also denoted
BV_{s}(\mathbb{R})
,
s=1/p
), which can be seen
as an intermediate space between
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
BV(\mathbb{R})

(although it is not an interpolated space between the two in the strict sense).

We recall that one-dimensional
2\times 2
hyperbolic systems of conservation laws
are partial differential equations of the following form:

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}=0\ \text{ for }\ (t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R},

(1.1)

where the unknown is
u=u(t,x):\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

and the flux function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, defined on some open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
satisfies the strict hyperbolicity condition that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}(u)

of the Jacobian matrix
Df(u)
are real and distinct:

\lambda_{1}(u)<\lambda_{2}(u).

(1.2)

Let us denote
r_{1}(u),r_{2}(u)
eigenvectors associated to these eigenvalues.
We will use an assumption on characteristic fields
(\lambda_{i},r_{i})
that is slightly broader
than the standard genuine nonlinearity or linear degeneracy.
Precisely, we will assume the property

r_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{i}\geq 0\text{ in }\Omega,

(
M
)

and we will refer to characteristic fields satisfying this condition as
monotone.

Naturally, fields satisfying
r_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{i}\leq 0
are also monotone,
since it suffices to reverse the direction of
r_{i}
.
Of course, this condition encompasses Lax’s conditions of genuine nonlinearity
(which means
r_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{i}>0
in
\Omega
and is further abridged as GNL)
and linear degeneracy (which means
r_{i}\cdot\nabla\lambda_{i}=0
in
\Omega

and is further abridged as LD).
We refer to
[
6
,
9
]
for general references on the topic of hyperbolic systems of conservation laws.

Our goal in this paper is to build entropy solutions
for such systems in the space of functions with finite
p
-variation class
{\mathcal{W}}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
.
We recall that the space
BV(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
can be defined as the space of functions
v:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

for which

TV(v):=\sup_{N}\sup_{x_{1}<\dots<x_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|v(x_{k+1})-v(x_{k})|<+\infty.

(1.3)

For
1\leq p<+\infty
, the
p
-variation of
v
is defined by analogy by

V_{p}(v):=\left(\sup_{N}\sup_{x_{1}<\dots<x_{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|v(x_{k+1})-v(x_{k})|^{p}\right)^{1/p},

(1.4)

and the class
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
of functions of finite
p
-variation is defined
as the set of functions
v:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for which
V_{p}(u)<+\infty
.
This space is naturally endowed with the norm:

\|v\|_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}:=\|v\|_{\infty}+V_{p}(v).

(1.5)

Let us briefly recall the definition of entropy solutions of (
1.1
).
An entropy couple for a hyperbolic system of conservation laws (
1.1
)
is defined as a couple of regular functions
(\eta,q):\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying:

\forall u\in\Omega,\ \ D\eta(u)\cdot Df(u)=Dq(u).

(1.6)

Then we use the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A function
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}))\cap C^{\frac{1}{p}}([0,T];L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}))
is called an
entropy solution of (
1.1
) when it is a weak solution in the sense of distributions
and when for any entropy couple
(\eta,q)
, with
\eta
convex, one has in the
sense of distributions

\eta(u)_{t}+q(u)_{x}\leq 0,

(1.7)

that is, for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{D}}((0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R})
with
\varphi\geq 0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\big(\eta(u(t,x))\varphi_{t}(t,x)+q(u(t,x))\varphi_{x}(t,x)\big)\,dx\,dt\geq 0.

(1.8)

In this paper, we prove the existence of entropy solutions of
2\times 2
systems (
1.1
)
in the class
\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 1
.

Consider a strictly hyperbolic
2\times 2
system (
1.1
) satisfying (
M
).
Let
p\in\left[1,\frac{3}{2}\right]
.
Let
\overline{u}\in\Omega
.
There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for any
u_{0}\in\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R})
such that

\|u_{0}-\overline{u}\|_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}<\varepsilon_{0},

(1.9)

there exists
u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{W}_{p}(\mathbb{R}))\cap C^{1/p}([0,+\infty);L^{p}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}))

an entropy solution of (
1.1
) with initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
,
satisfying moreover that for all
s,t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle V_{p}(u(t,\cdot))^{p}\leq V_{p}(u_{0})^{p}+\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u_{0})^{2p},

(1.10)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u(t,x)-u(s,x)|^{p}\,dx\leq\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u_{0})^{p}\,|t-s|.

(1.11)

Remark 1
.

A straightforward adaptation of an example by Young
[
22
]
proves
that one cannot hope for such a result to hold for
n\times n
hyperbolic systems,
n\geq 3
, at least
in the linearly degenerate case.

Remark 2
.

The proof gives actually a slightly more precise picture of (
1.10
), at least at the discrete level of
wave-front tracking approximations (as described below).
Indeed, the total production of new waves (created by interactions of waves of the same characteristic family),
measured by the total sum of their initial strengths in absolute value, is of order
\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u_{0})^{2p}
.
This somehow expresses that new waves are bounded in classical total variation.

These remarks are more precisely justified in Section
5
.

We now discuss several aspects of our result.

Important differences between
TV
and
V_{p}
.

First, let us emphasize some key differences between the usual total variation and the
p
-variation for
p>1
.
To illustrate those differences, we will consider a piecewise constant function
f

with successive jumps
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
located at
x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}
.
We observe that the
p
-variation is:

•

non-local
,
in the sense that the change in
p
-variation when modifying a jump
a_{i}
does not merely
depend on
a_{i}
,

•

non-monotone
,
in the sense that reducing
a_{i}
in absolute value while maintaining its sign does not
necessarily decrease the
p
-variation
(taking for instance
(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(5,-2,5)
,
p=2
and changing
a_{2}
to
-1
),

•

non-commutative with respect to jumps
,
in the sense that permuting the
a_{i}
affects
V_{p}(f)
,
as seen for instance on the above example,

•

partially insensitive to cancellations
, in the sense that merging two successive
discontinuities with opposite monotonicity does not necessarily affect the
p
-variation,
as seen on the same example as above,

•

sensitive to a bounded but variable magnification of jumps
,
in the sense that multiplying jumps by variable bounded coefficients may change dramatically
the
p
-variation (comparing for instance the cases of
a_{k}=(-1)^{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,n
,
which gives a
p
-variation of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{1/p})
,
and of
a_{k}b_{k}
where
b_{k}=1+\frac{(-1)^{k}}{10}
,
which gives a
p
-variation of order
\mathcal{O}(n)
),

•

of fluctuating homogeneity with respect to changes in jumps
,
in the sense that for instance adding a
(n+1)
-th discontinuity of size
h
to the right
of
x_{n}
can add to
V_{p}(f)^{p}
a value ranging from
|h|^{p}
to
pV_{p}(f)^{p-1}|h|

at leading order.

The fact that the opposite holds in the case of the total variation is frequently used when considering

BV
solutions of (
1.1
).

Previous works.

As mentioned in the introduction, in the genuinely nonlinear case, the existence of entropy solutions
for
2\times 2
systems at this level of regularity is not new:
this was proved by Glimm and Lax
[
13
]
under an additional
assumption that was later removed by Bianchini, Colombo and Monti
[
2
]
; see also the
related work by Cheverry
[
8
]
.
In this context, Theorem
1
should be considered as a result of propagation of regularity
in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
spaces (even if, as far as we know, uniqueness is not established for solutions of this regularity).
These results heavily rely on genuine nonlinearity and the associated effect
of decay of positive waves.
The result here relies on a completely different aspect of those systems, namely the weaker coupling property
of
2\times 2
systems.
This explains why our result is valid under the broader assumption (
M
) which allows
all sort of degeneracy of the characteristic speed.

Hyperbolic conservation laws in the context of Wiener spaces have been considered in different references
in the last ten years.
Let us first mention references in the case of scalar conservation laws.
Up to our knowledge, Bourdarias, Gisclon and Junca
[
4
]
were the first to consider

BV_{s}
spaces in the context of conservation laws: they prove in particular the decay of
TV_{s}
for solutions of
scalar conservation laws and study the regularizing effect for convex fluxes.
A regularity result for general fluxes, relying on the more general notion of
\Phi
-variation,
was obtained by Marconi
[
18
]
.
The notion of
\Phi
-variation was also used in Jenssen-Ridder
[
15
]
where precise time estimates
on the solutions are given.
Let us also mention other recent studies in the field:
Ghoshal-Guelmame-Jana-Junca
[
11
]
and Guelmame-Billel-Junca-Clamond
[
14
]
.

Concerning systems, there are fewer references.
In Bourdarias-Gisclon-Junca-Peng
[
5
]
, the authors consider a
2\times 2
triangular system related to chromatography,
composed of an uncoupled scalar conservation law and a transport equation; they study existence in
BV_{s}\times L^{\infty}
.
The reference Bourdarias-Choudhury-Guelmame-Junca
[
3
]
considers more general
2\times 2

systems with this triangular structure.

All of the above references concerning scalar conservation laws or systems of conservation laws in
BV_{s}

have a feature in common: they consider equations that are
p
-variation diminishing

in the field that is measured in
BV_{s}
.
This is a natural property for scalar conservation laws (and in the discrete case this can be justified
by Proposition
2.1
–
4
below), but can only apply to very particular systems.
The present paper is, up to our knowledge, the first to consider the case of general
2\times 2
systems
where interactions can increase the
p
-variation,
and to measure this increase of
p
-variation through waves interactions, whether by amplification effect in the case of
interactions of waves of different families, or by production of new waves in the case of interactions of waves
of the same family.

Ideas of proof.

To describe the first difficulty met when considering systems in
BV_{s}
, let us start with a simple example.
Consider a
2\times 2
system where one characteristic field (say, with negative speed) is LD and the other one
(say, with positive speed) is GNL.
Consider as an initial state a shock of family
2
on the left, and then a family of small oscillating
contact discontinuities of family
1
, giving a small
p
-variation, but a large total variation, on the right.
When interacting with the shock, the strength of these contact discontinuities will be amplified (or damped) after crossing
by a multiplicative factor (depending on the size of the shock and on the local states) which in a first approximation can be
considered constant.
This involves in general that the difference between the original strength of these contact discontinuities and their new one
will be
of large total variation
.
This has an important consequence: we will not be able to measure the differences in strength progressively
in time in absolute value.
In particular, one cannot hope to use Glimm’s approach in this situation, and we need to rely on

global-in-time
estimates, at least to understand the effect of interactions of waves of opposite families.

On the other hand, interactions of waves of the same family are responsible for the creation of new waves in the solution,
and for the complexity of the general wave pattern.
It seems quite difficult to approach these interactions with global-in-time estimates due to this pattern complexity,
and inter alia because the order (from left to right) of the newly created waves is difficult to predict;
as mentioned earlier this is quite relevant when considering the
p
-variation.

This leads to the following idea: to include in the measure of wave strengths a “preamplification factor”
that partially predicts their future strengths after interactions with waves of the opposite family, and to treat these
preamplified strengths by using a Glimm-type functional that measures their evolution through
interactions with waves of the same family.
(Actually, interactions of waves of opposite families will be considered as well, but this will concern
higher order corrections.)
In particular in the process the new waves will be estimated using absolute values, which eventually leads
to Remark
2
above.

Doing so, we face another difficulty: in our situation, we cannot use standard Glimm’s functional

\sum_{\text{all waves}}|\sigma_{\alpha}|+C\sum_{\alpha<\beta}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|,

(where
\sigma_{\alpha}
measures the jump across a wave
\alpha
)
because it is not uniformly bounded at initial time, since in general the initial data is not in
BV
.
A natural way to extend it would be to consider instead

\sum_{\text{all waves}}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}+C\sum_{\alpha<\beta}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p},

but:

–

the left term above does not control the
p
-variation,

–

the gain obtained by means of the right term at an interaction point is of order

|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p}
, which does not allow controlling errors of size

|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|^{k}
, whatever
k
.

Hence we introduce a functional that mimics Glimm’s one, but uses a notion of
nonlocal strength
, which when
measuring the strength of a wave
\alpha
does not only depend on
\alpha
, but is better adapted to the

p
-variation.
Since this Glimm-type functional relies on nonlocal, preamplified strengths, the evolution of this functional
is more intricate, and we will need a priori assumptions on
our (wave-front front-tracking) approximations to prove that it decays
(this part corresponds to “local-in-time estimates”).

Once this decay is obtained, we will have to prove that it indeed allows measuring the
p
-variation
of the approximation, that is, to remove the preamplification factors.
This is not straightforward, since as mentioned earlier, the
p
-variation is very sensitive to multiplication.
In particular, going back to our first example of a shock interacting with small oscillating contact discontinuities,
one has to ensure that the coefficients giving the amplification of these contact discontinuities across
the interaction do not vary too much.
(As we will see, this is connected to the so-called Love-Young inequality.)
Hence, we will also need a priori assumptions on the approximation in this part of the proof,
to measure the variation of interactions coefficients
along front lines
.
This is the part where the “global-in-time” estimates are operated.

Due to this need of a priori estimates, the proof takes the form of a large induction argument, where an a priori idea
of the
p
-variation of the solution, not only for given times but also along front-lines,
will allow getting a better estimate and go forward in time.

Finally, let us mention that time estimates in this case are not a direct consequence of the uniform in time

V_{p}
estimate that the above considerations allow obtaining.
We will need indeed a localized version of the estimates above to conclude.

2
Preliminary material

2.1
Functions of
p
-bounded variations

2.1.1
Elementary properties of the
p
-variation

Given a finite real sequence
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, we define its
p
-variation

that we denote
v_{p}(x)
as the following:

v_{p}(x):=\left(\sup_{k\leq n}\ \sup_{0=i_{0}<\dots<i_{k}=n}\ \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|x_{i_{j+1}}-x_{i_{j}+1}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

(2.1)

Correspondingly we define
maximal
p
-sum of
x
, which we denote
s_{p}(x)
, the version for partial sums,
that is:

s_{p}(x):=\left(\sup_{k\leq n}\ \sup_{0=i_{0}<\dots<i_{k}=n}\ \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\left|\sum_{\ell=i_{j}+1}^{i_{j+1}}x_{\ell}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}.

(2.2)

Clearly,

v_{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=s_{p}(x_{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}-x_{n-1}).

The connection with the
p
-variation of functions is straightforward:
given a step function
f
, its
p
-variation is given by

V_{p}(f)=s_{p}([f]_{i=1,\ldots,N}),

where
[f]_{1},\ldots,[f]_{N}
represent the jumps in
f
, from left to right.

Concerning
s_{p}
and
v_{p}
, we will use the following vocabulary.
In (
2.1
) or (
2.2
), we will refer to a family of indices
(i_{0},\ldots,i_{k})

satisfying
0=i_{0}<\dots<i_{k}=n
as a
partition
,
and in such a partition we will call a set of indices
\{i_{j}+1,\ldots,i_{j+1}\}

for some
j\in\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
a
component
of this partition.
We will say that such a partition is optimal when it achieves the supremum
in (
2.1
) or (
2.2
).

In the next statements, we give a few elementary properties of
s_{p}
and
v_{p}
.

Proposition 2.1

(Elementary properties of
s_{p}
and
v_{p}
)
.

1.

Let
1\leq p\leq p^{\prime}
and
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
Then
s_{p^{\prime}}(a)\leq s_{p}(a)
and
v_{p^{\prime}}(a)\leq v_{p}(a)
.

2.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
and
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
Then
s_{p}(a+b)\leq s_{p}(a)+s_{p}(b)
and
v_{p}(a+b)\leq v_{p}(a)+v_{p}(b)
.

3.

For some
C>0
depending only on
p
the following holds.
Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
and
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
Then
s^{p}_{p}(a+b)\leq s^{p}_{p}(a)+Cs_{p}(a)^{p-1}s_{p}(b)+2s^{p}_{p}(b)
.
In particular, if
s_{p}(b)\leq\kappa s_{p}(a)
,
then
s^{p}_{p}(a+b)\leq s^{p}_{p}(a)+(C+2\kappa^{p-1})s_{p}(a)^{p-1}s_{p}(b)
.

4.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
For
j=1,\ldots,k-1
, call
(b_{i}^{j})_{i=1\ldots k-1}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{j-1},a_{j}+a_{j+1},a_{j+2},\ldots,a_{k})
.
Then
s_{p}(b^{j})\leq s_{p}(a)
in all cases and
s_{p}(b^{j})=s_{p}(a)
when
a_{j}
and
a_{j+1}
have the same sign
(in particular swapping
a_{j}
and
a_{j+1}
does not change
s_{p}(a)
in this case).

5.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
and
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots k^{\prime}}
.
Denote
a\diamond b:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k^{\prime}})
.
Then we have
s_{p}(a)^{p}+s_{p}(b)^{p}\leq s_{p}(a\diamond b)^{p}
.

6.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
and
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots k^{\prime}}
.
Let
c=(c_{i})_{i=1\ldots k+k^{\prime}}
an interleaved sequence of
a
and
b
, that is,
a sequence whose elements are the collection of all
a_{i}
and
b_{i}

in such a way that the order of elements of
a
and
b
is preserved.
Then
v_{p}(a)\leq v_{p}(c)
and
|s_{p}(a)-s_{p}(b)|\leq s_{p}(c)\leq s_{p}(a)+s_{p}(b)
.

7.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
Consider
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots k^{\prime}}
, whose first terms are
a_{1}
repeated an arbitrary number of times,
then
a_{2}
repeated an arbitrary number of times, …, until
a_{k}
repeated an arbitrary number of times.
Then
v_{p}(b)\leq v_{p}(a)
.

8.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
.
Then
v_{p}(0,a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\leq|a_{1}|+v_{p}(a)
and
v_{p}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},0)\leq|a_{k}|+v_{p}(a)
.

9.

Let
(-c,c)
an interval in
\mathbb{R}
,
f\in W^{1,\infty}(-c,c)

and
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}\in(-c,c)^{k}
.
Then

v_{p}\left(f(a_{i})\right)\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}v_{p}(a).

10.

Consider
n
sequences
a^{j}=(a^{j}_{i})_{i=1\ldots k}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
,
and let
c_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}a^{k}_{i}
.
Then

v_{p}\left(c_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(v_{p}(a^{j})\prod_{k\neq j}\|a^{k}\|_{\infty}\right).

Proposition 2.2

(Sensitivity of
s_{p}
with respect to endpoint values)
.

1.

Let
n\geq 1
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}
.
Then the map
t\mapsto s_{p}^{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},t)
is convex, decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{-}

and increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

2.

Let
n\geq 1
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+1}
in
\mathbb{R}
. Then

s_{p}^{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})-s_{p}^{p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}p|x_{n}|^{p-1}|x_{n+1}|\ \text{ if }x_{n+1}\text{ and }x_{n}\text{ have the same sign,}\\
|x_{n+1}|^{p}\ \text{ in all cases.}\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

3.

The equivalent properties are valid mutatis mutandis
when varying the leftmost variable
x_{1}
rather than the rightmost one
x_{n+1}
.

2.1.2
The discrete Love-Young inequality and its consequence

A central tool in this paper is the following discrete Love-Young inequality,
see L.C. Young
[
21
, (5.1)]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
p,q\geq 1
such that

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1.

(2.4)

Consider the
2n
real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. Then one has

\left|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}x_{i}y_{j}\right|\leq\left(1+\zeta\left(\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}\right)\right)s_{p}(x)s_{p}(y),

(2.5)

where

\zeta(s):=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n^{s}}
is Riemann’s zeta function.

The importance of this inequality stems from the fact that it takes cancellations into account;
it would not hold with absolute values inside the sum.
It was introduced in
[
21
]
to generalize the Stieltjes integral.

We will in particular use the following consequence of the discrete Love-Young inequality.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
a=(a_{i})_{i=1\ldots n}
and
b=(b_{i})_{i=1\ldots n}
. Call
ab:=(a_{1}b_{1},\ldots,a_{n}b_{n})
.
Suppose that
p<2
.
Then for some constant
C>0
depending only on
p
,

s_{p}(ab)\leq Cs_{p}(a)\big(\|b\|_{\infty}+v_{p}(b)\big).

(2.6)

Corollary
2.1
is proved in Subsection
6.2
.

2.2
Monotone characteristic fields and interaction estimates

In this paragraph, we consider monotone characteristic fields, such as described in the introduction.
We first see how the wave curves can be constructed as in the genuinely nonlinear
and linearly degenerate cases.
Then we introduce interaction estimates that are slightly more precise
than the usual ones (but restricted to
2\times 2
systems.)

2.2.1
Wave curves for monotone characteristic fields

We recall that Lax’s
i
-th wave curve starting at
u_{0}\in\Omega
are defined
as the set of all (right) states that can be connected to
u_{0}
by means of (a succession of) elementary waves
(rarefaction/contact discontinuity/admissible shock) of that family.
Due to
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
, these are uniquely defined, even without any other
assumption than strict hyperbolicity.

We first recall that for linearly degenerate fields (
\nabla\lambda_{i}\cdot r_{i}=0
),
the Hugoniot locus coincides with the rarefaction curve, and they both correspond to contact discontinuities.
It follows that in that case, one can define the Lax curve in the same way
as in the genuinely nonlinear case (
\nabla\lambda_{i}\cdot r_{i}>0
),
that is, by choosing the rarefaction curve for
s\geq 0
and the shock curve for
s<0
.

The main result of this paragraph is that this remains true under the broader
condition (
M
).
Of course, the proof of existence of Lax curve under this assumption is not new,
since as mentioned before no condition is needed in addition to strict hyperbolicity.
This part does not depend on the fact that the system is
2\times 2
, so the next statement is written
in the general
n\times n
case.

We parameterize here the various
i
-curves
\Gamma
starting at
u_{0}
with

s=\ell_{i}(u_{0})\cdot(\Gamma(s)-u_{0}),

(2.7)

where
(\ell_{1}(u),\ldots,\ell_{n}(u))
is the dual basis of
(r_{1}(u),\ldots,r_{n}(u))
.

We denote
\mathcal{R}_{i}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(s,u_{0})
and
\mathcal{S}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(s,u_{0})

the
i
-th rarefaction curve and the
i
-th Hugoniot locus starting from
u_{0}
.
These are considered in both
s>0
and
s<0
cases.
We have the following elementary statement.

Proposition 2.5
.

Consider a strictly hyperbolic
n\times n
system. Under Assumption (
M
)
on the
i
-th characteristic field, given
u_{0}\in\Omega
,
Lax’s
i
-th curve starting from
u_{0}
is given by:

\mathcal{T}_{i}(s;u_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\mathcal{R}_{i}(s;u_{0})\text{ if }\ s\geq 0,\\
\mathcal{S}_{i}(s;u_{0})\text{ if }\ s\leq 0.\end{array}\right.

(2.8)

The proof of Proposition
2.5
is postponed to Subsection
6.3
.
Though this is rather classical, we cannot apply the standard proof for GNL case
due to the lack of usable Taylor series expansion in this case.

2.2.2
The Riemann problem and its variants

From now on, we go back to
2\times 2
systems, and work with Riemann coordinates.
More precisely, since the system under view is
2\times 2
, normalizing
r_{1}
and
r_{2}

so that
[r_{1},r_{2}]=0
, we may introduce near the base point
\overline{u}
coordinates
w=(w_{1},w_{2})=w[u]

defined by

\frac{\partial u}{\partial w_{i}}=r_{i}(u),\ \text{ for }i=1,2.

(2.9)

Accordingly, we can use the jump of
w_{1}
(respectively of
w_{2}
) to parameterize the
1
-wave curves
(resp. the
2
-wave curves). In particular, the rarefaction curves take a simple form in these coordinates:
for
w^{0}=(w_{1}^{0},w_{2}^{0})
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{1}(s;w^{0})=(w_{1}^{0}+s,w_{2}^{0})\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{R}_{2}(s;w^{0})=(w_{1}^{0},w_{2}^{0}+s).

(2.10)

Note that we will also use the rarefaction curve for
negative
waves.
We will refer to a wave between
u_{l}
and
u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma;u_{l})
,
\sigma<0

as a
compression wave
.

On the other side, due to the classical (at least) second-order tangency
between rarefaction curves and the Hugoniot locus, one can find smooth functions
\mathcal{D}_{i}
,
i=1,2

such that shock curves take the following form in Riemann coordinates:

\mathcal{S}_{1}(s;w^{0})=\big(w_{1}^{0}+s,w_{2}^{0}+s^{3}\mathcal{D}_{1}(s;w^{0})\big)\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{S}_{2}(s;w^{0})=(w_{1}^{0}+s^{3}\mathcal{D}_{2}(s;w^{0}),w_{2}^{0}+s).

(2.11)

Note that
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is also defined for positive values (still corresponding to the Hugoniot locus).

Since we are considering monotone characteristic fields, in accordance with Proposition
2.5
,
we can form Lax curves
\mathcal{T}_{i}
according to (
2.8
), which we parameterize by means
of the Riemann coordinates as above. Now in the sequel we will use
three
types of waves:

•

shock waves (SW) corresponding to
(u_{-},u_{+})
with

u_{+}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(\sigma,u_{-})
with
\sigma\leq 0
,

•

rarefaction waves (RW) corresponding to
(u_{-},u_{+})
with

u_{+}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma,u_{-})
with
\sigma\geq 0
,

•

compression waves (CW) corresponding to
(u_{-},u_{+})
with

u_{+}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma,u_{-})
with
\sigma\leq 0
.

All cases above include the possible case when the flux degenerates so that the wave
is actually a contact discontinuity, but we keep the above vocabulary (SW/RW/CW)
in that case.

With these three types of waves, we will use in the construction several variants of the Riemann problem.
All are gathered in the following statement.

Proposition 2.6
.

There exists a neighborhood
U
of
\overline{u}
and
c>0
such that the following holds.
Given
\mathcal{U}_{1}\in\{\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1}\}
and

\mathcal{U}_{2}\in\{\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{2}\}
, for any
u_{l},u_{r}\in U
,
there exists unique
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in(-c,c)
such that

u_{r}=\mathcal{U}_{2}(\sigma_{2};\mathcal{U}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l})),

(2.12)

with
\mathcal{U}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l})\in\Omega
.

The proof is standard in the case
\mathcal{U}_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{U}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{2}
,
and totally similar (simpler, even) in other cases, relying on the inverse mapping theorem.

2.2.3
Sharper interaction estimates

In what follows we will use interaction estimates that are stronger
(and of a slightly different form in the case of opposite families)
than classical Glimm’s estimates
[
12
]
,
relying in particular on the fact that the system is
2\times 2

and that we can use Riemann invariants to measure wave strengths.
A difference with classical interaction estimates
(besides the fact that we treat variants of the Riemann problem)
is that the error term is not the same for both outgoing waves.
Moreover, we treat all shocks in a similar fashion as what is done when
studying strong shocks (see e.g.
[
20
]
).

For that purpose we distinguish between interactions of waves of the same family
and interactions of waves of opposite families. Inside each of these propositions, we also consider
the possibility of a compression wave. We will refer to shock and rarefaction waves as
classical
waves
(as opposed to compression waves.)

We will use the following notation: given a wave
u_{+}=\mathcal{U}_{i}(\sigma;u_{-})

with
\mathcal{U}_{i}\in\{\mathcal{T}_{i},\mathcal{R}_{i}\}
,
we define
\sigma_{*}
as
\sigma_{*}=0
unless
(u_{-},u_{+})
is a shock in which case
\sigma_{*}=\sigma
.
In other words,
\sigma_{*}=0
if
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
\sigma_{*}=\sigma_{-}

for
\mathcal{U}_{i}=\mathcal{T}_{i}
. The open set
U
and the constant
c>0
mentioned in the next statements are the ones
obtained in Proposition
2.6
.

Interactions of waves of opposite families.

It is classical that interactions of waves of opposite family yield a quadratic error term.
Here we will use that for
2\times 2
systems, when the waves are measured with Riemann invariants,
the error is actually of fourth order.
This was already noticed in
[
7
]
(see the proof of Lemma 2 therein)
in the case of GNL fields, in a form that is slightly different from ours.

Proposition 2.7

(Interaction of waves of opposite families)
.

There exist two functions
\mathcal{C}^{1}=\mathcal{C}^{1}(u_{m};s_{1},s_{2})

and
\mathcal{C}^{2}=\mathcal{C}^{2}(u_{m};s_{1},s_{2})
of Lipschitz class on
U\times(-c,c)^{2}
,
such that was follows is valid.

1.
(Classical waves)
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in
U
such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sigma_{2};u_{l})\ \text{ and }\ u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{m}).

Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sigma^{\prime}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1};u_{l}))
with

\sigma_{1}^{\prime}=\sigma_{1}\big(1+\mathcal{C}^{1}(u_{m};\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{2})_{*}^{3}\big)\ \text{ and }\ \sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\sigma_{2}\big(1+\mathcal{C}^{2}(u_{m};\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{1})_{*}^{3}\big).

(2.13)

2.
(Classical/compression waves).
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in
U
such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sigma_{2};u_{l})\ \text{ and }\ u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{m}).

Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sigma^{\prime}_{2};\mathcal{R}_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1};u_{l}))
with

\sigma_{1}^{\prime}=\sigma_{1}\big(1+\mathcal{C}^{1}(u_{m};\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})(\sigma_{2})_{*}^{3}+\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma_{1}|^{2}(\sigma_{2})_{*}^{3}\big)\ \text{ and }\ \sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\sigma_{2},

(2.14)

and the equivalent holds when
u_{m}=\mathcal{R}_{2}(\sigma_{2};u_{l})\ \text{ and }\ u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{m})
.

3.
(Compression and rarefaction waves).
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in
U
such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{R}_{2}(\sigma_{2};u_{l})\ \text{ and }\ u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{m}).

Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{2}(\sigma_{2};\mathcal{R}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l}))
.

Of course, the above cases overlap a bit. Note that only the first two cases are somewhat new
(in particular in the fact that the functions
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
are Lipschitz).
See e.g. Bressan-Colombo
[
7
]
for a close statement.

Interactions of waves of the same family.

Here classical interactions estimates give an error term of third order (see again e.g.
[
7
]
).
Relying again on the fact that our system is
2\times 2
and that we measure wave strengths with Riemann invariants,
we can partially improve this order for the outgoing wave of the same family.

Proposition 2.8

(Interaction of waves of the same family)
.

Let
i\in\{1,2\}
.

1.
(Classical waves)
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in
U
such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{T}_{i}(\sigma_{i};u_{l})
and
u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i};u_{l})
.
Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l}))
with

\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma_{i}|^{3}|\sigma^{\prime}_{i}|^{3}(|\sigma_{i}|+|\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|)^{3}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{3-i}=\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma_{i}||\sigma^{\prime}_{i}|(|\sigma_{i}|+|\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|).

(2.15)

2.
(Shocks & compression waves).
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in
U
such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{T}_{i}(\sigma_{i};u_{l})
and
u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i};u_{m})
,
with
\sigma_{i}<0
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{i}<0

Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sigma_{2};\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l}))
with

\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}=\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma^{\prime}_{i}|^{3}(|\sigma_{i}|+|\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|)^{6}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{3-i}=\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma^{\prime}_{i}|(|\sigma_{i}|+|\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|)^{2}.

(2.16)

The equivalent is valid when
u_{m}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i};u_{l})
and

.

3.
(Compression and rarefaction waves).
Suppose that
u_{l},u_{m},u_{r}
are states in

such that

u_{m}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma_{i}^{\prime};u_{l})\ \text{ and }\ u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma_{i};u_{m})
.
Then one has
u_{r}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{i}^{\prime};u_{l})
.

.

Notice the unusual

error term in (

).
This is due to the transformation of a compression wave in a shock wave in such a situation.

These propositions are proved in Subsection
6.4
.
We will call
C_{*}
a bound for functions
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
in Proposition
2.7

and for all “

” in Proposition

.

3
Main ingredients

Given
\overline{u}\in\Omega
, we first introduce
r>0
such that:

•

\overline{B}(\overline{u};r)
is included in the neighborhood
U
introduced in Proposition
2.6
,
so the different Riemann problems with states in
\overline{B}(\overline{u};r)
are solvable
with intermediate states in
\Omega
;

•

the intervals
\{\lambda_{1}(u),u\in B(\overline{u};r)\}

and
\{\lambda_{2}(u),u\in B(\overline{u};r)\}
are disjoint.

We strengthen a bit the last requirement as follows.
Translating the reference frame by a constant speed if necessary,
we can suppose without loss of generality that for constants
\Lambda_{1}^{max}<0

and
\Lambda_{2}^{min}>0
, one has

\forall u\in\overline{B}(\overline{u};r),\ \ \lambda_{1}(u)\leq\Lambda_{1}^{max}<0<\Lambda_{2}^{min}\leq\lambda_{2}(u).

(3.1)

It suffices indeed to consider the modified flux
\widetilde{f}(u)=f(u)-\lambda u
for proper
\lambda
;
then entropy solutions
u(t,x)
of (
1.1
) with flux
f
correspond in a one-to-one manner
to entropy solutions
\widetilde{u}(t,x):=u(t,x+\lambda t)
of (
1.1
) with flux
\widetilde{f}
.
The requirement (
3.1
) is not essential;
it simplifies a bit the descriptions (and the figures) below.

As a first condition on
\varepsilon_{0}
in (
1.9
), we suppose that

u_{0}(\mathbb{R})\subset B(\overline{u},r/2)\ \text{ and }\ V_{p}(u_{0})\leq\frac{1}{2},

(3.2)

for any
u_{0}
fulfilling (
1.9
).

Above and all along this paper, we measure the
p
-variation of a vector-valued function
u:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

along the Riemann coordinates, that is,

V_{p}(u)^{p}:=V_{p}(w_{1}(u))^{p}+V_{p}(w_{2}(u))^{p}.

(3.3)

We now describe the main ingredients of the proof in the next paragraphs.

3.1
Wave front-tracking algorithm

In this section, we describe a front-tracking algorithm which is a variant of the one of Bressan-Colombo
[
7
]
;
we will use it to construct approximations of a solution.
Given a threshold
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
(where
\nu_{0}>0
is chosen small enough), we construct a piecewise constant function
on a polygonal subdivision of
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, where each line of discontinuity corresponds to a simple
1
or
2
-wave.

Convention on the propagation speed.

In what follows, all discontinuities will at first approximation travel at shock speed as defined below.
However, we will further modify a bit this speed.

•

For a wave of family
i
separating
u_{-}
and
u_{+}
(either a shock, a rarefaction wave
or a compression wave), the shock speed
\overline{\lambda}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+})
is defined as

\overline{\lambda}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+}):=\frac{\ell_{i}(u_{-})\cdot\left(f(u^{+})-f(u^{-})\right)}{\ell_{i}(u_{-})\cdot\left(u^{+}-u^{-}\right)},

(3.4)

which is consistent with (
6.5
) below and exact in the case of a shock.

•

All waves will travel by default at
modified
shock speed, that is, for a wave of family
i
separating

u_{-}
and
u_{+}
, at the speed
\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+})
defined as

\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+})=\overline{\lambda}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+})+\nu(w_{i}[u_{+}]-w_{i}[\overline{u}]).

(3.5)

This small modification is motivated by the possible local linear degeneracy of the characteristic speed.
This is a way to “genuinely nonlinearize” the characteristic speed in order to treat all waves
in a unified manner.
This will simplify a bit the forthcoming analysis.

Taking
\nu_{0}
small enough, and modifying
\Lambda_{1}^{max}
and
\Lambda_{2}^{min}
if necessary,
we can ensure that the modified shock speed satisfies, for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
,

\forall u_{-},u_{+}\in\overline{B}(\overline{u};r),\ \ \overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{1}(u_{-},u_{+})\leq\Lambda_{1}^{max}<0\ \text{ if }\ (u_{-},u_{+})\ \text{ is a }1\text{-wave},\\
\ \text{ and }\ 0<\Lambda_{2}^{min}\leq\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{2}(u_{-},u_{+})\ \text{ if }\ (u_{-},u_{+})\ \text{ is a }2\text{-wave}.

(3.6)

We will possibly modify these speeds again by a small amount in the next algorithm.

Construction of front-tracking approximations.

The construction of the approximations is a follows.

1.

(Approximation of the initial state.)

According to Proposition
2.4
we first introduce
a family of piecewise constant approximations
(u^{\nu}_{0})_{\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})}
satisfying

\forall\nu\in(0,\nu_{0}),\ V_{p}(u^{\nu}_{0})\leq V_{p}(u_{0}),\ \|u^{\nu}_{0}-\overline{u}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u_{0}-\overline{u}\|_{\infty}\ \text{ and }\ u^{\nu}_{0}\longrightarrow u_{0}\ \text{ in }\ L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})\text{ as }\nu\rightarrow 0^{+}.

(3.7)

For the rest of the construction,
\nu
is fixed in
(0,\nu_{0})
.

2.

(Initial solver.)
At each discontinuity
x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}
of
u^{\nu}_{0}

we solve the corresponding (standard) Riemann problem
(u^{\nu}_{0}(x_{i}^{-}),u^{\nu}_{0}(x_{i}^{+}))
.
We let each outgoing shock wave travel at modified shock speed, and we approximate outgoing rarefaction waves
by rarefaction fans as follows: given an
i
-rarefaction wave between

\omega_{-}
and
\omega_{+}=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\sigma,\omega_{-})
,
\sigma>0
, we consider the states:

\omega^{0}:=\omega_{-},\ \ \omega^{k}:=\mathcal{R}_{i}\left(\frac{k}{m},\omega_{-}\right)\text{ for }k=1,\ldots,m:=\left\lfloor\frac{\sigma}{\nu}\right\rfloor,\ \ \omega^{m+1}:=\omega_{+},

(3.8)

and replace the rarefaction wave
(\omega_{-},\omega_{+})
by the fan given by the states
\omega^{0},\ldots,\omega^{m+1}

separated by straight lines traveling at modified shock speed
\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(\omega^{k},\omega^{k+1})
.
Note that due to monotonicity of the characteristic field, one has

\overline{\lambda}_{i}(\omega^{k},\omega^{k+1})\geq\overline{\lambda}_{i}(\omega^{k-1},\omega^{k})

(see e.g. (
6.9
)).
Hence the convention (
3.5
) ensures that

\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(\omega^{k},\omega^{k+1})>\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(\omega^{k-1},\omega^{k})
.

If necessary, we further modify the speeds to ensure that no triplets
of such discontinuities meet at the same point and that all interaction times are distinct.
Doing so, we can moreover require that no discontinuities meet unless they would have met without this modification anyway,
that is, if two discontinuities
(u_{l},u_{m})
(on the left) and
(u_{m},u_{r})
(on the right),
then the original (modified shock) speed of
(u_{l},u_{m})
is strictly larger than the one of
(u_{m},u_{r})
.
To obtain this result, we accelerate by a small amount the leftmost involved front, in such a way that

|s-\overline{\lambda}^{\nu}_{i}(u_{-},u_{+})|\leq\nu|u_{+}-u_{-}|.

(3.9)

3.

(Solvers at interaction points.)

We call discontinuity lines
fronts
and points where two fronts meet
interaction points
.
At each interaction point, we consider the interaction of two fronts separating the states

(u_{l},u_{m})
(on the left) and
(u_{m},u_{r})
(on the right).

The way we approximate the outgoing Riemann solution
depends on the nature of the incoming fronts.

•

For interactions of
opposite families

(that is, when the two incoming fronts correspond to waves of different characteristic family),
we approximate outgoing waves with
single fronts of the same nature as the incoming one
,
whether their strength is less than
\nu
or not. Since the two fronts can correspond to rarefaction,
compression or shock waves, we describe the situation as follows.
We write
u_{m}=\mathcal{U}_{a}(\sigma_{2};u_{l})
with

\mathcal{U}_{a}\in\left\{\mathcal{R}_{2},\mathcal{T}_{2}\right\}

and
u_{r}=\mathcal{U}_{b}(\sigma_{1};u_{m})
with

\mathcal{U}_{b}\in\left\{\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{1}\right\}
.
Then we solve the outgoing Riemann problem in the form

u_{r}=\mathcal{U}_{b}(\sigma_{2}^{\prime};\mathcal{U}_{a}(\sigma_{1}^{\prime};u_{l}))
,
and let

3.2
Time horizon, front lines and modified
p
-variation

For each
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
, we consider the corresponding front-tracking approximation
u^{\nu}

on
[0,T_{\nu})\times\mathbb{R}
.

Time horizon.
For the forthcoming analysis, it will be useful to introduce a
time horizon

T\in(0,T_{\nu})

so that we focus on the restriction on
u^{\nu}
in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.
Of course,
T
is intended to be arbitrarily close to
T_{\nu}
.
The interest of such a time horizon is that at first we cannot rule out the possibility of an unbounded number of fronts
or an accumulation of them as
t\rightarrow T_{\nu}^{-}
.
On
[0,T]
, we know that the number of fronts at play is finite.
The time horizon is central in the forthcoming Glimm-type functional.

Front lines.

An important feature of the above front-tracking algorithm (made possible by condition (
M
)),
is that each front
\alpha
existing at time
t
in
u^{\nu}
can be uniquely followed forward in time until time
T_{\nu}
,
by following the outgoing front of the same characteristic family at each interaction point.
There is indeed one (and only one) outgoing front of family
i
, when this family is represented among the incoming
fronts.
We call such a continuous succession of fronts a
front line
.
Note that each front line is unequivocally associated with a characteristic family.

When restricted to a time interval
[0,T]
, maximal front lines (for inclusion) end up at time
T

and start either at
t=0
, or at an interaction point of fronts of the same family.
We will refer to the point where the front line was created as the front line’s
birthplace
. When a front
\alpha^{\prime}
belongs to the front line of a front
\alpha
, we will say that
\alpha
is an

ancestor
of
\alpha^{\prime}
.

An important feature of these front lines is that:

•

Front lines of the same characteristic family cannot cross: when they meet, they merge for further times
(actually a good image would be that they propagate side by side, keeping their left/right order).

•

Front lines of different characteristic family cross at most once: this is due to the strict separation of
characteristic speeds.

Ordering and families of fronts.

For each family
i\in\{1,2\}
, and at each time
t<T_{\nu}
, we can gather all
i
-fronts that cross
\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}
.
These fronts are naturally ordered from left to right.
Since front lines of the same family cannot cross, the order (in the large sense) of two front lines of the same family remains
the same for all times

at which they both exist. Given a front
\alpha
existing at time
t
, we denote
x_{\alpha}(t)
its position at that time as well.
For two fronts existing at time
t
, we will abusively write
\alpha<\beta
for

x_{\alpha}(t)<x_{\beta}(t)
.

For
t\in[0,T]
, we call
\mathcal{A}^{1}(t)
(respectively
\mathcal{A}^{2}(t)
)
the set of fronts of family
1
(resp. of family

) existing at time
t
, and

\mathcal{A}(t):=\mathcal{A}^{1}(t)\cup\mathcal{A}^{2}(t)

.
Given a front

we will denote
\mathcal{A}^{k}_{<\gamma}(t)

(respectively
\mathcal{A}^{k}_{>\gamma}(t)

strictly to the left (resp. right) of

As mentioned in the introduction, we construct a functional resembling Glimm’s one,
but using data from the future of the solution. The functional is constructed as follows.

We let
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
and associate the approximation
u^{\nu}
.
First, we fix a time horizon
T\in(0,T_{\nu})
(again intended to be close to
T_{\nu}

All the forthcoming notions depend on this time horizon and on the restriction of
u^{\nu}
on
[0,T]
.
In many cases, the dependence on
T
,

and
\nu
will not be reflected on the notation,
for the sake of brevity.

Convention on interaction times.

For interaction times, we will correspondingly distinguish times
t^{-}
and
t^{+}
.
Given
\alpha
a front crossing
\{t\}\times\mathbb{R}
, we write
\sigma_{\alpha}(t)
for its strength at time
t
,
taken immediately
before
the interaction in case of ambiguity.

Future interaction coefficients.

Consider two fronts
\alpha
(of family
1
) and
\beta
(of family
2
) existing at time
t>0

such that their front lines cross between times
t
and
T
.
We can associate the corresponding unique interaction point, say, separating
u_{l}
,
u_{m}
and
u_{r}
,
the
2
-front on the left separating
u_{l}
from
u_{m}
belonging to the front line of
\beta

(say, of strength
\sigma_{2}
),
the
1
-front on the right separating
u_{m}
from
u_{r}
belonging to the front line of
\alpha

(say, of strength
\sigma_{1}
).
In that case, we define the
future interaction coefficients
of
\alpha
and
\beta
as follows:

\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\alpha\beta}=\mathcal{C}^{1}(u_{m};0,\sigma_{2})\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\beta}=\mathcal{C}^{2}(u_{m};\sigma_{1},0)

(3.13)

where the functions
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{2}
were introduced in Proposition
2.7
.
In the case where
\alpha
and
\beta
do no cross between times

and

, we simply put

\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\alpha\beta}=\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\beta}=0.

We emphasize that these coefficients are those appearing at the time of the interaction;
they are determined in advance and used in the following functionals for times
t

before the interaction actually occurs (hence the expression “oracle” functional below).
We also underline the fact that these coefficients depend not only on
t

but also on the time horizon
T
.

Note also that the coefficients
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{\alpha\beta}
are independent of the fronts
\alpha
(or
\beta
)
whose front lines have merged before the time of interaction.

Finally, we will write
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{\alpha\beta}
and
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{\beta\alpha}
interchangeably for two fronts
\alpha
and
\beta
of opposite families.

Let
t>0
. For a
1

\alpha

In the same way, for a

(3.15)

We will use the following notation for the preamplification factor:

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t)=\prod_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}_{<\alpha}^{2}(t)}(1+\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\alpha\beta}(\sigma_{\beta}(t))_{*}^{3})\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{M}_{\beta}(t)=\prod_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{>\beta}^{1}(t)}(1+\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\beta}(\sigma_{\alpha}(t))_{*}^{3}).

Notice that, while the interaction coefficients
\mathcal{C}^{i}_{\alpha\beta}
are computed
at the future time of the interaction, the strengths

and
\sigma_{\beta}(t)

are the ones at time

Remark also that due to the convention for future interaction coefficients,

We can now define various interaction potentials associated with

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{2}}\\

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{3}^{k}(t):=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}|{\sigma}_{\alpha}|^{3}(1+\delta_{CW}(\alpha)),

\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{V}^{1}+\mathcal{V}^{2},\ \ \mathcal{Q}_{1}:=\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{1}+\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{1},\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{Q}_{3}:=\mathcal{Q}_{3}^{1}+\mathcal{Q}_{3}^{2}.

We can now define the following functional:

\Upsilon(t):=\mathcal{V}(t)+C_{2}\big[C_{1}\mathcal{Q}_{1}(t)+\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t)+\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t)\big],

\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{-})\leq\delta\mathcal{V}(t)\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{A}^{k}}\mathfrak{s}^{k}_{r}(\mu)(t^{+})\\
=\delta\mathcal{V}(t)\,\mathcal{V}^{k}(t^{+})\leq 2\delta\mathcal{V}(t)\,\mathcal{V}^{k}(t^{-}).

The other sum can be treated likewise, so we finally find that

\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{-})=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|).

(4.31)

Evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

The functional
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is treated exactly in the same way as
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
with the only difference that a term is removed from
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
in the case of
an interaction of fronts of opposite families.
Hence following the same lines as for
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
, we find

\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{-})=-\mathfrak{s}^{k}_{r}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{s}^{k}_{l}(\beta)+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|).

Now using Proposition
2.2
–
2
and (
4.8
), we have that

\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\geq\alpha)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(>\alpha)\geq|\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}|^{p}\geq 2^{-p}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\leq\beta)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(<\beta)\geq|\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{p}\geq 2^{-p}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p}.

We finally get

\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{-})\leq-2^{-p}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}\,|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p}+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{2}(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|).

Evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
.

The case of
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
is simpler, because
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
involves only standard
(local, unampflified) forces.
Using Proposition
2.7
and the fact that at an interaction point for different families,
the outgoing fronts have the same nature as the incoming ones, one finds,
using simply (
6.1
) for
p=3
,

\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{-})=\mathcal{O}(1)\,|\sigma_{\alpha}(t^{-})|^{3}|\sigma_{\beta}(t^{-})|^{3}.

Conclusion.

By
H(\tau)
and Proposition
4.2
we have
\mathcal{V}(t)\lesssim V_{p}(u_{0})^{p}
.
Hence using
p<2
, and relying on the
-2^{-p}|\sigma_{\alpha}(t^{-})|^{p}|\sigma_{\beta}(t^{-})|^{p}
term in the decay
of
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
, we easily find that
\Upsilon(t^{+})-\Upsilon(t^{-})\leq 0
in that case,
provided that
V_{p}(u_{0})
is small enough. This does not require
C_{1}
or
C_{2}
to be large.

4.3.2
Interactions within a family: monotone case

We consider two fronts
\alpha
and
\beta
of the same family (say, of family
1
),
with
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}>0
, interacting at time
t
.
We rely on Proposition
2.8
to measure the strengths of new waves.
We denote
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
a term allowing to measure the error in the interaction estimate, namely

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}|\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\sigma_{\beta}|\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ are both classical},\\
|\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\sigma_{\beta}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ is a shock and }\beta\text{ is a CW},\\
|\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\sigma_{\beta}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ is a CW and }\beta\text{ is a shock},\\
|\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}^{2}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}^{2}\sigma_{\beta}|+|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}+|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ are both CW with }\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}\leq-2{\nu},\\
0\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ are both CW with }\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}>-2{\nu}.\end{array}\right.

Call
\alpha^{\prime}
the outgoing front of family
1
;
there can be several outgoing fronts in family
2
, which we call
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
(all of the same sign).
Proposition
2.8
establishes that

\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}\ \text{ and }\ \sigma_{\mu_{1}}+\ldots+\sigma_{\mu_{k}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

(4.32)

The preamplification factors of
\alpha
and
\beta
do not change across the interaction (and coincide),
while the preamplification factor for the outgoing
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
are bounded, so that

\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}+\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}\ \text{ and }\ \widehat{\sigma}_{\mu_{1}}+\ldots+\widehat{\sigma}_{\mu_{k}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

(4.33)

Evolution of
\mathcal{V}
.

As before,
\mathcal{V}
is modified due to (
4.33
), and also because of the change
in the preamplification factors of fronts other than
\alpha
and
\beta
.

Evolution of
\mathcal{V}^{1}
.

Due to the newly created
2
-wave at the interaction point,
the
1
-fronts
\gamma
to the right of
\beta
get an additional factor
(only non-trivial when
k=1
and
\mu_{1}
is a shock) given by

(1+\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma}(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3})
.
Denoting
\{\gamma^{1}_{1},\ldots,\gamma^{1}_{M}\}
the
1
-fronts at time
t^{-}
as in (
3.17
), with
\beta=\gamma_{k}
,
this corresponds to multiplying the sequence

(\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{k}},\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{k+1}}\ldots,\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{M}})

by the sequence

(1,\ldots,1,1+\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{k+1}}(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3},\ldots,1+\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{M}}(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3})
,
that is to say, to add to it

(0,\ldots,0,\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{k+1}}(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3}\,\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{\mu_{k+1}}},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{M}}(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3}\,\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{\mu_{K}}})
.
Using Corollary
2.1
we estimate the
p
-sum of this term by

(\sigma_{\mu_{1}})_{*}^{3}\,v_{p}(0,\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{k+1}},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{1}_{\mu_{1}\gamma^{1}_{M}})\,s_{p}(\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{\mu_{k+1}}},\ldots,\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma^{1}_{\mu_{K}}})\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}\,\mathcal{V}^{1}(t^{-})^{\frac{1}{p}},

where we used Lemma
4.1
(since
H(\tau)
is valid)
and Proposition
2.1
–
8
.

Taking additionally into account the error due to
\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha},\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}
being transformed
into
\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha^{\prime}}
, using Proposition
2.1
–
3
&
4
,
we find that

\mathcal{V}^{1}(t^{+})\leq\mathcal{V}^{1}(t^{-})+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}^{1}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

Evolution of
\mathcal{V}^{2}
.

We now consider the change in the preamplification factors for
2
-fronts.
For
\gamma
of family
2
such that
\gamma<\alpha
, the factor evolves according to

\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}(t^{+})=\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}(t^{-})\frac{(1+\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\gamma}(\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime}})_{*}^{3})}{(1+\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\gamma}(\sigma_{\alpha})_{*}^{3})(1+\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\gamma}(\sigma_{\beta})_{*}^{3})}.

(4.34)

The fact that the coefficient
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{\alpha\gamma}
is the same for all three expressions
is due to the fact that all these fronts correspond to the same front-line when interacting with
\gamma
.
Hence here we set

f(x)=\frac{(1+x(\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime}})_{*}^{3})}{(1+x(\sigma_{\alpha})_{*}^{3})(1+x(\sigma_{\beta})_{*}^{3})},\ \ x\in[-C_{*},C_{*}],

(4.35)

and observe that here we have in all cases

\|f\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=\mathcal{O}(1)\Big((\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime}})_{*}^{3}-(\sigma_{\alpha})_{*}^{3}-(\sigma_{\beta})_{*}^{3}\Big)=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
.
Hence we find

\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(t)\leq\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta},

where, as in Subsection
4.3.1
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(t)
denotes the value obtained after updating
the preamplification factors in
\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})
.

The rest of the change in
\mathcal{V}^{2}
is due the new
2
-waves
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k}
which have to be included in
\mathcal{V}^{2}
.
Due to (
4.33
), we have
s_{p}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
.
Using Proposition
2.1
–
3
, we deduce

\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{+})-\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(t)\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\Big(\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}^{2}(t)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{p}\Big).

Finally, using the rough estimate
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}\lesssim\mathcal{V}_{1}(t^{-})^{1/p}
,
we find that

\mathcal{V}(t^{+})\leq\mathcal{V}(t^{-})+\mathcal{O}(1)\widetilde{\mathcal{V}}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

Evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
.

The modification of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{1}
has three sources:

•

the various
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\gamma)
and
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\gamma)
are modified
(when
\gamma<\alpha
and
\gamma>\alpha
, respectively) due to the change
in the preamplification factors that have to take into account the appearing of a new wave in family
2
,

•

the terms
(\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)+\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\beta))\,\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\gamma)
are
replaced with
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha^{\prime})\,\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\gamma)
and in the other direction,
terms
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\gamma)\,(\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\alpha)+\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta))
are
replaced with
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\gamma)\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\alpha^{\prime})
,

•

the term
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)
is removed
from the sum.

The change of the preamplification factors were considered when we studied the evolution of
\mathcal{V}
.
Reasoning as for (
4.31
), using again
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}\lesssim\mathcal{V}_{1}(t^{-})^{1/p}
,
we deduce that the first error can be estimated by

\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
.

For what concerns the second error, Proposition
2.1
–
4

proves that there would be no change if we had
\sigma_{\alpha^{\prime}}=\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}
.
Hence, the error is only due to the additional
\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}
term.
This involves with Proposition
2.1
–
3
that this second error can be measured by

\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}\mathcal{V}^{1}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}
.

For what concerns the removed term
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)
,
due to Proposition
2.2
–
2
and
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}>0
,
we have that

\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\geq\alpha)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(>\alpha)\geq p|\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{p-1}|\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}|\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\leq\beta)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(<\beta)\geq|\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}|^{p},

so using (
4.8
), the corresponding gain is

-\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)\leq-p\,2^{-2p}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|^{2p-1}.

(4.36)

Estimating in the other way around

\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\geq\alpha)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(>\alpha)\geq|\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}|^{p}\ \text{ and }\ \mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)=\mathcal{V}^{1}(\leq\beta)-\mathcal{V}^{1}(<\beta)\geq p|\widehat{\sigma}_{\alpha}|^{p-1}|\widehat{\sigma}_{\beta}|,

we also have

-\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)\leq-p\,2^{-2p}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2p-1}|\sigma_{\beta}|.

The modification of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{2}
has two sources:

•

the various
\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{r}(\gamma)
and
\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{l}(\gamma)
are modified
(when
\gamma\leq\alpha
and
\gamma\geq\alpha
, respectively) due to the change
in the preamplification factors that have to take into account the merging of
\alpha
and
\beta
,

•

the new interaction terms
\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{r}(\mu_{i})\,\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{l}(\gamma)
and

\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{r}(\gamma)\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{l}(\mu_{i})
.

Reasoning as before, using the same function
f
as for (
4.35
), we see that the error corresponding
to the first source can be estimated by

\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})\Big(\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{p}\Big)
.
For the second source, we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\mu_{i}|\lesssim\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
which with
Proposition
2.1
–
1
,
3
yields

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathfrak{s}^{2}_{r}(\mu_{i})\lesssim\mathcal{V}^{2}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{p}
, so that we can estimate the second error as the first one.

In total, using again
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}\lesssim\mathcal{V}_{1}(t^{-})^{1/p}
, we deduce that

\mathcal{Q}_{1}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}(t^{-})\leq-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|^{2p-1}-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2p-1}|\sigma_{\beta}|+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

(4.37)

Evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

The functional
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is treated exactly in the same way as
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
with the only difference that no term is removed from
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
here.
Hence we get here

\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{-})=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}.

(4.38)

Evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
.

We denote

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ is a shock and }\beta\text{ is a CW},\\
|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ is a CW and }\beta\text{ is a shock},\\
|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}+|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\text{ if }\alpha\text{ and }\beta\text{ are CW with }\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}\leq-2{\nu},\\
0\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then one deduces from (
4.32
) and the fact that
\alpha^{\prime}
is a CW only if
\alpha

and
\beta
are both CW with
\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}>-2\nu
, that

\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{+})\leq\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{-})+3(1+\delta_{CW}(\alpha^{\prime}))|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|\big(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|\big)-\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}^{3}.

(4.39)

Conclusion.

For
C_{1}
large enough in (
3.24
), the decay

-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|^{2p-1}-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2p-1}|\sigma_{\beta}|

compensates for the second term in the right-hand side of (
4.39
).
(We can see here that
C_{1}
is only needed for
p=\frac{3}{2}
.)
Then under
H(\tau)
, if
V_{p}(u_{0})
is small enough, and taking
C_{2}
large enough
(which is only needed if
p=1
) the terms

-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|^{2p-1}-\frac{p\,2^{-2p}}{2}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{2p-1}|\sigma_{\beta}|

and

-\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}

compensate for all terms containing
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}
. Hence we also find in that case that
\Upsilon(t^{+})-\Upsilon(t^{-})\leq 0
.

4.3.3
Interactions within a family: non-monotone case

As in the previous paragraph, we consider again two fronts
\alpha
and
\beta

of the same family (say, of family
1
), interacting at time
t
,
with this time
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}<0
.

Estimate of
\mathcal{V}
.

The estimates on the evolution of the parts
\mathcal{V}
of the functional are similar
in this case as the ones of the monotone case.
Even, they are simpler since there is no transformation of a compression wave in a shock here, and the error
term is systematically

\mathcal{E}_{\alpha\beta}=|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|)
.
Hence we find

\mathcal{V}(t^{+})\leq\mathcal{V}(t^{-})+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|\left(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|\right).

The evolution of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
,
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
is merely slightly different.

Estimate of
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
.

The evolution in
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
of the various
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\gamma)

and
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\gamma)
due to the change in the preamplification factors is similar
to the monotone case, but the estimates on the removed term
\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)
is
different due to non-monotonicity, that is, we can merely estimate here
(using again Proposition
2.2
–
2
and (
4.8
))

-\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{r}(\alpha)\mathfrak{s}^{1}_{l}(\beta)\leq-2^{-2p}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p}.

Hence we have here

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{1}^{k}(t^{-})\leq-2^{-2p}|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{p}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{p}+\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|\left(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{2}(t^{-})=\mathcal{O}(1)\mathcal{V}(t^{-})^{\frac{2p-1}{p}}|\sigma_{\alpha}||\sigma_{\beta}|\left(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|\right).

Estimate of
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
.

Here we benefit from the cancellation effect between
\sigma_{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\beta}
.
Note that due to the behavior of our front-tracking algorithm in the case of same-family
interaction, there cannot be a transformation of a CW into a shock here.

Using the fact that
\sigma_{\alpha}\sigma_{\beta}<0
, we have

|\sigma_{\alpha}+\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}-|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}-|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\leq-\frac{3}{4}\max(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|)^{2}\min(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|).

(For
a>b>0
, one has
(a-b)^{3}-a^{3}-b^{3}=-3a^{2}b+3ab^{2}-b^{3}
and one uses

3ab\leq b^{2}+\frac{9}{4}a^{2}
.)

Adding the errors of (
4.32
), we find

\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}_{3}(t^{-})\leq-\frac{3}{4}\max(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|)^{2}\min(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|)+\mathcal{O}(1)|\sigma_{\alpha}|^{3}|\sigma_{\beta}|^{3}\big(|\sigma_{\alpha}|+|\sigma_{\beta}|\big)^{3}.

Conclusion.

Relying on the decay

-\frac{3}{4}\max(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|)^{2}\min(|\sigma_{\alpha}|,|\sigma_{\beta}|)

of the
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
term,
we find again in that case that
\Upsilon(t^{+})-\Upsilon(t^{-})\leq 0
provided that
V_{p}(u_{0})

is small enough and that
C_{2}
is large enough in the case
p=1
.

4.3.4
Measures along curves

We consider
\Gamma
a wave measure curve in the strip
[0,\tau]\times\mathbb{R}
.
Again we only have to consider interaction times and to prove that at such
t
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(t^{+})+\mathcal{Q}(t^{+})\leq\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(t^{-})+\mathcal{Q}(t^{-})

(4.40)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{\Gamma}(t^{+})+\mathcal{Q}(t^{+})\leq\mathfrak{M}_{\Gamma}(t^{-})+\mathcal{Q}(t^{-}).

(4.41)

Evolution of
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}
.

Since in
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}
the preamplified forces
\widehat{\sigma}_{\gamma}
are frozen after
\gamma
has met

\Gamma
, we only have to consider the evolution of preamplified forces:

•

on the half-line to the left of
(t,\Gamma(t))
for
1
-lower and
2
-upper measure curves,

•

on the half-line to the right of
(t,\Gamma(t))
for
2
-lower and
1
-upper measure curves.

Call
I_{t}
the corresponding half-line at time
t
.
Then as for
\mathcal{V}
, the change of
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}
at time
t
is due to:

•

The change in the strength of the fronts that interact,
and the possible strength of the new outgoing waves.
This is compensated by
\mathcal{Q}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}(t^{-})
, as shown in the previous paragraphs.

•

The modification of the preamplification factors: here this concerns only fronts that cross
I_{t}
,
since other preamplification factors are frozen.
Notice in particular that when an interaction of opposite families is met on
I_{t}
, the corresponding
preamplification factor is not represented in
\mathfrak{M}_{\Gamma}(t)
, because this interaction takes place
in the limited component of
(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})\setminus\Gamma
.
Hence no factor
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
disappears from the preamplified strengths in
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(t)
.
Consequently, updating
\mathcal{V}_{\Gamma}(t)
corresponds to multiplying the preamplified strengths by

\left(1,\ldots,1,\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)_{\alpha>\Gamma(t)}\right)

if
\Gamma
is a
1
-lower or a
2
-upper measure curve, and by

\left(\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)_{\alpha<\Gamma(t)},1,\ldots,1,\right)

if
\Gamma
is a
2
-lower or a
1
-upper measure curve.
The
p
-variation
v^{p}_{p}\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)
has been estimated in the previous paragraphs. “Flattening” the sequence
\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}
on the left/right of
\Gamma(t)
, this gives:

\displaystyle v_{p}\left(1,\ldots,1,\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)_{\alpha>\Gamma(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{p}\left(0,\ldots,0,\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)_{\alpha>\Gamma(t)}-1\right)

(4.42)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle v_{p}\left(\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)-1\right)+\left\|\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)-1\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{p}\left(\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)\right)+\left\|\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{+})}{\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}(t^{-})}\right)-1\right\|_{\infty}.

The right-hand side has been estimated in the above sections; it can be absorbed by
\mathcal{Q}(t^{+})-\mathcal{Q}(t^{-})
,
see in particular (
4.27
) and (
4.34
)-(

4.5.1
Global existence of front-tracking approximations

The first step to conclude is to prove that
T_{\nu}=+\infty
, where

was defined in Subsection

.
In what follows we prove that the various conditions limiting

do not occur in finite time.

Finite number of fronts.

Let us prove that the number of fronts remains bounded on
[0,T_{\nu})
.
This argument is classical.
In the above front-tracking algorithm, when two fronts meet, there can be more than to outgoing fronts
only in the case of a same-family interaction, with an outgoing wave in the opposite family is a rarefaction
of strength at least
\nu>0
.
Due to the analysis of Paragraphs
4.3.2
and
4.3.3
, this implies a decay
of size
\nu
in
\Upsilon
, whatever the choice of time horizon
T
.
Since
\Upsilon
can be bounded independently of the time horizon,
such an event happens only finitely many times, and the total number of fronts remains bounded.

Admissibility of states.

Let us prove that we have an a priori bound on
\|u^{\nu}-\overline{u}\|_{\infty}
in
[0,T_{\nu})
,
so all states remain in
U
and
T_{\nu}
cannot be limited by Riemann problems needing states outside.
It is actually a direct consequence of
H(\tau)
being valid for all
\tau<T_{\nu}
.
Since
u^{\nu}
is constant on the left and on the right a compact set in space,
with states
u^{\nu}(-\infty)
and
u^{\nu}(+\infty)
satisfying

\|u^{\nu}(\pm\infty)-\overline{u}\|_{\infty}\leq\|u_{0}-\overline{u}\|_{\infty}
,
using the bound on
\widetilde{V}_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))
and
choosing
\varepsilon_{0}>0
in (
1.9
) suitably small ensures that all states remain
in
B(\overline{u};r)
for
r
fixed as previously.

Non accumulation of fronts.

The analysis here borrows arguments from
[
7
]
, but the front-tracking algorithm that we use allows
simplifying a bit the analysis.

Suppose that fronts accumulate at time
T_{\nu}
.
We call an extended front line a continuous locally piecewise affine line
t\mapsto\gamma(t)
defined on some
interval
[t_{0},T_{\nu})
, which follows fronts of a given characteristic family.
Due to finite speed of propagation, all extended front lines are uniformly Lipschitz and can be propagated
till time
T_{\nu}
included.
Due to the finite number of fronts, accumulations can occur only at a finite number of places
(that are final points of extended front lines).
Pick
x^{*}
one of them.
Due to the previous analysis, we can introduce a rectangle
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
,
with
h>0
small such that:

•

there is no interaction point with more than two outgoing fronts in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
.

Reducing again
h
if necessary, we can also require that

•

there is no interaction point
in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
for which an incoming compression front is transformed in a shock front.
There are indeed only a finite number of such points where this occurs, due to the analysis
of Subsection
4.3.2
: see in particular the
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha\beta}
term in (
4.37
),
which gives a minimal decay on
\Upsilon
independently of the time horizon.

It follows that the number of shock fronts is non-increasing in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
; reducing again

h
if necessary, we can suppose that

•

the number of shock fronts is constant in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
.

Since an incoming shock front generates an outgoing shock front in the same family (unless it disappears), we can follow
these shocks as disjoint front lines in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu})
.
It follows that we can reduce
h>0
in order that

•

all extended shock front lines in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
converge to
x^{*}
as

.

We denote them

correspond to fronts of the second family,
and
x_{k+1}(t),\ldots,x_{N}(t)
correspond to fronts of the first family.

Now other fronts present in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
are either rarefaction waves or compression waves.
We define
\mathcal{N}(t)
as the number of such fronts (RW or CW) of family
1
and to the right of
x_{k}(t)
plus
the number of such fronts (RW or CW) of family
2
and to the left of
x_{k+1}(t)
.
Interactions involving such fronts between themselves do not generate other fronts: they cross if it is an interaction
of opposite families, and they merge if it is an interaction of the same family.
When an
i
-front of RW/CW type meets a
(3-i)
-shock, they cross as well.
Finally, when an
i
-front of RW/CW type meets an
i
-shock, they merge as a shock and generate a RW/CW front in the other family,

\mathcal{N}(t)
decreases, because this generated
(3-i)

It follows that

is non-increasing, and decreases at each interaction of a RW/CW wave with a shock.

Hence, there are only finitely many interactions of this type.
Reducing

again, we can hence obtain

•

The only interactions in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu})\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
concern CW/RW between themselves.

Such an interaction in the same family makes the number of fronts decrease.
Hence interactions of CW/RW of the same family are finite, and reducing
h>0
again we obtain

•

The only interactions in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu})\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
concern CW/RW of opposite families.

In the case of interaction of CW/RW opposite families, the fronts simply cross.
Hence we can follow
n_{1}
the lines of
1

[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu})\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
and

n_{2}
the lines of
2
-CW/RW in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu})\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]
.
These generate at most

Finally, we see that there are only finitely many interaction points in
[T_{\nu}-h,T_{\nu}]\times[x^{*}-h,x^{*}+h]

which contradicts the fact that fronts accumulate at
(T_{\nu},x^{*})
.

This finally proves that

for all
\nu\in(0,\nu_{0})
. ∎

Proof of Proposition
2.8
(Interactions within a family).

We only treat interactions of the first family by symmetry.

1.
We consider the case of classical waves.
In that case, the second estimate on
\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}
is classical and not peculiar to
2\times 2
systems,
see e.g.
[
6
, Lemma 7.2]
.
Concerning the first inequality, we project

u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{2};\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l}))=\mathcal{T}_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1};\mathcal{T}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l}))
on the
w_{1}
-axis to obtain:

w_{1}[u_{r}]-w_{1}[\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l})]+\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}=\sigma^{\prime}_{1}+\sigma_{1}.

Since the wave between
\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l})
and
u_{r}
is of the second family
and due to the second-order tangency of rarefaction curves and Lax wave curves, we deduce

w_{1}[u_{r}]-w_{1}[\mathcal{T}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l})]=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}^{3}),

which proves the claim.

2.

In the case of a compression wave meeting a shock, we treat only interactions in family one
with the compression wave on the right, the other cases being similar.
We reason as previously.
We use the notation
\check{u}_{m}:=\mathcal{S}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l})
.
First, by the classical Glimm estimates, we have

\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}=\sigma_{1}+\sigma^{\prime}_{1}+\mathcal{O}(\sigma_{1}\sigma^{\prime}_{1})
.
In particular, we see that
\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}-\sigma_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1})

and that
\sigma_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1})
, since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
have the same sign.

Now we project

u_{r}=\mathcal{T}_{2}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{2};\mathcal{S}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l}))=\mathcal{R}_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1};\mathcal{S}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l}))

on the
w_{1}
/
w_{2}
-axes and use (
2.11
) to obtain:

(\widetilde{\sigma}_{2})_{*}^{3}\,\mathcal{D}_{2}(\check{u}_{m},{\sigma}_{2})+\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}=\sigma^{\prime}_{1}+\sigma_{1}\ \text{ and }\ \widetilde{\sigma}_{2}+\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{3}\,\mathcal{D}_{1}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1};u_{l})=\sigma_{1}^{3}\,\mathcal{D}_{1}(\sigma_{1};u_{l}).

(6.17)

We infer from the second equation and the regularity of
\mathcal{D}_{1}
that

\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)|\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}|^{2}|\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}-\sigma_{1}|
,
so that

\widetilde{\sigma}_{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)|\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}|^{2}|\sigma^{\prime}_{1}|
, which gives the second part of
(
2.16
).
Injecting in the first equation of (
6.17

3.
Finally, the case of two compression/rarefaction waves is a direct consequence of (

6.5
Estimate on the modified
p
-variation: proof of Lemma
3.1

Call
\gamma^{1}_{1},\ldots,\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}
the list of all
1
-fronts at time
t
,
and
\gamma^{2}_{1},\ldots,\gamma^{2}_{n_{2}}
the list of all
2
-fronts at time
t
, from left to right.
Now to get the
p
-variations
V_{p}(w_{1}(u^{\nu}))
and
V_{p}(w_{2}(u^{\nu}))
from
\widetilde{V}_{p}(u^{\nu})
,
we need to include the jumps of
w_{1}
at fronts
\gamma^{2}_{k}
(that we will denote
[w_{1}]_{\gamma^{2}_{k}}
)
in
s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}})
,
and the jumps of
w_{2}
at fronts
\gamma^{1}_{i}
(that we will denote
[w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{i}}
)
in
s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{n_{2}}})
, respectively.
Due to (
2.10
)-(
2.11
),
for any
k=1,\ldots,{n_{2}}
(resp.
i=1,\ldots,{n_{1}}
), one has
|[w_{1}]_{\gamma^{2}_{k}}|\lesssim|\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{k}}|^{3}

(resp.
|[w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{i}}|\lesssim|\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{i}}|^{3}
).
It follows that

s_{p}([w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{1}},\ldots,[w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}})\lesssim s_{1}(|\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{1}}|^{3},\ldots,|\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}}|^{3})\leq\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(t)}|\sigma_{\gamma}|^{3}\leq V_{3}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3}\leq V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3}.

(6.18)

Using Proposition
2.1
–
6
, we have

s_{p}([w_{2}]_{\gamma};\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(t))\leq s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{n_{2}}})+s_{p}([w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{1}},\ldots,[w_{2}]_{\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}})\leq s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{n_{2}}})+\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3},

and all the same,

s_{p}([w_{1}]_{\gamma};\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(t))\leq s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{1}_{n_{1}}})+\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3}.

Using the triangle inequality for the
p
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we find

V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))\leq\widetilde{V}_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))+\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3}.

Proposition
2.1
–
6
also gives us

s_{p}(\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\gamma^{2}_{n_{2}}})\leq s_{p}([w_{2}]_{\gamma};\gamma\in\mathcal{A}(t))+\mathcal{O}(1)V_{p}(u^{\nu}(t,\cdot))^{3},

Now instead of (
