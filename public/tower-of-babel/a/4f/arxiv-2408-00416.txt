Abstract
. The left and right diameters of a monoid are topological invariants defined in terms of suprema of lengths of derivation sequences with respect to finite generating sets for the universal left or right congruences. We compute these parameters for the endomorphism monoid End ⁡ ( C ) \End(C) of a chain C C . Specifically, if C C is infinite then the left diameter of End ⁡ ( C ) \End(C) is 2, while the right diameter is either 2 or 3, with the latter equal to 2 precisely when C C is a quotient of C ∖ { z } C{\setminus}\{z\} for some endpoint z z . If C C is finite then so is End ⁡ ( C ) , \End(C), in which case the left and right diameters are 1 (if C C is non-trivial) or 0.



Keywords
: Diameter, endomorphism monoid, chain, right congruence, generating set, derivation sequence.

: 20M20, 06A05, 20M10.

1.
Introduction

For a monoid
S
whose universal right congruence is generated by a finite set
U,
the right diameter of
S
with respect to
U
is the supremum of the minimum lengths of derivations for pairs
(a,b)\in S\times S
as a consequence of those in
U.
The right diameter of
S
is then the minimum of the set of all right diameters with respect to finite generating sets. Thus, a monoid has finite right diameter if and only if its universal right congruence is finitely generated and there is a bound on the length of sequences required to relate any two elements as a consequence of the generators. More precise definitions regarding right diameter will be given in Section
2
. The notion of left diameter is dual.

Monoids with finite (left) diameter were introduced by White, under the name ‘left pseudo-finite’, in the context of Banach algebras
[
21
]
. This work was motivated by a conjecture of Dales and Żelazko, stating that a unital Banach algebra in which every maximal left ideal is finitely generated is necessarily finite dimensional.

Monoids with finite left diameter were first studied systematically in
[
3
]
(again under the name ‘left pseudo-finite’), within the broader context of monoids having a finitely generated universal left congruence. The latter condition was shown to be equivalent to a number of previously-studied properties, including the homological finiteness property of being type left-
FP_{1}
and connectivity of certain Cayley graphs
[
3
, Theorem 3.10]
[
15
]
.

The article
[
10
]
investigated the existence and nature of minimal ideals in monoids with finite diameter. It was observed that the notion of having right/left diameter 1 can be reformulated in terms of the so-called diagonal acts. For a monoid
S,
the diagonal right
S
-act is the set
S\times S
under the right action given by
(a,b)c=(ac,bc).
A monoid has right diameter 1 if and only if its diagonal right act is finitely generated. The latter property had already been studied by Gallagher and Ruškuc
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Some of their most interesting results concern certain natural infinite monoids of transformations and relations. In particular, for any infinite set
X,
the diagonal left and right acts are monogenic (i.e. generated by a single pair) for the monoids of all binary relations on
X,
of all partial transformations on
X,
and of all (full) transformations on
X

[
8
]
.

The above work regarding transformation monoids was part of the motivation for the article
[
5
]
, which determines the left and right diameters of a large class of natural monoids of transformations and partitions. For instance, it is shown that the monoid of all injective mappings on
X
has right diameter 4, but its universal left congruence is not even finitely generated, and the monoid of all surjective mappings on
X
has left diameter 4, while its universal right congruence is not finitely generated. It is intriguing that all the diameters computed in
[
5
]
are no greater than 4.

Some of the most natural and well-studied monoids arise as endomorphism monoids of mathematical structures, be they relational or algebraic. Conversely, one way to classify complicated structures is via properties of their endomorphism monoids. Chains, also known as linearly/totally ordered sets, are important and well-known relational structures; see
[
4
]
for instance. Of course, a chain
C
may also be regarded as an algebraic structure, specifically a semigroup with the operation that takes the minimum (or, alternatively, the maximum) of two elements, and the order-preserving transformations of

are precisely the semigroup endomorphisms of

We denote the endomorphism monoid of a chain

by
\End(C)

Endomorphism monoids of infinite chains can possess a rather complex structure, even in the case of the chain

In the context of diameter, Gallagher has shown that for any infinite chain

Theorem A

For any infinite chain

Theorem B

For any infinite chain

2.
Diameters of Monoids

Throughout this section,
S
denotes a monoid. We denote the set of positive integers by
\mathbb{N},
and let
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

An equivalence relation
\rho
on
S
is a
right congruence
if
(a,b)\in\rho
implies
(as,bs)\in\rho
for all
s\in S
.
For
U\subseteq S\times S,
the
right congruence generated by

U
is the smallest right congruence on
S
containing
U
; we denote this right congruence by
\mbox{${\langle}$}U\mbox{${\rangle}$}.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
13
, Lemma I. 4. 37]

Let
S
be a monoid, and let
U
be a subset of
S\times S
. For any
a,b\in S
we have
(a,b)\in\mbox{${\langle}$}U\mbox{${\rangle}$}
if and only if there exists a sequence

a=u_{1}s_{1},\ v_{1}s_{1}=u_{2}s_{2},\ \dots,\ v_{n}s_{n}=b

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
(u_{i},v_{i})\in U
or
(v_{i},u_{i})\in U,
and
s_{i}\in S,
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

A sequence of the form given in Lemma
2.1
is referred to as a
U
-sequence from
a
to
b
of length
n
.
In the case that
n=0,
we interpret the
U
-sequence as
a=b.

The universal relation
\omega_{S}=S\times S
is certainly a right congruence on
S.
When viewing this relation as a right congruence, we shall denote it by
\omega_{S}^{r}
. Consider a generating set
U
of
\omega_{S}^{r}
. For any
a,b\in S,
let
d_{U}^{r}(a,b)
denote the least
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that there is a
U
-sequence from
a
to
b
of length
n.
It is easy to see that
d_{U}^{r}:S\times S\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a metric on
S.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
S
be a monoid such that
\omega_{S}^{r}
is finitely generated. For each finite generating set
U
of
\omega_{S}^{r}
, we call the diameter of the metric space
(S,d_{U}^{r})
the
right
U
-diameter
of
S
and denote it by
D_{r}(S;U)
; that is,

D_{r}(S;U)=\sup\{d_{U}^{r}(a,b):a,b\in S\}.

We define the
right diameter
of
S
to be

D_{r}(S)=\min\{D_{r}(S;U):\omega_{S}^{r}=\mbox{${\langle}$}U\mbox{${\rangle}$}\text{ where $U$ is finite}\}.

Note that if
U
and
U^{\prime}
are two finite generating sets for
\omega_{S}^{r},
then
D_{r}(S;U)
is finite if and only if
D_{r}(S;U^{\prime})
is finite
[
3
, Lemma 2.5]
.

For convenience, we shall often abuse terminology by saying that
\omega_{S}^{r}
is generated by a subset
V
of
S
to mean that
\omega_{S}^{r}
is generated by
V\times V
, and also write
D_{r}(S;V)
in place of
D_{r}(S;V\times V).
We note that
D_{r}(S)
is the minimum of all
D_{r}(S;V)
over finite
V\subseteq S
with
\omega_{S}^{r}=\mbox{${\langle}$}V\times V\mbox{${\rangle}$}.

As mentioned in Section
1
, there is an important connection between the notion of diameter and that of diagonal acts. Recall that the
diagonal right
S
-act
is the set
S\times S
with right action given by
(a,b)c=(ac,bc).
It is
generated
by a set
U\subseteq S\times S
if
S\times S=US:=\{us:u\in U,s\in S\},
and is
finitely generated
if it is generated by a finite set.

Proposition 2.3
.

[
10
, Proposition 3.6]

A non-trivial monoid
S
has right diameter 1 if and only if the diagonal right
S
-act is finitely generated.

Remark 2.4
.

A monoid has right (or left) diameter 0 if and only if it is trivial. Every finite, non-trivial monoid has right (and left) diameter 1.

3.
Preliminaries on Chains and their Endomorphisms

3.1.
Chains

A
chain
is a set equipped with a
total order
, i.e. a partial order under which any two elements are comparable.
2
2

2

In the literature, there are a number of other names used for chains, including totally ordered sets, tosets, linearly ordered sets and losets.

Let
\mathbf{C}=(C,\leq)
be a chain. Where no confusion arises, we identify
\mathbf{C}
with
C.
A
subchain
of
C
is a subset
D\subseteq C
totally ordered under the restriction of
\leq
to
D.
A subchain of
C
is called
proper
if its underlying set is not equal to
C.
An
interval
of
C
is a subchain
D
of
C
such that if
a\leq x\leq b
with
a,b\in D
then
x\in D
.
In Definition
3.1
all the subsets defined are intervals.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
C
be a chain, and let
a,b\in C
with
a\leq b
. We write

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}(a,b)&=&\{x\in C:a<x<b\},&[a,b)&=&\{x\in C:a\leq x<b\},\\
(a,b]&=&\{x\in C:a<x\leq b\},&[a,b]&=&\{x\in C:a\leq x\leq b\},\\
a^{\uparrow}&=&\{x\in C:a\leq x\},&a^{\downarrow}&=&\{x\in C:x\leq a\}.\end{array}

By
\mathbf{C}^{*}
we denote the chain obtained from
\mathbf{C}
by reversing the order; that is,
\mathbf{C}^{*}=(C,\geq).
We let
\mathbf{N}=(\mathbb{N},\leq)
,
\mathbf{Z}=(\mathbb{Z},\leq)
,
\mathbf{Q}=(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
and
\mathbf{R}=(\mathbb{R},\leq),
where in each case
\leq
is the natural order. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the interval
[1,n]
of
\mathbf{N}
by
\mathbf{n}.

Given a chain
a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots
of elements of
C,
we will occasionally abuse notation by writing the set
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots\}
as
\{a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots\}.

For subsets
A
and
B
of
C,
we write
A<B
if
a<b
for each
a\in A
and
b\in B
; if
A=\{a\}
we simply write this as
a<B.
Similarly, we write
A<b,

A\leq B,
etc. Note that we assume
A<\emptyset
and
A>\emptyset
are (vacuously) true, and interpret
A<\emptyset<B
as
A<B.

We say that
B
covers
A
if
A<B
and there is no
x\in C
with
A<x<B
.

An element
b\in C
is a
lower bound
of a subset
A\subseteq C
if
b\leq A
. There is at most one lower bound of
A
belonging to
A
; if such an element exists, it is called the
minimum
of
A
and is denoted by
\min(A).

Upper bounds
and
maximums
of subsets of
C
are defined dually, and the maximum of
A\subseteq C,
if it exists, is denoted by
\max(A).
An element that is either a minimum or a maximum of
C
is called an
endpoint
.

Given a chain
D
disjoint from
C,
we let
C+D
denote the chain
(C\cup D,\leq)
with order extending those of
C
and
D
and with
C<D
. Hence
D
covers
C
in
C+D.
We note that we will often abuse notation by writing
C+D
for non-disjoint
C
and
D,
by which we mean
C+D^{\prime}
where
D^{\prime}
is a copy of
D
disjoint from
C.

We call a subchain
A
of
C
an
initial segment
if
C=A+D
for some chain
D.
We dually define
terminal segments
.
A
cut
of
C
is a pair
(A,B)
such that
C=A+B
.
A cut
(A,B)
is said to be
proper
if both
A
and
B
are proper.
A proper cut
(A,B)
of
C
is a
gap
if
A
has no maximum and
B
has no minimum. We note that if
(A,B)
is a gap of
C,
then
A
contains a copy of
\mathbf{N},
and
B
contains a copy of
\mathbf{N}^{*}
, and hence
C
contains a copy of
\mathbf{N}+\mathbf{N}^{*}
.
If
C
contains no gaps then it is called
(Dedekind) complete
.

Clearly there is a one-to-one correspondence between the cuts of
C
and the initial (or terminal) segments of
C.
The set
\mathcal{D}(C)
of all cuts of
C
comes equipped with a total order
\leq_{DM}
given by

(A,B)\leq_{DM}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})\,\Leftrightarrow\,A\subseteq A^{\prime}\;(\Leftrightarrow B^{\prime}\subseteq B).

The chain
(\mathcal{D}(C),\leq_{DM})
is complete and is known as the
Dedekind-MacNeille completion of
C
(with minimum
(\emptyset,C)
and maximum
(C,\emptyset)
)
[
4
, Chapter 7]
.

3.2.
Homomorphisms and one-sided units

Let
C
and
D
be chains. A
homomorphism
from
C
to
D
is a map
\theta:C\to D
that is
order preserving
, i.e.
x\theta\leq y\theta
whenever
x\leq y.
An
embedding
is an injective homomorphism, and an
isomorphism
is a bijective homomorphism. We call
D
a
quotient
of
C
if there is a surjective homomorphism from
C
onto
D.
We note that every quotient of
C
gives rise to a partition of
C
into intervals, and vice versa.

Given a homomorphism
\theta:C\to D
and a subchain
E\subseteq D,
we denote by
E\theta^{-1}
the subchain
\{x\in C:x\theta\in E\}
of
C.
For
y\in D,
we abbreviate
\{y\}\theta^{-1}
to
y\theta^{-1}
.

An
endomorphism
of
C
is a homomorphism from
C
to
C.
The set
\End(C)
of all endomorphisms of
C
is a monoid under composition, whose identity
1_{C}
is the identity map on
C.
Injective elements of
\End(C)
are called
self-embeddings
of
C.

A map
\alpha\in\End(C)
is
regular
if there exists some
\beta\in\End(C)
such that
\alpha=\alpha\beta\alpha.

The following result, due to Mora and Kemprasit, describes all regular endomorphisms of chains.

Theorem 3.2
.

[
18
, Theorem 2.4]

Let
C
be a chain and
\alpha\in\End(C).
Then
\alpha
is regular if and only if the following conditions hold.

(i)

If
\im\alpha
has a lower bound in
C,
then
\min(\im\alpha)
exists.

(ii)

If
\im\alpha
has an upper bound in
C,
then
\max(\im\alpha)
exists.

(iii)

If
x\in C{\setminus}\!\im\alpha
is neither an upper bound nor a lower bound of
\im\alpha,
then at least one of
\max\{t\in\im\alpha:t<x\}
and
\min\{t\in\im\alpha:t>x\}
exists.

Given
x\in C,
the
constant map
on
x
is the map
c_{x}:C\to C
given by
yc_{x}=x
for all
y\in C.
It is clear that each
c_{x}
is a regular, indeed an idempotent, endomorphism of
C.

A map
\alpha\in\End(C)
is a
right unit
if there exists
\beta\in\End(C)
such that
\alpha\beta=1_{C}
; the element
\beta
is called a
right inverse
of
\alpha,
and
\alpha
is said to be
right invertible
.
Left units
and
left inverses
are defined dually. Clearly
\alpha
is a right (resp. left) unit of
\End(C)
if and only if it is a left (resp. right) inverse of some
\beta\in\End(C).

It is also clear that right/left units are regular. The following lemma describes the left units of
\End(C).

Lemma 3.3
.

Left units of
\End(C)
are precisely the surjective endomorphisms of
C.

Proof.

Clearly left units of
\End(C)
are surjective. For the converse, let
\beta
be a surjective endomorphism of
C.
For each
x\in C
choose
y_{x}\in x\beta^{-1}
, and define
\alpha:C\to C
by
x\alpha=y_{x}
. It is clear that
\alpha\in\End(C),
and for any
x\in C
we have
x\alpha\beta=y_{x}\beta=x,
so that
\alpha\beta=1_{C}
. Thus
\beta
is a left unit of
\End(C).

∎

Right units of
\End(C)
have a more complicated description. They are certainly self-embeddings, but the converse need not hold. Using the above description of regular endomorphisms of
C,
we quickly determine which self-embeddings are right invertible.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
C
be a chain and
\alpha\in\End(C).
Then the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

\alpha
is a right unit of
\End(C).

(2)

\alpha
is both injective and regular.

(3)

\alpha
is injective and the following conditions hold.

(a)

If
C
has no minimum, then
\im\alpha
has no lower bound in
C.

(b)

If
C
has no maximum, then
\im\alpha
has no upper bound in
C.

(c)

If
x\in C{\setminus}\!\im\alpha
is neither an upper bound nor a lower bound of
\im\alpha,
then at least one of
\max\{t\in\im\alpha:t<x\}
and
\min\{t\in\im\alpha:t>x\}
exists.

Proof.

(1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2). We have already observed that right units of
\End(C)
are injective and regular. For the converse, suppose
\alpha\in\End(C)
is injective and regular. Then
\alpha=\alpha\beta\alpha
for some
\beta\in S.
For any
x\in C,
we have
x\alpha=(x\alpha\beta)\alpha,
and hence
x=x\alpha\beta
by injectivity of
\alpha.
Thus
\alpha\beta=1_{C}
, as required.

(2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3). Let
\alpha
be injective. We need to show that
\alpha
is regular if and only if the conditions (a)-(c) hold. By Theorem
3.2
, regularity of
\alpha
is equivalent to the conditions (i)-(iii) of that theorem holding. Since
\alpha\in S
is injective, it is clear that
\min(\im\alpha)
exists if and only if
C
has a minimum, and hence (i) is equivalent to (a). Similarly, (ii) is equivalent to (b). Finally, (iii) is identical to (c).
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If
\alpha
is a self-embedding of a chain
C
that fails condition (3)(c) of Corollary
3.4
with witness
x\in C{\setminus}\!\im\alpha,
then (
x^{\downarrow}\alpha^{-1},x^{\uparrow}\alpha^{-1}
) is a gap of
C.

The following technical lemma will be crucial in our arguments to determine the exact value of the right diameter of
\End(C).

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
C
be a chain, let
x
be a fixed element of
C,
let
\alpha
be a right unit of
\End(C),
and let
Q=\{\beta\in\End(C):\alpha\beta=1_{C}\}
be the set of right inverses of
\alpha.
Then, for any
\beta\in Q
we have

x\beta\alpha=\begin{cases}\max(\im\alpha\cap x^{\downarrow})&\text{if }x\beta\alpha\leq x\\
\min(\im\alpha\cap x^{\uparrow})&\text{if }x\beta\alpha\geq x.\end{cases}

Consequently, the set
\{x\beta:\beta\in Q\}
has at most two elements.

Proof.

Let
Y_{1}=\im\alpha\cap x^{\downarrow}
and
Y_{2}=\im\alpha\cap x^{\uparrow}
. Then
\im\alpha=Y_{1}\cup Y_{2}
with
Y_{1}\leq Y_{2}
. Consider
\beta\in Q.
For any
y\in Y_{1}
, we have
y\leq x
and
y=u\alpha
for some
u\in C,
so that

y=u\alpha=u\alpha\beta\alpha=y\beta\alpha\leq x\beta\alpha.

Thus
Y_{1}\leq x\beta\alpha.
Hence, if
x\beta\alpha\leq x
(so that
x\beta\alpha\in Y_{1}
) then
x\beta\alpha=\max(Y_{1}).
Similarly, if
x\beta\alpha\geq x
then
x\beta\alpha=\min(Y_{2}).

Now, since the sets
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
do not depend on any
\beta\in Q,
and
\alpha
is injective, it follows that
|\{x\beta:\beta\in Q\}|\leq 2.

∎

4.
Endpoint-shiftability

In this section we study the condition appearing in Theorem
B
. We begin by providing equivalent formulations of this condition.

Proposition 4.1
.

For a chain
C
with an endpoint
z,
the following are equivalent:

(1)

C
is a quotient of
C{\setminus}\{z\}
;

(2)

there exists a left unit (surjective map)
\beta\in\End(C)
such that
z\beta^{-1}\neq\{z\}
;

(3)

there exists a right unit
\alpha\in\End(C)
such that
z\alpha\neq z
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). By assumption, there exists a surjective homomorphism
\theta:C{\setminus}\{z\}\to C.
Extend
\theta
to a map
\beta:C\to C
by setting
z\beta=z.
Then
\beta
is a surjective endomorphism of
C,
and hence a left unit of
\End(C)
by Lemma
3.3
, and
z\beta^{-1}\neq\{z\}.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3).
As in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we define a right unit
\alpha\in\End(C)
by
x\alpha=y_{x}\in x\beta^{-1}
(
x\in C
), but choose
y_{z}\neq z.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1). Let
\beta
be any right inverse of
\alpha.
Then
\beta
is a surjective endomorphism of
C.
Since
(z\alpha)\beta=z
and
z\alpha\neq z,
it follows that
\beta
restricts to a surjective homomorphism
C{\setminus}\{z\}\to C,
and hence
C
is a quotient of
C{\setminus}\{z\}.

∎

Definition 4.2
.

We say that a chain
C
is
endpoint-shiftable
if it has an endpoint
z\in C
and satisfies any (and hence all) of the three equivalent conditions of Proposition
4.1
. If, in fact,
C
is endpoint-shiftable with respect to a minimum (resp. maximum), then we call it
min-shiftable
(resp.
max-shiftable
).

The next few results concern min-shiftability; of course, there are obvious duals for max-shiftability. First, we provide several equivalent characterisations for a chain to be min-shiftable.

Proposition 4.3
.

For a chain
C
with a minimum, the following are equivalent:

(1)

C
is min-shiftable;

(2)

C^{*}
is max-shiftable;

(3)

C
is a quotient of one of its proper terminal segments;

(4)

for every chain
D,
the chain
C+D
is min-shiftable;

(5)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N},
the chain
\mathbf{n}+C
is a quotient of
C
;

(6)

for some
n\in\mathbb{N},
the chain
\mathbf{n}+C
is a quotient of
C
;

(7)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N},
the chain
\mathbf{n}+C
is min-shiftable;

(8)

for some
n\in\mathbb{N},
the chain
\mathbf{n}+C
is min-shiftable.

Proof.

Clearly
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2),

(5)\Rightarrow(6)
and
(7)\Rightarrow(8).
Let
z=\min(C).

(1)\Leftrightarrow(3).
The forward implication follows immediately from the definition of being min-shiftable together with Proposition
4.1
(1). For the converse, suppose that
C
is a quotient of a proper terminal segment
T
of
C.
Let
\varphi:T\to C
be a surjective homomorphism, and extend
\varphi
to a surjective homomorphism
\theta:C\to C
by letting
(C{\setminus}T)\theta=\{z\}.
Then
z\theta^{-1}\neq\{z\}
as
T\neq C.
Thus
C
is min-shiftable.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(4).
Suppose that
C
is min-shiftable. Then there exists a surjective homomorphism
\varphi:C{\setminus}\{z\}\to C.
For any chain
D,
clearly
z=\min(C+D),
and
\varphi
can be extended to a surjective homomorphism
\theta:(C+D){\setminus}\{z\}\to C+D
by letting
x\theta=x
for all
x\in D,
so that
C+D
is min-shiftable. For the converse, put
D=\emptyset.

(1)\Rightarrow(5).
Since
C
is min-shiftable, there exists a surjective map
\beta\in\End(C)
such that
z\beta^{-1}\neq\{z\}.
It is straightforward to show that
\gamma:=\beta^{n}:C\to C
is a surjective homomorphism with
|z\gamma^{-1}|\geq n+1.
Choose a surjective homomorphism
\varphi:z\gamma^{-1}\to\mathbf{n}+\{z\},
and define a map

\theta:C\to\mathbf{n}+C,\ x\mapsto\begin{cases}x\varphi&\text{if }x\in z\gamma^{-1}\\
x\gamma&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly
\theta
is a surjective homomorphism, as required.

(5)\Rightarrow(7)
and
(6)\Rightarrow(8)
follow from the already-established implication
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
.

(8)\Rightarrow(3).
There exists a surjective homomorphism
\beta:\mathbf{n}{\setminus}\{1\}+C\to\mathbf{n}+C.
It follows from the surjectivity of
\beta
that
i\beta<i
for each
i\in\{2,\dots,n\},
and hence
n\beta^{-1}\subseteq C,
which implies that
z\beta\in\mathbf{n}.
It now follows that
C\beta^{-1}
is a proper terminal segment of
C,
and that
C
is a quotient of
C\beta^{-1}
.
∎

The next result provides another means of constructing min-shiftable chains. First, recall that the Cartesian product
C\times D
of two chains
C
and
D
is a chain under the
lexicographical order
, given by
(x,y)\leq(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
if and only
x<x^{\prime}
or (
x=x^{\prime}
and
y\leq y^{\prime}
). Note that
(z,w)=\min(C\times D)
if and only if
z=\min(C)
and
w=\min(D).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
C
and
D
be chains with minima. If
C
is isomorphic to one of its proper terminal segments, or if
D
is min-shiftable, then
C\times D
is min-shiftable.

Proof.

Let
z=\min(C).
Since
C\times D\cong D+(C{\setminus}\{z\}\times D),
if
D
is min-shiftable then, by Proposition
4.3
, so is
C\times D.
Suppose now that
C
is isomorphic to a proper terminal segment
T
of
C,
and let
\varphi:C\to T
be an isomorphism. Then
C\times D
is isomorphic to its proper terminal segment
T\times D
via
(x,y)\mapsto(x\varphi,y).
Thus, by Proposition
4.3
, the chain
C\times D
is min-shiftable.
∎

We now present a number of natural examples of min-shiftable chains. Together with Propositions
4.3
and
4.4
, these examples generate a large class of endpoint-shiftable chains. First, recall that a chain
C
is
scattered
if
\mathbf{Q}
does
not
embed into
C
; otherwise,
C
is
non-scattered
.

Proposition 4.5
.

(1)

Every infinite well-ordered chain is min-shiftable.

(2)

If
C
is a countable, non-scattered chain with a minimum, then
C
is min-shiftable.

(3)

If
D
is a subchain of
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{1}
with both a minimum and a maximum, then
D+\mathbf{R}
is min-shiftable.

In particular, the following chains are min-shiftable:

\mathbf{N},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Q},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{N}^{*}+\mathbf{Q},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Q},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{R},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{N}^{*}+\mathbf{R},\quad\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{R}.

Proof.

(1) An infinite well-ordered chain is isomorphic to
\mathbf{N}+D
for some well-ordered chain
D
; this is folklore, but follows from
[
11
, Theorem 2.8]
. Moreover,
\mathbf{N}+D
is isomorphic to
\mathbf{N}{\setminus}\{1\}+D
via the map given by
n\mapsto n+1
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) and
x\mapsto x
(
x\in D
).

(2) By
[
2
, Proposition 17]
, we may write
C
as
C=C_{0}+C^{\prime}+C_{1}
where
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
are scattered and
\mathbf{Q}
is a quotient of
C^{\prime}
. Note that
C_{0}
is non-empty, with
\min(C)=\min(C_{0}),
while
C_{1}
is possibly empty. By
[
2
, Proposition 16(1)]
, every countable chain is a quotient of
\mathbf{Q}
; in particular,
C_{0}+C^{\prime}
is a quotient of
\mathbf{Q}.
It follows that
C_{0}+C^{\prime}
is a quotient of its proper terminal segment
C^{\prime}
, and is hence min-shiftable by Proposition
4.3
. Thus, by Proposition
4.3
again,
C=(C_{0}+C^{\prime})+C_{1}
is min-shiftable.

(3) By definition,
D=\mathbf{1}+D^{\prime}
for some terminal segment
D^{\prime}
. It is easy to see that
D
is a quotient of
\mathbf{R}.
It follows that
D
is also a quotient of
D^{\prime}+\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{1},
since we can map
D^{\prime}
, if non-empty, to
\min(D),
and
\mathbf{1}
to
\max(D).
It can also easily be shown that
\mathbf{R}\cong\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{R}.
Consequently,
D+\mathbf{R}
is a quotient of
D^{\prime}+\mathbf{R}\cong(D^{\prime}+\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{1})+\mathbf{R}.

∎

One can easily find natural examples of chains that are
not
endpoint-shiftable. Certainly chains without endpoints are not endpoint-shiftable.
Further examples include chains with endpoints that do not embed into any of their proper initial or terminal segments. Such chains certainly include all finite chains, and also the chains
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z},

\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{1}.

Our next example is a chain with a minimum (but no maximum) that is not min-shiftable but has self-embeddings that move the minimum.

Example 4.6
.

Let
C=\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{R},
and let
z=\min(C).
Certainly
C
has self-embeddings that move
z,
since
C
may be embedded into
\mathbf{R}.
Suppose for a contradiction that
C
is min-shiftable, and let
\alpha\in\End(C)
be a right unit with
z\alpha>z.

If there were some
x\in\mathbf{Z}
with
x\alpha\in\mathbf{Z},
then
[z,x]\alpha=[z\alpha,x\alpha]
would be a finite interval of
\mathbf{Z},
but
[z,x]
is infinite, so this would contradict that
\alpha
is injective. Thus
\mathbf{Z}\alpha\cap\mathbf{Z}=\emptyset,
and hence
\mathbf{Z}\alpha\subseteq\mathbf{R}
.
Let

A=\{x\in\mathbf{R}:x\leq y\text{ for some }y\in\mathbf{Z}\alpha\}\quad\text{ and }\quad B=\{x\in\mathbf{R}:x>\mathbf{Z}\alpha\}.

Clearly
\mathbf{R}=A+B,
with
\mathbf{Z}\alpha\subseteq A
and
\mathbf{R}\alpha\subseteq B,
and
A
has no maximum as
\mathbf{Z}
has no maximum. Since
\mathbf{R}
is complete, the proper cut
(A,B)
is not a gap, and hence
B
has a minimum, say
x.
We cannot have
x\in\mathbf{R}\alpha,
since
\mathbf{R}
has no minimum, so
x\notin\im\alpha.
But then
\{t\in\im\alpha:t<x\}=(\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z})\alpha
has no maximum, and
\{t\in\im\alpha:t>x\}=\mathbf{R}\alpha
has no minimum, contradicting Corollary
3.4
.

Remark 4.7
.

(1)

By Proposition
4.5
, the chain
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Q}
is min-shiftable. The key reason that the trick used in Example
4.6
does not work for
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{Q}
is that
\mathbf{Q}
is not complete.

(2)

Clearly
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{R}
is a non-scattered chain with a minimum. Thus, Example
4.6
demonstrates the necessity of the chain
C
of Proposition
4.5
(2) being countable.

(3)

By a similar argument as that of Example
4.6
, the chain
\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{R}+\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{1}
is not endpoint-shiftable but has self-embeddings that move both endpoints.

5.
Proofs of the Main Results

In this section we prove the main results of the paper.
For what follows, recall that the constants maps
c_{x}
of the chain
C
belong to
\End(C),
and note that for each
\theta\in\End(C)
we have
\theta c_{x}=c_{x}
and
c_{x}\theta=c_{x\theta}
. We now quickly prove Theorem
A
.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Let
S=\End(C).
By
[
9
, Theorem 4.5.4]
, the diagonal left
S
-act is not finitely generated. Thus, by the dual of Proposition
2.3
, the left diameter of
S
is not 1.
Now pick any
x\in C,
and let
U=\{1_{C},c_{x}\}.
For any
\theta,\varphi\in S,
we have a
U
-sequence

\theta=\theta 1_{C},\ \theta c_{x}=\varphi c_{x},\ \varphi 1_{C}=\varphi

of length 2, so that
D_{l}(S;U)\leq 2.
Thus
D_{l}(S)=2.

∎

The proof of Theorem
B
is considerably more complicated. We begin by showing that the right diameter of
\End(C)
is either 2 or 3.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
C
is an infinite chain, then
D_{r}(\End(C))
is either 2 or 3.

Proof.

Let
S=\End(C).
By
[
9
, Theorem 4.5.5]
and Proposition
2.3
, the monoid
S
does not have right diameter 1. In fact, this can also be deduced quickly from Lemma
3.6
, as follows. We give the proof since it is short and introduces methods that will be used later.

Suppose for a contradiction that
D_{r}(S;U)=1
for some finite set
U\subseteq S.
Fix any
x\in C.
For each
\alpha,\beta\in U
where
\alpha
is a right unit, let
J_{\alpha,\beta}=\{(x\beta)\gamma:\gamma\in S,\alpha\gamma=1_{C}\},
and let
J
be the union of all the
J_{\alpha,\beta}
. The set
J
is finite since
U
is finite and, by Lemma
3.6
, each
J_{\alpha,\beta}
is finite. Now pick any
y\in C{\setminus}J.
Then, by assumption, there exist
\alpha,\beta\in U
and
\gamma\in S
such that
1_{C}=\alpha\gamma
and
c_{y}=\beta\gamma.
But then
y=(x\beta)\gamma\in J,
a contradiction. Thus
S
has does not have right diameter 1.

We now prove that
D_{r}(S)\leq 3.
Fix any
x,y\in C
with
x<y,
and let
U=\{1_{C},c_{x},c_{y}\}.
We claim that
\omega_{S}^{r}=\mbox{${\langle}$}U\mbox{${\rangle}$}
with
D_{r}(S;U)\leq 3.
Consider
\theta,\varphi\in S.
We may assume without loss of generality that
x\theta\leq x\varphi.

Define a map

\gamma:C\to C,\ w\mapsto\begin{cases}x\theta&\text{if }w\leq x\\
x\varphi&\text{if }w>x.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
\gamma\in S.
Moreover, we have a
U
-sequence

\theta=1_{C}\theta,\ c_{x}\theta=c_{x}\gamma,\ c_{y}\gamma=c_{x}\varphi,\ 1_{C}\varphi=\varphi

of length 3, as required.
∎

Next, we prove that the endomorphism monoid of an endpoint-shiftable chain has right diameter 2.

Proposition 5.2
.

If
C
is an endpoint-shiftable chain, then
D_{r}(\End(C))=2.

Proof.

By symmetry we may assume that
C
is min-shiftable.
Let
S=\End(C).
By Proposition
5.1
we have
D_{r}(S)\geq 2.
Now let
z
be the minimum of
C,
and let
\alpha\in S
be a right unit such that
z\alpha>z.
Letting
U=\{\alpha,c_{z}\},
we claim that
\omega_{S}^{r}=\mbox{${\langle}$}U\mbox{${\rangle}$}
and
D_{r}(S;U)=2.
So, consider
\theta\in S.
Since
\alpha
is a right unit of
S,
there exists
\beta\in S
such that
\alpha\beta=1_{C}
. Define a map

\gamma:C\to C,x\mapsto\begin{cases}x\beta\theta&\text{ if }x\geq z\alpha,\\
z&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is straightforward to show that
\gamma\in S.
For each
x\in C
we have
x\alpha\gamma=x\alpha\beta\theta=x\theta,
so

\theta=\alpha\gamma,\ c_{z}\gamma=c_{z}

is a
U
-sequence of length 1 from
\theta
to
c_{z}
. Since
\theta
is arbitrary, it follows that
D_{r}(S;U)\leq 2.
Thus
D_{r}(S)=2.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

It is perhaps interesting that in each of the proofs of Theorem
A
and Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
, to obtain the upper bound on the diameter, only one ‘skeleton’ is required to connect any pair
\theta,\varphi\in S=\End(C),
where the
skeleton
of a
U
-sequence (
U\subseteq S
) refers to the sequence of elements of
U
appearing in the
U
-sequence. For instance, the skeleton in the proof of Proposition
5.1
is
(1_{C},c_{x},c_{x},c_{y},c_{x},1_{C})
.

We now turn to prove the converse of Proposition
5.2
. Our first step is to show that in order for the endomorphism monoid of a chain to have right diameter 2, it is necessary that the chain have an endpoint.

Proposition 5.4
.

If
C
is a chain with no endpoints, then
D_{r}(\End(C))=3.

Proof.

Let
S=\End(C),
and let
R
denote the set of right units of
S.
Aiming for a contradiction, suppose that
D_{r}(S;U)=2
for some finite set
U\subseteq S.

For each
\delta,\varepsilon\in U
(not necessarily distinct) define

B(\delta,\varepsilon)=\{x\in C:x\delta\leq x\varepsilon\}.

Since
C=B(\delta,\varepsilon)\cup B(\varepsilon,\delta),
at least one of
B(\delta,\varepsilon)
or
B(\varepsilon,\delta)
is unbounded above. Let

V=\{(\delta,\varepsilon)\in U\times U:B(\delta,\varepsilon)\text{ is unbounded above}\}.

For each
(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V
fix some
x(\delta,\varepsilon)\in B(\delta,\varepsilon),
and let
m=\max\{x(\delta,\varepsilon):(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V\}.
Then
C=m^{\downarrow}+D,
where
D=\{c\in C:c>m\}.
By the unboundedness assumption, for each
(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V
we may fix some
y(\delta,\varepsilon)\in B(\delta,\varepsilon)\cap D
. Let

X=\{x(\delta,\varepsilon)\beta:(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V,\,\beta\in U\}\and Y=\{y(\delta,\varepsilon)\beta:(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V,\,\beta\in U\},

and note that
X
and
Y
are finite.
Since
C
has no endpoints, by Corollary
3.4
the image of any map in
R
is unbounded both above and below in
C.
Thus, for each
\alpha\in U\cap R
we may fix some
p_{\alpha},q_{\alpha}\in C
with
p_{\alpha}<q_{\alpha}
such that
p_{\alpha}\alpha<X
and
q_{\alpha}\alpha>Y.
We then let

p=\min\{p_{\alpha}:\alpha\in U\cap R\}\and q=\max\{q_{\alpha}:\alpha\in U\cap R\},

so that
p\alpha<X
and
q\alpha>Y
for each
\alpha\in U\cap R.

Now fix some
u,v\in C
with
u<p<q<v,
and define
\theta\in S
by
m^{\downarrow}\theta=\{u\}
and
D\theta=\{v\}.
Since
D_{r}(S)=2,
there exists a
U
-sequence

1_{C}=\alpha\gamma_{1},\ \beta\gamma_{1}=\delta\gamma_{2},\ \varepsilon\gamma_{2}=\theta.

Note that
\alpha
is a right unit. We consider two separate cases.

Case 1
.
Suppose first that
(\delta,\varepsilon)\in V
. Let
x=x(\delta,\varepsilon)
and
y=y(\delta,\varepsilon).
Then, since
x\delta\leq x\varepsilon
(as
x\in B(\delta,\varepsilon)
) and
p\alpha<x\beta\in X,
we have

u=x\theta=x\varepsilon\gamma_{2}\geq x\delta\gamma_{2}=x\beta\gamma_{1}\geq p\alpha\gamma_{1}=p1_{C}=p,

and this contradicts
u<p.

Case 2
. Finally, suppose that
(\varepsilon,\delta)\in V.
Let
x=x(\varepsilon,\delta)
and
y=y(\varepsilon,\delta).
Then, since
y\varepsilon\leq y\delta
(as
y\in B(\varepsilon,\delta)
) and
q\alpha>y\beta\in Y,
we have

v=y\theta=y\varepsilon\gamma_{2}\leq y\delta\gamma_{2}=y\beta\gamma_{1}\leq q\alpha\gamma_{1}=q1_{C}=q,

and this contradicts
v>q.

∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
B
, we show in Proposition
5.8
that
D_{r}(\End(C))=3
if
C
has an endpoint but is not endpoint-shiftable. First, we establish some technical lemmas. In what follows, a map
\alpha\in\End(C)
is said to
fix
a subset
A\subseteq C

pointwise
if
x\alpha=x
for all
x\in A.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
C
be an infinite chain that has an endpoint but is not endpoint-shiftable. Then
C=M+D+N
for some finite chains
M
and
N
(where at least one of
M
and
N
is non-empty) and some chain
D
with no endpoints. Moreover, every right/left unit of
\End(C)
fixes
M\cup N
pointwise and restricts to a right/left unit of
\End(D).

Proof.

Let
S=\End(C)
. If
C
has no minimum, then we define
M=\emptyset.
So now suppose that
C
has a minimum, say
z_{1}
. Then
C=\{z_{1}\}+C_{1}
, where
C_{1}=C{\setminus}\{z_{1}\}
. If
C_{1}
has a minimum, say
z_{2}
, then
C=\{z_{1}<z_{2}\}+C_{2}
, where
C_{2}=C_{1}{\setminus}\{z_{2}\}
. This process must terminate at some point, for otherwise we would have

C=\{z_{1}<z_{2}<z_{3}<\cdots\}+C^{\prime}\cong\mathbf{N}+C^{\prime},

which is min-shiftable by Propositions
4.3
and
4.5
(1). So, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
C=\{z_{1}<\dots<z_{m}\}+C_{m}
where
C_{m}
has no minimum. We let
M=\{z_{1}<\dots<z_{m}\}.
The definition of
N
is analogous, and then
C=M+D+N
where
D=C_{m}{\setminus}N
has no endpoints.

Now consider
\alpha,\beta\in S
such that
\alpha\beta=1_{C}
, and let
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
be the restrictions of
\alpha,\beta
to
D,
respectively. We show that
\alpha
and
\beta
both fix
M\cup N
pointwise, and
D\alpha,D\beta\subseteq D.
It then follows that
\alpha^{\prime}\beta^{\prime}=1_{D}
, as desired.

First, we show that
\beta
fixes
M\cup N
pointwise and
D\beta\subseteq D.
To do so, suppose to the contrary that there exists
u\in C
such that
u\beta\in M\cup N
with
u\neq u\beta.
We may assume without loss of generality that
u\beta\in M.
With
M=\{z_{1}<\cdots<z_{m}\}
as above, suppose that
u\beta=z_{k}
. Since
\beta
is surjective and order preserving, it follows that
u>z_{k}
, and hence
z_{k}^{\uparrow}
is a quotient of its proper terminal segment
u^{\uparrow}
.
Thus, by Proposition
4.3
, the chain
z_{k}^{\uparrow}
is min-shiftable.
Now, either
C=z_{k}^{\uparrow}
(and is hence min-shiftable) or else

C=\{z_{1}<\dots<z_{k-1}\}+z_{k}^{\uparrow}\cong(\mathbf{k-1})+z_{k}^{\uparrow},

which is min-shiftable by Proposition
4.3
. We have reached a contradiction.

Now, if
x\in M\cup N
then
x=(x\alpha)\beta=x\alpha,
as
\beta
fixes
M\cup N
pointwise and
D\beta\subseteq D.
Thus
\alpha
fixes
M\cup N
pointwise. Since
\alpha
is injective, it follows that
D\alpha\subseteq D,
as required.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
C,

D
and
E
be pairwise-disjoint chains with
D
infinite, and let
K
and
L
be finite subsets of
C
and
E,
respectively. Then there exists a homomorphism
\theta:C+D+E\to D
such that:

(1)

\theta
is injective on
K\cup L
;

(2)

if
C=\emptyset
and
D
has no minimum, then
\im\theta
is unbounded below;

(3)

if
E=\emptyset
and
D
has no maximum, then
\im\theta
is unbounded above.

Proof.

If
C=E=\emptyset,
let
\theta=1_{D}
. Suppose now that
C\cup E\neq\emptyset.
Pick
x\in D
such that, letting
A=D\cap x^{\downarrow}
and
B=(D\cap x^{\uparrow}){\setminus}\{x\},
we have
|A|>|K|
and
|B|>|L|.
Clearly
D=A+B.

If both
C
and
E
are non-empty, let
C\theta\subseteq A,

D\theta=\{x\}
and
E\theta\subseteq B
, with
\theta
preserving order and being injective on
K\cup L.
If
C=\emptyset
and
D
has no minimum, let
\theta
fix
A
pointwise, let
B\theta=\{x\},
and let
E\theta\subseteq B
with
\theta
preserving order and being injective on
L.
The case that
E=\emptyset
and
D
has no maximum is symmetrical.
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
C=M+D+N
be a chain where
M
and
N
are finite and
D
has no endpoints. Let

(A_{1},B_{1})<_{DM}(A_{2},B_{2})<_{DM}\cdots<_{DM}(A_{n},B_{n})

be a finite chain of gaps of
C,
and let
F
be a finite subset of
D.
Then there exist terminal segments
T_{k}

(\neq\emptyset)
of
A_{k}

(1\leq k\leq n)
, initial segments
I_{k}

(\neq\emptyset)
of
B_{k}

(1\leq k\leq n)
, and intervals
D_{p}
of
D

(1\leq p\leq n+1)
such that

D=D_{1}+T_{1}+I_{1}+D_{2}+T_{2}+I_{2}+\cdots+T_{n}+I_{n}+D_{n+1},\quad\>F\subseteq D_{1}+D_{2}+\cdots+D_{n+1},

and for each
p\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
the set
F\cap D_{p}
contains no endpoints of
D_{p}
.

Proof.

First note that

D=(A_{1}\cap D)+(B_{1}\cap A_{2})+(B_{2}\cap A_{3})+\cdots+(B_{n-1}\cap A_{n})+(B_{n}\cap D),

with each summand being unbounded above and below. For each
k\in\{2,\dots,n\}
choose
x_{k},y_{k}\in B_{k-1}\cap A_{k}
such that
x_{k}<B_{k-1}\cap A_{k}\cap F<y_{k}
. Also let
y_{1}\in A_{1}\cap D
and
x_{n+1}\in B_{n}\cap D

be such that
A_{1}\cap F<y_{1}
and
x_{n+1}<B_{n}\cap F.
We then define

D_{1}=y_{1}^{\downarrow}\cap D,\quad D_{k}=[x_{k},y_{k}]\,\ (2\leq k\leq n)\quad\text{and}\quad D_{n+1}=x^{\uparrow}_{n+1}\cap D.

These then determine appropriate subchains
T_{k},I_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq n
) such that

A_{1}\cap D=D_{1}+T_{1},\>B_{k-1}\cap A_{k}=I_{k-1}+D_{k}+T_{k}\ (2\leq k\leq n)\>\text{ and }\>B_{n}\cap D=I_{n}+D_{n+1}.

Verification of the claimed properties is routine.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

If
C
is an infinite chain that has an endpoint but is not endpoint-shiftable, then
D_{r}(\End(C))=3.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.5
we may write
C=M+D+N
, where
M
and
N
are finite and
D
is unbounded below and above. We assume without loss of generality that
M\neq\emptyset
with minimum element
z.

Let
S=\End(C),
and let
R
be the set of right units of
S.
Suppose for a contradiction that
D_{r}(S;U)=2
for some finite
U\subseteq S.

Now, it follows from Lemma
3.6
that the set

J=\{(z\alpha)\gamma:\alpha\in U,\,\gamma\text{ is a right inverse of some }\beta\in U\cap R\}

has size at most
2|U|^{2}.

For each non-injective
\alpha\in U
fix some
a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}\in C
with
a_{\alpha}\neq b_{\alpha}
and
a_{\alpha}\alpha=b_{\alpha}\alpha,
and let

K=\{a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha}:\alpha\in U,\,\alpha\text{ is not injective}\}.

Let
F=M\cup N\cup J\cup K,
and note that
F
is finite.

Now define a subset
T
of
U
as follows. If
N\neq\emptyset,
let
T
be the set of all injective
\delta\in U{\setminus}R.
If
N=\emptyset
, let
T
be the set of all injective
\delta\in U{\setminus}R
such that
\im\delta
is unbounded above.

The remainder of the proof splits into two cases.

Case 1
:
T=\emptyset
. Choose
x\in D
such that
x\geq F\cap D.
(If
F\cap D\neq\emptyset,
we can take
x=\text{max}(F\cap D)
; otherwise, we can take
x
to be any element of
D.
) Let
C^{\prime}=D{\setminus}x^{\downarrow}
, so that
C=x^{\downarrow}+C^{\prime}+N.
Then
F\cap C^{\prime}=\emptyset.
By Lemma
5.6
, there exists
\theta\in S
with
\im\theta\subseteq C^{\prime}
such that
\theta
is injective on
F,
and if
N=\emptyset
then
\im\theta
is unbounded above in
C^{\prime}.
Since
D_{r}(S;U)=2,
there exists a
U
-sequence

\theta=\alpha\gamma,\ \beta\gamma=\alpha^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime},\ \beta^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}=1_{C}.

The map
\alpha\in U
is injective, since otherwise

a_{\alpha}\theta=a_{\alpha}\alpha\gamma=b_{\alpha}\alpha\gamma=b_{\alpha}\theta,
contradicting that
\theta
is injective on
F\supseteq K.

Moreover, if
N=\emptyset
then
\im\alpha
is unbounded above, since if
\im\alpha\leq q
then
\im\theta=\im\alpha\gamma\leq q\gamma,
contradicting that
\im\theta
is unbounded above. Since
T=\emptyset,
we conclude that
\alpha\in R.
Then, since
C
is not endpoint-shiftable, it follows from Lemma
5.5
that
\alpha
fixes
M\cup N
pointwise. Thus
x\gamma=x\theta
for all
x\in M\cup N.
Now, we may pick
y\in C
such that
z\beta\leq y\alpha.
Indeed, if
N=\emptyset
then, as noted above, the set
\im\alpha
is unbounded above; otherwise, the chain
C
has a maximum, say
w,
and then
z\beta\leq w=w\alpha
(as
w\in N
). Therefore, since
z\in M,
we have
z\alpha=z\leq z\beta\leq y\alpha,
and hence

z\theta=z\alpha\gamma\leq z\beta\gamma\leq y\alpha\gamma=y\theta.

Since
\im\theta\subseteq C^{\prime}
, and
C^{\prime}
is an interval, it follows that
z\beta\gamma\in C^{\prime}.
But
z\beta\gamma=z\alpha^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}\in J\subseteq F,
contradicting the fact that
C^{\prime}\cap F=\emptyset.

Case 2
:
T\neq\emptyset.
Since each
\delta\in T
is injective but not a right unit, it must fail at least one of the conditions (a)-(c) of Corollary
3.4
(3). But
C
has a minimum, so (a) vacuously holds. If
C
has no maximum then
N=\emptyset,
and hence, by the definition of
T,
if
\delta\in T
then
\im\delta
is unbounded above, so
\delta
satisfies (b). Thus, each
\delta\in T
fails condition (c), and hence, by Remark
3.5
, there exists
x_{\delta}\in C{\setminus}\!\im\delta
such that
x_{\delta}>z\delta
and

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}:=(\{t\in C:t\delta<x_{\delta}\},\{t\in C:x_{\delta}<t\delta\})

is a gap of
C
(so
x_{\delta}\in D
).
Let

(A_{1},B_{1})<_{DM}(A_{2},B_{2})<_{DM}\cdots<_{DM}(A_{n},B_{n})

be the chain of distinct gaps among
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
(
\delta\in T
) in the Dedekind-MacNeille completion.

Let
P
be the set of all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in U\times U
with
\alpha\in R
and
z\beta\notin\im\alpha.
For each
(\alpha,\beta)\in P,
choose a right inverse
\lambda_{\alpha}
of
\alpha,
and let
u_{\alpha,\beta}=z\beta\lambda_{\alpha}
. Since, by Lemma
5.5
, the maps
\alpha
and
\lambda_{\alpha}
fix
M\cup N
pointwise and map
D
to
D,
and we have
z\beta\notin\im\alpha
by the definition of
P,
it follows that
u_{\alpha,\beta}=(z\beta)\lambda_{\alpha}\in D\lambda_{\alpha}\subseteq D.
Moreover, by Lemma
3.6
we have

u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha=\begin{cases}\max(\im\alpha\cap(z\beta)^{\downarrow})&\text{if }u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha<z\beta\\
\min(\im\alpha\cap(z\beta)^{\uparrow})&\text{if }u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha>z\beta.\end{cases}

Now, applying Lemma
5.7
to the gaps
(A_{k},B_{k})
(
1\leq k\leq n
) and the finite set

G=(F\cap D)\cup\{u_{\alpha,\beta}:(\alpha,\beta)\in P\}\>(\subseteq D),

there exist terminal segments
T_{k}
of
A_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq n
), initial segments
I_{k}
of
B_{k}

(1\leq k\leq n),
and intervals
D_{p}
of
D

(1\leq p\leq n+1)
such that

D=D_{1}+T_{1}+I_{1}+D_{2}+\cdots+D_{n}+T_{n}+I_{n}+D_{n+1},\quad\>G\subseteq D_{1}+D_{2}+\cdots+D_{n+1},

and for each
p\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
the set
G\cap D_{p}
contains no endpoints of
D_{p}
. Note that each
T_{k}
has no maximum, and each
I_{k}
has no minimum.

For
(\alpha,\beta)\in P,
with
u_{\alpha,\beta}\in D_{p}
, say, we fix elements
x_{\alpha,\beta},y_{\alpha,\beta}\in D_{p}
with
u_{\alpha,\beta}\in(x_{\alpha,\beta},y_{\alpha,\beta})
; such elements exist as
u_{\alpha,\beta}
is not an endpoint of
D_{p}
. Since
\alpha
is injective, we have
u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha\in(x_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha,y_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha).
It follows that
z\beta\in(x_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha,y_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha).
Indeed, if
u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha<z\beta,
then certainly
x_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha<z\beta,
and we have
z\beta<y_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha
as
u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha=\max(\im\alpha\cap(z\beta)^{\downarrow}).
The case in which
u_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha>z\beta
is similar.

Now, choose
x\in D_{n+1}+N
such that
x\geq F\cup\{y_{\alpha,\beta}:(\alpha,\beta)\in P\},
and write
D_{n+1}+N
as
D^{\prime}+E
where
D^{\prime}=(D_{n+1}+N)\cap x^{\downarrow}
. Observe that
E\cap F=\emptyset.
Moreover, if
N\neq\emptyset
then
x=\max(C),
in which case
E=\emptyset
; otherwise,
E
is unbounded above.
We now have

C=M+D_{1}+T_{1}+I_{1}+D_{2}+\cdots+D_{n}+T_{n}+I_{n}+D^{\prime}+E.

Using Lemma
5.6
, we choose
\theta\in S
such that:

(1)

(M+D_{1}+T_{1})\theta\subseteq T_{1}
with
\theta
injective on
M+(F\cap D_{1})
and with
T_{1}\theta
unbounded above in
T_{1}
;

(2)

for each
k\in\{2,\dots,n\},
we have
(D_{k}+T_{k})\theta\subseteq T_{k}
with
\theta
injective on
F\cap D_{k}
and with
T_{k}\theta
unbounded above in
T_{k}
;

(3)

(I_{n}+D^{\prime})\theta\subseteq I_{n}
with
\theta
injective on
F\cap D^{\prime}
and with
I_{n}\theta
unbounded below in
I_{n}
;

(4)

\theta
fixes
\bigcup_{j=1}^{n-1}I_{j}\cup E
pointwise.

Note that
\im\theta\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{n}(T_{k}+I_{k})\cup E.
Our construction of
\theta
guarantees that
\im\theta\cap F=\emptyset,
that
\theta
is injective on
F,
and that
\im\theta
is unbounded above in the case that
N=\emptyset.

Since
D_{r}(S;U)=2,
there exists a
U
-sequence

\theta=\alpha\gamma,\ \beta\gamma=\alpha^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime},\ \beta^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}=1_{C}.

By the same argument as that in
Case 1
, the map
\alpha\in U
is injective, and if
N=\emptyset
then
\im\alpha
is unbounded above.

Suppose first that
\alpha\in R,
and note that
z\beta\gamma=z\alpha^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime}\in J\subseteq F.
If
z\beta\in\im\alpha,
then
z\beta\gamma\in\im\alpha\gamma=\im\theta,
contradicting that
\im\theta\cap F=\emptyset.
Thus
z\beta\notin\im\alpha,
so that
(\alpha,\beta)\in P.
Recalling that
x_{\alpha,\beta},y_{\alpha,\beta}\in D_{p}
for some
p\in\{1,\dots,n+1\},
and that
z\beta\in(x_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha,y_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha),
we also have

z\beta\gamma\in[x_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha\gamma,y_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha\gamma]=[x_{\alpha,\beta}\theta,y_{\alpha,\beta}\theta]\subseteq D_{p}\theta\subseteq\begin{cases}T_{p}&\text{if }p\leq n\\
I_{n}&\text{if }p=n+1.\end{cases}

But then
z\beta\gamma\in F\cap D\subseteq G
and
z\beta\gamma\in T_{1}+\cdots+T_{n}+I_{n}
, contradicting the fact that
G\subseteq D_{1}+\cdots+D_{n+1}
.

Finally, suppose that
\alpha\notin R,
so that
\alpha\in T.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}=(A_{k},B_{k})
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,n\},
and
T_{k}\alpha<x_{\alpha}<I_{k}\alpha
. It follows that

T_{k}\theta=T_{k}\alpha\gamma\leq x_{\alpha}\gamma\leq I_{k}\alpha\gamma=I_{k}\theta.

Since
T_{k}\theta\subseteq T_{k}
and
I_{k}\theta\subseteq I_{k}
, and
I_{k}
covers
T_{k}
, it follows that
x_{\alpha}\gamma
is either an upper bound of
T_{k}\theta
in
T_{k}
, or else a lower bound of
I_{k}\theta
in
I_{k}
. But this contradicts the properties of
\theta
given in (1)–(4).
This completes the proof.
∎

Now, combining Propositions
5.1
,
5.2
,
5.4
and
5.8
, we obtain Theorem
B
.
