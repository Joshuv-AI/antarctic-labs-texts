High-order phase reduction for coupled 2D oscillators
Erik T.K. Mau, Michael Rosenblum, Arkady Pikovsky

Abstract
Phase reduction is a general approach to describe coupled oscillatory units in terms of their phases, assuming that the amplitudes are enslaved. The coupling should be small for such a reduction, but one also expects the reduction to be valid for finite coupling. This paper presents a general framework allowing us to obtain coupling terms in higher orders of the coupling parameter for generic two-dimensional oscillators and arbitrary coupling terms. The theory is illustrated with an accurate prediction of Arnold’s tongue for the van der Pol oscillator exploiting higher-order phase reduction.



The description of coupled oscillators is one of the basic problems in nonlinear dynamics. For weak coupling, the units remain oscillating but can adjust their phases. This adjustment results in synchronization and many other effects related to the phase dynamics. Representation in terms of phases yields a simplified yet good quantitative characterization of the oscillating systems. To achieve this, one needs an accurate reduction from the original equations of motion to the phase dynamics equations, typically obtained approximately in the first order of the coupling strength. In this paper, we provide, for two-dimensional self-sustained oscillators, a theoretical perturbative framework for an improved reduction, which produces phase equations as expansions in the orders of a small parameter describing coupling.

I
Introduction

Phase approximation is a powerful tool widely used to analyze the dynamics of interacting self-sustained
oscillators
Winfree 1980
;
Kuramoto 1984
;
Hoppensteadt and Izhikevich 1980
;
Pikovsky et al. 2001
;
Ermentrout and Terman 2010
;
Nakao 2016
;
Monga et al. 2018
;
Pietras and Daffertshofer 2019
.
This approach parametrizes each limit-cycle system with only one variable, the phase, and thus reduces the dimensionality of the problem. Behind this reduction lies the assumption that the amplitudes are enslaved variables following the evolution of the phases. In many cases, the reduced equations yield an analytical solution, with the celebrated Kuramoto model being an example. Even when one has to analyze the phase dynamics numerically, the approach greatly simplifies the original problem because only one variable has to be followed for each oscillator.

Technically, the reduction to the phase dynamics relies on the smallness of the terms defining forcing or coupling of limit-cycle oscillators and, of course, on the proper definition of the phase. In the first order in the small parameter, one neglects the deviations of the amplitudes from the limit cycle, so only information about the phase in the vicinity of the limit cycle is needed (in the form of a set of isochrons or as a phase sensitivity function). However, one expects that the phase reduction is also valid for finite perturbation as long as the dynamics lie on an attracting high-dimensional torus spanned by the phases of interacting limit-cycle oscillators. For this, one needs to know the deviations of the amplitudes.
Despite the number of attempts to account for these deviations and thus go beyond the first approximation in the coupling
strength
Kurebayashi et al. 2013
;
Monga et al. 2018
;
Wilson and Ermentrout 2018a
;
Mauroy and Mezíc 2018
;
Wilson and Ermentrout 2019
;
Rosenblum and Pikovsky 2019a
;
Rosenblum and Pikovsky 2019b
;
León and Pazó 2019
;
Pérez-Cervera et al. 2020
;
Gengel et al. 2021
;
Kurebayashi et al. 2022
;
Bick et al. 2023
, the high-order phase reduction remains challenging.

In this communication, we describe the derivation of the high-order phase dynamics equations for generic two-dimensional limit-cycle oscillators.
Our technique relies on the normal form
phase-amplitude representation
Shilnikov et al. 1998
of the dynamics of a two-dimensional oscillator near the limit cycle.
For an illustration of the normal form, consider the Stuart-Landau oscillator

\dot{a}=(\zeta+\mathrm{i}\eta)a-(1+\mathrm{i}\alpha)|a|^{2}a\;,

where
a
is a complex variable and
\zeta,\eta,\alpha
are parameters.
Writing
a=\rho e^{i\theta}
, one easily checks that for
\zeta>0
the system has a stable circular limit cycle
with radius
\sqrt{\zeta}
. The
transformation
Wilson and Ermentrout 2018b

\displaystyle\phi(\rho,\theta)=\theta-\ln(\rho/\sqrt{\zeta})\;,\quad r=c(1-\zeta/\rho^{2})\;,

(1)

where
c
is any non-zero factor,
recasts the systems to the autonomous normal form
Shilnikov et al. 1998

\dot{\phi}=\eta-\alpha\zeta=\omega\;,\quad\dot{r}=\kappa r

(2)

in the whole basin of the limit cycle.
Here
\phi
is the phase,
\omega
is the frequency, and
\kappa=-2\zeta
is the Floquet exponent. Variable
r
quantifies deviation from the limit cycle; for brevity, we will call
r
the amplitude (it is also known as the isostable variable).

Essential for our analysis below is that for an
arbitrary smooth 2D system
, there exists a smooth variable substitution,
reducing this system to the normal form (
2
) near a periodic trajectory,
see Theorem 3.23 in Ref.
Shilnikov et al. 1998
. For higher-dimensional systems, the normal form can be more complex (e.g., for degenerate eigenvalues and in the case of resonances); this is a subject for future research.

In this communication, we exploit the perturbation technique to derive the phase coupling functions as a series in powers of the coupling strength
\varepsilon
. Our procedure is closely related to that of Gengel et al.
Gengel et al. 2021
but is not restricted to the Stuart-Landau system. First, we outline the derivation of the terms for a general system of
M
coupled 2D units. Next, we explicitly write the terms up to the order
\varepsilon^{3}
for two coupled oscillators and illustrate the approach by an application to the paradigmatic van der Pol model.

II
Generic many-body couplings

In this section, we sketch the derivation of the non-trivial
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
terms in the phase reduction for
M
generically coupled two-dimensional oscillators. Moreover, we outline the procedure to derive terms of arbitrary order.

We start by writing general equations for two-dimensional limit-cycle systems with states
{\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, indexed by
\mu\in\{1,\dots,M\}
:

\displaystyle\dot{\bf x}_{\mu}

\displaystyle={\bf G}_{\mu}({\bf x}_{\mu})+\varepsilon{\bf S}_{\mu}({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{M})\,.

(3)

Here
{\bf G}_{\mu}
determines the autonomous evolution of oscillator
\mu
, and
{\bf S}_{\mu}
encodes the coupling of this unit to all other oscillators. We assume that
{\bf G}_{\mu}
and
{\bf S}_{\mu}
are sufficiently smooth functions in all arguments. Since all systems exhibit stable limit cycles, for each uncoupled unit there exists a smooth transformation to coordinates
\phi_{\mu}=\Phi_{\mu}({\bf x}_{\mu})
and
r_{\mu}=P_{\mu}({\bf x}_{\mu})
which obey linear normal form equations for each oscillator
Shilnikov et al. 1998

(cf. Eq. (
2
)):

\dot{\phi}_{\mu}=\omega_{\mu}\,,\quad\dot{r}_{\mu}=\kappa_{\mu}r_{\mu}\,,

(4)

where
\omega_{\mu}
is the frequency of the limit-cycle oscillation and
\kappa_{\mu}<0
is the real-valued Floquet exponent.

The transformation functions fulfill the following equations

\displaystyle\omega_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mu}}\Phi_{\mu}\cdot{\bf G}_{\mu}\;,

(5)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu}P_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mu}}P_{\mu}\cdot{\bf G}_{\mu}\,.

(6)

Thus, the dynamics can be expressed in the phase-amplitude variables as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mu}+\varepsilon\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{r})\;,

(7)

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\mu}r_{\mu}+\varepsilon\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{r})\;,

(8)

where
\vec{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{M})^{\top}
and
\vec{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{M})^{\top}
. Here,
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
are the coupling functions in terms of the phases and the amplitudes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mu}}\Phi_{\mu}\cdot{\bf S}_{\mu}({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{M})|_{\vec{\phi},\vec{r}}\;,

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{r})=

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bf x}_{\mu}}P_{\mu}\cdot{\bf S}_{\mu}({\bf x}_{1},\dots,{\bf x}_{M})|_{\vec{\phi},\vec{r}}\,.

(10)

We remark that Eqs. (
7
-
10
) are equivalent to Eq. (
3
) as long as all states
{\bf x}_{\mu}
are in the domain of validity of transformations
{\bf x}\to(\phi,r)
. One can argue that this domain extends to the whole basin of attraction of the respective limit cycle
Turaev 2023
. However, we do not rely on this since the perturbation technique operates only in close vicinity of the cycle. So far, there has been no dimension reduction, and the new system has the same dimension
2M
. We also note that we use the normal form of all oscillators separately and do not perform the normal form analysis of the coupled system
Ashwin and Rodrigues 2016
;
Nijholt et al. 2022
; thus, no resonant/non-resonant conditions appear below.

We aim to derive a reduced model incorporating only the phases
\phi_{\mu}
. We achieve that by assuming that for a given (small) coupling strength
\varepsilon
, the dynamics, possibly after a transient time, is restricted to a
M
-dimensional torus fully parametrized by the phases. In other words, we assume that, in the long-time evolution, the amplitudes
r_{\mu}=R_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})
are functions of phases. Then, we write the asymptotic phase dynamics as

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\mu}=\omega_{\mu}+\varepsilon\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{R}(\vec{\phi}))\,,

(11)

where
\vec{R}=(R_{1},\dots,R_{M})^{\top}
. Since for
\varepsilon=0
one has
R_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})=0
, we expect that
R_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})
are small for small
\varepsilon
. Thus, we adopt a standard perturbation approach and represent the unknown functions as power series in
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n}(\vec{\phi})\varepsilon^{n}\,,\;R_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}R_{\mu;n}(\vec{\phi})\varepsilon^{n}\,.

(12)

Although we expect that the expansion for
R_{\mu}
starts with a linear term
\sim\varepsilon
, we start the formal expansion from
n=0
for simplicity of notations; later, we will see that
R_{\mu;0}=0
.

Keeping for definiteness the terms up to the order
\varepsilon^{3}
, we can represent the phase dynamics as:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\mu}=\omega_{\mu}+\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;0}\varepsilon+\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;1}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;2}\varepsilon^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})\,.

(13)

Here and in the following, we omit the functions’ arguments
\vec{\phi}
.

To eliminate the amplitudes from the model, we need an equation that determines
R_{\mu}
; or, equivalently, a set of equations that determine
R_{\mu,n}
in different orders
\varepsilon^{n}
. First, generally
\dot{r}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\dot{\phi}_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu}\,.

(14)

Equating Eq. (
14
) with the r.h.s of Eq. (
8
), substituting
\dot{\phi}_{\nu}
by the r.h.s of Eq. (
7
) and rearranging terms yields

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu}R_{\mu}-\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\omega_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu}=\varepsilon\left(\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu}-\mathcal{F}_{\mu}\right)\,.

(15)

We remark that we use a notation
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi},\vec{R}(\vec{\phi}))=\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}(\vec{\phi})
and analogously for
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
. Equation (
15
) with
2\pi
-periodic boundary conditions defines
R_{\mu}
, but is not immediately solvable. Therefore, we use the expansion (
12
) intending to obtain a set of equations to solve for
R_{\mu;n}
consecutively, starting with
n=1
. By inserting Eq. (
12
) for
R_{\mu}
, an
\varepsilon
-expansion for
\mathcal{F}
(analogous to
\mathcal{Q}
in Eq. (
12
)), and the term

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{\nu;\ell}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu;n-\ell}\varepsilon^{n}\,,

(16)

which follows from the Cauchy product formula, into Eq. (
15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\kappa_{\mu}R_{\mu;n}-\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\omega_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu;n}\right)\varepsilon^{n}=\varepsilon\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{\mu;n}\varepsilon^{n}\,.

(17)

Here,
C_{\mu;n}
is defined as

\displaystyle C_{\mu;n}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\mathcal{Q}_{\nu;\ell}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu;n-\ell}-\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n}\,.

(18)

By matching terms of the same power in
\varepsilon
in Eq. (
17
), we obtain a set of equations determining all
R_{\mu;n}
. First, the terms of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})
yield
R_{\mu;0}=0
, reflecting that the amplitudes vanish asymptotically without coupling.
Next, by writing for clarity the arguments of the unknown terms explicitly, we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mu}R_{\mu;n}(\vec{\phi})-\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\omega_{\nu}\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}R_{\mu;n}(\vec{\phi})=C_{\mu;n-1}(\vec{\phi})

(19)

for all
n\geq 1
. This is an inhomogeneous linear partial differential equation; the r.h.s. comes from the previous order of expansion and is a known function of
\vec{\phi}
. Because the unknown functions
R_{\mu;n}(\vec{\phi})
are
2\pi
-periodic in their arguments, we straightforwardly write the Green’s function of the equation in the Fourier space, cf.
Gengel et al. 2021
. The solution reads:

\displaystyle R_{\mu;n}=\Xi_{\mu}[C_{\mu;n-1}]\;,

(20)

where the operator
\Xi_{\mu}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mu}[f](\vec{\phi})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{f(\vec{\phi}-\vec{\varphi})}{(2\pi)^{M}}\sum_{\vec{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{M}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\varphi}}}{\kappa_{\mu}+\mathrm{i}\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\omega}}\,\mathrm{d}\vec{\varphi}

(21)

and
f
is a
2\pi
-periodic test function. Here,
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\omega}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}\alpha_{\nu}\omega_{\nu}
denotes the scalar product, and same for
\vec{\alpha}\cdot\vec{\varphi}
. The operator
\Xi_{\mu}
is linear and commutes with each
\partial_{\phi_{\nu}}
.
Noteworthy, the denominator in (
21
) does not vanish for any
\vec{\alpha}
; thus, there are no small divisors in this perturbation technique.

Equation (
20
) yields an expression for each
R_{\mu;n}
. However, the functions
\mathcal{Q}_{\nu;m}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu;m}
appearing in
C_{\mu;n}
are not directly available from the definitions of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{F}
given by Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
). To write an expression for
R_{\mu;n}
in terms of the original coupling functions, we additionally need to express
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{F}
from Eqs. (
7
,
8
) as expansions in powers of
R_{\mu}
(these expansions are well-defined because
R_{\mu}\sim\varepsilon
). We write

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{k}\text{ with }\sum_{\nu}k_{\nu}=n\end{subarray}}Q_{\mu;\vec{k}}\prod_{\nu=1}^{M}R_{\nu}^{k_{\nu}}\,,

(22)

where
\vec{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{M}
denotes a multi-index. This expression is practical, since
Q_{\mu;\vec{k}}
can be obtained from the derivative of
\mathcal{Q}
with respect to
r
evaluated at the limit cycle:

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;\vec{k}}=\left(\prod_{\nu=1}^{M}k_{\nu}!\partial_{r_{\nu}}^{k_{\nu}}\right)\mathcal{Q}|_{\vec{r}=0}\,.

(23)

We will use the same notation for
\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{k}\text{ with }\sum_{\nu}k_{\nu}=n\end{subarray}}F_{\mu;\vec{k}}\prod_{\nu=1}^{M}R_{\nu}^{k_{\nu}}\,.

(24)

By inserting the
\varepsilon
-expansions of
R_{\mu}
from Eq. (
12
) into Eq. (
22
) we identify each
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n}
(or
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n}
) with an expression containing
R_{\nu;m}
and
Q_{\nu;\vec{k}}
(or
F_{\nu;\vec{k}}
) by collecting terms of the same power in
\varepsilon
. Substituting these expressions in (
18
), we obtain the r.h.s. for determining the amplitudes in the next order, etc.
For the terms
\mathcal{O}(1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;0}=Q_{\mu;(0,\dots,0)}

(25)

directly. This corresponds to the standard Winfree form if the direction of the driving term
{\bf S}_{\mu}
is constant and independent of the state of the system
\mu
.

Next, the terms of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
yield

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;1}=\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}Q_{\mu;\vec{e}_{\nu}}R_{\nu;1}\,,

(26)

where
\vec{e}_{\nu}
is
0
everywhere except for the
\nu
-th place, where it is
1
.
We obtain
C_{\mu;0}
from Eq. (
18
) as

\displaystyle C_{\mu;0}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{F}_{\mu;0}=-F_{\mu;(0,\dots,0)}

(27)

and write according to Eq.
20
:

\displaystyle R_{\mu;1}=-\Xi_{\mu}[F_{\mu;(0,\dots,0)}]\;.

(28)

We finally obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;1}=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}Q_{\mu;\vec{e}_{\nu}}\Xi_{\nu}[F_{\nu;(0,\dots,0)}]\,.

(29)

Eq. (
29
) yields the first non-trivial term of the phase-reduced model of generically coupled two-dimensional oscillators. We demonstrate the advantage of the corresponding
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
phase model over the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
model in Sec.
IV
.
We remind that we can analogously conclude

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu;1}=-\sum_{\nu=1}^{M}F_{\mu;\vec{e}_{\nu}}\Xi_{\nu}[F_{\nu;(0,\dots,0)}]\,.

(30)

Now, we highlight that the procedure used to derive
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;1}
can be further exploited to derive
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n}
by iterations for an arbitrarily large
n
. Assume we have appropriate expressions for all functions up to and including order
n
, i.e.,
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n}
as well as
R_{\mu;n}
. We want to obtain the terms of next highest order
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n+1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n+1}
and
R_{\mu;n+1}
. To start with the latter, we use Eq. (
20
), which requires
C_{\mu;n}
. We check in Eq. (
18
) that
C_{\mu;n}
requires only terms up to order
n
. Thus, we obtain
R_{\mu;n+1}
. To infer
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n+1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n+1}
, we need to collect the terms of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{n+1})
in Eq. (
22
). Those will contain the accessible functions
Q_{\nu;\vec{k}}
and
F_{\nu;\vec{k}}
and
R_{\nu;m}
where
m\in\{1,\dots,n+1\}
and
\nu\in\{1,\dots,M\}
. Thus, we also obtain
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n+1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n+1}
, what closes the iteration loop.

Though the evaluation of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;n}
becomes cumbersome very quickly, one can, in principle, continue to derive them for an arbitrarily large
n
by repeating that procedure. The required functions
Q_{\nu;\vec{k}}
and
F_{\nu;\vec{k}}
can be computed from the phase-amplitude transformation in the vicinity of the limit cycle, which can be obtained numerically (see Ref.
Wilson 2020
and appendix
A
).
We will demonstrate this procedure by deriving
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;2}
, and consequently the
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{3})
phase model, for the special case of two coupled oscillators in Sec.
III
.

III
Higher-order coupling functions for two coupled oscillators

For the case of two coupled oscillators, many expressions simplify.
The next term in the expansion for
\mathcal{Q}
reads

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;2}=Q_{\mu;(2,0)}R^{2}_{1;1}+Q_{\mu;(1,0)}R_{1;2}\\
+Q_{\mu;(1,1)}R_{1;1}R_{2;1}+Q_{\mu;(0,1)}R_{2;2}+Q_{\mu;(0,2)}R^{2}_{2;1}\,.\end{gathered}

(31)

To evaluate that, we require
R_{\mu;2}
and, thus, also
C_{\mu;1}
. Setting
n=1
in Eq. (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle C_{\mu;1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1;0}\partial_{\phi_{1}}R_{\mu;1}+\mathcal{Q}_{2;0}\partial_{\phi_{2}}R_{\mu;1}-\mathcal{F}_{\mu;1}\;.

(32)

Replacing
R_{\mu;1}
(Eq. (
28
)),
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;0}
(Eq. (
25
)), and
\mathcal{F}_{\mu;1}
(analogous to Eq. (
29
)) yields

\displaystyle C_{\mu;1}=\quad

\displaystyle Q_{1;(0,0)}\Xi_{\mu}[\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{\mu;(0,0)}]+Q_{2;(0,0)}\Xi_{\mu}[\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{\mu;(0,0)}]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle F_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}]-F_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}]\,.

(33)

Thus, we get

\displaystyle R_{\mu;2}=\quad

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mu}[Q_{1;(0,0)}\Xi_{\mu}[\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{\mu;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mu}[Q_{2;(0,0)}\Xi_{\mu}[\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{\mu;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mu}[F_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\Xi_{\mu}[F_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}]]\,.

(34)

We obtain
\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;2}
by inserting
R_{\mu;1}
(Eq. (
28
)) and
R_{\mu;2}
(Eq. (
34
)) into Eq. (
31
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\mu;2}=\quad

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(2,0)}(\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}])^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[Q_{1;(0,0)}\Xi_{1}[\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{1;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[Q_{2;(0,0)}\Xi_{1}[\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{1;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(1,1)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}]\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[Q_{1;(0,0)}\Xi_{2}[\partial_{\phi_{1}}F_{2;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[Q_{2;(0,0)}\Xi_{2}[\partial_{\phi_{2}}F_{2;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(1,0)}\Xi_{1}[F_{1;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,1)}\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}]]

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle Q_{\mu;(0,2)}(\Xi_{2}[F_{2;(0,0)}])^{2}\,.

(35)

This completes the derivation of the coupling function for two oscillators up to order
\varepsilon^{3}
.

IV
Higher-order coupling for a driven system

We illustrate the general results of Sec.
II
for the simplest case of a harmonically driven van der Pol oscillator

\displaystyle\dot{x}

\displaystyle=y+\varepsilon\cos(\phi_{2})\;,

(36)

\displaystyle\dot{y}

\displaystyle=ay(1-x^{2})-x\;,

(37)

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}\;,

(38)

where we set
a=1.4
. In the following, we will refer to it as the ’full model’. Here,
{\bf x}_{1}=(x,y)
is the state of the van der Pol oscillator, i.e., oscillator
1
. Oscillator
2
represents a mere driving: since
{\bf S}_{2}=0
here, the response functions
\mathcal{Q}_{2}=\mathcal{F}_{2}=0
and the amplitude deviation
r_{2}=0
of the second oscillator vanish.

Since
{\bf S}_{1}
is independent of the state of oscillator
1
and constant in direction (because it enters only in one Eq. (
36
)), we can factorize the
coupling functions:
\mathcal{Q}_{1}(\phi_{1},r_{1},\phi_{2})=Z(\phi_{1},r_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})
, where
Z=\nabla\Phi\cdot(1,0)^{\top}
, and
\mathcal{F}_{1}(\phi_{1},r_{1},\phi_{2})=I(\phi_{1},r_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})
, where
I=\nabla P\cdot(1,0)^{\top}
. Evaluated at the limit cycle
r_{1}=0
,
Z(\phi_{1},0)
and
I(\phi_{1},0)
represent the standard phase and amplitude response curves, and we denote them as
Z_{0}(\phi_{1})
and
I_{0}(\phi_{1})
in the following. Moreover, we define
Z_{1}(\phi_{1})=\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{1}}\left.Z(\phi_{1},r_{1})\right|_{r_{1}=0}

Thus, the derivatives of the response functions

,
\mathcal{F}_{1}

\displaystyle Q_{1,(1,0)}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=Z_{1}(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\;,

\displaystyle F_{1,(0,0)}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=I_{0}(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\;,

(41)

and we conclude

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1;0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=Z_{0}(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\;,

(42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{1;1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=-Z_{1}(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})\Xi_{1}[I_{0}(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})]\,.

(43)

\Xi_{1}

Fourier modes to approximate

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{1}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}+\mathcal{Q}_{1;0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\varepsilon+\mathcal{Q}_{1;1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\varepsilon^{2}\;,

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{2}\;,

(45)

where

and

(required to evaluate the operator

\varepsilon=0

\varepsilon

V
Discussion

Summarizing, we presented a general framework for performing phase reduction for limit-cycle oscillators in higher orders of a small parameter that determines the coupling and/or forcing. The approach exploits the normal-form coordinates introduced separately for each oscillator. According to the general theory of smooth dynamical systems, these coordinates exist for arbitrary two-dimensional oscillators with a limit cycle. The situation is more subtle in a higher-dimensional case and will be considered elsewhere; see also
von der Gracht et al. 2023
. While the normal coordinates are proven to exist, their practical implementation needs a strongly nonlinear analysis of the original equations, which can be performed numerically, as outlined in appendix
A
. The resulting coupling terms (Eqs. (
25
,
29
) in general
M
-dimensional case and Eq. (
35
) for two coupled oscillators) are obtained through an iterative procedure, the only nontrivial element of which is solving a linear PDE for the amplitude deviations.

We stress that our approach applies to a generic coupling, not only to a pair-wise one, as often assumed in the analysis of oscillator populations. Of course, for pair-wise couplings, some expressions can be potentially simplified. We notice that for a pair-wise coupling, the
phase-coupling terms remain pair-wise in the leading order, but higher-order terms contain many-body (triplet, quadruplet, etc.) interactions; see discussion in Ref.
Gengel et al. 2021
. We also stress that the present approach does not allow for calculating the range of validity (in terms of the perturbation strength
\varepsilon
) of the derived phase equations.
One can assume that the equations are valid as long as the amplitudes are algebraic functions of the phases. This is equivalent to the condition that an invariant torus exists in the system’s phase space. This condition is also used in a similar technique to obtain high-order phase equations
von der Gracht et al. 2023
, which appeared after the present study had been completed.

Appendix A
Obtaining the normal form (phase-isostable) transformation close to the limit cycle

To compute the phase-amplitude coupling functions
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{F}
that are necessary for the construction of a phase model, we need the phase-amplitude transformations
\Phi
and
P
, at least in the vicinity of the limit cycle. These functions allow for representing the corresponding Jacobian matrix
{\bf A}
of the transformation as an expansion in powers of
r
:

\displaystyle{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}\Phi&\partial_{y}\Phi\\
\partial_{x}P&\partial_{y}P\\
\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\bf A}_{n}\frac{r^{n}}{n!}\,.

(46)

With that construction, the information for the
n
-th derivative of
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{F}
with respect to
r
evaluated at
r=0
is contained in the phase-dependent matrices
{\bf A}_{1},\dots,{\bf A}_{n}
. For the purpose of deriving an
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
phase model we need
{\bf A}_{0}
and
{\bf A}_{1}
. We will detail their inference from the dynamical model in the following.

First, we define the reverse transformation from the phase and the amplitude
to Cartesian coordinates
{\bf x}=(x,y)^{\top}
as
{\bf X}(\phi,r)=(X(\phi,r),Y(\phi,r))^{\top}
and write
{\bf X}
as an asymptotic expansion in
r
:

\displaystyle{\bf X}(\phi,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\bf X}_{n}(\phi)\frac{r^{n}}{n!}\;,

(47)

where
{\bf X}_{n}=(X_{n},Y_{n})^{\top}
. Its Jacobian matrix
{\bf J}
reads

\displaystyle{\bf J}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\phi}X&\partial_{r}X\\
\partial_{\phi}Y&\partial_{r}Y\\
\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\bf J}_{n}\frac{r^{n}}{n!}\;,

(48)

where

\displaystyle{\bf J}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\phi}X_{n}&X_{n+1}\\
\partial_{\phi}Y_{n}&Y_{n+1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(49)

Since
{\bf J}
is the inverse of
{\bf A}
, the equation
\mathbb{1}={\bf A}{\bf J}
holds and we conclude

{\bf A}_{0}={\bf J}_{0}^{-1}
by setting
r=0
. Moreover, we obtain

\partial_{r}{\bf A}=-{\bf A}\cdot\partial_{r}{\bf J}\cdot{\bf A}

by differentiating with respect to
r
, ultimately leading to

{\bf A}_{1}=-{\bf A}_{0}{\bf J}_{1}{\bf A}_{0}\,

by setting
r=0
. Thus, to obtain the matrix elements of
{\bf A}_{1}
and
{\bf A}_{0}
, we need
{\bf J}_{0}
and
{\bf J}_{1}
, hence
{\bf X}_{0}
,
{\bf X}_{1}
and
{\bf X}_{2}
.

The limit cycle
{\bf X}_{0}
can be obtained by integrating the system forward in time sufficiently long. This also yields the period
T=2\pi/\omega
of the system. An arbitrarily chosen point on the limit cycle
{\bf X}_{0}(0)
is assigned the phase
\phi=0
.

In the next step, we compute
{\bf X}_{1}
and
\kappa
from the linearization around the limit cycle. Using the autonomous phase-amplitude dynamical equations

\dot{\phi}=\omega
,
\dot{r}=\kappa

we find

\displaystyle\dot{\bf x}={\bf G}({\bf X}(\phi,r))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\omega\partial_{\phi}{\bf X}_{n}+n\kappa{\bf X}_{n})\frac{r^{n}}{n!}

(50)

by differentiating Eq. (
47
) with respect to time
t
. By evaluating
\partial_{r}{\bf G}({\bf X}(\phi,r))
at
r=0
we thus conclude

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0}(\phi))\cdot{\bf X}_{1}=\omega\partial_{\phi}{\bf X}_{1}+\kappa{\bf X}_{1}\;,

(51)

where
{\bf D}_{\bf G}
is the Jacobian of
{\bf G}
defined by

\displaystyle{\bf D}_{\bf G}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x}{\bf G}_{x}&\partial_{y}{\bf G}_{x}\\
\partial_{x}{\bf G}_{y}&\partial_{y}{\bf G}_{y}\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(52)

Rearranging terms, we find

\omega\partial_{\phi}{\bf X}_{1}=({\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0}(\phi))-\kappa\mathbb{1})\cdot{\bf X}_{1}

and given the transformation

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{1}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa t}{\bf X}_{1}(\omega t)

(53)

we arrive at the standard linearized dynamical equation for small deviations around the limit cycle

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf u}}_{1}={\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0}(\omega t))\cdot{\bf u}_{1}\,.

(54)

According to Floquet theory, Eq. (
54
) is solved by

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{1}(t)={\bf M}(t)\cdot{\bf u}_{1}(0)\;,

(55)

where
{\bf M}
is the principal fundamental solution, that we find by numerically integrating Eq. (
54
) in the interval
0\leq t\leq T
with initial conditions
{\bf u}_{1}(0)\in\{(1,0)^{\top},(0,1)^{\top}\}
. The non-unity eigenvalue of the monodromy matrix
{\bf M}(T)
is the Floquet multiplier
\mathrm{e}^{\kappa T}
from which we derive the real Floquet exponent
\kappa<0
.

Thus, transforming back to
{\bf X}_{1}
yields

{\bf X}_{1}(\phi)=\mathrm{e}^{-\kappa\phi/\omega}{\bf M}\left(\phi/\omega\right){\bf X}_{1}(0)
,
\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi)
.
Since we require
{\bf X}_{1}(2\pi)={\bf X}_{1}(0)
, the initial condition
{\bf X}_{1}(0)
has to be an eigenvector of
{\bf M}(T)
corresponding to the non-unity Floquet multiplier. We fix the scaling of the isostable coordinate by choosing
||{\bf X}_{1}(0)||=-1
inside the limit cycle, where
||\cdot||
denotes the standard Euclidean norm.

To find
{\bf X}_{2}
, we again employ Eq. (
50
) to obtain

\displaystyle{\bf C}({\bf X}_{0},{\bf X}_{1})+{\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0})\cdot{\bf X}_{2}=\omega\partial_{\phi}{\bf X}_{2}+2\kappa{\bf X}_{2}\,.

(56)

Here and in the following, we omit the notation of argument
\phi
for conciseness. The term
{\bf C}
is defined component-wisely as

\displaystyle{\bf C}_{x,y}({\bf X}_{0},{\bf X}_{1})

\displaystyle={\bf X}_{1}^{\top}\cdot{\bf H}_{{\bf G}_{x,y}}({\bf X}_{0})\cdot{\bf X}_{1}

(57)

where
{\bf H}
is the Hessian matrix defined as

\displaystyle{\bf H}_{f}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial^{2}_{x}f&\partial_{y}\partial_{x}f\\
\partial_{x}\partial_{y}f&\partial^{2}_{y}f\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(58)

We get the equation for
{\bf X}_{2}
by rearranging terms as

\displaystyle\omega\partial_{\phi}{\bf X}_{2}=({\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0})-2\kappa\mathbb{1}){\bf X}_{2}+{\bf C}({\bf X}_{0},{\bf X}_{1})\,.

(59)

By introducing
{\bf u}_{2}
as

{\bf u}_{2}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{2\kappa t}{\bf X}_{2}(\omega t)\;,

we derive its dynamical equation as

\displaystyle\dot{{\bf u}}_{2}={\bf D}_{\bf G}({\bf X}_{0}(\omega t)){\bf u}_{2}+{\bf C}({\bf X}_{0}(\omega t),{\bf u}_{1}(t))\;,

(60)

where we used Eq. (
53
) to replace
{\bf X}_{1}
by
{\bf u}_{1}
. Since
{\bf X}_{0}
and
{\bf u}_{1}
are known, this inhomogeneous linear ODE can be solved with Floquet theory. In fact, we employ the principal fundamental solution
{\bf M}(t)
from Eq. (
55
) to write the general solution as

\displaystyle{\bf u}_{2}(t)={\bf M}(t){\bf u}_{2}(0)+\tilde{{\bf u}}_{2}(t)\,.

(61)

Here,
\tilde{{\bf u}}_{2}
is the special solution to Eq. (
60
) with initial condition
\tilde{{\bf u}}_{2}=0
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle{\bf X}_{2}(\phi)=\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa\phi/\omega}\left({\bf M}(\phi/\omega){\bf X}_{2}(0)+\tilde{{\bf u}}_{2}(\phi/\omega)\right)\;,

(62)

where the initial condition
{\bf X}_{2}(0)={\bf X}_{2}(2\pi)
has to satisfy

\displaystyle{\bf X}_{2}(0)=(\mathbb{1}-\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa T}{\bf M}(T))^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-2\kappa T}\tilde{{\bf u}}_{2}(T)\,

(63)

to ensure the
2\pi
-periodicity. With
{\bf X}_{0}
,
{\bf X}_{1}
and
{\bf X}_{2}
, we construct
{\bf J}_{0}
and
{\bf J}_{1}
, and thus compute
{\bf A}_{0}
and
{\bf A}_{1}
.

For the case of a non-parametrically driven oscillator with the driving term acting in
x
-direction, as presented in Sec.
IV
, the response functions follow directly as
Z_{0}=(A_{0})_{11}
,
Z_{1}=(A_{1})_{11}
and
I_{0}=(A_{0})_{21}
.
