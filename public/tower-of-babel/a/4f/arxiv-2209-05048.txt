Optimal Hamiltonian simulation for time-periodic systems
Kaoru Mizuta, Keisuke Fujii

Abstract
The implementation of time-evolution operators U ⁡ ( t ) U(t) , called Hamiltonian simulation, is one of the most promising usage of quantum computers. For time-independent Hamiltonians, qubitization has recently established efficient realization of time-evolution U ⁡ ( t ) = e − i ​ H ​ t U(t)=e^{-iHt} , with achieving the optimal computational resource both in time t t and an allowable error ε \varepsilon . In contrast, those for time-dependent systems require larger cost due to the difficulty of handling time-dependency. In this paper, we establish optimal/nearly-optimal Hamiltonian simulation for generic time-dependent systems with time-periodicity, known as Floquet systems. By using a so-called Floquet-Hilbert space equipped with auxiliary states labeling Fourier indices, we develop a way to certainly obtain the target time-evolved state without relying on either time-ordered product or Dyson-series expansion. Consequently, the query complexity, which measures the cost for implementing the time-evolution, has optimal and nearly-optimal dependency respectively in time t t and inverse error ε \varepsilon , and becomes sufficiently close to that of qubitization. Thus, our protocol tells us that, among generic time-dependent systems, time-periodic systems provides a class accessible as efficiently as time-independent systems despite the existence of time-dependency. As we also provide applications to simulation of nonequilibrium phenomena and adiabatic state preparation, our results will shed light on nonequilibrium phenomena in condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry, and quantum tasks yielding time-dependency in quantum computation.

In this paper, we develop an optimal and/or nearly-optimal quantum algorithm for time-periodic Hamiltonian simulation.
The key idea of our formalism is mapping time-dependent systems to effective time-independent systems in the infinite-dimensional Floquet-Hilbert space, obtained by the Fourier series expansion in time.

This leads to smaller query complexity and simpler oracles of our algorithm compared to the truncated Dyson-series algorithm.
In addition, we provide simulation of the Fermi-Hubbard model under light and adiabatic state preparation as potential applications.
Thus, our protocol will shed light on the promising usage of quantum computers for nonequilibrium quantum many-body phenomena in condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry, and the optimal control in quantum computation.

Preliminaries

2.1
Review on Floquet theory

We briefly review Floquet theory to analyze Schrödinger equation under a time-periodic Hamiltonian,

i\derivative{t}\ket{\psi(t)}=H(t)\ket{\psi(t)},\quad H(t+T)=H(t),

(1)

and its solution

\ket{\psi(t)}=U(t)\ket{\psi(0)},\quad U(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential{t^\prime}H(t^{\prime})\right).

(2)

Here, the time-evolved state of the system of interest is represented by
\ket{\psi(t)}
, and we assume that it is defined on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
The set of states
\{\ket{\psi_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H})}
denotes a certain choice of the basis of
\mathcal{H}
.
Floquet theorem says that the solution
\ket{\psi(t)}
is always written in the form of

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H})}c_{\alpha}e^{-i\varepsilon_{\alpha}t}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)},\quad\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t+T)}=\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)},

(3)

similar to Bloch theorem for spatially-periodic systems. Here,
\varepsilon_{\alpha}\in[-\pi/T,\pi/T)
and
\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)}\in\mathcal{H}
are respectively called quasienergy and Floquet state.
Time-periodicity of
H(t)
and
\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)}
allows the Fourier series expansion as

H(t)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}H_{m}e^{-im\omega t},\quad\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{m}}e^{-im\omega t},

(4)

with the frequency
\omega=2\pi/T
. The hermiticity of
H(t)
implies
H_{-m}=H_{m}^{\dagger}
. We assume
\norm{H(t)}<\infty
(
\norm{\cdot}
; operator norm), which results in
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\norm{H_m}^{2}<\infty
. Introducing an auxiliary degree of freedom
\{\ket{l}\}_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}
(sometimes called photon number) to relate
\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{l}}\leftrightarrow\ket{l}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{l}}
, every pair of
\varepsilon_{\alpha},\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)}
can be obtained by the time-independent eigenvalue equation,

\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{l}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{l}}\right)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{l}\ket{\phi_{\alpha}^{l}}\right),

(5)

with the effective Hamiltonian defined by

\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes(H_{0}-l\omega)+\sum_{l,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{l}\bra{l+m}\otimes H_{-m}.

(6)

Intuitively, this time-independent Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
describes a static one-dimensional system, where each
l
-th site has potential energy
H_{0}-l\omega
, as Fig.
1
.
Off-diagonal terms
H_{-m}
represent hopping by
-m
sites, which can be recognized as either emission or absorption of
|m|
photons.
We remark that the difficulty of time-dependence does not vanish by this mapping; it is translated into infinite dimensionality of the space spanned by
\{\ket{l}\ket{\psi_{j}}\,|\,l\in\mathbb{Z},\,j=1,2,\ldots,\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H})\}
, which is called Floquet-Hilbert space or Sambe space
[
44
]
.
While the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is often used for identifying
\varepsilon_{\alpha},\ket{\phi_{\alpha}(t)}
, it can be employed also for directly determining the dynamics as follows
[
45
]
;

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-il\omega t}\braket{l|e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|0}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(7)

We can extract the dynamics under a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
from that under the time-independent one
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
by preparing auxiliary systems labeled by
\ket{l}
.
However, we note that applying optimal algorithms for time-independent systems to simulating this extended dynamics is not straightforward, since the Floquet-Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional.

2.2
Review on qubitization technique

Here, we briefly review so-called qubitization technique to efficiently implement
e^{-iHt}
for time-independent Hamiltonian
H
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

[
18
]
.
It begins with assuming the existence of block-encoding, that is, we suppose an
n_{a}
-qubit auxiliary state
\ket{G}_{a}
(called an oracle state) and a unitary gate
O
on the space
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n_{a}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
(called an oracle gate) satisfying

\braket{G|O|G}_{a}=\frac{H}{\alpha},\quad\alpha\geq\norm{H}.

(8)

The inequality
\alpha\geq\norm{H}
comes from the norm of the unitary gate as
\norm{O}=1
, and the parameter
\alpha
represents a typical energy scale of the whole system.
Here, the oracles
O
and
\ket{G}_{a}
are supposed to be efficiently implemented; the unitary gates
O
and
G
, which realizes the oracle state from a reference state
\ket{0}_{a}
as
G\ket{0}_{a}=\ket{G}_{a}
, require at most
C\in\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
elementary gates.

The explicit construction of the oracles has been explored for certain classes of static Hamiltonians.
For instance, when the Hamiltonian
H
is given by a linear combination of unitary (LCU) as

H=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\alpha_{j}U_{j},\quad\alpha_{j}\geq 0,\quad\text{$U_{j}$; unitary},

(9)

a possible choice of the oracles is

O=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{j}\bra{j}_{a}\otimes U_{j},\quad\ket{G}_{a}=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha}}\ket{j}_{a},

(10)

with
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\alpha_{j}
.
The number of ancillary qubits
n_{a}
scales as
\log j_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Since
j_{\mathrm{max}}
is
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
for typical
N
-site systems, the number of elementary gates for the oracles amounts to
C\in\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
.
LCUs cover various types of Hamiltonians exemplified by quantum spin systems composed of local Pauli operators and fermionic systems in condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry
[
46
]
.
The block-encoding of other Hamiltonians, such as sparse-access matrices and purified density matrices, has been also revealed
[
18
,
47
,
48
]
.

Once we find out the oracles
O
and
\ket{G}_{a}
, we can organize a unitary gate
W_{q}
, implemented by
\order{1}
additional ancillary qubits and
\order{q}
-times usage of
O
,
G
, and
\order{n_a}
elementary gates, which satisfies

W_{q}\ket{0}^{\otimes\left\{n_{a}+\order{1}\right\}}\ket{\psi}=\ket{0}^{\otimes\left\{n_{a}+\order{1}\right\}}e^{-iHt}\ket{\psi}+\order{\varepsilon_q},

(11)

for every state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Here, the error
\varepsilon_{q}
comes from approximating
e^{-iHt}
by a certain degree-
q
polynomial of
H
, and decays as
\varepsilon_{q}\in\order{(\alpha t / q)^q}
.
The scaling of
q
required for making the error
\varepsilon_{q}
smaller than an acceptable error
\varepsilon
is given by the following formula;

q\in\Theta\left(\alpha t+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(e+(\alpha t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon))}\right).

(12)

In order to determine the explicit form of
W_{q}
, we resort to quantum signal processing
[
17
]
.
It dictates that only
\mathrm{poly}\left(q\right)
-time classical computation is required for this purpose.

To summarize the qubitization technique, we require the following computational resources to implement the time-evolution operator
e^{-iHt}
with an acceptable error
\varepsilon
(See also Table
1
);

•

Number of ancillary qubits;
n_{a}+\order{1}

•

Query complexity;
q
[Eq. (
12
)]

•

Number of elementary gates;

\order{\left\{ \alpha t + \frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(e + (\alpha t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon) )}\right\} (C+n_a)}.

(13)

Here, the query complexity is defined by the complexity counted by the number of oracles, corresponding to
q
in this case, and gives the scaling of the number of elementary gates below.
Significantly, the query complexity takes an additive form as

\alpha t+o(\log(1/\varepsilon))\in\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)t+o(\log(1/\varepsilon)).

(14)

The
o(\log(1/\varepsilon))
term scales as
\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
under
1/\varepsilon\to\infty
with fixed time
t
.
Its scaling is known to be optimal both in
t
and
1/\varepsilon
for simulating generic time-independent Hamiltonians
[
47
]
.

In our algorithm for time-periodic Hamiltonians, we exploit qubitization as a subroutine to implement time evolution under an effective Hamiltonian in Floquet theory.
We can achieve the query complexity for time-periodic systems in the additive form like Eq. (
14
), while the conventional truncated Dyson-series algorithm for generic time-dependent systems needs the query complexity in a multiplicative form
\alpha t\times o(\log(\alpha t / \varepsilon))
.

3.1
Overview of Algorithm

We first show an overview of the algorithm for time-evolution under a time-periodic Hamiltonian
H(t+T)=H(t)
.
Throughout the main text, we assume that the Fourier components of the Hamiltonian
H(t)
vanishes at a certain cutoff
m_{\mathrm{max}}\in\order{1}
;

H_{m}=0,\quad\text{if}\quad|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}.

(15)

(This discussion can be extended to cases where
H_{m}
exponentially decays in
|m|
. See Appendix
C
.).

The central attempt in the algorithm is to efficiently simulate Eq. (
7
),

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-il\omega t}\bra{l}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)},

(16)

on quantum circuits.
The time-dependency is erased by mapping the dynamics to the one on the Floquet-Hilbert space, which results in the following two benefits.
First, we do not discretize the time with infinitesimal intervals.
We instead rely on Fourier indices
l\in\mathbb{Z}
, which are originally discrete, and actually they lead to much better accuracy with the same auxiliary degrees of freedom.
Second, we can use various Hamiltonian simulation algorithms for time-independent systems.
Since the qubitization technique has achieved the best query complexity in the time
t
and the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
, exploiting it for
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
accelerate the simulation of time-periodic systems.

On the other hand, in order to simulate the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
via Eq. (
16
) on quantum circuits, we also have several problems to be tackled.
First, the Floquet-Hilbert space is infinite dimensional. We have to introduce truncation at a certain Fourier index
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, and consider a finite set of indices defined by

D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\{-l_{\mathrm{max}}+1,-l_{\mathrm{max}}+2,\ldots,l_{\mathrm{max}}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}.

(17)

Then, we focus on the approximate dynamics by

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes(H_{0}-l\omega)+\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{m\neq 0;l+m\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}\bra{l+m}\otimes H_{-m},

\displaystyle\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}e^{-il\omega t}\bra{l}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(19)

The finite-dimensional space, spanned by
2l_{\mathrm{max}}\times\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H})
states
\{\ket{l}\ket{\psi_{j}}\,|\,l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}},\,j=1,\ldots,\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{H})\}
, is called the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space.
While
l_{\mathrm{max}}\to\infty
reproduces
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\to\ket{\psi(t)}
, it is nontrivial how we should choose
l_{\mathrm{max}}
to achieve the accuracy
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)} - \ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon
.
The second problem is the small success probability of post-selecting the ancillary state.
Even if we succeed in approximation with finite
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, Eq. (
19
) requires a projection to a unnormalized ancillary state
\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}
for the state in the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space,

\ket{\Psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}=e^{-i\omega t\sum_{l}\ket{l}\bra{l}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(20)

In the actual computation, we need post-selection to an ancillary state given by

\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}.

(21)

Success probability of the projection is given by
\norm{\braket{a^{l_\imax}|\Psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}^{2}\simeq\braket{\psi(t)|\psi(t)}/(2l_{\mathrm{max}})=1/(2l_{\mathrm{max}})
.
As
l_{\mathrm{max}}
increases to ensure the accuracy, the expected time to successfully obtain
\ket{\psi(t)}
becomes longer in proportion to it.
The final problem is about implementation of
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
.
While the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
is time-independent, its structure is complicated due to the additional degrees of freedom labeled by
\{\ket{l}\}
.
It is nontrivial whether the optimal Hamiltonian simulation algorithm, i.e. the qubitization technique, provides the optimality for time-periodic systems.

Our algorithm relying on the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space efficiently simulates the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with resolving the above problems.
The significant developments are composed of the following steps;

(a)

Decision of the Fourier index for truncation,
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, to achieve an allowable error
\varepsilon
.

(b)

Amplification by symmetry and oblivious amplitude amplification to enhance the success probability up to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.

(c)

Efficient implementation of
\exp( -i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t)
by the qubitization technique.

In Step (a), we explicitly derive the upper bound of
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
in a similar way to the Lieb-Robinson bound.
We show that the choice of
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, based on

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\Theta\left(\gamma t+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log( e + (\gamma t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon) )}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t}(\norm{H(t)-H_0}),

(23)

is sufficient to make the error smaller than
\varepsilon
.
Here,
\gamma
gives the scale of the time-dependent terms in
H(t)
.
The second step (b) plays a role in amplifying the success probability of the post selection from
1/(2l_{\mathrm{max}})
to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
The first protocol, which we call amplification by symmetry, exploits the symmetry of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
which is always present and inherent in time-periodic systems.
It brings the success probability from
1/(2l_{\mathrm{max}})
to
1/4-\order{\varepsilon}
only with small cost of
\order{\log l_\imax}
elementary gates.
Following this, we apply the oblivious amplitude amplification
[
16
]
, reminiscent of Grover’s search algorithm
[
49
]
. The success probability is further amplified from
1/4-\order{\varepsilon}
to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
Exploiting these two kinds of amplification, we can obtain the target state
\ket{\psi(t)}
only with a little additional resource that does not change the scaling.
The final step (c) constructs conversion of the effective Hamiltonian so that
\exp( -i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t)
can be efficiently implemented.
The original effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
is not suitable for the qubitization technique, since the additional degrees of freedom
\ket{l}
makes its block-encoding inefficient.
To resolve this problem, we derive an alternative effective Hamiltonian such that the desired evolution
\exp( -i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t)
is accurately reproduced.
The block-encoding for the alternative effective Hamiltonian requires
\order{1}
-times queries to the oracles for each Fourier component
\{H_{m}\}
, which is important for achieving optimal or nearly-optimal dependence in
t
and
1/\varepsilon
.

3.2
Main results

Here, we summarize the computational resources for computing the dynamics
\ket{\psi(t)}
under a time-periodic Hamiltonian
H(t)
.
We construct two different quantum algorithms depending on the time scale of the dynamics.
The first case is an adiabatic-like case, where we are interested in the dynamics over
\order{1}
periods as
\omega t\in\order{1}
.
We call it “adiabatic” since adiabatic dynamics under sufficiently large period
T
is a typical target, while
T
is not required to be large.
The other case is generic long-time regime, in which we focus on dynamics over multiple periods as
\omega t\in\Omega(1)
.
The algorithms for both cases follow Steps (a)-(c) of Section
3.1
; the number of ancillary qubits is determined by the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
of Step (a), and the number of elementary gates comes mainly from the cost of implementing
\exp( -i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t)
in Step (c).

We assume that the Fourier component
H_{m}
becomes zero for
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
with an
\order{1}
constant
m_{\mathrm{max}}
. The block-encoding for each
H_{m}
is supposed to be given by an oracle unitary gate
O_{m}
and an
n_{a}
-qubit oracle state
\ket{G_{m}}
as

\braket{G_{m}|O_{m}|G_{m}}=\frac{H_{m}}{\alpha_{m}},\quad\alpha_{m}>0.

(24)

Each oracle state
\ket{G_{m}}
is generated by trivial states as
\ket{G_{m}}=G_{m}\ket{0}
.
We define the energy scale of the Hamiltonian
H(t)
by

\alpha=\sum_{|m|\leq m_{\mathrm{max}}}\alpha_{m},

(25)

and suppose that
\{\alpha_{m}\}_{m}
can be embedded into an
\order{1}
-qubit quantum system by

G_{\mathrm{coef}}\ket{0}=\sum_{|m|\leq m_{\mathrm{max}}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\alpha}}\ket{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{2m_{\mathrm{max}}+1}.

(26)

The query complexity is defined by the number of queries to the oracles
O_{m}
,
G_{m}
, and
G_{\mathrm{coef}}
, where each of them is supposed to require at-most
C
elementary gates.
We summarize the computational resources for simulating
\ket{\psi(t)}
in the adiabatic-like regime and the generic long-time regime respectively by the following theorems.

Theorem 1
.

(Resource for adiabatic-like regime)

Suppose that we are interested in the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
from an arbitrary initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
over
\order{1}
periods.
The computational resources to obtain it with the acceptable error and the failure probability smaller than
\order{\varepsilon}
are summarized as follows;

•

Number of ancillary qubits;

n_{a}+\order{\log(\gamma t) + \log\log(1/\varepsilon)}.

(27)

•

Scaling of query complexity;

\alpha t+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log( e + \{ \alpha t + o(\log(1/\varepsilon))\}^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon) )}.

(28)

The
o(\log(1/\varepsilon))
term in the above denominator scales as

\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log(e+ (\gamma t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon))}.

(29)

•

Additional gates per query;

\order{n_a + \log(\gamma t) + \log\log(1/\varepsilon)}.

(30)

The query complexity has optimal scaling in the time
t
and nearly-optimal scaling in the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
.

Theorem 2
.

(Resource for long-time regime)

Suppose that we are interested in the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
from arbitrary initial states
\ket{\psi(0)}
over multiple periods
\omega t\in\Omega(1)
.
The computational resources to obtain it with the acceptable error and the failure probability smaller than
\order{\varepsilon}
are summarized as follows;

•

Number of ancillary qubits;

n_{a}+\order{\log(\gamma/\omega) + \log\log(\omega t/\varepsilon)}.

(31)

•

Scaling of query complexity;

\alpha t+\frac{\omega t\log(\omega t/\varepsilon)}{\log(e+\{ \alpha/\omega+ o(\log(\omega t /\varepsilon))\}^{-1} \log(\omega t/\varepsilon))}.

(32)

The
o(\log(\omega t /\varepsilon))
term in the above denominator scales as

\frac{\log(\omega t /\varepsilon)}{\log(e + (\gamma/ \omega)^{-1} \log(\omega t /\varepsilon))}.

(33)

•

Additional gates per query;

\order{n_a + \log(\gamma/\omega) + \log\log(\omega t /\varepsilon)}

(34)

The scaling of the query complexity is optimal in time
t
for practical problems up to
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
-time, while it is formally nearly-optimal.
It is nearly-optimal in the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
.

The parameters
\alpha

), respectively represent energy scales of the overall terms and the time-dependent terms in

They typically scale as

N
.
In contrast, the frequency

\order{N^0}

\alpha

).
While the above theorems are about time-periodic Hamiltonians with vanishing Fourier components at

\norm{H_m}\lesssim e^{-\order{|m|}}
.
See Appendix

4
Building an appropriate truncated Floquet-Hilbert space

This section is devoted to deriving the proper truncation order for the Floquet-Hilbert space, following (a) in Section
3.1
.
In order to achieve desirable accuracy for the exact time-evolved state with the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space, we should obtain an exact upper bound on its error.
Here, we prove the Lieb-Robinson bound in the Floquet-Hilbert space in Section
4.1
, and determine a proper truncation order based on it in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Lieb-Robinson bound in Floquet-Hilbert space

The proper choice of the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
is determined so that the approximate state
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
[See Eq. (
19
)] can reproduce the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with satisfying the relation
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon
.
From the explicit formula Eq. (
19
), we can see that it is important to observe how the transition amplitude
\braket{l|e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}|0}
behaves for sufficiently large
l
.
We first show the upper bound on this transition amplitude in Theorem
3
.
Since it is reminiscent of the Lieb-Robinson bound in single-particle quantum systems
[
50
]
, we call it the Lieb-Robinson bound in the Floquet-Hilbert space.

Theorem 3
.

(Bound on transition amplitude)

We assume
H_{m}=0
for
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
. Then, for indices
l,l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
|l-l^{\prime}|\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
, the transition amplitude is bounded by

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\gamma t}{\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}\right)^{\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil},

(36)

where the parameter
\gamma
is defined by Eq. (
23
).

Proof.—

We will omit the superscripts
l_{\mathrm{max}}
for some operators introduced here since they are not important. We first employ the interaction picture. With the unitary operation defined by

\mathscr{U}_{0}(t)=e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{0}t},\quad\mathscr{H}_{0}=\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes(H_{0}-l\omega),

(37)

the time evolution operator
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
is represented as

\displaystyle e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{0}(t)\mathscr{U}_{I}(t),

(38)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{0}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\mathscr{H}_{I}(t^{\prime})\right).

(39)

Here, the Hamiltonian in the interaction picture is defined by

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{0}(t)^{\dagger}(\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}-\mathscr{H}_{0})\mathscr{U}_{0}(t)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l}\sum_{m>0}(e^{im\omega t}\ket{l}\bra{l+m}\otimes H_{-m}^{I}(t)+\text{h.c.}),

\displaystyle H_{-m}^{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{iH_{0}t}H_{-m}e^{-iH_{0}t},

(41)

where the summation in the second line is taken over
l,m
such that
l,l+m\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
Using the Dyson series expansion, we obtain

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\norm{\braket{l|\ms{U}_I(t)|l^\prime}}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\norm{\braket{l|\ms{H}_I(t_n) \hdots\ms{H}_I(t_1)|l^\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\sum_{\{l_i\}}\prod_{i=1}^n \braket{l_i|\ms{H}_I(t_i)|l_{i-1}}}.

In the last equality, we employ the identity
\sum_{l_{i}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l_{i}}\bra{l_{i}}=I
for
n-1
times.
The summation
\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}
is taken over
l_{i}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
under the fixed
l_{0}=l^{\prime}
and
l_{n}=l
.
Each product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\braket{l_{i}|\mathscr{H}_{I}(t_{i})|l_{i-1}}
represents a complex transition amplitude from
\ket{l^{\prime}}
to
\ket{l}
along the path
\ket{l^{\prime}}\to\ket{l_{1}}\to\ldots\to\ket{l_{n-1}}\to\ket{l}
under
\mathscr{H}_{I}(t_{i})
.
Since the Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{I}(t_{i})
shifts the Fourier index
\ket{l_{i}}
by at-most
m_{\mathrm{max}}
due to Eq. (
), the low order terms labeled by
n<|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}
disappears.
This results in

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\norm{\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^n \ms{H}_I(t_i) |0}}

(43)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\left(\sup_{t}(\norm{\ms{H}_I(t)})\right)^{n}

Here,
\mathscr{H}_{I}(t)
is a Toeplitz matrix that satisfies
\braket{l|\mathscr{H}_{I}(t)|l^{\prime}}=e^{-i(l-l^{\prime})\omega t}H_{l-l^{\prime}}^{I}(t)-H_{0}\delta_{l-l^{\prime},0}
.
This leads to the upper bound of its operator as follows
[
51
]
,

\displaystyle\norm{\ms{H}_I(t)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{t^{\prime}}\left(\norm{\sum_{m \in\bbZ} (e^{im\omega t} H_{-m}^I(t) - H_0 \delta_{m,0}) e^{im\omega t^\prime}}\right)

(44)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{t^{\prime}}\left(\norm{H(t-t^\prime) - H_0}\right)=\gamma.

We also use the following inequality;

\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}\leq 2\frac{x^{n_{0}}}{n_{0}!},\quad\text{if}\quad n_{0}\geq 2x\geq 0.

(45)

This relation can be easily confirmed by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{n_{0}}}{n_{0}!}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{x^{n-n_{0}}}{n(n-1)\ldots(n_{0}+1)}\leq\frac{x^{n_{0}}}{n_{0}!}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}.

(46)

For the indices
l,l^{\prime}
satisfying
|l-l^{\prime}|\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
, we can substitute
x=\gamma t
and
n_{0}=\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil
. This results in the bound,

\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}\leq 2\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil|l-l^{\prime}|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}.

(47)

The inequality from the Stirling formula,

n_{0}!\geq 2\left(\frac{n_{0}}{e}\right)^{n_{0}},

(48)

ensures the inequality Eq. (
36
).
\quad\square

The Lieb-Robinson bound in the transition amplitude provides a guide for choosing a proper truncation order.
By setting
l^{\prime}=0
, Eqs. (
35
) and (
36
) say that the contributions to
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
from indices satisfying
|l|\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
rapidly decay as
l^{-l}
.
Thus,
\order{\gamma t}
becomes a possible truncation order.
However, we note that the change in
l_{\mathrm{max}}
affects
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
also via the change in the support of the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
By taking it into account with using the Lieb-Robinson bound, we obtain the exact upper bound on the error between
\ket{\psi(t)}
and
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
as follows.

Theorem 4
.

(Floquet-Hilbert space truncation)

We consider the approximate time-evolved state obtained from the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space,
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
, given by Eq. (
19
).
Then, its deviation from the exact one
\ket{\psi}
is bounded by

\norm{\ket{\psi(t)} - \ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq 20m_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!},

(49)

if the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
satisfies
l_{\mathrm{max}}\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
.

Proof.—

We evaluate the convergence of
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
.
For different orders
l_{\mathrm{max}},l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}
satisfying
l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}>l_{\mathrm{max}}
, we compute the difference,

\norm{\ket{\psi^{l_\imax^\prime}(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2},

(50)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in(D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})}\norm{\bra{l} e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax^\prime}t} \ket{0}}e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}t}\ket{0},

(51)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\norm{\bra{l} e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax^\prime}t}-e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax}t} \ket{0}}e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}t}-e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}.

(52)

The first error comes from truncating the order of the post-selected state
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
Using Theorem
3
directly implies

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}\leq\sum_{l\in(D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil|l|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil|l|/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}\leq 2\sum_{l=l_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}.

(53)

The summation
\sum_{l=l_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}
can be divided based on
l

(\text{mod. }m_{\mathrm{max}})
, and each summation corresponds to the left hand side of Eq. (
45
) with
n_{0}\geq\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil
.
In other words, for
l_{\mathrm{max}}\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
, we obtain the following inequality,

\sum_{l=l_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}\leq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}.

(54)

As a result, the first error
\varepsilon_{1}
is bounded by
4m_{\mathrm{max}}(\gamma t)^{\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}/(\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!
.

The second error
\varepsilon_{2}
comes from truncating the order of the effective Hamiltonian.
In a similar way to the proof of Theorem
3
, each term is bounded by

\norm{\bra{l} e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax^\prime}t}-e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax}t} \ket{0}}e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}t}-e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\norm{\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^n \ms{H}_I^{l_\imax^\prime}(t_i) - \prod_{i=1}^n \ms{H}_I^{l_\imax}(t_i)|0}},

(55)

where we explicitly write the size
l_{\mathrm{max}},l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}
for
H_{I}(t)
[See Eq. (
40
)].
In a similar manner to Eq. (
42
),
\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{H}_{I}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}(t_{i})|0}
and
\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{H}_{I}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t_{i})|0}
respectively represent transition amplitudes summed over all possible paths
\ket{0}\to\ket{l_{1}}\to\ldots\to\ket{l_{n-1}}\to\ket{l}
under
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}(t)
and
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
.
Their differences survive only when the path goes across the domain
D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
, where these two Hamiltonians have different actions.
In other words, low order terms with
n<\{(l_{\mathrm{max}}-0)+(l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)/m_{\mathrm{max}}
should vanish.
Considering that the norm of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
and
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}(t)
is both bounded by
\gamma
, we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{n=\lceil(2l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}2\gamma^{n}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil(2l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil(2l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 16m_{\mathrm{max}}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}.

In the last inequality, we again use the relation Eq. (
54
).
Taking the limit
l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}\to\infty
for
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
reproduces the result of Theorem
4
.
\quad\square

4.2
Truncation order of Floquet-Hilbert space

We hereby determine the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
so that
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
can reproduce the exact time-evolved state with a desirable error up to
\order{\varepsilon}
.
By using the inequality from the Stirling formula, Eq. (
48
) to Theorem
4
, we obtain the error bounded by

\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq 10m_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\frac{em_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t}{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)^{l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}},

(57)

for
l_{\mathrm{max}}\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
.
The truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
is chosen so that the right hand side can be smaller than
\varepsilon
.
To this aim, we should evaluate a function
f(x)=(\kappa/x)^{x}
[
\kappa>0
], which is known to be dealt with the Lambert W function
W(x)
satisfying
W(x)e^{W(x)}=x

[
52
]
.
Here, we rely on the resulting proposition
[
47
]
.

Proposition 5
.

The function
f(x)=(\kappa/x)^{x}
[
\kappa>0
] is monotonically decreasing in
x\geq\kappa/e
, and satisfies the following inequality for
0<\eta<1
;

f(x)\leq\eta,\quad^{\forall}x\geq e\kappa+\frac{4\log(1/\eta)}{\log(e + \kappa^{-1} \log(1/\eta))}.

(58)

See Lemma 59 in the full version of Ref.
[
47
]
for the proof.
Based on the above proposition, we choose
l_{\mathrm{max}}
by

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{max}}=m_{\mathrm{max}}+\left\lceil e^{2}m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t+\frac{4m_{\mathrm{max}}\log(10m_\imax/ \varepsilon)}{\log(e + (e\gamma t)^{-1} \log(10m_\imax/ \varepsilon))}\right\rceil,

(59)

so that the error can be bounded from above as

10m_{\mathrm{max}}\left(\frac{em_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t}{l_{\mathrm{max}}-m_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)^{(l_{\mathrm{max}}-m_{\mathrm{max}})/m_{\mathrm{max}}}\leq\varepsilon.

(60)

We note that this choice does not violate the condition
l_{\mathrm{max}}\geq 2m_{\mathrm{max}}\gamma t
, which is required for Theorem
4
.
The monotonicity of
f(x)
ensures
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon
under the above choice (The additional term
m_{\mathrm{max}}
is attached for later calculation, especially for Appendix
B.1
) and
B.2
).

Let us discuss how
l_{\mathrm{max}}
increases in the time
t
and the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
.
The form of the error in
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, given by Eq. (
49
), is the same as that for qubitization in the query complexity
q
, given by
\varepsilon_{q}\in\order{(\alpha t /q)^q}
.
Therefore, its scaling can be determined in a similar way, which results in

l_{\mathrm{max}}\in\Theta\left(\gamma t+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log( e+ (\gamma t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon) )}\right).

(61)

To reproduce the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space, we should prepare an ancillary system labeled by
\{\ket{l}\}_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
The number of qubits for such ancillary system is at-most

\order{\log l_\imax}\subset\order{\log(\gamma t) + \log\log(1/\varepsilon)}.

(62)

5
Amplitude amplification of the dynamics

In this section, we show two kinds of amplification to achieve sufficiently high success probability of extracting the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
.

As we stated in Section
3.1
, the approximate state
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
cannot be directly realized on quantum circuits.
After simulating the dynamics in the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space to get
\ket{\Psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
defined by Eq. (
20
), we make a projection to
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
Although the resulting renormalized state is sufficiently close to
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
and also the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
as

\frac{\braket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}|\Psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}}{\norm{\braket{a^{l_\imax}|\Psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}}=\frac{\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}}{\norm{\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}}=\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon}

(63)

for
l_{\mathrm{max}}
given by Eq. (
59
), we should be careful of the low success probability; it becomes
\norm{\braket{a^{l_\imax}|\Psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}^{2}\in\order{(l_\imax)^{-1}}
.

We need approximately
\order{\gamma t}
-times trials of the post selection, and every trial is expected to require at-least
\order{t}
complexity for implementing
\ket{\Psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}=e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi(0)}
.
Therefore, the naive implementation based on Eq. (
19
) is not efficient for the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
in that the expected computational time reaches
\order{t^2}
.

We resolve this problem by the amplification of the success probability up to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
in the next section.
The first one, which exploits the symmetry of the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, amplifies from
\order{l_\imax^{-1}}
to
\order{1}
.
The latter one following this, which is reminiscent of the Grover’s search algorithm, allows the success probability
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
As discussed later, using only either one fails to efficiently compute
\ket{\psi(t)}
.

5.1
Amplification by symmetry

We introduce the amplification exploiting the symmetry of the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
First of all, we specify the symmetry here; it is about the translation of the photon number from
\ket{l}
to
\ket{l+m}
.
When we define the translation operator on the Floquet-Hilbert space by
\mathscr{T}_{m}=\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}}\ket{l}\bra{l+m}\otimes I
,
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
satisfies the translation symmetry,

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{m}^{\dagger}\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\mathscr{T}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}+m\omega,

(64)

\displaystyle\braket{l|e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{il^{\prime}\omega t}\braket{l-l^{\prime}|e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}|0}.

(65)

Since the symmetry is present as long as the time-periodicity
H(t+T)=H(t)
holds, the amplification discussed here is always available in our algorithm.

Here, we provide two tasks for the amplification by symmetry.
First, we slightly extend the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space to
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, where the Fourier index
\ket{l}
is chosen from
l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\{-4l_{\mathrm{max}}+1,-4l_{\mathrm{max}}+2,\ldots,4l_{\mathrm{max}}\}
.
The number of additional qubits required for this extension from
\mathbb{C}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
is three, and hence this does not affect the scaling of the computational resources.
The second task is to preprocess the initial state by

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)},

(66)

where the unitary operator
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
is expressed by

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\left(\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\bra{0}+\ldots\right)\otimes I.

(67)

The unitary operator
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
nontrivially acts only on the ancillary space, and it can be implemented with
\order{\log l_\imax}
gates
(e.g. for
l_{\mathrm{max}}
such that
\log_{2}l_{\mathrm{max}}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\mathrm{Had}^{\otimes\log_{2}(2l_{\mathrm{max}})}\otimes I
with the Hadamard gate
\mathrm{Had}
).
This process plays a role of making the initial state approximately translation-invariant with the width of Fourier indices,
2l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
The remaining procedures after the above two tasks is the same as those for the original protocol in Section
3.1
.
Reflecting that the ancillary Hilbert space is extended to
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}
, we evolve the above uniform state
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}
by
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
and
\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes I
, and make a projection to
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
The resulting state
\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
is calculated as follows.

\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\equiv\bra{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}.

(68)

The point of this process is that the above state
\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
can reproduce
\ket{\psi(t)}
with
\order{1}
amplitude owing to the translation symmetry of the effective Hamiltonian, Eqs. (
64
) and (
65
).
We give its brief explanation in this section, while the rigorous derivation is provided in Appendix
B.1
.
Although we do not have the exact translation symmetry represented by Eqs. (
65
) due to the finite-size effect of
l_{\mathrm{max}}
, it is expected to approximately hold as

\bra{l}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}\simeq e^{il^{\prime}\omega t}\bra{l-l^{\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}.

(69)

if the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
is sufficiently large.
As a matter of fact, we can derive the exact upper bound on the difference between the left- and right-hand sides based on the Lieb-Robinson bound (See Lemma
8
in Appendix
B.1
).
We proceed the discussion with assuming the approximate relation Eq. (
69
) here.
The resulting state of the process
\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
can be roughly computed as follows;

\displaystyle\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}e^{-il\omega t}\bra{l}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}\ket{\psi(0)}

(70)

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}e^{-i(l-l^{\prime})\omega t}\bra{l-l^{\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\sum_{l^{\prime}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}(\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon}.

The second equality comes from Theorem
4
, considering that the summation of
l-l^{\prime}
over
l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
is sufficient to suppress the error up to
\order{\varepsilon}
.
As a result, we obtain

\ket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\simeq\frac{1}{2}(\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon}).

(71)

Let us focus on the success probability of the projection onto
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
It is provided by
\braket{\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)|\bar{\psi}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}=1/4+\order{\varepsilon}
, which is much larger than the original one
1/(2l_{\mathrm{max}})
.
Therefore, the amplification protocol by symmetry enables us to avoid
\order{t}
-times repetition of the time evolution
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}t}
in contrast to the original protocol.
Intuitively, this drastic improvement can be understood as a result of the interference under the translation symmetry, as Fig.
2
.
As we can see from Eq. (
20
), the target state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is extracted from
\ket{\Psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
via the accompanying ancillary state
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
, which is uniform in the Fourier index
\ket{l}
.
When we begin with the non-uniform initial state
\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}
, the resulting state after the time evolution is also non-uniform.
Since it involves
(1/\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}})\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}e^{ikl}\ket{l}
for
k\in(2\pi/2l_{\mathrm{max}})\mathbb{Z}
(different eigenstates of the translation operator) with approximate equal weight, the amplitude of the desirable component
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
is comparably small as
1/\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
In contrast, when we employ the uniform initial state
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}
, only the uniform components that includes
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
are amplified while the other components cancels one another.
It can be viewed also as the interference of different initial states
\ket{l^{\prime}}\ket{\psi(0)}
in
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}
as shown in Fig.
2
, and this is why the amplification protocol achieves
\order{1}
amplitude of
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}
.

We summarize the amplification by symmetry with adding the exact results obtained in Appendix
B.1
.
We prepare the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, and make the initial state uniform as
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{\psi(0)}
.
As a result, this process is described by a unitary gate

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)=(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}},

(72)

where the last unitary gate
(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}
is added to replace the projection to
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
by the one to
\ket{0}
.
This approximately realizes the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with
1/4+\order{\varepsilon}
success probability as

\bra{0}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}\simeq\frac{1}{2}(\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon}).

(73)

The probability
1/4
comes from the ratio of the width of the initial state
\ket{a^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
to that of the projected ancillary state
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}
.
The exact version, derived based on the Lieb-Robinson bound in Appendix
B.1
, is stated as follows, and ensures the validity of the discussion here.

Theorem 6
.

(Amplification by symmetry)

We designate the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}\in\Theta(\gamma t+\log(1/\varepsilon)/\log\log(1/\varepsilon))
by Eq. (
59
). Then,

\norm{\bra{0} \ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{l_\imax} (t) \ket{0} \ket{\psi(0)} - \frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

(74)

is satisfied for an arbitrary initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}\in\mathcal{H}
.

We finally note that the amplification solely by this process cannot achieve the success probability
1-\order{\varepsilon}
with keeping the efficiency.
Analogous to the above formulation, when we set the widths of the supports of the initial ancillary state, the effective Hamiltonian, and the projected ancillary state to
pl_{\mathrm{max}}
,
ql_{\mathrm{max}}
,
ql_{\mathrm{max}}
(
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\geq p+2
) respectively, the success probability becomes
p/q
.
However, the probability larger than
1-\varepsilon
demands the relation,

1-\varepsilon<\frac{p}{q}\leq\frac{p}{p+2}.

(75)

This implies
p\geq 2(1-\varepsilon)/\varepsilon
, and hence the number of ancillary qubits reaches at-least
\log(p l_\imax)\in\order{\log(\gamma t/\varepsilon)}
.
In addition, the query complexity includes a term proportional to the dimension of the ancillary system,
ql_{\mathrm{max}}
as discussed later in Section
6
.
This implies that the query complexity increases linearly in
1/\varepsilon
, destroying the original logarithmic scaling.
Thus, relying solely on this amplification protocol is not suitable for efficient simulation of the dynamics.
We resolve this problem by the oblivious amplitude amplification below.

). Interference takes place within the region

, and thereby enhances the amplitude of the uniform target state

5.2
Oblivious amplitude amplification

The above amplification based on the translation symmetry of
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}
enhances the amplitude of
\ket{\psi(t)}
from
1/\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}}
to
1/2-\order{\varepsilon}
.
Here, we introduce another amplification, called the oblivious amplitude amplification
[
16
]
, to achieve the amplitude (or the success probability)
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.

The starting point of this amplification is the result of the previous section
5.1
.
Equation (
73
), or equivalently Theorem
6
, indicates that the consequence of the amplification by symmetry is written as

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}=\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}(\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon})+\ket{\Psi^{\perp}},

(76)

with an additional term
\ket{\Psi^{\perp}}\in\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
satisfying

(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes I)\ket{\Psi^{\perp}}=0.

(77)

The state
\ket{\Psi^{\perp}}
generally depends on
\ket{\psi(0)}
and
t
.
The oblivious amplitude amplification takes a similar strategy to that of the Grover’s search algorithm, in which we compose the following two unitary operators;

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(2\ket{0}\bra{0}-I)\otimes I,

(78)

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\mathscr{R}[\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)]^{\dagger}\mathscr{R}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t).

(79)

The first one
\mathscr{R}
reverses the sign of
\ket{l}
for
l\neq 0
, and it is implemented with
\order{\log l_\imax}
gates.
The second one
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
plays a role in enhancing the amplitude of
\ket{\psi(t)}
up to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
Its action on any initial state
\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}
is computed as follows;

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathscr{R}(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}+\ket{\Psi^{\perp}}\right)+\order{\varepsilon}

(80)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathscr{R}\left\{\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}-(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}\right\}+\order{\varepsilon}.

In the second equality, we use the relation obtained by applying
(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}
to Eq. (
76
).
Next, we evaluate

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0,\psi(t)}=2\ket{0}\left(\bra{0}(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0}\right)\ket{\psi(t)}-(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0,\psi(t)}.

(81)

Theorem
6
indicates that the time evolution operator
U(t)
is approximated as

\norm{\braket{0|\ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{l_\imax}(t)|0} - \frac{1}{2} U(t)}-\frac{1}{2}U(t)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3},

(82)

and hence the relation

\norm{\braket{0|[\ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{l_\imax}(t)]^\dagger|0} - \frac{1}{2} U(t)^\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}U(t)^{\dagger}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}

(83)

is also satisfied.
This provides the relation,

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}=\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}-(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon},

(84)

and substituting this into Eq. (
80
) results in

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}+\order{\varepsilon},

(85)

for an arbitrary initial state
\ket{\psi(0)}
.

This result indicates that the operation
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
generates the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with the amplitude
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
Or equivalently, as an exact bound for the error, we can derive the inequality,

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\norm{\ms{U}_\mr{amp2}^{l_\imax}(t) \ket{0} \ket{\psi(0)} - \ket{0} \ket{\psi(t)}}\ket{\psi(0)}-\ket{0}\ket{\psi(t)}\leq\varepsilon.

(86)

The coefficient comes from the fact that an error bounded by
\varepsilon/3
appears due to Theorem
6
every time we call Eq. (
76
).
The amplification protocol
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
employs
3
times queries to
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
.
Reflecting that the operations
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
and
\mathscr{R}
require relatively a little resource (at-most
\order{\log l_\imax}
elementary gates and complexity), the resource for
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
has the same scaling as the one for implementing the time evolution operators
e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}
and
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
.

As well as the amplification by symmetry, relying only on the oblivious amplitude amplification fails to efficiently enhances the success probability to obtain
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi(t)}
from
\order{l_\imax^{-1}}
to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.
When we do not use the first amplification, the protocol of the oblivious amplitude amplification is given by

\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{amp2},p}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=-\left\{\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{orig}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathscr{R}(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{orig}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}\mathscr{R}\right\}^{p}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{orig}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}},

(87)

where the operation
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{orig}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
represents the time evolution without the amplification by symmetry, defined by

\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{orig}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)=(\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{ini}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})^{\dagger}e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}.

(88)

Applying
\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{amp2},p}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
to the initial state
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi(t)}
returns
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi(t)}
whose amplitude increases from
1/\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}}
approximately in proportion to
p
.
The integer
p
should be
\order{\sqrt{l_\imax}}
to achieve the amplitude
1-\order{\varepsilon}
, reminiscent of the Grover’s search algorithm.
In other words,
\order{\sqrt{l_\imax}}
times call of
e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}
and
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
is required when we use
\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{amp2},p}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
This is why we suggest the combination of the two amplification protocols, with which
\order{1}
times call of
e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}
and
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
can amplify the amplitude of
\ket{0}\otimes\ket{\psi(t)}
from
1/\sqrt{2l_{\mathrm{max}}}
to
1-\order{\varepsilon}
.

6
Block-encoding of Effective Floquet Hamiltonian

In the previous section, we show that the combination of the two kinds of amplification protocols, implemented by
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
[See Eq. (
86
)], provides the target time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with an arbitrarily small error
\order{\varepsilon}
.
The computational resource for
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp2}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
is mostly determined by that for
e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}
and
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
.
Our strategy is to implement these two time evolution operators by the qubitization technique
[
18
]
.
As discussed in Section
5.2
, the computational resources are determined by how to introduce the block-encoding of the two static Hamiltonians,
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes I
(linear potential Hamiltonian) and
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
(effective Hamiltonian).
The aim of this section is to obtain an efficient block-encoding of them and to evaluate the costs required to implement
e^{-it\sum_{l}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}}
and
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
.

6.1
Block-encoding of linear potential Hamiltonian

We compose block-encoding of the linear potential Hamiltonian,

\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}l\omega\ket{l}\bra{l}\otimes I.

(89)

By simple calculation, it can be written in the form of an LCU,

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\frac{\omega}{2}V_{l}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes I,

(90)

\displaystyle V_{l}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l^{\prime}=l}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{l^{\prime}}\bra{l^{\prime}}-\sum_{l^{\prime}=-4l_{\mathrm{max}}+1}^{l-1}\ket{l^{\prime}}\bra{l^{\prime}}.

(91)

Using the block-encoding formalism of LCUs by Eq. (
10
), we immediately obtain the oracle unitary gate and the oracle state by

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}(\ket{l}\bra{l})_{b}\otimes V_{l}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes I,

(92)

\displaystyle\braket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}|\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}|a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}_{b}=\frac{\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}{4l_{\mathrm{max}}\omega}.

(93)

The subscript
b
for the states
\ket{l}_{b}
and
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}_{b}
represents a new ancillary system introduced for the block-encoding, requiring the number of qubits
n_{b}\in\order{\log l_\imax}
.
Combining Eqs. (
91
) and (
92
), the oracle unitary gate is rewritten by

\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\left(\sum_{l,l^{\prime};l^{\prime}-l\geq 0}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\bra{l,l^{\prime}}-\sum_{l,l^{\prime};l^{\prime}-l<0}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\bra{l,l^{\prime}}\right)\otimes I.

(94)

To implement this oracle, we use a comparator defined by

\mathrm{Comp}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\ket{0}_{b^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\ket{0}_{b^{\prime}}&\text{if $l^{\prime}\geq l$},\\
\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\ket{1}_{b^{\prime}}&\text{if $l^{\prime}<l$},\end{cases}

(95)

with a single-qubit ancillary system
b^{\prime}

[
53
]
.
We can immediately confirm the relation,

\mathrm{Comp}^{\dagger}Z_{b^{\prime}}\mathrm{Comp}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\ket{0}_{b^{\prime}}\ket{\psi}=\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{l,l^{\prime}}\ket{0}_{b^{\prime}}\ket{\psi},

(96)

for arbitrary inputs
l,l^{\prime}\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
and
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
, where
Z_{b^{\prime}}
denotes a Pauli
Z
operator on the system
b^{\prime}
.
Since the comparator on
n
qubits can be composed of
\order{n}
elementary gates, the oracle unitary gate
\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
requires at-most
\order{\log l_\imax}
gates.
The oracle state
\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}_{b}
, which has equal weights in
\ket{l}
, can be prepared by
\order{\log l_\imax}
elementary gates.

We implement the time-evolution
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
with accuracy
1-\order{\varepsilon}
by qubitization.
The number of additional gates, other than the block-encoding, is proportional to the number of ancillary qubits for the oracle
\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
, that is, we need
\order{\log l_\imax}
additional gates per query.
Based on Section
2.2
, it can be executed with the following resources;

•

Number of ancillary qubits;
\order{\log l_\imax}

•

Scaling of overall complexity;

\left\{l_{\mathrm{max}}\omega t+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log( e + (l_\imax\omega t)^{-1} \log(1/\varepsilon) )}\right\}\log l_{\mathrm{max}}.

(97)

6.2
Block-encoding of effective Hamiltonian

We hereby provide a way to efficiently implement
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
by the qubitization technique.
We find out that naive block-encoding of the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
, given by Eq. (
), faces at the severe increase of the complexity in
l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
This originates from the fact that the number of nontrivial terms in
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
increases linearly in
l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
To avoid this problem, we take an alternative approach which yields only
\order{\log l_\imax}
complexity as follows;

1.

Find a refined effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}
on the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, such that
e^{-i\mathscr{H}t}
accurately reproduces
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
with an arbitrarily small error
\order{\varepsilon}
.

2.

Construct block-encoding of
\mathscr{H}
with composing of efficient implementation of its oracle unitary gate and oracle state.

First, we specify the assumption for the time-periodic Hamiltonian
H(t)
.
Here, we suppose two kinds of the oracles that can be accessed.
The first one is about the block-encoding of each Fourier component
H_{m}
(

6.2.1
Construction of a refined effective Hamiltonian

Let us compose a refined effective Hamiltonian, which accurately reproduces the dynamics of

To come to the point, such a Hamiltonian equipped with suitability for efficient block-encoding is given by

6.2.2

The benefit of employing the refined effective Hamiltonian

instead of the original one
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

We construct an oracle unitary gate

and an oracle state

for it.
With the help of the additional terms in Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff},\mathrm{pbc}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{|m|\leq m_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathrm{Add}_{m}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes H_{m}-\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}},

(107)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Add}_{m}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l\oplus m}\bra{l}.

(108)

The unitary gate

is a full quantum adder that translates an index

by

modulo
8l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
It can be implemented by
\order{\log l_\imax}
complexity and elementary gates.

Then, to construct the block-encoding, we prepare four kinds of auxiliary systems labeled by

The system

-qubit system prepared for the block-encoding of

[See Eq. (

].
The other systems

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\ket{0}\bra{0}_{d}\otimes\sum_{|m|\leq m_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{m}\bra{m}_{c}\otimes\mathrm{Add}_{m}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes O_{m}-\ket{1}\bra{1}_{d}\otimes\mathscr{O}_{\mathrm{LP}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}},

\ket{G_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}=G_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{w}_{d}(G_{\mathrm{coef}}\ket{0}_{c})\ket{a^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}_{b}\ket{0}_{a}^{\otimes n_{a}},\\

(112)

with using the quantum circuit

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{d}\otimes\sum_{|m|\leq m_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{m}\bra{m}_{c}\otimes I_{b}\otimes(G_{m})_{a},

Illustrative examples

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{0}}\ket{0}\ket{\bar{G}_{0}}+\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{1}}\ket{1}\ket{\bar{G}_{1}}}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{0}+\bar{\alpha}_{1}}}.

Here,
(\bar{O}_{0},\ket{\bar{G}_{0}},\bar{\alpha}_{0})
and
(\bar{O}_{1},\ket{\bar{G}_{1}},\bar{\alpha}_{1})
respectively provides the block-encoding of the time-independent Hamiltonians
\bar{H}_{0}
and
\bar{H}_{1}
as Eq. (
8
).
The oracles
O_{\pm 1}
and
\ket{G_{\pm 1}}
gives the block-encoding of
H_{\pm 1}
as
\braket{G_{\pm 1}|O_{\pm 1}|G_{\pm 1}}=2H_{\pm 1}/(\bar{\alpha}_{0}+\bar{\alpha}_{1})
.
According to the standard adiabatic theorem
[
42
]
, the duration required for approximating the target state
\ket{\bar{\psi}_{1}}
by the adiabatically-evolved state
U(T/4)\ket{\bar{\psi}_{0}}
with accuracy
1-\order{\varepsilon}
roughly amounts to
T\gtrsim\norm{\bar{H}_0 - \bar{H}_1}/\varepsilon\Delta^{2}
, where
\Delta
denotes the minimal gap upon the instantaneous eigenstate of
H(t)
continuously connecting
\ket{\bar{\psi}_{0}}
and
\ket{\bar{\psi}_{1}}
.
Therefore, in the adiabatic state preparation based on our algorithm, the query complexity counted by the oracles of the two static Hamiltonians
\bar{H}_{0}
and
\bar{H}_{1}
amounts to

\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{0}+\bar{\alpha}_{1}}{\varepsilon\Delta^{2}}\norm{\bar{H}_0 - \bar{H}_1}+o(\log(1/\varepsilon)).

(156)

Although the second term is buried by the first one polynomially increasing in
1/\varepsilon
, the cost has better scaling in
1/\varepsilon
compared to the cases where similar schedules for
H(t)
are tackled with the Trotterization [
\sim(1/\varepsilon)^{1+2/p}
] or the truncated Dyson-series algorithm [
\sim(1/\varepsilon)\log(1/\varepsilon)
].

In adiabatic quantum computation, the required time in
1/\varepsilon
can be improved via more sophisticated scheduling, such as local adiabatic interpolation
[
72
]
, boundary cancellation
[
73
,
74
]
and quasi-adiabatic processes
[
75
]
.
In particular, the last one has achieved the query complexity poly-logarithmic in
1/\varepsilon
with the help of the truncated Dyson-series algorithm.
If we can find time-periodic Hamiltonians which are consistent with such sophisticated schedules, it will offer a more efficient protocol for the adiabatic state preparation.

9
Discussion and Conclusion

We conclude our paper with summarizing the results.
In this paper, we focus on time-dependent systems with time-periodicity, and organize an efficient implementation of their time-evolution operators.
Once we prepare the oracles which embeds each Fourier component and each coefficient of the Hamiltonian, a series of unitary operations on the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space extract the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with an allowable error
1/\varepsilon
, whose query complexity are both optimal or nearly-optimal in
t
and
1/\varepsilon
.
In addition, it has an additive scaling
\alpha t+o(\log(1/\varepsilon))
[adiabatic-like regime] or
\alpha t+\omega t\times o(\log(1/\varepsilon))
[generic long-time regime], and hence achieves the cost sufficiently close to that of the best algorithm for time-independent systems
[
18
]
, despite the existence of time-dependency.
As exemplified by nonequilibrium quantum many-body phenomena and adiabatic state preparation, our algorithm will contribute to pioneering applications of quantum computers for various aims, in condensed matter physics, quantum chemistry, and quantum computation.

We finally discuss some potential future directions of our results.
The first one is to explore efficient implementation of meaningful functions in time-periodic systems, other than the time-evolution operator
U(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp(-i \int_0^t H(t^\prime) \dd t^
\prime)
.
In our algorithm, we exploit the qubitization technique to implement the exponential function,
e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}
, in the effective Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
As the qubitization technique allows to efficiently apply various polynomial functions, we expect that our algorithm can be extended for various aims other than the unitary time evolution discussed here.
For instance, our discussion on the Lieb-Robinson bound in Section
4
is valid also when a time-periodic Hamiltonian
H(t)
is non-hermitian.
This suggests that our algorithm may be available also for solving time-periodic linear differential equations
[
76
]
, exemplified by dissipative quantum may-body systems
[
77
,
78
]
.
While we do not expect exponential speedup due to the non-unitarity in general, it will offer an efficient way both in time and desirable accuracy.
It would be important to clarify what kind of function is useful in time-periodic systems, how the Lieb-Robinson bound and the amplitude amplification should be modified, and then how the computational cost is affected.

Another significant direction is to seek for useful tasks that can be efficiently tackled with time-periodic Hamiltonians.
As we have shown throughout the paper, time-periodic Hamiltonians can be simulated more efficiently generic time-dependent Hamiltonians with computational resources close to those for time-independent Hamiltonians.
This means that quantum tasks which inevitably requires time-dependent operations, such as adiabatic state preparation, can be optimized by tuning their schedules in a time-periodic way.
It will be important to clarify what kind of tasks can be addressed by time-periodic Hamiltonians and how our algorithm provides better scaling in
1/\varepsilon
for their costs, which we leave for future work.

\displaystyle\norm{\braket{l|e^{-i \ms{H}_{\eff,\mr{pbc}}^{4l_\imax} t} - e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4l_\imax} t}|l^\prime}}\leq\sum_{n=n(l^{\prime},l)}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\left\{\left(\sup_{t}(\norm{\ms{H}_{\mr{pbc},I}(t)})\right)^{n}+\gamma^{n}\right\},

(186)

where the Hamiltonian in the interaction picture is defined by

\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{pbc},I}(t)=[\mathscr{U}_{0}(t)]^{\dagger}\left(\sum_{m\neq 0}\mathrm{Add}_{m}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes H_{m}\right)\mathscr{U}_{0}(t).

(187)

We again use the property of circulant matrices, and obtain
\norm{\ms{H}_{\mr{pbc},I}(t)}\leq\gamma
, which leads to

\displaystyle\norm{\braket{l|e^{-i \ms{H}_{\eff,\mr{pbc}}^{4l_\imax} t} - e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4l_\imax} t}|l^\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n(l^{\prime},l)}^{\infty}2\frac{(\gamma t)^{n}}{n!}\leq 4\left(\frac{\gamma t}{n(l^{\prime},l)}\right)^{n(l^{\prime},l)}.

(188)

As a result, we get the difference between the original and refined effective Hamiltonians as

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{0}\ms{U}_\mr{amp1,pbc}^{4l_\imax}(t) - \ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{4l_\imax}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)} }\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1,pbc}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)-\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l\in D^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}4\frac{(\gamma t)^{n(l_{\mathrm{max}},l)}}{n(l_{\mathrm{max}},l)!}

(189)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\sum_{l=3l_{\mathrm{max}}-2m_{\mathrm{max}}}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma t)^{\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil}}{(\lceil l/m_{\mathrm{max}}\rceil)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\left(\frac{3\gamma t}{3l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}-2}\right)^{3l_{\mathrm{max}}/m_{\mathrm{max}}-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{250(m_{\mathrm{max}})^{2}}.

As stated by Theorem
6
, the original amplification protocol
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
accurately generates
\ket{\psi(t)}
as Eq. (
181
).
Therefore, the one under the refined effective Hamiltonian is also justified as

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{0} \ms{U}_\mr{amp1,pbc}^{l_\imax} (t) \ket{0} \ket{\psi(0)} - \frac 12 \ket{\psi(t)}}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1,pbc}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\norm{\bra{0}\ms{U}_\mr{amp1,pbc}^{4l_\imax}(t)-\ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{4l_\imax}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)} }\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1,pbc}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)-\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}+\norm{\bra{0}\ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{4l_\imax}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)} - \frac 12 \ket{\psi(t)}}\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)\ket{0}\ket{\psi(0)}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{19}{1000(m_{\mathrm{max}})^{2}}\varepsilon^{3}.

(190)

This bound is actually smaller than
\varepsilon/3
, and we complete the proof of Theorem
7
.
\quad\square

(191)

for

The norm of
H_{0}
is arbitrary as long as it is bounded.
The effective Hamiltonian in the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space,
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
, is the same as Eq. (
), but it is dense in the basis

C.1
Truncation order of Floquet-Hilbert space

We first determine the proper truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
for the Floquet-Hilbert space, as we did in Section
4
.
To this aim, we begin with deriving the Lieb-Robinson bound on the transition rate, corresponding to Theorem
3
.

Theorem 9
.

(Bound on transition rate)

We assume
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
with certain positive constants
h
and
\zeta
.
Then, for
l,l^{\prime}
such that
|l|,|l^{\prime}|\leq l_{\mathrm{max}}
, the transition rate is bounded from above by

\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{|l-l^{\prime}|-2\beta\zeta^{\prime}ht}{\zeta^{\prime}}+2/\beta\right).

(192)

Here,
\beta
and
\zeta^{\prime}
are positive constants defined by

\displaystyle\beta=(1-e^{-1/\zeta})^{-1},\quad\zeta^{\prime}=(1/\zeta-1+e^{-1/\zeta})^{-1}.

(193)

Proof.—
We prove the theorem in a similar way to Theorem
3
.
We start from the Dyson series expansion in the interaction picture [See Eq. (
42
)],

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\sum_{\{l_i\}}\prod_{i=1}^n \braket{l_i|\ms{H}_I(t_i)|l_{i-1}}}

(194)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\braket{l_i|\ms{H}_I(t_i)|l_{i-1}}},

where we insert the identity
\sum_{l_{i}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l_{i}}\bra{l_{i}}=I
for
n-1
times.
The summation
\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}
is taken over
l_{i}\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
under fixed
l_{0}=l^{\prime}
and
l_{n}=l
.
We introduce a new variable
m_{i}=l_{i}-l_{i-1}
instead of using
\{l_{i}\}
, and then the above integrand is bounded by

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\braket{l_i|\ms{H}_I(t_i)|l_{i-1}}}

(195)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\delta_{l^{\prime}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n},l}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{H_{-m_i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\delta_{l^{\prime}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n},l}h^{n}e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|/\zeta}.

Since
l^{\prime}+m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}=l
implies
|m_{1}|+\ldots+|m_{n}|\geq|l-l^{\prime}|
, it can be further bounded as follows,

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|-|l-l^{\prime}|\right)h^{n}e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|/\zeta}

(196)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2h)^{n}\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}-|l-l^{\prime}|\right)e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}/\zeta}.

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle(2h)^{n}S_{n}(|l-l^{\prime}|).

Here,
\theta(x)
is a step function, defined by
\theta(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
and
\theta(x)=0
otherwise.
The summation
S_{n}(M)
is defined by

S_{n}(M)=\sum_{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}=0}^{\infty}\theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}-M\right)e^{-\sum_{i}m_{i}/\zeta},

(197)

which satisfies
S_{1}(M)=\sum_{m=M}^{\infty}e^{-m/\zeta}=\beta e^{-M/\zeta}
.

Let us evaluate the upper bound on
S_{n}(M)
.
We split the summation over
m_{n}
into the one over
m_{n}\leq M-1
and the one over
m_{n}\geq M
, which results in

S_{n}(M)=\sum_{m_{n}=0}^{M-1}e^{-m_{n}/\zeta}S_{n-1}(M-m_{n})+\beta^{n}e^{-M/\zeta}.

(198)

We use this relation recursively until
S_{1}
appears.
After the single use of this equality, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{n}(M)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{n}=0}^{M-1}\sum_{m_{n-1}=0}^{M-m_{n}-1}e^{-(m_{n}+m_{n-1})/\zeta}S_{n-2}(M-m_{n}-m_{n-1})

(199)

\displaystyle\qquad+\beta^{n-1}e^{-M/\zeta}\sum_{m_{n}=0}^{M-1}1+\beta^{n}e^{-M/\zeta},

where we use
e^{-m_{n}/\zeta}\leq 1
for the second term.
By repeating this calculation
n-1
times, we arrive at

\displaystyle S_{n}(M)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{M-1}\ldots\sum_{m_{n-1}=0}^{M-1-M_{n-2}}\right)e^{-M_{n-1}/\zeta}S_{1}(M-M_{n-1})

(200)

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-M/\zeta}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\beta^{n-k+1}\left(\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{M-1}\ldots\sum_{m_{k-1}=0}^{M-1-M_{k-2}}\right)1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-M/\zeta}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta^{n-k+1}\left(\sum_{m_{1}=0}^{M-1}\ldots\sum_{m_{k-1}=0}^{M-1-M_{k-2}}\right)1,

where
\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{k}
is denoted by
M_{k}
.
In the above formula, the summation of
1
over
\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1}
represents the number of lattice points included in a
(k-1)
-dimensional pyramid
P_{k-1}^{M}=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})\in[0,M-1]^{k}\,|\,0\leq\sum_{i}x_{i}\leq M-1\}
, and hence it is bounded by the volume of
P_{k-1}^{M+2}
.
This leads to the relation,

\displaystyle S_{n}(M)\leq\beta^{n}e^{-M/\zeta}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\{\beta^{-1}(M+2)\}^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\leq\beta^{n}e^{-(1/\zeta-1/\beta)M+2/\beta},

(201)

We define a positive constant
\zeta^{\prime}
by
1/\zeta^{\prime}=1/\zeta-1/\beta
as Eq. (
193
).
The above inequality enables to evaluate the integrand
F_{n}
as

F_{n}\leq(2h)^{n}S_{n}(|l-l^{\prime}|)\leq(2\beta h)^{n}e^{-|l-l^{\prime}|/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

(202)

Finally, going back to the inequality Eq. (
194
), we arrive at the bound on the transition rate,

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}(2\beta h)^{n}e^{-|l-l^{\prime}|/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}\leq e^{2\beta ht-|l-l^{\prime}|/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

(203)

This completes the proof of the theorem.
\quad\square

Theorem
9
says that the bound on the transition amplitude from
\ket{l^{\prime}}
to
\ket{l}
exponentially decays in the distance
|l-l^{\prime}|
for Hamiltonians with
\norm{H_m}\lesssim e^{-\order{|m|}}
.
The decay is relatively slow compared to the cases
H_{m}=0
(
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
) showing
\order{|l-l^\prime|^{-|l-l^\prime|}}
.
In fact, this result is reminiscent of the Lieb-Robinson bound for short-ranged interacting systems
[
79
,
80
,
81
]
, which provides exponentially-decaying correlation functions under interactions
U_{ij}\sim e^{-\order{|i-j|}}
.
Based on Theorem
9
, we assess how the exact time-evolved state can be approximated by the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space, and determine the proper truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
We summarize the result by the following theorem, which is a counterpart of Theorem
4
.

Theorem 10
.

(Floquet-Hilbert space truncation)

We assume
\norm{H_m}\leq Ce^{-|m|/\zeta}
.
The exact time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
is approximated by the truncated state
\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}
[See Eq. (
19
)] as

\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq 4\zeta^{\prime}e^{2\beta ht-(l_{\mathrm{max}}-1)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

(204)

Proof.—

The derivation is similar to the one for Theorem
4
.
According to Eqs. (
50
)-(
52
), we should evaluate the following two values,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}=\sum_{l\in(D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})}\norm{\bra{l} e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax^\prime}t} \ket{0}}e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}t}\ket{0},\quad\varepsilon_{2}=\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\norm{\bra{l} e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax^\prime}t}-e^{i\ms{H}_\eff^{l_\imax}t} \ket{0}}e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}t}-e^{i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0},

(205)

where the left hand side of Eq. (
204
) is bounded by
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
under
l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}\to\infty
.
Theorem
9
soon concludes the upper bound of
\varepsilon_{1}
;

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in(D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}})}e^{2\beta ht-|l|/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}

(206)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{l_{\mathrm{max}}-1}^{\infty}\differential xe^{2\beta ht-x/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\zeta^{\prime}e^{2\beta ht-(l_{\mathrm{max}}-1)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

The second error
\varepsilon_{2}
can be evaluated by the Dyson series expansion like Eq. (
42
); each term of
\varepsilon_{2}
in Eq. (
205
) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\norm{\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^n \ms{H}_I^{l_\imax^\prime}(t_i) - \prod_{i=1}^n \ms{H}_I^{l_\imax}(t_i)|0}}.

(207)

As stated in the proof of Theorem
3
, each of
\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{H}_{I}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}(t_{i})|0}
and
\braket{l|\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathscr{H}_{I}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t_{i})|0}
is decomposed into a product of transition amplitudes via a path
\ket{0}\to\ket{l_{1}}\to\ldots\to\ket{l_{n-1}}\to\ket{l}
.
Their difference appears only when the path goes across
D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}^{\prime}}\backslash D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}
.
In other words, denoting the summation over the set of
\{l_{i}\}
taking such nontrivial paths by
\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}^{\prime}
, we reach

\displaystyle[\text{Eq. (\ref{Aeq3:thm_truncation_proof_1})}]\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\differential t_{n}\ldots\int_{0}^{t_{2}}\differential t_{1}\sum_{\{l_{i}\}}\,^{\prime}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\braket{l_i | \ms{H}_I^{l_\imax^\prime}(t_i) | l_{i-1}}}.

(208)

When we define the hopping distance
m_{i}
by
m_{i}=l_{i}-l_{i-1}
, it should satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|\geq(l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)+(l_{\mathrm{max}}-0)
for
\{l_{i}\}
such that a nontrivial path is organized.
This results in the relation described by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{\{m_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\norm{H_{-m_i}}\right)\times\theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|-2l_{\mathrm{max}}+|l|\right)

(209)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2ht)^{n}}{n!}S_{n}(2l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\beta ht)^{n}}{n!}e^{-(2l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\zeta^{\prime}e^{2\beta ht-(l_{\mathrm{max}}-1)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

Combining this inequality with
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
and Eq. (
206
), we obtain Eq. (
204
), which completes the proof.
\quad\square

Theorem
10
determines the proper truncation order of the Floquet-Hilbert space for the algorithm.
When we aim at the desirable accuracy as
\norm{\ket{\psi(t)}-\ket{\psi^{l_\imax}(t)}}-\ket{\psi^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)}\leq\varepsilon
, it is sufficient to choose the truncation order

by

\displaystyle l_{\mathrm{max}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil 2\beta\zeta^{\prime}ht+\zeta^{\prime}\log(1/\varepsilon)+\zeta^{\prime}\log(4\zeta^\prime)+\frac{2\zeta^{\prime}}{\beta}+1\right\rceil\in\Theta\left(ht+\log(1/\varepsilon)\right).

(210)

The result has similar scaling in
t
and
1/\varepsilon
to the one for Hamiltonians with a finite number of Fourier components, described by Eq. (
59
), other than the factor of
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
.
While the actual
m_{\mathrm{max}}
is infinite for exponentially-decaying Fourier components as

, the parameters
h
and

play a role of
\gamma
and

respectively.

C.2
Amplitude amplification

We next verify the validity of the amplitude amplification protocols, discussed in Section
5
, for time-periodic Hamiltonians with exponentially-decaying Fourier components
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
.

The first amplification relies on the translation symmetry of the effective Hamiltonian, as discussed in
5.1
.
To show its extension, we begin with discussing the approximate translation symmetry in the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space, which is a counterpart of Lemma
8
.

Lemma 11
.

(Approximate translation symmetry)

We assume
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
, and consider the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
.
The transition rate has an approximate translation symmetry in that it satisfies

\displaystyle\norm{\bra{l} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4 l_\imax} t} \ket{l^\prime} - e^{il^\prime\omega t} \bra{l \ominus l^\prime} e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4 l_\imax} t} \ket{0}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{l^{\prime}}-e^{il^{\prime}\omega t}\bra{l\ominus l^{\prime}}e^{-i\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}t}\ket{0}\leq 2e^{2\beta ht-(8l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|-|l^{\prime}|)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

(211)

Proof.—
The proof is done in a similar manner to that of Lemma
11
.
We consider a perturbation
\tilde{\mathscr{H}}_{b}(t)
designated by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathscr{H}}_{b}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{(l,m)\in\partial\tilde{F}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\ket{l}\bra{l\oplus m}\otimes e^{8il_{\mathrm{max}}\omega t}H_{-m}+\text{h.c.},

(212)

with
\partial\tilde{F}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\{(l,m)\,|\,l\in D^{4_{\mathrm{max}}},\,8l_{\mathrm{max}}-l+1\leq m\leq 8l_{\mathrm{max}}-1\}
.
This Hamiltonian indicates hopping terms that go across the boundaries
\ket{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
and
\ket{-4l_{\mathrm{max}}+1}
.
Let
\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{pert}}(t)
denote a time evolution operator under
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}+\tilde{\mathscr{H}}_{b}(t)
.
Then, due to the exact translation symmetry in the interaction picture, the transition amplitude
\braket{l|\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{pert}}(t)|l^{\prime}}
satisfies

\braket{l|\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{pert}}(t)|l^{\prime}}=e^{il^{\prime}\omega t}\braket{l\ominus l^{\prime}|\tilde{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathrm{pert}}(t)|0}.

(213)

The difference of transition amplitudes between
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}+\tilde{\mathscr{H}}_{b}(t)
and
\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
is bounded in a similar way to Eqs. (
207
) and (
209
).
The difference survives only when the trajectory
\ket{l^{\prime}}\to\ket{l_{1}}\to\ket{l_{n-1}}\to\ket{l}
pass through the boundaries
\ket{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
and
\ket{-4l_{\mathrm{max}}+1}
via
\tilde{\mathscr{H}}_{b}(t)
, and then its length
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}|
with
m_{i}=l_{i}-l_{i-1}
should be equal to or larger than
(4l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|)+(4l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l^{\prime}|)
.
We obtain its upper bound in a similar way to Eq. (
209
),

\displaystyle\norm{\braket{l |\tilde{\ms{U}}_\mr{pert}(t)-e^{-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4 l_\imax}t}|l^\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{n}\frac{(2ht)^{n}}{n!}S_{n}(8l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|-|l^{\prime}|)

(214)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{2\beta ht-(8l_{\mathrm{max}}-|l|-|l^{\prime}|)/\zeta^{\prime}+2/\beta}.

Using this relation twice and the symmetry Eq. (
213
) leads to Eq. (
211
).
\quad\square

With the usage of this approximate translation symmetry, we can organize the amplitude amplification by symmetry like Theorem
6
.
We prepare the truncated Floquet-Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{8l_{\mathrm{max}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}
, and make the initial state uniform in
\ket{l}
with
l_{\mathrm{max}}\in\Theta(ht+\log(1/\varepsilon))
.
The state resulting from the time evolution
\exp(-i \ms{H}_\eff^{4l_\imax} t)
outputs the target time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with amplitude
1/2
as follows.

Theorem 12
.

(Amplification by symmetry)

We assume
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
, and choose the truncation order
l_{\mathrm{max}}\in\order{ht+\log(1/\varepsilon)}
by Eq. (
210
).
Let us summarize the amplification protocol relying on the symmetry by
\mathscr{U}_{\mathrm{amp1}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}(t)
, whose explicit formula is given by Eq. (
72
).
Then, it generates the time-evolved state
\ket{\psi(t)}
with
\order{1}
amplitude as

\norm{\braket{0|\ms{U}_\mr{amp1}^{l_\imax}(t)|0}\ket{\psi(0)} - \frac{1}{2} \ket{\psi(t)}}\ket{\psi(0)}-\frac{1}{2}\ket{\psi(t)}\leq\frac{5}{64(\zeta^{\prime})^{2}}\varepsilon^{3}.

(215)

It is reasonable to assume that the desirable error
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, not greater than the constant
8\zeta^{\prime}/\sqrt{15}

C.3.2

We compose block-encoding of the refined effective Hamiltonian

We begin with describing the assumption on time-periodic Hamiltonians
H(t)

If we adopt the one for the main text as Eq. (

.
To avoid this complexity, we instead consider time-periodic Hamiltonians
H(t)
written by

The Fourier components are given by

In other words, adding

-iM_{j}

In the following discussion, the oracles

and

In addition, we also assume the access to the coefficients

for

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{coef}}^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}\ket{0}\ket{0}=\sum_{m\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha_{j}^{m}}{\alpha^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}}\ket{m}\ket{j},\quad\alpha^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}=\sum_{m\in D^{l_{\mathrm{max}}}}\sum_{j=1}^{j_{\mathrm{max}}}\alpha_{j}^{m}.

(230)

where we use

C.4
Algorithm and comparison

The algorithm for computing the time-evolved state for time-periodic Hamiltonian with
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
is almost the same as those in Section
7
.
We separately consider adiabatic-like cases and generic long-time cases.
The computational resources are obtained by replacing
\gamma
in Table
1
by
h
.
The query complexity is determined by the coefficients of
j_{\mathrm{max}}C
and
C^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
in the number of overall elementary gates.
Their scaling is summarized as follows;

Adiabatic-like cases.—

The scaling of the query complexity is optimal in the time
t
and nearly-optimal in the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
.

•

Number of ancillary qubits;
n_{a}+\order{\log j_\imax+ \log(\gamma t) + \log\log(1/\varepsilon)}
.

•

Query complexity;
\order{\alpha t + \log(1/\varepsilon)}
.

•

Additional gates per query;
\order{ n_a + \log j_\imax+ \log(\gamma t) + \log\log(1 /\varepsilon) }
.

Generic long-time cases.—

The scaling of the query complexity is nearly-optimal in the time
t
and the inverse error
1/\varepsilon
.
For practical problems up to
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
-time, it becomes optimal in the time
t
.

•

Number of ancillary qubits;
n_{a}+\order{\log j_\imax+ \log(\gamma/\omega) + \log\log(\omega t/\varepsilon)}
.

•

Query complexity;
\order{\alpha t + \omega t \log(\omega t/\varepsilon)}
.

•

Additional gates per query;
\order{ n_a + \log j_\imax+ \log(\gamma/ \omega) + \log\log(\omega t/\varepsilon)}
.

We note that
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
factor in the query complexity is buried by
l_{\mathrm{max}}
.
This difference from the cases
H_{m}=0
(
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
) essentially originates from the form of the Lieb-Robinson bound dictated by
9
.
A slightly slow decay in the transition amplitude results in slightly larger cost for the cases
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
, compared to the results in the main text.
This leads to the nearly-optimal dependence of the query complexity in
1/\varepsilon
.
Anyway, since the factor
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
is not so large compared to others, the relation to the quantum algorithms for time-independent and generic time-dependent Hamiltonians, discussed in Section
7
, is maintained.
Importantly, in the query complexity, the whole energy scales
\alpha t
and
\gamma t
are separated from the
\order{\log(1/\varepsilon)}
term; the query complexity is usually much smaller than that of the truncated Dyson-series algorithm, and close to that of the qubitization technique.
The number of ancillary qubits, which scales as
\order{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)}
for an allowable error
\varepsilon
, also lies between those for these algorithms.

We also mention about the complexity of the oracles for the cases
\norm{H_m}\leq he^{-|m|/\zeta}
.
In contrast to the cases
H_{m}=0
(
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
), we require a quantum circuit which embeds the function
\alpha_{j}^{m}
to the amplitude of a quantum state with
\order{j_\imax(\gamma t + \log(1/\varepsilon))}
or
\order{j_\imax(\gamma/\omega+ \log(\omega t/\varepsilon))}
degrees of freedom, by the oracle
G_{\mathrm{coef}}^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}
defined by Eq. (
228
).
While integrable functions are efficiently implemented
[
54
]
, the number of elementary gates for the worst cases amounts to the dimension of the quantum state, i.e.
C^{4l_{\mathrm{max}}}\in

\order{j_\imax(\gamma t + \log(1/\varepsilon))}
or
\order{j_\imax(\gamma/\omega+ \log(\omega t/\varepsilon))}
.
While the preparation of oracles is relatively difficult compared to the cases
H_{m}=0
(
|m|>m_{\mathrm{max}}
), we emphasize that it is much easier than that of the truncated Dyson-series algorithm.
In the truncated Dyson-series algorithm, we need more or less an oracle that embeds the information of time-dependent Hamiltonians at every discretized time into a quantum state.
Such an oracle is exemplified by Eq. (
135
) for generic Hamiltonians
[
19
]
.
For time-dependent LCU Hamiltonians or sparse-access Hamiltonians, it requires a quantum state holding all the coefficients
\alpha_{j}(t)
at every discretized time
t
as an oracle
[
20
]
.
The number of elementary gates per oracle can be proportional to that of the discretized time,
\order{j_\imax\omega\gamma t/ \alpha\varepsilon}
, at the worst case.
Therefore, our algorithm also improves the complexity of the oracles as well as the query complexity, compared to the truncated Dyson-series algorithm.

Appendix D
Efficient algorithm for high-frequency regimes with
\omega\simeq\alpha,\gamma

In Section
7.2
, we concentrate on the generic long-time regime with the frequency
\omega\in\order{N^0}
for the number of sites, while the whole energy scales
\alpha,\gamma
are given by
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
.
Here, we show its reasonableness by showing that quantum simulation of broad time-periodic Hamiltonians under the frequency comparable to the whole energy scale can be almost attributed to that of time-independent Hamiltonians.

As a generic setup for time-periodic Hamiltonians, we assume the locality [= the system involves at-most
\order{1}
-body interactions] and the extensiveness.
The latter one means that the energy scale per site, represented by
\lambda
, is bounded.
In our definitions, the scale
\lambda
is approximated by
\order{\alpha/N, \gamma/N}
.
When the ratio of the frequency
\omega
to the local energy scale
\lambda
is sufficiently large as
\omega/\lambda\gg 1
, we can apply the perturbative expansion in
(\omega/\lambda)^{-1}
, called the high-frequency expansion
[
59
,
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
]
.
Recent developments in generic local and extensive Floquet systems have revealed the accurate upper bound on the error as follows
[
60
]
;

\displaystyle\norm{U(nT) - e^{-i H_\mr{FM}^{l} nT}}\leq C_{1}\lambda NnTe^{-C_{2}l_{0}}+C_{3}nN(\lambda T)^{l+2}(l+1)!.

(243)

In the above inequality,
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, and
C_{3}
represent some positive constants, and the integer
l_{0}\in\order{\omega/\lambda}
provides the optimal truncation order of the perturbation theory, while the actual truncation order
l
(
\leq l_{0}
) is optional.
The time-independent Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{l}
is the
l
-th order Floquet-Magnus expansion, given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{l}=\sum_{l^{\prime}=0}^{l}H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(l^{\prime})},\quad H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(0)}=H_{0},\quad H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2iT}\int_{0}^{T}\differential t_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\differential t_{2}[H(t_{1}),H(t_{2})],

(244)

where each
l^{\prime}
-th order term in
\omega/\lambda
, represented by
H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{(l^{\prime})}
, involves
l^{\prime}
-fold multi-commutators of
H(t)
(See Ref.
[
60
]
for the explicit formula).

Based on the above formalism, we see that the long-time dynamics at
t=(n+\delta)T\gg T
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in[0,1)
can be computed in a trivial way when
\omega
is comparable to
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
.
In this regime, the integer
l_{0}
is
\order{N}
due to the local energy scale
\lambda\in\order{\alpha/N, \gamma/N}
.
Then, we simulate the dynamics
U(t)=U(\delta T)U(nT)
with approximating the latter one
U(nT)
by Eq. (
243
).
We choose the truncation order
l
by
\order{N^0}
.
Since
H(t)
generally has
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
local terms, the Floquet-Magnus expansion
H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{l}
for such
l\in\order{N^0}
can be identified with
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
-time classical computation, and
H_{\mathrm{FM}}^{l}
is also composed of
\mathrm{poly}\left(N\right)
local terms.
Therefore, we can apply the qubitization technique to the implementation of
\exp(-i H_\mr{FM}^l nT)
with the cost designated by Eq. (
13
).
Since the right hand side of Eq. (
243
) is bounded by
\order{n N^{-l-1}}
, the replacement of
U(nT)
by
\exp(-i H_\mr{FM}^l nT)
with an allowable error
\varepsilon
is valid as long as

n\leq\mathrm{Const.}\times\varepsilon N^{l+1}.

(245)

We usually employ a polynomial accuracy
\varepsilon\in\order{N^{-\nu_1}}
in quantum computation.
The assumption for the frequency, represented by
\omega\in\order{N^{\nu_2}}
, implies the period
T\in\order{N^{-\nu_2}}
.
Therefore, by properly choosing the
\order{N^0}
truncation order
l\geq\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}-1
, we can efficiently simulate
U(nT)
for arbitrary polynomial time
nT\in\order{N^{l+1-\nu_1-\nu_2}}
with the qubitization technique for time-independent Hamiltonians.
The remaining micromotion
U(\delta T)
can be implemented with our protocol for the adiabatic-like regime (See Section
7.1
), but it does not affects the computational cost due to
\delta T\ll nT
.

In short, the dynamics under time-periodic Hamiltonians
H(t)
with
\omega\sim\alpha,\gamma
can be simulated with the time-independent Hamiltonian approaches.
As a result, it is reasonable to concentrate on the cases where
\omega
is negligible compared to
\alpha
and
\gamma
, as we do in Section
7.2
.
