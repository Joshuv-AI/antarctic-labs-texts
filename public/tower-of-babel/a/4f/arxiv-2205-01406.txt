Winding vectors of topological defects: Multiband Chern numbers
Axel Fünfhaus, Thilo Kopp, Elias Lettl

Abstract
Chern numbers can be calculated within a frame of vortex fields related to phase conventions of a wave function. In a band protected by gaps the Chern number is equivalent to the total number of flux carrying vortices. In the presence of topological defects like Dirac cones this method becomes problematic, in particular if they lack a well-defined winding number. We develop a scheme to include topological defects into the vortex field frame. A winding number is determined by the behavior of the phase in reciprocal space when encircling the defect’s contact point. To address the possible lack of a winding number we utilize a more general concept of winding vectors. We demonstrate the usefulness of this ansatz on Dirac cones generated from bands of the Hofstadter model.

I
Introduction

Chern numbers characterize topologically invariant properties of two-dimensional insulators
Avron et al. 1983
. They are computed within the Berry formalism
Simon 1983
. In the case of gapped bands the problem reduces to computing the Berry curvature and integrating it over a suitable closed manifold such as the Brillouin zone. Instead of analyzing the Berry curvature one can also use a frame of vortex fields of the complex wave functions
Kohmoto 1985
;
Hatsugai 1993
;
Hatsugai 2004
: The manifold is divided into patches, in which the wave function has to be defined uniquely according to an appropriate phase convention. One then rewrites the integral over the Berry curvature using Stokes’ theorem as a line integral along the edges of these patches. This results in determining the winding number of the phase of the transition function
\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi(\bm{k})}
, which relates the phases of the wave functions in adjacent patches with phase difference
\chi(\bm{k})
. Extending
\chi(\bm{k})
over the entire manifold generates a field of vortices, the number of which can readily be counted and is equal to the Chern number. This method has been successfully applied to calculate the Chern numbers of free electrons in a magnetic field
Fradkin 2013
, electrons in a periodic potential
Kohmoto 1985
;
Kohmoto 1989
;
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1990
or fractional Chern insulators
Hafezi et al. 2007
;
Gerster et al. 2017
and other systems with degenerate ground state multiplets
Hatsugai 2004
;
Hatsugai 2005
.

Problems arise, once Bloch bands become gapless due to Dirac cones. In that case it remains to compute the Chern number for the connected bands jointly. The Berry formalism then has to be modified. One has to work with a non-Abelian Berry holonomy instead of a mere phase factor
Vanderbilt 2018
. The determinant of this unitary matrix still allows the definition of a gauge invariant total Berry phase, so a Chern number can be assigned. Introducing multiband vortex fields in this case is possible
Hatsugai 2004
, but providing a simpler solution without major modifications seems desirable.

In this paper, we want to develop a scheme to determine the joint Chern number of bands that are connected by Dirac cones using single-band vortex fields. Our testing ground will be the Hofstadter model, which can exhibit Dirac cones. Chern numbers of the Hofstadter band structure can be calculated with the Berry formalism of non-degenerate states
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Kohmoto 1989
, however the bands connected by Dirac cones can only be analyzed implicitly with this approach. The Dirac cones of the Hofstadter model lack a winding number which imposes an additional challenge. The winding in a two-level system is commonly defined by the change of the phase difference of the two components of an eigenstate in reciprocal space around the crossing point (compare e.g. Ref.
Park and Marzari 2011
). Dirac cones with missing winding number have to be described with winding vectors instead, originally proposed in Ref.
Montambaux et al. 2018
. We will calculate the Chern numbers of bands in the Hofstadter model connected by Dirac cones through a rotation of the winding vectors in pseudospin space. This gives their Dirac cones the same form as in the common toy Hamiltonian
Montambaux et al. 2009

\hat{H}^{\pm}(\bm{k})=\hbar v_{\text{F}}(\pm k_{\text{x}}\sigma_{\text{x}}+k_{\text{y}}\sigma_{\text{y}})

(1)

for Dirac fermions with a well-defined winding number of
\pm 1

Park and Marzari 2011
. Here,
v_{\text{F}}
is the Fermi velocity and
\sigma_{\text{x/y}}
are Pauli matrices in pseudospin space.

This paper is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
we recapitulate the vortex field formalism for the determination of Chern numbers (Sec.
II.1
), then we consider the issue of topological defects with well-defined winding numbers (Sec.
II.2
) and finally we apply it exemplarily to the Qi–Wu–Zhang (QWZ) model (Sec.
II.3
). Sec.
III
is devoted to the Hofstadter model. We show how to treat Dirac cones with non-trivial winding vectors for the Hofstadter model with half a flux quantum per lattice site (Sec.
III.1
). Then a scheme for the computation of the Chern numbers for any flux threading of the Hofstadter model, including those with Dirac cones, will be presented (Sec.
III.2
). In Appendix
A
we portray the algebra of magnetic translation operators of the Hofstadter model, the results of which will be used to give some details on calculations concerning the weak coupling limit of the Hofstadter model in Appendices
B
and
C
. In Appendix
D
we explain how the presence of topological defects gives rise to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant which manifests itself in a discontinuity of the vortex fields.

Patches and vortex fields

The Chern number of the

(2)

denotes the differential surface vector of the torus and

(3)

of the

;
Thouless et al. 1982
. We shall have a total of
q

matrix. The Berry connection requires eigenstates in reciprocal space to have a well-defined derivative. This is guaranteed by “fixing the gauge” locally in

2

1

1

This is because the projector of eigenstates is smooth, which follows from Eq. (D1) in

and the fact that

is only supposed to have analytic functions as matrix elements. This also ensures that the line bundle of the
n
-th band is smooth.
. “Singularities”, points where the Berry connection diverges, must then originate from the phase convention resulting in a discontinuity of the wave function at some points. With a different phase convention those phases can be made well-defined in the neighborhoods of these points, not on the entire torus.

How do we “fix the gauge” and where are the singular points of a given phase convention? Let
|{\varPhi_{\text{I}}}\rangle
be some normalized state in the Hilbert space. For convenience, we choose
|{\varPhi_{\text{I}}}\rangle
to be constant, although it is only required to be smooth. Let

|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{I}}}\rangle=\frac{\hat{P}_{\varPsi}^{n}(\bm{k})|{\varPhi_{\text{I}}}\rangle}{|\langle{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})|\varPhi_{\text{I}}}\rangle|}=\e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\text{I}}(\bm{k})}|{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})}\rangle.

(4)

If a band has a non-trivial Chern number (

) then
S_{\text{I}}\neq\emptyset
. At
\bm{k}_{j}\in S_{\text{I}}
we need a second phase convention, where corresponding eigenstates
|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{II}}(\bm{k})}\rangle
are smooth at
\bm{k}_{j}
. A neighborhood of
\bm{k}_{j}
is denoted as a patch

. Within the patches
P=\bigcup_{j}P_{j}
a different phase convention is set up with some other normalized state
|{\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle

|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{II}}(\bm{k})}\rangle=\frac{\hat{P}_{\varPsi}^{n}(\bm{k})|{\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle}{|\langle{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})|\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle|}=\e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\text{II}}}|{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})}\rangle,

(5)

where

and
|{\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle
are chosen such that the condition
\langle{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})|\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle\neq 0
is fulfilled for all
\bm{k}\in P
. In turn there must be a set of points
S_{\text{II}}\subset T^{2}\setminus P
, where
\langle{\varPsi^{n}(\bm{k})|\varPhi_{\text{II}}}\rangle=0
.

For
\bm{k}\in P\setminus S_{\text{I}}
the wave functions
|{\varPsi_{\text{I}}^{n}(\bm{k})}\rangle
and
|{\varPsi_{\text{II}}^{n}(\bm{k})}\rangle
can be related by the transition function

|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{I}}(\bm{k})}\rangle=|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{II}}(\bm{k})}\rangle\e^{\mathrm{i}(\varphi_{\text{I}}-\varphi_{\text{II}})}=|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{II}}(\bm{k})}\rangle\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi(\bm{k})}

(6)

and as a result their Berry connections are related like

\bm{A}^{n}_{\text{I}}=\bm{A}^{n}_{\text{II}}+\mathrm{i}\e^{-\mathrm{i}\chi}\bm{\nabla}_{\bm{k}}\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi}.

(7)

Note that we can vary the size of patches

, so we can define
\chi(\bm{k})
everywhere except at
\bm{k}\in S_{\mathrm{I}}\cup S_{\mathrm{II}}
.

Now Stokes’ theorem can be applied and results in line integrals along the oriented boundary of the patches
\partial P
(provided
\partial P\cap S_{\text{II}}=\emptyset
)

2

2

In mathematical terms the function
\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi}
is a transition function, which connects the two locally defined sections
|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{I}}}\rangle
and
|{\varPsi^{n}_{\text{II}}}\rangle
of the line bundle of the
n
-th band. The transition function contains the information how “twisted” the line bundle is, i.e. the Chern number. Usually this transformation is called “gauge transformation” in the literature, even though
\mathrm{i}\e^{-\mathrm{i}\chi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi}
is not an exact differential form. It does however leave the equations of motion invariant, as, analogously to Dirac’s magnetic monopole argument

has to be an integer).

C_{n}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\partial P}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\left(\bm{A}^{n}_{\text{II}}-\bm{A}^{n}_{\text{I}}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\partial P}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\Arg(\e^{\mathrm{i}\chi}).

(8)

8

(9)

as the azimuth and

as the radius (see e.g. Fig.
2
; to make vortices easier to spot, the azimuth is further represented by the color of the vector field). Then calculating

is equivalent to counting all the vortices, where the radius does not vanish and where the vortex field is not continuously differentiable. These vortices are associated with flux tubes of monopole charges (see Sec.
II.2
), so we call them ‘‘flux carrying vortices’’
3
3

3

We want to add that there is an interesting relation between the origin of these discontinuities in the vortex field and the fact
Bott and Chern 1965
that one can also determine the Chern number by calculating the algebraic sum of zeros of a smooth, holomorphic section of the line bundle in question: It is the restraint of having normalized wave functions that leads to discontinuities—flux carrying vortices—at these former zero points.
. There are also vortices where
\langle{\varPhi_{\text{II}}|\varPsi^{n}_{\text{I}}}\rangle
does vanish. These “trivial vortices” must not be summed up to calculate the Chern number as the field is smooth at the vortex centers.

|{\varPhi_{\text{I}}}\rangle
and

whose first component is non-negative real if

It is instructive to include certain two-dimensional topological defects—Dirac cones—into the analysis. They correspond to Weyl points, topological defects that exist in three dimensions. They are responsible for the emergence of flux carrying vortices. It is well known that Chern numbers can be related to monopole charges in a solid torus parametrization of the Hamiltonian matrix

III
Hofstadter model

The Hofstadter Hamiltonian exhibits Dirac cones that break the usual division of having clockwise or counterclockwise winding. The Hofstadter Hamiltonian is a tight-binding model on a square lattice in an external magnetic field
Hofstadter 1976
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}=

\displaystyle-t_{a}\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{q}\left(c_{m,n}^{\mu+1}\right)^{\dagger}c_{m,n}^{\mu}+\text{h.c.}

(16)

\displaystyle-t_{b}\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}\displaystyle\sum_{\mu=1}^{q}\left(c_{m,n+1}^{\mu}\right)^{\dagger}c_{m,n}^{\mu}\e^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\varphi\mu}+\text{h.c.},

where

is the position of the (magnetic) unit cell,
\mu
a sublattice index,
t_{a}
and

are hopping parameters, and

. The additional phase factors (in comparison to the field free case) break the translation symmetry of the square lattice, requiring the unit cells to consist of

lattice sites. They are the Peierls phases

;
Hofstadter 1976
and can be conceptualized as Aharonov–Bohm phases originating from a flux piercing through each plaquette of the square lattice. They can be calculated by replacing the field-free hopping terms

(17)

and depend on the choice of

. We picked

. The flux per lattice site is

in units of the flux quantum

, and

is the external magnetic field (the lattice constants are set equal to one). The explicit inclusion of a sublattice is chosen to avoid the unusual Fourier transformation sometimes applied by authors, e.g. in

;

;

. There, a Brillouin zone of dimensions

q

16

c_{k_{\text{x}},k_{\text{y}}}^{\mu}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}}\displaystyle\sum_{m,n}\e^{-\mathrm{i}k_{\text{x}}qm}\e^{-\mathrm{i}k_{\text{y}}n}c_{m,n}^{\mu},

where

\displaystyle t_{a}c_{\mu+1}^{\dagger}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mu}-t_{a}c_{\mu}^{\dagger}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mu+1}

(20)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2t_{b}\cos(k_{\text{y}}+2\pi\varphi\mu)c_{\mu}^{\dagger}c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\mu}

IV
Conclusion

In this paper we have studied the effect of topological defects on Chern numbers of gapless bands. These defects manifest themselves as Dirac cones that can be characterized with winding vectors. This generalizes the notion of winding numbers, which cannot be defined if the winding vector is placed in the
\sigma_{\text{x}}
–
\sigma_{\text{y}}
-plane. Such a situation occurs in the Hofstadter model. We have shown that in a weak coupling limit all of its Dirac cones lack a well-defined winding number. In that case Chern numbers of bands can be calculated by rotating their characteristic winding vectors
\bm{w}
to have a non-vanishing
\sigma_{\text{z}}
-component. Then a combination of single-band vortex fields of the involved bands leads to the correct Chern number. This procedure has been successfully applied on the Hofstadter model which to our knowledge has not been done in the literature.

The next logical step for future research would be to extend the notion of winding vectors and winding numbers to Dirac cones and topological defects in Hermitian
q\times q
,
q>2
matrices. It is possible to work with effective
2\times 2
matrices by projecting the Hamiltonian onto the eigenbasis of the contact point of the Dirac cone, as outlined in
Montambaux et al. 2018
;
Kaufmann et al. 2020
: With the contact point
\bm{k}_{0}
the Hamiltonian can be written as
\hat{H}=\hat{H}(\bm{k}_{0})+\hat{H}_{1}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{0})+\mathcal{O}((\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{0})^{2})
. If
U(\bm{k}_{0})
diagonalizes
\hat{H}(\bm{k}_{0})
and
\hat{P}
projects onto the eigenbasis of the contact point of
\hat{H}(\bm{k}_{0})
then
\hat{P}U^{\dagger}(\bm{k}_{0})\hat{H}_{1}(\bm{k}-\bm{k}_{0})U(\bm{k}_{0})\hat{P}
provides an effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian of a Dirac cone that can be attributed a winding vector. A promising study ground for this ansatz could again be the Hofstadter model. We have already found earlier that in the limit
t_{a}\to 0
two Dirac cones located at
k_{\text{y}}
and
k_{\text{y}}+\pi
exist in the same eigenspace and have opposite winding. We suspect that this might be the case in the isotropic case, too, as one finds

\hat{H}_{1}(k_{\text{x}}-k_{\text{x}}^{0},k_{\text{y}}-k_{\text{y}}^{0})=\hat{H}_{1}(k_{\text{x}}-k_{\text{x}}^{0},-k_{\text{y}}-(k_{\text{y}}^{0}+\pi))

(45)

and

U(k_{\text{x}}^{0},k_{\text{y}}^{0}+\pi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}0&\openone\\
\hline\cr(-1)^{q/2}\openone&0\\
\end{array}\right)U(\bm{k}_{0}).

(46)

This study may be interesting for inquiring merging scenarios of Dirac cones beyond

Delplace and Montambaux 2010

Finally, it should be noted that the vortex field approach does not provide us with a new type of topological invariant but a more efficient way to compute Chern numbers that shows its strength if the position of vortices can be determined based on some symmetries. It would be interesting to use this fact in computationally expensive problems like in the study of correlated Chern insulators, where symmetries can indeed be used to predict the location of a topological phase transition in an associated manifold

Appendix D
Möbius bundle description of topological defects in 2D

Consider the toy Hamiltonian

\hat{H}^{+}(\bm{k})=k_{\text{x}}\sigma_{\text{x}}+k_{\text{y}}\sigma_{\text{y}}.

(66)

Around a circle
S^{1}
at
\bm{k}=0
we may choose smooth complex eigenstates. These have a

gauge freedom, as we can multiply eigenstates with a complex phase factor
\e^{\mathrm{i}\phi(\bm{k})}
. In mathematical terms eigenstates correspond to sections of a line bundle
E

Kaufmann et al. 2020

E
is given by the Cartesian product of the base space
S^{1}
times the manifold associated to the structure group
U(1)
, the fiber, which is also
S^{1}
, because there exist eigenstates that are globally smooth. This results in the trivial bundle

, which does not indicate by itself an associated topological invariant of this problem. Let us now consider the Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{\mathcal{R}}^{+}(\bm{k})=k_{\text{x}}\sigma_{\text{x}}+k_{\text{y}}\sigma_{\text{z}},

(67)

which can be obtained from Eq. (
66
) by unitary rotation. Since Eq. (
67
) is a real Hermitian matrix, we are allowed to choose real eigenstates. However, it turns out that these cannot be made globally smooth. If we restrict ourselves to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\simeq\{+1,-1\}
structure group, then sections are defined on a Möbius bundle with fiber
[-1,1]
, which locally looks like
U_{j}\times[-1,1]
, where
\{U_{j}\}
are open sets that cover
S^{1}

Klinkhamer and Rupp 2003
;
Kiritsis 1987
. Normalized eigenstates are then sections, which are defined on the boundary of the Möbius band
\partial[-1,1]=\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. This allows the definition of a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariant of a topological defect. Let
|{\varPsi(\theta)}\rangle
be an eigenstate of Eq. (
67

is a parametrization of the base space. With Eq. (
13
) one will find that real eigenstates obey

n

2

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

Klinkhamer and Rupp 2003

Kiritsis 1987

The quantization of the Berry phase will hold for any Hamiltonian with Dirac cones if a real Möbius bundle construction as discussed above is possible, which is for example the case for graphene, as its Hamiltonian can also be made purely real by a continuous unitary transformation.

The relation between the twist of the bundle and the presence of Dirac cones can be further clarified, by considering a special solid torus parametrization of the

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=(2+r\cos k_{\text{y}})\cos k_{\text{x}}

(68)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=(2+r\cos k_{\text{y}})\sin k_{\text{x}}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=r\sin k_{\text{y}},

where e.g.
