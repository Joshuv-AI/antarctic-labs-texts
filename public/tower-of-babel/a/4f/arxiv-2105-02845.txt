A Unifying and Canonical Description of Measure-Preserving Diffusions
Alessandro Barp 1,2 , So Takao 3 , Michael Betancourt 4 , Alexis Arnaudon 5 , Mark Girolami 1,2

Abstract
. A complete recipe of measure-preserving diffusions in Euclidean space was recently derived unifying several MCMC algorithms into a single framework. In this paper, we develop a geometric theory that improves and generalises this construction to any manifold. We thereby demonstrate that the completeness result is a direct consequence of the topology of the underlying manifold and the geometry induced by the target measure P P ; there is no need to introduce other structures such as a Riemannian metric, local coordinates, or a reference measure. Instead, our framework relies on the intrinsic geometry of P P and in particular its canonical derivative, the deRham rotationnel, which allows us to parametrise the Fokker–Planck currents of measure-preserving diffusions using potentials. The geometric formalism can easily incorporate constraints and symmetries, and deliver new important insights, for example, a new complete recipe of Langevin-like diffusions that are suited to the construction of samplers. We also analyse the reversibility and dissipative properties of the diffusions, the associated deterministic flow on the space of measures, and the geometry of Langevin processes. Our article connects ideas from various literature and frames the theory of measure-preserving diffusions in its appropriate mathematical context. Keywords. MCMC, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, Measure-preserving Diffusions, Geometric Statistics, Langevin Processes

Ideally, the construction should solely depend on the target measure

Main contributions and Structure of the Paper

In this article, we derive the characterisation of
P
-preserving diffusions for any smooth positive measure
P
on an arbitrary
n
-dimensional manifold
\mathscr{M}
, thus
solving this problem in complete generality.
Just as importantly, we derive connections with other fields such as Poisson geometry, thermodynamics, and cohomology that explains how the construction fits in the bigger picture of mathematics.
By leveraging the geometry generated by
P
, we provide a canonical construction for these diffusions, without introducing any other structures on
\mathscr{M}
, such as coordinates, a connection, a reference measure, a metric, or other mechanical structures like a Poisson bi-vector field.

Following the ideas from de Rham and Koszul,
we rely on the
P
-musical isomorphisms
P^{\flat}
and
P^{\sharp}
between multi-vector fields and twisted differential forms to construct the
P
-rotationnel

\text{curl}_{P}\equiv P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}
(introduced in §
3
),
which transforms the calculus of twisted differential forms associated to the exterior derivative
\mathbf{d}
into a measure-informed calculus of multi-vector fields
associated to
\text{curl}_{P}
.
The operators
P^{\flat}
and
\text{curl}_{P}
induce the canonical geometry of the target
P
, which can then be used to show that
any

P
-preserving diffusion on
any
manifold takes the form

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}&=\underbrace{\overbrace{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{Fokker--Planck potential}}+\overbrace{P^{\sharp}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{topological obstruction}}}_{\text{conservative }L^{2}(P)\text{-antisymmetric}}+\overbrace{\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{dissipative drift }}+\underbrace{Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\text{Stratonovich noise}}}^{L^{2}(P)\text{-symmetric fluctuation-dissipation balance}}.\end{split}

Here, the conservative term
\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
characterises the set of all
P
-preserving vector fields.
Indeed, by analogy with the construction of the magnetic vector potential in physics,
we can locally build for any
P
-preserving vector field
X
, a ‘bi-vector potential’
\mathzapfc{A}
such that
X=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})
.
We will see that globally, this holds up to topological obstructions, which is represented by the term
P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
, where
\gamma
is a representative of the
(n-1)
-twisted de Rham cohomology group.
In addition to this, the dissipative term
{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
balances the fluctuations introduced by the arbitrary noise process
Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}
, as in the fluctuation-dissipation theorem of statistical physics, ensuring that the stochastic and dissipative components of (
2
), when combined, are also
P
-preserving.

Furthermore, when the target
P
is expressed as
P=e^{-H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, where
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
is an arbitrary smooth positive reference measure on
\mathscr{M}
, equation (
2
) can be decomposed as follows:

(3)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=&\underbrace{\overbrace{\underbrace{(\quad\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})}_{\text{volume-preserving}}+\underbrace{X{A}_{H}}_{\text{density-preserving}})\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{local potential of Fokker--Planck current}}\quad+\overbrace{P^{\sharp}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{topological obstruction}}}_{\text{conservative }L^{2}(P)\text{-antisymmetric drift}}\\
&+\overbrace{(\underbrace{-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}}_{\text{density-dissipative drift }}+\underbrace{\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}}_{\text{volume-dissipative drift }})\,\mathrm{d}t+\underbrace{Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\text{Stratonovich noise}}}_{\text{volume-preserving }L^{2}(\mu_{\mathscr{M}})\text{-symmetric diffusion}}}^{L^{2}(P)\text{-symmetric fluctuation-dissipation balance}},\end{split}

where
X{A}_{H}\equiv\mathzapfc{A}(\mathbf{d}H,\cdot)
is a vector field generated by the log-density
H
, which we shall later refer to as an
\mathzapfc{A}
-
Hamiltonian vector field
.
In Euclidean space, we can show that (
3
) boils down to (
1
), thus showing that the integrability condition is not inherent to measure-preserving diffusions, but rather comes from the Fourier analysis used by the authors to derive the Euclidean recipe.

To derive this result, we begin in §
2
by building on ideas from Poisson mechanics
to construct a general
P
-preserving diffusion on
\mathscr{M}
that naturally extends the Euclidean recipe (
1
).
However, since the resulting diffusion is
constructed from the Euclidean recipe,
it does not take into account
the non-trivial topological features of the sample space, and is thus
incomplete, meaning that not every
P
-preserving diffusion can be expressed in that form.
To remedy this,
we rely on the geometry induced by
P
in §
3
to derive the complete system (
2
).
In §
4
, we discuss the important case when
\mathscr{M}
is compact, and show how the topological obstructions can be directly expressed using harmonic forms of an arbitrary Riemannian metric.
The reversibility properties of (
2
) will then be discussed in §
5
,
where we derive a general condition for the generator to be reversible up to some measure-preserving diffeomorphism, that generalises the momentum flip used in HMC to obtain a well-defined Metropolis–Hastings correction.
In §
6
, we
give an example of some new insights gained by the geometric formulation, by deriving a new recipe of
volume-free
e^{-H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
-preserving diffusions
, a subclass of (
3
) in which the volume terms
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})
and
\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
vanish.
The volume-free property is shared by both the underdamped and standard overdamped Langevin processes, and it not only simplifies the practical implementation of the diffusion, but confers it important geometric guarantees, making this volume-free subclass of diffusions particularly suited as a starting point to construct Langevin-like samplers. For the remaining sections, in §
7
, we study non-degenerate processes on manifolds, and characterise
P
-preserving diffusions that are expressed in terms of Riemannian Itô noise.
In §
8
, we discuss the deterministic flow on the space of smooth measures associated to
P
-preserving diffusions,
and derive a simple formula for the rate of change of functionals over measures, such as the KL divergence and other information entropies.
In §
9
, we discuss the geometry and generalisation of the underdamped Langevin diffusions to manifolds, which we can use to construct an irreversible Langevin-based sampler,
that generalises both the second-order Langevin HMC on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as well as the Euler–Poincaré diffusions on Lie groups, of which the original HMC algorithm is a special case.
Finally, in
§
10
and §
11
we briefly discuss the ergodicity and history of measure-preserving diffusions.

Notation

Throughout,
\mathscr{M}
is an arbitrary
n
-dimensional smooth manifold, and in particular, no assumptions are made on its connectedness, orientability, or compactness.
We denote by

\mathfrak{X}^{k}(\mathscr{M})
and
\Omega^{k}(\mathscr{M})
the spaces of
k
-vector fields and
k
-forms, i.e., antisymmetric, contravariant and covariant tensor fields of rank
k
respectively.
The space of vector fields is denoted
\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\equiv\mathfrak{X}^{1}(\mathscr{M})
, and given
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
,
\mathcal{L}_{X}
denotes the associated Lie derivative.
The exterior derivative on differential forms is denoted using the bold font
\mathbf{d}
, and the space of smooth
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on
\mathscr{M}
is denoted
C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
.
We say that
P
is a
smooth measure
if it is Radon measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the null sets of the manifolds (generated by the charts).
This means that over any coordinate chart
(x^{i})
, we can write
P=p\,\mathbf{d}x
, where
\mathbf{d}x
is the local Lebesgue measure, and
p:\mathscr{M}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, which we will assume to be smooth.
We say that
P
is positive if it is globally supported (its support is
\mathscr{M}
) and its local densities
p
above are positive smooth functions - in which case we denote its divergence on vector fields by
\text{div}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\to C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, defined as
\text{div}_{P}(X)P\equiv\mathcal{L}_{X}P
,
and let
L^{2}(P)
be the space of square
P
-integrable
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions on
\mathscr{M}
.
Given a diffeomorphism
\mathcal{R}:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
, we denote by
\mathcal{R}^{*}
and
\mathcal{R}_{*}
the induced pullback and pushforward on tensor fields respectively.

Stochastic Differential Equations On Manifolds

The standard Itô stochastic processes, such as the Wiener and Ornstein-–Uhlenbeck processes, are typically defined over the real line. Multivariate stochastic processes over the real numbers are then built up by injecting these processes along each coordinate axis.
A general manifold, however, is not rigid enough to admit this kind of construction without being endowed with a connection, which allows a multivariate stochastic process to be defined locally and then developed into a stochastic process that evolves over the manifold
[
149
,
125
,
58
,
136
,
101
]
.
Conveniently, Stratonovich processes do not require this machinery and can be defined over general manifolds using vector fields that direct the local noise
[
90
,
101
]
. We shall denote the Stratonovich differential by
\circ\,\mathrm{d}
, and the standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
W_{t}

[
25
,
140
]
.

2.
A General Measure-Preserving Diffusion on Manifolds

In this section, we present a step-by-step construction of a general class of measure-preserving diffusions that extends the Euclidean recipe (
1
) to manifolds in a natural way. To do this, our strategy is to inspect each term in (
1
) and replace them with its natural counterparts on manifolds, and finally showing that the resulting diffusion is indeed measure-preserving.

First, we replace the Lebesgue measure
\mathbf{d}x
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with an arbitrary smooth and positive reference measure

\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
on
\mathscr{M}
, hereafter referred to as the
volume measure
(note that there is no analogue of
\mathbf{d}x
on general non-homogeneous manifolds).
Typically,
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
is a measure that is canonically induced by additional structures on
\mathscr{M}
, for instance a Haar, uniform, Riemannian or symplectic measure.
The assumptions on the target
P
guarantee that it can be written as a
Gibbs distribution
with respect to some “Hamiltonian” function
H:\mathscr{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and “inverse temperature”
\beta>0
, given as

(4)

\displaystyle P\equiv p_{\infty}\,\mu_{\mathscr{M}}\propto e^{-\beta H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}.

The Hamiltonian then represents the unnormalised log-density
H\propto\log p_{\infty}
of the target measure.

Let us first consider the scenario in which the antisymmetric component
Q
in (
1
) vanishes.
In this case, we observe that the complete recipe consists of a general noise contribution

\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
associated to an arbitrary positive semi-definite matrix
D
, corrected by a drift component

(-D\nabla H+\nabla\cdot D)\,\mathrm{d}t
. This latter drift term is obtained by the following well-known result
[
140
, Prop. 4.5]
(the notion of reversibility will be discussed carefully in §
5
).

Proposition 2.1

(
[
134
]
)
.

Given a vector field
X
on
\mathscr{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the target measure
P\equiv p_{\infty}\mathbf{d}x
is a solution to the stationary Fokker–Planck equation for the Itô SDE
\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=X(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
when

\nabla\cdot\mathfrak{J}(p_{\infty})=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathfrak{J}(p_{\infty})\equiv p_{\infty}X-\nabla\cdot(p_{\infty}D).

Moreover,
\mathrm{d}Z_{t}
is a
p_{\infty}\mathbf{d}x
-preserving reversible process, that is, the Fokker–Planck current
\mathfrak{J}(p_{\infty})
vanishes, if and only if

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\frac{1}{p_{\infty}}\nabla\cdot(p_{\infty}D)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}=(-D\nabla H+\nabla\cdot D)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Hence, given the random noise
\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
, the deterministic drift
(-D\nabla H+\nabla\cdot D)\mathrm{d}t
provides the necessary and sufficient correction to ensure that the diffusion is reversible with respect to the target measure
p_{\infty}\mathbf{d}x
.
To extend this idea to manifolds, we begin by replacing the Itô differential in the noise contribution
\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
, with Stratonovich differentials, as these do not require a connection on
\mathscr{M}

[
136
, Sec. 4]
.
Specifically, we replace the Itô noise
\sqrt{2D}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
with the Stratonovich noise
Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}
, where
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\}

is a generic family of ‘noise’ vector fields.
To proceed further, we shall rely on the following useful result (proved in
A.1
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Given smooth vector fields
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
on
\mathscr{M}
, consider the Stratonovich SDE

(5)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=X(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}\,.

Its generator is given by

\mathcal{L}f=Xf+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}Y_{i}f
, see for example
[
24
]
. The Fokker–Planck operator, viewed as the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
in
L^{2}(\mu_{\mathscr{M}})
is then

(6)

\mathcal{L}^{*}f=\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}\left(-fX+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}(f)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}f\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)\,.

Now, for
P
to be an invariant measure of the system, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{L}^{*}e^{-\beta H}=0
.
Mirroring the Euclidean case, we choose the drift
X
to ensure that
the
Fokker–Planck current

\mathfrak{J}(f)\equiv-fX+\frac{1}{2}Y_{i}(f)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}f\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}

vanishes when
f
is the target density
e^{-\beta H}
. This leads to the choice

X\equiv-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i},

which naturally replaces the term
-D\nabla H+\nabla\cdot D
in the Euclidean recipe.
Specifically, we have

\mathfrak{J}(e^{-\beta H})=0\quad\iff\quad\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\left(-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

and in particular, the stationary Fokker–Planck equation

\mathcal{L}^{*}(e^{-\beta H})=\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathfrak{J}(e^{-\beta H}))=0

is satisfied.
We note that this
is an instance of the
fluctuation-dissipation relation
,
where the volume-distortion
caused by the noise
Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}
is exactly balanced
by the volume-dissipative drift
X\equiv-\frac{\beta}{2}Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}\mathrm{d}t+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
.

We now consider the general case in which
Q
does not necessarily vanish.
We note that geometrically, the drift term
Q\nabla H
in (
1
) represents a vector field that depends linearly on the gradient of the log-density
H
.
While manifolds are generally non-linear spaces, their tangent and cotangent bundle provide linear spaces over each point
x\in\mathscr{M}
, namely, the tangent space
T_{x}\mathscr{M}
of vectors, to which
Q\nabla H|_{x}
belongs, and the cotangent space
T^{*}_{x}\mathscr{M}
of covectors, to which
\nabla H|_{x}
belongs.

Geometrically, maps from
\Omega^{1}(\mathscr{M})
to
\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})
, such as
\nabla H\mapsto Q\nabla H
that are linear at each point on
\mathscr{M}
are called
vector bundle morphisms
, since they are compatible with the vector bundle structures.
A vector bundle morphism can be conveniently represented using a

bracket

\mathzapfc{B}\in\Gamma(T\mathscr{M}\otimes T\mathscr{M})
(i.e., a contravariant tensor field of rank two),
which assigns to the log-density
H
, a
\mathzapfc{B}
-Hamiltonian vector field

X_{H}^{\mathzapfc{B}}
via the relation
X_{H}{B}\equiv\mathzapfc{B}(\mathbf{d}H,\cdot)
. Hence, we deduce that the linear operator
Q
may be interpreted as a bracket
\mathzapfc{B}
on manifolds and the vector field
X_{H}^{\mathzapfc{B}}
is a natural candidate for generalising the term
Q\nabla H
in the Euclidean recipe.

Example 2.3
.

\mathzapfc{B}
-Hamiltonian vector fields are ubiquitous in science. For example, they include Riemannian gradient flows, Hamiltonian vector fields associated to Poisson structures, and in particular the ones associated to a symplectic structure,
4-gradient vector fields generated by a Lorentzian metric over spacetime, and thermodynamic flows, which we shall come back to in §
5
.

On the other hand,
it is not a-priori clear how to interpret the final term “
\nabla\cdot Q
” in (
1
) intrinsically,
as this would mean “differentiate the rank two tensor field
\mathzapfc{B}
to turn it into a vector field” – such an operation does not exist on general manifolds.
To proceed, we make the following ansatz for the measure-preserving diffusion on
\mathscr{M}

(7)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\big(X_{H}{B}+Y\big)\mathrm{d}t+\big(-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\big)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

where
Y
is currently an unspecified vector field that will later generalise the term “
\nabla\cdot Q
” in (
1
).
Meanwhile, we have the following result (proof in §
A.2
):

Theorem 2.4
.

The Gibbs measure (
4
) is preserved under the bracket diffusion (
7
)
if and only if the vector field
Y
satisfies

(8)

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(X_{p_{\infty}}{B}-\beta p_{\infty}Y)=0\,.

We shall now use this result to make sense of the vector field
Y
.
Observe that by analogy with the Euclidean case, we want to obtain a complete recipe that is valid for any target distribution
P
,
and thus for any choice of density
p_{\infty}
with respect to a convenient volume measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
.
Since
Y
should not depend on the choice of the target density, we expect the identity
(
8
) to hold for
any
positive density
p_{\infty}
.
In particular, setting
p_{\infty}\equiv
constant, we have
X_{p_{\infty}}{B}=0
, implying that
Y

must preserve the reference measure, i.e.,
\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y)=0
.
If we assume this latter condition, then (
8
) can be written as

(9)

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(X_{p_{\infty}}{B})=\beta Y(p_{\infty}),

which holds for any density
p_{\infty}
.
When
\mathzapfc{B}
is a
Poisson structure
(i.e., it is antisymmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity),
equation (
9
) is precisely the definition of
modular vector fields

Y

in Poisson mechanics
(see
[
51
,
52
,
159
]
).
Specifically, we can associate to a bracket
\mathzapfc{B}
and a volume measure

3.
Local and Global Completeness of

To work with the geometry and topology of

Let

-linear isomorphism

Example 3.1
.

As an interesting example that shows the relevance of the

-musical isomorphisms in the context of statistics, observe that if

, then

yields the

Crucially for our purpose, the

-musical isomorphisms

and

allow us to construct a canonical derivative associated to

\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions.

Definition 3.2

(
[
98

)
.

The
P
-
rotationnel
of a
k
-vector field for some integer

\text{curl}_{P}\equiv P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}:\mathfrak{X}^{k}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{k-1}(\mathscr{M}),

where
\mathbf{d}
also denotes the extension of the exterior derivative to twisted forms

for any

An important property of the operator

is that it does not depend on the normalisation of

(i.e., if

\text{curl}_{aP}=\text{curl}_{P}

In the context of measure-preserving diffusions, we are particularly interested in the action of
\text{curl}_{P}
on vector fields and bi-vector fields, which is given as follows.
In the case
k=1
(i.e., vector fields), the
P
-rotationnel recovers the divergence operator

\text{curl}_{P}|_{\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})}=\text{div}_{P},

and in the case

we have the following result showing that

maps bi-vector fields to their modular vector field (
10

).

Theorem 3.3
.

Given a volume manifold
(\mathscr{M},P)
, we have the identity

\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})=X^{P}{A}.

It follows that we can replace the terms

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}

\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})

\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}

suggesting that the rotationnel plays a central role in the construction of measure-preserving diffusions.
To realise the full potential of
\text{curl}_{P}
in our context, we need the following lemma (proved in §

Lemma 3.4
.

The Fokker–Planck operator of the SDE (
5

formal adjoint of the generator
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the dual pairing
\left<f,P\right>_{*}\mapsto\int f\mathbf{d}P

\mathcal{L}^{*}P=-\mathcal{L}_{X}P+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\mathcal{L}_{Y_{i}}\mathcal{L}_{Y_{i}}P.

If
P
is also positive, then the density of the smooth measure
\mathcal{L}^{*}P
with respect to

is given by

\text{div}_{P}\left({\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}-X\right)
, and we call

\mathfrak{J}(P)\equiv{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}-X

Thus

\mathcal{L}^{*}P=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathfrak{J}(P))P=\text{div}_{P}(\mathfrak{J}(P))P.

Hence, the condition that

\mathcal{L}^{*}P=0\quad\iff\quad\text{div}_{P}\left(\mathfrak{J}(P)\right)=0.

Now, as discussed in §

\text{curl}_{P}\circ\text{curl}_{P}=P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}\circ P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}=P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}=0,

Accordingly, the measure

-free multi-vector fields on

In particular, the first homology group

Remark 3.6

(Fokker–Planck Operators)
.

The Fokker–Planck operator was defined as the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to the pairing
\left<f,P\right>_{*}=\int_{\mathscr{M}}f\mathbf{d}P
.
However in the previous section, it was defined as the adjoint with respect to the pairing

defined as
\left<f,h\right>_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}=\int fh\mathbf{d}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

The difference between these two pairings is that the former is a special case of the standard Poincaré bilinear form on the manifold

, i.e.,
\left<\alpha,\beta\right>_{*}\equiv\int\alpha\wedge\beta
, while the latter is associated to the induced measure-informed bilinear form via the
P
-musical flattening, that is,

\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{P}\equiv\left<\cdot,P^{\flat}\cdot\right>_{*}

The relation between the various definitions of the Fokker–Planck operator is then

\left<\mathcal{L}f,P\right>_{*}=\left<f,\mathcal{L}^{*}P\right>_{*}=\left<f,\text{div}_{P}(\mathfrak{J}(P))P\right>_{*}=\left<f,\text{div}_{P}(\mathfrak{J}(P))\right>_{P}=\left<f,\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathfrak{J}(e^{-H}))\right>_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}.

When focussing on just the
local
representations of
P
-preserving diffusions, we can also use the musical isomorphisms of
P
to consider a
“
P
-twisted’’ Poincaré lemma
2
2

2

The standard Poincaré lemma states that any closed differential form

\mathbf{d}\beta

P
-twisted’ Poincaré lemma is the measure-informed analogue to this on the space of multi-vector fields.
, which states that any vector field

that preserves the measure

can be expressed locally as
curled vector fields
(see theorem
B.1
). More precisely, this means that we can find a neighbourhood
\iota_{U}:U\hookrightarrow\mathscr{M}
around any point in
\mathscr{M}
, where
\iota_{U}
is the inclusion, and a locally defined
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(U)
for which

holds.
Combining these results with the fact that the Fokker–Planck current of
P
-preserving diffusions is
\text{div}_{P}
-free, we obtain the following complete characterisation, or recipe, of
P
-preserving diffusions:

Theorem 3.7

(Local and global completeness of
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions)
.

The smooth, positive target measure

is a stationary measure of the general diffusion process

(15)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=X(Z_{t})\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}\,

if and only if
the drift takes the form

X=\text{curl}_{\iota_{U}^{*}P}(\mathzapfc{A})+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\iota_{U}^{*}P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
on a neighbourhood
\iota_{U}:U\hookrightarrow\mathscr{M}
of any point, for some local antisymmetric bracket
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(U)

(16)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\text{curl}_{\iota_{U}^{*}P}(\mathzapfc{A})\mathrm{d}t+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\iota^{*}_{U}P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

which we will also refer to as
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions since as discussed below, they recover the original

-diffusions (
11

is written in the form (
4

Hence, the class of
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions is locally complete. Furthermore, they are globally complete, that is, any
P
-preserving diffusions is of the form

More generally,

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\left(\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

for some
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})

For example in classical mechanics, the Newton force

\int_{\lambda}F=\int_{\tilde{\lambda}}F

Such force fields are represented by potential energy functions

V
such that

F=\mathbf{d}V

Similarly, in the theory of electromagnetism, Gauss’s and Faraday’s laws may be represented in the form
\mathbf{d}\bf{F}=0

\bf{F}
is the electromagnetic 2-form. These laws are equivalent to the existence of a magnetic potential 1-form

\bf{F}=\mathbf{d}{\bf}\bf A

[
9
]

The situation is analogous in the context of

\text{div}_{P}(\mathfrak{J}(P))=0

\mathfrak{J}(P)=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})

Thus we recover the result that if

\mathzapfc{A}

Finally, to recover the

, and a density-preserving term
X_{H}{A}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{X_{H}{A}}p_{\infty}=0.

Remark 3.8
.

Let us briefly explain why
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}
is called “curl”.
Note that when
\mathscr{M}=\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\mathbf{d}\bf{x}

\equiv\mathbf{d}x\,\mathbf{d}y\,\mathbf{d}z
, we can write any bi-vector field
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
as
\mathzapfc{A}\equiv\mathzapfc{A}_{x}\partial_{y}\wedge\partial_{z}+\mathzapfc{A}_{y}\partial_{z}\wedge\partial_{x}+\mathzapfc{A}_{z}\partial_{x}\wedge\partial_{y}
, and
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}
corresponds to the classical curl of the “vector field”
(\mathzapfc{A}_{x},\mathzapfc{A}_{y},\mathzapfc{A}_{y})
:

\text{curl}_{\mathrm{d}\bf{x}}(\mathzapfc{A})=\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{y}}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{z}}{\partial y}\right)\partial_{x}+\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{z}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{x}}{\partial z}\right)\partial_{y}+\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{x}}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{y}}{\partial x}\right)\partial_{z}.

In particular, any
\mathbf{d}\bf{x}
-preserving vector field can be written as above, which we may also view as a sum of Hamiltonian vector fields on the coordinate 2-surfaces:

\text{curl}_{\mathrm{d}\bf{x}}(\mathzapfc{A})=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{y}}{\partial z}\partial_{x}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{y}}{\partial x}\partial_{z}\right)}_{\text{Ham. field of }\mathzapfc{A}_{y}\text{ on x-z plane}}+\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{z}}{\partial x}\partial_{y}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{z}}{\partial y}\partial_{x}\right)}_{\text{Ham. field of }\mathzapfc{A}_{z}\text{ on x-y plane}}+\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{x}}{\partial y}\partial_{z}-\frac{\partial\mathzapfc{A}_{x}}{\partial z}\partial_{y}\right)}_{\text{Ham. field of }\mathzapfc{A}_{x}\text{ on z-y plane}}.

(Recall the Hamiltonian vector field of
H
in Darboux coordinates
(q,p)
is

\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\partial_{q}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\partial_{p}
).

Remark 3.9
.

In the Euclidean space recipe, the terms associated with the
2^{nd}
-order tensors

\nabla\cdot D
and
\nabla\cdot Q
look identical – they are obtained by applying the “divergence”
\nabla\cdot
to
D
and
Q
, which are both second-order objects (i.e., tensors with two indices).
In our geometric formulation, we observe that
\nabla\cdot D
is actually obtained by differentiating the noise vector fields
\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
which are first-order tensors, while
\nabla\cdot Q
, corresponding to the term
\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})
in our formulation, genuinely involves differentiating a second-order tensor
\mathzapfc{A}
.
In particular, while it is true that
\text{curl}\circ\text{curl}=0
, the equality
\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot=0
is only valid when applied to the antisymmetric component, i.e.,

\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot D\neq 0,\qquad\nabla\cdot\nabla\cdot Q=0.

The geometric formulation properly distinguishes these operations:

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}\left(\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)=Y_{i}(\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i}))+\left(\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})\right)^{2},\qquad\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}\left(\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)=0,

so that while
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})
is always volume-preserving, this is generally not the case for
\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
(an important exception is the Langevin diffusion as we shall discuss in §
6
).

In the next section we will describe the important scenario where the sample space
\mathscr{M}
is compact, in which case the topological obstructions can be represented explicitly in terms of harmonic forms.

4.
Measure-Preserving Diffusions on Compact Manifolds

We saw previously
that when the topology of the sample space contain topological obstructions, it is necessary to add an additional term in
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions representing the non-triviality of the homology of
P
.
For compact orientable manifolds, we may use the de Rham–Hodge–Kodaira’s decomposition of differential forms to construct the Fokker–Planck current of general
P
-preserving measures, as shown in the following theorem (proved in §
A.7
).

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be a compact orientable Riemannian manifold and let
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}\equiv\text{vol}
and
\nabla\cdot
denote respectively the Riemannian measure and divergence.
Then, any
e^{-H}\text{vol}
-preserving diffusion has the form

(17)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}\equiv\left(X_{H}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\left(\nabla\cdot Y_{i}-Y_{i}(H)\right)Y_{i}-\nabla\cdot\mathzapfc{A}+e^{H}\sharp\star^{-1}\zeta\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

where
\star
is the Hodge star operator,
\sharp
is the Riemannian musical isomorphism,
\mathzapfc{A}
is an antisymmetric tensor and
\zeta
is a harmonic
(n-1)
-form (i.e., it satisfies “Maxwell’s equations”
\mathrm{d}\zeta=0
,
\mathrm{d}\star\zeta=0
).

A similar result can be found in
[
90
]
, although the authors assume that the diffusion is also non-degenerate (i.e., its generator is elliptic) in order to obtain a Riemannian metric from the noise process (the Riemannian Brownian motion), which is then used to turn the Fokker–Planck current
\mathfrak{J}(P)
into a 1-form that can be analysed through its de Rham–Hodge–Kodaira decomposition.
In order to clarify the roles played by
the assumptions of compactness and non-degeneracy, we have treated them separately, the latter which can be found in §
7
.

Note that in the above theorem
4.1
, we do not make any assumptions on the noise, although it assumes that we can express our target measure
P
in terms of the Riemannian measure
vol
, which might be inconvenient in practice.
Topologically, the presence of the harmonic term in (
17
) may be understood from the fact that on compact orientable manifolds, the twisted de Rham cohomology groups are isomorphic to the space of harmonic forms.
It follows that
P
-preserving vector fields have the form
\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})+P^{\sharp}(\zeta)
for an antisymmetric bracket
\mathzapfc{A}
and a non-zero harmonic
(n-1)
-form
\zeta
(see §
B
).
Hence, on compact manifolds, any
P
-preserving diffusions take the form

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\underbrace{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})\,\mathrm{d}t+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\mathzapfc{A}\text{-diffusion}}+\underbrace{P^{\sharp}(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{harmonic obstruction}},

for some harmonic
(n-1)
-form
\zeta
associated to an arbitrary Riemannian metric.

5.
Reversibility

Recall that the Euclidean recipe for measure-preserving diffusions (
1
) depends entirely on an antisymmetric matrix
Q
and a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix
D
. On the other hand, the geometric generalisation we have derived in previous sections is constructed using a bi-vector field
\mathzapfc{A}
and a set of noise-vector fields
(Y_{i})
.
In order to make this connection clearer,
we now discuss the symmetric/antisymmetric decomposition of
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions and its relation to the notion of reversibility.
For this, we first note that the noise vector fields
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
canonically generate a symmetric bracket, denoted

\mathzapfc{S}\equiv Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i}
, by setting

\mathzapfc{S}(\mathbf{d}f,\mathbf{d}g)\equiv Y_{i}(f)Y_{i}(g)\quad\text{ for any }f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})\,.

Introducing the notation

(18)

\{f,g\}{B}\equiv\mathzapfc{B}(\mathbf{d}f,\mathbf{d}g),

for a general bracket
\mathzapfc{B}
,
we see that the symmetric bracket defined above is
dissipative
, in the sense that it satisfies the dissipative property

\{f,f\}{S}=\sum_{i}Y_{i}(f)^{2}\geq 0\,,

which further implies

\{f,g\}{S}^{2}\leq\{f,f\}{S}\{g,g\}{S}.

This contrasts with the antisymmetric bracket, which has the conservative property

\{f,f\}{A}=0\,.

Remark 5.1
.

In the context of mechanics, special forms of (symmetric) dissipative brackets
\mathzapfc{S}
have been considered by several authors to model dissipative components of mechanical systems in an attempt to cast these systems from an algebraic framework. Examples include the metriplectic bracket
[
93
,
132
,
74
,
76
,
129
]
, double-bracket
[
32
,
26
]
, and selective-decay bracket
[
67
]
. Whereas in these works the symmetric structures are constructed in an ad hoc manner, it would be interesting to understand them as arising from noise vector fields chosen to model fluctuations, as we do here. This connection shall be further explored in a separate paper by the authors.

Example 5.2
.

When the bi-vector field
\mathzapfc{A}
has nice properties such as symmetries, it can be desirable to employ it to construct the noise vector-fields. This may be done using “noise functions"
(H_{i})
and choosing
Y_{i}\equiv X{A}_{H_{i}}
. Such a mechanism was used to construct a coordinate-independent irreversible MCMC sampler on Lie groups in
[
7
]
. In that case, the bracket
\mathzapfc{S}
safisfies

\mathzapfc{S}(\mathbf{d}f,\mathbf{d}g)=\mathzapfc{A}(\mathbf{d}H_{i},\mathbf{d}f)\mathzapfc{A}(\mathbf{d}H_{i},\mathbf{d}g)
, and the generator of the diffusion has a double bracket form (this should not be confused with the notion of “double brackets” in the sense of Brockett and Bloch).

A bracket that is decomposed into the sum of an antisymmetric and dissipative bracket is known as a
thermodynamic bracket
. It follows that
P
-preserving diffusions are parametrised by thermodynamic brackets up to topological obstructions.
Thus, many properties of the diffusion can be studied through its thermodynamic bracket; for example in §
8
, we will see that the thermodynamic bracket of the diffusion provides a simple formula to evaluate the rate of change of functionals on volume measures along the diffusion.

If we define the divergence of the dissipative bracket
\mathzapfc{S}
constructed above by
\text{div}_{P}(\mathzapfc{S})\equiv\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
, we can decompose the drift of the
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusion into components associated with the dissipative and antisymmetric brackets:

(19)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\underbrace{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{antisymmetric}}+\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(\mathzapfc{S})\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{dissipative}}+\underbrace{Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\text{noise}},

which for
P=e^{-\beta H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, further decomposes into (compare with (
1
))

(20)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\beta\left(X_{H}{A}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X_{H}{S}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\left({\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{S})+\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}.

The following corollary derives a decomposition of the corresponding generator into symmetric and anti-symmetric parts, generalizing the standard result in the Euclidean case
[
140
]
. This result is useful as for instance, it enables us to build the most general Stein operators on manifolds available from the generator approach.

Corollary 5.3
.

The generator of a
P
-preserving diffusion expressed in the form of (
2
) can be written as (recall the definition of the differential operator
X^{P}{B}
in (
10
))

(21)

\mathcal{L}f=\underbrace{X^{P}{A}(f)+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)(f)}_{L^{2}(P)\text{-antisymmetric}}+\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}{S}(f)}_{L^{2}(P)\text{-symmetric}}.

Moreover,
{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}{S}
is symmetric in
L^{2}(P)
, while
X^{P}{A}
and
P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
are both antisymmetric in
L^{2}(P)
. That is,

\left<X^{P}{A}f,h\right>_{P}=-\left<f,X^{P}{A}h\right>_{P},\qquad\left<X^{P}{S}f,h\right>_{P}=\left<f,X^{P}{S}h\right>_{P},

where
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>_{P}
denotes the
L^{2}(P)
pseudo-inner product,
\left<f,h\right>_{P}\equiv\int fh\,\mathbf{d}P
.
Hence, the generator
\mathcal{L}
is symmetric if and only if
X{A}^{P}+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)=0
.
In general, the generator of (
5
) satisfies
\mathcal{L}={\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}{S}
if and only if the Fokker-Planck current of
P
vanishes, in which case, we say that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the
detailed balance condition
, and the diffusion is
reversible
.
Finally, we have that
{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}{S}
is non-positive, i.e.,

\left<{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}{S}(f),f\right>_{P}\leq 0

for all
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
.

We refer the readers to §
A.8
for the proof.

Remark 5.4

(Carré du champ operator)
.

The generator
\mathcal{L}
of any diffusion defines a
carré du champ operator
by

\Gamma(f,h)\equiv{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\left(\mathcal{L}(fh)-f\mathcal{L}h-h\mathcal{L}f\right)

over appropriate algebras of functions. These play an important role in the study of reversible diffusions (see

[
10
]
and references therein). Using (
21
), we see that the symmetric bracket
\mathzapfc{S}
is in fact equivalent to the carré du champ operator
\Gamma
.

Corollary 5.5
.

For any
f,h\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, the carré du champ operator of a
P
-preserving diffusion is precisely the dissipative bracket generated by the noise

\Gamma(f,h)=\{f,h\}{S}.

The
L^{2}(P)
-symmetry of reversible Markov processes confers them important theoretical properties that are useful for example in the study of their convergence to equilibrium

[
10
,
140
]
.
However, they also form a restrictive class of diffusions that often have slow convergence properties
[
53
,
138
]
.
The decomposition of the generator above allows us to show that for appropriate transformations
\mathcal{R}:\mathscr{M}\to\mathscr{M}
, the generator of the
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusion is
reversible up to
\mathcal{R}
,
which extends the notion of “reversibility up to momentum flip” of the Langevin diffusion in Euclidean space, usually associated with improved mixing properties
[
61
,
152
,
153
]
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a target-preserving diffeomorphism, which is an
\mathzapfc{A}
-antimorphism and a
\mathzapfc{S}
-morphism, that is

\mathcal{R}_{*}\mathzapfc{A}=-\mathzapfc{A},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{*}\mathzapfc{S}=\mathzapfc{S}.

Then, the generator of the
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusion (
21
) is
reversible up to
\mathcal{R}
. That is, we have

\left<f,\mathcal{L}h\right>_{P}=\left<\mathcal{L}\mathcal{R}^{*}f,\mathcal{R}^{*}h\right>_{P},\quad\forall f,h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M}).

Example 5.7
.

For instance, if
\mathcal{R}_{*}Y_{i}=\pm Y_{i}
, then
\mathcal{R}_{*}\mathzapfc{S}=\mathzapfc{S}
, and this is precisely what happens in the underdamped Langevin diffusion on phase space
(q,p)
(see below), wherein
Y_{i}
are proportional to
\partial_{p}
, and the momentum-flip
\mathcal{R}:(q,p)\mapsto(q,-p)
flips the noise fields
\mathcal{R}_{*}Y_{i}=-Y_{i}
.

Combining the results from the previous sections, we have the following interpretations of the various components of measure-preserving diffusions:

(22)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=&\underbrace{\overbrace{\underbrace{(\quad X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}_{\mathzapfc{A}}}_{\text{volume-preserving}}+\underbrace{X{A}_{H}}_{\text{density-preserving}})\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{local potential of Fokker--Planck current}}\quad+\overbrace{P^{\sharp}(\gamma)\,\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{topological obstruction}}}_{\text{conservative }L^{2}(P)\text{-antisymmetric drift}}\\
&\qquad\qquad+\underbrace{(\underbrace{-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}}_{\text{density-dissipative drift }}+\overbrace{\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}}_{\text{volume-dissipative drift }})\,\mathrm{d}t+\underbrace{Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\text{Stratonovich noise}}}^{\text{volume-preserving }L^{2}(\mu_{\mathscr{M}})\text{-symmetric diffusion}}}_{L^{2}(P)\text{-symmetric fluctuation-dissipation balance}}.\end{split}

In particular, we note the following:

(i)

X_{H}{A}
(or
-Q\nabla H
) is the
p_{\infty}
-preserving (
X_{H}{A}(p_{\infty})=0
)
\mathzapfc{A}
-Hamiltonian vector field generated by an antisymmetric bracket
\mathzapfc{A}
;

(ii)

X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}{A}
(or
\nabla\cdot Q
) is a generalisation of the modular vector field from Poisson mechanics, which preserves the volume measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
and describes how the
\mathzapfc{A}
-Hamiltonian vector fields
X_{f}{A}
distort the reference measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, i.e.,

X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}{A}=0
iff
X_{f}{A}
preserves
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
for all
f
.

(iii)

When
X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}{A}
is added to
X_{H}{A}
, the resulting vector field
X^{P}{A}
is
P
-preserving.
On contractible manifolds such as
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Fokker–Planck current of any
P
-preserving diffusion can be written as
X^{P}{A}
for some
\mathzapfc{A}
; otherwise, for (global) completeness, a topological obstruction term
P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
parametrised by the
(n-1)^{th}
-twisted de Rham cohomology group
\gamma\in H^{n-1}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
must also be added by theorem
3.7
.
The resulting generator
X^{P}{A}+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
is antisymmetric in
L^{2}(P)
. On compact oriented manifolds, this topological contribution can be parametrised by harmonic forms, as discussed in §
4
.

(iv)

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
(or
\nabla\cdot D
minus the Itô-to-Stratonovich correction) represents the distortion of the volume measure along the noise vector fields, and is usually dissipative;

(v)

-Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}
(or
-D\nabla H
) is the rate of change of the target log-density along the noise fields.

(vi)

The overall noise contribution
(iv)
+
(v)
is generated by the second-order “modular” operator

{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{P}_{\mathzapfc{S}}
, which is symmetric in
L^{2}(P)
.

6.
Complete Recipe of Volume-Free
P
-preserving Diffusions

As noted in the introduction, obtaining a complete recipe of
P
-preserving diffusions
allows practitioners to focus
on the tuning of the parameters
\{\mathzapfc{A},(Y_{i})\}
, as well as its numerical implementation.
Using the geometric formalism, we now discuss a particularly interesting class of parameters inspired by
two classes of
P
-preserving diffusions that play a particularly central role in many applications, namely, the underdamped and overdamped Langevin processes, which are used for example to construct MALA and HMC respectively.

The overdamped Langevin process on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
targets a measure of the from

P\propto e^{-H}\mathbf{d}q
,
and corresponds to the choices

Q=0
and
D=
arbitrary positive-definite constant matrix in the Euclidean recipe (
1
):

(23)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-D\nabla H+\sqrt{2D}\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Since
D
is a positive-definite contravariant tensor, we may think of it as a Riemannian co-metric. Thus, the drift

D\nabla H
corresponds to a Riemannian gradient flow. On the other hand, the underdamped Langevin process evolves on the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and preserve target measures of the form

P=\mu_{H}\propto e^{-H(q,p)}\mathbf{d}q\,\mathbf{d}p
.
Starting from the Euclidean complete recipe, this is obtained by setting

Q\equiv-J\equiv-\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
-I&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad D\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&C\end{pmatrix}

where
J
is the symplectic matrix and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a positive semi-definite matrix
[
153
, Sec. 2.2.3]
. This gives us the second-order Langevin process (also called the underamped Langevin process)

(24a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}Q_{t}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{p}H(Q_{t},P_{t})\mathbf{d}t,

(24b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}P_{t}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{q}H(Q_{t},P_{t})\mathbf{d}t-C\nabla_{p}H(Q_{t},P_{t})\mathbf{d}t+\sqrt{2C}\mathbf{d}W_{t},

which models the fluctuating dynamics of a Hamiltonian system coupled to a thermostat. These systems have been widely used to construct samplers, as shown in
[
40
,
49
,
106
,
138
,
153
]
, by splitting it into a Hamiltonian and thermostat process.

An interesting property that they both share is that, while being measure-preserving, they appear to be fully specified by the target log-density
H
and the random noise term.
In other words, the reference measure terms (
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})
and
\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}
) are non-existent in both cases, which greatly simplifies the implementation of such processes. Thus, in this section, we are interested in characterising the sub-class of
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions

(25)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\underbrace{\left(X_{H}{A}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta X_{H}{S}\right)\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{density-drift}}+\underbrace{\left({\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{S})+\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{reference-measure-drift}}+\underbrace{Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}}_{\text{random noise}},

for which the terms involving the reference-measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
(we call this the ‘reference-measure drift’ in (
25
)) vanish. We refer to this subclass of
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusions as
volume-free
P
-preserving diffusions
.

While it is unclear how to even approach this problem from the Euclidean recipe/formalism, our geometric formalism provides an immediate characterisation of such processes in the case where the noise-fields
(Y_{i})
are (pointwise) linearly independent.
Indeed, in this case, we have

\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}=0\quad\iff\quad Y_{i}=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})+P^{\sharp}(\alpha)

and

\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})=0\quad\iff\quad\mathzapfc{A}=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathcal{V})+P^{\sharp}(\beta),

for some 3-vector field
\mathcal{V}\in\mathfrak{X}^{3}(\mathscr{M})
, noise bi-vector fields
\mathzapfc{A}_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})
(these are unrelated to the deterministic bi-vector field
\mathzapfc{A}
), and appropriate topological contributions
\alpha\in H^{n-1}_{dR}(\mathscr{M}),\beta\in H^{n-2}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
(see theorem
B.1
).

Hence, ignoring topological obstructions, volume-free
P
-preserving diffusions are characterised by
parameters obtained through the rotationnels of higher-order tensors,

\{\mathzapfc{A},(Y_{i})\}=\{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathcal{V}),\left(\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\right)\}.

In particular, up to topological obstructions, such diffusions take the form

(26)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=X_{H}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})}\mathrm{d}t-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X_{H}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\otimes\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})}\mathrm{d}t+\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}

and moreover, yield several geometric guarantees:
first of all, the noise fields
(Y_{i})
are automatically
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
-preserving (i.e.,
\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})=0
) by construction,
and secondly, any
\mathzapfc{A}
-Hamiltonian vector fields
X_{f}{A}
are also
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
-preserving for any
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, since by theorem
3.3
we have

0=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)=X{A}^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(f)=\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(X_{f}{A}).

The latter property is crucial in HMC to avoid the appearance of Jacobians in the Metropolis-Hastings step, which are expensive to compute.

This suggests the following high-level strategy to sample using the volume-free
P
-preserving diffusions such that it maintains many of the geometric features that are key to the success of Hamiltonian-based Monte Carlo algorithms.

(1)

Begin by considering the complete recipe of
P
-preserving diffusions

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\left(X^{P}{A}+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}.

In general, it is not possible to obtain a computationally tractable expression for the solution to this system, since the solution must possess some symmetry for it to be tractable, whereas the target measure typically do not possess such symmetries. This leads us to the next step.

(2)

Decompose the target
P
as
P\propto e^{-H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, where
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
is an appropriate reference measure, for which tractable
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
-preserving flows can be obtained.
Hence,
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
is usually an invariant measure, such as the Lebesgue or Haar measure, or a simple probability measure, such as a Gaussian measure. The complexity of the target
P
is then entirely contained within its density
e^{-H}
.
We thus have the expression

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\big(X_{H}{A}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}\big)\mathrm{d}t+\big(X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}{A}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\big)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

(3)

In order to reduce the complexity of the target density
e^{-H}
, we further split it into simpler components,
e^{-H}=\prod_{j}e^{-H_{j}}
.
This is one of the important benefits associated to the lifting procedure used in HMC and the underdamped Langevin process, wherein the target density is complex, but its lift typically decomposes nicely into a
potential

V(q)
and
kinetic

T(p)
term, both of which are simpler to handle in the lifted space since
V
is
p
-independent and
T
is
q
-independent.
Hence, when such simpler components do not exist, we must lift the process to some phase space, i.e., vector bundle, over
\mathscr{M}
where such decompositions exist.

(4)

Split the diffusion further into an
L^{2}(P)
-symmetric process

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\mathzapfc{S}}\equiv-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\sum_{j}Y_{i}(H_{j})Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

and an
L^{2}(P)
-antisymmetric, deterministic process

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\mathzapfc{A}}\equiv\Big(\sum_{j}X_{H_{j}}{A}+X^{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}{A}+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)\Big)\mathrm{d}t.

Hereafter, we will discard the topological obstruction term
P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
for simplicity, as they are not necessary for measure-preservation.

(5)

Restrict the reversible component to volume-free processes, so that we get

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}{S}=-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\sum_{j}X_{H_{j}}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\otimes\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})}\mathrm{d}t+\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

which ensures that the noise vector fields
Y_{i}=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})
are volume-preserving, similar to the Langevin system.
On
\mathscr{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, this can be implemented with an explicit integrator, as shown in
[
29
]
and on more general manifolds
\mathscr{M}
, the process can be lifted to a vector bundle over
\mathscr{M}
and choosing the noise-fields to be
vertical
, the process will evolve purely on the fibres (which are vector spaces), where we can integrate this explicitly.

(6)

For the irreversible component, setting
\mathzapfc{A}=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})
, the deterministic process simplifies to

\frac{\mathrm{d}Z_{t}^{\mathzapfc{A}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\equiv\sum_{j}X_{H_{j}}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})},

which can be implemented with a palindromic splitting integrator

[
130
,
77
]
, that approximates the
P
-preserving flow of
X_{H}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})}
with the composition of the flows of
X_{H_{j}}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})}
.
Importantly, as discussed above, the choice
\mathzapfc{A}=\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})
guarantees that the splitting integrator will be volume-preserving.
Essentially, the splitting method used to compute the irreversible process simplifies the implementation by decomposing the complicated target
e^{-H}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
into simpler targets
e^{-H_{j}}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, for which the corresponding flows are easier to construct, while still ensuring that the resulting numerical integrator is
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
-preserving.
Here, again, if
H
is too complex and does not have a sufficiently nice decomposition, it will be necessary to lift the process to an appropriate phase space where such decompositions exist.
In general, it is desirable to choose the potentials
\{\mathcal{V},(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\}
that share the symmetries of the reference measure, in order for the tensor fields

\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{V})
and
\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})
to inherit these symmetries.

Example 6.1

(Shadows)
.

Choosing
\mathzapfc{A}
to be a Poisson structure with invariant measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
(such as those discussed in
[
44
,
159
,
52
]
)
further ensures that
the splitting integrator used to integrate
the irreversible deterministic component
will have a modified energy, called the shadow Hamiltonian, as a result of the Jacobi identity.
In other words, the numerical integrator will itself be a
\mathzapfc{A}
-Hamiltonian vector field with respect to the shadow Hamiltonian, and this feature is important to the success of HMC
(see
[
105
,
94
,
154
,
92
,
142
,
30
]
).
When the Poisson structure is constructed on a vector bundle over
\mathscr{M}
, such as the cotangent bundle, and the noise fields are chosen to be vertical, we obtain a natural generalisation of
[
7
]
, where an irreversible HMC algorithm on compact Lie groups was obtained, following the SOL-HMC construction in
[
139
]
(see also §
9
).

In general, the Brownian motion
\mathrm{d}W_{t}
(resp. its Riemannian generalisation, discussed below) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(resp. on a Riemannian manifold) only preserves the Lebesgue measure
\mathbf{d}x
(resp. the Riemannian measure).
By choosing
H=0
and therefore
P=\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
, the diffusion (

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t},

which may be thought of as the general class of

-preserving Brownian motions
, wherein the Euclidean Brownian motion
\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}
is directed along

7.
Itô Diffusions with Riemannian Brownian Noise

We now consider noise processes that are driven by Riemannian Brownian motion, which have powerful ergodic properties.
We say that a diffusion process is
non-degenerate
when its generator is elliptic, and in this case, we can find a
Riemannian metric
\mathzapfc{M}
for which the generator takes the form

(27)

\mathcal{L}=X+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\Delta,\quad\text{ for some drift }\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\,,

where
{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\Delta
is the
Laplace-Beltrami operator
associated with the metric
\mathzapfc{M}
(see
[
90
,
6
]
).
The diffusion
B_{t}
generated by
{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\Delta
on
\mathscr{M}
is called the

Riemannian Brownian motion
(see
[
86
]
), and such processes are
used for instance in the construction of stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
To get a glimpse of how this noise process is related to the Stratonovich noise discussed earlier, note that when the dissipative bracket
\mathzapfc{S}\equiv Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})
is positive definite, it defines a Riemannian co-metric on
\mathscr{M}
.
Conversely, given a Riemannian metric
\mathzapfc{M}
on
\mathscr{M}
, there always exists a local expansion of the co-metric
\mathzapfc{M}^{-1}
in terms of a
finite set of vector fields
(Y_{i})
, as
\mathzapfc{M}^{-1}=Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i}
, since
\mathzapfc{M}^{-1}
is positive definite (this is analogous to taking the square-root of a positive definite matrix on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

The following theorem gives a full characterisation of
P
-preserving diffusions generated by (
27
) (see §
A.9
for the proof).

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
vol
,
\nabla
and
\nabla\cdot
be the Riemannian measure, gradient and divergence respectively.
Any
P\propto p_{\infty}\text{vol}
-preserving diffusion generated by (
27
) takes for some
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})
, the form (up to the usual topological obstruction term)

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}&=\underbrace{-X_{\log p_{\infty}}{A}(Z_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\nabla\cdot\mathzapfc{A}(Z_{t})\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{irreversible drift}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\overbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\nabla\log p_{\infty}(Z_{t})\mathrm{d}t}^{\text{Riemannian gradient flow}}+\overbrace{\mathrm{d}B_{t}}^{\text{Riemannian Brownian motion}}}_{\text{reversible Riemannian overdamped Langevin system}}.\end{split}

We defer the discussion on the ergodicity of (
28
) on paracompact manifolds in §
10
.
In the reversible case
\mathzapfc{A}=0
,
the diffusion (
28
) gives us precisely the
Riemannian overdamped Langevin equation
, used to construct the Riemann Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm (MALA) when the Riemannian metric is obtained from an information divergence (see
[
68
,
117
]
).
On the other hand, the case
\mathzapfc{A}\neq 0
is also of interest to us as it is well-known that the existence of an irreversible component can accelerate convergence to the target distribution, as demonstrated in
[
88
,
53
,
145
]
(a detailed analysis of the optimal drift for constant
\mathzapfc{A}
is provided in
[
109
]
).
Alternatively, reversible overdamped Langevin systems (
\mathzapfc{A}=0
) with appropriate choices of Riemannian metric
\mathzapfc{M}
can also lead to accelerated convergence relative to the overdamped Langevin process (
23
), as shown in
[
1
]
.

Remark 7.2

(Reference Measures)
.

We point out that the Riemannian measure associated to the metric of the generator is sometimes not an appropriate choice of reference measure.
This has in fact caused considerable confusion in the statistical literature when a target on Euclidean space is expressed in terms of the Riemannian measure instead of the Lebesgue measure

[
34
,
115
,
161
]
.
In that case, denoting by
p_{\mathscr{M}}
the density of
P
with respect to an appropriate reference measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
and
p_{\infty}
the density associated with the Riemannian measure
vol
, we can simply use the relation
\log p_{\infty}=\log p_{\mathscr{M}}-\log\frac{\mathbf{d}\text{vol}}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}
to convert (
28
) into a corresponding expression based on the measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
.
For example on Euclidean space, it is well-known that the Riemannian Brownian motion
B_{t}
can be expressed as

\mathrm{d}B_{t}=\sqrt{\mathzapfc{M}^{-1}}\mathrm{d}W_{t}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\Gamma{M}\,\mathrm{d}t
, where
\Gamma{M}^{i}\equiv\mathzapfc{M}^{jk}\Gamma^{i}_{jk}=\mathzapfc{M}^{ir}\partial_{r}\log\sqrt{\mathzapfc{M}}-\partial_{r}\mathzapfc{M}^{ir}
and
W_{t}
is the standard Euclidean Brownian motion.
Further, if
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\mathbf{d}x
, then
\mathbf{d}\text{vol}/\mathbf{d}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\sqrt{\mathzapfc{M}}
and together with the explicit expression for
\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, we can use this to express (
28
) in terms of the local Lebesgue density, as in
[
161
]
. One should note however that
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\mathbf{d}x
is not a meaningful measure on general manifolds and therefore this expression only makes sense on Euclidean space.

In local charts, one can also recover the Riemannian overdamped Langevin system directly from our
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusion (
22
) as we show below.
First, let
(Y_{i})
be a family of vector fields on
\mathscr{M}
such that
\mathzapfc{S}\equiv Y_{i}\otimes Y_{i}
is a positive definite tensor field. We then set
\mathzapfc{M}\equiv\mathzapfc{S}^{-1}
, which defines a Riemannian metric tensor.
Now taking the reference measure
\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
to be the Riemannian measure
vol
(locally,
\text{vol}=\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}\,\mathbf{d}x
where
|\mathzapfc{M}|
denotes the local determinant of
\mathzapfc{M}
), the
\mathzapfc{A}
-diffusion (
22
) with
\mathzapfc{A}=0
becomes

(29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-\frac{\beta}{2}Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\text{vol}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}.

For the first term, we have
Y_{i}(H)Y_{i}=X^{\mathzapfc{M}^{-1}}_{H}=\nabla H
, so it is the Riemannian gradient of
H
. In local coordinates, the second term reads

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\text{vol}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\left(\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}Y_{i}^{j}\right)Y_{i}^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\left(\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}Y_{i}^{j}Y_{i}^{k}\right)-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}^{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}Y_{i}^{k},

and the random noise terms are related by the Stratonovich-to-Itô correction

\displaystyle\underbrace{Y_{i}^{k}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}}_{\text{Stratonovich noise}}=\underbrace{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}^{j}\frac{\partial Y_{i}^{k}}{\partial x^{j}}\,\mathrm{d}t}_{\text{Stratonovich-to-It\^{o} correction}}+\underbrace{Y_{i}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}}_{\text{It\^{o} noise}}.

Hence putting this together, we have

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\text{vol}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}^{k}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\left(\sqrt{|\mathzapfc{M}|}Y_{i}^{j}Y_{i}^{k}\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i},

which is precisely the local expression for the Riemannian Brownian motion
\mathrm{d}B_{t}

[
85
]
. Thus, in local charts, (
29
) becomes

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-\frac{\beta}{2}\nabla H\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}B_{t},

which is exactly the Riemannian overdamped Langevin system.

Remark 7.3

(Riemannian Brownian Motion from the Orthonormal Frame Bundle)
.

We show here that the Riemannian Brownian motion can also be obtained globally as a projection of a Stratonovich diffusion defined on the orthonormal frame bundle.
Specifically, if we define the canonical horizontal vector field
L_{i}
on the orthonormal frame bundle
\pi_{O}:O(\mathscr{M})\to\mathscr{M}
, then the diffusion
\mathrm{d}O_{t}=L_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}
reduces to the Riemannian Brownian motion
[
90
, theorem 4.2]
.
Moreover
L_{i}(\pi_{O}^{*}H)L_{i}
is
\pi_{O}
-related to
\nabla H
, as follows from equations (4.12) and (4.22) in
[
90
]
. Indeed, locally
L_{i}=e^{r}_{i}\partial_{q^{r}}-\Gamma^{\ell}_{sa}e^{s}_{i}e^{a}_{b}\partial_{e^{\ell}_{b}}
where
(e_{s})_{s}
is an orthonormal frame,
so
L_{i}(\pi_{O}^{*}H)L_{i}=e^{r}_{i}\partial_{q^{r}}(H)e^{s}_{i}\partial_{q^{s}}=\mathzapfc{M}^{rs}\partial_{q^{r}}(H)\partial_{q^{s}}
.
Hence the Riemannian overdamped Langevin process

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=-\frac{\beta}{2}\nabla H\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}B_{t},

is the projection under
\pi_{O}
of the Stratonovich diffusions

\mathrm{d}Q_{t}=-\frac{\beta}{2}L_{i}(\pi^{*}_{O}H)L_{i}\mathrm{d}t+L_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}^{i}
.

8.
Deterministic Flow of Measure-preserving Diffusions on the Space of Volume Measures

In this section, we describe the rate of change of functionals along the diffusion process.
The rate of change of a curve of volume measures
\mu_{t}
along a diffusion process
Z_{t}
is given by the forward Kolmogorov equation,

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}^{*}\mu_{t}
.
In particular, if
Z_{t}
is an arbitrary
P
-preserving diffusion, combining (
13
) and theorem
3.7
, we find that the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\text{div}_{\mu_{t}}\left(-\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})-P^{\sharp}(\gamma)+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}(\text{div}_{\mu_{t}}(Y_{i})-\text{div}_{P}(Y_{i}))Y_{i}\right)\mu_{t}

describes the evolution over the space of smooth measures of
\mu_{t}
towards the stationary distribution
P
.
Decomposing at each
t
the target
P
with respect to the reference measure
\mu_{t}
, as in (
11
), and using the fact
\text{curl}\circ\text{curl}=0
we can simplify this expression to

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\text{div}_{\mu_{t}}\left(X{A}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}\left(\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}\right)Y_{i}\right)\mu_{t}-\mathbf{d}\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P}(\mu^{\sharp}_{t}(\gamma))\mu_{t}.

Let us for the moment ignore the topological obstruction for simplicity. Observe that

\mu_{t}
satisfies the continuity equation

\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}+\text{div}_{\mu_{t}}\left({\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{\mathzapfc{S}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}-X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}\right)\mu_{t}=0.

Hence, the rate of change of KL divergence along the curve
\mu_{t}
is

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|P)=-\int\text{div}_{P}\left({\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{\mathzapfc{S}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}-X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}\right)\mu_{t},

and if Stokes’ theorem hold
3
3

3

that is
\int\mathbf{d}i_{{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{\mathzapfc{S}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}-X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}}\mu_{t}=0
, we can further write (recall the notation (
18
))

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|P)=-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\int\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\mathzapfc{S}}\,\mu_{t}.

Since
\mathzapfc{S}
is a dissipative bracket and
\mu_{t}
is a smooth positive measure, this integral is non-negative for all
t
, and it follows that

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}\mathrm{KL}(\mu_{t}\|P)\leq 0,

in concordance with the Euclidean case, see for example
[
120
]
.

More generally, consider a functional
F
on the space of smooth measures.
Important families of such functionals include the linear functionals

F_{f}(Q)\equiv\left<f,Q\right>_{*}=\int fQ,

for some
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, and the functionals

F^{P}_{h}(Q)\equiv\int h\left(\frac{\mathbf{d}Q}{\mathbf{d}P}\right)Q,

parametrised by a choice of function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
which include the KL divergence and other functionals that arise in a wide range of applications
[
96
,
119
,
158
,
65
]
.
The functional derivatives
\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q}
with
F=F_{f}
and
F_{h}^{P}
are
f
and
h\left(\frac{\mathbf{d}Q}{\mathbf{d}P}\right)
respectively.
For any bracket
\mathzapfc{B}
, we define the
integral bracket
on the space of measures by

\{f,h\}_{\int{B}}(Q)\equiv\int\{f,h\}{B}\,Q,

provided the integral converges (for example,
f
or
h
is compactly supported).
The following proposition shows that the integral thermodynamic bracket associated to the diffusion characterises the rate of change of
F
along the process (see §
A.10
for the proof).
It can be used to optimize the brackets in the measure-preserving diffusion for the given task,
for example to improve the decay of a statistical divergence (see e.g.
[
135
]
) along the process to speed-up convergence to equilibrium.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
F
be a functional on the space of volume measures, and suppose
\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
(or more generally that Stokes’ theorem holds).
The rate of change of
F
along the
P
-preserving diffusion
is then given by

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\int_{\mathcal{T}}}(\mu_{t})+\left<\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P},\mu^{\sharp}_{t}(\gamma)\left[\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right]\right>_{\mu_{t}}

where
\mathcal{T}
is the thermodynamic bracket

\mathzapfc{S}/\sqrt{2}-\mathzapfc{A}
, and
\gamma
the topological obstruction.

9.
Underdamped Langevin Diffusions on Manifolds

As discussed in §
6
, an important feature common to both the underdamped and overdamped Langevin processes is the fact that they are both measure-preserving despite having no explicit reference measure contribution.
Therein we have derived the complete characterisation of these Langevin-like measure-preserving systems.

In this section, we will introduce another perspective regarding the underdamped Langevin process (
24

Since the underdamped Langevin system evolves on a vector bundle

and we want the noise process to live entirely in the vertical direction, we can ask what are the measures
\mu_{\mathscr{F}}
on
\mathscr{F}
for which all vertical vector fields are
\mu_{\mathscr{F}}

This motivates the notion of a

defined as a pair

such that

•

•

is

The
\mathzapfc{A}

As we will see below, when

An important example of a Langevin pair and target

10.

Ergodicity plays an important role in many of the applications in which measure-preserving diffusions are employed, so here we discuss the conditions that the drift and diffusion vector fields

First, let us denote by

In general, it is well-known that if the diffusion satifies:

(i)

, that is if the Markov semigroup

associated with the process

7

7

The Markov semigroup is defined as

maps all bounded measurable functions into continuous functions, and

(ii)

By construction,

-diffusions have a (strictly) positive invariant measure, and thus must be irreducible.
On the other hand, a sufficient condition to ensure that the strong Feller property

In particular, on compact manifolds

is entirely made of rotationnels:

(39)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}Z_{t}&=\left(X_{H}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathcal{V})}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta X_{H}^{\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})\otimes\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathzapfc{A}_{i})}\right)\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}\\
&=-\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(i_{\mathbf{d}H}\mathcal{V})+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\beta\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(i_{\mathbf{d}H}\mathcal{\mathzapfc{A}}_{i})\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathcal{\mathzapfc{A}}_{i})+\text{curl}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(\mathcal{\mathzapfc{A}}_{i})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}.\end{split}

This insight may be useful in tackling the ergodicity problem in the non-elliptic case, especially when combined with the fact
\text{curl}_{P}
has natural properties with respect to Lie brackets since it is a derivation of the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket, a multi-vector generalisation of the Lie bracket
[
98
]
.

We should also note that the dynamical ergodicity of the underlying stochastic process is not sufficient for building algorithms that are robust enough for practical use – it only provides an asymptotic guarantee for the behaviour of empirical averages of exact realizations of the stochastic process. The condition says nothing about the non-asymptotic behaviour of exact realizations nor any behaviour of the numerical discretizations to which we are limited to in practice.
For example, MALA is obtained by applying an explicit Euler-Maruyama scheme to the overdamped Langevin process (
23
), and composing it with an accept-reject step. However, it is well-known that the algorithm does not maintain the ergodicity properties of the continuous process that it is derived from (see
[
147
]
for example).

To guarantee that the algorithm will be useful in practice, we need to bound the convergence of the discretized stochastic process towards its asymptotic limit, if one exists. Recent progress has been made in understanding the converge of both exact and discretized Langevin diffusions in Wasserstein distances
[
54
,
39
,
143
,
56
]
, although these results are limited to sufficiently nice target distributions that limit their practical utility.

Classic statistical results do not consider the convergence of diffusions themselves, but rather the Metropolis-Hastings transitions that use the discretized diffusions as a proposal distribution. Using coupling techniques, they demonstrate when the convergence admits geometric bounds in the total variation distance. Although these bounds are not particularly tight, they ensure the existence of central limit theorems which then allow for the convergence to be estimated well empirically.

When moving beyond diffusions to more general second-order Markov processes the problem becomes even harder. The limited theoretical results
[
55
,
118
]
focus largely on necessary conditions for geometric bounds in the total variation distance and hence the existence of central limit theorems. Although these conditions are not sufficient to guarantee any particular non-asymptotic behavior, they motivate empirical diagnostics that help practitioners identify target distributions beyond the scope of the algorithm.

11.
A Brief History of Measure-Preserving Diffusions

The history of measure-preserving processes is a long one, and in this section, we only aim to provide a handful of previous works that are directly related to this one.
While in the machine learning community, the characterisation of measure-preserving diffusions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
was popularised in the recent NeurIPS article

[
121
]
,
anterior closely related results can be found in the SDE literature.
For example in 1977, Robert Graham discusses the covariance of the Fokker–Planck equation, in the context of non-degenerate diffusions, and uses the Riemannian metric associated to the noise to define a Riemannian divergence which allows him to differentiate second-order tensors
[
73
]
(a nice discussion of the work of Graham is also provided in
[
60
]
).
By analogy with Maxwell’s equations, Graham notes that the Fokker–Planck current must be the Riemannian divergence of some anti-symmetric tensor field, which is precisely the result provided in
[
121
, Theorem 2]
.
The covariance of the Fokker–Planck equation and the diffusion process is also discussed in
[
19
]
and
[
128
]
, where the latter article derives conditions
for the diffusion to be reversible (see also
[
134
]
).
A less intuitive characterisation of the Fokker–Planck current of measure-preserving diffusions is also given in
[
87
]
and in the case of non-degenerate diffusions on compact oriented manifolds, the book
[
90
]
effectively derives a complete recipe using the Riemannian metric derived from the noise to transform the Fokker–Planck vector field into a 1-form, that is then studied via its Hodge–de Rham decomposition.

A major shortcoming of these references is that they all assume the noise to be non-degenerate, in order to equip the manifold with a Riemannian metric, as well as the orientability of the manifold to work with differential forms.
Yet, many important measure-preserving diffusions are degenerate, such as (the deterministic) Hamiltonian systems, or even the underdamped Langevin process.
While on Euclidean space, we have a “natural” metric that we can use to differentiate second-order tensors, such metrics do not exist on general manifolds.
Concurrently to this article, a covariant formulation was introduced in
[
48
]
to remove the dependence on the metric.
However contrary to our recipe, this work relies on local coordinates, and does not take into account the presence of topological obstruction, thus leading to a recipe which is only valid locally, since on manifolds, there are divergence-free vector fields that cannot be globally expressed as the
P
-rotationnel of a bi-vector field.
Moreover, the relation with the canonical geometry of
P
is not shown,
and as we have illustrated in §
6
, our geometric framework offers new insights even on Euclidean space.
More importantly, the intrinsic geometry of
P
allows us to re-contextualise the theory of measure-preserving diffusion within the realm of differential geometry.

In the bigger picture, we see that these results are a combination of two things:
the construction of the Fokker–Planck equation
[
62
,
141
,
97
,
146
]
and
its expression in geometric form as discussed above (see also
[
137
]
),
combined with geometric characterisations of divergence-free vector field.
Such characterisations have already been studied in several works on geometric integrators
[
130
,
77
]
and have been known for at least a century, as seen in the work
[
45
]
.
For example, the fact that vector fields that preserve the Lebesgue measure can be written as the divergence of an antisymmetric matrix (without any integrability assumption), which is all that is needed to obtain
[
121
, Theorem. 2]
,
goes back at least to the works of Poincaré and Volterra in the 1880s, e.g.

[
157
]
.

12.
Conclusion

In this work, building on from results in Poisson mechanics, geometry, topology, physics, and statistics, we have
presented the complete and canonical characterisation of measure-preserving diffusions on arbitrary manifolds,
which play a central role in many areas of science, both in terms of mathematical modelling and in statistics/machine learning. Our general framework provides a sound mathematical basis to design and study them.
It not only extends and contextualise the results obtained in
[
121
]
for the Euclidean case, and improves it by removing the integrability constraint,
but more importantly provides an elegant interpretation from a purely topological standpoint, relying solely on the geometry of the volume manifold
(\mathscr{M},P)
.
This is achieved by constructing
potentials
for the Fokker-Planck current in the same way as how physical ‘potentials’ such as the potential energy and the magnetic potentials are constructed in classical mechanics. On contractible sample spaces, the resulting diffusion is specified, just as with thermodynamic systems, by two ‘brackets’: an antisymmetric
\mathzapfc{A}
that presents itself as the ‘potential’ for the Fokker-Planck current, and a dissipative one
\mathzapfc{S}
, that is generated by the noise vector fields
(Y_{i})
.
Moreover, when the topology of the manifold is non-trivial (e.g., it is not connected), we also need to take into account an extra topological obstruction term to achieve global completeness, which we have shown to be parametrised by harmonic forms on compact orientable manifolds, and non-zero elements of a twisted de Rham cohomology group in general.

In addition to fully characterising the measure-preserving diffusions,
we have also studied their reversibility, associated flows on the space of volume measures,
the generalisations of Langevin processes to manifolds,
and introduced a new recipe for volume-free diffusions that are well-suited to the construction of Langevin-like sampling algorithms.
Our canonical formulation properly takes into account the critical assumptions on the target measure, namely, that it is smooth and globally supported,
which allows us to analyse the diffusions through measure-informed versions of known results in differential geometry (obtained using isomorphisms induced by the target).
From a practical point of view, having intrinsic results that focus on the target measure and do not make any extra assumptions imply that these can be applied regardless of the particular application, such as physics and machine learning.
In future works, we will further address how to develop efficient MCMC algorithms to sample from manifolds using this complete recipe, and furthermore, we aim to extend this framework in the context of infinite-dimensional diffusions, as considered in
[
22
,
100
,
139
]
, which may be useful for applications in stochastic climate modelling and data assimilation
[
31
,
64
,
99
,
122
,
123
,
124
]
.

A.1.
Derivation of Fokker–Planck operator

Lemma A.1
.

Given smooth vector fields
X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}
on
\mathscr{M}
, consider the Stratonovich SDE

(40)

\mathrm{d}Z_{t}=X(Z_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+Y_{i}(Z_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}W^{i}_{t}\,.

Then its generator is given by

\mathcal{L}f=Xf+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}Y_{i}f
.
The Fokker–Planck operator, viewed as the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
in
L^{2}(\mu_{\mathscr{M}})
(if the boundary
\partial\mathscr{M}
is non-empty we restrict to functions that vanish on the boundary) is given by

(41)

\mathcal{L}^{*}f=\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}\big(-fX+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(fY_{i})Y_{i}\big)=\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}\left(-fX+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}Y_{i}(f)Y_{i}+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}f\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\right)\,.

Proof.

Vector fields satisfy Leibniz rule

\displaystyle\int gX(f)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int X(gf)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}-\int fX(g)\mu_{\mathscr{M}},

and note that
\int_{\mathscr{M}}\text{div}(gX)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int_{\mathscr{M}}\mathrm{d}i_{gX}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int_{\partial\mathscr{M}}i_{gX}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int_{\partial\mathscr{M}}gi_{X}\mu_{\mathscr{M}}
which vanishes if
g|_{\partial M}=0
(here
\text{div}\equiv\text{div}_{\mu_{\mathscr{M}}}
).
Hence

\displaystyle\int gX(f)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle=-\int gf\text{div}(X)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}-\int fX(g)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle=\int f\big(-g\text{div}(X)-X(g)\big)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle=\int f\big(-\text{div}(gX)\big)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int f\mathcal{L}^{*}g\mu_{\mathscr{M}}.

Now let us compute the adjoint of the diffusion component.
First note that

\displaystyle Y_{k}(gY_{k}f)

\displaystyle=\text{div}\big(gY_{k}fY_{k}\big)-gY_{k}f\text{div}(Y_{k})=

\displaystyle=\text{div}\big(gY_{k}fY_{k}\big)-\text{div}\big(fg\text{div}(Y_{k})Y_{k}\big)+f\text{div}\big(g\text{div}(Y_{k})Y_{k}\big)

and

\displaystyle Y_{i}gY_{i}f=\text{div}(fY_{i}gY_{i})-f\text{div}(Y_{i}gY_{i}).

It follows that if
f,g
vanish on the boundary

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\int gY_{i}Y_{i}f\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle={\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\int\Big(Y_{i}\big(gY_{i}f\big)-Y_{i}gY_{i}f\Big)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle={\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\int\Big(f\text{div}\big(g\text{div}(Y_{i})Y_{i}\big)+f\text{div}(Y_{i}gY_{i})\Big)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}

\displaystyle=\int f{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\text{div}\big(\text{div}(gY_{i})Y_{i}\big)\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\int f\mathcal{L}^{*}g\mu_{\mathscr{M}}.

∎

Corollary A.3
.

\mu_{\mathscr{M}}=\mathrm{d}x

\sigma_{ij}\equiv Y^{i}_{j}

A.4.
Derivation that Curl is Modular Field

This result was proved in the thesis of one of the authors
[
14
]
, but the proof relies on introducing Schouten–Nijenhuis brackets.
Thus we here include a new direct and more constructive proof:

Proof.

We will prove the equivalence by demonstrating that the action of the modular vector field and the curl vector field are equal for all smooth functions
f
,

\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)\cdot P=X^{P}_{\mathzapfc{A}}(f)\cdot P.

The curl vector field is defined implicitly by the action

i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}P=\mathbf{d}\left(P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)

which implies that for any
f

(44)

\mathbf{d}f\wedge i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}P=\mathbf{d}f\wedge\mathbf{d}\left(P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right).

To simplify the left hand side note that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}\left(\mathbf{d}f\wedge P\right)

\displaystyle=i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}(\mathbf{d}f)\cdot P-\mathbf{d}f\wedge i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}P

\displaystyle=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)\cdot P-\mathbf{d}f\wedge i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}P,

or

\mathbf{d}f\wedge i_{\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})}P=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)\cdot P.

For the right hand side we take

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}\left(\mathbf{d}f\wedge(P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A}))\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{d}^{2}f\wedge P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})-\mathbf{d}f\wedge\mathbf{d}\left(P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{d}f\wedge\mathbf{d}\left(P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right).

Substituting both results in Equation (
44
) then gives

(45)

\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)\cdot P=-\mathbf{d}\left(\mathbf{d}f\wedge P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right).

Now we use the fact that for any function

P^{\sharp}\left(\mathbf{d}f\wedge P^{\flat}(\mathzapfc{A})\right)=i_{\mathbf{d}f}\mathzapfc{A}

[
52
]
,
so that equation (
45
) becomes

\displaystyle\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})(f)\cdot P=-\mathbf{d}P^{\flat}\left(i_{\mathbf{d}f}\mathzapfc{A}\right)=\mathbf{d}P^{\flat}\left(X_{f}{A}\right)=\text{div}_{P}(X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{f})\cdot P

as desired.
∎

A.5.
Derivation of Adjoint of Integration Pairing

Lemma A.4
.

The Fokker–Planck operator of the Stratonovich SDE (

A.10.
Rate of Change of Functionals along Measure-preserving Diffusions

Proposition A.10
.

Let
F
be a functional on the space of volume measures, and suppose
\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
(or that more generally Stokes’ theorem holds).
The rate of change of
F
along the
P
-preserving diffusion
is given by

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\int_{\mathcal{T}}}(\mu_{t})+\left<\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P},\mu^{\sharp}_{t}(\gamma)\left[\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right]\right>_{\mu_{t}}

where
\mathcal{T}
is the thermodynamic bracket

\mathzapfc{S}/\sqrt{2}-\mathzapfc{A}
, and
\gamma
the topological obstruction.

Proof.

Differentiating we have

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\frac{\partial\mu_{t}}{\partial t}=\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\text{div}_{\mu_{t}}\left(X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{\mathzapfc{S}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}\right)\mu_{t}.

If
\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}
is compactly supported, or more generally provided Stokes theorem holds,
we have

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=-\int\left(X^{\mathzapfc{A}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right)-{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}X^{\mathzapfc{S}}_{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}}\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right)\right)\mu_{t}.

Hence

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=-\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\int_{\mathzapfc{A}}}(\mu_{t})+{\mathchoice{\textstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptstyle{1\over 2}}{\scriptscriptstyle{1\over 2}}{1/2}}\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\int{S}}(\mu_{t}),

which can be expressed as

\frac{\mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{d}t}F(\mu_{t})=\left\{\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right\}_{\int_{\mathcal{T}}}(\mu_{t})

via the thermodynamic bracket
\mathcal{T}=\mathzapfc{S}/\sqrt{2}-\mathzapfc{A}
of the diffusion.

For the topological obstruction contribution,
note that since
\mu_{t}^{\sharp}(\gamma)
is
\mu_{t}
preserving,

-\int\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\mu_{t}^{\sharp}(\gamma)\left(\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P}\right)\mu_{t}=\int\mu_{t}^{\sharp}(\gamma)\left(\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu_{t}}\right)\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P}\mu_{t}.

Note that when

we have

\frac{\delta\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\|P)}{\delta\mu_{t}}=\log\frac{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}}{\mathbf{d}P}=-\log\frac{\mathbf{d}P}{\mathbf{d}\mu_{t}},

so we recover the formula for the rate of change of KL.
∎

using any trivialisation of the orientation line bundle induced by a trivialisation of
\mathscr{M}
, as explained in
section 7
[
27
]
(on orientable manifolds this reduces do the standard de Rham complex).
Using this (extended) exterior derivative, we define the
P
-rotationnel as

\text{curl}_{P}=P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat},

which satisfies
\text{curl}_{P}\circ\text{curl}_{P}=0
since
\mathbf{d}\circ\mathbf{d}=0
.
In particular when applied to vector fields the
P
-rotationnel acts as the divergence operator
\text{curl}_{P}=\text{div}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\to C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})
, which follows from

\mathcal{L}_{X}P=\mathbf{d}i_{X}P=P^{\flat}\text{curl}_{P}(X)=\text{curl}_{P}(X)P,

together with the definition of
\text{div}_{P}(X)
as the function satisfying
\text{div}_{P}(X)P=\mathcal{L}_{X}P
.
If
f
is a function, observe that
(fP)^{\flat}=fP^{\flat}
, and so if
f
is non-vanishing,
(fP)^{\sharp}=P^{\sharp}\circ\frac{1}{f}
.
Hence

(51)

\text{curl}_{fP}=P^{\sharp}\circ\frac{1}{f}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ f\circ P^{\flat}\equiv P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}_{f}\circ P^{\flat},\qquad\text{or}\qquad\text{curl}_{Q}=P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}_{\frac{\mathbf{d}Q}{\mathbf{d}P}}\circ P^{\flat},

where
\mathbf{d}_{f}\equiv\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{d}\log|f|\wedge
is the distorted de Rham derivative. In particular, the
P
-rotationnel does not depend on the normalisation constant of
P
, an important requirement in many statistical applications, where the target distribution or statistical model is only known up to normalisation.
Importantly, we have the following key result showing the homology groups defined by the boundary operator
\text{curl}_{P}
(since
\text{curl}_{P}\circ\text{curl}_{P}=0
) are isomorphic to the twisted de Rham cohomology groups. As usual we denote by
[\cdot]
the equivalence classes.

Theorem B.1
.

The isomorphism
P^{\flat}
descends to an isomorphism between the homology groups
\mathcal{H}_{P}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})
of
\text{curl}_{P}
and the twisted de Rham cohomology groups
\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
. Hence

\text{Dyn}(P)\cong\text{curl}_{P}\left(\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})\right)\oplus P^{\sharp}\left(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{dR}\left(\mathscr{M}\right)\right),

and any
P
-preserving dynamics will be globally the
P
-rotationnel of some
\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})
iff the
(n-1)
de twisted Rham cohomology is trivial.
Moreover, if
U\subset\mathscr{M}
is an open subset, then
\text{curl}_{P}|_{U}=\text{curl}_{P|_{U}}
.
Hence the set of
P
-preserving dynamics is precisely the set of locally curl vector fields

\text{Dyn}(P)=\text{Curl}_{loc}(P)

where
\text{Curl}_{loc}(P)=\{X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M}):\forall q,\text{there is a neighbourhood }U\text{ and }\mathzapfc{A}\in\mathfrak{X}^{2}(U){s.t.,}X=\text{curl}_{P|_{U}}(\mathzapfc{A})\}
.

Proof.

The
P
-derivative

\text{curl}_{P}\equiv P^{\sharp}\circ\mathrm{d}\circ P^{\flat}:\mathfrak{X}^{k}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{k-1}(\mathscr{M})
is a vector space homomorphism, satisfying
\text{curl}_{P}\circ\text{curl}_{P}=0
, that it is a boundary operator on the chain complex of
k
-multi-vector fields, and thus

\text{Im}\left(\text{curl}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}^{\ell+1}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\right)
is a linear subspace of

\ker\left(\text{curl}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{\ell-1}(\mathscr{M})\right)
.
We can then define the
\ell^{th}
Holomology group

\mathcal{H}_{P}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\equiv\frac{\ker\left(\text{curl}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{\ell-1}(\mathscr{M})\right)}{\text{Im}\left(\text{curl}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}^{\ell+1}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\right)},

and in particular the first one provides information on
P
-preserving vector fields
(using
\text{curl}_{P}(X)=\text{div}_{P}(X)
)

\mathcal{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathscr{M})\equiv\frac{\ker\left(\text{div}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\to C^{\infty}(\mathscr{M})\right)}{\text{Im}\left(\text{curl}_{P}:\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathfrak{X}(\mathscr{M})\right)}.

Notice that the map
[\cdot]\circ P^{\flat}:\mathfrak{X}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
descends to a map
\mathcal{H}_{P}^{\ell}(\mathscr{M})\to\mathcal{H}^{n-\ell}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
, since

[\cdot]\circ P^{\flat}(X+\text{curl}_{P}(Y))=[P^{\flat}(X)+\mathbf{d}i_{Y}P]=[P^{\flat}(X)]
.
The map is surjective since
P^{\flat}
is, and injective since

[P^{\flat}(X)]=[P^{\flat}(Y)]

\implies

[P^{\flat}(X-Y)]=0\implies P^{\flat}(X-Y)=\mathbf{d}\alpha\implies X-Y=\text{curl}_{P}(Z)\implies[X]=[Y]
.
In particular,
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{dR}(\mathscr{M})
is trivial iff
\mathcal{H}_{P}^{1}(\mathscr{M})
is, in which case every divergence-free vector field is the
\text{curl}_{P}
of a bi-vector field
\mathzapfc{A}
.

In general, we can still use Poincaré lemma (or Volterra theorem, as it was proved by Vito Volterra
[
157
]
) and the properties of
\text{curl}_{P}
to show that any
P
-preserving vector field is locally a curl vector field.
Denoting the inclusion by
\iota_{U}:U\hookrightarrow\mathscr{M}
, we have

\iota_{U}^{*}(P^{\flat}(Y))=\iota_{U}^{*}(i_{Y}P)=i_{Y_{U}}\iota_{U}^{*}P=i_{Y_{U}}P_{U}=P_{U}^{\flat}(Y_{U})
, where
P_{U},Y_{U}
denote their restriction to
U
, and we have used proposition 7.4.10
[
2
]
;
hence
\iota_{U}^{*}\circ P^{\flat}=P_{U}^{\flat}\circ|_{U}
.
Setting
Y=P^{\sharp}(\alpha)

this yields

P^{\sharp}(\alpha)|_{U}=P_{U}^{\sharp}(\iota^{*}_{U}\alpha)
for any twisted form
\alpha
.
Hence

|_{U}\circ\text{curl}_{P}\equiv|_{U}\circ P^{\sharp}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}=P^{\sharp}_{U}\circ\iota_{U}^{*}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}=P^{\sharp}_{U}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ\iota_{U}^{*}\circ P^{\flat}=P^{\sharp}_{U}\circ\mathbf{d}\circ P^{\flat}_{U}=\text{curl}_{P_{U}}\circ|_{U}.

Thus,
\text{curl}_{P}(Y)=0

iff
P^{\sharp}\circ\mathrm{d}\circ P^{\flat}(Y)=0
iff
\mathrm{d}\circ P^{\flat}(Y)=0
(since
P^{\sharp}
is a linear isomorphism).
By Poincaré Lemma, this holds iff around any point there is an open neighbourhood
U
over which
P^{\flat}(Y)|_{U}=\mathrm{d}\alpha
for some twisted (
\dim(\mathscr{M})-2)
-form
\alpha
on
U
.
Then

\mathrm{d}\alpha=\iota_{U}^{*}(P^{\flat}(Y))=P_{U}^{\flat}(Y_{U})
,
and
P_{U}^{\flat}(Y|_{U})=\mathrm{d}\alpha
iff

Y_{U}=P^{\sharp}_{U}\mathrm{d}\alpha=\text{curl}_{P_{U}}(\mathzapfc{A})
where
\mathzapfc{A}\equiv P^{\sharp}_{U}(\alpha)
is a 2-vector field on
U
.

Note that by Poincaré duality if
\mathscr{M}
has a finite good cover we may alternatively work with the first compactly supported de Rham cohomology group.
The de Rham cohomology groups may be very large, though they must be finite dimensional when
\mathscr{M}
is compact.
Moreover, in that case, they are isomorphic to the vector spaces of harmonic forms, and the following results follows:

Corollary B.2
.

If
\mathscr{M}
is compact and orientable, then

\text{Dyn}(P)\cong\text{curl}_{P}\mathfrak{X}^{2}(\mathscr{M})\oplus P^{\sharp}\left(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\mathscr{M}\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
is the space of harmonic
n-1
-forms associated to an arbitrary Riemannian metric. In other words, any
P
-preserving vector field on a compact orientable manifold has the form

X=\text{curl}_{P}(\mathzapfc{A})+P^{\sharp}(\gamma)
.
