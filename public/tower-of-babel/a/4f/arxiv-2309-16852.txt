Spreading in graphs
Boštjan Brešar, Tanja Dravec, Aysel Erey, Jaka Hedžet

Abstract
Several concepts that model processes of spreading (of information, disease, objects, etc.) in graphs or networks have been studied. In many contexts, we assume that some vertices of a graph G G are contaminated initially, before the process starts. By the q q -forcing rule, a contaminated vertex having at most q q uncontaminated neighbors enforces all the neighbors to become contaminated, while by the p p -percolation rule, an uncontaminated vertex becomes contaminated if at least p p of its neighbors are contaminated. If given a set S S of initially contaminated vertices all vertices eventually become contaminated when continuously applying the q q -forcing rule (respectively the p p -percolation rule), S S is called a q q -forcing set (respectively, a p p -percolating set) in G G . In this paper, we consider sets S S that are at the same time q q -forcing sets and p p -percolating sets, and call them ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading sets. Given positive integers p p and q q , the minimum cardinality of a ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading set in G G is a ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading number, σ ( p , q ) ​ ( G ) \sigma_{(p,q)}(G) , of G G . While q q -forcing sets have been studied in a dozen of papers, the decision version of the corresponding graph invariant has not been considered earlier, and we fill the gap by proving its NP-completeness. This, in turn, enables us to prove the NP-completeness of the decision version of the ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading number in graphs for an arbitrary choice of p p and q q . Again, for every p ∈ ℕ p\in\mathbb{N} and q ∈ ℕ ∪ { ∞ } q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\} , we find a linear-time algorithm for determining the ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading number of a tree, where in the case p ≥ 2 p\geq 2 we apply Riedl’s algorithm from [Largest and smallest minimal percolating sets in trees, Electron. J. Combin. 19 (2012) Paper 64] on p p -percolation in trees. In addition, we present a lower and an upper bound on the ( p , q ) (p,q) -spreading number of a tree and characterize extremal families of trees. In the case of square grids, we combine some results of Bollobás from [The Art of Mathematics: Coffee Time in Memphis. Cambridge Univ. Press, New York, 2006], and the AIM Minimum Rank-Special Graphs Work Group from [Zero forcing sets and the minimum rank of graphs, Linear algebra Appl. 428 2008 1628–1648], and new results on ( 2 , 1 ) (2,1) -spreading and ( 4 , q ) (4,q) -spreading to obtain σ ( p , q ) ​ ( P m ​ □ ​ P n ) \sigma_{(p,q)}(P_{m}\Box P_{n}) for all ( p , q ) ∈ ( ℕ ∖ { 3 } ) × ( ℕ ∪ { ∞ } ) (p,q)\in(\mathbb{N}\setminus\{3\})\times(\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}) and all m , n ∈ ℕ m,n\in\mathbb{N} .

Introduction

Zero forcing was independently introduced by mathematical physicists in the study of quantum systems
[
9
]
and by linear algebraists in connection with the maximum nullity of real symmetric matrices
[
2
]
. Since then, zero forcing received a lot of attention by graph theorists; see
[
7
,
14
,
18
,
19
]
for some recent papers and the monograph
[
17
]
that surveys various studies of zero forcing. The notion of zero forcing appears also in the analysis of the diverse processes, in which the need of uniform framework led to the introduction of the generalization of zero forcing named
k
-forcing
[
3
]
.

Suppose that vertices of a graph
G
are assigned one of the two possible colors, white and blue. The color change rule in
k
-forcing is defined as follows:
if a blue vertex
u
has at most
k
white neighbors, then change color of all white neighbors of
u
to blue
. In particular, when
k=1
, this is the color change rule for zero forcing.
If for a set of vertices
S\subseteq V(G)
, which are initially colored blue, continuous application of the color change rule enforces all vertices of
G
to become blue, then
S
is a
k
-forcing set
of
G
(resp., a
zero forcing set
of
G
when
k=1
). The
k
-forcing number
of
G
,
F_{k}(G)
, is the minimum cardinality of a
k
-forcing set of
G
, while the
zero forcing number
, when
k=1
, is denoted by
Z(G)
, and so
Z(G)=F_{1}(G)
.

The concept of bootstrap percolation has a similar definition to that of zero forcing, yet a different motivation. It was introduced as a simplified model of a magnetic system in 1979
[
11
]
, and was later studied on random graphs and also on deterministic graphs. Let us mention that one of the first graphs in which bootstrap percolation was studied were grids
[
5
,
6
]
.

Again let all vertices of a graph
G
be colored either white or blue. (In some applicable contexts, blue vertices are called “infected” and white vertices “uninfected”.) A set
S\subseteq V(G)
of blue vertices is an
r
-neighbor bootstrap percolating set
, or simply, an
r
-percolating set
of
G
if by repeatedly applying the rule:
if a white vertex
u
has at least
r
blue (infected) neighbors, then change color of
u
to blue
, all vertices of
G
eventually become blue. The
r
-neighbor bootstrap percolation number
of
G
,
m(G,r)
, is the minimum cardinality of an
r
-neighbor bootstrap percolating set of
G
.

Motivated by the similarity of the definitions of the above two concepts, we introduce a common generalization of
k
-forcing and bootstrap percolation.
Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, and let vertices of a graph
G
be colored either white or blue. If a white vertex
w
has at least
p
blue neighbors, and one of the blue neighbors of
w
has at most
q
white neighbors, then by the
spreading color change rule
the color of
w
is changed to blue. A set
S
is a
(p,q)
-
spreading set
for
G
if initially exactly the vertices of
S
are colored blue and by repeatedly applying the spreading color change rule all the vertices of
G
are eventually turned to blue. The
(p,q)
-
spreading number
,
{\sigma_{(p,q)}}(G)
, of a graph
G
is the minimum cardinality of a
(p,q)
-spreading set.

The aim of this paper is to initiate the study of spreading in graphs. The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we specify connections with some specializations of
(p,q)
-spreading, such as bootstrap percolation,
P_{3}
-convexity and
q
-forcing. We also give some basic preliminary results. In Section
3
, we concentrate on complexity issues regarding the computation of the
(p,q)
-spreading number. We prove that the decision version of determining
{\sigma_{(p,q)}}(G)
, where
G
is an arbitrary graph, is an NP-complete problem. We do this by translating the decision version of determining the
q
-forcing number of
G
, for which we also prove that it is NP-complete. The latter result was, to the best of our knowledge, not known before. In Section
4
, we consider spreading in trees. First, we present several algorithms to compute
{\sigma_{(p,q)}}(T)
, where
T
is a tree. We prove that
F_{q}(T)
equals the size of a smallest partition of
T
into induced subtrees of maximum degree at most
q
, which enables us to present a linear-time algorithm to determine
\sigma_{(1,q)}(T)
for a tree

and
q\in\mathbb{N}
. We also note that Riedl’s algorithm
[
22
]
, which computes the
p
-neighbor bootstrap percolation of a tree in linear time, where
p\geq 2
, works for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Second, we also find an upper and a lower bound for the
(p,q)
-spreading number of a tree and characterize the trees that attain these bounds. Section
5
is devoted to spreading in square grids. By combining some known results (say, about the 2-neighbor bootstrap percolation in grids due to Bollobás
[
6
]
) with some new results, we obtain the
(p,q)
-spreading number of all grids for all
p
and
q
with the sole exception when
p=3
. Finally, we state some concluding remarks and propose several open problems in Section
6
.

Remark 2.1

3
NP-completeness

Given a graph
G
and positive integers
p
and

(p,q)
-spreading problem is to find a
(p,q)
-spreading set of minimum size in

-Spreading

Input
: A graph
G=(V,E)
and positive integers
p,q
and
k

Question
: Is there a
(p,q)
-spreading set in

A special case of

-spreading problem is obtained when we set

,
15
,

-forcing number

and equals

We will deal with this version of the problem separately, and formulate the corresponding decision problem as follows:

q
-Forcing

Input
: A graph
G=(V,E)
and positive integers
q
and
k

Question
: Is there a
q
-forcing set in
G
of size at most

?

It is clear that both problems are in NP, and it suffices to argue this for the
(p,q)
-Spreading
problem. Let

be a graph, and

be positive integers. If a set

of size

-spreading set in

First, we prove NP-completeness of the

-Forcing

-forcing

We need the following definition. Let

be a graph and

-forcing set of

-forcing process starts vertices of

are blue, vertices of

are white and the

whose white neighbors are vertices

-

Let

Lemma 3.3

Let

be a graph and let

with
p\geq 2
.
If

is obtained from

Proof.
Let

be a minimum

, and let

be the set of vertices that are initially set blue within the

-spreading process in

. Note that the leaves in

make their neighbors in

has

blue leaf-neighbors. Hence, each vertex of

already has at least

-spreading that each white vertex must have at least

has a blue neighbor also in

. Thus, the process of changing colors with respect to the

-spreading rules takes the same steps as the color change process with respect to
q
-forcing in

. Hence,

is a
(p,q)
-spreading set in

, and we readily infer that

.

For the reversed inequality, let

be a minimum
(p,q)
-spreading set of

. Note that necessarily

, for otherwise none of the leaves would ever become blue, because they each have at most one neighbor and

. In addition,

, because each

becomes blue due to its

-forcing set of

-forcing set of

\square

4.1

The zero forcing problem on trees has been studied in several papers. It is well known that the zero forcing number

of a tree

; see

-forcing, for which we need the following definition. Let

denote the size of the smallest partition of

into subsets inducing trees of maximum degree at most

. Recall that

and

Theorem 4.1

Let

be a tree and

.

Proof.

First, let

-set of

q
white neighbors. For each

let

. Then whenever a vertex

from

has at most
q

(note that

For the induction step, let

Theorem 4.7

Let

5
Spreading in grids

In this section, we determine the
(p,q)
-spreading number of grids for all possible pairs
(p,q)
with the exception when
p=3
. Let
G=P_{m}\square P_{n}
, and without loss of generality let
n\leq m
.

Let
p=1
, that is consider the
q
-forcing problem in grids. If
q=1
, that is, considering the zero forcing problem, it was proved in
[
2
]
that
Z(G)=F_{1}(G)=\sigma_{(1,1)}(G)=n
. Clearly, we also have
F_{q}(G)=1
for
q\geq 2
.

The case when
p=3
and
q\geq 4
, that is,
{\sigma_{(p,q)}}(G)=m(G,3)
, was recently studied in
[
16
,
23
]
, but it seems that this is a difficult problem for which only partial results are known. This is the only case of grids that we will not resolve.

If
p>\Delta(G)=4
, Remark
2.1
(2) implies that
{\sigma_{(p,q)}}(G)=|V(G)|=mn
. The case
p=4
is resolved in the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1

If
m,n\geq 3
are positive integers, then
\sigma_{(4,q)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=2m+2n-4+\lfloor\frac{(m-2)(n-2)}{2}\rfloor
for all
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Let
V(P_{m})=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
and
V(P_{n})=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and let
S
be a
(4,q)
-spreading set of
P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n}
. (Let us call vertices
x\in P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n}
with
\deg(x)<4

boundary vertices
.) By Remark
2.1
(1), all
2m+2n-4
boundary vertices of
P_{m}\Box P_{n}
are contained in
S
. From the remaining
(m-2)\times(n-2)
grid, we claim that we must select vertices that form a vertex cover. Indeed, suppose that there exists an edge
uv
such that
u\notin S
and
v\notin S
. Then in any step of the color changing procedure,
u
and
v
each have at most three blue neighbors, and thus
u
and
v
cannot be colored blue, and so
S
is not a
(4,q)
-spreading set, a contradiction. As the minimum vertex cover number of the
(m-2)\times(n-2)
grid is
\lfloor\frac{(m-2)(n-2)}{2}\rfloor
, we get
|S|\geq 2m+2n-4+\lfloor\frac{(m-2)(n-2)}{2}\rfloor
. For the converse, one can easily check that the boundary vertices of
P_{m}\Box P_{n}
together with the minimum vertex cover set of the remaining
(m-2)\times(n-2)
grid forms a
(4,q)
-spreading set and thus
\sigma_{(4,q)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})\leq 2m+2n-4+\lfloor\frac{(m-2)(n-2)}{2}\rfloor
.
\square

6
Concluding remarks

It follows from Remark
2.1
that
\sigma_{(3,4)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=\sigma_{(3,\infty)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
for any
m,n\geq 3
, since
\Delta(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=4
. Unfortunately, the exact values of the bootstrap percolation numbers
m(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n},3)
(
=\sigma_{(3,\infty)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
)
are not known in general; see
[
16
,
23
]
for some known results and bounds, and
[
12
]
for a related study.
However, we believe that
\sigma_{(3,3)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=\sigma_{(3,4)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=\sigma_{(3,\infty)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
, and pose this as a conjecture.

Conjecture 6.1

If
m,n\geq 3
, then
\sigma_{(3,3)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})=\sigma_{(3,4)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
.

Figure 5:
Minimum
(3,3)
- and
(3,1)
-spreading sets of
P_{3}\,\square\,P_{3}
.

One might ask, if the conjecture is proven to be true, whether one can extend the equality even further. While we did not find an answer to the question whether
\sigma_{(3,2)}(G)=\sigma_{(3,3)}(G)
is true for all grids
G
, we can show that at least
\sigma_{(3,1)}(G)
is not always equal to
\sigma_{(3,3)}(G)
if
G
is a grid. See the example of
G=P_{3}\,\square\,P_{3}
in Figure
5
, from which it is clear that
\sigma_{(3,1)}(G)>\sigma_{(3,2)}(G)=\sigma_{(3,3)}(G)=\sigma_{(3,4)}(G)
, therefore in general
\sigma_{(3,1)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})\neq\sigma_{(3,3)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
.

Even if one can prove Conjecture
6.1
, this does not necessarily mean that one can determine
\sigma_{(3,3)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
or
\sigma_{(3,4)}(P_{m}\,\square\,P_{n})
. Based on some earlier partial results, this could be a hard problem, which we pose next.

Problem 1

Determine
\sigma_{(3,q)}(P_{m}\Box P_{n})
, where
q\leq 4
, for all positive integers
m
and
n
.

Obtaining upper and lower bounds on
\sigma_{(3,q)}(P_{m}\Box P_{n})
would be a natural way to approach Problem
1
. Alternatively, one could aim to obtain exact values while fixing one of the integers
m
or
n
; we again refer to
[
16
,
23
]
for known values.

Hypercubes, alias
n
-cubes, are one of the most important graph classes with numerous applications; one can define the
n
-cube,
Q_{n}
, as
K_{2}\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,K_{2}
, where there are
n
factors. In particular, it is known that
\sigma_{(1,1)}(Q_{n})=2^{n-1}

[
2
]
. In addition,
\sigma_{(2,\infty)}(Q_{n})=\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil+1
, as proved in
[
4
]
, and
\sigma_{(3,\infty)}(Q_{n})=\lceil\frac{n(n+3)}{6}\rceil+1
, as proved in
[
21
]
. A natural problem is to determine the
(p,q)
-spreading numbers of
n
-cubes for other values of
p
and
q
.

Problem 2

Determine
\sigma_{(p,q)}(Q_{n})
, where
Q_{n}
is the
n
-cube of dimension
n
for all
p\in\mathbb{N},q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

The most natural generalizations of hypercubes are Hamming graphs, which are the Cartesian products of several complete graphs. Problem
2
could thus be extended to determining
\sigma_{(p,q)}(K_{r_{1}}\Box\cdots\Box K_{r_{n}})
, where
K_{r_{i}}
are complete graphs. This problem was considered for
P_{3}
-convexity in
[
8
]
, where it was proved that
\sigma_{(2,\infty)}(K_{r_{1}}\Box\cdots\Box K_{r_{n}})=\lceil n/2\rceil+1
.
