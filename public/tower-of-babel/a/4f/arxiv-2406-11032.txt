1.
Introduction

The matrix inverse eigenvalue problem consists in reconstructing a specific structured matrix from a prescribed spectral data. This topic is of great interest in different
branches of mathematics, continually providing new and surprising results (see for
example
[
10
,
22
,
33
,
34
]
and the references there).

From the celebrated work
[
36
]
by Hubert S. Wall, it follows that there exists a
unique
matrix

(1.1)

J_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}-b_{0}&1&&&\\
-b_{1}&0&1&&\\
&-b_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
&&&-b_{n-1}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

with
b_{k}>0
, for
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, whose characteristic polynomial is
(x+1)^{n}
(see also
[
32
,
35
,
37
]
for more details). Wall’s argumentation is based on the stability of the characteristic polynomial, i.e. on that all its zeros have negative real parts — which is of course true for
(x+1)^{n}
.

By analogy with nilpotent matrices, one introduces
unipotent
matrices: an
n\times n
matrix is called unipotent if its characteristic polynomial is
(x-1)^{n}
, or, equivalently, if all its eigenvalues are equal to
1
. Various disciplines deal with unipotent matrices, cf. e.g.
[
6
,
21
,
28
,
38
]
. We consider an extension of this definition, namely matrices with only a single eigenvalue labelled by
a
. In brief, we are looking for an “
a
-potent” matrix of the form (
1.1
) where
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

Matrices of the form (
1.1
) are called Schwarz matrices.
Such matrices are very important in automatic control theory and signal processing (see, for example,
[
8
]
), and several general inverse problems have been previously considered in
[
3
,
7
,
11
,
13
,
16
]
. So, here we consider the inverse eigenvalue problem for a
Schwarz matrix whose characteristic polynomial is

(1.2)

p(z)=(z-a)^{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}a^{n-i}z^{i},

where
a
is a given nonzero complex number. That is, we want to construct a Schwarz matrix (
1.1
) with only one eigenvalue
a
.

Since
J_{k}
is the leading principal
k\times k
submatrix of

J_{n}
, with
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the polynomials

P_{k}(z)=\det(zI_{k}-J_{k})\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n\,,

where
I_{k}
is the identity matrix of order
k
and

P_{n}(z)=p(z)
, satisfy the following three-term recurrence
relation:

\displaystyle P_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1\,,

(1.3)

\displaystyle P_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z+b_{0}\,,

\displaystyle P_{k+1}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle zP_{k}(z)+b_{k}P_{k-1}(z)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n-1\,.

By the Favard theorem, see
1
1

1

In
[
27
]
, this extended version is attributed to Chihara, although in essence
it already existed in
[
17
]
.

[
9
, pp. 21–22]
, the
sequence
\{P_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
is orthogonal w.r.t. a linear
functional. Observe that, given arbitrary
n>1
and
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, this functional is not positive and cannot be written as an integral w.r.t. a measure on the real line (otherwise
J_{n}
would have
n
distinct real eigenvalues). Accordingly, for
n>1
the numbers
b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}
in (

) cannot be chosen negative while
b_{0}
remains real.
As is seen from Section
3.2
, the support of the orthogonality functional is natural to take consisting of a unique point

.

We find the aforementioned matrix
J_{n}
, see the values of
b_{0}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}
in, resp., (
2.9
) and (
2.23
). The sequence
\{P_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
satisfying (

The explicit expression of the polynomial
P_{k}(z)
for
a=1
is given in (

is in (

It turns out that three-term recurrence relations for the Bessel
polynomials
[
24
]
have a form similar to (
1.3
),
so Bessel polynomials are also characteristic polynomials
of Schwarz matrices, and for
a=-\dfrac{1}{n}
the polynomials

approximate Bessel polynomials uniformly
on the compact sets of the complex plane as
n\to+\infty
. Moreover,
the support of the functional of orthogonality for Bessel
polynomials on the real line may also be treated as consisting of one point of essential singularity.
Thus, we can treat polynomials
\{P_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
as
a discrete finite analogue of Bessel polynomials.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2

. In Sections

and
3.2

investigate properties of the orthogonal polynomials

and find their functional of orthogonality on the real line.
In Section

Bessel polynomials and provide some calculations showing that
the zeroes of

approach the zeroes of Bessel polynomials
for
a=-\dfrac{1}{n}
as

.
In Section

for varying

and

to

is large.

2.
Explicit formula for Schwarz matrix

To find the entries of the matrix
J_{n}
or, similarly, the
coefficients of the three-term recurrence
relations (
1.3
), we consider the
rational function

(2.1)

R(z)=b_{0}((zI_{n}-J_{n})^{-1}e_{1},e_{1})=\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)},

where
e_{1}
is the first coordinate vector. Here the polynomial

q(z)
, multiplied by
b_{0}
, is the characteristic polynomial of the
principal submatrix of
J_{n}
(
1.1
) constructed
from
J_{n}
by deleting its first row and the first column:

q(z)=b_{0}\det\begin{bmatrix}z&-1&0&&\\
b_{2}&z&-1&&\\
&b_{3}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
&&&b_{n-1}&z\\
\end{bmatrix}.

Observe that the polynomial
p(z)
, the characteristic polynomial
of the matrix
J_{n}
, can be represented as follows

(2.2)

p(z)=Q(z)+q(z)\,,

where

Q(z)=\det\begin{bmatrix}z&-1&0&&\\
b_{1}&z&-1&&\\
&b_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&-1\\
&&&b_{n-1}&z\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

It is easy to see that
q(z)
is an even polynomial whenever
n
is
odd, and is an odd polynomial otherwise, while the
polynomial
Q(z)
is odd if
n
is odd, and is even if
n
is even.
Therefore, by (
2.2
), the polynomial
q(z)
is the
even (odd) part of the polynomial
p(z)
whenever
n
is odd (even). This
means that
q(z)
can be obtained from
p(z)
in the following way

q(z)=\dfrac{p(z)-(-1)^{n}p(-z)}{2}\,,

so that

(2.3)

\displaystyle q(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\binom{n}{1}az^{n-1}-\binom{n}{3}a^{3}z^{n-3}-\binom{n}{5}a^{5}z^{n-5}-\cdots

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{n}{2j+1}a^{2j+1}z^{n-2j-1}.

For the calculation of the entries of the matrix
J_{n}
we first study the rational
function
R(z)
defined in (
2.1
).

Theorem 2.1
.

The rational function
R(z)=\dfrac{q(z)}{p(z)}
has the form

R(z)=\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{z-a}+\dfrac{\alpha_{1}}{(z-a)^{2}}+\cdots+\dfrac{\alpha_{n}}{(z-a)^{n}}=\dfrac{s_{0}}{z}+\dfrac{s_{1}}{z^{2}}+\dfrac{s_{2}}{z^{3}}+\cdots,

where

(2.4)

\alpha_{k}=-2^{k-1}a^{k}\binom{n}{k}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n\,,

and

(2.5)

s_{m}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}a^{m-k+1}\binom{m}{k-1}\alpha_{k}=-a^{m+1}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}2^{k-1}\binom{m}{k-1}\binom{n}{k}.

Proof.

Expanding the polynomial
q(z)
into the Taylor series at the point

a
, we obtain

R(z)=\dfrac{q(a)}{(z-a)^{n}}+\dfrac{q^{\prime}(a)}{1!(z-a)^{n-1}}+\dfrac{q^{\prime\prime}(a)}{2!(z-a)^{n-2}}+\cdots+\dfrac{q^{(n-1)}(a)}{(n-1)!(z-a)}\,,

so

\alpha_{k}=\dfrac{q^{(n-k)}(a)}{(n-k)!}\,\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n\,.

From (
2.3
), we have

\dfrac{q^{(n-k)}(z)}{(n-k)!}=-\sum_{j=1}^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{n}{2j+1}\binom{n-2j-1}{n-k}a^{2j+1}z^{k-2j-1}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n,

which implies

(2.6)

\alpha_{k}=\dfrac{q^{(n-k)}(a)}{(n-k)!}=-a^{k}\binom{n}{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{k}{2j+1}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n.

Now substituting

2^{k-1}=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\tfrac{k-1}{2}\right\rfloor}\binom{k}{2j+1}

into (
2.6
), we get (
2.4
).

To establish (
2.5
), we recall that, for any nonnegative integer
number
\ell
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{(z-a)^{\ell}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\binom{m+\ell-1}{\ell-1}\dfrac{a^{m}}{z^{m+\ell}}\,,

so

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\alpha_{k}}{(z-a)^{k}}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\binom{m+k-1}{k-1}a^{m}\alpha_{k}}{z^{m+k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{z^{m+1}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a^{m-k+1}\binom{m}{k-1}\alpha_{k}\,.

This formula together with (
2.4
) gives us (
2.5
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the numbers
s_{m}
satisfy the following recurrence

(1+m)a^{2}s_{m}+2nas_{m+1}-(3+m)s_{m+2}=0\,.

A careful consideration shows that the moments

are polynomials in

, and can be
represented as

s_{m}(n)=-\dfrac{a^{m+1}n}{(m+1)!}\,p_{m}(n)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;m=0,1,2,\ldots,

where

x>0
. In fact, the integral itself converges in the right half-plane, so it generates a measure
of orthogonality for the polynomials

with the support on the imaginary axis.

It is not hard to identify this system of orthogonal polynomials: on comparing the

2\end{matrix}\,\Big|\,2\right)=\frac{m!}{i^{m}}P_{m}^{(1)}\left(in;\frac{\pi}{2}\right),

so the corresponding orthogonality measure is
\left|\Gamma(1-x)\right|^{2}

with

,

Lemma 2.3
.

The minors

, for

Indeed, the

of the minor

has

(2.11)

\mathbf{C}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}s_{k-1}\\
\vdots\\

If we change columns as follows

(2.12)

\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-a)^{k-j}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\mathbf{C}_{j}\,,

for

is the

th new column, then the minor

does not change its value.
From (
2.11

we obtain that the

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{k})_{\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-a)^{k-j}\binom{k-1}{j-1}s_{j+\ell-2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-a)^{k-j}\binom{k-1}{j-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a^{j+\ell-1-i}\binom{j+\ell-2}{i-1}\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a^{k+\ell-1-i}\alpha_{i}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{k-1-j}\binom{k-1}{j}\binom{j+\ell-1}{i-1}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a^{k+\ell-1-i}\binom{\ell-1}{i-k}\alpha_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a^{\ell-i}\binom{\ell-1}{i-1}\alpha_{k+i-1}\,,

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+3)}{(i+j-2)!}+\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+2)}{(i+j-1)!}=\\
\\
=(n+1)\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+3)}{(i+j-1)!}\end{array}

is used repeatedly for each element of the

th column, for
j=m,m-1,\ldots,2
. Thus, we conclude
that the determinant does not change its value, while its
2

3
rd, …,
m
th columns have the
common factor
n+1
. Therefore, we have

T_{m}=(n+1)^{m-1}\prod_{k=1}^{m}k!\binom{n}{k}\,\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\dots&\tfrac{(n-1)\cdots(n-m+2)}{m!}\\
\tfrac{1}{2!}&\tfrac{1}{3!}&\dots&\tfrac{(n-2)\cdots(n-m+1)}{(m+1)!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{m!}&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}&\dots&\tfrac{(n-m)\cdots(n-2m+3)}{(2m-1)!}\end{vmatrix}

Now we use the similar procedure for the
3

4

\ldots
,

(m+1)
th columns. Namely, we add the

th column to the
m
th,

th column to the

th, etc., and use the identity

\begin{array}[]{l}\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+4)}{(i+j-2)!}+\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+3)}{(i+j-1)!}=\\
\\
=(n+2)\dfrac{(n-i)(n-i-1)\cdots(n-i-j+4)}{(i+j-1)!}\end{array}

repeatedly for each element of the

th column, for

.
Again we conclude that the determinant does not change its value,
while its

4

\ldots

m
th columns have the common

. So we have

T_{m}\displaystyle=(n+1)^{m-1}(n+2)^{m-2}\prod_{k=1}^{m}k!\binom{n}{k}\,\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\cdots&\tfrac{(n-1)\cdots(n-m+3)}{m!}\\
\tfrac{1}{2!}&\tfrac{1}{3!}&\cdots&\tfrac{(n-2)\cdots(n-m+2)}{(m+1)!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{m!}&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}&\dots&\tfrac{(n-m)\cdots(n-2m+4)}{(2m-1)!}\end{vmatrix}

Consequently, from a straightforward inductive argument, we get

\displaystyle T_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{m-1}(n+\ell)^{m-\ell}\prod_{k=1}^{m}k!\binom{n}{k}\,\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{m!}\\
\tfrac{1}{2!}&\tfrac{1}{3!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{m!}&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(2m-1)!}\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{m}k!\binom{n}{k}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}j!\binom{n+j}{j}\,\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{m!}\\
\tfrac{1}{2!}&\tfrac{1}{3!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{m!}&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(2m-1)!}\end{vmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m!\binom{n}{m}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}(2k)!\binom{n+k}{n-k}\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{m!}\\
\tfrac{1}{2!}&\tfrac{1}{3!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\tfrac{1}{m!}&\tfrac{1}{(m+1)!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{(2m-1)!}\end{vmatrix}\,.

Note that the determinant

\begin{vmatrix}\tfrac{1}{1!}&\tfrac{1}{2!}&\cdots&\tfrac{1}{m!}\\

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{m}

\displaystyle(-1)^{\tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}}\dfrac{m!\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}(2k)!\binom{n+k}{n-k}}{m!\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{m-1}(2k)!\binom{m+k}{m-k}}

3.
Orthogonal polynomials

In this section, we consider the sequence
\{P_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
of the
characteristic polynomials of the leading principal minors of the
matrix (
2.25
) satisfying the three-term recurrence
relation (
1.1
)–(
1.3
):

P_{0}(z)=1,\quad P_{1}(z)=z-an,

(3.1)

P_{k+1}(z)=zP_{k}(z)+\dfrac{a^{2}(n^{2}-k^{2})}{(2k-1)(2k+1)}P_{k-1}(z)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n-1.

We give their explicit representations and study their even and odd parts. Then we show that
\{P_{k}(z)\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
are orthogonal w.r.t. a linear functional supported at the only point
z=a
. We also find the inverse Fourier transform of the corresponding distributional weight and depict the zeroes of the polynomials
P_{k}(z)
for various
a
and relations between
k
and
n
as
n\to+\infty
.

3.1.
Basic properties

For the current subsection we assume
a=1
keeping in mind that the general case

a\neq 0
may be obtained by

(3.2)

a^{-k}P_{k}(az)=P_{k}(z)\big|_{a=1},

which immediately follows from (
3.1
). Observe that
the monic Jacobi polynomials

\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,n)}(z)=\frac{2^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}}P_{k}^{(-n,n)}(z)\qquad\left(=\frac{(-2)^{k}}{\binom{2k}{k}}P_{k}^{(n,-n)}(-z)\right)

satisfy for
k\geq 1
the three-term recurrence relation
[
9
, p. 153, eq. (2.29)]

\widehat{P}_{k+1}^{(-n,n)}(z)=z\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,n)}(z)-\frac{4k(k-n)(k+n)k}{(2k-1)(2k+1)(2k)^{2}}\widehat{P}_{k-1}^{(-n,n)}(z),

which on reduction of similar terms turns into (
3.1
) with
a=1
. Similarly,

\widehat{P}_{0}^{(-n,n)}(z)\equiv 1

and

\widehat{P}_{1}^{(-n,n)}(z)=z-n
.
Therefore, our
P_{k}(z)
are exactly
\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,n)}(z)
, namely,
for all
k=0,\dots,n,

(3.3)

P_{k}(z)=\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,n)}(z)=(-2)^{k}\frac{(n+1)_{k}}{(k+1)_{k}}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\genfrac{.}{|}{0.0pt}{0}{-k,\ k+1}{n+1}\dfrac{1+z}{2}\right).

Also,

P_{k}(z)\big|_{a=1}=\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)_{k}}\left(\dfrac{1-z}{1+z}\right)^{n}\dfrac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\left[(1-z)^{n-k}(1+z)^{n+k}\right],

(1-z^{2})P_{k}^{\prime\prime}(z)+2(n-z)P_{k}^{\prime}(z)+k(k+1)P_{k}(z)=0.

Although the three-term recurrence degenerates to a two-term recurrence
for
k=n
, the sequence
\{P_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
may be further extended to an
infinite sequence. Properties of infinite sequences of the monic
polynomials
\{\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,\beta)}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
with
\beta\notin\{-1,-2,\dots\}

were studied in
[
1
]
, where orthogonality was introduced via a bilinear form.

Now, on introducing two auxiliary families of polynomials via

(3.4)

f_{0}(z)=1\,,\quad f_{1}(z)=z\,,\quad\text{and}\quad f_{k+1}(z)=zf_{k}(z)+\dfrac{n^{2}-k^{2}}{4k^{2}-1}f_{k-1}(z)

and

(3.5)

g_{0}(z)=0\,,\quad g_{1}(z)=1\,,\quad\text{and}\quad g_{k+1}(z)=zg_{k}(z)+\dfrac{n^{2}-k^{2}}{4k^{2}-1}g_{k-1}(z)

with
k=1,\dots,n-1
, one immediately obtains that the decomposition

P_{k}(z)=f_{k}(z)-ng_{k}(z)

is valid for all
k
. The formula (
3.4
) determines monic orthogonal polynomials supported on the imaginary axis, and (
3.5
) gives exactly the corresponding monic numerator polynomials.
In particular, for each
k
the zeroes of
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
are all simple and pure imaginary, they also interlace on the imaginary axis, see
[
9
, p. 86]
.
Therefore, the Hermite-Biehler theorem implies that all zeroes of the polynomial
P_{k}(z)=f_{k}(z)-ng_{k}(z)
have positive real parts,
[
25
]
(see also
[
20
]
and references there). That is to say, all zeroes of the polynomial
P_{k}
for arbitrary
a\neq 0
lie in the half-plane
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\operatorname{Re}\left(z/a\right)>0\}
, which is clearly seen on the figures in Sections
3.3
and
3.4
.

In fact, the polynomials
f_{k}
and
g_{k}
may also be expressed via classical families of orthogonal polynomials.
The three-term relations (
3.4
)
and (
3.5
) are known to determine monic versions of the so-called
associated Jacobi polynomials
\widehat{P}_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z;c)
, namely,

f_{k}(z)=\widehat{P}_{k}^{(-n,-n)}(z;n)\quad\text{and}\quad g_{k}(z)=\widehat{P}_{k-1}^{(-n,-n)}(z;n+1).

The explicit representation of the associated Jacobi polynomials is due to
Wimp
[
23
, p. 182]
, however our specific case allows to write these polynomials using the simple expressions

f_{k}(z)=\frac{(-1)^{k}P_{k}(-z)+P_{k}(z)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad g_{k}(z)=\frac{(-1)^{k}P_{k}(-z)-P_{k}(z)}{2n}.

Note that the sequence
\{f_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
consists of the so-called (monic) exceptional Jacobi polynomials
[
23
, p. 97]
, although the parameter
\alpha=-n
is non-standard for them.
Observe also that
f_{k}
and
-ng_{k}
turn into
Q
and
q
from (
2.2
) on letting
k=n
.

3.2.
Orthogonality

Now, let us introduce the following linear functional

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f(x))
acting on
(n-1)
th differentiable
functions
f
and depending on the positive integer number
n
and
the (complex) nonzero number
a
:

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f(x))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{f^{(k-1)}(a)\alpha_{k}}{(k-1)!}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{f^{(k-1)}(a)}{(k-1)!}\dfrac{q^{(n-k)}(a)}{(n-k)!}=\dfrac{1}{(n-1)!}\dfrac{d^{n-1}[f(x)q(x)]}{dx^{n-1}}\Big|_{x=a}\,.

where the numbers
\alpha_{k}
are defined in (
2.4
).

Alternatively, assuming that
f
is analytic near the point
a
this functional may be expressed in a more usual form — as an integral with a continuous (complex-valued) Jacobi weight
(z-a)^{-n}(z+a)^{n}
over the circle
|z-a|=2|a|
, which is typical for the Jacobi polynomials with such parameters, cf.
[
17
, p. 222]
.
The weight is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, so the circle may be replaced with any Jordan curve enclosing the point
z=a
.
Due to Cauchy’s integral formula, this integral will essentially express the same functional
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}
, namely:

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f)&=\dfrac{1}{(n-1)!}\dfrac{d^{n-1}[f(z)q(z)]}{dz^{n-1}}\Big|_{z=a}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{|z-a|=2|a|}\frac{f(z)q(z)}{(z-a)^{n}}dz\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{|z-a|=2|a|}f(z)\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{z-a}{z-a}\right)^{n}-\left(\frac{z+a}{z-a}\right)^{n}\right)dz\\
&=\frac{i}{4\pi}\oint\limits_{|z-a|=2|a|}f(z)\left(\frac{z+a}{z-a}\right)^{n}dz.\end{split}

With this functional we can represent the rational function
R(z)

defined in (
2.1
) as follows

(3.6)

R(z)=\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}\left(\dfrac{1}{z-x}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{\alpha_{k}}{(z-a)^{k}}\,.

Moreover, from (
3.2
) we have

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(x^{m})=s_{m}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;m=0,1,\ldots,

where
s_{m}
are the moments defined in (
2.5
). In fact,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(x^{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\dfrac{(x^{m})^{(k-1)}\Big|_{x=a}}{(k-1)!}\,\alpha_{k}=a^{m}\alpha_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\dfrac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+2)a^{m-k+1}}{(k-1)!}\,\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{m}{k-1}a^{m-k+1}\alpha_{k}=s_{m}\,.

We now prove that the polynomials
P_{k}(z)
, for
k=0,1,\ldots,n-1
, defined in (
3.1
) are
orthogonal w.r.t. the functional (
3.2
),
that is,

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(P_{m}(x)P_{k}(x))=C_{m}\delta_{km}\ \ \quad\text{for}\quad m,k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\,,

where
\delta_{km}
is the Kronecker symbol. To this end, we find the three-term recurrence relations for monic polynomials
Q_{m}(z)

orthogonal w.r.t. the functional
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f)
and compare the obtained relations with (
3.1
). Firstly,
let us note that all the minors
D_{m}(R)
,
m=1,\ldots,n
, defined in (
2.8
) are nonzero, i.e.,

D_{m}(R)\neq 0\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;m=1,\ldots,n,

by (
2.18
), so such a sequence exists (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
). These orthogonal polynomials
have the form
[
23
, p. 21]

(3.7)

Q_{m}(z)=\dfrac{1}{D_{m}(R)}\begin{vmatrix}s_{0}&s_{1}&s_{2}&\dots&s_{m}\\
s_{1}&s_{2}&s_{3}&\dots&s_{m+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
s_{m-1}&s_{m}&s_{m+1}&\dots&s_{2m-1}\\
1&z&z^{2}&\dots&z^{m}\\
\end{vmatrix}\,,

for
m=1,\ldots,n
. The roots of the last polynomial

(partially)
define the functional
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f)
. If
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{a}(f)
is
a positive or negative functional,

would have exactly

real distinct roots that are the growth point of the measure
generated by

, see, e.g.,
[

. But the

is neither a positive nor a negative
functional, since the minors

are not all positive nor of
alternating signs
(\mbox{sgn}\,D_{m}(R)\neq(-1)^{m}
, for

The polynomials

satisfy a three-term recurrence relation

Q_{m+1}(z)=(z-c_{m+1})Q_{m}(z)-d_{m+1}Q_{m-1}(z).

It is well known, e.g.,
[

, that

so the polynomials

satisfy the recurrence relation

(3.9)

where

and

.

Furthermore, if

then (cf., e.g.,
[

]
)

(3.10)

c_{1}=A_{11}\,,\quad c_{m+1}=A_{1m}-A_{1,m+1}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;m=1,\ldots,n-1\,,

and, from (

A_{1,m}=-\dfrac{D^{\prime}_{m}(R)}{D_{m}(R)}\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;m=1,\ldots,n.

where

s_{1}&s_{2}&s_{3}&\cdots&s_{m-1}&s_{m+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

To find the formula for the minors

, we need the
following lemma which can be proved in the same way as Lemma

.

Lemma 3.1
.

The minors

3.3.
Relation to Bessel polynomials

The Bessel polynomials
\{B_{n}(x)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence of polynomials satisfying the differential
equation

x^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}+(2x+2)\dfrac{dy}{dx}=n(n+1)y.

They occur naturally in the theory of traveling spherical waves
[
24
]
. These (monic) polynomials
satisfy the following three-term recurrence relations

(3.17)

\begin{array}[]{l}B_{0}(z)=1,\quad B_{1}(z)=z+1,\\
\\
B_{k+1}(z)=zB_{k}(z)+\dfrac{1}{4k^{2}-1}B_{k-1}(z)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,2,\ldots\end{array}

The Bessel polynomials were introduced by H.L. Krall and O. Frink in
[
24
]
. They satisfy complex orthogonality conditions

\int\limits_{C}B_{n}(z)B_{m}(z)e^{-2/z}dz=0,\qquad\quad m\neq n,

where
C
is a closed path in the complex plane enclosing the origin. Their orthogonality on the real axis
was a subject of discussion for many years until this problem was resolved in a few different ways,
see
[
12
,
14
,
29
]
. We note that on the real line, the support of
the functional of orthogonality for the Bessel polynomials consists of a unique point
z=0
which is an essential
singularity. Moreover, from the recurrence relations (
3.17
), it follows that every
monic Bessel polynomial is the characteristic polynomial of a Schwarz matrix defined by these relations and, therefore,
is stable. Since the polynomials
\{P_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
satisfying (
3.1
)
and orthogonal w.r.t. the functional
\mathcal{L}_{a}^{n}
defined in (
3.2
) are
characteristic polynomials of Schwarz matrices stable for
a<0
, and the support of the functional
\mathcal{L}_{a}^{n}

consists of one point, we may say that
\{P_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n-1}
can be treated as a finite discrete analogue of Bessel
polynomials. Additionally, if one sets
a=-\dfrac{1}{n}
, then the three-term recurrence relations (
3.1
)
for the polynomials
P_{k}(z)
take the form

\begin{array}[]{l}P_{0}(z)=1,\quad P_{1}(z)=z+1,\\[5.0pt]
P_{k+1}(z)=zP_{k}(z)+\dfrac{P_{k-1}(z)}{4k^{2}-1}-\dfrac{1}{n^{2}}\,\dfrac{k^{2}}{4k^{2}-1}P_{k-1}(z)\,,\quad\mbox{for}\;k=1,\ldots,n-1,\end{array}

from which it follows that as
n\to+\infty
, these recurrence relations are close to (
3.17
).
Thus, the polynomials
P_{k}(z)
approach
B_{k}(z)
coefficient-wise, and, therefore,
on the compact subsets of the complex plane. So for
a=-\dfrac{1}{n}
, sufficiently large
n
, and relatively small
k
(comparing with
n
)
one can consider the polynomials
P_{k}(z)
as an approximation of the monic Bessel polynomials
B_{k}(z)
.
Thus, we can expect that for a fixed
k
and large
n
the zeroes of
P_{k}(z)
will approximate zeroes of
B_{k}(z)
, and
as
k\to+\infty
and
n\to+\infty
with
\dfrac{k}{n}\to 0
the zeroes of
P_{k}(z)
will approach the same curve as the
zeroes of
B_{k}(z)
for
k\to+\infty
, see
[
4
,
5
]
and references there.
Figures
3.2
and
3.2
show the zeroes of the polynomials
B_{50}(z)
and
B_{75}(z)
,
respectively, and the zeroes of
P_{50}(z)
and
