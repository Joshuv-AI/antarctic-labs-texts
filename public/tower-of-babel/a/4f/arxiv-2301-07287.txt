Exotic quantum subgroups and extensions of affine Lie algebra VOAs – part I
Terry Gannon

Abstract
Prototypical rational vertex operator algebras are associated to affine Lie algebras 𝔤 ( 1 ) \mathfrak{g}^{(1)} at positive integer level k k . These share the same representation theory as quantum groups at roots of unity and physically correspond to the Wess-Zumino-Witten theories. One would like to identify the full (bulk) conformal field theories whose chiral halves are those VOAs. Mathematically, these correspond to the module categories for their representation theory. Until now, this has been done only for 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 2 ) \mathfrak{sl}(2) (famously, the A-D-E classification of Cappelli-Itzykson-Zuber) and 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 3 ) \mathfrak{sl}(3) . The problem reduces to knowing the possible extensions of those VOAs, and the tensor equivalences between those extensions. Recent progress puts the tensor equivalences in good control, especially for 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( n ) \mathfrak{sl}(n) . Our paper focuses on the extensions. We prove that, for any simple 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} , there is a bound K ⁡ ( 𝔤 ) K(\mathfrak{g}) growing like the rank-cubed, such that for any level k > K ⁡ ( 𝔤 ) k>K(\mathfrak{g}) , the only extensions are generic (simple-current ones). We use that bound to find all extensions (hence module categories) for 𝔤 = 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 4 ) \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}(4) and 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 ​ ( 5 ) \mathfrak{sl}(5) . In the sequel to this paper, we find all extensions for all simple 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} of rank ≤ 6 \leq 6 .

Introduction

The chiral halves of 2-dimensional conformal field theories (CFTs) are often identified with vertex operator algebras (VOAs)
{\mathcal{V}}
. The friendliest examples, corresponding to so-called completely rational theories, have a semi-simple representation theory Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
called a modular fusion category (MFC) (also called a modular tensor category in the literature).

The prototypical examples of completely rational VOAs and MFCs come from (finite-dimensional complex) simple Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
at level
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, which we denote by
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
and
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)=\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k))
respectively. These categories also arise from quantum groups
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
at roots of unity
q
. Their simple objects are parametrised by level
k
highest weights
\lambda\in P_{+}^{k}(\mathfrak{g})
of the affine Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}
; the tensor unit is
\mathbf{1}=(0,0,\ldots,0)
.

The challenge of classifying all possible full (bulk) CFTs corresponding to a given pair of chiral halves is a fundamental one, going back to
[
37
]
and successfully studied by many in the 1990s and early 2000s (see e.g.
[
25
,
50
,
54
]
. The case of
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
(for any level
k
) falls into the famous A-D-E classification of Cappelli-Itzykson-Zuber
[
8
]
. Following several insights by the subfactor community, a full CFT has been formulated categorically as a
module category

[
52
]
of the given MFC, which is the perspective we take.

Given a completely rational VOA
{\mathcal{V}}
, each indecomposable module category for the MFC Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
is characterized by a triple
({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L},{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R},{\mathcal{F}})
, where the VOAs
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R}
are both extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}
, and
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L})\to\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R})
is a braided tensor equivalence (see e.g. Corollary 3.8 of
[
13
]
).

In the physics literature this description of module categories appears as the
naturality
of
[
49
]
. The extensions correspond to anyon condensations.
Physically, the trivial case
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L}={\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R}={\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
and
{\mathcal{F}}=\,
Id corresponds to the Wess-Zumino-Witten model
[
37
]
of strings living on the compact simply connected Lie group
G
corresponding to
\mathfrak{g}
. More generally, when the target
G
is replaced with
G/Z
for some subgroup of the centre, then
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L,R}
are so-called simple-current extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, and the equivalence
{\mathcal{F}}
will likewise be built from simple-currents. Simple-currents are the invertible simple objects of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, and the extensions and equivalences they generate are completely understood. In the VOA language,
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
is a simple-current extension of
{\mathcal{V}}
iff
{\mathcal{V}}
is the orbifold
({\mathcal{V}}^{e})^{G}
(i.e. the fixed-points of a
G
-action) by some (finite) abelian group
G
of automorphisms.

Braided tensor equivalences
{\mathcal{F}}
between MFC
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
of affine Lie algebra type are in relatively good control, thanks to Edie-Michell
[
19
]
(building on
[
34
,
36
]
), who completes this for
\mathfrak{g}=A_{r},B_{r},C_{r}
and
G_{2}
at all levels. The analogous work for the simple-current extensions of
\mathfrak{g}=A_{r}
is completed in
[
20
]
. Extending this to all
\mathfrak{g}
should be immanent. The result is that almost all equivalences come from simple-currents and outer automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Thus the biggest uncertainty in the classification of module categories for the
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
is determining the possible extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. This is the question addressed in this paper. It is also the part of the module category classification which is of direct importance to the VOA community. Until this paper, the answer was known only for
\mathfrak{g}=A_{1}
and
\mathfrak{g}=A_{2}
, though Ocneanu has announced
[
50
]
progress for other
A_{r}
without supplying a proof.

We show that almost all extensions of the VOAs
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
are simple-current extensions. By an
exotic extension
of
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
we mean one which is not a simple-current one. As we shall see, a relatively common source of exotic extensions are those of Lie type, where
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
is also an affine Lie algebra VOA – an example is
{\mathcal{V}}(A_{1},10)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(C_{2},1)
. These are well-understood.

There is a contravariant metaphor between finite groups and rational VOAs, where quotients of groups correspond to extensions of VOAs. In this metaphor, the results of this paper are analogous to the foundational group theoretic result that given a finite group of Lie type, e.g.
SL_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, the quotient by the centre is almost always simple. The level corresponds to the finite field, and the simple-currents to the centre. Our result is analogous: after extending
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
by simple-currents, the resulting VOA is almost always
completely anisotropic
(i.e. cannot be further extended).

Pushing this metaphor a little further, recall that the generic finite simple group is of Lie type. It is tempting then to guess that the MFC of a completely anisotropic VOA will generically be ‘of Lie type’, whatever that may mean (presumably include arbitrary Deligne products of arbitrary Galois associates of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
). Compare this to Remark 6.6 in
[
12
]
.

The finite subgroups of SU(2) fall into an A-D-E classification, like the module categories for
A_{1}
. For this reason, Ocneanu
[
50
]
introduced the term
quantum subgroup
. More precisely, a
quantum module
denotes any module category
({\mathcal{V}}_{L}^{e},{\mathcal{V}}_{R}^{e},{\mathcal{F}})
for the MFC
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, whilst a quantum subgroup denotes those of pure extension type
({\mathcal{V}}^{e},{\mathcal{V}}^{e},\mathrm{Id})
. The latter are in natural bijection with the
connected étale algebras
of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
.

The basic combinatorial invariant of a MFC is the
modular data
, a unitary matrix representation
\rho
of the modular group SL
{}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. It is generated by a symmetric matrix
S=\rho\left({0\atop 1}{-1\atop 0}\right)
(coming from the Hopf link) and a diagonal matrix
T=\rho\left({1\atop 0}{1\atop 1}\right)
(coming from the ribbon twist). For
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, the
S
-entries come from Lie group characters evaluated at elements of finite order, and the
T
-entries from the second Casimir.

Fix a quantum subgroup of Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
, i.e. an extension
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
of
{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
M
be any simple
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
-module. Then
M
restricts to a
{\mathcal{V}}
-module

\mathrm{Res}(M)\cong\oplus_{\mu}B_{M,\mu}\mu
, where the
\mu
are simple
{\mathcal{V}}
-modules and the multiplicities
B_{M,\mu}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
form the
branching rule matrix
. The branching rules intertwine the modular data of
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
:

BS=S^{e}B\,,\ \ BT=T^{e}B\,.

(1.1)

Hence
{\mathcal{Z}}=B^{t}B
commutes with
S
and
T
(recall that
S
and
S^{e}
are symmetric), and is called the
modular invariant
of the quantum subgroup. Moreover,
B_{M,\mathbf{1}}=\delta_{M,\mathbf{1}^{e}}
, where
\mathbf{1}={\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathbf{1}^{e}={\mathcal{V}}^{e}
are the tensor units in the MFC Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
and Mod
({\mathcal{V}}^{e})
respectively.

The physics literature (e.g.
[
8
]
) emphasized these modular invariants, expressed as the formal generating function (partition function)
\sum_{\lambda,\mu}{\mathcal{Z}}_{\lambda,\mu}\chi_{\lambda}\overline{\chi_{\mu}}
. But inequivalent quantum subgroups can have identical modular invariants (see Section 6), and seemingly healthy modular invariants may not be realized by a quantum subgroup (e.g. at
B_{4}
level 2).
Nevertheless, there is close to a bijection between the modular invariants and the module categories, at least for
\mathfrak{g}
of small rank, though this coincidence plays no role in our analysis.

Restriction has an adjoint, called
(alpha-)induction
. More precisely, each extension
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
of
{\mathcal{V}}
corresponds to a commutative algebra object
{\mathcal{A}}
in Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
: the object is
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathrm{Res}({\mathcal{V}}^{e})
and multiplication
\mu\in\mathrm{Hom}({\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}})
comes from the vertex operator structure
Y^{e}(u,z)v
on
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
. A quantum subgroup
{\mathcal{A}}
of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
fails to be exotic iff
{\mathcal{A}}
is a sum of invertible objects (simple-currents) in
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
.
Induction is a tensor functor taking Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
to the fusion category Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
of
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules. In terms of the contravariant metaphor,
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules for
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
are the analogues here of projective representations for a group
G
; just as projective representations become ordinary representations for some extension of
G
, so these
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules become ordinary VOA modules for the subVOA
{\mathcal{V}}
. Restriction Rep
({\mathcal{A}})\to\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}})
is the forgetful functor.
The MFC Mod
({\mathcal{V}}^{e})
is the full subcategory of
local
or
dyslectic

{\mathcal{A}}
-modules, which we also denote by Rep
({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
. So local
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules correspond to projective representations with trivial multiplier. Restriction limited to Rep
({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
is what we call
B
.

The following observation is the starting point to how induction constrains quantum subgroups, in the affine Lie algebra categories
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. Let
\lambda^{*}
denote the dual of
\lambda
(more precisely,
\lambda^{*}
is the highest weight of the contragredient module
L(\lambda)^{\vee}
of
\mathfrak{g}
),
\rho
the Weyl vector of
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\kappa=k+h^{\vee}
the shifted level. Here
(\lambda|\mu)
denote the usual invariant bilinear form of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Ocneanu’s Lemma.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{V}}^{e}
be a quantum subgroup of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. Let
\lambda
be a level
k
highest weight satisfying
(\lambda+\lambda^{*}+2\rho|\lambda+\lambda^{*})<2\kappa
. Then
Ind
(\lambda)

is a simple
{\mathcal{A}}
-module.

It should be repeated that the area has been profoundly influenced by the subfactor community, and Ocneanu in particular. See e.g.
[
25
,
50
,
51
]
for an abundance of ideas. In particular,
Ocneanu realised that the Lemma implies that, for any
\mathfrak{g}
, there is a threshold
K(\mathfrak{g})
such that, for any level
k>K(\mathfrak{g})
, no extension of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
is exotic
[
50
]
. Unfortunately the details of his argument never appeared, nor did an estimate for
K(\mathfrak{g})
. To our knowledge, the first published proof appears implicitly in
[
57
]
.
In that important paper, Schopieray found explicit estimates for
K(\mathfrak{g})
when
\mathfrak{g}
is rank 2: in particular,
19\,896
for
C_{2}
and
18\,271\,135
for
G_{2}
. His method should extend to all Lie algebras, with an estimate for
K(\mathfrak{g})
which grows exponentially with the square of the rank of
\mathfrak{g}
. These bounds are far too large to be used in classification efforts.

Fortunately Schopieray’s estimates can be tightened significantly. Here we couple Ocneanu’s Lemma to the Galois symmetry of modular data
[
10
]
, to find
\lambda
such that Ind
(\lambda)
is both simple and local. This gives much tighter thresholds.
Our main result, proved in Section 4, is:

Main Theorem.

For any simple
\mathfrak{g}
there exists a threshold
K(\mathfrak{g})
such that, for any level
k>K(\mathfrak{g})
, any quantum subgroup of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
must be a simple-current extension. As the rank
r\to\infty
,
K(\mathfrak{g})\in O(r^{3+\epsilon})
for all
\epsilon>0
.

We also address uniqueness: for a given object
{\mathcal{A}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)

{\mathcal{A}}

Incidentally, none of the MFC Mod
({\mathcal{V}}^{e})=\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
of these exotic extensions deserve the name exotic. On the other hand, there are MFC we would call exotic (e.g. the doubles of the fusion categories of Haagerup type

Surely, when rank
>6
, most exotic quantum subgroups will continue to be Lie-type conformal embeddings. We expect that, given any simple
\mathfrak{g}
, there are at most four or five levels where all their exotic quantum subgroups reside. For example,
\mathfrak{g}=E_{7}
appears to have exactly three exotic quantum subgroups (namely at

, two of which are of Lie type) and
\mathfrak{g}=E_{8}
appears to have only one (at
k=30
, of Lie type). In short, apart from simple-current extensions and Lie-type conformal embeddings (both of which are completely understood), all other extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
appear to be extremely rare, with very few in each rank, but perhaps infinitely many as one runs over all ranks.

\mathfrak{g}
is semisimple largely reduce to those of the simple summands, using a contragredient analogue of Goursat’s Lemma. More precisely, Theorem 3.6 in

respectively, and a braided tensor equivalence

. We learn in this paper that when

, the categories Rep
({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
for any connected étale algebra (quantum subgroup)

in
[

]
.

The Galois associate

of a MFC

is itself a MFC. The quantum subgroups (and quantum modules) of

and

are in natural bijection. For example, the MFC

The connected étale algebras (quantum subgroups) are known for two other large classes of categories.
When the MFC is pointed (i.e. all simples are simple-currents), these algebras obviously must be simple-current extensions and so are under complete control. The MFC Rep

The exotic quantum subgroups

In this section we list all quantum subgroups in the MFC

, or equivalently all conformal extensions of the rational VOA

2.1
Lie theoretic background and notation

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a simple Lie algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. See
[
41
]
for the basic theory of the affine Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}
, which underlies much of the following material. The level
k
highest weights of
\mathfrak{g}^{(1)}
(which parametrise up to equivalence the simple objects in the modular fusion category
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},\kappa)
, and equivalently the simple modules of the VOA
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
) form the set

P_{+}^{k}(\mathfrak{g})=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{r}\,|\,\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{\vee}\lambda_{i}\leq k\right\}

(2.1)

where
a_{i}^{\vee}
are the co-marks of
\mathfrak{g}
. We also write
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\Lambda_{i}
for
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{r})
, where
\Lambda_{i}
are the fundamental weights. Note that for large level
k
,

\|P_{+}^{k}(\mathfrak{g})\|\approx\frac{k^{r}}{r!a_{1}^{\vee}\cdots a_{r}^{\vee}}\,.

(2.2)

Formulas are often simplified by shifting these by the Weyl vector
\rho=(1,1,\ldots,1)
and shifting the level by the dual Coxeter number,
\kappa=k+h^{\vee}
: hence define

P_{\!++}^{\,\kappa}(\mathfrak{g})=\rho+P_{+}^{k}(\mathfrak{g})
.
We denote the tensor unit in
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
by
\mathbf{1}
, which in
P_{\!++}^{\,\kappa}(\mathfrak{g})
is
(1,1,\ldots,1)
.
The dual (contragredient) of
\lambda
is denoted
\lambda^{*}
or
C\lambda
.

Scale the invariant bilinear form
(\lambda|\mu)
on
\mathfrak{g}
so the highest root has norm-squared 2, and use it to identify the Cartan subalgebra and its dual, as in Section 6.2 of
[
41
]
. Then the roots and coroots are related by
a_{i}\alpha_{i}=a_{i}^{\vee}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
, and the
fundamental weights
\Lambda_{i}
satisfy
(\Lambda_{i}|\alpha_{j}^{\vee})=\delta_{i,j}
. For
\lambda\in P_{\!++}^{\,\kappa}(\mathfrak{g})
write

\lambda_{0}=\kappa-\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}^{\vee}\lambda_{i}\,.

(2.3)

The central charge of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
is
c=k\,\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{g})/\kappa
. Its modular data is

\displaystyle T_{\lambda,\mu}=\,

\displaystyle\delta_{\lambda,\mu}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(h_{\lambda}-c/24)}

(2.4)

\displaystyle S_{\lambda,\mu}=\,

\displaystyle x\sum_{w\in W}\mathrm{det}(w)\exp]\left(-2\pi\mathrm{i}\,\frac{(\lambda|w(\mu))}{\kappa}\right)

(2.5)

where
h_{\lambda}=((\lambda|\lambda)-(\rho|\rho))/(2\kappa)
is the
conformal weight
,
W
is the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
and
x
is the square-root of a positive rational. Next subsection we spell this out in full detail for
\mathfrak{g}=A_{r}
.
Useful is

(\rho|\rho)=h^{\vee}\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{g})/12\,.

(2.6)

For any simple
\mathfrak{g}
, define
f_{\mathfrak{g}}
to be the smallest positive integer
f
for which
f\rho
is a sum of coroots. In particular,
f_{G_{2}}=3
,
f_{E_{6}}=f_{E_{8}}=1
,
f_{A_{r}}=1
when
r
is even,
f_{D_{r}}=1
when
r\equiv 0,1
(mod 4), and
f_{\mathfrak{g}}=2
otherwise.

The
simple-currents
are the invertible objects in
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. They are classified in
[
27
]
.
With one exception (
E_{8}
at
k=2

in Section 1.3 of
[

) of the weight. This coincides with

\lambda\mapsto J\otimes\lambda=:J\lambda

As in any MFC, associated to a simple-current

(2.7)

3
Background

3.1
Modular fusion categories

The standard reference for tensor categories is
[
22
]
.
A
fusion category
has direct sums
\lambda\oplus\mu
, tensor (also called fusion) products
\lambda\otimes\mu
, a tensor unit
\mathbf{1}
, and duals
\lambda^{*}
. It
is semi-simple with finitely many isomorphism classes
[\lambda]
of simple objects, the Hom-spaces are finite-dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{C}
, and End
(\mathbf{1})=\mathbb{C}
.
A
modular fusion category (MFC)
, also called a
modular tensor category
, is a fusion category
\mathcal{C}
with a braiding
c_{\lambda,\mu}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda\otimes\mu,\mu\otimes\lambda)
satisfying a certain nondegeneracy condition. Examples of MFCs are
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is a simple Lie algebra and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. These are discussed in Section 2.

Associated with any MFC is
modular data
, an SL
{}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
-representation

\rho
on the formal
\mathbb{C}
-span of the (equivalence classes of) simple objects. The matrix
T=\rho\left({1\atop 0}{1\atop 1}\right)
is diagonal and
S=\rho\left({0\atop 1}{-1\atop 0}\right)
is unitary and symmetric. More precisely, given a MFC,
\rho
is uniquely defined up to tensoring by a one-dimensional representation: the
S
-matrix is uniquely determined up to a global sign (which we can naturally fix by requiring that
S
has a strictly positive row) and
T
is then well-defined up to an arbitrary third root of 1. The
ribbon twist

\theta(\lambda)=T_{[\lambda],[\lambda]}\overline{T_{[\mathbf{1}],[\mathbf{1}]}}
is independent of these choices. Define
C_{[\lambda],[\mu]}:=\delta_{[\lambda^{*}],[\mu]}
. Then
S^{2}=C
and

S_{[\lambda^{*}],[\mu]}=\overline{S_{[\lambda],[\mu]}}
. All examples we study are
pseudo-unitary
, i.e.
S_{\lambda,\mathbf{1}}>0
for all
\lambda
, which simplifies things somewhat.

Fix once and for all a representative
\lambda
for each class
[\lambda]
. Let Fus
({\mathcal{C}})
denote the
fusion ring
(Grothendieck ring) of
{\mathcal{C}}
.
By semi-simplicity, the product decomposes as
\lambda\otimes\mu\cong\oplus_{\nu}N_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}\nu
, where the multiplicities
N_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
are called fusion coefficients.
Verlinde’s formula says

N_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}=\sum_{\phi}S_{\lambda,\phi}S_{\mu,\phi}\overline{S_{\nu,\phi}}/S_{\mathbf{1},\phi}\,,

(3.1)

where we sum over the representatives
\phi
.
Combining this with the relation
(ST)^{3}=S^{2}
yields
[
7
]

S_{\lambda,\mu}=\theta(\lambda)\,\theta(\mu)\sum_{\nu}\overline{\theta(\nu)}N_{\lambda,\mu}^{\nu}S_{\mathbf{1},\nu}\,.

(3.2)

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any MFC.
A
simple-current
is a simple object
\lambda
in
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\lambda\otimes\lambda^{*}\cong\mathbf{1}
. Using the tensor product, the (equivalence classes of) simple-currents in
{\mathcal{C}}
form an abelian group denoted
{\mathcal{J}}({\mathcal{C}})
. Recall (
2.7
).

By a
fusion subring

F
of the fusion ring Fus
({\mathcal{C}})
of an MFC
{\mathcal{C}}
, we mean the formal
\mathbb{Z}
-span of any collection of (equivalence classes of) simples in
{\mathcal{C}}
which is closed under tensor product. For example, the
\mathbb{Z}
-span
\mathbb{Z}{\mathcal{J}}
of any subgroup
{\mathcal{J}}\leq{\mathcal{J}}({\mathcal{C}})
of simple-currents is one. By Corollary 4.11.4 of
[
22
]
, a fusion subring is also closed under taking duals.

The
centralizer

C_{\mathcal{C}}(P)
of a subset
P\subseteq\mathrm{Irr}({\mathcal{C}})
is all
\lambda\in\mathrm{Irr}({\mathcal{C}})
satisfying

\frac{S_{\lambda,\mu}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mu}}=\frac{S_{\lambda,\mathbf{1}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\ \ \forall\mu\in P\,.

(3.3)

Then
\lambda\in C_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)
iff
\mu\in C_{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda)
.
An important example is, for any
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq{\mathcal{J}}({\mathcal{C}})
,

C_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{J}})=\{\lambda\in\mathrm{Irr}({\mathcal{C}})\,|\,\varphi_{J}(\lambda)=\varphi_{J}(\mathbf{1})\ \forall J\in{\mathcal{J}}\}\,.

(3.4)

Proposition 3.1.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any pseudo-unitary MFC and
P
any subset of Irr
({\mathcal{C}})
. Then the
\mathbb{Z}
-span
\mathbb{Z}C_{\mathcal{C}}(P)
is a fusion subring of Fus
({\mathcal{C}})
.

Proof.
For any
\nu\in{\mathcal{C}}
, let
N_{\nu}
denote the (non-negative) matrix
(N_{\nu})_{\nu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime}}=N_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}^{\nu^{\prime\prime}}
corresponding to tensoring in Fus
({\mathcal{C}})
by
\nu
. Then Verlinde’s formula (
3.1
) tells us the eigenvalues of
N_{\nu}
are
S_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}/S_{\mathbf{1},\nu^{\prime}}
, with corresponding eigenvector the
\nu^{\prime}
column of
S
. Hence,
S_{\nu,\mathbf{1}}/S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}
is the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue, corresponding as it does to a strictly positive eigenvector, so

\frac{S_{\nu,\mathbf{1}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\geq\left|\frac{S_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\nu^{\prime}}}\right|\ \ \forall\nu^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}\,.

(3.5)

For any
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in C_{\mathcal{C}}(P)
and
\mu\in P
, we find

\sum_{\nu}N_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}^{\nu}\frac{S_{\nu,\mu}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mu}}=\frac{S_{\lambda,\mu}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mu}}\frac{S_{\lambda^{\prime},\mu}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mu}}=\frac{S_{\lambda,\mathbf{1}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\frac{S_{\lambda^{\prime},\mathbf{1}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}=\sum_{\nu}N_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}^{\nu}\frac{S_{\nu,\mathbf{1}}}{S_{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{1}}}\,.

Applying the triangle inequality and (
3.5
) to this, we find that
\nu\in C_{\mathcal{C}}(P)
whenever the fusion coefficient
N_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}^{\nu}\neq 0
. In other words, the span
\mathbb{Z}C_{\mathcal{C}}(P)
is closed under fusion products.

QED to Proposition 3.1

All fusion subrings for
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
were classified by Sawin
[
55
]
. We need:

Theorem 3.2.
(Sawin
[

]
)
Any fusion subring of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
is of the form
\mathbb{Z}{\mathcal{J}}
or
\mathbb{Z}C_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{J}})
, unless

or

3.2
Vertex operator algebras

For the basic theory and terminology of vertex operator algebras (VOAs), see e.g.
[
45
]
.
All VOAs
{\mathcal{V}}
in this paper are
completely rational
, i.e.
{\mathcal{V}}
is
C_{2}
-cofinite, regular, simple, self-dual and of CFT-type. Huang
[
39
]
proved that the category Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
of modules of a completely rational
{\mathcal{V}}
is a MFC. The tensor unit
\mathbf{1}
is
{\mathcal{V}}
itself.

Unlike the modular data of a MFC, the modular data of a VOA is uniquely determined.
In any
{\mathcal{V}}
-module
M
, the Virasoro operator
L_{0}
is diagonalizable with rational eigenvalues. The minimal eigenvalue exists and is called the
conformal weight

h_{M}
. Then the
T
-matrix is
T_{M,N}=\delta_{M,N}\exp(2\pi i(h_{M}-c/24))
where
c\in\mathbb{Q}
is the
central charge
of
{\mathcal{V}}
(
c
fixes the third root of 1 for
T
). The one-point functions of
{\mathcal{V}}
form a vector-valued modular form for the modular data
\rho
; most importantly, this holds for the graded-dimensions (characters)
\chi_{M}(\tau)
of the
{\mathcal{V}}
-modules.

All VOAs considered here (both the
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
and their conformal extensions) have the property that their conformal weights
h_{M}
for simple
M
are
\geq 0
, and
h_{M}=0
only for
M={\mathcal{V}}
. It is easy to show from
\chi_{M}(-1/\tau)=\sum_{N}S_{M,N}\chi_{N}(\tau)
that Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
for such
{\mathcal{V}}
is pseudo-unitary:

0\leq\lim_{q\to 1^{-}}\frac{\chi_{M}(q)}{\chi_{\mathcal{V}}(q)}=\lim_{q\to 0^{+}}\frac{\sum_{P}S_{M,P}\chi_{P}(q)}{\sum_{P}S_{{\mathcal{V}},P}\chi_{P}(q)}=\frac{S_{M,{\mathcal{V}}}}{S_{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{V}}}}\,.

We are interested in the VOAs

3.3
Algebras in categories and conformal extensions

One can think of a fusion category (resp. MFC) as a categorification of (commutative) rings. In the same sense, a categorification of their modules is called
a
module category
. The formal definition is given in
[
52
]
; see also Chapter 7 of
[
22
]
. There are obvious notions of equivalence and direct sums
of module categories, and of indecomposability.

An algebra in say a MFC
{\mathcal{C}}
consists of an object
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, a multiplication
\mu\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}})
satisfying the associativity constraint, and a unit
e\in\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{1},{\mathcal{A}})
. We can speak of commutative algebras, as well as (left) modules of
{\mathcal{A}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
, in the obvious way. Again, see
[
52
,
22
]
for formal definitions.
The Main Theorem of
[
52
]
says that each indecomposable module category of
{\mathcal{C}}
is equivalent to the category Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
of left modules in
{\mathcal{C}}
of some associative algebra
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with unit. We are interested though in module categories of pure extension type (recall the discussion in the Introduction). The corresponding algebras are étale:

Definition 3.4.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a MFC. We call
{\mathcal{A}}
an
étale algebra
for
{\mathcal{C}}
if
{\mathcal{A}}
is a commutative associative algebra in
{\mathcal{C}}
and the category Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
is semisimple. We call
{\mathcal{A}}
connected
if dim Hom
(\mathbf{1},{\mathcal{A}})=1
. For
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, a connected étale algebra is also called a
quantum subgroup.

The quantum subgroups of
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
are the basic objects we wish to identify. In pseudo-unitary categories like
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
, a connected étale algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
always has trivial ribbon twist, i.e.
\lambda\in{\mathcal{A}}
implies
\theta(\lambda)=1
(Lemma 2.2.4 in
[
57
]
). We assume pseudo-unitarity throughout this paper.

Étale algebras go by other names in the literature – e.g.
[
54
]
calls them commutative symmetric special Frobenius algebras.
Any
{\mathcal{C}}
always has at least one connected étale algebra:
{\mathcal{A}}=\mathbf{1}
. Any étale algebra is canonically a direct sum of connected ones. Any étale algebra is necessarily self-dual:
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}^{*}
(see Remark 3.4 of
[
12
]
). An intrinsic characterization of
{\mathcal{A}}
for which Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
is semisimple, is that
{\mathcal{A}}
be
separable
(see Proposition 2.7 in
[
12
]
). Section 3 of
[
12
]
, as well as
[
43
]
, collect many properties of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

When
{\mathcal{A}}
is connected étale, Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
is a fusion category (see Section 3.3 of
[
12
]
). In Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
, write
M\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}N
and Hom
{}_{\mathcal{A}}(M,N)
.

Let Rep
({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
be the full subcategory of
local
(dyslectic)
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules
[
43
]
. A simple
{\mathcal{A}}
-module
M
is local iff the ribbon twist
\theta(M)
is scalar (Theorem 3.2 of
[
43
]
), i.e. all
\lambda\in M
have the same value of
\theta(\lambda)
. Although Rep
({\mathcal{A}})
is usually not braided, Rep
({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
will be a MFC (Theorem 4.5 of
[
43
]
).

Restriction

\mathrm{Res:Rep}({\mathcal{A}})\to{\mathcal{C}}
is the forgetful functor sending an
{\mathcal{A}}
-module to the underlying object in
{\mathcal{C}}
. It has an adjoint Ind
:{\mathcal{C}}\to\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})
, called
(alpha-)induction
sending
\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
to a canonical
{\mathcal{A}}
-module structure on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\lambda
(Section 1 of
[
43
]
). For all
{\mathcal{A}}
-modules
M
and
\lambda\in{\mathcal{C}}
, we have

\mathrm{Hom}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(\mathrm{Ind}\,\lambda,M)=\mathrm{Hom}(\lambda,\mathrm{Res}\,M)\,.\

(3.6)

Moreover, Ind is a tensor functor, Ind(
\lambda^{*})=(\mathrm{Ind}\,\lambda)^{*}
, and

\mathrm{Res(Ind}(\lambda)\otimes_{{\mathcal{A}}}M)=\lambda\otimes\mathrm{Res}\,M\,.

(3.7)

Recall the notion of conformal embedding
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
of VOAs from last subsection.
Our starting point is the observation that equivalence classes of conformal extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}
are in bijection with equivalence classes of connected étale algebras in Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
. Two étale algebras
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
are equivalent if there is an isomorphism
\phi\in\mathrm{Hom}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
intertwining in the obvious way the maps defining unit, multiplication etc. This requires Res
\,{\mathcal{A}}\cong\mathrm{Res}\,{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
, but that is not sufficient (e.g. at the end of Section 3.4 we describe a situation where the inequivalent algebras with the same restriction are parametrized by
H^{2}(G;\mathbb{C}^{\times})
for some group

). The key result is:

Theorem 3.5.

[
43
,
40
,
11

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
be a completely rational VOA with MFC
{\mathcal{C}}=\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}})
. Assume the conformal weights
h_{\lambda}
are positive for all simple
{\mathcal{V}}
-modules
\lambda\not\cong\mathbf{1}={\mathcal{V}}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

a conformal extension
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
of
{\mathcal{V}}
;

(ii)

a connected étale algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}\cong{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
as objects in
{\mathcal{C}}
(with multiplication coming from the vertex operators in
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
).

Moreover, the MFC Mod
({\mathcal{V}}^{e})
is equivalent to
\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}
. The forgetful functor
\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})^{\mathrm{loc}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
restricts
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
-modules to
{\mathcal{V}}
-modules. Two algebras

are equivalent iff the corresponding extensions

Note that thanks to restriction, the conformal weights of
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}
-modules are a subset of those of the
{\mathcal{V}}
-modules, so Mod

is pseudo-unitary since Mod

-modules are a solitonic generalization of the notion of VOA module – see Remark 2.7 in

For example, let
{\mathcal{W}}
be a holomorphic VOA (i.e. one with Mod

{\mathcal{A}}
-module

A more pertinent example is the extension

3.5
The Galois symmetry

Recall that
\xi_{n}=\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}/n)
. In this subsection we restrict to the MFCs
{\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. Recall that the coroot lattice
Q^{\vee}
is even, and its dual
Q^{\vee*}
is the weight lattice. As in Section 2.1,
{W}
is the (finite) Weyl group and
\rho
is the Weyl vector. We see from (

S
-entries

for

manifestly live in the cyclotomic field

and

is such that

The Galois group Gal(

Crucial for us is a Galois symmetry

(a)

(b)

4.1
The algebra constraint

The condition that

is equivalent to the condition that

equals 0 only for

be the minimum of

as we run over all candidates

Ocneanu’s Lemma 4.1.
Let

be a quantum subgroup for

for some
\lambda\in P^{\,\kappa}_{\!++}(\mathfrak{g})
, then Ind
(\lambda)\in\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})

Proof.
Write
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. By Lemma 2.4 of
[
52
]

\mathrm{dim\,End}_{\mathrm{Rep}({\mathcal{A}})}(\mathrm{Ind}(\lambda))=\mathrm{dim\,Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda,{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\lambda)=\mathrm{dim\,Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(\lambda\otimes\lambda^{*},{\mathcal{A}})\,.

Hence if Ind
(\lambda)
is not simple, then there would exist some
\mu\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
\mu\neq\mathbf{1}
, such that
\mu
appears in the decomposition of the fusion product
\lambda\otimes\lambda^{*}
. In particular,
\mu=\lambda+\lambda^{*}-\rho-\alpha
for some root vector

. Since
\theta(\mu)=1
and
\mu\neq\mathbf{1}
, we have
(\mu|\mu)\geq 2\kappa+(\rho|\rho)
. But for
\nu\in P^{\,\kappa}_{\!++}(\mathfrak{g})
and any simple root

of
\mathfrak{g}

(\nu|\nu)-(\nu-\alpha_{j}|\nu-\alpha_{j})>2(\nu-\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{j})\geq 2(\rho-\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{j})=2a_{j}^{\vee}/a_{j}-2a_{j}^{\vee}/a_{j}=0

The first inequality is because
(\alpha_{j}|\alpha_{j})>0
; the second is because
\nu-\rho
is a sum of fundamental weights.
So the norm of
\mu
is bounded above by that of
\lambda+\lambda^{*}-\rho
.
QED to Ocneanu’s Lemma

5.3

Section 5.1 shows that any exotic quantum subgroup for

occurs at 11 possible pairs

matches one of Lie type (not just those 11 examples).
We work at the level of VOAs; equivalence of VOA extensions are defined in Section 3.2.

Lie-type conformal extensions

is a Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
, so
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)\subset{\mathcal{W}}_{{\mathrm{Lie}}}
. Thus the central charges satisfy
c({\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k))\leq c({\mathcal{W}}_{{\mathrm{Lie}}})\leq c({\mathcal{W}})
. But
c({\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k))=c({\mathcal{W}})
by hypothesis, so
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)\subset{\mathcal{W}}_{{\mathrm{Lie}}}\subset{\mathcal{W}}
are all conformal. The restriction of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
to
\mathfrak{g}
is obtained from Res
\,\mathbf{1}^{e}
by deleting from it all simple
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
-submodules with conformal weight
h_{\lambda}>1
, and replacing
\mathbf{1}
with the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}
; as the restriction of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
to
\mathfrak{g}
is obtained similarly, the restrictions of
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
agree. Hence the dimensions of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
and
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
are equal. Running through the list of
[
4
,
56
]
, we see that only
(\mathfrak{g},k)=(A_{5},6),(A_{7},10),(B_{4},2)
have multiple Lie-type conformal extensions, but the dimensions of the extended Lie algebras disagree. Thus
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\cong\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
as Lie algebras, and
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\cong{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g}^{e},1)
as VOAs. Since the restrictions of
{\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lie}}
and
{\mathcal{W}}
to
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)
must agree,
{\mathcal{W}}={\mathcal{W}}_{\mathrm{Lie}}\cong{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g}^{e},1)
as VOAs.

We need to show the associated Lie algebra embeddings
\iota_{1}:\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{e}
and
\iota_{2}:\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}
are VOA-equivalent.
Following
[
18
,
48
]
, we call embeddings
\iota_{i}

linearly conjugate
if for any representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}^{e}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, the restrictions
\rho\circ\iota_{i}
are equivalent as representations of
\mathfrak{g}
. By Theorem 1.5 of
[
18
]
and Theorem 2 of
[
48
]
, our
\iota_{i}
are linearly conjugate. More precisely, when
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\not\cong D_{r}
for any
r
, it suffices to confirm this for
\rho
being the first fundamental representation
\Lambda_{1}
. For
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\cong A_{r},B_{r},C_{r},E_{6}
,
\Lambda_{1}\in P_{+}^{1}(\mathfrak{g}^{e})
so is included in the branching rules. For
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\cong E_{7},E_{8},G_{2},F_{4}
,
\Lambda_{1}
is the adjoint representation of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
and the restriction of that to
\mathfrak{g}
equals the
h_{\lambda}=1
part of Res
\,\mathbf{1}^{e}
, as described last paragraph. When
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\cong D_{r}
, we see that
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{r}\in P_{+}^{1}(\mathfrak{g}^{e})
suffices.

Theorem 3 of
[
48
]
now tells us
\iota_{i}
are conjugate (hence VOA-equivalent) when either
\mathfrak{g}\cong A_{1}
, or Im
\,\iota_{i}
are both
regular subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}^{e}
, or
\mathfrak{g}^{e}\cong A_{r},B_{r},C_{r},G_{2},F_{4}
for some
r
. In fact, if one of Im
\,\iota_{i}
is a regular subalgebra, so must be the other, since
\mathfrak{g}\subset\mathfrak{g}^{e}
is regular for a conformal extension
{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g},k)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g}^{e},1)
iff rank
\,\mathfrak{g}=\mathrm{rank}\,\mathfrak{g}^{e}
.

For
\mathfrak{g}^{e}=D_{r}
, Theorem 4 of
[
48
]
says that, because
\iota_{i}
are linearly conjugate, if they are not conjugate then
\iota_{2}=\alpha\circ\iota_{1}
where
\alpha
is outer (i.e. not inner). But in this case
\iota_{i}
are clearly VOA-equivalent (necessarily
\alpha
here would permute
P_{+}^{k}(\mathfrak{g}^{e})
but preserve restriction).

The only conformal embeddings into
E_{6}
for which linear conjugates aren’t necessarily conjugates, are
{\mathcal{V}}(A_{2},9)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(E_{6},1)
and
{\mathcal{V}}(G_{2},3)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(E_{6},1)
, but in both cases the embeddings coincide up to an outer automorphism of
E_{6}
and so are VOA-equivalent (Theorem 11.1 in
[
18
]
, Corollary 3 of
[
48
]
). For conformal embeddings into
E_{7}
and
E_{8}
, linear conjugacy implies conjugacy and hence VOA-equivalence.

QED to Theorem 5.4

For example, the two extensions
{\mathcal{V}}(D_{8},1)\subset{\mathcal{V}}(E_{8},1)
are distinguished by Res
\,\mathbf{1}^{e}
. The requirement that
\mathfrak{g}
be simple is only used in showing that
{\mathcal{W}}\cong{\mathcal{V}}(\mathfrak{g}^{e},1)
.

Thanks to Step 4, Theorem 5.4 proves that all exotics for

are of Lie type, and are unique. Compare our argument to that of

]
for

Recall that a module category for Mod
({\mathcal{V}})
is a triple
({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L},{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R},{\mathcal{F}})
where
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L,R}
are extensions of
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}:\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L})\to\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R})
is a braided tensor equivalence. Associated to a module category is its modular invariant
{\mathcal{Z}}=B_{R}^{t}\Pi B_{R}
,
where
B_{L,R}
are the branching (restriction) matrices associated to
{\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L,R}\supset{\mathcal{V}}
and
\Pi
is the bijection Irr
(\mathrm{Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{L}))\to\mathrm{Irr(Mod}({\mathcal{V}}^{e}_{R}))
. We identify the modular invariant
{\mathcal{Z}}
with the formal combination
\sum_{\lambda,\mu}{\mathcal{Z}}_{\lambda,\mu}\chi_{\lambda}\overline{\chi_{\mu}}
.

Consider any
{\mathcal{C}}(A_{r},k)
. Write
\bar{r}=r+1
, and choose
\bar{k}=\kappa
if
k\bar{r}
is odd, otherwise put
\bar{k}=k
. Choose any divisor
d
of
\bar{r}
; if
d
is even we require as well that
\bar{r}\bar{k}/d
be even. Then we get a module category with modular invariant

{\mathcal{Z}}[{\mathcal{J}}_{d}]_{\lambda,\mu}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\delta^{d}(t(\lambda)+j\bar{r}\,\bar{k}/(2d))\,\delta_{\mu,J^{j\bar{r}/d}\lambda}

(6.1)

where
\delta^{y}(x)=1
or 0 depending, respectively, on whether or not
x/y\in\mathbb{Z}

by
\langle J^{d^{\prime}}\rangle
where
