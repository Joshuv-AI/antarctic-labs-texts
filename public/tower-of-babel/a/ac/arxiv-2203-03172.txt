Human-State-Aware Controller for a Tethered Aerial Robot Guiding a Human by Physical Interaction
Mike Allenspach, Yash Vyas, Matthias Rubio, Roland Siegwart, Marco Tognon

Abstract
With the rapid development of Aerial Physical Interaction, the possibility to have aerial robots physically interacting with humans is attracting a growing interest. In our one of our previous works [ 1 ] , we considered one of the first systems in which a human is physically connected to an aerial vehicle by a cable. There, we developed a compliant controller that allows the robot to pull the human toward a desired position using forces only as an indirect communication-channel. However, this controller is based on the robot-state only, which makes the system not adaptable to the human behavior, and in particular to their walking speed. This reduces the effectiveness and comfort of the guidance when the human is still far from the desired point. In this paper, we formally analyze the problem and propose a human-state-aware controller that includes a human’s velocity feedback. We theoretically prove and experimentally show that this method provides a more consistent guiding force which enhances the guiding experience.

INTRODUCTION

Aerial
Physical Interaction (APhI) is seeing a constant growth of interest. Motivated by scientific challenges, as well as industrial and economical potentials, the robotics community is conceiving and studying new aerial robots able to interact with and manipulate the environment

Those go from new multi-rotor platforms that are fully-, omni-, and over-actuated

With the advances in manipulation capabilities of aerial robots, it is natural that the research community starts looking with interest at one of the most challenging types of physical interaction: the one with humans
[
6

.
The highly complex dynamics of humans, the instability of aerial robots, and the strict requirement for safety, make
physical Human-Aerial Robot Interaction
(pHARI) an extremely hard to solve, and by far poorly addressed, research problem.
Most of the works presented so far focus on contactless interaction between human and aerial robots.
These investigate

i)

bilateral teleoperation methods to help humans control single and multiple aerial vehicles
[
7
,
8
]
;

ii)

the use of natural communication means like gestures, speech, and gaze direction to let the human interface with the robot
[
9
,
10
]
; or

iii)

the social acceptance of aerial robots
[
11
]
.

Fig. 1:
Representation of the aerial human-tethered guiding system.

[
12
]
presents a preliminary work addressing pHARI, but the interaction with the human is limited to simple contacts.
On the other hand, in
[
1
]
we considered one of the first systems in which a human and an aerial robot are tightly coupled by a physical connection.
Taking inspiration by the literature of tethered aerial vehicles used for physical interaction
[
13
,
14
]
, the human holds a handle which is in turn connected to an aerial vehicle through a cable (see Fig.
1
).
For this system, we designed an admittance-based control strategy that allows the robot to safely “physically guide” the human toward a desired position by exploiting the cable as a force-based communication channel.
Although the community already proposed contactless approaches for guiding humans with aerial vehicles, e.g., based on sound
[
15
]
or vision
[
16
]
, the use of forces as more direct feedback represents a strong novelty that suits for persons with visual/hearing limitations.

In
[
1
]
the robot is shown to be able to steer the human to the desired location, both in theory and real experiments.
However, during the tests we noticed that if the human is very confident and follows the robots with a relatively high walking speed, the felt guiding force would reduce with respect to (w.r.t.) the desired one. In the extreme case, the cable can become slack, effectively making the human stop and wait for a non-zero guiding force.
This clearly represents an undesired behavior that reduces the effectiveness and comfort of the aerial guidance system.
Intuitively, this is because the controller in
[
1
]
is based on the robot-state only and does not consider any feedback w.r.t. the human-state.
This shows the need of more human-related feedback and adaptation laws in robot controllers for pHRI tasks.

This requirement motivated the present work in which we first formally analyze the mentioned problem, finding the mathematical reasoning behind it.
The study of the equilibria and relative stability of the system when the human is pulled by a constant force, formally confirms our first hypothesis: a reduction of the felt force by the human w.r.t. the desired one that depends on the human velocity.
With a better definition and understanding of the problem, we propose a new human-state-aware controller aiming at a zero guiding force error, independently to human walking speed.
In particular, we modify the controller in
[
1
]
to include an additional feedback w.r.t. the human velocity.
Again, by a formal study of the equilibria and stability of the system, we theoretically confirm that when the desired guiding force is constant, the force error asymptotically converges to zero.
Real experiments comparing the two strategies validate the theoretical findings.

II
MODELING

We study a system where a human and an aerial robot physically interact through a cable (see Fig.
1
). The latter connects the vehicle to a handle held by the human.
The system modeling follows the approach in
[
1
]
.
Considering a generic frame
\mathcal{F}_{\star}
, we define by
O_{\star}
its origin and by
\{\bm{x}_{\star},\bm{y}_{\star},\bm{z}_{\star}\}
its unit axes.
We then define an
inertial frame
,
\mathcal{F}_{W}=\{O_{W},\bm{x}_{W},\bm{y}_{W},\bm{z}_{W}\}
with
\bm{z}_{W}
opposite to gravity.

To describe the human-state, we define a
body frame
,
\mathcal{F}_{H}=\{O_{H},\bm{x}_{H},\bm{y}_{H},\bm{z}_{H}\}
, rigidly attached to the handle such that
O_{H}
coincides with the point where the cable is attached. The position and linear velocity of the human expressed in
\mathcal{F}_{W}
are given by the vectors
\bm{p}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{v}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively.
Similarly, we describe the aerial vehicle-state by the one of the
body frame

\mathcal{F}_{R}=\{O_{R},\bm{x}_{R},\bm{y}_{R},\bm{z}_{R}\}
, rigidly attached to it.
Its position and linear velocity are given by the vectors
\bm{p}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{v}_{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively, both expressed in
\mathcal{F}_{W}
.
We neglect the rotational part since it is not relevant for the following derivations.

As commonly done in the state of the art
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, we approximate the human’s dynamics with the one of a mass-spring-damper system:

\displaystyle m_{H}\dot{\bm{v}}_{H}=-\bm{g}_{H}-\bm{B}_{H}\bm{v}_{H}+\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{f}_{g}+\bm{u}_{H},

(1)

where
m_{H}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the symmetric positive definite matrix
\bm{B}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{>0}
are the mass and apparent damping, respectively;
\bm{f}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the cable force applied to the human at
O_{H}
;
\bm{g}_{H}=m_{H}g\bm{z}_{W}
;
g\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the gravitational constant; and
\bm{f}_{g}
is the ground reaction force. Further,
\bm{u}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the sum of all forces applied by the human that generate a translational motion.
We assume that the forces in (
1
) are such that
\dot{\bm{v}}_{H}^{\top}\bm{z}_{W}=\bm{v}_{H}^{\top}\bm{z}_{W}=0
, i.e., the human is constrained on the ground.

To ensure a safe physical interaction with the environment, and so with the human, a common solution consists of using an
admittance control strategy
together with a position controller on the robot side
[
20
]
.
This strategy allows to make the robot behave as a second order system with a specific admittance (mass, stiffness, and damping) subjected to the measured external force acting on the robot.
Under this control scheme, closed loop dynamics of the robot are:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{v}}_{R}=\bm{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(-\bm{B}_{A}\bm{v}_{R}-\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{u}_{A}+\bm{\delta}\right),

(2)

where the two symmetric positive definite matrices
\bm{M}_{A},\bm{B}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}_{>0}
are the virtual inertia and damping of the robot.

\bm{u}_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an additional input that will be defined in the following to achieve the desired control goal.

\bm{\delta}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a bounded variable that takes into account all the tracking errors due to model mismatches and uncertainties, input and state limits, external disturbances, and estimation errors.

Finally, we model the cable as a unilateral spring along its principal direction. Following the Hooke’s law, we write the cable force applied to
O_{H}
as

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{c}

\displaystyle=t_{c}(\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert){\bm{l}_{c}}/{\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert}

(3)

\displaystyle t_{c}(\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}k_{c}(\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert-\bar{l}_{c})&\text{ if }\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert-\bar{l}_{c}>0\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases},

(4)

where
t_{c}(\lVert\bm{l}_{c}\rVert)
represents the cable internal force intensity,
\bm{l}_{c}=\bm{p}_{R}-\bm{p}_{H}
,
\bar{l}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the constant nominal length of the cable, and
k_{c}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the constant elastic coefficient.
The force produced at the other end of the cable, namely to the robot at
O_{R}
, is equal to
-\bm{f}_{c}
as shown in (
2
).

To write the full system dynamics, i.e., human and controlled robot connected by the cable, we define the system state vector as
\bm{x}=[\bm{p}_{H}^{\top}\;\,\bm{v}_{H}^{\top}\;\,\bm{p}_{R}^{\top}\;\,\bm{v}_{R}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{12}
. Considering (
1
) and (
2
), we can write the state dynamics as
\dot{\bm{x}}=\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{u}_{A})
, where:

\displaystyle\bm{f}(\bm{x},\bm{u}_{A})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{H}\\
\frac{1}{m_{H}}\left(-\bm{g}_{H}-\bm{B}_{H}\bm{v}_{H}+\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{f}_{g}\right)\\
\bm{v}_{R}\\
\bm{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(-\bm{B}_{A}\bm{v}_{R}-\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{u}_{A}\right)\end{bmatrix},

(5)

and
\bm{f}_{c}
is computed as in (
3
).
For the sake of deriving the control law and proving the asymptotic stability in nominal conditions, we consider zero human forces
\bm{u}_{H}=\bm{0}
and modeling errors
\bm{\delta}=\bm{0}
. In Sect.
IV-B
we shall discuss the robustness of the method when these conditions are not met.

In
[
1
]

III-A

Equilibria and Stability Analysis under
\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})

III-A
1
Steady state solution

In regular conditions, the following theorem holds:

Theorem 1
.

Given the desired human position
{\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, such that
\bm{z}_{W}^{\top}{\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}=0
, we consider the control law
\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{A}=\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x}):=\bm{K}_{H}\bm{e}_{R}+{\bm{f}_{c}^{r}},

(6)

where

,

,
\bm{e}_{R}={\bm{p}_{R}^{r}}-\bm{p}_{R}
is the robot position error,
{\bm{p}_{R}^{r}}
is the robot reference position, and
{\bm{f}_{c}^{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a constant forcing input, defined as follow

\displaystyle{\bm{f}_{c}^{r}}

\displaystyle={f}_{z}\bm{z}_{W}

(7)

\displaystyle{\bm{p}_{R}^{r}}

\displaystyle={\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}+{\bm{f}_{c}^{r}}\left(\frac{1}{k_{c}}+\frac{\bar{l}_{c}}{\lVert{\bm{f}_{c}^{r}}\rVert}\right),

(8)

with
{f}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then,
the zero velocity equilibrium
\bar{\bm{x}}=[{\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}^{\top}\;\,\bm{0}^{\top}\;\,{\bm{p}_{R}^{r}}^{\top}\;\,\bm{0}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}
of the system (
5
) under the control law (
6
) is asymptotically stable if

.

Thanks to the proof available in
[
1
]
, we know that at steady state the system will converge to
\bar{\bm{x}}
, i.e., the human will be in the desired position.
Nevertheless, during experiments we noticed an undesired behavior during transient, in particular when the human is far from the desired position.
While the human walks following the cable force provided by the robot, the latter is less than the desired one.
Moreover, the force error increases with the human speed increasing.
This makes the guidance less effective.
In extreme cases, the force might be very close to zero or even be zero, forcing the human to stop and wait for the tension on the cable to be re-established.

III-A
2
Steady state solution under constant control input

To better characterize this phenomena, we consider the case in which the human is far from the desired position. In this condition, we saturate
\bm{e}_{R}
in (
6

It results that

Theorem 2
.

Under the condition
\bm{u}_{A}=\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})=\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{H}(t)=\bm{v}_{R}(t)=\bm{v}^{\star}:=\left(\bm{B}_{A}+\bm{B}_{H}\right)^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}{\gamma}_{x}\;\,{\gamma}_{y}\;\,0\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\bm{x}^{\star}(t)
is of equilibrium for the system (
5
) under the condition
\bm{u}_{A}(t)=\bm{\gamma}
.
At equilibrium, accelerations must be indefinitely zero, i.e.,
\dot{\bm{v}}_{R}=\dot{\bm{v}}_{H}=\ddot{\bm{v}}_{R}=\ddot{\bm{v}}_{H}=\bm{0}

Applying this condition to (

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=-\bm{g}_{H}-\bm{B}_{H}\bm{v}_{H}+\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{f}_{g}

(10)

\displaystyle=-\bm{B}_{A}\bm{v}_{R}-\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\dot{\bm{f}}_{c}.

(12)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{\bm{p}^{\prime}_{R}}=\bm{p}_{R}-\bm{p}_{R}^{\star}&{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{R}}=\bm{v}_{R}-\bm{v}^{\star}\\

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\prime}(\bm{x}^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{H}}\\

Therefore let us consider the following Lyapunov function:

\displaystyle V(\bm{x}^{\prime})=V_{1}(\bm{x}^{\prime})+V_{2}(\bm{x}^{\prime})+V_{0},

(19)

where

\displaystyle V_{1}(\bm{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(m_{H}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{H}}^{\top}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{H}}+{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{R}}^{\top}\bm{M}_{A}{{\bm{v}}^{\prime}_{R}}\right)

Remark
.

When
\bm{u}_{A}=\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})=\bm{\gamma}
, from Theorem
2
, we can compute the force that eventually is applied to the human.
Replacing the condition (
9
) into (
5
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{c}=\bm{\gamma}-\bm{B}_{A}\bm{v}^{\star},

(24)

which implies
\lVert\bm{f}_{c}\rVert<\lVert\bm{\gamma}\rVert
, i.e., the human feels a lower force w.r.t. the desired one which reduces the effectiveness of the guiding strategy.
Moreover, the more the human walks fast (modeled as a low damping
\bm{B}_{H}
), the less the force they feel on the cable.
Intuitively, looking at (
2
), the problem comes from the fact that the robot wants to apply the desired force
\bm{u}_{A}
but trying to stay still (due to damping term).
While the human walks, the two actions are opposite and the system converges to a state where the two are in equilibrium, but none of the two objectives are fully satisfied.

To avoid this problem, the robot must consider the human’s speed while performing the guiding task.
Along this line, in the next subsection we propose a modification of (
6
),
\bm{\Gamma}_{H}(\bm{x})
, and we analyze the respective stability properties.

IV
CONTROL

We propose to modify the control action (
6
) as follow:

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{A}=\bm{\Gamma}_{H}(\bm{x})=\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})+\bm{B}_{A}\bm{v}_{H},

(25)

such that, replacing (
25
) into (
2
) yields new robot dynamics:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{v}}_{R}=\bm{M}_{A}^{-1}\left(\bm{B}_{A}(\bm{v}_{H}-\bm{v}_{R})-\bm{f}_{c}+\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})\right).

(26)

Notice that the latter includes a control action proportional to the velocity difference between human and robot.

IV-A

Equilibria and Stability Analysis under
\bm{\Gamma}_{H}(\bm{x})

To keep a symmetry with the previous section, we first shall study the steady state solution of the system under
\bm{\Gamma}_{H}(\bm{x})
for regular conditions and verify that
\bar{\bm{x}}
is still asymptotically stable.
We then consider the case when
\bm{\Gamma}(\bm{x})=\bm{\gamma}
to show that
\bm{\Gamma}_{H}(\bm{x})
solves the problem of reduced guiding force, which in turn results in a more continuous guidance.

IV-A
1
Steady state solution

Given the new controller (

Theorem 3
.

Given the human position reference
{\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\bm{z}_{W}^{\top}{\bm{p}_{H}^{r}}=0
and
{\bm{p}_{R}^{r}}

Experimental Setup

The employed system is shown in Fig.

A measure of the cable force

Position and velocity estimates

With increasing average walking speed however, larger force errors can be observed in Fig.

Furthermore, an increase in both velocity and force error standard deviation indicates an increasingly oscillatory behavior.
When the human walks slowly, the guidance force will raise toward the desired value (force error decreases). However, it in turn makes the human speed-up.
The robot, having no feedback on the human speed, will keep its current motion, thus making the guiding force reduce again (force error increases).
This reduction can make the cable go slack, eventually forcing the human to slow down or even stop until tension is re-established. The full cycle will then repeat.

On the other hand, the proposed human velocity feedback controller

Not only that, the almost constant standard deviations shown in Fig.

The overall user experience is more comfortable.

VI
CONCLUSIONS

This article discusses the control of a system composed of an aerial robot physically coupled by a cable to the hand of a human being.
The control problem consists in making the robot capable to safely guide the human to a desired point using forces only as an indirect communication channel.
A first compliant control law that ensure the asymptotic stability of the desired human position was already developed in a previous work.
This paper aims at addressing some of the encountered issues linked to the absence of any feedback nor adaptation to the human-state.
In particular, we noticed the cable force being less than the desired value when the human walks at a considerable speed.

First, the suggested decrease in cable force with increasing human walking speed under saturated control input is formally analyzed.

In turn, the preexisting controller is modified to include additional feedback w.r.t. the human velocity. This reduces the guiding force error and makes the guidance more efficient. Indeed, formal study of the equilibria and stability confirms asymptotic convergence to zero force error. Furthermore, the system is passive w.r.t. bounded human intent forces and tracking errors due to non-idealities.

The theoretical findings are validated in real-world flight tests with a group of three subjects.
Experiments that mimic different human damping confirm the consistent and overall improved force tracking performance of the novel controller.
Cable tension is maintained at all times, thus making the guidance more efficient and comfortable for the human.

Although the proposed control law was successfully evaluated for different human damping values, it currently requires manual retuning if this parameter changes.
Future works will thus include the development of an online estimation and adaptation law to properly detect and react to changes in the human dynamic parameters.
