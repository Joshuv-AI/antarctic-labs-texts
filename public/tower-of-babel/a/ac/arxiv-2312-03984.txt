Taking control of compressible modes: bulk viscosity and the turbulent dynamo 2023 Taking control of compressible modes: bulk viscosity and the turbulent dynamo – D
James R. Beattie, Christoph Federrath, Neco Kriel, Justin Kin Jun Hew & Amitava Bhattacharjee

Abstract
Many polyatomic astrophysical plasmas are compressible and out of chemical and thermal equilibrium, and yet, due to Stokes’ hypothesis, a means to carefully control the decay of compressible modes in these systems has largely been neglected. This is especially important for small-scale, turbulent dynamo processes, which are known to be sensitive to the effects of compression. To control the viscous properties of the compressible modes, we perform supersonic, visco-resistive dynamo simulations with additional bulk viscosity ν bulk \nu_{\text{bulk}} , deriving a new ν bulk \nu_{\text{bulk}} Reynolds number Re bulk \text{Re}_{\text{bulk}} , and viscous Prandtl number P ​ ν ≡ Re bulk / Re shear \text{P}\nu\equiv\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}/\text{Re}_{\text{shear}} , where Re shear \text{Re}_{\text{shear}} is the shear viscosity Reynolds number. For 10 − 3 ≤ P ​ ν ≤ ∞ 10^{-3}\leq\text{P}\nu\leq\infty , we explore a broad range of statistics critical to the dynamo problem, including the integral and spectral energy ratios, growth rates, and the magnetic E mag ​ ( k ) E_{\rm mag}(k) and kinetic E kin ​ ( k ) E_{\text{kin}}(k) energy spectrum. We derive a general framework for decomposing E mag E_{\rm mag} growth rates into incompressible and compressible terms via orthogonal tensor decompositions of ∇ ⊗ 𝒗 \nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}} , where 𝒗 \mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}} is the fluid velocity. We find that compressible modes play a dual role, growing and decaying E mag E_{\rm mag} , and that field-line stretching is the main driver of growth, even in supersonic dynamos. In the absence of ν bulk \nu_{\text{bulk}} ( P ​ ν → ∞ \text{P}\nu\to\infty ), compressible modes pile up on small-scales, creating an apparent spectral bottleneck, which disappears for P ​ ν ≈ 1 \text{P}\nu\approx 1 . As P ​ ν \text{P}\nu decreases, compressible modes are dissipated at increasingly larger scales, in turn suppressing incompressible modes through a coupling between viscosity operators. We emphasise the importance of further understanding the role of ν bulk \nu_{\text{bulk}} in compressible astrophysical plasmas and direct numerical simulations that include compressibility.

In this study, we take the first steps toward describing the appropriate framework for bringing bulk viscosity into the context of turbulent dynamos. This includes defining new dimensionless plasma parameters, the bulk viscous Reynolds number, and the viscous Prandtl number, and making a number of important measurements that probe the role of this type of viscosity for the dynamo. Our results are not only broadly applicable to general poly-atomic plasmas, but also highly important for doing controlled studies of supersonic, magnetised gases, where spatial and temporal properties of compressible modes are controlled through an explicit viscosity operator. Our study is organised in the following manner. In

Section 5

Section 8

Fluid Viscosity & the velocity gradient tensor

2.1
Bulk and shear viscosity

The viscous momentum equation for a fluid is,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot\mathbb{F}=\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma},

(4)

where
\mathbb{F}
is a tensor of conserved momentum fluxes, and
\bm{\sigma}
is viscous stress tensor. Before kinetic theory showed us the way to describe
\bm{\sigma}
through perturbations away from thermal equilibrium in the form of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution function
it was an empirical fact that for Newtonian fluids
\bm{\sigma}
was proportional to the velocity gradient tensor
\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
, as,

\displaystyle\|\bm{\sigma}\|\sim\|\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\|,

(5)

where
\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}=\partial_{i}v_{j}
is the tensor product. By decomposing
\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
into the rate of expansion
\mathbb{B}
, the traceless, symmetric, rate of strain
\mathbb{S}
, and the traceless, antisymmetric, rate of rotation
\mathbb{A}
, tensor components, one can gain some insight into the structure of this type of viscosity,

\displaystyle\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{B}+\mathbb{S}+\mathbb{A},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I},

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}+[\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}]^{T}\right)-\frac{1}{3}(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}-[\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}]^{T}\right).

(9)

Because
\mathbb{S}
is traceless,
\text{tr}(\mathbb{S})=\mathbb{S}_{xx}+\mathbb{S}_{yy}+\mathbb{S}_{zz}=0
, it describes volume-preserving (incompressible and isochoric) transformations of fluid elements, and likewise for
\mathbb{A}
; where
\text{tr}\left\{\ldots\right\}
is the trace operator. The difference is that all diagonal elements of
\mathbb{A}
are identically zero, hence there are no components of
\mathbb{A}
orthogonal to the fluid element, whereas
\text{tr}(\mathbb{S})
sums to zero, hence the orthogonal forces exist, but cancel out over the whole element. Furthermore,
\mathbb{S}
is symmetric so
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{S}^{T}
, and
\mathbb{A}
is anti-symmetric,
\mathbb{A}=-\mathbb{A}^{T}
, which constrains the types of transformations that are allowable for each tensor.

From this decomposition, it is clear that only
\mathbb{B}
and
\mathbb{S}
, contribute to the fluid viscosity, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{j}v_{i}-\partial_{i}v_{j})=-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_{z}&\omega_{y}\\
\omega_{z}&0&-\omega_{x}\\
-\omega_{y}&\omega_{x}&0\end{pmatrix},

(10)

where
\omega=\nabla\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
is the fluid vorticity, and
\epsilon_{ijk}
is the Levi-Civita tensor. This is exactly the rate of rotation tensor, hence
\mathbb{A}
cannot be a source of fluid viscosity (see our derivation in
Appendix
A
; note that for
\sqrt{\mathbb{S}:\mathbb{S}}=0
,
\mathbb{A}
describes the rigid body rotation of a fluid element along its path – formerly, antisymmetric matrices form a representation of the
\text{SO}(3)
group, describing infinitesimal rotations of the fluid elements;
Choudhuri 1998

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=

\displaystyle 2\nu_{\text{shear}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}+[\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}]^{T}\right)-\frac{1}{3}(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\nu_{\text{bulk}}(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I},

(11)

where
\nu_{\text{shear}}
and
\nu_{\text{bulk}}
are the coefficients of bulk viscosity and the shear viscosity, respectively

where

As

Kumar 2023
suggest in their review of bulk viscosity in fluid dynamics, the theory for bulk viscosity has largely been neglected in the literature. Here we summarise the few studies that are relevant to bulk viscosity in the context of fluid plasma turbulence.
Chen
et al. 2019
studied forced homogeneous isotropic (and hydrodynamical) turbulence (HIT) for moderate sonic Mach numbers
\mathcal{M}\leq 0.6
and Taylor miscroscale Reynolds numbers
\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}\approx 100
. Through the kinetic energy spectrum, they showed that for large
\nu_{\text{bulk}}/\nu_{\text{shear}}

Pan &
Johnsen 2017
studied the effect of bulk viscosity in decaying hydrodynamical turbulence, over a the same range of
\nu_{\text{bulk}}/\nu_{\text{shear}}
that we do in our study, finding that high bulk viscosities enhanced the total decay rate of the kinetic energy by both suppressing the compressible modes in magnitude, and through the compressible mode-pressure and the compressible-incompressible mode interactions. However, they found that the incompressible modes were reasonably insensitive to bulk viscosity.
Touber 2019
studied the eigen-modes of the Navier-Stokes equation with the addition of bulk viscosity, showing that in the Stokes (viscous) regime, significant bulk viscosity may be able to disrupt the small-scale, incompressible structures (the building blocks for enstrophy,
\omega^{2}

\ell_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
(undoubtedly transitioning into the Stokes regime), then we should expect there to be an effect on the turbulent dynamo, since
\ell_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
is responsible for the magnetic field growth. To situate the bulk viscosity into a regular SSD formalism, we now define the dimensionless numbers that we can use to parameterise the additional bulk viscous fluxes in the momentum equation, and thus, our simulations.

\displaystyle+2\nu_{\text{shear}}\rho\Big\{\frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}+[\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}]^{T}\right)-\frac{1}{3}\left(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\right)\mathbb{I}\Big\}

\rho
is the gas density, and
\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{S}}}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}{\partial t}\rightarrow\frac{\rho_{0}V}{T}\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{v}}}}}}{\partial\hat{t}}=\frac{\rho_{0}V^{2}}{L}\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{v}}}}}}{\partial\hat{t}}.

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{0}V^{2}}{L}\frac{\partial\hat{\rho}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{v}}}}}}{\partial\hat{t}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L}\widehat{\nabla}\cdot\Bigg[\frac{B^{2}}{4\pi}\left(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{b}}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{b}}}}}-\frac{\hat{b}^{2}}{2}\mathbb{I}\right)-V^{2}\rho_{0}\hat{\rho}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{v}}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{\hat{v}}}}}

\mathbb{A}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{M}:\mathbb{A}&=\text{tr}\left\{\mathbb{M}\mathbb{A}^{T}\right\}=-\text{tr}\left\{\mathbb{M}\mathbb{A}\right\},\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left\{\mathbb{M}(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left\{\mathbb{M}(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\right\}\\
&=0,\end{split}

(36)

which means we expect
\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):\mathbb{A}}\right\rangle_{\V}/\left\langle{b^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}\equiv 0
. More broadly, this means turbulent magnetic fields are prevented from undergoing any kind of local winding to enhance or decay the field, quite unlike large-scale toroidal fields, which can undergo linear growth via winding
(
Aguilera-Miret et al. 2023b
)
. The final term is the compression, which is the projection of the magnetic field onto the dilatational components of the fluid element. This term has a negative sign, with the total sign contribution of the term solely determined by the divergence, because

\displaystyle\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I}}\right\rangle_{\V}=\text{tr}\left\{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}})(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\right\}\sim b^{2}(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}).

(37)

Hence, for
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}<0
, e.g., shocked fluid, the compressions enhance the growth rate by a local factor
(1/6)|\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}|
. However, for rarefactions this term reduces the growth rate by the same factor. Therefore, one can ask, on average, in a volume, is the fluid dilated
\left\langle{\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}\right\rangle_{\V}>0
or compressed
\left\langle{\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}\right\rangle_{\V}<0
? It is well known that for supersonic compressible turbulence, most of the fluid in the volume is
\left\langle{\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}\right\rangle_{\V}>0
, with only a very small amount of the fluid contained with the
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}<0
structures (
Passot
& Vázquez-Semadeni 1998
;
Schmidt et al. 2008
;
Hopkins 2013
;
Squire &
Hopkins 2017
;
Mocz &
Burkhart 2019
;
Beattie et al. 2022c
and also see the
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
probability density function in
Chen
et al. 2019
and in Appendix A1 and A2 in
Sur &
Subramanian 2023
). Hence as the compressions become stronger (for e.g., larger
\mathcal{M}
), this term, on average, ought to act to reduce the growth rate of the volume-averaged magnetic energy, which has been shown in numerical simulations
(
Federrath
et al. 2011
)
, however, not from evolving the bulk viscosity, but rather changing
\mathcal{M}
.

In the nonlinear induction stage
Equation 2
,
\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}\sim E_{\text{kin}}(\ell_{\rm eq})
, on energy equipartition scale
\ell_{\rm eq}

(
St-Onge
et al. 2020
;
Galishnikova
et al. 2022
;
Beattie et al. 2023
)
. Hence the growth term in
Equation 30
,
2\gamma\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, becomes

\displaystyle 2\gamma t_{0}\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}\sim\frac{\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}E_{\text{kin}}^{1/2}(\ell_{\rm eq})}{\ell_{\rm eq}}t_{0}\sim\frac{E_{\text{kin}}^{3/2}(\ell_{\rm eq})}{\ell_{\rm eq}}t_{0},

(38)

which is approximately constant, giving rise to linear growth as described in
Section 1
.

3.3
Dynamo saturation

Let us explore
Equation 30
in the saturated stage. Setting
\partial_{t}\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}=0
for statistically stationary saturation,

\displaystyle\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):\mathbb{S}}\right\rangle_{\V}-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I}}\right\rangle_{\V}=

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\eta\left\langle{(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}})}\right\rangle_{\V},

(39)

previously derived in
Yousef et al. 2007
for incompressible turbulence. Very simply put, when the growth terms balance with the dissipation, the dynamo saturates. However, these may balance in at least three different ways: (1) the growth terms may be suppressed such that they eventually find their way to
\eta\left\langle{(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}})}\right\rangle_{\V}
, or (2)
\eta\left\langle{(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}})}\right\rangle_{\V}
may grow, eventually balancing with the growth terms, or (3) there is a mixture of both. For
\mathcal{M}\approx 0.2
turbulence,
Sur &
Subramanian 2023
showed that
\eta\left\langle{(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}})}\right\rangle_{\V}
reduced by a small amount as the dynamo saturates
6
6

6

Note that for
\mathcal{M}\approx 10
turbulence
Seta &
Federrath 2021
showed that the Ohmic dissipation probability density function (the current; not simply the
1^{\rm st}
moment, that we are commenting upon here) reduced in the saturation. This is also shown for the supersonic runs in
Sur &
Subramanian 2023
.
, hinting that it is the
1^{\rm st}
moment growth terms that need to shrink to eventually reach a saturation. Hence, the incompressible growth term,
\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):\mathbb{S}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, is suppressed (presumably by the magnetic tension
\sim\nabla\cdot[\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}]
).
Schekochihin
et al. 2004b
showed that this could be achieved through anisotropising the velocity field along folded field structures in
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}
. Furthermore,
Seta &
Federrath 2021
showed that in the saturation,
|\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{u}}}}\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}|\approx 0
for both
\mathcal{M}=0.1
and
\mathcal{M}=10
dynamos, which suggests that all tensor projection growth terms weaken in the saturation.

Moreover, as shown in
Sur &
Subramanian 2023
for the
\mathcal{M}>1
dynamo, the compressible growth term,
(1/6)\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, is suppressed by the magnetic field pressure gradients
\nabla b^{2}/(8\pi)
that are preferentially parallel to
\nabla\rho
, opposing compression, since shocked gas can generate large
\nabla b^{2}
, organising the magnetic field into filamentary structures that are supported by magnetic pressure
(
Molina
et al. 2012
;
Beattie et al. 2022c
;
Kriel et al. 2023
)
. This is relevant in regions of high
\nabla b^{2}
(corresponding to high mass), but in the magnetic voids,
(1/6)\left\langle{(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}):(\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})\mathbb{I}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, which are the more volume-filling state of the gas, these terms act to reduce
\left\langle{E_{\rm mag}}\right\rangle_{\V}/\left\langle{E_{\text{kin}}}\right\rangle_{\V}
, which ought to decrease final saturation of the dynamo. We will revisit these volume-averaged terms, computing them through the temporal evolution of the simulations in both the kinematic and saturated stage in
Section 5
.

4
Numerical simulations & Methods

4.1
Visco-resistive magnetohydrodynamic fluid model

We use a modified version of the finite volume MHD code
flash

(
Fryxell
et al. 2000
;
Dubey
et al. 2008
)
, utilising a second-order conservative MUSCL-Hancock 5-wave approximate Riemann scheme
(
Bouchut
et al. 2010
;
Waagan et al. 2011
)
, and
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}
parabolic diffusion flux cleaning
(
Marder 1987
)
to solve the 3D, visco-resistive, isothermal, compressible MHD equations with a stochastic acceleration field acting to drive the turbulence,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}})

\displaystyle=0,

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}{\partial t}-\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}-\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\right.\hskip 50.00008pt\ignorespaces

\displaystyle\left.-\left(c_{s}^{2}\rho+\frac{b^{2}}{8\pi}\right)\mathbb{I}+\rho\bm{\sigma}\right]

\displaystyle=\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}},

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}}{\partial t}-\nabla\times\left[\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}-\eta\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{j}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(42)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(43)

where the viscous stress tensor is

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}

\displaystyle=2\nu_{\rm shear}\mathbb{S}+\nu_{\rm bulk}\mathbb{B},

(44)

using the same definitions as in
Section 2.1
. We solve the equations on a triply periodic domain of dimension
\V\equiv L^{3}
, with uniform grids of
128^{3}-1024^{3}
cells (we show a detailed convergence study in
Appendix
C
), where
\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}(t)}\right\rangle_{\V}=\left\langle{\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}(t)}\right\rangle_{\V}=0
(i.e., no net magnetic flux over
\V
, in the zero-momentum frame of
\V
),
c_{s}
is the sound speed,
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{j}}}}=\nabla\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}/(4\pi)
is the current density, and
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}
is the stochastic turbulent acceleration source term that drives the turbulence.

4.2
Turbulent driving

The turbulent source term
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}
follows an
Uhlenbeck &
Ornstein 1930
process with finite
e
-fold correlation time,
t_{0}=\ell_{0}/\left\langle{\mathit{v}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}

(
Schmidt et al. 2009
;
Federrath et al. 2010
)
. The field
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}
is constructed in Fourier space with energy isotropically injected on the peak scale
|\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}L/2\pi|=2
(equivalently,
\ell_{0}=L/2
) and falls off to zero with a parabolic spectrum within
1\leq|\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}L/2\pi|\leq 3
. On
\ell_{0}
we use
t_{0}
and the energy injection rate to control the rms velocity, which we set to
\left\langle{\mathit{v}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}^{1/2}/c_{s}=\mathcal{M}=1.0
in the kinematic stage of the dynamo, ensuring that we have compressible modes for the bulk viscosity to control. Furthermore, we inject energy such that
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}_{\perp}^{2}/\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}_{\parallel}^{2}=2
, corresponding to 2:1 energy in incompressible
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}_{\perp}=0
and compressible
\nabla\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}_{\parallel}=0
mode components of
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}}
, ensuring that the compressible modes are being replenished on large-scales. See
Federrath et al. 2010
and
Federrath
et al. 2022
for more details about the
TurbGen
driving source. We integrate each of the experiments from
t/t_{0}=0
to
t/t_{0}=100
, writing the 3D field variables to disk every
t/t_{0}=0.1
for post-processing.

4.3
Setting Reynolds and Prandtl numbers

Following
Section 2
, we parameterise the visco-resistive properties of our simulations with five numbers. Throughout the study we fix
\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}=1000
, corresponding to setting
\nu_{\text{shear}}=1\times 10^{-3}\ell_{0}^{2}/t_{0}
in
Equation 20
. This ensures that the incompressible modes are in a state of turbulence in our simulations
(
Frisch 1995
)
. We then vary

between

\nu_{\text{bulk}}=0

Rm

,

4.4
Initial field conditions

Following, e.g.,
Kriel et al. 2022
, we initialise the velocity field to
|\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}(x,y,z,t=0)|/c_{s}=0
, with units
c_{s}=1
and the density field
\rho(x,y,z,t=0)=\rho_{0}
, where the density has units
\rho_{0}=1
. Likewise,
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}
has units of
c_{s}\rho_{0}^{1/2}=1
, and is initialised as a Gaussian field in
k
-space with a parabolic spectral power distribution matching the turbulent forcing function. The total energy in the turbulent fluctuations of the field is set such that
\left\langle{E_{\text{mag},0}}\right\rangle_{\V}/\rho_{0}c_{s}^{2}=10^{-10}=\beta^{-1}
, where
\left\langle{E_{\text{mag},0}}\right\rangle_{\V}
is the initial volume integral energy and
\beta
is the plasma beta. This corresponds to an incredibly weak seed magnetic field compared to the constant thermal energy, allowing for the simulations to spend a significant time in the kinematic stage of the dynamo.

4.5
Helmholtz decomposition of velocity field

Any vector field can be written as a vector sum of curl-free and divergence-free vectors – the Helmholtz decomposition of a vector field. Because we are seeking to understand the role of bulk viscosity on the compressible modes in the supersonic dynamo, we specifically decompose our
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
into orthogonal compressible
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}
(
\nabla\times\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}=0
) and incompressible
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}
(
\nabla\cdot\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}=0
) modes, such that,

\displaystyle\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}=\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}+\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}.

(45)

This is easily done in Fourier space, where

\displaystyle\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}(\bm{\ell})=\mathfrak{Re}\left\{\int\operatorname{d}\!{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}}\,[\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}\cdot\tilde{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}})]\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}/k^{2}e^{2\pi i\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}\cdot\bm{\ell}}\right\},

(46)

is the real
\mathfrak{Re}
component of the inverse Fourier transform of the Fourier transformed velocity
\tilde{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}(\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}})
projected along
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}}}
. Then
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}=\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}-\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}
, ensuring that

Summary and Conclusions

We perform three-dimensional, visco-resistive direct numerical simulations of trans-sonic, magnetohydrodynamic turbulence undergoing magnetic field amplification and saturation through the small-scale dynamo, including viscous effects from both the shear and bulk viscosity. We list the conclusions below.

9.1
Bulk viscosity in the turbulent dynamo

To appropriately place bulk viscosity into a small-scale dynamo framework, we derive and define two new dimensionless numbers to study the role of bulk (volume) viscosity, the “bulk viscous Reynolds number"
\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}
,
Equation 20
, which is the ratio between Reynolds stress and the bulk viscosity, as well as the “viscous Prandtl number"
\text{P}\nu
,
Equation 21
, which is the ratio between the bulk and shear viscosities (or equivalently, Reynolds numbers). For
\text{P}\nu<1
,
\text{P}\nu
defines the size of a sub-bulk-viscous range,
k_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}>k>k_{\nu_{\text{bulk}}}
, and for
\text{P}\nu>1
, the sub-shear-viscous range,
k_{\nu_{\text{bulk}}}>k>k_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
, which we illustrate in
Figure 1
. Intuitively, the bulk viscosity acts like a Laplacian-like viscosity operator that primarily effects the compressible modes, starting at high-
k
and shifting to low-
k
as
\text{P}\nu
decreases.

In the
\text{P}\nu>1
regime, which simulations without explicit bulk viscosity will be in (
\text{P}\nu=\infty
), have a strong, high-
k
build-up of compressible modes that appear as a bottleneck in the total kinetic energy spectrum. For
\text{Pm}_{\text{shear}}=1
plasmas (
Equation 25
), where
k_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}=k_{\eta}
, this results in low-levels of magnetisation and high-levels of compressible kinetic energy building up on these scales. Whether or not this is desirable behaviour for simulations of astrophysical plasmas is therefore sensitive to the exact nature of bulk viscosity in the plasma of interest, but in general, the nature of bulk viscosity is largely unknown with a very limited amount of exploration in the literature both in the laboratory and in simulation.

In the
\text{P}\nu<1
regime, the compressible and incompressible modes become coupled through the viscosity operators in the sub-bulk-viscous range. This means that as
k_{\nu_{\text{bulk}}}
shifts to low-
k
,
k_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
shifts with it, which for fixed
\text{Pm}_{\text{shear}}
also brings
k_{\eta}
to lower
k
modes. When
\text{P}\nu
is very low, such that
t_{\nu_{\text{bulk}}}\sim t_{0}
, there are significant changes in the shape of all energy spectra, dynamo integral energy statistics, and of course, an extremely strong suppression of compressible modes. If
\text{P}\nu
is closer to one, the impact the bulk viscosity has on the dynamo is only in the high-
k
modes, and such, integral energies, growth rates, and saturations are largely unaffected. Future work is required to determine the relations for the scaling between
k_{\nu_{\text{bulk}}}
,
k_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
and
k_{\eta}
in the
\text{P}\nu<1
regime, where the coupling is strong.

9.2
Compressibility in the turbulent dynamo

To understand the role of compressible modes in the kinematic and saturated dynamo stages, we derive the
1^{\rm st}
moment magnetic energy equation that separates out the effects of compressible and incompressible modes, in the sense that they are truly orthogonal contributions to the growth rate and saturation. This includes three main terms: volume-preserving stretching, volume-preserving winding and volume-changing compressions (
Equation 7
–
9
, respectively). Winding is orthogonal to the magnetic field tensor, hence one cannot locally wind a turbulent magnetic field to grow it (see
Equation 36
). By directly computing the growth terms (
Figure 7
), we find that stretching is always the most dominant pathway for growing the magnetic field, even in the supersonic dynamo, and irrespective of the phase of the dynamo. This explains why
\ell_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}/\ell_{\eta}\sim\text{Pm}_{\text{shear}}^{1/2}
, (the stretching rate at
\ell_{\nu_{\text{shear}}}
balances with resistive dissipation rate) is true for the compressible regime, which was recently found in
Kriel et al. 2023
. This is in tension with some recent results i.e.,
Sur &
Subramanian 2023
, but the tension is removed if they carefully remove the trace of
\mathbb{S}
in their decomposition (see
footnote 4
). Compression plays a dual, almost symmetric (at least at
\mathcal{M}=1
) role, in both growing and decaying the magnetic field through compressions and dilatations. There is however a slight negative skew in the compression growth term, which means on average the compressions oppose the growth, resulting in slower growth rates and lower saturations than for the incompressible dynamo counterparts. By splitting the velocity filed into compressible and incompressible modes, and analysing the saturation across
k
space, we come to the conclusion that at least for our parameter range, for the compressible modes, it is only the very low-
k
modes that are strong enough to influence the field through the late-kinematic stages and saturation. However, it is also the low-
k
modes that dominate the integral energies, hence why changing the compressibility through various, large-scale parameterisations (changing
\mathcal{M}
, changing the compressible mode energy at the driving scale) is able to change the integral statistics and saturation energies in the turbulent dynamo.

Appendix D
Compressible and incompressible
1^{\rm st}
moment energy equations

We stated the Stokes limit for the compressible and incompressible
1^{\rm st}
moment kinetic equations in
Section 6.3
. Here we derive the full equations and take the Stokes limit to demonstrate how we got to
Equation 56
and
Equation 55
. We start with the dimensionless isothermal momentum equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}^{2}_{\text{A}}}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}}}-\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}^{2}}\rho\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{M}^{2}_{\text{A}}}b^{2}\mathbb{I}\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{2\rho}{\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}}\mathbb{S}+\frac{\rho}{\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}}\mathbb{B}\right)

(78)

as in
Section 2.2
, and take the Stokes limit,
\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}=\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}\rightarrow 0
, which maps us to the dynamical equations for the high-
k
, viscous modes in the sub-bulk-viscous range (see
Figure 1
),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}}\mathbb{S}+\frac{1}{\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}}\mathbb{B}\right)

(79)

where we have assumed
\nabla\rho
is small compared to
\sim\nabla\otimes\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}
. Now taking the dot product with
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}
and taking ensemble averages,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\left\langle{\rho\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}^{2}}\right\rangle_{\V}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\left\langle{E_{\text{kin},\perp}}\right\rangle_{\V}}{\partial t}

(80)

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla\cdot\left(\frac{2}{\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}}\mathbb{S}+\frac{1}{\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}}\mathbb{B}\right)}\right\rangle_{\V},

(81)

utilising
\left\langle{v_{j}\partial_{t}\rho v_{i}}\right\rangle_{\V}=\partial_{t}\left\langle{\rho v_{j}v_{i}}\right\rangle_{\V}

(
Wang
et al. 2018b
)
. Decomposing the remaining total velocity terms into
\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}=\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}+\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}
, the first term is simply,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S}}\right\rangle_{\V}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}+\frac{4}{3}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}}\right\rangle_{\V},

(82)

and the second term is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla\cdot\mathbb{B}}\right\rangle_{\V}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}}\right\rangle_{\V},

(83)

hence

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left\langle{E_{\text{kin},\perp}}\right\rangle_{\V}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}^{-1}}{3}+\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}^{-1}\right)\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}}\right\rangle_{\V}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}^{-1}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}}\right\rangle_{\V}.

(84)

Likewise for the
1^{\rm st}
moment compressible kinetic energy equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\left\langle{E_{\text{kin},\parallel}}\right\rangle_{\V}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}^{-1}}{3}+\text{Re}_{\text{bulk}}^{-1}\right)\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}}\right\rangle_{\V}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\text{Re}_{\text{shear}}^{-1}\left\langle{\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\parallel}\cdot\nabla^{2}\mathrm{{\mn@boldsymbol{\mathit{v}}}}_{\perp}}\right\rangle_{\V}.

(85)
