Asymptotic consistency of the WSINDy algorithm in the limit of continuum data
Daniel A. Messenger, David M. Bortz

Abstract
In this work we study the asymptotic consistency of the weak-form sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics algorithm (WSINDy) in the identification of differential equations from noisy samples of solutions. We prove that the WSINDy estimator is unconditionally asymptotically consistent for a wide class of models which includes the Navier-Stokes equations and the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation. We thus provide a mathematically rigorous explanation for the observed robustness to noise of weak-form equation learning. Conversely, we also show that in general the WSINDy estimator is only conditionally asymptotically consistent, yielding discovery of spurious terms with probability one if the noise level is above some critical threshold and the nonlinearities exhibit sufficiently fast growth. We derive explicit bounds on the critical noise threshold in the case of Gaussian white noise and provide an explicit characterization of these spurious terms in the case of trigonometric and/or polynomial model nonlinearities. However, a silver lining to this negative result is that if the data is suitably denoised (a simple moving average filter is sufficient), then we recover unconditional asymptotic consistency on the class of models with locally-Lipschitz nonlinearities. Altogether, our results reveal several important aspects of weak-form equation learning which may be used to improve future algorithms. We demonstrate our results numerically using the Lorenz system, the cubic oscillator, a viscous Burgers growth model, and a Kuramoto-Sivashinsky-type higher-order PDE.

Introduction

1.1
Overview

A widespread challenge in the natural sciences is to create a mathematical model which makes accurate predictions, is mathematically analyzable, and amenable to parameter estimation from data. Typically, parameters exhibit a nonlinear relationship with the observed data, which explains the widespread use of nonlinear least-squares methods to fit parameters by minimizing the difference between the experimental data and numerical simulations. In many cases, parameter uncertainty is layered on top of uncertainty in the model itself, which has led to the field of
model selection

[
1
,
2
]
, whereby criteria are devised to select an appropriate (e.g. parsimonious) model from a host of candidate models.

A subject of fervent study in recent years has been the use of data to learn the correct model equations for a given phenomenon, along with the model parameters, without resorting to the laborious nonlinear least-squares approach which often involves many expensive forward solves of candidate models. With equations in hand, one can conduct further experiments computationally which may not be immediately accessible in laboratory conditions. The weak-form sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics algorithm (WSINDy), introduced in
[
19
,
18
]
, is one such equation learning algorithm that has been observed to offer several advantages over other equation learning methods, including robustness to noise, high accuracy, low computational cost, and extensibility across many modeling paradigms
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.
WSINDy is based off of the SINDy algorithm
[
6
,
26
]
, which popularized the sparse equation learning technique and demonstrated its viability in discovering ordinary differential equations, partial differential equations, normal forms, and reduced-order models, to name a few. For other important works on sparse equation learning see
[
40
,
31
,
28
,
16
]
and the references therein.

The key difference between SINDy and WSINDy is that the latter discretizes an integral representation of the dynamics, avoiding computation of pointwise derivatives. This leads to implicit noise filtering effects and relaxed constraints on the smoothness of the underlying ground-truth data. In addition to WSINDy as introduced in
[
19
,
18
]
, these and other advantages of weak-form equation learning have now been reported in several other studies
[
29
,
25
,
23
,
37
,
36
,
11
,
24
,
7
,
32
,
3
]
, leading to a consensus that exploiting integration can provide significant improvements in the discovery of equations from data. Nevertheless, other promising methods for reducing the deleterious effects of noise do exist, see in particular works that incorporate automatic differentiation
[
16
,
17
]
, ensembling
[
10
]
, and improved optimization methods
[
39
,
14
]
. However, each of these techniques can easily be combined with a weak-form approach, as demonstrated in
[
10
,
5
]
.

The goal of this paper is to provide rigorous justification for the observed robustness to noise of WSINDy and other weak form methods. We accomplish this by determining conditions under which a WSINDy model is asymptotically consistent with the true underlying model in the limit of continuum data. We consider the performance of WSINDy on noisy samples
\mathbf{U}^{(m)}
of some solution
u
of a differential equation on grids
(\mathbf{X}^{(m)},\mathbf{t}^{(m)})
of increasing resolution within an underlying spatiotemporal domain
\Omega\times(0,T)
. This is cast as a support recovery problem for the support
\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
of the true weight vector
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, which is nonzero only on the subset of terms that exist in the true model. It should be noted that throughout we assume that the true model is contained in the library of models considered, however extension beyond this case is possible (see Section
6
discussion point II).

As we will see, for each class of models examined, there exists a critical noise level
\sigma_{c}>0
such that for noise levels
\sigma<\sigma_{c}
, we have
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}
satisfying
\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
with probability rapidly approaching one as
m\to\infty
, where
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}
is the WSINDy model at discretization level
m
, and
m
is inversely proportional to the volume element of the spatiotemportal grid
(\mathbf{X}^{(m)},\mathbf{t}^{(m)})
. Most importantly, we identify a large class of systems for which
\sigma_{c}=\infty
, leading to unconditional support recovery as
m\to\infty
. We also prove that suitably denoising the data leads to unconditional asymptotic consistency on the class of models with locally-Lipschitz nonlinearities, in particular this is true if a simple moving average filter is used.

1.2
Related work

Despite the widespread use and development of sparse data-driven equation learning algorithms applied to dynamical systems since the onset of SINDy, very few studies have examined the performance of these methods from a mathematically rigorous perspective. The authors of
[
33
]
and
[
30
]
report sparse recovery guarantees for chaotic and structured ODE systems (respectively), however each result requires the availability of point-wise derivatives. Other results include convergence of the STLS algorithm used in SINDy
[
41
]
and recovery guarantees for sparse polynomial regression
[
13
]
, however the former only asserts convergence to a local minimum of the
\ell_{0}
-regularized least-squares cost function, and the latter relies on sparsity of the measurement noise. The authors of
[
27
]
have recently proved that surrogate models learned using WSINDy converge to the true dynamics as the basis used to represent the dynamics grows in dimension, although this result concerns only continuum noise-free data.

In this work we undertake the problem of analyzing the performance of WSINDy applied to noisy discrete datasets by utilizing a suitable continuum limit, whereby many practical insights can be gleaned. One important related work is
[
12
]
, in which the authors show that with local polynomial differentiation together with filtering kernels that obey certain asymptotics, the LASSO estimator is asymptotically consistent for PDE recovery problems from quadratic libraries of arbitrary spatial differentiation order. This approach appears to be extendable to a wide class of model libraries, but requires the so-called
mutual incoherence
condition on the design matrix, which is restrictive in settings with correlated columns (a common paradigm in sparse equation learning from data). In addition, the noise-free data in
[
12
]
is assumed to be classical, which we show in this work is not necessary if one employs the weak form. Nevertheless, we conjecture that results in the present article may be combined with those in
[
12
]
to yield asymptotically consistent algorithms for models with combined weak and classical derivative evaluation.

Summary of results

As alluded to above, in this work we focus on the performance of WSINDy in the continuum limit as the computational grid

2.3
Controllable bias

In this section we informally discuss in what sense the bias in the continuum problem is controllable, such that WSINDy applied to

In terms of the continuum linear system and noise-free continuum linear system, for a fixed candidate weight vector

\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(\mathbf{b}^{(m)}-\mathbf{G}^{(m)}\mathbf{w}\right)=\overline{\mathbf{b}}-\overline{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{G}^{\star}(\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\mathbf{w})+(\mathbf{G}^{\star}-\overline{\mathbf{G}})\mathbf{w}+(\overline{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{b}^{\star}).

is still guaranteed for polynomial and trigonometric systems

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[T^{(m)}]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varphi(t_{i})f\star\rho(u(t_{i}))\Delta t^{(m)}=\int_{\text{supp}\left(\varphi\right)}\varphi(t)f\star\rho(u(t))\,dt

(2.14)

and

\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{V}[T^{(m)}]=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Delta t^{(m)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\varphi(t_{i})\right)^{2}\Big(f^{2}\star\rho(u(t_{i}))-(f\star\rho(u(t_{i})))^{2}\Big)\Delta t^{(m)}\right)=0.

In other words, all entries of the linear system
(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})
converge to deterministic quantities of the form (
2.14
), where
f
has been replaced by
f\,\star\,\rho
, representing a bias between the continuum and noise-free problems.

If the trial function library
{\mathcal{F}}
is such that
\{f\star\rho\ |\ f\in{\mathcal{F}}\}\in\text{span}({\mathcal{F}})
, then a linear transformation exists between the continuum least-squares solution
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}
and true solution
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, despite the inherent bias. This turns out to be the case for polynomial and trigonometric
f
, in which case for moderate noise levels and reasonably well-behaved dynamics, recovery of the correct model in the limit of large
m
follows from a single round of thresholding. This partially explains the observed robustness to noise in
[
18
]
. We now informally derive these linear transformations for polynomial and trigonometric libraries.

When
f
is polynomial, the bias
f\star\rho-f
is a polynomial
of lower degree
. Specifically, with
f(x)=x^{k}
, we have that

f\star\rho(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}{k\choose j}M_{k-j}(\rho)x^{j}=f(x)+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}{k\choose j}M_{k-j}(\rho)x^{j},

where
M_{j}(\rho)
denotes the
j
th moment of the distribution
\rho
. For example if
\rho
is a white Gaussian noise distribution, then
3
3

3

The double factorial for an integer
n
is defined
n!!:=n(n-2)\cdots(1)
, with
(0)!!=(-1)!!=1
.

M_{j}(\rho)=\begin{cases}(j-1)!!\sigma^{j},&j\text{ even}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The monomial library
P^{(q)}=(1,x,\dots,x^{q})
thus transforms under cross-correlation with Gaussian
\rho
as

P^{(q)}\star\rho=P^{(q)}\mathbf{A}^{(q)},

where
\mathbf{A}^{(q)}
is defined

\mathbf{A}^{(q)}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}{j\choose i}(j-i-1)!!\sigma^{j-i},&j\geq i,\ (j-i)\ \text{even}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.15)

In words,
\mathbf{A}^{(q)}
is upper triangular with
1
’s along the diagonal and
0
’s along odd superdiagonals. The least-squares solution to the continuum problem is then given by
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}=(\mathbf{A}^{(q)})^{-1}\mathbf{w}^{\star}
, and using that (see Lemma
C.1
in Appendix
C
)

\left(\mathbf{A}^{(q)}\right)^{-1}_{i,j}=(-1)^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{(q)}_{i,j},

the resulting coefficient error obeys the bound,

\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C\sigma^{2}\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}.

The constant
C=C(p_{\max},\sigma)
depends on the maximum degree monomial
p_{\max}
in the true model, as well as the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
, and takes modest values for small
p_{\max}
:

C(1,\sigma)=0,\quad C(2,\sigma)=1,\quad C(3,\sigma)=3,\quad C(4,\sigma)=6+3\sigma^{2},\quad C(5,\sigma)=10+15\sigma^{2}.

Hence, if
p_{\max}
is not too large and the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
is moderate, then spurious terms in
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}
will be removed by a single round of thresholding.

The situation is even nicer for trigonometric functions. With
f=e^{i\omega x}
, we have

f\star\rho(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\omega(x+y)}\rho(y)dy=e^{i\omega x}\widehat{\rho}(\omega)=\hat{\rho}(\omega)f(x)

where
\widehat{\rho}
is the Fourier transform
\rho
up to scaling. A library of trigonometric terms
F^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=(e^{i\omega\cdot(\cdot)})_{\omega\in\boldsymbol{\omega}}
then satisfies

F^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\star\rho=F^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

where
\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is diagonal. In this case, under mild restrictions on
\widehat{\rho}
, solving the continuum least squares problem produces
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}
with supp
(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0})=
supp
(\mathbf{w}^{\star})
and

\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\max_{\omega\in\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\star}}|1-\hat{\rho}(\omega)|\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\star}\subset\boldsymbol{\omega}
is the set of trigonometric frequencies present in the true model. In the case of Gaussian white noise we have

\widehat{\rho}(\omega)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}}{2}\right),

so that for
\sigma\leq\frac{0.14}{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}}
, the vector
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}
will be 99% accurate.

Remark 2.2
.

For some noise distributions
\rho
it is possible to have
\text{supp}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}\right)\subsetneq\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
on trigonometric functions if
\widehat{\rho}(\omega)=0
at some
\omega\in\boldsymbol{\omega}
. Consider the uniform distribution
\rho=(2a)^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{[-a,a]}
. Then
\widehat{\rho}(n\pi/a)=0
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, which leads to the restriction
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right\|_{\infty}<\frac{\pi}{a}
in order for
\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
to be invertible. In this case the maximum allowable frequency is inversely proportional to the standard deviation
\sigma=a/\sqrt{3}
, which is not unreasonable: perturbations of the solution
u
that are comparable to the periods of trigonometric terms will render their frequencies unobservable.

Remark 2.3
.

An understandable concern of using a weak-form recovery method is the biased recovery outlined in this section. However, we emphasize that the weak form is still advantageous over many strong-form methods which rely on point-wise derivative estimates. For a finite difference derivative operator
D
, the mean-squared error in the noisy derivative approximation of a one-dimensional derivative
u^{\prime}(t)
using data
\mathbf{U}=u(\mathbf{t})+\epsilon
sampled at resolution
\Delta t
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\rho}[|u^{\prime}(t)-D\mathbf{U}(t)|^{2}]={\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\Delta t^{2}}\right),

where
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{V}[\rho]
. In this form, there is no hope of convergence as
\Delta t\to 0
for any fixed
\sigma>0
unless the data is suitably denoised as
\Delta t
is brought to zero. As we will see in this work, weak-form recovery methods do not suffer from instabilities as
\Delta t\to 0
.

2.4
Assumptions

Here we list the main assumptions used in the results below, along with a brief overview of their motivations. Specific results will specify which of the following assumptions are in effect.

2.4.1
Regularity of the true solution

In order to present results in as general a context as possible with respect to solution regularity, we specify the regularity of the true solution

by defining the following function spaces. For open, bounded

, and for

(2.16)

where

is the space of functions on

with weak derivatives up to order

in

. The spaces

are similar to the broken Sobolev spaces used in the analysis of discontinuous Galerkin finite element methods (see e.g.
[
15
]
). We assume that
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
for some
k>(d+1)/2

2.2
) with coefficients
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{w}^{\star}
and that any points of reduced regularity (e.g. discontinuities) are contained to the boundaries between subdomains

ensures by the Sobolev embedding theorem that

is bounded and piecewise Hölder continuous on each

. We show in Appendix
B

that this is sufficient regularity for the trapezoidal rule to converge for integrals of smooth functions of

.

2.4.2
Sampling model

We assume that every pointwise evaluation

4

With

2.4.3
Model library

We assume the collection of multi-indices

Reference test function

We assume that

2.4.5
Conditioning of the noise-free system

In this work we ensure that the recovery problem is solvable by assuming that the noise-free continuum matrix
\mathbf{G}^{\star}
is full-rank. This implies several assumptions:

(I)

The underlying PDE has a unique representation of the form (
2.2
) with coefficients
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
over the library
({\mathcal{F}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
.

(II)

The reference test function
\psi

({\mathcal{F}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})

are such that the set of vectors (with Query points
{\mathcal{Q}}
arranged into a vector)

\left(\partial^{\alpha^{s}}\psi*f_{j}(u)({\mathcal{Q}})\right)_{s=1,\dots,S,\ j=1,\dots,J}

are linearly independent. In particular, the number of convolution query points is not smaller than the library size:
K\geq\mathfrak{J}
, where

is the number of query points,

is the number of differential operators in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
,
J
is number of functions
{\mathcal{F}}
, and
\mathfrak{J}=SJ
is the total number of terms in the model library.

Remark 2.4
.

The most restrictive assumption above is the full rank assumption on
\mathbf{G}^{\star}
in
2.4.5
. This implies that there does not exist
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}\neq 0
such that
u
satisfies an additional constraint of the form

0=\sum_{s,j}\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}_{s,j}\partial^{\alpha^{s}}f_{j}(u).

Such constraints do indeed arise in practice (e.g. the divergence-free constraint in the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations). While this cannot be directly checked in practice from noisy data sets, the condition number of the weak-form linear system provides an excellent guide for the conditioning of the underlying noise-free system, and is reflected in the choice of reference test function
\psi
, query points
{\mathcal{Q}}
, and library
({\mathcal{F}},\boldsymbol{\alpha})
, all of which the user has control over.

Ultimately, the full-rank assumption is due to our use of the STLS algorithm for performing sparse regression, however this can be relaxed if other algorithms are chosen, and extensions of STLS to the rank-deficient case are possible. However, due to spurious terms arising in the continuum limit, we conjecture that some form of thresholding-based sparse regression is advantageous (over e.g. greedy methods or
\ell_{1}
-regularization).

3
Exponential concentration of the WSINDy linear system

Using concentration results for sums of heavy-tailed random variables recently proved in
[
35

4

with an exponential convergence rate. We will need the following lemmas to connect existing results with the specific form of the entries of the matrix
\mathbf{G}^{(m)}

Using the terminology of
[
4
]

\mathbf{G}^{(m)}
can be modelled using a common rate function.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

|f(x)|\leq C_{f}\left(1+|x|^{p}\right),

(3.1)

for some
p\geq 1
and
C_{f}>0
, and let
\rho
be a symmetric sub-Gaussian distribution. For bounded sequences of real numbers
(\alpha_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(u_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and for
\epsilon_{i}\sim\rho

i.i.d.

, define the random variables
Y_{i}=\alpha_{i}f(u_{i}+\epsilon_{i})
. Then for any
\kappa\geq\left\|{\rho}\right\|_{\text{SG}}
and

, it holds that the right tails of

are captured by a common rate function

,

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{i}>t\right)\leq\exp(-I(t)),

where

I(t):=\mathbbm{1}_{(t^{*},\infty)}(t)\left[\frac{1}{\kappa^{2}}\left(\left(\frac{t}{C_{f}\alpha^{*}}-1\right)^{1/p}-u^{*}\right)^{2}-\log(2)\right]=\frac{t^{2/p}}{\kappa^{2}(C_{f}\alpha^{*})^{2/p}}I_{0}(t),

(3.2)

for
\alpha^{*}=\sup_{i}|\alpha_{i}|
,
u^{*}=\sup_{i}|u_{i}|
, and
t^{*}:=C_{f}\alpha^{*}\left(1+\left(u^{*}+\kappa\sqrt{\log(2)}\right)^{p}\right)
. Moreover,

is monotonically increasing from

to

over

, and is defined in the proof in Appendix
D

The next lemma allows one to uniformly bound a sequence of independent, non-identically distributed random variables of the form in Lemma
3.1

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Y_{i}
be defined under the same conditions as Lemma
3.1
and choose

. Then there exists

such that the sum

satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(|S_{m}-\mathbb{E}S_{m}|>mt\right)\leq\begin{cases}2\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}I(mt)\right)+2m\exp\left(-I(mt)\right),&t\geq t_{m}(\beta)\\
2\exp\left(-\frac{mt^{2}}{2\overline{v}(\beta)}\right)+2m\exp\left(-\frac{mt_{m}(\beta)^{2}}{\overline{v}(\beta)}\right),&0\leq t<t_{m}(\beta),\end{cases}

(3.3)

where
t_{m}(\beta):=\sup\{t\geq 0\ :\ t\leq\beta\overline{v}(\beta)\frac{I(mt)}{mt}\}\to 0
in m.

We now present a main result concerning concentration of the WSINDy discrete linear system to its continuum variant. Throughout we use the notation
\left\|{\mathbf{G}}\right\|_{\vec{p}}
to denote the vector

\mathbf{G}
stretched into a column vector, and the notation

to mean the concatenation of matrix

with vector

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose each function in library
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies the growth bound (

\mathbb{P}\Big(\left\|{(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})-(\overline{\mathbf{G}},\overline{\mathbf{b}})}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{\infty}}>t\Big)\leq\begin{cases}K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}(mt)^{2/p_{\max}}\right)+K\mathfrak{J}m\exp\left(-c(mt)^{2/p_{\max}}\right),&t\geq t_{m}\\
K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{mt^{2}}{2\overline{v}}\right)+K\mathfrak{J}m\exp\left(-\frac{mt_{m}^{2}}{\overline{v}}\right),&0\leq t<t_{m},\end{cases}

(3.4)

where the rate factor

Remark 3.1
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
reveals several ways that the rate of concentration can be increased. The most effective is to lower the growth-rate

and decrease

4
Asymptotic consistency

In this section we provide asymptotic consistency results in the form of support recovery of the true model coefficients
\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
in the limit as

(recall

is the number of points that the reference test function

In Section

with high-probability from the raw (un-filtered) data as

, provided
\sigma<\sigma_{c}

and the assumptions in Section

3

\sigma_{c}
may be derived, in Section
4.1
we focus on proving conditions for

, requiring an additional assumption on
(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
(see Remark
4.3
), is presented in Appendix
E
and may be extended to the case of arbitrary symmetric sub-Gaussian noise using Lemma
C.3
. Furthermore, Lemma
C.3
provides a general mechanism for deriving bounds on
\sigma_{c}
specific to any symmetric sub-Gaussian noise distribution
\rho
. Explicit bounds on
\sigma_{c}
may be highly informative for future algorithmic developments.

To summarize, we first prove in Lemma
4.2
that for noise levels below some critical noise
\sigma_{c}^{\prime}
there exists a feasible
\widehat{\lambda}
for which the STLS solution of associated continuum problem recovers the true support
S^{\star}
. We use properties of the Gaussian moment matrix to present explicit bounds
\sigma_{c}^{\prime}
. We then show in Lemma
4.3
that there exists
\sigma_{c}\leq\sigma_{c}^{\prime}
below which the one-shot MSTLS algorithm (defined below) applied to the continuum problem produces
\widehat{\mathbf{w}}
satisfying
\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\right)\subset S^{\star}
. The utility of Lemma
4.3
lies in the fact that the one-shot MSTLS algorithm involves no hyperparameters, hence avoids the task of selecting a feasible
\widehat{\lambda}
. We then combine these results with classical stability of the least-squares problem (Lemma
4.4
) and the concentration results in Section

\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)\subset S^{\star}
with high probability for the MSTLS solution

on the linear system
(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})
, provided
\sigma<\sigma_{c}
and

is large enough. This result is extended to yield
\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)=S^{\star}
with high probability in Appendix
E
, and for general symmetric sub-Gaussian noise with the aid of Lemma
C.3
.

In Section
4.2
, we consider the case of filtering the data before applying the WSINDy algorithm. Examining the case of simple moving average filters, which may easily be extended to a wider class of filters, we prove the exponential concentration of
(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})
to
(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
, in contrast to
(\overline{\mathbf{G}},\overline{\mathbf{b}})
. This implies that for a wider class of libraries (only requiring
{\mathcal{F}}
to be locally Lipschitz) and arbitrary symmetric sub-Gaussian noise, that we get
\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)\subset S^{\star}
with high probability using the hyperparameter-free MSTLS algorithm. As before, this is strengthened to
\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)=S^{\star}
if an additional condition on
(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
is satisfied.

4.1
Asymptotic consistency without filtering

Recall from Section
2.3
that for trigonometric and polynomial libraries we have
\text{span}({\mathcal{F}}\star\rho)\subset\text{span}({\mathcal{F}})

2.4.1

2.4.4

\mathbf{A}=\text{blkdiag}(\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(p_{\max})},\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{s=1},\dots,\underbrace{\mathbf{A}^{(p_{\max})},\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{s=S})

(4.1)

where

F^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\star\rho=F^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\qquad P^{(p_{\max})}\star\rho=P^{(p_{\max})}\mathbf{A}^{(p_{\max})}

such that
\overline{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{G}^{\star}\mathbf{A}
. For all symmetric noise distributions
\rho

\mathbf{D}^{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\text{diag}(\hat{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\omega})),\qquad\mathbf{A}^{(p_{\max})}_{ij}:=\delta_{ij}+\mathbf{L}_{ij}^{(p_{\max})}=\begin{cases}{j\choose i}M_{j-i}(\rho)&0\leq i\leq j\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

provided the highest moment

We can decompose
\mathbf{A}
into diagonal and off-diagonal matrices as follows,

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{L}

(4.2)

Lemma 4.1

Let

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\rho
be a Gaussian mean-zero noise distribution and

For

Now assume that

or

Remark 4.1
.

Lemma

\displaystyle\qquad-\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}}\right\|\left(\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{(m),0}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\mathbf{w}^{(m),\widehat{\lambda}}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}}\right\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\delta_{2}-\left(\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\mathfrak{J}}+\sqrt{K\mathfrak{J}}\left(\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}}\right\|_{2}+\delta_{1}\right)+2C\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}}\right\|_{2}\right)t

\displaystyle=:\delta_{2}-C^{\prime}t

where we used the upper-bound
t<\frac{\delta_{1}}{2C}
and the fact that

\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}-\mathbf{w}^{(m),\widetilde{\lambda}}}\right\|_{\infty}<Ct

(4.17)

for any
\lambda,\widetilde{\lambda}
that result in
\text{supp}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{(m),\widetilde{\lambda}}\right)
, since (
4.16
)
implies that
\left\|{\mathbf{G}^{(m)}_{S^{\prime}}-\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\prime}}}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{\infty}}<t
on any subset
S^{\prime}\subset\{1,\dots,\mathfrak{J}\}
.
Hence, for sufficiently large
m
and any

t<\min\left\{\frac{\sigma_{\mathfrak{J}}(\overline{\mathbf{G}})}{\sqrt{2K\mathfrak{J}}},\ \frac{\delta_{1}}{2C},\ \frac{\delta_{2}}{C^{\prime}}\right\}:=\tau,

we get that
\text{supp}\left(\text{MSTLS}^{(1)}(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})\right)\subset S^{\star}
with probability exceeding
1-2K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}\left(mt\right)^{2/p_{\max}}\right)
.

To prove the coefficient accuracy, if
\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\right)=S^{\star}
(i.e.
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}=\mathbf{w}^{(m),\widehat{\lambda}}
), (
4.17
) implies that

\left\|{{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}-(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}})^{\dagger}\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{\infty}<Ct

where
C=C(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S}^{\star},(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S}^{\star})^{\dagger}\overline{\mathbf{b}})
is again defined in (
4.11
). Furthermore, properties of
\mathbf{A}
imply that for
C^{\prime\prime}
depending on the maximum polynomial degree
p
and maximum frequency
\omega_{\max}
appearing in the true model we have
\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}}-\mathbf{G}_{S^{\star}}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}<C^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{2}
, which together with Lemma
E.1
implies that

\left\|{(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}}^{\dagger}-\mathbf{G}_{S^{\star}}^{\dagger})\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}<\widetilde{C}\sigma^{2}.

Altogether, if
\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\right)=S^{\star}
, then with probability exceeding
1-2K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2}\left(mt\right)^{2/p_{\max}}\right)
it holds that

\left\|{{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|{{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}-(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}})^{\dagger}\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{(\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}})^{\dagger}\overline{\mathbf{b}}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ct+\widetilde{C}\sigma^{2}\leq C^{\prime}(t+\sigma^{2}).

Remark 4.3
.

As alluded to at the beginning of this section, if we make an additional assumption on the noise-free continuous data, we can strengthen Lemma

to ensure

for all sufficiently small

with high probability for all sufficiently small

and sufficiently large

. This condition is the following:

\mu^{\star}:=\min_{S\subsetneq S^{\star}}\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|}{\left\|{\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|}-\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}}>0.

(4.18)

Using that
\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\mathbf{b}^{\star}=\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S}\mathbf{w}^{\star}_{S}
, in words, this says that the contribution of each subset of true terms

that is orthogonal to the subspace spanned by the remaining terms (
\text{span}(\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S})

(\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}})\left\|{\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|

satisfies

4.2
Asymptotic consistency with filtering

In this section we show that filtering the data prior to building the linear system
(\mathbf{G}^{(m)},\mathbf{b}^{(m)})
results in unconditional convergence of estimators to the true weights, in other words
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\to\mathbf{w}^{\star}
in probability, provided the filtering window scales appropriately.

We define the filtered data
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m)}
with respect to a discrete convolutional filter
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
as

\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m)}=\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}*\mathbf{U}^{(m)},

where
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}^{\nu}\times\cdots\times n_{d+1}^{\nu}}
satisfies
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{1}}=1
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}>0
(i.e
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
is a discrete probability distribution). For convenience we perform symmetric reflection of the data at the boundaries to maintain the same number of data points in
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m)}
and
\mathbf{U}^{(m)}
. The filter
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
is characterized by its
filter width
in total number of gridpoints at level
m
:

m^{(\nu)}:=\prod_{i=1}^{d+1}n_{i}^{\nu}.

The class of
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
resulting in convergence is large, however for simplicity we restrict our attention to the simple moving average filter, in which case each entry of
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
is equal to
1/m^{(\nu)}
. In particular, we show that the moving average filter provides convergence so long as
m^{(\nu)}\gtrsim m^{\alpha}
as
m\to\infty
for some
\alpha<1
, and as before,
m
is the number of points that the test function
\psi
is support on at the grid resolution
(\Delta x^{(m)},\Delta t^{(m)})
. Since the simple moving average filter reduces variance by a factor of
1/m^{(\nu)}=\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{2}}^{2}
, we conjecture that the results below hold for other filters satisfying
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{2}}^{2}\leq m^{-\alpha}
.

We denote the WSINDy linear system built using the filtered data
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m)}
as

(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)}).

Our approach is to show that
(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)})\to(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
in probability, after which the full-rank assumption on
\mathbf{G}^{\star}
implies that the least-squares solution
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{0}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)})^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)}
converges to
\mathbf{w}^{\star}
. The main hurdle in proving this is the correlations that arise from filtering the data, since entries of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)})
are now sums of
correlated
heavy-tailed random variables, which falls outside of the analysis of works
[
35
,
4
]
. The proof can be found in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let assumptions
2.4.1
,
2.4.2
, and
2.4.4

hold and let
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}
be a simple moving average filter with
m^{(\nu)}\gtrsim m^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Further, let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a library of locally Lipschitz functions such that
\forall f\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}
,

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}|x-y|\left(1+|x-y|^{p_{\max}-1}\right)

for some
C_{\mathcal{F}}\geq 0
and
p_{\max}\geq 1
. Then for
t>\mathfrak{t}(m)
, where
\mathfrak{t}(m)={\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\alpha\left(\frac{k}{d+1}-\frac{1}{2}\right)})
and
k
is the Sobolev regularity of
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}(D)
, and sufficiently large
m
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)})-(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})\|_{\infty}>t\right)\leq 3K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2^{1+p_{\max}}}[m(t-\mathfrak{t}(m))]^{2/{p_{\max}}}\right).

where
c
is the same rate from Theorem
3.1
.

Remark 4.4
.

A more careful analysis will lead to the rate
c
replaced by a variable rate
c^{*}(m)
(bounded below by
c
from Theorem
3.1
) that increases as
\sim m^{\alpha}
, since filtering transforms the sub-Gaussian noise distribution at each
m
into a new distribution
\rho^{(m)}
that has a reduced variance
\sigma^{2}/m^{\alpha}
.

Remark 4.5
.

As well as factors which serve to increase the rate
c
in Theorem
4.2
, convergence is largely dictated by how rapidly
\mathfrak{t}(m)
decreases. This is determined by three deterministic effects: (1) the bias
|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}-\mathbf{U}^{(m),\star}|
between the filtered and unfiltered noise-free data, (2) the distributional convergence of
\rho^{(m)}
to
\delta_{0}
as
m\to\infty
, and (3) the convergence of the trapezoidal rule on functions
\partial\psi^{\alpha^{s}}(\cdot)f(u(\cdot))
. In the case that
u
is locally polynomial of degree
q+1
, higher-order filters can be used to reduce (1) to
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\frac{(q+1)(1-\alpha)}{d+1}})
. However, in most cases (2) still dominates, limiting
\mathfrak{t}(m)
to
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\alpha/2})
. If
{\mathcal{F}}
contains only analytic functions, then (2) acquires the rate
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\alpha})
. Higher-order filters are of no help in decreasing (2) since
|f*\rho^{(m)}(u)-f*\delta_{0}(u)|={\mathcal{O}}(\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}}\right\|_{2}^{2})
, and
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{(m)}}\right\|_{2}^{2}
is minimized at the simple moving average filter. For (3), if
u\in H^{k}
with
k>(d+1)/2
and
f_{j}
are smooth, then the convergence rate of the trapezoidal rule is
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-k+(d+1)/2})
, which leads to rapidly decreasing
\mathfrak{t}(m)
with higher
k
, and thus faster entry into the region of exponential concentration. Ultimately, while filtering the data enables concentration to the noise-free problem, it is clear that more work is needed to make filtering useful in practice due to these intermediate biases.

Lastly, Lemma
4.4
and Theorem
4.2
directly imply the following coefficient accuracy.

Corollary 4.1
.

Under the same conditions as Theorem
4.2
, it holds that

\left\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m),0}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ct

with probability exceeding
1-3K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2^{1+p_{\max}}}[m(t-\mathfrak{t}(m))]^{2/{p_{\max}}}\right)
,
where
C=C(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{w}^{\star})
is defined in Lemma
4.4
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{w}}^{(m),0}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)})^{\dagger}\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}
is the filtered least squares solution.

Remark 4.6
.

Since it is not advised to simply use the least-squares solution as in the previous corollary (which will in general not be sparse), we note that Theorem
4.2
directly implies that
\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\right)\subset\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
with high probability as in Theorem
4.1
, where here
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}=\text{MSTLS}^{(1)}(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)})
is the one-shot MSTLS solution on the filtered linear system
(\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)},\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}^{(m)})
. Moreover, in the case of filtered data the result is not restricted to trigonometric and polynomial libraries, but holds for any locally Lipschitz library
{\mathcal{F}}
. Finally, condition (
4.18
) implies full support recovery
\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}^{(m)}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
with high probability from filtered data, using similar arguments as in Appendix
E
.

5
Numerical Experiments

We now test the theoretical results in the previous sections using four example problems: (1) the Lorenz system, (2) a hyper-diffusive Kuramoto-Sivashinsky (KS)-type equation, (3) a cubic oscillator, and (4) a nonlinear viscous Burgers-type model. Examples (1) and (2) are ordinary and partial differential equations, respectively, which do not exhibit a critical noise (equivalently,
\sigma_{c}=\infty

m\to\infty
across the noise spectrum. In contrast, systems (3) and (4) do exhibit a finite critical noise
\sigma_{c}
, and in these cases we show that a simple moving average filter is sufficient to enable convergence for
\sigma\geq\sigma_{c}
.

Remark 5.1
.

For cases of unconditional convergence (examples (1) and (2)) we do not report results for recovery from filtered data, as these were consistently worse than their unfiltered counterparts. This is supported by Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
, as convergence for the filtered linear system is slower than for raw data.

For each example we subsample a high-accuracy fine-grid simulation of the system in order to test the performance at different test function support size

5.4.2
Nonlinear Viscous Burgers

In our last example we examine the model

\partial_{t}u=(0.01)\partial_{xx}u+(-0.5)\partial_{x}(u^{2})+(-1)u^{3}+(2)u^{2}+(1)u^{0},

(5.8)

which allows for explicit computation of the critical noise level

similar to the cubic oscillator. Specifically, the

\sigma

10^{-2}\sigma_{c}\approx 0.0006
to
10\sigma_{c}\approx 0.5774
. In terms of the noise ratio
\sigma_{NR}

\left\|{\mathbf{U}^{(m),\star}}\right\|_{stdev^{\prime}}\approx 1.16

\sigma_{NR}
in the range
0.0005
to
0.5
.

We simulate (
5.8
) on a fine grid
(\mathbf{X}^{f},\mathbf{t}^{f})\subset[-1,1]\times[0,1.5]

(64,57)

6
Discussion

In this work we have provided an in-depth analysis of the WSINDy algorithm in the limit of continuum data. We have presented results in as general a framework as possible, in particular showing convergence for non-smooth solutions of differential equations. This analysis includes several key results that can be used to improve algorithms for equation learning in general. In particular, we identify that WSINDy models may in general be only conditionally consistent with the ground truth, and that spurious terms may arise in the limit if the noise level is above a critical threshold. Included in this result is the identification of a large class of models for which WSINDy
does
recover the correct model in the limit for any noise level, which explains the previously report robustness of weak-form methods.

We also examine a filtered WSINDy approach, and identify that filtering the data leads to unconditional convergence for a wide range of systems and noise distributions. We propose that optimal filtering be a major priority for future algorithms, as the theoretical advantages of filtering are undeniable, yet is well-known that filtering can lead to much worse results if disadvantageous filters are use (see e.g.
[
9
]
).

As far as other numerical results, we demonstrated that the theoretical findings are in fact exhibited in practice. In particular, filtering the data enables recovery of systems beyond the critical noise level. Also, crucially, for system which
do not
exhibit a critical noise level, we do observe unconditional convergence, no matter how high the orders of spatial derivatives are in true model (as in the Hyper-KS case).

We now offer several ideas for extensions and combination of our results with existing results.

(I)

We have examined only models that can be integrated-by-parts to put all derivatives onto the test function. For other such terms, it is possible to use local-polynomial differentiation as in
[
12
]
and then apply the weak form to get convergence. We conjecture that local polynomial filtering may also provide advantages over the simple moving average filter employed here for general data filtering, hence a hybrid weak-form / local-polynomial discretization appears advantageous both theoretically and in practice.

(II)

In this work we have restricted our analyses to cases where the true model is contained in the model library. A recent work
[
27
]
has proven the convergence of WSINDy surrogate models using terms that are not restricted to the true support set. A fruitful future direction would be to merge these results and our current findings to extend results in
[
27
]
to the case of discrete and noisy data.

(III)

While results are presented here for sub-Gaussian noise distributions, extensions to heavier-tailed noise distributions are of course possible. For instance, the results above carry over immediately to sub-exponential noise albeit with slower concentration rates.

(IV)

We have analyzed the MSTLS algorithm from
[
18
]
due to its practical performance and speed. We also conjecture that thresholding-based sparse regression routines may offer an advantage in the case highly-correlated linear system. However, alternative sparse optimization techniques as employed in
[
8
]
and
[
5
]
may offer additional advantages with regard to the biases introduced by nonlinear functions in
{\mathcal{F}}
. On the other hand, suitably rescaling the data and coordinates as introduced in
[
18
]
has the ability to increase the critical noise threshold
\sigma_{c}
(e.g. by increasing the lower bound in equation (
4.5
)). Altogether, we believe that our results may inform future developments in sparse regression algorithms for equation learning.

Appendix B
Convergence of the trapezoidal rule for
{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open subset and
k>n/2
. Define
r=\left\lceil k-\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil-1
and
\alpha=k-\frac{n}{2}-r
. By the Sobolev embedding theorem, we have

H^{k}(U)\subset C^{r,\gamma}(U)

where
\gamma=\alpha
if
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and if
\alpha=1
the embedding holds for any
\gamma\in(0,1)
. The Hölder space
C^{r,\gamma}(U)
is defined

C^{r,\gamma}(U)=\left\{f\in C(U)\ :\ L:=\max_{|\beta|\leq r}\sup_{x,y\in U}\frac{|\partial^{\beta}f(x)-\partial^{\beta}f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\gamma}}<\infty\right\}.

The following lemma verifies that the trapezoidal rule converges for functions
\partial^{\alpha^{s}}\psi(\cdot)f(u(\cdot))
appearing in the WSINDy linear system, so long as
u\in{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}(\Omega\times(0,T))
and
\psi
,
f
are smooth. For smoother functions the convergence rate is greater.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
g\in{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}(D)
have compact support where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and bounded. Then the multidimensional trapezoidal rule approximation of
\int_{D}g(x)dx
over a grid
\mathbf{X}\subset\overline{D}
with uniform mesh width
h
takes the form

I_{h}(g)=h^{n}\sum_{x_{i}\in\mathbf{X}}g(x_{i}).

Then we have the following cases:

(i)

If
k>\frac{n}{2}
and we also have
g\in H^{k}(D)
, then for
C
independent of
h
it holds that

\left|I_{h}(g)-\int_{D}g(x)dx\right|\leq Ch^{k-\frac{n}{2}}.

(ii)

If
\frac{n}{2}<k\leq\frac{n}{2}+1
, then for
C
independent of
h
it holds that

\left|I_{h}(g)-\int_{D}g(x)dx\right|\leq Ch^{k-\frac{n}{2}},

(iii)

If
k>\frac{n}{2}+1
, then for
C
independent of
h
it holds that

\left|I_{h}(g)-\int_{D}g(x)dx\right|\leq Ch.

Proof.

Case
(i)
follows from the compact support of
g
using Fourier analysis. We now examine cases
(ii)
and
(iii)
. By the definition of
{\mathcal{H}}^{k,\infty}(D)
, there exists a finite partition
D=D_{1}\cup\cdots\cup D_{\ell}
where each subdomain
D_{i}
has Lipschitz boundary. Define the interior boundary

{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{L}\partial D_{i}\right)\setminus\partial D

and the
\varepsilon
-tube of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}

{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}={\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}+B_{n}(0,\varepsilon)

where
B_{n}(0,\varepsilon)
is the ball of radius
\varepsilon
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
centered at
0
. Due to the regularity of the boundaries, the surface measure of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}
is finite, hence the volume of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}|{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}|=0
. Fix
h>0
so that
D
is partitioned into
M=|D|/h^{n}
hypercubes and choose
\varepsilon<h/2
so that
|{\mathcal{S}}_{\varepsilon}|\leq|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|h
. Any given hypercube
K
that intersects
{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}
contributes a worst-case error of

\left|\frac{h^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}g_{j}-\int_{K}g(x)dx\right|\leq 2\left\|{g}\right\|_{\infty}h^{n},

where
g_{j}=g(x^{(j)})
for vertices
(x^{(j)})_{j=1}^{2^{n}}
of
K
. There are fewer than
M\frac{h|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|}{|D|}
such hypercubes.

For case
(ii)
, using the mean value theorem and the Hölder regularity, we have that integration over hypercubes
K
with
K\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}=\emptyset
satisfies

\left|\frac{h^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}g_{j}-\int_{K}g(x)dx\right|=\left|\frac{h^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}\left(g_{j}-g(\widetilde{x})\right)\right|\leq\frac{Lh^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}|x^{(j)}-\widetilde{x}|^{\gamma}\leq L\sqrt{n}^{k-\frac{n}{2}}h^{k+\frac{n}{2}}

where
g(\widetilde{x})=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}g(x)dx
. Here
L
is the Hölder constant for
g
in
C^{0,k-\frac{n}{2}}(D)
. This gives us the error

E\leq\left(M\frac{h|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|}{|D|}\right)2\left\|{g}\right\|_{\infty}h^{n}+ML\sqrt{n}^{k-\frac{n}{2}}h^{k+\frac{n}{2}}

\leq 2\left\|{g}\right\|_{\infty}|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|h+L|D|\sqrt{n}^{k-\frac{n}{2}}h^{k-\frac{n}{2}}\leq Ch^{k-\frac{n}{2}}.

For case
(iii)
, we have that
g\big|_{D_{i}}\in C^{1,\gamma}
where
\gamma=\min(1,k-\frac{n}{2}-1)
for
i=1,\dots,\ell
, hence we can exploit smoothness in the 1st derivative. In the 1D case (
n=1
), using that

g(x)=g(0)+\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(y)dy=g(h)-\int_{x}^{h}g^{\prime}(y)dy

we have the following:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{h}{2}(g(0)+g(h))-\int_{0}^{h}g(x)dx\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{h}\left(\int_{0}^{x}g^{\prime}(y)dy-\int_{x}^{h}g^{\prime}(y)dy\right)dx\right|

(switch order of integration)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{y}(g^{\prime}(x)-g^{\prime}(y))dxdy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L}{2}\int_{0}^{h}\int_{0}^{y}(y-x)^{\gamma}dxdy

\displaystyle=\frac{L}{2(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{2+\gamma}.

We can extend this to the trapezoidal rule in
n
dimensions as follows. Let
K
be a hypercube satisfying
K\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}=\emptyset
with vertices
(x^{(j)})_{j=1}^{2^{n}}
. Then

\left|\frac{h^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}g_{j}-\int_{K}g(x)dx\right|=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left|\int_{K}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}g(z_{j}(t))dt\right)dx\right|

where each
z_{j}
is a connected curve satisfying
z_{j}(0)=x^{(j)}
and
z_{j}(1)=x
, using that

g(x)=g(x^{(j)})+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}g(z_{j}(t))dt,\qquad j=1,\dots,2^{n}.

Since this holds for any absolutely continuous curves
z_{j}
, we can select curves to cancel analogously to the 1D case. Let each
z_{j}
take the path from
x_{j}
to
x
along the coordinate axes. For example, let
x^{(1)}=(0,\dots,0)
,
x^{(2)}=(h,0,\dots,0)
and consider the paths

z_{1}:x^{(1)}\to(x_{1},0,\dots,0)\to(x_{1},x_{2},0,\dots,0)\to\cdots\to x.

z_{2}:x^{(2)}\to(x_{1},0,\dots,0)\to(x_{1},x_{2},0,\dots,0)\to\cdots\to x.

Then combining the first edges of each path in the sum above leads to the term

I=\int_{K}\left(\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\partial_{x_{1}}g(y_{1},0,\dots,0)dy_{1}-\int_{x_{1}}^{h}\partial_{x_{1}}g(y_{1},0,\dots,0)dy_{1}\right)dx

upon which we can use the same trick as before to swap the order of integration, leading to

I\leq\frac{L}{(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{2+\gamma+(n-1)}.

Each of the
2^{n}
paths
z_{j}
have
n
edges, each of which gets paired with another edge to yield a similar bound, leading to an overall
n2^{n}/2
such error terms, or

\left|\frac{h^{n}}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{2^{n}}g_{j}-\int_{K}g(x)dx\right|\leq\frac{n2^{n-1}}{2^{n}}\frac{L}{(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{2+\gamma+(n-1)}=\frac{n}{2}\frac{L}{(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{1+\gamma+n}.

This holds for all
K\cap{\mathcal{S}}_{int}=\emptyset
, and the overall error satisfies

E\leq\left(M\frac{h|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|}{|D|}\right)2\left\|{g}\right\|_{\infty}h^{n}+M\frac{n}{2}\frac{L}{(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{1+\gamma+n}

\leq 2\left\|{g}\right\|_{\infty}|{\mathcal{S}}_{\text{int}}|h+\frac{n}{2}\frac{L|D|}{(\gamma+1)(\gamma+2)}h^{1+\gamma}\leq Ch.

Appendix C
Moment matrix lemmas

Lemma C.1
.

Define the Gaussian moment matrix

\mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1,&i=j\\
{j\choose i}(j-i-1)!!\sigma^{j-i},&j>i,\ (j-i)\ \text{even}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(C.1)

Then the inverse of
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}
is given by

(\mathbf{A}^{(p)})^{-1}_{i,j}:=(-1)^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{i,j}.

Proof.

Define
\mathbf{B}^{(p)}_{i,j}:=(-1)^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{i,j}
and consider an entry of the product
\mathbf{C}_{i,j}=\left(\mathbf{A}^{(p)}\mathbf{B}^{(p)}\right)_{i,j}
. We first note that
\mathbf{C}_{i,j}=0
if either
j<i
or
j-i
is odd, the former due to the fact that
\mathbf{C}
is upper triangular, the latter because both
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(p)}
have a “checkerboard” sparsity pattern with zeros on odd superdiagonals, a pattern which is inherited by
\mathbf{C}
. Next since both
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(p)}
have 1’s along the diagonal,
\mathbf{C}
will also have 1’s along the diagonal. For the remaining entries, in other words
\mathbf{C}_{i,j}
with
j-i
an even positive integer,

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{j-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=i\\
k-i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{j}{k\choose k-i}{j\choose j-k}(k-i-1)!!(j-k-1)!!(-1)^{\frac{j-k}{2}}

(C.2)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{j-i}{j\choose j-i}(j-i)!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=i\\
k-i\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{j}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{j-k}{2}}}{(k-i)!!(j-k)!!}

(C.3)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{j-i}{j\choose j-i}(j-i)!\sum_{\ell=0}^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\frac{(-1)^{\frac{j-i-2\ell}{2}}}{(2\ell)!!(j-i-2\ell)!!}

(C.4)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{\sigma^{j-i}{j\choose j-i}(j-i)!}{2^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\left(\frac{j-i}{2}\right)!}(-1)^{\frac{j-i}{2}}\right)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\frac{j-i}{2}}(-1)^{\ell}{\frac{j-i}{2}\choose\ell}\right)

(C.5)

\displaystyle=0,

(C.6)

where we used the identities

{p\choose q}{p-q\choose j}={p\choose q+j}{q+j\choose j},\qquad p-q\geq j\geq 0

and

\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}(-1)^{\ell}{p\choose\ell}=0,\qquad p\geq 1.

This shows that
\mathbf{C}
is the identity, so that
\mathbf{B}^{(p)}=(\mathbf{A}^{(p)})^{-1}
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}=\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{L}^{(p)}
be the Gaussian moment matrix defined in (
C.1
). Then it holds that

\max\{\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{2}\}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1}\leq\exp\left(\sigma^{2}{p\choose 2}\right)

and

\max\{\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{2}\}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1}\leq\sigma^{2}{p\choose 2}\exp\left(\sigma^{2}{p\choose 2}\right).

Proof.

Set
\alpha=\sigma^{2}{p\choose 2}
. First, note that the maximum entry along any superdiagonal of
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}
occurs in the
p
th column, i.e.

\max_{j-i=k}\mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{ij}=\mathbf{A}^{(p)}_{p-k,p}:=y_{k}

where for
k
even,

y_{k}={p\choose p-k}(k-1)!!\sigma^{k}=\frac{{p\choose p-k}(k-1)!!}{{p\choose 2}^{\frac{k}{2}}}\alpha^{k/2}

and for
k
odd, we have
y_{k}=0
. This implies that the
p
th column achieves the maximum column sum, so that
\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1}=\left\|{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{1}

\left\|{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{1}
, hence
\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1}
. Now let

be the Toeplitz matrix formed by the vector
\mathbf{y}

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})=\begin{bmatrix}y_{0}&y_{1}&\cdots&y_{p}\\
y_{p}&y_{0}&\cdots&y_{p-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

Since the entries of
\mathbf{A}^{(p)}
are positive, we have

\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{1}=\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1},

where
\left\|{\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{1}

and
\left\|{\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{x}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\mathbf{y}}\right\|_{1}
is achieved by

. Revisiting the entries of
\mathbf{y}
, we then have for

so that

k\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{p}\frac{\alpha^{k/2}}{\left(\frac{k}{2}\right)!}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}\frac{\alpha^{\ell}}{\ell!}\leq\exp(\alpha).

The inequalities for

\max\{\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{\infty},\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{2}\}\leq\left\|{\mathbf{L}^{(p)}}\right\|_{1}=\sum_{k=2}^{p}y_{k}=\alpha\sum_{\ell=0}^{\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor}\frac{\alpha^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{1}{\ell+1}\leq\alpha\exp(\alpha).

locally Lipschitz and with polynomial growth, we get

|\rho^{(m)}\star f_{j}(x)-f_{j}(x)|\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\sqrt{M_{2}(\rho^{(m)})}+M_{p_{\max}}(\rho^{(m)})\right)\leq C_{\mathcal{F}}C_{\sigma}\frac{\sigma}{m^{\alpha/2}}

where we used Jensen’s inequality and sub-Gaussianity of
\rho^{(m)}
to relate higher moments to the variance, and
C_{\sigma}
depends on
p_{max}
and
\rho
but not
m
. It should be noted that this rate also increases to
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\alpha})
for polynomial and trigonometric libraries
{\mathcal{F}}
.

Hence for
m
large enough, it suffices to consider

\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1}+I_{2}>t^{\prime}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(I_{1}>t^{\prime}/2\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(I_{2}>t^{\prime}/2\right):=E_{1}+E_{2},

where
t^{\prime}=t-\sum_{i=3}^{5}I_{i}
, and for
k>1+\frac{d+1}{2}
,
t^{\prime}=t-\widetilde{C}m^{-\frac{\alpha}{d+1}}
. For
E_{2}
we are in the same situation as in Theorem
3.1
, hence for
t^{\prime}>0
we can use Corollary
3.1
to get the exponential concentration:

E_{2}=\mathbb{P}\left(\|\widehat{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}-\overline{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}\|_{\overrightarrow{\infty}}>t^{\prime}/2\right)\leq 2K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2^{1+2/{p_{\max}}}}(mt^{\prime})^{2/{p_{\max}}}\right).

This leaves
E_{1}
, which we now show also yields exponential concentration, but at a sub-Gaussian rate. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}_{ksj}-\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{ksj}^{(m)}|>t^{\prime}/2\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(|\left\langle\partial^{\alpha^{s}}\psi_{k},f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)})-f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}+\widehat{\epsilon}^{(m)})\right\rangle_{m}|>t^{\prime}/2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\partial^{\alpha^{s}}\psi_{k}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|{f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)})-f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}+\widehat{\epsilon}^{(m)})}\right\|_{\overrightarrow{1}}>mt^{\prime}/(2\gamma T|\Omega|)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}\left(|f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i})-f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)})|>Ct^{\prime}\right)

where
C=(2\gamma T|\Omega|\left\|{\partial^{\alpha^{s}}\psi_{k}}\right\|_{\infty})^{-1}
. (Here we replaced the volume element
(\Delta x)^{d}\Delta t
with the equivalent expression
\gamma\frac{T|\Omega|}{m}
using that
|\text{supp}\left(\psi\right)|=\gamma T|\Omega|
, for some
\gamma<1
.) Now, define the sets

A_{i}=\{|f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i})-f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widehat{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i})|>Ct^{\prime}\},

B_{i}=\{|\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)}|>1\}.

We get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i})-f_{j}(\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}_{i}+\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)})|>Ct^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{i})+\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{i}^{c})

where, using sub-Gaussianity,

\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{i})\leq\mathbb{P}(B_{i})\leq\widetilde{C}e^{-\frac{m^{\alpha}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

and using the locally Lipschitz condition on
f_{j}
, together with the independent of
\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}
and
\widehat{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{i}\cap B_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\{C_{\mathcal{F}}(|\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)}|+|\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)}|^{p_{\max}})>Ct^{\prime}\}\cap\{|\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)}|<1\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(m)}_{i}-\widehat{\epsilon}_{i}^{(m)}|>\frac{C}{2C_{\mathcal{F}}}t^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{C}e^{-\frac{m^{\alpha}}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{Ct^{\prime}}{2C_{\mathcal{F}}}\right)^{2}}.

Hence, summing over all
i
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(|\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}_{ksj}-\widehat{\mathbf{G}}_{ksj}^{(m)}|>t^{\prime}/2\right)\leq\widetilde{C}me^{-c_{1}m^{\alpha}(t^{\prime})^{2}}

for some fixed
c_{1}>0
, which shows that the term
E_{2}
asymptotically dominates
E_{1}
.

Overall this shows that
\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}
converges to
\mathbf{G}^{\star}
elementwise, and since the number of elements is fixed, we can take a union bound to conclude that
\|\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{G}^{\star}\|_{\infty}\to 0
in probability at the same rate. We can characterize this convergence as follows. (1) For each
m
we acrue a deterministic error
\mathfrak{t}(m)
due to the bias introduced when averaging the clean data, as well as the integration errors. The worst-case asymptotic order of this error is
{\mathcal{O}}(m^{-\alpha\min\{\frac{1}{d+1},\frac{k}{d+1}-\frac{1}{2}\}})
and arises from
I_{4}
, the difference between
\overline{\mathbf{G}}^{(m),\star}
which uses the filtered clean data
\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}^{(m),\star}
and
\mathbf{G}^{(m),\star}
which uses the clean data
\mathbf{U}^{(m),\star}
(in effect,
I_{4}
is the combined bias and integration error). (2) For
t>\mathfrak{t}(m)
and large enough
m
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{G}^{\star}\|_{\infty}>t\right)\leq 3K\mathfrak{J}\exp\left(-\frac{c}{2^{1+2/{p_{\max}}}}[m(t-\mathfrak{t}(m))]^{2/{p_{\max}}}\right),

using the worst-case concentration rate arising from the error
I_{2}
.

Appendix E
Full support recovery

As indicated in Remark
4.3
, in this section we extend the results (
4.9
) of Lemma
4.3
and (
4.12
) of Theorem
4.1
to subset equality

\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)\qquad\&\qquad\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right),

respectively, with the addition of a constraint on the linear system
(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
. First we need another stability lemma similar to Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
have rank
n
and let
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{E}
be a perturbed system satisfying
\left\|{\mathbf{E}}\right\|_{2}<\varepsilon
. For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, there exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
\mathbf{A}
such that the following stability holds for the pseudoinverse
\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}:=(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}
and projection operator
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}:=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}
:

\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}}\right\|_{2}<C\varepsilon\qquad\&\qquad\left\|{\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}-\mathbf{P}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}}}\right\|_{2}<C^{\prime}\varepsilon.

(E.1)

Proof.

The pseudoinverse of
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
is given by

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{T}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{T}=(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B})^{-1}(\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}+(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}),

provided
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B})^{-1}
exists, where

\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}+(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{E}).

A sufficient condition for invertibility of
\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B}
is

\left\|{\mathbf{B}}\right\|_{2}<1,

which is guaranteed for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
since
\left\|{\mathbf{B}}\right\|_{2}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
. In this case using the Von Neumann series for
(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{B})^{-1}
, we get that

\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}+\widetilde{\mathbf{E}},

where

\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}=(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-\mathbf{B})^{k}\left(\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}+(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{E}^{T}\right).

Since
\mathbf{E}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
and
\mathbf{B}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
, we have
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon)
, hence

\left\|{\mathbf{A}^{\dagger}-\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}}\right\|_{2}={\mathcal{O}}(\varepsilon).

The same stability readily applies to
\mathbf{P}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}}=\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{A}}+\mathbf{A}\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}+\mathbf{E}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\dagger}
.

We can now use this stability to extend Lemma
4.3
to yield full support recovery on the continuum problem for small enough
\sigma^{2}
, provided condition (
E.2
) is satisfied.

Lemma E.2
.

Let
\rho
be a Gaussian mean-zero noise distribution and let
p
be the maximum polynomial degree appearing in the true model. Assume that
(\mathbf{G}^{\star},\mathbf{b}^{\star})
satisfy

\mu^{\star}:=\min_{S\subsetneq S^{\star}}\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|}{\left\|{\mathbf{b}^{\star}}\right\|}-\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}}>0.

(E.2)

Then there exists a critical noise level
\sigma_{c}
such that for any
\sigma\leq\sigma_{c}
the estimator
{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}=\text{MSTLS}^{(1)}(\overline{\mathbf{G}},\overline{\mathbf{b}})
satisfies

\text{supp}\left({\widehat{\mathbf{w}}}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right).

(E.3)

Proof.

In Lemma
4.3
we already showed that

(a)

For sufficiently small
\sigma
there exists
\widehat{\lambda}\in\boldsymbol{\lambda}
such that
\text{supp}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)
, where
\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}=\text{STLS}^{(1)}(\overline{\mathbf{G}},\overline{\mathbf{b}},\widehat{\lambda})
.

(b)

For sufficiently small
\sigma
we have

\frac{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}\right)}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}<\frac{1}{\mathfrak{J}}

which implies that for
\lambda\in\boldsymbol{\lambda}
with
\lambda<\widehat{\lambda}
, we have

{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\lambda})<{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda).

Assume that
\sigma
is small enough so that (a) and (b) hold, and consider
\lambda\in\boldsymbol{\lambda}
with
\lambda>\widehat{\lambda}
. All we need to show is that for sufficiently small
\sigma
, we have
{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\lambda})<{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)
in this case as well. Indeed, by construction of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, for
\lambda>\widehat{\lambda}
we have

\text{supp}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}\right)\subsetneq S^{\star}.

Setting
S=S^{\star}\setminus\text{supp}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}\right)
, we see that

{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\lambda})-{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)=\frac{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\widehat{\lambda}}\right)}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}-\frac{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}\right)}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}+\frac{|S|}{\mathfrak{J}}.

Using (b), and rewriting using the projection operator, we have

{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\lambda})-{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)<-\frac{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{G}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{0}-\overline{\mathbf{w}}^{\lambda}\right)}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}+\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}}=-\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}+\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}}.

Now, for small enough
\sigma
, using stability of the projection operator, we have

\max_{S\subsetneq S^{\star}}\left\|{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}-\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}}\right\|_{2}<C\sigma^{2},

since properties of
\mathbf{D}
and
\mathbf{L}
imply

\overline{\mathbf{G}}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}-\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}=\mathbf{G}^{\star}(\mathbf{A}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}-\mathbf{I}_{S^{\star}\setminus S})=\mathbf{G}^{\star}(\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{I})_{S^{\star}\setminus S}+\mathbf{G}^{\star}\mathbf{L}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}={\mathcal{O}}(\sigma^{2}).

Hence, using the reverse triangle inequality we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}(\widehat{\lambda})-{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle<-\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}+\frac{\left\|{\left(\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}-\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{G}^{\star}_{S^{\star}\setminus S}}\right)\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}{\left\|{\overline{\mathbf{b}}}\right\|_{2}}+\frac{|S|+1}{\mathfrak{J}}

\displaystyle<-\mu^{\star}+C\sigma^{2}

which is negative for all
\sigma^{2}<\mu^{\star}/C
. Thus, to recover the full support on systems satisfying (
E.2
), in addition to other constraints derived on
\sigma_{c}
, it holds that
\sigma_{c}^{2}\geq\mu^{\star}/C
.

The previous Lemma, together with results shown in Section
4.1
, directly imply the following, using similar techniques as Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem E.1
.

Let Assumptions
2.4.1
-
2.4.5

E.2

\sigma_{c}>0
and a stability tolerance
\tau
, both independent of
m
, such that for all
\sigma<\sigma_{c}
and

, and for sufficiently large

, it holds that

\text{supp}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}\right)=\text{supp}\left(\mathbf{w}^{\star}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\|{\widehat{\mathbf{w}}^{(m)}-\mathbf{w}^{\star}}\right\|_{\infty}<C^{\prime}(t+\sigma^{2})

(E.4)

with probability exceeding

\partial_{t}u=(1)\partial_{xxxx}u+(0.75)\partial_{xxxxxx}u+(-0.5)\partial_{x}(u^{2})+(0.1)\partial_{xxx}u^{2}.

(F.1)

Motivation for equation

Appendix G
Variance estimation

We estimate the noise variance using the following procedure. Let
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{2L+1}
satisfy the discrete moment conditions

M_{k}(\mathbf{f})=\sum_{j=-L}^{L}j^{k}\mathbf{f}_{f}=0,\quad k=0,\dots,M_{\max}.

and

\left\|{\mathbf{f}}\right\|_{2}=1.

Now consider data
\mathbf{U}=u(\mathbf{t})+\epsilon
such that
u
is locally a polynomial of degree
M_{\max}
over intervals
(t-L\Delta t,t+L\Delta t)
and
\epsilon
is mean zero i.i.d. noise with variance
\sigma^{2}
. Then the data
\mathbf{U}
will satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[|(\mathbf{f}*\mathbf{U})^{2}_{i}\right]=\sigma^{2},\quad i\in\mathbb{Z},

hence

\left\|{\mathbf{f}*\mathbf{U}}\right\|_{rms}\approx\sigma

to very high accuracy. We use this approach and take
\mathbf{f}
to be 6th-order finite difference weights normalized so that
\left\|{\mathbf{f}}\right\|_{2}=1
.
