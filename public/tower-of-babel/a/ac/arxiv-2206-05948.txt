Low noise optimization of an electron beam current transformer for conventional radiotherapy up to ultra high dose rate irradiations.
S. Salvador

Abstract
Ultra high dose rate electron beams also known as FLASH radiotherapy is becoming of importance in several preclinial cancer treatment studies. However, due to the dose rate used during the irradiation sessions, no real time dose monitoring device exists to date. In this work, we present the development of a beam current transformer (BCT), from the choice of the ferromagnetic component and the realisation of the shielding to the design of a front-end electronics based on a trans-impedance circuit in order to perform a low noise optimization of the detector. The BCT prototype is able to monitor a beam current range from 1.2 µA to 200 mA with a rise time constant better than 20 ns and a droop rate of the signal below 0.05% ⋅ \cdot µs -1 . Preliminary in-situ measurements are also presented. The goal is to combine the BCT system which measure in real time the beam current, to an ionisation chamber monitoring the beam shape and position in order to provide a reliable dose monitoring system.



1
Introduction

Ultra High Dose Rate radiotherapy is a promising cancer treatment under research, that involves an almost instantaneous delivery of a high radiation dose (
\geq
40\text{\,}\mathrm{Gy}
) in only a few pulses (
\leq
200\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
)
[
1
]
. The popularity of Ultra High Dose Rate is due to the FLASH effect, a biological effect that induces a tumour control, as in Conventional Radiotherapy, and a significant reduction of the toxicities induced on healthy tissues (skin, organs at risk)
[
2
]
. There is no indication that the sparing effectiveness of FLASH-RT would rest on the type of radiation but most of the pre-clinical studies investigating the FLASH effect have been conducted so far using electron beams generated by dedicated or modified clinical LINACs with energies not exceeding
20\text{\,}\mathrm{MeV}

[
3
,
4
]
. The chemical and biological mechanisms explaining the FLASH effect are still under investigation, however, a difference in term of oxygen concentration and cell reparation processes might explain the sparing effect between tumour and healthy cells
[
5
]
.

From a clinical point of view, FLASH Radiotherapy could allow for more effective treatment of radio-resistant tumours and would greatly reduce the number of sessions associated with a treatment, thus reducing the number of visits to the clinic, the congestion of schedules for clinicians and the overall cost of the treatment
[
6
]
.

Before implementation in clinics, some issues must be solved. In particular, a reliable and accurate monitoring system for measuring the dose in real time must be developed. In conventional treatments, the Ionization Chamber is the reference dosimeter used in all clinical electrons and protons facilities but suffer from an upper limit on the dose rate. Above
0.01\text{\,}\mathrm{Gy}
, higher rates of the physico-chemical reactions (recombination and electronic capture) occurring in the gas induce a loss of the charge collection efficiency that leads to non-linear and non-proportional behaviors rendering them useless for beam monitoring in the case of FLASH Radiotherapy
[
7
]
. In order to resolve these issues, we propose a monitoring system that will rely on a combination of a Beam Current Transformer detector for properly measuring the beam current and an Ionization Chamber that will monitor the beam position and shape.

In this work, we will focus solely on the design and characterization of the Beam Current Transformer and its front-end electronics in terms of noise and temporal responses.
The challenge of the detector design is to be able to monitor very low electron beam current for Conventional Radiotherapy with
6\cdot 10^{-5}\text{\,}\mathrm{Gy}
instantaneous dose rate (around
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro A}
of beam current with a pulse of
3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
, a pulse rate of
180\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}
and a mean dose rate of
2\text{\,}\mathrm{Gy}
) while maintaining a good linearity for instantaneous dose rates up to
10\text{\,}\mathrm{Gy}
(
200\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
within the pulse) in the case of Ultra High Dose Rate and a maximum surface of irradiation of
78.5\text{\,}{\mathrm{cm}}^{2}
.

In the following, parameters that are frequency dependent have been made bold (such as
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{HP}}
) for clarity purposes.

2
BCT requirements

Beam Current Transformers have been widely used in accelerator facilities for measuring the beam current time profile
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
They consist of a toroidal ferrite core which conducts the magnetic field lines induced by the beam current due to its high magnetic permeability
\mu_{r}
, generating a current through a winding of
N
turns of a Kapton wrapped copper wire, and a front-end electronics.

Figure
1
shows the sketch of a BCT system and its simplified equivalent electronic diagram where a load resistor
R_{l}
is used to measure the current
i_{b}/N
that circulates in the winding.

Figure 1
:
(a) Schematic of the Beam Current Transformer based on a toroidal ferrite core in grey, a winding of
N
turns in black lines and a front-end electronics. (b) Simplified equivalent electronic diagram of the Beam Current Transformer with
L
, the self inductance that models the ferrite core,
R_{l}
the load resistor and the low pass filter of the oscilloscope used for acquisition.

The self inductance
L
, also represented in the diagram, that models the ferrite core is given by:

L=A_{0}\cdot N^{2},

(2.1)

where
A_{0}
is the effective inductance which corresponds to the inductance of the ferrite for 1 turn of winding.
A_{0}
is defined by the geometry of the ferrite and its magnetic properties. It’s given by the ferrite manufacturer.

At the output of the winding, a voltage
V_{\mathrm{OUT}}
is measured through
R_{l}
using an oscilloscope having a low-pass filter.
V_{\mathrm{OUT}}
can be evaluated as:

V_{\mathrm{OUT}}=\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{HP}}\cdot\frac{R_{l}}{N}\cdot i_{b},

(2.2)

where
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{LP}}=1/(1+\tau_{\mathrm{LP}}\cdot j\omega)
with
\omega=2\pi f
, is the transfer function of the low pass filter of the oscilloscope with
\tau_{\mathrm{LP}}
the rise time constant of the filter.
\mathbf{H}_{\mathrm{HP}}=\tau_{\mathrm{HP}}\cdot j\omega/(1+\tau_{\mathrm{HP}}\cdot j\omega)
is the transfer function of the high pass filter made by the ferrite core and the winding of
N
turns with
\tau_{\mathrm{HP}}=L/R_{l}=A_{0}\cdot N^{2}/R_{l}
the drop time constant of the filter.

Figure
2
shows the output signal
V_{\mathrm{OUT}}
(blue line) measured in response to a square impulse from a pulse generator (black line) of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
and a width of
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
. This measurement was made using an oscilloscope with a low-pass filter cut-off frequency of
20\penalty\
MHz.

Figure 2
:
The output voltage
V_{\mathrm{OUT}}
(blue line) measured in response to a current from a pulse generator of
1\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
and a width of
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
(black line) showing (a) the drop of the signal and (b) its rise time constant
\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}
.

A drop of the signal is clearly visible on the measured pulse (figure
2
) and defines the so-called Droop Rate parameter as the percentage of signal lost after
1\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
. Here, the Droop Rate value was measured at 7%
\cdot
µs
-1
.
Furthermore, the rise time constant
\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}
illustrated in figure
2
corresponds to the time after a 63% increase in the signal from the base line. The drop time constant can also be defined as a function of the Droop Rate by
\tau_{\mathrm{HP}}=63/\mathrm{DR}
which corresponds to the time after a 63% decrease of the signal from its maximum. These parameters characterize the time response of the system.

In order to be used for either Conventional Radiotherapy and FLASH Radiotherapy irradiation, the Beam Current Transformer system requirements are: i)
\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}
must be less than
20\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
to accurately measure the beam time profile. This impacts mostly the accuracy for FLASH Radiotherapy irradiation as the system will be used in an integrating mode in the case of Conventional Radiotherapy irradiation; ii) A current measurement relative standard uncertainty of less than 1% to comply with the dose measurement protocol with a plane parallel ionisation chamber for electron radiotherapy
[
11
]
. To achieve this, a conservative position is to require a Droop Rate lower than 1% after
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
of beam pulse, which is the maximum pulse width value that the ElectronFlash machine can deliver
[
3
]
. The Droop Rate must therefore be less than 0.25%
\cdot
-1
to avoid
a posteriori
numerical Droop Rate corrections which depend on the beam pulse shape and irradiation conditions. iii) The spectral frequency band of interest of the system [
f_{\mathrm{min}}
;
f_{\mathrm{max}}
] is deduced using the drop and rise time constant by
f_{\mathrm{min}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{\mathrm{HP}}}=630
Hz and
f_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}}=8
MHz; iv) In order to detect low beam currents, of the order of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro A}
in Conventional Radiotherapy irradiation, the Root Mean Square of the beam current noise must be lower than this value.

The front-end electronic modules have been designed to meet all these requirements and are detailed in the following sections.

3
Trans-impedance front-end electronics

The solution proposed in this paper uses a front-end electronics based on a Trans-Impedance circuit. Typically, a Trans-Impedance circuit is composed of an Operational Amplifier and a feedback impedance
\bm{Z}_{f}
which corresponds to a resistor,
R_{f}
, in parallel with a capacitance
C_{f}
, as shown in figure
3
.
R_{f}
defines the trans-impedance gain of the circuit. The Trans-Impedance device behaves like a low-pass filter with a near-zero input impedance.

Figure 3
:
Equivalent electronic diagram of the trans-impedance front-end electronics with a feedback impedance
\bm{Z}_{f}
, an input current
i_{f}
and an Operational Amplifier.

The output voltage of the Trans-Impedance front-end electronics is given by:

V_{\mathrm{OUT}}=-\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot i_{f},

(3.1)

where
\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{f}}=\frac{R_{f}}{1+jR_{f}\cdot C_{f}\cdot\omega}
is the feedback impedance of the Trans-Impedance device with a time constant of
\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}=R_{f}C_{f}
.
i_{f}
is the current that circulates in the feedback components.

In order to cover a wide range of beam currents from
10\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro A}
to
200\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
, one Trans-Impedance module per type of irradiation will be used, each having a different gain: the feedback resistor will be 10 k
\Omega
for Conventional Radiotherapy and 100
\Omega
in FLASH Radiotherapy. The choice of the feedback capacitance was imposed by the rise time constant value specified in section
2
as:

\tau_{\mathrm{rise}}=R_{f}C_{f}\leq 20\penalty\ \mathrm{ns}.

(3.2)

If we admit the feedback resistor values previously defined, in each mode
C_{f}
is limited to:

\mathrm{{ConventionalRadiotherapy}:}\penalty\ C_{f}\leq 2\penalty\ \mathrm{pF\penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ {FLASHRadiotherapy}:}\penalty\ C_{f}\leq 200\penalty\ \mathrm{pF}.

(3.3)

4
Beam current noise definition

The Root Mean Square beam current noise,
\sigma_{b}
, is a function of the noise of the front-end electronics output,
\sigma_{\mathrm{OUT}}
, the number of turns
N
of the winding as well as the feedback resistor,
R_{f}
.

Replacing the load resistor from figure
1
by the Trans-Impedance circuit described in section
3
,
\sigma_{b}
can be expressed as:

\sigma_{b}=\frac{N}{R_{f}}\cdot\sigma_{\mathrm{OUT}}

(4.1)

The Root Mean Square output voltage noise
\sigma_{\mathrm{OUT}}
can be computed using the Power Spectral Density of noise of the front-end electronics, over the frequency band defined for the system from
f_{\textrm{min}}=630
Hz to
f_{\textrm{max}}=8
MHz, such as:

\sigma_{\mathrm{OUT}}=\sqrt{\int_{f_{\mathrm{min}}}^{f_{\mathrm{max}}}\mathbf{PSD}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}f}.

(4.2)

For the evaluation of the
PSD
, three noise files of 1 MSa each with different sampling rate (50 kSa/s, 1 MSa/s and 100 MSa/s) were acquired at the output of the system with an oscilloscope
[
12
]
to cover the large system bandwidth.

As an example, figure
4
shows the square-root of the Power Spectral Density of noise measured for 5 turns of winding in red. In comparison the square-root of Power Spectral Density of noise of the oscilloscope is plotted in blue. For the front-end electronics, a Conventional Radiotherapy Trans-Impedance module was used with an OPA211 amplifier
[
13
]
. The Power Spectral Density of noise is a function of the amplifier voltage noise source,
S_{e,\mathrm{OPA}}
, the number of winding turns
N
and the feedback resistor noise defined by
\frac{4kT}{R_{f}}
which depends on the Boltzmann constant,
k
and the temperature
T
.
S_{e,\mathrm{OPA}}
is given by the Operational Amplifier constructor.

Figure 4
:
Square-root of measured Power Spectral Density of noise in red for 5 turns of winding and the Conventional Radiotherapy Trans-Impedance front-end electronics module used with an OPA211 amplifier. The oscilloscope Power Spectral Density of noise is also plotted (blue line) and the required bandwidth cut-off frequencies are plotted by green dashed lines.

The voltage output noise is then computed by integrating the Power Spectral Density of noise over the bandwidth of interest defined by the application. Finally, the output voltage noise was multipled by
N/R_{f}
in order to obtain the beam current noise.

Experimental set-up

The toroidal ferrite core used for the system was chosen from the Vacuumschmelze company (reference L2160-V074 in

14
]

As a Beam Current Transformer system is basically an antenna, any Electro-Magnetic perturbations that goes through its surface of detection result in the acquisition of extra noise sources. A shielding was then designed to reduce those perturbations to the minimum in the considered bandwidth. This shielding was made using two copper tubes of 11.5 cm length and 1.3 mm thick and an aluminium part to hold the ferrite core. Figure

9
Conclusion

This paper proposes a complete system of Beam Current Transformer detector that provided a real time monitoring of the pulsed electron beam current from conventional to ultra-high dose rate irradiation. The used technology is based on a ferromagnetic component, which focus the magnetic field lines of the beam current. A signal is generated through a winding and processed by a trans-impedance circuit in order to amplify and correct the drop of the signal.

A new design principle is proposed in this article and an experimental optimization of the front-end electronic of the detector is performed. The prototype was tested by calibration measurements using a pulse generator to simulate the pulse beam current. Preliminary measurements were performed in Conventional Radiotherapy and FLASH Radiotherapy electron beam by varying the pulse width and the dose rate. The fluence of the beam is measured in the beam current range [
1.2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro A}
;
200\text{\,}\mathrm{mA}
] and the droop rate of the signal is corrected below 0.05%
\cdot
-1
.
