Interlacing and monotonicity of zeros of Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials
Andrei Martínez-Finkelshtein, Rafael Morales

Abstract
. Information about the behavior of zeros of classical families of multiple or Hermite–Padé orthogonal polynomials as functions of the intrinsic parameters of the family is scarce. We establish the interlacing properties of the zeros of Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials when one of the three main parameters is increased by 1 1 , extending the work of [ 5 ] . We also show their monotonicity with respect to (large values) of the parameter γ \gamma , representing in the electrostatic model of the zeros the size of the positive charge fixed at the origin, as well as monotonicity with respect to the endpoint of the interval of orthogonality. These results are extended to zeros of multiple Jacobi-Laguerre and Laguerre-Hermite polynomials using asymptotic relations between these families.

1.
Introduction

The Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
can be defined using the Rodrigues formula,

(1.1)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)=\frac{1}{2^{n}n!}(z-1)^{-\alpha}(z+1)^{-\beta}\left(\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{n}\left[(z-1)^{n+\alpha}(z+1)^{n+\beta}\right].

For
\alpha,\beta>-1
, they are orthogonal on
[a,b]=[-1,1]
with respect to the weight

w_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta},

that is,

\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)x^{k}w_{\alpha,\beta}(x)\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,\dots,n-1.

In consequence, all zeros

(1.2)

-1<x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta)<\dots<x_{n,n}(\alpha,\beta)<1

of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
are real, simple, and belong to
(-1,1)
. Their electrostatic interpretation, found by Stieltjes in 1885, is an elegant result that offers intuition about their behavior as functions of the parameters
\alpha,\beta
. Namely, zeros (
1.2
) provide a unique minimizer of the logarithmic energy

E_{total}(X)=E_{mutual}(X)+\sum_{x\in X}\varphi(x),\quad X=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset[-1,1]^{n},

among all
n
point configurations on
[-1,1]
, where

E_{mutual}(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{1\leq k<j\leq n}\log\frac{1}{\lvert x_{k}-x_{j}\rvert},

and the external field
\varphi(x)
acting on
X
is given by two fixed charges of mass
(\beta+1)/2
and
(\alpha+1)/2
at
-1
and
1
, respectively:

\varphi(x)=\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\lvert x-1\rvert}+\frac{\beta+1}{2}\log\frac{1}{\lvert x+1\rvert}.

Since we conclude that the size of
\alpha
(resp.,
\beta
) is proportional to the strength of repulsion of the endpoint
1
(resp.,
-1
), it is a basis for the conjecture that each zero
x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta)
should behave monotonically with respect to
\alpha
and
\beta
. This intuition was rigorously substantiated by Szegő in
[
11
, p. 115]
:

Theorem A
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the zeros (
1.2
) of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)
decrease with respect to
\alpha
and increase with respect to
\beta
.

The monotonicity with respect to the parameters can also be recast in terms of the interlacing of zeros of polynomials from the same family. In what follows, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
the family of real–rooted algebraic polynomials of
degree
n
.

Definition 1.1

(Interlacing)
.

Let
(a,b)\subset\mathbb{R}
,
p
and
q
two polynomials, and let
a<\lambda_{1}(p)<\cdots<\lambda_{k}(p)<b
and
a<\lambda_{1}(q)<\cdots<\lambda_{j}(q)<b
be the zeros of
p
and
q
, respectively, all simple, that that belong to
(a,b)
. We say that
q

interlaces
p
on
(a,b)
, and denote it by
p\prec q
on
(a,b)
, if
j=k
and

(1.3)

\lambda_{1}(p)<\lambda_{1}(q)<\lambda_{2}(p)<\lambda_{2}(q)<\cdots<\lambda_{k}(p)<\lambda_{k}(q),

or if
j=k-1
and

(1.4)

\lambda_{1}(p)<\lambda_{1}(q)<\lambda_{2}(p)<\lambda_{2}(q)<\cdots<\lambda_{n-1}(p)<\lambda_{k-1}(q)<\lambda_{k}(p).

Moreover, if
(a,b)=\mathbb{R}
, we simplify the notation by simply writing
p\prec q
.

It is clear that the monotonicity of zeros of Jacobi polynomials (Theorem
A
) is equivalent to the interlacing of families like
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and
P_{n}^{(\alpha+t,\beta)}
for some range of
t>0
(and similarly, for the parameter
\beta
). A result is this direction was found in
[
6
]
:

Theorem B
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
and
t\in(0,2)
. Then

P_{n}^{(\alpha+t,\beta)}(x)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x),\qquad P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+t)}(x).

The Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{(\gamma)}
can be defined by their explicit representation in terms of the terminating generalized hypergeometric series,

L_{n}^{(\gamma)}(x)=\frac{(\gamma+1)_{n}}{n!}{}_{1}F_{1}(-n;\gamma+1;x),

and for
\gamma>-1
satisfy the orthogonality relations

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{j}L_{n}^{(\gamma)}(x)x^{\gamma}e^{-x}\,dx\\
=0,\quad j=0,1,\dots,n-1.

The Hermite polynomials
H_{n}
are the third classical family of polynomials; they satisfy the orthogonality relations

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{j}H_{n}(x)e^{-x^{2}}dx=0,\quad j=0,1,\dots,n-1,

see
[
8
, §4.6]
. There are well-known limit relations that connect all three classical families; see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 18.7(iii)]
.

Multiple
(or
Hermite–Padé
)
polynomials
are a natural generalization of the classical notion of orthogonal polynomials. Typically, they come in two flavors, but this paper focuses on the so-called Type II polynomials. To define them, we need a multi-index
\vec{n}=\left(n_{1},\dots,n_{r}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and a
r
-tuple of positive Borel measures
\left\{\mu_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{r}
, with an additional assumption that all the moments

\int|x|^{k}\,d\mu_{j}(x),\quad j=1,\dots,r,\quad k\geq 0,

exist. A monic polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
of degree
\left|\vec{n}\right|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
, if it exists, is called a
Type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
if it satisfies the following orthogonality conditions:

\int x^{k}P_{\vec{n}}(x)d\mu_{j}(x)=0\quad\text{for}\quad k=0,1,\dots,n_{j}-1\quad\text{and}\quad j=1,\dots,r.

In general, the existence of such a polynomial for a specific
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
cannot be guaranteed. If they do exist for all multi-indices
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, we say that the system of measures
\left\{\mu_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{r}
is
perfect
. One of the simplest examples of a perfect system is the so-called
Angelesco case
, when the convex hull of the support of each
\mu_{j}
is a closed interval
I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that the interiors of
\left\{I_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{r}
are pairwise disjoint. If measures
\mu_{j}
are additionally classical (or more generally, semiclassical) weights of orthogonality, the corresponding systems receive the name of multiple Jacobi-Jacobi (or Angelesco-Jacobi), Jacobi-Laguerre and Laguerre-Hermite orthogonal polynomials; see
[
8
]
and the definitions below for details. As their classical counterparts, they may also depend on a number of parameters.

One of the features of the Angelesco system is that all zeros in
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
are real and simple, and each interval
I_{j}
contains exactly
n_{j}
of these zeros. However, their electrostatic model, in the spirit of Stieltjes’ work, was given only recently, in
[
10
]
. Unlike the standard case, the model involves charges of opposite sign, some of them located at the zeros of the so-called electrostatic partner
S
of
P_{\vec{n}}
, a polynomial that can be constructed using an explicit formula. In the Angelesco case, the zeros of
S
partially interlace with those of
P_{\vec{n}}
(and the location of the zeros corresponds to a critical point of the gradient of the vector energy rather than a global minimum). The complexity of this vector equilibrium makes the analysis of the behavior of the zeros of
P_{\vec{n}}
as functions of the parameters of the weight highly nontrivial.

In this paper, we will present results about the interlacing and monotonicity of the zeros of multiple Angelesco-Jacobi, Jacobi-Laguerre, and Laguerre-Hermite orthogonal polynomials. All main results are collected in Section
2
. Section
3
contains some structure relations for Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials, as well as proof of the interlacing properties of their zeros. The behavior of the arithmetic and geometric means of these zeros is proven in Section
4
, and these results are used in the proof of the monotonicity properties in Sections
5
and
6
. Finally, the extension of some of these results to the zeros of the Jacobi-Laguerre and Laguerre-Hermite polynomials is established in Section
7
.

2.
Main results

Angelesco-Jacobi, known also as Jacobi-Jacobi polynomials (see
[
1
]
), are Hermite–Padé polynomials
P_{\vec{n}}
,
\vec{n}=(n_{1},n_{2})
, satisfying orthogonality relations

\begin{split}&\int_{a}^{0}x^{k}P_{\vec{n}}(x)(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}|x|^{\gamma}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{1}-1,\\
&\int_{0}^{1}x^{k}P_{\vec{n}}(x)(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{2}-1,\end{split}

with
a<0
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
. To make the dependence on the parameters explicit, we denote these polynomials by
P_{\vec{n}}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
.
1
1

1

Notice that the role of the parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
can differ in the literature.
Thus, this is a type II multiple orthogonal polynomial for the Angelesco system given by the same weight function
\omega(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert x\right\rvert}^{\gamma}
on the intervals
[a,0]
and
[0,1]
.

We will consider the diagonal case

(2.1)

\vec{n}=(n,n);

to simplify notation, in what follows and under assumption (
2.1
), we will write
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
instead of
P_{\vec{n}}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
, keeping in mind that

\deg P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=2n.

Correspondingly, exactly
n
zeros of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
are in
(a,0)
, and the other
n
, in
(0,1)
.

As pointed out in the Introduction, we assume these polynomials to be monic. They can be defined via a Rodrigues formula
[
12
, Section 3.5]

(2.2)

\begin{split}(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)&P^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)\\
&=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n}\left[(1-x)^{\alpha+n}(x-a)^{\beta+n}x^{\gamma+n}\right]\end{split}

with

(2.3)

c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\binom{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{n};

here and in what follows, we use the generalized definition of the binomial coefficients,

\binom{a}{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\Gamma(a+1)}{\Gamma(a-k+1)k!},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\},\quad a>k.

Among the consequences of (
2.2
) are the raising operator identity

(2.4)

\begin{split}(2n+\alpha+&\beta+\gamma+1)(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}P^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)\\
&=-\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1-x)^{\alpha+1}(x-a)^{\beta+1}x^{\gamma+1}P^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x;a)\right],\end{split}

and an explicit expression (see
[
8
, Section 23.3.1]
),

(2.5)

c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j},

where for
0\leq j\leq n-k
and
0\leq k\leq n
,

(2.6)

d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\binom{n+\alpha}{k}\binom{n+\beta}{j}\binom{n+\gamma}{n-k-j}.

The zero interlacing of consecutive
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
, in the spirit of Theorem
B
, is not known (for the interlacing of zeros of the nearest neighbor polynomials, see
[
7
]
). However, de Santos
[
5
]
proved the following result (see
[
5
, Lemma 2.1, iv)]
), that we formulate using Definition
1.1
:

Theorem C
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
,
a<0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\text{ on }(a,0),

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\text{ on }(0,1).

It is convenient to remind the reader that
P_{n}
(resp.,
P_{n-1}
) has exactly
n
(resp.,
n-1
) zeros on
(a,0)
and on
(0,1)
.

Remark 2.1
.

A simple observation is that the assertion of this theorem is equivalent to

x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a).

This result shows that the zeros of two consecutive diagonal Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials interlace at each subinterval if the decrease in degree is accompanied by an increase in the value of each parameter
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in

One of the main results of this paper is the following refinement of Theorem

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
,
a<0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

. Then

(i)

x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
;

(ii)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec x(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)
;

(iii)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\quad\text{on }(a,0),

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\quad\text{on }(0,1).

Although the electrostatic model for
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
, mentioned above, involves both positive and negative moving charges and is quite complicated to analyze, it still provides a correct intuition on the behavior of zeros as functions of the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
(representing the size of the positive charges fixed at the endpoints
x=a
and
x=1
, respectively), as a result found in
[
5
]
shows:

Theorem D
.

The zeros of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
decrease with respect to
\alpha
and increase with respect to
\beta
. Furthermore, if
\alpha=\beta
and
a=-1
, the negative zeros decrease with respect to
\gamma
, and the positive zeros increase with respect to
\gamma
.

This theorem claims the monotonicity of zeros as functions of the parameter
\gamma
(the “size” of the positive charge at
x=0
) in the most straightforward symmetric setting. When the symmetry is broken, the electrostatic model allows us to expect monotonicity, at least for large values of
\gamma
, when the charge at the origin outpowers the interaction of individual positive and negative unit charges. This is indeed the case, as the following result shows:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>-1
,
a<0
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for all sufficiently large values of
\gamma
, the negative zeros of
P^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)
decrease with respect to
\gamma
, and its positive zeros increase with respect to
\gamma
.

We conjecture that the assertion is valid for all values of the parameter
\gamma>-1
.

Finally, although intuitively obvious, we establish the monotonicity of all zeros of the Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials as functions of the left endpoint
a
:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a<0
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
. The zeros of the polynomial
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)
are increasing with respect to
a
.

This result, although predictable for the zeros in
(a,0)
, is less obvious for the whole interval in
(a,1)
.

Given
a<0
and
\beta,\gamma>-1
, the
Jacobi-Laguerre polynomials

L_{\vec{n}}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
,
\vec{n}=(n_{1},n_{2})
, are Hermite–Padé polynomials that satisfy the orthogonality relations

\begin{split}&\int_{a}^{0}x^{k}L^{(\beta,\gamma)}_{\vec{n}}(x)(x-a)^{\beta}|x|^{\gamma}e^{-x}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{1}-1,\\
&\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}L^{(\beta,\gamma)}_{\vec{n}}(x)(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}e^{-x}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{2}-1.\end{split}

The
Laguerre-Hermite polynomials

H_{\vec{n}}^{(\gamma)}(x;a)
, for
\gamma>-1
, satisfy

\begin{split}&\int_{-\infty}^{0}x^{k}H^{(\gamma)}_{\vec{n}}(x)|x|^{\gamma}e^{-x^{2}}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{1}-1,\\
&\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}H^{(\gamma)}_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{\gamma}e^{-x^{2}}\,dx=0,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n_{2}-1.\end{split}

As in the classical case, these families of multiple orthogonal polynomials are connected by the following asymptotic relations (see
[
12
, formulas (3.55) and (3.57)]
):

(2.7)

\displaystyle L_{\vec{n}}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{n_{1}+n_{2}}P_{\vec{n}}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x/\alpha;a/\alpha),

(2.8)

\displaystyle H_{\vec{n}}^{(\gamma)}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{(n_{1}+n_{2})/2}P_{\vec{n}}^{(\alpha,\alpha,\gamma)}(x/\sqrt{\alpha};-1).

As in the case of Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials, we will focus on the diagonal (
2.1
), adopting the notation
L_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x;a)
and
H_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)
instead of
L_{\vec{n}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x;a)
and
H_{\vec{n}}^{(\beta)}(x)
, respectively. Again, being in each case an Angelesco system, exactly
n
zeros are strictly positive, while the other
n
are strictly negative (and in the interval
(a,0)
in the case of polynomials

Using the asymptotic relations (
2.7
)–(
2.8
), we extend the interlacing result of Theorem
2.2
to the Jacobi-Laguerre and Laguerre-Hermite polynomials:

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
a<0
and
\beta,\gamma>-1
, the following interlacing holds:

(1)

L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec L_{n}^{(\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec xL_{n-1}^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).

(2)

\begin{split}L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec L_{n-1}^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\quad&\text{on }(a,0),\\
L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec L_{n-1}^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\quad&\text{on }(0,\infty).\end{split}

(3)

\begin{split}H_{n}^{(\gamma+1)}(x)\prec H_{n}^{(\gamma)}(x)\prec H_{n-1}^{(\gamma+1)}(x)\quad&\text{on }(-\infty,0),\\
H_{n}^{(\gamma)}(x)\prec H_{n}^{(\gamma+1)}(x)\prec H_{n-1}^{(\gamma+1)}(x)\quad&\text{on }(0,\infty).\end{split}

Finally, the monotonicity of zeros as a function of the end point
a
(Theorem
2.4

L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
via the limit relation (

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Theorem
2.2
is a consequence of the following structure relations for Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials. To our knowledge, they are new and can have an independent interest. To formulate this result, we define for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
the coefficients

(3.1)

\begin{split}\mathfrak{A}_{n}&=\mathfrak{A}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{2n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+1}{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+1}>0,\\
\mathfrak{B}_{n}&=\mathfrak{B}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=1-\mathfrak{A}_{n}=\frac{n}{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+1}>0\end{split}

(where we omit the dependence on the rest of the parameters when these values are clear).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
, and
\mathfrak{A}_{n}=\mathfrak{A}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{n}=\mathfrak{B}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
be as defined above. Then

(i)

P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+\mathfrak{B}_{n}x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a);

(ii)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+\mathfrak{B}_{n}(x-1)xP_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a);

(iii)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+\mathfrak{B}_{n}(x-a)(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).

Proof.

From the explicit expression (
2.6
) and the identity

\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}

it follows that for
k\geq 1
,

d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)=d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)+d^{(n-1)}_{j,k-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1).

Using it in (
2.5
), we get that

\displaystyle c_{n}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n-1)}_{j,k-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{n}d^{(n)}_{j,0}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)(x-1)^{n}x^{j}(x-a)^{n-j}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}.

Notice that
d^{(n)}_{j,0}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)=d^{(n)}_{j,0}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
, so that

c_{n}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}\\
+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n-1)}_{j,k-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}.

Shifting the summation variable
k
, we get

c_{n}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}\\
+x(x-a)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-k}d^{(n-1)}_{j,k}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)(x-1)^{n-1-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-1-j}.

After dividing the identity by
c_{n}(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)
and using (
2.5
) we get
(i)
.

The rest of the assertions are proved using similar arguments.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

By Remark
2.1
, we have that

x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a).

These are polynomials with zeros in
[a,1)
and degree exactly
2n
. It is a well-known and easy to prove the fact that their convex combination preserves interlacing (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
]
or
[
9
, Lemma 4.5]
), which implies that with
\mathfrak{A}_{n}>0
,
\mathfrak{B}_{n}>0
defined in (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle x(x-a)

\displaystyle P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}_{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+\mathfrak{B}_{n}x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a).

The identity
(i)
of Theorem
3.1
implies part
(i)
of the theorem, that is,

x(x-a)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha+1,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a).

The assertion
(ii)
of the theorem is proved similarly.

Let us prove
(iii)
, and specifically, the interlacing on
(a,0)
. By Theorem
C
,

(3.2)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\text{ on }(a,0).

In what follows, for
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
, we denote by

(3.3)

x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<x_{n,2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<\dots<x_{n,2n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)

the zeros of
P^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)
. Recall that
a<x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<0
for
j=1,\dots,n
, and
0<x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<1
for
j=n+1,\dots,2n
. With this notation, (
3.2
) means that

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle<x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<x_{n-1,1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a)<x_{n,2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<

\displaystyle\dots<x_{n,n-1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<x_{n-1,n-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a)

\displaystyle<x_{n,n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<0.

Since
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
and
P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)
are both of even degree with all their zeros on
(a,1)
, we get that

(3.4)

\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a;a)\right)=\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(a;a)\right)=1,

and that for
j=1,\dots,n-1
,

(3.5)

\begin{split}\mathop{\rm sign}&\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)\\
&=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x_{n-1,j}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a);a)\right)\\
&=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,j+1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)=(-1)^{j+1}.\end{split}

Recall that by
(iii)
of Theorem
3.1
,

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+\mathfrak{B}_{n}(x-a)(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)

with
\mathfrak{A}_{n}>0
,
\mathfrak{B}_{n}>0
defined in (
3.1
). Since
(x-a)(x-1)<0
on
(a,1)
, we have by (
3.5
) that for
j=1,\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm sign}

\displaystyle(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x_{n-1,j}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a);a))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x_{n-1,j}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a);a)\right),

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm sign}

\displaystyle\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,j+1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,j+1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x_{n-1,j}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a);a)\right).

In other words,
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)
presents a sign change (and hence a root) in every subinterval

\left(x_{n-1,j}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a),x_{n,j+1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)\right),\quad j=1,\dots,n-1.

Moreover, by (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(a;a)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a;a)\right)=1,

while by (
3.5
),

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm sign}

\displaystyle\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)

\displaystyle=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a);a)\right)=-1,

which means that another sign change (and the remaining root) of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)
on
(a,0)
is located in
(a,x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a))
.

In summary,

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle<x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1;a)<x_{n,1}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<x_{n-1,1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a)

\displaystyle<x_{n,2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1;a)<x_{n,2}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<x_{n-1,2}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a)<

\displaystyle\dots<x_{n-1,n-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1;a)<x_{n,n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1;a)

\displaystyle<x_{n,n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)<0,

which exactly means that

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\prec P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\prec P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a)\quad\text{on }(a,0),

as stated.

The proof of the interlacing on
(0,1)
follows similar arguments, using that

\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0;a)\right)=-\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(0;a)\right)=1.

∎

4.
Mean behavior of the zeros

Here we gather some technical lemmas that will be useful in the next section.

Lemma 4.1

(Geometric Mean)
.

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
, the geometric mean of the absolute values of the zeros of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
is an increasing function of
\gamma
.

Proof.

By (
2.5
),

(4.1)

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0;a)=\frac{d^{(n)}_{0,0}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}{c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}\,a^{n}=\binom{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{n}^{-1}\binom{n+\gamma}{n}\,a^{n},

so that

(4.2)

\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0;a)\right|=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n+1)\Gamma(n+\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3n+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}\left|\,a\right|^{n}.

Taking the logarithmic derivative of this identity with respect to
\gamma
and using the following known identity
[
4
, Section 5.7(ii)]
,

\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\Gamma(z)}=\psi(z)=-\gamma_{e}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+z}\right),

(where
\gamma_{e}
is Euler’s constant) we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0)\right|}{\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0)\right|}=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n+1}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3n+1}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+n+\gamma+1}\right)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+1}-\frac{1}{k+\gamma+1}\right).

After simplification, we arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0)\right|}{\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0)\right|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{k+\gamma}-\frac{1}{k+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha+\gamma+2n}{(k+\gamma)(k+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n)}.

Since by assumption,
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
, each term in the sum on the right-hand side is positive, so that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0)\right|>0.

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Under the assumptions above, and with notation (
3.3
),

(4.3)

\begin{split}\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)=a,&\quad j=1,\dots,n\\
\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)=1,&\quad j=n+1,\dots,2n.\end{split}

Proof.

Since
|x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)|<|a|
for
j=1,\dots,n
, and
|x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)|<1
for
j=n+1,\dots,2n
, we conclude that

(4.4)

\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left|x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)\right|\leq\left|a^{n}\right|.

On the other hand, taking the limit in (
4.2
) as
\gamma\to+\infty
and using Stirling’s formula
[
4
, Formula 5.11.E7]
, we get that

\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}\left|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(0;a)\right|=\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\left|x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)\right|=\left|a^{n}\right|.

Combining it with the upper bound (
4.4
) we obtain (
4.3
).
∎

Our next result concerns the arithmetic mean of the zeros of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
,

A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{2n}\,\sum_{j=1}^{2n}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a).

Lemma 4.3

(Arithmetic Mean)
.

For
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
,

(4.5)

A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(2n+\beta+\gamma)+a\left(2n+\gamma+\alpha\right)}{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}.

Proof.

Using (
2.5
) and the relation between the arithmetic mean of the zeros and the coefficient of
x^{2n-1}
of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x)
we get that

(4.6)

c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\left((n-k)+(n-j)a\right).

By the identity

\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{a}{j}\binom{b}{m-j}=\binom{a+b}{m}

we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(n-k)

\displaystyle=n\frac{2n+\beta+\gamma}{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\binom{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{n},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)(n-j)

\displaystyle=n\frac{2n+\gamma+\alpha}{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\binom{3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma}{n}.

Combining these two formulas in (
4.6
) finally yields (
4.5
).
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

For
\alpha,\beta>-1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.7)

\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}A_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a)=\lim_{\gamma\to+\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)=0.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of differentiating (
4.5
) with respect to
\gamma
:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}A^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(a)=\frac{1}{2n}\,\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma;a)=\frac{n+\alpha+(n+\beta)a}{2(3n+\alpha+\beta+\gamma)^{2}}.

∎

5.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

One of the main tools in the proof of Theorems
C
and
D

in
[

]

]

f_{n-1}(x,a,\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\\

where, as in (

.

With a shorter notation

(5.3)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\,x_{n,j}(\gamma)=-\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}f_{n-1}(x_{n,j}(\gamma);\gamma+1)}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{n-1}(x_{n,j}(\gamma);\gamma+1)},\qquad j=1,\dots,2n.

Thus, we can prove the theorem by analyzing the sign of the derivative

Observe that

(5.4)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}f_{n-1}(x;\gamma+1)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{x},&n=1,\\

as needed.

,

\Gamma_{1}>-1

(5.5)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\gamma}\,y_{n-1,j}(\gamma)<0&\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,n-1,\\

notice that by Theorem

\gamma>\Gamma_{2}

(5.7)

\begin{split}a<y_{n-1,i}(\gamma)<\frac{1}{2}a&\qquad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n-1,\\

4.4

\Gamma_{3}>-1

6.
Proof of Theorem
2.4

We need the following result to understand the behavior of the zeros of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)
as functions of the endpoint
a
:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1
. Then

\displaystyle(x-a)\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle=\frac{-(n+\beta+1)(1+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n)}{1+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)+(\beta+1)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle=-nP_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)+\frac{n(n+\beta+1)}{1+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3n}x(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).

Proof.

We prove the first identity by using the Rodrigues formula (
2.2
): differentiating it with respect to
a
, the right-hand side yields

(6.1)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\biggl((1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)&P^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)\biggr)\\
=(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}&c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\\
-\beta(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta-1}&x^{\gamma}c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a),\end{split}

and on the left-hand side,

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\Biggl(

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n}\left[(1-x)^{\alpha+n}(x-a)^{\beta+n}x^{\gamma+n}\right]\Biggr)

\displaystyle=(n+\beta)\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{n}\left[(1-x)^{\alpha+n}(x-a)^{\beta+n-1}x^{\gamma+n}\right]

(6.2)

\displaystyle=-(n+\beta)(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta-1}x^{\gamma}c_{n}(\alpha,\beta-1,\gamma)P^{(\alpha,\beta-1,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a).

Combining (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) and diving through by
(1-x)^{\alpha}(x-a)^{\beta-1}x^{\gamma}
we obtain the following equation,

(x-a)c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)-\beta c_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)\\
=-(n+\beta)c_{n}(\alpha,\beta-1,\gamma)P^{(\alpha,\beta-1,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a),

which is the first equality claimed in the lemma.

The second identity can be derived from the explicit expression (
2.5
).
Differentiating it with respect to
a
, we get

\displaystyle c_{n}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(n-j)d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-1-j},

so that

(6.3)

\begin{split}c_{n}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)&(a-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)\\
&=n\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}\\
&-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}jd^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}.\end{split}

By (
2.5
),

(6.4)

\begin{split}nc_{n}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)&P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)\\
&=n\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}d^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}.\end{split}

On the other hand, by (
2.6
), for
j>0
we have

jd^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)=(n+\beta+1)d^{(n-1)}_{j-1,k}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1),

which implies that

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}&\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}jd^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}=(n+\beta+1)\times\\
&\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1-k}d^{(n-1)}_{j,k}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j+1}(x-a)^{n-1-j},\end{split}

or equivalently, by (
2.5
),

(6.5)

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}&\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}jd^{(n)}_{j,k}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(x-1)^{n-k}x^{k+j}(x-a)^{n-j}=\\
&(n+\beta+1)c_{n-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)x(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).\end{split}

Using the equations (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) in (
6.3
), we get

\begin{split}&c_{n}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)(a-x)\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)=nc_{n}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)\\
&-(n+\beta+1)c_{n-1}(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)x(x-1)P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a),\end{split}

which is equivalent to the second identity of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

From the implicit differentiation of

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)=0

with respect to the parameter
a
, we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)=-\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial a}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)}{\frac{\partial}{\partial x}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)}

Using Lemma
6.1
to replace the numerator on the right-hand side, we obtain

(6.6)

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)=\frac{(n+\beta+1)(1+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+2n)}{1+\alpha+\beta+\gamma+3n}\times\\
\frac{P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)}{\left(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)-a\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)}.

Recall that
x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)>a
for all
j=1,\dots,2n
. Furthermore,
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)
is a polynomial of degree
2n
, with
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(a;a)>0
and simple zeros on
(a,1)
, which implies that

\mathop{\rm sign}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(a,\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)\right)=(-1)^{j},\quad j=1,\dots,2n.

Analogously,
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)
is a polynomial of degree
2n
, with
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}(a;a)>0
. The assertion
(ii)
of Theorem
2.2
implies that

\mathop{\rm sign}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a);a)\right)=(-1)^{j},\quad j=1,\dots,2n.

Gathering these facts in (
6.6
) yields

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}x_{n,j}(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma;a)>0,\quad j=1,\dots,2n.

∎

7.
Proof of Theorem
2.5

The results obtained for the Angelesco-Jacobi polynomials can be extended to other families of multiple orthogonal polynomials of type II, namely, the Jacobi-Laguerre and Laguerre-Hermite polynomials. Indeed, due to the analyticity of the functions involved, we can take appropriate limits in (
2.4
) (the fact that the limits and differentiation commute can be easily justified using Cauchy’s formula), and (
2.7
)–(
2.8
) yield the following raising operator identities:

(7.1)

(x-a)^{\beta}x^{\gamma}e^{-x}L^{(\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)=-\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x-a)^{\beta+1}x^{\gamma+1}e^{-x}L^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x;a)\right],

(7.2)

x^{\gamma}e^{-x^{2}}H^{(\gamma)}_{n}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{\gamma+1}e^{-x^{2}}H^{(\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x)\right].

Remark 7.1
.

Since the roots of
L^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x;a)
are simple, using (
7.1
)-(
7.2
), we conclude that
L^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x;a)
and
L^{(\beta,\gamma)}_{n}(x;a)
do not have common zeros. The same assertion is valid for the polynomials
H^{(\gamma+1)}_{n-1}(x)
and
H^{(\gamma)}_{n}(x)
.

The following generalization of the structure relations, obtained in Theorem
3.1
, take place:

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta,\gamma>-1
. Then

(1)

L_{n}^{(\beta+1,\gamma)}(x;a)=L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)-xL_{n-1}^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).

(2)

L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma+1)}(x;a)=L_{n}^{(\beta,\gamma)}(x;a)-(x-a)L_{n-1}^{(\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x;a).

Proof.

By
(ii)
of Theorem
3.1
,

(7.3)

\begin{split}\alpha^{2n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta+1,\gamma)}&(x/\alpha;a/\alpha)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}\alpha^{2n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x/\alpha;a/\alpha)+\\
&\mathfrak{B}_{n}\alpha^{2n-1}(x/\alpha-1)xP_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1,\gamma+1)}(x/\alpha;a/\alpha),\end{split}

where
\mathfrak{A}_{n}=\mathfrak{A}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and
\mathfrak{B}_{n}=\mathfrak{B}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=1-\mathfrak{A}_{n}
were defined in (
3.1
). From their explicit expression,

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\mathfrak{A}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha\mathfrak{B}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=1.

Thus, taking the limit in (
7.3
) as
\alpha\to\infty
we get assertion
(1)
of the theorem. Similar arguments, now with assertion
(iii)
of Theorem
3.1
, yield
(2)
.
∎

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma>-1
. Then

(7.4)

H_{n}^{(\gamma+1)}(x)=H_{n}^{(\gamma)}(x;a)-\frac{1}{2}H_{n-1}^{(\gamma+1)}(x;a).

Proof.

By
(iii)
of Theorem
3.1
,

(7.5)

\alpha^{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha,\gamma+1)}(x/\sqrt{\alpha};-1)=\mathfrak{A}_{n}\alpha^{n}P_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha,\gamma)}(x/\sqrt{\alpha};-1)+\\
\mathfrak{B}_{n}(x/\sqrt{\alpha}+1)(x/\sqrt{\alpha}-1)\alpha^{n}P_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1,\alpha+1,\gamma+1)}(x/\sqrt{\alpha};-1),

\mathfrak{A}_{n}=\mathfrak{A}_{n}(\alpha,\beta,\alpha)

).

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Part

)
