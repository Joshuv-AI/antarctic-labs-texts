Canonical extensions of locally compact frames Journal: Topology and its Applications
Tomáš Jakl

Abstract
Canonical extension of finitary ordered structures such as lattices, posets, proximity lattices, etc., is a certain completion which entirely describes the topological dual of the ordered structure and it does so in a purely algebraic and choice-free way. We adapt the general algebraic technique that constructs them to the theory of frames. As a result, we show that every locally compact frame embeds into a completely distributive lattice by a construction which generalises, among others, the canonical extensions for distributive lattices and proximity lattices. This construction also provides a new description of a construction by Marcel Erné. Moreover, canonical extensions of frames enable us to frame-theoretically represent monotone maps with respect to the specialisation order.

Introduction

Initially canonical extensions were defined by Jónsson and Tarski
[
21
,
22
]
in order to understand topological duals of Boolean algebras with additional operations. The theory of canonical extensions was later extended to distributive lattices, posets, proximity lattices, coherent categories and more
[
15
,
14
,
13
,
12
,
16
,
26
,
3
]
.

Intuitively, canonical extension of a distributive lattice algebraically represents the lattice embedding of the compact open subsets of its Stone dual into the complete lattice of saturated sets, that is, upsets with respect to the specialisation order. Because the construction is purely algebraic, canonical extension of a distributive lattice can be thought of as a point-free and choice-free description of the frame of all saturated subsets of its dual space.

There is nothing specific about distributive lattices and their Stone duals. We can ask whether there is an analogous construction for frames and topological spaces. In particular, the frame of open sets of a topological space always embeds into the frame of saturated subsets. The problem therefore is to give a choice-free and purely frame-theoretic or algebraic construction of the frame of saturated subsets.

We solve this question for the class of locally compact frames. This is a wide class of frames which includes all compact regular frames as well as coherent frames. In fact our technique works for an even more general class of frames, that is, for frames with enough compact fitted sublocales.
We show that every locally compact frame canonically embeds into a complete lattice, which it meet-generates, and which is isomorphic to the frame of saturated subsets of the dual space of the frame we started with. From this it follows that the canonical extension of the frame is a completely distributive lattice, although this fact is not choice-free. Moreover, we also show how to lift perfect homomorphisms between locally compact frames to complete lattice homomorphisms between their canonical extensions.

Our construction generalises the distributive case of Sam van Gool
[
26
]
which solves this question for proximity lattices and, therefore, also indirectly for stably compact frames. Moreover, it turns out that our construction is exactly the same as the one considered by Marcel Erné in
[
5
]
in which he constructs a certain frame extension for every preframe. We also show how one can use canonical extensions to represent monotone maps with respect to specialisation order, in a point-free way.

2.1
Topological spaces

Every topological space
(X,\tau)
carries the so-called
specialisation order
on the set of its points:

\forall x,y\in X.\quad x\leq_{\tau}y\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall U\in\tau.\ x\in U\implies y\in U.

Saturated
subsets of a space are those subsets which are upward closed with respect to the specialisation order. We denote the set of all saturated subsets/upsets of a space
(X,\tau)
by
\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})
, or sometimes just
\mathsf{Up}(X)
. Note that a subset of a space
M\subseteq X
is saturated if and only if
M=\bigcap\{U\in\tau~|~M\subseteq U\}
or equivalently if there is a collection
\mathscr{U}\subseteq\tau
such that
M=\bigcap\mathscr{U}
(Proposition 4.2.9 in
[
18
]
). Note also that all open subsets of a space are saturated and so
\tau\subseteq\mathsf{Up}(X)
.

2.2
Galois adjunctions

Order preserving function
f\colon A\to B
between complete lattices preserves arbitrary joins if and only if there is a monotone function
g\colon B\to A
(in fact, meet preserving) such that

f(a)\leq b\quad\text{iff}\quad a\leq g(b),

or equivalently

f(g(b))\leq b\quad\text{iff}\quad a\leq g(f(a)).

We call
f
the
left (Galois) adjoint
and
g
the
right (Galois) adjoint
. It follows that
f=fgf
and
g=gfg
and so by setting
c=gf
we obtain a
closure operator

c\colon A\to A
on
A
which satisfies the following conditions, for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A
:

a\leq a^{\prime}\implies c(a)\leq c(a^{\prime}),\quad a\leq c(a),\quad\text{and}\quad c(c(a))\leq c(a).

The fixpoints of
c\colon A\to A
form a complete lattice, in the order inherited from
A
.

Note that if we start from two
antitone
maps
f\colon A\rightleftarrows B\colon g
such that
a\leq g(b)
iff
b\leq f(a)
then we speak of an
antitone Galois adjunction
. In this case both maps
f
and
g
transform arbitrary joins into meets. In fact, antitone Galois adjunctions are historically the more standard setting.

2.3
Frames

A complete lattice
L
is a
frame
if, for every
A\subseteq L
and
b\in L
,

(\bigvee A)\wedge b=\bigvee_{a\in A}(a\wedge b).

Similarly, a
preframe

P
is a poset which has finite meets, directed joins and, for every
directed

D\subseteq P
and
b\in P
,

(\dirvee D)\wedge b=\dirvee_{d\in D}(d\wedge b),

where
\dirvee D
denotes the supremum of the directed set
D
. Homomorphisms of frames (resp. preframes) are the maps which preserve arbitrary joins (resp. directed joins) and finite meets. We say that a poset is a
coframe
if it is a frame in the opposite order and, similarly, it is a
co-preframe
if it is a preframe in the opposite order.

Every topological space
(X,\tau)
gives rise to a frame
\mathcal{O}(X,\tau)=\tau
and, vice versa, every frame
L
gives rise to a space
\mathsf{pt}(L)
whose points are the frame homomorphisms
p\colon L\to\mathbf{2}
into the two-element frame
\mathbf{2}
. The topology of
\mathsf{pt}(L)
consists of the sets of the form
\{p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)~|~p(a)=1\}
, for some
a\in L
. Moreover, the mappings
X\mapsto\mathcal{O}(X)
and
L\mapsto\mathsf{pt}(L)
extend to a pair of dually adjoint functors, with the actions on morphisms defined as

\displaystyle f\colon X\to Y

\displaystyle\kern 5.0pt\longmapsto\kern 5.0pt\mathcal{O}(f)\colon\mathcal{O}(Y)\to\mathcal{O}(X),\ V\mapsto f^{-1}[V],\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle h\colon L\to M

\displaystyle\kern 5.0pt\longmapsto\kern 5.0pt\mathsf{pt}(h)\colon\mathsf{pt}(M)\to\mathsf{pt}(L),\ p\mapsto p\circ h.

In case when a frame is isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}(X)
, for some space
X
, we say that the frame is
spatial
or that it has
enough points
. Conversely, spaces which are homeomorphic to
\mathsf{pt}(L)
, for some frame
L
, are called
sober
.

2.5
Sublocales

Next we introduce the frame-theoretic analogue of subspaces. A subset
S
of a frame
L
is a
sublocale
if
S
is the set of fixpoints of some
nucleus

\nu\colon L\to L
, where nucleus is a closure operator which preserves binary meets.

Every sublocale
S
, in the order derived from
L
, is a frame and the nucleus
\nu
restricts to an
onto
frame homomorphism
L\twoheadrightarrow S
. In fact, every onto frame homomorphism gives rise to a nucleus, and vice versa.

Finally, the lattice of sublocales
\mathcal{S}(L)
, ordered by set inclusion, forms a coframe with meets computed as intersections, and embeds the original frame
L
via the map

a\in L\kern 5.0pt\mapsto\kern 5.0ptU_{a}=\{x~|~x=x\to a\}.

Sublocales of the form
U_{a}
are called
open sublocales
and their complements in
\mathcal{S}(L)
, which always exist, are called
closed sublocales
. For a more in-depth discussion about sublocales, nuclei and frame quotients we refer the reader to
[
25
]

Abstract description

3.1
Canonical extensions for distributive lattices

To motivate our definition of canonical extension for frames we first recall the construction for distributive lattices (for details see e.g.
[
4
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
). Given a distributive lattice
A
, its canonical extension
e\colon A\hookrightarrow A^{\delta}
is a lattice embedding of
A
into a complete lattice
A^{\delta}
uniquely described by the following two conditions

(Dense)

For all
u,v\in A^{\delta}
,
u\leq v
if, for every filter
F\subseteq A
and ideal
I\subseteq A
,
\bigwedge e[F]\leq u
and
v\leq\bigvee e[I]
implies
\bigwedge e[F]\leq\bigvee e[I]
.

(Compact)

If
\bigwedge e[F]\leq\bigvee e[I]
for some filter
F\subseteq A
and ideal
I\subseteq A
, then
F\cap I\not=\emptyset
.

Density simply means that every element of the canonical extension
A^{\delta}
is both a join of meets and a meet of joins of elements of
A
.

Note that up until recently it was preferred to write
A^{\sigma}
to denote the canonical extension of
A
. However, this notation conflicts the notation for the two different extensions,
\sigma
– and
\pi
-extension, of monotone maps – the first defined as a join of meets and the other as a meet of joins (and there is no reason why one should have any preference over the other).

Remark 3.1
.

The intuition behind the two axioms is that
e\colon A\hookrightarrow A^{\delta}
represents the embedding of compact opens of
X
into the complete lattice of saturated subsets of
X
, where
X
is the Stone dual (spectral) space of
A
. Under this interpretation, ideals of
A
correspond to the open sets via the mapping
I\mapsto\bigvee e[I]
and, similarly, filters correspond to the compact saturated subsets via
F\mapsto\bigwedge e[F]
.

Density then simply means that every saturated subset
M
of
X
is uniquely determined by compact saturated sets
K
such that
K\subseteq M
and also by open sets
U
such that
M\subseteq U
. Further, compactness means that
K\subseteq U
implies that there is a compact open
O
such that
K\subseteq O\subseteq U
.

Note that, by assuming the axiom of choice, we can identify the elements of
A^{\delta}
that correspond to the principal upsets
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux
in the specialisation order of
X
as those elements which are completely join prime. We see that
A^{\delta}
represents the points of
X
as well as its upsets in one structure and so it effectively contains all information about the space
X
.
[
11
]

Existence and uniqueness of the canonical extension
A\hookrightarrow A^{\delta}
follow from a general theory of polarities as described, for example, in
[
10
]
. Let us recall some of the basics of the theory that goes back to Birkhoff
[
2
]
. A
polarity
is a triple
(X,Y,Z)
where
X
and
Y
are sets and
Z\subseteq X\mathclose{\times}Y
is a relation. This data induces the following pair of antitone maps between
\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)
and
\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)
, in the subset order:

\displaystyle p\colon\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)\to\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y),\quad M\mapsto\{y\in Y~|~\forall x\in M.\ xZy\}

\displaystyle q\colon\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)\to\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X),\quad N\mapsto\{x\in X~|~\forall y\in N.\ xZy\}

Since
N\subseteq p(M)
iff
M\subseteq q(N)
, the maps
p
and
q
constitute an antitone Galois adjunction and
\phi=q\circ p
is a closure operator on
\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)
. Set
\mathscr{G}(X,Y,Z)\subseteq\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)
to be the set of Galois closed sets, i.e. sets
\{M\subseteq X~|~\phi(M)=M\}
. There are also two maps

f\colon X\to\mathscr{G}(X,Y,Z),\ x\mapsto\phi(\{x\}),\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptg\colon Y\to\mathscr{G}(X,Y,Z),\ y\mapsto q(\{y\}).

Fact 3.2

(e.g., Section 2 in
[
10
]
)
.

Let
(X,Y,Z)
be a polarity. Then the complete lattice
C=\mathscr{G}(X,Y,Z)
has the following properties.

1.

For any
u\in C
,

u=\bigvee\{f(x)~|~x\in X,\ f(x)\leq u\}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptu=\bigwedge\{g(y)~|~y\in Y,\ u\leq g(y)\}.

2.

For any
x\in X
and
y\in Y
,
f(x)\leq g(y)
iff
xZy
.

3.

For any

and
g^{\prime}\colon Y\to C^{\prime}
also satisfying the conditions (1) and (2) there is a unique complete lattice isomorphism
\iota\colon C^{\prime}\to C
such that
\iota\circ f^{\prime}=f
and
\iota\circ g^{\prime}=g
.

Consequently, for a distributive lattice
A
, setting
X=\mathsf{Filt}(A)
,
Y=\mathsf{Idl}(A)
, and
Z=\{(F,I)~|~F\cap I\not=\emptyset\}
gives us that, for the complete lattice
C=\mathscr{G}(X,Y,Z)
, the composite

e\colon A\xrightarrow{~a~\mapsto~\mathord{\downarrow}\mkern 1.0mua~}\mathsf{Idl}(A)\xrightarrow{\hskip 8.19447ptg\hskip 8.19447pt}C

is the canonical extension of

, e.g.
[

]
.

3.2
The same construction for spaces

The construction mentioned above can be rewritten purely in terms of the Stone dual
(X,\tau)
of the distributive lattice
A
. As we mentioned in Remark
3.1
, the frame of ideals is isomorphic to
\tau
and the coframe of filters is isomorphic to the poset of compact saturated subsets
\mathsf{KS}(X)
of
X
, ordered by set inclusion. Moreover, the relation
FZI
expresses the fact that
K_{F}\subseteq U_{I}
where
K_{F}
is the compact saturated subset corresponding to
F
and
U_{I}
is the open set corresponding to
I
.

This intuition allows us to obtain the frame
{\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})}
entirely from this data. Furthermore, this construction works for all topological spaces, not just those arising as spectra of distributive lattices.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a topological space. Then

\mathscr{G}(\mathsf{KS}(X),\tau,Z)
is isomorphic to
{\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})}

where
KZU
iff
K\subseteq U
.

Proof.

By Fact
3.2
it is enough to check that the embeddings
f\colon\mathsf{KS}(X)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})
and
g\colon\tau\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})
satisfy the conditions (1) and (2).

The first condition is immediate as, for a saturated subset
M
of
X
,
M=\bigcup\{\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux~|~x\in M\}
and
M=\bigcap\{U\in\tau~|~M\subseteq U\}
. The second condition follows immediately from the definition of
Z
.

We see that the proof of Proposition

relies on the fact that the principal upsets in the specialisation order

are compact and saturated. In the following we will try to mimic this construction in a purely frame-theoretic setting.

3.3
Canonical extension for frames

By the Hofmann–Mislove Theorem, compact saturated subsets of a sober space are in a bijective correspondence with Scott-open filters of its lattice of opens. Therefore, we expect that the best candidate for the canonical extension of a frame
L
, by example of Proposition
3.3
, is the lattice
\mathscr{G}(L^{\wedge},L,Z)
where, for
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
a\in L
,
\kappa Za
iff
a\in\kappa
. The expression for
Z
represents the statement that the compact saturated set corresponding to
\kappa
is a subset of the open set corresponding to
a
.

Before we understand what is the structure of the lattice we obtained this way, let us first describe the canonical extension
L^{\delta}
of
L
based on the expected mutual relationship between Scott-open filters
L^{\wedge}
and (abstract) open sets
L
when embedded into
L^{\delta}
. Since it is expected that the compact saturated subset corresponding to a Scott-open filter is obtained as the “intersection” of all the opens it contains, it is natural to consider the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
L
be a frame. A monotone mapping
e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
into a complete lattice
L^{\delta}
is a
canonical extension
of
L
if

(Dense)

For all
u,v\in L^{\delta}
,
u\leq v
if, for all
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
a\in L
, satisfying
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq u
and
v\leq e(a)
implies
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(a)
.

(Compact)

If
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(a)
for some
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
a\in L
, then
a\in\kappa
.

Note that
L^{\delta}
is not required to be distributive, nor even a frame.

Example 3.5
.

Let us check that the definition holds for our motivating example. We check that, for a sober space
(X,\tau)
, the embedding
\tau\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(X,\leq_{\tau})
is a canonical extension of
\tau
.

Compactness follows immediately from the Hofmann–Mislove Theorem because, for a Scott-open filter
\kappa\subseteq\tau
and an open
U\in\tau
,

\bigcap\kappa\subseteq U
iff
U\in\kappa.

For density, let
M\not\subseteq N
for some upsets
M
and
N
. Since
N
is equal to
\bigcap\{U\in\tau~|~N\subseteq U\}
, there must be a
U\in\tau
such that
M\not\subseteq U
and
N\subseteq U
. Pick an

. Then
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux\not\subseteq U
but
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux\subseteq M
. For the Scott-open filter
\kappa=\{V\in\tau~|~\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux\subseteq V\}
corresponding to
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux
we see that
\bigcap\kappa\not\subseteq U
.

We postpone the proof of existence and uniqueness of canonical extensions for the moment and focus on the properties that follow immediately from the definition.

Since the expression
\bigwedge e[\kappa]
appears repeatedly in our theory, we define a shorthand. For a map
e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
, set

e^{\wedge}\colon L^{\wedge}\to L^{\delta},\quad\kappa\mapsto\bigwedge e[\kappa]=\bigwedge\{e(a)~|~a\in\kappa\}.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
L
be a frame and
e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
be its canonical extension. Then

1.

e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
is a preframe homomorphism preserving 0.

2.

e^{\wedge}\colon L^{\wedge}\to L^{\delta}
is an injective co-preframe homomorphism

(recall that
L^{\wedge}
is taken to be ordered by reverse inclusion).

3.

For every
u\in L^{\delta}
,

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\bigwedge\{e(a)~|~a\in L,\ u\leq e(a)\}\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0pt

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=\bigvee\{e^{\wedge}(\kappa)~|~\kappa\in L^{\wedge},\ e^{\wedge}(\kappa)\leq u\}.

4.

If
e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
is injective, then it is a frame homomorphism.

5.

If
L
is locally compact, then
e
is an injective frame homomorphism.

Proof.

(1) First we check that
e
preserves 0 and 1. For the former, by density of
e
, it is enough to check that whenever
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(0)
and
0\leq e(a)
then also
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(a)
. From compactness applied to the first inequality we get that
0\in\kappa
and, from monotonicity,
e(0)\leq e(a)
. Hence,
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(0)\leq e(a)
. We check that
1\leq e(1)
by a similar argument. Let
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
a\in L
be such that
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq 1
and
e(1)\leq e(a)
. Since
1\in\kappa
and
e
is monotone,
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(1)\leq e(a)
.

To check that
e
preserves finite meets and directed joins we only need to check one inequality because
e
is monotone. Let
a,b\in L
. Assume that
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(a)\wedge e(b)
and
e(a\wedge b)\leq e(c)
for some
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
c\in L
. By compactness
a,b\in\kappa
. Because
\kappa
is a filter,
a\wedge b\in\kappa
and so
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(c)
.

Lastly, to check that
e(\dirvee D)\leq\dirvee e[D]
for some directed
D\subseteq L
, assume that
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(\dirvee D)
and that
\dirvee e[D]\leq e(a)
for some
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and
a\in L
. Compactness and the fact that
\kappa
is Scott-open give that
d\in\kappa
for some
d\in D
and, consequently,
\bigwedge e[\kappa]\leq e(d)\leq\dirvee e[D]\leq e(a)
.

(2) For injectivity assume that
e^{\wedge}(\kappa)=e^{\wedge}(\lambda)
. Then, for an
a\in\lambda

As we explained at the beginning of Section

Extensions of maps

In the theory of canonical extensions, when extending maps between lattices to maps between their canonical extensions, two constructions are usually considered. The so-called
\sigma

\pi

Before we define the two extensions let us introduce a new convention. Writing the applications of the maps
e\colon L\to L^{\delta}
and
e^{\wedge}\colon L^{\wedge}\hookrightarrow L^{\delta}
everywhere gets tiring at times. For this reason, by abuse of notation, we will write elements of
L^{\delta}
in the image of

. For example, for a monotone map

\kappa\leq a\quad\text{and}\quad\bigwedge f[\kappa]\leq f(a)

are interpreted as

e^{\wedge}(\kappa)\leq e(a)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigwedge e[f[\kappa]]\leq e(f(a)),\quad\text{respectively.}\quad

Furthermore, if
f
is a preframe homomorphism, then taking the preimage of a Scott-open filter gives again a Scott-open filter. In such case, we denote the corresponding monotone map by

f^{\dagger}\colon M^{\wedge}\to L^{\wedge},\quad\lambda\mapsto f^{-1}[\lambda].

Then, with the convention from above,
f^{\dagger}(\lambda)\leq u
means
e^{\wedge}(f^{\dagger}(\lambda))\leq u

With this in mind, let us define the two extensions

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigvee\{\bigwedge f[\kappa]~|~\kappa\in L^{\wedge},\ \kappa\leq u\},

\displaystyle f^{\pi}\colon u

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigwedge\{f(a)~|~a\in L,\ u\leq a\}.

In the following we show that calling those maps “extensions” is justified by the fact that, under mild assumptions, they both indeed extend the original map.

Lemma 6.1

Let

Lemma 6.2

For monotone maps

6.1
Perfect maps

In order to proceed with our theory, we have to assume further properties about our maps. We say that a monotone map
f\colon L\to M
between frames is

if, for every
\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
, the smallest filter containing

f^{\wedge}\colon\kappa\mapsto\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mu\{\bigwedge M~|~M\subseteq f[\kappa]\text{ and $M$ is finite}\}

is a well defined map
L^{\wedge}\to M^{\wedge}
.

Note that, in the locally compact case, this definition agrees with the other definitions for frame homomorphism known under the same name.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let

be a frame homomorphism with

Proof.

The strategy of the proof is the same as in the proof of Theorem IV-1.4 in

Observe that if a monotone map

\displaystyle f^{\sigma}\colon u

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigvee\{f^{\wedge}(\kappa)~|~\kappa\in L^{\wedge},\ \kappa\leq u\}.

7
Monotone maps and spectra

One of the reasons for studying canonical extensions of frames is that it allows us to represent monotone functions with respect to the specialisation order directly in the category of frames.

Let
(X,\tau)
and
(Y,\rho)
be locally compact sober spaces. It is known that monotone maps
(X,\leq_{\tau})\to(Y,\leq_{\rho})
are in a bijective correspondence with continuous maps
(X,\mathsf{Up}(X))\to(Y,\rho)
. Moreover, since
\tau^{\delta}\cong\mathsf{Up}(X)
(by Example
3.5
and Theorem
4.2
), we also have that

\textbf{Top}((X,\mathsf{Up}(X)),\,(Y,\rho))\cong\textbf{Frm}(\rho,\,\mathsf{Up}(X))\cong\textbf{Frm}(\rho,\tau^{\delta}),

where
\textbf{Top}(X,Y)
is the set of continuous maps
X\to Y
and
\textbf{Frm}(L,M)
is the set of frame homomorphisms
L\to M
. In other words, we obtain:

Proposition 7.1
.

For locally compact frames
L
and
M
, monotone maps
\mathsf{pt}(L)\to\mathsf{pt}(M)
, with respect to the specialisation orders, are in a bijective correspondence with the frame homomorphisms
M\to L^{\delta}
.

Next, for a locally compact frame
L
and its topological dual
(X,\tau)
, we know that the embeddings
e\colon L\hookrightarrow L^{\delta}
and
L\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(\mathsf{pt}(L))\cong\mathsf{Up}(X)
are isomorphic but this does not mean that the spaces
(X,\mathsf{Up}(X))
and
\mathsf{pt}(L^{\delta})
are homeomorphic. The embedding
L\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(X)
arises from the continuous map
(X,\mathsf{Up}(X))\to(X,\tau)
but, as we will see, the onto map
\mathsf{pt}(e)\colon\mathsf{pt}(L^{\delta})\twoheadrightarrow\mathsf{pt}(L)
can in general be a different map.

In the following we examine the relationship between the spaces
(X,\mathsf{Up}(X))
and
\mathsf{pt}(L^{\delta})
.
First, let us look at the interplay between the spectra of the original frame and its canonical extension.

Observation 7.2
.

Let
L
be any frame and let
\mathbf{2}
be the two element frame.

1.

\mathbf{2}
is isomorphic to its canonical extension
\mathbf{2}^{\delta}
.

2.

All frame homomorphisms
L\to\mathbf{2}
are perfect.

Proof.

(1) Since
\mathbf{2}^{\wedge}\cong\mathbf{2}
, it is easy to check that the identity mapping
\mathbf{2}\to\mathbf{2}
is both dense and compact. (2) The image of a Scott-open filter under a homomorphism
L\to\mathbf{2}
is automatically a Scott-open filter on
\mathbf{2}
.
∎

Let
L
be a locally compact frame. The frame embedding
e\colon L\hookrightarrow L^{\delta}
gives rise to a continuous map

\displaystyle\mathsf{pt}(e)\colon

\displaystyle\mathsf{pt}(L^{\delta})\to\mathsf{pt}(L),\quad r\colon L^{\delta}\to\mathbf{2}\kern 5.0pt\longmapsto\kern 5.0ptr\circ e\colon L\to\mathbf{2}.

An immediate consequence of Observation
7.2
is that there is also a map in the opposite direction:

\displaystyle\mathsf{pt}(L)\to\mathsf{pt}(L^{\delta}),\quad p\colon L\to\mathbf{2}\kern 5.0pt\longmapsto\kern 5.0ptp^{\delta}\colon L^{\delta}\to\mathbf{2}^{\delta}\cong\mathbf{2}.

It is also immediate to check that
p^{\delta}(u)
, for a
u\in L^{\delta}
, is computed as
\bigwedge p[F_{u}]
where
F_{u}=\{a\in L~|~u\leq a\}
(we follow the convention from Section
6
).

As a result, we obtain that
\mathsf{pt}(e)
is onto. Indeed, let
p\colon L\to\mathbf{2}
be a frame homomorphism. For an
a\in L
,

(\mathsf{pt}(e)(p^{\delta}))(a)=p^{\delta}(e(a))=\bigwedge p[F_{a}]=\bigwedge p[\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua]=p(a).

This is no surprise as
e
is injective. However, because
p^{\delta}
preserves all meets (Corollary
6.7
), we see that
\mathsf{pt}(e)
stays onto even if we restrict it to the subspace
\mathsf{pt}_{\bigwedge}(L^{\delta})
of frame homomorphism

which preserve all meets. This makes sense since
L^{\delta}
lives in the category of completely distributive lattices and so its spectrum should be computed there.

To show that
\mathsf{pt}(e)
is injective, when restricted to
\mathsf{pt}_{\bigwedge}(L^{\delta})
, let
r\colon L^{\delta}\to\mathbf{2}
be a complete lattice homomorphism and let
u\in L^{\delta}
. We have that

(r\circ e)^{\delta}(u)=\bigwedge_{a\in F_{u}}(re)^{\delta}(a)=\bigwedge_{a\in F_{u}}r(a)=r(u)

where the first equality holds because
(r\circ e)^{\delta}
preserves meets and
u
is equal to the meet
\bigwedge F_{u}
in
L^{\delta}
, the second equality follows from Lemma
6.1
and the last equality holds because
r
preserves meets.

We have showed that there is a bijection between the points of
\mathsf{pt}(L)
and
\mathsf{pt}_{\bigwedge}(L^{\delta})

\mathsf{pt}(L)
with the topology of upsets.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
L
be a locally compact frame and

be its dual space. Then,
\mathsf{pt}_{\bigwedge}(L^{\delta})

We know that

. What is left to show is that the points of
\mathsf{pt}_{\bigwedge}(L^{\delta})
separate opens. Let

in

\kappa\in L^{\wedge}
and

such that

and

[

and

and
p^{\delta}(u)\leq p^{\delta}(a)=0
.
∎

\mathsf{RIdl}(A)^{\delta}
.

Sam van Gool also shows that his canonical extension lifts the natural morphisms of join-strong proximity lattices (the so-called
j-morphisms
) to maps between the corresponding canonical extensions. Such lifted maps preserve all meets and, when assuming the axiom of choice, it can be shown that they also preserve all joins
[
26
]
. Since j-morphisms between proximity lattices correspond exactly to frame homomorphisms between the corresponding stably compact frames, our Theorem
6.6
improves van Gool’s result in sense that we do not need to use any choice principle for perfect morphisms.

8.3
The Boolean case

It turns out that canonical extensions of Boolean algebras
{B\hookrightarrow B^{\delta}}
, which we showed how to obtain by frame-theoretic means in Section
8.1
, also have a different frame-theoretic description. First, recall that a frame is
subfit
if it satisfies the following condition

(\forall a,b)\qquad a\not\leq b\implies\exists c\ \ a\vee c=1\kern 5.0pt\text{and}\kern 5.0ptb\vee c\not=1

Let us refer to a recent result by Richard Ball and Aleš Pultr
[
1
]
for subfit frames.

Fact 8.3
.

Let
L
be a subfit frame and
M
a complete Boolean algebra. If
L
embeds into
M
in such a way that, whenever
x<y
in
M
, then there are
a<b
in
L
such that

x\wedge b\leq a\quad\text{and}\quad y\vee a\geq b,

then
M
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)
, which is the frame of sublocales of
L
which are joins of closed sublocales.

Moreover, Ball and Pultr
[
1
]
also show that
\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathcal{O}(X))\cong\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)
for every T
1
-space
X
. On the other hand, we know that
B^{\delta}
is a complete Boolean algebra which is, assuming the Axiom of Choice, isomorphic to
\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(\mathsf{spec}(B))
, where
\mathsf{spec}(B)
is the Stone dual to
B
. This means that the canonical extension of
B
(seen as a lattice), the canonical extension of the frame
\mathsf{Idl}(B)
, and
\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathsf{Idl}(B))
are all isomorphic. In fact, we can prove the same statement without the detour to spaces and without the axiom of choice.

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
B
be a Boolean algebra, then
B^{\delta}
(resp.
\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
) is isomorphic to
\mathcal{S}_{c}(\mathsf{Idl}(B))
.

Proof.

We show that the embedding
e\colon\mathsf{Idl}(B)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
satisfies the conditions of Fact
8.3
. Observe that
\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
is a complete Boolean algebra since
B^{\delta}
is (Theorem 6.3 in
[
12
]
) and since
B^{\delta}\cong\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
(Proposition
8.1
).

Next, let
x<y
in
\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
. By density of
e
, there is an ideal
I\in\mathsf{Idl}(B)
such that

\displaystyle x\leq e(I)\quad\text{and}\quad y\not\leq e(I).

(1)

Next, by density of the lattice canonical extension
i\colon B\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Idl}(B)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
, there is a filter
F\in\mathsf{Idl}(B)
such that

\displaystyle\bigwedge i[F]\leq y\quad\text{and}\quad\bigwedge i[F]\not\leq e(I)=\bigvee i[I].

(2)

Hence, compactness of
i
gives that
F\cap I=\emptyset
or, in other words, that
f\not\leq i
for every
f\in F
and
i\in I
. Since
f\not\leq i
iff
i\vee\neg f\not=1
, we see that the join of ideals
I\vee\neg F
, where
\neg F=\{\neg f~|~f\in F\}
, is not equal to the ideal
\mathord{\downarrow}\mkern 1.0mu1
. Set
a=e(I\vee\neg F)
and
b=1
. Finally we check that the pair
a<b
satisfies the two required inequalities. By (
1
) we have that

x\wedge b=x\leq e(I)\leq e(I\vee\neg F)=a

and by (
2
) we have that

y\vee a\geq y\vee e(\neg F)\geq\bigwedge i[F]\vee e(\neg F)=1=b.

To see why the penultimate equality holds, let
\bigwedge i[F]\vee e(\neg F)=\bigwedge i[F]\vee\bigvee i[\neg F]\leq i(z)
for some
z\in B
. We see that
z\in F
by compactness of
i
. Moreover, because
i(\neg z)\leq\bigvee i[\neg F]\leq i(z)
and so
\neg z\leq z
, it must be that
z
is equal to 1 (in
B
).
∎

Note that the present theorem cannot be generalised to distributive lattices because canonical extensions of those are not complete Boolean algebras in general, as required by Fact
8.3
.

9
Final remarks

In this paper we have outlined the basics of the theory of canonical extensions for frames. By the example of how powerful the theory of canonical extensions has shown to be for posets and lattices, it is natural to ask whether this can happen for frames too. For example, already with the present work we can lift operations on frames to their canonical extensions. As in the classical theory of canonical extensions, one should expect that such lifted operations will preserve certain types of equations and also that the original operations correspond to certain relations on the spectrum of the frame (in the spirit of
[
15
,
11
]
).

However, the current results are only a starting point. It is not clear yet how to generalise the theory to arbitrary frames. Our construction works well for locally compact frames or, more generally, for frames with enough compact fitted sublocales (Proposition
5.1
).

Another missing piece of the puzzle concerns
\sigma
– and
\pi
-extensions of maps. All continuous maps between sober spaces lift to continuous maps between their canonical extensions (the preimage of an upset, by a continuous map, is always an upset). For this reason we would expect that homomorphisms between frames lift to complete lattice homomorphisms between the corresponding canonical extensions. However, we were not able to prove this without assuming perfectness. Maybe a more general construction of canonical extensions needs to be considered first.

Finally, it is well known that a complete Boolean algebra is completely distributive if and only if it is atomic, which is a fact that relies on a non-constructive choice principle. For this reason, one cannot expect to prove that the canonical extension of an arbitrary frame is completely distributive. This is because in the special case when we take the frame
\mathsf{Idl}(B)
, for a Boolean algebra
B
, the canonical extension
\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
is known to be a complete Boolean algebra (Theorem 6.3 in
[
12
]
) and so proving complete distributivity for
\mathsf{Idl}(B)^{\delta}
requires non-constructive principle. It remains open, however, whether canonical extensions of frames are always frames and coframes. Note that this is always the case for canonical extensions of distributive lattices (Theorem 3 in
[
11
]
).
