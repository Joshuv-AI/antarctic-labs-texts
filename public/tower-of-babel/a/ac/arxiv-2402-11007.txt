Stability of Quasiperiodic Superconductors
Nicole S. Ticea, Julian May-Mann, Jiewen Xiao, Erez Berg, Trithep Devakul

Abstract
We study the effects of quasiperiodicity on the stability of conventional and unconventional superconductors. Quasiperiodicity is modelled using the three-dimensional Aubry-André (AA) model, a system in which electrons are coupled to a translation-symmetry-breaking potential that is incommensurate with the underlying lattice. Upon increasing the strength of the quasiperiodic potential, the single-particle eigenstates undergo a transition from a ballistic to diffusive character. Here, we study the instability of the model towards superconductivity. We find that in the ballistic regime, the system is unstable towards both s s -wave and p p -wave superconductivity. In contrast, only the conventional s s -wave instability survives in the intermediate diffusive regime. Our result suggest a version of Anderson’s theorem for quasiperiodic systems, relating the normal state dynamics to the stability of conventional and unconventional superconductivity. These findings are relevant vis-à-vis recent studies of superconductivity in quasiperiodic moiré structures.

Because translational symmetry is broken in systems with either quasiperiodicity or random disorder, one might expect quasiperiodic systems to behave similarly to those with disorder. Quasiperiodic lattices, however, possess two or more spatially periodic orders that are incommensurate with each other
Levine and Steinhardt 1986
. Because of these periodic orders, quasiperiodic systems—unlike disordered systems—are highly structured in momentum space; the diffraction pattern of the ideal, infinite quasicrystal can be characterized by a self similar arrangement of Bragg peaks, densely tiled throughout reciprocal space
Levine and Steinhardt 1984
. A paradigmatic quasiperiodic system is the Aubry-André (AA) model in one spatial dimension (1D)
Aubry and André 1980
, a model of fermions on a lattice subject to a periodically-modulating onsite potential that is incommensurate with the lattice. As the potential strength is tuned, this system exhibits a transition at finite potential strength from an phase with ballistic states (i.e., momentum-space localized states) to a phase with real-space localized states. In contrast, randomly disordered systems in one dimension tend towards localization for infinitesimal disorder. The AA model can also be generalized to 3D
Devakul and Huse 2017
, where it displays two finite-potential-strength transitions: first from a ballistic to a diffusive phase, and then from the diffusive phase to a real-space-localized phase. For random systems in 3D, the ballistic-to-diffusive transition occurs at infinitesimal disorder strength.

Fan et al. 2021

An advantage of working with the AA model is that the quasiperiodicity can be tuned by adjusting the strength of the potential, in the same way that one can study the effects of random disorder simply by tuning the strength of the disordered potential. This makes it possible to directly compare the effects of disorder and quasiperiodicity on superconductivity. In randomly disordered systems, low levels of non-magnetic disorder do not affect

In the three-dimensional (3D) AA model, we find that weak-coupling instabilities of both conventional

For unconventional superconductivity, the instability vanishes once the strength of the quasiperiodic potential exceeds a critical value. This critical value coincides with the transition from the ballistic to the diffusive phase of the normal state; that is to say, weak coupling unconventional superconductivity only occurs in the ballistic phase of the 3D AA model. For conventional superconductivity, the weak coupling instability persists throughout the ballistic and diffusive phases. For finite strength couplings, we find that the

\displaystyle\langle c_{\bm{r}\downarrow}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}\downarrow}(0)\rangle\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\uparrow}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime\prime}\uparrow}(0)\rangle

\displaystyle+~

\displaystyle\langle c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime\prime}\uparrow}(0)\rangle\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}\downarrow}(0)\rangle\Big],

, leading to an expected decrease of the superconducting transition temperature when the quasiperiodic potential dominates. These results agree with our previous analysis of the susceptibilities. Namely, we find that
p
-wave superconductivity is robust in the ballistic phase but is suppressed in the diffusive phase, while
s
-wave remains robust well into the localized phase.

V
CONCLUSIONS

Having described the effects of quasiperiodicity on
s
- and
p
-wave superconductivity, one might naturally wonder whether these results generalize to other pairing symmetries as well. Owing to a lack of rotation symmetry in the model, there is no sharp distinction between, for example,
s
- and
d
-wave pairing symmetries. Nevertheless, in Appendix
B
, we present results on spin-singlet nearest neighbour pairing, which reduces to the
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
pairing term in the
v\to 0
limit. We find good qualitative agreement between the
p
-wave and nearest-neighbour spin-singlet data, suggesting that the essential element resulting in the suppression of the log divergence is not the pairing symmetry, but rather whether the gap integrates to zero over the Brillouin zone.

In summary, we have shown that quasiperiodicity behaves quite unlike random disorder in the context of unconventional superconductivity. We find that
p
-wave superconductivity is robust up to a finite critical value of the potential strength
v_{c1}
. This value corresponds to the ballistic-to-diffusive transition of the non-interacting model.
s
-wave superconductivity, conversely, survives in all three phases—ballistic, diffusive, and localized.
Our results do not rule out the possibility of unconventional superconductivity in quasiperiodic twisted trilayer graphene
Uri et al. 2023
; however, further theoretical studies in 2D and including microscopic details are necessary to fully explore this possibility.

Appendix C
BdG Hamiltonian and Self-Consistency Equations

We can write the lattice Hamiltonian of a general superconducting system as

H=-\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime};\alpha}h^{\alpha}_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\alpha}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha}+V_{\text{int}},

(3)

(We now use
\alpha
to denote spin, to eliminate confusion in subsequent parts of this derivation). The interaction term can be decomposed into singlet and triplet parts:

V_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{4}}\sum_{m=0}^{3}g^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})^{\dagger}_{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{1}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}}(c_{\bm{r}\alpha_{1}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{3}})^{\dagger}c_{\bm{r}\alpha_{2}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{4}}

(4)

The tensor
g^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}
contains information about the pairing symmetry;
g_{0\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}
is the singlet component, while
g_{1\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}},g_{2\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}},g_{3\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}
are the triplet parts. For example, the following choices will give us the pairing symmetries considered in this paper:

•

g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}}^{0}=V_{0}
and
g_{\bm{r}\neq\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{0}=g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{m\neq 0}=0
gives
s
-wave pairing

•

g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}}^{0}=V_{0}
,
g_{\langle\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}\rangle}^{0}=V_{1}
, and
g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{m\neq 0}=0
gives spin-singlet nearest-neighbour pairing

•

g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{0}=g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{1}=g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{2}=0
and
g_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{3}=V_{1}
yields spin-triplet pairing of type
\left(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\right)/\sqrt{2}

We now perform a mean field decomposition on Eq.
4
:

V_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}...\alpha_{4}}\sum_{m=0}^{3}g^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})^{\dagger}_{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{1}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}}\left[\langle c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{3}}\rangle c_{\bm{r}\alpha_{2}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{4}}+c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\alpha_{1}}c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{3}}\langle c_{\bm{r}\alpha_{2}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{4}}\rangle\right]

(5)

We can define the order parameter as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r},\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{3}d^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})_{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}}

(6)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-d_{x}(\bm{k})+id_{y}(\bm{k})&d_{z}(\bm{k})+\Delta_{s}(\bm{k})\\
d_{z}(\bm{k})-\Delta_{s}(\bm{k})&d_{x}(\bm{k})+id_{y}(\bm{k})\end{pmatrix}

where

d^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}=g^{m}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}\sum_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}}(\sigma_{m}i\sigma_{2})_{\alpha_{3}\alpha_{1}}\langle c_{\bm{r}\alpha_{1}}c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\alpha_{3}}\rangle

(7)

In more transparent notation,

\displaystyle d^{0}\equiv\Delta_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta^{+-}-\Delta^{-+}\right)

(8)

This is the spin-singlet order parameter, where
+(-)
is spin up (down). The spin-triplet order parameter, conversely, is a vector, and it is given by

\displaystyle d^{m>0}\equiv\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Delta^{--}-\Delta^{++},-i(\Delta^{--}+\Delta^{++}),\Delta^{+-}+\Delta^{-+}\right)

(9)

The self-consistency equation for mean-field spin-singlet
s
-wave is therefore:

\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}}^{\text{s},s}=\frac{V_{0}}{2}\Big(\langle c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}c_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\rangle-\langle c_{\bm{r}\downarrow}c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\rangle\Big)

(10)

For spin-singlet nearest-neighbour pairing, we have:

\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\text{s},\text{nn}}=\frac{V_{1}}{2}\Big(\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\uparrow}c_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\rangle-\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\rangle\Big)

(11)

And, finally, for
p
-wave spin-triplet pairing, the order parameter is given by

\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\text{p},p}=\frac{V_{1}}{2}\Big(\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\uparrow}c_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\rangle+\langle c_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\rangle\Big)

(12)

We diagonalize equation
4
by introducing the Bogoliubov quasiparticle operators
(\gamma_{n},\gamma_{n}^{\dagger})
:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\\
c^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\bm{r}n\uparrow}&v^{*}_{\bm{r}n\uparrow}\\
v_{\bm{r}n\downarrow}&u^{*}_{\bm{r}n\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\\
\gamma^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}

(13)

In this basis,
4
is diagonalized, with eigenequations given by

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{H}_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}&\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\\
\Delta^{\dagger}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\bm{r}}&-\mathcal{H}^{*}_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\uparrow}\\
v_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}=E_{n}\begin{pmatrix}u_{\bm{r}\uparrow}\\
v_{\bm{r}\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}

(14)

We now have three sets of self-consisten equations, one set for each of the pairing symmetries we are interested in studying here. By substituting Eq.
13
into
10
,
11
, and
12
, we can derive expressions for the order parameters with respect to the Bogoliubov coefficients:

\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{V_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}{4}\sum_{n}(u_{\bm{r}\uparrow}^{n}v_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}^{n*}+h.c.)\tanh{\left(\frac{E_n}{k_BT}\right)}

(15)

for spin-singlet pairing, and

\Delta_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}=\frac{V_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}}{2}\sum_{n}\Big[u_{\bm{r}\uparrow}^{n}v_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\downarrow}^{n*}f(-E_{n})+u_{\bm{r}^{\prime}\uparrow}^{n}v_{\bm{r}\downarrow}^{n*}f(E_{n})\Big]

(16)

for spin-triplet (
p
-wave) superconductivity. In the above expression,
f(E_{n})
is the Fermi-Dirac distribution evaluated at energy
E_{n}
.

Appendix D
Linearized Gap Equation

We are interested in calculating the pair susceptibilities,

\displaystyle\chi_{ab;cd}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau\langle\psi_{ab}(\tau)\psi^{\dagger}_{cd}(0)\rangle

(17)

for the spin-singlet and triplet configurations, with the singlet operator defined as

\psi^{\text{s}}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle c_{a\uparrow}c_{b\downarrow}\rangle-\langle c_{a\downarrow}c_{b\uparrow}\rangle\Big)

(18)

and triplet operator

\psi^{\text{p}}_{ab}=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\langle c_{a\uparrow}c_{b\downarrow}\rangle+\langle c_{a\downarrow}c_{b\uparrow}\rangle\Big)

(19)

Substituting these equations for the operators into Eq.
17
, we have

\chi_{ab;cd}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau\Big[\langle c_{a\downarrow}(\tau)c_{c\downarrow}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle\langle c_{b\uparrow}(\tau)c_{d\uparrow}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle\pm\langle c_{a\downarrow}(\tau)c_{d\downarrow}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle\langle c_{b\uparrow}(\tau)c_{c\uparrow}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle\Big]

(20)

The
+
sign is for spin-singlet pairing, while the
-
is for spin-triplet. We solve for the pair susceptibility by diagonalizing the non-interacting Hamiltonian with quasiperiodic disorder:
H_{0}=\sum_{n}E_{n}a^{\dagger}_{n}a_{n}
. Making the substitution
c_{\bm{r}\sigma}=\sum_{n}U^{*}_{\bm{r}n\sigma}a_{n}
, we can calculate the Green’s function

\langle c_{a\sigma}(t)c_{b\lambda}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle=\sum_{n}U^{*}_{an\sigma}U_{bn\lambda}e^{-\tau E_{n}}\times[1-f(E_{n})]

(21)

Substituting this expression in
20
, we can derive an expression for the susceptibility in terms of the eigenstates of the single-particle Hamiltonian:

\chi_{ab;cd}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n,m}\left(\frac{1-f(E_{n})-f(E_{m})}{E_{n}+E_{m}}\right)\times\Big(U^{*}_{an\downarrow}U_{cn\downarrow}U^{*}_{bm\uparrow}U_{dm\uparrow}\pm U^{*}_{an\uparrow}U_{dn\uparrow}U^{*}_{bm\downarrow}U_{cm\downarrow}\Big)

(22)

Once again, we remind the reader that the
+
sign is for spin-singlet superconductivity, while the
-
sign is reserved for spin-triplet.

Appendix E
Superfluid stiffness

The signature of the onset of the superconducting phase is the Meissner effect, in which a sufficiently weak magnetic field is expelled from the bulk of the superconductor up to a finite penetration depth,
\lambda
. The penetration depth is inversely proportional to the superfluid stiffness. Therefore, the superfluid stiffness and its temperature dependence set a criterion for superconductivity. In this section, we will derive a formula for the superfluid stiffness in the basis of the BdG coefficients following the derivation in
Zhu 2016
. In the presence of an electric field applied in the
\hat{x}
direction, the system will respond with a current,

J_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)=\sigma_{xx}(\bm{r}_{i},\omega)E_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t),

(23)

where
\sigma_{xx}(\mathbf{r}_{i},\omega)
is the local conductivity at the
i
th site. We will work in the Coulomb gauge, where the potential can be expressed as

A_{x}(\bm{r},t)=-\frac{i}{\omega}E_{x}(\bm{r},t)=-\frac{i}{\omega}\Xi_{x}e^{i(\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}-\omega t)}

(24)

In the presence of this gauge field, the hopping elements pick up a complex exponential factor:

-\sum_{ij,\sigma}t_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}\to-\sum_{ij,\sigma}\tilde{t}_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}

(25)

where
\tilde{t}=t_{ij}e^{i\phi_{ij}(t)}
, for gauge phase
\phi_{ij}(t)\equiv eA_{ij}=eA_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)(x_{i}-x_{j})
. Expanding
\tilde{t}
up to second order in
A
, we have

-\sum_{ij,\sigma}\tilde{t}_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}\approx-\sum_{ij,\sigma}t\left[1+i\phi_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{ij}^{2}\right]c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}

(26)

Moving forward, we restrict the sum to next nearest neighbours on a lattice with spacing
a
. The new Hamiltonian, in the presence of the gauge field
A
, can be written in terms as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{\text{int}}-it\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,\sigma}\left[\phi_{ij}(t)c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}+h.c.\right]+\frac{1}{2}t\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle,\sigma}\left[\phi^{2}_{ij}(t)c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{j,\sigma}+h.c.\right]

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{\text{int}}-iea\sum_{i,\sigma}\left[A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i+\hat{x}},t)c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}c_{i,\sigma}-A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i+\hat{x}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{2}a^{2}}{2}\sum_{i,\sigma}\left[A^{2}_{x}(\bm{r}_{i+\hat{x}},t)c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}c_{i,\sigma}-A^{2}_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)c^{\dagger}_{i}c_{i+\hat{x}}\right]

To simplify this expression, we introduce the paramagnetic current
J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t)
and the local kinetic energy density
K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)
, which are defined as follows:

\displaystyle J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i})

\displaystyle=it\sum_{i,\sigma}\left[c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}c_{i,\sigma}-c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}\right]

(27)

\displaystyle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})

\displaystyle=-t\sum_{i,\sigma}\left[c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}c_{i,\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}c_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}\right]

(28)

Therefore, the Hamiltonian in the presence of the gauge potential can be expressed as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{0}+H_{\text{int}}-ea\sum_{i}A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i})-\frac{e^{2}a^{2}}{2}\sum_{i}A_{x}^{2}(\bm{r}_{i},t)K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})

(29)

\displaystyle\equiv H_{0}+H_{\text{int}}+H^{\prime}(t)

The total
x
-oriented current is given by the sum of the paramagnetic and diamagnetic pieces:

J_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)=-\frac{\delta H^{\prime}(t)}{\delta A-x(\bm{r}_{i},t)}=eaJ_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i})+e^{2}a^{2}K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)

(30)

Moving forward, we set the lattice constant
a=1
. To determine the superfluid stiffness, we must take the expectation value of the total current:

\displaystyle\langle J_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=e\langle J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle-e^{2}\langle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)

(31)

\displaystyle\approx ie\int_{-\infty}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t),H^{{}^{\prime}P}_{I}(t^{\prime})]\rangle-e^{2}\langle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)

In going from the first to the second line above, we applied the Kubo formula to find
\langle J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t)\rangle
. Here,

\displaystyle H^{{}^{\prime}P}_{I}(t^{\prime})=-e\sum_{i,\sigma}A_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime})J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime})

(32)

This is the perturbative piece of the Hamiltonian (due just to the paramagnetic term) in the interaction picture. After expressing the current operator in Fourier space,

J_{x}^{P}(\bm{q},t)=\sum_{i}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r},t),

(33)

we can derive

\displaystyle\langle J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\omega}\Xi_{x}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\differential t^{\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},t^{\prime})]\rangle e^{-i\omega t^{\prime}}

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\omega}\Xi_{x}e^{-i\omega t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{\omega}E_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}

In the second line we made the substitution
t^{\prime\prime}=t-t^{\prime}
. The total current density is therefore

\displaystyle\langle J_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)\rangle=\frac{e^{2}}{\omega}E_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}-\frac{ie^{2}}{\omega}\langle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle E_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)

(35)

To find the local conductivity, we divide by the local electric field:

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\bm{r}_{i},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle J_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)\rangle}{E_{x}(\bm{r}_{i},t)}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\omega}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{r}_{i},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}-\frac{ie^{2}}{\omega}\langle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle

To eliminate atomic fluctuations, we average over the spatial variable
\bm{r}_{i}
, yielding

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\bm{r}_{i},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{N\omega}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{q},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}-\frac{ie^{2}}{N\omega}\langle K_{x}\rangle

(37)

Here,
\langle K_{x}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\langle K_{x}(\bm{r}_{i})\rangle
. We can extend the limits to
\pm\infty
by introducing a Heaviside multiplier,
\Theta(t^{\prime\prime})
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{xx}(\bm{r}_{i},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{N\omega}e^{-i\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{i}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential t^{\prime\prime}\Theta(t^{\prime\prime})\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{q},t^{\prime\prime}),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle e^{i\omega t^{\prime\prime}}-\frac{ie^{2}}{N\omega}\langle K_{x}\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{i\omega}\left[-\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q},\omega)+\langle K_{x}\rangle\right]

Where we’ve defined the retarded correlation function of the particle current operator as

\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q},t)=-\frac{i}{N}\Theta(t)\langle[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]\rangle,

(39)

and its corresponding Fourier transform,

\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q},\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dte^{i\omega t}\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q},t)

(40)

Technically, we calculate the current-current correlator in the Matsubara formalism and then perform the analytic continuation
i\omega_{n}\to\omega+i\delta
. This means that the conductivity formula is actually given by

\sigma_{xx}(\bm{q},\omega)=\frac{e^{2}}{i(\omega+i\delta)}\left[-\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q},\omega)+\langle K_{x}\rangle\right]

(41)

The onset of superconductivity is characterized by the Meissner effect, which involves the current response to a static (
\omega=0
) and transverse gauge potential (
\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{A}=0
). For the Coulomb gauge,
\mathbf{A}
is entirely along the
\hat{x}
direction, so we must only satisfy
\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{A}=q_{x}A_{x}=0
. Allowing for arbitrary
\mathbf{A}
, this means that
q_{x}=0
. If the numerator approaches a finite limit as
\omega\to 0
, then the real part of
\sigma_{xx}(\omega)
will contain a delta function contribution
D_{s}\delta(\omega)
with Drude weight

\frac{D_{s}}{\pi e^{2}}=\frac{\rho_{s}^{*}}{4\pi}=-\langle K_{x}\rangle+\Pi_{xx}(q_{x}=0,q_{y}\to 0,\omega=0),

(42)

where
\rho^{*}_{s}
is the superfluid density. Crucially, we first set
\omega=0
and
q_{x}=0
.
Then
we take the limit
q_{y}\to 0
.
The kinetic energy density is straightforward to compute:

\displaystyle\langle K_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{t}{N}\sum_{i,\sigma}\sum_{n(E_{n}\geq 0)}\left\{f(E_{n})\left[u^{n*}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{n}+\text{c.c}\right]+(1-f(E_{n}))\left[v^{n}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}v_{i,\sigma}^{n*}+\text{c.c}\right]\right\}

(43)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2t}{N}\sum_{i}\sum_{n(E_{n}\geq 0)}\left\{f(E_{n})\left[u^{n*}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}u_{i,\sigma}^{n}+\text{c.c}\right]+(1-f(E_{n}))\left[v^{n}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}v_{i,\sigma}^{n*}+\text{c.c}\right]\right\}

In going to the second line, we account for the sum over spins with a factor of 2. Next, let us compute the current-current term. We aim to calculate

\displaystyle=[J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t)+J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)+J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]

(44)

\displaystyle=[J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]+[J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]

\displaystyle+[J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]+[J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]

\displaystyle=[J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\uparrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]+[J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\downarrow}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]

In going to the last line, we use the fermion commutation relation. Because our system has spin-flip symmetry, we can rewrite this as

[J_{x}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]=2[J_{x,\sigma}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\sigma}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)],

(45)

where
\sigma
can represent either
\uparrow
or
\downarrow
; for our purposes, it does not matter. We can rewrite the current-current correlator as

[J_{x,\sigma}^{P}(\bm{q},t),J_{x,\sigma}^{P}(-\bm{q},0)]=(it)^{2}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}e^{-i\bm{q}(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j})}[\hat{A}+\hat{B}+\hat{C}+\hat{D}]

(46)

where we’ve defined

\displaystyle\hat{A}

\displaystyle=[c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}(t)c_{i,\sigma}(t),c^{\dagger}_{j+\hat{x},\sigma}(0)c_{j,\sigma}(0)]

(47)

\displaystyle\hat{B}

\displaystyle=-[c^{\dagger}_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}(t)c_{i,\sigma}(t),c^{\dagger}_{j,\sigma}(0)c_{j+\hat{x},\sigma}(0)]

\displaystyle\hat{C}

\displaystyle=-[c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}(t)c_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}(t),c^{\dagger}_{j+\hat{x},\sigma}(0)c_{j,\sigma}(0)]

\displaystyle\hat{D}

\displaystyle=[c^{\dagger}_{i,\sigma}(t)c_{i+\hat{x},\sigma}(t),c^{\dagger}_{j,\sigma}(0)c_{j+\hat{x},\sigma}(0)]

Upon rewriting these operators in the BdG basis, we end up with an expression for the current-current correlator in momentum-frequency space

\Pi_{xx}(\bm{q})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,m}\left\{\frac{A_{nm}(\bm{q})\left[A^{*}_{nm}(\bm{q})+D_{nm}(-\bm{q})\right]}{E_{n}-E_{m}}\right\}\times\left[f(E_{n})-f(E_{m})\right]

(48)

Here, the sum is over
all
energy eigenvalues. To find the superfluid stiffness, we must then take the limit
q_{x}=0,q_{y}\to 0
, as described in Equation
42
. The transition temperature is directly proportional to this quantity:

T_{c}=\frac{D_{s}}{\pi e^{2}}=-\langle K_{x}\rangle+\Pi_{xx}(q_{x}=0,q_{y}\to 0,\omega=0)

(49)

Appendix F
Uniform susceptibility

To understand how the uniform susceptibility is relevant to understanding weak coupling instabilities, let us write the linearized gap equation in the following form,

\displaystyle\lambda F_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}=-\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}V_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}F_{\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}.

(50)

where
F_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}
is a normalized eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, and
\chi
is the susceptibility in either the s- or p-wave channel. If there is a weak coupling instability, then
\lambda\rightarrow+\infty
as the temperature
T\rightarrow 0
. To proceed let us expand
F_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}
according to the relative coordinate of the function,
\bm{\delta}
, and
\bm{r}
, which can be treated as the center of mass coordinate. Fourier transforming the gap function with respect to
\bm{r}
, we have that

F_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}=\frac{1}{L^{d/2}}\sum_{\bm{k}}F_{\bm{k},\bm{\delta}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}

(51)

In terms of the Fourier components, the linearized gap equation is

\displaystyle\lambda F_{\bm{k},\bm{\delta}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}V_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}F_{\bm{k},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}e^{i(\bm{k}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}-\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r})}

(52)

The
\bm{k}=0
component of the solution can then be written in the suggestive form as

\displaystyle\lambda F_{0,\bm{\delta}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}V_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}F_{0,\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}-\sum_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\neq 0}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}V_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}F_{\bm{k},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}.

(53)

From this we find that if the uniform susceptibility,

\displaystyle\chi^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}

(54)

diverges as
T\rightarrow 0
, then
\lambda
must also diverge in this limit, indicating a weak coupling instability.

To reach this conclusion, we have made three notable assumptions. First, that the interaction
V_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}
is non-zero and only depends on the relative coordinate
\bm{\delta}
. This is simply a choice of interactions. Second, there is not another divergence in the sum over
\bm{k}\neq 0
that exactly cancels out the divergence of the uniform susceptibility. This assumption innocuous, as an exact cancellation of two divergent terms would be fine-tuned. Third, that
F_{0,\delta}\neq 0
. It should be noted that there are two important, and physically relevant, cases where
F_{0,\delta}=0
. First, it can be the case that the superconductivity oscillates with a fixed wave-vector, i.e., it is a pair density wave. In this case,
F_{0,\delta}=0
and
|F_{\bm{k},\delta}|
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
number for some finite number of
\bm{k}
’s and is zero otherwise. Second, it can be the case that the superconductivity is real space localized. For example, where, in real space,
F_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}=\delta_{\bm{r},\bm{R}}
for some fixed
\bm{R}
. the Fourier transform of this is then
F_{\bm{k},\delta}=\frac{1}{L^{d/2}}e^{i\bm{R}\cdot\bm{k}}
in
d
-dimensions. Therefore, in the thermodynamic limit,
L\rightarrow\infty
,
F_{\bm{k},\bm{\delta}}\rightarrow 0
. Hence, a weak coupling instability towards either a pair density wave or localized state will not lead to a divergence in the uniform susceptibility.

Averaging procedure

In the main text, we are compute the following quantity:

\displaystyle\bar{{\chi}}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{(NL)^{6}}\sum_{{{m}}{{n}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1-f(E_{{n}})-f(E_{{m}})}{E_{{n}}+E_{{m}}}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{p}}e^{i\bm{\delta}\cdot(\bm{k}+\bm{\phi}_{s})}{U}^{*{{n}}}_{-\bm{k}}(-\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s}){U}^{*{{m}}}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s})

(55)

\displaystyle\times\Big[e^{-i\bm{\delta}\cdot(\bm{p}+\bm{\phi}_{s})}{U}^{{n}}_{-\bm{p}}(-\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s}){U}^{{m}}_{\bm{p}}(\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s})\pm e^{-i\bm{\delta}\cdot(\bm{p}-\bm{\phi}_{s})}{U}^{{n}}_{\bm{p}}(\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s}){U}^{{m}}_{-\bm{p}}(-\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s})\Big].

Here, we use
{H}(\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s})
to refer to the quasiperiodic Hamiltonian defined on a lattice of size
{L}
where the hopping terms have a phase factor
\bm{\phi}_{s}
and quasiperiodic potential has an offset
\bm{\theta}_{s}
. We use
N
to denote the number of realizations of
{L}
and
\bm{\phi}_{s}
. The
{n}
th eigenstate is denoted
{U}^{{n}}_{\bm{k}}(\bm{\phi}_{s},\bm{\theta}_{s})
. The sum over
s
corresponds to averaging over twists and potential offsets. For simplicity, we set
\bm{\theta}_{s}=\bm{\phi}_{s}
, and average over both twists and potential offsets simultaneously. Specifically, we average over
N\approx 35
realizations of the Hamiltonian at every
v
, for both the quasiperiodic and randomly-disordered lattices. In the case of quasiperiodicity, we generate potential realizations by randomly sampling a single
\bm{\phi}
for each realization of the lattice. In the randomly-disordered case, we sample
\bm{\phi}
randomly on each site. We subsequently average over all
{\chi}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
at fixed
v
. We also compute the standard error of the mean, which appears as a ribbon in Figure
3
.

We will now explain how averaging over different values of
\bm{\phi}_{s}
allows us to approximate the behaviour of much larger systems. Here, we will assume that the potential offset
\bm{\theta}
vanishes, as the value of
\bm{\theta}
does not affect this discussion. For a system of size
\tilde{L}
with untwisted boundardy conditions, the uniform susceptibility is

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{L}^{6}}\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\chi_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}

(56)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{L}^{6}}\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum_{\tilde{m}\tilde{n}}\frac{1-f(E_{\tilde{n}})-f(E_{\tilde{m}})}{E_{\tilde{n}}+E_{\tilde{m}}}\Big[\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{n}}_{\bm{r}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{n}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{m}}_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{m}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}\pm\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{n}}_{\bm{r}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{n}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{m}}_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{m}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\Big].

We will now argue for approximating this quantity using a system of size
{L}
, such that
N{L}=\tilde{L}
for some large integer
N
. The Hamiltonian for the full system of size
\tilde{L}
will be given by
\tilde{H}
, while the Hamiltonian for the size-
{L}
system will be given by
{H}
. The kinetic parts of both
\tilde{H}
and
{H}
are the same, while the quasiperiodic parts differ on account of the rational approximant used to generate the potential. Let us use
\mathbf{B}_{\tilde{L}}
to denote the rational approximant to the quasiperiodic ordering vector on a system of size
\tilde{L}
. If the rational approximants are both close to the actual irrational ordering vector, then
\mathbf{B}_{\tilde{L}}\approx\mathbf{B}_{{L}}
.

Since
\tilde{H}
corresponds to a
\tilde{L}^{3}
matrix, computational limitations prevent us from calculating the uniform susceptibility of
\tilde{H}
when
\tilde{L}
is large. However, we shall argue that the uniform susceptibility of
\tilde{H}
can be well approximated by considering
\bm{\phi}_{s}
averages of
{H}(\bm{\phi}_{s})
, the quasiperiodic Hamiltonian defined on the system of size
{L}
, where we have attached a phase of
\phi_{s,i}
to the hopping term in the
\hat{u}_{i}
direction. Under a gauge transformation, this is equivalent to twisting the boundary condition in the
\hat{u}_{i}
direction by
{L}\phi_{s,i}

\bm{\phi}
take on the following values:

\phi_{s,i}=\frac{2\pi s_{i}}{L}=\frac{2\pi s_{i}}{N{L}}~~~~~s_{i}\in\{1,...,N\}

(57)

Specifically, we are calculating the Fourier transform of the uniform susceptibility of
{H}

\bm{\phi}

\displaystyle\bar{{\chi}}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{L^{6}}

\displaystyle\sum_{mn}\frac{1-f(E_{{n}})-f(E_{{m}})}{E_{{n}}+E_{{m}}}

(58)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{s=0}^{N-1}\sum_{\bm{r}\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{{L}-1}\Big[{U}^{*{{n}}}_{\bm{r}}(\bm{\phi}_{s}){U}^{{n}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\bm{\phi}_{s}){U}^{*{{m}}}_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}(\bm{\phi}_{s}){U}^{{m}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}(\bm{\phi}_{s})\pm{U}^{*{{n}}}_{\bm{r}}U^{{{n}}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}(\bm{\phi}_{s}){U}^{*{{m}}}_{\bm{r}+\bm{\delta}}(\bm{\phi}_{s}){U}^{{m}}_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\bm{\phi}_{s})\Big]

where we use
{U}(\bm{\phi}_{s})

{H}(\bm{\phi}_{s}))

F.0.1
Ballistic phase

We will first show that
\tilde{\chi}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
is well approximated by
\tilde{\chi}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
in the ballstic phase. We begin by considering the uniform susceptibility in momentum space. Here, we consider
\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}=0
for simplicity, though these results can be easily generalized to nonzero
\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}

\bm{k}\approx\bm{p}

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}^{0}_{0,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tilde{L}^{6}}\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\tilde{m}\tilde{n}}\frac{1-f(E_{\tilde{n}})-f(E_{\tilde{m}})}{E_{\tilde{n}}+E_{\tilde{m}}}\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{n}}_{-\bm{k}}\tilde{U}^{*\tilde{m}}_{\bm{k}}\Big[\tilde{U}^{\tilde{n}}_{-\bm{k}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{m}}_{\bm{k}}\pm\tilde{U}^{\tilde{n}}_{\bm{k}}\tilde{U}^{\tilde{m}}_{-\bm{k}}\Big]

(59)

Separately, consider the system of size
N^{3}{L}^{3}=\tilde{L}^{3}
composed of stitching together
N^{3}
copies of the Hamiltonian
H
. We will refer to this Hamiltonian as
H^{\prime}

is almost equivalent to
\tilde{H}
, except for the fact that the potential is constructed using rational approximant

F.0.2
Diffusive & localized regimens

In the localized or diffusive regimes (i.e.,
v>v_{c1}
), there is a characteristic length scale
l
such that the real space susceptibility calculated using
\tilde{H}
,
\tilde{\chi}_{\bm{r},\bm{r}+\bm{\delta};\bm{r}^{\prime},\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
falls off exponentially for
|\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}|>l

Dodaro and Kivelson 2018
. In the localized regime,
l
is the real space localization length, in the diffusive regime,
l
is thermal coherence length
\sqrt{\frac{\hbar D}{T}}
, which is finite at finite temperature. Hence, if
{L}\gg l
, then the uniform susceptibility calculated using
\tilde{H}
and
{H}(\bm{\phi}=0)
should be equivalent. Furthermore, if
{L}\gg l
twisting boundary conditions will not affect the susceptibility, as twisting boundary conditions will only affect the susceptibility when
\bm{r}
and
\bm{r}^{\prime}
are far separated. Based on these two observations, we conclude that
\tilde{\chi}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
will be well approximated by
\bar{{\chi}}^{0}_{\bm{\delta},\bm{\delta}^{\prime}}
.
