Full-waveform Approximation of Finite-Sized Acoustic Apertures: Forward and Adjoint Wavefields
Ashkan Javaherian and Seyed Kamaledin Setarehdan

Abstract




Abstract

The acoustic wave equation describes wave propagation in terms of volumetric radiation sources (
s
) or surface sources, which can be monopole or dipole in nature. For surface sources, boundary value problems provide wavefield representations using the Kirchhoff-Helmholtz or Rayleigh-Sommerfeld integrals. This study examines the equivalence between the analytic forms of monopole and dipole integral formulas and their full-waveform approximations. Leveraging this equivalence, we introduce a measurement operator that maps the volumetric pressure—computed by solving the wave equation—onto the pressure detected at the surface of the receivers. Furthermore, we derive the adjoint of a forward operator that incorporates this measurement operator and show that it matches the time-reversed interior-field dipole integral formula evaluated on the receiver surface. These findings have significant implications for both forward and inverse problems in acoustics, particularly in applications requiring precise amplitude approximations, such as therapeutic ultrasound optimization, attenuation reconstruction, and photoacoustic tomography.

The forcing term on the right-hand side of this second-order PDE can be either time-instantaneous or time-varying. For time-varying sources, a key example is the radiation from vibrating acoustic apertures.

The solution to the wave equation is typically non-unique. To obtain a unique solution, Cauchy initial conditions, defined in terms of the wavefield and its time derivative at the initial time, are enforced. These initial conditions establish a causal relationship between the solution wavefield and the source, ensuring that the solution wavefield vanishes prior to the onset of radiation from the source. Thus, solving the wave equation can be formulated as uniquely representing the propagated wavefield in terms of the radiation source (forcing term) by applying the causality conditions.

Let us delve into this problem in more detail. Let
d\in\{2,3\}
denote the number of dimensions of the medium. The radiation source,
s

d

acoustic aperture

The former integral is suitable for apertures with arbitrary geometries, such as spheres or cylinders, while the latter is used for infinite-plane apertures.

Typically, the integrand in these formulas is a function of the pressure and its normal derivative over the surface. However, since the wave equation establishes a dependency between these quantities on the surface, the solution wavefield is well-defined if the associated integral formula is expressed in terms of only one of these quantities, i.e., either the pressure or its normal derivative. Consequently, to obtain a unique solution using these surface integral formulas, in addition to enforcing Cauchy conditions that satisfy causality, boundary conditions must also be imposed on the chosen solution space. The choice of the solution space and the boundary conditions depends on the physics of the problem.

For example, consider a vibrating piston surrounded by a

\frac{\partial p}{\partial\bm{n}}

On the other hand, under a

2.
Wave Equation in the Time Domain

This section considers the propagation of acoustic waves from a real-valued space-and-time-varying source in an infinite, isotropic, and homogeneous medium in free space. Let
\bm{x}=\big[\bm{x}^{1},\dots,\bm{x}^{d}\big]^{T}
denote a spatial position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
as the number of dimensions. The analysis presented here is performed for
d=3
but holds for
d=2
by replacing volumes with surfaces and surfaces with lines. (For instance, a line source in a 3D medium produces acoustic waves that propagate as cylindrical waves, equivalent to an omnidirectional point source in a 2D medium.)

The real-valued wavefield satisfies the inhomogeneous wave equation, expressed as

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}p(\bm{x},t)-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bigg[\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla p(\bm{x},t)\bigg]=s(\bm{x},t).

Here, the term on the right-hand side,
s(\bm{x},t)
, is the forcing term, referred to here as the
radiation source
, which is compactly supported in the spatio-temporal region
\Lambda_{s}=\{\bm{x}\in\nu_{s},\ t\in(0,T_{s})\}
. Here,
\nu_{s}
is a
d
-dimensional space (a volumetric region for
d=3
), and
(0,T_{s})
denotes the radiation time of the source. The radiation source
s
has units of
\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-\text{d}}\,\text{s}^{-2}
and is assumed to be square-integrable over
\chi_{s}
.

Additionally,
c
represents the velocity of wave propagation in the medium (units:
\text{ms}^{-1}
), and
\rho_{0}
denotes the ambient density of the medium (units:
\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-\text{d}}
). The pressure wavefield
p
, the unknown parameter of the wave equation, has units of
\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{2-\text{d}}\,\text{s}^{-2}
(or Pascal).

Assuming a constant value for
\rho_{0}
, the wave equation (
1
) simplifies to its canonical form:

(2)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\bigg]p(\bm{x},t)=s(\bm{x},t).

2.1.
Cauchy Conditions for Unique Solution

Typically, the solution to the wave equation is nonunique. A unique solution is obtained by confining the wavefield to a particular solution that is causally related to the source, i.e., a wavefield
p
that vanishes prior to the initial time
t=0
of the source radiation. By imposing the Cauchy conditions

(3)

\displaystyle p_{+}(\bm{x},t)\big|_{t=0}=0,\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{+}(\bm{x},t)\big|_{t=0}=0,

a causal solution to the wave equation (
2
) is obtained, where the subscript
+
denotes causality. Solving the inhomogeneous wave equation (
2
) with the causality conditions (Cauchy conditions (
3
)) is referred to as the
radiation problem
.

By contrast, if the forcing term
s
is set to zero, and the Cauchy conditions (
3
) are replaced with arbitrary and inhomogeneous fields at
t=0
, the wave equation (
2
) becomes an
initial-value problem
.

2.2.
Green’s Function Solution to the Wave Equation

Consider the wave equation (
2
) as a radiation problem for a particular choice of source,
s(\bm{x},t)=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
, where
\delta(\cdot)
is the Dirac delta distribution, and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
are free parameters in the space and time domains, respectively. In an infinite free space, the Green’s function solution to the wave equation satisfies

(4)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\bigg]g(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

For brevity, from now on, the Green’s function will be written in the form

(5)

\displaystyle g(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=g(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime}),

where
\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}=\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}
and
t_{\mathfrak{d}}=t-t^{\prime}
. Similar to the wave equation (
2
), a unique solution to Eq. (
4
) is obtained by assuming a causality condition for the Green’s function, i.e.,
g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=0
for
t_{\mathfrak{d}}<0
. For
d=3
, the causal Green’s function satisfies

(6)

\displaystyle g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\delta(t_{\mathfrak{d}}-\frac{x_{\mathfrak{d}}}{c})}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}},

where
x_{\mathfrak{d}}=|\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}|
. Rewriting the wave equations (
2
) and (
4
) in the forms

(7)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{t^{\prime}}^{2}}-\nabla_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}^{2}\bigg]p_{+}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=s(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

and

(8)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{t^{\prime}}^{2}}-\nabla_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}^{2}\bigg]g(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t-t^{\prime})=\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

where
\bm{x}
and
t
are fixed parameters in a spatio-temporal region
\Lambda=\{\bm{x}\in\nu,\ t\in(t_{0},t_{1})\}
, within which the radiation problem is solved.

Now, multiplying Eq. (
7
) by
g(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})
and Eq. (
8
) by
p_{+}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, then subtracting the modified Eq. (
8
) from the modified Eq. (
7
), yields
[
63
]
:

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\bigg[g\big[\frac{\partial^{2}p_{+}}{\partial{t^{\prime}}^{2}}\big]-\big[\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial{t^{\prime}}^{2}}\big]p_{+}\bigg]-\bigg[g\big[\nabla^{2}p_{+}\big]-\big[\nabla^{2}g\big]\,p_{+}\bigg]=gs-\delta(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}})\delta(t_{\mathfrak{d}})\,p_{+}.

Integrating the left-hand side of Eq. (
9
) over the spatio-temporal solution region
\Lambda
gives

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\!dt^{\prime}\int_{\nu}\!d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\bigg[g\big[\frac{\partial p_{+}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\big]-\big[\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}\big]p_{+}\bigg]-\nabla\cdot\bigg[g\big[\nabla p_{+}\big]-\big[\nabla g\big]\,p_{+}\bigg].

Taking the temporal integral of the first term in (
10
), applying divergence theorem to the second term, and integrating the right-hand side of Eq. (
9
) over
\Lambda
gives
[
63
]
:

(11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{c^{2}}\int_{\nu}d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ \bigg[g\big[\frac{\partial p_{+}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\big]-\big[\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}\big]p_{+}\bigg]\bigg|_{t^{\prime}=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\!dt^{\prime}\int_{\partial\nu}\!dS^{\prime}\ \bigg[g\big[\frac{\partial p_{+}}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}\big]-\big[\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}\big]p_{+}\bigg]=\\
&\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\!dt^{\prime}\int_{\nu}\!d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ gs-\begin{cases}p_{+},&\text{if }\bm{x}\in\nu,t\in[t_{0},t_{1}],\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\end{split}

where
\bm{n}^{\prime}
(or
\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
) is an outward unit normal vector to the surface
\partial\nu
.

2.2.1.
Primary Solution

A primary solution for all space and all time can be obtained by choosing
g
as the causal Green’s function,
g_{+}
, and extending the spatio-temporal set
\Lambda
to infinity. Accordingly, the limits
\nu\to\infty
,
t_{0}\to-\infty
, and
t_{1}\to\infty
are taken.

For the first term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
11
), the assumption of causality ensures that
p_{+}
vanishes at
t=-\infty
, and
g
vanishes at
t=+\infty
. As a result, this term drops out. Furthermore, since a causal Green’s function satisfies
g(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=0
unless
x_{\mathfrak{d}}=ct_{\mathfrak{d}}
, the contribution from the surface
\partial\nu
with an infinite radius vanishes for any finite and arbitrarily large value of
t_{1}

[
63
]
. Consequently, the second term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
11
) also vanishes.
Therefore, the primary solution for
p_{+}
at any pair of
\bm{x}
and
t
lying in the domain
\Lambda
satisfies

(12)

\displaystyle p_{+}(\bm{x},t)=\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\int_{\nu_{s}}d\bm{x}^{\prime}\,g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})s(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}).

The
primary
formula (
12
) expresses the solution wavefield in terms of the radiation source
s
, which is the forcing term on the right-hand side of the wave equation. Given the assumption of infinite extension for the domain
\Lambda
, this solution holds over all space and all time.

2.2.2.
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz Solution

This section describes the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz solution
to the wave equation. The underlying theory originated in optics to model light diffraction through small apertures on a dark screen and was later extended to acoustics to describe the radiation from a vibrating piston in a baffle. While the primary solution in Eq. (
12
) directly maps the forcing term
s
to the wavefield solution
p_{+}
via an integral over the volumetric source
\nu_{s}
, many practical problems quantify the source over an external surface. For such cases, a more applicable formulation can be derived.

Consider a finite volume
\nu
that contains the source volume
\nu_{s}
and is bounded by a closed surface
\partial\nu
. The solution space is confined to the volume
\nu^{\mathcal{C}}
, which lies outside
\partial\nu
. By assuming causality for the solution
p_{+}
in Eq. (
7
) over the time interval
(t_{0},t_{1})
, where
t_{0}\to-\infty
and
t_{1}\to+\infty
, and applying the causality of the Green’s function
g_{+}
in Eq. (
8
), the solution procedure remains identical to that in Section
2.2.1
. However, the integral in Eq. (
11
) is now evaluated over
\nu^{\mathcal{C}}
rather than
\nu
.

As before, the first term on the left-hand side of Eq. (
11
) vanishes due to the causality of
p_{+}
and
g_{+}
, and the contribution from the surface at infinite radius also vanishes, as discussed in Section
2.2.1
. Consequently, the field
p_{+}
in the solution space
\nu^{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies the following integral equation
[
63
]
:

(13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\,\bigg[\big[\frac{\partial g_{+}}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}\big]p_{+}-g_{+}\big[\frac{\partial p_{+}}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}\big]\bigg]=\begin{cases}p_{+}(\bm{x},t)&\bm{x}\in\nu^{\mathcal{C}}\\
0&\bm{x}\in\nu,\end{cases}

where
dS^{\prime}
(or
dS(\bm{x}^{\prime})
) is the elemental area on the surface and
\bm{n}^{\prime}
(or
\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
) is the unit vector normal to the surface
\partial\nu
, directed outward from the interior volume
\nu
to the exterior volume
\nu^{\mathcal{C}}
.

In Eq. (
13
), the formula for the exterior volume is known as the
first Helmholtz identity
. It describes the wavefield
p_{+}
outside the surface
\partial\nu
as a function of the field and its normal derivative on
\partial\nu
. The formula for the interior volume is referred to as the
second Helmholtz identity
, which is a homogeneous integral equation that relates the field to its normal derivative on the surface
\partial\nu
.

Due to the dependence established by the
second Helmholtz identity
, the
first Helmholtz identity
becomes an overdetermined problem unless appropriate boundary conditions are imposed. To ensure a well-posed formulation, the integrand in Eq. (
13
) must depend solely on the wavefield, its normal derivative, or a combination of both. These conditions are satisfied by imposing
Dirichlet
,
Neumann
, or
Robin
boundary conditions, respectively. The application of the first two conditions leads to the
dipole
and
monopole
integral formulas, respectively.

2.2.3.
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld solution

The
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld
integral formula arises from solving the boundary-value problem of the Helmholtz wave equation under the assumption of a source mounted on an infinite planar surface. To derive this formula, the solution space is defined as a half-space bounded by an infinite plane, denoted as
\partial\nu
, and an infinite-radius hemisphere. For simplicity, the bounding plane is taken as
\bm{x}^{d}=0
, and the solution is sought in the half-space
\bm{x}^{d}>0
.

As discussed previously, the associated
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
integral formula is over-determined. To make this problem well-posed, boundary conditions of the
Dirichlet
or
Neumann
form must be imposed on the plane
\partial\nu
. A common method for addressing this boundary-value problem is the
method of images

[
63
]
. Here,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
represent the positions of the general field point and the source point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively, with both assumed to lie in the half-space
\nu^{+}=\{\bm{x}:\bm{x}^{d}>0\}
.

For the source point
\bm{x}^{\prime}=\big[\bm{x}^{\prime 1},...,\bm{x}^{\prime d}\big]^{T}
, a mirror-image source point
\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=\big[\bm{x}^{\prime 1},...,-\bm{x}^{\prime d}\big]^{T}
is introduced, ensuring that
\bm{x}
and
\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime}
lie on opposite sides of the plane. Consequently,
\delta(\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=0
. The Helmholtz equation with an augmented forcing term is then defined as:

(14)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{\prime 2}}-\nabla_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}^{2}\bigg]g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=\big[\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})+\delta(\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\big]\delta(t_{\mathfrak{d}}),

where the Green’s function
g_{+}^{N}
satisfies:

(15)

\displaystyle g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=g_{+}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime},t_{\mathfrak{d}})+g_{+}(\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime},t_{\mathfrak{d}}).

Following the same approach as above, the augmented Green’s function vanishes on the hemisphere with an infinite radius. For a source point
\bm{x}^{\prime}
approaching the plane
\bm{x}^{d}=0
, it follows that
|\bm{x}-\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\prime}|=|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}|
. Consequently, the Green’s function satisfies:

(16)

\displaystyle g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})|_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\partial\nu}=2g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})|_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\partial\nu},\quad\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})|_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\partial\nu}=0.

Substituting
g_{+}^{N}
into the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
formula yields the monopole formula:

(17)

\displaystyle p_{+}^{N}(\bm{x},t)=-a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}p_{+}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big],

where
a_{p}=2
. Here,
\bm{x}\in\nu^{+}
is the solution half-space
\{\bm{x}:\bm{x}^{d}>0\}
, and
\bm{n}^{\prime}
is the unit normal vector to the plane
\bm{x}^{d}=0
, directed into the solution space
\nu^{+}
. The integral is evaluated over the surface
\partial\nu=\{\bm{x}:\bm{x}^{d}=0\}
.

Alternatively, imposing a homogeneous
Dirichlet
boundary condition for the Green’s function on
\partial\nu
results in the dipole formula:

(18)

\displaystyle p_{+}^{D}(\bm{x},t)=a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ \Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}g_{+}^{D}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\Big]p_{+}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),

where
g_{+}^{D}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})|_{\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\partial\nu}=0
and
a_{p}=2
.

Equation (
17
) is known as the
monopole integral formula
and corresponds to the
rigid-baffle
condition, where the normal derivative of the wavefield vanishes on the baffle except at the acoustic aperture. Similarly, Eq. (
18
) is the
dipole integral formula
and corresponds to the
soft-baffle
condition, where the wavefield itself vanishes everywhere except at the acoustic aperture.

3.
Monopole and dipole formulas in terms of actions of the causal Green’s function on the surface source

This section is added to explicitly derive the well-determined Green’s function solutions to the wave equation in terms of the actions of the causal Green’s function.

3.1.
Monopole formula

As previously discussed, the monopole integral formula is derived under the assumption of a vibrating piston mounted on a rigid baffle. This assumption enforces the condition that the normal derivative of the wavefield vanishes on the baffle, except within the aperture. Consequently, employing a causal Green’s function, denoted by
g_{+}^{N}
, which satisfies a homogeneous
Neumann
boundary condition on the surface
\partial\nu
—either enclosing the source or extending as an infinite plane—the resulting surface integral depends solely on the normal pressure derivative, given by

-\frac{\partial p_{+}}{\partial\bm{n}^{\prime}}.

This term characterizes a monopole source. Accordingly, the time-domain formulations of the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
and
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld
integral equations can be expressed as:

(19)

\displaystyle p_{+}^{N}(\bm{x},t)=a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ g_{+}^{N}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\Big[\rho_{0}\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}u^{\bm{n}}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\Big],

where the replacement

(20)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}}p=-\rho_{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u^{\bm{n}},

with
u^{\bm{n}}=\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}
, has been used. Here,
\rho_{0}
is the mass density, and
u^{\bm{n}}
is the normal component of the velocity vector
\bm{u}
over the surface. Additionally, for the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
solution,
\bm{x}\in\nu^{\mathcal{C}}
and
a_{p}=1
, and for the
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld
solution,
\bm{x}\in\nu^{+}
and
a_{p}=2
are used. The former and latter apply to arbitrary-shaped (non-plane) and flat-plane apertures, respectively.

3.2.
Dipole formula

The dipole formula is derived under the assumption of a soft baffle, on which the pressure vanishes everywhere except the front face of the vibrating piston. Correspondingly, using a causal Green’s function
g_{+}^{D}
, which satisfies a homogeneous
Dirichlet
boundary condition over
\partial\nu
, the integral formula depends solely on the wavefield
p_{+}
, representing a dipole source, when it is confined to a surface. For analytic (or ray-based) methods used to approximate the dipole integral formula, it is convenient to reformulate the formula in terms of weighted actions of the causal Green’s function. Substituting the time-domain Green’s function (
6
) into the dipole variants of the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
or
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld
formulas yields:

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{+}^{D}(\bm{x},t)&=a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ \Big[\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}g_{+}^{D}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\Big]p(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\\
&=a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ \Big[\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}}\Big]\ g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\frac{1}{c}\Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{t_{\mathfrak{d}}}\Big]p(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\end{split}

where
\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}=\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}
is the distance vector. Here,
\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}/x_{\mathfrak{d}}
is the
obliquity factor
, which weights the actions of the Green’s function on a surface source, decomposed into
far-field
and
near-field
terms. In the second line of formula (
21
), applying the
far-field approximation
, where
\frac{\partial p}{\partial t^{\prime}}\gg\frac{p}{t_{\mathfrak{d}}}
, which is equivalent to
kx_{\mathfrak{d}}\gg 1
in the frequency domain, and valid in regions sufficiently far from the aperture, allows neglecting the
p/t_{\mathfrak{d}}
term. Thus, the
far-field approximation
of Eq. (
21
) becomes:

(22)

\displaystyle p_{+}^{D}(\bm{x},t)\approx a_{p}\int_{0}^{T_{s}}dt^{\prime}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS^{\prime}\ \Big[\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}}\Big]\ g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\ \bigg[\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}p(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigg].

It should be noted that
a_{p}=1
and
a_{p}=2
are used for the
Kirchhoff-Helmholtz
and
Rayleigh-Sommerfeld
formulas, respectively.

Remark 1
.

Using ray-based methods, it is computationally more efficient to express the integral formula (
21
) in terms of a solid angle element
d\Omega_{\bm{x}}\big(S(\bm{x}^{\prime})\big)
, defined as the angle subtended by an infinitesimal area
dS
corresponding to the point
\bm{x}^{\prime}
on the surface of the aperture, as seen from any arbitrary field point
\bm{x}
. The solid angle relates to the obliquity factor through the formula:

(23)

\displaystyle d\Omega_{\bm{x}}\big(S(\bm{x}^{\prime})\big)=\frac{dS^{\prime}}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}^{2}}\ \Big[\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}}\Big].

In the next section, we demonstrate how a scalar-valued mass source or a vector-valued force source can be defined and incorporated into the equations of continuity and motion. This formulation ensures that the resulting system of wave equations approximates the monopole formula (
19
) and the dipole integral formula (
21
), respectively.

4.
Full-waveform Approximation of the Wave Equation in the Time Domain

This section presents a full-waveform approximation of the acoustic wave equation (
1
). Specifically, it introduces a semi-numerical framework for approximating the time-domain
Primary solution
(
12
) of the wave equation, which directly relates a causal wavefield to a
d
-dimensional radiation source
s
. Furthermore, the semi-numerical system is extended to describe the wavefield in terms of sources confined to a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface, allowing solutions of the
monopole
integral formula (
19
) and the
dipole
integral formula (
21
).

4.1.
Systems of Coupled Wave Equations

This subsection presents a semi-numerical framework for approximating the time-domain
Primary solution
derived in Eq. (
12
). To this end, we first rearrange the wave equation (
1
) by moving the second term on the left-hand side to the right-hand side, resulting in

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}p(\bm{x},t)}{\partial t^{2}}=\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bigg[\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla p(\bm{x},t)\bigg]+s(\bm{x},t),

where
s(\bm{x},t)
represents a finite-volume source that radiates within a finite time and remains square-integrable over the space-time domain.

4.1.1.
System of Wave Equations for solving the Primary formula

The wave equation (
24
) can be reformulated into coupled first-order equations, resulting in the time-stepping system
[
2
]
:

(25)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla p(\bm{x},t),\\
&\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(\bm{x},t)=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)+s_{m}(\bm{x},t),\\
&p(\bm{x},t)=c^{2}\rho(\bm{x},t).\end{split}

where the first two lines are approximated using numerical integration in time. The input to this system is the
mass source

s_{m}
, which is related to the radiation source
s
by:

(26)

\displaystyle s(\bm{x},t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}s_{m}(\bm{x},t)\rightleftarrows s_{m}(\bm{x},t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ s(\bm{x},t^{\prime}).

Here,
s_{m}
has units of
\text{kg m}^{-\text{d}}\text{s}^{-1}
.

4.1.2.
System of Coupled Wave Equations for solving the surface integral formulas

The coupled first-order wave equations (
25
) can be used to describe the wavefield in terms of surface sources through the monopole and dipole integral formulas:

(27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)=-\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\nabla p(\bm{x},t)+\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}(\bm{x},t),\\
&\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(\bm{x},t)=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)+\mathcal{S}_{m}(\bm{x},t),\\
&p(\bm{x},t)=c^{2}\rho(\bm{x},t).\end{split}

In this formulation, the first line is the equation of
motion
with
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
representing a vector-valued force source, while the second line is the equation of
continuity
with
\mathcal{S}_{m}
remaining the scalar-valued mass source.

The following section describes how including a mass source
\mathcal{S}_{m}
in the equation of continuity within the wave equation system (
27
) allows for modeling the monopole integral formula. Furthermore, it explains how incorporating a vector-valued force source
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
in the equation of motion enables the wave equation system to model the dipole integral formula (
21
).

4.2.
Source Approximation

Let
\bm{x}_{s}
be a position in the source region. We begin by partitioning the source domain, which can be either a
d
-dimensional volume
\nu_{s}
or a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface
\partial\nu
(for
d=3
, a bounding surface or a flat plane), into a union of non-overlapping primitive elements. These elements, denoted as
e
, belong to the set
\{1,\dots,N_{e}\}
and are defined by their vertices
j
, where
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{j}\}
, referred to as
nodes
. Accordingly,
l(e)
represents the set of nodes connected to element
e
and contains
N_{l(e)}
members. We assume the field
f
is confined to the surface and varies on
\bm{x}_{j}
through the relation
f(\bm{x}_{j})=N_{j}\,a_{0,j}\,f_{\text{emitter}}
, with
a_{0,j}
being the sensitivity coefficient satisfying
\sum_{j}a_{0,j}=1
, and
f_{\text{emitter}}
a scalar value. (Further details will be given in Section
6.5
.)

For
d=3
, the elements are chosen as tetrahedra for a volumetric source and triangles for a surface source. Similarly, in the case of
d=2
, the volumetric source reduces to a surface source, while the surface source becomes a line source.

To facilitate the approximation, we introduce
\delta_{v}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})\in C^{1}
as a first-order smeared and differentiable approximation of the Dirac delta distribution
\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime})
. The function
\delta_{v}
serves two primary purposes:
1) It provides a smeared delta function in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at
\bm{x}
, evaluated at
\bm{x}_{s}
.
2) It accounts for sampling in the volumetric space at the point
\bm{x}
.

4.2.1.
s_{m}
in terms of a
d
-dimensional radiation source
s

For numerically approximating the time-domain primary formula (
12
), a mass source in terms of the radiation source
s
can be approximated using the formula

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}s_{m}(\bm{x},t)&=\int_{\nu_{s}}d\bm{x}_{s}\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\big[\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ s(\bm{x}_{s},t^{\prime})\big]\ \ \\
&\approx\frac{1}{N_{l(e)}}\ \sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}v_{e}\sum_{j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\nu_{s},j\in l(e)}\delta_{v}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\big[\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ s(\bm{x}_{j},t^{\prime})\big].\end{split}

In Eq. (
28
), for
d=3
, the element
e
is a tetrahedron with
v_{e}
the volume of element
e
, and
N_{l(e)}=d+1
, i.e.,
e
has
d+1
connected nodes.

Solving the system (
27
) with a mass source defined by Eq. (
28
) is equivalent to solving the wave equation:

(29)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\bigg]p(\bm{x},t)=\int_{\nu_{s}}d\bm{x}_{s}\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{S})s(\bm{x}_{s},t).

4.2.2.
\mathcal{S}_{m}
in terms of a monopole source
\rho_{0}u^{\bm{n}}
, defined on a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface

For numerically approximating the monopole integral formula (
19
), a generalized mass source is defined in the form

(30)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{(m,u^{\bm{n}})}(\bm{x},t)&=a_{p}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS(\bm{x}_{s})\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\big[\rho_{0}\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{s},t)\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{s})\big]\ \ \\
&\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\ \sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e)}\delta_{v}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\big[\rho_{0}\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{j},t)\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\big].\end{split}

Accordingly, solving the system (
27
) with a mass source defined by Eq. (
30
) is equivalent to solving the wave equation:

(31)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\bigg]p(\bm{x},t)\approx a_{p}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS(\bm{x}_{s})\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\big[\rho_{0}\frac{\partial\bm{u}(\bm{x}_{s},t)}{\partial t}\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{s})\big].

4.2.3.
\mathcal{S}_{m}
in terms of a dipole source
p
, defined on a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface

As discussed above, by a far-field approximation
kx_{\mathfrak{d}}\gg 1
, the dipole formula (
21
) is reduced to the formula (
22
). In some literature, by an assumption
\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\big[\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}/x_{\mathfrak{d}}\big]=1
, which is equivalent to assuming the acoustic aperture omnidirectional, the formula (
22
) has been approximated using the semi-numerical system (
27
), and a generalized mass source

(32)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{(m,p)}(\bm{x},t)&=a_{p}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS(\bm{x}_{s})\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\ \big[\frac{1}{c}\ p(\bm{x}_{s},t)\big]\ \ \\
&\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\ \sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e)}\delta_{v}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\ \big[\frac{1}{c}\ p(\bm{x}_{j},t)\big].\end{split}

It must be emphasized here that the assumption
\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\big[\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}/x_{\mathfrak{d}}\big]=1
may not hold when
\bm{x}^{\prime}
is confined to a surface.

4.2.4.
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
in terms of a dipole source
p
, defined on a
(d-1)
-dimensional surface

This subsection explains how the system of coupled first-order wave equations (
27
) solves the time-domain dipole integral formula (
21
), which is defined in terms of a dipole source
p
, confined to a surface. Accordingly, a vector-valued force source
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
is defined as:

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}(\bm{x},t)&=a_{p}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS(\bm{x}_{s})\ \delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\ \bigg[\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\ p(\bm{x}_{s},t)\ \bm{n}(\bm{x}_{s})\bigg]\ \ \\
&\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\ \sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e)}\delta_{v}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{j})\ \bigg[\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\ p(\bm{x}_{j},t)\ \bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\bigg],\end{split}

where
\bm{n}(\bm{x}^{\prime})
(or
\bm{n}^{\prime}
) is the unit vector normal to the surface of the source.

Solving the system (
27
) with a force source defined by Eq. (
33
) is equivalent to solving the wave equation:

(34)

\displaystyle\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\nabla^{2}\bigg]p(\bm{x},t)=-a_{p}\ \int_{\partial\nu}dS(\bm{x}_{s})\ \nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{s})\cdot\Big[p(\bm{x}_{s},t)\ \bm{n}(\bm{x}_{s})\Big].

The semi-numerical system (
27
), which employs
\mathcal{S}_{m}=0
and a force source
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{f}
defined by Eq. (
33
), is significant from the perspective of inverse problems. In the next section, we show that the adjoint of the wave equation (
2
) is described by a time-reversed variant of the wave equation (
34
).

5.
Adjoint and Time-reversal Operators

As we analyze the integral formulations associated with finite-sized acoustic emitters, it is equally valuable to derive the adjoint operator under the assumption of finite-sized acoustic receivers, utilizing the integral representations obtained earlier.

5.1.
Forward Operator

In this section, we define the forward operator for which the adjoint operator will be derived. As discussed in Section
2
, we assume that the source is compactly supported in the spatio-temporal region

\Lambda_{s}=\left\{\bm{x}\in\nu_{s}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\ t\in(0,T_{s})\right\}.

It has been shown that if
\nu
is an open set containing
\nu_{s}
and bounded by a closed surface
\partial\nu
, the wavefield can be expressed as an integral formula involving the field and its normal derivative on
\partial\nu
. However, since our focus here is on the reception process, we define a forward operator that maps the volumetric radiation source
s(\bm{x},t)\in\Lambda_{s}
directly to the pressure field measured at the receiver’s surface. To achieve this, we assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open domain bounded by a closed surface
\partial\Omega
. We assume that
\nu_{s}\subset\Omega
. The measured pressure, denoted by
m
, is defined over the surface
\partial\Omega
for the time interval
(0,T)
.

The forward operator, which maps a volumetric source
s
to the pressure field detected by the receiver, is defined as

(35)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{A}:C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Omega\times(0,T)\big)\rightarrow C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big),\\
&\mathcal{A}[s](\bm{x}_{r},t)=\mathcal{R}\big[w_{\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)}(\bm{x},t)\,p(\bm{x},t)\big],\end{split}

where the forward operator is expressed as the composition
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{P}
, with
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}
denoting the
wave propagation
and
measurement
operators, respectively, as introduced below.

5.1.1.
Wave Propagation Operator
\mathcal{P}

We define the wave propagation operator under the assumption that the wavefield
p(\bm{x},t)
satisfies the governing equation (
12
) in free space. The operator
\mathcal{P}
is given by

(36)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}:C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Omega\times(0,T)\big)\rightarrow C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)\\
&\mathcal{P}[s](\bm{x},t)=w_{\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)}(\bm{x},t)\ p(\bm{x},t),\end{split}

where
p(\bm{x},t)\in\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big)
is the wavefield in free space satisfying Eq. (
12
), and
w_{\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)}(\bm{x},t)
is a window function that restricts the pressure field in free space to the spatio-temporal region accessible to the receivers,
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here,
\Gamma
represents the spatial region where the wavefield is observable by the surface
\partial\Omega
. By this definition,
\partial\Omega
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional hypersurface contained in
\Gamma
, meaning that
\partial\Omega\subset\Gamma
.

5.1.2.
Measurement operator
\mathcal{R}

The measurement operator
\mathcal{R}
maps the wavefield in the region
\Gamma
onto the hypersurface
\partial\Omega
contained in it. It is defined as

(37)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}:C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)\rightarrow C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big),\\
&\mathcal{R}[p](\bm{x}_{r},t)=\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{r})\cdot\int_{\Gamma}\delta_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{x}_{r}-\bm{x})\ \nabla p(\bm{x},t)\,d\bm{x},\end{split}

where
n(\bm{x}_{r})
is the unit outward normal to the surface
\partial\Omega
at the point
\bm{x}_{r}
, and
\delta_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{x}_{r}-\bm{x})
represents the Dirac delta distribution confined to the surface
\partial\Omega
.

5.2.
Adjoint Operator

Having defined the forward operator, we now proceed to derive its adjoint.

Lemma 1
.

The adjoint of the wave propagation operator
\mathcal{P}
, defined by Eqs. (
12
) and (
36
), is given by

(38)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{P}^{*}:C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)\rightarrow C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big),\\
&\mathcal{P}^{*}[h](\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\ g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},T-t^{\prime}-t)\ h(\bm{x},T-t),\end{split}

Proof.
Using the fact that the primary formula (
12
) holds for all space and time, we extend the source domain to ensure that the adjoint operator is well-defined.

The adjoint operator
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, with respect to the standard
L^{2}
bilinear form in
C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)
and
C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big)
, must satisfy:

(39)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\ h(\bm{x},t)\ w_{\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)}(\bm{x},t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})s(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ s(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\ \mathcal{P}^{*}[h](\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

for any
h\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)
and
s\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big)
. We start with rearranging the left-hand-side of this equality to the form

(40)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}dt^{\prime}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}d\bm{x}^{\prime}\ s(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})\bigg[\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\ g_{-}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\ \ h(\bm{x},t)\bigg],

where we have used the anti-causal Green’s function satisfying

(41)

\displaystyle g_{-}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=g_{+}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},-t_{\mathfrak{d}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\frac{\delta(t_{\mathfrak{d}}+\frac{x_{\mathfrak{d}}}{c})}{x_{\mathfrak{d}}},

and the reciprocity of the Green’s function

(42)

\displaystyle g(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})=g(-\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}}).

Now, in Eq. (
40
), by applying the changes of variables
t_{\mathfrak{d}}\rightarrow t_{\mathfrak{d}}+T-T
and
t\rightarrow T-t
, and considering the right-hand side of Eq. (
39
), we obtain the adjoint operator
\mathcal{P}^{*}
as defined in Eq. (
38
).

Lemma 2
.

The adjoint of the measurement operator
\mathcal{R}
, defined by Eq. (
37
), is given by

(43)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}^{*}:C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big)\rightarrow C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big),\\
&\mathcal{R}^{*}[m](\bm{x},t)=-\int_{\partial\Omega}dS(\bm{x}_{r})\ \nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{r})\cdot\Big[m(\bm{x}_{r},t)\bm{n}_{-}(\bm{x}_{r})\Big],\end{split}

where
\bm{n}_{-}(\bm{x}_{r})=-\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{r})
is the unit inward normal to the surface
\partial\Omega
at point
\bm{x}_{r}
.

Proof.
The adjoint operator
\mathcal{R}^{*}
with respect to the standard
L2
Bilinear form in
C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big)
and
\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)
has to satisfy

(44)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\partial\Omega}dS(\bm{x}_{r})\ m(\bm{x}_{r},t)\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{r})\cdot\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\ \delta_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{x}_{r}-\bm{x})\nabla p(\bm{x},t)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\ \mathcal{M}^{*}[m](\bm{x}_{r},t)\ p(\bm{x},t)

for any
m\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big)
and
p\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\Gamma\times(0,T)\big)
. On the left-hand side of this equality, performing an integration by parts in the domain
\Gamma
and rearranging the terms yield

(45)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}\bigg[-\int_{\partial\Omega}dS(\bm{x}_{r})\ \nabla\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{r})\cdot\Big[m(\bm{x}_{r},t)\bm{n}_{-}(\bm{x}_{r})\Big]\bigg]\ p(\bm{x},t)

Considering the right-hand side of Eq. (
44
), Eq. (
45
) yields
\mathcal{R}^{*}[g]
, as defined in Eq. (
43
).

Lemma 3
.

The action of the adjoint operator
\mathcal{A}^{*}=\mathcal{P}^{*}\ \mathcal{R}^{*}
on any
m\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big)
is given by:

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\mathcal{A}}^{*}[m](\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime},T-t^{\prime}),

where
p^{*}
is the free-space solution to the adjoint wave equation:

(47)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\bigg[\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{\prime 2}}-\nabla_{\bm{x}^{\prime}}^{2}\bigg]p^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=-\int_{\partial\Omega}dS(\bm{x}_{r})\ \nabla\delta(\bm{x}^{\prime}-\bm{x}_{r})\cdot\Big[m(\bm{x}_{r},T-t^{\prime})\bm{n}_{-}(\bm{x}_{r})\Big]\quad\\
\end{split}

for any

(48)

\displaystyle p^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}=0}=0,\ \ \ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}p^{*}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}=0}=0,\quad\text{in}\quad\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Proof.
The proof is obvious from Lemmas 1 and 2.

From Eq. (

Note that, in contrast to our defined forward operator

(49)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}[m]=\chi_{\big(\Omega\times(0,T)\big)}\mathcal{A}^{*}[m],

5.3.
Time-Reversal Operator

As shown in Section
2
, for a source contained within a region
\nu
, which is bounded by a closed surface
\partial\nu
, the wavefield
p_{+}(\bm{x},t)
satisfies the homogeneous wave equation in the region exterior to
\partial\nu
. In this exterior region, the field can be expressed as the solution to an exterior boundary-value problem for the homogeneous wave equation, given by an integral representation evaluated on the surface
\partial\nu
.

For times
t
greater than the turn-off time
T_{s}
of the source, the wavefield within the interior region
\Omega
also satisfies the homogeneous wave equation and can be represented as the solution to an interior boundary-value problem in terms of an integral formula evaluated on the surface
\partial\Omega
.

Following the same approach as in Section
2.2
, and taking the limits
t_{0}\to-\infty
and
t_{1}\to\infty
, we obtain the following
interior-field
integral equation
[
63
]
:

(50)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int_{\partial\Omega}dS\,\bigg[g_{-}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}}m(\bm{x},t)-\frac{\partial g_{-}}{\partial\bm{n}}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},t_{\mathfrak{d}})m(\bm{x},t)\bigg]=\begin{cases}p_{+}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime}),&\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega,\\
0,&\bm{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{\mathcal{C}},\end{cases}

where we have used the anti-causal Green’s function, as defined in (
41
).

The integral formula (
50
) holds for times
t>T_{s}
, where the source term
s
vanishes in
\Omega
. Additionally, the exterior region is given by
\Omega^{\mathcal{C}}=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega
,
\bm{n}(\bm{x})
denotes the unit outward normal to the surface
\partial\Omega
, and
dS(\bm{x})
represents the infinitesimal surface element at position
\bm{x}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Now, by imposing a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
, applying the change of variables
t_{\mathfrak{d}}\rightarrow t_{\mathfrak{d}}+T-T
and
t\rightarrow T-t
, and replacing
\bm{n}
with
-\bm{n}_{-}
, where
\bm{n}_{-}
is the unit inward normal to
\partial\Omega
, the action of time-reversal operator

\mathcal{A}^{T}
on any
m\in C_{0}^{\infty}\big(\partial\Omega\times(0,T)\big)
is
given by:

(51)

\displaystyle A^{T}[m](\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=p^{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime},T-t^{\prime}),

where
p^{T}
satisfies

(52)

\displaystyle p^{T}(\bm{x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{\partial\Omega}dS\ \left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{n}_{-}}g_{+}^{D}(\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}},T-t^{\prime}-t)\right]m(\bm{x},T-t),

which is a time-reversed variant of the interior-field dipole integral formula evaulated on the surface
\partial\Omega

46

represents the

denotes the time-reversed wavefield. Similar to the adjoint operator, the time-reversed wavefield is restricted to the region

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{T}[m]=\chi_{\big(\Omega\times(0,T)\big)}\mathcal{A}^{T}[m].

The only difference between the time-reversal operator and the adjoint operator is the application of the Dirichlet boundary condition

6.2.
Band-limited Dirac Delta Distribution

A Dirac delta distribution in the spatial domain is equivalent to 1 for all frequencies in the spatial-frequency domain. However, full-field approximation of the acoustic wave equation is done on sampled positions, which support a finite range of frequencies. Accordingly, a Dirac delta distribution,
\delta
, can be replaced by a scaled band-limited Dirac delta function,
\delta_{v}
, whose approximation on a
d
-dimensional discretised grid and using a
Fourier
method gives

(54)

\displaystyle\delta_{v}(\bm{X}-\bm{x})\approx\frac{1}{v_{\bm{X}}}\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}),

where
v_{\bm{X}}=\prod_{\zeta=1}^{d}\Delta x^{\zeta}
is the volume of each voxel in the computaional grid, and the band-limited Dirac delta distribution is given by

(55)

\displaystyle\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x})\approx\prod_{\zeta=1}^{d}\frac{\sin\Big(\frac{\pi(X^{\zeta}-x^{\zeta})}{\Delta x^{\zeta}}\Big)}{N_{n}^{\zeta}\sin\Big(\frac{\pi(X^{\zeta}-x^{\zeta})}{N_{n}^{\zeta}\ \Delta x^{\zeta}}\Big)}.

Here,
\prod_{\zeta=1}^{d}
denotes the product of terms associated with the Cartesian coordinates. Approximating the
\delta_{b}
function using the formula (
55
) is computationally expensive. For sufficiently large
N^{\zeta}
, the band-limited Dirac delta function can be approximated more efficiently through the formula

(56)

\displaystyle\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x})\approx\prod_{\zeta=1}^{d}\ \text{sinc}\big(\frac{\pi(X^{\zeta}-x^{\zeta})}{\Delta x^{\zeta}}\big),

and then neglecting the magnitudes smaller than a threshold
0<\varepsilon<0.1
.

6.3.
Discretized Mass and Force Sources

This section outlines the discretization of the mass and force sources, following the triangulation procedure introduced in Section
4.2
. For a source defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
), the volume (or area) of each element
e
is denoted by
v_{e}
(or

).

Substituting the band-limited Dirac delta function (
56

(57)

\displaystyle\bar{s}_{m}(\bm{X},\mathfrak{t})\approx\frac{\Delta t}{N_{l(e)}}\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}v_{e}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\nu_{s},j\in l(e),\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})>\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{v}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})\sum_{\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}=1}^{\mathfrak{t}}s(\bm{x}_{j},\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}),

where
\varepsilon
is a scalar value ranging between 0 and 1, balancing accuracy and computational cost.

Similarly, the full discretization of the formulas (
30
) and (
32
) yields:

(58)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{(m,u^{\bm{n}})}(\bm{X},\mathfrak{t})\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e),\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})>\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{v}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})\left[\rho_{0}u^{\bm{n}}(\bm{x}_{j},\mathfrak{t})\right],

and

(59)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}_{(m,p)}(\bm{X},\mathfrak{t})\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e),\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})>\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{v}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})\left[\frac{p(\bm{x}_{j},\mathfrak{t})}{c}\right].

Furthermore, the full discretization of the force source formula (
33

(60)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mathcal{S}}}_{f}(\bm{X},\mathfrak{t})\approx\frac{a_{p}}{N_{l(e)}}\sum_{e=1}^{N_{e}}s_{e}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j|\bm{x}_{j}\in\partial\nu,j\in l(e),\delta_{b}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})>\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{v}(\bm{X}-\bm{x}_{j})\left[\frac{p(\bm{x}_{j},\mathfrak{t})}{\rho_{0}}\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\right].

Note that using Eq. (

(far-field) and

On the other hand, Algorithm

•

•

6.5.
Discretization of the Measurement Operator

The measurement operator can be approximated as follows. First, the measured data
m(\bm{x}_{r},t)
in the continuous domain
\partial\Omega
is mapped onto disjoint subsets of
\partial\Omega
, referred to as
receivers
. Similar to Section
4.2
, each receiver is subdivided into a union of non-overlapping primitive shapes (triangular elements)
e\in\{1,\dots,N_{e}\}
, with vertices
j\in\{1,\dots,N_{j}\}
, referred to as
nodes
.

We focus on a single receiver. The measured data at node
j
can then be approximated using the formula:

(61)

\displaystyle m(\bm{x}_{j},t)\approx\bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\cdot\sum_{i\mid\delta_{b}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})>\varepsilon}\frac{v_{i}}{s_{j}}\delta_{b}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})\nabla\bar{p}(\bm{X}_{i},t),

where
m(\bm{x}_{j},t)
represents the pressure detected at node
j
over time, and
v_{i}
is the volume associated with grid point
i
on the computational grid. As mentioned earlier, for a regular grid, we set
v_{\bm{X}}=v_{i}
for all
i
, where
v_{i}
represents the volume of each voxel in the computational domain. Additionally,
s_{j}
is the surface area associated with node
j
on the receiver, and
0<\varepsilon<0.1
is a parameter balancing accuracy and computational cost.

The pressure detected by each receiver is then computed using the apodization formula:

(62)

\displaystyle m_{\text{receiver}}(t)=\sum_{j}a_{j}\,m(\bm{x}_{j},t),

where
a_{j}
is the apodization (sensitivity) coefficient corresponding to node
j
on the receiver’s surface, given by

(63)

\displaystyle a_{j}=\frac{s_{j}}{s_{\text{receiver}}}a_{0,j},

where
a_{0,j}
is the sensitivity coefficient associated with node
j
, satisfying
\sum_{j}a_{0,j}=1
, and
s_{\text{receiver}}
denotes the total surface area of the receiver.

The pressure detected by each receiver is then approximated by

(64)

\displaystyle m_{\text{receiver}}(t)\approx\frac{v_{\bm{X}}}{s_{\text{receiver}}}\sum_{j}a_{0,j}\ \bm{n}(\bm{x}_{j})\cdot\sum_{i\mid\delta_{b}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})>\varepsilon}\delta_{b}(\bm{x}_{j}-\bm{X}_{i})\ \nabla\bar{p}(\bm{X}_{i},t).

This formulation has a clear physical interpretation. For each receiver, it approximates the net force applied to the receiver’s surface by integrating the normal pressure gradient over the region accessible to the receiver. The resulting net force is then normalized by the receiver’s surface area and averaged through the sensitivity coefficients
a_{0,j}
, yielding an approximation of the pressure exerted on the receiver’s surface.

8.
Discussion and Conclusion

Approaches for solving the acoustic wave equation can be broadly categorized into analytic and full-waveform methods. Analytic methods are suitable for homogeneous or weakly heterogeneous (smoothly varying) media, as they can account for refraction effects and singly scattered waves
[
65
,
66
,
69
,
70
]
. In contrast, full-waveform approaches are well-suited for handling complex heterogeneities, sharp transitions, and higher-order scattering phenomena in acoustic media
[
3
]
.

In some Geophysical applications, acoustic waves propagate with wavelengths significantly larger than the size of the acoustic aperture. For such cases, transducers are often approximated as single points
[
11
]
. However, this assumption is not applicable to many biomedical applications where high frequencies are utilized
[
61
,
62
,
71
]
. Therefore, to achieve accurate approximations of acoustic waves using full-waveform approaches, particularly in terms of amplitude, it is essential to account for the finite-size effects of acoustic apertures.

This study explored the equivalence between analytic and full-waveform approaches. Specifically, we demonstrated how isotropic mass sources and vector-valued force sources should be defined, discretized, and incorporated into the full-waveform approach (Algorithm
1
) to ensure solutions align with their associated analytic formulas.

To achieve this, an equivalence was first established between the analytic primary formula (
12
), defined in terms of a volumetric radiation source
s
, and its equivalent full-waveform approximation. It was demonstrated that the full-waveform approach can be represented as an integral of the causal Green’s function acting on the radiation source over its volumetric and temporal domain. This equivalence was numerically validated in Section
7.1
.

The established equivalence between the analytic and full-waveform approaches for modeling the primary formula was then extended to the monopole integral formula (
19
). This formula describes acoustic waves in terms of a monopole source,
-\frac{\partial p}{\partial\bm{n}}=\rho_{0}\frac{\partial u^{\bm{n}}}{\partial t}
, confined to a finite-sized surface and directed outwardly normal to the surface.

In addition, the dipole integral formula (
21
) is equivalent to an integral of the normal derivatives of the causal Green’s function acting on a dipole source,
p
, confined to a surface. This integral formula can be reformulated into the second line in Eq. (
21
), which represents the wavefield as an integral of the obliquity-corrected Green’s function acting on a source split into far-field and near-field components.

The far-field formula (
22
) is a simplified version of Eq. (
21
), where the near-field source term,
p/\big[ct_{\mathfrak{d}}\big]
, has been neglected. It was shown that a full-waveform approximation of the far-field dipole formula (
22
), using Algorithm
1
and a discretized mass source defined by Eq. (
59
), implicitly enforces an omnidirectionality assumption,
\bm{n}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{x}_{\mathfrak{d}}/x_{\mathfrak{d}}=1
. This assumption does not hold for finite-sized apertures, leading to significant errors in the approximated wavefield (Figure
7
) when compared to the analytic solution of the dipole formula (
21
) (Figure
7
).

In contrast, a full-waveform approximation of the dipole integral formula (
21
), implemented using Algorithm
1
with a discretized force source defined by Eq. (
60
), yielded a wavefield solution (Figure
7
) that closely matches the analytic solution obtained using the
Field II
toolbox
[
65
,
66
]
. This agreement is further corroborated by the pressure profile approximated over time at receiver 10, as shown in Figure
8
.

From the perspective of inverse problems, the observed agreement between the full-waveform approximation of the dipole integral formula (
21
) and its analytic solution is highly significant. This consistency validates approximating the time-reversal analytic formula derived in Section
5.3
—a time-reversed interior-field variant of the dipole integral formula—using a full-waveform approach that employs a time-reversed variant of Algorithm
1
in conjunction with a force source defined by a time-reversed variant of Eq. (
60
). Furthermore, it was demonstrated that the derived time-reversal formula (Eq. (
52
)) is equivalent to the adjoint wave equation, as established in Section
5.2
.

In the time-reversal formula (
52
),
p
represents the pressure data measured over time on a boundary surface or the residual function when used in iterative frameworks, such as error minimization algorithms
[
35
,
36
,
38
]
or
Neumann series
iterations
[
32
,
37
]
. The next step is to conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the derived full-waveform approximation of the adjoint wave equation, accounting for the finite size of receivers in practical settings. This operator, which incorporates the analytic angular dependence of the receivers, will be compared to other established time-reversal and adjoint operators in the literature, with a particular focus on its integration within adjoint-assisted optimization methods or
Neumann-series
framework for solving inverse problems in biomedical acoustics.

To better understand the practical impact of our study, consider that an analytic approximation of an interior-field variant of the dipole integral formula (Eq. (
21
)) has been widely used as the back-projection step in photoacoustic tomography for acoustically homogeneous media
[
61
,
62
]
. Our study extends this approach to heterogeneous media by analytically deriving and numerically validating its equivalent full-waveform approximation.
