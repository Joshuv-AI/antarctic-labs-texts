Control theory and splitting methods
Karine Beauchard, Adrien Busnot Laurent, Frédéric Marbach

Abstract
Our goal is to highlight some deep connections between numerical splitting methods and control theory. We consider evolution equations of the form x ˙ = f 0 ​ ( x ) + f 1 ​ ( x ) \dot{x}=f_{0}(x)+f_{1}(x) , where f 0 f_{0} encodes non-reversible dynamics, motivating schemes that involve only forward flows of f 0 f_{0} . In this context, a splitting method can be interpreted as a trajectory of the control-affine system x ˙ ​ ( t ) = f 0 ​ ( x ⁡ ( t ) ) + u ⁡ ( t ) ​ f 1 ​ ( x ⁡ ( t ) ) \dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+u(t)f_{1}(x(t)) , associated with a control u u that is a finite sum of Dirac masses. The goal is then to find a control such that the flow generated by f 0 + u ⁡ ( t ) ​ f 1 f_{0}+u(t)f_{1} is as close as possible to the flow of f 0 + f 1 f_{0}+f_{1} . Using this interpretation and classical tools from control theory, we revisit well-known results on numerical splitting methods and prove several new ones. First, we show that there exist numerical schemes of arbitrary order involving only forward flows of f 0 f_{0} , provided one allows complex coefficients for f 1 f_{1} . Equivalently, for complex-valued controls, we prove that the Lie algebra rank condition is equivalent to small-time local controllability. Second, for real-valued coefficients, we show that the well-known order restrictions are linked to so-called “bad” Lie brackets from control theory, which are known to obstruct small-time local controllability. We investigate the conditions under which high-order methods exist, thanks to a basis of the free Lie algebra that we recently constructed.

Introduction

In this article, we highlight some deep connections between numerical splitting methods and control theory and use this perspective to provide new proofs of known results and conjectures in the order theory of splitting methods.
We focus on situations where
f_{0}
encodes heuristically non-reversible dynamics, so that one is interested in schemes only involving forward flows of
f_{0}
.

1.1
Order theory for splitting methods

Splitting methods

Splitting methods aim to solve numerically evolution equations of the form

\dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+f_{1}(x(t))+\dots+f_{p}(x(t)),

(1.1)

where the flows associated with the vector fields
f_{j}
are easy to integrate numerically with high precision or an exact solution is available.
For simplicity, we restrict to the case
p=1
.

For a given time
T
, a splitting method approximates
x(T)
by composing flows associated with the
f_{j}
.
A standard splitting method is of order
N
for (
1.1
) if for all smooth vector fields
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
, the following estimate holds

x(T)=e^{\alpha_{1}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}Tf_{1}}\dots e^{\alpha_{k}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{k}Tf_{1}}x(0)+\underset{T\to 0}{O}(T^{N+1}),

(1.2)

where
e^{\alpha_{j}Tf_{j}}
denotes the flow at time
\alpha_{j}T
associated with the vector field
f_{j}
.

For instance, the Lie–Trotter splitting is of order one:

x(T)=e^{Tf_{0}}e^{Tf_{1}}x(0)+\underset{T\to 0}{O}(T^{2}),

(1.3)

and the Strang splitting is of order two:

x(T)=e^{T/2f_{0}}e^{Tf_{1}}e^{T/2f_{0}}x(0)+\underset{T\to 0}{O}(T^{3}).

(1.4)

Splitting methods are popular for their straightforward implementation, versatility, accuracy, and stability.
They also exhibit good geometric properties
[
9
,
26
,
39
]
, for instance preserving energy, volume, symmetries, or symplecticity.
They are widely used for the numerical approximation of ODEs and PDEs
[
10
,
22
,
36
]
, or in stochastic settings, for instance in molecular dynamics
[
12
,
34
]
.

Order theory

In this paper, we focus on the construction of high-order splittings (and not on the stability analysis or the preservation of geometric properties).
The order theory of splitting methods relies on the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula or the Magnus formula
[
37
]
.
In particular, methods of arbitrarily high order can be constructed, for instance with composition methods.
The derivation of the order conditions can be found in
[
39
]
.
The modern formulation is based on the algebraic framework of free Lie algebras
[
46
]
or Hopf algebras (see the word series
[
40
,
41
]
and the review
[
9
, Sec. 2]
).
Free Lie algebras are also used in control theory
[
3
,
52
]
.

Order theory for semigroups

The order theory becomes more involved when positivity constraints are imposed on the coefficients.
We study the existence of splitting schemes with coefficients in
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
, that is, when
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{A}
,
\beta_{i}\in\mathbb{B}
for all
i
.
For non-reversible problems (such as the heat equation or stochastic equations), one must impose a condition of the form
\alpha_{i}>0
on the coefficients in (
1.2
).
In this context, it is known
[
10
,
25
,
48
,
53
]
that the maximum order for splitting methods with coefficients in
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
(sometimes called forward splitting methods) is
N=2
.

To go further, a possible solution is to use complex coefficients.
Complex coefficients first appeared in the context of Hamiltonian systems
[
13
]
and quantum mechanics
[
2
,
43
]
with low order, then in
[
11
,
29
]
for parabolic problems, in the spirit of
[
28
]
.
The large number of complex solutions for the order conditions offers more flexibility in the choice of coefficients, and can lead to schemes with smaller truncation errors and new symmetries.
On the other hand, the use of complex arithmetic incurs an additional cost when solving real-valued problems, and extending the flows to the complex plane must be handled carefully to avoid order reduction.

The papers
[
11
,
29
]
use symmetric composition methods to create splitting methods in
(\mathbb{C}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{+})
up to order 14 (i.e. where all coefficients are complex numbers with positive real part), though the error constants deteriorate in some cases
[
7
]
.
In
[
7
]
, it is proved that splitting methods in
(\mathbb{C}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{+})
exist up to order 44, by building upon a splitting method of order 6 in
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{+})
.
In this paper, we prove in particular that splittings in
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
exist up to any order, thereby
positively answering the open question
raised in
[
7
, Remark 2.7]
(with unconstrained complex
\beta_{i}
).

Commutator flows and degeneracies

An alternative solution to go beyond the order barrier is to introduce flows associated to specific commutators in the splitting methods, assuming that these flows are explicitly available.
The first example in the literature is the Takahashi–Imada splitting
[
54
]
(see also
[
19
,
33
,
47
]
).
In the context of Hamiltonian dynamics, such splittings are known as
splitting methods with modified potentials

[
35
,
47
,
58
]
.
We refer to
[
9
, Sections 3.2 and 8]
for a survey of splittings with commutators in the literature.

One must distinguish two concepts (see
Section

8.3
).
First, one can look for general splitting methods where one allows the use of commutator flows.
Second, one can exploit the degeneracies of specific systems for which some commutators vanish.
Motivated by
[
10
]
, we provide new existence results for schemes in
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
with commutators and necessary degeneracy conditions to obtain high order schemes.
In particular, we
prove the conjecture
of
[
18
, Section V]
.

Link with control theory

The link between splitting methods with
\mathbb{A}=\mathbb{R}^{+}
and control theory is the following.
A splitting method can be seen as a trajectory of the control system

\dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+u(t)f_{1}(x(t))

(1.5)

associated with a control
u
that is a sum of Dirac masses.
Here,
\alpha_{j}
denotes the duration of the
j
-th step and
\beta_{j}
the amplitude of the corresponding jump.
The goal is that the flow of the time-dependent vector field
f_{0}+u(t)f_{1}
on
[0,T]
approximates
e^{T(f_{0}+f_{1})}
for some
T>0
.

With this in mind, we revisit known results on numerical splitting methods, and prove new ones.
In particular, we identify Lie brackets that are obstructions to both high order numerical splitting methods and small-time local controllability.

1.2
Definitions and notations

1.2.1
Formal brackets and evaluated Lie brackets

Definition 1.1

(Formal brackets)
.

Let
X=\{X_{0},X_{1}\}
be a set of two indeterminates.
We denote by
\operatorname{Br}(X)
the
free magma
over
X
, which can be defined by induction:
X\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)
and, if
a,b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
, then the ordered pair
(a,b)
belongs to
\operatorname{Br}(X)
.
For
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
, let
n_{0}(b)
and
n_{1}(b)
be the number of
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
appearing in
b
.
The length of
b
, denoted
|b|
, thus satisfies
|b|=n_{0}(b)+n_{1}(b)
.

Definition 1.2
.

We define the formal brackets
M_{0}:=X_{1}
, and for
j\geq 1
,

M_{j}:=(M_{j-1},X_{0})\quad\text{and}\quad W_{j}:=(M_{j-1},M_{j}).

(1.6)

The brackets
M_{j}
contain
X_{1}
only once and satisfy
|M_{j}|=j+1
.
The brackets
W_{j}
contain
X_{1}
twice and satisfy
|W_{j}|=2j+1
.
For instance
M_{1}=(X_{1},X_{0})
and
W_{1}=(X_{1},(X_{1},X_{0}))
.

Definition 1.3

(Lie bracket of vector fields)
.

For smooth vector fields
f,g
, denote by
[f,g]=\operatorname{ad}_{f}(g)
their Lie bracket which is the smooth vector field defined by
[f,g](x)=Dg(x)f(x)-Df(x)g(x)
.
We also use the notation
\operatorname{ad}_{f}^{n+1}(g)=[f,\operatorname{ad}_{f}^{n}(g)]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 1.4

(Evaluated Lie bracket)
.

If
f_{0},f_{1}
are smooth vector fields and
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
, we denote by
f_{b}
the vector field obtained by replacing each
X_{j}
with
f_{j}
in
b
.
For instance, with the notations above,
f_{M_{0}}=f_{X_{1}}=f_{1}
,
f_{M_{1}}=[f_{1},f_{0}]
and
f_{W_{1}}=[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{0}]]=\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{2}(f_{0})
.

1.2.2
Splitting methods

In the sequel, we rely on the following definition of the order of a splitting method, which reflects the asymmetry between coefficients allowed along
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.

Definition 1.5

(Order of a splitting method with
(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B})
coefficients)
.

Let
\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B}\subset\mathbb{C}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
We say that
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k})\in\mathbb{A}^{k}
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})\in\mathbb{B}^{k}
with
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
is a
splitting method of order (at least)
N
when, for all
f_{0},f_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{K}^{d};\mathbb{K}^{d})
(with
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
),

e^{T(f_{0}+f_{1})}=e^{\alpha_{1}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}Tf_{1}}\dotsb e^{\alpha_{k}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{k}Tf_{1}}+\underset{T\to 0}{O}\left(T^{N+1}\right)

(1.7)

in the following sense: for all
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
, there exists
C=C(f_{0},f_{1},x_{0})
such that, for
T
small enough,

\left|e^{T(f_{0}+f_{1})}x_{0}-e^{\alpha_{1}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}Tf_{1}}\dotsb e^{\alpha_{k}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{k}Tf_{1}}x_{0}\right|\leq CT^{N+1}.

(1.8)

When complex coefficients appear, we implicitly assume that the vector fields are holomorphic, so that their flows are well defined for sufficiently small complex times (see also
Section

5.1
).

In
Definition

1.5
, the parameters
k,\alpha,\beta
must be independent of
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
, and
T
.
A weaker notion consists in requiring (
1.7
) only for a subclass of pairs of vector fields, possibly satisfying additional structural relations, enabling higher-order methods.

Definition 1.6

(Splitting method relative to a subclass)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a set of pairs of vector fields.
We say that a splitting method is
of order
N
relative to
\mathcal{P}
when (
1.7
) holds for any
(f_{0},f_{1})\in\mathcal{P}
.

The splitting methods of
Definition

1.5
only involve flows of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.
For some systems, although the flow of
f_{0}+f_{1}
is not directly available, one may also have access to the flows of some specific commutators of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
(for example, the flow of
f_{W_{1}}=[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{0}]]
, and more generally the flow of
f_{b}
for some
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
).
This leads to the following definition where we allow a set
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
of “enabled” brackets.

Definition 1.7

(Splitting method with commutator flows)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
.
With the notations of
Definition

1.5
, a
splitting method of order
N
involving
X_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}
is specified by additional data
b_{1},\dotsc,b_{k}\in\mathcal{E}
such that

e^{T(f_{0}+f_{1})}=e^{\alpha_{1}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{1}T^{|b_{1}|}f_{b_{1}}}e^{\alpha_{2}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{2}T^{|b_{2}|}f_{b_{2}}}\dotsb e^{\alpha_{k}Tf_{0}}e^{\beta_{k}T^{|b_{k}|}f_{b_{k}}}+\underset{T\to 0}{O}\left(T^{N+1}\right).

(1.9)

Setting
\alpha_{j}=0
allows one to concatenate multiple flows associated with different enabled brackets.

1.2.3
Controllability

Given smooth vector fields
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}
defined in a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
, we consider the control-affine system

\dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+u_{1}(t)f_{1}(x(t))+\dotsb+u_{m}(t)f_{m}(x(t)),

(1.10)

where
u_{j}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K})
are the control inputs.
When there is no control in front of
f_{0}\neq 0
, such a system is called “with drift” and one typically assumes that
f_{0}(0)=0
and studies its behavior near the equilibrium
(x,u)=(0,0)
.
When there is a control
u_{0}(t)
in front of
f_{0}
(or equivalently when
f_{0}=0
), the system is called “driftless” and
(x,u)=(0,0)
is still an equilibrium.

Among the many notions of controllability (see
[
6
, Section 1.2]
for further discussion), we use:

Definition 1.8

(Small-state STLC)
.

We say that (
1.10
) is
small-state small-time locally controllable
at
0
when, for every
T>0
, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
r>0
such that, for every target state
x^{*}\in B(0,r)
, there exists
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K}^{m})
such that the solution to (
1.10
) associated with the initial condition
x(0)=0
and the control
u
satisfies
x(T)=x^{*}
and
x([0,T])\subset B(0,\delta)
.

1.3
Main results

1.3.1
Arbitrary order
(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
splitting methods

It is well-known that splitting methods with
(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
coefficients of arbitrary order exist.
In our notation, one has the following classical result.

Theorem 1.9
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists an
(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order
N
.

Without sign constraints on the real coefficients, an
(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
splitting method corresponds to a trajectory of the driftless control-affine system

\dot{x}(t)=u_{0}(t)f_{0}(x(t))+u_{1}(t)f_{1}(x(t))

(1.11)

with state
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and control
(u_{0},u_{1}):\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
are piecewise constant functions with disjoint supports and do not depend on
(f_{0},f_{1})
.

In control theory, the analogue of
Theorem

1.9
is the following result, known as the Chow–Rashevskii necessary and sufficient condition for the controllability of driftless control-affine systems
[
20
,
44
]
, involving the “Lie algebra rank condition”
\operatorname{Lie}_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{0},f_{1})(0)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\operatorname{Lie}_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{0},f_{1})
denotes the Lie algebra spanned over
\mathbb{R}
by
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
(see
[
21
, Theorems 3.17 and 3.19]
).

Theorem 1.10
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be real-analytic vector fields on a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
System (
1.11
) is small-state STLC with real-valued controls
u_{0},u_{1}
if and only if
\operatorname{Lie}_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{0},f_{1})(0)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

1.3.2
Arbitrary order
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
splitting methods

In
[
7
, Remark 2.7]
, it is mentioned that the existence of
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
splitting methods of arbitrary order is an open question. We provide here the following positive answer.

Theorem 1.11
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
splitting method of order
N
.

Theorem

1.11
extends the lower order methods recalled in
Section

1.1
to arbitrary orders.
It is an abstract existence result.
Indeed, our proof relies on an inversion argument, and only provides a way to compute an approximate solution
(\alpha,\beta)
.

An
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
splitting method corresponds to a trajectory of the scalar-input system (
1.5
) with state
x(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and control
u
which is a finite sum of Dirac masses with complex amplitudes.
The following control statement is the analogue of
Theorem

1.11
for the control system (
1.5
).

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be holomorphic vector fields on a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
with
f_{0}(0)=0
.
System (
1.5
) is small-state STLC with complex-valued control
u
if and only if
\operatorname{Lie}_{\mathbb{C}}(f_{0},f_{1})(0)=\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

For control theorists,
Theorem

1.12
may seem surprising at first sight.
Indeed, for control-affine systems with drift of the form (
1.5
) and real-valued controls
u
, no necessary and sufficient condition for controllability is known.
In particular, the Lie algebra rank condition does not imply the controllability, as shown by the canonical example on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\begin{cases}\dot{x}_{1}=u\\
\dot{x}_{2}=x_{1}^{2}\end{cases}

(1.12)

for which
f_{1}(0)=(1,0)
and
[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{0}]](0)=(0,2)
, so that
\operatorname{Lie}_{\mathbb{R}}(f_{0},f_{1})(0)=\mathbb{R}^{2}
but, for real-valued
u
, one has
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
so
\dot{x}_{2}\geq 0
, preventing controllability, since one cannot reach a target state
(x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*})
with
x_{2}^{*}<0
starting from the initial state
(0,0)
.

Theorem

1.12
is nevertheless reasonable, since, as in (
1.12
), all known obstructions to controllability rely on the presence of positive drifts in the dynamics (see
[
6
]
).
In particular, one checks that (
1.12
) is controllable in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with complex-valued controls
u(t)\in\mathbb{C}
since
i^{2}=-1
.

1.3.3
The first obstruction to controllability and to
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting methods

It is well known that splitting methods with
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
coefficients suffer from severe order limitations.
In particular, one has the following result (see e.g.
[
10
]
).

Theorem 1.13
.

The maximal order of an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method is 2.

We claim in the sequel that the source of this order restriction is the positive-definite quadratic form governing the coordinate associated with the “bad” bracket
W_{1}=(X_{1},(X_{1},X_{0}))
in the free system (
3.3
) and that this is linked with a well-known obstruction to controllability.

An
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method corresponds to a trajectory of the control system (
1.5
) with state
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and control
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
u
is a finite sum of Dirac masses with real amplitudes.
In control theory, the following result, due to Sussmann
[
50
, Proposition 6.3]
(see also
[
6
, Theorem 1.10]
for a modern proof using the Magnus formula and ruling out small-state STLC), is the first necessary condition for controllability.

Theorem 1.14
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be smooth vector fields on a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f_{0}(0)=0
.
If the system (
1.5
) is small-state STLC then
f_{W_{1}}(0)\in\operatorname{span}\{f_{M_{\nu}}(0)\mid\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

An analogue of
Theorem

1.14
for splitting methods is the following result.

Theorem 1.15
.

Let
f_{0},f_{1}
be smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order
3
relative to
(f_{0},f_{1})
, then
f_{W_{1}}
and
f_{M_{2}}
are linearly dependent.

The difference between the compensation conditions in
Theorems

1.14
and
1.15
comes from the scaling.
In small-state STLC, the control
u
is small: the displacement along
f_{W_{1}}(0)
is quadratic in
u
, whereas the displacements along the
f_{M_{\nu}}(0)
are linear, so any of them may compensate for the drift generated by
W_{1}
. By contrast, in
Definition

1.5
, the coefficients of a splitting method are independent of the final time
T
, so compensation can only occur between brackets of the same total length. Since the only other bracket of length
3
is
M_{2}=((X_{1},X_{0}),X_{0})
, the compensation condition only involves
f_{W_{1}}
and
f_{M_{2}}
. If the coefficients were allowed to depend polynomially on
T
, the condition could involve
f_{W_{1}}
and
\{f_{M_{\nu}}\mid\nu\leq 2\}
.

Another indication that
W_{1}
is the source of
Theorem

1.13
is that this order restriction disappears as soon as one can compute the flow of
f_{W_{1}}
(in fact, the flow of
-f_{W_{1}}
already suffices).

Theorem 1.16
.

There exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order 4 involving
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
,
W_{1}
.

This observation already appears in
[
10
, Section 5]
, where an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R}^{+})
splitting method involving
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
, and
-W_{1}
is constructed.
Our proof relies on control theory and yields only an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
scheme, but it extends readily to higher-order schemes (see below).
As in
Theorem

1.11
above and
Theorems

1.17
and
1.18
below, our positive results are abstract existence statements: the proofs provide a way to approximate
(\alpha,\beta)
, but not to compute it exactly.

1.3.4
The next obstructions to controllability and to
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting methods

The obstruction
W_{1}
is far from being the only one.
In fact, even in situations where the first necessary conditions
f_{W_{1}}(0)\in\operatorname{span}\{f_{M_{\nu}}(0)\mid\nu\in\mathbb{N}\}
(for control theory) or
f_{W_{1}}
and
f_{M_{2}}
are linearly dependent (for splitting) hold, or one incorporates the flow of
f_{W_{1}}
, other obstructions occur.
The next obstruction comes from
W_{2}=\operatorname{ad}^{2}_{(X_{1},X_{0})}(X_{0})
(see
Definition

1.2
).

In the spirit of
[
5
]
for control theory, we exhibit a list of obstructions to the construction of
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting methods, which are associated with quadratic quantities in the control (or coefficients along
f_{1}
).
We prove the following results.

Theorem 1.17
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
f_{0},f_{1}
be smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f_{W_{j}}=0
for
j=1,\dots,N-1
. If there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order
(2N+1)
relative to
(f_{0},f_{1})
then
f_{M_{2N}}
and
f_{W_{N}}
are linearly dependent.

Theorem 1.18
.

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order
2N
involving
X_{0}
and
X_{1},W_{1},\dots,W_{N-1}
, and this is the maximal possible order of such methods.

Theorem

1.18
was conjectured in 2004 in
[
18
, Section V]
, which contains a proof for
N=2
and
N=3
.
The case
N=2
was also investigated numerically in
[
10
, Section 5]
and theoretically in
[
1
]
.

The philosophy is iterative.
First,
W_{1}
obstructs order
3
for methods involving only
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
; it is therefore natural to add
W_{1}
, or equivalently to restrict to vector fields such that
f_{W_{1}}=0
.
Second,
W_{2}
obstructs order
5
for methods involving
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
, and
W_{1}
; one is then naturally led to add
W_{2}
, or to assume
f_{W_{1}}=f_{W_{2}}=0
.
Iterating this principle yields a purely quadratic obstruction theory for splitting methods.
Other approaches are of course possible, for instance by adjoining other elements of
\mathcal{L}(X)
or by considering different degeneracies, and the algebraic formalism developed here can be used to test them.

The control-theoretic counterpart to
Theorem

1.18
is the following new result.

Theorem 1.19
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1},W_{1},\dots,W_{N-1}\}
.
For any smooth vector fields
f_{0},f_{1}
on a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f_{0}(0)=0
and
\mathcal{L}_{2N}(f)(0)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the system

\dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)f_{b}(x(t))

(1.13)

is small-state STLC.

1.4
Open problems

We conclude with several open questions suggested by the results of this work.

1.

Do there exist
(\mathbb{C}^{+},\mathbb{C}^{+})
splitting methods of arbitrary order?

Theorem

1.11
yields
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{C})
splitting methods of arbitrary order, which is a different problem.

2.

For which sets of enabled brackets
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
does there exist splitting methods of arbitrary order?

By
Theorem

1.18
,
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1}\}\cup\{W_{j}\mid j\geq 1\}
works, but other sets could too.

3.

What are the sharp degeneracy conditions on a pair of smooth vector fields
f_{0},f_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that guarantee the existence of a splitting method of order
N
for this pair?

Theorem

1.17
only yields a partial answer (for instance for
N=2
, we assume that
f_{W_{1}}=0
to obtain information on
f_{W_{2}}
).

Prerequisites about control-affine systems

In this section
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
denotes the base field.
Given
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and smooth vector fields
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}
on
\mathbb{K}^{d}
with
f_{0}(0)=0
, we consider the control-affine system

\dot{x}(t)=f_{0}(x(t))+u_{1}(t)f_{1}(x(t))+\dotsb+u_{m}(t)f_{m}(x(t)).

(2.1)

We assume in this section that the vector fields
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}
are all complete.
In particular, for any
T>0
,
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K}^{m})
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
, it is standard that system (
2.1
) admits a unique Carathéodory solution on
[0,T]
with initial condition
x_{0}
.
We denote it by
x(t;f,u,x_{0})
.

In
Section

2.1
, we define a set of impulsive controls, and the associated solutions.
In
Section

2.2
, we state the equivalence between exact and approximate local controllability of system (
2.1
).
In
Section

2.3
, we present an “extension” argument, which is often used to prove its controllability.

2.1
Impulsive controls and associated solutions

To relate controllability to splitting, we will use the following class of impulsive controls.

Definition 2.1

(Set
\mathfrak{D}
of impulsive controls)
.

Given
T>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we denote by
\mathfrak{D}
the set of
impulsive controls
on
[0,T]
with values in
\mathbb{K}^{m}
.
Each
u\in\mathfrak{D}
is a finite ordered list
(\tau_{k},\beta_{k},i_{k})_{k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
where
0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\dotsb\leq\tau_{n}\leq T
are the times of the pulses,
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{K}
their amplitudes, and
1\leq i_{k}\leq m
the indices of the active control channels.

The notation
\mathfrak{D}
does not record the choice of
T
,
\mathbb{K}
or
m
, but there will be no ambiguity when it is used in the article.
When the impulse times are pairwise distinct, an impulsive control may be identified with the vector-valued measure
\beta_{1}e_{i_{1}}\delta_{\tau_{1}}+\dotsb+\beta_{n}e_{i_{n}}\delta_{\tau_{n}}
.
If several impulses occur at the same time, this identification is no longer sufficient, because it does not record their order.

Definition 2.2

(Solution associated with an impulsive control)
.

Let
T>0
,
u\in\mathfrak{D}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
.
We define an associated
càdlàg
(right-continuous, left limits at each point)
impulsive trajectory
to (
2.1
) as follows.
Set
y_{0}:=x_{0}
,
\tau_{0}:=0
and define inductively, for
k=1,\dotsc,n
,

y_{k}^{-}:=e^{(\tau_{k}-\tau_{k-1})f_{0}}y_{k-1},\qquad y_{k}:=e^{\beta_{k}f_{i_{k}}}y_{k}^{-}.

(2.2)

We then set, for
t\in[0,T]
,

x(t;f,u,x_{0}):=e^{(t-\tau_{k_{t}})f_{0}}y_{k_{t}},\qquad k_{t}:=\max\{k\in\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket\mid\tau_{k}\leq t\}.

(2.3)

The state
x(t)
contains the contribution of all the ordered impulses up to time
t
.
In particular, when
0=\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}
, one may have
x(0)=e^{\beta_{1}f_{i_{1}}}(x_{0})\neq x_{0}
.

Example 2.3
.

The Lie–Trotter and Strang splitting of (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) can be seen as associated with the impulsive controls
u=((0,T,1))
and
u=((T/2,T,1))
.

For a more elaborate example, if
u=((0,1,1),(1,2,1),(3,-7,2),(3,5,3))
, then

x(t;f,u,x_{0})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{tf_{0}}e^{f_{1}}x_{0}&\text{ if }t\in[0,1),\\
e^{(t-1)f_{0}}e^{2f_{1}}e^{f_{0}}e^{f_{1}}x_{0}&\text{ if }t\in[1,3),\\
e^{(t-3)f_{0}}e^{5f_{3}}e^{-7f_{2}}e^{2f_{0}}e^{2f_{1}}e^{f_{0}}e^{f_{1}}x_{0}&\text{ if }t\in[3,\infty).\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
T>0
,
u\in\mathfrak{D}
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
.
There exists a sequence
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K}^{m})
of piecewise-constant controls, bounded in
L^{1}
, such that:

\forall t\in(0,T],\quad\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}x(t;f,u^{\varepsilon},x_{0})=x(t;f,u,x_{0}).

(2.5)

Proof.

Let
u=(\tau_{k},\beta_{k},i_{k})_{k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}\in\mathfrak{D}
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, define

I_{k}^{\varepsilon}:=\begin{cases}[(k-1)\varepsilon,k\varepsilon],&\text{if }\tau_{k}=0,\\[4.30554pt]
[\,\tau_{k}-(n-k+1)\varepsilon,\;\tau_{k}-(n-k)\varepsilon\,],&\text{if }\tau_{k}>0.\end{cases}

(2.6)

Since the sequence
(\tau_{k})_{k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket}
is nondecreasing, one checks that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough the intervals
I_{k}^{\varepsilon}
are pairwise disjoint and ordered in the same way as the indices
k
.
Moreover, if
\tau_{k}=0
then
I_{k}^{\varepsilon}
lies to the right of
0
, while if
\tau_{k}>0
then
I_{k}^{\varepsilon}
lies to the left of
\tau_{k}
.

We define a piecewise-constant
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K}^{m})
by

u^{\varepsilon}(t):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\beta_{k}}{\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{I^{\varepsilon}_{k}}(t)e_{i_{k}}.

(2.7)

Then
\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}|\beta_{k}|
and, for each
t\in(0,T]
,
x(t;f,u^{\varepsilon},x_{0})\to x(t;f,u,x_{0})
.
∎

2.2
Exact and approximate STLC

For
T>0
and either
\mathcal{U}=\mathfrak{D}
or
\mathcal{U}=L^{1}
, define the set of reachable states for system (
2.1
) as

R_{T}(\mathcal{U}):=\{x(T;f,u,0)\mid u\in\mathcal{U}\}\subset\mathbb{K}^{d}.

(2.8)

Since
f_{0}(0)=0
, one checks that
R_{T}(\mathcal{U})
is non-decreasing with respect to
T
.

In the sequel, we will use exact and approximate small-time local controllability interchangeably, since they are equivalent in our setting (see
Section

A.2
for a self-contained proof).

Proposition 2.5

(STLC)
.

Assume that
(\operatorname{Lie}\{f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\})(0)=\mathbb{K}^{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{U}=\mathfrak{D}
or
\mathcal{U}=L^{1}
.
The following notions are equivalent:

\displaystyle\quad-\quad\text{small-time local exact $\mathcal{U}$-controllability:}

\displaystyle\forall T>0,\quad 0\in\operatorname{int}R_{T}(\mathcal{U}),

\displaystyle\quad-\quad\text{small-time local approximate $\mathcal{U}$-controllability:}

\displaystyle\forall T>0,\quad 0\in\operatorname{int}\overline{R_{T}(\mathcal{U})}.

Moreover, approximate
\mathfrak{D}
-STLC and
L^{1}
-STLC are equivalent (so their exact counterparts too).

Proposition 2.6
.

For any
T>0
, one has
\overline{R_{T}(\mathfrak{D})}=\overline{R_{T}(L^{1})}
.

Proof.

On the one hand, given
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, by
Proposition

2.4
, there exists a sequence of regularizations
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{K}^{m})
for which
x(T;f,u^{\varepsilon},0)\to x(T;f,u,0)
.

On the other hand, given
u\in L^{1}
, by density we first approximate it by a sequence of step functions.
Within each step of duration
\tau>0
, control value
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{m}
, and starting from an initial point
x_{0}
, the Lie–Trotter product formula (see
Lemma

A.7
) yields

e^{\tau(f_{0}+\alpha_{1}f_{1}+\dotsb+\alpha_{m}f_{m})}(x_{0})=\lim_{N\to+\infty}\left(e^{\frac{\tau}{N}f_{0}}e^{\frac{\tau\alpha_{1}}{N}f_{1}}\dotsb e^{\frac{\tau\alpha_{m}}{N}f_{m}}\right)^{N}(x_{0}).

(2.9)

Hence we can construct a sequence
u^{N}\in\mathfrak{D}
for which
x(T;f,u^{N},0)\to x(T;f,u,0)
.
∎

2.3
Extension method

A classical strategy to establish the STLC of a system is to apply “extension” or “saturation” operations, to enlarge the set
\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
, until the following result can be applied.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
\operatorname{span}\left\{f_{1}(0),\dots,f_{m}(0)\right\}=\mathbb{K}^{d}
, then system (
2.1
) is STLC.

Proof.

One may assume that the vectors
f_{1}(0),\dotsc,f_{d}(0)
are linearly independent.
By the inverse mapping theorem, the map
F:\alpha\in\mathbb{K}^{d}\mapsto e^{\alpha_{d}f_{d}}\dots e^{\alpha_{1}f_{1}}(0)\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
is a local diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
around
0
.
For
x^{*}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
small enough, set
\alpha=F^{-1}(x^{*})
and define

u=((T,\alpha_{k},k))_{k\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}\quad\text{so}\quad x(T;f,u,0)=e^{\alpha_{d}f_{d}}\dotsb e^{\alpha_{1}f_{1}}e^{Tf_{0}}(0)=F(\alpha)=x^{*}.

(2.10)

Hence
0\in\operatorname{int}\overline{R_{T}(\mathfrak{D})}
.
By
Propositions

2.5
and
2.6
, system (
2.1
) is STLC.
∎

An example of legitimate extension is given by the following statement.

Proposition 2.8
.

For
m\geq 2
, system (
2.1
) is
small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable iff the extended system

\dot{x}=f_{0}(x)+u_{1}f_{1}(x)+\dotsb+u_{m}f_{m}(x)+u_{m+1}[f_{1},f_{2}](x)

(2.11)

is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable.

Proof.

The
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of the system (
2.1
) involve the composition of a finite number of flows of the form
e^{a_{j}f_{j}}
with
a_{j}\in\mathbb{K}
(and
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
).
The
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of the extended system (
2.11
) involve the composition of a finite number of flows of the form
e^{a_{j}f_{j}}
and
e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{2}]}
with
a_{j},\alpha\in\mathbb{K}
(and
a_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
).
Thus, it suffices to prove that one can approximate flows of the form
e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{2}]}
by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of the initial system (
2.1
).

Uniformly within any compact set of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
, as
t\to 0
, one has

e^{-\alpha\sqrt{t}f_{1}}e^{-\sqrt{t}f_{2}}e^{+\alpha\sqrt{t}f_{1}}e^{+\sqrt{t}f_{2}}(y)=y+\alpha t[f_{1},f_{2}](y)+O(t\sqrt{t}).

(2.12)

Hence (see
Lemma

A.6
), for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
, as
N\to\infty
,

\left(e^{-\frac{\alpha}{N}f_{1}}e^{-\frac{1}{N}f_{2}}e^{+\frac{\alpha}{N}f_{1}}e^{+\frac{1}{N}f_{2}}\right)^{N^{2}}(x_{0})\to e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{2}]}(x_{0}),

(2.13)

which proves that we can approximate the flow
e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{2}]}
by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of (
2.1
).
∎

Combining
Lemmas

2.7
and
2.8
, we recover the following famous result.

Corollary 2.9
.

If
(\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\right\})(0)=\mathbb{K}^{d}
, then system (
2.1
) is STLC.

3.1
Free algebras

Definition 3.1

(Free associative algebra)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}(X)=\mathcal{A}^{0}(X)\oplus\mathcal{A}^{1}(X)\oplus\dotsb
be the algebra of noncommutative polynomials in
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathcal{A}^{n}(X)
is the finite dimensional
\mathbb{K}
-vector space spanned by words of length
n
over
X
(e.g.
\mathcal{A}^{0}(X)=\mathbb{K}
and
\mathcal{A}^{1}(X)=\mathbb{K}X_{0}\oplus\mathbb{K}X_{1}
).

Definition 3.2

(Lie bracket)
.

The algebra
\mathcal{A}(X)
is endowed with the Lie bracket
[a,b]:=ab-ba
, which satisfies
[a,a]=0
and the Jacobi identity
[a,[b,c]]+[c,[a,b]]+[b,[c,a]]=0
.

Definition 3.3

(Free Lie algebra)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}(X)
be the Lie subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}(X)
generated by
X
, i.e. its smallest vector subspace containing
X_{0},X_{1}
and stable under Lie brackets.
There is an
evaluation
map
e
from the free magma
\operatorname{Br}(X)
defined in
Definition

1.1
to
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
It is defined by
\text{e}(X_{i})=X_{i}
for
X_{i}\in X
and
\text{e}((b_{1},b_{2}))=[\text{e}(b_{1}),\text{e}(b_{2})]
for
b_{1},b_{2}\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
.
Then
\text{e}(\operatorname{Br}(X))
spans
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Definition 3.4

(Formal power series)
.

Let
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
be the unital associative algebra of formal power series in
X_{0}
,
X_{1}
: its elements are sequences
a=(a^{\langle n\rangle})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
usually written
a=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}a^{\langle n\rangle}
, where
a^{\langle n\rangle}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}(X)
; in particular,
a^{\langle 0\rangle}\in\mathbb{K}
is the constant term.
Let
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(X)
be the Lie algebra of formal power series
a\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
for which
a^{\langle n\rangle}\in\mathcal{L}(X)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

For
a\in\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
with
a^{\langle 0\rangle}=0
,

\exp(a)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k!}\quad\text{ and }\quad\log(1+a)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}a^{k}

(3.1)

are well defined elements of
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
.
Moreover, the following identities hold in
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
:

\exp(\log(1+a))=1+a\quad\text{and}\quad\log(\exp(a))=a.

(3.2)

3.2
The formal differential equation

Fix a finite subset
\mathcal{E}
of
\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
and a control
u=(u_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{E}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{K}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
.
We consider the formal differential equation posed on
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
:

\dot{S}(t)=S(t)\left(X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)b\right)\quad\text{and}\quad S(0)=1.

(3.3)

In (
3.3
), and in all this work, we identify
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
with its evaluations
\text{e}(b)
in
\mathcal{L}(X)
, or in
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(X)
.

Definition 3.5
.

The
solution to (
3.3
)
is the formal-series-valued function
S:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
, denoted
S(t,X,u)
, whose homogeneous components
S^{\langle n\rangle}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathcal{A}^{n}(X)
are defined, for every
t\geq 0
, by
S^{\langle 0\rangle}(t)=1
and, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, by

S^{\langle n\rangle}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\left(S^{\langle n-1\rangle}(\tau)X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(\tau)S^{\langle n-|b|\rangle}(\tau)b\right)\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.4)

with the convention that
S^{\langle n\rangle}=0
for
n<0
.

Iterating this integral formula yields an expansion of
S(t,X,u)
in
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
, called the Chen series
[
15
,
16
]
and popularized in control theory by
[
23
]
.
The following proposition emphasizes an important homogeneity property of this solution.

Proposition 3.6

(Dilation
\Lambda
)
.

For
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{K}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
and
a\in\mathbb{K}
, define
\lambda_{a}u
by
(\lambda_{a}u)_{b}:=a^{n_{1}(b)}u_{b}
for
b\in\mathcal{E}
.
Then, for all
t\geq 0
,
S(t,X,\lambda_{a}u)=\Lambda_{a}S(t,X,u)
where
\Lambda_{a}:\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)\to\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
is the unique morphism of algebras such that
\Lambda_{a}(X_{0})=X_{0}
and
\Lambda_{a}(X_{1})=aX_{1}
.

Proof.

If
u
is continuous, since
\Lambda_{a}
is a morphism of algebras, one has

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}[\Lambda_{a}S]=\Lambda_{a}\dot{S}=\Lambda_{a}\Big[S\big(X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}b\big)\Big]=(\Lambda_{a}S)\big(X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}(\lambda_{a}u)_{b}b\big)

(3.5)

where we used that
\Lambda_{a}X_{0}=X_{0}
and
\Lambda_{a}(u_{b}b)=u_{b}\Lambda_{a}(b)=u_{b}a^{n_{1}(b)}b=(\lambda_{a}u)_{b}b
.
This proves that
\Lambda_{a}S(t,X,u)
satisfies the same differential equation as
S(t,X,\lambda_{a}u)
.
So both are equal.
When
u\in L^{1}

3.3
A Magnus-type representation formula

It is known since the work of Magnus
[
37
]
in 1954 that the solution to the linear system (

) can be written as the exponential of a Lie series.
We describe here a slightly different representation which better reflects the particular role of
X_{0}
(see
[
3
]
for an in-depth comparison of both representation formulas).
We need the following definitions.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}(X)
denote the Lie ideal of
\mathcal{L}(X)
generated by
X_{1}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\oslash}(X)
the associated space of formal power series.
One has
\mathcal{L}(X)=\mathbb{K}X_{0}\oplus\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}(X)

Moreover, Lazard’s elimination (see
[
56

) proves that

is actually isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}(\{M_{j}\mid j\in\mathbb{N}\})
, the free Lie algebra generated by the family
(M_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
(recall
Definition

1.2
).

Definition 3.8
.

For

, we define the map
\Omega_{b}:\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\oslash}(X)\rightarrow\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\oslash}(X)
by

\Omega_{b}(\mathcal{Z}):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{n}}{n!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}(b),

(3.6)

where
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are the Bernoulli numbers (see
Section

A.4.2
).

Proposition 3.9
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{K}^{|\mathcal{E}|})

\mathcal{Z}(t,X,u):=\log(\exp(-tX_{0})S(t,X,u))
in
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
, so that one has

S(t,X,u)=\exp(tX_{0})\exp(\mathcal{Z}(t,X,u)).

(3.7)

Then the map
\mathcal{Z}
inherits the same homogeneity properties as the map
S
(see
Proposition

3.6
).
Moreover, for every
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{K}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
,

\mathcal{Z}(\cdot,X;u)
solves the formal differential equation on
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\oslash}(X)

\dot{\mathcal{Z}}(t)=[\mathcal{Z}(t),X_{0}]+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)\Omega_{b}(\mathcal{Z}(t))

(3.8)

with initial condition
\mathcal{Z}(0)=0
.
Thus, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\mathcal{Z}(t,X,u)\in\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\oslash}(X)
.

As in
Definition

3.5
, the formal differential equation (
3.8
) is understood in projection on the finite dimensional spaces
\mathcal{A}^{n}(X)
and in the integral sense.

Proof.

In this proof, to simplify notations, we write
\mathcal{Z}(t)
and
S(t)
instead of
\mathcal{Z}(t,X,u)
and
S(t,X,u)
.
We have
\exp(\mathcal{Z}(t))=\exp(-tX_{0})S(t)
.
Thus we deduce from (
3.3
) that

\exp(-\mathcal{Z}(t))\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\exp(\mathcal{Z}(t))=-\exp(-\mathcal{Z}(t))X_{0}\exp(\mathcal{Z}(t))+X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)b.

(3.9)

We deduce from the formula (
A.25
) that

\dot{\mathcal{Z}}(t)=g_{0}(\mathcal{Z})+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)\Omega_{b}(\mathcal{Z}),

(3.10)

where

\begin{split}g_{0}(\mathcal{Z})&:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{n}}{n!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\big(X_{0}-\exp(-\mathcal{Z})X_{0}\exp(\mathcal{Z})\big)\\
&=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{n}}{n!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{k}(X_{0})\\
&=-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{m}(X_{0})\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\binom{m}{n}B_{n}=\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}(X_{0})\end{split}

(3.11)

and the last equality results from (
A.22

∎

Remark 3.10

In this article, using

3.5
Lie algebra rank condition

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
g_{0}:=[\cdot,X_{0}]
and
g_{1}:=\Omega_{X_{1}}
smooth vector fields on
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
.
As in
Definition

1.4
, denote
b\mapsto g_{b}
the Lie algebra morphism generated by
X_{i}\mapsto g_{i}
.
Then
g_{b}=\Omega_{b}
for every
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
.
As a consequence
(\operatorname{Lie}\left\{g_{0},g_{1}\right\})(0)=\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
.

Proof.

Step 1: We prove that, for every
b,b^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
then
[\Omega_{b},\Omega_{b^{\prime}}]=\Omega_{[b,b^{\prime}]}
.

For
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
, we define a smooth vector field
F_{b}
on
\mathcal{A}_{N}(X)
by
F_{b}(S)=Sb
. Then, for every
b,b^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
, we have
[F_{b},F_{b^{\prime}}]=F_{[b,b^{\prime}]}
.
The set
G^{\oslash}_{N}(X)=\{\exp(\mathcal{Z})\mid\mathcal{Z}\in\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)\}
is a submanifold of
\mathcal{A}_{N}(X)
with dimension
\dim\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
and the
F_{b}
are vector fields on
G^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
.
The logarithm is a diffeomorphism from
G^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
to
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
with reciprocal
\exp:\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)\to G^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
.
With this formalism, for
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
, the vector field
\Omega_{b}
on
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
is the push-forward of
F_{b}
by the logarithm:
\Omega_{b}(\mathcal{Z})=(\log_{*}F_{b})(\mathcal{Z})=D\log(\exp(\mathcal{Z}))F_{b}(\exp(\mathcal{Z}))
.
Thus for all
b,b^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
,
[\Omega_{b},\Omega_{b^{\prime}}]=\log_{*}[F_{b},F_{b^{\prime}}]=\log_{*}F_{[b,b^{\prime}]}=\Omega_{[b,b^{\prime}]}
(see
[
3
, Lemma 89]
).

Step 2: We prove by induction on
j\in\mathbb{N}
that
g_{M_{j}}=\Omega_{M_{j}}
.
The initialization for
j=0
results from the definitions:
g_{M_{0}}=g_{X_{1}}=g_{1}=\Omega_{X_{1}}
. Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
. We assume
g_{M_{j}}=\Omega_{M_{j}}
. Then

\begin{split}g_{M_{j+1}}(\mathcal{Z})&=[g_{M_{j}},g_{0}](\mathcal{Z})=[\Omega_{M_{j}},g_{0}](\mathcal{Z})=[\Omega_{M_{j}}(\mathcal{Z}),X_{0}]-D(\Omega_{M_{j}})(\mathcal{Z})\cdot[\mathcal{Z},X_{0}]\\
&=[M_{j},X_{0}]+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{n}}{n!}\left([\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}(M_{j}),X_{0}]-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n-1-k}\operatorname{ad}_{[\mathcal{Z},X_{0}]}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{k}(M_{j})\right)\\
&=M_{j+1}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}B_{n}}{n!}\operatorname{ad}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{n}(M_{j+1})=\Omega_{M_{j+1}}(\mathcal{Z})\end{split}

(3.18)

where the last line results from the Jacobi identity.

Step 3: We prove that for every
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
then
g_{b}=\Omega_{b}
.

Recalling
Definition

3.7
of
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}(X)
, the evaluation in
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
of
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
is a linear combination of iterated Lie brackets of the
M_{j}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus, by Step 1 and Step 2,
g_{b}=\Omega_{b}
.
In particular
g_{b}(0)=b
, which entails the Lie algebra rank condition.
∎

Thus, the following equality holds in

(4.6)

On the one hand, let

and
\underline{u}=\underline{u}_{X_{1}}=1
.
Using (

) and (

\begin{split}e^{T(g_{0}+g_{1})}(0)=\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,X,\underline{u})&=\log(\exp(-TX_{0})S_{N}(T,X,\underline{u}))\\
&=\log(\exp(-TX_{0})\exp(T(X_{0}+X_{1}))).\end{split}

(4.7)

In particular,

is a polynomial in

of degree
\leq N
.

On the other hand, let

be the Lie algebra morphism on

defined by

.
A computation proves that

, so is a polynomial in

of degree
\leq N

Therefore, the estimate (

\forall T\in\mathbb{R},\qquad x(1;Tg,u,0)=e^{T(g_{0}+g_{1})}(0).

(4.8)

Finally, for

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(1,X,u)=x(1;g,u,0)=\log(\exp(-X_{0})\exp(X_{0}+X_{1}))

∎

In this section,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
and we prove
Theorems

1.11
and

.
After a preliminary remark on complex-valued systems, we reduce in

these theorems to the controllability of the Magnus system, of which we give different proofs in

5.1
A remark on complex-valued systems

For complex-valued systems, some care is needed.
In particular, we explain why we always implicitly assume that the vector fields are
holomorphic
in this paper when defined on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

Lie brackets of holomorphic vector fields.

For
f,g\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, one can define their Lie bracket exactly as announced in
Definition

1.3
by setting

[f,g](z):=Dg(z)f(z)-Df(z)g(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}f_{k}(z)\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_{k}}-g_{k}(z)\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_{k}},

(5.1)

where the differentials are the
\mathbb{C}
-linear derivatives of
f
and
g
.
Then
[f,g]\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and the bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
defines a
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear operation (which is also anti-symmetric and satisfies the Jacobi identity).
In particular, this entails the existence of the following morphisms.

Lemma 5.1
.

Given
f_{0},f_{1}\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, there exists a unique morphism of
\mathbb{C}
-Lie algebras from
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
to
\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
mapping
X_{0}
to
f_{0}
and
X_{1}
to
f_{1}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\operatorname{Op}_{\mathbb{C}}
denote the algebra of linear endomorphisms of
\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C})
.
We identify a vector field
f\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
with the associated first-order differential operator of
\operatorname{Op}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

Given
f_{0},f_{1}\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, there exists a unique morphism of
\mathbb{C}
associative algebras from
\mathcal{A}_{\mathbb{C}}(X)
to
\operatorname{Op}_{\mathbb{C}}
, mapping
X_{0}
to
f_{0}
and
X_{1}
to
f_{1}
.

Realification.

Let
\iota:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined by
\iota(x+iy):=(x,y)
.
Given a vector field
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, one can define its
realification
as its pushforward by the diffeomorphism
\iota
:

(\iota_{*}f)(x,y):=\begin{pmatrix}\Re f(x+iy)\\
\Im f(x+iy)\end{pmatrix}.

(5.2)

This defines a vector field
\iota_{*}f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d};\mathbb{R}^{2d})
.

If
f,g\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, the Cauchy–Riemann relations imply that

\iota_{*}[f,g]_{\mathbb{C}}=[\iota_{*}f,\iota_{*}g]_{\mathbb{R}},

(5.3)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathbb{C}}
is the
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear Lie bracket of holomorphic vector fields defined in (
5.1
) and
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\mathbb{R}}
is the Lie bracket on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
of
Definition

1.3
.

Counterexamples.

With non-holomorphic vector fields, things break apart.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, smooth but not necessarily holomorphic, one can still try to define their Lie bracket through realification as

[[f,g]]:=\iota_{*}^{-1}\circ[\iota_{*}f,\iota_{*}g]

(5.4)

However, the bracket
[[\cdot,\cdot]]
is not
\mathbb{C}
-bilinear.
For instance, take
d:=1
and
f(z):=\Re(z)
.
Then
\iota_{*}f(x,y)=(x,0)
and
\iota_{*}(if)(x,y)=(0,x)
, which entails that

[[f,if]]=if\neq 0=i[[f,f]].

(5.5)

In particular, this prevents one from constructing a morphism of
\mathbb{C}
Lie algebras from
\mathcal{L}(X)
to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
mapping
X_{0}
to
f_{0}
and
X_{1}
to
f_{1}
when
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are not holomorphic.

Flows.

First, realification is compatible with flows (even without holomorphy).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
.
Write
\Phi_{t}^{f}
for the local flow of
\dot{z}=f(z)
and
\Phi_{t}^{\iota_{*}f}
for the local flow of
\dot{\xi}=(\iota_{*}f)(\xi)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Then, for all
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
t>0
for which both sides are defined,

\iota\circ\Phi_{t}^{f}(z_{0})=\Phi_{t}^{\iota_{*}f}\circ\iota(z_{0}).

(5.6)

Proof.

Fix
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and set
z(t):=\Phi_{t}^{f}(z_{0})
, so
\dot{z}(t)=f(z(t))
and
z(0)=z_{0}
.
Define
\xi(t):=\iota(z(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
By the chain rule and the definition of pushforward,

\dot{\xi}(t)=D\iota(z(t))\dot{z}(t)=D\iota(z(t))f(z(t))=(\iota_{*}f)(\xi(t)),\quad\text{and}\quad\xi(0)=\iota(z_{0}).

(5.7)

Thus
\xi(t)
solves the real ODE with vector field
\iota_{*}f
and initial data
\iota(z_{0})
.
By uniqueness of solutions,
\xi(t)=\Phi_{t}^{\iota_{*}f}(\iota(z_{0}))
, which is exactly (
5.6
).
∎

Second, when
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{C}^{d};\mathbb{C}^{d})
, for
t>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, real-time flows of
\alpha f
and
f
satisfy the compatibility relation
\Phi^{\alpha f}_{t}=\Phi^{f}_{\alpha t}
by real time reparametrization.
If
f
is holomorphic, its flow is also well defined for complex times (see
[
30
, Chapter I, Section 1A]
) and this relation holds for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
by complex time reparametrization.
In particular, the complex flows of the form
e^{\alpha tf}
in our complex splitting methods can be unambiguously understood as one or the other.

If
f
is not holomorphic, due to the possible lack of commutation of
f
and
if
(recall (
5.5
)), this relation may break.
For example, with
d=1
and
f(z)=\Re(z)
as above, on the one hand
\Phi^{if}_{t}(z_{0})=x_{0}+i(y_{0}+tx_{0})
and on the other hand
\Phi^{f}_{t}(z_{0})=e^{t}x_{0}+iy_{0}
.
This is a real-analytic expression of
t
which admits a holomorphic extension, albeit different from
\Phi^{if}_{t}(z_{0})
.

5.2
Reduction to the controllability of the Magnus system

Theorem

1.12
states an equivalence of the form STLC
\Leftrightarrow
LARC, in which the forward implication is classical thus we only prove the reverse one.
Thanks to
Theorem

4.6
, the key point is to prove the
\mathfrak{D}
-controllability in

of the associated Magnus system

\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}(t)=[\mathcal{Z}_{N}(t),X_{0}]+u(t)\Omega_{X_{1}}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}(t)).

(5.8)

where

is a finite sum of Dirac masses with complex-valued amplitudes.
More precisely, by
Theorem

4.6
, the following result entails both

1.11
and

.

Proposition 5.4
.

For every
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, system (

) is controllable.

Proposition

can be deduced from a general result by Sussmann (see
Section

).
Since this result is quite involved, we give below two easier self-contained direct proofs.
The first one, in
Section

, is very natural for people from control theory because it mimics that of Chow’s theorem (

1.10
).
The second one, in

5.5

Both contain an inversion argument (hidden in the proof of
Proposition

2.5

5.3
A proof based on Sussmann’s general result

With controls
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{C})
, one can apply Sussmann’s general theorem
[
52
, Theorem 2.4]
to the control-affine system (
5.8
).
Let
N\geq 2
and
\xi:=e^{\frac{2i\pi}{N}}
.
Consider the morphism of algebra
\Lambda_{\xi}
of
Proposition

3.6
.
With Sussmann’s terminology
\Upsilon:=\{\operatorname{Id},\Lambda_{\xi},\dots,\Lambda_{\xi}^{N-1}\}
is a “finite group of input symmetries”.
The
\Upsilon
-fixed elements of
\mathcal{L}(X)
are spanned by the
b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
such that
n_{1}(b)\in N\mathbb{N}
, i.e.
b=X_{0}
or
n_{1}(b)\in N\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
In any case,
g_{b}(0)=0
in
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)

Moreover, since no compensation has to be done, there is no need to provide a one parameter group of dilations.
For
N=1
, one can use
\Upsilon=\{\operatorname{Id},\Lambda_{-1}\}
.

5.4
A proof similar to that of Chow’s theorem

As for Chow’s theorem, the proof uses a trajectory from
0
to
0
around which an inversion argument works.
For Chow’s theorem, one uses the time reversibility of the driftless system to build this trajectory.
For the system with drift (
5.8
), we need another argument, proved in
Proposition

5.8
.

Definition 5.5

(Concatenation)
.

For
u\in L^{1}((0,T);\mathbb{C})
and
v\in L^{1}((0,T^{\prime});\mathbb{C})
, we denote by
u\diamond v:(0,T+T^{\prime})\to\mathbb{C}
their concatenation

(u\diamond v)(t)=\begin{cases}u(t)\quad&\text{ if }t\in(0,T),\\
v(t-T)&\text{ if }t\in(T,T+T^{\prime}).\end{cases}

(5.9)

By extension, we also write
u\diamond v
when
u,v\in\mathfrak{D}
(and there is no ambiguity with the time intervals).

Definition 5.6

(Valuation)
.

For
S\in\mathcal{A}(X)
, define the valuation of
S
,
\operatorname{val}(S)
as the largest integer
\ell\geq 0
such that
S\in\mathcal{A}^{\ell}(X)+\mathcal{A}^{\ell+1}(X)+\dotsb
(or, equivalently,
\pi_{\ell-1}S=0
).

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
N,\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and, for
j=1,2
,
u_{j}\in\mathfrak{D}
such that
\operatorname{val}(\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{j},X,u_{j}))\geq\ell
.
Then
\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{1}+T_{2},X,u_{1}\diamond u_{2})-\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{1},X,u_{1})-\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{2},X,u_{2})
has valuation at least
\ell+1
.

Proof.

To simplify the notations, we omit
X
in
S_{N}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.
By uniqueness of the solution to (
3.16
), we have
S_{N}(T_{1}+T_{2},u_{1}\diamond u_{2})=S_{N}(T_{1},u_{1})S_{N}(T_{2},u_{2})
.
By (
3.15
), this implies

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{1}+T_{2},u_{1}\diamond u_{2})=\log\left(\exp(-T_{2}X_{0})\exp(\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{1},u_{1}))\exp(T_{2}X_{0})\exp(\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T_{2},u_{2}))\right).

(5.10)

The BCH formula (
A.37
) of
Proposition

A.13
gives the conclusion.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
N\geq 2
.
For any
T>0
and
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, there exists
v\in\mathfrak{D}
such that

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(N^{N}T,X,u\diamond v)=0.

(5.11)

Proof.

To simplify the notations, we omit the
X
in
\mathcal{Z}_{N}
.

Let
\xi_{k}:=e^{\frac{2ik\pi}{N}}
.
For any
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, by the homogeneity property of
Proposition

3.9
,

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,\xi_{k}u)=\Lambda_{\xi_{k}}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,u)

(5.12)

where

3.6
.
In particular,
\operatorname{val}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,\xi_{k}u)=\operatorname{val}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,u)
.
Define

\Gamma(u):=u\diamond\xi_{1}u\diamond\dotsb\diamond\xi_{N-1}u.

(5.13)

By
Proposition

,

\operatorname{val}\Big[\mathcal{Z}_{N}(NT,\Gamma(u))-\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,\xi_{k}u)\Big]\geq\operatorname{val}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,u)+1.

(5.14)

Moreover, by the properties of the roots of unity,
\Lambda_{\xi_{0}}+\dotsb+\Lambda_{\xi_{N-1}}=0

Indeed, when

with

Hence,

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,\xi_{k}u)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Lambda_{\xi_{k}}\right)\mathcal{Z}_{N}(T,u)=0.

(5.15)

By induction, we obtain
\operatorname{val}\mathcal{Z}_{N}(N^{\ell}T,\Gamma^{\ell}(u))\geq\ell+1

\mathcal{Z}_{N}(N^{N}T,\Gamma^{N}(u))=0

∎

Now, we can prove the controllability of (

5.5
A proof based on an extension argument

We give a proof relying on the strategy presented in

.
Let

and

be holomorphic vector fields on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
f_{0}(0)=0
.
We consider system (
2.1
).

Proposition 5.9
.

Assume that:

•

the flows associated with the
f_{j}
are globally defined;

•

the Lie algebra
\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\right\}
is nilpotent of step

.

System (
2.1
) is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable if and only if the extended system

\dot{x}=f_{0}(x)+u_{1}f_{1}(x)+\dotsb+u_{m}f_{m}(x)+u_{m+1}[f_{1},f_{0}](x)

(5.18)

is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable.

Proof.

One implication is immediate: if (
2.1
) is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable, then so is the extended system (
5.18
), since the latter reduces to the former when
u_{m+1}=0
.

We prove the converse implication.
It is enough to treat the case
m=1
and show that an extra flow
e^{\beta[f_{1},f_{0}]}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, can be approximated arbitrarily well by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of (
2.1
).

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
We define
u_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\delta_{t=0}-\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\delta_{t=\varepsilon}
.
Then, the solution map of system (
2.1
) satisfies

x(\varepsilon;f,u_{\varepsilon},x_{0})=e^{-\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}e^{\varepsilon f_{0}}e^{+\frac{\beta}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}(x_{0})=e^{H_{0}^{\varepsilon}}(x_{0})

(5.19)

where, by
Lemma

A.12
,

H_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon f_{0}+\beta[f_{1},f_{0}]+\sum_{k=2}^{R-1}\frac{\beta^{k}}{k!\varepsilon^{k-1}}\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{k}(f_{0}).

(5.20)

Fix coefficients
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{R}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{R}\alpha_{j}=1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\forall k\in\llbracket 2,R\rrbracket,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{R}\alpha_{j}^{k}=0.

(5.21)

For example, using symmetric sums and Newton’s identities, one can consider the roots of the polynomial
p(z):=\sum_{r=0}^{R}(-1)^{r}z^{R-r}/r!
.
For a given control
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, we define

\Gamma(u):=\alpha_{1}u\diamond\dotsb\diamond\alpha_{R}u.

(5.22)

For
n,k\geq 0
, let
\mathcal{V}_{n,k}
be the span of all iterated Lie brackets in
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
with exactly
k
occurrences of
f_{1}
and total length
>n+k
.
We claim that, for every
n\geq 0
,

x(R^{n}\varepsilon;f,\Gamma^{n}(u_{\varepsilon}),x_{0})=e^{H_{n}^{\varepsilon}}(x_{0})\quad\text{where}\quad H_{n}^{\varepsilon}=\beta[f_{1},f_{0}]+\sum_{k=2}^{R}\Xi_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon),

(5.23)

where
\Xi_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{V}_{n,k}
and
O(\varepsilon)
is a finite linear combination of brackets whose coefficients are
O(\varepsilon)
.

The case
n=0
follows from (
5.20
).
Assume now that (
5.23
) holds for some
n\geq 0
.
By homogeneity, for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,

x(R^{n}\varepsilon;f,\alpha\Gamma^{n}(u_{\varepsilon}),x_{0})=e^{H_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}(x_{0})\quad\text{where}\quad H_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=\alpha\beta[f_{1},f_{0}]+\sum_{k=2}^{R}\alpha^{k}\Xi_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}+O(\varepsilon).

(5.24)

By definition of
\Gamma
,

x(R^{n+1}\varepsilon;f,\Gamma^{n+1}(u_{\varepsilon}),x_{0})=e^{H_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha_{R})}\cdots e^{H_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha_{1})}(x_{0}).

(5.25)

Let
H_{n+1}^{\varepsilon}
be the
\operatorname{BCH}_{R}
logarithm given by
Proposition

A.13
.
By (
A.37
) and (
5.21
), its linear part is

\beta\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{R}\alpha_{j}\Bigr)[f_{1},f_{0}]+\sum_{k=2}^{R}\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{R}\alpha_{j}^{k}\Bigr)\Xi_{n,k}^{\varepsilon}=\beta[f_{1},f_{0}].

(5.26)

Any other
\operatorname{BCH}_{R}
term is a bracket involving at least two factors among the
H_{n}^{\varepsilon}(\alpha_{j})
.
Since

[[f_{1},f_{0}],\mathcal{V}_{n,k}]\subset\mathcal{V}_{n+1,k+1},\qquad[\mathcal{V}_{n,k},\mathcal{V}_{n,\ell}]\subset\mathcal{V}_{2n,k+\ell},\qquad[\mathcal{V}_{n,k},O(\varepsilon)]\subset\underset{k^{\prime}\geq k}{\oplus}\mathcal{V}_{n+1,k^{\prime}},

(5.27)

all these terms belong to
\oplus_{k\geq 2}\mathcal{V}_{n+1,k}+O(\varepsilon)
, which proves the induction step.

Taking
n=R-2
and using the step
R
nilpotency, we obtain
H_{R-2}^{\varepsilon}=\beta[f_{1},f_{0}]+O(\varepsilon)
.
Moreover, the total time is
R^{R-2}\varepsilon\to 0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we can prove the controllability of (
5.8
).

Proof of
Proposition

5.4
.

Let
g_{0},g_{1}
be as in
Proposition

3.14
.
We want to prove the controllability of

\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{N}=g_{0}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)g_{b}(\mathcal{Z}_{N})

(5.28)

with
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1}\}
.
By
Proposition

5.9
, it suffices to prove the
\mathfrak{D}
-controllability of the system (
5.28
) with
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1},[X_{1},X_{0}]\}
.
Iterating the argument, it suffices to prove the
\mathfrak{D}
-controllability of the system (
5.28
) with
\mathcal{E}=\{M_{0},M_{1},\dots,M_{N-1}\}
.
By
Proposition

2.8
, it suffices to prove that this system is
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable with
\mathcal{E}
the set of iterated brackets of length at most
N
of the elements of

.
By

3.7
, they span
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{N}(X)
.
Moreover, by

, for every

.
Thus the Lie algebra rank condition is satisfied and, by

∎

6
High order methods using commutator flows

In this section, the base field of all vector spaces is
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.
Our goal is to prove
Theorem

1.19
and the existence part of
Theorem

1.18
.
By
Theorem

4.6
, these are consequences of the following statement on the controllability of the Magnus system:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1},W_{1},\dots,W_{N-1}\}
, then the Magnus system

\dot{\mathcal{Z}}_{2N}(t)=[\mathcal{Z}_{2N},X_{0}]+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)\Omega_{b}(\mathcal{Z}_{2N}(t))

(6.1)

set on
\mathcal{L}^{\oslash}_{2N}(X)
, is
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable.

6.1
An extension argument

Our proof of
Proposition

6.1
relies on an extension argument, as introduced in
Section

2.3
.
Let
f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
f_{0}(0)=0
and consider the control-affine system (
2.1
).

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that:

•

the flows associated with the
f_{j}
are globally defined;

•

the Lie algebra
\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f_{0},f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\right\}
is nilpotent of step
R\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
;

•

\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{2}(f_{0})=0
or
\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{2}(f_{0})\in\{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}\}
.

Then system (
2.1
) is small-time locally
approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable iff the extended system

\dot{x}=f_{0}(x)+u_{1}f_{1}(x)+\dots+u_{m}f_{m}(x)+u_{m+1}[f_{1},f_{0}](x)

(6.2)

is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable.

Proof.

One implication is immediate: if (
2.1
) is small-time locally approximately
\mathfrak{D}
-controllable, then so is the extended system (
6.2
), since the latter reduces to the former when
u_{m+1}=0
.

We prove the converse implication.
It is enough to show that an extra flow
e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, can be approximated arbitrarily well by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of (
2.1
).
Since
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}=e^{\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}+O(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, it is enough to approximate
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}
for arbitrarily small
\varepsilon>0
.

First case:
\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{2}(f_{0})=0
.

By
Lemma

A.12
, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}e^{\varepsilon f_{0}}e^{+\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}=e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}.

(6.3)

Hence
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}
is exactly realized by the original system with the ordered impulsive control
u=((0,\alpha/\varepsilon,1),(\varepsilon,-\alpha/\varepsilon,1))
.

Second case:
\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{2}(f_{0})=f_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

Set
g_{k}:=\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{k}(f_{0})
for
k\geq 0
.
Since
\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f_{0},\dots,f_{m}\right\}
is nilpotent of step
R
, we have
g_{k}=0
for
k>R
.
Moreover, by
Proposition

2.8
, we may assume that
g_{k}\in\{f_{1},\dotsc,f_{m}\}
for
2\leq k\leq R
.

We first note that for any
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{R}\in\mathbb{R}
, the flow
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\beta_{2}g_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{R}g_{R}}
can be approximated arbitrarily well, in time
\varepsilon
, by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of (
2.1
).
Indeed, each
g_{k}
with
k\geq 2
is one of the control vector fields, so the Lie–Trotter formula
Lemma

A.7
gives

\left(e^{\frac{\varepsilon}{N}f_{0}}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{N}g_{2}}\dotsb e^{\frac{\beta_{R}}{N}g_{R}}\right)^{N}\longrightarrow e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\beta_{2}g_{2}+\cdots+\beta_{R}g_{R}}\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

(6.4)

It therefore remains to choose
\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{R}
so that conjugation by
e^{\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}
produces exactly
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}
.
For
k=2,\dots,R
, choose
\beta_{k}
recursively so that

\frac{\alpha^{k}}{k!\varepsilon^{k-1}}+\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}\frac{\alpha^{l}}{l!\varepsilon^{l}}\beta_{k-l}=0.

(6.5)

This is a triangular linear system, hence it has a unique solution.
By
Lemma

A.12
,

e^{-\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\sum_{r=2}^{R}\beta_{r}g_{r}}e^{+\frac{\alpha}{\varepsilon}f_{1}}=e^{F},\quad\text{where}\quad F:=\sum_{l=0}^{R}\frac{\alpha^{l}}{l!\varepsilon^{l}}\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{l}\left(\varepsilon f_{0}+\sum_{r=2}^{R}\beta_{r}g_{r}\right).

(6.6)

Since
\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{l}(f_{0})=g_{l}
and
\operatorname{ad}_{f_{1}}^{l}(g_{r})=g_{r+l}
, this becomes

F=\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]+\sum_{k=2}^{R}\left(\frac{\alpha^{k}}{k!\,\varepsilon^{k-1}}+\sum_{l=0}^{k-2}\frac{\alpha^{l}}{l!\,\varepsilon^{l}}\beta_{k-l}\right)g_{k}.

(6.7)

By (
6.5
), all coefficients of
g_{k}
for
k\geq 2
vanish, so
F=\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]
.
Thus
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+\alpha[f_{1},f_{0}]}
can be approximated arbitrarily well by
\mathfrak{D}
-controlled trajectories of (
2.1
).
∎

.

Let

be the vector fields on

Then

for every

for every

.

7
Order restrictions for signed real-valued methods

In this section the base field is
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.
We prove the upper bound of
Theorem

1.18
, using a representation of the solutions
S_{N}(t,X,u)
to (
3.16
) as a product over an ordered basis, involving the
coordinates of the second kind
(see
[
42
]
for an application to integration methods).

7.1
Representation of the state as a product

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{B}
be an ordered basis of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
.
For
b\in\mathcal{B}
, let
b^{*}
be the dual basis element,
V_{b}^{+}:=\operatorname{span}\{c\in\mathcal{B}\mid c>b\}
and
\mathbb{P}_{b}^{+}
the projection on
V_{b}^{+}
(parallel to
\operatorname{span}\{c\in\mathcal{B}\mid c\leq b\}
).

Definition 7.1
.

We say that
\mathcal{B}
is
triangular
when, for all
b<c\in\mathcal{B}
, one has
[b,c]\in V_{b}^{+}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

If the basis
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1}<\dotsb<b_{\ell}\}
is triangular, then the following map is a global analytic diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
onto the submanifold
G_{N}(X):=\exp(\mathcal{L}_{N}(X))
of
\mathcal{A}_{N}(X)
:

(\xi_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\mapsto\exp(\xi_{b_{\ell}}b_{\ell})\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}b_{1}).

(7.1)

The
\xi_{b}
are called
coordinates of the second kind
.

Proof.

It is a particular case of
[
17
]
or
[
55
, Theorem 3.18.11]
for solvable Lie groups.

Since
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
is nilpotent,
\exp
and
\log
are globally-defined polynomial inverses between
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
and
G_{N}(X)
, and the
\operatorname{BCH}_{N}
polynomial of
Proposition

A.13
is finite.

Surjectivity
.
Let
S=\exp Z_{1}\in G_{N}(X)
.
Define
\xi_{b_{k}}
and
Z_{k+1}
for
k=1,\dotsc,\ell
, by the recursion

\xi_{b_{k}}:=\langle b_{k}^{*},Z_{k}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{k+1}:=\operatorname{BCH}(Z_{k},-\xi_{b_{k}}b_{k}).

(7.2)

Since
\mathcal{B}
is triangular,
Z_{k+1}\in V_{b_{k}}^{+}
.
In particular,
Z_{\ell+1}=0
and
S=\exp(\xi_{b_{\ell}}b_{\ell})\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}b_{1})
.

Injectivity
.
Given
S=\exp(\xi_{b_{\ell}}b_{\ell})\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}b_{1})=\exp(\xi_{b_{\ell}}^{\prime}b_{\ell})\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}^{\prime}b_{1})
, the same recursion iteratively proves that
\xi_{b_{k}}=\xi_{b_{k}}^{\prime}
for
k=1,\dotsc,\ell
.

Regularity
.
Since
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
is nilpotent, the forward map is polynomial.
The inverse map is obtained by finitely many compositions of
\log
and
\operatorname{BCH}_{N}
, hence is polynomial too.
∎

Thus, it makes sense to represent the state
S_{N}(t,X,u)
as a product of the form (
7.1
), where the coefficients
\xi_{b}
will depend on
t
and
u
.
As a counterpart to Magnus’ expansion of
[
37
]
representing the solution as a single exponential, Wei and Norman proved in
[
57
]
the following result.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1}<\dotsb<b_{\ell}\}
be an ordered basis of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
.

For
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
and
u=(u_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{E}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
, the solution to (
3.16
) is given by

S_{N}(t,X,u)=\exp(\xi_{b_{\ell}}(t,u)b_{\ell})\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}(t,u)b_{1})

(7.3)

if and only if the coordinates
(\xi_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
satisfy
\xi_{b}(0,u)=0
and the following relation in
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
:

\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\dot{\xi}_{b_{k}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\exp(-\xi_{b_{j}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{j}})\right)b_{k}=X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}b.

(7.4)

Proof.

Let
P(t)
denote the right-hand side of (
7.3
).
First,
P(0)=1
if and only if
\xi_{b}(0,u)=0
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}
.
Moreover, explicitly differentiating in time, we obtain

\dot{P}=P\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\exp(-\xi_{b_{j}}b_{j})\right)\dot{\xi}_{b_{k}}b_{k}\left(\prod_{j=k-1}^{1}\exp(\xi_{b_{j}}b_{j})\right).

(7.5)

By the conjugation
Lemma

A.11
,
P
and
S_{N}
satisfy the same ODE if and only if (
7.4
) holds.
∎

The relations (
7.4
) are of the form
M(\xi)\dot{\xi}=U
where
U=X_{0}+\sum u_{b}b
is given.
Since
M
depends polynomially on
\xi
and
M(0)=\operatorname{Id}
, one can always solve (
7.4
) locally in time
[
57
, Theorem 1]
.
Under a
stronger
triangularity assumption of the form
[b,c]\in V_{c}^{+}
for all
b<c\in\mathcal{B}
,
M
is triangular, and system (
7.4
) can be directly inverted globally
[
57
, Theorem 2]
.

In fact, our triangularity assumption
Definition

7.1
is sufficient to solve (
7.4
) globally.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{1}<\dotsb<b_{\ell}\}
be a triangular basis of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
.

For
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}
and
u=(u_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{E}}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
, the solution to (
7.4
) is explicitly given by

\xi_{b}(t,u)=\sum\left\langle b^{*},\operatorname{ad}_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{r}}^{+}\dotsb\operatorname{ad}_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{1}}^{+}(c)\right\rangle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\xi_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}(s,u)\dotsb\xi_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}(s,u)}{m_{r}!\dotsb m_{1}!}u_{c}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

(7.6)

summing over
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{1}<\dotsb<a_{r}<b\in\mathcal{B}
,
m_{1},\dotsc,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
c\in\mathcal{E}\cup\{X_{0}\}
, with
u_{X_{0}}\equiv 1
.

Proof.

Set
U_{1}(t):=X_{0}+\sum_{b\in\mathcal{E}}u_{b}(t)b
.
For
k=1,\dotsc,\ell
, we define recursively
\xi_{b_{k}}
and
U_{k+1}
by

\dot{\xi}_{b_{k}}:=\langle b_{k}^{*},U_{k}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad U_{k+1}:=\exp(\xi_{b_{k}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{k}})\mathbb{P}_{b_{k}}^{+}U_{k}.

(7.7)

Since
\mathcal{B}
is triangular,
U_{k+1}\in V_{b_{k}}^{+}
.
By induction, (
7.4
) is equivalent to the reduced system

\sum_{i=k}^{\ell}\dot{\xi}_{b_{i}}\left(\prod_{j=k}^{i-1}\exp(-\xi_{b_{j}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{j}})\right)b_{i}=U_{k}.

(7.8)

For
k=1
this is exactly (
7.4
).
For
k>1
, the
i=k
term is
\dot{\xi}_{b_{k}}b_{k}
, while the
i>k
terms are in
V_{b_{k}}^{+}
.
Thus, pairing (
7.8
) with
b_{k}^{*}
yields
\dot{\xi}_{b_{k}}=\langle b_{k}^{*},U_{k}\rangle
, and applying
\exp(\xi_{b_{k}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{k}})\mathbb{P}_{b_{k}}^{+}
to
(
7.8
) removes the
i=k
term and gives (
7.8
) with
k
replaced by
k+1
and
U_{k+1}
as in (
7.7
).

Finally, unfolding the recurrence relation for
U_{k}
gives

U_{k}=\exp(\xi_{b_{k-1}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{k-1}})\mathbb{P}^{+}_{b_{k-1}}\dotsb\exp(\xi_{b_{1}}\operatorname{ad}_{b_{1}})\mathbb{P}^{+}_{b_{1}}U_{1}.

(7.9)

Expanding each exponential and using
\xi_{b_{k}}(0)=0
yields the explicit global formula (
7.6
).
∎

Generalization to impulsive controls and splitting methods

In

We now extend our definitions to

(7.10)

.
For

7.3
Link with Hall sets and Sussmann’s product

Unlike
\mathcal{A}(X)
– whose monomials form a basis – there is no canonical basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
Hall sets (stemming from
[
27
]
and generalized in
[
49
,
56
]
) constitute a wide family of bases of
\mathcal{L}(X)
, which includes many well-known bases of
\mathcal{L}(X)
such as the historical length-compatible Hall bases of
[
27
]
or the Chen–Fox–Lyndon basis of
[
14
]
.
We refer the interested reader to
[
4
, Section 1.4]
for a more gentle introduction and more thorough details (see also
[
45
, Chapter 4]
).

Definition 7.7
.

A
Hall set
is a subset
\mathcal{B}
of
\operatorname{Br}(X)
, containing
X
, with a total order
<
such that

•

for all
a,b\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
,
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}
iff
a<b\in\mathcal{B}
and, either
b\in X
or
b=(b^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})
with
b^{\prime}\leq a
,

•

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{B}
such that
(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}
, one has
a<(a,b)
.

Proposition 7.8
.

If
\mathcal{B}
is a Hall set, then
\text{e}(\mathcal{B})
is a triangular basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.

Proof.

By
[
56
, Theorem 1.2]
,
\text{e}(\mathcal{B})
is a basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
The triangularity comes from the axioms of a Hall set and the associated decomposition algorithm of
[b,c]
on
\text{e}(\mathcal{B})
(see
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
)
∎

Given a Hall set
\mathcal{B}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, one can consider
\mathcal{B}_{N}:=\{b\in\mathcal{B};|b|\leq N\}
.
Then, up to identifying brackets with their evaluation,
\mathcal{B}_{N}
is a (triangular) basis of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
.

Using an iterative proof based on Lazard’s elimination, as in
Section

7.1
, Sussmann proved in
[
51
]
the following representation result (see
[
3
, Theorem 64]
for the proof for non-length-compatible Hall sets) for the solution to (
3.16
) in the case
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1}\}
.
In this particular case, the inductive formula (
7.12
) for the coordinates of the second kind has a very nice structure which has proven fruitful for control theory (see
[
6
, Section 1.6]
).

Proposition 7.9
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{B}
a Hall set.
The solution to
\dot{S}_{N}=S_{N}(X_{0}+uX_{1})
for a given
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{R})
satisfies

S_{N}(t,X,u)=\underset{b\in\mathcal{B}_{N}}{\overleftarrow{\prod}}\exp\left(\xi_{b}(t,u)b\right)

(7.11)

where for any
b\in\mathcal{B}_{N}
, decomposing
b=\operatorname{ad}_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\dotsb\operatorname{ad}_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}(X_{j})
, one has (with
u_{X_{0}}\equiv 1
),

\xi_{b}(t,u)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\xi_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}(s,u)\dotsb\xi_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}(s,u)}{m_{r}!\dotsb m_{1}!}u_{X_{j}}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

(7.12)

Proof.

By
Proposition

7.8
,
\mathcal{B}_{N}
is a triangular basis of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
so
Section

7.1
applies and it suffices to check that the general formula (
7.6
) reduces to (
7.12
) for the case of a Hall basis and
\mathcal{E}=\{X_{1}\}
.

On the one hand, by
Definition

7.7
, for any
b\in\mathcal{B}
, there exists a unique maximal factorization
b=\operatorname{ad}_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\dotsb\operatorname{ad}_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}(X_{j})
where
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{1}<\dotsb<a_{r}\in\mathcal{B}
,
m_{1},\dotsc,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
j\in\{0,1\}
.

On the other hand, by
Definition

7.7
given any
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{1}<\dotsb<a_{r}\in\mathcal{B}
,
m_{1},\dotsc,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
j\in\{0,1\}
, the term

\operatorname{ad}_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{r}}^{+}\dotsb\operatorname{ad}_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{1}}^{+}(X_{j})

(7.13)

either vanishes in
\mathcal{L}(X)
or belongs to
\mathcal{B}
.

Thus (
7.12
) is the only non-vanishing term in (
7.6
).
∎

7.4
Choice of a good basis and computation of the coordinates

Designing a splitting method for the flow of
f_{0}+f_{1}
that uses only forward flows of
f_{0}
(but both forward and backward flows of
f_{1}
) is an asymmetric question.
This asymmetry appears in the associated control-affine system (
1.5
) and must be reflected when selecting a basis of
\mathcal{L}(X)
.
In
[
6
, Section 3]
, we constructed a Hall set
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
tailored to control applications, capturing this asymmetry.
We will not need its full definition here, only the following fact.

Proposition 7.10
.

There exists a Hall set
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
containing the elements of (
1.2
), as well as the
W_{j,\nu}:=((\dotsb(W_{j},X_{0}),\dotsc),X_{0})
with
X_{0}
appearing
\nu\geq 1
times, ordered as follows

X_{1}=M_{0}<\cdots<M_{\nu}<M_{\nu+1}<\dots<W_{j}<W_{j,\nu}<W_{j+1}<\cdots<X_{0}.

(7.14)

The evaluations of these elements in
\mathcal{L}(X)
form a basis of
\operatorname{span}\{\text{e}(b)\mid b\in\operatorname{Br}(X),n_{1}(b)\leq 2\}
.

Proposition 7.11
.

For
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\star}\setminus\{X_{0}\}
and
u=(u_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{E}}\in L^{1}
, the associated coordinates of the second kind satisfy

\begin{split}&\dot{\xi}_{X_{1}}=u_{X_{1}},\quad\dot{\xi}_{M_{\nu}}=\xi_{M_{\nu-1}}+u_{M_{\nu}},\quad\dot{\xi}_{X_{0}}=1,\\
&\dot{\xi}_{W_{j}}=\frac{1}{2}\xi_{M_{j-1}}^{2}+u_{W_{j}}+\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{j-1-i}\xi_{M_{i}}u_{M_{2j-1-i}},\end{split}

(7.15)

for
\nu,j\geq 1
with the convention that
u_{b}=0
when
b\notin\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

We use the homogeneity properties of Hall bases: for
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}
and
a\in\operatorname{Br}(X)
, if
\langle b^{*},a\rangle\neq 0
, then
|a|=|b|
,
n_{0}(a)=n_{0}(b)
and
n_{1}(a)=n_{1}(b)
.
Differentiating (
7.6
), for any
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}
,

\dot{\xi_{b}}=\sum\left\langle b^{*},\operatorname{ad}_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{r}}^{+}\dotsb\operatorname{ad}_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{P}_{a_{1}}^{+}(c)\right\rangle\frac{\xi_{a_{r}}^{m_{r}}\dotsb\xi_{a_{1}}^{m_{1}}}{m_{r}!\dotsb m_{1}!}u_{c},

(7.16)

summing over
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{1}<\dotsb<a_{r}<b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}
,
m_{1},\dotsc,m_{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
c\in\mathcal{E}\cup\{X_{0}\}
, with
u_{X_{0}}\equiv 1
.

•

For
b\in\{X_{0},X_{1}\}
, since
|b|=1
, the only non-zero term in (
7.16
) is
r=0
and
c=b
.

•

For
b=M_{\nu}
with
\nu\geq 1
, we have
n_{1}(M_{\nu})=1
.
Hence either (
r=0
and
c=M_{\nu}
) or (
r=1
,
m_{1}=1
,
a_{1}=M_{\nu-1}
and
c=X_{0}
).
Indeed, terms with
r\geq 2
or with
r=1
and
m_{1}\geq 2
contain at least two occurrences of
X_{1}
.

•

For
b=W_{j}
with
j\geq 1
, we have
n_{1}(W_{j})=2
and
|W_{j}|=2j+1
.

–

If
r=0
, then
c=W_{j}
, which gives the term
u_{W_{j}}
.

–

If
r=1
, we distinguish cases.

*

If
m_{1}=1
,
c=X_{0}
yields no contribution, so
c=M_{l}
and
a_{1}=M_{i}
with
M_{i}<M_{l}
.
Repeated Jacobi yields
\langle b^{*},[M_{i},M_{l}]\rangle=(-1)^{i-j+1}\mathbf{1}_{i+l=2j-1}
, which gives the sum in (
7.15
).

*

If
m_{1}=2
, then
a_{1}=M_{j-1}
, which gives the term
\frac{1}{2}\xi_{M_{j-1}}^{2}
.

–

If
r=2
, then we are considering a term of the form
[M_{i},[M_{l},X_{0}]]
with
M_{i}>M_{l}
and
M_{i}<(M_{l},X_{0})
, impossible by (
7.14
).∎

7.5
A positivity argument

By
Theorem

4.5
, a splitting method is a
u\in\mathfrak{D}
such that
S_{N}(1,X,u)=\exp(X_{0}+X_{1})
.
An explicit reference control
\underline{u}\in L^{1}
such that
S_{N}(1,X,\underline{u})=\exp(X_{0}+X_{1})
is
\underline{u}_{X_{1}}=1
and
\underline{u}_{b}=0
for
b\neq X_{1}
.
The following positivity argument will be used multiple times in the sequel.

Proposition 7.12
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{B}^{\star}\setminus\{X_{0}\}
such that
M_{N},\dotsc,M_{2N}\notin\mathcal{E}
and
W_{N}\notin\mathcal{E}
.
Let
u\in\mathfrak{D}
such that
\xi_{b}(1,u)=\xi_{b}(1,\underline{u})
for all
b\in\{M_{N},\dots,M_{2N}\}
.
Then

\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u)>\xi_{W_{N}}(1,\underline{u}).

(7.17)

Proof.

Let
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{E}|})
be an order-preserving regularization of
u
given by
Proposition

2.4
.
For all
b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{N}
and
t\in(0,1]
, by
Proposition

7.2
and
Definition

7.5
, one has
\xi_{b}(t,u^{\varepsilon})\to\xi_{b}(t,u)
.
Set

v^{\varepsilon}(t):=\xi_{M_{N-1}}(t,u^{\varepsilon}),\qquad v(t):=\xi_{M_{N-1}}(t,u),\qquad\underline{v}(t):=\xi_{M_{N-1}}(t,\underline{u})=\frac{t^{N}}{N!}.

(7.18)

Since
M_{N},\dots,M_{2N}\notin\mathcal{E}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
\nu\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, the equations (
7.15
) give

\delta_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}:=\xi_{M_{N+\nu}}(1,u^{\varepsilon})-\xi_{M_{N+\nu}}(1,\underline{u})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-t)^{\nu}}{\nu!}\bigl(v^{\varepsilon}(t)-\underline{v}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(7.19)

For each
\nu\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, since
\xi_{M_{N+\nu}}(1,u^{\varepsilon})\to\xi_{M_{N+\nu}}(1,u)=\xi_{M_{N+\nu}}(1,\underline{u})
, we have
\delta_{\nu}^{\varepsilon}\to 0
.
Since
\underline{v}(t)=t^{N}/N!
belongs to the span of the polynomials
(1-t)^{\nu}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}\underline{v}(t)\bigl(v^{\varepsilon}(t)-\underline{v}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t=o(1).

(7.20)

Since
M_{N},\dots,M_{2N-1},W_{N}\notin\mathcal{E}
, the formula (
7.15
) yields

\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u^{\varepsilon})-\xi_{W_{N}}(1,\underline{u})=\frac{1}{2}\|v^{\varepsilon}-\underline{v}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}+\int_{0}^{1}\underline{v}(t)\bigl(v^{\varepsilon}(t)-\underline{v}(t)\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}t.

(7.21)

Since
\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u^{\varepsilon})\to\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u)
and

v^{\varepsilon}(t)\to v(t)
for every
t\in(0,1]
, Fatou’s lemma gives

\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u)-\xi_{W_{N}}(1,\underline{u})\geq\frac{1}{2}\|v-\underline{v}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(7.22)

It remains to prove that
v\neq\underline{v}
in
L^{2}(0,1)
.
Since
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, let
I\subset(0,1)
be a nonempty interval containing no impulse time.
For
\varepsilon
small enough,
u^{\varepsilon}\equiv 0
on
I
, so
(v^{\varepsilon})^{(N)}\equiv 0
, so
v^{(N)}\equiv 0
.
On the contrary,
\underline{v}^{(N)}\equiv 1
.
Hence
v\neq\underline{v}
and
\xi_{W_{N}}(1,u)>\xi_{W_{N}}(1,\underline{u})
.
∎

7.6
Proof of the upper bound of
Theorem

1.18

Proof.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Assume that there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order
(2N+1)
involving
X_{0}
and
X_{1},W_{1},\dotsc,W_{N-1}
.
By
Proposition

7.6
, there exists
u\in\mathfrak{D}
such that, for all

with

, one has

.

High order methods relying on degeneracies

In this section the base field of all the vector spaces is
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.
Our goal is to prove
Theorem

1.17
.
A key ingredient is the error estimate associated with Sussmann’s product, presented in
Section

8.1
.
In this section, the estimates on products of flows have the same sense as in
Definition

1.5
.

8.1
Error estimate for Sussmann’s product

We consider the system (
1.5
) with scalar control
u
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Hall set,
(\xi_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
be the coordinates of the second kind associated with
\mathcal{B}
(see (
7.12
)),
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and

.
For every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

or
\mathfrak{D}
,

(8.1)

Proof.

We deduce from
[
3
, Proposition 104]
that
for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists
C=C(f,x_{0},N)
such that, for every
u\in L^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R})
and

small enough,

\left|x(1;Tf,u,x_{0})-\underset{b\in\mathcal{B}_{N}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{\xi_{b}(1,u)T^{|b|}f_{b}}x_{0}\right|\leq C\|u\|_{L^{1}}^{N+1}T^{N+1}.

(8.2)

For
u\in\mathfrak{D}
, we consider the regularization
u^{\varepsilon}\in L^{1}
of
Proposition

2.4
.
They satisfy
\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}}=O(1)
and
\xi_{b}(1,u^{\varepsilon})\to\xi_{b}(1,u)
as

and

.

) for

.
∎

8.2
Proof of the
Theorem

1.17

We first formulate a technical lemma.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
f_{1},g_{1},\dotsc,f_{N},g_{N}
be smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\underset{j\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j}f_{j}}=\underset{j\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j}g_{j}}+\underset{T\to 0}{O}(T^{N+1}).

(8.3)

Then,
f_{j}=g_{j}
for every
j\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
.

Proof.

We prove by induction on
k\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
that
f_{k}=g_{k}
.
Let
k\in\llbracket 1,N\rrbracket
.
Assume that
f_{j}=g_{j}
for
j<k
.
Then (
8.3
) implies

\underset{j\in\llbracket k,N\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j}f_{j}}=\underset{j\in\llbracket k,N\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j}g_{j}}+\underset{T\to 0}{O}(T^{N+1}).

(8.4)

By considering the Taylor expansion of (
8.4
), we obtain,
e^{T^{k}f_{k}}=e^{T^{k}g_{k}}+O(T^{k+1})
.
Moreover
(e^{T^{k}f_{k}}-e^{T^{k}g_{k}})=T^{k}(f_{k}-g_{k})+O(T^{k+1})
thus
f_{k}=g_{k}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.17
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and
f_{0},f_{1}
be smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
f_{W_{j}}=0
for any

.
We assume that there exists a splitting method of order
(2N+1)
relative to
(f_{0},f_{1})
.
Let
u\in\mathfrak{D}
be the associated control and
\underline{u}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
identically equal to

.
Then,

\forall x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad x(1;Tf,u,x_{0})=x(1;Tf,\underline{u},x_{0})+O(T^{2N+2}).

(8.5)

Consider the Hall set
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
, and
\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{2N+1}
its elements of length at most
2N+1
.
By
Proposition

8.1
, equation (

) implies that

\underset{b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{2N+1}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,u)f_{b}}=\underset{b\in\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{2N+1}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,\underline{u})f_{b}}+O(T^{2N+2}).

(8.6)

Let
\mathcal{R}_{N}:=\{X_{1},M_{1},\dots,M_{2N},W_{N},X_{0}\}
,
\mathcal{E}:=\{W_{1},\dots,W_{N-1}\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{E}}
the ideal of
\mathcal{L}_{2N+1}(X)
generated by
\mathcal{E}
.
For any
b\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{E}}
,
f_{b}=0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{B}^{\star}_{2N+1}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{N}\subset\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{E}}
.
Thus (
8.6
) implies

\underset{b\in\mathcal{R}_{N}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,u)f_{b}}=\underset{b\in\mathcal{R}_{N}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,\underline{u})f_{b}}+O(T^{2N+2}).

(8.7)

Since
X_{0}
is the maximal element of
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
(thus involved in the last term of both products) and

\underset{b\in\mathcal{R}_{N}^{\oslash}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,u)f_{b}}=\underset{b\in\mathcal{R}_{N}^{\oslash}}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{|b|}\xi_{b}(1,\underline{u})f_{b}}+O(T^{2N+2})

(8.8)

where
\mathcal{R}_{N}^{\oslash}:=\mathcal{R}_{N}\setminus\{X_{0}\}
.
From this estimate, using that
e^{\varepsilon g}e^{\varepsilon h}=e^{\varepsilon(g+h)}+O(\varepsilon^{2})
to gather the terms for

and

\underset{j\in\llbracket 0,2N-1\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j+1}\xi_{M_{j}}(1,u)f_{M_{j}}}e^{T^{2N+1}F_{u}}=\underset{j\in\llbracket 0,2N-1\rrbracket}{\overrightarrow{\prod}}e^{T^{j+1}\xi_{M_{j}}(1,\underline{u})f_{M_{j}}}e^{T^{2N+1}F_{\underline{u}}}+O(T^{2N+2})

where

.
By

, this implies

\forall j\in\llbracket 0,2N-1\rrbracket,\qquad(\xi_{M_{j}}(1,u)-\xi_{M_{j}}(1,\underline{u}))f_{M_{j}}=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad F_{u}=F_{\underline{u}}

If there exists

Otherwise, we deduce from (

and

8.3
Using commutator flows versus using degeneracies

As highlighted in the introduction, one must distinguish between two related but different notions: using commutator flows to obtain a method valid for all systems (for which one can compute these flows) as in
Section

6
, or using degeneracies to obtain a method valid only for systems exhibiting these as in
Section

8
.
With our vocabulary, one has the following result.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subset\operatorname{Br}(X)\setminus\{X_{0}\}

If there exists an
(\mathbb{R}^{+},\mathbb{R})
splitting method of order

involving
X_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}
, then there exists a splitting method of order
N
involving only
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
, relative to the set

.
The reciprocal is false.

Proof.

The direct implication is straightforward.
If one assumes that
f_{b}=0
for all

, the associated flows satisfy

, so they disappear from the splitting method involving

and the elements of
\mathcal{E}
.

A counterexample to the reciprocal is given by the case

, we know a splitting method of order

However, there does not exist a splitting method of order

and
X_{1}
,
M_{1}
.

Indeed, let us assume that such a method exists.
By
Proposition

7.6
, there exists a control

such that

for every

, where the

are the coordinates of the second kind associated with
\mathcal{B}^{\star}
,
\mathcal{E}
and
\underline{u}=(\underline{u}_{X_{1}},\underline{u}_{M_{1}})=(1,0)

Since

∎

(A.1)

Swapping

Thus

.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let

be an open neighborhood of
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\mathcal{F}
a family of smooth vector fields on
V
.
Assume that
(\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{F})(x_{0})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then, for all
T>0
, there exist
f_{1},\dots,f_{d}\in\mathcal{F}
,
and an open subset
\Omega\subset(0,T)^{d}
such that the map

\Phi:\mathfrak{t}=(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{d})\mapsto e^{t_{d}f_{d}}\dotsb e^{t_{1}f_{1}}(x_{0})

(A.3)

is a smooth embedding of
\Omega
into
V
.
In particular,
D\Phi
has rank
d
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

Choose

such that

is a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By continuity, there exists an open neighborhood

of

such that, for every

are still linearly independent, hence

.

We prove by induction on

the following statement:

There exist

and an open subset
\Omega_{k}\subset(0,T)^{k}
such that the map
\Phi_{k}:\mathfrak{t}=(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{k})\mapsto e^{t_{k}f_{k}}\dotsb e^{t_{1}f_{1}}(x_{0})
is a smooth embedding of
\Omega_{k}
into

.

Initialization:

.

First, there exists
f_{1}\in\mathcal{F}
such that

.
Otherwise, all elements of
\operatorname{Lie}\mathcal{F}
would vanish at

, contradicting

.
Choosing such an

is well-defined and smooth on a neighborhood of

.
Since

is an embedding.

Assume the statement proved for some

Since

Exact and approximate local controllability

We prove the equivalence of small-time local exact and approximate controllability, with controls in
\mathcal{U}=\mathfrak{D}
or
\mathcal{U}=L^{1}
, under the Lie algebra rank condition assumption.

Proof of
Proposition

2.5
for
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\mathcal{U}=\mathfrak{D}
or
\mathcal{U}=L^{1}
.
Assume that small-time local approximate
\mathcal{U}
-controllability holds and fix
T>0
.
Let
\delta>0
such that
B(0,\delta)\subset\overline{R_{T/3}(\mathcal{U})}
.

Since the Lie algebra rank condition
(\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f_{0},\dotsc,f_{m}\right\})(0)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds, by
Proposition

A.2
, for any
\eta>0
there exist
g_{1},\dots,g_{d}\in\{f_{0},\dots,f_{m}\}
and an open subset
\Omega\subset(0,\eta)^{d}
such that the endpoint map
\Phi(\mathfrak{t}):=e^{-t_{d}g_{d}}\dotsb e^{-t_{1}g_{1}}(0)
is a smooth embedding of
\Omega
into
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Up to reducing
\eta
, we can assume that
\eta<T/(3d)
and that
\Phi(\Omega)\subset B(0,\delta)
.

Using the approximate controllability, there exists
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\bar{x}:=x(\frac{T}{3};f,\bar{u},0)\in\Phi(\Omega)
.
Thus, there exists a unique
\bar{\mathfrak{t}}\in\Omega
such that
\bar{x}=\Phi(\bar{\mathfrak{t}})
, meaning
e^{\bar{t}_{1}g_{1}}\dotsb e^{\bar{t}_{d}g_{d}}(\bar{x})=0
.

For
\varepsilon\in[0,\eta)
and
\mathfrak{t}\in\Omega
, define

\Psi_{\mathcal{U}}(\varepsilon,\mathfrak{t}):=\begin{cases}e^{\varepsilon f_{0}}e^{t_{1}g_{1}}\dotsb e^{t_{d}g_{d}}(\bar{x})&\text{if }\mathcal{U}=\mathfrak{D},\\
e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+t_{1}g_{1}}\dotsb e^{\varepsilon f_{0}+t_{d}g_{d}}(\bar{x})&\text{if }\mathcal{U}=L^{1}.\end{cases}

(A.6)

Since
\bar{x}\in R_{T/3}(\mathcal{U})
and
\eta<T/(3d)
, using that
e^{\tau f_{0}}(0)=0
for any
\tau\geq 0
(since
f_{0}(0)=0
), we obtain by construction that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\eta)
and
\mathfrak{t}\in\Omega
,
\Psi_{\mathcal{U}}(\varepsilon,\mathfrak{t})\in R_{T}(\mathcal{U})
.

Let
P(\mathfrak{t},y):=e^{t_{1}g_{1}}\dotsb e^{t_{d}g_{d}}(y)
.
Since
P(\mathfrak{t},\Phi(\mathfrak{t}))=0
, we have
D_{\mathfrak{t}}P(\bar{\mathfrak{t}},\bar{x})+D_{y}P(\bar{\mathfrak{t}},\bar{x})D_{\mathfrak{t}}\Phi(\bar{\mathfrak{t}})=0
.

D_{\mathfrak{t}}\Phi(\bar{\mathfrak{t}})
is an isomorphism because
\Phi
is a smooth embedding by
Proposition

A.2
.

D_{y}P(\bar{\mathfrak{t}},\bar{x})
is an isomorphism because
P(\bar{\mathfrak{t}},\cdot)
is a diffeomorphism.
Thus
D_{\mathfrak{t}}P(\bar{\mathfrak{t}},\bar{x})=D_{\mathfrak{t}}\Psi_{\mathcal{U}}(0,\bar{\mathfrak{t}})

Hence, by the implicit function theorem, for
\varepsilon>0
and
x^{*}
small enough, there exists
\mathfrak{t}\in\Omega
such that
\Psi_{\mathcal{U}}(\varepsilon,\mathfrak{t})=x^{*}
.
Hence
0\in\operatorname{int}R_{T}(\mathcal{U})
and small-time local exact
\mathcal{U}
-controllability holds.
∎

A.3
The tangent vectors method

We recall a classical method to prove the controllability of control-affine systems, relying on so-called “tangent vectors” (see
[
24
,
32
]
for early occurrences, and
[
38
, Section 4]
for an in-depth presentation).
A light version of this method combines elementary concatenation estimates (see

A.4
) with a Brouwer fixed-point argument (see

A.5
).

Lemma A.4
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
be smooth vector fields on a neighborhood of
0
in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
with
f_{0}(0)=0
.
There exist
C,\delta,r>0
such that, for every
x_{0}\in B(0,r)
,
u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{+};\mathbb{K}^{m})
and

such that

, the solution to (
2.1
) satisfies

|x(t;f,u,x_{0})-x_{0}-x(t;f,u,0)|\leq C|x_{0}|(t+\|u\|_{L^{1}}).

(A.7)

Moreover, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there exists
C_{n}>0
such that, given times

\big|x(T;f,u,0)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}x(T_{j};f,u_{j},0)\big|\leq C_{n}(T+\|u\|_{L^{1}})\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|x(T_{j};f,u_{j},0)|.

Proof.

The first estimate follows from Grönwall’s lemma.
It entails the second one by induction.

Proposition A.5
.

m\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

•

A.4

A.4.1
Lie–Trotter product formula

Lemma A.6
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{K}^{d};\mathbb{K}^{d})
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
such that the solution to
\dot{x}=f(x)
with
x(0)=x_{0}
is well-defined on
[0,1]
.
Let
(\Phi_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a family of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
maps on
\mathbb{K}^{d}
, such that, for some
\sigma\in(0,1]
, uniformly on a closed ball containing
x([0,1])
in its interior,

\Phi_{t}(y)=y+tf(y)+O(t^{1+\sigma})

(A.15)

Then, as
N\to+\infty
,

\left|\Phi_{\frac{1}{N}}^{N}(x_{0})-e^{f}(x_{0})\right|=O\left(N^{-\sigma}\right).

(A.16)

Proof.

Let
K
denote the assumed closed ball containing the full trajectory
x([0,1])
in its interior.
By assumption, and regularity of
f
, there exists
C,T>0
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
y,y^{\prime}\in K
,

|\Phi_{t}(y)-e^{tf}(y)|\leq Ct^{1+\sigma}\quad\text{and}\quad|e^{tf}(y)-e^{tf}(y^{\prime})|\leq(1+Ct)|y-y^{\prime}|

(A.17)

Let
y_{0}:=x_{0}
.
For
0\leq k<N
, let
x_{k+1}:=x((k+1)/N)=e^{f/N}(x_{k})
and
y_{k+1}:=\Phi_{1/N}(y_{k})
.
We want to prove that
y_{N}\to x_{N}=x(1)
.
As long as
y_{k}\in K
, we have

|y_{k+1}-x_{k+1}|\leq\left(1+\frac{C}{N}\right)|y_{k}-x_{k}|+\frac{C}{N^{1+\sigma}}.

(A.18)

Hence
\varepsilon_{k}:=(1+C/N)^{-k}|y_{k}-x_{k}|
satisfies
\varepsilon_{0}=0
and
\varepsilon_{k+1}\leq\varepsilon_{k}+C/N^{1+\sigma}
so
\varepsilon_{k}\leq Ck/N^{1+\sigma}
.
Thus
|y_{k}-x_{k}|\leq\frac{C}{N^{\sigma}}(1+\frac{C}{N})^{N}\leq\frac{Ce^{C}}{N^{\sigma}}
.
In particular
y_{k}
stays in
K
and the estimate closes.
∎

A.4.2
Bernoulli numbers

Definition A.8
.

We use the notation
(B_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to denote the Bernoulli numbers, which are defined (using the modern NIST sign and indexing convention) by the identity

\forall z\in\mathbb{C},|z|<2\pi,\qquad\frac{z}{e^{z}-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}B_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=1-\frac{z}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}B_{2n}\frac{z^{2n}}{(2n)!}.

(A.21)

Lemma A.9
.

The Bernoulli numbers satisfy, for every
n\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}B_{k}

\displaystyle=0,

(A.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{B_{k}}{n+1-k}

\displaystyle=0.

(A.23)

Proof.

These can be proved using the generating series (
A.21
) of the Bernoulli numbers, by identification in
z=(e^{z}-1)\times(z/(e^{z}-1))
for (
A.22
) and in
1=((e^{z}-1)/z)\times(z/(e^{z}-1))
for (
A.23
).
∎

Lemma A.10
.

Let
T>0
and
z\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];{\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)})
. Then, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\exp(z(t))=\exp(z(t))\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)!}\operatorname{ad}^{n}_{z(t)}(\dot{z}(t)),

(A.24)

\dot{z}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}B_{k}}{k!}\operatorname{ad}^{k}_{z(t)}\left(\exp(-z(t))\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\exp(z(t))\right)

(A.25)

Proof.

The regularity assumption
z\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X))
is to be understood for projections on the finite dimensional spaces
\mathcal{A}^{n}(X)
.
We have

\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\exp(z(t))&=\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{z^{k}(t)}{k!}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}z^{j}(t)\dot{z}(t)z^{k-j}(t)\\
&=\exp(z(t))\left(\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}}{l!}z^{l}(t)\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(k+1)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}z^{j}(t)\dot{z}(t)z^{k-j}(t)\right).\end{split}

(A.26)

Letting
n:=k+l
and
i:=l+j
, we obtain that

\begin{split}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\exp(z(t))&=\exp(z(t))\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}z^{i}(t)\dot{z}(t)z^{n-i}(t)\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{l}\binom{n+1}{l}\end{split}

(A.27)

The following formulas, which can be proved by induction using Pascal’s rule,

\displaystyle\sum_{l=0}^{i}(-1)^{l}\binom{n+1}{l}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i},

(A.28)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}z^{i}yz^{n-i}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\operatorname{ad}^{n}_{z}(y)

(A.29)

give the conclusion.
Of course, if
z\in W^{1,1}((0,T);\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X))
(i.e. absolutely continuous), equation (
A.24
) remains true as an equality in
L^{1}((0,T);\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X))
, i.e. holding for almost every
t\in(0,T)
.

From the change of index
n=k+\ell
and the combinatorial relation (
A.23
) we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}B_{k}}{k!}\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}}{(\ell+1)!}\operatorname{ad}^{k+\ell}_{z(t)}(\dot{z}(t))=\dot{z}(t),

(A.30)

which implies (
A.25
).
∎

Proof.

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}(X)
:

\exp(a)b\exp(-a)=\left(\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{q!}a^{q}\right)b\left(\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{p}}{p!}a^{p}\right).

(A.32)

Using the change of index
m=p+q
, we deduce

\exp(a)b\exp(-a)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{p=0}^{m}(-1)^{p}\frac{m!}{(m-p)!p!}a^{m-p}ba^{p}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{m}(b),

(A.33)

which is the claimed equality.
∎

Lemma A.12
.

If
f,g
are smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{K}^{d}
such that
\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f,g\right\}
is nilpotent of step
N
, as long as the flows are defined, one has

e^{-f}e^{g}e^{f}=e^{h}\qquad\text{ where }\qquad h=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{k!}\operatorname{ad}_{f}^{k}(g).

(A.34)

Proof.

For any
a,b\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, from
Lemma

A.11
, we obtain

\exp(a)\exp(b)\exp(-a)=\exp(\exp(a)b\exp(-a))=\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{k}(b)\right).

(A.35)

Thus, for any
a,b\in\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
, we have the equality in
\mathcal{A}_{N}(X)
:

\operatorname{BCH}_{N}(\operatorname{BCH}_{N}(a,b),-a)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{k!}\operatorname{ad}_{a}^{k}(b).

(A.36)

Hence the conclusion follows from
Proposition

A.13
.
∎

A.4.4
The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula

Proposition A.13
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
There exists a Lie polynomial
\operatorname{BCH}_{N}
in two indeterminates
A
and
B
such that, for any
A,B\in\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
, the following equality holds in
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
:

\operatorname{BCH}_{N}(A,B)=\log\left(\exp(A)\exp(B)\right)=A+B+\text{finite sum of brackets of $A$ and $B$.}

(A.37)

Moreover, if
f
and
g
are smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{K}^{d}
such that
\operatorname{Lie}\left\{f,g\right\}
is nilpotent of step
N
, as long as the flows are defined, one has

e^{g}e^{f}=e^{\operatorname{BCH}_{N}(f,g)}.

(A.38)

Proof.

The first statement is merely the historical Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula.
The consequence for nilpotent vector fields is proved in
[
31
, Remark A.1]
for analytic vector fields, and in
[
3
, Section 5.2.1]
without analyticity.
∎
