Finite difference method on flat surfaces with a flat unitary vector bundle.
Siarhei Finski

Abstract




Abstract.

We establish an asymptotic relation between the spectrum of the discrete Laplacian associated to discretizations of a half-translation surface with a flat unitary vector bundle and the spectrum of the Friedrichs extension of the Laplacian with von Neumann boundary conditions.

As an interesting byproduct of our study, we obtain Harnack-type estimates on “almost harmonic” discrete functions, defined on the graphs, which approximate a given surface.

The results of this paper will be later used to relate the asymptotic expansion of the number of spanning trees, spanning forests and weighted cycle-rooted spanning forests on the discretizations to the corresponding zeta-regularized determinants.

Introduction

Many natural combinatorial invariants of a finite graph
G
can be expressed through the spectrum
{\rm{Spec}}(\Delta_{G})
of the combinatorial Laplacian
\Delta_{G}
, defined as the difference between degree and adjacency operators. For example, the number of connected components corresponds to the multiplicity of 0 in
{\rm{Spec}}(\Delta_{G})
.
The largest eigenvalue of
\Delta_{G}
in a regular graph
G
is twice the degree of the vertices if and only the graph is bipartite.
The smallest positive eigenvalue of
{\rm{Spec}}(\Delta_{G})
is related to the Cheeger isoperimetric constant, which detects “bottlenecks”, see Cheeger
[
3
]
.

According to a famous Matrix-tree theorem of Kirchoff, the product of non-zero eigenvalues of
\Delta_{G}
corresponds to the number of marked spanning trees on
G
. This theorem has been generalized by Forman in
[
9
, Theorem 1]
to the setting of a unitary line bundle on a graph and by Kenyon in
[
12
, Theorems 8,9]
to vector bundles of rank
2
endowed with
{\rm{SL}}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-connections (we give a precise meaning for those notions in Section
2.1
).
They showed that a sum of cycle-rooted spanning forests (cf.
[
12
, §4]
for related definitions), weighted by some function depending on the monodromy of the connection, calculated over the cycle, can be expressed through the determinant of the Laplacian associated with the vector bundle (see (
1.1
)).

Now, if instead of considering a single graph
G
with a vector bundle over it, we consider a family
\Psi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
of graphs endowed with vector bundles
F_{n}
, constructed as approximations of a certain surface
\Psi
with a flat unitary vector bundle
F
, we could ask ourselves how the spectral invariants of
(\Psi_{n},F_{n})
would behave, as
n\to\infty
.

Such families of graphs arise naturally in many contexts in mathematics, when one studies some continuous quantity or process by using scaling limits of grid-based approximations.

The surfaces we consider in this article are called
half-translation
surfaces
(\Psi,g^{T\Psi})
. This means that the metric
g^{T\Psi}
over
\Psi
is flat and has conical singularities
{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)
of angles
k\pi
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}\setminus\{2\}
, see Figure
1
for an example. The name half-translation comes from the fact that it has a ramified double cover, which is a translation surface, i.e. it can be obtained by identifying parallel sides in some polygonal domain in
\mathbb{C}
(see Section
2.1
).

Analysis on flat surfaces

In this section we recall some results about functional analysis on flat surfaces and introduce the main objects of this article. More precisely, in Section
2.1
, we recall the basics of flat surfaces, pillowcase covers, and we briefly introduce the main notions on vector bundles over graphs.
In Section
2.2
, we define the Friedrichs extension of the Laplacian and study some of its spectral properties.
Finally, in Section
2.3
, we give an alternative description of the domain of the Friedrichs extension by describing the singularities of the functions from it.

2.1
Pillowcase covers and their discretizations

Here we recall the definition of flat surfaces, pillowcase covers, explain some properties of discretizations of pillowcase covers and give a short introduction to vector bundles over graphs.

By Gauss-Bonnet theorem, the only closed Riemann surface admitting a flat metric has the topology of the torus.
However, any Riemann surface can be endowed with a flat metric having a finite number of cone-type singularities.
Let’s explain this point more precise.

A cone-type singularity is a Riemannian metric

ds^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}dt^{2},

(2.1)

on the manifold

C_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(r,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}r>0;t\in\real/\theta\mathbb{Z}\},

(2.2)

where
\theta>0
.
In what follows, when we speak of cones, we assume
\theta\neq 2\pi
.
By a
flat metric with a finite number of cone-type singularities
we mean a metric defined away from a finite set of points such that there is an atlas for which the metric looks either like the standard metric on
2
, or like the conical metric (
2.1
) on an open subset of (
2.2
).

Let
M
be a compact surface endowed with a flat metric with cone-type singularities of angles
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
at points
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
.
By Gauss-Bonnet theorem (cf. Troyanov
[
16
, Proposition 3]
):

\sum_{i=1}^{m}(2\pi-\theta_{i})=2\pi\chi(M),

(2.3)

where
\chi(M)
is the Euler characteristic of
M
.
Although we will not need this in what follows, by a theorem of Troyanov
[
16
, Théorème, §5]
, this is the only obstruction for the existence of a metric with cone-type singularities of angles
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
at points
P_{1},\ldots,P_{m}
in a conformal class of
M
.

In this article we are primarily interested in compact surfaces endowed with a metric with a finite number of cone-type singularities of angles
\pi k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Those Riemann surfaces can also be described by possession of an atlas, defined away from the singularities of the metric, such that the transition maps between charts are given by
z\to\pm z+{\rm{const}}
(cf.
[
17
, §3.3]
).
In literature, such surfaces are called
half-translation surfaces
.
In case if all the angles are of the form
2\pi k
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, it can be proven that the atlas can be chosen in such a way so that the transition maps between charts are given by
z\to z+{\rm{const}}
, and the surface in this case is called a
translation surface
.
Clearly, from any closed half-translation surface, we can construct a translation surface as a double cover, ramified over the conical points with angles
(2k+1)\pi
,
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

We denote by
{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)
the conical points of the surface
\Psi
, and by
{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
the points where two different smooth components of the boundary meet (corners).
We denote by
\angle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\to\real
the function which associates to a conical point its angle and by
\angle\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)\to\real
the function which associates the interior angle between the smooth components of the boundary.

It is easy to see that for a half-translation surface, a notion of a
straight line
makes sense, and for translation surfaces, even a notion of a
ray
(or a
direction
) is well-defined.

There is an alternative description of closed
half-translation surfaces
with prescribed line (resp.
translation surfaces
with prescribed direction) in terms of Riemann surfaces endowed with a meromorphic quadratic differential with at most simple poles (resp. a holomorphic differential). The zeros
1

1

-1

of order

in this description correspond to the conical points with angles
(k+2)\pi
(resp.
2\pi(k+1)
). In this description, one could easily identify the moduli spaces of closed half-translation surfaces with line (resp. translation surfaces with direction) with corresponding Hodge bundles on the moduli of curves (cf.
[

). The orders of zeros of a meromorphic quadratic differential (resp. of a holomorphic differential) induce stratifications of those moduli spaces.

For the most part of this paper, we will be interested in considering special type of half-translation surfaces which are called
pillowcase covers
. Those are surfaces with a fixed tiling by squares of equal sizes.
In case if the surface has no boundary, those surfaces can be thought as rational points in so-called period coordinates (cf.
[
17
, §7.1]
) inside the respective stratas of the moduli space, and thus, the set of pillowcase covers form a dense subset of the corresponding moduli spaces (similarly to the set of tori with rational periods inside the moduli space of tori).

, the edges of
\Psi_{n}
have at most double multiplicity, see Figure
4
.
Moreover, the number of edges with double multiplicity is equal to
\#\{P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\angle(P)=\pi\}
. We are, thus, working with multigraphs, but by abuse of notation, we call them graphs.

Figure 4:
An example of multiple edges in a discretization. The surface is obtained by identifying the edges along the directed lines. The edges of the graph are dotted.

Now, by a
vector bundle

V
on a graph
G=(V(G),E(G))
, we mean the choice of a vector space
V_{v}
for any
v\in V(G)
so that for any
v,v^{\prime}\in V(G)
, the vector spaces
V_{v}
and
V_{v^{\prime}}
are isomorphic.
The set of
sections

{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)
of
V
is defined by

{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)=\oplus_{v\in V(G)}V_{v}.

(2.7)

A
connection

\nabla^{V}
on a vector bundle
V
is the choice for each edge
e=(v,v^{\prime})\in E(G)
of an isomorphism
\phi_{vv^{\prime}}
between the corresponding vector spaces
\phi_{vv^{\prime}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V_{v}\to V_{v^{\prime}}
, with the property that
\phi_{vv^{\prime}}=\phi_{v^{\prime}v}^{-1}
. This isomorphism is called the
parallel transport
of vectors in
V_{v}
to vectors in
V_{v^{\prime}}
.

A
Hermitian metric

h^{V}
on the vector bundle
V
is a choice of a positive-definite Hermitian metric
h_{v}
on
V_{v}
for each
v\in V(G)
. We say that a connection
\nabla^{V}
is unitary with respect to
h^{V}
if its parallel transports preserve
h^{V}
.

The
Laplacian

\Delta_{G}^{V}
on
(V,\nabla^{V})
is the linear operator
\Delta_{G}^{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)\to{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)
, defined for
f\in{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)
by (
1.1
).
Remark that unlike Laplace–Beltrami operator on a smooth manifold, we don’t use the metric to define the Laplacian (
1.1
).

Consequently, in general, the operator
\Delta_{G}^{V}
is not self-adjoint, see for example
[
12
, §3.2 and equation (1)]
.
However, if one assumes that the connection is unitary with respect to
h^{V}
, then it becomes self-adjoint (cf. Kenyon
[
12
, §3.3]
).

More precisely, one can extend the definition of a vector bundle to the edges of
G
.
A vector bundle
V^{\prime}
over
V(G)\oplus E(G)
is a choice of a vector spaces
V_{e}
for each edge
e\in E(G)
as well as
V_{v}
for each vertex
v\in V(G)
.
A connection
\nabla^{V^{\prime}}
on
V^{\prime}
is a choice of a connection
\nabla^{V}
on
V
as well as connection isomorphisms
\phi_{ve}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V_{v}\to V_{e}
,
\phi_{ev}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}V_{e}\to V_{v}
for each
v\in V(G)
and
e\in E(G)
, and satisfying
\phi_{ve}=\phi_{ev}^{-1}
and

\phi_{vv^{\prime}}=\phi_{ev^{\prime}}\circ\phi_{ve}
.
Similarly to
{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)
, we defined the set of sections of
{\rm{Map}}(E(G),V)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\oplus_{e\in E(G)}V_{e}
.

Quite easily, for any vector bundle
V
and a connection
\nabla^{V}
on
V(G)
, we may extend it to a vector bundle
V^{\prime}
and a connection
\nabla^{V^{\prime}}
on
E(G)\oplus V(G)
.
Note, however, that such a choice would not be unique.
If the initial vector bundle
V
is endowed with a Hermitian metric
h^{V}
, for which the connection
\nabla^{V}
is unitary, then one might endow the vector bundles
V_{e}
,
e\in E(G)
with metrics and choose the connections
\phi_{ev}
,
e\in E(G)
,
v\in V(G)
so that
\nabla^{V^{\prime}}
is unitary as well.

There is a natural map
\nabla^{V}_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)\to{\rm{Map}}(E(G),V)
, defined as follows

(\nabla^{V}_{G}f)(e)=\phi_{t(e)e}f(t(e))-\phi_{h(e)e}f(h(e)),\qquad f\in{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V),

(2.8)

where
t(e)
and
h(e)
are tail and head respectively of an oriented edge
e
.
We also define the operator
(\nabla^{V}_{G})^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Map}}(E(G),V)\to{\rm{Map}}(V(G),V)
by the formula

((\nabla^{V}_{G})^{*}f)(v)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(G)\\
t(e)=v\end{subarray}}\phi_{ev}f(e).

(2.9)

It is an easy verification (cf. Kenyon
[
12
, §3.3]
) that for the Laplacian, defined by (
1.1
), we have

\Delta_{G}^{V}=(\nabla^{V}_{G})^{*}\nabla^{V}_{G}.

(2.10)

Note, however, that in general
(\nabla^{V}_{G})^{*}
is not the adjoint of
\nabla^{V}_{G}
with respect to the appropriate
L^{2}
-metrics.
But if the connection
\nabla^{V}
is unitary, it is indeed the case, cf.
[
12
, §3.3]
.

In this article, all our connections are unitary, and thus, by (
2.10
), the associated discrete Laplacians are self-adjoint and positive.

2.2
Properties of Friedrichs extension of the Laplacian

In this section we study the Laplacian
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
associated to a flat surface
\Psi
with conical singularities and piecewise geodesic boundary endowed with a flat unitary vector bundle
(F,h^{F},\nabla^{F})
over
\overline{\Psi}
, (
1.2
).
The content of this section is certainly not new, but we weren’t able to find a complete reference for all the results contained here.

We consider
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
as an operator acting on the functional space
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
, where

\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\Big\{f\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}\big(\Psi\setminus{\rm{Ang}(\Psi)}\big)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\nabla_{n}f=0\text{ over }\partial\Psi\Big\},

(2.11)

where
n
is the normal to
\partial\Psi
.
Unlike in the case of a manifold with smooth boundary, the operator
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
is in general not essentially self-adjoint.

Let
{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
denote the maximal closure of
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
. In other words, for
u\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
, we have
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
if and only if
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
, where
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u
is viewed as a current.

Let’s denote by
W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)
the Sobolev space on
\Psi
, defined as

W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)=\Big\{u\in L^{p}(\Psi,F)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\nabla^{l}u\in L^{p}(\Psi,F)\quad\text{ for }\quad 0\leq l\leq k\Big\}.

(2.12)

We denote by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)}
the norm on
W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)
, given for
u\in W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)
by

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}_{W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)}=\sum_{0\leq l\leq k}\big\|\nabla^{l}u\big\|_{L^{p}(\Psi,F)}.

(2.13)

We use the following shorthand notations
H^{k}(\Psi,F)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=W^{k}_{2}(\Psi,F)
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{H^{k}(\Psi,F)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{W^{k}_{2}(\Psi,F)}
.

Theorem 2.2

(Rellich-Kondrachov)
.

The inclusion
H^{1}(\Psi,F)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Psi,F)
is compact.

Proof.

The mentioned inclusion is trivially continuous. Let’s prove that it is compact.

Geometrically it is trivial that one can choose a finite number of functions
f_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Psi\to\{0,1\}
,
i\in I
such that for any
x\in\Psi
away from a negligible set, there exists exactly one
i\in I
such that
f_{i}(x)=1
, and, for any
i\in I
,
U_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}={\rm{supp}}(f_{i})
is isomorphic to a subdomain of
\mathbb{C}
with piecewise smooth boundary.
In particular, in the notations of Adams
[
1
, p. 66]
,
U_{i}
,
i\in I
, satisfy the cone property.
By
[
1
, Theorem 6.2, Page 144]
, Rellich-Kondrachov theorem holds for any subsets of
\mathbb{C}
, satisfying the cone property. Thus, the inclusions
H^{1}(U_{i})\hookrightarrow L^{2}(U_{i})
are compact for any
i\in I
.
Note that we assumed that
(F,h^{F},\nabla^{F})
is flat unitary, and thus we can trivialize it over any star-like domain. In other words, we may interpret the section of
F
over
U_{i}
as functions.
Thus, the inclusions
H^{1}(U_{i},F)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(U_{i},F)
are compact as well.

Now, let
u_{j}
,
j\in J
be some bounded sequence in
H^{1}(\Psi,F)
.
For
i\in I
, consider a sequence
f_{i}u_{j}\in H^{1}(U_{i},F)
,
j\in J
. Since the set
I
is finite, by the mentioned version of Rellich-Kondrachov theorem, one could choose a subsequence of
u_{k}
,
k\in K\subset J
such that for any
i\in I
, the sequence
f_{i}u_{j}
,
j\in K
converges in
L^{2}(U_{i},F)
.
However, by the choice of
f_{i}
, the following identity

u_{k}=\sum_{i\in I}f_{i}u_{k}

(2.14)

holds in
L^{2}(\Psi,F)
. Thus, the sequence
u_{k}
,
k\in K
converges in
L^{2}(\Psi,F)
, and as a consequence, the inclusion
H^{1}(\Psi,F)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Psi,F)
is compact.
∎

For any positive symmetric operator, one can construct in a canonical way a self-adjoint extension, called Friedrichs extensions, through the completion of the associated quadratic form, cf.
[
15
, Theorem X.23]
.
Once the definition is unraveled, the domain
{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
of the Friedrichs extensions of the Laplacian
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
on
\Psi
with von Neumann boundary conditions on
\partial\Psi
is given by

{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})={\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})\cap H^{1}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F),

(2.15)

where
H^{1}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
is the closure of
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
in
H^{1}(\Psi,F)
.
The value of the Friedrichs extensions of the Laplacian
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
on
f\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
is defined in the distributional sense. By the definition of
{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
, it lies in
L^{2}(\Psi,F)
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The spectrum of
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
is discrete.

Proof.

Since the Friedrichs extension is non-negative by construction (cf.
[
15
, Theorem X.23]
), the kernel of
1+\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
is empty.
Thus, the inverse
(1+\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})^{-1}
is well-defined. By (
2.15
), the image of
(1+\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})^{-1}
lies in
H^{1}(\Psi,F)
.
By this and Theorem
2.2
, we deduce that
(1+\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})^{-1}
is a compact operator.
In particular, it has a discrete spectrum with only one possible accumulation point at
0
, which of course implies that the spectrum of
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
is discrete.
∎

Now, recall that for a smooth codimension
1
submanifold
\Gamma\subset\Psi
, transversal to the boundary
\partial\Psi
, and
k\geq\frac{1}{p}
, the
trace
(or restriction) operator

W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)\to W^{k-\frac{1}{p}}_{p}(\Gamma,F),\qquad\qquad f\mapsto f|_{\Gamma},

(2.16)

is well-defined, cf.
[
10
, Theorem 1.5.1.1]
.
In the following proposition and after, we apply (
2.16
) implicitly when we mention an integration over
\Gamma
of a function from
W^{k}_{p}(\Psi,F)
,
k\geq\frac{1}{p}
.

Proposition 2.4

(Green’s identity)
.

For any open subset
U\subset\Psi
with piecewise smooth boundary
\partial U
not passing through
{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)
and
{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
, and any
u,v\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
, we have

\big<\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u,v\big>_{L^{2}(U,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}v\big>_{L^{2}(U,F)}-\int_{\partial U}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial U},

(2.17)

where
n
is the outward normal to the boundary
\partial U
, and to simplify the notations, we omit the pointwise scalar product induced by
h^{F}
in the last integral.

Remark 2.5
.

For
u,v\in{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
, the statement (
2.17
) is false. For example, take a trivial vector bundle, and for a radial function
r
, centered in some conical singularity, take
u=1
and
v=\log(r)
, multiplied by some bump function away from the singularity.

Proof.

First of all, let’s explain why all the terms on the right-hand side of (
2.17
) are well-defined.
We fix a smooth neighborhood
V
of
\partial U
with smooth boundary
\partial V
such that
V\cap({\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi))=\emptyset
.
Since
V
is a smooth domain, from elliptic estimates and the fact that
u,\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
, we deduce that the restriction
u|_{V}
of
u
to
V
, viewed as a distribution, lies in
H^{2}(W,F)
.
Thus by the trace theorem (
2.16
), for the normal
n
of
\partial U
, we have
\nabla_{n}^{F}u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial U,F)
and
v\in H^{\frac{3}{2}}(\partial U,F)
.
In particular, the integral
\int_{\partial U}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial U}
is well-defined.
By (
2.15
), we see that the product
\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}v\big>_{L^{2}(U,F)}
is well defined as well.

Now, let’s prove (
2.17
) for
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
and
v\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
.
Let
W\subset\Psi
,
W\cap({\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi))=\emptyset
be an open set with smooth boundary
\partial W
, such that

{\rm{supp}}(v)\subset W.

(2.18)

As before, we deduce that the restriction
u|_{W}
of
u
to
W
lies inside of
H^{2}(W,F)
.
By Grisvard
[
10
, Theorem 1.5.3.3]
, we see that

\big<\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u,v\big>_{L^{2}(W,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}v\big>_{L^{2}(W,F)}-\int_{\partial W}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial W}.

(2.19)

Now, by (
2.18
) and the fact that
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
, we deduce

\big<\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u,v\big>_{L^{2}(W,F)}=\big<\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u,v\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}.

(2.20)

By (
2.18
) and the fact that
u\in H^{1}(\Psi,F)
, we deduce

\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}v\big>_{L^{2}(W,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}v\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}.

(2.21)

By (
2.18
), the fact that
u\in H^{2}(W,F)
, thus,
\nabla_{n}^{F}u\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial W,F)\subset L^{2}(\partial W,F)
, and the identity
\nabla_{n}u=0
over
\partial\Psi
, we deduce that

\int_{\partial W}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial W}=\int_{\partial U}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial U}.

(2.22)

From (
2.19
)-(
2.22
), we conclude that (
2.17
) holds for
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
and
v\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
.

Now, let’s argue why (
2.17
) also holds for
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
and
v\in H^{1}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
. Indeed, by definition of the space
H^{1}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
, there is a sequence
v_{m}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
such that
v_{m}\to v
in
H^{1}(\Psi,F)
. Then, since
\nabla_{n}^{F}u\in L^{2}(\partial U,F)
, we see by the continuity of the trace operator that

\int_{\partial U}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot v_{m}dv_{\partial U}\to\int_{\partial U}\nabla_{n}^{F}u\cdot vdv_{\partial U}.

(2.23)

Similarly, the first and the second terms of (
2.17
) associated to
v_{m}
converge to the respective terms associated to
v
. In the limit we obtain that (
2.17
) holds for
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
and
v\in H^{1}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
.
By (
2.15
), we see that it implies that (
2.17
) holds for
u,v\in{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

The kernel of
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
consists of flat sections of
F
. In other words,

\ker\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}\simeq H^{0}(\Psi,F).

(2.24)

Proof.

First of all, it’s easy to see that the flat sections are from
{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
, and they are trivially from the kernel of
\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}
. Now, let
u\in\ker(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
. Then from Proposition
2.4
, we deduce that

\big<\Delta_{\Psi}^{F}u,u\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F}u,\nabla^{F}u\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}.

(2.25)

Thus, we see that
\nabla^{F}u=0
, which means that
u
is a flat section.
∎

2.3
Domain of Friedrichs Laplacian through singularities

In this section we give a more explicit description of the domain of the Friedrichs extension of the Laplacian. This description will later play an important role in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We don’t claim originality on this section but again we weren’t able to find a complete reference for it.

We describe
{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi}^{F})
by prescribing some asymptotical behavior near
{\rm{Con}(\Psi)}\cup{\rm{Ang}(\Psi)}
to the functions from it. All our considerations are local, so by choosing local flat frames, we may and we will assume in all the proofs that our flat vector bundle is a trivial line bundle.

To begin, we suppose that
\partial\Psi=\emptyset
and that
(F,h^{F},\nabla^{F})
is a trivial line bundle.
Then it is sufficient to consider a surface
\Psi
with only one conical point
P
of the conical angle
\theta=\angle(P)
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the functions
C^{k}_{\pm,\theta}
on the model cone
C_{\theta}
, (
2.2
), by

\displaystyle C^{k}_{\pm,\theta}(r,\phi)=r^{\pm\frac{2\pi k}{\theta}}\exp\Big(\sqrt{-1}\frac{2\pi k\phi}{\theta}\Big),\qquad\text{for }k>0,

(2.26)

\displaystyle C^{0}_{+,\theta}(r,\phi)=1,\qquad\qquad\qquad C^{0}_{-,\theta}(r,\phi)=\log(r).

This is easily verifiable that those functions are formal solutions to the homogeneous problem
\Delta_{C_{\theta}}u=0
on the cone
C_{\theta}
, (
2.2
).
Notice that the functions
C^{k}_{\pm,\theta}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
belong to the
L^{2}
-space
L^{2}(C_{\theta})
of the cone
C_{\theta}
, with respect to the conical metric (
2.1
).

Let
\chi_{P}
be a smooth function on
\Psi
which is equal to
1
near
P
and such that in a vicinity of the support of
\chi_{P}
, the manifold
\Psi
is isometric to
C_{\theta}
.
We use this isometry to view the function
\chi_{P}C^{k}_{\pm,\theta}
as a function on
\Psi
.

Let
{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}(\Delta_{\Psi})
denote the minimal closure of
\Delta_{\Psi}
, viewed as an operator on
\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi)
. In other words, for
u\in L^{2}(\Psi)
, we have
u\in{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}(\Delta_{\Psi})
if and only if there exists a sequence of functions
u_{m}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi)
such that
u_{m}\to u
in
L^{2}(\Psi)
and
\Delta_{\Psi}u_{m}\to w
for some
w\in L^{2}(\Psi)
. Clearly, in this case, we have
\Delta_{\Psi}u=w
on the level of currents.

Recall that the maximal domain
{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi})
was defined in the beginning of Section
2.2
.

Proposition 2.7

(Mooers
[
14
, Proposition 2.3]
)
.

The following identity holds

{\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi})={\rm{Dom}}_{\min}(\Delta_{\Psi})+\sum_{0\leq k<\frac{\theta}{2\pi}}\langle\chi_{P}C^{k}_{+,\theta}\rangle+\sum_{0\leq k<\frac{\theta}{2\pi}}\langle\chi_{P}C^{k}_{-,\theta}\rangle,

(2.27)

where by
\langle v\rangle
we mean a vector space spanned by the vector
v
.

Now, the description of the set of all self-adjoint extensions of
\Delta_{\Psi}
looks as follows, cf. Mooers
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
.
Denote by
\mathcal{M}
the linear subspace of
L^{2}(\Psi,F)
spanned by the functions
\chi_{P}C^{k}_{\pm,\theta}
with
0\leq k<\frac{\theta}{2\pi}
.
The dimension,
2d
, of
\mathcal{M}
is even.
To get a self-adjoint extension of
\Delta_{\Psi}
one chooses a subspace
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathcal{M}
of dimension
d
such that for any
u,v\in\mathcal{N}
, we have

\big<\Delta_{\Psi}u,v\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi)}-\big<u,\Delta_{\Psi}v\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi)}=0.

(2.28)

To any such
\mathcal{N}
there corresponds a self-adjoint extension of
\Delta_{\Psi}
with domain
{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}(\Delta_{\Psi})+\mathcal{N}
.

The domain of the
Friedrichs extension

{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi})
corresponds to the choice of functions
\chi_{P}C^{k}_{+,\theta}
with
0\leq k<\frac{\theta}{2\pi}
.
To verify this, by Proposition
2.7
and the description above, since the number of those functions is exactly equal to
\frac{1}{2}\dim({\rm{Dom}}_{\max}(\Delta_{\Psi})/{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}(\Delta_{\Psi}))
, it is enough to prove that those functions are inside of
{\rm{Dom}}_{Fr}(\Delta_{\Psi})
.
But this follows by an explicit construction of a sequence of functions
f_{\epsilon}^{k}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0}(\Psi\setminus\{P\})
,
\epsilon>0
satisfying
f_{\epsilon}^{k}\to\chi_{P}C^{k}_{+,\theta}
and
\nabla f_{\epsilon}^{k}\to\nabla(\chi_{P}C^{k}_{+,\theta})
in
L^{2}(\Psi)
, as
\epsilon\to 0
.
For example, take

f_{\epsilon}^{k}=\big(\chi_{P}(r,\phi)-\chi_{P}(r/\epsilon,\phi)\big)C^{k}_{+,\theta}.

(2.29)

To make further notation easier, for
P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)
, we denote

C^{k}_{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\chi_{P}C^{k}_{+,\angle(P)}.

(2.30)

Now let’s come back to the original surface
\Psi
with piecewise geodesic boundary
\partial\Psi
and a flat unitary vector bundle
(F,h^{F},\nabla^{F})
over
\overline{\Psi}
.
Consider a double manifold

Now, by Sobolev embedding theorem, cf.
[
1
, p.97]
, for
p>1
, the following inclusion holds

W^{4}_{p}(\Psi,F)\hookrightarrow\mathscr{C}^{2}(\overline{\Psi},F).

(3.30)

Again, Adams in
[
1
, p.97]
gives a proof of the inclusion (
3.30
) for domains verifying cone property, but by decomposing

into sectors which clearly satisfy this property, the inclusion (

We conclude by (

), (

) and (

).
∎

3.2
Linearization functional, a proof of Theorem
3.2

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
3.2
.
The main idea is similar to the one from Section
3.1
, but the methods are crucially different.
We start from an eigenvector
f_{i}^{n}
corresponding to an eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}^{n}
of the discrete model
n^{2}\cdot\Delta_{\Psi_{n}}^{F_{n}}
and construct by “linearization”
L_{n}(f_{i}^{n})\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
.
We prove that the associated Rayleigh quotients are close enough. Then Theorem
3.2
would follow from a simple application of the min-max theorem.

We note that all the statements of this section are local in nature, so we will constantly write them in local flat frames of
F
without saying it explicitly.
All the final objects can be written using parallel transport and they do not depend on the choice of the frame.

Let’s define the “linearization” functional
L_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})\to\mathscr{C}^{0}(\Psi,F)
.
Recall that the sets
V_{n}(P)
and
U_{n}(P)
were defined in (
2.4
), (
2.6
).
First, for
f\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
, let’s define
f^{avg}\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
by averaging the function
f
on
V_{n}(P)
, i.e.

f^{avg}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\begin{cases}\hfill\frac{1}{\#V_{n}(P)}\sum_{Q\in V_{n}(P)}f(Q),&\text{ if there is $P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi),v\in V_{n}(P)$, }\\
\hfill f(v),&\text{ otherwise. }\\
\end{cases}

(3.31)

By definition, the functional
L_{n}
satisfies the following property

L_{n}(f)=L_{n}(f^{avg}).

(3.32)

Now, we define
L_{n}
by describing explicitly the value of
(L_{n}f)(z)
for any
z\in\Psi
and any
f\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
, satisfying
f=f^{avg}
.

Suppose
z\in U_{n}(P)
for some
P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
. We define

(L_{n}f)(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=f(Q),\quad\text{where}\quad Q\in V_{n}(P).

(3.33)

By the assumption, we have
f=f^{avg}
, so
(L_{n}f)(z)
doesn’t depend on the choice of
Q
.

Next, suppose that
{\rm{dist}}(z,\partial\Psi)<\frac{1}{2n}
and that for any
P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
, we have
z\notin U_{n}(P)
.
Then let
P,Q\in V(\Psi_{n})
be the two closest points to
z
in
V(\Psi_{n})
.
In case if there are several choices, take any.
The geometrical place of points
z
, satisfying
{\rm{dist}}(z,\partial\Psi)<\frac{1}{2n}
, and having
P
and
Q
as their closest points in
V(\Psi_{n})
, is a rectangle. We denote this rectangle by
R
.
The points
P
and
Q
have either the same
x
or
y
coordinates, where
x
and
y
are linear coordinates having axes parallel to the boundaries of tiles of
\Psi
.
Suppose that they share the same
y
coordinate.
We renormalize
x
coordinate so that it satisfies
x(P)=1
and
x(Q)=0
. Then we define

(L_{n}f)(z)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=f(P)x(z)+f(Q)(1-x(z)).

(3.34)

Finally, suppose that
z
is in none of the cases considered above.
Consider some triangulation of
\Psi\setminus(B_{\Psi}(\frac{1}{2n},\partial\Psi)\cup(\cup_{P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)}U_{n}(P)))
with vertices at
V(\Psi_{n})
.
Let
P,Q,R\in V(\Psi_{n})
be the vertices of the triangle

containing
z
.
If there are several choices, take any.
We define
(L_{n}f)(z)
as the value of the unique linear function
L
at

, satisfying

,
L(Q)=f(Q)
and
L(R)=f(Q)
(with respect to the coordinates

as in the previous step).
This procedure describes

completely.
For a schematic description of the functional

, see Figure

for

More precisely, Proposition

Now, let’s suppose that the graph

is a part of some graph

Extend

to

in such a way so that

is invariant under this involution.

Now, if there is

such that

averages

near

The “linearization” functional satisfies a number of important properties.
To describe one of them, denote by
{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}^{vN}(\nabla^{F})
the minimal extension of the exterior derivative twisted by
F
, i.e.

\nabla^{F}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)\to\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,T^{*}\Psi\otimes F).

(3.35)

In other words, a function
g\in L^{2}(\Psi,F)
lies in
{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}^{vN}(\nabla^{F})
if and only if there is a sequence
f_{n}\in\mathscr{C}^{\infty}_{0,vN}(\Psi,F)
such that
f_{n}\to g
and
\nabla^{F}(f_{n})\to h
in

for some

. If
g
and
h
are as described, in the distributional sense, we have
\nabla^{F}g=h
. Then

Proposition 3.6
.

For any
f\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
, we have

L_{n}(f)\in{\rm{Dom}}_{\min}^{vN}(\nabla^{F}).

(3.36)

The proof of Proposition
3.6
is rather direct, and it relies only the density results of smooth functions inside the Sobolev space
W^{1}_{\infty}
. It will be given in the end of this section.

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose that
f,g\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
satisfy
f=f^{avg},g=g^{avg}
. Then the following identity holds

\big<\nabla^{F}\big(L_{n}(f)\big),\nabla^{F}\big(L_{n}(g)\big)\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F_{n}}_{\Psi_{n}}f,\nabla^{F_{n}}_{\Psi_{n}}g\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi_{n},F_{n})}.

(3.37)

The proof of Proposition
3.7
is a direct calculation, and it is given in the end of this section.

Now, for an eigenvector
f_{i}^{n}\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, corresponding to the eigenvalue

of

, the functional

satisfies the following proposition, the proof of which uses Theorem

and will be given in the end of this section.

Proposition 3.8
.

For any
\phi\in\mathscr{C}^{1}(\overline{\Psi})
,
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, as

, the following estimation holds

\big<\phi L_{n}(f_{i}^{n}),L_{n}(f_{j}^{n})\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\big<\phi\cdot f_{i}^{n},f_{j}^{n}\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi_{n},F_{n})}+o(1).

(3.38)

Remark 3.9
.

In this article we will only apply Proposition
3.8
for
\phi=1
, but for further references we state it more generally.

Now we can state the most important result of this section.

Theorem 3.10
.

For any
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, as
n\to\infty
, the following estimation holds

\big<\nabla^{F}\big(L_{n}(f_{i}^{n})\big),\nabla^{F}\big(L_{n}(f_{j}^{n})\big)\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi,F)}=\big<\nabla^{F_{n}}_{\Psi_{n}}f_{i}^{n},\nabla^{F_{n}}_{\Psi_{n}}f_{j}^{n}\big>_{L^{2}(\Psi_{n},F_{n})}+o(1).

(3.39)

Our proof of Theorem
3.10
relies on the following technical statement, to the proof of which we devote a separate Section
4
.

Theorem 3.11

(Harnack-type inequality)
.

We fix
\lambda>0
. Suppose that a sequence
f_{n}\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\Psi_{n}),F_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(\Psi_{n},F_{n})}^{2}=n^{2}
, satisfies

\big|\Delta_{\Psi_{n}}^{F_{n}}f_{n}\big|\leq\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}f_{n},

(3.40)

Then, as

, the following limit holds

\max_{(P,Q)\in E(\Psi_{n})}\big|f_{n}(P)-f_{n}(Q)\big|\to 0.

(3.41)

Remark 3.12
.

Essentially this theorem says that if a sequence of discrete functions is “nearly harmonic”, then asymptotically this sequence is “continuous”.

Let’s see how Theorem
3.11
and Proposition

imply Theorem

3.43

4
Harnack-type inequality for discrete Laplacian

The theory of harmonic functions on lattices has received considerable attention in the past; for example, see Duffin
[
6
]
, Kenyon
[
11
]
, Bücking
[
2
]
… Much less attention has been drawn to functions which are
almost harmonic
in the sense that their Laplacian is not zero, but evaluated at some point, it is close to zero in comparison with the value of the function itself at this point.
The main goal of this section is to give a proof Theorem
3.11
, which is a statement in this direction.

This section is organized as follows. In Section
4.1
, we give a proof of Theorem
3.11
modulo Theorem
4.1
, which gives a uniform upper bound on the values of almost-harmonic function in terms of the square root of the logarithm of the number of vertices in a graph.
In Section
4.2
, we give a proof of Theorem
4.1
by assuming Theorem
4.2
, which gives uniform constant bound on the values of almost-harmonic function away from the conical and angular singularities.
Finally, in Section
4.3
, we prove Theorem
4.2
by using discrete potential theory.

C_{0}(\lambda)=C\sqrt{\lambda}+C(\lambda)
, where
C(\lambda)
is from Theorem
4.2
.

Now, let’s show that the bound (
4.21
) can be “bootstraped” to Theorem
4.1
.
In fact, by Theorem
4.2
, we only need to prove Theorem
4.1
in the neighborhood of
{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
.

Let’s fix
P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)
.
Recall that the set
V_{n}(P)
was defined in (
2.4
).
Consider a function
h_{P}
, given by

h_{P}(Q)={\rm{dist}}_{\Psi_{n}}(Q,V_{n}(P))^{2},\qquad Q\in V(\Psi_{n}).

(4.25)

We verify that inside of
B_{\Psi_{n}}(n,V_{n}(P))
, we have

\Delta_{\Psi_{n}}^{F_{n}}h_{P}\leq-1.

(4.26)

Let
C_{0}(\lambda)
be from (
4.21
).
Consider a function

g_{n}=f_{n}+2C_{0}(\lambda)\sqrt{\log(n)}\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}h_{P}.

(4.27)

Then by (
3.40
), (
4.21
) and (
4.26
), we see that over
B_{\Psi_{n}}(n,V_{n}(P))
, we have

\Delta_{\Psi_{n}}^{F_{n}}g_{n}<0.

(4.28)

Thus, by the maximum principle, the maximum of
g_{n}
on
B_{\Psi_{n}}(r,V_{n}(P))
,
r\leq n
is attained on
S_{\Psi_{n}}(r,V_{n}(P))
.
Thus, for any
\epsilon
, satisfying
0<\epsilon<1
, we have

\max_{Q\in B_{\Psi_{n}}(\epsilon n,V_{n}(P))}\Big(f_{n}(Q)+2C_{0}(\lambda)\sqrt{\log(n)}\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}h_{P}(Q)\Big)\\
\leq\max_{R\in S_{\Psi_{n}}(\epsilon n,V_{n}(P))}f_{n}(R)+2C_{0}(\lambda)\epsilon^{2}\lambda\sqrt{\log(n)}.

(4.29)

From Theorem
4.2
, we see that there is
C_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)>0
such that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}R\notin B_{\Psi_{n}}(\epsilon n,P)\\
P\in{\rm{Con}}(\Psi)\cup{\rm{Ang}}(\Psi)\end{subarray}}|f_{n}(R)|\leq C_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon).

(4.30)

In particular, we have

\max_{R\in S_{\Psi_{n}}(\epsilon n,V_{n}(P))}f_{n}(R)\leq C_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon).

(4.31)

From (
4.25
), (
4.29
) and (
4.30
), we conclude that for any
0<\epsilon<1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

\max_{Q\in B_{\Psi_{n}}(\epsilon n,V_{n}(P))}f_{n}(Q)\leq 2C_{0}(\lambda)\epsilon^{2}\sqrt{\log(n)}+C_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon).

(4.32)

Similarly, we can bound
f_{n}
from below.
By this, (
4.30
) and (
4.32
), we conclude.
∎

4.3
Interior bounds on almost harmonic functions, a proof of Theorem
4.2

The main goal of this section is to get interior bounds on almost harmonic functions and to prove Theorem
4.2
.
The proof of this theorem relies heavily on the discrete potential theory introduced by Duffin,
[
6
]
, in dimension 3 and developed by Kenyon,
[
11
]
, in dimension 2.

Since Theorem
4.2
is a statement about the value of
f_{n}
at points far away from conical and angle singularities, it would follow from the following two theorems.
The first one corresponds to the bound on the interior points of
\Psi
.

Theorem 4.3
.

We fix
\lambda>0
.
There is a constant
C(\lambda)>0
, such that for any sequence
f_{n}\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\mathbb{Z}^{2}),\mathbb{C})
satisfying
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0))}^{2}\leq n^{2}
, and

\big|\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{n}\big|\leq\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}|f_{n}|,\qquad\text{over}\qquad\qquad B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0),

(4.33)

we have the following bound

\big|f_{n}(0)\big|\leq C(\lambda).

(4.34)

And the second one corresponds to the bound on the points near the boundary
\partial\Psi
.

Theorem 4.4
.

We fix
\lambda>0
.
There is a constant
C(\lambda)>0
, such that for any sequences
P_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
and
f_{n}\in{\rm{Map}}(V(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{C})
satisfying
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert f_{n}\right\rVert}_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(n,P_{n}))}^{2}\leq n^{2}
and

\big|\Delta_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}f_{n}\big|\leq\frac{\lambda}{n^{2}}|f_{n}|,\qquad\text{over}\qquad B_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(n,P_{n}),

(4.35)

we have the following bound

\big|f_{n}(P_{n})\big|\leq C(\lambda).

(4.36)

The proofs of those statements are similar and use Green functions on balls in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Let’s start with Theorem
4.3
. Consider
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
with its nearest-neighbor graph structure and a standard injection
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
For simplicity, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|z|\leq n\},

(4.37)

where
|z|
is the modulus of
z
(and not the distance between
0
and
z
in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
).

None of the Propositions
4.5
,
4.6
,
4.8
are new.
We, nevertheless, recall their proofs for completeness and to use them later in the corresponding statements on
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
.

Proposition 4.5

(Duffin
[
6
, p. 242]
)
.

There exists a function
G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
satisfying

\displaystyle{\rm{\displaystyle supp}}(G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})\subset B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0),

(4.38)

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}})(z)=\begin{cases}\hfill 1,&\text{ for }z=0,\\
\hfill 0,&\text{ for }z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\setminus\{0\}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We prove Proposition
4.5
iteratively.
Let
G_{n}^{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
be a function, equal to
1
at
0
and
0
otherwise.
Construct
G_{n}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
by

G_{n}^{i}(z)=\begin{cases}\hfill 1,&\text{ for }z=0,\\
\hfill\frac{1}{4}\big(\sum_{(z_{1},z)\in E(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}G_{n}^{i-1}(z_{1})\big),&\text{ for $z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\setminus\{0\}$,}\\
\hfill 0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(4.39)

Continuing this process gives a sequence of functions
G_{n}^{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
By induction, it’s easy to see that this sequence is increasing and bounded above by
1
.
Thus, the sequence
G_{n}^{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, converges to a function
G_{n}^{\infty}
, satisfying
0\leq G_{n}^{\infty}\leq 1
.
Clearly,
G_{n}^{\infty}
is harmonic on
B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\setminus\{0\}
.
If
G_{n}^{\infty}
were harmonic at
0
, then by the maximum principle it follows that
G_{n}^{\infty}
takes maximal value at
S_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n+1,0)
.
But it is clearly false, since it takes zero values there.
Thus, we see that
(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}G_{n}^{\infty})(0)>0
, and so the function
G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}
, defined by

G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}G_{n}^{\infty})(0)}G_{n}^{\infty},

(4.40)

satisfies the hypothesizes (
4.38
).
∎

Proposition 4.6

(Duffin
[
6
, Theorem 2, §3]
in dimension
3
, Kenyon
[
11
, Theorem 7.3]
in dimension
2
)
.

There exists a unique function
G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
such that for any
z,w\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2},z})G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z,w)=\begin{cases}\hfill 1,&\text{ for }z=w,\\
\hfill 0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4.41)

\displaystyle G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z,z)=0,

\displaystyle G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z,w)=O(\log|z-w|),\text{ as }|z-w|\to\infty,

where by
\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2},z}
we mean a Laplacian evaluated with respect to the
z
-coordinate.
Moreover, as
|z-w|\to\infty
, such a function satisfies the following asymptotic expansion

G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z,w)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|z-w|-\frac{\gamma_{EM}}{2\pi}+O\Big(\frac{1}{|z-w|}\Big),

(4.42)

where
\gamma_{EM}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Remark 4.7
.

The precise value of
\gamma_{EM}
is not important in what follows. It is, however, of vital importance that the expansion (
4.42
) is up to the term
O(1/|z-w|)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

S_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)\setminus B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n-1,0).

(4.43)

Using Proposition
4.6
, we can now get

Proposition 4.8

(Duffin
[
6
, Lemma 2]
in dimension
3
, Bücking
[
2
, Proposition A.3]
in dimension
2
)
.

Let
G_{n}
be a function from Proposition
4.5
.
There is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

\max_{z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)}G_{n}(z)\leq\frac{C}{n}.

(4.44)

Proof.

Consider a function
f_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
, given by

f_{n}(z)=G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z)-G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(0,z)-\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)-\frac{\gamma_{EM}}{2\pi}.

(4.45)

From (
4.38
) and (
4.42
), we see that there is
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

\max_{z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n+1,0)}|f_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{C}{n}.

(4.46)

However, by Propositions
4.5
,
4.6
, the function
f_{n}
satisfies

(\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{n})(z)=0,\qquad\text{for }z\in B_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0).

(4.47)

Thus, by the maximal principle and (
4.46
), we conclude that

\max_{z\in S_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)}|f_{n}(z)|\leq\frac{C}{n}.

(4.48)

By (
4.42
) and (
4.48
), we conclude.
∎

As a consequence of Proposition
4.8
, we get the following

Proposition 4.9
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)
, we have

\displaystyle G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z)\leq C\log\Big(\frac{n+2}{|z|+1}\Big),

(4.49)

\displaystyle\|G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}\leq Cn.

Proof.

We begin with a proof of the first bound of (
4.49
).
Consider
f_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\real
, defined by

f_{n}=G_{n+1}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}-G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}.

(4.50)

By (
4.38
), the function
f_{n}
is harmonic in
B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)
.
Thus, by the maximal principle, it attains its maximum on
S_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n+1,0)
.
By this and Proposition
4.8
, we see that there is
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

|f_{n}|\leq\frac{C}{n}.

(4.51)

From this, we conclude by induction that

G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z)\leq\sum_{i=|z|}^{n}\frac{C}{i}.

(4.52)

By standard bounds on the partial harmonic sums, we obtain from (
4.52
) the first estimate in (
4.49
).
Let’s prove that the second estimate in (
4.49
) follows from the first.

In fact, by the first bound in (
4.49
), we see that there is a constant
C>0
, which depends only on the sets
\angle({\rm{Con}}(\Psi))
and
\angle({\rm{Ang}}(\Psi))
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, the following holds

\|G_{n}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})}^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{i}\Big)^{2}i.

(4.53)

It is only now left to prove that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

A_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{i}\Big)^{2}i\leq Cn^{2}.

(4.54)

To show (
4.54
), it is enough to establish that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
:

B_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=A_{n}-A_{n-1}\leq Cn.

(4.55)

We trivially have

B_{n}=\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)^{2}n+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bigg(\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{i}\Big)^{2}-\log\Big(\frac{n}{i}\Big)^{2}\bigg)i.

(4.56)

Now, clearly, there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{i}\Big)^{2}-\log\Big(\frac{n}{i}\Big)^{2}\leq\frac{C}{n}\log\Big(\frac{n}{i}\Big),

(4.57)

\displaystyle\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)^{2}n\leq\frac{C}{n}.

By (
4.56
) and (
4.57
), to prove (
4.55
), it is enough to prove that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

C_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{i}\Big)i\leq Cn^{2}.

(4.58)

To show (
4.58
), it is obviously enough to establish that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

D_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C_{n}-C_{n-1}\leq Cn.

(4.59)

But we have the following identity

D_{n}=\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)n+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\log\Big(\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)i,

(4.60)

from which, along with the second bound from (
4.57
), we deduce (
4.59
).
∎

Finally we are ready to give

Proof of Theorem
4.3
.

Similarly to (
4.6
), we define
\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0)\subset E(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
By (
1.15
), we see that for any
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
r\leq n
, the following discrete analogue of Green identity holds

\big<\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{n},G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big>_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0))}-\big<f_{n},\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big>_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0))}\\
=\sum_{e\in\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0)}\Big(f_{n}(h(e))G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(t(e))-f_{n}(h(e))G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(t(e))\Big).

(4.61)

However, by Proposition
4.9
and (
4.33
), we deduce that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
r\leq n
, we have

\big<\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}f_{n},G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big>_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0))}\leq C\lambda.

(4.62)

By (
4.38
), we have

\big<f_{n},\Delta_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\big>_{L^{2}(B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0))}=f_{n}(0).

(4.63)

From Proposition
4.8
and (
4.38
), we see that there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
n/2\leq r\leq n
, we have

\Big|\sum_{e\in\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0)}\Big(f_{n}(h(e))G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(t(e))-f_{n}(h(e))G_{r}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(t(e))\Big)\Big|\leq\frac{C}{n}\sum_{e\in\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0)}|f_{n}(h(e))|

(4.64)

By combining (
4.61
)-(
4.64
), we see that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
n/2\leq r\leq n
, we have

|f_{n}(0)|\leq C\lambda+\frac{C}{n}\sum_{e\in\partial B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(r,0)}|f_{n}(h(e))|.

(4.65)

By taking average of (
4.65
) for
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
n/2\leq r\leq n
, we have

|f_{n}(0)|\leq C\lambda+\frac{2C}{n^{2}}\sum_{P\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)}|f_{n}(P)|.

(4.66)

By mean inequality and the assumptions on the norm of
f_{n}
, for some constant
C>0
, we have

\bigg(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{P\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)}|f_{n}(P)|\bigg)^{2}\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}\sum_{P\in B_{\mathbb{C}}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(n,0)}|f_{n}(P)|^{2}\leq C.

(4.67)

By (
4.66
) and (
4.67
), we conclude.
∎

Now, let’s prove Theorem
4.4
.
The proof is parallel to Theorem
4.3
modulo minor modifications.

We denote by
\mathbb{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\operatorname{Im}(z)\geq 0\}
the upper half-plane.
We will implicitly use the following (non-standard) injection

\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{H},\qquad(a,b)\mapsto b+\sqrt{-1}\Big(a+\frac{1}{2}\Big).

(4.68)

The shifting by
\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}
will be used Proposition
4.11
.
For
P\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote

QB(r,P)=\Big\{z\in\mathbb{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\big|(z-P)(z-\overline{P})\big|\leq r^{2}\Big\}.

(4.69)

We stress our that
z-P,z-\overline{P}\in\mathbb{C}
should be interpreted through (
4.68
).
The sets
\partial QB(r,P_{n})\in E(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z})
,
QS(r,P_{n})\in V(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z})
are defined similarly to (
4.6
) and (
4.43
).
We have the following analogue of Proposition
4.5
.

Proposition 4.10
.

For any
P\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
r\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, there is a function
G_{P,r}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\real
, satisfying

\displaystyle{\rm{\displaystyle supp}}(G_{P,r}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}})\subset QB(r,P),

(4.70)

\displaystyle(\Delta_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}G_{P,r}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}})(z)=\begin{cases}\hfill 1,&\text{ for }z=P,\\
\hfill 0,&\text{ for }z\in QB(r,P)\setminus\{P\}.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is a formal repetition of the proof of Proposition
4.5
.
∎

We have the following weak analogue of Proposition
4.6
.

Proposition 4.11
.

There exists a function
G_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\real
such that for any
z,w\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
:

\Delta_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z},z}G_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(z,w)=\begin{cases}\hfill 1,&\text{ for }z=w,\\
\hfill 0,&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(4.71)

and the following asymptotic expansion, as
|z-w|\to\infty
, holds

G_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(z,w)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\Big(\log|z-w|+\log|z-\overline{w}|\Big)-\frac{\gamma_{EM}}{\pi}+O\Big(\frac{1}{|z-w|}\Big),

(4.72)

where
\gamma_{EM}
is the Euler-Mascheroni constant.

Proof.

It suffices to take

G_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(z,w)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z-w,0)+G_{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(z-\overline{w},0),

(4.73)

where the points
z-w,z-\overline{w}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
should be evaluated through the injection (
4.68
). The shifting by
\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}
assures that the resulting function
G_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(z,w)
satisfies (
4.71
).
∎

Now, by repeating line in line the proofs of Propositions
4.8
,
4.9
, only by replacing each use of Proposition
4.6
by Proposition
4.11
, we get the following two propositions

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
r\geq 4{\rm{dist}}_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(P,\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z})
, and
G_{r,P}
be as in Proposition
4.10
.
There is a constant
C>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, we have

\max_{z\in QS(r,P)}G_{r,P}(z)\leq\frac{C}{r}.

(4.74)

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
r\geq 4{\rm{dist}}_{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}}(P,\{0\}\times\mathbb{Z})
, and
G_{r,P}
be as in Proposition
4.10
.
There is a constant
C>0
such that for any
z\in QB(r,P)
, the following bounds hold

\displaystyle G_{r,P}(z)\leq C\log\Big(\frac{r+2}{|P-z|+1}\Big),

(4.75)

\displaystyle\|G_{r,P}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z})}\leq Cr.

By repeating formally the proof of Theorem
4.3
, only replacing the use of Propositions
4.8
,
4.9
by Propositions
4.12
,
4.13
, we get Theorem
4.4
.
