Abstract
A square is a word of the form u ​ u uu . In this paper we prove that for a given finite word w w , the number of distinct square factors of w w is bounded by | w | − | Alph ​ ( w ) | + 1 |w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|+1 , where | w | |w| denotes the length of w w and | Alph ​ ( w ) | |\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)| denotes the number of distinct letters in w w . This result answers a conjecture of Fraenkel and Simpson stated in 1998.



1
Introduction

The study of patterns in a word is
related to several topics and it has
various applications in several research domains, ranging from practical applications to theoretical considerations.
Among these patterns, we can mention palindromes and repetitions such as squares, cubes, periods, overlaps, etc.
They appear in different contexts in computer science such as data compression (LZW overlap), searching algorithms, structure of indexes
[
11
]
, digital geometry
[
4
]
, in number theory about Diophantine approximation and transcendence statements
[
1
]
, and in physics in connection with Schrödinger operators
[
2
]
, among several others.

Palindromic complexity has received noticeable attention and a comprehensive survey was provided by Allouche et al.
[
2
]
.
In particular, Droubay, Justin and Pirillo established that the number of distinct palindromic factors of a word is bounded by its length plus one and that finite Sturmian (and even episturmian) words realize it
[
6
]
.

For the square factors the situation seems to be significantly harder to handle.
Indeed, Fraenkel and Simpson conjectured in
[
8
]
that the number of distinct non-empty squares of a finite word
w
is bounded by its length
|w|
and they proved that this number is bounded by
2|w|
.
After that Ilie
[
9
]
strengthened this bound to
2|w|-\Theta(\log(|w|))
; Lam
[
10
]

improved this result to
\frac{95}{48}|w|
; Deza, Franek and Thierry
[
5
]

achieved a bound of
\frac{11}{6}|w|
; Thierry
[
13
]
refined this bound to
\frac{3}{2}|w|
. We remark that the Fraenkel and Simpson’s conjecture implies that the square complexity of a finite word is also bounded by its length plus one by counting the empty word. In this article, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1

For any finite word
w
, let
S(w)
denote the number of its distinct square factors,
|w|
denote the length of
w
and
|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|
denote the number of distinct letters in
w
, then we have

S(w)\leq|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|+1.

This result is a stronger version of the conjecture of Fraenkel and Simpson stated in
[
8
]
.

The strategy of the proof is as follows: for a given word
w
, we first recall the definition of Rauzy graph and define small circuits in it; we then prove that the total number of small circuits in the union of the Rauzy graphs of
w
is bounded by
|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|
; finally we conclude by proving that there exists an injection from the set of distinct nonempty squares of
w
to the set of small circuits in the union of the Rauzy graphs of
w
.

2
Preliminaries

Let us recall the basic terminology about words. By
word
we mean a finite concatenation of symbols
w=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The
length
of
w
, denoted
|w|
, is
n
and we say that the symbol
w_{i}
is at the
position

i
. The set
\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)=\left\{w_{i}|1\leq i\leq n\right\}
is called the
alphabet
of
w
and its elements are called
letters
. Let
|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|
denote the cardinality of
\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)
. A word of length
0
is called the
empty word
and it is denoted by
\varepsilon
. For any word
u
, we have
u=\varepsilon u=u\varepsilon
.

A word
u
is called a
factor
of
w
if
w=pus
for some words
p,s
. The set of all factors of
w
is denoted by
\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
. For any integer
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq|w|
, let
L_{w}(i)
be the set of all length-
i
factors of
w
and let
C_{w}(i)
denote the cardinality of
L_{i}(w)
.

Two finite words
u
and
v
are
conjugate
when there exist words
x,y
such that
u=xy
and
v=yx
.
The conjugacy class of a word
w
is denoted by
[w]
. Let
w=w_{1}w_{2}...w_{t}
be a finite word, for any integer
i
satisfying
1<i\leq t
, let us define
w_{s}(i)=w_{i}w_{i+1}...w_{t}
and
w_{p}(i)=w_{1}w_{2}...w_{i-1}
and let
w_{s}(1)=w
and
w_{p}(1)=\epsilon
.
Thus,
[w]=\left\{w(i)|w(i)=w_{s}(i)w_{p}(i),i=1,2,...t\right\}
.

For any natural number
k
, we define the
k
-power
of a finite word
u
to be the concatenation of
k
copies of
u
, and it is denoted by
u^{k}
. Particularly, a
square
is a word
w
of the form
w=uu
.
A word
w
is said to be
primitive
if it is not a power of another word. Let
\hbox{\rm Prim}(w)
denote the set of primitive factors of
w
. For any word
u
and any rational number
\alpha
, the
\alpha
-power of
u
is defined to be
u^{a}u_{0}
where
u_{0}
is a prefix of
u
,
a
is the integer part of
\alpha
, and
|u^{a}u_{0}|=\alpha|u|
. The
\alpha
-power of
u
is denoted by
u^{\alpha}
. Let
\alpha
be a positive rational number larger than
1
, the word
w
is said to be
of the period
\alpha
if there exists a word
u
such that
w=u^{\alpha}
.

Here we recall some basic lemmas concerning repetitions:

Lemma 1
(Fine and Wilf
[
7
]
)

Let
w
be a word having
k
and
l
for periods. If
|w|\geq k+l-\gcd(k,l)
then
\gcd(k,l)
is also a period of
w
.

Lemma 2
(Lyndon and Schützenberger
[
12
]
)

Let
x
and
y
be two words such that
xy=yx
. Then there exist a primitive
p
and two non-negative integers
i,j
such that
x=p^{i}
and y =
p^{j}
.

Lemma 3
(Lyndon and Schützenberger
[
12
]
)

Let
x,y,z
be three words such that
xy=yz
and
|y|\neq 0
. Then there exist two words
u,v
with
|u|\neq 0
and some positive integer
i
such that

x=uv,y=(uv)^{i}u,z=vu
.

Here we recall some elementary definitions and proprieties concerning graphs from Berge
[
3
]
.

A
(non-oriented) graph
consists of a nonempty set of
vertices

V
and a set of
edges

E
. A vertex
a
represents an endpoint of an edge and an edge joins two vertices
a,b
.
A
chain
is a sequence of edges
e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{k}
, in which each edge
e_{i}
(
1<i<k
) has one vertex in common with
e_{i-1}
and the other vertex in common with
e_{i+1}
. A
cycle
is a finite chain which begins with a vertex
x
and ends at the same vertex. A graph is called
connected
if for any couple of vertices
a,b
in this graph, there exists a chain which begins at
a
and ends at
b
.

A graph is called
oriented
if its edges are oriented from one endpoint to the other. An oriented graph is called weakly connected if it is connected as a non-oriented graph.

Let
G
be a connected graph and let
\left\{e_{1},e_{2}\cdots e_{l}\right\}
,
\left\{v_{1},v_{2}\cdots v_{s}\right\}
denote respectively the edge set and the vertex set of
G
. The number
\chi(G)=l-s+1
is called the
cyclomatic number
of
G
.

Let
C
be a cycle in
G
. A vector
\mu(C)=(c_{1},c_{2}\cdots c_{l})
in the
l
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{l}
is called the
vector-cycle corresponding to
C
if
c_{i}
is the number of visits of the edge
e_{i}
in the cycle
C
for all
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq l
. The cycle
C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{k},...
are said to be
independent
if their corresponding vectors are linearly independent.

Lemma 4
(Theorem 2, Chapter 4 in
[
3
]
)

the cyclomatic number of a graph is the maximum number of independent cycles in this graph.

3
Rauzy graph and small circuits

We first recall the definition of Rauzy graph. Let
w
be a word of length
k
. For any integer
n
such that
1\leq n\leq k
, let the Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{n}(w)
be an oriented graph whose set of vertices is
L_{w}(n)
and the set of edges is
L_{w}(n+1)
;
an edge
e\in L_{w}(n+1)
starts at the vertex
u
and ends at the vertex
v
, if
u
is a
prefix and
v
is a suffix of
e
. We remark that
\Gamma_{n}(w)
is a weakly connected graph for any
n
. Let us define
\Gamma(w)=\cup_{n=1}^{k}\Gamma_{n}(w)
.

Let
\Gamma_{i}(w)
be a Rauzy graph of
w
. A sub-graph in
\Gamma_{i}(w)
is called an
elementary circuit
if there are
j
distinct vertices
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{j}
and
j
distinct edges
e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{j}
for some integer
j
, such that for each
k
with
1\leq k\leq j-1
, the edge
e_{k}
starts at
v_{k}
and ends at
v_{k+1}
, and for the edge
e_{j}
, it starts at
v_{j}
and ends at
v_{1}
, further,
j
is called the
size
of the circuit. We let this circuit be denoted by
\left\{\left\{v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{j}\right\},\left\{e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{j}\right\}\right\}
. The
small circuits
in the graph
\Gamma_{i}(w)
are those elementary circuits whose sizes are no larger than
i
.

Lemma 5

Let
w
be a finite word and let
\Gamma_{r}(w)
be a Rauzy graph of
w
for some
r
. Then for any small circuit
C
on
\Gamma_{r}(w)
, there exists a unique primitive word
q
, up to conjugacy, such that
|q|\leq r
and the vertex set of
C
is
\left\{p^{\frac{r}{|p|}}|p\in[q]\right\}
and its edge set is
\left\{p^{\frac{r+1}{|p|}}|p\in[q]\right\}
.

Proof

Let
C
be a small circuit on
\Gamma_{r}(w)
of size
l
, then it contains
l
distinct vertices, say
v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{l}
, and
l
distinct edges, say
e_{1},e_{2},...,e_{l}
. For each
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq l
, let us define
p_{i}
to be a word by concatenating consecutively the last letter of words
e_{i},e_{i+1},...,e_{i+l-1}
with
e_{r}=e_{r-l}
if
r\geq l+1
. The words
p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{l}
are pairwisely conjugate. Further, for each
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq l
, from the fact that the edges in the order of
e_{i},e_{i+1},...,e_{i+l-1}
form a circuit, we can deduce that
v_{i}
is a suffix of
v_{i}p_{i}
. Thus, there exists a word
p^{\prime}_{i}
of length
l
such that
v_{i}p_{i}=p^{\prime}_{i}v_{i}
. From Lemma
3
, there exists two words
u_{i},t_{i}
with
|u_{i}|\neq 0
and a nonnegative integer
j
such that
p^{\prime}_{i}=u_{i}t_{i},p_{i}=t_{i}u_{i}
and
v_{i}=(u_{i}t_{i})^{j}u_{i}
. Consequently,
v_{i}=(p^{\prime}_{i})^{\frac{r}{l}}
. Further, as
p^{\prime}_{i}
and
p_{i}
are conjugate, the words
p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2},...,p^{\prime}_{l}
are also pairwisely conjugate. Thus, the vertex set of
C
is
\left\{p^{\frac{r}{|p|}}|p\in[p_{1}]\right\}
and its edge set is
\left\{p^{\frac{r+1}{|p|}}|p\in[p_{1}]\right\}
.

Here we prove the primitivity of
p_{1}
. If
p_{1}
is not primitive, then there are two distinct integers
r,s
satisfying
1\leq r,s\leq l
such that
p^{\prime}_{r}=p^{\prime}_{s}
, and further,
v_{r}=v_{s}
. This contradicts the hypothesis that
C
contains
l
distinct vertices.

From the previous lemma, each small circuit can be uniquely identified by two parameters: a primitive word
p
defined in the lemma and an integer
i
such that
\Gamma_{w}(i)
is the Rauzy graph in which the circuit is located. Let all small circuits be denoted by
C(p,i)
with the parameters defined as above.

Let
\prec
be a lexicographic order over
\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
, for a given small circuit, let us define the
maximal edge
of this circuit to be the lexicographically greatest element in its edge set.

Lemma 6

Each small circuit contains exactly one maximal edge. Further, the maximal edges of the small circuits in the same Rauzy graph are pairwisely distinct.

Proof

From Lemma
5
the edge set of any small circuit is of the form
\left\{p^{t}|p\in[q]\right\}
such that
t>1
and
q
is primitive. Thus,
u^{t}
is the lexicographically greatest element in
\left\{p^{t}|p\in[q]\right\}
if and only if
u
is the lexicographically greatest element in
[q]
. Hence the maximal edge of each small circuit is unique.

For the second part, let us suppose that
v
is the maximal edge of two distinct small circuits
C(p,r)
and
C(q,r)
. From Lemma
5
, there are two positive integers
i\geq 1,j\geq 1
and two words
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
which are respectively a prefix of
p,q
such that
v=p^{i}p^{\prime}=q^{j}q^{\prime}
,where
p^{\prime},q^{\prime}
can be
\varepsilon
. First we state that
|p^{\prime}|\neq|q^{\prime}|
. Otherwise,
p^{i}=q^{j}
. However, in this case, from Lemma
1
,
p
and
q
can not be both primitive. Without loss of generality, let us suppose
|p^{\prime}|>|q^{\prime}|
, thus
|p^{i}|<|q^{j}|
. Then there exists a prefix
p^{\prime\prime}
of
p
such that
|p^{\prime\prime}|<|p|
and
q^{j}=p^{i}p^{\prime\prime}
. However,
p^{\prime\prime}
is also a prefix of
p^{i}
. Thus, there exists a word
w
such that
q^{j}=p^{\prime\prime}wp^{\prime\prime}
and
p^{i}=p^{\prime\prime}w
.

Now let us prove that
v
cannot be the maximal edge of
C(q,r)
. It is enough to prove that
q^{j}=p^{\prime\prime}wp^{\prime\prime}
is not the lexicographically greatest element in
[q^{j}]
. Let us consider two other conjugates of
q^{j}
:
(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}w
and
w(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}
. We claim that
(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}w\neq p^{\prime\prime}wp^{\prime\prime}
. Otherwise, from Lemma
2
, there exists a primitive
a
and two positive integers
t_{2}>t_{1}>0
such that
p^{\prime\prime}=a^{t_{1}},p^{\prime\prime}w=a^{t_{2}}
. However, in this case,
p^{j}=p^{\prime\prime}w=a^{t_{2}}
and
|a|<|p|
. Once more from Lemma
1
,
p
and
a
can not be both primitive. If
(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}w\prec p^{\prime\prime}wp^{\prime\prime}
, then
p^{\prime\prime}w\prec wp^{\prime\prime}
, but if so,
p^{\prime\prime}wp^{\prime\prime}\prec w(p^{\prime\prime})^{2}
. We conclude.

Now let us define the
Circuit Arrangement Order

\prec_{CAO}
for the small circuits on
\Gamma(w)
. Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be two small circuits in a same Rauzy graph

, we define

if

, where

is the maximal edge of the cycle

. From Lemma

, the small circuits in the same Rauzy graph are well-ordered under
\prec_{CAO}
.

Lemma 7

Let
w
be a finite word and let
\Gamma_{r}(w)
be a Rauzy graph of
w
for some
r
. Then the small circuits in
\Gamma_{r}(w)
are independent.

Proof

Let
C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{k}
be the small circuits in
\Gamma_{r}(w)
satisfying

C_{1}\prec_{CAO}C_{2}\prec_{CAO}\cdots\prec_{CAO}C_{k}.

If there exist some real numbers
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{k}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\mu(C_{i})=0
, where
\mu(C_{i})
is the vector-cycle corresponding to
C_{i}
, then we claim that
\alpha_{k}=0
. In fact, if we let
e_{(C_{k})}
be the maximal edge of
C_{k}
, then from Lemma
6
, the coefficient
c_{(C_{k})}
in
\mu(C_{i})
is
1
if
i=k
and
0
if
i<k
. Thus,
\alpha_{k}=0
. Doing this argument recurrently, we prove that
\alpha_{i}=0
for all
i
. Consequently,
C_{1},C_{2},\cdots,C_{k}
are independent.

Lemma 8

Let

be a finite word, then for any Rauzy graph
\Gamma_{r}(w)

Lemma 9

Let

Proof

Let

4
Injection from nonempty squares to small circuits

Let
w
be a finite word. For any square
u^{2}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
, there exists a primitive word
v\in\hbox{\rm Prim}(w)
and a positive integer
n
such that
u^{2}=v^{2n}
. Thus, we can gather the squares in terms of their smallest period. Let
v\in\hbox{\rm Prim}(w)
, a square
u^{2}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
is called
in the class of
v
if there is a primitive word
t\in[v]
and a positive integer
n
such that
u^{2}=t^{2n}
. Let
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
be the set of all squares in the class of
v
. Two classes
\hbox{\rm Class}(u)
and
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
are equal only if
u
and
v
are conjugate.

Now given a class
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
, let us define its
index
to be an integer
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)
such that
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)=\max\left\{n|n\in\mathbb{N^{+}},\exists u\in[v],\;\textrm{such that}\;u^{2n}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)\right\}
. Without loss of generality, a class is denoted by
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
if
v
is a primitive such that
v^{2n}
is in the class where
n
is the index of this class. In other words,
v^{2\hbox{\rm Index}(v)}\in\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
. From the definition, the elements in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
are all of the form
v_{s}(i)v^{2j-1}v_{p}(i)
with
1\leq i\leq|v|
and
1\leq j\leq\hbox{\rm Index}(v)
, let
v(i,j)
denote the square
v_{s}(i)v^{2j-1}v_{p}(i)
.

Example 3

Let
r=abcabcabcabca
. The squares
(abc)^{2},(bca)^{2},(cab)^{2},(abc)^{4},(bca)^{4}
are in the same class. The index of this class is
4
and this class can be denoted by
\hbox{\rm Class}(abc)
or
\hbox{\rm Class}(bca)
. However, it cannot be denoted by
\hbox{\rm Class}(cab)
, because
(cab)^{4}\not\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(r)
.

Lemma 10

Let
v
be a primitive factor of a finite word
w
such that
|v|=l
and that
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
is nonempty. If
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)\geq 2
, then for any couple of integers
(i,j)
satisfying
1\leq i\leq l
and
1\leq j\leq\hbox{\rm Index}(v)
, there exists a small circuit
C(v,jl+i-1)
in
\Gamma_{jl+i-1}(w)
. Further, there exists an injective function
f_{v}
which associates each square
v(i,j)
in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
to the small circuit
C(v,jl+i-1)
in
\left\{C(v,jl+i-1)|1\leq i\leq l,1\leq j\leq\hbox{\rm Index}(v)\right\}
.

Proof

For a given couple of integers
(i,j)
, it is enough to prove that

\left\{u^{j+\frac{i}{l}}|u\in[v]\right\}\subset\hbox{\rm Fac}(w).

If it is the case, then there exists a circuit in
\Gamma_{w}(jl+i-1)
such that its edge set is
\left\{u^{j+\frac{i}{l}}|u\in[v]\right\}
, further, it can be identified by
C(v,jl+i-1)
. From the hypothesis that
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)\geq 2
and
v^{2\hbox{\rm Index}(v)}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
, we have
v^{j+2}\in\hbox{\rm Fac}(w)
for all
j
satisfying
1\leq j\leq\hbox{\rm Index}(v)
and easily check that
\left\{u^{j+\frac{i}{l}}|u\in[v]\right\}\subset\hbox{\rm Fac}(v^{j+2})
.

The injectivity of the function is from the fact that the circuits
C(v,jl+i-1)
are pairwisely distinct. In fact, if there are two pairs of integers
(j_{1},i_{1}),(j_{2},i_{2})
satisfying
1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq l
,
j_{1}\geq j_{2}
and
j_{1}l+i_{1}-1=j_{2}l+i_{2}-1
, then
(j_{1}-j_{2})l=i_{1}-i_{2}
. However,
i_{1}-i_{2}<l
, hence
0\leq j_{1}-j_{2}<1
. Thus,
j_{1}=j_{2}
and
i_{1}=i_{2}
.

Lemma 11

Let
v
be a primitive factor of a finite word
w
such that
|v|=l
and that the size of
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
is
t
. If
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)=1
, then for each integer
i
such that
1\leq i\leq t
, there exists a small circuit
C(v,l+i-1)
in
\Gamma_{l+i-1}(w)
. Thus, there exists a bijective function
f_{v}
which associates each square in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
to one of the small circuits in
\left\{C(v,l+i-1)|1\leq i\leq t\right\}
.

Proof

It is enough to prove that for any positive integer
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq t
,

S_{i}=\left\{u^{1+\frac{i}{l}}|u\in[v]\right\}\subset\hbox{\rm Fac}(w).

If so, there exists a circuit in
\Gamma_{l+i-1}
such that its edge set is exactly
S_{i}
. Let us first consider the elements in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
. Under the hypothesis that
\hbox{\rm Index}(v)=1
, the elements in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
are all conjugates of
v^{2}
, thus they are all of the form
v_{s}(j)vv_{p}(j)
for some
j
. Consequently, the size of
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
is no larger than the number of conjugates of
v
, thus,
t\leq l
. Now let us consider the elements in
S_{i}
. For any given
i
satisfying
1\leq i\leq t
and any
u^{1+\frac{i}{l}}\in S_{i}
, there exists a couple of integers
(r,s)
satisfying
s\equiv r+i\;(\mod l)
and
u^{1+\frac{i}{l}}=v_{s}(r)v_{p}(s)
. For any
v_{s}(j)vv_{p}(j)\in\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
, a word
v_{s}(r)v_{p}(s)\in S_{i}
is not a factor of
v_{s}(j)vv_{p}(j)
if and only if
s<j<r
. Further, from the relation
s\equiv r+i\;(\mod l)
, we have
r-s=i
. Thus, for any
v_{s}(r)v_{p}(s)\in S_{i}
, there are at most
i-1
distinct words
v_{s}(j)vv_{p}(j)\in\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
which do not contain
v_{s}(r)v_{p}(s)
as a factor. However, from the hypothesis, there are exactly
t\geq i
elements in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
. Hence there exists at least one word in
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
such that
v_{s}(r)v_{p}(s)
is a factor of this word. Thus, all elements in
S_{i}
are factors of
w
.

Lemma 12

There exists an injective function
f
from the set of nonempty squares of
w
to the set of small circuits in
\Gamma(w)
.

Proof

Let us consider the function
f
satisfying that for any
\hbox{\rm Class}(v)
,

f|_{\hbox{\rm Class}(v)}=f_{v},

with
f_{v}
defined in the previous lemmas. Here we prove that this function is injective. If
C(p,i)
and
C(q,j)
are two images of two squares under
f
such that
C(p,i)=C(q,j)
, from Lemma
5
,
p=q
and
i=j
. However, if
p=q
, the pre-image of these two circuits are in the same class, and from the previous lemmas, the function
f
over a class is injective. Thus, these two squares are the same. Hence
f
is injective.

Proof
( of Theorem
1
)

From Lemma
12
and Lemma
9
, the number of distinct nonempty squares,
S(w)-1
, satisfies the equation

S(w)-1\leq sc(w)\leq|w|-|\hbox{\rm Alph}(w)|.
