Computing the optimal BWT of very large string collections
Davide Cenzato ∗, Veronica Guerrini †, Zsuzsanna Lipták ∗, and Giovanna Rosone † ∗ University of Verona, Italy † University of Pisa, Italy davide.cenzato@univr.it veronica.guerrini@di.unipi.it zsuzsanna.liptak@univr.it giovanna.rosone@unipi.it

Abstract
It is known that the exact form of the Burrows-Wheeler-Transform (BWT) of a string collection depends, in most implementations, on the input order of the strings in the collection. Reordering strings of an input collection affects the number of equal-letter runs r r , arguably the most important parameter of BWT-based data structures, such as the FM-index or the r r -index. Bentley, Gibney, and Thankachan [ESA 2020] introduced a linear-time algorithm for computing the permutation of the input collection which yields the minimum number of runs of the resulting BWT. In this paper, we present the first tool that guarantees a Burrows-Wheeler-Transform with minimum number of runs (optBWT), by combining i) an algorithm that builds the BWT from a string collection (either SAIS-based [Cenzato et al., SPIRE 2021] or BCR [Bauer et al., CPM 2011]); ii) the SAP array data structure introduced in [Cox et al., Bioinformatics, 2012]; and iii) the algorithm by Bentley et al. We present results both on real-life and simulated data, showing that the improvement achieved in terms of r r with respect to the input order is significant and the overhead created by the computation of the optimal BWT negligible, making our tool competitive with other tools for BWT-computation in terms of running time and space usage. In particular, on real data the optBWT obtains up to 31 times fewer runs with only a 1.39 × 1.39\times slowdown. Source code is available at https://github.com/davidecenzato/optimalBWT.git .

2. Basics

Let
\Sigma
be a finite ordered alphabet of size
\sigma
. We use the notation
S=S[1..n]
for a string of length
n
over
\Sigma
,
S[i]
for its
i
’th character, and
S[i..j]
for the substring
S[i]\cdots S[j]
, for
i\leq j
. By convention
S[i..j]=\varepsilon
if
i>j
, where
\varepsilon
denotes the empty string. The length of string
S
is denoted
|S|
. Substrings of
S[1..n]
of the form
S[1..i]
are called
prefixes
, and substrings of the form
S[i..n]

suffixes
; we denote the
i
’th suffix of
S
by
\textrm{Suf}_{i}(S)
. A substring (prefix, suffix) of
S
is called
proper
if it does not equal
S
. A
rotation
(also called
conjugate
) of string
S
is a string of the form
S[i..n]S[1..i-1]
, for some
1\leq i\leq n
. The
reverse
of string
S
is denoted
S^{\mathrm{rev}}=S[n]\cdots S[1]
. Note that we index strings from
1
.
The
Parikh vector
of a string
S
is an integer vector

(p_{1},\ldots,p_{\sigma})
, where
p_{j}=|\{i\mid S[i]={\tt a}_{j}\}|
gives the multiplicity in
S
of the
j
’th character
{\tt a}_{j}\in\Sigma
. A
run
in
S
is a maximal substring consisting of the same character.
For example, the string
{\tt CAAACCCTTTTG}
has Parikh vector
(3,4,1,4)
and
5
runs.

To mark the end of string
S
, often a new character (usually denoted
$
) not belonging to
\Sigma
is appended to it;
{\tt\$}
is set to be smaller than all characters in
\Sigma
. When convenient, we simply write
S[n+1]={\tt\$}
.

The
lexicographic order
on strings is defined as
S<_{\mathrm{lex}}T
if
S
is a proper prefix of
T
, or if there exists an index
1\leq i
such that
S[i]<T[i]
and for all
j<i
,
S[j]=T[j]
. The
colexicographic order
, or
colex order
, is defined as
S<_{\mathrm{colex}}T
if
S^{\mathrm{rev}}<_{\mathrm{lex}}T^{\mathrm{rev}}
. (The colex order is sometimes referred to as
reverse lexicographic order
, or
rlo
, see
[
11

17
]
).

Let
S
be a string over
\Sigma
and
S[n+1]={\tt\$}
. The
suffix array

[
20
]

\mathrm{SA}
of
S
is a permutation of the indices

such that
\mathrm{SA}[i]=j
if
\textrm{Suf}_{j}(S)
is the
i
’th suffix of
S
in lexicographic order among all suffixes of
S
.
The
Burrows-Wheeler-Transform
(BWT)
[
9
]
of
S
is defined as a permutation
L
of the characters of
S
:
L[i]={\tt\$}
if
\mathrm{SA}[i]=1
, and
L[i]=T[\mathrm{SA}[i]-1]
otherwise.
3
3

3

Since we assume that
S
is terminated by a
$

[
9
]
:
L[i]
is the last character of the
i
’th rotation of

A

is a multiset of strings
{\cal M}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}\}
, where each
S_{i}
is assumed to be terminated by a different dollar-character
{\tt\$}_{i}
and
{\tt\$}_{1}<{\tt\$}_{2}<\ldots<{\tt\$}_{k}
. Let
|S_{i}|=n_{i}
, then
||{\cal M}||=\sum_{i}n_{i}+k
is the total length of the collection.

The
\mathrm{BWT}
of

can be defined as the classical
\mathrm{BWT}
of the concatenated string
S_{1}\$_{1}S_{2}\$_{2}\cdots S_{k}\$_{k}
.
Alternatively, it can be defined without concatenation as follows: Let
\textrm{Suf}_{t}[S_{j}]
be the
i
’th suffix in lexicographic order, among all suffixes of strings in
{\cal M}
, then
\mathrm{BWT}[i]=\$_{j}
if

, and
\mathrm{BWT}[i]=S_{j}[t-1]
otherwise.

recursion level up. The SAP-array within a step can be computed along with the SA while inducing the L- and S-type suffixes. This is achieved via an adaptation of the inducing step that allows to propagate the information that we are within a shared suffix:
Let
S_{i}[t..n_{i}]
be a shared suffix; if at least two positions are preceded by the same character
c
then
cS_{i}[t..n_{i}]
corresponds to another SAP-interval. Since all occurrences of the same suffix are listed together in the SA, we can compute all SA-values in the new SAP-interval sequentially during the inducing step. This is carried out keeping track of suffixes starting with the same character, and updating the SAP-array accordingly in case they are induced by the same shared suffix.

3.3 Computing BWT and SAP using BCR

BCR algorithm is based on the idea of right-to-left scanning, at the same time, all the
k
strings and building the
\mathrm{BWT}
through
\ell+1
iterations, where
\ell
is the length of the longest string.
At each iteration, BCR considers a “slice” of (at most)
k
characters from the strings:
it starts by concatenating the symbols preceding all
{\tt\$}_{i}
, for all
i
, building a
partial
\mathrm{BWT}
(
\mathrm{BWT}_{0}
). Then, at iteration
j
, for
j=1,\ldots,k
, the symbols circularly preceding the suffixes
S_{i}[n_{i}-j+1..n_{i}]
(for all
1\leq i\leq k
) are inserted in the partial
\mathrm{BWT}_{j-1}
by simulating the insertion of these suffixes in the list of suffixes of length
h
(for all
h<j
) lexicographically sorted.

During the
j
’th step, we are able to compute and propagate from one iteration to the next the SAP-interval information (see also
[
2
,
3
,
11
]
4
4

4

Note that unlike
[
11
]
, we compute SAP-intervals for the current iteration.
). Indeed, when inserting symbols circularly preceding a shared suffix
S_{i}[n_{i}-j+1..n_{i}]
(for some
i
),
we can deduce the length of the SAP-interval that these symbols form (i.e., their number). Furthermore, we can distinguish whether a SAP-interval is an interesting interval or not (i.e., the symbols form a equal-character run), so that we can incrementally build along with the
\mathrm{BWT}
both the SAP-array and the reduced SAP-array.
