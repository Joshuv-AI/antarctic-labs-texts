Abstract
. We establish an optimal regularity result for parametrized two-dimensional stationary varifolds. Namely, we show that the parametrization map is a smooth minimal branched immersion and that the multiplicity function is constant. We provide some applications of this regularity result, especially in the calculus of variations for the area functional.

1.
Introduction

Geometric measure theory was born with the ambition of giving a general satisfactory solution to Plateau’s problem. After the work of Douglas and Radó in the two-dimensional case, the need was felt for an unparametrized approach, in order to overcome the difficulties arising in higher dimensions, seeking a weak notion of submanifold compatible with the calculus of variations. A successful theory was proposed only in 1960 by Federer and Fleming, in
[
17
]
. Their
theory of currents
merged the abstract homological framework of general de Rham’s currents with the seminal analytic ideas of generalized hypersurface (thought as a boundary of a finite-perimeter set), proposed by Caccioppoli and De Giorgi, and of rectifiable set, introduced by Besicovitch and his school. Integral currents satisfy a suitable weak compactness property and their area, called
mass
, is lower semicontinuous in the natural weak topology. These two crucial properties allow to use the so-called direct method to find a minimizer for the mass. The interior regularity for area minimizing currents in codimension one is due to the combined contributions of De Giorgi, Fleming, Almgren, Simons and Federer (see e.g.
[
44
, Chapter 7]
), while in arbitrary codimension the optimal interior regularity has been achieved by the successive efforts from Almgren, De Lellis and Spadaro (see
[
5
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
).

However, this weak notion of submanifold allows for
cancellation of mass
under weak limits. This phenomenon is not desirable in many variational problems, such as the min-max procedures to construct unstable critical points of the area, since in these instances one usually infers the nontriviality of the critical point from the value of its mass (one cannot rule out a trivial critical point by homotopical or homological means, as opposed to Plateau’s problem). To overcome this difficulty, in his unpublished notes
[
4
]
Almgren introduced the idea of
varifold
, which is simply a Radon measure on the Grassmannian bundle of a manifold. Having a measure on this bundle, rather than just on the base space, is fundamental in order to define the correct notion of pushforward under a diffeomorphism, thus obtaining a meaningful definition of stationarity extending the notion of minimal submanifold. As varifolds are just measures, their mass is retained under weak limits.

An important class of varifolds is formed by the integer rectifiable ones, namely those varifolds which can be represented as a countable superposition, with positive integer coefficients, of
k
-rectifiable sets. This is the kind of varifolds which is most commonly used and studied, since it is more concrete than the general definition but still enjoys compactness properties. The fundamental reference on general varifolds is
[
2
]
, where the compactness of integer stationary varifolds (or more generally of integer varifolds with locally bounded first variation) is proved, along with their regularity on a dense open set, rectifiability criteria for general varifolds and other important estimates. We mention that the existence and regularity theory for varifolds arising from min-max problems, started by Almgren, was later taken over by Pitts, Rubinstein and Smith (see
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
45
]
).

In this work we will consider integer stationary varifolds, in arbitrary codimension, admitting a parametrization in the following sense: they are induced by a weakly conformal map
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(\Sigma,{\mathcal{M}^{m}})
, where
\Sigma
is a closed Riemann surface and
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
is either a closed Riemannian manifold or
\mathbb{R}^{q}
itself, together with a multiplicity function
N\in L^{\infty}(\Sigma,\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})

on the domain
. They are required to satisfy a natural stationarity property which is local in the domain: namely, we assume that, for almost all domains
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
, the varifold induced by the map
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega}}
with the multiplicity function
{\left.\kern-1.2ptN\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega}}
is stationary in the complement of the compact set
\Phi(\partial\omega)
(see Definition
2.2
and Remark
2.3
for the precise definitions). Throughout the paper, such objects will be called
parametrized stationary varifolds
.

Our main result is the following, which appears in Corollary
5.8
and Remark
5.9
in the body of the paper.

Theorem 0.

The triple
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
is a parametrized stationary varifold, in a compact manifold
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
or in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
itself, if and only if
\Phi
is a smooth conformal harmonic map and
N
is a.e. constant. In this case,
\Phi
is a minimal branched immersion.

In fact we also get a local version of the result, which holds for
local parametrized stationary varifolds
: see Definition
2.9
and Theorem
5.7
.
Let us stress the fact that the result holds in
arbitrary codimension
and does not assume any
stability
hypothesis on the image varifold.

On the other hand, everywhere regularity for integer stationary varifolds does not hold without additional assumptions, not even in low dimension (see e.g. the picture below): Pitts was able to obtain the optimal regularity for varifolds arising via min-max in codimension one by introducing the stronger concept of
almost minimizing
varifold. So far, the regularity theory of integer stationary varifolds is still very incomplete and well understood only in special cases. Some notable examples are the structure theorem by Allard–Almgren for one-dimensional varifolds (see
[
3
]
), Allard’s almost everywhere regularity result for the case of constant multiplicity (see
[
2
, Section 8]
) and, for the stable, codimension one case, Schoen–Simon regularity theorem (see
[
43
]
) under the assumption that the singular set has locally finite
\mathcal{H}^{n-2}
-measure, which was recently reduced to an optimal assumption in a monumental work by Wickramasekera (see
[
46
]
). Also, the standard strata composing the singular set of a stationary varifold are now known to be rectifiable (see
[
34
]
). The almost everywhere regularity in full generality is still an open problem.

In the present situation, the parametrized structure of the varifold and the natural corresponding stationarity condition turn out to be a good replacement for the stable codimension one assumption (as well as other ad-hoc conditions to exclude singularities arising from self-intersecting minimal hypersurfaces) which was considered in much of the previous literature on the regularity theory. As it will be apparent in the paper, regularity stems from a subtle interaction between stationarity and the topological information of being parametrized.

The possibility to localize the stationarity with respect to the domain is crucially used in many places in order to get our main regularity result. The weak conformality of
\Phi
also happens to be important, since it ensures that the map
\Phi
is holomorphic when the codomain is the plane, a fact which we establish in Section
3
: the peculiar properties of nonconstant holomorphic functions, namely that they are branched covering maps and that they cannot vanish to infinite order, turn out to be useful several times.

Our main result relies on the previous treatment of the simpler situation where
N
is constant (see
[
40
]
, by the second author; see also Theorem
2.13
below). We point out that the starting point of the strategy of
[
40
]
, i.e. the observation that Lebesgue points
z
for
\Phi
and
d\Phi
(with
d\Phi(z)\neq 0
) belong to the regular set of
\Phi
, does not apply when
N
is a priori not constant. This difficulty is due to the fact that we cannot invoke Allard’s
\varepsilon
-regularity result in this more general situation.

We will actually show that
N
is a.e. constant and
\Phi
satisfies the harmonic map equation
-\Delta\Phi=A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)
in any local conformal chart. These two facts are essentially equivalent to each other, in view of the preliminary work
[
40
]
, and in some situations it will be easier to establish the former, while in other instances (where Lemma
5.1
is invoked) the latter is more convenient. The smoothness of weak solutions to the harmonic map equation was first proved in full generality by Hélein (see
[
23
, Section 4.1]
) and then obtained again by the second author in
[
38
]
, as a consequence of a general result for linear elliptic systems with an antisymmetric potential. In our situation the smoothness of
\Phi
is a classical fact, since for parametrized stationary varifolds the map
\Phi
is easily seen to be continuous (see Proposition
2.4
).

We want to give a simple one-dimensional example illustrating why, in spite of the difficulties surrounding integer stationary varifolds, one should expect to get much more information from the notion of parametrized stationary varifold (and eventually the regularity). The picture depicts a portion of an integer 1-rectifiable stationary varifold in
S^{2}
(e.g. the union of three geodesic segments joining the north and south poles, with an angle
\frac{2\pi}{3}
between any two of them), with multiplicity 2 a.e. This varifold has a nontrivial singular set.

This varifold can be parametrized by a map
\Phi:S^{1}\to S^{2}
, as shown in the picture. However, the parametrized varifold
(S^{1},\Phi,1)
fails to satisfy the local stationarity condition, as witnessed by the highlighted part on the right. Of course, this example is just a heuristic motivation for the hope to obtain the full regularity, as no effective structure theorem is known for two-dimensional stationary varifolds.

As already said, in view of
[
40
]
, the problem of showing that
N
is a.e. constant is of the same difficulty. In this sense our result can be seen as a
constancy theorem
in the domain, for the class of parametrized stationary varifolds. The classical constancy theorem asserts that a stationary varifold in a smooth connected manifold of the same dimension has constant density (see
[
44
, Theorem 41.1]
). Showing that it holds in other settings and under weaker assumptions is a challenging topic in geometric measure theory: we just mention that its truth for stationary varifolds with support contained in a Lipschitz graph (of the same dimension) is a difficult result by Duggan (see
[
14
, Corollary 4.5]
).

A natural question is how to produce parametrized stationary varifolds within the calculus of variations for the area functional. Indeed, one motivation for studying this class of varifolds comes from the previous work
[
39
]
by the second author, who developed a new theory for the construction of immersed (possibly branched) minimal surfaces with arbitrary topology, by means of a viscosity method. Namely, one first finds an immersion
\Phi:\Sigma\to M
which is critical for the perturbed area functional

A^{\sigma}(\Phi):=\int_{\Sigma}\,d\text{vol}+\sigma^{2}\int_{\Sigma}(1+\left|A\right|^{2})^{p}\,d\text{vol},\quad 2<p<\infty\text{ fixed},

where
\Sigma
is a Riemann surface (chosen afterwards to make
\Phi
conformal), the second
A
is the second fundamental form of the immersion and
d\text{vol}
is the volume form induced by
\Phi
. The coercivity of this functional ensures that it enjoys a sort of Palais–Smale condition up to diffeomorphisms. The second author shows that, choosing a sequence
\Phi_{j}
of critical points of
E^{\sigma_{j}}
satisfying a certain entropy condition and making the domains converge in the Deligne–Mumford compactification, then the surfaces
\Phi_{j}(\Sigma_{j})
converge precisely to a parametrized stationary varifold (up to bubbling and nodal points), with a pointwise multiplicity possibly bigger than 1.

Bringing the main result of
[
39
]
together with the one of this paper, one gets the following.

Corollary 0.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\operatorname{Imm}(\Sigma,{\mathcal{M}^{m}}))
be a collection of families of
C^{1}
immersions and assume that
\mathcal{F}
is stable under isotopies (through homeomorphisms) of the space
\operatorname{Imm}(\Sigma,{\mathcal{M}^{m}})
. Then the min-max value

\inf_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\max_{\Phi\in F}\left|\Phi(\Sigma)\right|

is the area of a branched, possibly disconnected immersed minimal surface, with a locally constant integer multiplicity.

Such families
\mathcal{F}
arise for instance when considering sweep-outs by surfaces realizing some homotopy class: see e.g.
[
8
]
for examples and further discussion.
The previous corollary should be put in perspective with the existing literature on min-max for surfaces as follows.

•

For 1-parameter families of spheres, namely for a mountain pass min-max, some results about compactness and absence of energy loss were obtained by Jost: see e.g.
[
25
]
(which more generally deals with the min-max of harmonic maps). We also refer to the work by Jost–Struwe in
[
26
]
for related results in the context of Plateau’s problem in the Euclidean space.

•

For 1-parameter families of spheres, Colding and Minicozzi in
[
9
]
proved the existence of a minimal sphere, using an ingenious harmonic replacement technique.

•

Again for 1-parameter families, Colding–Minicozzi approach for finding minimal spheres by means of harmonic maps was recently extended by Zhou in
[
47
]
to arbitrary genus, by adding the conformal class as an additional variable in the variational problem.

•

In codimension one, the aforementioned Almgren–Pitts theory allows to construct smooth embedded minimal (hyper)surfaces from a min-max in a suitable space of cycles. This approach was then refined and adapted to other settings (see e.g.
[
10
]
and
[
31
]
) and has been responsible for many important applications (see e.g.
[
30
]
). For a survey on a simplified approach, which is a combination of the contributions of Almgren, Meeks, Pitts, Simon, Smith and Yau, we refer the reader to the paper
[
8
]
by Colding and De Lellis (which deals for simplicity with 1-parameter sweepouts).

•

Still in codimension one, another min-max approach based on the link between minimal surfaces and phase transitions was recently proposed by Guaraco in
[
20
]
.
It allows to interpret certain sweepouts in terms of level sets of functions and, in three dimensions, it allows to obtain multiplicity one and the expected Morse index for the resulting minimal surface: this was recently shown by Chodosh and Mantoulidis for generic metrics in
[
7
]
.

During the investigation of this problem, as a potential intermediate step towards the regularity, we asked ourselves whether any conformal solution
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
to the so-called
conductivity equation

-\operatorname{div}(N\nabla\Phi)=0
(for some bounded measurable
N
with values in positive integers) is necessarily harmonic. Actually, we can give a positive answer to this question as a consequence of the main theorem.

Corollary 0.

Assume
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
is weakly conformal,
N\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
and

-\operatorname{div}(N\nabla\Phi)=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q}).

Then
\Delta\Phi=0
and, if
\Phi
is nonconstant,
N
is a.e. constant.

We refer to Theorem
6.2
in the body of the paper. However, we are not aware of any purely analytic proof of this fact and leave it as an open problem to find such a proof. We are able to succeed in this task in the planar case
q=2
: see Theorem
6.1
.

2.
First properties of parametrized stationary varifolds

Let
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
be either a closed embedded
C^{\infty}
-smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{q}
or
\mathbb{R}^{q}
itself, where
q\geq m\geq 2
are arbitrary integers. Let
\Sigma
be a closed connected Riemann surface.

A map
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{q})
is
weakly conformal
if, for a.e.
x\in\Sigma
,
d\Phi(x)
is either zero or a linear conformal map, with respect to the conformal structure of
\Sigma
. For any such map we call
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\Sigma
the set of Lebesgue points for both
\Phi
and
d\Phi
and we let
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}:d\Phi(0)\neq 0\right\}
(hence
d\Phi(x)
is injective and conformal for
x\in{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
).

Definition 2.1 (almost every domain).

We say that a certain property holds
for almost every domain

\omega\subseteq\Sigma
if, for any nonnegative
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, the property holds for
\omega=\left\{\rho>t\right\}
, for a.e. regular value
t>0
(so in particular it holds for
\Sigma
, as is seen by choosing
\rho\equiv 1
).
Similarly, given an open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, a property holds
for almost every domain

\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
if, for any nonnegative
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
, the property holds for
\omega=\left\{\rho>t\right\}
, for a.e. regular value
t>0
. ∎

In the definition below, we will implicitly restrict to the regular values
t>0
of
\rho
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial\left\{\rho>t\right\}}}
has a continuous representative (which are a set of full measure, by Sard’s theorem and
[
15
, Theorem 4.21]
) and, with a slight abuse of notation,
\Phi(\partial\omega)
will denote the image by this continuous representative.

Definition 2.2 (parametrized stationary varifold).

A triple
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
with
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(\Sigma,\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
N\in L^{\infty}(\Sigma,\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
and
\Phi(\Sigma)\subseteq{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
is called a
parametrized integer 2-rectifiable stationary varifold
(in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
) if
\Phi
is nonconstant, weakly conformal and if, for almost every domain
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
,

(2.1)

\int_{\omega}N\Big(\left\langle d(F(\Phi));d\Phi\right\rangle_{h}-F(\Phi)\cdot A(\Phi)(d\Phi,d\Phi)_{h}\Big)\,d\text{vol}_{h}=0

for all
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
.
Here
h
is an arbitrary Riemannian metric compatible with the conformal structure of
\Sigma
(its choice does not matter, by conformal invariance),
A(X,Y)=-\nabla_{X}^{\mathbb{R}^{q}}Y
denotes the second fundamental form of
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
(
A=0
if
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
) and
A(\Phi)(d\Phi,d\Phi)_{h}
is defined in local coordinates by
\sum_{i,j}h^{ij}A(\Phi)(\partial_{i}\Phi,\partial_{j}\Phi)
. ∎

We will usually just say that
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
is a
parametrized stationary varifold
.

Remark 2.3 (equivalent definition).

The definition is clearly independent of the particular representatives of
\Phi
,
d\Phi
,
N
. Calling
\mathcal{G}
the set of Lebesgue points for both
\Phi
and
d\Phi
and applying Lemma
A.2
to a finite atlas of conformal charts, we see that
\Phi(\mathcal{G})
is
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-rectifiable (and
\mathcal{H}^{2}
-measurable). Moreover, again by Lemma
A.2
, the area formula applies and (
2.1
) amounts to say that for almost every
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
the 2-rectifiable varifold

\mathbf{v}_{\omega}:=(\Phi(\mathcal{G}\cap\omega),\theta_{\omega}),\quad\theta_{\omega}(p):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{G}\cap\omega\cap\Phi^{-1}(p)}N(x)

is stationary in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
, as is easily seen by writing
F=\pi_{T{\mathcal{M}^{m}}}F+\pi_{T^{\perp}{\mathcal{M}^{m}}}F
.
In particular, the generalized mean curvature of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
is bounded (in
L^{\infty}
) by
\sqrt{2}\max_{\mathcal{M}^{m}}\left|A\right|
. ∎

Proposition 2.4 (continuity of
\bm{\Phi}
).

The map
\Phi
has a continuous representative. This representative (still called
\Phi
) satisfies the stationarity property for every open subset
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
, namely

\int_{\omega}N\Big(\left\langle d(F\circ\Phi);d\Phi\right\rangle_{h}-F(\Phi)\cdot A(\Phi)\left(d\Phi,d\Phi\right)_{h}\Big)\,d\text{vol}_{h}=0

for all
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
. Moreover, if
\omega
is connected,
\Phi(\overline{\omega})=\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
unless
\Phi
is constant on
\omega
.

Proof.

Let
\phi:U\to\Omega
be a local conformal chart (with
U\subseteq\Sigma
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
) and let
\Psi:=\Phi\circ\phi^{-1}
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}:=\phi(\mathcal{G}\cap U)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}^{f}}:=\phi({\mathcal{G}^{f}}\cap U)
. For any
x\in\Omega
and any
r<\frac{1}{2}\dist(x,\partial\Omega)
we can apply Lemma
A.3
(with
\tau=1
) and obtain a radius
r^{\prime}\in(r,2r)
such that (
2.1
) applies with
\omega=\phi^{-1}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))
and such that

(2.2)

\diam\Psi(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))\leq\sqrt{4\pi}\left(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\quad\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})=0.

Let us assume that
\Psi
is not (a.e.) constant on
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
. If
z\in{\overline{B}}_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)\cap\widetilde{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
we have
\Psi(z)\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
, as

\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Psi(z)))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Psi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(\Psi(z)))\cap B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}(N\circ\phi^{-1})\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}>0

for all
s>0
. Hence, since
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, by Lemma
A.1
the essential image of
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}}
is included in
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
. The converse inclusion trivially holds, as well, so we conclude that the essential image of
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}}
coincides with
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
; in particular, the latter includes the compact set
\Gamma:=\Psi(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))
(by the second part of (
2.2
)).

Moreover, since in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Gamma
the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
has generalized mean curvature bounded by
\sqrt{2}\left\|A\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
, from the monotonicity formula
[
44
, Theorem 17.6]
and
[
44
, Remark 17.9(1)]
we deduce

\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}(N\circ\phi^{-1})\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\geq\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(p))\geq e^{-(\sqrt{2}\left\|A\right\|_{L^{\infty}})s}\cdot\pi s^{2}

for all
p\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)\setminus\Gamma
and all
s\leq\dist(p,\Gamma)
. If
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
is compact, choosing
s:=\dist(p,\Gamma)\leq\diam{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
and recalling (
2.2
), we conclude that

(2.3)

\begin{split}&\diam\Psi(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\cap B_{r}^{2}(x))\leq\diam\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)\leq\diam\Gamma+2\max_{p\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)}\dist(p,\Gamma)\\
&\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\pi}+\left(e^{(\sqrt{2}\left\|A\right\|_{L^{\infty}})\diam{\mathcal{M}^{m}}}\frac{\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

If instead
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
, then we have

(2.4)

\begin{split}&\diam\Psi(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\cap B_{r}^{2}(x))\leq\diam\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)\leq\diam\Gamma+2\sup_{p\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)}\dist(p,\Gamma)\\
&\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\pi}+\left(\frac{\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2\pi}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

This estimate for
\diam\Psi(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\cap B_{r}^{2}(x))
is trivially true also when
\Psi
is a.e. constant on
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
. The last expressions are infinitesimal as
r\to 0
, locally uniformly in
x
. We infer that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}}}
is locally uniformly continuous on
\Omega
and thus has a continuous representative. This shows that
\Phi
has a continuous representative.

We record here another estimate for
\diam\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
independent of
\diam{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
, which will be useful later. All the points in
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
have distance at most
2D+2
from
\Gamma
, where
D:=\frac{e^{H}}{\pi}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(\mathbb{R}^{q})
and
H
is an upper bound for the generalized mean curvature of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Gamma
: if this were not the case, we would have
\Phi
nonconstant on
\omega
and thus
\Gamma\subseteq\Phi(\overline{\omega})=\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
. By connectedness of
\Phi(\overline{\omega})
we could find points
p_{j}\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
such that
\dist(p_{j},\Gamma)=2j
, for
1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor D\right\rfloor+1
, and since the balls
B_{1}^{q}(p_{j})
are disjoint we would have

\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(\mathbb{R}^{q})\geq\sum_{j}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{1}^{q}(p_{j}))\geq(\left\lfloor D\right\rfloor+1)e^{-H}\pi>De^{-H}\pi,

which is a contradiction. We deduce that

(2.5)

\diam\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)\leq\diam\Gamma+\frac{4e^{H}}{\pi}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(\mathbb{R}^{q})+4.

We now show the statement about the stationarity property. If
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
then we can find a nonnegative
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\omega)
such that
\rho=1
on the compact set
\omega\cap\Phi^{-1}(\operatorname{supp}\left(F\right))
. For almost every
t\in(0,1)
the stationarity property (
2.1
) holds in
\left\{\rho>t\right\}
, so

\int_{\left\{\rho>t\right\}}N\Big(\left\langle d(F\circ\Phi);d\Phi\right\rangle_{h}-F(\Phi)A(\Phi)\left(d\Phi,d\Phi\right)_{h}\Big)\,d\text{vol}_{h}=0

and clearly the left-hand side does not change if we replace
\left\{\rho>t\right\}
with
\omega
.

Finally, the last statement is obtained with the same argument used in the first part of the proof.
∎

From now on, we will always assume that the map
\Phi
is continuous, for any parametrized stationary varifold.

Proposition 2.5 (negligibility of
\bm{\Sigma\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}}
in the target).

We have
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Phi(\Sigma\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}))=0
.

Proof.

As already observed in Remark
2.3
, the area formula can be applied on subsets of
\mathcal{G}
. In particular, since
d\Phi=0
on
\mathcal{G}\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
, we get
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Phi(\mathcal{G}\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}))=0
.
In order to show that
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Phi(\Sigma\setminus\mathcal{G}))=0
, we pick any local conformal chart
\phi:U(\subseteq\Sigma)\to\Omega(\subseteq\mathbb{C})
and, as in the previous proof, we set
\Psi:=\Phi\circ\phi^{-1}
and

\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}:=\phi(\mathcal{G}\cap U)
.

Fix an arbitrary
\delta>0
and an open set
W\subseteq\Omega
containing
\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}
. For any
z\in W
we can find a radius
r<\frac{1}{2}\dist(z,\partial W)\wedge 1
such that

\overline{C}\left(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\delta
, where
\overline{C}
is the constant appearing in the right-hand side of (
2.3
) (or (
2.4
) if
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
), and

\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq 8\int_{B_{r}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}+4r^{2}:

indeed, if such
r
did not exist, for
j
big enough we would have
(2^{-j})^{2}\leq\int_{B_{2^{-j}}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{8}\int_{B_{2^{-j+1}}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
, hence
(2^{-j})^{2}\leq\int_{B_{2^{-j}}^{2}(z)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=O(2^{-3j})=o((2^{-j})^{2})
, which is a contradiction. By Besicovitch covering theorem, we can extract countably many balls
B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i})
from this collection with
\bm{1}_{W}\leq\sum_{i}\bm{1}_{B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i})}\leq\mathfrak{N}\bm{1}_{W}
, for some universal constant
\mathfrak{N}
. By inequality (
2.3
) (or (
2.4
)) we have
\diam\Phi(B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i}))<\delta
and

\begin{split}\sum_{i}\frac{\pi}{4}(\diam\Phi(B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i})))^{2}&\leq\frac{\pi{\overline{C}}^{2}}{4}\sum_{i}\int_{B_{2r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i})}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\\
&\leq 2\pi{\overline{C}}^{2}\sum_{i}\int_{B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i})}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}+{\overline{C}}^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{r_{i}}^{2}(x_{i}))\\
&\leq 2\pi{\overline{C}}^{2}\mathfrak{N}\int_{W}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}+{\overline{C}}^{2}\mathfrak{N}\mathcal{L}^{2}(W).\end{split}

Since
\delta
was arbitrary, we get
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Phi(\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}))\leq 2\pi{\overline{C}}^{2}\mathfrak{N}\int_{W}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}+{\overline{C}}^{2}\mathfrak{N}\mathcal{L}^{2}(W)
. Since
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{G}})=0
and
W
was arbitrary as well, we arrive at
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\Phi(\Omega\setminus\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}))=0
.
∎

Proposition 2.6 (structure of fibers).

For any
p\in{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
the compact set
\Phi^{-1}(p)
has finitely many connected components. If
x\in{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
then
x
is isolated in
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))
.

Proof.

Since the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\Sigma}
is stationary, the limit

M:=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Sigma}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(p))}{\pi s^{2}}

exists. We claim that the number of connected components of
\Phi^{-1}(p)
is not greater than
M
. If this were not the case, we could split
\Phi^{-1}(p)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{J}K_{j}
, where the subsets
K_{j}
are disjoint and compact and
J>M
is a finite integer (if the maximum value of
J
such that this can be done were at most
M
, then one of the subsets would be disconnected, contradicting the maximality). We could then find disjoint open neighborhoods
\omega_{j}\supseteq K_{j}
and we would have

M=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Sigma}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(p))}{\pi s^{2}}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{J}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{j}}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(p))}{\pi s^{2}}\geq J

(by
[
44
, Remark 17.9(1)]
: notice that
\Phi
must be nonconstant on any connected component of
\omega_{j}
intersecting
K_{j}
, hence by Proposition
2.4

p\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{j}}\right\|\right)
). This is a contradiction.

Assume now
x\in{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
and call
K_{x}
the connected component of
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))
containing
x
. It suffices to show that
K_{x}=\left\{x\right\}
, since we already know that
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))
is a finite union of compact connected sets. If
\phi
is a local conformal chart centered at
x
and
\Psi:=\Phi\circ\phi^{-1}
, as in the proof of Proposition
2.8
below we can find a radius
r^{\prime}>0
such that
\Phi(x)=\Psi(0)\not\in\Psi(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))
. Hence
K_{x}\subseteq\phi^{-1}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))
and, since
r^{\prime}
is arbitrarily small, we deduce
K_{x}=\phi^{-1}(\left\{0\right\})=\left\{x\right\}
.
∎

We now define a more robust representative
\widetilde{N}
of the multiplicity function
N
, which is canonically defined everywhere and is upper semicontinuous. We point out that (a priori)
\widetilde{N}
could take values in
\left[1,\infty\right)
instead of
\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\}
.

Definition 2.7 (robust representative of
\bm{N}
).

Given
x\in\Sigma
, we call
K_{x}
the connected component of
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))
containing
x
and we let

\widetilde{N}(x):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega\supseteq K_{x},\\
\Phi(x)\not\in\Phi(\partial\omega)\end{subarray}}\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(x)))}{\pi s^{2}}.

The limit exists and is at least
1
, by the stationarity of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
(which contains
\Phi(x)
) and the fact that
\Phi(x)\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|\right)
(by Lemma
2.4
, since
\Phi
is necessarily nonconstant on the connected component of
\omega
containing
x
). Notice that
\widetilde{N}=\widetilde{N}(x)
on
K_{x}
. Moreover, the infimum is actually a minimum and is achieved whenever
\overline{\omega}
is disjoint from the compact set
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))\setminus K_{x}
. ∎

Proposition 2.8 (upper semicontinuity of
\widetilde{\bm{N}}
).

The function
\widetilde{N}
is upper semicontinuous and
\widetilde{N}\geq 1
. Moreover,
\widetilde{N}=N
a.e. on
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
.

Proof.

We already observed that
\widetilde{N}\geq 1
everywhere. Let
\lambda>1
and
x\in\Sigma
such that
\widetilde{N}(x)<\lambda
. Choose any open set
\omega\supseteq K_{x}
with
\overline{\omega}
disjoint from
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))\setminus K_{x}
, so that
\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(x)))}{\pi s^{2}}<\lambda
. Whenever
z\in\omega
is close enough to
x
we have
\Phi(z)\not\in\Phi(\partial\omega)
, so
K_{z}\subseteq\omega
and by definition

\widetilde{N}(z)\leq\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(z)))}{\pi s^{2}}.

But as
z\to x
we have
\Phi(z)\to\Phi(x)
. Hence, eventually
\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(z)))}{\pi s^{2}}<\lambda
(see
[
44
, Corollary 17.8]
) and so
\widetilde{N}(z)<\lambda
.

Assume now
x\in{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
and
\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}\left|N-N(x)\right|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=o(r^{2})
,
\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi-\nabla\Phi(x)\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=o(r^{2})
. Fix any open set
\omega
containing
x
. Let
\phi
be a local conformal chart centered at
x
and set
\Psi:=\Phi\circ\phi^{-1}
,
\alpha:=\left(\frac{\left|\nabla\Psi(0)\right|}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{-1}
. For any
s>0
small enough we have

\begin{split}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(x)))&\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\alpha s}^{2}(0)\cap\Psi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(\Psi(0)))}(N\circ\phi^{-1})\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\\
&\geq\frac{1}{2}N(x)\left|\nabla\Psi(0)\right|^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big(B_{\alpha s}^{2}(0)\cap\Psi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(\Psi(0)))\Big)\\
&\phantom{\geq}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\alpha s}^{2}(0)}\left|(N\circ\phi^{-1})\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}-N(x)\left|\nabla\Psi(0)\right|^{2}\right|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}.\end{split}

By
[
15
, Theorem 6.1]
, the function
s^{-1}(\Psi(\alpha s\,\cdot)-\Psi(0))
converges to
\alpha\left\langle\nabla\Psi(0),\cdot\right\rangle
(which is a linear isometry) in measure on
B_{1}^{2}(0)
, hence the first term in the right-hand side is
\pi N(x)s^{2}+o(s^{2})
. Moreover, the function
N\circ\phi^{-1}(\alpha s\,\cdot)
converges to
N(x)
in measure and is bounded by
\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
, while
\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}(\alpha s\,\cdot)\to\left|\nabla\Psi(0)\right|^{2}
in
L^{1}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
. So the last term in the right-hand side is
o(s^{2})
.
This shows that
\widetilde{N}(x)\geq N(x)
.

Fix now any
0<\varepsilon<\alpha^{-1}
. By Lemma
A.4
, applied to
y\mapsto\Psi(y)-\Psi(0)-\left\langle\nabla\Psi(0),y\right\rangle
, we can find a radius
r^{\prime}
such that

\left|\Psi(r^{\prime}y)-\Psi(0)-\left\langle\nabla\Psi(0),r^{\prime}y\right\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon r^{\prime}

for all
y\in S^{1}
.
Thus, choosing
\omega:=\phi^{-1}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))
and applying the monotonicity formula,

\widetilde{N}(x)\leq e^{(\sqrt{2}\left\|A\right\|_{L^{\infty}})(\beta-\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{(\beta-\varepsilon)r^{\prime}}^{q}(\Phi(x)))}{\pi(\beta-\varepsilon)^{2}(r^{\prime})^{2}}\leq(1+O(r^{\prime}))\frac{\int_{B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}(N\circ\phi^{-1})\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}}{2\pi(\beta-\varepsilon)^{2}(r^{\prime})^{2}},

where
\beta:=\frac{\left|\nabla\Psi(0)\right|}{\sqrt{2}}=\alpha^{-1}
.
Since
r^{\prime}
is arbitrarily small, we get
\widetilde{N}(x)\leq\frac{N(x)\beta^{2}}{(\beta-\varepsilon)^{2}}
. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
we get the converse inequality
\widetilde{N}(x)\leq N(x)
.
∎

It is useful to introduce the following local notion of parametrized stationary varifold.

Definition 2.9 (local counterpart).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be open. A triple
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
with
\Phi\in W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
N\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
and
\Phi(\Omega)\subseteq{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
is called a
local parametrized stationary varifold
(in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
) if
\Phi
is weakly conformal and if, for almost every domain
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
,

\int_{\omega}N\Big(\left\langle\nabla(F(\Phi));\nabla\Phi\right\rangle-F(\Phi)\cdot A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)\Big)\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=0

for all
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathcal{M}^{m}}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
, with
A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi):=A(\Phi)(\partial_{1}\Phi,\partial_{1}\Phi)+A(\Phi)(\partial_{2}\Phi,\partial_{2}\Phi)
.
We also require that

(2.6)

\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(p))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Phi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(p))}N\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=O(s^{2}),

uniformly in
p\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
. ∎

Notice that, in the last definition, the map
\Phi
is allowed to be constant. The technical assumption (
2.6
) will be used only in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, which in turn is used in Sections
3
and
5
.

If
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)

) holds thanks to the monotonicity formula satisfied by

. ∎

2.4
applies to the local case as well (with

Remark 2.12 (isolating sets with high
\widetilde{\bm{N}}
).

A useful fact which will be used in Sections
3
and
5
is the following: if
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
is a local parametrized stationary varifold and
x\in\Omega
satisfies
\widetilde{N}(x)<\infty
, then for any
0<\varepsilon<1
we can find a neighborhood
K_{x}\subseteq\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
with
\Phi(\omega)\cap\Phi(\partial\omega)=\emptyset
and such that
\omega\cap\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y))=K_{y}
whenever
y\in\omega
has
\widetilde{N}(y)\geq\widetilde{N}(x)-\varepsilon
.

Indeed, let
K_{x}\subseteq\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
with
\overline{\omega}
disjoint from
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))\setminus K_{x}
, so that
\widetilde{N}(x)
is the density of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
at
\Phi(x)
.
If
y_{1},y_{2}\in\omega
have the same image and are close enough to
K_{x}
, then
K_{y_{1}},K_{y_{2}}\subseteq\omega
(since
\Phi(y_{1})=\Phi(y_{2})\not\in\Phi(\partial\omega)
) and the density of
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
at
\Phi(y_{1})=\Phi(y_{2})
is less than
\widetilde{N}(x)+1-\varepsilon
(by upper semicontinuity of the density). Hence,
if
K_{y_{1}}\neq K_{y_{2}}
, calling
K_{y_{i}}\subseteq\omega_{i}\subseteq\omega
two disjoint neighborhoods with
\Phi(y_{i})\not\in\Phi(\partial\omega_{i})
we get

\begin{split}\widetilde{N}(y_{1})+1&\leq\widetilde{N}(y_{1})+\widetilde{N}(y_{2})\leq\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{1}}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(y_{1})))}{\pi s^{2}}+\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{2}}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(y_{2})))}{\pi s^{2}}\\
&\leq\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{q}(\Phi(y_{1})))}{\pi s^{2}}<\widetilde{N}(x)+1-\varepsilon.\end{split}

The claim is established by shrinking
\omega
and by replacing it with
\omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\partial\omega))
. ∎

Theorem 2.13 (constant multiplicity case).

Let
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
be a parametrized stationary varifold in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
.
If

is a.e. constant (and hence can be changed to

without affecting the stationarity), then

3.
Regularity of parametrized stationary varifolds in a polyhedral cone

This section addresses the regularity problem for local parametrized stationary varifolds in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, under the additional constraint that they are contained in a finite union of 2-dimensional planes through the origin.
We will need a nontrivial result in planar topology, which we state and prove below.

3.1.
A topological lemma about triods

Definition 3.1 (generalized triod).

A
generalized triod
in
S^{2}
is a quadruple
T=(K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
such that:

•

\emptyset\neq K\subseteq S^{2}
is compact and connected;

•

\gamma_{i}\in C^{\infty}([0,1],S^{2})
are injective regular curves (i.e.
\dot{\gamma}(t)\neq 0
for all
t\in[0,1]
);

•

K
,
\gamma_{1}(\left[0,1\right))
,
\gamma_{2}(\left[0,1\right))
,
\gamma_{3}(\left[0,1\right))
are pairwise disjoint;

•

\gamma_{i}(1)\in K
.

We will denote
\operatorname{supp}\left(T\right):=K\sqcup\gamma_{1}(\left[0,1\right))\sqcup\gamma_{2}(\left[0,1\right))\sqcup\gamma_{3}(\left[0,1\right))
. ∎

The proof of the following lemma is inspired by the proof of a simpler statement which appears in
[
1
, Lemma 2.15]
.

Lemma 3.2 (uncountably many triods intersect).

Let
(T_{j})_{j\in J}
be a collection of generalized triods in
S^{2}
such that
\operatorname{supp}\left(T_{j}\right)\cap\operatorname{supp}\left(T_{j^{\prime}}\right)=\emptyset
for any
j\neq j^{\prime}
. Then
J
is at most countable.

Proof.

We equip the set
\mathcal{T}
of all generalized triods in
S^{2}
with the following metric: given
T=(K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
,
T^{\prime}=(K^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime},\gamma_{3}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
we set

d(T,T^{\prime}):=d_{H}(K,K^{\prime})+\sum_{i}\max_{t\in[0,1]}d_{S^{2}}(\gamma_{i}(t),\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(t)),

where
d_{S^{2}}
denotes the spherical distance on
S^{2}
and
d_{H}
is the corresponding Hausdorff distance on the set of all nonempty compact subsets of
S^{2}
.

Since the metric space
(\mathcal{T},d)
is separable, it suffices to show that any triod
T_{j_{0}}
is isolated in
\left\{T_{j}\mid j\in J\right\}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
. Let
T_{j_{0}}=(K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
.

Case 1:

\gamma_{1}(\left[0,1\right)),\gamma_{2}(\left[0,1\right)),\gamma_{3}(\left[0,1\right))

do not belong to the same connected component of

S^{2}\setminus K
.
Assume for instance that
\gamma_{1}(\left[0,1\right))
and
\gamma_{2}(\left[0,1\right))
belong to different connected components: then, letting

\varepsilon:=\min\left\{d_{S^{2}}(\gamma_{1}(0),K),d_{S^{2}}(\gamma_{2}(0),K)\right\}>0,

any different triod
T_{j}=(K^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime},\gamma_{3}^{\prime})
satisfies
d(T_{j_{0}},T_{j})\geq\varepsilon
. Indeed, if this were not the case,
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(0)
would lie in the same component of
S^{2}\setminus K
as
\gamma_{1}(0)
(since the spherical ball
B_{\varepsilon}^{S^{2}}(\gamma_{1}(0))
is a connected subset of
S^{2}\setminus K
) and similarly for
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(0)
. But this contradicts the fact that
\operatorname{supp}\left(T_{j}\right)
is a connected subset of
S^{2}\setminus K
.

Case 2:

\gamma_{1}(\left[0,1\right)),\gamma_{2}(\left[0,1\right)),\gamma_{3}(\left[0,1\right))

belong to the same connected component

U

of

S^{2}\setminus K
.
Since
K
is connected, there exists a diffeomorphism

\upsilon:U\to B_{1}^{2}(0)\subseteq\mathbb{C}

(indeed,
S^{2}\setminus U
is connected and we can apply
[
41
, Theorems 13.11 and 14.8]
).
For
t\in\left[0,1\right)
let
\alpha_{i}(t):=\upsilon\circ\gamma_{i}(t)
.
Notice that
\lim_{t\to 1}\left|\alpha_{i}(t)\right|=1
.
Up to applying another diffeomorphism, we can assume that
\left|\alpha_{i}(0)\right|=\frac{1}{2}
and
\left|\alpha_{i}(t)\right|>\frac{1}{2}
for
t\in(0,1)
and
i=1,2,3
(e.g. by adapting the argument in
[
27
, Theorem II.5.2]
).

Let
s_{i}:=\min\left\{t:\left|\alpha_{i}(t)\right|=\frac{3}{4}\right\}>0
. Moreover, for any
\tau\in\left(\frac{3}{4},1\right)
let

r_{i}(\tau):=\min\left\{t:\left|\alpha_{i}(t)\right|=\tau\right\}>s_{i}.

By Jordan’s closed curve theorem for piecewise smooth curves, the points
\alpha_{i}(0)
and
\alpha_{i}(r_{i}(\tau))
are in the same order on the circles
\left\{\left|z\right|=\frac{1}{2}\right\}
and
\left\{\left|z\right|=\tau\right\}
.
The curves
\alpha_{i}([0,r_{i}(\tau)])
and the circles
\left\{\left|z\right|=\frac{1}{2}\right\}
,
\left\{\left|z\right|=\tau\right\}
bound three disjoint domains
R_{1}(\tau)
,
R_{2}(\tau)
,
R_{3}(\tau)
; we adopt the convention that
R_{i}(\tau)
is the region whose closure is disjoint from
\alpha_{i}([0,r_{i}(\tau)])
. Let

(3.1)

\delta:=\inf_{\tau\in\left(\frac{3}{4},1\right)}\min_{i}d_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{i}(0),\overline{R_{i}(\tau)})

and notice that, since

d_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{i}(0),\overline{R_{i}(\tau)})=d_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{i}(0),\partial R_{i}(\tau))=d_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{i}(0),\partial R_{i}(\tau)\setminus\partial B_{\tau}^{2}(0)),

we have
\delta>0
.

Assume now
T_{j}=(K^{\prime},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime},\gamma_{3}^{\prime})
satisfies
d(T_{j_{0}},T_{j})<\varepsilon
. If
\varepsilon
is small enough, we have:

•

\operatorname{supp}\left(T_{j}\right)\subseteq U
(this is obtained arguing as in the first case), so that we can define
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t):=\upsilon\circ\gamma_{i}^{\prime}(t)
for
t\in[0,1]
;

•

\upsilon(K^{\prime})\subseteq\left\{\frac{3}{4}<\left|z\right|<1\right\}
;

•

\max_{t\in[0,s_{i}]}\left|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t)-\alpha_{i}(t)\right|<\delta^{\prime}
, for some
\delta^{\prime}\leq\delta
to be chosen later;

•

\alpha_{i}^{\prime}([s_{i},1])\subseteq\left\{\left|z\right|>\frac{1}{2}\right\}
.

Let
t_{i}^{\prime}:=\max\left(\left\{t:\left|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t)\right|=\frac{1}{2}\right\}\cup\left\{0\right\}\right)<s_{i}
. We claim that

(3.2)

\left|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t_{i}^{\prime})-\alpha_{i}(0)\right|<\delta.

If
t_{i}^{\prime}=0
this is trivial, while otherwise
\left|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t_{i}^{\prime})\right|=\frac{1}{2}
and

\dist(\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t_{i}^{\prime}),\alpha_{i}([0,s_{i}]))\leq\left|\alpha_{i}^{\prime}(t_{i}^{\prime})-\alpha_{i}(t_{i}^{\prime})\right|<\delta^{\prime},

which yields our claim once
\delta^{\prime}
is chosen so small that

\Big\{z:\left|z\right|=\frac{1}{2},\dist(z,\alpha_{i}([0,s_{i}]))<\delta^{\prime}\Big\}\subseteq B_{\delta}^{2}(\alpha_{i}(0))

(if such
\delta^{\prime}
did not exist, we could find points
\left|z_{k}\right|=\frac{1}{2}
with
\dist(z_{k},\alpha_{i}([0,s_{i}]))\to 0
and
\left|z_{k}-\alpha_{i}(0)\right|\geq\delta
; up to subsequences we could assume
z_{k}\to z_{\infty}
, for some
z_{\infty}\in\alpha_{i}([0,s_{i}])
with
\left|z_{\infty}\right|=\frac{1}{2}
, hence
z_{\infty}=\alpha_{i}(0)
, contradiction).

Fix now any
\tau
such that
\max\left\{\left|z\right|:z\in\upsilon(\operatorname{supp}\left(T_{j}\right))\right\}<\tau<1
.
The connected set

\upsilon(K^{\prime})\sqcup\alpha_{1}^{\prime}(\left(t_{1}^{\prime},1\right))\sqcup\alpha_{2}^{\prime}(\left(t_{2}^{\prime},1\right))\sqcup\alpha_{3}^{\prime}(\left(t_{3}^{\prime},1\right))

is contained in
\left\{\frac{1}{2}<\left|z\right|<\tau\right\}
and is disjoint from
\alpha_{1}(\left[0,1\right))\sqcup\alpha_{2}(\left[0,1\right))\sqcup\alpha_{3}(\left[0,1\right))
, so it is contained in some region
R_{i_{0}}(\tau)
and, in particular,
\alpha_{i_{0}}^{\prime}([t_{i_{0}}^{\prime},1])\subseteq\overline{R_{i_{0}}(\tau)}
. But, using (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), we infer that
\alpha_{i_{0}}^{\prime}(t_{i_{0}}^{\prime})\not\in\overline{R_{i_{0}}(\tau)}
. This contradiction shows that such
T_{j}
with
d(T_{j_{0}},T_{j})<\varepsilon
cannot exist, completing the treatment of the second case.
∎

3.2.
Planar case

We now show the regularity in the special case where the parametrized varifold is contained in a plane.

Theorem 3.3 (regularity in codimension 0).

Let
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
be a local parametrized stationary varifold in
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C}
defined on a bounded connected open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
\Phi^{-1}(p)
is compact for all
p\in\mathbb{C}
. Then
\Phi
is holomorphic or antiholomorphic.

Proof.

We recall that, under these hypotheses,
\Phi^{-1}(p)
has always finitely many connected components and the upper semicontinuous function
\widetilde{N}\geq 1
is everywhere finite (see Remark
2.11
). It suffices to show that
\Delta\Phi=0
: once this is done, since
\Phi
is necessarily nonconstant we can pick any
z_{0}\in\Omega
such that
\nabla\Phi(z_{0})\neq 0
and, by weak conformality, there is an
r>0
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\partial_{z}\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{B_{r}^{2}(z_{0})}}=0
or
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\partial_{\overline{z}}\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{B_{r}^{2}(z_{0})}}=0
; the statement then follows by the analyticity of
\partial_{z}\Phi
and
\partial_{\overline{z}}\Phi
.

We further make the following assumptions, which will be dropped in Step 4 below:

(i)

\Phi
extends continuously to
\overline{\Omega}
and
\Phi(\partial\Omega)\cap\Phi(\Omega)=\emptyset
;

(ii)

\Phi(\Omega)\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is open and the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}
equals
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})\mathbf{v}(\Phi(\Omega))
, for some
x_{0}
in
\Omega
,
\mathbf{v}(\Phi(\Omega))
denoting the canonical varifold associated to
\Phi(\Omega)
.

We show that in this situation the theorem holds, by strong induction on
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})
. Notice that
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})
is necessarily an integer, since
\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}
has integer multiplicity.

Step 1.
If
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})=1
then
\widetilde{N}=1
everywhere: indeed, for every
z\in\Omega
and every
K_{z}\subseteq\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
we have

1\leq\widetilde{N}(z)\leq\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{2}(\Phi(z)))}{\pi s^{2}}\leq\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{2}(\Phi(z)))}{\pi s^{2}}=1.

By Proposition
2.8
we can replace
N
with
\widetilde{N}
without affecting the stationarity of
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
, hence by Theorem
2.13
we have
\Delta\Phi=0
.

Assume now
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})>1
. Fix any
y\in\Omega
and choose a point
y_{i}
in every connected component
K_{i}
of
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y))
. Choosing disjoint neighborhoods
K_{i}\subseteq\omega_{i}\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
we have

\sum_{i}\widetilde{N}(y_{i})=\lim_{s\to 0}\sum_{i}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{i}}\right\|(B_{s}^{2}(\Phi(y)))}{\pi s^{2}}=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}\right\|(B_{s}^{2}(\Phi(y)))}{\pi s^{2}}=\widetilde{N}(x_{0}),

since
\Phi(y)\not\in\overline{\Phi(\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}\omega_{i})}
. We deduce that the following dichotomy is true: for any
y\in\Omega
, either
\widetilde{N}(y)=\widetilde{N}(x_{0})
and
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y))
is connected, or
\widetilde{N}(y)\leq\widetilde{N}(x_{0})-1
and
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y))
has at least two components.
We can assume that
\widetilde{N}
is not identically equal to
\widetilde{N}(x_{0})
, since otherwise we are done as in the base case of the induction.

Step 2.
We claim that, by inductive hypothesis,
\Phi
is holomorphic or antiholomorphic on each connected component of the set

\Omega_{0}:=\big\{\widetilde{N}\leq\widetilde{N}(x_{0})-1\big\}=\big\{\widetilde{N}<\widetilde{N}(x_{0})\big\},

which is open by Proposition
2.8
. For any
y_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
we can take an open set
K_{y_{0}}\subseteq\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega_{0}
with
\overline{\omega}
disjoint from
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y_{0}))\setminus K_{y_{0}}
. Possibly replacing
\omega
with the connected component of
\omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\partial\omega))
containing
y_{0}
, we observe that
\omega
satisfies the same hypotheses as
\Omega
, as well as (i)–(ii): the only nontrivial task is to check (ii), which we do below.

By the constancy theorem
[
44
, Theorem 41.1]
applied to
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
, which is stationary in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
, the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
equals a nontrivial constant multiple of
\mathbf{v}(W)
, where
W
is the connected component of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
containing the connected set
\Phi(\omega)
. Since
\Phi(\omega)
is relatively closed in
W
, we deduce
W=\Phi(\omega)
. Finally, by definition of
\widetilde{N}(y_{0})
we must have
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}=\widetilde{N}(y_{0})\mathbf{v}(W)
.
Thus, the inductive hypothesis applies and we deduce
\Delta\Phi=0
on
\omega
. Since
y_{0}
was arbitrary, we get
\Delta\Phi=0
on
\Omega_{0}
and our claim is established.

Step 3.
Notice that
\Phi(\Omega_{0})
is nonempty and open, being
\Phi
nonconstant on every connected component of
\Omega_{0}
. We call
D\subset\Omega_{0}
the relatively closed, discrete set of points where
\nabla\Phi
vanishes. The map
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Omega_{0}}}:\Omega_{0}\to\Phi(\Omega_{0})
is proper, thanks to the fact that
\Phi(\Omega_{0})
and
\Phi(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\Omega_{0})
are disjoint, so
\Phi(D)
is a relatively closed, discrete subset of
\Phi(\Omega_{0})
. Hence,
D^{\prime}:=\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(D))
is still relatively closed in
\Omega_{0}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Omega_{0}\setminus D^{\prime}}}
, being a proper local diffeomorphism onto
\Phi(\Omega_{0})\setminus\Phi(D)
, is a covering map.

Let
\Omega_{max}:=\big\{\widetilde{N}=\widetilde{N}(x_{0})\big\}
, which is closed in
\Omega
. Due to Lemma
5.1
, we can assume that
\Phi(\Omega_{max})
is uncountable.
Observe that
\Phi(\Omega_{max})
is relatively closed in the open set
\Phi(\Omega)
, being
\Phi
a proper map, and
\Phi(\Omega)=\Phi(\Omega_{0})\sqcup\Phi(\Omega_{max})
by the dichotomy of Step 1.
Take two distinct points
p,q\in\Phi(\Omega_{0})
and choose any ball
p,q\not\in\overline{B}\subseteq\Phi(\Omega)
such that
\Phi(\Omega_{max})\cap B
is uncountable.
We consider a foliation of curves on the connected set
\Phi(\Omega)
as in the picture (which illustrates the position of
p,q,B
up to a diffeomorphism of
\Phi(\Omega)
).

We can assume that uncountably many of these curves intersect
\Phi(\Omega_{max})
: if this does not happen, it means that uncountably many points of
\Phi(\Omega_{max})
lie on a single horizontal segment in
B
, so it suffices to apply a diffeomorphism which rotates
B
slightly.
Uncountably many such curves do not intersect
\Phi(D)=\Phi(D^{\prime})
, as well. For any such good curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to\Phi(\Omega)
(with
\gamma(0)=p
,
\gamma(1)=q
) we let

a:=\min\left\{t:\gamma(t)\in\Phi(\Omega_{max})\right\},\quad 1-b:=\max\left\{t:\gamma(t)\in\Phi(\Omega_{max})\right\}.

We clearly have
0<a\leq 1-b<1
.
We can lift
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\gamma\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{[a/3,2a/3]}}
to two curves
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
in
\Omega_{0}\setminus D^{\prime}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\gamma\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{[1-2b/3,1-b/3]}}
to a curve
\gamma_{3}
in
\Omega_{0}\setminus D^{\prime}
, thanks to the dichotomy observed in Step 1 and the fact that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\Omega_{0}\setminus D^{\prime}}}
is a covering map.

Finally, the compact set
K:=\Phi^{-1}(\gamma([2a/3,1-2b/3]))
is connected: assume by contradiction that it splits into two disjoint compact sets
A\sqcup B
. For each point
z\in\gamma([2a/3,1-2b/3])
the fiber
\Phi^{-1}(z)
lies either in
A
or in
B
: this is clear if
z\in\Phi(\Omega_{max})
, since then
\Phi^{-1}(z)
is connected; if
z\not\in\Phi(\Omega_{max})
we can travel
\gamma
back or forward until we hit a point
w\in\Phi(\Omega_{max})
and the corresponding lifted curves will necessarily accumulate against
\Phi^{-1}(w)
(thanks to the properness of
\Phi
), so they lie either all in
A
or all in
B
. We infer that
\gamma([2a/3,1-2b/3])=\Phi(A)\sqcup\Phi(B)
, which contradicts the connectedness of
[2a/3,1-2b/3]
.

Thus any such good curve produces a generalized triod
(K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
and these triods are disjoint from each other. Since there are uncountably many such triods, this contradicts Lemma
3.2
. The inductive proof is complete.

Step 4.
We now drop the extra assumptions (i)–(ii). This is done with the same argument of Step 2: for any
y_{0}\in\Omega
we can find a neighborhood
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
satisfying (i)–(ii), hence
\Delta\Phi=0
on
\omega
. We deduce that
\Delta\Phi=0
on all of
\Omega
.
∎

Corollary 3.4 (constant multiplicity in codimension 0).

Under the same hypotheses,
N
is a.e. constant.

Proof.

Since
U:=\left\{\nabla\Phi\neq 0\right\}\subseteq\Omega
is connected, it suffices to show the claim locally in
U
. Fix
z_{0}\in U
. We can find a connected open neighborhood
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset U
such that
\Phi
is injective on
\overline{\omega}
. Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
,
\Phi(\omega)
is open and
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}=\theta\mathbf{v}(\Phi(\omega))
for some
\theta
. By definition of
\widetilde{N}
and Proposition
2.8
,
N=\widetilde{N}=\theta
a.e. on
\omega
.
∎

3.3.
Conical case

We now gradually move to the case where the varifold is contained in a finite union of planes.

Lemma 3.5 (regularity in a dihedral angle).

Let
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
be a local parametrized stationary varifold in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
defined on a bounded connected open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
. Assume that
\Phi^{-1}(p)
is compact for all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and that
\Phi
takes values in the union of two
2
-dimensional closed half-planes
H_{a}
,
H_{b}
with common boundary. Then
\Delta\Phi=0
.

Proof.

The idea is to straighten the two half-planes into a single plane and then apply Corollary
3.4
. We can assume that
q=3
and, by Theorem
3.3
, that the two half-planes are not contained in a single plane.
Up to translations and rotations,
\Phi(\Omega)\subseteq H_{a}\cup H_{b}
, where

H_{a}:=\left\{\lambda v_{1}+\mu v_{2}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\mu\in\left[0,\infty\right)\right\},\ H_{b}:=\left\{\lambda v_{1}+\mu v_{3}\mid\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\mu\in\left[0,\infty\right)\right\},

v_{1}:=(1,0,0),\quad v_{2}:=(0,\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\quad v_{3}:=(0,-\cos\theta,\sin\theta),

for some
0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}
. Let

S:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathbb{C},\quad S(x,y,z):=\left(x,\frac{y}{\cos\theta}\right)

and
\Psi:=S\circ\Phi
. This map is still weakly conformal: indeed, if
x
is a Lebesgue point for
\nabla\Phi
and
\nabla\Phi(x)=0
, then the same holds for
\Psi
; if instead
\nabla\Phi(x)
has full rank, then
d\Phi(x)
takes values in the linear span of
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
, or in the linear span of
v_{1}
,
v_{3}
(since
\liminf_{r\to 0}r^{-1}\left\|\Phi(x+ry)-\Phi(x)-r\left\langle\nabla\Phi(x),y\right\rangle\right\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}=0
, by Lemma
A.4
) and the claim follows from the chain rule.

We now show that
(\Psi,N)
is still a local parametrized stationary varifold, i.e. that

\int_{\omega}N\left\langle\nabla(X\circ\Psi);\nabla\Psi\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=0

for any
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
and any vector field
X\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Psi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Let

A:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\quad P:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y:=P^{t}A^{-1}(X\circ S).

Notice that, although
Y
does not have compact support, it vanishes in a neighborhood of
\Phi(\partial\omega)
. Since we know that
\Phi(\overline{\omega})
is compact, we have

\int_{\omega}N\left\langle\nabla(Y\circ\Phi);\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=0.

But, viewing
S
also as a matrix,
\nabla Y=P^{t}A^{-1}(\nabla X\circ S)S
and
P=AS
, thus

\begin{split}\left\langle\nabla(Y\circ\Phi);\nabla\Phi\right\rangle&=\left\langle P^{t}A^{-1}(\nabla X\circ S\circ\Phi)\nabla(S\circ\Phi);\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle P^{t}A^{-1}\nabla(X\circ\Psi);\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle S^{t}\nabla(X\circ\Psi);\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle\nabla(X\circ\Psi);S\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\\
&=\left\langle\nabla(X\circ\Psi);\nabla\Psi\right\rangle.\end{split}

This shows the stationarity of
(\Psi,N)
. By Corollary
3.4
,
N
is a.e. constant. By Theorem
2.13
, this implies
\Delta\Phi=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.6 (regularity in a wedge of several half-planes).

The same conclusion holds if
\Phi
takes values in the union of finitely many (distinct) closed half-planes
\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
with a common boundary
C=\partial H_{i}
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\Delta\Phi=0
near any point
x_{0}\in\Omega
. By Theorem
3.3
we have
\Delta\Phi=0
on
\Phi^{-1}(H_{i}\setminus C)
, for all
i
, so we can assume
\Phi(x_{0})\in C
.
By Remark
2.12
, shrinking
\Omega
if necessary, we can further assume that
\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(y))
is connected whenever
\widetilde{N}(y)\geq\widetilde{N}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{2}
and that
\Phi
extends continuously to
\overline{\Omega}
with
\Phi(\Omega)\cap\Phi(\partial\Omega)=\emptyset
.

By induction on
\lfloor 2\widetilde{N}(x_{0})\rfloor
,
\left\lfloor\cdot\right\rfloor
denoting the integer part, we can also assume that in this situation we have
\Delta\Phi=0
on the open set
\big\{\widetilde{N}<\widetilde{N}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\big\}
(using e.g.
\lfloor 2\widetilde{N}(x_{0})\rfloor=1
as the base case, which is vacuously true).

We pick any
0<r<\dist(\Phi(x_{0}),\Phi(\partial\Omega))
and let
\omega:=\Phi^{-1}(B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0})))\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega
, as well as
\omega_{i}:=\omega\cap\Phi^{-1}(H_{i}\setminus C)
. If
\omega_{i}
is nonempty for at most two values of
i
, then we are done by Lemma
3.5
, applied to the connected components of
\omega
. Thus, we suppose e.g. that
\omega_{i}\neq\emptyset
for
i=1,2,3
.
Applying the constancy theorem to the varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\omega_{i}}
, which is a nontrivial stationary varifold in
B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0}))\cap(H_{i}\setminus C)
, and using the fact that
\Phi(\omega_{i})
is relatively closed in this set, we infer that

\Phi(\omega_{i})=B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0}))\cap(H_{i}\setminus C).

for
i=1,2,3
.
Let
C^{\prime}:=C\cap\Phi\Big(\big\{x\in\omega:\widetilde{N}(x)\geq\widetilde{N}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\big\}\Big)
, which is closed in
B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0}))
, being
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega}}:\omega\to B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0}))
a proper map. If
C^{\prime}
is countable, then we are done by applying Lemma
5.1
to
\omega
.

Otherwise, we can pick for each
c\in C^{\prime}
a segment
\beta_{i}([0,1])\subseteq B_{r}^{q}(\Phi(x_{0}))\cap H_{i}
perpendicular to
C
with
\beta_{i}(1)=c
(for
i=1,2,3
). Apart from (at most) countably many exceptions, these segments do not intersect the singular values of
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega_{i}}}
. So, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
, the curves
\beta_{i}([0,1/2])
can be lifted to smooth regular curves
\gamma_{i}
in
\omega_{i}
and, setting
K:=\Phi^{-1}(c)\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\beta_{i}([1/2,1])
,
(K,\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3})
is a generalized triod. This gives an uncountable family of disjoint generalized triods, contradicting Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Theorem 3.7 (regularity in a polyhedral cone).

Let
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
be a local parametrized stationary varifold in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, with
\Omega
connected. Assume that
\Phi
takes values in the union of finitely many (distinct)
2
-dimensional planes
\Sigma_{1},\dots,\Sigma_{k}
passing through the origin. Then
\Phi
takes values in a single plane
\Sigma_{i_{0}}
and, once we identify it with
\mathbb{C}
, it is holomorphic or antiholomorphic (but possibly constant).

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\Phi
is nonconstant.
It suffices to show that
\Delta\Phi=0
: then, by weak conformality,
\Phi(\Sigma)
cannot be contained in a finite union of lines, i.e. for some
i_{0}
we have
\Phi^{-1}\left(\Sigma_{i_{0}}\setminus\bigcup_{i\neq i_{0}}\Sigma_{i}\right)\neq\emptyset
; thus on this open set we have
\pi_{\Sigma_{i_{0}}^{\perp}}\Phi=0
and we deduce that this holds on all of
\Omega
by analyticity. The statement then follows by weak conformality, as in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Fix now
x_{0}\in\Omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(0)
and let
J:=\left\{j:\Phi(x_{0})\in\Sigma_{j}\right\}
. We pick any radius
r<\dist(x_{0},\partial\Omega)
such that

\Phi(\overline{B}_{r}^{2}(x_{0}))
intersects only the planes
\Sigma_{j}
with
j\in J
.
Notice that
\bigcup_{j\in J}\Sigma_{j}
is a finite union of half-planes with common boundary
\left\{t\Phi(x_{0}):t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
. We assume that the weak gradient coincides with the classical one on the biggest open subset of
\Omega
where
\Phi
is smooth and we call
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
the set of Lebesgue points for
\nabla\Phi
where
\nabla\Phi\neq 0
.

For
x\in B_{r}^{2}(x_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
we have
\liminf_{s\to 0}s^{-1}\left\|\Phi(x+sy)-\Phi(x)-s\left\langle\nabla\Phi(x),y\right\rangle\right\|_{C^{0}(S^{1})}=0
, by Lemma
A.4
. Hence, we can find an open neighborhood
\omega\subset B_{r}^{2}(x_{0})
of
x
such that
\Phi(x)\not\in\Phi(\partial\omega)
.
Possibly replacing
\omega
with the connected component of
\omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\partial\omega))
containing
x
, we can even assume
\Phi(\omega)\cap\Phi(\partial\omega)=\emptyset
. Hence we can apply Lemma
3.6
on
\omega
, obtaining
\Delta\Phi=0
near
x
. In particular, this shows that
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}\setminus\Phi^{-1}(0)
is open and
\Delta\Phi=0
on
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}\setminus\Phi^{-1}(0)
.

Using Fubini’s theorem and
[
15
, Theorem 4.21]
, we can pick an
r^{\prime}<r
such that the map
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})}}
is absolutely continuous (with weak derivative given by the chain rule) and
\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=0
. For any relatively open subset
U\subseteq\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})
containing
\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Phi(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\cap U))\leq\int_{U}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

by definition of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
. Since
U
is arbitrary, we deduce

\mathcal{H}^{1}(K)=0,\quad K:=\Phi(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus{\mathcal{G}^{f}}).

Fix now any
x\in B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus\Phi^{-1}(K)
. Assume
\partial B_{s}^{2}(x_{0})\cap\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x))\neq\emptyset
for all
\left|x-x_{0}\right|<s<r^{\prime}
. Then we can find a sequence
s_{\ell}\uparrow r^{\prime}
and points
y_{\ell}\in\partial B_{s_{k}}^{2}(x_{0})
such that
\Phi(y_{\ell})=\Phi(x)
and
y_{k}\to y_{\infty}
, for some
y_{\infty}\in\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})
. Necessarily we have
y_{\infty}\in{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
, as
\Phi(y_{\infty})=\Phi(x)\not\in K
, but this contradicts the fact that
\Phi
is injective near
y_{\infty}
.

Thus there exists a radius
s<r^{\prime}
such that
x\in B_{s}^{2}(x_{0})
and
\Phi(x)\not\in\Phi(\partial B_{s}^{2}(x_{0}))
. Again we can let
\omega
be the connected component of
B_{s}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(\partial B_{s}^{2}(x_{0})))
containing
x
and we can apply Lemma
3.6
on
\omega
. This shows that
\Delta\Phi=0
on
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})\setminus\Phi^{-1}(K)
.

Finally, by Lemma
5.1
and
\mathcal{H}^{1}(K)=0
, we have
\Delta\Phi=0
on
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{0})
. So
\Delta\Phi=0
on
\Omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(0)
and we deduce that
\Delta\Phi=0
on all of
\Omega
, again by Lemma
5.1
.
∎

4.
Blow-up of a parametrized stationary varifold

Let
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
be a local parametrized stationary varifold.
Let us fix a sequence of points
(x_{k})\subseteq\Omega
and a sequence of radii
(r_{k})
such that

0<r_{k}<\frac{1}{2}\dist(x_{k},\partial\Omega)
. We let

\ell_{k}^{2}:=\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2},\quad\Phi_{k}:=\ell_{k}^{-1}(\Phi(x_{k}+r_{k}\cdot)-\Phi(x_{k})),\quad N_{k}:=N(x_{k}+r_{k}\cdot),

\nu_{k}:=\frac{1}{2}N_{k}\left|\nabla\Phi_{k}\right|^{2}\bm{1}_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\mathcal{L}^{2},\quad\mu_{k}:=(\Phi_{k})_{*}\nu_{k}.

Notice that the functions
\Phi_{k},N_{k}
are defined on
B_{2}^{2}(0)
, so the definition of the measures
\nu_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
, on
B_{2}^{2}(0)
and
\mathbb{R}^{q}
respectively, makes sense. Throughout the section we will assume that there exist two constants
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\geq 1
such that

•

0<\int_{B_{2r_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}\int_{B_{r_{k}}^{2}(x_{k})}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
; (4.1)

•

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mu_{k}(B_{s}^{q}(p))\leq C^{\prime\prime}\pi s^{2}
for all
s>0
and all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
; (4.2)

•

\ell_{k}\to 0
. (4.3)

We will show the following result.

Theorem 4.1 (parametrized blow-up).

Up to subsequences, there exist a map
\Phi_{\infty}\in W^{1,2}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
, a function
N_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
and a
quasiconformal homeomorphism
\varphi_{\infty}\in W^{1,2}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\Omega_{\infty})
(for some bounded open set
\Omega_{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
), with
\varphi_{\infty}(0)=0
, such that

\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}\quad\mbox{in }C^{0}_{loc}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q}),\qquad\nabla\Phi_{k}\rightharpoonup\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\quad\text{in }L^{2}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q\times 2}),

\frac{1}{2}N_{k}\left|\nabla\Phi_{k}\right|^{2}\mathcal{L}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}N_{\infty}\left|\partial_{1}\Phi_{\infty}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{\infty}\right|\mathcal{L}^{2}\quad\text{as Radon measures}.

Moreover,
\Phi_{\infty}\circ\varphi_{\infty}^{-1}
is weakly conformal and
(\Omega_{\infty},\Phi_{\infty}\circ\varphi_{\infty}^{-1},N_{\infty}\circ\varphi_{\infty}^{-1})
is a local parametrized stationary varifold in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

We refer the reader to
[
24
, Chapter 4]
and
[
28
]
for the theory of quasiconformal homeomorphisms in the plane. Before proving this theorem we shall establish a number of intermediate results. Many arguments are similar to those used in
[
39
]
.

First of all, since
\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{k}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq C^{\prime}
and
\nu_{k}(B_{2}^{2}(0))\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
, up to subsequences there exists
\Phi_{\infty}\in W^{1,2}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
such that
\Phi_{k}\rightharpoonup\Phi_{\infty}
in
W^{1,2}(B_{2}^{2}(0))
and there exists a finite Radon measure
\nu_{\infty}
on
B_{2}^{2}(0)
such that
\nu_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\nu_{\infty}
in
B_{2}^{2}(0)
.
We can also assume that, for all
j\geq 0
,

\mu_{k,j}:=(\Phi_{k})_{*}\left(\bm{1}_{B_{2-2^{-j}}^{2}(0)}\nu_{k}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu_{\infty,j}\quad\text{as }k\to\infty

in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
, for some finite measure
\mu_{\infty,j}
.
Since
\mu_{\infty,j}\leq\mu_{\infty,j+1}
and
\mu_{\infty,j}(\mathbb{R}^{q})\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
, the measure
\mu_{\infty}:=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mu_{\infty,j}

is defined and is again finite.

Lemma 4.2 (uniform convergence
\bm{\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}}
).

The measure
\nu_{\infty}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, i.e.
\nu_{\infty}=m\mathcal{L}^{2}
for some nonnegative
m\in L^{1}(B_{2}^{2}(0))
. Moreover,
\Phi_{\infty}
is continuous and
\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
C^{0}_{loc}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
. Finally, for any open subset
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset B_{2}^{2}(0)
,

\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{\omega})\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega)),

where
\operatorname{conv}(\cdot)
denotes the convex hull.

Proof.

We introduce the
oscillation set

\mathcal{O}:=\bigg\{x\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\nu_{\infty}\right):\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}}{\nu_{\infty}(B_{r}^{2}(x))}=0\bigg\}.

Step 1.
We show that
\nu_{\infty}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}^{2}
on the Borel set
B_{2}^{2}(0)\setminus\mathcal{O}
.
Let
E\subseteq B_{2}^{2}(0)\setminus\mathcal{O}
be a Borel set with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(E)=0
. It suffices to show that
\nu_{\infty}(K)=0
for any compact subset
K\subseteq E\cap\operatorname{supp}\left(\nu_{\infty}\right)
(since this implies that
\nu_{\infty}(E\cap\operatorname{supp}\left(\nu_{\infty}\right))=0
and thus
\nu_{\infty}(E)=0
, as required). We define the Borel sets

F_{j}:=\bigg\{x\in K:\inf_{0<r\leq\overline{r}}\frac{\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}}{\nu_{\infty}(B_{r}^{2}(x))}\geq 2^{-j}\bigg\},\quad\overline{r}:=\frac{1}{2}\dist(K,\partial B_{2}^{2}(0))

and observe that
K=\bigcup_{j}F_{j}
. Fix
j
and an open set
E\subseteq V\subseteq B_{2}^{2}(0)
. Letting
r_{V}:=\frac{1}{2}\dist(K,\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus V)\leq\overline{r}
, we choose a maximal (finite) subset
\left\{x_{i}\right\}
of
F_{j}
such that
\left|x_{i}-x_{i^{\prime}}\right|\geq r_{V}
for
i\neq i^{\prime}
. We have

F_{j}\subseteq\bigcup_{i}B_{r_{V}}^{2}(x_{i}),\quad\sum_{i}\bm{1}_{B_{2r_{V}}^{2}(x_{i})}\leq\mathfrak{N}

for some universal constant
\mathfrak{N}
.
We deduce that

\nu_{\infty}(F_{j})\leq\sum_{i}\nu_{\infty}(B_{r_{V}}^{2}(x_{i}))\leq 2^{j}\sum_{i}\int_{B_{2r_{V}}^{2}(x_{i})}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq 2^{j}\mathfrak{N}\int_{V}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}.

Letting
V
range along a sequence of open sets
V_{\ell}\supseteq E
with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(V_{\ell})\to 0
, we deduce
\nu_{\infty}(F_{j})=0
. Hence,

\nu_{\infty}(K)\leq\sum_{j}\nu_{\infty}(F_{j})=0.

Step 2.
We show that
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
.
Fix any
x\in B_{2}^{2}(0)
and any
r<\frac{1}{2}\dist(x,\partial B_{2}^{2}(0))
. Using Lemma
A.5
and Lemma
A.3
we select a radius
r^{\prime}\in(r,2r)
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}}\to{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}}
in
L^{\infty}
, for some subsequence
(\Phi_{k_{i}})
, and

(4.4)

\diam\Phi_{\infty}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))\leq\sqrt{4\pi}\left(\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

(we are implicitly referring to the continuous representative of
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}}
). Since
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
is a local parametrized stationary varifold in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
, the varifolds
\mathbf{v}_{k_{i}}
issued by
(\Phi_{k_{i}},N_{k_{i}})
from the domain
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
have generalized mean curvature bounded by
O(\ell_{k_{i}})
(in
L^{\infty}
) in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi_{k_{i}}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))
. As a consequence of assumption (
•
‣
4
), up to further subsequences they converge to a varifold
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
(in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
) which is stationary in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi_{\infty}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))
.

Moreover, by Proposition
2.4
,
0=\Phi_{k}(0)\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)
unless
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)=\emptyset
. Using estimate (
2.5
) we infer that the sets
\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k}\right\|\right)
are all included in a unique compact set. It follows that
\mathbf{v}_{\infty}
has compact support, hence by
[
44
, Theorem 19.2]
(which applies to general stationary varifolds)
\operatorname{supp}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\infty}\right)\subseteq K:=\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)))
. It follows that

(4.5)

\sup_{z\in\overline{B}_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)}\dist(\Phi_{k_{i}}(z),K)\to 0:

if this were not the case, up to subsequences we could find
z_{k_{i}}\in B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
such that
\dist(\Phi_{k_{i}}(z_{k_{i}}),K)\geq\varepsilon
. Eventually
\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}})\in\operatorname{supp}\left(\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k_{i}}\right\|\right)
(by Proposition
2.4
, since eventually
\Phi_{k_{i}}
must be nonconstant on
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
), so we can assume that
\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}})\to p_{\infty}
and

\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\infty}\right\|(\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/2}^{q}(p_{\infty}))\geq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{k_{i}}\right\|(\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/2}^{q}(\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}})))\geq\pi\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4},

thanks to the monotonicity formula and the fact that
\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/2}^{q}(\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}}))\cap\Phi_{k_{i}}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))=\emptyset
eventually. This, however, contradicts the fact that
\overline{B}_{\varepsilon/2}^{q}(p_{\infty})\cap K=\emptyset
. Using (
4.5
) and (
•
‣
4
) we deduce that

\nu_{\infty}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\nu_{k_{i}}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x))\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mu_{k_{i}}(\Phi_{k_{i}}(B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)))\leq C^{\prime\prime}\pi(\diam K)^{2}.

From (
4.4
) and the fact that the convex hull preserves the diameter, we have
(\diam K)^{2}\leq 4\pi\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Hence,

\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}}{\nu_{\infty}(B_{r}^{2}(x))}\geq\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}C^{\prime\prime}}.

It follows that
\mathcal{O}=\emptyset
.

Step 3.
We show that
\Phi_{\infty}
has a continuous representative. Since
\Phi_{k_{i}}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
L^{2}(B_{2}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
, from (
4.5
) we infer that
\Phi_{\infty}(z)\in K
for a.e.
z\in B_{r}^{2}(x)
. In particular, this must happen whenever
z
is a Lebesgue point. This, together with the estimate for
\diam K
, proves that
\Phi_{\infty}
is locally uniformly continuous on the set of its Lebesgue points, hence it has a continuous representative.

Step 4.
Assume now by contradiction that
\Phi_{k}
does not converge locally uniformly to (the continuous representative of)
\Phi_{\infty}
. Then we can find a subsequence
\Phi_{k_{i}}
and points
x_{k_{i}}
, lying in a compact subset of
B_{2}^{2}(0)
, such that

\left|\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}})-\Phi_{\infty}(x_{k_{i}})\right|\geq\varepsilon.

We can further assume that
x_{k_{i}}\to x_{\infty}\in B_{2}^{2}(0)
. Let
r<\frac{1}{2}\dist(x_{\infty},\partial B_{2}^{2}(0))
be such that
4\pi\int_{B_{2r}^{2}(x_{\infty})}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}
. Up to subsequences, we can repeat the argument of the two previous steps and conclude that

\dist(\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}}),K)\to 0,\quad\Phi_{\infty}(x_{\infty})\in K,

where again
K:=\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x_{\infty})))
for a suitable
r^{\prime}\in(r,2r)
. In particular we have
\limsup_{i\to\infty}\left|\Phi_{k_{i}}(x_{k_{i}})-\Phi_{\infty}(x_{\infty})\right|\leq\diam K\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
, which is the desired contradiction.

Step 5.
Finally, let us turn to the last part of the statement. We can assume that
\omega
is connected. We already know that
\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}
uniformly on
\partial\omega
, so we can repeat the argument of Step 2, with
B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(x)
replaced by
\omega
, and conclude that
\dist(\Phi_{k}(z),\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega)))\to 0
for all
z\in\omega
, from which it follows that
\Phi_{\infty}(z)\in\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega))
.
∎

Lemma 4.3 (convergence of localized measures in the target).

We have
\mu_{\infty}=(\Phi_{\infty})_{*}\nu_{\infty}
and, for any
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset B_{2}^{2}(0)
with
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\partial\omega)=0
,

(\Phi_{k})_{*}(\bm{1}_{\omega}\nu_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}(\Phi_{\infty})_{*}(\bm{1}_{\omega}\nu_{\infty})

as Radon measures in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
.

Proof.

We first show the second statement. Consider any nonnegative
\rho\in C^{0}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
.
By Lemma
4.2
we have
\nu_{\infty}(\partial\omega)=0
, so approximating
\bm{1}_{\omega}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})
from above and below by functions in
C^{0}_{c}(B_{2}^{2}(0))
we get

\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{k}\to\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{\infty}.

Moreover, thanks to the local uniform convergence
\Phi_{k}\to\Phi_{\infty}
and
\nu_{k}(B_{2}^{2}(0))\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}
,

\bigg|\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{k})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{k}-\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{k}\bigg|\leq\frac{1}{2}C^{\prime}\left\|N\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\left\|\rho(\Phi_{k})-\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\omega)}\to 0

as
k\to\infty
and the claim follows, since

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho\,d(\Phi_{k})_{*}(\bm{1}_{\omega}\nu_{k})=\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{k})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{k}\to\int_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\bm{1}_{\omega}\,d\nu_{\infty}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho\,d(\Phi_{\infty})_{*}(\bm{1}_{\omega}\nu_{\infty}).

Choosing
\omega=B_{2-2^{-j}}^{2}(0)
we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho\,d\mu_{\infty,j}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}\rho\,d\mu_{k,j}=\int_{B_{2-2^{-j}}^{2}(0)}\rho(\Phi_{\infty})\,d\nu_{\infty}.

The first statement now follows by letting
j\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma 4.4 (behaviour at bad points).

Let
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\subseteq B_{2}^{2}(0)
denote the set of points
z
where

5.
Regularity in the general case

This section is devoted to the proof of the main regularity result (see Theorem
5.7
and Corollary
5.8
below). We first show a removable singularity criterion. Its proof consists of a standard capacity argument in the target

and could be well known to the expert community. We include it both for the reader’s convenience and because it is the only place where the technical assumption (

Lemma 5.1 (removable singularities).

Let
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
be a local parametrized stationary varifold in
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
(possibly
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
). Assume that
\Phi
satisfies

-\Delta\Phi=A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)

in the distributional sense on
\Omega\setminus S
, for some relatively closed
S\subseteq\Omega
with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Phi(S))=0
. Then the equation is satisfied on the whole
\Omega
and, as a consequence,
\Phi
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth.

Proof.

Let
v\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
we can cover the compact set
K:=\Phi(S\cap\operatorname{supp}\left(v\right))
by a finite union of balls
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{r_{i}}^{q}(p_{i})
with centers on this set and
\sum_{i}r_{i}<\varepsilon
. Let
\rho_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{q})
be a function which equals
0
on
B_{r_{i}}^{q}(p_{i})
,
1
on
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus B_{2r_{i}}^{q}(p_{i})
and has
\left\|\nabla\rho_{i}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2r_{i}^{-1}
.

Since
v_{\varepsilon}:=v\,\prod_{i}(\rho_{i}\circ\Phi)
vanishes near
S\cap\operatorname{supp}\left(v\right)
, the function
v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,2}\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is supported in a compact subset of
\Omega\setminus S
. Thus, using the hypothesis and a standard approximation argument,

(5.1)

\int_{\Omega}\left\langle\nabla\Phi,\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)v_{\varepsilon}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}.

We claim that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the left-hand side converges to
\int_{\Omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(K)}\left\langle\nabla\Phi,\nabla v\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Indeed, let us write

\nabla v_{\varepsilon}=\bigg(\prod_{i}\rho_{i}\circ\Phi\bigg)\nabla v+v\sum_{i}\bigg(\prod_{j\neq i}\rho_{j}\circ\Phi\bigg)\nabla(\rho_{i}\circ\Phi).

The first term converges to
\bm{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(K)}\nabla v
in
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
. On the other hand, by (
2.6
),

\begin{split}&\Big|\int_{\Omega}\Big\langle\nabla\Phi,v\sum_{i}\Big(\prod_{j\neq i}\rho_{j}\circ\Phi\Big)\nabla(\rho_{i}\circ\Phi)\Big\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big|\\
&\leq 2\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i}r_{i}^{-1}\int_{\Phi^{-1}(B_{2r_{i}}^{q}(p_{i}))}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\\
&\leq 4\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i}r_{i}^{-1}\left\|\mathbf{v}_{\Omega}\right\|(B_{2r_{i}}^{q}(p_{i}))=4\left\|v\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i}O(r_{i})\to 0\end{split}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This establishes the claim. Moreover, the right-hand side of (
5.1
) converges to
\int_{\Omega\setminus\Phi^{-1}(K)}A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)v\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
. Finally, in order to establish (
5.1
) with
v
in place of
v_{\varepsilon}
, we observe that
\nabla\Phi=0
a.e. on
\Phi^{-1}(K)
: indeed, by the area formula (see Lemma
A.2
),

\int_{\Phi^{-1}(K)}\left|\nabla\Phi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=2\int_{K}\bigg(\sum_{y\in\Phi^{-1}(p)\cap\mathcal{G}}1\bigg)\,d\mathcal{H}^{2}(p)=0,

since
\mathcal{H}^{2}(K)=0
. The smoothness of
\Phi
follows from the continuity of
\Phi
and
[
33
, Section 3.4]
.
∎

Definition 5.2 (admissible points).

Given a local parametrized stationary varifold
(\Omega,\Phi,N)
, a point

is said to be

Theorem 5.7 (regularity in the general case).

Let

[

Proof.

Assume that
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega

\put(53.0,28.0){$x_{0}$}
\put(70.0,32.5){$x_{1}$}
\put(60.5,34.0){$y_{0}$}
\end{overpic}

With the same notation and the same argument used in Step 2, we get
N^{\prime}(0)=\widetilde{N}(y_{0})
. Actually, we also have
N^{\prime}(\varphi_{\infty}(\overline{y}))\geq k\gamma
: letting
\omega^{\prime\prime}
be a smooth neighborhood of
\overline{y}
with
\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y})\not\in\Phi_{\infty}(\partial\omega^{\prime\prime})
and
\Phi_{\infty}^{-1}(\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y}))\cap\omega^{\prime\prime}=\left\{\overline{y}\right\}
, as soon as
\Phi_{j}(r_{j}^{-1}(y_{j}-y_{0}))\not\in\Phi_{j}(\partial\omega^{\prime\prime})
and
r_{j}^{-1}(y_{j}-y_{0})\in\omega^{\prime\prime}
the varifold

\mathbf{v}_{(\omega^{\prime\prime},\Phi_{j},N_{j})}=(\ell_{j}^{-1}(\cdot-\Phi(y_{0})))_{*}\mathbf{v}_{(y_{0}+r_{j}\omega^{\prime\prime},\Phi,N)}

has density at least
\widetilde{N}(y_{j})\geq k\gamma
at
\ell_{j}^{-1}(\Phi(y_{j})-\Phi(y_{0}))=\Phi_{j}(r_{j}^{-1}(y_{j}-y_{0}))\to\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y})
, has infinitesimal mean curvature in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi_{j}(\partial\omega^{\prime\prime})
and its mass measure converges to
(\Phi_{\infty})_{*}(\bm{1}_{\omega^{\prime\prime}}\nu_{\infty})
, by Lemma
4.3
. Thus, by the monotonicity formula,
N^{\prime}(\varphi_{\infty}(\overline{y}))
(i.e. the density of this last measure at
\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y})
) is at least
k\gamma
.

Since
k\gamma+1>\widetilde{N}(y_{0})
, as observed in Step 2 we must have
\Phi_{\infty}^{-1}(0)=\left\{0\right\}
and
\Phi_{\infty}^{-1}(\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y}))=\left\{\overline{y}\right\}
.
Recall that, as in Step 2, the map
\Psi:=\Phi_{\infty}\circ\varphi_{\infty}^{-1}
takes values in a plane and is an entire holomorphic function, up to suitable identification of this plane with
\mathbb{C}
.
Since it has two values having only one preimage, by Picard’s great theorem it does not have an essential singularity at
\infty
and is thus a polynomial. Actually, Picard’s great theorem can be easily avoided: by Corollary
5.6
the Dirichlet energy
\int_{B_{R}^{2}(0)}\left|\nabla\Phi_{\infty}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
grows at most polynomially in
R
and, by inspecting the proof of
[
24
, Theorem 4.30]
(as well as inequalities (4.21) and (4.24) in
[
24
]
), we see that
\sup_{z\in B_{R}^{2}(0)}\left|\varphi_{\infty}^{-1}\right|(z)
also grows at most polynomially, hence the same is true for
\int_{B_{R}^{2}(0)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
and thus (by the mean value property for harmonic functions) for
\sup_{z\in B_{R}^{2}(0)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|(z)
, i.e.
\Psi
is a polynomial. Since
\Psi^{-1}(0)=\left\{0\right\}
, it must have the form

\Psi(z)=cz^{\overline{k}}

for some
\overline{k}
and finally the fact that
\Psi^{-1}(\Phi_{\infty}(\overline{y}))
is a singleton gives
\overline{k}=1
. We deduce that
\Psi^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. Arguing as in the proof of Corollary
3.4
, we get
N_{\infty}\circ\varphi_{\infty}^{-1}=N^{\prime}(0)\geq k\gamma
a.e. near
0
.

We finally show that
\Phi_{j}\to\Phi_{\infty}
in
W^{1,2}_{loc}(H,\mathbb{R}^{q})
. In particular, for any small ball
B\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset H
close enough to
0
, this will contradict the estimate

\begin{split}&\kappa\gamma\int_{B}\left|\partial_{1}\Phi_{\infty}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{\infty}\right|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\nu_{\infty}(B)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\nu_{j}(B)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B}N_{j}\left|\nabla\Phi_{j}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\alpha\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{B}\left|\partial_{1}\Phi_{j}\wedge\partial_{2}\Phi_{j}\right|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2},\end{split}

where
\alpha
is the biggest integer smaller than
\kappa\gamma
(the last inequality comes from the fact that
N_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
and
N_{j}=\widetilde{N}(y_{0}+r_{j}\cdot)
a.e. on
\left\{\nabla\Phi_{j}\neq 0\right\}
, together with the fact that eventually
y_{0}+r_{j}B\subseteq B_{r}^{2}(x_{1})\subseteq\Omega_{k}\setminus\mathcal{C}_{k}
).

Fix any
U\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset H
. Since eventually
-\Delta\Phi_{j}=\ell_{j}A(\Phi(y_{0}+r_{j}\cdot))(\nabla\Phi_{j},\nabla\Phi_{j})
on
U
and the right-hand side converges to
0
in
L^{1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{q})
, we get

-\Delta\Phi_{\infty}=0

on
U
and hence (since
U
was arbitrary) on
H
. Fix any nonnegative
\rho\in C^{\infty}_{c}(H)
with
\rho=1
on
U
. Setting
\Psi_{j}:=\Phi_{j}-\Phi_{\infty}
, we have
-\Delta\Psi_{j}=\ell_{j}A(\Phi(y_{0}+r_{j}\cdot))(\nabla\Phi_{j},\nabla\Phi_{j})
and thus

\begin{split}&\int_{H}\rho\left|\nabla\Psi_{j}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}+\int_{H}\Psi_{j}\cdot\left\langle\nabla\rho,\nabla\Psi_{j}\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=\int_{H}\left\langle\nabla(\rho\Psi_{j});\nabla\Psi_{j}\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\\
&=\ell_{j}\int_{H}\rho\Psi_{j}\cdot A(\Phi(y_{0}+r_{j}\cdot))(\nabla\Phi_{j},\nabla\Phi_{j})\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}.\end{split}

But both the right-hand side and the second term in the left-hand side converge to
0
. Hence,

\int_{U}\left|\nabla\Psi_{j}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\leq\int_{H}\rho\left|\nabla\Psi_{j}\right|^{2}\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\to 0.

Step 4.
From the previous step we have
\partial\mathcal{A}_{k}\cap\Omega_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{k}
, hence
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is open. Since
\gamma<1
and
\widetilde{N}
is integer-valued on
\mathcal{A}_{k}
(by Step 2),
\widetilde{N}
takes exactly a single value here. We can then apply Theorem
2.13
(as replacing
N
with
\widetilde{N}
does not affect the stationarity) and obtain that the partial differential equation holds on
\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

Step 5.
From the two previous steps, it follows that
-\Delta\Phi=A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)
on the open set
\Omega_{k}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Using Lemma
5.3
and Lemma
5.1
, we deduce that the partial differential equation holds on the whole
\Omega_{k}
(and
\Phi
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth on
\Omega_{k}
). This completes the induction.

Step 6.
The extra assumptions made at the beginning of the proof can be dropped by arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
and using the upper semicontinuity of
\widetilde{N}
.
For the fact that
\Phi
is a branched immersion on a connected component
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
where it is nonconstant, we refer the reader to the proofs of
[
22
, Theorems 1 and 2]
and
[
21
, Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2]
. Finally, let
D\subset\Omega^{\prime\prime}
denote the discrete subset where
\nabla\Phi=0
. Whenever a connected
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\Omega^{\prime\prime}\setminus D
is such that
\Phi(\omega)\cap\Phi(\partial\omega)=\emptyset
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega}}
is an embedding, the constancy theorem (see
[
44
, Theorem 41.1]
) implies that
\widetilde{N}
is constant on
\omega
. Since
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\setminus D
is connected, Proposition
2.8
gives that
N
is a.e. constant on
\Omega^{\prime\prime}\setminus D
, hence on
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
.
∎

Corollary 5.8 (global regularity).

If
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
is a parametrized stationary varifold, then
\Phi
solves
-\Delta\Phi=A(\Phi)(\nabla\Phi,\nabla\Phi)
in local conformal coordinates,
\Phi
is a
C^{\infty}
-smooth branched immersion and
N
is a.e. constant.

Remark 5.9 (converse statement).

We notice that the converse statement holds as well: namely, if
\Phi:\Sigma\to{\mathcal{M}^{m}}
is a nonconstant weakly conformal, weakly harmonic map and
N
is a positive integer, then
(\Sigma,\Phi,N)
is a parametrized stationary varifold. Indeed, for almost every
\omega\subseteq\Sigma
, the continuous representative of
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial\omega}}
coincides with the trace (by
[
15
, Theorem 5.7]
this holds whenever
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e. point of
\partial\omega
is a Lebesgue point for
\Phi
). Thus, for any smooth
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
{\left.\kern-1.2ptF(\Phi)\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\omega}}
has zero trace on
\partial\omega
and (
2.1
) follows. Notice that we did not need Hélein’s regularity result to show this assertion: on the contrary, we can immediately deduce the continuity of
\Phi
(and hence the smoothness) from Proposition
2.4
. ∎

6.
An application to the conductivity equation

In this section we illustrate an application of Theorem
5.7
to the regularity theory for the conductivity equation

-\operatorname{div}(N\nabla\Phi)=0\quad\text{on }B_{1}^{2}(0).

This partial differential equation was already investigated by many authors: see e.g.
[
6
,
16
,
29
]
. We show below that, assuming
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
weakly conformal and
N\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
,
\Phi
is necessarily harmonic and
N
is a.e. constant, unless
\Phi
is itself constant.

This statement initially originated as a possible intermediate step in order to achieve Theorem
5.7
, but as a matter of fact we are able to prove the former only as a consequence of the latter. It would be interesting to find an independent, purely PDE-theoretic proof.

We can do this in the case
q=2
, where the following slightly stronger result holds.

Theorem 6.1 (regularity for
\bm{q=2}
).

Assume
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{2})
is weakly conformal,
N\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
is bounded below by a positive constant and

(6.1)

-\operatorname{div}(N\nabla\Phi)=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{2}).

Then
\Phi_{1}+i\,\Phi_{2}
is holomorphic or antiholomorphic and, if
\Phi
is nonconstant,
N
is a.e. constant.

Proof.

First of all,
\Phi
is continuous (see e.g.
[
19
, Section 4.4]
). We can assume that
\Phi
is nonconstant, so that the set
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}\neq\emptyset
of Lebesgue points
z
for
\nabla\Phi
with
\nabla\Phi(z)\neq 0
is nonempty.
Notice that
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are both nonconstant: otherwise e.g.
\nabla\Phi_{1}
would be a.e.
0
and in particular we would have
\nabla\Phi_{1}=0
on
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
, contradicting the weak conformality.
From (
6.1
) and standard Hodge theory, we can find real functions
\Psi_{k}\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
with

N\nabla\Phi_{k}=-\nabla^{\perp}\Psi_{k},\quad\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1}),

for
k=1,2
. This equation can be equivalently rewritten as

(6.2)

N\partial_{z}\Phi_{k}=i\partial_{z}\Psi_{k}\quad\text{or}\quad N\partial_{\overline{z}}\Phi_{k}=-i\partial_{\overline{z}}\Psi_{k}.

Let
f_{k}:=\Phi_{k}+i\,\Psi_{k}
. We have

\partial_{\overline{z}}f_{k}=(1-N)\partial_{\overline{z}}\Phi_{k},\quad\partial_{{z}}f_{k}=(1+N)\partial_{{z}}\Phi_{k}.

We define the Beltrami coefficient
\mu_{k}
on
\mathbb{C}
as

\mu_{k}:=\frac{(1-N)\partial_{\overline{z}}\Phi_{k}}{(1+N)\partial_{{z}}\Phi_{k}}\bm{1}_{B_{1}^{2}(0)\cap{\mathcal{G}^{f}}}.

Notice that, by weak conformality,
\partial_{z}\Phi_{k}\neq 0
on
{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
. Our hypotheses on
N
clearly imply

\|\mu_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}}<1

and
f_{k}
satisfies the Beltrami equation

\partial_{\overline{z}}f_{k}=\mu_{k}\partial_{z}f_{k}

on
B_{1}^{2}(0)
. Let
\varphi_{k}
be the normal solution of

\partial_{\overline{z}}\varphi_{k}=\mu_{k}\partial_{z}\varphi_{k}

(see
[
24
, Theorem 4.24]
). As already pointed out in the proof of Theorem
4.1
,
\varphi_{k},\varphi_{k}^{-1}\in W^{1,2}_{loc}(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{C})
are homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}
mapping negligible sets to negligible sets and

\partial_{\overline{w}}\varphi_{k}^{-1}=-(\mu_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1})\overline{\partial_{w}\varphi_{k}^{-1}}.

By the chain rule (see
[
28
, Lemma III.6.4]
),
h_{k}:=f_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1}
(defined on
\varphi_{k}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
) satisfies

\partial_{\overline{w}}h_{k}=(\partial_{z}f_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1})\partial_{\overline{w}}\varphi_{k}^{-1}+(\partial_{\overline{z}}f_{k}\circ\varphi_{k}^{-1})\overline{\partial_{w}\varphi_{k}^{-1}}=0

and is thus a nonconstant holomorphic function. Pick now any
z_{0}\in B_{1}^{2}(0)
such that the points
\varphi_{1}(z_{0})
and
w_{0}:=\varphi_{2}(z_{0})
satisfy
h_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}(z_{0}))\neq 0
and
h_{2}^{\prime}(w_{0})\neq 0
: by holomorphicity of
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, this holds true for all
z_{0}
outside a discrete, relatively closed subset
D\subset B_{1}^{2}(0)
.

By the Cauchy–Riemann equations, the harmonic map
\Phi_{2}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}=\Re h_{2}
has nonzero differential at
w_{0}
. By the inverse function theorem, there exists a local chart
\psi
centered at
w_{0}
, with some ball
B_{\delta}^{2}(0)
as its image, such that

(6.3)

\Phi_{2}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}(y)-\Phi_{2}(z_{0})=y_{2}\quad\text{on }B_{\delta}^{2}(0).

Since
\Phi
is weakly conformal, we have
(\partial_{z}\Phi_{1})^{2}+(\partial_{z}\Phi_{2})^{2}=0
a.e., hence there exists a measurable function
\varepsilon\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\left\{-1,1\right\})
such that

(6.4)

\partial_{z}\Phi_{1}(z)=i\varepsilon(z)\partial_{z}\Phi_{2}(z)\quad\text{a.e. on }B_{1}^{2}(0).

Combining (
6.2
) and (
6.4
) we obtain

\nabla\Psi_{1}=\varepsilon N\nabla\Phi_{2}

a.e. Using (
6.3
) and the chain rule again, we get

\partial_{1}(\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1})=0,\quad\partial_{2}(\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1})=(\varepsilon N)\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}

a.e. and, since
\partial_{12}^{2}(\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1})=0
distributionally, we deduce that

(\varepsilon N)\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}(y)=\partial_{2}(\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1})(y)=g(y_{2})

a.e. on
B_{\delta}^{2}(0)
, for a suitable
g\in L^{\infty}((-\delta,\delta))
, as is immediately verified e.g. by mollification.

Let
G
be a Lipschitz primitive of
g
on
(-\delta,\delta)
. We have

\nabla(\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1})=\nabla(G(y_{2}))\quad\text{on }B_{\delta}^{2}(0),

so up to subtracting a constant from
G
we obtain

\Psi_{1}\circ\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}(y)=G(y_{2})

and finally, using (
6.3
),

(6.5)

\Psi_{1}=G\circ(\Phi_{2}-\Phi_{2}(z_{0}))\quad\text{on }\varphi_{2}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}(B_{\delta}^{2}(0))\ni z_{0}.

Since
f_{1}=\Phi_{1}+i\Psi_{1}
is injective in a neighborhood of
z_{0}
(being
h_{1}^{\prime}(\varphi_{1}(z_{0}))\neq 0
), from (
6.5
) we deduce that
\Phi
is injective on some neighborhood
B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0})
.

We claim that, as a consequence,
\det(\nabla\Phi)
has a constant sign on
B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
: indeed, the induced map

\Phi_{*}:H_{1}(\partial B_{r}^{2}(z))\to H_{1}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{\Phi(z)\right\})

is clearly independent of
z\in B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0})
and
0<r<\dist(z,\partial B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0}))
, once the two groups are canonically identified with
\mathbb{Z}
. But, for any
z\in B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0})\cap{\mathcal{G}^{f}}
, applying Lemma
A.4
to
\Phi-\Phi(z)-\left\langle\nabla\Phi(z),\cdot-z\right\rangle
we can find a small radius
r
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Phi\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r}^{2}(z)}}
is homotopic to
\Phi(z)+\left\langle\nabla\Phi(z),\cdot-z\right\rangle
(as a map
\partial B_{r}^{2}(z)\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{\Phi(z)\right\}
). Thus the above map
\Phi_{*}
coincides with the multiplication by
\sgn\det(\nabla\Phi(z))
and our claim follows.

From this fact and the weak conformality assumption, on
B_{\eta}^{2}(z_{0})
either
(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})
or
(\Phi_{1},-\Phi_{2})
satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations.
In particular,
\Phi
is real analytic on the connected set
B_{1}^{2}(0)\setminus D
. Since locally we have either
\partial_{\overline{z}}(\Phi_{1}+i\Phi_{2})=0
or
\partial_{z}(\Phi_{1}+i\Phi_{2})=0
, by analyticity
\Phi_{1}+i\Phi_{2}
is globally holomorphic or antiholomorphic on
B_{1}^{2}(0)\setminus D
, hence also on
B_{1}^{2}(0)
. Finally, (
6.1
) gives

\partial_{1}\Phi_{k}\partial_{1}N+\partial_{2}\Phi_{k}\partial_{2}N=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}^{2}(0))

for
k=1,2
(as
\Delta\Phi_{k}=0
). Since
\nabla\Phi_{1}
and
\nabla\Phi_{2}
are smooth and linearly independent outside a closed discrete set, we infer
\nabla N=0
here and thus, by connectedness,
N
is a.e. constant.
∎

We now prove the result for arbitrary
q
.

Theorem 6.2 (regularity for
\bm{q>2}
).

Assume
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
is weakly conformal,
N\in L^{\infty}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{N}\setminus\left\{0\right\})
and

-\operatorname{div}(N\nabla\Phi)=0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q}).

Then
\Delta\Phi=0
and, if
\Phi
is nonconstant,
N
is a.e. constant.

Proof.

As in the previous proof, we notice that
\Phi
is continuous. We can assume that
\Phi
is not constant. The triple
(B_{1}^{2}(0),\Phi,N)
satisfies Definition
2.9
(with
{\mathcal{M}^{m}}=\mathbb{R}^{q}
), except possibly for the technical condition (
2.6
): indeed, for any
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}^{2}(0)
and any
F\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega),\mathbb{R}^{q})
,
F(\Phi)\bm{1}_{\omega}
lies in
W^{1,2}_{0}(B_{1}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
and thus
\int_{\omega}N\left\langle\nabla(F(\Phi));\nabla\Phi\right\rangle\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=0
.

Assume, without loss of generality, that
\Phi_{1}
is not constant and let
\Psi_{1}
,
f_{1}
,
\varphi_{1}
and
h_{1}
be the functions constructed as in the preceding proof. Let
D\subset\varphi_{1}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
be the discrete set of points where
h_{1}^{\prime}=0
, or equivalently (by the Cauchy–Riemann equations) where
\nabla(\Phi_{1}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1})=0
. From the chain rule and the fact that
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{1}^{-1}
map negligible sets to negligible sets (see
[
28
, Lemma III.6.4]
and
[
24
, Lemma 4.12]
) we deduce that, for a.e.
x\in B_{1}^{2}(0)
,

\nabla\varphi_{1}(x)
is invertible,
\nabla(\Phi_{1}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1})(\varphi_{1}(x))\neq 0
and
\nabla\Phi_{1}(x)
is given by the composition of these differentials. Hence,
\nabla\Phi\neq 0
a.e. and thus has full rank a.e. (by weak conformality).

Let
S\subseteq B_{1}^{2}(0)
denote the complement of the biggest open subset where
\Delta\Phi=0
.
We remark that, given
x\in B_{1}^{2}(0)
, if there exists a neighborhood
\omega\subset\hskip-1.0pt\subset B_{1}^{2}(0)
with
\Phi(x)\cap\Phi(\partial\omega)=\emptyset
then
x\not\in S
: indeed,
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
is stationary in
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus\Phi(\partial\omega)
, so by the monotonicity formula
\mathbf{v}_{\omega}
satisfies (
2.6
) for
p\in B_{\rho}^{q}(\Phi(x))
and
s<\rho
, where
\rho:=\frac{1}{2}\dist(\Phi(x),\Phi(\partial\omega))
. Thus, replacing
\omega
with
\omega\cap\Phi^{-1}(B_{\rho/2}^{2}(\Phi(x)))
, the triple
(\omega,\Phi,N)
is a local parametrized stationary varifold and Theorem
5.7
gives
\Delta\Phi=0
near
x
. In particular, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.7
, we infer that
x\not\in S
for any Lebesgue point
x
for
\nabla\Phi
with
\nabla\Phi(x)\neq 0
. Being
\nabla\Phi\neq 0
a.e., we get that
\mathcal{L}^{2}(S)=0
and that
\Phi
is nonconstant on any ball outside
S
.

Moreover,
N
is a.e. constant on every connected component
U
of
B_{1}^{2}(0)\setminus S
, since on
U
we have
\partial_{1}\Phi_{k}\partial_{1}N+\partial_{2}\Phi_{k}\partial_{2}N=0
for all
k=1,\dots,q
and the (classical) differential
\nabla\Phi
has full rank except for a discrete set (being
\Phi
a nonconstant harmonic function on
U
), which does not disconnect
U
.

It suffices to show that
S\subseteq\varphi_{1}^{-1}(D)
, since then any point of
S
is a removable singularity and thus
S=\emptyset
. Assume by contradiction that there exists a point
x_{0}\in S\setminus\varphi_{1}^{-1}(D)
.
Let
\psi:V\to(-1,1)^{2}
be a local chart centered at
\varphi_{1}(x_{0})
and such that

(6.6)

\Phi_{1}\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}(y)=\Phi_{1}(x_{0})+y_{2}\quad\text{on }(-1,1)^{2}.

Let
\Xi:=\Phi\circ\varphi_{1}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}
. We claim that the negligible set
S^{\prime}:=\psi(V\cap\varphi_{1}(S))
, relatively closed in
(-1,1)^{2}
, has the following property: if
y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in S^{\prime}
, then
\Xi^{-1}(\Xi(y))
contains either
\left(-1,y_{1}\right]\times\left\{y_{2}\right\}
or
\left[y_{1},1\right)\times\left\{y_{2}\right\}
. If this were not true, we could find
a\in(-1,y_{1})
and
b\in(y_{1},1)
with
\Xi(a,y_{2}),\Xi(b,y_{2})\neq\Xi(y)
. Given any
\varepsilon<1-\left|y_{2}\right|
, by (
6.6
) we would have

\Xi(y)\not\in\Xi(\partial([a,b]\times[y_{2}-\varepsilon,y_{2}+\varepsilon]))
. But, as remarked earlier, this would imply
y\not\in S^{\prime}
.

This horizontal segment, i.e. either
\left(-1,y_{1}\right]\times\left\{y_{2}\right\}
or
\left[y_{1},1\right)\times\left\{y_{2}\right\}
, has to be contained in
S^{\prime}
(being
\Xi
locally injective at points outside
S^{\prime}
, with at most countably many exceptions). As a consequence we have
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\left\{t:(t,y_{2})\in S^{\prime}\right\})>0
and thus, by Fubini’s theorem and
\mathcal{L}^{2}(S^{\prime})=0
, we infer

(6.7)

((-1,1)\times\left\{t\right\})\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(-1,1).

Pick now any
\mu>0
with
((-1,1)\times\left\{\mu\right\})\cap S^{\prime}=\emptyset
.
Recall that
(0,0)\in S^{\prime}
and let
\lambda:=\max\left\{t<\mu:(0,t)\in S^{\prime}\right\}\geq 0
. From (
6.7
) it follows that the open set
((-1,1)\times(\lambda,\mu))\setminus S^{\prime}
is connected. We infer that
N
is a.e. constant on
\varphi_{1}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}((-1,1)\times(\lambda,\mu))
, hence
\Delta\Phi=0
on this open set. However, this contradicts the weak conformality assumption: let

\omega:=\varphi_{1}^{-1}\circ\psi^{-1}\left(\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)\times\left(\lambda,\frac{\lambda+\mu}{2}\right)\right),

on which
\Delta\Phi=0
, and take any homeomorphism
\upsilon:\overline{\omega}\to\overline{B}_{1}^{2}(0)
biholomorphic on
\omega
(using Riemann’s mapping theorem and
[
18
, Theorem I.3.1]
).
We have
\Phi_{1}\circ\upsilon^{-1}>\lambda
on
B_{1}^{2}(0)
, as well as
\Phi_{1}\circ\upsilon^{-1}=\lambda
and
\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1}
constant on some open arc
A
in
\partial B_{1}^{2}(0)
. Since
\Delta(\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1})=0
on
B_{1}^{2}(0)
, by standard regularity theory
\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1}
is smooth in a neighborhood of
A
in
\overline{B}_{1}^{2}(0)
. But, by Hopf’s maximum principle, the radial derivative of
\Phi_{1}\circ\upsilon^{-1}
is nonzero on
A
. So, by weak conformality of
\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1}
, the tangential derivative of
\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1}
does not vanish on
A
. This contradicts the fact that
\Phi\circ\upsilon^{-1}
is constant on
A
.
∎

This appendix collects some useful general facts about Sobolev functions on the plane. Most of them are standard results, with the possible exception of Lemmas

and

; however, we preferred to include the proofs since a precise reference for these statements is not immediately available in the literature.

Lemma A.1 (essential image of a Sobolev map).

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be an open connected set.
Let
\Psi\in W^{1,1}_{loc}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{q})
and assume that any point in a nonempty measurable set
E\subseteq\Omega
is a Lebesgue point for
\Psi
, as well as
\nabla\Psi=0
a.e. on
\Omega\setminus E
. Then the essential image of
\Psi
equals
\overline{\Psi(E)}
.

We recall that the essential image of
\Psi
is the (closed) set of values
p\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
such that
\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Psi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(p)))>0
for all
s>0
, or equivalently it is the support of the positive measure
\Psi_{*}(\bm{1}_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{2})
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in E
,
s>0
and let
p:=\Psi(x)
. By Chebyshev’s inequality

\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{r}^{2}(x)\setminus\Psi^{-1}(B_{s}^{q}(p)))\leq s^{-1}\int_{B_{r}^{2}(x)}\left|\Psi-\Psi(x)\right|\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}=o(r^{2}),

so
p
belongs to the essential image. We deduce that
\overline{\Psi(E)}
is included in the essential image. Conversely, assume that
B_{s}^{q}(p)
is disjoint from
\overline{\Psi(E)}
. We can find a function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{q},\mathbb{R}^{q})
with
\rho=\operatorname{id}
on
\mathbb{R}^{q}\setminus B_{s}^{q}(p)
and
\rho=\operatorname{id}+e_{1}
on
B_{s/2}^{q}(p)
.
By the chain rule,

\nabla(\rho\circ\Psi)=(\nabla\rho\circ\Psi)\nabla\Psi=\nabla\Psi

a.e. on
\Omega
, since
\nabla\Psi=0
a.e. on
\left\{\nabla\rho\circ\Psi\neq\operatorname{id}\right\}
.
Thus,
\rho\circ\Psi-\Psi
is constant a.e. But this function vanishes on
E
, hence
0
belongs to its essential image (by the same argument used above). Thus
\rho\circ\Psi=\Psi
a.e. and we infer that

\Psi^{-1}(B_{s/2}^{q}(p))\subseteq\left\{\rho\circ\Psi\neq\Psi\right\}
is negligible. This shows that
p
does not belong to the essential image.
∎

is absolutely continuous (a.e.), with derivative given by the chain rule.
The elementary inequality

\left\|\Psi\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))}\leq\bigg|\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-1.65277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-1.65277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.15695pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.13998pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.13998pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-0.82639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.09999pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.09999pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}\Psi\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\bigg|+\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq\frac{\left\|\Psi\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))}}{\sqrt{2\pi r^{\prime}}}+\left\|\nabla\Psi\right\|_{L^{1}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0))}

gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.5 (strong subconvergence on a.e. slice).

If
\Psi_{k}\rightharpoonup\Psi_{\infty}
in
W^{1,2}(B_{R}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
, then for a.e.
r^{\prime}\in(0,R)
there exists a subsequence
(k_{i})
such that

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}
and
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}
have absolutely continuous representatives and

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}\to{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}.

Proof.

We observe that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}
coincides with the trace of
\Psi_{\infty}
on
\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)
for a.e.
r^{\prime}\in(0,R)
: actually, this happens whenever
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e. point on
\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)
is a Lebesgue point of
\Psi_{\infty}
(see
[
15
, Theorem 5.7]
); the same holds for
\Psi_{k}
. Now, by Fatou’s lemma,

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{R}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}(\left|\Psi_{k}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla\Psi_{k}\right|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\,dr^{\prime}\\
&\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{0}^{R}\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}(\left|\Psi_{k}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla\Psi_{k}\right|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\,dr^{\prime}\\
&=\liminf_{k\to\infty}\left\|\Psi_{k}\right\|_{W^{1,2}}^{2}<\infty.\end{split}

Hence, for a.e.
r^{\prime}\in(0,R)
,

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}(\left|\Psi_{k}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla\Psi_{k}\right|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}<\infty,

which means that there exists a subsequence
(k_{i})
such that

\sup_{i}\int_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}(\left|\Psi_{k_{i}}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla\Psi_{k_{i}}\right|^{2})\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}<\infty.

We can also assume that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}
equals the trace, that it has a
W^{1,2}
representative with weak derivative given by the chain rule and that the analogous statements hold also for
\Psi_{k_{i}}
.

In particular, the sequence
\left({\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}\right)
is bounded in
W^{1,2}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
: by the compact embedding into
C^{0}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
we deduce that (up to subsequences)
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}\to f
in
L^{\infty}
, for some
f\in C^{0}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
. By the weak continuity of the trace, we have
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{k_{i}}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}\rightharpoonup{\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}
in
L^{2}(\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0),\mathbb{R}^{q})
, hence
f={\left.\kern-1.2pt\Psi_{\infty}\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\partial B_{r^{\prime}}^{2}(0)}}

\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
∎

Lemma A.6 (almost planar conformal matrices).

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q\times 2}
denote the set of matrices
M
with
\sum_{i=1}^{q}M_{ij}M_{ik}=\frac{|M|^{2}}{2}\delta_{jk}
, where
|M|
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
M
(
\mathcal{C}
can be identified with the set of linear weakly conformal maps
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{q}
). For any
\tau>0
there exists
C=C(\tau,q)
such that

|M_{11}M_{21}+M_{12}M_{22}|+|M_{11}^{2}+M_{12}^{2}-J(M)|+|M_{21}^{2}+M_{22}^{2}-J(M)|\leq\tau|M|^{2}+C\sum_{i=3}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{2}M_{ij}^{2}

for all
M\in\mathcal{C}
, where
J(M):=\left|M_{11}M_{22}-M_{12}M_{21}\right|
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that, for a sequence
(M^{k})\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, we have

\begin{split}&|M_{11}^{k}M_{21}^{k}+M_{12}^{k}M_{22}^{k}|+|(M_{11}^{k})^{2}+(M_{12}^{k})^{2}-J(M^{k})|+|(M_{21}^{k})^{2}+(M_{22}^{k})^{2}-J(M^{k})|\\
&>\tau|M^{k}|^{2}+k\sum_{i=3}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(M_{ij}^{k})^{2}.\end{split}

By homogeneity we can assume
|M^{k}|=1
for all
k
. As a consequence,
\sum_{i=3}^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{2}(M_{ij}^{k})^{2}\to 0
. So the sequence has a limit point
M^{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times 2}
satisfying

(A.2)

M^{\infty}\in\mathcal{C},\qquad M_{ij}^{\infty}=0\quad\text{for }i=3,\dots,q\text{ and }j=1,2,

|M_{11}^{\infty}M_{21}^{\infty}+M_{12}^{\infty}M_{22}^{\infty}|+|(M_{11}^{\infty})^{2}+(M_{12}^{\infty})^{2}-J(M^{\infty})|+|(M_{21}^{\infty})^{2}+(M_{22}^{\infty})^{2}-J(M^{\infty})|\geq\tau.

But conditions (
A.2
) force
\begin{pmatrix}M_{11}^{\infty}&M_{12}^{\infty}\\
M_{21}^{\infty}&M_{22}^{\infty}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&\mp b\\
b&\pm a\end{pmatrix}
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
so the left-hand side of the last inequality vanishes, yielding the desired contradiction.
∎

Lemma A.7 (
\bm{L^{1,\infty}}
-variance estimate).

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
C=C(\varepsilon)
such that

\begin{split}&\big\|f-(f)_{B_{r/2}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1,\infty}(B_{r/2}^{2}(0))}\leq\varepsilon\big\|f-(f)_{B_{3r/4}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{3r/4}^{2}(0))}\\
&+C\sup\Big\{\Big|\int_{B_{r}^{2}(0)}f\nabla\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big|\,;\ \phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{r}^{2}(0)),\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\Big\},\end{split}

for all
f\in L^{1}(B_{r}^{2}(0))
, where
(f)_{B_{s}^{2}(0)}:=\,\mathchoice{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\displaystyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-1.65277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\displaystyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 2.08334pt\hskip-2.08334pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\textstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-2.08334pt\hskip-2.77774pt\raisebox{-1.65277pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.2pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\textstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.77774pt\hskip 2.08334pt}\kern-7.49994pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.72743pt\hskip-1.72743pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.72743pt\hskip-2.45831pt\raisebox{-1.15695pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.13998pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.13998pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.45831pt\hskip 1.72743pt}\kern-6.78813pt}{\hbox{\hskip 1.57988pt\hskip-1.57988pt\hbox{$\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\phantom{\int}$}$}\hskip-1.57988pt\hskip-2.29166pt\raisebox{-0.82639pt}{\hbox{$\rule{0.09999pt}{0.0pt}\rule{0.09999pt}{0.0pt}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$}$}}\hskip-2.29166pt\hskip 1.57988pt}\kern-6.49301pt}\int_{B_{s}^{2}(0)}f\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}
.

Proof.

By scaling-invariance and homogeneity, we can assume that
r=1
and that the supremum in the right-hand side equals
1
. Thanks to Hahn–Banach theorem applied to the functional

(\partial_{1}\phi,\partial_{2}\phi)\mapsto\int_{B_{1}^{2}(0)}f\partial_{i}\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2},\quad\text{on }\left\{(\partial_{1}\phi,\partial_{2}\phi)\mid\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B_{1}^{2}(0))\right\}\subseteq C_{c}^{0}(B_{1}^{2}(0))^{2},

we can find finite Borel measures
g_{ij}\in\mathcal{M}(B_{1}^{2}(0))
(for
i,j=1,2
) with

(A.3)

\displaystyle\left\|g_{ij}\right\|(B_{1}^{2}(0))\leq 1,\qquad\partial_{i}f=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}g_{ij}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B_{1}^{2}(0)).

By convolution with a mollifier
\rho_{\delta}
, we can assume
f,g_{ij}\in C^{\infty}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
and that (
A.3
) holds in the ball
B_{3/4}^{2}(0)
: once we show that (
A.3
), together with the requirement that
\big|\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}f\nabla\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\big|\leq 1
whenever
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
and
\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
, imply

(A.4)

\displaystyle\big\|f-(f)_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1,\infty}(B_{1/2}^{2}(0))}\leq\varepsilon\big\|f-(f)_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))}+C

for the mollified
f
, the result follows for the original function by letting
\delta\to 0
(notice that the above requirement holds if
\delta
is small enough).
We choose a cut-off function
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
such that
\chi=1
on a neighborhood of
\overline{B}_{1/2}^{2}(0)
. Notice that

\partial_{i}(\chi f)=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}(\chi g_{ij})-\sum_{j}\partial_{j}\chi g_{ij}+\partial_{i}\chi f,

so we get the following distributional identity in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
:

\begin{split}(1-\Delta)(\chi f)&=(1-\Delta)\chi f-\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}(\chi g_{ij})+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}\chi g_{ij}+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{j}\chi\partial_{i}g_{ij}-\sum_{i}\partial_{i}\chi\partial_{i}f\\
&=(1-\Delta)\chi f-\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}(\chi g_{ij})+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}\chi g_{ij}+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{j}\chi\partial_{i}g_{ij}-\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\chi\partial_{j}g_{ij}\\
&=(1-\Delta)\chi f-\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}(\chi g_{ij})+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}^{2}\chi g_{ij}+\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}(\partial_{j}\chi g_{ij})-\sum_{i,j}\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}\chi g_{ij}).\end{split}

Applying
(1-\Delta)^{-1}
to both sides and observing that the operators

(1-\Delta)^{-1}
,
(1-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla
and
(1-\Delta)^{-1}\nabla^{2}
correspond to Hörmander–Mikhlin Fourier multipliers, we obtain

\chi f=(1-\Delta)^{-1}((1-\Delta)\chi f)+r,

where the Schwartz function
r
satisfies
\left\|r\right\|_{L^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq C^{\prime}
for some absolute constant
C^{\prime}
.
The desired estimate (
A.4
) now follows from Lemma
A.8
below.
∎

Lemma A.8 (
\bm{L^{1}}
-estimate for a commutator).

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
C=C(\varepsilon)
such that

\begin{split}&\big\|(1-\Delta)^{-1}((1-\Delta)\chi f)-(f)_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2}^{2}(0))}\leq\varepsilon\big\|f-(f)_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))}\\
&+C\sup\Big\{\Big|\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}f\nabla\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big|\,;\ \phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0)),\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\Big\},\end{split}

for all
f\in C^{\infty}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))\cap L^{1}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
.

Proof.

We can assume
(f)_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}=0
and
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))}=1
.
If the statement is false, we can find a sequence
(f_{n})
of such functions with

(A.5)

\begin{split}&\big\|(1-\Delta)^{-1}((1-\Delta)\chi f_{n})-(f_{n})_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2}^{2}(0))}\\
&\geq\varepsilon+n\sup\Big\{\Big|\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}f_{n}\nabla\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\Big|\,;\ \phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0)),\left\|\nabla\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1\Big\}.\end{split}

We remark that, for any
h\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
,

(A.6)

(1-\Delta)^{-1}h=K*h\quad\text{on }B_{1/2}^{2}(0),\qquad K:=1_{B_{2}^{2}(0)}\mathcal{F}^{-1}((1+4\pi^{2}|\xi|^{2})^{-1})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}).

In particular, thanks to (
A.6
), the left-hand side of (
A.5
) is bounded by a constant and we deduce
\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}f_{n}\nabla\phi\,d\mathcal{L}^{2}\to 0
for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
. There exists a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})
such that
f_{n_{k}}\mathcal{L}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\mu
, for some
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
. From the identity
\int_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}\nabla\phi\,d\mu=0
for all
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{3/4}^{2}(0))
we infer
\mu=c\bm{1}_{B_{3/4}^{2}(0)}\mathcal{L}^{2}
(with
c
a real constant).
Hence, letting

h_{k}:=(1-\Delta)\chi f_{n_{k}},\quad h_{\infty}:=c(1-\Delta)\chi,

we have
h_{k}\mathcal{L}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}h_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{2}
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and, since
\mu(\partial B_{1/2}^{2}(0))=0
, we deduce that
(f_{n_{k}})_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}\to c
.
We also have

(A.7)

\psi*h_{k}\to\psi*h_{\infty}\quad\text{in }C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

for any
\psi\in C^{0}_{c}(B_{2}^{2}(0))
: if this were not true, up to further subsequences we could find
\delta>0
and
(x_{k})\subseteq B_{3}^{2}(0)
such that

(A.8)

\left|\psi*h_{k}(x_{k})-\psi*h_{\infty}(x_{k})\right|\geq\delta

and we could also assume that
x_{k}\to x_{\infty}\in\overline{B}_{3}^{2}(0)
; but
\psi(x_{k}-\cdot)\to\psi(x_{\infty}-\cdot)
uniformly, so

\psi*h_{k}(x_{k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi(x_{k}-\cdot)\,d(h_{k}\mathcal{L}^{2})\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi(x_{\infty}-\cdot)\,d(h_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{2})=\psi*h_{\infty}(x_{\infty}),

while trivially
\psi*h_{\infty}(x_{k})\to\psi*h_{\infty}(x_{\infty})
, contradicting (
A.8
) for
k
large enough.

Moreover,
K*h_{k}\to K*h_{\infty}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, as is readily seen from (
A.7
) by approximating
K
with functions
\psi\in C^{0}_{c}(B_{2}^{2}(0))
in the
L^{1}
-topology.
Using (
A.6
), it follows that

1_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}(1-\Delta)^{-1}h_{k}\to 1_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}(1-\Delta)^{-1}h_{\infty}=1_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}(1-\Delta)^{-1}(c(1-\Delta)\chi)=c1_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}

in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. However, for
k
large enough, this contradicts the inequality

\big\|(1-\Delta)^{-1}((1-\Delta)\chi f_{n_{k}})-(f_{n_{k}})_{B_{1/2}^{2}(0)}\big\|_{L^{1}(B_{1/2}^{2}(0))}\geq\varepsilon.\qed
