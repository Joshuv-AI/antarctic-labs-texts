The Uncertainty Principle and Classical Amplitudes Preprint: SAGEX-21-31-E, QMUL-PH-21-56
Andrea Cristofoli, Riccardo Gonzo, Nathan Moynihan, Donal O’Connell, Alasdair Ross, Matteo Sergola, Chris D. White

Abstract
We study the variance in the measurement of observables during scattering events, as computed using amplitudes. The classical regime, characterised by negligible uncertainty, emerges as a consequence of an infinite set of relationships among multileg, multiloop amplitudes in a momentum-transfer expansion. We discuss two non-trivial examples in detail: the six-point tree and the five-point one-loop amplitudes in scalar QED. We interpret these relationships in terms of a coherent exponentiation of radiative effects in the classical limit which generalises the eikonal formula, and show how to recover the impulse, including radiation reaction, from this generalised eikonal. Finally, we incorporate the physics of spin into our framework.

2
Negligible Uncertainty

In classical electrodynamics, a key role is played by the field strength
F_{\mu\nu}(x)
. This object is a complete gauge-invariant characterisation of the field; once it is known, quantities such as the energy-momentum radiated to infinity and the field angular momentum are easily determined. In a quantum description, the field becomes an operator
\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)
. In a semiclassical situation, the expectation value of this operator on a state
|\psi\rangle
should equal the classical field, up to negligible quantum corrections:

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)|\psi\rangle=F_{\mu\nu}(x)+\mathcal{O}(\hbar)\,.

(1)

Note that we have schematically indicated the presence of small, order
\hbar
, quantum corrections. More precisely, these corrections must be suppressed by dimensionless ratios involving Planck’s constant; the precise ratios depend on the actual physical context.

Since in the quantum theory a single-valued field is replaced by the expectation value of an operator, we must address the quintessentially quantum mechanical issue of uncertainty. The uncertainty can be characterised by the variance

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)|\psi\rangle\,.

(2)

In the domain of validity of the classical approximation, this variance must be negligible.

Precisely the same remarks hold in a quantum mechanical approach to GR. The curvature tensor
R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)
in the classical theory is replaced by the expectation value of the curvature operator
\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)
. The variance

\displaystyle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)\mathbbm{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(y)|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(y)|\psi\rangle

(3)

must be negligible.

This section is devoted to an investigation of this condition of negligible uncertainty. Working in the KMOC formalism at lowest order in perturbation theory, we will see that the expectations
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)S|\psi\rangle
and
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)\mathbbm{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(y)S|\psi\rangle
are determined by tree-level
six
-point amplitudes while
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)S|\psi\rangle
and
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}(x)S|\psi\rangle
are determined by
five
-point tree amplitudes.
(We have included factors of the
S
matrix here as we are mainly interested in variances of operators in the asymptotic future, given an initial state
|\psi\rangle
in the very far past.)
We must then face the question of how it can be that the variance is negligible.

2.3
Negligible variance?

We have now seen explicitly that the expectation of a single field strength is determined by a five-point amplitude, while the expectation of two field strengths is a six-point tree amplitude at lowest order in the coupling
g
. But for the uncertainty in the field strength to be negligible, we need the variance to be negligible. How can this happen?

Let us count powers of the coupling in the electromagnetic variance. The product of two field-strength expectations is

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)S|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)S|\psi\rangle\sim(\mathcal{A}_{5,0})^{2}\sim(g^{3})^{2}\,.

(40)

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)S|\psi\rangle\sim\mathcal{A}_{6,0}\sim g^{4}\,.

(41)

Thus the situation seems to very bad: the variance

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)S|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)S|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}(x)\mathbbm{F}_{\rho\sigma}(y)S|\psi\rangle

(42)

seems to be dominated by the expectation of two field-strength operators! For the classical limit to emerge as expected, we need the six-point tree amplitude to be suppressed somehow.

One possibility is that it is suppressed by powers of
\hbar
, but naively that is not the case. Consider, for example, the 6-point Feynman diagram shown in figure

\hbar

Zero-variance relations

Our discussion so far reveals that scattering amplitudes, viewed as Laurent series in
\hbar
, obey certain properties which permit the emergence of a classical limit through negligible uncertainty. This Laurent expansion can also be viewed as an expansion in small momentum transfers divided by the centre-of-mass energy
\sqrt{s}
(we will refer to this expansion as a transfer expansion below).
In fact, the emergence of the classical limit imposes an
infinite
set of these relationships, which we will call “zero-variance relations” on scattering amplitudes.
In this section we will describe the origin of these relations, and explicitly demonstrate a non-trivial example at one loop and five points.

3.1
Mixed variances

To see where these relationships are coming from, recall that the double field-strength expectation (
38
) depends on a six-point amplitude. We have seen that the dominant term is actually the six-point one-loop amplitude, occurring at next-to-leading order in the expansion in
g
. At this order an additional term contributes to the double field-strength expectation; this term is the product of two five-point amplitudes. Now, negligible uncertainty demands that the complete double field-strength expectation must be the product of two single field-strength expectations. At leading order in the coupling, and leading non-trivial order in
\hbar
, we conclude that there must exist a relationship between the leading-in-
\hbar
six-point one-loop amplitude and the product of two five-point trees.

Further examples of relationships between amplitudes can be obtained by considering expectations of three (or more) field strengths, leading to relationships between seven- (or higher-) point loop amplitudes and products of three (or more) five-point amplitudes.

Yet more relationships occur by considering expectations of products of operators including field strengths and momenta. For example,
consider the variance

\displaystyle V_{\mu\nu\rho}\equiv\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}|\psi\rangle\,.

(55)

This is the variance in a measurement of the initial momentum and the future field strength; it must be negligible in the classical regime. In a quantum-first approach, however, this variance will not vanish. Indeed it need not be real:

\displaystyle V_{\mu\nu\rho}^{*}=\langle\psi|\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S|\psi\rangle-\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}|\psi\rangle\neq V_{\mu\nu\rho}.

(56)

We can derive an interesting constraint on the five-point one-loop amplitude by demanding that imaginary part of this variance vanishes in the classical approximation. We therefore define

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}

\displaystyle=i(V_{\mu\nu\rho}^{*}-V_{\mu\nu\rho})

(57)

\displaystyle=i\langle\psi|\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S-S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)S\mathbbm{P}_{\mu}|\psi\rangle\,.

Expanding the states as usual, we easily find

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle\phi_{b}^{*}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{b}(p_{1},p_{2})i(p^{\prime}_{1\mu}-p_{1\mu})

(58)

\displaystyle\times\langle p^{\prime}_{1}p^{\prime}_{2}|i(\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T-T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x))+T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T|p_{1}p_{2}\rangle\,.

The factor
i(p^{\prime}_{1\mu}-p_{1\mu})
is important here: working at leading perturbative order, this factor is of order
\hbar
. It is also worth noting that we may write the expectation of the field strength itself as

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},

\displaystyle p_{1},p_{2})\,\phi_{b}^{*}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{b}(p_{1},p_{2})

(59)

\displaystyle\times\langle p^{\prime}_{1}p^{\prime}_{2}|i(\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T-T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x))+T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T|p_{1}p_{2}\rangle\,.

Thus the
i(p^{\prime}_{1\mu}-p_{1\mu})\sim\hbar
factor in the variance is the key distinction between the variance, which vanishes classically, and the field strength which of course should not vanish classically. As we have already seen that the field strength is related to five-point amplitudes, it is now clear that the condition of vanishing
\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}
will become a condition on five-point amplitudes.

It is useful to break the variance
\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}
up into two structures:

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle\phi_{b}^{*}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{b}(p_{1},p_{2})i(p^{\prime}_{1\mu}-p_{1\mu})

(60)

\displaystyle\times\langle p^{\prime}_{1}p^{\prime}_{2}|i(\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T-T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x))|p_{1}p_{2}\rangle\,,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(2)}=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle\phi_{b}^{*}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{b}(p_{1},p_{2})i(p^{\prime}_{1\mu}-p_{1\mu})

(61)

\displaystyle\times\langle p^{\prime}_{1}p^{\prime}_{2}|T^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\nu\rho}(x)T|p_{1}p_{2}\rangle\,.

Both of these objects are real, which is convenient in terms of keeping the expressions simple.

We may simplify these structures using the explicit expression for the field strength given in equation (
10

\mathcal{O}^{(1)}
we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hbar}}\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},p_{1},p_{2},k)\phi_{b}^{*}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{b}(p_{1},p_{2})\times

(62)

\displaystyle\times i(p_{1\mu}^{\prime}-p_{1\mu})\langle k^{\eta},p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}|T|p_{1},p_{2}\rangle\,\bar{k}^{\vphantom{\mu}}_{[\nu}\varepsilon^{(\eta)*}_{\rho]}\,e^{-i\bar{k}\cdot x}\,,

which should be compared to equation (
28

i(p_{1\mu}^{\prime}-p_{1\mu})

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\hbar^{9/2}\sum_{\eta}\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(\bar{k})\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{2}\,\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot\bar{q}_{1})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot\bar{q}_{2})\,

(63)

\displaystyle\times i\bar{q}_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{5}(p_{1}p_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+q_{1},p_{2}+q_{2},k^{\eta})\,\hat{\delta}^{4}(\bar{k}+\bar{q}_{1}+\bar{q}_{2})\,\bar{k}^{\vphantom{\mu}}_{[\nu}\varepsilon^{(\eta)*}_{\rho]}\,e^{-i(\bar{k}\cdot x+\bar{q}_{1}\cdot b)}\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle\,.

Referring back once more to equation (

\hbar

In order to control the
\hbar
expansion of scattering amplitudes, it is useful to introduce some further notation. Let us write the amplitudes as explicit Laurent series in
\hbar
building on equation (
35

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{5,0}(i\rightarrow f)=\hbar^{-7/2}\left(\mathcal{A}_{5,0}^{(0)}(i\rightarrow f)+\hbar\mathcal{A}_{5,0}^{(1)}(i\rightarrow f)+\cdots\right)\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{5,1}(i\rightarrow f)=\hbar^{-9/2}\left(\mathcal{A}_{5,1}^{(0)}(i\rightarrow f)+\hbar\mathcal{A}_{5,1}^{(1)}(i\rightarrow f)+\cdots\right)\,.

We have scaled out the dominant (inverse) power of
\hbar
; the quantities
\mathcal{A}_{n,L}^{(p)}
are
\hbar
-independent gauge-invariant sub-amplitudes; it is precisely these quantities that are related by our reasoning.
This expansion defines an infinite set of objects
\mathcal{A}_{n,L}^{(p)}
which could in principle be reassembled into the full amplitude.
They are a kind of partial amplitude, but distinct from the usual use of this term.
We will therefore refer to them as “fragmentary amplitudes”, or simply as “fragments.”

Since
\hbar
is dimensionful, it is useful to view these fragmentary amplitudes in a slightly different
way.
Amplitudes are functions of Mandelstam invariants; in the semi-classical region, we are expanding in powers of momentum transfers, such as
q^{2}=\hbar^{2}\bar{q}^{2}
at four points, divided by Mandelstam
s=(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}
. The semiclassical expansion is an expansion in powers of
\hbar\sqrt{-\bar{q}^{2}/s}
. More general amplitudes involve a richer set of momentum transfers
\bar{q}_{ij}^{2}
; our expansion is in powers of
\hbar\sqrt{-\bar{q}_{ij}^{2}/s}
.
We only consider amplitudes with two incoming massive particles.

We may also view the expansion as being in (inverse) powers of the large mass of the scattering particles
Luna:2017dtq
;
Damgaard:2019lfh
;
Aoude:2020onz
;
Haddad:2020tvs
.
This makes contact with effective field theory, especially heavy quark effective theory or, more generally, heavy particle effective theories as has been
emphasised in references
Damgaard:2019lfh
;
Aoude:2020onz
;
Haddad:2020tvs
.
Our fragmentary amplitudes correspond in this context to the standard HQET expansion in inverse powers of heavy masses.
It seems likely that a study of the properties of amplitudes in these theories would illuminate the structure of the fragmentary amplitudes.

Notice that this expansion is analogous, but different, to a soft expansion. In the soft expansion we take the momentum of an individual particle
soft.
In this transfer expansion we take the momenta in all messenger lines to be of the same order, and small compared to the incoming centre of
mass energy.
It is possible to perform the transfer expansion and then, in a second stage, to single out some line, say an outgoing photon, and take its momentum to be softer than all other messenger lines. This yields the soft limit of the transfer expansion.
It corresponds to the low-frequency limit in the classical approximation.
Interesting classical physics, including memory effects, appear in this region
Laddha:2018rle
;
Laddha:2018myi
;
Laddha:2018vbn
;

;

Now at classical order (
\hbar^{0}
) the tree level amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{5,0}
does not appear in
\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}
on account of the explicit factor
\hbar^{9/2}
in equation (

g
, non-trivial, classical contribution arises from the fragment
\mathcal{A}_{5,1}^{(0)}
. We conclude then that

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\eta}\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(\bar{k})\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{2}\,\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot\bar{q}_{1})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot\bar{q}_{2})\,\times

(65)

\displaystyle\times i\bar{q}_{\mu}\mathcal{A}_{5,1}^{(0)}(p_{1}p_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+q_{1},p_{2}+q_{2},k^{\eta})\,\hat{\delta}^{4}(\bar{k}+\bar{q}_{1}+\bar{q}_{2})\,\bar{k}^{\vphantom{\mu}}_{[\nu}\varepsilon^{(\eta)*}_{\rho]}\,e^{-i(\bar{k}\cdot x+\bar{q}_{1}\cdot b)}\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle\,.

The relevant fragmentary amplitude is the leading-in-
\hbar
five-point one-loop amplitude, sometimes known as the “superclassical” part of the one-loop amplitude. Of course in this context this fragment of the amplitude is contributing precisely at classical order.

Now the full
\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}
should vanish at classical order. Since
\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(1)}\neq 0

\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mu\nu\rho}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{\eta}\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(\bar{k})\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{2}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{w}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{w}_{2}\,\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot\bar{q}_{1})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot\bar{q}_{2})\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot\bar{w}_{1})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot\bar{w}_{2})

(66)

\displaystyle\times\bar{q}_{\mu}\,\hat{\delta}^{4}(\bar{k}+\bar{q}_{1}+\bar{q}_{2})\hat{\delta}^{4}(\bar{q}_{1}+\bar{q}_{2}-\bar{w}_{1}-\bar{w}_{2})\,\bar{k}^{\vphantom{\mu}}_{[\nu}\varepsilon^{(\eta)*}_{\rho]}\,e^{-i(\bar{k}\cdot x+\bar{q}_{1}\cdot b)}

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{A}_{5,0}^{(0)}(p_{1}p_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+w_{1},p_{2}+w_{2},k^{\eta})\mathcal{A}_{4,0}^{(0)}(p_{1}+w_{1},p_{2}+w_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+q_{1},p_{2}+q_{2})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle\,.

Comparing equations (

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{5,1}^{(0)}(p_{1}p_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+q_{1},p_{2}+q_{2},k^{\eta})

(67)

\displaystyle=-\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{w}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{w}_{2}\,\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot\bar{w}_{1})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot\bar{w}_{2})\hat{\delta}^{4}(\bar{q}_{1}+\bar{q}_{2}-\bar{w}_{1}-\bar{w}_{2})

\displaystyle\times\mathcal{A}_{5,0}^{(0)}(p_{1}p_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+w_{1},p_{2}+w_{2},k^{\eta})\mathcal{A}_{4,0}^{(0)}(p_{1}+w_{1},p_{2}+w_{2}\rightarrow p_{1}+q_{1},p_{2}+q_{2})\,.

Thus the dominant part of the five-point one-loop amplitude is given by the tree five-point and tree four-point amplitudes; we will check this relation explicitly in the next subsection.

Clearly this explicit example is one among an infinite set of relationships. Variances involving one field strength operator and two momenta will lead to relationships among two-loop five-point amplitudes and the product of one five-point tree and two four-point trees. We can continue, in principle, as far as we wish generating similar relations.
These negligible uncertainty relations generalise the well-known relations between multiloop four-point amplitudes required for eikonal exponentiation. Indeed consideration of expectations such as

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{1}}\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{2}}\cdots\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{n}}S|\psi\rangle\simeq\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{1}}|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{2}}|\psi\rangle\cdots\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{P}_{\mu_{n}}|\psi\rangle\,,

(68)

shows that there must be a relationship between the
n-1
loop four-point amplitude and the product of
n
tree amplitudes.

Thus we find a remarkable abundance of relationships between multiloop, multileg amplitudes, considered as Laurent series in
\hbar
, forced on us by the
absence
of uncertainty in the classical regime. In the next section of this article we will interpret these relationships in terms of a radiative generalisation of the eikonal exponentiation.

As well as finding explicit relations between different fragmentary amplitudes, we can use similar ideas to determine the
\hbar
scaling associated with fragments in the transfer expansion.
The kinds of multiple cancellations of
\hbar
powers we saw at six points must continue to occur at higher points. The reason again follows from considering expectations of products of more than two field-strength operators.

The arguments are based simply on counting powers of coupling and
\hbar
. We know that for the single expectation, at leading order, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}\rangle\sim g^{3}.

(69)

This means that we must also have
\langle\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rangle\sim(g^{3})^{n}
. Now we perform the KMOC analysis of
\langle\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rangle
. Following the steps of the calculation earlier in section
2.2
we find, schematically, that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbbm{F}^{n}\rangle\sim\hbar^{3n/2+2}\int\mathcal{A}_{4+n}.

(70)

These relations allow us to deduce two things. Firstly the relevant fragment of the complete amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{4+n}
must scale as
\hbar^{-3n/2-2}
. Secondly this fragment must have
3n
powers of the coupling
g
— this corresponds to having
n-1
loops. From this we can also infer the scaling of all other loop and tree amplitudes, in the classical limit, since each loop contributes an extra factor of
\hbar^{-1}
. In particular the tree scaling will be

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{4+n,0}\sim\hbar^{-n/2-3}.

(71)

This is consistent with the scaling we computed above for six points
(n=2)
, and we have also checked explicitly at seven points.
It is interesting to see how these two very simple power counting arguments have completely constrained the
\hbar
scaling of all
2\rightarrow 2+n
amplitudes.

We now turn to verifying equation (

4
Generalising the Eikonal

We have now seen that the uncertainty, or the variance, in the measurement of a scattering observable can be computed in terms of amplitudes and, moreover, that the classical absence of uncertainty leads to an infinite set of relationships among fragments of amplitudes expanded in powers of
momentum transfer, which is a Laurent series in
\hbar

In a purely conservative limit, these relationships can be understood in terms of eikonal exponentiation. Our goal now is to review the eikonal formula, emphasising its connection to final state dynamics. We will then build on this eikonal state to incorporate radiative dynamics as a kind of coherent state so that the variance is naturally small.

4.4
Extension with coherent radiation

The exponentiated eikonal final state of equation (

We have also seen that coherent states naturally enforce minimal uncertainty for radiation.
Now let us put these two ideas together to form a proposal for an eikonal-type final state in the fully dynamical, radiative, case.

It is very natural to consider a modification of the eikonal formula which includes radiation, and indeed this idea has received attention

g^{4}
) tree-level six-point
amplitude. Of course this is as it should be: we saw that the six-point tree is suppressed in the classical region in
section
2.4
. Similarly the seven-point tree and one-loop amplitudes are suppressed, etc.

As a final remark, note that we did not introduce any normalisation factor in our proposal. This may be surprising in view of the necessity to
normalise the coherent state as in equation (
111
). The origin of the difference is simply that unitarity must already
guarantee the normalisation of the final state. As is by now well understood, at two loops the eikonal function
\chi
ceases to be real in the
radiative case. Instead the imaginary part of
\chi
is related to
\sum_{\eta}|\alpha^{(\eta)}|^{2}
; this supplies the necessary normalisation.

4.5
Radiation reaction

Once there is radiation, there must also be radiation reaction: the particle’s motion must change in the radiative case relative to the conservative case to account for the loss of momentum to radiation. In this section we will see that the waveshape indeed contributes to the impulse of a particle in the manner required.

We begin by acting on our conjectural final state, equation (
124
), with the momentum operator of the field corresponding to particle 1:

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}^{\mu}_{1}S|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\;\tilde{\phi}_{b}(x_{1},x_{2})\,e^{i(p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot x_{1}+p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot x_{2})/\hbar}

(138)

\displaystyle\times\,e^{i[q\cdot(x-x_{1}+x_{2})+\chi(x_{\perp};s)]/\hbar}\exp\left[\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\alpha^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(k)\right]\,p_{1}^{\prime\mu}|p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\rangle\,.

The operator simply inserts a factor
p_{1}^{\prime\mu}
. We proceed by rewriting this factor in terms of a derivative
-i\hbar\partial/\partial x_{1\mu}
acting on the exponential factor in the first line of equation (
138
), and then integrating by parts. Neglecting the boundary term, the result is

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}^{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\;\,e^{i(p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot x_{1}+p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot x_{2})/\hbar}e^{i(q\cdot x+\chi(x_{\perp};\,s))/\hbar}

(139)

\displaystyle\times i\hbar\partial_{1}^{\mu}\left(\tilde{\phi}_{b}(x_{1},x_{2})e^{iq\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})/\hbar}\exp\left[\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\,\alpha^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(k)\right]\right)\,|p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\rangle\,.

Expanding out the derivative, we encounter three terms. In the first, the derivative acts on the spatial wavefunction: as usual in quantum mechanics, this term will evaluate (in the expectation value of the final momentum) to the contribution of the initial momentum. The second term arises when the derivative operator acts on
e^{iq\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})/\hbar}
, which inserts a factor of
q^{\mu}
. This term is familiar from equation (
97
) in section
4.2
, and contributes to the impulse as a (suitably projected) derivative of the eikonal function. Only the final term is new: it involves the waveshape, and must then be the origin of radiation reaction in our approach.

Since we have discussed the conservative impulse in detail in section
4.2
, we
focus on the final (new) term here. In this term, the derivative brings down a factor of

\displaystyle\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\,\partial_{1}^{\mu}\alpha^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(k).

(140)

Now to extract the momentum observable we multiply by
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}
. Since this will introduce yet more integrals, it is helpful to define a modified KMOC style ‘classical average angle brackets’
\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle...\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle
, defined by

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle...\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{Q}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}y\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}y_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}y_{2}\,\tilde{\phi}_{b}^{*}(y_{1},y_{2})\tilde{\phi}_{b}(x_{1},x_{2})\;

(141)

\displaystyle\times e^{i(p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot(x_{1}-y_{1})+p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot(x_{2}-y_{2})/\hbar}e^{i(q\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})-Q\cdot(y_{2}-y_{1}))/\hbar}e^{i(q\cdot x+\chi(x_{\perp};\,s)-Q\cdot y-\chi^{*}(y_{\perp};\,s))/\hbar}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\,\alpha^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})\alpha^{(\eta)}{}^{*}(k,y_{1},y_{2})\right](...)\,.

The
a^{\dagger}(k)
will act on this left state will produce a delta function and
\alpha^{*}

\alpha^{*}

\displaystyle\langle\mathbbm{P}_{1}^{\mu}\rangle_{\textrm{reaction}}=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle i\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\,\alpha^{(\eta)*}(k,y_{1},y_{2})\partial_{1}^{\mu}\alpha^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle.

(142)

Note that we have hidden integrals over the
y_{i}
and
x_{i}
in the definition of the brackets. While this expression for the momentum is not manifestly real, we anticipate that the result of the integration is real in the classical limit.

Notice that the manipulations leading to equation (
142

124
)).
However equation (
142
) involves a number of integrals which would need to be performed to arrive at a concrete expression for the impulse. In section
4.2
, we performed these integrals by stationary phase. In the present (radiative) case a similar approach would be possible, but the stationary phase conditions are significantly more complicated. For example, demanding the phase of the
q
integral to be stationary leads to a condition involving the variables
x_{2}
and
x_{1}

integrals should be stationary leads to an equation involving the integral of a quadratic function of the waveshape. In this way the stationary phase conditions involve an intricate interplay of the eikonal and the waveshape. Of course this is as it should be: the complexity of radiation reaction must be captured by the final state.

As a simpler sanity check of our machinery, we evaluate the radiation reaction contribution to the impulse at lowest non-trivial perturbative order in electrodynamics. In
KMOC
the leading order radiation reaction term was written as

I^{\mu}_{rad}=e^{6}\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle\int{\mathrm{d}}\Phi(\bar{k})\prod_{i=1,2}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}_{i}\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}\bar{q}^{\prime}_{i}\,\bar{q}_{1}^{\mu}\,\mathcal{Y}(\bar{q}_{1},\bar{q}_{2},\bar{k})\mathcal{Y}^{*}(\bar{q}^{\prime}_{1},\bar{q}^{\prime}_{2},\bar{k})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle\,,

(143)

with

(144)

It is straightforward to verify that this is equivalent to the right-hand side of equation (
142

133
),
the match between the two expressions (
143
) and (
142

141

Equation (
143

KMOC
).

We note in passing that the expectation
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{F}_{\mu\nu}S|\psi\rangle
(or
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\mathbbm{R}_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}S|\psi\rangle
) can be determined in a similar way.
The annihilation operators in the field strength operator immediately bring down a single power of the waveshape.
At leading non-trivial order in
g
, it is then straightforward to see that the field strength is determined by the five-point
tree amplitude (specifically the leading fragment in
\hbar
) consistent with reference
Cristofoli:2021vyo
.
Similarly, the momentum radiated into messengers can be computed as the expectation

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\,k^{\mu}\,a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(k)a_{\eta}(k)S|\psi\rangle\,.

(145)

In this case, the creation and annihilation operators bring down the waveshape times its conjugate.
At leading perturbative order, the momentum radiated is the square of the five-point tree, as observed in
KMOC
.
The result is also consistent with classical field theory: in that context, radiation is described by the energy-momentum tensor,
which is quadratic in the field strength. Finally, we note that conservation of momentum holds as discussed in

.

5
The radiative final state from Schwinger proper time methods

In (
124

coherent state. In this section, we note that one may provide a
partial justification of this result by considering the leading soft
emission at low energies. Infrared divergences are a good playground to understand the structure of classical scattering: while
Heissenberg:2021tzo
considered the conservative case, here we extend the discussion to real radiation. This can be done using established methods which have been used in relatively recent literature on the
eikonal approximation

\displaystyle|\psi_{\text{in}}\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1},p_{2})\,\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\;\tilde{\phi}(x_{1},x_{2})\,e^{i(p_{1}\cdot x_{1}+p_{2}\cdot x_{2})/\hbar}e^{i(b\cdot p_{1})/\hbar}\left|p_{1}p_{2}\right\rangle,

(146)

\displaystyle|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\;\tilde{\phi}(x_{1},x_{2})\,e^{i(p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot x_{1}+p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot x_{2})/\hbar}

\displaystyle\times\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,e^{iq\cdot x/\hbar}e^{ib\cdot p_{1}^{\prime}/\hbar}e^{-ib\cdot q/\hbar}e^{iq\cdot(x_{2}-x_{1})/\hbar}e^{i\chi(x_{\perp};s)/\hbar}

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[\sum_{\eta}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(k)\beta^{(\eta)}(k,x_{1},x_{2})a^{\dagger}_{\eta}(k)\right]\,|p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\rangle\,,

(147)

where we use

rather than

to refer to the coherent
state parameter, as we will ultimately calculate this only in a

\displaystyle|\psi_{\text{out}}\rangle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x_{2}\;\tilde{\phi}(x_{1},x_{2})\,e^{i(p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot x_{1}+p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot x_{2})/\hbar}

The situation being considered here is similar to the analysis of

Let us first recall a useful result from the Schwinger formalism

\displaystyle\times\left\{\left[\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}),\exp\left(i\chi(x_{\perp})/\hbar\right)\right]^{b_{1},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}+\exp\bigg({i\chi(x_{\perp})/\hbar}\bigg)^{b_{1},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}}\mathcal{O}_{1}(q)_{~a_{1}}^{a_{1}^{\prime}}\right\}\,\xi_{1}^{a_{1}}\xi_{2}^{a_{2}}\,.

Two matrix structures have appeared under the integral: one is a commutator, while the second is a more simple matrix product.
This structure is a generalisation of the structure discussed in
Maybee:2019jus
,
where the second term was called the “direct” term in contrast to the “commutator” term.

We will shortly perform the
q
and
x
integrals in equation (
208
) using stationary phase.
In preparation for doing so, it is convenient to rewrite the equation such that the matrix

\exp({i\chi(x_{\perp})/\hbar})

stands to the left of any other matrices.
Then in the evaluation of the overlap
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}[\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}),S]|\psi\rangle
, the matrix and (on the solution of the
stationary phase conditions) its inverse will simplify.
We only have to move the eikonal operator though the commutator term in equation (
208
); we can do so using
the Baker-Hausdorff lemma in the form
sakurai_napolitano_2017

\displaystyle=e^{i\chi/\hbar}\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\chi,\mathcal{O}]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}[\chi,[\chi,\mathcal{O}]]+\cdots+\frac{(-i)^{n}}{n!\hbar^{n}}[\chi,[\chi,[\chi,...[\chi,\mathcal{O}]]]...]\right)\,,

(209)

where the last term contains
n
nested commutators involving
\chi
.

So far, we have not yet taken advantage of any simplifications available for large, classical spins.
Classical spin representations must be large, in the sense that the spin quantum number
S
times
\hbar
is a classical angular momentum
S\hbar
.
It can be useful to think of this as the limit
S\rightarrow\infty
,
\hbar\rightarrow 0
with
S\hbar
fixed.
Large spin representations have the property that

\displaystyle\langle S_{\mu\nu}S_{\rho\sigma}\rangle=\langle S_{\mu\nu}\rangle\langle S_{\rho\sigma}\rangle+\textrm{small}\,,

(210)

where
S_{\mu\nu}
is a spin Lorentz generator and
\langle S_{\mu\nu}\rangle
is its expectation value on the spin state.
The key point here is that the small correction term is of order
S\hbar^{2}
compared to the explicit term on the right-hand
side, which is of order
S^{2}\hbar^{2}
(see appendix A of
delaCruz:2020bbn
for a recent review). We would therefore like
to take advantage of this kind of simplification in the context of equation (
208
).

In fact, we can easily take advantage of this simplification provided we first use the Baker-Hausdorff lemma (
209
)
to move the eikonal operator to the left.
The reason is that this exposes the infinite series on the right-hand-side of equation (
209
) which is a series
in inverse powers of
\hbar
.
These inverse powers are compensated by powers in commutators of operators; for example in
the case of spin we have

\displaystyle[\mathbb{W}_{\mu},\mathbb{W}_{\nu}]=-i\hbar\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\mathbb{W}^{\rho}\mathbbm{P}^{\sigma}\,.

(211)

Had we not simplified the matrix structure and first tried to replace operators by expectation values we would make an error because
the small correction in equation (
210
), which is intimately related to the commutator (
211
),
would be omitted.

Having taken advantage of the Baker-Hausdorff lemma, then, we may replace all operators by commutators.
In doing so, we must retain the infinite set of non-vanishing commutators.
This is easily done: we simply introduce the Poisson bracket notation defined by

\displaystyle\{\langle\mathbb{W}_{\mu}\rangle,\langle\mathbb{W}_{\nu}\rangle\}=-\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\langle\mathbb{W}^{\rho}\rangle\langle\mathbbm{P}^{\sigma}\rangle\,.

(212)

Notice that the Poisson brackets are directly inherited from the underlying algebraic structure of the quantum field theory. We have scaled out appropriate factors of
i\hbar
.

We therefore pass from equation (
208
) to

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\phi_{b}(p_{1}^{\prime}-q,p_{2}^{\prime}+q)\,e^{iq\cdot x/\hbar}

(213)

\displaystyle\times\left(\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp},s;\langle w\rangle)/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}+\mathcal{O}_{1}(q)e^{i\chi(x_{\perp},s;\langle w\rangle)/\hbar}\right)|p_{1}^{\prime},\xi_{1};p_{2}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\rangle,

where all operators have been replaced with scalar functions, at the expense of introducing Poisson brackets. The notation is

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp},s;\langle w\rangle)/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}\equiv e^{i\chi/\hbar}\left(-\{\mathcal{O},\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{\mathcal{O},\chi\}\}+\cdots\right).

(214)

We emphasise that
\{\cdot,\cdot\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}
is
not
a Poisson bracket: it is simply convenient notation for the commutator
structure in equation (
209
).

In this form, the eikonal has also become a scalar function
\chi(x_{\perp},s;\langle w\rangle,\ldots)
which now depends on the expectation value of operators, eg Pauli-Lubanski
\langle w\rangle
(and/or in the Yang-Mills case, the colour).
We can therefore perform the
x
and
q
integrals by stationary phase, following precisely the methods of section
4.2
.
The stationary phase conditions are

\displaystyle q_{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\chi(x_{\perp})\,.

(215)

Note again that the scalar eikonal depends on variables other than
x_{\perp}
which we have suppressed.
Because of this additional dependence,
q^{\mu}_{*}
need not be proportional to
x_{\perp}^{\mu}
, and will in general point along some other direction in the plane perpendicular to
\tilde{p}_{1}
and
\tilde{p}_{2}
. The final result for the out state is then

\displaystyle|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\,\phi_{b}(p_{1}^{\prime}-q_{*},p_{2}^{\prime}+q_{*})e^{iq_{*}\cdot(x_{*})/\hbar}

(216)

\displaystyle\times\left(\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}^{\prime}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}+\mathcal{O}_{1}(q_{*})e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\right)|p_{1}^{\prime},\xi_{1};p_{2}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\rangle.

We can evaluate
\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}
on the stationary phase too, which results in

\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}=\int d\Phi(p_{1},p_{2})\phi_{b}^{\dagger}(p_{1}-q_{*},p_{2}+q_{*})e^{-iq_{*}\cdot(x_{*})/\hbar}e^{-i\chi^{\dagger}(x_{\perp*})}\langle p_{1},\xi_{1};p_{2},\xi_{2}|,

(217)

such that

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{O}_{1}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|S^{\dagger}[\mathcal{O}_{1},S]|\psi\rangle

(218)

\displaystyle=\int d\Phi(p_{1},p_{2})|\phi(p_{1}-q_{*},p_{2}+q_{*})|^{2}\left(e^{-i\chi^{\dagger}(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}+\mathcal{O}_{1}(q_{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle e^{-i\chi^{\dagger}(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\left\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*})/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}+\mathcal{O}_{1}(q_{*})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle\,.

The Baker-Hausdorff lemma in the form of equation (
214
) immediately tells us how to expand the Poisson bracket, leading to a neat expression for the change in
\mathcal{O}_{1}

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathcal{O}_{1}(q_{*})-\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}),\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}),\chi\}\}-\frac{1}{6}\{\chi,\{\chi,\{\mathcal{O}_{1}(p_{1}),\chi\}\}\}+\cdots.

(219)

where we recall that
\chi
depends on

along with spin, colour etc, and we have dropped the brackets since we are in the fully classical regime.
It is interesting to compare this expression to equation 7.20 in reference

.
Evidently our equation (

) captures a similar exponentiation, though details of the structure are different.
It would be interesting to compare and contrast these two approaches in more detail in future.

As a first check, we consider the momentum operator of particle one, which acts trivially in spin-space meaning we can choose

\mathcal{O}_{1}=\mathbb{P}_{1}^{\mu},~~~~~\rightarrow~~~~~{\mathcal{O}_{1}}^{a}_{~b}(p)=p_{1}^{\mu}\delta^{a}_{~b}.

(220)

Since the identity commutes with everything in spin space, only the
\mathcal{O}(q_{*})
term contributes, giving

\Delta p_{1}^{\mu}=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle q_{*}^{\mu}\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle=-\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle\partial^{\mu}\chi(x_{\perp})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle,

(221)

which matches the expression given in eq. (

Let us emphasise that we expect equation (

We will examine these two cases in more detail below.
It is useful to note that for operators without momentum dependence, the term involving
q_{*}

6.2
Zero variance in spin space

It is possible to prove, similarly to what we have done for the radiation case in section
4.3
, that the generic spin quantum state for the massive particle is necessarily a coherent spin state once we impose the uncertainty principle. Let’s first define a coherent spin state according to the covariant
SU(2)
Schwinger construction, following
Aoude:2021oqj
.
A generic spin vector can be expressed using a set of
2s+1
harmonic oscillators

\displaystyle=\delta^{a}_{b}\qquad\bm{{S}}=\frac{\hbar}{2}a^{\dagger}_{a}\bm{{\sigma}}^{a}_{b}a^{b},

(222)

where
\bm{{\sigma}}^{a}_{b}
are the standard Pauli matrices. Using (
222
), it is easy to see that the spin vector
\bm{{S}}
obeys the expected algebra
[S^{i},S^{j}]=i\hbar\epsilon^{ijk}S^{k}
. Spin coherent states are then defined as eigenstates of
a_{a}
:

\displaystyle|\alpha^{S}\rangle:=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\alpha}_{a}\alpha^{a}}e^{\alpha^{a}a^{\dagger}_{a}}|0\rangle\rightarrow a_{a}|\alpha^{S}\rangle=\alpha_{a}|\alpha^{S}\rangle\,.

(223)

We can now represent the most generic quantum spin state of the initial and the final massive particles in our two-body scattering problem in terms of a density matrix. Such a construction has been developed in the quantum optics literature
giraud2008classicality
, and for our setup it corresponds to dressing the external incoming and outgoing massive momentum states with the following spin density matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{S}_{\text{in}}:=\int\prod_{i=1}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha^{S}_{i}\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})|\alpha_{1}^{S}\rangle|\alpha_{2}^{S}\rangle\langle\alpha_{2}^{S}|\langle\alpha_{1}^{S}|

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{S}_{\text{out}}:=\int\prod_{i=1}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\alpha^{S}_{i}\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})|\alpha_{1}^{S}\rangle|\alpha_{2}^{S}\rangle\langle\alpha_{2}^{S}|\langle\alpha_{1}^{S}|,

where in the classical limit the P-representation is separable
giraud2008classicality

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{1}^{S})\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{2}^{S})

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{1}^{S})\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{2}^{S}).

We now impose the uncertainty principle for the generic expectation value of the spin operator,

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{in}}}(\bm{{S}}_{i}\bm{{S}}_{j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hbar\to 0}}{{=}}\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{in}}}(\bm{{S}}_{i})\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{in}}}(\bm{{S}}_{j})\qquad\text{for}\,\,\,i=1,2

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{out}}}(\bm{{S}}_{i}\bm{{S}}_{j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\hbar\to 0}}{{=}}\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{out}}}(\bm{{S}}_{i})\text{Tr}_{\rho^{S}_{\text{out}}}(\bm{{S}}_{j})\qquad\text{for}\,\,\,i=1,2\,.

Following some simple steps, it is easy to see that this is equivalent to demanding the zero-variance property for both
\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{i}^{S})
and
\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{i}^{S})
(
i=1,2
). Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{in}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})

\displaystyle=\delta(\alpha_{1}^{S}-\alpha_{1,*,\text{in}}^{S})\delta(\alpha_{2}^{S}-\alpha_{2,*,\text{in}}^{S})

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{S}_{\text{out}}(\alpha_{1}^{S},\alpha_{2}^{S})

\displaystyle=\delta(\alpha_{1}^{S}-\alpha_{1,*,\text{out}}^{S})\delta(\alpha_{2}^{S}-\alpha_{2,*,\text{out}}^{S}).

This implies that spin coherent states are suitable to represent the quantum-mechanical state of classical spin particles, and more generally an exponentiation of the spin degrees of freedom is required in the classical limit. This is also consistent with the fact that we should expect to not have any entanglement in the spin sector
Aoude:2020mlg
.

6.3
Classical Colour from the Eikonal

We now turn to explicit examples. Before discussing spin, it is useful to explore the simpler example of classical colour observables. In this case, the eikonal has colour indices and the operator we consider is the colour operator
\mathbb{C}
. The change in the classical colour is then given by eq. (
218
)

\Delta c^{a}_{1}=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle e^{-i\chi^{\dagger}(x_{\perp*},c^{i})/\hbar}\left\{c_{1}^{a},e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*},c^{i})/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle,

(224)

where
c^{a}=\langle\psi|\mathbb{C}|\psi\rangle
, and the
\mathcal{O}(q_{*})
term has dropped out since the colour does not depend on momentum. The first few terms in the Baker-Hausdorff expansion are given by

\Delta c^{a}_{1}=-\{c_{1}^{a},\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{c_{1}^{a},\chi\}\}+\cdots,

(225)

Using the properties of Poisson brackets, namely that
\{A,f(B)\}=\{A,B\}\frac{\partial f}{\partial B}
, along with the relation
\{c^{a},c^{b}\}=f^{abc}c^{c}
, we can write the first bracket as

\{c_{1}^{a},\chi\}=\{c_{1}^{a},c_{1}^{b}\}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial c_{1}^{b}}=f^{abc}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial c_{1}^{b}}c_{1}^{c}.

(226)

At order
\mathcal{O}(g^{4})
, we expect contributions from the tree-level and one-loop in the first bracket, as well as the tree-level terms in the second bracket. The tree-level and one-loop eikonals are given by
delaCruz:2020bbn
;
delaCruz:2021gjp

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(x_{\perp},c^{i})

\displaystyle=g^{2}(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})\frac{\gamma}{4\pi\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}\log\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|,

(227)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(x_{\perp},c^{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{4}(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})^{2}(m_{1}+m_{2})}{32\pi m_{1}m_{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}|x_{\perp}|},

where
L
is an infrared cutoff. We can plug the tree-level eikonal into the bracket to find, at first order,

\displaystyle\Delta c^{a,0}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\{c_{1}^{a},\chi_{1}\}=\frac{\gamma g^{2}f^{abc}c_{1}^{b}c_{2}^{c}}{4\pi\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}\log\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|.

(228)

The second bracket is given by

\displaystyle\Delta c^{a,1}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{c_{1}^{a},\chi\}\}

(229)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{4}\gamma^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}(\gamma^{2}-1)}\log^{2}\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|f^{abc}\left[\{\chi,c_{2}^{b}\}c_{1}^{c}+c_{2}^{b}\{\chi,c_{1}^{c}\}\right]

(230)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{4}\gamma^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}(\gamma^{2}-1)}\log^{2}\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|\left[f^{abc}f^{bde}c_{1}^{c}c_{1}^{d}c_{2}^{e}-f^{abc}f^{bde}c_{2}^{c}c_{2}^{d}c_{1}^{e}\right].

(231)

The contribution from the one-loop eikonal is evaluated in the first bracket to give

\displaystyle\{c_{1}^{a},\chi_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{4}(m_{1}+m_{2})}{32\pi m_{1}m_{2}\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}|x_{\perp}|}\left\{c_{1}^{a},(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})^{2}\right\}

(232)

\displaystyle=-\frac{g^{4}(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})f^{abc}c_{1}^{b}c_{2}^{c}}{16\pi\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}|x_{\perp}|}\left(\frac{1}{m_{1}}+\frac{1}{m_{2}}\right).

Putting this together gives the full
\mathcal{O}(g^{4})

\displaystyle\Delta c_{1}^{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma g^{2}f^{abc}c_{1}^{b}c_{2}^{c}}{4\pi\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}\log\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|+\frac{g^{4}(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})f^{abc}c_{1}^{b}c_{2}^{c}}{16\pi\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}|x_{\perp}|}\left(\frac{1}{m_{1}}+\frac{1}{m_{2}}\right)

(233)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\frac{g^{4}\gamma^{2}}{(4\pi)^{2}(\gamma^{2}-1)}\log^{2}\left|\frac{x_{\perp}^{2}}{L^{2}}\right|\left[f^{abc}f^{bde}c_{1}^{c}c_{1}^{d}c_{2}^{e}-f^{abc}f^{bde}c_{2}^{c}c_{2}^{d}c_{1}^{e}\right].

This matches the NLO colour impulse in the literature

6.4
Classical Spin from the Eikonal

The angular impulse for particle one is given by

\displaystyle\Delta s_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\langle\Psi|S^{\dagger}[\mathbb{W}_{1}^{\mu},S]|\Psi\rangle

(234)

\displaystyle=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle e^{-i\chi^{\dagger}(x_{\perp*},s_{i})/\hbar}\left\{s_{1}^{\mu}(p_{1}),e^{i\chi(x_{\perp*},s_{i})/\hbar}\right\}_{\textrm{B.H.}}+s_{1}^{\mu}(q_{*})\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle,

(235)

where the expection of the Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector is

\langle p,a_{i}|\mathbb{W}^{\mu}|p^{\prime},a_{j}\rangle\equiv ms_{ij}^{\mu}(p)\hat{\delta}(p-p^{\prime}).

(236)

The spin is more complicated to consider than colour, since the Pauli-Lubanski operator is a product of both the linear and angular momentum operators. This means that the

\Delta s_{1}^{\mu}=s_{1}^{\mu}(q_{*})-\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\}\}+\cdots

The first term is the spin vector of particle one evaluated at

(238)

where the spin tensor is defined as

. We can input this definition to find that the leading order contribution can be expressed

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\mu}(q_{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m_{1}^{2}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}q_{*\nu}\epsilon_{\rho\sigma\lambda\tau}p_{1}^{\lambda}s_{1}^{\tau}(p_{1})=\frac{1}{m_{1}^{2}}\left((q_{*}\cdot p_{1})s_{1}^{\mu}(p_{1})-(s_{1}\cdot q_{*})p_{1}^{\mu}\right)

\displaystyle\Delta s_{1}^{\mu}

\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{1\alpha}}=\frac{4Gm_{2}\gamma}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-1}}\frac{\epsilon^{\alpha\mu\nu\rho}u_{1\mu}x_{\perp\nu}u_{2\rho}}{|x_{\perp}|^{2}},

(242)

(243)

which matches the existing literature

7
Discussion

The central theme of this paper has been that the classical limit emerges from scattering amplitudes via an infinite set of relationships satisfied by
multiloop, multileg amplitudes in a transfer expansion. These relationships arise from requiring negligible uncertainty in the measurement of
observables computed from amplitudes in the correspondence limit where the classical approximation is valid. One can write scattering amplitudes
in an exponential form as a result of these relationships, as has long been recognised in the conservative sector through the eikonal approximation.
We have argued that the same holds for radiative physics.

While we focused on the eikonal approach to classical dynamics, it is worth emphasising that we could have phrased our discussion in terms of other,
closely related, quantities. Recently the radial action has received particular attention in the literature
Bern:2021dqo
; this radial action is a
classical limit of the usual quantum phase shifts
Kol:2021jjc
, and is a close cousin of the eikonal function
Bautista:2021wfy
.

The eikonal and the radial action arise directly in other approaches to gravitational dynamics. While this paper started with quantum field theory,
undergraduate classes start more simply with worldline actions — and indeed pragmatic approaches to gravitational phenomena start with
analogous worldline theories
Goldberger:2004jt
;
Mogull:2020sak
;
Mougiakakos:2021ckm
;
Jakobsen:2021smu
;
Jakobsen:2021lvp
;
Jakobsen:2021zvh
. Nevertheless there is one inescapable fact about the dynamics that quantum field theory makes blatantly
clear: radiation is
not
suppressed in fully relativistic physics. Thus in our approach radiation is encoded in
\alpha
, fully as important
as
\chi
.
Similarly the physics of radiation reaction is
clear from the outset and is a consequence of a basic principle: conservation of momentum.

One of our central observations was that six-point tree amplitudes are suppressed in the classical region. We showed explicitly that this suppression hold in scalar QED, but our more general arguments indicate that the suppression should hold in general relativity
Britto:2021pud
and in perturbative/classical applications of Yang-Mills theory.
This suppression of the six-point tree amplitude is consistent with classical intuition. Classical electrodynamics, for example, involves two main ingredients: knowledge of the electromagnetic field, and knowledge of the particle motion. As our work has focussed on determining the asymptotic properties of particles and waves, the relevant part of the classical field is the radiation field: this is the part which determines the energy and momentum in the field, for example. Similar remarks hold in gravity.
In a quantum approach, asymptotic particle motion is captured by the eikonal function (or the radial action.) The radiation field is described at the leading quantum level by the five-point amplitude
Luna:2017dtq
;
KMOC
;
Cristofoli:2021vyo
. Our work generalises this last statement: we have argued that the all-order radiation field is described by the waveshape parameter
\alpha
, itself determined by the all-order five-point amplitude together with the eikonal function.
As the four- and five-point amplitudes are enough to describe the classical dynamics, it makes sense that higher-point amplitudes are classically suppressed.
Incidentally, our work does not mean that six (or higher) point amplitudes can never be of use in classical computations: they may appear for example inside cuts as building-blocks of other observables.

From a classical perspective, the waveshape
\alpha
is essentially
Cristofoli:2021vyo
a Newman-Penrose scalar: either
\phi_{2}
in gauge theory or
\psi_{4}
in gravity. Our contention is then that a complete description of the classical radiative dynamics is given by knowledge of one of these scalars, and knowledge of the eikonal function
\chi

full

\chi
, not just the scattering angle. Indeed in a radiative context, knowledge of the angle needs to be supplemented by knowledge of the outgoing energy to fully determine the outgoing particle trajectory.

Spin angular momentum is also a key aspect of classical dynamics, in particular in the context of black hole dynamics. In section
6
we built on our eikonal description of classical motion to make contact with spin (and colour) at the conservative level. This is particularly natural for us since it is well-known by now that classical spinning particles have a natural interpretation as coherent states of large occupation number in spin space. In Yang-Mills theory, the Wong dynamics of colour arises in a very analogous manner

through coherent states in colour space

.

We emphasise that we made no attempt to prove that the final semi-classical state really is of the precise form given in equation (

). We provided arguments in support of the exponentiation based on path-integral techniques, and indeed at leading non-trivial order it is clear that the radiation is described by a coherent state. Beyond this order much less is known. It certainly seems possible
that the waveshape
\alpha
could depend, in general, on more variables. The structure of the final state we suggested does allow for radiation reaction,
so that the

and
\alpha
are not independent of one another. However it could certainly be that tail effects induce a closer link between the two
functions. Since progress in this field is currently very rapid we can look forward to exploring the exponentiation of radiation in far greater detail before long.

Appendix B
Projection in the plane of scattering

The relation between the eikonal impact parameter
x^{\mu}_{\perp}
and
b^{\mu}
can be stated clearly by first introducing some notation. Let us define the following four-vectors in momentum space

\displaystyle\begin{cases}e_{0}^{\mu}\equiv N_{0}(\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{1}+\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{2})\\
e_{q}^{\mu}\equiv N_{q}(\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{1}-\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{2})-N_{0q}(\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{1}+\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{2})\ ,\end{cases}

(249)

where the normalization factors
N_{0}
,
N_{q}
and
N_{0q}
are fixed by requiring
e^{2}_{0}=1
,
e^{2}_{q}=-1
and
e_{0}\cdot e_{q}=0
. By definition of
x^{\mu}_{\perp}
, the following identities hold:

\displaystyle e_{0}\cdot x_{\perp}=0\quad,\quad e_{q}\cdot x_{\perp}=0\ .

(250)

As a consequence, we can write the projection of
x^{\mu}
on the plane orthogonal to
\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{1}
and
\tilde{p}^{\mu}_{2}
as

\displaystyle x^{\mu}_{\perp}=x^{\mu}-(x\cdot e_{0})e_{0}^{\mu}+(x\cdot e_{q})e^{\mu}_{q}\ ,

(251)

where the different signs in the the last two terms are a consequence of
e_{0}^{\mu}
being time-like while
e_{q}^{\mu}
space-like. Using (
251
) we can easily compute some of the derivatives involved in the evaluation of the stationary phase on
x^{\mu}
such as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial x_{\perp}^{2}}{\partial x_{\mu}}=2x_{\perp,\nu}(\eta^{\mu\nu}-e_{0}^{\mu}e_{0}^{\nu}+e_{q}^{\mu}e_{q}^{\nu})=2x^{\mu}_{\perp}\ .

(252)

Another example where the use of (
251
) is useful is when we apply the stationary phase for the integral over
q^{\mu}
. In this case, the stationary condition for
q^{\mu}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=b^{\mu}+\left.q_{\nu,*}\frac{\partial x_{\perp}^{\nu}}{\partial q_{\mu}}\right|_{q=q_{*}}\ ,

(253)

where
q_{*}^{\mu}
satisfies the stationary phase condition on
x^{\mu}
given by
q^{\mu}_{*}=-2\chi^{\prime}(x_{\perp})x^{\mu}_{\perp}
. One of the advantages in the definition (
249
) of
e_{0}^{\mu}
is that it is
q^{\mu}
independent so that the previous stationary condition can be expressed as

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=b^{\mu}+\left.q_{\nu,*}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{\mu}}\bigg[(x\cdot e_{q})e_{q}^{\nu}\bigg]\right|_{q=q_{*}}\,.

(254)

Since
q_{*}^{\mu}
is parallel to
x^{\mu}_{\perp}
, we know that
q_{*}\cdot e_{q}\propto x_{\perp}\cdot e_{q}=0
, and so equation (
254
) simplifies to

\displaystyle x^{\mu}=b^{\mu}+\left.q_{\nu,*}(x\cdot e_{q})\frac{\partial e_{q}^{\nu}}{\partial q_{\mu}}\right|_{q=q_{*}}\,.

(255)

The remaining derivative can be easily performed using the definition of
e_{q}^{\mu}
. The result is

\displaystyle\left.\left.q_{\nu}\frac{\partial e_{q}^{\nu}}{\partial q_{\mu}}\right|_{q=q_{*}}=-(N_{q}\>q^{\mu})\right|_{q=q_{*}}\,.

(256)

We can then write the eikonal impact parameter as

\displaystyle x^{\mu}_{\perp}=b^{\mu}-(x\cdot e_{0})e_{0}^{\mu}-\left.\big[(x\cdot e_{q})N_{q}q^{\mu}\big]\right|_{q=q_{*}}+\left.\big[(x\cdot e_{q})e_{q}^{\mu}\big]\right|_{q=q_{*}}\ .

(257)

The scalar products
x\cdot e_{0}
and
x\cdot e_{q}
, which can be viewed as Lagrange multipliers for the phase which we are minimizing, are fixed by requiring the eikonal impact parameter to be orthogonal to
e_{0}^{\mu}
and
e_{q}^{\mu}
. A straightforward calculation gives

\displaystyle x^{\mu}_{\perp}=b^{\mu}-\left.\big[(e_{q}\cdot b)(N_{q}q^{\mu}-e^{\mu}_{q})\big]\right|_{q=q_{*}}\ .

(258)

Expressing
e^{\mu}_{q}
in terms of
p^{\mu}_{1}
,
p^{\mu}_{2}
and
q^{\mu}
we obtain

\displaystyle x^{\mu}_{\perp}=b^{\mu}-\left.\bigg[(e_{q}\cdot b)\big[N_{0q}(p^{\mu}_{1}+p^{\mu}_{2})-N_{q}(p^{\mu}_{1}-p^{\mu}_{2})\big]\bigg]\right|_{q=q_{*}}\,,

(259)

which agrees — when evaluated in the center of mass frame — with the expression for the eikonal impact parameter in (
103
), where
\tilde{N}_{q}=-(e_{q}\cdot b)N_{q}
and
\tilde{N}_{0q}=(e_{q}\cdot b)N_{0q}
.

Appendix C
Unitarity of the conservative final state

After stationary phase in
q^{\mu}
and
x^{\mu}
, the final state in the conservative sector (that is, no radiation and hence no radiation reaction) is

\displaystyle S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\left|p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right\rangle e^{iq_{*}(s)\cdot x_{*}(s)/\hbar}e^{i\chi\left(x_{*,\perp}(s);s\right)/\hbar}\phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-q_{*}(s),p_{2}^{\prime}+q_{*}(s)\right)\ ,

(260)

where
q_{*}(s)
and
x_{*}(s)
are solutions of the stationary phase condition depending on the Mandelstam variable
s=(p_{1}^{\prime}+p_{2}^{\prime})^{2}
. To check unitarity of the final state we proceed by computing

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})\,|\phi\left(p^{\prime}_{1}-q_{*}(s),p^{\prime}_{2}+q_{*}(s)\right)|^{2}\,,

(261)

taking advantage of the absolute value to simplify the wavepackets using equation (
22
).
It is useful to think of
q_{*}=\Delta p
as the total impulse on each particle, recognising
p^{\prime}_{1}-\Delta p
and
p^{\prime}_{2}+\Delta p
as the
initial
momenta of the particles:

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})\,|\phi\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)|^{2}\,,

(262)

In the CM frame, these initial momenta are rotations of the final momenta through the scattering angle
\Psi
so that we may also write

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})\,|\phi\left(R_{1}(\Psi)p^{\prime}_{1},R_{2}(\Psi)p^{\prime}_{2}\right)|^{2}\,,

(263)

where
R_{i}(\Psi)
perform the appropriate rotation from initial to final momenta for each particle.
In a general frame, these operators are Lorentz transformations.

The basic idea now is to change variables from the final momenta
p_{i}^{\prime}
to the initial momenta
R_{i}(\Psi)p_{i}^{\prime}
, exploiting Lorentz invariance of the phase-space integration measure, aiming to show that

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}\Phi(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2})\,|\phi\left(p^{\prime}_{1},p^{\prime}_{2}\right)|^{2}=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\,.

(264)

However, the rotations depend on the momenta of each particle, and hence the change of variables is not immediate.

To examine this issue, consider a helpful choice of wavepacket defined by

\displaystyle\phi(p_{1},p_{2})=\sqrt{2U\cdot p_{1}\,2U\cdot p_{2}}\,\phi_{\textrm{CM}}((p_{1}+p_{2})/2)\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(p_{1})\,,

(265)

with
U
being the time-like unit vector defining the CM frame.
The factor
\phi_{\textrm{CM}}((p_{1}+p_{2})/2)
in this wavefunction controls the CM motion, so it could be chosen to be a product of four Gaussians (of appropriately narrow width) which define the CM energy and constrain the total spatial momenta in the CM frame to be zero (to a sufficiently good approximation).
The factor
\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(p_{1})
controls the relative motion in the CM frame.
It is helpful to think of this factor as defining the direction of the spatial momentum
\bm{{n}}\equiv\bm{{p}}_{1}/|\bm{{p}}_{1}|
in the CM frame, in particular choosing
\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(p_{1})=\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(\bm{{n}})
.
Then the wavepacket for the initial momenta can be written in terms of final momenta as

\displaystyle\phi(p_{1},p_{2})=\sqrt{2U\cdot p^{\prime}_{1}\,2U\cdot p^{\prime}_{2}}\,\phi_{\textrm{CM}}((p^{\prime}_{1}+p^{\prime}_{2})/2)\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(R_{1}(\Psi)\bm{{n}}^{\prime})\,,

(266)

using the fact that
U\cdot\Delta p=0
.
Here
\bm{{n}}^{\prime}\equiv\bm{{p}}^{\prime}_{1}/|\bm{{p}}_{1}|
in the unit vector in the direction of the final momentum of particle 1 in the CM frame.

Rewriting the integral as an integral over the CM momentum
P^{\prime}=(p^{\prime}_{1}+p^{\prime}_{2})/2
and the relative momentum
p^{\prime}_{\mathrm{rel}}=p^{\prime}_{1}-p^{\prime}_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}P^{\prime}\,|\phi_{\textrm{CM}}(P^{\prime})|^{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p^{\prime}_{\textrm{rel}}\,|\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(R_{1}(\Psi)\bm{{n}}^{\prime})|^{2}\,.

(267)

Now the key point is whether we can change variable in the relative motion to remove the rotation. The answer is yes: although the rotation depends on the magnitude of the relative momentum, it does not depend on the angle of the relative momentum.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\langle\psi|S^{{\dagger}}S|\psi\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}P^{\prime}\,|\phi_{\textrm{CM}}(P^{\prime})|^{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}p^{\prime}_{\textrm{rel}}\,|\phi_{\textrm{rel}}(\bm{{n}}^{\prime})|^{2}=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1\,,

(268)

as desired.

Appendix D
Review of Schwinger proper time method

In this appendix, we present a short review of the Schwinger proper
time method, which allows propagators in quantum
field theory can be rewritten in terms of path integrals in ordinary
quantum mechanics. We set
\hbar=1
only for this appendix, but we have restored for the application of this formalism in section
5
. Consider first the Feynman propagator for a free scalar field of mass
m
:

\displaystyle D_{F}(x-y)=\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{i}{k^{2}-m^{2}}e^{ik\cdot(x-y)}

(269)

which satisfies the position space equation

\displaystyle i(\hat{H}-i\varepsilon)D_{F}(x-y)=\delta^{(d)}(x-y),\quad\hat{H}=\square+m^{2}

(270)

where we have introduced the Klein-Gordon operator
\hat{H}
, which appears in the quadratic terms in the scalar field Lagrangian. Equation (
270
) corresponds to the well-known fact that the propagator is associated with the inverse of the operator
\hat{H}
, and we may formally write

\displaystyle-i(\hat{H}-i\varepsilon)^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dTe^{-iT(\hat{H}-i\varepsilon)},

(271)

where the
i\varepsilon
prescription guarantees convergence of the integral, and
T
is conventionally called a Schwinger parameter. The integral in (
271
) contains the operator

\displaystyle\hat{U}(T)=e^{-i\hat{H}T},

(272)

and if we interpret
T
as a time variable. this has the known form of the evolution operator in quantum mechanics where
\hat{H}
is the Hamiltonian, given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\hat{p}^{2}+m^{2}.

(273)

We can introduce a Hilbert space of states on which this Hamiltonian acts. Complete sets are provided by the position or momentum states
\{|x\rangle\}
or
\{|p\rangle\}
(eigenstates of
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
respectively). Consider the evolution operator sandwiched between a state of given initial position and final momentum. For a small time separation
\delta T
, one finds

\displaystyle\langle p|e^{-i\hat{H}\delta T}|x\rangle=e^{-iH\delta T+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta T^{2}\right)}\langle p|x\rangle

(274)

where
H
denotes the replacement of the position and momentum operators in
\hat{H}
with their corresponding eigenvalues. Note that, strictly speaking, we must ensure that the Hamiltonian is first
written in Weyl-ordered form, such that all momentum operators appear to the left of all position ones. This is not an issue for the Hamiltonian of eq.(
273
), but will be relevant when a gauge field is included. The above simple result applies only for small time separations. For a large time separation
T
, one may divide the time interval into
N
steps

\displaystyle\delta T=\frac{T}{N}

(275)

where the limit
N\rightarrow\infty
will eventually be taken. Then we may introduce a complete set of intermediate position and momentum states at each time-step, so that the matrix element of the evolution operator between a final momentum state
\left|p_{f}\right\rangle
and initial position state
\left|x_{i}\right\rangle
becomes

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle p_{f}|e^{-i\hat{H}T}|x_{i}\rangle&=\int dx_{1}\ldots dx_{N}\int dp_{0}\ldots dp_{N-1}\langle p_{f}|e^{-i\hat{H}\delta t}|x_{N}\rangle\\
&\times\langle x_{N}|p_{N-1}\rangle\langle p_{N-1}|e^{-i\hat{H}\delta t}|x_{N-1}\rangle\langle x_{N-1}|p_{N-2}\rangle\langle p_{N-2}|e^{-i\hat{H}\delta t}|x_{N-2}\rangle\ldots\\
&\times\langle x_{1}|p_{0}\rangle\langle p_{0}|e^{-i\hat{H}\delta t}|x_{i}\rangle.\end{aligned}

(276)

Introducing the notation
x_{0}\equiv x_{i},p_{N}\equiv p_{f}
for the fixed boundary conditions, we can write the previous equation more succinctly as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle p_{f}|e^{-i\hat{H}T}|x_{i}\rangle&=\int dx_{1}\ldots\int dx_{N}\int dp_{0}\ldots dp_{N-1}\exp\left[-i\sum_{k=0}^{N}H\left(p_{k},x_{k}\right)\delta T\right]\\
&\times\prod_{k=0}^{N}\langle p_{k}|x_{k}\rangle\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\langle x_{k+1}|p_{k}\rangle\\
&=\int dx_{1}\ldots\int dx_{N}\int dp_{0}\ldots dp_{N-1}\exp\left[-i\sum_{k=0}^{N}H\left(p_{k},x_{k}\right)\delta T\right]\\
&\times\left[\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\exp\left(i\frac{p_{k}\cdot\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)}{\delta T}\delta T\right)\right]e^{-ip_{f}\cdot x_{N}},\end{aligned}
