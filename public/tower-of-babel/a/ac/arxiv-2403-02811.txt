Linear quadratic control of nonlinear systems with Koopman operator learning and the Nyström method
Edoardo Caldarelli, Antoine Chatalic, Adrià Colomé, Cesare Molinari, Carlos Ocampo-Martinez, Carme Torras, Lorenzo Rosasco

Abstract
In this paper, we study how the Koopman operator framework can be combined with kernel methods to effectively control nonlinear dynamical systems. While kernel methods have typically large computational requirements, we show how random subspaces (Nyström approximation) can be used to achieve huge computational savings while preserving accuracy. Our main technical contribution is deriving theoretical guarantees on the effect of the Nyström approximation. More precisely, we study the linear quadratic regulator problem, showing that the approximated Riccati operator converges at the rate m − 1 / 2 m^{-1/2} , and the regulator objective, for the associated solution of the optimal control problem, converges at the rate m − 1 m^{-1} , where m m is the random subspace size. Theoretical findings are complemented by numerical experiments corroborating our results.

Introduction

Nonlinear dynamical systems are ubiquitous, and pose a great challenge in terms of system identification and control.
A powerful approach to deal with nonlinear dynamical systems is provided by the
Koopman operator
framework
[
30
,
9
,
37
,
10

In this approach, the nonlinear dynamical system is transformed through a set of nonlinear functions, called
observables
, so that the dynamics of the transformed states are
linear

and can be used to reconstruct the states of the system.
The Koopman operator, at the basis of this technique, was originally introduced in the context of autonomous dynamical systems
[
30
]

[
31

, the Koopman operator formulation allows to apply linear control techniques, which are well understood and efficient to compute.
As discussed by

[

A key challenge to apply the Koopman operator approach is to choose the suitable space of observable
functions, both with and without considering the control input
[
31
]
.
Common choices include splines
[
31
]
, polynomial or Fourier bases
[
1
]
, and neural networks
[
51
,
56
,
23
]
, see also
[
32
,
24
,
19
]
.

In this paper, we consider observables in a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space

[
4
]

[

]
. This choice was already mentioned in
[

and has been recently analyzed in

,

for the forecasting and analysis of dynamical systems.
Kernel methods are popular in machine learning
[

]
, as an RKHS has the advantage of being a possibly infinite dimensional space and corresponds to universal approximators
[

and their computation reduces to finite-dimensional numerical problems

]

When efficiency is needed, further approximations are however required.
In this paper, we use the so-called Nyström method,

which can be interpreted as a dimensionality reduction technique. More precisely, we approximate the dynamics of the observable functions in the RKHS using projections on random and data-dependent finite-dimensional subspaces
of functions
[
55
,
43
]

The effect of this approximation has been characterized for supervised machine learning, see, e.g.,
[
48
,
40

[

36

Besides the Nyström method, as an alternative kernel approximation, Nüske et al.
[
41

study the case of random Fourier features for dynamical system identification.

In this paper, we combine the above ideas and develop an efficient and accurate control approach for nonlinear dynamical systems, based on using the Koopman operator framework together with kernels and the Nyström method. Our main technical contribution is the analysis of the approximation due to the Nyström method but, unlike previous works, we consider a control setting.
We focus on the linear quadratic regulator (LQR) method, which has appealing theoretical properties, such as an analytic form of the optimal solution, and easily allows to deal with multi-input-multi-output systems.
Our main
result studies the impact of the Nyström approximation on the optimal control problem. Namely, in a fixed design analysis, we
quantify the error introduced by the Nyström approximation to an empirical, kernel-based estimator of the nonlinear dynamics. We further show how this error propagates to the solution of the optimal control problem, if such an empirical estimator is employed in an LQR.
Instrumental to our analysis are recent results on learning and controlling
linear
systems
[

,
35
,
13
]
.
We complement our theoretical analysis with some numerical experiments.
The combination of the Koopman approach and the LQR

has been applied to challenging robot learning problems
[

]
, also involving soft robots
[

. Here, we assess the proposed control pipeline on a classic control benchmark, i.e., the Duffing oscillator

To conclude, we observe that concurrent to our work, Driessen

2
Background and notation

In this paper, we consider
a nonlinear controlled discrete dynamical system,
which is approximated for control purposes by a surrogate dynamical system whose dynamics are linear both in the lifted state and in the input variable. This type of heuristic approximation, previously considered for instance by Korda et al.
[
31
]
, has been empirically shown to be suited to nonlinear systems that are
affine
in the control input, and have no coupled terms between state and input variables. While this may be seen as a restriction of the class of dynamical systems that can be modeled with our approximation, we can observe that it is already a fairly general model, see, e.g., the dynamical systems we study in Section 6, or the soft robot studied in
[
22
]
.

We denote the state vector as
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the output as
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
, and the control input vector as
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
.
For a sequence of control inputs
(\uvec_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, an initial state
\xvec_{0}
, and a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{d+n_{u}}\to\R^{d}
, we are interested in studying
deterministic nonlinear dynamical systems described by the difference equation

\mathbf{x}_{t+1}=f(\mathbf{x}_{t},\mathbf{u}_{t}),

(1)

where
t\in\mathbb{N}
denotes the discrete-time instant.
As mentioned before, the nonlinear map is considered to be affine in the control variable, and without coupled terms, i.e., for
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{d}\to\R^{d}
,
\mathfrak{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{n_{u}}\to\R^{d}
,

f{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\xvec,\uvec)}=g(\xvec)+\mathfrak{B}\uvec.

The state and the input can be merged in an augmented
finite-dimensional
state, denoted by
\wvec=[\mathbf{x}^{T},\mathbf{u}^{T}]^{T}\in\R^{d+n_{u}}
. We will use both notations interchangeably throughout the article.
For a suitable space
\mathcal{A}
of observable functions
\xi
, the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
can be defined as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{K}\xi)(\wvec_{t})

\displaystyle=\xi(\wvec_{t+1}),\quad\forall\xi∊\mathcal{A}.

(2)

The definition in (
2
) follows the Koopman with inputs and control (KIC) formalism
[
47
]
.
Note that when the control is prescribed by a state-feedback gain, this definition is related to the standard definition of the Koopman operator for the associated autonomous dynamics.
An alternative definition of the Koopman operator involving sequences of controls propagated using the shift operator has also been proposed
[
31
]
.
However, all definitions of the Koopman operator with control suffer from a common drawback, which is that they implicitly assume that a space of observable
\mathcal{A}
invariant under the action of the Koopman operator indeed exists.

As we will discuss, a fundamental component of our learning framework is the notion of RKHS
[
4
]
. For an input space
\Xcal
, an RKHS
H
is a Hilbert space of scalar functions on
\mathcal{X}
for which there exists a
k\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Xcal\times\Xcal\rightarrow\R
, the
reproducing kernel
, so that, for
any
\chi\in\Xcal
and
f\in H
, it holds

k(\chi,\cdot)\in H
and
f(\chi)=\innerprod{f,k(\chi,\cdot)}_{H}
. The latter notation denotes the inner product in the RKHS. An RKHS is a potentially infinite-dimensional space with universality properties.
Some examples of reproducing kernels are given by the Gaussian kernel, or by the Matérn family of kernels, which can be linked to Sobolev spaces.
Note that the Koopman operator is linear in the observable
ξ
. It can thus be seen as a convenient way to build a linear approximation of the original dynamical system,
at the cost of manipulating the lifted state
ξ(\mathbf{w})
.

In this paper, we take inspiration from the Koopman formalism in order to devise a data-driven approximation of the dynamics in the RKHS suitable to use with an LQR.
We focus on the error induced by the use of the Nyström approximation, however we do not relate the learned dynamics in the RKHS to the definition of the Koopman operator with control in (
2
).

. Besides,

.

3
Koopman system identification

As discussed previously, we focus on approximations of the original dynamical system which are linear
in the control input.
In the following, we will show how to model such a linearity in the control input. We will also detail the regression problems that are solved in order to retrieve the data-driven, kernel-based dynamics, and show how such kernel-based dynamics can be transformed to vector-valued dynamics by leveraging the Nyström method.

3.1
Choosing the Koopman lifting function

In order to define a suitable state and control transformation, we consider the RKHS
\mathcal{H}₁
associated to a stationary positive definite kernel

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{1}\times\R^{n_{u}}

\displaystyle\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{d+n_{u}}\rightarrow\mathcal{H},\ \phi(\wvec)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\vvec{\psi(\xvec){\\
}\uvec}}.

(3)

The choice of the stationary kernel allows to lift the data through an infinite-dimensional nonlinear transformation. Moreover,

is linear in the control input variable, which allows to use linear control techniques on the system of interest. However,

We will show in Section

4
Kernels and Koopman LQR

Once we have estimated the state-space representation of the dynamical system of interest, we can use linear predictive control techniques, as the Koopman approach transforms a non-linear system in a linear one
[
31
]
.
In the following, we focus on the LQR,
and more particularly on the infinite horizon LQR
[
35
]

due to its theoretically appealing properties.
The key idea is that the optimization problem at the basis of the LQR is rendered tractable by using a finite dimensional embedding of the state and inputs, which can be achieved by sketching techniques, as discussed in Section
3
.

LQR for exact dynamics

We first consider using the exact kernel
\kr
and the transformation
ϕ
.
We consider a control objective that is quadratic in the lifted state and control inputs, via the weighting operators
Q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
R\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{n_{u}}\rightarrow\R^{n_{u}}
.
Then, the LQR strategy requires to solve the following optimal control problem, for which a time-invariant analytical solution is available:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\uvec_{1},\dots}\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{T}\langle z_{i},Qz_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},R\mathbf{u}_{i}\rangle_{\R^{n_{u}}}

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\ }

\displaystyle\eqref{e:initial_state},\eqref{eq:dynamics_compound}.

LQR for approximated dynamics

When the dynamics are approximated by using the Nyström approach as in (
18
), the optimal control problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\uvec_{1},\dots}\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{T}

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{z}_{i},Q\tilde{z}_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}+\langle\mathbf{u}_{i},R\mathbf{u}_{i}\rangle_{\R^{n_{u}}}

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\ }

\displaystyle\eqref{e:n_initial_state},\eqref{eq:nystrom_dynamics_compound}.

According to (
22
), the problem in (
25
) can equivalently be rewritten in the basis
\Bout
for weighting matrices
\tilde{Q}\in\R^{m\times m}
,

\tilde{Q}=(\Kmout^{\dagger})^{1/2}\Stildeout Q\Stildeout^{*}(\Kmout^{\dagger})^{1/2},

(26)

and
R
, as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{u}_{0},\uvec_{1},\dots}\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=0}^{T}

\displaystyle\langle\tildezvec_{i},\tilde{Q}\tildezvec_{i}\rangle_{\mathcal{\R}^{m}}+\ip{\mathbf{u}_{i},R\mathbf{u}_{i}}_{\R^{n_{u}}}

(27)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\ }

\displaystyle\eqref{e_n_vec_initial_state},\eqref{eq:nystrom_dynamics_vector_partial}.

Based on (
26
), problems (
25
) and (
27
) are equivalent, and yield the same optimal control sequence in form of state feedback
[
21
]
. Practically, one can choose
\tilde{Q}=C^{*}Q^{\prime}C
for some
Q^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{d}\to\R^{d}
, and
C
is the reconstruction matrix defined in (
23
) in order to define an objective that can be interpreted as a penalization of the states. Denoting the optimal gain resulting from (
25
) as
\tilde{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{H}_{1}\rightarrow\R^{n_{u}}
, the optimal control law is

\uvec_{k}=\tilde{K}\tilde{z}_{k}.

From (
22
), we have that the state-feedback input can be rewritten as

\uvec_{k}=\tilde{K}\Bout\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_{k},

with
\tilde{K}\Bout\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\R^{m}\rightarrow\R^{n_{u}}
being the solution of the LQR problem in (
27
). When dealing with the true nonlinear dynamics in Section
6
, we consider a state-feedback control law of the form

\uvec_{k}=\tilde{K}\Pi_{\textrm{out},\mathbf{x}}\psi(\xvec_{k}),

(28)

where
\xvec_{k}
is the true state of the system, to perform control in closed loop.

Overall pipeline

The content of this section can be summarized in a whole pipeline that can be used for linear identification and control of nonlinear systems. Such a pipeline consists of the following steps:

1.

sample Nyström input and output landmarks
\ldmi{1},…,\ldmi{m}
and
\ldmo{1},…,\ldmo{m}
uniformly from the training set;

2.

compute the operators in (
21
) to obtain a data-driven linear system;

3.

use these linear dynamics to solve problem (
27
) and get the optimal state-feedback gain.

5
Theoretical analysis

In this section, we perform a theoretical analysis of the proposed system identification method, and assess its effect on the LQR problem. In particular, we will compare the Nyström-based approach with the data-driven model based on the exact kernel. Overall, we show that the Nyström-based model of (
18
) is a provably accurate approximation of the dynamics in (
12
), that can be safely used for control purposes, in place of the intractable infinite-dimensional model of (
12
).

Layout

In Section
5.1
, we state the assumptions at the basis of our analysis. In Section
5.2
, we bound the error, introduced by the Nyström approximation, on operators
\A
and
\B
from (
12
) (compound in the transition operator
\G
). In Section
5.3
, we show that the Riccati operator obtained with the Nyström dynamics is close to the one obtained with the exact kernel. Finally, in Section
5.4
, we use the aforementioned results to show that, when plugging the optimal control from (
25
) into the dynamics of (
12
), the LQR objective function is close to the one obtained when solving (
24
) directly.

5.1
Hypotheses

In this section, we introduce the hypotheses of our theoretical derivations in the next sections.

Assumption 1

(Bounded kernel)
.

The stationary kernel
k
is bounded, i.e.,
there exists a positive constant
\supfmap<∞
such that

k(\xvec,\xvec^{\prime})\leq\supk

for any
\xvec,\xvec^{\prime}∊\bR^{d}
.

Note that under Assumption
1
, it holds in particular

\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Zx\right\rVert}\leq κ

and
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Sx\right\rVert}\leq κ.

Indeed, for any

, it implies
\n{ψ(x)}=\sqrt{k(x,x)}≤κ
, and thus for any

with
\n{g}≤1
, it holds

\displaystyle\n{\Zx g}_{2}

\displaystyle=n^{-1/2}\n{[\ip{g,ψ(\xvec_{2})},\dots,\ip{g,ψ(\xvec_{n+1})}]ᵀ}_{2}

\displaystyle≤n^{-1/2}\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}\n{g}\texttwosuperior κ\texttwosuperior\right)^{1/2}≤κ,

and a similar argument holds for
\Sx
.

The following assumptions are standard and guarantee that the LQR problems in (
24
) and (
25
) are well-posed and admit an analytical solution in the form of a static state-feedback gain
[
21
]
. Minimal assumptions on
\A
,
\B
from (
13
) and (
14
), and

from (

), for stabilizability and detectability to hold, could be derived by leveraging the theoretical deployments reported, e.g., in
[

]
.

Assumption 2

(Stabilizability,
[
21
]
)
.

The dynamical systems in (
12
) and (
18
) are
stabilizable
, i.e.,

\exists M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Hspace_{1}\rightarrow\R^{n_{u}}\textrm{\ such that\ }{\rho(\A+\B M)<1}
, and
\exists\tilde{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Hspace_{1}\rightarrow\R^{n_{u}}\textrm{\ such that\ }{\rho(\nA+\nB\tilde{M})<1},

where
\rho(W)
is the spectral radius of operator
W
.

Stabilizability assumes that the dynamical systems can be driven to zero with a suitable state-feedback gain. This is a weaker assumption than controllability (i.e., assuming that the system can be driven to any location via the choice of suitable feedback gain), that is often used in the analysis of the LQR algorithm
[
35
]
. However, the controllability assumption in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is not satisfied by the Nyström dynamics, as the
\tilde{z}
functions always live in the subspace
\Hout
, as we discussed in Section
3.3
.

Assumption 3

(Detectability,
[
21
]
)
.

The dynamical systems in (
12
) and (
18
) are
detectable
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\exists t,s,b,d\geq 0\textrm{\ such that\ }\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\A^{t}z\right\rVert}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\geq b\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z\right\rVert}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\innerprod{z,\sum_{i=0}^{s}\A^{i*}Q\A^{i}z}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\geq d\innerprod{z,z}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}},

\displaystyle\exists t,s,b,d\geq 0\textrm{\ such that\ }\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nA^{t}\tilde{z}\right\rVert}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\geq b\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\tilde{z}\right\rVert}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\Rightarrow\innerprod{\tilde{z},\sum_{i=0}^{s}\nA^{i*}Q\nA^{i}\tilde{z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\geq d\innerprod{\tilde{z},\tilde{z}}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}.

Detectability assumes that, if unstable dynamics happen in the linear system, these must be observed (and taken into account in the design of the LQR objective function).

Assumption 4
.

Let
Q
,
R
be the weights of the LQR problem (
24
), and let
P
be a solution of the discrete algebraic Riccati equation

P=A^{*}PA-A^{*}PB(R+B^{*}PB)^{-1}B^{*}PA+Q.

(29)

Then,
\sigma_{\textrm{min}}(P)\geq 1
.

As discussed by Mania et al.
[
35
]
, this technical assumption can be fulfilled by re-scaling
Q
and
R
accordingly. Indeed, if
P
is a solution of (
29
), then
\eta P,\eta>0
is also a solution, provided that
Q
and
R
are multiplied by
\eta
.

5.3

In this section, we show that the Riccati operators for problems in (

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle F(P,A,B)$}}

\displaystyle F(P,A,B)=0,

5.4
Convergence analysis of the LQR objective function

In the previous subsections, we showed that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A-\Atilde\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert B-\Btilde\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
for a sufficiently large number of Nyström landmarks
m
. We further showed that this implies that
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P-\Ptilde\right\rVert}\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. Now, we are interested in assessing how suitable the Nyström dynamics are for solving the LQR problem, instead of using the intractable, infinite-dimensional dynamics associated to an exact kernel. In order to do so, we first solve the LQR problem from (
25
) based on the Nyström approximation of the dynamics. Then, we plug the optimal control sequence retrieved in this way in the exact kernel dynamics of (
12
), and compare the LQR objective function with this control sequence, and the control sequence we would obtained by solving the problem in (
24
). This type of error analysis resembles the one in
[
35
]
, in the context of certainty equivalence. In order to do so, let us define
two control sequences (
\tuveco_{0},\tuveco_{1},\dots)
, and (
\uveco_{0},\uveco_{1},\dots)
.
Moreover, for a given initial condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}

(37)

Then, we upper bound the error
\cJhat-\mathcal{J}
.

(Convergence rate for

be as in (

\cJhat
as in (

\rho(L)\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}<1

\Gamma=1+\max\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert B\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert P\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert K\right\rVert}\}
. Under Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
, if
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A-\Atilde\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
,
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert B-\Btilde\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon

assuming there exists some function

The proof of this result can be obtained by following the same steps of

6.1

In this section, we assess how the control law defined by (

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we have studied the combination of the Koopman operator framework with reproducing kernels and the Nyström method, to design linear control for nonlinear dynamical systems. In a novel theoretical analysis, we have linked novel error rates, due to the Nyström approximation, on the dynamical system’s representation to its effect on the linear quadratic regulator problem. Lastly, we have evaluated the effectiveness of the proposed method both on two classic control benchmark, and on the task of dynamic cloth manipulation.
