Walraswap: a solution to uniform price batch auctions
Sergio Andrés Yuhjtman, Flashbots

Abstract
Consider a finite set of trade orders and automated market makers (AMMs) at some state. We propose a solution to the problem of finding an equilibrium price vector to execute all the orders jointly with corresponding optimal AMMs swaps. The solution is based on Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem. We discuss computational aspects relevant for realistic situations in public blockchain activity.

More generally, for a set of orders given by the market, we can expect to have often more demand from one side than from the other. In those cases the AMM’s liquidity will provide the missing supply. Thus, making use of the AMMs seems to lead to larger volume and therefore larger social welfare in general.

Let us now summarize the main result.
Throughout the text, we will not consider the interests of AMMs’ liquidity providers. We think of AMMs as passive machines. 2
Brouwer’s fixed-point setting

This section introduces the theoretical framework needed to apply Brouwer’s
fixed point theorem to our case.

Trade orders express amounts to trade as a function of the prices.
We will use supply functions
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\varphi}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that take positive
values at coordinate
i
when the agent is selling the
i
-th token
and negative values when buying. The absolute value
|\varphi_{i}(p)|

indicates the amount to be sold or bought if the prices
are
p
. They satisfy two basic conditions:

\varphi(\lambda p)=\varphi(p)\ \forall\lambda>0,\forall p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}

meaning that only relative prices are meaningful, and
p\cdot\varphi(p)=0

which means that the net value of any trade must be zero (the tokens bought must exactly compensate for the tokens sold). This condition
may be called Walras’s law.

This is essentially the same as the “example from economics” in
[
13
]
. However our supply functions are not defined on the border,
i.e. at price vectors
p
where
p_{i}=0
for some
i
. For this reason we
need to introduce
conditions of behaviour at the border that hold in our case and still allow to apply the theorem.
These are conditions (2) and (3) in definition
2.1
.

Definition 2.1
.

We call
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
an “admissible supply function”,
or simply a “supply function” if

(0)
\varphi(\lambda p)=\varphi(p)

\forall\lambda>0
,
\forall p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
, and
\varphi
is continuous.

(1)
\varphi(p)\cdot p=0\ \forall p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}

(2)
\varphi_{i}
is bounded above
\forall i
.

(3) The functions
\psi_{i}(p)=\varphi_{i}(p)p_{i}
extend continuosly to

({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})^{n}\setminus 0\xrightarrow{\psi_{i}}{\mathbb{R}}
.

Observe that condition (1), Walras’s law, can be written in terms of
\psi
as
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}=0
. While
\varphi_{i}
expresses the supply amount of token
i
,
\psi_{i}
expresses the supply value.

Proposition 2.2
.

Following the notation of definition
2.1
, we have
\psi_{i}(p)\leq 0
if
p_{i}=0
for any admissible supply function
\varphi
and any index
i\in I_{n}
.

Proof.

By condition (2),
\varphi_{i}\leq M
for some constant
M
. Therefore,
\varphi_{i}(q)q_{i}\leq Mq_{i}
.
Taking the limit
q\to p
we obtain
\psi_{i}(p)\leq 0
.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

An admissible supply function
\varphi
is said to be “strict” if
it satisfies
\psi_{i}(p)<0
for
p_{i}=0
, for every
i\in I_{n}
.
The maps
\psi_{i}
are those defined in definition
2.1
.

In words, a strict supply function is a supply function such that the demanded
value (not the demanded amount) for a token tends to a positive number when its price goes to zero.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a strict supply function.
Then there is a
p\in{\mathcal{P}}_{n}
such that
\varphi(p)=0
.

Proof.

Let

\Delta=\left\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}/x_{i}\geq 0,\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1\right\}

be the
(n-1)
-simplex, and
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\Delta}}=\{x\in\Delta/x_{i}>0\ \forall i\}
. Consider the function
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\Delta}}\xrightarrow{\rho}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
defined by

\rho_{i}(q)=q_{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{M_{i}}\varphi_{i}(q/M)\right)

where for each index
i
,
M_{i}>0
is an upper bound for
\varphi_{i}
, and

q/M
denotes the vector whose
i
-coordinate is
q_{i}/M_{i}
.

The function
\rho
has the following properties:

(a)
\rho_{i}(q)\geq 0

\forall q\in\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\circ}}{{\Delta}}
.

(b)
\sum_{i=i}^{n}\rho_{i}(q)=1
.

(c)
\rho_{i}(q)
extends continuously to
\Delta
.

Property (a) holds because
M_{i}
is an upper bound for
\varphi_{i}
,
(b) follows from Walras’s law,
\varphi(p)\cdot p=0
while (c) follows from the extension property, (3) in
2.1
.

From properties (a), (b), (c) we conclude that
\rho
is a continuous function

\Delta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Delta

By Brouwer fixed-point theorem, we have a
\tilde{q}\in\Delta
such that

\rho(\tilde{q})=\tilde{q}
. Let us call
p=\tilde{q}/M
. After simplification,
we have
\psi(p)=0
. Since
\varphi
is strict,
we conclude
p\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\varphi(p)=0
.
∎

Remark 1.

The auxiliary function
\rho
used in the proof above is different from the one used by Scarf in
[
13
]
. Both choices work equally well. We opted for
\rho
in order to show this alternative, and also because the condition
\rho_{i}(q)\geq q_{i}
takes the nicer form

\varphi_{i}(p)\leq 0
. This condition is a basic building block in the approximation algorithm.

Remark 2.
An example of a supply function that has no roots is, for n = 2

\varphi(p_{1},p_{2})=\left(-\frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}},1\right)

In this case
\psi(1,0)=(0,0)
.

Proposition 2.5
.

The sum of two supply functions is a supply function. If one of them
is strict, then the sum is strict.

Proof.

It is straightforward. The strictness part makes use of proposition
2.2

∎

It will be useful to establish a definition and some simple facts

Definition 2.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\varphi}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an admissible supply function, and

I\subset I_{n}
. We say that
\varphi
is supported at

\varphi(p)
does not depend on
p_{i}
for every

.

Proposition 2.7
.

An admissible supply function
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\varphi}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
supported
at
I\subset I_{n}
satisfies
\varphi_{i}(p)=0
for every
i\notin I
.

Moreover, if
I=\{i_{1},...,i_{m}\}
, the restricted function

{\mathcal{P}}_{m}\xrightarrow{\tilde{\varphi}}{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, defined by

\tilde{\varphi}(p)=\pi\varphi(\hat{p})
, where
\hat{p}
is any vector
such that
\hat{p}_{i_{j}}=p_{j}
, and
\pi(x)_{j}=x_{i_{j}}

Let
i\notin I
. Take

that only differ at the

By hypothesis we have
\varphi(p)=\varphi(q)
.
Then we have

\varphi_{i}(p)=0

The proof of the second assertion is just a sequence of trivial checks.

Conversely, we can extend admissible functions by adding trivial variables.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
m\leq n
,

Proposition 3.1

Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}\xrightarrow{\varphi}{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be an admissible supply function. Then there is a

\lim_{\infty}g\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

Conversely, given any

Proof.

Given
\varphi

{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\xrightarrow{g}{\mathbb{R}}

. Then

\varphi_{1}(p_{1},p_{2})=\varphi_{1}(p_{1}/p_{2},1)=g(p_{1}/p_{2})

We have

For

\psi_{1}(p)=p_{1}g(p_{1}/p_{2})

If

\psi_{1}(s,0)=\lim_{r\to 0}\psi_{1}(s,r)=\lim_{r\to 0}sg(s/r)=s\lim_{r\to\infty}g(r)

Trade orders as admissible supply functions

Here we construct the admissible supply functions representing trade
orders. For simplicity we will only introduce orders that operate a pair of tokens.
In practice, this is typically the case, however this is not a restriction of our method or a condition in the main theorem

A limit sell order is a trade order that sells a specified amount of one token if the price is not worse than a specified price. If we assume that the order allows to be filled partially, then
it can be well approximated by a continuous supply function.

A continuous limit sell order function is a

and

if
r\geq r_{2}

Continuous limit sell order functions satisfy the conditions of lemma

Given

close to

and take any increasing interpolation (e.g. linear interpolation) between

Definition 4.2

A continuous limit buy order function is an

5
AMMs and their associated supply functions

Consider an AMM that operates two tokens A and B, at a certain state. We can swap A for B or B for A. If we limit to only swapping A for B,
we can model the AMM as a function
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\xrightarrow{f}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
.
For every incoming amount
x
of token A, the AMM returns an amount

f(x)
of token B. The function
f
considers the pool fee, so that it reflects the actual behaviour of the AMM. As a simple example, for a constant product AMM without pool fees, at a state such that the reserves are
a
and
b
, we have
f(x)=bx/(a+x)
.
The function
f
has some properties that we collect in the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

An AMM function is a map
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\xrightarrow{f}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
with the following properties:

(a) it is increasing,

(b)
f(0)=0
,

(c) it is bounded above,

(d) it is concave. Moreover it is strictly concave on
f^{-1}\big((0,M)\big)
, where

M=\sup Im(f)
. Namely, for
x,y\geq 0
such that
f(x),f(y)<M
, we have

f(tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2})>tf(x_{1})+(1-t)f(x_{2})\ \ \ \forall\ 0<t<1

(e)
\partial_{+}f(0)<\infty
.

Conditions (a), (b) and (c) must hold for any reasonable AMM, while conditions
(d) and (e) hold for almost every AMM currently in use.

In the following proposition we collect the properties of concave functions that
we will need. For most readers, it is recommended to assume that
f

is differentiable with
f^{\prime}
strictly decreasing.
Thus,
\partial_{+}f=f^{\prime}
and
h_{f}(r)

introduced below is the inverse of
f^{\prime}

on the region
r\in(0,f^{\prime}(0)]
and
h_{f}(r)=0
for
r\geq f^{\prime}(0)
.
In this way, all the technical details in the proof may be skipped without losing any important point.

In applications, the more general case may appear as piecewise differentiable functions. As a concrete example, for Uniswap v3 pools
[
3
]
differentiability breaks at prices where there is no liquidity: the right derivative jumps from the end price of a region with available liquidity to the starting price of the next region with available liquidity or to zero if there is no such next region.

In (d) we ask the concavity to be strict with the exception of the region where
f
attains the maximum, whenever this region is not empty. The strictness is necessary for the continuity of
h_{f}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\xrightarrow{f}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
be an AMM function. Call
M=\sup Im(f)
.
The following properties hold:

(I) The right derivative exists at every point
x\geq 0
.
Moreover,
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\xrightarrow{\partial_{+}f}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is decreasing and right continuous.

f
is continuous.

(II)
\partial_{+}f
is strictly decreasing in
f^{-1}\big((0,M)\big)
.

\lim_{\infty}\partial_{+}f=0
.

(III) The map
{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\xrightarrow{h_{f}}{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0},\ h_{f}(r)=\min\{x\geq 0/\partial_{+}f(x)\leq r\}
is decreasing and continuous.

(IV)
h_{f}(\partial_{+}f(x))=x
for every
x
such that
f(x)<\sup Im(f)

h_{f}(r)=0
, for every
r\geq\partial_{+}f(0)
.

(V)
rh_{f}(r)\leq f(h_{f}(r))
for every
r>0
.

For the sake of brevity we do not include all the details in the proof.

Proof.

The properties in (I) are standard properties of concave functions. For the convenience of
the reader we include a guide to its proof.

Whenever we have three numbers
0\leq x_{1}<x_{2}<x_{3}
, by the concavity condition,
the point
B=(x_{2},f(x_{2}))
is not below the line through the points
A=(x_{1},f(x_{1}))
and
C=(x_{3},f(x_{3}))
. Thus, we have
slope(BC)\leq slope(AC)\leq slope(AB)
.
The existence of the lateral derivatives can be proved from this fact.
From the same fact it follows that
\partial_{+}f
is decreasing.

\partial_{+}f\geq 0
because
f
is increasing. Continuity of

follows from the existence of lateral derivatives.

Right continuity of
\partial_{+}f
can be proved with the help of a statement similar to Lagrange mean value theorem: for
r_{1}<r_{2}
there is an

\partial_{+}f(r)
is greater than or equal to the slope of the line through
(r_{1},f(r_{1}))
and
(r_{2},f(r_{2}))

(II) Having
\partial_{+}f(x_{1})=\partial_{+}f(x_{2})
for some

is constant in

. In this case we would have

equal to a constant on that interval, contradicting strict concavity.

\lim_{\infty}\partial_{+}f=0
is a consequence of the condition

(III), (IV) For

Since
\partial_{+}f

is an interval.
We have

We obtain
h_{f}
decreasing since
C_{r_{1}}\subset C_{r_{2}}

. Now we can check (IV). Take

.
Since

h_{f}(\partial_{+}f(x))=x

The other assertion in (IV) follows

for

To show continuity of

at

First assume

We have

7
On the computation of the proposed solution

In blockchain applications, a system running batch auctions
will typically perform executions periodically, possibly one per
minute or a frequency in that scale.
From one execution to the other, the prices are not expected to change too much.
This means that we can assume to have a very good
a priori
approximation, which is the latest price vector.

For a general continuous map from the
n
-dimensional simplex to itself, Scarf developed
an algorithm for computing a fixed point
[
13
]
. It is not obvious how to apply
this algorithm to take advantage from our starting approximation. This is precisely the problem solved by Tuy, Thoai and Muu in
[
14
]
. We leave as an open problem the investigation of the different scenarios where this method allows to compute good enough approximations in the required amount of time.

Another way to further reduce the running time of the algorithm in practice is to lower the dimension
n
by restricting the topology of the exchange graph, so that it is possible to divide
and parallelize the problem.

The auctioneer system has to decide which tokens it will allow in the auction. Among those
it can also decide which pairs of tokens are allowed to operate directly, through trade orders or AMMs. In a public blockchain there typically is a main token. For many secondary tokens, the auctioneer may decide to include them but only allowing them to operate directly against
the main token. By doing so, the problem can be parallelized. For each of these secondary tokens,
we have a bidimensional system that can be solved through bisection. More generally, the auctioneer can decide to include clusters of tokens connected to the main token, but with
no direct connection with any other token of the system.
In this case, we can still divide the problem and parallelize the computation.
The following proposition gives the underlying general statement for these type of situations.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{n}\xrightarrow{\varphi}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be an admissible supply function,

I,J\subset I_{n}
with
I\cap J=\{k\}
.
Assume
\varphi=\varphi_{I}+\varphi_{J}
with
\varphi_{I}
and
\varphi_{J}

admissible functions supported at
I
and
J
respectively.
If
\varphi(p)=0
, we have
\varphi_{I}(p)=0
and
\varphi_{J}(p)=0
.

Proof.

Let
p
be such that
\varphi(p)=0
. By proposition
2.7
, for any

i\in I\setminus J
it holds
(\varphi_{J})_{i}(p)=0
. Thus

(\varphi_{I})_{i}(p)=0
. Analogously, for
j\in J\setminus I
it holds

(\varphi_{J})_{j}(p)=0
.

Since
\varphi_{I}
is admissible,

\sum_{i\in I}p_{i}(\varphi_{I})_{i}(p)=0
, whence
p_{k}(\varphi_{I})_{k}(p)=0
,
and therefore
(\varphi_{I})_{k}(p)=0
. Analogously
(\varphi_{J})_{k}(p)=0
.
∎

8
Comparison with recent work

In “Augmenting Batch Exchanges with Constant Function Market
Makers”
[
12
]
, the authors study batch auctions including AMMs,
so that the object of study is exactly the same as here. They state several properties that a desired solution may have and propose possible solutions to achieve some of these properties. They also provide a convex program to compute these equilibrium points, applying a fixed point method.

We will now focus on the differences between
[
12
]
and our work.
Probably the most important difference in the premises is that they treat AMMs as agents participating in the auction, so that AMM swaps will receive the same effective price as the trade orders. This assumption leads to their theorem 1.6 which states that this treatment for AMMs is incompatible with the
price coherence
assumption, i.e. the condition that there is a price vector that determines the final prices of AMMs. On the other hand, our point of view is that AMMs are not agents and there is no apparent reason why the swaps should receive the same effective price as agents
3
3

3

We associate an agent to each AMM, but this should be seen as a technical device for the proof of theorem
6.1
.
. This allows us to fulfill price coherence, which is desirable as we explained in the introduction.
In
[
12
]
this possibility is considered in section 4 by allowing a post-process after the batch trade. Allowing a post-process means that the original “same price” assumption is disregarded, since the end result is equivalent to a single execution that violates it. It is worth to point out that the price vector corresponding to the final state of the AMMs produced by the post-processing step will be typically different from the original execution price vector.

The convex method proposed in section 5 of “Augmenting Batch Exchanges…” is not likely to be applicable to compute the solution expressed by theorem
6.1
(b). This is because such solution is equivalent to a zero of the admissible supply function defined in the proof. There, the supply functions associated to AMMs do not satisfy
assumption 2
from
[
12
]
. Most notably, they are not monotonic and the limit at infinity is zero.
