Abstract
. In this paper, the concept of coderivatives at infinity of set-valued mappings is introduced. Well-posedness properties at infinity of set-valued mappings as well as Mordukhovich’s criterion at infinity are established. Fermat’s rule at infinity in set-valued optimization is also provided. The obtained results, which give new information even in the classical cases of smooth single-valued mappings, provide complete characterizations of the properties under consideration in the setting at infinity of set-valued mappings.

1.
Introduction

The theory of coderivatives (or generalized derivatives) is concerned with the differential properties of set-valued mappings. In the finite dimensional case, this theory associates with a set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a point
(x,y)
in the graph of
F,
a set-valued mapping
D^{*}F({x},{y})\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n},
which is called the
coderivative
of
F
at
(x,y).
The theory
is recognized for many its applications to variational analysis and optimization, as seen for instance in the books
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
10
,
15
,
16
,
19
,
21
]
with the references therein.

Mordukhovich
[
13
]
introduced the coderivative mappings
D^{*}F({x},{y})
for the purposes of deriving necessary optimality conditions in optimization problems with nonsmooth constraints and establishing a maximum principle in the optimal control of differential inclusions.
These mappings enjoy a variety of nice properties, which are important for applications. For example, well-posedness properties of set-valued mappings such as the linear openness (or covering property), the metric regularity and the Lipschitz-like continuity (or Aubin property) can be characterized in terms of coderivatives; also, Fermat’s rules (i.e., the first-order necessary optimality conditions) for set-valued optimization problems can be formulated via coderivatives. For more details, see
[
16
]
.

The aim of this paper is to study the behavior of set-valued mappings in
neighborhoods of infinity.
To do this, following the idea of Mordukhovich
[
13
]
, we define and study
coderivatives at infinity
of set-valued mappings. The obtained results, which give new information even in the classical cases of smooth single-valued mappings, are aimed ultimately at applications to diverse problems of variational analysis and optimization following the now-familiar pattern for coderivatives in the classical cases.

At this point, we would like to mention that several novel notions of variational analysis, including tangent/normal cones at infinity to unbounded sets and subdifferentials at infinity for extended-real-value functions, are introduced and studied in the recent papers
[
17
,
12
]
.

Contributions.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping whose graph is closed, and let
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a Jelonek value of
F
, i.e., there exist sequences
x_{k}\to\infty
and
y_{k}\to y
with
y_{k}\in F(x_{k}).
With notation and definitions given in the next sections, the main contributions of this paper are as follows.

\bullet

The coderivative
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})
of
F
at
(\infty,\overline{y})
is introduced and studied; in particular, basic calculus rules (e.g., sum rule and chain rule) for this coderivative are provided.

\bullet

It will be shown that the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

\mathrm{ker}\,D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})=\{0\}.

(ii)

F
is linearly open around
(\infty,\overline{y}).

(iii)

F
is metrically regular around
(\infty,\overline{y}).

(iv)

F^{-1}
is Lipschitz-like around
(\overline{y},\infty).

\bullet

A version at infinity of Mordukhovich’s criterion will be established; namely, the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(0)=\{0\}.

(ii)

F
is Lipschitz-like around
(\infty,\overline{y})
and
F^{-1}
is open around
(\overline{y},\infty).

(We note that this equivalence is a bit different from the classical one.)

\bullet

Fermat’s rule at infinity in set-valued optimization is given. Namely, consider the set-valued optimization problem with explicit geometry constraints

(P)

\mathrm{minimize}_{K}\ F(x)\quad\textrm{ subject to }\quad x\in\Omega,

where
K\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a pointed closed convex cone with nonempty interior and
\Omega
be an unbounded closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
It will be demonstrated that if
\overline{y}\not\in F(\Omega)
is a weak efficient value for (
P
) in which the following constraint qualification at infinity holds

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(0)\cap\big(-N_{\Omega}(\infty)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\},

then there exists a nonzero vector
c^{*}\in K^{+}
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(c^{*})+N_{\Omega}(\infty),

where
N_{\Omega}(\infty)
is the
normal cone to
\Omega
at infinity
and
K^{+}
is the
positive polar
of
K.

It should be noted that, due to the unboundedness of neighborhoods at infinity, the coderivative at infinity of set-valued mappings enjoy some properties, which are different from the classical ones; see Theorem
4.5
.

We confine our study to the finite dimensional case for two reasons. First of all, to lighten the exposition, we would like to concentrate on the basic ideas without technical and notational complications. Furthermore, some of the constructions used and the results obtained are peculiar to finite dimensions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Some definitions and preliminary results from variational analysis are recalled in Section
2
. The notions of normal cone, coderivative and subdifferential at infinity are introduced and studied in Section
3
.
Well-posedness properties at infinity of set-valued mappings as well as Mordukhovich’s criterion at infinity are presented in Section
4
. Necessary optimality conditions at infinity for set-valued optimization problems are derived in Section
5
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation and definitions

Throughout this work we deal with the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the usual scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and the corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|.
We denote by
\mathbb{B}_{r}(x)
the closed ball centered at
x
with radius
r;
when
{x}
is the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we write
\mathbb{B}_{r}
instead of
\mathbb{B}_{r}({x}),
and when
r=1
we write
\mathbb{B}
instead of
\mathbb{B}_{1}.
We will adopt the convention that
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
and
\sup\emptyset=-\infty.

For a nonempty set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
the closure, interior, convex hull and conic hull of
\Omega
are denoted, respectively, by
\mathrm{cl}\,{\Omega},

\mathrm{int}\,{\Omega},

\mathrm{co}\,\Omega
and
\mathrm{cone}\,\Omega.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,+\infty)
and
\overline{\mathbb{R}}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}.
For an extended-real-valued function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}},
we denote its
effective domain
and
epigraph
by, respectively,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ f(x)<\infty\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{epi}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\ |\ f(x)\leq r\}.

We call
f
a
proper
function if
f(x)<\infty
for at least one
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
or in other words, if
\mathrm{dom}f
is a nonempty set.
The function
f
is said to be
lower semi-continuous
if for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the inequality
\liminf_{x^{\prime}\to{x}}f(x^{\prime})\geq f({x})
holds.

Given a nonempty set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
associate with it the
distance function

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in\Omega}\|x-y\|\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and define the
Euclidean projector of

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\Omega
by

\displaystyle\Pi_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y\in\Omega\ |\ \|x-y\|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega)\}.

If
\Omega=\emptyset,
we let
\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega):=\infty
for all
x.
The
indicator function

\delta_{\Omega}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
of the set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }x\in\Omega,\\
\infty&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By definition,
\Omega
is closed if and only if
\delta_{\Omega}
is lower semi-continuous. The set
\Omega
is said to be
locally closed
at a point
{x}
(not necessarily in
\Omega
) if
\Omega\cap V
is closed for some closed neighborhood
V
of
{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We say that
\Omega
is
locally closed
if it is
locally closed
at every point
x\in\Omega.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping. The
domain
,
kernel
and
graph
of
F
are taken to be the sets

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}F

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid F(x)\not=\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{ker}F

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid 0\in F(x)\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{gph}F

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid y\in F(x)\}.

The
Painlevé–Kuratowski outer limit
of
F
is given by

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x^{\prime}\to{x}}F(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\exists x_{k}\to{x},\exists y_{k}\in F(x_{k}),y_{k}\to y\}.

The inverse mapping
F^{-1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined by
F^{-1}(y):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid y\in F(x)\};
obviously
(F^{-1})^{-1}=F.

The mapping
F
is called
positively homogeneous
if
0\in F(0)
and
tF(x)\subset F(tx)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t>0;
in this case, we let

\displaystyle\|F\|

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup\big\{\|y\|\mid y\in F(x)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \|x\|\leq 1\big\}.

2.2.
Normal cones, coderivatives and subdifferentials

Here we recall some definitions and properties of normal cones to sets, coderivatives of set-valued mappings and subdifferentials of real-valued functions, which can be found in
[
15
,
16
,
21
]
.

Definition 2.1

(normal cones)
.

Consider a set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a point
{x}\in\Omega.

(i)

The
regular normal cone
(known also as the
prenormal
or
Fréchet normal cone
)
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}({x})
to
\Omega
at
{x}
consists of all vectors
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

\displaystyle\langle\xi,x^{\prime}-{x}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle o(\|x^{\prime}-{x}\|)\quad\textrm{ as }\quad x^{\prime}\to{x}\quad\textrm{ with }\quad x^{\prime}\in\Omega.

(ii)

The
limiting normal cone
(known also as the
basic
or
Mordukhovich normal cone
)
N_{\Omega}({x})
to
\Omega
at
{x}
consists of all vectors
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that there are sequences
x_{k}\to{x}
with
x_{k}\in\Omega
and
\xi_{k}\rightarrow\xi
with
\xi_{k}\in\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x_{k}),
or in other words,

\displaystyle{N}_{\Omega}({x})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\Omega}{x}}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}({x}^{\prime}),

where
x^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\Omega}{x}
means that
x^{\prime}\rightarrow{x}
with
x^{\prime}\in\Omega.

If
x\not\in\Omega,
we put
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}({x}):=\emptyset
and
{N}_{\Omega}({x}):=\emptyset.

Remark 2.2
.

(i) It is well known that
N_{\Omega}(\overline{x})
is closed cone (may be non-convex), while
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\overline{x})
is closed convex cone.

(ii) If
\Omega
is a manifold of class
C^{1},
then for every point
x\in\Omega,
the normal cones
\widehat{N}({x};\Omega)
and
N({x};\Omega)
are equal to the normal space to
\Omega
at
{x}
in the sense of differential geometry; see
[
21
, Example 6.8]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a locally closed set. Then for any
x\in\Omega,
we have the following relationships

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle\xi,v\rangle\leq 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in T_{\Omega}(x)\},

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x^{\prime}\rightarrow x}\Big[\mathrm{cone}\big(x^{\prime}-\Pi_{\Omega}(x^{\prime})\big)\Big],

where
T_{\Omega}(x)
stands for the contingent cone to
\Omega
at
x\in\Omega,
i.e.,

\displaystyle T_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\Omega-x}{t}.

Definition 2.4

(coderivatives)
.

Consider a set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a point
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\mathrm{gph}F.
Assume that
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\overline{x},\overline{y}).

The (basic) coderivative
of
F
at
(\overline{x},\overline{y})
is the set-valued mapping
D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid(u,-v)\in N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

By definition, the mapping
D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})
is positively homogeneous.

Definition 2.5

(subdifferentials)
.

Consider a extended-real-valued function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and a point
{x}\in\mathrm{dom}f.

(i)

The
regular
(or
Fréchet
)
subdifferential
of
f
at
{x}
is

\widehat{\partial}f({x}):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid(u,-1)\in\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}({x},f({x}))\}.

(ii)

The
limiting
(or
Mordukhovich
)
subdifferential
of
f
at
{x}
is

\partial f({x}):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid(u,-1)\in{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}({x},f({x}))\}.

(iii)

The
singular subdifferential
of
f
at
{x}
is

\partial^{\infty}f({x}):=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid(u,0)\in{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}({x},f({x}))\}.

If
x\not\in\mathrm{dom}f,
we put
\widehat{\partial}f({x}):=\emptyset,

\partial f({x}):=\emptyset
, and
\partial^{\infty}f({x}):=\emptyset.

2.2.1.
Known facts

The following four lemmas are well known; see
[
15
,
16
,
21
]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any closed set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and point
{x}\in\Omega,
we have

\displaystyle\partial\delta_{\Omega}({x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial^{\infty}\delta_{\Omega}({x})\ =\ N_{\Omega}({x}).

Lemma 2.7
.

For any point
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
we have

\displaystyle\partial\|\cdot-\overline{x}\|(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbb{B}&\quad\textrm{ if }x=\overline{x},\\
\frac{x-\overline{x}}{\|x-\overline{x}\|}&\quad\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Lemma 2.8

(Fermat rule)
.

If a function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
has a local minimum at
\overline{x}\in\mathrm{dom}f,
then
0\in\partial f(\overline{x}).

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
f_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
,
i=1,\dots,m
with
m\geq 2
, be lower semi-continuous at
\overline{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let all but one of these functions be Lipschitz around
\overline{x}.
Then the following inclusion holds:

\displaystyle\partial\left(f_{1}+\cdots+f_{m}\right)(\overline{x})

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\partial f_{1}(\overline{x})+\cdots+\partial f_{m}(\overline{x}).

We also recall the Ekeland variational principle (see
[
8
,
9
]
).

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space, and
f\colon(X,d)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper, lower semi-continuous and bounded from below function. Let
\epsilon>0
and
x_{0}\in X
be given such that

\displaystyle f(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in X}f(x)+\epsilon.

Then, for any
\lambda>0
there is a point
x_{1}\in X
satisfying the following conditions

(i)

f(x_{1})\leq f(x_{0}),

(ii)

d(x_{1},x_{0})\leq\lambda,
and

(iii)

f(x_{1})\leq f(x)+\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}d(x,x_{1})
for all
x\in X.

3.
Normals, coderivatives and subdifferentials at infinity

3.1.
Normal cones at infinity

In our geometric approach to coderivatives, we start with constructing normal cones to nonempty subsets of finite-dimensional spaces.
To see this, let
\Omega
be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Suppose that
\Omega
is
locally closed
at
(\infty,\overline{y}),
i.e., there are a constant
R>0
and a closed neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that the set

\displaystyle\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\|x\|\geq R\textrm{ and }y\in V\}

is closed. Obviously, if the set
\Omega
is closed then it is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y})
for every
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

The following definition is inspired by the works of Mordukhovich
[
15
,
16
]
; see also the recent papers
[
17
,
12
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
normal cone to the set
\Omega
at
(\infty,\overline{y})
is defined by

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y),

where
{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}
means that
\|x\|\rightarrow\infty
and
y\rightarrow\overline{y}
with
(x,y)\in\Omega.

By definition, it is not hard to see that
N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})
is a (possibly empty) closed cone.

Example 3.2
.

(i) Let
\Omega:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid x\geq y\}.
For
(x,y)\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}\{(0,0)\}&\textrm{ if }x>y,\\
\{(t,-t)\mid t\geq 0\}&\textrm{ if }x=y.\end{cases}

Therefore, for any
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)\ =\ \{(0,0)\}.

(ii) Let
\Omega:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid y\geq e^{x}\}.
For
(x,y)\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}\{(0,0)\}&\textrm{ if }y>e^{x},\\
\{(te^{x},-t)\mid t\geq 0\}&\textrm{ if }y=e^{x}.\end{cases}

Therefore, for
\overline{y}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)\ =\ \begin{cases}\emptyset&\textrm{ if }\overline{y}<0,\\
\{0\}\times(-\infty,0]&\textrm{ if }\overline{y}=0,\\
\{(0,0)\}&\textrm{ if }\overline{y}>0.\end{cases}

Proposition 3.3
.

The following equality holds:

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}N_{\Omega}(x,y).

Proof.

Indeed, we know that
\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)\subset N_{\Omega}(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}.
By definition, hence

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x,y)\ \subset\ \mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}N_{\Omega}(x,y).

For the converse, take any
\xi\in\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})}N_{\Omega}(x,y),
which means that there exist sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\Omega
and
\xi_{k}\in N_{\Omega}(x_{k},y_{k})
such that
(x_{k},y_{k})\to(\infty,\overline{y})
and
\xi_{k}\to\xi.
By definition, for each
k>0,
there are
(x_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})\in\Omega
and
\xi_{k}^{\prime}\in\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})
such that
\|(x_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})-(x_{k},y_{k})\|<\frac{1}{k}
and
\|\xi_{k}^{\prime}-\xi_{k}\|<\frac{1}{k}.
Clearly,
(x_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y})
and
\xi_{k}^{\prime}\to\xi
as
k\to\infty.
By definition, therefore
\xi\in{N}_{\Omega}(\infty,\overline{y}).

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
be locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y})
such that

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y})\cap\big(-N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}.

Then
\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}),
and

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y})+N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y}).

Proof.

We first show that there exist a constant
R>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}}(x,y)\cap\big(-N_{\Omega_{2}}(x,y)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}

for all
(x,y)\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
with
\|x\|\geq R
and
y\in V.

Indeed, if this were not true, there would exist sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
and
\xi_{k}\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(x_{k},y_{k})\cap(-N_{\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k})
such that
\|x_{k}\|\to\infty,y_{k}\to\overline{y}
and
\xi_{k}\neq 0.
Since
N_{\Omega_{1}}(x_{k},y_{k})
and
N_{\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k})
are cones, we can assume that
\|\xi_{k}\|=1
for all
k.
Passing to a subsequence if necessary we may assume that the sequence
\xi_{k}
converges to some
\xi.
Clearly,
\|\xi\|=1
and
\xi\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y})\cap\big(-N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y})\big),
in contradiction to our assumption.

We now prove the desired inclusion. To this end, take any
\xi\in N_{\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y}).
By Proposition
3.3
, there exist sequences
x_{k}\in\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}
and
\xi_{k}\in N_{\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k})
such that
\|x_{k}\|\to\infty,y_{k}\to\overline{y}
and
\xi_{k}\to\xi.
Then for all
k
sufficiently large, we have

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}}(x_{k},y_{k})\cap\big(-N_{\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}.

It follows from
[
16
, Theorem 2.16]
that

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}\cap\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k})

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle N_{\Omega_{1}}(x_{k},y_{k})+N_{\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k}).

Consequently, we can write
\xi_{k}=u_{k}+v_{k}
for some
u_{k}\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(x_{k},y_{k})
and
v_{k}\in N_{\Omega_{2}}(x_{k},y_{k}).
There are two cases to be considered.

Case 1: the sequence
u_{k}
is bounded.

Then the sequence
v_{k}
is bounded too. Passing to subsequences if necessary we may assume that
u_{k}
and
v_{k}
converge to some
u
and
v,
respectively. Clearly,
\xi=u+v,
and by Proposition
3.3
, we have
u\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y})
and
v\in N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y}).

Therefore
\xi\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y})+N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y}).

Case 2: the sequence
u_{k}
is unbounded.

Then the sequence
v_{k}
is unbounded too. Since
u_{k}+v_{k}
is a convergent sequence, it is easy to see that the sequence
\frac{u_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}
is bounded. Passing to subsequences if necessary we may assume that the sequences
\frac{u_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}
and
\frac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}
converge to some
u
and
v,
respectively. Clearly,
u\in N_{\Omega_{1}}(\infty,\overline{y}),v\in N_{\Omega_{2}}(\infty,\overline{y})
and
u=-v\neq 0,
in contradiction to our assumption.
∎

3.2.
Coderivatives at infinity

In this subsection, the
coderivatives at infinity
of set-valued mappings will be defined in terms of the normal cones at infinity to the graphs of the mappings in question. Namely, let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping. We will associate with
F
the
Jelonek set

\displaystyle J(F)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\exists(x_{k},y_{k})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,y)\}.

Let
\overline{y}\in J(F),
and suppose in this subsection that
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}).

Definition 3.5
.

The coderivative
of
F
at
(\infty,\overline{y})
is the set-valued mapping
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;(u,-v)\in N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,\overline{y})\}\quad\textrm{ for }\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

By definition, it is easy to see that the coderivative of
F
at
(\infty,\overline{y})
is a closed, positively homogeneous mapping.

Example 3.6
.

(i) Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{1\}&\textrm{ if }x>0,\\
[-1,1]&\textrm{ if }x=0,\\
\{-1\}&\textrm{ if }x<0,\\
\end{cases}

A direct calculation shows that
J(F)=\{-1,1\},
and for
\overline{y}\in J(F)
we have

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(0,v)\mid v\in\mathbb{R}\},

which yields
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)=\{0\}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}.

(ii) Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}[0,x]&\textrm{ if }x\geq 0,\\
x^{2}&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

A direct calculation shows that
J(F)=[0,+\infty)
and

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{(0,v)\mid v\leq 0\}&\textrm{ if }\overline{y}=0,\\
\{0,0\}&\textrm{ if }\overline{y}>0.\end{cases}

Hence

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,0)(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{0\}&\textrm{ if }v\geq 0,\\
\emptyset&\textrm{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

and if
\overline{y}>0,
then

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{0\}&\textrm{ if }v=0,\\
\emptyset&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(iii) Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by

F(x_{1},x_{2}):=\{x_{1}\}.
A direct calculation shows that
J(F)=\mathbb{R},
and for
\overline{y}\in J(F)
we have

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{(v,0,-v)\mid v\in\mathbb{R}\},

which yields
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)=\{(v,0)\}
for all
v\in\mathbb{R}.

(iv) Consider an unbounded closed set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and define the
indicator mapping

\Delta_{\Omega}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
\Omega
by

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Omega}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{0\}&\textrm{ if }x\in\Omega,\\
\emptyset&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By definition, we have
J(\Delta_{\Omega})=\{0\}
and
\mathrm{gph}\Delta_{\Omega}=\Omega\times\{0\}.
Hence

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{gph}\Delta_{\Omega}}(\infty,0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{gph}\Delta_{\Omega}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\infty,0)}\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}\Delta_{\Omega}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{gph}\Delta_{\Omega}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\infty,0)}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x)\times N_{\{0\}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\xrightarrow{\Omega}\infty}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x)\times\mathbb{R}^{m},

which yields

\displaystyle D^{*}\Delta_{\Omega}(\infty,0)(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\xrightarrow{\Omega}\infty}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Let us present the following property, which easily follows from Proposition
3.3
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\overline{y}\in J(F).
For all
{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
one has

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})({v})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathop{\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}}\limits_{(x,y)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,\overline{y})}}\limits_{v^{\prime}\to{v}}{D^{*}F(x,y)(v^{\prime})}.

Next we present some calculus rules for coderivatives of set-valued mappings.
Recall that the sum of two set-valued mappings
F_{1},F_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{R}^{m}}
is defined by

\displaystyle(F_{1}+F_{2})(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y_{1}+y_{2}\mid y_{1}\in F_{1}(x),y_{2}\in F_{2}(x)\}\quad\textrm{ for all }\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},
we let

\displaystyle J^{+}(F_{1}+F_{2},y)\ :=\ \{(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}

\displaystyle\mid

\displaystyle\exists x_{k}\rightarrow\infty,\ \exists y_{1,k}\in F_{1}(x_{k}),\ \exists y_{2,k}\in F_{2}(x_{k}),

\displaystyle(y_{1,k},y_{2,k})\to(y_{1},y_{2}),\ y_{1}+y_{2}=y\}.

Theorem 3.8

(sum rule)
.

Suppose
F:=F_{1}+F_{2}
for closed-graph set-valued mappings
F_{1},F_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and let
\overline{y}\in J(F).
Assume both of the following conditions are satisfied.

(a)

(boundedness condition):
There exist constants
R>0,\rho>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
such that whenever
\|x\|>R,

y_{i}\in F_{i}(x)
and
y_{1}+y_{2}\in V,
one has
\|y_{i}\|<\rho
for
i=1,2.

(b)

(constraint qualification):
For every
(y_{1},y_{2})\in J^{+}(F_{1}+F_{2},\overline{y})
we have

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(\infty,y_{1})(0)\cap\big(-D^{*}F_{2}(\infty,y_{2})(0)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}.

Then
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}),
and for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we have

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\bigcup_{(\overline{y}_{1},\overline{y}_{2})\in J^{+}(F_{1}+F_{2},\overline{y})}D^{*}F_{1}(\infty,\overline{y}_{1})(v)+D^{*}F_{2}(\infty,\overline{y}_{2})(v).

Proof.

The condition (a), together with the closedness of the graphs of the mappings
F_{1}
and
F_{2},
ensures that
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}).

Next observe that, by increasing
R
and shrinking
V
if necessary, we may assume

(1)

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(x,y_{1})(0)\cap\big(-D^{*}F_{2}(x,y_{2})(0)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R},

y_{1}\in F_{1}(x)
and
y_{2}\in F_{2}(x)
with
y_{1}+y_{2}\in V.

(Indeed, if this is not true, there are sequences
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y_{1,k}\in F_{1}(x_{k}),y_{2,k}\in F_{2}(x_{k})
and
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\|x_{k}\|\to\infty,

y_{1,k}+y_{2,k}\to\overline{y}
and

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},y_{1,k})(0)\cap\big(-D^{*}F_{2}(x_{k},y_{2,k})(0)\big).

This, together with the positive homogeneity of coderivative mappings, yields

\displaystyle\dfrac{u_{k}}{\|u_{k}\|}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},y_{1,k})(0)\cap\big(-D^{*}F_{2}(x_{k},y_{2,k})(0)\big).

Passing to subsequences if necessary and applying Proposition
3.7
, we get
(\overline{y}_{1},\overline{y}_{2})\in J^{+}(F_{1}+F_{2},\overline{y})

(which comes from the condition (a)) and a unit vector
u
satisfying

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(\infty,\overline{y}_{1})(0)\cap\big(-D^{*}F_{2}(\infty,\overline{y}_{2})(0)\big),

which contradicts the condition (b).)

Take any
u\in D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)
. In view of Proposition
3.7
, we find sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}F,

u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
u_{k}\in D^{*}F(x_{k},y_{k})(v_{k})
such that

\displaystyle x_{k}\to\infty,\;y_{k}\to\overline{y},\;u_{k}\to u\ \textrm{ and }\ v_{k}\to v.

By definition, there are
y_{1,k}\in F_{1}(x_{k})
and
y_{2,k}\in F_{2}(x_{k})
such that
y_{k}=y_{1,k}+y_{2,k}.
By the condition (a), we may assume that
y_{1,k}
and
y_{2,k}
converge to some
\overline{y}_{1}
and
\overline{y}_{2},
respectively. Clearly,
(\overline{y}_{1},\overline{y}_{2})\in J^{+}(F_{1}+F_{2},\overline{y}).

Now putting (
1
) together with
[
16
, Theorem 3.9]
(see also
[
21
, Theorem 10.41]
), for all
k
sufficiently large we can write

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{1,k}+u_{2,k},

where
u_{1,k}\in D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},y_{1,k})(v_{k})
and
u_{2,k}\in D^{*}F_{2}(x_{k},y_{2,k})(v_{k})
. We consider two following cases.

Case 1: the sequence
u_{1,k}
is bounded.

Then the sequence
u_{2,k}
is bounded too. Passing to subsequences if necessary we may assume that the sequences
u_{1,k}
and
u_{2,k}
converge to some
u_{1}
and

respectively. By Proposition
3.7

as required.

Case 2: the sequence
u_{1,k}
is unbounded.

Then the sequence
u_{2,k}
is unbounded too. By the positive homogeneity of coderivative mappings, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{u_{1,k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},y_{1,k})\left(\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}\right)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\dfrac{u_{2,k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}\ \in\ D^{*}F_{2}(x_{k},y_{2,k})\left(\dfrac{v_{k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}\right).

Since
u_{k}=u_{1,k}+u_{2,k}
is a convergent sequence, it is easy to see that the sequence
\frac{u_{2,k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}
is bounded. Passing to subsequences if necessary we may assume that the sequences
\frac{u_{1,k}}{\|u_{1,k}\|}
and

converge to some

and

respectively. Clearly,

and

moreover, by Proposition

and

∎

Composition of
F_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{p}
with

is defined by

\displaystyle F_{2}\circ F_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid F_{1}(x)\cap F_{2}^{-1}(y)\neq\emptyset\}\quad\textrm{ for all }\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

to get a set-valued mapping
F_{2}\circ F_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}.
For each
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we let

\displaystyle J^{\circ}(F_{2}\circ F_{1},y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\mid\exists x_{k}\rightarrow\infty,\ \exists z_{k}\in F_{1}(x_{k}),\ \exists y_{k}\in F_{2}(z_{k}),\ z_{k}\to z,\ y_{k}\to y\}.

Theorem 3.9

(chain rule)
.

Suppose
F:=F_{2}\circ F_{1}
for closed-graph set-valued mappings
F_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
and
F_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{R}^{m}},
and let
\overline{y}\in J(F).
Assume both of the following conditions are satisfied.

(a)

(boundedness condition):
There exist constants

and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}

for all

and

\displaystyle F_{1}(x)\cap F_{2}^{-1}(y)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{R^{\prime}}.

(b)

(constraint qualification):
For every

one has

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{2}(z,\overline{y})(0)\cap\mathrm{ker}D^{*}F_{1}(\infty,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}.

Then
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}),
and

\displaystyle D^{*}(F_{2}\circ F_{1})(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\overline{z}\in J^{\circ}(F_{2}\circ F_{1},\overline{y})}D^{*}F_{1}(\infty,\overline{z})\circ D^{*}F_{2}(\overline{z},\overline{y}).

Proof.

The condition (a), together with the closedness of the graphs of the mappings
F_{1}
and
F_{2},
implies that
\mathrm{gph}F
is locally closed at
(\infty,\overline{y}).

Next observe that, by increasing
R
and shrinking
V
if necessary, we may assume

(2)

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{2}(z,y)(0)\cap\mathrm{ker}D^{*}F_{1}(x,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R},y\in V
and
z\in F_{1}(x)\cap F_{2}^{-1}(y).

(Indeed, if this is not true, there are sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\to(\infty,\overline{y}),z_{k}\in F_{1}(x_{k})\cap F_{2}^{-1}(y_{k})
and
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle u_{k}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{2}(z_{k},y_{k})(0)\cap\mathrm{ker}D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},z_{k}).

\displaystyle\dfrac{u_{k}}{\|u_{k}\|}

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{2}(z_{k},y_{k})(0)\cap\mathrm{ker}D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},z_{k}).

3.7
, we get
\overline{z}\in J^{\circ}(F_{2}\circ F_{1},\overline{y})

u
satisfying

\displaystyle\in

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m},
and take any

By Proposition
3.7
, there are sequences
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

2
) together with

, Theorem 3.11]
(see also
[
21
, Theorem 10.37]

\displaystyle D^{*}(F_{2}\circ F_{1})(x_{k},y_{k})(v_{k})

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle D^{*}F_{1}(x_{k},z_{k})\circ D^{*}F_{2}(z_{k},y_{k})(v_{k}),

\widehat{u}_{k}
is bounded.

\widehat{u}_{k}

\widehat{u}.

3.7
, then

3.3.
Subdifferentials at infinity

We define next the
basic and singular limiting subdifferentials at infinity
of extended-real-valued functions geometrically via the normal cones at infinity to the epigraphs of the functions in question. Namely, let
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a lower semi-continuous function and
\overline{y}\in J(f).
Recall that

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{epi}f}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}(x,r).

Definition 3.10
.

The
limiting and singular subdifferentials
of
f
at
(\infty,\overline{y})
are defined by

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (u,-1)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y})\},

\displaystyle\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\ |\ (u,0)\in N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y})\}.

The next result provides a simple formula for the normal cones
N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y}),
and hence, for the subdifferentials
\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})
and
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y}).

Proposition 3.11
.

We have

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,f(x))\longrightarrow(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}(x,f(x)).

Proof.

The inclusion
\supset
is clear from the definition of
N_{\mathrm{epi}f}(\infty,\overline{y}).

For the inclusion
\subset,
we see that for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}f
and
r\geq f(x),

\displaystyle T_{\textrm{epi}f}(x,f(x))

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle T_{\textrm{epi}f}(x,r),

which in combination with Lemma
2.3
yields

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{\textrm{epi}f}(x,r)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\widehat{N}_{\textrm{epi}f}(x,f(x)).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{epi}f}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}(x,r)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{(x,f(x))\longrightarrow(\infty,\overline{y})}\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{epi}f}(x,f(x)),

as required.
∎

The alternative formulas for the subdifferentials at infinity are given below.

Proposition 3.12
.

We have

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathop{\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}}\limits_{(x,f(x))\to(\infty,\overline{y})}}{\partial f(x)},

\displaystyle\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathop{\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}}\limits_{(x,f(x))\to(\infty,\overline{y})}}\limits_{r\searrow 0}{r\partial f(x)}.

Proof.

The proof is omitted because it is similar to
[
12
, Proposition 4.4]
.
∎

Example 3.13
.

(i) Consider the smooth function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto\sin x
. We have
J(f)=[-1,1]
and
\partial f(x)=\{\cos x\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. By virtue of Proposition
3.12
,
\partial f(\infty,0)=\{-1,1\},
which is not a singleton set, while
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,0)=\{0\}.

(ii) Consider the smooth function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\mapsto e^{x}
. We have
J(f)=\{0\}
and
\partial f(x)=\{e^{x}\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In view of Proposition
3.12
,
\partial f(\infty,0)=\{0\}
and
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,0)=[0,+\infty).

We consider the
epigraphical
set-valued mapping
E_{f}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
associated with
f
by

\displaystyle E_{f}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{r\in\mathbb{R}\mid r\geq f(x)\}.

It is clear that
\textrm{gph}E_{f}=\textrm{epi}f
and
\textrm{dom}E_{f}=\textrm{dom}f.
Moreover we have

\displaystyle\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{*}E_{f}(\infty,\overline{y})(1)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})\ =\ D^{*}E_{f}(\infty,\overline{y})(0).

The next result shows that we can replace
E_{f}
in the coderivative representation of
\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})
by the function
f
itself, having also a useful relationship between
\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})

and
D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(0).

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
f
be lower semi-continuous and
\overline{y}\in J(f).
Then we have the following statements.

(i)

\partial f(\infty,\overline{y})=D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(1).

(ii)

\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})\subset D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(0).

Proof.

(i) Take any
u\in\partial f(\infty,\overline{y}).
By Proposition
3.12
, there are sequences
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k})
such that
x_{k}\to\infty
,
f(x_{k})\to\overline{y}
and
u_{k}\to u.
In view of
[
16
, Theorem 1.23]
,
u_{k}\in D^{*}f(x_{k})(1)
. Letting
k\to\infty
and employing Proposition
3.7
, we obtain
u\in D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(1).

Conversely, let
u\in D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(1)
. By Proposition
3.7
, we find sequences
x_{k},u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
with
u_{k}\in D^{*}f(x_{k})(v_{k})
such that
x_{k}\to\infty,u_{k}\to u,

f(x_{k})\to\overline{y}
and
v_{k}\to 1.

Then for all
k
sufficiently large, we have
v_{k}\neq 0
and so
\dfrac{u_{k}}{v_{k}}\in D^{*}f(x_{k})(1),
which, together with
[
16
, Theorem 1.23]
, yields
\dfrac{u_{k}}{v_{k}}\in\partial f(x_{k}).
Letting
k\to\infty,
we obtain
u\in\partial f(\infty,\overline{y}).

(ii) Pick any
u\in\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y}).
By Proposition
3.12
, there are sequences
x_{k},u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
with

u_{k}\in\partial f(x_{k}),
and
r_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
x_{k}\to\infty,

f(x_{k})\to\overline{y},

r_{k}\searrow 0
and
r_{k}u_{k}\to u
. By
[
16
, Theorem 1.23]
, we have
u_{k}\in D^{*}f(x_{k})(1)
and hence
r_{k}u_{k}\in D^{*}f(x_{k})(r_{k})
as
r_{k}>0
. Letting
k\to\infty
and employing Proposition
3.7
, we obtain
u\in D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(0).

∎

Remark 3.15
.

The inclusion (ii) in the above proposition can be strict. A simple example on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is furnished by

\displaystyle f(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}0&\textrm{ if }x_{1}<0,\\
-\sqrt[3]{x_{1}}&\textrm{ if }x_{1}\geq 0,\end{cases}

which has
J(f)=(-\infty,0].
For this function at
\overline{y}:=0,
we have

\partial^{\infty}f(\infty,\overline{y})=(-\infty,0]\times\{0\},
while
D^{*}f(\infty,\overline{y})(0)=(-\infty,+\infty)\times\{0\}.

4.
Well-posedness and Mordukhovich’s criterion at infinity

In this section, in the setting at infinity, basic well-posedness properties of set-values mappings will be characterized completely in terms of coderivatives.
Let us start with the following result in which the property in (ii) is openness around
(\infty,\overline{y})
with linear rate
\mu,
the property in (iii) is the metric regularity of
F
around
(\infty,\overline{y})
with constant
\ell,
whereas the property in (iv) is the Lipschitz continuity of
F^{-1}
around
(\overline{y},\infty)
with constant
\ell;
for the related works, see

[
1
,
4
,
14
,
18
,
20
]

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping of closed graph, and let
\overline{y}\in J(F).
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(coderivative nonsingularity):

\mathrm{ker}\,D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})=\{0\}.

(ii)

(linear openness at infinity):
There exist constants
R>0,\mu>0,\epsilon>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\big(F(x)+\mu r\mathbb{B}\big)\cap V

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F(x+r\mathbb{B})\quad\textrm{ whenever }\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}\ \textrm{ and }\ r\in(0,\epsilon).

(iii)

(metric regularity at infinity):
There are constants
R>0,\ell>0,\gamma>0,
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,F^{-1}(y))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\,\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y\in V
with
\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))<\gamma.

(iv)

(inverse Lipschitz-like property at infinity):
There are constants
R>0,\ell>0,
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle F^{-1}(y^{\prime})\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}\big)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F^{-1}(y)+\ell\|y^{\prime}-y\|\mathbb{B}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad y^{\prime},y\in V.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Assume that
\mathrm{ker}\,D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})=\{0\}.
Since
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})
is a closed positively homogeneous mapping, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{*}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\,\{\|u\|\mid u\in D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v)\textrm{ and }\|v\|=1\}\ >\ 0.

Take any
\mu\in(0,\mu_{*}).
We will show that there exist constants
R>0,\epsilon>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\big(F(x)+\mu r\mathbb{B}\big)\cap V

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F(x+r\mathbb{B})\quad\textrm{ whenever }\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}\ \textrm{ and }\ r\in(0,\epsilon).

By contradiction, there are sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\to(\infty,\overline{y})
and
r_{k}\searrow 0
as well as
z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying the following conditions

\displaystyle y_{k}\in F(x_{k}),\quad\|z_{k}-y_{k}\|\leq\mu r_{k},\quad z_{k}\notin F(x_{k}+r_{k}\mathbb{B}).

Fix
k
and define the function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\|y-z_{k}\|+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}(x,y),

which is lower semi-continuous as the graph of
F
is closed. Clearly
\inf_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}\varphi(x,y)=0
and
\varphi(x_{k},y_{k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty.

Applying the Ekeland variational principle (see Theorem
2.10
) to
\varphi
with
\epsilon:=\mu r_{k},\lambda:=r_{k},
the initial point
(x_{k},y_{k}),
and the metric space

(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},d),
where

\displaystyle d((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\sqrt{r_{k}}\|y-y^{\prime}\|\quad\textrm{ for }\quad(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m},

we find a pair
(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}\,F
such that

\displaystyle\|\overline{x}_{k}-x_{k}\|+\sqrt{r_{k}}\|\overline{y}_{k}-y_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r_{k}

and
(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})
is a global minimizer of the lower semi-continuous function

\displaystyle\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\|y-z_{k}\|+{\mu}\big(\|x-\overline{x}_{k}\|+\sqrt{r_{k}}\|y-\overline{y}_{k}\|\big)+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}(x,y).

By Fermat’s rule (see Lemma
2.8
), then
(0,0)\in\partial\psi(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k}).
Note that
\overline{y}_{k}\neq z_{k}
as
\overline{y}_{k}\in F(\overline{x}_{k})\subset F(x_{k}+r_{k}\mathbb{B})
and
z_{k}\notin F(x_{k}+r_{k}\mathbb{B}).
In view of Lemmas
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.9
, we have

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\{0\}\times\frac{\overline{y}_{k}-z_{k}}{\|\overline{y}_{k}-z_{k}\|}+{\mu}\big(\mathbb{B}\times\sqrt{r_{k}}\mathbb{B}\big)+{N}_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k}).

Hence, there is a vector
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|\leq{\mu},\quad 1-\mu\sqrt{r_{k}}\leq\|v_{k}\|\leq 1+\mu\sqrt{r_{k}}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad u_{k}\in D^{*}F(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})(v_{k}).

Passing to subsequences if necessary, we may assume that
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(u,v).
Certainly
\|u\|\leq\mu
and
\|v\|=1.
Observe that
(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})\to(\infty,\overline{y}).
Hence
u\in D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v),
which contradicts the choice of
\mu.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii). Let
\ell:=\frac{1}{\mu^{-1}}
and
0<\gamma<\mu\epsilon.
Take any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y\in V

with
\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))<\gamma
and put
r:=\ell\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))<\epsilon.
Without loss of generality we can assume that
r>0
because otherwise the conclusion is trivial. Since the graph of
F
is closed, there is
y^{\prime}\in F(x)
such that

\displaystyle\|y-y^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))\ =\ \frac{r}{\ell}\ =\ \mu r.

Hence

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle y^{\prime}+\mu r\mathbb{B}\ \subset\ F(x)+\mu r\mathbb{B}.

The condition (ii) implies that
y\in F(x+r\mathbb{B}).
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,F^{-1}(y))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r\ =\ \ell\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x)),

as required.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv). Choose a positive constant
\epsilon<\gamma
such that
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\overline{y})\subset V.
Let
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon/2}(\overline{y})
and take any
x\in F^{-1}(y^{\prime})\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}\big).
We have
y\in V
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|y-y^{\prime}\|\ \leq\ \|y-\overline{y}\|+\|y^{\prime}-\overline{y}\|\ \leq\ \epsilon\ <\ \gamma.

The condition (iii) implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x,F^{-1}(y))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\,\mathrm{dist}(y,F(x))\ \leq\ \ell\,\|y^{\prime}-y\|,

which yields the desired inclusion.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i). Assume that there are constants
R>0,\ell>0
and
\epsilon>0
such that

(3)

\displaystyle F^{-1}(y^{\prime})\cap\big(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}\big)

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F^{-1}(y)+\ell\|y-y^{\prime}\|\mathbb{B}\quad\textrm{for all}\quad y,y^{\prime}\in V:=\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\overline{y}).

We will show that
\mathrm{ker}\,D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})=\{0\}.

To see this, let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be such that
0\in D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(v).
By Proposition
3.7
, there exist sequences
(x_{k},y_{k})\in\mathrm{gph}F
and
(u_{k},-v_{k})\in\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}F}(x_{k},y_{k})
such that
(x_{k},y_{k})\to(\infty,\overline{y})
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(0,v)
as
k\to\infty.
In particular,
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y_{k}\in\overline{y}+\dfrac{\epsilon}{2}\mathbb{B}
for all
k
sufficiently large. If
v_{k}=0
for infinitely many
k,
then
v=0
and there is nothing to prove. So, suppose that
v_{k}\neq 0
for all
k.

By the definition of
\widehat{N}_{\mathrm{gph}F}(x_{k},y_{k}),
for each
k>0
we can find
\delta_{k}>0
such that

(4)

\displaystyle\langle u_{k},x-x_{k}\rangle-\langle v_{k},y-y_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\big(\|x-x_{k}\|+\|y-y_{k}\|\big)

for all
(x,y)\in\mathrm{gph}F\cap\big(\mathbb{B}_{\delta_{k}}(x_{k})\times\mathbb{B}_{\delta_{k}}(y_{k})\big).

Fix
k
sufficiently large. Let
y^{\prime}:=y_{k}
and
y:=y_{k}-\frac{1}{\ell}r\frac{v_{k}}{\|v_{k}\|}
with
0<r<\min\{\delta_{k},\dfrac{\ell\epsilon}{2}\}.
Certainly
x_{k}\in F^{-1}(y^{\prime})\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in V.
This, together with the inclusion (
3
), ensures the existence of
x\in F^{-1}(y)
such that

\displaystyle\|x-x_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\|y-y^{\prime}\|\ \leq\ r.

Therefore, by the inequality (
4
), we get easily that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\ell}r\|v_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r\|u_{k}\|+\frac{1}{k}(r+\frac{1}{\ell}r),

which yields

\displaystyle\|v_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\|u_{k}\|+\frac{1}{k}(\ell+1).

Finally, letting
k\to\infty
we obtain
v=0
as required.
∎

Example 4.2
.

Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by

F(x):=\{0\}\cup[x,+\infty).

We have
J(F)=\{0\},

N_{\mathrm{gph}F}(\infty,0)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}
and

\mathrm{ker}\,D^{*}F(\infty,0)=\mathbb{R}.
Hence
F
does not satisfy the equivalent conditions (ii)-(iv) in Theorem
4.1
.

A set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
can be identified in a set with its

distance function

d_{F}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}},\quad(x,y)\mapsto\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big)=\inf\{\|y-v\|\;|\;v\in F(x)\}.

Clearly
F
is uniquely determined by
d_{F},
so every property of
F
must correspond to a property of
d_{F}
and vice versa. The following result reveals the property of
d_{F}
that corresponds to Lipschitz-like continuity of
F
and indicates obviously why that notion has a natural significance; see also
[
20
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Proposition 4.3

(distance characterization of the Lipschitz-like property at infinity)
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping whose graph is closed, and let
\overline{y}\in J(F).
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

F
is Lipschitz-like around
(\infty,\overline{y}),
i.e., there exist constants
R>0,\ell>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
satisfying the inclusion

(5)

\displaystyle F(x)\cap V

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F(x^{\prime})+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|\mathbb{B}\quad\text{ for all }\quad x,x^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(V)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}.

(ii)

There exist constants
R>0,\ell>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that

\displaystyle\left|d_{F}(x,y)-d_{F}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell\big(\|x-x^{\prime}\|+\|y-y^{\prime}\|\big)

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(V)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y,y^{\prime}\in V.

Proof.

Observe first that (ii) is equivalent to the existence of constants
R>0,\ell>0
and a neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(V)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y\in V
we have

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big)+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|

This is true because the function
d_{F}(x,y)
is Lipschitz in
y
with modulus
1
for all
x\in F^{-1}(V).

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). If the inequality (
6
) holds for constants
R>0,\ell>0
and some neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y},
then for every
x,x^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(V)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y\in F(x)\cap V
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big)+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|\ =\ \ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|,

because
\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big)=0.
Hence
y\in F(x^{\prime})+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|\mathbb{B},
as required in (
5
) for Lipschitz-like continuity.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). Assume that (
5
) holds for constants
R>0,\ell>0
and some neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}.
Then for all
x,x^{\prime}\in F^{-1}(V)\setminus\mathbb{B}_{R}
and
y\in V
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x^{\prime})+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|\mathbb{B}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\cap V\big).

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x^{\prime})\big)-\rho

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)+\rho\mathbb{B}\big)

for any
\rho\geq 0,
so

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x^{\prime})\big)-\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\cap V\big).

Therefore, it suffices to show that for a neighborhood
V^{\prime}
of
\overline{y}
with
V^{\prime}\subset V
we have for all
x\in F^{-1}(V^{\prime})
and
y\in V^{\prime},

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\cap V\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big).

To see this, choose
\epsilon>0
small enough that
\overline{y}+\epsilon\mathbb{B}\subset V,
and let
V^{\prime}:=\overline{y}+(\epsilon/3)\mathbb{B}.
Then
V^{\prime}\subset V,
and for arbitrary
x\in F^{-1}(V^{\prime})
and
y\in V^{\prime}
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|y-\overline{y}\|+\mathrm{dist}\big(\overline{y},F(x)\big)\ \leq\ \frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\ =\ \frac{2\epsilon}{3}.

Since
F(x)
is closed, there exists
y^{\prime}\in F(x)
such that
\|y-y^{\prime}\|=\mathrm{dist}\big({y},F(x)\big).
Hence

\displaystyle\|y^{\prime}-\overline{y}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|y^{\prime}-y\|+\|y-\overline{y}\|\ \leq\ \frac{2\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\ =\ \epsilon,

which yields
y^{\prime}\in V,
and so
\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\cap V\big)=\mathrm{dist}\big(y,F(x)\big),
as required.
∎

It is well known from Mordukhovich’s criterion (see
[
16
, Theorem 3.3(iii)]
) that
for a closed graph set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a point
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\mathrm{gph}F
we have
D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})(0)=\{0\}
if and only if
F
is Lipschitz-like around
(\overline{x},\overline{y}),
which means that there are a constant
\ell>0
and neighborhoods
U
of
\overline{x}
and
V
of
\overline{y}
such that

\displaystyle F(x)\cap V

\displaystyle\subset

\displaystyle F(x^{\prime})+\ell\|x-x^{\prime}\|\mathbb{B}\quad\text{ for all }\quad x,x^{\prime}\in U.

On the other hand, it will be demonstrated in Theorem
4.5
and Example
4.4
(i) below that if
D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(0)=\{0\}
then
F
is Lipschitz-like around
(\infty,\overline{y}),
but not conversely.

Example 4.4
.

(i) Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\{\frac{1}{x+1}\}&\textrm{ if }x\in\mathbb{N},\\
\emptyset&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

which has
J(F)=\{0\}.
By definition, it is easy to check that
D^{*}F(\infty,0)(0)=\mathbb{R},
while
F
is Lipschitz-like around
(\infty,0)
with modulus
1.
Note that for any open neighborhood
V
of
0
in
\mathbb{R},
the inverse image
F^{-1}(V)
is not open.

(ii) Consider the set-valued mapping
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}
defined by
F(x_{1},x_{2}):=\{x_{1}-x_{2}^{2}\}.

We have

and
D^{*}F(\infty,0)(0)=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}.
Moreover,
F
is not Lipschitz-like around
(\infty,0),
while
F^{-1}(V)
is open for any open subset
V
of
\mathbb{R}.

The equivalence of (i) with (ii) in the next result can be seen as a
version at infinity of Mordukhovich’s criterion
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping of closed graph, and let
\overline{y}\in J(F).

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(0)=\{0\}.

(ii)

There exist constants
R>0,\ell>0
and an open neighborhood
V
of
\overline{y}

5.
Fermat’s rule at infinity

In this section we establish
necessary optimality conditions at infinity
for weak efficient values for set-valued optimization problems.

Let
K\subsetneq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a pointed closed convex cone with nonempty interior which defines a partial order
\leqslant_{K}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
by the relation:

\displaystyle y_{1}\leqslant_{K}y_{2}\quad\textrm{ if and only if }\quad y_{2}-y_{1}\in K.

Recall that the
positive polar
of
K
is defined to be the set

\displaystyle K^{+}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{c^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\langle c^{*},y\rangle\geq 0\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad y\in K\}.

By definition,
K^{+}
is a closed convex cone.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping of closed graph and
\Omega
be an unbounded closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Consider the set-valued optimization problem with explicit geometry constraints

(P)

\mathrm{minimize}_{K}\ F(x)\quad\textrm{ subject to }\quad x\in\Omega.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that
\overline{y}\in\mbox{\rm cl}\,F(\Omega)
is a
weak efficient value
for (
P
) if

\displaystyle\left(\overline{y}-{\rm int}K\right)\cap F(\Omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset.

Let
\overline{y}\in\mbox{\rm cl}\,F(\Omega)
be a weak efficient value for (
P
). It is well known in the theory of set-valued optimization (see, for example,
[
16
, Theorem 9.22 and Corollary 9.23]
) that if
\overline{y}\in F(\overline{x})

for some
\overline{x}\in\Omega
satisfying the constraint qualification

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})(0)\cap\big(-N_{\Omega}(\overline{x})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\},

then there exists a vector
c^{*}\in K^{+}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\overline{x},\overline{y})(c^{*})+N_{\Omega}(\overline{x}).

Here, we are interested in the case where
\overline{y}\not\in F(\Omega).
To this end, recall from
[
12
, Definition 3.1]
that the
normal cone to the set
\Omega
at infinity
is defined by

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\xrightarrow{\Omega}\infty}\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(x).

It is not hard see that (see
[
12
, Proposition 3.5]
)

\displaystyle N_{\Omega}(\infty)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\xrightarrow{\Omega}\infty}N_{\Omega}(x).

Now we are in a position to state our main result of this section concerning necessary optimality conditions for (
P
).

Theorem 5.2

(Fermat’s rule at infinity)
.

Let
\overline{y}\in\mbox{\rm cl}\,F(\Omega)\setminus F(\Omega)
be a weak efficient value for (
P
) satisfying the constraint qualification at infinity

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(0)\cap\big(-N_{\Omega}(\infty)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}.

Then there exists a vector
c^{*}\in K^{+}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}F(\infty,\overline{y})(c^{*})+N_{\Omega}(\infty).

In order to prove the theorem, we fix an element
e\in{\rm int}K
and define the function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle\varphi(y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf\{\lambda\in\mathbb{B}\mid\lambda e\in y+K\}.

Lemma 5.3
.

The following properties hold:

(i)

\varphi
is well defined and globally Lipschitz on
\mathbb{R}^{m}.

(ii)

For any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m},
we have

\displaystyle\partial\varphi(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{c^{*}\in K^{+}\mid\langle c^{*},e\rangle=1,\langle c^{*},y\rangle=\varphi(y)\}.

Proof.

See
[
7
, Lemma 2.4]
and
[
11
, Section 5.2]
.
∎

.

There are two cases to be considered.

Case 1:
\Omega

In this case,

Since
\overline{y}\in\mbox{\rm cl}\,F(\mathbb{R}^{n})\setminus F(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

If the sequence

Define the Lipschitz function

We first claim that

Note that

\displaystyle\inf_{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}}\big(\psi+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}\big)(x,y)

\displaystyle\inf_{(x,y)\in{\mathrm{gph}F}}\psi(x,y)\ =\ 0.

Case 1.1:
\psi(x_{k},y_{k})=0

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial\big(\psi+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}\big)(x_{k},y_{k}).

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial\psi(x_{k},y_{k})+\partial\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}(x_{k},y_{k})

\displaystyle\partial\psi(x_{k},y_{k})+N_{{\mathrm{gph}F}}\big((x_{k},y_{k})\big)

\displaystyle\{0\}\times\partial\varphi(y_{k}-\overline{y}).

Therefore, in view of Lemma

\displaystyle\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}},

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\partial\big(\psi+\delta_{\mathrm{gph}F}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}}\|\cdot-(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})\|\big)(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k}).

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{0\}\times\partial\varphi(\overline{y}_{k}-\overline{y})+N_{{\mathrm{gph}F}}(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k})+\sqrt{\epsilon_{k}}\mathbb{B}.

This, together with Lemma
5.3
, yields the existence of
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
with

and

with

such that

\displaystyle(u_{k},v_{k}-c^{*}_{k})

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle N_{{\mathrm{gph}F}}(\overline{x}_{k},\overline{y}_{k}).

As in the proof of Case 1.1, we can see that the sequence

has a cluster point

Since
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(0,0)
as

we get

which means that

Case 2:
\Omega
is a proper subset of

Define the set-valued mapping

Applying Case 1 to the set-valued mapping

(7)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle D^{*}(F+\Delta_{\Omega})(\infty,\overline{y})(c^{*}).

On the other hand, we know from Example
3.6
(iv) that

\displaystyle=

Observe that the assumptions in Theorem
3.8
with respect to the set-valued mappings

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in
