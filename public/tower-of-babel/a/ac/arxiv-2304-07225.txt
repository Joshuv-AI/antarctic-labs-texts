Distributed detection of ARMA signals
João Domingos, João Xavier

Abstract
This paper considers a distributed detection setup where agents in a network want to detect a time-varying signal embedded in temporally correlated noise. In contrast to most previous work, which considers only a constant signal embedded in temporally uncorrelated noise, in this paper both signal and noise can have a general auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) temporal structure. The signal of interest is the impulse response of an ARMA filter, and the noise is the output of yet another ARMA filter which is fed white Gaussian noise. For this extended problem setup, which can prompt novel behaviour, we propose a comprehensive solution. First, we extend the well-known running consensus detector (RCD) to this correlated setup; then, we design an efficient implementation of the RCD by exploiting the underlying ARMA structures; and, finally, we derive the theoretical asymptotic performance of the RCD in this ARMA setup. It turns out that the error probability at each agent exhibits one of two regimes: either (a) the error probability decays exponentially fast to zero or (b) it converges to a strictly positive error floor. While regime (a) spans staple results in large deviation theory, regime (b) is new in distributed detection and is elicited by the ARMA setup. We fully characterize these two scenarios: we give necessary and sufficient conditions, phrased in terms of the zero and poles of the underlying ARMA models, for the emergence of each regime, and provide closed-form expressions for both the decay rates of regime (a) and the positive error floors of regime (b). Our analysis also shows that the ARMA setup leads to two novel features: (1) the threshold level used in RCD can influence the asymptotics of the error probabilities and (2) some agents might be weakly informative , in the sense that their observations do not improve the asymptotic performance of RCD and, as such, can be safely muted to save sensing resources. Numerical simulations illustrate and confirm the theoretical findings.



I
Introduction

Binary detection addresses the problem of deciding which statistical source, out of two possible ones, generated some observed data.
Detection can be carried out in centralized or distributed setups, depending mainly on how the data is observed.
In centralized setups, a single agent observes the whole data and can address the decision problem by itself. In distributed setups the data is globally observed by multiple agents, but each agent observes only a local portion of it. Because each agent now observes only part of the global data, no single agent can decide by itself in an optimal way; agents are better off addressing the decision problem cooperatively.

I-A

Distributed setup

This paper considers detection in a distributed setup, in which the global data is a discrete-time, vector-valued signal of dimension
N

i

•

(noise only): the global data is just noise,

where both the signal and the noise have pre-specified—but otherwise generic—auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) temporal structures.

I-C

Contributions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that considers distributed detection with temporally correlated observation noise. In more detail, we claim three concrete contributions:

1.

By exploring the structure of ARMA filters, we show that the running consensus detector (RCD) can be efficiently implemented with computational and memory requirements independent of the number of observations;

2.

More importantly, we fully characterize the asymptotic performance of RCD, proving
that the probability of false alarm and miss detection can exhibit two asymptotic regimes: (a) the probabilities decay exponentially fast to zero, or (b) they converge to strictly positive error floors. Regime (b) is novel in the distributed context and arises from the generic ARMA setup. We
clarify which regime is triggered on the basis of the zeros and poles of the ARMA models at play, and derive closed-form expressions
for both the exponential decay rates and the error floors; such expressions reveal that the threshold constant used in RCD is irrelevant in regime (a) but highly relevant for the limiting error floors of regime (b).

3.

We show that the ARMA setup can also induce
weakly informative
agents. These are agents whose data cannot improve the asymptotic performance of the RCD. Their data can thus be discarded without hurting this asymptotic metric. Such property might be explored in network compression schemes where RCD could run in a (possibly much smaller) connected network obtained by pruning most (if not all) of the weakly informative agents.

II-B

\mathcal{Z}
transforms

The
\mathcal{Z}
-transform of a sequence
(x(k))_{k\geq 0}
is the complex-valued function

\displaystyle X(z^{-1})=\sum_{k\geq 0}x(k)\,z^{-k},\quad z\in\mathcal{ROC}\subseteq\mathbf{C},

(1)

where
\mathcal{ROC}:=\{z\in\mathbf{C}\colon\sum_{k\geq 0}|x(k)\,z^{-k}|<\infty\}
denotes its region of convergence (ROC). In this paper we consider only
\mathcal{Z}
-transforms that are rational,

\displaystyle X(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=A\,\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{q}(1-z_{j}\,z^{-1})}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}(1-p_{j}\,z^{-1})},\quad|z|>\rho,

(2)

with
A
being a constant,
the roots
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{q}
of the numerator polynomial being the zeros of
X(z^{-1})
, and the roots
\{p_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
of the denominator polynomial being the poles of
X(z^{-1})
; in this case,
\mathcal{ROC}:=\{z\colon|z|>\rho\}
, with
\rho:=\text{max}\{|p_{j}|\colon j\}
.

Partial fraction expansion.

It is well known that the sequence
\left(x(k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
can be retrieved back from its
\mathcal{Z}
-transform. For rational
\mathcal{Z}
-transforms such retrieval can be carried out by the standard technique of partial fraction expansion: assuming that
X(z^{-1})
in (
2
) is strictly proper (orders
p,q
satisfy
p>q
) and has only simple poles (
p_{m}\neq p_{n}
for
m\neq n
), first represent
X(z^{-1})
as

\displaystyle X(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{r_{j}}{1-p_{j}\,z^{-1}},

(3)

with residuals
r_{j}
given by

\displaystyle r_{j}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{j}\,z^{-1})\,X(z^{-1})\Big|_{z=p_{j}};

(4)

now, invert each of the
p
simple fractions to get

\displaystyle x(k)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\,r_{j}\,p_{j}^{k},

(5)

which shows that the sequence
\left(x(k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
is a sum of
p
discrete-time complex exponential signals
p_{j}^{k}
.

ARMA filters.
Consider an autoregressive moving average (ARMA)
(p,q)
filter with coefficients
\{b(j)\}_{j=1}^{p},\{a(j)\}_{j=1}^{q}
, and let
\left(x(k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
be an input signal, with
\left(y(k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
being the corresponding output signal. The input and output signals are related in the time-domain by the linear recursion

\displaystyle y(k)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}b(j)\,y(k-j)+x(k)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}a(j)\,x(k-j),

(6)

(with the understanding that
x(k)=0
for
k<0
)
and in the
\mathcal{Z}
-domain by

\displaystyle Y(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q}b(j)\,z^{-j}}{1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}a(j)\,z^{-j}}\,X(z^{-1}).

(7)

The nomenclature autoregressive moving average appears since, in (
6
), we have
p

and
q
moving average terms of

. Equations (
6
) and (
7
) are equivalent representations of an ARMA

filter, the time representation (

) being more useful for analysis.

We address the problem of designing a collaborative algorithm that advances through local interactions between agents and leads them to collectively decide which hypothesis,

III-A

Sensing and communication models

Specifically, under hypothesis

\displaystyle H_{1}

\displaystyle:\kern 5.0pty_{i}(k)=\theta_{i}(k)+n_{i}(k),\kern 5.0pti=1,\dots,N

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle:\kern 5.0pty_{i}(k)=n_{i}(k),\kern 5.0pti=1,\dots,N,

We assume that both the noise

\displaystyle n_{i}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{i}}a_{i}(j)\,n_{i}(k-j)+\sigma\,\epsilon_{i}(k)+\sigma\,\sum_{j=1}^{q_{i}}b_{i}(j)\,\epsilon_{i}(k-j)\,

(10)

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{p}_{i}}\bar{a}_{i}(j)\,\theta_{i}(k-j)+A\,\delta(k)+A\,\sum_{j=1}^{\bar{q}_{i}}\bar{b}_{i}(j)\,\delta(k-j),

III-B

Assumptions

We work under the following three assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

(Noise is spatially independent)

For each time instant
k
, the additive noise
n_{i}(k)
is independent across agents, meaning that the vector
n(k)=\{n_{i}(k)\}_{i=1}^{N}
has independent components. Note that, however, each component
\left(n_{i}(k)\right)_{k\geq 0}
can be temporally correlated;

Assumption 2
.

(Network is connected)

The communication network
G
is a connected graph, that is, there exists a path (possibly, with multiple hops) joining any two agents.

This is a common (and necessary) assumption, for, otherwise, the observations of isolated agents could not be taken into account. For further reference, we associate with
G
a pre-chosen symmetric
weight
matrix
W\in\mathbf{R}^{N\times N}
that conforms to the sparsity of the network (
W_{i,j}=0
if and only if
j\notin\mathcal{N}_{i}
) and enjoys an eigenvalue decomposition of the form

\displaystyle W=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbf{1}_{N}&{S}\end{pmatrix}}_{Q_{W}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&\\
&D\end{pmatrix}}_{\Lambda_{W}}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{T}\\
{S}^{T}\end{pmatrix}}_{Q_{W}^{T}},

(12)

where
D\in\mathbf{R}^{(N-1)\times(N-1)}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries

satisfying

|d_{n}|<1
, for
1\leq n\leq N-1
, and
S\in\mathbf{R}^{N\times(N-1)}
has orthonormal columns (
S^{T}S=I
), each column orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}
, that is
S^{T}\boldsymbol{1}_{N}=0
.

Note that
W
is therefore both row-stochastic (
W\mathbf{1}_{N}=\mathbf{1}_{N}
) and column-stochastic (
\boldsymbol{1}_{N}^{T}W=\boldsymbol{1}_{N}^{T}
). It is well known that such matrices exist and can even be obtained in a distributed way, for instance, when

is filled with Metropolis weights.

Assumption 3
.

(ARMA Models)

For each agent
i
, the rational
\mathcal{Z}
-transform

\displaystyle\dfrac{A}{\sigma}\frac{1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{q_{i}}a_{i}(j)\,z^{-j}}{1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p_{i}}b_{i}(j)\,z^{-j}}\,\frac{1+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\bar{q}_{i}}\bar{b}_{i}(j)\,z^{-j}}{1-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\bar{p}_{i}}\bar{a}_{i}(j)\,z^{-j}}

is assumed to have no zero-pole cancellations (this entails no loss of generality). Let

and

be its number of zeros and poles, respectively, with the

poles denoted by
\{p_{i,1},p_{i,2},\ldots,p_{i,P_{i}}\}
. We assume

(the

-transform is strictly proper), all poles are simple (

) and

\displaystyle 1\geq\rho_{i}=|p_{i,1}|>|p_{i,2}|\geq\cdots\geq|p_{i,P_{i}}|.

(14)

So the
\mathcal{Z}
-transform of each agent is stable and the maximum absolute value of its poles, denoted

, is attained by a unique pole (the pole denoted by

IV
Extending the running consensus detector (RCD) to the ARMA setup

We propose that agents solve cooperatively the global decision problem by resorting to an extension of the running consensus detector (RCD)
[
16
,
32
,
37
]
. Our extension retains the generic template of the standard RCD and works as follows: at each time
k
, each agent
i
forms its updated local decision variable
l_{i}(k)\in\mathbf{R}
by combining in an affine manner the latest decision variables
l_{j}(k-1)
communicated by its neighbours
j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}
with the history of its own local observations

\boldsymbol{y}_{i}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}y_{i}(0)\\
y_{i}(1)\\
\vdots\\
y_{i}(k)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbf{R}^{k+1}.

Specifically, stack the local decision variables
l_{i}(k)
,
1\leq i\leq N
, in a global decision vector
l{(k)}\in\mathbf{R}^{N}
; we suggest that this vector follow the dynamics

\displaystyle l{(k)}

\displaystyle=W\,l{(k-1)}+\eta{(k)},

(15)

for
k\geq 0
(with initial conditions
l(-1)=0
), with
\eta(k)\in\mathbf{R}^{N}
being an innovation vector where each component
i
depends in an affine manner on the history of local observations of agent
i
, that is,

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(k)=\mathcal{A}_{i,k}\big(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}(k)\big).

(16)

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{i,k}:\mathbf{R}^{k+1}\mapsto\mathbf{R}
is an affine map to be detailed in the next section
V
. The dynamics (
15
) has a simple interpretation: the first term on the right-hand side blends local decision variables among neighbors, while the second term blends past observations of each agent with own latest ones.

Once each agent
i
has formed its updated local decision variable
l_{i}(k)
, it can immediately use it to produce a local decision
D_{i}(k)\in\{0,1\}
about the global environment:

\displaystyle D_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{if }l_{i}(k)\geq\gamma\\
0,&\mbox{if }l_{i}(k)<\gamma,\end{cases}

(17)

where the threshold
\gamma\in\mathbf{R}
is a design parameter; intuitively, lowering
\gamma
biases the decision towards
H_{1}
and vice-versa.
We discuss the choice of
\gamma
after establishing the long run performance of detectors (
17
) (see the discussion immediately after Theorem
3
). For now,
\gamma
is just a fixed parameter.

V
Implementing efficiently the extended RCD

In this section, we first propose an affine map
\mathcal{A}_{i,k}

to be used in (
16

\mathcal{A}_{i,k}
acts on an ever-expanding history vector

V-A

Motivating
\mathcal{A}_{i,k}
through a single-agent network

Consider a network with
N=1

k
, the sole agent

V-B

Obtaining recursively the Cholesky decomposition of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}{(k)}

Exploiting signal structures, such as ARMA, to devise recursive algorithms has been a key idea in statistical signal processing, e.g., in filtering based on the innovations process
[
41
]
. For example, in Section 4.4 of
[
41
]
Kailath et.al provide efficient implementations of the L-D-L and Cholesky decompositions of an exponentially correlated process. Similar approaches can be deployed, here, to recursively obtain the Cholesky factor
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}(k)
in (

Theorem 1
.

(Recursion for the Cholesky factor)

The Cholesky factor
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}(k)
in (
24
) is given by

\boldsymbol{L}_{i}(k)=\sigma\,\big(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k)}\big)^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{B}_{i}{(k)},

where the strictly lower-triangular matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k)}
and the lower-triangular matrices
\boldsymbol{B}_{i}{(k)}
obey the recursion

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k)}&0\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(k+1)^{T}&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(34)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k+1)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)&0\\
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}(k+1)^{T}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

The vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}(k)
in (
35
) start at
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(0)=0
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}(0)=1
and are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}(k+1)^{T}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(a_{i}(k+1)\,\dots\,a_{i}(1)\Big)&,k+1\leq p_{i}\\[5.0pt]
\Big(0_{k+1-p_{i}}^{T}\,\,a_{i}(p_{i})\,\dots\,a_{i}(1)\Big)&,k+1>p_{i},\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}(k+1)^{T}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\Big(b_{i}(k+1)\,\dots\,b_{i}(1)\Big)&,k+1\leq q_{i}\\[5.0pt]
\Big(0_{k+1-q_{i}}^{T}\,\,b_{i}(q_{i})\,\dots\,b_{i}(1)\Big)&,k+1>q_{i}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

From (
10
), vector
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}(k)
in (
20
) satisfies

\displaystyle(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}(k))\boldsymbol{n}_{i}(k)=\sigma\,\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(k),

(40)

with matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{i}(k)
and
\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)
defined as above, and

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(k)
denoting a zero-mean white Gaussian vector:
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(k)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{k+1})
. Because
I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}(k)
is a lower triangular matrix with ones on the main diagonal, its inverse exists (and is also lower triangular with ones in the main diagonal). This yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{i}(k)=\sigma\,(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}(k))^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(k).

(41)

Thus, the covariance of
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}(k)
is

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}(k)=\boldsymbol{L}_{i}{(k)}\,\boldsymbol{L}_{i}{(k)}^{T}
, with
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}(k)=\sigma\,\big(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k)}\big)^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{B}_{i}{(k)}
, as claimed. Finally, note that
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}{(k)}
, being the product of two lower-triangular matrices with positive diagonal entries, is itself such a matrix; thus the mentioned
\boldsymbol{L}_{i}{(k)}
is indeed the Cholesky factor of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}(k)
.
∎

.

(ARMA filter interpretation)

From

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sigma\,(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}(k))^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}_{i}(k)}_{\boldsymbol{L}_{i}{(k)}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}(k)=\boldsymbol{z}_{i}(k)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{i}{(k)}\,\widehat{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma}\big(I_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{A}_{i}{(k)}\big)\,\boldsymbol{z}_{i}{(k)}.

To conclude, agent

VI
Theoretical analysis of RCD

In this section we analyze the performance of the local RCD detectors (
17
).
Specifically, for the local detector of agent

, we study the long time behaviour (

) of the two error probabilities, that is, the probabilities of false alarm and miss,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{F,i}(k)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(D_{i}(k)=1),

(48)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{M,i}(k)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{H_{1}}(D_{i}(k)=0).

. Hereafter, we denote by

Which regime is activated depends on the growing rate of

(50)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{1}(k)\\
\vdots\\
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{N}(k)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbf{R}^{N(k+1)},

(51)

with
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}(k)
defined in (
26
). Note that

where

is as in (
32
);

(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(m))
from

.

We break up the analysis of the error probabilities in two steps, summarized as follows:

1.

Assuming, momentarily, that the sequence (

where

is one of the time-scalings in
\mathcal{F}
, theorem

\displaystyle-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}_{F,i}(k)}{f(k)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\beta_{F}

(53)

\displaystyle-\frac{\log\mathbb{P}_{M,i}(k)}{f(k)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\beta_{M},

(54)

as

, for some explicit constants

. Thus, in regime (a), the probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{F,i}(k)
and

]
,
[

]
,
[

]

In sharp contrast, for the novel regime (b), the probabilities of error settle into a positive error floor, so

In Theorem

VIII
Conclusion

This paper considers a distributed detection setup in which both the signal and noise are allowed to follow generic ARMA models, a flexibility that allows to imprint almost any desired temporal correlation in the noise signal by approximating
\mathcal{Z}
transforms.

To handle this ARMA setup, we suggested an extension of the running consensus detector (RCD) and showed that it can be implemented efficiently at each agent,
with computational requirements independent of time and proportional to the sparsity of the communication network.

As our theoretical analysis shows, the ARMA setup can lead to a novel regime in the asymptotic behavior of the error probabilities. Specifically, in simple standard (non-ARMA) setups, the probabilities of error of the local detectors typically decay exponentially fast to zero; the ARMA setting, however, can trigger an additional regime in which the probabilities of error converge to a positive error floor. We fully characterize these two regimes by deriving closed-form expressions for both the exponential decay rates and the positive error floors, as a function of the underlying ARMA models. Our analysis also reveals two features not present in standard detection literature. Firstly, the threshold constant used in RCD greatly influences the error floors attained in the additional regime. Secondly, the ARMA setup can lead to a split of agents into strongly informative ones and weakly informative ones, the latter being agents whose measurements can be safely discarded (or not made at all) if so desired, for these measurements do not impact the asymptotic performance of RCD. Our theoretical findings are confirmed by a set of numerical simulations.
