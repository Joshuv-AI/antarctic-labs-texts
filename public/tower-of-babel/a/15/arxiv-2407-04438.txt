Statistical reduced order modelling for the parametric Helmholtz equation
Lucas Hermann, Matthias Bollhöfer, Ulrich Römer

Abstract
Predictive modeling involving simulation and sensor data at the same time, is a growing challenge in computational science. Even with large-scale finite element models, a mismatch to the sensor data often remains, which can be attributed to different sources of uncertainty. For such a scenario, the statistical finite element method (statFEM) can be used to condition a simulated field on given sensor data. This yields a posterior solution which resembles the data much better and additionally provides consistent estimates of uncertainty, including model misspecification. For frequency or parameter dependent problems, occurring, e.g. in acoustics or electromagnetism, solving the full order model at the frequency grid and conditioning it on data quickly results in a prohibitive computational cost. In this case, the introduction of a surrogate in form of a reduced order model yields much smaller systems of equations. In this paper, we propose a reduced order statFEM framework relying on Krylov-based moment matching. We introduce a data model which explicitly includes the bias induced by the reduced approximation, which is estimated by an inexpensive error indicator. The results of the new statistical reduced order method are compared to the standard statFEM procedure applied to a ROM prior, i.e. without explicitly accounting for the reduced order bias. The proposed method yields better accuracy and faster convergence throughout a given frequency range for different numerical examples.



keywords
statFEM, Moment Matching, Gaussian Processes, Adjoint error estimator

1
Introduction

In engineering and physics, but also in many other disciplines such as chemistry and finance, partial differential equations (PDEs)
Borthwick 2016
serve as a very fundamental mathematical description of a given problem. Generally, most PDEs are hard or impossible to solve analytically. To find an approximation of the solution, numerical methods such as the finite element method (FEM)
Brenner and Scott 2008
are used. Both the model assumptions, i.e. choice of the differential operator, parameters and boundary conditions, which lead to the PDE and the approximative nature of the numerical method contribute to a discrepancy between the solution of the numerical model and the physical reality. Collecting data with sensors may yield relatively accurate, albeit noisy, observations at discrete locations in the computational domain. The numerical solution, on the other hand, yields a continuous solution at every point in the domain. Combining the numerical solution with the sparse observation data to estimate the state of a system is referred to as
data assimilation

Law et al. 2015
. One data assimilation technique is the recently proposed statistical FEM (statFEM)
Girolami et al. 2021
. In statFEM, a statistical model for measured sensor data is introduced in which the data is additively composed of the numerical FE solution, a model error and measurement noise. All these terms are modelled as Gaussian Processes (GP)
Rasmussen and Williams 2005
, which enables data assimilation through Bayesian principles. The stochastic solution of the numerical problem is considered as a so-called Bayesian
prior
. This prior can be conditioned on measurement data to lead to an improved solution, called the
posterior
. Accounting for uncertainty in the FE model, a Gaussian prior for the state is obtained by first-order-second moment analysis. Despite acknowledging the uncertainty, the model may still be misspecified, in particular for complex physical processes. Hence, a model error GP is introduced, which is assumed to be mean-free, i.e. a possible discrepancy between model and reality will be considered through a higher variance of the model error. Adopting an empirical Bayes approach, the hyperparameters of the covariance function are learned from the data first, before the solution GP is conditioned on the data, where measurement error is also accounted for.

Some engineering problems require solving PDEs depending on deterministic parameters in addition to the uncertain model inputs. This concerns frequency domain problems, i.e. the Helmholtz equation in acoustics
Ihlenburg 1998
, Maxwell’s equations in electromagnetism
Maxwell 1865
or the equations of motion in structural dynamics
Timoshenko and Young 1990
. Concerning the latter, possible use cases can be found e.g. in the domain of structural health monitoring, where the state of e.g. a large scale structure
Rytter 1993
;
Farrar et al. 2001
or smaller scale aerospace turbine components is constantly observed by means of sparsely located sensors and a structural dynamics model which has to be updated for the full frequency range once new measurements come in
Heffernan et al. 2022
. The resulting FEM models can become very complex and expensive to solve even for a single frequency. Hence, computing a parameter sweep, i.e. solving the system at many frequencies within a desired interval, quickly becomes prohibitively expensive. Through reduced order models (ROM), the solution of e.g. a FEM system of equations with a large number of degrees of freedom (DOF) can be approximated by solving a significantly smaller ROM system of equations. The most prominent techniques for model order reduction are projection based ROM methods
Benner et al. 2015
. Examples of those are proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) or Krylov subspace methods. As, in general, neither of these ROM methods are a perfectly accurate surrogate for the FEM, also the ROM approximation error has to be taken into account, see for instance
Silva et al. 2023
. In contrast to the model to reality error, this ROM approximation error has a well-known structure, governed by the error, or residual, equation.

Here, an adjoint based error estimator
Hartmann 2008
;
Paul Rouse et al. 2022
is derived which doesn’t involve solving the full order system matrix. This additional error is then included into the data model. In principle, the ROM error could also be estimated as part of the model error. Here, this will be referred to as the classical approach. However, as it is possible to estimate the ROM error without solving the full system, it seems sensible to separate it from the general model error and hence have a better prior for the total error.
In this paper, a statistical reduced order modeling approach, referred to as statROM, is introduced. It is shown that this approach leads to a better approximation of the data generating process and faster convergence of the error. The method is applied to a FEM approximation of the Helmholtz equation in 1D with few DOFs to study the convergence properties in much detail. Data are generated from a fine FEM discretisation. The reduced order model is constructed with a moment matching Krylov subspace method. Next, a 2D Helmholtz scattering problem with significantly more DOFs is evaluated to study how the method scales.

The paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, the Helmholtz equation is introduced as the model problem. Section 3 outlines ROM and moment matching methods in particular. Also, an adjoint based ROM error estimator is derived. In Section 4, GPs and statFEM are introduced before deriving the statROM procedure. Section 5 is dedicated to numerical examples. Lastly, we draw a conclusion.

2
Model problem

We consider the Helmholtz equation as an example of a frequency dependent PDE, which serves as a model problem, e.g. for wave-based non-destructive testing applications. More specifically, the model PDE is given as

\displaystyle\Delta u(\bm{x})+k^{2}\kappa(\bm{x})u(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=-f(\bm{x}),\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }D,

(1)

\displaystyle u(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=d(\bm{x}),\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}},

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(\bm{x})}{\partial n}

\displaystyle=g(\bm{x}),\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}},

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(\bm{x})}{\partial n}-\mathrm{i}k\beta u(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}},

(4)

on a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d=1,2,3
, where
\partial D
is a Lipschitz boundary for
d\geq 2
that can be approximated exactly by polygons or polyhedra. A sound soft boundary condition can be realized with
d(\bm{x})=0
. The Neumann boundary can be separated into a homogeneous, i.e. sound hard reflecting part
\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},0}
and an inhomogeneous part
\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},g}
, where
g\neq 0
. Damping can be considered by introducing the Robin boundary condition on
\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}
, where
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}
and/or
\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}
may be empty. Furthermore, there holds
k\in\mathbb{R}=\frac{\omega}{c}
, for the wave number, with
\omega
denoting the angular frequency and
c
the speed of sound. Several quantities in the model problem are assumed to be unknown and hence, exhibit uncertainty. These are the material parameter
\kappa
, the inhomogeneity in the Neumann boundary condition
g
and also the forcing
f
.

The corresponding weak formulation is posed on the (complex) function space

V=H^{1}(D)=\{v\in L^{2}(D)\ |\ \|v\|_{V}^{2}=\int_{D}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v^{*}dx+\int_{D}vv^{*}dx<\infty\},

(5)

where
v^{*}
denotes the complex conjugate of
v
, and the subspace

V_{0}=\{v\in V\ |\ v|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}=0\}.

(6)

The weak formulation reads: find
u\in V_{0}
such that

s(u,v)-k^{2}m(u,v)-ik\beta d(u,v)=F(v),\ \forall v\in V,

(7)

where the different quantities are defined as

\displaystyle s(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v^{*}dx,

\displaystyle\text{(stiffness)},

(8)

\displaystyle m(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\kappa uv^{*}dx,

\displaystyle\text{(mass)},

(9)

\displaystyle d(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{I}}}uv^{*}dx,

\displaystyle\text{(damping)},

(10)

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},g}}gv^{*}dx+\int_{D}fv^{*}dx

\displaystyle\text{(forcing)},

(11)

see
Atalla and Sgard 2015
;
Ihlenburg and Babuška 1995
for details on the weak formulation.

We introduce a conforming tetrahedral discretization of the domain and lowest order, piece-wise continuous, basis functions. For details, the reader is referred to
Brenner and Scott 2008
;
Logg et al. 2012
. Let
V_{0,h},V_{h}
denote the corresponding discrete function spaces with and without homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition. Then, the discrete formulation reads: find
u_{h}\in V_{0,h}
such that

s(u_{h},v_{h})-k^{2}m(u_{h},v_{h})-ik\beta d(u_{h},v_{h})=F(v_{h}),\ \forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.

(12)

To prove that for all non-resonant
\omega\in[\omega_{\text{min}},\omega_{\text{max}}]
the weak formulation has a unique solution, continuity of
s,m,d,f
and a Gårding inequality to invoke the Fredholm alternative are required. Details on the proof and the required assumptions can be found in
Ihlenburg 1998
and will not be discussed in any detail here.

After FE-discretization, we obtain the following system of equations

\underbrace{(\bm{S}-k^{2}\bm{M}-ik\beta\bm{D})}_{=:\bm{A}}\bm{u}=\bm{f},

(13)

with
\bm{M}
the mass matrix,
\bm{D}
the damping matrix and
\bm{S}
the stiffness matrix. In (
13
),
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
denotes the system matrix,
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
the nodal coefficients and
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the source vector.

At this stage, we also introduce random influences, which model unknown material parameters and forcings. More precisely,
\kappa,f,g
will be modelled as a random fields on a common probability space
(\Theta,\Sigma,\mathcal{P})
and we obtain

\bm{A}(\omega,\theta)\bm{u}(\omega,\theta)=\bm{f}(\theta),\ \forall\omega\in[\omega_{\text{min}},\omega_{\text{max}}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}

(14)

almost surely, where
\theta\in\Theta
denotes an elementary random outcome. The dependence of the Helmholtz equation on a high-dimensional parameter vector, originating from a random field approximation, poses additional challenges, which have recently attracted interest in the context of forward uncertainty quantification
Spence and Wunsch 2023
.

3
Reduced Order Modelling

In many situations in practice, the size of the discrete system
N
is very large, which makes it impossible to solve problem (
13
) for many different values of the angular frequency
\omega
or to efficiently perform an uncertainty analysis. Therefore, from the FEM system of equations, a Reduced Order Model (ROM) is typically derived, which can be used for multi-query tasks. As the model problem is defined in frequency domain, an uncertainty analysis is especially expensive, because it needs to be repeated for each point in the frequency domain. With a suitable ROM that describes the underlying FE-problem accurately, a tremendous reduction of numerical cost can be achieved, which in turn enables time-critical applications where, for instance, the model has to be conditioned on new incoming sensor data very frequently.

Throughout the paper, projection based ROMs
Benner et al. 2015
will be used, where a projection matrix
\bm{V}
projects the system components onto a much smaller space through Galerkin projection.
We will consider multiple ROMs, which are constructed for specific random realizations. Hence, we fix
\theta
in this subsection and omit it, for simplicity. The FEM and corresponding ROM system read

FEM

\displaystyle:\quad\bm{A}(\omega)\bm{u}(\omega)=\bm{f},

(15)

ROM

\displaystyle:\quad\bm{A}_{r}(\omega)\bm{u}_{r}(\omega)=\bm{f}_{r},

(16)

where
\bm{A}_{r}(\omega)\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r},\bm{u}_{r}(\omega),\bm{f}_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{r}
and
r\ll N
. The FE solution and the ROM solution vector are connected by a projection matrix
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times r}
, as

\bm{u}(\omega)\approx\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r}(\omega)=\tilde{\bm{u}}(\omega),

(17)

as well as
\bm{A}_{r}=\bm{V}^{*}\bm{A}\bm{V}
and
\bm{f}_{r}=\bm{V}^{*}\bm{f}
. For the projection matrix there holds
\bm{V}^{*}\bm{V}=\bm{I}
, hence it is orthogonal.

There are various methods available to construct the projection matrix. Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) is based on so-called snapshots of the FE model. For these, the FE system is solved at a set of parameter samples and the resulting vectors are stored as columns in a matrix. The columns of
\bm{V}
are then spanned by the first
r
principal components. POD is not the most efficient method for the model problem under consideration. Hence, a different method, introduced in the next subsection, will be used in this work.

A class of projection based ROMs which is especially well suited for dynamical systems
Römer et al. 2021
are the moment matching methods. We adopt the adaptive order rational Arnoldi-type method (AORA) as proposed in
Bodendiek and Bollhöfer 2013
and employ in particular the formulation for second order systems as put forth in
Römer et al. 2021
. Moment matching is based on rational interpolation
Berrut et al. 2005
and the resulting ROM satisfies the Hermite interpolation conditions up to some order
Benner et al. 2015
. To generate a projection matrix
\bm{V}
which has the moment matching property, the system components of the dynamical 2nd order system (
12
) are first used to derive a generalized Krylov subspace

\mathcal{K}(\bm{M},\bm{D},\bm{S},\bm{f})=G_{k}(-\bm{A}(\bar{\omega})^{-1}\bm{A}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega}),-\bm{A}(\bar{\omega})^{-1}i\bm{M},\bm{A}(\bar{\omega})^{-1}\bm{f})

(18)

with

G_{k}(\bm{L},\bm{B},\bm{s})=\mathrm{span}\{\bm{s}_{0},\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{m-1}\},\;\bm{s}_{0}=\bm{s},\bm{s}_{1}=\bm{L}\bm{s}_{0},\,\\
\text{and}\,\bm{s}_{l}=\bm{L}\bm{s}_{l-1}+\bm{B}\bm{s}_{l-2}\,\text{for}\,l\geq 2

(19)

in the case of only a single expansion frequency
\bar{\omega}
and the number of matched moments
m
. Here,
\bm{A}^{\prime}(\bar{\omega})=-2\bar{\omega}\bm{M}+i\beta/c\bm{D}
. Then, one possibility to build the actual projection matrix is using a second-order rational Arnoldi-type method. This guarantees orthonormality in the columns of
\bm{V}
. In principle, Arnoldi-type methods work by transforming a matrix
\bm{A}
into the upper Hessenberg form
\bm{H}
by

\bm{A}=\bm{V}\bm{H}\bm{V}^{*}\;.

(20)

This happens iteratively and can be stopped after a certain number of iterations in order to obtain a reduced order approximation of
\bm{A}
.
With the projection matrix from the rational Arnoldi method, there holds for the reduced order system

(-\omega^{2}\bm{M}_{r}+ik\beta\bm{D}_{r}+\bm{S}_{r})\bm{u}_{r}(\omega)=\bm{f}_{r},

(21)

where
\bm{M}_{r}=\bm{V}^{*}\bm{M}\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{r\times r}
and similar expressions hold for
\bm{S}
and
\bm{D}
. The error of this moment matching ROM method depends on the number of matched moments
m
and the choice of the expansion frequencie(s)
\bar{\omega}
. The transfer function of the ROM matches the Taylor series coefficients up to order
m
of its full order counterpart, expanded around
\bar{\omega}
.

4
Statistical Reduced Order Models

In this section we introduce a statistical model based on the reduced order framework of Section
3
. We first show, how a ROM can be used to efficiently carry out uncertainty forward propagation, followed by a presentation of the statFEM and statROM procedure, respectively.

4.1
Forward Problem

The statFEM approach
Girolami et al. 2021
is in its core based on Gaussian Processes (GPs), which form a class of Bayesian non-parametric models that are well established in statistics and machine learning
Murphy 2012
. In a first step, a GP model over the FE solution is formed, which is obtained by propagating uncertainty in material parameters, the geometry or excitations through the FE model.
Here, to account for the uncertainty both in the material parameter and the right-hand-side, a ROM is used. Then, in a second step, data can be used for updating the state and for reducing the prediction uncertainty. In this way, statFEM makes use of GPs to fuse a solution from a FE solver with data. When modeling the state as a GP, normally distributed model and data errors give rise to a normally distributed posterior, hence Bayesian updating can be carried out in closed form. This in turn is convenient for a monitoring perspective, where fast sampling free updating procedures are desired.

In our model, the uncertainty is in the simplest case assumed to be present in the inhomogeneous Neumann boundary condition. More precisely, we assume
g
to be a random field

g(\bm{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{g}(\bm{x}),c_{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})),

(22)

with the covariance defined via the Matérn kernel

c_{g}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|}{l}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\|\bm{x}-\bm{x}^{\prime}\|}{l}\right),

(23)

with the scaling parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\Gamma(\nu)
the Gamma function and
K_{\nu}
a modified Bessel function
(
Abramowitz and Stegun 1965
, p.84 ff.)
,
Rasmussen and Williams 2005
. Moreover,
l\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the lengthscale parameter and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
controls the smoothness of the kernel.
For
\nu\to\infty
(
23
) becomes the squared exponential kernel. The Matérn kernel is used here because of its flexibility to represent variable smoothness.

We employ the following discrete representations of the mean and covariance function of the corresponding forcing term
\bm{f}_{g}
,

(\bm{\mu}_{g})_{i}=\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},g}}\mu_{g}\phi_{i}^{*}\mathrm{d}x,

(24)

where
\phi_{i}(\bm{x})
denotes the nodal FE basis function associated to node
\bm{x}_{i}
of the mesh, as well as

(\bm{C}_{g})_{i,j}=\left(\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},g}}\phi_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)c_{g}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})\left(\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N},g}}\phi_{j}^{*}\,\mathrm{d}x\right).

(25)

In view of the linear dependence of
\bm{f}_{g}
on
g
, the source term can be represented by a multivariate Gaussian

\bm{f}_{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{g},\bm{C}_{g}).

(26)

We also assume that the remaining stochastic forcing over the domain, which by a slight abuse of notation is again denoted with
f
, is a Gaussian random field

f(\bm{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{f}(\bm{x}),c_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}))

(27)

with
c_{f}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})
defined again via the Matérn kernel.
Applying the same discretization principle as before, we obtain

\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{f},\bm{C}_{f}).

(28)

We further consider uncertainty in the material parameter, given as

\kappa(\bm{x})\approx\exp(\mu_{\kappa}(\bm{x})+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\Psi_{i}(\bm{x})\xi_{i}),

(29)

obtained as the Karhunen-Loève expansion of the covariance
c_{\ln(\kappa)}
,
where
\xi_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are independent and
(\lambda_{i},\bm{\Psi}_{i})
are the eigenpairs of the covariance operator induced by
c_{\ln(\kappa)}
. The expansion has been truncated after
k
terms according to some truncation criterion, e.g., the fraction of explained variance.

Remark
.

Complex-valued Gaussian random vectors can be characterized by the mean, covariance and pseudo-covariance. The pseudo-covariance can play an important role in GP regression, see for instance
Bect et al. 2023
. Here, we treat the real and imaginary parts independently with individual Gaussian distributions based on the same covariance, which corresponds to setting the pseudo-covariance to zero. This is referred to as circular case in the signal processing literature.

Now the system matrix depends on the Gaussian vector
\bm{\xi}
and the stochastic parametric FOM reads

\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}(\theta))\bm{u}(\theta)=\bm{f}(\theta)+\bm{f}_{g}(\theta),

(30)

where
\bm{\xi},\bm{f},\bm{f}_{g}
are independent random vectors on the same probability space.

In the original statFEM paper
Girolami et al. 2021
, the authors employ a perturbation approach to propagate forward probabilistic material coefficients that appear in the FEM system matrix. We, instead, apply a Quasi Monte Carlo (QMC) method
Morokoff and Caflisch 1995
with the joint input
\bm{\eta}=(\bm{\xi}^{\top},\bm{f}^{\top},\bm{f}_{g}^{\top})^{\top}
to approximate the prior mean and covariance of
\bm{u}
. To this end, let
\bm{\eta}^{(i)}
, denote a sequence based on digital nets with random shifting
Kuo and Nuyens 2016
, where the inverse CDF is used to rescale the QMC points initially defined on
[0,1]
. Note that, because of the linearity of
\bm{A}
, the randomness in the right-hand-side could be propagated analytically to the solution. However, we employ the QMC sampling approach in the same way for all uncertain inputs, for simplicity. We construct for each sample
\bm{\xi}^{(i)}
the ROM projection matrix
\bm{V}^{(i)}
as defined in Section
3
.
Then, we can efficiently solve the system

(\bm{V}^{(i)})^{*}\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)})\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}^{(i)}(\omega)=(\bm{V}^{(i)})^{*}(\bm{f}^{(i)}+\bm{f}_{g}^{(i)}),

(31)

for a large number of frequency points
\omega
and right-hand-sides and repeat the procedure for different
\bm{\xi}^{(i)}
.

Compared to the QMC method, a perturbation approach or surrogate methods, such as generalized polynomial chaos (gPC)
Xiu and Karniadakis 2002
, would be faster for a small numbers of parameters. The QMC approach is however well-suited for handling the moderately large amount of uncertain inputs, while yielding a higher order convergence compared to pure MC.

After applying the standard QMC-estimates of the mean and covariance function based on the sample points
\bm{u}^{(i)}
, the final ROM-QMC approximated, parameter-dependent, GP prior reads

\bm{u}(\omega)\approx\tilde{\bm{u}}(\omega)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}(\omega),\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}(\omega)).

(32)

4.2
statFEM

The statFEM, introduced in
Girolami et al. 2021
, is a method to assimilate data with a stochastic FE-based prior model. Contrary to traditional Bayesian parameter estimation methods, which infer an unknown model parameter, statFEM updates the
state
, i.e., the FE-solution. The statFEM relies on GP models for the state, the data and the unknown model error, which are connected through the statistical generating model

\bm{y}=\bm{z}+\bm{e}=\rho\bm{P}\bm{u}+\bm{d}+\bm{e}.

(33)

In (
33
), the data
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
are decomposed into the true but unknown states at the
n_{y}
sensor locations
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
and the data error
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
. The true state
\bm{z}
can further be expressed as a combination of the FE prediction
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, scaled by an unknown factor
\rho
and projected onto the sensor locations with the projection matrix
\bm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times N}
, and a statistical representation of the model error
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}
.

For the time being, we assume that the prior
p(\bm{u})\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}},\bm{C}_{\bm{u}})
is fully available and postpone the discussion on numerical approximations errors. Then, the model error is given the difference between the exact model response and the true state at the sensor locations and essentially a deterministic quantity, that can be reduced by improving the model. The choice of modeling
\bm{d}
in a statistical way is made out of convenience, but also to improve the data assimilation accuracy, see
Calvetti et al. 2018
for example. More precisely, following
Girolami et al. 2021
we set

\bm{d}\sim p(\bm{d}|\bm{\alpha})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{C_{\bm{d}}}(\bm{\alpha}))

(34)

with
\bm{\alpha}
the hyperparameters of the chosen kernel function. Here, the Matérn kernel (
23
) is also chosen for the model error. The hyperparameters are estimated by maximising the marginal likelihood, i.e. the likelihood of the data given the hyperparameters
p(\bm{\bm{y}|\bm{\alpha}})
. This is detailed in Section
4.4
. Please note, that the scale of the model error will be reflected in the parameter
\sigma
of (
23
). Hence, an improvement of the model will result in a smaller estimated value of
\sigma
and hence a smaller uncertainty band for the model error, see also
B. Narouie et al. 2023
. Evaluating the kernel function at the measurement positions, the GP is represented by a multivariate Gaussian with a covariance matrix
\bm{C_{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}\times n_{y}}
.

The assumption of normally distributed data is ubiquitous and often justifiable for specific sensors and measurement equipment. Hence,
\bm{e}
is modelled as a multivariate Gaussian

\bm{e}\sim p(\bm{e})=\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{C_{e}})

with zero mean and the covariance matrix
\bm{C_{e}}=\sigma_{e}^{2}\bm{I}
.

After the hyperparameters
(\bm{\alpha},\rho)
are learned based on the given data
\bm{y}
, the prior state is conditioned on the same observation data, following the empirical Bayes procedure
Casella 1985
. This leads to an optimal set of hyperparameters and a posterior state which accounts for both FE solution, data and model uncertainty.

Despite being very recent, the method has already been adopted in more practical scenarios and has also been studied and developed further to account for different use cases. First elements of error analysis are provided in
Papandreou et al. 2023
;
Karvonen et al. 2022
. In
Duffin et al. 2022
, numerical difficulties which arise when scaling the method from academic examples to bigger systems are analysed and overcome by introducing a low-rank approach to keep memory usage small and the linear algebra tractable. Relying on Langevin dynamics, an efficient way to solve the statFEM forward problem, without using full PDE solves, is introduced in
Akyildiz et al. 2022
. An adaptation of statFEM for non-linear and time dependent problems is introduced in
Duffin et al. 2020
. Furthermore, an application in civil engineering is demonstrated in
Febrianto et al. 2022
, where statFEM is used to enable digital-twinning of a structure. An application in solid mechanics has been proposed in
B. Narouie et al. 2023
, where a prior derived from a simple material model is conditioned on data which stem from a more complex material model. Therein, the forward problem is solved using a polynomial chaos approach.

Before we adapt the statFEM data generating model to reflect the usage of approximate ROMs, we recall the conditioning procedure formulated in
Girolami et al. 2021
.
Let
\bm{Y}
denote the matrix containing
n_{o}
readings
\bm{y}_{i},i=1\ldots,n_{o}
of the sensor vector stacked column-wise. With (
33
), the posterior moments read

\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}|\bm{Y}}=\bm{C}_{\bm{u}|\bm{Y}}\left(\rho\bm{P}^{T}(\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}+\bm{C}_{\bm{e}})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\bm{y}_{i}+\bm{C}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}}\right)

(35)

and

\bm{C}_{\bm{u}|\bm{Y}}=\left(\rho^{2}n_{o}\bm{P}^{T}(\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}+\bm{C}_{\bm{e}})^{-1}\bm{P}+\bm{C}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}

(36)

with
n_{o}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
the number of observations per sensor and

p(\bm{Y}|\bm{u})=p(\bm{y}_{1}|\bm{u})p(\bm{y}_{2}|\bm{u})\ldots p(\bm{y}_{n_{o}}|\bm{u})=\prod_{i=1}^{n_{o}}p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{u}),

(37)

where we assumed independence of the different readings. From the posterior and the inferred model error, the inferred true response can be found as

p(\bm{z}|\bm{Y})=\mathcal{N}(\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}|\bm{Y}},\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\bm{u}|\bm{Y}}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}).

(38)

Finally, the predicted data distribution at new sensor locations, with
\hat{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\hat{y}}\times n_{u}}
, reads

p(\hat{\bm{y}}|\bm{Y})=\mathcal{N}(\rho\hat{\bm{P}}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}},\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\bm{u}|\bm{y}}\hat{\bm{P}}^{T}+\hat{\bm{C}}_{d}+\hat{\bm{C}}_{e})

(39)

for the
n_{\hat{y}}
DOFs where no observations are made. These findings shall now be adapted to the case of a ROM prior with a controllable approximation error.

4.3
ROM error estimation

Contrary to the FE-to-reality error, the error introduced by the ROM is easier to assess and it is desirable to include an estimate of it into the data generating model. The ROM approximation error can be estimated in an expensive but accurate way by sampling and comparing the FE solution and ROM prediction at each sample point.
The so-obtained ROM error samples are
\bm{d}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)=\bm{u}^{(i)}(\omega)-\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)
, from which the mean and variance can be estimated. This procedure is of limited use here, however, since it would be better to use
\bm{u}^{(i)}
in the data assimilation step right-away. Hence, we are interested in finding a different error indicator, which is accurate enough to improve the state estimation and at the same time the additional cost of computing the error indicator must be moderate.

We assume that the error is smooth enough over the computational domain so that again a GP model can be used to represent it. In particular, we estimate the ROM error at a few randomly chosen points and condition a GP to obtain an approximation over the entire domain. It is important to quantify the ROM error over the entire domain, not only at the sensor locations, to improve also the prediction of the true response
\bm{z}|\bm{Y}
and
\hat{\bm{y}}|\bm{Y}
. Since, in this approach, the error is only needed at a few points, we can employ adjoint methods. We follow an approach similar to the one presented in
Paul Rouse et al. 2022
, where the authors estimated the full FEM error field, contrary to the full ROM error field as desired here. Consider the solution
\bm{u}^{(i)}
and the adjoint equation

(\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)}))^{*}\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)=\bm{p}_{\ell},

(40)

where
\bm{p}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{u}(\omega)\approx u(\omega,\bm{x}_{\ell})
. Then, the error related to
\bm{x}_{\ell}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d_{r,\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\bm{p}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{u}^{(i)}(\omega)-\bm{p}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)

(41)

\displaystyle=((\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)}))^{*}\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega))^{*}\left(\bm{u}^{(i)}(\omega)-\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega))^{*}\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)})\left(\bm{u}^{(i)}(\omega)-\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega))^{*}\left(\bm{f}^{(i)}+\bm{f}_{g}^{(i)}-\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)})\bm{V}^{(i)}\bm{u}_{r}^{(i)}(\omega)\right).

(44)

This, however, still involves solving (
40
), i.e., a full order system of equations. To reduce the computational effort, we again construct a ROM as

(\bm{A}_{r}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)}))^{*}\bm{q}_{r,\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)=\bm{p}_{r,\ell}^{(i)},

(45)

where
(\bm{A}_{r}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)}))^{*}=(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)})^{*}(\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)}))^{*}\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)}
,
\bm{q}_{r,\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)=\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)}\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)
,
\bm{p}_{r,\ell}^{(i)}=(\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)})^{*}\bm{p}_{\ell}
and
\bm{q}_{\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)=\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)}\bm{q}_{r,\ell}^{(i)}(\omega)
. Please note that the construction of
\bm{V}_{\mathrm{adj},\ell}^{(i)}\neq\bm{V}^{(i)}
has to be carried out additionally in the offline phase. However, running the moment matching algorithm again for
i=1,\ldots,I
is very cheap as we can reuse the LU decomposition of
\bm{A}(\omega,\bm{\xi}^{(i)})
which had to be computed already to find the ROM prior.

Remark
.

Note that for multiple right-hand-sides, we can use AORGA (Adaptive order rational global Arnoldi)
Bodendiek 2013
;
Jbilou et al. 1999
instead of AORA, which would decrease the number of needed projection matrices. Instead of a right hand side vector, AORGA expects a right hand side matrix to find a projection basis.

We pick a problem-dependent (small) number of points
\bm{X}=[\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{n_{\mathrm{adj}}}]
throughout the domain and compute the adjoint error estimate
\bm{d}_{r,\mathrm{adj}}
, i.e., the mean over the QMC sample.
Then, a zero mean GP prior with Matérn kernel
c_{d_{r}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})

d_{r}(\bm{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,c_{d_{r}}(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}))

(46)

is conditioned on the vector of pointwise estimated errors
\bm{d}_{r,\mathrm{adj}}
to find the posterior at the points set
\hat{\bm{X}}

\hat{\bm{d}}_{r}|\bm{d}_{r,\mathrm{adj}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\hat{\bm{d}}_{r}},\bm{C}_{\hat{\bm{d}}_{r}}),

(47)

where

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{\hat{\bm{d}}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\hat{\bm{X}},\bm{X}}\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\bm{X},\bm{X}}^{-1}\bm{d}_{r,\mathrm{adj}}

(48)

\displaystyle\bm{C}_{\hat{\bm{d}}_{r}}

\displaystyle=\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\hat{\bm{X}},\hat{\bm{X}}}-\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\hat{\bm{X}},\bm{X}}(\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\bm{X},\bm{X}}^{-1}+\bm{I}\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{2})\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\bm{X},\hat{\bm{X}}}

(49)

and
\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r};\hat{\bm{X}},\bm{X}}
denotes the prior covariance evaluated at the point sets
\hat{\bm{X}}
and
\bm{X}
, respectively and a similar convention applies for the other matrices. Also,
\bm{\sigma}_{\mathrm{adj}}^{2}
is the vector of variances obtained while sampling
\bm{d}_{r,\mathrm{adj}}
.

In the next sections, this ROM approximation error indicator will be included into the statFEM posterior update and it will be shown that this can help improving the posterior mean estimate.

Remark
.

If the problem is subject only to a stochastic right hand side - called
\bm{f}
for simplicity - it is possible to compute a prior for
\bm{d}_{r}
in closed form.
In turn, the number of required projection matrices can be reduced. Let
\bm{f}_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\bm{f}_{r}},\bm{C}_{\bm{f}_{r}})
be the reduced stochastic right-hand-side. We propagate its distribution to
\bm{u}_{r}
via

\bm{A}_{r}(\omega)\bm{u}_{r}(\omega)=\bm{f}_{r}.

(50)

First, the mean can be computed in closed form as
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u}_{r}}(\omega)=\bm{A}_{r}(\omega)^{-1}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{f}_{r}}
. Moreover,

\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{f}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{f}},\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{C}_{\bm{f}}\bm{q}_{\ell})

(51)

and

\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{A}(\omega)\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r}(\omega)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{B}(\omega)\bm{\mu}_{\bm{f}_{r}},(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{B}(\omega))^{*}\bm{V}^{*}\bm{C}_{f}\bm{V}(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{B}(\omega)))

(52)

with
\bm{B}=\bm{A}\bm{V}\bm{A}_{r}^{-1}
. Please note that
\bm{V}
is constructed with a deterministic
\bm{A}
and a specific realization of
\bm{f}
- as before - and hence, is deterministic in this case. Therefore, we obtain the following prior for the ROM-error

d_{\ell}(\omega)\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}(\bm{V}-\bm{B}(\omega))\bm{\mu}_{\bm{f}_{r}},\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{C}_{\bm{f}}\bm{q}_{\ell}+(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{B}(\omega))^{*}\bm{V}^{*}\bm{C}_{f}\bm{V}(\bm{q}_{\ell}^{*}\bm{B}(\omega))).

(53)

4.4
statROM

A standard statFEM posterior update can of course be conducted, with the prior FE solution replaced with a ROM. However, the error model employed in (
55
) is rather crude and the additional ROM approximation error would simply inflate
\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}
. In the last section, we have formulated a numerically inexpensive way to approximate the ROM error for every point in the domain. Knowing more about the numerical error should, intuitively, lead to a better solution, i.e., a sharper estimated model error and, for the predictive observation density, a posterior mean closer to the observed data. Therefore, in this section, the statistical generating model and the posterior update equations are modified to account for the ROM approximation error. We label the resulting updating procedure as statistical reduced order modeling (statROM).

In analogy to the statFEM case, beginning with the statFEM statistical generating model (
33
), we expand the FE solution term as

\bm{y}=\rho\bm{P}(\bm{u}-\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r}+\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r})+\bm{d}+\bm{e},

(54)

which yields

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\rho\bm{P}\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r}+\rho\bm{P}(\bm{u}-\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r})+\bm{d}+\bm{e}

(55)

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle=\rho\bm{P}\bm{V}\bm{u}_{r}+\rho\bm{P}\bm{d}_{r}+\bm{d}+\bm{e}

(56)

with
\bm{d}_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
representing the ROM approximation error, which is obtained by interpolating the ROM error GP at the FE mesh nodes.
Unlike the model error
\bm{d}
, the ROM error indicator involves a non-zero mean. Hence, a bias induced by the ROM can directly be corrected and the ROM approximation error does not only inflate the variance. The statistical generating model is now used to derive the posterior update equations.

To condition the ROM prior on the data, at first, the joint distribution is constructed as

p(\tilde{\bm{u}},\bm{y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\\
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{y}}\end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}})(\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}})^{*}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left((\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}})(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{y}})^{*}\right)\\
\mathbb{E}\left((\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{y}})(\tilde{\bm{u}}-\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}})^{*}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left((\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{y}})(\bm{y}-\bm{\mu}_{\bm{y}})^{*}\right)\end{pmatrix}\right)\;.

Conditioning
\tilde{\bm{u}}
on
\bm{y}
now yields for the posterior mean

\begin{split}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{y}}=\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\rho\bm{C}_{\tilde{u}}\bm{P}^{\top}\left[\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{K}_{\bm{y},r}\right]^{-1}\left(\bm{y}-\left(\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}}\right)\right)\end{split}

(57)

with with
\bm{K}_{\bm{y}_{r}}=\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}+\bm{C}_{\bm{e}}
and for the covariance

\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{y}}=\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}\left[\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{K}_{\bm{y}_{r}}\right]^{-1}\rho\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}

(58)

where we recall that
\tilde{\bm{u}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}},\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}})
is the ROM-QMC prior solution. For multiple readings, i.e.
n_{o}>1
, we obtain

\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}=\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\rho\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}\left[\rho^{2}n_{o}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{K}_{\bm{y},r}\right]^{-1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\bm{y}_{i}-\rho n_{o}\left(\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}+\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}}\right)\right)

(59)

and for the covariance

\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}=\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho n_{o}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}\left[\rho^{2}n_{o}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{K}_{\bm{y}_{r}}\right]^{-1}\rho\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}.

(60)

Hence, for the statROM posterior there holds

\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}},\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}).

(61)

The equations can also be derived by directly using Bayes’ law as shown in
Girolami et al. 2021
.
Adapting the argumentation of
Girolami et al. 2021
to the present setting, we can deduce that for a covariance term
\bm{K}_{\bm{y},r}
which is small in comparison to
\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}
, the posterior mean tends to
(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\bm{y}_{i}-n_{o}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}})/(\rho n_{o})
and the posterior covariance tends to
\bm{K}_{\bm{y}_{r}}/\rho^{2}
. For a large covariance term, the posterior is close to the prior.
The higher the number of observations
n_{o}
, the more the posterior covariance tends to zero and the mean to the empirical mean of the ROM-error corrected observations
(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\bm{y}_{i}-n_{o}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}})/n_{o}
.

4.4.1
Hyperparameter Learning

To learn the posterior hyperparameters from data, the marginal likelihood has to be derived for the statROM case, as well. For a single observation there holds for the statROM data model

p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{\alpha},\rho)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n_{y}/2}\lvert\bm{K}\rvert}\cdot\\
\cdot\exp\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}(\bm{y}_{i}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}})^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}})\right)

(62)

with
n_{y}
the number of sensors
\bm{y}_{i}
and

\bm{K}=\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\rho^{2}\bm{P}\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r}}\bm{P}^{\top}+\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}+\bm{C}_{\bm{e}}

(63)

the covariance of (
55
). Taking the natural logarithm yields

\log p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{\alpha},\rho)=-\dfrac{n_{y}}{2}\log(2\pi)-\dfrac{1}{2}\log\lvert\bm{K}\rvert\\
-\dfrac{1}{2}(\bm{y}_{i}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}})^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}(\bm{y}_{i}-\rho\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}-\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}}).

(64)

As (
37
) holds, we obtain

\log p(\bm{Y}|\bm{\alpha},\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{o}}\log p(\bm{y}_{i}|\bm{\alpha},\rho).

(65)

The marginal likelihood is minimized using a gradient based optimizer. In this case, L-BFGS-B
Liu and Nocedal 1989
is used. It is visible in (
64
) that the misspecified ROM prior mean at the sensor locations
\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}}
is corrected by the mean error estimate
\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}}
.

Predictive Observation Density

As derived in
Girolami et al. 2021
for the statFEM case, we find in analogy the statROM predictive observation density, i.e., an estimate for the real underlying process at sensor locations as

p(\bm{z}|\bm{Y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\rho\bm{P}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}+\rho\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}},\rho^{2}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{C}_{\bm{d}_{r}}+\bm{C}_{\bm{d}}\right)

(66)

and for points where no data has been observed
\hat{\bm{y}}
, as

p(\hat{\bm{y}}|\bm{Y})=\mathcal{N}\left(\rho\hat{\bm{P}}\bm{\mu}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}+\rho\bm{\mu}_{\bm{d}_{r}},\hat{\bm{C}}_{\bm{d}_{r}}+\hat{\bm{C}}_{\bm{d}}+\hat{\bm{C}}_{\bm{e}}+\rho^{2}\hat{\bm{P}}\bm{C}_{\tilde{\bm{u}}|\bm{Y}}\hat{\bm{P}}^{\top}\right).

(67)

By a slight abuse of notation, we denote with
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{Y_{d_{r}}}
the entire predictive field. It is visible that the posterior mean and covariance are corrected by the model error and ROM error terms. Following the reasoning in
Girolami et al. 2021
, the predictive observation density now tends, in contrast to the plain posterior, to
\bm{y}
for the mean and to
2\bm{C}_{d}+\bm{C}_{d_{r}}+\bm{C}_{e}
for the covariance, hence to the data generating process.

5
Numerical Examples

We consider two numerical examples to illustrate the new methods. The first one is a 1D example of the Helmholtz equation with random forcing and a random heterogeneous material coefficient. It is used to show the convergence properties of the proposed statROM approach compared to the classical approach without a ROM error estimator. The second example is more involved and deals with solving the Helmholtz equation in a 2D scattering scenario with a misspecified random forcing. For both settings, we compare the predictive observation density derived from the classical data model (
38
) and from the proposed statROM data model (
67
). In both cases, the prior state is computed using the ROM. We denote the predictive state for the classical approach with
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{Y}
and for the proposed statROM approach with
\tilde{\bm{u}}_{Y_{d_{r}}}
.

5.1
One-dimensional problem

To illustrate the method and to show the convergence properties, we begin by investigating the Helmholtz equation in 1D. We consider an inhomogeneous Neumann boundary condition at
x_{n}=0
and no impedance boundary condition, hence, the problem is real-valued. We choose
f_{g}\sim\mathcal{N}(\frac{\pi^{2}}{50},0.02^{2})
. At
x_{n}=1
, a homogeneous Neumann boundary condition, i.e. a sound hard reflective boundary, is applied. The material parameter random field is parameterized with
\sigma_{\kappa}=\sqrt{5e-2},\;l_{\kappa}=0.3
for an exponential kernel function. The FE mesh consists of
100
elements and linear Ansatz functions are employed. We first investigate the convergence of the ROM prior itself. Then we condition with the standard statFEM error model to obtain a reference, followed by a statROM update. Finally, we investigate the convergence of the predictive posterior solution towards the data generating process.

In Figure
1
, the 1D problem setup is exemplary shown for
\omega/2\pi=460
Hz. We generate synthetic data, by adding noisy perturbations to a full order reference
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u};\mathrm{ref}}(\omega)
with
N_{\mathrm{ref}}=1000
DOFs. The data are collected on
n_{y}=11
equally spaced sensor locations throughout the domain. Per sensor,
n_{o}=20
observations are taken. The FEM prior
\bm{u}
is the solution of an FE simulation, obtained with the same setting as
\bm{\mu}_{\bm{u};\mathrm{ref}}(\omega)
, with
N=101
DOFs and QMC sampling. The model error, hence, is only due to discretization. The ROM prior
\tilde{\bm{u}}
, which is shown here for a moment matching ROM with
m=5
moments matched around
\bar{\omega}/2\pi=100
Hz, neither follows the data nor the FEM prior. Hence, there clearly is a significant ROM approximation error. This error is supposed to decrease with more matched moments
m
. In the next Section, the convergence of the ROM prior towards the FEM prior with increasing

is analyzed.

5.1.1
ROM convergence

As the statFEM error estimates were analyzed in
Karvonen et al. 2022
and estimates of the FEM error for the Helmholtz equation have been discussed extensively in the literature
Ihlenburg and Babuška 1995
, we put the main emphasis here on the convergence of the ROM prior approximation and the statROM update.
All errors are specified as the expected value of the wave number dependent
H_{k}^{1}
energy norm
Spence and Wunsch 2023

\|\mu_{u;\mathrm{ref}}-{\mu}_{\tilde{{u}}}\|_{H_{k}^{1}}^{2}=k^{-2}\|\nabla(\mu_{u;\mathrm{ref}}-{\mu}_{\tilde{{u}}})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\|\mu_{u;\mathrm{ref}}-{\mu}_{\tilde{{u}}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}},

(68)

where discrete quantities are interpolated with the underlying FE basis functions. The lower bound of the error always depends on the wave number, i.e. the physical setting of the problem as shown in
Ihlenburg and Babuška 1995
.

To compute the ROM approximation error, the approximated FE solution serves as a reference.

5.1.2
statROM Convergence

In the next paragraphs, the error between the data generating process and the results of the different approaches is evaluated for different ROM fidelities to show the convergence properties.

5.2
2D acoustic scattering example

The setting for the 2D acoustic scattering example differs from the 1D example in the choice of boundary conditions and discretization. We define an unstructured mesh on a square domain. In the middle, a circular scatterer is placed. All outer boundaries are defined to be absorbing. The boundary of the scatterer is defined to be homogeneous Dirichlet. As source term, an incident wave over the whole domain is defined. Contrary to the 1D example, we introduce additional model error by altering the source term defined to generate the ground truth.

6
Conclusion

A ROM adaptation of statFEM has been developed. The Bayesian prior is not constructed using FEM but with a ROM based on a moment matching approach. This is necessary if the frequency dependent full order model is too expensive to be solved at many frequency steps. Compared to the classical approach applied to a ROM prior, statROM performs better because the ROM error is estimated beforehand. This was shown by computing the error and convergence properties of the classical and the proposed approach compared to a full order reference solution. The new method enables solving the prior in frequency sweeps much faster as it is the case for a full order model while estimating the ROM error.

The estimation of ROM error is achieved using an adjoint estimator, which notably circumvents the need for inverting the full order system matrix. This estimated error is then incorporated into the statistical data model formulation. Introducing this estimated error into the data model has been shown to reduce the overall estimated model error and bring the model’s mean closer to the ground truth.

For systems with a high dimensional material coefficient, approximated with the Karhunen–Loève expansion, a QMC approach led to an accurate prior formulation.
This methodology has been applied and its convergence properties showcased through a 1D Helmholtz problem. In a more complex 2D scattering problem with significantly more degrees of freedom and a stochastic forcing defined as a random field, it could be shown that the method can be scaled to some extend.

As a next step, efforts are directed towards parameterizing the ROM, which currently can be applied primarily to frequency sweeps. Another important extension to the method is finding a way to keep the memory requirements low, especially for the covariance matrices in larger systems. As soon as parallelization on the frequency grid is applied, several large matrices have to be kept in memory. E.g., the approach by
Koh and Cirak 2023
, where sparse precision matrices replace their dense covariance counterpart, could be adapted to work in the setting presented here. Also, a practical application in aerospace acoustics is considered as a feasible applied problem for statROM.
