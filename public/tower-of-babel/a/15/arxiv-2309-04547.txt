Abstract
We discover several surprising relationships between large classes of seemingly unrelated foundational problems of financial engineering and fundamental problems of hydrodynamics and molecular physics. Solutions in all these domains can be reduced to solving affine differential equations commonly used in various mathematical and scientific disciplines to model dynamic systems. We have identified connections in these seemingly disparate areas as we link together small wave-like perturbations of linear flows in ideal and viscous fluids described in hydrodynamics by Kevin waves to motions of free and harmonically bound particles described in molecular physics by Klein-Kramers and Kolmogorov equations to Gaussian and non-Gaussian affine processes, e.g., Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and Feller, arising in financial engineering. To further emphasize the parallels between these diverse fields, we build a coherent mathematical framework using Kevin waves to construct transition probability density functions for problems in hydrodynamics, molecular physics, and financial engineering. As one of the outcomes of our analysis, we discover that the original solution of the Kolmogorov equation contains an error, which we subsequently correct. We apply our interdisciplinary approach to advance the understanding of various financial engineering topics, such as pricing of Asian options, volatility and variance swaps, options on stocks with path-dependent volatility, bonds, and bond options. We also discuss further applications to other exciting problems of financial engineering. Keywords : affine processes, Kelvin waves, Klein-Kramer equation, Kolmogorov equation, stochastic volatility, path-dependent options MSC2020 Classification : 34A34, 35A22, 42A38, 60H10, 76E99, 91G20

Introduction

1.1
Background

The Fourier transform is the most powerful tool of an applied mathematician,
who can use it to solve linear partial differential equations (PDEs) with
spatially constant coefficients, analyze time series and perform many other
vital tasks. The principal building block of the n-dimensional Fourier
method is the wave function:

\begin{array}[]{c}H\left(t,\mathbf{x,k}\right)=a\left(t\right)\exp\left(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}\right),\end{array}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{k}
are
n
-dimensional vectors,
\cdot

denote the scalar product,
a\left(t\right)
is the amplitude, and
\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is the phase. Depending on the circumstances,
the amplitude can be a scalar or a vector. Substituting
H
into a PDE with
spatially constant coefficients, we reduce the problem of interest to a
system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) or a single ODE when
a\left(t\right)
is scalar.

In this paper, we study PDEs with coefficients linearly dependent on
x
,
which we call affine PDEs. Hence, we must use a more general approach and
consider the wave function with a time-dependent wave vector:

\begin{array}[]{c}H\left(t,\mathbf{x,k}\right)=a\left(t\right)\exp\left(i\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{x}\right),\end{array}

(2)

To the best of our knowledge,
Kelvin 1887
and
Orr 1907
were the
first to use such building blocks to analyze the stability of the steady
motions of a perfect fluid. Subsequently and independently, affine PDEs and
the associated wave functions were used by many researchers in various
areas, including the theory of stochastic processes, physics, biology, and
mathematical finance, to mention a few. In this respect, the
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) and Feller processes are archetypal; see
Uhlenbeck & Ornstein 1930
;
Chandresekhar 1943
;
Feller 1951
;
Feller 1952
. For financial
applications of affine processes see
Duffie & Kan 1996
;
Duffie et al. 2000
;
Dai & Singleton 2000
;
Duffie et al. 2003
;
Filipovic 2009
, among others.

We use Kelvin waves of the form (
2
) in order to study transition
probability density functions (t.p.d.fs) for affine stochastic processes,
which can be either degenerate, i.e., having more independent components
than the sources of uncertainty, or non-degenerate, when every component has
its source of uncertainty. Our main objective is to link various problems of
financial engineering with their counterparts occurring in fluid dynamics
and molecular physics and showcase the interdisciplinary nature of
quantitative finance and financial modeling. Finding such connections allows
us to understand better how to model, price, and risk-manage various
financial instruments and provide additional intuition regarding their
salient features. This work continues our previous efforts in this
direction; see
Lipton & Sepp 2008
;
Lipton 2018
, Chapter 12.

There are several approaches one can use to solve affine equations
efficiently. For instance, Lie symmetries are a powerful tool for studying
certain classes of affine equations. Numerous authors describe general
techniques based on Lie symmetries; see, e.g.,
Ovsiannikov 1982
;
Ibragimov 1985
;
Olver 1986
;
Bluman & Kumei 1989
, while their specific applications are
covered by
Berest 1993
;
Aksenov 1995
;
Craddock & Platen 2004
;
Craddock 2012
;
Kovalenko et al. 2014
,
among many others. Reductions of a given equation to a simpler, solvable
form is another powerful method that can be successfully used in many
instances; see, e.g.,
Chandresekhar 1943
;
Carr et al. 2002
;
Lipton et al. 2014
;
Lipton 2018
,
Chapter 9. Finally, Kelvin waves (or affine ansatz) provide another
approach, which is the focus of the present paper; see also
Duffie & Kan 1996
;
Dai & Singleton 2000
;
Duffie et al. 2003
;
Lipton & Sepp 2008
;
Filipovic 2009
;
Lipton 2018
, Chapter 12.

1.2
Main Results and Paper Structure

This paper builds a coherent mathematical framework using Kelvin waves as a
powerful and versatile tool for studying t.p.d.fs in the context of generic
affine processes. We discover previously uncovered connections among large
classes of apparently unrelated problems from hydrodynamics, molecular
physics, and financial engineering. All these problems require solving
affine differential equations, i.e., equations with coefficients, which
linearly depend on spatial variables. We discuss some classical results and
derive new ones related to 1) small wave-like perturbations of linear flows
of ideal and viscous fluids described by Euler and Navier-Stokes equations,
respectively; 2) motions of free and harmonically bound particles described
by the Klein-Kramers equations and the hypoelliptic Kolmogorov equation; and
3) dynamics of financial assets and market movements driven by Gaussian and
non-Gaussian affine processes, such as the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck and Feller. We
augment these processes by introducing subordinate processes for auxiliary
variables, such as integrals over the original mean-reverting variable. We
develop a uniform mathematical formalism to construct t.p.d.fs for the
abovementioned problems.

Our analysis identifies an error in the original solution of the Kolmogorov
equation, which we subsequently rectify. This work, in addition to
recovering and improving several well-known results, also derives new ones.
For instance, we find a considerably more convenient expression for t.p.d.fs
in the case of processes with stochastic volatility. Moreover, our analysis
reveals an unexpected similarity between the propagation of vorticity in
two-dimensional flows of viscous incompressible fluid and the motion of a
harmonically bound particle, which we use to find a new explicit expression
for the vorticity of a two-dimensional flow in terms of the Gaussian density.

Finally, we apply the new methodology to various financial engineering
topics, such as pricing Asian options, volatility and variance swaps,
options on stocks with path-dependent volatility, and bonds and bond
options. In contrast to the classical treatment, we view fixed-income
products as path-dependent, which allows us to gain additional intuition
regarding their pricing and risk management. We also highlight the
flexibility of our interdisciplinary framework to incorporate additional
complexities and to have natural applications to other financial engineering
problems, such as jump-diffusion processes (verbatim) and, more generally,
processes driven by affine pseudo differential equations.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces the Euler
equations describing the evolution of the perfect fluid. We show that these
equations have exact solutions with the velocity linearly and pressure
quadratically depending on spatial coordinates. The linearized Euler
equations govern small perturbations of these solutions known as the
celebrated Kelvin waves. We demonstrate how to use Kelvin waves to study the
stability of the exact linear solutions. Subsequently, we utilize Kevin
waves as our primary tool in our methodology and apply it to other areas of
study. We also briefly cover the Navier-Stokes equation for the viscous
fluid and show how to modify the ODEs for Kelvin waves to account for
viscosity. In Section
5.5
, we return to this topic, establishing
deep connections between the two-dimensional vorticity equations and the
Klein-Kramers equation underpinning molecular physics, which allows us to
derive a new formula expressing the vorticity as a Gaussian density, and the
stream function as a solution of the corresponding Poisson equation.

A word about notation is in order. By its very nature, papers like the

2
Kelvin Waves

2.1
Euler Equations

In what follows, we use Kelvin waves as our primary tool. We dedicate this
section to their brief description.

Recall that the Euler system of partial differential equations (PDEs)
describing the motion of an inviscid, incompressible fluid has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{V\cdot}\nabla)\mathbf{V+}\nabla\left(\frac{P}{\rho}\right)=0,\\
\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V=}0\mathbf{,}\end{array}

(3)

where
t
is time,
\mathbf{x}
is the position,
V
\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)
is the velocity vector,
P\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)

is the pressure,
\rho
is the constant density,
\nabla
is the gradient;
see, e.g.,
Chandrasekhar 1961
. In Cartesian coordinates, Eqs (
3
) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial t}+\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{j=1}^{3}V_{j}\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(\frac{P}{\rho}\right)=0,\\
\\
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\partial V_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=0.\end{array}

(4)

It can be shown that Eqs (
3
) have a family of solutions
\left(\mathbf{V}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right),P\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
,
linearly depending on spatial coordinates:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{V}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x,\ \ \ }\frac{P\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)}{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{M}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x\cdot x,}\end{array}

(5)

where the matrices
\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)
,
\mathfrak{M}\left(t\right)
, such that

\begin{array}[]{c}Tr\left(\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathfrak{M}\left(t\right)=\mathfrak{M}^{\ast}\left(t\right),\end{array}

(6)

satisfy the following system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs):

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)}{dt}+\mathfrak{L}^{2}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{M}\left(t\right)=0.\end{array}

(7)

We are interested in the behavior of perturbations of solutions given by Eq. (
5
), which we denote by
\left(\mathbf{v}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right),p\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
. By neglecting the quadratic
term
(
v
\mathbf{\cdot}\nabla)
v
, we can write the
system of PDEs for
\left(\mathbf{v,}p\right)
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+(\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x\cdot}\nabla)\mathbf{v+}\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{v+}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)=0,\\
\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v=}0\mathbf{.}\end{array}

(8)

It has been known for a long time that linear PDEs (
8
) have
wave-like solutions of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\mathbf{v}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right),\frac{p\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)}{\rho}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)\exp\left(i\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{x-\xi}\left(t\right)\right)\right),b\left(t\right)\exp\left(i\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{x-\xi}\left(t\right)\right)\right)\right),\end{array}

(9)

where
\left(\mathbf{a}\left(t\right),b\left(t\right)\right)
are
time-dependent amplitudes, and
\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)
is the
time-dependent wave vector; see
Kelvin 1887
;
Orr 1907
;
Craik & Criminale 1986
;
Friedlander & Lipton-Lifschitz 2003
. We call these solutions the Kelvin waves. The point we
emphasize below is that the so-called affine ansatz is a special instance of
Kelvin wave. This observation allows us to draw similarities among seemingly
unrelated topics, which, in turn, facilitates their holistic and
comprehensive study.

The pair
\mathbf{a}\left(t\right),\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)

satisfies the following system of ODEs:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d\mathbf{\xi}\left(t\right)}{dt}-\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\xi}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\xi}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{\xi}_{0},\\
\\
\frac{d\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)}{dt}+\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Gamma}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0},\\
\\
\frac{d\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)}{dt}+\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)-2\frac{\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)}{\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)}\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)=0,\ \mathbf{a}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{a}_{0},\\
\\
\ \mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{0}=0.\end{array}

(10)

Here and below, the superscript
T
stands for transpose, and
\cdot

denotes the scalar product. The corresponding
p\left(t\right)
can be
found via the incompressibility condition. It is easy to show that for
t\geq 0
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{\xi}\left(t\right)=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{\xi}_{0},\ \ \ \mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)=0.\end{array}

(11)

Typically, Eqs (
10
) are used to study the stability of the linear
flow. Such a flow is unstable whenever
\left\|\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)\right\|\rightarrow\theta
for some choices of
\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0}
; see
Bayly 1986
;
Bayly et al. 1996
;
Lifschitz 1995
. Moreover, it can
be shown that the same instabilities occur in general three-dimensional
flows; see
Friedlander & Vishik 1991
;
Lifschitz & Hameiri 1991
;
Friedlander & Lipton-Lifschitz 2003
.

Interestingly,
Chandrasekhar 1961
pointed out that the superposition of
the linear flow (
5
) and the Kelvin wave (
9
), i.e.,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\tilde{V}}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x+v}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right),\\
\\
\frac{\tilde{P}\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)}{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{M}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x\cdot x+}\frac{p\left(t,\mathbf{x}\right)}{\rho},\end{array}

(12)

satisfies the nonlinear Euler equations (
3
) since the nonlinear
term
v
\cdot\nabla
v
vanishes identically due to
incompressibility.

1

Thus, even the greatest minds occasionally can be myopic – it took eighty
years for fluid dynamists to connect the dots and observe that
\left(\mathbf{\tilde{v}},\tilde{p}/\rho\right)
solve the nonlinear Euler
equations.

2.2
Navier-Stokes Equations

The motion of the incompressible viscous fluid is described by the
celebrated Navier-Stokes equations of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{V}}{\partial t}+(\mathbf{V\cdot}\nabla)\mathbf{V-}\nu\Delta\mathbf{V+}\nabla\left(\frac{P}{\rho}\right)=0,\\
\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{V=}0\mathbf{,}\end{array}

(13)

where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity; see, e.g.,
Chandrasekhar 1961
.
It is clear that linear flows, given by Eq. (
5
), are unaffected by
viscosity, hence they satisfy Eqs (
14
). Viscosity does affect
their small perturbations, which are governed by the following equations:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t}+(\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x\cdot}\nabla)\mathbf{v+}\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{v\mathbf{-}}\nu\Delta\mathbf{v+}\nabla\left(\frac{p}{\rho}\right)=0,\\
\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v=}0\mathbf{.}\end{array}

(14)

The viscous version of Eqs (
10
) has the form; see
Lifschitz 1991
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d\mathbf{\xi}\left(t\right)}{dt}-\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\xi}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\xi}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{\xi}_{0},\\
\\
\frac{d\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)}{dt}+\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Gamma}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{0},\\
\\
\frac{d\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)}{dt}+\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)-2\frac{\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)}{\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)}\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)+\nu\left|\mathbf{k}\left(t\right)\right|^{2}\mathbf{a}\left(t\right)=0,\ \mathbf{a}\left(0\right)=\mathbf{a}_{0},\\
\\
\ \mathbf{\Gamma}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{a}_{0}=0.\end{array}

(15)

We shall show in Section
5.5
that in the two-dimensional case, the
Navier-Stokes equations for small perturbations of linear flows are more or
less identical to the Fokker-Planck equations for harmonically bound
articles, which is surprising.

3
Klein-Kramers Equation

3.1
Langevin Equation

We start with the Langevin equation for particles moving in a potential
field and impacted by random forces; see
Langevin 1908
. This section
uses the same notation as in
Chandresekhar 1943
.

Consider an underdamped Brownian particle. In contrast to the standard
Brownian motion, which is overdamped, we assume that the frictions are
finite, so that we must treat the momentum as an independent degree of
freedom, and describe a particle’s state as a pair
\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{u}\right)
, where
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{u}
are its position
and velocity, respectively. We consider a
d
-dimensional space, with
d=1

and
d=3
of particular interest. Hence, the corresponding Langevin
equations are

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}=\mathbf{u},\\
\\
\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=-\beta\mathbf{u-}\frac{\nabla V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)}{m}+\sqrt{\frac{2\beta k_{B}T}{m}}\mathbf{\eta}\left(t\right),\\
\\
\left\langle\mathbf{\eta}^{T}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\eta}\left(t^{\prime}\right)\right\rangle=\mathbf{I}\delta\left(t-t^{\prime}\right).\end{array}

(16)

Here
m
is the particle mass,
\beta
is the friction coefficient,
k_{B}

is the Boltzmann constant,
T
is the temperature,
V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)
is the external potential,
\mathbf{\eta}\left(t\right)
is a
d
-dimensional Gaussian white noise, and
\mathbf{I}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix. Below we denote the ratio
\beta k_{B}T/m
as
q
.

Of course, we can rewrite Eqs (
16
) as a system of stochastic
differential equations (SDEs):

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{u}dt,\\
\\
d\mathbf{u}=-\beta\mathbf{u}dt\mathbf{-}\frac{\nabla V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)}{m}dt+\sqrt{2q}d\mathbf{W}\left(t\right),\end{array}

(17)

where
\mathbf{W}\left(t\right)
is a standard
d
-dimensional Wiener
process.

3.2
Klein-Kramers Equation

Fokker, Planck, and their numerous followers derived and studied the forward
parabolic equation for the transition probability density
W\left(\tau,\mathbf{\rho,\upsilon},t,\mathbf{r,u}\right)
associated with a
stochastic process. For the stochastic process governed by SDEs (
17
), the corresponding equation, called the Klein-Kramers equation, has the
form:

\begin{array}[]{c}W_{t}-qW_{\mathbf{uu}}+\mathbf{u}W_{\mathbf{r}}-\left(\left(\beta\mathbf{u}+\frac{\nabla V\left(\mathbf{r}\right)}{m}\right)W\right)_{\mathbf{u}}=0,\\
\\
W\left(\tau,\mathbf{\rho,\upsilon},\tau,\mathbf{r,u}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\rho}\right)\delta\left(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{\upsilon}\right).\end{array}

(18)

The backward parabolic Kolmogorov equation has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}W_{\tau}+qW_{\mathbf{\upsilon\upsilon}}+\mathbf{\upsilon}W_{\mathbf{\rho}}-\left(\beta\mathbf{\upsilon}+\frac{\nabla V\left(\mathbf{\rho}\right)}{m}\right)W_{\mathbf{\upsilon}}=0,\\
\\
W\left(t,\mathbf{\rho,\upsilon},t,\mathbf{r,u}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{\rho}\right)\delta\left(\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{\upsilon}\right).\end{array}

(19)

Details are given in
Fokker 1914
;
Planck 1917
;
Klein 1921
;
Chapman 1928
;
Kolmogoroff 1931
;
Kolmogoroff 1933
;
Kolmogoroff 1934
;
Kramers 1940
;
Chandresekhar 1943
;
Risken 1989
;
Hänggi et al. 1990
, as well as a multitude of subsequent sources. For
fascinating historical details see
Ebeling et al. 2008
.

3.3
Chandrasekhar’s Solutions

In a well-known survey article,
Chandresekhar 1943
described elegant
solutions of Eq. (
18
) for a free particle and a harmonically bound
particle, which he derived by using clever changes of coordinates.

First, we shall consider a free particle in
1D
. For such a particle,
Chandresekhar 1943
writes the corresponding Klein-Kramers equation as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}W_{t}-qW_{uu}+uW_{r}-\beta uW_{u}-W=0,\\
\\
W\left(\tau,\rho\mathbf{,}\upsilon,\tau,r\mathbf{,}u\right)=\delta\left(r-\rho\right)\delta\left(u-\upsilon\right),\end{array}

(20)

and shows that

\begin{array}[]{c}W=\frac{1}{2\pi\left(FG-H^{2}\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(FS^{2}-2HRS+GR^{2}\right)}{2\left(FG-H^{2}\right)}\right),\end{array}

(21)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}S=u-e^{-\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}\upsilon,\ \ \ R=r-\rho-\frac{1-e^{-\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\beta}\upsilon,\\
\\
F=\frac{q}{\beta^{3}}\left(-3+4e^{-\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}-e^{-2\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}+2\beta\left(t-\tau\right)\right),\\
\\
G=\frac{q}{\beta}\left(1-e^{-2\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}\right),\ \ \ H=\frac{q}{\beta^{2}}\left(1-e^{-\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)^{2}.\end{array}

(22)

Since we assumed that stochastic drivers are uncorrelated, the t.p.d.f.
W^{\left(3\right)}
can be presented as a product of three
1D
t.p.d.f.
W^{\left(1\right)}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}W^{\left(3\right)}=W_{1}^{\left(1\right)}W_{2}^{\left(1\right)}W_{3}^{\left(1\right)}=\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}\left(FG-H^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(F\left|\mathbf{S}\right|^{2}-2H\mathbf{R}\cdot\mathbf{S}+G\left|\mathbf{R}\right|^{2}\right)}{2\left(FG-H^{2}\right)}\right),\end{array}

(23)

where
\mathbf{u=}\left(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\right)
, etc.

To analyze a harmonically bound particle, we can proceed as follows; see

Chandresekhar 1943
. As usual, the harmonic potential
V\left(x\right)

has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(x\right)=\frac{m\omega^{2}x^{2}}{2}.\end{array}

(24)

Let
\mu_{\pm}
be the roots of

\begin{array}[]{c}\mu^{2}+\beta\mu+\omega^{2}=0,\\
\\
\mu_{\pm}=\frac{-\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\omega^{2}}}{2}.\end{array}

(25)

Define new variables

\begin{array}[]{c}\xi=e^{-\mu_{-}\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(\mu_{+}r-u\right),\ \ \ \eta=e^{-\mu_{+}\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(\mu_{-}r-u\right),\\
\\
\hat{\xi}=\left(\mu_{+}\rho-\upsilon\right),\ \ \ \hat{\eta}=\left(\mu_{-}\rho-\upsilon\right),\end{array}

(26)

and coefficients

\begin{array}[]{c}a=\frac{q}{\mu_{+}}\left(1-e^{-2\mu_{+}\left(t-\tau\right)}\right),\ \ \ b=\frac{q}{\mu_{-}}\left(1-e^{-2\mu_{-}\left(t-\tau\right)}\right),\\
\\
h=-\frac{2q}{\left(\mu_{+}+\mu_{-}\right)}\left(1-e^{-\left(\mu_{+}+\mu_{-}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}\right).\end{array}

(27)

Then

\begin{array}[]{c}W=\frac{e^{\beta\left(t-\tau\right)}}{2\pi\sqrt{ab-h^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{a\left(\xi-\hat{\xi}\right)^{2}+2h\left(\xi-\hat{\xi}\right)\left(\eta-\hat{\eta}\right)+b\left(\eta-\hat{\eta}\right)^{2}}{2\left(ab-h^{2}\right)}\right).\end{array}

(28)

While
Chandresekhar 1943
stopped at Eq. (
28
), for practical
applications, it is more useful to represent the exponent as an explicit
quadratic form of
r
and
u
, which we do below.

4
Kolmogorov Equation

4.1
Summary of Kolmogorov’s paper

In a remarkable (and remarkably concise) note, Kolmogorov considers a system
of particles in
n
-dimensional space with coordinates
q_{1},...,q_{n}
,
and velocities
\dot{q}_{1},...,\dot{q}_{n}
, assumes the probability
density function

\begin{array}[]{c}g\left(t,q_{1},...,q_{n},\dot{q}_{1},...,\dot{q}_{n},t^{\prime},q_{1}^{\prime},...,q_{n}^{\prime},\dot{q}_{1}^{\prime},...,\dot{q}_{n}^{\prime}\right)\end{array}

exist for some time
t^{\prime}>t
, and reveals (without any explanation)
an analytical expression for
g
in the one-dimensional case; see
Kolmogoroff 1934
. This note is the third in a series of papers; the previous
two being
Kolmogoroff 1931
;
Kolmogoroff 1933
.

Kolmogorov makes the following natural assumptions:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left|\Delta q_{i}-\dot{q}_{i}\Delta t\right|=o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(29)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta q_{i}\right)^{2}=o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(30)

where
\Delta t=t^{\prime}-t
. Eqs (
29
), (
30
) imply

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta q_{i}\right)=\dot{q}_{i}\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(31)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta q_{i}\Delta q_{j}\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta q_{i}\right)^{2}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta q_{j}\right)^{2}}=o\left(\Delta t\right).\end{array}

(32)

Furthermore, under very general assumptions, the following relationships hold

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\right)=f_{i}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(33)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\right)^{2}=k_{ii}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(34)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\Delta\dot{q}_{j}\right)=k_{ij}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(35)

where
f,k
are continuous functions. Eqs (
30
), (
34
) imply

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\Delta\dot{q}_{j}\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\right)^{2}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{j}\right)^{2}}=o\left(\Delta t\right).\end{array}

(36)

Under some natural physical assumptions, it follows that
g
satisfies the
following differential equation of the Fokker-Planck type:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}=-\sum\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}^{\prime}}g-\sum\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime}}\left\{f_{i}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}+\sum\sum\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime 2}}\left\{\left\{k\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}\right\}.\end{array}

(37)

In the one-dimensional case, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}=-\dot{q}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{\prime}}g-\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{q}^{\prime}}\left\{f\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\dot{q}^{\prime 2}}\left\{\left\{k\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}\right\}.\end{array}

(38)

These equations are known as ultra-parabolic Fokker-Plank- Kolmogorov
equations due to their degeneracy.

When
f
and
k
are constants, the fundamental solution of Eq. (
38
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}g=\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi k^{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left[\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}-f\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{4k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)}-\frac{3\left[q^{\prime}-q-\frac{\dot{q}^{\prime}+\dot{q}}{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{k^{3}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{3}}\right\}.\end{array}

(39)

One can see that
\Delta\dot{q}
is of the order
\left(\Delta t\right)^{1/2}
. At the same time

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta q=\dot{q}\Delta t+O\left(\Delta t\right)^{3/2}.\end{array}

(40)

One can prove that a similar relation holds for the general Eq. (
37
).

The previous Section shows that physicists derived Eqs (
37
), (
38
) at least a decade earlier than Kolmogorov, which puts the
Kolmogorov equation into a broader context.

Kolmogorov equations fascinated mathematicians for a long time and generated
a great deal of research; see, e.g.,
Weber 1951
;
Hörmander 1967
;
Kuptsov 1972
;
Lanconelli et al. 2002
;
Pascucci 2005
;
Ivasishen & Medynsky 2010
;
Duong & Tran 2018
, among others.

4.2
Challenge and Response

Despite its undoubting brilliance, the original Kolmogorov’s paper has
several issues.

First, Eqs (
37
), (
38
)
are not
the Fokker-Planck
equations associated with the process described by Eqs (
33
) - (
35
), since they miss the prefactor
1/2
in front of the diffusion
terms. The corrected multivariate equation has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}=-\sum\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}^{\prime}}g-\sum\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime}}\left\{f_{i}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\sum\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\dot{q}_{i}^{\prime}\partial\dot{q}_{j}^{\prime}}\left\{\left\{k\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}\right\},\end{array}

(41)

while the corresponding one-dimensional equation has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial g}{\partial t^{\prime}}=-\dot{q}^{\prime}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{\prime}}g-\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot{q}^{\prime}}\left\{f\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\dot{q}^{\prime 2}}\left\{\left\{k\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)g\right\}\right\}.\end{array}

(42)

Alternatively, Eqs (
34
), (
35
) can be altered as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\right)^{2}=2k_{ii}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right),\end{array}

(43)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{E}\left(\Delta\dot{q}_{i}\Delta\dot{q}_{j}\right)=2k_{ij}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\Delta t+o\left(\Delta t\right).\end{array}

(44)

We prefer to update the Fokker-Planck equation.

Second,
g
given by Eq. (
39
) does not solve Eq. (
38
).
It also does not satisfy the (implicit) initial condition

\begin{array}[]{c}g\left(t,q,\dot{q},t,q^{\prime},\dot{q}^{\prime}\right)=\delta\left(q^{\prime}-q\right)\delta\left(\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}\right),\end{array}

(45)

where
\delta\left(.\right)
is the Dirac
\delta
-function. The fact
that expression (
39
) does not solve Eq. (
38
) can be
verified by substitution. However, it is easier to verify this statement via
dimensional analysis. The dimensions of the corresponding variables and
coefficients are as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\left[t\right]=\left[t^{\prime}\right]=T,\ \ \ \left[q\right]=\left[q^{\prime}\right]=L,\ \ \ \left[\dot{q}\right]=\left[\dot{q}^{\prime}\right]=T^{-1}L,\ \ \ \left[g\right]=T^{1}L^{-2},\\
\\
\left[f\right]=T^{-2}L,\ \ \ \left[k\right]=T^{-3}L^{2}.\end{array}

(46)

It is easy to show that
g
is scale-invariant, so that

\begin{array}[]{c}g\left(\lambda^{2}t,\lambda^{3}q,\lambda\dot{q},\lambda^{2}t^{\prime},\lambda^{3}q^{\prime},\lambda\dot{q}^{\prime};\lambda^{-1}f,k\right)=\lambda^{-4}g\left(t,q,\dot{q},t^{\prime},q^{\prime},\dot{q}^{\prime};f,k\right).\end{array}

(47)

The original Kolmogorov formula contains two typos, making it dimensionally
incorrect since the term

\frac{3\left[q^{\prime}-q-\frac{\dot{q}^{\prime}+\dot{q}}{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{k^{3}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{3}}

in the exponent is not nondimensional, as it should be, and has dimension
T^{6}L^{-1}
, while the prefactor

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi k^{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{2}}\end{array}

has dimension
T^{4}L^{-1}
, instead of the right dimension
TL^{-2}
.

Third, due to yet another typo, the solution given by Eq. (
39
)
does not converge to the initial condition in the limit
t^{\prime}\rightarrow t
. Indeed, asymptotically, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}g\sim P^{H}\left(\frac{k^{3}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{3}}{6},q^{\prime}-q\right)P^{H}\left(2k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right),\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}\right)\rightarrow 4\delta\left(q^{\prime}-q\right)\delta\left(\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}\right).\end{array}

(48)

Here
P^{H}
is the standard heat kernel:

\begin{array}[]{c}P^{H}\left(\tau,\eta\right)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2\tau}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau}}.\end{array}

(49)

However, not all is lost. Dimensional analysis shows that the correct
solution
g\left(t,q,\dot{q},t^{\prime},q^{\prime},\dot{q}^{\prime};f,k\right)
of Eq. (
38
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}g=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left[\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}-f\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{4k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)}-\frac{3\left[q^{\prime}-q-\frac{\dot{q}^{\prime}+\dot{q}}{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{3}}\right\},\end{array}

(50)

which is not far from Kolmogorov’s formula. The correct solution of Eq. (
42
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}g=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\left[\dot{q}^{\prime}-\dot{q}-f\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{2k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)}-\frac{6\left[q^{\prime}-q-\frac{\dot{q}^{\prime}+\dot{q}}{2}\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)\right]^{2}}{k\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)^{3}}\right\}.\end{array}

(51)

4.3
Direct Verification

In order to avoid confusion, from now on, we shall change the notation to
make the formulas easier to read. Specifically, we assume that
x

represents the position of a particle at time
t
and
\xi
its position at
time
\tau
, while
y
represents its velocity at time
t
, and
\theta

its velocity at time
\tau
, so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\left(t,q,\dot{q}\right)\rightarrow\left(\tau,\xi,\theta\right),\ \ \ \left(t^{\prime},q^{\prime},\dot{q}^{\prime}\right)\rightarrow\left(t,x,y\right).\end{array}

(52)

One of our objectives is deriving the (corrected) Kolmogorov formula from
first principles using Kelvin Waves. Subsequently, we show how to use it in
the financial mathematics context.

The governing SDE can be written as

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=bdt+\sqrt{a}dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta.\end{array}

(53)

The corresponding Fokker-Planck- Kolmogorov problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)-\frac{1}{2}aP_{yy}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)\\
\\
+yP_{x}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)+bP_{y}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,x,y,\tau,\xi,\theta\right)=\delta\left(x-\xi\right)\delta\left(y-\theta\right).\end{array}

(54)

We claim that the solution of Eqs (
54
) is as follows:

P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi at^{2}}\exp\left(-\Phi\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)\right),

(55)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)^{2}}{2at}+\frac{6\left(x-\xi-\frac{\left(y+\theta\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{2}\right)^{2}}{at^{3}}=\frac{A^{2}}{2}+6B^{2},\end{array}

(56)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}A=\frac{\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)}},\ \ \left[A\right]=1,\\
\\
B=\frac{\left(x-\xi-\frac{\left(y+\theta\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}},\ \ \ \left[B\right]=1.\end{array}

(57)

Let us check that
P
satisfies the Fokker-Plank equation and the initial
conditions. We have:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi_{t}=-\left(\frac{A^{2}}{2\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{bA}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)}}+\frac{18B^{2}}{\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{6\left(y+\theta\right)B}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}}\right),\\
\\
\ \Phi_{x}=\frac{12B}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}},\ \ \ \Phi_{y}=\frac{A-6B}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)}},\ \ \ \Phi_{yy}=\frac{4}{a\left(t-\tau\right)},\end{array}

(58)

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{P_{t}}{P}=-\frac{2}{\left(t-\tau\right)}-\Phi_{t},\ \ \ \frac{P_{x}}{P}=-\Phi_{x},\ \ \ \frac{P_{y}}{P}=-\Phi_{y},\ \ \ \frac{P_{yy}}{P}=-\Phi_{yy}+\Phi_{x}^{2},\end{array}

(59)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}^{K}-\frac{1}{2}aP_{yy}^{K}+yP_{x}^{K}+bP_{y}^{K}\\
\\
=P^{K}\left(-\frac{2}{\left(t-\tau\right)}-\Phi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}a\Phi_{yy}-\frac{1}{2}a\Phi_{y}^{2}-y\Phi_{x}-b\Phi_{y}\right)\\
\\
=P^{K}\left(-\frac{2}{\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{A^{2}}{2\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{bA}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)}}+\frac{18B^{2}}{\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{6\left(y+\theta\right)B}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}}\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2}{\left(t-\tau\right)}-\frac{\left(A-6B\right)^{2}}{2\left(t-\tau\right)}-\frac{12yB}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}}-\frac{b\left(A-6B\right)}{\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)}}\right)\\
\\
=0.\end{array}

(60)

When
\left(t-\tau\right)\rightarrow 0
we have the following asymptotic
expression:

\begin{array}[]{c}P^{K}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)\sim P^{H}\left(\frac{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}{12},x-\xi\right)P^{H}\left(a\left(t-\tau\right),y-\theta\right)\\
\\
\rightarrow\delta\left(x-\xi\right)\delta\left(y-\theta\right).\end{array}

(61)

4.4
Solution via Kelvin Waves

Now, we wish to derive Kolmogorov’s formula by using Kelvin waves (or an
affine ansatz). To this end, we start with the problem of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H_{t}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)-\frac{1}{2}aH_{yy}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)\\
\\
+yH_{x}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)+bH_{y}\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)=0,\\
\\
H\left(\tau,x,y,\tau,\xi,\theta,k,l\right)=\exp\left(ik\left(x-\xi\right)+il\left(y-\theta\right)\right).\end{array}

(62)

Here

\begin{array}[]{c}\ \left[k\right]=L^{-1},\ \ \ \left[l\right]=TL^{-1},\ \ \left[H\right]=1.\end{array}

(63)

By using the well-known results concerning the inverse Fourier transform of
the
\delta
-function, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)dkdl.\end{array}

(64)

To calculate
H
, we use the affine ansatz and represent it in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)=\exp\left(\Psi\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)\right),\end{array}

(65)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y,k,l\right)=\alpha\left(t\right)+ik\left(x-\xi\right)+i\beta\left(t\right)y-il\theta.\end{array}

(66)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{H_{t}}{H}=\Psi_{t}=\left(\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\beta^{\prime}\left(t\right)y\right),\ \ \ \frac{H_{x}}{H}=\Psi_{x}=ik,\\
\\
\frac{H_{y}}{H}=\Psi_{y}=i\beta\left(t\right),\ \ \ \frac{H_{yy}}{H}=\Psi_{y}^{2}=-\beta^{2}\left(t\right).\end{array}

(67)

Using this ansatz in Eqs (
62
) yields:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}a\beta^{2}\left(t\right)+i\beta^{\prime}\left(t\right)y+iky+ib\beta\left(t\right)=0,\\
\\
\alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\ \ \ \beta\left(\tau\right)=l,\end{array}

(68)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}a\beta^{2}\left(t\right)+ib\beta\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
\beta^{\prime}\left(t\right)+k=0,\ \ \ \beta\left(\tau\right)=l.\end{array}

(69)

Straightforward calculation shows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\beta\left(t\right)=-k\left(t-\tau\right)+l,\\
\\
\alpha\left(t\right)=-\frac{1}{2}a\left(\frac{k^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}{3}-kl\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}+l^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)\right)-ib\left(-\frac{k\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}+l\left(t-\tau\right)\right).\end{array}

(70)

Equations (
64
,
65
,
66
,
70
) yield:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\\
\\
\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}a\left(\frac{k^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}{3}-kl\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}+l^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+ik\left(x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\right)+il\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\right)dkdl.\end{array}

(71)

It is clear that
P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)
can be viewed as
the characteristic function of the Gaussian density in the
\left(k,l\right)
space, evaluated at the point
\left(x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)\right.

\left.+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2},y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{\left(\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\right)\right)^{1/2}}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\\
\\
\times G\left(t,k,l\right)\exp\left(ik\left(x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\right)+il\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\right)dkdl,\end{array}

(72)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}G\left(t,k,l\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\left(\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\right)\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
l\end{array}\right)\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}k\\
l\end{array}\right)\right),\end{array}

(73)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}\mathbb{=}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{12}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}&\frac{6}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}\\
\frac{6}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}&\frac{4}{a\left(t-\tau\right)}\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\right)=\frac{12}{a^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)^{4}}.\end{array}

(74)

As before,
\cdot
denotes the scalar product. Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi a\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}\exp\left(-\Omega\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)\right),\end{array}

(75)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\\
y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\end{array}\right)\cdot\mathfrak{C}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\\
y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\end{array}\right)\\
\\
=\frac{6\left(x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}+\frac{6\left(x-\xi-y\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2}\right)\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}+\frac{2\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)^{2}}{a\left(t-\tau\right)}\\
\\
=6\left(B-\frac{A}{2}\right)^{2}+6\left(B-\frac{A}{2}\right)A+2A^{2}\\
\\
=6B^{2}-6AB+\frac{3A^{2}}{2}+6AB-3A^{2}+2A^{2}\\
\\
=\frac{A^{2}}{2}+6B^{2}\\
\\
=\Phi\left(t,x,y\,,\xi,\theta\right),\end{array}

(76)

as expected. This calculation completes our derivation of the corrected
Kolmogorov formula.

P
is a bivariate Gaussian distribution. Completing the square, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Phi=\frac{\left(y-\theta-b\left(t-\tau\right)\right)^{2}}{2a\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{6\left(x-\xi-\frac{\left(y+\theta\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{2}\right)^{2}}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}\\
\\
=\frac{6}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}\left(x-p\right)^{2}-\frac{6}{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}\left(x-p\right)\left(y-q\right)+\frac{2}{a\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(y-q\right)^{2},\end{array}

(77)

and represent
P
the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)}\left(\frac{\left(x-p\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{x}^{2}}-\frac{2\rho\left(x-p\right)\left(y-q\right)}{\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}}+\frac{\left(y-q\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{y}^{2}}\right)\right)}{2\pi\sigma_{x}\sigma_{y}\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}},\end{array}

(78)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma_{x}=\sqrt{\frac{a\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}{3}},\ \ \ \sigma_{y}=\sqrt{a\left(t-\tau\right)},\ \ \ \rho=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2},\\
\\
p=\xi+\theta\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{b\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}}{2},\ \ q=\theta+b\left(t-\tau\right).\end{array}

(79)

5
Gaussian Processes

5.1
The General Case

We now consider a more general Kolmogorov-type SDE solvable via the Kelvin
(or affine) ansatz:

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(x\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xx\right)}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xy\right)}\mathbf{y}_{t}\right)dt,\ \ \ \mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\xi,}\\
\\
d\mathbf{y}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(y\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yx\right)}\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yy\right)}\mathbf{y}_{t}\right)dt+\mathfrak{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\left(yy\right)}d\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\left(y\right)},\ \ \ \mathbf{y}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\theta,}\end{array}

(80)

where
\mathbf{x}_{{}_{t}}=\left(x_{m}\right)
and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(x\right)}=\left(b_{m}^{\left(x\right)}\right)
are
\left(M\times 1\right)
column vectors,
\mathbf{y}_{t}=\left(y_{n}\right)^{T}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(y\right)}=\left(b_{n}^{\left(y\right)}\right)
are
\left(N\times 1\right)
column vectors,
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xx\right)}=\left(b_{mm^{\prime}}^{\left(xx\right)}\right)
is
\left(M\times M\right)
matrix,
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xy\right)}=\left(b_{mn}^{\left(xy\right)}\right)
is
\left(M\times N\right)
matrix,
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yx\right)}=\left(b_{nm}^{\left(xx\right)}\right)
is
\left(N\times M\right)
matrix,
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yy\right)}=\left(b_{nn^{\prime}}^{\left(xx\right)}\right)
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\left(yy\right)}=\left(\sigma_{nn^{\prime}}\right)
are
\left(N\times N\right)
matrices. Below, we assume that the corresponding coefficients
are time-dependent. As usual,
\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\left(y\right)}
is a
standard
N
-dimensional Brownian motion.

More compactly, we can write the system of SDEs as follows:

\begin{array}[]{cc}d\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)dt+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\left(yy\right)}d\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\left(y\right)}\end{array}\right),&\mathbf{z}_{\tau}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\xi}\\
\mathbf{\theta}\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(81)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{y}\end{array}\right),\ \ \ \mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}^{\left(x\right)}\\
\mathbf{b}^{\left(y\right)}\end{array}\right),\ \ \ \mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xx\right)}&\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xy\right)}\\
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yx\right)}&\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yy\right)}\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(82)

so that
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}
are
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector, and
\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}
is a
\left(I\times I\right)
matrix, where
I=M+N
. We also introduce a scalar
b^{\left(z\right)}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xx\right)}\right)+\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yx\right)}\right)
.

The corresponding Fokker-Plank problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathfrak{A}P_{\mathbf{yy}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)\\
\\
+\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(z\right)}\mathbf{z}\right)\cdot P_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\mathbf{z},\tau,\mathbf{\zeta}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\xi}\right)\delta\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{\theta}\right),\end{array}

(83)

where
\mathfrak{A}
is the covariance matrix,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A}=\left(a_{nn^{\prime}}\right)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_{nk}\sigma_{n^{\prime}k}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\left(yy\right)}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\left(yy\right)T}.\end{array}

(84)

Explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N}a_{nn^{\prime}}P_{y_{n}y_{n^{\prime}}}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}\left(b_{i}^{\left(z\right)}+\sum\limits_{i^{\prime}=1}^{I}b_{ii^{\prime}}^{\left(zz\right)}x_{i^{\prime}}\right)P_{z_{i}}+b^{\left(z\right)}P=0,\end{array}

(85)

We use the same Kelvin ansatz as before and represent
P
in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z,m}\right)d\mathbf{m},\\
\\
H\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z,m}\right)=\exp\left(\Psi\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z,m}\right)\right),\\
\\
\Psi\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z,m}\right)=\alpha\left(t\right)+i\Upsilon\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{\zeta},\end{array}

(86)

where
\mathbf{m}\mathcal{=}\left(m_{i}\right)
is an
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector,
\mathbf{m}=\left(\mathbf{k,l}\right)^{T}
,
\mathbf{k}\mathcal{=}\left(k_{m}\right)
is an
\left(M\times 1\right)

column vector,
\mathbf{l}=\left(l_{n}\right)~
is an
\left(N\times 1\right)
column vector,
\mathbf{\Upsilon=}\left(\upsilon_{i}\right)

is an
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector,
\ \mathbf{\Upsilon}=\left(\mathbf{\Gamma},\mathbf{\Delta}\right)^{T}
,
\mathbf{\Gamma}=\left(\gamma_{m}^{\left(x\right)}\right)~
is an
\left(M\times 1\right)

column vector
,

\mathbf{\Delta}=\left(\delta_{n}^{\left(y\right)}\right)~
is an
\left(N\times 1\right)
column vector, and

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(\tau\right)=\left(\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(\tau\right),\mathbf{\Delta}\left(\tau\right)\right)^{T}=\mathbf{m}=\left(\mathbf{k,l}\right)^{T}.\end{array}

(87)

As before:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{H_{t}}{H}=\Psi_{t}=\left(\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}\right),\ \ \ \frac{H_{\mathbf{x}}}{H}=\Psi_{\mathbf{x}}=i\mathbf{\Gamma}\left(t\right),\\
\\
\frac{H_{\mathbf{y}}}{H}=\Psi_{\mathbf{y}}=i\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right),\ \ \ \frac{H_{\mathbf{yy}}}{H}=\Psi_{\mathbf{y}}^{2}=-\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Delta}^{T}\left(t\right).\end{array}

(88)

The equations for
\alpha,\mathbf{\Upsilon}
have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\mathbf{z}\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}=0.\end{array}

(89)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)T}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(\tau\right)=\mathbf{m}=\left(\mathbf{k,l}\right)^{T},\end{array}

(90)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+b^{\left(z\right)}=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0.\end{array}

(91)

Let
\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)
is the fundamental solution of the
homogeneous ODE (
91
), i.e., the matrix such that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}^{\prime}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)T}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\mathfrak{I},\end{array}

(92)

where
\mathfrak{I}
is the identity matrix. The well-known Liouville’s
formula, which we shall use shortly, yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\det\left(\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)=\exp\left(-\int_{\tau}^{t}b^{\left(z\right)}\left(s\right)ds\right).\end{array}

(93)

The solution of Eq. (
91
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)=\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}.\end{array}

(94)

It is convenient to write
\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)
in the block
form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(xx\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)&\mathfrak{L}^{\left(xy\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)&\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(95)

It follows from Eq. (
90
) that

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\left(t\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}-i\mathbf{m\cdot d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right),\end{array}

(96)

where
\mathfrak{C}^{-1}
is an
I\times I
positive-definite matrix split
into four blocks of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(\tau,s\right)ds&\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(\tau,s\right)ds\\
\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(\tau,s\right)ds&\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(\tau,s\right)ds\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(97)

while
\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}=\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(x\right)},\mathbf{d}^{\left(y\right)}\right)^{T}
,
\mathbf{d}^{\left(x\right)}

and
\mathbf{d}^{\left(y\right)}
are
\left(M\times 1\right)
and
\left(N\times 1\right)
column vectors, and
\varpi
is a scalar:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}\left(s\right)ds,\end{array}

(98)

\begin{array}[]{c}\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}b^{\left(z\right)}\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(99)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)\\
\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}+i\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{z}-i\mathbf{m}\cdot\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathbf{\zeta}\right)-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}+i\mathbf{m}\cdot\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z-d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{-\zeta}\right)-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right).\end{array}

(100)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I/2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G\left(t,\mathbf{m}\right)\\
\\
\times\exp\left(i\mathbf{m}\cdot\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z-d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{-\zeta}\right)\right)d\mathbf{m},\end{array}

(101)

where
G\left(t,\mathbf{m}\right)
is the density of a multivariate
Gaussian distribution in the
\mathbf{m}
-space. It is clear that
P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)
is proportional to the
characteristic function of
G
evaluated at the point
\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z-d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{-\zeta}\right)
, so that

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I/2}}\\
\\
\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z-d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{-\zeta}\right)\cdot\mathfrak{C}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z-d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{-\zeta}\right)\right).\end{array}

(102)

93
) allows one to rewrite (

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\mathrm{N}\left(\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right),\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right),\end{array}

(103)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{=}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathbf{\zeta}\right).\end{array}

(104)

5.2
Example: Kolmogorov Process

We now wish to extend the Kolmogorov formula to the case when
b
and
a

are functions of time. In order to make connections with other cases a bit
easier to draw, we use the specific notation:
b\left(t\right)
and
a\left(t\right)
. The corresponding SDE has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=b\left(t\right)dt+\sqrt{a\left(t\right)}dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta.\end{array}

(105)

Accordingly, Eq. (
92
) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}\left(\tau,t\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(106)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
-\left(t-\tau\right)&1\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\left(t-\tau\right)&1\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(107)

Once we know
\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)
, we can compute
\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)
,
\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(d^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,t\right),d^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{T}
,
\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
d^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\phi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\phi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\end{array}

(108)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\phi_{i}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(s-\tau\right)^{i}b\left(s\right)ds,\ \ \ \ \ \psi_{i}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(s-\tau\right)^{i}a\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(109)

Next, we calculate
\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)
, and
\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\left(t-\tau\right)\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\left(t-\tau\right)&1\end{array}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}-2\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(110)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{=}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\phi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\xi+\left(\phi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)+\theta\right)\left(t-\tau\right)\\
\phi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)+\theta\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(111)

Accordingly,
P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)
is a bivariate
Gaussian distribution of the form (
103
), with

\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma_{x}^{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}-2\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right),\ \ \ \sigma_{y}^{2}=\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right),\\
\\
\rho\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\left(\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\sqrt{\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}-2\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(t-\tau\right)+\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}},\\
\\
p\left(\tau,t\right)=-\phi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\xi+\left(\phi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)+\theta\right)\left(t-\tau\right),\ \ \ q\left(\tau,t\right)=\phi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)+\theta.\end{array}

(112)

It is easy to see that Eq. (
112
) coincides with Eq. (
78
)
when
a,b
are constant.

5.3
Example: OU Process

5.3.1
OU Process

For benchmarking purposes, we start with deriving the with the well-known
t.p.d.f. for the OU process:

\begin{array}[]{c}dy=\left(\chi\left(t\right)-\kappa\left(t\right)y\right)dt+\varepsilon\left(t\right)dZ,\ \ \ y\left(\tau\right)=\theta.\end{array}

(113)

The corresponding Fokker-Planck problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}P_{yy}\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)+\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)P_{y}\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)-\kappa P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\delta\left(y-\theta\right).\end{array}

(114)

The associated function
H\left(\tau,\theta,t,y,l\right)
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\alpha\left(t\right)+i\Delta\left(t\right)y-il\theta\right),\end{array}

(115)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}+i\chi\Delta-\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
\Delta^{\prime}-\kappa\Delta=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l.\end{array}

(116)

It is clear that

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta\left(\tau,t\right)=e^{\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}l,\\
\\
\alpha\left(\tau,t\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)l^{2}-\left(\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds\right)il+\eta\left(\tau,t\right).\end{array}

(117)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\eta\left(\tau,t\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}\kappa\left(s\right)ds,\end{array}

(118)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}e^{2\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\varepsilon^{2}\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(119)

Thus, we obtain the following well-known expression:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)l^{2}}{2}\right.\\
\\
\left.+\left(e^{\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}y-\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds-\theta\right)il+\eta\left(\tau,t\right)\right)dl\\
\\
=\frac{\exp\left(\eta\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(e^{\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}y-\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds-\theta\right)^{2}}{2\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)\\
\\
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hat{\psi}_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(y-\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(s,t\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds-e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\theta\right)^{2}}{2\hat{\psi}_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right),\end{array}

(120)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\psi}_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{\exp\left(2\eta\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}e^{-2\eta\left(s,t\right)}\varepsilon^{2}\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(121)

For further discussion, see the original paper by
Uhlenbeck & Ornstein 1930
, as
well as
Chandresekhar 1943
;
Risken 1989
, and references therein.

For time-independent parameters, Eq. (
120
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{\pi\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{\kappa}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(y-\frac{\chi}{\kappa}-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(\theta-\frac{\chi}{\kappa}\right)\right)^{2}}{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{\kappa}}\right).\end{array}

(122)

5.3.2
Degenerate Augmented OU Process

In this subsection, we consider an augmented one-dimensional OU process:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\chi\left(t\right)-\kappa\left(t\right)y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon\left(t\right)dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta.\end{array}

(123)

To align our analysis with the existing body of work, we switch from the
general notation, used above, to a specific one customary used for the OU
process. Here and below, the word “augmentation” means that we expand the original process by
incorporating its integral or other path-dependent characteristics, such as
running maximum or minimum as part of the process. The augmentation is very
useful for handling large classes of path-dependent options; details can be
found in
Lipton 2001
, Chapter 13.

In the case in question, Eq. (
92
) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}\left(\tau,t\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&-\kappa\left(t\right)\end{array}\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(124)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
-\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(s,t\right)}ds&e^{\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds&e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(125)

where
\eta
is given by Eq. (
118
).

Now, we can compute
\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)
,
\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(d^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,t\right),d^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{T}
,
\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(126)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}e^{2\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\varepsilon^{2}\left(s\right)ds,\\
\\
\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=-\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{s}e^{\eta\left(s^{\prime},t\right)}ds^{\prime}\right)e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\varepsilon^{2}\left(s\right)ds,\\
\\
\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{s}e^{\eta\left(s^{\prime},t\right)}ds^{\prime}\right)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(127)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
d^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\int_{\tau}^{s}e^{\eta\left(s^{\prime},s\right)}ds^{\prime}\right)\chi\left(s\right)ds\\
\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds,\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(128)

\begin{array}[]{c}\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)=-\eta\left(\tau,t\right).\end{array}

(129)

Next, we calculate
\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)
, and
\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds\\
0&e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds&e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\end{array}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)&h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(130)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\psi_{0}\left(\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds\right)^{2}-2\psi_{1}\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds+\psi_{2},\\
\\
h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\psi_{0}\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds-\psi_{1}\right)e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)},\\
\\
h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\psi_{0}e^{-2\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}.\end{array}

(131)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}p\left(\tau,t\right)\\
q\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\int_{\tau}^{s}e^{\eta\left(s^{\prime},s\right)}ds^{\prime}\right)\chi\left(s\right)ds+\xi\\
\\
+\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{-\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}ds\left(\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds+\theta\right),\\
\\
e^{-\eta\left(\tau,t\right)}\left(\int_{\tau}^{t}e^{\eta\left(\tau,s\right)}\chi\left(s\right)ds+\theta\right),\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(132)

Thus,
P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)
is a bivariate Gaussian
distribution of the form (
103
) with the covariance matrix
\mathfrak{H}
, given by Eq. (
131
) centered at the point
\mathbf{r=}\left(p,q\right)^{T}
given by Eq. (
132
). Explicitly, we have

\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma_{x}^{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right),\ \ \ \sigma_{y}^{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right),\ \ \ \rho\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)}{\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}.\end{array}

(133)

When
\chi,\kappa,\varepsilon
are constant, the above formulas become
significantly simpler. Namely,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\frac{1-e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\kappa}&e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
\frac{1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\kappa}&e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(134)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(e^{2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)}{2\kappa},\\
\\
\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)^{2}}{2\kappa^{2}},\\
\\
\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(e^{2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-4e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}+3+2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)\right)}{2\kappa^{3}},\end{array}

(135)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{d}^{\left(z\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}d^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
d^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\left(t-\tau\right)-\frac{e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1}{\kappa}\right)\frac{\chi}{\kappa}\\
\left(e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)\frac{\chi}{\kappa}\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(136)

\begin{array}[]{c}\varpi\left(\tau,t\right)=-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right),\end{array}

(137)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)&h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\\
\\
h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa^{3}}\left(-3+4e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}+2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)\right),\\
\\
h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)^{2},\\
\\
h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{2\kappa}.\end{array}

(138)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi+\frac{\left(\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)-\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\right)\chi}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\theta}{\kappa}\\
\\
\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\chi}{\kappa}+e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\theta\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(139)

Thus,
P\left(\tau,\xi,\theta,t,x,y\right)
is a bivariate Gaussian
distribution of the form (
103
) with the covariance matrix
\mathfrak{H}
, given by Eq. (
138
) and the expected value
\mathbf{r=}\left(p,q\right)^{T}
given by Eq. (
139
).

Below, we need to find the marginal distribution of
x
, which we denote by
Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,\theta,t,x\right)
. It is well known that
marginal distributions of a multivariate Gaussian distribution are also
Gaussian, so that:

\begin{array}[]{c}Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,\theta,t,x\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\exp\left(\frac{\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right),\end{array}

(140)

where
h_{0}
is given by Eqs (
138
). At the same time, the density
of marginal distribution for
y
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}Q^{\left(y\right)}\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\exp\left(\frac{\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right),\end{array}

(141)

where
h_{2}
is given by Eqs (
138
), which is the familiar density
of the OU process derived in the previous section.

5.4
Example: Evolution of Free and Harmonically Bound Particles

We can use the above results to revisit the motion of free and harmonically
bound particles considered in Section
3
.

To describe a free particle, we assume that
\chi=0
. Eq. (
138
)
does not change, while Eq. (
139
) becomes a bit simpler:

\begin{array}[]{c}\left(p\left(\tau,t\right),q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{T}=\left(\xi+\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\theta}{\kappa},e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\theta\right)^{T}.\end{array}

(142)

It is clear that Eqs (
21
), (
22
) and (
138
), (
142
) are in agreement.

Analysis of a harmonically bound particle requires additional efforts. In
the case in question, Eq. (
92
) can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}^{\prime}\left(\tau,t\right)+\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\omega^{2}\\
1&-\kappa\end{array}\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(143)

The corresponding second-order equation is

\begin{array}[]{c}l^{\prime\prime}-\kappa l^{\prime}+\omega^{2}l=0,\end{array}

(144)

while the characteristic equation and its solutions can be written as
follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{2}-\kappa\lambda+\omega^{2}=0,\\
\\
\lambda_{\pm}=\frac{\kappa\pm\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}}}{2}=-\mu_{\mp},\end{array}

(145)

where
\mu_{\pm}
are given by Eq. (
25
). It is easy to check that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\left(\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)&\omega^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\\
-\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)&\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(146)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)&-\omega^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\\
\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)&-\left(\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(147)

\begin{array}[]{c}\det\mathfrak{L=}\left(\det\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{=}\mathsf{E}_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}=\exp\left(\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)\right),\end{array}

(148)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathsf{E}_{+}=e^{\lambda_{\pm}\left(t-\tau\right)}.\end{array}

(149)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\mathrm{N}\left(\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right),\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right),\end{array}

(150)

with

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{r}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{=}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathbf{\zeta}.\end{array}

(151)

Here

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r=}\left(p,q\right)^{T}=\left(\frac{\left(\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\xi+\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\theta\right)}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}}},-\frac{\left(\omega^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\xi+\left(\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\theta\right)}{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}}}\right)^{T}.\end{array}

(152)

In the limit
\omega^{2}\rightarrow 0
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{r=}\left(p,q\right)^{T}=\left(\xi+\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\theta}{\kappa},e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\theta\right)^{T}.\end{array}

(153)

Next,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\varepsilon^{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)T}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(s\right)ds&\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)T}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(s\right)ds\\
\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)T}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(s\right)ds&\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)T}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(s\right)ds\end{array}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\psi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\psi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\psi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right)\equiv\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{\psi}_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)&-\bar{\psi}_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
-\bar{\psi}_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\bar{\psi}_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(154)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\psi}_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(s\right)-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(s\right)\right)^{2}ds\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\kappa}\left(\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{2}\left(t\right)-4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(t\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(t\right)+\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{2}\left(t\right)-\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)\right),\end{array}

(155)

\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\psi}_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(s\right)-\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(s\right)\right)\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(s\right)-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(s\right)\right)ds\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}^{2}\left(t\right)-2\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(t\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(t\right)+\mathsf{E}_{-}^{2}\left(t\right)\right),\end{array}

(156)

\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\psi}_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(s\right)-\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(s\right)\right)^{2}ds\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\kappa\omega^{2}}\left(\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{2}\left(t\right)-4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{+}\left(t\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\left(t\right)+\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{2}\left(t\right)-\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)\right).\end{array}

(157)

Further,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{-1}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)&h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\\

\bar{h}_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)&\bar{h}_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(158)

\begin{array}[]{c}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{h}_{0}&\bar{h}_{1}\\
\bar{h}_{1}&\bar{h}_{2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)&\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\\

\\
\times\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bar{\psi}_{2}&-\bar{\psi}_{1}\\

\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)&-\left(\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}-\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\right)\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(159)

Straightforward but very tedious calculation yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{h}_{0}=\frac{\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}{2\kappa\omega^{2}}\left(-\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}+4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}-\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}+\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right),\\
\\
\bar{h}_{1}=\frac{\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}-2\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}+\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}\right),\\
\\
\bar{h}_{2}=\frac{\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}{2\kappa}\left(-\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}+4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}-\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}+\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right),\end{array}

(160)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}h_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa\omega^{2}\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}\left(-\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}+4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}-\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}+\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right),\\
\\
h_{1}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}\left(\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}-2\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}+\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}\right),\\
\\
h_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa\left(\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right)}\left(-\kappa\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-2}+4\omega^{2}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-1}\mathsf{E}_{+}^{-1}-\kappa\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}^{-2}+\kappa^{2}-4\omega^{2}\right).\end{array}

(161)

In the limit

\begin{array}[]{c}h_{0}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa^{3}}\left(-3+2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)+4e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right),\\

so that Eqs. (

5.5
Example: Evolution of Vorticity of Two-Dimensional Flows

We shall briefly return to our point of departure and consider strictly
two-dimensional flows; see
Friedlander & Lipton-Lifschitz 2003
. Velocity fields of such
flows have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{V}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\left(V_{1}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right),V_{2}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right)\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{v}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\left(v_{1}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right),v_{2}\left(t,x_{1},x_{2}\right)\right).\end{array}

(163)

By virtue of incompressibility, we can introduce the so-called stream
functions such that

\begin{array}[]{c}V_{1}=-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{2}},\ \ \ V_{2}=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{1}},\ \ \ v_{1}=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{2}},\ \ \ v_{2}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{1}},\end{array}

(164)

and define the (scalar) vorticity as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\Delta\Psi,\ \ \ \omega=\Delta\psi.\end{array}

(165)

Contour lines of
\Psi
are called streamlines of the flow.

By using the above definitions, we write the two-dimensional the
Navier-Stokes equations as equations for the stream and vorticity:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{2}}\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{1}}\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial x_{2}}-\nu\Delta\Omega=0,\\
\\
\Delta\Psi-\Omega=0.\end{array}

(166)

It is clear that time-independent stream functions
\Psi
, quadratically
dependent of
\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)
generate exact solutions of Eqs (
166
). We are interested in fields consisting of pure strain and
pure rotation. The corresponding
\Psi
have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi\left(x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{4}\left(w\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}\right)-2sx_{1}x_{2}\right),\end{array}

where
\omega>s
, to ensure that streamlines are elliptic rather than
hyperbolic, so that

\begin{array}[]{c}V_{1}=-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(sx_{1}-wx_{2}\right),\ \ \ V_{2}=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{1}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(wx_{1}-sx_{2}\right).\end{array}

(167)

Small perturbations
\psi
of the time-independent quadratic stream
function
\Psi
satisfy the following equations:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{2}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x_{1}}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{2}}-\nu\Delta\omega=0,\\
\\
\Delta\psi-\omega=0.\end{array}

(168)

We wish to study the first equation (
168
) in isolation, which we
write explicitly as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{2}\left(sx_{1}-wx_{2}\right)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(wx_{1}-sx_{2}\right)\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial x_{2}}-\nu\Delta\omega=0,\end{array}

(169)

and supply with the initial condition at time
\tau
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\omega\left(\tau,x_{1},x_{2}\right)=\delta\left(x_{1}-\xi_{1}\right)\delta\left(x_{2}-\xi_{2}\right).\end{array}

(170)

Once the solution of Eqs (
169
), (
170
) is found, we can
find
\psi
by solving the corresponding Laplace equation.

Surprisingly, we can view this equation as the Fokker-Planck equation
associated with the following SDEs for
\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left(x_{1t},x_{2t}\right)

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{z}_{t}=\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}_{t}dt+\mathbf{\Sigma}d\mathbf{W}_{t},\ \ \ \mathbf{z}_{\tau}=\left(\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\right)^{T},\end{array}

(171)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{B=}\frac{1}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&-w\\
w&-s\end{array}\right),\ \ \ \mathbf{\Sigma=}\sqrt{2\nu}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right).\end{array}

(172)

5.1
results. Eq. (

-w&-s\end{array}\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right).\end{array}

6
Non-Gaussian Processes

6.1
The General Case

For financial applications, it is useful to consider a more general equation
governing SDE of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{x}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(x\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xx\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(xy\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{y}_{t}\right)dt,\\
\\
d\mathbf{y}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(y\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yx\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{x}_{t}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{y}_{t}\right)dt\\
\\
+\mathfrak{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(t\right)\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{D}\left(t\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)^{1/2}d\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\left(y\right)},\\
\\
\mathbf{x}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\xi,\ \ \ y}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\theta,}\end{array}

(195)

or, more compactly,

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)dt\\
\\
+\left(\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\left(yy\right)}\left(t\right)\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{D}\left(t\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)^{1/2}d\mathbf{W}_{t}^{\left(y\right)}\end{array}\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{z}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\zeta=}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\xi}\\
\mathbf{\theta}\end{array}\right),\end{array}

(196)

Here, in addition to the quantities introduced in the previous section, we
define an
\left(N\times 1\right)
column vector
\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}
, and an
\left(N\times I\right)
matrix
\mathfrak{D}
. It is
convenient to introduce vectors
\mathbf{d}^{\left(i\right)}
equal to the

i
-th column of
\mathfrak{D}
.

Since the corresponding
\left(N\times N\right)
covariance matrix
\mathfrak{A}
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A=}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{D}\left(t\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)^{1/2}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{D}\left(t\right)\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)^{1/2}\right)^{T},\end{array}

(197)

it linearly depends on
\mathbf{z}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A=}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}+\mathfrak{D}\mathbf{z}\right)\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}^{T}=\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}+\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{M+N}\mathfrak{A}^{\left(i\right)}z_{i},\end{array}

(198)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\right)\Sigma^{T},\ \ \ \mathfrak{A}^{\left(i\right)}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(i\right)}\right)\Sigma^{T}.\end{array}

(199)

In contrast to the Gaussian case, Eqs (
196
) have to be defined in
the domain
\boldsymbol{D}
such that

\boldsymbol{D=}\left\{\left.\mathbf{z}\right|\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}+\mathfrak{D}\mathbf{z\geq 0}\right\},

(200)

rather than in the whole space.

In financial engineering, covariance matrices of the form (
197
) were
introduced by
Dai & Singleton 2000
, they are discussed by
Duffie et al. 2003
;
Filipovic 2009
and many others.

The corresponding Fokker-Plank problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\xi},\mathbf{\theta,}t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathfrak{A}P_{\mathbf{zz}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\xi},\mathbf{\theta,}t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)\\
\\
+\left(\mathbf{b}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\cdot P_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\xi},\mathbf{\theta,}t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\xi},\mathbf{\theta,}t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\mathbf{z},\tau,\mathbf{\zeta}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(201)

Integral (
86
) expressing
P
in terms of
H
of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z,m}\right)=\exp\left(\alpha\left(t\right)+i\Upsilon\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(202)

holds. The equations for
\alpha,\mathbf{\Upsilon}
have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\mathbf{z}\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}=0,\end{array}

(203)

or, more explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(i\right)}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)z_{i}\\
\\
+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}+\mathfrak{B}^{\left(zz\right)}\mathbf{z}\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}=0.\end{array}

(204)

Thus, the system of ODEs for
\alpha,\mathbf{\Upsilon}
can be written as
follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{b}^{\left(z\right)}\left(t\right)+b^{\left(z\right)}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\mathbf{\Upsilon}_{i}^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(i\right)}\mathbf{\Delta}\left(t\right)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}i\mathfrak{B}_{ij}^{\left(zz\right)}\mathbf{\Upsilon}_{j}\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}_{i}\left(\tau\right)=m_{i}.\end{array}

(205)

In the case in question, the equation for
\mathbf{\Upsilon}
is no longer
linear. Instead,
\mathbf{\Upsilon}
satisfies the so-called matrix Riccati
equation. Such equations are important for several applications, such as
optimal control. Solving a matrix Riccati equation is quite hard, so it is
more an art than a science. We shall report some of the results in this
direction below. However, in the one-dimensional case, the corresponding
Riccati equation can be converted into the second-order ODE, which can be
solved explicitly when the coefficients
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathbf{b}
,
b

are time-independent.

6.2
Example: Feller Process

6.2.1
Feller Process

For benchmarking purposes, we start with deriving the with the well-known
t.p.d.f. for the Feller process; see
Feller 1951
;
Feller 1952
:

\begin{array}[]{c}dy=\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)dt+\varepsilon\sqrt{y}dZ,\ \ \ y\left(\tau\right)=\theta.\end{array}

(206)

To guarantee that the process does not hit zero, we assume that

\begin{array}[]{c}\vartheta=\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1>0.\end{array}

(207)

The corresponding Fokker-Planck problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}yP_{yy}+\left(\chi-\varepsilon^{2}-\kappa y\right)P_{y}-\kappa P=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\delta\left(y-\theta\right).\end{array}

(208)

The associated function
H\left(\tau,\theta,t,y,l\right)
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\alpha\left(t\right)+i\Delta\left(t\right)y-il\theta\right),\end{array}

(209)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+i\left(\chi-\varepsilon^{2}\right)\Delta-\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
\ \ i\Delta^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}-i\kappa\Delta=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l.\end{array}

(210)

Thus,
\Delta
solves a nonlinear Riccati equation, which can be linearized
via a familiar substitution

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=\frac{2i\Omega^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{2}\Omega}.\end{array}

(211)

As a result, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime\prime}-\kappa\Omega^{\prime}=0,\ \ \ \Omega\left(\tau\right)=1,\ \ \ \Omega^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}l}{2},\end{array}

(212)

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}-2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\left(\ln\left(\Omega\right)\right)^{\prime}-\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0.\end{array}

(213)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\frac{2\kappa-i\varepsilon^{2}\left(e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)l}{2\kappa},\end{array}

(214)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime}=-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}l}{2},\end{array}

(215)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=\frac{2i\Omega^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{2}\Omega}=\frac{2\kappa le^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{2\kappa e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-i\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)l},\end{array}

(216)

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha=\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)+2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(\frac{2\kappa-i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)}{2\kappa}\right),\end{array}

(217)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)+2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(\frac{2\kappa-i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(e^{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)}{2\kappa}\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+il\left(\frac{2\kappa}{2\kappa e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}y-\theta\right)\right).\end{array}

(218)

New variable,
l\rightarrow\eta
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\eta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(\tau-\tau\right)}\right)}{4\kappa e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}l,\ \ \ l=\frac{4\kappa e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(\tau-\tau\right)}\right)}\eta.\end{array}

(219)

and rescaled
H
,
Hdl\rightarrow\tilde{H}d\eta
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{H}=2M\exp\left(2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(1-2i\eta\right)+2Mi\eta\left(\frac{y}{1-2i\eta}-\bar{\theta}\right)\right),\end{array}

(220)

where
M
appears due to the change of variables, and
N
is a prefactor:

\begin{array}[]{c}M=\frac{2\kappa}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(\tau-\tau\right)}\right)},\\
\\
N=\frac{4\kappa e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(\tau-\tau\right)}\right)}=2MZ,\\
\\
Z=e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)},\\
\\
\bar{\theta}=e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\theta=Z\theta.\end{array}

(221)

Finally,

\begin{array}[]{c}Q\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{2M}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(1-2i\eta\right)+2Mi\eta\left(\frac{y}{1-2i\eta}-\bar{\theta}\right)\right)d\eta,\end{array}

(222)

A well-known formula yields

\begin{array}[]{c}Q\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=Me^{-M\left(y+\bar{\theta}\right)}\left(\frac{\bar{\theta}}{y}\right)^{-\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{-\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{y\bar{\theta}}\right)\\
\\
=Me^{-M\left(y+\bar{\theta}\right)}\left(\frac{y}{\bar{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{y\bar{\theta}}\right),\end{array}

(223)

see, e.g.,
Lipton 2001
and references therein. The density
Q\left(y\right)
integrates to one:

\begin{array}[]{c}\int_{0}^{\infty}Q\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)dy=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u-v}\left(\frac{v}{u}\right)^{\vartheta/2}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\sqrt{uv}\right)dv=1,\end{array}

(224)

where
u=M\bar{\theta},\ \ \ v=My
. We shall use this fact below.

This derivation can be extended to cover augmented Feller processes, as we
shall do next. It is worth noting that studying the Feller process with
time-dependent coefficients is rather tricky and remains to be wholly done;
see, however,
Masoliver 2016
;
Giorno & Nobile 2021
and references therein.

6.2.2
Degenerate Augmented Feller Process

Equations

\begin{array}[]{c}dx=ydt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy=\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)dt+\varepsilon\sqrt{y}dZ,\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta.\end{array}

(225)

The Fokker-Planck equation:

\begin{array}[]{c}0=P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(yP\right)_{yy}+yP_{x}+\left(\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)P\right)_{y}\\
\\
=P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}yP_{yy}-\varepsilon^{2}P_{y}+yP_{x}+\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)P_{y}-\kappa P\\
\\
=P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}yP_{yy}+yP_{x}+\left(\chi-\varepsilon^{2}-\kappa y\right)P_{y}-\kappa P.\end{array}

(226)

The corresponding function
H
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\alpha\left(t\right)+ik\left(x-\xi\right)+i\Delta\left(t\right)y-il\theta\right),\end{array}

(227)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+i\Delta^{\prime}y+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}y+iky+i\Delta\left(\chi-\varepsilon^{2}-\kappa y\right)-\kappa=0,\\
\\
\alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l.\end{array}

(228)

Thus

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+i\left(\chi-\varepsilon^{2}\right)\Delta-\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\Delta^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta^{2}-i\kappa\Delta+ik=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l,\end{array}

(229)

As before, we can linearize the Riccati equation for
\Delta
via a
two-step linearization

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=\frac{2i\Lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}},\ \ \ \Lambda=\frac{\Omega^{\prime}}{\Omega},\end{array}

(230)

which yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}-2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\left(\ln\left(\Omega\right)\right)^{\prime}-\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
\Omega^{\prime\prime}-\kappa\Omega^{\prime}-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}\Omega=0,\ \ \ \Omega\left(\tau\right)=1,\ \ \ \Omega^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}l}{2}.\end{array}

(231)

We can represent
\Omega
in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=c_{+}e^{\lambda_{+}\left(t-\tau\right)}+c_{-}e^{\lambda_{+}\left(t-\tau\right)},\end{array}

(232)

where
\lambda_{\pm}
are solutions of the characteristic equation:

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{2}-\kappa\lambda-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}=0,\\
\\
\lambda_{\pm}=\mu\pm\zeta,\\
\\
\mu=\frac{\kappa}{2},\ \ \ \zeta=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+2i\varepsilon^{2}k}}{2},\end{array}

(233)

and
c_{\pm}
satisfy the following system of linear equations:

\begin{array}[]{c}c_{+}+c_{-}=1,\\
\\
\lambda_{+}c_{+}+\lambda_{-}c_{-}=-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}l}{2}.\end{array}

(234)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}c_{+}=-\frac{\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)}{4\zeta},\ \ \ c_{-}=\frac{\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)}{4\zeta}.\end{array}

(235)

It is useful to note that

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}=\mu^{2}-\zeta^{2}=-\frac{i\varepsilon^{2}k}{2}.\end{array}

(236)

For the sake of clarity, we introduce the following notation:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathsf{E}_{0}=e^{\mu\left(t-\tau\right)},\ \ \ \mathsf{E}_{\pm}=e^{\pm\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}.\end{array}

(237)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(\frac{-\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}}{4\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(238)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime}=\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(\frac{-\lambda_{+}\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{-}\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}}{4\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(239)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=\frac{2i\left(-\lambda_{+}\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{-}\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(-\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\end{array}

(240)

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha=2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(\frac{-\left(2\lambda_{-}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\left(2\lambda_{+}+i\varepsilon^{2}l\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}}{4\zeta}\right)+\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}.\end{array}

(241)

Accordingly,
H
can be written in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right.\\
\\
+2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(\frac{2\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)-i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{4\zeta}\right)\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\left(2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)+i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\right)y}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(2\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)-i\varepsilon^{2}l\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\right)}-il\theta\right).\end{array}

(242)

Change of variables:

\begin{array}[]{c}\eta=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)l}{4\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\\
\\
l=\frac{4\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\eta}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\equiv N\eta.\end{array}

(243)

allows one to factorize
H
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{H}=\tilde{H}_{1}\tilde{H}_{2},\end{array}

(244)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{H}_{1}=\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{2\zeta}\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)y}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\right),\\
\\
\tilde{H}_{2}=2M\exp\left(2\left(\frac{\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}-1\right)\ln\left(1-2i\eta\right)+2Mi\eta\left(\frac{y}{1-2i\eta}-\bar{\theta}\right)\right).\end{array}

(245)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}M=\frac{8\zeta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\\
\\
Z=\frac{\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)^{2}}{4\zeta^{2}}=\frac{N}{2M},\\
\\
\bar{\theta}=Z\theta.\end{array}

(246)

Integration with respect to
\eta
can be done analytically

\begin{array}[]{c}Q_{2}\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{2M}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{H}_{2}d\eta=Me^{-M\left(y+\bar{\theta}\right)}\left(\frac{y}{\bar{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{y\bar{\theta}}\right).\end{array}

(247)

which allows us to calculate
P
via a single inverse Fourier transform:

\begin{array}[]{c}P=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{2\zeta}\right)-Ry\right)\\
\\
\times Me^{-M\left(y+\bar{\theta}\right)}\left(\frac{y}{\bar{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{y\bar{\theta}}\right)dk\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{2\zeta}\right)\right)\\
\\
\times Me^{-\left(M+R\right)y-M\bar{\theta}}\left(\frac{y}{\bar{\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{y\bar{\theta}}\right)dk\end{array}

(248)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}R=-\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}.\end{array}

(249)

This expression is not as convenient to deal with due to the presence of the
term
\exp\left(-Ry\right)
. We wish to rewrite it in a form better
suited for further calculations. Further transformations to use the density
and transform extra dependence on
y
to dependence on
\theta
. We proceed
as follows. Define

\begin{array}[]{c}Z_{1}=\left(\frac{M}{M+R}\right)^{2}Z.\end{array}

(250)

Then

\begin{array}[]{c}Me^{-\left(M+R\right)y-MZ\theta}\left(\frac{y}{Z\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2M\sqrt{yZ\theta}\right)\\
\\
=\frac{M}{\left(M+R\right)}\left(M+R\right)e^{-\left(M+R\right)y-\frac{\left(M+R\right)^{2}}{M}Z_{1}\theta}\left(\frac{M}{M+R}\right)^{\vartheta}\\
\\
\times\left(\frac{y}{Z_{1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\left(M+R\right)\sqrt{yZ_{1}\theta}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\frac{M}{M+R}\right)^{\vartheta+1}e^{-\left(\frac{\left(M+R\right)^{2}}{M}-\left(M+R\right)\right)Z_{1}\theta}\left(M+R\right)e^{-\left(M+R\right)\left(y+Z_{1}\theta\right)}\\
\\
\times\left(\frac{y}{Z_{1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\left(M+R\right)\sqrt{yZ_{1}\theta}\right)\\
\\
=\left(\frac{M}{M+R}\right)^{\vartheta+1}e^{-\frac{MRZ}{\left(M+R\right)}\theta}\left(M+R\right)e^{-\left(M+R\right)\left(y+Z_{1}\theta\right)}\\
\\
\times\left(\frac{y}{Z_{1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\left(M+R\right)\sqrt{yZ_{1}\theta}\right),\end{array}

(251)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(t,x,y\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{M\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{2\zeta\left(M+R\right)}\right)-\frac{MRZ\theta}{M+R}\right)\\
\\
\times\left(M+R\right)e^{-\left(M+R\right)\left(y+Z_{1}\theta\right)}\left(\frac{y}{Z_{1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\left(M+R\right)\sqrt{yZ_{1}\theta}\right)dk.\end{array}

(252)

Explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{c}M+R=\frac{2\left(\left(\lambda_{+}^{2}+\lambda_{-}^{2}\right)-\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}^{2}+\mathsf{E}_{-}^{2}\right)\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\left(-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\\
\\
\frac{M}{M+R}=\frac{\left(\lambda_{+}-\lambda_{-}\right)^{2}}{\left(\lambda_{+}^{2}+\lambda_{-}^{2}\right)-\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}^{2}+\mathsf{E}_{-}^{2}\right)},\\
\\
\frac{MRZ}{M+R}=-\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\end{array}

(253)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(t,x,y\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\zeta}{\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}}\right)+\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\theta}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\right)\\
\\
\times\left(M+R\right)e^{-\left(M+R\right)\left(y+Z_{1}\theta\right)}\left(\frac{y}{Z_{1}\theta}\right)^{\frac{\vartheta}{2}}I_{\vartheta}\left(2\left(M+R\right)\sqrt{yZ_{1}\theta}\right)dk.\end{array}

(254)

Since the integral over
y
is equal to one, we can represent the marginal
distribution of
x
in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(t,x\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)e^{ik\left(x-\xi\right)}dk,\end{array}

(255)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\digamma\left(t,k\right)=\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\zeta}{\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}}\right)+\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\theta}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\right).\end{array}

(256)

Here
\mu,\zeta
are given by Eqs. (
233
). It is easy to check
that
Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(t,x\right)
integrates to one:

\begin{array}[]{c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q\left(t,x\right)dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)\exp\left(ik\left(x-\xi\right)\right)dkdx\\
\\
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)\delta\left(k\right)dk=\digamma\left(t,0\right)\\
\\
=\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\lambda_{-}\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=1.\end{array}

(257)

The expected value of
x
has the form:

In this Section, we consider several instances when an augmented OU process

6.3.1
Degenerate Augmented OU Process, I

Let
y_{t}
be a stochastic process and
z_{t}
be its moving average. Then

\begin{array}[]{c}z_{t}=\varkappa\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\varkappa\left(t-s\right)}y_{s}ds.\end{array}

(284)

A simple calculation yields:

\begin{array}[]{c}dz_{t}=\varkappa\left(y_{t}-z_{t}\right)dt.\end{array}

(285)

We assume that the process
y_{t}
is path-dependent, because its volatility

\sigma
depends on its moving average
z_{t}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}\left(y_{t}-z_{t}\right)},\end{array}

(286)

where
a_{0}>0
,
a_{1}<0
, in order to capture the skew. Thus, we can write
the governing degenerate system of SDEs as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}dy_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{0}+a_{1}\left(y_{t}-z_{t}\right)\right)dt+\sqrt{a_{0}+a_{1}\left(y_{t}-z_{t}\right)}dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\\
\\
dz_{t}=\varkappa\left(y_{t}-z_{t}\right)dt,\ \ \ z_{\tau}=\varrho.\end{array}

(287)

The Fokker-Planck equation

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{0}+a_{1}\left(y-z\right)\right)\left(P_{yy}+P_{y}\right)+\varkappa\left(y-z\right)P_{z}-\left(a_{1}+\varkappa\right)P=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\theta,\varrho,\tau,y,z\right)=\delta\left(y-\theta\right)\delta\left(z-\varrho\right).\end{array}

(288)

A representative Kelvin mode

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\alpha+i\Gamma y-ik\theta+i\Delta z-il\varrho\right).\end{array}

(289)

The system of ODEs for
\alpha,\Gamma,\Delta
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+\frac{a_{0}}{2}\left(\Gamma^{2}-i\Gamma\right)-\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\varkappa\right)=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\Gamma^{\prime}+\frac{a_{1}}{2}\left(\Gamma^{2}-i\Gamma\right)+i\varkappa\Delta=0,\ \ \ \Gamma\left(\tau\right)=k,\\
\\
i\Delta^{\prime}-\frac{a_{1}}{2}\left(\Gamma^{2}-i\Gamma\right)-i\varkappa\Delta=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l.\end{array}

(290)

Eqs (
290
) are matrix Riccati equations. In general, such equations
are very difficult to solve. However, the case under consideration is one of
relatively rare instances when a matrix Riccati equation can be solved
explicitly. We start with an observation:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma^{\prime}+\Delta^{\prime}=0,\ \ \ \Gamma\left(\tau\right)+\Delta\left(\tau\right)=k+l,\end{array}

(291)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=k+l-\Gamma.\end{array}

(292)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}i\Gamma^{\prime}+\frac{a_{1}}{2}\left(\Gamma^{2}-i\Gamma\right)-i\varkappa\Gamma+i\varkappa\left(k+l\right)=0,\ \ \ \Gamma\left(\tau\right)=k.\end{array}

(293)

As usual,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma=\frac{2i\Omega^{\prime}}{a_{1}\Omega},\end{array}

(294)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime\prime}-\frac{\left(a_{1}+2\varkappa\right)}{2}\Omega^{\prime}-\frac{ia_{1}\varkappa\left(k+l\right)}{2}=0,\\
\\
\Omega\left(\tau\right)=1,\ \ \ \Omega^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=-\frac{ia_{1}k}{2},\end{array}

(295)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\frac{\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(-\left(2\lambda_{-}+ia_{1}k\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\left(2\lambda_{+}+ia_{1}k\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{4\zeta},\end{array}

(296)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime}=\frac{\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(-\lambda_{+}\left(2\lambda_{-}+ia_{1}k\right)\mathsf{E}_{+}+\lambda_{-}\left(2\lambda_{+}+ia_{1}k\right)\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{4\zeta},\end{array}

(297)

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda_{\pm}=\mu\pm\zeta,\ \ \ \mu=\frac{\left(a_{1}+2\varkappa\right)}{4},\ \ \ \zeta=\frac{\sqrt{\left(a_{1}+2\varkappa\right)^{2}+8ia_{1}\varkappa\left(k+l\right)}}{4}.\end{array}

(298)

Eq. (
292
) yields:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta=-\frac{2i\Omega^{\prime}}{a_{1}\Omega}+k+l.\end{array}

(299)

Now,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Gamma^{2}-i\Gamma=-\frac{2i\left(\Gamma^{\prime}+\varkappa\Delta\right)}{a_{1}},\end{array}

(300)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{ia_{0}}{a_{1}}\left(\Gamma^{\prime}+\varkappa\Delta\right)+\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\varkappa\right),\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0.\end{array}

(301)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha=\frac{ia_{0}}{a_{1}}\left(\Gamma-k\right)+\frac{2a_{0}\varkappa}{a_{1}^{2}}\ln\left(\Omega\right)+\frac{ia_{0}\varkappa}{a_{1}}\left(k+l\right)\left(t-\tau\right)+\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\varkappa\right)\left(t-\tau\right)\\
\\
=-\frac{2a_{0}\Omega^{\prime}}{a_{1}^{2}\Omega}-\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}ik+\frac{2a_{0}\varkappa}{a_{1}^{2}}\ln\left(\Omega\right)+\frac{a_{0}\varkappa\left(t-\tau\right)}{a_{1}}i\left(k+l\right)+\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\varkappa\right)\left(t-\tau\right).\end{array}

(302)

Finally,

\begin{array}[]{c}H=\exp\left(\alpha+i\Gamma y-ik\theta+i\Delta z-il\varrho\right)\\
\\
=\exp\left(\left(\frac{a_{1}}{2}+\varkappa\right)\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{2\Omega^{\prime}}{a_{1}\Omega}\left(y-z+\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}\right)+\frac{2a_{0}\varkappa}{a_{1}^{2}}\ln\left(\Omega\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+i\left(k+l\right)\left(z+\frac{a_{0}\varkappa\left(t-\tau\right)}{a_{1}}\right)-ik\left(\theta+\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}\right)-il\varrho\right).\end{array}

(303)

We consider
y,z
, such that

\begin{array}[]{c}y-z+\frac{a_{0}}{a_{1}}>0,\end{array}

(304)

in order to make sure that
\sigma
given by Eq. (
286
) and the
integrand (
303
) are well defined.

6.3.2
Degenerate Augmented OU Process, II

On occasion, problems seemingly not of the type given by Eq. (
195
)
can be cast in the proper form via a suitable trick. Consider, for example,
the following system of SDEs:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}^{2}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dZ_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta.\end{array}

(305)

Superficially, it does not belong to the class of processes we have studied
above. However, by introducing a new variable
z_{t}=y_{t}^{2}
, we can
expand Eqs (
305
) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=z_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dZ_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\\
\\
dz_{t}=\left(\varepsilon^{2}+2\chi y_{t}-2\kappa z_{t}\right)+2\varepsilon y_{t}dZ_{t},\ \ \ z_{\tau}=\theta^{2}.\end{array}

(306)

These equations are “almost” in the
suitable form. The only snag is that we cannot claim that
y_{t}=\sqrt{z_{t}}
since
y_{t}
is not always positive.

The corresponding Fokker-Planck problem can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}P_{yy}-2\varepsilon^{2}yP_{yz}-2\varepsilon^{2}zP_{zz}\\
\\
+zP_{x}+\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)P_{y}+\left(\varepsilon^{2}+2\chi y_{t}-2\kappa z_{t}\right)P_{z}-3\kappa P=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,x,y,z\right)=\delta\left(x-\xi\right)\delta\left(y-\theta\right)\delta\left(z-\theta^{2}\right).\end{array}

(307)

As usual,
H
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}H\left(t,x,y,z,k,l,m\right)=\exp\left(\alpha\left(t\right)+ik\left(x-\xi\right)+i\Delta\left(t\right)y-il\theta+i\Xi\left(t\right)z-im\theta^{2}\right).\end{array}

(308)

The corresponding set of ODEs for
\alpha,\Delta,\Xi
is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta^{2}+i\chi\Delta+i\varepsilon^{2}\Xi-3\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\Delta^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\Xi-i\kappa\Delta+2i\chi\Xi=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l,\\
\\
i\Xi^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{2}\Xi^{2}-2i\kappa\Xi+ik=0,\ \ \ \Xi\left(\tau\right)=m.\end{array}

(309)

These equations are matrix Riccati equations.

Once again, the corresponding matrix Riccati equation can be solved
explicitly. Since the third equation is separable, and hence can be viewed
as a scalar Riccati equation, we can start with a familiar ansatz:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Xi=\frac{i\Omega^{\prime}}{2\varepsilon^{2}\Omega}.\end{array}

(310)

The corresponding characteristic equation and its solutions are as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{2}-2\kappa\lambda-2\varepsilon^{2}ik=0,\\
\\
\mu=\kappa,\ \ \ \zeta=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+2i\varepsilon^{2}k}.\end{array}

(311)

As usual, we can write:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=e^{\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(\frac{-\left(\lambda_{-}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}+\left(\lambda_{+}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{-\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}}{2\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(312)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega^{\prime}=e^{\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}\left(\frac{-\lambda_{+}\left(\lambda_{-}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}+\lambda_{-}\left(\lambda_{+}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{-\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}}{2\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(313)

\begin{array}[]{c}\Xi=\frac{i\left(-\lambda_{+}\left(\lambda_{-}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}+\lambda_{-}\left(\lambda_{+}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{-\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}\left(-\left(\lambda_{-}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}+\left(\lambda_{+}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)e^{-\zeta\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}.\end{array}

(314)

To linearize Eqs. (
309
) as a whole, we use the following ansatz:

\begin{array}[]{c}\Xi=\frac{i\Omega^{\prime}}{2\varepsilon^{2}\Omega},\ \ \ \Delta=\frac{i\delta}{\Omega},\ \ \ \alpha=\frac{\lambda}{\Omega}+g\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\Omega\right)+3\kappa\left(t-\tau\right),\end{array}

(315)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Omega=\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(c_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}+c_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right),\\
\\
\delta=\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(a_{0}+a_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}+a_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right),\\
\\
\lambda=\mathsf{E}_{0}\left(b_{0}+b_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}+b_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right).\end{array}

(316)

and
g
is a constant to be determined. Our ansatz is useful since terms
proportional to
\sim 1,\mathsf{E}_{+},\mathsf{E}_{-}
balance each other,
which allows us to find the coefficients explicitly. Initial conditions
complete the picture. The actual calculation is omitted for brevity. The
result is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}c_{+}=-\frac{\left(\lambda_{-}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)}{2\zeta},\ \ \ c_{-}=\frac{\left(\lambda_{+}+2i\varepsilon^{2}m\right)}{2\zeta},\\
\\
a_{0}=-il+\frac{\chi\lambda_{+}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta}c_{+}-\frac{\chi\lambda_{-}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta}c_{-},\ \ \ a_{+}=-\frac{\chi\lambda_{+}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta}c_{+},\ \ \ a_{-}=\frac{\chi\lambda_{-}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta}c_{-},\\
\\
g=\frac{\chi^{2}\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}}{2\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{2}},\ \ \ b_{0}=\frac{\chi\mu}{\zeta^{2}}a_{0},\\
\\
b_{+}=-c_{+}b_{0}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\zeta}a_{0}^{2}-\frac{\chi^{2}\mu^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}}c_{+}c_{-},\ \ \ b_{-}=-c_{-}b_{0}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\zeta}a_{0}^{2}+\frac{\chi^{2}\mu^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}\zeta^{3}}c_{+}c_{-}.\end{array}

(317)

Although these expressions can be simplified further and then substituted in
the function
H
to obtain the corresponding t.p.d.f, we shall not pursue
this line of research here. We shall report the outcome of our investigation
elsewhere.

6.3.3
Non-Degenerate Augmented OU Process

In this Section, we study an affine process of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}y_{t}^{2}dt+y_{t}dW_{t},\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dZ_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\end{array}

(318)

which is at the heart of the Stein & Stein model; see
Stein & Stein 1991
who
considered the special case of zero correlation,
dW_{t}dZ_{t}=0
and
Schöbel & Zhu 1999
who studied the general case of arbitrary correlation,
dW_{t}dZ_{t}=\rho dt
. Further details are given in Section
7
.

Precisely as before, we can introduce a new variable
z_{t}=y_{t}^{2}
, and
expand Eqs (
318
) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}z_{t}dt+y_{t}dW_{t},\ \ \ x_{\tau}=\xi,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dZ_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\\
\\
dz_{t}=\left(\varepsilon^{2}+2\chi y_{t}-2\kappa z_{t}\right)+2\varepsilon y_{t}dZ_{t},\ \ \ z_{\tau}=\theta^{2}.\end{array}

(319)

It is clear that Eqs (
319
) are affine.

The corresponding Fokker-Planck problem can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}zP_{xx}-\rho\varepsilon yP_{xy}-2\rho\varepsilon zP_{xz}-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}P_{yy}-2\varepsilon^{2}yP_{yz}-2\varepsilon^{2}zP_{zz}\\
\\
-\frac{1}{2}zP_{x}+\left(\chi-\kappa y\right)P_{y}+\left(\varepsilon^{2}+2\chi y-2\kappa z\right)P_{z}-3\kappa P=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,x,y,z\right)=\delta\left(x-\xi\right)\delta\left(y-\theta\right)\delta\left(z-\theta^{2}\right).\end{array}

(320)

We can use
H
given by Eq. (
308
) and write the set of ODEs for
\alpha,\Delta,\Xi
is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\Delta^{2}+i\chi\Delta+i\varepsilon^{2}\Xi-3\kappa=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\Delta^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{2}\Delta\Xi+\left(\rho\varepsilon k-i\kappa\right)\Delta+2i\chi\Xi=0,\ \ \ \Delta\left(\tau\right)=l,\\
\\
i\Xi^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{2}\Xi^{2}+2\left(\rho\varepsilon k-i\kappa\right)\Xi+\frac{k\left(k-i\right)}{2}=0,\ \ \ \Xi\left(\tau\right)=m.\end{array}

(321)

As mentioned above, this system can be linearized and solved analytically,
which was discovered earlier by
Stein & Stein 1991
;
Schöbel & Zhu 1999
. We can repeat
the result obtained in the previous Section verbatim, except for Eq. (
311
). The corresponding characteristic equation and its solutions can
be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda^{2}-2\left(\kappa+i\rho\varepsilon k\right)\lambda-\varepsilon^{2}k\left(k-i\right)=0,\\
\\
\mu=\kappa+i\rho\varepsilon k,\ \ \ \zeta=\sqrt{\bar{\rho}^{2}\varepsilon^{2}k^{2}-i\varepsilon^{2}k+\kappa^{2}+2i\rho\kappa\varepsilon}.\end{array}

(322)

The rest of the formal analysis is the same. But the asymptotic behavior of
the t.p.d.f. is, of course, different.

7
Financial Instruments

7.1
Arithmetic and Geometric Brownian Motions and Their
Generalizations

The original approach to modeling financial assets was developed by
Bachelier, who assumed that prices
y_{t}
of such instruments are governed
by an arithmetic Brownian motion; see
Bachelier 1900
:

\begin{array}[]{c}dS_{t}=rS_{t}dt+\hat{\sigma}dW_{t},\ \ \ S_{\tau}=s.\end{array}

(323)

Here,
r
is the risk-neutralized drift,
\hat{\sigma}
is the volatility,
and
W_{t}
is a Wiener process;
r
,
\hat{\sigma}
are dimensional
quantities,
\left[r\right]=T^{-1}
,
\left[\sigma\right]=\$T^{-1/2}
.
The process for
S_{t}
given by Eq. (
323
) is affine; in fact, it
is an OU process with mean-repulsion instead of mean-reversion. Accordingly,
the corresponding t.p.d.f. is given by a modified Eq. (
122
):

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,s,t,S\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{\pi\hat{\sigma}^{2}\left(e^{2r\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)}{\kappa}}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(S-e^{r\left(t-\tau\right)}s\right)^{2}}{\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}\left(e^{2r\left(t-\tau\right)}-1\right)}{r}}\right).\end{array}

(324)

By virtue of this formula, one can price the so-called European options,
which depend solely on the terminal value of
S
at option’s maturity. For
instance, the price of call and put options with maturity
t
and strike
K

with payoffs of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(t,S_{t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(S_{t}-K\right),0\right\},\end{array}

(325)

where
\nu=1
for a call, and
\nu=-1
for a put. The corresponding
formulas have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,s\right)=\nu\left(s-e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}K\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{s-e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}K}{\hat{\sigma}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)+\hat{\sigma}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathrm{n}\left(\frac{s-e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}K}{\hat{\sigma}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right),\end{array}

(326)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{\sigma}\left(\tau,t\right)=\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{e^{2r\left(t-\tau\right)}-1}{2re^{2r\left(t-\tau\right)}}.}\end{array}

(327)

Subsequently, the academic community concluded that using a geometric
Brownian motion as a driver is more appropriate; see
Boness 1964
;
Samuelson 1965
;
Black & Scholes 1973
;
Merton 1973
. At present, the basic assumption is that
the price
S_{t}
of an underlying financial instrument follows a geometric
Brownian motion process with constant coefficients:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=rdt+\sigma dW_{t},\ \ \ S_{\tau}=s.\end{array}

(328)

Here,
r
is the risk-neutralized drift, and
\sigma
is the volatility.
These are dimensional quantities,
\left[r\right]=T^{-1}
,
\left[\sigma\right]=T^{-1/2}
.

The well-known logarithmic change of variables,

\begin{array}[]{c}y_{t}=\ln\left(S_{t}\right),\end{array}

(329)

results in the following process for
y_{t}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}dy_{t}=\left(r-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\right)dt+\sigma dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\ln\left(s\right)=\theta.\end{array}

(330)

The t.p.d.f. for the process
y_{t}
is Gaussian:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\theta,t,y\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(y-\theta-\left(r-\sigma^{2}/2\right)\left(t-\tau\right)\right)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)}\right).\end{array}

(331)

Call and put prices with maturity
t
and strike
K=e^{k}
have terminal
payoffs of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(t,y_{t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(e^{y_{t}}-e^{k}\right),0\right\}.\end{array}

(332)

Eq. (
331
) can be used to derive the celebrated
Black & Scholes 1973

closed-form formula for their prices at time
\tau
:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\left(e^{\theta}\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\theta-k+\left(r+\sigma^{2}/2\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sigma\sqrt{t-\tau}}\right)-e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)+k}\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\theta-k+\left(r-\sigma^{2}/2\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sigma\sqrt{t-\tau}}\right)\right),\end{array}

(333)

where
\mathrm{N}\left(.\right)
is the cumulative normal function.

Since the hedging and speculation needs of market participants cannot be
satisfied by European options alone, the whole industry emerged to design,
price, and hedge the so-called exotic options, with payoffs depending on the
entire price trajectory between inception and maturity. Prices of the
fundamental financial instruments, such as forwards and European calls and
puts, depend on the underlying prices only at maturity. However, the prices
of many other instruments depend on the entire underlying price history
between the instrument’s inception and maturity. Typical examples are
barrier, American, Asian, lookback, and passport options; see, e.g.,
Lipton-Lifschitz 1999
;
Lipton 2001
and references therein. Moreover, the prices of bonds
also depend on the history of the interest rates and credit spreads
throughout their life. This section shows how to price some path-dependent
financial instruments using the methodology developed in the previous
sections.

In addition, it was realized very soon after the seminal paper by
Black & Scholes 1973
was published, that in practice, it provides a rather poor
description of reality. Hence, considerable efforts were dedicated to
developing more adequate models. Such models include the jump-diffusion,
local volatility, path-dependent volatility, stochastic volatility,
local-stochastic volatility, rough volatility, and culminate in the
universal volatility model; see
Merton 1976
;
Stein & Stein 1991
;
Bick & Reisman 1993
;
Heston 1993
;
Derman & Kani 1994
;
Dupire 1994
;
Rubinstein 1994
;
Hobson & Rogers 1998
;
Jex et al. 1999
;
Lewis 2000
;
Lipton 2001
;
Boyarchenko & Levendorsky 2002
;
Hagan et al. 2002
;
Lipton 2002
;
Bergomi 2015
;
Reghai 2015
;
Gatheral et al. 2018
;
Gershon et al. 2022
, and references therein.

Replacing a constant volatility for a geometric Brownian motion with
stochastic volatility driven by a Feller process results in the popular
Heston model; see
Heston 1993
. the governing SDEs:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=\sqrt{v_{t}}dW_{t},\ \ \ S_{\tau}=s,\\
\\
dv_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa v_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon\sqrt{v_{t}}dZ_{t},\ \ \ v_{\tau}=\nu,\end{array}

(334)

where
dW_{t}dZ_{t}=\rho dt
. The logarithmic change of variables, given by
Eq. (
334
), yields Eqs (
268
).

Replacing a constant volatility with stochastic volatility driven by an OU
process results in the (less popular) Stein & Stein model; see
Stein & Stein 1991
;
Schöbel & Zhu 1999
. The corresponding SDEs have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=\sqrt{v_{t}}dW_{t},\ \ \ S_{\tau}=s,\\
\\
dv_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa v_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dZ_{t},\ \ \ v_{\tau}=\nu,\end{array}

(335)

The logarithmic change of variables reduces Eqs (
335
) to Eqs (
318
).

We shall show how to use formulas derived in Sections
5
,
6

in the context of financial engineering.

7.2
Asian Options with Geometric Averaging

The most basic path-dependent options are fixed strike Asian calls and puts,
whose payoff depends on the underlying value averaged between the inception
and maturity. Such options are popular for commodity and energy trading and
in many other circumstances. The average
A_{\tau,t}
on the interval
\left[\tau,t\right]
can be defined in several ways. The simplest and, as
a result, the most popular is an arithmetic average:

\begin{array}[]{c}A_{\tau,t}=\frac{1}{t-\tau}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}S_{s}ds.\end{array}

(336)

A less frequent, but technically easier to deal with, alternative is a
geometric average:

A_{\tau,t}=\exp\left(\frac{1}{t-\tau}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}\ln\left(S_{s}\right)ds\right).

(337)

The payoff of an Asian option with maturity
t
and fixed strike
K
is

V\left(t,A_{\tau,t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(A_{\tau,t}-K\right),0\right\},

(338)

as before,
\nu=1
for a call, and
\nu=-1
for a put. For the floating
strike, the payoff is

V\left(t,S_{t},A_{\tau,t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(S_{t}-kA_{\tau,t}\right),0\right\},

(339)

where the non-dimensional parameter
k
is called weighting; typically,
k=1
.

Analytical pricing of Asian options with arithmetic averaging is notoriously
tricky; see
Geman & Eydeland 1995
;
Rogers & Shi 1995
;
Lipton 1999
;
Lipton 2001
. At the same time,
pricing Asian options with geometric averaging can be done quickly; see
Barrucci et al. 2001
;
Lipton 2001
;
Di Francesco & Pascucci 2005
, and references therein. Here we show
how to price such options using formula (
103
) derived in Section
5
. An alternative approach based on the path integral method is
discussed in
Devreese et al. 2010
. We define

\begin{array}[]{c}y_{t}=\ln\left(S_{t}\right),\ \ \ x_{t}=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\tau}^{t}y_{s}ds.\end{array}

(340)

Then

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=0,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(r-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)dt+\sigma dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\ln\left(S_{\tau}\right)\equiv\theta.\end{array}

(341)

The value of the option can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)=e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{x^{\ast}}^{\nu\infty}P\left(x_{t}\right)\left(\exp\left(\frac{x_{t}}{t-\tau}\right)-\exp\left(\ln K\right)\right)dx_{t},\end{array}

(342)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}x^{\ast}=\left(t-\tau\right)\ln K.\end{array}

(343)

Since Eq. (
341
) is a special case of Eq. (
105
), we can use
Eqs (
112
) to obtain the marginal distribution for
x_{t}
, which is
a Gaussian distribution of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(x,y\right)=\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-p\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{x}^{2}}},\\
\\
\sigma_{x}^{2}=\frac{\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)^{3}}{3},\ \ \ p=\ln\left(S_{\tau}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(r-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)^{2}.\end{array}

(344)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)=\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)-\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right),\end{array}

(345)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)=e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{x^{\ast}}^{\nu\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-p\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}+\frac{x}{\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{x}^{2}}}dx\\
\\
=\nu e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(r+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}S_{\tau}\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\ln\left(S_{\tau}/K\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(r+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)/3}}\right),\end{array}

(346)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)=e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{x^{\ast}}^{\nu\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x-p\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}+\ln\left(K\right)\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{x}^{2}}}dx\\
\\
=\nu e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}K\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\ln\left(S_{\tau}/K\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(r-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)/3}}\right).\end{array}

(347)

Finally, we obtain a well-known formula for the price of a fixed strike
Asian option with geometric averaging:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,S_{\tau}\right)=\nu e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(r+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}S_{\tau}\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\ln\left(S_{\tau}/K\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(r+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{6}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)/3}}\right)\\
\\
-\nu e^{-r\left(t-\tau\right)}K\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\ln\left(S_{\tau}/K\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(r-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)\left(t-\tau\right)}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\left(t-\tau\right)/3}}\right).\end{array}

(348)

Of course, a similar formula holds when
r,\sigma
are time-dependent. Our
derivation, although very simple, seems to be new.

7.3
Volatility and Variance Swaps and Swaptions

7.3.1
Volatility Swaps and Swaptions

Recall that the Stein-Stein stochastic volatility model assumes that the
volatility is driven by an OU process; see
Stein & Stein 1991
. We wish to find
Green’s function associated with the following augmented SDE:

\begin{array}[]{c}dx_{t}=y_{t}dt,\ \ \ x_{\tau}=0,\\
\\
dy_{t}=\left(\hat{\chi}-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\hat{\varepsilon}dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\hat{\theta},\end{array}

(349)

which describes the evolution of the volatility
\sigma_{t}\equiv y_{t}

and its integral
x_{t}
. It is clear that Eqs (
349
) are identical
to Eqs (
123
).

We get the bivariate Gaussian distribution for
\left(x,y\right)
with
covariance matrix
\mathfrak{H}
given by Eq. (
138
), and means
\left(p,q\right)
given by Eq. (
139
):

\begin{array}[]{c}p=\frac{\left(\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)-\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\right)\hat{\chi}}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\hat{\theta}}{\kappa},\\
\\
q=\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)\hat{\chi}}{\kappa}+e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\hat{\theta}.\end{array}

(350)

Since the marginal distribution of
x_{t}
given by Eq. (
140
) is
Gaussian, the fair strike of a volatility swap with maturity
t
is simply
the expected value of
x_{t}/\left(t-\tau\right)
:

\begin{array}[]{c}VolSwap=\frac{\hat{\chi}}{\kappa}+\left(\hat{\theta}-\frac{\hat{\chi}}{\kappa}\right)\Xi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right),\end{array}

(351)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Xi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\left(1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}.\end{array}

(352)

Here
\left[VolSwap\right]=\left[\hat{\chi}/\kappa\right]=\left[\theta\right]=T^{-1/2}
.

Of course, one can calculate the expected value of
x_{t}/\left(t-\tau\right)
via more straightforward means. To this end, Eq. (
351
)
can be derived directly by taking expectations of SDE (
349
).
However, as we shall see in the following subsection, Eq. (
140
)
allows one to solve more interesting problems, such as calculating prices of
bonds and bond options, see below.

Moreover, by virtue of the marginal distribution
Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,\theta,t,x\right)
given by Eq. (
140
), we can price
volatility swaptions with payoffs of the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(t,x_{t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(x-\hat{\varpi}\right),0\right\}.\end{array}

(353)

The prices
V\left(\tau,\theta\right)
are as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\hat{\varpi}}^{\nu\infty}\left(x-\hat{\varpi}\right)Q^{\left(x\right)}\left(\tau,\theta,t,x\right)dx\\
\\
=\frac{\nu}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\hat{\varpi}}^{\nu\infty}\left(x-\hat{\varpi}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)dx\\
\\
=\left(\nu\left(p\left(\tau,t\right)-\hat{\varpi}\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\frac{\left(p\left(\tau,t\right)-\hat{\varpi}\right)}{\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\right)+\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}\mathrm{n}\left(\frac{\left(p\left(\tau,t\right)-\hat{\varpi}\right)}{\sqrt{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\right)\right).\end{array}

(354)

It is clear that formula (
354
) is a variant of the Bachelier
formula (
326
); see
Bachelier 1900
.

7.3.2
Variance Swaps and Swaptions

In contrast to volatility, which, despite common misconceptions, can be
negative, variance must be nonnegative. Accordingly, the easiest way to
model is it to use the augmented Feller process with
\vartheta>0
; see Eq.
(
225
).

By using Eq. (
259
), we can immediately obtain the following
expression for the fair value of a variance swap for the Feller process:

\begin{array}[]{c}VarSwap=\frac{\chi}{\kappa}+\left(\theta-\frac{\chi}{\kappa}\right)\Xi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right),\end{array}

(355)

where
\left[VarSwap\right]=\left[\chi/\kappa\right]=\left[\theta\right]=T^{-1}
. We use hats in this Section to avoid confusing parameters
for the OU and Feller processes. Formulas (
351
), (
355
)
look the same but deal with the volatility and variance, respectively, so
the corresponding parameters have different meanings.

Alternatively, we can use the augmented OU process, see Eqs (
305
).
Averaging away stochastic terms, we get the following formula for the fair
price of the variance swap:

\begin{array}[]{c}VarSwap=\frac{\hat{\chi}^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}+\frac{2\hat{\chi}\left(\kappa\hat{\theta}-\hat{\chi}\right)}{\kappa^{2}}\Xi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{\left(\kappa\hat{\theta}-\hat{\chi}\right)^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\Xi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right),\end{array}

(356)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Xi_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\left(1-e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}\right)}{2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}.\end{array}

(357)

We see that Eqs (
355
), (
356
) provide different
expressions for the fair value of a variance swap, although these
expressions asymptotically agree. This fact reflects the so-called model
risk - by using different models, one gets different answers to the same
question.

By virtue of Eq. (
255
), we can calculate the price of a variance
swaption:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\varpi}^{\nu\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\nu\left(x-\varpi\right)\digamma\left(t,k\right)e^{ikx}dkdx\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)\left(\nu\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\varpi}^{\nu\infty}\left(x-\varpi\right)e^{ikx}dx\right)dk\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi}\underset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)e^{ik\varpi}\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{\varpi}^{\nu\infty}e^{\left(ik-\nu\epsilon\right)x}dx\right)\\
\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi}\underset{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\digamma\left(t,k\right)e^{ik\varpi}}{\left(ik-\nu\epsilon\right)^{2}}dk,\end{array}

(358)

where
\digamma\left(t,k\right)
is given by Eq. (
256
).

For a long time, variance and volatility swaps occupied a niche in the
financial products universe. Recently, they received a somewhat unexpected
boost from cryptocurrency trading. It turned out that these swaps are
beneficial for hedging the so-called impermanent loss generated by automated
market makers; see
Lipton & Treccani 2021
;
Lipton & Sepp 2022
;
Cartea et al. 2023
;
Fukusawa et al. 2023
, among
others.

7.4
Path-Dependent Volatility Model

Hobson & Rogers 1998
initially proposed path-dependent volatility models;
subsequently, they were studied by many authors; see
Davis 2004
;
Di Francesco & Pascucci 2004
;
Di Francesco & Pascucci 2005
;
Lipton & Reghai 2023
among others. They present a viable
alternative to the more popular local volatility modes developed by
Bick & Reisman 1993
;
Derman & Kani 1994
;
Dupire 1994
;
Rubinstein 1994
.

The main advantage of path-dependent volatility models compared to their
local volatility brethren is that the former deal with volatility functions
depending on a non-dimensional argument, such as
S_{t}/A_{t}
, where
S_{t}

is the stock price, and
A_{t}
is its average, say,
\sigma=\sigma\left(S_{t}/A_{t}\right)
, while the latter use volatilities depending on a
dimensional argument
S_{t}
,
\sigma=\sigma\left(S_{t}\right)
, which
is conceptually unsound and results in model dynamics deviating from the one
observed in the market. The problem with path-dependent models is that it is
very hard to build an analytically tractable version of the model, which
complicates gaining the necessary intuition or benchmarking numerical
solution. In this Section, we develop such a model using results derived in Section
6.3
.

The model we propose is the first one to have a semi-analytical solution.
The dynamics is adapted from Section
6.3
, Eq. (
287
) as
follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{dS_{t}}{S_{t}}=\sqrt{c_{0}+c_{1}\ln\left(\frac{S_{t}}{A_{t}}\right)}dW_{t},\ \ \ S_{\tau}=s,\\
\\
A_{t}=\exp\left(\varkappa\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\varkappa\left(t-t^{\prime}\right)}\ln S_{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}\right),\ \ \ A_{\tau}=a.\end{array}

(359)

We do not describe in detail how
S_{t}
, and, hence,
A_{t}
, behave when
t<\tau
, since it becomes unimportant provided that
\varkappa\left(t-\tau\right)
is sufficiently large. For instance, we can assume that
S_{t}\equiv s
, when
t<\tau
, then
a=s
. In Eq. (
359
), we use
the same type of arithmetic averaging as in Eq. (
337
).

In logarithmic variables
x_{t}=\ln\left(S_{t}\right)
,
y_{t}=\ln\left(A_{t}\right)
, Eqs (
359
) assume the form given by Eqs (
287
). Accordingly, the t.p.d.f. has the form (
86
) with
H

given by Eq (
303
). Thus, we can price European options by
calculating explicit integrals. Several useful shortcuts for calculating the
corresponding integrals will be presented elsewhere due to the lack of space.

7.5
Bonds and Bond Options

7.5.1
Vasicek Model

We can use formulas derived in the previous subsection to price bonds and
bond options in the popular Vasicek and Hull-White models; see
Vasicek 1977
;
Hull & White 1990
. Recall that Vasicek postulated the following dynamics
for the short interest rate
y_{t}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}dy_{t}=\left(\chi-\kappa y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\end{array}

(360)

alternatively,

\begin{array}[]{c}dy_{t}=\kappa\left(\zeta-y_{t}\right)dt+\varepsilon dW_{t},\ \ \ y_{\tau}=\theta,\end{array}

(361)

where
\kappa\zeta=\chi
.

At time
\tau
, the price of a bond maturing at time
t
, which we denote
by
\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)
, boils down to solving the
following classical backward problem:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{Z}_{\tau}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)+\left(\chi-\kappa\theta\right)\mathtt{Z}_{\theta}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\mathtt{Z}_{\theta\theta}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)-\theta\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)=0,\\
\\
\mathtt{Z}\left(t,\theta,t\right)=1.\end{array}

(362)

The standard affine ansatz yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)=\exp\left(\mathtt{A}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)\theta\right)\\
\\
\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\kappa},\\
\\
\mathtt{A}\left(\tau,t\right)=\left(\frac{\chi}{\kappa}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa^{2}}\right)\left(\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)-\left(t-\tau\right)\right)-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa}\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\\
\\
=\left(\frac{1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\kappa}-\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\frac{\chi}{\kappa}-\frac{3\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa^{3}}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\kappa^{3}}e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa^{3}}e^{-2\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2\kappa^{2}}\left(t-\tau\right)\\
\\
=\left(\frac{1-e^{-\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}}{\kappa}-\left(t-\tau\right)\right)\frac{\chi}{\kappa}+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}.\end{array}

(363)

We can use formulae derived in the previous section to come up with an
alternative derivation. Introduce
x_{t}=\int_{\tau}^{t}y_{s}ds
. The
distribution of
\left(x_{t},y_{t}\right)
is given by Eq. (
103
)
with the covariance matrix
\mathfrak{H}
, given by Eq. (
138
) and
the expected value
\left(p,q\right)^{T}
given by Eq. (
139
).
Accordingly, the price of a bond can be written as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)=\mathbb{E}\left\{e^{-x_{t}}\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{0}}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x-\frac{\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}}dx\\
\\
=e^{-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}}=\exp\left(\mathtt{A}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)\theta\right),\end{array}

(364)

so that Eqs. (
363
) and (
364
) are in agreement.

We now show how to use the knowledge of the joint Gaussian distribution for
\left(x_{t},y_{t}\right)
to price an option on zero coupon bond maturing
at time
\bar{t}>t
. The payoff of a European option with strike
K
has the
form:

V\left(t,y_{t}\right)=\max\left\{\nu\left(\exp\left(\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)y_{t}\right)-\exp\left(\ln K\right)\right),0\right\}

(365)

where, as before,
\nu=1
for a call, and
\nu=-1
for a put. At maturity
t
, the payoff is independent on
x
, however, at inception it does depend
on the realized value of
x_{\tau,t}
. By using Eqs (
103
), (
138
), (
139
), we can write
V\left(\tau,\theta\right)

(recall that here
\xi\equiv 0
) as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)-\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right),\end{array}

(366)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{1/2}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\nu\infty}^{\mathsf{y}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right.\right.\\
\\
\left.-2h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)\\
\\
\left.-x+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)y\right)dxdy,\end{array}

(367)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{1/2}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\nu\infty}^{\mathsf{y}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right.\right.\\
\\
\left.-2h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)\\
\\
\left.-x+\ln K\right)dxdy,\end{array}

(368)

with
h_{i}
given by Eq. (
138
),
\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\right)=h_{0}h_{2}-h_{1}^{2}
. Here
\mathsf{y}^{\ast}
is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathsf{y}^{\ast}=\frac{\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\ln K}{\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)}.\end{array}

(369)

First, we consider
\mathcal{J}_{1}
. Completing the square, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}-2h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)-x+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)y\\
\\
=-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)^{2}\right.\\
\\
\left.-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)\\
\\
-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right).\end{array}

(370)

Integrating over
x
, we obtain the following expression for
\mathcal{J}_{1}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{e^{-p+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q}}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{2}}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\nu\infty}^{\mathsf{y}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right.\right.\\
\\
\left.\left.+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}+2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)\right)dy.\end{array}

(371)

Completing the square one more time, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}}+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}+2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\\
\\
=-\frac{\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)+h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2},\end{array}

(372)

so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{e^{-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\mathsf{-}\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\\
\\
\times\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\nu\infty}^{\mathsf{y}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)+h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)dy\\
\\
=\nu e^{-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\mathsf{-}\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}}\\
\\
\times\mathrm{N}\left(\frac{\nu\left(\mathsf{y}^{\ast}-q+h_{1}+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\right)}{\sqrt{h_{2}}}\right).\end{array}

(373)

It is easy to see that
\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)
is
given by Eq. (
373
) with
\nu=1
and
\mathsf{y}^{\ast}=\infty
,
so that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)=e^{-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\mathsf{-}\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\frac{\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}}.\end{array}

(374)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\frac{\nu\left(\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\ln K-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{B}^{2}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\sqrt{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)}\right).\end{array}

(375)

Eq. (
374
) is the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation for bond prices in the
Vasicek model. Its direct verification is left to the reader as a useful
exercise. By using (
374
), it is easy but tedious to show that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{1}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)}{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K}\right)}{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}+\frac{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}{2}\right)\right).\end{array}

(376)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)=\sqrt{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right).\end{array}

(377)

Second, we consider
\mathcal{J}_{2}
. We proceed in the same way as before.
Completing the square, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right.\\
\\
\left.-2h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)-x+\ln K\\
\\
=-\frac{1}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\left(h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(\left(x-p\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}\right)^{2}\right.\\
\\
\left.-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}\right)-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\ln K.\end{array}

(378)

Integration over
x
yields

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\frac{e^{-p\left(\tau,t\right)+\ln K}}{\sqrt{2\pi h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{-\nu\infty}^{\mathsf{y}^{\ast}}\exp\left(-\frac{-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\right)dy.\end{array}

(379)

Completing the square one more time, we get:

\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{-\frac{\left(h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)-\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2\det\left(\mathfrak{H}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}\\
\\
=-\frac{\left(y-q\left(\tau,t\right)+h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{2}}{2h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}+\frac{h_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)}{2}.\end{array}

(380)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu e^{-p+\frac{h_{0}}{2}+\ln K}\mathrm{N}\left(\frac{\nu\left(\mathsf{y}^{\ast}-q\left(\tau,t\right)+h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\sqrt{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\right)\\
\\
=\nu\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K\mathrm{N}\left(\frac{\nu\left(\mathtt{A}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)-\ln K-\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)q\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)h_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\mathtt{B}\left(t,\bar{t}\right)\sqrt{h_{2}\left(\tau,t\right)}}\right).\end{array}

(381)

Once again, we can represent
\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right)

in a more intuitive form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{J}_{2}\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)}{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K}\right)}{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}-\frac{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}{2}\right)\right).\end{array}

(382)

Finally, we arrive at the following familiar expression for the bond option
price:

\begin{array}[]{c}V\left(\tau,\theta\right)=\nu\left(\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)}{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K}\right)}{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}+\frac{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}{2}\right)\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.-\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K\mathrm{N}\left(\nu\left(\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,\bar{t}\right)}{\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)K}\right)}{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}-\frac{\Sigma\left(\tau,t,\bar{t}\right)}{2}\right)\right)\right).\end{array}

(383)

7.5.2
CIR Model

The CIR model postulates that the short rate follows the Feller process; see

Cox et al. 1985
. Accordingly, the bond price can be calculated by using Eq. (
255
) with
\xi=0
, and
k=-i
:

\begin{array}[]{c}B=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q\left(t,x\right)e^{-x}dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\digamma\left(t,k\right)e^{\left(ik-1\right)x}dkdx=\digamma\left(t,-i\right),\end{array}

(384)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\digamma\left(t,-i\right)=\exp\left(\frac{2\chi\mu\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\zeta}{\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}}\right)\right.\\
\\
\left.+\frac{2\lambda_{+}\lambda_{-}\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)\theta}{\varepsilon^{2}\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\right),\end{array}

(385)

with

\begin{array}[]{c}\mu=\frac{\kappa}{2},\ \ \ \zeta=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+2\varepsilon^{2}}}{2}.\end{array}

(386)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{Z}\left(\tau,\theta,t\right)=e^{\mathtt{A}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)\theta},\end{array}

(387)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{A}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\chi\kappa\left(t-\tau\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{2\chi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{2\zeta\mathsf{E}_{+}}{\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}\right),\\
\\
\mathtt{B}\left(\tau,t\right)=\frac{\left(\mathsf{E}_{+}-\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)}{\left(\lambda_{+}\mathsf{E}_{+}-\lambda_{-}\mathsf{E}_{-}\right)},\end{array}

(388)

which coincides with the standard expressions given by
Cox et al. 1985
.

7.6
Further Extensions

Formulas derived in Sections
5
,
6
can be used to solve
numerous problems of financial engineering within a consistent framework
based on Kevin waves. For instance, these formulas allow one to calculate
t.p.d.fs and price options in Heston, Stein & Stein models, expand these
models by incorporating jumps, stochastic interest rates, and default
intensities driven by OU or Feller processes, describe risky bonds, and
solve several other complex but practically significant problems. Some
features of proposed mathematical formalism can be extended to other
processes, which become affine after suitable changes of variables.

Our novel mathematical tools and techniques developed using Kelvin waves can
be adapted and applied to studying mean-reverting trading strategies in
financial markets; see
Lipton & Lopez de Prado 2020
. We shall report the corresponding
results elsewhere.

8
Conclusions

In this paper, we have developed a unified approach to finding t.p.d.fs for
affine processes via the integral representation based on Kelvin waves. Our
methodology transcends disciplinary boundaries and reveals deep connections
between problems in hydrodynamics, molecular physics, stochastic processes,
and financial engineering.

Some of these problems are degenerate because they have more independent
variables than sources of uncertainty, and others are not. Our method can
handle both situations equally well.

In particular, we discovered an unexpected connection between the Langevin
equation for underdamped Brownian motion and the vorticity equation for
two-dimensional flows of a viscous incompressible fluid.

We used the Kelvin wave expansions to solve many essential financial
problems, such as pricing of Asian options with geometric averaging, finding
an analytically tractable model for processes with path-dependent
volatility, deriving convenient expressions for t.p.d.fs for processes with
stochastic volatility, and finding prices of bonds and bond options by
augmenting the process for the short rate with a process for its integral.

The method developed in the paper can address problems with varying degrees
of complexity and contributes to the understanding and modeling of
stochastic systems in diverse fields. It can be expanded in several
directions, mainly by studying jump-diffusion models or, if desired, by
considering general affine pseudo-differential equations.

Appendix A
Killed Gaussian Process

We no longer assume that the governing system of SDEs is degenerate,
although we do not exclude such possibility and write the governing system
of SDEs as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)dt+\mathbf{\Sigma}d\mathbf{W}_{t},\ \ \ \mathbf{z}_{\tau}=\mathbf{\zeta}.\end{array}

(389)

Below, we assume that the corresponding coefficients are time-dependent. We
also assume that the process is killed with intensity
\iota
linearly
depending of
\mathbf{z}
, namely,

\begin{array}[]{c}\iota=c+\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{z},\end{array}

(390)

where
c
is a scalar, and
\mathbf{c}^{\left(z\right)}
is a
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector.

The Fokker-Plank equation for a killed process assuming an undefined or
”killed” state at some random time has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathfrak{A}P_{\mathbf{zz}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)\\
\\
+\left(\mathbf{b}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}\right)\cdot P_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)+\left(b+c+\mathbf{c\cdot z}\right)P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\mathbf{z},\tau,\mathbf{\zeta}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{\zeta}\right).\end{array}

(391)

Here
\mathfrak{A}
is the covariance matrix,

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A}=\left(a_{nn^{\prime}}\right)=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\sigma_{nk}\sigma_{n^{\prime}k}=\mathbf{\Sigma\Sigma}^{T},\end{array}

(392)

and
b=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathfrak{B}\right)
.

Explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{n^{\prime}=1}^{N}a_{nn^{\prime}}P_{x_{n}x_{n^{\prime}}}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}\left(b_{n}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}b_{nn^{\prime}}x_{n^{\prime}}\right)P_{x_{n}}\\
\\
+\left(b+c+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}^{\left(x\right)}x_{n}\right)P=0,\end{array}

(393)

We use the familiar Kelvin ansatz and represent
P
in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H\left(t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)d\mathbf{m},\\
\\
H\left(t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)=\exp\left(\Psi\left(t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)\right),\\
\\
\Psi\left(t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)=\alpha\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-i\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{\zeta},\end{array}

(394)

where
\mathbf{m}\mathcal{=}\left(m_{i}\right)
is
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector,
\mathbf{\Upsilon}=\left(\upsilon_{i}\right)~
is
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector, and

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(\tau\right)=\mathbf{m}.\end{array}

(395)

As before:

\begin{array}[]{c}\frac{H_{t}}{H}=\Psi_{t}=\left(\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}\right),\\
\\
\frac{H_{\mathbf{z}}}{H}=\Psi_{\mathbf{z}}=i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right),\\
\\
\ \frac{H_{\mathbf{zz}}}{H}=\Psi_{\mathbf{z}}^{2}=-\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{T}\left(t\right).\end{array}

(396)

The coupled equations for
\alpha,\mathbf{\Upsilon}
have the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\left(\mathbf{b}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}\right)+b+c+\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{z}=0.\end{array}

(397)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{b}+b+c=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\end{array}

(398)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{T}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)-i\mathbf{c}=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(\tau\right)=\mathbf{m}.\end{array}

(399)

Let
\mathfrak{L}\left(t\right)
is the fundamental solution of the
homogeneous ODE, i.e., the matrix such that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}^{\prime}\left(\tau,t\right)+\mathfrak{B}^{T}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,\tau\right)=\mathfrak{I},\end{array}

(400)

The solution of Eq. (
399
) has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)=\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}+i\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}^{-1}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathbf{c}\left(s\right)ds\equiv\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(\mathbf{m}+i\mathbf{e}\left(\tau,t\right)\right),\\
\\
\mathbf{e}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathbf{\mathfrak{L}}^{-1}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathbf{c}\left(s\right)ds.\end{array}

(401)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\mathbf{m}-i\mathbf{m\cdot d}-\varpi,\end{array}

(402)

where

I\times I
positive-definite matrix of the

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,s\right)ds,\end{array}

(403)

while
\mathbf{d}
is an
\left(I\times 1\right)

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{d}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,s\right)\left(\mathbf{b}\left(s\right)+\mathfrak{A}\left(s\right)\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,s\right)\mathbf{e}\left(s\right)\right)ds,\end{array}

(404)

and

\begin{array}[]{c}\varpi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)=\int_{\tau}^{t}b\left(s\right)ds,\\
\\

(405)

Accordingly,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi\left(t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{m}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathfrak{C}^{-1}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{m}+i\mathbf{m}\cdot\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{d}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\\
\\
-\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{e}\left(\tau,t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-\varpi\left(\tau,t\right).\end{array}

(406)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta},\mathbf{\theta,}t,\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}\right)=\frac{\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{e}\left(\tau,t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-\varpi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)-\varpi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I/2}}\\
\\
\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}...\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G\left(t,\mathbf{m}\right)\exp\left(i\mathbf{m}\cdot\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{d}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\right)d\mathbf{m},\end{array}

(407)

where

is the density of a multivariate
Gaussian distribution in the
\mathbf{m}

\begin{array}[]{c}P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=\frac{\det\left(\mathfrak{C}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)^{1/2}\exp\left(-\mathfrak{L}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{e}\left(\tau,t\right)\cdot\mathbf{z}-\varpi_{0}\left(\tau,t\right)-\varpi_{1}\left(\tau,t\right)\right)}{\left(2\pi\right)^{I/2}}\\

\times\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{d}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\mathcal{\cdot}\mathfrak{C}\left(\tau,t\right)\left(\mathfrak{L}^{T}\left(\tau,t\right)\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{d}\left(\tau,t\right)-\mathbf{\zeta}\right)\right).\end{array}

Appendix B
Killed Non-Gaussian Process

The non-Gaussian governing SDE has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}d\mathbf{z}_{t}=\left(\mathbf{b}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)dt+\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}+\mathfrak{D}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)^{1/2}d\mathbf{W}_{t},\end{array}

(417)

where
\mathbf{z}
,
\mathbf{b}
,
\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}
are
\left(I\times 1\right)
vectors, and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
\mathfrak{B}
,
\mathfrak{D}
are
\left(I\times I\right)
matrices. It is convenient to
introduce vectors
\mathbf{d}^{\left(i\right)}
equal to the
i
-th column
of
\mathfrak{D}
. The corresponding
\left(I\times I\right)
covariance
matrix
\mathfrak{A}
linearly depends on
\mathbf{z}
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A=}\mathbf{\Sigma}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}+\mathfrak{D}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}^{T}=\mathfrak{A}^{0}+\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{I}z_{i}\mathfrak{A}^{i},\end{array}

(418)

where

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathfrak{A}^{0}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(0\right)}\right)\Sigma^{T},\ \ \ \mathfrak{A}^{i}=\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathbf{d}^{\left(i\right)}\right)\Sigma^{T}.\end{array}

(419)

As in the previous appendix, we no longer restrict ourselves to the
degenerate (Klein-Kramers and Kolmogorov) case, and allow
\Sigma

to be a full-rank matrix. Once again, we assume that the process is killed
with intensity
\iota
linearly depending of
\mathbf{z}
, namely,

\begin{array}[]{c}\iota=c+\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{z},\end{array}

(420)

where
c
is a scalar, and
\mathbf{c}^{\left(z\right)}
is a
\left(I\times 1\right)
column vector.

The corresponding Fokker-Plank problem has the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{t}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\left(\mathfrak{A}^{0}+\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{I}z_{i}\mathfrak{A}^{i}\right)P_{\mathbf{zz}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)\\
\\
+\left(\mathbf{\hat{b}}+\mathfrak{B}\mathbf{z}_{t}\right)\cdot P_{\mathbf{z}}\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)+\left(b+c+\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{z}\right)P\left(\tau,\mathbf{\zeta,}t,\mathbf{z}\right)=0,\\
\\
P\left(\tau,\mathbf{z},\tau,\mathbf{\zeta}\right)=\delta\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{\zeta}\right),\end{array}

(421)

The equations for
\alpha,\Upsilon
generalize Eqs (
205
). They
can be written in the form:

\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}\left(t\right)\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)+i\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathbf{b}\left(t\right)+b\left(t\right)+c\left(t\right)=0,\ \ \ \alpha\left(\tau\right)=0,\\
\\
i\mathbf{\Upsilon}_{i}^{\prime}\left(t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)\cdot\mathfrak{A}^{\left(i\right)}\mathbf{\Upsilon}\left(t\right)+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}i\mathfrak{B}_{ij}\mathbf{\Upsilon}_{j}\left(t\right)+c_{i}=0,\ \ \ \mathbf{\Upsilon}_{i}\left(\tau\right)=m_{i}.\end{array}

(422)

As in the case without killing, finding an analytical solution to a
multi-dimensional Riccati equation is generally impossible. However, in the
time-independent one-dimensional case, it can be done. Solution becomes
particularly simple in the special case when
\mathfrak{A}^{\left(0\right)}=0
. The most important case is the killed one-dimensional Feller
process.
