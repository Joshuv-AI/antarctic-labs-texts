Superconductivity in the vicinity of an isospin-polarized state in a cubic Dirac band
Zhiyu Dong and Leonid Levitov

Abstract
We present a theory of superconducting pairing originating from soft critical fluctuations near isospin-polarized states in rhombohedral trilayer graphene. Using a symmetry-based approach, we determine possible isospin order types and derive the effective electron-electron interactions mediated by isospin fluctuations. Superconductivity arising due to these interactions has symmetry and order parameter structure that depend in a unique way on the “mother” isospin order. This model naturally leads to a superconducting phase adjacent to an isospin-ordering phase transition, which mimics the behavior observed in experiments. The symmetry of the paired state predicted for the isospin order type inferred in experiments matches the observations. These findings support a scenario of superconductivity originating from electron-electron interactions.

Before closing, we comment on the unique testable signatures of SC induced by critical fluctuations. The pairing interaction is due to a collective mode with momenta
\sim 2K
that softens near the isospin-polarization instability. Electrons in different valleys interact by exchanging such soft excitations, generating pair hopping of the form
(K,K^{\prime})\to(K^{\prime},K)
,
(K^{\prime},K)\to(K,K^{\prime})
. In the process the momentum of each electron changes by
\pm 2K
, a value large on the scale of
k_{F}
and the typical Thomas-Fermi screening parameter. This suggests that introducing screening by a proximal gate

;
XLiu
can serve as a direct test of the pairing mechanism. Indeed, only the relatively long-wavelength harmonics of Coulomb interaction are screened by a gate whereas the harmonics responsible for the
\sim 2K
momentum transfer remain unscreened. Since the long-wavelength harmonics, which are screened out, are responsible for a pair breaking effect in the Cooper channel, we expect SC to be enhanced upon the introduction of screening. An observation of such SC enhancement
would facilitate identifying the unconventional pairing mechanism.

In summary, superconductivity driven by critical soft modes of isospin-polarized states appears to be a viable framework to understand the interplay between different correlated states in RTG, in particular superconductivity observed near the phase boundaries of the isospin-polarized states.

This framework is generic and will be straightforward to generalize to other isospin-polarized orders observed in RTG, in particular, the state “SC2” that occurs at an interface between so-far-poorly-understood phases with partially and fully polarized isospin.
A SC phase originating from an isospin-polarized phase with broken time reversal symmetry is expected to be a spin-triplet (and, likely, have a p-wave orbital structure). This is consistent with the observation of SC2 phase being resilient under magnetic fields well in excess of the Pauli threshold fields. Generalizing this approach to describe pairing in other phases is an interesting direction for future work. Once confirmed, it will lend strong support to superconductivity arising exclusively from repulsive interactions.

Below, we determine the form of the single-particle Hamiltonian allowed by symmetry. The Hamiltonian is a bilinear quantity in Dirac spinors
\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}=(\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}AK},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}BK},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}AK^{\prime}},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}BK^{\prime}})^{T}
, defined so that the momentum
{\boldsymbol{p}}
for valley
K
and
K^{\prime}
components is measured relative to the corresponding points,
K
or
K^{\prime}
.
The Hamiltonian in general takes the form of a
4\times 4
matrix sandwiched between spinors
\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
and
\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}
:

H=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}M(p)\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}},\quad M(p)=h_{ij}({\boldsymbol{p}})\tau_{i}\sigma_{j},\quad i,j=0,1,2,3.

(18)

As always, the repeated indices are summed over; the quantities
\tau_{i}
’s are the Pauli matrices in valley basis (
K/K^{\prime}
),
\sigma_{j}
’s are the Pauli matrices in sublattice basis (
A/B
). By
\sigma_{0}=\tau_{0}=1
we denote the
2\times 2
identity matrix.

As in the main text, for conciseness, the spin indices will be suppressed. Hiding spin indices accounts for the fact that our single-particle Hamiltonian is spin-independent and the interactions depend on density but not on spin. The Stoner instability discussed below will involve polarization of valley degrees of freedom and no spin polarization. Likewise, the superconducting orders of interest will have a simple (singlet) spin structure, which justifies using a short-hand notation in which spin indices are suppress, as in Eq.(
18
).

For reader’s sake, we comment on how the above expressions would change if the spin indices were included explicitly. The Hamiltonian and spinors would then take the following form

H=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}},s,s^{\prime}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}},s}^{\dagger}M(p)\delta_{ss^{\prime}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}},s^{\prime}},\quad\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}s}=(\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}AKs},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}},BKs},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}AK^{\prime}s},\psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}BK^{\prime}s})^{T},\quad s,s^{\prime}=\uparrow,\downarrow,

(19)

where the Kronecker
\delta_{ss^{\prime}}
indicates the conservation of spin and the degeneracy of two spin species. This spin structure reflects negligible spin-orbit coupling in graphene-based systems. Therefore, throughout this paper, the short-hand form of Hamiltonian given in Eq.(
18
) where all spin indices are suppressed will be sufficient for our needs.

The tensors
\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}
appearing in the Hamiltonian, Eq.(
18
), transform under different irreducible representations of the space group. To determine how these quantities transform, we consider how the elements of the space group act on Dirac spinors. The three-fold rotation operator
C_{3}
leaves the states at points
K
and
K^{\prime}

invariant, whereas the mirror operator
\sigma_{d}

swaps the states at points
K
and
K^{\prime}
without causing any other changes.
Translation generates a valley-dependent phase factor when hitting Dirac spinors. Next, we analyze how Dirac particle-hole bilinear quantities transform under the space group. Namely, the valley-diagonal terms transform under either
A_{1}
or
A_{2}
representations of
C_{3v}
, and are invariant under lattice translations. The valley-off-diagonal Dirac particle-hole bilinear quantities transform under the symmetry group of
K
point since the total momentum of an intervalley particle-hole pair is
2K
(or, equivalently,
-K
). In this way, we find all possible Dirac particle-hole bilinears can be decomposed into three 1D representations
A_{1,\Gamma}
,
A_{2,\Gamma}
and
A_{\pm K}
of the space group. Further classifying them by whether they are even or odd under time reversal (represented by superscripts
+,-
), we obtain the second column in Table.
4
.

Next, we determine the symmetry of
p
-dependent functions up to cubic form. Basically,
p
-odd and
p
-even terms are even and odd under time reversal. Meanwhile, the cubic term proportional to
\cos 3\phi_{p}
(where
\phi_{p}
is defined as the angle between
p
and
K
vector) picks up a minus sign under the reflection
\sigma_{d}
which maps
\phi_{p}\rightarrow\pi-\phi_{p}
, so it belongs to
A_{2\Gamma}^{-}
representation. In comparison, the cubic term proportional to
\sin 3\phi_{p}
is invariant under
\sigma_{d}
, so it belongs to
A_{1\Gamma}^{-}
representation. The results are summarized in the third column of Table.
4
.

As the Hamiltonian must be invariant under space group, the momentum dependence of each term in Hamiltonian has to transform in the same way as the Dirac particle-hole bilinear quantities. Therefore, we construct the symmetry-allowed terms by matching the second and third rows in Table
4
, yielding the following form of the single-particle Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}M({\boldsymbol{p}})\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}

(20)

\displaystyle M({\boldsymbol{p}})=h_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}})1\sigma_{1}+h^{\prime}_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}})1_{4}+h_{3}({\boldsymbol{p}})1\sigma_{3}+h_{1}({\boldsymbol{p}})\left(\alpha\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}+\alpha^{\prime}\tau_{3}1+\alpha^{\prime\prime}\tau_{3}\sigma_{3}\right)+h_{2}({\boldsymbol{p}})1\sigma_{2}

where

\displaystyle h_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}})=\Delta_{0}+\frac{p^{2}}{2m},\quad h^{\prime}_{0}(p)=\Delta^{\prime}_{0}+\frac{p^{2}}{2m^{\prime}},

(21)

\displaystyle h_{1}({\boldsymbol{p}})=\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle h_{2}({\boldsymbol{p}})=\beta\left(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3}\right),

(23)

\displaystyle h_{3}({\boldsymbol{p}})=D+\frac{p^{2}}{2m_{3}}.

(24)

These are the expressions used in the main text.

I.2
Particle-particle and particle-hole susceptibilities

In this section, we derive the form of susceptibilities used in the analysis of instabilities in the particle-hole and particle-particle channels. We start with the Hamiltonian introduced in main text:

H=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}\left[h_{1}({\boldsymbol{p}})\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}+h_{2}({\boldsymbol{p}})1_{2}\sigma_{2}+D1_{2}\sigma_{3}\right]\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}.

(25)

which gives the electron’s Green’s function:

G(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})=\left[i\omega+\mu-\left(h_{1}({\boldsymbol{p}})\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}+h_{2}({\boldsymbol{p}})1_{2}\sigma_{2}+D1_{2}\sigma_{3}\right)\right]^{-1}

(26)

On the left hand side of Stoner criterion Eq.
(
7
), the integral of two green’s functions sandwiching
O
has the meaning of a particle-hole susceptibility. To solve the Stoner criterion, we have to first evaluate the particle-hole susceptibility. Below, we calculate this quantity in matrix form:

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}G(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})\otimes G(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{(i\omega+\mu)^{2}1_{4}\otimes 1_{4}+h_{1}^{2}\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}+h_{2}^{2}1_{2}\sigma_{2}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{2}+D^{2}1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}+(i\omega+\mu)D(1_{4}\otimes 1\sigma_{3}+1\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{4})}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left[-a_{0}1_{4}\otimes 1_{4}+a_{1}\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}+a_{2}1_{2}\sigma_{2}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{2}+a_{3}1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}\right].

(27)

Here the
O(D)
term in the second line vanishes because the quantity under the integral is a full derivative in
\omega
which vanishes upon integration over
\omega
. Here, we have used that the
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are both odd functions of
p
. In the last line, the prefactors
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are defined as:

\displaystyle a_{0}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)^{2}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4E}\Theta(E-|\mu|),

(28)

\displaystyle a_{1}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\alpha^{2}\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)^{2}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\alpha^{2}\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}{4E^{3}}\Theta(E-|\mu|),

(29)

\displaystyle a_{2}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\beta^{2}(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3})^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)^{2}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\beta^{2}(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3})^{2}}{4E^{3}}\Theta(E-|\mu|),

(30)

\displaystyle a_{3}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{D^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)^{2}}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{D^{2}}{4E^{3}}\Theta(E-|\mu|).

(31)

Here, we have obtained the matrix-form particle-hole susceptibility. With this, we can solve the Stoner criterion Eq.
(
7
) by contracting the quantity evaluated above with the
O
matrix placed in between the Green’s functions
G(i\omega,p)
and
G(i\omega,p)
.

On the left hand side of the linearized pairing gap equation Eq.
(
14
), we take only the part that depends on frequency and momentum and carry out the integration, the quantity we get in the particle-particle susceptibility. We evaluate quantity can be evaluate as follows:

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}G(i\omega,p)\otimes G(-i\omega,-p)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{(i\omega+\mu)(-i\omega+\mu)1_{4}\otimes 1_{4}-h_{1}^{2}\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}-h_{2}^{2}1_{2}\sigma_{2}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{2}+D^{2}1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle+\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{(i\omega+\mu)D1_{4}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}+D(-i\omega+\mu)1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{4}.}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}

(32)

\displaystyle=\tilde{a}_{0}1_{4}\otimes 1_{4}-\tilde{a}_{1}\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}-\tilde{a}_{2}1_{2}\sigma_{2}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{2}+\tilde{a}_{3}1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}+\tilde{b}\left(1_{4}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}+1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{4}\right),

(33)

\tilde{a}_{0},\tilde{a}_{1},\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{3},\tilde{b}
are defined as follows

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{0}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\omega^{2}+\mu^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{E\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E^{2}-\mu^{2}}+\frac{|\mu|\Theta(\mu-E)}{\mu^{2}-E^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{1}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\alpha^{2}\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E(E^{2}-\mu^{2})}+\frac{\Theta(\mu-E)}{|\mu|(\mu^{2}-E^{2})}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{2}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\beta^{2}(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3})^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}\beta^{2}(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3})^{2}\left(\frac{\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E(E^{2}-\mu^{2})}+\frac{\Theta(\mu-E)}{|\mu|(\mu^{2}-E^{2})}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{3}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{D^{2}}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}D^{2}\left(\frac{\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E(E^{2}-\mu^{2})}+\frac{\Theta(\mu-E)}{|\mu|(\mu^{2}-E^{2})}\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{b}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\mu D}{\left(-(i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)\left(-(-i\omega+\mu)^{2}+E^{2}\right)}=\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}\mu D\left(\frac{\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E(E^{2}-\mu^{2})}+\frac{\Theta(\mu-E)}{|\mu|(\mu^{2}-E^{2})}\right).

E({\boldsymbol{p}})
is the absolute value of the energy in the two bands:
E({\boldsymbol{p}})=\sqrt{h_{0}({\boldsymbol{p}})^{2}+h_{2}({\boldsymbol{p}})^{2}+D^{2}}
.

Here, we have obtained the matrix-form particle-particle susceptibility. With this, we are able to solve the linearized pairing gap equation Eq.(
14
) by contracting the quantity found above with the
O
matrix placed in between the Green’s functions
G(i\omega,p)
and
G(i\omega,p)
.

I.3
Stoner instability

In this section, we derive the variational form of the Stoner criterion used in main text and use it to determine the channel with strongest instability. We start with the matrix form of Stoner criterion for an arbitrary orbital order
\langle\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}O\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\rangle
:

-V_{0}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}G(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})OG(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})=O,

(34)

where
G(i\omega,p)
is the electron’s Green’s function and
O
can be an arbitrary
4\times 4
complex-valued matrix. Since this equation is invariant under multiplying
O
by a prefactor, we can choose the normalization of
O
so that
\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}O^{\dagger}O=1
.

Eq. (
34
) is essentially an eigenvalue equation for
O
. To see this, we can express
O
using Pauli matrices:
O=d_{ij}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}
, where
d_{ij}
is a 16-dimensional complex-valued vector, dummy indices are summed over. The normalization of
O
yields the normalization of
d
vector:
|d_{ij}|^{2}=1
. Then, we can define a matrix
M
so that

-\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}G(i\omega,p)\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}G(i\omega,p)=M_{ij,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\tau_{i^{\prime}}\sigma_{j^{\prime}},

(35)

then Eq.(
34
) is equivalent to

M_{ij,i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}d_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{V_{0}}d_{ij},

(36)

which is an eigenvalue equation with
\frac{1}{V_{0}}
taking on a role of an eigenvalue.

Our goal is to identify the channel which is most unstable to valley polarization. This is equivalent to finding the channel in which pairing can be triggered by the smallest
V_{0}
at zero temperature. Eq.(
35
) indicates that this is equivalent to determining an eigenvector
|d\rangle
that corresponds to the largest eigenvalue of
M
. Let
\lambda_{*}
be the largest eigenvalue, then

\lambda_{*}=\max F[O],\quad F[O]=\frac{\langle d|M|d\rangle}{\langle d|d\rangle}=-\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d^{2}{\boldsymbol{p}}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[O^{\dagger}G(i\omega,p)OG(i\omega,p)\right].

(37)

where we have used the normalization
\langle d|d\rangle=|d_{ij}|^{2}=1
. Thus, our problem is equivalent to finding a matrix
O
(or, equivalently, a vector
|d\rangle
) that maximizes the function
F(O)
.

Next, from Eq.(
27
), we notice that the off-diagonal terms vanish upon integration over
\omega
and
p
. Therefore,

\int d\omega d^{2}pG(i\omega,p)\otimes G(i\omega,p)
only contains the diagonal terms
1_{4}\otimes 1_{4}
,
\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}
,
1_{2}\sigma_{2}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{2}
,
1_{2}\sigma_{3}\otimes 1_{2}\sigma_{3}
. These terms, when sandwiching an arbitrary component of
O
on the left-hand-side of Eq.(
34
), can only yield the same matrix component. Say, plugging
O=\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}
in Eq.(
34
) yields an expression proportional to
\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}
, and so on.
As a result, the function
F(O)
takes a diagonal form:

F[O]=\sum_{ij}|d_{ij}|^{2}\chi_{ij},\quad\chi_{ij}=-\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}G(i\omega,p)\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}G(i\omega,p)\right].

(38)

The quantity
\chi_{ij}
has the meaning of a particle-hole susceptibility in the channel
O_{ij}
. From Eq.(
38
) we see that, all that needs to be done to maximize
F
is to find the largest susceptibility
\chi_{ij}
and set
d_{ij}=1
, letting other components
d_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
vanish. Accordingly, we plug Eq.(
27
) into Eq.(
34
) to obtain

\chi_{ij}=-\left[-a_{0}+a_{1}u_{1}+a_{2}u_{2}+a_{3}u_{3}\right],

(39)

where

\displaystyle u_{1}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}\tau_{3}\sigma_{1}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}\right],

\displaystyle u_{2}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[1_{2}\sigma_{2}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}1_{2}\sigma_{2}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}\right],

\displaystyle u_{3}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[1_{2}\sigma_{3}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}1_{2}\sigma_{3}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}\right].

We note that all
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
are of a positive sign.
The signs of different terms in Eq.(
39
) are such, that
\chi_{ij}
takes maximal value if and only if
u_{0}=u_{2}=u_{3}=-1
, which leads to two solutions
i=1,j=1
and
i=2,j=1
. Therefore, the following two channels have the strongest Stoner instability:

O=\tau_{1}\sigma_{1},\quad\tau_{2}\sigma_{1}.

(40)

Being off-diagonal in valleys, these states describe isospin-polarized phases with valley coherence spontaneously generated as a result of Stoner’s instability.
To assess whether these two candidate orders can play a role of mother states for superconductivity below we consider isospin fluctuations for these two states.

I.4
Pairing interaction mediated by the soft isospin fluctuation modes

As in the main text, we use
\phi
to represent the collective mode that undergoes softening near the boundary of a partially isospin ordered (PIP) phase. The soft mode can be described by a Hamiltonian of the following form

H_{s}=\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}+\frac{K}{2}|\partial_{x}\phi|^{2}+\frac{J}{2}|\phi|^{2}+\frac{U}{2}|\phi|^{4},

(41)

where
U
is always positive,
J>0
outside the PIP phase,
J<0
inside the PIP phase. As the field
\phi
describes the fluctuation of the PIP order parameter, it transforms under the space group in the same way as
\Psi^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi
. The soft-mode Hamiltonian Eq.(
41
) respects the full symmetry of the system since both
|\phi|^{2}
and
|\partial_{x}\phi|^{2}
are invariant under the space group. From this Hamiltonian, the Green’s function for soft mode in disordered (normal) phase is

D_{\phi\phi}(i\omega,p)=-\langle\phi(i\omega,p)\phi(-i\omega,-p)\rangle=-\frac{2}{\omega^{2}+Kp^{2}+J}.

(42)

We use the following Hamiltonian to represent the coupling between soft modes and electrons:

H_{e-s}=\gamma\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}}\phi_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}+h.c.,

(43)

Here, we note that the field
\phi
in this model carries momentum
2K
since the Dirac bilinear
\Psi^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi
carries momentum
2K
. This soft-mode-electron coupling Hamiltonian also respects the full symmetry of the system.
Taking the PIP order of the form
\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
, and using the perturbation theory at the second order of
\gamma
, we obtain a soft-mode-mediated electron-electron interaction
represented by the following action:

A_{ee}=\sum_{\omega,\nu,\nu^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{i\omega,p}\overline{\Psi}_{i\nu^{\prime}+i\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}+{\boldsymbol{p}}}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi_{i\nu^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\overline{\Psi}_{i\nu-i\omega,{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{p}}}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi_{i\nu,{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(44)

where
\Psi_{i\nu,{\boldsymbol{k}}}
and
\overline{\Psi}_{i\nu,{\boldsymbol{k}}}
are four-component Fermionic fields describing four-component Dirac electrons.

Outside the
\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
phase,
\tilde{V}(i\omega,p)
takes the form

\tilde{V}_{i\omega,p}=-\gamma^{2}(-D_{\phi\phi}(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}}))=-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\omega^{2}+\Omega_{p}^{2}},

(45)

where
\Omega_{p}=\sqrt{Kp^{2}+J}
.

The soft-mode-mediated interaction inside PIP phase takes on a different form. Inside
\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
phase, isospin polarization has a nonzero expectation value
\phi_{0}=\langle\Psi^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi\rangle\neq 0
. Since we are interested in the fluctuations of the order parameter, we define
\phi=\phi_{0}+\delta\phi
where
\delta\phi=\delta\phi_{\parallel}+i\delta\phi_{\perp}
, where parallel and perpendicular components correspond to amplitude and phase fluctuations. Then, we rewrite Eq.(
41
) in terms of
\delta\phi
:

\displaystyle H_{s}[\phi]=H_{s}[\phi_{0}]+H_{s}[\delta\phi(x,t)],

(46)

\displaystyle H_{s}[\phi_{0}]=\frac{J}{2}|\phi_{0}|^{2}+\frac{U}{2}|\phi_{0}|^{4},

(47)

\displaystyle H_{s}[\delta\phi(x,t)]=\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\delta\phi|^{2}+\frac{K}{2}|\partial_{x}\delta\phi|^{2}+\left(\frac{J}{2}+3U|\phi_{0}|^{2}\right)\delta\phi_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{U}{2}\phi_{\parallel}^{4}.

(48)

By minimizing
H_{s}[\phi_{0}]
, we find
|\phi_{0}|^{2}=-\frac{J}{2U}
(which is positive since
J<0
). Using this result, Eq.(
48
) is simplified to:

H_{s}[\delta\phi(x,t)]=\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\delta\phi|^{2}+\frac{K}{2}|\partial_{x}\delta\phi|^{2}-J\delta\phi_{\parallel}^{2}+\frac{U}{2}\delta\phi_{\parallel}^{4}.

(49)

The resulting propagator of soft fluctuations is

D_{\delta\phi\delta\phi}(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})=-\langle\delta\phi_{\parallel}(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})^{2}+\delta\phi_{\perp}(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}})^{2}\rangle=-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+Kp^{2}-2J}-\frac{1}{\omega^{2}+Kp^{2}}.

(50)

The coupling between the soft mode and electrons can be written, accordingly, as a sum of two parts:

H_{e-s}=\gamma\phi_{0}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}+\gamma\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{q}}}\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{q}}}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}+{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}+{\rm h.c.}.

(51)

where
\delta\phi_{\boldsymbol{q}}=\int dx\delta\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})e^{-i{\boldsymbol{q}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}
The first term of this expression can be absorbed into the single-particle qHamiltonian, whereas the second term generates a soft-mode-mediated interaction between electrons. The resulting effective e-e interaction is of a form similar to Eq.(
44
); the expression of
\tilde{V}
inside ordered phase is slightly different from the one in the disordered phase. We find:

\tilde{V}_{i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}}}=-\gamma^{2}(-D_{\delta\phi\delta\phi}(i\omega,{\boldsymbol{p}}))=-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\omega^{2}+Kp^{2}-2J}-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\omega^{2}+Kp^{2}},

(52)

In summary, putting the two cases together, the effective e-e interaction mediated by fluctuations takes the form of Eq.(
44
), with
\tilde{V}
in the two cases given by

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{i\omega,p}=\begin{cases}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{-\omega^{2}-\omega_{p\parallel}^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{-\omega^{2}-\omega_{p\perp}^{2}},\quad\text{inside $\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}$ phase},\\
\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{-\omega^{2}-\Omega_{p}^{2}},\quad\text{outside $\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}$ phase}.\end{cases}

(53)

where

\Omega_{p}=\sqrt{Kp^{2}+J},\quad\omega_{p\perp}=\sqrt{K}p,\quad\omega_{p\parallel}=\sqrt{Kp^{2}-2J}.

(54)

In the main text we exploit this interaction as a pairing glue for superconductivity. Softening of the polarization mode
\phi
near the PIP phase boundary leads to a stronger interaction at the PIP onset, which explains that in the measured phase diagram superconductivity occurs at the PIP phase boundary.

I.5
Identifying the strongest pairing channel

As we have shown in the main text, the linearized pairing gap equation in an arbitrary pairing channel
\langle\Psi^{T}\tilde{O}\Psi\rangle
takes the following form:

-\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\tilde{V}O^{T}G^{T}(-i\omega,-p)\tilde{O}G(i\omega,p)O=\tilde{O},

(55)

where
O=\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
is the PIP order parameter.
Here, the quantity
\tilde{O}
can be an arbitrary complex-valued
4\times 4
matrix. We note that we have ignored the frequency and momentum dependence of
\tilde{V}
for simplicity.

We note that the matrix structure in Eq.(
55
) resembles Eq.(
34
). Analyzing Eq.(
55
) in a same way as in the analysis of Eq.(
34
) above, we see
that the problem of strongest pairing channel is equivalent to maximizing the following function:

\tilde{F}[\tilde{O}]=-\int\frac{d\omega d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\left[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}O^{T}G^{T}(-i\omega,-p)\tilde{O}G(i\omega,p)O\right]

(56)

under the normalization constraint
\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tilde{O}=1
.

Plugging the propagators
G
and
G^{T}
in Eq.
56
, and carrying out integration over
\omega
and
p
yields

\displaystyle\tilde{F}[O]=\tilde{a}_{0}\tilde{u}_{0}-\tilde{a}_{1}\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{a}_{2}\tilde{u}_{2}+\tilde{a}_{3}\tilde{u}_{3}+\tilde{b}\tilde{v}

(57)

Here
\tilde{V}<0
, the quantities
\tilde{u}_{0}
,
\tilde{u}_{1}
,
\tilde{u}_{2}
,
\tilde{u}_{3}
and
\tilde{v}
are quadratic functions of
\tilde{O}
,
and the coefficients
\tilde{a}_{i}
’s and
\tilde{b}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{0}=\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\tilde{A}(E),\ \ \tilde{a}_{1}=\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\alpha^{2}\left(p_{1}^{3}-3p_{1}p_{2}^{2}\right)^{2}\tilde{B}(E),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{2}=\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\beta^{2}(3p_{1}^{2}p_{2}-p_{2}^{3})^{2}\tilde{B}(E),

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{3}=\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}D^{2}\tilde{B}(E),\quad\tilde{b}=\int\frac{d^{2}p}{\left(2\pi\right)^{2}}\mu D\tilde{B}(E),

where we have introduced notations
\tilde{A}(E)=\frac{1}{4}(\frac{E\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E^{2}-\mu^{2}}+\frac{|\mu|\Theta(|\mu|-E)}{\mu^{2}-E^{2}})
and
\tilde{B}(E)=\frac{1}{4}(\frac{\Theta(E-|\mu|)}{E(E^{2}-\mu^{2})}+\frac{\Theta(|\mu|-E)}{|\mu|(\mu^{2}-E^{2})})
.
The quantities
\tilde{u}_{i}
’s and
\tilde{v}
, which describe possible superconducting pairing order types, are defined as

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{0}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\tilde{O}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}],\quad\tilde{u}_{1}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{2}1\tilde{O}\tau_{2}1],

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{2}=-\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{3}\tilde{O}\tau_{1}\sigma_{3}],\quad\tilde{u}_{3}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{2}\tilde{O}\tau_{1}\sigma_{2}],

\displaystyle\tilde{v}=\frac{1}{4}{\rm\,Tr\,}[\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}\{\tilde{O},1\sigma_{3}\}\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}],

(58)

where
\{\tilde{O},1\sigma_{3}\}=\tilde{O}1\sigma_{3}+1\sigma_{3}\tilde{O}
.

As a reminder,
\tilde{O}
can be an arbitrary complex-valued
4\times 4
matrix, so we express it under Pauli matrix basis
\tilde{O}=\tilde{d}_{ij}\tau_{i}\sigma_{j}
(
i,j=0,1,2,3
) where
\tilde{d}_{ij}
’s are complex numbers. With this, we see that
\tilde{u}_{0}
,
\tilde{u}_{1}
,
\tilde{u}_{2}
and
\tilde{u}_{3}
generates diagonal terms
|d_{ij}|^{2}
, whereas
\tilde{v}
generates off-diagonal terms that hybridize the
\tau_{i}1
and
\tau_{i}\sigma_{3}
components of
\tilde{O}

, i.e.
d_{i3}^{*}d_{i0}
and
d_{i0}^{*}d_{i3}
. As a result, the candidate channels that maximize the function
\tilde{F}[\tilde{O}]
should take one of the following forms

\tilde{O}=\tau_{i}\sigma_{1},\tau_{i}\sigma_{2},\tau_{i}(\xi 1+\eta\sigma_{3})

(59)

where
\xi
and
\eta
are complex numbers.

Plugging these three candidate forms into
\tilde{F}[\tilde{O}]
, after a straightforward calculation we find

\displaystyle\max(\tilde{F}[\tau_{i}\sigma_{1}])=\tilde{F}[\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}]=\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{1}-\tilde{a}_{2}-\tilde{a}_{3}

\displaystyle\max(\tilde{F}[\tau_{i}\sigma_{2}])=\tilde{F}[\tau_{3}\sigma_{2}]=\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{a}_{2}-\tilde{a}_{3}

(60)

whereas for
\tau_{i}(\xi 1+\eta\sigma_{3})
channel,
\tilde{F}[\tilde{O}]
is maximized at

\tilde{O}=\tau_{1}(\xi_{*}1+\eta_{*}\sigma_{3}),

(61)

The maximal value of
\tilde{F}
is

\tilde{F}[\tau_{1}(\xi_{*}1+\eta_{*}\sigma_{3})]=\tilde{a}_{1}+\tilde{a}_{2}+\sqrt{(\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{3})^{2}-4\tilde{b}^{2}}

(62)

where
\xi_{*}=\frac{-\tilde{a}_{0}-\tilde{a}_{3}}{\sqrt{(\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{3})^{2}-4\tilde{b}^{2}}}
,
\eta_{*}=\frac{2\tilde{b}}{\sqrt{(\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{3})^{2}-4\tilde{b}^{2}}}
.
Using the expression of
\tilde{a}_{0}
,
\tilde{a}_{3}
and

\tilde{b}
, it is not hard to show that
\sqrt{(\tilde{a}_{0}+\tilde{a}_{3})^{2}-4\tilde{b}^{2}}>\tilde{a}_{0}-\tilde{a}_{3}
. Therefore, comparing Eq.(
60
) and Eq.(
62
), we conclude that the strongest pairing channel is the one given in Eq.(
61
)

Lastly, we discuss the case when PIP order is
\tau_{2}\sigma_{1}
. In this case, one can repeat the same procedure. The form of Eq.(
56
) will be unchanged, while Eq.(
58
) should be rewritten using the following substitution:

\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\tilde{O}\tau_{1}\rightarrow-\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{2}\tilde{O}\tau_{2},\quad\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{2}\tilde{O}\tau_{2}\rightarrow-\tilde{O}^{\dagger}\tau_{1}\tilde{O}\tau_{1}

(63)

Under this substitution, the values of
\tilde{F}
for
\tau_{1}\sigma_{j}
and
\tau_{2}\sigma_{j}
channels (
j=0,1,2,3
) are invariant, while the values of
\tilde{F}
in
\tau_{3}\sigma_{j}
and
\tau_{0}\sigma_{j}
channels pick up a minus sign as compared to their values in the case when the PIP order is
\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
. With this in mind, it is straightforward to write down the value of
\tilde{F}
in all channels. Ultimately, we find that the maximal values of
\tilde{F}
in each of the three types of channels
\tau_{i}\sigma_{1}
,
\tau_{i}\sigma_{2}
and
\tau_{i}(\xi 1+\eta\sigma_{3})
are the same as Eq.(
60
) and Eq.(
62
). Therefore, no matter which of the two candidate PIP orders
\tau_{1}\sigma_{1}
and
\tau_{2}\sigma_{1}
is chosen as the actual PIP order, the strongest pairing instability always occurs in the pairing channel given in Eq.(
61
).
