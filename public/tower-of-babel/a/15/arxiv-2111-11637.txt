On the Capacity of MISO Optical Intensity Channels With Per-Antenna Intensity Constraints
Ru-Han Chen, Longguang Li, Jian Zhang, Wenyi Zhang, Jing Zhou

Abstract
This paper investigates the capacity of general multiple-input single-output (MISO) optical intensity channels (OICs) under per-antenna peak- and average- intensity constraints. We first consider the MISO equal-cost constrained OIC (EC-OIC) , where, apart from the peak- intensity constraint, average intensities of inputs are equal to arbitrarily preassigned constants. The second model of our interest is the MISO bounded-cost constrained OIC (BC-OIC) , where, as compared with the EC-OIC , average intensities of inputs are no larger than arbitrarily preassigned constants. By leveraging tools from quantile functions, stop-loss transform and convex ordering of nonnegative random variables, we prove two decomposition theorems for bounded and nonnegative random variables, based on which we equivalently transform both the EC-OIC and the BC-OIC into respective single-input single-output channels under a peak- intensity and several stop-loss mean constraints. Capacity lower and upper bounds for both channels are established, based on which the asymptotic capacity at high and low signal-to-noise-ratio are determined. Index terms — Channel capacity, Gaussian noise, intensity-modulation and direct-detection (IM/DD), multiple-input single-output, per-antenna intensity constraint, optical wireless communication.

Channel Model, Problem Formulation, and Summary of Results

2.1
Channel Model

Consider an
n_{\textnormal{T}}\times 1
(
n_{\textnormal{T}}\geq 2
) MISO
OIC

Y=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{X}}+Z,

(1)

where the
n_{\textnormal{T}}
-dimensional vector
\mathbf{X}=\left(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
denotes the channel input, whose entries denote optical intensities emitted from LED transmitters, and hence, are nonnegative:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{k}}\geq 0,\quad\forall k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]};

where the
n_{\textnormal{T}}
-dimensional vector
{\mathbf{h}}=\left(h_{1},\cdots,h_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
denotes the constant channel gain vector with positive entries, taking into account LED conversion factors, path loss, and the photodetector responsivity, etc., the sum of which is normalized to be
unity
, i.e.,

\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{i}=1;

where
Z
denotes the overall effect of the ambient shot noise induced by background radiations and the thermal noise of electronic devices, which is approximated to follow the Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
, i.e.,

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

and is independent of
\mathbf{X}
; and where
Y
denotes the photocurrent output. It should be noted that the output
Y
can be negative even if the input
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{X}}
is always nonnegative.

In this paper, we assume that full channel knowledge is available at both
the transmitter and the receiver
. Considering the limited dynamic range of LED devices and the requirement of illumination quality or energy consumption, the input
\mathbf{X}
is subject to one of the following two types of per-antenna
intensity
constraints:

1.

Equal-cost constraints: the channel input is subject to per-antenna peak-
intensity constraints
and average-
intensity
equality constraints, i.e.,

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle 0\leq X_{k}\leq 1},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\right]=\alpha_{k},\quad\forall k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]},

where the constant
\alpha_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]
denotes the ratio of the average
intensity
to the maximum allowed peak
intensity
of the
k
-th transmitter.

2.

Bounded-cost constraints: the channel input is subject to per-antenna peak-
intensity
constraints and average-
intensity
inequality constraints, i.e.,

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\displaystyle 0\leq X_{k}\leq 1},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\right]\leq\alpha_{k},~\quad\forall k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]},

where the constant
\alpha_{k}\in\left[0,1\right]
is the ratio of the maximum allowed average
intensity
to the maximum allowed peak
intensity
of the
k
-th transmitter.

For brevity of expression,
we
let
{\bm{\alpha}}=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
. Without loss of generality, we assume the entries of
{\bm{\alpha}}
are ordered as
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
. In the rest of the paper, we call the
OIC
under input constraints (
2
) as the
EC-OIC
, and under constraints (
3
) as the
BC-OIC
.

We rewrite the MISO channel (
1
) as

Y=S+Z,

(4)

where the equivalent input is

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S&\triangleq\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{X}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}X_{k}.\\
\end{aligned}

(5)

Then
S
must satisfy

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}0\leq S\leq 1},

(6)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}}

(7)

for the
EC-OIC
or

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}}

(8)

for the
BC-OIC
.

2.3
Outline of Approach

Herein, without introducing any new terminology, we provide an outline of the approach in the paper, which will help the reader grasp our basic idea.

•

Stage 1:
We have already shown that it is sufficient to consider the capacity problem in the case where
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
and
\alpha_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
; see Sec.
2.2
.

•

Stage 2:
Then we turn to the problem of for what distributions can a random variable
S
, satisfying
0\leq S\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}[S]=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}
, be decomposed as
S=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}X_{k}
with
0\leq X_{k}\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=\alpha_{k}
. This problem is tackled in two steps.

\circ

Necessity:
If
S
can be decomposed in the above form, then
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S-\left(\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}}h_{k}\right)\right)_{+}\right]\leq\sum_{k\in\mathcal{J}^{\rm c}}h_{k}\alpha_{k}
for all
2^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
subsets
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[n_{\textnormal{T}}]
.

\circ

Sufficiency:
If the above expectation condition is satisfied for
n_{\textnormal{T}}-1
subsets
\mathcal{J}=[1]
,
[2]
,
\cdots
, and
[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1]
, then
S
indeed has such a decomposition. For this, we give a constructive proof, where a procedure for generating the desired decomposition is presented.

•

Stage 3:
Next, we answer the similar problem of under which conditions the random variable
S
, satisfying
0\leq S\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}[S]\leq\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}
, can be decomposed as
S=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}X_{k}
with
0\leq X_{k}\leq 1
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]\leq\alpha_{k}
. It is shown that if sufficient conditions in Stage 2 are fulfilled, there exists such type of decomposition for
S
as well.

•

Stage 4:
The above results on decomposition lead to alternative capacity expressions for the MISO EC-OIC and the MISO BC-OIC. These expressions suggest that either channel is equivalent to some SISO OIC with certain corresponding constraints. Therefore, the subsequent capacity analysis can be significantly simplified.

•

Stage 5:
By relaxing some of the input constraints to be satisfied and using existing information-theoretic techniques, several capacity upper and lower bounds are derived, and then asymptotic capacities are obtained by showing tightness of some bounds.

Unless otherwise stated, the supports of random variables under our consideration are subsets of the interval

The cumulative distribution function

\displaystyle Q_{\!X}(p)\triangleq\inf\left\{x\in[0,1]:\,p\leq F_{\!X}(x)\right\},

for

,
2
2

2

Since the random variable

is bounded from above, we define

on
\left(0,1\right]

\left(0,1\right)
.
which correspondingly is non-decreasing and left-continuous. The following well-known property of quantile functions can be immediately derived by the definition (
15

Proposition 2 (
[
39

The quantile function

satisfies

\displaystyle F_{\!X}(x)\geq p~\textnormal{if and only if}~x\geq Q_{\!X}(p),

for any

.

{IEEEproof}

Omitted.

A consequence of the Galois inequality is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.

Let

be a random variable uniformly distributed on the interval
\left(0,1\right]

, we have

\displaystyle X\overset{\text{d}}{=}Q_{\!X}(U),

(17)

and furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\!X}(p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p,

(18)

where

Since a monotonic function on a closed interval must be Riemann integrable, we can define the stop-loss transform (SLT) of a random variable as follows.

The SLT is a well-known concept in actuarial science for ordering risks

Several

(P1)

(P2)

(P4)

Here we present an alternative geometrical interpretation of the SLT,

Then the area of

\displaystyle=\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!X}(t),~t\in\left[0,1\right].

{IEEEproof}

The contrapositive of Proposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle\!X}_{t}=\left\{(p,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:y\geq t,~0\leq 1-p<1-F_{\!X}(y)\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle\!X}_{t}

for

3.4
Comonotonic Distribution

Research on comonotonicity arises naturally in risk analysis since the upper bound (in the sense of convex ordering) of the sum of several random variables with given marginals is achieved by a comonotonic distribution
[
43
,
44
]
. In the following, we introduce two relevant definitions.

Definition 3

(
[
44
]
)
.

A set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is comonotonic if
\mathbf{x}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
or
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{x}
holds for any
\mathbf{x}

and

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 4

(
[
43
,
44
]
)
.

A random vector
\mathbf{X}=\left(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
is comonotonic if either one of following equivalent propositions holds:

•

there is a comonotonic set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathbb{P}\left\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{X}\right\}=1
;

•

\mathbf{X}\overset{\text{d}}{=}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(Q_{\!X_{\!1}}(U),\cdots,Q_{\!X_{\!n}}(U)\right)}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
, where the random variable
U
is uniformly distributed on
\left(0,1\right]
and
Q_{\!X_{\!k}}(\cdot)
is the quantile function of
X_{k}
for
k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n\right]}
.

As a special statistical dependence structure, comonotonicity is used to describe the phenomenon that several random variables always
vary
in the same direction, i.e., simultaneously non-decreasing or non-increasing.

3.5
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-Decomposability

We first introduce the following notation. For any index set
\mathcal{J}\subseteq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]}

\displaystyle{\mathsf{H}}_{\mathcal{J}}\triangleq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{J}}h_{i},

(27)

and
hence,
the cumulative sum of channel gains
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i},~\forall\,k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]},

(28)

with
\mathsf{H}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[0]}}=0
.

Furthermore, denote the weighted average
intensity
of the last
n_{\textnormal{T}}-k
transmitters as

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{k}=\frac{\sum_{i={k+1}}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{i}\alpha_{i}}{1-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}},~\forall k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1\right]},

(29)

with
\bar{\alpha}_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}=0
. Clearly, we have
\bar{\alpha}_{0}>\bar{\alpha}_{1}>\cdots>\bar{\alpha}_{n_{\textnormal{T}}-1}=a_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}>\bar{\alpha}_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
.

Next, we define the
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposability of a random variable, which is a crucial concept in this paper.

Definition 5
.

A random variable

is said to be
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable if there exists a random vector
\mathbf{W}=\left(W_{1},\cdots,W_{n_{\textnormal{T}}}\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
satisfying
\mathsf{supp}\,\mathbf{W}\subseteq[0,1]^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{W}\right]=\bm{\alpha}
such that

S=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{W}
.

It is clear that any equivalent input signal
S
(given by (
5
)) of the
EC-OIC
must be
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable. Conversely, if a random variable
S
is
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable, there exists a channel input
\mathbf{W}
feasible to the
EC-OIC
such that
S
is the corresponding equivalent input. Thus, we conclude that a random variable
S
is a feasible equivalent input for the
EC-OIC

if and only if

S
is
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable.
We remind the reader that the above statement does not necessarily hold for the BC-OIC. Instead, for the BC-OIC,
S
is a feasible equivalent input
if and only if
there exists a vector
\mathbf{a}
satisfying
\bm{0}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{a}\preccurlyeq\bm{\alpha}
such that
S
is
\left(\mathbf{h},\mathbf{a}\right)
-decomposable.

A particular subclass of
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposability defined as follows will be also useful in our subsequent analysis.

Definition 6
.

A random variable
S
is said to be
comonotonically
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable
if there exists a
random vector
\mathbf{X}^{\rm c}=\left(Q_{\!X_{\!1}}\left(U\right),\cdots,Q_{\!X_{\!n_{\textnormal{T}}}}\left(U\right)\right)^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}
, satisfying
\mathsf{supp}\,\mathbf{X}^{\rm c}\subseteq[0,1]^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}
and
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{X}^{\rm c}\right]=\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle S=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{X}^{\rm c}=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(U),

(30)

where
U
is a random variable uniformly distributed on
\left(0,1\right]
and
Q_{\!X_{\!k}}(\cdot)
is the quantile function of
X_{k}
for
k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]}
.

3.6
Maximally Convex Distribution

We develop the definition and properties of maximally convex distributions, which
will be very useful
throughout this paper.

Definition 7
.

A discrete random variable
\bar{S}_{\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}}
is said to
obey

\mathsf{supp}\,\bar{S}_{\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}}=\left\{0,\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[1]},\,\cdots,\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1]},\,1\right\}

The following properties of the maximally convex distribution can be easily verified.

Proposition 5.

The SLT of

Directly from Definition

\bar{S}_{\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}}

\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)

Let

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\bar{S}_{\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}}-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)_{+}

for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[R_{k}\right]=\sum_{m=k+1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{m}\alpha_{m}

is equivalent to a SISO

Due to the monotonicity of the SISO capacity

]
, the capacity-achieving

can be further restricted to

4.2
Equivalent Capacity
Expressions

The above two theorems actually state necessary and sufficient conditions under which a random variable

is a feasible equivalent input for the EC-OIC or the BC-OIC, respectively. These naturally lead to alternative expressions of the capacities
\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{E}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)
and
\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{B}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)
as follows.

Corollary 2 (Equivalent capacity expression
for EC-OIC
).

The capacity
\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{E}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)
can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{E}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)=\sup_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathscr{P}_{\!S}}}\mathop{}\!\textnormal{I}\left(S;S+Z\right),

(38)

where the supremum is over all probability laws
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathscr{P}_{\!S}}
on
S
with
\mathsf{supp}\,S\subseteq[0,1]
and under
stop-loss mean
constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha};

(39a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)_{+}\right]\leq\left(1-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)\bar{\alpha}_{k},~\forall\,k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1\right]}.

(39b)

{IEEEproof}

Theorem
1
implies that an input
\mathbf{X}
is feasible to the
EC-OIC
if and only if
the corresponding
equivalent input
S=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{X}
satisfies
\mathsf{supp}\,S\subseteq[0,1]
,
\mathbb{E}[S]=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}
and
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]})\leq\left(1-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)\bar{\alpha}_{k}
for
k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1\right]}
. Corollary
2
thus immediately follows.

For the BC-OIC, we exhibit a similar expression of
\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{B}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)
in the following.

Corollary 3 (Equivalent capacity expression
for BC-OIC
).

The capacity
\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{B}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)
can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}_{\textnormal{B}}\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha},\sigma\right)=\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathscr{P}_{\!S}}}\,\mathop{}\!\textnormal{I}\left(S;S+Z\right),

(40)

where the supremum is over all probability laws
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathscr{P}_{\!S}}
on
S
with
\mathsf{supp}\,S\subseteq[0,1]
and under
stop-loss mean
constraints:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)_{+}\right]\leq\left(1-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)\bar{\alpha}_{k},~\forall\,k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\{0\}\cup\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}-1\right]}.

(41)

{IEEEproof}

Accroding to Theorem
2
, an input
\mathbf{X}
is feasible to the BC-OIC if and only if the corresponding equivalent input

satisfies
\mathsf{supp}\,S\subseteq[0,1]
,
\mathbb{E}[S]\leq\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\bm{\alpha}
and
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]})\leq\left(1-\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[k]}\right)\bar{\alpha}_{k}

2

Corollaries

the EC-OIC
, a maximally correlated multivariate binary input has been shown to maximize the variance of the equivalent input in
[

, which lets the transmitter with the maximum average
intensity
grow prior to the others. We remark that both the minimum-energy signaling and the maximally correlated multivariate binary input can be regarded as special classes of comonotonic distributions. For the above reasons, it is natural to investigate the behaviors of comonotonic inputs in MISO OICs of our interest.

Decomposition of the quantile of a nonnegative and bounded random variable

Given a comonotonic input
\mathbf{X}
with mean
\bm{\alpha}
, the equivalent input
S=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{X}
must be comonotonically
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable. According to Definition
6
,
the quantile
Q_{\!S}(p)
of
S
is a mixture of quantiles as
Q_{\!S}(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}(p)
. Hence, a random variable
S
with the quantile

is comonotonically
\left(\mathbf{h},\bm{\alpha}\right)
-decomposable if and only if there exist
n_{\textnormal{T}}
quantiles
Q_{\!X_{\!k}}(p)
(
k\in{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left[n_{\textnormal{T}}\right]}
) satisfying the following three conditions:

1.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}\!:\!(0,1]\!\to\![0,1]
is non-decreasing and left-continuous;

2.

\int_{0}^{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p=\alpha_{k}
;

3.

Q_{\!S}(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{n_{\textnormal{T}}}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
,
\forall p\in(0,1]
.

Thus, the core problem
in
comonotonic decomposition of a random variable is how to decompose its quantile function
Q_{\!S}(p)
. In Figure
5
, we present a heuristic geometrical interpretation of the strategy for constructing desired quantiles. Suppose that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
is known and is the quantile of
R_{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}(U)}
. The curve of quantile sum
\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
must lie in the green region due to boundedness of quantiles, i.e., larger than zero, less than
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
and
\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}h_{k}
, and within a curved strip of width
h_{m}
below
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
. In addition, to meet the integral requirement of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!m}}}(p)
, the sum quantile
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
must satisfy
\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{1}\leq h_{m}\alpha_{m}
and
\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{1}+\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{2}\geq h_{m}\alpha_{m}
. Note that the magenta region
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle R_{m}}_{\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[m-1]}}
(the right-hand side is defined in Proposition
4
) and the yellow region
\mathcal{A}_{3}
is a vertical translate of
\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle R_{m}}_{h_{m}}
. Then we have

\displaystyle~~~~\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{1}+\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p-\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}\alpha_{k}-\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{A}_{3}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}\alpha_{k}-\textnormal{Area}~\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle R_{m}}_{h_{m}}.

(63)

Thus, to guarantee the existence of
Q_{m}(p)
with a required definite integration value, the most greedy approach in constructing
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
is to let the areas of
\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle R_{m}}_{h_{m}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\scriptscriptstyle R_{m}}_{\mathsf{H}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}[m-1]}}

be
as small as possible, i.e., the area of
\sum_{k=1}^{m}h_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q_{\!X_{\!k}}}(p)
should be preempted at the bottom. From above considerations,
in Proposition
9
we construct and analyze the “most greedy” class of quantile functions, i.e., so-called greedily-constructed quantiles, which coincide with
Q_{\!S}(p)
before a threshold and otherwise coincide with a downward translate of
Q_{\!S}(p)
.

Proposition 9.

Let
S
be a random variable on
[0,1]
and define
the greedily-constructed quantile as the following
nonnegative parametric function

\displaystyle\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(p;v,z){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\triangleq}\begin{cases}Q_{\!S}(p)&,~\mbox{$0<p\leq F_{\!S}(v)$};\\
v&,~\mbox{$F_{\!S}(v)<p\leq F_{\!S}(v+z)$};\\
Q_{\!S}(p)-z&,~\mbox{$F_{\!S}(v+z)<p\leq 1$},\\
\end{cases}

(64)

where the variable
p\in(0,1]
, and parameters
v\in[0,1]
and
z\in\left[0,1-v\right]
. Then

1.

\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(p;v,z)
is the quantile function of
R=\varphi\left(S;v,z\right)
, where

\displaystyle\varphi\left(s;v,z\right)=\begin{cases}s&,~\mbox{$s\leq v$},\\
v&,~\mbox{$v<s\leq v+z$},\\
s-z&,~\mbox{$s>v+z$}.\end{cases}

(65)

2.

The SLT of
R
is

\displaystyle\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!R}(t)=\begin{cases}\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(t)-\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(v)+\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(v+z)&,~\mbox{$t\leq v$},\\
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(t+z)&,~\mbox{$t>v$}.\end{cases}

(66)

{IEEEproof}

We first rewrite

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(S;v,z\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(Q_{\!S}(U);v,z\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}Q_{\!S}(U)&,~\mbox{$Q_{\!S}(U)\leq v$}\\
v&,~\mbox{$v<Q_{\!S}(U)\leq v+z$}\\
Q_{\!S}(U)-z&,~\mbox{$Q_{\!S}(U)>v+z$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}Q_{\!S}(U)&,~\mbox{$U\leq F_{\!S}(v)$}\\
v&,~\mbox{$F_{\!S}(v)<U\leq F_{\!S}(v+z)$}\\
Q_{\!S}(U)-z&,~\mbox{$U>F_{\!S}(v+z)$}\end{cases}

(67)

\displaystyle=\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(U;v,z),

(68)

where (
67
) follows from the Galois inequality (
16
).

Since
Q_{\!S}(p)
is the quantile of
S
, the function
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(p;v,z)
is left-continuous on the interval
p\in(0,1]
and
piecewise
non-decreasing on the intervals
\left(0,F_{\!S}(v)\right]
,
\left(F_{\!S}(v),F_{\!S}(v+z)\right]
and
\left(F_{\!S}(v+z),1\right]
, respectively. Recall that
Q_{\!S}\left(F_{\!S}(v)\right)=\inf\left\{s:F_{\!S}(s)\geq F_{\!S}(v)\right\}
and hence
Q_{\!S}\left(F_{\!S}(v)\right)\leq v
.
Next, based on the contrapositive of Proposition
2
, we have
Q_{\!S}(p)>v+z
for any real number
p\in\left(F_{\!S}(v+z),1\right]
. Hence, the parametric function
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(p;v,z)
is non-decreasing and left-continuous on the interval
p\in(0,1]
. Proposition
3
shows that
\psi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(U;v,z)
is the quantile function of
R
.

It is clear that
0\leq R\leq S
, and hence,
\mathsf{supp}\,R\subseteq[0,1]
. We notice that if
t>v
,
\left(\varphi\left(s;v,z\right)-t\right)_{+}=\left(s-t-z\right)_{+}
. Then

\displaystyle\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!R}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(R-t\right)_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\varphi\left(S;v,z\right)-t\right)_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(S-t-z\right)_{+}\right]

\displaystyle=\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\!S}(t+z),~t>v.

(69)

The
other

\displaystyle~~~~\left(\varphi\left(s;v,z\right)-t\right)_{+}

\displaystyle=(s-t)_{+}-\left(\left(s-v\right)_{+}+v-t\right)+\left(\left(s-v-z\right)_{+}+v-t\right)

\displaystyle=(s-t)_{+}-(s-v)_{+}+(s-v-z)_{+}

(70)

holds for

With a slight abuse of terminology, we also refer to the expression (

We will see later that, components of a decomposable random variable can be constructed via the greedily-constructed quantiles.

8
Conclusion

In this paper, we consider two types of MISO
OIC
s under per-antenna
intensity
constraints.
We first express their capacities as a maximization problem
over distributions of the sum random variable
S=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{X}
. The main
challenge is to equivalently transform per-antenna average-
intensity constraints
on

\mathbf{X}
to constraints on
S
, since the mapping from
\mathbf{x}

onto
s=\mathbf{h}^{\textup{{\tiny T}}}\mathbf{x}
is not injective. This problem is solved by finding the
necessary and sufficient conditions for two types of decomposition of a bounded and nonnegative random variable.
Specifically, we show that both types of original MISO OICs with per-antenna
intensity
constraints can be transformed into
a SISO one with an
amplitude constraint
and several
stop-loss mean
constraints.
