Abstract
. We investigate the extension of Monadic Second Order logic, interpreted over infinite words and trees, with generalized “for almost all” quantifiers interpreted using the notions of Baire category and Lebesgue measure.

1.
Introduction

Monadic Second Order logic (MSO) is an extension of first order logic (FO) allowing quantification over subsets of the domain (
\forall X.\,\phi
) and including a membership predicate between elements of the domain and its subsets (
x\in X
). Two fundamental results about MSO, due to Büchi and Rabin respectively, state that the MSO theories of
(\mathbb{N},<)
and of the
full binary tree
(see Section
3
for precise definitions) are decidable.

A different kind of extension of first order logic which has been studied in the literature, starting from the seminal works of Mostowski
[
21
]
, is by means of
generalized quantifiers
. A typical example is the “there exists infinitely many” quantifier:

\mathcal{M}\models\exists^{\infty}x.\,\phi\ \Longleftrightarrow\text{ the set $\{m\in M\mid\phi(m)\}$ is infinite.}

The formula
\neg(\exists^{\infty}x.\,(x=x))
holds only on finite models and is therefore not expressible in first order logic. Hence, in general,
\textnormal{FO}\subsetneq\textnormal{FO}+\exists^{\infty}
. Note however that on some fixed structures the logic
FO
and
\textnormal{FO}+\exists^{\infty}
may have the same expressive power. This is the case, for example, for the structure
(\mathbb{N},{<})
.

It is natural to try to merge the two approaches and consider extensions of MSO with generalized quantifiers. In this context, one can consider generalized first order quantifiers as well as generalized second order quantifiers. For example, the second order generalized quantifier (
\exists^{\infty}X.\phi
) has the following meaning:

\mathcal{M}\models\exists^{\infty}X.\,\phi\ \Longleftrightarrow\text{the set $\{A\subseteq M\mid\phi(A)\}$ is infinite.}

The topic of
MSO
with generalized second order quantifiers is not new. In particular, Bojańczyk
in 2004 (
[
3
]
) has initiated an investigation of the
unbounding quantifier
(
\mathsf{U}X.\,\phi
)

\mathcal{M}\models\mathsf{U}X.\,\phi\ \Longleftrightarrow\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.\,\exists A\subseteq M.\,\big(\text{$A$ is finite, $|A|\geq n$, $\phi(A)$}\big).

It was shown recently that the
\textnormal{MSO}+\mathsf{U}
theory of
(\mathbb{N},{<})
is undecidable
[
7
]
.

Bárány, Kaiser and Rabinovich have investigated in
[
2
]
the second order quantifier
\exists^{\infty}
mentioned above and also the cardinality quantifier
\exists^{\mathfrak{c}}
defined as:

\mathcal{M}\models\exists^{\mathfrak{c}}X.\,\phi\Longleftrightarrow\text{the set $\{A\subseteq M\mid\phi(A)\}$ has cardinality $\mathfrak{c}=2^{\aleph_{0}}$.}

Interestingly it was shown that, on the structures
(\mathbb{N},{<})
and the full binary tree, the quantifiers
\exists^{\infty}
and
\exists^{\mathfrak{c}}
can be eliminated. That is, to each formula of
\textnormal{MSO}+\exists^{\mathfrak{c}}+\exists^{\infty}
corresponds (effectively) a semantically equivalent formula of
MSO
.

In a series of manuscripts in the late 70’s (see
[
25
]
for an overview) H. Friedman investigated two kinds of first-order generalized quantifiers called (Baire)
Category quantifier

(\forall^{\ast})
and (full probability)
Measure quantifier
(
\forall^{{=}1}
), respectively.

The goal of this article is to collect recent results (from
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
) regarding extensions of
MSO
with second order Category and Measure quantifiers. The meaning of such second order quantifiers is given as follows:

\mathcal{M}\models\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi\Longleftrightarrow\text{the set $\{A\subseteq M\mid\phi(A)\}$ is comeager.}

and

\mathcal{M}\models\forall^{{=}1}X.\,\phi\Longleftrightarrow\text{the set $\{A\subseteq M\mid\phi(A)\}$ has Lebesgue measure $1$.}

We will exclusively be interested in the properties of
\textnormal{MSO}+\forall^{\ast}
and
\textnormal{MSO}+\forall^{{=}1}
interpreted over the two fundamental structures
(\mathbb{N},{<})
and the full binary tree. Therefore, the domain of
\mathcal{M}
is always countable and the space of subsets
A\subseteq M
is a copy of the Cantor space
2^{\omega}
where the notions of (co)meager sets and Lebesgue (i.e., coin-flipping) measure are well defined. See Section
2
for precise definitions.

Results regarding
S1S
.

By denoting with
S1S
the
MSO
theory of
(\mathbb{N},{<})
we prove (Theorem
10
) that the Category quantifier (
\forall^{\ast}
) can be (effectively) eliminated. Therefore
S1S+\forall^{\ast}=S1S
and its theory is decidable. On the other hand, the addition of the Measure quantifier (
\forall^{{=}1}
) leads to an undecidable theory (Theorem
15
). This was proved in
[
18
]
using recent results from the theory of probabilistic automata. In this article we give a different proof of undecidability of
S1S+\forall^{{=}1}
based on an interpretation of Bojańczyk’s undecidable theory
S1S+\mathsf{U}
.

Results regarding
S2S
.

By denoting with
S2S
the
MSO
theory of the full binary tree, the theory of
S1S+\forall^{{=}1}
can be interpreted within
S2S+\forall^{=1}
. As a consequence
S2S+\forall^{=1}
has an undecidable theory (Theorem
20
).

Results regarding
S2S
with generalized path quantifiers.

Another interesting way to extend
S2S
with variants of Friedman’s Category and Measure quantifiers is to restrict the quantification to range over infinite branches (paths) of the full binary tree. This leads to the definition of the path-Category (
\forall^{\ast}_{\pi}X.\,\phi
) and the path-Measure (
\forall^{{=}1}_{\pi}X.\,\phi
) second-order quantifiers.

A path
\pi
in the full binary tree is an infinite set of vertices which can be uniquely identified with an infinite set of direction
\pi\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\omega}
. By denoting with
\mathcal{P}
the collection
\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\omega}
we define:

\forall^{\ast}_{\pi}X.\,\phi\Longleftrightarrow\text{$\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}\mid\phi(\pi)\}$ is comeager as a subset of $\mathcal{P}$}

and

\forall^{{=}1}_{\pi}X.\,\phi\Longleftrightarrow\text{$\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}\mid\phi(\pi)\}$ has measure $1$ as a subset of $\mathcal{P}$}

We show that the path-Category quantifier can be (effectively) eliminated so that
S2S+\forall^{\ast}_{\pi}\equiv S2S
. On the other hand, we show that
S2S+\forall^{{=}1}_{\pi}\supsetneq S2S
is a strict extension of
S2S
. The question of whether
S2S+\forall^{{=}1}_{\pi}
has a decidable theory remains open from this work. See the list of open problems in Section
10
.

Related Work.

A significant amount of work has been devoted to the study of properties of regular sets of words and infinite trees with respect to Baire Category and probability. For example, a remarkable result of Staiger (
[
24
, Theorem 4]
) states that a regular set
L\!\subseteq\!\Sigma^{\omega}
of
\omega
-words is comeager if and only if
\mu(L)\!=\!1
, where
\mu
is the probability (coin flipping) measure on
\omega
-words. Furthermore the probability
\mu(L)
can be easily computed by an algorithm based on linear programming and it is always a rational number (see, e.g., Theorem 2 of
[
11
]
). Staiger’s theorem was rediscovered by Varacca and Völzer and has been used to develop a theory of fairness for reactive systems
[
27
]
.

In another direction, Gräedel have investigated determinacy, definability, and complexity issues of Banach–Mazur games played on graphs
[
13
]
. Our results regarding
S1S+\forall^{\ast}
are closely related to the results of Gräedel.

The study of Category and Measure properties of regular sets of infinite trees happens to be more complicated. Indeed, due to their high topological complexity, it is not even clear if such sets are Baire-measurable and Lebesgue measurable. These properties were established only recently (see
[
14
]
for Baire measurability and
[
12
]
for Lebesgue measurability). Furthermore, it has been shown in
[
19
]
that the equivalent of Staiger’s theorem fails for infinite trees. That is, there exist comeager regular sets of trees having probability
0
.

The problem of computing the probability of a regular set of infinite trees is still open in the literature. An algorithm for computing the (generally irrational) probability of regular sets definable by game automata is presented in
[
19
]
but a solution for the general case is still unknown (and the problem could be undecidable). The result of Theorem
18
proved in this article imply that it is possible to determine the Baire category of regular sets definable by game automata. A solution to the general case is unknown.

All the results mentioned above contribute to the theory of regular sets of
\omega
-words (
S1S
definable) and infinite trees (
S2S
definable). The novelty of the approach followed in this research is instead to augment the base logic (
S1S
or
S2S
) with generalized quantifiers capable of expressing properties related to Baire category and probability, and to study such logical extensions.

Interestingly, a different but related approach has been explored by Carayol, Haddad and Serre in
[
8
]
(see also
[
9
,
10
]
and the habilitation thesis
[
23
]
).
Rather than extending the base logic, it is the base (Rabin) tree automata model which is modified. The usual acceptance condition “there exists a run such that
all
paths are accepting” is replaced by the weaker “there exists a run such that
almost all
paths are accepting“. When
almost all
is interpreted as “for a set of measure
1
”, this results in the notion of qualitative automata of
[
8
]
.
The connections between this automata-oriented and our logic-oriented approach is further discussed in Section
9
.

2.
Technical Background

In this section we give the basic definitions regarding descriptive set theory, probability measures and tree automata that are needed in this work.

The set of natural numbers and their standard total order are denoted by the symbols
\omega
and
{<}
, respectively. Given sets
X
and
Y
we denote with
X^{Y}
the space of functions
X\to Y
. We can view elements of
X^{Y}
as
Y
-indexed sequences
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in Y}
of elements of
X
. Given a finite set
\Sigma
, we refer to
\Sigma^{\omega}
as the collection of
\omega
-words over
\Sigma
. The collection of
finite
sequences of elements in
\Sigma
is denoted by
\Sigma^{\ast}
. As usual we denote with
\epsilon
the empty sequence and with
ww^{\prime}
the concatenation of
w,w^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\ast}
. The prefix order on sequences is denoted
{\preceq}
i.e.,
u\preceq w
if
u
is a prefix of
w
.

We identify finite sequences
\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
as vertices of the infinite full binary tree so that
\epsilon
is the root and each vertex
w\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
has
w\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}
and
w\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}
as children. We denote by
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
the set
\Sigma^{\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}}
of functions labelling each vertex with an element in
\Sigma
. Elements
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
are called
trees over
\Sigma
.

2.1.
Games

In this paper we work with games of perfect information and infinite duration commonly referred to as Gale–Stewart games. In what follows we provide the basic definitions. We refer to
[
16
, Chapter 20.A]
for a detailed overview.

Gale–Stewart games are played between two players: Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
and Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}
. A game is given by a set of configurations
V
; an initial configuration
v_{0}\in V
; a winning condition
W\subseteq V^{\omega}
; and a definition of a
round
. A
round
starts in a configuration
v_{n}
; then players perform a fixed sequence of choices; and depending on these choices the round ends in a new configuration
v_{n+1}
. Each game specifies what are the exact choices available to the players and how the successive configuration
v_{n+1}
depends on the decisions made.

A
play
of such a game is an infinite sequence of configurations
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots
where the configuration
v_{n+1}
is a possible outcome of the
n
-th round that starts in a configuration
v_{n}
. A play is winning for Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
if it satisfies the winning condition (i.e.
(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots)\in W
); otherwise the play is winning for Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}
. A
strategy
of a player is a function that assigns to each finite sequence of configurations
(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
a way of resolving the choices of that player in the consecutive
n
-th round that starts in
v_{n}
. A play is
consistent
with a strategy if it can be obtained following that strategy. A strategy of a player is
winning
if all the plays consistent with that strategy are winning for the player. We say that a game is
determined
if one of the players has a winning strategy.

2.2.
Topology

Our exposition of topological and set–theoretical notions follows
[
16
]
.

The set
\{0,1\}^{\omega}
endowed with the product topology (where
\{0,1\}
is given the discrete topology) is called the
Cantor space
. The Cantor space is a Polish space (i.e., a complete separable metric space) and is
zero-dimensional
(i.e., it has a basis of
clopen
sets). Given a finite set
\Sigma
with at least two elements, the spaces
\Sigma^{\omega}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
are both homeomorphic to the Cantor space.

A subset
A\subseteq X
of a topological space is
nowhere dense
if the interior of its closure is the empty set, that is (
\texttt{int}(\texttt{cl}(A))=\emptyset
. A set
A\subseteq X
is of
(Baire) first category
(or
meager
) if
A
can be expressed as countable union of nowhere dense sets. A set
A\subseteq X
which is not meager is
of the second (Baire) category
.
The complement of a meager set is called
comeager
.
Recall that a set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\omega}
is a
G_{\delta}
set
if it can be expressed as a countable intersection of open sets. The following property of comeager sets will be useful. A subset
B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{\omega}
is comeager if and only if there exists a dense
G_{\delta}
set
A\subseteq B
which is comeager.

A set
B\subseteq X
has the
Baire property
if
X=U\triangle M
, for some open set
U\subseteq X
and meager set
M\subseteq X
, where
\triangle
is the operation of symmetric difference
X\triangle Y=(X\cup Y)\setminus(X\cap Y)
.

We now describe Banach–Mazur game (see
[
16
, Chapter 8.H]
for a detailed overview) which characterizes Baire category. We specialize the game to zero–dimensional Polish spaces, such as
\Sigma^{\omega}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
, since we only deal with this class of spaces in this work.

{defi}

[Banach–Mazur Game]
Let
X
be a zero–dimensional Polish space.
Fix a payoff set
A\subseteq X
. The configurations of the game
\mathbf{BM}(X,A)
are non-empty clopen sets
U\subseteq X
; the initial configuration is
X
. Consider a round starting in a configuration
U_{n}
. In such a round, first Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}
chooses a non-empty clopen set
U^{\prime}_{n}\subseteq U_{n}
and then Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
chooses a non-empty clopen set
U_{n+1}\subseteq U^{\prime}_{n}
. The consecutive configuration is
U_{n+1}
. An infinite play of that game is won by Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
if
\displaystyle\bigcap_{n\in\omega}U_{n}\cap A\neq\emptyset
and Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}
wins otherwise.

The standard way of defining that game is to represent it as a form of a Gale–Stewart game.

Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}

U^{\prime}_{0}

U^{\prime}_{1}

\dots

Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}

U_{1}

U_{2}

\dots

For a proof of the following result see, e.g., Theorem 8.33 in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
X
be a zero–dimensional Polish space. If
A\subseteq X
has the Baire property, then
\mathbf{BM}(X,A)
is determined and Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
wins iff
A
is comeager.

If
X
is the space of
\omega
-words or infinite trees over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, the above game can be simplified as in Definitions
2.2
and
2.2
below.

{defi}

Fix a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{\omega}
of
\omega
-words over
\Sigma
. The
Banach–Mazur game
on
L
(denoted
\mathbf{BM}(L)
) is the following game with configurations of the form
(s,r)
where
s\in\Sigma^{\ast}
is a finite word and
r\in\{\boldsymbol{\exists},\boldsymbol{\forall}\}
. The initial configuration
(s_{0},r_{0})
is
(\epsilon,\boldsymbol{\forall})
. Consider a round
n=0,1,\ldots
that starts in a configuration
(s_{n},r_{n})
. In this round, the player
r_{n}
chooses a finite word
s_{n+1}
that contains
s_{n}
as a strict prefix and the round ends in the configuration
(s_{n+1},\bar{r_{n}})
where
\bar{r_{n}}
is the opponent of
r_{n}
.

An infinite play of this game is winning for
\boldsymbol{\exists}
if
\alpha\in L
, where
\alpha=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}s_{n}
is the unique
\omega
-word that has all
s_{n}
as prefixes.

Now we move to the tree variant of this game. A
tree-prefix
is a partial function
s\colon\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}\rightharpoonup\Sigma
where the domain
\mathrm{dom}(s)
is finite, prefix-closed, and each node of
\mathrm{dom}(s)
has either zero (a leaf) or two (an internal node) children in
\mathrm{dom}(s)
. We say that
s^{\prime}

extends

s
if
s^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
,
s\subseteq s^{\prime}
, and each leaf of
s
is an internal node of
s^{\prime}
.

L\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}

\Sigma
. The
Banach–Mazur game
on
L
(denoted
\mathbf{BM}(L)

(s,r)
where
s
is a tree-prefix and
r\in\{\boldsymbol{\exists},\boldsymbol{\forall}\}
. The initial configuration
(s_{0},r_{0})
is
(\emptyset,\boldsymbol{\forall})

n=0,1,\ldots
that starts in a configuration
(s_{n},r_{n})
. In this round, the player
r_{n}
chooses a tree-prefix
s_{n+1}
that extends
s_{n}
and the round ends in the configuration
(s_{n+1},\bar{r_{n}})
where
\bar{r_{n}}
is the opponent of
r_{n}
. An infinite play of this game is winning for Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
if
t\in L
, where
t=\bigcup_{n\in\omega}s_{n}
.

A language

of
\omega
-words or trees over

has a winning strategy in the Banach–Mazur game on

2.3.
Probability Measures

We summarize below the basic concepts related to Borel measures. For more details see, e.g,
[
16
, Chapter 17]
.

In what follows, let
X
be a zero–dimensional Polish space. The smallest
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
X
containing all open sets is denoted by
\mathcal{B}
and its elements are called
Borel sets
. A
Borel probability measure
on
X
is a function
\mu\colon\mathcal{B}\to[0,1]
such that:
\mu(\emptyset)=0
,
\mu(X)=1
and, if
\{B_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
is a sequence of disjoint Borel sets,
\mu\big(\bigcup_{n}B_{n}\big)=\sum_{n}\mu(B_{n})
.

A subset
A\subseteq X
is a
\mu
-null set
if
A\subseteq B
for some Borel set
B\subseteq X
such that
\mu(B)=0
.

A subset
A\subseteq X
is a
F_{\sigma}
set
if it can be expressed as a countable union of closed sets. Every Borel measure
\mu
on a
X
is
regular
: for every Borel set
B
there exists an
F_{\sigma}
set
A\subseteq B
such that
\mu(A)=\mu(B)
.

Lebesgue measure on
\omega
-words.

For a fixed finite non-empty set
\Sigma=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
, we will be exclusively interested in one specific Borel measure
\mu_{\omega}
on the space
\Sigma^{\omega}
which we refer to as
Lebesgue measure
(on
\omega
-words). This is the unique Borel measure satisfying the equality
\mu_{w}(B_{n=a})=\frac{1}{k}
where
B_{n=a}=\{(a_{i})_{i\in\omega}\mid a_{n}=a\}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a\in\Sigma
. Intuitively, the Lebesgue measure on
\omega
-words generates an infinite sequence
(a_{0},a_{1},\dots)
by labelling the
n
-th entry with some letter
a\in\Sigma
chosen randomly and uniformly. This is done independently for each position
n\in\omega
.

Lebesgue measure on trees.

Similarly, for a finite non-empty set
\Sigma=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}
, the
Lebesgue measure
on trees
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
is the unique Borel measure, denoted by
\mu_{t}
, satisfying
\mu_{t}(B_{v=a})=\frac{1}{k}
where
B_{v=a}=\{t\in\Sigma^{\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}}\mid t(v)=a\}
, for
v\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
and
a\in\Sigma
. Once again, the Lebesgue measure on trees generates an infinite tree over
\Sigma
by labelling each vertex
v
with a randomly chosen letter
a\in\Sigma
. This is done independently for each vertex
v\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
.

2.5.
Alternating automata

Given a finite set
X
, we denote with
\mathcal{DL}(X)
the set of expressions
e
generated by the grammar

e::=x\mid e\wedge e\mid e\vee e\quad\text{for $x\in X$}

An
alternating tree automaton
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=\langle\Sigma,Q,q_{\mathrm{I}},\delta,\mathcal{F})
where
Q
is a finite
set of states
,
q_{\mathrm{I}}\in Q
is the
initial state
,
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to\mathcal{DL}(\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}\times Q)
is the
alternating transition function
,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(Q)
is a set of subsets of
Q
called the
Muller condition
.
The Muller condition
\mathcal{F}
is called a
parity condition
if there exists a
parity assignment

\pi\colon Q\to\omega
such that:

\mathcal{F}=\big\{F\subseteq Q\mid(\max_{q\in F}\pi(q))\textnormal{ is even}\}
.

An alternating automaton
\mathcal{A}
over an alphabet
\Sigma
defines, or “accepts”, a set of
\Sigma
-trees. The
acceptance
of a tree
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
is defined via a two-player (
\boldsymbol{\exists}
and
\boldsymbol{\forall}
) game of infinite duration denoted as
\mathcal{A}(t)
. The configurations of the game
\mathcal{A}(t)
are of the form
\langle u,q\rangle
or
\langle u,e\rangle
with
u\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
,
q\in Q
, and
e\in\mathcal{DL}(\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}\times Q)
(we can implicitly restrict to the set of sub-formulae of the formulae appearing in the alternating transition function
\delta
).

The game
\mathcal{A}(t)
starts in the configuration
\langle\epsilon,q_{\mathrm{I}}\rangle
. Game configurations of the form
\langle u,q\rangle
, including the initial state, have only one successor configuration, to which the game progresses automatically. The successor state is
\langle u,e\rangle
with
e=\delta(q,a)
, where
a=t(u)
is the labelling of the vertex
u
given by
t
. The dynamics of the game at configurations
\langle u,e\rangle
depends on the possible shapes of
e
.
If
e=e_{1}\vee e_{2}
, then Player
\boldsymbol{\exists}
moves either to
\langle u,e_{1}\rangle
or
\langle u,e_{2}\rangle
. If
e=e_{1}\wedge e_{2}
, then Player
\boldsymbol{\forall}
moves either to
\langle u,e_{1}\rangle
or
\langle u,e_{2}\rangle
. If
e=(\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q)
then the game progresses automatically to the state
\langle u\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q\rangle
. Lastly, if
e=(\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q)
the game progresses automatically to the state
\langle u\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q\rangle
.
Thus a play in the game
\mathcal{A}(t)
is a sequence
\Pi
of configurations, that can look like

\Pi=(\langle\epsilon,q_{\mathrm{I}}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q_{2}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{3}\rangle,\dots,\langle\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{4}\rangle,\dots),

where the dots represent part of the play in configurations of the form
\langle u,e\rangle
. Let
\mathrm{inf}(\Pi)
be the set of automata states
q\in Q
occurring infinitely often in the configurations of the form
\langle u,q\rangle
of
\Pi
. We then say that the play
\Pi
of
\mathcal{A}(t)
is winning for
\boldsymbol{\exists}
, if
\mathrm{inf}(\Pi)\in\mathcal{F}
. The play
\Pi
is winning for
\boldsymbol{\forall}
otherwise. The
language
of
\Sigma
-trees defined by
{\mathcal{A}}
denoted
\mathrm{L}({\mathcal{A}})
is the collection
\{t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}\mid\text{$\boldsymbol{\exists}$ has a winning strategy in the game $\mathcal{A}(t)$}\}
.

An alternating automaton for
\omega
-words is defined analogously, except that
\delta\colon Q\times\Sigma\to\mathcal{DL}(Q)
. In that case the the first coordinate of configurations of the game
{\mathcal{A}}(\alpha)
is always a natural number, the game moves from a configuration
(n,q)
to the configuration
(n+1,e)
with
e=\delta(q,a)
, where
a=\alpha(n)
is the labelling of the position
n
given by
\alpha
. In that case
\mathrm{L}({\mathcal{A}})\subseteq\Sigma^{\omega}
.

Types of automata.

An alternating automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
is
non-deterministic
if all the transitions of
{\mathcal{A}}
are of the form

\big((\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{1,\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}}){\land}(\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q_{1,\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}})\big)\ \lor\ \big((\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{2,\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}}){\land}(\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q_{2,\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}})\big)\ \lor\ \ldots\ \lor\ \big((\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{k,\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}}){\land}(\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q_{k,\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}})\big).

An alternating automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
is a
game automaton
if all the transitions of
{\mathcal{A}}
are of the form
(\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},q_{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L}})\mathbin{\chi}(\mathtt{\scriptstyle R},q_{\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}})
with
{\mathbin{\chi}}={\lor}
or
{\land}
.
An alternating automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
is
deterministic
if it is a game automaton with
{\mathbin{\chi}}={\land}
in all transitions.

3.
Syntax and Semantics of Monadic Second Order Logic

In this section we define the syntax and the semantics of the Monadic Second-order logic interpreted over the linear order of natural numbers (denoted
S1S
) and over the full binary tree (denoted
S2S
). This material is entirely standard. A detailed exposition can be found in
[
26
]
.

3.1.
Logic
S1S
and
\omega
-words.

The syntax of
S1S
formulas is given by the grammar

\phi\,::=\,x<y\mid x\in X\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\forall x.\,\phi\mid\forall X.\,\phi

where
x,y
and
X,Y
range over countable sets of
first-order variables
and
second-order variables
, respectively.

The semantics of
S1S
formulas on the structure
(\omega,{<})
is defined as expected by interpreting the formula
\forall X.\,\phi
as quantifying over all subsets
X\subseteq\omega
and the relation (
{\in}
) as the membership relation between elements
x\in\omega
and sets
X\subseteq\omega
.

For example the formula

\exists X.\,\forall x.\,\big((x\in X)\Rightarrow(\exists y.\,y\in X\wedge x<y)\big)

expresses the existence of an unbounded set
X
of natural numbers.

We write
\phi(\vec{x},\vec{X})
to specify that the first-order and second-order variables of
\phi
are
\vec{x}
and
\vec{X}
, respectively.

We can always assume that the free variables of a formula
\phi
are all second-order variables, i.e.,
\phi=\phi(\emptyset,\vec{X})
. Indeed if
\phi(\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\},\vec{Y})
is not of this form, we can consider the semantically equivalent formula
\psi(\emptyset,\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\}\cup\vec{Y})
, having second-order
\vec{X}
variables (constrained to be singletons) simulating first-order ones.

Each set
X\subseteq\omega
can be identified as the corresponding
\omega
-word over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
. Hence a
S1S
formula
\phi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
defines a collection of
n
-tuples of
\omega
-words over
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
or, equivalently, a collection of
\omega
-words over the alphabet
\Gamma=\{0,1\}^{n}
.
The
language defined by
\phi
, denoted by
\mathrm{L}(\phi)\subseteq\Gamma^{\omega}
is the set of
\omega
-words over
\Gamma
satisfying
\phi
.

The following theorem and proposition are well known.

Theorem 5
.

The theory of
S1S
is decidable. The expressive power of
S1S
is effectively equivalent with alternating / non-deterministic / deterministic Muller automata.

Proposition 6
.

For every
S1S
formula
\phi
, the language
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
is a Borel subset of
\Gamma^{\omega}
.

Hence for every
S1S
formula
\phi
, the language
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
has the Baire property and is Lebesgue measurable.

3.2.
Logic
S2S
and infinite trees

We now introduce, following a similar presentation, the syntax and the semantics of the MSO logic on the full binary tree (
S2S
).

The
full binary tree
is the structure
(\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast},\textnormal{succ}_{L},\textnormal{succ}_{R})
where
\textnormal{succ}_{L}
and
\textnormal{succ}_{R}
are binary relations on
\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
relating a vertex with its left and right child, respectively:

\textnormal{succ}_{L}=\{(w,w\mathtt{\scriptstyle L})\mid w\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}\}\qquad\textnormal{succ}_{R}=\{(w,w\mathtt{\scriptstyle R})\mid w\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}\}

The syntax of
S2S
formulas is given by the grammar

\phi\,::=\,\textnormal{succ}_{L}(x,y)\mid\textnormal{succ}_{R}(x,y)\mid x\in X\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\forall x.\,\phi\mid\forall X.\,\phi

where
x,y
and
X,Y
range over countable sets of
first-order variables
and
second-order variables
, respectively.

The semantics of
S2S
formulae on the full binary tree is defined by interpreting the formula
\forall X.\,\phi
as quantifying over all subsets
X\subseteq\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
and
x\in X
as the membership relations between vertices
x\in\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
and sets
X\subseteq\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
.

For example the formula

\exists X.\,\forall x.\,\big((x\in X)\Rightarrow(\exists y.\,y\in X\wedge\textnormal{succ}_{L}(x,y))\big)

expresses the existence of a set of vertices which is closed under taking left children.

As for
S1S
, we can assume without loss of generality that the free variables of a
S2S
formula
\phi
are all second-order.

Each set
X\subseteq\{\mathtt{\scriptstyle L},\mathtt{\scriptstyle R}\}^{\ast}
can be identified as a tree
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Sigma}
over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
. Hence a
S2S
formula
\phi(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
defines a collection of
n
-tuples of trees in
{\mathcal{T}}_{\{0,1\}}
or, equivalently, a collection of trees in
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Gamma}
over the alphabet
\Gamma=\{0,1\}^{n}
. The set of
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Gamma}
satisfying the formula
\phi
is called the
language defined by
\phi
and is denoted by
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
.

The following theorem and proposition are well known.

Theorem 7
.

The theory of
S2S
is decidable. The expressive power of
MSO
over infinite trees is effectively equivalent with alternating / non-deterministic Muller automata. The expressive power of deterministic automata is strictly weaker.

Proposition 8
.

For every
S2S
formula
\phi
, the language
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
is a
\mathbf{\Delta}^{1}_{2}
subset of
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Gamma}
.

It has recently been shown that
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
is always Lebesgue measurable and has the Baire property
[
12
]
.

Proposition 9
.

For every
S2S
formula
\phi
the language
\mathrm{L}(\phi)
has the Baire property and is Lebesgue measurable.

4.
S1S extended with the category quantifier

In this section we define the syntax and semantics of the logic
S1S
extended with the
category quantifier
(
\forall^{\ast}
). The syntax of
S1S+\forall^{\ast}
extends that of
S1S
with the new second-order quantifier
\forall^{\ast}
as follows:

\phi\ \ ::=\ \ x<y\mid x\in X\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\forall x.\,\phi\mid\forall X.\,\phi\mid\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi

The semantics of the category quantifier is specified as follows.

\mathrm{L}\big(\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi(X,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\big)=\Big\{w\in\Gamma^{\omega}\mid\text{$\{w^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\omega}\mid\phi(w^{\prime},w)\}$ is comeager}\Big\}

where
\Gamma=\{0,1\}^{n}
.

In other words, an
n
-tuple
w=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n})
, with
w_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{\omega}
, satisfies the formula
\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi(X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
if for a large (comeager) collection of
w^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{\omega}
, the extended tuple
(w^{\prime},w)
satisfies
\phi(X,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
. Informally,
w
satisfies

6.
S2S extended with the category quantifier

In this section we consider the extension of the monadic second order logic of the full binary tree (
S2S
) with the category quantifier.

The syntax of
S2S+\forall^{\ast}
extends that of
S2S
with the new second-order quantifier
\forall^{\ast}
as follows:

\phi\,::=\,\textnormal{succ}_{L}(x,y)\mid\textnormal{succ}_{R}(x,y)\mid x\in X\mid\neg\phi\mid\phi_{1}\vee\phi_{2}\mid\forall x.\,\phi\mid\forall X.\,\phi\mid\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi

The semantics of the category quantifier is specified as follows.

\mathrm{L}\Big(\forall^{\ast}X.\,\phi(X,Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})\Big)=\Big\{t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Gamma}\mid\textnormal{ the set }\{t^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\{0,1\}}\mid\phi(t^{\prime},t)\textnormal{ holds}\}\big)\textnormal{ is comeager}\Big\}

where
\Gamma=\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Our main theorem is the following result, showing that the category quantifier preserves
S2S
-definability when the inner formula can be recognised by a game automaton.

10.
Open Problems

In this concluding section we present a list of problems left open from this work. This is by no means exhaustive and simply reflects the authors’ view on what might be the most important, difficult and far-reaching questions encountered in the process of working on these topics.

Problem 1.
Is the theory of
S2S+\forall^{\ast}
decidable?

The stronger statement
S2S=S2S+\forall^{\ast}
was formulated as a theorem in
[
18
]
but, as discussed in the Introduction and Section
6
), the proof contained a mistake. The proof is correct only for languages defined by game automata.

Problem 2.
It is well known that
S2S
definable sets always belong to the
\mathbf{\Delta}^{1}_{2}
class of the projective hierarchy. Is this also true for
S2S+\forall^{\ast}
definable sets?

Problem 3.
In terms of reverse mathematics as shown in
[
17
]
, decidability of the theory
S2S
is equivalent to determinacy of all Gale–Stewart games with the winning condition being a Boolean combination of
F_{\sigma}
sets (see
[
17
]
for a precise formulation). Is this characterization also valid for
S2S+\forall^{\ast}
?

Problem 4.
Is the theory
S2S+\forall^{{=}1}_{\pi}
decidable?

Problem 5.
