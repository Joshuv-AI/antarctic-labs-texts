You Wouldn’t Permutahedron
Astra Kolomatskaia

Abstract
We develop formulas that define permutahedral commutation coherence relations of all orders. To illustrate the result geometrically, we begin by defining a rigid transformation of the ( n + 1 ) ({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}) -permutahedron into a n {\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n} -cube of dimensions 1 × 2 × ⋯ × n {\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}\times\cdots\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n} . With a fictitious assumption, we then ‘define’ the corresponding coherence relations ‘up to associativity’ as an instance of a semi-simplicial type in the language of Displayed Type Theory . This is not a formal result in type theory, but we expect this to translate into one as soon as the problem of defining associahedral coherences is solved in a type theory with semi-simplicial types. On the other hand, this not-strictly-well-typed definition may be used to produce well-typed formulas in a restricted setting.

Prelude

The permutahedral coherence relations share a close connection with the problem of constructing semi-simplicial types (SSTs), and we presently motivate them as such. A semi-simplicial type is intuitively a semi-simplicial set valued in homotopy types, and there are two non-rigorous notions specifying what this should mean.

The facets of a
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1})
-permutahedron are described by the ordered partitions of the first
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}
letters into non-empty groups that are internally unordered.
[
Pon23
]
For example, two facets of the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}5}
-permutahedron are given by
[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}]
and
[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}]
; note that within each group the letters are alphabetically ordered. We can describe an algorithm, similar to the above, that extracts from each facet a region of the cube
[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}]\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}]\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}]\times\cdots\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]

We mark, with double square brackets, the
‘active’
group to which the latest letter was added. We think of a group as an uncertain ordering of its constituent letters. Thus, for example, given
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}
, we extract not an index, but rather a range
\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\right)
for possible positions of the latest letter
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}
. In general, this range is not discrete, as we think of the act of permuting letters as a kind of continuous deformation. In order for the regions to be disjoint, we take the range to be open, which corresponds to thinking of
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}
as consisting of strictly intermediate stages of the deformation. With this in mind, the two examples above would be sent to the regions
\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}\right\}\times\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}\right\}\times\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\right\}\times\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\right)\times\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}3},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}4}\right)
and
\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}\right\}\times\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\right)\times\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}\right\}\times\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\right)\times\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}4}\right)
, respectively.

In what follows, we modify this algorithm slightly; since this process always begins with
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\mathord{\hskip 1.0pt\text{--}\hskip 1.0pt}\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}\mapsto\left\{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}\right\}
, for the last letter
\left(\mathord{\hskip 1.0pt\text{--}\hskip 1.0pt}\right)
, we drop this singleton range and omit the
\left([{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}]\times\mathord{\hskip 1.0pt\text{--}\hskip 1.0pt}\right)
from the cube. We thus consider the resulting regions to lie in the cube
[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}]\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}]\times\cdots\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]
, which is the standard
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}
-cube of dimensions
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}\times\cdots\times{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}
. Note that the top dimensional facet consisting of a single group is always sent to the whole interior of the cube.

Theorem
: Every point
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}:[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}]\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}]\times\cdots\times[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}_{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}=\left(\right)
. The point
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}_{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0}
is said to lie in the unique facet
[\mathord{\hskip 1.0pt\text{--}\hskip 1.0pt}]
of the

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}

-pemutahedron. Note that, if
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}

of the
\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}\right)
-pemutahedron, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}G}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}k}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1}}={\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}G}
that extend
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}
by the first letter.

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}p}

Interlude

From the above, we know how to rigidly transform the
({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}1})
-permutahedron into an
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}
-cube. In this interlude, we discuss the forms that our equations corresponding to these cubes will take.

Note that a decomposition of the boundary of a cube does not automatically give rise to conveniently expressible equations. For example, in Figure
1
, we saw that the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}\times{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}3}
faces looked like the following diagram on the left, and we can write a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}2}
-categorical composition of these rectangles by first composing each column vertically, and then composing the results horizontally. On the other hand, such a convenient composition is not possible to express in the following
tatami mat
diagram to the right.

We shall see that the former case is representative, and that the general coherence equations can be expressed as cube filling problems in which the faces are expressed as iterated compositions along successive axes.

Note that along the middle vertical axis in the rectangle, the composite of the lines on the left would be something like
\left([{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\right)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]
whereas on the right, it would be something like
[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}\left([{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}C}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}]\,[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}]\right)
. Unfortunately, in many settings, path composition is only be weakly associative, and putative formulas would have to account for reassociation.
We would like to avoid this.
For this reason, we work in a particularly nice setting for dealing with (higher) homotopies. This will be the setting of
cubical Moore paths
.

Let
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}
be a space,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}
, and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}:{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\mathbb{N}}
, then we define
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}
to be the subspace of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}^{[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]}
consisting of such
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}^{[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]}
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0})\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n})\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}
. There is an evident composition operation
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}c}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}c}
obtained by concatenation; note that this operation is definitionally associative.

Now suppose that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}:[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}]\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Top}}

continuously, and that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n})

\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}PathP}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}
to be the subspace of dependent functions
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}:({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}:[{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}])\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}0})\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n})\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}
. If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}
are composable lines of spaces, then there is a dependent composition operation
\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}PathP}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}c}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}PathP}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}m}}~({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}0}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}Y}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}1})~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}c}
, which is likewise definitionally associative.

If
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}
is a composition operation, on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}

and a path

, we have that
{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\text{.}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\right):\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Path}}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}b})~({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}c})
. This is a whiskering operation that we can define in terms of our primitive operations. Note that whiskering distributes over composition definitionally, such that:

{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\text{.}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~\left(\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\beta}}\right)~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\right)\equiv\left({\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\text{.}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\alpha}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\right)\right)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vbox{\hbox{$\displaystyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\centerdot$}}}{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\centerdot$}}}}\left({{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\text{.}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\beta}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}i}\right)}\right)

Appendix: Calculating Simplex Types

In this appendix, we present a simplified version of
Displayed Type Theory (dTT)
, where, in particular, we omit the modal aspects of the type theory and use Russell style universes. We are concerned not only with the theory of display and semi-simplicial types, but also with the practice of implementing a rather narrow
Racket
program for calculating the
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}n}
-simplex types of
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Perm}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}X}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mu}
.

Introduction

Consider the standard proof for the normalisation of Simply Typed Lambda Calculus in terms of computability predicates. For every type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
of the theory, we define a predicate on the terms
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
, denoted
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
. At the base type, we have that
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Base}}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
holds just in case
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
normalises. At arrow types, this predicate is defined recursively; we say that
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f}
holds just in case
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f}
normalises, and if for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
such that
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}
holds, we have that
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}\right)
holds. The proof then proceeds by structural induction on the syntax of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
to demonstrate that we always have that
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\langle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}~\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}\kern-1.94444pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{\rangle}}}$}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
holds.

The phenomenon in this proof is known as
unary parametricity
. In the above, we had that the predicates were metatheoretical as the statement
‘
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
normalises’
is not an internal construction to type theory. However, we may also consider theories that
internalise
the parametricity operation. In the case of dTT, this internalisation takes the following form – for every
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
, then we get
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
. That is, every type
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
defines a predicate
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}
on its terms, and every term
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
satisfies its corresponding predicate, such that we have a proof
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
. The operation
d
is known as
display
. In the case of arrow types, we have that
\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}\right){{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f}\equiv\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\right)\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}B}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}f}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}\right)
, which mirrors the recursive definition of the computability predicate above.

Meta-Abstraction

Displayed Type Theory differed from all other previously existing internal parametricity theories in that its parametricity was only
partially
internal. In particular, display is an example of a new notion called an
indexed modality
. In general, a modality is a non-binding operation that changes the context. For example, in the case the formulation of dependent modal logic using locks, if we want to form the type
{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\square}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
in context
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}
, that is, if we want to make the judgement
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\square}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
, then we must have
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma},~\text{\faIconFromMacro{faLock}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\square}}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
. Morally, the lock is a restriction on variable use rules in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}
, and
to first approximation
the lock modifies
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}
by getting rid of certain variables.

Display is a much more insidious kind of modality. If we have
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
. Here
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}
is a context of double the length of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}
in which every variable
\big({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\big)
is replaced with
\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A},~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}{{}^{\prime}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}a}\right)
, that is, the newly introduced variables are the parametricity witnesses of the existing variables. Thus, instead of
deleting
variables from the context, as with the
{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\square}}
modality, display
introduces
new variables without giving them a binding, which is very problematic. dTT deals with this issue by introducing modal guards, a theory which occupies many pages of the dTT paper.

From the perspective of a programmer, however, a much simpler version of the theory just states that display may only be applied to closed terms. Hence:

\frac{\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}}\frac{\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}}

In fact, we have
\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}\equiv{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}.~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}
, so the first rule is a special case of the second.

However, from the perspective of the implementer of the programming language, we would like display to compute by structural recursion on the term, which would involve going under binders. Thus, although the user is not allowed to call display on open terms, the language implementation must.

In this appendix, we use Russel style universes and pun terms and types, which differs from usual theoretical practice. We will thus write the judgement
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}\;\mathsf{term}
to say that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
is a term of some type (possibly of the universe). We then represent the notion of an open term with a
meta-abstraction
:

\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}\;\mathsf{term}}{\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}\right.}\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}}{\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}:\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}}

Meta-abstractions themselves are not first-class terms of the programming language, and must be fully applied to a list of terms to become one:

\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\delta}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}}\right.\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}\;\mathsf{term}}\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\delta}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}}\right.\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\vdash{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}}

We may then identify closed terms
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}
with their meta-abstractions
\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}t}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,[\,]:\left(\right)}
.

\triangleleft

Display on Meta-Abstractions

Two fundamental ingredients that we will need are
décalage
and the
evens projection
:

\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\;\mathsf{ctx}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}\;\mathsf{ctx}}\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\sigma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}ev}}}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}\Rightarrow{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Delta}}

Display on meta-abstractions then takes the following form:

\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}\right.}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}}\right.}

The first rule that we will give for computing display is as follows:

\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}\equiv\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\lambda}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}:\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}\right)\text{.}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}A}\to\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}Type}}\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}}

\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}\right.\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}\left.\;\mathsf{term}\middle/_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}}\right.\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}:\mathopen{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{[}}}$}}\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathcal{A}}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}d}}}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}~\left({\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathfrak{t}}~{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}ev}}}}\right)\,\mathclose{\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}\kern-1.59721pt\hbox{${{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}\boldsymbol{]}}}$}}_{\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\gamma}^{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}+}\,:\,{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\Gamma}{{}^{\mathsf{{\color[rgb]{0,0.4,0}D}}}}}}
