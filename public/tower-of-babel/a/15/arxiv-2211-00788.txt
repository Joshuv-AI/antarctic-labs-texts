Quantum K K -invariants and Gopakumar–Vafa invariants I. The quintic threefold
Y.-C. Chou, Y.-P. Lee

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture of Jockers–Mayr and Garoufalidis–Scheidegger, relating genus zero quantum K K -invariants and Gopakumar–Vafa invariants on the quintic threefold.



0.
Introduction

0.1.
Relating enumerative invariants on quintic threefold

On the Calabi–Yau threefolds (CY3), there are two sets of
integral
enumerative invariants.
The first, called the
Gopakumar–Vafa invariants

[
GV98a
,
GV98b
]
, was introduced
in theoretical physics as “new topological invariants on
Calabi–Yau threefolds
”, counting the
“numbers of BPS states”. We refer the readers to
[
MT18
]
and references therein for various mathematical definitions of these invariants. There is a simple relation between the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants and the Gromov–Witten invariants (
1.1
) (
1.2
),

\begin{split}&\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}~\sum_{\beta\in H_{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})}{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{g,\beta}q^{\beta}\lambda^{2g-2}\\
=&\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}~\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}~\sum_{\beta\in H_{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})}{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{g,\beta}{\frac{1}{k}}\left(2\sin\left({\frac{k\lambda}{2}}\right)\right)^{2g-2}q^{k\beta},\end{split}

which all viable mathematical definitions must satisfy. In this paper, we will use the above (invertible) relation as the definition of the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants (in terms of Gromov–Witten invariants). The integrality of this ad hoc definition was proven by E. Ionel and T. Parker
[
IP18
]
.

The second is the
quantum
K
-invariants

[
Giv00
,
Lee04
]
, a
K
-theoretic variant of the (cohomological) Gromov–Witten invariants. Whereas Gromov–Witten theory produces
rational
enumerative invariants, quantum
K
-theory, counting (the alternating sum of) the rank of sheaf cohomology, produces
integral
invariants by definition.
The close relationship between the quantum
K
-theory and quantum cohomology was understood since the early phase of quantum
K
-theory. In fact, many
K
-theoretic results were inspired by their cohomological counterparts. See, e.g.,
[
GL03
,
Lee04
]
. In
[
GT14
]
and subsequent works, A. Givental and his collaborators completely characterized genus zero quantum
K
-invariants in terms of genus zero Gromov–Witten invariants.

Therefore, there is a relation between quantum
K
-invariants and Gopakumar–Vafa invariants for Calabi–Yau threefolds via Gromov–Witten invariants. In
[
JM19
]
and
[
GS22
, Conjecture 1.1]
,
H. Jockers, P. Mayr and S. Garoufalidis, E. Scheidegger proposed a simple
linear
relation between these two sets of integral invariants for
quintic threefold
at genus zero. They also provided numerical evidence for this conjecture.
The main purpose of this article is to prove this conjecture as stated in
[
GS22
, Conjecture 1.1]
, cf. Conjecture
1.1
in Section
1
. According to
[
GS22
, Section 3.3]
, this also proves the version proposed by Jockers and Mayr.

We plan to generalize these results to other Calabi–Yau threefolds (Part II) and to higher genus (Part III) in future works.
In particular, some heuristic relations to the “multiple cover formula” will be discussed in Part II, where the proof of genus
0
result for all Calabi–Yau threefolds will use different arguments.

0.2.
Contents of the paper

Conjecture
1.1
is phrased in terms of an explicit expression for the
small
J^{K}
-function
in quantum
K
-theory, which is a generating function of
K
-theoretic Gromov-Witten invariants, or quantum
K
-invariants. For a smooth projective variety
X
, the quantum
K
-invariants are defined as

\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(\Phi_{1})\dots\tau_{d_{n}}(\Phi_{n})\rangle^{X,{K}}_{g,n,\beta}:=\chi\Big(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta);\Big(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}{\rm ev}_{i}^{*}(\Phi_{i})L_{i}^{d_{i}}\Big)\otimes\mathcal{O}^{{\rm vir}}\Big)\in\mathbb{Z}.

It gives a deformation of the ordinary
K
-ring
K^{0}(X)
of
X
, analogous to the relation between quantum cohomology and ordinary cohomology. We consider the generating function, the
big
J^{K}
-function
, which determines the
genus
0
quantum
K
-theory of
X
:

\begin{split}t\rightarrow&J^{{K}}(t,q,Q)\\
&:=(1-q)+t(q)+\sum_{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}\sum_{n,d}\frac{Q^{d}}{n!}\langle\frac{\Phi^{\alpha}}{1-qL},t(L),\dots,t(L)\rangle^{X,{K}}_{0,n+1,d}.\end{split}

The
small

J
-function is a specialization of the big
J
-function to
t=0
. Jockers–Mayr and Garoufalidis–Scheidegger formulated an explicit expression of the small
J^{K}
-function in terms of the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants.
We recall the basic definitions and formulations of quantum
K
-theory and Gopakumar–Vafa invariants in Section
1
. The Conjecture
1.1
is formulated in Section
1.4
.

In Section
2
, we recall necessary formulations and results from the works of A. Givental and his collaborators. There are generally two approaches to compute the genus zero quantum
K
-theory for the quintic threefold.
In
[
GT14
]
, Givental and Tonita prove that genus 0
K
-theoretic GW-invariants of
X
can be expressed in terms of its cohomological ones. Their framework can be understood as the
adelic characterization
of quantum
K
-theory. This is done by applying a virtual version of Kawasaki’s Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch formula, expressing Euler characteristics of vector bundles over a Deligne–Mumford
stack
(moduli of stable maps in our case) in terms of integrations over the components of its inertia stack, called Kawasaki strata.
By analyzing the combinatorial structure of inertia stacks of moduli of stable map, they derive a recursive relation relating invariants in different strata and then give the characterization of genus
0

K
-theoretic GW-invariants in terms of the cohomological ones. See Section
2.3
for details.

Another approach is called the
explicit reconstruction theorem
, which in principal gives an algorithm to compute all quantum
K
-invariants from an initial condition. There are various versions of the reconstruction theorem. A particular case of sufficient initial condition for the quintic threefold is given by Givental’s
I^{K}
-function. See
[
LP04
]
,
[
Giv15b
]
and
[
Ton18
]
.

In principal, either the
virtual orbifold Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch
or the
reconstruction theorem
gives an explicit algorithm to compute the
J^{K}
-function. In particular, a computation up to degree
7
is given in
[
GS22
]
using the explicit reconstruction alone.
However, the complexity for explicit computation grows very fast as the degrees increase. In Section
3
, we combine both methods and utilize the
analytic properties
of the generating functions to give a proof of Conjecture
1.1
.

To streamline the proof, some computations needed to establish the proof are left to Section
4
. In the appendix, an alternative proof of Theorem
3.4
is given via the explicit reconstruction. This is to show that one can in principle prove a theorem using either reconstruction theorem or Riemann–Roch, when the complexity of the former is manageable.

1.
GW, GV, QK and the JMGS Conjecture

1.1.
Gromov–Witten invariants

Let
X
be a smooth complex projective variety, and
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)
be the M. Kontsevich’s moduli space of
n
-pointed, genus
g
, degree
\beta
stable maps.
Given
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, there is an evaluation map

\begin{split}{\rm ev}_{i}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)&\rightarrow X\\
[f:(C;x_{1},\dots,x_{n})]&\mapsto f(x_{i}),\end{split}

and a line bundle
L_{i}:=x_{i}^{*}w_{\mathcal{C}/\overline{\mathcal{M}}}
on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)
, where
w_{\mathcal{C}/\overline{\mathcal{M}}}
is the relative dualizing sheaf of the universal curve
\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)
and
x_{i}:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is the
i
-th mark point.

(Cohomological) Gromov-Witten invariants of
X
are defined to be

\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(\phi_{1})\dots\tau_{d_{n}}(\phi_{n})\rangle^{X,{H}}_{g,n,\beta}:=\pi^{H}_{*}\left(\cup_{i=1}^{n}{\rm ev}_{i}^{*}(\phi_{i})c_{1}(L_{i})^{d_{i}}\cap[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{{\rm vir}}\right)\in\mathbb{Q},

where

\pi:\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)\to pt:=\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{C})

is the structural map and
\pi^{H}_{*}
is the (cohomological) pushforward to the point.
Here
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\in H(X)
,
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and
[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{{\rm vir}}
are the (cohomological) virtual fundamental classes.

In this paper, we will be concerned with only genus zero invariants, which can be encoded into a formal power series, called genus-0 descendant potential of
X
:

F^{{H}}_{0}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\beta}\frac{Q^{\beta}}{n!}\langle t(L),\dots,t(L)\rangle^{X,{H}}_{0,n,\beta}.

Here the sum is over all curve class
\beta\in H_{2}(X)_{\geq 0}
and
Q^{\beta}
are formal variables, called the Novikov variables, which keep track of the curve classes.
t(q)
stands for any polynomial of one variable with coefficients in
H(X)
.
That is
\displaystyle t(q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}t_{k}^{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}q^{k}
with
\{\phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N}
a basis in
H(X)
.

1.2.
Gopakumar–Vafa invariants

In theoretical physics, R. Gopakumar and C. Vafa in
[
GV98a
,
GV98b
]
introduced new topological invariants on
Calabi–Yau threefolds
(CY3)
X
, called
Gopakumar–Vafa invariants
.
These invariants represent the counts of “numbers of BPS states” on
X
. Unlike the Gromov–Witten invariants, which are defined for any symplectic manifolds or orbifolds, Gopakumar–Vafa invariants only make sense for Calabi–Yau threefolds (or variants).

The virtual dimensions for moduli spaces (of stable maps) to CY3 are always equal to the number of marked points. That is,

\operatorname{vdim}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)=n.

By general properties of the moduli spaces, more precisely the string equation, the divisor equation and dilaton equation, all Gromov–Witten invariants can be easily reconstructed from

{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{g,\beta}:=\pi^{H}_{*}\left([\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,0}(X,\beta)]^{{\rm vir}}\right)=\int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,0}(X,\beta)]^{{\rm vir}}}1.

That is, all
n
-pointed invariants with descendants can be recovered from
0
-pointed counting.
In fact, a
closed formula
was obtained in
[
FL19
]
in terms of generating functions. We will therefore focus on
{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{g,\beta}
.

There have been various attempts at defining Gopakumar–Vafa invariants mathematically. We refer the readers to
[
MT18
]
and references therein. Currently, the Gopakumar–Vafa invariants are defined for
0
-pointed curves only, without insertions. Generalizations to
n
-pointed invariants with insertion are expected to be compatible with the dilaton and divisor equations. In that case, the counting of the BPS states is again reduced to similarly defined
{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{g,\beta}
.

A remarkable relation stated in
[
GV98a
,
GV98b
]
between GV and GW can be expressed in terms of generating functions:

(1.1)

\begin{split}&\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}~\sum_{\beta\in H_{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})}{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{g,\beta}q^{\beta}\lambda^{2g-2}\\
=&\sum_{g=0}^{\infty}~\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}~\sum_{\beta\in H_{2}(M,\mathbb{Z})}{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{g,\beta}{\frac{1}{k}}\left(2\sin\left({\frac{k\lambda}{2}}\right)\right)^{2g-2}q^{k\beta},\end{split}

In this paper, we are mainly concerned with genus zero invariants on
X

(1.2)

\begin{split}{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{g=0,\beta=d[\operatorname{line}]}=:{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{d}&=\sum_{e|d}\frac{1}{e^{3}}{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{d/e},\\
{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{g=0,\beta=d[\operatorname{line}]}=:{{\mathrm{GV}}}_{d}&:=\sum_{e|d}\frac{\mu(e)}{e^{3}}{{\mathrm{GW}}}_{d/e},\end{split}

where we have used the Möbius inversion and

1.3.
Quantum
K
-invariants

The formulation of

K
-theory
is similar to that of Gromov–Witten theory.
The

quantum
K
-invariants

\langle\tau_{d_{1}}(\Phi_{1})\dots\tau_{d_{n}}(\Phi_{n})\rangle^{X,{K}}_{g,n,\beta}:=\chi\Big(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta);\Big(\otimes_{i=1}^{n}{\rm ev}_{i}^{*}(\Phi_{i})L_{i}^{d_{i}}\Big)\otimes\mathcal{O}^{{\rm vir}}\Big)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Here
\Phi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{n}\in K^{0}(X)
,
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\mathcal{O}^{{\rm vir}}
is the virtual structure sheaf on
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)

[

]

F^{{K}}_{0}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\beta}\frac{Q^{\beta}}{n!}\langle t(L),\dots,t(L)\rangle^{X,{K}}_{0,n,\beta}.

Here the sum is over all curve class
\beta\in H_{2}(X)_{\geq 0}
.
t(q)
stands for any Laurent polynomial of one variable
q
with coefficients in
K^{0}(X)

t(q)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}t_{k}^{\alpha}\Phi_{\alpha}q^{k}

with
\{\Phi_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{N}
a basis in
K^{0}(X)
. We may rewrite

as a more transparent form as:

F^{{K}}_{0}(t,Q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\beta}\frac{Q^{\beta}}{n!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}\\
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\{1,\dots,N\}\end{subarray}}t_{k_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots t_{k_{n}}^{\alpha_{n}}\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\Phi_{\alpha_{1}}),\dots,\tau_{k_{n}}(\Phi_{\alpha_{n}})\rangle^{X,{K}}_{0,n,\beta}.

K^{0}(X)
here stands for the topological
K

The symplectic loop space formalism

Let

Remark 2.1

Let

\mathcal{K}

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}&=\mathcal{K}_{+}\oplus\mathcal{K}_{-}\\
&:=K[q,q^{-1}]\,\oplus\,\{f(q)\in\mathcal{K}|f(0)\neq\infty,\ f(\infty)=0\}.\end{split}

The big

\begin{split}t\mapsto&J^{{K}}(t):=J^{{K}}(t,q,Q)\\

The definition of

e_{i}(k):=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{i}\leq k}\frac{1}{1-q^{j_{1}}}\cdots\frac{1}{1-q^{j_{i}}}.

For the third equality, the following equalities are used

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1-q^{r}-q^{r}(P-1)}&=\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}\sum_{i=0}^{3}\Big(\frac{q^{r}}{1-q^{r}}\Big)^{i}(P-1)^{i}\\
&=\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}\sum_{i=0}^{3}\Big(\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}-1\Big)^{i}(P-1)^{i}\\
&=\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}\frac{1}{P}\sum_{i=0}^{3}(-1)^{i}\Big(\frac{1}{(1-q^{r})}\Big)^{i}\Big(\frac{1-P}{P}\Big)^{i},\end{split}

\begin{split}1-q^{r}-q^{r}(P^{5}-1)&=(1-q^{r})\Big(1-\frac{q^{r}}{1-q^{r}}(P^{5}-1)\Big)\\
&=(1-q^{r})\Big(P^{5}-\frac{P^{5}-1}{1-q^{r}}\Big)\\
&=P^{5}(1-q^{r})\Big(1+\Big(\frac{1}{1-q^{r}}\Big)\Big(\frac{1-P^{5}}{P^{5}}\Big)\Big).\end{split}

Note that in order to completely expand in terms of the basis,
1/P
should have been rewritten as

\frac{1}{P}=\frac{1}{1-(1-P)}=1+(1-P)+(1-P)^{2}+(1-P)^{3}.

However, the precise form of expansion is not needed and (
3.1
) will suffice for our purpose below.

Fix a root of unity
\zeta
. Let
f\in\mathcal{K}=K^{0}(X)(q)[\![Q]\!]
. We will expand the
Q
-coefficients
f
as a Laurent series in
(1-\zeta q)
,
K^{0}(X)[(1-\zeta q)^{-1},(1-\zeta q)]\!]
, in terms of the basis
\{(1-P)^{i}\}_{i=0}^{3}
for
K^{0}(X)
.

Define
the
degree
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
as follows:

\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}((1-\zeta q)^{i}(1-P)^{j}):=i+j,\quad\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(0):=\infty.

For an inhomogeneous element, the degree is defined to be the lowest degree of the homogeneous parts.
We then have

(3.2)

\begin{split}\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(a\cdot b)&\geq\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(a)+\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(b)\\
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(a+b)&\geq\operatorname{min}\{\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(a),\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}(b)\}.\end{split}

Indeed, one might wish to consider
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
a
discrete “semi-valuation”
, and hence defines an adic topology, for these localized functions.
Expansion of
f\in\mathcal{K}
in the ascending order of
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
, i.e., the
K
-valued Laurent series in
(1-\zeta q)
, is denoted as
(f)_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
and is called the
localization of
f
at
q=\zeta^{-1}
. For
f,g\in\mathcal{K}
, write

f\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{n}g\quad\mbox{(resp.\ $f\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}g$)},

if
(f)_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
and
(g)_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
agree up to degree
\leq n
(resp. they agree to all degrees).

Remark 3.1
.

The semi-valuation above can be extended to the entire
\mathcal{K}
by extending the totally ordered group from
\mathbb{Z}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus 2}
and define the degree of an element
f
in
K^{0}(X)[(1-\zeta q)^{-1},(1-\zeta q)]\!][\![Q]\!]
as

{\deg_{\zeta^{-1}}}\left((1-\zeta q)^{i}(1-P)^{j}Q^{d}\right)=(d,i+j),

with the total ordering on
\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus 2}
being the lexicographic ordering. We will not use this but stick to the
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any roots of unity
\zeta
, the
Q
-coefficients of
[J^{{K}}]_{-}
are sums of terms with non-negative degrees with respect to
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}

Proof.

The reconstruction theorem (
2.2
) states that

\begin{split}&J^{{K}}(0)=\\
&\sum_{d\geq 0}I^{{K}}_{d}Q^{d}\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(q,Q)(1-Pq^{d})^{i}.\end{split}

The right hand side of the formula has three parts, and we will show that each part has
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\geq 0
.
In the first part, we claim that
I^{K}_{d}
have
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\geq 0
. This can be seen from the expansion of
I^{K}_{d}(q)
in (

) and the paragraphs following it. In particular, the factor

(1-q)^{-1}I^{K}_{0d}(q)=\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{5d}(1-q^{r})}{\prod_{r=1}^{d}(1-q^{r})^{5}}

is actually a polynomial in

and

has poles of order no greater than
i
at

.

On the other hand, the last part of formula,
\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(q,Q)(1-Pq^{d})^{i}
, consists of only the polynomial in
q
, as proven in Theorem
2.13
. Its

-coefficients therefore have
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\geq 0
.

In the middle
\exp

\epsilon_{0}(Q)=0
. Assuming the claim, the
\exp

\begin{split}&\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\\
=&\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)\\
=&\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-q^{kd}-q^{kd}(P^{k}-1))^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right).\end{split}

One see clearly that the exponent has
\deg_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\geq 0
. We now return to the proof of claim. By definition
\epsilon_{00}=0
, and assume that
\epsilon_{0M^{\prime}}=0
for all

is a polynomial of

.
Following the reconstruction theorem and in particular (

), we have

\epsilon_{0M}=-\sum_{d_{1}+d_{2}=M}I^{K}_{0d_{1}}(1)r_{0d_{2}}(1).

Note that the LHS is a constant in

while the RHS always contains a factor

. We have therefore,
e_{0M}=\mathrm{RHS}(q=1)=0

The theorem follows by the semi-valuation property (

∎

The small

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0)&=1+\frac{1-P}{5}\sum_{M\geq 1}Q^{M}\sum_{r\leq M}\sum_{\zeta^{r}=1}\Big(\frac{a_{M,\zeta}}{1-\zeta q}\Big)\\

where

is the sum over all primitive

-th roots of unities.

Proof.

By Remark

, the partial fraction expansion of

consists of the

We know that

have poles only at
r
-th roots of unity for

. This can be seen from the reconstruction theorem as follows.

As in the proof of Theorem

, we employ the reconstruction theorem (

In (

In the above expression, we observe that the terms involving

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

. If

are not integers, we have

Otherwise, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1-qL}&=\frac{1-(\zeta^{-1}L-1)+(\zeta^{-1}L-1)^{2}}{1-\zeta q}\\

Theorem 3.6
.

Proof.

\frac{1}{(1-q^{M})^{3}}&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\frac{1}{M^{3}(1-\zeta q)^{3}}+\frac{3(M-1)}{2M^{3}(1-\zeta q)^{2}}+\frac{2M^{2}-3M+1}{2M^{3}(1-\zeta q)}\\

where

Start with the coefficient of

\begin{split}&5\,{\rm Coeff}\Big(\frac{1}{1-q}J^{{K}}(0);(1-P)^{2}Q^{M}\Big)\\

&\qquad+\mbox{ regular terms}\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(3)}}{1-\zeta q}+\frac{\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mathrm{GV}}^{(3)}_{M/r}}{(1-\zeta q)^{2}}\right)+\mbox{ regular terms}.\end{split}

Similarly for the coefficient of
(1-P)^{3}Q^{M}

\begin{split}&5\,{\rm Coeff}\Big(\frac{1}{1-q}J^{{K}}(0);(1-P)^{3}Q^{M}\Big)\\
&=\sum_{k|M}\left(\frac{M+k^{2}-\frac{M}{k}-1}{1-q^{k}}+\frac{\frac{M}{k}+3}{(1-q^{k})^{2}}-\frac{2}{(1-q^{k})^{3}}\right){\mathrm{GV}}_{M/k}\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\sum_{k|\frac{M}{r}}\left(\frac{M+(rk)^{2}-\frac{M}{rk}-1}{1-q^{rk}}+\frac{\frac{M}{rk}+3}{(1-q^{rk})^{2}}-\frac{2}{(1-q^{rk})^{3}}\right){\mathrm{GV}}_{M/rk}\\
&\qquad+\mbox{ regular terms}\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\sum_{k|\frac{M}{r}}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}}{(1-\zeta q)}\Big((rk)+\frac{M}{rk}+\frac{-M-1}{(rk)^{3}}\right.\Big)\\
&\qquad\left.+\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}}{(1-\zeta q)^{2}}\Big(\frac{M+3}{(rk)^{3}}\Big)+\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}}{(1-\zeta q)^{3}}\Big(\frac{-2}{(rk)^{3}}\Big)\right)+\mbox{ regular terms}\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}\sum_{k|\frac{M}{r}}\left(\frac{M{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(-1)}+{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(1)}-\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(3)}-\frac{1}{M^{3}}{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(3)}}{1-\zeta q}\right.\\
&\qquad\left.+\frac{\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M/r}^{(3)}}{M^{2}}+\frac{3{\mathrm{GV}}^{(3)}_{M/r}}{M^{3}}}{(1-\zeta q)^{2}}+\frac{\frac{-2{\mathrm{GV}}^{(3)}_{M/r}}{M^{3}}}{(1-\zeta q)^{3}}\right)+\mbox{ regular terms}.\end{split}

This coincides with the equation
3.5
by taking

. The proof is now complete.

∎

Lemma 4.1

{\rm td}(T_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}}){\rm ch}\Big(\frac{1}{{\rm Tr}(\Lambda^{*}N^{*}_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}})}\Big)=:r^{-n}+T_{0,n+2,d}(\zeta),

Lemma 4.2
.

For any Calabi-Yau threefolds
X

\langle\tau_{k_{1}}(\phi_{1}),\dots,\tau_{k_{n}}(\phi_{n})\rangle_{g,n,\beta\neq 0}^{\mathrm{tw}}=0,

\begin{split}\int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)]^{{\rm vir}}}T\ &\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\psi_{i}^{k_{i}}{\rm ev}_{i}^{*}\phi_{i}\Big)\\

Appendix A
Alternative proof of Theorem
3.4

In this Appendix, we give another proof of Theorem
3.4
using only reconstruction theorem.

We start with the localization at
q=1
, which will relate to the reconstruction theorem in cohomological theory, which is recalled as follows:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{-z}J^{H}(0)&=1+\sum_{d\geq 0}Q^{d}\Big(\frac{d{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}H^{2}}{(-z)^{2}}-\frac{2{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}H^{3}}{(-z)^{3}}\Big)\\
&=\frac{1}{-z}\sum_{d\geq 0}I^{H}_{d}Q^{d}\exp\Big(\frac{(dz-H)\tau(Q)}{z}\Big)c(Q),\end{split}

for some uniquely determined
\tau(Q),c(Q)\in\mathbb{Q}[\![Q]\!]
.

Theorem A.1
.

Degree
0
terms with
\zeta=1
are given as follows:

\begin{split}a_{M,1}&=0,\\
c_{M,1}&=\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}_{M}^{(3)}}{M^{2}}=M{\mathrm{GW}}_{M},\\
f_{M,1}&=\frac{-2{\mathrm{GV}}_{M}^{(3)}}{M^{3}}=-2{\mathrm{GW}}_{M}.\end{split}

Proof.

We localize the reconstruction theorem at
q=1
:

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0)\\
\equiv_{q=1}^{0}&\sum_{d\geq 0}\Big(\frac{1}{1-q}I^{K}_{d}\Big)_{q=1}Q^{d}\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)_{q=1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(q,Q)(1-Pq^{d})^{i}\right)_{q=1}.\end{split}

We analyze the
I^{K}
term, the
\exp
term and the polynomial term at the end individually.

Lemma A.2
.

\Big(\frac{1}{1-q}I^{{K}}\Big)_{q=1}\equiv_{q=1}^{0}\Big(\frac{1}{-z}I^{H}(0)\Big)\Big|_{z=-(1-q),H=1-P}

Proof.

Since we consider only the degree
0
part, it suffices to compute the following terms in the expansion of
I^{{K}}
:

\begin{split}\left(\frac{\prod_{r=1}^{5d}(1-q^{r})}{(\prod_{r=1}^{d}(1-q^{r}))^{5}}\right)_{q=1}&\equiv_{q=1}^{0}\frac{(5d)!}{(d!)^{5}}\\
\Big(e_{i}(k)(1-P)^{i}\Big)_{q=1}&\equiv_{q=1}^{0}s_{i}(k)\Big(\frac{1-P}{1-q}\Big)^{i},\end{split}

where

s_{i}(k)=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{i}\leq k}\frac{1}{j_{1}}\dots\frac{1}{j_{k}}.

After replacing all terms in the expansion of
I^{{K}}
with their degree 0 part in the above computation and the substitution
(1-q)=-z
and
1-P=H
, we get

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{-z}\sum_{d\geq 0}Q^{d}\frac{(5d)!}{(d!)^{5}}\Big(1+s_{1}(5d)(\frac{5H}{-z})+e_{2}(5d)(\frac{5H}{-z})^{2}+e_{3}(5d)(\frac{5H}{-z})^{3}\Big)\\
&\Big(1-5e_{1}(d)(\frac{H}{-z})+(15e_{1}(d)^{2}-5e_{2}(d))(\frac{H}{-z})^{2}-(35e_{1}(d)^{3}-30e_{1}(d)e_{2}(d))(\frac{H}{-z})^{3}\Big),\end{split}

which is exactly the expansion of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{-z}I^{H}(0)
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

\begin{split}&\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Psi^{k}(\epsilon_{i}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}\right)_{q=1}\equiv_{q=1}^{0}\\
&\qquad\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{k(1-q)}\right)\exp\left(\sum_{k>0}-\frac{d\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\right)\end{split}

Proof.

For each

, we observe that

\begin{split}\frac{1-P^{k}q^{kd}}{k(1-q^{k})}&\equiv_{q=1}^{0}\frac{(1-P^{k})}{k(1-q^{k})}+\frac{1-q^{kd}}{k(1-q^{k})}\equiv_{q=1}^{0}\frac{(1-P)}{k(1-q)}+\frac{d}{k}\\
\frac{(1-P^{k}q^{kd})^{i}}{k(1-q^{k})}&\equiv_{q=1}^{0}0,\mbox{ for $i>1$}.\end{split}

The lemma follows.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(q,Q)(1-Pq^{d})^{i}\equiv_{q=1}^{0}r_{0}(1,Q)

Proof.

For the degree 0 term of the polynomial, we simply put
q=1
and
P=1
.
∎

Now we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0)\\
\equiv_{q=1}^{0}&\left(\frac{1}{-z}\sum_{d\geq 0}I^{H}_{d}Q^{d}\exp\Big(-\tau(Q)\frac{H}{z}\Big)\exp\Big(d\tau(Q)\Big)\Big(c(Q)\Big)\right)\Big|_{z=-(1-q),H=1-P},\end{split}

where

\tau(Q)=-\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k},\quad c(Q)=r_{0}(1,Q).

The above expression coincides with the reconstruction theorem in cohomological theory:

\begin{split}\frac{1}{-z}J^{H}(0)&=\frac{1}{-z}\sum_{d\geq 0}I^{H}_{d}Q^{d}\exp\Big(-\tau(Q)\frac{H}{z}\Big)\exp\Big(d\tau(Q)\Big)\Big(c(Q)\Big)\\
&=1+\sum_{d\geq 0}Q^{d}\Big(\frac{d{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}H^{2}}{(-z)^{2}}-\frac{2{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}H^{3}}{(-z)^{3}}\Big).\end{split}

We conclude that the degree 0 term in the localization at
q=1
coincides with cohomological computation after the substitution
1-q=-z
and
1-P=H
.

∎

Let
\zeta
be a primitive
r
-th roots of unity. We generalize the above computation to all degree 0 terms.

Theorem A.5

(=Theorem
3.4
)
.

All degree 0 terms are given as follows:

\begin{split}a_{M,\zeta}&=0,\\
c_{M,\zeta}&=\frac{{\mathrm{GV}}^{(3)}_{M/r}}{M^{2}}=\frac{\frac{M}{r}{\mathrm{GW}}_{M/r}}{r^{2}},\\
f_{M,\zeta}&=\frac{-2{\mathrm{GV}}^{(3)}_{M/r}}{M^{3}}=\frac{-2{\mathrm{GW}}_{M/r}}{r^{3}}.\end{split}

All invariants are defined to be zero if
M/r
is not integer.

Proof.

Using the reconstruction theorem again but localize at
q=\zeta^{-1}
, we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{1-q}J^{{K}}(0)\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\Big[\sum_{i\geq 0}I^{{K}}_{ri}(q)Q^{ri}\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{i\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\Big)\Big]\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{rk(1-\zeta q)}\Big)\\
&\left[\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\pi_{+}(I_{j}^{{K}})(\zeta^{-1})\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}q^{kl}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(\zeta^{-1},Q)(1-\zeta^{-j})^{i}\Big)Q^{j}\right].\end{split}

For the above expression, we use the following observations:

•

For
m=ri+j
with
0\leq j<r
, we have

\begin{split}I^{{K}}_{m}(q)&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}I^{{K}}_{rk}(q)\cdot\pi_{+}(I^{{K}}_{j})(q)\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}I^{{K}}_{rk}(q)\cdot\pi_{+}(I^{{K}}_{j})(\zeta^{-1}),\end{split}

where
\pi_{+}:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}_{+}
is the projection map.

•

With the same notation, we have

\begin{split}&\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P^{k}q^{km})}{k(1-q^{k})}\Big)\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))(1-q^{km})}{k(1-q^{k})}\Big)\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))\cdot kq^{km}(1-P)}{k(1-q^{k})}\Big)\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{m-1}q^{kl}\Big)\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{rk(1-\zeta q)}\Big)\\
&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{i\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\Big)\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}q^{kl}\Big)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{rk(1-\zeta q)}\Big).\end{split}

Our expression of
\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0)
fits into the following lemma:

Lemma A.6
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{K}
, and
h,h^{\prime}\in K[q]
. Assume that

f\cdot g=1\ ({\rm mod}Q),\quad f\cdot g\cdot h|_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}=1,

and that

\Psi^{r}((f)_{q=1})(\zeta q)\cdot\Psi^{r}((g)_{q=1})(\zeta q)\cdot h^{\prime}(q)\Big|_{\mathcal{K}_{+}}\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1},0}1.

Then we have

(1)

h^{\prime}(q)=\Psi^{r}(h)(\zeta q)
.

(2)

\begin{split}\Psi^{r}((f)_{q=1})(\zeta q)&\Psi^{r}((g)_{q=1})(\zeta q)h^{\prime}(q)\Big|_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}\\
&=\Psi^{r}((f)_{q=1})(\zeta q)\Psi^{r}((g)_{q=1})(\zeta q)\Big|_{\mathcal{K}_{-}}\cdot\Psi^{r}(h(1)).\end{split}

Proof.

h^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by induction on
Q
since we assume that
h^{\prime}\in K[q]
. It suffices to prove that

\Psi^{r}(h)(\zeta q)=h^{\prime}(q)=\Psi^{r}((h)_{q=1})(\zeta q),

which follows from
(h)_{q=1}(q)=h(q)
since
h\in K[q]
.

For the second statement, notice that

\Psi^{r}(h)(\zeta q)\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\Psi^{r}(h(1)).

∎

We claim that:

\begin{split}&\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\pi_{+}(I_{j}^{K})(\zeta^{-1})\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}q^{kl}\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{3}r_{i}(\zeta^{-1},Q)(1-\zeta^{-j})^{i}\Big)Q^{j}\\
=&\Psi^{r}(r_{0}(1,Q)).\end{split}

We prove the claim by taking
h(q)=r_{0}(q,Q)
in Lemma
A.6
. To apply the lemma, we need the following two observations:

•

\frac{1}{1-q}I^{K}(q)\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\Psi^{r}\Big((\frac{1}{1-q}I^{K}(q))_{q=1}\Big)(\zeta q)_{1-P=>\frac{1-P}{r}}.

The description at the end means that replacing
1-P
by
\frac{1-P}{r}
. The results follows from

\begin{split}\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{5rd}(1-q^{k})}{\prod_{k=1}^{rd}(1-q^{k})^{5}}\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\frac{(5d)!}{(d!)^{5}},\quad\frac{1}{1-q^{rd}}\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\frac{1}{rd(1-\zeta q)}.\end{split}

•

\begin{split}&\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{rk(1-\zeta_{r}q)}\Big)\\
\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}&\Psi^{r}\Big(\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{k}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{k(1-q)}\Big)\Big)_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=>\zeta q\\
1-P=>\frac{1-P}{r}\end{subarray}}.\end{split}

The description at the end means that replacing
q
by
\zeta q
and
1-P
by
\frac{1-P}{r}
.

Taking
\displaystyle f=\frac{1}{1-q}I^{K}(q)
and
\displaystyle g=\exp\Big(\sum_{k>0}\frac{\Psi^{rk}(T_{1}(Q))(1-P)}{rk(1-\zeta q)}\Big)
in the lemma, we conclude the claim. Changing the variables from
1-P
to
\frac{1-P}{r}
does not change any arguments and conclusions in the lemma.

Finally, we get

\begin{split}\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0)&\equiv_{q=\zeta^{-1}}^{0}\Psi^{r}\Big((\frac{1}{1-q}J^{K}(0))_{q=1}\Big)_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=>\zeta q\\
1-P=>\frac{1-P}{r}\end{subarray}}\\
&=1+\frac{(1-P)^{2}}{r^{2}}\sum_{d\geq 1}Q^{rd}\Big(\frac{d{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}}{(1-\zeta q)^{2}}\Big)+\frac{(1-P)^{3}}{r^{3}}\sum_{d\geq 1}Q^{rd}\Big(\frac{-2{\mathrm{GW}}_{d}}{(1-\zeta q)^{3}}\Big).\end{split}

This proves the theorem.
∎
