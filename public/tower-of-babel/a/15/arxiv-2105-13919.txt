Reward Biased Maximum Likelihood Estimation for Learning in Constrained MDPs
Rahul Singh

Abstract
We use the Reward Biased Maximum Likelihood Estimation (RBMLE) algorithm Mete et al. 2020 to learn optimal policies for constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs). We analyze the learning regrets of RBMLE.



keywords
Reinforcement Learning; Markov Decision Process; Adaptive Control

Introduction

Consider a controlled Markov chain with finite state space
X
, finite action set
U
, and controlled transition
probabilities
\mathbb{P}(x(t+1)=y~|x(t)=x,u(t)=u)=p(x,y,u)
, where
x(t)\in X
denotes the state at time
t
, and
u(t)\in U
denotes the action
taken at time
t
. A reward
r(x,u)
is received when action
u
is taken in state
x
.

Let
\bar{r}(\pi,p)
and
\bar{c}(\pi,p)
denote the average reward and cost under a stationary policy
\pi
when the true value of MDP parameter is
p
,

\bar{r}(\pi,p):=\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}r(x(t),u(t)).

Let
R^{\star}(p)
denote the maximal long-term average reward of the solution to the following CMDP when the parameter
p
is known:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\liminf_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}r(x(t),u(t))

\displaystyle\mbox{ s.t. }\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}c(x(t),u(t))\leq c_{ub}.

We consider the case where the transition probabilities
p
are only known to belong a set
\Theta
, but otherwise unknown.

A stationary randomized policy
\pi
maps each state
x
to a probability distribution over the space of actions
U
, i.e.,
\pi(x):X\mapsto\Delta(U)
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\pi^{\star}
be an optimal stationary randomized policy for the following CMDP

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\bar{r}(\pi)\mbox{ s.t. }\bar{c}(\pi)\leq c_{ub}.

(1)

Let
\hat{\pi}
be the policy from
\Pi_{F}
that is “closest” to
\pi^{\star}
(each
\pi^{\star}(x)\in\Delta(U)
is mapped to the closest point in the tesselation). Then we have that

\displaystyle\bar{r}(\hat{\pi})\geq r^{\star}-\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2},\mbox{ and }\bar{c}(\hat{\pi})\leq c_{ub}+\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}.

(2)

Furthermore, the “discretization error”

r^{\star}-\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\pi)\leq c_{ub}}\bar{r}(\pi),

is
O(\epsilon)
.

Proof.

(
2
) follows directly from Lemma
17
. To prove the result on discretization error, construct a modified form of the CMDP (
1
) in which the bound on cost is replaced by
c_{ub}-\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}
. Using Theorem
22
we conclude that the optimal reward of this modified CMDP is lower bounded by
r^{\star}-\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}\frac{\hat{\eta}}{\eta}
. Consider the discretization to this solution. It follows from (
2
) that this discretized solution is feasible for the problem

\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\pi)\leq c_{ub}}\bar{r}(\pi),

while its reward is lower bounded by
r^{\star}-\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}\frac{\hat{\eta}}{\eta}-\epsilon\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}
(denote this by
y
). Proof is then completed by noting that the optimal discretized solution to the problem

\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\pi)\leq c_{ub}}\bar{r}(\pi)

has a reward which is necessarily greater than
y
.
∎

2
System Model

We consider the MDP described in Section
1
, assuming,
without loss of generality, that
r(x,u),c(x,u)\in[0,1]
for all
(x,u)\in X\times U
.
We denote by
\Pi_{SD}
the set of all stationary deterministic policies that map
X
into
U
,
by
\Pi_{S}
the set of all stationary possibly randomized policies. In general, solving a CMDP requires randomization. Note that the set of stationary randomized policies is uncountably infinite. Thus, while deriving regret bounds, we will assume that we restrict ourselves to a finite set of policies
\Pi_{F}
.
\Pi_{F}
could be obtained by discretizing the simplex
\Delta(U)
. Thus in each state
x
, a policy from the set
\Pi_{F}
will pick an element from this finite set, and randomize amongst the actions using this distribution. Denote

\Pi^{\star}(\theta):=\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{F}}\left\{\bar{r}(\theta,\pi),\mbox{ s.t. }\bar{c}(\theta,\pi)\leq c_{ub}\right\},

and let
r^{\star}(p)
the optimal value.

Definition 2
.

(Unichain MDP)
Under a stationary policy
\pi
, let

\tau^{\pi}_{x,y}
denote the time taken to hit the state
y
when started in state
x
. The MDP is called unichain if
\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pi}_{x,y}]
is finite for all
(x,y,\pi)
.

Definition 3
.

(Mixing Time)
For a unichain MDP with parameter
p
, its mixing time
T_{p}
is defined as

\displaystyle T_{p}:=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{s}}\max_{x,y\in X}\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pi}_{x,y}].

Its “conductivity”
\kappa_{p}
is

\displaystyle\kappa_{p}:=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{s}}\max_{x\in X}\frac{\max_{y\neq x}\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pi}_{x,y}]}{2\mathbb{E}[\tau^{\pi}_{x,x}]}.

Definition 4
.

(Kullback-Leibler divergence) For
p_{2}=\{p_{2}(x)\}
absolutely continuous with respect to
p_{2}=\{p_{2}(x)\}
the KL-divergence between them is

\displaystyle KL(p_{1},p_{2}):=\sum_{x\in X}p_{1}(x)\log\frac{p_{1}(x)}{p_{2}(x)}.

(3)

Definition 5
.

(Gaps)
The reward gap is defined as follows,

\Delta_{\min,r}:=R^{\star}(p)-\max_{\{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\pi,p)\leq c_{ub}\}}\bar{r}(\pi,p).

The cost gap is defined similarly as follows,

\Delta_{\min,c}:=\min_{\left\{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\pi,p)>c_{ub}\right\}}\bar{c}(\pi,p)-c_{ub}.

\Delta_{\min,r}
is the reward gap, while
\Delta_{\min,c}
is the cost gap.

\blacksquare

For two integers
x,y
, we use
[x,y]
to denote the set
\{x,x+1,\ldots,y\}
and for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
we let
a\vee b:=\max\{a,b\}
.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that the following information is known about the unknown transition probabilities
p(x,y,u)
:

•

the set of tuples
(x,y,u)
for which
p(x,y,u)=0
,

•

a lower bound
p_{\min}
on the non-zero transition probabilities,

\displaystyle p_{\min}:=\min\limits_{(x,y,u):p(x,y,u)>0}p(x,y,u).

(4)

We let
\Theta
be the following set

\displaystyle\Theta:=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\theta\in[0,1]^{|X|\times|X|\times|U|}:\theta(x,y,u)=0\mbox{ if }~p(x,y,u)=0,\sum_{y\in X}\theta(x,y,u)=1\;\forall\;(x,u),\theta(x,y,u)\geq 0\Bigg\}.

(5)

We occasionally refer to
\theta\in\Theta
as a “parameter” describing the model or transition probabilites.

\blacksquare

Learning Regrets: The work
Singh et al. 2020
shows that in order to judge the performance of a learning algorithm
\phi
designed to solve the CMDP (
1
) when the controlled transition probabilities
p
are unknown, an appropriate metric is the tuple consisting of the following two regrets: a) Reward regret
\mathcal{R}_{r}(T;\phi)
, and b) Cost Regret,
\mathcal{R}_{c}(T;\phi)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{r}(T):

\displaystyle=r^{\star}(p)T-\sum_{t=1}^{T}r(x(t),u(t)),

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{c}(T):

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}c(x(t),u(t))-T(c_{ub}+\Delta_{\min,c}).

(7)

\mathcal{R}_{r}(T)
keeps track of the sub-optimality in reward collection, while
\mathcal{R}_{c}(T)
measures the amount of “over-utilization” of the control cost during the learning phase. Note that the definitions of regrets imply that our benchmark is
\Pi_{F}
. In what follows, we denote
\tilde{c}_{ub}:=c_{ub}+\Delta_{\min,c}
. a recent work on learning in constrained MDPs is
Efroni et al. 2020
.

3
The RBMLE-Based Learning Algorithm

If
\theta=\{\theta(x,y,u):(x,y,u)\}\in\Theta
denotes an MDP parameter (controlled transition probabilities), denote by
\theta(x,u)
the vector
\left\{\theta(x,y,u):y\in X\right\}
.
Let
n(x,u;t)
be the number of times an action
u
has been applied in state
x
until time
t
, and by
n(x,y,u;t)
the number of
x\to y
one-step transitions under the application of action
u
. Let
\hat{p}(t)=\left\{\hat{p}(x,y,u;t):x,y\in X,u\in U\right\}
be the empirical estimate of
p
at time
t
, with
\hat{p}(x,y,u;t)
the MLE of
p(x,y,u)
at time
t
,

\displaystyle\hat{p}(x,y,u;t):=\frac{n(x,y,u;t)}{n(x,u;t)\vee 1}\;\forall\;x,y\in X\text{ and }u\in U.

(8)

The RBMLE algorithm:

The algorithm evolves in an episodic manner. We let
\tau_{k}
denote the starting time of the
k
-th episode, and
\mathcal{E}_{k}:=[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1}-1]
the set of time-slots that comprise it. The episode durations increase exponentially with episode index, with
|\mathcal{E}_{k}|=2^{k}
. Clearly
\tau_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-1}|\mathcal{E}_{\ell}|
. Throughout we abbreviate
\hat{p}(\tau_{k})
as
\hat{p}_{k}
,
n(x,y;\tau_{k})
as
n_{k}(x,y)
and
n(x,y,u;\tau_{k})
as
n_{k}(x,y,u)
.
At the beginning of each episode
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, the RBMLE algorithm determines the following quantities
1
1

1

Throughout the paper, a pre-specified priority order is used to choose a particular maximizer in
arg\max
if needed.
:

(i)

A “reward-biased MLE” of the true system parameter, which is denoted
\theta_{k}
:

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\bigg\{\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{S}:\bar{c}(\theta,\pi)\leq c_{ub}}\bigg\{\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta,\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta(x,u)\right)\bigg\}\bigg\},

(9)

where

\displaystyle\alpha(t):=a\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right),

(10)

and the constants
a,b
are chosen by the agent and must satisfy the following conditions,

\displaystyle b>2,\text{ and }a>\frac{|X|^{3}|U|}{2p_{\min}\Delta_{\min}}.

(11)

(ii)

A stationary (possibly randomized) policy

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\in\underset{\pi\in\Pi_{F}:\bar{c}(\theta_{k},\pi)\leq c_{ub}}{\arg\max}\bar{r}(\theta_{k},\pi).

(12)

Thereafter, it chooses controls for the current episode, i.e. for times
t\in\mathcal{E}_{k}
by sampling
u(t)
from the distribution
\pi_{k}(x(t))
.

Intuitive Description of RBMLE
: The RBMLE algorithm takes an optimistic view towards solving the problem by giving a “push” or bias towards parameters with higher average rewards. As we will show, this allows it to explore the parameter space adequately, so that its “learning regret” is low. However, since it has to also ensure that the time-average cost expenditures are below
c_{ub}
(with a high probability), it also enforces the constraint
\bar{c}(\theta,\pi)\leq c_{ub}
.

4
Preliminary Results

An equivalent Index description of the RBMLE learning algorithm:

We begin by showing that the RBMLE algorithm is an “index rule”
(
Gittins et al. 2011
)
. Though this characterization does not yield us simple computational procedures to implement the RBMLE algorithm, it is used while analyzing its learning regret.

Lemma 6
.

At the beginning of each episode
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, RBMLE attaches an index
I_{k}(\pi)
to each
\pi\in\Pi_{S}
,

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi):=\max_{\theta\in\Theta:\bar{c}(\pi,\theta)\leq c_{ub}}\bigg\{\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta,\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta(x,u)\right)\bigg\}.

(13)

In case
\bar{c}(\pi,\theta)>c_{ub}
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, we let
I_{k}(\pi)=-\infty
. The policy
\pi_{k}
, as defined in (
12
) has the largest index, i.e.,

\displaystyle\pi_{k}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{S}}I_{k}(\pi).

(14)

Within
\mathcal{E}_{k}
, RBMLE implements the policy with the highest value of the RBMLE index.
\blacksquare

Define the following “confidence interval”
\mathcal{C}(t)
at time
t
associated with the empirical estimate
\hat{p}(t)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(t):=\bigg\{\theta\in\Theta:|\theta(x,y,u)-\hat{p}(x,y,u;t)|\leq d_{1}(x,u;t),\forall(x,y,u)\in X\times X\times U\bigg\},

(15)

where

\displaystyle d_{1}(x,u;t):=\sqrt{\frac{\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right)}{n(x,u;t)}},

(16)

and
b
is as in (
11
). Define also the set
\mathcal{G}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}:=\left\{\omega:p\in\mathcal{C}(t),\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

(17)

The following result shows concentration of
\hat{p}(t)
around the true MDP’s parameter
p
.

Lemma 7
.

The probability that
p
lies in
\mathcal{C}(t)
is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p\in\mathcal{C}(t))>1-\frac{2}{t^{2b-1}|X|^{2}|U|},\;\forall\;t\in\mathbb{N}.

For a stationary policy
\pi\in\Pi_{S}
, define
\theta_{k,\pi}
as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{k,\pi}\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta:\bar{c}(\pi,\theta)\leq c_{ub}}\bigg\{\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta,\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta(x,u)\right)\bigg\}.

(18)

We now derive a lower bound on the index of any optimal stationary policy
\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star}(p)
that holds with
high probability.

Lemma 8
.

On the set
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, the index
I_{k}(\pi^{\star})
of any optimal policy
\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star}(p)
is lower bounded as follows:

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi^{\star})\geq\alpha(\tau_{k})(1-\gamma)~R^{\star}(p),~\forall k=1,2,\ldots,K,

where

\displaystyle 1\geq\gamma\geq\frac{|X|^{3}|U|}{2a\cdot p_{\min}J^{\star}(p)}.

(19)

For a policy
\pi\in\Pi_{S}
, we let
\pi(x)
denote the probability distribution over
U
using which action is chosen when state is equal to
x
. Consider the set of actions
\{u:\pi(x,u)>0\}
chosen by
\pi
with positive probability in state
x
. Next, we show that if the state-action pairs
\{(x,u):\pi(x,u)>0\}
corresponding to a sub-optimal policy
\pi
have been visited for a sufficiently large number of times, then its index
I_{k}(\pi)
is lower than the index of any optimal policy.

We now show that the quantity
{\theta_{k,\pi}}
(
18
) for a policy
\pi
does not deviate “a lot” from the MLE
\hat{p}_{k}
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\pi\in\Pi_{S}
be feasible for the true MDP
p
, i.e.,
\bar{c}(\pi,p)\leq c_{ub}
. Then,

\displaystyle|\theta_{k,\pi}(x,y,u)-\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)|\leq d_{2}(x,u;\tau_{k}),(x,y,u)\in X\times X\times U,

where

\displaystyle d_{2}(x,u;t):=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha(\tau_{k})}{2~n_{k}(x,u)}\left(1+\frac{1}{2a|X|p_{\min}}\right)},\forall(x,u)\in X\times U.

(20)

Suppose that the number of visits to each state-action pair from the set
\{(x,u):\pi(x,u)>0\}
, until the time
\tau_{k}
is lower bounded as follows,

\displaystyle n(x,u;\tau_{k})>\frac{\alpha(\tau_{k})}{c^{2}}\;\forall(x,u)\text{ s.t. }\pi(x,u)>0.

(21)

Then, the distance between
\theta_{k,\pi}
and
p
is bounded as follows:

\displaystyle|\theta_{k,\pi}(x,y,\pi(x))-p(x,y,\pi(x))|<c\left[\sqrt{.5\left(1+\frac{1}{a}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\right]~~\forall\;x,y\in X.

(22)

Proof.

Recall that the index
I_{k}(\pi)
(
13
) is given as follows,

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)=\max_{\theta\in\Theta:\bar{c}(\pi,\theta)\leq c_{ub}}\bigg\{\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta,\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta(x,u)\right)\bigg\}.

(23)

Since
\theta_{k,\pi}
maximizes the expression on the r.h.s., the index can be written as follows,

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta_{k,\pi},\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta_{k,\pi}(x,u)\right).

(24)

Since
\pi
is feasible for the true MDP, we obtain the following

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)\geq\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(p,\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),p(x,u)\right).

(25)

By using inverse Pinsker’s inequality we get

\displaystyle KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),p(x,u)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2p_{\min}}\big(\sum\limits_{y\in X}|p(x,y,u)-\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)|\big)^{2}.

(26)

On the set
\mathcal{G}_{1}
(
17
), we have
|\theta(x,y,u)-\hat{p}(x,y,u;t)|\leq d_{1}(x,u;t)
. Substituting this bound into the above inequality, we obtain the following

\displaystyle KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),p(x,u)\right)\leq|X|\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right).

(27)

Upon combining this with (
25
), we deduce that on
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, the following holds,

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)\geq\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(p,\pi)-|X|\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right).

(28)

Substituting the expression (
24
) for
I_{k}(\pi)
we get

\displaystyle\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta_{k,\pi},\pi)-\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta_{k,\pi}(x,u)\right)\geq\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(p,\pi)-\frac{1}{2p_{\min}}|X|\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right),

or

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta_{k,\pi}(x,u)\right)\leq\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(\theta_{k,\pi},\pi)-\alpha(\tau_{k})\bar{r}(p,\pi)+\frac{1}{2p_{\min}}|X|\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right).

Since the average reward
\bar{r}(\theta,\pi)\in[0,1]
for all
\theta\in\Theta
and
\pi\in\Pi_{S}
, the above inequality reduces to

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,u)}n_{k}(x,u)KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta_{k,\pi}(x,u)\right)\leq\alpha(\tau_{k})+\frac{1}{2p_{\min}}|X|\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right).

By using Pinsker’s inequality
(
Cover 1999
)
, we can bound KL-divergence as follows:

\displaystyle|\theta_{k,\pi}(x,y,u)-\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}KL\left(\hat{p}_{k}(x,u),\theta_{k,\pi}(x,u)\right)\;\forall x,y\in X\text{ and }u\in U.

Upon combining the above two inequalities we deduce that on
\mathcal{G}_{1}
we have the following bound

\displaystyle|\theta_{k,\pi}(x,y,u)-\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)|^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha(\tau_{k})+\frac{|X|}{2p_{\min}}\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right)}{2~n_{k}(x,u)}.

(29)

Upon using
\alpha(t)=a\log\left(t^{b}|X|^{2}|U|\right)
(
10
) in the above, we get

\displaystyle|\theta_{k,\pi}(x,y,u)-\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\alpha(\tau_{k})\left(1+\frac{1}{2a|X|p_{\min}}\right)}{2~n_{k}(x,u)}}.

(30)

We now show (
22
). As is shown in Lemma
7
, the distance between

and
\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)
can be bounded by

. We showed just now that the distance between
\theta_{k}(x,y,u)
and
\hat{p}_{k}(x,y,u)
can be bounded by

Corollary 10
.

Define the function

\displaystyle g(a):=\left[\sqrt{.5\left(1+\frac{1}{a}\right)}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\right],

(31)

and let

\displaystyle c=\frac{\beta\Delta_{\min}}{g(a)\kappa_{p}|X|^{2}},

(32)

where the parameter
\beta
satisfies
\beta<1
. Let the number of visits to different state-action pairs satisfy the condition (
21
). Then,

\displaystyle\bar{r}(\pi,\theta_{k,\pi})\leq\bar{r}(\pi,p)+\beta\Delta_{\min},\forall\pi\in\Pi_{S}.

(33)

Thus, the index
I_{k}(\pi)
of a sub-optimal policy can be upper-bounded as follows,

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)\leq\alpha(T)\left[\bar{r}(\pi,p)+\beta\Delta_{\min}\right].

(34)

Furthermore, if

\displaystyle a>\frac{|X|^{2}|U|}{2(1-\beta)\Delta_{\min}p_{\min}},

(35)

then

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)\leq I_{k}(\pi^{\star}),\mbox{ where }\pi^{\star}\in\Pi^{\star}(p).

(36)

\blacksquare

Lemma 11
.

Let
\pi
be an infeasible policy that satisfies the following,

\displaystyle\bar{c}(\pi,p)>c_{ub}+(w+c)\kappa_{p},

(37)

where
w>0
satisfies (
39
). Assume that the number of visits to different state-action pairs satisfy the condition (
21

\mathcal{G}_{1}
, the index

can be upper-bounded as follows:

\displaystyle I_{k}(\pi)\leq\alpha(T)-\alpha(T)\frac{w^{2}}{c^{2}}.

(38)

If

\displaystyle w\geq c\left[1+\frac{|X|^{2}|U|}{2ap_{\min}}\right]^{.5},

(39)

then the index

is less than the index of an optimal policy.

Proof.

Throughout this proof we assume that we restrict to a sample path lying in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
. Let
\theta\in\Theta
be such that
\bar{c}(\pi,\theta)\leq c_{ub}
. Since the cost incurred by
\pi
on the system with parameter value equal to true value

\bar{c}(\pi,p)\geq c_{ub}+(w+c)\kappa_{p}
, it follows from the perturbation bound in Lemma

\|\theta-p\|\geq w+c
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|\theta-\hat{p}_{k}\|

\displaystyle=\|(\theta-p)+(p-\hat{p}_{k})\|

\displaystyle\geq\|(\theta-p)\|-\|(p-\hat{p}_{k})\|

\displaystyle\geq w+c-c

\displaystyle=w(say),

(40)

where the last inequality also uses the fact that on
\mathcal{G}_{1}
we have
\|p-\hat{p}_{k}\|\leq d_{1}(x,u;t)
. Now, consider the problem (
9
) that is to be solved for deriving
\theta_{k}
. The expression within the braces in (
9
) that corresponds to maximization over the set of policies
\Pi_{S}
for a fixed
\theta
can be bounded as follows

Regret Analysis

We now utilize the results of previous section in order to obtain an upper bound on the expected regret of the RBMLE algorithm. We begin by decomposing the cumulative regrets
\mathcal{R}_{r}(\phi,T),\mathcal{R}_{c}(\phi,T)
of the learning rule

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{r}(\phi,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(R^{\star}(p)|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}r(x(t),u(t))\Big),

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{c}(\phi,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(\sum_{t\in\mathcal{E}_{k}}c(x(t),u(t))-c_{ub}|\mathcal{E}_{k}|\Big).

(45)

Since the RBMLE algorithm implements a single stationary policy
\pi_{k}
during

, we obtain the following bounds on the expected regrets (Lemma 12,
Mete et al. 2020
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}_{r}(\phi,p,T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K(T)}\mathbb{E}\Big(R^{\star}(p)|\mathcal{E}_{k}|-|\mathcal{E}_{k}|\bar{r}(\pi_{k},p)\Big)+T_{p}K(T),

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{R}_{c}(\phi,p,T)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K(T)}\mathbb{E}\Big(|\mathcal{E}_{k}|\bar{c}(\pi_{k},p)-\tilde{c}_{ub}|\mathcal{E}_{k}|\Big)+T_{p}K(T),

(47)

is the number of episodes till

in the episodes

chosen in that episode. The last term

Theorem 22
.

Let the MDP
p

1

2

denotes optimal reward value of the CMDP

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi}\inf_{\lambda}~~\bar{r}(\pi)+\sum_{i=1}^{M}\lambda_{i}\left(c^{ub}_{i}-\bar{c}_{i}(\pi)\right),
