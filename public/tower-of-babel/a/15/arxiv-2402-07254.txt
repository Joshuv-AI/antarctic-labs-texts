Abstract
. Horrocks proved in 1964 [ Hor ] that vector bundles on ℙ n {\mathbb{P}}^{n} without intermediate cohomology split as direct sum of line bundles. This result has been the starting point of a great research activity on other varieties, showing interesting connections with derived categories and other areas. We follow some paths into this fascinating story, which has classical roots. The story has a culmination with the trichotomy result (finite/tame/wild) for arithmetically Cohen Macaulay (ACM) varieties obtained by Faenzi and Pons-Llopis in 2021. This is an expanded version of the talk given at the conference ”Homemade Algebraic Geometry” in July 2023 at Alcalá de Henares celebrating Enrique Arrondo’s 60th birthday.

Introduction

A vector bundle
E
on a variety
X
polarized by
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
has no intermediate cohomology if
H^{i}(X,E\otimes{\mathcal{O}}(t))=0

\forall t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
0<i<\dim X
. This brief survey about bundles without intermediate cohomology is divided into three sections.

In the first one we recall cohomological criteria for a vector bundle on projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and on smooth quadrics
Q_{n}
to split as a sum of line bundles. Bundles on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

or
Q_{n}
without intermediate cohomology are finitely many, up to twist with line bundles.
This means that
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and
Q_{n}
are
finite
.

In the second section we consider the approach to these topics with derived categories.
The analogous facts are the Beilinson description od the derived category of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and the Kapranov description of the derived category of
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
.
The description of the derived category of Grassmannians by Kapranov is a breakthrough in the story. Bundles on Grassmannians without intermediate cohomology make families of
arbitrarily large dimension, this means that any Grassmannian different from
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
or
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{3})
is
wild
.

A variety polarized with a very ample line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
is called arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay (ACM) when
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
has no intermediate cohomology
In the third section we expose the classification of bundles on ACM varieties into the three classes finite/tame/wild, the
trichotomy
.

The style of the exposition is informal and concerns the development of main ideas through examples.
There are three open problems left respectively as questions
1.10
,
1.17
,
2.9
. I thank Daniele Faenzi for several useful discussions.

The Horrocks criterion, the spinor bundles, classical roots on ACM bundles

1.1.
The Horrocks criterion on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
and its classical roots

The starting point of this story is the remarkable

Theorem 1.1
.

Horrocks criterion (1964)
[
Hor
]

Let
E
be a vector bundle on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}({\mathbb{C}})
.

E=\oplus_{i=1}^{r}{\mathcal{O}}(a_{i})
for some integers
a_{i}

\Longleftrightarrow

H^{i}(E(t))=0,\quad 1\leq i\leq n-1,\forall t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

This result characterizes bundles on
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
without intermediate cohomology
, called also arithmetically Cohen-Macaulay(ACM) bundles.

Note for
n=1
the condition in the criterion is empty and we get

Corollary 1.2
.

(Segre-Grothendieck) Any vector bundles
E
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
split as the sum of line bundles,
that is

E=\oplus_{i=1}^{r}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}(a_{i})

for some integers
a_{i}
.

Corollary
1.2
is commonly attributed to Grothendieck although, as often happens in Mathematics, Grothendieck
was not the first to prove it.
A short history is contained in
[
OSS
, §2.4]
.

We list of some proofs in reverse chronological order, revising slightly the list in
[
OSS
, §2.4]
,
where most of the original references are quoted.

•

Grauert-Remmert (1975), the most popular proof, available also in
[
OSS
, Theorem 2.1.1]
,

•

Grothendieck (1956),

•

Hilbert (1906), in the language of matrices on
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
,

•

Bellatalla (1901), his diploma thesis with C. Segre supervision

]
,

•

C. Segre, for rank
2
(1883), a geometric argument
[
S1
]
,

•

For the convenience of the reader we recall the sketch of the Grauert-Remmert proof of Segre-Grothendieck Cor.
1.2
.
Let
E
be a vector bundle on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.

\bullet

Step 1
, up to twist by a line bundle, we may assume
h^{0}(E)\neq 0
,
h^{0}(E(-1))=0
.
Let
{\mathcal{O}}\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{s}}E
be a section, it has no zeroes, otherwise if
s(x)=0
then
s
is a section of
E\otimes I_{x}=E(-1)
.

\bullet

Step 2
, we get the exact sequence

(1.1)

0\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}{\mathcal{O}}\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{s}}E\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}F\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}0

where
F=\oplus_{i=1}^{r-1}{\mathcal{O}}(b_{i})
splits by induction on the rank.

\bullet

Step 3
, tensoring (
1.1
) by
{\mathcal{O}}(-1)
we get

\underset{\displaystyle\overset{\displaystyle\shortparallel}{0}}{H^{0}(E(-1))}\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}H^{0}(\oplus_{i=1}^{r-1}{\mathcal{O}}(b_{i}-1))\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}\underset{\displaystyle\overset{\displaystyle\shortparallel}{0}}{H^{1}({\mathcal{O}}(-1))}

hence
b_{i}\leq 0
.

\bullet

Step 4
, the sequence (
1.1
) splits since
Ext^{1}(F,{\mathcal{O}})=H^{1}(F^{\vee})=0
.

For a modern exposition of Segre paper
[
S1
]
we refer to
[

Segre worked in the setting of ruled varieties. In modern notations, for a
2

The goal was to construct a section of

, and one lifts the isomorphism

from the line to the whole

This approach has been nicely exposed in

Giuseppe Gherardelli wrote in 1943 the following remarkable result, under Severi advice.
It can be considered the ancestor of Horrocks criterion.

Theorem 1.3

(G. Gherardelli)
.

[
Gh
]

Let

be smooth.
Then
C
it is a complete intersection if and only if the following two conditions hold

•

(i)
C
is subcanonical, that is
K_{C}={\mathcal{O}}(e)_{|C}
for some
e\in{\mathbb{Z}}

•

(ii)
C
is projectively normal, that is the restriction map

H^{0}({\mathbb{P}}^{3},{\mathcal{O}}(k))\to H^{0}(C,{\mathcal{O}}(k))
is surjective.

Actually the assumptions in
[
Gh
]
on

were weaker, but Gherardelli can conclude that

is smooth. In the setting of ACM bundles, the assumption that makes Theorem

to work is that

is a

After Serre correspondence (
[

, chap. 1 §5]
and
[

is obtained as the zero locus of a section
of a rank

is a complete intersection.
In this optic, Gherardelli Theorem for curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
is equivalent to Horrocks criterion for
2
-bundles on
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
, indeed the condition (ii) is equivalent to the vanishing

which from the sequence

0\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}E\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}I_{C,{\mathbb{P}}^{3}}(c_{1}(E))\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}0

is equivalent to

, and the second vanishing

follows from Serre duality.

1.2.
Spinor bundles and the splitting criterion on quadrics

On
quadrics
there is a notable class of bundles without intermediate cohomology beyond line bundles, the
spinor bundles
.

The linear spaces contained in a smooth quadric
Q_{n}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n+1}
have maximal dimension
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
Linear spaces contained in a quadric are called isotropic, since a linear space
{\mathbb{P}}(L)
is contained in
Q_{n}
if and only if

Q(v,w)=0

\forall v,w\in L
where
Q(-,-)
is the bilinear form associated to the quadric.

Let for simplicity
n=2k+1
(
n
odd). The spinor variety
S_{k+1}
parametrizes the isotropic linear spaces of maximal dimension

(1.2)

S_{k+1}=\left\{{\mathbb{P}}^{k}\in Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{k},{\mathbb{P}}^{2k+2})|{\mathbb{P}}^{k}\subset Q_{2k+1}\right\}

and it has easily seen to have dimension
\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}
. The first cases are
S_{1}={\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and
S_{2}={\mathbb{P}}^{3}
. By the Plücker embedding,
S_{k+1}
is embedded in the large projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
with
N={{2k+3}\choose{k+1}}-1
. The striking fact is that
{\mathrm{P}ic}(S_{k+1})={\mathbb{Z}}
and

{\mathcal{O}}_{Gr}(1)_{|S_{k+1}}={\mathcal{O}}_{S_{k+1}}(2)

so that the spinor variety
S_{k+1}
has another embedding corresponding to the “half” line bundle
{\mathcal{O}}(1))
(replacing
{\mathcal{O}}(2)
) in the projective space of a vector space of smaller dimension
2^{k+1}
, the famous spin representation

(\mathrm{Spin}{\mathbb{C}}^{2k+1})
, that we recall briefly in Remark
1.4
.

We have the incidence variety

\mathbb{F}\colon=\left\{(x,{\mathbb{P}}^{k})\in Q_{2k+1}\times S_{k+1}|x\in{\mathbb{P}}^{k}\right\}

with the two projections
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
and

S^{\vee}=(p_{1})_{*}(p_{2})^{*}{\mathcal{O}}(1)
is the dual of the spinor bundle. Since the tangent space
T_{x}Q_{2k+1}
meets

Q_{2k+1}
in a cone with vertex
x
over a smaller quadric
Q_{2k-1}
, the fibers of
p_{1}
are isomorphic to
a smaller spinor variety
S_{k}
. This proves that
{\mathrm{r}ank}S=h^{0}(S_{k},{\mathcal{O}}(1))=2^{k}
.

On even dimensional quadrics
Q_{2k}
there are two families of maximal isotropic subspaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
, both parametrized by the same spinor variety
S_{k}
encountered in the odd case,
and accordingly two spinor bundles
S^{\prime}
,
S^{\prime\prime}
of rank
2^{k-1}
. In
[
Ott88
]
it is described
how the spinor bundles restrict to hyperplane quadrics, this argument allows to prove directly
by induction that
{\mathrm{r}ank}S=2^{k}
.

It is useful to compare the following facts.

•

The Grassmannian
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{k},{\mathbb{P}}^{n})
is the unique closed
SL(n+1)
-orbit in
{\mathbb{P}}(\wedge^{k+1}{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})
.

•

The Veronese variety
v_{d}({\mathbb{P}}^{n})
is the unique closed
SL(n+1)
-orbit in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Sym}^{d}{\mathbb{C}}^{n+1})
.

•

The spinor variety
S_{k}
is the unique closed
Spin(2k+1)
-orbit in
{\mathbb{P}}(\mathrm{Spin}{\mathbb{C}}^{2k+1})
.

Remark 1.4
.

We take the opportunity to expose the spin representation in a nutshell,
as can be taught at undergraduate level.
In an algebra course,
{\mathbb{C}}
is commonly introduced as
{\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}i
where you declare
i^{2}=-1
.

In a linear algebra course,
{\mathbb{C}}
may be introduced as the
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebra

{\mathbb{C}}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\
b&a\end{bmatrix}|a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\}

, where
i=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
satisfies
i^{2}=-1
.
This has the advantage that the imaginary unit is a concrete real
2\times 2
matrix.

In a second course, you may introduce the quaternion as the
{\mathbb{C}}
-algebra

{\mathbb{H}}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}z&-w\\
\overline{w}&\overline{z}\end{bmatrix}|z,w\in{\mathbb{C}}\right\}

and now you pose

{\mathbf{i}}=\begin{bmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{j}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},{\mathbf{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix}

they satisfy the usual rules, and they are a realization of the Pauli matrices (with a different normalization).
The quaternion conjugate corresponds to the operation
{\overline{A}}^{t}
on the matrix
A

and this allows to define quaternion norm as
|q|^{2}=q\overline{q}
for
q\in{\mathbb{H}}
.

Now
{\mathbb{H}}={\mathbb{R}}\oplus{\mathbb{I}}
contains
S^{3}=\{q||q|=1\}
which shows the remarkable fact that
S^{3}
has a group structure, indeed it is a real Spin group.

Construct the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}S^{3}&\to&SO(3)=SO({\mathbb{I}})\\
q&\mapsto&(x\mapsto q^{-1}xq)\end{array}

which is
2:1
group homomorphism, since the fibers consist of
\{-q,q\}
.

It follows that
S^{3}
is the universal covering of
SO(3)
, we pose
S^{3}=Spin(3)
and the natural inclusion

S^{3}\subset GL(2,{\mathbb{C}})
as quaternions of norm
1
identifies
S^{3}=SU(2)
and is the
2
-dimensional
real spin representation of
Spin(3)
. On complex numbers we have, analogously,
Spin(3)=SL(2)
.

The general
Spin
representation follows this path by replacing the algebra of quaternions
{\mathbb{H}}
with the more general Clifford algebra.

The spinor bundle is ACM, so that to get a splitting criterion analog to Horrocks criterion, it is necessary to add a further condition,
as in the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

Splitting Criterion on Quadrics,
[
Ott89
]

Let
E
be a vector bundle on
Q_{n}
, let

be a spinor bundle. Then

E=\oplus_{i=1}^{r}{\mathcal{O}}(a_{i})

\Longleftrightarrow

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}H^{i}(E(*))=0,&1\leq i\leq n-1\\
H^{n-1}(E\otimes S(*))=0,&\end{array}\right.

Nevertheless, Horrocks criterion can be generalized in a second way, by describing the class of ACM bundles on quadrics.

.

Characterization of ACM bundles on Quadrics, Knörrer,
[

Let

\textrm{Splitting Criterion on\ }Q_{n}\Longleftarrow\textrm{Kn\"{o}rrer Characterization}

His technique, by successive “killing of

1.4.
Grassmannians and beyond, the appearance of many ACM bundles

It remained an open problem to characterize ACM bundles on Grassmannians.
Since the universal and the quotient bundle are ACM one could at first hope that they are the only ones or just a few ones.
The following result by Arrondo, Graña in 1999 destroyed this hope.

Arrondo - Grana (1999) prove that ACM bundles on
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{4})
make big families,
in particular there exist
non homogeneous
ACM bundles.

Theorem 1.12

(Arrondo, Graña)
.

[
AG
]

There are families of positive dimension of vector bundles
E
on ACM bundles on
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{4})
.
Some of these examples are non homogeneous under
SL(5)
-action.

This result is a turning point in the story since shows that the structure of ACM bundles can be in general
quite complicated.
It is worth that the class of ACM bundles on
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{4})
is explicitly described, up to extensions of known bundles.

What is in embryo in
[
AG
]
is the concept that
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{4})
is wild, that is, it carries families of ACM bundles with arbitrarily large dimension. This was understood later and popularized by Hartshorne, see the section
3
.
Wilderness of any Grassmannian different from projective spaces and
Gr({\mathbb{P}}^{1},{\mathbb{P}}^{3})
was definively settled by Costa and Mirò-Roig in
[
CMR16
]
.

2.1.
The Grothendieck groups and a bit of K-theory

Let
X
be a smooth variety. Let
K^{0}(X)
(resp.
K_{0}(X)
) be the free group generated by isomorphism classes of vector bundles (resp. coherent
{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
-modules) modulo the relation

[A]-[B]+[C]=0

when we have the exact sequence

0\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}A\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}B\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}C\smash{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits}0

.

K^{0}(X)
is called the Grothendieck group of vector bundles on
X
and
K_{0}(X)
is called the Grothendieck group of coherent sheaves on
X
.

The following is one of the main results by Grothendieck in his SGA6, we recommend the readable proof by Murre in
[
Mur
, §2.10]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Grothendieck)
.

(1)

K^{0}(X)=K_{0}(X)

(2)

the Chern character gives a ring isomorphism

ch\colon K^{0}(X)\otimes{\mathbb{Q}}\to CH(X)_{{\mathbb{Q}}}

where
CH(X)_{{\mathbb{Q}}}
is the rational Chow ring.

For any rational homogeneous variety
X=G/P
, every element of the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
gives an element of the tangent space at
[P]

which, with
G
-action, defines a section of
TX
. It is a well known fact that all sections are defined in this way,
that is
H^{0}(TX)={\mathfrak{g}}
.

This fact is important since it allows to compute the zero locus of a general section of
TX
, hence, by Gauss-Bonnet Theorem,
the topological Euler characteristic
\chi(X)
. It is well known that the category of
G
-bundles on
G/P
is equivalent to the category of
P
-modules. The tangent bundle
T(G/P)
is associated to an
irreducible
representation of
P

just in a few cases which are called irreducible Hermitian symmetric. They are the three classical cases listed in Proposition
2.2
, smooth quadrics (where
\chi Q_{n}
is equal to
n+1
if
n
is odd and to
n+2
if
n
is even)
and just two exceptional cases for the groups
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
that we skip for simplicity. The following result is well known (indeed all Betti numbers are known),
but we think it is worth to expose the simple and unified proof for these three cases.

Proposition 2.2
.

(1)

\mathrm{rank}K_{0}\left(Gr(k,n)\right)=\chi\left(Gr(k,n)\right)={n\choose k}

(2)

\mathrm{rank}K_{0}\left(LG(n+1)\right)=\chi LG(n+1)=2^{n}\left(\textrm{see}(\ref{eq:LGk})\right)

(3)

\mathrm{rank}K_{0}\left(Spin_{n}\right)=\chi Spin_{n}=2^{n}\left(\textrm{see}(\ref{eq:spinor})\right)

Proof:

In all three cases a section is given by a map
A\colon V\to V
.
In case (i) it is general, in case (ii) it preserves the quadric
Q
(that is
AQ+QA^{t}=0
), in case (iii)
it preserves the skew-symmetric form
J
(that is
AJ+JA^{t}=0
).
We may assume, by genericity, that
A
has
n
distinct eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalues.
In case (i), the section
A
vanishes on the linear spaces
{\mathbb{C}}^{k}

which are span of
k
among the
n
eigenvalues, they are
{n\choose k}
.
In case (ii), we have the further restriction that the subspace is isotropic.
The eigenvectors of
A
satisfy
vQv^{t}=0
.
We may assume we have an orthonormal basis
\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\right\}

with
Q(e_{i},f_{j})=\delta_{ij}
,
Q(e_{i},e_{j})=Q(f_{i},f_{j})=0
.
The isotropic subspaces are generated by choosing exactly one between
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots n
,
so we have
n
binary choices and
2^{n}
subspaces.
Hence the isotropic
C^{n}
are
2^{n}
(
2^{n-1}
in one family) and the isotropic
C^{k}
are
{n\choose k}2^{k}
.
In case (iii) the construction is analogous. Now all vectors are isotropic,
in particular the eigenvectors are isotropic.
