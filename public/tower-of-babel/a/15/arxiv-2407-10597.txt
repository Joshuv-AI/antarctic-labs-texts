Multilevel Regularized Newton Methods with Fast Convergence Rates Thanks: Submitted to the editors July 2024.
Nick Tsipinakis, Panos Parpas

Abstract
We introduce new multilevel methods for solving large-scale unconstrained optimization problems. Specifically, the philosophy of multilevel methods is applied to Newton-type methods that regularize the Newton sub-problem using second-order information from a coarse (low dimensional) sub-problem. The new regularized multilevel methods provably converge from any initialization point and enjoy faster convergence rates than Gradient Descent. In particular, for arbitrary functions with Lipschitz continuous Hessians, we show that their convergence rate interpolates between the rate of Gradient Descent and that of the cubic Newton method. If, additionally, the objective function is assumed to be convex, then the proposed method converges with the fast 𝒪 ⁡ ( k − 2 ) \mathcal{O}(k^{-2}) rate. Hence, since the updates are generated using a coarse model in low dimensions, the theoretical results of this paper significantly speed-up the convergence of Newton-type or preconditioned gradient methods in practical applications. Preliminary numerical results suggest that the proposed multilevel algorithms are significantly faster than current state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction

Let
f_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a twice differentiable function. In this work we are interested in solving the unconstrained optimization problem:

\mathbf{x}_{H}^{*}=\underset{\mathbf{x}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}{\operatorname{arg\ min}}{f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})}.

(1)

The state-of-the-art second-order methods for solving (
1
) are the Newton method
[
14
]
, Cubic Newton methods
[
15
,
17
]
and regularized Newton methods
[
3
,
13
]
. Among them, the pure Newton method has a rich and powerful theory that shows that the pure Newton method has a quadratic convergence rate. Unfortunately, its rich theory can only be established locally, in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{x}_{H}^{*}
. Imposing extra assumptions, such as strict convexity, it is possible to show global rates using a damping parameter (damped Newton method).
However, the damped Newton method has typically worse convergence rate than the
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
of Gradient Descent
[
16
]
, where
k
is the iteration number. The Cubic Newton method effectively addresses the theoretical limitations of Newton’s method by adding a regularization term on the diagonal of the Hessian matrix. Assuming only the Lipschitz continuity of the Hessian matrix, the Cubic Newton method has the global state-of-the-art
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3/2})
convergence rate (for gradient norm minimization), where
\varepsilon>0
is the algorithm’s tolerance. In addition, for general convex functions, the method enjoys the fast
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate (for function residual minimization). Both rates are better than those of Gradient Descent (
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
and
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
, respectively), justifying the extra computational cost imposed by forming the Hessian matrix and solving the resulting system of linear equations to obtain the Newton direction. Even though, these fast rates are achieved by solving a sub-problem at each iteration (which adds an extra computational cost to the Newton method), the Cubic Newton method is considered the method of choice when solving medium-sized non-convex problems. On the other hand, when minimizing general convex functions, it possible to show the same fast rates using gradient regularization
[
3
,
13
]
. These methods do not need to solve the cubically regularized sub-problem and thus are faster than the Cubic Newton.

The main limitations of the classical second-order methods emerge when
N
is very large, which typically arises in modern optimization problems. Under this regime, Newton-type methods either drastically slow-down or, even worse, they cannot be applied due to memory issues. Therefore, to take advantage of the valuable second-order information in large-scale optimization, multilevel or subspace methods offer an attractive alternative [1, 18, 19]. These methods effectively transfer the computations of the search directions in a
coarse
level (subspace) where forming and inverting the Hessian matrix can be achieved efficiently. However, these methods, as we discuss in detail in the next section, suffer from the same limitations as the pure Newton method. In particular, they typically obtain worse global convergence rates than Gradient Descent. It is even difficult to establish local convergence rates that are faster than
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
, and therefore their extra computational cost compared to Gradient Descent cannot be justified.
The objective of this paper is to address the computational limitations of regularized Newton-type methods using a multilevel or randomized optimization framework. The proposed approaches retain the robust theoretical guarantees of these methods, including rapid global convergence rates, even for non-convex problems, thereby maintaining their advantage over first-order methods.

To achieve the desired rates, the deterministic multilevel approach requires computing a trial step at each iteration to dynamically assess efficiency each iterate according to a principled rule. Although this trial step may increase the computational cost relative to classical multilevel methods, the theory is important because it establishes convergence rates comparable to those of exact Newton methods and it provides a paradigm for designing reduced second-order methods with such fast convergence rates. Building on these insights, we develop a family of randomized regularized Newton methods that removes the need for a trial step while preserving the theoretical advantages of the deterministic multilevel method introducing a positive probability.

1.1
The Conventional Multilevel Method and other Related Work

We present the main components of the conventional (Newton-type) multilevel method
[
11
,
19
,
21
]
. In the multilevel literature the subscripts
H
and
h
are used to denote quantities in the fine (original) or coarse level, respectively, e.g.,
f_{H}
will be the fine model subject to minimization in problem (
1
) and
f_{h}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
n<N
, will be the coarse model.
Further, we define
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times n}
and
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N}
as the prolongation and restriction operators, respectively. These operators will be used to transfer the information between the coarse and fine levels. As such, given a point
\mathbf{x}_{H,k}
, at iteration
k
, the initial point in the coarse level will be denoted as
\mathbf{x}_{h,0}\coloneqq\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}_{H,k}
. Then the coarse model is defined as follows:

f_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{h})\coloneqq\langle\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H}),\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}_{h,0}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}_{h,0}),\mathbf{x}_{h}-\mathbf{x}_{h,0}\rangle.

(2)

If the objective function is strongly convex, then
\mathbf{R}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{H})\mathbf{P}\succ 0
, and thus the coarse model has a unique solution.

Let
\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*}
be its minimum point and define the vector
\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*}-\mathbf{x}_{h,0}
. Note that
\mathbf{d}_{h,k}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and needs to be
prolongated
to the original space. In particular, the
coarse direction
is defined by prolongating
\mathbf{d}_{h,k}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{h}^{*}-\mathbf{x}_{h,0})
. Putting these concepts all together, the conventional multilevel method performs the following updates:

\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{H,k+1}&=\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-t_{H,k}\mathbf{P}_{k}\underset{\mathbf{d}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\operatorname{min}}\left\{\langle\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{H}),\mathbf{d}_{h}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{H})\mathbf{P}\mathbf{d}_{h},\mathbf{d}_{h}\rangle\right\}\\
&=\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-t_{H,k}\mathbf{P}_{k}[\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\mathbf{P}_{k}]^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k}),\end{split}

(3)

where
t_{H,k}>0
is the step size parameter.
The analysis of multilevel methods is typically performed for fixed
\mathbf{R}_{k}
, say
\mathbf{R}
, for all iterations
k
. It is known that the coarse directions may not always be effective. Ineffective directions occur when
\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\in\operatorname{null}(\mathbf{R})
which implies
\mathbf{d}_{H}=0
. This issue is addressed by alternating between the fine and coarse levels when computing the search directions according to some appropriate conditions. Let
\mu\in(0,\min(\|\mathbf{R}\|,1)]
and
\varepsilon>0
. The coarse steps will be used if

\|\mathbf{R}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\|\geq\mu\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\|\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{R}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\|>\varepsilon.

(4)

On the other hand, if the conditions above are violated, which imply
\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\approx 0
, the conventional multilevel method computes the direction in the fine level, which is equivalent to performing the (damped) Newton method. In total, the cost of a single iteration for the multilevel method is
\mathcal{O}(nN+n^{3})
which is much lower than the
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}+N^{3})
iteration cost of the Newton method.

Even though the conventional multilevel method is able to mitigate the computational limitations of second-order methods, by construction, it inherits all the theoretical drawbacks of the Newton method. That is, superior convergence rates to Gradient Descent can only be established locally, while global rates are not better
[
11
,
19
]
. Other works on multilevel methods fail to improve the rate of convergence of Gradient Descent even locally
[
7
,
18
,
21
]
. Another limitation, besides the unsatisfactory rates of convergence, is that
their updates (in
eq.

3
) are not well-defined for solving problem (
1
) in the absence of strong convexity. Even when convexity is present, multilevel or subspace methods based on randomization fail to improve the
\mathcal{O}(k^{-1})
convergence rate of Gradient Descent
[
2
,
1
]
.
Similar limitations are observed in several randomized sub-space methods. In particular, methods such as
[
6
,
10
]
suffer from the same drawbacks as the conventional multilevel method since they require positive definite Hessians. Moreover, although the stochastic sub-space cubic Newton method
[
9
]
alleviates this assumption, requiring positive semi-definite Hessians, the
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate is attained only when
n=N
.

1.2
Contributions

In view of the related work, we summarize the contributions of this paper:

1.

We introduce a family of regularized multilevel methods in order to speed-up the convergence of second-order methods for large-scale optimization.

2.

The resulting regularized multilevel methods are analyzed for non-convex functions with Lipschitz continuous Hessians and for general convex functions. We show global convergence results in both cases. Furthermore, for non-convex functions with Lipschitz continuous Hessians, we show that the convergence rate of the proposed methods interpolates between the rate of Gradient Descent and the rate of the Cubic Newton method. In addition, for general convex functions, the regularized Newton-type multilevel method enjoys the fast
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
convergence rate of Cubic Newton method.

3.

When
\mathbf{R}
is a (particular) random matrix, we provide probabilistic results that allow the proposed methods to always perform iterations in the coarse level. Thus, such results further reduce the computational cost of the proposed methods. In the randomized case we show that it is possible to achieve the fast rate of
[
13
]
, under an appropriate choice of
\mathbf{R}
(
definition

11
) and specific problem structures.

4.

We perform numerical experiments on real-world problems to show that the regularized multilevel methods achieve comparable rates compared to the Cubic Newton method. Moreover, we illustrate that the proposed methods are significantly faster in wall-clock time against both Gradient Descend and the Cubic Newton method.

Regularized Multilevel Algorithms

If
eq.

1
is not convex, then the coarse model in
eq.

2
of the conventional multilevel method is not suitable since the reduced Hessian matrix may not be positive definite or invertible. As a result, in order to obtain an effective coarse direction, one needs to replace the reduced Hessian matrix with an appropriate approximation. Let
\mathbf{B}_{h}\coloneqq\mathbf{B}_{h}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, be a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix and
\alpha_{k}>0
. Then, we substitute
\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\mathbf{P}_{k}
in
eq.

2
with
\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n}\succ 0
(in
section

4
we will discuss possible candidates for
\mathbf{B}_{h,k}
), and thus the update rule of the regularized multilevel method can be derived by minimizing the coarse model, which implies

\mathbf{x}_{H,k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k}).

(5)

Note that, unlike the conventional method does, the regularized multilevel method does not require a step-size parameter. Instead, it adds a positive scalar to the diagonal of
\mathbf{B}_{k}
. Therefore, a suitable
\alpha_{k}>0
should be added to ensure a sufficient decrease in the value of the objective function at each iteration. Since
\mathbf{B}_{h,k}
is an approximation of
\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\mathbf{P}_{k}
it is natural to define the error of this estimation

s_{h}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{H}}\|\mathbf{B}_{h}-\mathbf{R}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\mathbf{P}\|.

(6)

As with the standard multilevel method, one should alternate between the coarse and fine levels whenever
\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})=0
to ensure the progress of scheme (
5
).
Note that, unlike
eq.

4
, where a fixed
\mathbf{R}_{k}
is used, here we allow
\mathbf{R}_{k}
to vary at each iteration, thereby enhancing the algorithm’s applicability. To achieve fast convergence rates we introduce an additional condition alongside
eq.

4
. In particular, given a sequence of
\mathbf{R}_{k}
, we use the following conditions to determine the space the search direction will be computed

\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\geq\mu\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|\geq\mu\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|,

(7)

where
\mu\in(0,\min\{\|\mathbf{R}_{k-1}\|,\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\|,1\})
.
Since the second inequality in (
7
) depends on
k+1
iterate, evaluating it requires computing a trial step at each iteration. The new condition emerges from our theory and we will see later that it effectively ensures the algorithm’s rapid convergence.
If conditions
eq.

7
are satisfied we use
eq.

5
, otherwise we use,

\mathbf{x}_{H,k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-(\mathbf{B}_{H,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k}),

(8)

where
\mathbf{B}_{H,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{B}_{H,k}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a symmetric positive semi-definite approximation of the true Hessian matrix.
As a result, when the new condition is rejected, the method will compute both coarse and the full regularized Newton step at an iteration
k
. In such scenario the method forms expensive iterates. We address this issue in section 4.

As in
eq.

6
, we define the estimation error in the original space

s_{H}\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}_{H}}\|\mathbf{B}_{H}-\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})\|.

(9)

On the other hand, if the problem is convex it is natural to employ the (reduced) Hessian matrix.
That is,

\mathbf{x}_{H,k+1}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})\mathbf{P}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k}),&\text{if \lx@cref{creftype~refnum}{eq: conditions new} holds}\\
\mathbf{x}_{H,k}-(\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H,k}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(10)

Observe that if
n=N
then we only use the second case in
eq.

10
above (since
\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{I}_{N}
).
Further, the reduced or exact Hessian matrix should be preferred in the convex setting because a faster convergence rate is expected due to the fact that
s_{h}=s_{H}=0
. The regularized multilevel algorithms for non-convex and convex settings are presented in
algorithm

1
and
algorithm

2
, respectively. Further, the algorithms require knowledge of either
s_{H},s_{h}
or the Lipschitz constant
L
for computing
\alpha_{k}
, however these parameters are unknown in practice. Nevertheless, a simple line-search process can be applied. We will discuss these practical details in
section

Convergence Analysis

In this section we present the convergence analysis of Algorithms
1
and
2
and derive the rates of convergence. Throughout this section, a vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be denoted with bold lower-case letters while a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with upper-case bold letters. Further,
\mathbf{I}_{p}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, denotes the
p\times p
identity matrix. To simplify the notation, we discard the subscript
H
from now onwards. In particular, the quantities
\mathbf{x},f(\mathbf{x}),\nabla f(\mathbf{x})
and
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})
will be assigned to
\mathbf{x}_{H},f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H}),\nabla f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})
and
\nabla^{2}f_{H}(\mathbf{x}_{H})
, respectively, unless noted otherwise. We use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the standard Euclidean norm of a vector or a matrix. It holds
\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}
and
\|\mathbf{A}\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A})}}
where
\lambda_{\max}
corresponds to the largest eigenvalue of a square matrix
[
12
]
. For two symmetric matrices
\mathbf{B},\mathbf{D}
we write
\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{D}\succeq 0
if and only if
\langle\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{x}\rangle\geq 0.

3.1
Main inequalities and assumptions

Our theory requires that the objective function has Lipschitz continuous Hessian matrices.

Assumption 1
.

There exist a scalar
L>0
such that for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
it holds

\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{y})\|\leq L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|.

We also make the following standard assumption on the operators.

Assumption 2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It holds
\mathbf{R}_{k}=\mathbf{P}_{k}^{T},\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{R}_{k})=n
and the sequence
(\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\|)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded from above.

The above assumption on
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{R}
is typical and is satisfied by a number of choices that we discuss in
section

4
. Furthermore,
Assumption

2
implies that there exist some
\omega\in(0,\infty)
such that

\omega\coloneqq\sup\{\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\|\ |\ k\in\mathbb{N}\}.

(11)

We further define the following quantities:

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\coloneqq[(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{h,k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}_{H,k}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

Note that
\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq 0
and it is a quantity analogous to the Newton decrement
[
16
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{H,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
eq.

5
. Then

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}

(12)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{d}_{H,k},\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq(s_{h}-\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}+\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}

(13)

Proof.

The equality in
eq.

12
follows directly from the definitions of
\mathbf{d}_{H,k}
and
\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})
. As for
eq.

13
, we have that
\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{d}_{H,k},\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\rangle

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}-\mathbf{B}_{h,k})(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{h,k}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}-\mathbf{B}_{h,k})(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\quad-\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq s_{h}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}+\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}-\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}

where for the last inequality we used
\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{A}\|\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}
, in particular,

\displaystyle(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))^{T}(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}-\mathbf{B}_{h,k})(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq s_{h}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2},

and thus (
13
) has been proved.

The following bounds will be useful in our analysis several times:

\begin{split}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|\leq\|(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\|\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\leq\frac{\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{\alpha_{k}},\ \|\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|\leq\omega\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|,\end{split}

(14)

where for the result on the left we have used that if
0\prec\mathbf{A}\preceq\mathbf{B}
, then
\|\mathbf{A}\|\leq\|\mathbf{B}\|
. The next result justifies the choice of
\alpha_{k}
in

Lemma 2
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and suppose that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by
eq.

5
. If
\alpha_{k}\geq s_{h}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}}
, then

\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|\leq 2\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|.

Proof.

It holds

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{B}_{h,k}\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|+\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\|(\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}-\mathbf{B_{k}})\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|+\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\omega\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+\mathbf{d}_{H,k})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|+(s_{h}+\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle=\omega\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\left(\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t\mathbf{d}_{H,k})-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\right)\mathsf{d}t\ \mathbf{d}_{H,k}\right\|+(s_{h}+\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\omega\int_{0}^{1}\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k}+t\mathbf{d}_{H,k})-\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\mathsf{d}t\ \|\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|+(s_{h}+\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Ass. }\ref{ass: lipschitz cont}}{\leq}\frac{\omega L\|\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|^{2}}{2}+(s_{h}+\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{ineq: upper bound d_H})}{\leq}\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}}{2}+(s_{h}+\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{ineq: upper bound d_H})}{\leq}\left(\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2\alpha_{k}}+s_{h}+\alpha_{k}\right)\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|\leq 2\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|.

The next result shows that the sequence
(f(\mathbf{x}_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{k}
as in
eq.

5
, is non-increasing given
\alpha_{k}
in
algorithm

.

Lemma 3
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and suppose that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in N}
is generated by
eq.

5
. If

\alpha_{k}\geq s_{h}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}}

Proof.

By
Assumption

1
it can be shown that (see
[

]
for a proof)

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\ \ +\frac{L}{6}\|\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}_{k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle=f(\mathbf{x}_{k})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{d}_{H,k},\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\rangle+\frac{L}{6}\|\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|^{3}.

Using

12

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq-\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}[(s_{h}-\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}+\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}]+\frac{L}{6}\|\mathbf{d}_{H,k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{ineq: upper bound d_H})}{\leq}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(s_{h}-\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}+\frac{\omega^{3}L}{6}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{3}

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{ineq: upper bound d_H})}{\leq}-\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(s_{h}-\alpha_{k})\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}+\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{6\alpha_{k}}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_{k}-s_{h}-\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{3\alpha_{k}}\right)\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{2},

where the last inequality follows from our assumption on
\alpha_{k}

\alpha_{k}-s_{h}-\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{3\alpha_{k}}>0
.

Moreover, from
\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n}\succeq\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n},
we have that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}\preceq\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\mathbf{I}_{n}

\displaystyle\iff\mathbf{x}^{T}\left((\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-1}-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}\mathbf{I}_{n}\right)\mathbf{x}\leq 0,\ \text{for all}\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Thus, setting
\mathbf{x}\coloneqq(\mathbf{B}_{h,k}+\alpha_{k}\mathbf{I}_{n})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})

\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{\alpha_{k}}

(15)

3.2
The Non-Convex Case

In this section we show global convergence of a subsequence of
algorithm

1
to a stationary point of
f
and derive its convergence rate. We will need the following definition

(\mathrm{g}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\coloneqq(\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}.

Next, we combine the results in (
15
) and
lemma

3
to obtain a reduction in the value of the objective function in terms of the norm of the gradient.

Corollary 4
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold and suppose that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in N}
is generated by
eq.

5
. If

\alpha_{k}\geq s_{h}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}}
, then

f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{\mu^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|^{2}}{8\alpha_{k}}.

Proof.

By
lemma

3
and
lemma

2
we have that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\geq\frac{\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2}}{2}\overset{(\ref{ineq: lower bound lamda})}{\geq}\frac{\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|^{2}}{2}\geq\frac{\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|^{2}}{8\alpha_{k}}\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|^{2}}{8\alpha_{k}}

where the last inequality holds because we generate the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

in the coarse
level and thus (
7
) is satisfied.

Remark 5
.

If we additionally assumed that
(\mathrm{g}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded, then the result of
corollary

4
could be used to prove that
(\mathrm{g}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a null sequence. That is, by telescoping the above bound and using
\alpha_{k}\coloneqq s_{h}+\sqrt{\omega^{3}\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}
we get

f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})\geq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{\mu^{2}\mathrm{g}_{i+1}^{2}}{8\left(s_{h}+\sqrt{\omega^{3}\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{i}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)}.

Therefore, as
k\rightarrow\infty
, the series converges, which together with the boundness
(\mathrm{g}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of imply that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathrm{g}_{k}=0
. Thus, the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, constructed in
eq.

5
, converges to a stationary point of problem (
1
) from any initialization point
\mathbf{x}_{0}
.

Unfortunately, even under this assumption, the convergence rate of
(\mathrm{g}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is unknown. Nevertheless, we can derive a rate of convergence for one of its subsequences, namely

\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*}\coloneqq\min_{0\leq i\leq k}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{i})\|.

The proof of the next result is parallel to that of
[
3
]
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathrm{g}_{i}\geq\varepsilon
for all
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
. Then

k\leq\left\lfloor\frac{8(f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f^{*})}{\mu^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{s_{h}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+2\ln{\frac{\mathrm{g}_{0}}{\varepsilon}}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

From
corollary

4
and since
\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\leq\omega\mathrm{g}_{k}
we take

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\mathrm{g}_{k+1}^{2}}{8\left(s_{h}+\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{k+1}}{\mathrm{g}_{k}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\left(s_{h}\mathrm{g}_{k}^{-2}+\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{k}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{k+1}}{\mathrm{g}_{k}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\left(s_{h}\varepsilon^{-2}+\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)},

where in the last inequality we used the assumption
\mathrm{g}_{k}\geq\varepsilon
. Define

C_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\frac{\mu^{2}}{8\left(s_{h}\varepsilon^{-2}+\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2}}\right)}.

Telescoping the previous bound we obtain

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})\geq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq C_{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{i+1}}{\mathrm{g}_{i}}\right)^{2}.

Using the inequalities

\exp(x)\geq 1+x\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\geq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}

we have that

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f(\mathbf{x}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq C_{\varepsilon}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{i+1}}{\mathrm{g}_{i}}\right)^{2}\geq C_{\varepsilon}k\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{i+1}}{\mathrm{g}_{i}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}=C_{\varepsilon}k\left(\frac{\mathrm{g}_{k}}{\mathrm{g}_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}\geq C_{\varepsilon}k\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{g}_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{k}}

\displaystyle=C_{\varepsilon}k\exp\left(\frac{2}{k}\ln\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{g}_{0}}\right)\geq C_{\varepsilon}k\left(1+\frac{2}{k}\ln\frac{\varepsilon}{\mathrm{g}_{0}}\right)

and thus, solving for
k
, the result follows.

The result in
lemma

6
is key for obtaining the convergence rate. Effectively, it reveals the first iteration
k
, (smallest
k\in\mathbb{N}
), such that
\mathrm{g}_{k}<\varepsilon
. It is now easy to obtain the convergence rate of the subsequence
(\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 7
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Suppose also that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by (
5
) and

\alpha_{k}=s_{h}+\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}},

where
s_{h}
is defined in
eq.

6
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
and for

K_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{8(f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f^{*})}{\mu^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{s_{h}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+2\ln{\frac{\mathrm{g}_{0}}{\varepsilon}}\right\rfloor+1

we have that
\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*}<\varepsilon
for all
k\geq K_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. From
lemma

6
it holds directly that if
k\geq K_{\varepsilon}
, then there exists at least one
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}
such that
\mathrm{g}_{i}<\varepsilon
. Thus, by the definition of
\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*}
we have that
\mathrm{g}_{K_{\varepsilon}}^{*}<\varepsilon
. Since also
(\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, it holds
\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*}<\varepsilon
for all
k>K_{\varepsilon}
, which completes the proof.

theorem

7
shows that if all the iterations were performed in the coarse level, then we could reach tolerance
\varepsilon>0
after at least
K_{\varepsilon}
iterations. The convergence rate in
theorem

7
effectively interpolates between the convergence rate of gradient descent and cubic Newton algorithms (plus a logarithmic constant) according to the values of
s_{h}
and
\mu
. For instance, if
s_{h}
is large then the method converges in
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
iterations, while if
s_{h}=0
the method converges in
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-\frac{3}{2}})
iterations.
As for
\mu
, recall that it is a user-defined parameter which controls the number of steps taken between the coarse and fine levels. Therefore, the user has access to the rate of convergence by tuning
\mu
, nevertheless that large values in
\mu
are more likely to require (expensive) iterations with the full model. We complete the convergence analysis of
algorithm

1
by providing a similar result, but now all iterations are performed in the fine level.

Theorem 8
.

Let
Assumption

1
hold. Suppose also that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by
eq.

8
and

\alpha_{k}=s_{H}+\sqrt{\frac{L\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}},

where
s_{H}
is defined in
eq.

9
. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
and

K_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\left\lfloor 8(f(\mathbf{x}_{0})-f^{*})\left(\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}}+\frac{s_{H}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)+2\ln{\frac{\mathrm{g}_{0}}{\varepsilon}}\right\rfloor+1

we have that
\mathrm{g}_{k}^{*}<\varepsilon
for all

.

The proof of the theorem can be obtained by simply replacing

with
s_{H}
and
\mathbf{R}
with
\mathbf{I}_{N}
in

, and thus it is omitted. Hence, Theorems
7
and
8
provide the complete picture of the convergence rate of
algorithm

1
. Further, we emphasize that this rate is better than that of Gradient Descent, which requires
\mathcal{O}(\frac{M}{\varepsilon^{2}})
iterations, where
M>0
such that
\|\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x})\|\leq M
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. This is because

and

3.3
The Convex Case

In this section we additionally assume that problem
eq.

1
is convex and analyze
algorithm

2
with the new assumption. Recall that the results in
lemma

1
,
lemma

2
and
lemma

3
require only Assumptions
1
and
2
. Since here the reduced Hessian is positive semi-definite the results of the aforementioned lemmas hold for
\mathbf{B}_{h,k}\coloneqq\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\mathbf{P}_{k}
. In this case it holds
s_{h}=0
. We immediately obtain the following results.

Corollary 9
.

Let
f
be a convex function and Assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Suppose also that the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in N}
is generated by the first equation in
eq.

10
. If

\alpha_{k}\geq\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}}
, then the following inequality are satisfied

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|\leq 2\alpha_{k}\|\mathbf{d}_{h,k}\|,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{1}{2}\hat{\lambda}(\mathbf{x}_{k})^{2},

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\frac{\mu^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|^{2}}{8\alpha_{k}}.

Let
f^{*}\coloneqq f(\mathbf{x}^{*})
and further define

R\coloneqq\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\{\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|:f(\mathbf{x})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{0})\}.

(16)

Applying the proof technique of
[
13
, Theorem 1]
to our results we show
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{k^{2}})
convergence rate of
algorithm

2
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
f
be a convex function and assumptions
1
and
2
hold. Suppose also that
R<\infty
, the sequence
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by
algorithm

2
and set

\alpha_{k}=\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|}{2}}.

Then, there exists
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that convergence rate of
algorithm

2
is given by

f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-f^{*}\leq\frac{128^{2}\omega^{4}R^{3}L}{2\mu^{4}\left(1+\frac{3k}{4(\log_{2}\frac{\omega}{\mu}+3)}\right)^{2}},\ \ \text{for all}\ k\geq K_{0}

(17)

Proof.

Let us fix an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
First, we analyze the case where
algorithm

2
generates the updates always in the subspace, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by
eq.

5
. Then, by
corollary

9
and the fact that
\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|\leq\omega\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|
we have

f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\|^{2}}{8\|\mathbf{R}_{k}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{3}L}{2}}}\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\mathrm{g}_{k+1}^{2}}{8\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}},

(18)

where
\mathrm{g}_{k}\coloneqq\|\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\|
. Since the sequence
(f(\mathbf{x}_{m}))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, then by
eq.

16
it holds
\|\mathbf{x}_{m}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\|\leq R
, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, by convexity and the Cauchy Schwartz inequality we have that

f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-f^{*}\leq\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k}),\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}^{*}\rangle\leq R\mathrm{g}_{k}.

(19)

As in the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, we define the following index sets:
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}\coloneqq\{m\in\mathbb{N}:\mathrm{g}_{m+1}\geq\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{g}_{m}\}
and
\mathcal{I}_{k}\coloneqq\{m\in\mathcal{I}_{\infty}:m\leq k\}
and the rate of convergence depends on the size of
\mathcal{I}_{k}
.

Let
D_{k}\coloneqq\frac{3k}{2(\log_{2}\frac{\omega}{\mu}+3)}
. By the definition of
\mu
in (
4
) we get
0<D_{k}\leq\frac{k}{2}
, and thus
D_{k+1}\geq D_{k}
which implies
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}D_{k}=\infty
.

Case 1:

|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\geq D_{k}
. Then,
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}
contains infinitely many elements. Thus there exists a subsequence
(m_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathcal{I}_{\infty}=\{m_{0},m_{1},\ldots\}
.
Therefore, from (
18
) we get

f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}+1})\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}+1}^{2}}{8\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\geq\frac{\mu^{2}}{128\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}}^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Further,
m_{\ell+1}\geq m_{\ell}+1
and since
(f(\mathbf{x}_{m}))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing we have that

f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell+1}})\geq\frac{\mu^{2}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}+1}^{2}}{8\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\geq\frac{\mu^{2}}{128\omega^{2}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}}\mathrm{g}_{m_{\ell}}^{\frac{3}{2}}.

Next, let
C\coloneqq\frac{\mu^{2}}{128\omega^{2}R^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{L}{2}}}
and combine the above inequality with (
19
) to obtain

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell+1}})-f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})\leq-C[f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f^{*}]^{\frac{3}{2}}\iff

\displaystyle\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell+1}})-f^{*}}{C}\leq\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f^{*}}{C}-(f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}(f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell+1}}-f^{*})

Then, using the notation
\Delta_{\ell}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f^{*}}{C}}
we take
\Delta_{\ell+1}^{2}\leq\Delta_{\ell}^{2}-C^{\frac{3}{2}}\Delta_{\ell}^{3}
and note that
\Delta_{\ell}\leq\frac{1}{C^{\frac{3}{2}}}
, for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Using this we obtain the following inequality

\frac{1}{\Delta_{\ell+1}}-\frac{1}{\Delta_{\ell}}=\frac{\Delta_{\ell}-\Delta_{\ell+1}}{\Delta_{\ell}\Delta_{\ell+1}}=\frac{\Delta_{\ell}^{2}-\Delta_{\ell+1}^{2}}{\Delta_{\ell+1}\Delta_{\ell}(\Delta_{\ell}+\Delta_{\ell+1})}\geq C^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\Delta_{\ell}^{3}}{2\Delta_{\ell}^{3}}=\frac{C^{\frac{3}{2}}}{2}.

Next, by telescoping the last inequality we have that

\frac{1}{\Delta_{\ell}}\geq\frac{1}{\Delta_{0}}+\ell\frac{C^{\frac{3}{2}}}{2}\geq C^{\frac{3}{2}}+D_{k}\frac{C^{\frac{3}{2}}}{2},

where the last inequality holds from the assumption
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\geq D_{k}
and the lower bound on
\Delta_{0}
. Inverting this inequality we can derive the convergence rate for the first case. First we take

f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\ell}})-f^{*}\leq\frac{1}{[(1+\frac{D_{k}}{2})C]^{2}},\ \ \text{for all}\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}.

Then, since
|\mathcal{I}_{k}|\geq D_{k}
, there exist
\tilde{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{\tilde{\ell}}\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Thus,
m_{\tilde{\ell}}\leq k
and
f(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq f(\mathbf{x}_{m_{\tilde{\ell}}})

Example:
Let

6
Conclusions

We develop new multilevel methods that are based on the regularization of the Hessian matrix or an appropriate preconditioning matrix. We analyze these methods for arbitrary functions with Lipschitz continuous Hessian matrices and for general convex functions. For the former class of functions we prove a rate of convergence that interpolates between the rate of Gradient Descent and that of the Cubic Newton method, and we describe instances of the method and discuss problem structures such that our rates are expected to be faster than Gradient Descent. For general convex functions we show the fast
\mathcal{O}(k^{-2})
rate. Our numerical experiments verify the fast rates in real-world problems in both non-convex and convex settings.
