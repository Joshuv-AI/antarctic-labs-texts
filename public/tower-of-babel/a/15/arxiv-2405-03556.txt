On free bases of Banach spaces
Eva Pernecká, Jan Spěvák

Abstract
. We call a closed subset M M of a Banach space X X a free basis of X X if it contains the null vector and every Lipschitz map from M M to a Banach space Y Y , which preserves the null vectors can be uniquely extended to a bounded linear map from X X to Y Y . We then say that two complete metric spaces M M and N N are ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalent if they admit bi-Lipschitz copies M ′ M^{\prime} and N ′ N^{\prime} , respectively that are free bases of a common Banach space satisfying span ⁡ M ′ = span ⁡ N ′ \operatorname{span}M^{\prime}=\operatorname{span}N^{\prime} . In this note, we compare ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalence with some other natural equivalences on the class of complete metric spaces. The main result states that ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalent spaces must have the same Čech-Lebesgue covering dimension. In combination with the work of Godard, this implies that two complete metric spaces with isomorphic Lipschitz-free spaces need not be ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalent. Also, there exist non-homeomorphic ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalent metric spaces, and, in contrast with the covering dimension, the metric Assouad dimension is not preserved by ℳ ​ ℴ ​ 𝓁 \mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol} -equivalence.

1.
Introduction

Vector spaces throughout this paper are considered over the field
{\mathbb{R}}
of real numbers. The zero vector of a vector space
X
is denoted by
0_{X}
.
We say that two normed spaces are
isomorphic
provided that there exists a linear bijection between them which is simultaneously a homeomorphism. Similarly, we call two metric spaces
bi-Lipschitz isomorphic
if there exists a bijection between them that is Lipschitz and whose inverse is also Lipschitz. By covering dimension of a metric space we mean the Čech-Lebesgue covering dimension (or equivalently the large inductive dimension) of its underlining topological space.

We will now introduce two notions that will be central to our work.

Definition 1.1
.

Given a Banach space
X
, we say that its closed subset
M
is a

free basis
of
X
provided that
0_{X}\in{M}
and
for every Banach space
Y
and every Lipschitz map

which preserves zero vectors it is possible to uniquely extend

to a bounded linear map
\hat{f}:X\to Y
.

We note that every complete metric space is a free basis of some Banach space. This is thanks to the existence of free Banach spaces recalled at the beginning of Section
2
(see Remark
2.2
(i)).

Definition 1.2
.

Given two complete metric spaces

and

, we say that

and

are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent

and

of common Banach space such that

, and that

The notation of

\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent if and only if their corresponding normed spaces of

We will study the relations of

-equivalence to some other natural equivalences on the class of complete metric spaces. More specifically, we will deal with equivalences given by: bi-Lipschitz isomorphisms, homeomorphisms, covering dimension, and isomorphisms of the corresponding Lipschitz-free spaces (which we will refer to as

Obviously, a bi-Lipschitz isomorphism is a homeomorphism, and homeomorphisms preserve covering dimension. Also, bi-Lipschitz isomorphic spaces are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}

It is proved in

2.
Some basic properties of free bases and
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence

A pointed metric space is a metric space
M
with a distinguished base point
e_{M}\in M
. We consider a base point of a normed space to be its null vector.
Given a Banach space
Y
, we denote by
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M,Y})
the Banach space of all
Y
-valued Lipschitz maps that preserve the base points, endowed with the norm which assigns to each
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M,Y})
its Lipschitz number
L(f)
. As usually, we write
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
instead of
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M,{\mathbb{R}}})
.

Recall that given a pointed metric space
M
, the Lipschitz-free space
\mathcal{F}({M})
is the unique (up to a linear isometry) Banach space characterized by the following universal property (see
[
20
, Theorem 3.6]
):
There is an isometry
\delta:M\to\mathcal{F}({M})
which preserves the base points and for every Banach space
Y
and every
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M,Y})
, there is a unique bounded linear map
\bar{f}:\mathcal{F}({M})\to Y
making the following diagram commutative

(1)

\textstyle{M\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\delta}
\textstyle{\mathcal{F}({M})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\bar{f}}
\textstyle{Y,}

and such that
||{\bar{f}}||=L(f)
, where
L(f)
is the Lipschitz number of
f
. We will write
\delta_{M}
instead of
\delta
when we need to emphasize the underlying metric space
M
. Moreover, for
Y={\mathbb{R}}
the map that assigns to each
f
the bounded linear functional
\bar{f}
as in (
1
) is a linear isometry from
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
onto
\mathcal{F}({M})^{*}
(see for instance
[
20
, Theorem 3.3]
). The weak
∗

\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
induced by
\mathcal{F}({M})
agrees on bounded sets with the topology of point-wise convergence of functions.
It is a simple and folklore fact, that
\mathcal{F}({M})
does not depend on the choice of a base point of
M
.

Now we relate the notions of free bases and
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence to Lipschitz-free spaces. It is immediate in view of the extension property of the free bases and the universal property (
1
) of the Lipschitz-free spaces.

Observation 2.1
.

(i)

If
M
is a free basis of a Banach space
X
, then
X
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}({M})
.

(ii)

Two complete metric spaces
M,N
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent if and only if there exists an isomorphism between
\mathcal{F}({M})
and
\mathcal{F}({N})
that maps
\operatorname{span}\delta_{M}(M)
onto
\operatorname{span}\delta_{N}(N)
.

In the following remark, we summarize some basic properties of free bases and
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces that follow from the definitions and Observation
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

(i)

For a metric space
M
,
\delta(M)
is a free basis of
\mathcal{F}({M})
. Since
\mathcal{F}({M})
exists for every metric space
M
, it follows that every complete metric space can be isometrically embedded into some Banach space as its free basis.

(ii)

Complete metric spaces
M,N
are
\mathcal{F}
-equivalent if and only if they admit bi-Lipschitz copies
M^{\prime},N^{\prime}
, respectively, that are free bases of a common Banach space.

(iii)

By
[
20
, Corollary 3.46]
, infinite-dimensional reflexive Banach spaces do not admit free bases.

(iv)

Note that if
M
is a free basis of
X
, then there is
K>0
such that for every Lipschitz map
f
from
M
to a Banach space
Y
that preserves the base point, its bounded linear extension
\hat{f}:X\to Y
satisfies
||\hat{f}||\leq K\cdot L(f)
. Indeed, the extension
\hat{\delta}:X\to\mathcal{F}({M})
of the isometric embedding
\delta:M\to\mathcal{F}({M})
is an isomorphism of
X
and
\mathcal{F}({M})
. From (
1
) we get
\hat{f}=\bar{f}\circ\hat{\delta}
. Since
{||\bar{f}||}=L(f)
, it suffices to put
K=||{\hat{\delta}}||
. Thus the definition of a free basis can be expressed formally stronger by requiring the existence of such a
K>0
.

(v)

If
M
is a free basis of a Banach space
X
, then
M\setminus\{0_{X}\}
must be linearly independent and
\operatorname{span}{M}
is dense in
X
. Indeed, the first property follows from the existence of linear extensions of Lipschitz maps from
M
to the whole
X
(or just
\operatorname{span}M
), and the second follows from its uniqueness, both required by the definition of the free basis.

We proceed by characterizing the
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence of metric spaces in terms of the corresponding spaces of Lipschitz functions.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
M
and
N
be metric spaces such that there exists a linear isomorphism
T
from
\mathcal{F}({M})
onto
\mathcal{F}({N})
. Then
T(\delta_{M}(M))\subseteq\operatorname{span}(\delta_{N}(N))
if and only if the adjoint
T^{*}:\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({N})\to\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
is continuous in the topology of point-wise convergence.

Proof.

First assume that
T(\delta_{M}(M))\subseteq\operatorname{span}(\delta_{N}(N))
and consider a net
(f_{i})\subseteq\operatorname{Lip}_{0}(N)
(not necessarily bounded) that converges point-wise to some
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}(N)
. Fix
x\in M
. Then there exist
k\in{\mathbb{N}}

y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in N
,

such that
T(\delta_{M}(x))=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{n}\delta_{N}(y_{n})
and clearly

T^{*}(f_{i})(x)=\langle T(\delta_{M}(x)),f_{i}\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{n}f_{i}(y_{n})\underset{i}{\longrightarrow}\sum_{n=1}^{k}a_{n}f(y_{n})=T^{*}(f)(x).

Hence
T^{*}
is continuous with respect to the topologies of point-wise convergence.

For the converse, assume that there exists
x\in M
such that
T(\delta_{M}(x))\notin\operatorname{span}(\delta_{N}(N))
. For any finite
F\subseteq N
, find by the Hahn-Banach theorem an

such that
\langle T(\delta_{M}(x)),f_{F}\rangle=1
and
f_{F}\upharpoonright_{\operatorname{span}\{\delta_{N}(y):y\in F\}}=0
. Then
(f_{F})
, directed by the set inclusion, is a net in
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}(N)
that converges point-wise to the zero function in
\operatorname{Lip}_{0}(N)
. Indeed, for any

and any

we have
f_{F}(y)=\langle\delta_{N}(y),f_{F}\rangle=0
. However, for every finite

and

does not converge point-wise to

\operatorname{Lip}_{0}(M)
. So we conclude that if
T^{*}
is continuous in the topology of point-wise convergence, then
T(\delta_{M}(M))\subseteq\operatorname{span}(\delta_{N}(N))
.
∎

Proposition 2.4

Complete metric spaces

\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({N})

3.
Construction of
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces

In this section, we present two constructions of
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces that provide important examples.

The following fact is a part of
[
3
, Theorem 3.9 and Proposition 4.2]
translated to the language of free bases.

Fact 3.1
.

Let
M
be a free basis of a Banach space
X
. Define
\mu:M\to X
by

\mu(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0_{X}&\mbox{for $x=0_{X}$}\\
\frac{x}{\left|\left|x\right|\right|}&\mbox{for $x\in M\setminus\{0_{X}\}$}.\end{array}\right.

If
0_{X}
is an isolated point of
M
, then

(1)

\mu(M)
is a free basis of
X
(in particular,
\mu(M)
is complete);

(2)

\mu
is a homeomorphic embedding.

In particular, if
0_{X}
is an isolated point of
M
, then
M
and
\mu(M)
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent and homeomorphic metric spaces, and
\mu(M)
is bounded.

Remark 3.2
.

Fact
3.1
and Remark
2.2
(i) show that every unbounded complete metric space with an isolated point is
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent to a homeomorphic bounded complete metric space. Since bounded and unbounded metric spaces are not bi-Lipschitz isomorphic, this provides a source of examples of
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces that are not bi-Lipschitz isomorphic.

Now we proceed to building examples of
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces that are not even homeomorphic.

Similarly as in
[
20
]
, given two pointed metric spaces
M,N
, their sum
M\coprod N
is their disjoint union with glued base points, and the metric remains the same on
M
and
N
and we define the distance of each
x\in M
and

as
d(x,y)=d(x,e_{M})+d(e_{N},y)
. Following
[
20
]
, if
C
is a closed subset of a complete metric space
M
, then
\sim
stands for the equivalence relation on
M
such that
x\sim y
if
x=y
or if
x,y\in C
. We define the distance of two equivalence classes
[x],[y]
as
d([x],[y])=\min(d(x,y),d(x,C)+d(y,C))
. By
[
20
, Proposition 1.26]
this distance is a complete metric on the set
M/\sim
of the equivalence classes. We denote this quotient metric space by
M/C
. If
M
is a pointed metric space, we consider
M/C
to be a pointed metric space with a base point being the equivalence class which contains the base point of
M
.

The quotient metric spaces can be used to produce
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent metric spaces as follows. Fact
3.3
can be extracted from
[

Fact 3.3
.

Let
M
be a complete metric space and
\varphi:M\to M
a Lipschitz retraction. Pick a base point
e_{M}\in\varphi(M)
arbitrarily. Then
M
is
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent to
\varphi(M)\coprod(M/\varphi(M))
.

Proof.

Denote
q:M\to M/\varphi(M)
the natural quotient map. It is not difficult to verify that the mapping
T:\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({\varphi(M)\coprod(M/\varphi(M))})\to\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
defined by
T(f)=f\circ\varphi+f\circ q
is an isomorphism with the inverse given for a function
g\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M})
by

T^{-1}(g)(x)=\begin{cases}g(x)&\textup{ if }x\in\varphi(M),\\
g(y)-g(\varphi(y))&\textup{ if }x=[y]\in M/\varphi(M).\end{cases}

Moreover, it is a homeomorphism in the topology of point-wise convergence. We can now apply Proposition
2.4
to conclude the
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence.
∎

A counterpart of this construction for free bases of Banach spaces takes the following form.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
M
be a free basis of a Banach space

\pi:X\to X

\sigma:X\to\ker\pi

\sigma(x)=x-\pi(x)

If
\pi(M)\subseteq M

\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is a free basis of

.
In particular,
M

\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent.

Before proving the theorem, we show that the set
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is closed.

Lemma 3.5

The sets

Proof.

For this argument, we consider

.

To show that
\sigma(M)
is closed in
X
, we pick a Cauchy sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of elements of
\sigma(M)
arbitrarily and find its subsequence which converges to an element of
\sigma(M)
.

If
\left|\left|a_{n}\right|\right|\to 0
, then
a_{n}\to 0_{X}\in\sigma(M)
and we are done. Otherwise, there is
\varepsilon>0
and a subsequence
(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of
(a_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\left|\left|b_{n}\right|\right|>\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
pick
x_{n}\in M
such that
b_{n}=x_{n}-\pi(x_{n})
. Thus we have

(3)

\left|\left|b_{n}\right|\right|=\left|\left|x_{n}-\pi(x_{n})\right|\right|>\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

As
(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is Cauchy, we may fix
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\left|\left|b_{k}-b_{l}\right|\right|<\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }k,l>n_{0}.

From (
2
) and (
3
) we get

(4)

\left|\left|b_{k}-b_{l}\right|\right|=\left|\left|x_{k}-\pi(x_{k})-x_{l}+\pi(x_{l})\right|\right|=\left|\left|x_{k}-x_{l}\right|\right|+\left|\left|\pi(x_{k})-\pi(x_{l})\right|\right|\mbox{ for all }k,l>n_{0}.

Because
(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is Cauchy, so is
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
by (
4
). Since
M
is complete,
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has limit
x\in M
. Since
\pi
is bounded,
(\pi(x_{n}))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has limit
\pi(x)
. Hence
(b_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
has limit
x-\pi(x)=\sigma(x)\in\sigma(M)
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Now we can continue to prove the theorem above.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Pick a Banach space
Y
and
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M),Y})
arbitrarily.
Define
h:M\to Y
by

h(x)=f(\pi(x))+f(\sigma(x))\mbox{ for every }x\in M.

We claim that
h\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M,Y})
. Indeed, for
x,y\in M
we have

\left|\left|h(x)-h(y)\right|\right|=\left|\left|f(\pi(x))-f(\pi(y))+f(\sigma(x))-f(\sigma(y))\right|\right|\leq{L(f)(\left|\left|\pi\right|\right|+\left|\left|\sigma\right|\right|)}\left|\left|x-y\right|\right|.

Thus
h
can be extended to a bounded linear map
\hat{h}
on
X
.

Let us show, that the restriction of
\hat{h}
to
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is
f
.

Indeed, for
x\in\pi(M)
we have
\pi(x)=x
and consequently

\hat{h}(x)=h(x)=f(\pi(x))+f(\sigma(x))=f(x)+f(\sigma\circ\pi(x))=f(x)+f(0_{X})=f(x).

Similarly, given
x\in\sigma(M)
, there is
m\in M
such that
x=\sigma(m)=m-\pi(m)
. Thus

\hat{h}(x)=\hat{h}(m-\pi(m))=h(m)-h(\pi(m))=f(\pi(m))+f(\sigma(m))-f(\pi\circ\pi(m))-f(\sigma\circ\pi(m))=

=f(\pi(m))+f(x)-f(\pi(m))-f(0_{X})=f(x).

To summarize:
\hat{h}
is a bounded linear map which extends
f
, and this extension is unique because
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is linearly dense in
X
.
Since
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is a closed subset of
X
by Lemma
3.5
, this shows that
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
is a free basis of
X
.

Obviously,
\operatorname{span}M=\operatorname{span}(\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M))
. Hence
M
and
\pi(M)\cup\sigma(M)
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent.
∎

Using the quotient spaces, we can find examples demonstrating that
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces need not be homeomorphic.

Example 3.6
.

There are non-homeomorphic
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces.
Indeed, since
{\mathbb{R}}
is a Lipschitz retract of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, the spaces
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{R}}\coprod{\mathbb{R}}^{2}/{\mathbb{R}}
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent by Fact
3.3
.

Note that, by Corollary
4.4
, two
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces must have the same covering dimension. Nevertheless, the next example shows that Assouad dimension of a metric space is not preserved by
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence. This dimension was introduced in
[
7
]
. For the definition and its properties we refer the reader to
[
15
]
, where it is explained, that a metric space is doubling if and only if it has a finite Assouad dimension
[
15
, Facts 3.3]
. We shall also need the trivial facts, that every metric space with infinite bounded uniformly discrete subset has infinite Assouad dimension and that the Assouad dimension of natural numbers (with the usual metric) is equal to 1.

Example 3.7
.

There are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces such that one has Assouad dimension 1 and the other has Assouad dimension
\infty
. In particular, there are two
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent spaces one of which has the doubling property and the other one does not.

Indeed, if
M=\{0,x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\}
is an arbitrary bounded separable uniformly discrete space, then
\mathcal{F}({M})
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}({{\mathbb{N}}})
via the isomorphism
T:\mathcal{F}({M})\to\mathcal{F}({{\mathbb{N}}})
given by
T(\delta_{M}(x_{n}))=\delta_{{\mathbb{N}}}(n)-\delta_{{\mathbb{N}}}(n-1)
. So we can use Observation
2.1
(ii) to conclude that
M
and
{\mathbb{N}}
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent.

4.
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalence preserves the covering dimension

Given topological spaces
M
and
N
,we denote the fact that
F
is a mapping from
M
to the power set of
N

by
F:M\Rightarrow N
. We say that
F:M\Rightarrow N
is finite-valued, if
F(x)
is a finite subset of
N
for all
x\in M
. Further, we say that
F
is
lower semi-continuous

(abbreviated by
lsc
), if for every open
U\subseteq N
the set

\{x\in M:F(x)\cap U\neq\emptyset\}

is open in
M
.

As in
[
19
, Definition 3.3]
, given a linear basis
M
of a vector space
X
we define the finite-valued support function
\supp_{M}:X\Rightarrow M
by letting
\supp_{M}(a)
be the unique set
K_{a}\subseteq M
such that

a=\sum_{x\in K_{a}}r_{x}x\mbox{, where all $r_{x}$ are non-zero reals.}

Here we use the convention that sum over an empty set is the null vector.

The following relatively simple fact is a direct consequence of
[
19
, Proposition 5.6]
.

Fact 4.1
.

Let
M
be a linear basis of a vector space
X
and
\mathcal{T}
some topology on
X
. Assume that for every
\mathcal{T}\upharpoonright_{M}
-closed subset
A
of
M
and all
x\in M\setminus A
there is a continuous linear functional
f:(X,\mathcal{T})\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(A)\subseteq\{0\}
and
f(x)\neq 0
. Then
\supp_{M}:(X,\mathcal{T})\Rightarrow(M,\mathcal{T}\upharpoonright_{M})
is lsc.

[
18
, Corollary 5.4]

provides us with the following fact, where
\dim M
stands for the covering dimension of the metric space
M
.

Fact 4.2
.

For
i\in\{0,1\}
suppose that
M_{i}
is a perfectly normal space, and
F_{i}:M_{1-i}\Rightarrow M_{i}
is a finite-valued lower semi-continuous mapping. If for every
y\in M_{1}

there exists
x\in M_{0}
with
x\in F_{0}(y)
and
y\in F_{1}(x)
, then
\dim M_{1}\leq\dim M_{0}
.

Combining the above two facts, we can derive the following result about the correspondence of dimensions.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
X
be a vector space endowed with a topology
\mathcal{T}
. For
i\in\{0,1\}
let
M_{i}
be a linear basis of
X
which is perfectly normal with respect to the topology
\mathcal{T}
. Assume that for every
\mathcal{T}\upharpoonright_{M_{i}}
-closed subset
A
of
M_{i}
and all
x\in M_{i}\setminus A
there is a continuous linear functional
f:(X,\mathcal{T})\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
f(A)\subseteq\{0\}
and
f(x)\neq 0
. Then
\dim M_{0}=\dim M_{1}
.

Proof.

By Fact
4.1
, the support function
\supp_{M_{i}}:(X,\mathcal{T})\Rightarrow(M_{i},\mathcal{T}\upharpoonright_{M_{i}})
is lsc for
i\in\{0,1\}
. Denote by
F_{i}
the restrictions of
\supp_{M_{i}}
to
M_{1-i}
. It follows from the linear independence of
M_{i}
that for all
y\in M_{i}
there is
x\in M_{1-i}
such that
x\in F_{1-i}(y)
and
y\in F_{i}(x)
. Hence
\dim M_{i}\leq\dim M_{1-i}
for both
i\in\{0,1\}
by Fact
4.2
.
∎

Finally, we show that Theorem
4.3
applies to
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent complete metric spaces.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
M
and
N
are
\mathcal{M}\hskip-1.5pt\mathpzc{ol}
-equivalent complete metric spaces, then
\dim M=\dim N
.

Proof.

Since the covering dimension is preserved by bi-Lipschitz maps, we may and will assume that
M
and
N
are free bases of a common Banach space
Y
and
\operatorname{span}M=\operatorname{span}N
.

Put
X=\operatorname{span}M=\operatorname{span}N
,
M_{0}=M\setminus\{0_{X}\}
,
M_{1}=N\setminus\{0_{X}\}
and consider
X
with the norm topology inherited from
Y
. We claim that the conditions of Theorem
4.3
are satisfied. Indeed, for both
i\in\{0,1\}
,
M_{i}
is linearly independent by Remark
2.2
(v) and thus it is a linear basis of
X=\operatorname{span}M_{i}
. Moreover, given a closed subset
A\subset M_{i}
and
x\in M_{i}\setminus A
, there is a function
f\in\operatorname{Lip}_{0}({M_{i}\cup\{0_{X}\}})
such that
f(A)\subseteq\{0\}
and
f(x)=1
. Since
M_{i}\cup\{0_{X}\}
is a free basis, such a function can be extended to a bounded linear functional on
X
and the equality
\dim M_{0}=\dim M_{1}
follows.

To complete the proof it suffices to observe that given a point
x
of a perfectly normal space
M
(of a metric space in particular), we have
\dim M=\dim M\setminus\{x\}
. This folklore fact follows from the Countable Sum Theorem
[
9
, 3.1.8]
and from the Subspace Theorem for the Čech-Lebesgue dimension of perfectly normal spaces
[
9
, 2.1.3 and 3.1.19]
, and from the fact that open subsets of a perfectly normal space are
F_{\sigma}
-sets.
∎

.
