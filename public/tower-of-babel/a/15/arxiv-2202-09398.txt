Provably Private Distributed Averaging Consensus: An Information-Theoretic Approach
Mohammad Fereydounian, Aryan Mokhtari, Ramtin Pedarsani, Hamed Hassani

Abstract
In this work, we focus on solving a decentralized consensus problem in a private manner. Specifically, we consider a setting in which a group of nodes, connected through a network, aim at computing the mean of their local values without revealing those values to each other. The distributed consensus problem is a classic problem that has been extensively studied and its convergence characteristics are well-known. Alas, state-of-the-art consensus methods build on the idea of exchanging local information with neighboring nodes which leaks information about the users’ local values. We propose an algorithmic framework that is capable of achieving the convergence limit and rate of classic consensus algorithms while keeping the users’ local values private. The key idea of our proposed method is to carefully design noisy messages that are passed from each node to its neighbors such that the consensus algorithm still converges precisely to the average of local values, while a minimum amount of information about local values is leaked. We formalize this by precisely characterizing the mutual information between the private message of a node and all the messages that another adversary collects over time. We prove that our method is capable of preserving users’ privacy for any network without a so-called generalized leaf , and formalize the trade-off between privacy and convergence time. Unlike many private algorithms, any desired accuracy is achievable by our method, and the required level of privacy only affects the convergence time.

In this paper, we focus on a classic distributed computing problem in which a group of connected agents aim to find the average of their local values, also known as the

A common feature in most classic consensus algorithms is the requirement for sharing the local values with neighboring nodes. However, this approach is, indeed, problematic in settings that nodes are not willing to share their exact local value (opinion or belief) due to

To provide privacy in the consensus problem, the first step is to define a measure that quantifies it. Differential privacy

6

8

is the most studied measure of privacy for an algorithm running over a dataset. This notion measures the privacy based on the statistical dependency of an algorithm’s output to the perturbation of a single element of the input dataset. Noting that the classic consensus method is a deterministic procedure, the most common approach for a private consensus algorithm in the literature is that each node perturbs its signals by some form of noise such that the resulting stochastic algorithm is differentially private

Adding perturbing noises affects the convergence properties compared to non-private consensus algorithms in two ways: (i) It leads to a non-exact limit, and (ii) it compromises the convergence rate, i.e., it leads to slower convergence rates, e.g., slower than the rates achieved by
[
29

. While some methods address the non-exact limit by adding zero-sum correlated noise
[
13
,
21
]
, no prior study has addressed both (i) and (ii) simultaneously. This issue persists for any iterative method that perturbs in some way the communication messages at each iteration either randomly or in a deterministic manner. This includes works that use methods like output masking
[

[

[

Indeed, differential-privacy is a powerful measure for indicating the level of privacy of a learning algorithm running over a dataset. This is done by quantifying the statistical dependency of the algorithm’s output to a perturbation of a single input data point. This approach is best applicable when a full list of data points is accessible by the adversary. Alas, in a consensus setting, the data available at one user, i.e., the messages it collects from its neighbors over time, is different from the data available at other users. By injecting extra noise to all communication messages in a consensus algorithm, the differential-privacy approaches mask the private values against an adversary that can eavesdrop all messages. The cost to defeat this adversary appears on convergence side as discussed above. However, in a setting that the adversary can gain access to only a single node’s information, such approaches provide more than necessary privacy with an unwanted cost. Hence, to handle the consensus setting with such type of adversary and asymmetric data distribution, differential privacy must be replaced with a more compatible privacy measure.

Motivated by this point, we provide a novel information-theoretic scheme to measure and analyze privacy leakage in consensus problems. We further provide simple noise-aggregation methods to reach the exact consensus in a private manner while achieving the fastest rate that a non-private consensus method can achieve. These advantages are based on the fact that our proposed
provably private averaging consensus
(PPAC) method only modifies the initial values of the consensus dynamic and leaves the consensus procedure intact. Moreover, we use a probabilistic model where the private values are random variables, which makes our method fundamentally different from most of the prior work. In this probabilistic framework, we introduce a new measure of privacy leakage based on the mutual information between the initial value of a node and the set of messages that is available at another node. In this way, we can capture how private the initial value of a node is with respect to any other node in the network. Most of our theoretical results hold regardless of the shape of the distribution of noises and private values. Next, we state our contributions:

1.

Given a network structure and a consensus matrix, we propose an algorithm for reaching consensus among the nodes, while keeping the initial values private throughout the algorithm and preserving the fastest rate that a non-private consensus method can achieve.

2.

We formalize a continuous notion based on the mutual information function that measures the privacy leakage of any (victim) node at another (adversary) node that can be (optimally) tuned by our adjustable noise parameters.

3.

We provide a precise characterization of the information flow of a private value over the network over time. Having this, even in ill-shaped structures that leak privacy, we can determine the amount of information that an adversary receives as a function of time and compute the waiting time until recovering a private value.

In summary, this paper has two main messages: (i) Privacy from a node’s perspective can be measured and efficiently analyzed based on the concept of mutual information. (ii) Splitting private messages into fragments prior to running the classical consensus method maintains the convergence guarantees and ensures the privacy for most structures. No further additive noise is required.

Problem Setup

We consider the averaging dynamic described in (
3
) under the constraint that the original messages
u_{i}
must be kept private from other nodes. Satisfying this requirement rules out the naive initialization of
v_{i}(0)=u_{i}
. Therefore, we seek new initializations
v_{i}(0)
to fulfill the privacy and convergence requirements while following the consensus dynamic (
3
).
Moreover, we assume that the initial values
u_{i}
are independent scalar-valued continuous random variables, but a similar argument is applicable to a discrete case. Moreover, for a vector-valued
u_{i}
with independent coordinates, our results can be applied to each coordinate.

Adversary model.

We assume all nodes are honest but curious, meaning that they follow the protocols and communication principles of the network, but they might be willing to recover the initial value of other nodes in the network. This is equivalent to the situation in which an adversary obtains access to the messages received by a single node.
Moreover, we assume that nodes (adversaries) do not collude in recovering private messages and the underlying graph and the full consensus matrix are accessible by all nodes (adversaries).

Privacy model.

Consider arbitrary distinct nodes
i
and
j
and suppose node
i
is curious about node
j
’s private value
u_{j}
. Node
i
only has access to the
u_{i}
and all messages it sends to or receives from its neighbors over time. Denote the concatenation of all these by
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
. The privacy fails if node
i
can deterministically recover
u_{j}
from
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
. Otherwise, it is important to know how close node
i
can statistically estimate
u_{j}
using
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
.
This can be evaluated by finding how much information
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
reveals about
u_{j}
which can be formalized by the mutual information between the joint distribution of the elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
and
u_{j}
, denoted by
\mathcal{I}(\mathfrak{D}_{i}\,;u_{j})\in[0,\infty]
. Achieving zero mutual information is impossible since the consensus goal
u^{*}
itself reveals some information about
u_{j}
. Upcoming sections reveal how tuning the model’s parameters push
\mathcal{I}(\mathfrak{D}_{i}\,;u_{j})
toward
0
as much as possible.

Privacy Leakage Measure

We first provide a mathematical definition to formalize the notion of privacy leakage that was earlier described in Section
3

We start with mathematically formalizing the concatenation of all data that a node

collects, which was earlier denoted by
\mathfrak{D}_{i}
in

3

The first data available at node

. Note that

is a redundant data, since it is simply the subtraction of other pure noises from

. In the preparation phase, node

receives

from its neighbors. Moreover, when the consensus procedure starts, at time

, node

transmits

to node

so that it can compute its new update for the next iteration.
Hence, up to time

, node

and

. Hence, the concatenation of node

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)=\left\{u_{i}\right\}\times\left\{\Gamma_{i\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}\setminus\left\{m_{i}\right\}}\times\left\{\Gamma_{\ell i}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}}\times\left\{v_{\ell}(\tau)\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i},0\leq\tau\leq t}.

(9)

The notation

is also applicable and represents all the data that node

can gather if the consensus runs forever. To measure the privacy leakage of

at node

up to time

, we consider the mutual information between

and

Considering the definition of

Note that smaller

6.4
).

2.

If the network contains a specific sub-structure called a generalized leaf, defined in Definition
6.7
, some nodes can deterministically recover the private values of some other nodes in the first consensus iteration, i.e., privacy fails.

3.

If the network does not contain a generalised leaf, no node can fully recover a private value (Lemma
6.5
and
Theorem

6.8
). In this case,
\pi_{i}^{(j)}
, defined in Definition
4.3
, measures the amount of leakage of
u_{j}
to node
i
, statistically. We obtain a closed form
\pi_{i}^{(j)}(t)=1/2\log(1+\sigma_{j}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{a})
for some vector
\mathbf{a}
and matrix
\Sigma
(
Theorem

6.6
). In
Section

6
, we discuss the construction of
\mathbf{a}
and
\Sigma
through several steps. In this construction, the quantities
\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}
, the variances of noisy fragments
\Gamma_{k\ell},\,(k,\ell)\in S
, appear as linear terms in the elements of
\Sigma
. This shows that
\pi_{i}^{(j)}
is a decreasing function in terms of
\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}
.

4.

PPAC leaves the convergence limit and rate of the classical averaging consensus intact. Moreover, the convergence time to achieve an
\epsilon
-accurate solution by
PPAC scales as
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon))
and it also increases proportional to the logarithm of noise parameters (
Theorem

7.2
). This shows that tuning
\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}
adjusts the trade-off between privacy and convergence time.

Next, we discuss the novel techniques used to achieve the above results. The baseline in obtaining our results is transforming the information-/graph-theoretical formulation of the problem into a linear-algebraic form by writing the data collection at each node as a matrix-vector decomposition
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)=\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)\mathbf{g}
, where
\mathbf{g}
is the vector consisting of the
2m
randomness sources of the model. As we show later, the possibility of leakage and properties of information flow over the graph can be translated into constraints on
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
.

While the size of
\mathbf{g}
is fixed, each new iteration adds
\deg(i)
rows to
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
and
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
. The decomposition
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)=\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)\mathbf{g}
reveals that, after sufficiently many iterations, all new rows are linear combinations of previous ones. This leads to the fact that finitely many iterations
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
have new information. Moreover, we constructively find
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}
by running a basis-pursuit procedure on the rows of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
. As a next step, we write each element
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
as a function of the elements of
W^{t}
. Having this, we then use Cayley-Hamilton theorem (see
[
26
]
) on matrix powers to show that
t_{k}\leq n-1
. The aforementioned decomposition also paves the way to translate the waiting time for node
i
to receive the first message containing non-redundant information about
u_{j}
in terms of the distance
d_{G}(i,m_{j})
, which leads to
\operatorname{ecc}_{G}(i)-2\leq t_{k}
.

Considering the data collection
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
on only non-redundant data points corresponding to
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}
for
r\leq k
gives
\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{r}
and accordingly
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
. The next tool to obtain the results of
Theorem

5.1
is the fact that we translated the ability of deterministic recovery of
u_{j}
by node
i
into the rank-deficiency of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
when its
j
-th column is removed. Having this, the core technique in obtaining the main results of this paper is transforming the aforementioned rank-deficiency condition into the existence of a specific substructure in the graph called a
generalized leaf
. A generalized leaf with head
j
and tail
i
consists of
i
being connected to all degree
2
neighbors of
j
(and possibly
j
itself) which is illustrated in
Figure

1
. While it is straightforward to see why in a generalized leaf with head
j
and tail
i
, node
i
can fully recover
u_{j}
, it is far more challenging to prove that this is indeed the only possible scenario for a full recovery of some private value. We prove this fact based on a refined analysis (see
Section

B.7
) that shows, assuming there are no generalized leaves, the
j
-th column of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
can be written in terms of other columns and thus removing the
j
-th column does not decrease the rank of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
.

Figure 1:
All possible forms of a generalized leaf with head
j
and tail
i
: (i) When node
i
is the only neighbor of node
j
; (ii) When node
j
is not adjacent to node
i
and only has neighbors of degree two and node
i
is adjacent to all of them; (iii) The situation of case (ii) while the edge
\{i,j\}
is also added.

By distinguishing the only case for a full (deterministic) recovery, the next step is to analyse and reduce the partial (stochastic) recovery of private values. Assuming that there are no generalized leaves in the graph, under having Gaussian distribution for all randomness sources in the model, we then show that joint distribution of the elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{r}
is a non-degenerate Gaussian distribution that has an invertible covariance matrix and compute the mutual information between this joint distribution and
u_{j}
. This leads to a closed-form expression for
\pi_{i}^{(j)}(t)
and shows how it can be decreased by the model’s tunable parameters.

Privacy Analysis

In this section, we first introduce a representation of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
in the form of a matrix-vector decomposition (
Section

6.1
), and then use it to formally state our results on privacy (

6.2

6.1
Matrix-Vector Decomposition for
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)

To obtain
Theorem

5.1
, we start by finding a representation of the elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
in terms of the randomness sources of our model. To this aim, let
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the matrix whose
ij
-th element is
\Gamma_{ij}
defined in Algorithm
1
. Based on the preparation phase, we have
\mathbf{v}{(0)}=\Gamma^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}
which results in

\mathbf{v}{(t)}=W^{t}\mathbf{v}{(0)}=W^{t}\Gamma^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad v_{i}{(t)}=\left[\mathbf{v}(t)\right]_{i}=\left[W^{t}\Gamma^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}\right]_{i}=\left[W^{t}\right]_{i*}\Gamma^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}.

(11)

Consider all the (independent) sources of randomness in our model which are
\left\{u_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
and
\left\{\Gamma_{ij}\right\}_{(i,j)\in S}
. Equation (
11
) implies that
v_{i}(t)
can be written as a linear combination of these quantities. The following expression suggests a notation for this linear combination:

v_{i}{(t)}=\sum_{j\in[n]}\alpha_{j}^{i}(t)\,u_{j}+\sum_{(k,\ell)\in S}\beta_{k\ell}^{i}(t)\,\Gamma_{k\ell}.

(12)

(
11
) implies that the coefficients in (
12
) can be obtained in terms of the elements of
\left[W^{t}\right]_{i*}
as

\alpha_{j}^{i}(t)=\left[W^{t}\right]_{i\,m_{j}},\quad\beta_{k\ell}^{i}(t)=\left[W^{t}\right]_{i\,\ell}-\left[W^{t}\right]_{i\,m_{k}}.

(13)

To express (
12
) in a vector-multiplication form, we define
\mathbf{g}
to be a
2m
-dimensional (column) vector that is the concatenation of all sources of randomness and
\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)
to be the concatenation of the coefficients as follows: (Note that
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
.)

\mathbf{g}=\left\{u_{j}\right\}_{j\in[n]}\times\left\{\Gamma_{k\ell}\right\}_{(k,\ell)\in S},\quad\mathbf{p}_{i}(t)=\left\{\alpha_{j}^{i}(t)\right\}_{j\in[n]}\times\left\{\beta_{k\ell}^{i}(t)\right\}_{(k,\ell)\in S}.

(14)

Hence,
v_{i}{(t)}
can be written as

v_{i}{(t)}=\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\top}(t)\,\mathbf{g}.

(15)

Similar to
v_{i}(t)
in (
15
), other elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
are also linear combinations of the elements of
\mathbf{g}
and can be written in vector-multiplication forms in terms of
\mathbf{g}
. To this aim, we can consider matrices
\Delta_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}
(where both are of size
\deg_{G}(i)\times 2m
) such that

\displaystyle\left\{u_{i}\right\}\times\left\{\Gamma_{i\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}\setminus\left\{m_{i}\right\}}=\Delta_{i}\,\mathbf{g},\quad\quad\left\{\Gamma_{\ell i}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}}=\Lambda_{i}\,\mathbf{g}.

(16)

Putting together the representation of the elements of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
in terms of
\mathbf{g}
in (
15
), and (
16
), we obtain the matrix
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
by vertically concatenating the matrices
\Delta_{i}
,
\Lambda_{i}
, and appending vectors
\mathbf{p}_{\ell}(t)
to the end. More formally, to be compatible with our notation for concatenation of vectors, we write

\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)=\left\{\left[\Delta_{i}\right]_{s*}\right\}_{s}\times\left\{\left[\Lambda_{i}\right]_{s\ast}\right\}_{s}\times\left\{\mathbf{p}_{\ell}^{\top}(\tau)\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i},0\leq\tau\leq t},\quad\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)=\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)\mathbf{g}.

(17)

Recall that
[\cdot]_{s\ast}
denotes the
s
-th row. Since we set the notation only for the concatenation of vectors, to concatenate matrices
\Delta_{i}
and
\Lambda_{i}
, we first split them into their rows and then concatenate the rows.

6.2
Formal Statement of Privacy Results

In this section, we formally state the privacy results using the linear-algebraic representations obtained in
Section

6.1
. As the first step, we seek to spot the iterations at which node
i
receives a message that contains new information. To do so, we define
\mathbf{a}_{s}
as the
s
-th row of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}(t)
for some
t\geq s
. We then run a basis-pursuit procedure over
\left\{\mathbf{a}_{s}\right\}_{s=0}^{\infty}
, as described in
Section

2
. Suppose
\mathcal{B}(\left\{\mathbf{a}_{s}\right\}_{s=0}^{\infty})
is the basis obtained by the basis-pursuit scheme. It is straightforward to confirm that the elements
[\Delta_{i}]_{s*}
and
[\Lambda_{i}]_{s*}
, for all
s
, are all linearly independent and lie in this basis. Denote the rest of the elements of the basis by
\mathbf{p}_{j_{1}}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{j_{k}}(t_{k})
, where
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in N_{i}
and
0\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{k}
. More formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(\left\{\mathbf{a}_{s}\right\}_{s=0}^{\infty})=\left\{\left[\Delta_{i}\right]_{s*}:s\right\}\cup\left\{\left[\Lambda_{i}\right]_{s*}:s\right\}\cup\left\{\mathbf{p}_{j_{1}}^{\top}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{j_{k}}^{\top}(t_{k})\right\}.

(18)

We want to show that the elements of
\mathcal{B}(\left\{\mathbf{a}_{s}\right\}_{s=0}^{\infty})
are all the data points at node
i
that matter and the rest of data points are indeed redundant. To this aim, we first need the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

Define
((j_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(j_{k},t_{k}))
in (
18
) to be the informative sequence of node
i
. Moreover, for a given integer
0\leq t\leq\infty
, define
q(t)
to be the largest
r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
such that
t_{r}\leq t
.

Next, let
\mathfrak{D}^{r}_{i}
be the accumulated data that considers the data points corresponding to the informative sequence at node
i
, i.e., the messages coming from neighbors
j_{1},\ldots,j_{r}
at times
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}
for some
r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. More formally,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{r}_{i}=\left\{u_{i}\right\}\times\left\{\Gamma_{i\ell}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}\setminus\left\{m_{i}\right\}}\times\left\{\Gamma_{\ell i}\right\}_{\ell\in N_{i}}\times\left(v_{j_{1}}(t_{1}),\ldots,v_{j_{r}}(t_{r})\right).

(19)

As a next step, we seek to formally show that the information content of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
equals that of
\mathfrak{D}^{q(t)}_{i}
, with
q(t)
defined in Definition
6.1
.
This claim is proved in the following statement.

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with
n\geq 2
under Assumption
4.1
. Moreover, consider
q(t)
from Definition
6.1
. Suppose
0\leq t\leq\infty
is given and let
r=q(t)
. Having
\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{r}
defined in (
19
), for any
j\neq i
, we have
\mathcal{I}(\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)\,;\,u_{j})=\mathcal{I}(\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{r}\,;\,u_{j}).

Theorem
6.2
implies that
\mathcal{I}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{i}(\infty)\,;\,u_{j}\right)=\mathcal{I}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{k}\,;\,u_{j}\right)
, suggesting that all but finitely many messages are redundant. The following proposition upperbounds the largest informative time instance
t_{k}
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Consider Algorithm
1
under Assumption
4.1
and let
((j_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(j_{k},t_{k}))
be the informative sequence at node
i
. If
\mu_{W}
is the minimal polynomial of matrix

, then

(20)

Proposition
6.3
states that no more information about private values will be exchanged after
n

, it can be determined whether or not an adversary is able to recover a private value. It is also interesting to know how many consensus rounds a (curious) node

for the first time.
The following proposition is dedicated to this result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Consider Algorithm
1
under Assumption
4.1
and let
((j_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(j_{k},t_{k}))
be the informative sequence at node
i
from Definition
6.1
. Further, suppose the underlying graph is connected and for the consensus matrix,

, we have
W\geq 0
, and
W\sim A_{G}
. Let
t=\tau_{j}^{(i)}
be the minimum

r(G)-2\leq\operatorname{ecc}_{G}(i)-2\leq t_{k}
.

Analogous to (
16
), we can define the data collection on non-redundant data based on the concept of the informative sequence at one node and its representation in terms of
\mathbf{g}
in a matrix-vector multiplication format. To this aim, first consider the informative sequence
((j_{1},t_{1}),\ldots,(j_{k},t_{k}))
at node
i
and for
r\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, define

\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}=\left\{\left[\Delta_{i}\right]_{s*}\right\}_{s}\times\left\{\left[\Lambda_{i}\right]_{s\ast}\right\}_{s}\times\left\{\mathbf{p}_{j_{\ell}}^{\top}(t_{\ell})\right\}_{1\leq\ell\leq r},\quad\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{r}=\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}\mathbf{g},

(21)

and note that
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
is a full row-rank matrix.
Equation

21
represents all the informative data points at node
i
in terms of the model’s randomness sources, i.e., in terms of the elements of
\mathbf{g}
. In this representation, each column of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
corresponds to the coefficients of one randomness source for all data points. As we show later, the difference between the rank of
\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}
before and after removing the column corresponding to a private value determines the privacy-preserving properties of the network. Towards this argument, let
\mathfrak{R}_{i,-j}^{r}
be the matrix obtained by removing the column corresponding to

. We use

to emphasize that this column is removed. Two cases are possible in this situation:

\displaystyle\text{Case 1:}\,\,\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathfrak{R}_{i,-j}^{r}\right)=\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\text{Case 2:}\,\,\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathfrak{R}_{i,-j}^{r}\right)=\operatorname{rank}\left(\mathfrak{R}_{i}^{r}\right)-1.

(23)

We will show that under Case
2
, node

can deterministically recover

while under Case
1
, this is not possible. We start by presenting the following lemma stating that under Case

, the privacy fails.

Lemma 6.5
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with

under Assumption
4.1
. Moreover, consider
r=q(t)
from Definition
6.1
and suppose (
23
) holds for some

. Then node

can fully recover

using
\mathfrak{D}^{r}_{i}
.

Lemma
6.5
shows that under Case

, node

can deterministically recover

is a deterministic function of

, which implies that

is finite under Case

. This implies that

is not a deterministic function of
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
and thus node

cannot fully recover

using
\mathfrak{D}_{i}(t)
. Thus, Case

holds if and only if node
i
can deterministically recover
u_{j}
. To analyse Case
1
, we need to define
\mathcal{S}_{-j}
in the following, which is the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{g}
when the element

is removed:

\mathcal{S}_{-j}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\left\{\sigma_{i}^{2}\right\}_{i\in[n]\setminus\left\{j\right\}}\times\left\{\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}\right\}_{(k,\ell)\in S}\right).

(24)

Note that the parameters in (

) are previously defined in Definition
4.2
. The following theorem computes
\pi_{i}^{(j)}(t)
under Case
1
, for a Gaussian model.

Theorem 6.6
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with
n\geq 2
under Assumption
4.1
. Suppose

is given and let

as defined in Definition
6.1
. Moreover, suppose

22

, and

\pi_{i}^{(j)}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\sigma_{j}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{a}\right),

(25)

where

, and

Note that for
(k,\ell)\in S

\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}

and Case

Definition 6.7

(Generalized Leaf)
.

Consider graph

with distinct vertices

\left\{i,j\right\}
may or may not be in

. Then,

7
Convergence Analysis

Next, we study the convergence properties of Algorithm
1
. We first formalize the notion of convergence time based on the waiting time required to achieve an
\epsilon
-accurate estimation of the convergence limit. We denote this quantity by
t_{\epsilon}
and define it as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

For
\epsilon>0
and
\mathbf{v}(t)
in (
11
), let
t_{\epsilon}
be the minimum time
t
such that
\left\|\mathbf{v}(t)-\mathbf{u}^{*}\right\|_{2}\!\leq\!\epsilon
.

Note that the convergence rate (
5
) is conceptually different from the convergence time defined in Definition
7.1
. While the rate represents the ”slope” of a convergence, the convergence time refers to the time when the iterations arrive the vicinity of the solution. This for example, implies that a larger distance between the initial values and the final solution leads to a larger convergence time under the same convergence rate. Having this definition, the convergence results of this paper is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 7.2
.

Consider Algorithm
1
(PPAC) under Assumption
4.1
. Further, suppose
W\mathds{1}_{n}=W^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}=\mathds{1}_{n}
and
\rho(W-{1}/{n}\mathds{1}_{n}\mathds{1}_{n}^{\top})<1
. Then, we have:

1.

In the limit, PPAC converges to the exact solution, i.e.,
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{v}(t)=\mathbf{u}^{*}
.

2.

The convergence rate in part (i) is the same as the convergence rate of an ordinary (non-private) consensus, meaning that (
5
) holds for PPAC too.

3.

The expected convergence time for achieving an
\epsilon
-accurate solution is

\mathbb{E}\left[t_{\epsilon}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1+\mu_{\max}^{2}+\sigma_{\max}^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)\right),

(26)

where
\mu_{\max}
and
\sigma_{\max}^{2}
are from Definition
4.2
.

Note that the conditions
W\mathds{1}_{n}=W^{\top}\mathds{1}_{n}=\mathds{1}_{n}
and
\rho(W-{1}/{n}\mathds{1}_{n}\mathds{1}_{n}^{\top})<1
, as mentioned in
Section

2
are essential for the fundamental convergence properties of the classical averaging consensus. Further, we refer to
Appendix

A
for simulation results on convergence.

Remark 7.3
.

The trade-off between privacy and the convergence time in our model can be explained as follows. Earlier in this section, we mentioned that increasing
\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}
for
(k,\ell)\in S
(or similarly
\sigma_{\max}^{2}
) decreases the privacy leakage
\pi_{i}^{(j)}(t)
due to
Equation

25
, while it increases the convergence time due to
Equation

26
. Hence, the quantities
\sigma_{k\ell}^{2}
for
(k,\ell)\in S
can be seen as the tuning parameters that adjust the position in the trade-off between privacy and the convergence time.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a novel private consensus
averaging algorithm and analyzed its privacy from an
information-theoretic perspective. We specifically
used the mutual information function between all the
information available at the adversary node and the
initial value of the victim to measure how private the
value of the victim is with respect to the attacker.
Our results show that the convergence rate of our proposed method is similar to the convergence rate of a
classic non-private consensus method, for any level of
privacy. More importantly, any level of accuracy can
be achieved via our proposed method. Our results also
show that there exists a trade-off between the level of
information-theoretic privacy and convergence time.

B.2
Theorem
6.2

We first formally prove the fundamental property of a basis-pursuit procedure. Then we express a lemma stating that redundant data can be ignored when computing mutual information. Equivalently, if there is a spanning set that generates all other arguments of a mutual information function, considering this spanning set is enough and other elements can be ignored. The proof is based on the fact that the basis resulted from a basis-pursuit procedure is an example of such spanning sets. We start by formally proving fundamental properties of a basis-pursuit procedure.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider
\left\{\mathbf{a}_{i}\right\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
where
\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathbf{a}_{0}\neq\mathbf{0}
and let

be obtained from a basis pursuit procedure. Then,

and the elements of

are linearly independent. Let

where

, letting
r
be the largest number such that

, we have

\mathbf{a}_{i}\in\operatorname{span}\left(\left\{\mathbf{a}_{t_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{t_{r}}\right\}\right).

(27)

Proof.

By construction of
\mathcal{B}(\cdot)
, any finite subset of

is linearly independent. Thus,
|B|\leq\dim\left(\mathbb{R}^{n}\right)=n
. Moreover, if

, (

) trivially holds. Therefore, suppose

and

, we have

and thus

.
∎

Letting
\mathbf{Y}=\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\right)
, we have
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}]=\mathbf{0}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}=\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\mathrm{I}_{n}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\right)^{\top}A\left(\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X}]\right)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\underbrace{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{\frac{1}{2}}A\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{B}\mathbf{Y}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i,\,j}B_{ij}Y_{i}Y_{j}\right]

(54)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(B)=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{\frac{1}{2}}A\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\operatorname{tr}\left(A\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}\right).

(55)

Lemma

eases the proof of the following lemma that computes the mutual information with specific form of arguments that will appear later in the proof of Theorem

Lemma B.4
.

Suppose
(X,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})
is a multivariate normal where
X\perp\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k})^{\top}
and assume
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}
is invertible. Then

\mathcal{I}\left(X+a_{1}Y_{1},\ldots,X+a_{k}Y_{k}\,;\,\,X\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\right),

(56)

where
\mathbf{a}=\left[a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\right]^{\top}
.

Proof.

Denote
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}\left(X+a_{1}Y_{1},\ldots,X+a_{k}Y_{k}\,;\,\,X\right)
and
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Y}+X\mathbf{a}=\left[Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k}\right]^{\top}
and note that
Z_{i}=X+a_{i}Y_{i}
for
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
. By definition,

\mathcal{I}=\int f_{X,\mathbf{Z}}\left(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\right)\log\frac{f_{X,\mathbf{Z}}\left(x,z_{1},\ldots,z_{k}\right)}{f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Z}}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}z_{1}\ldots\mathrm{d}z_{k}.

(57)

To obtain the joint distribution of
(X,\mathbf{Z})
, the following transformation is useful:

\left[\begin{array}[]{l}X\\
\mathbf{Z}\end{array}\right]=J\left[\begin{array}[]{l}X\\
\mathbf{Y}\end{array}\right],\quad J=\left[\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{a}&\mathrm{I}_{k}\end{array}\right],\quad\operatorname{det}(J)=1,

(58)

which results in

f_{X,\mathbf{Z}}(x,\mathbf{z})=f_{X,\mathbf{Y}}(x,\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)=f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x).

(59)

By replacing (
59
) into (
57
) and cancelling out the term
f_{X}
, we get

\mathcal{I}=\int f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)\log\frac{f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)}{f_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}.

(60)

To compute the terms in (
60
), we need a quick discussion. Letting
\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\mathbf{Y}},\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)
and
\mathbf{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{\mathbf{Z}},\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)
, we know that

\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}=\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}+\mathbf{a}\mu_{X},

(61)

and we can relate
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}
as follows:

\operatorname{Cov}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}X\\
\mathbf{Z}\end{array}\right]\right)=J\operatorname{Cov}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{l}X\\
\mathbf{Y}\end{array}\right]\right)J^{\top}=J\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{X}^{2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\end{array}\right]J^{\top}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{X}^{2}&*\\
&\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}+\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\end{array}\right]

(62)

\Longrightarrow\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}=\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}+\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}.

(63)

Since
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\succ 0
, from (
63
) we conclude that
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}\succ 0
and thus
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}
is invertible. Therefore,
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}
and
\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}
exist. Having this, we can write

\frac{f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)}{f_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{z})}=\frac{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\right).

(64)

Replacing (
64
) into (
60
) leads to the computation of (
60
) in three terms as follows:

\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{1}+\mathcal{I}_{2}+\mathcal{I}_{3},

(65)

where the first term is

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\int f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)\log\frac{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}\right|}{\left|\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right|}.

(66)

The second term is

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\,\mathrm{d}x

(67)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int f_{X}(x)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x

(68)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int f_{X}(x)\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}^{-1}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\quad\quad\text{due to Lemma~\ref{integ}}

(69)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{I}_{k}\right)}{2}\int f_{X}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=-\frac{k}{2}.

(70)

And the third term can be obtained as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int f_{X}(x)f_{\mathbf{Y}}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{a}x)\left(\mathbf{z}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{z}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{z}\,\mathrm{d}x

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int f_{X}(x)\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\right]\,\mathrm{d}x.

(72)

For each given
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we can compute the argument of the integration in (
72
) as follows.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\right]=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)\right]+

(73)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[2\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{a}x+\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\right]+

(74)

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{a}x+\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{a}x+\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right).

(75)

To compute the right-hand side of the latest equation, note that (

) can be obtained using Lemma

as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}-\mu_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Y}}\right)

(76)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}-\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right)\right)\quad\text{due to \eqref{sigma_rel}}

(77)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathrm{I}_{k}\right)-\sigma_{X}^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right)

(78)

\displaystyle=k-\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}.

(79)

Having (
79
) together with the fact that (
74
) is zero and replacing

in (

), we can put together the three terms and write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{a}x-\mu_{\mathbf{Z}}\right)\right]=k-\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}+\left(x-\mu_{X}\right)^{2}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}.

(80)

Replacing (
80
) in (
72
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(k-\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}\int f_{X}(x)\left(x-\mu_{X}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x

(81)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{X}^{2}\,\mathbf{a}^{\top}\Sigma_{\mathbf{Z}}^{-1}\mathbf{a}=\frac{k}{2}.

(82)

Putting the values of
\mathcal{I}_{1}
,
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{3}
from (

), (

), and (

(83)

To simplify further, note that from (

Theorem

Lemma B.5
.

Let

(137)

Proof.

Note that

\left\|\mathbf{x}(t)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\alpha^{t}\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

(138)

When one is not considering
t\geq 0

(139)

Then by taking the logarithm of both sides of
\alpha^{t}\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}=\epsilon
, we have

\tilde{t}_{\epsilon}\log\alpha+\log\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}=\log\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tilde{t}_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\cdot\log\frac{\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}}{\epsilon}.

(140)

Note that here
\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}
can go to zero and cause a negative

. Hence, if we are interested in minimum non-negative time, we can define

t_{\epsilon}=\min\left\{t\geq 0\mid\left\|\mathbf{x}(t)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\right\}.

(141)

Having

\left\|\mathbf{x}(t)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}

if

otherwise. This fact together with (

t_{\epsilon}\leq\left\lceil\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\alpha}}\cdot\log\frac{1+\left\|\mathbf{x}(0)-\mathbf{x}^{*}\right\|_{2}}{\epsilon}\right\rceil.

(142)
