A Non-parametric Approach to Inference about the Tail of a Continuous or a Discrete Distribution
Jialin Zhang, Zhiyi Zhang

Abstract
This article introduces a non-parametric information-theoretic approach to inference about the tail of a continuous or a discrete distribution. Leveraging a new concept named tail profile – a set of information-theoretic quantities developed from results of domains of attraction on countable alphabets – theoretical evidence supports the identification of specific discrete distributional tail types through a sequence of plots. The approach discerns tail types by bench-marking against exponential, and three thicker-than-exponential families: near-exponential, sub-exponential, and power-law (zipf, Pareto). For tails thicker-than-exponential, the approach also provides point and interval estimates for some of the underlying distribution parameters. While primarily designed to streamline the selection of discrete parametric models for detailed statistical analysis, a supporting theorem enables the method’s extension use to continuous data, stating that binning continuous data with a common width preserves the tail decay rate under certain conditions. Simulations are presented to demonstrate the method’s performance across various scenarios.



1
Introduction

‘In certain situations it is of interest to draw inference about the behavior of a distribution function in the tails without assuming that a particular parametric form for the distribution function holds globally’ —
Hill 1975
. This task is inherently complex due to two primary reasons. Firstly, the distributional behavior in the tail may differ markedly from its behavior in the remainder of the sample space. Consequently, it becomes ambiguous how sample data, predominantly observed in the high-frequency region of the sample space, may be aptly used for such determination. Secondly, observations in the tail are rare and sparse. Consequently, making inference about the tail is an inherent challenging task regardless of the sample size.

As a motivating example, in risk management, modeling the distributional tail of the log return of commodities, such as the stock price of Amazon, is often paramount. Central to this is determining the most appropriate class of parametric tail probability models for a given commodity. Should one opt for Gaussian or exponential tail decay? Alternatively, are near-exponential, sub-exponential, or power-law decays better fits? Established tests like
Shaphiro and Wilk 1965
test and
Kolmogorov 1933
-
Smirnov 1948
test, alongside quantile-quantile plots and regression, may ascertain how well a specific distribution aligns with sample data. However, an approach that pinpoints a tail’s distribution class without prior assumptions would expedite preliminary analyses, streamlining the selection of potential parametric models.

Hill 1975
introduced a simple approach offering a point estimate for power-law decaying tails.
Davis and Resnick 1984
expands Hill’s result with interval estimates. Ever since, many research aimed at improving and extending Hill’s perspective, including but not limited to
Hall and Welsh 1984
,
Smith 1987
,
Alves 1995
,
Feuerverger and Hall 1999
, and
Németh and Zempléni 2020
. Most of the existing research visited the long-standing research question of tail inference in perspective of extreme value theory and domains of attraction on continuous space. Interested readers may refer to
Haan and Ferreira 2006
. This article extends the work in a new perspective – methodology that originates from
Zhang 2018
, domains of attraction on countable alphabets. The proposed approach not only facilitates point and interval estimations for both continuous and discrete power-law tails, but also touches various other families of thick-tail distributions, be they continuous or discrete.

The proposed method draws inspiration from Turing’s formula. Often referred to as the Good-Turing formula – introduced by
Good 1953
but widely attributed to Alan Turing – this formula estimates the total probability associated with elements in a discrete sample space that are not represented in an independent and identically distributed (
iid
) sample of size
n
. Statistical properties of Turing’s formula have been studied by many, including most notably
Robbins et al. 1968
,
Esty et al. 1983
,
Chao et al. 1988
,
Zhang and Huang 2008
, and
Zhang and Zhang 2009
. For a comprehensive introduction to the subject, interested readers may refer to
Zhang 2016
.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section
2
the proposed method is described. In Section
3
, use of the proposed method is described with Amazon stock return data. Section
4
provides numerical studies, including a rework on
Hill 1975
’s example data to compare the results, and simulation studies to evaluate the tail classifier’s performance under various distributions. Section
5
discusses the selection of tail profile. The article concluded in Section
6
with all proofs postponed to
A
.

2.1
Tail Profile

Let
\mathbf{p}=\{p_{k};k\geq 1\}
be an ordered probability distribution
on a countably infinite alphabet
\mathscr{X}=\{\ell_{k};k\geq 1\}

p_{k}\geq p_{k+1}

\{Y_{k};k\geq 1\}

\{\hat{p}_{k}=Y_{k}/n;k\geq 1\}

, where

is the indicator function, for
r=1,\cdots,n
, and
\pi_{0}=\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}1[Y_{k}=0]
, which is the total probability associated with all letters of

\mathscr{X}
that are missing in the sample. In the literature,
\pi_{0}
is often referred to as the “missing probability”, and

as the “sample coverage of the population” or just simply the “coverage”. Interest readers may refer to
Chao and Lee 1992
for additional discussion. Turing’s formula is given by
T={N_{1}}/{n}
. Motivated by Turing’s formula, tail index and tail profile are defined in Definition
1
.

Definition 1

(Tail index and tail profile)
.

Let
\zeta_{v}=\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}(1-p_{k})^{v}
. Let

\tau_{v}=v\zeta_{v}

(1)

be referred to as the

, and
\{\tau_{v};v=v_{1},\cdots,v_{2}\}
, where

and
v_{2}
are two positive integers satisfying

, be referred to as a

.

To see that
\tau_{v}
is tail-relevant, it suffices to note that Turing’s formula is itself tail-relevant. In particular, Turing’s formula provides a good estimator (see
Robbins et al. 1968
) for
\pi_{0}
. Moreover, the probabilities
p_{k}
in this sum typically correspond to very small values, so for large
n
these outcomes lie in the tail (either by intuition or by definition). Moreover,
\operatorname{E}(\pi_{0})=\sum_{k\geq 1}p_{k}\,(1-p_{k})^{n}=\zeta_{n}={\tau_{n}}/{n}
. This connection demonstrates the tail-relevance of
\tau_{v}
.

The tail index defined in (
1
), or more precisely the tail profile
\{\tau_{v}:v_{1}\leq v\leq v_{2}\}
, forms the basis for the proposed tail classifier. What enables the classifier is the existence of an unbiased estimator of
\zeta_{v}
for every positive integer
v
(with
v\leq n-1
); namely,

\displaystyle Z_{v}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{v+1}[n-(v+1)]!}{n!}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left[\hat{p}_{k}\prod_{j=0}^{v-1}\left(1-\hat{p}_{k}-\frac{j}{n}\right)\right].

(2)

Proposed by
Zhang and Zhou 2010
,
Z_{v}
is a
U
-statistic constructed based on Turing’s formula as its kernel function of degree
v
, and it exhibits many desirable statistical properties. Most importantly,
\operatorname{E}(Z_{v})=\zeta_{v}
; hence, by defining
T_{v}=vZ_{v}
, it follows that
\operatorname{E}(T_{v})=\tau_{v}
for
v\leq n-1
. The observed tail profile is therefore

\{T_{v}\}=\{T_{v};v=1,\cdots,n-1\},

(3)

which may be viewed as an unbiased estimator of
\{\tau_{v}\}=\{\tau_{v};v=1,\cdots,n-1\}
.

introduces the theoretical support of the classifier. The use of the classifier is described in Section
2.3

2.2
Theoretical Support

The central idea of the proposed classifier is based on the following Theorem

Theorem 1
.

Let
\tau_{v}

(4)

Zhang 2018
studied the behavior of
\tau_{v}
for different types of
\mathbf{p}
, particularly with various tail types, and defined several domains of attraction for all distributions on
\mathscr{X}
. Essentially, for distributions with thick-tailed distributions,
\tau_{v}\rightarrow\infty
. To give a sense for such types of distributions,
Zhang 2016
described a sufficient condition for

Proposition 1

(Theorem 6.3 in
Zhang 2016
)
.

If
p_{k+1}/p_{k}\rightarrow 1
, then
\tau_{v}\rightarrow\infty
as
v\rightarrow\infty
.

This is to say that if
p_{k}
decays slowly then
\tau_{v}\rightarrow\infty
. Examples of distribution satisfying
p_{k+1}/p_{k}\rightarrow 1
include,

\displaystyle\text{Power Tail: }\quad p_{k}

\displaystyle\propto{1}/{k^{\lambda}},\quad k\geq k_{0},\quad\lambda>1,\quad k_{0}\geq 1,

(5)

\displaystyle\text{Sub-Exponential Tail: }\quad p_{k}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\lambda k^{\alpha}\right),\quad k\geq k_{0},\quad\lambda>0,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\quad k_{0}\geq 1,

(6)

\displaystyle\text{Near-Exponential Tail: }\quad p_{k}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-{\lambda k}/{\ln^{\beta}k}\right),\quad k\geq k_{0},\quad\lambda>0,\quad\beta>0,\quad k_{0}\geq 1.

(7)

The distributions described above exhibit tail decay rates ranging from very slow to almost exponential. They belong to the Turing-Good family (see
Zhang 2018

\tau_{v}\to\infty
as

Examples of distribution satisfying

distributions with exponential tails,

, for
k\geq k_{0}
,
where
\lambda>0
and
k_{0}\geq 1
. These distributions belong to a domain called the Molchanov family (see

\tau_{v}
perpetually oscillates between two positive constants without a limit as

Discussions on Tail Profile Selection

Selecting an appropriate tail profile is crucial yet challenging.
Hill 1975
’s approach has spurred debate over choosing a suitable tail index (e.g.,
Hall and Welsh 1985
and
Beirlant et al. 1996
), and similar challenges arise here. The following discussion outlines key insights, though further research is warranted.

In practice, the minimum
v
should exceed
\exp\left(\max(\hat{\theta},e)\right)
, where
\hat{\theta}
is the point estimate for the selected tail type. The maximum
v
is guided by the entropic plots; unusual patterns at high
v
values may indicate an upper boundary beyond which estimates become unreliable. The lower bound ensures that
\ln x_{v}>1
(see Lemma
2
in
A.1
). Excessively large
v
values can strain computational accuracy and inflate estimation variance. For example, while
v
up to 3000 is acceptable in R 4.4.2 without Rmpfr (see
Maechler et al. 2024
), simulation studies in Section
4.2
generally suggest that
v
values up to 100 are sufficient. Wide confidence bands may signal that
v
is too large.

When selecting a tail profile, avoid unusual patterns such as sectional downward trends or inflection points. For instance, if Plot 0 trends upward, then Plots 1, 2, and 3 should increase monotonically without inflection.

Furthermore, if any confidence interval has a lower bound of 0 or an upper bound that is infinite (or 1 for sub-exponential tails), it suggests that the sample data do not support the chosen tail profile. Similarly, if a point estimate lies outside its corresponding confidence interval, the profile is likely inappropriate.

In summary, an acceptable tail profile should satisfy:

(1)

v>\exp\Bigl(\max(\hat{\theta},e)\Bigr)
, where
\hat{\theta}
is the tail’s point estimate.

(2)

If Plot 0 trends upward, then Plots 1–3 must be monotonically increasing without inflection.

(3)

Plot 1 should not be convex (concave up), since no valid distribution has a tail thicker than a power law.

(4)

If Plot 1 is linear, then Plots 2 and 3 should be convex.

(5)

If Plot 2 is linear, then Plot 1 should be concave (concave down) and Plot 3 convex.

(6)

If Plot 3 is linear, then Plots 1 and 2 should be concave.

(7)

The lower bound of the selected tail type’s confidence interval should be nonzero.

(8)

For power or near-exponential tails, the upper confidence bound should be finite.

(9)

For sub-exponential tails, the upper confidence bound should be less than 1.

(10)

The point estimate should lie within its corresponding confidence interval.

If any of these conditions are violated, it is advisable to adjust the selection of
v
. Failure to meet these criteria may indicate insufficient sample information to reliably infer the tail behavior.

6
Conclusion

The article introduces a tail classifier based on domains of attraction for countable alphabets. This method extends Hill’s approach to more general settings, enabling inference on tails that decay sub-exponentially, near-exponentially, or exponentially (or thinner). It provides both point estimates and conservative interval estimates for certain parameters. Although the method was originally developed for countable alphabets, Theorem
3
in
A.3
extends its application to continuous distributions by discretizing continuous data into equal-width bins.

The tail classifier is implemented in the R package
TailClassifier
available on CRAN, and it can be conveniently used to identify the tail type of a distribution for refined data analysis. In addition, it can assess whether a tail follows a specific family of distributions. For example, Taylor’s power law (see
Taylor 1961
)—an empirical relationship in ecology linking the variance of species counts per unit area to the corresponding mean via a power law—can be visually evaluated using the proposed method without assuming any specific power-law parameters.

Binning Certain Continuous Variable with Equal Width Preserves Tail Information

Lemma 5
.

Let
X
be a continuous variable with tail probability
x^{-\lambda}
for
x\geq x_{0}>0
. The distribution obtained by binning
X
into equal-width bins has the same tail decay rate as
X
.

Proof.

Given
p_{x}\propto x^{-\lambda}
, for bin width
c
, the probability mass for large
x
is:

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+c}t^{-\lambda}dt

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(x+c)^{1-\lambda}-x^{1-\lambda}}{1-\lambda}\approx x^{-\lambda}.

Thus, binning preserves the tail decay rate.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
X
be a continuous variable with tail probability
e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}
for
x\geq x_{0}>0
. Binning
X
into equal-width bins preserves the tail decay rate.

Proof.

Given
p_{x}\propto e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}
, for bin width
c
, the probability mass is:

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+c}e^{-\lambda t^{\alpha}}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{x+c}e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}e^{-\lambda(t^{\alpha}-x^{\alpha})}dt\approx ce^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}.

Hence, the tail decay rate remains unchanged.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Let
X
be a continuous variable with tail probability
e^{-\lambda x/(\ln x)^{\beta}}
for
x\geq x_{0}>0
. Binning
X
into equal-width bins preserves the tail decay rate.

Proof.

Since
p_{x}\propto e^{-\lambda x/(\ln x)^{\beta}}
is integrable, define

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x+c}e^{-\lambda t/(\ln t)^{\beta}}dt

\displaystyle\approx cG^{\prime}(x)\approx ce^{-\lambda x/(\ln x)^{\beta}}.

Thus, the tail decay rate is preserved.
∎

Theorem 3

Let
