Analysis of a one dimensional energy dissipating free boundary model with nonlinear boundary conditions. Existence of weak solutions
Benoît Merlet, Juliette Venel, Antoine Zurek

Abstract
. This work is part of a general study on the long-term safety of the geological repository of nuclear wastes. A diffusion equation with a moving free boundary in one dimension is introduced and studied. The model describes some mechanisms involved in corrosion processes at the surface of carbon steel canisters in contact with a claystone formation. The main objective of the paper is to prove the existence of weak solutions to the problem which are maximal in time. For this, a time semidiscrete minimizing movements scheme based on a Wasserstein-like distance is introduced. The existence of solutions to the scheme is proved. Then, using a priori estimates, it is shown that as the time step goes to zero these solutions converge up to extraction towards a maximal weak solution to the free boundary model.

1.
Introduction

This work is motivated by the study of the so-called Diffusion Poisson Coupled Model (DPCM) introduced in
[
2
]
by Bataillon
et al.
This system was developed to model the corrosion of a steel plate in contact with a solution. In particular, it is relevant for describing the corrosion of steel canisters containing nuclear wastes which are confined in a glass matrix and stored at a depth of several hundred meters in a claystone layer. Since this storage method is considered by various countries, its reliability requires investigations. In particular, wastes stay radioactive for several hundred of years and it is important to understand the
long term
behaviour of the system. Our main concern is about corrosion and the quantity of hydrogen molecules released during the process which can lead to safety issues. As it is not possible to perform physical experiments at these time scales, the use of reliable models (such as DPCM mentioned above) allowing
in silico
experiments are required. However, to design accurate numerical methods capable of predicting the values of the relevant physical quantities over a long time, it is necessary to understand the mathematical properties of the model.

Let us briefly explain the main features of the DPCM. This is a one dimensional free boundary system. The space is decomposed in three regions: the oxide layer is in contact on one side with the claystone, viewed as an aqueous solution and on the other side with the metal. The DPCM is a system of drift-diffusion equations describing the evolution inside the oxide layer of charge carriers (electrons, Fe
3+
and O
2+
cations) and coupled with a Poisson equation governing the dynamics of the electrical potential. The positions of the solution/oxide layer and oxide layer/metal interfaces evolve along time according to some given ordinary differential equations. Besides, the electrochemical reactions only occur at these interfaces (
i.e.
there are no reaction terms in the bulk of the three regions). These electrochemical reactions are modelled by some nonlinear Fourier boundary conditions at the interfaces.

Due to the numerous coupling of the equations and their definition on a domain with free boundary the mathematical study of the DPCM is a challenging task. So far, only a few results are available in the literature. In
[
8
,
9
]
the well-posedness of the system has been proved for a simplified version of the DPCM where the positions of the interfaces are fixed. A finite-volume scheme approximating the solutions to the DPCM has been proposed in
[
3
]
. The numerical experiments with relevant physical data presented in
[
2
,
3
]
suggest the existence of a global solution to the system. In particular, the existence of travelling wave solutions is established: after a transient time both interfaces move at the same speed, the width of the oxide domain remains constant and the charge carriers and the electrical potential admit a stationary profile. The existence of such travelling wave solutions has been proved in
[
7
]
for a reduced model which assumes electroneutrality in the oxide layer. Thanks to a computer-assisted proof, the existence of travelling wave solutions for the “full” DPCM has been obtained in
[
5
]
. More recently, in
[
6
]
another simplified model (only two species are considered and the interfaces are fixed) is proposed with some changes in the nonlinear boundary conditions that correct a thermodynamical inconsistency of the initial model. This modification makes the mathematical study more tractable in the case of a fixed domain but the well-posedness of the complete free boundary problem is still open.

Up to now, no existence result has been proved for the evolutionary DPCM with free boundaries.
Some of the main difficulties come from the evolution of positions of the free boundaries which are governed by nonlinear equations.
Besides, the structure of the system and in particular its lack of obvious gradient flow structure prevents the derivation of classical
a priori
estimates which would lead (even formally) to a positive lower bound for the width of the oxide layer along time.
1

This general idea might possibly work but we have not found the right way to implement it.
To bypass this difficulty we adapt the approach of Portegies and Peletier in
[

]
which makes use of tools from optimal transport for studying a moving boundary problem. Indeed, in
[
23

, these authors introduce a one dimensional parabolic free boundary model with two moving interfaces describing the variation of the length of a piece of crystal by dissolution/precipitation. The thermodynamical consistency of the model is deeply connected with its gradient-flow structure with respect to some Wasserstein metric. In particular, the existence of solutions to this system is obtained thanks to a Jordan, Kinderlehrer and Otto (JKO) minimizing scheme (see
[
16
]
). The relevance of this approach in the context of parabolic equations in a fixed domain is well known, see for instance
[
1
,
16
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
24
]
and the idea to recast some free boundary problems in the Wasserstein gradient-flow setting seems promising.

The main goal of the present work is to show that this idea is effective for the DPCM model. For this, we consider a free boundary model which, compared to the DPCM, is very simple in the bulk of the oxide layer but retains all the difficulties related to the nonlinear boundary conditions and to the equation of motion of the oxide-metal interface.

1.1.
Presentation of the reduced model

Let us first explain the phenomena that our reduced model has to describe. We only consider the evolution of the density
\rho
of oxygen atoms inside the oxide layer.

In the original model of

We neglect the other charged species as well as the influence of the electrical potential in this domain so that

Next, we fix the position of the interface solution/oxide layer at

(-\infty,0)

for

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(x,t)-\partial^{2}_{x}\rho(x,t)=0

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{for }x\in[0,X(t)],\,t\in[0,T],

(1.2a)

\displaystyle\rho_{-}\leq\rho(0,t)\leq\rho_{+},

(1.2b)

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad\begin{cases}\phantom{aaa}\rho(0,t)=\rho_{+}&\quad\mbox{if }\partial_{x}\rho(0,t)>0,\\

The conditions in (

From a physical point of view, the choice of (

However, there is another phenomenon, neglected in the simplified model considered here, which is irreversible, namely the dissolution of the oxide layer. Indeed, the iron in the aqueous solution, rather than possibly reconstituting the oxide layer, will form oxide complexes, less organized and with a porous structure (rust). For this reason, considering nonlinear monotonic and
non-smooth

In the sequel we will use (

(1.3)

\partial_{x}\rho(0,t)\,(\rho(0,t)-\eta)\geq 0\quad\text{ for every }\rho_{-}\leq\eta\leq\rho_{+}.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

1.2.
Evolution of the Gibbs free energy

In
[
6
]
, the authors consider another simplification of the DPCM and study its well-posedness. In that paper, the space domain is the interval
(0,1)
(no free boundary) and the system describes the evolution of the density
u_{1}
of ferric cations, the density of electrons
u_{2}
and the electric potential
v_{0}
. The system is rewritten by expressing the fluxes
J_{i}
of each chemical species (ferric cations for
i=1
and electrons for
i=2
) on the form

J_{i}=-\sigma_{i}\partial_{x}\xi_{i},

where
\sigma_{i}
and
\xi_{i}
are respectively the mobility and the electrochemical potential of the species
i
. The study relies heavily on the evolution of the physical relevant local quantities (concentrations and electrothermodynamical potentials of each species) as well at the evolution of the global Gibbs energy of the system. As already said, we consider here instead the density of oxygen and by contrast, we retain the free boundary at the oxide/metal interface from the complete model of
[
2
]
. The mathematical treatment of the evolution of this interface is one of the two objectives of the present work (the other one being the treatment of the irreversible boundary condition at
x=0
). However, we follow
[
6
]
by interpreting the system (
1.1
) with a thermodynanymical point of view. More precisely, we consider that the flux of oxygen is given by

J=-\sigma\partial_{x}\xi,

with

\sigma=1/\rho\qquad\text{and}\quad\xi=\log(\rho)+\beta,

for some constant
\beta
. As soon as we consider the evolution of
\rho
in the bulk of the oxide layer the chemical potential may be defined up to an additive constant and the value of
\beta
is irrelevant. However, this is no longer true when we consider exchanges and chemical reactions at the boundary. Here,
\beta>0
(and a new parameter
\theta>0
) are defined from
\rho_{-},\rho_{+}
by the following equations

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\exp(-\beta-\theta)=\rho_{-}\\
\exp(-\beta+\theta)=\rho_{+}\end{array}\right\}\ \iff\\
\log\rho_{\pm}+\beta=\pm\theta\ \iff\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\beta=-\dfrac{1}{2}\log\left(\rho_{-}\rho_{+}\right)\\
\theta=\dfrac{1}{2}\log\left({\rho_{+}/\rho_{-}}\right)\end{array}\right\}.

Now, we define the density of Gibbs energy function in the oxide layer as

(1.5)

f(r):=r(\log r+\beta-1)\qquad\text{for }r\geq 0,

so that

\xi=f^{\prime}(\rho)=\log\rho+\beta.

We also define the Gibbs energy density in the metal domain as
-\rho_{\sharp}
. Then, if we consider an oxide domain
(0,X)
and an oxygen density
\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
\rho\equiv 0
on
(X,+\infty)
, the associated Gibbs energy in a large domain
(0,\Lambda)
(with
\Lambda>X
) is given by

\int_{0}^{X}f(\rho(x))\,dx+\int_{X}^{\Lambda}(-\rho_{\sharp})\,dx=\int_{0}^{X}f(\rho(x))\,dx+\rho_{\sharp}X-\rho_{\sharp}\Lambda.

Finally, we get rid of the last constant term and define the total Gibbs energy of the pair
(\rho,X)
as

(1.6)

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho,X):=\int_{0}^{X}f(\rho(x))\,dx+\rho_{\sharp}X.

Let us now study the evolution of the Gibbs energy of a solution (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). Let
(\rho,X)
be a smooth solution in the space-time domain

(1.7)

D_{T}:=\{(x,t):0<t<T,\,0<x<X(t)\},

and let us denote

\Psi(t):={\bm{\Psi}}(\rho(t),X(t)),

with the notation
\rho(t):=\rho(\cdot,t)
.
We compute

\dot{\Psi}(t)=\int_{0}^{X(t)}\partial_{t}\rho(x,t)\left(\log\rho(x,t)+\beta\right)\,dx+f(\rho_{X})\dot{X}(t)+\rho_{\sharp}\dot{X}(t)=:A(t)+B(t)+C(t),

where we use the shorthands
\rho_{X}
for
\rho(X(t),t)
. Similarly, in the computations below, we write
\partial_{x}\rho_{X}
for
\partial_{x}\rho(X(t),t)
,
\rho_{0}
for
\rho(0,t)
and
\partial_{x}\rho_{0}
for
\partial_{x}\rho(0,t)
.

Using (
1.1a
) and integrating by parts, the first term rewrites as

A(t)=-\int_{0}^{X(t)}\dfrac{(\partial_{x}\rho(x,t))^{2}}{\rho(x,t)}\,dx-\partial_{x}\rho_{0}f^{\prime}(\rho_{0})+\partial_{x}\rho_{X}f^{\prime}(\rho_{X})=:A_{0}(t)+A_{1}(t)+A_{2}(t).

We first notice that the bulk term
A_{0}(t)
is nonpositive and is therefore a dissipation term. The term
A_{1}(t)
accounts for the fluxes through the interface
x=0
. We have

A_{1}(t)\,=-\partial_{x}\rho_{0}\,f^{\prime}(\rho_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{f}}}{{=}}-\partial_{x}\rho_{0}(\log(\rho_{0})+\beta).

Combining the boundary condition (
1.2b
) with (
1.4
), we see that either
\partial_{x}\rho_{0}=0
or

\pm\partial_{x}\rho_{0}>0\implies\rho_{0}=\rho_{\pm}\implies\log(\rho_{0})+\beta=\pm\theta.

In any case, we have

A_{1}(t)\,=-\theta|\partial_{x}\rho_{0}|\leq 0.

Eventually, we gather the boundary terms at
X(t)
, namely
A_{2}(t)
,
B(t)
,
C(t)
. We compute

\displaystyle R_{X}(t)

\displaystyle:=A_{2}(t)+B(t)+C(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{P.c}}}}{{=}}\partial_{x}\rho_{X}f^{\prime}(\rho_{X})+f(\rho_{X})\dot{X}(t)+\rho_{\sharp}\dot{X}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{P.c}}}{{=}}\dot{X}(t)\big(-\rho_{X}f^{\prime}(\rho_{X})+f(\rho_{X})+\rho_{\sharp}\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phantom{\eqref{P.c}}}}{{=}}\dot{X}(t)\left(-\rho_{X}+\rho_{\sharp}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{P.e}}}{{=}}-\lambda\left(\dot{X}(t)\right)^{2}\ \leq 0.

In summary, we have for
t\in[0,T)
,

(1.8)

\dot{\Psi}(t)=-\int_{0}^{X(t)}\dfrac{\left(\partial_{x}\rho(x,t)\right)^{2}}{\rho(x,t)}\,dx-\theta\left|\partial_{x}\rho(0,t)\right|-\lambda\left(\dot{X}(t)\right)^{2},

and the three terms in the right hand side are nonpositive and correspond to different dissipation phenomena, respectively: in the bulk of the domain, at the left interface and at the right interface.

The form of the Lyapunov functional
{\bm{\Psi}}
is typical of Wasserstein gradient flow systems. This suggests using the techniques developed for the latter to analyze our model. However, we cannot claim that (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) admits a gradient flow structure even in the generalized sense introduced by Mielke in
[
20
]
. Indeed, in this theory, one has to specify an energy functional (or driving functional) and a
quadratic
dissipation potential. In our case, using (
1.8
), the energy functional and the dissipation potential are clearly identified. But, due to the linear dissipation term
-\theta\left|\partial_{x}\rho(0,t)\right|
in (
1.8
), we cannot recast the dynamics of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) in an Hilbertian setting and interpret this system as a generalized gradient flow. Nevertheless, the ideas developed in
[

,

]
can be applied in our case in order to prove the existence of weak solutions. In particular, we use a semidiscrete scheme inspired from the JKO-scheme.

1.5.
A few words about the proof of Theorem
1.2

The existence of a weak maximal solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) is established by considering a discretization in time of the system through a minimizing movements scheme
à la De Giorgi
. The problem which is solved at each discrete time is an optimization problem inspired by the JKO scheme.
6
6

6

The JKO scheme of
[
16
]
produces approximate solutions of the Fokker–Planck equation in the whole space.
Here the minimization drives the evolution of the oxygen density in the bulk but also the position of the moving interface
X
and the Signorini boundary conditions at
x=0
. This is reflected, on the one hand, in the driving energy (the same Gibbs free energy introduced above with an additional term
p_{\tau}
penalizing large fluxes at
x=0
) and on the other hand, in the underlying metric (which contains in particular a Wassertein diistance between measure with possibly different masses and supports).

The energy, the metric and the basic optimization step are introduced in Section
2
. The rest of this section is devoted to the properties of the minimizers for one step of the scheme.

The idea of using a minimizing movement scheme to treat moving boundaries is taken from
[
23
]
. However, to take into account the double Signorini boundary condition at
x=0
we are led to introduce a linear term in the metric (see (
2.14
)) which breaks the nice Riemannian-like structure. The most difficult part of the proof of Theorem
1.2
is to establish that the candidate solution obtained in the limit by sending the time step to 0 satisfies the weak form (
1.18
) of this condition. Indeed, the Signorini inequalities involve the trace of
\partial_{x}\rho
for which we do not even have uniform bounds. To improve the regularity of the solution we add a penalty function
p_{\tau}
to the driving energy. This allows us to obtain a bound on
\Lip(\rho)
which degenerates as the time step goes to 0 but which is sufficient, when combined to mollification techniques, to get (
1.18
) at the limit.

,
\nu
as above.

(a)

The optimization problem (
2.1
) admits a dual formulation:

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{I}\varphi\,d\mu+\int_{J}\psi\,d\nu,

where

\mathcal{S}=\left\{(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}):\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\leq(x-y)^{2}\text{ for every }(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\right\}.

(b)

There exists a unique optimal transport plan
\gamma\in{\varPi}(\mu,\nu)
associated with the optimization problem (
2.1
).
This is the unique monotonic transport plan of
{\varPi}(\mu,\nu)
in the sense that
(x_{2}-x_{1})(y_{2}-y_{1})\geq 0
for any
(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\in\supp\gamma
.

(c)

If moreover,
\mu
is atomless then this optimal transport plan
\gamma
is induced by a nondecreasing map
T
such that
\gamma=(\mathrm{id},T)\,\!_{\#}\,\mu
. Recalling the notation
I=[a,b]
,
J=[c,d]
, we have in particular,

\mu([a,x))=\nu([c,T(x))\qquad\text{ for }x\in(a,b].

In this case, there exists a unique (up to an additive constant) Lipschitz function
\Phi
, called Kantorovich potential, such that there holds

\Phi^{\prime}(x)=x-T(x)\quad\mbox{for almost every }x\in I.

As a consequence,

(2.2)

\Lip(\Phi)\leq 2\max_{x\in I,\,y\in J}|x-y|=2\max(|d-a|,|b-c|).

(d)

Assuming that
\mu=p\mathcal{L}
with
p\leq C
and that
\nu
decomposes as
\nu=q\mathcal{L}+\tilde{\nu}
with
\tilde{\nu}\perp\mathcal{L}
and
q\geq\kappa>0
, then
T
is Lipschitz continuous with
0\leq T^{\prime}\leq C/\kappa
and there holds

p(x)=T^{\prime}(x)q(T(x))\qquad\text{ for almost every }x\in(a,b).

2.2.
An unbalanced optimal transport problem

Let
\rho^{0},\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})
be compactly supported. We wish to define a Wasserstein-like distance between
\rho^{0}
and
\rho
. As we do not assume that
\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)
, we cannot transport the measure
\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}
on
\rho\mathcal{L}
and

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho^{0}\mathcal{L},\rho\mathcal{L})=+\infty\qquad\text{whenever }\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\neq\mathbf{M}(\rho).

Here, we start by balancing the masses by adding a Dirac measure at
x=0
. We set:

(2.3)

\mu_{\rho^{0},\rho}:=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,\mathbb{R}_{+}+(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}))_{+}\,\delta_{0},

where
x_{+}:=\max(x,0)
and
\delta_{0}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x=0
.
Symmetrically, we set

\mu_{\rho,\rho^{0}}=\rho\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,\mathbb{R}_{+}+(\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho))_{+}\,\delta_{0}.

For shortness, we write

(2.4)

\mu^{0}:=\mu_{\rho^{0},\rho},\quad\qquad\mu:=\mu_{\rho,\rho^{0}}.

These measures are compactly supported and satisfy
\mu^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\mu(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. We define

(2.5)

\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0},\rho):=\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\mu^{0},\mu\right).

We see that
\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0},\rho)<\infty
for any pair of compactly supported functions
\rho^{0},\rho\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Remark 2.2
.

Using the dual formulation in Theorem
2.1
(a), we see that

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho^{0},\rho)=\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{S}}B((\rho^{0},\rho),(\varphi,\psi)),

where we introduce the bilinear functional,

B((\rho^{0},\rho),(\varphi,\psi)):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho^{0}\varphi\,+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho\psi\,+\left[\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right]_{+}\varphi(0)-\left[\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho)\right]_{+}\psi(0).

Let us consider two sequences
(\rho^{0}_{j})
and
(\rho_{j})
of nonnegative functions in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
all supported in
[0,\Lambda]
for some
\Lambda>0
and such that,

\rho^{0}_{j}\longrightarrow\rho^{0},\qquad\rho_{j}\longrightarrow\rho\qquad\text{weakly-$\star$ in }\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\text{ as }j\uparrow\infty,

that is,

\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho^{0}_{j}\phi\,\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\uparrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho^{0}\phi\,,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho_{j}\phi\,\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\uparrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\rho\phi\,\qquad\text{for every }\phi\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}).

In particular, by taking
\phi\equiv 1
, we have
\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}_{j})\to\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
and
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})\to\mathbf{M}(\rho)
.

For such sequences and for
(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{S}
, there holds

\lim_{j\uparrow\infty}B((\rho^{0}_{j},\rho_{j}),(\varphi,\psi))=B((\rho^{0},\rho),(\varphi,\psi)).

Taking the supremum over
(\varphi,\psi)\in\mathcal{S}
we obtain

\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0},\rho)\leq\lim_{j\uparrow\infty}\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0}_{j},\rho_{j}).

In other words, the distance
\mathbf{W}_{2}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to the weak-
\star
convergence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
in the subset of nonnegative and uniformly compactly supported functions.

According to Theorem
2.1
, the measure
\mu
is sent monotonically on the measure
\mu^{0}

.
In this case we define

by

\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}\rho^{0}\,=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho),

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0})=\int_{0}^{X}(x-T_{-}(x))^{2}\,\rho(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}y^{2}\,\rho^{0}(y)\,dy.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

In the sequel, we set by convention

(2.10)

\begin{cases}\ell_{-}=0&\text{ when }\mathbf{M}(\rho)\geq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}),\\
\ell_{+}=0&\text{ when }\mathbf{M}(\rho)\leq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}).\end{cases}

2.4.
Definition of one step of the minimizing movement scheme

In the rest of Section
2
, the time step
\tau>0
is fixed as well with the coefficients
\hat{\rho}_{+}\geq\rho_{-}>0
,
\rho_{\sharp}>0
and
\lambda>0
as in assumption (H1) of Theorem
1.2
.

Let us first introduce the set of candidates for the minimization problem.

(2.11)

\mathbb{A}:=\left\{(\rho,X)\in L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\times(0,+\infty):\rho\geq 0\ \text{ and }\ \rho\equiv 0\text{ on }(X,+\infty)\right\}.

For
\rho\in L_{c}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, we denote

(2.12)

x_{\rho}:=\inf\left\{x\geq 0:\rho\equiv 0\text{ almost everywhere on }(x,+\infty)\right\}=\inf\left\{x\geq 0:\supp\rho\subset[0,x]\right\}.

We then define the following subset of
\mathbb{A}
,

(2.13)

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}:X=x_{\rho},\ \rho_{|[0,X]}\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}([0,X]),\ \rho>0\text{ on }[0,X]\right\}.

Remark that for
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
,
\rho
does not vanish identically (indeed
x_{\rho}=X>0
). Notice also that
X
is uniquely determined by
\rho
so that we could get rid of the explicit mention of
X
. However it is convenient to see
\mathcal{A}
as a subset of
\mathbb{A}
.

Energy functional.
We repeat the definition (
1.6
) of the functional
{\bm{\Psi}}:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
:

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho,X):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f(\rho(x))\,dx+\rho_{\sharp}X,

where (recall (
1.5
)),
f(r)=r(\log r+\beta-1)
.

Distance on
L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
For every
(\rho,X)
,
(\rho^{0},X^{0})\in L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
we define the following “distances”
\textbf{d}_{1}
,
\textbf{d}_{2}
on
\mathbb{A}
,

(2.14)

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{1}\left((\rho,X),(\rho^{0},X^{0})\right)

\displaystyle:=\theta\left|\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right|,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{2}^{\,2}\left((\rho,X),(\rho^{0},X^{0})\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)+\lambda\left(X-X^{0}\right)^{2}.\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt

We notice that
\textbf{d}_{1}
is built on a
L^{1}
-distance and that
\textbf{d}_{2}
is modelled on a
L^{2}
-distance (the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}
is thought of as a Riemannian metric on sets of positive measures).

Penalty term.
Let
\omega\in(0,1)
to be fixed later. We define
p_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

(2.16)

p_{\tau}(m):=\dfrac{1}{2\tau^{1-\omega}}\left(|m|-\tau^{1-\omega}\right)_{\!+}^{\,2}\qquad\text{for }m\in\mathbb{R},

where we recall that
\tau>0
is the time step. The function
p_{\tau}
is used as a technical penalization term to ensure some Lipschitz bounds on the solutions of the minimization problem (
2.19
) below (see Proposition
2.16
). The exponent
\omega\in(0,1)
will be fixed at the end of the proof (see the condition (
3.47
) of Proposition
3.12
).

Minimization problem.
We define the functional
\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}:\,\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
by

(2.17)

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X):=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\textbf{d}_{2}^{\,2}\left((\rho,X),(\rho^{0},X^{0})\right)+\textbf{d}_{1}\left((\rho,X),(\rho^{0},X^{0})\right)\\
+p_{\tau}\left(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right)+{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho,X).

Expanding all terms, the functional decomposes into a bulk term (depending on the function
\rho
), a left boundary term (depending on
\mathbf{M}(\rho)
) and a right boundary term (depending on
X
).

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)=

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)+\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f(\rho(x))\,dx

(2.18)

\displaystyle+\theta\left|\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right|+p_{\tau}\left(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\lambda\left(X-X^{0}\right)^{2}+\rho_{\sharp}X.

Eventually, we introduce the optimization problem

(2.19)

\min_{(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}}\,\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X).

2.5.
Existence of a minimizer and first properties

Throughout the paper, we assume that
(\rho^{0},X^{0})\in\mathcal{A}
and
\rho_{-}\leq\rho^{0}(0)\leq\rho_{+}
so that the assumption (H2) of Theorem
1.2
holds true. Using the Direct Method of the Calculus of Variation, we establish in Theorem
2.3
that (
2.19
) admits at least one solution when the time step
\tau>0
is small enough. Then, in Theorem
2.5
we establish that such minimizer
(\rho,X)
belongs to
\mathcal{A}
with
\log\rho
of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
[0,X]
, we describe the boundary condition satisfied by
\rho
at
x=0
and we give a bound on
|\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})|
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let us denote

R:=\rho_{\sharp}-\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad P(\rho^{0},X^{0}):=\int_{0}^{X^{0}}f(\rho^{0})\,+\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}\,\,X^{0}={\bm{\Psi}}(\rho^{0},X^{0})-RX^{0}.

Provided that

(2.20)

X^{0}>\dfrac{\tau R}{\lambda}+\sqrt{\dfrac{\tau^{2}R^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\dfrac{2\tau}{\lambda}P(\rho^{0},X^{0})}\,,

the minimization problem (
2.19
) admits at least one solution
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Since
X^{0}>0
, the condition (
2.20
) holds true for
\tau>0
small enough (depending on
(\rho^{0},X^{0})
).

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
(\rho_{j},X_{j})\in\mathbb{A}
be a minimizing sequence for (
2.19
) indexed by
j\geq 1
. Considering the candidate
(\rho,X)=(\rho^{0},X^{0})
, we may assume that

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{j},X_{j})\leq\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho^{0},X^{0})={\bm{\Psi}}(\rho^{0},X^{0}).

In particular,

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho_{j},X_{j})+\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}\leq{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho^{0},X^{0})\qquad\text{for }j\geq 1.

Using the definition (
1.6
) of
{\bm{\Psi}}
and rearranging the terms we get

\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}+\rho_{\sharp}(X_{j}-X^{0})\leq\int_{0}^{X^{0}}f(\rho^{0})\,-\int_{0}^{X_{j}}f(\rho_{j})\,.

We then use
\min_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}f=-\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}
to obtain

\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}+(\rho_{\sharp}-\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}})(X_{j}-X^{0})\leq\int_{0}^{X^{0}}f(\rho^{0})\,+\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}X^{0}.\\

Denoting
x:=X_{j}-X^{0}
and defining, as in the statement of the proposition,

R:=\rho_{\sharp}-\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}\quad\text{ and }\quad P:=P(\rho^{0},X^{0}):=\int_{0}^{X^{0}}f(\rho^{0})\,+\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}X^{0},

the last inequality rewrites as

(2.21)

x^{2}+2\dfrac{\tau R}{\lambda}x-\dfrac{2\tau P}{\lambda}\leq 0.

Notice that

P=\int_{0}^{X^{0}}f(\rho^{0})\,+\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}}\,X^{0}\geq(\min f+\sqrt{\rho_{+}\rho_{-}})X^{0}=0.

Computing the roots of the polynomial function of
x
in the left hand side of (
2.21
), we have

(2.22)

X_{j}=X^{0}+x\geq X^{0}-\dfrac{\tau R}{\lambda}-\sqrt{\dfrac{\tau^{2}R^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\dfrac{2\tau P}{\lambda}}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{X0large}}}{{\geq}}\,\varepsilon,

for some
\varepsilon>0
.

Now, we write

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho^{0},X^{0})\geq\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{j},X_{j})\geq\int_{0}^{X_{j}}f(\rho_{j})\,+\rho_{\sharp}X_{j}+\dfrac{\lambda(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}}{2\tau}.

By convexity, of
f
, the first term of the right hand side satisfies

\int_{0}^{X_{j}}f(\rho_{j})\,\geq X_{j}f(\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})/X_{j}),

and writing
Y_{j}:=\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})/X_{j}
, we get

(2.23)

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho^{0},X^{0})\geq f(Y_{j})X_{j}+\rho_{\sharp}X_{j}+\dfrac{\lambda(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}}{2\tau}.

The term
\rho_{\sharp}X_{j}+\lambda(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}/(2\tau)
is bounded from below and since
X_{j}\geq\varepsilon>0
by (
2.22
) and
\lim_{+\infty}f=+\infty
, (
2.23
) implies that the sequence
(Y_{j})
is bounded. By continuity of
f
, the sequence
(f(Y_{j}))
is bounded and using again (
2.23
) with this information, we deduce that
X_{j}
is bounded. Now, as
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})=Y_{j}/X_{j}\leq Y_{j}/\varepsilon
, we get that
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j}))=\|\rho_{j}\|_{L^{1}}
is bounded.

Consequently, there exists
X\geq\varepsilon
such that, up to extraction,
X_{j}\to X
as
j\uparrow\infty
. Let us fix
\Lambda>0
such that
X_{j}\leq\Lambda
for
j\geq 1
(hence
\rho_{j}\equiv 0
on
(X_{j},+\infty)
). We see that the sequence
(\int_{0}^{\Lambda}\rho_{j}\log\rho_{j}\,)
is uniformly bounded and we deduce that the sequence
(\rho_{j})
is equi-integrable. By Dunford–Pettis theorem, there exists
\rho\in L^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}
such that, up to extraction,
\rho_{j}\to\rho
weakly in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. By Remark
2.2
, we have

\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j\uparrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \mathbf{M}(\rho)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0},\rho)\leq\liminf_{j\uparrow\infty}\mathbf{W}_{2}(\rho^{0},\rho_{j}).

Eventually, by convexity and lower semicontinuity of
f
the weak convergence
\rho_{j}\to\rho
yields

\int f(\rho)\,\leq\liminf_{j\uparrow\infty}\int f(\rho_{j})\,.

We conclude that

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)\leq\liminf_{j\uparrow\infty}\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{j},X_{j}),

so that
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}
is a minimizer of (
2.19
).
∎

(Existence of a minimizer in

)
.

Let

\mathcal{C}^{1,1}

\mathcal{C}^{\infty}

Proof.

Let
(\rho,X)
be a minimizer of (
2.19
). We start by noticing that
X>0
being fixed,
\rho
solves a one step of a classical JKO scheme. Let us be more specific. With the convention (
2.10
) on
\ell_{-}
and
\ell_{+}
, we denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}^{0}

\displaystyle:=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(\ell_{-},X^{0})+[\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})]_{+}\delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(0,X^{0})+[\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})]\delta_{0}&\text{ if }\mathbf{M}(\rho)\geq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}),\\
\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(\ell_{-},X^{0})&\text{ if }\mathbf{M}(\rho)<\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}).\end{cases}

By construction
\supp\tilde{\mu}^{0}=[\ell_{-},X^{0}]
,
\tilde{\mu}^{0}(\mathbb{R}_{+})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)
and
\rho\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(0,X)
minimizes the functional

(2.24)

\nu\mapsto\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\tilde{\mu}^{0},\nu)+\int_{0}^{X}f(v)\,,

in the set of measures

\mathcal{M}_{\rho}=\left\{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}([0,X]):\nu([0,X])=\mathbf{M}(\rho),\ \nu=v\mathcal{L}\,\text{ for some }v\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})\right\}.

In other words,
\rho
is the solution of one step of a classical JKO scheme in
[0,X]
with initial data
\tilde{\mu}^{0}
and time step
\tau>0
. This is a well known situation, for which we refer to
[
24
, Chapter 7.4.1]
. Recaling the notation
T_{+}
or
T_{-}
of (
2.7
), (
2.9
) for the optimal transport map we set, for
x\in[0,X]
,

(2.25)

T(x):=\begin{cases}T_{+}(x)&\text{ if }\mathbf{M}(\rho)\geq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}),\\
T_{-}(x)&\text{ if }\mathbf{M}(\rho)<\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}).\end{cases}

In both cases,
\tilde{\mu}^{0}=T\,\!_{\#}\,\rho
.
The function
\rho
being a minimizer of (
2.24
) in
\mathcal{M}_{\rho}
, we conclude from
[
24
, Chapter 7.4.1]
, that this minimizer is unique and Lipschitz continuous. Besides, using the notation of Theorem
2.1
, the Kantorovitch potential
\Phi
associated with the transport
T
is Lipschitz continuous and we have the relation

(2.26)

\dfrac{\Phi(x)}{\tau}+\log\rho(x)=C\qquad\mbox{ for }x\in[0,X],

for some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular,
\rho>0
in
[0,X]
which proves that
x_{\rho}=X
. We conclude that
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
.

Next, we note from point (d) of Theorem
2.1
, we have

(2.27)

T^{\prime}(x)=\rho(x)/\rho^{0}(T(x)),

for almost every
x\in[\ell_{+},X]
and from point (c) of the same theorem we have

(2.28)

\Phi^{\prime}(x)=x-T(x)\qquad\mbox{for }x\in[0,X].

Since
T^{\prime}
is bounded on
[\ell_{+},X]
by (
2.27
) and vanishes on
[0,\ell_{+}]
, we deduce that
T\in\mathcal{C}^{0,1}([0,X])
. In particular, the relation (
2.28
) implies that
\Phi\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}([0,X])
. Thus, by (
2.26
) it holds

\log\rho\,\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}([0,X]).

Hence, we conclude from (
2.27
) and assumption (H2) of Theorem
1.2
, that
T\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}([\ell_{+},X])
. Eventually, if
\ell_{+}>0
, since
T
vanishes in
[0,\ell_{+}]
, the relation (
2.26
) implies

\rho(x)=C\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2\tau}\right)\qquad\mbox{for }x\in[0,\ell_{+}],

so that
\rho\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}([0,\ell_{+}])
. This ends the proof of the theorem.
∎

2.6.
The optimization problem (
2.19
) as a time discretisation of (
1.1
)

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
be a minimizer of the optimization problem (
2.19
).Then, there holds

(2.29)

\int_{0}^{X}\left|\rho^{\prime}(x)\right|^{2}\,dx\leq\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\,\dfrac{\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0})}{\tau^{2}}.

Next, for every
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\dfrac{\rho(x)-\rho^{0}(x)}{\tau}\,\psi(x)\,dx-\dfrac{\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})}{\tau}\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{X}\rho^{\prime}(x)\,\psi^{\prime}(x)\,dx=Q_{\tau}(\psi),

where the right hand side satisfies

(2.30)

\left|Q_{\tau}(\psi)\right|\leq\dfrac{\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{2\tau}\,\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0})+\dfrac{\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{\tau}\,\left(\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}y\,\rho^{0}(y)\,dy\right).

Proof.

We use the notation of the proof of Theorem
2.5
. In particular,
T
is defined by (
2.25
). From (
2.26
) and (
2.28
), we have
(\log\rho)^{\prime}=(T(x)-x)/\tau
on
[0,X]
,
hence

(2.31)

\rho^{\prime}(x)=\dfrac{T(x)-x}{\tau}\rho(x)\qquad\text{for }x\in[0,X].

Step 1.

Using (
2.31
), squaring and integrating on
[0,X]
, we have,

\int_{0}^{X}(\rho^{\prime})^{2}(x)\,dx=\dfrac{1}{\tau^{2}}\int_{0}^{X}(T(x)-x)^{2}\rho^{2}(x)\,dx\leq\dfrac{\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{\tau^{2}}\int_{0}^{X}(T(x)-x)^{2}\rho(x)\,dx.

This proves (
2.29
).

Step 2.
Let
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Multiplying the last identity by
\psi^{\prime}
and integrating on
[0,X]
, we obtain,

\int_{0}^{X}\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\dfrac{x-T(x)}{\tau}\,\rho(x)\,dx+\int_{0}^{X}\rho^{\prime}(x)\,\psi^{\prime}(x)\,dx=0.

We then only have to show that

(2.32)

\int_{0}^{X}\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\dfrac{x-T(x)}{\tau}\,\rho(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\dfrac{\rho(x)-\rho^{0}(x)}{\tau}\psi(x)\,dx-\dfrac{\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})}{\tau}\psi(0)+Q_{\tau}(\psi),

for some
Q_{\tau}(\psi)
satisfying the estimate (
2.30
). We consider two cases.

Case 1,
\mathbf{M}(\rho)\geq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.
Writing the Taylor expansion,

\psi(T_{+}(x))=\psi(x)+\psi^{\prime}(x)(T_{+}(x)-x)+\int_{x}^{T_{+}(x)}(T_{+}(x)-y)\,\psi^{\prime\prime}(y)\,dy,

we get

(2.33)

\psi^{\prime}(x)(x-T_{+}(x))=\psi(x)-\psi(T_{+}(x))+R_{1}(x)\qquad\text{with}\quad|R_{1}(x)|\leq\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\dfrac{(T_{+}(x)-x)^{2}}{2}.

Multiplying by
\rho(x)/\tau
and integrating, we obtain,

(2.34)

\int_{0}^{X}\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\dfrac{x-T_{+}(x)}{\tau}\,\rho(x)\,dx=\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx-\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(T_{+}(x))\,dx+Q_{\tau}(\psi),

where
Q_{\tau}(\psi)
satisfies

\left|Q_{\tau}(\psi)\right|\leq\dfrac{\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0}).

Recalling the definition (
2.7
) of the mapping
T_{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(T_{+}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\ell_{+}}\psi(0)\,\rho(x)\,dx+\int_{\ell_{+}}^{X}\psi(T_{+}(x))\,\rho(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\right]\psi(0)+\int_{0}^{X^{0}}\rho^{0}(y)\,\psi(y)\,dy.

Putting this in (
2.34
), we conclude that (
2.32
) holds in this case.

Case 2,
\mathbf{M}(\rho)<\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.
Applying (
2.33
) with
T_{-}
instead of
T_{+}
, we obtain

(2.35)

\int_{0}^{X}\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\dfrac{x-T_{-}(x)}{\tau}\,\rho(x)\,dx=\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx-\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(T_{-}(x))\,dx+Q^{a}_{\tau}(\psi),

with

(2.36)

\left|Q_{\tau}^{a}(\psi)\right|\leq\dfrac{\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0}).

Using the definitions (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) of
\ell_{-}
and
T_{-}
, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{X}\rho(x)\,\psi(T_{-}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\ell_{-}}^{X^{0}}\rho^{0}(y)\,\psi(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{X^{0}}\rho^{0}(y)\,\psi(y)\,dy-\left[\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho)\right]\psi(0)-\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}\rho^{0}(y)\left(\psi(y)-\psi(0)\right)\,dy.

Putting this in (
2.35
), we obtain

(2.37)

\int_{0}^{X}\psi^{\prime}(x)\,\dfrac{x-T_{-}(x)}{\tau}\,\rho(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(x)\,\dfrac{\rho(x)-\rho^{0}(x)}{\tau}\,dx-\dfrac{\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})}{\tau}\psi(0)\\
+Q_{\tau}^{a}(\psi)+Q_{\tau}^{b}(\psi),

where the last term is

Q_{\tau}^{b}(\psi):=\dfrac{1}{\tau}\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}\rho^{0}(y)\,(\psi(y)-\psi(0))\,dy\quad\text{ for which }\quad\left|Q_{\tau}^{b}(\psi)\right|\leq\dfrac{\|\psi^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}}{\tau}\left(\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}y\rho^{0}(y)\,dy\right).

Eventually, (
2.32
) follows from the last estimate, (
2.36
) and (
2.37
). This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

2.7.
Behavior of the minimizers at the fixed interface

The next result describes the boundary condition satisfied by

at

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
be a minimizer of (

), then we have the following alternatives.

(2.38)

\begin{cases}\log\rho(0)=\log\rho_{+}+p^{\prime}_{\tau}(\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho))&\text{if }\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})>\mathbf{M}(\rho),\\

Recalling the definition (
2.16
) of the penalty function
p_{\tau}
, we notice that for
M>0
,
p^{\prime}_{\tau}(M)=(M\tau^{\omega-1}-1)_{+}\geq 0
.

\rho
at

according to (

.

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

We split the proof in four steps. In each step, we introduce some values
m_{\varepsilon}
and some candidates
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
depending on
\varepsilon>0
. Please note that these objects are not the same from one step to the next.

Step 1.
We first consider the case
\mathbf{M}(\rho)\leq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
and we set
M:=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\leq 0
. We use the notation
\ell_{-}
from (
2.8
),
i.e.

\int_{0}^{\ell-}\rho^{0}\,=-M,

and we define
\mu^{0}=\rho^{0}\,\mathcal{L}
and
\mu=\rho\,\mathcal{L}+(-M)\delta_{0}
. By Theorem
2.1
, there exists a nondecreasing Lipschitz mapping
S_{0}:[0,X^{0}]\to[0,X]
such that
\mu=S_{0}\,\!{}_{\#}\,\mu^{0}
. In particular,

S_{0}=0
on
[0,\ell_{-}]
and
S_{0}
is increasing on
[\ell_{-},X^{0}]
with
S_{0}([\ell_{-},X^{0}])=[0,X]
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
small enough so that
y_{\varepsilon}:=\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}<X^{0}
and let us define

x_{\varepsilon}:=S_{0}(y_{\varepsilon})\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\ell_{-}}^{\ell_{-}+\varepsilon}\rho^{0}\,>0.

We notice that since
\rho^{0},\rho\in\mathcal{A}
and
S_{0}
is Lipschitz continuous, there exists some
c>0
such that

c\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq x_{\varepsilon}\leq c^{-1}\sqrt{\varepsilon}\quad\text{ and}\quad c\varepsilon\leq m_{\varepsilon}\leq c^{-1}\varepsilon.

Let us set for
x\geq 0
,

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x):=\begin{cases}\rho(x)-\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}&\text{for }x\in[0,x_{\varepsilon}],\\
\ \quad\rho(x)&\text{for }x\in(x_{\varepsilon},X].\\
\ \ \quad 0&\text{for }x>X.\end{cases}

The pair
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
is a candidate for the optimization problem (
2.19
) which satisfies
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-m_{\varepsilon}
. Since
\rho
is continuous and positive on
[0,X]
and
m_{\varepsilon}/x_{\varepsilon}=O(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, we have
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. For such
\varepsilon
we have by optimality of
(\rho,X)
,

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)\leq\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{\varepsilon},X).

This rewrites as

(2.39)

q(\varepsilon):=\int_{0}^{x_{\varepsilon}}f(\rho(x))\,dx-\int_{0}^{x_{\varepsilon}}f\left(\rho(x)-\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}\right)\,dx-\theta m_{\varepsilon}+p_{\tau}(-M)-p_{\tau}(-M+m_{\varepsilon})\\
\leq\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\left(\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\right).

Sending
\varepsilon
to 0 we have for the left hand side of (
2.39
),

(2.40)

q(\varepsilon)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{\sim}}\ \left[f^{\prime}(\rho(0))-\theta-p_{\tau}^{\prime}(-M)\right]m_{\varepsilon}=\left[\log\rho(0)-\log\rho_{+}-p_{\tau}^{\prime}(\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho))\right]m_{\varepsilon}.

Here, we have used the identities
f^{\prime}(r)=\log r+\beta
and
-\beta+\theta=\log\rho_{+}
from the definitions of
\theta
,
\beta
and
f
in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Recalling
c\varepsilon\leq m_{\varepsilon}\leq c^{-1}\varepsilon
, it is enough to establish that

(2.41)

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)=o(\varepsilon),

to deduce from (
2.39
) and (
2.40
) that

(2.42)

\log\rho(0)\leq\log\rho_{+}+p_{\tau}^{\prime}(\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-\mathbf{M}(\rho)).

Let us prove (
2.41
). The optimal transport map sending
\mu^{0}
to
\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}+(-M+m_{\varepsilon})\delta_{0}
is the unique non-decreasing mapping
S_{\varepsilon}:[0,X^{0}]\to[0,X]
such that
S_{\varepsilon}\,\!{}_{\#}\,\mu^{0}=\mu_{\varepsilon}
. We have by Theorem
2.1
,

(2.43)

\begin{cases}S_{\varepsilon}(y)=0&\text{for }0\leq y\leq\ell_{-}+\varepsilon,\\
S_{\varepsilon}(x)=S_{0}(x)&\text{for }y_{\varepsilon}=\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\leq y\leq X^{0},\\
\displaystyle\int_{\ell_{-}+\varepsilon}^{y}\rho^{0}(y)\,dy=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{S_{\varepsilon}(y)}\left(\rho(x)-\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}\right)\,dx&\text{for }0\leq\ell_{-}+\varepsilon\leq y\leq y_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Notice that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough,
S_{\varepsilon}
is Lipschitz continuous with a uniform Lipschitz constant.

Let us now estimate
|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|
. We first observe that

S_{\varepsilon}=S_{0}\quad\text{on }[0,\ell_{-}]\cup[\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon},X^{0}].

Besides, since
S_{0}(\ell_{-})=0
and
S_{\varepsilon}=0
on
[0,\ell_{-}+\varepsilon]
, we have

|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|\leq\Lip(S_{0})\varepsilon\quad\text{ on }[\ell_{-},\ell_{-}+\varepsilon].

For the remaining values, we deduce from the last case of (
2.43
) that for
y\in[\ell_{-}+\varepsilon,\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}]
,

\left[\rho(S_{\varepsilon}(y))-\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}\right]S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)=\rho^{0}(y)=\rho(S_{0}(y))S_{0}^{\prime}(y).

Rearranging the terms we obtain,

\rho(S_{0}(y))\left[S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)-S_{0}^{\prime}(y)\right]=(\rho(S_{0}(y))-\rho(S_{\varepsilon}(y)))S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)+\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}S_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y).

Denoting
\eta:=\min_{[0,X]}\rho>0
, this leads to

\left|(S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0})^{\prime}\right(y)|\leq\dfrac{\Lip(S_{\varepsilon})}{\eta}\left[\dfrac{m_{\varepsilon}}{x_{\varepsilon}}+\Lip(\rho_{\,|[0,X]})|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|(y)\right].

Integrating the above inequality and using the estimates
|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|(\ell_{-}+\varepsilon)\leq\Lip(S_{0})\varepsilon
and
m_{\varepsilon}/x_{\varepsilon}\leq c^{-2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, we obtain that there exists
C\geq 0
such that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough,

|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|(y)\leq C\left(\varepsilon+\int_{\ell_{-}+\varepsilon}^{y}|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|(z)\,dz\right)\qquad\text{for }y\in\left[\ell_{-}+\varepsilon,\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right].

By Gronwall lemma we deduce that

|S_{\varepsilon}-S_{0}|\leq C^{\prime}\varepsilon\qquad\text{on }\left[\ell_{-}+\varepsilon,\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right],

for some
C^{\prime}\geq 0
. Increasing
C^{\prime}
if necessary, we have
C^{\prime}\geq\Lip(S_{0})
and the inequality holds true also on
[\ell_{-},\ell_{-}+\varepsilon)
. Now, we can estimate

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\ell_{-}}^{\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(\left[y-S_{\varepsilon}(y)\right]^{2}-\left[y-S_{0}(y)\right]^{2}\right)\rho^{0}(y)\,dy\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\ell_{-}}^{\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\left(S_{\varepsilon}(y)+2y+S_{0}(y)\right)\,|S_{\varepsilon}(y)-S_{0}(y)|\rho^{0}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\leq 2\max(X^{0},X)\left(\max_{[0,X^{0}]}\rho^{0}\right)\int_{\ell_{-}}^{\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}}C^{\prime}\varepsilon\,dy

\displaystyle=2C^{\prime}\max(X^{0},X)\left(\max_{[0,X^{0}]}\rho^{0}\right)\varepsilon^{3/2}=o(\varepsilon).

This establishes (
2.41
) and thus (
2.42
). Let us notice here that in the limit case
M=0
, we have
p^{\prime}_{\tau}(M)=0
and (
2.42
) leads to

(2.44)

\mathbf{M}(\rho)=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\ \implies\ \log\rho(0)\leq\log\rho_{+},

which is the second inequality of the case
\mathbf{M}(\rho)=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
of the proposition.

Step 2.
Let us now assume that
M=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})<0
and let us establish the reverse inequality of (
2.42
). Instead of sending more mass from
\mu^{0}
to the point
x=0
, we send less. More precisely, for
0<\varepsilon<1
such that
\varepsilon<\ell_{-}
we define
y_{\varepsilon}:=\ell_{-}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}<X^{0}
and

m_{\varepsilon}:=-\int_{\ell_{-}-\varepsilon}^{\ell_{-}}\rho^{0}\,<0.

Then, denoting,
x_{\varepsilon}=S_{0}(y_{\varepsilon})
as above we introduce a new candidate
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
for the optimization problem (
2.19
),

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x):=\begin{cases}\rho(x)-m_{\varepsilon}/x_{\varepsilon}&\text{for }x\in[0,x_{\varepsilon}],\\
~\quad\rho(x)&\text{for }x\in(x_{\varepsilon},X].\end{cases}

We have
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-m_{\varepsilon}
and repeating the computations of Step 1, we obtain (
2.40
) and (
2.41
). As in Step 1, the right hand side of (

) is negligible compared to
|m_{\varepsilon}|\sim\rho^{0}(\ell_{-})\varepsilon
. However, now

and when we send
\varepsilon
to 0, we obtain the reverse inequality of (
2.42
). This proves the case
\mathbf{M}(\rho)<\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
of the proposition.

Step 3.
We now assume that
M:=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\geq 0
. For
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon<X
, we define the candidate
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
for (
2.19
) by

(2.45)

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}\rho(x)+\varepsilon&\text{for }0\leq x\leq\varepsilon,\\
\quad\rho(x)&\text{for }x>\varepsilon.\end{cases}

We have
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)+\varepsilon^{2}
. By optimality of
(\rho,X)
we have
\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)\leq\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)
which reads,

(2.46)

q(\varepsilon):=\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f(\rho(x))\,dx-\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}f(\rho(x)+\varepsilon)\,dx-\theta\varepsilon^{2}+p_{\tau}(M)-p_{\tau}(M+\varepsilon^{2})\\
\leq\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\left(\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\right).

Sending
\varepsilon
to 0 we have for the left hand side of (

),

(2.47)

q(\varepsilon)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varepsilon\downarrow 0}}{{\sim}}\ \left[-f^{\prime}(\rho(0))-\theta-p_{\tau}^{\prime}(M)\right]\varepsilon^{2}=-\left[\log\rho(0)-\log\rho_{-}+p_{\tau}^{\prime}(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}))\right]\varepsilon^{2},

where we have used
f^{\prime}(r)=\log r+\beta
and
-\beta-\theta=\log\rho_{-}
from the definitions (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). Next, we estimate the right hand side of (
2.46
). By definition,
\mu^{0}=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}+M\delta_{0}
and

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}\right).

Similarly, denoting
\mu^{0}_{\varepsilon}:=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}+(M+\varepsilon^{2})\delta_{0}
, we have

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}_{\varepsilon}\right).

Let
\gamma
be the optimal transport plan from
\mu

\mu^{0}
. Setting

\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma+\varepsilon\left(\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(0,\varepsilon)\right)\otimes\delta_{0},

we have
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\in{\varPi}(\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}_{\varepsilon})
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}_{\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\iint_{[0,X]\times[0,X^{0}]}|x-y|^{2}\,d\gamma_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\iint_{[0,X]\times[0,X^{0}]}|x-y|^{2}\,d\gamma(x,y)+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\varepsilon}x^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}\right)+\varepsilon^{4}/3=\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)+\varepsilon^{4}/3.

Using this inequality in the right hand side of (
2.46
), dividing by
\varepsilon^{2}
and sending
\varepsilon
to 0 we deduce from (
2.47
) the inequality

(2.48)

\log\rho(0)\geq\log\rho_{-}-p_{\tau}^{\prime}(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})).

In particular,

\mathbf{M}(\rho)=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})\ \implies\ \log\rho(0)\geq\log\rho_{-}.

Together with (
2.44
), this proves the proposition in the case
\mathbf{M}(\rho)=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.

Step 4.
Eventually, we assume that
M=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})>0
and we establish the reverse inequality of (
2.48
).
For this, contrarily to (
2.45
) we remove a small mass from
\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}
near 0. Let
\varepsilon>0
such that
\varepsilon^{2}<M
,
\varepsilon<X
and
\varepsilon<\min_{[0,X]}\rho
, we define a candidate
(\rho_{\varepsilon},X)\in\mathbb{A}
for (
2.19
) by

\rho_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}\rho(x)-\varepsilon&\text{for }0\leq x\leq\varepsilon,\\
\quad\rho(x)&\text{for }x>\varepsilon.\end{cases}

We have
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{\varepsilon})=\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\varepsilon^{2}
. Following the reasoning of the previous step, the optimality of
(\rho,X)
now leads to

(2.49)

\log\rho(0)-\log\rho_{-}+p_{\tau}^{\prime}(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}))\leq\dfrac{1}{2\tau\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\right)+o(1).

In order to establish that the right hand side is negligible we proceed as in the previous step. We define the transport plan from
\mu_{\varepsilon}:=\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L}
to
\mu_{0}-\varepsilon^{2}\delta_{0}
as

\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma-\varepsilon\left(\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,(0,\varepsilon)\right)\otimes\delta_{0},

which belongs to
{\varPi}(\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{L},\mu^{0}_{\varepsilon})
(recall that
\varepsilon^{2}<M
and
\varepsilon<\min_{[0,X]}\rho
so that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a positive measure).
The rest of the proof is exactly as in Step 3. We obtain that

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{4}/3,

which with (
2.49
), this leads to the reverse inequality of (
2.48
) and settles the case
\mathbf{M}(\rho)>\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.
∎

2.8.
The optimisation problem with a prescribed mass
\mathbf{M}(\rho)

In Subsection
2.9
, in order to establish some bounds on
u
and
\Lip(u)
for
u=(\log\rho)_{|[0,X]}
, we will work with smooth approximations of
\rho^{0}
on
[0,X^{0}]
. The goal of the current subsection is to justify this approximation procedure.

We study here a simplification of the optimisation problem (
2.19
) where the total mass
\mathbf{M}(\rho)
of the candidates is given.
For
M>0
, we define

\mathbb{A}_{M}:=\left\{(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}:\mathbf{M}(\rho)=M\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{A}_{M}:=\{(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}:\mathbf{M}(\rho)=M\}.

Given
(\rho^{0},X^{0})\in\mathcal{A}
, we consider the optimisation problem:

(2.50)

\min_{(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}}\,\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X).

In this setting, the terms
\theta|\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})|
and
p_{\tau}(\mathbf{M}(\rho)-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0}))
in the definition of the functional
\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}
(see (
2.18
)) are irrelevant. Removing those terms, we define

(2.51)

\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X):=\dfrac{\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)+\lambda\left(X-X^{0}\right)^{2}}{2\tau}+{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho,X),

so that, for
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
, there holds

\mathbf{J}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)=\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X)+\theta|M-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})|+p_{\tau}(M-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})).

As a consequence, the optimization problem (
2.50
) is equivalent to

(2.52)

\min_{(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}}\,\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho,X).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(\rho^{0},X^{0})\in\mathcal{A}
and
M>0
. The optimization problem (
2.52
) has a unique solution
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
. We denote it

\Upsilon^{\tau}((\rho^{0},X^{0}),M):=(\rho,X).

Besides,
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
and
\rho
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
[0,X]
.

Remark 2.9
.

By uniqueness, if
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
is a minimizer of the original problem (
2.19
) then

(\rho,X)=\Upsilon^{\tau}((\rho^{0},X^{0}),\mathbf{M}(\rho)).

However, we do not claim that (
2.19
) admits a unique minimizer. We only have that, if
(\rho_{0},X_{0})
,
(\rho_{1},X_{1})
are minimizers of (
2.19
), there holds
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{1})=\mathbf{M}(\rho_{0})\implies(\rho_{1},X_{1})=(\rho_{0},X_{0})
.

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

Step 1. Existence and regularity.
The method is similar to the one used in Subsection
2.5
for the existence of a solution to the original optimization problem (
2.19
). First, we introduce a reference candidate
(\hat{\rho},X^{0})\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
defined by
\hat{\rho}=(M/X^{0}){\bf{1}}_{[0,X^{0}]}
. Next,
as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we consider a minimizing sequence
(\rho_{j},X_{j})\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
for (
2.52
). By optimality, there holds,

\limsup_{j\uparrow\infty}\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{j},X_{j})\leq\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\hat{\rho},X^{0})=:Q(\rho^{0},X^{0},M).

Hence, for
j\geq 1
,

{\bm{\Psi}}(\rho_{j},X_{j})+\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X^{0}-X_{j})^{2}\leq Q(\rho^{0},X^{0},M)+o(1).

This leads to

(2.53)

\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X_{j}-X^{0})^{2}+\rho_{\sharp}\,X_{j}\leq Q(\rho^{0},X^{0},M)-\int_{0}^{X_{j}}f(\rho_{j})+o(1).

Now, since
r\mapsto f(r)=r(\log r+\beta-1)
is convex and
\mathbf{M}(\rho_{j})=M
we have by Jensen’s inequality,

\int_{0}^{X_{j}}f(\rho_{j})\geq X_{j}f\left(\dfrac{M}{X_{j}}\right)=\ M\dfrac{f\left(M/X_{j}\right)}{M/X_{j}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle X_{j}\downarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ +\infty,

where we used
f(r)/r\to+\infty
as
r\uparrow\infty
. Therefore, sending
X_{j}
to 0 in (
2.53
) yields the contradiction

\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}(X^{0})^{2}\leq-\infty.

We deduce that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
X_{j}\geq\varepsilon
for every
j\geq 1
. Now, we can follow the proof of Theorem
2.3
after (
2.22
) and conclude that the optimization problem (
2.52
) admits at least one solution
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
. Then, proceeding exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
, we obtain that
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
and that
\log\rho
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
on
[0,X]
.

Step 2. Preliminaries about convexity along generalized geodesics.
We still have to establish that the minimizer of the optimization problem (
2.52
) is unique. This is done in Step 3 using the notion of generalized geodesics (see
[
24
, Chapter 7.3.3]
). For the convenience of the reader, we give here the definitions and prove all the necessary results in our particular setting.

Let
\nu
,
\nu_{0}
,
\nu_{1}
be three positives measures on
\mathbb{R}
. We make the following assumptions:

(h1)

0<\nu(\mathbb{R})=\nu_{0}(\mathbb{R})=\nu_{1}(\mathbb{R})<\infty
.

(h2)

\nu
,
\nu_{0}
,
\nu_{1}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. We write,

\nu=\sigma\mathcal{L},\qquad\nu_{0}=\sigma_{0}\mathcal{L},\qquad\nu_{1}=\sigma_{1}\mathcal{L}.

(h3)

The supports of
\sigma
,
\sigma_{0}
and
\sigma_{1}
are bounded intervals of the following form:

\supp\sigma=[a,b]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\supp\sigma_{i}=[0,d_{i}]\quad\text{for }i=0,1.

(h4)

There exists
\kappa\in(0,1)
such that for
\rho\in\{\sigma,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\}
,

\kappa\leq\rho\leq 1/\kappa\quad\text{ almost everywhere on }\supp\rho.

For
i=0,1
, let
S_{i}:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be the unique optimal transport map from
\nu
to
\nu_{i}
. It is nondecreasing and characterized by

(2.54)

\int_{a}^{y}\sigma(z)\,dz=\int_{0}^{S_{i}(y)}\sigma_{i}(x)\,dx\qquad\text{for }y\in[a,b].

In particular,
S_{i}\,\!{}_{\#}\,\nu=\nu_{i}
. We also observe that differentiating (
2.54
), we get

(2.55)

\sigma(y)=S_{i}^{\prime}(y)\sigma_{i}(S_{i}(y))\quad\text{for almost every }y\in[a,b].

We deduce from (h4) that
S_{i}
is increasing and Lipschitz continuous with

(2.56)

S_{i}(0)=a\qquad\text{and}\qquad\kappa^{2}\leq S_{i}^{\prime}\leq\kappa^{-2}\qquad\text{almost everywhere on }[a,b].

Next, we define for
t\in[0,1]
,

(2.57)

S_{t}:=(1-t)S_{0}+tS_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu_{t}:=S_{t}\,\!{}_{\#}\,\nu.

By construction, for
t=0
and
t=1
,
\nu_{t}
coincides with
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
. Moreover, for
t\in[0,1]
,
\nu_{t}
is a positive measure. The path
t\in[0,1]\mapsto\nu_{t}
is called a generalized geodesic from
\nu_{0}
to
\nu_{1}
. We also see that
\nu_{t}(\mathbb{R})=\nu(\mathbb{R})
and from (
2.56
) we get that
S_{t}
is Lipschitz continuous with

\kappa^{2}\leq S_{t}^{\prime}\leq\kappa^{-2}\qquad\text{almost everywhere on }[a,b].

We deduce that
\nu_{t}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and writing
\nu_{t}=\sigma_{t}\mathcal{L}
, we have

(2.58)

\supp\sigma_{t}=[0,d_{t}]\quad\text{with }d_{t}:=(1-t)d_{0}+td_{1},

and

(2.59)

\kappa^{3}\leq\sigma_{t}\leq\kappa^{-3}\qquad\text{almost everywhere on }[0,d_{t}].

Let us now establish the convexity of
t\in[0,1]\mapsto\mathcal{F}(\nu_{t})
for three different types of functionals.

Step 2.1.

Let
\hat{x},\hat{q}_{0},\hat{q}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\hat{q}_{1}\geq 0
. Recalling (
2.58
), we see that

(2.60)

t\in[0,1]\mapsto\hat{q}_{1}(d_{t}-\hat{x})^{2}+\hat{q}_{0}d_{t}\qquad\text{ is convex}.

Step 2.2.

Let
Z:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable bounded function. We see from (
2.57
) that

(2.61)

E_{Z}:t\in[0,1]\mapsto\int_{a}^{b}|S_{t}(y)-Z(y)|^{2}\sigma(y)\,dy=\int|S_{t}-Z|^{2}\,d\nu\qquad\text{ is convex}.

We use this in Step 3, with
Z(y)=y
and
Z(y)=y_{+}
. In the former case,
E_{Z}(t)=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu,\nu_{t})
.

Step 2.3. Generalized strict convexity of the entropy.

Let us define for
t\in[0,1]

\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t}):=\int_{0}^{d_{t}}f(\sigma_{t}(x))\,dx,

where we recall that
f(r)=r(\log r+\beta-1)
. We claim that

(2.62)

t\mapsto\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t})\text{ is convex on }[0,1]\text{ and strictly convex if }\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{0}.

Here
\nu_{1}
is a translate of
\nu_{0}
means that
\sigma_{1}(x)=\sigma_{0}(x-\hat{x})
for some
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us establish the claim. For
r>0
we denote
g(r):=f(r)/r=\log r+\beta-1
. By (
2.59
), we have
\sigma_{t}\geq\kappa^{3}>0
on
[0,d_{t}]
and we can write

\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t})=\int_{0}^{d_{t}}g(\sigma_{t}(x))\sigma_{t}(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}g(\sigma_{t}(S_{t}(y)))\sigma(y)\,dy.

As in (
2.55
), we have
\sigma_{t}(S_{t}(y))=\sigma(y)/S_{t}^{\prime}(y)
for almost every
y\in[a,b]
, which leads to

\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t})=\int_{a}^{b}g\left(\dfrac{\sigma(y)}{(1-t)S_{0}^{\prime}(y)+tS_{1}^{\prime}(y)}\right)\sigma(y)\,dy.

Now for every
c>0
, the mapping
r\in(0,+\infty)\mapsto g(c/r)=-\log(r)+\log(c)+\beta-1
is strictly convex and we see that
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t})
is convex. If moreover,
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{0}
then
S_{0}\neq S_{1}
which implies that
S_{0}^{\prime}\neq S_{1}^{\prime}
on a subset of
[a,b]
with positive measure (recall that
S_{0}(0)=S_{1}(0)=a
). In such case, the strict convexity of
r\mapsto g(c/r)
implies that
t\mapsto\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{t})
is also strictly convex. This proves the claim (
2.62
).

Step 3. Uniqueness.
Let
(\rho_{0},X_{0})
,
(\rho_{1},X_{1})\in\mathcal{A}_{M}
be two minimizers of the optimization problem (
2.52
) and let us establish that
(\rho_{0},X_{0})=(\rho_{1},X_{1})
. In the sequel, note carefully the distinction between
\rho^{0}
and
\rho_{0}
. We treat successively the cases
M\leq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
and
M>\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.

Step 3.1,
M\leq\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.
Let
\ell_{-}\in[0,X^{0})
such that

\int_{0}^{\ell_{-}}\rho^{0}=\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-M.

Denoting
\nu=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,[\ell_{-},X^{0}]
and
\nu_{i}=\rho_{i}\mathcal{L}
for
i=0,1
, we have, with the notation of Step 2,

\sigma=\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,[\ell_{-},X^{0}],\qquad\sigma_{i}=\rho_{i}\quad\text{for }i=0,1,

and

[a,b]=[\ell_{-},X^{0}],\qquad[0,d_{i}]=[0,X_{i}]\quad\text{for }i=0,1.

Besides the measures
\nu
,
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{2}
satisfy the assumptions (h1)–(h4). Now, we define
\nu_{t}
,
d_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}
for
t\in[0,1]
as in Step 2. We see that
(\rho_{t},X_{t}):=(\sigma_{t},d_{t})\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
for
t\in[0,1]
. Next, recalling the definition (
2.51
) of
\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}
,
we have for
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{t},X_{t})=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left((\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})-M)\delta_{0},\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}\>{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}\hskip-0.2pt\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt,width=7.0pt,depth=0.0pt}\,[0,\ell_{-}]\right)\\
+\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\nu,\nu_{t}\right)+\left[\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}\left(d_{t}-b\right)^{2}-\rho_{\sharp}d_{t}\right]+\int_{0}^{d_{t}}f(\sigma_{t}(x))\,dx.

The first term is constant. For the second term, denoting
Z(y)=y
, we have

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\nu,\nu_{t}\right)=E_{Z}(t),

and by (
2.61
), this is a convex function of
t
. The third term is also convex by (
2.60
) and by (
2.62
), so is the last term. Moreover, this last term is strictly convex if
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{0}
. Since
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
are optimal this is impossible. Hence,
\nu_{1}
is a translate of
\nu_{0}
and we have in fact
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}
, that is
(\rho_{0},X_{0})=(\rho_{1},X_{1})
. This establishes the uniqueness of the minimizer of (
2.52
) in this case.

Step 3.2,
M>\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
.
Let us set
m:=M-\mathbf{M}(\rho^{0})
. We start with a preliminary remark. We have for
(\rho,X)\in\mathbb{A}_{M}
,

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho^{0},\rho)=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(m\delta_{0}+\rho^{0}\mathcal{L},\rho\mathcal{L}).

In order to apply the results of Step 2 we replace the measure
\mu^{0}=m\delta_{0}+\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}
by a measure which is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, namely,

\nu:={\bf 1}_{[-m,0]}+\rho^{0}\mathcal{L}.

Denoting
S:[-m,X^{0}]\to\mathbb{R}
the nondecreasing transport map from
\nu
to
\rho
. Writing
\nu=\sigma\mathcal{L}
, we observe that

(2.63)

\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho,\rho^{0})=\int_{-m}^{0}(S(y))^{2}\sigma(y)\,dy+\int_{0}^{X^{0}}(S(y)-y)^{2}\sigma(y)\,dy=\int_{-m}^{X^{0}}(S(y)-y_{+})^{2}\sigma(y)\,dy,

with
y_{+}=\max(y,0)
. Now, we set
\nu_{i}=\rho_{i}\mathcal{L}
for
i=0,1
and we use the notation of Step 2: we also introduce the densities
\sigma_{0}
,
\sigma_{1}
, the supports
[a,b]=[-m,X^{0}]
, and
[0,d_{i}]=[0,X_{i}]
for
i=0,1
and the maps
S_{0}
,
S_{1}
. Next, we define
S_{t}
,
\sigma_{t}
and
[0,d_{t}]=[0,(1-t)X_{0}+tX_{1}]
. We see that
(\rho_{t},X_{t}):=(\sigma_{t},d_{t})\in\mathbb{A}_{m}
for
t\in[0,1]
. Now, using (
2.63
), we compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{t},X_{t})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{t},\rho^{0}\right)+\left[\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}\left(d_{t}-b\right)^{2}-\rho_{\sharp}d_{t}\right]+\int_{0}^{d_{t}}f(\sigma_{t}(x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2\tau}E_{Z}(t)+\left[\dfrac{\lambda}{2\tau}\left(d_{t}-b\right)^{2}-\rho_{\sharp}d_{t}\right]+\int_{0}^{d_{t}}f(\sigma_{t}(x))\,dx,

where
Z(y):=y_{+}
for
y\in\mathbb{R}
. Exactly as in the previous case,
\mathbf{I}^{\tau}_{(\rho^{0},X^{0})}(\rho_{t},X_{t})
is a convex function of
t
and it is strictly convex if
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{0}
. As this would contradict the optimality of
(\rho_{0},X_{0})
,
(\rho_{1},X_{1})
, we deduce again that
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}
, that is
(\rho_{0},X_{0})=(\rho_{1},X_{1})
. As a conclusion, the minimizer is unique in all the cases. This ends the proof of Proposition
2.8
.
∎

The next result gives the boundary condition satisfied by
\Upsilon^{\tau}((\rho^{0},X^{0}),M)
at
x=X
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
(\rho^{0},X^{0})\in\mathcal{A}
, let
M>0
and
(\rho,X):=\Upsilon^{\tau}((\rho^{0},X^{0}),M)
. There holds,

(2.64)

\lambda\dfrac{X-X^{0}}{\tau}=\rho(X)-\rho_{\sharp}.

Remark 2.11
.

By Remark
2.9
any minimizer
(\rho,X)\in\mathcal{A}
for the problem (
2.19
) satisfies (
2.64
).

Proof.

We consider here variations of
X
(and of
\rho
in the neighborhood of
X
). Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
with
\varepsilon<X^{2}
. We consider as a competitor for the optimisation problem (
2.52
) the pair,
(\rho_{\pm\varepsilon},X_{\pm\varepsilon})
defined by
X_{\pm\varepsilon}:=X\pm\varepsilon
and

\rho_{\pm\varepsilon}(x):=\begin{cases}~\quad\rho(x)&\text{for }0\leq x\leq X-\sqrt{\varepsilon},\\
c_{\pm\varepsilon}\rho(A_{\pm\varepsilon}(x))&\text{for }X-\sqrt{\varepsilon}<x\leq X\pm\varepsilon,\\
~\quad\ 0&\text{for }x>X\pm\varepsilon.\end{cases}

Here
A_{\pm\varepsilon}
is the increasing affine transformation sending
(X-\sqrt{\varepsilon},X\pm\varepsilon]
onto
(X-\sqrt{\varepsilon},X]
and
c_{\pm\varepsilon}
is the factor ensuring
\int\rho_{\pm\varepsilon}=\int\rho
. We compute,

c_{\pm\varepsilon}=\left(1\pm\sqrt{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{\pm\varepsilon}(x))=X-\sqrt{\varepsilon}+c_{\pm\varepsilon}\left(x-(X-\sqrt{\varepsilon})\right).

By optimality of
\rho
, we have

\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)+\lambda\dfrac{\left(X-X^{0}\right)^{2}}{2\tau}+\int_{X-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{X}f(\rho)\,+\rho_{\sharp}X\\
\leq\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\pm\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)+\lambda\dfrac{\left(X_{\pm\varepsilon}-X^{0}\right)^{2}}{2\tau}+\int_{X-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{X\pm\varepsilon}f(\rho_{\pm\varepsilon})\,+\rho_{\sharp}(X\pm\varepsilon).

Substituting the values of
(\rho_{\pm\varepsilon},X_{\pm\varepsilon})
and simplifying we get

(2.65)

-\lambda\left(\pm\varepsilon\dfrac{X-X^{0}}{\tau}\right)\mp\varepsilon\rho_{\sharp}+\int_{X-\sqrt{\varepsilon}}^{X}\left(f(\rho(x))-\dfrac{1}{c_{\pm\varepsilon}}f(c_{\pm\varepsilon}\rho(x))\right)\,dx\\
\leq\dfrac{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}}{2\tau}+\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\left(\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho_{\pm\varepsilon},\rho^{0}\right)-\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\rho,\rho^{0}\right)\right).

Recalling that
\rho
is continuous and positive on

A_{\tau}^{11}

A_{\tau}^{11}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{\left(w_{\tau}\right)^{2}-\left(\sigma_{-\tau}w_{\tau}\right)^{2}}{\tau}\,dx\,dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{\left(w_{\tau}-\sigma_{-\tau}w_{\tau}\right)^{2}}{\tau}\,dx\,dt=:A_{\tau}^{11a}+A_{\tau}^{11b}.

Considering the first term, we get after a discrete integration by parts in time,

A_{\tau}^{11a}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T-\tau}\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{\sigma_{\tau}\phi-\phi}{\tau}\,\left(w_{\tau}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dt-\dfrac{1}{2\tau}\int_{-\tau}^{0}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\left(w_{\tau}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dt,

where we assume that
\tau
is small enough so that
\phi\equiv 0
on
(T-\tau,T)
. By (
3.37
), in the last integral, we have
w_{\tau}(t)=w^{0}=\chi\rho^{0}
for
-\tau<t\leq 0
. The
L^{2}
-convergence
\rho_{\tau}\to\rho
then leads to

(3.53)

\lim_{\tau\downarrow 0}A_{\tau}^{11a}=-\int_{0}^{T}\dot{\phi}\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{(w)^{2}}{2}\,dx\,dt-\phi(0)\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{(w)^{2}}{2}(x,0)\,dt.

Now, rearranging the remaining terms, we see that

\displaystyle A_{\tau}^{11b}+A_{\tau}^{12}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{(w_{\tau,\delta}-w_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau}\,dx\,dt+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{(w_{\tau,\delta}-\sigma_{-\tau}w_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\geq-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\dfrac{(w_{\tau,\delta}-w_{\tau})^{2}}{\tau}\,dx\,dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{moll.bound1}}}{{\geq}}\ -\dfrac{C_{4}\,\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}}{2}\,\tau^{2\omega^{\prime}-2\omega-1}\to 0,\quad\mbox{as }\tau\downarrow 0.

With (
3.53
), this proves (
3.48
).

Step 3. Lower bound for
A_{\tau}^{3}
.
We write

A_{\tau}^{3}=\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\left(w_{\tau,\delta}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{0}^{X_{*}}w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\left(w^{\prime}_{\tau}-w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\right)\,dx\,dt=:A_{\tau}^{31}+A_{\tau}^{32}.

By Lemma
3.11
there holds on the one hand
\|w_{\tau,\delta}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{1}
. On the other hand, we deduce from the convergence
\tilde{w}_{\tau}\to w
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,T))
and (
3.44
) (with
\omega^{\prime}>\omega
) that
w_{\tau,\delta}\to w
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,T))
. This implies that
w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\to w^{\prime}=\left(\chi\rho\right)^{\prime}
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times(0,T))
and we get by the lower semicontinuity of the functional
v\mapsto\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X_{*}}v^{2}(x,t)\phi(t)\,dx\,dt
with respect to weak convergence,

(3.54)

\liminf_{\tau\downarrow 0}A_{\tau}^{31}\geq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{X_{*}}\phi\left(w^{\prime}\right)^{2}\,dx\,dt.

Next, we rewrite the term
A_{\tau}^{32}
as

A_{\tau}^{32}=\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}-w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\right)\,dx\,dt-\int_{0}^{T}\phi\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,X_{*}]}w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}-w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\right)\,dx\,dt=:A_{\tau}^{32a}+A_{\tau}^{32b}.

Let us treat the term
A_{\tau}^{32a}
. We recall that we have
\zeta=\xi\ast\xi
with
\xi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
. Setting
\xi(y):=\xi(y/\delta)/\delta
we have
\zeta_{\delta}=\xi_{\delta}\ast\xi_{\delta}
and since
\xi
is even, there holds for
t\in(0,T)
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}(t)\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}\ast\zeta_{\delta}(t)\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\ast\xi_{\delta})\ast\xi_{\delta}\right]\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\ast\xi_{\delta}\right)^{2}\,dx.

Thereby,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}-w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}\right)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\ast\xi_{\delta}\right)^{2}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\left[\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\ast\xi_{\delta}\right]\ast\xi_{\delta}\right)^{2}\,dx\geq 0.

We get the inequality from
\|z\ast\xi_{\delta}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\xi_{\delta}\|_{L^{1}}\|z\|_{L^{2}}=\|z\|_{L^{2}}
with
z=\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\ast\xi_{\delta}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Now, since
\phi\geq 0
we deduce that
A_{\tau}^{32a}\geq 0
.

For
A_{\tau}^{32b}
we observe that
w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}(x,t)=0
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus(-2\delta,X_{*}+\delta)
. Therefore, applying the
L^{\infty}
bounds (
3.42
) of Lemma
3.11
on
\tilde{w}^{\prime}_{\tau}
and
w^{\prime}_{\tau,\delta}
we get

\left|A_{\tau}^{32b}\right|\leq 6\,C_{2}^{2}\,\|\phi\|_{L^{1}}\delta\tau^{-2\omega}=6\,C_{2}^{2}\,\|\phi\|_{L^{1}}\tau^{\omega^{\prime}-2\omega}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau\downarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ 0.

With (
3.54
) and
A_{\tau}^{32a}\geq 0
this proves (
3.50
).

Step 4. Control of
A_{\tau}^{6}
.

The estimate (
3.31
) of Proposition
3.8
leads to

\displaystyle\left|A_{\tau}^{6}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}}\,\left(\left\|\left(\chi w_{\tau,\delta}\right)^{\prime\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|\left(\chi w_{\tau,\delta}\right)^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\tau^{1-2\omega}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}\left(\|\tilde{w}_{\tau}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\tilde{w}_{\tau}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|w_{\tau,\delta}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\tau^{1-2\omega}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{moll.bound2}}}{{\leq}}\ C^{\prime\prime}\tau^{1-3\omega-\omega^{\prime}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau\downarrow 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ 0,

where the constants
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\geq 0
only depend on
\phi
,
\chi
,
(\rho^{0},X^{0})
and on the parameters of the problem. This completes the proof of Proposition
3.12
.
∎

3.6.
Proof of the main result, Theorem
1.2

We already have established in Corollary
3.9
that, assuming
0<\omega<1/2
, the pair
(\rho,X)
obtained in Proposition
3.4
satisfies conditions (a) to (f) of Definition
1.1
.

Choosing
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in(0,1)
satisfying (
3.47
) (for instance
\omega=1/16
,
\omega^{\prime}=3/4
as in Remark
3.13
), and putting the limits of Proposition
3.12
in (
3.46
) we get that
(\rho,X)
also satisfies the variational inequality (
1.18
) which is condition (g) of Definition
1.1
. We conclude that
(\rho,X)
is a weak solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) in the sense of Definition
1.1
.

We now send
\mathcal{T}
to infinity. Let
\mathcal{T}_{k}:=2^{k}
for
k\geq 0
. With obvious notation we get a sequence of solutions
(\rho_{k},X_{k})
defined on
[0,\overline{T}_{k}]
with
\overline{T}_{k}
nondecreasing and such that
(\rho_{k+1},X_{k+1})=(\rho_{k},X_{k})
on
[0,\overline{T}_{k}]
. We set
\overline{T}:=\lim_{k\uparrow\infty}\overline{T}_{k}
and define
(\rho,X)
by

(\rho(t),X(t)):=(\rho_{k}(t),X_{k}(t))\quad\text{ for }0\leq t<\overline{T}_{k}.

We have either
\overline{T}_{k}\to+\infty
or the sequence
\overline{T}_{k}
is stationary after some index
k_{0}\geq 1
. In this case
\overline{T}=\overline{T}_{k_{0}}
and by Proposition
3.4
(b) we have
X(\overline{T})=0
so that
(\rho,X)
satisfies the conclusion (a) of Theorem
1.2
.

We also have
\hat{\rho}_{-}\leq\rho\leq\hat{\rho}_{+}
on
\supp\rho=\overline{D_{\overline{T}}}\,
so that the conclusion (b) of the theorem holds true. Finally, the conclusion (c) follows from Proposition
3.2
(a) and from the lower semicontinuity of
{\bm{\Psi}}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
