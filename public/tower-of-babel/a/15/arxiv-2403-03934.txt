Abstract
. An open stochastic system à la Jan Willems is a system affected by two qualitatively different kinds of uncertainty – one is probabilistic fluctuation, and the other one is nondeterminism caused by a fundamental lack of information. We present a formalization of open stochastic systems in the language of category theory. Central to this is the notion of copartiality which models how the lack of information propagates through a system (corresponding to the coarseness of σ \sigma -algebras in Willems’ work). As a concrete example, we study extended Gaussian distributions, which combine Gaussian probability with nondeterminism and correspond precisely to Willems’ notion of Gaussian linear systems . We describe them both as measure-theoretic and abstract categorical entities, which enables us to rigorously describe a variety of phenomena like noisy physical laws and uninformative priors in Bayesian statistics. The category of extended Gaussian maps can be seen as a mutual generalization of Gaussian probability and linear relations, which connects the literature on categorical probability with ideas from control theory like signal-flow diagrams.

1.
Introduction

When modelling the behavior of systems in computer science and engineering, we encounter different kinds of uncertainty

(1)

Probabilistic uncertainty
, where we don’t know the exact value of some quantity of interest, but we know its statistical distribution. Our knowledge is specified by a
probability distribution
over the set
X
of possible values of the quantity. A typical example of such a distribution is the
normal
or
Gaussian
distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
over the real line, with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

(2)

Nondeterministic uncertainty
, where the true quantity can be
any
feasible value, but we have no statistical information beyond that. Such uncertainty can be modelled using the
subset

R\subseteq X
of the feasible values. Such subsets often arise from equational constraints such as natural laws.

(3)

A lack of information
because the quantity of interest can only be measured incompletely. For example, given two variables
x,y
, we can only measure their difference
x-y
. Mathematically, such lack of information can be formalized using
equivalence relations

\Phi\subseteq X\times X
, where instead of a quantity
x\in X
, we only have access to its equivalence class
[x]_{\Phi}\in X/\Phi
. A related notion, which we discuss prominently in this article, is a measurable space
(X,\mathcal{E})
with a very coarse
\sigma
-algebra that gives only ‘fuzzy’ access to the elements of
X
.

Category theory has emerged as a unifying language for studying systems and their composition. Markov categories
[
Fri20
]
in particular correspond to theories of uncertainty and information flow, and have been used in a variety of applications
[
FGP21
,
FR20
,
FGPR20
]
. In this article, we will give a mathematical analysis of these three kinds of uncertainty in terms of Markov categories. While the semantics of probabilistic and nondeterministic uncertainty are well-known, our model for lack of information in terms of equivalence relations is novel. We propose a notion of
copartial map
that is formally dual to partial maps. In general, copartiality and nondeterminism have different rules of composition (Section
4.1
), but we will see that these notions coincide in the case of linear relations (Proposition
5
).

Our main objective
then becomes to model systems under multiple kinds of uncertainty. Examples can be physical systems that obey both relational constraints and feature probabilistic noise, or improper priors in statistical inference.

There is a large body of literature on trying to combine the mathematical theories of probability and nondeterminism into a single well-behaved theory (e.g.
[
BSV20
,
DPS18
,
Che17
,
GP20
,
VW06
]
). Such approaches are often structured around presenting the relevant theories as monads, and then combining the monads in a suitable way, such as using a distributive law. However it has been shown that no such distributive law can exist in the case of distribution monad (probability) and powerset monad (nondeterminism)
[
VW06
,
ZM19
]
. A weak distributive law combining these monads does exist
[
GP20
]
but results in a composite monad of
convex subsets of distributions
that no longer has some desirable properties (commutativity). This area is sometimes known as
imprecise probability
and remains an active area of research (e.g.
[
LCS25
]
).

In this work, we take a departure from the monadic approach and focus on combining probability and lack-of-information uncertainty. A framework encompassing both of these forms of uncertainty has been formulated in Willems’
open stochastic systems

[
Wil13
]
. We will recall what these are, and then work towards a categorical treatment of the matter.

Willems argues that the variables

Equational constraints from natural laws are not the only application of open systems. A different use-case arises with uninformative priors in statistical inference:

{exa}

If

It is unclear under this setup how we should model situations where we completely lack information, either because we have no prior over

Willems’ compositional approach and the categorical language fit well together. For example, aspects of Willems’ work on relational constraints has been categorified in
[
FSR16
]
. We wish to extend this approach to systems with relational and stochastic constraints, like the noisy resistor. However, we run into two immediate difficulties:

(1)

The first concerns the use of the word “open”. To Willems, this means a lack of information, modelled by a coarse
\sigma
-algebra. In category theory, “open” generally refers to morphisms
X\to Y
with inputs, as opposed to mere states
\mathrm{I}\to Y
. We will investigate how these notions are related, and can be unified in a single framework.

(2)

Secondly, in measure-theoretic probability, the objects are usually measurable spaces
(X,\mathcal{E})
. That is, the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{E}
is treated as fixed, because it is part of the objects. In open stochastic systems on the other hand, the
\sigma
-algebra varies, as different computations will result in different
\sigma
-algebras. We can thus no longer treat
\sigma
-algebras as part of the objects, but must make them part of the morphisms. This leads us to formalize the phenomenon of
copartiality
.

To elaborate the second point, let us revisit the noisy resistor. We would like model its behavior as a joint state
P_{VI}:\mathrm{I}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Composing with the projections
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

We address these challenges as follows: We first introduce a notion of
extended Gaussian distribution
, which precisely corresponds to Willems’ Gaussian linear systems. We then define a Markov category of
extended Gaussian maps
which formalizes how these distributions compose.

Roughly speaking, an extended Gaussian distribution is a pair
(\psi,\mathbb{D})
of a subspace
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a Gaussian distribution on the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
. We may write this in additive notation
\psi+\mathbb{D}
, the same way we write elements of quotients as cosets, e.g.
(1+2\mathbb{Z})\in\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. The subspace
\mathbb{D}
is called the fibre of the distribution, and corresponds to the nondeterministic contribution. If
\mathbb{D}=0
, we are talking about ordinary Gaussian distributions.

For example, the noisy resistor can be described by the extended Gaussian distribution

P_{VI}=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right)+\left\{\begin{pmatrix}V\\
I\end{pmatrix}\,|\,V=RI\right\}

We can straightforwardly compute
P_{V}=P_{I}=\mathbb{R}
; this means that they can take arbitrary values on the real line but no statistical distribution is known. On the other hand, computing the distribution of
V-RI
returns, as expected, the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.

1.1.
Contribution

We answer the following questions: How can Willems’ framework of open stochastic systems be captured with category theory, and how does his notion of interconnection be reconciled with categorical composition?

We demonstrate this by defining a category
\mathsf{GaussEx}
of extended Gaussian maps, which faithfully combines Gaussian probability with nondeterminism. The states of
\mathsf{GaussEx}
correspond precisely to
Gaussian linear systems
of Willems. We describe these entities both in measure-theoretic terms, as well as through abstract entities obeying certain composition laws (without reference to measure theory).

We introduce
copartiality
as a way to formalize lack of information via maps into quotients. For linear maps, copartiality and relational nondeterminism coincide, which allows us to see
\mathsf{GaussEx}
as a mutual generalization of Gaussian probability and linear relations. Then, we return to Willems’ measure-theoretic approach and prove that categorical composition and his notion of interconnection coincide (Theorem
8
).

2.
Open Stochastic Systems

In this section, we introduce Gaussian linear systems in the sense of Willems, and our abstract description of them, which we term an
extended Gausssian distribution
. We begin by briefly recalling the measure-theoretic foundations of probability. For a standard reference, see
[
Kal97
]
.

Notation:
We write
f[A]
for the image of a subset
A\subseteq X
under a function
f:X\to Y
. We use the Iverson brackets for indicator functions, i.e. the expression
[\phi]
evaluates to
1
if
\phi
is true, and
0
otherwise.

A
\sigma
-algebra
on a set
X
is a collection
\mathcal{E}
of subsets of
X
which contains
\emptyset
and is closed under countable unions and complementation. For topological spaces like
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will consider the Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing all open subsets.

A
measurable space
is a pair
(X,\mathcal{E})
of a set and a
\sigma
-algebra on it. A function
f:(X,\mathcal{E})\to(Y,\mathcal{F})
between measurable spaces is called
measurable
if
f^{-1}(F)\in\mathcal{E}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
. We will refer to spaces like
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
equipped with their Borel
\sigma
-algebra as
Borel spaces
. The product of two measurable spaces
(X,\mathcal{E})
and
(Y,\mathcal{F})
is the set
X\times Y
equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{F}
, that is the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the measurable rectangles
E\times F
for
E\in\mathcal{E},F\in\mathcal{F}
. The product of two Borel spaces is again Borel. Measurable spaces and measurable functions form a category
\mathsf{Meas}
.

A
probability measure
or
distribution
on
(X,\mathcal{E})
is a function
P:\mathcal{E}\to[0,1]
which satisfies
P(X)=1
and

P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}E_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}P(E_{i})

for all disjoint collections
(E_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
of sets in
\mathcal{E}
. An important example is the Dirac distribution
\delta_{x}
at point
x\in X
, which is defined by
\delta_{x}(E)=[x\in E]
.

For a probability measure
P
and a measurable function
f:(X,\mathcal{E})\to(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))
, the integral is denoted by
\int f(x)P(\mathrm{d}x)
. If
f:(X,\mathcal{E})\to(Y,\mathcal{F})
is a measurable function and
P
is a probability measure on
(X,\mathcal{E})
, we can define a
pushforward measure

f_{*}P
on
(Y,\mathcal{F})
by
f_{*}P(F)=P(f^{-1}(F))
. For two distributions
P_{1},P_{2}
on
(X_{1},\mathcal{E}_{1}),(X_{2},\mathcal{E}_{2})
respectively, their product distribution
P_{1}\otimes P_{2}
on
(P_{1}\times P_{2},\mathcal{E}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{2})
is defined on measurable rectangles by
(P_{1}\otimes P_{2})(E_{1}\times E_{2})=P_{1}(E_{1})P_{2}(E_{2})
and uniquely extended to a probability measure to the whole product
\sigma
-algebra.

A
probability space
is a tuple
(X,\mathcal{E},P)
of a measurable space equipped with a probability measure. In this setting, the measurable sets

are also called

. In Willems’ terminology, a probability space is called a
stochastic system
, and we will use both names interchangeably.

2.1.
Gaussian distributions

The class of probability distributions we will be concerned with in this article are the
Gaussian distributions
, also known as multivariate normal distributions. A univariate Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}
has the form

P(A)=\int_{A}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}x

for numbers
\mu,\sigma^{2}
, which are the mean and variance of
P
. We denote this probability measure as
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
. Note that the Dirac distribution is considered a special case of a Gaussian distribution with variance
0
, i.e.
\delta_{\mu}=\mathcal{N}(\mu,0)
.

Similarly, a multivariate Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is fully characterized by its mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and its covariance matrix, which is a positive-semidefinite matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We write this distribution as
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)
, and define
\mathsf{Gauss}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to be the set of all Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

This means that we can often manipulate Gaussian distributions without resorting to measure-theoretic computation. Instead, we can treat them as abstract, formal entities that obey certain transformation rules. We will leverage this in our categorical presentation, and develop a similar, abstract presentation of extended Gaussian distributions in Section

2.2.
Open Linear Systems

We now come to the central notion of Willems. Let
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a vector subspace. A
Borel cylinder
parallel to
\mathbb{D}
is a Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is closed under translations along
\mathbb{D}
, i.e.
A+\mathbb{D}\subseteq A
. The Borel cylinders define a sub-
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\,|\,A+\mathbb{D}\subseteq A\}

of the Borel sets.

{defi}

We write
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
for the measurable space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
. We call a stochastic system
linear
if its underlying measurable space is
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
, and call
\mathbb{D}
the fibre of the system. We call a linear system
closed
if its fibre vanishes; in this case, its
\sigma
-algebra is all of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Otherwise, the
\sigma
-algebra is strictly smaller, and we call the system
open
.

The space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
has similarities to the quotient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
. We spell out their precise relationship in Section
6.2
. In an open linear system, only events parallel to
\mathbb{D}
are assigned a probability. Openness thus corresponds to a lack of information about the system.

{defi}

An
extended Gaussian distribution

\chi
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the restriction of a Gaussian probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the Borel cylinders
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We call
\mathbb{D}
the fibre of the distribution.
A
Gaussian system
is a space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with an extended Gaussian distribution. We write
\mathsf{GaussEx}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for the set of extended Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

It is important that the
\sigma
-algebra (or equivalently) the fibre is part of the specification of an extended Gaussian distributions. Different distributions
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
will in general be defined on different
\sigma
-algebras; the underlying measurable space of a Gaussian system with fibre
\mathbb{D}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
.

{exa}

Every Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is also a an extended Gaussian distribution, with fibre
\mathbb{D}=0
.

{exa}

Every subspace
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be seen as an extended Gaussian distribution with fibre
\mathbb{D}
, namely the restriction of
\delta_{0}
to
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Explicitly, we have

\mathbb{D}:\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,1],\quad\mathbb{D}(A)=[0\in A]

We think of
\mathbb{D}
as an idealized uniform distribution. It indicates an arbitrary point in the subspace
\mathbb{D}
, about which we have no further information.

We will see at the end of this section that the general extended Gaussian distribution is a sum (convolution) of an ordinary Gaussian distribution and a subspace.

Kernel representation

Our chief example of extended Gaussian distribution arises from pulling back Gaussian distributions under a surjective map.

{defi}

Let
p:(X,\mathcal{E})\to(Y,\mathcal{F})
be a surjective measurable function, and
\psi:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
a distribution on
Y
. Then we can form the pullback
\sigma

p^{-1}[\mathcal{F}]=\{p^{-1}(F):F\in\mathcal{F}\}

and define a measure
p^{-1}(\psi):p^{-1}[\mathcal{F}]\to[0,1]
as

p^{-1}(\psi)(p^{-1}(F))=\psi(F)

for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
Measurability ensures that
p^{-1}[\mathcal{F}]\subseteq\mathcal{E}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra.

We will revisit the details of the pullback construction in Section
6.2
.

Proposition 1
.

For every surjective linear map
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
and Gaussian distribution
\psi\in\mathsf{Gauss}(\mathbb{R}^{k})
, we have that the pullback distribution
p^{-1}(\psi)
is an extended Gaussian distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with fibre
\mathbb{D}=\ker(p)
. Every extended Gaussian distribution
\chi

Arithmetic of Quotients

We wish to give transformation rules of extended Gaussian distributions the same way as in Proposition
2.1

on

This is analogous to asking what the sum of two equivalence classes

4.

Gaussian maps could faithfully be interpreted as channels, by interpreting objects as the Borel spaces

4.1.

A partial function between sets

Let

which commutes with the legs. We will always consider spans up to equivalence. The following subclasses of spans are of importance

(1)

a
relation
is a span where
f,g
are jointly monic, i.e. the pairing
\langle f,g\rangle:A\to X\times Y
is a monomorphism

(2)

a
partial map
is a span where the left leg
f
is monic. The apex
A
represents the domain of the partial map.

Spans compose by pullback. A pullback of relations is not necessarily a relation; instead an image factorization has to be used. For partial maps, the two types of composition agree – a pullback of partial maps is another partial map. The category
\mathsf{Par}(\mathbf{C})
of

Just as partial maps are maps out of subobjects,

(1)

[

]

(2)

a

is a cospan whose right leg

5.
Interconnection

After discussing extended Gaussian maps as abstract categorical entities, we will now tie things back to Willems’s measure-theoretic approach to composition.

6.1.
Categorical Models of Probability Theory

Markov categories (and related formalisms such as copy-delete and hypergraph categories) are one widely used axiomatization of what it means to be a categorical model of probability. They focus on the monoidal structure of stochastic computation (parallel vs sequential composition, correlation vs independence). We have used the language of Markov categories here because our notion of extended Gaussian morphisms exhibits this kind of structure and satisfies the relevant axioms. This type of categorical model also fits well with call-by-value semantics of probabilistic programming languages
[
Sta17
,
Ste21b
]
.

There are other approaches to categorical probability with different goals in mind. We give a brief and non-exhaustive overview, and comment on the extent that they might relate to open stochastic systems.

Probability theory exhibits rich dualities between measure-theoretic and functional-analytic structures such as C*-algebras or von Neumann algebras (known as Gelfand duality). This approach is the basis for generalizations of probability and topology such as noncommutative geometry, free and quantum probability, and has seen extensive categorical treatment (e.g.
[
FJ15
,
Pav22
]
). Extended Gaussians fit well into this picture, and we will mention some of their duality theory in the remainder of this section.

Effectus theory
[
CJWW15
]

6.2.
\sigma
-algebras and Weak Quotients

Intuitively, a Gaussian system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with fibre
\mathbb{D}
is given by a Gaussian distribution on a quotient vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
. The quotient space is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
where
k=n-\dim(\mathbb{D})
, so we could explain a Borel structure on the quotient and talk about Gaussian distributions directly. However, Willems’ definition uses the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
, which is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the coarse
\sigma
-algebra of Borel cylinders
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. What is the relationship between these two kinds of quotient?

We have a measurable projection
p:\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,D\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
. This map fails to have an inverse in the category of measurable spaces
\mathsf{Meas}
, because on underlying sets,
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
is not injective. The induced channel
\langle p\rangle:\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}\leadsto\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
on the other hand is invertible! We write its inverse as
p^{\sim 1}:\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}\leadsto\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
, and define it as follows:

p^{\sim 1}:(\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D})\times\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to[0,1],\quad p^{\sim 1}(A|[x])=[x\in A]

(5)

Note that the value of
p^{\sim 1}(A|[x])
is well-defined precisely because we only evaluate it on Borel cylinders parallel to
\mathbb{D}
.

That is, in the category
\mathsf{Stoch}
, it does not matter whether we work with
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
. On the level of measurable functions however, there is a difference. This is reminiscent of point-free topology, where there are maps
\mathcal{O}_{Y}\to\mathcal{O}_{X}
between frames that do not come from any continuous map between topological spaces
X\to Y
. We call the construction
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
a
weak quotient
in analogy to this, following the discussion of Moss and Perrone in
[
MP23
, Appendix A]
. Our discussion fits their framework, with
\mathcal{B}_{\mathbb{D}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
being an instance of an
invariant
\sigma
-algebra
for the translations in
\mathbb{D}
.

The difference between
\mathsf{Meas}
and
\mathsf{Stoch}
also concerns a problem which has remained largely implicit in Willems:
What is the correct notion of equivalence of systems?

If we work with channels, we work point-free and need not bother with weak quotients. On the other hand, the distinction between open and closed system is not stable under isomorphism in
\mathsf{Stoch}
. The space
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\!\!/\,\mathbb{D}
is open, but isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}/\mathbb{D}
, which is Borel (closed). We will now recall some of the categorical structure for isomorphisms in
\mathsf{Stoch}
:

A channel
\kappa:(X,\mathcal{E})\leadsto(Y,\mathcal{F})
is called

if it is 0/1-valued, i.e.
\kappa(F|x)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in X,F\in\mathcal{F}
. A channel

is deterministic if and only if it is deterministic in the sense of Definition
3

[
Fri20
]

\mathsf{Stoch}_{\mathsf{det}}
of
\mathsf{Stoch}

\mathsf{Stoch}_{\mathsf{det}}
is

6.3.
Variance-Precision Duality and Quadratic Functions

So far, we have described extended Gaussian distributions as categorical and measure-theoretic objects. We will briefly sketch how they naturally arise naturally from an analytic point of view. For simplicity, we will only consider
centered Gaussians
of mean zero.

A Gaussian distribution with nonsingular, i.e. positive definite, covariance matrix
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
has a density function over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the form

f(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}\Omega x\right)

(6)

The matrix
\Omega
appearing in the density, known as the
precision
or
information matrix
, is the inverse of the covariance matrix
\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}
. Nonsingular Gaussian distributions thus have two equivalent representations, a
precision form
and a
covariance form
, which are related to each other by inversion. The two forms each have convenient properties for different applications

(1)

covariance is additive for convolution, i.e. we have

\Sigma_{\psi_{1}+\psi_{2}}=\Sigma_{\psi_{1}}+\Sigma_{\psi_{2}}

because variances of independent variables add.

(2)

precision is additive for conditioning. That is we have

\Omega_{\psi_{1}\bullet\psi_{2}}=\Omega_{\psi_{1}}+\Omega_{\psi_{2}}

where
\psi_{1}\bullet\psi_{2}
denotes the interconnection of (
4
). That is because when conditioning, densities multiply, so precisions add.

Figure 4.
Graphs of partial quadratic functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(kernel
\mathbb{D}
shown in red)

The duality between covariance and precision form is lost once we consider Gaussians whose covariance matrix is singular. Such distribution no longer admit a density over all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(1)

What is the precision form of a singular Gaussian distribution?

(2)

What would be the meaning of a singular precision matrix?

The answer to the second question is precisely extended Gaussian distributions, whose fibre is
\mathbb{D}=\ker(\Omega)
. Under this viewpoint, they have been studied for example in
[
Jam73
]
. To understand this duality, it is useful to represent Gaussian distributions as quadratic functions:

Let

\mathbb{D}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma

\mathbb{D}^{\bot}

\mathbb{D}

if
\phi

otherwise, that is we use
+\infty
to indicate partiality. We note that

is reminiscent of a negative log-density (
