Abstract
Given a graph G G and a vertex set X X , the annotated treewidth 𝗍𝗐 ⁡ ( G , X ) {\sf tw}(G,X) of X X in G G is the maximum treewidth of an X X -rooted minor of G G , i.e., a minor H H where the model of each vertex of H H contains some vertex of X X . That way, 𝗍𝗐 ⁡ ( G , X ) {\sf tw}(G,X) can be seen as a measure of the contribution of X X to the tree-decomposability of G G . We introduce the logic CMSO/tw as the fragment of monadic second-order logic on graphs obtained by restricting set quantification to sets of bounded annotated treewidth. We prove the following Algorithmic Meta-Theorem (AMT): for every non-trivial minor-closed graph class, model checking for CMSO/tw formulas can be done in quadratic time . Our proof works for the more general CMSO/tw + dp logic, that is CMSO/tw enhanced by disjoint-path predicates. Our AMT can be seen as an extension of Courcelle’s theorem to minor-closed graph classes where the bounded-treewidth condition in the input graph is replaced by the bounded-treewidth quantification in the formulas. Our results yield, as special cases, all known AMTs whose combinatorial restriction is non-trivial minor-closedness.



Keywords:
Algorithmic Meta-Theorems, Monadic second-order Logic, Model checking, Annotated treewidth, Hadwiger number.

1.2
Our techniques

Theorem

2
capitalizes on the main algorithmic potential of the Graph Minors series of Robertson and Seymour.
Namely, it exploits the “locality” of sets of bounded annotated treewidth and follows the scheme of the irrelevant-vertex technique of
[
68
]

This approach is part of a greater framework that we introduce, concerning
reductions between meta-theorems
, where
the task of finding irrelevant vertices for a problem is “localized” in a way that makes it (meta-)algorithmically tractable.
In what follows, we provide a brief exposition of the principal ideas of our proofs.

Simplifying instances.

Our approach to model checking is based on the irrelevant-vertex technique, which was introduced by Robertson and Seymour for the design of an algorithm solving the
Disjoint Paths
problem
[
68
]
.
The core idea of this technique, from its original setting
[
68
]
to a striking majority of its subsequent applications
in algorithm design for minor-free graphs, is that a candidate solution for the problem can be “rerouted away” from some sufficiently internal part of a grid, typically emerging from a structure called
flat wall
.
This allows the characterization of the “avoidable” vertices as
irrelevant
, in the sense that their removal does not change the
yes
/
no
status of the instance.
The repeated removal of such vertices results in an instance of bounded treewidth that is equivalent to the original instance. In the reduced instance, bounded treewidth can be used
algorithmically, typically via explicit dynamic programming or using the meta-result of
Proposition

1
, in order to solve the problem.

However, the above approach does not readily work for model checking. The reason is that, even for
FO
,
the removal of irrelevant vertices from a graph that has unbounded treewidth (or is even just planar) for some given fixed formula
\varphi
would result to a bounded-treewidth graph that is equivalent, in terms of satisfaction of
\varphi
, this
would contradict the undecidability of the satisfiability problem
(i.e., whether, for a given formula
\varphi
,
there is some graph satisfying
\varphi
)
for
FO
;
see e.g.
[
55

Such an obstacle can be avoided by “shifting” from the initial problem with input
(G,\varphi)

to an alternative problem in which the input graph
G

of its vertex set, for some

depending on
\varphi

are asked to satisfy some new formula

Initially, each

This idea, originating in

Reductions between AMTs.

In our setting,
irrelevant vertices for the annotated type are obtained via a “local-global” argument.
We detect an area of the graph where “

In other words, we describe a way to localize the task of finding irrelevant vertices in a two-fold manner:
“locally” irrelevant vertices are also “globally” irrelevant, and such vertices can be found

Novelties of our approach.

As discussed earlier, the notion of reduction between AMTs that we introduce is a new concept that stems from the approach of
[
30
,
40
]
and evolves it in a more abstract and general way, aiming to establish it as a base for the study and the proof of other AMTs. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that the irrelevant-vertex technique is abstracted as a
method
for the derivation of AMTs in such a general setting.

The crucial property of having bounded annotated treewidth is not only allowing us to obtain a fragment of
\mathsf{CMSO}
for which model checking
can
be tractable on unbounded-treewidth inputs (without contradicting general complexity assumptions; see
[
43
, Section 9]
),
but also permits to see sets of bounded annotated treewidth as “local” sets inside a grid-like structure. In fact, sets of bounded annotated treewidth are
local
inside
flat railed annuli
: systems of concentric cycles in a graph together with a collection of disjoint paths orthogonally traversing them, in such a way that there are no long “jumps” in the graph that can connect distant cycles or paths (see
Figure

2
).
The locality of sets of bounded annotated treewidth allow us to lift locality arguments for
FO
and, in particular, argue that for every tuple of sets of bounded annotated treewidth inside a flat railed annulus, we can find a “strip” of the railed annulus that is disjoint of these sets and crops out some part of them; see
Lemma 17
. This property lies in the very base of our arguments toward showing the Local-Global-Irrelevancy Condition in our reduction.

In fact, this property is essential to prove both items (b) and (c) of the
Local-Global-Irrelevancy Condition
. Let us particularly stress that, in order to prove (c), one has to show that there is a way to “locally” reduce the
R_{i}
’s while not changing the (partial) annotated type of the (partial) annotated graph.
For
R_{i}
’s that correspond to first-order quantification, one may simply locally restrict
R_{i}
only to “representative” vertices that represent
the satisfaction of all different annotated types of one level less and discard duplicates, eventually leading to a “small” number of representative vertices for each
R_{i}
. This was the approach of
[
40
,
38
]
.
In the case of
MSO
-quantification,
this approach fails because the (union of) these representative sets have unbounded size and therefore we should not hope to bound the total number of sets in a locally representative
R_{i}^{\prime}
.
To deal with this, we use the fact that the union of representative sets has bounded annotated treewidth (
Proposition

30
).
In a large flat railed annulus, we can find a strip disjoint from these sets. We use this observation to iteratively “zoom” inside such a strip to
prove that we can restrict each
R_{i}
after in an area that is disjoint from the already restricted
R_{1},\ldots,R_{i-1}

Lemma

31

Another cornerstone of our approach is that the quantifier
\exists_{k}X
(as well as
\forall_{k}X

FO
+

Shifting to disjoint paths, we can use existing tools to achieve the

in the presence of disjoint-paths predicates,

.
In our work,

The framework of flat railed annuli that we present in this paper, which serves as the main combinatorial base of the

4.5
Finding a flat railed annulus

We now formally define the notion of treewidth and of a tree decomposition.
A

where

1.

\bigcup_{t\in V(T)}\chi(t)=V(G),

for every edge

such that

3.

for every

The

4.6
Linkages in railed annuli

A

in a graph

is a subgraph

of
G
whose connected components are paths of at least one edge.
The
paths
of a linkage are its connected components and we denote them by
\mathcal{P}(L)
.
The

of

is the number of its paths, i.e.,
|\mathcal{P}(L)|
.
The
terminals
of a linkage

, denoted by
T(L)
, are the endpoints of the paths of
L
, and the
pattern
of
L
is the set
\{\{s,t\}\mid\mathcal{P}(L)\text{ contains some $(s,t)$-path}\}
.
Two linkages

of
G
are
equivalent
, which we denote by
L_{1}\equiv L_{2}
, if they have the same pattern.
By definition, given two linkages
L_{1},L_{2}
such that
V(L_{1})\cap V(L_{2})=T(L_{1})\cap T(L_{2})
, the graph
L_{1}\cup L_{2}
is a linkage and
T(L_{1}\cup L_{2})
is the symmetric difference of
T(L_{1})
and
T(L_{2})
, i.e.,
T(L_{1}\cup L_{2})=(T(L_{1})\setminus T(L_{2}))\cup(T(L_{2})\setminus T(L_{1})).

In other words, when taking the union of two linkages, the resulting vertices of degree two are not considered as terminals anymore.

Therefore, for every

(5)

7
Conclusions and directions for further research

In this paper we proved that model checking on non-trivial
minor-closed graph classes is tractable for problems expressible
in the newly introduced logic
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}+{\sf dp}
(
Theorem

2
). We wish to stress
that our proof is constructive, in the sense that one can construct a (meta-)algorithm that receives as an input a formula
\textphi\in\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}+{\sf dp}
and an integer
h
, and outputs an algorithm
\mathbb{A}_{\textphi,h}

that, given an
K_{h}
-minor-free graph
G
, outputs whether

G\models\textphi
or not.
Theorem

2
holds also for structures where the combinatorial restriction is that the Gaifman graph
excludes
H
as a minor. In our presentation we adopted the graph-theoretic setting, as the proof for the “more general” version on structures is
not essentially different but notationally much heavier.

On the choice of
{\sf tw}
in the parametrization of the quantifiers.

Note that, in the definition of
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}
, based on the parameterized quantification introduced in (
2
), annotated treewidth plays a pivotal role. The annotated version of every
minor-monotone graph parameter
{\sf p}
(such as treewidth, as it is our case) was defined in
[
76
]
by setting
{\sf p}(G,X):=\max\{{\sf p}(H)\mid\mbox{$H$ is an $X$-minor of $G$}\}
. Likewise, one may
parameterize the quantification in
CMSO
formulas by
setting

\exists_{{\sf p}\leq k}X\ \textphi\coloneqq\exists X\ ({\sf p}(G,X)\leq k\wedge\textphi),

hence defining the fragment
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}
of
\mathsf{CMSO}
.
Certainly, if
{\sf p}
is any parameter that is functionally larger than
{\sf tw}
, then
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}
is more expressive than
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}
(recall that a parameter
{\sf p}
is
functionally larger
than a parameter
{\sf p}^{\prime}
if there is a function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
such that for every graph

G
,
{\sf p}^{\prime}(G)\leq f({\sf p}(G))
). But what if we consider
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}

for some parameter
{\sf p}
that is
not
functionally larger than
{\sf tw}
? Such a parameter might be, for instance, the Euler genus, the apex
12
12

12

The
apex number
of a graph
G
is the minimum number of vertices whose removal yields a planar graph.
number, or the Hadwiger number. In such a case, the parameter
{\sf p}
would be bounded for all planar graphs, which, in turn,
would imply that, on planar graphs,
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}
would be as expressive as
\mathsf{CMSO}
. This, combined with the impossibility results of
[
52
,
53
,
57
]
(see also
[
43
, Section 9]
)
implies that no AMT as the one of
Theorem

2
can be expected for
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}

when
{\sf p}
is not functionally larger than treewidth. In this sense,
the choice of treewidth in the definition of
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}
is the
best possible

for an AMT as the one in
Theorem

2
. This justifies the use of
\exists_{k}X\ \textphi
as a simplified way to write
\exists_{{\sf tw}\leq k}X\ \textphi
in (
2
), and indicates that
Theorem

2
is “optimal” in what concerns the choice of the fragment
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}
.

The introduction of
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}
for every minor-monotone
parameter introduces a “fine-grained” viewpoint of
CMSO

whose algorithmic properties, in our opinion, deserve
independent research. Is it possible to generate analogous hierarchies
for other partial ordering relations and other fragments of
CMSO
, such as
CMSO
1
where quantification is allowed only on vertex sets?

Further than minors.

The next question is whether and when we may go further than the
combinatorial threshold of minor-closedness. The next horizon for this might be topological minor-closedness, where we know that

FO+conn
and the more general
FO+dp
admit tractable model checking because of the results in
[
65
]
and
[
72
]
, respectively.
We are not in position to conjecture whether it is possible to replace, in
Theorem

2
,
H
-minor-freeness by

H
-topological-minor-freeness.
However, we cannot exclude that this might be possible for
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf p}
for some suitable choice of
{\sf p}
such as
{\sf vc}
(vertex cover) or
{\sf td}
(treedepth).
Note that for all logics that subsume
FO+conn
, such as
FO+dp
,
{\sf\tilde{\Theta}}^{\sf dp}
, or
CMSO/tw+dp
(cf.
Figure

1
),
H
-topological-minor-freeness is the most general combinatorial condition we may hope for if we restrict to monotone graph classes (assuming a mild condition on the efficiency
of the encoding), as proved in
[
65
]
.

Extending the predicates.

Another direction is to consider variants or extensions of
\mathsf{CMSO}/{\sf tw}
that use more expressive predicates than the disjoint paths. In the end of
Subsection

2.2
we introduced the
logic
\mathsf{CMSO/tw}\!+\!\mathsf{dp}^{+}
where we additionally demand the disjoint paths to avoid some vertex set. Our proofs can be easily adapted in order to work for this more general logic as follows. First we note that after replacing the use of the disjoint-paths predicate by
\mathsf{dp}^{+}
in the notion of annotated types, semi-annotated types, and layered types, all results from
Section

3
and
Section

5
are directly obtained for
\mathsf{CMSO/tw}\!+\!\mathsf{dp}^{+}
. Moreover, in what concerns
Proposition

21
from
Subsection

5.2
, let us stress that because of the Linkage Combing Lemma
[
39
, Corollary 3.4]
the obtained linkages
\tilde{L}_{1},\tilde{L}_{2}
can be assumed to have the additional property that
(\tilde{L}_{1}\cup\tilde{L}_{2})\setminus V({\sf Leveling}_{(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{R})}^{\langle\mathcal{C}_{\bar{w}}\rangle}(G))\subseteq L\setminus V({\sf Leveling}_{(\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{R})}^{\langle\mathcal{C}_{\bar{w}}\rangle}(G))
. Using this additional assumption, the core result of the proof of the
Local-Global-Irrelevancy Condition
in
Section

6
, namely
Lemma

28
, can be proved for
\mathsf{CMSO/tw}\!+\!\mathsf{dp}^{+}
. The only modification comes in the last part of the proof, where the additional assumption is used to guarantee that if the linkage
L
is disjoint from the sets in
\bar{V}^{\mathsf{in}}
then the same holds for the linkage
\tilde{L}_{1}\cup\tilde{L}_{2}
.

Another direction is to consider parity conditions in the paths of
{\sf dp}_{k}(s_{1},t_{1},\ldots,s_{k},t_{k})
. We believe that our technique can be applied in this direction as well.

Irrelevant vertices in sub-quadratic time.

An insisting open question is whether (or when) it is possible to improve the quadratic running time of the algorithms implied by
Theorem

2
. Notice that this running time
is quite linked to the idea of the irrelevant vertex technique, given that the irrelevant parts
are removed one by one and the cost their detection is already linear.
An improvement of the running time in
Theorem

2

would require either the detection of “many” irrelevant vertices at once
(this is been achieved so far for particular modification problems such as in
[
45
]
) or
would be based on new ideas beyond the irrelevant vertex technique.

Reductions between AMTs.

In
Subsection

2.3
we defined a concept of reduction between AMTs based on the notion of
vertex irrelevancy
and the fact that
“local irrelevancy”, certified by some AMT, may imply “global irrelevancy”, yielding AMTs on more general combinatorial structures.
We defined this reducibility notion in a most abstract way as we believe that it might be a good base for the study, the understanding, and the proof of other AMTs in the future.
