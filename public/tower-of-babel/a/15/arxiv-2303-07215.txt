Surface instability in a nematic elastomer
Morgan Barnes, Fan Feng, John S. Biggins

Abstract
Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) are soft phase-changing solids that exhibit large reversible contractions upon heating, Goldstone-like soft modes and resultant microstructural instabilities. We heat a planar LCE slab to isotropic, clamp the lower surface then cool back to nematic. Clamping prevents macroscopic elongation, producing compression and microstructure. We see that the free surface destabilizes, adopting topography with amplitude and wavelength similar to thickness. To understand the instability, we numerically compute the microstructural relaxation of a “non-ideal” LCE energy. Linear stability reveals a Biot-like scale-free instability, but with oblique wavevector. However, simulation and experiment show that, unlike classic elastic creasing, instability culminates in a cross-hatch without cusps or hysteresis, and is constructed entirely from low-stress soft modes.



Liquid crystal elastomers (LCEs) are actuating solids that recall the dramatic shape transformations of biological tissues. Microscopically, LCEs are networks of LC polymers
Warner and Terentjev 2007
. Actuation occurs via the isotropic-nematic phase transition, which biases conformations along the director, generating large elongations on cooling, and muscular contraction on heating
Küpfer and Finkelmann 1991
;
De Gennes et al. 1997
. Correspondingly, LCEs fabricated with spatial director profiles can actuate into complex surfaces
White and Broer 2015
such as cones
Modes et al. 2011
;
Guin et al. 2018
or faces
Aharoni et al. 2018
;
Barnes and Verduzco 2019
. The symmetry breaking character of the isotropic-nematic transition also endows LCEs with Goldstone-like “soft-modes” in which deformations induce director rotation at almost zero energy/stress
Golubović and Lubensky 1989
;
Warner et al. 1994
. Such modes enable actuation by modest electric fields
Urayama et al. 2006
;
Corbett and Warner 2009
and generate martensitic
Bhattacharya 2003
microstrucural instabilities
Verwey et al. 1996
;
Finkelmann et al. 1997
;
DeSimone and Dolzmann 2002
. However, despite progress in idealized cases
DeSimone and Dolzmann 2002
;
Conti et al. 2002
;
Biggins 2009
, we still lack a realistic coarse-grained constitutive model that accounts for microstructure, preventing analysis of macroscopic LCEs under load.

Mechanical instabilities like buckling provide an attractive additional route to complex morphing without pre-patterning. For example, the Biot creasing instability sculpts cusped folds at the free surface of soft solids under compression
Biot 1965
;
Trujillo et al. 2008
;
Hohlfeld and Mahadevan 2011
;
Tallinen et al. 2013
, and underpins morphogenesis of villi
Shyer et al. 2013
and sulci/gyri
Tallinen et al. 2014
. Here, we combine experiment, theory and computation to tackle the analogous compressive surface instability in LCEs, which is profoundly enriched by soft modes and microstructure.

To explain the instability, we first model the LCE as a simple rubber slab undergoing a spontaneous elongation whilst clamped below, leading to a displacement field
\mathbf{u}
(from isotropic) and deformation gradient
\bm{\Lambda}={\bf I}+\nabla\mathbf{u}
. A standard neo-Hookean rubber with shear modulus
\mu

W_{NH}(\bm{\Lambda})={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mu\mathrm{Tr}(\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}^{\rm T})
, and is strictly incompressible
\mathrm{Det}{(\bm{\Lambda})}=1
. Accordingly, the LCE energy is
W_{NH}(\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}_{s}^{-1})
, where
\bm{\Lambda}_{s}(\mathbf{n})=\lambda_{s}{\mathbf{n}}\otimes{\mathbf{n}}+\lambda_{s}^{-1/2}({\bf I}-{\mathbf{n}}\otimes{\mathbf{n}})
is the spontaneous elongation. Minimizing this energy in an infinite planar slab with a clamped foundation is known to generate a standard Biot-type surface instability, with cusped furrows (creases) appearing sub-critically at the linear threshold of
\lambda_{s}\approx 2.27

Biot 1965
;
Hong et al. 2009
, and being global minimizers beyond the non-linear nucleation threshold of
\lambda_{s}\approx 1.77

Hong et al. 2009
;
Hohlfeld and Mahadevan 2011
(SI Sec. V
sup
). We confirm these expectations with 3D finite elements, using a bespoke C-code from
Tallinen et al. 2013
that divides the slab into constant strain tetrahedra, and moves nodes via damped Newtonian dynamics. These calculations exhibit regular crease-lines perpendicular to the director, agreeing with previous elastomer experiments
Cai et al. 2012
and numerics
Tallinen et al. 2013
, but disagreeing with the smooth cross-hatch in LCEs.

{\mathbf{n}}
can rotate within the LCE, enriching the energy with soft modes. Explicitly minimizing over (unit) directors leads to an energy depending solely on deformation

(1)

Furthermore, the energy is susceptible to microstructure (Fig.
2
(a,b)). E.g., if an LCE cools forming
{\mathbf{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{y}}
and is then stretched along
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
, then at an imposed stretch of
\lambda_{s}^{3/2}
, it will again attain a low energy state with
{\mathbf{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}
. However, intermediate pure stretches are not soft, as the corresponding ground states require shear. Nevertheless, the LCE can accommodate pure stretch macroscopically by rapidly switching between bands of alternating shear: a laminar “stripe-domain” micro-structure
Finkelmann et al. 1997
that is geometrically compatible and soft.

More generally, any pure shear can be expressed as
{\bf S}(\bm{\gamma},\hat{\mathbf{m}})={\bf I}+\bm{\gamma}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{m}}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{m}}
defines the shear plane and
\bm{\gamma}
, orthogonal, encodes magnitude/direction. A lamination averaging to
\bm{\Lambda}
is then constructed as:

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}=f\bm{\Lambda}_{1}+(1-f)\bm{\Lambda}_{2}\mathrm{\ \ \ \ where}

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}_{1}={\bf S}((1-f)\mathbf{a},\hat{\mathbf{m}})\bm{\Lambda},\mathrm{\ \ \ \ and\ \ \ \ }\bm{\Lambda}_{2}={\bf S}(-f\mathbf{a},\hat{\mathbf{m}})\bm{\Lambda},

and
0<f<1
is the volume fraction of
\bm{\Lambda}_{1}
. If the resultant energy,
fW(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})+(1-f)W(\bm{\Lambda}_{2})
, is lower than
W(\bm{\Lambda})
then microstructure is favoured. The full relaxation of
W_{I}(\bm{\Lambda})
has been constructed
DeSimone and Dolzmann 2002
, showing that any
\bm{\Lambda}
with principle stretches less extreme than
\lambda_{s}
may be achieved softly with double laminates. Since this set contains
\bm{\Lambda}={\bf I}
, the LCE can microstrucurally accommodate the experimental compression, motivating turbidity but not macroscopic surface instability.

The final missing physics is non-ideality: fabrication imprinted a preferred director
{\mathbf{n}}_{0}
, breaking degeneracy. Imprinting generates an additional bulk anchoring term (akin to
E
or
B
fields in liquid nematics
Ye et al. 2007
) parameterized by a small coefficient
\alpha
penalizing rotation away from
{\mathbf{n}}_{0}

Verwey and Warner 1997
:

\displaystyle W_{NI}(\bm{\Lambda})=\min_{\mathbf{n}}

\displaystyle\left(W_{NH}(\bm{\Lambda}\bm{\Lambda}_{s}^{-1}({\mathbf{n}}))\right.

(3)

\displaystyle\left.+{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\alpha\lambda_{s}\mu\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\Lambda}({\bf I}-{\mathbf{n}}_{0}\otimes{\mathbf{n}}_{0})\bm{\Lambda}^{\rm T}{\mathbf{n}}\otimes{\mathbf{n}}\right)\right).

We again minimize over
{\mathbf{n}}
by collecting the relevant terms as
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\mu\mathrm{Tr}({\bf M}{\mathbf{n}}\otimes{\mathbf{n}})
, and directing
{\mathbf{n}}
along
{\bf M}

in finite elements to simulate the LCE slab (SI Sec. VII). An initial calculation clamping top and bottom surfaces (
\mathbf{\Lambda}=\mathbf{I}

Releasing the top produces promising thickness-scale topography, but atop mesh-scale oscillations. Ultimately, this approach is unsatisfactory, as the (non quasi-convex

;
DeSimone and Dolzmann 2002
) energy’s minimizers are infinitely fine microstructures. Physically, resolution requires a Frank energy
{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}K|\nabla{\mathbf{n}}|^{2}
which smooths director variation over the “nematic penetration depth”
l\sim\sqrt{K/\mu}\sim 10^{-8}\mathrm{m}

Warner and Terentjev 2007
, regularizing the interfaces
Finkelmann et al. 1997
. Incorporating Frank energy enables converged mesh-independent results
Zhou and Bhattacharya 2021
, but requires meshes fine compared to
l
that are infeasible for macroscopic samples.

Instead, we adopt a two-scale approach, first computing the microstructural relaxation of
W_{NI}
for any average deformation, then using this relaxed energy for macroscopic analysis. This relaxation problem is solved analytically for films in tension
Finkelmann et al. 1997
;
Conti et al. 2002
, but a 3D result remains distant so we construct it numerically. The space of 3D deformations
\bm{\Lambda}
is 9D, however incompressibility, frame indifference, and uniaxial material symmetry allow the energy to be parameterized by just four scalar invariants
Spencer 1972
;
Destrade et al. 2013
. We may thus re-express any nematic energy as
W(\bm{\Lambda})=w(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{3},\theta,\phi)
,
\theta
,
\phi
being the latitude and longitude of
{\mathbf{n}}_{0}
in the (reference) stretch axes. To relax the energy, we construct a grid of
\sim
50k points over this space, spanning
1\leq\Lambda_{3}\leq 4
with
\delta\Lambda_{3}=0.1

\bm{\Lambda}_{t}(\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{3},\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle W_{R1}(\bm{\Lambda})=\min_{f,\hat{\mathbf{m}},\mathbf{a}\perp\hat{\mathbf{m}}}fW_{NI}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})+(1-f)W_{NI}(\bm{\Lambda}_{2}).

(4)

Linear stability analysis offers further insight. Owing to finite compressibility, the equilibrium equations admit a transitionally invariant base state
\bm{\Lambda}_{0}={\bf I}+\gamma\hat{\mathbf{z}}\otimes\hat{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle W_{N}=W_{N}(\bm{\Lambda}^{0})+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}W_{N}}{\partial\Lambda_{ij}\partial\Lambda_{lm}}\bigg|_{\bm{\Lambda}^{0}}\Lambda^{1}_{ij}\Lambda^{1}_{lm}.

Our work thus shows that the exotic constitutive law of LCEs still supports a compressive surface instability, with similar scale-free properties to Biot creasing, but also fundamental differences in pattern, criticality and singularity. We anticipate similar behavior will be found in other soft solids with distinctive constitutive laws: highly anisotropic solids (fiber reinforced elastomers, biological tissues), phase changing solids, active nematic solids and mechanistic meta-materials are all important and analogous examples. Hopefully, such studies will also generate a stronger intuition for Biot-type instabilities, and ultimately enable a predictive analytic framework. Within LCEs, our rigorously homogenized constitutive law also enables quantitative mechanical engineering including studies of other instabilities

The LCE surface instability is also an exemplar of instabilities providing complex morphing without correspondingly complex fabrication. A limitation is that this approach provides little control over pattern. However, some more complex morphing may be regained by combining instability with simple patterned fabrication. As an example, we repeat our experiment, using an LCE slab containing a radial director pattern (fabricated by forming a cone during programming), which generates cross-hatches in a striking log-spiral (Fig.
5
(c,d)).
The instability’s switchable high amplitude topography suggests applications in smart surfaces. For example, a droplet sitting atop the instability (Fig.
5
(e,f)) forms a Wenzel state

;

;
Oopath et al. 2022
. Further smart-surface applications could include switchable aerodynamics, haptics, adhesion and friction.
